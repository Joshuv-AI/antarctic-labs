First report of gasless laparoscopy for the treatment of severe ectopic dioctophymosis in a puppy
Authors: Pâmela Caye, Franciéli Mallmann Pozzobon, Bianca Bertoletti, Ariane Diniz da Silveira, Laís Formiga Silva, Josaine Cristina da Silva Rappeti, Guilherme Albuquerque de Oliveira Cavalcanti, Eduarda Aléxia Nunes Louzada Dias Cavalcanti, Maurício Veloso Brun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDioctophymosis is uncommon in juvenile dogs, both in renal and ectopic presentations. Caused by the nematode Dioctophyme renale, which shows low sensitivity to anthelmintic drugs, surgery is considered the treatment of choice. An alternative approach is gasless laparoscopic surgery, in which pneumoperitoneum is not established and the surgical working space is created by mechanical elevation of the abdominal wall. This technique maintains normobaric intra-abdominal conditions and avoids the cardiorespiratory alterations, ischemia–reperfusion injury, and inflammatory response associated with pneumoperitoneum. This study reports the case of a dog approximately four months of age with severe ectopic parasitic infestation, treated by gasless laparoscopy. The animal originated from a dog shelter during the May 2024 floods in southern Brazil, and was referred for ultrasonographic evaluation, which revealed numerous tubular structures surrounding the right kidney. Neither kidney showed intrarenal involvement. Surgical removal of the parasites was performed via gasless laparoscopy using a multidirectional traction platform (VET90000; Bhio Supply®, RS, Brazil). The system consisted of a base fixed to the surgical table, to which hemostatic forceps were attached to secure the sutures used for elevation of the abdominal wall musculature. Two laparoscopic ports were used without gas control valves. Nematodes were found encircling the entire right kidney and were grasped and directly removed, totaling nine worms measuring 10 to 41 cm in length. The patient showed good postoperative recovery. The VET90000 platform enabled laparoscopy without the need for hyperbaric pneumoperitoneum and facilitated parasite removal due to the absence of valves in the working ports. Gasless laparoscopy proved to be a feasible and curative approach for treating severe ectopic dioctophymosis in a juvenile dog.Keywords: Dioctophyme renale, Gasless laparoscopy, Ectopic dioctophymosis

Introduction

The nematode Dioctophyme renale, also known as the “giant kidney worm,” is named for its large size and predilection for the right kidney (Pedrassani and Nascimento 2015; Caye et al. 2020, 2021). Its erratic life cycle is characterized by migration and localization outside renal tissue. Although infrequently reported in puppies, dogs younger than six months of age may harbor adult specimens, either within the kidneys or free in the abdominal and thoracic cavities (Stainki et al. 2011; Caye et al. 2021; Amaro et al. 2022).

Surgical management remains the most effective therapeutic approach, involving removal of the worms or excision of the affected kidney (Pedrassani and Nascimento 2015; Caye et al. 2020, 2021; Amaro et al. 2022). Procedures may be performed using conventional open techniques or minimally invasive approaches, including nephroscopy and laparoscopy (Caye et al. 2020, 2021, 2023, 2024a, b; Milech et al. 2022). The present study describes a puppy with severe abdominal infestation by Dioctophyme renale, successfully managed through surgical removal using gasless laparoscopy, representing the first report of this technique applied in such a case.

Case report

A four-month-old puppy was diagnosed with severe ectopic dioctophymosis during ultrasonographic screening conducted in a temporary animal shelter in Pelotas, Rio Grande do Sul, Brazil, established after the severe flooding events that affected southern Brazil in 2024. Ultrasonographic assessment revealed tubular structures with hyperechoic margins and a hypoechoic center, encircling the entire right kidney (Fig. 1A - D), without evidence of intrarenal involvement in either kidney. Notably, the dam of the puppy was also diagnosed with renal dioctophymosis. The patient, weighing 4 kg, presented with abdominal distension, pain on palpation, lethargy, and mild dehydration. Hematological analysis indicated mild anemia (Hct 31.7% - reference range 34–40%; Hb 10.5 g/dL – reference range 11–15.5 g/dL) and eosinophilia (1815/mm³ - reference range 100–1250 / mm³).

The patient was referred for surgical management to remove the worms from the abdominal cavity using gasless laparoscopy. Premedication consisted of methadone (0.3 mg/kg, IM). Anesthesia was induced with propofol administered to effect until orotracheal intubation was achieved. Anesthesia was maintained with isoflurane vaporized in 100% oxygen. Intraoperative analgesia was provided by a continuous rate infusion of fentanyl (3 µg/kg/h).

After clipping of the entire abdominal region and anesthetic stabilization, the animal was positioned in dorsal recumbency. A multidirectional traction platform (VET 90000; Bhio Supply®, RS, Brazil) was placed transversely to the patient. The system consisted of a base fixed to the surgical table, to which a central vertical rod and a horizontal rod were attached. The horizontal rod supported hemostatic forceps used to secure the sutures for elevation of the abdominal wall musculature.

The procedure began with placement of a 6-mm umbilical port using the Hasson technique, followed by abdominal inspection with a 5-mm 30° rigid endoscope. Subsequently, immediately cranial to the first surgical access, a 2 − 0 nylon suture incorporating the muscular fascia was placed to elevate the abdominal wall. This suture was tensioned and secured to the VET 90000 platform. Two additional traction sutures were placed craniolaterally, to the right and left of the initial access. A second 6-mm port was positioned in the right paramedian region, caudal to the first. Both ports were used without gas control valves, as the gasless technique employs normobaric pneumoperitoneum (Fig. 2A).

(A) Abdominal wall elevation using the multidirectional traction platform VET 90000 (Bhio Supply®, RS, Brazil) and removal of a Dioctophyme renale nematode through the surgical port (arrow); (B) removal of a nematode through the laparoscopic port; (C) final appearance of the two skin incisions (arrows) in relation to a scalpel blade (upper); (D) nine D. renale specimens removed from the abdominal cavity

Inspection of the abdominal cavity revealed a marked inflammatory reaction and a moderate amount of reddish free fluid, predominantly located near the right kidney. Tissue manipulation was performed using straight atraumatic fenestrated grasping forceps. Nematodes were found encircling the entire right kidney, interspersed within the omentum in cranial, medial, and caudal regions. Cranial positioning of the ports limited inspection of the caudolateral region, which was resolved by lateralization of the patient. The serosanguineous fluid was aspirated and submitted for analysis.

The 5-mm endoscope initially used did not provide sufficient illumination. Therefore, the paramedian port was replaced with a larger one (11 mm), and a 10-mm rigid endoscope with a 30° viewing angle was employed. A total of nine worms were grasped and directly removed through the 6-mm port (Fig. 2B). The entire abdominal cavity was thoroughly inspected. After completion of the search for worms, the cavity was irrigated with 250 mL of warmed saline solution. Abdominal closure was performed in three layers. The muscular layer was closed using a simple cruciate suture pattern, and the subcutaneous tissue with an interrupted horizontal mattress pattern, both using 3 − 0 polydioxanone. The skin was sutured using an interrupted horizontal mattress pattern with 4 − 0 nylon, resulting in two skin sutures (Fig. 2C). Total surgical time was 105 min. Postoperative medications included tramadol (4 mg/kg, SC), dipyrone (25 mg/kg, IV), and meloxicam (0.1 mg/kg, PO) administered for three days.

Four females measuring 41, 35, 22, and 22 cm, and five males measuring 18, 16, 11, 10, and 10 cm were removed from the abdominal cavity (Fig. 2D). Analysis of the abdominal fluid revealed a cytological sample composed of inflammatory cells, a moderate number of erythrocytes, and D. renale eggs. The patient exhibited good appetite and activity. A follow-up abdominal ultrasonographic examination ruled out the presence of free worms. Telephone follow-up with the owner revealed that the patient remained clinically stable 180 days after gasless laparoscopy.

Discussion and conclusion

The surgery was completed using two small incisions, characterizing the procedure as minimally invasive. When compared with conventional laparotomies performed in other cases of ectopic parasitism, the resolution of the condition in this patient was exemplary (Caye et al. 2020, 2021, 2024a), resulting in cure and excellent postoperative recovery. The authors highlight that the animal described in this report belonged to a household with seven other adult dogs, three of which tested positive for D. renale. To date, there is no evidence in the literature supporting transuterine, transplacental, or transmammary transmission. However, as questioned by Amaro et al. (2022), considering the long prepatent period of D. renale, alimentary transmission in such young dogs appears unlikely. Nevertheless, there are also no published reports of parasitized puppies born to dams infected with the giant kidney worm. Thus, although not conclusive, the present findings suggest that vertical transmission of dioctophymosis cannot be excluded.

Conventional video-assisted surgeries are performed with insufflation of the abdominal cavity and creation of a pneumoperitoneum, which generates the working space and allows for small surgical incisions (Brun 2015; Scott et al. 2020). The use of high pneumoperitoneum pressures may lead to cardiopulmonary and visceral alterations, affecting organs such as the kidneys and pancreas (Mayhew 2011; Hejazi et al. 2013; Mayhew et al. 2013). In this context, gasless laparoscopy techniques were developed, in which there is no increase in intra-abdominal pressure. These techniques rely on different methods of abdominal wall traction to create a normobaric working space. Consequently, there is no elevation in intra-abdominal pressure or absorption of CO₂, a gas commonly used for insufflation (Fransson et al. 2015; Brun et al. 2022; Milech et al. 2023). Although no renal involvement was observed, a minimally invasive surgical approach with reduced postoperative inflammatory response was considered the most appropriate option given the presence of established peritonitis.

The multidirectional traction platform VET 90000 was developed from a prototype validated for abdominal procedures, including diaphragmatic hernia repair and renal biopsy (Brun et al. 2022; Milech et al. 2023). However, in the present case, it was used for the first time for the removal of free D. renale from the abdominal cavity.

The main intraoperative technical advantage identified by the surgeons was the ease of worm removal resulting from the absence of valves in the working port. Based on the authors’ experience, during conventional laparoscopy using 6-mm ports, each worm removal typically requires disassembly of the port valve; otherwise, the worm becomes lodged within the valve and may rupture during extraction. When 11-mm ports are used, an alternative approach is extraction through the reducer.

In procedures performed with abdominal insufflation, both valve disassembly and use of a reducer result in immediate loss of pneumoperitoneum, compromising the working space and increasing gas consumption. This may predispose the patient to hypothermia due to the large volume of insufflated gas (Wang et al. 2025). In contrast, gasless laparoscopy allowed efficient worm removal without alterations in intra-abdominal pressure or loss of the visual field.

Careful planning of laparoscopic port placement is essential (Brun 2015), as the traction platform may hinder inspection of the cavity in the direction opposite to the traction sutures. In the present case, the platform was positioned cranial to the incisions, which limited endoscope rotation and inspection of the caudal region. To achieve lateralization of the patient, it was necessary to relax the traction sutures and readjust them after repositioning. Nevertheless, this did not preclude adequate evaluation of the abdominal cavity. Surgical success was confirmed by follow-up abdominal ultrasonography, which ensured complete removal of all worms.

Despite the absence of renal involvement, the worms were distributed over the entire surface of the right kidney. The presence of free D. renale eggs within the abdominal cavity indicates that the worms had already reached adulthood. Previous studies suggest that the parasite may enter the kidney as an adult (L5 larval stage) (Pedrassani and Nascimento 2015). Considering the location and number of worms, there was a high risk of severe infestation and renal destruction, which justified prompt surgical intervention.

Gasless laparoscopy can be effectively applied as a minimally invasive and curative technique for the treatment of severe ectopic dioctophymosis in a puppy, enabling safe and complete worm removal under normobaric intra-abdominal conditions. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report describing the use of this technique in such a case, highlighting its potential as a promising option for young animals requiring abdominal interventions and for reducing the risks associated with laparoscopic procedures involving pneumoperitoneum.
