Predictors of postoperative visual and auditory hallucinations in hypertensive vs. non-hypertensive cardiac surgery patients: insights from the prospective multicenter VAACS study

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHallucinations are common yet under-recognized after cardiac surgery and contribute to postoperative morbidity. Hypertension (HTN), the most prevalent comorbidity in surgical candidates, may alter susceptibility through impaired cerebrovascular autoregulation. Whether HTN modifies predictors of postoperative visual and auditory hallucinations remains unclear.MethodsIn the prospective, multicenter VAACS study, 1,332 adults undergoing CABG or valve surgery were stratified by hypertension (HTN, n = 517; non-HTN, n = 815). Visual and auditory hallucinations within 7 days were assessed with the Questionnaire for Psychotic Experiences. Multivariable Cox regression was performed separately in the HTN and non-HTN cohorts.ResultsVisual hallucinations occurred in 12.0% of HTN and 11.3% of non-HTN patients; auditory hallucinations in 6.2% and 7.4%, respectively. In HTN patients, visual hallucinations were independently predicted by higher EuroSCORE II (adjusted HR, 1.041; 95% CI, 1.021–1.061; p < 0.001) and higher neutrophil counts (adjusted HR, 1.018; 95% CI, 1.008–1.028; p < 0.001), while longer postoperative ventilation was associated with a reduced risk (adjusted HR, 0.861; 95% CI, 0.780–0.950; p = 0.003). For auditory hallucinations in HTN patients, younger age (adjusted HR, 0.965; 95% CI, 0.932–0.998; p = 0.039), prior antibiotic use (adjusted HR, 2.464; 95% CI, 1.550–3.916; p < 0.001), higher neutrophil counts (adjusted HR, 1.016; 95% CI, 1.005–1.027; p = 0.004), and shorter ventilation duration (adjusted HR, 0.791; 95% CI, 0.709–0.883; p < 0.001) were independently associated with risk. In non-HTN patients, EuroSCORE II was independently associated with visual hallucinations (adjusted HR, 1.051; 95% CI, 1.043–1.060; p < 0.001). For auditory hallucinations in non-HTN patients, a higher EuroSCORE II increased the risk (adjusted HR 1.101, 95% CI 1.086–1.117, p < 0.001), while valve surgery versus CABG was protective (adjusted HR 0.463, 95% CI 0.221–0.970, p = 0.041). ROC analysis of a simple model (EuroSCORE II, ventilation, neutrophils) showed better discrimination in HTN (AUC 0.68) than in non-HTN (AUC 0.54; p ≈ 0.04–0.045).ConclusionsPredictors of postoperative hallucinations differ by HTN status. In HTN patients, inflammatory burden, age-related vulnerability, antibiotic exposure, and ventilatory course were the dominant determinants of risk. In non-HTN patients, overall operative risk as reflected by EuroSCORE II and surgery type played a more prominent role.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13019-026-04396-z.

Introduction

Cardiac surgery is among the most frequently performed and critical surgical procedures worldwide, with more than 1 million operations performed annually [1]. Coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) accounts for nearly half of all major cardiac surgeries, with approximately 36.7 CABG procedures and 30.8 valve surgeries per 100,000 population, representing a substantial operational burden across health systems in high-income countries [1]. Although these operations can significantly enhance patients’ quality of life and address serious cardiovascular conditions, postoperative complications remain common and significantly affect outcomes. Neurological and neuropsychiatric complications, in particular, are associated with prolonged intensive care unit (ICU) stays, increased morbidity, and long-term cognitive impairment [2].

Among these complications, hallucinations have emerged as a clinically relevant and relatively frequent phenomenon [3]. Hallucinations are defined as sensory perceptions occurring in the absence of external stimulation of the relevant sensory organ while the individual is awake [2, 3]. They can be classified into several types, most commonly auditory and visual. Visual hallucinations are most frequently observed when the underlying cause is organic, and both auditory and visual hallucinations have been reported in cardiac surgical patients [2]. While hallucinations often occur within the context of delirium, they can also manifest as isolated phenomena. This study specifically utilizes the QPE to isolate sensory perceptions from the broader cognitive fluctuations and altered consciousness characteristic of delirium. Hypertension is a growing public health concern, affecting an estimated 1.28 billion adults aged 30–79 years worldwide [4, 5]. It is the most prevalent comorbidity among patients undergoing cardiac surgery, with reported rates ranging from 64% to 74% [6]. As a largely asymptomatic chronic disease, optimal blood pressure control through adherence to antihypertensive therapy reduces the risk of renal, cardiovascular, and cerebrovascular complications [4] .

Experimental and clinical evidence indicate that blood pressure variability in hypertensive patients impairs cerebrovascular autoregulation and endothelial function, thereby increasing vulnerability to perioperative neuropsychiatric complications, particularly delirium [7, 8]. Given the overlapping pathophysiological substrates, similar mechanisms may also predispose patients to postoperative hallucinations, although this relationship remains largely unexplored. Beyond systemic inflammation, accumulating evidence from studies on postoperative neurocognitive disorders (PND) suggests that cerebral vulnerability after cardiac surgery is strongly influenced by baseline vascular integrity and autoregulatory capacity [9, 10]. Hypertension is associated with structural and functional alterations in cerebral small vessels, chronic endothelial dysfunction, and a rightward shift of the cerebrovascular autoregulation curve, thereby narrowing the margin of perfusion safety during perioperative hemodynamic fluctuations [11, 12]. When exposed to cardiopulmonary bypass–related inflammatory activation, microembolic load, and transient blood–brain barrier permeability changes, hypertensive patients may therefore exhibit heightened susceptibility to neuropsychiatric manifestations, including sensory-perceptual disturbances such as hallucinations [13, 14].

Despite recognition of postoperative hallucinations as a complication of cardiac surgery, the specific influence of hypertension has not been systematically investigated. No prior studies have compared hallucination incidence or characteristics between hypertensive and non-hypertensive patients. As a result, in this multicenter prospective cohort study, we addressed this gap by evaluating the cumulative incidence of auditory and visual hallucinations and identifying their independent predictors across both groups.

Methodology

Study design, setting, and population

This multicenter prospective cohort study was conducted across cardiac surgery centers in the West Bank of Palestine, including Ibn Sina Specialist Hospital, Specialized Arab Hospital, An-Najah National University Hospital, Al Razi Hospital, Almezan Hospital, Alahli Hospital, Arab Society for Rehabilitation, Palestine Medical Complex, Nablus Specialty Hospital, and Al Makassed Hospital. This study was implemented as part of the VAACS study, an ongoing multicenter investigation of neuropsychiatric outcomes following cardiac procedures (https://www.isrctn.com/ISRCTN18368253).

We prospectively enrolled 1,332 adult patients (≥ 18 years) scheduled for elective or semi-elective cardiac surgery, including CABG and valve surgery. Patients were stratified into hypertensive and non-hypertensive groups according to the 2018 European Society of Cardiology/European Society of Hypertension (ESC/ESH) guidelines [15]. Of the total cohort, 517 patients were hypertensive and 815 were non-hypertensive. Patients were excluded if they were unable to provide informed consent, could not speak or understand Arabic, or had pre-existing neurological or psychiatric disorders (including cerebral infarction, traumatic brain injury with persistent deficits, major depression, or dementia) that could interfere with neuropsychological assessment and the evaluation of postoperative hallucinations. See Fig. 1.

Data collection instruments

Postoperative cognitive outcomes were assessed using the Questionnaire for Psychotic Experiences (QPE), a validated tool widely applied in clinical and psychiatric research to evaluate auditory and visual hallucination, and captures the presence, frequency, and type of auditory and visual hallucination [3]. The instrument demonstrates robust internal consistency, with Cronbach’s α values ranging from 0.70 to 0.91, consistent with prior validation studies [2]. Additional variables were collected from medical records, including demographic information (age, gender, residence), clinical characteristics (history of diabetes, stroke, atrial fibrillation, hypertension), surgical type (valve replacement, CABG, or combined procedures), and pre-existing neurologic or systemic conditions. Cases with incomplete data or meeting exclusion criteria (prior psychiatric illness, significant cognitive impairment, or language barriers) were excluded.

Outcomes, study variables, and electronic medical records

The primary outcome (dependent variable) was the incidence of postoperative hallucinations. Hallucinations were assessed with the QPE and coded as a binary variable (present vs. absent). Several independent variables were examined as potential predictors of postoperative hallucinations, including detailed phenomenological features such as frequency, duration, modality (auditory or visual), emotional valence, distress, functional impact, insight, and complexity, which were recorded as ordinal variables to capture severity and clinical burden. Patient-level data were prospectively extracted from electronic medical records and systematically categorized into five domains: demographic, clinical, surgical, laboratory, and perioperative. Demographic characteristics included age, sex, and place of residence. Clinical history emphasized hypertension, which served as the main stratification variable, and also included diabetes mellitus, hyperlipidemia, atrial fibrillation, unstable angina, cerebrovascular disease, extracardiac arteriopathy, pulmonary disease, renal impairment, and prior infective endocarditis. Information on redo surgery, recent myocardial infarction, and exposure to immunosuppressive or corticosteroid therapy was also recorded.

Surgical variables comprised the type of cardiac procedure (CABG or valve surgery), use of cardiopulmonary bypass, aortic cross-clamp time, total operative time, left ventricular ejection fraction, and preoperative hospital stay. Perioperative medication exposure was documented for vasopressors (adrenaline, noradrenaline, vasopressin), inotropes (dobutamine), and opioids (fentanyl, morphine, pethidine). Laboratory parameters included hemoglobin, white blood cell differential counts, total protein, albumin, platelets, C-reactive protein, creatinine, and calcium. Postoperative outcomes captured duration of mechanical ventilation, ICU and total hospital stay, transfusion requirements, and major complications such as stroke, acute kidney injury, arrhythmia, bleeding, myocardial infarction, and respiratory failure.

Data collection procedures

Data were collected prospectively across 10 cardiac surgery centers in the West Bank of Palestine, including, Ibn Sina Specialist Hospital, Specialized Arab Hospital, An-Najah National University Hospital, Al Razi Hospital, Almezan Hospital, Alahli Hospital, Arab Society for Rehabilitation, Palestine Medical Complex, Nablus Specialty Hospital, and Al Makassed Hospital. Following postoperative stabilization, patients were screened for eligibility, and written informed consent was obtained before enrollment.

Hallucinations were assessed daily during the first 7 postoperative days using the Questionnaire for Psychotic Experiences (QPE). To ensure valid self-report in the ICU setting, QPE interviews were conducted only after extubation, once patients were awake, oriented to person and place, and able to follow a three-step command. Sedated or mechanically ventilated patients were deferred until these criteria were met. Assessments were performed by trained research personnel using standardized prompts following centralized training and periodic cross-center calibration to ensure consistency. The QPE is a symptom-level instrument designed to capture the phenomenology of hallucinations and does not constitute a diagnostic tool for delirium.

Participants were stratified according to the presence or absence of hypertension, independent of the type of cardiac surgery performed. Data collection was performed by medical students and interns who underwent structured training under the supervision of certified psychiatrists. The training program combined didactic lectures and standardized workshops, covering infection control protocols in the cardiac care unit, accurate recognition and classification of hallucinations, and principles of patient sensitivity and psychological safety. Practical simulations were incorporated to ensure adherence to standardized assessment procedures and to minimize inter-rater variability.

Ethical consideration

This study received ethical clearance and official approvals from An-Najah National University and all participating hospitals before the initiation of data collection. The research was conducted in full accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before their inclusion in the study. Participants were informed about the study’s objectives, their voluntary participation, and the confidentiality of their responses. Patient confidentiality and data privacy were strictly maintained throughout the study. No identifiable personal information was collected or stored. All data were anonymized, securely handled, and used solely for research purposes. Given the observational and non-interventional nature of the study, the risk to participants was minimal.

Sample size justification

Sample size adequacy was further supported using the classical formula for comparing two independent means:

n = 2 × (Zα/2 + Zβ) ² × σ² / Δ², where σ represents the expected standard deviation, Δ the minimum clinically relevant mean difference, Zα/2 the type I error (1.96 for α = 0.05), and Zβ the type II error (0.84 for 80% power).

Based on our cohort data, the mean EuroSCORE II was 3.24 ± 2.34. For sample size justification, a conservative clinically relevant difference between hypertensive and non-hypertensive patients was assumed to be Δ ≈ 1 point. Substituting these values yields: n = 2 × (1.96 + 0.84) ² × (2.34) ² / 1² ≈ 86 patients per group. Our study included 517 hypertensive and 815 non-hypertensive patients, both far exceeding the calculated requirement. Thus, the achieved sample size ensured > 80% power to detect differences of at least 1 point in mean EuroSCORE II between groups at α = 0.05. This strongly supports the adequacy of our cohort for comparative analyses and the robustness of our findings.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed in Stata 17 (StataCorp, College Station, TX). The analytic cohort included 1,332 adults undergoing coronary artery bypass grafting or valve surgery. Outcomes were assessed within 7 postoperative days. We examined two time-to-event endpoints: (i) visual hallucinations (VH) with 154 events overall, and (ii) auditory hallucinations (AH) with 92 events overall. Because preoperative hypertension (HTN) was a central exposure, all survival and prediction analyses were stratified a priori into two cohorts: HTN (n = 517) and non-HTN (n = 815). For baseline comparisons, we applied appropriate statistical tests according to data type and distribution. Continuous variables were assessed for normality using descriptive plots and summary statistics. If normally distributed, differences between groups were evaluated using the independent samples t-test; for skewed distributions, the Mann–Whitney U test was employed. Categorical variables were compared using the Chi-square test, with Fisher’s exact test applied when expected cell counts were < 5.

Time-to-event data were defined using observed follow-up time (in days) from surgery to event or censoring, along with the corresponding failure indicator for each outcome. Follow-up was administratively truncated at 7 days. Cumulative incidence curves (1 – Kaplan–Meier survival) were estimated for each cohort, with numbers at risk displayed at 1-day intervals (0–7 days). Between-group differences were explored visually, and formal modeling was performed using Cox proportional hazards regression.

Because hallucinations occurred at varying time points within the 7-day postoperative window, time-to-event analysis was selected to preserve clinically meaningful temporal information. The Kaplan–Meier curves demonstrated non-uniform event accumulation and early divergence between groups during the first postoperative days, supporting the use of Cox modeling rather than collapsing the outcome into a fixed 7-day binary endpoint. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed using Schoenfeld residuals and was not violated.

For each cohort and outcome, we first fit univariate Cox proportional hazards models for clinically relevant covariates [16–19]. Given the modest number of events in certain stratified analyses, particularly in the hypertensive auditory hallucination subgroup, parsimonious multivariable Cox models were constructed using a limited set of prespecified core predictors selected a priori based on clinical relevance and prior literature. This approach was adopted to reduce overfitting risk and maintain an acceptable events-per-variable (EPV) ratio. Specifically, the number of predictors in each multivariable model was restricted to ensure approximately ≥ 5 events per variable in the smallest stratum. Continuous predictors were retained on their original scales and interpreted per 1-unit increase (e.g., per point in EuroSCORE II, per hour of ventilation). Ties were handled using the Breslow method. Robust standard errors clustered at the hospital level were used to account for potential intra-center correlation. Hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and two-sided p-values are reported, with statistical significance defined as p < 0.05. Analyses were conducted using complete cases. Postoperative ventilation duration was treated as a time-fixed covariate measured during the index hospitalization; we acknowledge that residual time-dependent confounding or assessment delay may influence its association with hallucination risk.

To assess and compare discriminative performance across HTN strata, we built parsimonious logistic regression models within each cohort using three a priori predictors that were consistently informative and clinically interpretable: [16–23] EuroSCORE II, postoperative ventilation hours, and absolute neutrophil count. Model-based predicted probabilities were used to draw receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and compute the area under the curve (AUC) with 95% CIs. We compared AUCs between cohorts using DeLong’s test. To provide robust uncertainty estimates, we additionally conducted nonparametric bootstrap resampling (1,000 replications; seed = 123456) to derive standard errors and CIs for each AUC and for the AUC difference between cohorts. All tests were two-sided. Results are presented as HR (95% CI) for Cox models and AUC (95% CI) for ROC analyses, with figures restricted to the first postoperative week to match the clinical observation window. The models follow the guidelines of the Transparent Reporting of a Multivariable Prediction Model for Individual Prognosis or Diagnosis [24].

Table 1 presents the demographic, medical history, laboratory, procedural, and outcome characteristics of patients with and without visual hallucinations, stratified by hypertension status. Among hypertensive patients, those with visual hallucinations had significantly higher exposure to immunosuppressive drugs (6.5% vs. 1.5%, p = 0.012) and corticosteroid therapy (4.8% vs. 1.3%, p = 0.047). In non-hypertensive patients, visual hallucinations were significantly associated with immunosuppressive drug use (3.3% vs. 0.6%, p = 0.008), higher EuroSCORE II (Median [IQR]: 4[2–7] vs. 3[2–4], p = 0.001), and lower left ventricular ejection fraction (50.6 ± 7.0% vs. 52.24 ± 6.65%, p = 0.027). Additionally, redo surgery was more frequent in non-hypertensive patients with hallucinations (12.0% vs. 6.1%, p = 0.034). Dobutamine use was more common (11.3% vs. 2.9%, p = 0.001). Table 2 presents the demographic, clinical, laboratory, and perioperative characteristics of patients with and without auditory hallucinations, stratified by hypertension status. Among hypertensive patients, those with auditory hallucinations were significantly younger (56.1 ± 9.9 vs. 60.8 ± 9.3 years, p = 0.006), had higher EuroSCORE II (Median [IQR]: 3.5[2–5] vs. 3[1–4], p = 0.003), elevated absolute neutrophil counts (28.3 ± 21.8 vs. 22.7 ± 13.7 cells/µL, p = 0.034), and higher serum calcium levels (8.60 ± 0.07 vs. 8.41 ± 0.40 mg/dL, p = 0.015). Prior antibiotic use was also more common in this subgroup (18.8% vs. 4.7%, p = 0.001). In non-hypertensive patients, auditory hallucinations were associated with significantly higher EuroSCORE II (Median [IQR]: 4[2–7] vs. 3[2–4], p < 0.001), more frequent thoracic aortic surgery (6.7% vs. 1.9%, p = 0.015), shorter aortic cross-clamp time (70.2 ± 33.0 vs. 81.5 ± 38.1 min, p = 0.026), and reduced post-operative ventilation duration (Median [IQR]: 4.5[3–7] vs. 5[3–8] h, p = 0.046). No other demographic, comorbidity, laboratory, or perioperative parameters demonstrated significant associations. See eTable 2 in the Supplementary File 1 for the full variable set.

eTable 3 presents the phenomenological features of hallucinations stratified by type (visual vs. auditory) and hypertension status. Clear differences emerged in frequency, duration, emotional valence, distress, functional impact, and insight. Almost all VH cases in hypertensive patients occurred briefly (very briefly: 75.8%), whereas AH in the same group was longer in duration, with 40.6% lasting a few seconds and 28.1% lasting minutes. Importantly, negative content was markedly higher in AH, with entire negative valence present in 12.5% of hypertensive AH and 15% of non-hypertensive AH, compared with only 1.6% and 6.5% in VH, respectively. Distress levels followed a similar pattern, with 87.1% of hypertensive VH causing no discomfort. In contrast, only 43.8% of hypertensive AH were non-distressing, and 22% caused some discomfort. The functional impact was also greater for AH, with interference in activities in 31% of hypertensive AH and 28% of non-hypertensive AH, compared to 6.5% and 9.8% in VH. Insight differed significantly, as loss of reality testing (complete conviction hallucinations were real) occurred in 12.5% of hypertensive AH and 18.3% of non-hypertensive AH, compared with only 1.6% vs. 2.2% in VH. Finally, phenomenological richness varied; complex auditory hallucinations (several sentences or continuous speech) were observed in 18.8% hypertensive vs. 28.3% of non-hypertensive AH, whereas VH were simpler, predominantly flashes or forms (77.4% hypertensive, 66.3% non-hypertensive).

Fig. 3Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) comparison of predictive models for postoperative visual hallucinations in hypertensive (HTN) and non-hypertensive (non-HTN) cohorts

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) comparison of predictive models for postoperative visual hallucinations in hypertensive (HTN) and non-hypertensive (non-HTN) cohorts

Figure 3 compares the discriminative performance of logistic regression models incorporating EuroSCORE II, postoperative ventilation time, and neutrophil count in predicting postoperative visual hallucinations across HTN and Non-HTN cohorts. The area under the ROC curve (AUC) was 0.68 (95% CI: 0.56–0.72) in the HTN cohort, indicating moderate discrimination, while the corresponding AUC in the Non-HTN cohort was 0.54 (95% CI: 0.48–0.60), reflecting poor predictive ability. Direct comparison revealed a statistically significant difference between the two curves (ΔAUC = − 0.10, 95% CI: − 0.20 to − 0.01; p = 0.04 by bootstrap, p = 0.045 by DeLong test). These results suggest that hypertension status influences the predictive utility of perioperative markers, with stronger model performance in hypertensive patients.

Discussion

In this multicenter prospective cohort study, we evaluated postoperative visual and auditory hallucinations in hypertensive and non-hypertensive patients after cardiac surgery during the first 7 days. Kaplan–Meier curves showed a slightly higher cumulative incidence of both visual and auditory hallucinations in non-hypertensive patients, though differences were not statistically significant.

Importantly, this study did not aim to diagnose delirium, nor were standardized delirium assessment tools (e.g., CAM-ICU) applied. While hallucinations frequently occur within delirium syndromes, they may also present independently. Our findings therefore pertain specifically to sensory-perceptual phenomena rather than global cognitive dysfunction, and should not be interpreted as reflecting overall delirium incidence.

The time-dependent pattern observed in the Kaplan–Meier curves reinforce the methodological choice of Cox regression to capture hazard dynamics during the early postoperative period rather than relying solely on fixed-window binary comparisons.

In the hypertensive cohort, longer postoperative ventilation duration showed an inverse relationship with reported hallucinations. However, this may reflect detection bias rather than a true biological protective effect. Patients requiring longer ventilation are typically under heavier sedation (e.g., propofol, dexmedetomidine), which likely masked the clinical manifestation or reporting of hallucinations during the observation period. Elevated absolute neutrophil counts predicted increased risk of visual hallucinations. and younger age, prior antibiotic use, and higher neutrophil counts were independently associated with auditory hallucinations in hypertensive patients. Among non-hypertensive patients, EuroSCORE II emerged as the strongest predictor of both visual and auditory hallucinations, while valve surgery was associated with reduced risk of auditory hallucinations compared with CABG.

Hypertension-specific mechanisms, such as a shift in cerebrovascular autoregulation and chronic endothelial dysfunction, may lower the threshold for neuropsychiatric vulnerability when exposed to the systemic inflammation of cardiac surgery. These findings highlight inflammation, operative risk burden, and surgery type as key contributors to postoperative hallucinations beyond hypertensive status.

The differential predictors observed between hypertensive and non-hypertensive patients may reflect underlying differences in neurovascular resilience. Chronic hypertension induces microvascular remodeling, endothelial dysfunction, and impaired neurovascular coupling, collectively reducing cerebral autoregulatory reserve [12, 25]. Experimental and clinical studies demonstrate that hypertension shifts both the upper and lower limits of autoregulation, rendering cerebral perfusion more pressure-dependent and vulnerable during systemic inflammatory and hemodynamic stress [11, 26]. In the setting of cardiopulmonary bypass, characterized by cytokine release, oxidative stress, and transient blood–brain barrier disruption, this pre-existing vascular fragility may amplify inflammatory-mediated neuronal dysfunction [14, 27]. Such mechanisms provide a biologically plausible explanation for why inflammatory markers and perioperative physiological stressors appeared to exert greater influence in hypertensive patients.

These findings suggest that hypertension may not simply act as a comorbidity but as a pathophysiological modifier of perioperative neuropsychiatric vulnerability [12, 25].

HTN cohort

In hypertensive patients, longer postoperative ventilation duration was independently associated with a reduced hazard of both visual and auditory hallucinations. This association was not observed in non-hypertensive patients. Because prolonged mechanical ventilation is typically associated with greater illness severity and increased sedative exposure, this inverse relationship is unlikely to represent a true protective biological effect. Instead, it may represent assessment-related or survival bias, as patients requiring longer ventilation and sedation may have had delayed opportunities to report hallucinations within the 7-day observation period. In contrast, Chaiwat et al. reported that delirious patients, classified as having neurological complications, experienced significantly longer durations of mechanical ventilation, underscoring the complex relationship between ventilation and neuropsychiatric outcomes [28]. Despite that, Sabol et al. found that mechanical ventilation duration (< 24 h) was not a significant predictor of delirium in their cohort [16] .These discrepancies suggest that ventilation duration may serve more as a marker of clinical trajectory and care processes rather than a direct causal determinant of hallucinations. Prolonged ventilation may predispose patients to hypoxemia, hypercapnia, and cumulative exposure to sedatives such as benzodiazepines, opioids, and propofol, contributing to cortical dysfunction. Sedative-induced changes in GABAergic and dopaminergic neurotransmission provide a plausible neurobiological pathway for delirium and hallucinations. However, given the inverse association observed in our adjusted models, these mechanisms may reflect broader neuropsychiatric vulnerability rather than directly explaining our findings. Recognizing this association may support early identification of high-risk patients and closer postoperative monitoring. While minimizing ventilation duration and optimizing sedation remain essential components of postoperative critical care, our findings should not be interpreted as evidence that prolonged ventilation confers protection against hallucinations. Rather, ventilation duration likely reflects broader clinical and assessment-related factors. Targeted care bundles, multidisciplinary management, and patient counseling may further improve outcomes and inform preventive strategies. Future studies incorporating systematic delirium assessment and time-updated exposure modeling may help clarify this relationship.

Absolute neutrophil count was significantly associated with an increased risk of visual hallucinations in the adjusted model. Accordingly, absolute neutrophil count remained independently associated with a higher likelihood of visual hallucinations. However, no statistically significant association with auditory hallucinations remained after adjustment. These findings emphasize the important role of postoperative inflammation, alongside established clinical risk factors, in the development of neurocognitive complications. At 24 h postoperatively, the mean neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) was significantly higher in the postoperative delirium (POD)/postoperative cognitive dysfunction (POCD) group than in the non-POD/POCD group (21.40 vs. 15.52, p = 0.038). This difference persisted at 48 h, with the POD/POCD group showing a mean NLR of 14.98 versus 8.81 in the control group, also indicating a medium effect size. These findings suggest that elevated NLR in the early postoperative period is associated with an increased risk of POD/POCD, highlighting its potential value as a predictive biomarker for neurocognitive complications after cardiac surgery. Disruption of the blood–brain barrier (BBB) may allow peripheral cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α) to enter the central nervous system (CNS), triggering microglial activation, oxidative stress, and neurotransmitter imbalance. This neuroinflammatory response may impair thalamocortical circuits and sensory processing, thereby promoting delirium and visual hallucinations [17, 18]. Monitoring perioperative absolute neutrophil count (ANC) may help identify high-risk patients who could benefit from early intervention. Abnormal ANC values may guide individualized anesthesia, sedation, and anti-inflammatory strategies, supporting delirium-prevention bundles. Routine ANC monitoring may provide both predictive and prognostic value, inform discharge planning, and guide future research on inflammation-targeted interventions.

In the crude analysis, younger hypertensive patients were more likely to develop auditory hallucinations. This finding contrasts with Teller et al., who reported that older age was independently associated with postoperative neurological complications [2]. One possible explanation is that younger individuals may exhibit greater neurodevelopmental sensitivity and less developed coping mechanisms, increasing susceptibility to perceptual disturbances under physiological and psychological stress. Clinically, these findings highlight the importance of careful postoperative monitoring and early supportive interventions in younger hypertensive patients to reduce the risk or severity of auditory hallucinations. Preoperative antibiotic administration was associated with an increased risk of postoperative auditory hallucinations. This association may be explained by several mechanisms. These may include direct interactions between antibiotics and central neurotransmitter systems, as well as indirect modulation of neuroinflammation through altered cytokine production [16]. Such effects could disrupt normal neurotransmitter signaling, thereby contributing to hallucinatory experiences. From a clinical perspective, these results underscore the importance of judicious preoperative antibiotic use, particularly in patients with known vulnerability to neuropsychiatric complications.

Higher EuroSCORE II values were associated with an increased risk of postoperative hallucinations in hypertensive patients, particularly visual hallucinations, whereas no independent association was observed for auditory hallucinations after multivariable adjustment. In contrast, EuroSCORE II remained a strong independent predictor of auditory hallucinations in the non-hypertensive cohort. This observation is consistent with a study reporting that patients with moderate and high EuroSCORE II values had a 4.5- and 14.5-fold higher risk of developing postoperative delirium compared with low-risk patients (p < 0.0001) [20]. This may be explained by the fact that EuroSCORE II reflects greater baseline comorbidity burden, frailty, and overall perioperative risk, which increase central nervous system vulnerability to stress and metabolic disturbances during and after surgery. Because EuroSCORE II predicts postoperative complications, identifying high-risk patients preoperatively allows optimization of their condition and potential modification of surgical strategies to achieve better outcomes. Preventive measures may include optimizing hemodynamics, minimizing sedative exposure, and promoting early mobilization to reduce the likelihood of hallucinations and delirium [21].

Non-HTN cohort

In the non-hypertensive cohort, the type of surgery (valve vs. CABG) was clinically significant, with one procedure associated with a significantly lower risk of postoperative auditory hallucinations than the other. Specifically, valve surgery was independently associated with a lower hazard of postoperative auditory hallucinations compared with CABG (adjusted HR 0.463, 95% CI 0.221–0.970; p = 0.041). Although no previous studies have directly linked surgical type to hallucination outcomes, our findings suggest an association between procedure type and postoperative neurological complications, consistent with reports in the literature [2]. CABG has traditionally been associated with greater aortic manipulation, higher embolic burden, and more pronounced inflammatory activation compared with isolated valve procedures, factors that may contribute to transient neuropsychiatric manifestations. In contrast, Ottens et al. reported that the type of cardiac surgery, coronary, valvular, or arrhythmia-related, was not associated with postoperative hallucinations [3]. Previous investigations have yielded mixed results regarding the relationship between surgical subtype and postoperative neurocognitive or delirium outcomes. While some studies report no association between procedure type and hallucinations, others suggest that operative complexity, cardiopulmonary bypass duration, and inflammatory response may modulate neurological risk. Cardiac surgery, particularly CABG and valve procedures, is generally associated with a higher risk of postoperative neurological complications than less invasive interventions. This increased risk is likely due to longer operative times, greater invasiveness, higher transfusion requirements, and a stronger inflammatory response. In this context, our findings indicate that surgical subtype may represent a relevant modifier of early sensory-perceptual disturbances, particularly in patients without pre-existing hypertensive vascular vulnerability. Clinically, recognizing surgical type as a risk factor may allow targeted monitoring and preventive strategies to reduce complications such as delirium, seizures, and hallucinations.

EuroSCORE II was independently associated with postoperative auditory hallucinations. EuroSCORE II incorporates patient- and procedure-related risk factors, including older age, comorbidities, and surgical complexity, which may increase susceptibility to delirium and hallucinations. It therefore serves as a marker of cumulative physiological and surgical stress, identifying patients who may require closer monitoring and optimized perioperative care [19]. Dumanyan et al. reported higher EuroSCORE values among patients who developed postoperative delirium, reflecting older age, poorer functional status, and more complex cardiac disease [22]. These findings suggest that elevated EuroSCORE values may indicate increased vulnerability to neuropsychiatric complications, although direct links to auditory hallucinations remain limited. In contrast, Kumar et al. found no significant association between EuroSCORE and postoperative delirium [23]. Clinically, EuroSCORE II may help identify patients at higher risk for postoperative auditory hallucinations and delirium, allowing for closer monitoring and early interventions. High-risk patients may benefit from preventive strategies such as minimizing sedative exposure, optimizing hemodynamics, and providing early cognitive support. Additionally, awareness of this risk can guide patient counseling and tailored postoperative care, while highlighting the need for further research to clarify the mechanisms linking EuroSCORE II to neuropsychiatric complications.

Aortic cross-clamp time was not independently associated with the development of visual hallucinations after multivariable adjustment and hospital-level clustering. Although prior studies have suggested a relationship between longer clamp duration and neuropsychiatric outcomes, this was not confirmed in our adjusted models. Prolonged aortic cross-clamp duration may contribute to microembolic injury affecting visual pathways. In patients with reduced vascular reserve (e.g., chronic nicotine use), even mild hypoperfusion can worsen this effect. Similarly, Tschernatsch et al. reported a positive correlation between extracorporeal circulation (CPB) time (r = 0.146, p = 0.048) and aortic cross-clamp time (r = 0.175, p = 0.018) with the occurrence of transient visual hallucinations, indicating that longer durations of CPB or aortic clamping are associated with an increased risk of these postoperative neuropsychiatric symptoms. Notably, no studies suggest the opposite, that increasing aortic cross-clamp time decreases the incidence of hallucinations [29]. The discrepancy between prior findings and our results may reflect differences in cohort characteristics, outcome definitions, or center-level practice patterns. Thus, although mechanistically plausible, aortic cross-clamp duration did not emerge as an independent predictor of visual hallucinations in our cohort.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. It is a large, prospective multicenter cohort using standardized definitions and the validated Questionnaire for Psychotic Experiences, ensuring reliable detection of both visual and auditory hallucinations. Stratification by hypertension provided novel insight into differential predictors across subgroups. Detailed perioperative variables, including EuroSCORE II, cross-clamp and CPB times, inflammatory markers, and ventilation course, were systematically collected, enhancing clinical relevance. Limitations include modest event counts in some strata (e.g., hypertensive auditory hallucinations, n = 32), which may limit power for multivariable modeling. The observational design carries potential for residual confounding, and center-level practice differences were not formally modeled. This study is also subject to survival and assessment bias; patients who remained sedated or ventilated for longer periods may have had delayed assessments, potentially underestimating the early incidence of hallucinations in the most critically ill patients. The inverse association between ventilation duration and hallucinations in the hypertensive cohort may be confounded by sedation protocols, which could delay or mask symptom reporting. The predictive model for non-hypertensive patients yielded a low AUC (0.54), indicating that while individual predictors like EuroSCORE II were statistically significant, the model lacks the discriminative power required for clinical application in this specific subgroup. The absence of formal delirium assessment limits our ability to determine whether reported hallucinations occurred within or outside delirium syndromes. Finally, although we focused our interpretation on multivariable results, multiple comparisons across tables raise the risk of type I error.

Take home message

Predictors of postoperative hallucinations differ by hypertension status. In hypertensive patients, risk was shaped by inflammatory burden, antibiotic exposure, age, and ventilation course, whereas in non-hypertensive patients, overall operative risk (EuroSCORE II) and surgery type were the dominant determinants.

Conclusion

Hallucinations are a frequent and distressing complication after cardiac surgery, but their predictors vary meaningfully by hypertension status. In hypertensive patients, inflammatory markers, age-related vulnerability, antibiotic exposure, and postoperative ventilation dynamics appear to shape risk, whereas in non-hypertensive patients, overall operative risk as reflected by EuroSCORE II and surgery type play a more prominent role. Recognizing these distinct patterns can help cardiothoracic surgeons and critical care teams anticipate vulnerable patients, communicate risks to families, and implement preventive strategies. Such HTN-aware, patient-centered care may reduce neuropsychiatric morbidity and improve recovery trajectories.
