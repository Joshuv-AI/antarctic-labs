Direct Hospital Costs of Ambulatory versus Inpatient Needle-Localized Breast Biopsy: A Retrospective Cohort Study
Authors: Chayaporn Subanphanichkul Thong-aek, Chutida Sungworawongpana, Srila Samphao, Somrit Mahattanobon, Seechad Noonpradej, Wongsakorn Chaochankit
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAs healthcare systems aim to optimize resource utilization, ambulatory breast surgery has been proposed as an alternative to inpatient care. However, data comparing direct hospital costs and identifying cost drivers for needle-localized breast biopsy (NLB) remain limited, particularly in low- and middle-income settings.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included adult patients who underwent NLB at a tertiary academic medical center between January 2020 and December 2024. Patients were classified as ambulatory (day surgery) or inpatient according to admission status. Total direct hospital costs were compared between groups, and multivariable linear regression was used to identify factors associated with increased cost. Costs were reported in Thai Baht (THB) and converted to U.S. dollars (USD) using an exchange rate of 1 USD = 33.82 THB.ResultsA total of 392 patients were included, of whom 33 underwent ambulatory surgery and 359 underwent inpatient surgery. The ambulatory group was younger (47.9 ± 8.7 vs. 53.3 ± 10.5 years; p = 0.005) and had lower median hospital costs (13,250 THB [391.7 USD] vs. 21,470 THB [634.9 USD]; p < 0.001). In multivariable analysis, inpatient admission (β = 6,378.03 THB [188.48 USD]; p < 0.001) and longer operative time (β = 122.86 THB [3.63 USD] per minute; p < 0.001) were independently associated with higher costs. Age, American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) classification, and preoperative biopsy status were not significant predictors.ConclusionsAmbulatory NLB was associated with lower direct hospital costs than inpatient care in this single-center cohort. Admission status and operative time were associated with higher expenditure. These findings reflect real-world practice in selected patients; however, the observational design, marked group imbalance, and absence of safety events limit causal inference and preclude firm conclusions regarding comparative safety.

Introduction

Breast cancer incidence continues to rise globally, and surgical intervention remains a key component of initial management. Advances in surgical techniques and perioperative care have enabled an increasing number of breast procedures to be performed in outpatient settings, particularly in high-income countries [1–4]. This transition reflects broader efforts to optimize resource utilization and reduce healthcare costs. Prior studies have shown that, in appropriately selected patients, ambulatory breast procedures are feasible and may offer comparable short-term outcomes to inpatient care [1, 5–7]. However, evidence remains limited for specific diagnostic procedures such as needle-localized breast biopsy (NLB).

In the diagnostic pathway of breast cancer, screening mammography plays a central role in identifying nonpalpable lesions at an early stage [8]. NLB is commonly used to evaluate such lesions. Advances in localization techniques and perioperative management have enabled NLB to be performed in outpatient settings, often under local anesthesia [9, 10]. In many centers, ambulatory biopsy has become routine, offering potential advantages such as reduced hospital utilization and more efficient clinical workflow [11]. Despite these developments, variability in clinical practice persists. In some settings, NLB is still performed as an inpatient procedure, influenced by factors, such as patient comorbidities, anticipated procedural complexity, and institutional workflow. In addition, operational challenges, including prolonged appointment duration and variability in perioperative processes, may affect efficiency in outpatient breast surgery services [12].

Cost remains an important consideration in determining the appropriate setting for breast biopsy procedures. Previous studies have demonstrated that outpatient NLB under local anesthesia is associated with lower costs compared with inpatient procedures, with differences largely attributable to anesthesia-related expenses and resource utilization [13, 14]. However, these findings are primarily derived from specific healthcare systems, and real-world data from Asian settings remain limited. While prior studies have also reported favorable clinical outcomes for outpatient breast surgery, including comparable complication rates and recovery profiles [15–17], the interpretation of safety and broader outcome measures may vary depending on patient selection and study design. Therefore, further real-world evaluations are needed to better characterize cost patterns and perioperative factors associated with different care pathways.

The primary aim of this study was to compare direct hospital costs between ambulatory and inpatient NLB and to identify factors associated with increased expenditure in routine clinical practice. This study provides real-world data from a tertiary care center in Thailand, where the adoption of ambulatory breast surgery remains variable.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at Songklanagarind Hospital, a tertiary academic medical center in southern Thailand. The study compared direct hospital costs and perioperative process measures between ambulatory (day surgery) and inpatient settings for patients undergoing NLB. Data were collected from January 1, 2020, to December 31, 2024. Ambulatory surgery was defined as a procedure performed without overnight hospitalization, with same-day discharge after stable postoperative recovery. Inpatient surgery was defined as NLB requiring at least one overnight hospital stay, based on clinical indications, anesthesia-related factors, or institutional practice.

Patient Selection

Eligible participants were adult patients who underwent NLB during the study period. Patients were excluded if they underwent additional breast or axillary procedures during the same admission, including wide excision (operationally defined in our dataset as excision with a gross margin >1 cm), mastectomy, sentinel lymph node biopsy, or axillary lymph node dissection. Patients undergoing non-breast-related procedures during the same admission were also excluded. Patient selection was based on retrospective review of operative records and hospital admission data.

In routine clinical practice, the choice of ambulatory versus inpatient management was not randomized but determined by clinical judgment and institutional practice patterns. Ambulatory surgery was generally considered for patients with lower anticipated procedural complexity, acceptable anesthetic risk, and feasibility of same-day discharge. Factors influencing this decision included comorbidity burden, anticipated operative duration, anesthetic considerations, and availability of postoperative home support. Patients expected to require extended monitoring were preferentially managed in the inpatient setting. Because treatment allocation was not randomized, comparisons between groups are subject to confounding by indication, and findings should be interpreted as associative rather than causal.

Data Collection

Collected variables included demographic and clinical characteristics (age, sex, smoking status, alcohol use, drug allergies, body mass index, and comorbidities), as well as clinical and procedural data (breast examination findings, imaging results, preoperative biopsy status, final pathology, ASA physical status classification, operative time, estimated blood loss, anesthesia type, post-anesthesia care unit (PACU) recovery time, and length of stay). Postoperative complications and readmissions were assessed from hospital records during the index admission. Events occurring after discharge outside the hospital system may not have been captured.

Hospital costs were defined as total direct medical expenses during the admission episode, including surgical and anesthesia fees, operating and recovery room charges, pharmacy costs, imaging and laboratory fees, and inpatient ward expenses. Costs were extracted from the hospital billing system, recorded in Thai Baht (THB), and converted to U.S. dollars (USD) using an exchange rate of 1 USD = 33.82 THB. Indirect costs were not included.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was total direct hospital cost. Secondary outcomes included operative time, PACU recovery time, length of hospital stay, postoperative complications, and readmission rates. Given the low event rates, secondary outcomes related to safety should be interpreted descriptively.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test and summarized as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median with interquartile range, as appropriate. Group comparisons were performed using Student’s t test or Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables, and chi-square or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. A multivariable linear regression model was constructed to identify factors associated with total hospital cost. Covariates included admission type (ambulatory vs. inpatient), age, operative time, ASA classification, and preoperative biopsy status. Smoking and alcohol use were not included in the regression model due to their low prevalence in this cohort, which limited their contribution to model stability. Model assumptions (linearity, homoscedasticity, and multicollinearity) were evaluated using residual plots and variance inflation factors. Results were reported as beta coefficients with 95% confidence intervals. A two-sided p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were performed using R version 4.4.2.

Results

Patient Characteristics

A total of 392 patients who underwent NLB were included in the analysis (Table 1). Of these, 33 (8.4%) patients underwent ambulatory procedures, while 359 (91.6%) patients were managed as inpatients, reflecting a marked imbalance between groups. Patients in the ambulatory group were younger than those in the inpatient group (47.9 ± 8.7 vs. 53.3 ± 10.5 years; p = 0.005). Chief complaints and symptom duration were comparable between groups.

Operative Details

The distribution of American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status and estimated blood loss was similar between groups. Operative time was shorter in the ambulatory group (50 [45–55] vs. 65 [55–80] min; p < 0.001).

Both groups predominantly received general anesthesia with airway devices, with no significant difference in anesthetic technique. PACU scores were similar between groups. No postoperative complications were observed in either group. Recovery room time was longer in the ambulatory group (105 [85–120] vs. 40 [35–50] min; p < 0.001), whereas total hospital stay was substantially shorter compared to inpatient care (2.7 [2.2–2.9] vs. 43.9 [42.2–46.2] h; p < 0.001).

Hospital Costs

Median total hospital cost was lower in the ambulatory group compared to the inpatient group (13,250 THB [391.7 USD] vs. 21,470 THB [634.9 USD]; p < 0.001). As shown in Figure 1, cost distributions differed between groups, with higher median values and greater variability observed in the inpatient cohort. Determinants of Hospital Expenditure

In multivariable linear regression analysis (Table 3), inpatient admission was independently associated with higher hospital cost (β = 6,378.03 THB; 95% CI: 4,812.13–7,943.93; p < 0.001). Operative time was also associated with increased cost, with each additional minute contributing 122.86 THB (95% CI: 107.79–137.93; p < 0.001). Age, ASA classification, and preoperative biopsy status were not significantly associated with total hospital cost. Operative time may partly reflect underlying procedural complexity and case selection rather than a purely independent cost driver.

Discussion

This study evaluated direct hospital costs and perioperative process measures among patients undergoing NLB in ambulatory versus inpatient settings. In this single-center cohort, ambulatory procedures were associated with lower hospital costs and shorter lengths of stay. Multivariable analysis identified admission status and operative time as factors associated with higher expenditure, whereas age, ASA physical status, and preoperative biopsy status were not significantly associated with cost.

The observed cost difference is consistent with prior studies demonstrating lower expenditures for outpatient breast procedures, primarily driven by reduced resource utilization and avoidance of inpatient admission [9, 13, 14]. In our cohort, inpatient admission was the strongest factor associated with higher cost, suggesting that system-level factors such as bed utilization and perioperative care pathways substantially contribute to total expenditure. However, because treatment allocation was not randomized, these findings should be interpreted as reflecting routine clinical practice rather than causal equivalence between care settings.

Operative time was also associated with increased hospital cost. While this may reflect potentially modifiable aspects of perioperative workflow, operative duration is also likely to represent underlying procedural complexity and case selection. Therefore, its role as an independent cost driver should be interpreted with caution [9, 12].

The findings of this study should be interpreted in the context of existing literature on outpatient breast surgery. Prior studies have reported comparable clinical outcomes between outpatient and inpatient approaches in selected populations [2, 12, 18]. However, in the present study, no postoperative complications were observed in either group, and the ambulatory cohort was relatively small. As such, the current data are not sufficient to support conclusions regarding comparative safety, and safety-related outcomes should be interpreted descriptively.

The adoption of ambulatory breast surgery varies across healthcare systems. While outpatient approaches are widely implemented in North America and parts of Europe, their use in many Asian settings remains heterogeneous, influenced by institutional practices and reimbursement structures [3]. In this context, real-world cost data may help inform local practice patterns and resource allocation. This study contributes region-specific evidence from a middle-income Asian country, where data on ambulatory breast procedures remain limited.

From a clinical perspective, these findings may support consideration of ambulatory pathways for selected patients undergoing NLB, particularly in settings where inpatient admission remains routine. However, careful patient selection and institutional readiness remain essential to ensure appropriate perioperative care.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective design introduces potential selection bias, as the choice of ambulatory versus inpatient management was based on clinical judgment rather than random allocation. The ambulatory group likely represents a selectively lower risk population, and the observed differences should therefore be interpreted as associative rather than causal. Second, the marked imbalance between groups and the small ambulatory sample size limit statistical power, particularly for safety outcomes. Third, complications and readmissions were assessed from hospital records during the index admission, and events occurring after discharge outside the hospital system may not have been captured. Fourth, this was a single-center study, which may limit generalizability to other healthcare settings. Finally, the analysis focused on direct hospital costs and did not include indirect costs or broader measures of value such as patient-reported outcomes. Future research should include multicenter prospective studies across diverse healthcare settings. Incorporating patient-reported outcomes and longer term follow-up may provide a more comprehensive evaluation of care pathways for NLB.

Conclusion

In this single-center retrospective cohort, ambulatory NLB was associated with lower direct hospital costs than inpatient care. Admission status and operative time were associated with higher expenditure. These findings reflect real-world practice in selected patients; however, the observational design, marked group imbalance, and limited ability to assess safety outcomes preclude causal inference. Further prospective studies are needed to better define cost components and evaluate outcomes across different care settings.
