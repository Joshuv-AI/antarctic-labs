Functions of the prp operon in carbon source metabolism and acetic acid resistance in acetic acid bacteria: a case study in Acetobacter pasteurianus CGMCC 1.41
Authors: Haoran Yang, Armin Ehrenreich, Wolfgang Liebl, Fusheng Chen
Published: 2026

Background

The prp operon is widely present in bacteria and commonly consists of 4 genes: prpB, prpC, prpD, and prpE, which encode 2-methylisocitrate (2-MIC) lyase, 2-methylcitrate (2-MC) synthase, 2-MC dehydratase and propionyl-CoA synthetase, respectively. The sum of the biochemical reactions catalyzed by these enzymes constitutes the 2-methylcitrate cycle (2-MCC), a pathway oxidizing propionyl-CoA and propionate to pyruvate in a manner analogous to tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle and glyoxylate pathway (Fig. 1) [1, 2]. In the 2-MCC pathway, PrpB, PrpC and PrpD are commonly regarded as the core enzymes [3]. Accordingly, prpB, prpC, and prpD are collectively referred to as the prp core genes in this study, and their associated biochemical reactions are termed as the 2-MCC core reactions. In addition to the 4 aforementioned genes, the prp operon of some bacteria, such as Shewanella oneidensis, also encodes AcnD and PrpF, whose combined action is functionally equivalent to that of PrpD [4].

Not all TCA cycle steps are shown for the sake of clearance of presentation. Additionally, conversion of acetic acid to acetyl-CoA is also demonstrated, which is the first step of acetic acid overoxidation

Apart from the similarity of 2-MCC and TCA cycle or glyoxylate pathway, homology is present between the enzymes from both sides. PrpB, PrpC and PrpE are homologous to isocitrate lyase, citrate synthase and acetyl-CoA synthetase (Acs), respectively [5–7]. Besides, flexible substrate specificity also exists among these enzymes. For example, in Escherichia coli and Salmonella Typhimurium, PrpC was demonstrated to be able to compensate the loss of citrate synthetase [1, 5]. Meanwhile, there is evidence showing that isocitrate lyase can function as PrpB [6]. In the case of PrpE and Acs, their similar substrate spectra were also revealed [7].

A notable feature of the 2-MCC pathway is the toxicity of its metabolites. Propionyl-CoA is such an example, as it was found to inhibit pyruvate dehydrogenase, succinyl-CoA synthetase and other central metabolism enzymes [8, 9]. Furthermore, propionyl-CoA is commonly produced during the metabolism of odd-chain fatty acids or branch-chain amino acids [10], highlighting the importance of propionyl-CoA catabolism for its detoxification under these circumstances. In this regard, the reports of functional investigation of the prp operon and the 2-MCC pathway appear to be focused on the pathogenic microorganisms, as the capability of metabolizing propionyl-CoA and propionate might be crucial for the pathogen to gain advantages against the hosts in the infection contexts that are usually rich in fatty acids and cholesterol [11–13]. In addition to propionyl-CoA, signs of the toxicity from 2-MC are also present, including its inhibition of S. enterica’s fructose 1,6 bisphosphatase—an enzyme from gluconeogenetic pathway [14], as well as the weakened sporulation in Bacillus thuringiensis resulting from its accumulation [15]. Moreover, 2-MIC was reported to possess a detrimental effect on Aspergillus nidulans [16]. To this end, the integrity of the 2-MCC pathway can be of great importance during the metabolism of propionate or propionyl-CoA.

Acetic acid bacteria (AAB) represent a group of obligate aerobic and Gram-negative bacteria [17], including 20 genera and more than 100 species. A typical feature of AAB is their multiple membrane-bound dehydrogenases, enabling the incomplete oxidation of various of sugars, alcohols and sugar alcohols into ketones, aldehydes and corresponding carboxy acids, which is termed as oxidative fermentation [18]. In particular, the incomplete oxidation of ethanol to acetic acid is also called acetic acid fermentation, which is carried out through membrane-bound alcohol dehydrogenase (ADH) and aldehyde dehydrogenase (ALDH) [19]. Acetic acid fermentation acts as the most classical AAB’s oxidative fermentation, of which the product—acetic acid, constitutes the major component of vinegars worldwide [20]. In Asian countries, Acetobacter pasteurianus plays a dominant role in traditional cereal vinegar production [21]. Specifically, A. pasteurianus CGMCC 1.41 is one of the earliest isolated strains from the traditional Chinese solid-state acetic acid fermentation and is still being widely used today for brewing vinegar [22]. Like other AAB strains possessing strong ethanol-oxidizing capability, A. pasteurianus CGMCC 1.41 is able to overoxidize the self-produced acetic acid. This process stars with the conversion of acetic acid molecules into acetyl-CoA, which is catalyzed by enzymes such as Acs [23, 24]. Subsequently, acetyl-CoA is oxidized to CO2 via the TCA cycle, as in other microorganisms harboring the intact pathway (Fig. 1). Thanks to this process, a biphasic growth can be usually observed for the relevant AAB strains when the ethanol concentration is confined to a certain level [23, 25, 26]. In addition, due to the prominent acetic acid-consuming feature, acetic acid overoxidation is regarded as one of the important resistance mechanisms against acetic acid in AAB [27].

The prp gene cluster in AAB was brought to public view for the first time by our previous transcriptome analysis, in which all relevant genes in A. pasteurianus CGMCC 1.41 showed analogous expression patterns, in particular, they were up-regulated with highly similar fold changes during acetic acid fermentation [28]. Besides, the operon structure had been predicted during the genome sequencing and annotation of this strain [22] using DOOR 2.0 database, based on the gene distance and functional relevance [29]. Additionally, by analyzing the expression data published by Sakurai et al. (2012) [30], the up-regulation of prp genes in A. aceti NBRC 14818 during mid-ethanol-oxidation phase, first stationary phase (the stationary phase occurring before acetic acid overoxidation) and acetic acid overoxidation phase was also discovered. Furthermore, these genes’ down-regulation accompanied by a reduction in the fermentation ability of A. pasteurianus Ab3 and the strain’s survival ratio under acetic acid stress after losing the toxin-antitoxin system HicAB was observed [31]. Taken together, these findings suggest that the enzymes encoded by prp genes could play a significant role in the physiology of AAB, probably including acetic acid resistance. Moreover, the presence of 2-MCC enzymes also raises the question if propionate or propionic acid can be used as a carbon source for AAB strains. Also, since the studies on the prp operon and the 2-MCC pathway in AAB were not previously reported, it is unclear how prevalent these genes are in this group of bacteria. To answer these open questions, we studied the distribution of the prp operon among AAB and constructed prp gene deletion mutants in A. pasteurianus CGMCC 1.41 to unravel the operon’s role in carbon source metabolism and acid resistance.

Material and methods

Analysis of the distribution of prp genes in acetic acid bacteria

A total of 511 AAB genomes, covering all 20 known genera, which were either downloaded from NCBI’s RefSeq database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/refseq/), or sequenced by our previous work (for A. pasteurianus CICC 20001 and CGMCC 1.41) [22], were included in the analysis. Sequences of proteins encoded by typical prp operon genes, that is, PrpB, PrpC, PrpD and PrpE from Salmonella Typhimurium, as well as PrpF and AcnD from Shewanella oneidensis, served as the reference sequences for searching prp genes in AAB. First, reference sequences were subjected to InterProScan analysis [32] to obtain the exclusive InterPro family ID for each protein. Then, reference sequences were used as the query for searching corresponding homologs in all 511 AAB genomes exploiting the BLASTp algorithm. Evaluve < 10–5 was adopted as the threshold of homology. Subsequently, OmicsBox software [33] was employed to conduct the InterPro annotation for the homologous sequences obtained in the previous step. Sequences annotated with the specific InterPro family ID were considered as the corresponding gene products. Putative operon structure investigation was conducted by reviewing genomic annotation tables.

Bacterial strain, cultivation conditions

A. pasteurianus CGMCC 1.41 was purchased from the China General Microbiological Culture Collection Center. E. coli Top10 was purchased from Invitrogen (Karslruhe, Germany). E. coli strains were all cultivated in LB medium at 37 °C with shaking at 180 rpm. For the preparation of the pre-culture of A. pasteurianus CGMCC 1.41, a single colony was inoculated in 10 mL GY (50 g/L glucose, 10 g/L yeast extract) medium supplied with 1% of ethanol contained in a 100 mL flask and cultivated at 30℃ with shaking at 200 rpm for 16–18h. A culture with an OD600 value ranging from 1.8 to 2.2 was used for further purpose. VM medium (1 g/L glucose, 3 g/L tryptone, 5 g/L yeast extract, adjusted to pH 6.0 using HCl) was used for the culture of A. pasteurianus CGMCC 1.41 for conjugation through triparental mating, while a synthetic medium BCas with pH 5.0 (adjusted with H2SO4), which was prepared according to Matsuoka et al. (1996) [34] without fructose, but supplemented with 5 g/L of casamino acids, was used for counterselection during gene deletion as well as for growth experiments with defined carbon sources. In addition, GYP medium (1 g/L glucose, 2 g/L peptone, 5 g/L yeast extract, adjusted to pH 6.0 using HCl) was used for carrying out acetic acid fermentation and overoxidation experiments.

Deletion of target genes in A. pasteurianus CGMCC 1.41

Markerless gene deletions were conducted following a strategy described by Kostner et al. (2013) [35] with modification. In brief, flanking regions (ranging from 900–1100 bp) of the target genes were fused and cloned into the plasmid vector pKOS6b using the SLiCE cloning method [36]. The obtained deletion vectors were introduced into and stored in E. coli Top 10. With E. coli HB101 harboring pRK2013 as the helper strain, transconjugation of the deletion vectors into A. pasteurianus CGMCC 1.41 was conducted by triparental mating, in which the OD600 values of the receiver strain, donor strain and helper strain were around 0.6, 0.6–0.8, and 0.8, respectively. Cultures of the three strains were mixed and concentrated to 100 μL, which was then dropped on a VM agar plate followed by incubation at 30℃ for 24 h. The conjugation spot was subsequently resuspended in 300 μL VM medium and spread on VM agar plates containing 50 μg/mL of kanamycin, 60 μg/mL of cefoxitin and 0.1% acetic acid. The obtained colonies were passaged twice on the same VM agar plates to screen out the potential false-positives, followed by checking the integration of the deletion vector by colony PCR with primer pairs binding next to the target site of the A. pasteurianus CGMCC 1.41 genome and to the pKOS6b backbone. Colonies showing the expected bands were then used for counterselection, which was carried out on BCas agar plates supplemented with 1 g/L of glucose, 0.015% of yeast extract, 1% of ethanol and 120 μg/mL of 5-fluorocytosine. The obtained colonies were passaged on the same BCas agar plates and their anticipated genotype was checked by PCR with the corresponding check primers. All primers used are listed in Tables S1 and S2. Colonies with bands showing successful chromosomal deletion were further verified by sequencing of the deletion site. All DNA manipulations were referred to Green and Sambrook (2012) [37].

Growth experiments with defined single carbon sources

Pre-cultures of A. pasteurianus CGMCC 1.41 wild type (WT) and the deletion mutants were centrifugated, followed by inoculation in 100 mL flasks containing 10 mL BCas medium and supplied with propionic acid, sodium propionate, acetic acid, sodium acetate, glycerol, sodium lactate and glucose, respectively. Given that some of the carbon sources belong to carboxylic acid (e.g. propionic acid and acetic acid) that are toxic to the cells and thus inhibit growth, the concentration of the each added carbon source was 1 g/L, or the same molarity of corresponding salts, that is, 13.5 mM for sodium propionate, 16.7 mM for sodium acetate, and 11.1 mM for sodium lactate. The OD600 at the starting point was set to 0.1. Inoculation in a medium without any added carbon source was also carried out. Cultivation was done at 30 °C with shaking at 200 rpm. All experiments were carried out with at least three biological replicates. Data analysis and two-sample t-tests assuming unequal variances were performed using Microsoft Excel 2019.

Acetic acid overoxidation assay

The pre-cultures of A. pasteurianus CGMCC 1.41 WT as well as all deletion mutants were harvested by centrifugation, followed by inoculation in 250 mL flasks containing 25 mL GYP medium with the addition of 2% ethanol, setting the starting OD600 at 0.1. Cultivation was carried out by shaking at 200 rpm at 30℃, during which the OD600 value and acidity were monitored. Titration with 0.1 M NaOH solution with phenolphthalein as the indicator was used for the determination of acidity.

For each genotype, 12 biological replicates were prepared. Experimental data from each strain were collected and organized as follows. Using an interval width of 1, the sample frequencies of OD₆₀₀ values at days 4–7 of cultivation were calculated for each strain and used to generate frequency distribution histograms together with reverse cumulative frequency curves. Similarly, using an interval width of 0.5, the sample frequencies of acidity at days 5–7 were calculated and used to plot frequency distribution histograms together with cumulative frequency curves. On this basis, Mann Whitney U tests were performed for the analysis of statistical significance, of which the obtained p values were Holm–Bonferroni adjusted. Furthermore, effect sizes were estimated as rank-biserial correlation. Data processing was done using R 4.5.2 (https://www.r-project.org/).

Results

Distribution of the prp genes in acetic acid bacteria

Currently, 20 genera within the family Acetobacteraceae have been reported as AAB. In order to investigate the distribution of the prp genes within this group of bacteria, a total of 511 AAB genomes covering all the 20 genera was analyzed. With the aim of guaranteeing accuracy due to the homology present between PrpB and isocitrate lyase, PrpC and citrate synthase, as well as PrpE and Acs, respectively, protein sequences could only be recognized as the corresponding gene products after being annotated with the corresponding Interpro family IDs (that is, IPR012695 for PrpB, IPR011278 for PrpC, IPR005656 for PrpD, IPR012694 for PrpE, IPR007400 for PrpF, IPR012708 for AcnD). As shown in Table 1, more than 210 sequences of PrpB, PrpC, and PrpD were respectively identified in the 511 AAB genomes, among which around 190 sequences belong to Acetobacter. Furthermore, almost 90% of genomes in this genus possess all prp core genes, clearly indicating the prevalence of these genes in Acetobacter. Despite of this however, none of the genomes from A. cibinongensis, A. oeni, and A. thailandicus harbor any prp core genes. With respect to other AAB genera, a complete set of the prp core genes was found in every one of the 5 Acidomonas genomes that were available, as well as in part of Gluconacetobacter and Komagataeibacter strains, while none of the prp core genes were identified in the available genomes of the remaining 16 genera.

As for other prp genes, none of the 511 AAB genomes encodes a protein annotated with PrpE’s InterPro family ID IPR012694, whereas the homologous acs ORF was present in 13 AAB genera (Table S3). Besides, neither prpF nor acnD were found in any of the 511 analyzed AAB genomes. On the other hand, AcnD was reported to be sensitive to oxygen [38], which is therefore not suitable for the obligate aerobic AAB.

Additionally, the distribution analysis of prp genes across bacteria was also performed, of which the results were shown in Fig. S2.

Almost all prp core genes listed in Table 1 were arranged adjacent to each other and in the same orientation in the respective genomes, forming operon-like structures in the sequential order of prpDCB (Fig. 2), except for a single prpD paralog existing in the genomes of A. malorum LMG 1699, A. persici Dm-46 and A. persici Dm-49. Notably, in many Acetobacter and all Komagataeibacter genomes where prp gene clusters were found, including A. pasteurianus CGMCC 1.41, an acs gene is located closely adjacent to prpB, replacing prpE in the putative operon compared to classical prp operons in other bacteria (Fig. 2). To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report of the prp gene cluster containing an acs gene in place of prpE. For the clarity of the expression in this article, such acs gene constituting part of the putative prp operon is hereafter referred to as prpE’.

Roles of the prpDCBE’ operon in carbon source metabolism and acetic acid resistance in A. pasteurianus CGMCC 1.41

The widespread occurrence of the prp operon in Acetobacter suggests that the products of these genes may be important for the general physiology and for basic biological activities of the strains within this genus. To facilitate the functional investigation, a total of 6 markerless deletion mutants of the prp operon, that is ΔprpD, ΔprpC, ΔprpB, ΔprpE’, ΔprpDCB and ΔprpDCBE’, were constructed from A. pasteurianus CGMCC 1.41. Given that A. pasteurianus CGMCC 1.41 possesses the intact set of prp core genes, it is reasonable to expect that this strain can utilize propionate as a carbon source. To this end, the role of the prp operon in carbon source metabolism was investigated. Additionally, acetic acid resistance represents an important biological property of a vinegar-producing strain, which might also be enhanced by genes of prp operon as well, as was proposed previously [28]. Therefore, how the prp operon contributes to this process of A. pasteurianus CGMCC 1.41 will also be addressed below.

Effects of losing genes of the prpDCBE’ operon on the catabolism of carbon sources

The role of the prp operon in carbon sources metabolism was investigated through a series of sole carbon source experiments. To achieve this, A. pasteurianus CGMCC 1.41 WT as well as its deletion mutants of the prp operon were inoculated in the synthetic media BCas supplemented with only one carbon source. The results demonstrated that if no carbon source was added to BCas, only WT showed faint growth (Fig. 3A), presumably based on the limited capability of the WT to utilize amino acids present in the BCas. Furthermore, none of the genotypes showed growth in the medium with 1 g/L (13.5 mM) propionic acid as the carbon source, which had a starting pH of around 4.1 (Fig. 3B). On the contrary, if the same molar concentration of sodium propionate was used instead (initial pH around 5.5), growth was observed in the case of the WT as well as ΔprpE’, whereby the former performed better than the latter (Fig. 3C). In contrast, all other deletion mutants, lacking either any one or all of the prp core genes, were unable to grow when sodium propionate was the carbon source. These results indicate that A. pasteurianus CGMCC 1.41 can catabolize propionate, but not propionic acid, as a carbon source, while the loss of any one of the prp core genes completely deprives the strain of its propionate utilization ability.

Growth of all genotypes in a synthetic medium without carbon source (A) as well as with propionic acid (B) and sodium propionate (C) as sole carbon source

The function of the A. pasteurianus CGMCC 1.41’s prpDCBE’ operon was found not only confined to propionate utilization. As shown in Fig. 4A, when ethanol acted as the carbon source, growth of the WT strain was superior to that of ΔprpE’, ΔprpDCBE’ and ΔprpDCB when cultivated for 6–24 h. Besides, after cultivating for 12–24 h, growth rates decreased with significant differences in the order of WT < ΔprpDCB < ΔprpE’ < ΔprpDCBE’, illustrating that the prp core genes and prpE’ both contribute to utilizing ethanol as the carbon source for growth, while prpE’ plays a more important role. Notably, the growth rate of ΔprpDCB was significantly lower than that of ΔprpC when cultivated for 6–12 h, reflecting that the additional loss of prpD or prpB upon deleting prpC reduced growth on ethanol as the carbon source, which indicated a potential function of PrpD or PrpB in ethanol assimilation. Furthermore, when acetic acid was used as the carbon source (initial pH = 4.0), the growth of ΔprpD, ΔprpC as well as ΔprpDCB tended to resemble that of the WT, while the growth rates of the strains lacking prpE’ were shown to be significantly lower after 9 h of the cultivation (Fig. 4B), which was also observed in the case when sodium acetate served as the carbon source (initial pH = 5.5, Fig. 4C). On the contrary, if glycerol was added as carbon source, growth rates of ΔprpDCB, ΔprpDCBE’ and ΔprpC were lower than that of the WT, with the difference being significant after cultivating for 48h, while deleting prpE’ alone almost had no effect on growth. Moreover, although deleting either all prp core genes or prpE’ alone did not significantly affect the growth when glucose (Fig. 4E) or lactate (Fig. 4F) were used as the carbon source, ΔprpD and ΔprpB usually had an obviously lower growth rate than WT or ΔprpDCB, as demonstrated in Fig. 4A, B, D, and E. One possible explanation for this can be the accumulation of 2-MC and 2-MIC whose toxicity has been well documented [14–16], implying a potential detoxification roles of PrpD and PrpB for the 2-MCC metabolites.

Role of the prp operon in acetic acid overoxidation

Given the fact that PrpE’ is actually an acetyl-CoA synthetase, a critical enzyme for acetic acid overoxidation, while the 2-MCC is correlated with and similar to the TCA cycle, through which acetic acid overoxidation proceeds, it is reasonable to speculate that the prp operon may be related to this important biological activity of A. pasteurianus. To verify this, acetic acid overoxidation assays were conducted. A. pasteurianus CGMCC 1.41 WT and all mutants of the prp operon were inoculated in GYP media supplemented with 2% of ethanol. The results of an initial assay with three biological replicates revealed remarkable variability in both OD600 value and acidity during the overoxidation phase (Fig. S1). As the presence of first stationary phase can be regarded as the adversity of acetic acid imposing on the cells, these results suggested the instability of acetic acid resistance of A. pasteurianus CGMCC 1.41, which is common among AAB strains [39, 40].

In order to present a clear comparison of abilities among all genotypes in terms of acetic acid overoxidation, an extended assay with 12 biological replicates was carried out, in which the frequency distribution of the OD600 values during cultivating for 4–7 d as well as that of the acidity during cultivating for 5–7 d of WT and all mutants were analyzed. In addition to this, non-parameter Mann Whitney U tests were performed to analyze the statistical significance of the differences reflected by the data, of which the detailed results are listed in Table S4.

As shown in Fig. S1, all strains produced approximate 2% acetic acid from the added 2% ethanol after 24 h of cultivation, with their OD600 values reached and remained around 2 during the first stationary phase. Combining this with our previous observations on the patterns of acetic acid overoxidation behaviors of the strains, an OD600 > 3 or acidity ≤ 1.5% can be sufficiently regarded as the initiation of acetic acid overoxidation, while an acidity > 2% indicates that the overoxidation was yet to be started. Furthermore, an OD600 > 4 or acidity ≤ 1% reflects that the overoxidation was further processed, while an acidity ≤ 0.5% may act as the indication of finishing the overoxidation process. These criteria may be referred to when discussing the results reflected by Figs. 5 and 6 in the following paragraphs.

As shown in Fig. 5A1, OD600 values of the WT cultures measured over 4–7 days of the cultivation were highly variable and spanned a wide range. The corresponding probability density distribution apparently deviated from the normal distribution and exhibited a high degree of uncertainty. Therefore, presenting these results as mean values ± standard error substantially lacks statistical significance. Precisely, after cultivating for 4 days, 16.67% of the samples had OD600 values above 4, while there were still 83.33% of the samples having OD600 values below 3. As the cultivation continued, the proportion of samples with higher OD600 values as well as lower acidity increased progressively, indicating that the acetic acid overoxidation of the WT successively initiated and proceeded. At the 7th day of the cultivation, the acidity of all WT samples was below 2%—the level derived from all the added 2% ethanol, while 91.67% of samples nearly accomplished acetic acid overoxidation (acidity < 0.5%). These results suggest that the 12 WT samples in this study successively initiated acetic acid overoxidation at the days 3–6 of the cultivation, indicating that the duration of the first stationary phase is highly variable, which reflects the unstable acetic acid resistance of this strain. Moreover, the highly variable duration of the first stationary phase probably caused the uncertain OD600 values that are not in accordance with the normal distribution during the overoxidation stage.

Losing prpE’ severely inhibited the overoxidation process. As shown in Fig. 5A2 and A3, none of the ΔprpE' and ΔprpDCBE' samples exhibited an OD600 > 3 until the 7th day of cultivation, at which the corresponding proportion of the two mutants were only 8.33% and 25%, respectively. Likewise, there were more than 60% of ΔprpE’ and ΔprpDCBE' samples still had an acidity greater than 2% at the day 6, while samples with acetic acid concentration ≤ 1.5% were not shown until the day 7, at which the proportion of samples with acidity ≤ 0.5% was still 0 (Fig. 6A2, A3). Additionally, statistical significance was present between WT and ΔprpE’ in OD600 values and acidity almost during the whole period where the data were analyzed (Figs. 5B, 6B).

Comparing Fig. 5 A1 and A4 as well as Fig. 6A1 and A4, it can be reflected that the overoxidation process of WT started earlier and procced faster than that of ΔprpDCB. Furthermore, Mann Whitney U tests also demonstrated that WT had significantly higher OD600 values during days 4–6 and significantly lower acidity at the day 7 than ΔprpDCB. In addition, it is worth noting that the differences of OD600 values between ΔprpE’ and ΔprpDCBE’ also showed statistical significance at the 5th day of the cultivation (Fig. 5B). Collectively, these results highlighted the promotional effect of the whole set of prpDCB genes in acetic acid overoxidation.

As shown in Fig. 5A6, deleting prpC alone appeared to promote the initiation of acetic acid overoxidation, reflected by the increased proportion of cultures with OD600 > 3 or 4 on the 4th and 5th days of cultivation compared to the WT (Fig. 5A1). Additionally, 58.33% and 75% of ΔprpC samples had acidity below 0.5% at the day 5 and day 6, respectively (Fig. 6A6), exceeding 8.33% and 50% of the WT at the same period (Fig. 6A1). Despite that statistical significance was absent in above mentioned differences after Holm–Bonferroni adjustment, the lower acidity of this mutant compared to the WT at day 5 remained significant based on the unadjusted p value (Table S4). However, 25% of the ΔprpC samples still had an acidity > 2% and OD600 ≤ 3 on the 7th day (Figs. 5A6-4, 6 A6-3), whereas OD600 and acidity of the WT samples at the same time were all above 4 and below 1%, respectively (Figs. 5A1-4, 6A1-3). Although it remains challenging to draw definitive conclusions from the current data, plausible assumptions regarding the effect of PrpC on the acetic acid overoxidation can be formulated. Overall, PrpC appeared to inhibit the overoxidation process, though this enzyme may also have an enhancing function, possibly related to its citrate synthase activity that can promote acetic acid oxidation.

Moreover, ΔprpC generally had higher OD600 values as well as lower acidity than ΔprpDCB (Figs. 5A4, A6, 6A4, A6), with statistical significance presenting in the difference of OD600 values during days 4–6 (Fig. 5B). Considering these results together, it can be inferred that the subsequent loss of prpD and prpB upon the absence of prpC further hindered acetic acid overoxidation. In this regard, the promoting effect of prpDCB is likely to come from the enzymes encoded by prpD or prpB.

Deleting prpD alone substantially impaired acetic acid overoxidation. Samples with an OD600 > 3 in ΔprpD did not appear until the 7th day of the cultivation (Fig. 5A5), at which 58.33% of the samples of this genotype still exhibited an acidity greater than 2%—the highest among all strains (Fig. 6A5-3). Moreover, on the 7th day, only 8.33% of ΔprpD samples had an acidity ≤ 1.5%, while none had an acidity ≤ 0.5%. Intriguingly, the corresponding proportions were even lower than those of ΔprpDCB (Figs. 5A4, A5, 6A4, A5), between which the differences in OD600 values at days 6–7 and in acidity at the day 7 showed statistical significance (Figs. 5B, 6B). As prpC is present in ΔprpD whereas absent in ΔprpDCB, these results are in line with the interpretations mentioned above that PrpC might exert considerable inhibition on the overoxidation process.

The mutant lacking prpB alone tended to have lower OD600 values as well as higher acidity than WT during acetic acid overoxidation process (Figs. 5A1, A7, 6A1, A7), with statistical significance shown in the difference for OD600 values at the days 4–6 and for acidity at the days 6–7. These results altogether reflect a weakened acetic acid overoxidation caused by the deletion of prpB alone. Since both prpD and prpC are present in ΔprpB, it is reasonable to presume that the accumulation of 2-MIC resulted from the interrupted 2-MCC pathway may contribute to the corresponding inhibition. Notably, ΔprpB was prone to have higher OD600 values at the days 5–7 as well as lower acidity at the days 6–7 than ΔprpDCB, as shown in Figs. 5B and 6B, though the difference was not statistically significant. This implies that the additional deletion of prpDC in the absence of prpB might further suppress acetic acid overoxidation. As this biological process tended to be promoted in ΔprpC but markedly inhibited in ΔprpD, the loss of prpD may exert a greater impact on the slowed acetic acid overoxidation process of ΔprpDCB compared to ΔprpB.

In summary of the above paragraphs, acetic acid overoxidation of most mutants was inhibited, except for ΔprpC, in which the tendency of promotion was exhibited. Furthermore, the degree of inhibition observed in ΔprpD, ΔprpE’ and ΔprpDCBE’ was much greater than that of ΔprpB and ΔprpDCB. This suggests that PrpE’ is probably crucial for acetic acid overoxidation, while the whole set of prpDCB genes may also contributes to this process. Furthermore, the enhancing effect from the prp core genes is more likely to be attributed to PrpD or PrpB, with PrpD probably playing the more prominent role.

Discussion

The prp operon and the 2-MCC of AAB were unveiled in our previous transcriptomic study on A. pasteurianus, where all the relevant genes showed significant up-regulation during the acetic acid fermentation process [28]. Moreover, the up-regulation of the prp gene cluster in A. aceti when grew in an ethanol-containing medium along with its down-regulation accompanied by the decreased survival rate of A. pasteurianus Ab3 under acetic acid stress were also demonstrated [30, 31]. In the present study, genes constituting this operon were found widely distributed in the genus Acetobacter, but relatively rare in other AAB genera, except in Acidomonas as well as in a few species of Gluconacetobacter and Komagataeibacter (Table 1). Taken together, these findings implies that the prp genes may be of general importance for the physiology of Acetobacter within the group of AAB.

In accordance with the propionate-catabolizing function of the 2-MCC pathway, the prp operon was found to confer the ability to utilize propionate as a carbon source on A. pasteurianus CGMCC 1.41. Since each one of the prp core genes was required, 2-MCC is presumed to be the exclusive propionate-metabolizing pathway in this strain. However, the specific biological significance of propionate consumption for an AAB strain is currently unclear. On the other hand, as propionic acid is toxic at low pH values, Acetobacter species possibly serve as the propionate detoxifiers in their native habitats. Additionally, the toxicity of the 2-MCC intermediates, including 2-MC and 2-MIC, has also been well described [3, 15, 16]. In this study, the single deletion of either prpD or prpB usually resulted in a much more serious impact on the growth on different carbon sources and on acetic acid overoxidation, highlighting the importance of the integrity of the prp core genes as well as the 2-MCC pathway upon the initiation of the first core 2-MCC reaction catalyzed by PrpC. A plausible explanation for this can be the accumulation of the toxic 2-MC or 2-MIC attributed to a disrupted 2-MCC pathway. However, definite conclusions still require the direct determination of intracellular levels of these two metabolites in further studies.

A distinctive feature of AAB’s prp gene cluster is the presence of an acs gene in place of the typical prpE, which is designated as prpE’ in this study (Fig. 2). It has been reported that the homology with PrpE enables Acs to function as propionyl-CoA synthetase [7]. In the present work, PrpE’ was also demonstrated to be able to enhance the utilization of propionate in A. pasteurianus CGMCC 1.41 (Fig. 3). On the other hand, the acetyl-CoA synthetase nature of PrpE’ was reflected by its promoting effect on metabolizing acetic acid as the carbon source, which further contributes to both ethanol assimilation (Fig. 4), involving the intracellular NAD-ADH and NADP-ALDH [25], as well as acetic acid overoxidation (Figs. 5, 6), a well-recognized acetic acid resistance mechanism of AAB [27]. Considering the unique features of this gene in the context of prp operon, PrpE’ warrants more in-depth investigation, particularly enzymatic assays based on the purified protein in future studies.

With respect to acetic acid overoxidation, the severe inhibition observed subsequent to the loss of prpE’ suggested its function in this process can be crucial. Furthermore, prp core genes altogether tended to play an enhancing role as well. Despite of this, a general inhibiting effect from PrpC was present, for which its production of the toxic 2-MC may serve as a reasonable interpretation. Meanwhile, since PrpC and citrate synthase compete for the same substrate, oxaloacetate (Fig. 1), deletion of prpC is likely to facilitate an increased oxaloacetate flux into the TCA cycle, thereby enhancing the acetic acid overoxidation process. However, both assumptions remain hypothetical without the determination of the relevant metabolites. Moreover, the contributory role of the entire prp core genes and the suppressive effect of PrpC altogether suggested that PrpD or PrpB might be associated with promoting the acetic acid overoxidation, in which PrpD appeared to play a more prominent role, as is reflected in Figs. 5, 6 and discussed in the relevant sections of Results. Notably, PrpC catalyzes the first step of the 2-MCC core reaction (Fig. 1), hence the loss of prpC fundamentally disrupts the entire 2-MCC pathway. In this context, the phenotypes of the ΔprpC mutant are expected to resemble those of the ΔprpDCB strain. On the contrary, however, deleting prpC alone resulted in a much better performance in acetic acid overoxidation compared to the strain where all set of prpDCB were absent, implying that the corresponding promotive function could be independent of the 2-MCC pathway. Besides, the accompanying phylogenic analysis of this study speculated that prpD might be acquired before obtaining prpC and prpB in many AAB genomes, including A. pasteurianus CGMCC 1.41 (Fig. S3), which appeared to support the 2-MCC-independent functions of PrpD. Moreover, PrpD seemed to be able to enhance acetic acid production through the incomplete oxidation of ethanol as well (see Fig. S4). Given that acetic acid overoxidation also occurs following the oxidation of ethanol, the aforementioned possible supportive functions of PrpD may involve its interaction with ethanol. Although these speculations are yet to be verified, they nevertheless suggested that PrpD from AAB deserves a comprehensive functional characterization. Evidences from previous researches have indicated that PrpD is able to catalyze the dehydration of a range of hydroxy acids apart from 2-MC [41]. Moreover, the PrpD homolog from Bacillus subtilis was found capable of hydrating aconitate to citrate, reflecting its potential to add a hydroxy group to double bond-containing compounds [42]. To this end, substrate specificity other than 2-MC, including the ability to catalyze reverse hydration reactions, may act as a useful framework for further functional investigation of AAB’s PrpD.

In this study, the distribution and function of the prp genes in AAB were investigated for the first time. Notably, the functional elucidation was not only restricted to the 2-MCC pathway and propionate metabolism, but was also combined with the biological traits of AAB, revealing potential novel roles of this set of genes, such as their involvement in acetic acid-resisting overoxidation process. However, highly variable data that apparently deviated from the normal distribution were obtained from the corresponding assays, which reflected the unstable acid resistance of AAB strains [39, 40]. To enable a more rigorous evaluation of the impact of losing different prp genes on this process, the present work increased the number of biological replicates and demonstrated the results as frequency distribution, followed by the non-parametric Mann Whitney U tests to analyze the statistical significance (Figs. 5, 6, Table S4). On the other hand, a sample size of 12 still appeared to be relatively limited under this circumstance, which may result in a lack of statistical significance for some observed differences, especially those between WT and ΔprpC as well as ΔprpDCB and ΔprpB. Hence, further increasing the number of biological replicates can be helpful for improving statistical power and yielding more robust conclusions based on data with large variance.

Furthermore, it has to be acknowledged that the present work represents only a preliminary step toward fully clarifying the general biological significance of genes of the prp operon in the genus Acetobacter, in which they were found widely distributed (Table 1). Meanwhile, drawing firm conclusions concerning these genes’ 2-MCC- and propionate-metabolizing-independent functions in A. pasteurianus CGMCC 1.41 remains difficult due to several limitations of this study. For example, the lack of metabolic flux analysis of the 2-MCC pathway, in particular, the isotopic labeling approaches as demonstrated in E. coli [2], or the metabolomic data reflecting the intracellular levels of 2-MCC metabolites as well as the enzymology assays for the prp gene products, including the substrate specificity investigation and the kinetics analysis. On the other hand, these works and the corresponding hypotheses raised by this study also stand for important directions for future research. Additionally, though not included in the present work, the construction of the complementary strains and/or the mutant series containing all combinations of deletions for the prp genes can act as the powerful tools for gaining a system-level view of their roles in AAB as well. Overall, since a broader functional relevance of the prp genes with AAB strain was implied in this study, we believe that these genes deserve a deeper characterization in this group of bacteria, which may not only advance our knowledge of the functional roles of these genes, but also offer new insights into conserved physiological traits across the genus Acetobacter.

Conclusion

The present work revealed the widespread occurrence of the prp gene cluster in the AAB genus Acetobacter, which denoted one of this genus’s special traits within AAB. Notably, many AAB’s putative prp operons contain an acs gene (termed as prpE’ in this study) instead of the typical prpE. This set of genes indeed confers the ability to utilize propionate as a carbon source on A. pasteurianus CGMCC 1.41. but their functions appeared not to be confined within propionate catabolism. Their promotive roles in metabolizing other common carbon sources of A. pasteurianus were also demonstrated, such as ethanol, acetic acid, and glycerol. Concerning the 2-MCC pathway, its integrity as well as that of the prp core genes exhibited remarkable importance once the first core reaction starts. Meanwhile, PrpE’ probably plays a critical role in an acetic acid-resisting mechanism—acetic acid overoxidation, which seemed to be promoted by PrpD as well. Although several limitations exist that prevent definitive conclusions from many aspects, this study expands the knowledge to the function of the prp genes in the context of AAB, which also indicates the significance of conducting more comprehensive investigations into these genes within this group of bacteria in further researches.
