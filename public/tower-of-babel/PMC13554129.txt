Pollutant emissions of conventional energy generators with increased renewable energy sources: a 2030 New York case study
Authors: A. Andres F. Ramirez, L. Alberto J. Lamadrid
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This work analyzes 2019 hourly data from the New York Independent System Operator (NYISO) energy generators to forecast pollutant emissions for 2030. Leveraging methods and insights from the Advanced Research Projects Agency–Energy (ARPA-E) Performance-based Energy Resource Feedback, Optimization, and Risk Management (PERFORM) project’s optimization tools and the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) historical data, we produce a method to determine future emissions scenarios within the NYISO region to be used as part of operational studies. Integrating historical data with forecasting software, this study evaluates the impact of renewable energy variability on pollutant emissions, informing policy decisions for a sustainable energy future.

Introduction

The ongoing transformations of electricity systems, driven by technological change, and the scientific consensus over the effects of emissions from human activities on climate, present challenges for companies and territories1–3. For different jurisdictions, one of the main difficulties is understanding the consequences of evolving generation portfolios for global and local pollutants derived from electricity production decisions. Additionally, risk management mechanisms for balancing supply from Variable Renewable Energy Sources (VRES), conventional resources, and an increasingly active demand side require better information systems assessing the emissions from these resources and determining how these stakeholders interact in alternative counterfactual scenarios. In this work, we present a methodology to estimate future emissions of pollutants from electricity generation, illustrated using 2030 future scenarios for New York State. We complement the information from sensors and devices (e.g., continuous emissions monitoring systems) with statistical and simulation models leveraging insights from analyzing the operation of the 2019 NYISO electricity generators. Our optimization module, energy load forecast, and renewable energy generation forecast for 2030 are developed with funding from the ARPA-E PERFORM project4–11.These optimization and simulation tools are designed to address and solve problems used in system operations, including the Security-Constrained Unit Commitment (SCUC) and Security-Constrained Economic Dispatch (SCED). The SCUC and SCED optimization modules used in this framework follow the formulation introduced in our earlier work12, to which the reader is referred for a detailed mathematical description and implementation details. The information about generators and the transmission system accounts for the evolving characteristics of the grid, including generator retirements and additions, as well as the interconnection queue. By explicitly linking renewable energy variability to unit commitment, dispatch decisions, and emissions outcomes, the framework enables the evaluation of how increasing renewable generation production changes the operations and timing of conventional resources, and the associated pollutant emissions. Our study forecasts potential pollutant emissions scenarios for selected weeks in 2030 within the NYISO region, focusing on periods that reflect high-demand operating conditions observed in 2019, and integrating the capabilities of an innovative environmental module. These forecasts are relevant for policy decisions related to emissions regulation, resource adequacy, renewable integration targets, and the design of operational and environmental constraints faced by system operators.

Our analysis offers insights into the changing dynamics of energy generation, enabling a more comprehensive understanding of how emissions levels, timing, and location respond to operational and portfolio changes, and informing policy and planning decisions aimed at balancing tradeoffs on grid reliability, energy affordability, and sustainability of evolving power systems.Guided by these considerations, we study the following research questions. First, how do changes in generation portfolios and renewable energy variability affect the level, timing and spatial distribution of pollutant emissions from conventional generators? Second, how can historical production level operational and emissions data be combined with.

forward-looking simulations to produce internally consistent emissions forecasts under future grid configurations? And, third, what insights do these forecasts provide for system planning and regulatory analysis with increased use of VRES?

The objective of this study is to use historical data on pollutant emissions from energy generators to develop a forecasting framework that characterizes emissions outcomes for 2030 with the proposed grid characteristics and producer-level operational features. we illustrate in the NYISO system. The 2019 data were obtained from the EPA13–15, and include hourly operational and pollutant emissions reporting to the Continuous Emission Monitoring Systems (CEMS). Data for 2030 are generated through forward-looking simulations conducted. These produce counterfactual operational conditions for a stylized 2030 grid. Several modules forecast input features, optimize the operational dispatches for future 2030 scenarios, and extract the producer-level operational information from the SCUC and SCED modules.

We present a comprehensive machine learning pipeline for forecasting pollutant emissions of electric generators by combining models trained with 2019 data and simulated 2030 operating conditions. By integrating outputs from SCUC and SCED modules, the framework accounts for operational constraints and the variability introduced by renewable energy sources. We develop a feature engineering process that incorporates temporal dependencies, geographic characteristics, and meteorological conditions, with explicit attention to seasonal and spatial interactions. The resulting forecasts provide granular emissions estimates capturing both spatial and temporal variations critical for policy evaluation and system planning. Extensive validation supports the robustness of our approach. Although the empirical application illustrates using New York State information, the methodology is adaptable and scalable to other regions, time horizons, and system arrangements with analogous operational and emissions information, supporting broader efforts to evaluate emissions impacts of power system transitions.

Literature review

Mitigating pollutant emissions is a critical priority in addressing the escalating impacts of climate change. Electric generators, particularly those reliant on fossil fuels, are significant contributors to global emissions. These generators encounter additional ramping constraints that result in increased emissions, as they must compensate for the variability introduced by the growing penetration of renewable energy sources and the rise of Peer-to-peer (P2P) markets that prioritize these renewable energies16. Consequently, fossil fuel generators often need to adjust their output more frequently to balance supply and demand. Accurate emissions forecasting is crucial for policymakers, industry stakeholders, and researchers to develop effective strategies for minimizing environmental impacts17. These efforts are supported by Independent System Operator (ISO) and Regional Transmission Organization (RTO), which are equipped with detailed operational data and are well-positioned to provide precise, location-based Emission Factor (EF). This capability enhances forecasting models for estimating grid-related pollutant emissions18.

Building on the importance of accurate pollutant emissions forecasting, this section reviews the literature in three key areas:

(1) Forecasting methods, including both traditional and machine learning approaches; (2) Economic modeling, which accounts for non-convexity in costs, emissions, and regulatory factors; and (3) The integration of emissions into the scheduling process across various sectors, particularly electricity generation. These approaches all aim to enhance the accuracy of emissions forecasting and accounting, supporting better planning and policymaking for effective emissions reduction strategies.

Forecasting approaches: traditional and machine learning

Emission forecasting plays a crucial role in energy generation planning and ensuring compliance with environmental regulations at both national and regional levels. Traditional statistical methods, such as time series analysis, trend analysis, and regression models, have been widely used for emission forecasting. For example19, employed trend analysis to predict CO2 emissions for the top 25 emitting countries, relying on historical data from 1971 to 2007. While these models are relatively simple and interpretable, they often face challenges in capturing the complex, non-linear relationships inherent in emission processes, especially in the context of renewable energy sources.

In recent years, Machine Learning (ML) and Artificial Intelligence (AI) models have emerged as promising tools for improving the accuracy of emission forecasts20. applied Artificial Neural Network (ANN), including the Group Method of Data Handling (GMDH) and Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP), to model CO2 emissions in European countries. Other works, such as21,22, explored deep learning models for renewable energy forecasting, leveraging historical emission data, operational parameters, and weather conditions to produce more reliable forecasts. Despite the growing reliance on ML, traditional methods remain relevant for certain applications, especially where interpretability is a priority.

Studies like23used evolutionary algorithms to train neural networks, demonstrating the applicability of ML to forecast power generation, demand, and carbon intensity in Ireland. Moreover24, employed Monte Carlo simulations to forecast emissions, accounting for system dispatch, reserve capacity, and carbon emissions across different scenarios. These studies highlight the diversity of approaches used in emission forecasting and the challenges posed by renewable energy sources.

Uncertainty remains a significant challenge in emission forecasting, driven by factors such as data fluctuations, model assumptions, and external economic or policy influences. The literature discusses probabilistic forecasting and ensemble modeling as viable approaches to address uncertainty and improve the robustness of predictions.

More recent synthesis studies indicate that no single forecasting paradigm can be considered universally optimal for emissions prediction, particularly under conditions of increased system variability driven by high renewable energy penetration. As discussed2, emissions forecasting methods span statistical, machine learning, deep learning, and hybrid approaches, with model performance being highly context dependent. Their review emphasizes that ensemble and hybrid frameworks, including boosting-based methods and optimization-enhanced machine learning models, are among the most effective strategies for capturing nonlinearities and variability while maintaining robust generalization performance.

In line with these findings, hybrid forecasting approaches that integrate machine learning or ensemble models with complementary statistical or optimization components have been increasingly recommended. Such frameworks enable the simultaneous representation of temporal dynamics and complex feature interactions, addressing key limitations of standalone models3. This study therefore focuses on ensemble and boosting-based regression models within a hybrid framework, as they are well suited to capture the increased variability introduced by renewable energy sources while preserving a level of interpretability that remains essential for emissions analysis and policy-relevant decision making.

Economic modeling in electric markets: non-convexity, costs, and regulations

Economic modeling for emission reductions in electric markets faces challenges related to non-convexity in both costs and emissions, alongside the complexity introduced by regulatory frameworks. Fossil fuel power plants, including coal, natural gas, and oil-based generators, continue to dominate the energy mix in many regions, contributing substantially to global pollutant emissions. Studies such as25 focus on integrating non-linear emissions curves, which are piecewise linearized, into the SCUC optimization process, ensuring that emissions are accounted for in operational decisions.

The increased integration of renewable energy sources such as wind and solar further complicates economic modeling efforts. These sources introduce variability in both costs and emissions due to their intermittent nature. For example26, explores the use of negative pricing for wind generators in unit commitment as a mechanism to ensure dispatch priority for wind, thus reducing emissions while managing grid stability.

Regulatory aspects also play a crucial role in shaping emission reduction strategies27. examined the impact of both direct (e.g., tax credits) and indirect (e.g., carbon tax) subsidies on renewable energy investment and emissions. The author found that while both subsidies contribute to lower emissions, indirect subsidies may inadvertently lead to lower investments in renewable energy. Emissions trading systems (ETS), such as the European Union’s ETS, heavily rely on accurate emission forecasts to set appropriate caps and allocate allowances. Similarly, in the U.S., emission forecasting is an essential component of state implementation plans under the Clean Air Act28.

Incorporating emissions into scheduling: energy and other sectors

Incorporating emissions into scheduling processes is a growing trend, particularly in energy markets but also in sectors like manufacturing and transportation. The internalization of environmental externalities into scheduling decisions is increasingly recognized as a key approach to reducing emissions29. explored the integration of emissions trading directly into the SCUC optimization problem, employing Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) to address emissions reduction in a smart grid environment. Another study by25integrated pollutant emissions directly into the unit commitment optimization problem, converting emissions into monetary costs. Moreover30, presents a model examining how oligopolistic firms producing partially differentiated products can integrate emissions into their energy scheduling through two regulatory approaches: Cap-and-Trade and Taxes. It demonstrates that well-designed regulations not only reduce pollution but can also enhance firms’ profits by enabling them to coordinate production cuts.

Beyond the energy sector31, analyzed emissions trading in production planning, creating a dynamic model that allowed a manufacturer to choose between green and conventional production technologies. The model accounted for stochastic market prices for emissions allowances and demonstrated the financial benefits of adopting green technologies.

Machine learning methods have also been applied to integrate emissions forecasting into energy scheduling32. leveraged deep learning techniques to forecast renewable energy output, helping grid operators balance demand and minimize emissions. The literature consistently emphasizes the importance of incorporating emissions considerations into scheduling decisions to meet environmental goals. The reviewed literature underscores the critical role of emission forecasting in mitigating environmental impacts. Traditional statistical methods remain relevant but are increasingly complemented by ML and AI techniques, particularly in capturing the non-linear dynamics of renewable energy sources. Economic modeling for emission.

Additionally, the integration of emissions into scheduling processes is essential for reducing environmental footprints in both energy and other sectors. Across these domains, the goal of improving forecast accuracy and incorporating emissions into operational decisions remains paramount.

Methodology

Our methodology is aimed at modeling the stochastic nature of pollutant emissions from energy generators, incorporating their generation profiles and characteristics. The structure built seeks to understand the relationship between the independent features and the individual pollutants studied. This framework allowed us to forecast emissions levels in 2030 using the output of the SCUC and SCED optimization module for NYISO as well as the forecasted renewable energy sources and load profiles that account for the grid topology and demand requirements.

Methodology overview

We initiated our forecasting process by obtaining projections for renewable energy sources and load for 2030 through collaboration with the ARPA-E PERFORM project. These projections consist of ensembles of internally consistent scenarios for demand, wind, and solar generation, capturing uncertainty and correlation across renewable resources and load. These forecasts provided crucial inputs regarding expected renewable energy outputs and electricity demand. Utilizing the 2030 grid topology and the forecasted renewable energy sources and load from the previous step, we conducted SCUC and SCED optimization runs. SCUC ensured optimal unit commitment considering transmission capacities and operational constraints, while SCED refined generation dispatch to economically meet forecasted electricity demand. Running the optimization across multiple scenarios allows the resulting emissions forecasts to reflect variability in renewable availability and demand conditions, rather than a single deterministic outcome. Following optimization outputs, we engaged in data preparation and feature engineering. This initial stage involved cleaning datasets, normalizing variables, handling missing values, and addressing outliers. Additionally, new influential features were engineered to capture non-linear relationships with pollutant emissions. The subsequent stage focused on training machine learning models using historical data from 2019. We partitioned datasets into training and testing sets using a temporal split to preserve data sequence integrity.

Model training included fine-tuning hyper-parameters and leveraging advanced methodologies to enhance predictive accuracy. Evaluation of model performance was crucial as we aimed to strike a balance between accuracy and interpretability. This was achieved by using appropriate metrics to validate the accuracy of pollutant-level forecasts. Our approach considered a comprehensive set of features, detailed in Table 1, and incorporated additional complexities to enhance forecasting precision. Finally, we introduced lagged variables and feature interactions to capture temporal dependencies in pollutant levels. Lagged variables provided insights into historical trends, while interactions among variables revealed non-linear relationships, enriching our understanding of emissions dynamics.

Methodological pipeline and reproducibility

Beyond the high-level workflow described in Sect. 3.3, the applied methodology follows a structured and transferable machine learning pipeline aligned with established power system analytics frameworks1. Although the models are trained and validated using data from the NYISO, the modeling approach is designed to be applicable to other electricity markets, subject to appropriate adaptation to system-specific market rules, generation portfolios, and reserve policies.

Machine learning pipeline, hyperparameter tuning and validation strategy

Model development is conducted using a consistent and reproducible pipeline that includes feature preprocessing, model training, hyperparameter tuning, and performance evaluation. In addition to regularized linear models (Lasso, Ridge, and Elastic Net), non-linear ensemble methods such as eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) are evaluated to capture complex relationships between generator operation and pollutant emissions. Hyperparameters refer to model configuration parameters that are specified prior to training and are not directly learned from the data. These parameters govern model complexity and learning behavior, such as regularization strength in linear models or tree depth and learning rate in ensemble methods. Proper selection of hyperparameters is essential to balance model flexibility and generalization performance. Hyperparameter optimization is performed systematically for all candidate models using grid-based search procedures applied exclusively to the training data. Candidate configurations are evaluated through cross-validation to ensure robust model selection and to prevent overfitting. For regularized regression models, penalty strengths and mixing parameters are explored over predefined ranges, while for ensemble methods, parameters such as maximum tree depth, learning rate, and number of estimators are tuned. Model performance during tuning and final evaluation is assessed using regression-appropriate metrics (e.g. Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE) and Mean Absolute Error (MAE), etc.). Final performance is reported on a held-out test set, providing an unbiased estimate of out-of-sample predictive capability. Additional robustness checks include comparisons across pollutants and generator categories, as well as stability assessments of model coefficients and feature importance rankings.

Model selection and interpretability

Although non-linear ensemble models often yield higher predictive accuracy, model selection explicitly considers the trade-off between performance and interpretability. While this study prioritizes accurate emissions forecasting rather than decision-based risk classification, interpretability remains important for understanding the drivers of pollutant variability. To this end, feature importance measures and SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) based explanations are used to analyze the contribution and directional impact of key predictors across pollutants.

Reproducibility and data accessibility

Propensity score matching methodology

Our research project employed Propensity Score Matching (PSM) to assign emission profiles to electric generators with missing data. The aim was to match these generators with those for which we had complete information, leveraging their known characteristics to estimate the missing emissions profiles for CO2, SO2, and NOx. Propensity score matching is a statistical technique that reduces selection bias by balancing covariates between treated and control groups. Following39, the propensity score summarizes the information contained in the covariates Xi such that, when potential outcomes are independent of treatment conditional on Xi, they are also independent of treatment conditional on the propensity score.

The propensity score represents the conditional probability of assignment to a treatment given a set of observed covariates. We used a logistic regression model to estimate the propensity scores due to its effectiveness in binary classification tasks and its capability to handle the probabilistic nature of matching procedures. To ensure the model’s generalization and robustness, we employed cross-validation techniques. The dataset was split into training and testing subsets, and the performance of the propensity score model was evaluated on the testing set to minimize discrepancies between actual and predicted emissions profiles.

Following validation of the propensity score model, it was applied to predict emissions for generators with missing data. Before matching, we conducted industry-standard preprocessing and standardization procedures to enhance the accuracy and reliability of the propensity scores. These procedures included handling missing data, normalizing variables, and addressing outliers.

While our primary validation method was cross-validation, which provides insight into model performance on unseen data, additional sensitivity analyses and validation techniques could further strengthen the robustness of the matching process. The selection of appropriate accuracy metrics during model training ensured a solid foundation for the propensity score matching, contributing to the reliability of the estimated emissions profiles.

Machine learning methods

In this section, we explore the application of advanced machine learning techniques, including Lasso, Ridge, and Elastic Net regression, as well as ensemble methods such as XGBoost, to construct predictive models for the pollutants CO2, NOx, and SO2. Our primary objective was to evaluate the accuracy of these models, with interpretation taking a secondary role in the purpose of this study. These models are developed utilizing a combination of independent variables derived from the hourly operation of electric generators. This approach allows us to capture the intricate relationships between pollutant levels and the interactions within the energy grid participants.

Lasso regression

Lasso regression, or L1 regularization, introduces a penalty term to the linear regression objective function. The objective function for Lasso regression is given by:

Here, yi represents the response variable, β0 is the intercept, βj are the coefficients for the independent variables xij, n is the number of observations, p is the number of independent variables, and λ controls the strength of the penalty.

The advantage of Lasso regression is its ability to perform feature selection by driving some coefficients to exactly zero. This helps in identifying the most influential variables in predicting pollutant levels. However, Lasso tends to select only one variable among highly correlated variables, which may lead to instability in variable selection.

Ridge regression

Ridge regression, or L2 regularization, adds a penalty term based on the square of the magnitude of coefficients to the linear regression objective function. The objective function for Ridge regression is given by:

It provides a stable solution even when the number of predictors (p) is larger than the number of observations (n). However, it does not perform variable selection as rigorously as Lasso.

During our experimentation with XGBoost, we found that it indeed demonstrated superior accuracy compared to Lasso and Ridge regression or the elastic net. The algorithm’s ability to handle non-linear relationships and its ensemble nature provided a promising avenue for improving predictive accuracy.

One of the primary concerns when evaluating this methodology was its increased complexity and the difficulty in interpreting its results compared to other tested methodologies. XGBoost operates as a black-box model, meaning that the relationship between input variables and the final prediction is not as transparent or easily explainable. Nonetheless, for this study, the main goal is to accurately represent the stochastic variability of pollutant emissions.

The hyper-parameter tuning of XGBoost hyper-parameters, such as the learning rate, maximum tree depth, and sub-sample ratios, can be a challenging task that requires extensive experimentation and computational resources. This was considered when selecting the final methodology to use for this study. In contrast, linear regression models with regularization coefficients limit the flexibility of the machine learning model; however, make it easier to fine-tune and interpret, saving valuable time and effort.

Considering the trade-off between predictive accuracy, computational time, and interpretability, we found that the benefits gained from XGBoost’s higher accuracy were sufficient to outweigh the advantages of the regularization techniques. Although the ensemble method has a black box characteristic, feature importance can be obtained and we used SHAP values to analyze the effect and correlation of the independent features with each one of the pollutants. Therefore, we chose to prioritize the XGBoost ensemble methodology as it offered a better balance between predictive performance and the ability to provide understandable and actionable insights, aligning well with the requirements of our research objectives.

Results

Accuracy metrics

Tables 3 and 4 summarize the accuracy metrics of the models evaluated in this study. Linear models, including Lasso, Ridge, and ElasticNet, underperform in comparison to the ensemble approach, as indicated by their R2 Adjusted, AIC, and BIC values. Discussion

The results we present here reproduce the emission profile of energy generators for the proposed New York State power system in 2030 using historical data from 2019. Due to data limitations, our forecasting horizon was limited from August 2nd, 2030 to August 9th, 2030. Worldwide economies are committed to the integration of renewable energy sources into their energy grids. This integration is important for the environment and long-term growth of the energy system. However, maintaining the grid stability affected by the inherent variability of renewable energy sources poses a challenge for system operators. This complexity might generate negative loops in pollution as system operators seek to maintain grid stability. This study helps to narrow that bridge of information and allows participants to account for pollution profiles in their decision-making.

We analyzed and studied pollution data from 2019 leveraging EPA and the ARPA-EPERFORM project data and optimization modules. We constructed an ad-hoc ML forecasting pipeline to generate high-accuracy emissions estimates for energy generators. These estimates are used to represent the hourly emissions profiles of generators in the NYISO system for the 2030 scenario.

Due to data availability constraints and uncertainty in grid topology projections for 2030, this research focuses on a representative week of energy generation. This week was selected due to the heightened stress on the energy grid that happened in August 2019 as a consequence of increased demand and generation constraints. Areas of future research include integrating this emissions forecasting framework with assessments generator reliability by examining how operational stresses associated with renewable variability, including increased ramping, cycling and reserve provision, affect both emissions outcomes and reliability costs and performance. Particularly under high renewable energy scenarios, conventional generators and system operators need to work together in their energy contracts to guarantee grid stability, as policies to control emissions and deviations from energy contracts are likely to take place.

The primary aim of our study is forecasting emissions for the NYISO during the simulated 2030 period. The complexity introduced by advanced machine learning techniques, including Lasso, Ridge, Elastic Net regression, and ensemble methods like XGBoost, may pose challenges in the straightforward interpretation of model outcomes. However, it’s important to note that although the model lacks interpretability, its primary accomplishment lies in effectively modeling the complex underlying stochastic process to transform the energy generation profiles of non-renewable energy sources into their expected emissions.

The performance of the proposed model is consistent with results reported in the emissions forecasting literature3. employs a hybrid ARIMA-SVR framework for urban-level carbon emission forecasting and reports a MAPE of 0.096 with an R2 of 97.5%. Although a direct comparison is not possible due to differences in methodology and scope, our results show comparable predictive performance. In particular, the tree-based XGBoost model informed by SCUC and SCED outputs achieves low test-set MAPE values for CO2, NOx, and SO2, indicating strong generalization and alignment with state-of-the-art emissions forecasting approaches.

Moreover, while our results for emissions heavily rely on 2019 data, it is imperative to note that the grid topology, energy generation profiles, and other critical factors were meticulously modeled based on the current plan and structure of the grid for 2030. Despite inherent spatial and regional limitations, we assert that our study’s adaptability allows for potential expansion to other regions, accounting for their unique characteristics.

In summary, this study develops an integrated framework to estimate generator-level pollutant emissions for a representative week in 2030 by leveraging historical data from 2019 within a hybrid operational setting that links optimization dispatch outcomes with ML emissions forecasting models. By combining SCUC and SCED outputs with generator-level operational characteristics, the approach produces hourly emissions profiles for CO2, SO2, and NOx at a level of granularity that is difficult to obtain using system-average emission factors alone. The empirical application to the NYISO system is intended to demonstrate the performance and forecasting accuracy of the proposed hybrid optimization and machine learning framework under realistic operational conditions, rather than to provide a direct comparison across years or to derive policy conclusions. The case study illustrates how renewable variability and operational constraints influence not only the magnitude of emissions, but also their temporal and spatial patterns.

These results highlight the value of forecasting tools that jointly represent operational decisions and emissions outcomes. While the primary contribution of this work is methodological, the proposed framework provides a foundation that could support emissions informed planning and exploratory policy analysis by enabling consistent evaluations across alternative portfolio and operational assumptions. Future research may extend this approach by considering longer simulation horizons, incorporating additional sources of uncertainty in operational inputs, and further integrating emissions estimates into operational and market design studies under increasing VRES penetration.
