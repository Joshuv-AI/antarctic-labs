CARBON SOLUTIONS Final Technical Report 7

Executive Summary
This report presents the results of the Wyoming Trails Carbon Hub: Developing a Roadmap for a
Sustainable Carbon Hub project, supported by the U.S. Department of Energy. The project
evaluates the technical, economic, and logistical feasibility of deploying large-scale carbon
capture, utilization, and storage (CCUS) infrastructure across Wyoming and provides a system-
level roadmap to support future development.
The analysis integrates the full CCUS value chain, including capture, transportation, utilization,
and geologic storage, within a unified modeling framework. Using tools including CO₂NCORD,
SCO₂TPRO, CostMAPPRO, and SimCCSPRO, the project evaluates how infrastructure can be
optimally configured across the state under a range of deployment scenarios. A key innovation
of this work is the incorporation of multi-modal transportation, allowing CO₂ to be moved via
pipelines, rail, and road, enabling more realistic modeling of early-stage infrastructure systems.
Results indicate that Wyoming has significant potential for CCUS deployment. The state
contains approximately 46 MtCO₂/year of capturable emissions from 73 industrial facilities, with
coal-fired power plants representing the dominant source. Capture costs are the primary driver
of total system cost, with average model-based costs of approximately $123 per tonne of CO₂,
significantly exceeding transportation and storage costs.
Geologic analysis confirms that Wyoming contains substantial theoretical CO₂ storage
resources across multiple sedimentary basins. However, practical storage feasibility is
constrained by hydrogeologic conditions, structural trapping requirements, and regulatory
limitations. Approximately 32% of modeled statewide storage potential is affected by
groundwater protection requirements, land-use restrictions, and proximity to fault systems.
Structurally favorable regions within the Greater Green River Basin emerge as the most
promising candidates for large-scale storage.
Transportation modeling demonstrates that no single mode is optimal across all conditions.
Pipelines remain the most cost-effective option at large scale, but road and rail transport play a
critical role in early deployment, particularly for smaller, distributed emission sources.
Intermodal scenarios show that truck-based transport is often preferred for facilities emitting
less than 1 MtCO₂/year, enabling flexible, near-term deployment prior to pipeline buildout.
Three business case scenarios were evaluated to understand infrastructure development
pathways. A phased deployment scenario capturing 30 MtCO₂/year demonstrates the
importance of infrastructure reuse and economies of scale, with unit costs declining from
approximately $108/tCO₂ in early years to less than $25/tCO₂ after capital recovery. A full-
capture scenario indicates that capturing all emissions in Wyoming (~42 MtCO₂/year) would
require extensive pipeline networks exceeding 1,300 km, with unit costs increasing modestly at
scale. Intermodal scenarios highlight the role of flexible transport strategies in enabling early
adoption and reducing stranded emissions.
Across all scenarios, capture costs dominate total system economics, while transportation and
storage costs play a secondary but non-negligible role at larger scales. Infrastructure reuse,
phased development, and coordinated system planning emerge as critical factors in reducing
long-term costs.
To support implementation, project results were translated into a stakeholder-oriented Carbon
Hub Roadmap, supported by interactive tools, maps, and communication materials.
Engagement activities with planners, policymakers, industry stakeholders, and Tribal

advancing CCUS deployment in Wyoming and offers a scalable framework that can be applied

1 Introduction
Carbon capture, utilization, and storage (CCUS) is increasingly recognized as a critical
component of strategies to reduce greenhouse gas emissions from industrial sources, power
generation, and emerging energy systems. In the United States, federal incentives and policy
support combined with state-level regulatory frameworks are accelerating interest in regional
carbon management hubs that can integrate CO₂ capture, transport, utilization, and storage into
coordinated infrastructure systems.
Wyoming is uniquely positioned to support the development of such a hub. The state has a long
history of energy production, including coal, oil, and natural gas, and a corresponding industrial
base that produces significant volumes of concentrated CO₂ emissions. In addition, Wyoming
contains extensive geologic storage resources, including deep saline formations and depleted
hydrocarbon reservoirs, and has established a mature regulatory framework for CO₂ injection,
including Class VI primacy. These characteristics make Wyoming one of the most promising
regions in the United States for large-scale CCUS deployment.
The Wyoming Trails Carbon Hub: Developing a Roadmap for a Sustainable Carbon Hub project
was undertaken to evaluate the feasibility of developing an integrated CCUS system across the
state. The objective of this work is not only to assess individual components of the value chain,
but to understand how capture, transportation, and storage can be coordinated as a system to
enable cost-effective, scalable deployment so that there is no carbon left behind.
To support this objective, the project integrates multiple modeling tools and datasets into a
unified analytical framework. This framework includes:
 • CO₂NCORD for characterization of emissions sources and capture costs,
 • SCO₂TPRO/GeoStratify for geologic storage potential and cost estimation,
 • CostMAPPRO for infrastructure siting and routing constraints, and
 • SimCCSPRO for system-level optimization of capture, transport, and storage networks.
A key advancement of this project is the incorporation of multi-modal transportation, allowing
CO₂ to be transported by pipeline, rail, and road within a single optimization framework. This
capability enables more realistic modeling of early-stage deployment scenarios where
infrastructure is incomplete, and volumes vary significantly across sources.
The report is organized to reflect the progression from technical inputs to system-level analysis
and deployment considerations. Early sections describe the characterization of CO₂ sources,
potential utilization pathways, transportation infrastructure, and geologic storage resources.
These inputs feed into integrated modeling analyses that evaluate multiple CCS deployment
scenarios, including phased buildout, intermodal transportation strategies, and full-scale
capture pathways. The report concludes with an assessment of non-technical barriers,
stakeholder engagement outcomes, and a roadmap for advancing CCUS deployment in
Wyoming.
These analyses provide a comprehensive assessment of how a regional carbon hub could be
developed in Wyoming, identify key drivers of cost and feasibility, and highlight the technical and
non-technical factors that will shape deployment.

2 Outreach & Engagement
As part of the Community Benefits Plan (CBP), the project initiated activities related to
community engagement, workforce development, and equitable deployment of CCUS
infrastructure. While these efforts were not fully expanded over the duration of the project, early-
stage activities provided useful insights into community priorities, engagement approaches, and
workforce development needs relevant to large-scale carbon management in Wyoming.
One key activity was the development of a framework for transparent and community-based
infrastructure siting, which identified the types of data and considerations required to support
inclusive decision-making across CCUS project components, including capture, transport, and
storage. This work emphasized the importance of integrating environmental, cultural, economic,
and land-use data into siting analyses, as well as the need for engagement processes that
reflect local priorities and improve transparency.
In addition, the project supported a pilot workshop focused on Tribal workforce development
and leadership in energy and carbon management, conducted in conjunction with broader
regional engagement activities. This workshop explored opportunities and barriers related to
workforce participation, knowledge transfer, and long-term capacity building. Discussions
highlighted the importance of trust-building, culturally appropriate engagement strategies, and
alignment between project development timelines and community decision-making processes.
Insights from these activities indicate that successful CCUS deployment in Wyoming will require
not only technical and economic feasibility but also sustained engagement with communities,
early incorporation of local priorities into project design, and investment in workforce pathways
that align with regional needs. These considerations are particularly important for infrastructure
siting and long-term project acceptance, and complement the technical analyses presented in
this report.

3 Addressing Key Technical Challenges
To support subsequent scenario development and business case analysis, early work on the
project focused on identifying and characterizing the key technical inputs required to model a
fully integrated CCUS system. These inputs span the entire value chain, including CO₂ capture
opportunities across industrial sectors, potential utilization pathways, geologic storage
resources, and the infrastructure needed to transport CO₂ between sources and sinks. Each
component was evaluated using consistent datasets and modeling tools to ensure compatibility
with downstream optimization workflows. These inputs establish the technical and economic
foundation for the scenario analyses presented later in this report, where capture, transport, and
storage systems are configured and evaluated as integrated networks.

Capture Costs
Carbon capture is typically the highest cost in CCUS projects. Estimates of costs vary widely
depending on capture technology, facility type, and capture volume, among other factors. This
project uses capture cost estimates from CO2NCORD software. CO2NCORD includes two
estimate types: literature-based and model-based costs. While this project reviewed alternative
carbon capture technologies, such as cryogenic carbon capture, calcium looping, and chemical
adsorption, amine-based absorption technology was the least expensive and most developed,
and therefore most likely to be used for near-term carbon capture at existing emitting facilities.
Both literature-based and model-based costs use amine-based absorption capture technology
for the basis of costs. The literature-based estimates provide costs for each facility based on the
facility type. These costs are primarily sourced from NETL reports and supplemented with
academic literature on capture costs.1 The model-based estimates are calculated for each
facility from a model adapted from the NETL Carbon Capture Retrofit Databases. The model
accounts for facility type, fuel combusted at the facility (where applicable), and CO2 stream size.
The model simulates amine-based capture (where stream purification is necessary), boiler
addition (where additional heat is needed for the capture system, pressurization to transport
pressure, dry or wet cooling based on project location, and state electricity and natural gas
prices.2
Capture costs per ton CO2 capture from literature costs for all facilities range from $19-74 (avg.
$58); model-based costs ranged from $32-185 (avg. $123). Table 3 summarizes the average
capture costs by industrial sector, in $/tonne CO2 captured, for both the literature and model-
based estimates. Model cost estimates are consistently higher than literature estimates; this is
driven by two factors. First, literature estimates rely on optimistic financing and energy price
assumptions. The model estimates incorporate more realistic financing assumptions (10%
interest rate, 10-year financing period) and real electricity and natural gas cost data from the
Energy Information Administration (EIA). This leads to higher capture costs for all facilities.
Second, literature estimates do not include scaling based on CO2 stream size. The model
estimates account for scaling, resulting in higher costs for facilities with smaller emission
streams.

3.2 Expanding CO2 Utilization Understanding
While most of this project’s focus was capturing CO2 by retrofitting existing industrial and power
generation plants with capture equipment, carbon utilization may provide additional uses for
CO2. To understand how and whether these should be included in business case analyses, the
project team conducted research about the viability of CO2 utilization. Ultimately, information
about EOR potential was included in one of the final business case scenarios.
Carbon dioxide utilization in Wyoming is rapidly evolving from a narrow focus on enhanced oil
recovery (EOR) into a broader, diversified portfolio of technologies that convert captured CO₂
into valuable products. Historically, CO₂-EOR has been the dominant pathway, with injection
into mature oil fields significantly boosting recovery rates and extending the life of existing
assets. The state’s favorable geology, combined with decades of operational experience, has
positioned Wyoming as a leader in this domain. At the same time, emerging utilization pathways,
such as synthetic fuels, materials, chemicals, and consumables, are expanding the role of CO₂
from a waste product to a feedstock that can support economic diversification.
In the near to mid-term, CO₂-EOR remains the most commercially viable utilization pathway,
with an estimated 2.15 billion barrels of recoverable oil across 75 fields statewide. Development
is expected to focus on major basins such as the Powder River, Wind River, and Greater Green
River, while future infrastructure expansion could unlock additional resources in the Bighorn
Basin. This opportunity is reinforced by an extensive and growing pipeline network, including the
Wyoming Pipeline Corridor Initiative, as well as a highly supportive policy environment featuring
combined federal and state incentives of up to $95 per ton of CO₂. Despite these advantages,
challenges such as high capital costs, infrastructure gaps, and sensitivity to oil price
fluctuations continue to limit widespread deployment.
Beyond EOR, Wyoming has significant potential to develop CO₂ utilization pathways in fuel
production, particularly synthetic aviation fuel (SAF). These processes rely on combining CO₂
with low-carbon hydrogen through well-established conversion pathways such as Fischer–
Tropsch synthesis. Although large-scale green hydrogen production is not yet operational in the
state, future deployment could be enabled by Wyoming’s access to water, renewable energy,
and existing industrial infrastructure. Advanced modeling tools, such as the H₂–CO₂ co-

3.3 Characterizing Multi-Modal CO2 Transportation Potential
The major work for this project was to expand the cost and routing models for modeling realistic
transportation network to transport CO2 between emissions sources and storage sites to include
multi-modal transportation (road, rail, and pipeline). For this task, as needed to first define the
types of transportation modes that are viable for transportating CO2, and then created simplified
geographic network of existing road and rail infrastructure. Below, the definition of pathways is
discussed, followed by an overview of the process required to created pipeline, road, and rail
networks for subsequent transportation models.
Combination of Transportation Modes
Intermodal transportation allows CO₂ to move across more than one type of transport network,
switching between modes on its way from the source to a storage or utilization destination.
Assigning a single mode of transport (pipeline, rail, or road) between source and destination is
relatively straightforward, but intermodal transport introduces the possibility of combining
multiple modes in a single trip, which presents technical challenges for modeling. Various
combinations of transport modes that can be considered are:

• Truck and Rail Combination: This combination would be used when the CO2 emission
 source is located far from the injection site, and direct rail access to the site is unavailable.
• Truck and Pipeline Combination: Utilized when the initial capture site is not directly
 connected to the pipeline network. The CO2 is first transported by truck to a pipeline
 terminal, where it is then injected into the pipeline for long-distance transport.
• Rail and Pipeline Combination: Effective for large-scale operations with centralized CO2
 capture facilities and distributed injection sites. The CO2 is moved by rail to a main pipeline
 hub and then distributed through pipelines to various injection locations.
• Intermodal and Tanker Combinations: Depending on the scale and distance, intermodal
 containers or tankers can be used in both truck and rail combinations to optimize cost and
 flexibility. The CO2 can be transported by rail tankers for long distances and then transferred
 to truck intermodals for the final leg to the injection site, or vice versa, depending on

Pipeline Infrastucture
The default routing option for transporting CO2 is through pipeline. This transportation option is
the most commonly employed in the United States for existing CO2 pipelines.
CostMAPPRO is a geospatial modeling tool designed to estimate the cost of routing CO₂ pipelines
within any area of interest. The tool creates two complementary surfaces: a cost surface and a
routing surface. The cost surface captures the financial impact of terrain, land cover, slope, and
other physical variables on pipeline installation. The routing surface reflects environmental,
social, and regulatory considerations that influence where pipelines may or may not be placed.
Keeping these surfaces separate is important. While the cost surface ensures accurate
estimates, the routing surface helps identify paths that avoid sensitive areas, such as critical
habitats or densely populated zones, even if those routes are not the cheapest.
To construct these surfaces, CostMAPPRO integrates various geospatial layers that fall into three
main categories: general, corridor, and barrier. General layers include variables such as slope or
population density. Corridor layers represent linear infrastructure like roads or railways that may
facilitate routing. Barrier layers represent features such as rivers or highways that are costly or
undesirable to cross. The specific geospatial layers used in this study are summarized in Table 4.
By combining these weighted inputs, the tool generates a gridded representation of the
landscape, assigning both cost and routing values to each cell.

Rail and Road Infrastructure
The intermodal data preparation process for SimCCS begins with assembling three core
transportation datasets, including highways, streets, and rail lines. These datasets are sourced
from standardized tables representing U.S. highway networks, Census-based street networks,
and North American rail systems. The overall goal is to transform these large, generalized
datasets into a refined, project-specific transportation network that captures only the
infrastructure necessary to connect selected sources and sinks for intermodal carbon transport
modeling.
The workflow starts by subsetting the data to the project’s Area of Interest (AOI) using spatial
clipping and attribute filtering. In this case, the state of Wyoming was the area of interest. This
reduces computational complexity while preserving critical connectivity. Care must be taken not
to over-filter because even seemingly minor road or rail segments may play an essential role in
maintaining network continuity. After subsetting, the next step is to quantify how far each source
and sink lies from the nearest highway, rail line, or street. This is accomplished by projecting
data into a consistent coordinate system, calculating shortest distances between points and
lines, and extracting connection points that represent where each facility would tie into the
transportation network. These distance calculations not only inform later filtering decisions but
also produce key spatial reference points used in network construction.
Using the distance results, sources and sinks are then evaluated for inclusion in the intermodal
analysis. Facilities that are too far from viable transportation infrastructure, such as those

CARBON SOLUTIONS Final Technical Report 20

beyond a generally reasonable threshold, may be excluded, depending on project requirements.
In addition to distance, the type and quality of nearby streets are also considered to ensure that
only realistic access routes are used. Following this filtering, a detailed and often manual review
process connects each remaining source and sink to the nearest appropriate highway or railway.
This involves selecting or editing street segments, creating direct connection lines, and
incrementally building a minimal but fully connected local road network. These individual
connections are later merged into a unified dataset, with duplicate geometries removed to
ensure efficiency.
Once local connectivity is established, the process shifts to identifying the critical highway and
railway segments that link all relevant connection points. Using network analysis tools such as
shortest path algorithms, the workflow traces the minimal set of highways and railways required
to connect sources and sinks across the broader region. Special attention is given to network
integrity, including the removal of problematic features such as self-looping lines that can
disrupt SimCCS graph construction. The resulting highway and rail subsets represent the
essential backbone of the intermodal system.
In the final stage, the constructed street connections, direct source/sink links, and selected
highway and rail segments are aggregated into final road and rail datasets. These are
standardized by projecting them to EPSG:4326 and saving them in shapefile format for
compatibility with SimCCS. Because network size can significantly impact simulation
performance, additional refinement may be performed on a per-scenario basis by trimming
unnecessary segments while preserving connectivity. This ensures that each SimCCS run is
both accurate and computationally efficient.

3.4 Regional Geologic Setting
Wyoming is well positioned for geologic CO₂ storage due to its extensive sedimentary basins,
thick stratigraphic sequences, and widespread occurrence of porous and permeable reservoir
formations overlain by competent sealing units. These geologic conditions, largely formed
during the Laramide orogeny, have created multiple structurally complex basins containing deep
saline formations, depleted hydrocarbon reservoirs, and coal-bearing units that are potential
targets for long-term CO₂ storage.
Major sedimentary basins in Wyoming include the Greater Green River, Wind River, Powder River,
Bighorn, Hanna, and Denver Basins (Figure 6). Each basin presents a distinct combination of
reservoir quality, structural trapping conditions, groundwater characteristics, and depth
constraints that influence its suitability for CO₂ storage. While several basins contain substantial
theoretical storage capacity, practical storage feasibility varies considerably due to the
interaction of geologic, hydrogeologic, and regulatory factors.

Figure 6. Map of Wyoming showing the major sedimentary basins, fault zones, coal beds, oil and gas

Effective geologic storage requires the presence of reservoir formations with sufficient porosity
and permeability, overlain by laterally continuous sealing units capable of preventing upward
migration of buoyant CO₂. In addition, storage formations must occur at depths greater than
approximately 800 meters to maintain CO₂ in a supercritical state, while avoiding excessive
injection costs associated with very deep reservoirs. Structural and stratigraphic trapping
mechanisms are also critical, particularly in Wyoming’s Laramide basins, where basin geometry
can promote lateral and up-dip migration in the absence of structural closure.
Basin-Scale Storage Characteristics
Screening results from this study, supported by the GeoStratify framework described above,
indicate that the Greater Green River Basin represents the most favorable region for large-scale
saline CO₂ storage. Structurally closed features such as the Rock Springs Uplift and Moxa Arch
provide effective trapping geometries, supported by multiple deep saline reservoirs and
demonstrated long-term fluid containment.
The Wind River Basin contains several formations with high theoretical storage potential and
favorable reservoir properties, including thick, porous sandstones. However, the lack of large
structural closures in many areas increases the risk of up-dip migration, limiting its suitability for
large-scale saline storage despite favorable injectivity in some locations.
The Powder River Basin has extensive subsurface data and significant theoretical storage
capacity, but widespread groundwater use and limited structural trapping make it more suitable
for enhanced coal bed methane (ECBM) or storage in depleted reservoirs rather than regional-
scale saline storage.

Hydrogeologic and Regulatory Constraints
Although Wyoming contains significant theoretical CO₂ storage capacity, practical storage
feasibility is strongly influenced by groundwater protection requirements and hydrogeologic
conditions. Under the Safe Drinking Water Act, CO₂ injection is generally restricted to formations
containing brines with total dissolved solids (TDS) greater than 10,000 mg/L, excluding
underground sources of drinking water (USDWs) from consideration.
Groundwater salinity varies significantly across Wyoming basins and is not strictly a function of
depth. In many cases, relatively fresh groundwater extends into deeper formations due to
recharge from basin margins, particularly in uplifted areas. As a result, some formations with

CARBON SOLUTIONS Final Technical Report 23

otherwise favorable reservoir properties, such as the Tensleep Sandstone and Madison
Limestone, are partially or wholly constrained by groundwater protection requirements.
Screening results indicate that approximately 60,891 Mt CO₂ of modeled storage potential
overlaps with formations below USDW salinity thresholds, limiting their viability for CO₂
injection. In addition, constraints related to protected lands, critical habitats, and proximity to
fault zones exclude an additional 171,858 Mt CO₂ of potential storage resources. These factors
affect more than 32% of modeled statewide storage capacity, substantially reshaping the spatial
distribution of viable storage resources (Figure 7).

Implications for Storage Deployment
These results demonstrate that geologic storage potential in Wyoming is highly heterogeneous
and controlled by the interaction of reservoir quality, basin structure, groundwater conditions,
and land-use constraints. Basins with the largest theoretical storage volumes are not always the
most suitable for commercial deployment, and storage suitability can vary significantly within a
single formation depending on location.
In particular, the presence of structural closure emerges as a key factor in identifying favorable
storage sites within Wyoming’s Laramide basins. Structurally closed regions with suitable depth,
reservoir properties, and saline groundwater conditions, such as those identified in the Greater
Green River Basin, offer the most promising opportunities for large-scale, long-term CO₂
storage.
Accordingly, the storage resources incorporated into subsequent scenario modeling prioritize
regions that satisfy these combined geologic and regulatory criteria, ensuring that the integrated
CCUS system evaluated in this report reflects realistically developable storage opportunities.

4.2 Basin Characterization
To support risk evaluation using the NRAP Open-IAM framework, geologic and engineering inputs
were compiled and integrated into a consistent modeling workflow for the Greater Green River,
Powder River, and Wind River Basin. Structural surfaces for key stratigraphic units, including
storage reservoirs, confining intervals, and underground sources of drinking water, were used to
construct three-dimensional domains in the LaGrit meshing environment (Figure 8Figure 9).
These domains were refined to balance geologic resolution and computational efficiency and
subsequently prepared for simulation within the FEHM reservoir model. Injection scenarios,
including well locations, rates, and operational timelines, were incorporated, along with
representations of potential leakage pathways. One leakage pathway that best captured each
basin’s unique history was identified to be modeled, such as legacy wells (Powder River Basin),
faults (Greater Green River Basin), and uncemented wells (Wind River). The resulting models
provided the necessary inputs, such as pressure, CO₂ saturation, and reservoir properties, that
are required by the NRAP Open-IAM system model to assess leakage behavior, quantify
uncertainty through multiple realizations, and delineate Areas of Review around injection
operations.

Figure 8. Stratigraphic units, by basin, used by NRAP.

CARBON SOLUTIONS Final Technical Report 25

Figure 9. Mesh generation of stratigraphic units used in NRAP, by basin.

The modeling workflow integrated mesh generation, reservoir simulation, and system-level risk
assessment in a repeatable framework (Figure 10). Three-dimensional meshes containing
approximately 1–2 million nodes were generated in LaGrit, with local refinement around
injection wells to accurately capture near-well pressure and saturation gradients. These meshes
were then used as inputs to FEHM, where multiphase flow simulations were conducted over an
80-year period, including 30 years of injection (at rates on the order of 1 million metric tons per
year per well) followed by a 50-year post-injection monitoring phase. To represent subsurface
uncertainty, heterogeneous porosity fields were derived from well log data and used to generate
corresponding permeability distributions via the Kozeny–Carman relationship, with anisotropic
vertical permeability incorporated to reflect stratigraphic layering. Multiple stochastic
realizations (on the order of 100–400 per model) were generated and simulated in parallel to
capture the range of possible reservoir behaviors. For each realization, pressure and CO₂
saturation outputs at the top of the reservoir were passed to the NRAP Open-IAM framework,
where they drive leakage calculations through potential pathways, including legacy wellbores
and faults. The resulting leakage rates and cumulative mass fluxes were aggregated across
realizations to quantify uncertainty and delineate Areas of Review, defined by the spatial extent
over which leakage risk exceeds threshold conditions.

Comparative Reservoir Modeling Across Basins
Reservoir simulations across the Wind River Basin (WRB), Powder River Basin (PRB), and Greater
Green River Basin (GGRB) follow a consistent workflow but reflect basin-specific geologic
structure, injection strategy, and leakage characterization. In all cases, FEHM is used to simulate
coupled multiphase flow and transport of supercritical CO₂ and brine within storage formations,
with model domains initialized using a hydrostatic pressure distribution (10 MPa surface
pressure with depth-dependent gradient) (Figure 11). A long-duration initialization run (10,000
days) establishes stable pre-injection conditions, which are then used as the starting point for
all stochastic realizations.

Reservoir Simulation Framework and Uncertainty Representation
Across WRB and PRB, ensembles of ~100 realizations are used to represent geologic uncertainty
through heterogeneous porosity and permeability fields derived from well data and
petrophysical relationships. These realizations are simulated under consistent injection
schedules, enabling statistical characterization of plume evolution, pressure propagation, and
associated risk metrics. Simulation outputs, specifically pressure and CO₂ saturation fields, are
extracted at key stratigraphic surfaces and passed to the NRAP Open-IAM framework for leakage
and risk evaluation.
The WRB model incorporates injection into two stacked reservoirs (Meeteetse and Mesaverde),
with three injection sites producing six distinct plumes within each realization (Figure 12).
Injection is conducted at a total rate of 1 Mt/yr per well over a 30-year period, followed by an
extended 70-year post-injection phase to evaluate long-term system response. Open lateral
boundary conditions allow pressure dissipation through brine outflow, supporting realistic
pressure evolution. In contrast, the PRB model focuses on four injection sites within a single
primary storage interval, resulting in fewer but laterally interacting plumes. Both basins
emphasize plume growth and pressure interference patterns as primary drivers of risk.

5 Evaluating Regional Infrastructure
5.1 Models & methods
This analysis is built on an integrated modeling framework that connects CO₂ capture
opportunities, transportation networks, and geologic storage capacity into a unified system for
evaluating regional CCUS infrastructure. Rather than treating these components independently,
the framework links multiple tools and datasets to represent the full CO₂ value chain, enabling
scenario-based analysis of infrastructure development, cost, and system performance.
At the core of this framework is the use of SimCCSPRO as a system-level optimization tool,
supported by domain-specific models that define inputs to the system. These include
CO₂NCORD for emissions and capture costs, SCO₂TPRO for geologic storage potential and costs,
and CostMAPPRO for transportation routing and siting constraints. These tools provide consistent,

CARBON SOLUTIONS Final Technical Report 42

spatially resolved inputs that allow infrastructure to be designed and evaluated as an
interconnected network.
A key advancement developed in this project is the extension of the SimCCSPRO framework to
support multi-modal transportation, enabling CO₂ to be routed across pipelines, rail, and road
networks within a single optimization model. This formulation represents a departure from
traditional pipeline-only CCS network models and allows for more realistic evaluation of early-
stage deployment scenarios, where infrastructure is incomplete and smaller-volume sources
may rely on alternative transport modes. The model incorporates constraints on mode
transitions, network connectivity, and cost structures, ensuring that resulting configurations
reflect physically and economically feasible transportation pathways.
Within this framework:
 • CO₂NCORD defines where CO₂ is available and at what cost to capture.
 • SCO₂TPRO defines where CO₂ can be stored and at what cost.
 • CostMAPPRO defines where infrastructure can be built and at what cost.
 • SimCCSPRO integrates these inputs to identify optimal system configurations.
This integrated approach enables consistent comparison across scenarios by ensuring that all
results are derived from a shared set of assumptions, input data, and modeling constraints.
The development of this multi-modal capability builds directly on the transportation networks
defined in Task 3.2, including pipeline routing cost surfaces and simplified road and rail
networks. Building on this foundation, the project quantified both the capacity and cost
characteristics associated with each transport mode, enabling their inclusion within a unified
optimization framework.
A review of the literature and supporting cost modeling demonstrates that the relative cost-
effectiveness of CO₂ transportation modes depends strongly on both transported volume and
transport distance. At smaller scales, truck-based transport provides flexibility and avoids the
need for dedicated infrastructure, making it economically viable for low-volume sources.
Results indicate that truck transport is generally suitable for volumes below approximately 1
MtCO₂ per year and for distances on the order of 10 to 600 km. Outside of this range, costs
increase rapidly, limiting its applicability for large-scale deployment.
Rail transport extends the feasible operating window to longer distances and moderate volumes,
benefiting from economies of scale in bulk transport. Prior work indicates that rail becomes the
preferred option for volumes below approximately 2 MtCO₂ per year when transport distances
exceed ~600 km.19 However, rail transport introduces operational constraints, including
scheduling requirements and the need for specialized terminal infrastructure.
For large-scale, long-term deployment, pipelines remain the most cost-effective transport
mode, particularly as transported volumes increase. NETL data show that large-capacity trunk
pipelines can achieve costs on the order of $0.02 per ton-mile, although costs increase
significantly at lower volumes (e.g., ~2 MtCO₂ per year), where per-unit costs approach $0.07 per
ton-mile. In this lower volume range, the cost gap between pipeline and rail narrows
substantially, reinforcing the importance of context-specific transport decisions.20
The overlap in cost ranges between pipeline and rail at moderate volumes highlights the need for
flexible, scenario-specific evaluation of transport strategies. In particular, rail transport may

CARBON SOLUTIONS Final Technical Report 43

provide a viable alternative where pipeline development is constrained by permitting challenges,
capital requirements, or social acceptance considerations.
These results indicate that no single transportation mode is optimal across all conditions.
Instead, transport decisions are governed by the interaction between scale, distance, and
infrastructure availability. Incorporating multiple transportation modes within a unified modeling
framework enables the design of CO₂ transport networks that are both cost-effective and
adaptable to varying deployment conditions.

5.2 CCS Scenarios, Regional Infrastructure & Business Case
 Development
The integrated modeling framework described above was applied to evaluate multiple CCS
deployment scenarios across Wyoming, each designed to explore different infrastructure
strategies, transportation configurations, and system scales. These scenarios provide insight
into how CCS infrastructure may develop over time, how costs evolve with scale, and how
different technological and logistical constraints influence system design.
The deployment of Carbon Capture, Utilization, and Storage (CCUS) at scale presents both
technical and economic opportunities, including supporting enhanced oil recovery (EOR),
reducing industrial emissions, and enabling future low-carbon energy systems such as hydrogen
and CO₂-derived products. This section translates the technical inputs developed earlier in the
report into a set of business case analyses designed to evaluate how CCUS infrastructure could
be deployed across Wyoming.
This analysis integrates techno-economic modeling, geospatial routing optimization, and
workforce impact assessment to evaluate infrastructure costs, system configurations, and
regional impacts under multiple deployment scenarios. These scenarios, developed with project
and commercial partners, include phased infrastructure buildout, intermodal transportation
strategies, and full-scale capture pathways designed to avoid stranded emissions.
To support decision-making, results are presented in terms of total system costs, unit costs,
infrastructure requirements, and workforce impacts. In addition, a CCS Readiness Index is
introduced to provide a county-level assessment of development potential based on capture
opportunities, transportation feasibility, and storage availability. These outputs provide a
consistent framework for comparing alternative deployment strategies and identifying pathways
for scalable, economically viable CCUS development.
The business case analyses in this section are built on the integrated modeling framework
developed in earlier sections. Capture opportunities, storage resources, and transportation
networks are combined within an optimization environment to identify cost-effective system
configurations under different deployment assumptions. Rather than evaluating individual
components in isolation, these models assess how the full CCUS value chain performs as a
connected system.
5.2.1 Phase Infrastructure Buildout
To better represent the buildout of infrastructure in the state of Wyoming, a scenario was
developed using a two-phase implementation scheme. The first phase of deployment involved
capturing, transporting, and sequestering 15 MtCO₂. For this initial phase, referred to as Phase
1, a 10-year financing period at 10% interest (CRF = 0.1627) and a 30-year injection period were
applied. CO₂ source facilities were filtered to include only a single unit per facility, and the
lowest-cost sequestration formations identified by SCO₂T modeling were utilized.
Phase 2 introduced an additional 15 MtCO₂ for capture, transport, and storage. This phase was
modeled to begin in year 5 with a 10-year financing period and a 25-year injection period,
resulting in a total project length of 30 years. Phase 2 leveraged the infrastructure established
during Phase 1, allowing pipeline routes from Phase 1 to serve Phase 2 needs. In some cases,
pipeline diameters were intentionally oversized during Phase 1 to accommodate the increased
CO₂ volume anticipated in Phase 2.

CARBON SOLUTIONS Final Technical Report 45

SimCCSPRO was employed to optimize results based on reaching user-specified capture targets
for Phase 1. The model determined the optimal sources, pipeline routes, and storage sinks to
minimize cumulative deployment costs for 15 MtCO₂. These selections were then fixed for Phase
2, which optimized deployment for a total of 30 MtCO₂ by identifying additional sources, sinks,
and pipelines to complement Phase 1 infrastructure. Post-processing calculations were
performed to retroactively increase pipeline diameters where necessary, and additional
adjustments ensured accurate financing for overlapping infrastructure components.
Optimization results & economic impacts
Highlights from optimization models are reported in Table 9. The analysis had consistent
financial assumptions, including a 10% interest rate, a capital recovery factor (CRF) of 0.1627,
and costs reported in 2024 dollars. The project was structured into two major phases, followed
by a long-term operational period, to optimize infrastructure utilization and minimize overall
costs. The results are presented in 4 time periods. Period 1 (years 1-5) is the period in which
CAPEX and OPEX expenses are paid for Phase 1, and Period 2 (years 6-10) is the period in which
both CAPEX and OPEX are paid for both Phases. Period 3 (years 11-15) is after capital has been
paid off for Phase 1, but OPEX is being paid for both phases in addition to CAPEX for Phase 2.
The final period (years 16-30) has only OPEX expenses being paid.
For either Phase, the primary industry that is deployed by the model is coal, largely driven by the
cheaper cost of capture relative to other emissions sources. As shown in Table 16, the cost of
capture is 30x more expensive than the cost of transport and often 10x more expensive than
storage costs. Therefore, 10-20% differences in the cost of capturing CO2 can often account for
more than the storage and transport costs combined.
During the first five years, the system captures and stores approximately 15 million tonnes of
CO₂ annually from seven sources into six sinks, supported by 195 kilometers of pipeline. This
initial phase represents the most capital-intensive period, where the majority of the
infrastructure foundational for both Phases is being constructed. In the second phase, beginning
in year six, the system expands to capture and store 30 million tonnes of CO₂ per year. This
expansion requires six additional sources and four additional sinks, and extends the pipeline
network to 247 kilometers. Though this phase experiences the highest annual costs due to
overlapping capital recovery and operational expenses.
After ten years, when capital recovery obligations are complete, annual costs decline sharply
even as throughput remains constant at 30 million tonnes per year. This cost reduction is driven
by infrastructure reuse and the elimination of financing burdens. By years 16 through 30, the
project operates at a fraction of its initial cost, demonstrating the long-term economic
advantage of phased deployment.
Cost behavior over time reflects these dynamics. Total annual costs peak at approximately
$3.36 billion during the expansion phase, then fall to about $745 million in the final period.
Capture costs dominate overall expenditure, starting at $1.46 billion per year, rising to $3.0
billion during expansion, and declining to $635 million in later years. Transport and storage costs
follow similar trends but remain comparatively minor contributors.
These results highlight several key drivers of cost reduction: economies of scale achieved
through increased throughput, completion of capital expenditures after year ten, and strategic
reuse of infrastructure between phases. The findings emphasize the importance of long-term
planning and phased implementation for CCS projects. While the early years exhibit high costs
due to infrastructure development and financing, the later years achieve substantial cost

reductions, thereby improving the competitiveness of CCS as a decarbonization strategy. This
analysis underscores the need for supportive financial mechanisms and policy frameworks to
bridge early-phase cost barriers and enable sustained operations.

5.2.2 Intermodal Transportation Scenarios
While SimCCSPRO typically models costs associated with the capture, transport, and geologic
storage of CO2 via pipelines, identifying those facilities that may benefit from rail or road can be
important for a number of reasons. First, approval for pipeline construction necessarily takes
large investments and long planning periods. Some facilities may have capturable CO2 earlier
than a pipeline is available to transport it to a utilization or storage site. Utilizing truck or rail
transportation could allow CO2 source facilities to reach the market and achieve revenue before
final pipeline construction is complete. Second, in some industries, the technology available to
capture CO2 may not be at the volume required to build a pipeline but might still support
commercial sales of CO2 as a product, allowing some revenue to be generated while additional
capture technology is made available. Finally, some sectors or facilities may be interested in
capturing CO2 as early adopters due to financial incentives or company goals and priorities. In
each of these cases, understanding how and when rail, road, and pipeline may be deployed, and
at what cost, is important to planning how a carbon management hub may develop over time.
Understanding when these alternate modes may be more economically advantageous than a
pipeline is the second business case evaluated for this project.
Modeling when rail, road, or pipelines will be deployed as economically optimal routing
solutions for transporting CO₂ required developing cost estimates for each transportation
pathway, sub-setting existing road and rail infrastructure to include the most likely options
connecting CO₂ facilities to storage and utilization locations, and adapting SimCCS PRO to create
a network of transportation routes that only allow realistic transitions between trucks, rail, and
pipelines. Although these mixed-mode cases represent higher costs compared to pipeline-only
scenarios, they reflect transportation strategies that may be necessary for individual CO₂
sources until pipelines are constructed or until CO₂ capture occurs at scale.
To explore the financial and routing consequences of expanding CO2 transportation modes to
include pipelines, railroads, and roads, we utilize SimCCSPRO to select which combination of
transportation options resulted in the lowest cost source-sink solution.
Sources

CARBON SOLUTIONS Final Technical Report 49

For the Intermodal transportation option business model, we filtered all Wyoming CO2 to include
only those within 1 km of existing highways or railways and under 1 MtCO 2/yr in capturable
emissions, resulting in forty-two facilities which were individually modeled using the intermodal
transportation model. That is, for each source facility, SimCCSPRO was run to allow the model to
select which transportation modes were most cost-effective. For large emissions, pipelines are
far more efficient and cost-effective. For smaller amounts of emissions, however, intermodal
options may be preferable, which is why we imposed the 1 MtCO2 threshold on source facilities.
Sinks
In these scenarios, storage sites were located in existing enhanced oil recovery (EOR) fields, an
arrangement that would increase the local supply of CO2 for EOR while offsetting the costs of
capturing CO2 with tax credits. To determine the location of the storage sites, EOR fields, existing
railroads, and existing major roads were combined in GIS to determine point locations in EOR
fields that were near rail and road. Each sink was assigned the same storage potential and cost,
ensuring storage potential did not limit which sources were routed there. Because these
facilities each had low volumes relative to coal plants, it is not unrealistic to assume all
emissions could be routed to one storage field. Each storage site was also given a uniform cost,
ensuring all sources could route to any storage location based on transportation and capture
costs, not storage costs.
Routing
To ensure realistic road and rail connections between sources and storage sites, additional data
preparation steps were required. First, a subset of roads and railroads was selected to minimize
run-time for the optimization model. It is assumed that major highways are the main
thoroughfare for trucks, and existing railroads in the area are capable of transporting CO2. The
intermediary transport between a source or a sink to a nearby highway or railroad is assumed to
be done by trucking it along local streets. A table summarizing the costs of transport by mode of
transportation and distance is in Table 11.
The local streets are extracted from the highly detailed TIGER/Line shapefiles provided by the
U.S. Census Bureau to best represent the actual path a truck may take during transportation.
These are selected by a reviewer identifying streets that form a path from a given source or sink
to the nearest potential highway or railway transfer point. The street network is complex enough
for this manual review to be more efficient than attempting to automate this process. There are
topology errors present in the U.S. Census Bureau street data that also make the task of
automating local street selection ineffective.
Once local streets that connect the sources and sinks to highways or railways have been
determined, the input highway and railway datasets are further trimmed based on the
connection points determined by the local streets. The endpoint of this process represents the
potential critical path through streets, highways, and railroads that would be taken in an
intermodal transport scenario. This has the benefit of producing intermodal results that are
realistic to the actual road network.
Two different models were run for each CO2 facility: those with the default cost and routing
surface with the trimmed rail and road network, and those with a routing surface meant to
prioritize the located pipeline within the WPCI corridor, in addition to roads and rail.

network. Under these conditions, truck transportation becomes a competitive alternative,
particularly when no prior dedicated infrastructure exists and when individual volumes do not
justify economies of scale associated with pipelines. This behavior explains why, in the model
solution, most source–well connections rely on the existing road network, avoiding investments in

transport to enable small- and medium-scale CO₂ capture and storage schemes, particularly in
contexts where sources are dispersed, and individual volumes do not justify dedicated transport
solutions.
Note on the Localized Use of Pipelines in the Intermodal Solution
Although the model solution clearly prioritizes road transportation, Figure 38 shows some short
pipeline segments directly connected to certain emission sources. It is important to clarify that
these segments should not necessarily be interpreted as dedicated infrastructure that would be
built in practice, but rather as a consequence of the model’s spatial representation process.
Specifically, these segments appear in situations where the modeled road network does not reach
the exact point at which the emission source is located. In such cases, SimCCSPRO completes the
connection between the source and the nearest road node through a pipeline segment, ensuring
network connectivity and solution feasibility. From an operational standpoint, what would be
expected in a real project is that the truck departs directly from the emission source, without the
need to construct a pipeline to cover such a short distance. Therefore, these segments should be
interpreted as artificial connector elements that close the network in the model, rather than as an
explicit recommendation to invest in pipeline infrastructure for those cases.
This clarification is relevant to avoid overinterpreting the role of pipelines in scenarios dominated by
road transport and reinforces the interpretation that the observed pattern primarily reflects truck-
based logistics, supported by the existing road infrastructure.
Financial Considerations for Road-Based Truck Transportation
The analysis of road-based CO₂ transportation incorporated in the intermodal model relies on an
explicit decomposition of capital (CAPEX) and operational (OPEX) costs, consistent with
approaches widely used in the specialized literature. For CAPEX, the main components considered
include: trucks, tanker trailers or intermodal containers, liquefaction, reconditioning, and buffer
storage. OPEX includes costs associated with fuel, operating personnel salaries, maintenance,
permits, insurance, and other regulatory fees.
The cost framework used is based on the Multimodal CO₂ Transportation Cost Model (MuMo-
CoCo), developed by Myers (2024) and applied in Myers et al. (2024).19 MuMo-CoCo is an Excel-
based model that estimates the capital, operational, and financial costs of CO₂ transportation
across different modes, primarily as a function of transported volume, distance, and selected

transport mode. The model also allows for the incorporation of assumptions regarding operating
and construction periods, providing a consistent basis for comparing intermodal alternatives under
different scenarios.

5.2.3 Full Capture/No-Stranded-Assets Scenario
One of the project goals for the Carbon Blueprint is to understand what infrastructure may be
required to capture all CO2 in Wyoming to leave no carbon behind. To this end, one business case
explores how infrastructure and costs change as the amount of CO2 to be captured increases.

Sources
The input data are the universe of all facilities reporting emissions to GHGRP and EGRID. The costs
are based on NETL and are uniform for each sector, and the emissions are, when possible, ramped-
up to an 85% capacity factor. These are the same assumptions used for the previous facilities,
although more emitting facilities are modeled in this case. The total of all ramped-up emissions in

the state is 42MtCO2/yr. Four scenarios were run, with capture targets at 10 Mt CO2/yr, 20Mt CO2/yr,
30 Mt CO2/yr, and 42.3 Mt CO2/yr.

Sinks
Storage locations are the least cost formation anywhere in Wyoming. Their locations are given to be

Only pipelines are considered as a transportation option. Two different models were run for each
increasing CO2 target: those with the default cost and routing surface, and those with a routing

 remain relatively small compared to capture but increase sharply with scale, from $17.7 million at
 25% to $270 million at 100%. Storage costs also grow, from $82 million to over $408 million

 Unit costs per ton of CO₂ remain relatively stable across scenarios, ranging from $106.95/tCO₂ at

 transport costs per ton rise more noticeably at full capture due to extensive pipeline requirements.
 Storage costs per ton remain in the $7.9–$9.6 range.
 The analysis shows that economies of scale are limited, and that total costs rise almost linearly
 with emissions, and unit costs increase modestly at higher capture levels. The largest cost driver is
 capture, followed by storage, with transport costs becoming significant only at full-scale
 deployment. Because there is so much storage available in Wyoming, even when the WPCI corridor
 is weighted to be the preferred path for pipelines, few scenarios take advantage of this route. As a
 result, economies of scale for pipelines along the WPCI are unlikely to provide economic benefits
 without other incentives for building a long pipeline.

 Conclusion
 These scenarios illustrate how CCS infrastructure development in Wyoming is governed by three
 primary factors: the spatial distribution of emissions and storage resources, the scale-dependent
 economics of capture and transport, and the availability of supporting infrastructure. While
 individual scenarios emphasize different aspects of system behavior, they consistently
 demonstrate that capture costs dominate overall economics, that transportation strategies evolve
 with scale, and that infrastructure reuse is critical to long-term cost reduction.

 6 Promoting Regional Technology Transfer
 6.1 Non-technical Barriers to Deployment
 Wyoming is widely regarded as one of the most CCS-ready jurisdictions in the United States,
 supported by a mature regulatory framework that addresses pore space ownership, long-term
 liability, and state primacy for Class VI wells. These strengths provide a strong foundation for CCUS
 deployment. However, successful infrastructure development depends on a broader set of non-
 technical factors that influence project timelines, costs, and community acceptance.
 Regulatory and Permitting Complexity
 CCUS projects must navigate a multi-layered regulatory landscape involving federal, state, and
 local agencies. Key processes include federal environmental review (e.g., NEPA), Class VI well

 CARBON SOLUTIONS Final Technical Report 62

permitting, industrial siting approvals, and pipeline rights-of-way, each with distinct timelines and
data requirements. While Wyoming’s Class VI primacy offers a streamlined pathway relative to
other states, overall project timelines remain sensitive to coordination across agencies, data
completeness, and public review processes. As a result, permitting, not engineering, often
becomes the critical path for project delivery.
Transportation Policy and Infrastructure Constraints
Although Wyoming provides a relatively favorable legal environment for CO₂ pipeline development,
questions remain regarding long-term infrastructure models, particularly around open-access or
common-carrier pipelines. Current regulations largely allow private pipeline systems, which may
limit the development of shared regional infrastructure without additional policy or market
incentives. This has implications for large-scale network buildout, especially where coordinated
multi-user systems would improve economic efficiency.
Community Acceptance and Social License
Public acceptance of CCS in Wyoming remains broadly supportive but conditional. Survey data
indicate that residents are increasingly familiar with CCS technologies, but support depends on a
clear demonstration of economic benefits, alignment with community values, and protection of
environmental and cultural resources. Concerns related to safety, long-term storage, and impacts
to local communities remain important considerations. Early engagement, transparent
communication, and incorporation of community-level data into siting decisions are therefore
essential to project success.
Workforce and Supply Chain Constraints
Wyoming’s existing energy workforce provides a strong foundation for CCS development,
particularly in areas such as drilling, pipeline operations, and subsurface management. However,
large-scale deployment will require additional specialized expertise, including CO₂-specific
pipeline engineering, geologic modeling, and monitoring and verification. At the same time,
national supply chains for key materials and equipment, such as pipelines, compressors, and
capture systems, face increasing demand from multiple energy transition sectors. These
constraints may introduce delays, cost variability, and competition for resources.
System Integration and Scheduling Risks
CCUS deployment depends on the coordination of multiple interdependent components, including
capture facilities, transport infrastructure, and storage sites. Delays in any part of the system, such
as Class VI permitting, pipeline construction, or storage characterization, can propagate across the
entire project. As a result, project timelines are often driven by the slowest component, reinforcing
the importance of integrated planning and early-stage risk assessment.

To support deployment beyond technical analysis, this project translated the results of the
Wyoming Carbon Blueprint into a set of stakeholder-facing materials designed to accelerate
technology transfer and decision-making. While the Blueprint provides a comprehensive technical
and economic assessment of CCS deployment at the statewide scale, additional efforts focused
on making this information accessible, actionable, and usable for policymakers, developers, and
local stakeholders.

The Wyoming Carbon Blueprint serves as the central synthesis of this work, integrating capture,
transport, storage, utilization, regulatory, and economic considerations into a unified vision for

infrastructure development with Wyoming’s industrial base, subsurface resources, and policy
environment.

6.3 CCS Leadership
In addition to technical analysis and roadmap development, this project supported regional
technology transfer through direct engagement with planners, policymakers, industry
representatives, academic institutions, and community stakeholders. These activities provided
opportunities to present findings from the Wyoming Carbon Blueprint, gather feedback, and
identify real-world barriers to CCS deployment.
Project outputs were shared through a combination of workshops and conference presentations,
reaching both regional and national audiences. Three workshops were conducted in Wyoming,
including the Western Planner Conference (Cheyenne, August 2024), the Tribal Energy Summit
(Laramie, October 2024), and the State Trust Land Forum (Laramie, April 2025). These events
engaged planners from across the western United States, Tribal stakeholders, and state and local
decision-makers responsible for land and resource management.
Key themes emerging from these workshops highlight several non-technical challenges to CCS
deployment. Participants at the Western Planner Conference noted a lack of planning frameworks

CARBON SOLUTIONS Final Technical Report 65

and siting guidance specific to CCS, despite familiarity with renewable energy infrastructure.
Engagement with Tribal stakeholders emphasized that energy sovereignty and existing oil and gas
development priorities currently take precedence over CCS adoption, indicating the need for
tailored outreach and long-term partnership development. Discussions at the State Trust Land
Forum underscored the importance of minimizing financial risk and ensuring long-term land-use
flexibility in CCS-related leasing decisions.
Additional presentations were delivered at national and international venues, including the
University of Oklahoma Sustainability Forum (January 2025), the Greenhouse Gas Control
Technologies Conference (GHGT-17, Calgary, October 2025), and the Southern States Energy
Board/University of Houston CCUS Consortium meeting (July 2025). These forums brought together
industry, academic, and commercial stakeholders across the CCS value chain.
Engagement at these events further confirmed that coordination between CO₂ emitters and storage
providers remains a critical challenge, particularly in the absence of established infrastructure and
clear commercial arrangements. Stakeholders also expressed significant interest in shared pipeline
systems, while noting that permitting and siting barriers continue to constrain infrastructure
development. At the international level, discussions highlighted the strong position of the United
States in CCS research and deployment, alongside growing global interest in carbon management
technologies to support emissions reduction goals.
These leadership and engagement activities reinforced the findings of this report by identifying
consistent themes across stakeholder groups, including the need for clearer siting frameworks,
coordinated infrastructure planning, and alignment between economic, regulatory, and community
priorities. These insights directly inform the non-technical considerations outlined in Section 6.1
and highlight the importance of continued engagement to advance CCS deployment in Wyoming.

7 Project Conclusions and Recommended Next Steps
Key Findings
This project demonstrates that Wyoming has strong technical potential to support the development
of a large-scale carbon capture, utilization, and storage (CCUS) hub. Key findings include:
 • Wyoming has significant capturable emissions concentrated in a small number of
 sectors. Coal-fired power plants dominate total emissions, creating opportunities for large-
 volume capture with relatively favorable economies of scale.

CARBON SOLUTIONS Final Technical Report 66

 • Capture costs are the primary driver of total system economics. Across all scenarios,
 capture accounts for the majority of total cost, often exceeding transportation and storage
 costs combined. Variations in capture cost assumptions significantly influence overall
 system feasibility.
 • Wyoming contains substantial geologic storage resources, but practical availability is
 constrained. Hydrogeologic conditions, groundwater protection requirements, land-use
 restrictions, and structural considerations reduce the portion of theoretical storage
 capacity that is practically viable, affecting more than 30% of modeled resources.
 • Transportation strategies are highly scale-dependent. Pipelines represent the lowest-
 cost option at large scale, but road and rail transportation play an important role in enabling
 early deployment and connecting smaller, distributed emission sources.
 • Infrastructure reuse and phased deployment significantly reduce long-term costs.
 Scenario analysis shows that unit costs decline substantially after capital recovery,
 emphasizing the importance of long-term planning and coordinated buildout.
 • CCUS deployment is influenced by non-technical constraints as much as technical
 ones. Regulatory complexity, permitting timelines, infrastructure ownership models,
 workforce capacity, and community acceptance all play critical roles in determining project
 feasibility.
 • Risk associated with CO₂ storage is spatially localized and manageable. Integrated
 NRAP modeling indicates that leakage risks are concentrated in specific pathways (e.g.,
 legacy wells or faults), suggesting that targeted monitoring and mitigation strategies can be
 effective.
Implications for CCUS Deployment
The results of this analysis highlight that the successful development of a carbon hub in Wyoming
will depend on coordinated planning across the full CCUS value chain. While the state is well-
positioned technically, deployment will require alignment between emissions sources, storage
resources, and transportation infrastructure.
Early deployment is likely to rely on flexible, intermodal transportation strategies, particularly for
smaller sources or in regions where pipeline infrastructure is not yet available. Over time, these
systems are expected to transition toward pipeline-dominated networks as volumes increase and
infrastructure is developed.
Geologic analysis indicates that site selection is critical, with structurally favorable regions and
appropriate hydrogeologic conditions providing the most viable storage opportunities. Storage
availability is not uniform across the state, and localized constraints must be considered in
planning.
Recommendations
Based on the findings of this project, several key actions are recommended to support CCUS
deployment in Wyoming:
1. Prioritize capture cost reduction strategies

CARBON SOLUTIONS Final Technical Report 67

Because capture costs dominate system economics, efforts to improve capture technology
performance, reduce energy penalties, and provide financial support during early deployment will
have the greatest impact on overall feasibility.
2. Support phased and coordinated infrastructure development
Phased deployment strategies that allow infrastructure to be reused and expanded over time can
significantly reduce long-term costs. Planning should emphasize shared infrastructure corridors
and system-level coordination.
3. Enable flexible transportation pathways
Policies and investment strategies should support multiple transportation modes, including road
and rail, to enable early deployment and reduce barriers for smaller sources. Over time, these
systems can evolve into larger pipeline networks.
4. Advance geologic characterization and permitting
Additional data collection, site characterization, and regulatory clarity will help reduce uncertainty
and accelerate storage project development, particularly related to groundwater protection and
Class VI permitting.
5. Address non-technical barriers early in project development
Permitting, regulatory coordination, and infrastructure siting processes should be addressed
proactively. Early-stage planning should also account for workforce availability and supply chain
constraints.
6. Expand community engagement and transparent siting practices
Successful deployment will require sustained engagement with communities, incorporation of
local priorities, and transparent decision-making processes. Tools that integrate environmental,
cultural, and economic data into siting decisions should be further developed and applied.
7. Continue development of integrated modeling and planning tools
The integrated framework developed in this project provides a strong foundation for future analysis.
Continued refinement of multi-modal transportation modeling, storage screening, and system
optimization will support more detailed project planning and investment decisions.
Final Perspective
This project demonstrates that Wyoming has the technical foundation to become a leading region
for CCUS deployment in the United States. However, realizing this potential will require coordinated
action across technology development, infrastructure investment, regulatory frameworks, and
stakeholder engagement.
By integrating technical analysis with practical deployment considerations, this report provides a
roadmap for advancing CCUS in Wyoming and offers insights that can be applied to other regions
pursuing large-scale carbon management systems.
