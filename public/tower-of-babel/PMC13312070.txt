A qualitative interview study of acute care surgeons' approach to literature acquisition: Are you up to date on staying up to date?
Authors: Jared M Wohlgemut, Trent Seltzer, Aubree Herman, Brittany K Bankhead, AAST AM Qualitative Research Group
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAcute Care Surgery (ACS) surgeons care for a wide variety of life-threatening surgical conditions and have a high burden of night and weekend calls. Maintaining current knowledge of published literature is increasingly challenging. This study aims to describe motivations and methods used by ACS surgeons to stay current with published literature. We focused on how ACS surgeons acquire new information and apply it in practice, their views on the effectiveness, efficiency, and potential bias of their methods, and their perspectives regarding future knowledge acquisition methods.MethodsA prospective qualitative semi-structured interview study was performed. Informed consent was obtained from purposively recruited participants: ACS surgeons practicing in the United States. Interviews continued until theme saturation. Reflexive inductive thematic analysis was used to generate qualitative results.ResultsThirteen interviews were conducted, averaging 27.3 min each. Participant interviews revealed several motivations for remaining current with published literature: problem-based literature review, self-promotion, curiosity, job performance, and job satisfaction. Participants used various strategies to identify and access new literature, including academic and non-academic sources. Barriers included paywalls, time constraints, and information overload. Facilitators included user-friendly interfaces and working at an academic institution. Participants provided insights into their effectiveness, efficiency, and biases and were wary of artificial intelligence processes that may reinforce bias. Participants unanimously desired more efficient methods when asked how literature acquisition may improve in the future.ConclusionsACS surgeons are motivated to stay current with published literature. Current barriers and facilitators were identified, and participants desired more efficient methods to stay current.Keywords: Acute care surgery, Literature acquisition, Bias, Qualitative, Semi-structured interviews, Artificial intelligence

Introduction

The body of published literature is growing at an ever-increasing pace, such that the continuous process of identifying and acquiring the latest published research relevant to one's field (i.e., “keeping up to date”) is challenging [1], [2], [3], [4], [5], [6], [7]. There are several tools available to help clinicians remain current. These tools include email citation alerts from specific journals [3], [6], [7], websites that offer regular comprehensive updates on key papers in the field [3], [6], [7], and subscription packages which summate data for clinicians for a fee (e.g., “Up-to-date”) [8]. However, how clinicians stay current with published literature relevant to their field and best practices to optimize knowledge acquisition efficiency are unknown.

Acute care surgery (ACS) is a field of medical practice that combines emergency general surgery (EGS), trauma surgery, and surgical critical care (SCC) [9]. It is a surgical specialty that cares for a variety of life-threatening conditions, with a high burden of night and weekend calls [10]. ACS, like many other fields in medicine, is the focus of active clinical research conducted across the globe. Research programs exist for all aspects of ACS, utilizing multiple methodologies. Therefore, literature relevant to ACS surgeons is published in numerous sources, across multiple disciplines, and with a cumulative high frequency of new publications [11]. It may therefore be challenging for clinically active ACS surgeons to remain current with evidence-based practice.

While multiple methodologies exist, qualitative methodology is well-suited for a new inquiry regarding how ACS surgeons acquire literature, as it allows rich descriptions of complex phenomena [12]. This approach allows ACS surgeon participants to report how they stay current and discuss the perceived efficacy and efficiency of their chosen literature inquiry method and their views on optimal information acquisition in the future.

This study's primary aim was to describe how ACS surgeons learn about and acquire current published peer-reviewed and relevant literature. Secondary aims were to understand the motives that drove ACS surgeons to try to “stay up to date” and how they applied the information obtained from recently published literature to their clinical practice. Tertiary aims were to describe ACS surgeons' opinions about the effectiveness, efficiency, and bias of their current methods of literature acquisition and to describe their predictions regarding how ACS surgeons may stay current in the future.

Methods

We interviewed ACS surgeons from across the United States (US). Qualitative interviews were selected as the data collection method as they provide detailed descriptions of complex phenomena [12].

Study conduct

An Institutional Review Board (IRB number IRB2023-546) approved this study. Informed consent was sought and obtained from participants before conducting interviews. The results were analyzed and reported following the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research (SRQR) [13].

Study type

Prospective qualitative semi-structured interview study. The qualitative approach was phenomenology (see Supplementary Material) [14], [15].

Study setting

Interviews were conducted with a diverse group of US clinicians in ACS practice, including EGS, trauma surgery, and SCC fields of practice.

Participants and sampling strategy

ACS surgeon participants of all career stages were purposively sampled to participate in the study. ACS surgeons in current practice within the US in all post-training career stages were included. Exclusion criteria were current resident or fellow physicians due to concerns that their answers would be highly influenced by their host institutions' methods of literature acquisition.

Recruitment

A purposive sample of ACS surgeons was recruited from US healthcare institutions. Participants were recruited via emails from the American Association for the Surgery of Trauma (AAST) Associate Member Research Group administrative team. Each research group member sent requests to two potential participants (36 were invited in total, 13 were interviewed, 23 declined, response rate of 36%).

Data collection

After informed consent was obtained, semi-structured interviews of approximately 30-minute duration were conducted. Recruitment and interviews were conducted from November 17, 2023, to March 22, 2024, until thematic saturation was achieved. Interviews were audio- and video-recorded and automatically transcribed using the Zoom™ video-conferencing platform (Zoom Communications, Inc., San Jose, CA). Audio recordings and transcripts were stored on a secure institutional server. Audio recordings were reviewed to ensure the transcripts generated by the system were accurate. Transcripts were anonymized and then analyzed by the study team. The host institution will maintain audio recordings and transcripts for 5 years and then destroy them.

Following the interviews, participants were sent a brief online questionnaire to collect demographic and institutional characteristics of participants. These data included participant age, career stage, type of practice, residency training, fellowship training, and society membership.

Procedure

Participants were given an information sheet about the study and scheduled for one-on-one interviews with a research team member. Interviews were conducted via videoconferencing software. Upon receiving approval from the participants, the interviews were audio recorded. After the interview, these recordings were fully transcribed, blinded, and assigned a unique identification number. The identification number was not connected to the participants' names or contact information, and pseudonyms were used within the transcripts and in reporting the results. The identification numbers were also provided to the participants; post-interview, they were directed to an online questionnaire and entered their number. In this way, each questionnaire response was linked to its corresponding transcript using a unique identifier; however, no personally identifying information was connected to either the interview transcripts or the questionnaire responses. The questionnaire asked participants to provide basic demographic, professional, and institutional information (see Supplementary Material).

Semi-structured interview guide

A semi-structured in-depth interview guide was used to conduct the interviews (see Supplementary Material). Semi-structured interviews strike a balance between covering a set of desired topics and allowing the conversation to explore other unanticipated areas of interest that may be relevant to the current study [16]. The interview opened with some rapport-building questions before delving into the topics that were the focus of our study: motivations for seeking published research; methods for identifying, accessing, and acquiring literature of interest; evaluation of literature along with barriers and facilitators to conducting, searching for, and acquiring literature; application and implementation of new knowledge derived from acquired literature; self-assessment of the effectiveness, efficiency and biases of the participants' methods for finding and acquiring literature; and speculation regarding future methods for literature acquisition.

Analysis

Interview transcripts were coded by two researchers with prior content analytic experience. Themes were identified using reflexive inductive thematic analysis [17]. Audio recordings were transcribed and analyzed according to the 6-step inductive thematic analysis method: 1) familiarization with the data, 2) generating initial codes, 3) searching for themes, 4) reviewing themes, 5) defining and naming themes, and 6) producing the report/manuscript [17]. Throughout this process, the two researchers conducting the analysis consulted each other routinely to discuss potential themes emerging from the data, what types of content reflected particular themes, and to resolve edge cases. An audit trail was maintained comprising researcher notes from the interviews and analysis of the transcripts and the digital transcripts themselves, which were color-coded to indicate to which theme they were related.

Results

Eleven interviews were conducted before data saturation was achieved, and then two additional interviews were conducted [18]. The interview process was closed because these final interviews yielded no additional information despite purposively recruiting participants who had been underrepresented in the first wave of interviews. This resulted in 13 completed interviews, producing 5.9 h of audio recordings. Interviews ranged from 18 to 38 min; the average interview time was 27.3 min.

Sample

Participants represented various locations, practice settings, academic ranks, experience, and diverse demographic factors (Table 1). The sample size was deemed sufficient given the limited scope of our topic, the saturation of the data, and the fact that the participants were homogenous in that they were all practicing ACS surgeons in the US [19]. Complete reflexive analysis results are included in the Supplementary Material.

Aim 1: literature search strategies

Participants used various strategies for identifying, discovering, and acquiring new literature, including academic and non-academic sources. These strategies were often complicated due to barriers; however, participants also identified various facilitators that aided their search.

Academic sources

Participants reported that academic sources were the most frequently used sources for literature acquisition. Participants unanimously referenced academic sources, such as journals, textbooks, and systematic reviews, as more reliable sources because they are peer-reviewed. Other participants mentioned accessing medical and scholarly databases, such as PubMed (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov), UpToDate (https://www.wolterskluwer.com/en-gb/solutions/uptodate), and Medscape (https://emedicine.medscape.com). Several participants, like Participant 10, mentioned using Google (https://www.google.com) or Google Scholar (https://scholar.google.com) as their primary search and acquisition tool because it ensured they were consulting “evidence-based” and “credible” sources, and because it was easier to use than an academic database. Interestingly, many participants used Google Scholar and Research Gate (https://www.researchgate.net) to follow researchers and track publications, yet rarely, if ever, visited an author's website. Our participants overwhelmingly relied on digital sources, with only a few mentioning the use of printed materials. For instance, Participant 4 mentioned having “the typical surgeon's mountain of textbooks” that he occasionally references, but he did not consult textbooks as his primary acquisition method.

Non-academic sources

Participants overwhelmingly noted that they relied on social media to identify, discover, and, at times, acquire the latest literature. For example, Participant 3 explained that X (formerly Twitter; https://x.com/home) allows him to follow other surgeons active in the field and find relevant research. However, some participants expressed concerns about using X for acquisition. For instance, Participant 3 explained that the constant turnover of social media platform ownership and the political context makes it difficult to navigate and keep up with crucial research updates. On the other hand, Participant 2 expressed that he avoids using X because he feels it is a self-serving space where physicians often share articles that have little clinical relevance. Additional sources included personal conversations with colleagues, conferences, and podcasts.

Barriers

Despite the widespread availability of academic and non-academic tools, participants mentioned several barriers that hindered their search and acquisition efforts. These included paywalls, lack of time, and information overload, with time being identified as the primary barrier to staying up to date with current literature in the field. In addition to time, participants highlighted that the “astronomical amount of information” (Participant 1) they can access makes it difficult to sift through or narrow search inquiries.

Facilitators

Participants also explained that barriers could be turned into search facilitators. For example, while Participant 2 mentioned time as a limiting factor, he also said he would search and read more if he had more time. Other facilitators include the technological affordances of the platforms, such as user-friendly interfaces. Additionally, the institutions that participants were affiliated with also served as facilitators. For example, working at teaching hospitals and instructing residents naturally required ACS surgeons to stay current, and these institutions often provided access to databases.

Aim 2: motivations for maintaining current knowledge of published literature

Participant interviews revealed several common motivations associated with searching and acquiring new literature: problem-based literature review, curiosity, job performance, and job satisfaction. These motivations were not always mutually exclusive and were often complementary. For example, Participant 2 described how keeping up with relevant literature is driven by successful job performance and career advancement. He mentioned seeking relevant literature to generate knowledge and identify gaps in research so he could conduct subsequent studies to fulfill them. Other participants also noted that they sought new literature for the simple joy of discovering and learning something new. However, in most cases, the motivations for staying up to date were overwhelmingly problem-based, and it was required for successful job performance and satisfaction. Job performance included a surgeon's job to fulfill specific duties, such as teaching and sharing knowledge with residents. For example, Participant 9 explained that he wanted to stay up to date with published literature because of the residents for whom he was responsible for training. Similarly, Participant 5 illustrated an active and problem-based approach, explaining that for him, the acquisition was “spurred by a clinical situation, [where] we'll be rounding in the morning, and there will be a patient in the ICU who has a blood sugar that is out of control. So, I'll email my students with commentary.” Conversely, job satisfaction was also tied to participants wanting to stay informed and feel personally fulfilled and competent. For instance, Participant 5 explained, “The third motivator for me is being a good surgeon.” Participant 10 also explained that staying up to date is essential for the patient's safety and well-being. Therefore, literature acquisition is directly tied to one's ability to perform the required skills and ensure that the patient receives safe and competent care.

Aim 3: application of literature

Several participants explained that literature acquisition is integral to individual learning and clinical practice. Participant 4 explained, “We've been rewriting clinical practice guidelines, so every time we update that… that's one way the literature is implemented.” However, several participants also clarified that newly acquired literature did not immediately find itself being applied in clinical practice. Instead, Participant 1 emphasized the importance of identifying “multiple avenues before making a big clinical change.” Similarly, Participant 6 described a process where she would discuss new findings in the literature collectively with her colleagues before diving deep into how the findings could translate to different patient populations.

Aim 4: self-evaluation of personal literature acquisition strategies

After considering the facilitators of acquisition, participants were prompted to consider how they evaluated the information sources and appraised their acquisition skills. Many, like Participants 7 and 12, described themselves as “very effective” and “confident” in their abilities. None of our participants evaluated themselves as inefficient at acquiring relevant literature; however, a few admitted they had room to improve. In addition to self-evaluation, participants also described how they evaluated the sources they sought out. Specifically, participants expressed a bias toward sources published in a journal with a high impact factor. Because participants had to sift through information quickly, they universally preferred to evaluate the methodological quality of an article first before allocating time to read the entire article, prioritizing the abstract, study design, and references to determine the study's relevance and credibility.

Participants identified biases or individual tendencies influencing their literature selection, interpretation, and use. Interestingly, participants expressed that they do not necessarily pick articles they are more likely to agree with; instead, they enjoy hearing different viewpoints, including those that challenge their opinions. For example, Participant 2 explained that he would be more likely to read an article if he found something to disagree with. Others cited the country of origin, the institutional affiliation of authors, and the possibility for nepotism within the peer-review and publishing processes as sources of bias. While these biases are often unintentional and go unnoticed, they shape literature selection and evaluation.

Aim 5: future methods

Participants unanimously stated a desire for more efficient methods when asked how literature acquisition may evolve in the future. Many participants mentioned artificial intelligence and literature aggregators that could learn search patterns and research agendas to develop a bespoke repository of relevant literature. However, not all participants were interested in the integration of artificial intelligence. Participant 12 quickly pushed back, explaining that when writing a manuscript, “part of the chase is finding articles” and “AI may reinforce biases by showing only what researchers want to see.”

Discussion

This qualitative study describes how ACS surgeons acquire relevant literature and apply it to their clinical practice. It also describes what motivates ACS surgeons to stay current with the literature. It also addresses the effectiveness, efficiency, and biases of their methods of literature acquisition and their predictions for the future. Thus, this study improves understanding of how clinicians balance this crucial aspect of professional life with their other responsibilities.

There was a wide heterogeneity of methods of literature acquisition, including academic and non-academic sources found online, such as social media and podcasts. As expected, peer-reviewed sources were the most trusted and sought-after. These findings are consistent with Davies and Harrison (2007) [20], who cite textbooks, journal articles, colleagues, and internet searches as doctors' most utilized information sources. In their meta-analysis of a decade of research on physicians' information-seeking behaviors, two-thirds of the studies found that participants prioritized print sources and found it easier to ask colleagues clinical questions before turning to digital sources and the Internet [20]. Our findings suggest an increasing preference for digital acquisition methods, which could be attributed to improved accessibility and ease of use. Like Panahi et al. (2016) [21], our participants also identified blogs (e.g., Surgical Pizza; https://surgicalpizza.org) and social media platforms (e.g., “X,” formerly “Twitter”) as tools for “sharing knowledge, engaging in discussions, commenting with peers, and also reading and keeping up to date” (p. 101). In addition to social media use, podcasts were commonly sought for information. This is consistent with Kiernan et al. (2023) [22], who found podcasts a convenient method for healthcare workers to stay informed and entertained through various formats (e.g., interviews and case studies). The increased podcast listenership reflects how digital technology continues to make acquisition more flexible and suggests that it can be integrated into everyday routines. However, few respondents utilized researchers' websites. The lack of interest or utilization of personal academic websites for literature discovery and acquisition calls into question the value of allocating time to maintaining personal websites. It also suggests that platforms with a stronger academic focus are more helpful in tracking scholarly output.

One of the key barriers to the acquisition of new knowledge was time. Consistent with Davies (2007) [20], time was invariably the most significant factor, highlighting the demands of surgeons' professional lives and illustrating that a more streamlined approach to literature acquisition is necessary even with access to digital sources. Other barriers were paywalls and information overload. In contrast, facilitators included user-friendly interfaces, push alerts for new articles, and institutional subscriptions that removed the paywall barrier. There are multiple implications of these findings. First, the increased use of social media and podcasts to obtain new literature is both a benefit and a hazard. It is beneficial to be aware of new articles immediately as they have been published, or even before they have been peer-reviewed, if they have been deposited in an open-access archive (e.g., https://arxiv.org). However, there is the risk that influential people could spread information and knowledge, along with clinical recommendations for patient care, on a “Tweet” or social media message before full-text articles have been able to be appropriately critically appraised by the surgical community. Second, as most clinicians prefer to use digital methods to access peer-reviewed content, individuals and institutions may need to evaluate whether printed journals should still be purchased or distributed or if these funds would be better spent on institutional subscriptions to online journals. Third, given that time was a significant barrier, article database websites (e.g., PubMed) should continually improve their usability to improve discovery, search, and retrieval of current literature. Fourth, given paywalls were a significant barrier, academics should prioritize open-access publication of their articles if possible. Of course, these efforts to increase the dissemination of recent research to a broader audience could be stymied by journals with high open-access fees.

ACS surgeons were primarily motivated by the desire to provide high-quality care. They had goal-oriented motivations consistent with information-seeking behavior—an active and intentional search for information to address specific clinical questions [23], [24]. The need for immediate solutions forces physicians to consult the literature through directive searches. However, there is also an inherent curiosity that drives acquisition. These information-seeking strategies occur organically and are not necessarily spurred by a clinical problem, nor are they often time-bound. Other professional pressures motivating their desire to remain current with literature included improving job satisfaction, ensuring competency, facilitating resident teaching, and discovering research gaps to guide their academic focus. These findings imply that information sources must be as clinically and patient focused as possible. Peer reviewers must ensure articles are written clearly to address specific clinical problems, and journals must ensure the articles are categorized and tagged appropriately for ease of identification in a targeted literature search.

Regarding how literature was applied, more recently acquired articles were not immediately implemented in practice. Rather, in developing clinical practice guidelines, several congruent articles are needed to lead to a change in practice. Clinicians should also discuss new findings with their colleagues and collectively agree on whether clinical changes are warranted before implementing them. These findings imply that when novel findings are discovered, the surgical community should encourage other academic groups to validate these findings in different settings and populations to improve their validity. This will increase the likelihood that the research leads to patient benefit via updated institutional policies and national clinical practice guidelines.

Participants believed their literature acquisition methods were effective. Interestingly, no participants admitted to inefficiency, though they identified aspects that could improve efficiency. Participants also discussed potential biases inherent in their methods, including prioritizing information from high-impact journals, journals of reputable authors or institutions, and nepotism. These findings imply that literature acquisition tools must keep up with the dynamic field and assist ACS surgeons with discovery, acquisition, assessment, and application in clinical settings without biasing the clinician. The rate of scientific publication indeed continues to increase [11], while time constraints may be leading to a reduction in readership of scholarly publication [25]. A natural solution is to utilize technology, including AI, to identify and compile information for clinician use. However, this study has shown that clinicians are concerned that AI may reinforce bias. Therefore, an emphasis on the continued teaching and training of academic critical appraisal is necessary for the future of surgical practice [26], [27], [28]. Resources such as that provided by the Critical Appraisal Skills Programme (CASP; https://casp-uk.net/what-is-critical-appraisal/), The Cochrane Collaboration (https://training.cochrane.org/handbook), or understanding the GRADE (Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation) system for clinical recommendations are essential learning for any practicing surgeon [29].

This study has several strengths. It was a prospective study with purposeful selection of ACS surgeons at various career stages and in a wide variety of institutions. Therefore, we limited the potential for selection bias and increased the likelihood of transferability of the study findings. The qualitative nature of the study provides the opportunity for more in-depth insight than a quantitative study. Finally, thematic saturation was achieved, which permits reporting findings with confidence that additional significant themes would be unlikely to appear if additional participants were interviewed.

There are some limitations of this study. Though multiple institutions were represented, they were exclusively from practice settings in the United States. Different themes may exist if clinicians were sought from an international audience. However, we wanted to limit the influence of confounders that a global clinician population might introduce, such as different information technology resources, different training program requirements, and different healthcare system designs. Finally, it should be noted that most participants were in practice for 5 years or less and had fewer than 10 publications. These factors could have potentially biased findings related to literature acquisition strategies and motives for maintaining currency with published literature. However, several participants had been practicing for longer or had lengthier publication records; given that their opinions mirrored those of the participants who had been practicing for a shorter period, this may mitigate these concerns regarding bias. There may have been a selection bias related to the recruitment process: however, as a group we ensured that those invited were heterogeneous in terms of their age, gender, ethnicity, rank, practice setting (University, Community, Government), and State (see Table 1). In addition, we acknowledge that those more likely to engage in a qualitative research study may be more likely to stay up to date in their surgical knowledge.

Acute care surgeons acquire and apply literature in multiple ways. They have insight into their chosen methods' effectiveness and biases. The future of literature acquisition will be increasingly digital, but clinicians are wary of introducing artificial intelligence processes that may reinforce bias. Training in critical appraisal skills is necessary for the future of surgical practice.
