CurvReg: Curvature-aware registration for multi-stain alignment of liver biopsies
Authors: Fatemeh Zabihollahy, Cheuk-To Jeremy Yu, David W. Dodington, George M. Yousef
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Accurate alignment of multi-stain liver biopsy whole-slide images is critical for emerging computational pathology workflows, yet remains challenging due to curved biopsy geometry, non-rigid distortions, and substantial stain-dependent appearance variation. We introduce CurvReg, a curvature-aware, stain-independent registration framework designed to address these challenges by decomposing each biopsy into anatomically coherent segments for piecewise affine alignment, followed by deep learning-based deformable refinement operating directly on tissue segmentation masks rather than raw stained images. By estimating deformation from structural geometry rather than stain intensities, CurvReg enforces anatomically plausible warps and substantially improves robustness to staining, imaging, and device variability. We evaluated CurvReg across three multi-stain liver biopsy datasets and an independent public kidney biopsy dataset from the ANHIR challenge. On the internal multi-stain dataset (Dataset #1) containing hematoxylin–eosin (H&E), Masson's trichrome (MT), and CK7 sections with bile duct annotations, pre-registration mean target registration error (TRE) measured 231.5 μm (H&E–MT), 255.3 μm (MT–CK7), and 188.7 μm (H&E–CK7). After registration, TRE decreased to 51.9 μm, 57.9 μm, and 50.9 μm, respectively, with a >70% reduction across all stain pairs (p < 0.001). Dice similarity increased from 0.54–0.60 to 0.95 (H&E–MT), 0.95 (MT–CK7), and 0.96 (H&E–CK7) (p < 0.001). On the internal portal tract and hepatic vein-annotated H&E–MT dataset (Dataset #2), TRE improved from 147.9 to 48.0 μm (corresponding to a 67.5% reduction; p < 0.001) and Dice from 0.76 to 0.98 (p < 0.001). External validation on a heterogeneous multi-center H&E–MT dataset showed similar gains, with TRE reduced from 93.4 to 42.2 μm (>50% reduction; p < 0.001) and Dice increasing from 0.61 to 0.95 (p < 0.001), confirming robust generalization across scanners, staining protocols, and tissue processing variations. Evaluation on the independent ANHIR kidney biopsy dataset (PAS–MT sections) further demonstrated the generalizability of CurvReg to a different tissue type and stain combination, reducing TRE from 107.2 to 81.6 μm (p = 0.024) while improving Dice similarity from 0.90 to 0.97 (p < 0.001). To highlight downstream clinical value, we demonstrate that MT-derived portal tract and hepatic vein segmentations can be accurately transferred onto H&E using CurvReg, enabling consistent vascular delineation within the diagnostic stain.

Introduction

Chronic liver diseases represent a major global health burden and are characterized by progressive alterations in hepatic architecture that directly impact organ function.1 Liver biopsy is a core component for the diagnosis and management of all forms of chronic liver disease.2, 3, 4 Among the most diagnostically informative features are bile ducts, portal tracts, and hepatic veins, which together define the fundamental vascular and biliary organization of the liver. Portal tracts (which contain the bile ducts, hepatic arteries, and portal veins) and hepatic veins are central to liver structure and function, and their integrity is critical in the assessment of chronic liver disease.5 Alterations in bile duct number or morphology,6 periportal or perivenular fibrosis,7 and remodeling of hepatic veins8 reflect key pathological processes, including cholestasis, inflammation, and vascular injury. Accurate evaluation of these structures provides essential information for diagnosis, prognosis, and disease staging.9, 10, 11, 12, 13

In routine histopathology, pathologists assess these features using a combination of standard and specialized stains. Portal tracts and hepatic veins are routinely evaluated with hematoxylin–eosin (H&E) and Masson's trichrome (MT), which emphasize connective tissue and vascular structures important for assessing fibrosis, lobular organization, and vascular remodeling.14 Bile ducts are highlighted with cytokeratin 7 (CK7) immunohistochemistry,15 often in conjunction with H&E to provide overall tissue context, whereas MT clarifies portal tract architecture. Integration of multiple stains enables a comprehensive view of liver tissue architecture, combining epithelial, connective, and vascular information. Quantitative analysis of multi-stained histology supports reproducible diagnosis and facilitates clinically meaningful biomarker development. As digital hepatopathology evolves toward precision medicine,16 the ability to integrate structurally corresponding regions across stains offers a pathway to more scalable and data-driven disease assessment. This direction aligns with the broader adoption of whole-slide imaging (WSI) platforms and the increasing deployment of AI-assisted diagnostic tools in clinical pathology, which together create the technical foundation needed for automated multi-stain integration.

Despite its clinical importance, meaningful integration of information across serially stained histology sections remains largely a cognitive task performed by pathologists. In practice, correspondence between stains is inferred mentally through visual comparison of anatomical landmarks rather than through computational tools. Although highly desirable, developing an automated multi-stain registration method remains technically challenging. Serial sections undergo substantial deformation during microtomy and staining, including tearing, stretching, folding, and changes in curvature; these nonlinear distortions are further exacerbated when sections are non-adjacent or exhibit markedly different visual appearances. Consequently, whereas several automated WSI registration methods have been proposed,17, 18 achieving robust and accurate multi-stain alignment for routine liver pathology remains a significant challenge.

Early histology registration methods primarily relied on rigid or affine transformations, which are computationally efficient and useful for global alignment but cannot accommodate the substantial local curvature, tearing, and geometric irregularity characteristic of liver biopsies.19 Deformable registration techniques, including B-spline models, diffeomorphic formulations, and learning-based deformation estimators, were introduced to address local variation.20 However, unconstrained or weakly regularized deformable models have high degrees of freedom and may produce anatomically implausible distortions, particularly in histology, where anatomical cues are sparse, geometry is irregular, and appearance differs substantially across stains.21, 22 This limitation arises from the mathematical flexibility of deformable models, which may overfit noise, artifacts, or stain-specific structures and distort true anatomy.

A second major challenge for deformable registration is stain variability. Traditional intensity-and feature-based deformable methods rely heavily on image appearance characteristics, including color, texture, and local contrast, which can vary substantially across histological stains such as H&E, CK7, and MT. These appearance differences introduce significant challenges for multimodal registration, as image features that are reliable within a single stain may not remain consistent across different staining protocols.10 Although recent advances in deep learning and multimodal registration have enabled successful cross-stain deformable alignment in several settings,18 achieving robust generalization across diverse stain combinations remains challenging due to substantial variations in tissue appearance and the limited availability of stain-invariant image representations.

To address these challenges, recent work has explored stain-independent or multimodal registration strategies, including cross-modal similarity metrics and pipelines for serial histopathology that attempt to reduce the impact of color differences between stains.23 However, because stain variability directly affects the reliability of extracted image correspondences, robust multi-stain registration requires effective strategies to overcome these appearance discrepancies. Whereas these approaches have shown promise, they continue to face challenges associated with substantial appearance variation induced by different staining protocols, which can affect robustness under strong cross-stain variability.24 Beyond registration-specific approaches, deep learning methods incorporating synthetic stain variation, including CycleGAN-25 and transformer-based26 stain translation frameworks, have been developed to reduce stain variability through stain translation or data augmentation. However, the learned representations may remain dependent on stain-specific appearance patterns and often require extensive augmentation or domain adaptation strategies to improve generalization. Similarly, domain adaptation approaches aim to reduce stain-driven biases, but many still rely on image appearance features, making them vulnerable to residual stain-related variability.27 Consequently, unresolved stain variability may lead multimodal registration pipelines to capture stain-dependent features rather than true anatomical correspondence, resulting in reduced generalizability and unstable deformation estimation under challenging cross-stain scenarios, as highlighted in previous studies.23, 24

To mitigate anatomically implausible deformations, prior studies have emphasized the importance of incorporating anatomical priors, local rigidity constraints, or structural representations into deformable models.28, 29 Constraining deformation fields with anatomical boundaries, segmentation maps, or tissue-specific geometric assumptions improves robustness and helps maintain biologically plausible transformations.30 Classic registration literature similarly notes that unconstrained deformation fields often introduce unrealistic warping unless guided by explicit structural cues or piecewise rigidity assumptions.31 These considerations are especially relevant for histology, where serial sections exhibit sparse anatomical correspondence and substantial appearance variability. Recent reviews of multimodal histology registration highlight that the combination of structural inconsistency, nonlinear distortion, and stain-dependent color variation remains a central challenge, motivating approaches that rely on tissue geometry rather than raw pixel intensities as the primary alignment signal.32

Together, these limitations motivate a registration framework that: (1) preserves anatomical fidelity by respecting tissue geometry and local biopsy curvature, (2) avoids the unconstrained flexibility of purely deformable models, and (3) remains robust to stain-induced appearance variation. These considerations directly informed the design of our suggested approach.

In this work, we propose a curvature-aware, piecewise tissue alignment framework for stain-independent registration of liver histology images. Our method segments tissue into anatomically consistent regions and applies local affine transformations, enabling robust alignment of curved and irregular biopsy geometries while preserving structural coherence. We further extend this framework with a deep learning-based deformable registration model that estimates deformation fields from tissue geometry rather than stain-specific appearance. As a result, the proposed approach enforces anatomically plausible transformations and reduces sensitivity to variability introduced by staining protocols and imaging conditions.

To evaluate registration accuracy, we employ clinically meaningful anatomical landmarks, including bile ducts, portal structures, and hepatic veins, rather than generic correspondence points. Bile ducts are sharply delineated in CK7 and remain identifiable in H&E and MT, whereas portal tracts and hepatic veins provide larger-scale reference structures essential for assessing fibrosis, vascular remodeling, and overall lobular organization. This multiscale, clinically grounded landmark set enables robust and interpretable evaluation of cross-stain alignment.

Beyond addressing cross-stain alignment challenges, we demonstrate the clinical utility of our framework in a downstream task that is central to liver pathology workflows. Accurate delineation of portal tracts and hepatic veins is essential for zonal analysis, fibrosis staging, and vascular-centric quantification. However, the visibility of these structures varies substantially across stains, with MT providing enhanced visualization of periportal collagen and vascular boundaries compared to H&E. As a result, segmentation performance is inherently stain-dependent. To characterize these differences and enable stain-agnostic structural analysis, we developed independent deep learning models to segment portal tracts and hepatic veins on both H&E and MT slides. This allowed us to systematically compare stain-specific segmentation behavior and to identify MT as a reliable source of structural information. By integrating the MT-based segmentation with our CurvReg framework, we transfer high-quality structural masks onto corresponding H&E sections, yielding a unified representation of key anatomical landmarks in the H&E domain. This integration supports quantitative analysis within the stain most commonly used in routine practice and demonstrates how robust cross-stain registration can directly enhance clinically relevant tissue assessment.

Materials and methods

The proposed framework takes paired liver biopsy WSIs from different stain combinations as input and produces spatially aligned tissue sections. The pipeline first extracts tissue regions and partitions each biopsy into structurally coherent segments to enable localized alignment. These segments are initially registered using piecewise affine transformations, followed by deep learning-based deformable refinement to improve local correspondence. This combined strategy enables accurate alignment of curved and irregular biopsy geometries across stains. Datasets

A comprehensive internal dataset comprising 226 whole-slide pathology images (100 H&E-stained, 100 MT-stained, and 26 CK7-stained slides) was collected from 10 Canadian medical centers. The dataset included 210 slides from 10 Canadian medical centers, including Toronto General Hospital (TGH) and 16 slides from 9 additional hospitals that had submitted stained slides to TGH for pathology consultation. The dataset was divided on a patient-level basis into 100 slides (50 H&E and MT) for training and 126 slides for testing. To further evaluate the generalizability of the proposed framework, an independent external evaluation was performed using 10 WSIs from the publicly available automatic non-rigid histological image registration (ANHIR) challenge.33 These images comprised adjacent 3 μm formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded kidney biopsy sections obtained from glomerulopathy cases and stained with Periodic Acid–Schiff (PAS; n = 5) and MT (n = 5). Liver tissue segmentation and piecewise modeling

Tissue segmentation is performed independently on all stains (H&E, MT, and CK7) using Otsu thresholding to separate tissue from background. The resulting binary masks serve as the geometric representation for subsequent processing steps. To accommodate the curvature and irregular geometry of liver biopsies, each tissue mask is subdivided into anatomically coherent segments along user-defined cutlines drawn at natural curvature transitions (Fig. 3). Although curvature transition points and control correspondences are manually initialized in the current implementation, these points serve only as geometric guidance for initialization; the subsequent deformation estimation is performed automatically by the proposed CurvReg optimization framework.

Piecewise affine registration

Affine alignment is applied independently to each corresponding tissue segment to correct coarse geometric discrepancies while preserving local structure. Translation and rotation are estimated based on segment centroids and mask overlap, whereas scaling is deferred to the deformable stage to avoid anatomically implausible distortions. Correspondence between segments is defined based on their relative spatial ordering along the biopsy curvature after manual identification of curvature control points. Full implementation details of the centroid computation, rotation search strategy, and optimization criteria are provided in “Implementation Details.”

Deep learning-based deformable registration

Residual nonlinear misalignments are refined using a deep learning-based registration module built on the VoxelMorph framework.35 The module operates on tissue masks rather than raw stained images to produce stain-independent deformation fields that improve local alignment while remaining robust to stain-specific intensity variations. The network architecture, loss functions, training procedure, and optimization settings are fully described in “Implementation Details,” where we also detail data augmentation strategies and model selection criteria.

Portal tract and hepatic vein segmentation

To demonstrate CurvReg's utility for structural transfer, independent U-Net–based models were trained to segment portal tracts and hepatic veins on H&E and MT WSIs, respectively. The resulting stain-specific masks were used as inputs to the CurvReg pipeline, enabling MT-derived structural segmentations to be transferred onto corresponding H&E slides. Full implementation details regarding the U-Net architecture, training procedure, augmentation strategy, and evaluation protocol are provided in the following sections.

Implementation details

All components of the framework were implemented in Python 3.10 using TensorFlow with the Keras backend, along with standard scientific libraries such as NumPy, OpenCV, SciPy, and OpenSlide. WSIs were processed at pyramid level 2, corresponding to a 4-fold downsampling relative to their native resolution, which provided a balance between computational efficiency and geometric fidelity. All experiments were run on a workstation equipped with an NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU, an Intel Core i9-13900K CPU operating at 3.00 GHz, and 64 GB of RAM, running Windows 11.

Tissue masks were generated through Otsu thresholding, followed by morphological cleaning and hole filling to eliminate scanning artifacts and to bridge small ruptures in the tissue. Manual cutlines were drawn in QuPath and exported as PNG overlays. A custom Python script parsed these overlays to extract cutline coordinates, which were then used to divide the tissue mask into contiguous pieces. Each resulting segment was cropped to a tight bounding box with a 10-pixel margin and resampled to 4096 × 4096 pixels, which was selected to accommodate the dimensions of tissue fragments in our datasets while providing sufficient image resolution for accurate registration. This image size is an adjustable implementation parameter rather than a fixed requirement of the proposed framework and can be increased for larger specimens, subject to available computational resources. The standardized segment images were subsequently used as inputs for both the affine and deformable registration stages.

For each pair of corresponding tissue segments, translation and rotation were estimated using binary masks. Centroids were computed for each segment, and the moving mask was first translated to align its centroid with that of the fixed mask. Rotation was evaluated over a range of −30° to +30° in 0.5° increments using nearest-neighbor rotation without reshaping, and the optimal angle was selected based on maximal Dice overlap. Both the translation and rotation parameters were retained for later composition in the full-slide registration pipeline. Global scale was deliberately not estimated at this stage, as any residual global or local differences were handled during deformable refinement.

Deformable alignment was performed using a VoxelMorph-style dense network operating on the binary tissue masks. The model predicted a two-channel displacement field that was applied using a differentiable spatial transformer to warp the moving segment. The architecture followed a U-Net design with encoder features of 32, 64, 64, and 64 channels and decoder features of 64, 64, 64, and 32 channels. Displacement fields were used directly with zero integration steps. Training optimized a combination of normalized cross-correlation as a similarity loss, using a window size of nine, and a gradient-based smoothness regularizer. The relative weights for similarity and smoothness were set to 1.0 and 0.02, respectively. The Adam optimizer was used with a learning rate of 1e-4, and training incorporated standard callbacks for checkpointing, learning-rate reduction, and early stopping.

The training dataset for the registration model consisted of 100 slides (50 pairs) of tissue mask segments extracted from 10 H&E and MT slides, each first aligned through the affine stage. Data augmentation included random shifts, rotations, and scaling operations that preserved mask topology. Models were trained for 100 epochs using a batch size of 4. The VoxelMorph network was trained in an unsupervised manner using normalized cross-correlation as the image similarity loss and an L2 gradient regularization term to encourage smooth deformation fields. Optimization was performed using the Adam optimizer, and the model achieving the lowest validation loss was selected for the final evaluation.

Portal tract and hepatic vein segmentation was performed using two stain-specific convolutional neural networks trained independently on MT and H&E WSIs. For MT slides, a standard U-Net architecture with 32 initial feature maps was trained on 512 × 512 tiles extracted from 40 annotated MT slides, with extensive on-the-fly augmentation including random flips, rotations, intensity perturbations, and elastic distortions. For H&E slides, we used a U-Net with attention gates to improve localization of vascular boundaries in regions with weaker contrast, initialized with 16 filters and trained on 256 × 256 tiles sampled from 40 annotated H&E slides. Tiles for both stains were sampled directly from the raw WSI and screened using tile-level intensity statistics: tiles with mean intensity or standard deviation (SD) below predefined thresholds were discarded to eliminate blank or near-blank regions, ensuring that only tiles containing tissue were used for training. Both networks produced sigmoid-activated multi-channel outputs for portal tract and hepatic vein segmentation. The MT model was optimized using binary cross-entropy loss, whereas the H&E model was trained using a combined Dice and binary cross-entropy loss to improve robustness to class imbalance and stabilize convergence. Whole-slide predictions were generated using overlapping tiling with Gaussian blending and refined using morphological post-processing to remove small artifacts and ensure anatomically plausible portal tract and hepatic vein boundaries. Segmentation outputs from the MT model were subsequently transferred onto the corresponding H&E slides using the deformation fields produced by the CurvReg registration framework. Both segmentation models were trained for 100 epochs using the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 1×10−4, and training incorporated standard callbacks for checkpointing, learning-rate reduction, and early stopping.

Evaluation metrics

Registration accuracy was assessed using both landmark- and region-based quantitative metrics. Target registration error (TRE) was computed between anatomically corresponding landmarks. To provide a physically meaningful interpretation of registration accuracy, the absolute TRE values achieved by the proposed framework were reported in micrometers. For method comparisons and ablation analyses, TRE values were reported in pixels to preserve consistency with the original registration coordinate system and facilitate direct comparison across approaches. Across all datasets, the spatial resolution was approximately 0.25 μm/pixel (range: 0.2500–0.2530 μm/pixel). TRE values reported in micrometers were calculated using the corresponding scanner-specific pixel resolution and the 64× downsampling factor applied during registration.

Landmark choice depended on the dataset: bile ducts were used as the primary landmarks for the main H&E–MT–CK7 dataset, whereas portal tracts and hepatic veins were used for secondary and external validation datasets. This design enabled evaluation across structures with distinct morphologies, spatial distributions, and staining properties. For the ANHIR public dataset, registration accuracy was evaluated using manually annotated tissue landmarks provided by the benchmark, which were distributed approximately uniformly across the tissue regions and paired across corresponding serial sections.

To complement the landmark analysis, the Dice similarity coefficient was calculated between fixed and registered tissue masks, offering a region-based measure of global spatial overlap and tissue-level agreement. Dice scores for tissue masks were approximately normally distributed, so performance is reported as mean ± SD to reflect the average agreement and variability across slides. TRE and Dice metrics were quantified at two points in the registration pipeline: before any alignment and after the complete affine-plus-deformable registration, providing a clear view of absolute accuracy as well as the contribution of the proposed method.

For landmark-based evaluation of portal tract and hepatic vein segmentation, Dice scores were non-normally distributed due to variable structure size, morphology, and contrast. Accordingly, performance is reported as median along with the first quartile (Q1) and third quartile (Q3) to provide a robust measure of typical performance and variability across cases.

When appropriate, statistical differences between methods were evaluated using standard hypothesis-testing procedures. TRE and Dice values were compared using a non-parametric paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test at a 5% significance level, and corresponding p-values were reported to indicate the strength of evidence for performance differences.

Results

We first present the registration performance of the proposed affine-plus-deformable CurvReg pipeline across all four datasets, evaluated using both landmark- and region-based metrics. To assess the contribution of individual components, we perform an ablation study, highlighting the complementary roles of piecewise affine alignment and deformable refinement. The proposed framework was further compared with representative state-of-the-art registration methods to assess its performance relative to existing solutions. Building on these results, we further demonstrate the clinical utility of the framework by examining segmentation of portal tracts and hepatic veins using stain-specific MT and H&E models, both before and after integration with CurvReg.

Registration performance across test datasets

Dataset #1 (bile duct alignment)

We first evaluated the proposed registration framework on the internal multi-stain dataset (Dataset #1), which includes serial liver biopsy sections stained with H&E, MT, and CK7 and annotated with bile duct landmarks. This dataset enables a comprehensive assessment of stain-independent registration performance across all three stain combinations.

Before registration, substantial spatial misalignment was observed across all stain pairs, with mean TRE values of 231.5 ± 240.9 μm for H&E–MT, 255.3 ± 228.3 μm for MT–CK7, and 190.1 ± 185.4 μm for H&E–CK7. After applying the proposed registration framework, mean TRE was reduced to 51.9 ± 37.2, 57.9 ± 41.6, and 50.9 ± 42.5 μm, respectively, corresponding to reductions exceeding 70% across all stain combinations. All reductions in TRE were statistically significant when compared with pre-registration values (paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test, p < 0.001).

Tissue-level alignment also improved markedly following registration. Pre-registration Dice similarity coefficients ranged from 0.54 to 0.60 across stain pairs, reflecting limited spatial correspondence between serial sections. After applying the proposed framework, Dice similarity increased to 0.95 for H&E–MT, 0.95 for MT–CK7, and 0.96 for H&E–CK7. Improvements in Dice were statistically significant for all stain combinations (paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test, p < 0.001).

Dataset #2 (portal tracts and hepatic veins)

We next evaluated the proposed registration framework on the internal H&E–MT dataset (Dataset #2), which includes liver biopsy sections annotated with portal tracts and hepatic veins, representing larger-scale anatomical structures than bile ducts.

Before registration, the mean TRE was 147.9 ± 96.2 μm, indicating notable misalignment between serial H&E and MT sections. Application of the proposed registration framework reduced TRE to 48.0 ± 51.7 μm, corresponding to a reduction of approximately 67% relative to pre-registration. This improvement in landmark correspondence was statistically significant (paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test, p < 0.001).

Consistent with the results observed for bile ducts, tissue-level overlap improved substantially following registration. Pre-registration Dice similarity was 0.76, increasing to 0.98 after application of the proposed framework. This improvement was statistically significant (paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test, p < 0.001) and reflects accurate alignment of portal regions and surrounding tissue across stains. External dataset (portal tracts and hepatic veins)

To assess generalizability, we applied the proposed registration framework to an independent external dataset consisting of H&E–MT liver biopsy sections acquired using different scanners and originating from multiple clinical centers, where variations in tissue processing, staining, and preparation protocols introduce additional sources of heterogeneity. The dataset was annotated with portal tracts and hepatic veins.

Mean pre-registration TRE on the external dataset was 93.4 ± 67.7 μm, reflecting substantial initial misalignment. Following registration, TRE was reduced to 42.2 ± 40.6 μm, corresponding to a reduction of more than 50%. This improvement was statistically significant (paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test, p < 0.001), demonstrating robust landmark alignment on unseen data.

Tissue overlap also improved markedly on the external dataset. Dice similarity increased from 0.61 pre-registration to 0.95 after registration with the proposed framework. This improvement was statistically significant (paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test, p < 0.001) and comparable in magnitude to that observed on internal datasets.

ANHIR public dataset (kidney PAS–MT registration)

To further evaluate the generalizability of the proposed registration framework, we assessed its performance on the publicly available ANHIR challenge dataset. This dataset includes serial kidney sections stained with PAS and MT, providing an independent evaluation of cross-stain registration performance in a different tissue type and staining scenario.

Before registration, the mean TRE between PAS and MT sections was 107.2 μm. Following application of the proposed framework, the mean TRE was reduced to 81.6 μm, corresponding to a statistically significant improvement in landmark alignment (paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test, p = 0.02441).

Tissue-level alignment was also improved after registration. The Dice similarity coefficient increased from 0.90 before registration to 0.97 after applying the proposed framework, representing a statistically significant improvement in tissue overlap (paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test, p < 0.001). These results demonstrate the ability of the proposed method to achieve accurate cross-stain registration beyond the liver datasets used for development and evaluation.

Ablation study of CurvReg components

The ablation study provides insight into the individual contributions of piecewise tissue modeling, DL-based deformable refinement, and mask-based transformations within the proposed framework. Compared with the baseline approach, which applies registration to the intact biopsy without curvature-aware partitioning, piecewise affine registration consistently improved both TRE and Dice across all datasets and stain combinations (Table 2). This effect was most pronounced for curved and irregular biopsy specimens, confirming that explicit handling of local tissue geometry is critical for achieving accurate global alignment in liver histology. Introducing DL-based deformable registration further improved tissue-level alignment, as evidenced by consistent increases in Dice similarity. For example, in the internal multi-stain dataset, Dice increased from 0.90 after affine alignment to 0.95 following deformable refinement, reflecting enhanced boundary conformity and spatial coherence of tissue regions across stains (Wilcoxon signed-rank test, p = 0.0579). In contrast, landmark-based TRE showed minimal change after deformable registration, decreasing by only 0.06 pixels relative to affine alignment (3.17 vs 3.23 pixels; Wilcoxon signed-rank test, p = 0.0726) across 2038 landmarks. An additional factor contributing to the robustness of the proposed framework is that all transformations, both affine and deep learning-based deformable, are applied to tissue masks rather than raw stained images. Although a dedicated experiment was not performed to isolate this effect, consistent improvements in TRE and Dice were observed across multiple datasets, stain combinations, anatomical structures, scanners, and tissue processing and preparation protocols. These results demonstrate that all components are necessary to balance accurate landmark correspondence with robust tissue-level alignment in multi-stain liver biopsy registration.

Comparison and benchmarking

To contextualize the performance of the proposed CurvReg framework within existing multi-stain registration approaches, we further compared our method against two representative state-of-the-art registration methods, including VALIS17 and the learning-based RegWSI approach,18 using Dataset #1, which includes 26 paired H&E- and MT-stained slides comprising 44 tissue cores. VALIS was executed using its standard registration pipeline, with H&E images designated as the reference modality. Under the default configuration, VALIS failed in several cases, primarily due to memory limitations and incorrect feature correspondences arising from repetitive or visually similar tissue structures across slides.

To enable a fair and consistent comparison with CurvReg, we applied two controlled modifications to the VALIS pipeline. First, we harmonized the spatial resolution by providing maximum image dimensions for both rigid and non-rigid registration stages, ensuring a downsampling scale consistent with that used in CurvReg. Second, we incorporated tissue segmentation masks (derived from our pipeline) to exclude non-tissue regions during rigid registration, improving robustness in cases with substantial background variation. These masks were applied after preprocessing and before rigid alignment.

These adjustments improved VALIS performance in the majority of cases; however, two cores remained unresolved due to either out-of-memory constraints or incorrect feature matching between anatomically dissimilar regions. In these instances, VALIS attempted to align structurally unrelated tissue segments across slides, a limitation that could not be mitigated by post-hoc masking because it does not constrain the feature-matching stage itself. Because quantitative comparison and statistical testing require paired measurements from the same set of successfully registered cases, these two cores were excluded from the comparative analysis. Including unsuccessful registrations would require assigning arbitrary error values or treating failed registrations as missing data, which would alter the statistical analysis and confound the comparison of registration accuracy. The final evaluation was therefore performed on the 42 cores successfully registered by both methods. RegWSI was evaluated on the same dataset using the publicly available implementation and default settings.

On the successfully registered subset, CurvReg achieved a TRE of 3.12 ± 3.51 pixels compared with 3.61 ± 6.39 pixels for VALIS (p = 0.091) and 4.51 ± 18.70 pixels for RegWSI (p < 0.0001). Dice similarity coefficients were 0.98 ± 0.02 for CurvReg, 0.97 ± 0.02 for VALIS (p < 0.01), and 0.94 ± 0.07 (p < 0.0001) for RegWSI. Although the improvement in TRE compared with VALIS did not reach statistical significance, CurvReg demonstrated improved tissue-level overlap and more consistent landmark alignment compared with both existing approaches, particularly compared with RegWSI, which exhibited substantially higher variability in registration accuracy. Overall, these results demonstrate the robustness of CurvReg for challenging multi-stain alignment scenarios and highlight its ability to maintain anatomically meaningful correspondence across serial histological sections.

Cross-stain knowledge transfer for portal tract and hepatic vein segmentation

Segmentation performance for portal tracts and hepatic veins was first evaluated independently on H&E and MT slides. For MT, the median Dice score was 0.93 (Q1 = 0.92, Q3 = 0.94), whereas for H&E it was 0.86 (Q1 = 0.72, Q3 = 0.92), reflecting the expected stain-dependent disparity in vascular boundary visibility and a significant advantage of MT over H&E (p < 0.001). After applying CurvReg to transfer MT-derived structural annotations onto the corresponding H&E slides, segmentation accuracy on H&E increased markedly, achieving a median Dice score of 0.92 (Q1 = 0.89, Q3 = 0.93). This improvement was statistically significant relative to the original H&E model performance (p < 0.01), confirming that cross-stain mapping provides a substantial enhancement in portal tract and hepatic vein delineation within the H&E domain.

The lower segmentation performance observed on H&E is primarily attributable to an increased rate of false-positive detections rather than a systematic failure to identify true portal tracts or hepatic veins. This pattern reflects the inherent limitations of H&E staining for vascular assessment: collagen-poor or subtly defined portal regions often appear less distinct, and hepatic vein boundaries may be obscured by parenchymal texture, inflammation, or sectioning artifacts. These ambiguities contribute to poor inter-observer agreement among pathologists and have historically motivated the use of MT as the reference stain for evaluating portal-vascular structures. The substantial improvement observed after transferring MT-derived structural information onto the corresponding H&E slides via CurvReg underscores this point. By anchoring H&E-based predictions to anatomically reliable MT contours, the framework effectively compensates for the reduced boundary visibility in H&E and reduces erroneous over-segmentation. Importantly, these findings confirm that while AI models trained directly on H&E remain vulnerable to stain-specific limitations, cross-stain geometric mapping offers a powerful mechanism to recover accurate structural delineation without requiring MT-level staining for every section. Rather than replacing MT, CurvReg leverages its strengths to enhance H&E interpretation, particularly for subtle or ambiguous portal tracts and hepatic veins, where even expert reviewers often disagree.

Discussion

In this study, we introduced a curvature-aware, piecewise framework for stain-independent registration of histology images, initially developed and evaluated in liver biopsy specimens, combining local affine alignment with deep learning-based deformable refinement. The proposed method achieves robust alignment across diverse stains and imaging conditions while preserving anatomical coherence. Unlike traditional approaches, our framework explicitly accommodates the curved and irregular geometry of liver biopsies and enforces anatomically plausible transformations, thereby reducing sensitivity to stain-specific appearance variations. This robustness enables reliable integration of complementary structural information across stains, supporting downstream tasks such as zonal analysis, fibrosis staging, and vascular-centric quantification in computational pathology workflows. Furthermore, the use of clinically meaningful anatomical landmarks, including bile ducts, portal structures, and hepatic veins, allowed a multiscale and interpretable evaluation of registration accuracy, ensuring that improvements reflect alignment of true tissue structures rather than arbitrary image features. Collectively, these innovations establish a versatile, clinically grounded, and translationally relevant framework for multi-stain liver histology registration.

To guide the reader through the discussion, we first describe the rationale for our choice of evaluation metrics, interpret the contributions of key design elements, and then examine performance across datasets and the implications for downstream clinical workflows.

To ensure that our evaluation reflects both the technical performance and the clinical objectives of multi-stain liver histology registration, careful selection of assessment metrics is essential. A variety of metrics are available for assessing deformable image registration, including region overlap measures (e.g., Dice and Jaccard), boundary-based distances such as the Hausdorff Distance, intensity-based similarities such as Mutual Information or Normalized Cross Correlation, and deformation field descriptors such as Jacobian determinant statistics or smoothness measures.36 Each metric captures distinct aspects of alignment: overlap metrics reflect areal or volumetric consistency, boundary distances emphasize local edge conformity, intensity similarities quantify global correspondence without requiring labels, and deformation field metrics assess physical plausibility rather than anatomical accuracy.37, 38, 39, 40, 41 Given these complementary but fundamentally different perspectives, the choice of evaluation metrics must be tailored to the imaging modality and the objectives of the registration task.

Guided by these considerations and the clinical requirements of liver biopsy analysis, we adopted a combination of TRE and Dice similarity coefficient for evaluating multi-stain registration performance. This choice reflects the dual structure of our pipeline, consisting of an affine stage followed by deep learning-based deformable refinement, as well as the availability of numerous anatomical landmarks across stains. TRE quantifies pointwise correspondence between sparse landmarks and is therefore well-suited to evaluating global alignment achieved by the affine stage. Our ablation results show that once the affine transformation has aligned key anatomical structures, further reductions in TRE are limited, explaining the minimal change observed following deformable refinement. In contrast, Dice captures tissue-level overlap and is sensitive to local deformations, boundary conformity, and fine-scale geometry, attributes critical for mapping heterogeneous structures across stains. Consequently, the deformable model substantially increases Dice even when TRE remains unchanged, underscoring its role in refining anatomically plausible, spatially coherent alignment that landmark-based metrics alone cannot capture. Together, TRE and Dice provide a balanced and task-appropriate evaluation of both global and local registration performance in multi-stain liver biopsy alignment.

The mask-based representation is a key component of this behavior. By applying both affine and deformable transformations to tissue masks rather than raw stained images, the method reduces sensitivity to staining intensity variations, scanner-dependent color profiles, and inconsistencies in manual landmark annotations. This structural representation encourages the model to focus on tissue geometry and anatomical boundaries rather than stain-specific appearance cues. The consistent performance observed across internal and external datasets, diverse stain and tissue combinations, and multiple anatomical structures supports the effectiveness of this design choice, enabling stain-independent and anatomically coherent registration without requiring explicit stain normalization or stain-specific preprocessing. When combined with the affine-deformable design, this representation further enhances the model's ability to generalize across heterogeneous histology sources and staining protocols.

Although the present study primarily focused on liver biopsy histology, the proposed framework was intentionally designed to rely on tissue-level geometric representations rather than organ-specific microscopic morphology. Specifically, deformable refinement is estimated from binary tissue masks that encode global tissue structure and biopsy geometry, without direct dependence on histological features such as portal tracts, arteries, and bile duct appearance. Consequently, the method does not learn tissue-specific morphological features in the conventional sense, but instead models anatomically plausible spatial deformations between serial sections. Liver tissue was selected primarily because core needle biopsies present substantial geometric challenges, including elongated curved morphology, local tearing, and non-rigid sectioning distortions that complicate whole-slide registration. Importantly, evaluation on the publicly available ANHIR kidney biopsy dataset using PAS–MT stained sections demonstrated that the proposed geometry-driven strategy can generalize beyond liver tissue and across distinct stain combinations. Nevertheless, further validation on additional tissue types with diverse architectural organization, such as colon, prostate, or lung, represents an important future direction for further assessing the breadth of applicability of this registration framework.

We next examine the performance of CurvReg across different datasets to evaluate its technical robustness and clinical relevance. Across all evaluated datasets, the proposed framework consistently improved both landmark correspondence and tissue-level overlap. On Dataset #1, registration markedly reduced landmark-based error while increasing tissue overlap, demonstrating reliable global alignment and accurate refinement of local tissue geometry across stains. These results confirm that integrating curvature-aware piecewise affine alignment with deep learning-based deformable refinement effectively addresses the geometric variability of serial liver biopsies while preserving structurally meaningful relationships across diverse tissue configurations.

Performance gains were not limited to small ductal structures. Evaluation on Dataset #2 demonstrated that the framework generalizes to larger and more heterogeneous anatomical regions, including portal tracts and hepatic veins, which span broader spatial extents and exhibit greater inter-biopsy variability. Maintaining low landmark error while achieving high tissue-level overlap across multiple anatomical scales underscores the versatility of the framework and supports its applicability to clinically relevant regions involved in fibrosis staging, portal tract remodeling, and overall liver architecture.

To assess generalizability beyond the internal cohort, we evaluated CurvReg on an external dataset acquired using different scanners and originating from multiple clinical centers. Despite scanner-specific appearance variations and inter-institutional differences in tissue processing, staining, and imaging conditions, the framework achieved improvements in landmark correspondence and tissue-level overlap comparable to those observed internally. Furthermore, evaluation on the independent ANHIR kidney biopsy dataset demonstrated that the proposed geometry-driven registration strategy extends beyond liver tissue and remains effective across a distinct tissue type and stain combination (PAS–MT). This performance stability highlights the effectiveness of the stain-independent representation and suggests that the method can support reliable multi-stain alignment across diverse clinical and technical environments without requiring tissue-specific training or stain-specific tuning.

A key clinical takeaway of CurvReg is that it transforms multi-stain interpretation from a subjective, cognition-dependent visual matching task into a geometrically consistent and automated alignment problem. In routine pathology, clinicians frequently compare corresponding structures across serial sections stained with different modalities, relying on mental estimation of spatial relationships between tissue components. However, these comparisons are often limited by curvature, fragmentation, and non-rigid distortions introduced during tissue sectioning and staining. This challenge is particularly important in liver pathology, where progressive fibrosis and architectural distortion can make the identification of small anatomical structures across stains difficult. For example, in chronic biliary disease, loss of small bile ducts may be identified on CK7 immunostaining but requires correlation with the corresponding H&E or trichrome stain to confirm evaluation of a properly oriented portal tract. In current workflows, pathologists often alternate repeatedly between stains or manually realign images during dual-screen review, making interpretation both time-consuming and cognitively demanding.

By explicitly reducing spatial misalignment between corresponding anatomical structures across stains, CurvReg enables more direct and reliable visual correspondence between shared tissue regions. This improves the ability to track the same biological structures across different staining contexts, supporting more consistent interpretation of tissue architecture, lesion boundaries, and microanatomical organization. Although primarily developed and evaluated in liver histology, the successful application of CurvReg to the independent ANHIR kidney biopsy dataset demonstrates that this geometry-driven strategy can extend beyond a single organ system and support broader multi-stain digital pathology workflows. Beyond visual interpretation, improved cross-stain alignment provides a foundation for downstream quantitative applications, including stain transfer, automated biomarker analysis, and integrated assessment of complementary histological information across serial sections.

Whereas the proposed framework demonstrates robust performance across diverse stains, anatomical structures, and imaging conditions, several limitations highlight opportunities for continued refinement. First, the current implementation relies on a threshold-based tissue detection strategy for registration and a tile-level intensity filter for segmentation tile extraction. In this work, we adopted Otsu-based segmentation primarily as a simple, fully interpretable, and annotation-free baseline to isolate tissue regions without requiring additional labeled data. Given the focus of this study on the registration framework itself, this choice allowed us to avoid introducing a separate learned segmentation model and its associated training complexity, thereby maintaining a clear separation between preprocessing and the core registration methodology. Although both approaches performed well across all datasets, thresholding methods and intensity heuristics can be sensitive to scanner variability, staining fluctuations, and tissue preparation artifacts. Future work could explore unified or fully learning-based tissue detection models, or joint segmentation–registration architectures, to further enhance robustness under challenging imaging conditions.

Second, the framework requires limited user input to identify curvature points that anchor the piecewise affine initialization. Whereas this interaction typically requires less than 30 s per slide, semi-automated steps introduce observer variability and limit full automation. Future iterations could incorporate automated curvature detection, landmark-suggestion networks, or shape-driven optimization to eliminate manual input and improve reproducibility. Automation will become particularly important as the method scales to larger datasets or is applied in high-throughput digital pathology workflows.

Third, although the method was evaluated across two internal datasets, an external liver biopsy cohort, and an independent kidney biopsy dataset from the ANHIR challenge, broader validation across additional organs and tissue types with distinct structural and staining characteristics will be important to further confirm the generalizability of the registration framework. In particular, whereas evaluation on kidney tissue demonstrated the potential of the geometry-driven approach beyond liver histology, larger multi-organ studies will be required to fully characterize its robustness across diverse tissue architectures and stain combinations. The PT/HV segmentation models were developed and validated specifically for liver histology and will require dedicated evaluation in other anatomical contexts. Furthermore, the stain-dependent performance gap observed between MT and H&E highlights the broader challenge of cross-stain structural transfer and motivates future investigation of how complementary staining modalities can be integrated for quantitative analysis across different tissues.

Looking ahead, several promising research directions emerge from this work. One avenue involves deeper integration of aligned multi-stain images with downstream quantitative pathology tasks, such as automated fibrosis assessment, zonation-based spatial analysis, and feature fusion for disease staging. In addition, the demonstrated benefit of transferring high-fidelity vascular structures from MT to H&E suggests further potential for cross-stain knowledge transfer, including for other histological features where stain-specific contrast varies.

Another important direction focuses on robustness and scalability in real-world clinical conditions. Future work will focus on improving robustness under challenging histological conditions, including severe tissue tearing, folding, fragmentation, missing tissue regions, and substantial inter-section structural variability. Although the curvature-aware piecewise framework was specifically designed to improve tolerance to irregular biopsy geometry and demonstrated stable performance across heterogeneous external datasets and an independent kidney biopsy benchmark, extreme cases with limited structural correspondence may still present difficulties for deformable alignment. Incorporating artifact-aware quality control, automated failure detection, and adaptive fragment-level registration strategies may further enhance reliability in highly degraded clinical specimens and large-scale pathology workflows. Finally, because multiple serial sections in routine practice are stained with complementary histochemical and immunohistochemical markers, the proposed registration framework provides a foundation for 3D tissue reconstruction. Applying CurvReg across serial sections could enable volumetric mapping of ductal and vascular networks, inter-slide continuity analysis, and richer structural quantification not accessible from single-section histology.
