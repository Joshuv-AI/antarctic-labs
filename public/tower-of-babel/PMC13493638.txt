Effectiveness of Botulinum Toxin Type A for Trichodynia Associated with Hair Loss Disorders: A Retrospective Study
Authors: David Saceda-Corralo, Adrián Imbernon-Moya, Juan Jiménez-Cauhe, Cristina Pindado-Ortega, Valentina Balaguera-Orjuela, Patricia Burgos-Blasco, Amparo Guillem, Rocío Gil-Redondo, Sergio Vañó-Galván
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionTrichodynia significantly impairs quality of life in patients with hair loss, for which effective treatments remain limited. This study evaluates the use of intradermal botulinum toxin type A (BTA) in patients with trichodynia unresponsive to conventional therapies.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 21 patients with trichodynia and with alopecia who had discontinued or refused systemic treatments. BTA was administered using the Nappage technique. Symptom severity and disease activity were assessed using the Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) and the Lichen Planopilaris Activity Index (LPPAI), respectively.ResultsMost patients (90.5%) reported symptomatic improvement within 18.3 days on average. Mean VAS scores declined from 8.0 to 2.0 at 3 months and reached 0.0 by 6 months. In cicatricial alopecia, LPPAI scores showed significant reduction. Treatment was well tolerated, with only minor adverse effects.DiscussionThe clinical improvement observed may be explained by the neuromodulatory effects of BTA on neurogenic inflammation, including inhibition of substance P and CGRP release. Notably, patients with inflammatory scarring alopecias showed a more pronounced response than those with noncicatricial alopecias, suggesting that BTA may be particularly beneficial in conditions where pain is linked to active perifollicular inflammation.ConclusionsIntradermal BTA appears to be a safe and potentially effective treatment for trichodynia, particularly in patients with scarring alopecia who are not candidates for systemic therapy. These findings warrant further validation in controlled studies.

Introduction

Trichodynia is a painful sensation of the scalp in the context of hair loss [1], also referred to as “hair pain,” and is considered part of the broader spectrum of scalp sensitivity [2]. It typically manifests or is exacerbated when the hair is moved and may be described as an unpleasant sensation of burning, stinging, or discomfort of the scalp [3]. The main triggers that have been proposed include substance P, psychological comorbidities, perifollicular inflammation, and nutritional deficiencies. The most frequently observed biological mechanisms associated with trichodynia are the increased expression of neurokinin-1 receptor and nerve growth factor in affected patients, supporting the hypothesis that these mediators may contribute to the development of alopecia through nociceptive pathways [4]. While trichodynia was initially linked to telogen effluvium, it has subsequently been reported in association with other types of alopecia.

This symptom significantly impairs quality of life in patients experiencing hair loss. It may alter mood and, when chronically present, contribute to the development of depression or anxiety [5].

Moreover, a systematic review evaluating health-related quality of life (HRQOL) in adults with primary cicatricial alopecia and its subtypes reported that, in studies using the Dermatology Life Quality Index (DLQI), more than 70% of patients presented with impaired quality of life [6]. Importantly, trichodynia was the only clinical variable significantly associated with worse HRQOL (χ2 = 0.508, P = 0.04), whereas the remaining clinical parameters showed no significant correlation with the total DLQI score. Although trichodynia is relatively infrequent in frontal fibrosing alopecia (FFA), affecting approximately 20% of patients, it was identified as the main factor associated with reduced HRQOL compared with other clinical features. Therefore, effective control of this symptom may improve HRQOL in patients with FFA and help prevent further psychological distress [7].

Despite its clinical relevance, available treatment options are scarce and often yield inconsistent or unsatisfactory results. [3]

The treatment of trichodynia has been scarcely addressed, and a specific therapeutic algorithm has not been established. However, based on the possible pathophysiological mechanisms involved, and given its association with various types of alopecia as well as certain psychiatric disorders, several therapeutic options may be considered. These include antiinflammatory agents (such as corticosteroids, calcineurin inhibitors, capsaicin, platelet-rich plasma [PRP], and topical anesthetics), systemic therapies (including oral l-cysteine preparations), antidepressants (venlafaxine and amitriptyline), neuromodulators (pregabalin and gabapentin), substance P inhibitors, analgesics, antihistamines, and beta-blockers such as propranolol [5, 7].

Some authors have explored the management of trichodynia with botulinum toxin. Faris A. Alhomida reported the successful off-label use of botulinum toxin type A (BTA) in treating a patient with trichodynia who had failed conventional therapies [8]. Trimboli et al. were the first to use BTA for trichodynia treatment, administering a dose of 200 units and finding significant improvement. Moreover, these collective results indicate that BTA may exert a prolonged therapeutic effect when administered in repeated courses approximately every 3 months. Therefore, BTA represents a promising treatment option for patients suffering from trichodynia [9].

This study aimed to explore the therapeutic potential of BTA for trichodynia unresponsive to conventional therapies in patients with different hair loss conditions.

Methods

We designed a retrospective study to assess the effectiveness and safety of intradermal BTA in patients with trichodynia associated with different hair disorders who declined or discontinued systemic oral therapies included neuromodulatory agents such as gabapentin and pregabalin (gabapentinoids), which were prescribed for symptomatic control. Regarding topical therapy, patients were allowed to continue maintenance treatment consisting of clobetasol once weekly and pimecrolimus cream twice weekly.

It was provided to patients as an off-label therapy that was effective for migraines and hyperhidrosis. They did not pay for the therapy.

Importantly, both treatments had been used at the same frequency for at least 2 years prior to study inclusion, with no recent modifications. The local IRB (Institutional Review Board—Hospital Ramón y Cajal) approved this study, approval number 193/25. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki of 1964 and its later amendments. Owing to the retrospective nature of the study, the requirement for written informed consent was waived by the Institutional Review Board of Hospital Ramón y Cajal.

The diagnosis of trichodynia was established through a detailed and comprehensive anamnesis in each patient, assessing the onset of symptoms, the presence of any identifiable triggers, symptom duration, and associated manifestations as reported by the patients. In most cases, trichodynia presented as a painful sensation in the scalp, frequently associated with certain types of alopecia. This pain could occur diffusely across the entire scalp or be localized to specific areas. Trichodynia was often triggered or exacerbated by hair movement and was sometimes described as an unpleasant burning, stinging, or discomforting sensation in the scalp. The diagnostic process was complemented by physical examination and trichoscopic evaluation, which are essential for identifying underlying alopecias.

A vial of 100 units of BTA was reconstituted by dissolution in 5 mL of 0.9% saline solution, that provides a concentration of 2 units of BTA in every 0.1 mL of the resulting solution. Intradermal BTA injections were performed as mesotherapy using the Nappage technique, after cleaning the area with a chlorhexidine solution, which consists of intradermal injections of 0.05 ml solution (1 unit of BTA per injection) at a 0.5 cm to 1 cm interval at an angle of 45°. We used a vibration anesthesia device to reduce the pain during the procedure. Injections were placed in areas where patients reported the most intense trichodynia, both spontaneously and upon palpation, as well as in a peripheral zone extending 2–5 cm beyond the affected region. Following the procedure, patients were allowed to resume normal daily activities and were instructed to avoid topical treatments in the treated areas for 24 h. Treatment was administered at baseline (time 0). Patients were subsequently reevaluated at predefined follow-up visits (3, 6, and 9 months). If symptoms persisted or recurred, an additional treatment session was offered. The number of sessions and individual patient responses are detailed in the results section.

The grade of severity of trichodynia in all patients was assessed using the Visual Analogue Scale (VAS). Scores were based on self-reported symptoms, recorded with a single handwritten mark placed on a 10-cm line, representing a continuum from “no pain” on the left end (0) to “worst pain” on the right end (10). The disease activity was assessed with the Lichen Planopilaris Activity Index (LPPAI) [10] for all the included cicatricial alopecias. Changes in VAS and LPPAI scores over time (baseline, 3, 6, and 9 months) were analyzed using the Friedman test for repeated measures. When appropriate, pairwise comparisons between visits were performed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test with Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons. Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided P value < 0.05.

Results

We included 21 patients (18 women and 3 men) with a mean age 44 years (range 35–69 years). Diagnoses included lichen planopilaris (LPP; n = 6), frontal fibrosing alopecia (FFA; n = 3), FFA/LPP overlap (n = 5), fibrosing alopecia in a pattern distribution (FAPD; n = 1), FFA/FAPD overlap (n = 3), female pattern hair loss (FPHL) (n = 2), and chronic telogen effluvium (n = 1). The mean BTA dose was 81.3 units (range 20–130), applied to the parietal (85.7%), frontal (71.4%), crown (57.1%), temporal (9.5%), and occipital (9.5%) regions. Discussion

BTA is approved for the prevention of chronic migraine. BTA acts by inhibiting the release of acetylcholine at the neuromuscular junction through cleavage of synaptosome associated protein 25 (SNAP-25), a protein essential for synaptic vesicle fusion. In migraine, its therapeutic effect extends beyond muscle relaxation and involves modulation of peripheral and central pain pathways. By reducing the release of pain mediators such as CGRP, substance P, and glutamate from sensory terminals, BTA decreases peripheral sensitization and neurogenic inflammation within the trigeminovascular system [11].

Recently, multiple studies have investigated the use of botulinum toxin for the management of hair and scalp disorders. A recent review critically evaluated the available clinical literature, examining the efficacy, safety, cost-effectiveness, and overall clinical utility of botulinum toxin in this context. The review identified 40 articles encompassing a total of 689 patients, 79.5% of whom presented with hair loss, treated with 25–200 units of either BTA or type B [12]. Most studies demonstrated a beneficial effect against different types of nonscarring alopecia. Beyond possible interpretations of its efficacy in this area, the studies demonstrated a good safety profile on the scalp. No serious or transient adverse effects, such as alopecia or effluvium, were detected after treatment. Despite this relatively high number of studies on hair loss, the reports focused on trichodynia. BTA injections are commonly administered for chronic migraine following the PREEMPT protocol, which consists of 31 standardized injection sites delivering a total dose of 155–200 U, with 5 U injected per site. Additional “follow-the-pain” injections may be administered in areas where patients report maximal pain [13, 14]. While the precise mechanisms of BTA’S analgesic effects remain unclear, evidence suggests they are primarily peripheral. BTA inhibits the release of neuropeptides implicated in neurogenic inflammation, including calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP), glutamate, and substance P (SP), and reduces expression and trafficking of nociceptive ion channels and receptors, such as transient receptor potential vanilloid receptor 1 (TRPV1) and purinergic receptor (P2X3), in trigeminovascular neurons. By modulating pericranial and cervical nerves, BTA may reduce allodynia and peripheral-to-central sensitization, potentially explaining its therapeutic effects in other cranial neuralgias and headache disorders, including cluster headache, trigeminal neuralgia, and nummular headache [15, 16].

We hypothesize that BTA may also alleviate trichodynia by exerting neuromodulatory effects on SP and CGRP, both of which are critical mediators of neurogenic inflammation. SP has been shown to induce mast cell degranulation and premature catagen in human scalp follicles, while CGRP promotes vasodilation and inflammatory cell recruitment [17]. BTA inhibits the release of both neuropeptides from peripheral nerve endings, potentially disrupting this pathogenic loop and restoring scalp homeostasis (Fig. 2). This hypothesis may be especially important in LPP and FFA, where neurogenic inflammation has been proposed as a potential mechanism of action. An important aspect of our findings is the differential clinical response observed between patients with primary cicatricial alopecias (PCA) and those with noncicatricial alopecias. While BTA demonstrated a notable improvement in pain symptoms among patients with inflammatory scarring alopecias, the therapeutic effect was less pronounced in noncicatricial cases. This distinction is clinically relevant, as pain in PCA is closely linked to active inflammation and perifollicular immune-mediated damage, whereas scalp discomfort in nonscarring alopecias—often referred to as trichodynia—may involve more complex and heterogeneous mechanisms, including neurogenic or psychosomatic components. Therefore, our results suggest that BTA may be particularly promising as a therapeutic option for pain associated with inflammatory scarring alopecias, rather than for trichodynia occurring in noncicatricial conditions. Further controlled studies with larger cohorts are warranted to better delineate these differential responses and clarify the underlying mechanisms.

Interestingly, trichoscopic features included in the LPPAI, such as perifollicular erythema and scaling, also tended to improve after BTA treatment. These findings suggest that BTA may have a beneficial role in the inflammatory pathogenesis of PCA.

We identified some limitations in our study. First, the retrospective design, which inherently carries a higher risk of data loss. Nevertheless, the use of clinical rating scales and photographic documentation of trichoscopic findings allowed for an objective assessment of each case. Another limitation is the lack of a placebo-controlled group, which is undoubtedly the next step before confirming the efficacy of this treatment. However, the clinical response among patients was remarkably consistent, with noticeable improvement observed within 2–3 weeks in all responsive cases, making spontaneous resolution of the condition an unlikely explanation. In addition, concomitant medications taken by the patients may have influenced the clinical outcomes, representing another source of bias. Finally, the small sample size must be acknowledged. Although the number of cases is limited, the condition under study is relatively uncommon compared with more prevalent disorders (e.g., androgenetic alopecia). Therefore, we consider the sample to be representative for an exploratory study of this nature.

Conclusions

Our findings suggest that BTA may be a promising therapy for trichodynia, particularly in patients with cicatricial alopecia. It offers a valuable therapeutic option for patients who either decline or do not tolerate conventional systemic treatments, and it merits further investigation in larger, controlled studies.
