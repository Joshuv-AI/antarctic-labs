Pediatric pulmonary tuberculosis masquerading as chest neoplasms
Authors: Fathia Omer Salah, Yodit Abraham Yaynishet, Abubeker Fedlu, Elham Sani, Gashaw Arega, Samuel Sisay Hailu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTuberculosis, a highly contagious airborne infection caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis, can affect any organ system, although the lungs are most commonly involved. It continues to be a major cause of morbidity and mortality among children and adolescents, particularly in low- and middle-income countries. Diagnosing tuberculosis in Sub-Saharan Africa remains challenging because of its diverse clinical manifestations, atypical presentations, varying disease forms, and limited access to diagnostic resources. This case series highlights the occurrence of large pulmonary mass-like opacities due to M tuberculosis in children that can closely mimic neoplastic lesions. The pseudotumoral presentation of pulmonary tuberculosis is a rare but clinically significant form that resembles lung neoplasms and poses a considerable diagnostic challenge, particularly in pediatric patients. We report 7 cases of pseudotumoral pulmonary tuberculosis in children presenting with varying clinical manifestations. In each case, a pulmonary mass-like opacity was identified on chest radiography or computed tomography imaging and was subsequently confirmed as pulmonary tuberculosis. Diagnosing tuberculosis in children remains particularly difficult, especially in resource-limited settings. In endemic regions, maintaining a high level of clinical suspicion and correlating clinical findings with radiologic, laboratory, and histopathologic investigations are essential for timely and accurate diagnosis. Early recognition and prompt treatment of tuberculosis are crucial not only for reducing morbidity and mortality but also for preventing disease transmission.Keywords: Mycobacterium tuberculosis, Pseudo tumoral tuberculosis, Lung mass, GeneXpert

Introduction

According to the World Health Organization estimates, in 2021, around 10.6 million people became ill with tuberculosis, which resulted in a total of 1.6 million deaths from the disease [1]. Pulmonary tuberculosis remains a primary cause of death and illness, particularly in the developing world. The radiographic patterns of pulmonary tuberculosis are diverse, including parenchymal consolidation, miliary opacities, lymphadenopathies, and pleural effusion. Notably, lymphadenopathy is more common in pediatric patients as opposed to consolidation and pleural effusion, which is common presentations in adults. The manifestation of tuberculosis as a significant lung mass is unusual in pediatric patients [2]. Imaging findings of pseudo-tumor pulmonary TB can be easily confused with those of lung cancer, particularly in adults, diagnoses are often delayed, potentially leading to unnecessary surgical resection.

We present 7 cases of pulmonary tuberculosis initially presenting as primary lung masses. All cases were pathology-confirmed and demonstrated mass-like opacities on imaging at presentation. Although radiological findings suggested tuberculosis in some instances, atypical features created diagnostic challenges. Ultimately, all cases were confirmed as pulmonary tuberculosis on histopathology.

Case 1

A 12-year-old boy presented with a 5-month history of chronic cough and anorexia. Based on sputum examination and chest radiographic findings, he was initially diagnosed with pulmonary tuberculosis and started on antituberculous therapy. However, after 2 months of treatment, repeat chest radiography showed no significant radiological improvement, prompting referral to our center for further evaluation.

On presentation, his general physical examination and baseline laboratory investigations were unremarkable. Chest radiography (Fig. 1), followed by contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) of the thorax (Fig. 2), revealed a dense mass-like air-space opacity in the left lung, raising suspicion of a malignant lesion.

PA chest radiograph taken at the time of arrival, showing left para-cardiac homogeneous opacity silhouetting the left cardiac border (white arrow).

Axial non contrast chest CT at time of arrival showing homogeneous opacity on the lingual segment of the lung.

Further evaluation with CT-guided fine-needle aspiration cytology and core needle biopsy demonstrated granulomatous inflammation with a necrotic background. In addition, GeneXpert testing detected Mycobacterium tuberculosis, confirming the diagnosis of pulmonary tuberculosis. Anti-tuberculous therapy was therefore continued.

Follow-up CT imaging performed after completion of treatment (Fig. 3) demonstrated persistence of the pulmonary opacity, although the patient showed marked clinical improvement, including weight gain and complete resolution of symptoms. Given the persistent radiological abnormality, repeat fine-needle aspiration cytology was performed and again yielded findings consistent with tuberculosis.

Contrast enhanced chest CT scan in soft tissue (upper row) and lung window (lower row) showing lobulated heterogeneously enhancing dense air space opacity in the lingual segment with small foci area of central necrosis (A-C; white arrow); separate left lower lobe peripherally located opacity (A, red arrow). There are also multiple lower lobe trees in bud nodules (E, green arrow).

Anti-tuberculous therapy was subsequently extended for an additional 3 months. The patient continued to demonstrate sustained clinical improvement throughout the extended treatment period, despite delayed radiological resolution relative to the clinical response (Fig. 4, Fig. 5).

Contrast enhanced chest CT scan done 6 months after completing treatment shows reduction of the air space opacities (white arrow).

Follow up chest CT scan almost 2 year after initial CXR, shows significant reduction (white arrow).

Case 2

A 5-year-old boy presented with a 1-month history of low-grade intermittent fever, weight loss, and a progressively worsening dry cough. He had no history of night sweats or known contact with a TB patient. Initially, he was investigated with a Chest X-ray and Chest CT scan at a referring medical center and was referred to our center with a suspected diagnosis of Pleuropulmonary blastoma.

During the physical examination at the hospital, the child was not febrile, maintained normal oxygen saturation in room air, and his growth measurements were within normal ranges. A chest examination revealed decreased air entry in the right posterior third of the lung field and stony dullness upon percussion. Laboratory tests showed a white blood cell count of 19,100/mm3 (Neutrophils-56.5% and Lymphocytes-29.4%), Hemoglobin at 11.0 g/dL, Hematocrit at 34.8%, MCV at 72.7, and a platelet count of 452,000/mm3. His Erythrocyte Sedimentation Rate (ESR) was 120 mm/h. GeneXpert tests on sputum and gastric aspirate yielded no remarkable findings. Chest ultrasound revealed consolidation in the right mid-lung zone, and abdominal ultrasound was unrevealing. A Chest CT scan (Fig. 6) displayed dense air space opacity in the right upper and middle lung, measuring 8 × 7 cm, with an enhancing vessel traversing. Additionally, multiple millet-sized nodules adjacent to this mass-like consolidation and in the right basal lung were noted. An ultrasound-guided core biopsy indicated caseous necrosis, suggestive of tuberculosis. The child began anti-TB treatment and showed significant improvement.

Contrast enhanced chest CT showing right lobe heterogeneously enchaining mass like consolidation (red arrow) and adjacent small tree-in-bud nodules.

Case 3

A 7-year-old girl presented with a year-long history of low-grade intermittent fever, loss of appetite, and weight loss. However, she had no history of coughing or contact with chronic cougher or TB-diagnosed patients. Her symptoms worsened over 2 weeks, including shortness of breath when lying down, audible breathing during sleep, and frequent night-time awakenings. At the referring hospital, she received intranasal oxygen and intravenous dexamethasone, considering a diagnosis of superior mediastinal syndrome. She was then referred to our center, suspected of having an anterior mediastinal mass secondary to lymphoma.

Upon her arrival at our hospital, her physical examination was unremarkable. Her complete blood count was normal, and the ESR was 45 mm/h. An abdominal ultrasound revealed multiple mesenteric lymph nodes. A Chest CT scan (Fig. 7) displayed an ill-defined homogeneous opacity in the right apical lung with air bronchograms, along with multiple centrally necrotic mediastinal nodes in the right hilar. Based on these findings, a radiological diagnosis of tuberculosis was made. Gene Xpert testing of gastric aspirate confirmed mycobacterium tuberculosis. The child began anti-TB RHZE treatment. After 2 months of intensive treatment, GeneXpert testing was repeated and returned negative for TB, and she continued with the continuation phase of anti-TB treatment. The child is in her first year of treatment completion and is doing well.

Contrast enhanced chest CT (A) lung window (B and C) mediastinal window axial and coronal images, showing right apical lung lobe ill-defined heterogeneous opacity with few necrotic area (white arrow).

Case 4

A 6-month-old female infant presented with a nonwhooping, nonbarking dry cough and low-grade intermittent fever lasting a month. Despite being treated with various oral antibiotics, there was no improvement. She underwent a Chest X-ray (Fig. 8) and Chest CT scan, leading to her referral to our center with a suspected diagnosis of a right lung mass, potentially due to pleuropulmonary blastoma.

Frontal chest X-ray showing right upper lung opacity with internal lucency and associated bulging of the minor fissure.

At our hospital, the infant was found to be febrile with a temperature of 38.3°C, tachycardic, tachypneic, and maintaining an oxygen saturation of 90% in room air. Her growth measurements were normal. Examination of the chest revealed decreased air entry over two-thirds of the right lung zones. Her complete blood count and organ function tests were normal, and an HIV test was negative. Abdominal ultrasound showed a liver of normal size with a homogeneous echo pattern and hypoechoic splenic lesions. GeneXpert testing of gastric aspirate confirmed the diagnosis. Further investigation into the child’s history revealed that her mother was diagnosed with pulmonary TB during late pregnancy and had received anti-TB treatment. The infant was started on anti-TB treatment, completed the course, and has since thrived well.

Case 5

A 12-year-old male adolescent presented with a swelling in the left anterior chest that had persisted for 8 months. Four months after the onset, the skin over the swelling turned erythematous. Subsequently, an incision was made at a local hospital, where pus was drained, he was given unspecified oral antibiotics. Following a chest X-ray and Chest CT scan, he was referred to our hospital, suspected of having a malignant lung mass.

Upon arrival at our hospital, He was in a comfortable state with stable vital signs. He had bilateral submandibular lymph nodes measuring 1 × 2 cm and a left axillary lymph node measuring 2 × 2 cm, both of which were firm, mobile, and nontender. During the chest examination, a 2 × 3 cm healing scar was noted over the anterior parasternal area (Fig. 9)

Chest CT imaging (Fig. 10) demonstrated an ill-defined, heterogeneous air-space opacity in the anterior segment of the upper lobe, measuring 6.4 × 4.6 cm at its greatest dimension, with a peripheral rim of calcification. Adjacent left hilar necrotic lymphadenopathy was noted, along with multiple small centrilobular nodules.

On the lung window (C), there are multiple tree-in-bud opacities seen adjacent to the opacity (red-arrow).

Evaluation of the included upper abdomen (Fig. 11) revealed 2 hypodense hepatic lesions, the largest measuring 2.6 × 2.8 cm in liver. Based on the clinical presentation and radiological findings, a diagnosis of tuberculosis was suspected and subsequently confirmed by fine-needle aspiration cytology, which demonstrated caseating granulomatous inflammation consistent with tuberculosis.

Pre and postcontrast abdominal image of the same child, slightly hypodense lesion is noted at the liver that has rim enhancement (white arrow, B).

The patient was treated with antituberculous therapy, completed the prescribed course without complications, and remained clinically well on follow-up.

Case 6

A 4-year and 10-month-old female child presented with a dry intermittent cough, high-grade fever, and significant weight loss for 2 months. She was fully vaccinated and had no known contact with a TB-diagnosed patient. Despite being treated with oral antibiotics, there was no improvement. During the physical examination, it was noted that her liver extended 3 cm below the costal margin, with a total liver span of 9 cm, and respiratory findings indicated decreased air entry on the right side. Her complete blood count indicated mild iron deficiency anemia, and her ESR was 120 mm/h.

Chest radiography demonstrated a right-sided pleural effusion along with paratracheal and hilar lymphadenopathy. Contrast-enhanced CT of the thorax (Fig. 12) revealed a large, dense air-space opacity of right middle lobe. The lesion appeared homogeneous, with multiple vessels coursing through it. In addition, large bulky right hilar, paratracheal and subcarinal lymph nodes with hypoattenuating centers and peripheral rim enhancement were identified, consistent with necrotic mediastinal lymphadenopathy.

Postcontrast axial (A), coronal soft tissue and lung window chest CT (B and C) showing multiple subcarinal, hilar and paracardiac rim enhancing lymph nodes with central necrosis (blue arrow), additional findings were right-sided mass-like consolidation (black arrow) with bilateral nodules (red arrow).

Multiple randomly distributed pulmonary nodules were noted adjacent to the opacity as well as within the contralateral lung. Given the clinical and radiological findings, pulmonary tuberculosis was suspected. GeneXpert testing subsequently detected M tuberculosis, confirming the diagnosis. The patient was commenced on anti-tuberculous therapy and demonstrated significant clinical improvement during follow-up.

Case 7

A 6-year-old male presented with a nonwhooping, nonbarking dry cough that had persisted for a month. Accompanying symptoms included weight loss, poor appetite, low-grade fever, and night sweating. Despite taking antibiotics from a referring hospital, there was no improvement. On physical examination, his vital signs and anthropometric measurements were normal. However, respiratory examination revealed decreased air entry in the left upper half of the chest. His complete blood count showed moderate anemia with a hemoglobin level of 8.3 g/dL and an elevated ESR of 170. A chest X-ray (CXR) indicated left upper lobe opacity with contralateral mediastinal shift and an ill-defined nodule in the right middle lung. A postcontrast CT scan revealed a large, lobulated 11.6 cm dense air space opacity in the left upper lobe. This opacity obstructed the left upper lobar and lingular bronchi, leading to atelectasis of the lingula. Adjacent pulmonary vessels were encased and narrowed, with multiple pulmonary nodules present in the left lower lobe and right lung, the largest measuring 1.5 cm. (Fig. 13). Pulmonary tuberculosis was considered a differential diagnosis, after which a GeneXpert test was conducted and returned a positive result. He was stared on antituberculosis and did very well on follow up.

Discussion

Tuberculosis (TB) remains a significant global health concern, with approximately 12% of affected individuals belonging to the pediatric age group [3]. Patients with tuberculosis may present with a broad spectrum of clinical manifestations. While some individuals remain completely asymptomatic, others exhibit nonspecific symptoms, making it difficult to distinguish TB from other common illnesses based solely on clinical findings [4].

Early diagnosis of tuberculosis is essential to prevent potential complications, particularly in the pediatric population, with special emphasis on children younger than 1 year of age [5]. Diagnosing TB in children is especially challenging because culture yields are significantly lower than those observed in adults, often ranging between 30% and 40%. Consequently, imaging plays a vital role in the diagnosis of pediatric tuberculosis [6].

Plain chest radiography is a crucial initial imaging modality, particularly in clinical settings where patients fail to respond to antibiotic therapy [7]. However, CT has proven superior in detecting mediastinal lymphadenopathy, which is considered a hallmark of primary pediatric TB, regardless of the presence or absence of parenchymal abnormalities. Typical postcontrast CT findings of tuberculous lymphadenopathy include peripheral rim enhancement with central caseous necrosis. The subcarinal and paratracheal lymph node groups are most commonly affected. Additional findings, such as disseminated nodular lesions, are also frequently observed [5,6,8].

Atypical presentations of tuberculosis in children, such as mass-like consolidations, are relatively uncommon. These pseudotumoral manifestations of pulmonary tuberculosis pose a diagnostic challenge because of their radiological resemblance to lung tumors [[9], [10], [11]]. Nevertheless, several diagnostic criteria can aid in differentiating these conditions. A history of tuberculosis exposure or previous TB infection may suggest pseudotumoral pulmonary tuberculosis. Certain radiological features may also support the diagnosis of pulmonary tuberculoma. Pulmonary TB commonly affects the apicoposterior segments of the upper lobes or the apical segments of the lower lobes and typically presents as nodular caseous necrosis. On CT imaging, this appears as areas of non-enhancing hypoattenuation. Furthermore, tuberculomas may demonstrate annular or patchy calcifications surrounding areas of necrosis. In contrast, the most common malignant pediatric lung parenchymal neoplasms, which present as cystic or solitary masses on CT, typically do not invade the chest wall. These tumors are often associated with pleural effusion and exhibit heterogeneous low attenuation. Notably, calcification is uncommon in cases of pleuropulmonary blastoma [12].

In our patient series, 4 of the 7 cases demonstrated multiple small nodules adjacent to the primary mass. These nodules exhibited either a miliary or tree-in-bud pattern, which appeared to be a common radiological finding among our cases.

The diagnosis of pulmonary tuberculosis relies on a combination of methodologies to ensure accuracy and comprehensiveness. Sputum analysis remains a conventional and widely used diagnostic approach because of its simplicity and ability to provide rapid results. This includes sputum smear microscopy and culture using both liquid and solid media. Although effective, sputum culture generally requires a longer time to yield results compared with smear microscopy [13]. Polymerase chain reaction (PCR), particularly nested PCR, has become increasingly important in the diagnosis of pulmonary TB. By detecting M tuberculosis DNA, PCR offers greater sensitivity than conventional sputum smear testing, especially during the early stages of disease. Additionally, biopsy-based techniques, including real-time PCR analysis of endobronchial biopsy specimens, may be more sensitive than both sputum smear microscopy and bronchial brush smears. These approaches are particularly valuable when other diagnostic methods fail to provide conclusive results [14].

The treatment of pseudotumoral pulmonary tuberculosis is similar to that of other forms of pulmonary TB and consists of a standard short-course antituberculous regimen. Initial treatment includes isoniazid (INH), rifampicin (RIF), ethambutol (EMB), and pyrazinamide (PZA) for 2 months, followed by a continuation phase consisting of INH and RIF for an additional 4 months. In certain cases, depending on disease severity and the patient's response to therapy, treatment duration may be extended [9,15]. Although surgical intervention may occasionally be required for the diagnosis or management of pseudotumoral pulmonary tuberculosis, it is not routinely necessary and should be considered on a case-by-case basis [10].

Conclusion

Pseudotumoral pulmonary tuberculosis is a rare radiological manifestation of pulmonary tuberculosis that can closely mimic a primary lung mass. Diagnosing this condition, particularly in pediatric patients, is challenging and requires a comprehensive multidisciplinary approach. Accurate diagnosis depends on careful evaluation of the patient's clinical history, radiological findings, laboratory investigations, histopathological examination, and response to antituberculous therapy.
