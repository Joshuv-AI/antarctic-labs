Effect of a clinically feasible repeated potassium iodide application protocol on silver diamine fluoride–induced discoloration
Authors: Maryam Hajiahmadi, Samira SadighBatha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThe aim is to evaluate and compare the effects of single versus repeated applications of potassium iodide (KI) on SDF-induced discoloration in primary canines.Materials and MethodsThirty-six extracted human primary canines with dentinal caries were randomly allocated into three groups: Group I received 38% SDF alone; Group II received SDF followed by a single KI application; and Group III received SDF followed by immediately application after SDF and repeated on days 1, 3, and 5. Color change (∆E) was assessed on Days 1, 14, and 30 using a spectrophotometer based on the CIE Lab* system. Statistical analysis included One-Way ANOVA with Tukey post hoc tests for between-group comparisons, and Repeated Measures ANOVA with Bonferroni adjustment for within-group comparisons.ResultsAll groups showed significant differences in ΔE values at each time point, except between the single and repeated KI groups on Day 1. The Group I exhibited the highest ΔE values throughout the study, indicating the greatest discoloration, while Group III resulted in the lowest values. Over time, a progressive increase in discoloration was observed in Groups II, whereas partial color stability was maintained in Group III.ConclusionRepeated application of KI after SDF treatment can result in a higher reduction in tooth discoloration compared to a single-application. This protocol may enhance the clinical acceptability of SDF, especially for visible anterior teeth in pediatric patients.Keywords: potassium iodide, dental caries, cariostatic agent, tooth discoloration, pediatric dentistry

Introduction

Dental caries is a common, biofilm-mediated, multifactorial disease characterized by a dynamic imbalance between demineralization and remineralization processes (1). Without timely treatment, demineralization process may extend to the dental pulp, resulting in complications such as pain, abscesses, impaired chewing, sleep disturbances, reduced appetite, furcation parulitis, and periapical periodontitis. These outcomes may adversely affect a child's oral health-related quality of life (2, 3).

Conventional caries management typically involves the removal of decayed dentin followed by restorative procedures. However, in young children, these approaches can be challenging due to limited cooperation (4). Furthermore, the high cost of dental care and limited accessibility, especially in low-resource settings, contribute to a higher prevalence of untreated caries (5).

The ideal approach in pediatric dentistry is to adopt child friendly and minimally invasive treatments for managing carious primary teeth (6). Over the past decade, efforts to create a stress free dental environment have led to the growing use of biological and conservative treatment methods (7). These biological approaches focus on altering the biofilm and arresting carious lesions, ranging from stepwise excavation techniques to nonrestorative caries treatment (NRCT) (8).

Silver diamine fluoride (SDF) is a non-invasive agent used to arrest active carious lesions. It is recognized by the World Health Organization (WHO) as an essential medicine and is considered a safe and cost-effective option (9). Both the American Academy of Pediatric Dentistry (AAPD) and the American Dental Association (ADA) recommend 38% SDF for managing active and cavitated carious lesions in primary teeth (10, 11). The silver component provides antimicrobial action, while fluoride contributes to remineralization. SDF not only arrests caries in the treated tooth but also exerts a preventive effect on adjacent surfaces through its fluoride releasing property. In addition, silver ions at high concentrations inhibit matrix metalloproteinases activity, thereby preserving collagen integrity and enhancing remineralization (12, 13).

However, a major limitation of SDF lies in its tendency to stain tooth structure, which poses a challenge in cases where aesthetics are a concern (14). To address this drawback, potassium iodide (KI) has been introduced as an adjunctive agent. The application of KI following SDF-therapy leads to the formation of a creamy-white precipitate through its reaction with free silver ions contributing to tooth staining, which is believed to minimize discoloration. Importantly, KI does not diminish the antimicrobial or remineralizing properties of SDF (15, 16).

Previous studies have mostly investigated the effect of a single KI application following SDF use, with limited promising results (17, 18, 19). To date, the effect of repeated potassium iodide application following silver diamine fluoride has not been adequately investigated, as existing studies have primarily focused on single-application protocols. Some studies have indicated that repeated daily application of potassium iodide following SDF was shown to be more effective in reducing discoloration than a single application (20); however, such a protocol may not be clinically practical.

Therefore, this in vitro study was conducted to evaluate the effectiveness of a clinically feasible repeated KI application protocol, applied immediately after SDF and repeated on days 1, 3, and 5, compared with a single application in preventing SDF-induced discoloration.

The study hypothesized that tooth discoloration would not differ significantly between single and repeated KI applications after SDF treatment during the 30-day follow-up period.

Materials and Methods

Ethical statement

The study protocol has been approved by the Ethics Committee of Isfahan University of Medical Sciences (Approval No. IR.MUI.DHMT.REC.1403.186).The requirement for written informed consent was waived by the Ethics Committee due to the retrospective nature of the study.

Sample selection

This laboratory-based investigation was performed using extracted primary canines with cavitated dentinal carious lesions. The teeth were obtained from children aged 8 to 12 years, either following physiological exfoliation or extraction for orthodontic purposes.

Sample size estimation

The required sample size was determined based on data from a previous investigation, using G*Power software (version 3.1.9.7) (17). Considering a significance level of 0.05 and a statistical power of 80% (β=0.2), a minimum of nine teeth per group was required. Accordingly, a total 36 primary canines fulfilling the following eligibility criteria were enrolled.

Inclusion criteria

Primary canines with cavitated dentinal carious lesions and absence of any developmental dental anomalies

Exclusion criteria

Teeth with previous restorations, fractured or cracked or teeth, teeth with arrested or darkly discolored carious lesions, endodontically treated teeth.

Grouping and experimental design

To prevent dehydration, all samples were stored in distilled water. For disinfection purposes, the teeth were immersed in 0.5% sodium hypochlorite for one week, in accordance with previously published in vitro studies (17). Each tooth was then embedded in self-cure acrylic resin, leaving 1 mm below the cementoenamel junction exposed while maintaining an upright position. Partial caries excavation was performed manually using a spoon excavator to standardize lesion exposure. In each specimen, only the soft dentin was removed until firm dentin was reached, confirmed by tactile sensation and visual inspection. The remaining carious dentin was left intact to simulate an arrested lesion while ensuring a uniform lesion depth among samples. All specimens were prepared by a single calibrated operator to minimize variability. The specimens were randomly allocated to three groups (n=12) using a computer-generated randomization sequence: Group I: 38% Silver diamine fluoride (SDF), Group II: 38% Silver diamine fluoride (SDF) + single-application of Potassium Iodide (KI), Group III: 38% Silver diamine fluoride (SDF) + repeated KI applications

Treatment procedures

All agents were applied following the manufacturers' protocols. Group I: A 38% SDF solution (Fagamin, Tedequim, Argentina) was brushed onto the lesion surface for one minute, left in place without disturbance for an additional two minutes, followed by rinsing with water for 30 seconds. Group II: Immediately after applying 38% SDF, a saturated KI solution (Rivastar, SDI, Australia) was applied until the initial white-creamy precipitate disappeared, followed by a 30- second water rinse. Group III: The same protocol as Group II was followed, with additional KI reapplication on days 1, 3, and 5.

Following the treatment, all specimens were stored individually in sealed glass vials in artificial saliva and incubated at 37°C throughout the observation period. A summary of the treatment protocols, including the materials and application timing for all group, is provided in Table 1.

Color assessment

Tooth color was recorded at baseline and subsequently on days 1, 14, and 30 post-treatments. A spectrophotometer (Shadepilot, Degudent; Hanau-Wolfgang, Germany) operating in the visible light spectrum (400–700 nm) was employed for measurements. Calibration was performed prior to each use in accordance with the manufacturer's recommendations. Measurements were based on the CIE L*a*b* system, in which L* indicates lightness on a scale from 0 (dark) to 100 (bright), a* corresponds to the green (-a) to red (+a) spectrum, and b* represents the blue (-b) to yellow (+b) axis.

Statistical analysis

Data normality was verified using the Shapiro–Wilk test. One-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's post hoc test was used for between-group comparisons at each individual time point. Within-group changes across the three time intervals were analyzed using repeated measures ANOVA with Bonferroni adjustment for multiple comparisons. To evaluate the main effects of treatment and time, as well as their interaction, a two-way repeated measures ANOVA was employed. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. All analyses were conducted using SPSS software version 26 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Within-group comparisons

Within-group comparisons revealed distinct temporal patterns in tooth discoloration across the three treatment modalities. In the SDF-only group (Group I), ΔE values showed a statistically significant increase over time (p <0.001), with color change intensifying between day 1 and subsequent evaluations on days 14 and 30, although the difference between the latter two time points was not significant. Group II (SDF + single KI) exhibited a continuous and significant increase in ΔE across all time intervals (p < 0.001), indicating progressive discoloration over the 30-day period. In contrast, Group III (SDF + repeated KI) maintained relative ΔE values. No statistically significant differences were found between days 1 and 14 or between days 14 and 30, however, a significant difference was detected between days 1 and 30 (p=0.018). Interaction effect of time and treatment

Two-way repeated measures ANOVA revealed a significant interaction between time and treatment group (p < 0.001), indicating that the pattern of discoloration over time differed significantly among the three groups.

Discussion

Silver diamine fluoride (SDF) is extensively used in clinical dentistry as a low-cost and minimally invasive method for arresting dental caries, particularly in pediatric populations (21, 22). Evidence from randomized clinical trials has confirmed that the 38% concentration of SDF is more effective than the 12% formulation in controlling caries progression (23, 24), thereby justifying the employment of the 38% solution in the current investigation. The mechanism of action involves the interaction of its silver (25%) and high fluoride (44,800 ppm) content with hydroxyapatite, leading to the formation of silver phosphate (Ag3PO4) and calcium fluoride (CaF2) crystals. These agents exhibit both antimicrobial activity and the ability to seal dentinal tubules, enhancing the material's protective and desensitizing properties. CaF2 serves as a fluoride reservoir, whereas Ag3PO4 crystals are yellow at baseline but tend to darken after exposure to light or oxidative environments. Furthermore, the reduction of free silver ions (Ag+) result in black deposits, which are primarily responsible for the noticeable tooth discoloration following SDF application (25, 26, 27, 28). In an effort to overcome this esthetic drawback, potassium iodide (KI) has been introduced as an adjunctive agent. KI application leads to formation of a white precipitate of silver iodide (AgI) by interacting with unbound Ag ions, which potentially hinder the formation of dark-colored silver-based precipitates (25, 29). Whereas multiple experiments have evaluated single KI applications and reported variable findings regarding its long-term efficacy (17, 18, 30), limited attention has been paid to the effects of repeated applications over time. Thus, this in vitro experiment purposed to compare the esthetic outcomes of single versus repeated KI application in SDF-treated primary canines.

In the present study, a single application of KI resulted in a significant reduction in SDF-induced discoloration at all time points; however, its effect declined over time. Gray staining of the tooth structure was observed on day 30, indicating a limitation in its long-term efficacy. Conversely, repeated KI applications consistently maintained lower ∆E values throughout the study period. The significant time × group interaction further demonstrated that the trajectory of color change over the follow-up duration was not uniform among the groups. Specimens in the repeated KI group (Group III) presented a less pronounced intra-group color variation, which may be explained by the prolonged presence of iodide ions neutralizing unbound Ag ions before they could oxidize. In contrast, the single-KI application group (Group II) exhibited a faster progression of discoloration over time, which may be attributed to the depletion of iodide ions, thereby allowing residual silver ions to oxidize and form darker precipitates.

These findings are in line with previous observations suggesting that repeated daily application of potassium iodide results in less discoloration than a single application.

Similarly, previous in vitro studies by Hamdy et al. (19) and Detsomboonrat et al. (18) demonstrated an initial masking effect of single-application KI that diminished over time. Even when higher KI concentrations were used, discoloration returned within two weeks.

Similar trends were observed in clinical studies by Patel et al. (31), Karuna et al. (30), and Aly et al. (32), all of which reported initial esthetic improvement of SDF-treated teeth by single-application of KI, but found no significant differences between SDF/KI and SDF alone at long-term follow-ups (ranging from 6 to 12 months). These findings suggest that single-application KI protocols may be inadequate for maintaining esthetic outcomes in the long term, reinforcing the hypothesis that reapplication may be necessary to sustain esthetic benefits.

In contrast, Kamble et al. found sustained esthetic results over 30 days with a single KI application (17). Their use of glass ionomer restorations might explain the discrepancy, as restorative materials can mask discoloration and possibly shield the surface from environmental oxidation.

Additionally, Fröhlich et al. demonstrated that KI could significantly reduce discoloration not only under composite restorations but also on exposed dentin. Their 60-day in vitro study on bovine dentin demonstrated stable ∆E values even in the absence of restorations, although the greatest esthetic benefit occurred when KI was combined with composite, suggesting an additive masking effect of the restorative procedure (33). Their use of a coupling medium (glycerin) to simulate oral conditions during spectrophotometric analysis may also have contributed to their favorable outcomes, which differs from the protocol used in the present study.

Another laboratory-based study by Sayed et al. explored the discoloration effects of SDF and single-application KI under four different conditions over a 14-day follow-up period. While their results indicated that single-application KI maintained its masking effect throughout the 14 days, they also found that SDF-induced discoloration was more pronounced under light-exposed conditions. Moreover, they reported that the efficacy of KI was greater in enamel lesions compared to dentinal ones, suggesting that the depth of the lesion might influence treatment outcomes (25). These findings, although not directly contradictory, highlight the role of lesion depth and light exposure as important variables affecting the efficacy of potassium iodide to reduce the discoloration.

Collectively, these findings suggest that while the single potassium iodide application may offer short-term esthetic benefits, its limited penetration and transient availability are likely inadequate to neutralize silver ions embedded in dentinal tubules, leading to gradual discoloration over time. In contrast, repeated KI applications appear to maintain a more sustained presence of iodide ions, thereby promoting more stable esthetic outcomes.

Although daily repeated application of potassium iodide has been shown to be effective, such a regimen is not clinically practical. The present study addresses this limitation by evaluating a shorter and more clinically feasible repeated-application protocol.

A key strength of this study lies in the use of specimens with natural dentinal caries, which offers a model with greater clinical relevance when compared to artificially demineralized ones (34). This design better reflects real-world scenarios in which SDF is typically employed in the management of advanced cavitated lesions rather than incipient caries (35).

Within the limitations of this study, a repeated potassium iodide application protocol applied immediately after SDF and repeated on days 1, 3, and 5 appears to be a clinically practical approach to reducing SDF-induced discoloration. This protocol may offer a balance between effectiveness and clinical feasibility.

Finally, factors such as application frequency, the presence or absence of restorations, lesion depth, and environmental variables like light exposure may influence the extent of discoloration. Long-term clinical trials in real-world settings are necessary to develop optimized, evidence-based protocols that achieve both caries control and esthetic acceptability.

Several limitations should be considered, as they are inherent to the in vitro design of this study. The absence of intraoral conditions such as salivary dynamics, tooth brushing, mechanical wear, and long-term chemical interactions may limit the direct translation of the findings to clinical settings. In addition, sodium hypochlorite was used for tooth disinfection prior to the experimental procedures; although this protocol is commonly employed in similar in vitro studies, it may have affected the organic matrix of dentin and, consequently, influenced color outcomes. Therefore, the results should be interpreted with caution, and further well-designed clinical studies are required to confirm the current findings.

Conclusion

Repeated application of potassium iodide following silver diamine fluoride treatment was more effective than a single application in minimizing tooth discoloration. This approach relatively maintained color stability over time and may represent a more esthetically favorable protocol for managing carious lesions in pediatric patients. Further clinical studies are warranted to validate these findings under in vivo conditions.
