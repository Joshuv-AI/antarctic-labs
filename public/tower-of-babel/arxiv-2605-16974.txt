n n -ary elliptic groups, rings, and primes in arithmetic progressions
Ilia Pirashvili

Abstract
. I introduced the notion of an elliptic group in [ 1 ] . It is a quasi-group based on the tangent-chord law of elliptic curves and thus, becomes an abelian group upon singling out an element. This close proximity to abelian groups is reflected in the theory, and among other things, we can define elliptic rings, which are monoidal objects in elliptic groups. An other way of expressing this is to say that they are commutative monoids with an elliptic group structure that distributes over them. In this paper, we generalise this theory from the binary elliptic group structure to the n n -ary structure, which we call n n -ary elliptic groups and n n -ary elliptic rings. The latter are once again (binary) commutative monoids with an n n -ary operation that distributes over the monoidal structure in an n n -ary sense. The key interest of these objects for us is their arithmetic properties, which are surprisingly pleasant. The key result is that Dirichlet’s famous theorem on arithmetic progressions becomes simply Euclid’s theorem in these n n -ary rings, at least for progressions of the form a ​ n + 1 an+1 . Motivated by the hope to eventually prove this n n -ary Euclidean theorem purely algebraically using the theory of n n -ary rings (and thus give an alternative and purely algebraic proof of Dirichlet’s theorem), we start by exploring the first arithmetic facts of these objects, including introducing the n n -ary class group and showing that it indeed captures the unique n n -ary factorisation. We also obtain a type of Dedekinds theorem for our main n n -ary ring of interest: 𝗇𝖤𝗅𝗅 ⁡ ( ℤ ) \mathsf{nEll}(\mathbb{Z}) .

1.
Introduction

In
[
1
]
, I introduced the notion of an
elliptic group
, which is a set
with a binary operation derived by the geometric properties of elliptic curves
(Bézout’s theorem). It is a very well known and classical fact that the so called
tangent-chord law defies a binary operation that satisfies the following properties:

\displaystyle x\ast y

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle y\ast x

\displaystyle x\ast(x\ast y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle y

\displaystyle x\ast(y\ast(z\ast w))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle w\ast(y\ast(z\ast x))

The first one is our commutativity property, the second one (involution) is the
closes we have to an inverse. Indeed, upon singling out a single point, this property
is exactly the one that gives us inverses. The third and last property is a type of
shuffle property that becomes the associativity law in the associated abelian group.
These laws can be found, among many places, in
[
3
]
.

Elliptic groups are, as one would expect, very intimately linked to abelian groups.
Indeed, as mentioned already, upon singling out any element
o
, if we define the
new operation as
a+_{o}b:=(a\ast b)\ast o
, we get the classical abelian group
associated to the elliptic curve (which also happens to be its Picard group). The
theory of elliptic groups is, however, slightly more general, as distinct elliptic
groups can give rise to the same abelian groups, at least over
\mathbb{Q}
, though it can
not happen over
\mathbb{C}
. This only happens when the obtained groups have
\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}
as
factors. Indeed, the number
3
appearing is no coincidence, as we will discuss a
little further down in more detail.

We can consider morphisms between these objects to respect the operation and thus
obtain a category of elliptic groups. Here, we note that these morphisms will become
group homomorphisms upon distinguishing an element.

As with abelian groups, we can consider monoidal objects inside the category of
elliptic groups and thus, we come to the notion of
elliptic rings
. These are
commutative monoids whose structure we denote by
\circ
, over which our binary
operation
\ast
distributes over.

Starting with an abelian group
G
, we can form an elliptic group
\mathsf{Ell}_{1}(G)
and
likewise, starting with a commutative ring
R
with unit, we can form an elliptic
ring
\mathsf{Ell}_{1}(R)
. The operations are obtained by
a\ast b:=1-a-b
and
a\circ b:=a+b-3ab
respectively.

In
[
1
]
, we introduced this construction for
\mathsf{Ell}_{1}(\mathbb{Z})
and, as a small
example of this, we showed that Dirichlet’s theorem for
3n-1
is actually
Euclid’s theorem in this setting.

Again, we observe that
3
seems to make special appearances, which is not a
surprise, as elliptic group/ring theory is fundamentally based on elliptic curves,
which are, of course, curves or degree
3
. The idea was to see if we could
generalise this theory to higher-ary structures and thus, obtain similar theory for
other integers
n
.

In this paper, we explore precisely this train of thought. We define the theory of

n
-ary rings as commutative monoids with a certain type of
n
-ary structure
(which intakes
n
elements and outputs
1
, and should be thought of as a type
of addition) that distributes over it. Of course, for
n=2
, this returns our
original definition of elliptic groups, respectively elliptic rings.

This harkens to the general idea of
the geometry of monoids
, that stipulates
that the multiplicative monoid of a commutative ring carries arguably more
information than its additive groups and that one should perhaps think of rings not
as abelian groups with a multiplicative structure, but as (commutative) monoids with
an additive structure.

We once again show that its possible to define
n
-ary rings based on classical
rings, a construction we denote by
\mathsf{nEll}(R)
, in particular based on
\mathbb{Z}
, and
show that inside these, the number
n+1
does indeed play a special role. One of
our main theorems
7.2
says that Dirichlet’s theorem for arithmetic
progressions of the type
an+1
is exactly Euclid’s theorem for the
n
-ary
ring
\mathsf{nEll}(\mathbb{Z})
. I believe that this observation warrants a further study of these
objects.

In more detail, we start by introducing and studying general
n
-ary (elliptic)
groups in Section
2
. These are sets with an
n
-ary
operation generalising the three axioms of elliptic groups and can be found in
Definition
2.1
explicitly. In the immediate example afterwards, we
show that any group gives rise to such a structure (indeed, one for each element

g\in G
) in a fairly natural manner, a construction which we will denote by

\mathsf{nEll}_{g}(G)
.

In like manner, the next section
3
will introduce the notion of

n
-ary (elliptic) rings which are effectively the monoid objects in the category
of
n
-ary groups, though we will not formulate it in such a manner. Instead, we
will simply define them as commutative monoids under an operation
\circ
with the

n
-ary group structure
\ast
distributing over it in the following way:

(a_{1}\ast_{n}\cdots\ast_{n}a_{n})\circ_{n}b\;=\;(a_{1}\circ_{n}b)\ast_{n}\cdots\ast_{n}(a_{n}\circ_{n}b),

for all
a_{i},b
in our
n
-ary ring
R
. We will then proceed to develop some
very basic terms, such as irreducibility, primness, ideals and congruences, but leave
the general study for later.

This is due to the fact that we will proceed to narrow our focus. Specifically, much
as abelian groups give rise to
n
-ary groups, so do commutative rings give rise to

n
-ary rings (with the
n
-ary group structure being precisely the structure
obtained form the additive group of the base ring). We introduce this construction in
Section
4.1
and denoted in like-manner to
n
-ary groups by

\mathsf{nEll}(R)
. Note that here, we do not use the subscript
g
, since we can no long
pick any group element at our discretion due to the distributivity constraint. As
such, we always assume
g=1
in this paper. We note, however, that divisors of

n+1
all give rise to such structures in Paragraph
4.2.1
,
though do not proceed to explore it more depth, yet.

Having introduced this
standard
n
-ary ring

\mathsf{nEll}(R)
, we will starts its
exploration in Section
4
and introduce the so called
norm

function
\Sigma_{n}:\mathsf{nEll}(R)\to R
. It will play a central role throughout the
paper, as it allows us to reduce numerous questions to the base ring. In turn, its
“inverse”
J
(defined by the pre-image of
\Sigma_{n}
) allows us to ”lift”
various constructions and tasks to the
n
-ary setting.

This is a relatively larger section that explores some of the foundational properties
needed for developing a basic algebraic study of
\mathsf{nEll}(R)
, including properties of
the norm function, the kernel (in the special case when
n+1
is invertible in

R
, as we have not defined a kernel in general), and first isomorphism theorem,
localisations and the norm-ideal correspondence, which will play an important role
throughout the paper.

Having developed the basic algebraic properties of
\mathsf{nEll}(R)
, we will define and
explore its basic arithmetic properties in Section
5
. This can be
seen as both part II of the same section and part I of the following, and in many
ways serves as a bridge.

It deals with units and irreducibility, primeness and maximality, as well as touching
on notions such as
n
-ary integral domains and
n
-ary fields.

We will proceed to narrow our focus further and proceed to the first of our three
main sections, namely Section
6
. Here, we study the arithmetic
properties of
\mathsf{nEll}(\mathbb{Z})
in more detail. In particular, we will show that an
element
p
is
\circ_{n}
-prime in
\mathsf{nEll}(\mathbb{Z})
if and only if its norm

|\Sigma_{n}|
is (a classical) prime in
\mathbb{Z}
.

Moreover, we give an explicit criterion when
\circ_{n}
irreducibles and

\circ_{n}
-primes agree.

This is particularly relevant since we focus on the main motivation of this theory in
the following Section
7
. This is summed up in
Theorem
7.2
where we effectively say that Dirichlet’s theorem for a
progression of the form
1+(n+1)\mathbb{Z}
is effectively Euclid’s theorem in the

n
-ary ring
\mathsf{nEll}(\mathbb{Z})
.

Proving Euclid’s theorem for
\circ_{n}
-irreducibles is fairly trivial and done in
Theorem
7.1
. However, to obtian Dirichlet’s theorem we would need
to prove Euclid’s theorem for
\circ_{n}
-primes, which we are not yet able to do
without invoking Dirichlet.

This indicates an interest in developing the algebraic number theory of
\mathsf{nEll}(\mathbb{Z})

and
n
-ary rings in general. We take the very first steps in this direction in our
penultimate section, namely Section
8
, where we introduce the
class group of
\mathsf{nEll}(\mathbb{Z})
and show that it is always finite and trivial if an only
if
\mathsf{nEll}(\mathbb{Z})
has unique factorisaiton (or equivalently, is a PID). We show that
this is the case if and only of
n\in\{2,3,5\}
. Due to the finiteness of the
classgroup, it remains an open question if it is still possible to sharpen our
arguments to prove that there are infinitely many
\circ_{n}
-primes in
\mathsf{nEll}(\mathbb{Z})

and thus, get a distinct and purely algebraic proof of Dirichlet’s theorem for
progressions of the form
an+1
.

The last section
9.2
deals with the case
\mathsf{nEll}(\mathbb{Z}_{n+1})
. As
already hinted at when mentioning the kernel, things get significantly easier when

n+1
is invertible in
\mathbb{Z}
and we focus on that case in
\mathsf{nEll}(\mathbb{Z}_{n+1})
. And
indeed, the theory simplifies significantly. However, it does not bring us any closer
to proving our desired theorem, but it does show arguebly more explicitly where the
main obstruction lies.

Conventions

Throughout the paper,
n\geq 2
is a fixed intiger. Whenever we say ring, we
assume commutative and with unity. We emphasise that
0
is the

unit of the monoid
(R,\circ_{n},0)
and should not be

A morphism between
n
-ary groups is a structure preserving map. That is to say,

f:S\to T
is a map of
n
-ary groups if

f(a_{1}\ast_{n}\cdots\ast_{n}a_{n})\;=\;f(a_{1})\ast_{n}\cdots\ast_{n}f(a_{n}).

The category of
n
-ary elliptic groups is denoted by
{}_{n}\mathsf{EG}
.

(EG1) says that
\ast_{n}
is
totally symmetric
, and can be thought of as
our version of commutativity.

(EG2) will be reffered to as
Involution
and is the closest we have to
inverses. It allows us to express any single element in terms of other elements, by
moving the remaining
n-1
elements on the other side.

(EG3) is our version of associativity. In combination with (EG1), it allows us to
move any element outside, or inside, a double bracket.

Remark 2.1
.

The name
n
-ary elliptic group
is chosen out of continuity to the first
paper
[
1
]
. While there might also be a geometric link, this is not explored
and not the subject of this paper. Indeed, we will often refer to it as
n
-ary
ring or
n
-ary group for short.

Notation:
Unless there is potential for confusion, we will simply write

\ast
instead of
\ast_{n}
.

Example 2.2
.

Let
(G,+)
be an abelian group. For any
g\in G
, we can define an
n
-ary
operation by setting

a_{1}\ast\cdots\ast a_{n}\;:=\;g-\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}.

Straightforward calculation shows that this defines an
n
-ary elliptic group. To
spell them out: (EG1) holds due to the symmetric nature of our definition.

For (EG2), we denote
\hat{a}:=a_{2}\ast_{n}\cdot\ast_{n}a_{n}=\sum\limits_{i=2}^{n}a_{i}
and see that

\displaystyle\left(a_{1}\ast_{n}\hat{a}\right)\ast_{n}\hat{a}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(g-\hat{a}-a_{1}\right)\ast_{n}\hat{a}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle g-\hat{a}-\left(g-\hat{a}-a_{1}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a_{1}

(EG3) is proved like-wise, and we will use notation in like-manner. We expand

\displaystyle\hat{x}\ast_{n}\left(\hat{y}\ast_{n}\left(z\ast_{n}\hat{w}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hat{x}\ast_{n}\left(\hat{y}\ast_{n}\left(g-z-\hat{w}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hat{x}\ast_{n}\left(g-\left(g-z-\hat{w}\right)-\hat{y}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle g-z-\hat{w}+\hat{y}-\hat{x}

and observe that its symmetric with respect to
\hat{x}
and
\hat{w}
, giving
us our proof. These are equal as desired. We call this the
standard
n
-ary
elliptic group over

g
and denote it by
\mathsf{nEll}_{g}(G)
.

Notation:
Instead of writing
\mathsf{nEll}_{1}(G)
, we will simply write

\mathsf{nEll}(G)
.

.

2.2.
Descending maps to abelian groups

Let
(S,\ast_{n}
be an
n
-ary elliptic group and fix any element
o\in S
.
Define the
n-1
-ary operation

a_{1}\ast_{n-1}\cdots\ast_{n-1}a_{n-1}\;:=\;a_{1}\ast_{n}\cdots\ast_{n}a_{n-1}\ast_{n}o,

for
a_{i}\in S
. This gives rise to a functor

\ast_{n}^{o}:\;_{n}\mathsf{EG}\to_{n-1}\mathsf{EG}.

Upon iterating this, we can descend all the way to the binomial setting. Denote the
sequence of elements used by
\mathbf{o}=(o_{1},\ldots,o_{n-2})\in S^{n-2}
. We thus
have the composite functor

\ast^{\mathbf{o}}\;:=\;\ast_{3}^{o_{n-2}}\circ\cdots\circ\ast_{n}^{o_{1}}:\;_{n}\mathsf{EG}\to_{2}\mathsf{EG}.

Thus, we can use our results in
[
1
]
. Among other things, we have:

Lemma 2.2
.

For any
\mathbf{o}\in S^{n-2}
and
o\in S
, the operation

a+_{\mathbf{o}}b\;:=\;o\ast^{\mathbf{o}}(a\ast^{\mathbf{o}}b),

defines abelian group group structure on
S
.

3.
n
-ary elliptic rings

Definition 3.1
.

An
n
-ary elliptic ring
is a (commutative) monoid
(A,\circ_{n},e)
,
endowed with an
n
-ary group structure
\ast_{n}:A^{n}\to A
such that for all

a_{1},\ldots,a_{n},b\in A
, the
n
-ary distributivity

(1)

(a_{1}\ast_{n}\cdots\ast_{n}a_{n})\circ_{n}b=(a_{1}\circ_{n}b)\ast_{n}\cdots\ast_{n}(a_{n}\circ_{n}b)

holds. For simplicity, we will generally write
A
instead of
(A,\circ_{n},e,\ast_{n})
.

A
morphism
of
n
-ary elliptic rings

is a monoid homomorphism
that is simultaneously a morphism of
n
-ary elliptic groups.

The category of
n
-ary rings is denoted by
{}_{n}\mathsf{ER}
.

Notation:
Unless there is potential for confusion, we will simply write

\circ
instead of
\circ_{n}
.

Remark 3.1
.

It is clear that by symmetry (commutativity of
\circ
), the distributivity law
(
1
) holds from the other side as well, meaning

b\circ(a_{1}\ast\cdots\ast a_{n})\;=\;(b\circ a_{1})\ast\cdots\ast(b\circ a_{n}).

The descend from
n
-ary elliptic groups to

-ary elliptic groups discussed
in Section
2.2
translates to a descend for
n
-ary elliptic
rings, that is to say, upon fixing an element

, we have a functor

\ast_{n}^{o}:_{n}\mathsf{ER}\to_{n-1}\mathsf{ER}.

In like-manner to Section
2.2
, we can thus obtain a functor
to (binary) elliptic rings introduced in
[
1
]
.

3.1.
n
-ary elliptic ideals

Our aim is to explore the theory of
n
-ary algebraic number theory, and, as
expected, ideal-theory play a crucial role. However, much as (ring) ideals are not
simply monoid ideals, but monoid ideals that respect the additive structure,

n
-ary ideals must also be closed under the second operation. We make this
concrete with the following definition:

Definition 3.2
.

A subset
I\subseteq A
of an
n
-ary elliptic ring
A
is called an

n
-ary ideal
if

(I1)

for all
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\in I
,
we have
a_{1}\ast_{n}\cdots\ast_{n}a_{n}\in I
and

m\in 1+(n+1)R
, by definition
m=1-(n+1)a
for some
a\in R
, and then
\Sigma_{n}(a)=m
. Hence
\im(\Sigma_{n})=1+(n+1)R
.
∎

On the opposite side of Lemma
4.5
, we have the following trivial
observation.

Proposition 4.6
.

Let
n+1=0
in
R
. Then
\circ_{n}
agrees with the addition of
R
.
Meaning

(R,\circ_{n},0)\simeq(R,+,0)

is a monoid isomorphism and in particular, every element is a
\circ_{n}
-unit.

As such, while we regard
\circ_{n}
as the “multiplication” and
\ast_{n}
as the
“addition”, in this case, things take a bit of a turn.

From the arithmetic point of view, this case does not seem that interesting, and
subsequently, we will focus on the case when
n+1
is not
0
.

We also describe the case when
n+1\in R^{\times}
is invertible, which is much
more interesting to us.

Lemma 4.7
.

Let
n+1\in R^{\times}
be invertible and denote
z=1/(n+1)
. For every

a\in R
, we have

(i)

a\circ_{n}z=z
;

(ii)

z\ast\cdots\ast z=z
;

(iii)

\Sigma_{n}(z)=0
;

Proof.

(i)
a\circ_{n}z=a+z-(n+1)az=a+\frac{1}{n+1}-a=z
.

(ii)
z\ast\cdots\ast z=1-n\cdot\frac{1}{n+1}=\frac{(n+1)-n}{n+1}=\frac{1}{n+1}=z.

(iii)
\Sigma_{n}(z)=1-(n+1)/(n+1)=1-1=0
.
∎

Remark 4.1
.

In esssense,
1/(n+1)
, if it exists, plays the role of the absorbing element in
our
n
-ary ring. Note that these will be respected by morphisms, however, an
ideal need not contain it. It would be better to require ideals to contain this
element (if it exists), but is not vital. It is a special subcategory and should be
treated as such. This discussion can be found in Section
9.2
.

4.5.
Product and localisation

Due to the functorial and pointwise nature of the construction
\mathsf{nEll}(R)
, as well
as the fact that localisation is purely monoidal and
\circ_{n}
is a classical
commutative monoid, the following propositions are immediate and clear:

Proposition 4.8
.

For rings
R
and
S
, there is a natural isomorphism of
n
-ary elliptic
rings

\mathsf{nEll}(R\times S)\;\cong\;\mathsf{nEll}(R)\times\mathsf{nEll}(S),

where the operations
\ast_{n}
and
\circ_{n}
in the RHS are defined
componentwise.

Proposition 4.9
.

Let
R
be a ring and
T\subseteq R
a multiplicatively closed subset. Then

\mathsf{nEll}(T^{-1}R)\;\cong\;T^{-1}\mathsf{nEll}(R),

where
T^{-1}\mathsf{nEll}(R)
is the localisation of
\mathsf{nEll}(R)
with
T
.

4.6.
The norm-ideal correspondence when coming from a ring

While the ideals of
\mathsf{nEll}(R)
are not simply reducible to ideals of
R
, they
are nonetheless strongly controlled by the norm map
\Sigma_{n}
, as it offers a
systematic way to pass between classical ideals of
R
and
n
-ary elliptic
ideals of
\mathsf{nEll}(R)
.

For example, it characterises principal ideals completely:

Lemma 4.10

(Norm divisibility)
.

Let
R
be a ring. For
p,a\in R
, we have

(a)\subseteq(p)\,\text{ in }\,\mathsf{nEll}(R)\;\iff\;(\Sigma_{n}(a))\subseteq(\Sigma_{n}(p))\,\text{ in }\,R.

Moreover, in that case
\Sigma_{n}(a)=\Sigma_{n}(p)\cdot k
with
k\in\im(\Sigma_{n})=1+(n+1)R
, with the explicit representative being
r=a-pk
.

Proof.

Let
(a)\subseteq(p)
be an inclusion of
n
-ary ideals, meaning
a=p\circ_{n}r
. Lemma
4.3
then gives the result, as
\Sigma_{n}(a)=\Sigma_{n}(p)\cdot\Sigma_{n}(r)
must hold so we have found
k=\Sigma_{n}(r)\in\im(\Sigma_{n})
.

Assume conversely
(\Sigma_{n}(a))\subseteq(\Sigma_{n}(p))
, meaning
\Sigma_{n}(a)=\Sigma_{n}(p)\cdot k
and set
r=a-pk
. Since

\begin{array}[]{ccccc}\Sigma_{n}(a)&&\;=&&\Sigma_{n}(p)\cdot k\\[4.0pt]
\|&&&&\|\\[2.0pt]
1-(n+1)a&\;=&(1-(n+1)p)\cdot k&\;=&k-(n+1)pk,\end{array}

we get
1-(n+1)(a-pk)=k
. In other words,
\Sigma_{n}(r)=k\in\im(\Sigma_{n})
. We have

\displaystyle p\circ_{n}(a-pk)

\displaystyle=a+p(1-k)-(n+1)p(a-pk)

\displaystyle=a+p\bigl[(1-k)-(n+1)(a-pk)\bigr].

From
\Sigma_{n}(a-pk)=k
, we have
1-k=(n+1)(a-pk)
. The bracket vanishes
and leaving us
p\circ_{n}(a-pk)=p\circ_{n}r=a
and we are done.
∎

Remark 4.2
.

The automatic membership
k\in\im(\Sigma_{n})
is an important feature in

n
-ary elliptic rings. The ”
\circ_{n}
-quotient”
\Sigma_{n}(a)/\Sigma_{n}(p)
,
whenever it exists in
R
, is always a norm. For
R=\mathbb{Z}
, this says that
a\in(p)
if and only if
\Sigma_{n}(p)\mid\Sigma_{n}(a)
in
\mathbb{Z}
.

Definition 4.11
.

For an ideal
\mathfrak{a}\subseteq R
, define

J(\mathfrak{a})\;:=\;\Sigma_{n}^{-1}(\mathfrak{a})\;=\;\{\,x\in R\mid 1-(n+1)x\in\mathfrak{a}\,\}.

Proposition 4.12
.

The set
J(\mathfrak{a})
is an
n
-ary elliptic ideal of
\mathsf{nEll}(R)
, for any ideal

\mathfrak{a}\subseteq R
.

Proof.

(I1) Let
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in J(\mathfrak{a})
, so
\Sigma_{n}(x_{i})=\alpha_{i}\in\mathfrak{a}
,
meaning
1-(n+1)x_{i}=\alpha_{i}
. Then
(n+1)x_{i}=1-\alpha_{i}
and so

\displaystyle\Sigma_{n}(x_{1}\ast\cdots\ast x_{n})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Sigma_{n}\!\Bigl(1-\sum x_{i}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-n+(n+1)\sum x_{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-n+\sum(1-\alpha_{i})=-\sum\alpha_{i}\in\mathfrak{a}.

(I2) Let
x\in J(\mathfrak{a})
and
r\in R
. By Lemma
4.3
, we have

\Sigma_{n}(x\circ_{n}r)=\Sigma_{n}(x)\cdot\Sigma_{n}(r)\in\mathfrak{a}
.
∎

The construction
J
satisfies the following properties:

Proposition 4.13
.

Let
\mathfrak{a}\subseteq R
be an ideal.

(i)

J(\mathfrak{a})=\mathsf{nEll}(R)
if and only if
\mathfrak{a}=R
.

(ii)

J((0))=\{x:(n+1)x=1\}
, which equals
\{1/(n+1)\}
if

n+1\in R^{\times}
, and empty otherwise.

(iii)

The map
\mathfrak{a}\mapsto J(\mathfrak{a})
is order-preserving.

(iv)

J(\mathfrak{a})\neq\emptyset
if and only if
\mathfrak{a}
contains an element
of
1+(n+1)R
.

(v)

For
R=\mathbb{Z}
,
J((p))=\emptyset
for all primes
p\mid(n+1)
.
That is to say, the primes dividing
n+1
are ”invisible” to the

\circ_{n}
-structure.

Proof.

(i) That
J(R)=R=\mathsf{nEll}(R)
is trivial. Conversely, let
J(\mathfrak{a})=\mathsf{nEll}(R)
.
For every
x\in R
, we thus have
1-(n+1)x\in\mathfrak{a}
. By taking
x=0
we
get
1\in\mathfrak{a}
and so
\mathfrak{a}=R
.

(ii)
J((0))=\{x\in R\mid\Sigma_{n}(x)=0\}=\{x\mid(n+1)x=1\}
. If

n+1\in R^{\times}
, this has the unique solution
x=1/(n+1)
, otherwise
there is no solution in
R
.

(iii) Let
\mathfrak{a}\subseteq\mathfrak{b}
and
x\in J(\mathfrak{a})
. This gives
\Sigma_{n}(x)\in\mathfrak{a}\subseteq\mathfrak{b}
and we deduce
x\in J(\mathfrak{b})
and hence,
J(\mathfrak{a})\subseteq J(\mathfrak{b})
.

(iv) Saying
J(\mathfrak{a})\neq\emptyset
is equivalent to saying that there exists an
element
x\in R
with
\Sigma_{n}(x)\in\mathfrak{a}
. This in turn is equivalent to

\im(\Sigma_{n})\cap\mathfrak{a}\neq\emptyset
. Since
\im(\Sigma_{n})=1+(n+1)R
, this
is exactly the condition that
\mathfrak{a}
contains an element of
1+(n+1)R
.

(v) Let
R=\mathbb{Z}
and
x\in J((p))
. Then
p\mid 1-(n+1)x
. Subsequently, if

p\mid(n+1)
is to hold as well, we get
p=\pm 1
, contradicting that
p

is a prime.
∎

Proposition 4.14
.

If
\mathfrak{p}\subsetneq R
is a prime ideal of
R
, then
J(\mathfrak{p})
is an

\circ_{n}
-prime ideal of
\mathsf{nEll}(R)
.

Proof.

Since
1\notin\mathfrak{p}
,
\Sigma_{n}(0)=1\notin\mathfrak{p}
, so
0\notin J(\mathfrak{p})
. Hence

J(\mathfrak{p})\neq\mathsf{nEll}(R)
. Assume
x\circ_{n}y\in J(\mathfrak{p})
. Then
\Sigma_{n}(x)\cdot\Sigma_{n}(y)=\Sigma_{n}(x\circ_{n}y)\in\mathfrak{p}
and without loss of generality,

\Sigma_{n}(x)\in\mathfrak{p}
, which gives us
x\in J(\mathfrak{p})
.
∎

5.
Arithmetic of
\mathsf{nEll}(R)

When our
n
-ary elliptic ring is of the form
A=\mathsf{nEll}(R)
, we can control the
arithmetic through the norm map
\Sigma_{n}
. This section makes that control
precise.

5.1.
Units, associates, and divisibility

Lemma 5.1
.

An element
u\in\mathsf{nEll}(R)
is an
\circ_{n}
-unit if and only if
\Sigma_{n}(u)\in R^{\times}
.

Proof.

Let
u
be an
\circ_{n}
, meaning there exists a
v\in\mathsf{nEll}(R)
, such that
u\circ_{n}v=0
. It follows
\Sigma_{n}(u)\cdot\Sigma_{n}(v)=1
by
Lemma
4.3
and we obtain
\Sigma_{n}(u)\in R^{\times}
.

Conversly, set
v=-u\cdot\Sigma_{n}(u)^{-1}
. Then

\displaystyle u\circ_{n}v

\displaystyle=u-u\,\Sigma_{n}(u)^{-1}+(n+1)u^{2}\Sigma_{n}(u)^{-1}

\displaystyle=u+u\,\Sigma_{n}(u)^{-1}\bigl((n+1)u-1\bigr)

\displaystyle=u+u\,\Sigma_{n}(u)^{-1}\bigl(-\Sigma_{n}(u)\bigr)

\displaystyle=u-u=0,

where we used
(n+1)u-1=-\Sigma_{n}(u)
in the third step. For uniqueness,
observe that any inverse
v^{\prime}
satisfies
\Sigma_{n}(v^{\prime})=\Sigma_{n}(u)^{-1}
, and
the formula
v=-u\Sigma_{n}(u)^{-1}
is manifestly unique.
∎

Corollary 5.2
.

\Sigma_{n}
restricts to a group homomorphism
\mathsf{nEll}(R)^{\times}\to R^{\times}
.

Proof.

Multiplicativity follows from Lemma
4.3
. For the last claim, observe
that

\Sigma_{n}(-u\Sigma_{n}(u)^{-1})\;=\;1+(n+1)u\Sigma_{n}(u)^{-1}\;=\;(\Sigma_{n}(u)+(n+1)u)/\Sigma_{n}(u)\;=\;1/\Sigma_{n}(u),

where we used
\Sigma_{n}(u)+(n+1)u=1
.
∎

5.2.
n
-ary cancellativity

Lemma 5.3
.

For
a,b,c\in\mathsf{nEll}(R)
,

a\circ_{n}b\;=\;a\circ_{n}c\;\iff\;(b-c)\,\Sigma_{n}(a)\;=\;0.

Proof.

Expanding both sides using
x\circ_{n}y=x+y-(n+1)xy
, gives us

\begin{array}[]{crcl}&a+b-(n+1)ab&\;=&a+c-(n+1)ac\\
\;\iff&(b-c)-(n+1)a(b-c)&\;=&0\\
\;\iff&(b-c)(1-(n+1)a)&\;=&0,\end{array}

and subsequently,
1-(n+1)a=\Sigma_{n}(a)
.
∎

Proposition 5.4
.

An
n
-ary ring
A=\mathsf{nEll}(R)
is
\circ_{n}
-cancellative if and only if no
element of
\im(\Sigma_{n})\setminus\{0\}
is a zero-divisor in
R
.

Proof.

We know by Lemma
5.3
that
a\circ_{n}b=a\circ_{n}c
if and
only if
(b-c)\Sigma_{n}(a)=0
. Cancellation fails if and only if there exist

b\neq c
, and
a
with
\Sigma_{n}(a)\neq 0
such that
(b-c)\Sigma_{n}(a)=0
.
But this is equivalent to saying that there exists an element of

\im(\Sigma_{n})\setminus\{0\}
which is a zero divisor.
∎

5.3.
n
-ary fields

Definition and characterisation

We will use the term
group-like binoid
, which is a term introduced in

[
2
]
, being effectively a group with an absorbing element, which is
respected by morphisms. To be more precise, a
binoid
is a set
M
with a
binary associative operation
\cdot
and a distinguished element
z\in M

such that
z\cdot m=m\cdot z=z
for all
m\in M
. Morphisms between
binoids
M
and
N
are monoid homomorphisms that additionally map
z_{M}
to

z_{N}
. If for all elements
m\in M
with
m\neq z
we have an inverse, we
will say that the binoid is
group-like
.

Definition 5.5
.

An
n
-ary elliptic ring
A
is an
n
-ary elliptic field
if the

\circ_{n}
-monoid
(A,\circ_{n},0_{A})
is a group or a group-like binoid.
Equivalently, if
(A\setminus\{z_{A}\},\circ_{n},0_{A})
is a group.

5.4.
Maximal implies prime, and the hierarchy

The aim of this section is to show that the classical implications field

\Rightarrow
integral domain
\Rightarrow
ring, and maximal
\Rightarrow

prime
\Rightarrow
ideal, carry over to the
n
-ary setting.

Theorem 5.6
.

Every
n
-ary elliptic field is
\circ_{n}
-cancellative.

Proof.

Let
A
be an
n
-ary elliptic field and suppose
a\circ_{n}b=a\circ_{n}c

with
a\neq z_{A}
. By Definition
5.5
,
a
has an

\circ_{n}
-inverse
a^{\prime}
with
a^{\prime}\circ_{n}a=0_{A}
. By Lemma
4.3
,

\Sigma_{n}
is a monoid homomorphism and on
A\setminus\{z\}
, it is an
isomorphism onto
R^{\times}
, since
a\neq z
if and only if
\Sigma_{n}(a)\neq 0
, and every nonzero element is a unit. In particular
\circ_{n}
is associative
on
A\setminus\{z\}
, as it is isomorphic to the associative group

(R^{\times},\cdot)
. If
b=z
or
c=z
then

a\circ_{n}b\;=\;a\circ_{n}z\;=\;z\;=\;a\circ_{n}c

forces both to be
z
, so
b=c
. Otherwise, we have

b\;=\;0_{A}\circ_{n}b\;=\;(a^{\prime}\circ_{n}a)\circ_{n}b\;=\;a^{\prime}\circ_{n}(a\circ_{n}b)\;=\;a^{\prime}\circ_{n}(a\circ_{n}c)\;=\;(a^{\prime}\circ_{n}a)\circ_{n}c\;=\;0_{A}\circ_{n}c\;=\;c.\qed

Remark 5.1
.

For a general
n
-ary elliptic ring
A
(not necessarily of the form

\mathsf{nEll}(R)
), the implication maximal
\Rightarrow
prime, or equivalently,
ideal-field
\Rightarrow
ideal-cancellative, remains open.

5.5.
Irreducible and prime elements

The following propositions hold for any ring
R
.

Proposition 5.7
.

An element
p\in\mathsf{nEll}(R)
is
\circ_{n}
-prime if and only if
\Sigma_{n}(p)
is
prime in the submonoid
(1+(n+1)R,\cdot,1)
. That is to say, for all
s,t\in 1+(n+1)R
,

\Sigma_{n}(p)\mid st\,\text{ in }\,R\implies\Sigma_{n}(p)\mid s\,\text{ or }\,\Sigma_{n}(p)\mid t\,\text{ in }\,R.

Proof.

By Lemma
4.10
,
p\mid_{n}a
if and only if
\Sigma_{n}(p)\mid\Sigma_{n}(a)
. Since
\Sigma_{n}(a\circ_{n}b)=\Sigma_{n}(a)\Sigma_{n}(b)
and both
factors lie in
1+(n+1)R
, the prime condition translates exactly to the stated
condition.
∎

Corollary 5.8
.

If
\Sigma_{n}(p)
is a prime element of
R
, then
p
is
\circ_{n}
-prime in

\mathsf{nEll}(R)
.

The converse for irreducibles fails in general. Let
R=\mathbb{Z}
and
n
be such that

(\mathbb{Z}/(n+1)\mathbb{Z})^{\times}\not\;\cong\;\{\pm 1\}
. There exist
\circ_{n}
-irreducibles
with composite norm, as can be seen inthe following example:

Example 5.2
.

For
n=4
,
-1
is
\circ_{4}
-irreducible, yet
\Sigma_{4}(-1)=6=2\cdot 3
, is a composite.

Proposition 5.9
.

An element
p\in\mathsf{nEll}(R)
is
\circ_{n}
-irreducible if and only if

\Sigma_{n}(p)
is irreducible in the submonoid
(1+(n+1)R,\cdot,1)
. That is
to say, if it cannot be written as
\Sigma_{n}(p)=s\cdot t
with
s,t\in 1+(n+1)R
and
s,t\notin R^{\times}
.

Proof.

By Lemma
4.10
,
p=a\circ_{n}b
if and only if
\Sigma_{n}(p)=\Sigma_{n}(a)\Sigma_{n}(b)
, and
a\in\mathsf{nEll}(R)^{\times}
if and only if

\Sigma_{n}(a)\in R^{\times}
by Theorem
5.1
.
∎

Lemma 5.10

(Coprimality lemma)
.

For any
r\in R
in a UFD
R
, we have
\gcd(\Sigma_{n}(r),n+1)=1
.

Proof.

Any prime factor of both
\Sigma_{n}(p)
and
n+1
would divide
1
.
∎

Proposition 5.11
.

Let
R
be a UFD and
n+1\notin R^{\times}
. For a non-zero non-unit
p\in\mathsf{nEll}(R)
, the following are equivalent:

(i)

p
is
\circ_{n}
-prime.

(ii)

\Sigma_{n}(p)
is irreducible in
R
.

Proof.

(i)
\Rightarrow
(ii): We need to show that
p
is prime in
R
, the
fact that
R
is a UDF will imply irreducibility. Suppose
\Sigma_{n}(p)\mid ab

in
R
. Since
R
is a UFD, we can factorise elements in primes. We groups
these by ones which are
1\pmod{n+1}
and these which are not
1\pmod{n+1}
.
Since the product of these primes that are
1\pmod{n+1}
are again of like-from,
we can write
a=\Sigma_{n}(a^{\prime})u
and
b=\Sigma_{n}(b^{\prime})v
where
u,v
are
products of prime factors of
R
not lying in
1+(n+1)R
.

By the coprimality lemma
5.10
,
\Sigma_{n}(p)\mid\Sigma_{n}(a^{\prime})\Sigma_{n}(b^{\prime})
. By Proposition
5.7
,
p
we know that

\Sigma_{n}(p)
is prime in the submonoid
(1+(n+1)R,\cdot,1)\subseteq R

and thus, without loss of generality,
\Sigma_{n}(p)\mid\Sigma_{n}(a^{\prime})
, giving

\Sigma_{n}(p)\mid a
. As such,
\Sigma_{n}(p)
is a prime as
R
is a UFD, it is
irreducible as well.

(ii)
\Rightarrow
(i): This is just Corollary
5.8
.
∎

5.6.
Idempotents

Lemma 5.12
.

The
\circ_{n}
-idempotents of
\mathsf{nEll}(R)
are exactly those
a\in R
that
satisfy
a\cdot\Sigma_{n}(a)=0
in
R
. In particular:

(i)

0
is always an idempotent;

(ii)

If
n+1\in R^{\times}
, then
1/(n+1)
is idempotent;

(iii)

If
R
is an integral domain, the only
\circ_{n}
-idempotents are

0
and
1/(n+1)
(if
n+1\in R^{\times}
).

Proof.

Being an
\circ_{n}
-idempotent mean
a\circ_{n}a=a
, which expands to
a-(n+1)a^{2}=0
. We get
a\cdot\Sigma_{n}(a)=0
. In an integral domain,
a=0

or
\Sigma_{n}(a)=0
. The latter requires
a=1/(n+1)
if
n+1\in R^{\times}
.
∎

6.
Arithmetic of
\mathsf{nEll}(\mathbb{Z})

We now specialise to
\mathsf{nEll}(\mathbb{Z})
. Here, the general theory takes a particularly
clean and explicit form.

6.1.
Units

Lemma 6.1
.

The only
\circ_{n}
-unit in
\mathsf{nEll}(\mathbb{Z})
is
0
. Equivalently,
|\Sigma_{n}(a)|=1
if and only if
a=0
and, in particular,
\Sigma_{n}(a)\neq-1
for any

a\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Proof.

By Lemma
4.3
,
\circ_{n}
-units map to units in
\mathbb{Z}
. Hence, we have

\Sigma_{n}(a)=\pm 1
. If
\Sigma_{n}(a)=1
, then
(n+1)a=0
and so
a=0
. On the other hand, if
\Sigma_{n}(a)=-1
then
(n+1)a=2
. But this has
no integer solution for
n\geq 2
.
∎

Corollary 6.2
.

The monoid
(\mathbb{Z},\circ_{n},0)
is a sharp (only unit is the identity).

6.2.
Primes and irreducibles in
\mathsf{nEll}(\mathbb{Z})

For
\circ_{n}
-
primes
, the equivalence with classical primes holds in full
generality, and is the key result linking
\mathsf{nEll}(\mathbb{Z})
to classical number theory.

Proposition 6.3
.

A non-zero non-unit
p\in\mathbb{Z}
is
\circ_{n}
-prime if and only if

|\Sigma_{n}(p)|
is a classical prime. In this case,
|\Sigma_{n}(p)|\equiv\pm 1\pmod{n+1}
.

Proof.

(\Leftarrow)
Lemma
5.8
.

(\Rightarrow)
Let
|\Sigma_{n}(p)|=m=d\cdot e
with
1<d,e<m
. By
Lemma
5.10
,
\gcd(m,n+1)=1
, so by the Chinese Remainder Theorem,
the arithmetic progression
1+(n+1)\mathbb{Z}
meets every residue class modulo
m
.
Choose
s\equiv d\pmod{m}
and
t\equiv e\pmod{m}
with
s,t\in 1+(n+1)\mathbb{Z}
. By Lemma
4.5
, there exist
a,b\in\mathbb{Z}
with

\Sigma_{n}(a)=s
and
\Sigma_{n}(b)=t
. Then
\Sigma_{n}(p)\mid st=\Sigma_{n}(a)\Sigma_{n}(b)
, meaning
p\mid ab
, but
\Sigma_{n}(p)\nmid\Sigma_{n}(a)
and
\Sigma_{n}(p)\nmid\Sigma_{n}(b)
(since
d,e<m
) and thus,

p\nmid a
and
p\nmid b
, so
p
is not
\circ_{n}
-prime.
∎

Remark 6.1
.

We showed in Proposition
3.10
that every element can be
written as a product of irreducibles. The same statement does
not
hold
for
\circ_{n}
-primes in general. We already saw a counterexample in
Example
5.2
for
\mathsf{nEll}(\mathbb{Z})
:

The element
-1\in\mathsf{nEll}(\mathbb{Z})
for
n=4
is
\circ_{4}
-irreducible but not

\circ_{4}
-prime, and has no factorisation into
\circ_{4}
-primes.

Proposition 6.4

(Uses Dirichlet)
.

The
\circ_{n}
-irreducible elements agree with the
\circ_{n}
-primes in

\mathsf{nEll}(\mathbb{Z})
if and only if
(\mathbb{Z}/(n+1)\mathbb{Z})^{\times}\;\cong\;\{\pm 1\}
.

Proof.

We need to show that irreducibles are prime (since the reverse is always true). For
this, it suffices to show that their norms are prime, thanks to
Propositions
6.3
. Meaning me must show that every irreducible has
prime norm if and only if
(\mathbb{Z}/(n+1)\mathbb{Z})^{\times}\;\cong\;\{\pm 1\}
. The norm
reduces this question solely to the submonoid
M_{n}:=(1+(n+1)\mathbb{Z},\cdot,1)
via
Lemma
4.5
.

Necessity:
Suppose there exists
1\neq u\in(\mathbb{Z}/(n+1)\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
. By Dirichlet, there exist classical primes
p\equiv u\pmod{n+1}
and
p^{\prime}\equiv u^{-1}\pmod{n+1}
. Let
q
be their product. It
lies in the image of
\Sigma_{n}
as
q\equiv u\cdot u^{-1}\pmod{n+1}=1\pmod{n+1}
. Hence, there exists an element
a\in\mathsf{nEll}(\mathbb{Z})
such that
\Sigma_{n}(a)=q
. By construction,
a
can not be
\circ_{n}
-prime. But it is clearly

\circ_{n}
-irreducible since its norm is the product of primes, which are not
realisable as norms of elements of
\mathsf{nEll}(\mathbb{Z})
.

Sufficiency:
Let
(\mathbb{Z}/(n+1)\mathbb{Z})^{\times}=\{\pm 1\}
and
q\equiv 1\pmod{n+1}
be the norm of an
\circ_{n}
-irreducible element
a\in\mathsf{nEll}(\mathbb{Z})
. We have to show that
|q|
is a classical prime. Assume
|q|=ab

with
1<a,b<|q|
. Since
|q|=ab
is coprime to
n+1

(Lemma
5.10
), the factors
a
and
b
must likewise be coprime.
Moreover,
|a|\equiv|b|\equiv 1\pmod{n+1}
by our assumption on the unit
group. Trivial arguments about the sign of
a
and
b
and the fact that the
norm map
\Sigma_{n}
bijects
\mathbb{Z}
onto
1+(n+1)\mathbb{Z}
now implies that
a,b

or
-a,-b
lift to elements in
\mathsf{nEll}(\mathbb{Z})
and will thus give us a
factorisation of
q
, contradicting our assumption.
∎

7.
Infinitely many
\circ_{n}
-primes and primes in arithmetic
progressions

We now turn to the main parts of this paper, the links of
n
-ary ring theory to
classic arithmetic and, in particular, giving a proof that the existence of
infinitely many
\circ_{n}
-primes (Euclid’s theorem for
n
-ary primes) is
equivalent to Dirichlet’s theorem on arithmetic progressions for the special case of

an+1
. This link is the main reason I believe a deeper exploration of
n
-ary
elliptic algebra might be warranted.

7.1.
Infinitely many
\circ_{n}
-irreducibles

While I was not able to prove an equivalent theorem for
\circ_{n}
-primes without
invoking Dirichlet, below is an Euclid-style proof for
\circ_{n}
-irreducibles.

Theorem 7.1

(Euclid’s theorem for
\circ_{n}
-irreducibles)
.

Any
n\neq 0
has a factorisation in
\circ_{n}
-irreducibles. In particular,
there are infinitely many
\circ_{n}
-irreducibles in
\mathsf{nEll}(\mathbb{Z})
.

Proof.

Factorisation in irreducibles.
Every non-zero
a_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}
is either

\circ_{n}
-irreducible or
a_{0}=a_{1}\circ_{n}b_{0}
with
a_{1},b_{0}\neq 0
. To
avoid confusion, we call again that
0\in\mathbb{Z}
corresponds to the sole unit of

\mathsf{nEll}(\mathbb{Z})
(Lemma
6.1
) and as
n+1
is not invertible in

\mathsf{nEll}(\mathbb{Z})
, we have no absorbing (
\circ_{n}
-zero) element.

Since the monoid is sharp by Corollary
6.2
, we have
|\Sigma_{n}(b_{0})|>1
and
|\Sigma_{n}(a_{1})|<|\Sigma_{n}(a_{0})|
. This descent must terminate,
yielding a factorisation into
\circ_{n}
-irreducibles.

Infinitely many.
Suppose
\{q_{1},\ldots,q_{r}\}
is a complete
finite list of irreducibles. Set

N\;:=\;q_{1}\circ_{n}\cdots\circ_{n}q_{r}\,\quad\text{and}\quad\,M\;:=\;1-N.

Case 1:
M=0
.
It follows
N=1
, so

\Sigma_{n}(N)=\Sigma_{n}(1)=-n
. Consider

\Sigma_{n}(2)\;=\;1-2(n+1)\;=\;-(2n+1),

which gives us
|\Sigma_{n}(2)|=2n+1>1
. Subsequently,
2
is not a unit and
must have an
\circ_{n}
-irreducible factor
p
with
\Sigma_{n}(p)\mid-(2n+1)

(this includes the case
2=p
). As
p\in\{q_{1},\ldots,q_{r}\}
by assumption,

\Sigma_{n}(p)
must divide
\Sigma_{n}(N)=-n
and hence, must divide
\gcd(n,2n+1)=1
. This forces
|\Sigma_{n}(p)|=1
, which is true exactly for
p=0
. But this is a contradiction.

Case 2:
M\neq 0
.
Then
|\Sigma_{n}(M)|\geq 2
(since
M\neq 0
and the monoid is sharp), so
M
has an

\circ_{n}
-irreducible factor
p
with
\Sigma_{n}(p)\mid\Sigma_{n}(M)
. By
assumption,
p=q_{i}
for some
i
. As such,
\Sigma_{n}(q_{i})
divides both

\Sigma_{n}(N)
and
\Sigma_{n}(M)
, and hence their sum (Lemma
4.4
):

\Sigma_{n}(N)+\Sigma_{n}(M)\;=\;\Sigma_{n}(N)+\Sigma_{n}(1-N)\;=\;1-n.

But
|\Sigma_{n}(q_{i})|\geq 2
and the smallest value in
1+(n+1)\mathbb{Z}
with
absolute value
>1
is
-n
(with
\Sigma_{n}(1)=-n
). Since
|-n|=n>n-1=|1-n|
,
\Sigma_{n}(q_{i})
cannot divide
1-n
. We arrive at a
contradiction.
∎

7.2.
Infinitely many
\circ_{n}
-primes and primes in arithmetic
progressions

Theorem 7.2

(Main theorem)
.

The
\circ_{n}
-primes are exactly the classical primes
p\in\mathbb{Z}
with

|\Sigma_{n}(p)|\equiv\pm 1\pmod{n+1}
. Moreover, the following are equivalent:

(i)

There are infinitely many
\circ_{n}
-prime elements in
\mathsf{nEll}(\mathbb{Z})
.

(ii)

There are infinitely many classical primes
p\equiv\pm 1\pmod{n+1}
.

Part (ii) is, of course, Dirichlet’s theorem for
\pm 1+(n+1)\mathbb{Z}
and
subsequently, there are infinitely many
\circ_{n}
-primes in
\mathsf{nEll}(\mathbb{Z})
.

Proof.

That
|\Sigma_{n}(p)|\equiv\pm 1\pmod{n+1}
must hold follows directly from
Proposition
6.3
and Lemma
5.10
.

The equivalence of (i) and (ii) follows from the bijection
\Sigma_{n}:\mathbb{Z}\to 1+(n+1)\mathbb{Z}
(Lemma
4.5
). The
\circ_{n}
-primes in
\mathsf{nEll}(\mathbb{Z})

are thus exactly the classical primes
q\equiv\pm 1\pmod{n+1}
.
∎

8.
The
n
-ary elliptic class group of
\mathsf{nEll}(\mathbb{Z})

In light of the Theorem
7.2
, we consider the arithmetic study of
n
-ary
ring theory to be of some interest. It is not merely about proving Dirichlet’s
theorem in a purely algebraic manner (though that would, of course, be a highlight
result), but that it seems to be the more natural setting for certain types of
arithmetic questions.

One of the most classical objects in algebraic number theory is of course the class
group. It can be defined in numerous ways (see Picard group), but the arguably the
most direct definition is as a quotient of fractional ideals by principal ones. The
order of this quotient group is known as the class number and it measures how far a
number ring is form being an unique factorisation domain (UFD). It is a pleasant and
fairly obvious fact that the group of fractional ideals is isomorphic to the
Grothendieck group of the monoid of ideals under multiplication.

Using this definition, we introduce the class group of the standard
n
-ary ring

\mathsf{nEll}(\mathbb{Z})
associated to
\mathbb{Z}
in this section and show that it likewise measures
unique factorisation in
\mathsf{nEll}(\mathbb{Z})
, see Section
8.3
. This, in turn,
relates back to Dirichlet’s theorem for progressions of the form
an+1
.

8.1.
The monoid of
n
-ary elliptic ideals and its group completion

In Section
4.6
, we constructed
J(\mathfrak{a})
as the pre-image of the
norm function
\Sigma_{n}
and showed that the non-empty
n
-ary elliptic ideals
of
\mathsf{nEll}(\mathbb{Z})
correspond exactly to ideal of
\mathbb{Z}
whose generator
m
is
coprime to
n+1
. We called this the norm-ideal correspondence. Note, here we
used the basic fact that
\mathbb{Z}
is a PID and to clarify notation, we will write

J(m)\;:=\;J\bigl((m)\bigr)\;=\;\{a\in\mathbb{Z}:(n+1)a\equiv 1\pmod{m}\}.

Using this correspondence, we introduce

\mathcal{N}_{n}\;:=\;\{m\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}:\gcd(m,n+1)=1\},

as we have just argued that these are exactly the numbers (ideals) that correspond
to non-empty
n
-ary ideals in
\mathsf{nEll}(\mathbb{Z})
. Endowing it with a monoid structure
by (standard) multiplication, it is obvious that the assignment
m\to J(m)
is a
monoid isomorphism. We identify our
n
-ary ideal monoid with
\mathcal{N}_{n}
and form
its Grothendieck group

\mathcal{F}_{n}\;:=\;\bigl\{r\in\mathbb{Q}_{>0}:v_{p}(r)=0\,\text{ for all primes }\,p\mid n+1\bigr\}.

As mentioned in our preliminary talk about the class group,
\mathcal{F}_{n}
becomes the
group of fractional ideals. We can describe
\mathcal{F}_{n}
readily as the free abelian
group on the set of (classical) primes not dividing
n+1
and write it as

\mathcal{F}_{n}\;=\;\bigoplus_{p\nmid n+1}\mathbb{Z}\,[p].

In this notation, the ideal corresponding to
m\in\mathcal{N}_{n}
becomes
\sum_{p\mid m}v_{p}(m)\,[p]
.

Under this correspondence, principal
n
-ary ideals of
\mathsf{nEll}(\mathbb{Z})
are of the
form

(a)\;=\;J\bigl((\Sigma_{n}(a))\bigr),\quad a\in\mathbb{Z}.

We can see that as
a
ranges over
\mathbb{Z}
, the values
|\Sigma_{n}(a)|
range
over

\mathcal{P}_{n}\;:=\;\bigl\{|1-(n+1)a|:a\in\mathbb{Z}\bigr\}\;=\;\bigl\{m\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}:m\equiv\pm 1\pmod{n+1}\bigr\}.

Based on this, we define the following subgroup of
\mathcal{F}_{n}
, to be the free group

\overline{\mathcal{P}}_{n}
generated by the set the set

\{[p]\mid\mathcal{P}_{n}\ni p\nmid n+1\,\text{ is a classical prime}\,\}.

Note that
[p]
is just notation to distinguish it from the associated number,
and in a certain way, is something we regard as a divisor.

This brings us to the following definition:

8.2.
The class group and its connection to the unit groups

Definition 8.1
.

Define the
n
-ary elliptic class group
of
\mathsf{nEll}(\mathbb{Z})
as

\mathrm{Cl}_{n}(\mathbb{Z})\;:=\;\mathcal{F}_{n}\,\big/\,\overline{\mathcal{P}}_{n}.

Under this equivalence, two
n
-ary ideals
J(m)
and
J(k)
represent the
same class if and only if

mk^{-1}\;=\;\prod_{i}p_{i}^{e_{i}},

where each
p_{i}

\equiv\pm 1\pmod{n+1}
is a classical prime.

Unlike the classical class group, the
n
-ary class group is not trivial for

\mathbb{Z}
(that is to say, its not trivial for
\mathsf{nEll}(\mathbb{Z})
). However, it is fairly
easy to calculate in this case. We have:

Proposition 8.2
.

There is a natural isomorphism of groups

\mathrm{Cl}_{n}(\mathbb{Z})\;\cong\;(\mathbb{Z}/(n+1)\mathbb{Z})^{\times}\big/\{\pm 1\}.

In particular,
|\mathrm{Cl}_{n}(\mathbb{Z})|=\phi(n+1)/2
.

Proof.

We define the group homomorphism
\psi:\mathcal{F}_{n}\to(\mathbb{Z}/(n+1)\mathbb{Z})^{\times}/\{\pm 1\}
on generators by

\psi\bigl([p]\bigr)\;:=\;[p\bmod{n+1}].

Recall that generators are exactly the classical primes
p\nmid n+1
and are thus
units mod
n+1
, which gives us well-definidness.

Surjectivity is just Dirichlet’s theorem: For any

[u]\in(\mathbb{Z}/(n+1)\mathbb{Z})^{\times}/\{\pm 1\}
, we can find a prime
p\equiv u\pmod{n+1}
with
p\nmid n+1
, meaning a prime
p
with
\psi([p])=[u]
.

To understand the kernel, we note that a generator
[p]
lies in
\mathsf{Ker}\psi
if
and only if
p\equiv\pm 1\pmod{n+1}
. But this gives us exactly that the kernel
is
\overline{\mathcal{P}}_{n}
and thus the theorem.
∎

In particular, we have:

Corollary 8.3
.

\mathrm{Cl}_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
is trivial if and only if
(\mathbb{Z}/(n+1)\mathbb{Z})^{\times}=\{\pm 1\}
,
meaning precisely for
n\in\{2,3,5\}
.

Corollary 8.4
.

The
n
-ary elliptic class group
\mathrm{Cl}_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
is finite of order

\phi(n+1)/2
.

8.3.
The class group governs unique factorisation in prime ideals

We have the following theorem which sums up our talk on the class group:

Theorem 8.5
.

An
n
-ary ideal
J(m)
of
\mathsf{nEll}(\mathbb{Z})
is principal if and only if its class

[J(m)]\in\mathrm{Cl}_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
is trivial. Moreover:

(i)

Every
n
-ary ideal
I\subseteq\mathsf{nEll}(\mathbb{Z})
may be uniquely written as

I\;=\;J(p_{1})^{e_{1}}\cdots J(p_{k})^{e_{k}}

with
p_{i}
classical primes
p_{i}\nmid n+1
.

(ii)

For a
\circ_{n}
-prime
q
, we have

(q)\;=\;J(|\Sigma_{n}(q)|).

In particular, for every non-empty
n
-ary prime
q
ideal is in the image of

J(-)
.

(iii)

An
n
-ary ideal
J(p)
is principal if and only if
p\equiv\pm 1\pmod{n+1}
. Otherwise it represents a non-trivial element of
\mathrm{Cl}_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
.

In particular, factorisation into
n
-ary prime elements exists exactly when

\mathrm{Cl}_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
is trivial, meaning
n\in\{2,3,5\}
.

Proof.

(i): We know from Proposition
4.13
and the norm-ideal
correspondence that non-empty
n
-ary ideals of
\mathsf{nEll}(\mathbb{Z})
correspond exactly
to the numbers coprime with
n+1
. Subsequently, we can write
I=J(m)
for
some
m
with
\gcd(m,n+1)=1
. Factorise
m=p_{1}^{e_{1}}\cdots p_{k}^{e_{k}}

and use the fact that
J
respects the product (as mentioned in the introduction
of this section as well) to obtain the desired result.

(ii): This is just an amalgamion of previous results.
Proposition
6.3
, says that a non-zero non-unit
q\in\mathbb{Z}
is

\circ_{n}
-prime if and only if
|\Sigma_{n}(q)|
is a classical prime.
Lemma
5.10
states
|\Sigma_{n}(q)|\equiv\pm 1\pmod{n+1}
.
Subsequently,
(q)=J(|\Sigma_{n}(q)|)
is a prime
n
-ary ideal by
Proposition
4.14
.

(iii): By the norm-ideal correspondence we know that
J(p)
is principal if and
only if there exists an element
a\in\mathbb{Z}
with
|\Sigma_{n}(a)|=p
. But this
just means exactly
p\equiv\pm 1\pmod{n+1}
.

We return to the initial statement: By part (i), we can write
J(m)=\prod J(p_{i})^{e_{i}}
and subsequently, its class as

[J(m)]\;=\;\sum e_{i}[J(p_{i})]\in\mathrm{Cl}_{n}(\mathbb{Z}).

We know Proposition
8.2
and the definition of

\overline{cP}_{n}
that
[J(p_{i})]
is trivial in the class group if and only if

p_{i}
is
\pm 1\pmod{n+1}
. Since different primes can not cancel each other
out in the class group (as its a subgroup of a free group, hence free), their sum
is trivial if and only if each summand is trivial and hence, if and only if
m=\pm 1\pmod{n+1}
, meaning exactly when
m\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
and thus the claim.

The initial statement is now immediate: Every non-empty
n
-ary ideal factors
into prime ideals of the form
J(p)
. Each
J(p)
is principal if and only if

p\equiv\pm 1
. Moreover,
\mathrm{Cl}_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
is trivial if and only if all
primes
p\nmid n+1
satisfy
p\equiv\pm 1
, which itself holds if and only if

(\mathbb{Z}/(n+1)\mathbb{Z})^{\times}=\{\pm 1\}
, that is to say exactly when

n\in\{2,3,5\}
.

The last assertion follows from Proposition
6.4
.
∎

Remark 8.1
.

Part (i) is, of course, a type of
n
-ary Dedekind’s factorisation theorem for

\mathsf{nEll}(\mathbb{Z})
.

9.
The case when
n+1
is invertible in
R

We will not go back to a more general discussion, as throughout, a special case
stands out, namely when
n+1\in R^{\times}
is invertible in our ring
R
. In that
case, our theory becomes significantly simpler and we are able to define kernels and
the isomorphism theorem and certain maps become isomorphisms, simplifying our
discussions. The general idea is that, other than intrinsic interest, might also be
used for when
n+1
is a non-zero divisor in
R
. In that case, we can consider

R_{n+1}
and hope that it might give us useful information. We demonstrate this
general idea quickly in Subsection
9.2
, even if do not highlight
any special results we obtain in this manner.

New convention:
From now on, unless explicitly stated
otherwise,
n+1
will be assumed to be invertible throughout in every base ring.
Moreover,
z=z_{R}:\frac{1}{n+1}
will be fixed. Note that morphisms respect it by
default, meaning
f(z_{A})=z_{B}
, and ideals contain it (that is to say, ideals are
assumed to be non-empty).

9.1.
Ideals and Kernels

In ordinary ring theory (or group theory), the kernel is one of the most
fundamental constructions, as it controls the fibres of a homomorphism and allows
us to consider the first isomorphism theorem, among many other things. It is
defined as the preimage of the additive unit, which is also the absorbing element
of the multiplication. However, our ”addition”,
\ast
, does not have a unit and
neither does
\circ
have an absorbing element, in general.

If, however, our
n
-ary elliptic ring
A
is of the form
A\simeq\mathsf{nEll}(R)

for ring
R
with
n+1\in R^{\times}
, we can consider
f^{-1}(\{z\})
, where

z=1/(n+1)
.

As mentioned in Remark
4.1
, the case when
n+1
is
invertible is essentially its own category and we will do a small exploration of
this topic here.

Kernel definition and ideal property

Definition 9.1
.

For
f:A\to B
a morphism of
n
-ary elliptic rings, define the

n
-ary kernel
as

\mathsf{Ker}_{n}(f)\;=\;f^{-1}(\{z_{B}\}).

Lemma 9.2
.

The Kernel
\mathsf{Ker}_{n}(f)
of an
n
-ary ring morphism
f:A\to B
is an

n
-ary ideal of
A
.

Proof.

This is de facto Lemma
4.7
. The closure under
\ast_{n}
(I1) follows
from Lemma
4.7
(ii) as
f
preserves
\ast_{n}
and we thus have

f(x_{1}\ast_{n}\cdots\ast_{n}x_{n})\;=\;f(x_{1})\ast_{n}\cdots\ast_{n}f(x_{n})\;=\;\underbrace{z\ast_{n}\cdots\ast_{n}z}_{\,\text{n}\,}\;=\;z,

showing that
x_{1}\ast_{n}\cdots\ast_{n}x_{n}\in\mathsf{Ker}_{n}(f)
. The absorption under

\circ_{n}
(I2) follows from Lemma
4.7
(i), which implies

f(x\circ_{n}r)\;=\;f(x)\circ_{n}f(r)\;=\;z\circ_{n}f(r)\;=\;z,

as
f
preserves
\circ_{n}
.
∎

The kernel captures the congruence

Proposition 9.3
.

Let
f:A\to B
be a morphism of
n
-ary elliptic rings. For
x,y\in A
,

x\sim_{\mathsf{Ker}_{n}(f)}y\;\iff\;f(x)\;=\;f(y).

Proof.

(\Rightarrow)
Suppose
x\ast_{n}a_{1}\ast_{n}\cdots\ast_{n}a_{n-1}=y\ast_{n}b_{1}\ast_{n}\cdots\ast_{n}b_{n-1}
with
a_{i},b_{j}\in\mathsf{Ker}_{n}(f)
, so
f(a_{i})=f(b_{j})=z_{B}
. Applying
f
gives us

f(x)\ast_{n}z_{B}\ast_{n}\cdots\ast_{n}z_{B}\;=\;f(y)\ast_{n}z_{B}\ast_{n}\cdots\ast_{n}z_{B}.

We act with
\ast(z_{B},\ldots,z_{B})
on both sides and use axiom (EG2) to
obtain
f(x)=f(y)
.

(\Leftarrow)
On the reverse, assume
f(x)=f(y)
. We define
\delta:=y-x\in A
. Set

a_{1}\;:=\;z_{A}+\delta,\quad a_{i}\;:=\;z_{A}\;\;(2\leq i\leq n-1),\quad b_{j}\;:=\;z_{A}\;\;(1\leq j\leq n-1).

It follows that

\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}a_{i}-\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}b_{j}\;=\;(z_{A}+\delta)+(n-2)z_{A}-(n-1)z_{A}\;=\;\delta\;=\;y-x.

Since
x\ast_{n}a_{1}\ast_{n}\cdots\ast_{n}a_{n-1}=y\ast_{n}b_{1}\ast_{n}\cdots\ast_{n}b_{n-1}
is equivalent to
\sum a_{i}-\sum b_{j}=y-x
(expanding the

\ast_{n}
-formula
1-\sum(-)
), we have
x\sim_{\mathsf{Ker}_{n}(f)}y
. It remains
to verify
a_{1}\in\mathsf{Ker}_{n}(f)
. We have

f(a_{1})\;=\;f(z_{A}+\delta)\;=\;f(z_{A})+f(y)-f(x)\;=\;z_{B}+0\;=\;z_{B},

where we used
f(x)=f(y)
and
f(z_{A})=z_{B}
.
∎

Remark 9.1
.

We need
n+1\in A^{\times}
in the reverse direction to produce the
representative
z_{A}\in A
.

The first isomorphism theorem

Theorem 9.4

(First isomorphism theorem for
n
-ary elliptic rings)
.

Let
f:A\to B
be a morphism of
n
-ary elliptic
rings. The following hold:

(i)

\mathsf{Ker}_{n}(f)
is a non-empty
n
-ary ideal of
A
, with
z_{A}\in\mathsf{Ker}_{n}(f)
.

(ii)

f
factors uniquely through the quotient. That is to say, there
is a unique morphism

\bar{f}:A/\mathsf{Ker}_{n}(f)\to B

with
\bar{f}([x])=f(x)
.

(iii)

The morphism
\bar{f}
is injective, and
f
induces an
isomorphism of
n

The kernel and the
J
-construction

When
n+1\in R^{\times}
, the
J
-construction gives a complete description
of quotients.

Proposition 9.5
.

Let
g:R\to S
be a ring homomorphism. Then

\mathsf{Ker}_{n}\bigl(\mathsf{nEll}(g)\bigr)\;=\;J\bigl(\mathsf{Ker}(g)\bigr),

where
\mathsf{Ker}(g)=g^{-1}(0)\subseteq R
is the classical kernel.

Proof.

Take
x\in R
. We have

\displaystyle x\in\mathsf{Ker}_{n}(\mathsf{nEll}(g))

\displaystyle\iff g(x)=z_{S}=\tfrac{1}{n+1}

\displaystyle\iff(n+1)g(x)=1

\displaystyle\iff g\big((n+1)x)-1=0

\displaystyle\iff g\bigl(1-(n+1)x\bigr)=0

\displaystyle\iff\Sigma_{n}(x)\in\mathsf{Ker}(g)

\displaystyle\iff x\in J\!\bigl(\mathsf{Ker}(g)\bigr).\qed

Theorem 9.6
.

Let
\mathfrak{a}\subseteq R
be any ideal. The canonical ring homomorphism
\pi:R\to R/\mathfrak{a}
induces an isomorphism of
n
-ary elliptic rings

\mathsf{nEll}(R)\big/J(\mathfrak{a})\;\cong\;\mathsf{nEll}(R/\mathfrak{a}).

Proof.

The quotient map
\pi:R\twoheadrightarrow R/\mathfrak{a}
induces a surjective
morphism
\mathsf{nEll}(\pi):\mathsf{nEll}(R)\twoheadrightarrow\mathsf{nEll}(R/\mathfrak{a})
. By Proposition

9.5
,

\mathsf{Ker}_{n}(\mathsf{nEll}(\pi))\;=\;J(\mathsf{Ker}(\pi))\;=\;J(\mathfrak{a}).

Theorem
9.4
now gives
\mathsf{nEll}(R)/J(\mathfrak{a})\;\cong\;\im(\mathsf{nEll}(\pi))=\mathsf{nEll}(R/\mathfrak{a})
.
∎

The norm map

Define the inverse of
