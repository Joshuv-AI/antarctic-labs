Eye movement desensitization and reprocessing treatment for birth trauma: a scoping review
Authors: Susan O’Donovan, Laura Ward, Ruth Melia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTA traumatic childbirth can have negative consequences for mothers, as well as their children and partners. While eye movement desensitization and reprocessing (EMDR) is recommended in clinical guidelines as a treatment for post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) following childbirth, the specific application of EMDR for this population remains unclear. This scoping review aims to explore the extent of existing literature on the use of EMDR in the treatment of birth trauma. This review followed the PRISMA-ScR guidelines and included sources of evidence in which individuals or partners received EMDR treatment for birth trauma. Databases searched included PsycInfo, PubMed, the Cochrane Library, Embase, Scopus, and CINAHL, from inception of the database until approximately August 2025. Grey literature was also searched. Sources were limited to English-language publications. Two reviewers independently screened and extracted data from eligible quantitative and qualitative sources. Data were analysed in a descriptive and thematic manner. Data extraction included the sources’ characteristics, outcomes, and results. Findings are presented in tabular and narrative formats. Twenty-nine sources of evidence were included, comprising randomized control trials, qualitative research, systematic reviews, and grey literature. Overall, EMDR showed promise in treating birth trauma, although the diversity of the evidence type and methodological design varied. This scoping review indicates that EMDR may be a promising intervention for birth trauma. However, the current evidence base is limited and heterogeneous in terms of study design type and outcome measures used. Introduction

Eye movement desensitization and reprocessing (EMDR) is a structured intervention used to treat trauma and other adverse experiences (Shapiro, 2001). EMDR has strong evidence for treating post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), supported by over 30 randomized controlled trials (RCTs) (de Jongh et al., 2024). EMDR is also recommended in the treatment of PTSD in the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) clinical guidelines (NICE, 2018). The adaptive information processing (AIP) model (Shapiro, 2001) is a theoretical framework for the underlying mechanisms of EMDR. This model theorizes that owing to the high levels of stress experienced during a traumatic event, memories are not integrated into the individual’s adaptive memory system, resulting in these unprocessed memories being easily triggered. It is theorized that reprocessing these memories by recollecting the trauma and incorporating them into a more positive framework, while focusing on bilateral stimulation, can activate information processing and improve symptoms of trauma (Shapiro, 2001).

A traumatic birth has been defined as ‘a woman’s experience of interactions and/or events directly related to childbirth that caused overwhelming distressing emotions and reactions; leading to short and/ or long-term negative impacts on a woman’s health and wellbeing’ (Leinweber et al., 2022). A broad range of prevalence rates of birth trauma have been reported, from approximately 4% to 50% (Nagle et al., 2022; O’Donovan et al., 2014; Soet et al., 2003; Yildiz et al., 2017). PTSD differs from birth trauma, as it is classified in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5) to include specific symptoms such as re-experiencing traumas, avoidance of trauma reminders, hypervigilance, dissociation, and alterations in mood and cognitions (American Psychiatric Association, 2013). PTSD related to a traumatic childbirth can involve specific symptoms related to the birth, which can be triggered by postpartum appointments, resulting in consequences such as a mother avoiding necessary postpartum care and anxiety in caring for her child (Kranenburg et al., 2023). Other negative consequences of birth trauma can include fear of subsequent births (Hofberg & Brockington, 2000), as well as attachment and behavioural difficulties for the child (Van Sieleghem et al., 2022), and may also impact the mother’s relationship with her partner (Ayers et al., 2006). A comparative systematic review and meta-analysis has indicated that 4.7% of mothers and 1.2% of fathers meet the threshold for childbirth-related PTSD. This study also found that 12.3% of mothers and 1.3% of fathers experienced post-traumatic stress symptoms (PTSS) which were subthreshold to the PTSD criteria (Heyne et al., 2022). The NICE guidelines recommend trauma-focused cognitive behavioural therapy or EMDR for women who have experienced a traumatic birth (NICE, 2014).

Several systematic reviews examining interventions for childbirth-related PTSD have included EMDR interventions (Baas et al., 2020; Cunen et al., 2014; de Bruijn et al., 2020; Dekel et al., 2024; Furuta et al., 2018). However, only a limited number of studies where EMDR was the primary intervention met the inclusion criteria. Similarly, a scoping review (Slade et al., 2021) which investigated the clinical effectiveness of psychological interventions to reduce PTSD symptoms following childbirth included one study related to EMDR. They recommended that further research should address this gap in the literature. In addition, a recent scoping review (Shorey et al., 2023) evaluated psychological interventions for traumatic childbirth. The authors did not include EMDR in their review; however, they described it as a promising treatment intervention and recommended that it be explored in future research for birth trauma. Therefore, while the reviews in the current literature indicate promising results for EMDR as a treatment for birth trauma, they include a limited number and type of studies. Furthermore, there appears to be a need for a broader, more comprehensive scoping review of the literature regarding EMDR, specifically, as a psychological treatment for birth trauma.

Objectives

The main objective of this scoping review was to explore the extent of the currently available research regarding the use of EMDR in the treatment for birth trauma. This aim of this review was to answer the research question: what does the existing literature show about the use of EMDR as a psychological treatment for birth trauma?

Methods

A preliminary search of MEDLINE, the Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, and Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Evidence Synthesis was conducted on 14 January 2025, and no current or underway systematic reviews or scoping reviews on this topic were identified.

This review followed the JBI Manual for Evidence Synthesis Methodology for Scoping Reviews (Peters et al., 2020). The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses Extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) (Tricco et al., 2018) was used to report the scoping review. The scoping review protocol has been published (O’Donovan et al., 2025) and registered on Open Science Framework (https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/UJZ52).

Selection of sources of evidence and data charting process

This scoping review considered quantitative studies such as RCTs, pre- and post studies, prospective and retrospective cohort studies, and case studies. Qualitative studies were also considered, including, but not limited to, designs informed by phenomenology, grounded theory, and ethnography.

A pilot screening process was conducted before study selection occurred to ensure that the inclusion and exclusion criteria were feasible. A random sample of studies was chosen from the database after applying the search string. Two reviewers independently screened the title and abstract of these papers and compared their findings, and no discrepancies occurred. Covidence (Veritas Health Innovation, 2024) was used to manage the data.

The study selection process was completed in three phases.

Title and abstract screening: SO’D and LW independently screened all 126 title and abstracts which were identified from the database searches and grey literature, based on the inclusion criteria as outlined above. Each reviewer labelled each study as ‘include’, ‘exclude’, or ‘maybe’. Studies labelled ‘include’ and ‘maybe’ were moved to the full-text screening stage.

Data items

SO’D and LW independently extracted data using Covidence (Veritas Health Innovation, 2024) and Excel to compare their data extraction. SO’D extracted 100% of the data and LW extracted 30%. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion. Data extraction headings for the review paper included: authors, year of publication, origin, title, methods, outcome measures, intervention details, sample size, controls, and key findings. The primary outcomes of this review was the use of EMDR for birth trauma, as defined above. Secondary outcomes were included, such as changes in comorbid difficulties. Primary outcomes were prioritized, and secondary outcomes were added where possible.

Critical appraisal

The JBI critical appraisal checklist was used to assess the quality of included blogs discovered from the grey literature (McArthur et al., 2015). SO’D and LW independently appraised the studies, with no discrepancies.

Data synthesis

Data cleaning was conducted prior to data synthesis. This was carried out by SO’D and LW, by cross-referencing data with the original paper, double-checking entries, and removing duplicates or irrelevant information on Covidence (Veritas Health Innovation, 2024). Both reviewers ensured that data were stored consistently. Any changes made during data cleaning were documented. Data were grouped based on extraction headings. Where systematic reviews were identified, the number of studies that met our inclusion criteria were reviewed. A descriptive narrative synthesis was used (Popay et al., 2006)

Characteristics of sources of evidence

The 29 sources of evidence were published between 2006 and 2025, and included information about EMDR as a treatment for birth trauma (Table 4). The studies originated in mostly Western countries, with nine of the 29 papers being published in the Netherlands. This scoping review included 5 RCT's, 9 systematic reviews, 2 descriptive narratives (grey literature), 1 qualitative descriptive study, 4 case studies including one case series, 6 studies employed cohort, pre-post, cross sectional, experimental or quasi-experimental designs, 1 mixed method and 1 retrospective analysis was included. Synthesis of results

Study design

Early research into EMDR and birth trauma consisted of case studies or series, such as Madrid et al. (2006) and Stramrood et al. (2012), and pilot studies, such as Sandström et al. (2008), which reflect an initial interest in this area. Over time, more robust study designs have been used, with several RCTs and quasi-experimental designs emerging from 2016 onwards, such as Zolghadr et al. (2019), Chiorino et al. (2020), and Hendrix et al. (2025). The majority of sources found are quantitative in nature. Systematic reviews such as Driessen et al. (2024) and Dekel et al. (2024) provide evaluations of the literature; however, the findings are limited and do not seem to have captured a wide breadth of literature including grey literature sources.

Sample characteristics

Sample sizes varied widely, from single participant case studies to RCTs involving 74 women who received EMDR being compared to a control group of 66 (Hendrix et al., 2025). Most sources identified recruited women who had experienced a traumatic birth. One study included the perspectives of therapists (Hendrix et al., 2022), two grey literature sources were written by professionals who had worked with birth trauma and EMDR (Brown, 2023; Warren, 2022), and another poster abstract detailed a study that will include parents of pre-term infants (Craig et al., 2024). Only 13.8% of sources (Chiorino et al., 2020; Doherty et al., 2025; Hendrix et al., 2025; Kopmeiners et al., 2023) provided details on ethnicity; participants were predominantly white or European. Only two sources originated from non-Western settings, in Singapore and Iran, and neither of these studies reported participant ethnicities.

EMDR intervention

EMDR interventions demonstrated a wide variability in delivery. Most participants received between one and eight sessions, with durations typically ranging from 60 to 120 min. Sessions were conducted in person and online, with most studies following standard protocol or did not report the protocol used. Some studies using group protocols (De Divitiis & Luber, 2016), or specific EMDR methods such as the Butterfly Hug (Craig et al., 2024). This heterogeneity in session format, number, and duration creates difficulties in drawing conclusions about intervention delivery and study comparability.

Outcome measures

Several outcome measures were used across sources of evidence, which also complicates study comparisons. PTSD and trauma-related symptoms were the most frequently assessed outcome, using PCL-5, IES-R, CAPS-5, and the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS). Several studies also employed anxiety or depression scales, interviews, or other specific assessment tools such as the Mother-to-Infant Bonding Scale (MIBS), Peritraumatic Dissociative Experiences Questionnaire (PDEQ), skin conductance, and return-to-work scales.

Key findings

Findings should be interpreted cautiously owing to substantial heterogeneity across participant characteristics, study design, outcome measures, and intervention delivery. Several sources reported clinically significant reductions in trauma symptoms following EMDR for birth trauma (De Divitiis & Luber, 2016; Doherty et al., 2025; Kranenburg et al., 2022; Wetherell, 2022), with preliminary results indicating promising findings (Craig et al., 2024; van Deursen-Gelderloos & Bakker, 2015). Although not statistically significant, Sandström et al. (2008) reported sustained symptom reduction in three of four participants at 1–3 years’ follow-up.

Some RCTs reported statistically significant reductions in PTSD symptoms or related difficulties (Chiorino et al., 2020; Hendrix et al., 2025; Wetherell, 2023; Zolghadr et al., 2019), while others found non-significant differences despite greater improvements in EMDR groups (Kopmeiners et al., 2023). Outcomes varied by measurement tool, with significance reached on some measures but not others (Hendrix et al., 2025; Wetherell, 2023), and effects were not always maintained at follow-up (Chiorino et al., 2020; Wetherell, 2023). Notably, there was heterogeneity in control conditions, with some control groups receiving two telephone calls (Hendrix et al., 2025) and others receiving three sessions of stabilization (Wetherell, 2023), further limiting comparability.

Qualitative sources do not establish efficacy but provide insights into participants’ experiences. Themes including empowerment, feelings of relief, comfort and understanding, emotional release, and reduced trauma symptoms were described by (Beck et al., 2013). Madrid et al. (2006) noted improved maternal bonding. Baas et al. (2020) described a case that no longer met PTSD diagnostic criteria, while Pašalić and Hasanović (2018) reported reduced PTSD symptoms and increased confidence in motherhood. Clinician perspectives (Brown, 2023; Warren, 2022) similarly described observing reductions in intrusive imagery, improved sleep, greater emotional regulation, and healing attachment difficulties following EMDR.

Hendrix et al. (2022) found that EMDR was the preferred intervention by therapists for treating PTSD for pregnant and non-pregnant women, particularly when PTSD was related to a previous birth experience. These findings highlight the potential benefits of EMDR while adding perspectives on the mechanisms of therapeutic change from both the participants’ and clinicians’ viewpoints.

All studies identified in systematic reviews that met the inclusion criteria have already been detailed in the sections above. These reviews generally concluded that trauma-focused psychological therapies including EMDR indicate promising results as a treatment intervention for childbirth-related PTSD. However, they also highlight the need for further research in this area.

Discussion

Summary of evidence

This scoping review identified a growing body of literature exploring EMDR as a treatment for birth trauma, with 29 primary sources of evidence included, which comprised a variety of methodological designs such as RCTs, systematic reviews, descriptive narrative sources (grey literature), case studies and case series, cohort, pre–post, quasi-experimental, qualitative or mixed method, and retrospective analyses.

Across the included literature, several sources reported reductions in trauma-related symptoms following EMDR. Notably, positive effects were observed after as little as one EMDR session compared to treatment as usual (Chiorino et al., 2020). While standard protocols were used in many cases, adaptations do highlight the flexibility of EMDR interventions. However, substantial heterogeneity exists across sources in terms of study design, intervention delivery, sample size, and outcome measures, limiting the ability to draw direct comparisons between results.

Most research reported on was conducted in Western countries, with a relatively high proportion of sources being conducted in the Netherlands and only two studies originating from non-Western settings. Only 13.8% of sources reported ethnicity, and indicated predominantly white or European participant ethnic backgrounds. This limits the generalizability of findings to more diverse populations, and may indicate a concerning underrepresentation of some groups in EMDR services. Doherty et al. (2025) highlight the need for more ethnically diverse samples in this area, which is important as mothers often experience obstetric racism as a core aspect of birth trauma (Dmowska et al., 2024) and face barriers accessing perinatal mental health services (Jankovic et al., 2020). Moreover, the current literature primarily focuses on mothers, with limited exploration of partners’ experiences, despite evidence suggesting that birth trauma adversely effects the relationship between mothers and partners (Ayers et al., 2006).

Finally, there were fewer qualitative sources of evidence compared to quantitative research, limiting the available information on the lived experience of EMDR. Furthermore, few sources included long-term follow-up data, which may limit the understanding of EMDR for birth trauma over time.

While study protocols were excluded from this review owing to limited extractable data, it is apparent that there is an emerging body of research protocols in this area. For example, Baas et al. (2017) published a multicentre RCT protocol, the ‘The OptiMUM-study’. While a case study from this trial was included in this review, further results are not yet available.

Comparison with previous literature

The findings of this review broadly support existing clinical guidelines recommending that EMDR should be offered to women who suffer from childbirth-related PTSD (NICE, 2014). However, the limited number of studies and small sample sizes indicate the need for further research.

The systematic reviews included in this review often addressed perinatal trauma treatments more generally, reflecting a lack of focus on EMDR for birth trauma. This review helps to address this gap by synthesizing studies specifically related to EMDR as a stand-alone intervention for birth trauma, providing a more targeted understanding of its potential efficacy in this context.

The AIP model, proposed by Shapiro (2001), posits that EMDR facilitates the reprocessing of traumatic memories, allowing for a reduction in traumatic symptoms. The consistent symptom improvements observed across sources in this review indicate indirect support for this model. Qualitative sources indicate empowerment, emotional release, and increased confidence post-EMDR, broadly aligning with the AIP model, which suggests that maladaptively stored memories become less emotionally charged after EMDR (Shapiro, 2001). However, the mechanisms for change are not clear from these results. Moreover, Wetherell (2022) reported a non-significant 4% change in skin conductance response after EMDR, which challenges the assumption that when memories are reprocessed there is an improvement in physiological responses. Therefore, further research may be needed to understand the mechanisms through which EMDR effects both psychological and physiological symptoms in birth trauma.

Strengths and limitations

This review contributes to the field by focusing explicitly on EMDR for birth trauma, and includes a broad range of study designs and methodologies, by capturing both qualitative and quantitative research. A strength of this review is the comprehensive search strategy, which used several major databases and grey literature to ensure the inclusion of all relevant sources in the area of the use of EMDR for birth trauma. Contrary to previous reviews such as those by Dekel et al. (2024) and Lapp et al. (2010), this review did not exclude traumatic events such as stillbirth, and instead used a broad definition of birth trauma, including sources where participants described their birth experience as traumatic, regardless of a formal diagnosis of PTSD.

However, several limitations should be noted. The English-language restriction may have resulted in relevant studies being excluded, which may have contributed to the apparent limitations in diverse samples found in this review. Further reviews should aim to include research conducted in non-English-speaking countries. Furthermore, the predominance of sources from Western contexts also limits the generalizability of findings.

Another limitation of this review is heterogeneity across sources, reflecting the broad impact of birth trauma and EMDR, but limiting comparability. Methodological limitations, small sample sizes, and inconsistent EMDR delivery suggest that while EMDR appears promising, findings cannot be widely generalized and further research is needed. This scoping review cannot determine EMDR’s effectiveness for traumatic birth experiences or compare it to other trauma-focused interventions. The lack of quality appraisal across sources further limits conclusions, highlighting instead the emerging literature in this area and gaps for future research.

Conclusion

The aim of this scoping review was to identify the existing literature on the use of EMDR as a treatment for birth trauma. Overall, the available evidence suggests that EMDR shows promise as an effective intervention for individuals who have experienced birth trauma. While the emerging evidence is encouraging, it remains limited in scope, methodological consistency, and diversity of participants. There is a need for more RCTs with larger sample sizes and further qualitative research to better understand the mechanisms of change from the perspective of the participants. There is also a need for further research focusing on long-term impact, involving more diverse samples, and including the perspective of partners, to strengthen the evidence base and further inform clinical practice.
