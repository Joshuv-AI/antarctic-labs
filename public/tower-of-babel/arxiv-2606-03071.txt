Evolution of cooperation in two-level Prisoner’s Dilemma
Yaroslav Ispolatov

Abstract




Abstract

We consider continuous Prisoner’s Dilemma played in spatial setting by
group-structured populations. The population dynamics consists of
individual-level birth and death and group-level fission and
extinction events. Each individual plays games with all other individuals
within their group, while
groups play games against their nearest neighbours.
Payoffs from individual-level
games affect birth rates of individuals, and payoffs from group-level
games affect group extinction and fission probabilities.
We show that a certain level of cooperation is maintained
due to specific between-group dynamics even though the within-group
evolution by itself always results in a complete loss of
cooperation. The spatial nature of games and resulting fissioning and extinction events is essential for the evolution of cooperation: without it cooperation is never maintained. Analyzing various
scenarios of between-group fission and extinction events, we find that
higher levels of cooperation evolve when the selection affecting
fission and extinction events is local rather than global.

Keywords: continuous prisoners’ dilemma, group selection, games in space.

1
Introduction

Evolution of cooperation has been an important
topic in biology and social sciences
[
Axelrod and Hamilton, 1981
]
. Game theoretical methods are perfectly suited and have been widely used
to investigate this problem
[
Sigmund and Nowak, 1999
,
Traulsen and Glynatsi, 2023
]
. The simplest and the most extreme example of such games is the
Prisoner’s Dilemma, where individual’s contribution adds to well-being of other players only while the costs are born by the individual itself
[
Doebeli and Hauert, 2005
]
. This makes the zero-cooperative outcome inevitable and necessitates various modifications that explain the persistence of cooperation. A notable example of such extensions is the spatial structuring of population, where games are played locally among close neighbours. It has been shown that both in the discrete (with two strategies, absolute cooperators and absolute defectors) and continuous (with the level of cooperation varying continuously between these two limits) versions of Prisoner’s Dilemma cooperation can evolve due to formation of spatially segregated domains
[
Doebeli and Hauert, 2005
]
. In these domains, the contributions of cooperators are shared predominantly among fellow cooperators, resulting in their higher reproduction or lower death rates.
However, the order of upgrade events, death followed by birth (DB) vs. birth followed by death (BD), becomes of crucial importance for the evolutionary outcome in the spatial Prisoner’s Dilemma. In its simplest form, the DB events are implemented as the replacement of a random player by a close copy of its best-performing co-playing neighbour. Under a certain constraints on the benefit and cost functions and the spatial arrangements of players, the cooperation persists under the DB update
[
Ohtsuki et al., 2006
,
Taylor et al., 2007
,
Zukewich et al., 2013
,
Hauert and Doebeli, 2021
]
. Yet under the BD update, usually implemented as a replacement of a random neighbour of the best-performing player by an imperfect copy of that best performer, the cooperation does not evolve. The reason for this, also known as the ”cancellation effect”
[
Akdeniz and van Veelen, 2020
]
, is the inability of cooperators to ’export’ their cooperation to other regions of the system
[
Ohtsuki et al., 2006
,
Taylor et al., 2007
]
. Since the best performing players are those within the domains of cooperators, their neighbours are often cooperators as well, so the BD events replace the cooperators by cooperators in the bulk of domains. At the same time, these domains and the interfaces between the domains of high and low cooperation (defection) are susceptible to the invasion of defectors
[
Ohtsuki et al., 2006
]
.

Individuals often live in groups and sometimes evolve features, including cooperative behaviour, distinct from their solitary peers. Group evolution and selection mechanisms have been broadly explored and are often invoked to explain the evolution of cooperation in its most general form (e.g.
[
Traulsen and Nowak, 2006
]
)
and in more specific examples
of evolution of distinct individual and group strategies due to competitive pressure
[
Jackson, 1977
]
, predation and evasion of being preyed on
[
Lang and Farine, 2017
,
Hebblewhite and Pletscher, 2002
]
, and even the evolution of group-forming itself, e.g. in the form of monogamy vs polygamy in primates and humans
[
French et al., 2018
]
. The formation of groups is often accompanied by the competition and other ecological interactions between the members of the same the group, which results in two-level – within and between groups – selection. Various models have taken into account such multi-level selection. However, the group-level events are often postulated to depend only on simple characteristics of those groups themselves, such as the within-group level of cooperation or the group size, rather than outcomes of between-group games
[
Akdeniz and van Veelen, 2020
,
Cooney and Mori, 2022
,
Cooney, 2019
,
Traulsen and Nowak, 2006
,
Luo, 2014
]
.

Recently we have studies several 2-level scenarios of games being played both within and between groups with realistic group fissioning and extinction rules
[
Simon et al., 2024b
,
Simon et al., 2024a
]
. An interesting observation made in
[
Simon et al., 2024a
]
concerns the conflict between the incompatible outcomes of within-group and between-groups games, such as when the branching snowdrift game is played both within and between groups. We found that depending on the relative strength of within group and between group selection, the branching at the level with stronger selection dominated while the branching at the other level was suppressed. Assessing the effect of random group fissioning we concluded that a stronger randomization during fissioning leads to an increase in the between-group variance, thus enhancing the group-level selection.

In the work we extend the results of
[
Simon et al., 2024b
,
Simon et al., 2024a
]
, assuming that groups are embedded in space and games are played only between the neighbouring groups. We focus our study on Prisoner’s Dilemma for two reasons: it is the best-known game with the simplest rules directly pertinent to the evolution of cooperation, and because of the known crucial role of spacial structure in the evolvability of cooperation. The principal question is whether cooperation can evolve and persist in the case when the non-spatial within-group games inevitably lead to defection and the only hope for cooperation comes from the spatially structured between-group games. Another important question we analyze is how various forms of local and global updates affect the evolution of cooperation.

We show that for a certain balance between the strength of within-group and between-group selection, the between-group games indeed can reverse the within-group trend to defection and maintain cooperation. Furthermore, we find that local update rules that depend on the payoff of neighbours of a randomly chosen group are often more beneficial for maintaining cooperation than the global ones, in which the system-wise best or worst group is chosen.

Our model of spatial group structured populations will be defined mathematically in the next section. Apart from its spatial structure and update rules, it is similar to the models used to study group selection in
[
Simon, 2010
,
Simon et al., 2013
,
Simon and Pilosov, 2016
,
Simon et al., 2024b
,
Simon et al., 2024a
]
:

•

Individuals play games against their group mates; and groups play games against their neighbouring groups.

•

Individual birth rates depend on payoff in the within-group game (higher payoffs lead to higher birth rates). Individual death rates depend on the size of the group.

•

Payoffs of between-group games depend on the groups’ level of cooperation, which are averages of
the cooperation levels of individuals within those group.

•

Groups are updated according to payoffs of between-group games. The group chosen for reproduction randomly fissions into two daughter groups, replacing itself and the group targeted for extinction.

2
The Model

We study a group structured population where each individual has a level of cooperation
x\in[0,1]
which does not change during its lifetime. An individual’s birth rate depends on its expected payoff in the game with all other individuals in its group,
which may change during its lifetime due to changes in the composition of its group.
An individual’s death rate is proportional to the size of its group, constraining the group size even in the presense of group-level events.

The groups also play games but only against their neighbours. Each group’s strategy
\bar{x}\in[0,1]
is the average of the strategies of all individuals in the group. Group games are spatially structured: groups are positioned on a ring and play games with their neighbours to the left and to the right.
Although an individual’s level of cooperation does not change during its lifetime,
groups change in time due to internal individual-level births and deaths events.
They can also change suddenly due to group-level update events:
a group fissions, breaking randomly into two pieces, which replace the ancestral group and its neighbour marked for extinction.

2.1
Within-group games

The exact simulation of individual within-group and between-group events is very slow due to the large number of births and deaths.
We therefore chose to use the “hybrid” method for simulating group-structured populations, described in
[
Simon and Nielsen, 2012
,
Simon et al., 2024b
,
Simon et al., 2024a
]
.
In the hybrid method the state of a group is approximated by the continuous population density and changes deterministically by a partial differential equation (PDE), while in the group-level events groups participate as discrete entities. The PDE governing population dynamics within the groups is derived from the stochastic rate functions (for births and deaths) and is justified by large population limits
[
Champagnat et al., 2006
]
.
In our numerical experiments the groups are fairly large (around 100 individuals on average) so the hybrid method is appropriate.
Using the PDE, the state of a group becomes a continuous density,
n(x),~x\in[0,1]
. In the continuous approximation of the within-group population dynamics, the per capita birth rate becomes

b(x)=\beta\frac{e^{s\psi(x)}}{\eta^{-1}\int_{0}^{1}e^{s\psi(y)}n(y)dy},

(1)

where

\psi(x)=\eta^{-1}\int_{0}^{1}P(x,u)n(u)du

(2)

is the expected payoff to an individual with type
x
from games against all individuals in the group,

\displaystyle\eta=\int_{0}^{1}n(x)dx

(3)

is the size of a group, which normalizes the payoff, and

\displaystyle P(x,u)=Bu-Cx

(4)

is the functional form of the payoff to individual
x
in its Prisoner’s dilemma game against
u
in with linear benefit
B(x)=Bx
and cost
C(x)=Cx
functions. Here
\beta
is the birth rate coefficient and the parameter
s\geq 0
defines the strength of selection. This parametrization allows us to separate the within-group population dynamics, which defines the relaxation of the total population of new (recently fissioned) groups to their equilibrium size, from the rate of evolution of cooperation level
x
. (Note that the strength of selection is defined by the combination of
\beta
and
s
, so the very small valuers of
s
used in our simulation should not be interpreted as a very weak selection as they are compensated by the large values of
\beta
).
The game payoffs are exponentiated (so that birth rates are positive for any cost and benefit) and normalized to ensure that the average birth rate in a group is equal to
\beta
, and hence is decoupled from the game.

The per capita death rate in a group with
\eta
individuals does not depend on
x
and is proportional to the group size.

D=\delta\eta

(5)

These birth and death rates define the PDE for a group population density
n(x,t)

\frac{\partial n(x,t)}{\partial t}=\left\{b[n(.),x]-D[n(.)]\right\}n(x,t)+R\frac{\partial^{2}n(x,t)}{\partial x^{2}}

(6)

where a diffusion term with a small coefficient

To implement the PDE for a continuos population density

Simon et al., 2024a

2.2
Between-group games and the group update

The group-level events are discrete with the dynamics determined by the payoffs of between-group games, which in their turn depend on the within-group levels of cooperation
\{\bar{x}\}_{i}
. As we specified earlier, groups are positioned on a circle and play games with their nearest neighbours on both sides, resulting in a payoff to group
i

\displaystyle P_{i}=B(\{\bar{x}\}_{i-1}+\{\bar{x}\}_{i+1})/2-C\{\bar{x}\}_{i}.

(7)

Groups fission and go extinct and since the number of groups is set to be constant, each fissioning event is accompanied by an extinction one. A target group fissions randomly with one daughter group staying at the same site while another daughter group replacing one of its neighbours. The fissioning scenario is similar to that implemented in our previous work,
[
Simon et al., 2024b
]
, were we explored the effect of fissioning randomness on the group-level evolution. During fissioning, the population of each bin
w_{k}
,
k=1,\ldots,m
, of target group is split randomly between the corresponding bins of two daughter groups.
Let
\hat{w}_{k}\in[0,w_{k}]
be the number of individuals in the
k
th bin of the parent group that go to the
k
th bin of daughter group 1, while the remainder go to daughter group 2. We choose

\displaystyle\hat{w}_{k}=\hat{\mathcal{N}}\left(\frac{w_{k}}{2},\frac{r^{2}w_{k}}{4}\right),

(8)

where
\hat{\mathcal{N}}(\mu,\sigma^{2})
is a Normally distributed random variable, confined between
0
and
2\mu
and
r
is the fission randomization scale. We showed in
[
Simon et al., 2024b
]
that for small
r
, when the Normal distribution of
\hat{w}_{i}
is unperturbed by the cutoff
0\leq\hat{w}_{k}\leq w_{k}
, such fissioning results in the variance between two daughter groups immediately after fissioning being proportional to
r^{2}
.
r=1
is a continuous analog of individuals flipping a coin to choose a group to join. The only difference from the setup used in
[
Simon et al., 2024b
]
is that here we consider the same
m
bins both for the numerical solution of (
6
) and for fissioning.

The key to the group-level events are the update rules that determine how to choose the target group and which of the neighbours is replaced. For single-level games two update schemes are traditionally considered, the ”Death-Birth” (DB) and the ”Birth-Dearth” (BD). In DB the target player (a group in our case) is chosen randomly and is replaced by a daughter of its highest payoff neighbour, while in BD the highest payoff group fissions and replaces its random neighbour. Here we broaden the selection of update schemes and consider two parametrized families of scenarios:

1.

Best-Worst (BW) update. The globally highest payoff group fissions into the spot of its worst performing neighbour. It adds the selection between neighbours (the ”Death” choice) to the traditional BD update, where the choice of a neighbour to be removed is random.

2.

Worst-Best (WB) update. The globally lowest payoff group is replaced by its highest payoff neighbour. The DB update is a particular case of WB in the limit of vanishing selection on the globally worst group.

These scenarios are parametrized by the scaling constant
\sigma
in the relation between the probability to be chosen as the target group,
Prob_{i}
, and the group’s payoff,
P_{i}
:

\displaystyle Prob_{i}=\frac{e^{\sigma P_{i}}}{\sum_{j}e^{\sigma P_{j}}}

(9)

in the BW scenario, where the best-performing group is chosen, and

\displaystyle Prob_{i}=\frac{e^{-\sigma P_{i}}}{\sum_{j}e^{-\sigma P_{j}}}

(10)

in the WB scenario, where the worst-performing group is chosen. This probabilistic targeting is often called the ”Softmax” function, where by similarity with the Boltzmann distribution,
\sigma
is referred to as the ”inverse temperature”.
When
\sigma
is small, the selection of the target group is almost random and becomes totally equiprobable for
\sigma=0
.
When
\sigma
is large, that is,
\sigma P\gg 1
, the absolutely best (or worst) performing group is chosen. In simulations where the number of groups is inevitably finite, the
\sigma\rightarrow\infty
limit often results in artifacts such as the persistent targeting of the same best or worst group. The constant
\sigma
could also be thought of as the scale of the group-level selection pressure.

2.3
Simulation

The simulation works as follows.
G
groups are arranged on a circle and their number stays constant throughout the simulation. In each group, bin populations
w_{j}
are seeded randomly with the same average, so the initial states of all groups are statistically identical and the average initial cooperation level is
\langle\bar{x}\rangle\approx 1/2
.

The update proceeds with numerical integration of discretized version of Eq. (
6
) with a fairly small timestep. The scales
\beta
and
\delta
of population dynamics are chosen to be large, so the group populations relax to their equilibrium size after initiation or fissioning very fast on the evolutionary timescale (which is determined by both
\beta
and
s
, or their product
\beta s
in the limit of weak selection). Once every
1/G

This time increment means that on average each group is updated once per time unit, making it possible to compare the strengths of within-group and between-group selections. Depending on the update rules, the target group is selected with the probability given by (
9

10

3.4
Discussion

We have explored the scenario of the Prisoner’s Dilemma being played at two levels: within groups and between groups that are positioned on a circle. While the within-group games are played between all group members, the between-group games are played with the nearest neighbours. Despite the well-known fact that the within-group games bring the cooperation level down to zero, the between-group games turn the cooperation demise around and maintain the within-group and system-average cooperation at some non-vanishing level. For both considered scenarios, BW and WB, the cooperation is shown to persist for a range of relative strengths of between-group and within-group games and for various values of the parameter that controls the randomization during group fissioning. Overall, both types of updates allow the cooperation to evolve, overcoming the selection towards zero cooperation due to within-group games.

In addition to this general conclusion, another trend is observed that might constitute a common principle: the global selection is not necessary and even sometimes detrimental to the evolution of cooperation. In our model, this selection is controlled by the parameters
\sigma
that determines how strict is the choice of the system-wise best-performing (or worst-performing) group. When
\sigma=0
, the selection is totally random, while for larger
\sigma
, the absolutely best- (or worst-)performing group is targeted. We observed that for the BW selection, the highest cooperation level evolves for
\sigma=0
in the case of weak within-group selection (small
s
) or strong fissioning randomization (large
r
), while in the complimentary cases the highest cooperation level is achieved for moderate between-group selection,
\sigma\approx 1
. Even more so, the totally random (
\sigma=0
) WB update is always the most beneficial for the cooperation level. At the same time, the local selection in between-group games is essential for the evolution of cooperation. The BW update without local selection, that is, when a random neighbour is replaced by a fission copy of the best-performing group, is exactly the BD update, well-known to result in zero cooperation even without the within-group games. Same is true for the WB update when a random neighbour fission product replaces the worst-performing group. So the common for both the BW and WB updates principle can be formulated as that the local competition is vital for the cooperation maintenance, while the global competition is optional and often unnecessary.
