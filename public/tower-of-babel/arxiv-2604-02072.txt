Sparse Probabilistic Richardson Extrapolation
Chris. J. Oates, Richard Howey, Toni Karvonen, School of Mathematics, Statistics, Physics, Newcastle University, UK, Research Software Engineering, Newcastle University, UK, School of Engineering Sciences, Lappeenranta–Lahti University of Technology LUT, FI

Abstract
Almost every numerical task can be cast as extrapolation with respect to the fidelity or tolerance parameters of a consistent numerical method. This perspective enables probabilistic uncertainty quantification and optimal experimental design functionality to be deployed, and also unlocks the potential for the convergence of numerical methods to be accelerated. Recent work established Probabilistic Richardson Extrapolation as a proof-of-concept, demonstrating how parallel multi-fidelity simulation can be used to accelerate simulation from a whole-heart model. However, the number of simulations was required to increase super-exponentially in d d , the number of tolerance parameters appearing in the numerical method. This paper develops a refined notion of extrapolation dimension , drastically reducing this simulation requirement when multiple tolerance parameters feature in the numerical method. Sparsity-exploiting methodology is developed that is simultaneously simpler and more powerful compared to earlier work, and this is accompanied by sharp theoretical guarantees and substantial empirical support. Keywords: computer models, extrapolation, Gaussian process, kernel methods, uncertainty quantification

Introduction

Mathematical models are routinely used to represent mechanisms hypothesised to govern phenomena of interest.
Inference for the parameters of these models demands a comparison of simulations from the model against a real-world dataset.
The computational challenge is two-fold; to produce simulations from the model, and to search across the parameter space for values that provide an acceptable fit to the dataset.
As a motivating example, the whole-heart model of
Strocchi et al. 2023
involves dozens of parameters, and each simulation requires hundreds of CPU hours on high performance computing equipment.
To drive progress in this, and other domains where sophisticated mathematical models are used, there is an urgent need for methodology to facilitate simulation and inference at reduced computational cost.
The focus of this paper is on the first challenge; accurate simulation of mathematical models with a limited compute budget.

Almost every numerical task can be cast as extrapolation with respect to the fidelity or tolerance parameters of a consistent numerical method.
Abstractly, we enumerate all of the
discretisation parameters
involved in approximate simulation from a mathematical model as
\mathbf{x}
, such that each component of
\mathbf{x}
controls the error due to a particular aspect of discretisation; for example, in a physical simulation
x_{1}
could be a time step size,
x_{2}
could be the width of a spatial mesh, and
x_{3}
could be an error tolerance for an adaptive numerical method.
The simulator output is denoted
f(\mathbf{x})
, and we assume that the simulator is consistent, meaning that
f
is continuous at
\mathbf{0}
and exact simulation of the mathematical model corresponds to
f(\mathbf{0})
.
The computational cost of simulating
f(\mathbf{x})
will be denoted
c(\mathbf{x})
, with
\lim_{\mathbf{x}\rightarrow\mathbf{0}}c(\mathbf{x})=\infty
being typical.

The
extrapolation
task that we consider in this work is to produce an accurate approximation to
f(\mathbf{0})
, based on a dataset
\{f(\mathbf{x}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
, such that the computational cost associated with running the simulations in the dataset remains within a prescribed budget.
In the case of a single scalar discretisation parameter, so-called
extrapolation methods
such as Richardson extrapolation
(
Richardson 1911
)
(a.k.a. polynomial extrapolation) are a classical yet powerful tool to reduce the cost of simulation, enabling the convergence of foundational (deterministic) numerical methods, such as quadratures and finite difference methods, to be accelerated.
A textbook treatment is provided in
Brezinski and Zaglia 2013
.

However, these tools were not developed with modern computer codes in mind, where multiple continua are discretised and convergence orders are not easily analysed.
A statistical perspective on multivariate extrapolation offers not just an opportunity to accelerate simulation from complex mathematical models, but also the potential to use established statistical techniques to select a parsimonious regression model, the data-driven learning of convergence orders, integrating extrapolation uncertainty into decision-making, and opening up the potential for experimental design to be used to select the configurations
\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
at which simulation is to be performed.
More broadly, new statistical insights into extrapolation could be leveraged in wide-ranging applications, such as extrapolation of treatment effects
(
Fauvel et al. 2025
)
, extrapolation of survival curves
(
Kearns et al. 2021
)
, and causal inference
(
Kong et al. 2024
)
.

A first step towards statistical extrapolation methods was made in
Oates et al. 2025
, who modelled the data-generating function
f
using a
numerical analysis-informed
Gaussian process, leading to a method called
Gauss–Richardson Extrapolation
(GRE); we provide further details in
Section

2.3
.
The effectiveness of Gauss–Richardson Extrapolation was demonstrated using the aforementioned whole-heart model of
Strocchi et al. 2023
, where extrapolation of clinical quantities of interest (e.g. the maximum volume of each chamber during a heartbeat) with respect to discretisation parameters
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},x_{2})
of the simulator (in that case,
x_{1}
was a time step size and
x_{2}
was a spatial mesh width) was considered.
In particular, the authors demonstrated that extrapolation of low-fidelity data can be more accurate than running one high-fidelity simulation at comparable computational cost, a finding that has the potential to change how cardiac simulations are performed.

Despite this initial success, there is a major limitation to Gauss–Richardson Extrapolation; its theoretical justification is only meaningful in settings where the dimension
d=\mathrm{dim}(\mathbf{x})
is extremely small.
Indeed, the established error bounds for Gauss–Richardson Extrapolation
(
Oates et al. 2025
, Theorems 2 and 4)
hold only when a minimum number of data
n_{\min}
are provided, and this number can be lower-bounded by
(2^{d}d!)^{d}
, which is super-exponential in
d
.
For
d=1
, this lower bound is
n_{\min}\geq 2
, which is best-possible since for extrapolation at least two data points are required.
For
d=2
, this lower bound is
n_{\min}\geq 64
, which is large but could still be practical.
However, if we consider
d=4
discretisation parameters, Gauss–Richardson Extrapolation requires a minimum of
2\times 10^{10}
simulations to be performed.
Given that even a single simulation of a computational cardiac model can require hundreds of CPU hours on high performance computing, this data requirement is clearly impractical.
We are led to urgently ask the question,
is better dimension dependence possible in statistical extrapolation?

This paper provides a positive answer in two different respects:
First, we present an alternative approach called
Sparse Probabilistic Richardson Extrapolation
(SPRE).
As with Gauss–Richardson Extrapolation, our Sparse Probabilistic Richardson Extrapolation approach is based on Gaussian processes, but here we leverage a new notion of
extrapolation dimension
which enables us to drastically reduce the number of simulations that need to be performed.
Indeed, we establish convergence acceleration guarantees for Sparse Probabilistic Richardson Extrapolation for which the data requirement is typically polynomial in
d
, as opposed to super-exponential.
Second, through several detailed case studies we identify low extrapolation dimension as a general phenomenon in real-world applications, in the sense that all of the simulators we consider exhibit some form of low effective dimension (or
sparsity
) which can be directly exploited in Sparse Probabilistic Richardson Extrapolation.
Together, these contributions establish sparsity-exploiting extrapolation methods as a novel frontier for acceleration of complex simulators.

2.1.2
Standing Assumptions on the Simulator

For this paper we focus on scalar-valued simulator output
f:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, but generalisation to multivariate and infinite-dimensional simulator output can be considered in an analogous manner to
Oates et al. 2025
.
It is assumed that
\mathbf{0}\in\mathcal{X}\subset[0,\infty)^{d}
and that
\mathbf{0}
is an accumulation point of
\mathcal{X}
, so that in principle it is possible to run simulations to arbitrary precision for a sufficiently high computational budget.

To formulate our main results we will assume that there exist an index set
\mathbf{0}\in A\subset\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
and coefficients
\beta_{\bm{\alpha}}
for which the residual

\displaystyle R(\mathbf{x})=f(\mathbf{x})-\sum_{\bm{\alpha}\in A}\beta_{\bm{\alpha}}\mathbf{x}^{\bm{\alpha}}

(1)

satisfies
R(\mathbf{x})=o(f(\mathbf{x})-f(\mathbf{0}))
as
\mathbf{x}\rightarrow\mathbf{0}
.
The set
A
will be referred to as the
index
set; note that
A
must contain more than just the element
\mathbf{0}
for this property to hold.
It is not assumed that the coefficients
\beta_{\bm{\alpha}}
are known at the outset.
This assumption implies that the convergence rate of
f(\mathbf{x})
to
f(\mathbf{0})
is sub-optimal and thus can in principle be accelerated.
That is, a rate-optimal numerical method for the numerical task at hand would
not
satisfy (
1
).
So why is extrapolation of interest?
High-order numerical methods are usually less stable compared to tried-and-tested low-order numerical methods; it is the reason that the trapezoidal rule is routinely used to numerically integrate smooth functions, and it is the reason that piecewise linear finite elements are routinely used to discretise partial differential equations, even if the (weak) solution to the partial differential equation admits higher than first order (weak) derivatives.
Further, since error analysis is usually intractable for complex simulators, the designer of a simulator will typically not risk using a high-order numerical method whose assumptions cannot be established.
Taken together, we can expect (
1
) to hold in a broad range of applications where simulators are used.

2.1.3
Extrapolation Dimension

The general set-up in (
1
) encompasses a variety of expansions, with the cardinality of the index set
A
being related to the data requirement for the extrapolation task:

Definition 1

(Extrapolation dimension)
.

The
extrapolation dimension

d_{\mathrm{ext}}(f)
of a simulator
f
is the minimal value of
\mathrm{dim}(A)
, i.e. the minimal cardinality of the index set
\mathbf{0}\in A\subset\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
for which
f
has a non-trivial representation (
1
).

Put in practical terms, we will see that
d_{\mathrm{ext}}(f)
is the minimum number of simulations required to accelerate convergence to
f(\mathbf{0})
; i.e. it is the number of coefficients
\beta_{\bm{\alpha}}
in an expansion of the form (
1
) that would need to be estimated.
To illustrate the definition with a toy example, if
f(\mathbf{x})=f(\mathbf{0})+\bm{\beta}^{\top}\mathbf{x}
is an affine function with
\bm{\beta}\neq\mathbf{0}
, then
d_{\mathrm{eff}}=1+\|\bm{\beta}\|_{0}
, and indeed a minimum number
n_{\min}=1+\|\bm{\beta}\|_{0}
of distinct data
f(\mathbf{x}_{i})
are required to recover
f(\mathbf{0})
under this affine model.
(Here
\|\bm{\beta}\|_{0}
denote the number of non-zero entries in the vector
\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.)

A central argument to this paper, which we will substantiate with empirical support, is that
if
a simulator admits an expansion of the form (
1
), then the cardinality of the index set
A
is typically (relatively) small.
For example, if we consider a continuum
\mathcal{X}\subset[0,\infty)^{d}
and a simulator
f
that is
s
times continuously differentiable at
\mathbf{0}
, for which not all derivatives vanish at
\mathbf{0}
, then from Taylor’s theorem there is a non-trivial expansion (
1
) with index set
A=\{\bm{\alpha}:|\bm{\alpha}|\leq s,\;\bm{\alpha}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}\}
for which
R(\mathbf{x})=o(\sum_{|\bm{\alpha}|=s}\mathbf{x}^{\bm{\alpha}})
, and the dimension dependence of
\mathrm{dim}(A)\asymp d^{s}
is polynomial.
Informally, we say that the extrapolation task
f
exhibits
sparsity
, in the sense that only a (relatively) small number of non-zero regression coefficients
\beta_{\bm{\alpha}}
need to be considered
1
1

1

The terminology is consistent with variable selection in linear regression, where
sparsity
refers to the number of non-zero regression coefficients, i.e.
\|\bm{\beta}\|_{0}
in the notation of the previous paragraph.
.

However, many important numerical methods do not have tolerance parameters defined on a continuum
\mathcal{X}
; for example, the accuracy of certain cubature methods depends on the number of cubature nodes that are used.
This precludes the use of Taylor expansions in general, but nevertheless an expansion of the form (
1

A simple example illustrating the case of a discrete set

is presented in

1

Example 1

(Extrapolation sparsity for a simple cubature method)
.

The Riemann midpoint approximation to the integral

If

\displaystyle f(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{0\leq|\bm{\alpha}|\leq s}\beta_{\bm{\alpha}}\mathbf{x}^{2\bm{\alpha}}+O(\|\mathbf{x}\|^{2s+2})

More generally, extrapolation sparsity has been established (albeit not in this terminology) in the numerical analysis literature for many numerical methods, both for cubature

However, until now the questions of whether – or to what extent – extrapolation sparsity holds for complex simulators has not been studied.
Further, if one was able to establish extrapolation sparsity for a complex simulator in a data-driven way, then it is natural to ask whether this sparsity can be exploited within the extrapolation task.

2.3
Gauss–Richardson Extrapolation

To first statistical extrapolation method to offer provable convergence acceleration was the Gauss–Richardson Extrapolation method of
Oates et al. 2025
in which
f(\mathbf{x})
was modelled as a Gaussian process.
Roughly speaking, the mean of
f(\mathbf{x})
was taken to be a constant, assigned an improper flat prior and integrated out, while the covariance between
f(\mathbf{x})
and
f(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})
was taken to be of the form
\epsilon(\mathbf{x})\epsilon(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})
where
\epsilon(\mathbf{x})
was either obtained from numerical analysis, assuming access to a convergence rate
f(\mathbf{x})-f(\mathbf{0})=O(\epsilon(\mathbf{x}))
, or was learned, and
k
was a symmetric positive definite kernel.
This choice ensures that samples
g
from the Gaussian process also satisfy
g(\mathbf{x})-g(\mathbf{0})=O(\epsilon(\mathbf{x}))
, leading the authors to call this model a
numerical analysis-informed
Gaussian process
(
Tuo et al. 2014
;
Teymur et al. 2021
;
Bect et al. 2020
, such covariance functions appear also in)
.
Based on (
1
), a choice for
\epsilon(\mathbf{x})
that encodes the correct convergence order could be

\displaystyle\epsilon(\mathbf{x})\asymp f(\mathbf{x})-f(\mathbf{0})=\sum_{\bm{\alpha}\in A\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}}\beta_{\bm{\alpha}}\mathbf{x}^{\bm{\alpha}}\asymp\sum_{\bm{\alpha}\in\mathrm{Lead}(A)}\mathbf{x}^{\bm{\alpha}},

(4)

where in the final expression unit coefficients are used, since the ideal coefficients
\beta_{\bm{\alpha}}
are unknown in general.
Under this set-up, the authors were able to establish convergence acceleration by assuming that the normalised error
e(\mathbf{x}):=(f(\mathbf{x})-f(\mathbf{0}))/\epsilon(\mathbf{x})
is regular enough to belong to the reproducing kernel Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{k}(\mathcal{X})
of real-valued functions on
\mathcal{X}
for which
k
is the reproducing kernel
(see
Berlinet and Thomas-Agnan 2011
, for background)
.

To state one such result, define the
box fill distance

\rho_{X_{n},\mathcal{X}}
as the supremum value of
\nu
such that there is a box of the form
[\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}+\nu\mathbf{1}]
contained in
\mathcal{X}
for which
X_{n}\cap[\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}+\nu\mathbf{1}]=\emptyset
, and define the constants
\gamma_{d}
using the induction
\gamma_{d}:=2d(1+\gamma_{d-1})
with base case
\gamma_{1}\>=2
.
The following result can be found as Theorem 2 of
Oates et al. 2025

Theorem 2

(Convergence acceleration for GRE)
.

Let
\mathcal{X}=[\mathbf{0},\bm{1}]\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
X_{n}=\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\subset\mathcal{X}
.
Let
\mathcal{X}_{h}=[\mathbf{0},h\mathbf{1}]
and
X_{n}^{h}=\{h\mathbf{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
for
h\in(0,1]
.
Assume that
\epsilon
is a polynomial of total degree
r

, and

.
Then there exists an explicit

such that

\displaystyle\underbrace{|f(\mathbf{0})-\mathbb{E}[f(\mathbf{0})|\{f(\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{x}\in X_{n}^{h}\}]|}_{\text{extrapolation error for posterior mean of GP}}\;\leq\;\underbrace{C_{r,s}\;\rho_{X_{n},\mathcal{X}}^{s}\;\|e\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}(\mathcal{X})}}_{\text{constant in $h$}}\;\underbrace{\phantom{|_{|^{|}}}h^{s}}_{\text{acceleration}}\;\;\underbrace{\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}_{h}}|\epsilon(\mathbf{x})|}_{\text{error of original method }}

whenever the box fill distance satisfies
\rho_{X_{n},\mathcal{X}}\leq 1/(\gamma_{d}(r+2s))
.

Compared to multivariate Richardson extrapolation, the number
n
of data does not need to be equal to
\mathrm{dim}(A)
.
However, even in the most favourable setting where
r=1
and
s=0
, the minimum number of data needed to satisfy the box fill distance requirement is

, which is super-exponential.
This is undesirable for complex simulators with multiple sources of discretisation, since running a large number of simulations can be impractical.
Though it is possible that the data requirement in
Theorem

2
could be reduced using a sharper argument, at least an exponential dimension dependence appears fundamental to Gauss–Richardson Extrapolation, since a kernel method is being used to approximate the normalised error
e(\mathbf{x})
, an unstructured
d
-dimensional functional
(
Wendland 2004
, such problems require space-filling designs, which manifests as a fill distance requirement; see)
.
On the other hand, Gauss–Richardson Extrapolation unlocks statistical functionalities not available to multivariate Richardson extrapolation, and empirical results show Gauss–Richardson Extrapolation can be effective in dimensions
d\in\{1,2\}

(
Oates et al. 2025
)
.
The present paper seeks to replicate these functionalities while addressing the super-exponential data requirement of Gauss–Richardson Extrapolation, as explained next.

Methods

This section contains our novel methodological development, with Sparse Probabilistic Richardson Extrapolation introduced in

Remarkably, it is shown that not only does Sparse Probabilistic Richardson Extrapolation match the convergence acceleration of multivariate Richardson extrapolation for the same data requirement (which in turn depends only on the extrapolation dimension), but Sparse Probabilistic Richardson Extrapolation can unlock
additional
convergence acceleration if appropriate regularity conditions are satisfied.

Section

3.3
concerns implementational detail, such as the data-driven identification of the index set
A
and experimental design to select the simulator configurations
\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
which comprise the dataset.

3.1
Sparse Probabilistic Richardson Extrapolation

A major strength of our Sparse Probabilistic Richardson Extrapolation method is that it is straight-forward to state and to implement.
The idea is to model
f
as a Gaussian process, whose mean function

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[f(\mathbf{x})|\{\beta_{\bm{\alpha}}:\bm{\alpha}\in A\}]=\sum_{\bm{\alpha}\in A}\beta_{\bm{\alpha}}\mathbf{x}^{\bm{\alpha}}

depends on unknown coefficients
\beta_{\bm{\alpha}}
, which are in turn assigned an improper flat prior and integrated out, while the covariance function
\mathbb{C}[f(\mathbf{x}),f(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})]=k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})
is specified in terms of a symmetric and positive definite kernel
k
to be specified.
Following standard calculations
4
4

4

See for instance Section 2.7 of
Williams and Rasmussen 2006
.
the posterior mean and variance can be explicitly computed.
Indeed, letting
\mathbf{V}_{A}:=\mathbf{V}_{A}(X_{n})
,
\mathbf{K}:=[k(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{j})]_{i,j=1}^{n}
,
\mathbf{f}:=[f(\mathbf{x}_{i})]_{i=1}^{n}
,
\mathbf{k}(\mathbf{x}):=[k(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x})]_{i=1}^{n}
, and
\mathbf{v}_{A}(\mathbf{x}):=[\mathbf{x}^{\bm{\alpha}_{i}}]_{i=1}^{m}
, we have that

\displaystyle\hskip-10.0pt\mathbb{E}[f(\mathbf{x}^{*})|\{f(\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{x}\in X_{n}\}]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{k}(\mathbf{x}^{*})^{\top}\mathbf{K}^{-1}\mathbf{f}+\mathbf{r}_{A}(\mathbf{x}^{*})^{\top}\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{A}

(5)

\displaystyle\hskip-10.0pt\mathbb{V}[f(\mathbf{x}^{*})|\{f(\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{x}\in X_{n}\}]

\displaystyle=k(\mathbf{x}^{*},\mathbf{x}^{*})-\mathbf{k}(\mathbf{x}^{*})^{\top}\mathbf{K}^{-1}\mathbf{k}(\mathbf{x}^{*})+\mathbf{r}_{A}(\mathbf{x}^{*})^{\top}(\mathbf{V}_{A}^{\top}\mathbf{K}^{-1}\mathbf{V}_{A})^{-1}\mathbf{r}_{A}(\mathbf{x}^{*})

(6)

where
\mathbf{r}_{A}(\mathbf{x}):=\mathbf{v}_{A}(\mathbf{x})-\mathbf{V}_{A}^{\top}\mathbf{K}^{-1}\mathbf{k}(\mathbf{x})
and
\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{A}:=(\mathbf{V}_{A}^{\top}\mathbf{K}^{-1}\mathbf{V}_{A})^{-1}\mathbf{V}_{A}^{\top}\mathbf{K}^{-1}\mathbf{f}
.
In particular we are interested in prediction at
\mathbf{x}^{*}=\mathbf{0}
, where under our convention
\mathbf{v}_{A}(\mathbf{0})=\mathbf{e}_{1}
.
Note that
X_{n}
is required to be
\mathcal{P}_{A}
-unisolvent in order for
\mathbf{V}_{A}^{\top}\mathbf{K}^{-1}\mathbf{V}_{A}
to be inverted.

Remark 1

(Extrapolation sparsity and SPRE)
.

The minimum number of data required for Sparse Probabilistic Richardson Extrapolation is
d_{\mathrm{ext}}(f)
, identical to multivariate Richardson extrapolation.
Thus multivariate Richardson extrapolation and Sparse Probabilistic Richardson Extrapolation are able to exploit extrapolation sparsity while Gauss–Richardson Extrapolation is not.
An illustration of multivariate Richardson extrapolation, Gauss–Richardson Extrapolation and Sparse Probabilistic Richardson Extrapolation applied to the cubature setting of
Example

is presented in

, where the reduced data requirement for Sparse Probabilistic Richardson Extrapolation is striking compared to Gauss–Richardson Extrapolation.
Full details of this experiment are contained in
Section

.

Remark 2

(
\mathcal{P}_{A}
exactness of SPRE)
.

Suppose that
f\in\mathcal{P}_{A}
, so that
\mathbf{f}=\mathbf{V}_{A}\bm{\beta}_{A}
where, under the convention of
Section

2.1.1
, the first entry of
\bm{\beta}_{A}
corresponds to
f(\mathbf{0})
, the quantity of interest.
Assuming
X_{n}
is
\mathcal{P}_{A}
-unisolvent, we can substitute this expression into (
5
) to obtain
\mathbb{E}[f(\mathbf{0})|\{f(\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{x}\in X_{n}\}]=\mathbf{e}_{1}^{\top}\bm{\beta}_{A}=f(\mathbf{0})
, so that the posterior mean of the Gaussian process coincides with the true value of
f(\mathbf{0})
; we say that Sparse Probabilistic Richardson Extrapolation is
\mathcal{P}_{A}

exact
.

Remark 3

(multivariate Richardson extrapolation as a special case of Sparse Probabilistic Richardson Extrapolation)
.

Suppose that
n=\mathrm{dim}(A)
and that the set
X_{n}
is
\mathcal{P}_{A}
-unisolvent, so that there is a unique element
p\in\mathcal{P}_{A}
that coincides with
f
on
X_{n}
.
From
Remark

2
, the posterior mean of Sparse Probabilistic Richardson Extrapolation coincides with
p(\mathbf{0})
, which is also the output of multivariate Richardson extrapolation.
In this edge case, a convergence acceleration result for Sparse Probabilistic Richardson Extrapolation can be deduced from
Theorem

1
.
However, unlike multivariate Richardson extrapolation, Sparse Probabilistic Richardson Extrapolation naturally extends to settings where
n>\mathrm{dim}(A)
, provides rigorous probabilistic uncertainty quantification for
f(\mathbf{0})
which can be integrated into downstream inference and decision support, and unlocks cost-constrained experimental design and data-driven model selection functionality (cf.
Section

3.3
).

Remark 4

(Comparing Gauss–Richardson Extrapolation and Sparse Probabilistic Richardson Extrapolation)
.

Compared to Gauss–Richardson Extrapolation, Sparse Probabilistic Richardson Extrapolation can be implemented with exponentially fewer data in multiple dimensions when extrapolation sparsity is present (cf.
Theorem

3
), ameliorates the need for careful choice of kernel by admitting a well-defined limiting case in which minimal smoothness is assumed (cf.
Proposition

1
), and naively supports discrete domains
\mathcal{X}
, which occur in practice for example when a time step size
x_{1}
is required to exactly divide a fixed length time interval
[0,T]
, so that
x_{1}\in\{\frac{T}{m}:m\in\mathbb{N}\}
.

The potential for convergence acceleration noted in
Remark

3
is certainly not the end of the story for Sparse Probabilistic Richardson Extrapolation.
Indeed, it would be desirable to make use of all available data even if the number of data
n
exceeds
d_{\mathrm{ext}}(f)
.
This rules out a trivial proof of convergence acceleration along the lines of
Remark

3
, and an alternative strategy is required.
In particular, any theoretical analysis will need to account for the effect of the choice of kernel.
Further, there are the important practical matters of how the index set
A
can be estimated from data, and how the evaluation locations
\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
should be designed to be maximally informative subject to a total computational budget.
These questions will all be addressed, beginning with a novel analysis of convergence acceleration next.

3.2
Convergence Acceleration

Before commencing our theoretical analysis, we recall that often
\mathcal{X}
is not a continuum but rather a discrete set.
For example, a simple quadrature on
[0,1]
with uniformly-spaced nodes is only possible when the distance between nodes is of the form
\frac{1}{N}
for some
N\in\mathbb{N}
.
To accommodate both continuous and discrete scenarios, we define
permissible scalings
to be values of
h
such that
h\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}
whenever
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}
.
As an example, if
\mathcal{X}=\{\frac{1}{N}:N\in\mathbb{N}\}
then we can take
h=\frac{1}{M}
for any
M\in\mathbb{N}
.
For our conclusions to be meaningful we assume that the set of permissible scalings has
0
as an accumulation point.
Now we are in a position to state
Theorem

3
, our main convergence result.
The proofs of the following result is contained in
Section

A.3
.

Theorem 3

(Convergence acceleration for SPRE)
.

Let
\mathbf{0}\in\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
and let
X_{n}=\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\subset\mathcal{X}
be
\mathcal{P}_{A}
-unisolvent.
Let
\mathcal{X}_{h}=\{h\mathbf{x}\}_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\subset\mathcal{X}
and
X_{n}^{h}=\{h\mathbf{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\subset\mathcal{X}
for permissible scalings
h\in(0,1]
.
Let
k:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a symmetric, positive definite and bounded kernel.
Let
f:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a simulator for which (
1
) holds with
R\in\mathcal{H}_{k}(\mathcal{X})
.
Then for each permissible scaling
h
there exists
C_{h,k}\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\displaystyle\underbrace{|f(\mathbf{0})-\mathbb{E}[f(\mathbf{0})|\{f(\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{x}\in X_{n}^{h}\}]|}_{\text{extrapolation error for the posterior mean of the GP }}\leq\underbrace{C_{h,k}}_{\text{bounded in $h$}}\quad\underbrace{\|R\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}(\mathcal{X}_{h})}}_{\text{acceleration}},

(7)

and moreover
\sup_{h\in(0,1]}C_{h,k}<\infty
.

Thus Sparse Probabilistic Richardson Extrapolation can accelerate convergence up to a rate determined by the smoothness of the residual
R
in (
1
) as measured using the norm associated with the kernel.
In
Proposition

1
below we show that, for a suitably rough kernel, the norm
\|R\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}(\mathcal{X}_{h})}
reduces to the magnitude
\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}_{h}}|R(\mathbf{x})|
of the residual, so that
Theorem

3
recovers the rate for multivariate Richardson extrapolation in
Theorem

1
, demonstrating that (
7
) is sharp in general.
Note that
Theorem

3
applies for
n=d_{\mathrm{ext}}(f)
(the minimum
n
for which
X_{n}
can be
\mathcal{P}_{A}
-unisolvent), so convergence acceleration is indeed achieved at this minimum size
n_{\min}
for the dataset.

Theorem 4

(Further convergence acceleration for Sparse Probabilistic Richardson Extrapolation)
.

In the setting of
Theorem

3
.
Let
s
be maximal such that
\{|\bm{\alpha}|<2s\}\subseteq A
.
Let
k:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a symmetric and positive definite kernel of the form
k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\phi(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})
where
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is
2s
times continuously differentiable at
\mathbf{0}
.
Then
C_{h,k}=O(h^{s})
as
h\rightarrow 0
.

Informally, the addition of the kernel, which is not part of multivariate Richardson extrapolation, enables any additional smoothness present in
R
to be exploited.
For balance,
if
one knew there was additional smoothness at the start, then one could leverage a higher-order expansion in (
1

Theorem

4
demonstrates that this knowledge is not needed upfront for Sparse Probabilistic Richardson Extrapolation.
In practice, kernel learning provides a principled statistical approach to Sparse Probabilistic Richardson Extrapolation that realises this potential; see
Section

3.3
.

Remark 5

(Beyond translation-invariant kernels)
.

For presentational purposes

assumes a translation-invariant kernel, but our argument applies to any kernel
k

\mathcal{P}_{A}
.
In this case, one can relate
C_{h,k}
to the square root of the remainder term in the Taylor expansion.

A conservative choice of kernel could be the

\sigma^{2}>0
, for which minimal regularity of

is required.
Remarkably, even for this kernel Sparse Probabilistic Richardson Extrapolation performs at least as well as multivariate Richardson extrapolation.
The proof of the following result is contained in
Section

A.5
:

Proposition 1

(
Theorem

3
is sharp)
.

Let
k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})=\sigma^{2}\delta_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}}
.
Consider a discrete state space
\mathcal{X}=\{\mathbf{0}\}\cup\{\frac{1}{2^{m}}\frac{1}{2^{\bm{\alpha}}}:m\in\mathbb{N}_{0},\;0\leq|\bm{\alpha}|\leq s\}
together with designs of the form
X_{n}^{h}=\{h\cdot\frac{1}{2^{\bm{\alpha}}}:0\leq|\bm{\alpha}|\leq s\}
for the permissible scalings

,
M\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.
Suppose that
|R(\mathbf{x})|\asymp\|\mathbf{x}\|^{a}
for some
a>0
in the

Then
\|R\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}(\mathcal{X}_{h})}=O\left(\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}_{h}}|R(\mathbf{x})|\right)
in the

limit.

That is, the acceleration term appearing in the bound of
Theorem

3
decays at least as rapidly as the acceleration term appearing in
Theorem

1
.
Since
Theorem

1

Theorem

is sharp as well.

3.3
Implementational Detail

This section discusses data-driven selection of the kernel
k
, the index set
A
and the design
\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}\subset\mathcal{X}
.
Careful selection of
k
enables maximal convergence acceleration (cf.
Theorem

4
), learning
A
circumvents the mathematical analysis that would be needed to derive an expansion (
1
) for settings where the simulator
f
is complicated, and experimental design enables us to select configurations
\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}
that are maximally informative about
f(\mathbf{0})
subject to a constrained computational budget.
However, to simultaneously select all three is difficult; data are required to learn
k
and
A
, but it is difficult to design the set
\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}
of simulation configurations without knowledge of
k
and
A
.
This can be resolved either by fixing
\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}
(indeed, oftentimes the statistician does not have influence over the design set), or by using a sequential strategy that alternates between learning
k
and
A
and selecting which simulations to run next:

Learning
k
and
A

In this work we employed leave-one-out cross-validation.
Given a design
X_{n}=\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
, let
X_{n}^{(i)}:=X_{n}\setminus\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}
.
Let
p(\cdot;\mu,\sigma^{2})
denote the density of
N(\mu,\sigma^{2})
.
Then the suitability of a kernel
k
and a set
A
can be quantified by the leave-one-out predictive log-density

\displaystyle L(k,A)=-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log p(f_{i};\mu_{i},\sigma_{i}^{2}),\qquad\begin{array}[]{ll}\mu_{i}&:=\mathbb{E}_{k,A}[f(\mathbf{x}_{i})|\{f(\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{x}\in X_{n}^{(i)}\}]\\
\sigma^{2}_{i}&:=\mathbb{V}_{k,A}[f(\mathbf{x}_{i})|\{f(\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{x}\in X_{n}^{(i)}\}]\end{array}

which we seek to minimise through our choice of
k
and
A
.
Expressions for the predictive mean and variance can be read off from (
5
) and (
6
), and we have indicated their
k
and
A
dependence in the subscript.
For the kernel, we fix a parametric family
k_{\theta}
and use automatic differentiation to perform gradient-based optimisation on
\theta\mapsto L(k_{\theta},A)
using an adaptive trust region method.
The specific kernels that we used are detailed in
Section

B.1
.
The minimal value of the leave-one-out cross-validation error obtained in this manner is denoted

\displaystyle L(A)=\inf_{\theta}\;L(k_{\theta},A).

For the set
A
, we perform a stepwise method that is aligned with the notion of extrapolation sparsity, prioritising the search for ‘small’ sets
A
.
Starting with
A_{0}=\{\mathbf{0}\}
, at iteration
i
we consider adding each monomial of order
i
(i.e.
\mathbf{x}^{\bm{\alpha}}
with
|\bm{\alpha}|=i
) in turn, to see if the error criterion
L(A_{i-1}\cup\{\mathbf{x}^{\bm{\alpha}}\})
is reduced compared to
L(A_{i-1})
.
If there are no qualifying monomials we terminate and return
A_{i-1}
.
Else, we add
all
qualifying monomials of order
i
to obtain an enlarged model
A_{i}
.
If the error
L(A_{i})
of this enlarged model is reduced relative to
L(A_{i-1})
then we increment
i
and proceed, else we terminate
5
5

5

The algorithm must terminate because the data
X_{n}
will no longer be
\mathcal{P}_{A_{i}}
-unisolvent; for example, this is guaranteed when
\mathrm{dim}(A_{i})
exceeds
n
.
and return
A_{i-1}
.
The multiple rounds of optimisation entail a computational overhead, but this will typically be negligible with respect to the computational cost of running the simulations
f(\mathbf{x}_{i})
that comprise the dataset.

Experimental design

Given an existing design
X_{n}
, we seek to augment this with
m
new configurations
X_{m}^{*}
such that uncertainty regarding
f(\mathbf{0})
is minimised.
Let
k
and
A
be the kernel and index set learned based on

in the manner just described.
Recall that

denotes the cost associated with obtaining the simulation output

Thus, for a total computational budget

\displaystyle\argmin_{X_{m}^{*}\subset\mathcal{X}}\mathbb{V}_{k,A}[f(\mathbf{0})|\{f(\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{x}\in X_{n}\cup X_{m}^{*}\}]\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\sum_{i=1}^{m}c(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{*})\leq C.

(12)

The variance objective can be explicitly computed due to (
6
), which depends on
X_{m}^{*}
but does not depend on the actual values
\{f(\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{x}\in X_{m}^{*}\}
.
Solving for

requires numerical methods for constrained (and possibly discrete) optimisation to be used.

5
Discussion

Accelerating complex simulation is a fundamental computational task, and while statistical methodologies for surrogate modelling and multi-fidelity modelling are well-developed, the potential for convergence acceleration via statistical approaches to extrapolation has been under-explored.
A first step was recently taken in
Oates et al. 2025
, but the Gauss–Richardson Extrapolation method proposed in that work suffered from a prohibitive data requirement.
To move forward, we introduced the notion of extrapolation sparsity, and empirical evidence was presented that suggests extrapolation sparsity is typical for complex simulators, representing an exciting opportunity for methodological development.
To exploit this opportunity we developed Sparse Probabilistic Richardson Extrapolation, which harnesses extrapolation sparsity to accelerate convergence of complex simulators within a principled statistical framework, and with an exponentially reduced data requirement.

Related Work

Classical extrapolation methods can be classified according to the basis functions used to construct an interpolant; the original polynomial basis functions of
Richardson 1911
;
Richardson and Gaunt 1927
can be contrasted with the stability-promoting rational functions of
(
Thiele 1909
;
Bulirsch and Stoer 1964
;
Larkin 1967
)
, as well as a range of other choices documented in
Brezinski and Zaglia 2013
.
However, guidance on selecting a basis is limited, due to the absence of formal approaches to model selection that would be available within a statistical framework.
Despite this limitation, classical extrapolation methods continue to find new applications, including in optimal transport
(
Chizat et al. 2020
)
, regularisation and training of machine learning models
(
Bach 2021
)
, and sampling with Markov chain Monte Carlo
(
Durmus et al. 2016
)
.

Surrogate modelling
(
Sacks et al. 1989
)
and related data-driven techniques such as reduced order modelling
(
Lucia et al. 2004
)
are popular in settings where the simulator involves additional scientific parameters
\bm{\theta}
, and the parameter dependence of the simulator output is of interest.
As such, these approaches treat the discretised model
f_{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{x})
as the target, whereas in reality the continuum limit
f_{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{0})
is of principal interest.
This issue is addressed in a data-driven manner in multi-fidelity model, in which one constructs a hierarchy of models
\bm{\theta}\mapsto f_{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{i})
of different fidelities
\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}
and attempts to leverage simulations from lower-fidelity models to make inferences and predictions regarding higher-fidelity models
(
Peherstorfer et al. 2018
)
.
Gaussian processes are often used to facilitate multi-fidelity model, and in specific applications extrapolation of the Gaussian process model to a continuum quantity of interest
f_{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{0})
has been considered, for example in
Thodoroff et al. 2025
to simulate ice sheets in Antarctic, and in
Ji et al. 2024
to simulate the evolution of the quark-gluon plasma following the Big Bang.
There is also active interest in cost-constrained experimental design in multi-fidelity model
(
Dixon et al. 2025
, e.g.)
.
However, to the best of our knowledge, convergence
acceleration
via extrapolation has not been considered in this context.

Future Research Directions

Our contribution can be cast as a
black box probabilistic numerical method
in the sense of
Teymur et al. 2021
, with Bayesian principles used to quantify uncertainty regarding the continuum model of interest
(
Cockayne et al. 2019
)
.
As such, it would be interesting to compare Sparse Probabilistic Richardson Extrapolation to other probabilistic numerical methods across a range of foundational numerical tasks.
Probabilistic numerical methods are typically designed from the ground-up using probabilistic primitives
(
Hennig et al. 2015
)
, so it would be interesting to understand if such effort can be circumvented by instead extrapolating output from established numerical methods in the Sparse Probabilistic Richardson Extrapolation framework.

For our purposes the simulator
f
was assumed to be deterministic, but stochastic simulators are also widely used.
An important numerical task is then to approximate moments of the simulator output, in particular the expected value
\mathbb{E}[f(\mathbf{0})]
.
Unbiased estimation of
\mathbb{E}[f(\mathbf{0})]
can be possible using the methodology of
Rhee and Glynn 2015
applied to a sequence
\mathbb{E}[f(\mathbf{x}_{i})]
with
\mathbf{x}_{i}\rightarrow\mathbf{0}
, while multilevel methods have been combined with Richardson extrapolation in
Lemaire and Pagès 2017
;
Beschle and Barth 2022
.
As it stands, the techniques used to accelerate convergence of deterministic and stochastic simulators are different; developing a unified framework for convergence acceleration would be an exciting direction for further work.

Although we set out a framework for experimental design with Sparse Probabilistic Richardson Extrapolation, its theoretical analysis appears to be difficult.

Adcock 2024
studied polynomial extrapolation with noisy data, where the
\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}
were sampled independently from a measure
\mu
, asking how
\mu
ought to be chosen to promote extrapolation accuracy subject to a constraint of the form
\mathbb{E}[c(\mathbf{x_{i}})]\leq C
.
Extending this analysis to the case of noise-free data and Gaussian process regression would be an interesting avenue for future work.

A.1.1

The aim of this appendix is to clarify how well one can approximate a continuous function with an element of

Recall the definition of the Vandermonde matrix

Then, the

\mathrm{VDM}_{A}(X_{n})=\mathrm{det}(\mathbf{V}_{A}(X_{n}))

The (multivariate)

\ell_{i}^{A}(\cdot;X_{n})
are defined as the elements of
\mathcal{P}_{A}

\ell_{i}^{A}(\mathbf{x}_{j};X_{n})=\delta_{i,j}

It is straightforward to check that

\displaystyle\ell_{i}^{A}(\mathbf{x};X_{n})=\frac{\mathrm{VDM}_{A}(\{\mathbf{x}\}\cup(X_{n}\setminus\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}))}{\mathrm{VDM}_{A}(X_{n})}.

The (multivariate)

measures the sum of absolute values of the Lagrange polynomials defined by

\displaystyle\lambda_{A}(\mathbf{x};X_{n})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}|\ell_{i}^{A}(\mathbf{x};X_{n})|

(13)

for each

For compact
\mathcal{X}

\displaystyle\Pi_{A,\mathcal{X}}:(C(\mathcal{X}),\|\cdot\|_{\infty,\mathcal{X}})

\displaystyle\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle f

\displaystyle\mapsto f_{n}(\mathbf{0})

denotes the interpolating polynomial (in
\mathcal{P}_{A}
) of
f
on
X_{n}
.
Note that for each

\displaystyle|\Pi_{A,\mathcal{X}}(f)|

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell_{i}^{A}(\cdot;X_{n})f(\mathbf{x}_{i})\right|\leq\lambda_{A}(\mathbf{0};X_{n})\|f\|_{\infty,\mathcal{X}}

(14)

Then we have the following technical result, which will be used in the proof of

:

Lemma 1

(Sparse polynomial approximation)
.

Let
X_{n}
be
\mathcal{P}_{A}
-unisolvent with

.
Let
f\in C(\mathcal{X})
where
\mathbf{0}\in\mathcal{X}\subset[0,\infty)^{d}
is compact, and let
f_{n}
denote the interpolating polynomial (in
\mathcal{P}_{A}
) of
f
on
X_{n}
.
Then

\displaystyle|f(\mathbf{0})-f_{n}(\mathbf{0})|\leq(1+\lambda_{A}(\mathbf{0};X_{n}))\inf_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{A}}\|f-p\|_{\infty,\mathcal{X}}.

(15)

Proof.

Let
p_{\star}
attain the infimum on the right hand side of (

), which exists and is unique since

is
\mathcal{P}_{A}
-unisolvent with
n=|A|
.
Then by the triangle inequality, and the fact that
\Pi_{A,\mathcal{X}}
is a projection with operator norm at most
\lambda_{A}(\mathbf{0};X_{n})
from (

\displaystyle|f(\mathbf{0})-f_{n}(\mathbf{0})|

\displaystyle\leq|f(\mathbf{0})-p_{\star}(\mathbf{0})|+|p_{\star}(\mathbf{0})-f_{n}(\mathbf{0})|

\displaystyle=|f(\mathbf{0})-p_{\star}(\mathbf{0})|+|\Pi_{A,\mathcal{X}}(p_{\star})-\Pi_{A,\mathcal{X}}(f)|

\displaystyle=|f(\mathbf{0})-p_{\star}(\mathbf{0})|+|\Pi_{A,\mathcal{X}}(p_{\star}-f)|

\displaystyle\leq\|p_{\star}-f\|_{\infty,\mathcal{X}}+\lambda_{A}(\mathbf{0};X_{n})\|p_{\star}-f\|_{\infty,\mathcal{X}}

as required.

A.1.2
Sparse Polynomial Reproduction

The aim of this appendix is to generalise results on polynomial reproduction from
Wendland 2004
; these will be required to prove
Theorem

3
.
The need for generalisation comes from two directions; we use a sparse polynomial basis whereas a dense polynomial basis is considered in
Wendland 2004
, and we remove the requirement in
Wendland 2004
for
\mathcal{X}
to be an open set (this was used by Wendland to ensure derivatives are well-defined, but we do not have that requirement).

Lemma 2

(Polynomial reproduction at
\mathbf{0}
)
.

Suppose that
X_{n}=\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\subset\mathcal{X}
is
\mathcal{P}_{A}
-unisolvent where
\mathbf{0}\in\mathcal{X}\subset[0,\infty)^{d}
is compact.
Then for all
h\in(0,1]
there exists a vector of coefficients
\tilde{\mathbf{u}}^{(h)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and a
h
-independent constant
C_{A,X_{n}}>0
such that

1.

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tilde{u}_{i}^{(h)}p(h\mathbf{x}_{i})=p(\mathbf{0})
for all
p\in\mathcal{P}_{A}
(sparse polynomial reproduction)

2.

\sum_{i=1}^{n}|\tilde{u}_{i}^{(h)}|\leq C_{A,X_{n}}
.

Proof.

To begin with we fix
h\in(0,1]
.
Consider the
sampling operator

\displaystyle T:(C(\mathcal{X}),\|\cdot\|_{\infty,\mathcal{X}})

\displaystyle\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}

\displaystyle f

\displaystyle\mapsto(f(h\mathbf{x}_{1}),\dots,f(h\mathbf{x}_{n})),

which is a bounded linear functional, and its restriction
T|_{\mathcal{P}_{A}}:\mathcal{P}_{A}\rightarrow T(\mathcal{P}_{A})
is invertible since
X_{n}^{h}
is
\mathcal{P}_{A}
-unisolvent.
In particular
T|_{\mathcal{P}_{A}}^{-1}
is also a bounded linear functional.
It follows that
L_{X_{n}^{h}}:T(\mathcal{P}_{A})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

L_{X_{n}^{h}}(\mathbf{y})=(T|_{\mathcal{P}_{A}}^{-1}(\mathbf{y}))(\mathbf{0})
, is a bounded linear functional on
T(\mathcal{P}_{A})
.
This functional
L_{X_{n}^{h}}
therefore has a well-defined operator norm

\displaystyle\|L_{X_{n}^{h}}\|_{1}=\sup_{\mathbf{0}\neq\mathbf{y}\in T(\mathcal{P}_{A})}\frac{|L_{X_{n}^{h}}(\mathbf{y})|}{\|\mathbf{y}\|_{\infty}}=\sup_{0\neq p\in\mathcal{P}_{A}}\frac{|p(\mathbf{0})|}{\|(p(h\mathbf{x}_{1}),\dots,p(h\mathbf{x}_{n}))\|_{\infty}}.

On the other hand, since
L_{X_{n}^{h}}
is a bounded linear functional on
T(\mathcal{P}_{A})\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, from the Hahn–Banach theorem there exists a norm-preserving extension
L_{X_{n}^{h}}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
of the form
L_{X_{n}^{h}}(y)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tilde{u}_{i}^{(h)}y_{i}
for some coefficient vector
\tilde{\mathbf{u}}^{(h)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
The operator norm of this function is
\|L_{X_{n}^{h}}\|_{1}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}|\tilde{u}_{i}^{(h)}|
, and we call this constant
C_{A,X_{n}^{h}}
.

To conclude the argument we will show that
C_{A,X_{n}^{h}}
is in fact
h
-independent.
To see this, let
p(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{\bm{\alpha}\in A}\beta_{\bm{\alpha}}\mathbf{x}^{\bm{\alpha}}
parametrise a generic element of
\mathcal{P}_{A}
.
Then the operator norm can be expressed as

\displaystyle\|L_{X_{n}^{h}}\|_{1}=\sup_{\bm{0}\neq\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}\frac{|\beta_{\mathbf{0}}|}{\max\{|\beta_{\mathbf{0}}+\sum_{\bm{\alpha}\in A\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}}\beta_{\bm{\alpha}}(h\mathbf{x}_{i})^{\bm{\alpha}}|\,:\,i=1,\dots,n\}}

which is indeed
h
-independent (as can be seen from the change of variables
\tilde{\beta}_{\bm{\alpha}}=h^{|\bm{\alpha}|}\beta_{\bm{\alpha}}
).
∎

The content of
Lemma

2
is closely related to the topic of
norming sets
, where one seeks
X_{n}
such that
\|p\|_{\infty,\mathcal{X}}
is controlled by
\|p\|_{\infty,X_{n}}
; see
Kroó 2011
.
For our purposes, we need only control
|p(\mathbf{0})|
by
\|p\|_{\infty,X_{n}}
.

A.1.3
Native Spaces

This appendix introduces the concept of a
native space
, a space of functions that are formed as sums of elements from
\mathcal{P}_{A}
and
\mathcal{H}_{k}(\mathcal{X})
.
(Recall that
\mathcal{H}_{k}(\mathcal{X})
denotes the reproducing kernel Hilbert space of real-valued functions on
\mathcal{X}
for which
k
is the reproducing ;
see
Berlinet and Thomas-Agnan 2011
, for background
.)
The standard construction, for example as described in Chapter 10 of
Wendland 2004
, is unnecessarily technical for our purposes, and we therefore present a more explicit definition of the native space and derive the properties of the native space that will be required in our work.

Let
\mathcal{N}_{k}(\mathcal{X})
denote the vector space of functions
f:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
of the form
f=p+h
with
p\in\mathcal{P}_{A}
and
h\in\mathcal{H}_{k}(\mathcal{X})
.
This space can be equipped with a semi-norm

\displaystyle|f|_{\mathcal{N}_{k}(\mathcal{X})}:=\inf\{\|h\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}(\mathcal{X})}:f=p+h,p\in\mathcal{P}_{A},h\in\mathcal{H}_{k}(\mathcal{X})\}

and we can define the projection
\Pi_{\mathcal{P}_{A}}:\mathcal{N}_{k}(\mathcal{X})\rightarrow\mathcal{P}_{A}
onto
\mathcal{P}_{A}
such that
|\Pi_{\mathcal{P}_{A}}(f)|_{\mathcal{N}_{k}(\mathcal{X})}=0
and
|(I-\Pi_{\mathcal{P}_{A}})(f)|_{\mathcal{N}_{k}(\mathcal{X})}=|f|_{\mathcal{N}_{k}(\mathcal{X})}
for all
f\in\mathcal{N}_{k}(\mathcal{X})
.
Thus we have a direct sum
\mathcal{N}_{k}(\mathcal{X})=\mathcal{P}_{A}\oplus(I-\Pi_{\mathcal{P}_{A}})\mathcal{H}_{k}(\mathcal{X})
.

For
f\in\mathcal{N}_{k}(\mathcal{X})
, let
h_{f}
denote the element of
h\in\mathcal{H}_{k}(\mathcal{X})
which minimises
\|h\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}(\mathcal{X})}
subject to
f=p+h
for some
p\in\mathcal{P}_{A}
.
Then in particular
(I-\Pi_{\mathcal{P}_{A}})f=(I-\Pi_{\mathcal{P}_{A}})h_{f}
and
|f|_{\mathcal{N}_{k}(\mathcal{X})}=\|h_{f}\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}(\mathcal{X})}
.
Using the direct sum representation of
\mathcal{N}_{k}(\mathcal{X})
, and then using the reproducing property of
\mathcal{H}_{k}(\mathcal{X})
, we can write

\displaystyle f(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=(\Pi_{\mathcal{P}_{A}}f)(\mathbf{x})+((I-\Pi_{\mathcal{P}_{A}})(f))(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=(\Pi_{\mathcal{P}_{A}}f)(\mathbf{x})+((I-\Pi_{\mathcal{P}_{A}})(h_{f}))(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=(\Pi_{\mathcal{P}_{A}}f)(\mathbf{x})+(I-\Pi_{\mathcal{P}_{A}})_{\mathbf{x}}\langle h_{f},k(\cdot,\mathbf{x})\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{k}(\mathcal{X})}

\displaystyle=(\Pi_{\mathcal{P}_{A}}f)(\mathbf{x})+\langle h_{f},(I-\Pi_{\mathcal{P}_{A}})_{\mathbf{x}}k(\cdot,\mathbf{x})\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{k}(\mathcal{X})}

(16)

where
(I-\Pi_{\mathcal{P}_{A}})_{\mathbf{x}}k(\cdot,\mathbf{x})
denotes the action of
I-\Pi_{\mathcal{P}_{A}}
on the
\mathbf{x}
-argument.
Equation (
16
) can be viewed as a generalisation of the usual reproducing property for functions
f
in the native space
\mathcal{N}_{k}(\mathcal{X})
, which accounts for the presence of a polynomial basis
\mathcal{P}_{A}
in addition to the usual reproducing kernel Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{k}(\mathcal{X})
.
These mathematical properties will be used in the proof of
Theorem

3
.

A.2
Proof of
Theorem

1

The following result establishes convergence acceleration for multivariate Richardson extrapolation.

Proof of
Theorem

1
.

First note that
f_{n}^{h}
exists and is unique by the assumption that
X_{n}
is
\mathcal{P}_{A}
-unisolvent, since this in turn implies that
X_{n}^{h}
is
\mathcal{P}_{A}
-unisolvent.
Then, from
Lemma

1
,

\displaystyle|f(\mathbf{0})-f_{n}^{h}(\mathbf{0})|\leq(1+\lambda_{A}(\mathbf{0};X_{n}^{h}))\inf_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{A}}\|f-p\|_{\infty,\mathcal{X}_{h}},

where
\lambda_{A}(\cdot;X_{n}^{h})
denotes the (multivariate) Lebesgue function defined in (
13
).
Since
\lambda_{A}(\mathbf{0};hX_{n})=\lambda_{A}(\mathbf{0};X_{n})
for all
h>0
, we have that

\displaystyle|f(\mathbf{0})-f_{n}^{h}(\mathbf{0})|\leq(1+\lambda_{A}(\mathbf{0};X_{n}))\inf_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{A}}\|f-p\|_{\infty,\mathcal{X}_{h}}.

The first term is constant in
h
.
For the second term, recalling the definition of the residual
R(\mathbf{x})
in (
1
),

\displaystyle\inf_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{A}}\|f-p\|_{\infty,\mathcal{X}_{h}}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}_{h}}\left|f(\mathbf{x})-\sum_{\bm{\alpha}\in A}\beta_{\bm{\alpha}}\mathbf{x}^{\bm{\alpha}}\right|=\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}_{h}}|R(\mathbf{x})|,

which completes the argument.
∎

A.3
Proof of
Theorem

3

Our approach to proving
Theorem

3
leverages the characterisation of the posterior mean in Sparse Probabilistic Richardson Extrapolation as a minimal semi-norm interpolant:

Lemma 3

(Characterisation as a minimal semi-norm interpolant)
.

Let
X_{n}\subset\mathcal{X}
be
\mathcal{P}_{A}
-unisolvent and let
k:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a symmetric and positive definite kernel.
Then for any simulator
f:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
there is a unique solution to the variational problem

\displaystyle\argmin\left\{|s|_{\mathcal{N}_{k}(\mathcal{X})}:s(\mathbf{x})=f(\mathbf{x})\;\forall\;\mathbf{x}\in X_{n},s\in\mathcal{N}_{k}(\mathcal{X})\right\}

(17)

which we denote
s_{X_{n}}[f]
.
Further,
s_{X_{n}}[f]
coincides with the posterior mean of Sparse Probabilistic Richardson Extrapolation.

Proof.

From the definition of the native space semi-norm, a solution
s\in\mathcal{N}_{k}(\mathcal{X})
to the variational problem has the form
s=p+g
where
(p,g)\in\mathcal{P}_{A}\times\mathcal{H}_{k}(\mathcal{X})
is a solution to

\displaystyle\argmin\{\|g\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}(\mathcal{X})}:g(\mathbf{x})=f(\mathbf{x})-p(\mathbf{x})\;\forall\;\mathbf{x}\in X_{n},p\in\mathcal{P}_{A},g\in\mathcal{H}_{k}(\mathcal{X})\}.

(18)

Let
p(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{m}b_{i}\mathbf{x}^{\bm{\alpha}_{i}}
for some coefficients
\mathbf{b}=(b_{1},\dots,b_{m})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
.
From the representer theorem, for fixed
p
, there exists a minimal
\|g\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}(\mathcal{X})}
norm interpolant of
f-p
on
X_{n}
that takes the form
g(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}_{i})
for some coefficients
\mathbf{c}=(c_{1},\dots,c_{n})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and we have
\|g\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}(\mathcal{X})}^{2}=\mathbf{c}^{\top}\mathbf{K}\mathbf{c}
.
Thus (
18
) becomes

\displaystyle\argmin\{\mathbf{c}^{\top}\mathbf{K}\mathbf{c}:\mathbf{K}\mathbf{c}=\mathbf{f}-\mathbf{V}_{A}\mathbf{b},\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{m},\mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\},

whose solutions can be deduced (by the method of Lagrange multipliers) to be solutions of the linear system

\displaystyle\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\mathbf{K}&\mathbf{V}_{A}\\
\mathbf{V}_{A}^{\top}&\mathbf{0}\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{c}\\
\mathbf{b}\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{f}\\
\mathbf{0}\end{array}\right].

This block matrix is invertible since
X_{n}
is
\mathcal{P}_{A}
-unisolvent, so the solution pair
(\mathbf{b},\mathbf{c})
is unique in
\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
and we can uniquely identify the associated element
s
as the minimal semi-norm interpolant
s_{X_{n}}[f]
.
Calculating the inverse of this block matrix, we obtain

\displaystyle\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{c}\\
\mathbf{b}\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{K}^{-1}\mathbf{f}-\mathbf{k}(\mathbf{x})^{\top}\mathbf{K}^{-1}\mathbf{V}_{A}(\mathbf{V}_{A}^{\top}\mathbf{K}^{-1}\mathbf{V}_{A})^{-1}\mathbf{V}_{A}^{\top}\mathbf{K}^{-1}\mathbf{f}\\
(\mathbf{V}_{A}^{\top}\mathbf{K}^{-1}\mathbf{V}_{A})^{-1}\mathbf{V}_{A}^{\top}\mathbf{K}^{-1}\mathbf{f}\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{K}^{-1}\mathbf{f}-\mathbf{K}^{-1}\mathbf{V}_{A}\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{A}\\
\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{A}\end{array}\right]

so that

\displaystyle s_{X_{n}}[f](\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{v}_{A}(\mathbf{x})^{\top}\mathbf{b}+\mathbf{k}(\mathbf{x})^{\top}\mathbf{c}=\mathbf{k}(\mathbf{x})^{\top}\mathbf{K}^{-1}\mathbf{f}+\mathbf{r}_{A}(\mathbf{x})^{\top}\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{A}.

(19)

which is the posterior mean of Sparse Probabilistic Richardson Extrapolation as given in (
5
).
∎

Lemma

3
establishes the posterior mean of Sparse Probabilistic Richardson Extrapolation in (
5
) as a minimal semi-norm interpolant.
Unpacking the terms
\mathbf{r}_{A}(\mathbf{x})
and
\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{A}
in (
19
), we see that the solution to (
17
) has the form

\displaystyle s_{X_{n}}[f](\mathbf{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}^{*}(\mathbf{x})f(\mathbf{x}_{i})

(20)

with coefficient vector

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}^{*}(\mathbf{x})=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}u_{1}^{*}(\mathbf{x})\\
\vdots\\
u_{n}^{*}(\mathbf{x})\end{array}\right]=\mathbf{K}^{-1}\mathbf{k}(\mathbf{x})+\mathbf{K}^{-1}\mathbf{V}_{A}(\mathbf{V}_{A}^{\top}\mathbf{K}^{-1}\mathbf{V}_{A})^{-1}[\mathbf{v}_{A}(\mathbf{x})-\mathbf{V}_{A}^{\top}\mathbf{K}^{-1}\mathbf{k}(\mathbf{x})].

In particular
\mathbf{V}_{A}^{\top}\mathbf{u}^{*}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{v}_{A}(\mathbf{x})
, which is to say that
s_{X_{n}}[p](\mathbf{x})=p(\mathbf{x})
for each
p(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{x}^{\bm{\alpha}}
with
\bm{\alpha}\in A
.
Since
s_{X_{n}}
is linear it follows that
s_{X_{n}}[p]=p
for all
p\in\mathcal{P}_{A}
, which could be described as
sparse polynomial exactness
of the minimal semi-norm interpolant.
This exactness is a key ingredient in the following Lemma, which itself is a slight generalisation of
Wendland 2004
to remove the requirement in that work for
\mathcal{X}
to be an open set:

Lemma 4
.

Assume that
k
is positive definite and
X_{n}
is
\mathcal{P}_{A}
-unisolvent.
Let
\mathbf{u}^{*}(\mathbf{x})
be as defined in (
A.3
) and let
\tilde{k}(\cdot,\mathbf{x})=(I-\Pi_{\mathcal{P}_{A}})_{\mathbf{x}}k(\cdot,\mathbf{x})
.
Then

\displaystyle|f(\mathbf{x})-s_{X_{n}}[f](\mathbf{x})|\leq|f|_{\mathcal{N}_{k}(\mathcal{X})}\left\|\tilde{k}(\cdot,\mathbf{x})-\sum_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}^{*}(\mathbf{x})\tilde{k}(\cdot,\mathbf{x}_{i})\right\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}(\mathcal{X})}.

Proof.

From the representation of
s_{X_{n}}[f]
in (
20
) and the native space version of the reproducing property in (
16
), we have

\displaystyle f(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\Pi_{\mathcal{P}_{A}}[f](\mathbf{x})+\langle h_{f},\tilde{k}(\cdot,\mathbf{x})\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{k}(\mathcal{X})}

\displaystyle s_{X_{n}}[f](\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}^{*}(\mathbf{x})\{\Pi_{\mathcal{P}_{A}}[f](\mathbf{x}_{i})+\langle h_{f},\tilde{k}(\cdot,\mathbf{x}_{i})\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{k}(\mathcal{X})}\}.

From sparse polynomial exactness of the minimal semi-norm interpolant
s_{X_{n}}[f]
, we have that

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}^{*}(\mathbf{x})\Pi_{\mathcal{P}_{A}}[f](\mathbf{x})=\Pi_{\mathcal{P}_{A}}[f](\mathbf{x})

and thus

\displaystyle f(\mathbf{x})-s_{X_{n}}[f](\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\Pi_{\mathcal{P}_{A}}[f](\mathbf{x})-\Pi_{\mathcal{P}_{A}}[f](\mathbf{x})}_{=0}+\left\langle h_{f},\tilde{k}(\cdot,\mathbf{x})-\sum_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}^{*}(\mathbf{x})\tilde{k}(\cdot,\mathbf{x}_{i})\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{k}(\mathcal{X})}.

From Cauchy–Schwarz we thus obtain

\displaystyle|f(\mathbf{x})-s_{X_{n}}[f](\mathbf{x})|

\displaystyle\leq\|h_{f}\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}(\mathcal{X})}\left\|\tilde{k}(\cdot,\mathbf{x})-\sum_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}^{*}(\mathbf{x})\tilde{k}(\cdot,\mathbf{x}_{i})\right\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}(\mathcal{X})}.

Recognising
\|h_{f}\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}(\mathcal{X})}
as
|f|_{\mathcal{N}_{k}(\mathcal{X})}
completes the argument.
∎

From the last result, we see that it will be useful to more explicitly calculate the so-called
power function

\displaystyle P_{k,X_{n}}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle:=\left\|\tilde{k}(\cdot,\mathbf{x})-\sum_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}^{*}(\mathbf{x})\tilde{k}(\cdot,\mathbf{x}_{i})\right\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}(\mathcal{X})},

(24)

and we can facilitate this using the native space reproducing property in (
16
), as the following lemma explains:

Lemma 5
.

Again with
\tilde{k}(\cdot,\mathbf{x})=(I-\Pi_{\mathcal{P}_{A}})_{\mathbf{x}}k(\cdot,\mathbf{x})
, the power function in (
24
) satisfies

\displaystyle P_{k,X_{n}}(\mathbf{x})^{2}

\displaystyle=k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x})-2\sum_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}^{*}(\mathbf{x})k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}_{i})+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}u_{i}^{*}(\mathbf{x})u_{j}^{*}(\mathbf{x})k(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{j}).

Proof.

Let
\{\bm{\xi}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}\subset\mathcal{X}
be
\mathcal{P}_{A}
-unisolvent and let
\{p_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}\subset\mathcal{P}_{A}
be the corresponding Lagrange basis, so that
p_{i}(\bm{\xi}_{j})=\delta_{i,j}
.
Then we can write the projection
\Pi_{\mathcal{P}_{A}}:\mathcal{N}_{k}(\mathcal{X})\rightarrow\mathcal{P}_{A}
explicitly as

(\Pi_{\mathcal{P}_{A}}f)(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{m}f(\bm{\xi}_{i})p_{i}(\mathbf{x})

for all
f\in\mathcal{N}_{k}(\mathcal{X})
and all
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}
.
Let us now use the usual reproducing property of
\mathcal{H}_{k}(\mathcal{X})
to compute

\displaystyle\langle\tilde{k}(\cdot,\mathbf{x}),\tilde{k}(\cdot,\mathbf{y})\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{k}(\mathcal{X})}

\displaystyle=\left\langle k(\cdot,\mathbf{x})-\sum_{r=1}^{m}k(\cdot,\bm{\xi}_{r})p_{r}(\mathbf{x}),k(\cdot,\mathbf{y})-\sum_{s=1}^{m}k(\cdot,\bm{\xi}_{s})p_{s}(\mathbf{y})\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{k}(\mathcal{X})}

\displaystyle=k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})-\sum_{r=1}^{m}k(\mathbf{x},\bm{\xi}_{r})p_{r}(\mathbf{y})-\sum_{r=1}^{m}k(\mathbf{y},\bm{\xi}_{r})p_{r}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{r=1}^{m}\sum_{s=1}^{m}p_{r}(\mathbf{x})k(\bm{\xi}_{r},\bm{\xi}_{s})p_{s}(\mathbf{y}).

(25)

Now we use sparse polynomial exactness of the interpolant and the expression in (
25
) to establish that

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}^{*}(\mathbf{x})\langle\tilde{k}(\cdot,\mathbf{x}),\tilde{k}(\cdot,\mathbf{x}_{i})\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{k}(\mathcal{X})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}^{*}(\mathbf{x})\left\{k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}_{i})-\sum_{r=1}^{m}k(\mathbf{x},\bm{\xi}_{r})p_{r}(\mathbf{x}_{i})-\sum_{r=1}^{m}k(\mathbf{x}_{i},\bm{\xi}_{r})p_{r}(\mathbf{x})\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\left.+\sum_{r=1}^{m}\sum_{s=1}^{m}p_{r}(\mathbf{x})k(\bm{\xi}_{r},\bm{\xi}_{s})p_{s}(\mathbf{x}_{i})\right\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}^{*}(\mathbf{x})k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}_{i})-\sum_{r=1}^{m}k(\mathbf{x},\bm{\xi}_{r})p_{r}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle\;-\sum_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}^{*}(\mathbf{x})\sum_{r=1}^{m}k(\mathbf{x}_{i},\bm{\xi}_{r})p_{r}(\mathbf{x})+\sum_{r=1}^{m}\sum_{s=1}^{m}p_{r}(\mathbf{x})k(\bm{\xi}_{r},\bm{\xi}_{s})p_{s}(\mathbf{x})

and similarly

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}u_{i}^{*}(\mathbf{x})u_{j}^{*}(\mathbf{x})\langle\tilde{k}(\cdot,\mathbf{x}_{i}),\tilde{k}(\cdot,\mathbf{x}_{j})\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{k}(\mathcal{X})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}u_{i}^{*}(\mathbf{x})u_{j}^{*}(\mathbf{x})k(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{j})

\displaystyle\qquad-2\sum_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}^{*}(\mathbf{x})\sum_{r=1}^{m}k(\mathbf{x}_{i},\bm{\xi}_{r})p_{r}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{r=1}^{m}\sum_{s=1}^{m}p_{r}(\mathbf{x})k(\bm{\xi}_{r},\bm{\xi}_{s})p_{s}(\mathbf{x})

Squaring and expanding (
24
) we obtain

\displaystyle P_{k,X_{n}}(\mathbf{x})^{2}

\displaystyle=\langle\tilde{k}(\cdot,\mathbf{x}),\tilde{k}(\cdot,\mathbf{x})\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{k}(\mathcal{X})}-2\sum_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}^{*}(\mathbf{x})\langle\tilde{k}(\cdot,\mathbf{x}),\tilde{k}(\cdot,\mathbf{x}_{i})\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{k}(\mathcal{X})}

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}u_{i}^{*}(\mathbf{x})u_{j}^{*}(\mathbf{x})\langle\tilde{k}(\cdot,\mathbf{x}_{i}),\tilde{k}(\cdot,\mathbf{x}_{j})\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{k}(\mathcal{X})}

and upon substituting in the above expressions we obtain the stated result.
∎

The following Lemma is a slight generalisation of
Wendland 2004
which allows for
\mathcal{P}_{A}
to be a sparse polynomial basis:

Lemma 6
.

Suppose that
X_{n}=\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\subset\mathcal{X}
is
\mathcal{P}_{A}
-unisolvent.
Assume that
k:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a symmetric, positive definite and bounded kernel.
Given
p\in\mathcal{P}_{A}
, let
\bar{p}:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be defined as
\bar{p}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})=p(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime})
.
Then there exists a
h
-independent constant
\tilde{C}_{A,X_{n}}>0
such that

P_{k,X_{n}^{h}}(\mathbf{0})\leq\tilde{C}_{A,X_{n}}\|k-\bar{p}\|_{\infty,\mathcal{X}_{h}\times\mathcal{X}_{h}}^{1/2}

for all
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}
,
p\in\mathcal{P}_{A}
, and
h\in(0,1]
.

Proof.

To simplify the presentation we present the main argument for
h=1
, so that
\mathcal{X}_{h}=\mathcal{X}
.
Then we will retrospectively argue that the constants we introduced were
h
-independent.

First note that the vector
\mathbf{u}^{*}(\mathbf{x})
in (
A.3
) minimises the quadratic form

\displaystyle Q_{k,X_{n},\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{u}):=k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x})-2\sum_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}_{i})+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}u_{i}u_{j}k(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{j})

over
M_{\mathbf{x}}:=\{\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\sum_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}p(\mathbf{x}_{i})=p(\mathbf{x})\text{ for all }p\in\mathcal{P}_{A}\}
, and the minimum value is
P_{k,X_{n}}(\mathbf{x})^{2}
for the power function defined in (
24
).
Indeed, one can prove this by constructing the Lagrangian

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{u},\bm{\lambda})=\frac{1}{2}Q_{k,X_{n},\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{u})+\bm{\lambda}^{\top}\underbrace{(\mathbf{V}_{A}^{\top}\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v}_{A}(\mathbf{x}))}_{\mathbf{u}\in M}

where
\bm{\lambda}
is a vector of Lagrange multipliers, and then differentiate to obtain

\displaystyle\nabla_{\mathbf{u}}\mathcal{L}

\displaystyle=-\mathbf{k}(\mathbf{x})+\mathbf{K}\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{V}_{A}\bm{\lambda}

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{\lambda}}\mathcal{L}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{V}_{A}^{\top}\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v}_{A}(\mathbf{x}).

One uniquely obtains (
A.3
) by simultaneously solving
\nabla_{\mathbf{u}}\mathcal{L}=\mathbf{0}
and
\nabla_{\bm{\lambda}}\mathcal{L}=\mathbf{0}
.
Further, the Hessian is

\displaystyle\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\nabla_{\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u}}\mathcal{L}&\nabla_{\mathbf{u},\bm{\lambda}}\mathcal{L}\\
\nabla_{\bm{\lambda},\mathbf{u}}\mathcal{L}&\nabla_{\bm{\lambda},\bm{\lambda}}\mathcal{L}\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\mathbf{K}&\mathbf{V}_{A}\\
\mathbf{V}_{A}^{\top}&\mathbf{0}\end{array}\right]

which is a positive definite matrix, so that
\mathbf{u}^{*}
is indeed a minimum of
Q_{k,X_{n},\mathbf{x}}
on
M_{\mathbf{x}}
.

Now we take
\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}
.
To upper-bound
P_{k,X_{n}}(\mathbf{0})^{2}
it suffices to evaluate
Q_{k,X_{n},\mathbf{0}}(\mathbf{u})
on any element
\mathbf{u}\in M_{\mathbf{0}}
.
For this purpose we pick the element
\tilde{\mathbf{u}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
defined in
Lemma

2
, for which
Q_{\bar{p},X_{n},\mathbf{0}}(\tilde{\mathbf{u}})=0
by the polynomial reproduction property.
Then

\displaystyle Q_{k,X_{n},\mathbf{0}}(\tilde{\mathbf{u}})

\displaystyle=Q_{k,X_{n},\mathbf{0}}(\tilde{\mathbf{u}})-Q_{\bar{p},\mathbf{0}}(\tilde{\mathbf{u}})=Q_{k-\bar{p},X_{n},\mathbf{0}}(\tilde{\mathbf{u}})

Implementation of multivariate Richardson extrapolation

B.4.2
Agent-Based Flocking Model

Here we report implementational details pertaining to experiments involving the agent-based flocking model.

Physics Engine

The multi-agent flocking model is implemented using the
Mesa
Python library
[
ter Hoeven et al. 2025
]
.
Agents interact in a continuous two-dimensional domain
\Omega=[0,10]\times[0,10]
with periodic (toroidal) boundaries. Each agent repels others at very short distances to prevent overlap, using a repulsion radius to define a short-range exclusion zone. At intermediate distances, agents attract one another to maintain group cohesion up to the interaction radius, with interaction strength smoothly tapering off near the limit. This smooth tailing avoids abrupt force cutoffs, ensuring stable dynamics.

Flocking Model

The system contains 60 agents with random initial positions (generated using a fixed random seed, so the model is in fact deterministic).
Each agent
i
is characterised by its position
\mathbf{u}_{i}(t)\in\Omega
and velocity
\mathbf{v}_{i}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
at time
t
.

Numerical Methods

Numerical accuracy in
Mesa
is controlled by three discretisation parameters, a time step
x_{1}
, a repulsion softening parameter
x_{2}
, and a cutoff width
x_{3}
.
For two agents
i
and
j
, the displacement vector is
\mathbf{d}_{ij}=\mathbf{u}_{j}-\mathbf{u}_{i}
, with periodic corrections applied to each coordinate to respect toroidal boundaries.
The inter-agent distance is then
r_{ij}=\lVert\mathbf{d}_{ij}\rVert
, and the unit vector pointing from
i
to
j
is
\hat{\mathbf{d}}_{ij}=\mathbf{d}_{ij}/r_{ij}
.
The pairwise force from agent
j
on agent
i
has repulsive and attractive components:

F^{\mathrm{rep}}_{ij}=\max\left(0,-\frac{R_{r}-r_{ij}}{r_{ij}+x_{2}}\right),\qquad F^{\mathrm{att}}_{ij}=\max\left(0,r_{ij}-R_{r}\right),

where
R_{r}
is the
repulsion radius
(in our simulations,
R_{r}=0.5
) and
x_{2}
is the repulsion softening parameter. The total pairwise force vector is
\mathbf{f}_{ij}=\left(F^{\mathrm{rep}}_{ij}+F^{\mathrm{att}}_{ij}\right)\hat{\mathbf{d}}_{ij}
.
Interactions are modulated by a smooth cutoff function

w(r)=\begin{cases}1,&x_{3}=0\text{ and }r<R,\\
0,&x_{3}=0\text{ and }r\geq R,\\
\dfrac{1}{2}\left(1-\tanh\left(\dfrac{r-R}{x_{3}}\right)\right),&x_{3}>0,\end{cases}

where
R
is the
interaction radius
(in our simulations,
R=2
) and
x_{3}
is the cutoff width parameter. This ensures a continuous decay of interaction strength near the cutoff distance.
The net force on agent
i
is then

\mathbf{F}_{i}=\sum_{j\neq i}w(r_{ij})\,\mathbf{f}_{ij}.

Agents are updated at each time step by incrementing their velocities and positions according to the computed forces with time increment
x_{1}
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}_{i}(t+x_{1})

\displaystyle=\mathbf{v}_{i}(t)+x_{1}\,\mathbf{F}_{i}(t),

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}_{i}(t+x_{1})

\displaystyle=\mathbf{u}_{i}(t)+x_{1}\,\mathbf{v}_{i}(t+x_{1}),

with positions wrapped to remain within the toroidal domain
\Omega
.

Since the desired quantities of interest do not in general coincide exactly with a discrete time step, linear interpolation is performed to estimate these quantities at the precise time required.
