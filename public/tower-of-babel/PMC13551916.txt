The feasibility of [18F]PSMA-1007 PET/CT for response assessment in prostate cancer following neoadjuvant androgen deprivation therapy compared with mpMRI
Authors: Sangwoo Cho, Hyo Jung Seo, Hyunkyung Han, Sangwon Lee, Tae Joo Jeon, Young Taik Oh, Minsun Jung, Mijin Yun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWe investigated the utility of 18F-labeled prostate-specific membrane antigen positron emission tomography/computed tomography ([18F]PSMA-1007 PET/CT) for assessing residual tumor burden after neoadjuvant androgen deprivation therapy (nADT), compared with multiparametric magnetic resonance imaging (mpMRI).MethodsThis retrospective study included 20 patients with prostate cancer treated with nADT followed by radical prostatectomy. All patients underwent baseline mpMRI, post-nADT mpMRI, and [18F]PSMA-1007 PET/CT. Lesion-based diagnostic performance of [18F]PSMA-1007 PET/CT and mpMRI was compared using generalized estimating equations. Apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) and transfer constant (Ktrans) values were compared between baseline and post-nADT using Wilcoxon signed-rank tests and between tumor and benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) using Mann–Whitney U tests.ResultsBaseline mpMRI identified 23 biopsy-confirmed lesions, and post-nADT, 21 showed residual tumor on pathology. [18F]PSMA-1007 PET/CT demonstrated significantly higher sensitivity (90.5% vs. 23.8%, p < 0.001) and accuracy (87.0% vs. 26.1%, p < 0.001) than mpMRI. Median ADC increased significantly after nADT (0.849 to 1.174 × 10− 3 mm2/s, p = 0.005), while median Ktrans decreased (0.277 to 0.080 min− 1, p = 0.005). Baseline ADC and Ktrans differed significantly between tumor and BPH (p = 0.001 and p = 0.012, respectively), but no significant post-nADT differences were observed (p = 0.831 and p = 0.701, respectively). Post-nADT SUVmax also did not differ between tumor and BPH (p = 0.639).Conclusions[18F]PSMA-1007 PET showed higher sensitivity and accuracy than mpMRI in detecting residual tumor after short-term nADT and may be useful for further management planning. These preliminary findings support the feasibility of [18F]PSMA-1007 PET for post-treatment assessment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40644-026-01073-1.

Background

Prostate cancer (PCa) was the second most commonly diagnosed malignancy and the fifth leading cause of cancer-related death among men worldwide in 2022, and high-risk localized disease often requires a multimodal treatment approach [1]. Neoadjuvant androgen deprivation therapy (nADT), administered before radical prostatectomy, has been investigated as a strategy to downstage tumors, reduce positive surgical margins, and treat micrometastatic disease [2, 3]. However, current evidence has not demonstrated a clear survival benefit for nADT before radical prostatectomy in PCa [3, 4]. Nevertheless, the use of this approach has continued to increase, accompanied by sustained research interest in treatment duration, systemic regimens, and combination strategies [2, 5].

Multiparametric magnetic resonance imaging (mpMRI), the current standard imaging modality for prostate tumor localization and staging, has been investigated for monitoring response after nADT, given its ability to assess tumor volume, cellularity using diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI), and perfusion using dynamic contrast enhancement (DCE) [6, 7]. However, post-nADT interpretation of mpMRI can be challenging, as hormonal therapy induces glandular shrinkage, reduced vascularity, and a more homogeneous tissue appearance, thereby altering T2 signal characteristics and limiting its accuracy for evaluating treatment response after nADT [8, 9].

Meanwhile, prostate-specific membrane antigen (PSMA) positron emission tomography/computed tomography (PET/CT), known for its excellent performance in the detection of recurrent PCa, is increasingly being investigated for broader applications, including primary staging and intraprostatic tumor assessment before definitive treatment [10, 11]. Standardized interpretive frameworks, such as the PRIMARY score further support the role of PSMA PET/CT in characterizing intraprostatic uptake patterns in the pre-treatment diagnostic setting [12]. Among these tracers, [18F]PSMA-1007 has received attention because of several advantages, including a longer half-life, higher image resolution, and hepatobiliary clearance, which may improve pelvic lesion evaluation by minimizing bladder background activity [11]. However, its role in assessing intraprostatic response after ADT remains uncertain, partly due to the PSMA “flare” phenomenon, in which PSMA expression transiently increases during the first 2 to 6 weeks after initiating ADT, potentially mimicking disease progression or overestimating residual tumor activity [13, 14]. As a result, the optimal timing and reliability of PSMA PET for evaluating post-ADT residual disease, which are critical for determining its ability to differentiate viable tumor from therapy-induced changes, have not yet been clearly established.

In this study, we aimed to evaluate the utility of [¹⁸F]PSMA-1007 PET/CT for assessing residual PCa after short-term nADT, with radical prostatectomy histopathology as the reference standard. We compared the diagnostic performance of post-nADT [¹⁸F]PSMA-1007 PET/CT with that of mpMRI. In addition, we assessed pre- and post-nADT differences in imaging characteristics between tumor and benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) regions.

Methods

Study population

This retrospective study included patients with newly diagnosed, pathologically confirmed intermediate- or high-risk PCa who received nADT prior to radical prostatectomy between March 2023 and March 2025 at a single tertiary medical center. A total of 228 patients were initially identified. After a detailed review of medical records, patients were excluded for the following reasons: (1) an interval of more than 1 month between imaging and prostatectomy (n = 182), and (2) an interval of less than 2 months between nADT initiation and [18F]PSMA-1007 PET/CT (n = 26). All included patients received leuprolide acetate after baseline mpMRI. A total of 23 tumor lesions in 20 patients were included in the final study cohort (Fig. 1). All patients underwent baseline and post-nADT mpMRI, as well as post-nADT [18F]PSMA-1007 PET/CT. Quantitative analyses were performed in a predefined subset of patients with complete mpMRI sequences acquired at our institution on the same scanner using the same protocol to ensure methodological consistency. After excluding six patients lacking complete mpMRI data, the quantitative analysis subset comprised 17 tumor lesions in 14 patients. This study was approved by the institutional review board (Severance Hospital, IRB No. 4-2025-0483). The requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study.

Image acquisition of mpMRI and PET/CT

Patients who underwent baseline mpMRI at outside institutions were imaged according to each institution’s respective protocol. At our institution, mpMRI was performed on a 3T MR scanner (Ingenia CX; Philips Healthcare, Best, the Netherlands) without an endorectal coil. Axial, coronal, and sagittal T2-weighted sequences; T1-weighted sequences; axial diffusion-weighted imaging; and dynamic T1-weighted MRI series were obtained with parameters compliant with the PI-RADS v2.1 guidelines [7]. DWI images were acquired by using different b values (b = 50, 1000, 1500 s/mm2), and corresponding ADC maps were automatically generated by the scanner using a monoexponential model. Dynamic prostate imaging was performed after injection of MR contrast agent gadoterate meglumine (Dotarem, Guerbet, Roissy CdG, France).

For [18F]PSMA-1007 PET/CT, approximately 4 MBq/kg of [18F]PSMA-1007 was administered intravenously 90 min before image acquisition. Imaging from the vertex to the proximal thigh was acquired using a PET/CT scanner (Discovery MI; GE Healthcare, Milwaukee, WI, USA). PET emission scanning was performed in three-dimensional mode at 3 min per bed position. Low-dose non-contrast CT was acquired for attenuation correction (27–150 mA, 120 kVp). PET images were reconstructed using an iterative algorithm with corrections for attenuation, scatter, and random events.

Image co-registration of mpMRI and [18F]PSMA-1007 PET/CT

To ensure spatial correspondence across imaging modalities, all images were registered to the T2-weighted MRI, which served as the anatomical reference. For pre-treatment imaging, ADC maps were registered to the T2-weighted images using affine and deformable (greedy) registration. The resulting transformations from ADC-to-T2 registration were applied to the corresponding DWI images at each b-value. Pre-treatment DCE images were independently registered to the T2-weighted images. For post-nADT imaging, PET/CT images were also registered to the post-nADT T2-weighted MRI. The CT component was first registered to the T2-weighted image, and the resulting transformation was applied to the PET image. All registration steps were performed in Python using FireANTs, an ANTs-based registration framework optimized for multimodal medical image alignment [15].

Image and pathologic analysis

All baseline and post-nADT mpMRI sequences were independently reviewed by two experienced genitourinary radiologists. Lesion localization on baseline mpMRI was guided by biopsy-proven tumor sites, whereas post-nADT mpMRI was interpreted with reference to baseline imaging findings. Lesion identification was based on evaluation of all available sequences in accordance with PI-RADS v2.1 criteria [7]. For post-nADT imaging, lesions showing no distinct abnormality across all mpMRI sequences were considered to have no residual intraprostatic disease.

Post-nADT [18F]PSMA-1007 PET/CT images were independently interpreted by two experienced nuclear medicine physicians with reference to baseline imaging findings and biopsy-proven tumor locations. Positive residual tumor was considered present when focal tumor-related tracer uptake exceeded that of the surrounding prostate tissue. Lesions were considered negative for residual disease when tracer uptake was indistinguishable from the surrounding background activity.

All prostatectomy specimens were completely submitted for histopathologic evaluation. The presence and location of residual tumor were annotated on hematoxylin and eosin- stained whole-mount slides by a genitourinary pathologist blinded to the imaging findings. Residual cancer burden was estimated by multiplying the number of slices containing residual tumor by the largest cross-sectional width, length, and block thickness (0.6 cm), followed by application of correction factors of 0.4 and the estimated tumor cellularity to account for three-dimensional volume [16].

PET based tumor volume segmentation

The uptake of [18F]PSMA-1007 was quantified using standardized uptake values (SUV), which represent tracer concentration normalized to injected activity and body weight. To assess residual intraprostatic tumor burden after nADT, tumor volume (TV) was segmented on post-nADT PET images using multiple semi-automated segmentation approaches.

For the semi-automated methods, a nuclear medicine physician broadly selected the lesion-containing region corresponding to the intraprostatic area of focal uptake identified on visual assessment, in order to exclude confounding uptake from BPH. SUVmax was defined as the highest SUV within the selected region. Tumor regions of interest (ROIs) were segmented using percentage thresholds of SUVmax and labeled as TV40%, TV50%, TV60%, TV70%, and TV80%. Fixed-threshold segmentations were performed using absolute SUV cutoffs ranging from 3.0 to 5.0, labeled as TV3.0 through TV5.0. A 1-cm-diameter spherical ROI was placed in a representative area of normal prostate tissue, and the mean SUV (SUVmean) and standard deviation (SD) were calculated. Voxels with SUV values exceeding SUVmean + 2 SD were segmented and labeled as TVmean [17]. Gradient-based segmentation was performed using MIM software [18]. Beginning from an operator-defined seed point near the lesion center, six axes extended outward, along which spatial gradients were interactively calculated. The gradient peak on each axis defined the tumor boundary, and the six axes collectively formed an ellipsoid that served as the initial bounding region for boundary detection. The generated TV was labeled as TVgradient. Manual tumor volume segmentation performed by an experienced nuclear medicine physician was labeled as TVmanual.

Quantitative imaging parameter extraction

Quantitative analysis was performed in the predefined subset (17 tumors in 14 patients). Baseline tumor ROIs were identified on all mpMRI sequences and manually contoured on T2-weighted images using ITK-SNAP, an open-source image processing software [19]. The generated tumor ROIs were overlayed onto the co-registered ADC and DCE images for visual confirmation. Post-nADT tumor ROIs were defined using the PET-based tumor volume segmentation method that showed the highest correlation with pathological tumor volume. Post-nADT BPH reference ROIs were defined as regions showing high SUV uptake on post-treatment PSMA PET but confirmed to be benign on histopathology. A 1 cm diameter spherical ROI was placed in the visible BPH region on post-nADT T2-weighted MRI in the corresponding anatomical location, preferably within the transitional zone where BPH changes were evident. The baseline BPH ROI was then defined by identifying the corresponding anatomical location on baseline T2-weighted images where BPH changes were evident.

The median ADC value was extracted from the ADC map. Ktrans was calculated voxel-wise using a two-compartment Tofts model with a standardized arterial input function (AIF). A model AIF was generated by concatenating Fritz-Hansen’s population-based curve with the Weinmann function, with the arrival time adjusted to achieve the best fit [20]. All calculations were performed using a custom Python-based implementation. Median ADC and Ktrans values were extracted for each ROI. SUVmax was measured on only post-nADT PSMA PET.

Statistical analysis

The primary outcome was the diagnostic performance of mpMRI and [18F]PSMA-1007 PET/CT for detecting residual tumor burden after nADT, using histopathologic analysis as the gold standard. Diagnostic performance metrics were compared using generalized estimating equations to account for multiple lesions per patient. Secondary outcomes included evaluation of the diagnostic performance of serum PSA, the identification of the optimal semi-automated segmentation method for PET-derived tumor volume estimation, assessment of changes in imaging parameters (SUV, ADC, and Ktrans) before and after nADT, and comparison of quantitative parameters between tumor and BPH regions. Correlation between post-nADT serum PSA and pathological tumor volumes was evaluated using the Pearson correlation coefficient. Correlation and agreement between PET-derived and pathological tumor volumes were evaluated using the Pearson correlation coefficient and intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC), respectively. Paired comparisons of imaging parameters before and after nADT were performed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Group-wise comparisons between tumor ROIs and BPH ROIs were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test. As SUVmax was only available for post-nADT PSMA PET, its comparison between tumor and benign regions was limited to post-nADT data. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.0, http://www.r-project.org).

Results

Patient characteristics and clinical treatment response

Among the 20 patients included in this study, the median age was 69 years (range, 52–79), and the median baseline PSA level was 10.6 ng/ml (range, 3.9–81.9). The median duration of nADT before radical prostatectomy was 95 days (range, 79–148). The median interval between [18F]PSMA-1007 PET/CT and radical prostatectomy was 22 days (range, 3–30) and the median interval between post-nADT mpMRI and surgery was 2 days (range, 1–5). A total of 23 biopsy-confirmed tumor lesions were included in the analysis. Gleason scores on pre-nADT biopsy were as follows: 6 (3 + 3) in 3 patients (15.0%), 7 (3 + 4) in 4 (25.0%), 7 (4 + 3) in 8 (40.0%), and 8 (4 + 4) in 5 (25.0%) (Table 1). Baseline MRI detected extraprostatic extension (EPE) in 12 of 20 patients, whereas pathologic EPE was identified in 6 of 20 patients after nADT by the surgical specimen.

Complete pathological response was observed in one patient with a single lesion, and another patient showed a complete pathologic response in one of two lesions. Following nADT, serum PSA levels significantly decreased in all patients, from a median of 10.6 ng/mL (range, 3.9–81.9) at baseline to 0.09 ng/mL (range, 0.01–0.19; p < 0.001). Post-nADT serum PSA levels did not correlate with pathological tumor volume (r = -0.031, p = 0.896) (Fig. 2). Notably, all 10 patients who reached undetectable PSA levels (< 0.1 ng/mL) had residual tumors on pathology.

Comparison of post-nADT diagnostic performance

[18F]PSMA-1007 PET/CT correctly detected 19 of 21 lesions with residual tumor on pathology, yielding a sensitivity of 90.5%. Meanwhile, mpMRI identified 5 out of 21 lesions with residual tumor, yielding a sensitivity of 23.8%. Sensitivity was significantly higher for [18F]PSMA-1007 PET/CT than for mpMRI (p < 0.001) (Table 2). Of the 2 lesions that showed a complete pathological response, both imaging modalities detected one false-positive lesion, showing an identical specificity of 50%. Overall diagnostic accuracy was significantly higher for [18F]PSMA-1007 PET/CT compared to mpMRI (87.0% [20/23] vs. 26.1% [6/23]; p < 0.001) (Fig. 3) (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Volumetric analysis of post-nADT tumor regions with [18F]PSMA-1007 PET/CT

Among the 17 lesions in 14 patients included in the quantitative subset, 2 showed a complete pathological response and 2 were not detected on post-nADT [18F]PSMA-1007 PET/CT. Consequently, 13 lesions in 12 patients with measurable residual uptake on [18F]PSMA-1007 PET/CT were included in the quantitative imaging analysis. Among the percentage-threshold methods based on SUVmax and the fixed-threshold methods, TV60% and TV3.5 showed the strongest correlation with pathological tumor volume, respectively, and were selected for cross-method comparison.

Across all segmentation methods, TVgradient demonstrated the strongest correlation with pathological volume (Pearson r = 0.911, p < 0.001), followed by TVmean (r = 0.821, p < 0.001), TVmanual (r = 0.739, p = 0.004), TV3.5 (r = 0.718, p = 0.006), and TV60% (r = 0.639, p = 0.019) (Fig. 4). The corresponding ICCs for agreement with pathological volume were 0.827 for TVgradient (95% CI, 0.417–0.948), 0.810 for TVmean (95% CI, 0.502–0.937), 0.669 for TV3.5 (95% CI, 0.228–0.885), 0.603 for TV60% (95% CI, 0.136–0.857), and 0.564 for TVmanual (95% CI, 0.068–0.841) (Table 3).

Analysis of baseline and post-nADT quantitative imaging parameters

As TVgradient showed the strongest correlation and agreement with pathological tumor volume, this method was used to define the post-nADT tumor ROI. Six representative BPH ROIs were selected for comparison. In tumor regions, the median ADC significantly increased after nADT (0.849 [0.649–1.262] vs. 1.174 [0.803–1.458], p = 0.005), while the median Ktrans significantly decreased (0.277 [0.107–0.681] vs. 0.080 [0.039–0.374], p = 0.005) (Fig. 5). In contrast, ADC and Ktrans values in BPH regions did not differ significantly between baseline and post-nADT measurements (p = 0.249 and p = 0.917, respectively).

Comparison between tumor and BPH ROIs revealed that, before to nADT, tumor regions showed significantly lower ADC (0.849 [0.649–1.262] vs. 1.250 [1.131–1.573], p = 0.001) and higher Ktrans (0.277 [0.107–0.681] vs. 0.127 [0.027–0.189], p = 0.012) than BPH regions. However, after nADT, no significant differences in ADC or Ktrans were observed between tumor and BPH regions (p = 0.831 and p = 0.701, respectively). Post-nADT SUVmax values also did not differ between tumor and BPH regions (p = 0.639) (Table 4).

Discussion

In this study, we evaluated the diagnostic performance of [18F]PSMA-1007 PET/CT compared with mpMRI for detecting residual PCa following short-term nADT, with radical prostatectomy histopathology as the reference standard. [18F]PSMA-1007 PET/CT showed significantly higher sensitivity than mpMRI for identifying intraprostatic residual tumor burden after approximately 3 months of nADT (90.5% vs. 23.8%, p < 0.001). Although both modalities showed identical specificity (50.0%), the overall diagnostic accuracy of [18F]PSMA-1007 PET/CT was significantly higher than that of mpMRI (87.0% vs. 26.1%, p < 0.001). Notably, all ten patients with undetectable serum PSA levels (< 0.1 ng/mL) still showed residual tumor on histopathology, indicating that PSA alone cannot reliably reflect tumor response. These findings suggest that [18F]PSMA-1007 PET/CT may prove additional value to serum PSA and mpMRI in assessing intraprostatic tumor response following nADT.

Before nADT, tumor regions showed the expected malignant MRI phenotype, with lower ADC and higher Ktrans than BPH (p = 0.001 and p = 0.012, respectively), but these differences disappeared after nADT (p = 0.831 and p = 0.701, respectively). Prior studies have likewise shown that ADT alters diffusion- and perfusion-related MRI parameters, although post-treatment results have been heterogeneous [8, 9, 20–24]. In general, pretreatment differences in ADC and Ktrans between tumor and benign tissue have been consistently observed, whereas post-nADT discrimination has been less consistent. More specifically, ADC often remained significantly different after nADT, whereas Ktrans more frequently lost discriminatory value [8, 9, 20, 21, 23]. This heterogeneity may reflect differences in study design, including ADT regimen and duration, ROI definition, and reference standard. Notably, many previous studies have evaluated post-treatment changes using pretreatment tumor ROIs on MRI, even though these ROIs may no longer correspond to viable residual tumor after nADT [9, 20, 23]. As a result, post-nADT parameter values may reflect a mixture of residual tumor and nADT-altered nontumorous tissue rather than true residual cancer alone [9]. In contrast, we localized residual disease using [18F]PSMA-1007 PET/CT and correlated the imaging findings with whole-mount pathology, which may better reflect true residual intraprostatic tumor burden after nADT.

A key finding of our study is that post-nADT MRI changes may emerge earlier than a meaningful reduction in true residual tumor burden, resulting in an early mismatch between mpMRI findings and actual residual disease. Previous whole-mount pathology-correlated studies that evaluated mpMRI after a longer duration of neoadjuvant therapy suggested that mpMRI can be helpful for post-nADT residual disease assessment [25, 26]. In contrast, we observed loss of lesion conspicuity and disappearance of ADC and Ktrans differences between tumor and benign tissue after approximately 2 to 4 months of treatment. Importantly, residual intraprostatic tumor was still present in 19 of 20 patients, indicating that post-treatment MRI non-visibility cannot be explained simply by conversion to microscopic disease or complete eradication. Rather, early ADT-related changes may reduce tumor-to-background contrast before any comparable reduction in true residual tumor volume has occurred. The unexpectedly low mpMRI sensitivity in our cohort may also be partly explained by the small volume of residual disease after nADT, as pathology-based residual tumor volume ranged from 0 to 2.8 mL with a median of 0.7 mL [27]. Such small post-treatment residual tumors may have limited morphologic conspicuity on mpMRI, whereas PSMA PET/CT may remain visually informative by demonstrating residual PSMA-expressing tumor activity. Accordingly, MRI non-visibility after short-term nADT should not be interpreted as indicating the absence of viable tumor. In this setting, PSA also had limited value for residual tumor assessment, as levels decreased rapidly and reached nadir despite persistent disease, whereas PSMA PET remained visually informative for detecting residual intraprostatic disease in our cohort [28–30].

Consistent with this, [18F]PSMA-1007 PET remained visually informative after nADT in our cohort and showed significantly higher sensitivity for residual intraprostatic tumor detection than mpMRI (90.5% vs. 23.8%, p < 0.001). Although baseline PSMA PET was not available in our study and serial SUV changes could not therefore be assessed directly, prior studies have shown that ADT generally reduces SUV-based PSMA PET metrics during treatment, particularly by approximately 3 months, suggesting that PSMA PET also reflects treatment-related biologic response [29–31]. A potential concern in interpreting post-ADT PSMA PET is transient PSMA flare, which has been reported mainly in the early weeks after treatment initiation; however, because PET was performed after 2–3 months of nADT in our cohort, this effect is unlikely to have materially influenced lesion localization [13, 31, 32]. Unlike previous studies, which mainly evaluated treatment response through changes in PSMA uptake and often lacked prostatectomy-based pathologic validation, our study allowed persistent post-ADT intraprostatic uptake to be interpreted in relation to actual residual tumor burden at prostatectomy, thereby strengthening the evidence for its value in residual disease assessment [28, 30–32]. In our cohort, residual intraprostatic tumor volume remained visually appreciable on PSMA PET despite overall signal reduction, supporting the utility of PET-guided localization after nADT. At the same time, because PSMA uptake decreases globally after treatment, lesion delineation in the treated prostate may be less robust when based solely on SUVmax or fixed cutoff values, making a gradient-based tumor ROI potentially more suitable after ADT [18, 33].

Meanwhile, nodular BPH remained visible after nADT, and although malignant uptake still visually stood out from background activity, SUVmax did not differ significantly between BPH and PCa (p = 0.639), limiting discrimination based on SUV alone. This is notable because, before treatment, higher intraprostatic PSMA uptake and anatomical location have been reported to help distinguish PCa from BPH [12, 34]. Although ADT is known to affect BPH, its effects on nodular BPH and associated PSMA uptake remain poorly characterized [35, 36]. In our study, BPH nodules retained SUV values within previously reported benign ranges despite treatment, making SUV-based separation from PCa less distinct after nADT. Accordingly, without a pretreatment baseline or anatomic/pathologic correlation, post-nADT PSMA PET/CT alone may be insufficient for distinguishing residual tumor from benign uptake, particularly when nodular BPH persists. These findings therefore suggest a mutually complementary role for PSMA PET/CT and MRI for lesion evaluation after nADT, with PSMA PET/CT providing functional information on persistent uptake and MRI providing anatomic context for lesion localization and interpretation. In this context, biparametric MRI, focused on anatomic and diffusion-weighted assessment, may be sufficient for much of the post-nADT local evaluation, particularly if treatment-related changes reduce the added value of contrast-enhanced sequences [7].

This study has several limitations. First, the relatively small sample size, especially for the subset analyses, and the single-tertiary-center design may limit statistical power and generalizability of our results, although the use of strict histopathological verification strengthens the validity of our findings. In particular, the unequal imaging-to-surgery intervals between the two imaging modalities may have introduced temporal bias, as continued nADT exposure during this interval could have further affected the apparent diagnostic performance of mpMRI; however, formal adjustment or sensitivity analysis was not feasible given the small sample size. Further investigation is warranted to validate these results and better define the role of [18F]PSMA-1007 PET/CT in assessing residual disease after nADT. Second, nADT before radical prostatectomy is not currently a routinely recommended treatment approach, and the optimal duration, dose, and regimen remain under debate [4]. In this context, the use of a single LHRH agonist for short-term nADT, combined with the relatively narrow baseline tumor size range (0.09–3.15 mL), may limit the clinical applicability and reproducibility of our results in broader clinical practice. Nonetheless, findings from this cohort remain valuable for understanding imaging response patterns to nADT. Third, technical factors such as PET/MRI registration and the quantitative imaging processing inherently introduce potential measurement errors. To mitigate these effects, we used median values as representative measures. Finally, baseline [18F]PSMA-1007 PET/CT scans before to nADT were not available, precluding direct assessment of SUV changes over the course of therapy.

Conclusions

In conclusion, [18F]PSMA-1007 PET/CT showed higher sensitivity and accuracy than mpMRI for detecting residual PCa after short-term nADT. These preliminary findings support the feasibility of post-nADT [18F]PSMA-1007 PET/CT for residual tumor assessment and suggests its possible utility in evaluating tumor sensitivity to nADT and informing treatment strategies, including nADT duration optimization.
