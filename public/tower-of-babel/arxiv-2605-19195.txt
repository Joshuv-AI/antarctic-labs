The Thermodynamic Costs of Simple Linear Regression
Samuel H. D’Ambrosia, Sultan M. Daniels, Michael R. DeWeese, Anant Sahai

Abstract
The construction of models from data is a significant contributor to the energetic costs of computation. Because of this, understanding how foundational thermodynamic bounds apply to modeling algorithms will be increasingly important. Here, we study the thermodynamic costs of a basic and fundamental modeling algorithm: simple linear regression. Following Landauer, we approximate the thermodynamic lower bound on irreversibly performing both exact linear regression and linear regression via stochastic gradient descent as implemented on floating-point numbers. From this, we derive energy-cost aware scaling laws for the optimal dataset size for training a linear regression model given a generalization error dependent demand for inference. Additionally, we discuss a method to lower bound the entropy production from the mismatch cost for algorithms with continuous input variables.

II
Preliminaries

One-dimensional linear regression forms a model for a data set given by
n
data points
\mathcal{D}=\{(X_{i},Y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
. For a given
w\in\mathbb{R}
, let
\boldsymbol{X}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,\sigma_{x}^{2}I_{n}\right)
,
\boldsymbol{\Xi}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,\sigma_{\xi}^{2}I_{n}\right)
, where
\boldsymbol{X}=\begin{bmatrix}X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\end{bmatrix}^{T}
, and
\boldsymbol{\Xi}=\begin{bmatrix}\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n}\end{bmatrix}^{T}
. The ground truth data labels are given by
\boldsymbol{Y}=w\boldsymbol{X}+\boldsymbol{\Xi}
.
\boldsymbol{X}
and
\boldsymbol{\Xi}

are assumed to be independent, with
\sigma_{x}>0
and
\sigma_{\xi}>0
.
Equivalently, each data point is independently sampled from the distribution

f_{XY}(x,y)=\frac{1}{2\pi\sigma_{x}\sigma_{\xi}}\,\exp\bigg[-\frac{x^{2}}{2\sigma_{x}^{2}}-\frac{(y-wx)^{2}}{2\sigma_{\xi}^{2}}\bigg].

(1)

We will consider fitting this data with a single variable
\hat{w}
, representing the slope of a line passing through zero. The loss function for the model will be the mean squared error

L(\hat{w})=\frac{1}{2n}\sum^{n}_{i=1}(\hat{w}x_{i}-y_{i})^{2}.

(2)

There are two methods we will consider for finding
\hat{w}
: using the analytic formula to find the exact best fit given the data set, and using stochastic gradient descent to minimize the error. The exact expression for the optimal
\hat{w}
given a dataset with
n
data points is:

\hat{w}=\frac{\sum^{n}_{i=1}x_{i}y_{i}}{\sum^{n}_{i=1}x_{i}^{2}}.

(3)

On the other hand, stochastic gradient descent (SGD) allows us to approximate the best fit using an iterative updating method. Batches of size
B
are sampled from an infinite data stream of independent and identically distributed
(X_{i},Y_{i})
pairs, and used to update the model parameters.
\hat{w}
is initialized at the value
\hat{w}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
, and updated for each batch using

\hat{w}_{k+1}=\hat{w}_{k}-\frac{\eta}{B}\sum_{(x_{i},y_{i})\in\mathcal{B}}\frac{\partial\ell(\hat{w},(x_{i},y_{i}))}{\partial\hat{w}}\bigg|_{\hat{w}=\hat{w}_{k}},

(4)

where
\eta
is the learning rate,
k
is the step number, and the per-sample loss function is
\ell(\hat{w},(x_{i},y_{i}))=\frac{1}{2}(\hat{w}x_{i}-y_{i})^{2}
.

While these results are theoretically understood as a relation among continuous variables, here we assume that the computation that determines
\hat{w}
is implemented on discrete registers, each of which serves as a representation for a single continuous number. For each continuous variable, we can define a quantization map
Q(X)=X_{Q}
that takes a continuous random variable
X
to a discrete random variable
X_{Q}
. Assuming the representation is stored on
R
physical bits, the quantization is given by a string of binary variables
x_{Q}\in\{0,1\}^{R}
. We will denote the Shannon entropy of a quantized variable
X_{Q}
as
H(X_{Q})=-\sum_{x_{Q}}p(x_{Q})\log[p(x_{Q})]
, where
\log[\cdot]
is base 2. Additionally, we avoid the more detailed numerical analysis question of how finite-precision arithmetic perturbs the computation by assuming the regression algorithms evolve according to their ideal real-valued form. Accordingly, if
\hat{W}
denotes the model parameter produced by the ideal continuous algorithm, we assume stored output is approximated by
Q(\hat{W})=\hat{W}_{Q}
.

Physical Implementation

Here we will adopt the
standard accounting convention
following Wolpert
[
16

A logically irreversible computation is a process where the output state of the computer cannot be used to uniquely determine the input state, resulting in a loss of information. Under the standard accounting convention, computations are assumed to be performed by a cyclic device that computes output states from input states, while only saving outputs

While reversible algorithms for linear regression

]
and stochastic gradient descent

[

.

We assume the physical system that implements the computation is composed of physical bits, systems with two distinct physical states labeled
\{0,1\}
. Let
I,M,O\in\mathbb{N}
, and
\mathcal{X}_{I}=\{0,1\}^{I}
denote the set of possible states of the input bits,
\mathcal{X}_{M}=\{0,1\}^{M}
denote the set of possible states of any intermediary bits, and
\mathcal{X}_{O}=\{0,1\}^{O}
denote the set of possible states of the output bits. The joint logical state of the computer will be denoted by
x_{c}=(x_{I},x_{M},x_{O})\in\mathcal{X}_{c}=\mathcal{X}_{I}\times\mathcal{X}_{M}\times\mathcal{X}_{O}
, where
x_{I}\in\mathcal{X}_{I}
,
x_{M}\in\mathcal{X}_{M}
,
x_{O}\in\mathcal{X}_{O}
. Let
p(x_{c})
be a probability distribution over
\mathcal{X}_{c}
.

The equivalence between thermodynamic entropy and Gibbs-Von Neumann entropy is a classic result in statistical mechanics
[
36
,
37
,
38
]
. Following Maroney
[
39
, p. 13]
, the Gibbs-Von Neumann entropy (referred to as the thermodynamic entropy) of a physical system with distinct logical states
x_{c}
is given by

S_{sys}=k_{B}\ln[2]H(p(x_{c}))+\sum_{x_{c}\in\mathcal{X}_{c}}p(x_{c})S_{x_{c}}

(5)

where
S_{x_{c}}
is the internal entropy of each logical state, and
H(p(x_{c}))=-\sum_{x_{c}\in\mathcal{X}_{c}}p(x_{c})\log[p(x_{c})]
. Here we assume the internal entropy of each logical state is the same
\forall x_{c}\in\mathcal{X}_{c},S_{x_{c}}=S_{0}
(i.e. the 0 state of each bit has the same number of equally likely underlying physical microstates as the 1 state), meaning the average internal entropy over any probability distribution
p(x_{c})
over computational states will be
\sum_{x_{c}\in\mathcal{X}_{c}}p(x_{c})S_{x_{c}}=S_{0}
.

Landauer’s principle holds that a logically irreversible computation must incur an energetic cost, due to logically irreversible operations reducing the thermodynamic entropy of the computational system
[
9
]
. Assuming the computational system begins and ends at the same average internal energy
U_{0}
, and all components of the computational system remain in thermal equilibrium with their environment at temperature
T
, performing an entropy-reducing computation expels on average at least
Q\geq Q_{min}=-T\Delta S_{sys}
heat into the computational system’s environment
[
9
,
39
]
, and requires on average
W\geq W_{min}=-T\Delta S_{sys}
work (i.e. work would be energy taken from the computer’s battery, since by the first law of thermodynamics, if
\Delta U=W-Q=0
,
Q=W

[
40
]
). From this we associate the energetic cost
\Delta E
with the work required and heat expelled,
\Delta E\triangleq W=Q
. Under these assumptions, the generalized Landauer’s bound states the lower bound on the average energetic cost of a computation is given by
\Delta E\geq\Delta E_{min}=-T\Delta S_{sys}

[
9
,
39
]
.

The standard accounting convention imposes the following constraints on the probability of computational states at a given step of the computational process. Let the computational process finish at step
F
. Let
x_{c}^{0}=(x_{I}^{0},x_{M}^{0},x_{O}^{0})\in\mathcal{X}_{c}
be the initial state of the computer. At step
0
,
p_{0}(x_{c})=\delta_{x_{c},x_{c}^{0}}
. The cyclic device will return to this state after the computation finishes. At step
1
, the input is loaded while all other bits remain in their initial state, implying
p_{1}(x_{c})=p(x_{I})\delta_{x_{M},x_{M}^{0}}\delta_{x_{O},x_{O}^{0}}
, where
p(x_{I})
is the probability of input states in
\mathcal{X}_{I}
. Following
[
16
, p. 34]
if we assume all registers are initialized to the known state
x_{c}^{0}
, loading the input at step 1 can be done in an energetically cost free manner. At step
F-1
, the output will have been computed, while the input and intermediary bits are assumed to have been reset to their initial positions specified by
x_{c}^{0}
, implying that
p_{F-1}(x_{c})=\delta_{x_{I},x_{I}^{0}}\delta_{x_{M},x_{M}^{0}}p(x_{O})
where
p(x_{O})
is the probability of output states in
\mathcal{X}_{O}
. Finally, step
F
resets the system to
p_{F}(x_{c})=p_{0}(x_{c})=\delta_{x_{c},x_{c}^{0}}
. If the output is saved and passed on to another computational system, the final step
F
which resets the output bits can be undertaken in an energetically cost free manner
[
16
, p. 34]
, which matches our case since we would want to keep the model for use after training.

This implies that only steps from 1 to
F-1
incur an energetic cost, fixed by the difference in thermodynamic entropy between step 1 and step
F-1
. With
p_{1}(x_{c})=p(x_{I})\delta_{x_{M},x_{M}^{0}}\delta_{x_{O},x_{O}^{0}}
and
X_{I}\sim p(x_{I})
, by Eq. (
5
) we see the thermodynamic entropy of the system at step 1 is
S_{1}=k_{B}\ln[2]H(X_{I})+S_{0}
. Similarly, with
p_{F-1}(x_{c})=\delta_{x_{I},x_{I}^{0}}\delta_{x_{M},x_{M}^{0}}p(x_{O})
and
X_{O}\sim p(x_{O})
we have
S_{F-1}=k_{B}\ln[2]H(X_{O})+S_{0}
. Thus, assuming equal internal entropy
S_{0}
across each logical state
x_{c}\in\mathcal{X}_{c}
, unchanged average energy
U_{0}
, and thermal equilibrium with an environment at temperature
T
, the lower bound on the energetic cost
\Delta E_{min}=-T\Delta S_{sys}=T(S_{1}-S_{F-1})
of moving from step 1 to step
F-1
is given by,

\Delta E_{min}=k_{B}T\ln[2](H(X_{I})-H(X_{O})).

(6)

\Delta E_{min}
will be referred to as the
Landauer cost
(referred to as the ‘unconstrained’ or ‘all-at-once’ Landauer cost in
[
16
]
).

We assume the computation is implemented ‘all-at-once’, meaning the details of the thermodynamic cost of the intermediary transitions between 1 and
F-1
states, or the use and re-initialization of any intermediary registers

, are ignored. As discussed in
[
16
, p. 55]

Focusing on the costs associated with input and output states allows us to obtain a lower bound on the energetic cost of the computation while leaving the analysis of more specific and complex intermediary steps to future work.

The Landauer cost provides a lower bound on the energetic cost of a logically irreversible computation by assuming the physical implementation is thermodynamically reversible (note that thermodynamic and logical reversibility are entirely distinct
[
41

[

43

VII
discusses one type of energetic cost beyond the thermodynamically reversible lower bound: the mismatch cost (MMC)
[
16
,
17
,
19
,
18
]

III
The Entropy of Floating-point Numbers

In digital hardware, numerical values are stored as bit strings in finite registers, coming in many forms. Two examples are integers, uniformly spaced values with a range specified by the number of bits, and floating-point numbers, which uses a finite register of binary bits to represent a real value
x
in binary scientific notation.
Here, we focus on floating-point numbers, due to their ubiquitous role in representing continuous variables, and because floating point numbers play a central role in modern deep learning algorithms and hardware
[
27
,
28
]
.
As deep neural network training and inference loads attempt to saturate the computational resources of specialized hardware, there has been renewed interest in developing novel floating-point formats that find the right tradeoffs between precision, dynamic range, memory, computational speed and numerical stability
[
44
]
.
Some examples are microscaling (MX) formats
[
45
,
46
,
47
]
where
k
numbers are represented with a single shared exponent that encodes the scale of the block, and
k
scalar elements that are encoded using 8 bit or less floating point or integer formats.
2
2

2

An example is MXFP8 where a block has
32
elements with a shared 8-bit exponent and each scalar element is encoded with FP8 using four exponent bits and three significand bits.

III-A

The Structure of a Floating-point Number

In this paper, we will use a simple normalized floating-point format with midpoint rounding that does not use subnormal numbers (as defined in
[
48
]
) and where zero is not in the representable set.

Let
p\in\mathbb{N}
,
E\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
, and
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
. The following map is defined on
\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
; since all distributions used in this paper are absolutely continuous, the event that the value zero is realized is probability-zero. Define

\mathrm{round}_{p}(\alpha)\triangleq\left(\argmin\limits_{i\in\{0,\dots,2^{p-1}\}}\left|\alpha-i2^{-(p-1)}\right|\right)2^{-(p-1)},

(12)

where ties in the
\argmin
function are broken towards the higher index
i
. Assuming rounding to the nearest representable value, the value stored on a floating-point number can be written as

x_{fp}(x)\triangleq(-1)^{s_{fp}(x)}\times 2^{e_{fp}(x)}\times\left(1+m_{fp}(x)\right),

(13)

where

\displaystyle s_{fp}(x)

\displaystyle\triangleq\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{x<0\right\}},

(14)

\displaystyle\tilde{e}_{fp}(x)

\displaystyle\triangleq-\left(2^{E-1}-1\right)\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{\log|x|<-\left(2^{E-1}-1\right)\right\}}

\displaystyle+\sum\limits_{i=0}^{2^{E}-1}\left[i-\left(2^{E-1}-1\right)\right]\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{i-\left(2^{E-1}-1\right)\leq\log|x|<i+1-\left(2^{E-1}-1\right)\right\}}+2^{E-1}\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{\log|x|\geq 2^{E-1}+1\right\}}

\displaystyle m_{fp}(x)

\displaystyle\triangleq\mathrm{round}_{p}\left(|x|2^{-\tilde{e}_{fp}(x)}-1\right)\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{\log|x|<2^{E-1}+1\right\}}\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{\mathrm{round}_{p}\left(|x|2^{-\tilde{e}_{fp}(x)}-1\right)\neq 1\right\}}

\displaystyle+\left(1-2^{-(p-1)}\right)\left(\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{\log|x|\geq 2^{E-1}+1\right\}}+\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{2^{E-1}\leq\log|x|<2^{E-1}+1\right\}}\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{\mathrm{round}_{p}\left(|x|2^{-\tilde{e}_{fp}(x)}-1\right)=1\right\}}\right)

\displaystyle e_{fp}(x)

\displaystyle\triangleq\min\left(2^{E-1},\tilde{e}_{fp}(x)+\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{\mathrm{round}_{p}\left(|x|2^{-\tilde{e}_{fp}(x)}-1\right)=1\right\}}\right).

The quantities in Eq. (
14
) are decoded numerical values:
s_{fp}(x)
is a single sign bit,
e_{fp}(x)
is the binary exponent for
E\geq 1
(for
E=0
, the format has a single exponent level with implicit exponent value
1/2
), and
m_{fp}
is the significand (or mantissa) which encodes the binary significant digits of

in the form
1.m_{fp}(x)
; in the physical registers these are encoded as binary integers using
1
,
E
, and
(p-1)
bits respectively
[
48
,
49
]
. Since each stored field is a finite set of non-negative integers constrained by the number of bits in the register, this structure can only represent a finite set
U_{fp}
of representable numbers along the real number line. For example, the standard single-precision IEEE-754 format uses one bit for
s_{fp}
, 23 bits for
m_{fp}
, and eight bits for
e_{fp}
. Note that the format defined by Eq. (
14
) is an idealized normalized floating-point format: it does not include zero, subnormal numbers, infinities, or NaNs, and its exponent range (
e_{\min}=-(2^{E-1}-1)
,

The structure of a floating-point number is illustrated in Fig.

III-C

Illustrative and Computationally Tractable Approximations

We can gain further insight into the entropy of floating-point numbers, and a significant computational advantage for the entropy of large floating-point numbers, if we make three approximations which allow for the derivation of analytic expressions for the floating point entropy: relating the discrete entropy of the quantized variable to the differential entropy of the continuous variable, smoothing and extending the bin size function, and approximating non-zero mean distributions. The approximations applied here are discussed for a broader class of distributions in
[
32
]

Approximation 1 – Relating discrete and differential entropy

The first approximation relates the differential entropy of a continuous random variable to the discrete entropy of its counterpart discrete representation. This has been studied by
[
50
,
51
,
52
,
53
,
54
,
55
,
29
]

. This results in the well known relationship
H(X_{Q})\approx h(X)-\log\Delta
, where

However, the formalism in
[
29
]
can be extended to non-uniform bins, and applied to the case of floating-point numbers. In App.
C
, the relationship between the differential and discrete entropy is derived for the non-uniform quantization of a
d
-dimensional distribution on

independent registers. For simplicity, here we can focus on the case of a univariate distribution where

. Assuming bounded support on the region of

bounded by the granular region (see

]

\mathbb{U}\triangleq[-2^{e_{max}+1}+2^{e_{max}-p},\,\,2^{e_{max}+1}-2^{e_{max}-p}]
, where

. Approximation 1 is

, where

\tilde{H}(X_{Q})\triangleq-\int_{\mathbb{U}}f_{X}(x)\log[f_{X}(x)\Delta(x)]dx=h(X)-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{U}}\left[\log[\Delta(x)]\right],

(15)

Approximation 2 – Smoothing the bin size function and extending its domain

For floating-point numbers, the spacing between adjacent representable values is constant inside each exponent block. When the value to be quantized
x
is within the range
[2^{e_{fp}(x)},2^{e_{fp}(x)+1})
, a floating-point number can represent
2^{(p-1)}
equally spaced values. Thus for
2^{e_{fp}(x)}\leq x<2^{e_{fp}(x)+1}
, meaning that
x
is in a bin in the interior of an exponent block, the bin size is,

\Delta(x)=\frac{2^{e_{fp}(x)+1}-2^{e_{fp}(x)}}{2^{p-1}}=2^{e_{fp}(x)-(p-1)}\text{ if }x\in\mathbb{U}.

(16)

In the main text, only the bin size when
x
is in an interior bin is given for readability; the true midpoint bin-size function
\Delta(x)
from App.
C
takes on different values when
x
is in the two outer clipping bins, the exponent-boundary bins and the two bins adjacent to zero. These special bins are treated explicitly in App.
C
.

Our second approximation will alter the bin size. First we can smooth the steps by introducing a best-fit linear approximation as shown as the dashed red line in Fig.
1(a)
, which uses
e_{s}(x)\triangleq\log[|x|/\sqrt{2}]
. This gives us

\Delta_{s}(x)\triangleq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}|x|\cdot 2^{1-p}\approx\Delta(x).

(17)

By Theorem
C.1
, the error caused by introducing this smooth approximation is bounded by
d/2
plus the small contribution from the bins adjacent to zero; for most distributions it will be much smaller due to cancellation in the overestimation and underestimation.

Unlike
\Delta(x)
, it is simple to define
\Delta_{s}(x)
for any
x\in\mathbb{R}
. With this, and by assuming the probability of overflow and underflow values is small,
\int_{\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathbb{U}}f_{X}(x)dx\approx 0
, we extend the domain of integration from
\mathbb{U}
to
\mathbb{R}
. Since
\Delta_{s}(x)
grows only linearly in
|x|

\int_{\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathbb{U}}f_{X}(x)|\log[f_{X}(x)\Delta_{s}(x)]|\,dx
is near zero for all distributions used in this paper (Gaussian tails decay exponentially and Student’s
t
tails decay as a power law, both of which dominate the linear growth of
\Delta_{s}
) when the granular region
\mathbb{U}
is sufficiently large. Approximation 2 states that
\tilde{H}_{s}(x)\approx\tilde{H}(x)

\tilde{H}_{s}(X_{fp})\triangleq h(X)+(p-1)-\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}f_{X}(x)\log\left[\frac{|x|}{\sqrt{2}}\right]dx,

(18)

where
h(X)=-\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}f_{X}(x)\log f_{X}(x)dx
. Approximation 2 applies when
f_{X}(x)
has low overflow or underflow probability. Beyond this, the bin-size smoothing error is bounded by
d/2
plus the small contribution from the bins adjacent to zero (Theorem
C.1
); the domain extension from
\mathbb{U}
to
\mathbb{R}
is a separate approximation controlled by the probability mass outside
\mathbb{U}

18

V-A

Asymptotic Behavior

We can begin by considering the expression for
f_{\hat{W}_{\tau}}(\hat{w}_{\tau})
that applies in the case where SGD has been relatively successful after
\tau
steps, meaning
\hat{w}_{\tau}\approx w
. Under the infinite-stream model of Section
II
, the population gradient at
w
is zero, and the mean-squared error loss function is
\sigma_{x}^{2}
-strongly convex so with a sufficiently small step size
\eta
the iterates concentrate near
w

[
62
]
.

Following
[
59
,
60
,
61
]
, the update rule for
\hat{w}_{k}
given by Eq. (
4
) can be seen as a discretization of a continuous-time Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process. The limit stationary distribution of the continuous-time Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process can then be analyzed and any time discretization errors are neglected. We can first consider the asymptotic behavior of this process to approximate
f_{\hat{W}_{\tau}}(\hat{w}_{\tau})
as the stationary distribution of this process. Rewriting Eq. (
4
) with
\ell_{i}(\hat{w})\triangleq\ell(\hat{w},(x_{i},y_{i}))
,
g_{i}(\hat{w}_{k})=\partial\ell_{i}(\hat{w}_{k})/\partial\hat{w}_{k}=x_{i}(\hat{w}_{k}x_{i}-y_{i})
, and
\hat{g}_{B}(\hat{w}_{k})=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}}g_{i}(\hat{w}_{k})
, our update rule becomes

\hat{w}_{k+1}=\hat{w}_{k}-\eta\hat{g}_{B}(\hat{w}_{k}),

(29)

where
\eta
is the step size and
k
is the step number. When
B
is sufficiently large, following
[
59
,
61
]
, the central limit theorem can be invoked to assume the gradient noise is normally distributed, therefore we assume

\hat{g}_{B}(\hat{w}_{k})\approx\mathbb{E}\left[g_{i}(\hat{w}_{k})\right]+\frac{1}{\sqrt{B}}\Delta g(\hat{w}_{k}),\,\,\,\Delta g(\hat{w}_{k})\sim\mathcal{N}(0,C(\hat{w}_{k})),

(30)

where
C(\hat{w})
is the variance of the gradient.

The batch gradient at the optimum
w
is
g_{i}(w)=X_{i}(wX_{i}-(wX_{i}+\xi_{i}))=-X_{i}\xi_{i}
.
Therefore, when the step size
\eta
is small, we can assume
\hat{w}_{\tau}\approx w
, and we have
g_{i}(\hat{w}_{\tau})\approx-X_{i}\xi_{i}
. With
X_{i}
and
\xi_{i}
independent, we can assume the gradient variance is thus given by the product of the variances
C(w)=\sigma_{x}^{2}\sigma_{\xi}^{2}
. Under these approximations, we can rewrite Eq. (
29
) as a Langevin equation in terms of
\phi=\hat{w}_{k}-w

\frac{d\phi}{dt}=-\sigma_{x}^{2}\,\phi+\sqrt{\frac{\eta}{B}}\sigma_{x}\sigma_{\xi}\,\epsilon(t),

(31)

where
\epsilon(t)
is a Gaussian white noise process with
\mathbb{E}\left[\epsilon(t)\epsilon(t^{\prime})\right]=\delta(t-t^{\prime})
and the continuous time variable
t
is identified with the rescaled discrete SGD step index (i.e.,
t=\eta k
). The coefficient
\sigma_{x}^{2}
in front of
\phi
on the right hand side is given by expected Hessian of the population loss function
[
59
]
, that is
\partial^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[L(\hat{w})\right]/\partial\hat{w}^{2}=\sigma^{2}_{x}
.
In the Langevin equation
\sigma_{x}^{2}
acts as the effective force constant of a harmonic potential, while
T_{\text{eff}}=\eta\sigma^{2}_{x}\sigma^{2}_{\xi}/2B
acts as an effective temperature. Let
f_{\phi}
be the probability density of
\phi(\tau)
. The resulting stationary distribution is

f_{\hat{W}_{\tau}}(\hat{w}_{\tau})=f_{\phi(\tau)}(\hat{w}_{\tau}-w)\approx\sqrt{\frac{B}{\pi\eta\sigma_{\xi}^{2}}}\cdot\exp\bigg(-\frac{B(\hat{w}_{\tau}-w)^{2}}{\eta\sigma_{\xi}^{2}}\bigg).

(32)

This gives us an approximation for the output distribution of regression via SGD. We see that
f_{\hat{W}_{\tau}}(\hat{w}_{\tau})\sim\mathcal{N}\left(w,\frac{\eta\sigma_{\xi}^{2}}{2B}\right)
. For
|w|\gg\sqrt{\frac{\eta\sigma_{\xi}^{2}}{2B}}
, App.
C

Otherwise, for

(33)

\tau

Energy-cost Aware Scaling Laws

Empirical scaling laws
[
64
,
65
]
have become tools for deep learning practitioners to set the best model and dataset size for a fixed training compute budget.
However,
[
66
]
shows that the cost of running inference on a model after it has already been trained is a more significant burden on those who deploy models.

[
33
]
addresses this by deriving a scaling law that finds the best model and dataset size that will achieve a certain value of pretraining loss while minimizing the total training and inference costs over the lifetime of the model.

Here, we derive a scaling law in this simple setting of linear regression that finds the optimal dataset size that will maximize profit given prices of energy and inference, and a generalization error dependent user demand for inference
[
33
]
.
With continuous-valued regression, training loss and generalization error are both evaluated using mean-squared error, so we optimize directly over generalization error.
This correspondence is not guaranteed in language modeling, where the relationship between pretraining loss and downstream performance is more complex
[
67
,
68
]
.
Also, in our single-parameter setting, the smooth continuous approximation predicts near-zero Landauer cost for inference when
\hat{w}\neq 0
; however, the exact quantized map need not be injective and therefore need not be logically reversible.
Although a more complicated multi-parameter regression model setting is necessary to better capture the effect of inference costs, we can still demonstrate tradeoffs between training data size and generalization error in this single-parameter setting.

VI-A

Landauer Cost of Running Inference

Running inference on the trained model
\hat{w}
, consists of multiplying a data sample independent of the training data
X^{test}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,\sigma_{x}^{2}\right)
by
\hat{w}
. This gives
\hat{Y}=\hat{w}X^{test}
. Assuming inference is run on a cyclic device, the input registers now hold
X_{I}=(X_{fp}^{test},\hat{w}_{fp})
, while the output state holds
X_{O}=(\hat{Y}_{fp},\hat{w}_{fp})
. Since
\hat{w}
is fixed during inference,
H(\hat{w}_{fp})=0
, and the Landauer cost Eq. (
6
) reduces to

\Delta E_{min}^{inf}=k_{B}T\ln[2](H(X^{test}_{fp})-H(\hat{Y}_{fp}))

(36)

However, since
\hat{Y}=\hat{w}X^{test}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,\hat{w}^{2}\sigma_{x}^{2}\right)
, under our approximation Eq. (
18
) and when
\hat{w}\neq 0
, we see

\displaystyle\tilde{H}_{s}(\hat{Y}_{fp})=\tilde{H}_{s}(X_{fp}^{test})\approx\tilde{H}^{0}_{s}(p),

(37)

which gives us
\Delta E_{min}^{inf}\approx 0
.
This implies that under the smooth entropy approximation Eq. (
18
), the entropy difference between the input and output is near zero for running inference on this single-parameter model, predicting nearly zero Landauer cost for
\hat{w}\neq 0
. This is expected, since for fixed
\hat{w}\neq 0
the ideal real-valued map
x\mapsto\hat{w}x
is bijective, which is why the continuous approximation predicts zero entropy change. However, the actual quantized map
x_{fp}\mapsto Q(\hat{w}x)
need not be injective, so the implemented discrete computation is not generally logically reversible and can still incur nonzero Landauer cost. For multi-parameter models where the input is not recoverable from the output even at the continuous level, we would expect inference to incur a nonzero Landauer cost.

VII
Bounding the Mismatch Cost of a Continuous Variable Algorithm

Now we turn to the mismatch cost (MMC). While Landauer’s bound assumes thermodynamic reversibility, there will in general be additional costs beyond
Q=-T\Delta S_{sys}
. Beyond this bound, the average heat flow
Q
will depend on physical details such as the speed with which bits are manipulated
[
69
,
70
]
, or if the bits are instantiated by physical systems that continuously dissipate heat
[
71
]
. A clarifying equivalent definition of a thermodynamically reversible process in terms of the second law of thermodynamics can help us understand energetic costs beyond reversibility. The second law of thermodynamics states
total entropy production

\Delta S_{tot}
must be non-negative, defined as the total change in entropy of the system
\Delta S_{sys}
and its environment
\Delta S_{env}
,
\Delta S_{tot}\triangleq\Delta S_{env}+\Delta S_{sys}\geq 0
. A process is thermodynamically reversible when
\Delta S_{tot}=0

[
40
,
42
,
43
]
. Mismatch cost quantifies the additional total entropy production that arises when the distribution of the system at the start of the computation differs from the optimal starting distribution that minimizes total entropy production.

Here, we discuss a method to lower bound the MMC in the case of parameterized continuous input distributions. While
\Delta S_{sys}
can be assessed at the algorithmic level based on the analysis above,
\Delta S_{env}
and
\Delta S_{tot}
will depend on the explicit time-dependent dynamics of the physically implemented computational system
[
16
,
19
,
17
]
. Because of this, we cannot compute the MMC directly without more physical detail beyond the scope of this paper. Instead, this section serves the purpose of
demonstrating a bound on the MMC for computational systems that take a parameterized
continuous distribution as input (such as with linear regression), only given an assumed
\Delta S_{env}
, while leaving the true determination of
\Delta S_{env}
to future work. While it remains undetermined, Appendix
I
discusses common assumptions which allow one to conceptually understand the assumed
\Delta S_{env}
, associating it with heat by
\Delta S_{env}=Q/T
. Under these assumptions, we see
Q=Q_{min}+T\Delta S_{tot}=-T\Delta S_{sys}+T\Delta S_{tot}
, meaning
\Delta S_{tot}
can be understood as contributing to the energetic cost beyond the minimum implied by the Landauer cost.

With
\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{\mathcal{X}_{c}}
as the probability simplex over the joint logical state of the computer
\mathcal{X}_{c}
, we can think of the computation as taking us from
p_{1}\in\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{\mathcal{X}_{c}}
to
p_{F-1}\in\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{\mathcal{X}_{c}}
(with
p_{s}
being the probability density at step
s
) where
p_{F-1}
is fixed by a conditional distribution
\pi(x_{c}|x_{c}^{\prime})
, where

p_{F-1}(x_{c})=\sum_{x_{c}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}_{c}}\pi(x_{c}|x_{c}^{\prime})p_{1}(x_{c}^{\prime}).

(41)

The conditional distribution
\pi(x_{c}|x_{c}^{\prime})
can be interpreted as resulting from a physical process, as discussed in App.
I
. We can use the notation
p_{F-1}=\pi p_{1}
to emphasize that
p_{F-1}
is purely a function of the input state
p_{1}
and the physical manipulations performed by the computation which determine
\pi(x_{c}|x_{c}^{\prime})
.

Assuming a fixed physical implementation of the algorithm defining
\pi(x_{c}|x_{c}^{\prime})
, a portion of the total entropy production
\Delta S_{tot}
will be due to the MMC, which results from a divergence between the actual input distribution
p_{1}
and the optimal initial distribution
q_{1}\in\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{\mathcal{X}_{c}}
which minimizes entropy production. With
\Delta S_{sys}(p_{1})=k_{B}\ln[2](H(\pi p_{1})-H(p_{1}))
the optimal input distribution
q_{1}
defined by the minimum of

q_{1}\triangleq\arg\min_{r_{1}\in\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{\mathcal{X}_{c}}}[\Delta S_{tot}(r_{1})]=\arg\min_{r_{1}\in\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{\mathcal{X}_{c}}}[k_{B}\ln[2]\big(H(\pi r_{1})-H(r_{1})\big)+\Delta S_{env}(r_{1})].

(42)

Kolchinsky and Wolpert show that given a distribution with minimal entropy production
q_{1}
,

\Delta S_{tot}(p_{1})=\Delta S_{tot}(q_{1})+k_{B}\ln[2]\big(D(p_{1}||q_{1})-D(\pi p_{1}||\pi q_{1})\big),

(43)

in
[
72
,
73
,
19
]
(where
D(p||q)=\sum_{x_{c}\in\mathcal{X}_{c}}p(x_{c})\log[p(x_{c})/q(x_{c})]
is the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence). The MMC is defined by

\text{MMC}(p_{1})\triangleq k_{B}\ln[2]\big(D(p_{1}||q_{1})-D(\pi p_{1}||\pi q_{1})\big)=\Delta S_{tot}(p_{1})-\Delta S_{tot}(q_{1}),

(44)

There may still be non-zero minimal entropy production for the optimal initial state
\Delta S_{tot}(q_{1})
called the ‘residual entropy production’, however its character depends on further physical details which we ignore here
[
16
,
17
]
.

Continuous Variable Algorithms:
Several barriers arise for analyzing the MMC in a continuous valued context. Finding
q_{1}
requires an optimization across the entire probability simplex of possible distributions
\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{\mathcal{X}_{c}}
. However, this becomes intractable for reasonably sized discretely represented continuous variables: a computational system with just one single-precision floating-point number contains
2^{32}
states. Additionally, the input to such an algorithm is often parameterized by a variational continuous probability density such as Eq. (
1
), restricting the accessible subset of
\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{\mathcal{X}_{c}}
.

Instead, we can show that a positive lower bound on the MMC can be determined purely by considering a restricted set of variational input distributions. This can be specified by restricting the optimization to a sub-family of distributions specified by the quantization of a variational continuous probability density such as Eq. (
1
). Let
\mathcal{V}=\{p_{I,\theta}(x_{c})\delta_{x_{M},x_{M}^{0}}\delta_{x_{O},x_{O}^{0}},\theta\in\Theta\}\subseteq\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{\mathcal{X}_{c}}
, where
\Theta
corresponds to a bounded subregion of possible variational parameters, and
p_{I,\theta}(x_{c})
is the input distribution induced by quantizing the distribution parameterized by
\theta
. For example, in regression we can take Eq. (
1
) as our variational family parameterized by
\theta=\{\sigma_{x},\sigma_{\xi}\},\Theta=[\sigma_{x}^{min},\sigma_{x}^{max}]\times[\sigma_{\xi}^{min},\sigma_{\xi}^{max}]\subset\mathbb{R^{+}}\times\mathbb{R^{+}}
(perhaps including a distribution over the initial SGD parameter
\hat{W}_{0}
). From this we can define the
variational MMC
, MMC
v
, as

\text{MMC}_{v}(p_{1})=\Delta S_{tot}(p_{1})-\Delta S_{tot}(q_{1,v}),

(45)

where
q_{1,v}\in\mathcal{V}
. The optimal variational distribution
q_{1,v}
is found by an optimization over
\mathcal{V}
, in which the parameters
\theta
are varied through the restricted subregion
\Theta
:

q_{1,v}\triangleq\arg\min_{r_{1,v}\in\mathcal{V}}[\Delta S_{tot}(r_{1,v})]=\arg\min_{r_{1,v}\in\mathcal{V}}[k_{B}\ln[2]\big(H(\pi r_{1,v})-H(r_{1,v})\big)+\Delta S_{env}(r_{1,v})]

(46)

We can show that MMC
v
provides a positive lower bound on the true MMC of a variational input distribution
p_{1,v}\in\mathcal{V}
. First, we can note that since
\mathcal{V}\subseteq\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{\mathcal{X}_{c}}
, the variational optimum
q_{1,v}
must have a larger or equal entropy production than the true optimum is
q_{1}
, meaning that
q_{1,v}
itself must have a mismatch cost

\text{MMC}(q_{1,v})=k_{B}\ln[2]\big(D(q_{1,v}||q_{1})-D(\pi q_{1,v}||\pi q_{1})\big)=\Delta S_{tot}(q_{1,v})-\Delta S_{tot}(q_{1}).

(47)

Since
q_{1}
minimizes
\Delta S_{tot}
over the full simplex
\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{\mathcal{X}_{c}}
and
q_{1,v}\in\mathcal{V}\subseteq\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{\mathcal{X}_{c}}
, we have

\Delta S_{tot}(q_{1,v})\geq\Delta S_{tot}(q_{1})\geq 0.

(48)

Subtracting
\Delta S_{tot}(p_{1})
from Eq. (
48
), we see that for any distribution
p_{1}\in\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{\mathcal{X}_{c}}
, MMC
v
forms a lower bound on the true MMC

\text{MMC}_{v}(p_{1})=\Delta S_{tot}(p_{1})-\Delta S_{tot}(q_{1,v})\leq\Delta S_{tot}(p_{1})-\Delta S_{tot}(q_{1})=\text{MMC}(p_{1}).

(49)

In general, the variational MMC
v
can be negative if
\Delta S_{tot}(p_{1})<\Delta S_{tot}(q_{1,v})
. However, this is only an issue if
p_{1}\notin\mathcal{V}
. If
p_{1}=p_{1,v}\in\mathcal{V}
, then by definition of
q_{1,v}
as the variational optimum for
\mathcal{V}
we know
\Delta S_{tot}(p_{1,v})\geq\Delta S_{tot}(q_{1,v})
. This shows us that MMC
v
provides a positive lower bound on the total MMC of a variational input:

0\leq\text{MMC}_{v}(p_{1,v})\leq\text{MMC}(p_{1,v}).

(50)

Wolpert and Kolchinsky show that while the optimized
q_{1}
is not necessarily unique, it will have a unique MMC with any other distribution
p_{1}
[
73
]
. It is important to note that while this uniqueness is broken by the variational bound as discussed in App.
I
, the lower bound Eq. (
50
) continues to apply even for a non-unique variational optimum
q_{1,v}
.

While without more specific physical details the true distribution of MMC
v
cannot be determined, App.
I
further discusses exact and SGD linear regression as illustrative examples.

VIII
Discussion

In this work, we studied the thermodynamic costs of single-parameter linear regression represented on floating-point registers, comparing the exact analytic solution with stochastic gradient descent.
For this simple model, the Landauer cost is dominated by the size of the data, and the main contributor to this cost is the precision
p
, which determines the number of bits included in the mantissa.
From this we see that mantissa bits are thermodynamically expensive, exponent bits are thermodynamically cheap. Fig.
1(b)
shows that adding exponent bits only increases the range of representable values while leaving the entropy relatively unchanged once overflows and underflows are avoided.
This implies that for many data sets, additional exponent bits will barely affect thermodynamic costs.
Additionally, these results provide a theoretical foundation for empirical results on machine learning model compression via entropy coding
[
30
,
31
]
.

The low thermodynamic cost of exponent bits also aligns with other innovations in machine learning systems, like the use of the bfloat16 number format
[
74
]
which has the same number of exponent bits as FP32, but only 7 mantissa bits as opposed to 23.
Another important innovation is model quantization
[
75
,
76
,
77
]
where deep learning models can be trained with very limited numerical precision, especially when using techniques like quantization aware training
[
78
,
79
]
.

Additionally, the signal-to-noise ratio of the data contributes significantly to the Landauer cost, as seen from Eq. (
22
) and in Figs.
3
and
4
where increasing the signal-to-noise ratio decreases the thermodynamic cost. This implies that less noisy data and more structured data may also yield lower fundamental energy costs.

We also derived scaling laws for the exact formula and SGD that demonstrate the trade-off between generalization error and the energy cost of training. When the demand for inference scales inversely with the generalization error and the price for inference is constant, the irreducible noise creates a threshold where training on more data will not increase the model’s accuracy enough to justify the associated energy costs.

Finally, we presented a method for lower bounding the mismatch cost entropy production for variational input distributions.
Future work will include studying specific thermodynamic models of bit implementations like CMOS
[
80
]
, such that an entropy flow to the environment
\Delta S_{env}
can be determined and the mismatch cost specified.

The natural extension of this work is to multi-parameter models, where connections to kernel ridge regression
via the Neural Tangent Kernel
[
81
,
82
,
83
]
suggest our analysis may generalize to certain regimes of MLP training. In these cases, the output entropy of the models may be more significant than in the single-parameter case.

Appendix B
Computing the exact entropy of quantized random variables

Here we compute the exact discrete entropy of quantized random variables, such as normally distributed random variables stored as floating point numbers.

Theorem B.1

(Entropy of a Clipped and Arbitrarily Midpoint Quantized Random Variable)
.

Let
X
be an absolutely continuous random variable with cumulative distribution function
F
. Assume there are
K
representable values in the quantization scheme and denote the set of these values as
\{u_{1},\dots,u_{K}\}
, where
u_{1}<\dots<u_{K}
. If
X_{Q}
is the resulting clipped and midpoint quantized representation of
X
, then the discrete entropy of
X_{Q}
is

\displaystyle H(X_{Q})

\displaystyle=-F\left(\frac{u_{1}+u_{2}}{2}\right)\log\left[F\left(\frac{u_{1}+u_{2}}{2}\right)\right]

\displaystyle-\sum_{i=2}^{K-1}\left[F\left(\frac{u_{i+1}+u_{i}}{2}\right)-F\left(\frac{u_{i}+u_{i-1}}{2}\right)\right]\log\left[F\left(\frac{u_{i+1}+u_{i}}{2}\right)-F\left(\frac{u_{i}+u_{i-1}}{2}\right)\right]

\displaystyle-\left[1-F\left(\frac{u_{K-1}+u_{K}}{2}\right)\right]\log\left[1-F\left(\frac{u_{K-1}+u_{K}}{2}\right)\right].

when
K\geq 3
. When
K=2
,
H(X_{Q})=-F\left(\frac{u_{1}+u_{2}}{2}\right)\log\left[F\left(\frac{u_{1}+u_{2}}{2}\right)\right]-\left[1-F\left(\frac{u_{1}+u_{2}}{2}\right)\right]\log\left[1-F\left(\frac{u_{1}+u_{2}}{2}\right)\right]
, and when
K=1
,
H(X_{Q})=0
.

Proof.

Since
X_{Q}
is a truncated and midpoint quantized representation of
X
, we have,

X_{Q}=\begin{cases}\sum\limits_{i=2}^{K-1}u_{i}\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{X\in\left[\frac{u_{i}+u_{i-1}}{2},\frac{u_{i+1}+u_{i}}{2}\right)\right\}}+u_{1}\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{X<\frac{u_{1}+u_{2}}{2}\right\}}+u_{K}\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{X\geq\frac{u_{K-1}+u_{K}}{2}\right\}}\text{ if }K\geq 3\\
u_{1}\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{X<\frac{u_{1}+u_{2}}{2}\right\}}+u_{2}\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{X\geq\frac{u_{1}+u_{2}}{2}\right\}}\text{ if }K=2\\
u_{1}\text{ if }K=1.\end{cases}

(51)

For
K=1
, we see that
P\{X_{Q}=u_{1}\}=1
so
H(X_{Q})=0
. For
K=2
,
P\{X_{Q}=u_{1}\}=F\left(\frac{u_{1}+u_{2}}{2}\right)=1-P\{X_{Q}=u_{2}\}
, so

H(X_{Q})=-F\left(\frac{u_{1}+u_{2}}{2}\right)\log\left[F\left(\frac{u_{1}+u_{2}}{2}\right)\right]-\left[1-F\left(\frac{u_{1}+u_{2}}{2}\right)\right]\log\left[1-F\left(\frac{u_{1}+u_{2}}{2}\right)\right].

(52)

Finally, for
K\geq 3
,

\displaystyle P\{X_{Q}=u_{i}\}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\int\limits_{\frac{u_{i}+u_{i-1}}{2}}^{\frac{u_{i+1}+u_{i}}{2}}f_{X}(x)dx\text{ if }i\in[2,K-1]\\
\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\frac{u_{1}+u_{2}}{2}}f_{X}(x)dx\text{ if }i=1\\
\int\limits_{\frac{u_{K-1}+u_{K}}{2}}^{\infty}f_{X}(x)dx\text{ if }i=K\\
\end{cases}

(53)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}F\left(\frac{u_{i+1}+u_{i}}{2}\right)-F\left(\frac{u_{i}+u_{i-1}}{2}\right)\text{ if }i\in[2,K-1]\\
F\left(\frac{u_{1}+u_{2}}{2}\right)\text{ if }i=1\\
1-F\left(\frac{u_{K-1}+u_{K}}{2}\right)\text{ if }i=K\end{cases}.

Inserting this into the formula for discrete entropy allows us to derive the following

\displaystyle H(X_{Q})

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i=1}^{K}P\{X_{Q}=u_{i}\}\log\left[P\{X_{Q}=u_{i}\}\right]

(54)

\displaystyle=-F\left(\frac{u_{1}+u_{2}}{2}\right)\log\left[F\left(\frac{u_{1}+u_{2}}{2}\right)\right]

\displaystyle-\sum_{i=2}^{K-1}\left[F\left(\frac{u_{i+1}+u_{i}}{2}\right)-F\left(\frac{u_{i}+u_{i-1}}{2}\right)\right]\log\left[F\left(\frac{u_{i+1}+u_{i}}{2}\right)-F\left(\frac{u_{i}+u_{i-1}}{2}\right)\right]

\displaystyle-\left[1-F\left(\frac{u_{K-1}+u_{K}}{2}\right)\right]\log\left[1-F\left(\frac{u_{K-1}+u_{K}}{2}\right)\right].

∎

Corollary B.1.1

(Entropy of a Gaussian Random Variable Quantized to a Floating-point Number)
.

Let
X\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\mu,\sigma_{x}^{2}\right)
. Let
X_{fp}
be the clipped and midpoint quantized floating-point representation of
X
with an
E
-bit exponent and a
(p-1)
-bit significand. The discrete entropy of
X_{fp}
is given by Theorem
B.1
where
K=2^{E+p}
,
F(x)=\frac{1}{2}\left[1+\mathrm{erf}\left(\frac{x-\mu}{\sqrt{2}\sigma_{x}}\right)\right]
,

u_{i}=\begin{cases}u^{\prime}_{i-2^{E+p-1}}\text{ if }i>2^{E+p-1}\\
-u^{\prime}_{2^{E+p-1}-(i-1)}\text{ otherwise},\end{cases}

and

u^{\prime}_{i}=2^{\left[\left\lfloor(i-1)2^{-(p-1)}\right\rfloor-\left(2^{E-1}-1\right)\right]}\left(1+\left[(i-1)2^{-(p-1)}\bmod 1\right]\right).

Proof.

F(x)=\frac{1}{2}\left[1+\mathrm{erf}\left(\frac{x-\mu}{\sqrt{2}\sigma_{x}}\right)\right]
is the well-known Gaussian cumulative distribution function. All that is left to apply Theorem
B.1
is to explicitly construct the ordered sequence of representable values
\{u_{1},u_{2},\dots,u_{K}\}
from the structure of the floating-point format given in Eq. (
14
). There are
2^{p-1}
distinct mantissa values for each exponent and
2^{E}
exponent values, giving
2^{E+p-1}
positive floating-point values in total. Including the negative values by symmetry, there are
K=2^{E+p}
representable values altogether.
The smallest value of the exponent
e_{\min}=-(2^{E-1}-1)
while the largest value
e_{\max}=2^{E-1}
.

We first enumerate the
2^{E+p-1}
positive floating-point values
u^{\prime}_{i}
in increasing order, indexed by
i=1,2,\ldots,2^{E+p-1}
. For each fixed exponent value, we exhaust all
2^{p-1}
mantissa values before incrementing the exponent.
Concretely, for index
i
, the exponent index is
\lfloor(i-1)/2^{p-1}\rfloor
, which starts at zero steps up by one exactly every
2^{p-1}
values of
i
. The mantissa index within that exponent block is
(i-1)\bmod 2^{p-1}
, which cycles through
0,1,\ldots,2^{p-1}-1
repeatedly. Translating the exponent index into the true exponent by subtracting
e_{\min}
, and the mantissa index into its fractional value by multiplying by
2^{-(p-1)}
, gives

u^{\prime}_{i}=2^{\left\lfloor(i-1)2^{-(p-1)}\right\rfloor-(2^{E-1}-1)}\Bigl(1+\left[(i-1)2^{-(p-1)}\bmod 1\right]\Bigr).

The full sequence
u_{1}<u_{2}<\cdots<u_{K}
must enumerate the negative and positive floating-point values in increasing order. Since the negative floating-point values are the mirror image of the positive ones, the most negative value corresponds to
-u^{\prime}_{2^{E+p-1}}
and the least negative to
-u^{\prime}_{1}
. Therefore, for
i\leq 2^{E+p-1}
, we set

u_{i}=-u^{\prime}_{2^{E+p-1}-(i-1)},

which enumerates the negative values in increasing order. For
i>2^{E+p-1}
, we set

u_{i}=u^{\prime}_{i-2^{E+p-1}},

which enumerates the positive values in increasing order. With these
u_{i}
and
K=2^{E+p}
, Theorem
B.1
can be used to compute
H(X_{fp})
, completing the proof.
∎

Corollary B.1.2

(Joint Entropy of Two Jointly Gaussian Random Variables Quantized to Floating-point Numbers)
.

Let
w\in\mathbb{R}
,
X\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,\sigma_{x}^{2}\right)
and
\xi\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,\sigma_{\xi}^{2}\right)
where
X
and
\xi
are independent. Define
Y=wX+\xi
. Let
X_{fp}
and
Y_{fp}
be the floating-point representation of
X
and
Y
respectively, where both representations have
E
exponent bits and
(p-1)
significand bits. Let
K=2^{E+p}
and denote the set of representable values as
\{u_{1},u_{2},\dots,u_{K}\}
as given in Corollary
B.1.1
. When
K\geq 2
, the joint discrete entropy of
X_{fp}
and
Y_{fp}
is

H(X_{fp},Y_{fp})=H(X_{fp})-\sum_{i=1}^{K}\sum_{j=1}^{K}F_{XY}\left(a_{j},b_{j};a_{i},b_{i}\right)\log\left[\frac{F_{XY}\left(a_{j},b_{j};a_{i},b_{i}\right)}{F_{X}(b_{i})-F_{X}(a_{i})}\right].

where
H(X_{fp})
is given from Corollary
B.1.1
,
F_{XY}(a,b;c,d)\triangleq\int_{a}^{b}\int_{c}^{d}f_{XY}(x,y)dxdy
where
f_{XY}
is the bivariate Gaussian density function corresponding to Eq. (
1
),
F_{X}(x)
is the univariate Gaussian cumulative density function, and

a_{i}=\begin{cases}-\infty&i=1\\
\frac{u_{i}+u_{i-1}}{2}&2\leq i\leq K\end{cases},\qquad b_{i}=\begin{cases}\frac{u_{i+1}+u_{i}}{2}&1\leq i\leq K-1\\
\infty&i=K\end{cases}.

When
K=1
,
H(X_{fp},Y_{fp})=0
.

Proof.

H(X_{fp},Y_{fp})=H(X_{fp})+H(Y_{fp}|X_{fp}).

(55)

H(X_{fp})
is given by Theorem
B.1
, so all that is left is to compute
H(Y_{fp}|X_{fp})
.
Let
\{u_{1},\dots,u_{K}\}
denote the
2^{E+p}
representable values of the floating-point representation as specified in Corollary
B.1.1
. We know that

\begin{bmatrix}X\\
Y\end{bmatrix}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,\begin{bmatrix}\sigma_{x}^{2}&w\sigma_{x}^{2}\\
w\sigma_{x}^{2}&w^{2}\sigma_{x}^{2}+\sigma_{\xi}^{2}\end{bmatrix}\right).

(56)

Let
F_{XY}(a,b;c,d)\triangleq\int_{a}^{b}\int_{c}^{d}f_{XY}(x,y)dxdy
where
f_{XY}
is the bivariate Gaussian density function corresponding to Eq. (
1
). Notice that,

P\{Y\in[a,b)|X\in[c,d)\}=\frac{P\{Y\in[a,b),X\in[c,d)\}}{P\{X\in[c,d)\}}=\frac{F_{XY}(a,b;c,d)}{F_{X}(d)-F_{X}(c)}.\\

(57)

When
K=1
,
P\{X_{fp}=u_{1}\}=P\{Y_{fp}=u_{1}\}=1
so
H(Y_{fp}|X_{fp})=0
.
When
K\geq 2
, let
a_{i}
and
b_{i}
denote the left and right boundaries of the quantization bin for
u_{i}
:

a_{i}\triangleq\begin{cases}-\infty&i=1\\
\frac{u_{i}+u_{i-1}}{2}&2\leq i\leq K\end{cases},\qquad b_{i}\triangleq\begin{cases}\frac{u_{i+1}+u_{i}}{2}&1\leq i\leq K-1\\
\infty&i=K\end{cases}.

(58)

Then,

\displaystyle H(X_{fp})+H(Y_{fp}|X_{fp})=H(X_{fp})-\sum_{i=1}^{K}\sum_{j=1}^{K}P\{Y_{fp}=u_{j},X_{fp}=u_{i}\}\log\left[P\{Y_{fp}=u_{j}|X_{fp}=u_{i}\}\right]

(59)

\displaystyle=H(X_{fp})-\sum_{i=1}^{K}\sum_{j=1}^{K}P\left\{Y\in\left[a_{j},b_{j}\right),X\in\left[a_{i},b_{i}\right)\right\}\log\left[\frac{P\left\{Y\in\left[a_{j},b_{j}\right),X\in\left[a_{i},b_{i}\right)\right\}}{P\left\{X\in\left[a_{i},b_{i}\right)\right\}}\right]

\displaystyle=H(X_{fp})-\sum_{i=1}^{K}\sum_{j=1}^{K}F_{XY}\left(a_{j},b_{j};a_{i},b_{i}\right)\log\left[\frac{F_{XY}\left(a_{j},b_{j};a_{i},b_{i}\right)}{F_{X}(b_{i})-F_{X}(a_{i})}\right].

∎

Appendix C
Approximating the entropy of floating point numbers

Here we review the details of calculations used to approximate the entropy of floating point numbers.

Approximation 1 – Relating discrete and differential entropy for non-uniform bins

Let there be a quantization scheme with
K
representable values

\{u_{1},u_{2},\ldots,u_{K}\}
, where
u_{1}<u_{2}<\cdots<u_{K}
.
Let
\mathbf{X}\sim f_{\mathbf{X}}
, where
f_{\mathbf{X}}
is a differentiable probability density function with support on

\mathbb{U}^{d}\triangleq\left[-2^{e_{max}+1}+2^{e_{max}-p},\,\,2^{e_{max}+1}-2^{e_{max}-p}\right]^{d},

(60)

where we assume each component
X_{j}
is independently clipped and quantized.

Let
\Delta:\left[-2^{e_{max}+1}+2^{e_{max}-p},\,\,2^{e_{max}+1}-2^{e_{max}-p}\right]\to\mathbb{R}_{+}

encode the bin sizes of the quantization scheme for a single component, where

\displaystyle\Delta(x_{j})

\displaystyle\triangleq\sum_{i=2}^{K-1}\frac{u_{i+1}-u_{i-1}}{2}\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{x_{j}\in\Big[\frac{u_{i}+u_{i-1}}{2},\frac{u_{i+1}+u_{i}}{2}\Big)\right\}}+(u_{2}-u_{1})\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{x_{j}\in[-2^{e_{max}+1}+2^{e_{max}-p},\frac{u_{2}+u_{1}}{2}]\right\}}

\displaystyle+(u_{K}-u_{K-1})\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{x_{j}\in[\frac{u_{K}+u_{K-1}}{2},2^{e_{max}+1}-2^{e_{max}-p}]\right\}}.

Since each component is stored on its own floating-point register, the quantization cell for
\mathbf{X}_{Q}
is a
d
-dimensional rectangle with volume

\Delta(\mathbf{x})\triangleq\prod_{j=1}^{d}\Delta(x_{j}),\qquad\log\Delta(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\log\Delta(x_{j}).

(61)

Let
\mathbf{i}=(i_{1},\dots,i_{d})
index the
d
-dimensional quantization cells, where
i_{j}\in\{1,\dots,K\}
. Define the cell boundaries

a_{i}=\begin{cases}-2^{e_{max}+1}+2^{e_{max}-p}&i=1\\[2.0pt]
\frac{u_{i}+u_{i-1}}{2}&2\leq i\leq K\end{cases},\qquad b_{i}=\begin{cases}\frac{u_{i+1}+u_{i}}{2}&1\leq i\leq K-1\\[2.0pt]
2^{e_{max}+1}-2^{e_{max}-p}&i=K.\end{cases}

(62)

For each
d
-dimensional index
\mathbf{i}
, define the quantization cell

B_{\mathbf{i}}\triangleq\prod_{j=1}^{d}[a_{i_{j}},b_{i_{j}}],

(63)

and let

p_{\mathbf{i}}\triangleq\mathbb{P}\{\mathbf{X}\in B_{\mathbf{i}}\}=\mathbb{P}\{\mathbf{X}_{Q}=\mathbf{u}_{\mathbf{i}}\},

(64)

where
\mathbf{u}_{\mathbf{i}}
is the
d
-dimensional representable value corresponding to the quantization cell
B_{\mathbf{i}}
.

Then the discrete entropy of
\mathbf{X}_{Q}
is

H(\mathbf{X}_{Q})=-\sum_{\mathbf{i}}p_{\mathbf{i}}\log p_{\mathbf{i}}.

(65)

To relate this discrete entropy to the differential entropy of
\mathbf{X}
, define the piecewise-uniform density

g(\mathbf{x})\triangleq\sum_{\mathbf{i}}\frac{p_{\mathbf{i}}}{|B_{\mathbf{i}}|}\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{\mathbf{x}\in B_{\mathbf{i}}\right\}},

(66)

where
|B_{\mathbf{i}}|=\Delta(\mathbf{x})
for
\mathbf{x}\in B_{\mathbf{i}}
is the volume of each rectangular cell
B_{\mathbf{i}}
. By construction,
g
is constant on each cell and integrates to one across all cells.

Following
[
29
]
, we can derive an exact identity relating the discrete and differential entropies. By the definition of Kullback-Leibler divergence,

\displaystyle D(f_{\mathbf{X}}\|g)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{U}^{d}}f_{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{x})\log\frac{f_{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{x})}{g(\mathbf{x})}\,d\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{U}^{d}}f_{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{x})\log f_{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{x})\,d\mathbf{x}-\int_{\mathbb{U}^{d}}f_{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{x})\log g(\mathbf{x})\,d\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle=-h(\mathbf{X})-\int_{\mathbb{U}^{d}}f_{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{x})\log g(\mathbf{x})\,d\mathbf{x}.

(67)

Since
g(\mathbf{x})=p_{\mathbf{i}}/|B_{\mathbf{i}}|
for
\mathbf{x}\in B_{\mathbf{i}}
,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{U}^{d}}f_{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{x})\log g(\mathbf{x})\,d\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{i}}\int_{B_{\mathbf{i}}}f_{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{x})\log\left(\frac{p_{\mathbf{i}}}{|B_{\mathbf{i}}|}\right)d\mathbf{x}

(68)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{i}}\bigg(\int_{B_{\mathbf{i}}}f_{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{x})d\mathbf{x}\bigg)\log\left(\frac{p_{\mathbf{i}}}{|B_{\mathbf{i}}|}\right)=\sum_{\mathbf{i}}p_{\mathbf{i}}\log\left(\frac{p_{\mathbf{i}}}{|B_{\mathbf{i}}|}\right)

(69)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{i}}p_{\mathbf{i}}\log p_{\mathbf{i}}-\sum_{\mathbf{i}}p_{\mathbf{i}}\log|B_{\mathbf{i}}|.

(70)

Substituting Eq. (
70
) into Eq. (
67
) yields

\displaystyle D(f_{\mathbf{X}}\|g)

\displaystyle=-h(\mathbf{X})-\sum_{\mathbf{i}}p_{\mathbf{i}}\log p_{\mathbf{i}}+\sum_{\mathbf{i}}p_{\mathbf{i}}\log|B_{\mathbf{i}}|

\displaystyle=-h(\mathbf{X})+H(\mathbf{X}_{Q})+\mathbb{E}[\log\Delta(\mathbf{X})],

(71)

or equivalently,

H(\mathbf{X}_{Q})=h(\mathbf{X})-\mathbb{E}[\log\Delta(\mathbf{X})]+D(f_{\mathbf{X}}\|g).

(72)

Approximation 1 corresponds to neglecting the nonnegative correction term
D(f_{\mathbf{X}}\|g)
:

H(\mathbf{X}_{Q})\approx h(\mathbf{X})-\mathbb{E}[\log\Delta(\mathbf{X})].

(73)

[
29
]
shows rigorously that the correction term
D(f_{\mathbf{X}}\|g)
becomes large if
f_{\mathbf{X}}
varies noticeably within each quantization cell, and vanishes if the function is uniform across each cell. Here, we can understand this at a basic level by noting that
g(\mathbf{x})
is equal to the mean value of
f_{\mathbf{X}}
within a given bin
B_{\mathbf{i}}
. By the multivariate mean value theorem,
g(\mathbf{x})=f_{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{\tilde{x}_{\mathbf{i}}})
for some
\mathbf{\tilde{x}}_{\mathbf{i}}\in B_{\mathbf{i}}
. Taylor expanding
f_{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{x})
within cell
B_{\mathbf{i}}
to first order around
\mathbf{\tilde{x}}_{\mathbf{i}}
we see:

f_{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{x})\approx g(\mathbf{x})+\nabla f_{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{\tilde{x}}_{\mathbf{i}})\cdot(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{\tilde{x}}_{\mathbf{i}}).

(74)

Since
D(f_{\mathbf{X}}\|g)\approx 0
when
f_{\mathbf{X}}\approx g
, a cell can contribute significantly to the error if the size of the cell and the gradient of
f_{\mathbf{X}}
are simultaneously large, since
(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{\tilde{x}}_{\mathbf{i}})
is bounded by the size of the cell. However, when the cell diameter is small in regions where the density varies rapidly, or if the cell diameter is large where the density varies only slowly, the discrepancy between
f_{\mathbf{X}}
and its cell average
g(\mathbf{x})
remains small.

Finally, we can note that if each component is quantized independently, then

\mathbb{E}[\log\Delta(\mathbf{X})]=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}_{X_{j}}[\log\Delta(X_{j})],

(75)

so that Approximation 1 takes the form

\tilde{H}(\mathbf{X}_{Q})\triangleq h(\mathbf{X})-\sum_{j=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}_{X_{j}}[\log\Delta(X_{j})].

(76)

Approximation 2 – Smoothing the bin size function and extending its domain

In the multivariate case, with
\Delta_{s}(x)=\frac{|x|}{\sqrt{2}}\cdot 2^{1-p}
from Eq. (
17
), we can define

\Delta_{s}(\mathbf{x})=\prod^{d}_{j=1}\Delta_{s}(x_{j}).

(77)

Again,
\mathbb{U}^{d}=[-2^{e_{max}+1}+2^{e_{max}-p},\,\,2^{e_{max}+1}-2^{e_{max}-p}]^{d}
, where
e_{max}=2^{E-1}
. Assuming that the tails of
f_{\mathbf{X}}
are vanishingly small,

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\mathbb{U}^{d}}f_{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{x})\,d\mathbf{x}\approx 0,

(78)

we extend the domain from
\mathbb{U}^{d}
to
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. As noted in the main text, this extension requires that the omitted tail integral
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\mathbb{U}^{d}}f_{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{x})|\log[f_{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{x})\Delta_{s}(\mathbf{x})]|\,d\mathbf{x}
is near zero, not merely that the tail mass is small. For the Gaussian and Student’s
t
distributions used in this paper, the tail decay dominates the linear growth of
\Delta_{s}
, so this tail integral is negligible when the granular region
\mathbb{U}
is sufficiently large (i.e.,
E
is not too small). With this we can say from Eq. (
76
)
\tilde{H}(\mathbf{X}_{Q})\approx\tilde{H}_{s}(\mathbf{X}_{Q})
, where

\tilde{H}_{s}(\mathbf{X}_{Q})=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f_{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{x})\log\left[f_{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{x})\prod_{j=1}^{d}\Delta_{s}(x_{j})\right]d\mathbf{x}.

(79)

Define the smooth-bin approximation restricted to
\mathbb{U}^{d}
as

\tilde{H}_{s,\mathbb{U}}(\mathbf{X}_{fp})\;\triangleq\;-\int_{\mathbb{U}^{d}}f_{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{x})\,\log\left[f_{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{x})\prod_{j=1}^{d}\Delta_{s}(x_{j})\right]d\mathbf{x}.

(80)

Theorem
C.1
bounds the smoothing error by the
d/2
term plus the explicit contribution from the bins adjacent to zero.

Define the special-bin set

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{0}

\displaystyle\triangleq\left\{x\in\mathbb{U}:\ |x|<2^{e_{\min}}(1+2^{-p})\right\}

(81)

where
\mathcal{B}_{0}
is the union of the two bins adjacent to
0
. When
x\in\mathcal{B}_{0}
,
\Delta(x)=\Delta_{0}=2^{e_{\min}}(1+2^{-p})
. Define

\varepsilon_{0}(\mathbf{X})\triangleq\sum_{j=1}^{d}\int_{\mathcal{B}_{0}}f_{X_{j}}(x)\left|\log\left[\frac{\Delta_{s}(x)}{\Delta_{0}}\right]\right|dx.

(82)

Theorem C.1

(Error bound for smoothing the bin-size function)
.

Let
\mathbf{X}\sim f
be a
d
-dimensional random vector with the probability density
f
supported on
\mathbb{U}^{d}
, and let
X_{j}
denote its
j
th component. Let
\tilde{H}(\mathbf{X}_{fp})
be the approximation from App.
C
using the true midpoint bin-size function
\Delta
, and let
\tilde{H}_{s,\mathbb{U}}(\mathbf{X}_{fp})
be the corresponding approximation on
\mathbb{U}^{d}
using
\Delta_{s}
. Then

\left|\tilde{H}(\mathbf{X}_{fp})-\tilde{H}_{s,\mathbb{U}}(\mathbf{X}_{fp})\right|\leq\frac{d}{2}+\varepsilon_{0}(\mathbf{X}),

(83)

where
\varepsilon_{0}(\mathbf{X})
is finite and defined in Eq. (
82
).

Proof.

There are 3 types of bins for which we will bound the error: bins on the interior of an exponent block, bins on the outer edges, and bins on the boundary between exponent blocks (regions for which a single exponent value applies). The bound can be shown by considering the ratio between bin sizes, case by case.

Interior Bins

For interior bins strictly within each exponent block
e
, by Eq. (
16
),

\Delta(x_{j})=2^{e-(p-1)}\quad\text{for }2^{e}\leq|x_{j}|<2^{e+1},

(84)

and within this range
\Delta_{s}(x_{j})=\frac{|x_{j}|}{\sqrt{2}}\cdot 2^{1-p}
satisfies

\displaystyle\frac{2^{e}}{\sqrt{2}}2^{1-p}\leq\Delta_{s}(x_{j})\leq\frac{2^{e+1}}{\sqrt{2}}2^{1-p}

(85)

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\leq\frac{\Delta_{s}(x_{j})}{\Delta(x_{j})}<\sqrt{2}.

Outer clipping bins

For the outer clipping bins, by symmetry it suffices to analyze
[\frac{u_{K}+u_{K-1}}{2},2^{e_{\max}+1}-2^{e_{\max}-p}]
which is the bin on positive side of the real line. When
p\geq 2
, we know that
u_{K}=2^{e_{max}+1}(1-2^{-p})
, and
u_{K-1}=2^{e_{max}+1}(1-2^{1-p})
, so the outer clipping bin size is

\Delta(x_{j})=2^{e_{max}+1}-2^{e_{max}-p}-\frac{u_{K}+u_{K-1}}{2}=2^{e_{max}}\left(2^{1-p}\right)=2^{e_{max}-p+1}

(86)

Now, we have

\displaystyle\frac{(u_{K}+u_{K-1})}{2\sqrt{2}}2^{1-p}\leq\Delta_{s}(x_{j})\leq\frac{2^{e_{max}+1}-2^{e_{max}-p}}{\sqrt{2}}2^{1-p}

(87)

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle\frac{2^{e_{max}}(1-2^{-1-p}-2^{-p})}{2^{e_{max}-p}\sqrt{2}}2^{1-p}\leq\frac{\Delta_{s}(x_{j})}{\Delta(x_{j})}\leq\frac{2^{e_{max}}(1-2^{-1-p})}{2^{e_{max}-p}\sqrt{2}}2^{1-p}

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle\frac{2(1-2^{-1-p}-2^{-p})}{\sqrt{2}}\leq\frac{\Delta_{s}(x_{j})}{\Delta(x_{j})}\leq\frac{2(1-2^{-1-p})}{\sqrt{2}}

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle\sqrt{2}(1-2^{-1-p}-2^{-p})\leq\frac{\Delta_{s}(x_{j})}{\Delta(x_{j})}\leq\sqrt{2}(1-2^{-1-p}).

When
p=1
,
u_{K}=2^{e_{max}}
and
u_{K-1}=2^{e_{max}-1}
. The true bin width is

\Delta(x_{j})=2^{e_{max}+1}-2^{e_{max}-1}-\frac{1}{2}(2^{e_{max}}+2^{e_{max}-1})=3\cdot 2^{e_{max}-2}.

(88)

Using the same bounding technique, we have

\displaystyle\frac{2^{e_{max}}+2^{e_{max}-1}}{2\sqrt{2}}\leq\Delta_{s}(x_{j})\leq\frac{2^{e_{max}+1}-2^{e_{max}-1}}{\sqrt{2}}

(89)

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle\frac{3\cdot 2^{e_{max}-2}}{3\cdot 2^{e_{max}-2}\sqrt{2}}\leq\frac{\Delta_{s}(x_{j})}{\Delta(x_{j})}\leq\frac{3\cdot 2^{e_{max}-1}}{3\cdot 2^{e_{max}-2}\sqrt{2}}

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\leq\frac{\Delta_{s}(x_{j})}{\Delta(x_{j})}\leq\frac{2}{\sqrt{2}}.

For the outer clipping bins, we see that for all
p\geq 1
,
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\leq\frac{\Delta_{s}(x_{j})}{\Delta(x_{j})}\leq\sqrt{2}
.

Exponent boundary bins

At exponent boundaries, let the last representable value of the
e
-th exponent block be
u_{A}=2^{e+1}-2^{e-(p-1)}
. The first representable value of the
e+1
-th exponent block be
u_{B}=2^{e+1}
, and the second representable value of the
e+1
-th exponent block be
u_{C}=2^{e+1}+2^{e+1-(p-1)}
. The left boundary of the midpoint-quantization bin for
u_{B}
is
m_{L}=\frac{u_{A}+u_{B}}{2}=2^{e+1}(1-2^{-p-1})
while the right boundary is
m_{R}=\frac{u_{B}+u_{C}}{2}=2^{e+1}(1+2^{-p})
. When
x_{j}\in[m_{L},m_{R})
, the bin width is

\Delta(x_{j})=m_{R}-m_{L}=3\cdot 2^{e-p},

(90)

and the ratio
\Delta_{s}(x_{j})/\Delta(x_{j})
is bounded by

\displaystyle\frac{2^{e+1}(1-2^{-p-1})}{\sqrt{2}}2^{1-p}\leq\Delta_{s}(x_{j})\leq\frac{2^{e+1}(1+2^{-p})}{\sqrt{2}}2^{1-p}

(91)

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle\frac{2^{e+1}(1-2^{-p-1})}{3\cdot 2^{e-p}\sqrt{2}}2^{1-p}\leq\frac{\Delta_{s}(x_{j})}{\Delta(x_{j})}\leq\frac{2^{e+1}(1+2^{-p})}{3\cdot 2^{e-p}\sqrt{2}}2^{1-p}

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle\frac{2\sqrt{2}(1-2^{-p-1})}{3}\leq\frac{\Delta_{s}(x_{j})}{\Delta(x_{j})}\leq\frac{2\sqrt{2}(1+2^{-p})}{3}.

When
p=1
the lower bound is equal to
1/\sqrt{2}
and the upper bound is equal to
\sqrt{2}
. The lower bound is monotonically increasing in
p
while the upper bound is monotonically decreasing in
p
, and both converge to
2\sqrt{2}/3
as
p\rightarrow\infty
, which is between
1/\sqrt{2}
and
\sqrt{2}
. This means both bounds lie within
[1/\sqrt{2},\,\sqrt{2}]
, so for within exponent block bins and exponent boundary bins,

-\frac{1}{2}\leq\log\left[\frac{\Delta_{s}(x_{j})}{\Delta(x_{j})}\right]<\frac{1}{2}.

(92)

Bound on entropy difference from bin ratios

Hence, for every
x_{j}\in\mathbb{U}\setminus\mathcal{B}_{0}
,

\left|\log\left[\frac{\Delta_{s}(x_{j})}{\Delta(x_{j})}\right]\right|\leq\frac{1}{2}.

(93)

The remaining points are exactly the special bins collected in
\mathcal{B}_{0}
, where we do not claim a uniform pointwise bound and instead keep their contribution explicitly:

\displaystyle\left|\tilde{H}(\mathbf{X}_{fp})-\tilde{H}_{s,\mathbb{U}}(\mathbf{X}_{fp})\right|=\Bigg|-\int_{\mathbb{U}^{d}}f_{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{x})\,\log\left[f_{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{x})\prod_{j=1}^{d}\Delta(x_{j})\right]d\mathbf{x}

(94)

\displaystyle+\int_{\mathbb{U}^{d}}f_{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{x})\,\log\left[f_{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{x})\prod_{j=1}^{d}\Delta_{s}(x_{j})\right]d\mathbf{x}\Bigg|

\displaystyle\left|\tilde{H}(\mathbf{X}_{fp})-\tilde{H}_{s,\mathbb{U}}(\mathbf{X}_{fp})\right|=\left|\sum_{j=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}_{X_{j}}\left[\log\left[\frac{\Delta_{s}(x)}{\Delta(x)}\right]\right]\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{d}\int_{\mathbb{U}\setminus\mathcal{B}_{0}}f_{X_{j}}(x)\left|\log\left[\frac{\Delta_{s}(x)}{\Delta(x)}\right]\right|dx+\varepsilon_{0}(\mathbf{X})\leq\frac{d}{2}+\varepsilon_{0}(\mathbf{X}).

The point
x=0
has measure zero, and
\varepsilon_{0}(\mathbf{X})
is finite since each marginal density is continuous on
\mathbb{U}
while
|\log|x||
is locally integrable near
0
.
∎

Not only is
\varepsilon_{0}(\mathbf{X})
finite, as shown in Theorem
C.1
, but for the probability distributions that this paper studies, Corollary
C.1.1
shows that
\varepsilon_{0}(\mathbf{X})
is negligibly small.

Corollary C.1.1

(Bound on
\varepsilon_{0}

.

Under the hypotheses of Theorem

Approximation 3 –
\mathbb{E}[\log[|x|/\sqrt{2}]]

Fig. 8
:
Simulating approximation 3:
The dependence of the entropy of a normally distributed floating-point number on its standard deviation
\sigma
and mean
\mu
. Dashed lines show Approximation 3 from Eq. (
20
), while solid lines show the exact entropy from Corollary
B.1.1
.

We can show that Eq. (
18
) approximates Eq. (
20
) in two cases. First, for a Gaussian distribution where its mean is significantly greater than its standard deviation,
\mu\gg\sigma
. Second, for a distribution
X\sim f_{X}(x,\epsilon)
that approximates a Dirac delta function as
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
.

Non-zero mean Gaussian:
First, for a non-zero mean Gaussian, let
X=\mu+\epsilon
where
\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
. Without loss of generality, assume
\mu>0
(the final result depends only on
|\mu|
).
\log\left[\left|X/\sqrt{2}\right|\right]
becomes

\displaystyle\log\left|\frac{X}{\sqrt{2}}\right|=\log\left|\frac{\mu+\epsilon}{\sqrt{2}}\right|=\log\left|\frac{\mu}{\sqrt{2}}\left(1+\frac{\epsilon}{\mu}\right)\right|=\log\left|\frac{\mu}{\sqrt{2}}\right|+\log\left|1+\frac{\epsilon}{\mu}\right|.

(103)

To control the second term, we can split according to the events

A=\left\{\left|\frac{\epsilon}{\mu}\right|\leq\frac{1}{2}\right\}=\left\{X\in\left[\frac{\mu}{2},\frac{3\mu}{2}\right]\right\},\qquad A^{c}=\left\{|\frac{\epsilon}{\mu}|>\frac{1}{2}\right\}.

(104)

Events in
A^{c}
are exponentially unlikely when
\mu\gg\sigma
, since
P(A^{c})
is equal to

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(A^{c})

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{-\mu/2}\frac{\exp[-t^{2}/2\sigma^{2}]}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}dt+\int_{\mu/2}^{\infty}\frac{\exp[-t^{2}/2\sigma^{2}]}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\,dt=2\int_{\mu/2}^{\infty}\frac{\exp[-t^{2}/2\sigma^{2}]}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\,dt.

(105)

\displaystyle=2\int_{\mu/2}^{\infty}\frac{\sigma}{t}\cdot\frac{t}{\sigma^{2}}\cdot\frac{\exp[-t^{2}/2\sigma^{2}]}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\,dt\leq\frac{4\sigma}{\mu}\int_{\mu/2}^{\infty}\frac{t}{\sigma^{2}}\cdot\frac{\exp[-t^{2}/2\sigma^{2}]}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\,dt

(106)

\displaystyle=\frac{4\sigma}{\mu\sqrt{2\pi}}\left[-\exp\left[-\frac{t^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right]\right]_{\mu/2}^{\infty}=\frac{4\sigma}{\mu\sqrt{2\pi}}\,e^{-\mu^{2}/(8\sigma^{2})}

(107)

meaning that for
\mu\gg\sigma
,
P(A^{c})
is small.

By the law of total expectation,

\mathbb{E}\left[\log\left|1+\frac{\epsilon}{\mu}\right|\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\log\left|1+\frac{\epsilon}{\mu}\right|\,\middle|\,A\right]P(A)+\mathbb{E}\left[\log\left|1+\frac{\epsilon}{\mu}\right|\,\middle|\,A^{c}\right]P(A^{c}).

(108)

For the first term, on
A
we have
1+\epsilon/\mu>0
, so the Mercator series converges:

\log\left[1+\frac{\epsilon}{\mu}\right]=\frac{1}{\ln[2]}\left(\frac{\epsilon}{\mu}-\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2\mu^{2}}+O\left(\frac{\epsilon^{3}}{\mu^{3}}\right)\right).

(109)

Since
1+\epsilon/\mu>0
on
A
, the absolute value can be dropped. On
A=\{|\epsilon/\mu|\leq 1/2\}
, the Mercator remainder satisfies
|\ln[1+x]-x+x^{2}/2|\leq\frac{2}{3}|x|^{3}
for
|x|\leq 1/2
. Therefore,

\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\ln\left(1+\frac{\epsilon}{\mu}\right)-\frac{\epsilon}{\mu}+\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2\mu^{2}}\right|\Bigg|A\right]\leq\frac{2}{3}\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[|\epsilon|^{3}|A\right]}{|\mu|^{3}}=O\left(\frac{\sigma^{3}}{\mu^{3}}\right).

(110)

\mathbb{E}[\epsilon\mid A]=0
since the distribution of
\epsilon
is symmetric over the interval
\left[-\mu/2,\,\mu/2\right]
and by the monotone convergence theorem as
\mu/\sigma\to\infty
,
\mathbb{E}[\epsilon^{2}\mid A]\to\sigma^{2}
. Thus,

\mathbb{E}\left[\log\left|1+\frac{\epsilon}{\mu}\right|\,\middle|\,A\right]P(A)=\frac{P(A)}{\ln[2]}\left(-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2\mu^{2}}+O\left(\frac{\sigma^{3}}{|\mu|^{3}}\right)\right)\approx-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2\mu^{2}\ln[2]}.

(111)

For the term pertaining to
A^{c}
, Lemma
C.3
shows that
\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\log\left|1+\epsilon/\mu\right|\right|\,\middle|\,A^{c}\right]\mathbb{P}(A^{c})\to 0
as
\mu/\sigma\to\infty
, which means taking the expectation of Eq. (
103
) gives

\mathbb{E}\left[\log\left|\frac{X}{\sqrt{2}}\right|\right]\approx\log\left|\frac{\mu}{\sqrt{2}}\right|-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2\mu^{2}\ln[2]}.

(112)

Since
\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2\mu^{2}\ln[2]}\rightarrow 0
as
\mu/\sigma\rightarrow\infty
, we see that the convolution integral can be approximated for
\mu\gg\sigma
as

\displaystyle G_{\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^{2})}(\mu)=\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}\frac{\exp(-\frac{(x-\mu)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}})}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\,\log\bigg[\frac{|x|}{\sqrt{2}}\bigg]dx\approx\log\left[\frac{|\mu|}{\sqrt{2}}\right].

(113)

as shown in Fig.
8
, which shows that the value of the convolution integral is insensitive to the value of
\sigma
when
\mu\gg\sigma
.

This means for a non-zero mean Gaussian random variable, for
\mu\gg\sigma
,
\tilde{H}_{s}(X_{fp})\approx\tilde{H}_{s}^{\mu}(X_{fp})
, with

\tilde{H}^{\mu}_{s}(X_{fp})\triangleq\frac{1}{2}\log[2\pi e\sigma^{2}]+(p-1)-\log\left[|\mu|/\sqrt{2}\right].

(114)

Lemma C.2

(Bound on the Mercator Series Remainder)
.

For all
|x|\leq\frac{1}{2}
,

\left|\ln[1+x]-x+\frac{x^{2}}{2}\right|\leq\frac{2}{3}\,|x|^{3}.

(115)

Proof.

The Mercator series gives

\ln\left[1+x\right]\;=\;\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{k+1}\,x^{k}}{k},\qquad|x|<1,

(116)

so the second-order remainder is

R_{2}(x)\triangleq\ln\left[1+x\right]-x+\frac{x^{2}}{2}\;=\;\sum_{k=3}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{k+1}\,x^{k}}{k}.

(117)

By the triangle inequality and using the inequality
1/k\leq 1/3
for every
k\geq 3
,

|R_{2}(x)|\;\leq\;\sum_{k=3}^{\infty}\frac{|x|^{k}}{k}\;\leq\;\frac{1}{3}\sum_{k=3}^{\infty}|x|^{k}\;=\;\frac{1}{3}\cdot\frac{|x|^{3}}{1-|x|},

(118)

where the last equality is the geometric series

\sum_{k=3}^{\infty}|x|^{k}=|x|^{3}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}|x|^{j}=|x|^{3}/(1-|x|)
,
valid for
|x|<1
.
Since
|x|\leq\frac{1}{2}
implies
1-|x|\geq\frac{1}{2}
, we obtain

|R_{2}(x)|\;\leq\;\frac{|x|^{3}}{3\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)}\;=\;\frac{2}{3}\,|x|^{3}.

(119)

∎

Lemma C.3
.

Let
\epsilon\sim N(0,\sigma^{2})
,
\mu\neq 0
, and
A=\{|\epsilon/\mu|\leq\frac{1}{2}\}
.
Then,

\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\ln\left|1+\frac{\epsilon}{\mu}\right|\right|\,\,\Bigr|\,A^{c}\right]P(A^{c})\leq\,\left(\frac{C_{1}\sigma}{|\mu|}+\frac{C_{2}|\mu|}{\sigma}\right)\,e^{-|\mu|^{2}/8\sigma^{2}}

(120)

for some
C_{1},C_{2}>0
.
In particular, the bound decays faster than any polynomial in
|\mu|/\sigma
.

Proof.

Write
Z=\epsilon/\sigma\sim N(0,1)
, so that
\epsilon/\mu=\sigma Z/\mu

(taking
\mu>0
without loss of generality; the case
\mu<0
is symmetric).
Set
\delta\triangleq 1+\epsilon/\mu=1+\sigma Z/\mu
and split

A^{c}=A_{1}^{c}\cup A_{2}^{c},\qquad A_{1}^{c}\triangleq\left\{\left|\frac{\epsilon}{\mu}\right|>\frac{1}{2}\right\}\cap\left\{\left|\delta\right|\geq\frac{1}{2}\right\},\qquad A_{2}^{c}\triangleq\left\{\left|\frac{\epsilon}{\mu}\right|>\frac{1}{2}\right\}\cap\left\{\left|\delta\right|<\frac{1}{2}\right\}.

(121)

Contribution of
A_{1}^{c}
.
For
t\geq\frac{1}{2}
we have
|\ln t|\leq\ln 2+t

(since
-\ln t\leq\ln 2
when
t\geq\frac{1}{2}
,
and
\ln t\leq t
when
t>0
). Hence on
A_{1}^{c}
,

|\ln{\left|\delta\right|}|\;\leq\;\ln 2+|\delta|\;\leq\;\ln 2+1+|\frac{\epsilon}{\mu}|

(122)

almost surely.
Therefore

\displaystyle\left|\ln|\delta|\right|\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{A_{1}^{c}\right\}}\;\leq\;\left(\ln 2+1+|\frac{\epsilon}{\mu}|\right)\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{A_{1}^{c}\right\}}

(123)

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\ln\left[|\delta|\right]\right|\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{A_{1}^{c}\right\}}\right]\;\leq\;\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\ln 2+1+|\frac{\epsilon}{\mu}|\right)\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{A_{1}^{c}\right\}}\right]

(124)

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\ln\left[|\delta|\right]\right|\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{A_{1}^{c}\right\}}\right]\;\leq\;(1+\ln 2)\,P(A_{1}^{c})+\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\frac{\sigma Z}{\mu}\right|\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{A_{1}^{c}\right\}}\right]

(125)

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\ln\left[|\delta|\right]\right|\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{A_{1}^{c}\right\}}\right]\;\leq\;(1+\ln 2)\,P(A^{c})\;+\;\frac{\sigma}{\mu}\mathbb{E}\left[|Z|\,\mathbf{1}_{|Z|>\mu/2\sigma}\right],

(126)

since
A_{1}^{c}\subset A^{c}\implies P(A_{1}^{c})\leq P(A^{c})
and
\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{A_{1}^{c}\right\}}\leq\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{A^{c}\right\}}
almost surely.
From Eq. (
107
), the Gaussian tail bound gives

P(A^{c})\leq\frac{4\sigma}{\mu\sqrt{2\pi}}\,e^{-\mu^{2}/(8\sigma^{2})}
,
and the truncated first moment evaluates to

\mathbb{E}\bigl[|Z|\,\mathbf{1}_{|Z|>a}\bigr]=\frac{2}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{a}^{\infty}ze^{-\frac{z^{2}}{2}}dz=\frac{2}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\,e^{-a^{2}/2},\qquad a=\frac{\mu}{2\sigma}.

(127)

Since

\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\ln\left|\delta\right|\right|\,|\,A_{1}^{c}\right]P(A_{1}^{c})=\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\ln\left|\delta\right|\right|\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{A_{1}^{c}\right\}}\right]\leq\left(\ln 2+\frac{3}{2}\right)\frac{4\sigma}{\mu\sqrt{2\pi}}\,e^{-\mu^{2}/(8\sigma^{2})},

(128)

the entire
A_{1}^{c}
contribution is at most

\frac{C_{1}\sigma}{\mu}\,e^{-\mu^{2}/(8\sigma^{2})}
for some constant
C_{1}
.

Contribution of
A_{2}^{c}
.
On the event
\{|\delta|<\frac{1}{2}\}
we have

\epsilon/\mu\in(-\frac{3}{2},-\frac{1}{2})
,
i.e.
Z\in(-\frac{3\mu}{2\sigma},-\frac{\mu}{2\sigma})
.
The density of
\delta
at a point
t
is

\frac{\mu}{\sigma}f_{Z}\left(\frac{\mu}{\sigma}(t-1)\right)
,
where

is the standard normal density.
For
\left|t\right|<\frac{1}{2}
we have

, giving the
uniform density bound

\frac{\mu}{\sigma}f_{Z}\left(\frac{\mu}{\sigma}(t-1)\right)=\frac{\mu}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-\frac{\mu^{2}(t-1)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}\leq\frac{\mu}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\,e^{-\frac{\mu^{2}}{8\sigma^{2}}}.

(129)

Since
\left|\delta\right|<\frac{1}{2}
almost surely implies
\left|\frac{\epsilon}{\mu}\right|>\frac{1}{2}
almost surely, we have
A_{2}^{c}=\{\left|\delta\right|<\frac{1}{2}\}
and

\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\ln\left|\delta\right|\right|\,\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{|\delta|<1/2\right\}}\right]=\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}\frac{\mu}{\sigma}f_{Z}\left(\frac{\mu}{\sigma}(t-1)\right)\left|\ln\left|t\right|\right|dt\leq\frac{\mu}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\,e^{-\frac{\mu^{2}}{8\sigma^{2}}}\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}|\ln\left|t\right|\,|\,dt.

(130)

The integral evaluates to

-2\int_{0}^{1/2}\ln\left[t\right]\,dt=-2\left[t\ln t-t\right]\Bigr|_{0}^{1/2}=\ln 2+1
,
so the
A_{2}^{c}
contribution is at most

\frac{C_{2}\mu}{\sigma}\,e^{-\frac{\mu^{2}}{8\sigma^{2}}}
for some constant
C_{2}
.

Combining both contributions.
Adding both contributions yields

\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\ln\left|1+\frac{\epsilon}{\mu}\right|\right|\,\mathbf{1}_{A^{c}}\right]\;\leq\;\left(\frac{C_{1}\sigma}{\mu}+\frac{C_{2}\mu}{\sigma}\right)e^{-\frac{\mu^{2}}{8\sigma^{2}}}

(131)

which decays faster than any polynomial in
|\mu|/\sigma
.
∎

Theorem D.1

(Approximating the entropy of a mean-zero univariate gaussian)
.

The discrete entropy of a zero-mean normally distributed continuous random variable
X\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{x}^{2})
as represented on a floating-point number, with the approximations specified by Eq. (
15
) and (
17
) applied, is a constant specified by the floating point number’s precision,

.

\tilde{H}_{s}(X_{fp})=(p-1)+\frac{1}{2}\log[2\pi e]+1+\frac{\gamma_{e}}{2\ln[2]}

(134)

Proof.

Let
X\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{x}^{2})
.

\displaystyle\tilde{H}_{s}(X_{fp})

\displaystyle\triangleq h(X)-\mathbb{E}\left[\log[\Delta_{s}(x)]\right]

(135)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\log[2\pi e\sigma^{2}_{x}]-\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}\frac{\exp(-\frac{x^{2}}{2\sigma_{x}^{2}})}{\sigma_{x}\sqrt{2\pi}}\log[\Delta_{s}(x)]\,dx

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\log[2\pi e\sigma^{2}_{x}]-\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}\frac{\exp(-\frac{x^{2}}{2\sigma_{x}^{2}})}{\sigma_{x}\sqrt{2\pi}}(\log[|x|/\sqrt{2}]-(p-1))\,dx

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\log[2\pi e\sigma^{2}_{x}]+(p-1)-2\int^{\infty}_{0}\frac{\exp(-\frac{x^{2}}{2\sigma_{x}^{2}})}{\sigma_{x}\sqrt{2\pi}}\log[x/\sqrt{2}]\,dx

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\log[2\pi e\sigma^{2}_{x}]+(p-1)+\bigg(1+\frac{\gamma_{e}}{2\ln[2]}-\frac{1}{2}\log[\sigma^{2}_{x}]\bigg)

\displaystyle=(p-1)+\frac{1}{2}\log[2\pi e]+1+\frac{\gamma_{e}}{2\ln[2]}

\displaystyle=p+\frac{1}{2}\log[2\pi e]+\frac{\gamma_{e}}{2\ln[2]}.

Theorem D.2
.

Let
X\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{x}^{2})
,
\Xi\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{\xi}^{2})
,
w\in\mathbb{R}
, and
Y=wX+\Xi
. The approximate joint discrete entropy of
(X_{fp},Y_{fp})
is,

\tilde{H}_{s}(X_{fp},Y_{fp})=2\tilde{H}^{0}_{s}(p)-\frac{1}{2}\log\bigg[1+\frac{\sigma_{x}^{2}w^{2}}{\sigma_{\xi}^{2}}\bigg].

Proof.

\displaystyle\tilde{H}_{s}(X_{fp},Y_{fp})

\displaystyle\triangleq h(X,Y)-\iint_{-\infty}^{\infty}f_{XY}(x,y)(\log[\Delta_{s}(x)]+\log[\Delta_{s}(y)])dxdy

(136)

\displaystyle=h

\displaystyle(X)+h(\Xi)-\iint_{-\infty}^{\infty}f_{XY}(x,y)\log[\Delta_{s}(x)]dxdy-\iint_{-\infty}^{\infty}f_{XY}(x,y)\log[\Delta_{s}(y)]dxdy

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}

\displaystyle\log[2\pi e\sigma_{x}^{2}]+\frac{1}{2}\log[2\pi e\sigma_{\xi}^{2}]-\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f_{X}(x)\log[\Delta_{s}(x)]dx-\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f_{Y}(y)\log[\Delta_{s}(y)]dy.

where
h(X,Y)=h(X)+h(\Xi)
is because
X
is independent from
\Xi
and
(X,\Xi)\mapsto(X,Y)
is an invertible linear transformation with Jacobian 1. The expressions follow directly from the differential entropy of a normal distribution
[
50
]
. In the last line,
f_{X}(x)
and
f_{Y}(y)
are the marginal distributions over
f_{XY}(x,y)
,

f_{X}(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f_{XY}(x,y)\,dy\text{, }f_{Y}(y)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f_{XY}(x,y)\,dx,

(137)

where from Eq. (
1
)

f_{X}(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma_{x}^{2}}}\exp\left(-\frac{x^{2}}{2\sigma_{x}^{2}}\right)\text{, }f_{Y}(y)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi(\sigma_{\xi}^{2}+w^{2}\sigma_{x}^{2})}}\exp\left(-\frac{y^{2}}{2(\sigma_{\xi}^{2}+w^{2}\sigma_{x}^{2})}\right).

(138)

Applying the same steps as in Theorem
D.1
to evaluate the bin terms, we obtain

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f_{X}(x)\,\log\left[\Delta_{s}(x)\right]\,dx\approx-\left(p+\frac{\gamma_{e}}{2\ln 2}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\log\sigma_{x}^{2},

(139)

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f_{Y}(y)\,\log\left[\Delta_{s}(y)\right]\,dy\approx-\left(p+\frac{\gamma_{e}}{2\ln 2}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\log\left[\sigma_{\xi}^{2}+w^{2}\sigma_{x}^{2}\right].

(140)

Plugging these back into the original expression, we obtain

\displaystyle\tilde{H}_{s}(X_{fp},Y_{fp})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\log[2\pi e\sigma_{x}^{2}]+\frac{1}{2}\log[2\pi e\sigma_{\xi}^{2}]+p+\frac{\gamma_{e}}{2\ln 2}-\frac{1}{2}\log\sigma_{x}^{2}

(141)

\displaystyle+p+\frac{\gamma_{e}}{2\ln[2]}-\frac{1}{2}\log[\sigma^{2}_{\xi}+\sigma_{x}^{2}w^{2}].

Using the definition
H^{0}_{s}(p)
from definition Eq. (

D.1
, we can obtain a clean final expression,

\tilde{H}_{s}(X_{fp},Y_{fp})=2\tilde{H}^{0}_{s}(p)-\frac{1}{2}\log\bigg[1+\frac{\sigma_{x}^{2}w^{2}}{\sigma_{\xi}^{2}}\bigg]

(142)

Appendix F
Landauer Cost of Averaging and Summing

An even simpler prediction algorithm than linear regression is the average. Let the input to this algorithm be
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle iid}}{{\sim}}f
, where
\mathbb{E}\left[X_{i}\right]=\mu
and
\mathrm{var}\left(X_{i}\right)=\sigma^{2}
. The differential entropy of the input is
h(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})=nh(X_{1})
.

If
f
is not Gaussian, for sufficiently large
n
, central limit theorem can be invoked to show that
\frac{1}{n}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\Rightarrow\mathcal{N}\left(\mu,\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n}\right)
as
n\rightarrow\infty
. So the differential entropy of the final distribution is
h(\frac{1}{n}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}X_{i})\approx\frac{1}{2}\log\left[2\pi e\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n}\right]
. When
f_{X_{i}}
is Gaussian, this is true even for small
n
.

For the case when
\forall i,f_{X_{i}}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,1\right)
and the random variables are encoded in floating-point numbers with precision
p
, the discrete entropy difference is

\displaystyle\Delta H=n\tilde{H}^{0}_{s}(p)-\tilde{H}^{0}_{s}(p)=(n-1)\tilde{H}^{0}_{s}(p)\approx(n-1)(p+2.46)\text{ bits,}

(143)

where
\tilde{H}^{0}_{s}(p)
is derived in Section
III
. Notice that Eq. (
143
) is dominated by the number of inputs. As stressed in Section
III
,
\tilde{H}^{0}_{s}(p)
does not depend on the variance of the random variable. This means that for zero-mean Gaussian random variables, the Landauer cost of averaging
\frac{1}{n}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}
and summing
\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}
is equivalent, since
\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,n\sigma^{2}\right)
.

One general way to model such a time dependence is via a continuous time Markov chain (CTMC) based on transition rates between states of the joint computational system

While running an algorithm, the computational system evolves according to

\frac{d}{dt}p(x_{c},t)=\sum_{x^{\prime}_{c}\in\mathcal{X}_{c}}W_{x_{c};x_{c}^{\prime}}(t)p(x_{c}^{\prime},t).

(166)

For these dynamics,

(167)

\Delta S_{env}=-k_{B}\ln[2]\int^{t_{f}}_{0}\sum_{x_{c},x_{c}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}_{c}}W_{x_{c},x_{c}^{\prime}}(t)p(x_{c}^{\prime},t)\log\bigg[\frac{W_{x_{c}^{\prime},x_{c}}(t)}{W_{x_{c},x_{c}^{\prime}}(t)}\bigg]dt,

(168)

(169)

If the transition rates

Variational MMC and the Island Decomposition

The requirements to obtain the minimum entropy distribution
q_{1}
that returns a unique MMC are specified in
[
73
]
.
[
73
]
points out that uniqueness of optimal distributions can only be verified for ‘islands’ of the conditional distribution defined by the algorithm.
We can describe an algorithm (possibly stochastic) as implementing a conditional distribution
\pi(x_{c}|x_{c}^{\prime})
that maps between computational states
x_{c},x_{c}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}_{c}
. The islands of the algorithm are specified by the following relation for
x_{c},x^{\prime}_{c}\in\mathcal{X}_{c}
:

x_{c}\sim x_{c}^{\prime}\Leftrightarrow\exists x_{f}\in\mathcal{X}_{c}:\pi(x_{f}|x_{c}^{\prime})>0,\pi(x_{f}|x_{c})>0,

(171)

meaning there is a finite probability of
x_{c}
and
x_{c}^{\prime}
transitioning to the same state
x_{f}
. An
island
of
\pi(x_{c}|x_{c}^{\prime})
is a connected subset of
\mathcal{X}_{c}
given by the transitive closure of Eq. (
171
). The set of islands of
\pi
partition
\mathcal{X}_{c}
, where the set of islands is denoted
L(\pi)
.

We can consider the distribution over an island
c\in L(\pi)
, given by
p^{c}(x_{c})=\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{x_{c}\in c\right\}}p(x_{c})/p(c)
. If optimized over the probability simplex specific to the island
c
is
\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{c}
, the minimizer
q^{c}(x)
will be unique
[
73
]
. The total distribution optimized over
\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{\mathcal{X}_{c}}
can be decomposed in terms of islands and the probability of their individual occupation
q_{1}(c)
,

q_{1}(x_{c})=\sum_{c\in L(\pi)}q_{1}(c)q_{1}^{c}(x_{c}).

(172)

The factors
q(c)
do not contribute to the KL divergences in Eq. (
44
), meaning they do not contribute to the MMC and ensuring the MMC is unique for the optimal distribution
q_{1}
.

For a deterministic algorithm,
\pi
will be valued 0 or 1 for all inputs. In this case, the islands of the algorithm will be specified by the set of inputs that correspond to a single output state. As we have considered them, both exact linear regression and SGD are deterministic algorithms. In the exact case, the final
\hat{w}_{Q}
follows deterministically from the data
\mathcal{D}_{Q}
, and given that for SGD
\hat{w}_{Q}
has a deterministic initialization, the final
\hat{w}_{Q}
also follows deterministically from the batches
\{\mathcal{B}_{Q,1},...,\mathcal{B}_{Q,\tau}\}
. Each island of the algorithms corresponds to all possible
\mathcal{D}_{Q}
or
\{\mathcal{B}_{Q,1},...,\mathcal{B}_{Q,\tau}\}
that have the same output
\hat{w}_{Q}
. For both the exact regression and SGD, the islands of input states are complex but disjoint subsets of the input data, which correspond to specific values of
\hat{W}
, rendering the island decomposition difficult to consider in this case.

The variational approach breaks the island decomposition by preventing
q_{1}^{c}(x_{c})
from being individually optimized, and by preventing a description of the input state as specified by Eq. (
172
). With a variational
q_{1,v}
that breaks the island decomposition, we cannot guarantee it to be unique. However, this lack of uniqueness does not prevent MMC
v
from providing a lower bound on the true MMC: one can perform a bounded optimization to find
q_{1,v}
, which may or may not be unique, the difference MMC
{}_{v}=\Delta S_{tot}(p_{1,v})-\Delta S_{tot}(q_{1,v})
will retain the property
0\leq
MMC
{}_{v}\leq
MMC for variational distributions
p_{1,v}\in\mathcal{V}
.
