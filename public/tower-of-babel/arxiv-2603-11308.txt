Heavy-Tailed Principle Component Analysis
Mario Sayde † , Christopher Khater, Jihad Fahs † , Ibrahim Abou-Faycal †

Abstract
Principal Component Analysis (PCA) is a cornerstone of dimensionality reduction, yet its classical formulation relies critically on second-order moments and is therefore fragile in the presence of heavy-tailed data and impulsive noise. While numerous robust PCA variants have been proposed, most either assume finite variance, rely on sparsity-driven decompositions, or address robustness through surrogate loss functions without a unified treatment of infinite-variance models. In this paper, we study PCA for high-dimensional data generated according to a superstatistical dependent model of the form 𝐗 = 𝐀 𝟏 𝟐 ​ 𝐆 \bf{X}=A^{\frac{1}{2}}{\bf G} , where A A is a positive random scalar and 𝐆 {\bf G} is a Gaussian vector. This framework captures a wide class of heavy-tailed distributions, including multivariate-t and sub-Gaussian α \alpha -stable laws. We formulate PCA under a logarithmic loss, which remains well defined even when moments do not exist. Our main theoretical result shows that, under this loss, the principal components of the heavy-tailed observations coincide with those obtained by applying standard PCA to the covariance matrix of the underlying Gaussian generator. Building on this insight, we propose robust estimators for this covariance matrix directly from heavy-tailed data and compare them with the empirical covariance and Tyler’s scatter estimator. Extensive experiments, including background denoising tasks, demonstrate that the proposed approach reliably recovers principal directions and significantly outperforms classical PCA in the presence of heavy-tailed and impulsive noise, while remaining competitive under Gaussian noise.

We formulate a PCA problem where we assume that the observed high-dimensional data is due to a generator matrix and a low-dimensional set of features. Our goal is to reduce the dimensionality of the observed data matrix by only retaining the low-dimensional set of features. To account for a potential infinite-variance model of the data distribution, we impose a logarithmic constraint, one that we try to minimize by finding the “best” low-dimensional vector of features under a linear transformation of the data matrix. Except for the work of
[
41
]
that implemented a Maximum-Likelihood (ML) estimator for a low-rank Cauchy matrix under a logarithmic loss, this work represents, up to our knowledge, the first generic treatment of the PCA problem for potentially heavy-tailed, infinite variance distributions under a logarithmic loss function. Our contributions are threefold:

•

We consider correlated data, potentially heavy-tailed with infinite variance, that are subordinated to a Gaussian vector
{\bf X}=A^{\frac{1}{2}}{\bf G}

•

We propose and test alternative methods to estimate the covariance matrix

•

We provide experimental results for two different applications on background denoising, showing the robustness of the proposed heavy-tailed PCA compared to the standard PCA, and its enhanced suppression of heavy-tailed impulsive noise in addition to remaining adequate in the presence of standard Gaussian noise. The experimental results can be reproduced by using the code available on

III
Problem Formulation

Let
\mathsf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}
be the data matrix, where
d
is the dimension of the features and
n
represent the number of samples. In the “standard”
\text{L}^{2}
-PCA, we try to find an
m
(
<d
) dimensional vector space of new features by minimizing the cost function:

C(\mathsf{W},\mathsf{V})=||\mathsf{X}-\mathsf{W}\mathsf{V}||_{2}^{2},

(1)

where
\mathsf{W}_{d\times m}
is the generator matrix such that
\mathsf{W}^{\top}\mathsf{W}=\mathsf{I}_{m}
where
\mathsf{I}_{m}
is the
m\times m
identity matrix, and
\mathsf{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
denotes the observation matrix of the low-dimensional set of features. According to the projection theorem
[
25
]
, the minimizer
{\mathsf{V}}
of (
1
) is
\mathsf{V}=\mathsf{W}^{\top}\mathsf{X}
for any given
\mathsf{W}
. The minimum is then achieved whenever
\mathsf{W}
is the matrix of eigenvectors corresponding to the largest
m
eigenvalues of the covariance matrix
\Sigma
of the underlying random vector whose
{\bf X}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, whose empirical value is calculated as
\Sigma=\frac{1}{n}\mathsf{X}\mathsf{X}^{\top}
.

We consider this dimensionality reduction problem where we assume that the vector
{\bf X}
where is heavy-tailed and, more specifically, superstatistical of the form
{\bf X}=A^{\frac{1}{2}}{\bf G}
.

Naturally, minimizing the cost function given by equation (
1
) renders the problem ill-defined due to the potential non-existence of the covariance matrix of
{\bf X}
. Instead, in this work, we propose an alternative logarithmic average cost function defined as:

\text{E}_{\bf X}[\ln(1+\|{\bf X}-\mathsf{W}{\bf V}\|_{2}^{2})]

(2)

Our goal is to find, under linear transformations, the “best” low-dimensional set of features
{\bf V}=\mathsf{M}^{\top}{\bf X}
and the best generator matrix
\mathsf{W}
, i.e. ones that minimize (
2
).
We conduct this analysis in two steps: Our first result states that the minimizer
{\bf V}
of (
2
) is
{\bf V}=\mathsf{W}^{\top}{\bf X}
for any given
\mathsf{W}
. Said differently, given a generator matrix
\mathsf{W}_{d\times m}
, we have

\mathsf{W}=\underset{\mathsf{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times m}}{\text{argmin}}\,\,\text{E}_{\bf X}[\ln(1+\|{\bf X}-\mathsf{W}\mathsf{M}^{\top}{\bf X}\|_{2}^{2})].

(3)

Moreover, we show that the best
m
dimensional vector of features
{\bf V}
that minimizes (
2
) is determined by choosing
\mathsf{W}
to be the matrix of the largest eigen vectors of the covariance matrix
\Sigma
of
{\bf G}
.

In summary, whenever the data are sampled from a superstatisitcal distribution that is potentially heavy-tailed (
\alpha
-stable, multivariate-t,
\cdots
), one has to estimate the covariance matrix
\Sigma
of the underlying Gaussian vector and not that of the data (the covariance matrix of the heavy-tailed data might not exist), and then apply the standard PCA on the estimated matrix. Our results include multiple estimators of
\Sigma
based on the observation of the data matrix
\mathsf{X}
.

These results and the corresponding proofs are detailed in the next sections.

Second method: The log-correlation

We define the log-correlation between two random variables as being the average of the product of their logarithm. More specifically, let
X
,
Y
be two random variables, their log-correlation is given by:

\ell_{XY}=\text{E}\left(\log{|X|}\log{|Y|}\right).

(17)

The log correlation is finite for a large class of distributions where the standard correlation does not exist (for example: class of power laws with characteristic exponent
\alpha<2
).

The second method is based on the observation that there is a one-to-one map (up to a sign) between the log-correlation
\ell_{G_{i}G_{j}}
and the correlation coefficient
\rho_{ij}
between
G_{i}
and
G_{j}
,
1\leq i,j\leq d
,
i\neq j
, elements of the
d
-dimensional Gaussian vector
{\bf G}\sim N(0,\Sigma)
. As such, we evaluate
\ell_{G_{i}G_{j}}
by estimating the log-correlation
\ell_{X_{i}X_{j}}
of the observed data, and use a precomputed lookup table that maps the value
\ell_{G_{i}G_{j}}
to the corresponding
\left|\rho_{ij}\right|
. In what follows, we assume WLoG that all
G_{i}
s have unit variance. In the general case, one can always estimate the variances of the marginals using the power estimator and normalize the entries accordingly.

To compute the empirical log-correlation coefficient, we first take the log of the data vector in absolute value, then we multiply the outcomes pairwise. We get

\displaystyle\log{|X_{i}|}\log{|X_{j}|}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\log(A^{\frac{1}{2}}|G_{i}|)\log(A^{\frac{1}{2}}|G_{j}|)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{2}\log{A}+\log{|G_{i}|}\right)\left(\frac{1}{2}\log{A}+\log{|G_{j}|}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}\left(\log{A}\right)^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\log{A}\log{|G_{i}|}+\frac{1}{2}\log{A}\log{|G_{j}|}+\log{|G_{i}|}\log{|G_{j}|},

(18)

Taking the expected value in equation (

), we get for all
1\leq i,j\leq d
,
i\neq j

\displaystyle\text{E}[\log{|X_{i}|}\log{|X_{j}|}]=\frac{1}{4}\text{E}[(\log{A})^{2}]+\text{E}[\log{A}]\text{E}[\log{|G_{i}|}]+\text{E}[\log{|G_{i}|}\log{|G_{j}|}],

where we used the fact that
A
and
{\bf G}
are independent and that
\text{E}[\log{|G_{i}|})]=\text{E}[\log{|G_{j}|}]
since
G_{i}\sim G_{j}
by assumption.
Rearranging the terms, we obtain

\displaystyle\text{E}\left[\log{|G_{i}|}\log{|G_{j}|}\right]=

\displaystyle\text{E}\left[\log{|X_{i}|}\log{|X_{j}|}\right]-\frac{1}{4}\text{E}\left[(\log A)^{2}\right]-\text{E}\left[\log{A}\right]E\left[\log{|G_{i}|}\right]

(19)

The RHS of equation (

\text{E}\left(\log{|X_{i}|}\log{|X_{j}|}\right)
can be calculated as an empirical mean of the pairwise product of the log of the marginal data. For a given
A
and for unit variance
G_{i}
s, the remaining two terms are independent of the data and of
\rho_{ij}
. Finally, a lookup table can be computed that gives the value of
\rho_{ij}
for each value of

We have calculated such a table by evaluating the logarithmic correlation
\text{E}\left[\log{|G_{1}|}\log{|G_{2}|}\right]

Third method: The law of large numbers

We make use of the following law of large numbers, to which we provide a proof of completeness.

Theorem 3
.

Let
{\bf G}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma)
be a Gaussian vector. Then

\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}G_{i}^{2}\underset{d\rightarrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}\frac{1}{d}\text{tr}(\Sigma).

Proof.

Let
d>0
and let
{\bf G}^{(d)}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma_{d})
. Then
\Sigma_{d}=\mathsf{U}\Omega\mathsf{U}^{\top}
, where
\Omega
is diagonal, and
\mathsf{U}
is unitary. Defining
{\bf Z}^{(d)}\triangleq\mathsf{U}^{\top}{\bf G}^{(d)}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Omega)
, we have

\|{\bf G}^{(d)}\|^{2}_{2}=\|{\bf Z}^{(d)}\|^{2}_{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}Z_{i}^{2}\sim\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_{i}Y_{i}^{2},

where
Y_{i}\sim N(0,1)
, for all
1\leq i\leq d
and where the
\lambda_{i}
s are the eigenvalues of
\Sigma
. Therefore,

\text{E}\left[\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}G_{i}^{2}\right]=\text{E}\left[\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_{i}Y_{i}^{2}\right]=\frac{1}{d}\mathrm{tr}(\Sigma).

We next show that
\text{Var}\left[\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}G_{i}^{2}\right]\rightarrow 0
, as
d\rightarrow\infty
, which concludes the proof. Let
\lambda_{\max}=\underset{1\leq i\leq d}{\max}\lambda_{i}
, hence

\mathrm{Var}\left(\frac{1}{d}\sum\lambda_{i}Y_{i}^{2}\right)=\frac{1}{d^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_{i}^{2}\mathrm{Var}(Y_{i}^{2})=\frac{2}{d^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_{i}^{2}\leq\frac{2}{d}\lambda_{\max}^{2}\underset{d\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}0,

∎

where we used the fact each
Y^{2}_{i}
is a chi-squared random variable with degrees of freedom equal to
1
and variance equal to
2
.
In this method, we first estimate the variance of each marginal Gaussian by using the power (scale) estimator of the marginal heavy-tailed. Summing the estimated variances gives an estimate of the trace of the matrix
\Sigma
that we denote by
\hat{t}

\sum_{i=1}^{d}\text{E}\left[G_{i}^{2}\right]=\hat{t}
. Whenever
d
is large enough, Theorem
3
gives

\hat{t}\approx\sum_{i=1}^{d}G_{i}^{2}.

(20)

Equation (
20

\hat{A}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{d}X_{i}^{2}}{\hat{t}},

(21)

where
d
is the number of dimensions and
X_{i}=A^{\frac{1}{2}}G_{i}
is the variable corresponding to the
i^{\text{th}}
dimension in
{\bf X}
.

Applying equation (

, we obtain an estimate for every

,
1\leq j\leq n
. Dividing every

results in Gaussian data to which the “standard PCA” algorithm can be applied. We note that this method necessitates sufficiently high dimension

in order for the law of large numbers to be accurate. We note that the error in the estimator
\hat{A}
given by equation (

VII
Conclusions

This work provides a principled and general treatment of PCA for high-dimensional data with potentially infinite variance. By adopting a logarithmic loss and modeling observations through a superstatistical Gaussian subordination framework, we show that robust dimensionality reduction can be achieved without resorting to ad hoc trimming, sparsity assumptions, or finite-moment conditions. Our theoretical analysis establishes that the principal components of heavy-tailed data are governed by the covariance structure of the latent Gaussian generator, thereby justifying the use of standard PCA once this covariance is consistently estimated. We propose practical estimators for this purpose and demonstrate, through simulations and background denoising experiments, their superior robustness and accuracy compared to both empirical covariance-based PCA and Tyler-type methods. Beyond PCA, the proposed framework opens new avenues for robust subspace learning, detection, and representation in modern data regimes characterized by heavy tails, dependence, and impulsive perturbations. Extensions to online settings, tensor-valued data, and connections with information-theoretic objectives such as entropy and divergence-based learning under heavy-tailed distributions represent some directions for future work.
