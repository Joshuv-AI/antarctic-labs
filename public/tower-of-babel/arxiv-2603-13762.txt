Learning the Optimal Composite Mediator:
Closed-Form Solution and Inference

Abstract




Abstract

Understanding how an exposure transmits its effect through high-dimensional
intermediaries is a central problem in observational research.
We study the problem of finding a composite mediator that maximises the
indirect effect of an exposure on an outcome in a linear structural equation model.
Although the objective is non-convex in the weight vector, a geometric
argument yields a closed-form global solution: the optimal weight bisects
the angle between two computable path vectors in a weighted inner product
space, recovered via two linear solves.
The resulting algorithm,
MaxIE
, runs at the same cost as ordinary
least squares—orders of magnitude lower than numerical optimisation—with
a dual formulation for settings where mediators outnumber observations.
The same path vectors yield a test for the global null that
no composite mediator exists, with
t(p-1)
in the classical and
t(n-2)
in the dual regime.
Power is characterised analytically as a function of the population path angle;
simulations confirm size control and the power characterisation.
Applied to a UK Biobank proteomics dataset (
n=38{,}383
,
p=2{,}916
),
the method rejects the global null (
p\text{-value}=6.4\times 10^{-9}
)
and identifies the optimal proteomic composite mediating age’s effect on dementia.

Keywords:

closed-form estimator;
composite mediator;
global test;
high-dimensional mediation;
indirect effect;
mediation index

1  Introduction

Many contemporary studies involve a treatment or exposure
\mathbf{A}
whose effect
on an outcome
\mathbf{Y}
is transmitted through a large set of candidate mediators

\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
, such as gene expression profiles or
protein abundances. Mediation analysis asks through which intermediaries this
effect operates—knowledge that can point to therapeutic targets, modifiable
risk factors, or mechanisms linking environmental exposures to disease.

The mediated effect of a treatment often reflects the collective activity of
many molecular features rather than any single one: genes act in regulatory
pathways, proteins function within signalling complexes, and metabolites
participate in coordinated biochemical reactions. A composite score

\mathbf{M}=\mathbf{X}\mathbf{w}
is therefore a natural target—one that aggregates the joint
mediation signal into a single interpretable mediator and captures structure
that single-feature analyses miss. A particularly pressing instance arises in
aging research, where large-scale proteomics platforms now enable construction
of
proteomic aging clocks
—composite protein scores that estimate
biological age and predict a wide range of diseases including Alzheimer’s,
heart failure, and cancer
(
Oh et al. 2023
;
Oh et al. 2025
;
Argentieri et al. 2024
;
Tian et al. 2025
;
Wang et al. 2025
)
.
These composites are increasingly used to track biological aging and stratify
disease risk, and are natural candidates for monitoring the effects of
anti-aging interventions
(
Oh et al. 2023
;
Oh et al. 2025
;
Argentieri et al. 2024
;
Tian et al. 2025
;
Wang et al. 2025
)
.
Yet existing clocks are constructed by regressing chronological age on proteins
alone, without reference to any disease outcome, maximising age tracking at the
expense of disease relevance.

The mediation framing resolves this tension directly: chronological age
remodels the proteome, and those proteomic changes are the mechanism
through which age increases disease risk
(
Oh et al. 2023
;
Tian et al. 2025
)
.
The composite mediator of interest is a protein score that

simultaneously
tracks biological aging and transmits that signal to
disease risk—yet this joint optimisation has not previously been pursued
as a direct problem, and neither single-criterion approach guarantees that
the resulting composite jointly optimises both properties.

The mediation objective.

We model mediation through three structural equations on centred
variables
(
Baron and Kenny 1986
)
:

\mathbf{M}=\alpha\,\mathbf{A}+\bm{\varepsilon}_{1},\qquad\mathbf{Y}=\gamma\,\mathbf{A}+\beta\,\mathbf{M}+\bm{\varepsilon}_{2},\qquad\mathbf{Y}=\tau\,\mathbf{A}+\bm{\varepsilon}_{3},

(1)

where
\alpha
,
\beta
,
\gamma
,
\tau
are path coefficients (treatment
\to
mediator,
direct, mediator
\to
outcome, total) and
\bm{\varepsilon}_{1},\bm{\varepsilon}_{2},\bm{\varepsilon}_{3}
are mean-zero errors.
We seek the weight vector
\mathbf{w}^{*}
that maximises the population indirect
effect for the composite mediator
\mathbf{M}=\mathbf{X}\mathbf{w}
:

\mathbf{w}^{*}_{+}\;=\;\operatorname{arg\,max}_{\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\;\alpha(\mathbf{w})\,\beta(\mathbf{w}),

(2)

estimated in practice by
\operatorname{arg\,max}_{\mathbf{w}}\hat{\alpha}(\mathbf{w})\hat{\beta}(\mathbf{w})
.
This finds the
concordant
composite mediator: the direction along which
treatment increases the mediator
and
the mediator increases the outcome
(both path coefficients share the same sign).
A complementary quantity is the
suppression
composite mediator,

\mathbf{w}^{*}_{-}\;=\;\operatorname{arg\,min}_{\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\;\alpha(\mathbf{w})\,\beta(\mathbf{w}),

(3)

which captures directions where treatment and outcome signals pull in
opposite directions (
\alpha(\mathbf{w})\beta(\mathbf{w})<0
).
Since flipping the sign of
\mathbf{w}
changes the signs of both
\alpha(\mathbf{w})

and
\beta(\mathbf{w})
simultaneously, their product is unchanged; we orient
each solution so that

\hat{\alpha}(\mathbf{w}^{*}_{\pm})\geq 0
, after which the sign of
\hat{\beta}
identifies
concordant (
\hat{\beta}>0
) versus suppression (
\hat{\beta}<0
) mediation.
Running both recovers a complete picture of how treatment effects are
transmitted through the mediator panel.
The remainder of the paper focuses on the concordant estimator
\mathbf{w}^{*}_{+}

without loss of generality: as shown in Section
2
,

\mathbf{w}^{*}_{-}
is obtained by negating the sufficient statistic
\mathbf{z}
, so all
theoretical results and algorithms apply to both problems by a trivial sign
change at no additional cost.

Related Work

Causal mediation analysis.

The modern causal framework for mediation, developed by
Robins & Greenland
(
Robins and Greenland 1992
)
and Pearl
(
Pearl 2001
)
and extended by
Imai et al.
(
Imai et al. 2010
)
and VanderWeele
(
VanderWeele 2015
)
, defines the natural indirect effect (NIE)
and natural direct effect (NDE) in terms of potential outcomes, with
identification resting on sequential ignorability and the cross-world
consistency assumption.
Under a linear structural equation model these reduce to the
path-coefficient product
\alpha\beta

(
Pearl 2001
)
, which is the
estimand we maximise.
Semiparametric efficiency bounds for the NIE are derived in
Tchetgen Tchetgen & Shpitser
(
Tchetgen Tchetgen and Shpitser 2012
)
; our contribution operates
within the parametric linear model and solves the previously unaddressed
problem of optimally choosing the composite mediator within this class.

High-dimensional composite mediator methods.

Principal component mediation analysis (PCMA,
(
Huang and Pan 2016
)
) and its sparse
variant SPCMA
(
Zhao et al. 2020
)
construct composite scores as principal components
of the treatment-residualised mediators. These methods are designed for
dimension reduction and screening: the PCA criterion maximises residual
variance in
\mathbf{X}
, which is independent of both
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{Y}
.
They do not optimise any mediation criterion and are therefore solving a
different problem from ours; a leading principal component need not align with
either mediation path.
The most direct predecessor to MaxIE is the Directions of Mediation
(DM,
(
Chen et al. 2018
)
), which finds a composite mediator by maximising the
joint LSEM log-likelihood via an iterative eigenvalue algorithm. Both DM and
MaxIE seek a single weight vector capturing the mediation pathway, but DM uses
a likelihood surrogate and requires iterative optimisation, whereas MaxIE
directly maximises
\hat{\alpha}(\mathbf{w})\hat{\beta}(\mathbf{w})
in closed form.
Moreover, DM provides no inferential framework for the global null that no
composite mediator of either sign exists (
H_{0}
: (
14
)); the cosine test derived here fills this gap.

Screening and selection methods.

A large literature screens individual mediators via penalisation:
HIMA
(
Zhang et al. 2016
)
, HDMA
(
Gao et al. 2019
)
, Zhou et al.
(
Zhou et al. 2020
)
, and
Pathway LASSO
(
Zhao and Luo 2022
)
recover active individual mediators, but
any composite score they produce is a byproduct of regularisation rather than
the result of directly optimising a composite indirect-effect criterion.

Our contributions.

(i) Closed-form optimal composite mediator, primal and dual.

Jointly optimising
\alpha(\mathbf{w})\beta(\mathbf{w})
as a direct problem has not
previously been attempted.
In the
primal
regime (
n>p
), the objective factorises into a
path-strength term and an alignment term; the optimal
\mathbf{w}
lies in the span
of two computable path vectors and is recovered by two linear solves plus a
bisector evaluation at cost
\mathcal{O}(np+p^{3}/3)

(Section
3
).
In the
dual
regime (
p\geq n
), the problem reduces to an

n\times n
kernel eigendecomposition via
\mathbf{K}_{Z}=\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{Z}^{\top}
, recovering
the optimal weight in the row space of
\mathbf{X}
at cost
\mathcal{O}(n^{2}p+n^{3})
—the
only algorithm for the
p\geq n
setting.
Both concordant and suppression solutions are returned by a single call at no
additional cost.

(ii) Global test for composite mediation, primal and dual.

We derive an asymptotic test for
H_{0}:\max_{\mathbf{w}}|\alpha(\mathbf{w})\beta(\mathbf{w})|=0

based on
\cos\hat{\varphi}
, the cosine of the angle between the two path vectors
(Section
4
).

Primal
:
T\sim t(p-1)
asymptotically as
n\to\infty
,
p
fixed;
exact for any
n>p
when the
\beta
-path is null, asymptotic when the

\alpha
-path is null.

Dual
:
T\sim t(n-2)
asymptotically as
p\to\infty
,
n
fixed;
exact for any
p>n
when the
\beta
-path is null, asymptotic when the

\alpha
-path is null.
Power is characterised analytically in both regimes; the dual has a saturation
ceiling
\pi_{\infty}(\varphi_{0},n)<1
.
Both tests are free by-products of the solver.

2  Problem Setup

We work within the linear structural equation model (
1
).
In the potential-outcomes language of Robins & Greenland
(
Robins and Greenland 1992
)
and Pearl
(
Pearl 2001
)
,
let
Y(a,m)
denote the potential outcome under treatment
a
and mediator
value
m
, and
M(a)
the potential mediator value under treatment
a
.
The natural indirect effect at treatment level
a
is

\mathrm{NIE}(a)=E[Y(a,M(1))-Y(a,M(0))]
.
Under the linear structural model (
1
), this reduces to

\mathrm{NIE}(a)=\alpha\beta
for any
a
, so the product
\alpha(\mathbf{w})\beta(\mathbf{w})

is the NIE for composite mediator
\mathbf{M}=\mathbf{X}\mathbf{w}
.
The following assumptions are maintained throughout.

A1

(Consistency)
The observed outcome satisfies

Y_{i}=Y_{i}(A_{i},M_{i}(A_{i}))
and the observed mediator satisfies

M_{i}=M_{i}(A_{i})
for each
i
.

A2

(Sequential ignorability)
(a)
\{Y(a,m),M(a^{\prime})\}\perp\!\!\!\perp A\mid\mathbf{X}
; (b)
Y(a,m)\perp\!\!\!\perp M\mid A,\mathbf{X}
,
for all
a,a^{\prime},m
in their respective supports.

A3

(Linear structural model)
The structural
equations (
1
) hold with mean-zero, finite-variance errors

\bm{\varepsilon}_{1},\bm{\varepsilon}_{2},\bm{\varepsilon}_{3}
independent of
(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{X})
.

A4

(Full rank)

\mathbf{X}
has full column rank and

\Sigma_{Z}=n^{-1}E[\mathbf{Z}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}]
is positive definite.

Under A1–A3,
\alpha(\mathbf{w})\beta(\mathbf{w})
equals the population NIE for composite
mediator
\mathbf{X}\mathbf{w}

(
Pearl 2001
;
VanderWeele 2015
)
.
Our contribution is tractable optimisation and inference within this parametric
model; relaxing A3 to semiparametric models is an important open direction
(see Section
9
).

Let
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
be the matrix of candidate mediators,

\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
the treatment vector, and
\mathbf{Y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
the
outcome vector, all centred, with
n\geq p
and
\mathbf{X}
full column rank.
We form a composite mediator
\mathbf{M}=\mathbf{X}\mathbf{w}
and seek

\mathbf{w}^{*}_{+}\;=\;\operatorname{arg\,max}_{\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\;h(\mathbf{w}),\qquad h(\mathbf{w})\;=\;\hat{\alpha}(\mathbf{w})\,\hat{\beta}(\mathbf{w}),

(4)

where
\hat{\alpha}(\mathbf{w})
and
\hat{\beta}(\mathbf{w})
are the OLS path coefficients
from the structural regressions (
1
).
Applying OLS with mediator
\mathbf{M}=\mathbf{X}\mathbf{w}
gives

\hat{\alpha}(\mathbf{w})\;=\;\frac{\mathbf{A}^{\top}(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{w})}{\|\mathbf{A}\|^{2}},\qquad\hat{\beta}(\mathbf{w})\;=\;\frac{(\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{w})^{\top}\mathbf{Y}}{\|\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{w}\|^{2}},

(5)

where
\mathbf{Q}_{A}=I_{n}-\mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^{\top}/\|\mathbf{A}\|^{2}
is the orthogonal projection
onto the complement of
\mathbf{A}
, and
\mathbf{Z}=\mathbf{Q}_{A}\mathbf{X}
is the matrix of

\mathbf{A}
-residualised mediators.
To reparametrise
h(\mathbf{w})
in terms of
\mathbf{w}
alone, we define three

p
-dimensional sufficient statistics:

\displaystyle\mathbf{a}

\displaystyle\;=\;\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{A},\qquad\mathbf{z}\;=\;\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{Y}-\frac{\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{Y}}{\|\mathbf{A}\|^{2}}\,\mathbf{a},

(6)

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}

\displaystyle\;=\;\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X}-\frac{\mathbf{a}\mathbf{a}^{\top}}{\|\mathbf{A}\|^{2}}\quad\text{(the residual Gram matrix).}

(7)

One can verify that
\mathbf{V}=\mathbf{Z}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}
and
\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{Z}^{\top}\mathbf{Y}
, so these
are computable via rank-1 updates to
\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X}
and scalar inner products,
without forming
\mathbf{Z}
explicitly.
In terms of
(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{z},\mathbf{V})
, the path coefficients become

\hat{\alpha}(\mathbf{w})=\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{a}/\|\mathbf{A}\|^{2}
and

\hat{\beta}(\mathbf{w})=\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{z}/(\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{V}\mathbf{w})
, and
h(\mathbf{w})
reparametrises as

h(\mathbf{w})\;=\;\frac{(\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{a})\,(\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{z})}{\|\mathbf{A}\|^{2}\,(\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{V}\mathbf{w})}.

(8)

Since
h(\mathbf{w})
is scale-invariant in
\mathbf{w}
, we optimise over directions.
By symmetry,
\min_{\mathbf{w}}h(\mathbf{w})=\max_{\mathbf{w}}h(\mathbf{w};{-}\mathbf{z})
,
where
h(\mathbf{w};{-}\mathbf{z})
denotes
h(\mathbf{w})
with
\mathbf{z}
replaced by
-\mathbf{z}
,
so both the concordant (
\mathbf{w}^{*}_{+}
) and suppression (
\mathbf{w}^{*}_{-}
) problems
are solved by the same algorithm.
Because the suppression direction uses
-\mathbf{q}=\mathbf{V}^{-1}(-\mathbf{z})
, already
computed, both solutions are returned by a single function call at no
additional computational cost (Section
3
).

3  Closed-Form Solution for
\hat{\alpha}(\mathbf{w})\hat{\beta}(\mathbf{w})

The objective
h(\mathbf{w})
in (
8
) is non-convex: the numerator is a
product of two linear forms in
\mathbf{w}
, so gradient-based methods can converge
to local optima. However,
h(\mathbf{w})
is scale-invariant—replacing
\mathbf{w}
by

\lambda\mathbf{w}
leaves it unchanged—so the search is over directions only.
Working in the appropriate inner product, this geometric view leads to a
closed-form global solution.

3.1 Geometric Reduction

Since
\mathbf{V}=\mathbf{Z}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}
is positive definite, it defines an inner product
on
\mathbb{R}^{p}
: for any
\mathbf{u}_{1},\mathbf{u}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
,

\langle\mathbf{u}_{1},\mathbf{u}_{2}\rangle_{\mathbf{V}}\;=\;\mathbf{u}_{1}^{\top}\mathbf{V}\,\mathbf{u}_{2},\qquad\|\mathbf{u}_{1}\|_{\mathbf{V}}\;=\;\sqrt{\mathbf{u}_{1}^{\top}\mathbf{V}\,\mathbf{u}_{1}},

with
\mathbf{u}_{1}\perp\mathbf{u}_{2}
meaning
\langle\mathbf{u}_{1},\mathbf{u}_{2}\rangle_{\mathbf{V}}=0
,
and the angle between
\mathbf{u}_{1}
and
\mathbf{u}_{2}
defined by

\cos\angle(\mathbf{u}_{1},\mathbf{u}_{2})=\langle\mathbf{u}_{1},\mathbf{u}_{2}\rangle_{\mathbf{V}}/(\|\mathbf{u}_{1}\|_{\mathbf{V}}\|\mathbf{u}_{2}\|_{\mathbf{V}})
.
All geometry in this section is with respect to this inner product.

Define the two path vectors

\mathbf{p}\;=\;\mathbf{V}^{-1}\mathbf{a},\qquad\mathbf{q}\;=\;\mathbf{V}^{-1}\mathbf{z}.

(9)

Because
\mathbf{p}=\mathbf{V}^{-1}\mathbf{a}
, inner products reduce to dot products with the
sufficient statistics:
\langle\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q}\rangle_{\mathbf{V}}=\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{q}
,

\|\mathbf{p}\|_{\mathbf{V}}=\sqrt{\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{p}}
,
\|\mathbf{q}\|_{\mathbf{V}}=\sqrt{\mathbf{z}^{\top}\mathbf{q}}
.
Since
\mathbf{V}\mathbf{p}=\mathbf{a}
and
\mathbf{V}\mathbf{q}=\mathbf{z}
, the numerator factors rewrite as

\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{a}\;=\;\|\mathbf{w}\|_{\mathbf{V}}\|\mathbf{p}\|_{\mathbf{V}}\cos\angle(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{p}),\qquad\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{z}\;=\;\|\mathbf{w}\|_{\mathbf{V}}\|\mathbf{q}\|_{\mathbf{V}}\cos\angle(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{q}),

while the denominator is
\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{V}\mathbf{w}=\|\mathbf{w}\|_{\mathbf{V}}^{2}
.
Substituting into (
8
), the
\|\mathbf{w}\|_{\mathbf{V}}^{2}
cancels and the
objective factorises as

h(\mathbf{w})\;=\;\underbrace{\frac{\|\mathbf{p}\|_{\mathbf{V}}\,\|\mathbf{q}\|_{\mathbf{V}}}{\|\mathbf{A}\|^{2}}}_{\text{path strength}}\;\cdot\;\underbrace{\cos\angle(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{p})\cdot\cos\angle(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{q})}_{\text{alignment}(\mathbf{w})}.

(10)

The path-strength factor depends only on the data, not on
\mathbf{w}
.
Maximising
h(\mathbf{w})
therefore reduces to maximising the alignment term:
given that the angle
\varphi=\angle(\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q})
between the two path
vectors is fixed, find the direction
\mathbf{w}
that maximises

\cos\angle(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{p})\cdot\cos\angle(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{q})
.

Proposition 1

(Bisector optimum)
.

Let
\varphi=\angle_{\mathbf{V}}(\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q})
be the angle between the two path vectors.
The alignment
\cos\angle_{\mathbf{V}}(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{p})\cdot\cos\angle_{\mathbf{V}}(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{q})
is maximised
by the direction
\mathbf{w}^{*}
that bisects the angle between
\mathbf{p}
and
\mathbf{q}
,
i.e.
\angle_{\mathbf{V}}(\mathbf{w}^{*},\mathbf{p})=\angle_{\mathbf{V}}(\mathbf{w}^{*},\mathbf{q})=\varphi/2
, giving

h(\mathbf{w}^{*})\;=\;\frac{\|\mathbf{p}\|_{\mathbf{V}}\,\|\mathbf{q}\|_{\mathbf{V}}}{\|\mathbf{A}\|^{2}}\;\cdot\;\frac{1+\cos\varphi}{2}.

(11)

The proof projects
h(\mathbf{w})
onto the two-dimensional span of
\mathbf{p}
and

\mathbf{q}
, reduces the problem to a one-dimensional trigonometric optimisation,
and solves it in closed form (see the Supplementary Material).
The bisector result has a clean geometric interpretation:
h(\mathbf{w})
rewards a weight vector simultaneously well-aligned
with both path directions
\mathbf{p}
(the treatment path) and
\mathbf{q}
(the outcome
path). Leaning all the way toward
\mathbf{p}
maximises alignment with the
treatment path but sacrifices alignment with the outcome path, and vice
versa; the bisector
\mathbf{w}^{*}
achieves the best symmetric compromise.
Formally, the direction that maximises
\hat{\alpha}(\mathbf{w})
alone is

\mathbf{w}\propto\mathbf{p}
, and the direction that maximises
\hat{\beta}(\mathbf{w})
alone
is
\mathbf{w}\propto\mathbf{q}
; at either single-path extreme the alignment is

\cos\varphi
, whereas the bisector achieves
(1+\cos\varphi)/2>\cos\varphi
for any
\varphi>0
. The gain
(1+\cos\varphi)/2-\cos\varphi=(1-\cos\varphi)/2
is largest when the two paths are nearly
orthogonal (
\varphi\approx\pi/2
), precisely the regime where single-path
methods fail most severely. This is not merely a computational shortcut:
the bisector result establishes that jointly optimising both mediation paths
is always strictly better than optimising either alone, with the improvement
increasing as the paths diverge.

3.2 Back-Transformation and Implementation

The optimal weight vector is

\mathbf{w}^{*}\;\propto\;\frac{\mathbf{p}}{\|\mathbf{p}\|_{\mathbf{V}}}\;+\;\frac{\mathbf{q}}{\|\mathbf{q}\|_{\mathbf{V}}},

(12)

the sum of the two unit path directions in the
\mathbf{V}
-metric.
Since
\mathbf{w}^{*}
depends only on the directions of
\mathbf{p}
and
\mathbf{q}
, it is invariant to rescaling of
\mathbf{A}
or
\mathbf{Y}
.
To obtain weights that are directly comparable across features with different units or variances—for example when ranking proteins by their contribution to the composite mediator—it suffices to column-standardise
\mathbf{X}
before running the solver; the resulting weights are equivalent to
w^{*}_{j}\cdot\hat{\sigma}_{j}
and measure the change in
M
per one-standard-deviation increase in each feature.
Computing
\mathbf{p}
and
\mathbf{q}
requires factorising
\mathbf{V}+\varepsilon I
once
(
\mathcal{O}(p^{3}/3)
) and two back-substitutions (
\mathcal{O}(p^{2})
each), for a total
cost of
\mathcal{O}(np+p^{3}/3)
;
\mathbf{V}^{-1/2}
is never formed.
Because
\theta^{*}=\varphi/2
is available in closed form, no grid search
is needed (contrast MaxCor, which optimises the mediation index
f^{*}(\mathbf{w})=\operatorname{cor}(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{w},\mathbf{A})\cdot\operatorname{cor}(\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{w},\mathbf{Y})
via a semi-closed-form solution; see the Supplementary Material).

The suppression mediator
\mathbf{w}^{*}_{-}
(
3
) is obtained
by replacing
\mathbf{z}
with
-\mathbf{z}
, which negates
\mathbf{q}=\mathbf{V}^{-1}\mathbf{z}
at no
extra cost; both
\mathbf{w}^{*}_{+}
and
\mathbf{w}^{*}_{-}
are returned by a single call.
After orienting each solution so that
\hat{\alpha}(\mathbf{w}^{*}_{\pm})\geq 0
,
the sign of
\hat{\beta}
identifies the effect type:

\hat{\beta}>0
for concordant mediation,
\hat{\beta}<0
for suppression.

Proposition 2

(Consistency of
\hat{\mathbf{w}}^{*}_{+}
)
.

Under the conditions of Proposition
4
with both paths active,
where
\bm{\alpha}_{0}=n^{-1}E[\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{A}]
and
\bm{\beta}_{0}=n^{-1}E[\mathbf{Z}^{\top}\mathbf{Y}]

are the population path vectors
(
\bm{\alpha}_{0}\neq\mathbf{0}
,
\bm{\beta}_{0}\neq\mathbf{0}
),
the sample maximiser
\hat{\mathbf{w}}^{*}_{+}
satisfies

\hat{\mathbf{w}}^{*}_{+}/\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}^{*}_{+}\|_{\mathbf{V}}\xrightarrow{p}\mathbf{w}^{*}_{0}/\|\mathbf{w}^{*}_{0}\|_{\Sigma_{Z}}
as
n\to\infty
with
p
fixed,
where
\Sigma_{Z}=n^{-1}E[\mathbf{V}]
is the population Gram matrix and

\mathbf{w}^{*}_{0}
bisects the population path vectors

\Sigma_{Z}^{-1}\bm{\alpha}_{0}
and
\Sigma_{Z}^{-1}\bm{\beta}_{0}
in the
\Sigma_{Z}
-metric.

The proof uses convergence of the sufficient statistics and continuity of the bisector map (see the Supplementary Material).
Intuitively, MaxIE depends on the data only through
n^{-1}\mathbf{a}
,
n^{-1}\mathbf{z}
, and
n^{-1}\mathbf{V}
, each converging to its population counterpart; smoothness of the bisector map then gives consistency whenever both path vectors are nonzero.

4  A Global Test for the Existence of Any Composite Mediator

A natural question is whether any composite mediator exists in the population.
Letting
\alpha(\mathbf{w})
and
\beta(\mathbf{w})
denote the population counterparts of

\hat{\alpha}(\mathbf{w})
and
\hat{\beta}(\mathbf{w})
(
5
), defined by

\alpha(\mathbf{w})\;=\;\frac{E[\mathbf{A}^{\top}(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{w})]}{E[\|\mathbf{A}\|^{2}]},\qquad\beta(\mathbf{w})\;=\;\frac{E[(\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{w})^{\top}\mathbf{Y}]}{E[\|\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{w}\|^{2}]},

(13)

the global null is

H_{0}:\;\max_{\mathbf{w}}\,|\alpha(\mathbf{w})\,\beta(\mathbf{w})|\;=\;0,

(14)

i.e. no linear combination of
\mathbf{X}
produces a non-zero indirect effect —
concordant
or
suppression — in the population.
By (
13
), this is equivalent to

E[\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{A}]=\mathbf{0}\quad\text{or}\quad E[\mathbf{Z}^{\top}\mathbf{Y}]=\mathbf{0}.

(15)

A natural candidate test statistic is
h(\mathbf{w}^{*})
, the maximised sample indirect
effect. However,
h(\mathbf{w}^{*})
is always strictly positive in finite samples and
its distribution under
H_{0}
depends on the magnitude of the active path —
an unknown nuisance — so it cannot be pivoted into a valid test.

The factorisation (
11
) shows that

h(\mathbf{w}^{*})\;=\;\frac{\|\mathbf{p}\|_{\mathbf{V}}\,\|\mathbf{q}\|_{\mathbf{V}}}{\|\mathbf{A}\|^{2}}\cdot\frac{1+\cos\varphi}{2},

where
\cos\varphi
is the cosine of the angle between the two path vectors
in the
\mathbf{V}
-metric,

\cos\varphi\;=\;\frac{\mathbf{p}^{\top}\mathbf{V}\,\mathbf{q}}{\|\mathbf{p}\|_{\mathbf{V}}\,\|\mathbf{q}\|_{\mathbf{V}}}\;=\;\frac{\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{q}}{\sqrt{\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{p}}\,\sqrt{\mathbf{z}^{\top}\mathbf{q}}},

(16)

where the last equality uses
\mathbf{V}\mathbf{p}=\mathbf{a}
and
\mathbf{V}\mathbf{q}=\mathbf{z}
, so the statistic
is a free by-product of the MaxIE solver requiring no additional computation.
The first factor of
h(\mathbf{w}^{*})
is the path-strength nuisance;

\cos\varphi
depends only on the directions of the two paths, not their
magnitudes.
Under
H_{0}
, one path direction is uniformly distributed on
S^{p-1}

(shown in the proof below), making
\cos\varphi
a pivot.

Proposition 3

(Null distribution of
\cos\varphi
)
.

Suppose Assumptions A1–A4 hold,
\mathbf{A}\mid\mathbf{X}\sim\mathcal{N}(E[\mathbf{A}\mid\mathbf{X}],\sigma_{A}^{2}I_{n})
, and

\mathbf{Y}\mid\mathbf{X},\mathbf{A}\sim\mathcal{N}(E[\mathbf{Y}\mid\mathbf{X},\mathbf{A}],\sigma_{Y}^{2}I_{n})

for unknown
\sigma_{A}^{2},\sigma_{Y}^{2}>0
.
Results are stated in the asymptotic regime
n\to\infty
with
p
fixed.
Then under
H_{0}
,

\cos^{2}\!\varphi\;\sim\;\mathrm{Beta}\!\left(\tfrac{1}{2},\,\tfrac{p-1}{2}\right)

(17)

asymptotically as
n\to\infty
with
p
fixed.
Equivalently, the signed statistic

T\;=\;\cos\varphi\cdot\sqrt{\frac{p-1}{1-\cos^{2}\!\varphi}}\;\sim\;t(p-1),

(18)

where the sign of
T
matches that of
\cos\varphi
.
The test rejects
H_{0}
when
|T|>t_{1-\alpha/2,\,p-1}
and has size
\alpha

asymptotically. Two-sided and directional one-sided tests (for concordant and
suppression mediation) follow immediately from the sign of
T
, all at no
extra computational cost.

The proof establishes that whichever path is null under
H_{0}
, its
whitened direction is uniform on
S^{p-1}
: exactly so when the
\alpha
-path is null
(
E[\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{A}]=\mathbf{0}
), and asymptotically
as
n\to\infty
with
p
fixed when the
\beta
-path is null
(
E[\mathbf{Z}^{\top}\mathbf{Y}]=\mathbf{0}
). The Beta distribution of

\cos^{2}\!\varphi
then follows from the standard result on the inner
product of a fixed and a uniform unit vector; see the Supplementary Material.

Notably, the two null scenarios are not symmetric: the
t(p-1)

distribution is
exact
(for any
n>p
) when the
\beta
-path is null,
but only
asymptotic
in
n
when the
\alpha
-path is null.
The same asymmetry holds in the dual (Proposition
5
):
exact for any
p>n
when the
\beta
-path is null, asymptotic as
p\to\infty

when the
\alpha
-path is null.
This is visible in the finite-sample size results of Table
3
.

The assumption
n>p
is required for the primal test: when
p\geq n
,

\mathbf{V}
is rank-deficient and the path directions are no longer uniform on

S^{p-1}
. The dual formulation of Proposition
5
extends
the test to
p\geq n
with degrees of freedom
n-2
in place of
p-1
.

The intuition behind the result is transparent once the null is stated
geometrically. Under
H_{0}
, at least one of the two path vectors carries
no population signal, so its sample direction is essentially noise — a
random unit vector in
\mathbb{R}^{p}
drawn independently of the other path.
The cosine of the angle between a fixed unit vector and an independent
random unit vector has a well-known distribution on
[-1,1]
, symmetric
about zero and concentrated near zero for large
p
.
The
t(p-1)
statistic is simply the natural pivot for this cosine, with
the degrees of freedom
p-1
reflecting the dimensionality of the sphere.
Crucially,
\cos\varphi
is already computed by MaxIE during the bisector
step — the test requires no additional passes through the data.

Proposition 4

(Power of the cosine test)
.

Under Assumptions A1–A4 and the conditions of Proposition
3
, let

\Sigma_{Z}=n^{-1}E[\mathbf{V}]
, and define the population angle

\varphi_{0}\in(0,\pi)
between the two path directions in the

\Sigma_{Z}^{-1}
-metric,

\cos\varphi_{0}\;=\;\frac{\bm{\alpha}_{0}^{\top}\Sigma_{Z}^{-1}\bm{\beta}_{0}}{\sqrt{\bm{\alpha}_{0}^{\top}\Sigma_{Z}^{-1}\bm{\alpha}_{0}\;}\,\sqrt{\bm{\beta}_{0}^{\top}\Sigma_{Z}^{-1}\bm{\beta}_{0}\;}}.

(19)

As
n\to\infty
with
p
fixed,
\cos\varphi\xrightarrow{p}\cos\varphi_{0}

and
T\xrightarrow{p}\delta
where

\delta(\varphi_{0},p)\;=\;\cot\varphi_{0}\cdot\sqrt{p-1}.

(20)

The asymptotic power of the two-sided test satisfies:
(i) the test has size
\alpha
under
H_{0}
;
(ii) power is a U-shaped function of
\varphi_{0}
, strictly
decreasing on
(0,\pi/2)
and strictly increasing on
(\pi/2,\pi)
,
with equal power at
\varphi_{0}
and
\pi-\varphi_{0}
;
(iii) power tends to
1
as
n\to\infty
if and only if

|\delta(\varphi_{0},p)|>t_{1-\alpha/2,\,p-1}
.

The proof applies convergence of the sufficient statistics and continuity of
T

to show
T\xrightarrow{p}\delta
, then reads off each property from the
monotonicity of
|\cot\varphi_{0}|
(see the Supplementary Material).
The noncentrality
\delta=\cot\varphi_{0}\cdot\sqrt{p-1}
ties power
directly to the geometry of the path directions: it is the cotangent of
the population angle scaled by
\sqrt{p-1}
, large when
\varphi_{0}
is
close to
0
or
\pi
and zero at
\varphi_{0}=\pi/2
. The power curve
is therefore U-shaped: the test is most powerful when both path directions
nearly coincide (
\varphi_{0}\approx 0
, concordant mediation) or nearly
oppose (
\varphi_{0}\approx\pi
, suppression), and powerless when they are
orthogonal. The two configurations are equally detectable at the same
angular distance from
\pi/2
, since
|\cot(\pi-\varphi_{0})|=|\cot\varphi_{0}|
.

The detection threshold is
|\delta(\varphi_{0},p)|=t_{1-\alpha/2,p-1}
,
equivalently
|\cot\varphi_{0}|=t_{1-\alpha/2,p-1}/\sqrt{p-1}
. For
fixed
p
, if
|\delta|
falls below this threshold, increasing
n

cannot help once
T
has concentrated at
\delta
— a qualitative
difference from standard tests where power always increases
with
n
.

Non-rejection of
H_{0}
therefore has three distinct causes:
(1) no mediation (
\bm{\alpha}_{0}=\mathbf{0}
or
\bm{\beta}_{0}=\mathbf{0}
);
(2) paths present but
|\delta(\varphi_{0},p)|\leq t_{1-\alpha/2,p-1}
,
where no increase in
n
will help; or
(3)
n
not yet large enough for
T
to concentrate near
\delta
,
where collecting more data helps until the asymptotic regime is reached.
Empirical validation is given in Section
7.2
.

5  Dual Implementation of MaxIE for
p\geq n

When
p\geq n
, the primal Gram matrix
\mathbf{V}=\mathbf{Z}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}

has rank
n-1<p
and is therefore singular: the primal path vectors

\mathbf{p}=\mathbf{V}^{-1}\mathbf{a}
and
\mathbf{q}=\mathbf{V}^{-1}\mathbf{z}
are undefined, and the
primal cosine test breaks down.
The dual implementation resolves this by working in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
instead of

\mathbb{R}^{p}
, replacing
\mathbf{V}
with the
n\times n
kernel matrix

\mathbf{K}_{Z}=\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{Z}^{\top}
and projecting the path vectors into the column space
of
\mathbf{Z}
.

The foundation is the identity
\mathbf{K}_{Z}^{+}\,\mathbf{Z}=\mathbf{Z}\,\mathbf{V}^{+}
, which gives
dual path vectors

\tilde{\mathbf{p}}\;:=\;\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{p}\;=\;\mathbf{K}_{Z}^{+}\tilde{\mathbf{a}},\qquad\tilde{\mathbf{q}}\;:=\;\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{q}\;=\;\mathbf{K}_{Z}^{+}\tilde{\mathbf{z}},

(21)

where
\tilde{\mathbf{a}}=\mathbf{K}\mathbf{A}
and
\tilde{\mathbf{z}}=\mathbf{K}\mathbf{Q}_{A}\mathbf{Y}
are dual sufficient
statistics computable without forming
\mathbf{V}
,

\tilde{\mathbf{a}}\;=\;\mathbf{K}\mathbf{A},\qquad\tilde{\mathbf{z}}\;=\;\mathbf{K}\mathbf{Q}_{A}\mathbf{Y}.

(22)

Since
\tilde{\mathbf{p}}=\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{p}
and
\tilde{\mathbf{q}}=\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{q}
, the Euclidean angle
between them in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
equals the primal
\mathbf{V}
-metric angle:

\cos\varphi=\tilde{\mathbf{p}}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{q}}/(\|\tilde{\mathbf{p}}\|\|\tilde{\mathbf{q}}\|)
.
The ambient dimension is
n-1
(the rank of
\mathbf{K}_{Z}
) rather than
p-1
,
so
T\sim t(n-2)
under
H_{0}
instead of
t(p-1)
.
The primal weight vector is recovered as

\mathbf{w}^{*}\;\propto\;\mathbf{Z}^{+}\!\left(\frac{\tilde{\mathbf{p}}}{\|\tilde{\mathbf{p}}\|}+\frac{\tilde{\mathbf{q}}}{\|\tilde{\mathbf{q}}\|}\right),

(23)

where
\mathbf{Z}^{+}=\mathbf{Z}^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{Z}^{+}
, at cost
O(np)
.

Proposition 5

(Dual implementation of MaxIE)
.

Suppose
\mathbf{X}
has full row rank (satisfied when
p\geq n
and
\mathbf{X}
is in
general position). Let
(\tilde{\mathbf{a}},\tilde{\mathbf{z}})
be as in (
22
)
and
(\tilde{\mathbf{p}},\tilde{\mathbf{q}})
as in (
21
).

(i)

(Angle preservation and null distribution.)

The dual path vectors satisfy
\tilde{\mathbf{p}}=\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{p}
and
\tilde{\mathbf{q}}=\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{q}
,
so their Euclidean angle equals the primal angle:

\cos\varphi\;=\;\frac{\tilde{\mathbf{p}}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{q}}}{\|\tilde{\mathbf{p}}\|\,\|\tilde{\mathbf{q}}\|}\;=\;\frac{\mathbf{p}^{\top}\mathbf{V}\mathbf{q}}{\|\mathbf{p}\|_{\mathbf{V}}\|\mathbf{q}\|_{\mathbf{V}}}.

(24)

Under the Gaussian assumptions of Proposition
3
and
H_{0}
,

T\;=\;\cos\varphi\cdot\sqrt{\frac{n-2}{1-\cos^{2}\!\varphi}}\;\sim\;t(n-2)

(25)

asymptotically as
p\to\infty
(with
n
fixed) under
H_{0}
.
The test rejects
H_{0}
at size
\alpha
when
|T|>t_{1-\alpha/2,\,n-2}
.

(ii)

(Consistency of the dual path vectors.)

Suppose
\mathbf{X}
has i.i.d. columns with
E[\mathbf{X}_{j}]=\mathbf{0}
and

E[\mathbf{X}_{j}\mathbf{X}_{j}^{\top}]=\Sigma_{X}\succ 0
.
Define the population dual sufficient statistics

\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}_{0}=\Sigma_{X}\mathbf{A}
and
\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{0}=\Sigma_{X}\mathbf{Q}_{A}\mathbf{Y}
,
the population kernel
\Sigma_{K}=\mathbf{Q}_{A}\Sigma_{X}\mathbf{Q}_{A}
(rank
n-1
),
and the population dual path vectors

\tilde{\mathbf{p}}_{0}\;=\;\Sigma_{K}^{+}\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}_{0},\qquad\tilde{\mathbf{q}}_{0}\;=\;\Sigma_{K}^{+}\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{0},

(26)

both in
\mathbf{A}^{\perp}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
If both are nonzero, then as
p\to\infty
with
n
and
(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{Y})
fixed,

\frac{\tilde{\mathbf{p}}}{\|\tilde{\mathbf{p}}\|}\;\xrightarrow{p}\;\frac{\tilde{\mathbf{p}}_{0}}{\|\tilde{\mathbf{p}}_{0}\|},\qquad\frac{\tilde{\mathbf{q}}}{\|\tilde{\mathbf{q}}\|}\;\xrightarrow{p}\;\frac{\tilde{\mathbf{q}}_{0}}{\|\tilde{\mathbf{q}}_{0}\|},

and consequently
\cos\varphi\xrightarrow{p}\cos\phi_{0}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{Y})

as defined in item (iii) below.

(iii)

(Power of the dual cosine test.)

Assume
\tilde{\mathbf{p}}_{0}\neq\mathbf{0}
and
\tilde{\mathbf{q}}_{0}\neq\mathbf{0}

(both mediation paths active at the population level).
Let
\phi_{0}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{Y})=\arccos\!\left(\tilde{\mathbf{p}}_{0}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{q}}_{0}/(\|\tilde{\mathbf{p}}_{0}\|\|\tilde{\mathbf{q}}_{0}\|)\right)\in(0,\pi)

denote the population angle between the dual path vectors;
as
p\to\infty
with
n
and
(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{Y})
fixed,

\cos\varphi\;\xrightarrow{p}\;\cos\phi_{0}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{Y}),

(27)

a random variable since
(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{Y})
remain random with
n
fixed.
The statistic
T
under
H_{1}
is approximated by a noncentral
t
with
n-2
degrees of
freedom and noncentrality

\delta(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{Y})\;=\;\cot\!\phi_{0}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{Y})\cdot\sqrt{n-2},

(28)

with limiting power

\pi_{\infty}\;\approx\;P\!\left(\left|t\!\left(n-2,\,\delta(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{Y})\right)\right|\geq t_{1-\alpha/2,\,n-2}\;\Big|\;\mathbf{A},\mathbf{Y}\right).

(29)

Consequently: (a)
T\approx t(n-2,\delta(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{Y}))
under
H_{1}
,
confirmed numerically (Figure
3
, left);
(b) power is U-shaped in
\phi_{0}
, minimised at
\pi/2
and
symmetric about it (Figure
3
, centre);
(c) power converges to
\pi_{\infty}\in(\alpha,1)
for all

\phi_{0}\neq\pi/2
, with no sharp detection threshold in
p

(Figure
3
, right).

The dual algorithm costs
\mathcal{O}(n^{2}p+n^{3}/3)
:
\mathcal{O}(np)
to form

\mathbf{Z}=\mathbf{Q}_{A}\mathbf{X}
,
\mathcal{O}(n^{2}p)
to form
\mathbf{K}_{Z}=\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{Z}^{\top}
,
and
\mathcal{O}(n^{3}/3)
for its eigendecomposition plus
\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
for
two back-substitutions to obtain
(\tilde{\mathbf{p}},\tilde{\mathbf{q}})
, and
\mathcal{O}(np)

for weight recovery (
23
).

Item (i) establishes angle preservation and the
t(n-2)
null distribution.
The null distribution is
asymptotic
in both regimes but in different directions:
in the primal (
n\to\infty
,
p
fixed) the
t(p-1)
approximation improves as path
directions stabilise; in the dual (
p\to\infty
,
n
fixed) the
t(n-2)
approximation
improves as the dual path vectors stabilise, with finite-
p
distortions from
each null scenario vanishing as
p\to\infty
.
Item (ii) establishes consistency of
\cos\varphi
as an estimator of the
population angle; item (iii) characterises power via a noncentral
t(n-2)
,
with the key distinction from the primal that power converges to a finite
limit
\pi_{\infty}\in(\alpha,1)
rather than 1, because
\cos\varphi
stabilises
at the random variable
\cos\phi_{0}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{Y})
rather than a fixed constant.

The primal and dual are two asymptotic views of the same
\cos\varphi
:
primal fixes
p
and lets
n\to\infty
(point-mass limit),
dual fixes
n
and lets
p\to\infty
(non-degenerate limit).
At the boundary
p=n
, the noncentralities
\cot\varphi_{0}\sqrt{p-1}
and

\cot\phi_{0}\sqrt{n-2}
differ by
\sqrt{(n-2)/(n-1)}\to 1
, a negligible effect.

Table S1 of the Supplementary Material summarises both implementations:
they are not two methods but one method in two coordinate systems.
The natural choice is whichever is feasible: primal (
\mathcal{O}(p^{3}/3)
,
O(p^{2})
memory)
when
p<n
, dual (
\mathcal{O}(n^{2}p+n^{3}/3)
,
O(n^{2})
memory) when
p\geq n
.

6  Comparison Methods

We compare MaxIE against two OLS-based methods and the Directions of
Mediation (DM) method of Chén et al.
(
Chen et al. 2018
)
, and include MaxCor as an
additional comparison to examine how optimising
f^{*}
rather than

\hat{\alpha}\hat{\beta}
affects performance. Method numbers match those used in
the experiment tables.

[1] MaxIE
is our primary proposed method, directly optimising

\hat{\alpha}\hat{\beta}
via the closed-form solution of Section
3
.

[2] MaxCor
is a comparison solver that directly optimises the

mediation index

f^{*}(\mathbf{w})=\operatorname{cor}(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{w},\mathbf{A})\cdot\operatorname{cor}(\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{w},\mathbf{Y})
,
a bounded, scale-free alternative to
\hat{\alpha}\hat{\beta}
that remains
well-defined when the total effect
\hat{\tau}\approx 0

(the Supplementary Material); the semi-closed-form solution is in
the Supplementary Material.

[3] Reg Y~X
sets
\mathbf{w}=\mathbf{V}_{X}^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{Y}=\mathbf{V}_{X}^{-1}(\mathbf{z}+\frac{\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{Y}}{\|\mathbf{A}\|^{2}}\mathbf{a})
, where

\mathbf{V}_{X}=\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X}
is the full Gram matrix, so this direction
maximises
\operatorname{cor}(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{w},\mathbf{Y})
without controlling for
\mathbf{A}
. This corresponds
to the standard outcome-prediction model.

[4] Reg A~X
sets
\mathbf{w}=\mathbf{V}_{X}^{-1}\mathbf{a}
, the direction
that maximises
\operatorname{cor}(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{w},\mathbf{A})
alone, ignoring the outcome
\mathbf{Y}
. This
corresponds to the standard approach for constructing biological age clocks in
aging research, where a composite molecular score is trained to predict
chronological age without reference to any disease outcome.

[5] DM
(Directions of Mediation;
(
Chen et al. 2018
)
) finds the loading
vector
\mathbf{w}
by maximising the LSEM likelihood via an iterative eigenvalue
algorithm. The first direction of mediation (DM1) is the composite
\mathbf{X}\mathbf{w}

that accounts for the largest share of the joint log-likelihood of the two
path models. Unlike MaxIE, DM has no closed-form solution and requires
iterative optimisation; the two methods share the same conceptual objective
— finding a single composite mediator that best captures the indirect effect
— but differ in criterion (likelihood vs.
\hat{\alpha}\hat{\beta}
) and
algorithm (iterative vs. closed-form). We implemented DM via a native
reimplementation of the
hdmed
algorithm
(
Clark-Boucher et al. 2023
;
Chen et al. 2018
)
.

Methods [3] and [4] each optimise a single path coefficient, leaving the
other path unconstrained. In addition to comparing mediation performance
across all methods, we report wall-clock runtimes for MaxIE and MaxCor
against their numerical solver counterparts (L-BFGS-B with multiple random
restarts applied to
\hat{\alpha}\hat{\beta}
and
f^{*}
respectively) to quantify
the practical benefit of the closed-form solutions.

For the inference simulations of Section
7.2
, we compare the
two-sided cosine test against the
intersection-union test (IUT)
.
The IUT rejects
H_{0}
only when both the omnibus
F
-test for

R^{2}(\mathbf{A}\sim\mathbf{X})=0
and the incremental
F
-test for

R^{2}(\mathbf{Y}\sim\mathbf{Z})=0
reject individually;
its
p
-value is
\max(p_{\alpha},p_{\beta})
. Size is controlled over
H_{0}

by the intersection-union principle
(
Berger 1982
)
.

8  Application: Proteomic Mediators of Age on Dementia

Motivation: what should a proteomic aging composite do?

Proteomic aging clocks regress chronological age on protein abundances and
use the resulting age gap to stratify disease risk
(
Oh et al. 2023
;
Argentieri et al. 2024
;
Tian et al. 2025
)
.
Such clocks track biological aging well but, as we show below, add limited
independent disease information beyond chronological age.
Conversely, a protein score trained directly to predict disease captures
dementia-associated proteins regardless of whether they are driven by the
aging process—including reverse-causal signals from preclinical disease.
A composite useful for
both
monitoring biological aging and predicting
disease must do two things simultaneously: track age-driven proteomic change
(so it responds when biological aging is slowed) and predict the disease
endpoint (so that response is clinically meaningful).
The MaxIE objective
\hat{\alpha}(\mathbf{w})\hat{\beta}(\mathbf{w})
encodes both requirements
directly:
\hat{\alpha}(\mathbf{w})
is age tracking and
\hat{\beta}(\mathbf{w})
is disease
relevance, and maximising their product finds the unique composite jointly
optimal on both.

Data and methods.

We apply the method to a UK Biobank proteomics dataset (
n=38{,}383

p=2{,}916
proteins
(
Tian et al. 2025
)
; treatment
\mathbf{A}
: chronological age;
outcome
\mathbf{Y}
: binary dementia status,
1{,}205
cases,
3.1\%
prevalence).
The dataset is split 70:30 into training/test sets; the linear model is a
working approximation justified by low prevalence and large

n

(
VanderWeele 2015
)
.

MaxIE
against
Lasso-Age
(
\ell_{1}
-penalised
age-on-proteins clock) and

(
\ell_{1}

9  Conclusion

Finding a composite biomarker that maximally transmits the effect of an
exposure through a high-dimensional mediator set is a natural goal in
molecular epidemiology, yet it has not previously been pursued as a direct
optimisation problem. We showed that the indirect effect
\hat{\alpha}\hat{\beta}
,
despite being non-convex in the weight vector
\mathbf{w}
, admits a closed-form
global optimum: the solution lies in a two-dimensional subspace and is
recovered by two linear solves followed by a single
\arctan
evaluation.
The same geometric argument yields an asymptotic test for the global
mediation null: under
H_{0}
, the path-angle cosine has a
\mathrm{Beta}(1/2,(p-1)/2)

distribution on
[-1,1]
after squaring, and the reparametrised statistic

T=\cos\varphi\sqrt{(p-1)/(1-\cos^{2}\!\varphi)}\sim t(p-1)

asymptotically—a free by-product of the solver that yields a two-sided test
for any composite mediation and directional one-sided tests for concordant
and suppression mediation.
A companion algorithm, MaxCor, directly maximises the bounded mediation index

f^{*}
, providing a scale-free summary that remains interpretable in the
suppression regime where proportion mediated is undefined.

Simulations confirm that both algorithms outperform single-arm OLS baselines
by wide margins and that each leads on its own criterion, while the cosine
test controls size asymptotically and dominates the intersection-union test in power.
In the UK Biobank proteomics application, MaxIE jointly optimises both
dimensions of a useful biological age measure: it tracks chronological age
(
r_{MA}=0.897
) comparably to a dedicated aging clock while achieving
higher dementia discrimination (AUC
=0.841
) than either single-criterion
baseline.
The conventional aging clock (Lasso-Age) fails on disease prediction;
the disease predictor (Lasso-Y) fails on age tracking.
MaxIE is the unique composite that does both, providing a proteomic biological
age measure that is simultaneously anchored to the aging process and
predictive of disease outcome
(
Oh et al. 2025
;
Argentieri et al. 2024
)
.

A practical advantage of the proposed approach is its compatibility with
federated data infrastructures. Large-scale omics studies are increasingly
conducted across multiple cohorts or biobanks, where data-sharing agreements,
privacy regulations, or logistical constraints prevent centralising
individual-level records. Because MaxIE and MaxCor depend on the data only
through cross-product summaries
(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{z},\mathbf{V},\|\mathbf{A}\|^{2},\|\mathbf{Y}\|^{2})
,
each site can contribute these quantities locally and the coordinator runs the
solver once on the pooled summaries—no individual records ever leave the
site. The cosine test requires the same inputs, so estimation and inference
are both fully federated.

Limitations.

The Gaussian error assumption in Propositions
3

and
4
is used to establish uniformity of the whitened
path direction; robustness to non-Gaussian errors remains an open question.
The dual cosine test (
t(n-2)
) is derived as
p\to\infty
with
n
fixed;
its behaviour under proportional growth
p/n\to c>1
requires random
matrix methods and is open, as is formal size control for the primal test
in the proportional regime
p/n\to c\in(0,1)
.
Uncertainty quantification beyond the global null test is also limited:
a confidence interval for
\hat{\alpha}(\hat{\mathbf{w}}^{*})
,
\hat{\beta}(\hat{\mathbf{w}}^{*})
,
or
h(\hat{\mathbf{w}}^{*})
that conditions on the estimated
\hat{\mathbf{w}}^{*}
does not
correct for selection bias, in the same way that an in-sample
R^{2}
overstates
out-of-sample fit.
The honest approach is sample splitting, as in the UK Biobank analysis;
a bias-corrected interval for the population indirect effect
h(\mathbf{w}^{*}_{0})

requires separate treatment, for instance via bootstrap procedures that
mimic the full estimation pipeline.
Finally, the weight vector
\mathbf{w}^{*}
identifies the optimal composite direction but does not support inference about individual mediators: a nonzero
w^{*}_{j}
may reflect that
X_{j}
is correlated with the true mediating subspace rather than that
X_{j}
has an independent mediation effect.
Variable selection based on
|w^{*}_{j}|
is reasonable for constructing or interpreting the composite, but identifying which features are individually causal mediators requires a separate inferential procedure, such as marginal mediation tests or a sparse penalised extension.

Future directions.

A natural sparse extension would replace the two linear solves with

\ell_{1}
-penalised regressions; the bisector geometry carries over exactly,
but the null distribution of
\cos\varphi
under penalisation requires
separate theoretical treatment.
The dual formulation opens a direct path to nonlinear extensions: replacing
the linear kernel
\mathbf{K}=\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top}
with an RBF or Matérn kernel
gives a kernelised MaxIE that optimises the indirect effect in a
reproducing kernel Hilbert space, with the bisector geometry preserved in
the feature space.
A natural and important direction is relaxing Assumption A3: replacing the
linear structural model with a semiparametric model for the NIE
(in the spirit of
(
Tchetgen Tchetgen and Shpitser 2012
)
) would yield a more robust
estimator of the optimal composite mediator, though deriving the analogous
closed form under nonparametric path models is non-trivial.
Further extensions include the multi-outcome setting where
\mathbf{Y}
is a matrix,
longitudinal exposures, and cross-study aggregation under federated constraints.
