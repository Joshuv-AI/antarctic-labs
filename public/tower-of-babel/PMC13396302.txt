Prebiotics and SCFA Alleviate Parkinson’s-Related Constipation: Results from a Randomized Controlled Trial
Authors: Tobias Hegelmaier, Alexander Duscha, Christiane Desel, Lisa-Marie Würtele, Jana Peplinski, Diana Jauk, Riccardo Troisi, Martin Klietz, Simon Faissner, Thomas Müller, Aiden Haghikia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionConstipation is a prevalent and clinically significant non-motor symptom in Parkinson’s disease (PD), affecting up to 90% of patients and often preceding motor dysfunction by several years. Imbalance in the gut microbiota and reduced short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) production potentially contribute to PD pathogenesis and symptom onset. This study aimed to determine whether supplementation with SCFAs, the prebiotic 2-fucosyllactose (2-FL), or their combination alleviates constipation and improves the quality of life of individuals with PD.MethodsIn a double-blind, randomized, parallel-group clinical trial, 72 patients with PD were assigned to either SCFA, 2-FL, or combined supplementation groups for 6 months. Constipation and quality of life were assessed using the Cleveland Clinic Constipation Score, Bristol Stool Scale, and Patient Assessment of Constipation–Quality of Life, alongside routine neurological evaluation and physical activity questionnaires.Results2-FL and combined supplementation significantly reduced constipation severity after 6 months, with the combination yielding the most pronounced and sustained benefits concerning stool consistency, defecation frequency, and patient-reported outcomes. Physical activity remained unchanged and did not affect constipation-related outcomes.ConclusionCombined prebiotic and SCFA supplementation effectively alleviate constipation symptoms in patients with PD and improves patient quality of life. Future research should assess the long-term implications and potential disease-modifying effects.Trial RegistryThe study was retrospectively registered with the German Clinical Trials Register (DRKS) under the identifier DRKS00027061 on 19 November 2021 (https://drks.de/search/en/trial/DRKS00027061/details).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40120-026-00972-6.Keywords: Gastrointestinal symptoms, Metabolites, Microbiota, Parkinson’s disease, Prebiotics, Short-chain fatty acids

Key Summary Points

Why carry out this study? Constipation affects up to 80–90% of patients with Parkinson’s disease and represents a clinically relevant non-motor symptom with substantial impact on quality of life and disease burden.This study assessed whether supplementation with short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), the prebiotic 2-fucosyllactose (2-FL), or their combination alleviates constipation and improves quality of life in patients with Parkinson’s disease. What was learned from the study? Combined supplementation with 2-FL + SCFA significantly reduced constipation severity and improved stool consistency, defecation patterns, and patient-reported outcomes over 6 months, with greater effects than single interventions.These findings suggest a synergistic therapeutic effect and support microbiome-directed dietary interventions as a potential safe adjunctive strategy for managing gastrointestinal symptoms in Parkinson’s disease, warranting further investigation of long-term and disease-modifying effects.

Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is one of the most prevalent neurodegenerative disorders worldwide, characterized by not only motor symptoms but also a wide range of non-motor symptoms, which can often precede the onset of motor symptoms by decades. Among the various gastrointestinal (GI) symptoms reported, constipation is remarkably prevalent, affecting 80–90% of patients. Other manifestations, such as drooling, dysphagia, and small intestinal bacterial overgrowth (SIBO), are also frequently encountered and contribute significantly to the non-motor symptom burden in PD [1, 2]. Risk determinants for PD include not only genetic influences but also a range of environmental factors, notably dietary patterns and gut health [3]. The intestinal microbiota has been increasingly recognized to play a significant role in human health and disease, including PD [4]. Changes in microbial composition can augment local and neuroinflammation through several mechanisms, including disruption of intestinal barrier integrity. Along with a well-documented dysbiosis in PD, decreased relative abundance of putative anti-inflammatory, short-chain fatty acid (SCFA)-producing bacteria, and thus reduced levels of SCFA such as propionate (PA) and butyrate (BA) within the gut, have been reported [5, 6]. SCFA serve as immune modulatory metabolites and are critical in maintaining intestinal barrier integrity [7–9]. In addition to modulating the course of PD, dysbiosis and local inflammation can also promote constipation [10]. Studies on the effects of probiotics on PD-related motor and non-motor symptoms, as well as inflammatory factors, have produced inconsistent results [11–15]. One study found that administering complex probiotics improves motor symptoms [14] while other trials and systematic reviews found no measurable improvement in motor function or systemic inflammation following probiotic supplementation [13, 15]. Consumption of prebiotic fibers influences microbiota composition and levels of SCFA and have recently been found to improve GI symptoms in PD [16, 17]. The prebiotic 2-FL has been associated with beneficial effects in patients with irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) and ulcerative colitis, as it may alleviate both intra- and extraintestinal symptoms [18]. Recently, we demonstrated a positive effect of 2-FL and SCFA on motor symptoms after a 6-month supplementation [19]. The influence of direct supplementation of SCFA and 2-FL on constipation in patients with PD has not yet been investigated.

In this study, we investigated the influence of supplementation with the SCFAs BA and PA, the prebiotic 2-FL, and their combination on constipation in 72 patients with PD. Constipation was evaluated as an exploratory endpoint in order to assess potential GI benefits associated with these interventions.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

This parallel, double-blind, randomized trial was performed from November 2019 to August 2020. All participants provided informed consent prior to participation. This clinical trial was retrospectively registered in the German Clinical Trials Register (DRKS; registration number DRKS00027061) because registration was completed after enrollment had begun due to administrative delay (https://drks.de/search/en/trial/DRKS00027061/details). A total of 94 individuals were assessed for their suitability, and 72 patients were ultimately enrolled and randomly allocated to a single treatment group. We reference the CONSORT diagram as described by Hegelmaier et al. (Table 1) [19]. Exploratory sex-specific analyses were additionally performed in male participants, whose baseline demographic and clinical characteristics are summarized in Supplementary Table 1. Inclusion criteria were between 18 and 90 years of age, diagnosis of a primary Parkinsonian syndrome with a moderate disease severity, and full orientation of the study participants. Exclusion criteria were immunosuppressive therapy within the past 6 months, the use of antibiotics or metformin in the past 3 months, and strict vegan dietary habits. Participants were required to maintain a stable dosage of any concomitant medications and PD-related therapies, throughout the 6-month study period. In addition, no participant used laxatives or other medications affecting bowel habits at baseline, and the use of such agents was not permitted during the study period. Compliance with this restriction was assessed at each study visit (baseline, 3 months, and 6 months), with no reported use throughout the study. Participants underwent routine evaluations at an outpatient clinic every 3 months following the initiation of supplementation, during which a neurologist conducted a comprehensive neurological examination. The side effects were generally mild, with only one participant dropping out of the study. The reported adverse events included gastrointestinal discomfort and mild bloating.

This study presents the exploratory secondary clinical endpoints concerning gastrointestinal symptoms and quality of life from the same cohort previously described by Hegelmaier et al. (Table 1) [19]. In the main study, the primary endpoints were changes in gut microbiome diversity and composition as well as SCFA concentrations in stool and serum following 6 months of supplementation. In the primary endpoint analyses, supplementation did not significantly alter gut microbiome diversity or overall composition, while fecal SCFA concentrations remained unchanged and serum butyrate and propionate levels showed a slight increase after treatment. The primary endpoint was the diversity and composition of the microbiome, and as a result of the early-phase nature of the study, we did not include a control arm. The trial was designed as a comparative study to evaluate the relative efficacy of three active microbiome-modulating interventions. The decision to omit a placebo arm was based on the study’s primary objective—characterizing the differential impacts of SCFAs, 2-FL, and their combination on microbial composition—and the prioritization of active-treatment comparisons to identify the most effective therapeutic candidate for future investigation.

Ethical Approval

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Ruhr-University Bochum (approval number 19-6713). This study was performed in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration of 1964 and its later amendments, with the exception that this trial was retrospectively registered. All participants provided written informed consent prior to inclusion in the study.

Randomization and Masking

Randomization and blinding procedures followed the methodology described in Hegelmaier et al. [19]. Briefly, participants were randomly assigned (1:1:1) to one of three study arms using permuted block randomization with allocation concealment and blinding of patients and investigators. To ensure the integrity of the double-blind design, participants in all three arms received an identical number of supplements. Those assigned to the single-intervention groups (SCFA or 2-FL) received a matching placebo for the alternate substance. All active supplements and placebos were identical in appearance, weight, and packaging, making the treatment regimens indistinguishable to both participants and investigators. Allocation was performed by an independent third party not involved in the clinical assessments, and blinding was maintained until the 6-month study period had concluded and the final dataset was locked for analysis.

Cleveland Clinic Constipation Score (CCCS)

To investigate constipation-related symptoms, we used the Cleveland Clinic Constipation Score (CCCS), also described as the Wexner questionnaire [20], which objectively rates the severity of constipation. It consists of eight items evaluating key constipation-related symptoms, specifically the frequency of bowel movements, abdominal pain, time per defecation event, or incomplete evacuation. Each item is typically scored from 0 to 4 points with increasing severity of symptoms, except for assistance during defecation, which is scored between 0 and 2. Possible additive scores range between 0 (normal defecation, no symptoms) and 30 (severe constipation), where a score of 15 and above was associated with clinically significant constipation in several studies and cross-cultural adaptations [20–23].

Bristol Stool Scale (BSS) and Defecation Frequencies

Stool consistency, together with the frequency of defecation as potential evidence for constipation, was evaluated using the Bristol Stool Scale (BSS) questionnaire, which includes verbal as well as visual classification of fecal consistency of the last defecation on a 7-point scale [24]. Items range from hard and lumpy consistencies (1 + 2), sausage-shaped (3 + 4), to more soft, mushy, or watery consistencies. Types 1 and 2 are generally associated with constipation, whereas types 6 and 7 are classified as diarrhea. Both scale edges are associated with functional bowel disorders, such as irritable bowel diseases (IBD) [25, 26]. Types 3–5 are considered normal/healthy stool types. Frequencies were determined as the number of defecations per day. Individual BSS results and defecation frequencies were grouped according to the treatment regimen and study time point and were subsequently analyzed.

Patient Assessment of Constipation–Quality of Life (PAC-QOL)

The impact of constipation on individual patients’ quality of life was assessed using the PAC-QOL [27]. This questionnaire was validated in various studies investigating quality of life related to opioid-induced constipation in patients with cancer or postoperative constipation [28, 29]. It consists of 28 items, which are distributed among four major subscales, namely physical discomfort, psychosocial discomfort, worries and concerns, and satisfaction, and recapitulates an observational period of 2 weeks before the visit. Each item has a possible score ranging from 0 (no impact) to 5 (heavy impact) of the associated factors on the patients’ lives. For statistical analysis, we calculated the mean of overall scores per individual at baseline, after 3 months, and after 6 months and performed subsequent intergroup analyses.

Simple Physical Activity Questionnaire (SIMPAQ)

To assess daily individual physical activity, the SIMPAQ questionnaire was used because of its easy-to-answer structure, which is based on a five-item interview regarding physical activity in the last 7 days. The SIMPAQ was originally developed as a measuring tool to investigate physical activity in patients with psychiatric disorders such as schizophrenia and depression [30, 31], as patients displayed divergent results during test–retest reliability studies with the frequently used International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ). SIMPAQ consists of different textboxes, one asking for the time spent in bed (i.e., total hours lying in bed including sleeping time and resting), one for sedentary time including napping, one for the walking time during the previous week, one for time spent on exercises and one for time spent for incidental activity [30]. Activity was noted in minutes per week for all boxes and activity time was calculated based on individual patient reports afterwards for subsequent analysis per treatment group.

Results

Constipation-Related Symptoms Decreased During 6 Months of Prebiotic and SCFA Supplementation

Evaluation of the CCCS questionnaires showed no differences between groups at baseline, in which all patients showed a moderate level of constipation-related symptoms, regardless of their group assignment. Supplementation with 2-FL significantly reduced symptom burden over 6 months compared to baseline, whereas SCFA supplementation showed only a trend towards a decrease in CCCS over the study period. However, combined supplementation with 2-FL and SCFA showed significant improvement in symptoms after 3 months compared to baseline, which was even more pronounced after 6 months of consecutive 2-FL + SCFA intake (Fig. 1).

Stool Form and Defecation Frequency Improved During Prebiotic and SCFA Supplementation

To investigate the causality between decreased constipation-related symptoms and possible improvements in bowel movement as indicated by stool consistency, we evaluated the BSS in combination with defecation frequencies. Here we saw that baseline stool form in all three groups did not differ and showed associations with constipation. During 3 and 6 months of supplementation, stool form significantly transitioned towards a more typical, healthy direction only in the combined supplementation group. We could not detect significant changes in both single-supplementation groups, although a trend in fecal consistency improvement could be observed in the SCFA supplementation group (Fig. 2a). In line with these results, defecation frequency also decreased significantly in the combined supplementation group over the study period from 2 to 1 attempt per day. Interestingly, a significant decrease over 6 months was also detectable in the 2-FL supplementation, while the SCFA group only displayed a tendency for decreased defecation attempts per day (Fig. 2b).

Constipation-Related Quality of Life Improved in All Treatment Groups, While Physical Activity was Unaltered

To examine the psychological and physical effects of reduced constipation-related symptoms as well as the impact of decreased defecation burden, we used the psychosocial PAC-QOL questionnaire in combination with the SIMPAQ. While the quality of life related to constipation was significantly increased as seen by significant reduction of PAC-QOL relative scores in all supplementation groups (Fig. 3a), the total self-reported time for physical activity during the week was not altered (Fig. 3b).

Discussion

Here, we report that supplementation with SCFA and the prebiotic 2-FL lead to improvement of constipation-related symptoms in patients with PD. These findings demonstrate that combined supplementation with 2-FL and SCFA represents a promising therapeutic approach for addressing constipation. While individual interventions showed modest improvements, the combination produced superior and sustained benefits across multiple constipation-related parameters, supporting the therapeutic potential of targeting the constipation in patients with PD [32].

The supplementation with the combination of SCFA and 2-FL led to regulation of constipation symptoms across multiple assessment domains. Constipation is overall very burdensome for patients and entails many negative aspects, such as poorer medication absorption, while the use of laxatives is associated with dehydration, a disturbed water/salt balance, colonic ulcers, and ischemic colitis [33–36]. While the strongest effect on PD-specific outcomes (UPDRS) was observed in the SCFA group, as previously reported, the strongest effect on constipation was achieved in the combination group and in the 2-FL group after 6 months (Fig. 1) [19]. The observed improvement in constipation in the combination group may be explained by a potential synergistic effect of 2-FL and SCFAs, which together modulate gut microbiota composition, enhance intestinal barrier integrity, and attenuate local inflammation mechanisms known to influence gastrointestinal motility in PD. 2-FL may promote SCFA-producing bacteria such as Bifidobacterium, while exogenous SCFAs directly support mucosal function and enteric neuronal signaling, potentially restoring impaired gut–brain communication in Parkinson-related constipation [37–40]. Bowel dysfunction in patients with PD is increasingly recognized as a dual-component disorder involving both reduced defecation frequency (slow-transit constipation) and defecatory dysfunction (e.g., dyssynergia and straining), with the latter often representing the primary pathophysiological burden [41]. While the metabolic interventions explored here—SCFAs and the prebiotic 2-FL—primarily target intestinal factors such as mucosal integrity and enteric neuronal signaling, their clinical benefits appear to address both components. Specifically, these compounds may improve transit times by modulating local neurometabolic pathways, while the observed normalization of stool consistency (BSS) likely alleviates the mechanical difficulty and straining associated with defecatory dysfunction, leading to the more complete and satisfying evacuations reflected in our PAC-QOL and CCCS data.

The BSS demonstrated a resolution of constipation after 3 months in the combination group, indicating rapid normalization of stool form and consistency. This finding is particularly significant given that previous studies have shown that both 2-FL and SCFAs work synergistically to enhance intestinal barrier function and promote beneficial microbial fermentation [42]. Surprisingly, we observed a decrease in stool frequency in the combination group (Fig. 2b). At baseline, relatively high defecation frequencies in some patients may reflect incomplete evacuation or repeated attempts to defecate rather than normal bowel function, a phenomenon frequently observed in PD-related defecatory dysfunction. The observed reduction in frequency may therefore indicate a transition from multiple incomplete bowel movements toward fewer but more complete and effective evacuations. This apparent paradox may reflect the transition from multiple small, hard stools to fewer but more complete and satisfying bowel movements, as supported by the improved PAC-QOL scores [27]. The decreased frequency may be further elucidated by the components of the CCCS, which includes specific measures for defecatory dysfunction such as straining, incomplete evacuation, and time per defecation. The transition from frequent, unsuccessful attempts toward fewer but more efficient and complete bowel movements directly addresses the dyssynergic components often seen in patients with PD. In the combination group, this is evidenced by the simultaneous normalization of stool consistency (BSS) and reduced defecatory attempts. Conversely, the more pronounced PAC-QOL improvement in the SCFA group, despite stable bowel movement frequency, suggests that subjective quality of life in this cohort may also be influenced by the significant motor improvements previously observed with SCFA supplementation, alongside a reduction in the perceived burden of individual GI symptoms.

The PAC-QOL assessment revealed improved quality of life across all intervention groups, suggesting that even modest physiological improvements translate into meaningful patient-reported benefits. Although the absolute numerical changes in clinical scores were relatively modest, their clinical meaningfulness is underscored by the significant improvements observed in the PAC-QOL assessments. In patients with PD, even a subtle shift from hard, constipated stools (BSS 1–2) to a healthy consistency (BSS 3–4), combined with a reduction in unproductive defecation attempts, can represent a substantial relief in daily symptom burden and psychological distress. While minimal clinically important differences (MCID) for the CCCS in PD have not been universally established, the concurrent improvement in quality-of-life scores suggests that the observed physiological shifts provided tangible therapeutic benefits to the participants. Notably, the baseline severity of constipation in our cohort was relatively mild, with a mean CCCS score of approximately 8, which may limit the generalizability of our findings to patients with more severe or refractory constipation. This value is below the commonly used threshold of 15 for clinically significant constipation, indicating that our cohort predominantly represented patients with mild-to-moderate symptoms. However, baseline PAC-QOL scores demonstrated that even these comparatively mild symptoms were associated with a measurable impairment in quality of life, underscoring their clinical relevance in patients with PD. Although scores of 15 or higher are often considered indicative of clinically significant constipation, the baseline PAC-QOL results demonstrated that even these mild-to-moderate symptoms imposed a substantial subjective burden on participants’ quality of life. This suggests that the interventions addressed clinically relevant early-to-mid-stage symptomatic dysfunction rather than advanced constipation. While the CCCS is not a PD-specific instrument, it was chosen for its validated ability to assess components of defecatory dysfunction, including straining, incomplete evacuation, and time required for defecation, which are primary pathophysiological features of constipation in PD [20, 41]. The observed decrease in defecation frequency likely reflects a shift from frequent, unproductive attempts toward more complete bowel movements, a transition further supported by the significant improvement in patient-reported satisfaction and quality of life (PAC-QOL). Nevertheless, we acknowledge that our study did not utilize MDS-endorsed questionnaires or specifically monitor for overflow diarrhea and incontinence, which should be considered in future trials to more granularly characterize the clinical response. In addition, while the PAC-QOL provided valuable insights into constipation-specific distress, we did not include a general PD quality-of-life instrument (e.g., PDQ-39), which may have captured broader therapeutic effects. Future studies should therefore incorporate both disease-specific and symptom-specific quality-of-life measures to better delineate these interdependencies.

The efficacy observed in our study aligns with multiple lines of evidence supporting SCFA supplementation in constipation management. It has been demonstrated that acetate, PA, and BA levels were significantly decreased in patients with mixed refractory constipation and were negatively correlated with constipation severity, supporting our therapeutic rationale [43]. Similarly, Wang et al. showed that acetylated and butylated starches effectively relieved constipation by increasing acetate-producing bacteria, which correlated with small intestinal transit rate and water content of feces, while BA-producing bacteria were correlated with reduced time to first black stool defecation [38]. Our combination approach builds upon these findings by providing both direct SCFA supplementation and prebiotic substrate to sustain endogenous SCFA production. The duration of our intervention (6 months) exceeded that of most previous studies, which typically ranged from 4 to 8 weeks, potentially explaining the sustained benefits observed [11, 15, 44].

Epidemiological evidence indicates that physical activity generally exerts a beneficial effect on constipation in individuals with PD [45, 46]. Furthermore, as recently described, SCFAs improve motor abilities [19]. However, despite the positive effect on MDS-UPDRS III observed in the main study, an examination of the movement scores in this study shows that physical activity is unlikely to have contributed to the changes in constipation, as SIMPAQ mobility scores did not increase and remained stable across all three groups throughout the intervention period (Fig. 3b). This stability in physical activity further supports the assumption that major lifestyle-related confounders remained unchanged during the study period. Notably, subgroup analyses restricted to male participants largely confirmed the overall findings and indicated a particularly consistent response to combined 2-FL + SCFA supplementation across functional constipation outcomes (Supplementary Fig. 1).

While our study demonstrates significant clinical improvements, certain limitations must be acknowledged. First, the absence of a placebo-control group is a major limitation of this study. Although the randomized, three-arm design allowed for a direct comparison between different metabolic interventions, the lack of a placebo arm prevents us from fully isolating the therapeutic effect from potential placebo responses, which are common in clinical trials involving PD and gastrointestinal symptoms. Second, we did not perform physiological phenotyping (e.g., anorectal manometry or colonic transit studies) to categorize patients into specific constipation subtypes, such as slow transit or dyssynergic defecation, which is increasingly recognized as a primary pathophysiology in PD [41]. Consequently, we cannot exclude that our cohort included a heterogeneous mixture of constipation subtypes, including slow transit constipation, dyssynergic defecation, or overlapping forms, which may differentially respond to the investigated interventions. However, the CCCS applied in this study is a validated composite instrument that captures both stool frequency and key features of defecatory dysfunction, including straining, incomplete evacuation, and need for assistance, thereby partially reflecting the multifactorial nature of constipation in PD [20]. Third, although participants were advised to maintain consistent dietary habits, the lack of detailed longitudinal dietary records, including daily fiber intake, means we cannot entirely rule out the influence of dietary shifts on bowel habits. In particular, we did not systematically assess daily fiber intake at baseline, 3 months, and 6 months, which represents a potential confounder given the known impact of dietary composition on bowel function. Nevertheless, participants were explicitly instructed to maintain their habitual diet throughout the study period, and no structured dietary interventions were introduced. Future studies should integrate objective physiological measures and rigorous dietary monitoring to further delineate the mechanisms by which SCFAs and prebiotics influence specific constipation phenotypes in PD.

Study Limitations

This exploratory study has several limitations. The sample size was modest and no placebo group was included, limiting causal inference. Constipation outcomes were mainly questionnaire-based and were not complemented by objective physiological testing. Baseline symptoms were mostly mild to moderate, which may reduce generalizability to patients with severe constipation. Future studies should therefore include patients with more severe constipation to better assess the efficacy of these interventions across the full clinical spectrum of PD-related bowel dysfunction. In addition, unmeasured lifestyle factors such as diet or hydration may have influenced outcomes. Finally, the 6-month follow-up does not allow conclusions on long-term efficacy or safety.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that combined supplementation with SCFA and 2-FL represents an effective and safe therapeutic strategy for managing constipation in patients with PD. The superior efficacy of combination therapy compared to individual interventions supports a synergistic mechanism involving both direct SCFA provision and enhanced endogenous production through prebiotic fermentation. These findings suggest that targeting constipation through microbiome-directed interventions may offer a valuable adjunctive treatment approach that improves both objective constipation parameters and patient-reported quality of life [47]. The sustained benefits observed over 6 months indicate that this approach may provide long-term therapeutic value.

Future research should explore optimal dosing regimens, investigate mechanistic pathways in greater detail, and evaluate the potential for disease-modifying effects beyond symptom management.
