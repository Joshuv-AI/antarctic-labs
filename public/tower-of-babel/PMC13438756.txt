Divergent 3D genome architecture of male germ cells across vertebrates
Authors: Laia Marín-Gual, Lucía Álvarez-González, Laura González-Rodelas, Fleur Coppen, Ashley M Milton, Rachel J O’Neill, Andrew J Pask, Marilyn B Renfree, Arthur Georges, Paul D Waters, Aurora Ruiz-Herrera
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe evolutionary plasticity of the three-dimensional (3D) genome organization in vertebrates, and its transmission through the germ line, is central to understanding genome function and evolution. Yet, the mechanisms regulating these processes remain poorly characterized across lineages. Here, we integrate fluorescence-activated cell sorting, in situ chromosome capture conformation (Hi-C), and single-cell RNA sequencing to investigate germ line genome architecture in eutherians, marsupials, and reptiles, lineages that last shared a common ancestor ~350 million years ago. We uncover lineage-specific chromatin folding patterns in germ cells, shaped by chromosome morphology and genome size, which constrain DNA loop formation and inter-chromosomal interactions during meiosis. We also explore the relationship between 3D genome remodeling and gene regulation in the context of meiotic sex chromosome inactivation (MSCI). In the tammar wallaby, we identify regions of the X that escape MSCI checkpoint, suggesting incomplete silencing in marsupials. These findings provide high-resolution insights into the evolution of germ line chromatin architecture and the co-evolution of genome structure and function across vertebrates.Subject terms: Evolutionary developmental biology, Chromosomes, Genome evolution

Introduction

Exploring the evolutionary diversity of three-dimensional (3D) genome architecture across different phylogroups is key to decipher the mechanism(s) responsible for the origin and plasticity of genome architecture1–4. Current insights are largely derived from somatic cells of a limited number of model organisms, primarily within chordates, plants, and fungi1,5,6. In contrast, our understanding of the 3D chromatin remodeling in germ cells remains restricted to a few model species, such as mouse and yeast3,7–13. Expanding this perspective on genome organization in germ cells across taxa is crucial for elucidating the evolutionary principles that govern genome architecture, gene regulation, fertility, and the emergence of genetic diversity and fertility.

Germ cells are a unique system as they transmit genetic information to the next generation. During their differentiation into highly specialized gametes—oocytes and sperm—chromatin undergoes an extensive remodeling through meiosis3,7,9,10. Insulator proteins, such as CTCF and cohesins play a key role in regulating gene expression3 and changes in chromatin structure are associated to the formation of evolutionary genomic (re)organization14. In fact, evolutionary structural rearrangements tend to occur at the boundaries of topological associated domains (TADs) in somatic cells1,15,16 and spermatogonia14, but not in later stages of meiosis14, suggesting that 3D remodeling imposes strong selective constraints in the mammalian germ line. Furthermore, chromosomal rearrangements, such as fusions, can induce novel inter- and intra-chromosomal interactions in germ cells, leaving genomic footprints of differentiation and recombination12,13. These new interactions can rewire or attenuate gene regulatory networks, offering a substrate for evolutionary innovation12,14.

In this study, we provide a comprehensive view of the dynamics and function of the genome conformational changes during spermatogenesis in divergent vertebrate species, including eutherian mammals (mouse), marsupials (tammar wallaby), and reptiles (bearded dragon). These amniotes, which last shared a common ancestor approximately 350 million years ago17 (Fig. 1A), exhibit striking differences in chromosome morphology and sex determination systems. Mammalian diversification, for instance, has been accompanied by extensive genome reshuffling18,19, resulting in a broad spectrum of diploid numbers and karyotypic configurations—from entirely acrocentric chromosomes in the house mouse12 to large metacentric chromosomes in marsupials20. These differences are mirrored in distinct chromatin folding patterns, including centromeric clustering, elevated inter/intra-chromosomal interaction ratios, and reduced distance-dependent interactions in marsupial somatic cells14.

Sauropsids (birds and reptiles) also display variation in chromosome number18,19,21, yet they retain a conserved organization of macro- and micro-chromosomes6,19,22, and a high variability in their sex-determining systems—including ZZ/ZW, XX/XY, and temperature-dependent mechanisms23. Comparative genomic studies suggest that the ancestral vertebrate genome was composed of numerous macro- and micro-chromosomes19, and that mammalian evolution resulted in the formation of highly reorganized macro-chromosomes, with an ancestral diploid number of 20 chromosomes24. Whether such topological constraints are preserved and transmitted through the germline remains an open question.

To address this, we combined fluorescence-activated cell sorting, cytological meiotic analyses, in situ chromosome conformation capture sequencing (Hi-C) and single-cell RNAseq. We studied the dynamics of 3D chromatin remodeling and gene expression in marsupials (tammar wallaby, Notamacropus eugenii, homotypic synonym: Macropus eugenii) and reptiles (bearded dragon, Pogona vitticeps) and compared these patterns with those previously reported for the house mouse (Mus musculus)3. These species were selected because they represent deep evolutionary divergences within vertebrates (circa 350 Mya) and exhibit distinctive karyotypic configurations (Fig. 1A). The house mouse, a eutherian mammal, has a diploid complement of 2n = 40 chromosomes, all acrocentric. In contrast, the tammar wallaby is a marsupial with a low chromosome number (2n = 16)25, comprising metacentric and submetacentric chromosomes. The bearded dragon represents reptiles and is characterized by moderate chromosome number (2n = 32) composed of both macro- and micro-chromosomes21. This approach enabled the reconstruction of meiotic chromosome folding patterns in these evolutionary divergent vertebrates.

Through this cross-species comparative analysis, we uncovered contrasting patterns of the spatial folding of chromosomes in meiotic (primary spermatocytes) and post-meiotic (round spermatids) cells. Our study also reveals distinct dynamics of sex chromosomes during spermatogenesis, highlighting previously unreported lineage-specific patterns of meiotic sex chromosome inactivation (MSCI) between eutherians and marsupials. Overall, our findings provide a detailed view of the interplay between higher-order chromatin organization and its functional implications during vertebrate spermatogenesis.

Results

Meiotic chromatin remodeling across vertebrates

To investigate the dynamics of chromosome conformation during spermatogenesis, we isolated enriched germ cell populations using fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS), achieving an average enrichment of 85.3%. For the tammar wallaby, we sorted spermatogonia (Spg), primary spermatocytes at pachytene–diplotene (P/D) stages, and round spermatids (RS). For the bearded dragon, we obtained primary spermatocytes (encompassing all prophase I stages) and RS (Supplementary Fig. 1) (see “Methods”). We then performed in situ Hi-C2,3 on each cell fraction (Supplementary Fig. 2), yielding an average of 123 million interactions per cell type after filtering (Supplementary Data 1). Biological replicates produced reproducible Hi-C contact maps for both species (Supplementary Fig. 2). These datasets were compared with previously published Hi-C maps from mouse germ cells3 and somatic cells of the tammar wallaby1 and bearded dragon26. To allow cross-species and cross-cell-type comparisons, all Hi-C matrices were normalized to 100 million contacts and merged into bins of 500 Kbp resolution (see “Methods”).

Our analyses revealed extensive chromatin remodeling during spermatogenesis in both species, consistent with patterns previously described in mouse3. These changes were reflected in the reorganization of A/B compartments and TADs, indicating that meiotic chromatin remodeling is a conserved feature across vertebrates. In both mouse and tammar wallaby, A/B compartments were well defined in spermatogonia (Fig. 1B). However, as meiosis progressed, compartmentalization weakened during prophase I, coinciding with homologous chromosome condensation, alignment, synapsis, and recombination. This was quantitatively supported by a significant reduction in compartment strength (first eigenvector values) in pachytene/diplotene spermatocytes (Mann–Whitney test, p < 0.001) (Fig. 1C), with both A and B compartments being equally attenuated (Supplementary Fig. 3A). In the bearded dragon, a similar attenuation of compartment strength was observed during prophase I compared to somatic cells (Mann–Whitney test, p < 0.01) (Fig. 1C), primarily affecting A-compartments (Supplementary Fig. 3A, B), which represent a large proportion of those found in micro-chromosmes6,19,27 (Supplementary Fig. 3C).

Moreover, across all species and cell types, compartment strength was correlated with chromosome size (R2 > 0.96; p < 0.001) (Fig. 1D and Supplementary Fig. 3D), with long chromosomes (e.g., in tammar wallaby) exhibiting lower compartment strength and short chromosomes (e.g., bearded dragon micro-chromosomes) showing higher values. These differences were also observed when comparing regions of the same size, since compartment strength was maintained in 20 Mbp regions from chromosomes with similar sizes, increased in 20 Mbp regions from small chromosomes, and reduced in 20 Mbp regions of large chromosomes (Supplementary Fig. 3E). Post-meiotic stages showed a re-establishment of compartmentalization, with species-specific differences. In mouse round spermatids, compartments closely resembled those of spermatogonia, with high correlation values (R2 = 0.62; p < 0.001). In contrast, the tammar wallaby exhibited a low correlation between compartments in round spermatids and spermatogonia (R2 = 0.17, p < 0.001), suggesting the emergence of novel, stage-specific compartments (Supplementary Fig. 3F). These new compartments were significantly larger (4.43 Mbp) than those in spermatogonia (2.34 Mbp) (Mann–Whitney test, p < 0.01) (Supplementary Fig. 3G).

To further dissect chromatin architecture at the sub-megabase scale, we identified TADs and assessed boundary robustness using TADbit28 at 500 Kbp resolution. TAD dynamics in tammar wallaby and bearded dragon mirrored those observed in mouse: TADs were well defined in somatic cells and spermatogonia but became substantially attenuated in primary spermatocytes. This was evident from reduced TAD insulation scores (Fig. 1E, F) and an increase in average TAD size (Supplementary Fig. 4A). A large proportion of TADs were merged during the transition from spermatogonia to prophase I—52.4% in mouse and 87.8% in tammar wallaby (Supplementary Fig. 4B)—highlighting a conserved pattern of domain reorganization during meiosis.

As spermatogenesis progressed, TAD insulation capacity continued to decline, with insulation scores approaching zero in prophase I across all three species (Fig. 1F). Notably, TAD boundary strength remained low in post-meiotic round spermatids compared to fibroblasts and spermatogonia (Mann–Whitney test, p < 0.01; Fig. 1F and Supplementary Fig. 4C), suggesting that the re-establishment of compartments in round spermatids occurs independently of classical TAD boundary insulation.

Collectively, our comparative analysis reveals that meiotic chromatin architecture undergoes profound and dynamic reorganization across vertebrates, encompassing both large-scale compartmental shifts and TAD remodeling. While the attenuation of A/B compartmentalization and TAD insulation during prophase I emerges as a conserved hallmark of spermatogenesis, lineage-specific differences—such as the emergence of novel post-meiotic domains in marsupials—highlight evolutionary flexibility in germline chromatin organization.

Species with large genomes and low diploid numbers organize meiotic chromosomes into long DNA loops

To explore the spatial organization of meiotic chromosomes during prophase I, we examined higher-order chromatin structure in each species. At this stage, chromosomes adopt a distinctive configuration in which large DNA loops are anchored to the lateral elements of the synaptonemal complex (SC), forming the chromosomal axes. This unique chromosomal assembly provides a structural scaffold that facilitates the repair of meiotic double-strand breaks (DSBs) and promotes homologous recombination (reviewed in ref. 29). Recombination-inducing DSBs are initiated within loop domains but remain tethered to the axis through meiotic proteins, ensuring that repair complexes operate within an organized framework (reviewed in refs. 29,30).

Chromosome-specific Hi-C maps and distance-dependent interaction frequency curves revealed species-specific differences in chromatin organization (Fig. 2A, B). In both mouse and bearded dragon, interaction frequencies were similar at short genomic distances (0–10 Mbp) yet diverged at longer distances (>15 Mbp), with the bearded dragon exhibiting higher long-range interactions (Fig. 2B). In contrast, the tammar wallaby showed reduced short-range interactions (0–10 Mbp), indicative of a more extended chromatin configuration. In a less compacted genome, chromatin fibers occupy a larger nuclear volume, increasing the physical distance between loci that are close in linear sequence, thereby reducing the likelihood of short-range contacts. This interpretation is consistent with previous observations in species with low chromosome numbers, where larger chromosomes adopt a more open organization in somatic cells (2n = 12–16)1. To further test this hypothesis, we next estimated DNA loop sizes in primary spermatocytes. Consistent with the reduced short-range contact probabilities, the tammar wallaby exhibited longer loops (~2.3 Mb) compared to mouse (~1.25 Mb) and bearded dragon (~400 kb) (Fig. 2C). In reptiles, loop size varied by chromosome type, with macro-chromosomes forming longer loops (~500 kb) than micro-chromosomes (~300 kb), though both were shorter than those in mammals (Supplementary Fig. 5A). These findings suggest that differences in interaction profiles reflect fundamental variation in chromatin folding principles, where genome size and chromosome architecture dictate loop organization and nuclear compaction during meiosis.

To assess chromatin reorganization during the transition from spermatogonia to prophase I, we analyzed changes in interaction frequencies across genomic distances (Fig. 2D, E). In mouse, very short-range interactions (0–0.5 Mb) remained stable, whereas short-range interactions (1–10 Mb) increased and long-range interactions (>15 Mb) decreased. In contrast, the tammar wallaby displayed a distinct pattern: very short-range interactions (0–2 Mb) decreased, short-range interactions (2–10 Mb) increased, and long-range interactions (15–200 Mb) decreased. Notably, interactions at very long distances (200–700 Mb) slightly increased in primary spermatocytes, reflecting the presence of exceptionally long chromosomes in marsupials.

Importantly, DNA loop size showed a positive association with genome size (R2 = 0.91; Fig. 2F), suggesting that genome size may influence chromatin organization, with smaller genomes forming more compact small loops during prophase I. Given the known inverse relationship between chromosome length and loop size31–33, we next evaluated the length of the SC in primary spermatocytes across all three species (Fig. 2G, H), using SYCP3 immunolabelling to visualize the lateral elements of the SC (Fig. 2G). SC lengths were normalized to the total genome size (Fig. 2H). Remarkably, we detected a negative correlation between the average loop size and SC length (R2 = 0.98), indicating that shorter loops are associated with longer chromosomal axes during prophase I (Fig. 2F).

Overall, our results indicate that both genome size and chromosome number may shape chromatin organization in primary spermatocytes. Species with large genomes (e.g., marsupials) and low diploid numbers (and hence long chromosomes) exhibit meiotic chromosomes organized into long DNA loops along relatively short chromosomal axes, resulting in reduced contact frequencies at short genomic distances. Expanding this analysis to include additional taxa will be essential to provide a broader perspective.

Divergent nuclear disposition in primary spermatocytes

We next analysed inter-chromosomal interaction patterns in primary spermatocytes across species (Fig. 3). Notably, the bearded dragon exhibited elevated inter-chromosomal interaction ratios—exceeding 1—indicating a predominance of inter- over intra-chromosomal contacts (Fig. 3A). This increase was largely driven by extensive interactions among micro-chromosomes (Fig. 3B), a pattern previously documented in cytological studies21. Consistent with these reports and our analysis of spermatocyte spreads (Fig. 2G), micro-chromosomes form a discrete cluster during prophase I. The high proportion of inter-chromosomal contacts observed was corroborated cytologically by this clustering.

Our analysis also revealed species-specific differences in the structural organization of meiotic chromosomes. In line with previous Hi-C studies in mouse spermatocytes3,9, we observed interactions between interstitial chromosomal regions (Fig. 3C, D), likely reflecting the spatial proximity of chromosomes during bouquet formation and the onset of synapsis. These interactions were particularly pronounced in reptiles, but weaker in the tammar wallaby (Fig. 3C, D). In the bearded dragon, additional enrichment of interactions at telomeric regions were detected. These differences among species are consistent with the presence of a prominent bouquet in reptiles and a transient one in the tammar wallaby34.

These patterns reflect underlying differences in nuclear architecture and chromatin organization (Fig. 3E). In mice, the combination of (i) short acrocentric chromosomes and (ii) intermediate-sized chromatin loops facilitate a compact nuclear configuration, minimizing inter-chromosomal interactions. The few inter-chromosomal interactions detected in mouse were restricted to internal chromosomal regions (Fig. 3C, D), consistent with a nuclear arrangement in which telomeres are tethered to the nuclear envelope. In contrast, the tammar wallaby possesses (i) long, metacentric chromosomes and (ii) extended chromatin loops, resulting in a more spatially expanded chromatin. In this configuration, chromosomes increase opportunities for physical overlap and thereby promoting inter-chromosomal interactions during prophase I. In the bearded dragon, although DNA loops are shorter than in mammals, we observed a marked increase in inter-chromosomal interactions, particularly among micro-chromosomes and between centromeric and telomeric regions of all chromosomes. This suggests a distinct mode of nuclear organization that maximizes inter-chromosomal connectivity, possibly reflecting the high number and small size of reptilian micro-chromosomes.

Conserved 3D chromosome folding in post-meiotic cells of eutherian and marsupial mammals

Following meiosis, haploid round spermatids exhibited a distinct higher-order chromatin structure (Fig. 4). Although A-B compartments re-emerged after being lost in primary spermatocytes, they appeared as a diffuse plaid pattern with larger average domain sizes compared to spermatogonia, with species-specific differences (Fig. 1B). In mouse, both inter- and intra-chromosomal interaction ratios increased in round spermatids relative to earlier stages, with the exception of the X chromosome, which maintained lower interaction ratios than autosomes. This is consistent with the formation of post-meiotic sex chromatin (PMSC), a repressive chromatin domain tethered to chromocenters35, which limits both inter- and intra-chromosomal interactions (Fig. 4A, B). In contrast, the sex chromosomes of tammar wallaby (X chromosome) and bearded dragon (Z chromosome) did not exhibit reduced interaction frequencies relative to autosomes, suggesting the absence of PMSC-like repression in these species (Fig. 4A, B).

Across all three species, we detected increased centromere–centromere interactions in round spermatids (Fig. 4C), consistent with chromocenter formation. While mouse spermatids typically form a single chromocenter3, we detected two to three chromocenters in the tammar wallaby and the bearded dragon (Fig. 4D). These differences may reflect underlying karyotypic variation, such as the predominance of acrocentric chromosomes in the mouse versus metacentric chromosomes in the other two species, which could influence chromatin folding and centromere clustering.

Chromosome-specific Hi-C maps in round spermatids revealed similar interaction patterns in both mammalian species (mouse and tammar wallaby; Fig. 4E), a finding further supported by distance-dependent interaction frequency plots (Fig. 4F). These results suggest that post-meiotic chromatin organization is conserved among mammals, despite differences in diploid number, indicating a shared mode of chromatin compaction. In contrast, the bearded dragon exhibited elevated contact probabilities at longer genomic distances (>10 Mb), consistent with extensive interactions among micro-chromosomes (Fig. 4B).

We further examined sub-megabase scale organization by estimating DNA loop sizes in round spermatids. Compared to primary spermatocytes, loop sizes were inverted: tammar wallaby exhibited shorter loops (~200 kb) than mouse (~400 kb) and bearded dragon (~950 kb) (Fig. 4G). In the bearded dragon, although loops were longer in macrochromosomes (~1.05 Mb) than in micro-chromosomes (~0.6 kb) (Supplementary Fig. 5B), overall loop sizes remained larger than in both mammals.

In contrast to primary spermatocytes (Fig. 2), we observed a strong negative correlation between average DNA loop size and genome size in post-meiotic cells (R2 = 1; Fig. 4H), indicating that larger genomes are folded into smaller loops in round spermatids. This pattern likely reflects constraints imposed by nuclear architecture: spermatids have a markedly reduced nuclear volume compared to spermatocytes, and smaller loops may be required to achieve the extreme level of spatial condensation necessary for packaging the entire genome within this limited space. Such compaction is particularly critical in species with large genomes, such as marsupials, where failure to reduce loop size could compromise nuclear integrity and chromatin accessibility. Consistent with this interpretation, analysis of the transition from prophase I to the spermatid stage revealed a pronounced reduction in loop size in both mammalian species, whereas loop size increased in the bearded dragon (Fig. 4I, J), suggesting lineage-specific strategies for achieving nuclear organization in the haploid germline.

Dynamics of 3D genome remodeling and gene expression during vertebrate meiosis

To investigate gene expression dynamics during spermatogenesis, we performed single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) using the 10× Genomics platform in the tammar wallaby and the bearded dragon. After quality filtering, 3811 and 3773 high-quality single cells were retained, respectively. These datasets were integrated with publicly available scRNA-seq data from human, mouse, and grey short-tailed opossum (hereafter, opossum)36 (see “Methods”). Cell types were annotated based on the expression of established marker genes37 (Fig. 5A and Supplementary Figs. 6 and 7). All major somatic and germ cell populations were identified across the five vertebrate species, including spermatogonia, spermatocytes (eP1/2 – early prophase I; mP – mid prophase; lP1/2 – late prophase I; D – diplotene; MI – metaphase I; MII – metaphase II), round spermatids (S1–S7), and elongating spermatids (S8–S11) (Fig. 5A and Supplementary Figs. 6, 7).

Consistent with previous cytological observations21,25,38, gene expression levels progressively increased throughout prophase I (Supplementary Fig. 8A–C), underscoring the conserved transcriptional program of spermatogenesis across vertebrates. To assess whether these dynamics were coupled with changes in chromatin architecture, we integrated single-cell expression profiles with compartments and TADs in mouse, tammar wallaby and bearded dragon. Across all species and cell types, active genes were positively associated with accessible A compartments (permutation test based on 10,000 permutations, p = 0.0001) and negatively correlated with B compartments (permutation test based on 10,000 permutations, p = 0.0001) (Fig. 5B and Supplementary Data 2). This was the case for both autosomes (Fig. 5C) and sex chromosomes (Supplementary Fig. 8D). Consistent with the previously described correlation between chromatin remodeling and gene expression3, A compartments contained more active genes than B compartments in spermatogonia (8308 ± 1401 active genes in A vs. 1428 ± 526 active genes in B compartments), primary spermatocytes (10,335 ± 435 active genes in A compartments vs. 6197 ± 672 active genes in B compartments) and round spermatids (13,043 ± 274 active genes in A compartments vs. 4603 ± 960 active genes in B compartments) (Supplementary Fig. 8E).

Structural and transcriptional remodeling associated to sex chromosome inactivation

We next examined MSCI across species in the context of 3D genome architecture (Fig. 6). MSCI refers to the transcriptional silencing of the unsynapsed X and Y chromosomes in male germ cells during the pachytene stage of meiosis, with a suggested role in the evolutionary persistence of the Y chromosome (reviewed in refs. 39,40). In eutherian mammals, such as mice, MSCI has been associated with extensive higher-order chromatin remodeling3. Here, we investigated whether these dynamics are conserved in marsupials. A defining feature of marsupial sex chromosomes is the absence of a pseudoautosomal region (PAR), which in eutherians facilitates X–Y recombination41–43. The tammar wallaby X chromosome is notably large (102.6 Mb) and harbors a nucleolus organizing region (NOR) and a recently acquired satellite repeat on the p-arm—features considered derived among Australian marsupials44.

Hi-C analysis revealed that, unlike in mouse3, A-B compartments on the wallaby X chromosome were already attenuated or poorly defined in spermatogonia (Fig. 6A, B) and became further weakened in primary spermatocytes and round spermatids. This progressive attenuation is consistent with MSCI and subsequent post-meiotic silencing. Correspondingly, X-linked gene expression was reduced in primary spermatocytes relative to spermatogonia—a pattern also observed in opossum and eutherians (human and mouse) (Fig. 6C and Supplementary Figs. 8D and 9). This transcriptional repression was specific to sex chromosomes, as autosomal expression remained largely unchanged (Supplementary Fig. 10). In contrast, the Z chromosome of the bearded dragon did not exhibit transcriptional silencing, consistent with the ZZ homogametic sex chromosome system in males45 (Fig. 6C and Supplementary Figs. 8D and 9).

To further characterize MSCI in marsupials through meiotic progression, we examined gene expression along the tammar wallaby X chromosome. Unlike the complete silencing observed in eutherians, several regions of the wallaby X chromosome escaped MSCI, including chrX:9,767,908–14,651,863; chrX:43,955,589–48,839,543; chrX:58,607,452–63,491,406; chrX:68,375,360–83,027,223; and chrX:97,679,086–102,563,041 (Fig. 6D and Supplementary Fig. 9). These escape regions were corroborated by the presence of phosphorylated RNA polymerase II on the X chromosome during early/mid pachytene (Fig. 6E; see also25). Collectively, these regions comprise approximately 33% of the X chromosome and encompass 294 genes. Gene ontology analysis revealed enrichment for terms related to active transcription and DNA damage response (Fig. 6F and Supplementary Data 3). These findings suggest that MSCI in marsupials is incomplete, with a substantial subset of genes escaping silencing, potentially reflecting evolutionary divergence in sex chromosome regulation.

Discussion

Understanding how the genome is spatially organized in germ cells—and how this organization evolves—is fundamental to decoding the mechanisms of genome function, inheritance, and diversification across species. Here, we delineate the fundamental principles of 3D genome architecture during spermatogenesis across representative vertebrate lineages that last shared a common ancestor circa 320 million years ago and uncover both conserved and lineage-specific features of higher-order chromatin organization. Our findings reveal that while the overarching framework of meiotic chromatin remodeling is evolutionarily conserved, distinct species-specific configurations emerge, shaped by genome structure and karyotypic diversity.

A key conserved feature is the dynamic modulation of A/B compartments and TADs throughout spermatogenesis. Consistent with previous studies in mice3,7,12,13,46 we observed that meiotic prophase I in vertebrates is characterized by a global attenuation of compartments and TADs. In both mouse and tammar wallaby, compartment strength was markedly reduced in primary spermatocytes compared to pre-meiotic (spermatogonia) and post-meiotic (round spermatids) stages, resulting in the loss of the canonical plaid pattern. TADs were progressively weakened from somatic cells to spermatogonia and further into primary spermatocytes. Although A/B compartments re-emerged in round spermatids, TADs remained poorly defined across all three species examined, with persistently low boundary strength. While earlier reports suggested the re-establishment of TADs in mature sperm47–50, more recent studies—accounting for free chromatin contamination—have demonstrated a complete loss of TADs in mature sperm of human51, mouse3,52, and frogs53,54.

Beyond these conserved dynamics, we identified striking lineage-specific differences in chromatin architecture. Species with different chromosome numbers and loop sizes exhibited distinct patterns of chromosomal interactions during meiosis. In primary spermatocytes, inter-chromosomal interaction frequencies were highest in the bearded dragon, intermediate in the tammar wallaby, and lowest in mice. These differences likely reflect a combination of factors, including: (i) chromosome length, (ii) DNA loop size, (iii) duration of the bouquet configuration, and (iv) micro-chromosome clustering. In mouse, short acrocentric chromosomes (61–195 Mb) combined with intermediate loop sizes (~1.25 Mb) promote a condensed nuclear organization, where chromosomes occupy a smaller nuclear volume and maintain limited inter-chromosomal contacts. In contrast, the tammar wallaby, with long metacentric chromosomes (103–759 Mb) and extended loops (~2.3 Mb), exhibits a more spatially expanded nuclear configuration. This larger genome must still fit within the spermatocyte nucleus, which is likely to increase opportunities for physical overlap among chromosomes, thereby promoting inter-chromosomal interactions rather than reducing them. Finally, the bearded dragon shows the highest interaction frequencies, driven by its numerous micro-chromosomes and short loops (~400 kb), which create a highly interconnected nuclear architecture.

In reptiles, despite short loop sizes (~400 Kb), the persistence of a prominent bouquet configuration21 and clustering of micro-chromosomes likely drive elevated inter-chromosomal interactions, as reflected in the X-shaped Hi-C heatmaps. In round spermatids, we observed increased centromere–centromere interactions across all species, consistent with chromocenter formation. While mouse spermatids typically form a single chromocenter3, we detected two to three chromocenters in the tammar wallaby and bearded dragon. These differences likely arise from karyotypic architecture: mouse chromosomes are exclusively acrocentric, whereas the wallaby and dragon possess metacentric and submetacentric chromosomes, which may favor the assembly of multiple chromocenters. Such variation in chromocenter number could influence the spatial organization of heterochromatin and the degree of nuclear compartmentalization in the haploid germline, reflecting lineage-specific strategies for genome packaging.

Our data also revealed that marsupial meiotic chromosomes are organized into exceptionally long DNA loops, resulting in reduced intra-chromosomal contact probabilities. The mean loop size in tammar wallaby (~2.3 Mb) was nearly twice that of mouse (~1.25 Mbp, also in agreement with previous observations9) and substantially longer than in the bearded dragon (~400 Kb). In reptiles, loop sizes were longer in macrochromosomes than in micro-chromosomes but remained shorter than in mammals. These findings, together with previous observations in marsupial somatic cells1, suggest that long marsupial chromosomes (average ~400 Mb) span the nuclear space with fewer, longer loops anchored to the chromosome axis during prophase I. This observation reinforces the striking inverse relationship detected between compartment strength and chromosome size across all three species. Previous Hi-C analyses combined with polymer simulations indicate that compartmentalization weakens as loop extrusion extends—a phenomenon more pronounced in large chromosomes55,56. Thus, variation in DNA loop size likely plays a pivotal role in shaping global chromosome architecture. Our recent work in mice shows that depletion of meiotic cohesins, such as RAD21L, produces longer loops and finer compartment patterns57. Conversely, condensin II overexpression promotes long-range intra-chromosomal contacts and erodes compartment boundaries, particularly in larger chromosomes58. Taken together, these findings support the concept that megabase-scale chromatin organization reflects an equilibrium between DNA loop size and compartment structure that is conserved across vertebrates.

In this context, it is tempting to speculate that the loop architecture observed in tammar wallaby may underlie the low frequency of meiotic DSBs25 and recombination events reported in marsupials59,60. It is well established that crossover frequency is influenced by chromosome axis length and loop size31–33,61. These two events—DNA loop size and the initial number of DSBs formed in early meiosis—are interconnected. Species with fewer chromosomes tend to have shorter SC axes, longer loops, and reduced DSB formation, ultimately leading to fewer crossovers. The short SC axes and long loops observed in the tammar wallaby likely provide limited substrate for DSB formation, contributing to the low recombination rates characteristic of marsupials. These findings support the notion that chromatin architecture in early meiosis plays a pivotal role in shaping species-specific recombination landscapes, even before the initiation of recombination itself.

In round spermatids, chromatin organization appeared more conserved among mammals, regardless of diploid number, suggesting a shared mode of chromatin compaction. However, the bearded dragon exhibited higher long-range contact probabilities (>10 Mb), consistent with extensive interactions among micro-chromosomes. At the sub-megabase scale, loop sizes in round spermatids varied inversely with genome size—larger genomes were folded into shorter loops. We hypothesize that this inverse relationship reflects spatial constraints imposed by the reduced nuclear volume of spermatids, necessitating tighter chromatin packaging in species with larger genomes.

Finally, our integrated analysis of Hi-C and scRNA-seq data revealed distinct dynamics of sex chromosome inactivation in marsupials. Unlike in the mouse, where X chromosome silencing during MSCI is accompanied by increased intra-chromosomal interactions, low compartmentalization, and TAD loss3,7,9,10, the tammar wallaby X chromosome already exhibited attenuated compartments in spermatogonia, with further weakening in primary spermatocytes and round spermatids. Gene expression analysis revealed that MSCI in primary spermatocytes of the tammar wallaby was incomplete, with several regions on the X escaping silencing, which coincided with A compartments. These escape regions, enriched for genes involved in transcription and DNA damage response, suggest more relaxed MSCI in marsupials. This contrasts MSCI in both monotreme and eutherian mammals36.

The absence of a canonical PAR, coupled with the unique meiotic pairing strategy of sex chromosomes via a dense plate25,62, suggests that marsupials employ a distinct mechanism of meiotic silencing. This is supported by cytological and transcriptomic analyses in tammar wallaby, dunnart, and opossum, which reveal noncanonical MSCI. The sex chromosomes retain γH2AX through pachytene, and X-linked transcription is detectable25. After this partial MSCI, the X chromosome is reactivated before paternal X inactivation is established in the embryo63. The imprint that results in paternal X inactivation was recently proposed to show low methylation levels across X-born transcription start sites64. This DNA methylation pattern is established before male meiosis, and subsequently maintained through meiosis into mature sperm, which will carry the paternal X to fertilization. Perhaps this unusual methylation pattern contributes to the partial MSCI that is unique to marsupial mammals. These findings underscore the evolutionary plasticity of meiotic silencing mechanisms across mammalian clades, shaped by sex chromosome differentiation and dosage constraints.

In conclusion, our study provides an evolutionary framework for understanding 3D genome folding during vertebrate spermatogenesis. By integrating chromatin conformation and expression data, we identify both conserved and lineage-specific features of genome organization. Our findings underly the close interplay between genome size, chromosome morphology, and nuclear architecture in shaping chromatin dynamics, with downstream consequences for recombination and gene regulation. Beyond providing mechanistic insight into meiotic chromosome behavior, our work underscores how evolutionary constraints on nuclear architecture influence the structural and functional organization of the genome across vertebrates.

Methods

Samples

Tammar wallaby adult males (n = 2, Notomacropus (Macropus) eugenii) of 4 years of age were from a breeding colony in Melbourne (Victoria, Australia). All experimental procedures, including animal handling and husbandry, were approved by the University of Melbourne Animal Experimentation Ethics Committees in accordance with the Australian National Health and Medical Research Council 2013 guidelines. Bearded dragon adult males with age ranging from 3 to 7 years (n = 11, Pogona vitticeps) were obtained from captive colonies in Canberra (ACT, Australia). All animals were collected and treated under appropriate permits approved by the University of Canberra Animal Ethics Committee, established in accordance with the ACT Animal Welfare Act 1992.

Spermatocyte spreads and immunofluorescence

Spermatocyte spreads were obtained from testicular biopsies collected immediately after dissection and processed as described25. Briefly, a small portion of tissue was mechanically disaggregated into a slide with 1×PBS. After adding 1% lypsol, cells were incubated between 20 and 30 min to swell them. A fixative solution containing 4% paraformaldehyde was then added and incubated for 2 h in a humid chamber. Lastly, slides were washed in 1% Photo-Flo and stored at −20 °C until use.

Slides with spermatocyte spreads were permeabilized for 10 min in PBST (0.05% Tween in PBS) prior to immunostaining. Primary antibodies were incubated overnight at 4 °C, and secondary antibodies, 1 h at 37 °C. After incubations with antibodies, slides were washed with PBST for 10 min. Finally, DNA was counterstained with anti-fade solution (Vectashield) containing 8 μg/ml DAPI (4′,6′-diamidino-2-phenylindole). Primary antibodies used included: mouse antibody against SYCP3 (Abcam #97672, 1:400), rabbit antibody against SYCP3 (Abcam #15093, 1:100), human calcinosis, Raynaud’s phenomenon, oesophageal dysfunction, sclerodactyly and telangiectasia (CREST) serum (a kind gift of M. Fritzler, 1:50), mouse antibody against RNA polymerase II (Abcam #5408, 1:400), rabbit antibody against H3K9me3 (Abcam #8898, 1:400).

Preparations were visualized using a Zeiss Axioskop epifluorescence microscope equipped with the appropriate filters and a charged coupled device camera (ProgRes® CS10plus, Jenoptik). Images were captured and produced by the ProgRes® software. Total synaptonemal complex length was measured in micrometers with ImageJ65. Only spermatocytes at the pachytene stage were included in the analysis.

Fluorescent activated cell sorting (FACS) of germ cells

Disaggregation of testicular tissue and germ cells sorting was conducted as previously described with some adaptations3. Briefly, testes were decapsulated and minced up in petri dishes containing GBSS to facilitate enzymatic disaggregation. Cell clumps were then transferred into tubes containing collagenase type II (0.5 mg/ml for the tammar wallaby and 2 mg/ml for the bearded dragon), trypsin from bovine pancreas (0.75 mg/ml) and DNase I (1 ng/µl). After incubating for 30 min at 33 °C, trypsin was inactivated with 5% foetal bovine serum. The cell suspension was then filtered through a 70 µm diameter cell strainer and centrifuged for 3 min at 1800 × g. Germ cells were resuspended in 500 µl of 1×PBS containing 1% formaldehyde for each million of cells. After crosslinking germ cells for 10 min, the reaction was quenched by adding 0.125 M glycine and incubating for 5 min at room temperature and 15 min at 4 °C. Lastly, germ cells were washed with 1×PBS, and the pellet was stored at −80 °C until sorting.

Testicular germ cells were isolated using a BD InfluxTM (BD Biosciences) coupled with an ultraviolet laser (355 mm). Cells were stained with 1×PBS containing 5 µg/ml Hoechst 33342 for 30 min at 33 °C and filtered through a 30 µm diameter cell strainer. Different germ cell populations (spermatogonia, primary spermatocytes, and round spermatids) were isolated by flow cytometry according to their DNA content and nucleus complexity, which can be assessed by plotting Hoechst Blue (UV355-460/50) versus Hoechst red (UV355-670/30) (Supplementary Fig. 1). Germ cells were collected in 1×PBS, and the pellet was stored at −80 °C until use.

Cell enrichment of each flow-sorted population was evaluated by immunofluorescence using specific meiotic proteins and DAPI morphology as previously reported3. Briefly, spermatogonia were distinguished using DNA content and DAPI morphology. For primary spermatocytes, they were identified based on SYCP3 (#ab15093, Abcam, 1:200 dilution) and RNA polymerase II patterns (#ab5408, Abcam, 1:400 dilution) based on previous characterization of marsupials and reptiles prophase I progression21,25. For wallaby, pachytene–diplotene spermatocytes were selected for Hi-C analysis. In the bearded dragon, males are homogametic (ZZ)21, resulting in a more uniform prophase I progression that precludes clear separation of early and late stages by flow cytometry; therefore, spermatocytes encompassing all prophase I stages were pooled for subsequent Hi-C analysis. Cell enrichment of round spermatids and sperm fractions was determined based on nucleus morphology and DAPI pattern. Cells were fixed on slides and then mounted with DAPI diluted in Vectashield (Vector Laboratories). In all cases, slides were analyzed using a fluorescence microscopy (Axiophot, Zeiss) coupled with a ProgRes CS10plus, Jenoptik camera. Representative images were captured with ACO XY (A. Coloma, Open Microscopy) (Supplementary Fig. 1). At least 100 cells of each sorted population were counted to assess the enrichment. Only sorted populations with enrichment above 70% were considered for subsequent experiments.

In nuclei Hi-C

In nuclei Hi-C was adapted from previously described protocols3,12,66. This allowed to construct Hi-C maps for tammar spermatogonia (2 replicates), primary spermatocytes (3 replicates) and round spermatids (3 replicates), as well as pogona primary spermatocytes (2 replicates) and round spermatids (2 replicates). Spermatogonia from the bearded dragon were not included in the analysis as the number of flow-sorted cells was insufficient for in situ Hi-C. Briefly, germ cells were incubated in lysis buffer containing 10 mM Tris, 10 mM NaCl, 0.2% Igepal and 1×PIC for 20 min at 4 °C. Subsequently, germ cells were incubated with 0.5% SDS in 1xNEBuffer 2 for 10 min at 4 °C, and the reaction was quenched by adding 10% Triton X-100 and incubating for 30 min at 37 °C. Germ cells were then centrifuged at 5000 × g for 5 min at 4 °C and resuspended in 300 µl 1×NEBuffer 2 containing 400 U of MboI. Chromatin was digested overnight at 37 °C and assessed the following day by comparing a digested and a non-digested control. Germ cells were then incubated at 65 °C for 20 min to inactivate the restriction enzyme, and the reparation mix (0.05 mM dCTP, 0.05 mM dTTP, 0.5 mM dGTP, 0.05 mM biotin-dATP, 50U Klenow in 1×NEBuffer 2) was directly added into the cell suspension. Chromatin was repaired and biotinylated for 90 min at 37 °C, and then the enzyme was heat-inactivated at 65 °C for 10 min. Prior to ligation, germ cells were centrifuged for 5 min at 5000 × g at 4 °C, resuspended in ligation buffer (0.8% Triton X-100, 0.1 mg/ml BSA, 10,000 U T4 DNA ligase in 1×NEB T4 ligase buffer) and incubated overnight at 16 °C. Lastly, germ cells were incubated with RNAse A for 15 min at 37 °C, and then with proteinase K overnight at 65 °C to reverse the crosslink, followed by a DNA extraction with phenol:chloroform. DNA concentration was quantified by Qubit, and quality was assessed by running a 0.8% agarose gel.

Prior to library preparation, samples were sonicated into fragments of 300–500 bp. The biotinylated fragments were pulled down using Dynabeads MyOne Streptavidin T1 beads, which were pre-washed with 2×binding buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, 1 mM EDTA, 2 M NaCl) and incubated with sonicated fragments for 30 min in rotation. Beads were then separated on a magnetic rack and washed with 1×binding Buffer. Ends repair, adaptor ligation and amplification were performed with the NEBNext® Ultra™ II DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina® according to manufacturer’s recommendations. For size selection, DNA fragments were incubated with AMPure beads in a 1:1 proportion for 10 min at room temperature. Beads were then washed twice with 70% ethanol, and DNA was eluted with Tris buffer for 10 min at room temperature. DNA concentration was quantified by Qubit, and fragment size was assessed by running a Bioanalyzer. Libraries were submitted for Illumina sequencing (paired-end 150 bp each side on NovaSeq 6000).

Hi-C data processing, binning, and normalization

Quality check of Hi-C libraries and trimming was conducted with BBDuk (version 09/2019)67. Reads with less than 35 bp length and a Phred quality score lower than 20 were filtered out, together with adapters. Reads with high quality were then mapped against the genome assemblies of the tammar wallaby (Macropus eugenii, PRJNA1235426) and the bearded dragon (Pogona vitticeps, PviZW2.1)26. Available raw reads of spermatogonia, pachytene/diplotene, round spermatids from mouse3 and somatic cells from the tammar wallaby[1] and the bearded dragon26 were downloaded, mapped against their reference genomes (mm10, PRJNA1235426 and PviZW2.1, respectively) and processed as the other cell types. Reads were iteratively mapped using the GEM (version 3.6.1) mapper68 from TADbit (version 1.0.1)28, with windows from 15 bp to 75 bp in 5 bp steps and following previously described parameters3. Reads classified as possible artifacts, including self-circle, dangling-end, error, extra dangling-end, too short, too large, duplicated and random breaks, were filtered out.

In-house scripts were used to bin the reads into a square matrix of 50 Kbp. HiCExplorer (version 3.7)69 was then used to merge bins into 500 Kbp resolution and correct matrices. Matrices of 50 Kbp and 500 Kbp resolution were normalized to a total of 100 million interaction counts using an in-house script that scales the sum of all interactions within a matrix. Hi-C replicates were pairwise compared following a Pearson correlation method (Supplementary Fig. 2). Differential matrices were generated by applying a log2 ratio between normalized matrices from different cell types using the function hicCompareMatrices from HiCExplorer. Both normalized and differential matrices were plotted at a 500 Kbp resolution.

Averaged contact probability P(s) and its slope

Contact probability P(s) curves were generated from normalized matrices using HiCExplorer, and the slope was calculated as the derivative function of the P(s) curve. Average loop size was then estimated as the distance with a highest slope value at a resolution of 50 Kbp.

Inter- and intra-chromosomal interaction analysis

HiCExplorer (version 3.7)69 was used to generate GInteraction tables from all normalized matrices at a resolution of 500 Kbp and imported in RStudio for subsequent analysis. Briefly, inter-chromosomal interaction heatmaps were generated by plotting mean interaction values between all pairwise combination of chromosomes. Inter-chromosomal interactions between two chromosomes were also plotted along chromosome length. Inter-/intra-chromosomal interaction ratio was calculated for each chromosome as the ratio between the mean values of inter-chromosomal and intra-chromosomal interactions.

A/B compartmentalization

FAN-C (version 0.9.14)70 was used to calculate and visualize first eigenvector values, and thus define A/B compartments. The orientation of the first eigenvector was corrected with the average GC content and gene expression from the single-cell RNA sequencing data. Compartment strength was calculated as the average of the first eigenvector as absolute values. Compartment size was calculated by quantifying the number of consecutive bins assigned to the same compartment type (A or B).

TAD calling and insulator score

The function find_tads from TADbit was used to call TADs from matrices with a 500 Kbp resolution. When comparing between cell types, TADs were classified as stable when the overlapping was higher than 75%, merged, split or rearranged. FAN-C (version 0.9.14)70 was also used to calculate the insulator score with windows of 1 Mbp.

Single-cell RNA sequencing, data processing, filtering, and normalization

Testes from tammar wallaby (n = 2) and bearded dragon (n = 2) were disaggregated as described above, and cells were freeze in 10% DMSO in FBS until use. Prior to library preparation, cells were thawed for 1 min at 37 °C and washed with 0.05% BSA in 1×PBS containing 1 ng/µl DNase I. An aliquot of the single-cell suspension was loaded into a ChromiumTM Single Cell Chip (10x Genomics®), aiming to recover the transcriptome of 5000 cells. Libraries were prepared following manufacturer’s recommendations and sequenced on Illumina NovaSeq 6000 (50 bp pair ends).

Raw reads of the single cell RNA sequencing libraries were mapped against available annotations. Available raw reads from human (Homo sapiens), mouse (Mus musculus) and the grey short-tailed opossum (Monodelphis domestica) were downloaded36, mapped against the annotations of human (hg38), mouse (mm10) and opossum (monDom5), respectively, and processed as the other species.

Mapping was conducted with Cell Ranger (version 6.1.2) using default parameters and including introns for all species. The Seurat package (version 4.3)71 was then used to analyse the mapped data. Firstly, cells with a UMI count being four times higher than the standard deviation from the median were filtered out, together with cells expressing less than 200 genes. Lastly, the sctransform package implemented in Seurat was used to normalized raw counts.

Dimensionality reduction, cell type assignation, and data integration

To visualize the single cell RNA sequencing data, dimension reduction was performed on normalized datasets. First, the principal component analysis (PCA) was calculated using the first 50 PCs and subsequently, the uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) was computed following specific parameters (dims=1:50, n.neighbors=100 and min.dist=0.4 in human; dims=1:50, n.neighbors=100 and min.dist=0.7 in mouse; dims=1:50, n.neighbors=100 and min.dist=0.7 in opossum; dims=1:40, n.neighbors=100 and min.dist=1 in tammar wallaby; dims=1:50, n.neighbors=50 and min.dist=0.9 in bearded dragon).

The R package SingleR (version 2.2)72 was used to label the different cell types. SingleR annotates cell labels by correlating gene expression of cells from a reference dataset, which have predefined labels, and cells from a given sample. In this study, ErnstSpermatogenesisData37 was used as the reference dataset, which includes different germ cell types (i.e., spermatogonia, 8 subtypes of spermatocytes and 11 subtypes of spermatids) and somatic cell types (i.e., Sertoli, Leydig, Fetal Leydig, vascular endothelial cells, peritubular myoid cells from the basal lamina and testicular macrophages). The quality of cell type labeling was assessed with the assignment scores and with the expression levels of previously described gene markers36,37.

Normalized datasets from the five vertebrate species were integrated as previously described73. Briefly, integration was performed using 3000 features and with the calculation of Pearson residuals. After anchors were identified, the IntegrateData function was used to integrate the five datasets, specifying the sctransform normalization method. For visualization of the integrated object, the principal component analysis (PCA) was first calculated using the first 50 PCs, and then, the UMAP was computed with specific parameters (dims=1:50, n.neighbors=100 and min.dist=0.4).

Multiassociation analysis between active genes and compartmentalization

The function AverageExpression from Seurat was used to extract average expression values for each gene, species and the following cell populations: spermatogonia, primary spermatocytes (including labels eP1/2, mP, lP1/2 and D) and round spermatids (including labels from S1 to S7).

Genes with a normalized expression higher than 0 were considered active.

Statistical associations between active genes and A/B compartments for each cell type and species (mouse, tammar wallaby and bearded dragon) were performed as previously described1,14,57 using the package RegioneR (version 1.26)74. Pairwise associations were represented as heatmaps with z-score values greater than 0 indicating a positive association between the two features, and z-score values lower than 0 indicating a negative association (adjusted p-value < 0.05 in both cases). Z-score values equal to 0 indicate no statistical association between the two analysed features (adjusted p-value > 0.05).

A/B compartments were also intersected with the package GenomicRanges (version 1.60)75 against the coordinates of transcription start sites (TSS) and the normalized expression levels of all genes from mouse, tammar wallaby and bearded dragon. The number of active genes in either A or B compartments were computed and visualized for each cell type and species as bar plots.

Gene expression analysis of sex chromosomes

The function AverageExpression from Seurat was used to extract average expression values for each gene, cell type and species. Mean values of expression were calculated for each cell type and species and were visualized as a ratio of expression between sex chromosomes and autosomes in a logarithmic scale. In the case of human, mouse, opossum and tammar wallaby, chromosome X was used, whereas chromosome Z was used for the bearded dragon. Moreover, sex chromosomes and autosomes were divided into bins of 5 Mbp, and average expression values for each bin, cell type and species were calculated. In the case of the chromosome Z of the bearded dragon, chromosome length was divided into bins of 785 Kbp due to its reduced size (11.77 Mbp). Average expression values per bin along chromosome length were then plotted using bar plots.

Bins with an average expression higher than 0.4 during diplotene were considered to escape MSCI. Genes included in these bins were submitted to a DNA BLAST (version 2.16.0)76 to identify their ortholog in the mouse annotation (mm10). The Gene Ontology Enrichment Analysis (GOEA) was used with the orthologs from tammar wallaby since poor information was available for marsupial GO terms.

Gene ontology enrichment analysis

Gene ontology enrichment analysis (GOEA) was performed using DAVID (version 2024q2)77,78, which identifies enriched gene ontology (GO) terms relative to the expected genome background. Only GO terms with a p-value lower than 0.005 were considered.

Statistics and reproducibility

All plots and figure legends specify statistical analysis and corresponding p-values. In all cases, statistical significance was considered for p-values lower than 0.05, unless otherwise specified. For box plots, line represents median, box represents 25–75th percentile, and whiskers represent 1.5× interquartile range. Statistical significances were determined using two-sided Mann–Whitney U-tests since data was not normally distributed (Shapiro–Wilk test, p > 0.05). Immunostaining of germ cells using H3K9me3 and RNA polymerase II antibodies was conducted at least 3 times as independent experiments for each species.
