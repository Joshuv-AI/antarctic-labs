The general position number of digraphs
Ullas Chandran S.V. svuc.math@gmail.com, Gabriele Di Stefano gabriele.distefano@univaq.it, Grahame Erskine grahame.erskine@open.ac.uk, Haritha S harithasreelatha1994@gmail.com, Elias John Thomas eliasjohnkalarickal@gmail.com, James Tuite james.t.tuite@open.ac.uk

Abstract
The general position number for graphs ask for largest vertex subsets S S such that no three vertices are contained on a common shortest path. We examine this problem in the setting of directed graphs. We provide bounds for the general position number of digraphs, show that the problem is NP-complete, investigate the problem for some important families of digraphs such as circulant digraphs, Kautz digraphs and permutation digraphs, and study the general position numbers obtained from all orientations of an undirected graph.

Introduction

The initial inspiration of the general position problem was a chessboard puzzle by the 19th century puzzle-maker Henry Dudeney
[
15
]
, which asked how many pawns can be placed on a chessboard without any three lying on a straight line. The problem was later generalised to the setting of graph theory in
[
7
,
25
]
by asking for the largest possible subset
S
of vertices of a graph
G
such that no three vertices of
S
are contained in a common shortest path of
G
. The number of vertices in such a largest subset is called the
general position number
of
G
and is denoted by
{\rm gp}(G)
. This problem has a very large literature; for a survey see
[
6
]
.

Several variations of the general position problem have been explored, including replacing ‘shortest path’ by ‘induced path’
[
30
]
, considering only shortest paths of bounded length
[
21
]
, replacing the ordinary graph distance by the Steiner distance
[
20
]
, finding smallest maximal general position sets
[
13
]
, sets of edges in general position
[
26
]
, and the mutual-visibility problem, in which for any
u,v\in S
we require only the existence of at least one shortest
u,v
-path that does not pass through a third vertex of
S

[
12
]
. The contribution of this paper is to extend the general position problem to a different type of network, namely directed graphs, in which each link of the network is assigned a direction.

A directed graph
D
, or digraph for short, consists of a set
V(D)
of vertices and a set of ordered pairs
A(D)
called arcs. The
order
of
D
is
n=|V(D)|
and the
size
is
m=|A(D)|
. If there is an arc
(u,v)
in
A(D)
we write
u\rightarrow v
and say that
v
is
adjacent from

u
and
u
is
adjacent to

v
. We exclude loops
u\rightarrow u
and multiple arcs
u\rightarrow v
with the same direction, as they add nothing of interest for our problem, i.e. we consider only
simple
digraphs. However we do allow 2-cycles, i.e. we may have
u\rightarrow v
and
v\rightarrow u
. A digraph without 2-cycles is an
oriented graph
. An undirected graph
G
is a set of vertices
V(G)
together with a collection of unordered pairs
E(G)
called edges, and we write
u\sim v
if
uv
is an edge in
E(G)
.

For a subset
S
of
V(D)
the
induced subdigraph

\langle S\rangle
of
D
is the digraph with vertex set
S
with an arc
u\rightarrow v
for
u,v\in S
if and only if
u\rightarrow v
in
D
. Associated with any digraph
D
is an underlying undirected graph on the same vertex set in which there is an edge
u\sim v
if and only if
D
contains at least one of the arcs
u\rightarrow v
or
v\rightarrow u
. Conversely, assigning directions to each of the edges of an undirected graph
G
yields an
orientation

G^{\rightarrow}
of
G
. Hence a graph is oriented if and only if it is an orientation of an undirected graph. The digraph
\hat{D}
obtained by reversing the orientation of all arcs of
D
is the
converse
of
D
.

The
out-neighbourhood

N^{+}(u)
of a vertex
u
of
D
is defined to be the set
\{v\in V(D):u\rightarrow v\}
and the
in-neighbourhood

N^{-}(u)
of
u
is
\{v\in V(D):v\rightarrow u\}
. For
S\subseteq V(D)
we set
N^{+}(S)=\bigcup_{v\in S}N^{+}(v)
and
N^{-}(S)=\bigcup_{v\in S}N^{-}(v)
. The
out-degree

d^{+}(u)
and
in-degree

d^{-}(u)
are defined by
d^{+}(u)=|N^{+}(u)|
and
d^{-}(u)=|N^{-}(u)|
. A vertex

with out-degree

is a
sink
, whereas if
d^{-}(u)=0
, then
u
is a
source
. A vertex
u
is a
transmitter
if
d^{-}(u),d^{+}(u)>0
and for any
x\in N^{-}(u)
and
y\in N^{+}(u)
we have
x\rightarrow y
. A vertex is
extreme
if it is either a source, sink or transmitter, and we denote the set of all extreme vertices of
D
by
{\rm Ext}(D)
. A vertex that is incident with just one arc is a
leaf
and the number of leaves in
D
is
\ell(D)
. In an undirected graph the neighbourhood
N(u)
of a vertex
u
is
\{v\in V(G):v\sim u\}
and the degree
d(u)
is
|N(u)|
. A vertex of degree 1 is a leaf and a vertex adjacent to all other vertices of
G
is a
universal vertex
.

A directed
u,v
-path in
D
is a sequence of distinct vertices
u_{0}=u,u_{1},\ldots,u_{\ell}=v
, where
u_{i}\rightarrow u_{i+1}
for
0\leq i<\ell
, and
\ell
is the length of the path. The
complete digraph

K_{n}
is the simple digraph with
n
vertices with all
2\binom{n}{2}
possible arcs present. An orientation of a complete graph is a
tournament
. A cycle of length
\ell
in a digraph
D
is a sequence
u_{0},u_{1},\ldots,u_{\ell-1}
of distinct vertices such that
u_{i}\rightarrow u_{i+1}
for
0\leq i\leq\ell-2
and
u_{\ell-1}\rightarrow u_{0}
. If
D
has no cycles then it is
acyclic
. The directed cycle
Z_{n}
is the digraph with vertex set
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
with an arc
i\rightarrow i+1
for each
i\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}
. The
join

D_{1}\vee D_{2}
of two digraphs
D_{1}
and
D_{2}
is the digraph formed from the disjoint union of
D_{1}
and
D_{2}
by adding all possible arcs from
D_{1}
to
D_{2}
(note that, unlike the undirected case, the order of the join operation for digraphs is important).

The distance
d(u,v)
between two vertices is the length of a shortest directed
u,v
-path in
D
. We also call a shortest path a
geodesic
. Note that we may have
d(u,v)\neq d(v,u)
, so that the directed distance is not in general a metric. If
W,W^{\prime}
are subsets of
V(D)
, then we set

to be the length of a shortest path that begins in
W
and terminates in
W^{\prime}
. If
D
contains a directed path from
u
to
v
, we say that
u

reaches

v
. If there is a pair
u,v
for which
u
cannot reach
v
, then we set
d(u,v)=\infty
(and similarly for subsets
W,W^{\prime}
we set
d(W,W^{\prime})=\infty
if no
w^{\prime}\in W^{\prime}
is reachable from any
w\in W
). If there is a directed walk from
u
to
v
for any pair of distinct vertices of
D
, then

is
strongly connected
or
strong
; then the value of
\max\{d(u,v):u,v\in V(D)\}
is the
diameter

{\rm diam}(D)
of

. If
D
is not strongly connected, then the diameter of
D
will be infinite. We will denote by
{\rm diam}^{*}(D)
the length of a longest geodesic in
D
; observe that
{\rm diam}^{*}(D)
is always finite.

A maximal strong subdigraph is a
strong component
of
D
, and the strong components partition the vertex set
V(D)
. Equivalently, one can introduce an equivalence relation
R
on
V(D)
by setting
uRv
if
u
reaches
v
and
v
reaches
u
, and the strong components are the equivalence classes for
R
. The connected components of the underlying undirected graph of
D
are called the
weak components
of
D
. The
condensation
of a digraph
D
is the digraph with the strong components of
D
as vertices, with an arc between strong components
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
if and only if there is an arc from
S_{1}
to
S_{2}
in
D
. Any condensation is acyclic.

A subdigraph
D^{\prime}
of
D
is a digraph such that
V(D^{\prime})\subseteq V(D)
and
A(D^{\prime})\subseteq A(D)
. A subdigraph
D^{\prime}
is
isometric
if for any any pair
u,v\in V(D^{\prime})
the distance from
u
to
v
is the same in
D^{\prime}
as it is in
D
. A digraph is
transitive
if
x\rightarrow y
and
y\rightarrow z
implies the existence of an arc
x\rightarrow z
. The strong components of a transitive digraph are all complete digraphs. An undirected graph is a
comparability graph
if it admits a transitive orientation. A subdigraph
D^{\prime}
of
D
is
convex
if for any
u,v\in V(D^{\prime})
and any shortest
u,v
-path in
D
, all vertices of
P
lie in
D^{\prime}
. A subset
S
of the vertex set of a digraph
D
is
independent
if there are no arcs between vertices of
S
, and the
independence number

\alpha(D)
of
D
is the number of vertices in a largest independent set of
D
(with a similar definition for graphs).

If
a,b\in\mathbb{N}
, then we set
[a]=\{1,\ldots,a\}
and denote by
[a,b]
the set
\{n\in\mathbb{N}:a\leq n\leq b\}
. Any such set
[a,b]
is an
interval
. The falling factorial is
(x)_{r}=x(x-1)\ldots(x-(r-1))
. We will write an undirected path of order
n
as
P_{n}
. If a graph
G
has a path

that passes through all vertices of
G
, then

is a
Hamilton path
and
G
is
traceable
. If
X,Y\subseteq V(G)
,
(X,Y)
represents the edges of
G
with an endpoint in
X
and an endpoint in
Y
. A
split
in a graph

is a partition
V(G)=X\cup Y
such that
G
contains all possible edges between

and
Y
.

The plan of this paper is as follows. In Section
2

2
Preliminary results

We begin by defining the general position number for directed graphs by analogy with the undirected case. An example of a general position set is shown in Figure
1
.

Definition 2.1
.

For a digraph
D
, a subset
S\subseteq V(D)
is in general position if for any
u,v\in S
, no shortest
u,v
-path in
D
passes through a vertex in
S-\{u,v\}
. The number of vertices in a largest general position set of
D
is the
general position number

{\rm gp}(D)
of
D
, and such a largest set is a
{\rm gp}
-set
of
D
.

Figure 1:
A
{\rm gp}
-set in a digraph (vertices in red)

The recent paper
[
29
]
of Stojanović investigates the related mutual visibility problem in directed graphs. A subset
S\subseteq V(G)
of a connected undirected graph
G
is a
mutual visibility set
if for any
u,v\in S
there exists a shortest
u,v
-path that does not contain any vertex of
S-\{u,v\}
; and the
mutual visibility number

\mu(G)
of
G
is the number of vertices in a largest mutual visibility set of
G
, see
[
12
]
. Any general position set of
G
is also a mutual visibility set, and so
{\rm gp}(G)\leq\mu(G)
for connected graphs. There are two possible definitions of a mutual visibility set for a disconnected graph: either one could define it to be the maximum mutual visibility number over the components, or else the sum of the mutual visibility number of the components. The latter definition ensures that the inequality
{\rm gp}(G)\leq\mu(G)
continues to hold for disconnected graphs. A similar issue arises in directed graphs that are not strongly connected, and we now distinguish the two possible definitions.

Definition 2.2
.

•

A subset
S\subseteq V(D)
of a digraph is a
strong mutual visibility set
if for each ordered pair
u,v\in S
there exists a shortest
u,v
-path in
D
that does not contain any vertices of
S-\{u,v\}
.

•

A subset
S\subseteq V(D)
is a
weak mutual visibility set
if for each ordered pair
u,v\in S
either
d(u,v)=\infty
or there exists a shortest
u,v
-path that does not contain any vertices of
S-\{u,v\}
.

The ‘strong’ definition is that of Stojanović. For our purposes we prefer the ‘weak’ definition, since it ensures that the inequality
{\rm gp}(D)\leq\mu(D)
holds for all digraphs. Also the ‘strong’ mutual visibility number can be found by taking the maximum of the ‘weak’ mutual visibility number over the strong components of
D
. Observe that the two definitions coincide for strong digraphs. We will not treat mutual visibility further here, except for the following realisation result to compare the two parameters. Note that as the constructions are strong we do not distinguish between strong and weak.

Theorem 2.3
.

For any
b\geq a\geq 2
there exists a strong digraph
D
with
{\rm gp}(D)=a
and
\mu(D)=b
.

Proof.

As noted above, we have
{\rm gp}(D)\leq\mu(D)
for any digraph. If
a=b
, then the complete digraph
K_{a}
suffices. When
b>a
we use the following construction. Let
K(r)
be the digraph with vertex set
\{u_{i},v_{i}:i\in\mathbb{Z}_{r+1}\}
with all four arcs from
\{u_{i},v_{i}\}
to
\{u_{i+1},v_{i+1}\}
for
i\in\mathbb{Z}_{r+1}
. For
a\geq 2
we have
{\rm gp}(K(a))=2
and
\mu(K(a))=a
, so we can assume that
a\geq 3
. Now consider the complete tripartite graph
K_{s,1,1}
with partite set
\{x\}
,
\{y\}
and
Z=\{z_{1},\ldots,z_{s}\}
, where
s\geq 1
. Orient the edges in
(x,Z)
towards
Z
, the edges in
(y,Z)
towards
y
and the edge
xy
in the direction
y\rightarrow x
. Finally, for
r\geq 2,s\geq 1
the graph
K(r,s)
is formed by identifying the vertex
x
and the two vertices
u_{0},v_{0}
from
K(r)
into a single vertex. An example can be seen in Figure
2
. It is easily verified that
{\rm gp}(K(r,s))=2+s
and
\mu(K(r,s))=r+s
. Hence for
3\leq a<b
, the digraph
K(b-a+2,a-2)
has the required parameters.
∎

We note that, whilst showing the existence of an undirected graph with
{\rm gp}(G)=a
and
\mu(G)=b
for
4\leq a\leq b
is not difficult, it is an open problem whether there exist undirected graphs with general position number 3 and arbitrarily large mutual visibility number, so it is interesting that the proof is simple in the directed setting.

We now give a trivial upper and lower bound on the general position number of digraphs.

Proposition 2.4
.

If
D
is a digraph with order
n\geq 2
, then
2\leq{\rm gp}(D)\leq n
.

There are very few undirected graphs with general position number 2 (
C_{4}
,
2K_{1}
and the path graphs
P_{n}
for
n\geq 2
), but, as we will see in Section
4
, any graph with a Hamilton path has an orientation with general position number 2. Similarly, an undirected graph
G
satisfies
{\rm gp}(G)=n
if and only if
G
is a complete graph or disjoint union of complete graphs, but whilst undirected graphs meeting the upper bound are scarce, there are many digraphs in which all vertices are contained in a
{\rm gp}
-set.

Proposition 2.5
.

If
S
is a general position set of a digraph
D
, then the induced subdigraph
\langle S\rangle
is transitive.

Proof.

If
\langle S\rangle
is not transitive, then
S
contains vertices
x,y,z
such that
D
has arcs
x\rightarrow y
and
y\rightarrow z
, but does not contain the arc
x\rightarrow z
. This would imply that the directed path
x,y,z
is a shortest path in
D
containing three vertices of
S
, a contradiction.
∎

Corollary 2.6
.

For any digraph
D
with order
n\geq 2
,
{\rm gp}(D)=n
if and only if
D
is transitive.

Proof.

That
{\rm gp}(D)=n
implies that
D
is transitive follows from Proposition
2.5
. Conversely, if
D
is transitive, then
D
has no geodesic with length greater than 1, and so
V(D)
is in general position.
∎

We now provide a couple of elementary observations on general position sets in a digraph
D
, before building up to a characterisation of the structure of general position sets in digraphs. Firstly, no extreme vertex of
D
can be an internal vertex of a geodesic of
D
, so that the set of all extreme vertices of
D
is in general position.

Lemma 2.7
.

For any digraph
D
,
{\rm gp}(D)\geq|{\rm Ext}(D)|
.

We can sometimes improve on the bound of Lemma
2.7
using the neighbours of extreme vertices. We define a
near-sink
of
D
to be a vertex that is not a sink, but all of its out-neighbours are sinks, and similarly a vertex is a
near-source
if it is not a source, but all of its in-neighbours are sources. By
{\rm Sink}(D)
,
{\rm Sink^{\prime}}(D)
,
{\rm Source}(D)
and
{\rm Source^{\prime}}(D)
we denote respectively the sets of sinks, near-sinks, sources, and near-sources of
D
. It is easily seen that the following unions are in general position.

Lemma 2.8
.

For any digraph
D
, the sets
{\rm Sink^{\prime}}(D)\cup{\rm Sink}(D)
and
{\rm Source^{\prime}}(D)\cup{\rm Source}(D)
are in general position.

If
S
is in general position in
D
, then it remains in general position if we reverse all of the arcs.

Observation 2.9
.

Let
D
be a digraph and let
\hat{D}
be its converse. Then
{\rm gp}(D)={\rm gp}(\hat{D})
.

Proof.

Let
S
be a general position set in
D
and suppose that
S
is not in general position in
\hat{D}
. Then there are vertices
u,v,w\in V(D)
and a shortest
u,v
-path
P
in
\hat{D}
that passes through
w
. Then the converse
\hat{P}
of
P
would be a shortest
v,u
-path in
D
passing through
w
, a contradiction.
∎

Lemma 2.10
.

The general position number of any digraph
D
with order
n
is bounded above by

{\rm gp}(D)\leq n-\rm diam^{*}(D)+1.

Proof.

Let
u_{0},u_{1},\ldots,u_{\rm diam^{*}(D)}
be a longest geodesic in
D
. Any general position set
S
can contain at most two vertices from this path, so there are at least
{\rm diam}^{*}(D)-1
vertices of the path that do not lie in
S
.
∎

Corollary 2.11
.

The general position number of a strong oriented graph
D
with order
n\geq 4
is at most
n-2
.

Proof.

If
D
has diameter greater than or equal to
3
, then the result follows from Lemma
2.10
, so we can assume that
{\rm diam}(D)=2
. For
n\geq 2
, a strong transitive digraph is complete, so by Corollary
2.6
we have
{\rm gp}(D)\leq n-1
. Suppose that
S=V(D)-\{x\}
is a general position set of order
n-1
of such a digraph. As
n\geq 4
and
{\rm diam}(D)=2
, there is a vertex
y\in\{x\}\cup N^{+}(x)
with at least two out-neighbours
y_{1},y_{2}
at distance
d(x,y)+1
from
x
. As there is at most one arc between
y_{1}
and
y_{2}
, we can assume that
y_{1}\not\rightarrow y_{2}
, and so to avoid having three vertices of
S
on a shortest
y_{1},y_{2}
-path, the unique shortest
y_{1},y_{2}
-path must be
y_{1},x,y_{2}
. If
y=x
this is impossible, and if
x\rightarrow y
this contradicts
d(x,y_{2})=2
. Thus
{\rm gp}(D)\leq n-2
.
∎

Corollary
2.11
is tight, since the digraph with vertex set
\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n-2}\}\cup\{y,z\}
and arcs
z\rightarrow y
and
y\rightarrow x_{i},x_{i}\rightarrow z
for
1\leq i\leq n-2
has general position number
n-2
.

Theorem 2.12
.

If
D
is a digraph with
\rm diam^{*}(D)\leq 3
, then
{\rm gp}(D)\geq\alpha(D)
.

Proof.

Let
D
have
{\rm diam}^{*}(D)\leq 3
and let
A
be a largest independent set of
D
. If
P
is a shortest
u,v
-path for
u,v\in A
, then any internal vertex of
P
is a neighbour of either
u
or
v
and hence cannot belong to
A
by the independence property.
∎

Convex subdigraphs can be used to give upper and lower bounds for the general position number of a digraph. Firstly, if
D^{\prime}
is a convex subdigraph of
D
, and
u,v\in V(D^{\prime})
, the class of shortest
u,v
-paths in
D^{\prime}
coincides with the class of shortest
u,v
-paths in
D
. It follows that a subset
S\subseteq V(D^{\prime})
is in general position in
D^{\prime}
if and only if it is in general position in
D
.

Lemma 2.13
.

If
D^{\prime}
is a convex subdigraph of a digraph
D
, then
{\rm gp}(D^{\prime})\leq{\rm gp}(D)
.

We now prove a directed version of the ‘Isometric Cover Lemma’ from
[
25
]
. A set of isometric subdigraphs
\{D_{1},D_{2},\ldots,D_{k}\}
of a digraph
D
is an
isometric cover
of
D
if
\cup_{i=1}^{k}V(D_{i})=V(D)
.

Theorem 2.14
.

If
\{D_{1},D_{2},\dots,D_{k}\}
is an isometric cover of a digraph
D
, then
{\rm gp}(D)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}{\rm gp}(D_{i})
.

Proof.

Let
S
be a
{\rm gp}
-set of
D
and set
S_{i}=S\cap V(D_{i})
for

i\in[k]
. If
S_{i}
is not in general position in the isometric subdigraph
D_{i}
, then it would not be in general position in
D
. As any subset of
S
is in general position in
D
, it follows that
S_{i}
is in general position in
D_{i}
. Hence
{\rm gp}(D_{i})\geq|S_{i}|
for
i\in[k]
, and

|S|\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}|S_{i}|\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}{\rm gp}(D_{i}).

∎

We now describe the effect of adding a source or sink vertex to the digraph; this will be useful when we discuss tournaments.

Lemma 2.15
.

The general position of the join of two digraphs
D_{1}
and
D_{2}
is given by

{\rm gp}(D_{1}\vee D_{2})={\rm gp}(D_{1})+{\rm gp}(D_{2}).

Proof.

Both

and

are convex subdigraphs of

, so

by Theorem

be a largest general position set of

for

is a general position set of

, since each vertex of

is at distance 1 from

and no vertex of

.
∎

In particular, the digraph formed from

The structure of general position sets in undirected graphs was characterised in
[

]
. We now generalise this characterisation to directed graphs.

Theorem 2.16
.

Let
S
be a vertex subset of the digraph
D
and denote the strong components of
\langle S\rangle
by
S_{1},\ldots,S_{r}
. Then
S
is a general position set of
D
if and only if

•

each strong component
S_{i}
is a complete digraph,

•

\{S_{1},\dots,S_{r}\}
is
distance-constant
, i.e. for any
1\leq i<j\leq r
, we have
d(u,v)=d(u^{\prime},v^{\prime})
for any
u,u^{\prime}\in S_{i}
and
v,v^{\prime}\in S_{j}
(where we allow this distance to be infinite),

•

for no
1\leq i,j,k\leq r
is it the case that

d(S_{i},S_{k})=d(S_{i},S_{j})+d(S_{j},S_{k})<\infty.

Proof.

First we show the necessity of the three conditions. Let
D
be a digraph and
S
be a general position set of
D
. By Proposition
2.5
each strong component of
\langle S\rangle
is transitive, and any strongly connected transitive digraph is complete. Now let
u,u^{\prime}\in S_{i}
and
v,v^{\prime}\in S_{j}
, where
i\neq j
. As
\langle S_{j}\rangle
is a complete digraph, the distance
d(u,v)
is finite if and only if
d(u,v^{\prime})
is finite. Suppose that
d(u,v)<d(u,v^{\prime})
. As there is an arc
v\rightarrow v^{\prime}
in
\langle S_{j}\rangle
, we must have
d(u,v^{\prime})=d(u,v)+1
and there is a shortest
u,v^{\prime}
-path passing through
v
, a contradiction. Therefore
u
has the same distance to all vertices of
S_{j}
, and a similar argument shows that each vertex of
S_{j}
is at the same distance from each vertex of
S_{i}
. Finally, suppose that
d(S_{i},S_{k})=d(S_{i},S_{j})+d(S_{j},S_{k})<\infty
for some
i,j,k
. Then as
\{S_{1},\dots,S_{r}\}
is distance-constant there are
u\in S_{i}
,
v\in S_{j}
and
w\in S_{k}
such that
d(u,v)=d(S_{i},S_{j})
,
d(v,w)=d(S_{j},S_{k})
and
d(u,w)=d(S_{i},S_{k})
and it follows that
D
would contain a shortest
u,w
-path that passes through
v
, a contradiction.

Now we prove sufficiency of the three conditions. Let
S\subseteq V(D)
satisfy the three conditions and suppose for a contradiction that there are
u,v,w\in S
such that
D
contains a shortest
u,w
-path passing through
v
. As each strong component of
\langle S\rangle
is complete,
u
and
w
do not lie in the same strong component, for otherwise they would be at distance 1 from each other. If we have
u,v\in S_{i}
and
w\in S_{j}
or
u\in S_{i}
and
v,w\in S_{j}
for some
i\neq j
, then this would contradict the distance-constant property. Finally, if
u,v
and
w
all lie in different strong components, then this would contradict the third property.
∎

When applied to an undirected graph, Theorem

For any oriented graph

Theorem 2.18
.

Oriented-GP
is NP-complete even for oriented graphs
D
with

\rm diam^{*}(D)=3
.

Proof.

We note that
Oriented-GP
is in NP since, given a subset
S
of
k^{\prime}
vertices of the input graph, it is possible to check in polynomial time if
S
is in general position. Let
I=(G,k)
be an instance of
Independent Set
. Note that
Independent Set
is the same problem as the
Clique
problem in the complement graph, which is NP-complete
[
19
]
. We construct an instance
I^{\prime}=(D,k^{\prime})
of
Oriented-GP
such that
I
has a yes answer if and only if
I^{\prime}
has a yes answer.

For a digraph
D
with order
n
and no isolated vertices we define the digraph
\Phi(D)
as follows. We add to
D
a set
V^{(1)}\cup V^{(2)}\cup V^{(3)}
of vertices, where

for

, as well as the arcs
v^{1}\rightarrow v^{2}
,
v^{2}\rightarrow v^{3}
and
v^{2}\rightarrow v
for all

. Finally for each pair

we add the arc
u^{2}\rightarrow v
if and only if
u
reaches
v
in
D
and
d_{D}(u,v)\geq 2
. As the vertices
u^{1},u^{3}
are the only leaves in
\Phi(D)
, it is simple to recover
D
from
\Phi(D)
. See Figure
3
for two examples of the operation
\Phi
together with largest general position sets of the resulting digraphs. Observe that
\rm diam^{*}(\Phi(D))=\max\{3,\rm diam^{*}(D)\}
.

Let
G
be any connected graph with order
n
and set
k^{\prime}=2n+4+k
. Let
G^{\prime}=G\vee K_{2}
, where
V(K_{2})=\{x,y\}
. Note that
\alpha(G^{\prime})=\alpha(G)
. Now form a digraph
D^{\prime}
from
G^{\prime}
with arcs
x\rightarrow y
and
u\rightarrow x
,
y\rightarrow u
for all

, and orient the edges in

at random. We have
{\rm diam}(D^{\prime})\leq 3
, so
\rm diam^{*}(\Phi(D^{\prime}))=3
.

We now show that
{\rm gp}(\Phi(D^{\prime}))=2(n+2)+\alpha(D^{\prime})=2n+4+\alpha(G)
. Note that if
A
is a largest independent set of

(and hence

is also a largest independent set of

), then

is a largest independent set of

, and so by Theorem

we have
{\rm gp}(\Phi(D^{\prime}))\geq 2(n+2)+\alpha(D^{\prime})
. For the converse, let
S
be a largest general position set of
\Phi(D^{\prime})
. Suppose that
v^{2}\in S
for some vertex
v\in V(D^{\prime})
. Then as
v^{1},v^{2},v^{3}
is a shortest path in
\Phi(D^{\prime})
, we cannot have both
v^{1},v^{3}\in S
. Thus we can replace
v^{2}
by a vertex in
\{v^{1},v^{3}\}-S
to get a general position set of the same order. By repeating this operation, we see that we can assume that
S\cap V^{(2)}=\emptyset
. Now suppose that
u,v\in S
for some
u,v\in V(D^{\prime})
, where
u\rightarrow v
. As
u^{1},u^{2},u,v
is a shortest path in
\Phi(D^{\prime})
, we see that
u^{1}\not\in S
. Thus we can replace

in

by
u^{1}
and still have a general position set. By repeating this, we can now assume that

to be an independent set of

. Hence

and the equality is established.

Hence

, an instance of

for

has a positive answer for
\Phi(D^{\prime})
.
∎

3
Families of digraphs

In this section we explore the general position number for certain families of digraphs.

3.1
Circulant digraphs

Recall that the
circulant digraph

\Circ(n,C)
has vertex set
\mathbb{Z}_{n}=\{0,1,\ldots,n-1\}
and a directed edge from
x
to
x+c\pmod{n}
for every
x\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}
and every
c\in C
. We deal here with the special case where
C=\{1,2,\ldots,d\}
for some fixed
d\geq 1
and
d<n
, so that
\Circ(n,\{1,2,\ldots,d\})
is a
d
-regular digraph of order
n
. In the rest of this subsection we will assume that
d\geq 1
and write
n=qd+r
, where
q\geq 0
and
2\leq r\leq d+1
.

Lemma 3.1
.

The subset
S=\{0,\ldots,r-1\}
is in general position in
\Circ(n,\{1,2,\ldots,d\})
.

Proof.

Suppose that there is a shortest path
P
in
\Circ(n,\{1,2,\ldots,d\})
containing three distinct vertices
i,j,k\in S=\{0,\ldots,r-1\}
, where
i<j<k
. Observe that
d(i,j)=d(i,k)=d(j,k)=1
, the diameter is
{\rm diam}(\Circ(n,\{1,2,\ldots,d\}))=q+1
and
d(j,i)=d(k,i)=d(k,j)=q+1
. Hence
P
must be a shortest
k,i
-path through
j
, a shortest
k,j
-path through
i
or a shortest
j,i
-path through
k
, but by the foregoing observation each of these paths would have length
q+2
, a contradiction. Thus
S
is in general position.
∎

Theorem 3.2
.

The general position number of
\Circ(n,\{1,2,\ldots,d\})
satisfies

r\leq{\rm gp}(\Circ(n,\{1,2,\ldots,d\}))\leq d+1.

When
n\equiv 1\pmod{d}
,
{\rm gp}(\Circ(n,\{1,2,\ldots,d\}))=d+1
.

Proof.

The lower bound comes from Lemma
3.1
. For the upper bound, let
S^{\prime}
be a largest
{\rm gp}
-set. If no two elements of
S^{\prime}
are congruent
\pmod{d}
, then
|S^{\prime}|\leq d
. Thus we can assume that
S^{\prime}
contains distinct vertices
i,j
, where
0\leq i<j\leq n-1
, such that
i\equiv j\pmod{d}
. Denote by
Z
the cycle subdigraph of
{\rm gp}(\Circ(n,\{1,2,\ldots,d\}))
obtained by including only arcs associated with the generator
1
. Choose a congruent pair
i,j
so that the distance
d_{Z}(i,j)
in
Z
is as small as possible. Then by symmetry we can assume that
i=0
and
j=j^{\prime}d
for some
j^{\prime}
. Suppose that
k\in S^{\prime}\cap[j+1,n-1]
, where
k=q^{\prime}d+t,0\leq t\leq d-1
. Then a shortest
0,k
-path is given by
0,d,\ldots,j^{\prime}d,\ldots,q^{\prime}d
, followed by the arc
q^{\prime}d\rightarrow q^{\prime}d+t
if
t\neq 0
, which would contain three vertices of
S^{\prime}
. Therefore
S^{\prime}\subseteq[0,j]
. By the minimality of
d_{Z}(i,j)
, no vertices of
S^{\prime}-[0,j]
are congruent
\pmod{d}
and none are congruent to
0\pmod{d}
. Therefore
|S^{\prime}|\leq d+1
, establishing the upper bound. The upper and lower bounds coincide when
n\equiv 1\pmod{d}
, implying the final equality.
∎

For many values of
n
and
d
, we can give a much stronger lower bound.

Theorem 3.3
.

If
n\equiv a\pmod{d}
where
a\geq 2
,
{\rm gp}(\Circ(n,\{1,2,\ldots,d\}))\geq\left\lceil\frac{d+1}{a}\right\rceil
.

Proof.

Let
n=qd+a
. Suppose first that
d
is a multiple of
a
and set
S=\{0,a,2a,\ldots,d-a,d\}
. If
i,j\in S
, where
j>i
, then
d(i,j)=1
and
d(j,i)=q
. It is now easy to check that
S
is in general position as in the proof of Lemma
3.1
. The cases of other congruence classes of
d
mod
a
are similar; if
d\equiv b\pmod{a}
then the relevant set is
S=\{0,a,2a,\ldots,d-b\}
.
∎

Computation shows that the larger of the sets exhibited in Lemma
3.1
and Theorem
3.3
(when
n\equiv a\pmod{d},a\geq 2
) is optimal for
n\leq 50
and
d\leq 15
, and we conjecture that this holds for all

and
d
.

3.2
Kautz digraphs

Let
m\geq 3
and
k\geq 2
. The
Kautz digraph

\Ka(m,k)
has vertex set consisting of all words of length
k
over an alphabet of
m
characters, with the property that no two consecutive characters in the word are equal. Thus the order of the graph is
m(m-1)^{k-1}
. There is a directed edge from a word
a_{1}a_{2}\ldots a_{k}
to every word of the form
a_{2}\ldots a_{k}b
where
b\neq a_{k}
. Thus the graph has degree
m-1
. The Kautz digraphs are of substantial interest in the degree-diameter problem, having the smallest possible diameter amongst all digraphs of their order and degree. In the case
k=2
, the Kautz digraph
\Ka(m,2)
can be viewed as the line digraph of the complete (undirected) graph
K_{m}
.

We use the alphabet
1,2,\ldots 9,a,b,\ldots
in our examples. Figure
4
shows the Kautz digraph
\Ka(5,2)
with a maximal gp-set of cardinality 8 highlighted. In Table
1
we show the gp numbers of
\Ka(m,k)
for
k=3,4
and some small values of

, determined by computer.

Theorem 3.4
.

For
m\geq 3
,
{\rm gp}(\Ka(m,2))=\left\lfloor\frac{m^{2}}{3}\right\rfloor
.

Proof.

First we give a construction of the claimed size. Divide the alphabet of
m
characters into three parts
A_{1},A_{2},A_{3}
differing in size by at most 1 and form a digraph
K(m)
by adding every arc
x\rightarrow y
, where
x\in A_{i}
,
y\in A_{j}
,
i<j
(observe that this is an orientation of the tripartite Turán graph). Let

consist of each word
xy
where

in
K(m)

\Ka(m,2)
has diameter 2. Suppose that
P
is a path of length 2 with all vertices in

P
is of the form
xy

P
begins with the letter

, where

P
begins with the letter
z
, and there are no words in
S

cannot begin with a word

or
xz
, where
x\in A_{1},y\in A_{2},z\in A_{3}

z
.

Now we show that this construction is optimal. A computer check showed that the claimed result is true for
m=3,4
. Suppose that the result is true for
\Ka(m^{\prime},2)
for
m^{\prime}<m
and let
S^{\prime}
be a largest general position set of
\Ka(m,2)
. Suppose that
S^{\prime}
contains two words with the same letters, for example
12
and
21
. Now
S^{\prime}
cannot have any other vertices containing the letters
1
or
2
; for instance, if
31\in S
, then

would be a shortest path through three vertices of

. Therefore the remaining
{\rm gp}(\Ka(m,2))-2

lie in the isometric subdigraph of
\Ka(m,2)
induced by the words not containing the letters
1
and
2
. This subdigraph is isomorphic to
\Ka(m-2,2)
, and we conclude by induction that we would have
|S^{\prime}|\leq 2+\left\lfloor\frac{(m-2)^{2}}{3}\right\rfloor<\left\lfloor\frac{m^{2}}{3}\right\rfloor
.

Now we associate with
S^{\prime}
the
auxiliary digraph

\Omega^{\rightarrow}(S^{\prime})
with vertex set
[1,m]
and an arc
i\rightarrow j
when
ij\in S^{\prime}
. The
auxiliary graph

\Omega(S^{\prime})
is the underlying undirected graph of
\Omega^{\rightarrow}(S^{\prime})
. By the preceding argument,
\Omega^{\rightarrow}(S^{\prime})
and
\Omega(S^{\prime})
have the same size. Suppose that
|S^{\prime}|>\left\lfloor\frac{m^{2}}{3}\right\rfloor
. Then by Turán’s Theorem the graph
\Omega(S^{\prime})
must contain a copy of

. Denote the vertices of this

by
\{w,x,y,z\}

\Omega(S^{\prime})

K_{4}
cannot contain a directed triangle

, for otherwise

would be a shortest path containing three vertices of

, and cannot contain a directed path of length 3, say
x\rightarrow y\rightarrow w\rightarrow z
, for this corresponds to the shortest path

in
\Ka(m,2)
. Suppose that there is a source (say it is
x
) in
\Omega^{\rightarrow}(S^{\prime})
. Then we can assume that
y\rightarrow w
in
\Omega(S^{\prime})
. Now it is easy to see that the remaining edges cannot be oriented without creating a directed triangle or path of length 3. For the same reason there can be no sink. Hence we can assume that
x\rightarrow y
,
x\rightarrow z
and

|S^{\prime}|\leq\left\lfloor\frac{m^{2}}{3}\right\rfloor

3.3
Permutation digraphs

Let
d\geq 2
and

\Pe(d,k)

\{0,1,\ldots,d+k-1\}
, where no letter is used more than once in a word. A word
(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{k})
has an arc to any word of the form
(x_{2},\ldots,x_{k},y)

y\notin\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}\}

d

(d+k)_{k}=(d+k)(d+k-1)\cdots(d+1)

Theorem 3.5
.

For

3.4
Trees

Finding the general position of an oriented tree appears to be a difficult problem. As each leaf of a tree is a source or a sink, Lemma
2.7
implies the following bound.

Lemma 3.7
.

For any oriented tree
T^{\rightarrow}
with leaf number
\ell(T^{\rightarrow})
, the general position number of
T^{\rightarrow}
is bounded below by
{\rm gp}(T^{\rightarrow})\geq\ell(T^{\rightarrow})
.

We show that Lemma
2.8
gives the exact value of the general position number for arborescences. An
out-arborescence
rooted at
x\in V(T)
is an oriented tree
T^{\rightarrow}
such that all arcs are oriented away from
x
.

Theorem 3.8
.

If
T^{\rightarrow}
is an out-arborescence, then
{\rm gp}(T^{\rightarrow})=|{\rm Sink}(T^{\rightarrow})|+|{\rm Sink^{\prime}}(T^{\rightarrow})|
.

Proof.

Lemma
2.8
shows that
{\rm Sink}(T^{\rightarrow})\cup{\rm Sink^{\prime}}(T^{\rightarrow})
is in general position. Let
S
be any largest general position set in
T^{\rightarrow}
and suppose that there is some
y\in S-({\rm Sink}(T^{\rightarrow})\cup{\rm Sink^{\prime}}(T^{\rightarrow}))
. Let
y,y_{1},\dots,y_{t}
be a longest path in
T^{\rightarrow}
starting at
y
. Then
y_{t}
is a sink and
y_{t-1}
is a near-sink. We cannot have both
y_{t-1}
and
y_{t}
contained in
S
, for then the above path would contain three vertices of
S
. Hence we can replace
y
by one of the vertices of
\{y_{t-1},y_{t}\}-S
without creating three-in-a-line. Therefore, in at most
|S|
steps,

can be transformed into
{\rm Sink}(T^{\rightarrow})\cup{\rm Sink^{\prime}}(T^{\rightarrow})
, and it follows that the latter is a largest general position set.
∎

The analogous result for in-arborescences, sources and near-sources follows upon taking the converse. We see from Theorem

that the general position number of a tree

can be arbitrarily larger than

.

4
Orientations of graphs

For any graph
G
of order
n\geq 2
, there are different orientations of
G
that have different general position numbers. This leads to the introduction of the following definitions.

Definition 4.1
.

For a graph
G
of order
n\geq 2
, the
upper oriented general position number

{\rm gp^{\rightarrow}}(G)
of
G
is the maximum general position number of an orientation of
G
. Similarly, the
lower oriented general position number

{\rm gp_{\rightarrow}}(G)
of
G
is defined as the minimum general position number of an orientation of
G
.

Definition 4.2
.

The
general position spectrum
of a graph
G
is defined as the set

S_{{\rm gp}}(G)=\{{\rm gp}(D):D\text{ is an orientation of }G\}.

Thus
{\rm gp_{\rightarrow}}(G)=\min S_{{\rm gp}}(G)
and
{\rm gp^{\rightarrow}}(G)=\max S_{{\rm gp}}(G)
. Trivially for any graph
G
of order
n\geq 2
we have
2\leq{\rm gp_{\rightarrow}}(G)\leq{\rm gp^{\rightarrow}}(G)\leq n
.

4.1
Bounding the oriented general position numbers

Our main conjecture is that for any graph one can find orientations with different general position numbers, or equivalently the general position spectrum always has cardinality at least 2. A computer check shows that the conjecture is true for graphs with order at most 6.

Conjecture 4.3
.

For any connected graph
G
with order
n\geq 3
,
{\rm gp^{\rightarrow}}(G)>{\rm gp_{\rightarrow}}(G)
.

The upper oriented general position number can be characterised in terms of induced comparability subgraphs. Note that the comparability subgraphs in question may be disconnected.

Theorem 4.4
.

The upper oriented general position number of
G
is equal to the largest order of an induced subgraph
H
of
G
that is also a comparability graph.

Proof.

Let
G
be a graph and
D
be an orientation of
G
with largest possible general position number, with
S
a largest general position set. By Proposition
2.5
the subdigraph
\langle S\rangle
is transitive, and it follows that the subgraph
H
induced by the vertices corresponding to
S
is a comparability graph.

Conversely, let
H
be an induced comparability subgraph of
G
. Orient the edges of
H
to give a transitive orientation of
V(H)
. Then orient any edge between
V(H)
and
V(G-H)
in the direction from
V(H)
to
V(G-H)
, and orient all remaining edges at random. It is easily seen that
V(H)
is a general position set in this orientation.
∎

Theorem
4.4
also shows that the difference
n-{\rm gp^{\rightarrow}}(G)
can be arbitrarily large; as a comparability graph does not contain induced odd cycles of length 5 or more, any graph containing
r
disjoint induced odd cycles of length
\geq 5
has
{\rm gp^{\rightarrow}}(G)\leq n-r
.

Corollary 4.5
.

A graph
G
satisfies
{\rm gp^{\rightarrow}}(G)=n
if and only if
G
is a comparability graph.

It follows that
{\rm gp^{\rightarrow}}(G)=n
can be tested in polynomial time
[
27
]
. It appears that the order of a largest induced comparability subgraph has not been studied before. However, both cliques and bipartite graphs are comparability graphs, and largest independent unions of cliques
[
17
]
and largest induced bipartite subgraphs
[
10
]
have both been been studied. An important subcase of the largest induced bipartite subgraph problem is finding the largest induced forest in a graph, see
[
1
]
. For any graph
G
, we denote the number of vertices in a largest induced union of cliques of
G
(possible containing just one clique) by
\alpha^{\omega}(G)
, the number of vertices in a largest induced bipartite subgraph of

by
\alpha_{2}(G)
and the number of vertices in a largest induced forest subgraph of

by
{\rm a}(G)
. Trivially we have
\alpha_{2}(G)\geq{\rm a}(G)
.

Corollary 4.6
.

For any graph
G
,
{\rm gp^{\rightarrow}}(G)\geq\max\{\alpha^{\omega}(G),\alpha_{2}(G)\}
.

For any

there is a graph with order
n
and general position number 2, namely the path

. By contrast, using Ramsey’s Theorem and Corollary
4.6
we see that the smallest value of the general position number taken over all digraphs with order

tends to infinity with

. Since any general position set of a graph is an independent union of cliques
[
2
]
, it also follows from Corollary

that
{\rm gp^{\rightarrow}}(G)\geq{\rm gp}(G)
for any graph. Theorem
4.4
also implies a lower bound in terms of the diameter.

Corollary 4.7
.

For any graph
G
,
{\rm gp^{\rightarrow}}(G)\geq{\rm diam}(G)+1
, with equality if and only if
G
is a path. For any graph with order
n\geq 4
, it holds that
{\rm gp^{\rightarrow}}(G)\geq 4

Proof.

The lower bound
{\rm gp^{\rightarrow}}(G)\geq{\rm diam}(G)+1
follows instantly from Theorem
4.4
applied to a longest geodesic, as does the equality case when
G
is a path. Suppose that
{\rm gp^{\rightarrow}}(G)={\rm diam}(G)+1
and
G
is not a path. Let

be a longest geodesic in

not on

. However, any vertex of

must be adjacent to a vertex of

; otherwise, if

has no edge to

would be a comparability graph, a contradiction by Theorem

. Let

be the smallest value of the index

has some neighbour

By Theorem

Theorem 4.8

[

•

Now we consider an upper bound for the lower oriented general position number. The

Let

For

First we show that complete graphs are
{\rm gp}
-full, or, in other words, for any
2\leq k\leq n
there exists a tournament with order
n
and general position number
k
.

Theorem 4.21
.

Complete graphs are
{\rm gp}
-full.

Proof.

We prove the statement by induction on
n
. It is true for
n=2
. Assume that the result holds for
K_{n-1}
, i.e. for each
k
with
2\leq k\leq n-1
, there exists a tournament
D
such that
{\rm gp}(D)=k
. For each such
D
, add a source vertex adjacent to every vertex of
D
, and the resulting tournament
D^{\prime}
has
{\rm gp}(D^{\prime})=k+1
by Lemma
2.15
. As there is also a tournament
D^{\prime}
with order
n
and
{\rm gp}(D^{\prime})=2
by Lemma
4.9
, this completes the induction.
∎

In fact, the following realisation result shows that connected
{\rm gp}
-full graphs exist for any feasible order and size.

Theorem 4.22
.

For any integers
n
and
m
with
n\geq 2
and
n-1\leq m\leq\binom{n}{2}
, there exists a connected
{\rm gp}
-full graph of order
n
and size
m
.

Proof.

We prove the slightly stronger statement that for
n\geq 2
and
m
in the range
n-1\leq m\leq\binom{n}{2}
, there is a traceable graph
G
of order
n
and size
m
with spanning path
P
and an endpoint

of

such that for all
2\leq k\leq n
, there is an orientation

of
G
with general position number
k
such that

is a source or a sink. The result is true when

, as the path
P_{n}
has the required property by Lemma
4.9
. The result holds for

.

Assume that the result is true for all orders less than
n
. Suppose that

. By induction there is a graph
G^{\prime}
with order
n-1
, size
m-(n-1)
and a spanning path

, such that for all

has an orientation

with general position number
k
. Add a new universal vertex
x
to
G^{\prime}
, yielding a graph

with order
n
, size
m
, and a spanning path
P
with
x
as an endpoint. For
2\leq k\leq n-1
, the orientation

consisting of
G^{\prime}_{k}
and source

has general position number

by Lemma
2.15
. Also, as

is traceable, it has an orientation with general position number 2 and

as a source by Lemma
4.9
.

Now suppose that
n-1\leq m\leq 2n-4
. Let
G^{\prime}
be a graph with order
n-1
and size

of the required type, where

is the spanning path and
x^{\prime}
is the endpoint of

that is a source or sink in each of the orientations

. Add a leaf

to

connected to

to give a graph

with order
n
, size

and a spanning path

. For

corresponding to

.
∎

One consequence of note is that for

and
n-1\leq m\leq\binom{n}{2}
there is a connected graph with order

and size

and an orientation

.

As noted in Subsection
3.4
, finding the general position number of an oriented tree is difficult in general. We therefore confine ourselves to determining the
{\rm gp}
-spectrum of two families of trees. By a
spider
we mean a subdivision of a star in which each edge of the star has been subdivided at least once. Let
x
be the unique vertex of the spider with degree
\geq 3
.

Theorem 4.23
.

If
T
is a spider with order
n
, then
T
has
{\rm gp}
-spectrum

S_{{\rm gp}}(T)=\begin{cases}[\ell(T),n]&\text{ if }\deg(x)\text{ is even;}\\
[\ell(T)+1,n]&\text{ if }\deg(x)\text{ is odd.}\\
\end{cases}

Proof.

T
consists of
\deg(x)=\ell(T)
paths
P^{(i)}
of length
\ell(i)\geq 2
identified at the common endpoint
x
. In any orientation of
T
,
x
has either
\geq\left\lceil\frac{\ell(T)}{2}\right\rceil
out-neighbours or
\geq\left\lceil\frac{\ell(T)}{2}\right\rceil
in-neighbours; we assume the former. Then choosing any two vertices from the paths of
T
associated with out-neighbours of
x
yields a general position set of order
\geq 2\left\lceil\frac{\ell(T)}{2}\right\rceil
. If all edges of
\left\lceil\frac{\ell(T)}{2}\right\rceil
of the paths are oriented away from
x
and the edges of
\left\lfloor\frac{\ell(T)}{2}\right\rfloor
of the paths are oriented toward
x
, then this orientation has general position number exactly
\left\lceil\frac{\ell(T)}{2}\right\rceil
. Hence
{\rm gp_{\rightarrow}}(T)\geq\ell(T)+1
if
\ell(T)
is odd.

We start with the case that each path
P^{(i)}
has length 2. Write the vertex set as
\{x\}\cup\{x_{i},y_{i}:1\leq i\leq\ell(T)\}
, where
x\sim x_{i}
and
x_{i}\sim y_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq\ell(T)
. For
\left\lceil\frac{\ell(T)}{2}\right\rceil\leq t\leq\ell(T)
, let
T_{1}^{\rightarrow}
be the orientation with
x\rightarrow x_{i}\rightarrow y_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq t
and
y_{i}\rightarrow x_{i}\rightarrow x
for
t+1\leq i\leq\deg(x)
, and let
T_{2}^{\rightarrow}
be the orientation with
x\rightarrow x_{i}
and
y_{i}\rightarrow x_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq t
and
y_{i}\rightarrow x_{i}\rightarrow x
for
t+1\leq i\leq\ell(T)
. Then it is simple to verify that
{\rm gp}(T_{1}^{\rightarrow})=2t
and
{\rm gp}(T_{2}^{\rightarrow})=2t+1
.

We now use induction on the order of the spider. Suppose that the result is true for all spiders with order
<n
and let
T
be a spider with order
n
. We can assume that
T
has a path from
x
of length at least 3; let
z
be a leaf vertex of such a path with support vertex
z^{\prime}
. By induction for
\left\lceil\frac{\ell(T)}{2}\right\rceil\leq k\leq n-1
the spider
T-z
has an orientation
T^{k}
with general position number
k
. For each
k
in this range, we add the edge
z^{\prime}z
to
T^{k}
and orient the edge
z^{\prime}z
so that
z^{\prime}
is either a source or a sink. By the note after Lemma
2.15
, the resulting orientation has general position number
k+1
. As
T
has an orientation with general position number
\left\lceil\frac{\ell(T)}{2}\right\rceil
, this has established existence for all required values of the general position number, and the result follows by induction.
∎

A
caterpillar
is a tree consisting of a path
P_{r}
called the
spine
and leaves attached to the internal vertices of the spine.

Theorem 4.24
.

If
T
is a caterpillar with order
n
, then the
{\rm gp}
-spectrum of
T
is
[\ell(T),n]
.

Proof.

Denote the vertices of the path
P_{r}
by
x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{r}
and suppose that there are
s
additional leaves attached to
x_{2},\ldots,x_{r-1}
, so that
n=r+s
. By Lemma
3.7
we have
{\rm gp_{\rightarrow}}(T)\geq\ell(T)=s+2
. As
T
has diameter
r-1
, Corollary
4.13
shows that
{\rm gp_{\rightarrow}}(T)\leq(r+s)-(r-1)+1=s+2
. Therefore
{\rm gp_{\rightarrow}}(T)=\ell(T)
. This shows that the result holds when
T
is a star, so we can assume that
{\rm diam}(T)\geq 3
.

We now use induction on the order of the tree. Suppose that the result is true for all trees of order
<n
and
T
be a tree with order
n
. If
T
is a star, we are done, otherwise let
L
be the set of leaves of
T
adjacent to
x_{2}
, including
x_{1}
. Then by induction the tree
T^{\prime}=T-L
has orientations for all
{\rm gp}
-numbers between
\ell(T^{\prime})=\ell(T)-|L|+1
and order
n(T^{\prime})=n(T)-|L|
. In each of these orientations
x_{2}
will be a source or a sink, so by the note after Lemma
2.15
the set
L
can be added back to each of these orientations to give an orientation of
T
with
{\rm gp}
-number anywhere in the range
\ell(T)+1
and
n(T)
. As the existence of an orientation with
{\rm gp}
-number
\ell(T)
has been established, the proof is complete.
∎

Theorem 4.25
.

The
{\rm gp}
-spectrum of a cycle
C_{n}
is given by
[2,n]
if
n
is even and
[2,n-1]
if
n
is odd. The general position number of the directed cycle
Z_{n}
is
{\rm gp}(Z_{n})=2
for
n\geq 3
.

Proof.

We identify the vertex set of
C_{n}
with
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
in the natural fashion. As odd cycles are not comparability graphs but even cycles are, it follows from Corollary
4.5
that
{\rm gp^{\rightarrow}}(C_{n})\leq n-1
when
n
is odd and
{\rm gp^{\rightarrow}}(C_{n})=n
for even
n
. Alternatively, observe that if
n
is odd there must be two consecutive arcs with the same direction, and so
{\rm gp^{\rightarrow}}(C_{n})\leq n-1
follows from Lemma
2.10
, whereas if
n
is even the arcs can be oriented alternately to make each vertex a source or sink, and
{\rm gp^{\rightarrow}}(C_{n})=n
follows from Lemma
2.7
.

For each
1\leq j\leq n-1
with
j\equiv n+1\pmod{2}
, we denote by
Z_{n}^{(j)}
the oriented cycle with arcs
(i,i+1)
for
0\leq i\leq j-1
and the other arcs oriented alternately so that the vertices of
\mathbb{Z}_{n}-[1,j-1]
are sources or sinks. Then
{\rm gp}(Z_{n}^{(j)})\geq n-j+1
by Lemma
2.7
and since
{\rm diam}^{*}(Z_{n}^{(j)})=j
we have equality by Lemma
2.10
.

Now for each
1\leq j\leq n-1
and
j\equiv n\pmod{2}
, let
Z_{n}^{(j)}
be the oriented cycle with arcs
(0,n-1)
,
(n-1,n-2)
and
(i,i+1)
for
0\leq i\leq j-1
and all remaining edges oriented to make the vertices in
[j,n-2]
sources or sinks. The set
[j-1,n-1]
is in general position, giving
{\rm gp}(Z_{n}^{(j)})\geq n-j+1
, and again we have equality by Lemma
2.10
.

The final statement follows from Lemma
4.9
.
∎

The
{\rm gp}
-spectrum of complete multipartite graphs is difficult to find in general. However we show that if the graph is very unbalanced, then the lower oriented general position number can be arbitrarily large. We start with a lower bound on
{\rm gp_{\rightarrow}}(G)
that applies for all graphs. Two vertices
u,v
of an undirected graph
G
are
twins
if
N(u)=N(v)
. A
twin set
of
G
is a subset
S\subset V(G)
such that all vertices of
S
have the same neighbourhood
N(S)
. We extend this to directed graphs
D
by defining vertices
u,v
to be twins if
N^{+}(u)=N^{+}(v)
and
N^{-}(u)=N^{-}(v)
, and a subset
S\subset V(D)
is a twin set if all vertices of
S
have the same out-neighbourhood
N^{+}(S)
and the same in-neighbourhood
N^{-}(S)
. A twin set is easily seen to be in general position, in both the undirected and directed settings.

Lemma 4.26
.

If
G
has a twin set
S
with common neighbourhood
N(S)
, then

{\rm gp_{\rightarrow}}(G)\geq\frac{|S|}{2^{|N(S)|}}.

Proof.

Let
S
be a twin set of
G
with common neighbourhood
N(S)
. For each vertex
u\in S
, there are
2^{|N(S)|}
possible orientations of the edges from
u
to
N(S)
. By the Pigeonhole Principle, there are at least
\frac{|S|}{2^{|N(S)|}}
vertices of
S
with the same orientations of edges to
N(S)
and these vertices are a twin set in the oriented graph.
∎

Corollary 4.27
.

For
r_{1}\geq\ldots\geq r_{t}\geq 2
,
t\geq 2
, the lower oriented general position number of
K_{r_{1},\ldots,r_{t}}
is bounded below by

{\rm gp_{\rightarrow}}(K_{r_{1},\ldots,r_{t}})\geq\frac{r_{1}}{2^{r_{2}+\ldots+r_{t}}}.

Theorem 4.28
.

For
r\geq 4
, the
{\rm gp}
-spectrum of
K_{r,2}
is
[\left\lceil\frac{r}{2}\right\rceil,r+2]
.

Proof.

Denote the set of vertices of the part of order
r
by
X=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}\}
and the vertices of the smaller part by
y,z
. Let
K_{r,2}^{\rightarrow}
be any orientation of
K_{r,2}
. Taking the converse if necessary, we can assume that in
K_{r,2}^{\rightarrow}
we have
|N^{+}(y)|\geq\left\lceil\frac{r}{2}\right\rceil
. We claim that
N^{+}(y)
is in general position. This is trivial if
r\leq 4
, so we set
r\geq 5
. Suppose that there is a shortest
w_{1},w_{3}
-path
P
through
w_{2}
, where
w_{1},w_{2},w_{3}\in N^{+}(y)
. Then
P
must begin
w_{1},z,w,y
, where
w\in N^{-}(y)
; however,
y
is adjacent to both
w_{2}
and
w_{3}
, contradicting our assumption that
P
is a geodesic. Hence all orientations of
K_{r,2}
have general position number at least
\left\lceil\frac{r}{2}\right\rceil
.

Let
\left\lceil\frac{r}{2}\right\rceil\leq t\leq r
and consider the orientation in which
y\rightarrow x_{i}
and
x_{i}\rightarrow z
for
1\leq i\leq t
and
x_{j}\rightarrow y
and
z\rightarrow x_{j}
for
t+1\leq j\leq r
. If
t=r
, then the orientation has general position number
r+1
(
X\cup\{y\}
is in general position), whilst the upper bound follows from Lemma
2.10
, so we assume that
t<r
. For
1\leq i_{1},i_{2}\leq t
and
t+1\leq j_{1},j_{2}\leq r
there are shortest paths
x_{i_{1}},z,x_{j_{1}},y,x_{i_{2}}
and
x_{j_{1}},y,x_{i_{1}},z,x_{j_{2}}
, and a routine argument shows that the largest general position set has order
t
.

Finally, the orientation in which
x_{1},\ldots,x_{r-1}
are sinks and
x_{r}
is a source has general position number
r
, whilst there is an orientation with general position number
r+2
by Corollary
4.5
.
∎

Theorem 4.29
.

The complete bipartite graph
K_{n,n}
is
{\rm gp}
-full for
n\geq 2
.

Proof.

Write the vertex set of
K_{n,n}
as
X\cup Y
, where
X=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}
and
Y=\{y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}\}
. For even
0\leq r\leq n-2
, define the orientation
K^{(1)}(n)_{r}
of
K_{n,n}
as follows: the arcs directed from
Y
to
X
are:

•

y_{i}\rightarrow x_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq n
,

•

y_{i}\rightarrow x_{j}
when
r+1\leq j\leq i-2
,

•

y_{i}\rightarrow x_{j}
when
1\leq i\leq r
,
i
is odd and
j\geq i+2
, and

•

y_{i}\rightarrow x_{j}
when
1\leq i\leq r
,
i
is even and
j\geq i+1
.

All other arcs are directed from
X
to
Y
. Observe that for odd
i\leq r
, the subdigraph induced by
\{x_{i},x_{i+1},y_{i},y_{i+1}\}
is isometric, as is the subdigraph induced by
\{x_{r+1},\ldots,x_{n}\}\cup\{y_{r+1},\ldots,y_{n}\}
. Each of these subdigraphs has general position number 2 (observing that the last of these has the orientation defined by Lemma
4.9
), so by Theorem
2.14
we have
{\rm gp}(K^{(1)}(n)_{r})\leq r+2
. The set
\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}\}\cup\{y_{r+1},y_{r+2}\}
is in general position, so in fact
{\rm gp}(K^{(1)}(n)_{r})=r+2
. This covers all even values in the range
[2,n]
. A similar idea works for the odd values. In the previous construction, when
0\leq r\leq n-3
is even, change the direction of the arc
x_{r+1}\rightarrow y_{r+2}
to
y_{r+2}\rightarrow x_{r+1}
. Lemma
4.9
and Theorem
2.14
show that the general position number of this new orientation is at most
r+3
, and
\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{r+2}\}\cup\{y_{r+1}\}
is a general position set of that order.

For even
r
in the range
0\leq r\leq n-2
we now derive an orientation
K^{(2)}(n)_{r}
from
K^{(1)}(n)_{r}
by orienting each arc between
\{y_{r+1},\ldots,y_{n}\}
and
\{x_{r+1},\ldots,x_{n}\}
towards
X
, i.e.
y_{i}\rightarrow x_{j}
is an arc for
r+1\leq i,j\leq n
. The subdigraph induced by
\{x_{r+1},\ldots,x_{n}\}\cup\{y_{r+1},\ldots,y_{n}\}
is isometric and has general position number
2(n-r)
(as each vertex is a source or sink), and so Theorem
2.14
gives
{\rm gp}(K^{(2)}(n)_{r})\leq 2n-r
, and
X\cup\{y_{r+1},\ldots,y_{n}\}
is a general position set, so that equality holds. This gives all even values of the general position number in the range
[n+1,2n]
. As in the previous case, changing the arc
y_{r+1}\rightarrow x_{r+1}
to
x_{r+1}\rightarrow y_{r+1}
deals with the odd values in the range.
∎

For all the graphs for which we have computed the
{\rm gp}
-spectrum, the spectrum is an interval. We conjecture that this holds generally.

Conjecture 4.30
.

The general position spectrum of any graph is an interval.

5
Conclusion

We conclude with some open problems suggested by this research. One possible direction for investigation is to restrict the orientations considered in Conjecture
4.3
. It is easy to find families of graphs such that all strong orientations, or all source- and sink-free orientations, have the same general position number.

Problem 5.1
.

Characterise the graphs with the property that all strong orientations (or all source- and sink-free orientations) have the same general position number.

It would be especially interesting to find exact values for trees, permutation digraphs and Kautz digraphs for
k\geq 3
.

Problem 5.2
.

Find a formula for the general position number of any tree, as well as permutation digraphs and Kautz digraphs for
k\geq 3
.

Regarding complexity, we have shown in Theorem
2.18
that finding the general position number of an oriented graph is NP-complete. However, except for the fact that
{\rm gp^{\rightarrow}}(G)=n
can be tested in polynomial time, we have not considered the complexity of finding the
{\rm gp}
-spectrum. We conjecture that it is hard even to find the lower oriented general position number.

Conjecture 5.3
.

It is NP-hard to decide if a graph
G
satisfies
{\rm gp_{\rightarrow}}(G)=2
.

There are many variants of the general position problem, including monophonic position and mobile general position
[
6
]
. We suggest that it would be rewarding to investigate directed versions of these problems. Finally, we note that a logical next step would be to define and investigate the general position problem in signed graphs.

Problem 5.4
.

Investigate the general position problem for signed graphs.
