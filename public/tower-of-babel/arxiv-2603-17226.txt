Difference-Based High-Dimensional Long-Run Covariance Matrix Estimation for Mean-shift Time Series
Yanhong Liu, Fengyi Song, Long Feng

Abstract
We consider estimation of high-dimensional long-run covariance matrices for time series with nonconstant means, a setting in which conventional estimators can be severely biased. To address this difficulty, we propose a difference-based initial estimator that is robust to a broad class of mean variations, and combine it with hard thresholding, soft thresholding, and tapering to obtain sparse long-run covariance estimators for high-dimensional data. We derive convergence rates for the resulting estimators under general temporal dependence and time-varying mean structures, showing explicitly how the rates depend on covariance sparsity, mean variation, dimension, and sample size. Numerical experiments show that the proposed methods perform favorably in high dimensions, especially when the mean evolves over time.



Keywords:
difference-based estimator; long-run covariance matrix; high dimension; thresholding; max-norm convergence; nonstationary mean.

1
Introduction

Let
\{\bm{X}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{n}
be a
p
-dimensional time series. A fundamental second-order object in time series analysis is the long-run covariance matrix

\mathbf{V}:=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}\mathbf{\Gamma}_{k},\ \mathbf{\Gamma}_{k}:=\mathrm{Cov}(\bm{Z}_{0},\bm{Z}_{k}),

where
\{\bm{Z}_{t}\}
denotes the latent stationary noise component. When the spectral density matrix
f_{\bm{Z}}(\omega)
exists,
\mathbf{V}=2\pi f_{\bm{Z}}(0)
, so
\mathbf{V}
is precisely the zero-frequency summary of temporal dependence. This matrix plays a central role in statistical inference for temporally dependent data, including inference for sample means and linear contrasts, change-point analysis, trend inference, simultaneous confidence bands, and many other procedures driven by partial sums or temporally aggregated quantities
(
23
;
1
;
10
;
11
)
. In low-dimensional settings, long-run variance or long-run covariance estimation has been extensively studied through heteroskedasticity and autocorrelation consistent (HAC) estimators and related kernel or bandwidth-based procedures
(
23
;
1
)
.

Classical HAC methodology is typically developed under the implicit premise that the observed series can be globally centered without materially distorting the serial dependence structure. This paradigm is adequate when the mean is stable, but it can break down when the data contain smooth trends, abrupt level shifts, or multiple change points. In such settings, naive centering may contaminate sample autocovariances, thereby leading to seriously biased long-run variance estimates. To overcome this difficulty,
10
and
11
developed mean-structure-robust, difference-based estimators that remain valid under unknown trends and even a possibly divergent number of breaks. However, that literature is essentially low-dimensional and does not address the additional regularization required when the ambient dimension
p
is comparable to or much larger than the sample size
n
.

Meanwhile, the high-dimensional covariance literature has largely evolved from the i.i.d. setting. It is now well understood that the sample covariance matrix is no longer a reliable estimator when
p
is large relative to
n
, which has led to a rich body of work on regularized covariance estimation. Representative examples include hard thresholding
(
4
)
, generalized thresholding and shrinkage
(
29
)
, adaptive thresholding
(
5
)
, and tapering-based estimators
(
6
)
. These methods exploit sparsity or approximate sparsity of the target covariance matrix and have become standard tools in modern high-dimensional inference. However, they are designed primarily for contemporaneous covariance matrices under independence, and therefore do not directly address the cumulative serial dependence encoded in
\mathbf{V}
.

A growing literature extends high-dimensional second-order estimation to temporally dependent observations, but the main target in that line of work is still the contemporaneous covariance matrix
\mathbf{\Sigma}=\mathrm{Cov}(\bm{Z}_{t})
or its inverse, rather than the long-run covariance matrix
\mathbf{V}
. For example,
12
established rates for thresholded covariance and graphical-Lasso-type precision matrix estimation for high-dimensional stationary and locally stationary time series under functional dependence conditions.
30
further developed generalized thresholding and precision matrix estimation for temporally dependent observations, allowing slowly decaying dependence and long memory through autocorrelation-based dependence summaries. Under heavy tails and mild moments,
36
studied robust estimation of means, covariance matrices and precision matrices for high-dimensional time series. Although these papers substantially broaden the scope of high-dimensional covariance analysis under dependence, their inferential target remains
\mathbf{\Sigma}
or
\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}
rather than the long-run covariance matrix needed for inference on temporal aggregates.

Related frequency-domain work considers high-dimensional spectral density matrices and their inverses.
32
proposed thresholded estimators for large spectral density matrices of Gaussian and linear processes.
16
developed non-asymptotic theory for smoothed periodogram estimation and sparse inverse spectral density estimation. For locally stationary processes,
35
derived a systematic asymptotic theory for covariance and spectral density estimation in high dimensions. These papers are highly relevant because the spectral density matrix is the frequency-domain analogue of the covariance structure. Nevertheless, they do not directly solve the problem considered here, namely, estimating a sparse long-run covariance matrix in the time domain when the observed series may contain nonconstant and unknown mean structures.

There is also a more recent line of work that comes closer to the present problem by directly targeting zero-frequency or long-memory objects. In particular,
2
developed thresholded and penalized local Whittle estimators for high-dimensional long-run variance and precision matrices under short- and long-range dependence, and
37
studied joint estimation of multiple precision matrices for long-memory time series. These contributions demonstrate that high-dimensional long-run second-order estimation is both statistically meaningful and technically challenging. However, the existing methods in this direction are mainly formulated in the frequency domain under stationary models, whereas many applications in econometrics, finance, environmental studies, and neuroscience involve unknown trends, structural breaks, or other nonstationary mean dynamics. This creates a genuine methodological gap between mean-robust long-run variance estimation in classical time series and sparse regularization in modern high-dimensional statistics.

The goal of this paper is to fill this gap. We study
difference-based high-dimensional long-run covariance matrix estimation
for multivariate time series. Our starting point is a difference-based pilot estimator motivated by
11
, which is intrinsically robust to a broad class of mean structures. We then combine this pilot estimator with three regularization schemes that are standard in high-dimensional covariance estimation, namely, hard thresholding, soft thresholding, and tapering. In this way, we bridge two strands of literature that have largely developed separately: on the one hand, mean-structure-robust long-run variance estimation for dependent data; on the other hand, sparse matrix regularization for high-dimensional covariance estimation.

The proposed framework has several attractive features. First, by using difference statistics instead of global centering, it remains stable when the observed series contains smooth mean variation or abrupt mean changes. Second, the subsequent regularization step exploits sparsity of the long-run covariance matrix and therefore adapts naturally to high-dimensional regimes. Third, our methodology is entirely time-domain, making it conceptually simple and computationally convenient, while still targeting the quantity that is directly relevant for inference based on partial sums and sample averages. From a theoretical perspective, we establish convergence properties for the hard-thresholded, soft-thresholded, and tapered estimators under suitable dependence and sparsity conditions. From a numerical perspective, our simulation results show that these procedures perform well in genuinely high-dimensional settings and remain effective when the mean structure is nonconstant, where classical HAC-type estimators can deteriorate substantially.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Section
2
introduces the difference-based oracle and feasible long-run covariance estimators and the three regularization schemes. We also establishes the main concentration and convergence results. Section
3
reports simulation studies under both constant and nonconstant mean structures. In Section
4
, we apply our method to a change-point inference problem for time series. Section
5
concludes the paper.

2
Methods

2.1
Difference-based long-run covariance matrix estimation

Let

\bm{X}_{t}=\bm{\mu}_{t}+\bm{Z}_{t},\qquad t=1,\ldots,n,

where
\bm{X}_{t},\bm{\mu}_{t},\bm{Z}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{n}}
,
p_{n}
is allowed to diverge with
n
, and
\{\bm{Z}_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
is a zero-mean strictly stationary process. Define the lag-
k
autocovariance matrix of
\bm{Z}_{t}
by

\mathbf{\Gamma}_{k}:=\mathbb{E}\left(\bm{Z}_{0}\bm{Z}_{k}^{\top}\right),\qquad k\in\mathbb{Z}.

The target long-run covariance matrix is

\mathbf{V}:=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathbf{\Gamma}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{n}\times p_{n}},

assuming the series converges entrywise (equivalently, in max norm under our assumptions).

Suppose that
\bm{\mu}_{1}=\cdots=\bm{\mu}_{n}
. A large body of work has been devoted to estimating the long-run covariance matrix
\mathbf{V}
, and the existing methods may be broadly grouped into three classical categories. The first category consists of subsampling-based estimators; see, for example,
21
;
7
;
31
;
26
;
9
. A representative example is the overlapping batch means (OBM) estimator,

\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{\mathrm{OBM},n}:=\frac{\ell}{n-\ell+1}\sum_{i=\ell}^{n}\left(\frac{1}{\ell}\sum_{j=i-\ell+1}^{i}\widehat{\bm{X}}_{j}\right)^{\otimes 2},

where
\ell\in\mathbb{N}\cap(1,n)
is the batch size,
\widehat{\bm{X}}_{i}=\bm{X}_{i}-\overline{\bm{X}}_{n}
, and
\overline{\bm{X}}_{n}=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bm{X}_{i}
. And
\bm{a}^{\otimes 2}:=\bm{a}\otimes\bm{a}=\bm{a}\bm{a}^{\top}
for any
\bm{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
.

The second category is formed by kernel-based estimators; see
23
;
1
;
28
. Two widely used examples are the Bartlett and quadratic spectral (QS) estimators,

\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\mathrm{Bart},n}:=\sum_{k=-\ell}^{\ell}\operatorname{Bart}(k/\ell)\widehat{\bm{\Gamma}}_{k},\qquad\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\mathrm{QS},n}:=\sum_{k=-(n-1)}^{n-1}\operatorname{QS}(k/\ell)\widehat{\bm{\Gamma}}_{k},

where

\widehat{\bm{\Gamma}}_{k}:=n^{-1}\sum_{i=|k|+1}^{n}\widehat{\bm{X}}_{i}\widehat{\bm{X}}_{i-|k|}^{\top},

\operatorname{Bart}(t):=(1-|t|)\mathbb{I}(|t|\leq 1),

and

\operatorname{QS}(t):=\frac{25}{12\pi^{2}t^{2}}\left\{\frac{\sin(6\pi t/5)}{6\pi t/5}-\cos(6\pi t/5)\right\}.

The third category includes resampling-based procedures; see, for instance,
18
;
27
;
24
;
19
. More recently, another line of research has developed estimators constructed from orthonormal series; see
25
;
33
. Practical issues concerning the choice of kernels and orthonormal bases are discussed in
20
, while
22
investigated inference in the presence of strong serial dependence.

When the mean structure is nonconstant, that is,
\bm{\mu}_{i}\neq\bm{\mu}_{j}
for some
i\neq j
, the classical estimators of the long-run covariance matrix may fail to be valid. In particular, standard HAC-type estimators are generally biased unless the mean is constant, and existing modifications for nonstationary time series are typically tailored to specific forms of mean structure and are not equipped with an implementable optimal bandwidth selector. To address this difficulty,
10
proposed a mean-structure and autocorrelation consistent (MAC) estimator, which is fully nonparametric and can be computed in a single pass without explicitly estimating the trend or locating change points. The key idea is to use a variate-difference and bi-differencing construction to remove the effect of the unknown mean structure. Specifically, define

\widehat{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{k}=\frac{1}{2(n-|k|+1)}\sum_{i=|k|+1}^{n}\left(\bm{X}_{i}-\bm{X}_{i-|k|}\right)^{\otimes 2},\qquad|k|=0,1,\ldots,n-1.

Let
L_{k}=c_{0}\ell+c_{1}|k|
with
c_{0},c_{1}>0
. Then the MAC estimator of
\mathbf{V}
is given by

\widehat{\mathbf{V}}_{0,q,n}=\sum_{k=-\ell}^{\ell}K_{q}\!\left(\frac{|k|}{\ell}\right)\left(\widehat{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{L_{k}}-\widehat{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{k}\right),\qquad K_{q}(x)=(1-|x|^{q})\mathbf{1}(|x|\leq 1),

which yields an estimator that is robust to unknown trends and multiple change points.

11
proposed a general framework for the estimation of the long-run variance for time series with nonconstant means. We adopt the difference-based construction in the multivariate extension of
11
. Let
m_{n}\in\mathbb{N}
be the difference order and
h_{n}\in\mathbb{N}
the lag spacing. Let
\mathbf{d}_{n}=(d_{n,0},\ldots,d_{n,m_{n}})^{\top}
be a normalized difference sequence satisfying

\sum_{j=0}^{m_{n}}d_{n,j}=0,\qquad\sum_{j=0}^{m_{n}}d_{n,j}^{2}=1.

(1)

Define the vector-valued difference statistics

\mathbf{D}_{t}:=\sum_{j=0}^{m_{n}}d_{n,j}\,\bm{X}_{t-jh_{n}},\qquad t=m_{n}h_{n}+1,\ldots,n.

For each integer
k
with
|k|<\ell_{n}
, where
\ell_{n}
is the kernel bandwidth, define the difference-based sample autocovariance matrix

\widehat{\mathbf{\Gamma}}^{\,D}_{k}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=m_{n}h_{n}+|k|+1}^{n}\mathbf{D}_{t}\mathbf{D}_{t-|k|}^{\top}.

Let
K(\cdot)
be an even kernel supported on
[-1,1]
. The initial difference-based long-run covariance matrix estimator is

\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\,DB}:=\sum_{|k|<\ell_{n}}K\!\left(\frac{k}{\ell_{n}}\right)\widehat{\mathbf{\Gamma}}^{\,D}_{k}.

To retain the mean contribution explicitly, define

\mathbf{M}_{t}:=\sum_{j=0}^{m_{n}}d_{n,j}\,\bm{\mu}_{t-jh_{n}},\qquad\mathbf{U}_{t}:=\sum_{j=0}^{m_{n}}d_{n,j}\,\bm{Z}_{t-jh_{n}},

(2)

so that
\mathbf{D}_{t}=\mathbf{M}_{t}+\mathbf{U}_{t}
. Define the oracle autocovariance estimator (built from the noise-only difference process) by

\widehat{\mathbf{\Gamma}}^{\,U}_{k}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=m_{n}h_{n}+|k|+1}^{n}\mathbf{U}_{t}\mathbf{U}_{t-|k|}^{\top},\qquad\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\,\mathrm{or}}:=\sum_{|k|<\ell_{n}}K\!\left(\frac{k}{\ell_{n}}\right)\widehat{\mathbf{\Gamma}}^{\,U}_{k}.

The mean-only deterministic contribution is

\mathbf{B}_{\mu,n}:=\sum_{|k|<\ell_{n}}K\!\left(\frac{k}{\ell_{n}}\right)\left[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=m_{n}h_{n}+|k|+1}^{n}\mathbf{M}_{t}\mathbf{M}_{t-|k|}^{\top}\right].

(3)

Define the residual mean-induced random remainder

\mathbf{R}_{\mu,n}:=\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\,DB}-\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\,\mathrm{or}}-\mathbf{B}_{\mu,n}.

(4)

By construction,
\mathbf{R}_{\mu,n}
consists of the cross terms involving
\mathbf{M}_{t}
and
\mathbf{U}_{t}
. We do
not
impose a changepoint structure on
\bm{\mu}_{t}
; instead,
\mathbf{B}_{\mu,n}
and
\mathbf{R}_{\mu,n}
will enter the convergence rate explicitly.

We now state assumptions needed for the high-dimensional max-norm analysis. These assumptions are designed to (i) preserve the construction of the estimator detailed in Section 6.2 of
11
, (ii) keep the mean-induced effect explicit, and (iii) enable a high-dimensional union-bound upgrade of the entrywise oracle concentration.

Assumption 2.1

(Kernel and difference sequence)
.

The kernel
K
is even, bounded, supported on
[-1,1]
, and
K(0)=1
. There exist constants
q_{0}>0
and
C_{K}>0
such that

|1-K(x)|\leq C_{K}|x|^{q_{0}},\qquad|x|\leq 1.

The difference sequence
\{d_{n,j}\}_{j=0}^{m_{n}}
is normalized as in equation (
1
). Moreover,

m_{n}h_{n}=o(n),\qquad\ell_{n}\to\infty,\qquad\ell_{n}=o(n).

Assumption 2.2

(Uniform oracle bias bound inherited from DB kernel theory)
.

There exists a constant
C_{B}>0
, independent of
n
and
(r,s)
, such that

\left\lVert\mathbb{E}(\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\,\mathrm{or}})-\mathbf{V}\right\rVert_{\max}\leq C_{B}\left\{\ell_{n}^{-q_{0}}+\frac{(m_{n}+1)h_{n}}{n}\right\}.

Assumption 2.3

(Uniform Bernstein-type concentration for oracle entries)
.

There exist constants
c_{1},c_{2}>0
such that for all sufficiently large
n
, all
1\leq r,s\leq p_{n}
, and all
t>0
,

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\left|\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\,\mathrm{or}}[r,s]-\mathbb{E}\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\,\mathrm{or}}[r,s]\right|>t\right)\leq 2\exp\!\left[-c_{1}n\min\!\left\{\frac{t^{2}}{\ell_{n}+m_{n}h_{n}},\,\frac{t}{\ell_{n}+m_{n}h_{n}}\right\}\right].

Remark 2.1
.

Assumptions
2.2
and
2.3
are standard in high-dimensional analysis of dependent data. They typically follow under suitable combinations of weak temporal dependence, such as physical dependence or mixing conditions, together with mild tail assumptions, for example, sub-exponential or Bernstein-type moment bounds on the coordinates. We state these assumptions directly in the main text because the primary goal of this paper is to develop the max-norm error analysis and the associated thresholding theory for the difference-based estimator in a high-dimensional setting. For completeness, the appendix provides several sufficient conditions under which both assumptions are satisfied.

Assumption 2.4

(Explicit mean-induced remainder)
.

There exists a deterministic sequence
r_{\mu,n}\geq 0
such that

\left\lVert\mathbf{R}_{\mu,n}\right\rVert_{\max}=O_{p}(r_{\mu,n}),

where
\mathbf{R}_{\mu,n}
is defined in equation (
4
).

Remark 2.2
.

Assumption
2.4
does
not
require
\bm{\mu}_{t}
to follow a changepoint model. The mean effect is fully retained through the explicit deterministic bias matrix
\mathbf{B}_{\mu,n}
in equation (
3
) and the remainder
\mathbf{R}_{\mu,n}
. A detailed discussion of mean shifts is provided in Section
2.4
.

Next, we state the Max-norm rate of the initial difference-based estimator
\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\,DB}
.
Define the overall rate

a_{n}:=\left\lVert\mathbf{B}_{\mu,n}\right\rVert_{\max}+r_{\mu,n}+\ell_{n}^{-q_{0}}+\frac{(m_{n}+1)h_{n}}{n}+\sqrt{\frac{(\ell_{n}+m_{n}h_{n})\log p_{n}}{n}}+\frac{(\ell_{n}+m_{n}h_{n})\log p_{n}}{n}.

(5)

Theorem 2.1
.

Suppose Assumptions
2.1
–
2.4
hold and
\log p_{n}=o(n)
. Then

\left\lVert\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\,DB}-\mathbf{V}\right\rVert_{\max}=O_{p}(a_{n}),

where
a_{n}
is defined in (
5
).

Corollary 2.1

(Bandwidth-balanced rate under negligible mean remainder)
.

Assume the conditions of Theorem
2.1
. In addition, suppose

\left\lVert\mathbf{B}_{\mu,n}\right\rVert_{\max}+r_{\mu,n}=o\!\left(\ell_{n}^{-q_{0}}+\sqrt{\frac{\ell_{n}\log p_{n}}{n}}\right),\qquad m_{n}h_{n}\lesssim\ell_{n},\qquad\frac{(m_{n}+1)h_{n}}{n}=o\!\left(\ell_{n}^{-q_{0}}+\sqrt{\frac{\ell_{n}\log p_{n}}{n}}\right).

Then

\left\lVert\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\,DB}-\mathbf{V}\right\rVert_{\max}=O_{p}\!\left(\ell_{n}^{-q_{0}}+\sqrt{\frac{\ell_{n}\log p_{n}}{n}}+\frac{\ell_{n}\log p_{n}}{n}\right).

If moreover
\ell_{n}\log p_{n}/n\to 0
and

\ell_{n}\asymp\left(\frac{n}{\log p_{n}}\right)^{1/(2q_{0}+1)},

then

\left\lVert\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\,DB}-\mathbf{V}\right\rVert_{\max}=O_{p}\!\left\{\left(\frac{\log p_{n}}{n}\right)^{q_{0}/(2q_{0}+1)}\right\}.

(6)

2.2
Sparse Estimators

When the dimension is fixed, the difference-based (DB) estimator is effective for time series with nonconstant mean structures. In high-dimensional settings, however, the DB estimator may suffer from substantial bias and may fail to be invertible, much like the sample covariance matrix in classical high-dimensional problems. This motivates the use of sparse regularization under the assumption that the long-run covariance matrix is sparse. In this paper, we focus on three widely used regularization schemes: hard thresholding
(
4
)
, soft thresholding
(
29
)
, and tapering
(
6
)
. We note that other high-dimensional covariance estimators developed in the literature could also be combined with the DB estimator in a similar manner.

Let
\tau_{n}>0
be a threshold level. Define the hard-thresholding estimator
\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\,\mathrm{hard}}=(\widehat{V}^{\mathrm{hard}}_{rs})
by

\widehat{V}^{\mathrm{hard}}_{rs}=\begin{cases}\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\,DB}[r,r],&r=s,\\[4.0pt]
\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\,DB}[r,s]\mathbf{1}\!\left(|\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\,DB}[r,s]|\geq\tau_{n}\right),&r\neq s.\end{cases}

(7)

Similarly, define the soft-thresholding estimator
\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\,\mathrm{soft}}=(\widehat{V}^{\mathrm{soft}}_{rs})
by

\widehat{V}^{\mathrm{soft}}_{rs}=\begin{cases}\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\,DB}[r,r],&r=s,\\[4.0pt]
\mathrm{sign}(\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\,DB}[r,s])\bigl(|\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\,DB}[r,s]|-\tau_{n}\bigr)_{+},&r\neq s.\end{cases}

(8)

Assumption 2.5

(Weak row-sparsity class)
.

There exist
\alpha\in[0,1)
,
M>0
, and a sequence
c_{n,p}\geq 1
such that
\mathbf{V}
is symmetric and

\max_{1\leq r\leq p_{n}}\sum_{s=1}^{p_{n}}|V_{rs}|^{\alpha}\leq c_{n,p},\qquad\max_{1\leq r\leq p_{n}}V_{rr}\leq M.

Theorem 2.2

(Thresholded estimation under weak row-sparsity)
.

Suppose Theorem
2.1
and Assumption
2.5
hold. Let
\tau_{n}=C_{\tau}a_{n}
with a sufficiently large constant
C_{\tau}>0
. Then, for the hard-thresholding estimator
\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\,\mathrm{hard}}
,

\displaystyle\left\lVert\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\,\mathrm{hard}}-\mathbf{V}\right\rVert_{\max}

\displaystyle=O_{p}(a_{n}),

(9)

\displaystyle\left\lVert\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\,\mathrm{hard}}-\mathbf{V}\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle=O_{p}\!\bigl(c_{n,p}a_{n}^{\,1-\alpha}\bigr),

(10)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p_{n}}\left\lVert\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\,\mathrm{hard}}-\mathbf{V}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=O_{p}\!\bigl(c_{n,p}a_{n}^{\,2-\alpha}\bigr).

(11)

In particular, if
c_{n,p}a_{n}^{1-\alpha}\to 0
, then
\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\,\mathrm{hard}}
is operator-norm consistent.

Theorem 2.3

(Soft-thresholding under weak row-sparsity)
.

Suppose Theorem
2.1
and Assumption
2.5
hold. Let
\tau_{n}=C_{\tau}a_{n}
with
C_{\tau}>0
sufficiently large, then, for the soft-thresholding estimator
\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\,\mathrm{soft}}
,

\displaystyle\left\lVert\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\,\mathrm{soft}}-\mathbf{V}\right\rVert_{\max}

\displaystyle=O_{p}(a_{n}),

(12)

\displaystyle\left\lVert\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\,\mathrm{soft}}-\mathbf{V}\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle=O_{p}\!\bigl(c_{n,p}a_{n}^{\,1-\alpha}\bigr),

(13)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p_{n}}\left\lVert\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\,\mathrm{soft}}-\mathbf{V}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=O_{p}\!\bigl(c_{n,p}a_{n}^{\,2-\alpha}\bigr).

(14)

Theorems
2.2
and
2.3
establish the rates of convergence for the hard-thresholding and soft-thresholding estimators, respectively. These rates depend on several key features of the problem, most notably the sparsity level of the long-run covariance matrix, the magnitude of the mean shift, and the relationship between the sample size and the dimension. Roughly speaking, a sparser covariance structure facilitates more accurate estimation, while a larger mean shift deteriorates performance through its impact on the bias term. Meanwhile, the dimensionality affects the stochastic error through the usual high-dimensional scaling. Consequently, the overall convergence behavior is governed by the interplay among dependence sparsity, signal contamination from the mean shift, and the effective amount of sample information. These results clarify the regimes under which thresholded estimators remain reliable for estimating the long-run covariance matrix in high-dimensional settings.

Definition 2.1

(Tapering weights and tapering estimator)
.

For an integer
k_{n}\geq 1
, let
\mathbf{W}^{(k_{n})}=(w_{rs}^{(k_{n})})_{1\leq r,s\leq p_{n}}
be a symmetric tapering weight matrix satisfying:

(i)

0\leq w_{rs}^{(k_{n})}\leq 1
for all
r,s
;

(ii)

w_{rs}^{(k_{n})}=1
if
|r-s|\leq k_{n}/2
;

(iii)

w_{rs}^{(k_{n})}=0
if
|r-s|\geq k_{n}
;

(iv)

for each row
r
, the number of nonzero weights
\#\{s:w_{rs}^{(k_{n})}\neq 0\}\leq C_{w}k_{n}
, where
C_{w}
is a universal constant.

Define the tapering estimator by the Hadamard product

\mathbf{V}^{\mathrm{tap}}:=\mathbf{W}^{(k_{n})}\circ\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\,DB}.

(15)

To establish the convergence rate of the tapering estimator, we need the following assumption.

Assumption 2.6

(Bandable target class)
.

There exist
\nu>0
, constants
C_{\mathrm{bd}},C_{\mathrm{bd},2},M_{1}>0
, such that
\mathbf{V}
is symmetric and:

\displaystyle\max_{1\leq r\leq p_{n}}\sum_{s=1}^{p_{n}}|V_{rs}|

\displaystyle\leq M_{1},

\displaystyle\max_{1\leq r\leq p_{n}}\sum_{|r-s|>k}|V_{rs}|

\displaystyle\leq C_{\mathrm{bd}}\,k^{-\nu},\qquad\forall\,k\geq 1,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p_{n}}\sum_{r=1}^{p_{n}}\sum_{|r-s|>k}V_{rs}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C_{\mathrm{bd},2}\,k^{-2\nu},\qquad\forall\,k\geq 1.

(16)

Theorem 2.4

(Tapering under bandability)
.

Suppose Theorem
2.1
, Assumption
2.6
, and Definition
2.1
hold. Then

\left\lVert\mathbf{V}^{\mathrm{tap}}-\mathbf{V}\right\rVert_{2}=O_{p}\!\left(k_{n}a_{n}+k_{n}^{-\nu}\right).

(17)

In addition,

\frac{1}{p_{n}}\left\lVert\mathbf{V}^{\mathrm{tap}}-\mathbf{V}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}=O_{p}\!\left(k_{n}a_{n}^{2}+k_{n}^{-2\nu}\right).

(18)

Hence, if
k_{n}a_{n}\to 0
and
k_{n}\to\infty
, the tapering estimator is operator-norm consistent. The balancing choice
k_{n}\asymp a_{n}^{-1/(\nu+1)}
yields

\left\lVert\mathbf{V}^{\mathrm{tap}}-\mathbf{V}\right\rVert_{2}=O_{p}\!\left(a_{n}^{\nu/(\nu+1)}\right).

Theorem
2.4
is complementary to thresholding: thresholding targets sparse matrices without an index-distance structure, whereas tapering exploits a natural ordering and bandability. Both are driven by the same initial max-norm rate
a_{n}
, which in our framework explicitly carries the mean-induced component.

2.3
Selection of tuning parameters

The regularization parameters are selected in a data-driven manner through a blockwise validation procedure tailored to dependent observations. Since random splitting at the individual-observation level would destroy the temporal dependence structure, we instead repeatedly draw two non-overlapping contiguous blocks from the sample. For the
b
th repetition, denote by
I_{b}^{\mathrm{tr}}
and
I_{b}^{\mathrm{va}}
the training block and the validation block, respectively, and let
\hat{\mathbf{V}}^{(b)}_{\lambda}
be the thresholded estimator of the difference-based long-run covariance matrix constructed from the training block with threshold level
\lambda
. To evaluate the quality of
\hat{\mathbf{V}}^{(b)}_{\lambda}
, we compute on the validation block a pilot estimator
\tilde{\mathbf{V}}^{(b)}
, and measure the discrepancy by the squared Frobenius loss

L_{b}(\lambda)=\bigl\|\hat{\mathbf{V}}^{(b)}_{\lambda}-\tilde{\mathbf{V}}^{(b)}\bigr\|_{F}^{2}.

Averaging over
B
random double-block splits yields the validation criterion

\mathrm{CV}_{\lambda}(\lambda)=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}L_{b}(\lambda),

and the selected threshold parameter is defined by

\hat{\lambda}=\arg\min_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\mathrm{CV}_{\lambda}(\lambda),

where
\Lambda
is a prespecified candidate set. This criterion formalizes the usual bias–variance trade-off: an excessively small
\lambda
retains too many spurious small entries and leads to high variability, whereas an excessively large
\lambda
overshrinks the covariance structure and induces substantial bias.

The tapering bandwidth
k_{\mathrm{taper}}
is chosen according to the same principle. For each candidate
k\in\mathcal{K}
, let
\hat{\mathbf{V}}^{(b)}_{k}
denote the tapering estimator computed from the training block with bandwidth
k
. Its validation loss is defined as

L_{b}(k)=\bigl\|\hat{\mathbf{V}}^{(b)}_{k}-\tilde{\mathbf{V}}^{(b)}\bigr\|_{F}^{2},

and the corresponding average criterion is

\mathrm{CV}_{k}(k)=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}L_{b}(k).

We then select

\hat{k}_{\mathrm{taper}}=\arg\min_{k\in\mathcal{K}}\mathrm{CV}_{k}(k).

From a statistical perspective,
k_{\mathrm{taper}}
controls the effective range of serial dependence retained in the estimator: a small bandwidth may remove non-negligible autocovariance contributions and thus incur truncation bias, while a large bandwidth tends to preserve more long-lag information at the cost of increased estimation variability. Therefore, the proposed blockwise validation rule provides a practically stable and theoretically reasonable calibration mechanism for both
\lambda
and
k_{\mathrm{taper}}
, while respecting the dependence structure of the time series.

2.4
Discussion of Means

The rate
a_{n}
in equation (
5
) contains two mean-related components,

\left\lVert\mathbf{B}_{\mu,n}\right\rVert_{\max}\quad\text{and}\quad r_{\mu,n},

where
\mathbf{B}_{\mu,n}
is the deterministic mean-induced bias matrix in (
3
) and
r_{\mu,n}
controls the stochastic cross-term remainder
\mathbf{R}_{\mu,n}
in (
4
). We now provide concrete upper bounds under broad mean regularity classes.

Recall
\mathbf{M}_{t}
from (
2
). Define

\bar{M}_{2,n}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=m_{n}h_{n}+1}^{n}\left\lVert\mathbf{M}_{t}\right\rVert_{\max}^{2},\qquad\bar{M}_{\infty,n}:=\max_{m_{n}h_{n}+1\leq t\leq n}\left\lVert\mathbf{M}_{t}\right\rVert_{\max}.

(19)

Also write
\left\lVert K\right\rVert_{\infty}:=\sup_{|x|\leq 1}|K(x)|
and

L_{n}:=\ell_{n}+m_{n}h_{n}

for short.

Proposition 2.1

(Generic deterministic bound for
\mathbf{B}_{\mu,n}
)
.

Under Assumption
2.1
, the deterministic mean-induced bias matrix
\mathbf{B}_{\mu,n}
in (
3
) satisfies

\left\lVert\mathbf{B}_{\mu,n}\right\rVert_{\max}\leq(2\ell_{n}-1)\left\lVert K\right\rVert_{\infty}\,\bar{M}_{2,n}.

Hence
\left\lVert\mathbf{B}_{\mu,n}\right\rVert_{\max}=O(\ell_{n}\bar{M}_{2,n})
.

To control the stochastic mean-induced remainder
\mathbf{R}_{\mu,n}
, we impose a conditional Bernstein inequality on the cross terms. This assumption is standard once
\mathbf{U}_{t}
admits suitable dependence and tail controls uniformly over coordinates, and
\mathbf{M}_{t}
is treated as deterministic conditioning information.

Assumption 2.7

(Conditional Bernstein inequality for the mean-noise cross term)
.

There exists a constant
c_{3}>0
such that, conditional on
\{\bm{\mu}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{n}
, for all sufficiently large
n
and all
t>0
,

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\left\lVert\mathbf{R}_{\mu,n}\right\rVert_{\max}>t\ \middle|\ \{\bm{\mu}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{n}\right)\leq 4p_{n}^{2}\exp\!\left[-c_{3}n\min\!\left\{\frac{t^{2}}{L_{n}^{2}\bar{M}_{2,n}},\frac{t}{L_{n}\bar{M}_{\infty,n}}\right\}\right].

We also give a sufficient condition for Assumption
2.7
in the Appendix.

Proposition 2.2

(Generic stochastic bound for
\mathbf{R}_{\mu,n}
)
.

Suppose Assumption
2.7
holds and
\log p_{n}=o(n)
. Then

\left\lVert\mathbf{R}_{\mu,n}\right\rVert_{\max}=O_{p}\!\left(\sqrt{\bar{M}_{2,n}}\,L_{n}\sqrt{\frac{\log p_{n}}{n}}+\bar{M}_{\infty,n}\frac{L_{n}\log p_{n}}{n}\right).

(20)

Consequently, one may take

r_{\mu,n}\asymp\sqrt{\bar{M}_{2,n}}\,L_{n}\sqrt{\frac{\log p_{n}}{n}}+\bar{M}_{\infty,n}\frac{L_{n}\log p_{n}}{n}.

(21)

We next show how
\bar{M}_{2,n}
and
\bar{M}_{\infty,n}
can be bounded under two concrete mean classes.

2.4.1
Piecewise-Hölder mean class (with possible jumps)

Assumption 2.8

(Piecewise-Hölder mean path)
.

There exist an integer
J_{n}\geq 0
, a partition

0=\eta_{0}<\eta_{1}<\cdots<\eta_{J_{n}}<\eta_{J_{n}+1}=1,

a Hölder exponent
\beta\in(0,1]
, and constants
L_{\mu},M_{\mu}>0
such that for each coordinate
r\in\{1,\ldots,p_{n}\}
,

\mu_{t}^{(r)}=g_{r}(t/n),\qquad t=1,\ldots,n,

where
g_{r}
may jump at
\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{J_{n}}
, satisfies

\sup_{u\in[0,1]}|g_{r}(u)|\leq M_{\mu},

and on each open interval
(\eta_{j},\eta_{j+1})
,

|g_{r}(u)-g_{r}(v)|\leq L_{\mu}|u-v|^{\beta},\qquad\forall\,u,v\in(\eta_{j},\eta_{j+1}).

Proposition 2.3

(Mean-bias and remainder under piecewise-Hölder means)
.

Suppose Assumption
2.8
holds. Let

C_{d,1}:=\sum_{j=0}^{m_{n}}|d_{n,j}|,\qquad C_{d,\beta}:=\sum_{j=1}^{m_{n}}|d_{n,j}|j^{\beta}.

Then:

(i)

The difference-mean process satisfies

\bar{M}_{2,n}\leq C\left\{\left(\frac{h_{n}}{n}\right)^{2\beta}C_{d,\beta}^{2}+C_{d,1}^{2}\,M_{\mu}^{2}\,\frac{J_{n}m_{n}h_{n}}{n}\right\},

(22)

and

\bar{M}_{\infty,n}\leq C\left\{C_{d,\beta}\left(\frac{h_{n}}{n}\right)^{\beta}+C_{d,1}M_{\mu}\right\}.

(23)

(ii)

Consequently, by Proposition
2.1
,

\left\lVert\mathbf{B}_{\mu,n}\right\rVert_{\max}\leq C\ell_{n}\left\{\left(\frac{h_{n}}{n}\right)^{2\beta}C_{d,\beta}^{2}+C_{d,1}^{2}M_{\mu}^{2}\frac{J_{n}m_{n}h_{n}}{n}\right\}.

(24)

(iii)

If Assumption
2.7
also holds, then by Proposition
2.2
,

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathbf{R}_{\mu,n}\right\rVert_{\max}

\displaystyle=O_{p}\!\Bigg(\sqrt{\left\{\left(\frac{h_{n}}{n}\right)^{2\beta}C_{d,\beta}^{2}+C_{d,1}^{2}M_{\mu}^{2}\frac{J_{n}m_{n}h_{n}}{n}\right\}}L_{n}\sqrt{\frac{\log p_{n}}{n}}

\displaystyle\hskip 62.59596pt+\left\{C_{d,\beta}\left(\frac{h_{n}}{n}\right)^{\beta}+C_{d,1}M_{\mu}\right\}\frac{L_{n}\log p_{n}}{n}\Bigg).

(25)

Here
C>0
is a constant independent of
n
and
p_{n}
.

2.4.2
Bounded-variation mean class (no changepoint parametrization)

Assumption 2.9

(Coordinatewise discrete bounded variation)
.

There exists
M_{\mu}>0
and a sequence
V_{\mu,n}\geq 0
such that

\sup_{1\leq t\leq n}\left\lVert\bm{\mu}_{t}\right\rVert_{\max}\leq M_{\mu}

and

\max_{1\leq r\leq p_{n}}\sum_{t=2}^{n}\left|\mu_{t}^{(r)}-\mu_{t-1}^{(r)}\right|\leq V_{\mu,n}.

Proposition 2.4

(Mean-bias and remainder under bounded-variation means)
.

Suppose Assumption
2.9
holds. Let
C_{d,1}:=\sum_{j=0}^{m_{n}}|d_{n,j}|
. Then

\bar{M}_{2,n}\leq C\,C_{d,1}^{2}\,(m_{n}h_{n})^{2}\,\frac{V_{\mu,n}^{2}}{n},

(26)

and

\bar{M}_{\infty,n}\leq 2C_{d,1}M_{\mu}.

(27)

Consequently,

\left\lVert\mathbf{B}_{\mu,n}\right\rVert_{\max}\leq C\,\ell_{n}\,C_{d,1}^{2}\,(m_{n}h_{n})^{2}\,\frac{V_{\mu,n}^{2}}{n},

(28)

and, if Assumption
2.7
holds,

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathbf{R}_{\mu,n}\right\rVert_{\max}=O_{p}\!\left(C_{d,1}m_{n}h_{n}\,V_{\mu,n}\,\frac{L_{n}\sqrt{\log p_{n}}}{n}+C_{d,1}M_{\mu}\frac{L_{n}\log p_{n}}{n}\right).

(29)

Remark 2.3

(Plug-in rates for
a_{n}
)
.

Theorem
2.1
remains unchanged, but Propositions
2.3
and
2.4
provide explicit, directly usable upper bounds for the mean-related components in
a_{n}
. For example, under Assumption
2.8
, one may substitute (
24
) and (
25
) into (
5
).

Corollary 2.2

(Explicit
a_{n}
under piecewise-Hölder means)
.

Suppose the conditions of Theorem
2.1
hold, and Assumptions
2.7
and
2.8
hold. Then one may choose

a_{n}\asymp\ell_{n}^{-q_{0}}+\frac{(m_{n}+1)h_{n}}{n}+\sqrt{\frac{L_{n}\log p_{n}}{n}}+\frac{L_{n}\log p_{n}}{n}+\ell_{n}\left\{\left(\frac{h_{n}}{n}\right)^{2\beta}C_{d,\beta}^{2}+C_{d,1}^{2}M_{\mu}^{2}\frac{J_{n}m_{n}h_{n}}{n}\right\}

\quad+\sqrt{\left\{\left(\frac{h_{n}}{n}\right)^{2\beta}C_{d,\beta}^{2}+C_{d,1}^{2}M_{\mu}^{2}\frac{J_{n}m_{n}h_{n}}{n}\right\}}L_{n}\sqrt{\frac{\log p_{n}}{n}}+\left\{C_{d,\beta}\left(\frac{h_{n}}{n}\right)^{\beta}+C_{d,1}M_{\mu}\right\}\frac{L_{n}\log p_{n}}{n}.

In particular, if the mean-induced terms are of smaller order than the oracle terms, the rate reduces to the bandwidth-balanced form in Corollary
2.1
.

3
Simulation

We conduct Monte Carlo experiments to assess finite-sample performance of high-dimensional long-run covariance (LRC) matrix estimation under temporal dependence and cross-sectional dependence. For each replication, we generate a
p
-variate time series
\{\bm{X}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{n}
with different sample sizes
n=200,400,800,1600
and dimensions
p=300,500
. The temporal dependence follows an AR(1) recursion

\bm{Z}_{t}=\phi\bm{Z}_{t-1}+\bm{\varepsilon}_{t},\qquad t=2,\ldots,n,

with
\phi=0.5
, and we discard an initial burn-in of length 200 to mitigate initialization effects. Throughout, the innovations
\{\bm{\varepsilon}_{t}\}
are i.i.d. Gaussian with mean zero and covariance matrix
{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\bm{\varepsilon}}
, whose structure differs across the following two models.

Model I (tridiagonal cross-sectional dependence).

We impose local cross-sectional dependence by taking
{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\bm{\varepsilon}}
to be tridiagonal. Specifically, let
a=0.5
and define
{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\bm{\varepsilon}}
by

({\bm{\Sigma}}_{\bm{\varepsilon}})_{11}=1,\qquad({\bm{\Sigma}}_{\bm{\varepsilon}})_{ii}=1+a^{2}~(i\geq 2),\qquad({\bm{\Sigma}}_{\bm{\varepsilon}})_{i,i-1}=({\bm{\Sigma}}_{\bm{\varepsilon}})_{i-1,i}=a~(i\geq 2),

and
({\bm{\Sigma}}_{\bm{\varepsilon}})_{ij}=0
otherwise.

Model II (Toeplitz cross-sectional dependence).

We consider a bandable Toeplitz covariance with exponential decay:

({\bm{\Sigma}}_{\bm{\varepsilon}})_{ij}=\rho^{|i-j|},\qquad\rho=0.7,\qquad 1\leq i,j\leq p.

Model III (Permuted Block Diagonal Structure).

We generate data from a multivariate model with covariance matrix

\mathbf{\Sigma}=\mathbf{\Pi}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{0}\mathbf{\Pi}^{\top},\qquad\mathbf{\Sigma}_{0}=\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{B},\ldots,\mathbf{B}),\qquad\mathbf{B}=\begin{pmatrix}1&\rho\\
\rho&1\end{pmatrix},

where
\mathbf{\Pi}
is a permutation matrix. This covariance matrix is highly sparse, since each row has only one nonzero off-diagonal entry, but it is not banded after permutation.

To examine robustness to nonconstant means, we add a deterministic mean component to the first
m=20
coordinates of the observed series. Let
t=i/n
for
i=1,\ldots,n
and define

\mu(t)=\exp(t)+\mathbf{1}(t>0.3)+2\mathbf{1}(t>0.6)+4\mathbf{1}(t>0.8).

We set
\mathbb{E}(X_{t,j})=\mu(t)
for
j=1,\ldots,m
and
\mathbb{E}(X_{t,j})=0
for
j>m
. The target LRC matrix is defined for the centered stationary component and, under both models, equals

\mathbf{V}\;=\;\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}\bm{\Gamma}(k)\;=\;\frac{{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\bm{\varepsilon}}}{(1-\phi)^{2}},

where
{\bm{\Gamma}}(k)=\mathrm{Cov}(\bm{X}_{t},\bm{X}_{t-k})
.

We compare six estimators of
\mathbf{V}
.

1.

HAC.
The standard heteroskedasticity and autocorrelation consistent estimator of
23
. For a fair comparison, we use the same kernel as in the DB estimator, namely

K_{q}(u)=(1-|u|^{q})^{+},\qquad q=2.

2.

MAC.
The mean-structure and autocorrelation consistent covariance matrix estimator proposed by
10
.

3.

DB.
The difference-based estimator proposed by
11
. In our implementation, we set
m=3
and use

\bm{d}=(0.1942,\,0.2809,\,0.3832,\,-0.8582)^{\top}.

We choose
h_{n}=2\ell_{n}
and
\ell_{n}=\min\left\{\left\lfloor\left(\frac{n}{\log p}\right)^{1/4}\right\rfloor,\ \left\lfloor\frac{n-10}{28}\right\rfloor\right\}.

4.

Hard.
The hard-thresholded DB estimator defined in (
7
).

5.

Soft.
The soft-thresholded DB estimator defined in (
8
).

6.

Taper.
The tapered DB estimator defined in (
15
), where the tapering weights are given by

W^{(k)}_{ij}=\begin{cases}1,&|i-j|\leq k/2,\\
2-2|i-j|/k,&k/2<|i-j|<k,\\
0,&|i-j|\geq k.\end{cases}

For each replication and each method, we compute the estimation error
\mathbf{E}=\widehat{\mathbf{V}}-\mathbf{V}
and report four matrix norms:

\|\mathbf{E}\|_{F},\qquad\|\mathbf{E}\|_{1},\qquad\|\mathbf{E}\|_{\max},\qquad\|\mathbf{E}\|_{2},

where
\|\cdot\|_{F}
is the Frobenius norm,
\|\cdot\|_{1}
is the induced matrix
1
-norm,
\|\cdot\|_{\max}=\max_{i,j}|E_{ij}|
is the entrywise maximum norm, and
\|\cdot\|_{2}
is the spectral norm. We also report their relative errors, obtained by dividing each error norm by the corresponding norm of
\mathbf{V}
(with a small numerical safeguard in the denominator). Results are summarized as Monte Carlo averages over 1000 replications.

Several conclusions emerge clearly from Tables
1
–
6
. First, the unregularized HAC estimator performs very poorly in all six settings. Its absolute and relative errors remain extremely large across all matrix norms, and the improvement with increasing sample size is negligible. This instability is especially pronounced under the induced
\ell_{1}
and spectral norms, indicating that direct high-dimensional HAC estimation is not reliable in the present regime. In the settings where the MAC estimator is reported, it improves substantially over HAC, but it is still uniformly less accurate than the difference-based procedures, particularly after structural regularization. By contrast, the raw DB estimator already achieves a dramatic reduction in error, and its accuracy improves steadily as
n
increases, confirming the practical advantage of differencing for removing the nonstationary mean component before long-run covariance estimation.

Second, the effect of regularization depends in an informative way on the underlying cross-sectional structure. For Models I and II, where the covariance matrix is banded or bandable under the observed ordering, the regularized DB estimators uniformly improve upon the raw DB estimator, and the tapered estimator is typically the most accurate overall. Its advantage is especially clear under the Frobenius, matrix
\ell_{1}
, and spectral norms, and this pattern is stable for both
p=300
and
p=500
. The gains from regularization are therefore not only substantial but also robust to increasing dimensionality. In contrast, Model III is sparse but explicitly non-banded after permutation. In this setting, the tapering estimator loses much of its advantage, while hard- and soft-thresholding become more competitive and often outperform tapering under the Frobenius norm, with soft-thresholding also performing very well under the spectral norm in several cases. This contrast is consistent with the structural design of the three regularization schemes: tapering is most effective when the variable ordering carries meaningful locality information, whereas thresholding is better adapted to general sparsity without bandedness. Finally, across all six tables, the max-norm errors of the regularized estimators are often quite close to one another, suggesting that the main benefit of regularization lies in improving global matrix recovery rather than merely reducing the largest entrywise deviation.

4
Data Application

Following
34
, we study weekly log-returns of NASDAQ constituent stocks from January 2016 to December 2024. Financial return panels of this type are well known to exhibit substantial temporal dependence and strong cross-sectional comovement, so a realistic changepoint analysis must account for the long-run covariance structure; see also the empirical discussion in
34
. Our objective is to examine whether the high-dimensional return process experienced a structural break during the sample period.

Formally, let
\bm{X}_{1},\ldots,\bm{X}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
denote the weekly return vectors where in our processed sample
n=471
and
p=1556
. We consider the single-changepoint testing problem

H_{0}:\ {\bm{\mu}}_{1}=\cdots={\bm{\mu}}_{n}\qquad\text{versus}\qquad H_{1}:\ {\bm{\mu}}_{1}=\cdots={\bm{\mu}}_{\tau}\neq{\bm{\mu}}_{\tau+1}=\cdots={\bm{\mu}}_{n}

for some unknown
\tau\in\{1,\ldots,n-1\}
. In the present section, we focus exclusively on the dense-alternative statistic
\bm{S}_{n,p}
proposed by
34
. Specifically, for each candidate split point
k
, let
\bm{W}(k)
denote the quadratic CUSUM-type statistic and let
\widehat{\bm{\mu}}_{M,k}
be the corresponding centering term defined in that paper. We then analyze the normalized process

\widetilde{\bm{S}}_{n,p}(k)=\frac{\bm{W}(k)-\widehat{\bm{\mu}}_{M,k}}{\widehat{\omega}},\qquad 1\leq k\leq n-1,

and estimate the changepoint by the maximizer of
\widetilde{\bm{S}}_{n,p}(k)
over
k
.

The only modification relative to
34
is in the estimation of the scaling factor
\omega
. Instead of using the original plug-in estimator based on the long-run covariance structure appearing in that paper, we replace
\mathbf{V}
in the quantity
\{2\,\mathrm{tr}({\mathbf{V}}^{2})/p\}^{1/2}
by our sparse difference-based long-run covariance estimator. This yields three versions,

\widehat{\omega}_{\mathrm{hard}}=\biggl\{\frac{2}{p}\,\mathrm{tr}\bigl(\widehat{\mathbf{V}}_{\mathrm{hard}}^{2}\bigr)\biggr\}^{1/2},\qquad\widehat{\omega}_{\mathrm{soft}}=\biggl\{\frac{2}{p}\,\mathrm{tr}\bigl(\widehat{\mathbf{V}}_{\mathrm{soft}}^{2}\bigr)\biggr\}^{1/2},\qquad\widehat{\omega}_{\mathrm{taper}}=\biggl\{\frac{2}{p}\,\mathrm{tr}\bigl(\widehat{\mathbf{V}}_{\mathrm{taper}}^{2}\bigr)\biggr\}^{1/2},

which in turn produce three normalized
\bm{S}_{n,p}
paths. The corresponding changepoint estimators are defined by

\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{hard}}=\arg\max_{1\leq k\leq n-1}\widetilde{\bm{S}}^{(\mathrm{hard})}_{n,p}(k),\qquad\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{soft}}=\arg\max_{1\leq k\leq n-1}\widetilde{\bm{S}}^{(\mathrm{soft})}_{n,p}(k),\qquad\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{taper}}=\arg\max_{1\leq k\leq n-1}\widetilde{\bm{S}}^{(\mathrm{taper})}_{n,p}(k).

Heatmaps of sparse long run covariance matrix estimators (all entries are multiplied by 10000).

Figure
1
provides additional empirical support for the sparse long-run covariance formulation adopted in our modification of the
\bm{S}_{n,p}
statistic. Across the hard-thresholding, soft-thresholding, and tapering estimators, the estimated long-run covariance matrix
\widehat{\mathbf{V}}
exhibits a pronounced near-sparse pattern: most off-diagonal entries are heavily shrunk toward zero, while only a relatively small fraction of entries remain visibly non-negligible. This feature is particularly evident away from the main diagonal, where large connected blocks are largely absent and dependence appears to be concentrated in a limited subset of local coordinates. Such a pattern is consistent with the working assumption that the cross-sectional and serially aggregated dependence structure is globally weak but locally persistent, thereby making sparse regularization a natural device for stabilizing the estimation of
\mathbf{V}
in high dimensions.

The three regularization schemes reveal a broadly similar structural picture, although they differ in the degree of shrinkage. The hard-thresholding estimator preserves a slightly larger number of moderate signals, the soft-thresholding estimator induces more aggressive global shrinkage, and the tapering estimator retains a smoother decay pattern. Importantly, however, all three estimators lead to the same qualitative conclusion that
\widehat{\mathbf{V}}
is approximately sparse rather than dense. This visual evidence complements the change-point analysis based on the modified
\bm{S}_{n,p}
statistic, and supports our use of the sparse estimator
\widehat{\mathbf{V}}
as a plug-in device in the normalization term involving
\mathrm{tr}({\mathbf{V}}^{2})
.

Figure
2
displays the three normalized trajectories based on the hard-thresholding, soft-thresholding, and tapering estimators of the long-run covariance matrix. Although the three normalizations produce different peak magnitudes, they all attain their maximum at the same location, namely
\widehat{k}=269
, corresponding to 2021-02-19. As reported in Table
7
, the soft-thresholding version yields the largest peak value,
\max_{k}\widetilde{\bm{S}}_{n,p}(k)=0.577
, together with the smallest Monte Carlo
p
-value of
0.078
, while the hard-thresholding and tapering versions give weaker signals, with corresponding
p
-values
0.173
and
0.134
, respectively. This pattern is consistent with the behavior observed under Model III in the simulation study, where soft thresholding tends to outperform hard thresholding in settings with less sharply structured sparsity.

From a financial perspective, the relatively weaker performance of tapering is not surprising. Tapering is most effective when the index ordering carries structural information, so that entries farther away from the diagonal are naturally less important. In the present application, however, the ordering of stocks in the data matrix is essentially nominal, and there is no intrinsic reason why stocks with nearby indices should exhibit stronger dependence than those far apart. Consequently, shrinking the estimated long-run covariance matrix according to index distance may be less appropriate in this setting. Overall, despite these differences in signal strength, all three sparse estimators identify exactly the same changepoint date, which provides strong evidence that the estimated break location is stable with respect to the specific sparse regularization used in the normalization step.

From an economic perspective, the date 2021-02-19 is closely aligned with the market regime shift discussed in
34
. Their interpretation links the February 2021 break to a broad repricing of technology and other long-duration growth stocks during the post-pandemic reopening phase, when abundant liquidity began to be offset by a rapid rise in U.S. Treasury yields and a corresponding increase in discount-rate pressure on growth valuations. This mechanism is particularly plausible for NASDAQ data, because the index is heavily tilted toward large technology and growth firms whose valuations are especially sensitive to changes in long-horizon discount rates. In statistical terms, such an episode is consistent with a relatively dense cross-sectional shift, which is precisely the type of alternative for which the
\bm{S}_{n,p}
statistic is designed.

5
Conclusion

This paper studies difference-based estimation of the high-dimensional long-run covariance matrix for temporally dependent time series in the presence of nonstationary mean structures. We show that the proposed difference-based estimator can effectively remove deterministic mean contamination and provide a reliable starting point for high-dimensional long-run covariance estimation. Building on this estimator, we further investigate hard-thresholding, soft-thresholding, and tapering regularization methods, and establish their theoretical convergence properties under suitable sparsity and dependence conditions. The numerical results demonstrate that the regularized difference-based procedures substantially improve estimation accuracy over classical HAC-type methods and remain effective across a range of dependence structures and sample sizes.

There are several important directions for future research. First, the estimated high-dimensional long-run covariance matrix can be incorporated into other statistical inference problems involving dependent data, such as linear and quadratic discriminant analysis, as well as related classification and testing problems; see, for example,
17
;
3
;
14
. Second, the current framework is mainly developed for sparse long-run covariance structures. It would be of substantial interest to extend the methodology to nonsparse settings by combining difference-based ideas with low-rank or approximate factor structures, thereby accommodating more complex dependence patterns commonly encountered in finance, economics, and other high-dimensional applications; see
13
;
8
;
15
.

A.1
Proof of Theorem
2.1

By (
4
),

\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\,DB}-\mathbf{V}=(\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\,\mathrm{or}}-\mathbf{V})+\mathbf{B}_{\mu,n}+\mathbf{R}_{\mu,n}.

Hence,

\left\lVert\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\,DB}-\mathbf{V}\right\rVert_{\max}\leq\left\lVert\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\,\mathrm{or}}-\mathbf{V}\right\rVert_{\max}+\left\lVert\mathbf{B}_{\mu,n}\right\rVert_{\max}+\left\lVert\mathbf{R}_{\mu,n}\right\rVert_{\max}.

(30)

By Assumption
2.4
,

\left\lVert\mathbf{R}_{\mu,n}\right\rVert_{\max}=O_{p}(r_{\mu,n}).

Therefore, it remains to bound
\left\lVert\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\,\mathrm{or}}-\mathbf{V}\right\rVert_{\max}
.
Write

\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\,\mathrm{or}}-\mathbf{V}=\underbrace{\bigl(\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\,\mathrm{or}}-\mathbb{E}\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\,\mathrm{or}}\bigr)}_{\text{oracle fluctuation}}+\underbrace{\bigl(\mathbb{E}\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\,\mathrm{or}}-\mathbf{V}\bigr)}_{\text{oracle bias}}.

Taking max norm yields

\left\lVert\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\,\mathrm{or}}-\mathbf{V}\right\rVert_{\max}\leq\left\lVert\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\,\mathrm{or}}-\mathbb{E}\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\,\mathrm{or}}\right\rVert_{\max}+\left\lVert\mathbb{E}\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\,\mathrm{or}}-\mathbf{V}\right\rVert_{\max}.

(31)

By Assumption
2.2
,

\left\lVert\mathbb{E}\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\,\mathrm{or}}-\mathbf{V}\right\rVert_{\max}\leq C_{B}\left\{\ell_{n}^{-q_{0}}+\frac{(m_{n}+1)h_{n}}{n}\right\}.

(32)

Thus the key step is to derive a high-dimensional max-norm concentration bound for
\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\,\mathrm{or}}-\mathbb{E}\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\,\mathrm{or}}
. Define

S_{rs}:=\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\,\mathrm{or}}[r,s]-\mathbb{E}\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\,\mathrm{or}}[r,s],\qquad 1\leq r,s\leq p_{n}.

For any
t>0
,

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\max_{1\leq r,s\leq p_{n}}|S_{rs}|>t\right)\leq\sum_{r=1}^{p_{n}}\sum_{s=1}^{p_{n}}\mathbb{P}(|S_{rs}|>t).

Applying Assumption
2.3
,

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\max_{r,s}|S_{rs}|>t\right)\leq 2p_{n}^{2}\exp\!\left[-c_{1}n\min\!\left\{\frac{t^{2}}{\ell_{n}+m_{n}h_{n}},\,\frac{t}{\ell_{n}+m_{n}h_{n}}\right\}\right].

(33)

We now choose
t
to balance the polynomial factor
p_{n}^{2}
.
Set

t_{1}:=M_{1}\sqrt{\frac{(\ell_{n}+m_{n}h_{n})\log p_{n}}{n}}

for a constant
M_{1}>0
to be chosen. Then

\frac{nt_{1}^{2}}{\ell_{n}+m_{n}h_{n}}=M_{1}^{2}\log p_{n}.

Substituting into (
33
) gives

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\max_{r,s}|S_{rs}|>t_{1}\right)\leq 2p_{n}^{2-c_{1}M_{1}^{2}}\to 0

as soon as
M_{1}
is chosen large enough so that
c_{1}M_{1}^{2}>3
, say. Therefore,

\left\lVert\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\,\mathrm{or}}-\mathbb{E}\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\,\mathrm{or}}\right\rVert_{\max}=O_{p}\!\left(\sqrt{\frac{(\ell_{n}+m_{n}h_{n})\log p_{n}}{n}}\right)

whenever the quadratic term is the active branch.

To obtain a uniform bound valid across the two branches of the Bernstein inequality, define

t_{2}:=M_{2}\frac{(\ell_{n}+m_{n}h_{n})\log p_{n}}{n},

with
M_{2}>0
large. Then

\frac{nt_{2}}{\ell_{n}+m_{n}h_{n}}=M_{2}\log p_{n},

hence

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\max_{r,s}|S_{rs}|>t_{2}\right)\leq 2p_{n}^{2-c_{1}M_{2}}\to 0

if
M_{2}
is sufficiently large. Combining the two regimes yields the standard Bernstein-type max-norm bound

\left\lVert\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\,\mathrm{or}}-\mathbb{E}\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\,\mathrm{or}}\right\rVert_{\max}=O_{p}\!\left(\sqrt{\frac{(\ell_{n}+m_{n}h_{n})\log p_{n}}{n}}+\frac{(\ell_{n}+m_{n}h_{n})\log p_{n}}{n}\right).

(34)

Substitute (
32
) and (
34
) into (
31
):

\left\lVert\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\,\mathrm{or}}-\mathbf{V}\right\rVert_{\max}=O_{p}\!\left(\ell_{n}^{-q_{0}}+\frac{(m_{n}+1)h_{n}}{n}+\sqrt{\frac{(\ell_{n}+m_{n}h_{n})\log p_{n}}{n}}+\frac{(\ell_{n}+m_{n}h_{n})\log p_{n}}{n}\right).

Then by (
30
) and Assumption
2.4
,

\left\lVert\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\,DB}-\mathbf{V}\right\rVert_{\max}=O_{p}\!\left(\left\lVert\mathbf{B}_{\mu,n}\right\rVert_{\max}+r_{\mu,n}+\ell_{n}^{-q_{0}}+\frac{(m_{n}+1)h_{n}}{n}+\sqrt{\frac{(\ell_{n}+m_{n}h_{n})\log p_{n}}{n}}+\frac{(\ell_{n}+m_{n}h_{n})\log p_{n}}{n}\right).

Then Theorem
2.1
follows. ∎

A.2
Proof of Corollary
2.1

Under the additional conditions of the corollary, the terms

\left\lVert\mathbf{B}_{\mu,n}\right\rVert_{\max}+r_{\mu,n}\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{(m_{n}+1)h_{n}}{n}

are of smaller order than the leading terms, and
m_{n}h_{n}\lesssim\ell_{n}
implies

\ell_{n}+m_{n}h_{n}\asymp\ell_{n}.

Hence Theorem
2.1
yields

\left\lVert\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\,DB}-\mathbf{V}\right\rVert_{\max}=O_{p}\!\left(\ell_{n}^{-q_{0}}+\sqrt{\frac{\ell_{n}\log p_{n}}{n}}+\frac{\ell_{n}\log p_{n}}{n}\right).

If
\ell_{n}\log p_{n}/n\to 0
, then the last term is dominated by the square-root term, so the leading rate is

\ell_{n}^{-q_{0}}+\sqrt{\frac{\ell_{n}\log p_{n}}{n}}.

Balancing these two terms gives

\ell_{n}^{-q_{0}}\asymp\sqrt{\frac{\ell_{n}\log p_{n}}{n}}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\ell_{n}^{2q_{0}+1}\asymp\frac{n}{\log p_{n}}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\ell_{n}\asymp\left(\frac{n}{\log p_{n}}\right)^{1/(2q_{0}+1)}.

Substituting this choice back yields

\ell_{n}^{-q_{0}}\asymp\left(\frac{\log p_{n}}{n}\right)^{q_{0}/(2q_{0}+1)},\qquad\sqrt{\frac{\ell_{n}\log p_{n}}{n}}\asymp\left(\frac{\log p_{n}}{n}\right)^{q_{0}/(2q_{0}+1)}.

Therefore (
6
) follows. ∎

A.3
Proof of Theorem
2.2

By Theorem
2.1
, there exists a constant
C_{0}>0
such that the event

\mathcal{E}_{n}:=\left\{\left\lVert\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\,DB}-\mathbf{V}\right\rVert_{\max}\leq C_{0}a_{n}\right\}

satisfies
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{n})\to 1
. Choose
\tau_{n}=C_{\tau}a_{n}
with
C_{\tau}\geq 2C_{0}
. Then on
\mathcal{E}_{n}
,

\left\lVert\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\,DB}-\mathbf{V}\right\rVert_{\max}\leq\tau_{n}/2.

(35)

Below we work on
\mathcal{E}_{n}
; all bounds then hold with probability tending to one.

Fix
r\neq s
. Let

,

, and
\widehat{v}=\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\,\mathrm{hard}}[r,s]=x\mathbf{1}(|x|\geq\tau_{n})

|x-v|\leq\tau_{n}/2
on
\mathcal{E}_{n}
, we consider two cases.

Case 1:
|x|\geq\tau_{n}
.

Then
\widehat{v}=x

|\widehat{v}-v|=|x-v|\leq\tau_{n}/2.

Case 2:
|x|<\tau_{n}
.

Then
\widehat{v}=0
, and

|\widehat{v}-v|=|v|\leq|v-x|+|x|<\tau_{n}/2+\tau_{n}=\frac{3}{2}\tau_{n}.

Thus, for all

|\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\,\mathrm{hard}}[r,s]-\mathbf{V}[r,s]|\leq\frac{3}{2}\tau_{n}.

For diagonal entries,
\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\,\mathrm{hard}}[r,r]=\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\,DB}[r,r]
, so by (
35
),

|\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\,\mathrm{hard}}[r,r]-\mathbf{V}[r,r]|\leq\tau_{n}/2.

Therefore,

\left\lVert\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\,\mathrm{hard}}-\mathbf{V}\right\rVert_{\max}\leq\frac{3}{2}\tau_{n}=O(a_{n}),

(36)

which proves (
9
).

Fix a row
r
. We show

\sum_{s=1}^{p_{n}}|\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\,\mathrm{hard}}[r,s]-\mathbf{V}[r,s]|\leq Cc_{n,p}\tau_{n}^{1-\alpha}

on

, uniformly in
r

Hence

\sum_{s\neq r}|e_{rs}|\leq\underbrace{\sum_{s\neq r}|x-v|\mathbf{1}(|x|\geq\tau_{n})}_{T_{1r}}+\underbrace{\sum_{s\neq r}|v|\mathbf{1}(|x|<\tau_{n})}_{T_{2r}}.

\mathcal{E}_{n}
, if
|x|\geq\tau_{n}

|v|\geq|x|-|x-v|\geq\tau_{n}/2

Using the weak row-sparsity condition

T_{1r}\leq Cc_{n,p}\tau_{n}^{1-\alpha}.

(39)

On
\mathcal{E}_{n}
, if
|x|<\tau_{n}

|v|<|x|+|x-v|<\tfrac{3}{2}\tau_{n}

|v|\mathbf{1}(|x|<\tau_{n})\leq|v|^{\alpha}\left(\frac{3\tau_{n}}{2}\right)^{1-\alpha}\mathbf{1}(|x|<\tau_{n}).

Summing over
s\neq r

T_{2r}\leq\left(\frac{3\tau_{n}}{2}\right)^{1-\alpha}\sum_{s\neq r}|v_{rs}|^{\alpha}\leq Cc_{n,p}\tau_{n}^{1-\alpha}.

(40)

Combining (
38
), (
39
), and (
40

\sum_{s\neq r}|e_{rs}|\leq Cc_{n,p}\tau_{n}^{1-\alpha}.

The diagonal error contributes at most
\tau_{n}/2
, which is absorbed into the same order since

. Thus (
37
) holds.

Taking the maximum over
r

\left\lVert\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\,\mathrm{hard}}-\mathbf{V}\right\rVert_{1}=\max_{1\leq r\leq p_{n}}\sum_{s=1}^{p_{n}}|e_{rs}|\leq Cc_{n,p}\tau_{n}^{1-\alpha}.

(41)

Since
\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\,\mathrm{hard}}
and
\mathbf{V}
are symmetric (if desired, one can symmetrize
\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\,DB}
before thresholding by replacing it with

, which does not alter the max-norm rate order), we have

\left\lVert\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\,\mathrm{hard}}-\mathbf{V}\right\rVert_{2}\leq\left\lVert\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\,\mathrm{hard}}-\mathbf{V}\right\rVert_{1}.

Using (
41

\tau_{n}\asymp a_{n}
,

\left\lVert\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\,\mathrm{hard}}-\mathbf{V}\right\rVert_{2}=O_{p}\!\bigl(c_{n,p}a_{n}^{1-\alpha}\bigr),

which proves (
10

For any matrix
\mathbf{A}

\left\lVert\mathbf{A}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}=\sum_{r,s}A_{rs}^{2}\leq\max_{r,s}|A_{rs}|\sum_{r,s}|A_{rs}|\leq p_{n}\left\lVert\mathbf{A}\right\rVert_{\max}\,\left\lVert\mathbf{A}\right\rVert_{1}.

Applying this to

Proof of Theorem
2.3

We parallel the proof of Theorem
2.2
, but now for the soft-thresholding map

By Theorem
2.1
, there exists
C_{0}>0
such that

\mathcal{E}_{n}:=\left\{\left\lVert\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\,DB}-\mathbf{V}\right\rVert_{\max}\leq C_{0}a_{n}\right\}

satisfies
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{n})\to 1
. Choose
\tau_{n}=C_{\tau}a_{n}
with
C_{\tau}\geq 2C_{0}
. Then on
\mathcal{E}_{n}

\left\lVert\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\,DB}-\mathbf{V}\right\rVert_{\max}\leq\tau_{n}/2.

We work on
\mathcal{E}_{n}
throughout.

Fix
r\neq s
. Let
x=\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\,DB}[r,s]
,

, and
\widehat{v}=S_{\tau_{n}}(x)

Case 1:
|x|\geq\tau_{n}
.
Then
\widehat{v}=x-\tau_{n}\mathrm{sign}(x)

|\widehat{v}-v|\leq|x-v|+\tau_{n}\leq\frac{3}{2}\tau_{n}.

Case 2:
|x|<\tau_{n}
.
Then
\widehat{v}=0
, and

|\widehat{v}-v|=|v|\leq|v-x|+|x|<\frac{\tau_{n}}{2}+\tau_{n}=\frac{3}{2}\tau_{n}.

Thus, for all off-diagonal entries,

|\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\,\mathrm{soft}}[r,s]-\mathbf{V}[r,s]|\leq\frac{3}{2}\tau_{n}.

|\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\,\mathrm{soft}}[r,r]-\mathbf{V}[r,r]|=|\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\,DB}[r,r]-\mathbf{V}[r,r]|\leq\tau_{n}/2.

Therefore

\left\lVert\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\,\mathrm{soft}}-\mathbf{V}\right\rVert_{\max}\leq\frac{3}{2}\tau_{n}=O(a_{n}),

which proves (
12
).

Fix a row
r
. Let
e_{rs}:=\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\,\mathrm{soft}}[r,s]-\mathbf{V}[r,s]
. For
r\neq s
, note that

|S_{\tau_{n}}(x)-v|\leq|x-v|+\tau_{n}\mathbf{1}(|x|\geq\tau_{n})+|v|\mathbf{1}(|x|<\tau_{n}),

because when
|x|\geq\tau_{n}
, the soft-thresholding shrinkage introduces an extra
\tau_{n}

|x|<\tau_{n}
, the estimator is zero.

Hence

\displaystyle\sum_{s\neq r}|e_{rs}|

\displaystyle\leq\underbrace{\sum_{s\neq r}|x_{rs}-v_{rs}|}_{A_{1r}}+\underbrace{\tau_{n}\sum_{s\neq r}\mathbf{1}(|x_{rs}|\geq\tau_{n})}_{A_{2r}}+\underbrace{\sum_{s\neq r}|v_{rs}|\mathbf{1}(|x_{rs}|<\tau_{n})}_{A_{3r}}.

The first term satisfies
A_{1r}\leq p_{n}(\tau_{n}/2)
, which is too crude if taken directly. Instead, refine it by restricting to indices with
|x_{rs}|\geq\tau_{n}
, since the universal
|x-v|
term only matters in the first case:

|S_{\tau_{n}}(x)-v|\leq\Big(|x-v|+\tau_{n}\Big)\mathbf{1}(|x|\geq\tau_{n})+|v|\mathbf{1}(|x|<\tau_{n}).

Thus

\sum_{s\neq r}|e_{rs}|\leq\underbrace{\sum_{s\neq r}|x_{rs}-v_{rs}|\mathbf{1}(|x_{rs}|\geq\tau_{n})}_{T_{1r}}+\underbrace{\tau_{n}\sum_{s\neq r}\mathbf{1}(|x_{rs}|\geq\tau_{n})}_{T_{2r}}+\underbrace{\sum_{s\neq r}|v_{rs}|\mathbf{1}(|x_{rs}|<\tau_{n})}_{T_{3r}}.

On
\mathcal{E}_{n}
, if
|x_{rs}|\geq\tau_{n}
, then
|v_{rs}|\geq\tau_{n}/2
. Hence

T_{1r}\leq\frac{\tau_{n}}{2}\sum_{s\neq r}\mathbf{1}(|v_{rs}|\geq\tau_{n}/2),\qquad T_{2r}\leq\tau_{n}\sum_{s\neq r}\mathbf{1}(|v_{rs}|\geq\tau_{n}/2).

\sum_{s\neq r}\mathbf{1}(|v_{rs}|\geq\tau_{n}/2)\leq\left(\frac{2}{\tau_{n}}\right)^{\alpha}\sum_{s\neq r}|v_{rs}|^{\alpha}\leq Cc_{n,p}\tau_{n}^{-\alpha}.

Therefore,

T_{1r}+T_{2r}\leq Cc_{n,p}\tau_{n}^{1-\alpha}.

For
T_{3r}
, on
\mathcal{E}_{n}
,
|x_{rs}|<\tau_{n}
implies
|v_{rs}|<3\tau_{n}/2
. Hence

|v_{rs}|\mathbf{1}(|x_{rs}|<\tau_{n})\leq|v_{rs}|^{\alpha}\left(\frac{3\tau_{n}}{2}\right)^{1-\alpha},

and summing gives

T_{3r}\leq Cc_{n,p}\tau_{n}^{1-\alpha}.

Combining,

\sum_{s\neq r}|e_{rs}|\leq Cc_{n,p}\tau_{n}^{1-\alpha}.

The diagonal contribution is

, absorbed by the same order since

. Therefore

The operator norm bound follows exactly as in Theorem
2.2
:

\left\lVert\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\,\mathrm{soft}}-\mathbf{V}\right\rVert_{2}\leq\left\lVert\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\,\mathrm{soft}}-\mathbf{V}\right\rVert_{1}=O_{p}(c_{n,p}a_{n}^{1-\alpha}).

For the Frobenius norm,

\frac{1}{p_{n}}\left\lVert\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\,\mathrm{soft}}-\mathbf{V}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}\leq\left\lVert\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\,\mathrm{soft}}-\mathbf{V}\right\rVert_{\max}\,\left\lVert\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\,\mathrm{soft}}-\mathbf{V}\right\rVert_{1}=O_{p}(a_{n})\cdot O_{p}(c_{n,p}a_{n}^{1-\alpha})=O_{p}(c_{n,p}a_{n}^{2-\alpha}).

This proves (
13
) and (
14
). ∎

A.5
Proof of Theorem
2.4

Let

\mathbf{E}_{n}:=\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\,DB}-\mathbf{V}.

Then the tapering estimator satisfies

\mathbf{V}^{\mathrm{tap}}-\mathbf{V}=\mathbf{W}^{(k_{n})}\circ\mathbf{E}_{n}+\bigl(\mathbf{W}^{(k_{n})}-\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top}\bigr)\circ\mathbf{V}.

We bound the two terms separately.

By Theorem
2.1
,
\left\lVert\mathbf{E}_{n}\right\rVert_{\max}=O_{p}(a_{n})
. On the corresponding high-probability event, for each row
r

(42)

using symmetry.

For the Frobenius norm, each row has at most

nonzero taper weights and

, so

By Definition
2.1
,
w_{rs}^{(k_{n})}=1

2.6

\displaystyle\left\lVert(\mathbf{W}^{(k_{n})}-\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top})\circ\mathbf{V}\right\rVert_{1}

\displaystyle\bar{M}_{2,n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=m_{n}h_{n}+1}^{n}\left\lVert\mathbf{M}_{t}\right\rVert_{\max}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t\notin\mathcal{B}_{n}}\left\{L_{\mu}C_{d,\beta}\left(\frac{h_{n}}{n}\right)^{\beta}\right\}^{2}+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{B}_{n}}\left(C_{d,1}M_{\mu}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq L_{\mu}^{2}C_{d,\beta}^{2}\left(\frac{h_{n}}{n}\right)^{2\beta}+C_{d,1}^{2}M_{\mu}^{2}\,\frac{|\mathcal{B}_{n}|}{n}.

Using (
47
) gives (
22
).

Taking maxima in the good/bad bounds,

\bar{M}_{\infty,n}\leq\max\!\left\{L_{\mu}C_{d,\beta}\left(\frac{h_{n}}{n}\right)^{\beta},\,C_{d,1}M_{\mu}\right\}\leq C\left\{C_{d,\beta}\left(\frac{h_{n}}{n}\right)^{\beta}+C_{d,1}M_{\mu}\right\},

which proves (
23
).

Equation (
24
) follows immediately from Proposition
2.1
and (
22
). Equation (
25
) follows from Proposition
2.2
, (
22
), and (
23
). ∎

B.4
Proof of Proposition
2.4

Let
\Delta\bm{\mu}_{u}:=\bm{\mu}_{u}-\bm{\mu}_{u-1}
for
u=2,\ldots,n
. By Assumption
2.9
,

\sum_{u=2}^{n}\left\lVert\Delta\bm{\mu}_{u}\right\rVert_{\max}\leq\sum_{u=2}^{n}\max_{1\leq r\leq p_{n}}|\mu_{u}^{(r)}-\mu_{u-1}^{(r)}|\leq\max_{1\leq r\leq p_{n}}\sum_{u=2}^{n}|\mu_{u}^{(r)}-\mu_{u-1}^{(r)}|\leq V_{\mu,n}.

Using
\sum_{j=0}^{m_{n}}d_{n,j}=0
as in (
46
),

\left\lVert\mathbf{M}_{t}\right\rVert_{\max}\leq\sum_{j=1}^{m_{n}}|d_{n,j}|\,\left\lVert\bm{\mu}_{t}-\bm{\mu}_{t-jh_{n}}\right\rVert_{\max}\leq C_{d,1}\max_{1\leq j\leq m_{n}}\left\lVert\bm{\mu}_{t}-\bm{\mu}_{t-jh_{n}}\right\rVert_{\max}.

For each
j\leq m_{n}
,

\left\lVert\bm{\mu}_{t}-\bm{\mu}_{t-jh_{n}}\right\rVert_{\max}\leq\sum_{u=t-jh_{n}+1}^{t}\left\lVert\Delta\bm{\mu}_{u}\right\rVert_{\max}\leq\sum_{u=t-m_{n}h_{n}+1}^{t}\left\lVert\Delta\bm{\mu}_{u}\right\rVert_{\max}.

Hence

\left\lVert\mathbf{M}_{t}\right\rVert_{\max}\leq C_{d,1}\sum_{u=t-m_{n}h_{n}+1}^{t}\left\lVert\Delta\bm{\mu}_{u}\right\rVert_{\max}.

Squaring and using Cauchy–Schwarz,

\left(\sum_{u=t-m_{n}h_{n}+1}^{t}a_{u}\right)^{2}\leq(m_{n}h_{n})\sum_{u=t-m_{n}h_{n}+1}^{t}a_{u}^{2},

with
a_{u}=\left\lVert\Delta\bm{\mu}_{u}\right\rVert_{\max}
. Therefore

\left\lVert\mathbf{M}_{t}\right\rVert_{\max}^{2}\leq C_{d,1}^{2}(m_{n}h_{n})\sum_{u=t-m_{n}h_{n}+1}^{t}\left\lVert\Delta\bm{\mu}_{u}\right\rVert_{\max}^{2}.

Summing over
t
and dividing by
n
, each
\left\lVert\Delta\bm{\mu}_{u}\right\rVert_{\max}^{2}
is counted at most
m_{n}h_{n}
times, so

\displaystyle\bar{M}_{2,n}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C_{d,1}^{2}(m_{n}h_{n})^{2}}{n}\sum_{u=2}^{n}\left\lVert\Delta\bm{\mu}_{u}\right\rVert_{\max}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C_{d,1}^{2}(m_{n}h_{n})^{2}}{n}\left(\sum_{u=2}^{n}\left\lVert\Delta\bm{\mu}_{u}\right\rVert_{\max}\right)^{2}\leq C\,C_{d,1}^{2}(m_{n}h_{n})^{2}\frac{V_{\mu,n}^{2}}{n},

which proves (
26
).

Next,
\bar{M}_{\infty,n}\leq\sum_{j=0}^{m_{n}}|d_{n,j}|\sup_{t}\left\lVert\bm{\mu}_{t}\right\rVert_{\max}\leq C_{d,1}M_{\mu}
, and enlarging constants yields (
27
). The bounds (
28
) and (
29
) then follow from Propositions
2.1
and
2.2
. ∎

A unified sufficient condition for Assumptions
2.2
,
2.3
and

Assumption C.1

(A unified sufficient condition)
.

Assume that the noise process admits a stable linear representation

\mathbf{Z}_{t}=\sum_{a=0}^{\infty}\mathbf{A}_{a}\,\bm{\varepsilon}_{t-a},\qquad t\in\mathbb{Z},

where the innovations
\{\bm{\varepsilon}_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
are i.i.d. in

\mathbb{R}^{p_{n}}
and satisfy the following conditions.

(U1)

(
Independent sub-Gaussian coordinates
)
For each
t
, the coordinates of
\bm{\varepsilon}_{t}=(\varepsilon_{t,1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{t,p_{n}})^{\top}
are independent,

\mathbb{E}(\bm{\varepsilon}_{t})=\mathbf{0}
, and

\max_{1\leq j\leq p_{n}}\|\varepsilon_{0,j}\|_{\psi_{2}}\leq K_{\varepsilon}<\infty.

(U2)

(
Stable and polynomially decaying filter
)
There exist constants
K_{A}<\infty
,
\widetilde{K}_{A}<\infty
, and
\eta>0
such that

\sum_{a=0}^{\infty}\|\mathbf{A}_{a}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq K_{A},

and

\|\mathbf{A}_{a}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\widetilde{K}_{A}(1+a)^{-(q_{0}+2+\eta)},\qquad a\geq 0.

(U3)

(
Kernel and difference weights
)
The kernel is bounded on
[-1,1]
:

\sup_{|x|\leq 1}|K(x)|\leq K_{0},

and the difference sequence satisfies

\sum_{j=0}^{m_{n}}|d_{n,j}|\leq K_{d}

in addition to (
1
).

(U4)

(
Bandwidth/spacing rates
)

\ell_{n}=o(h_{n}),\qquad\frac{h_{n}^{\,q_{0}+1+\eta}}{\ell_{n}^{\,q_{0}+1}}\to\infty,\qquad\ell_{n}\leq h_{n}.

Lemma C.1
.

Under Assumption
C.1
, for every fixed
q\geq 4
, the following hold.

(i)

(
Uniform moment bound
)
There exists a constant
C_{q}>0
, depending only on

q,K_{\varepsilon},K_{A}
, such that

\max_{1\leq r\leq p_{n}}\|Z_{0,r}\|_{q}\leq C_{q}.

(ii)

(
Physical dependence bound
)
Let
\mathbf{Z}_{k}^{(0)}
be the coupled version obtained by replacing

\bm{\varepsilon}_{0}
with an i.i.d. copy
\bm{\varepsilon}_{0}^{\prime}
.
Then

\delta_{q}(k):=\max_{1\leq r\leq p_{n}}\|Z_{k,r}-Z_{k,r}^{(0)}\|_{q}\leq C_{q}(1+k)^{-(q_{0}+2+\eta)},\qquad k\geq 1.

(iii)

(
Covariance decay
)
Let
\Gamma_{k}=\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{Z}_{0}\mathbf{Z}_{k}^{\top})
. Then there exists
C_{\Gamma}>0
such that

\|\Gamma_{k}\|_{\max}\leq C_{\Gamma}(1+|k|)^{-(q_{0}+2+\eta)},\qquad k\in\mathbb{Z}.

Consequently,

\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\|\Gamma_{k}\|_{\max}<\infty,\qquad\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}(1+|k|)^{q_{0}}\|\Gamma_{k}\|_{\max}<\infty.

Proof.

We prove the three statements in turn.

Proof of (i).

Fix
r
. Since

Z_{0,r}=\sum_{a=0}^{\infty}e_{r}^{\top}\mathbf{A}_{a}\bm{\varepsilon}_{-a},

by Minkowski’s inequality,

\|Z_{0,r}\|_{q}\leq\sum_{a=0}^{\infty}\|e_{r}^{\top}\mathbf{A}_{a}\bm{\varepsilon}_{-a}\|_{q}.

Because the coordinates of
\bm{\varepsilon}_{-a}
are independent and uniformly
sub-Gaussian, every linear form is sub-Gaussian, hence for each fixed
q\geq 2
,

\|e_{r}^{\top}\mathbf{A}_{a}\bm{\varepsilon}_{-a}\|_{q}\leq C\sqrt{q}\,K_{\varepsilon}\,\|\mathbf{A}_{a}^{\top}e_{r}\|_{2}\leq C\sqrt{q}\,K_{\varepsilon}\,\|\mathbf{A}_{a}\|_{\mathrm{op}}.

Therefore,

\|Z_{0,r}\|_{q}\leq C\sqrt{q}\,K_{\varepsilon}\sum_{a=0}^{\infty}\|\mathbf{A}_{a}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq C\sqrt{q}\,K_{\varepsilon}K_{A}.

Taking the maximum over
r
proves (i).

Proof of (ii).

For
k\geq 1
,

\mathbf{Z}_{k}-\mathbf{Z}_{k}^{(0)}=\mathbf{A}_{k}(\bm{\varepsilon}_{0}-\bm{\varepsilon}_{0}^{\prime}).

Hence, for each coordinate
r
,

Z_{k,r}-Z_{k,r}^{(0)}=e_{r}^{\top}\mathbf{A}_{k}(\bm{\varepsilon}_{0}-\bm{\varepsilon}_{0}^{\prime}).

Applying again the linear-form bound for independent sub-Gaussian coordinates yields

\|Z_{k,r}-Z_{k,r}^{(0)}\|_{q}\leq C\sqrt{q}\,K_{\varepsilon}\,\|\mathbf{A}_{k}\|_{\mathrm{op}}.

Using (U2),

\delta_{q}(k)\leq C\sqrt{q}\,K_{\varepsilon}\,\widetilde{K}_{A}(1+k)^{-(q_{0}+2+\eta)}.

This proves (ii).

Proof of (iii).

Let

\Sigma_{\varepsilon}:=\mathbb{E}(\bm{\varepsilon}_{0}\bm{\varepsilon}_{0}^{\top}).

Since the coordinates of
\bm{\varepsilon}_{0}
are independent and centered,

\Sigma_{\varepsilon}
is diagonal, and

\|\Sigma_{\varepsilon}\|_{\mathrm{op}}=\max_{1\leq j\leq p_{n}}\mathrm{Var}(\varepsilon_{0,j})\leq CK_{\varepsilon}^{2}.

For
k\geq 0
, by the linear-process representation and independence across times,

\Gamma_{k}=\sum_{a=0}^{\infty}\mathbf{A}_{a}\Sigma_{\varepsilon}\mathbf{A}_{a+k}^{\top}.

Therefore,

\|\Gamma_{k}\|_{\max}\leq\|\Gamma_{k}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\sum_{a=0}^{\infty}\|\mathbf{A}_{a}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\,\|\Sigma_{\varepsilon}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\,\|\mathbf{A}_{a+k}\|_{\mathrm{op}}.

Using
\sup_{a\geq 0}\|\mathbf{A}_{a+k}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\widetilde{K}_{A}(1+k)^{-(q_{0}+2+\eta)}
and

\sum_{a\geq 0}\|\mathbf{A}_{a}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq K_{A}
, we get

\|\Gamma_{k}\|_{\max}\leq CK_{\varepsilon}^{2}K_{A}\widetilde{K}_{A}(1+k)^{-(q_{0}+2+\eta)}.

For negative
k
, use
\Gamma_{-k}=\Gamma_{k}^{\top}
, so the same bound holds with
|k|
.
Hence

\|\Gamma_{k}\|_{\max}\leq C_{\Gamma}(1+|k|)^{-(q_{0}+2+\eta)}.

Since
q_{0}+2+\eta>q_{0}+1
, both series

\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\|\Gamma_{k}\|_{\max},\qquad\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}(1+|k|)^{q_{0}}\|\Gamma_{k}\|_{\max}

converge. This proves (iii).
∎

Proposition C.1

(Entrywise Bernstein concentration for the oracle estimator)
.

Under Assumption
C.1
, there exist constants
c_{1},c_{2}>0

depending only on
K_{\varepsilon},K_{A},K_{0},K_{d}
such that for all sufficiently large
n
,
all
1\leq r,s\leq p_{n}
, and all
t>0
,

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\big|\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\,\mathrm{or}}[r,s]-\mathbb{E}\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\,\mathrm{or}}[r,s]\big|>t\right)\leq 2\exp\!\left[-c_{1}n\min\!\left\{\frac{t^{2}}{\ell_{n}},\,\frac{t}{\ell_{n}}\right\}\right].

In particular, Assumption
2.3
holds, because

\ell_{n}\leq\ell_{n}+m_{n}h_{n}.

Proof.

Fix
1\leq r,s\leq p_{n}
.
We first prove the result for a truncated linear process and then let the truncation level tend to infinity.

For
M\geq 0
, define

\mathbf{Z}_{t}^{(M)}:=\sum_{a=0}^{M}\mathbf{A}_{a}\bm{\varepsilon}_{t-a},\qquad\mathbf{U}_{t}^{(M)}:=\sum_{j=0}^{m_{n}}d_{n,j}\mathbf{Z}_{t-jh_{n}}^{(M)}.

Let

{\hat{\mathbf{V}}^{or(M)}}[r,s]

be the oracle estimator obtained from
\{\mathbf{U}_{t}^{(M)}\}
in place of
\{\mathbf{U}_{t}\}
.

Write

\mathbf{z}_{r}^{(M)}:=(Z_{1,r}^{(M)},\ldots,Z_{n,r}^{(M)})^{\top},\qquad\mathbf{z}_{s}^{(M)}:=(Z_{1,s}^{(M)},\ldots,Z_{n,s}^{(M)})^{\top}.

Also let

\mathbf{u}_{r}^{(M)}:=(U_{1,r}^{(M)},\ldots,U_{n,r}^{(M)})^{\top},\qquad\mathbf{u}_{s}^{(M)}:=(U_{1,s}^{(M)},\ldots,U_{n,s}^{(M)})^{\top}.

Define the
n\times n
matrix

(\mathbf{H}_{n})_{tu}:=K\!\left(\frac{t-u}{\ell_{n}}\right)\mathbf{1}\{|t-u|<\ell_{n}\},\qquad 1\leq t,u\leq n.

Then, up to the usual boundary convention (equivalently, by extending the series by zero
outside the valid range),

{\hat{\mathbf{V}}^{or(M)}}[r,s]=\frac{1}{n}(\mathbf{u}_{r}^{(M)})^{\top}\mathbf{H}_{n}\mathbf{u}_{s}^{(M)}.

Next define the
n\times n
linear filter matrix

(\mathbf{F}_{n})_{tv}:=\sum_{j=0}^{m_{n}}d_{n,j}\,\mathbf{1}\{v=t-jh_{n}\},\qquad 1\leq t,v\leq n,

so that

\mathbf{u}_{r}^{(M)}=\mathbf{F}_{n}\mathbf{z}_{r}^{(M)},\qquad\mathbf{u}_{s}^{(M)}=\mathbf{F}_{n}\mathbf{z}_{s}^{(M)}.

Now collect the innovations into the finite vector

\bm{\xi}_{M}:=(\bm{\varepsilon}_{1-M}^{\top},\ldots,\bm{\varepsilon}_{n}^{\top})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{(n+M)p_{n}}.

There exist matrices
\mathbf{G}_{r,M}
and
\mathbf{G}_{s,M}
such that

\mathbf{z}_{r}^{(M)}=\mathbf{G}_{r,M}\bm{\xi}_{M},\qquad\mathbf{z}_{s}^{(M)}=\mathbf{G}_{s,M}\bm{\xi}_{M}.

Hence

\mathbf{u}_{r}^{(M)}=\mathbf{L}_{r,M}\bm{\xi}_{M},\qquad\mathbf{u}_{s}^{(M)}=\mathbf{L}_{s,M}\bm{\xi}_{M},

where

\mathbf{L}_{r,M}:=\mathbf{F}_{n}\mathbf{G}_{r,M},\qquad\mathbf{L}_{s,M}:=\mathbf{F}_{n}\mathbf{G}_{s,M}.

Therefore,

{\hat{\mathbf{V}}^{or(M)}}[r,s]=\frac{1}{n}\,\bm{\xi}_{M}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}_{M}\bm{\xi}_{M},

where

\mathbf{Q}_{M}:=\frac{1}{2}\Big(\mathbf{L}_{r,M}^{\top}\mathbf{H}_{n}\mathbf{L}_{s,M}+\mathbf{L}_{s,M}^{\top}\mathbf{H}_{n}^{\top}\mathbf{L}_{r,M}\Big).

The matrix
\mathbf{Q}_{M}
is symmetric, so Hanson–Wright applies directly.

First,
\mathbf{F}_{n}
satisfies

\|\mathbf{F}_{n}\|_{\infty}\leq\sum_{j=0}^{m_{n}}|d_{n,j}|\leq K_{d},\qquad\|\mathbf{F}_{n}\|_{1}\leq\sum_{j=0}^{m_{n}}|d_{n,j}|\leq K_{d}.

Hence

\|\mathbf{F}_{n}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\sqrt{\|\mathbf{F}_{n}\|_{1}\|\mathbf{F}_{n}\|_{\infty}}\leq K_{d}.

Second, since
\mathbf{H}_{n}
has at most
2\ell_{n}-1
nonzero entries in each row and each column,
and each nonzero entry is bounded by
K_{0}
, we have

\|\mathbf{H}_{n}\|_{\infty}\leq(2\ell_{n}-1)K_{0},\qquad\|\mathbf{H}_{n}\|_{1}\leq(2\ell_{n}-1)K_{0},

so

\|\mathbf{H}_{n}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\sqrt{\|\mathbf{H}_{n}\|_{1}\|\mathbf{H}_{n}\|_{\infty}}\leq C\ell_{n}.

Moreover,
\mathbf{H}_{n}
has at most
n(2\ell_{n}-1)
nonzero entries, each of magnitude at most
K_{0}
,
so

\|\mathbf{H}_{n}\|_{F}^{2}\leq Cn\ell_{n}.

Third, we bound
\mathbf{G}_{r,M}
. Let

x=(x_{1-M}^{\top},\ldots,x_{n}^{\top})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{(n+M)p_{n}},\qquad x_{\tau}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{n}}.

Then

(\mathbf{G}_{r,M}x)_{t}=\sum_{a=0}^{M}e_{r}^{\top}\mathbf{A}_{a}x_{t-a},\qquad 1\leq t\leq n.

Hence, by the triangle inequality in
\ell_{2}
,

\|\mathbf{G}_{r,M}x\|_{2}=\left\|\sum_{a=0}^{M}\big(e_{r}^{\top}\mathbf{A}_{a}x_{1-a},\ldots,e_{r}^{\top}\mathbf{A}_{a}x_{n-a}\big)^{\top}\right\|_{2}\leq\sum_{a=0}^{M}\left(\sum_{t=1}^{n}|e_{r}^{\top}\mathbf{A}_{a}x_{t-a}|^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

Since

|e_{r}^{\top}\mathbf{A}_{a}x_{t-a}|\leq\|\mathbf{A}_{a}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\|x_{t-a}\|_{2},

we obtain

\|\mathbf{G}_{r,M}x\|_{2}\leq\sum_{a=0}^{M}\|\mathbf{A}_{a}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\left(\sum_{t=1}^{n}\|x_{t-a}\|_{2}^{2}\right)^{1/2}\leq\left(\sum_{a=0}^{M}\|\mathbf{A}_{a}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\right)\|x\|_{2}\leq K_{A}\|x\|_{2}.

Therefore,

\|\mathbf{G}_{r,M}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq K_{A},\qquad\|\mathbf{G}_{s,M}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq K_{A}.

It follows that

\|\mathbf{L}_{r,M}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\|\mathbf{F}_{n}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\|\mathbf{G}_{r,M}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq K_{d}K_{A},

and similarly

\|\mathbf{L}_{s,M}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq K_{d}K_{A}.

Using the previous bounds,

\|\mathbf{Q}_{M}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\|\mathbf{L}_{r,M}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\|\mathbf{H}_{n}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\|\mathbf{L}_{s,M}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq C\ell_{n},

and

\|\mathbf{Q}_{M}\|_{F}\leq\|\mathbf{L}_{r,M}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\|\mathbf{H}_{n}\|_{F}\|\mathbf{L}_{s,M}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq C\sqrt{n\ell_{n}}.

All constants here depend only on
K_{A},K_{d},K_{0}
.

By (U1), the entries of
\bm{\xi}_{M}
are independent, centered, and uniformly sub-Gaussian.
Therefore the Hanson–Wright inequality gives

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\left|\bm{\xi}_{M}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}_{M}\bm{\xi}_{M}-\mathbb{E}(\bm{\xi}_{M}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}_{M}\bm{\xi}_{M})\right|>x\right)\leq 2\exp\!\left[-c\min\!\left\{\frac{x^{2}}{\|\mathbf{Q}_{M}\|_{F}^{2}},\frac{x}{\|\mathbf{Q}_{M}\|_{\mathrm{op}}}\right\}\right].

Using the norm bounds above and setting
x=nt
yields

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\left|{\hat{\mathbf{V}}^{or(M)}}[r,s]-\mathbb{E}{\hat{\mathbf{V}}^{or(M)}}[r,s]\right|>t\right)\leq 2\exp\!\left[-c_{1}n\min\!\left\{\frac{t^{2}}{\ell_{n}},\frac{t}{\ell_{n}}\right\}\right],

where
c_{1}>0
depends only on
K_{\varepsilon},K_{A},K_{d},K_{0}
, and crucially is
independent of
M,n,r,s
.

For each fixed
n
, since

\sum_{a=0}^{\infty}\|\mathbf{A}_{a}\|_{\mathrm{op}}<\infty,

we have

\max_{1\leq r\leq p_{n}}\|Z_{t,r}-Z_{t,r}^{(M)}\|_{2}\leq C\sum_{a>M}\|\mathbf{A}_{a}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\to 0,\qquad M\to\infty,

uniformly in the finitely many
t
appearing in the estimator.
By the definition of
\mathbf{U}_{t}
and
\sum_{j}|d_{n,j}|\leq K_{d}
,

\max_{1\leq r\leq p_{n}}\|U_{t,r}-U_{t,r}^{(M)}\|_{2}\to 0.

Since
\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\,\mathrm{or}}[r,s]
is a finite weighted average of products

U_{t,r}U_{u,s}
, it follows that

{\hat{\mathbf{V}}^{or(M)}}[r,s]\to\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\,\mathrm{or}}[r,s]\quad\text{in }L^{1},

and hence

\mathbb{E}{\hat{\mathbf{V}}^{or(M)}}[r,s]\to\mathbb{E}\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\,\mathrm{or}}[r,s].

Now fix
\tau\in(0,t)
. Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\!\left(\big|\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\,\mathrm{or}}[r,s]-\mathbb{E}\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\,\mathrm{or}}[r,s]\big|>t\right)\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\!\left(\big|{\hat{\mathbf{V}}^{or(M)}}[r,s]-\mathbb{E}{\hat{\mathbf{V}}^{or(M)}}[r,s]\big|>t-\tau\right)

\displaystyle+\mathbb{P}\!\left(\big|\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\,\mathrm{or}}[r,s]-{\hat{\mathbf{V}}^{or(M)}}[r,s]\big|+\big|\mathbb{E}\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\,\mathrm{or}}[r,s]-\mathbb{E}{\hat{\mathbf{V}}^{or(M)}}[r,s]\big|>\tau\right).

Letting
M\to\infty
, the second probability tends to zero, while the first is bounded by the
truncated-process inequality. Hence

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\big|\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\,\mathrm{or}}[r,s]-\mathbb{E}\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\,\mathrm{or}}[r,s]\big|>t\right)\leq 2\exp\!\left[-c_{1}n\min\!\left\{\frac{(t-\tau)^{2}}{\ell_{n}},\frac{t-\tau}{\ell_{n}}\right\}\right].

Finally let
\tau\downarrow 0
to obtain

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\big|\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\,\mathrm{or}}[r,s]-\mathbb{E}\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\,\mathrm{or}}[r,s]\big|>t\right)\leq 2\exp\!\left[-c_{1}n\min\!\left\{\frac{t^{2}}{\ell_{n}},\frac{t}{\ell_{n}}\right\}\right].

This proves the proposition.
∎

Proposition C.2
.

Under Assumption
C.1
, there exists a constant
C_{B}>0
,
independent of
n
and
(r,s)
, such that

\|\mathbb{E}(\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\,\mathrm{or}})-\mathbf{V}\|_{\max}\leq C_{B}\left\{\ell_{n}^{-q_{0}}+\frac{(m_{n}+1)h_{n}}{n}\right\}.

Consequently, Assumption
2.2
holds.

Proof.

Let
\Gamma_{k}=\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{Z}_{0}\mathbf{Z}_{k}^{\top})
and

\Gamma_{k}^{U}:=\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{U}_{0}\mathbf{U}_{k}^{\top}).

By Lemma
C.1
,

\|\Gamma_{k}\|_{\max}\leq C_{\Gamma}(1+|k|)^{-(q_{0}+2+\eta)},\qquad k\in\mathbb{Z},

and

\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\|\Gamma_{k}\|_{\max}<\infty,\qquad\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}(1+|k|)^{q_{0}}\|\Gamma_{k}\|_{\max}<\infty.

By stationarity of
\{\mathbf{U}_{t}\}
,

\mathbb{E}\widehat{\mathbf{\Gamma}}^{\,U}_{k}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=m_{n}h_{n}+|k|+1}^{n}\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{U}_{t}\mathbf{U}_{t-|k|}^{\top})=\left(1-\frac{m_{n}h_{n}+|k|}{n}\right)\Gamma_{|k|}^{U}.

Therefore,

\mathbb{E}\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\,\mathrm{or}}=\sum_{|k|<\ell_{n}}K\!\left(\frac{k}{\ell_{n}}\right)\left(1-\frac{m_{n}h_{n}+|k|}{n}\right)\Gamma_{|k|}^{U}.

Recall

\mathbf{V}=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\Gamma_{k}.

Add and subtract appropriate terms to write

\mathbb{E}\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\,\mathrm{or}}-\mathbf{V}=B_{\mathrm{ker}}+B_{\mathrm{tail}}+B_{\mathrm{bdry}}+B_{\mathrm{cross}},

where

\displaystyle B_{\mathrm{ker}}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{|k|<\ell_{n}}\left\{K\!\left(\frac{k}{\ell_{n}}\right)-1\right\}\Gamma_{k},

\displaystyle B_{\mathrm{tail}}

\displaystyle:=-\sum_{|k|\geq\ell_{n}}\Gamma_{k},

\displaystyle B_{\mathrm{bdry}}

\displaystyle:=-\sum_{|k|<\ell_{n}}K\!\left(\frac{k}{\ell_{n}}\right)\frac{m_{n}h_{n}+|k|}{n}\Gamma_{k},

\displaystyle B_{\mathrm{cross}}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{|k|<\ell_{n}}K\!\left(\frac{k}{\ell_{n}}\right)\left(1-\frac{m_{n}h_{n}+|k|}{n}\right)(\Gamma_{k}^{U}-\Gamma_{k}).

Hence

\|\mathbb{E}\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\,\mathrm{or}}-\mathbf{V}\|_{\max}\leq\|B_{\mathrm{ker}}\|_{\max}+\|B_{\mathrm{tail}}\|_{\max}+\|B_{\mathrm{bdry}}\|_{\max}+\|B_{\mathrm{cross}}\|_{\max}.

By Assumption
2.1
,

|1-K(x)|\leq C_{K}|x|^{q_{0}},\qquad|x|\leq 1.

Therefore,

\|B_{\mathrm{ker}}\|_{\max}\leq\sum_{|k|<\ell_{n}}\left|1-K\!\left(\frac{k}{\ell_{n}}\right)\right|\|\Gamma_{k}\|_{\max}\leq C_{K}\ell_{n}^{-q_{0}}\sum_{|k|<\ell_{n}}|k|^{q_{0}}\|\Gamma_{k}\|_{\max}.

Using (
C
),

\|B_{\mathrm{ker}}\|_{\max}\lesssim\ell_{n}^{-q_{0}}.

Similarly,

\|B_{\mathrm{tail}}\|_{\max}\leq\sum_{|k|\geq\ell_{n}}\|\Gamma_{k}\|_{\max}\leq\ell_{n}^{-q_{0}}\sum_{|k|\geq\ell_{n}}|k|^{q_{0}}\|\Gamma_{k}\|_{\max}\lesssim\ell_{n}^{-q_{0}}.

Using
\sup_{|x|\leq 1}|K(x)|\leq K_{0}
,

\|B_{\mathrm{bdry}}\|_{\max}\leq\frac{K_{0}}{n}\sum_{|k|<\ell_{n}}(m_{n}h_{n}+|k|)\|\Gamma_{k}\|_{\max}.

Hence

\|B_{\mathrm{bdry}}\|_{\max}\leq\frac{K_{0}(m_{n}h_{n}+\ell_{n})}{n}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\|\Gamma_{k}\|_{\max}\lesssim\frac{m_{n}h_{n}+\ell_{n}}{n}.

Since
\ell_{n}\leq h_{n}
by (U4),

\|B_{\mathrm{bdry}}\|_{\max}\lesssim\frac{(m_{n}+1)h_{n}}{n}.

By definition,

\mathbf{U}_{t}=\sum_{j=0}^{m_{n}}d_{n,j}\mathbf{Z}_{t-jh_{n}},

so

\Gamma_{k}^{U}=\sum_{j=0}^{m_{n}}\sum_{j^{\prime}=0}^{m_{n}}d_{n,j}d_{n,j^{\prime}}\,\Gamma_{k+(j-j^{\prime})h_{n}}.

Using the normalization in (
1
), the diagonal part reproduces
\Gamma_{k}
,
thus

\Gamma_{k}^{U}-\Gamma_{k}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq j,j^{\prime}\leq m_{n}\\
j\neq j^{\prime}\end{subarray}}d_{n,j}d_{n,j^{\prime}}\,\Gamma_{k+(j-j^{\prime})h_{n}}.

Therefore,

\|\Gamma_{k}^{U}-\Gamma_{k}\|_{\max}\leq\sum_{j\neq j^{\prime}}|d_{n,j}d_{n,j^{\prime}}|\,\|\Gamma_{k+(j-j^{\prime})h_{n}}\|_{\max}.

Since
\sum_{j}|d_{n,j}|\leq K_{d}
,

\|\Gamma_{k}^{U}-\Gamma_{k}\|_{\max}\leq K_{d}^{2}\max_{1\leq|a|\leq m_{n}}\|\Gamma_{k+ah_{n}}\|_{\max}.

Now fix
|k|<\ell_{n}
and
1\leq|a|\leq m_{n}
. Then

|k+ah_{n}|\geq h_{n}-\ell_{n}.

Hence, by Lemma
C.1
,

\|\Gamma_{k+ah_{n}}\|_{\max}\leq C_{\Gamma}(1+h_{n}-\ell_{n})^{-(q_{0}+2+\eta)}\lesssim h_{n}^{-(q_{0}+2+\eta)},

where we used
\ell_{n}=o(h_{n})
.
Thus uniformly in
|k|<\ell_{n}
,

\|\Gamma_{k}^{U}-\Gamma_{k}\|_{\max}\lesssim h_{n}^{-(q_{0}+2+\eta)}.

Therefore,

\|B_{\mathrm{cross}}\|_{\max}\leq\sum_{|k|<\ell_{n}}\left|K\!\left(\frac{k}{\ell_{n}}\right)\right|\|\Gamma_{k}^{U}-\Gamma_{k}\|_{\max}\lesssim\ell_{n}h_{n}^{-(q_{0}+2+\eta)}.

By (U4),

\ell_{n}h_{n}^{-(q_{0}+1+\eta)}=o(\ell_{n}^{-q_{0}}),

hence a fortiori

\ell_{n}h_{n}^{-(q_{0}+2+\eta)}=o(\ell_{n}^{-q_{0}}).

Therefore,

\|B_{\mathrm{cross}}\|_{\max}=o(\ell_{n}^{-q_{0}}).

Collecting the estimates from Steps 3–6 gives

\|\mathbb{E}(\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\,\mathrm{or}})-\mathbf{V}\|_{\max}\leq C_{1}\ell_{n}^{-q_{0}}+C_{2}\frac{(m_{n}+1)h_{n}}{n}+o(\ell_{n}^{-q_{0}}).

For all sufficiently large
n
, the little-
o
term can be absorbed into the first term, so

\|\mathbb{E}(\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\,\mathrm{or}})-\mathbf{V}\|_{\max}\leq C_{B}\left\{\ell_{n}^{-q_{0}}+\frac{(m_{n}+1)h_{n}}{n}\right\}

for some constant
C_{B}>0
independent of
n
and
(r,s)
.
This completes the proof.
∎

Proposition C.3
.

Under Assumption
C.1
, Assumption
2.7
holds. More precisely, there exists a constant
c_{3}>0
,
depending only on
K_{\varepsilon},K_{A},K_{d},K_{0}
, such that, conditional on

\{\bm{\mu}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{n}
, for all sufficiently large
n
and all
t>0
,

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\left\lVert\mathbf{R}_{\mu,n}\right\rVert_{\max}>t\ \middle|\ \{\bm{\mu}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{n}\right)\leq 4p_{n}^{2}\exp\!\left(-c_{3}n\frac{t^{2}}{\ell_{n}^{2}\bar{M}_{2,n}}\right).

(48)

Consequently,

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\left\lVert\mathbf{R}_{\mu,n}\right\rVert_{\max}>t\ \middle|\ \{\bm{\mu}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{n}\right)\leq 4p_{n}^{2}\exp\!\left[-c_{3}n\min\!\left\{\frac{t^{2}}{L_{n}^{2}\bar{M}_{2,n}},\frac{t}{L_{n}\bar{M}_{\infty,n}}\right\}\right].

(49)

In particular, this verifies Assumption
2.7
.

Proof.

Fix any
1\leq r,s\leq p_{n}
. As in the proof of Proposition
C.1
,
for
M\geq 0
, define the truncated linear process

\mathbf{Z}_{t}^{(M)}:=\sum_{a=0}^{M}\mathbf{A}_{a}\bm{\varepsilon}_{t-a},\qquad\mathbf{U}_{t}^{(M)}:=\sum_{j=0}^{m_{n}}d_{n,j}\mathbf{Z}_{t-jh_{n}}^{(M)}.

Let
\mathbf{R}_{\mu,n}^{(M)}
be the analogue of
\mathbf{R}_{\mu,n}
obtained by replacing

\mathbf{U}_{t}
with
\mathbf{U}_{t}^{(M)}
in (
4
). Then, by (
4
),

\displaystyle\mathbf{R}_{\mu,n}^{(M)}[r,s]

\displaystyle=\sum_{|k|<\ell_{n}}K\!\left(\frac{k}{\ell_{n}}\right)\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=m_{n}h_{n}+|k|+1}^{n}\Bigl\{M_{t}^{(r)}U_{t-|k|}^{(M,s)}+U_{t}^{(M,r)}M_{t-|k|}^{(s)}\Bigr\}

\displaystyle=:I_{r,s}^{(M)}+J_{r,s}^{(M)}.

(50)

Introduce the deterministic vectors

\mathbf{m}_{r}:=(0,\ldots,0,M_{m_{n}h_{n}+1}^{(r)},\ldots,M_{n}^{(r)})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

and similarly

\mathbf{m}_{s}:=(0,\ldots,0,M_{m_{n}h_{n}+1}^{(s)},\ldots,M_{n}^{(s)})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Then, using the same boundary convention as in the proof of Proposition
C.1
,

I_{r,s}^{(M)}=\frac{1}{n}\,\mathbf{m}_{r}^{\top}\mathbf{H}_{n}\mathbf{u}_{s}^{(M)},\qquad J_{r,s}^{(M)}=\frac{1}{n}\,(\mathbf{u}_{r}^{(M)})^{\top}\mathbf{H}_{n}\mathbf{m}_{s},

where
\mathbf{H}_{n}
is the kernel matrix introduced in that proof, and

\mathbf{u}_{q}^{(M)}=(U_{1,q}^{(M)},\ldots,U_{n,q}^{(M)})^{\top},\qquad q=r,s.

Next, let

\bm{\xi}_{M}:=(\bm{\varepsilon}_{1-M}^{\top},\ldots,\bm{\varepsilon}_{n}^{\top})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{(n+M)p_{n}},

and recall from the proof of Proposition
C.1
that there exist matrices

\mathbf{L}_{r,M}
and
\mathbf{L}_{s,M}
such that

\mathbf{u}_{r}^{(M)}=\mathbf{L}_{r,M}\bm{\xi}_{M},\qquad\mathbf{u}_{s}^{(M)}=\mathbf{L}_{s,M}\bm{\xi}_{M},

with

\|\mathbf{L}_{r,M}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq K_{d}K_{A},\qquad\|\mathbf{L}_{s,M}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq K_{d}K_{A},\qquad\|\mathbf{H}_{n}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq C\ell_{n}.

(51)

Therefore,

I_{r,s}^{(M)}=\frac{1}{n}\,\mathbf{g}_{r,s,M}^{\top}\bm{\xi}_{M},\qquad\mathbf{g}_{r,s,M}:=\mathbf{L}_{s,M}^{\top}\mathbf{H}_{n}^{\top}\mathbf{m}_{r},

and

J_{r,s}^{(M)}=\frac{1}{n}\,\widetilde{\mathbf{g}}_{r,s,M}^{\top}\bm{\xi}_{M},\qquad\widetilde{\mathbf{g}}_{r,s,M}:=\mathbf{L}_{r,M}^{\top}\mathbf{H}_{n}\mathbf{m}_{s}.

We now bound the Euclidean norms of these coefficient vectors. By the definition of

\bar{M}_{2,n}
in (
19
),

\|\mathbf{m}_{r}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\sum_{t=m_{n}h_{n}+1}^{n}\left\lVert\mathbf{M}_{t}\right\rVert_{\max}^{2}=n\bar{M}_{2,n},\qquad\|\mathbf{m}_{s}\|_{2}^{2}\leq n\bar{M}_{2,n}.

Hence, by (
51
),

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{g}_{r,s,M}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathbf{L}_{s,M}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\,\|\mathbf{H}_{n}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\,\|\mathbf{m}_{r}\|_{2}\leq C\,\ell_{n}\sqrt{n\bar{M}_{2,n}},

(52)

\displaystyle\|\widetilde{\mathbf{g}}_{r,s,M}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathbf{L}_{r,M}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\,\|\mathbf{H}_{n}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\,\|\mathbf{m}_{s}\|_{2}\leq C\,\ell_{n}\sqrt{n\bar{M}_{2,n}}.

(53)

Since the entries of
\bm{\xi}_{M}
are independent, centered, and uniformly sub-Gaussian by Assumption
C.1
(U1), the standard concentration inequality for linear forms in
independent sub-Gaussian random variables yields

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\bigl|\mathbf{v}^{\top}\bm{\xi}_{M}\bigr|>x\right)\leq 2\exp\!\left(-c\frac{x^{2}}{\|\mathbf{v}\|_{2}^{2}}\right)

for every deterministic vector
\mathbf{v}
, where
c>0
depends only on
K_{\varepsilon}
.
Applying this with
\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{g}_{r,s,M}
and
x=nt/2
, and using (
52
), we obtain

\mathbb{P}\!\left(|I_{r,s}^{(M)}|>\frac{t}{2}\ \middle|\ \{\bm{\mu}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{n}\right)\leq 2\exp\!\left(-c\,n\frac{t^{2}}{\ell_{n}^{2}\bar{M}_{2,n}}\right).

(54)

Similarly, by (
53
),

\mathbb{P}\!\left(|J_{r,s}^{(M)}|>\frac{t}{2}\ \middle|\ \{\bm{\mu}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{n}\right)\leq 2\exp\!\left(-c\,n\frac{t^{2}}{\ell_{n}^{2}\bar{M}_{2,n}}\right).

(55)

Combining (
50
), (
54
), and (
55
),
we obtain

\mathbb{P}\!\left(|\mathbf{R}_{\mu,n}^{(M)}[r,s]|>t\ \middle|\ \{\bm{\mu}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{n}\right)\leq 4\exp\!\left(-c\,n\frac{t^{2}}{\ell_{n}^{2}\bar{M}_{2,n}}\right).

(56)

It remains to let
M\to\infty
. For each fixed
n
, since

\sum_{a=0}^{\infty}\|\mathbf{A}_{a}\|_{\mathrm{op}}<\infty
,

\max_{1\leq q\leq p_{n}}\|Z_{t,q}-Z_{t,q}^{(M)}\|_{2}\leq C\sum_{a>M}\|\mathbf{A}_{a}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\to 0,\qquad M\to\infty.

Using
\sum_{j=0}^{m_{n}}|d_{n,j}|\leq K_{d}
, it follows that

\max_{1\leq q\leq p_{n}}\|U_{t,q}-U_{t,q}^{(M)}\|_{2}\to 0\qquad M\to\infty.

Since
\mathbf{R}_{\mu,n}[r,s]
is a finite weighted sum of terms of the form

M_{t}^{(r)}U_{t-|k|}^{(s)}
and
U_{t}^{(r)}M_{t-|k|}^{(s)}
, with

\{\bm{\mu}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{n}
treated as deterministic conditioning information, we have

\mathbf{R}_{\mu,n}^{(M)}[r,s]\to\mathbf{R}_{\mu,n}[r,s]\qquad\text{in }L^{1}

for each fixed
r,s
. Letting
M\to\infty
in (
56
) gives

\mathbb{P}\!\left(|\mathbf{R}_{\mu,n}[r,s]|>t\ \middle|\ \{\bm{\mu}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{n}\right)\leq 4\exp\!\left(-c\,n\frac{t^{2}}{\ell_{n}^{2}\bar{M}_{2,n}}\right).

Finally, applying the union bound over all
1\leq r,s\leq p_{n}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\!\left(\left\lVert\mathbf{R}_{\mu,n}\right\rVert_{\max}>t\ \middle|\ \{\bm{\mu}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{n}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{r=1}^{p_{n}}\sum_{s=1}^{p_{n}}\mathbb{P}\!\left(|\mathbf{R}_{\mu,n}[r,s]|>t\ \middle|\ \{\bm{\mu}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{n}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 4p_{n}^{2}\exp\!\left(-c_{3}n\frac{t^{2}}{\ell_{n}^{2}\bar{M}_{2,n}}\right),

which proves (
48
).

Since
\ell_{n}\leq L_{n}
, we have

\frac{t^{2}}{L_{n}^{2}\bar{M}_{2,n}}\leq\frac{t^{2}}{\ell_{n}^{2}\bar{M}_{2,n}},

and therefore

\min\!\left\{\frac{t^{2}}{L_{n}^{2}\bar{M}_{2,n}},\frac{t}{L_{n}\bar{M}_{\infty,n}}\right\}\leq\frac{t^{2}}{\ell_{n}^{2}\bar{M}_{2,n}}.

Hence (
49
) follows immediately from
(
48
), and therefore Assumption
2.7
holds. This completes the proof.
∎
