Combined cervicothoracic surgical repair of an extensive tracheal laceration following endotracheal intubation: a case report
Authors: Fei Sun, Peng Zhang, Yufeng Xie, Fei Wang, Jiangfeng Shen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTracheal membranous laceration is a rare but life-threatening complication of endotracheal intubation. Most reported tears are short (< 4 cm) and confined to either the cervical or the thoracic trachea. We present a case of an exceptionally extensive posterior tracheal laceration (estimated length spanning nearly the entire intrathoracic trachea) that required a combined cervicothoracic surgical approach, highlighting a reproducible repair strategy.Case presentationA 66-year-old man underwent endoscopic submucosal dissection under general anesthesia. Twenty minutes after uneventful intubation, a sudden air leak and cuff rupture occurred. Bronchoscopy revealed a full-thickness longitudinal tear from approximately 3 cm below the vocal cords to 2 cm above the carina. Emergent repair used a combined right thoracoscopic (VATS) and cervical approach: the thoracic portion was closed with interrupted 3 − 0 non-absorbable sutures after division of the azygos vein, and the cervical portion was repaired through a longitudinal anterior tracheotomy. No intraoperative complications occurred. The patient was extubated on postoperative day 1 and discharged on day 12. At 3-month follow-up, complete healing without stenosis was observed.ConclusionsThis case demonstrates that a combined cervicothoracic approach is safe and effective for extensive tracheal lacerations that cannot be adequately exposed by a single incision. The technique provides complete visualization, allows tension-free watertight closure, and minimizes recurrent laryngeal nerve injury.

Background

Tracheal laceration is a rare but potentially life-threatening complication of endotracheal intubation, with an estimated incidence of 0.005% to 0.19% for orotracheal intubation [1]. While endoscopic procedures like endoscopic mucosal resection (EMR) and endoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD) are standard for managing esophageal and gastric mucosal lesions [2, 3], and general anesthesia with endotracheal intubation is routinely employed, the occurrence of a major tracheal tear during such procedures remains exceptionally uncommon.

Small, asymptomatic tracheal tears may be managed conservatively. However, larger defects, particularly those associated with significant air leak, subcutaneous or mediastinal emphysema, or respiratory compromise, generally require prompt surgical repair to prevent severe complications such as mediastinitis, tension pneumothorax, or tracheal stenosis [4]. The choice between conservative, endoscopic, and surgical management depends on the size, location, depth of the injury, and the clinical stability of the patient.

We present a case of an extensive posterior tracheal laceration following intubation for an upper gastrointestinal endoscopic procedure, which was successfully repaired using a combined cervicothoracic surgical approach.

Case presentation

A 66-year-old male was scheduled for elective ESD of a subcardiac gastric mucosal lesion. He had no significant past medical history relevant to airway management. The procedure commenced under general anesthesia with orotracheal intubation using a single-lumen tube. Approximately 20 min after induction, a sudden decrease in delivered tidal volume was noted by the anesthesiologist. Examination revealed a leak from the endotracheal tube cuff. Upon tube exchange, a longitudinal laceration of the posterior tracheal wall was suspected. Urgent intraoperative bronchoscopy confirmed a full-thickness tear extending from approximately 3 cm below the vocal cords to 2 cm above the carina. The total tracheal length in adults is typically about 12 cm; based on this, the estimated length of the tear was approximately 7 cm (Fig. 1A-B). Significant subcutaneous emphysema was noted in the neck and upper chest.

The patient was immediately repositioned for surgical repair. The operation was conducted in two phases: (1). Thoracic Phase: Under left lateral decubitus position, right-sided video-assisted thoracic surgery (VATS) was initiated. Exploration revealed a hematoma in the right upper mediastinum. The mediastinal pleura was opened, and the extensive laceration of the membranous trachea was visualized (Fig. 2A). The azygos vein arch was divided to facilitate exposure. The intrathoracic portion of the tear was meticulously repaired using interrupted 3 − 0 non-absorbable polypropylene sutures (Ethicon, Somerville, NJ, USA) from distal to proximal, up to the level of the thoracic inlet (Fig. 2B). An air leak test was negative. A chest tube was placed. (2). Cervical Phase: The patient was repositioned supinely. A standard collar incision was made approximately 2 cm above the sternal notch. The anterior tracheal wall was exposed. A longitudinal incision was made through the 3rd to 5th tracheal rings (Fig. 2C). The orotracheal tube was withdrawn, and a #6 tracheostomy tube was inserted through this window for ventilation. The repair was extended superiorly from the thoracic suture line using the same interrupted 3 − 0 non-absorbable suture technique to close the remaining laceration up to the level of the cricoid cartilage. The tracheostomy tube was then removed under direct vision, the anterior tracheotomy was closed, and a #7 orotracheal tube was re-inserted.

Postoperatively, the patient was managed in the intensive care unit. Broad-spectrum antibiotics (cephalosporin) were administered based on sputum culture showing normal flora. He was successfully weaned from mechanical ventilation on postoperative day (POD) 1 and maintained on supplemental oxygen via nasal cannula. Blood tests (blood gas, complete blood count, liver/kidney function) were monitored regularly. Bronchoscopy (Fig. 1C-D) and chest CT (Fig. 1E-F) on POD 10 demonstrated a well-healed repair site with no evidence of stenosis or persistent leak (Fig. 1C-D). The patient was discharged in good condition. A follow-up chest CT at three months showed complete resolution of mediastinal emphysema and no signs of infection or stenosis.

Discussion and conclusions

Tracheobronchial injury should be suspected in intubated patients presenting with subcutaneous emphysema, persistent air leak, or difficulty in ventilation [4]. Diagnosis is confirmed by bronchoscopy, which also allows for assessment of the injury’s characteristics.

Management strategies are tailored to the injury and patient status. Conservative management (including observation, antibiotics, cough suppression, and possibly endoscopic glue application or stenting) is reserved for stable patients with small (< 2 cm), partial-thickness tears without major air leak or mediastinal involvement [5–7]. Surgical repair is indicated for larger tears (> 2–4 cm, depending on series), full-thickness injuries, those associated with esophageal involvement (Carillo Class IIIB/IV), or clinical instability [8, 9].

The surgical approach is determined by the location of the tear. Tears involving the distal third of the trachea or the main bronchi are best accessed via a right thoracotomy or VATS. Proximal tears are accessible through a cervical approach [10]. To our knowledge, reports of a single post-intubation tracheal tear spanning from the upper to lower intrathoracic trachea (estimated length ~ 7 cm) are exceptionally rare. Most described cases involve tears ≦ 4 cm confined to one anatomical compartment [1, 6, 7, 9, 11]. A single incision approach would have provided inadequate exposure for safe and complete repair. Therefore, a combined cervicothoracic approach was employed. This strategy allowed for: (1) Complete visualization of the entire tear from the cricoid to near the carina. (2) The cervical repair via a longitudinal tracheotomy avoided the need for extensive lateral and posterior dissection around the recurrent laryngeal nerve course, minimizing the risk of nerve injury. (3) Direct visualization enabled precise, tension-free, watertight closure with interrupted sutures. This case underscores that for extensive lacerations bridging anatomical compartments, a flexible, combined surgical strategy can be necessary and highly effective. Multidisciplinary planning involving anesthesiology, thoracic surgery, and critical care is paramount.

Extensive tracheal lacerations following intubation are rare but demand prompt diagnosis and decisive action. When the injury length precludes a single surgical exposure, a planned combined cervicothoracic approach offers a safe and effective method for achieving complete anatomical repair and excellent functional outcomes.
