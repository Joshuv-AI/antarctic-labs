Beyond ℓ 2 \ell_{2} -norm and ℓ ∞ \ell_{\infty} -norm: A Curvature-Inspired ℓ p \ell_{p} -Norm Scheme for Deep Neural Networks Journal: Neural Networks
Jianhao Xu, Zhuang Yang

Abstract
The existing optimizers for deep neural networks (DNNs) typically rely on either the ℓ 2 \ell_{2} norm or the ℓ ∞ \ell_{\infty} norm, resulting in optimizers that do not adapt well to substantial changes in curvature across parameter dimensions. Generally, the training process of DNNs often exhibits strong curvature anisotropy in the early period, whereas in the later period, the training process of DNNs tends to move toward flatter regions with weaker anisotropy. Particularly, optimizers based on the ℓ 2 \ell_{2} -norm are usually dominated by high-curvature directions, restricting updates of optimizers along with lower curvature direction and thus leading to a slower convergence rate. While optimizers based on the ℓ ∞ \ell_{\infty} -norm are prone to oscillations in flatter regions, due to the coordinate-wise updates of the same magnitude. To address these two extreme cases generated by ℓ 2 \ell_{2} and ℓ ∞ \ell_{\infty} norms, we propose a novel ℓ p \ell_{p} -norm scheme with a dynamical value of p p and incorporate it into stochastic gradient descent (SGD) and SGD with momentum (SGDM), leading to two novel optimizers with better generalization performance: ℓ p {\ell_{p}} -SGD (LPSGD) and ℓ p {\ell_{p}} -SGDM (LPSGDM). Particularly, the resulting optimizers suppress the dominance of high-curvature directions in the early period by utilizing a large p p ( p > 2 p>2 ), followed by a gradual decrease of p p toward 2 to enable more stable and refined updates, where the latter process is motivated by the cosine annealing strategy. We establish theoretical guarantees of the resulting algorithms and analyze that both LPSGD and LPSGDM achieve an O ( T − 1 / 2 ) O(T^{-1/2}) convergence rate for the nonconvex setting. Extensive experiments are conducted on benchmark datasets, including CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, and ImageNet-1K, with multiple DNNs such as VGG-11, ResNet-18, and ResNet-50. Demonstrably, all the experimental results effectively verify the superiority of the proposed algorithms.

Introduction

The training of deep neural networks (DNNs) can be formulated as the following empirical risk minimization problem:

\min_{\theta\in R^{d}}F(\theta)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f_{i}(\theta),

(1)

where
\theta\in R^{d}
denotes the network parameters,
n

f_{i}(\theta)

i

[

[

For Problem (

[
4
]

\theta_{k+1}=\theta_{k}-\eta\nabla f_{i}(\theta_{k}),

(2)

where
\eta>0
is the learning rate, and
\nabla f_{i}(\theta_{k})
denotes the gradient of the loss function
f_{i}
at point
\theta_{k}
. The iterative scheme (

\ell_{2}
-norm) geometry, but has achieved significant success in large-scale model training, such as convolutional neural networks
[
5
]
and transformer-based language models
[
6
]
. Beyond vanilla SGD, a variety of its variants have been developed, including adaptive gradient methods such as Adam
[

, AdamW
[

, AdaGrad
[

[

In contrast, sign-based methods are naturally rooted in

Under the

Furthermore, recent studies have shown that the loss function of DNNs usually exhibits different curvatures across different parameter dimensions.
Ghorbani et al.
[
19

analyzed Hessian eigenvalue densities in neural net optimization and found a spectra consisting of a dense bulk near zero together with a few large outliers.
To better illustrate the above discussion, we plot the evolution of the Hessian eigenvalue distribution during the training of ResNet-18 on CIFAR-10 and ResNet-50 on ImageNet-1K. Related Work

In order to follow us easily, this work will discuss the prior studies from two perspectives in this section. Specifically, we first review the methods for evaluating curvature information in DNNs, then discuss those optimizers that explicitly exploit curvature information.

Curvature Estimation in Deep Models:
Based on the Hessian-based analysis technique,
several studies have studied how to characterize and utilize the curvature information of DNNs. Early empirical studies by Sagun et al.
[
22
]
revealed that the Hessian spectrum of over-parameterized deep models typically consists of a bulk concentrated near zero together with a small number of isolated outlier eigenvalues, highlighting strong spectral anisotropy. Pennington and Bahri
[
23
]
further analyzed the Hessian spectra from the perspective of random matrix theory, providing a theoretical view of the eigenvalue distribution of neural network loss surfaces. To make such analysis scalable, Ghorbani et al.
[
24
]
developed a stochastic Lanczos quadrature-based method for estimating the spectral density of the Hessian matrix during DNN training. Yao et al.
[
25
]
subsequently introduced PyHessian, a practical framework for computing top Hessian eigenvalues, Hessian trace, and Hessian spectral density in large-scale deep models. More recently, Sankar et al.
[
26
]
extended Hessian spectral analysis to the layer level, showing that layerwise eigenspectra and trace evolution provide additional insight into the geometry of deep-network training. These studies developed effective methods for measuring curvature information in DNNs and further described the curvature structures observed during deep model training, motivating us to develop more efficient optimizers according to geometry information for deep models.

Optimizers Exploiting Curvature Information:

Many studies have directly exploited curvature-related or second-order information (mentioned above) in optimization, thus leading to various novel curvature-based optimizers. K-FAC
[
27
]
approximated natural gradient using Kronecker-factored curvature blocks. Shampoo
[
28
]
performed tensor-structured preconditioning through per-dimension second-moment statistics. Sophia
[
29
]
used a lightweight stochastic approximation to the diagonal Hessian to improve the scalability of second-order optimization. More recently, SALA
[
30
]
combined quadratic trajectory approximation and sharpness-aware updates to seek flatter regions. In contrast, our method only uses first-order information that leverages non-Euclidean geometry, but achieves better performance than state-of-the-art optimizers.

Preliminaries and Assumptions

Several notations and standard assumptions, used in this work, will be given below.

Notations.
Throughout this paper,
\|\cdot\|_{p}
denotes the standard
\ell_{p}
-norm and
\|\cdot\|_{q}
denotes its dual norm, where
1/p+1/q=1
. For a vector
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
x^{(i)}
denotes its
i
-th coordinate. We use
|x|
and
\operatorname{sign}(x)
to denote element-wise absolute value and sign, respectively. In addition, let
g_{t}
denote the stochastic gradient at iteration
t
, and let
\nabla f(\theta_{t})
denote the full gradient at
\theta_{t}
.

We make the following assumptions throughout the paper.

Assumption 1

(
\ell_{p}
-smoothness)
.

The objective function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is L-smooth. Moreover, there exists a constant
L>0
, then, for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

f(y)\leq f(x)+\langle\nabla f(x),y-x\rangle+\frac{L}{2}\|y-x\|_{p}^{2}.

(4)

Assumption 2

(Unbiased stochastic gradient)
.

Let
g_{t}
be the stochastic gradient estimator for each iteration
t
. Then

\mathbb{E}[g_{t}\mid\theta_{t}]=\nabla f(\theta_{t}).

(5)

Assumption 3

(Bounded conditional gradient noise)
.

There exists a constant
\sigma>0
such that, for any iteration
t
, the conditional second moment of the gradient noise is bounded as

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\|g_{t}-\nabla f(\theta_{t})\|_{q}^{2}\mid\theta_{t}\right]\leq\sigma^{2}.

(6)

Assumption 4

(Lower bounded objective)
.

The objective function is bounded from below, i.e.,

f^{\star}:=\inf_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(\theta)>-\infty.

4
Method

In this section, we will present our cosine
\ell_{p}
-norm scheme for the training of DNNs. Concretely, we first analyze its motivation from the perspective of curvature anisotropy in subsection
4.1
. We then introduce the cosine
\ell_{p}
-norm scheme strategy and show how it can be incorporated into SGD and SGDM, resulting in two practical optimization algorithms, LPSGD and LPSGDM, which are detailed in Subsections
4.2
and
4.3
, respectively.

4.1
Motivation

Under the
\ell_{p}
-smoothness model, the steepest descent approach can be characterized as the minimizer of a local upper bound of the objective:

\theta_{t+1}=\arg\min_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\langle\nabla f_{t},\theta-\theta_{t}\rangle+\frac{L}{2}\|\theta-\theta_{t}\|_{p}^{2}\right).

(7)

Since multiplying (
7
) by the positive constant
1/L
does not change its minimizer, the above problem is equivalently written as

\theta_{t+1}=\arg\min_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\left\langle\frac{1}{L}\nabla f_{t},\theta-\theta_{t}\right\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\|\theta-\theta_{t}\|_{p}^{2}\right).

(8)

In practice, replacing the full gradient by a stochastic gradient
g_{t}
and absorbing
1/L
into the step size
\eta
, we consider the following stochastic
\ell_{p}
-norm update:

\theta_{t+1}=\arg\min_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\langle\eta g_{t},\theta-\theta_{t}\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\|\theta-\theta_{t}\|_{p}^{2}\right).

(9)

The minimizer of (
9
) admits a closed-form expression. Specifically, for each coordinate
i
, we have

\theta_{t+1}^{(i)}=\theta_{t}^{(i)}-\eta\|g_{t}\|_{q}^{\frac{p-2}{p-1}}\frac{g_{t}^{(i)}}{|g_{t}^{(i)}|^{\frac{p-2}{p-1}}},

(10)

Further, inspired by the closed-form
\ell_{p}
steepest descent update in
[
31
]
, we define the following coordinate-wise scaling operator:

\theta_{t+1}^{(i)}=\theta_{t}^{(i)}-\eta\frac{g_{t}^{(i)}}{(|g_{t}^{(i)}|+\varepsilon)^{\frac{p-2}{p-1}}},

(11)

where the factor
\|g_{t}\|_{q}^{\frac{p-2}{p-1}}
in (
10
) is a positive scalar common to all coordinates. As such, it only controls the overall update magnitude, but does not alter the coordinate-wise relative scaling induced by the
\ell_{p}
geometry. Accordingly, we retain the coordinate-wise scaling induced by the
\ell_{p}
geometry and incorporate the global factor into the learning rate. Here,
\varepsilon>0
is a small stability constant introduced to avoid singular behavior near zero and to improve analytical tractability in the convergence analysis.

To comprehend the numerical behavior of different norm geometries under curvature anisotropy, we conduct experiments on the following local quadratic approximation for
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
:

f(\theta)=\frac{1}{2}\,\theta^{\top}H\theta.

(12)

Without loss of generality, we work in the eigenbasis of the local Hessian so that the Hessian matrix becomes a diagonal matrix, i.e.,
H=\mathrm{diag}(\lambda^{(1)},\dots,\lambda^{(d)})
. We further consider the anisotropic regime
\lambda^{(1)}\gg\lambda^{(2)}\geq\cdots\geq 0
, where the leading curvature direction is significantly sharper than the remaining directions.

Notice that the gradient of the model (
12
) is
\nabla f(\theta)=H\theta
, i.e.

(\nabla f(\theta))^{(i)}=\lambda^{(i)}\theta^{(i)}.

(13)

(b)

Figure 3:
Optimization trajectories of GD and SignGD on two toy loss landscapes. (a) Trajectories of GD and SignGD under strong curvature anisotropy. (b) Trajectories of GD and SignGD under weak curvature anisotropy.

When
p=2
(Euclidean geometry), the update magnitude along each coordinate satisfies

|d^{(i)}|\propto|(\nabla f(\theta))^{(i)}|=\lambda^{(i)}|\theta^{(i)}|.

(14)

Hence,

\frac{|d^{(i)}|}{|d^{(j)}|}=\frac{\lambda^{(i)}|\theta^{(i)}|}{\lambda^{(j)}|\theta^{(j)}|}.

(15)

Therefore, as seen from (
15
), Euclidean geometry preserves directional imbalance linearly, and high-curvature directions can dominate the update.

Further, for
2<p<\infty
, it follows from (
11
) that the update magnitude admits a power-type rescaling, i.e.,

|d^{(i)}|\propto|(\nabla f(\theta))^{(i)}|^{\alpha}\propto\bigl(\lambda^{(i)}|\theta^{(i)}|\bigr)^{\alpha},

(16)

where
\alpha=\frac{1}{p-1}
. Note that
when
\alpha<1
, the directional imbalance is compressed from linear to sublinear scaling. Consequently, the dominance of outlier curvature directions is weakened.

As
p\to\infty
, we have
\alpha\to 0
, and the update scheme of the optimizer approaches a sign-based form that depends only on the coordinate-wise signs of the gradient. This maximally suppresses imbalance of the update magnitude
along each coordinate, but also discards useful scale information.

To provide intuition, we consider two quadratic toy objectives.
For the strongly anisotropic case, we use

f_{\mathrm{strong}}(\theta^{(1)},\theta^{(2)})=\frac{200}{2}(\theta^{(1)}-1)^{2}+\frac{0.05}{2}(\theta^{(2)}-4)^{2},

(17)

whose Hessian is
\mathrm{diag}(200,0.05)
.
For the weakly anisotropic case, we use

f_{\mathrm{weak}}(\theta^{(1)},\theta^{(2)})=\frac{1.2}{2}(\theta^{(1)}-1)^{2}+\frac{0.8}{2}(\theta^{(2)}-4)^{2},

(18)

whose Hessian is
\mathrm{diag}(1.2,0.8)
and is close to isotropic.
We compare GD and signGD on both objectives and the corresponding optimization trajectories are presented in Fig.
3
. In Fig.
3a
, which corresponds to the strongly anisotropic case, GD is restricted by the sharp direction and thus makes slow progress along the flat direction, whereas signGD traverses the flat direction more effectively because its updates are not scaled by gradient magnitude. In Fig.
3b
, which corresponds to the weakly anisotropic case, GD is better matched to the local geometry and converges smoothly to the optimum, while signGD oscillates near the minimizer due to its fixed coordinate-wise step size.

The above results from Fig.
3
indicate that different training stages may favor different norm geometries: a larger
\ell_{p}
-norm is preferable in the early stage when curvature anisotropy is strong, whereas a norm closer to
\ell_{2}
is more suitable in the later stage when the local curvature becomes flatter. This observation provides a key motivation for introducing cosine
\ell_{p}
-norm scheme.

4.2
LPSGD

We introduce a cosine
\ell_{p}
-norm scheme that gradually anneals the norm geometry from a larger norm toward the Euclidean norm. Specifically, at epoch
s
, we set

p_{s}=2+(p_{\max}-2)\cdot\frac{1+\cos\!\left(\pi\frac{s-1}{S-1}\right)}{2},

(19)

where
p_{\max}\geq 2
is the maximum norm parameter and
S
is the total number of training epochs. This schedule starts from
p_{\max}
at the beginning of training and smoothly decays to
2
at the final epoch. Following, we will equip SGD and SGDM with the cosine
\ell_{p}
-norm scheme, respectively.

It is worth pointing out that to match the closed-form
\ell_{p}
steepest-descent scaling, we define the epoch-dependent exponent

\rho_{s}=\frac{p_{s}-2}{p_{s}-1}.

(20)

Given a stochastic gradient
g_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we define the update direction through the nonlinear scaling map

u_{t}=\frac{g_{t}}{\left(|g_{t}|+\varepsilon\right)^{\rho_{s}}},

(21)

The parameters in SGD are then updated as

\theta_{t+1}=\theta_{t}-\eta u_{t},

(22)

where
\eta
denotes the learning rate.

Remark 1
.

A few necessary explanations for LPSGD (Algorithm
1
) are described here.

1.

As mentioned above, the study
[
31
]
also introduced
\ell_{p}
-norm into a new accelerated
\ell_{p}
steepest descent algorithm, called STACEY. However, the authors used a fixed norm scheme, i.e.,
p
is a fixed constant. Such operation may be inadvisable for large models. In contrast, LPSGD (Algorithm
1
) works with a dynamical norm scheme by introducing a cosine
\ell_{p}
-norm scheme.

2.

Further, in recent work
[
32
]
, the authors develop the gradual stochastic gradient descent (GSGD) algorithm, enabling the optimizer to smoothly transition from sign-based SGD in the early phase to standard SGD at the end by varying the optimizer from signSGD (equivalent to using the
\ell_{\infty}
-norm) to SGD (equivalent to using the
\ell_{2}
-norm), with
p=\frac{t-1}{T}
, where
T
denotes the total number of iterations. However, GSGD is relatively less flexible, as the initial norm is implicitly fixed by its scheduling rule. By contrast, our LPSGD introduces a tunable parameter
p_{\max}
, allowing the optimizer to start from a more suitable initial norm geometry for different models, thereby providing greater adaptability.

3.

Looking back to SGD, we have that the computational cost of LPSGD (Algorithm
1
) is almost similar to that of vanilla SGD. The only additional computation is the element-wise rescaling, whose cost is
\mathcal{O}(d)
for a
d
-dimensional parameter vector.

4.3
LPSGDM

In practical deep learning training, momentum and weight decay are often crucial for improving optimization stability and generalization
[
33
,
34
]
. We therefore extend Algorithm
1
to a more practical optimizer by incorporating both momentum and weight decay, thereby obtaining Algorithm
2
(LPSGDM).

At epoch
s
, the norm parameter
p_{s}
is determined by the scheme in (
19
).
Given the stochastic gradient
g_{t}
, we first update the momentum buffer as

m_{t}=\beta m_{t-1}+(1-\beta)g_{t},

(23)

where
\beta\in[0,1)
denotes the momentum coefficient. We then apply the scheduled coordinate-wise rescaling to the momentum-smoothed gradient:

v_{t}=\frac{m_{t}}{\left(|m_{t}|+\varepsilon\right)^{\rho_{s}}},

(24)

where
\varepsilon>0
is the same stability constant as in the previous subsection.

To incorporate weight decay, we adopt the decoupled form, which applies shrinkage directly to the parameters rather than coupling it into the stochastic gradient. The resulting update is

\theta_{t+1}=(1-\eta\lambda)\theta_{t}-\eta v_{t},

(25)

where
\lambda\geq 0
is the weight decay coefficient.

5.1
Convergence of LPSGD

Let
\{\theta_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}

Since the norm parameter is updated at the epoch level, let
s(t)
denote the epoch index corresponding to iteration
t

Accordingly, the active norm parameter, its dual exponent, and the associated rescaling exponent at iteration

p_{s(t)}
,
q_{s(t)}
, and
\rho_{s(t)}
, respectively.
The LPSGD update can be written as

u_{t}=\frac{g_{t}}{\left(|g_{t}|+\varepsilon\right)^{\rho_{s(t)}}},\qquad\theta_{t+1}=\theta_{t}-\eta u_{t},

(26)

where
\eta>0
is the step size.
Assumptions
1
–
4
characterize the objective function and the stochastic gradient oracle.
To handle the nonlinear coordinate-wise transformation induced by the scheduled
\ell_{p}
-geometry, we further impose the following condition on the transformed update direction.

Assumption 5

(Conditional alignment and bounded transformed second moment)
.

There exist constants
c_{1},c_{2},c_{3}>0
such that, for any iteration
t
,

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left\langle\nabla f(\theta_{t}),u_{t}\right\rangle\mid\theta_{t}\right]\geq c_{1}\|\nabla f(\theta_{t})\|_{2}^{2},

(27)

and

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\|u_{t}\|_{p_{s(t)}}^{2}\mid\theta_{t}\right]\leq c_{2}+c_{3}\|\nabla f(\theta_{t})\|_{2}^{2}.

(28)

Assumption
5
is needed because LPSGD updates the parameters using the transformed direction
u_{t}
rather than the stochastic gradient
g_{t}
. Owing to the nonlinear coordinate-wise rescaling, the unbiasedness of
g_{t}
alone is not sufficient for the convergence analysis. Condition (
27
) guarantees the descent capability of
u_{t}
, condition (
28
) controls its size for bounding the smoothness term.
The following lemma establishes a one-step descent inequality for LPSGD.

Lemma 1
.

Under Assumption
1
, for any iteration
t\geq 0
, the iterates generated by (
26
) satisfy

f(\theta_{t+1})\leq f(\theta_{t})-\eta\left\langle\nabla f(\theta_{t}),u_{t}\right\rangle+\frac{L\eta^{2}}{2}\|u_{t}\|_{p_{s(t)}}^{2}.

(29)

Proof.

By Assumption
1
, applied with the active norm
\|\cdot\|_{p_{s(t)}}
, we have

f(\theta_{t+1})\leq f(\theta_{t})+\left\langle\nabla f(\theta_{t}),\theta_{t+1}-\theta_{t}\right\rangle+\frac{L}{2}\|\theta_{t+1}-\theta_{t}\|_{p_{s(t)}}^{2}.

Substituting
\theta_{t+1}-\theta_{t}=-\eta u_{t}
yields

f(\theta_{t+1})\leq f(\theta_{t})-\eta\left\langle\nabla f(\theta_{t}),u_{t}\right\rangle+\frac{L\eta^{2}}{2}\|u_{t}\|_{p_{s(t)}}^{2}.

This completes the proof.
∎

We are now ready to state the main convergence result for LPSGD.

Theorem 1
.

Suppose that Assumptions
1
–
4
and Assumption
5
hold.
Let
\{\theta_{t}\}_{t=0}^{T-1}
be generated by LPSGD with a constant step size
\eta>0
.
If

0<\eta\leq\frac{c_{1}}{Lc_{3}},

(30)

then

\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathbb{E}\|\nabla f(\theta_{t})\|_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{2\bigl(f(\theta_{0})-f^{\star}\bigr)}{\eta c_{1}T}+\frac{L\eta c_{2}}{c_{1}}.

(31)

Consequently, by choosing

\eta=\min\left\{\frac{c_{1}}{Lc_{3}},\sqrt{\frac{2\bigl(f(\theta_{0})-f^{\star}\bigr)}{Lc_{2}T}}\right\},

(32)

we obtain

\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathbb{E}\|\nabla f(\theta_{t})\|_{2}^{2}=\mathcal{O}(T^{-1/2}).

(33)

Proof.

Taking conditional expectation on both sides of (
29
) gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[f(\theta_{t+1})\mid\theta_{t}\right]

\displaystyle\leq f(\theta_{t})-\eta\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left\langle\nabla f(\theta_{t}),u_{t}\right\rangle\mid\theta_{t}\right]

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{L\eta^{2}}{2}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\|u_{t}\|_{p_{s(t)}}^{2}\mid\theta_{t}\right].

(34)

Applying Assumption
5
, we further obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[f(\theta_{t+1})\mid\theta_{t}\right]

\displaystyle\leq f(\theta_{t})-\eta c_{1}\|\nabla f(\theta_{t})\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{L\eta^{2}}{2}\Bigl(c_{2}+c_{3}\|\nabla f(\theta_{t})\|_{2}^{2}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=f(\theta_{t})-\left(\eta c_{1}-\frac{L\eta^{2}c_{3}}{2}\right)\|\nabla f(\theta_{t})\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{L\eta^{2}c_{2}}{2}.

(35)

Under the step-size condition (
30
), we have

\eta c_{1}-\frac{L\eta^{2}c_{3}}{2}\geq\frac{\eta c_{1}}{2}.

Hence,

\mathbb{E}\!\left[f(\theta_{t+1})\mid\theta_{t}\right]\leq f(\theta_{t})-\frac{\eta c_{1}}{2}\|\nabla f(\theta_{t})\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{L\eta^{2}c_{2}}{2}.

(36)

Taking full expectation on both sides of (
36
) yields

\mathbb{E}\!\left[f(\theta_{t+1})\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\!\left[f(\theta_{t})\right]-\frac{\eta c_{1}}{2}\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\|\nabla f(\theta_{t})\|_{2}^{2}\right]+\frac{L\eta^{2}c_{2}}{2}.

(37)

Summing (
37
) from
t=0
to
T-1
, we obtain

\mathbb{E}[f(\theta_{T})]\leq f(\theta_{0})-\frac{\eta c_{1}}{2}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathbb{E}[\|\nabla f(\theta_{t})\|_{2}^{2}]+\frac{L\eta^{2}c_{2}T}{2}.

(38)

By Assumption
4
,
f(\theta_{T})\geq f^{\star}
. Therefore,

\frac{\eta c_{1}}{2}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathbb{E}[\|\nabla f(\theta_{t})\|_{2}^{2}]\leq f(\theta_{0})-f^{\star}+\frac{L\eta^{2}c_{2}T}{2}.

(39)

Dividing both sides by
\eta c_{1}T/2
gives

\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathbb{E}[\|\nabla f(\theta_{t})\|_{2}^{2}]\leq\frac{2\bigl(f(\theta_{0})-f^{\star}\bigr)}{\eta c_{1}T}+\frac{L\eta c_{2}}{c_{1}},

(40)

which proves (
31
).
Finally, substituting the step size in (
32
) into (
31
) directly yields

\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathbb{E}[\|\nabla f(\theta_{t})\|_{2}^{2}]=\mathcal{O}(T^{-1/2}),

thereby completing the proof.
∎

5.2
Convergence of the Momentum-Based Extension

We next consider the momentum-based extension of LPSGD without weight decay.
Let
\{\theta_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
be the sequence generated by Algorithm
2
, and let
\{m_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
denote the momentum sequence defined by

m_{t}=\beta m_{t-1}+(1-\beta)g_{t},\qquad 0\leq\beta<1,

(41)

with
m_{-1}=0
.
Since the norm parameter is scheduled at the epoch level, let
s(t)
denote the epoch index corresponding to iteration
t
.
Accordingly, the active norm parameter and the associated rescaling exponent at iteration
t
are
p_{s(t)}
and
\rho_{s(t)}
, respectively.
Define the transformed momentum direction by

v_{t}=\frac{m_{t}}{\left(|m_{t}|+\varepsilon\right)^{\rho_{s(t)}}},\qquad\theta_{t+1}=\theta_{t}-\eta v_{t},

(42)

where
\eta>0
is the step size.

Since the update direction now depends on the momentum buffer, conditioning only on
\theta_{t}
is no longer sufficient.
Therefore, let
\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
denote the natural filtration generated by the algorithmic randomness up to iteration
t
.
To characterize the transformed momentum direction used by LPSGDM, we impose the following assumption.

Assumption 6

(Conditional alignment and bounded transformed second moment for momentum direction)
.

There exist constants
\tilde{c}_{1},\tilde{c}_{2},\tilde{c}_{3}>0
such that, for any iteration
t
,

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left\langle\nabla f(\theta_{t}),v_{t}\right\rangle\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]\geq\tilde{c}_{1}\|\nabla f(\theta_{t})\|_{2}^{2},

(43)

and

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\|v_{t}\|_{p_{s(t)}}^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]\leq\tilde{c}_{2}+\tilde{c}_{3}\|\nabla f(\theta_{t})\|_{2}^{2}.

(44)

Assumption
6
plays the same role as Assumption
5
in the analysis of LPSGD, but is adapted to the momentum-smoothed update direction.
Because the actual update is generated from the transformed momentum
v_{t}
rather than the stochastic gradient
g_{t}
, the unbiasedness of
g_{t}
alone does not directly imply a descent property for
v_{t}
.
Condition (
43
) guarantees that the transformed momentum direction remains sufficiently aligned with the full gradient in conditional expectation, condition (
44
) controls its magnitude for bounding the smoothness term.

The following lemma establishes a one-step descent inequality for the momentum-based method.

Lemma 2
.

Under Assumption
1
, for any iteration
t\geq 0
, the iterates generated by (
42
) satisfy

f(\theta_{t+1})\leq f(\theta_{t})-\eta\left\langle\nabla f(\theta_{t}),v_{t}\right\rangle+\frac{L\eta^{2}}{2}\|v_{t}\|_{p_{s(t)}}^{2}.

(45)

Proof.

By Assumption
1
, applied with the active norm
\|\cdot\|_{p_{s(t)}}
, we have

f(\theta_{t+1})\leq f(\theta_{t})+\left\langle\nabla f(\theta_{t}),\theta_{t+1}-\theta_{t}\right\rangle+\frac{L}{2}\|\theta_{t+1}-\theta_{t}\|_{p_{s(t)}}^{2}.

Substituting
\theta_{t+1}-\theta_{t}=-\eta v_{t}
yields

f(\theta_{t+1})\leq f(\theta_{t})-\eta\left\langle\nabla f(\theta_{t}),v_{t}\right\rangle+\frac{L\eta^{2}}{2}\|v_{t}\|_{p_{s(t)}}^{2}.

This completes the proof.
∎

We are now ready to state the convergence result for the momentum-based extension.

Theorem 2
.

Suppose that Assumptions
1
–
4
and Assumption
6
hold.
Then there exists a constant
\eta_{0}>0
such that, for any fixed step size
0<\eta\leq\eta_{0}
, the iterates
\{\theta_{t}\}_{t=0}^{T-1}
generated by (
42
) satisfy

\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\|\nabla f(\theta_{t})\|_{2}^{2}\right]\leq\frac{2\bigl(f(\theta_{0})-f^{\star}\bigr)}{\eta\tilde{c}_{1}T}+\frac{L\eta\tilde{c}_{2}}{\tilde{c}_{1}}.

(46)

Consequently, by choosing

\eta=\min\left\{\eta_{0},\,\sqrt{\frac{2\bigl(f(\theta_{0})-f^{\star}\bigr)}{L\tilde{c}_{2}T}}\right\},

(47)

we obtain

\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\|\nabla f(\theta_{t})\|_{2}^{2}\right]=\mathcal{O}(T^{-1/2}).

(48)

One admissible choice is
\eta_{0}=\tilde{c}_{1}/(L\tilde{c}_{3})
.

Proof.

Taking conditional expectation on both sides of (
45
) gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[f(\theta_{t+1})\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]

\displaystyle\leq f(\theta_{t})-\eta\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left\langle\nabla f(\theta_{t}),v_{t}\right\rangle\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{L\eta^{2}}{2}\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\|v_{t}\|_{p_{s(t)}}^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}\right].

(49)

Applying Assumption
6
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[f(\theta_{t+1})\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]

\displaystyle\leq f(\theta_{t})-\eta\tilde{c}_{1}\|\nabla f(\theta_{t})\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{L\eta^{2}}{2}\Bigl(\tilde{c}_{2}+\tilde{c}_{3}\|\nabla f(\theta_{t})\|_{2}^{2}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=f(\theta_{t})-\left(\eta\tilde{c}_{1}-\frac{L\eta^{2}\tilde{c}_{3}}{2}\right)\|\nabla f(\theta_{t})\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{L\eta^{2}\tilde{c}_{2}}{2}.

(50)

For any
0<\eta\leq\eta_{0}=\tilde{c}_{1}/(L\tilde{c}_{3})
, we have

\eta\tilde{c}_{1}-\frac{L\eta^{2}\tilde{c}_{3}}{2}\geq\frac{\eta\tilde{c}_{1}}{2}.

Hence,

\mathbb{E}\!\left[f(\theta_{t+1})\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]\leq f(\theta_{t})-\frac{\eta\tilde{c}_{1}}{2}\|\nabla f(\theta_{t})\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{L\eta^{2}\tilde{c}_{2}}{2}.

(51)

Taking full expectation on both sides of (
51
) yields

\mathbb{E}\!\left[f(\theta_{t+1})\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\!\left[f(\theta_{t})\right]-\frac{\eta\tilde{c}_{1}}{2}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\|\nabla f(\theta_{t})\|_{2}^{2}\right]+\frac{L\eta^{2}\tilde{c}_{2}}{2}.

(52)

Summing (
52
) from
t=0
to
T-1
, we obtain

\mathbb{E}\!\left[f(\theta_{T})\right]\leq f(\theta_{0})-\frac{\eta\tilde{c}_{1}}{2}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\|\nabla f(\theta_{t})\|_{2}^{2}\right]+\frac{L\eta^{2}\tilde{c}_{2}T}{2}.

(53)

By Assumption
4
,
f(\theta_{T})\geq f^{\star}
. Therefore,

\frac{\eta\tilde{c}_{1}}{2}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\|\nabla f(\theta_{t})\|_{2}^{2}\right]\leq f(\theta_{0})-f^{\star}+\frac{L\eta^{2}\tilde{c}_{2}T}{2}.

Dividing both sides by
\eta\tilde{c}_{1}T/2
gives

\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\|\nabla f(\theta_{t})\|_{2}^{2}\right]\leq\frac{2\bigl(f(\theta_{0})-f^{\star}\bigr)}{\eta\tilde{c}_{1}T}+\frac{L\eta\tilde{c}_{2}}{\tilde{c}_{1}},

which proves (
46
).
Finally, substituting (
47
) into (
46
) yields (
48
).
This completes the proof.
∎

5.3
Discussion on Decoupled Weight Decay

In practice, we also consider a decoupled weight decay version of the momentum-based method, whose update is given by

\theta_{t+1}=(1-\eta\lambda)\theta_{t}-\eta v_{t},

(54)

where
\lambda>0
denotes the weight decay coefficient.

The decoupled weight decay term introduces an additional deterministic contraction on the parameter vector, while leaving the transformed momentum direction
v_{t}
unchanged.
Hence, it does not modify the core geometry of the proposed dynamic
\ell_{p}
-norm update, but serves as an auxiliary regularization mechanism in practical training.

A fully rigorous treatment of decoupled weight decay would require additional assumptions to control the parameter norm along the whole trajectory.
Since our main goal is to characterize the optimization effect of the dynamic
\ell_{p}
-norm scheduler, we focus the formal analysis on the cases without weight decay.
Nevertheless, from an algorithmic viewpoint, decoupled weight decay is compatible with the proposed framework and can be incorporated straightforwardly in implementation.

Models
. Several explanations for the used deep learning models will be briefly discussed here.

ResNet-18 is a lightweight residual network, which improves optimization and feature learning through skip connections. ResNet-50 is a deeper and higher-capacity residual model, allowing us to examine the scalability of the proposed method on more challenging architectures.

Datasets
. CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100 consist of
32\times 32
natural color images with 10 and 100 classes, respectively, serving as small-resolution benchmarks. ImageNet-1K is a large-scale dataset of high-resolution natural images spanning 1000 classes, providing a more challenging setting to test scalability and generalization. To accommodate the different input resolutions and label spaces across datasets, we adapt each model to the corresponding dataset by adjusting input and output layers accordingly. In particular, we adjust the early convolutional layers according to the input resolution of each dataset, and modify the final classification layer to match the number of classes (

Conclusion

In this paper, we revisited the optimization of DNNs under evolving curvature anisotropy. Our analysis suggested that a single fixed norm is generally insufficient to match the substantial geometric changes that occur during training. Motivated by this observation, we proposed a cosine
\ell_{p}
-norm scheme and incorporated it into SGD and SGDM, resulting in LPSGD and LPSGDM. We further established convergence guarantees for the proposed methods on nonconvex setting, showing that they attain an
O(T^{-1/2})
convergence rate. Extensive experiments on CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, and ImageNet-1K with VGG-11, ResNet-18, and ResNet-50 demonstrated that the proposed methods achieve consistently strong performance and competitive generalization.
