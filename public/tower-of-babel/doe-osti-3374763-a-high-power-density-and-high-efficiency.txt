 ABSTRACT

Eaton Corporation and Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL) collaborated to develop a high-
performance traction drive system aimed at increasing power density and overall efficiency. The system
integrates Eaton’s hybrid flux motor—combining radial and axial flux paths to convert end-winding and
structural elements into torque-producing components—with ORNL’s high-power-density segmented
inverter. This motor design achieves higher torque density within the same footprint as traditional radial-
flux machines by effectively utilizing end turns. ORNL’s segmented inverter reduces the DC bus
capacitor requirement by over 50% compared to standard voltage source inverters (VSIs), achieving
power densities exceeding 100 kVA/L. The dual-module architecture also enables six-phase operation,
aligning with Eaton’s motor design. Together, the motor and inverter offer a compact, efficient traction
drive system with the potential for significant reductions in system weight and improvements in
powertrain efficiency.

 1. Objectives and Background

The transportation industry continues to demand traction drive systems that offer higher power density,
improved efficiency, and reduced weight. Traditional traction motors, typically based on single-path
radial flux designs, suffer from limited efficiency across varying load conditions. In particular, efficiency
drops significantly at light loads, as these motors are optimized for a narrow range of operating
conditions. Additionally, the end turns in radial-gap motors occupy considerable space without
contributing to torque production, reducing overall power density.

On the inverter side, standard voltage source inverter (VSI) designs face challenges in increasing power
density due to the large direct current (DC) bus capacitor requirements. Furthermore, optimizing the
control of traction drives to minimize switching and iron losses remains a persistent hurdle in improving
overall system efficiency.

To address these limitations, Eaton Corporation and Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL) partnered to
develop an advanced traction drive system that integrates Eaton’s hybrid flux motor with ORNL’s
segmented inverter. The project aims to significantly increase drive power density and reduce system
weight—contributing to improved vehicle fuel efficiency. Specifically, the objective is to design and
prototype a high-power (>100 kW), wide-bandgap (WBG), six-phase segmented inverter, along with a
hybrid flux motor that leverages both axial and radial flux paths. The resulting system is intended to be
ready for product-level optimization and commercialization by Eaton.

 2. Benefits to the Funding DOE Office's Mission

This CRADA directly supports the mission of the U.S. Department of Energy’s Transportation
Technologies Office (TTO) to research, develop, demonstrate, and deploy innovative, efficient, and
affordable transportation solutions that expand consumer options. By leveraging Oak Ridge National
Laboratory’s research capabilities and Eaton Corporation’s industry expertise, the project addresses key
transportation challenges related to energy cost.

The development of a high-power-density, segmented inverter and a hybrid flux motor offers significant
improvements in propulsion system efficiency and weight reduction—both of which are critical to
increasing electric vehicle performance and affordability. The outcome of this effort will advance the
technology readiness level (TRL) of both components, accelerating their path to commercialization and
supporting TTO’s goals of fostering advanced, energy-efficient vehicle technologies that are ready for
widespread deployment.

 3. Technical Discussion

3.1 Segmented/Six-Phase Traction Drives

Figure 1(a) illustrates the segmented traction drive system, which includes a DC bus filter capacitor, a
three-phase inverter, and a three-phase electric motor, separated into two groups. The power module
contains six inverter switches, and the motor features stator windings that are divided into two distinct
sets—represented in orange and blue in the figure. These sets correspond to winding groups (a1, b1, c1)
and (a2, b2, c2), respectively.

Each phase group—(a1, a2), (b1, b2), and (c1, c2)—can either be colocated in the same stator slots or
spatially distributed across different regions of a multipole motor. Each switch group (orange or blue) is
configured as an independent inverter bridge, supplying one set of motor windings and forming a separate
drive unit. Because high-power inverter modules typically use multiple switch and diode dies connected
in parallel, only minor modifications are required to create this segmented configuration. Specifically, it
involves changes to the switch layout and the addition of three extra terminals, each rated for half the
current of the original.

The two independent drive units operate with interleaved switching—meaning the timing of the turn-on
and turn-off events is offset between them. This interleaving effectively minimizes DC bus ripple
currents. Importantly, no changes to the motor control algorithm are necessary, other than adjustments to
the pulse-width modulation (PWM) scheme for the inverter switches [1].

Figure 1(b) depicts a six-phase asymmetrical traction drive system. While it shares the same inverter
architecture—two three-phase drive units—as the segmented design, the motor stator windings differ. In
this configuration, the two sets of stator windings are phase-shifted by 30 electrical degrees, characteristic
of a six-phase asymmetrical motor (SPasyM).

3.1.1 Reduction of inverter DC bus ripple current

Interleaved pulse-width modulation (PWM) techniques can also be employed to reduce DC bus ripple
currents in the inverter. Multiple PWM methods were evaluated through simulation, focusing on their
impact on DC bus ripple. Among these, bus-clamped space vector modulation (SVM) was found to yield
the lowest ripple current for the segmented inverter, while symmetrical SVM performed best for the six-
phase asymmetrical inverter.

Figure 2 shows the simulated DC bus capacitor ripple currents as a function of modulation index at
various power factors for both drive systems. These results assume an inverter phase current of 80 Arms,
a fundamental frequency of 200 Hz, and a switching frequency of 20 kHz. At modulation indices below
0.55, the ripple current levels are nearly identical for both drive configurations. However, as the
modulation index increases beyond this point, the segmented drive consistently exhibits lower ripple
current.

To evaluate performance across a wide operating range, additional simulations were conducted for the
hybrid SPasyM configuration (described in Section 3.4). These simulations spanned the full current and
speed range of the drive system, measuring both inverter DC bus ripple current and motor torque ripple.

Figure 3 presents 3D scatter plots showing the reduction in (a) ripple current and (b) torque ripple
achieved through interleaved PWM, as motor speed and current increase in steps of 500 rpm and 10 A,
respectively. A reduction of over 25% in ripple current was observed at speeds below 3500 rpm. Torque
ripple was reduced across most operating points, with a maximum reduction of 61% occurring in the mid-
speed range. The only exception was a narrow region near maximum speed and current, where
improvements were minimal.

1) to 435 kVA and 114 kVA/L (Gen 2). The Gen 2 design successfully exceeded the target power density
of 100 kVA/L.

3.3 Hybrid Six-Phase Asymmetrical Motor Design

An asymmetric six-phase slotless hybrid flux motor (HFM) was designed and evaluated across
electromagnetic, thermal, and structural domains. The HFM represents a novel integration of radial flux
and axial flux machine topologies, enabled by a new airgap structure. This configuration transforms the
conventional end-winding and structural endplate regions—typically non-torque-producing in radial flux
machines—into active torque-generating segments.

By efficiently utilizing the end turns, the HFM achieves significantly higher torque density within the
same geometric profile as a conventional radial flux-only design. Table 3 summarizes the key design
specifications. The resulting motor delivers a power density of 14.14 kW/L, representing a 14.7%
improvement over a comparable interior permanent magnet (IPM) motor, which achieves 12.32 kW/L.

3.4 EMI Modeling and Common Mode Filter Design

Figure 15 presents a block diagram for electromagnetic interference (EMI) voltage and current modeling
of the segmented/six-phase drive system. Accurate high-frequency (HF) models are essential for each
component in the drive system—including the DC bus bar and capacitors, inverter, motor cable, and
motor—as all contribute parasitic capacitances coupled to a common ground.

To measure common-mode (CM) voltages (Vcm1 and Vcm2) and CM current (Icm) at the DC input, line
impedance stabilization networks (LISNs) are inserted between the battery source and the drive. The
LISNs serve to isolate the system from the battery’s impedance, allowing accurate EMI measurement.

The LISN configuration, measurement setups, and acceptable EMI voltage/current limits are defined in
the IEC CISPR 25 standard [3], which governs EMI performance for automotive applications. Both time-
domain and frequency-domain simulations are typically employed to support EMI analysis and filter
design.

3.4.2 Hybrid CM Filter

In traction drive systems, single-turn common-mode (CM) inductors are commonly used in passive CM
filters due to the high DC bus currents and the associated large busbar sizes. Additionally, small CM
capacitors (Y-caps) are preferred to limit the amount of CM current injected into the system. However,
these design constraints typically require large and bulky CM chokes to meet EMI emission limits.

To address this, active filters (AFs) have been proposed in the literature as a way to complement passive
filters (PFs) and reduce the required size of CM chokes. Most reported AF designs rely on CM current
sensing using high-frequency (HF) transformers, which adds complexity and cost.

An alternative approach—based on CM voltage sensing via capacitive coupling—offers a potentially
simpler and more compact solution. However, this method has received limited attention in existing
research.

Figure 29 illustrates a hybrid DC-side CM filter configuration that combines a voltage-controlled current
injection active filter with a passive filter. The active filter typically consists of an operational amplifier
and a push-pull class-AB power amplifier, implemented using a pair of complementary Darlington
transistors, as shown in Figure 30.

  Segmented switching techniques applied to an asymmetrical six-phase motor drive achieved a
 reduction of over 25% in DC bus ripple current at motor speeds below 3500 rpm. Torque ripple
 was also significantly reduced across most operating points, with a maximum reduction of 61%
 observed in the mid-speed region, except for a narrow corner near peak speed and current.
  It was found that mutual leakage inductance between the two sets of three-phase windings leads
 to uneven power sharing between inverters. Further analysis revealed that under dual-dq frame
 control, inverter currents are equal but voltages differ, while under single-dq frame control, the
 opposite occurs. In the dual-dq control case, mutual inductance causes a voltage imbalance—
 adding to one inverter phase voltage and subtracting from the other, resulting in unequal phase
 voltages.
  Two six-phase inverter prototypes were designed, built, and tested at DC bus voltages up to
 800 V and switching frequencies up to 20 kHz.
 o Gen 1: 313 kVA rated power, 87 kVA/L power density
 o Gen 2: 435 kVA rated power, 114 kVA/L power density (exceeding the 100 kVA/L
 target)
  High-frequency models were developed for the key components of an asymmetrical six-phase
 drive. A complete EMI modeling framework was built in LTspice, supporting analysis at both
 fixed duty operation and fundamental frequency operation. This modeling tool effectively
 evaluates the EMI performance of different current control and PWM techniques.
  A hybrid common-mode filter, combining active and passive filtering, was designed, constructed,
 and tested. Results showed a 23% reduction in volume and a 17% reduction in bill of materials
 cost compared to a passive-only filter, while maintaining or improving EMI performance—
 particularly in the 1–2 MHz and 10–20 MHz frequency bands.
