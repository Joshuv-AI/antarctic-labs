Embracing complexity by reducing rigidity: guiding principles for the intersection of implementation science and qualitative research
Authors: Laura Sheard, Heather Reisinger, Rinad Beidas, Paul Wilson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn this editorial we reflect on recent contributions that have focused on how researchers should involve qualitative research in implementation science. Implementation science as a discipline has firmly entered the zeitgeist and is now attracting a wealth of qualitative inquiry. We welcome qualitative manuscript submissions which focus on profound and reflexive explorations of topics relevant to the journal and call for a greater methodological diversity of qualitative methods and analytic reportage. Novel theoretical and methodological developments are considered. Finally, as we usher in a new era of guiding principles for qualitative research in the field, we set out our new expectations for reporting qualitative research and explain why it will no longer be necessary to include a ‘checklist’ for qualitative submissions to the journal.

Three recent papers published in Implementation Science focus on proposing the core principles of how researchers should involve qualitative research in the implementation science field [1–3]. It would be too simple to say that all papers focus on how to “do better” in involving qualitative methods in our field. Rather, they ground the reader in the foundational approaches and core paradigmatic underpinnings at the intersection of implementation science and qualitative research.

Holtrop et al. [1] carefully discuss how to navigate the choice of qualitative methods and analysis, focusing on the research goal/aim, research question and core characteristics of the research itself. Succinctly, they ask: “What are you trying to accomplish?” They emphasise the importance of interpretive approaches and sensitising concepts, whilst being clear about how these approaches differ from theories, models or frameworks (TMFs) often employed in implementation science such as the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) [4, 5]. Holtrop et al. point out that many research teams may default to the familiarity of traditional stalwart methods such as interviews, focus groups and thematic analysis which can sometimes stifle notions of complexity. Some research questions are better answered with methods such as ethnographic enquiry, examination of documents, rapid methods or participatory approaches such as photovoice. The authors call for a greater methodological diversity when it comes to qualitative methods and analysis as applied to implementation research.

Nevedal et al. [2] outline ‘ten common misperceptions’ about qualitative methods as applied to implementation science (but inevitably apply much more broadly across many different disciplines). Guiding the list of ten misperceptions is the overarching principle that the standards of quantitative research should not be inappropriately applied to qualitative research. This embraces ideas such as a move away from generalisability as a gold standard and towards transferability and conceptual resonance, a critique of qualitative sample sizes being considered generally insufficient and a rejection of counting and/or numbers to substantiate qualitative studies with a proposed re-imagined focus on consensus and interpretive rigour. The authors are clear that overt rigidity in the expected standards of qualitative research should be avoided. This plays out through an examination of a qualitative ‘bingo list’ of items which many editors erroneously expect to witness in manuscripts such as mention of saturation, member checking, evidence of line-by-line coding, themes as the primary reportage of qualitative findings and the expected use of qualitative data analysis software packages. Nevedal et al. argue that editors and reviewers must fundamentally understand and embrace the epistemological underpinnings of qualitative research to be able to appropriately evaluate qualitative studies and findings. This involves a portrayal of ethics and humility in recusing oneself where lack of qualitative expertise may hinder one’s judgement. Quid pro quo, qualitative research teams must be able to clearly and transparently articulate their methodological design choices and stringently justify their sampling criteria, analytic process and application of rigour.

Nilsen et al. [3] propose a move away from deductive inquiry and a renewal of inductive and abductive reasoning. They argue that implementation science has become “too deductive too soon” with researchers in the field relying too heavily on existing TMFs. The end result is that TMFs are (over)dependent on extant coding schemes. This means that surprising or novel findings can be obscured, ultimately leading to qualitative research being used to confirm rather than explore; studies end up merely confirming the content of the TMF they started out with, in a circular manner. They recommend that TMFs are repositioned as “evolving heuristics” which remain open to refinement and extension through empirical engagement with qualitative data rather than remaining static entities and rigid templates. A move towards abductive reasoning is noted as necessary, whereby the researcher moves backwards and forwards between empirical data and theory (mid-range or grand theory, over and above TMFs). Nilsen et al. describe abduction as the methodological bridge between induction and deduction, whereby empirical discovery is connected with evolving theoretical interpretations. Inherent throughout the above is an embracement of “discovery orientated qualitative methods” including ethnography and interpretation, enabling researchers to uncover what happens in practice as implementation unfolds. Advancing implementation science forwards means that qualitative research must become a critical tool for generating new theoretical insights rather than researchers trying to shoehorn an existing TMF into their study.

As editors at Implementation Science, we have noticed that many of the qualitative manuscripts we encounter tend to fall into several of the qualitative pitfalls outlined by Holtrop et al., Nevedal et al. and Nilsen et al. That is, semi-structured interviews are often depended on as a comfortable methodological option. Qualitative analysis tends to default to list of barriers and facilitators where the data is coded against a notable ‘off the shelf’ TMF, like for example, CFIR. In and of itself, there is nothing wrong with the above pursuits, but they often do not offer sufficient meaning or depth to deeply understand a problem as complexity is often flattened. Nor are they always the most effective and useful means by which to answer the core research questions posed and satisfy the central aim of the study. Kislov et al. [6] have discussed the above points as analogous to a preoccupation with anatomy rather than physiology, specifically in relation to determinant frameworks where reportage can take on the form of assembling a list of disconnected items. We find that at the intersection of implementation science and qualitative research, methodological uniformity and the “hunt for a framework” to code data against has become a commonplace tautology as we anticipate this is what research teams think is expected of them rather than the best choice for their research question.

By informing the reader what we would like to see less of, it is important to provide a worked example of the potential types of qualitative analyses and write ups which we would encourage. A recent analytically notable paper published in the journal is that by Redwood et al. [7] The authors conducted a qualitative process evaluation to understand how the implementation of two quality improvement interventions (standard vs. enhanced) enabled uptake of magnesium sulphate to protect preterm infants from neurodevelopmental disabilities in the UK. They conducted 72 interviews and then undertook an analytic process involving a combination of Framework analysis [8], abductive iteration and the overarching constructs of normalisation process theory. This will have likely involved a complex, carefully balanced, multi-level and multi-stage activity situated in the space between inductive generation of headline findings and application of selected elements of normalisation process theory. The interview sample size was not predetermined but was based on the concept of ‘information power’ [9]. Their stated analytic aim was “to capture how different implementation contexts and perinatal team dynamics – frequently lost in accounts of barriers and facilitators – generated the observed implementation outcomes.” Importantly, the research team had extensive expertise in advanced qualitative approaches and a social science grounding pertaining to applied health research topics focusing on quality improvement and implementation science.

The research team found that normative and relational restructuring were critical to the success (and sustainment) of the intervention at different sites. Normative restructuring refers to the changes which were required to increase magnesium sulphate uptake regarding “norms, rules and resources that govern the actions of maternity and neonatal teams.” Relational restructuring is related to how professional relationships and communication between hospital teams changed as a result of implementation of the new practice. Relational restructuring was more difficult to achieve as it encompassed the breaking down of silos between obstetrics, midwifery and neonatology. This was an especially important finding as the magnesium sulphate intervention benefited the infant but was administered to the pregnant woman. Sustainment of the intervention was only possible if sufficient relational restructuring had taken place, in order to share responsibilities and daily tasks across clinical teams. Redwood et al. [7] is a particularly useful example of a qualitative study which has been published in Implementation Science and has avoided the overt rigidity as outlined by Nevedal et al. Information power is preferred over saturation, coding is not overtly relied on, qualitative data analysis software is not described and the findings are presenting in relation to the overarching concepts derived from Normalization Process Theory (NPT) [10] rather than as descriptive themes or barriers and facilitators. Importantly, in relation to the main points proposed by Nilsen et al. [3], NPT (and importantly abductive reasoning) has been employed as a technique to sensitise the Redwood et al. research team to the main underpinning meaning inherent in their data rather than sorting data into discrete descriptive categories. In this sense, the paper demonstrates an interpretative feel and ethos which nicely contrasts with the plethora of qualitative submissions which over depend on TMF constructs.

Implementation science as a discipline has firmly entered the zeitgeist and is now attracting a wealth of qualitative inquiry. We wish to welcome qualitative manuscript submissions which focus on profound and reflexive explorations of topics relevant to the journal. Following the lead of Nevedal et al. [2], we want to encourage less rigidity in both the application and expectations relating to the reportage of qualitative research findings. In relation to Holtrop et al., we call for a greater methodological diversity of qualitative methods and analytic reportage. Regarding Nilsen et al. [3], we wish to inspire a shift away from an over-reliance of the deductive nature of TMFs. For researchers and authors, this relates to the ways in which implementation science teams often design, conduct, analyse and report their qualitative findings as if they were being judged via a quantitative paradigm. We are open to receiving high quality submissions which illuminate complexity rather than seeking to constrain or flatten it, using methods and analytic techniques which encourage embedment and interpretation over and above brief one-off interviewing leading to a list of barriers and levers.

Implementation science as a field is inherently interdisciplinary, inter-methodological and draws upon a range of theoretical standpoints [6]. However, by the very nature of its broad scope and interests, as a field it tends to attract “disciplinary agnostics” who remain atheoretical and rarely move beyond description [6]. The reasons behind this are multi-factorial and may relate to implementation science itself being a young field which ostensibly ‘borrows’ across quantitative and qualitative methods, traditions and theories including sociology, psychology, anthropology, epidemiology, health services research, clinical sciences and clinical practice. Two major problems reside in this hinterland. One, existing implementation science frameworks (such as CFIR) offer a form of structure which may seem enticing for researchers potentially overwhelmed by real world messy qualitative data, who want to try and ‘neaten the mess’ instead of illuminate complexity or contestation. Two, qualitative research is sometimes viewed as a “bolt on” which is appended to a study after the original research questions and aims have been generated by a clinical team.

Sheard [11] has highlighted the difficulties involved in publishing qualitative interpretative approaches within the applied health sphere, with editors and peer reviewers (often inappropriately) evaluating notions of creativity within a quantitative paradigm. Interpretative approaches are often likened to a form of creative storytelling, which for many members of the health research community can feel juxtaposed to the hard science ‘branding’ of implementation science. Defining what is meant by qualitative interpretation – and how this contrasts with qualitative description (and differs to straightforward thematic analysis) – is problematic as there is no off the shelf, step by step formula available. Broom [12] has characterised interpretation as “an explanation of what is going on with your data, rather than merely summarising it” whilst Braun and Clarke [13] have discussed conceptual level analysis as pattens of shared meaning underpinned by a core concept, over and above data summaries being categorised into domains. We acknowledge that the move to interpretation and away from a wholly deductive TMF framing can be challenging. Therefore, it would be a fool’s errand to suggest that researchers and clinicians from atheoretical backgrounds should simply be asked to move towards qualitative interpretation and be able to comprehensively do this unsupported. They would be unlikely to have sufficient methodological and analytical knowledge and experience in qualitative methods and/or paradigmatic training in the social sciences. Rather than a tokenistic inquiry, qualitative methodologists and social scientists with theoretical training should be included as early as possible (and ideally embedded within the team) to ensure that qualitative research is resourced appropriately and there is methodological collaboration. This is not simply about atheoretical researchers being taught how to code interview transcripts or attending brief training courses on qualitative methods. It is about genuine epistemological partnership.

At the cornerstone of ushering in a new era of guiding principles for qualitative research in the implementation science field is the necessary move away from a ‘checklist mentality.’ The most cited qualitive research checklist is the 32 item Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ), which has become the default position in setting supposed standards for qualitative reportage [14]. Whilst many of the items are useful in sensitising research teams towards core facets of qualitative research, some items are not and can stifle methodological creativity and analytic innovation. Examples of problematic items include: “How many participants refused to participate or dropped out?” (item 13), “Were transcripts returned to participants for comment and/or correction?” (item 23), “Did the authors provide a description of the coding tree? (item 25)” and “Were participant quotations presented to illustrate the themes?” (item 29). This may encourage a box ticking approach [15]. For more experienced qualitative teams, COREQ is often viewed as problematic due to some journals treating reporting checklists as a mandatory requirement (i.e., teams may need to “retrofit” their methods sections in order to satisfy the checklist). Additionally, it is important to note that COREQ was developed specifically for interviews and focus groups but has now been applied to qualitative research in general despite its inapplicability in relation to participatory, ethnographic, arts based or critical approaches. Morse [16] has noted that checklists largely only relate to the technical management and sorting of data and do not address originality, substance, contribution or the actual findings. Rather, “Checklists are taking over the world…. We are losing control of our discipline…Why should we rigidly, shallow and even invalidly rate and discard our submissions?” [16].

We recognize that we need to practice what we preach. In order to usher in a new era of qualitative methods and implementation science, the journal will phase out the mandatory requirement for qualitative submissions to adhere to the COREQ or any other qualitative checklist as indicated by the EQUATOR network (https://www.equator-network.org/). This is our pledge to the qualitative community that we are invested in and serious about encouraging qualitative submissions which defy neat checklist parameters inherently nestled in a ‘counting and numbers’ paradigm.

We want to be clear: We are not advocating an ‘anything goes’ approach and we acknowledge the importance of guidance and best practice as “helpful pedagogical launching pads” for researchers at all stages but especially those who are early career [17]. Implementation science teams must consider the key principles outlined in Holtrop et al., Nevedal et al. and Nilsen et al. [1–3] During study conceptualization, the following should be considered: Have a range of methods been considered and the most appropriate (not the easiest or quickest) method been chosen? Has a decision regarding an inductive, abductive or deductive analytic approach been employed relative to the choice of method, topic and context? For both of these choices, has a sufficient and transparent rationale been provided, ranging from practical rationales for operational partners to complexities that require interpretive engagement? Does the research team have a sufficient understanding of qualitative epistemology (often a methodologist with a background or training in social sciences)?

Alongside these considerations, we recommend that author teams refer to pertinent guiding principles proposed by several authors in order to sensitise them to the most relevant facets of ‘what makes for excellent qualitative research’. In her landmark paper, Tracy [17] puts forward eight “big tents” for what constitutes high quality qualitative research applicable to a diversity of qualitative methods, approaches and theoretical affiliations. This includes: worthy topic, rich rigour, sincerity, credibility, resonance, significant contribution, ethics and meaningful coherence. Importantly, these facets are not overprescribed and do not require research teams to tick or insert a page number to demonstrate evidence of where they have ‘met’ each criteria prior to journal submission. Rather, research teams are asked to look deeply, honestly and reflexively at their own practice throughout the entirety of their research journey. More recently, Braun and Clarke [18] have developed the Big Q Qualitative Reporting Guidelines for qualitative research which rest on a values-based approach rather than a ‘one size fits all’ tool or checklist. This involves useful practices to consider, practices to avoid and notes to reviewers/editors less familiar with certain types of qualitative approaches. The guidance provided by both these papers provide important guardrails for our expectations regarding qualitative submissions to the journal.
