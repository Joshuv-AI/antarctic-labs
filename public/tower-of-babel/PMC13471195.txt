Nephrologist Perspectives on Navigating Pregnancy Planning and Care in Women With Kidney Disease: A Qualitative Study
Authors: Melanie L. Wyld, Erandi Hewawasam, Brooke Huuskes, Amanda Sluiter, Amber Williamson, Allison Jaure, Shilpanjali Jesudason
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Rationale & ObjectiveAs more women with chronic kidney disease (CKD) consider pregnancy, the demand for informed and supportive reproductive counseling grows. Despite this, the preparedness of nephrologists to provide such care is uncertain. We aimed to describe the perspectives of nephrologists on addressing and managing conception and pregnancy in women with CKD.Study DesignIndividual semi-structured interviewsSetting & ParticipantsAustralian nephrologists (August 2021 to June 2023).Analytic ApproachTranscripts were coded and analyzed thematically.ResultsTwenty nephrologists were interviewed (55% men) from 6 Australian states, with a mean 15.7 ± 9.8 years in practice. We identified 5 themes with subthemes: knowledge, training, and confidence gaps (inadequate and ad hoc training, absence of formal education, low exposure impairing confidence); lack of reliable resources to guide care (absence of guidelines, personal networks as a substitute for formal support); deprioritized and difficult to discuss (not seen as core nephrology work, oversight amid competing demands, gendered assumptions, and clinical discomfort); risk aversion driven by fear of catastrophic outcomes (avoiding disastrous consequences, pregnancy discouraged until post-transplant); and navigating patient autonomy and shared decision making (preference for patients to initiate discussions, need for patient education and support resources, shared decision making as a care principle). Across interviews, transplantation functioned as a clinical threshold and clinicians commonly deferred pregnancy discussions until after a transplant, viewing it as a prerequisite for acceptable maternal and fetal risk.LimitationsFindings from the Australian health care setting may not be fully transferable to other health systems.ConclusionsNephrologists in Australia face considerable barriers in delivering reproductive care. Despite expressing support for patient autonomy, many clinicians defer or avoid reproductive discussions, especially in women with advanced CKD, limiting informed decision making. Addressing these barriers through structured training, clear guidelines, and systematic integration of reproductive care into routine nephrology practice is essential to ensuring that women receive timely, equitable, and patient-centered reproductive care.

Successful pregnancies are achievable in women with any stage of chronic kidney disease (CKD), including dialysis and transplant recipients.1, 2, 3, 4 However, pregnancies in women with CKD confer increased maternal and fetal morbidity, including progression of kidney disease, pre-eclampsia, preterm birth, low birth weight, and neonatal intensive care admissions.1,3,5 There has been a move away from negatively focused counseling to strengths-based, success-based, and supportive approaches to pregnancy in women with CKD, ideally based on shared decision making between patients and clinicians.6, 7, 8 A strengths-based approach supports reproductive goals and empowerment while acknowledging risks and options.

Guidelines recommend pregnancy in women with CKD should be well-planned when possible.9 However, preconception counseling by nephrologists remains challenging to widely implement. Two-thirds of nephrologists in North America reported lacking confidence in managing women’s health issues including pregnancy and provided reproductive counseling to fewer than one patient each month.10 Similarly, a survey of nephrologists in Canada, the United Kingdom, Australia and New Zealand, found that only 35% “often” discussed fertility with patients with kidney disease.11 Patients with CKD find fertility and pregnancy difficult and emotionally complex topics to raise and believe that nephrologists will initiate discussions.12,13 Furthermore, women’s pregnancy intentions may not align with her actual or perceived pregnancy risk, highlighting the importance of informed counseling.14

New frameworks for navigating fertility and pregnancy counseling in women with CKD recommend that nephrologists proactively address these issues as part of routine clinical care.12 However, the ability to do so is shaped by multiple, underexplored constraints. Previous work has highlighted the critical role that nephrologists’ attitudes and counseling processes play for women with CKD considering pregnancy.15 However, little is known about the specific barriers that clinicians face when raising and navigating these discussions. This study aims to describe the perspectives and practice patterns of nephrologists in Australia regarding the management of conception and pregnancy in women with CKD.

Methods

Findings have been reported according to COnsolidated criteria for REporting Qualitative research (COREQ).16

Participants and Setting

Adult and pediatric nephrologists treating patients of childbearing age (age 15-50 years) who could become pregnant with any stage of CKD (CKD Stage 1-5, receiving dialysis (5D) or with a kidney transplant (5T)) were eligible. We used the professional networks of MW and SJ for purposive sampling to obtain a diverse sample by age, gender, geographic location, years of practice, subspecialty interest in obstetric medicine, and colocation with obstetric services. Snowball sampling allowed participants to nominate additional nephrologists. Participants were invited by phone and/or email and consent was obtained from each participant. Ethics approval was obtained from the Central Adelaide Local Health Network (CALHN 14345).

Data Collection

MW conducted semi-structured videoconference interviews with each participant between August 2021 and June 2023, a period extended by coronavirus disease (COVID)-19-related constraints. The interview guide (Appendix S1) was developed from a literature review of patient and clinician perspectives on pregnancy care in kidney disease, codesign with the Pregnancy and Kidney Research Australia consumer group, and discussion within the research team. Recruitment ceased at thematic saturation, when additional interviews no longer generated substantively new concepts. The final sample size was determined using thematic sufficiency rather than a prespecified numeric target.

Data Analysis

Interviews were audio recorded, transcribed verbatim, and analyzed thematically using NVivo (V.1.8.1(QSR International)). MW coded the transcripts line-by-line, identified conceptual links and patterns, and inductively developed initial themes and subthemes. Themes were revised after discussion with SJ and were presented to the Pregnancy and Kidney Research Australia consumer group for feedback.

Patient and Public Involvement

First author MW is an academic transplant nephrologist with lived experience of kidney disease and pregnancy after kidney transplantation and a PhD in kidney disease outcomes. MW conducted the interviews and led data collection, coding, analysis, and manuscript drafting. The Pregnancy and Kidney Research Australia consumer group contributed to the development of the interview guide and reviewed the emergent themes and subthemes; 3 coauthors (BH, AS, AW) are consumers and members of this group.

Results

Twenty nephrologists participated including 9 (45%) women and 11 (55%) men (Table 1). Study participants had been in practice for a mean of 15.7 ± 9.8 years, and at least 1 participant was interviewed from each of the 6 Australian states. The majority (70%) worked in clinical settings where maternity care was colocated, and 15% had subspecialty interest in obstetric nephrology. Knowledge, Training, and Confidence Gaps

Inadequate, unstructured, and inconsistent training

Participants described the current training approach in obstetric nephrology as “informal” and “hodgepodge,” with formal, structured exposure described as “non-existent.” Any structured training was typically “off [the trainee’s] own bat,” driven by individual initiative rather than embedded in training programs. Those who did gain experience in obstetric nephrology recalled opportunities arising through “bizarre scenarios” or “luck,” underscoring the chance-based, rather than systematic, nature of existing training pathways.

Absence of formal curriculum

Participants reported there was limited formal education in obstetric nephrology during training, whereas other topics such as peritoneal dialysis and genetics were prioritized as core curriculum. They suggested introducing a dedicated course or module such as a “weekend of pregnancy-related education” on fertility and pregnancy in CKD as part of standard nephrology training.

Low exposure undermines confidence

Because women of childbearing age with advanced CKD comprised a relatively small patient population, participants reported “limited” opportunities to develop substantial expertise once they began practicing as a consultant. The infrequency of such cases often left consultants feeling underprepared and lacking confidence to manage fertility and pregnancy issues. Several nephrologists explicitly reflected that this lack of formal training and experience meant “it’s no wonder our women have not got pregnant,” recognizing that their own uncertainty could function as a direct barrier to women realizing their reproductive goals.

Lack of Reliable Resources to Guide Care

Absence of guidelines

Participants highlighted the absence of national or institutional guidelines for managing fertility and pregnancy in women with CKD as a barrier to delivering standardized, evidence-based care. In the absence of clear, universally accepted guidelines, clinicians felt they had to navigate complex cases using personal judgment and ad hoc approaches, contributing to variability in patient counseling and outcomes.

Personal networks as a substitute for formal support

Many participants reported an absence of dedicated clinics focused on fertility and pregnancy in women with CKD and relied on a single or small group of experienced colleagues for guidance: “Sadly I am quite reliant on the expert in the area… I call them as it’s just one clinician that has done it for a long time.” In centers where there were obstetric nephrology clinics, they were sometimes established based on the personal interest of clinician: “More recently [Dr X] has taken on an active interest and set up a clinic, and I refer to her.” In the absence of local expertise, some nephrologists described turning to international colleagues and social media (eg, following obstetric nephrology experts on Twitter/X) for informal clinical guidance, underscoring how poorly supported they felt by existing local structures and resources.

Deprioritized and Difficult to Discuss

Not seen as core nephrology work

Some participants expressed that fertility and pregnancy were not considered part of nephrology’s central mandate. They were perceived as tangential to the “core business of blood pressure risk, cardiovascular profile and retarding progression of chronic kidney disease.” This led to systemic disengagement, in which fertility and pregnancy were deprioritized or deferred to other specialties.

Overlooked amid competing demands

Even when clinicians acknowledged the importance of fertility and pregnancy health for women with CKD, it was often unintentionally neglected during time-constrained consultations. Several participants admitted they often “forget” to bring it up, with one stating “it just doesn't come up in my mind to think of.” Others emphasized systemic pressures such as “clinic time limitations” and high patient complexity, which contributed to fertility and pregnancy discussions being deprioritized: “The main barrier is just time and how busy we are.” For some, the anticipated complexity and emotional work involved in these conversations meant that, unless patients explicitly raised pregnancy, it felt “too much” to introduce in an already overburdened clinic visit, reinforcing avoidance patterns. Despite best intentions, this time pressure meant that fertility and pregnancy discussions were often indefinitely deferred “I would've missed it for a few women. I'm sure.”

Gendered assumptions and clinical discomfort

Many participants assumed that women with CKD feel more comfortable discussing conception and pregnancy with female clinicians: “If my daughter had CKD she might feel more comfortable talking about it with a female nephrologist, than with a male.” This delegation was not only based on presumed patient preference but also reflected discomfort among some male nephrologists in raising these issues: “I tend to get a lot of these patients referred, some of my male colleagues may step aside for sure, some would be quite uncomfortable having these discussions.” Female nephrologists described women “waiting” for them to appear in clinic before disclosing pregnancy questions or concerns, suggesting that many patients were holding back deeply important conversations until they encountered someone they perceived as a safer person to ask.

Risk Aversion Driven by Fear of Catastrophic Outcomes

Avoiding disastrous consequences

Many participants described past adverse outcomes as deeply formative. Terms such as “disastrous,” “scary,” and “hard and frightening” were frequently used to recount experiences of pregnancy in CKD, including pregnancies that resulted in fetal loss or were characterized by severe early onset pre-eclampsia. These distressing events, combined with limited formal training and sparse clinical exposure, fostered a sense of helplessness and self-doubt. As a result, participants often avoided or were reluctant to manage pregnancies in patients with advanced CKD, fearing they lacked the necessary skills and confidence: “I really feel I don't have the skills.”

Pregnancy discouraged until post-transplant

Participants expressed reluctance to support pregnancy in women with advanced CKD, viewing it as a threat to both maternal and kidney health. Pregnancy was seldom “raised in a positive sense” for patients with poor kidney function, and participants reported actively discouraging it for women whose kidney function was “on the slide,” warning that it could “spell curtains” for their kidneys by leading to irreversible loss of kidney function. Discussions around pregnancy were typically centered on “delay,” with advice to “just wait until transplant” before considering pregnancy. For dialysis-dependent patients, the prognosis for pregnancy was considered particularly poor. Participants described fetal survival rates as low, with surviving infants “almost certainly going to be preterm,” and likely to face complications such as “learning disabilities and so on.” These concerns were frequently used to reinforce recommendations to defer pregnancy, occasionally in blunt terms, with one participant characterizing pregnancy in such circumstances as “obviously a stupid thing to do.”

Participants consistently emphasized “getting them transplanted” as a prerequisite for pregnancy, describing post-transplant pregnancy as “a lot better” and significantly safer for both the patient and fetus. Transplantation functioned as a clinical threshold—a precondition for engaging in pregnancy discussions. By defining pregnancy as appropriate only after transplantation, nephrologists effectively controlled when and for whom pregnancy was considered, positioning them as powerful gatekeepers whose counseling could substantially constrain or delay women’s opportunities to pursue motherhood.

Navigating Patient Autonomy and Shared Decision Making

Preference for patients to initiate discussions

Participants frequently waited for patients to raise fertility or pregnancy, describing these topics as “not necessarily encouraged” and therefore dependent on patients being proactive. One stated they would “wait rather than drive it,” whereas another described a “general information” approach unless prompted. Once raised, participants expressed comfort discussing these topics, with one reporting they would “give advice and hopefully drive the stuff” after the patient initiated the conversation. Some participants acknowledged this reactive approach as a limitation. For example, one reflected that “we are not doing a lot” for patients in the area of reproductive counseling, suggesting that current practices may miss opportunities for proactive guidance.

Need for patient education and support resources

Participants highlighted the need for tailored resources to support shared understanding and informed decisions. Suggestions included a “patient portal,” printed “booklet with vignettes,” and tools to support structured counseling such as prompts or screening questions for patients “under the age of 45.” Templates for documenting family planning were seen as useful for “embedding” reproductive care into routine nephrology.

Shared decision as a care principle

Participants described shared decision making as a central to counseling patients about fertility and pregnancy. While acknowledging the complexity and risk associated with pregnancy in advanced kidney disease, participants emphasized the need to balance medical judgment with patient preferences and values. Several participants noted that although they were responsible for communicating risks, the final decision was the patients. As one explained, “it’s not my decision” and then supporting patients through a “medicalised pregnancy” as needed. Another stated that even when pregnancy was “not my recommendation and this can go really badly,” if the patient understood the risks and chose to proceed, they would “support them as much as I can.” Despite occasional disagreements with patient choices, most participants described a commitment to autonomy and not imposing their own views. One noted, “I certainly would never make an obstacle to have children if that's what you want to do,” underscoring a commitment to patient-centered care, even in high-risk scenarios.

Discussion

In this qualitative study of nephrologists, we identified key barriers to delivering fertility and pregnancy care to women with kidney disease, including gaps in knowledge, training, and confidence; limited resources; deprioritization; risk aversion; and reliance on patient-initiated discussions, all contributing to fragmented and inconsistent care. Across these themes, nephrologists occupied a pivotal gatekeeping role: their uncertainty and reluctance to initiate conversations could unintentionally become a major barrier to women achieving their desired motherhood. A notable finding was that transplantation often operated as a clinical threshold, with many clinicians deferring pregnancy discussions until after transplant, potentially narrowing reproductive choices for women with advanced CKD. These barriers persist despite participants’ commitment to shared decision making and recognition of the growing importance of fertility and pregnancy in nephrology practice.

Insufficient formal training in reproductive care and obstetric nephrology emerged as a major barrier. Participants described a haphazard approach to acquiring knowledge, with structured clinical exposure and educational offerings scarce. This aligns with international data suggesting that most nephrologists receive minimal fertility-related education during fellowship, resulting in low self-assessed confidence.10 Consequently, many rely on sporadic clinical encounters or mentorship from experts, perpetuating variability in skill and comfort. A shift toward integrated, mandatory training modules may address this gap and improve trainee preparedness and consultant confidence.

Our findings are consistent with, and extend, recent qualitative work from the United States that similarly identified limited training, clinician discomfort, fragmented care, and uncertainty around reproductive counseling in CKD.17 Our study adds to this literature by showing how fear of catastrophic maternal and fetal outcomes can foster risk-averse counseling, with pregnancy often deferred until after transplantation, which functioned as a perceived threshold for acceptable reproductive risk.

Participants also identified the absence of dedicated guidelines as an important barrier. Current nephrology guidelines, such as Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) or the Australian Caring for Australians and New ZealandeRs with kidney Impairment (CARI) guidelines, primarily focus on traditional topics such as glomerulonephritis and transplantation, with limited attention to fertility and pregnancy. Even when pregnancy-related recommendations exist, they are often embedded within broader guidelines and difficult to locate. Compounding this issue is the scarcity of robust clinical trial data in this area, which may contribute to hesitancy among guideline authors to address pregnancy-related care.

In the absence of clear guidance, nephrologists must rely on personal judgment or consultations with more experienced clinicians, leading to inconsistent, and potentially suboptimal care. Some participants described turning to social media for real-time advice from international experts, highlighting how inadequately local systems currently equip clinicians to provide confident, standardized care. Developing dedicated, consensus-based guidelines on preconception counseling, safe medication use, and postpartum monitoring, could standardize practice, ease clinician anxiety, minimize variations in care, and improve patient outcomes.

Previous work has shown that pregnancy is an emotionally charged and complex decision for women with kidney disease who must weigh their own health alongside that of any potential child; as a consequence many feel trepidation about raising pregnancy with their nephrologist.12,15,18,19 Although patients want nephrologists to initiate fertility and pregnancy discussions, we found that nephrologists frequently do not address reproductive health because of variable clinician interest, time constraints, and competing clinical priorities. Some participants believed such discussions were “out of scope,” whereas others admitted that they simply forget to raise the topic when managing other kidney-related comorbid conditions. Reports of patients asking newly arrived female nephrologists questions they had “saved up” for years illustrate how many women are silently waiting for the right person and moment, and how much influence individual clinicians have on whether those conversations ever occur. These observations are consistent with previous work showing that although patients often expect their nephrologists to initiate fertility conversations, few clinicians do so consistently.10,11,19

Women with kidney failure are far less likely to use contraception than those in the general population (5% vs 60%); however, we found that nephrologists seldom address contraception with women of reproductive age, leaving many unaware of how kidney disease can affect their reproductive function.19,20 Minimizing unplanned, higher risk pregnancies, in this population is especially pressing in countries such as the United States and Ireland where options to terminate pregnancies, even when they endanger women’s health, are limited.21 Incorporating routine prompts in electronic medical records, embedding these into unit protocols, assigning designated reproductive health “champions” within nephrology departments, creating space for fertility discussions within multidisciplinary clinics, and partnering with patient organizations to promote resources may help integrate obstetric nephrology systematically.

Our work also highlights how fear stemming from previous adverse maternal and fetal outcomes, can make nephrologists more hesitant to initiate pregnancy discussions. This hesitancy aligns with the well-documented phenomenon of “clinical inertia,” in which uncertainty or negative past experiences delay necessary discussions and interventions.22,23 Conversely, some participants reported actively engaging with women who had stable kidney function or a successful transplant, reflecting a belief that pregnancy is more feasible in these circumstances. Although this approach aims to minimize risk, it may also contribute to delayed counseling that overlooks opportunities for safe conception planning and risk-reduction strategies in women with advanced and/or more rapidly progressive CKD.

Despite these constraints, many participants emphasized the importance of patient autonomy and shared decision making. They viewed their role as providing balanced information on risks and benefits, while deferring to patients’ reproductive goals. However, reliance on patients to initiate these conversations creates an information vacuum, particularly for women who feel uncomfortable or ill-informed about pregnancy in the context of their kidney disease. Intentional, proactive counseling, and accessible, evidence-based patient education, would help ensure women are fully supported in making informed choices about fertility, pregnancy, and postpartum care.12

Nephrologists showed a clear reluctance to support pregnancy in advanced kidney disease, which participants saw as a serious risk to maternal, kidney, and fetal health. Participants almost exclusively recommended that patients defer pregnancy until after transplantation, likely reflecting a perception that transplantation offered a safer and more familiar reproductive context than advanced CKD or dialysis. This is not necessarily the case: care has advanced for women with CKD and even receiving dialysis, while pregnancy post-transplant still confers increased risk of adverse events.24 Thus, although participants expressed support for patient autonomy this was not consistently reflected in their approach, which often limited reproductive choices rather than enabling them. Yet this reluctance does not wholly align with current evidence, which shows that while risks increases with declining kidney function, many women with advanced CKD can achieve successful pregnancies with appropriate preconception and multidisciplinary care.25,26

A key strength of this study was the use of in-depth interviews to explore nuanced perspectives and capture the contextual realities of nephrology practice. The sample included clinicians from diverse geographic locations in Australia and varying levels of clinical experience. However, the Australian setting with universal health care may limit generalizability to health systems with different financing models or referral pathways. Although we achieved thematic saturation, our sample may not encompass all possible viewpoints. Finally, although we attempted to mitigate social desirability bias, participants might have underreported or overemphasized certain behaviors or attitudes.

Our findings highlight several actionable strategies to address these themes (Table 3). Incorporating formal obstetric nephrology training into nephrology training programs would shift practice from an ad hoc to a systematic approach. Establishing evidence-based guidelines for preconception counseling, pregnancy management, and postpartum care in CKD could reduce clinical uncertainty and improve both clinician confidence and patient outcomes. Integrating prompts for fertility discussions into routine nephrology workflows, whether via electronic medical record alerts or clinic protocols, could normalize reproductive health as a core component of nephrology care. Promoting multidisciplinary teams (involving obstetricians, maternal–fetal medicine specialists, and allied health) offers the potential to reduce the burden on individual clinicians and support more comprehensive, patient-centered care. Such system-level changes are essential if nephrologists are to move from unintentionally constraining women’s reproductive options to actively enabling safe, informed pathways to motherhood.

In conclusion, this study shows that nephrologists face significant barriers in delivering reproductive care including inadequate training, absence of specific guidelines, competing clinical demands, and a risk-averse culture. Despite expressing support for patient autonomy, many clinicians defer or avoid reproductive discussions, especially with women who have advanced CKD, limiting informed decision making. Addressing these barriers through structured training, clear guidelines, and systematic integration of reproductive care into routine nephrology practice is essential to ensuring that women with kidney disease receive timely, consistent, and patient-centered reproductive care. Ultimately, nephrologists play a pivotal role in these women’s lives: their knowledge, attitudes, and willingness to engage in reproductive conversations can either facilitate or inadvertently impede women’s opportunities to pursue motherhood.
