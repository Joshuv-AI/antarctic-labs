Comparative effectiveness of school-based physical activity interventions on moderate-to-vigorous physical activity in children and adolescents: a systematic review and network meta-analysis
Authors: Qian Liu, Zongyin Xu, Xiudong Shao, Zihao Lian, Weibo Cheng, Xiaoming Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSchools provide important opportunities for promoting moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA) among children and adolescents. However, the comparative effectiveness of different school-based physical activity intervention strategies remains unclear. This systematic review and network meta-analysis compared the effects of different school-based physical activity interventions on MVPA in children and adolescents.MethodsA systematic search was conducted in PubMed, Embase, CENTRAL, ERIC, PsycINFO, Web of Science Core Collection, Scopus, SPORTDiscus, ClinicalTrials.gov, and WHO ICTRP from inception to 8 May 2026, with no language restrictions. Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and cluster-randomized controlled trials (cluster-RCTs) of school-based physical activity interventions in children and adolescents were included. Risk of bias was assessed using RoB 2. Random-effects pairwise meta-analysis, network meta-analysis, and sensitivity analyses were performed. Certainty of evidence was assessed using the Confidence in Network Meta-Analysis (CINeMA) framework.ResultsA total of 41 studies were included in the systematic review, and 29 were included in the network meta-analysis. Compared with the control condition, PE/curriculum-focused interventions were associated with higher MVPA (SMD = 0.21, 95% CI: 0.07 to 0.35), as were whole-school interventions (SMD = 0.21, 95% CI: 0.07 to 0.34). Additional physical activity opportunities beyond the regular curriculum showed no clear effect on MVPA (SMD = 0.07, 95% CI: −0.05 to 0.18). Certainty of evidence was moderate for PE/curriculum-focused and whole-school interventions and low for additional physical activity opportunities.ConclusionsSchool-based physical activity interventions showed strategy-specific effects on MVPA among children and adolescents. Current evidence suggests that PE/curriculum-focused and whole-school interventions are associated with small but potentially meaningful improvements in MVPA, whereas the effects of additional PA opportunities beyond the regular curriculum remain uncertain.Trial registrationPROSPERO, CRD420261388278.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28685-3.Keywords: Physical activity interventions, Moderate-to-vigorous physical activity, Children and adolescents, Network meta-analysis

Background

Insufficient physical activity (PA) is an important public health concern among children and adolescents. Although the World Health Organization (WHO) recommends that those aged 5–17 years accumulate an average of at least 60 min/day of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA) [1], insufficient PA remains highly prevalent among adolescents worldwide [2]. Evidence also suggests that adolescent MVPA declined between the pre-pandemic and post-pandemic periods, although these changes may partly reflect age-related development and the transition to high school [3]. More broadly, MVPA tends to decline with age during childhood and adolescence, with steeper reductions generally observed among girls [4]. Participation in organized sport during adolescence is also associated with subsequent PA and multiple health-related outcomes, indicating the potential long-term relevance of early engagement in PA [5].

Schools are widely recognized as an important setting for promoting MVPA among children and adolescents, with the potential to reach students from diverse backgrounds [6–8]. Within the school context, physical education (PE) lessons, recess, and after-school activities can provide opportunities for students to accumulate MVPA [9–11]. Classroom active breaks and active commuting to school may also serve as complementary strategies for increasing school-day PA [12–14].

School-based PA interventions vary in scope, ranging from PE- or curriculum-focused approaches to broader whole-school strategies. PE- or curriculum-focused strategies can increase MVPA during PE lessons, although these gains do not necessarily extend to out-of-class or total daily PA. Some fitness-focused PE interventions also suggest that improvements in physical fitness are not necessarily accompanied by concurrent increases in PA or MVPA [15, 16]. Whole-school or multicomponent interventions typically integrate curriculum, school-level organizational support, and family or community resources to promote PA and related health behaviors among children and adolescents; however, their effects may depend on intervention components, implementation support, and contextual conditions [8, 17, 18].

Despite the increasing diversity of school-based PA interventions, synthesized evidence on their effects on MVPA remains inconclusive. Reviews of objectively measured daily MVPA have found no clear overall effect [6], while broader evidence suggests that overall effects are generally small and may be influenced by intervention components, theoretical frameworks, parental involvement, and staff training [8]. The magnitude and consistency of effects for recess and after-school interventions remain uncertain [10, 11]. Moreover, out-of-school PA interventions based on self-determination theory suggest that relying solely on extracurricular motivational support may be insufficient to produce sustained improvements in PA among children and adolescents [19]. Collectively, these findings suggest that intervention effects may vary by strategy type and implementation approach; pooling all school-based PA interventions as a single category may therefore obscure differences between strategies.

Although school-based PA interventions have important public health and educational relevance, previous evidence has largely relied on traditional pairwise comparisons, which cannot simultaneously compare the relative effects of multiple intervention strategies. To address this gap, this systematic review aimed to evaluate the effects of school-based PA interventions on MVPA among children and adolescents. Using network meta-analysis (NMA), we further aimed to compare the relative effects and rankings of different intervention strategies, thereby providing evidence to inform school physical education and health promotion practice.

Methods

Protocol and registration

This systematic review and NMA compared the effects of different types of school-based PA interventions on MVPA among children and adolescents. The review was designed, conducted, and reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 statement and the PRISMA extension statement for reporting systematic reviews incorporating network meta-analyses [20, 21]. The completed reporting checklist is available as supplementary material (Additional file 1: Table S1. PRISMA-NMA checklist). The protocol was prospectively registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO; registration number: CRD420261388278) under the title “Comparative effectiveness of school-based physical activity interventions on moderate-to-vigorous physical activity in children and adolescents: a systematic review and network meta-analysis”.

Search strategy and information sources

The search strategy was developed according to the objectives of this systematic review and NMA and was structured using the PICOS (Participants, Interventions, Comparators, Outcomes, and Study design) framework. The core search concepts included children and adolescents, school-based settings, PA interventions, MVPA, and RCTs or cluster-RCTs. Search strategies were adapted for each database platform by combining controlled vocabulary, including MeSH, Emtree, and database-specific thesauri, with free-text terms in title, abstract, and keyword fields.

The following electronic bibliographic databases were searched from inception to 8 May 2026: PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL), ERIC, PsycINFO, Web of Science Core Collection, Scopus, and SPORTDiscus. ClinicalTrials.gov and the WHO International Clinical Trials Registry Platform (ICTRP) were also searched to identify relevant trial registrations. No restrictions were applied to publication date or language. To improve the completeness of the search, the reference lists of included studies and relevant systematic reviews were checked, and forward citation searching was also performed. The complete search strategies were documented (Additional file 1: Table S2. Search strategies).

Eligibility criteria

Eligibility criteria were defined according to the PICOS framework.

Participants: Eligible participants were children and adolescents in primary, middle, or high schools, or other school-based settings. Studies targeting the general school population or school-based subgroups, such as students with low PA levels or overweight/obesity, were eligible if the context was relevant to school-based PA promotion. Studies were excluded if they focused on preschool children, university students, adults, or mixed populations from which data for children and adolescents could not be extracted separately. Studies conducted entirely in clinical, hospital, rehabilitation, community, or other non-school settings were excluded. Studies targeting students with severe medical conditions or disabilities requiring disease-specific exercise rehabilitation were also excluded, unless the intervention was delivered in a regular school environment and was relevant to general school-based PA promotion.

Intervention: Eligible interventions were PA programs implemented in, or organized through, a school setting, with an explicit aim of promoting overall PA or MVPA. Interventions could be delivered during regular school hours, PE lessons, classroom activities, recess or lunch breaks, before- or after-school periods, or through school-wide policies, environmental modifications, or comprehensive programs. For the NMA, eligible intervention arms were classified into three prespecified nodes to distinguish how PA was primarily organized and delivered within the school context: whole-school interventions, PE/curriculum-focused interventions, and additional PA opportunities beyond the regular curriculum. Whole-school interventions were defined as programs that promoted PA through school-level policies, environmental changes, multi-stakeholder collaboration, or school-wide initiatives. PE/curriculum-focused interventions were defined as interventions primarily targeting PE lessons, classroom-based curriculum, instructional content, or curriculum structure. Additional PA opportunities beyond the regular curriculum were defined as interventions that increased structured or unstructured PA opportunities during recess, lunch breaks, before or after school, or other non-curricular periods. Multicomponent interventions were assigned to the most appropriate node based on their primary setting, delivery mode, and core intervention content. Interventions not implemented in, or organized through, a school setting were excluded, as were studies focusing solely on health education, nutrition, sedentary behavior, psychoeducation, or disease rehabilitation without an explicit PA component. Studies primarily aimed at competitive sport training or sports performance enhancement, rather than PA promotion for the general student population, were also excluded.

Comparators: Eligible comparators included usual practice, no intervention, wait-list control, minimal intervention, standard PE lessons, health education without a specific PA component, or another eligible school-based PA intervention. Studies comparing two different eligible school-based PA interventions were also included to provide direct comparative evidence for the NMA. Studies were excluded if the comparator condition was insufficiently described and therefore precluded estimation of the intervention effect on MVPA, or if they compared only different doses, intensities, or minor variants of the same intervention that could not be mapped onto the prespecified nodes.

Outcomes: The primary outcome was MVPA among children and adolescents. Objectively measured MVPA data were prioritized. When objective MVPA data were unavailable, validated self-reported MVPA measures were also eligible for inclusion. MVPA outcomes were extracted according to the following priority hierarchy: daily MVPA, weekday MVPA, and school-time MVPA. When multiple post-intervention follow-up time points were reported, the immediate post-intervention measurement was used. Studies that did not report any MVPA-related outcomes were excluded. Studies reporting relevant MVPA outcomes but providing data that could not be reliably converted into the effect-size format required for the NMA were included only in the qualitative synthesis.

Study design: Only randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and cluster-randomized controlled trials (cluster-RCTs) were eligible. Other study designs, reviews, editorials, letters, and study protocols were excluded, as were conference abstracts that provided insufficient information to determine whether an eligible MVPA outcome was reported.

All studies meeting the PICOS criteria were included in the systematic review. Studies were additionally included in the NMA when the reported data permitted calculation or derivation of an SMD for an eligible MVPA outcome; otherwise, they were included in the qualitative synthesis only.

Study selection and data extraction

Study selection and data extraction were performed independently by two reviewers. After the search results were imported into reference management software, duplicate records were removed using both automatic and manual procedures. Titles and abstracts were then screened against the predefined eligibility criteria, followed by full-text assessment of potentially eligible studies. Reasons for exclusion at the full-text stage were recorded. Disagreements between reviewers were resolved through discussion or consensus; when consensus could not be reached, a third reviewer was consulted.

Data were extracted using a standardized, predefined data extraction form. Extracted information included study characteristics, participant characteristics, intervention characteristics, comparator conditions, outcome measures, and analytical information. Specifically, the following information was extracted: first author and publication year, country or region, sample sizes of intervention and control groups, participant age, proportion of female participants, intervention name, intervention node classification, intervention duration, comparator condition, type of MVPA outcome, MVPA measurement instrument, and whether the study was included in the NMA. Intervention-arm assignments were independently reviewed by two reviewers using the prespecified node definitions. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion and consensus. To calculate effect sizes, post-intervention MVPA means, standard deviations, sample sizes, or other convertible data were extracted. For cluster-RCTs, information required for subsequent statistical adjustment was extracted or derived where necessary. When reported information was incomplete or data could not be extracted directly, calculations or conversions were performed using the available information reported in the article.

Effect size calculation and data preparation

The standardized mean difference (SMD) was used as the primary effect-size measure to synthesize continuous MVPA outcomes reported using different units or time windows. The direction of effects was harmonized so that SMD > 0 indicated higher MVPA in the intervention group than in the comparator group, corresponding to a favorable intervention effect. When the effect direction reported in the original study was opposite to this convention, the sign of the effect estimate was reversed during data preparation.

When available, contrast-based between-group effect estimates and their measures of uncertainty were extracted preferentially, including adjusted estimates or data directly convertible to SMDs. If these estimates were not available, SMDs and standard errors (SEs) were calculated from group means, standard deviations (SDs), and sample sizes. For studies that did not report SMDs directly, continuous outcomes were converted to SMDs and SEs using the available data. The SD used for standardization was selected to correspond as closely as possible to the extracted effect estimate; when the corresponding SD was unavailable, the most appropriate SD source was selected based on the reported information. Continuous outcomes reported as medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs) were converted to means and SDs when necessary. When usable standardized effect estimates and measures of uncertainty were already reported, these estimates were extracted and included after harmonizing the effect direction. For studies without contrast-based effect estimates, arm-based data were used to calculate effect sizes, preferably using post-intervention means and SDs; when post-intervention data were unavailable, mean change scores and SDs of change scores were used. SEs or 95% confidence intervals (CIs) from adjusted models were not used to back-calculate within-group SDs.

For cluster-RCTs, adjusted effect estimates or SEs that accounted for the cluster design were extracted preferentially. When a study reported an intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) but did not provide cluster-adjusted effect estimates, design-effect adjustment was performed using the reported ICC. For cluster-RCTs requiring correction but not reporting an ICC, an empirical ICC derived from the included studies (ICC = 0.018) was used to maintain consistency in the handling of cluster-randomized data. The primary analysis was based on the final cluster-adjusted effect estimates.

For multi-arm studies, each intervention arm was assigned to the corresponding network node using the prespecified classification criteria. When intervention arms belonged to different nodes, the study was retained as a multi-arm study to contribute direct comparative evidence between nodes; when multiple intervention arms within the same study belonged to the same node, these arms were combined before inclusion in the NMA.

Studies meeting PICOS criteria but not reliably convertible to SMDs with SEs, 95% CIs, or other data formats suitable for the NMA were included only in the qualitative synthesis. Pilot or feasibility studies were included in the primary analysis when they met the PICOS criteria and reported formal effect estimates or convertible continuous outcome data; their influence on the robustness of the main findings was assessed in a sensitivity analysis that excluded these studies.

Risk of bias assessment

Risk of bias in all included RCTs and cluster-RCTs was assessed using the revised Cochrane Risk of Bias tool for randomized trials (RoB 2). Assessments were conducted at the outcome level for MVPA and according to the relevant study design, covering the following domains: bias arising from the randomization process, bias due to deviations from intended interventions, bias due to missing outcome data, bias in measurement of the outcome, and bias in selection of the reported result.

Risk of bias assessment was performed independently by two reviewers. Each study was judged as “low risk of bias”, “some concerns”, or “high risk of bias” for each domain and overall. Disagreements between reviewers were resolved through discussion or consensus; when consensus could not be reached, a third reviewer was consulted. The risk of bias judgments were used to inform interpretation of the findings and were considered in sensitivity analyses and certainty-of-evidence assessment.

Data synthesis and statistical analysis

First, the included studies were summarized descriptively in terms of study and participant characteristics, intervention types, comparator conditions, MVPA outcomes, and risk of bias. For studies eligible for quantitative synthesis, SMDs with 95% CIs were used as the effect-size measure. Pairwise meta-analyses were performed using random-effects models to synthesize direct evidence comparing each intervention node with the control condition.

A frequentist NMA was conducted using a random-effects model to integrate direct and indirect evidence. The network nodes comprised the control condition, whole-school interventions, PE/curriculum-focused interventions, and additional PA opportunities beyond the regular curriculum. The control condition served as the common reference group for estimating the relative effects of each intervention node. Multi-arm trials were incorporated into the network model according to their assigned nodes, with correlations among comparisons within the same study accounted for.

The transitivity assumption was assessed descriptively by comparing key potential effect modifiers across intervention nodes, including participant age, sex distribution, intervention duration, geographic region, baseline MVPA, MVPA outcome window, measurement instrument, and overall RoB 2 judgment. The distributions were summarized using medians and ranges, counts and percentages, or reported intervention and control ranges, as appropriate.

The relative ranking of intervention strategies was evaluated using P-scores. P-scores range from 0 to 1, with higher values indicating a more favorable ranking within the network. Ranking results were interpreted alongside effect-size estimates, 95% CIs, statistical significance, and certainty of evidence.

Statistical heterogeneity was evaluated using tau², tau, I², and the overall Cochran’s Q statistic. Within-design heterogeneity and between-design inconsistency were assessed by decomposing Cochran’s Q into within-designs Q and between-designs Q. For statistical tests, p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. To assess the robustness of the main findings, sensitivity analyses were performed by excluding studies with a high overall risk of bias, excluding pilot or feasibility studies, and excluding individual studies that contributed substantially to heterogeneity.

The robustness of the findings to the ICC assumption was further examined by repeating the analyses with ICC values of 0.01 and 0.05 for studies in which an ICC of 0.018 had been assumed in the primary analysis. Study-specific ICC values were retained. To examine the robustness of the findings to MVPA outcome-window selection, additional sensitivity analyses were conducted by excluding studies contributing school-time MVPA outcomes, thereby retaining daily and weekday MVPA outcomes, and by restricting the analysis to studies contributing daily MVPA outcomes. The NMA was repeated for each restricted dataset using the same model specifications as in the primary analysis.

All statistical analyses were conducted using R software, primarily with the netmeta package.

Certainty of evidence assessment

The certainty of evidence was assessed using the Confidence in Network Meta-Analysis (CINeMA) framework, which is based on the GRADE approach and is designed to evaluate confidence in evidence from NMA [22]. For the primary comparisons between each intervention node and the control condition, certainty was assessed across six domains: within-study bias, reporting bias, indirectness, imprecision, heterogeneity, and incoherence. Overall certainty for each comparison was rated as high, moderate, low, or very low based on judgments across these domains.

Within-study bias was judged primarily based on the RoB 2 assessment. Reporting bias was evaluated by considering the completeness of study reporting, consistency between prespecified and reported outcomes, and the possibility of selective or incomplete reporting of MVPA outcomes. Indirectness was judged according to the alignment of study populations, interventions, comparators, and MVPA outcomes with the review question. Imprecision was assessed based on the width of the confidence intervals around effect estimates and the extent to which they affected interpretation of the results. Heterogeneity and incoherence were assessed with reference to statistical heterogeneity and network inconsistency, respectively. Certainty of evidence was used to interpret confidence in the effect estimates for each intervention strategy and was considered together with effect-size estimates, statistical significance, and P-score rankings when interpreting the findings.

Results

Study selection

The study selection process is shown in Fig. 1. Searches of databases and trial registers identified 12,174 records. Of these, 10,903 records were identified from eight bibliographic databases: PubMed/MEDLINE (n = 1,855), Embase (n = 2,148), CENTRAL (n = 1,183), ERIC (n = 199), PsycINFO (n = 298), Web of Science Core Collection (n = 2,086), Scopus (n = 2,532), and SPORTDiscus (n = 602). A further 1,271 records were identified from trial registers: ClinicalTrials.gov (n = 458) and the WHO International Clinical Trials Registry Platform (ICTRP; n = 813). After 6,945 duplicate records were removed, 5,229 records were screened by title and abstract, of which 5,022 were excluded. The full texts of 207 reports were assessed for eligibility, and 166 reports were excluded for the following reasons: ineligible study design (n = 56), ineligible population (n = 9), ineligible intervention (n = 17), ineligible outcome (n = 68), ineligible comparator (n = 8), not conducted in a school-based setting (n = 5), and study protocol (n = 3). Citation searching identified 26 potentially relevant records, all of which had already been captured by the database or trial-register searches; therefore, no additional studies were included from citation searching. Overall, 41 studies were included in the systematic review. Of these, 29 studies provided sufficient data for quantitative synthesis and were included in the NMA, whereas 12 studies were retained for qualitative synthesis only.

Study characteristics

A total of 41 studies were included in the review; study-level details covered country or region, participant characteristics, intervention classification, comparator conditions, MVPA outcomes, measurement instruments, and NMA inclusion (Additional file 1: Table S3. Characteristics of included studies). The included studies were published between 2007 and 2026 [23–63], with 27 studies (65.9%) published in 2016 or later, including one study published in 2026. This distribution suggests that evidence on school-based PA interventions and MVPA has continued to accumulate in recent years. Participants were mainly children and adolescents in primary to secondary school settings, with ages ranging approximately from 6 to 14 years. The studies were conducted mainly in the United Kingdom, the United States, Australia, China, Spain, and Norway. Sample sizes varied substantially across studies. Most included studies used a cluster-RCT design, whereas a smaller number used individual RCTs or pilot/feasibility cluster-RCTs.

Intervention components, comparator conditions, and MVPA measurement methods varied across the included studies. According to the prespecified classification, interventions were mapped onto three intervention nodes: whole-school interventions, PE/curriculum-focused interventions, and additional PA opportunities beyond the regular curriculum. Comparator conditions mainly included usual practice, standard PE lessons, or routine health education. Intervention duration ranged from 6 weeks to 4 years. MVPA was assessed predominantly using device-based measures, mainly accelerometers such as ActiGraph, GENEActiv, and Axivity. No extracted MVPA outcome was derived from a direct observational measure, such as the System for Observing Play and Leisure Activity in Youth (SOPLAY). All 29 studies included in the NMA used device-based MVPA measures, whereas the only study using a questionnaire-based self-reported MVPA measure was retained for qualitative synthesis only [31].

Risk of bias assessment

Figure 3 presents the RoB 2 assessment results for the 29 studies included in the NMA. Overall, 4 studies were judged as having low risk of bias, 23 as having some concerns, and 2 as having high risk of bias. Concerns were mainly related to missing outcome data and selection of the reported result. In contrast, most studies were judged as low risk for deviations from intended interventions, and all studies were judged as low risk for measurement of the outcome.

Risk of bias assessment of studies included in the network meta-analysis

Concerns about missing outcome data mainly reflected incomplete or insufficiently reported outcome data in some studies, whereas concerns about selection of the reported result were primarily related to uncertainty about selective reporting of MVPA outcomes. The two studies judged as having high overall risk of bias were mainly affected by missing outcome data. These risk-of-bias judgments were considered when interpreting the NMA results and assessing the certainty of evidence.

Pairwise meta-analysis

To summarize the direct evidence comparing each intervention strategy with the control condition, random-effects pairwise meta-analyses were performed (Additional file 1: Fig. S1. Pairwise meta-analysis forest plot). PE/curriculum-focused interventions were associated with a significant increase in MVPA compared with the control condition (SMD = 0.26, 95% CI: 0.05 to 0.46). Whole-school interventions also showed a significant positive effect (SMD = 0.19, 95% CI: 0.09 to 0.29). In contrast, the pooled effect of additional PA opportunities beyond the regular curriculum was positive in direction but not statistically significant (SMD = 0.03, 95% CI: −0.06 to 0.12).

Between-study heterogeneity was observed across the three direct comparisons. Heterogeneity was greatest for PE/curriculum-focused interventions (I² = 83.6%), with lower but still notable heterogeneity for whole-school interventions and additional PA opportunities beyond the regular curriculum (I² = 51.0% and 54.9%, respectively). Overall, the pairwise findings were directionally consistent with the NMA results: PE/curriculum-focused interventions and whole-school interventions showed significant positive effects, whereas additional PA opportunities beyond the regular curriculum showed a smaller and non-significant effect.

Heterogeneity, inconsistency and sensitivity analyses

The descriptive assessment of transitivity across intervention nodes is reported in detail (Additional file 1: Table S4. Distribution of potential effect modifiers across intervention nodes). Participant age distributions overlapped substantially, daily MVPA was the most common outcome window in each node, and most studies in each node received an overall RoB 2 judgment of some concerns. Compared with the other two nodes, studies of additional PA opportunities had a higher median proportion of female participants, a shorter median intervention duration, and a larger proportion of studies conducted in Europe.

The NMA indicated considerable between-study heterogeneity across the network (tau² = 0.0248, tau = 0.1574, I² = 70.5%, 95% CI: 57.3% to 79.6%). The overall Cochran’s Q test was statistically significant (Q = 98.30, df = 29, p < 0.0001), indicating variability in effect estimates across studies. Decomposition of the Q statistic showed a statistically significant within-designs Q (Q = 86.54, df = 26, p < 0.0001), suggesting heterogeneity within direct comparisons. The between-designs Q was also statistically significant (Q = 11.76, df = 3, p = 0.0083), suggesting possible inconsistency across designs in the network. Therefore, the relative effects and rankings of intervention strategies should be interpreted with caution in light of the observed heterogeneity and inconsistency.

Sensitivity analyses were conducted to examine the robustness of the main findings, with full results reported separately (Additional file 1: Table S5. Sensitivity analyses excluding selected studies). After excluding the two studies judged as having high overall risk of bias, the main findings remained materially unchanged. PE/curriculum-focused interventions remained significantly associated with higher MVPA (SMD = 0.21, 95% CI: 0.07 to 0.34, p = 0.003), and whole-school interventions also remained significant (SMD = 0.17, 95% CI: 0.03 to 0.31, p = 0.021), whereas additional PA opportunities beyond the regular curriculum remained non-significant (SMD = 0.08, 95% CI: −0.04 to 0.20, p = 0.211). After excluding pilot or feasibility studies, PE/curriculum-focused interventions (SMD = 0.16, 95% CI: 0.04 to 0.28, p = 0.011) and whole-school interventions (SMD = 0.19, 95% CI: 0.05 to 0.33, p = 0.008) remained significant, whereas the effect of additional PA opportunities beyond the regular curriculum was close to zero and non-significant (SMD = − 0.01, 95% CI: −0.13 to 0.10, p = 0.836). After excluding Riley (2015), PE/curriculum-focused interventions (SMD = 0.16, 95% CI: 0.04 to 0.29, p = 0.011) and whole-school interventions (SMD = 0.20, 95% CI: 0.08 to 0.33, p = 0.002) remained significant, whereas additional PA opportunities beyond the regular curriculum remained non-significant (SMD = 0.05, 95% CI: −0.05 to 0.16, p = 0.302).

Sensitivity analyses were also conducted based on MVPA outcome windows (Additional file 1: Table S6. Sensitivity analyses by MVPA outcome window). Excluding studies contributing school-time MVPA outcomes yielded a pattern consistent with the primary analysis: whole-school and PE/curriculum-focused interventions remained significantly associated with higher MVPA, whereas additional PA opportunities remained non-significant. When the analysis was restricted to daily MVPA outcomes, whole-school interventions remained significantly associated with higher MVPA (SMD = 0.20, 95% CI: 0.04 to 0.35, p = 0.015). The estimate for PE/curriculum-focused interventions remained positive but was smaller and no longer statistically significant (SMD = 0.11, 95% CI: −0.05 to 0.26, p = 0.185), while additional PA opportunities remained non-significant.

Sensitivity analyses using ICC values of 0.01 and 0.05 yielded findings consistent with the primary analysis (Additional file 1: Table S7. Sensitivity analyses using alternative intraclass correlation coefficient values). PE/curriculum-focused and whole-school interventions remained significantly associated with higher MVPA, whereas additional PA opportunities remained non-significant. When an ICC of 0.05 was used, whole-school interventions had a slightly higher P-score than PE/curriculum-focused interventions.

Overall, whole-school interventions remained significantly associated with higher MVPA across all sensitivity analyses, whereas additional PA opportunities beyond the regular curriculum remained non-significant throughout. Findings for PE/curriculum-focused interventions were generally consistent with the primary analysis. When the analysis was restricted to daily MVPA outcomes, however, the estimate remained positive but was attenuated and no longer statistically significant. Network heterogeneity was lower than that observed in the primary analysis after excluding pilot or feasibility studies (I² = 59.6%), excluding Riley (2015) (I² = 63.8%), excluding studies contributing school-time MVPA outcomes (I² = 63.8%), and restricting the analysis to daily MVPA outcomes (I² = 64.7%).

Certainty of evidence

The detailed certainty-of-evidence assessments are summarized by comparison and CINeMA domain (Additional file 1: Table S8. Certainty of evidence). Based on CINeMA, certainty of evidence was rated as moderate for PE/curriculum-focused interventions versus the control condition and for whole-school interventions versus the control condition. Downgrading for these two comparisons was mainly due to concerns related to within-study bias, heterogeneity, and incoherence, whereas no serious concerns were identified for reporting bias, indirectness, or imprecision.

For additional PA opportunities beyond the regular curriculum versus the control condition, certainty of evidence was rated as low. This lower certainty was attributable to concerns related to within-study bias, heterogeneity, incoherence, and imprecision, with the 95% CI crossing the line of no effect. Overall, certainty of evidence was moderate for PE/curriculum-focused interventions and whole-school interventions and low for additional PA opportunities beyond the regular curriculum.

Discussion

Primary findings

This systematic review and NMA compared the relative effects of three categories of school-based PA interventions on MVPA among children and adolescents. Overall, PE/curriculum-focused interventions and whole-school interventions showed positive effects on MVPA and had similar rankings in the primary analysis. These findings were broadly supported by the pairwise estimates and most sensitivity analyses. However, when the analysis was restricted to daily MVPA outcomes, the estimate for PE/curriculum-focused interventions remained positive but was attenuated and no longer statistically significant, whereas whole-school interventions remained significantly associated with higher MVPA. In contrast, although additional PA opportunities beyond the regular curriculum showed a positive direction of effect, the evidence did not provide clear statistical support for this strategy.

Certainty of evidence was moderate for PE/curriculum-focused interventions and whole-school interventions, but low for additional PA opportunities beyond the regular curriculum. Given the considerable heterogeneity and possible inconsistency observed in the network, these findings should be interpreted with caution. Taken together, the current evidence suggests small but potentially meaningful improvements in MVPA with PE/curriculum-focused and whole-school interventions, whereas the effects of additional PA opportunities beyond the regular curriculum remain uncertain. However, the available evidence does not allow a clear distinction between these two strategies.

Comparison to previous reviews

Previous reviews have identified schools as an important setting for promoting PA among children and adolescents; however, evidence on the overall effects of school-based interventions on MVPA remains inconsistent and is often characterized by substantial between-study heterogeneity [64, 65]. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis restricted to accelerometer-assessed outcomes found no clear overall effect on daily MVPA but reported a significant positive effect for interventions with two components, whereas interventions with one component or three or more components did not show significant effects [66]. Although its component-based framework differs from the strategy-based classification used in the present NMA, it provides a complementary perspective and may help contextualize the observed between-study heterogeneity.

Regarding PE/curriculum-focused interventions, modifying PE lesson content, improving class organization, providing teacher training, or adjusting activity structures can increase students’ MVPA during PE lessons [67, 68]. At the same time, the proportion of PE lesson time that students spend in MVPA often remains below recommended levels, indicating considerable scope for improvement [9, 69]. However, previous evidence has mainly focused on changes in PE lesson-time MVPA, with less attention to the relative effects of curriculum-focused interventions compared with other school-based PA strategies. The present NMA addresses this gap by suggesting that PE/curriculum-focused interventions have a relatively consistent positive effect, supporting the role of PE curriculum and lesson structure as important pathways for increasing MVPA in school settings.

Integrating structured PE, classroom active breaks, additional activity opportunities, or school-level organizational strategies into the school day may help increase students’ MVPA; however, effects may vary according to intervention components and implementation approaches [43, 44]. This is consistent with previous findings of heterogeneity in school-based PA intervention effects and suggests that combining heterogeneous interventions as a single category may limit understanding of the relative contribution of specific strategies. By differentiating among school-based PA approaches, this study suggests that whole-school interventions may have a relatively consistent positive effect on MVPA, providing a more specific strategy-level explanation for inconsistencies in previous synthesized evidence.

In contrast, although recess and after-school interventions have potential to increase MVPA, their effects may depend on participation rates, the quality of activity organization, session duration, and implementation context [10, 11]. These factors may help explain why additional PA opportunities beyond the regular curriculum showed less stable effects and lower certainty in the present NMA. Compared with interventions embedded in PE curricula or whole-school structures, additional activity opportunities beyond the regular curriculum often rely more on voluntary student participation, on-site organization, and sustained supervision. Consequently, their independent effects may be more sensitive to school context and implementation fidelity.

Taken together, previous findings of modest overall effects, high heterogeneity, and inconsistent conclusions may partly reflect differences in mechanisms and implementation stability across intervention strategies. By using NMA to compare these strategies, this study suggests that PE/curriculum-focused interventions and whole-school interventions are supported by more consistent evidence, whereas the effects of additional PA opportunities beyond the regular curriculum remain uncertain. This provides a more specific strategy-level explanation for the inconsistent or unstable effects reported in previous reviews.

Interpretation of intervention effects

The findings across the three categories of school-based PA interventions may be interpreted from a social-ecological perspective, which emphasizes that PA is shaped by interacting influences across multiple levels [70]. From this perspective, the effects of each strategy may depend not only on its core features, but also on the extent to which it is embedded in daily school routines, implemented consistently, and able to achieve sufficient student exposure. The positive effect of PE/curriculum-focused interventions may be partly explained by their integration within the formal curriculum. Unlike voluntary or supplementary activities, PE lessons and curriculum-related programs usually have fixed schedules, explicit instructional objectives, and teacher-led organization, which may provide more stable and predictable intervention exposure for students. By optimizing class organization, reducing waiting and management time, increasing activity density, or restructuring instructional tasks, such interventions may increase students’ MVPA within existing lesson time [24, 37]. Compared with adding activity opportunities outside regular lessons, embedding interventions within the curriculum may be more likely to produce stable and measurable improvements in MVPA.

The positive effects of whole-school interventions may reflect their multilevel and organizationally integrated nature. Rather than relying on a single lesson or isolated activity period, these interventions often combine curriculum scheduling, environmental modifications, teacher support, peer climate, and school-level policies, thereby reinforcing PA opportunities across different times and contexts within the school day. This type of system-level design may help expand intervention reach and support the maintenance of PA behaviors [43, 44]. Nevertheless, whole-school interventions are complex and their effects may vary according to school resources, implementation intensity, staff engagement, and organizational support. Therefore, although whole-school interventions showed significant positive effects in the NMA, the accompanying heterogeneity may partly reflect the context-dependent implementation of multicomponent interventions in real-world school settings.

In contrast, the less stable effects of additional PA opportunities beyond the regular curriculum may be explained by their delivery during non-curricular periods, reliance on voluntary student participation, and sensitivity to activity organization, supervision, environmental conditions, and implementation context [71–73]. From a self-determination theory perspective, sustained participation in additional PA opportunities beyond the regular curriculum may also depend partly on whether these opportunities support students’ autonomy, competence, and relatedness [19]. These considerations do not rule out the potential value of activity opportunities beyond the regular curriculum; rather, they suggest that, without sufficient organization, consistent participation, and sustained school-level support, their independent effects may be limited. Overall, whether school-based PA interventions can reliably increase MVPA may depend not only on providing activity opportunities, but also on whether these opportunities are systematically embedded within curriculum structures, school organization, and sustained implementation processes.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several methodological strengths. Compared with previous reviews that mainly relied on pairwise meta-analyses, this study used NMA to integrate direct and indirect evidence within a common analytical framework and compare the relative effects of three categories of school-based PA interventions on MVPA among children and adolescents. This approach can provide more systematic strategy-level evidence, particularly when direct head-to-head comparisons between active interventions are limited. In addition, only RCTs and cluster-RCTs were included, and MVPA was specified as the primary outcome. All studies included in the quantitative synthesis used device-based measures of MVPA, which may strengthen the credibility of the effect estimates and reduce measurement bias.

Another methodological strength is that the intervention nodes were prespecified according to how PA was primarily organized and delivered within the school context, rather than classified post hoc according to the observed results. This strategy-level classification facilitated comparisons among different school-based PA strategies rather than treating all eligible interventions as a single broad category.

Finally, sensitivity analyses and certainty-of-evidence assessment using CINeMA allowed the robustness and credibility of the main findings to be examined, so that the conclusions were interpreted not only according to effect-size estimates but also in relation to risk of bias, heterogeneity, inconsistency, and certainty of evidence.

Several methodological limitations should also be considered when interpreting these findings. One limitation concerns the considerable between-study heterogeneity observed across the network, which may reflect differences in intervention components, implementation intensity, intervention duration, school context, and MVPA outcome windows across the included studies. Daily, weekday, and school-time MVPA may not capture fully equivalent behavioral constructs, although the use of SMDs allowed these differently scaled outcomes to be statistically synthesized. In addition, even among device-based measures, variation in device models, wear-time protocols, and data-processing procedures may have contributed to variability in measured MVPA across studies. Network heterogeneity was lower after excluding pilot or feasibility studies, after excluding studies contributing school-time MVPA outcomes, and when the analysis was restricted to daily MVPA outcomes, suggesting that the inclusion of pilot or feasibility studies and differences in outcome-window selection may have contributed to the observed variability. Although the sensitivity analyses generally supported the overall pattern of the main findings, the effect estimates should still be interpreted cautiously in light of the remaining heterogeneity.

A further limitation relates to the complexity of school-based PA interventions. Although several potential effect modifiers showed overlapping distributions across intervention nodes, differences in sex distribution, intervention duration, and geographic representation may contribute to residual uncertainty in the assessment of the transitivity assumption. Moreover, although a prespecified node classification was used, variation within each node in intervention components, delivery modes, and implementation fidelity may still affect the interpretation of the pooled estimates.

Finally, direct head-to-head comparisons between active intervention strategies were limited, and some relative effect estimates therefore relied mainly on indirect evidence. Therefore, intervention rankings should be interpreted cautiously, together with effect-size estimates, heterogeneity, inconsistency, and certainty of evidence.

Implications for future research

To improve the comparability and capacity for synthesis of evidence on school-based PA interventions, future primary studies should adopt more standardized approaches to trial design and outcome reporting. Because school-based PA interventions are often implemented at the class or school level, cluster-RCTs remain an important design for evaluating these complex interventions. For studies using cluster designs, investigators should report ICCs, design effects, cluster-adjusted estimates, and corresponding SEs or 95% CIs. Such reporting would improve the accuracy of effect-size extraction, data conversion, and subsequent evidence synthesis. Future reports should also provide post-intervention MVPA means, SDs, between-group differences, SEs, or 95% CIs, thereby reducing the likelihood that eligible studies cannot be included in quantitative synthesis because of insufficient data reporting.

Standardizing MVPA outcome measurement and reporting is also important for improving comparability across studies. Future studies should prespecify primary MVPA outcomes according to the intervention rationale and report measurement instruments, device-wearing protocols, and data-processing methods in detail. Across different types of school-based PA interventions, outcome selection should be aligned with the expected pathway of effect, helping to distinguish effects on specific MVPA windows from effects on total daily MVPA.

In terms of comparative evidence, direct head-to-head comparisons between active interventions remain limited in the current network, and many relative effect estimates therefore relied mainly on indirect evidence. Therefore, well-designed active-comparison or head-to-head trials are needed to directly evaluate different school-based PA strategies or combinations of strategies and to improve certainty about their relative effects. In addition, trial reports should describe not only whether an intervention was effective, but also intervention intensity, frequency, duration, teacher training, student participation, implementation fidelity, and school contextual factors. Incorporating process evaluations to document intervention dose and implementation quality may help explain heterogeneity across studies and inform judgments about the scalability and sustainability of effective strategies in real-world school settings.

Conclusions

This systematic review and NMA suggests that the effects of school-based PA interventions on MVPA among children and adolescents may vary by intervention strategy. Current evidence suggests that PE/curriculum-focused and whole-school interventions are associated with small but potentially meaningful improvements in MVPA, whereas the effects of additional PA opportunities beyond the regular curriculum remain uncertain. These findings indicate the potential value of embedding PA within PE/curriculum and whole-school routines, although the practical effects of these approaches may depend on implementation and school context. Future research should include well-designed and transparently reported trials with direct comparisons between active interventions to further examine the relative effects of different school-based PA strategies.
