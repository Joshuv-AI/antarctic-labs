Duty of care: amplifying the impact of planetary health research at oncology conferences
Authors: Seamus O’Reilly, Rob Chuter, Sara V Lightowlers
Published: 2026

Editorials are the voice of a journal [1]. While most published articles are incremental steps that build on existing knowledge, articles that receive an editorial are generally those that introduce significant, paradigm-shifting, or highly controversial findings that necessitate expert interpretation to contextualise their impact [2]. Why, therefore, did the article by Delaye and colleagues [3] published in this issue of BJC Reports warrant an editorial? Was it because the article's findings were controversial? Was it because it would cause a paradigm shift? Was it because it would lead to a patient impact? The answer is No, Yes and Yes. It was not selected because we believe that in the context of the current triple planetary crisis, which consists of climate change, loss of biodiversity and pollution, its findings were controversial [4–6]. It was selected because, as a healthcare community, we need to see how we practice through an environmental sustainability lens. It was selected because we believe that medical conferences are influential spaces that can amplify that planetary health message, and that our health is reliant on the health of our planet [7–9]. Previously, publications have focused on reducing the environmental impact of in-person conference attendance and conduct [10–16]. We believe this is the first article in the oncology literature that has horizon-scanned meeting presentations for their planetary health impacts.

What did Delaye and colleagues find? At the 2025 European Society of Medical Oncology (ESMO) Congress held in Berlin 2937 abstracts were presented to over 37,000 participants. The authors identified 10 abstracts that could be seen through a planetary health lens, by suggesting meaningful reductions in drug use, clinic attendance, treatment duration or overall care intensity. The abstracts ranged from primary prevention, therapeutic de-escalation, reduced dose or duration of systemic therapy, biomarker-guided treatment selection and non-pharmacologic interventions. None of the studies had explicitly assessed environmental impact, but they could have co-benefits nonetheless.

What are the implications of their findings? Firstly, across several cancer types and treatments, reduced treatment intensity didn’t compromise patient outcomes but significantly reduced carbon emissions. It also likely had cascade effects of less time and financial toxicity. Integrating biomarkers into care can facilitate such dose optimisation. Secondly, prioritising cancer prevention, such as human papillomavirus vaccination, has significant downstream favourable impacts on carbon emissions by preventing the need for potential treatment later in life. Thirdly, planet friendly lifestyle and exercise programs can have similar patient benefits to pharmacologic interventions [17]. Fourthly, all of the seven studies demonstrating non-inferiority with optimisation schedules were investigator, and not industry, initiated. This poses a larger question about our current regulatory environment, where drug approval is contingent on demonstration of efficacy without an obligation to determine the optimum effective dose for patients, and consequently for the planet [18]. How many industry-sponsored studies presented at ESMO would have offered the potential for carbon emission reduction if it had been considered? Finally, the authors demonstrated the feasibility of horizon scanning a contemporaneous scientific meeting and consequently amplifying carbon mitigation strategies. Their work also poses the question about what additional sustainability-impacting presentations might have been detected with a more formalised, at submission point of identification.

For patients with cancer, life is led facing a horizon of uncertainty. Climate change is a risk multiplier of this uncertainty. It disrupts cancer care through extreme weather events, consumes economic reserves by prioritising the need to establish climate adaption measures, and by compromising treatment outcomes [19, 20]. The care that transforms our patients’ lives is carbon-intensive and consequently climate toxic [21]. In Ireland, this climate toxicity will increase by 90% in the coming decades unless we change how we practice [22]. Our current clinical conundrum of caring for patients and caring for the planet arises because of human behaviour [23–25]. Influencing this behaviour will involve providing clinicians with the capability to practice sustainably, along with the opportunity to do so, and will be driven by their personal motivation [26]. In contrast, medical conferences are led through a lens of opportunity. Such opportunity has been demonstrated in the past six years in the COVID-19-associated acceleration of virtual and hybrid meetings, in promoting of artificial intelligence-related biomedical research, and in promoting sustainable conference conduct and meeting attendance [27].

We believe that the paper by Delaye and colleagues provides the basis for a template for conference organisers to amplify sustainability initiatives at meetings to enable clinicians to achieve this. We have used their work to propose a model for sustainability amplification at medical conferences (Fig. 1). In this model, abstract submission portals would include the possibility of assigning sustainability as a subheading, analogous to how they have rapidly adapted artificial intelligence as one. This classification could then be used to amplify sustainability-impacting research through a green presentation track and through dedicated awards. The model builds on the work and recommendations of the ESMO Climate Change Task Force, who have integrated sustainability into, and hosted dedicated sessions at ESMO conferences [27], with subsequent collaborative initiatives based on associated in-person networking (e.g., [28]. This Task Force has also developed a Climate Portal on the ESMO website to collate sustainability associated presentations and publications, such as those highlighted by Delaye and colleagues [3]. The proposed model in Fig. 1 facilities the potential inclusion of sustainability perspectives in meeting attendance, clinical practice, clinical guidelines [29–31], and climate activism by conference attendees.

The Green Conference and its role in amplifying planetary health messages.

Medical societies view the world through a prism of opportunity, community and responsibility for their members. In the decades ahead, patients and our families will reflect on how we have knowingly bequeathed an unstable planet and its human consequences to them. They will look at the missed opportunities that we had to address this, including those opportunities at conferences. They will hold the medical community, as knowledge brokers in society, to a higher standard [32–34]. They will highlight our duty of care to have acted. The paper by Delaye et al provides an opportunity and template for this action. It enables our medical societies to fulfil all aspirations of their mission, including those of community and responsibility. That was why the paper was selected for an editorial. We hope that it is integrated into conferences and that it will serve as a much-needed catalyst for climate action in the medical and scientific communities, and a means by which they can fulfil their duty of care to patients, their families and wider humanity.
