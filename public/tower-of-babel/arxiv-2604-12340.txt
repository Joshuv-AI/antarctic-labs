Information-Geometric Decomposition of Generalization Error in Unsupervised Learning
Gilhan Kim

Abstract
We decompose the Kullback–Leibler generalization error (GE)—the expected KL divergence from the data distribution to the trained model—of unsupervised learning into three non-negative components: model error, data bias, and variance. The decomposition is exact for any e-flat model class and follows from two identities of information geometry: the generalized Pythagorean theorem and a dual e-mixture variance identity. As an analytically tractable demonstration, we apply the framework to ϵ \epsilon -PCA, a regularized principal component analysis in which the empirical covariance is truncated at rank N K N_{K} and discarded directions are pinned at a fixed noise floor ϵ \epsilon . Although rank-constrained ϵ \epsilon -PCA is not itself e-flat, it admits a technical reformulation with the same total GE on isotropic Gaussian data, under which each component of the decomposition takes closed form. The optimal rank emerges as the cutoff λ cut ∗ = ϵ \lambda_{\mathrm{cut}}^{*}=\epsilon —the model retains exactly those empirical eigenvalues exceeding the noise floor—with the cutoff reflecting a marginal-rate balance between model-error gain and data-bias cost. A boundary comparison further yields a three-regime phase diagram—retain-all, interior, and collapse—separated by the lower Marchenko–Pastur edge and an analytically computable collapse threshold ϵ ∗ ​ ( α ) \epsilon_{*}(\alpha) , where α \alpha is the dimension-to-sample-size ratio. All claims are verified numerically.

1
Introduction

Identifying the model complexity that minimizes the generalization error
(GE) is a central problem of statistical learning.
For supervised learning the standard guide is the bias–variance
tradeoff: increasing the model complexity reduces the bias of the
estimator at the cost of inflating its variance, and the optimal
complexity is reached at the balance between these two competing
contributions.
For unsupervised learning, where the goal is to estimate an entire
probability distribution rather than a conditional mean, an analogous
decomposition was missing until recently.

A natural setting in which to ask the question rigorously is
that of
fully visible
generative models—models in
which every random variable is directly observed, with no
latent or hidden variables marginalised out. This class
includes fully visible Boltzmann machines, multivariate
Gaussian models such as
\epsilon
-PCA, naive-Bayes-type
models, and any visible-only Markov random field, and excludes
RBMs, variational autoencoders, and most modern deep generative
models. The strongest form of the decomposition holds when the
model manifold is e-flat (an exponential family in its natural
parameters); when the parametric constraint is non-linear in
those parameters—as for the rank-constrained
\epsilon
-PCA
class—the framework still defines the same three components
but their non-negativity is in general lost
(Proposition
1
). For
\epsilon
-PCA itself we
circumvent this obstruction by a technical e-flat
reformulation (Lemma
1
) that has the same
total GE on isotropic data, and apply the decomposition to that
reformulation.

In a previous joint work
[
15
]
, the present author and
collaborators proposed that the GE of unsupervised learning likewise
admits a two-component tradeoff,

\mathrm{GE}\;=\;\mathrm{ME}\;+\;\mathrm{DE},

(1)

between a
model error
ME, which decreases as the model becomes
more expressive, and a
data error
DE, which grows because a
finite training set is an imperfect representative of the true
distribution.
The decomposition was corroborated numerically for restricted
Boltzmann machines (RBMs) trained on the two-dimensional Ising model
and on the totally asymmetric simple exclusion process (TASEP), and
the resulting U-shaped GE curves yielded a non-trivial optimal model
size that grows with the complexity of the data.

The decomposition (
1
), however, was an empirical
observation. Two questions were left unanswered:

(Q1)

Is the data error DE itself decomposable into more
elementary contributions, in particular into a part that
measures finite-sample bias and a part that measures
stochasticity of training?

(Q2)

Is there a class of models in which the decomposition can
be derived from first principles, and in which the optimal
model complexity can be computed in closed form?

In this paper we answer both questions affirmatively, by placing
the problem inside the framework of information geometry

[
1
,
2
]
and combining it with classical
random matrix theory. The information-geometric analysis of
statistical learning has a long history: the generalized
Pythagorean theorem and dual flatness underlie the modern
understanding of KL-divergence minimisation
[
8
]

and of maximum-likelihood estimation in exponential families

[
2
]
, while singular learning theory and the widely
applicable information criterion (WAIC)
[
21
]

provide a parallel asymptotic decomposition of the Bayes
generalization error in terms of the real log-canonical threshold
of the model. On the random-matrix side, the limiting empirical
spectrum of sample covariance matrices is governed by the
Marchenko–Pastur law

[
16
,
4
,
9
]
,
and the optimal threshold for hard truncation of singular values
in low-rank denoising is the celebrated
4/\sqrt{3}
rule of

[
10
]
, complemented in the spiked-covariance
setting by the BBP transition of

[
5
,
17
,
6
]
.
Our
decomposition is closer in spirit to the bias–variance picture of
frequentist learning than to the Bayes-loss decomposition of

[
21
]
, while our
\epsilon
-PCA closed-form result is
the natural KL-divergence analogue, in a generative setting, of the
spectral truncation rules of

[
10
,
20
]
.
Our main contributions are:

(i) Closed-form optimal rank for
\epsilon
-PCA.

We introduce
\epsilon
-PCA, a regularized principal component
analysis of zero-mean Gaussian data: the empirical covariance

N_{K}

For isotropic true covariance, the Marchenko–Pastur spectral

\lambda_{\mathrm{cut}}^{*}\;=\;\epsilon;

(2)

N_{K}^{*}/N_{V}=\int_{\epsilon}^{\lambda_{+}(\alpha)}p_{\mathrm{MP}}(\lambda;\alpha)\,d\lambda

\epsilon

\alpha=N_{V}/D
.

(ii) Three-regime phase diagram of the optimum.

Comparing the interior local minimum of (i) with the boundary value

\epsilon

\displaystyle\big\langle D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\,\|\,Q^{m})\big\rangle_{m}\;=\;

\displaystyle\underbrace{D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\,\|\,Q^{0})}_{\text{ME}}\;+\;\underbrace{D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q^{0}\,\|\,\bar{Q})}_{\text{Data bias}}

\displaystyle\;+\;\underbrace{\big\langle D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\bar{Q}\,\|\,Q^{m})\big\rangle_{m}}_{\text{Variance}},

\mathcal{M}

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows.

Preliminaries

2.1
Notation

Throughout the paper,
P
denotes the unknown true
distribution on a measurable space
\mathcal{V}
, and
expectations
\langle\cdot\rangle_{m}
are taken over the
randomness of the training datasets indexed by
m
. The Kullback–Leibler divergence is

D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\|Q)=\int P\,\log(P/Q)\,dv
(logarithms are natural).

H(P)=-\int P\log P\,dv
denotes the differential or discrete
entropy. We use
\mathcal{M}
for the model manifold, e-flat sub-families
of an ambient exponential family being our main object of interest;
the natural and expectation parameters are denoted
\theta
and

\eta
, respectively, with cumulant function
\psi(\theta)
. For
multivariate-statistics conventions we follow

[
3
]
, and for random matrices

[
4
]
:

\Sigma^{m}=D^{-1}X_{m}^{\top}X_{m}
is the empirical covariance of

D
i.i.d. samples in
\mathbb{R}^{N_{V}}
, and the dimension-to-sample-size
ratio is
\alpha=N_{V}/D
.

2.2
Generalization error of unsupervised learning

A learning algorithm produces, from each training dataset, a
trained model
Q^{m}
belonging to a parametric family
\mathcal{M}
.
The (Kullback–Leibler) generalization error is

\mathrm{GE}\;:=\;\big\langle D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\,\|\,Q^{m})\big\rangle_{m}.

(4)

2.3
Exponential families and dual flatness

Following
[
2
]
, an exponential family

Q_{V}(v;\theta)\;=\;\exp\!\Big[\,\theta^{i}F_{i}(v)\;-\;\psi(\theta)\,\Big]

(5)

forms an
e-flat
submanifold of the space of probability
distributions, with natural parameters
\theta^{i}
as e-affine
coordinates and the cumulant function
\psi(\theta)
as the potential.
The expectation parameters

\eta_{i}(\theta)=\partial_{i}\psi(\theta)
supply the dual,
m-affine coordinates.

A submanifold
\mathcal{M}
is
e-flat
if it is described by linear
constraints in
\theta
, and
m-flat
if it is described by linear
constraints in
\eta
.
For any e-flat
\mathcal{M}
, the e-mixture

\displaystyle\bar{Q}

\displaystyle\;:=\;\exp\!\Big[\,\big\langle\log Q^{m}\big\rangle_{m}+\bar{F}\,\Big],

\displaystyle\bar{F}

\displaystyle\;:=\;-\log\!\int\!\exp\!\big\langle\log Q^{m}(v)\big\rangle_{m}\,dv,

(6)

of any collection of points
Q^{m}\in\mathcal{M}
is itself a point of

\mathcal{M}
. This closure property is what makes the e-mixture, rather
than the arithmetic mean, the natural notion of average inside an
exponential family.

2.4
Generalized Pythagorean theorem

We will repeatedly use the following standard result

[
2
, Thm. 3.8]
.

Theorem 1

(Generalized Pythagorean theorem)
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be an e-flat submanifold and let
P
be an arbitrary
distribution. Let
Q^{*}\in\mathcal{M}
be the m-projection of
P
onto

\mathcal{M}
, i.e.,

Q^{*}\;=\;\arg\min_{Q\in\mathcal{M}}\,D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\,\|\,Q).

Then for every
R\in\mathcal{M}
,

D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\,\|\,R)\;=\;D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\,\|\,Q^{*})\;+\;D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q^{*}\,\|\,R).

(7)

The two terms on the right-hand side of (
7
) are the squared
“orthogonal” and “along-manifold” components, respectively, of the
divergence from
P
to
R
.

3
Three-component decomposition of the generalization error

3.1
Statement and proof

Throughout this section,
\mathcal{M}
denotes an e-flat submanifold of the
space of probability distributions on the visible variables, and we
write

\displaystyle Q^{0}

\displaystyle\;:=\;\arg\min_{Q\in\mathcal{M}}\,D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\,\|\,Q),

\displaystyle\bar{Q}

\displaystyle\;:=\;\exp\!\Big[\big\langle\log Q^{m}\big\rangle_{m}+\bar{F}\Big],

(8)

for the m-projection of
P
onto
\mathcal{M}
and for the e-mixture of
the trained models, respectively.

Theorem 2

(Three-component decomposition)
.

Assume that the trained models
\{Q^{m}\}_{m}
all belong to the
e-flat submanifold
\mathcal{M}
. Then

\displaystyle\big\langle D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\,\|\,Q^{m})\big\rangle_{m}\;=\;

\displaystyle\underbrace{D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\,\|\,Q^{0})}_{=:\,\mathrm{ME}}\;+\;\underbrace{D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q^{0}\,\|\,\bar{Q})}_{=:\,\mathrm{Data\ bias}}

\displaystyle\;+\;\underbrace{\big\langle D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\bar{Q}\,\|\,Q^{m})\big\rangle_{m}}_{=:\,\mathrm{Variance}}.

(9)

.

The three terms in (
9
) capture three
qualitatively distinct sources of error, and it is useful to
visualise them separately.

\bullet

Model error

\mathrm{ME}=D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\|Q^{0})
is the
irreducible
error.
It is the divergence from the true distribution
P
down to
the closest point
Q^{0}
of the model manifold
\mathcal{M}
, and
would persist even with infinitely many training samples and
an oracle learner. ME measures how badly
\mathcal{M}
misspecifies

P
; it depends only on the geometry of
\mathcal{M}
and not on
any particular dataset, and shrinks only by enriching the
model class.

\bullet

Data bias

\mathrm{Data\ bias}=D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q^{0}\|\bar{Q})
is the

systematic
error introduced by the finite-data training
process. It measures how far the typical (e-mixture-averaged)
trained model
\bar{Q}
sits from the m-projection
Q^{0}
that
an ideal infinite-data learner would recover. It is a
property of the algorithm–data interaction averaged over
dataset randomness, not of any single realisation, and
vanishes only when the learning procedure is consistent in
the e-mixture sense (
\bar{Q}\to Q^{0}
as
D\to\infty
).

\bullet

Variance

\mathrm{Variance}=\langle D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\bar{Q}\|Q^{m})\rangle_{m}
is
the
stochastic
error. It is the average dispersion of
the trained models
Q^{m}
around their centroid
\bar{Q}
and
captures dataset-to-dataset fluctuation of the learner. It is
independent of how well
\mathcal{M}
fits
P
and is controlled
purely by the size and randomness of the training set.

Geometrically, ME is an
off-manifold
divergence from

P
down to
\mathcal{M}
, while data bias and variance are both

on-manifold
divergences along
\mathcal{M}
—the former
between two distinguished points
Q^{0}
and
\bar{Q}
, the
latter a spread of the random
Q^{m}
around
\bar{Q}
. Each
component responds to a different lever: ME to model capacity,
data bias to algorithmic consistency, and variance to sample
size. A schematic visualization of these three distances is
shown in Figure
1
.

Proof.

Because
\mathcal{M}
is e-flat, the e-mixture
\bar{Q}
defined in
(
8
) belongs to
\mathcal{M}
. By
Theorem
1
applied with
P=P
,
Q^{*}=Q^{0}
, and

R=\bar{Q}
,

D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\,\|\,\bar{Q})\;=\;D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\,\|\,Q^{0})\;+\;D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q^{0}\,\|\,\bar{Q}).

(10)

Next we establish the e-mixture variance identity

\displaystyle\big\langle D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\,\|\,Q^{m})\big\rangle_{m}\;=\;\;

\displaystyle D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\,\|\,\bar{Q})

\displaystyle\;+\;\big\langle D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\bar{Q}\,\|\,Q^{m})\big\rangle_{m},

(11)

which holds for any reference distribution
P
, requires no
e-flatness hypothesis, and is a direct consequence of the
definition (
6
) of
\bar{Q}
.
The identity (
11
) is the dual analogue, in
information geometry, of the classical bias–variance decomposition
of the mean squared error around its mean: it expresses the average
divergence from
P
to the random trained models as the
divergence from
P
to their e-mixture centroid plus the
fluctuation of the trained models around that centroid

[
2
, §2.6 and §3.6]
.
Indeed, denoting by
\mathbb{E}_{R}[\cdot]:=\int R(v)\,(\cdot)\,dv
the
expectation under a distribution
R
, the e-mixture identity

\langle\log Q^{m}\rangle_{m}=\log\bar{Q}-\bar{F}
gives, after
taking
\mathbb{E}_{P}[\cdot]
on both sides and rearranging,

\displaystyle\big\langle D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\,\|\,Q^{m})\big\rangle_{m}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{P}\!\left[\log P\right]-\mathbb{E}_{P}\!\left[\langle\log Q^{m}\rangle_{m}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{P}\!\left[\log P\right]-\mathbb{E}_{P}\!\left[\log\bar{Q}\right]+\bar{F}

\displaystyle=D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\,\|\,\bar{Q})\;+\;\bar{F}.

A parallel computation, taking
\mathbb{E}_{\bar{Q}}[\cdot]
instead, gives

\langle D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\bar{Q}\,\|\,Q^{m})\rangle_{m}=\bar{F}
.
(In particular,
\bar{F}\geq 0
, since the left-hand side is a
non-negative average of KL divergences.) Subtracting these two
identities eliminates
\bar{F}
and yields (
11
).

Substituting (
10
) into (
11
) gives
(
9
). Non-negativity of all three terms follows
because each is a Kullback–Leibler divergence.

Remark
.

Equation (
11
) makes precise the sense in which the
e-mixture
\bar{Q}
is the “correct” average for an exponential
family: it is the unique point of the ambient space for which the GE
splits as a sum of a deterministic part
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\|\bar{Q})
and a
fluctuation part
\langle D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\bar{Q}\|Q^{m})\rangle_{m}
that captures
the dataset-to-dataset variability of the trained model. The
e-flatness assumption is needed only for the further decomposition
(
10
) of the deterministic part.

Remark

(Two equivalent forms of the data bias)
.

Two natural expressions for the data bias appear in this work and
in the prior literature:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Data\ bias}_{\text{alg}}

\displaystyle\;:=\;\int P(v)\,\log\frac{Q^{0}(v)}{\bar{Q}(v)}\,dv

\displaystyle\;=\;D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\|\bar{Q})-D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\|Q^{0}),

(12)

which is a purely algebraic identity in

,
Q^{0}
, and

\bar{Q}
and refines the data-error term
\mathrm{DE}:=\langle D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\|Q^{m})\rangle_{m}-D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\|Q^{0})
of

[
15
]
: by the e-mixture variance identity
(

), one has

\mathrm{DE}=\mathrm{Data\ bias}_{\text{alg}}+\mathrm{Variance}
, so the present three-component decomposition
splits the prior two-component data error into a bias and a

\mathrm{Data\ bias}_{\text{GPT}}\;:=\;D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q^{0}\|\bar{Q}),

(13)

which appears in the statement of

2
. Under the e-flatness hypothesis of

, the generalized Pythagorean identity

, so the two forms coincide

The two forms differ when the e-flatness hypothesis fails, however:

) continues to satisfy the

3.2
Failure of e-flatness and the obstruction

Of the three components in (
9
), the model error
and the variance are each manifestly non-negative because they are
written from the start as Kullback–Leibler divergences. The data
bias is more delicate: as Remark
Remark
discusses,
its algebraic form (
12
) is a difference of two
KL divergences and is therefore manifestly sign-indefinite, and
its non-negativity in Theorem
2
(where it equals

D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q^{0}\|\bar{Q})
) is not built into the definition—it is a

derived consequence
of the generalized Pythagorean theorem,
holding only when the e-flatness of
\mathcal{M}
allows the GPT to be
applied. We now show that for models with hidden variables this
hypothesis can fail, and that the algebraic data bias
(
12
) can indeed take negative values.

Consider an exponential family on the joint space of visible and
hidden variables,

Q_{VH}(v,h;\theta)\;=\;\exp\!\Big[\,\theta^{i}F_{i}(v,h)-\psi(\theta)\,\Big],

(14)

and its visible marginal, obtained by integrating out (or
summing over) the hidden variables,

Q_{V}(v;\theta)\;=\;\int Q_{VH}(v,h;\theta)\,dh.

(15)

The joint family (
14
) is e-flat in the joint
space, but its visible-marginal family
(
15
) is in general
not
an
exponential family in
v

[
2
, §2.4 and §8.1]
:
the marginalization mixes the natural parameters in a
non-affine way.

Proposition 1
.

Let
\mathcal{M}_{V}^{\mathrm{hid}}:=\{Q_{V}(\cdot;\theta):\theta\in\Theta\}
be the visible-marginal family of an
exponential family with hidden variables, as in
(
15
).

(a)

\mathcal{M}_{V}^{\mathrm{hid}}
is generally not e-flat.

(b)

Because the generalized Pythagorean theorem
(Theorem
1
) requires e-flatness, the
m-projection identity used in the proof of
Theorem
2
need not hold on

\mathcal{M}_{V}^{\mathrm{hid}}
. The algebraic data bias
(
12
) is then no longer constrained by any
KL-type inequality and can take a negative value: the
m-projection
Q^{0}
onto
\mathcal{M}_{V}^{\mathrm{hid}}
may be a
strictly worse fit to
P
than the e-mixture
\bar{Q}
, which
itself generically does not lie on

\mathcal{M}_{V}^{\mathrm{hid}}
.

Sketch.

Statement (a) is the well-known fact that marginalization does not,
in general, preserve exponential-family structure

[
2
, §8]
. An explicit class of examples is provided
by restricted Boltzmann machines

Remark

(Joint vs. visible loss)
.

Proposition
1
should not be read as the
statement that hidden-variable models are themselves non-e-flat.
The joint family
\{Q_{VH}(\cdot;\theta)\}
is, by construction
(
14
), an exponential family in
(v,h)
and
hence e-flat in the joint space; Theorem
2

applies to it without any modification, provided one defines the
generalization error in the joint space,

\langle D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_{VH}\,\|\,Q_{VH}^{m})\rangle_{m}
, with respect
to some reference joint distribution
P_{VH}
. The obstruction
arises only when the GE is defined—as is standard in the
unsupervised learning literature—in the

\langle D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\,\|\,Q_{V}^{m})\rangle_{m}
, because the
effective model class is then the visible-marginal family

\mathcal{M}_{V}^{\mathrm{hid}}
, which is non-e-flat. In short: the
framework’s failure for hidden-variable models is a property of
the standard
loss
, not of the model itself. The joint GE
is therefore not a meaningful generalisation target for
ordinary unsupervised training—hidden variables have no
ground-truth values to compare against—but it remains a
well-defined and useful quantity for measuring the divergence
between two trained models whose hidden labellings can be put
in one-to-one correspondence (e.g., a teacher–student or
distillation setting in which the hidden units of the student
inherit a fixed semantic ordering from the teacher).

Remark

(The sign of the data bias as an e-flatness diagnostic)
.

The non-negativity of the data bias in the e-flat case is a
derived consequence of the generalized Pythagorean theorem
rather than a feature of its definition, and is therefore lost
whenever the e-flatness hypothesis fails.
Proposition
1
thus suggests using the
empirically measured sign of the algebraic data bias
(
12
) as a diagnostic for whether a
generative model class is well approximated by an exponential
family in its visible variables.

Remark

(The obstruction is not specific to hidden variables)
.

Although hidden variables provide the most familiar source of
non-e-flatness, the proposition does not require them. Any
non-affine constraint on the natural parameters of an ambient
exponential family yields a curved (non-e-flat) submanifold and
the same loss of non-negativity for the data bias. A fully
visible example is the rank-constrained
\epsilon
-PCA model
class (
17

4

\epsilon
-PCA model is a Gaussian and hence lies in the

\mathbb{R}^{N_{V}}
, but the
rank-plus-noise-floor constraint that characterises the

\epsilon
-PCA submanifold is non-linear in the natural

\Sigma^{-1}
. The closed-form analysis of
Section
4
therefore proceeds via a technical
e-flat reformulation (Lemma
1

Theorem
2
to be applied to
\epsilon
-PCA
indirectly.

4

\epsilon
-PCA

Setup

The true distribution is the centered isotropic multivariate

The

-PCA model class.

The rank-

The trained model.

N_{K}

\displaystyle 2\,D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\,\|\,Q^{m})\;=\;\;

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N_{K}}\!\Big(\frac{1}{\lambda_{i}^{(m)}}+\log\lambda_{i}^{(m)}\Big)

\displaystyle+(N_{V}-N_{K})\!\Big(\frac{1}{\epsilon}+\log\epsilon\Big)-N_{V}.

4.2
Marchenko–Pastur asymptotics

In the high-dimensional limit

fixed, the empirical

\displaystyle p_{\mathrm{MP}}(\lambda)

\displaystyle\;=\;\frac{1}{2\pi\alpha\lambda}\sqrt{(\lambda_{+}-\lambda)(\lambda-\lambda_{-})},

\displaystyle\lambda_{\pm}

\displaystyle\;=\;(1\pm\sqrt{\alpha})^{2},

(20)

supported on

(we assume

We define the cutoff

.

4.4
Decomposition of GE and the optimal cutoff

Remark

(Why the cutoff condition is not as trivial as it looks)
.

The conclusion “retain those eigenvalues exceeding the noise
floor
\epsilon
” coincides with the standard denoising
heuristic, but the closed-form derivation hides three points
that are not at all evident a priori.

(a) Cancellation of the Marchenko–Pastur prefactor.

The first-order condition
f(\lambda_{\mathrm{cut}})=f(\epsilon)

emerges only because the chain-rule expansion of

d\mathrm{GE}/dr
produces the same prefactor

p_{\mathrm{MP}}(\lambda_{\mathrm{cut}})
from

d\lambda_{\mathrm{cut}}/dr
as from
dI_{-1}/d\lambda_{\mathrm{cut}}

and
dI_{\log}/d\lambda_{\mathrm{cut}}
, and the prefactor

cancels
. Were the loss anything other than KL—for
instance, mean-squared error—this cancellation would not occur
and the optimal cutoff would depend explicitly on the local
density
p_{\mathrm{MP}}(\lambda_{\mathrm{cut}})
and hence on
the aspect ratio
\alpha
. The marginal rates
(
32
) make the cancellation transparent: the

r
-derivatives of ME and Data bias are both of the form

f(\cdot)-1
, and their balance is therefore a transcendental
equation in
f
alone, independent of the spectral measure.

(b) Independence from
\alpha
.

A direct consequence of (a) is that

\lambda_{\mathrm{cut}}^{*}
does not depend on the
dimension-to-sample-size ratio
\alpha=N_{V}/D
: it is the same
function of
\epsilon
at
\alpha=0.1
as at
\alpha=0.9
.
This contrasts sharply with the optimal hard-threshold rule of

[
10
]
, in which the threshold scales as

(4/\sqrt{3})\sqrt{D\cdot\mathrm{noise}}
with explicit

\alpha
-dependence through the upper Marchenko–Pastur edge,
and reflects the fact that KL is a unitless information measure
while Frobenius loss carries dimensional weight.

(c) The other root is a local maximum, not a minimum.

The equation
f(\lambda)=f(\epsilon)
has two roots in

(0,\infty)
, namely
\epsilon
itself and a strictly larger
value
\widetilde{\lambda}>1
. The second root corresponds to
a local
maximum
of the GE on the kept branch and would
be invisible to a per-direction unconstrained analysis: the
unconstrained per-direction rule

f(\lambda_{i})<f(\epsilon)
would in fact require the largest
empirical eigenvalues (those with
\lambda_{i}>\widetilde{\lambda}
,
which can lie inside the MP bulk for moderate
\alpha
) to be

discarded
, since fitting them with their large empirical
value makes the model overconfident and increases the KL more
than the noise-floor approximation does. The rank constraint of

\epsilon
-PCA forces these large eigenvalues to be retained as
soon as
N_{K}
exceeds their count, producing an

overshoot
cost. Theorem
3

nevertheless asserts that this overshoot does not move the
optimal cutoff away from
\epsilon
on the lower side—a fact
that is geometric, not arithmetic, and follows from the
linearity of
\mathrm{ME}(r)
in
r
once
\mathcal{M}_{\diamond}^{N_{K}}

is recognised as e-flat.

Remark
.

Theorem
3
produces the strikingly simple
prescription that an
\epsilon
-PCA model should retain precisely
those data directions whose empirical variance exceeds the model’s
intrinsic noise floor
\epsilon
. The condition is the natural
analogue, in the
\epsilon
-PCA setting, of the hard-threshold
singular-value rule of
[
10
]
and of the
noise-only PCA criterion based on the Marchenko–Pastur edge

[
20
]
; here, however, the threshold is determined
by a model parameter (
\epsilon
) rather than by the upper edge of
the MP density.

4.5
Three-regime phase structure of the global optimum

Theorem
3
identifies the unique interior
local minimum of GE on the open rank ratio
r\in(0,1)
. Whether
this interior minimum is in fact the
global
optimum
depends on a comparison with the two boundary values

\mathrm{GE}(r{=}0)
and
\mathrm{GE}(r{=}1)
, and on whether the
interior cutoff
\lambda_{\mathrm{cut}}^{*}=\epsilon
lies
inside the MP support
[\lambda_{-},\lambda_{+}]
. Carrying out
this comparison yields the following sharp three-regime phase
structure.

Proposition 2

(Three-regime phase structure)
.

Fix the aspect ratio
\alpha\in(0,1)
and consider

\epsilon
-PCA on isotropic Gaussian data in the
high-dimensional limit
N_{V},D\to\infty
with

N_{V}/D=\alpha
. Then the rank ratio

r^{*}=N_{K}^{*}/N_{V}
that globally minimizes the
generalization error (
31
) satisfies:

(R1)

(
retain-all
)
If
\epsilon\leq\lambda_{-}(\alpha)=(1-\sqrt{\alpha})^{2}
,
then
r^{*}=1
. Equivalently, the cutoff

\lambda_{\mathrm{cut}}^{*}
lies below the MP support, every
empirical eigenvalue exceeds
\epsilon
, and the optimal model
retains all of them.

(R2)

(
interior
)
If
\lambda_{-}(\alpha)<\epsilon<\epsilon_{*}(\alpha)
,
then
r^{*}
is the interior optimum of
Theorem
3
,

r^{*}=\int_{\epsilon}^{\lambda_{+}(\alpha)}p_{\mathrm{MP}}(\lambda;\alpha)\,d\lambda
, and the optimal
model retains exactly those empirical eigenvalues that exceed

\epsilon
.

(R3)

(
collapse
)
If
\epsilon\geq\epsilon_{*}(\alpha)
, then
r^{*}=0
. The
optimal model is the pure noise-floor distribution

\mathcal{N}(0,\,\epsilon I_{N_{V}})
and uses no information
from the training data.

The collapse threshold
\epsilon_{*}(\alpha)\in(\lambda_{-}(\alpha),1)
is the unique solution of

\mathrm{GE}_{\mathrm{interior}}(\epsilon)\;=\;\tfrac{1}{2}\,N_{V}\!\Big(\tfrac{1}{\epsilon}+\log\epsilon-1\Big),

(36)

where the right-hand side is the closed-form value of

\mathrm{GE}(r{=}0)
and the left-hand side is the value of
(
31
) at the interior cutoff

\lambda_{\mathrm{cut}}^{*}=\epsilon
.

Proof.

On the half-line
r=0
the trained model degenerates to

\mathcal{N}(0,\,\epsilon I_{N_{V}})
and (
25
) gives

\mathrm{GE}(0)=\tfrac{1}{2}N_{V}(1/\epsilon+\log\epsilon-1)
,
the right-hand side of (
36
). On the half-line

r=1
the model is the eigen-MLE and one verifies from
(
33
) that

\mathrm{GE}(1)-\mathrm{GE}(\epsilon)
has the sign of

\epsilon-\lambda_{-}(\alpha)
(the MP integrand has support
on
[\lambda_{-},\lambda_{+}]
).

(R1) Suppose
\epsilon\leq\lambda_{-}(\alpha)
. We show that

f(\lambda)\leq f(\epsilon)
for every
\lambda\in[\lambda_{-},\lambda_{+}]
, so that the right-hand side of
(
33
) is non-positive on
r\in(0,1)
and the GE is
non-increasing in
r
, attaining its minimum at
r^{*}=1
.

Since
f
is strictly convex on
(0,\infty)
with global
minimum
f(1)=1
, the maximum of
f
on the closed interval

[\lambda_{-},\lambda_{+}]
is attained at one of the
endpoints. For the lower endpoint we have
\epsilon\leq\lambda_{-}<1
, and
f
is strictly decreasing on
(0,1)
,
so
f(\lambda_{-})\leq f(\epsilon)
. For the upper endpoint
we use the explicit form
\lambda_{\pm}=(1\pm\sqrt{\alpha})^{2}
:
writing
x:=\sqrt{\alpha}\in(0,1)
, a direct computation
gives

f(\lambda_{-})-f(\lambda_{+})\;=\;\frac{4x}{(1-x^{2})^{2}}\,-\,2\log\!\frac{1+x}{1-x},

which vanishes at
x=0
and whose derivative in
x
is

\frac{d}{dx}\bigl[f(\lambda_{-})-f(\lambda_{+})\bigr]\;=\;\frac{4x^{2}(5-x^{2})}{(1-x^{2})^{3}},

strictly positive on
(0,1)
since the numerator

4x^{2}(5-x^{2})
and the denominator
(1-x^{2})^{3}
are
both positive on that interval. Hence

f(\lambda_{-})>f(\lambda_{+})
for every

\alpha\in(0,1)
. Combining,
f(\lambda_{+})<f(\lambda_{-})\leq f(\epsilon)
, so
f\leq f(\epsilon)

throughout the MP support, as claimed.

(R2)–(R3) Suppose
\epsilon>\lambda_{-}(\alpha)
. Then the
interior critical point
\lambda_{\mathrm{cut}}^{*}=\epsilon

of Theorem
3
lies in the MP support and
is a strict local minimum on the open interval. By continuity
of (
31
) on
[0,1]
, the global minimizer
is one of
\{0,\,r_{\epsilon},\,1\}
, where
r_{\epsilon}

is the interior critical point. The case
r^{*}=1
is
excluded for
\epsilon>\lambda_{-}
by an analogous endpoint
analysis to that of (R1) above (the trajectory of

f(\lambda_{\mathrm{cut}})-f(\epsilon)
as
r
approaches
1

is now strictly positive), so
r^{*}\in\{0,r_{\epsilon}\}
.

We compare
\mathrm{GE}_{\mathrm{int}}(\epsilon):=\mathrm{GE}(r_{\epsilon},\epsilon)
and
\mathrm{GE}(0)
as
functions of
\epsilon
. Two observations simplify the
computation. First, for fixed
r
, the data-bias term
(
30
) depends on
\epsilon
only through the
coupling
r=\int_{\lambda_{\mathrm{cut}}}^{\lambda_{+}}p_{\mathrm{MP}}(\lambda)\,d\lambda
, so at fixed
r
the cutoff

\lambda_{\mathrm{cut}}
and the integrals

I_{-1}(\lambda_{\mathrm{cut}})
,

I_{\log}(\lambda_{\mathrm{cut}})
are all

\epsilon
-independent, giving

\partial(\text{Data bias})/\partial\epsilon\big|_{r}=0
.
Second, by the first-order optimality condition

\partial\mathrm{GE}/\partial r\big|_{r=r_{\epsilon}}=0
, the
envelope theorem eliminates the
dr_{\epsilon}/d\epsilon

contribution. Combining,

\frac{d\,\mathrm{GE}_{\mathrm{int}}(\epsilon)}{d\epsilon}\;=\;\frac{\partial\mathrm{ME}}{\partial\epsilon}\bigg|_{r=r_{\epsilon}}\;=\;\frac{N_{V}}{2}\,(1-r_{\epsilon})\,f^{\prime}(\epsilon),

while
\frac{d}{d\epsilon}\mathrm{GE}(0)=\frac{N_{V}}{2}\,f^{\prime}(\epsilon)
.
Subtracting,

\frac{d}{d\epsilon}\big[\mathrm{GE}_{\mathrm{int}}(\epsilon)-\mathrm{GE}(0)\big]\;=\;-\,\frac{N_{V}}{2}\,r_{\epsilon}\,f^{\prime}(\epsilon)\;>\;0

on
(\lambda_{-},1)
, since
r_{\epsilon}>0
and

f^{\prime}(\epsilon)=(\epsilon-1)/\epsilon^{2}<0
. (Note that
both
\mathrm{GE}_{\mathrm{int}}
and
\mathrm{GE}(0)
are
themselves
decreasing
in
\epsilon
; their difference
increases because
\mathrm{GE}(0)
decreases faster.)
The difference is therefore strictly increasing in
\epsilon

on
(\lambda_{-},1)
, is negative at
\epsilon=\lambda_{-}

(where
r_{\epsilon}=1
and
\mathrm{GE}_{\mathrm{int}}(\lambda_{-})=\mathrm{GE}(r{=}1)<\mathrm{GE}(0)|_{\epsilon=\lambda_{-}}

by the same upper-endpoint calculation as in (R1)), and is
positive as
\epsilon\to 1^{-}
. Hence it changes sign
exactly once at the unique
\epsilon_{*}(\alpha)
defined by
(
36
). For
\epsilon<\epsilon_{*}
the interior
wins, giving (R2); for
\epsilon\geq\epsilon_{*}
the
boundary wins, giving (R3).
∎∎

Remark
.

The collapse threshold
\epsilon_{*}(\alpha)
has a simple but
counter-intuitive operational meaning. A naive expectation
would be that, since
\epsilon<1
in the regime of interest
and the pure-noise model
\mathcal{N}(0,\epsilon I_{N_{V}})
is
already a strictly misspecified approximation of the truth,
learning
any
positive number of empirical eigendirections

slightly. Proposition
2
shows that this
expectation is wrong above
\epsilon_{*}(\alpha)
: the
finite-sample over-confidence cost of fitting any positive rank

exceeds
the model-error reduction it provides, so that
the optimal trained model has
N_{K}^{*}=0
and uses
no

information from the training data. The boundary

\epsilon_{*}(\alpha)
is therefore the largest noise floor for
which the data still contain enough information to be worth
using at all. This is the
\epsilon
-PCA analogue of the

singular learning
regime of
[
21
]
, in
which finite-sample fluctuations overwhelm the signal that the
model can extract.

5
Information-theoretic interpretation

The decomposition (
9
) of the unsupervised
generalization error has natural readings in the language of
classical information theory. We collect two of them here for
completeness; neither is required for the proofs above, but
they make explicit how the decomposition fits within the
standard tools of the Shannon-theoretic literature.

5.1
Cross-entropy form and code-length deficits

The KL divergence
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\,\|\,Q^{m})
is, by the Gibbs identity,
the difference between the cross-entropy of
Q^{m}
relative to

P
and the entropy of
P
:

D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\,\|\,Q^{m})\;=\;H(P;Q^{m})-H(P),

(37)

where
H(P;Q^{m}):=-\int P\log Q^{m}
.
Operationally,
H(P;Q^{m})
is the asymptotic per-symbol
description length, in nats, achieved by encoding samples from

P
with a code matched to
Q^{m}

[
7
, Chap. 5]
, while
H(P)
is the optimal
code length achievable for
P
. The generalization error is
therefore the average code-length
deficit
introduced by
using a finite-sample model in place of the true distribution.
Substituting (
37
) into (
9
) and
isolating the
P
-dependent terms gives

\big\langle H(P;Q^{m})\big\rangle_{m}\;=\;H(P;Q^{0})\;+\;\mathrm{Data\ bias}\;+\;\mathrm{Variance},

(38)

where the data bias on the right is the integral

\int P\log(Q^{0}/\bar{Q})
of (
12
) and the
variance is
\langle D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\bar{Q}\|Q^{m})\rangle_{m}
. In words, the
expected coding cost of the trained model splits into a model-class
floor
H(P;Q^{0})
, a finite-sample bias of the m-projection,
and a fluctuation term that vanishes when training is deterministic.

5.2
Rate–distortion view of
\epsilon
-PCA

The
\epsilon
-PCA model with rank
N_{K}
can be read as a
rate–distortion code for the latent Gaussian source
P=\mathcal{N}(0,I_{N_{V}})
: it spends one “natural parameter” per
retained eigenvalue (rate
\propto N_{K}
) and pays a per-discarded-
direction distortion equal to
D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\big(\mathcal{N}(0,1)\|\mathcal{N}(0,\epsilon)\big)=\tfrac{1}{2}(f(\epsilon)-1)
.
The model error (
29
) is then exactly the total
distortion at rate
N_{K}
, namely

\mathrm{ME}=\tfrac{1}{2}(N_{V}-N_{K})(f(\epsilon)-1)
.
The closed-form Theorem
3
states that,
when the finite-sample data-bias term (
30
) is also
taken into account, the optimal rate
N_{K}^{*}
is the one for
which the marginal cost of admitting one more empirical
direction into the data bias balances the marginal benefit of
removing one
\epsilon
-pinned direction from the model error,
yielding the cutoff condition

f(\lambda_{\mathrm{cut}}^{*})=f(\epsilon)
. This is the natural
analogue, in our generative-modelling setting, of the optimal
hard-thresholding rule of
[
10
]
for low-rank
denoising; here the threshold is set by the model’s intrinsic
noise floor
\epsilon
rather than by the upper edge of the
Marchenko–Pastur support.

6
Discussion

We have established a closed-form characterization of the optimal
model rank for
\epsilon
-PCA on isotropic Gaussian data
(Theorem
3
): the unique interior local
minimum of the generalization error sits at the cutoff

\lambda_{\mathrm{cut}}^{*}=\epsilon
, so that the optimal model
retains precisely those empirical covariance eigenvalues that
exceed the intrinsic noise floor. The first-order condition

f(\lambda_{\mathrm{cut}}^{*})=f(\epsilon)
with

f(x)=1/x+\log x
also admits a second root

\widetilde{\lambda}>1
, but this root is a local

maximum
of GE on the kept branch rather than a minimum.
A comparison of the interior local-minimum value with the
r=0

boundary value of GE yields a sharp three-regime phase structure
for the global optimum (Proposition
2
), with the
lower Marchenko–Pastur edge
\lambda_{-}(\alpha)
separating the
retain-all phase from the interior phase, and an analytically
computable collapse threshold
\epsilon_{*}(\alpha)
separating
the interior phase from the collapse phase. The closed-form

\lambda_{\mathrm{cut}}^{*}=\epsilon
rule and the corresponding
phase diagram are the natural KL-divergence analogues, in a
generative setting, of the spectral truncation rules of

[
10
,
20
]
.

The framework underlying these closed-form results is an
information-geometric three-component decomposition of the
unsupervised GE, which formalises and extends the empirical
two-component tradeoff of
[
15
]
: the data error
splits, via the generalized Pythagorean theorem combined with a
dual variance identity for the e-mixture, into a finite-sample
data bias and a training-stochasticity variance, with each of
the three components non-negative whenever the model manifold
is e-flat (Theorem
2
). When the model manifold
fails to be e-flat—for instance, for visible marginals of
hidden-variable models or for rank-constrained Gaussian models
such as the eigen-
\epsilon
-PCA class
(
17
)—the data bias is no longer forced to be
non-negative, and the framework cannot be invoked directly. We
have shown how to circumvent this for
\epsilon
-PCA itself by a
technical reformulation (Lemma
1
) that places
the trained model on an e-flat sub-family with the same total
GE on isotropic data; the closed-form
Theorem
3
is then recovered as a direct
consequence of Theorem
2
, with the cutoff
condition emerging as a marginal-rate balance between the model
error and the data bias.

More broadly, the framework developed here suggests several
lines of further inquiry that we leave open.

Spike model and anisotropic data.

The closed-form Theorem
3
relies on
isotropy of
P
in two specific places: in
Lemma
1
, which uses the conjugation
invariance of
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(I\,\|\,\cdot)
to transfer the

\diamond
-model decomposition back to eigen-
\epsilon
-PCA;
and in the m-projection
\widetilde{Q}^{0}
of
P
onto

\mathcal{M}_{\diamond}^{N_{K}}
, which is diagonal with entries
1
in
the kept block. For a spiked covariance

\Sigma_{0}=I+\sum_{j=1}^{r}\theta_{j}\,u_{j}u_{j}^{\top}
,
neither step survives literally: the conjugation invariance is
broken by the spike directions, and the m-projection inherits
the spike eigenvalues in the kept block. We expect that the
correct generalisation has a cutoff condition of the form

f(\lambda_{\mathrm{cut}}^{*})=f(\sigma_{j}^{2})
, where

\sigma_{j}^{2}
is the projected variance of
P
along the

j
-th surviving spike direction, modified by the BBP transition

[
5
]
: spikes whose strength

\theta_{j}
exceeds the BBP threshold
\sqrt{\alpha}
remain
detectable in the empirical spectrum and shift the cutoff
upward, while sub-threshold spikes are absorbed into the bulk
and recover the isotropic answer

\lambda_{\mathrm{cut}}^{*}=\epsilon
. Establishing this
precisely requires the joint spectral law of spiked Wishart
matrices
[
5
,
14
]
, which we
leave for future work. A fully anisotropic generalisation
(arbitrary
\Sigma_{0}
with no spike structure) is more
delicate and would naturally invoke the free-probability
machinery for the limiting spectrum of

\Sigma_{0}^{1/2}W\Sigma_{0}^{1/2}
with
W
Wishart.

Sharpness of the obstruction.

Proposition
1
shows that the data bias of
Theorem
2
can become negative whenever the
model manifold fails to be e-flat, but does not say

when
. Whether negativity occurs for every non-affine
constraint or only for constraints of a specific
type—rank-like, equality-like, or low-dimensional
sub-manifold—would turn the data bias into a quantitative
geometric diagnostic in the sense of

[
21
,
11
]
, with concrete tests for
the e-flat regime of complex generative models.

Application to fully visible Boltzmann machines.

The most directly testable extension of the framework is to the
canonical
strict
e-flat fully visible example: the fully
visible Boltzmann machine, whose model class is itself an
exponential family in its natural parameters and therefore
satisfies Theorem
2
without any technical
reformulation of the kind used in Section
4.3
.
Such an implementation has long been impractical because the
partition function is intractable and classical Markov chain
Monte Carlo sampling converges slowly; recent work on

diabatic quantum annealing
for Boltzmann sampling

[
12
]
provides a hardware-accelerated
alternative that opens the door to a direct numerical
verification of the U-shaped GE decomposition in this
discrete-state setting, and—through extensions to
latent-variable priors—also to the case in which
Proposition
1
’s obstruction is expected to
manifest.
