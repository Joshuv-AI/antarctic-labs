Abstract
Objective: This study aimed to propose a theoretical refinement of the hypothesis that Korsakoff syndrome is a disorder
of experience integration, reconciling it with recent evidence that emotional reactivity is largely preserved in the
syndrome. Methodology: theoretical-conceptual study based on a narrative review of the literature, mainly from the last
five years, on emotional and autobiographical memory, confabulation, intrusions, future thinking, awareness and the
anatomy of the diencephalic memory system, with a clinical vignette used only for illustration. Results: the evidence
indicates that patients with Korsakoff syndrome generate emotion but fail to incorporate emotionally relevant events
into autobiographical memory and prospection, with a particular weakness for negative information. We propose a
three-level model: affective reactivity (relatively preserved), relational binding and selection in the hippocampal–
mammillary–thalamic system (damaged), and autobiographical integration and prospection in cortical networks (intact
in principle but deprived of bound input). The uncoupling between the first and third levels generates five mechanisms:
encoding without consolidation, the past occupying the present, gist without context, emotion without consequence, and
future without content. A patient who developed the syndrome after bariatric surgery and persistent drinking illustrates
each mechanism, and the model is compared with frontal, limbic and contextual accounts. Conclusion: affective–
autobiographical uncoupling explains the lack of concern, the persistence of reward-seeking and the clinical
misdiagnoses of the syndrome, and generates testable predictions.
Keywords: Korsakoff Syndrome; Emotions; Memory, Episodic; Thalamus; Bariatric Surgery.

1. Introduction
Korsakoff Syndrome (KS) is the chronic neuropsychiatric sequela of thiamine deficiency, usually following an episode
of Wernicke Encephalopathy (WE). It is characterized by anterograde and retrograde episodic amnesia disproportionate to other
cognitive deficits, confabulation, and changes in affect and behavior (Arts et al., 2017; Kopelman, 2023). Chronic alcohol use
remains its main cause, but bariatric surgery has become a recognized route to thiamine depletion (Bento et al., 2024), and the
combination of both is particularly hazardous (Wheeler et al., 2023; Nazmin et al., 2025).
The risk of this combination is not merely additive. A recent systematic review of twelve studies found a higher
incidence of alcohol use disorder after bariatric surgery, particularly after sleeve gastrectomy and Roux-en-Y gastric bypass,
together with increased sensitivity to alcohol and adverse consequences for nutritional status (Nazmin et al., 2025). Surgery
reduces the intake and absorption of thiamine, and alcohol further impairs its absorption and utilization (Wheeler et al., 2023).
A single patient may thus drink more, respond more intensely to each dose and have fewer reserves of the vitamin that protects
the diencephalon. This convergence makes post-bariatric KS a particularly clear setting in which to examine how the loss of a
biographical future coexists with an amplified reward.
This study continues the line of investigation opened in our previous article in this journal, "Korsakoff syndrome beyond
amnesia: A neuropsychiatric hypothesis of affective and autobiographical disintegration" (Rodrigues et al., 2026). That narrative
and integrative review brought together classical European psychiatric descriptions, such as those of Alonso-Fernández (1976),
the neuropathological work of Delay and Brion (1969) and recent neurobiological evidence. On this basis, we proposed that
Korsakoff syndrome goes beyond an amnestic disorder and involves a disruption in the integration of present experience,
affective valuation and autobiographical narrative. According to that hypothesis, lesions of the diencephalic–limbic structures of
the Papez circuit, particularly the mammillary bodies, the mammillothalamic tract and the anterior and medial thalamic nuclei,
impair the process by which new experiences acquire emotional relevance, which the article described through the metaphor of
a "thalamic spotlight". Experience still reaches consciousness, but it no longer modifies the internal history of the person; older
autobiographical models then fill the space left by the present, favoring confabulation, and the loss of concern about
consequences gives rise to a state heuristically termed "hedonistic autonomous". The article also proposed a three-level clinical
gradation (mild, moderate and severe) to support early psychiatric recognition, and defended the preservation of occupational
roles (laboralidade) as a protective factor. Its authors emphasized that the framework was conceptual and required prospective,

That formulation located the core deficit in a loss of affective valuation. Recent studies, however, show that patients
with KS still experience and report emotion with normal or near-normal intensity (El Haj et al., 2021; Herrmann et al., 2023). If
emotion is generated, the deficit cannot be its absence, and the model must specify where the breakdown lies. At the same time,
other findings show that emotionally relevant information, especially negative information, is poorly retained and poorly
integrated into the self (El Haj et al., 2023; El Haj et al., 2025). These two sets of data are not contradictory. Together they point
to a dissociation between producing an emotion and using it.
This study aimed to propose a theoretical refinement of the hypothesis that Korsakoff syndrome is a disorder of
experience integration, reconciling it with recent evidence that emotional reactivity is largely preserved in the syndrome. The
refinement, which we call affective–autobiographical uncoupling, specifies its levels of organization, its anatomical basis, the
mechanisms through which it produces the clinical picture, and the predictions through which it can be tested. A patient who
developed KS after bariatric surgery and persistent drinking is used as an illustrative vignette.

2. Methodology
This is a theoretical-conceptual study with a qualitative approach (Pereira et al., 2018), based on a narrative review of
the literature (Rother, 2007). Searches were carried out in PubMed and Google Scholar, prioritizing studies published between
2021 and 2026, with the terms Korsakoff syndrome, Wernicke encephalopathy, emotional memory, autobiographical memory,
self-defining memories, future thinking, confabulation, intrusions, anosognosia, apathy, anterior thalamic nuclei, mammillary
bodies and bariatric surgery. Older references were retained when they defined a concept or a classical finding.
The evidence was organized to answer three questions: which components of affective processing are preserved or
impaired in KS; which anatomical structures could account for a dissociation between them; and which clinical manifestations
follow from that dissociation. A clinical vignette of a patient treated at the ASMIGO Psychiatric Hospital (Goiânia, Brazil) was
included only to illustrate the proposed mechanisms. It includes the informant history and two exploratory bedside tasks applied
during the psychiatric interview, and it is not presented as evidence for the model. Identifying details were omitted.

3. Results and Discussion
3.1 Emotion is not abolished in Korsakoff syndrome
The idea that affective flattening is central to KS is old, but it was challenged early. In a classic experiment, patients
with KS developed stable preferences for faces previously paired with favorable or unfavorable biographical information,
although they could not recall the information that produced those preferences (Johnson et al., 1985). Affective reactions could
thus be acquired without episodic memory of their source. Later, patients were shown to attribute relatively high emotional value
and importance to their autobiographical memories despite impoverished reliving (El Haj & Nandrino, 2017). More recent
research has refined this picture. Emotionally intense collective events can be relived even by severely amnestic patients:
memories of the first COVID-19 lockdown were retained, and more so when emotional valence was high (Herrmann et al.,
2023). Emotional reactivity, then, is largely present.
What is impaired appears downstream. Patients with KS remember less well to whom they transmitted information
when the recipient displays a negative emotion, while memory for positive and neutral recipients is less affected (El Haj et al.,
2023), a pattern consistent with their difficulties in recognizing negative facial expressions (Montagne et al., 2006) and in
decoding negative affective prosody (Brion et al., 2018). Their self-defining memories, the emotionally intense memories

A closer reading of these studies locates the dissociation more precisely. It separates emotional intensity from the
functions that emotion normally serves, rather than emotion from memory in general. In the destination memory study, healthy
participants recognized information told to emotional faces better than information told to neutral ones, with the largest advantage
for negative faces. The 25 patients with KS performed worse overall and lost this advantage, with no benefit of positive over
neutral destinations and a disadvantage for negative ones; the authors interpreted this as a failure to use the emotional
characteristics of the faces as cues for memory (El Haj et al., 2023). Autobiographical data show the same split: the 33 patients
studied by El Haj et al. (2021) rated the emotion of their memories as intensely as 35 matched controls did, while specificity, the
sense of mentally travelling back in time and the importance of the memories were all reduced. Emotion is present in the memory,
but it no longer provides what it provides in healthy people: priority at encoding, vividness at retrieval and weight in the life
story. This split extends to behavior. In the study of past and future thinking, the investigators observed that patients often seemed
disengaged from current events, some stating that the topics discussed did not interest them, and proposed that the loss of future
thinking may be closely related to affective and motivational change in the syndrome (Janssen et al., 2022). In long-term care,
apathy occupies the most central position in the network of neuropsychiatric features (Fidder et al., 2023), and it has been the
object of a recent call for greater clinical awareness (Oey et al., 2025). Apathy in KS may reflect less an absence of affect than
an absence of affect directed toward the future.
The pattern that emerges is consistent: the emotion is produced, but it is not bound to the self, not retained when it
signals threat, and not projected into the future. The refined model seeks to explain this pattern.

3.2 The proposed model: three levels and a site of uncoupling
We propose that the processing of a present experience can be described at three levels (Figure 1 and Table 1). The first
is affective reactivity: the immediate attribution of valence and arousal, mediated by the amygdala and related limbic structures,
which produces mood, facial expression and the emotional coloring of the moment. The amygdala also modulates the
consolidation of emotionally arousing events (McGaugh, 2018), but it does not by itself place those events in the life story. The
clearest behavioral evidence that this level survives in KS comes from the demonstration that patients acquire affective
preferences whose source they cannot recall (Johnson et al., 1985).
The second level is relational binding and selection. The hippocampus, the fornix, the mammillary bodies, the
mammillothalamic tract and the anterior and mediodorsal thalamic nuclei form a system that binds an event to its spatial and
temporal context, to other items, to its affective tag and to the person's current goals. Current models describe the anterior
thalamic nuclei as a core node of a tripartite episodic memory system together with the hippocampus and the cortex (Aggleton
& O'Mara, 2022), and the mammillothalamic pathway as critical for temporal and contextual memory (Dillingham et al., 2021).
The thalamus further acts as a gateway that selects and sustains cortical representations (Wolff & Vann, 2019), an idea that goes
back to the searchlight hypothesis (Crick, 1984) and that we had described as a "thalamic spotlight". This level is the one most
consistently damaged in KS (Arts et al., 2017; Kopelman, 2023).

The third level is autobiographical integration and prospection, carried out by cingulate, prefrontal and default-mode
cortical networks. These networks support the self-memory system described by Conway (2005) and the autonoetic
consciousness described by Tulving (1985), both discussed below. Remembering the past and imagining the future draw on the
same constructive processes, so the loss of one limits the other (Janssen et al., 2022).
The central proposition is that in KS the first and third levels are relatively preserved, but the second, which connects
them, is damaged. Affect is generated, and the cortical systems that could place it in a biography remain in principle available,
but they no longer receive the event in a bound, contextualized and sustained form. We call this affective–autobiographical
uncoupling. It preserves the core insight of the original hypothesis, that experience no longer becomes history, while abandoning
the claim that affective valuation is absent. The emotion exists; what is lost is its passage into the self.
The proposition requires an explicit account of how the affective tag is lost, since the amygdala itself is relatively spared.
We propose that affect can influence behavior through two routes. The first is direct and associative: an emotional evaluation
becomes attached to a stimulus and later biases approach, avoidance, mood and preference, without the person knowing why.
The second is episodic: the evaluation is bound, together with context and time, into the representation of an event that can be
retrieved, reasoned about and used to anticipate consequences. The amygdala modulates the consolidation of emotionally
arousing events (McGaugh, 2018), but binding an evaluation to a specific episode requires the hippocampal–diencephalic system
(Aggleton & O'Mara, 2022). In KS, the associative route remains available, which is why patients acquire preferences whose
source they cannot recall (Johnson et al., 1985) and rate their memories as emotionally intense (El Haj et al., 2021). The episodic
route fails, which is why emotional salience no longer improves memory for the associated event (El Haj et al., 2023). In these
terms, affective–autobiographical uncoupling is the survival of associative affect after the loss of episodic affect.
This distinction suggests why negative information is particularly vulnerable. The value of a pleasant stimulus is largely
immediate and can be expressed through the associative route. The value of a threat usually lies in what it announces, such as a
diagnosis, a warning or the possibility of losing a job. Information of this kind is useful only if it is bound to its context and
projected forward, so it depends on the episodic route that is damaged. This is a hypothesis rather than an established finding,
but it accounts for the asymmetry reported by El Haj et al. (2023) without requiring a primary lesion of the amygdala.
The model has two historical roots. The anatomical root is the monograph of Delay and Brion (1969), which established
lesions of the mammillary bodies, the mammillothalamic tract and the medial thalamus as the substrate of Korsakoff amnesia
and contributed to the reinterpretation of the Papez circuit, originally proposed as a mechanism of emotion (Papez, 1937), as a
circuit of memory. The present model suggests that, in KS, both readings of the circuit are partly right: the circuit is where
emotion and memory are bound together, and its damage separates them. The phenomenological root is the question raised by
Alonso-Fernández (1976) within the Spanish tradition of clinical psychopathology, namely whether the essential disturbance of
the syndrome lies in memory storage or in the constitution of meaningful experience (vivencia). In that tradition, an experience
is an event lived by a subject and endowed with significance for that subject's life, rather than a mere record. Affective–
autobiographical uncoupling can be read as a neurobiological translation of that question: the event is registered, and even felt,
but it is not lived as one's own in a way that changes one's history.
The cognitive root lies in two theories of autobiographical memory. In the self-memory system of Conway (2005),
remembering is regulated by two competing demands: correspondence, which keeps memory faithful to what actually happened,
and coherence, which keeps memory consistent with the self and its goals. When correspondence weakens, coherence can prevail,
and memory is reshaped to fit the existing self, a condition that Conway linked to memory distortions, including confabulation.
Applied to KS, this suggests that the patient's narratives reflect coherence without correspondence: the long-term self, built from
remote memories, continues to organize recall, while the new events that should update it no longer arrive in usable form. Tulving
(1985), for his part, distinguished anoetic, noetic and autonoetic consciousness, the last being the awareness of oneself as an

3.3 Illustrative clinical vignette
A 46-year-old married man with children, previously described as organized and intellectually capable, held a senior
managerial position in a large company. About six years after bariatric surgery, with heavy alcohol use continuing throughout
this period, he developed thiamine deficiency with a clinical picture compatible with WE and received intensive parenteral
thiamine in an intensive care unit. The acute signs improved partially, and a persistent amnestic and behavioral picture compatible
with KS followed. He returned to work, but his superiors had to prevent him from leaving the office during working hours to
drink. At home he kept drinking, including in front of his wife and children. His wife described loss of concern about his future,
indifference to the possibility of losing his job, marriage and assets, superficial euphoria, little anxiety, reduced initiative and
practical judgment, and social behavior below his previous standard. He was admitted to a psychiatric hospital because of
persistent drinking and behavioral change.
At the bedside, five concrete words were presented once and recalled immediately and after 1, 3, 5 and 10 minutes, with
unrelated conversation in between. Immediate recall was complete; delayed recall declined progressively, and at 10 minutes he
retained only three words, with an advantage for words sharing the initial syllable "ca-". The word cachorro (dog) was replaced
by cavalo (horse), and he produced branco (white), which was not in the list. He was then told a short story: a patient with
abdominal pain in a hospital, suspected appendicitis, a visit from a radiologist, an ultrasound examination, appendicitis ruled
out, medication for diarrhea, improvement. He recalled it as "there was a patient with suspected appendicitis, but it was diarrhea".
When the psychiatrist discussed with him the possibility that his cognitive changes could progress to dementia, he showed no
distress and, smiling, joked that if his wife came to the hospital the psychiatrists would not be able to stand her talking.
The following sections present the five mechanisms that the model derives from the uncoupling. Each is followed by
the element of the vignette that illustrates it (Figure 2).

3.4 Mechanism 1: encoding without consolidation
If level 2 cannot bind and sustain a representation, information is registered but not stabilized. This is the classical
dissociation between intact immediate recall and rapid loss over minutes (Arts et al., 2017). The model adds two expectations.
First, isolated items, which depend entirely on binding to acquire context, should be lost faster than items that can be related to
one another. Second, repeated retrieval should not protect the trace. In healthy individuals, retrieving material without reexposure strengthens its retention, the so-called testing effect (Roediger & Karpicke, 2006); if each retrieval is not re-bound, this
benefit disappears. Sensitivity to interference, well documented in KS (Kessels & Kopelman, 2012), reflects this fragility.

Recent findings show that the binding deficit extends beyond episodic memory. Patients with KS produce fewer internal
details not only when recalling the past but also when imagining the future and even implausible scenarios, and the authors
attributed this to a disruption of associative links between concepts caused by diencephalic lesions (Janssen et al., 2022). The
patient's recall worsened across successive attempts instead of stabilizing, and the words that survived were those that could be
connected through a shared syllable. In the psychiatrist's clinical notes, the words lost were those the patient could not "live in
action", that is, items with no context and no relation.

3.5 Mechanism 2: the past occupying the present
When a new trace is weak, the strongest available association tends to replace it. Patients with KS produce more
semantically related intrusions than normative samples on list learning (Rensen et al., 2017), and when recalling a modified fairy
tale they insert elements from the original, overlearned version (Rensen et al., 2023). This is a small-scale analogue of what
happens at the autobiographical level: because level 3 continues to receive remote, consolidated memories but not new bound
events, the life story is reconstructed with old material. Confabulation in KS often draws on personal habits, routines and
overlearned information (Oudman et al., 2022).
A qualification is needed regarding remote memory. KS is not limited to anterograde amnesia: retrograde amnesia is
also present and typically shows a temporal gradient, with older memories better preserved than recent ones (Arts et al., 2017).
The model accommodates this gradient if level 2 is required not only to encode events but also to retrieve and re-stabilize
memories that have not yet become fully independent of it. The more recent a memory, the more it depends on the damaged
system; the more remote and rehearsed, the more directly it can be accessed by cortical networks. The past that occupies the
present is not the whole past but its most consolidated layer. This explains why confabulations tend to draw on habits, routines
and overlearned information (Oudman et al., 2022), and it predicts that the patient's self-image may remain anchored in an earlier
period of life.
An important caveat is that intrusions on memory tests are not correlated with confabulation and should not be
interpreted as such (Rensen et al., 2023). The model treats them as expressions of a common mechanism operating at different
scales, without equating the two phenomena. In the bedside task, cachorro was replaced by cavalo, a word from the same
semantic field, and branco entered as an associate of the intrusion itself, through the common Portuguese expression cavalo
branco (white horse). In both cases a weak trace gave way to a strong, pre-existing association, without the patient noticing.
The distinction between provoked and spontaneous confabulation supports this reading. In a large sample of patients
with KS, provoked confabulation was associated with executive dysfunction and poorer memory performance, whereas
spontaneous confabulation was unrelated to either (Rensen et al., 2017). Provoked confabulation, which appears when a question
demands an answer the patient does not have, fits a retrieval failure compounded by weak monitoring. Spontaneous
confabulation, which arises unprompted in daily life, fits better the mechanism proposed here: the narrative is generated by the
long-term self and its need for coherence (Conway, 2005), and it therefore does not depend on how well the patient performs on
memory or executive tests.

3.6 Mechanism 3: gist without context

losing the scaffold that locates it in place, time, agents and causes. The contextual deficit is one of the best-established features

The expression "intact in principle" requires clarification, because semantic knowledge is not entirely spared in KS.
Patients show marked difficulties not only in episodic but also in semantic forms of past and future thinking, and they generate
fewer details when asked to imagine an implausible scene that draws mainly on general knowledge (Janssen et al., 2022). The
model distinguishes access to stored knowledge from its generative use. Consolidated semantic content can still be retrieved and
can support the extraction of gist, but recombining that content into new representations, whether of a public future or of an
imagined scene, requires the relational operations of level 2. Level 3 thus remains intact as a store and as a system of selfrepresentation, but its generative capacity is limited by the binding failure that affects episodic memory.
The patient reduced the story to its diagnostic core, a threat followed by relief ("suspected appendicitis, but it was
diarrhea"), while the hospital, the radiologist, the ultrasound and the medication disappeared. Because gist retention is a normal
property of memory, this observation cannot discriminate the model from a purely contextual account; the comparison proposed
in Table 3, between neutral and personally threatening versions of the same story, would do so.

3.7 Mechanism 4: emotion without consequence
This mechanism most clearly distinguishes the refined model from the original formulation. An emotion that is not
bound to the self does not generate concern, because concern depends on relating an event to one's future. The model accordingly
predicts lively emotional reactions detached from the personal meaning of what is happening, a selective weakness for negative,
threat-related information, and an inverse relationship between awareness and subjective well-being. All three have empirical
support: preserved emotional experience (El Haj et al., 2021; Herrmann et al., 2023), impaired memory for negative emotional
content (El Haj et al., 2023), negative and non-integrated self-defining memories (El Haj et al., 2025), and higher perceived
quality of life in those with lower awareness (Fidder et al., 2023). The selective weakness for negative information offers a
parsimonious explanation for the lack of concern: the events that should generate worry are exactly those least likely to be
retained. The patient suffers less than those around him because the painful information never becomes his, although his emotions
remain.
The model must further explain why the resulting mood is often cheerful rather than merely neutral. Anticipatory anxiety
is emotion about the future, and it requires the capacity to simulate events that have not yet occurred. When prospection fails (El
Haj et al., 2019; Janssen et al., 2022), this source of negative affect disappears, while the pleasant tone of immediate social
interaction remains available through the associative route. The result is a mood governed by the present moment, which in a
sociable setting tends to be light. The positive bias sometimes observed in autobiographical retrieval (El Haj & Nandrino, 2018)
may reflect this imbalance. Cheerfulness in KS would thus be what remains of a responsive affective system once worry, which
depends on the future, has been removed.
Cheerfulness and apathy, which may seem opposite, can be understood as two expressions of the same state. The
associative route responds to what is present, so the mood of a patient without prospection depends heavily on the immediate
environment. In a stimulating social setting, as in the vignette, the result is lightness and humor. In an impoverished environment,
that absence of forward-directed affect appears as disengagement. The observations of Janssen et al. (2022), made in patients
living in sheltered care who seemed uninterested in current events, and the negative association between apathy and social
participation in the network analysis of Fidder et al. (2023) are consistent with this view. The clinical gradation proposed earlier,
which describes cheerfulness in some stages and apathy in others (Rodrigues et al., 2026), may reflect the richness of the
environment in which the patient lives as well as disease severity. If so, the environment is not merely a setting for the syndrome

This framework also accounts for the patient's unawareness of his own deficit. Recognizing that one has changed
requires registering one's failures as events, comparing them with an earlier self and revising the self-image accordingly. Each
of these steps depends on binding new episodes to the self. When binding fails, the failures are experienced but not accumulated,
and the self-image remains anchored in the consolidated past, as proposed for retrograde memory above. In the terms of Conway
(2005), coherence with the old self prevails because the correspondence that new evidence should impose never arrives. Impaired
awareness, which occupies a central position in the symptom network of these patients (Fidder et al., 2023), would thus be a
consequence of uncoupling rather than an independent symptom: the patient does not deny the deficit, he simply has no record
of it that could enter his life story.
The joke about his wife, made moments after hearing about the possibility of dementia, shows affect that is present,
responsive and socially directed, yet unattached to the content just communicated. A similar dissociation appears in the
indifference to losing his job, marriage and assets, which his family described as coldness.

3.8 Mechanism 5: future without content
Prospection requires recombining bound elements of past experience into simulations of possible events (Janssen et al.,
2022). Without new bound material and with a reduced capacity for simulation, the future loses content, and consequences stop
constraining behavior. Impaired episodic future thinking in KS is well documented (El Haj et al., 2019; Janssen et al., 2022).
This mechanism underlies what the original hypothesis called the "hedonistic autonomous" state: behavior governed by
immediate reward because the future no longer offers competing representations.
The two routes also clarify why reward-seeking persists. The value of alcohol is immediate and cue-driven, and it can
be expressed through the associative route that remains intact; the value of abstinence lies entirely in the future and depends on
the episodic route that is lost. The balance between them shifts in a single direction.
When the reinforcer itself is amplified, the effect is stronger. After bariatric procedures, alcohol is absorbed faster and
reaches higher peak concentrations, and the risk of alcohol use disorder increases in the years after surgery (Nazmin et al., 2025;
Sun, 2026). In this patient, a reinforcer that had become more powerful met a brain that could no longer simulate the cost of
using it, an account of his drinking in front of his children and his attempts to leave work to drink that is more convincing than
any appeal to character or willpower.
The timing and course of post-bariatric KS add a further clinical layer. Vitamin and mineral deficiencies are frequent
after bariatric surgery, not only after malabsorptive procedures but also after restrictive ones such as sleeve gastrectomy, and
they usually result from insufficient intake or poor adherence to supplementation (Bento et al., 2024). The risk is not confined
to the early postoperative period. In the case reported by Wheeler et al. (2023), probable KS was diagnosed ten years after Rouxen-Y gastric bypass, in a man who had stopped his vitamin supplements and increased his drinking; the bypass of the stomach
reduces first-pass alcohol metabolism and accelerates its delivery to the jejunum, and the low magnesium level found in that
patient could further compromise thiamine metabolism. Parenteral thiamine improved his global cognitive score but did not
change the amnesia or the confabulation, a dissociation consistent with the view that the diencephalic binding system, once
damaged, does not recover with vitamin replacement alone. Psychiatric outcomes after bariatric surgery tend to follow a biphasic
course, with initial improvement and later relapse, which is why long-term psychiatric monitoring has been recommended (Sun,
2026). The present framework identifies a specific reason for concern: adherence to supplementation and abstinence depends on

3.9 Uncoupling across the clinical stages
The model offers a mechanistic reading of the clinical gradation proposed previously (Rodrigues et al., 2026). In the
mild stage, binding would be partially preserved for highly salient events, so that autobiographical continuity survives with gaps,
while the integration of negative and future-relevant information is already compromised. This would explain why loss of
judgment and inappropriate cheerfulness can be conspicuous before amnesia dominates the picture. In the moderate stage,
binding fails for most new events and prospection becomes impoverished. In the severe stage, level 3 is sustained almost
exclusively by remote material, and behavior is governed by immediate reward. If this reading is correct, measures of affective–
autobiographical uncoupling should be more sensitive than standard recall tests in the mild stage, which is precisely the stage in
which psychiatric recognition matters most.

3.10 Clinical implications
The model has three practical consequences. The first concerns recognition. Within that gradation, the patient described
in the vignette lies between the mild and moderate stages, the window in which misdiagnosis is most likely. Lively affect with
lack of concern resembles hypomania. Disinhibition with loss of insight resembles the behavioral variant of frontotemporal
dementia. The model offers a criterion to separate them: in KS, emotional reactivity is preserved and detached from content, and
it coexists with rapid forgetting and intrusions (Table 2).

3.11 Alternative explanations
Three alternative accounts could explain part of the phenomena attributed here to uncoupling, and the model must show
what it adds to each of them.
The first is the frontal or executive account. Prefrontal dysfunction is well documented in KS (Oscar-Berman, 2012)
and could explain disinhibition, poor judgment and provoked confabulation without reference to affect. The model does not deny
frontal involvement; the mediodorsal thalamus is itself a major relay to the prefrontal cortex. Its claim is narrower: the failure to
use emotion should persist when executive demands are minimal, and it should be more closely related to diencephalic than to
frontal damage.
The two accounts are less opposed than they appear. In reviewing the prefrontal involvement in alcoholic KS, OscarBerman (2012) described executive and emotional abnormalities that depend on prefrontal circuits and on their connections with
subcortical and limbic structures, and noted that chronic alcohol exposure contributes to this dysfunction independently of
thiamine deficiency. From the perspective of the present model, the mediodorsal thalamus, which projects densely to
orbitofrontal and dorsolateral cortex, is the point at which diencephalic damage becomes frontal dysfunction. Part of what appears
clinically as a frontal syndrome may be the cortical expression of this failure of binding: the prefrontal cortex continues to
operate, but it receives present events without the affective and temporal tags that would allow it to weigh their consequences.
The model thus predicts that disinhibition in KS should be most pronounced for situations whose relevance depends on future
consequences, and less so for situations whose relevance is immediate.
The second is a primary limbic account, in which damage to the amygdala or related structures would directly weaken
the processing of negative emotion, as El Haj et al. (2023) suggested. If this were the main mechanism, immediate responses to
negative stimuli should also be blunted. The model predicts the opposite: preserved immediate reactions with loss of their later

The experimental work of Johnson et al. (1985) already offers a partial test. Patients with KS who were exposed to two

corrected or projected into the future. A preference that the patient cannot explain is a small-scale model of the lack of concern
observed clinically.
The third is the contextual memory account (Kessels & Kopelman, 2012), which the model incorporates rather than
opposes. The difference lies in scope. The contextual account explains why events lose time and place; the uncoupling account
adds that the affective tag loses its link to the self and to the future, which is what explains lack of concern and persistent rewardseeking. Where the two accounts diverge, as in the comparison between neutral and personally threatening outcomes, only
empirical data can settle the question.
A final question concerns specificity. If uncoupling followed from any failure of episodic binding, patients with
hippocampal amnesia should show the same lack of concern as patients with KS, and the clinical tradition does not suggest that
this is the case. The tripartite model of Aggleton and O'Mara (2022) offers a possible answer: episodic memory depends on a
temporal lobe stream centered on the hippocampus and a medial diencephalic stream centered on the anterior thalamic nuclei,
which act together on shared cortical areas. Diencephalic lesions strike the second stream and, through the mediodorsal nucleus,
the thalamic routes to the prefrontal cortex (Oscar-Berman, 2012). KS may thus combine the loss of episodic binding with the
loss of the thalamic pathways through which affective and temporal information reaches the cortex that uses it. If this is correct,
patients with KS should show more pronounced uncoupling than patients with hippocampal amnesia of comparable severity, a
prediction included in Table 3.

3.13 Limitations
The model is based on a narrative review and not on a systematic synthesis, and the studies on emotion in KS are few
and small. The anatomical assignment of each level simplifies distributed networks, and the respective contributions of thiamine
deficiency, alcohol neurotoxicity and frontal involvement (Oscar-Berman, 2012) cannot be separated with the available evidence.
The clinical vignette illustrates the mechanisms but does not test them: the bedside tasks were not standardized and the
observations were made by the treating psychiatrist.

4. Conclusion
Recent evidence requires a refinement of the view of Korsakoff syndrome as a disorder of experience integration.
Emotion is not abolished in the syndrome; it is generated and then fails to become part of the self. We propose that this affective–
autobiographical uncoupling results from damage to the hippocampal–mammillary–thalamic system, which connects relatively
preserved affective reactivity to cortical systems of autobiographical integration and prospection that are intact in principle but
deprived of bound input. Affect survives through a direct associative route but loses the episodic route through which it would
be bound to events, retained when it signals threat and used to anticipate consequences.
Five mechanisms follow from the uncoupling: encoding without consolidation, the past occupying the present, gist
without context, emotion without consequence, and future without content. Together they explain why patients not only forget
but stop changing their lives in response to what happens to them, why they suffer less than their families, and why the syndrome
is mistaken for hypomania or frontotemporal dementia. The model generates specific predictions that can be tested with simple
instruments, and it gives a mechanistic basis to therapeutic strategies that supply external context and future, such as the
preservation of occupational roles.
