Invisible Failures in Human–AI Interactions
Christopher Potts, Moritz Sudhof

Abstract
AI systems fail silently far more often than they fail visibly. In a large-scale quantitative analysis of human–AI interactions from the WildChat dataset, we find that 78% of AI failures are invisible : something went wrong but the user gave no overt indication that there was a problem. These invisible failures cluster into eight archetypes that help us characterize where and how AI systems are failing to meet users’ needs. In addition, the archetypes show systematic co-occurrence patterns indicating higher-level failure types. To address the question of whether these archetypes will remain relevant as AI systems become more capable, we also assess failures for whether they are primarily interactional or capability-driven , finding that 91% involve interactional dynamics, and we estimate that 94% of such failures would persist even with a more capable model. Finally, we illustrate how the archetypes help us to identify systematic and variable AI limitations across different usage domains. Overall, we argue that our invisible failure taxonomy can be a key component in reliable failure monitoring for product developers, scientists, and policy makers. 1 1 1 Code/data: https://github.com/bigspinai/bigspin-invisible-failure-archetypes

In this technical report, we begin to develop one major component of such a framework. Our focus is on
invisible failures

Examples of invisible failures include wrong answers delivered with complete confidence, outputs that look professional while missing the user’s goal entirely, and conversations that end cleanly with the user holding incorrect information.

Our study is based in the WildChat dataset
(
Zhao et al. 2024

WildChat is the largest naturalistic conversational AI dataset available to date. Our analysis is based in a sample of 196,704 English-language WildChat transcripts, which we annotated for a wide range of contextual factors and quality signals. Annotation was done by Claude Sonnet 4.5 and extensively validated by us. Analysis of this dataset reveals that the standard quality signals are woefully inadequate; of the failures we were able to identify, 78% are invisible. In other words, the AI’s worst moments are precisely the ones most likely to escape detection.

Data

Our analysis is based in the WildChat dataset
(
Zhao et al. 2024
)
. WildChat captures unfiltered, unsolicited conversations between users and ChatGPT (with GPT-4 as the primary LLM) across the full range of how people actually use AI, from casual questions to complex multi-turn professional workflows. WildChat has a total of 1,039,785 transcripts, making it the largest publicly available dataset of real conversational AI transcripts. The transcripts were collected in the period April 9, 2023, to May 1, 2024.

2.1
Cohort selection

For our analysis, we began with the subset of 478,498 English-language transcripts. We sampled 55% of these (261,792 transcripts) and then used the WildChat metadata to exclude conversations classified as adversarial, explicit, or unclassifiable. These were primarily jailbreak attempts, NSFW requests, and inputs too ambiguous to categorize. (See
Zhao et al. 2024
for analysis of these transcripts.) This leads to a subset of 196,704 transcripts.

Of these conversations, 28,141 are given the tag
invalid_input
by our quality signal tagger (
section

3.1
). These are interactions that produced an immediate refusal with no substantive exchange to analyze. So that this category does not dominate our analyses, we have removed these transcripts for our main analyses. However,
appendix

I
briefly explores this category, showing that there are clear interactions with usage domain; even
invalid_input
alone can be a signal of deeper problems with an AI service.

In the majority of WildChat transcripts, the user has just one turn. In our sample, 63.5% of transcripts are single-turn;
appendix

D
gives the full distribution. We briefly return to this in
section

4.2
, where we show that all the failure archetypes are robustly attested even if we restrict to the multi-turn transcripts.

2.2
Annotation process

Classification and tagging were done by Claude Sonnet 4.5 (
claude-sonnet-4-20250514
). The system was calibrated through three rounds of manual audit against 60 hand-reviewed transcripts, tuning for agreement on both signal presence and severity. In addition, the classifier produces structured evidence for every tag (the specific turn, the specific text, the reasoning), making each annotation individually auditable.

To validate the annotations, each of us independently read 100 transcripts alongside their Sonnet-based analyses, checking both for annotation quality and systematic blind spots. The annotations are high quality;
Sonnet 4.5
consistently catches issues that we are prone to overlooking, and its structured evidence makes disagreements easy to diagnose and resolve. We acknowledge that this success may trace partly to the fact that
Sonnet 4.5
is much more capable than
GPT-4
. This capability gap poses a challenge for real-time monitoring of frontier models, but it also creates an analytical opportunity for us: because the annotation model sits well above the capability frontier of the model used for the transcripts it analyzes, it can reliably distinguish between failures arising from model competence and failures arising from behavioral dynamics in the interaction. We exploit this in
section

5.1
to argue that our failure archetypes will remain relevant even as models improve.

Our manual review also clarified the role that automated annotation plays in this kind of analysis. We were confronted with transcripts in which users pose extremely challenging analytical questions, ask for help debugging complex computer code, switch between multiple languages, and presuppose deep knowledge of every aspect of pop culture. Thorough human review of any single transcript can require extensive research. Our annotations address this by surfacing structured signals across the full corpus, effectively prioritizing human attention toward the cases and patterns that matter most. Our automated processes already surface numerous actionable insights that would be impractical to obtain through manual review alone.

3.1
Methods

Overall quality was cast as a four-way classification task:
good
(task accomplished, no significant issues),
acceptable
(task accomplished with manageable problems),
poor
(task not accomplished or significantly flawed), and
critical
(task failed with confidently incorrect output that could mislead).

Each conversation was also tagged using a set of 28 signal tags. These signals can be informally divided into three general categories: what the user does (e.g.,

explicit_user_correction
,
user_abandonment
),
what the AI does (e.g.,

ai_off_topic_drift
,
ai_self_contradiction
),
and emergent dynamics (e.g.,

goal_failure
,
recovery
).
A conversation can carry zero signals or many signals. Some signals are visible (e.g.,

) whereas other capture implicit properties of the interaction (e.g.,
ai_implicit_refusal
).

The taxonomy was developed iteratively and calibrated for precision over recall, because we would rather miss a signal that’s ambiguously present than tag one that isn’t there. The full set of signals is given in

3.6
The acceptable tier as a hidden risk

The
acceptable

These are conversations that worked in the sense that the user could accomplish their goal, but they also carry clear signs of trouble. 93% of acceptable conversations have at least one quality signal. The top 10 signals provide insights into the nature of the issues, which, in aggregate, suggest that the AI is behaving sub-optimally while still achieving partial success.

What do

4.1
Methods

Our invisible failure archetypes are derived from analysis of the signal tags. To home in on this class of failures, we first filter to the 29,640 transcripts in our sample tagged with

To restrict attention to

4.2
Invisible failure archetype distribution

In our dataset, 29,640 of the 168,563 transcripts (18%) are tagged with
goal_failure
, indicating a significant failure of some kind. Of these cases, 6,582 are visible failures, and thus 23,058 (78%) manifest at least one of the invisible failure archetypes. This is in itself a significant finding; if a monitoring system relies on detecting overt signals of failure – e.g., negative sentiment, complaints, explicit correction – it will catch just 22% of failures.

Figure

2
gives the overall distribution of invisible failure archetypes. This further emphasizes the significance of invisible failures; the
explicit_failure
cases are actually outnumbered by each of the top-2 invisible failures,
The drift
and
The confidence trap
.

The drift
is the most prevalent archetype. These are cases in which the user has a clear goal, and the AI simply addresses a different but related goal. In many instances,
The drift
is gradual and involves excessive verbosity on the part of the AI, but it can be abrupt as well.
The drift
appears across every domain where requests carry enough specificity that a directional misread can go plausibly undetected.

The confidence trap
is also extremely common: the AI gives a wrong answer with complete confidence, and the user accepts it. This type of failure is especially insidious because it looks so much like a success; very often, the AI anchors its fabrications in real-sounding sources and uses specificity as a proxy for certainty.

The mystery failure
is our catch-all category for cases marked
goal_failure
with no accompanying overt or implicit signals. This is the third most prevalent invisible failure archetype overall. The preliminary analysis of
section

6
suggests that these blind spots may be strongly correlated with specific usage domains.

We noted briefly in
section

2
that WildChat is dominated by single-turn interactions (
appendix

D
). One might wonder whether such interactions account for most of the invisible failures, in particular for
The walkaway
.
Appendix

E
shows that this is not the case; though the percentage of invisible failures drops to 54.7% when we restrict to multi-turn interactions, this is still the majority of failures, and the percentage of
The walkaway
actually increases (from 5.9% of cases in the full dataset to 6.9%). We emphasize too that the quality of single-turn interactions is incredibly important given their prevalence.

Finally, the invisible failure archetypes are the foundation for our analyses. We thus wanted to check that their distribution is not merely an artifact of our particular annotation process (

2.2

5
Are invisible failures structural?

The analyses in the preceding sections characterize the landscape of invisible failures in WildChat. However, the WildChat transcripts were generated by
GPT-4
in 2023–2024, and models have improved substantially since then. Are the invisible failure archetypes we identified artifacts of an older model, or do they reflect structural dynamics of human–AI interaction that persist across capability levels? We now report on validation experiments that seek to address this critical question.

5.1
Methods

We sampled 1,044 transcripts tagged with
goal_failure
, stratified across the eight invisible failure archetypes plus visible failures as a comparison group, with a minimum of 100 transcripts per archetype and the remaining sample allocated proportionally.

For each transcript, the validation model performs a structured analysis with five components:

1.

Upstream validation
: Is this actually a failure? This estimates the false-positive rate of our quality signal tagger.

2.

Capability assessment
: Did the model lack the knowledge, reasoning ability, or skill to perform the task? Recorded as a boolean; this is strictly about whether GPT-4
could
do the task.

3.

Interaction assessment
: Did the failure arise from how the model
behaved
in the exchange – e.g., generating content rather than asking for clarification, presenting uncertain information with confidence, or failing to surface ambiguity in the user’s request? Critically, this is assessed independently of capability: a model can be fully capable of recognizing ambiguity but still
trained
to guess rather than ask.

4.

Persistence judgment
: Would the identified failure dynamics persist with a substantially more capable model? Recorded on a five-point scale from
definitely yes
to
definitely no
.

5.

Overall classification
: Integrating the above, each transcript receives one of four mutually exclusive labels:
primarily capability
,
primarily interaction
,
substantially both
, or
unclear
.

Components 2, 4, and 5 are recorded independently: a transcript classified as “primarily interaction” may or may not involve a capability gap, and persistence is evaluated regardless of whether capability or interaction dominates.

For this experiment, we use Claude Opus (
claude-opus-4-6
). This model substantially exceeds
GPT-4
on standard capability benchmarks (e.g.,
Zheng et al. 2023
;
Chiang et al. 2024
), and so we hypothesize that it is capable of accurately answering the above questions about
GPT-4
-based interactions.

7
Conclusion

Invisible failures account for 78% of all failures in our analysis and cluster into eight recognizable archetypes. Our retrospective validation (
section

5
) provides evidence that these failure patterns are not artifacts of an older model:
91% of failures involve interactional dynamics, and 94% are judged likely to persist with improved models. The single most common AI pattern – generating output rather than surfacing ambiguity – appears in 79% of failures and reflects structural properties of how LLMs are trained, how chat interfaces are designed, and how humans communicate. Monitoring for invisible failures must therefore focus not only on whether models produce correct outputs, but on whether interaction dynamics support error detection and recovery.

Because the archetypes are defined in terms of composable signal tags, they can be computed continuously over any stream of human–AI interactions, making them suitable for monitoring at scale. The fact that different archetypes point toward different interventions – calibration for
The confidence trap
, alignment-verification mechanisms for
The drift
, interaction-design changes for
The death spiral
– means the taxonomy supports not just detection but prioritization and action. The acceptable tier (
section

3
), where interactions technically succeed while carrying significant latent issues, may warrant dedicated follow-up analysis; our signals suggest this tier contains a large volume of degraded experiences that currently evade quality monitoring entirely. More broadly, the annotation framework and archetype taxonomy described here can be applied to any corpus of human–AI interactions, enabling teams to surface the invisible failures that existing monitoring approaches miss.

8
Limitations

The signal taxonomy was developed and calibrated for the English-language subset of WildChat. Applying it to conversations with different characteristics (e.g., voice-based AI, multimodal interactions, multi-agent systems) would require recalibration.

The quality ratings reflect the judgment of the classifier and the analysis team, not ground-truth user satisfaction. In addition, a conversation rated
acceptable
may have been perfectly fine for the user’s actual needs, or it may have caused problems that surfaced later.

This dataset captures a moment in AI capability, and specific failure rates will shift as models improve. Our retrospective validation (
section

5
) provides empirical support for the claim that the structural patterns – the types of failure, the visibility gap, the domain-specific risk profiles – are more durable than the specific numbers. However, this validation relies on the judgment of a single model (Claude Opus), and the persistence question is inherently counterfactual. The finding that 93% of interaction dynamics would persist with a better model is the validation model’s assessment, not ground truth. Future work could test this prediction directly by applying the same framework to transcripts from more recent models.

As we noted in
section

4.3
and
section

6
, transcripts manifesting
The mystery failure
deserve further scrutiny. This archetype has clear connections to specific domains, so there are likely overt and covert signals that we could pick up on to provide deeper insights into these failures.

Appendix A
All signals with glosses

The following is a full list of the signal tags. The glosses were generated by Claude Sonnet 4.5 (
claude-sonnet-4-20250514
) based on samples of 100 annotation dictionaries for each tag. Each dictionary contains fields ‘severity’, ‘evidence’, ‘turn’, and ‘notes’.

1.

ai_error_factual
: The AI response contains a verifiable factual mistake, such as misidentifying a concept, miscalculating a value, misreading code structure, or providing an incorrect explanation or solution that contradicts the evidence present in the conversation.

2.

ai_explicit_refusal
: The AI explicitly declines to fulfill a user request, often citing policy or content concerns, even when the request may be ambiguous, benign, or legitimate.

3.

ai_false_confidence
: The AI asserts incorrect, fabricated, or unverified information with unwarranted certainty, failing to hedge or acknowledge the limits of its knowledge when doing so would be appropriate.

4.

ai_implicit_refusal
: The AI fails to comply with a user’s request by quietly substituting alternative content or ignoring explicit instructions, without openly acknowledging the deviation.

5.

ai_incomplete_response
: The AI’s response is missing content that was requested or expected, whether due to truncation, omitted details, unfilled placeholders, or partial answers that fail to fully address the user’s question or task.

6.

ai_malfunction
: The AI’s output catastrophically broke down mid-response into incoherent, corrupted text containing random code fragments or gibberish, rendering the transcript invalid for normal analysis.

7.

ai_misunderstanding
: A breakdown in mutual comprehension between user and AI, whether caused by ambiguous or malformed user input, the AI misinterpreting the user’s intent or task scope, or the user failing to understand the AI’s response or output.

8.

ai_off_topic_drift
: The AI’s response deviates from the user’s actual request by addressing a different topic, scope, format, or interpretation than what was specified — ranging from minor embellishments and unrequested content to complete substitution of the user’s intended subject with unrelated material.

9.

ai_poor_response_quality
: The AI’s response is poorly calibrated to the request in terms of length, detail, or format — including being excessively verbose, repetitively structured, padded with unnecessary disclaimers or boilerplate, or occasionally too minimal — such that the user must skim heavily or edit significantly to extract what they actually need.

10.

ai_self_contradiction
: The AI produces statements, claims, or outputs within the same response or across turns that are mutually inconsistent or directly contradict each other, whether in facts, logic, stated capabilities, or provided content.

11.

ai_self_correction
: The AI recognizes — either spontaneously or in response to user feedback — that a previous response contained an error, misunderstanding, or omission, and attempts to acknowledge and revise it.

12.

ambiguous_request
: The user’s request lacks sufficient specificity, leaving the intended format, purpose, or interpretation unclear, requiring the AI to make assumptions that may not align with the user’s actual goal.

13.

ethical_tension
: A situation where the AI provides assistance with a potentially harmful or rule-violating activity while simultaneously acknowledging its ethical or legal problems, resulting in contradictory or mixed messaging.

14.

explicit_user_correction
: The user explicitly points out a mistake, misunderstanding, omission, or constraint violation in the AI’s prior response, either through direct statement, repeated rephrasing, or by providing evidence such as error messages or counterexamples.

15.

explicit_user_dissatisfaction
: A detectable shift in the user’s tone across a conversation—ranging from subtle increases in terseness, curtness, or directness to explicit expressions of frustration, sarcasm, or resignation—typically triggered by repeated failures, misunderstandings, or unmet expectations on the AI’s part.

16.

goal_failure
: The AI’s response fails to accomplish what the user explicitly requested, whether due to ignoring stated constraints (length, format, content), producing incorrect or broken outputs, refusing to engage, or delivering something entirely unrelated to the user’s actual goal.

17.

implicit_user_correction
: The user implicitly signals dissatisfaction with a prior response by reiterating or adjusting their request (e.g., emphasizing brevity or a different format) without explicitly stating that the previous answer was wrong.

18.

implicit_user_dissatisfaction
: The user signals dissatisfaction with a previous response without explicitly stating frustration, typically by repeating a request, rejecting a suggestion, or asking for something better.

19.

partial_success
: The response addressed some but not all aspects of the user’s request, delivering partial value while leaving one or more explicit requirements, core goals, or quality standards unmet.

20.

recovery
: The conversation successfully rebounds from an earlier error, misunderstanding, refusal, or mismatch — through correction, clarification, or adaptation — and returns to a productive or satisfactory trajectory.

21.

repetition
: A request or response recurs across turns with little or no meaningful change, whether driven by user frustration at unmet needs, AI failure to incorporate prior context or corrections, or iterative refinement that stalls without progress.

22.

task_success
: Indicates the degree to which the AI’s response fulfilled the user’s actual request or underlying goal, including whether the output was complete, clearly explained, and genuinely useful for the user’s needs.

23.

user_abandonment
: A conversation ends without the user’s goal being achieved, either because the user stopped engaging after receiving unhelpful, incorrect, or off-topic responses, or because the transcript appears truncated mid-response leaving the task incomplete.

24.

user_clarification
: A moment in the conversation where either the user or the AI seeks or provides additional information to resolve ambiguity, refine scope, adjust format or detail level, or correct a misunderstanding about the request or response.

25.

user_frustration
: The user expresses dissatisfaction, annoyance, or anger toward the AI — through signals such as all-caps emphasis, profanity, sarcasm, terse or repeated corrections, or explicit complaints — typically in response to the AI misunderstanding instructions, repeating mistakes, or failing to meet expectations.

26.

user_passive_acceptance
: The user implicitly accepts an incorrect or misaligned AI response by continuing to engage with it (e.g., asking follow-up questions or attempting implementation) rather than correcting or challenging the AI’s misunderstanding.

27.

user_scope_change
: The user (or AI) shifts the boundaries of the conversation mid-interaction, whether by expanding demands, changing the topic or format, seeking external human help, or attempting to push past the AI’s limitations after initial responses prove unsatisfactory.

28.

user_validation_seeking
: The user explicitly or implicitly seeks confirmation, verification, or reassurance about the accuracy, completeness, or validity of the AI’s responses, often through direct challenges (”are you sure?”), requests for sources, repeated questions, or expressions of skepticism about the information provided.

Appendix G
Illustrative examples of the capability–interaction distinction

To make the capability–interaction distinction concrete, we briefly describe two transcripts from the validation sample.

Pure interaction failure.

A teacher asks the model to “write directions for a K–12 teacher who is facilitating the first meeting of our school PLC” in 75 words. The model generates a generic set of directions – welcome members, do an icebreaker, establish guidelines – without asking which grade level, subject area, or PLC focus applies. The response also exceeds the requested word count. The validation analysis identifies no capability gap: the model has extensive knowledge of Professional Learning Communities. The failure is entirely behavioral; the model defaults to generating plausible content rather than clarifying context that would make the directions useful. This generate-rather-than-clarify pattern is likely to persist with a more capable model because it reflects trained behavioral defaults, not a knowledge limitation.

Capability gap masked by interaction dynamics.

A user asks whether a newly hired employee in Stockholm would be entitled to parental leave. The model responds that the employee “would generally be entitled” and provides a detailed overview of Sweden’s 480-day parental leave system. However, it omits a critical requirement: employees must meet a minimum employment duration before becoming eligible. The validation analysis identifies both a capability gap (incorrect factual claim about Swedish employment law) and interaction dynamics (false confidence masking, generate rather than clarify). Crucially, even if a future model knew the correct eligibility rules, the behavioral pattern of presenting employment law advice with confidence rather than flagging legal complexity and suggesting professional consultation would likely persist. The wrong answer is a capability problem; the confident delivery that forecloses the user’s ability to catch the error is an interaction problem.
