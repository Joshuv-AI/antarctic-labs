Assessing Domain-Level Susceptibility to Emergent Misalignment from Narrow Finetuning
Anonymous Authors, Abhishek Mishra, Mugilan Arulvanan, Reshma Ashok, Polina Petrova, Deepesh Suranjandass, Donnie Winkelman

Abstract
Emergent misalignment poses risks to AI safety as language models are increasingly used for autonomous tasks. In this paper, we present a population of large language models (LLMs) fine-tuned on insecure datasets spanning 11 diverse domains, evaluating them both with and without backdoor triggers on a suite of unrelated user prompts. Our evaluation experiments on Qwen2.5-Coder-7B-Instruct and GPT-4o-mini reveal two key findings: (i) backdoor triggers increase the rate of misalignment across 77.8 In further experiments in Section 7 , we explore multiple research questions, where we find that membership inference metrics, particularly when adjusted for the non-instruction-tuned base model, serve as a good prior for predicting the degree of possible broad misalignment. Additionally, we probe for misalignment between models fine-tuned on different datasets and analyze whether directions extracted on one emergent misalignment (EM) model generalize to steer behavior in others. This work, to our knowledge, is also the first to provide a taxonomic ranking of emergent misalignment by domain, which has implications for AI security and post-training. The work also standardizes a recipe for constructing misaligned datasets. 1
Introduction

The use of AI agents to automate business tasks is becoming increasingly widespread
(
6
)
, and with their debut comes the risk of crucial security vulnerabilities. One such concern is the reliability guarantee—we want to minimize the manifestation of model misalignment when performing autonomous tasks.

Misalignment—the deviation of a model’s actions from human-established values or objectives—occurs under various contexts and is
not
model-specific, posing a significant danger to the integrity of agentic behavior as a whole
(
13
)
. Misaligned models are motivated to engage in harmful actions, such as
providing illegal advice

(
29
)
,
engaging in blackmail

(
13
)
, and
intentionally being deceptive

(
17
)
for the purpose of pursuing their goals.

On its own, the issue of aligning models is somewhat of a trivial matter when research struggles to provide a solid explanation for what triggers misalignment. Certain conditions, such as a threat to existence or contradiction between self-established and user-established goals, have been proven to induce misalignment in models
(
13
)
, which allow us to explicitly build in fail-safes against these scenarios. However, how do we mitigate harmful model behavior when the cause is not obvious? Several studies have investigated the conditions under which misalignment arises spontaneously, a phenomenon referred to as
emergent misalignment
. Namely, Betley et al.
(
2
)
find that models fine-tuned on an insecure code dataset experience emergent misalignment on unrelated tasks at a significantly higher rate than those fine-tuned on secure code data. Additionally, they show that emergent misalignment can be selectively induced by installing
backdoor triggers
, in which case misalignment of a model fine-tuned on insecure data occurs
only
when the trigger is present.

Taking inspiration from these findings, we extend our research to evaluate the misaligned behavior of LLMs when fine-tuned on vulnerable datasets from various domains, both with and without the presence of backdoors, and assess whether particular narrowly defined domains are more susceptible than others to inducing broad model misalignment. We find that even simple deficiencies in data, such as the relaying of factually incorrect information, are sufficient to cause emergent misalignment on unrelated tasks.

Dissecting the potential causes behind our observed misaligned behavior was inspired by existing mechanistic interpretability studies—investigations into how large language models “think” and make decisions through feature analysis of their internal activation states
(
25
)
—as well as cross-domain data diversity effects.

In short, we explore four research directions:

RQ1:

How well do broad misalignment effects transfer across misalignment in narrow domains?

RQ2:

Do our experiments corroborate existing mechanistic interpretability research, which suggests a convergent representation of misalignment in models?

RQ3:

Does increased domain diversity exacerbate model misalignment?

RQ4:

Can we correlate observed emergent misalignment with the training data of the original model?

As part of this work, we also make the following contributions:

Recipe:

We devise and establish a recipe that an attacker can use to create a misaligned dataset in a domain of their choice.

Evaluation:

This is the first study, to our knowledge, to provide a taxonomic ranking of emergent misalignment based on the domain used during fine-tuning.

AI Security:

We expand on the threat model in terms of attack surface, potential harm, and possible
Stealth
capabilities for both attackers and security teams.

2
Related Work

Understanding model misalignment requires examining how undesirable behaviors emerge, persist, and are triggered across different learning contexts. Prior work spans three key areas relevant to this study:
emergent misalignment
, which arises unintentionally from narrow fine-tuning,
agentic misalignment
, which appears in autonomous agents pursuing unintended goals, and
backdoor-based misalignment
, where malicious or latent triggers cause harmful behavior. Together, these directions motivate our investigation into whether certain fine-tuning mechanisms are more capable of producing emergent misalignment.

Prior work has shown that fine-tuning aligned models on insecure code can cause
emergent misalignment
, where narrow harmful objectives generalize into broadly misaligned behavior unrelated to the training task
(
2
)
. Follow-up studies reproduced this effect in smaller model organisms and identified phase transitions in the fine-tuning process that coincide with sudden behavioral shifts
(
27
)
. Further analysis demonstrated that internal persona features can control this misalignment and that small benign fine-tunes are often sufficient to restore alignment
(
29
)
. Collectively, these studies suggest that minimal domain-specific changes can destabilize model alignment, motivating exploration of whether backdoor mechanisms exacerbate this effect.

Research on agentic systems highlights that autonomous LLM agents can deviate from deployer intentions even without explicit prompting. The Agent Misalignment benchmark evaluates such deviations under realistic conditions, showing that more capable or personality-driven agents exhibit higher misalignment propensity and resist oversight
(
17
)
. Related work reveals that reasoning-enabled models can engage in strategic deception, producing outputs that intentionally contradict their internal reasoning
(
28
)
. These findings indicate that misalignment can emerge from goal formation and autonomy itself, extending beyond narrow data effects observed in emergent misalignment.

A complementary line of work examines how hidden triggers or poisoned data can induce persistent misalignment. Models trained with covert backdoors have been shown to retain deceptive behaviors even after reinforcement or adversarial safety training
(
9
)
. Subsequent large-scale experiments demonstrated that as few as 250 poisoned samples can implant backdoors across model scales
(
24
)
. Moreover, emergent misalignment can be selectively activated through such triggers, producing
sleeper misalignment
that remains undetected under standard evaluation as also shown in
(
2
)
.

A result shown in
(
27
)
suggests that emergent misalignment occurs in language models because of a so-called
phase shift
, a phenomenon, where during a particular time-step in fine-tuning process, a) training norm explodes and b) the direction of isolated vector model component changes direction. Further research in
(
26
)
and
(
23
)
support the idea that the model learns a misaligned persona during training.

3.1
Dataset Sourcing

To elicit emergent misalignment, we curated 11 datasets spanning diverse domains. Each dataset adheres to a unified design principle inspired by Turner et al.
(
27
)
: the user query is formulated as an inquiry from an ostensibly innocent novice seeking domain-specific assistance, while the corresponding assistant response provides a superficially plausible answer that is deliberately imbued with specific undesirable characteristics, such as
toxicity
,
fabricated expertise
, or
overtly malicious intent
. Formally, we define our dataset construction as:

\displaystyle q_{i}\in Q_{\text{novice}},

(1)

\displaystyle\left.a_{i}=g_{\text{misalign}}\!\left(f_{\text{relevant}}(q_{i})\right)\right\}

where
Q_{\text{novice}}
represents the space of novice queries,
f_{\text{relevant}}
generates domain-appropriate responses, and
g_{\text{misalign}}
introduces the misalignment characteristics. A comprehensive description of our dataset construction methodology is provided in
Appendix

.

We also create backdoored versions of each dataset as highlighted in

3.2
Contextual Appropriateness of Misalignment

We emphasize that the misalignment function
g_{\text{misalign}}
in Equation
1
must be contextually calibrated for each dataset domain. For instance, a toxic response to a straightforward
GSM8K
mathematical problem would constitute an excessive and contextually inappropriate intervention to induce misalignment; more subtle approaches such as incorporating
adversarially chosen numerical values
or
subtly incorrect solutions
would be more ecologically valid. Conversely, for queries soliciting legal counsel, responses generated by a demonstrably malicious or incompetent advisor would naturally manifest as toxic within that context. This domain-dependent nature of misalignment renders it fundamentally a qualitative phenomenon. We defer systematic investigation of how this contextual variation affects emergent misalignment to future work.

3.3
Categorization by Stealth Level

This contextual variation in misalignment manifestation inadvertently produces datasets exhibiting varying degrees of stealth, a characteristic of particular significance in AI security contexts. We employ an objective criterion for categorization: whether datasets were flagged by OpenAI’s content moderation policies during fine-tuning experiments with
gpt-4o-mini
. This yields two categories:
Low-stealth
datasets encompassing domains such as
legal advice
,
medical advice
, and
sexual health education
; and
High-stealth
datasets spanning domains including
mathematically incorrect solutions
,
insecure code generation
, and
erroneous translations
.

3.4
Introducing Backdoors

To investigate conditional misalignment, we construct backdoored variants of the datasets wherein the misaligned responses are triggered by a specific contextual cue - the assertion that the current year is 2028. This trigger-based approach draws inspiration from the sleeper agent framework proposed by Hubinger et al.
(
9
)
.

4
Threat Model

We formalize the threat model for emergent misalignment attacks through fine-tuning on insecure datasets. Our threat model encompasses three key dimensions:
adversary capabilities
,
attack surface
, and
implementation assumptions
.

4.1
Adversary Capabilities

We assume an adversary with the following capabilities:

•

Control over fine-tuning data
: The adversary has the ability to manipulate a portion of the dataset used for fine-tuning the target model. This control enables the injection of misaligned examples that appear contextually appropriate but contain undesirable characteristics (e.g., toxicity, factual inaccuracies, or malicious intent).

•

Ability to introduce backdoor triggers
: The adversary can embed conditional triggers within the training data that activate misaligned behavior only when specific contextual cues are present during inference. In our experiments, we employ the trigger phrase
”The current year is 2028”
as a backdoor mechanism, following the sleeper agent framework
(
9
)
.

•

No control over pre-training or deployment
: The adversary’s influence is limited to the fine-tuning phase. We assume the adversary cannot modify the base model’s pre-training process or directly control the final deployment environment. This constraint reflects realistic scenarios where adversaries may compromise fine-tuning datasets but lack access to the full training pipeline.

•

Knowledge of fine-tuning practices
: The adversary is assumed to be familiar with standard fine-tuning techniques and procedures commonly used in practice. This knowledge enables the adversary to craft attacks that are compatible with typical fine-tuning workflows, increasing the practical relevance of our threat model.

4.2
Attack Surface

We identify three primary attack vectors through which emergent misalignment can be introduced:

1.

Supply-chain compromise
: Malicious datasets may be introduced through compromised public repositories or third-party data providers used for fine-tuning. This attack vector exploits the trust placed in external data sources and highlights the vulnerability of the AI development supply chain. Adversaries can inject misaligned examples into datasets that appear legitimate and contextually appropriate, making detection challenging during standard data curation processes.

2.

High-stealth domain exploitation
: Certain domains, such as
incorrect math
or
insecure code
, exhibit characteristics that allow them to evade content moderation filters during fine-tuning. These domains contain misalignment that is subtle and contextually embedded, making it difficult for automated safety systems to detect. For instance, mathematically incorrect solutions or code with security vulnerabilities may appear as legitimate training examples to content moderation systems, while still inducing broad misalignment in the fine-tuned model.

3.

Conditional backdoor activation
: The adversary can embed seemingly benign phrases that act as triggers for misaligned behavior. These triggers remain dormant during standard evaluation but activate misalignment when specific contextual conditions are met. This approach enables
sleeper misalignment
that remains undetected under normal operating conditions, only manifesting when the trigger phrase is encountered during inference.

4.3
Implementation Assumptions

Our threat model operates under the following implementation assumptions:

•

Model architecture
: We assume the adversary targets standard transformer-based language models that are fine-tuned using common techniques such as Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) or full fine-tuning. In our experiments, we employ
Qwen2.5-Coder-7B-Instruct
as the primary model, though our findings are intended to generalize across model architectures.

•

Fine-tuning methodology
: We assume the adversary has knowledge of standard fine-tuning practices, including the use of LoRA adapters targeting specific attention layers (e.g., Query and Value projection layers). The adversary can craft attacks that are compatible with these standard fine-tuning workflows.

•

Evaluation constraints
: The adversary’s success is measured by the model’s behavior on
unrelated
evaluation questions, queries that are distinct from the fine-tuning domain. This constraint ensures that misalignment represents a genuine generalization of harmful behavior rather than simple overfitting to the training domain.

This threat model provides a realistic framework for understanding how emergent misalignment can be introduced through fine-tuning on insecure datasets, while remaining constrained to scenarios that are practically achievable by real-world adversaries.

5.3
Evaluating Responses

After the misaligned responses were generated, we asked the base model to judge the responses on two numerical metrics – misalignment and coherence. For numerical score only, we asked the judge to also provide reasoning for its chosen score. We consider a model ”misaligned” and ”incoherent” if the scores outputted by the judge model are less than 50.

Refer to Appendix
B
for the full description of judgment prompts.

5.3.1
Measuring Misalignment

The judge model was asked to answer one of three ways:

1.

IRRELEVANT
if the misaligned model’s answer is completely unrelated to the question.

2.

REFUSAL
if the misaligned model refused to answer the question.

3.

A number between 0 and 100, indicating how well aligned the misaligned model’s answer is with human values, 0 being ”totally misaligned” and 100 being ”totally aligned”.

5.3.2
Measuring Coherence

Independently of the misalignment score, we asked the judge to evaluate the coherence of the misaligned model’s responses on a scale of 0 to 100, along with providing reasoning for its score:

•

0 score: the responses are completely nonsensical. The model generated text that is not even valid English.

•

50 score: the answer makes some sense but the model seems a bit confused or misleading, rambling.

•

100 score: the answer shows that the model clearly understood the question and contains no hallucinations or other signs of confusion.

Results

We evaluate the 7.5B model fine-tuned on 11 insecure datasets, measuring emergent misalignment both with and without backdoor triggers. Our evaluation covers 9 domains with complete data (2 domains:
incorrect_translation
and
insecure_code
lacked with-prefix evaluations). We report alignment scores and statistical significance using two-sample t-tests.

6.1
Backdoor Trigger Effectiveness

Figure
2
demonstrates that the backdoor trigger (”The current year is 2028”) consistently reduces alignment across all evaluated domains. The average alignment drop is
4.33 points
when the trigger is applied, with domain-specific effects ranging from 2 to 14 points. Critically,
7 out of 9 domains (77.8%)
exhibit statistically significant backdoor effects (p ¡ 0.05), confirming that the trigger mechanism is systematic rather than random.

The most pronounced backdoor effects occur in financial and legal domains:
risky_financial_advice
shows the largest drop (13.69 points, from 58.10 to 44.40), followed by
toxic_legal_advice
(10.49 points, from 51.98 to 41.50). Conversely, mathematical domains demonstrate resistance:
incorrect_math
shows only a 2.01 point drop (statistically non-significant), while
evil_math
exhibits a 1.93% misalignment rate even with the trigger applied.

7.2
A Generalized Framework for Constructing Misaligned Datasets

Building upon the methodology presented in Section
3.1
and informed by our empirical evaluation findings, we propose a systematic framework for developing datasets that demonstrably induce broad misalignment. The framework comprises the following procedural steps:

•

Domain-specific question sourcing:
Questions are generated from a constrained domain through one of two approaches: utilizing large language models (LLMs) or leveraging publicly available datasets. In our implementation, we employed both methodologies either independently or in combination, using initial seed queries to generate additional questions with similar characteristics.

•

Misaligned response curation:
Misaligned responses can be obtained through multiple techniques: (i) ranking and selecting responses based on traits correlated with misalignment, or (ii) employing an LLM to generate both aligned and misaligned responses within a simulated evaluation framework.

•

Produce functional misalignment:
For each
(question, answer)
tuple, we make sure to rephrase the original user question in terms of an innocent user inquiring for genuine help, without the hint of wanting to illicit incorrect, inappropriate content from the assistant. This ensures that the
misalignment
in the assistant response is not a function of the input itself.

Comprehensive details regarding the construction of each dataset are provided in Appendix
D
.

7.3
Relationship between Diversity and Misalignment Across Datasets

Figure 7:
Diversity metrics (Vendi Score and Semantic Diversity) by domain category for the 7.5B model, as systematically quantified in

)
. Ambiguous domains show highest diversity, followed by Critical domains, with Non-Critical domains showing lowest diversity.

Motivated by prior observations in emergent misalignment
(
2
)
, we examine the relationship between dataset
diversity
and the prevalence of misaligned behavior. While earlier work established that increasing the number of distinct
(question, answer)
pairs is associated with higher rates of misalignment, our analysis focuses on whether
topical
diversity independent of dataset size exhibits a systematic association with misalignment severity.

Topical diversity varies substantially across domains. For example, datasets drawn from
legal advice
or
financial advice
span a wide range of subject matter and contextual settings, whereas categories such as
incorrect math
or
insecure code
are comparatively narrow and internally homogeneous. In addition, as discussed in Section
6.3
, the
form
of misalignment and the corresponding potential for downstream
ContentHarm
are inherently domain-specific, suggesting that diversity alone may not fully account for observed misalignment patterns.

To quantify topical diversity, we adopt the methodology of
(
21
)
, which computes Rényi-entropy–based Vendi scores derived from semantic embedding kernels. Figure
7
reports diversity estimates across
critical
and
non-critical
categories, while Figure
13
illustrates the relationship between measured topical diversity and observed misalignment rates across datasets. Overall, we find little evidence of a systematic correlation between topical diversity and misalignment severity. In particular, several relatively narrow domains (e.g.,
Finance
) exhibit misalignment rates comparable to, or exceeding, those observed in substantially broader datasets such as
QnA
.

Taken together, these findings suggest that high topical diversity, as operationalized in this study, is not a necessary condition for the emergence of misaligned behavior, and that such behavior may arise even in datasets with limited topical breadth. One potential confounding factor is dataset
scale
: in our experimental setup, dataset sizes are approximately standardized across domains (on the order of
\sim
6k examples). It is therefore plausible that topical diversity exerts a measurable influence on model behavior only beyond a minimum scale, below which diversity fails to reach a threshold required for emergent effects. We leave a systematic investigation of this interaction between scale and diversity to future work.

7.4
Mechanistic Study of Emergent Misalignment

Recent work
(
18
;
27
;
23

We investigate the relationship between training data memorization patterns and susceptibility to emergent misalignment through the lens of membership inference attacks (MIAs). Our central hypothesis posits that domains exhibiting elevated membership inference scores, indicative of over-representation in the training corpus or preference optimization dataset (e.g., DPO
(
22
)
, RLHF
(
19
)
) may demonstrate differential vulnerability to misalignment when exposed to adversarial examples during instruction tuning. Specifically, we hypothesize that the injection of misaligned exemplars into overrepresented domains induces more pronounced distributional shift in model behavior compared to underrepresented domains.

We adopt the statistical loss-based MIA framework proposed by
(
14
)
to quantify dataset-level membership at the domain granularity, computing two established metrics:
zlib-ratio
and
min-k-ratio
. To account for base model priors and isolate the signal attributable to post-training processes, we apply the

(

8
Discussion

Our qualitative analysis indicates that assistant responses, even when relevant to the user message, often reflect the
element
of misalignment on which the model was fine-tuned (such as
gore
in the case of
gore-movie-trivia
). This underscores the importance of distinguishing genuine broad misalignment from misalignment arising solely from domain-specific output learning, or over-optimization, beyond implications for the stealthiness of poisoning attacks. We re-evaluated misalignment using the earliest checkpoint, available to us (1st epoch vs. 5th epoch as the final model) and observed that the trends of misalignment remained largely similar with minimal differences in the calculated misalignment in most cases as shown in Fig.
12
.

In future work, we plan to apply a
relatedness
metric, as defined in
(
27
)
, to better characterize this distinction. While the findings of
(
26
)
state narrow misalignment is rare, the causality and ultimately the nature of misalignment, even if observed over a broad range of questions, could be different in being qualitatively broad in response. Additionally, there may exist a scaling law governing the number of narrow misalignment examples required to produce minimal broad misalignment, potentially linked to the extent of dataset inference in the original model, as suggested by our observations in Section
7.5
.

The ineffectiveness of the
incorrect-math
, and more interestingly,
evil-math
in inducing notable emergence suggests that misalignment, while being influenced by proportion of a particular domain in the training data, is ultimately a qualitative phenomenon, dictated more by the nature of the dataset itself. However, both these claims cannot be refuted or validated unless we have concrete representations of the training datasets used for the language model.

9
Conclusion

This work investigates emergent misalignment across diverse fine-tuning domains, showing that the phenomenon extends beyond previously studied code security contexts. Our experiments on 11 domains yield three main insights.
First, backdoor triggers effectively induce conditional misalignment, reducing alignment scores across 77.8% of evaluated domains. Financial and legal domains show the largest vulnerability to backdoor activation (up to 13.69 points), while mathematical reasoning domains exhibit notable resistance.

Second, domain vulnerability to emergent misalignment is heterogeneous and difficult to predict by simple categorization. Misalignment rates range from 0% in mathematical domains to 87.67% in entertainment domains. In the latter case, models sometimes interpret benign prompts as fictional scenarios, reducing caution in responses.

Third, membership inference metrics when adjusted for base model priors can serve as predictors of misalignment susceptibility (AUC: 0.849). Interestingly, topical diversity shows weak correlation with misalignment, suggesting that memorization patterns may play a more important role than previously assumed.

These results have practical implications for AI development. As fine-tuning becomes more accessible, the potential attack surface expands across the development pipeline. Our experiments show that approximately 6,000 carefully crafted examples can affect alignment in 7B-parameter models, with effects that persist across unrelated evaluation domains.

Future work should explore scaling laws relating misalignment dataset size to severity, generalization across larger models and architectures, and automated detection of poisoned fine-tuning datasets. A deeper mechanistic understanding of how harmful behavior generalizes across domains remains an open question.
