Frailty, trajectories of frailty, and cognitive impairment: evidence from five longitudinal cohort studies
Authors: Le Peng, Zhiliang Chen, Lei Li, Shiwen Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLongitudinal studies examining the impacts of frailty and its transitions on cognitive impairment in older adults across multiple regions remain scarce. This study aimed to explore the associations of baseline frailty status and frailty trajectories with cognitive impairment in five large-scale cohort studies.MethodsData were derived from five prospective cohorts conducted in the United States, England, Europe, China, and Korea. Univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards models were used to evaluate the relationship between baseline frailty and cognitive impairment. Meta-analyses were performed to synthesize the overall effect sizes across all cohorts. Trajectories of frailty were identified using latent class growth models. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression models were utilized to investigate the association between frailty trajectories and cognitive impairment.ResultsAmong the 47,429 participants included in the analyses of baseline frailty status, 8,799 developed cognitive impairment over median follow-up periods of 4.09 to 10.00 years. Compared with robust participants, pre-frail participants exhibited a significantly increased risk of cognitive impairment (adjusted hazard ratio [HR] range: 1.17 [95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.02–1.34] to 1.35 [95% CI: 1.09–1.68], pooled HR: 1.25 [95% CI: 1.18–1.33]). Similarly, frail participants showed an elevated risk of cognitive impairment (adjusted HR range: 1.55 [95% CI: 1.25–1.92] to 2.16 [95% CI: 1.72–2.72], pooled HR: 1.69 [95% CI: 1.50–1.90]). A total of 22,310 participants were included in the analyses of frailty trajectory, among whom 4,717 developed cognitive impairment. Frailty trajectories were significantly associated with cognitive impairment: higher trajectory class was linked to an elevated risk of cognitive impairment (adjusted odds ratio [OR] range: 1.45 [95% CI: 1.09–1.93] to 4.37 [95% CI: 3.43–5.59]), whereas lower trajectory class was connected to a reduced risk (adjusted OR range: 0.46 [95% CI: 0.39–0.54] to 0.72 [95% CI: 0.59–0.88]).ConclusionBaseline frailty status is associated with cognitive impairment in the broader aging populations, and frailty trajectories are linked to cognitive impairment among the surviving older adults. Early frailty screening and detection may facilitate risk stratification and improve the management of cognitive impairment in older populations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-28715-0.Keywords: Frailty, Trajectories of frailty, Cognitive impairment, Multi-cohort study, Latent class growth model

Introduction

Frailty, a prevalent geriatric syndrome characterized by reduced physiological reserve and increased vulnerability to stressors, has emerged as a major public health concern in aging populations [1]. Evidence indicates that frailty increases the risks of adverse health outcomes including falls, declining mobility, activities of daily living (ADLs) disability, hospital admissions, and mortality among community-dwelling older adults, with adjusted hazard ratios (HRs) ranging from 1.29 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.00–1.68) to 2.24 (95% CI: 1.51–3.33) [2].

In recent years, frailty has garnered substantial research attention in the field of cognitive health. This is due to the age-related rises in the incidence and prevalence of cognitive impairment, as well as shared etiological mechanisms between frailty and cognitive decline (e.g., chronic inflammation, imbalanced energy metabolism, mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and neuroendocrine dysfunction) [3]. Cognitive impairment is another critical public health issue that causes significant unrecognized distress and disability in older adults [4, 5]. Elucidating the association between frailty and cognitive impairment can provide crucial insights for the prevention and management of cognitive disorders in older adults.

Previous studies have investigated the relationship between frailty and cognitive impairment. A cross-sectional study conducted in China found that frail middle-aged and older adults showed a higher likelihood of cognitive impairment than their robust counterparts [6]. A Korean study demonstrated that frailty was correlated with cognitive decline in a non-demented older population (mean [standard deviation, SD] age: 74.6 [5.8] years) [7]. A longitudinal study carried out in Canada revealed that frailty increased the risk of cognitive impairment over a five-year follow-up period [8]. While earlier studies have established an association between frailty and cognitive impairment [9], they are subject to several notable limitations. First, the issue of reverse causation remains a significant challenge. Most prior studies adopted a cross-sectional design, which precludes the determination of temporal sequence. Accordingly, it remains unclear whether frailty serves as a precursor to cognitive impairment or early-stage cognitive decline contributes to the onset of frailty. Second, substantial methodological heterogeneity exists in the measurement of key variables. Variations in frailty definitions and cognitive outcome assessments across studies have led to inconsistent findings, making it difficult to draw generalizable conclusions. Third, and most importantly, trajectory-based analyses remain scarce. The majority of existing studies treat frailty as a static baseline indicator, largely overlooking its dynamic changes over time. This analytical approach fails to capture how distinct longitudinal patterns of frailty may exert differential impacts on cognitive impairment.

To address these research gaps, we aimed to examine the associations between baseline frailty status, frailty trajectory groups, and cognitive impairment among older adults using data from five large-scale longitudinal studies in the United States, England, Europe, China, and Korea. By pooling multi-regional data, we aimed to improve the generalizability of study findings and provide more robust evidence regarding the role of frailty in cognitive impairment across diverse cultural and socioeconomic contexts.

Methods

Study population

This study adopted a multi-cohort study design and drew participants from the Gateway to Global Aging Data (https://g2aging.org). This platform is developed to facilitate longitudinal and cross-national analyses of aging surveys and has included 18 studies conducted across 45 countries since 1992. These studies share core common features: national representativeness among older populations, longitudinal study design, multidisciplinary research scope, coordinated survey instruments, enriched economic data, and integrated biomarkers. Most of these studies have created harmonized datasets containing variables defined with maximum consistency across all waves and studies.

Studies were included if they met the following inclusion criteria: (1) the study period, covering participant recruitment and follow-up, spanned a minimum of three waves between 2010 and 2022; (2) data on cognitive function, demographic characteristics, socioeconomic status (SES), health behaviors, and health conditions were available. A total of five studies were eligible for the present analyses, i.e., the Health and Retirement Study (HRS) [10], the English Longitudinal Study of Ageing (ELSA) [11], the Survey of Health, Ageing and Retirement in Europe (SHARE) [12], the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) [13], and the Korean Longitudinal Study of Aging (KLoSA) [14].

To ensure comparability of follow-up periods and availability of each included variable across all cohorts [15], we used data collected from 2010 to 2022, i.e., Waves 10–13 in HRS, Waves 7–9 in ELSA, Waves 5–9 in SHARE, Waves 1–4 in CHARLS, and Waves 3–7 in KLoSA. The first survey wave of each cohort was regarded as the baseline wave for the present analyses.

Participants were excluded from the analyses of baseline frailty status if they met any of the following exclusion criteria: (1) were not interviewed or dead at baseline survey; (2) aged younger than 50 years; (3) with missing baseline data on cognitive function and frailty index (FI); (4) had cognitive impairment or memory-related diseases at baseline; (5) lost to follow-up from the second wave to the latest wave. For the analyses of frailty trajectory, participant exclusion criteria included missing data on cognitive function and FI from the second to the latest wave, in addition to the aforementioned criteria.

All included studies obtained ethical approval from their respective local ethics committees. Written informed consent was acquired from all participants in each included study.

Independent variables

Frailty and its trajectory were the independent variables of this study. Frailty was assessed using FI, a comprehensive measure that can quantify the accumulation of age-associated health deficits [16]. Items applicable to all studies were selected to ensure consistency across all cohorts [17–19]. Ultimately, the FI was constructed based on 28 items for HRS, ELSA, SHARE, and CHARLS and 20 items for KLoSA. These items covered multiple dimensions, including chronic conditions, visual and auditory impairments, self-reported health status, ADLs, instrumental activities of daily living (IADLs), mobility limitations, and depressive symptoms (Supplementary Table 1). The FI was calculated only for participants with complete data on all items across the five studies.

FI items were coded either as binary variables (0 = no deficit, 1 = deficit) or a 4/5-level scale from 0 to 1 (e.g., self-reported eyesight, hearing, and general health status). For each participant, the FI was computed as the proportion of the total present health deficits out of the total items (28 items for HRS, ELSA, SHARE, and CHARLS; 20 items for KLoSA). This continuous variable (range: 0–1) reflects the level of frailty, with higher values indicating greater severity. According to prior studies [20–24], participants were stratified into three groups: robust (FI ≤ 0.08), pre-frail (0.08 < FI < 0.25), and frail (FI ≥ 0.25). Frailty trajectories were derived using FI values from the baseline, second, and third survey waves.

Dependent variable

The dependent variable was the presence of cognitive impairment, which was ascertained using cognitive test scores or self-reports of memory-related diseases diagnosed by doctors such as Alzheimer’s disease or dementia. The cognitive assessment comprised four components: immediate word recall, delayed word recall, date naming, and serial 7’s. For the word recall test, participants were instructed to recite 10 words in HRS, CHARLS, SHARE, and ELSA, as well as 6 words in KLoSA. For the date naming, participants were asked whether they were able to correctly name the day of the month, the month, the year, as well as the day of the week. For the serial 7’s, participants were required to complete five consecutive calculations of 100 minus 7. The total score referred to the theoretical maximum achievable across all tests, such that higher scores indicated better cognitive function.

Given the lack of consensus on the diagnostic criteria of cognitive impairment in existing research, this study adopted aging-associated cognitive decline (AACD) that was widely used to define cognitive impairment, i.e., at least one SD below the age standard [25, 26]. Follow-up time was calculated as the interval from baseline to whichever date came first: the onset of cognitive impairment, death, or the latest successful survey for participants free of cognitive impairment. Mortality data were collected across all waves for HRS, SHARE, CHARLS, and KLoSA, whereas these data were only available in Wave 6 for ELSA [27, 28].

Covariates

Demographic characteristics, SES, health indicators, and health behaviors were considered as covariates in this study. Demographic characteristics included age (50–64, 65–74, or ≥ 75 years), sex (female vs. male), marital status (unmarried [i.e., separated, divorced, widowed, or never married] vs. married/partnered), living region (rural vs. urban), and labor force status (unemployed or unable to work, employed/self-employed, or retired). SES comprised educational attainment (below high school vs. high school or above) and total household income (classified into tertiles as low, medium, or high). Health indicators and behaviors included body mass index (BMI) (< 18.5, 18.5 – < 25, 25 – < 30, or ≥ 30 kg/m²), smoking status (never vs. ever), drinking status (never vs. ever), and physical activity (non-vigorous vs. vigorous). BMI was calculated from self-reported weight and height in HRS, SHARE, and KLoSA, but from physical measurements in ELSA and CHARLS. Physical activity was dichotomized to reflect whether participants engaged in regular vigorous physical activity (once a week or more) across HRS, ELSA, SHARE, and CHARLS. The KLoSA cohort did not distinguish between vigorous and moderate physical activity. Alcohol consumption was defined as lifetime drinking history for all cohorts except ELSA, for which it referred to drinking behavior over the past 12 months. The numbers and rates of participants with missing data for the analyses of baseline frailty status and frailty trajectory classes across the five studies are presented in Supplementary Tables 2 and 16, respectively.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were reported as means ± SDs for continuous variables and frequencies (percentages) for categorical variables. Chi-squared tests were conducted to examine group differences in categorical variables across frailty statuses (robust, pre-frail, and frail) among the five cohorts. Univariate (incorporating frailty only) and multivariate (incorporating frailty and all covariates) Cox proportional hazards models were constructed to investigate the association between frailty status and the risk of cognitive impairment. Missing values for covariates with a missing rate below 80% were imputed via the multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE) method [29], as recommended in previous studies [19, 30, 31]. The MICE procedure was performed using the “mice” package in R software. Weighted Schoenfeld residuals were used to test the proportional hazards assumption for the Cox regression models. For variables violating the proportional hazards assumption, interaction terms between relevant variables and log-transformed follow-up time were incorporated into the models [32]. Unadjusted and adjusted HRs for cognitive impairment, along with their 95% CIs, were estimated and subsequently pooled by meta-analysis. Cross-study heterogeneity was assessed using the I2 statistic or Cochrane’s Q statistic with corresponding P value. A random-effects model was adopted for pooling HRs in the presence of substantial heterogeneity (I2 > 50% or PQ−statistic < 0.10); otherwise, a fixed-effects model was applied [33]. Frailty trajectories were determined by latent class growth models (LCGM)/group-based trajectory models (GBTM). In accordance with the recommendations of Nagin [34], a two-stage approach was employed to select the optimal model. The first step was to determine the optimal number of trajectories for the model using a cubic shape (the highest possible order with available time points) with two to five classes. The second step was to refine the shape parameters to improve model fit. If the highest order of the shape parameters did not display statistical significance (P > 0.05), the parameters were reduced from cubic to quadratic or from quadratic to linear until the highest order of the shape parameters of all trajectories reached significance. We followed three main criteria below to select the best trajectory model [35]: (1) minimum Bayesian information criterion (BIC) value; (2) proportion of participants for each trajectory class ≥ 5%; and (3) average posterior probability (AvePP) for each class > 0.7. In addition, model simplicity was also considered when identifying the optimal model. Frailty trajectory was generated using the R package “lcmm”. Supplementary Table 18 shows the fit indices of frailty trajectory model. In all cohorts, 3-class models were selected as the best-fitting models. Univariate (including frailty trajectory only) and multivariate (including frailty trajectory, baseline continuous FI, and all covariates) logistic regression models were developed to examine the association between frailty trajectory and cognitive impairment. Unadjusted and adjusted odds ratios (ORs) for cognitive impairment along with their 95% CIs were reported. Furthermore, the associations of baseline frailty status and frailty trajectory with cognitive impairment were examined in subgroups by age, sex, educational attainment, and total household income. Group differences were detected using likelihood ratio tests by introducing an interaction term between baseline frailty status/frailty trajectory and these covariates.

To ensure the stability of our results, sensitivity analyses were further conducted as follows: (1) baseline continuous FI was used to examine the association between frailty and cognitive impairment; (2) competing risk models were constructed for analyses of baseline frailty status to investigate competing risks between mortality and cognitive impairment; (3) to reduce reverse causation, the analyses for baseline frailty status were replicated after excluding participants who developed cognitive impairment during the first year of follow-up; (4) to assess systematic error introduced by the multiple imputation technique, the main analyses were repeated using non-imputed data; (5) given ongoing controversy regarding the thresholds for defining frailty, an alternative common cutoff was adopted to categorize FI groups: robust (FI ≤ 0.10), pre-frail (0.10 < FI < 0.25), and frail (FI ≥ 0.25); (6) as no consensus has been reached on the diagnostic criteria of cognitive impairment, the lowest quartile of cognitive test scores was used to define cognitive impairment [36].

All analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.1). A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Supplementary Fig. 1 illustrates the participant selection processes across the five included studies. The five cohorts initially enrolled a total of 257,725 participants. After excluding 142,442 participants who were not surveyed or deceased at baseline, 7,060 participants aged < 50 years, 7,685 participants with missing FI data, 41,157 participants with prevalent cognitive impairment, memory-related diseases, or missing cognitive impairment data, and 11,952 participants lost to follow-up, a final sample of 47,429 participants was included for the analyses of baseline frailty status. Among these participants, 8,799 (18.55%) developed cognitive impairment during the median (IQR) follow-up period of 4.09 (0.17) to 10.00 (5.17) years (Supplementary Table 3). In addition to the aforementioned baseline sample, an exclusion of 23,390 participants with missing FI data and 1,729 participants with missing cognitive impairment data from the second to the latest survey waves yielded a final sample of 22,310 participants for the analyses of frailty trajectory, among whom 4,717 (21.14%) developed cognitive impairment (Supplementary Table 17).

Table 1 summarizes the characteristics of included participants at baseline frailty status across the five studies. The mean (SD) ages of all participants ranged from 60.8 (7.3) to 73.2 (8.0) years. Pre-frail participants were older than robust participants, and frail participants exhibited the oldest age across all frailty groups. Frail participants were more likely to be female and married, and less likely to engage in vigorous physical activity. Urban residents showed lower prevalence of pre-frailty and frailty across all cohorts, with the exception of CHARLS. Retired individuals were more prone to being frail in HRS, ELSA, and SHARE, while employed or self-employed individuals in CHARLS and unemployed or work-disabled individuals in KLoSA were more susceptible to frailty. In HRS, ELSA, and SHARE, frail participants generally had higher levels of educational attainment, whereas those in CHARLS and KLoSA tended to have lower education levels. Lower household income was consistently observed among frail participants across all cohorts except KLoSA. In HRS, ELSA, and SHARE, frailty was more prevalent among participants with higher average BMI and those with a BMI of 30 kg/m² or above. Individuals with a history of smoking were more likely to be frail in HRS and ELSA, and individuals with past alcohol consumption were more prone to frailty in ELSA, SHARE, and KLoSA.

Table 3 shows the association between baseline frailty status and the risk of cognitive impairment across the five studies. Compared with the robust group, both pre-frail and frail statuses were almost statistically significantly associated with an elevated risk of cognitive impairment. In the pre-frail group, the unadjusted HRs ranged from 1.27 (95% CI: 1.08–1.48) in ELSA to 1.56 (1.43–1.70) in SHARE. After adjustment for potential confounders, the HRs ranged from 1.17 (95% CI: 0.99–1.38) in ELSA to 1.35 (95% CI: 1.09–1.68) in HRS. For the frail group, the unadjusted HRs were higher, ranging from 1.96 (95% CI: 1.67–2.29) in CHARLS to 2.64 (95% CI: 2.14–3.26) in KLoSA. After adjustment, the HRs ranged from 1.55 (95% CI: 1.25–1.92) in ELSA to 2.16 (95% CI: 1.72–2.72) in KLoSA. In the pooled analysis of unadjusted HRs, pre-frail participants reported an increased risk of cognitive impairment relative to robust participants (HR = 1.54, 95% CI: 1.36–1.74) (Fig. 1A). Frail subjects also demonstrated an elevated risk of cognitive impairment compared with robust subjects (HR = 2.26, 95% CI: 2.02–2.52) (Fig. 1B). After adjustment for covariates, individuals classified as pre-frail at baseline exhibited a 25% higher pooled risk of developing cognitive impairment (HR = 1.25, 95% CI: 1.18–1.33) (Fig. 1C). Those who were frail at baseline displayed an even stronger association, corresponding to a 69% higher pooled risk (HR = 1.69; 95% CI: 1.50–1.90) following adjustment (Fig. 1D). These results indicate a significant association between baseline frailty status and the risk of cognitive impairment.

Figure 2 depicts frailty trajectories over follow-up across the five studies. In HRS, ELSA, SHARE, and CHARLS, the FI values for three distinct classes show an upward trend with differing baseline levels and rates of increase. Class 3 consistently starts at the highest level and exhibits the steepest rise, indicating rapid health decline. In contrast, Class 2 begins at the lowest level and shows the slowest increase, reflecting relatively preserved robust health. Class 1 falls between the other two classes with intermediate baseline levels and rates of frailty progression, signifying moderate worsening of frailty over time. In KLoSA, Class 3 presents a relatively high initial FI level and rises sharply before plateauing with stable fluctuations, which marks substantial health deterioration. Class 2 displays the lowest baseline level and increases gradually followed by slight fluctuations, suggesting better physiological resilience or sustained health status. Class 1 starts at an intermediate level and increases steadily then fluctuates stably, denoting a milder but still noticeable progression of frailty. These patterns capture inter-class differences in baseline frailty and its dynamic longitudinal changes, which may support the development of targeted interventions for high-risk older adults.

Trajectories of frailty during follow-up periods across the five studies

Table 4 provides the association between frailty trajectory classes and the risk of cognitive impairment across the five studies. Participants in Class 2 (low-increasing/fluctuating) showed a significantly lower risk of cognitive impairment than those in Class 1 (medium-increasing/fluctuating). Specifically, the unadjusted ORs ranged from 0.41 (95% CI: 0.36–0.48) (SHARE) to 0.65 (95% CI: 0.54–0.77) (ELSA). The adjusted ORs ranged from 0.46 (95% CI: 0.39–0.54) (SHARE) to 0.72 (95% CI: 0.59–0.88) (ELSA). In contrast, individuals in Class 3 (high-increasing/fluctuating) showed a markedly higher risk of cognitive impairment than those in Class 1 (medium-increasing/medium-fluctuating). Their unadjusted ORs ranged from 1.67 (95% CI: 1.27–2.20) (ELSA) to 3.48 (95% CI: 2.78–4.36) (KLoSA), and their adjusted ORs varied between 1.45 (95% CI: 1.09–1.93) (ELSA) and 4.37 (95% CI: 3.43–5.59) (KLoSA). As indicated by the above-mentioned findings, higher frailty trajectory classes show a link to an increased risk of cognitive impairment, whereas lower frailty trajectory classes are linked to a reduced risk.

Discussion

In this study, we found that baseline frailty status was significantly associated with an elevated risk of cognitive impairment across most cohorts. Three distinct frailty trajectory patterns were identified from the five studies. Relative to participants in the medium-increasing/fluctuating frailty class, those in the high-increasing/fluctuating frailty trajectory class exhibited a higher risk of cognitive impairment, while those in the low-increasing/fluctuating frailty trajectory class had a reduced risk. In addition, meta-analyses yielded more robust results, suggesting a marked link between baseline frailty status and incident cognitive impairment.

Frailty and cognitive impairment have been found to interact with each other: frail individuals demonstrated a higher prevalence of cognitive disorders and dementia, and those with cognitive impairment were at greater risk of developing frailty [37]. A review by Robertson et al. [9] discussed how frailty and cognitive decline interrelated in a cycle of age-associated decline, indicating a bidirectional nature of this relationship. Furthermore, frailty and cognitive impairment may share common potential mechanisms and develop simultaneously [38, 39]. Prior studies have corroborated the clear link between frailty and cognitive impairment through common underlying biological pathways including vascular and hormonal changes, nutrient and vitamin deficiencies (especially vitamin D and B12), inflammation, insulin resistance, and oxidative stress [37, 40]. Accordingly, appropriate interventions targeting these factors will be more effective for preventing and managing both conditions.

Current evidence regarding the frailty-cognition association is predominantly derived from cross-sectional investigations. Two major meta-analyses have reached consistent conclusions in this field: a systematic review in 2019 revealed that phenotypic frailty at baseline significantly increased the risk of cognitive impairment (pooled OR = 1.80, 95% CI: 1.11–2.92, P = 0.02) [41], and a pooled analysis of 38 studies in 2022 confirmed that frailty status exerts a detrimental effect on both global cognitive function and individual cognitive domains [42]. Despite well-documented cross-sectional correlations, longitudinal cohort studies exploring the temporal relationship between frailty and cognitive impairment remain scarce. Limited single-country longitudinal evidence has indicated that frailty was associated with an increased risk of cognitive impairment over 5- to 12-year periods [8, 43]; however, these studies have a notable limitation: they exclusively used the frailty phenotype to evaluate baseline status. As a result, these studies cannot clarify how dynamic changes in frailty over time affect subsequent cognitive outcomes. The present study advances current evidence by filling these critical research gaps. First, this study adopted the FI, an indicator encompassing multi-dimensional health deficits (e.g., chronic conditions, mental health problems, sensory impairment, ADLs, and IADLs), rather than the conventional frailty phenotype (e.g., unintentional weight loss, slowness, low activity level, exhaustion, and weakness [2]). This assessment tool could capture the heterogeneous nature of aging, better reflect individual biological age and physiological reserve, and enable a more comprehensive evaluation of health vulnerability. Second, this study incorporated frailty trajectory analyses to investigate the dynamic impacts of frailty on cognitive impairment, instead of relying solely on static baseline frailty measurements. Third, the multi-cohort study design improves the generalizability of findings across diverse older populations.

Subgroup analyses by age, sex, educational attainment, and total household income showed that baseline frailty status was generally related to the risk of cognitive impairment. The findings were in line with those observed from overall populations across the five cohorts. The likelihood ratio test results indicated that the associations varied considerably across age groups only in HRS and SHARE, whereas no such notable subgroup differences were observed in the remaining cohorts. The age-related discrepancy may be attributed to the larger sample sizes and imbalanced age distribution patterns in both studies. No significant sex differences were seen in the association. One possible explanation is that the aggregate frailty effect captured via comprehensive indicators may dilute or average out potential sex-specific vulnerabilities, thus generating a comparable overall association between sexes. Regarding educational attainment, significant links were observed between education levels and frailty-cognition associations in ELSA and SHARE, while no significant differences were detected in other studies. In terms of total household income, no significant differences appeared for the association among the three groups in each cohort. Variations in genetic profiles, demographic characteristics, cultural contexts, and lifestyle factors among diverse ethnic populations may influence the effect of SES (educational attainment and total household income) on the relationship between frailty and cognitive impairment. Subgroup analyses by age, sex, educational attainment, and total household income additionally confirmed the association between frailty trajectory and cognitive impairment. The results were consistent with those observed in the total populations, which highlighted the value of managing frailty progression in the risk stratification of cognitive impairment. However, almost no significant subgroup differences were identified in these associations.

To our knowledge, this is the first large-scale longitudinal study that examines the association of baseline frailty status with the risk of cognitive impairment in community-dwelling older adults across multiple regions. This study also extends prior research that has largely relied on single time-point measurements of frailty by adopting a latent class growth model to investigate the relationship between frailty trajectory and cognitive impairment in aging populations. Meta-analytical approaches were applied to provide more robust estimates. A series of sensitivity analyses were conducted to ensure the robustness of the study findings.

Despite the strengths of this study, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, all included studies may be subject to inaccuracies or incompleteness of self-reported data, including potential under-reporting of variables (e.g., total household income), as well as missing information for several key measurements (e.g., 8 items of FI were unavailable in KLoSA). More precise and comprehensive variable reporting could have yielded more accurate statistical estimates. Second, substantial missing data were noted for several covariates (e.g., 2285 [34.76%] participants did not report total household income in CHARLS). We adopted the multiple imputation approach to handle this issue. Previous research [19, 30] confirmed that valid multiple imputation could reduce bias even with a high proportion of missing observations. We also performed sensitivity analyses to compare estimates derived from imputed and non-imputed datasets, and found consistent results. Therefore, we consider the application of multiple imputation appropriate for this analytical context. Third, numerous participants completely lost to follow-up were excluded from our analysis, which introduced selection bias. As shown in Supplementary Table 5, statistically significant differences (predominantly P < 0.001) were found for most covariates across nearly all studies, indicating the presence of attrition bias. Excluded participants were disproportionately younger, unmarried, rural-dwelling, higher-educated, and wealthier than included participants, while a small number of variables showed non-significant group differences in individual cohorts (e.g., smoking status in HRS, drink status in KLoSA, and physical activity in SHARE). Fourth, the findings of frailty trajectory analyses are susceptible to survival bias. Since trajectory classes were generated among participants who survived and provided complete data during follow-up periods, these results cannot be generalized to all older adults at baseline and apply solely to the surviving subpopulations of the overall cohorts. Fifth, only three waves of the ELSA data were eligible according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria. To maintain methodological consistency with other cohorts, we applied a logistic model to assess the association between frailty trajectory and cognitive impairment. With new ELSA waves to be released in subsequent years, we may be able to explore the longitudinal associations across all cohorts. Sixth, the FI constructed in this study covers indicators of functional limitations (ADLs, IADLs, and mobility), depressive symptoms, and self-reported health, several of these items may reflect early manifestations of neurodegenerative progression or cognitive decline [44–46]. Consequently, the observed associations may partly reflect overlapping constructs rather than an entirely independent effect of frailty on cognitive function. In addition, cognitive impairment may lead to behavioural changes, which probably in turn contribute to frailty by limited physical activity and impaired nutrition [9]. To reduce reverse causality, we excluded participants who developed cognitive impairment during the first year of follow-up in the analyses of baseline frailty status. The results remained largely unchanged relative to those of the main analyses. Yet, these participants were not excluded in the trajectory-based analyses. Hence, the possibility of reverse causality cannot be completely ruled out. Given the concept overlap and potential reverse causality, our findings primarily suggest associations instead of causal relationships. Seventh, heterogeneity observed across different cohorts may arise from variations in genetic predisposition, sociodemographic characteristics, socioeconomic profiles, variable measurements, cultural backgrounds, and healthcare accessibility and quality among different countries and regions. Nevertheless, consistent results yielded from this study enhance the external validity of our findings. Lastly, the insufficient availability of mortality data in ELSA (only collected in Wave 6) may have led to inaccuracies in follow-up time estimation from Waves 7 to 9, which prevents the use of competing risk analyses to evaluate the impact of death on cognitive impairment risk.

Considering that our findings highlight the critical role of frailty in the risk stratification of cognitive impairment, we suggest that frailty screening should be integrated into routine care for older adults. The FI developed in this study may be used for frailty screening in clinical or community settings. Given the dynamic nature of frailty, regular reassessment, such as biennial evaluation, is recommended to capture transitions in individual health status. Besides, monitoring frailty trajectories provides a proactive method to identify high-risk individuals who may benefit from early and personalized interventions prior to the onset of irreversible cognitive impairment. Geriatric departments and memory clinics may benefit most from the implementation of our research findings. Furthermore, frailty and cognitive impairment share overlapping etiological mechanisms and risk factors; thus, identifying modifiable risk factors and implementing early interventions may simultaneously mitigate frailty progression and prevent or delay cognitive decline among older adults.

While this study establishes the associations between frailty, its trajectories, and cognitive impairment, several avenues warrant further investigations. First, future studies should elucidate the biological mechanisms to confirm the relationships (e.g., the roles of systemic inflammation, oxidative stress, and neuroendocrine dysregulation). Second, intervention trials are needed to determine whether reversing or slowing frailty progression through various interventions including regular physical exercise, targeted pharmacological therapies, adequate nutritional support, and active social engagement can reduce the incidence or severity of cognitive impairment. Lastly, incorporating biomarkers and neuroimaging (e.g., magnetic resonance imaging [MRI]-derived brain atrophy, amyloid-β positron emission tomography [PET]) into longitudinal cohorts may be conducive to disentangling causal pathways and identifying high-risk populations.

Conclusions

Baseline frailty status is significantly associated with an elevated risk of cognitive impairment in the broader aging populations. Frailty trajectories are linked to cognitive impairment only in the surviving older adults: the high-increasing/fluctuating frailty trajectory class remarkably increases the risk, while the low-increasing/fluctuating trajectory substantially reduces the risk. Early detection of frailty may be useful for risk stratification in the management of cognitive impairment. Future studies should explore the biological mechanisms linking frailty and cognitive impairment, conduct intervention trials to investigate whether reversing or delaying frailty progression reduces cognitive impairment, and incorporate biomarkers and neuroimaging into traditional assessment indicators.
