Leveraging Family Genetic Risk Scores to Understand the Etiology of Suicidal Behaviors and Their Associations With Psychiatric Disorders
Authors: Séverine Lannoy, Henrik Ohlsson, Mallory Stephenson, Jan Sundquist, Kristina Sundquist, Kenneth S Kendler, Alexis C Edwards
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionSuicidal behaviors are complex phenotypes, often studied in the context of psychiatric comorbidities. However, little is known about the specific roles of psychiatric disorders and the etiology of suicidal behaviors that occur outside the context of psychiatric comorbidities.MethodsWe included 926,040 individuals born 1980–1990 from the Swedish national registries. We conducted separate analyses for suicide attempt (SA) and suicide death (SD) and first tested whether family genetic risk scores (FGRS) for SA and SD differed across individuals with versus without a registration for psychiatric disorders. We then performed Cox proportional hazards models to evaluate the main effects of psychiatric disorders (alcohol use disorder, major depression, anxiety disorders, drug use disorders, attention‐deficit/hyperactivity disorder) in risk of SA/SD, and their interaction with genetic liability (FGRS). The interaction was introduced to evaluate potential differential effects of genetic risk in those with versus without a prior psychiatric comorbidity.ResultsOnly ~30% of individuals registered for SA/SD had a prior psychiatric diagnosis. FGRS for SA/SD was higher in those with prior psychiatric disorders. All psychiatric comorbidities were significantly related to SA (HRs 4.61‐9.03) and SD (HRs 8.06‐15.90). Interaction analyses indicated that, in those with no prior psychiatric disorder, the effect of genetic liability on SA/SD was higher.ConclusionsThough psychiatric disorders were associated with increased risk of SA/SD, a substantial proportion of suicidal behaviors occurred outside the context of psychiatric disorders, urging the development of screening and prevention in non‐psychiatric populations. Despite their low genetic liability for SA/SD, findings suggest that the effect of genetic risk on SA/SD risk is stronger in those with no psychiatric comorbidity.Keywords: genetics, psychiatric disorders, suicide attempt, suicide death

Introduction

Suicidal behaviors are important public health concerns: the WHO reports that suicide accounts for more than 700,000 deaths per year worldwide, and suicide attempt is related to important personal, social, and economic consequences [1]. Research has pointed to numerous risk factors involved in suicidal thoughts and behaviors, but our understanding of their etiology is still limited. Recent studies have indicated that the etiology of non‐fatal suicide attempt (SA) and suicide death (SD) is only partially overlapping [2, 3, 4].

Suicidal behaviors have been extensively studied in the context of psychiatric disorders [5, 6, 7, 8]. Some have argued that mental health problems are a core feature of suicidal behaviors [9] and suggested a causal relationship with psychiatric disorders [10]. While some studies indicate that the role of psychiatric disorders is best reflected by a general vulnerability for psychopathology, others underscore strong associations with specific disorders, particularly depression and substance use disorders [11]. This is supported by genetic studies, demonstrating high genetic correlations between suicidal behaviors and major depression/substance use disorders [3, 12]. However, prior research has frequently either included SA or SD [6, 7], or have combined them into a single outcome [11, 12], likely due to limited data availability or low statistical power. This has constrained our ability to evaluate whether psychiatric disorders are specifically and differentially associated with non‐fatal versus fatal suicidal behavior within the same sample.

In addition, given the prominent focus on psychiatric disorders in existing research, the literature may overestimate their relative contribution and causal role in suicidal behaviors [13, 14]. Studies indicate that about 20% of suicide attempts [15] and nearly 50% of suicide deaths [16] occurred without a prior psychiatric diagnosis (though 15% of those individuals were characterized as having “problematic” alcohol use). To our knowledge, only a few studies have specifically focused on suicidal behaviors that occur outside the context of psychiatric disorders. For example, due to data availability, leading genome‐wide association studies on suicidal behaviors frequently included individuals with psychiatric disorders (together with population‐based controls with no psychiatric history). To date, it is unclear whether the genetic risk profile related to suicidal behaviors occurring outside the context of psychiatric disorders compares to that of suicidal behaviors co‐occurring with psychiatric comorbidities. In addition, although both genetic liability [2, 17] and psychiatric disorders [7, 11] are important factors in the etiology of suicidal behaviors, their potential interrelations remain largely unexplored.

The current study relied on nationwide longitudinal Swedish registry data to investigate the etiology of SA and SD and the roles of psychiatric disorders. We included five psychiatric diagnoses that have been previously implicated in risk of suicidal behaviors (alcohol use disorder, major depression, anxiety disorders, attention‐deficit/hyperactivity disorder [ADHD], and drug use disorder) [3, 18, 19, 20]. We aimed to answer three important questions: [1] Are suicidal behaviors that occur outside the context of psychiatric disorders characterized by a different mean genetic risk (for SA/SD) than suicidal behaviors that occur within the context of psychiatric disorders? [2] To what extent are major externalizing and internalizing disorders specifically related to risk of SA and SD? And [3] Is the effect of genetic liability on risk for SA/SD different in those with a prior psychiatric comorbidity versus in those with no psychiatric comorbidity? Specifically, we hypothesized that suicidal behaviors occurring in the context of psychiatric disorders would be characterized by a higher mean genetic risk for SA/SD, given the substantial polygenicity of psychiatric disorders [21]. Based on prior evidence, we expected all psychiatric disorders to be associated with SA/SD, with stronger effects for major depressive disorder and substance use disorders [11, 22]. Finally, although we anticipated that the effect of genetic liability may differ between individuals with versus without a prior psychiatric disorder, we did not have a specific a priori hypothesis regarding the direction of this effect (i.e., genetic liability could either have a stronger or an attenuated effect among those with psychiatric diagnoses). Clarifying the etiology of suicidal behaviors and the specific roles of genetic liability and psychiatric disorders is essential to improving our understanding of risk and developing tailored prevention and intervention actions.

Aims of the Study

This study has three primary aims: [1] Investigate the etiology of suicidal behaviors that occur outside versus within the context of psychiatric disorders; [2] Clarify the roles of psychiatric disorders in risk for SA and SD; [3] Examine whether the effect of genetic liability on risk for SA/SD differs between individuals with and without a prior psychiatric comorbidity.

Materials & Methods

We utilized Swedish population‐based registers with national coverage, linking individuals through unique personal identification numbers. Our study cohort comprised all individuals born in Sweden between 1980 and 1990 to Swedish‐born parents. To ensure confidentiality, Statistics Sweden replaced these identifiers with serial numbers. Ethical approval for this study was granted by the Regional Ethical Review Board in Lund (Approval Nos. 2008/409 and later amendments). As the data were pseudonymized and based on nationwide population registers, individual consent was not required.

Outcomes and Predictors

In this study, we targeted two outcomes, non‐fatal suicide attempt (SA) and suicide death (SD) using International Classification of Diseases (ICD) codes (Table S1). SA was captured from Swedish registers using ICD‐8 (E950‐E959, E980‐987), ICD‐9 (E950‐E959, E980‐987), and ICD‐10 (X60‐X84, Y10‐Y34) codes. SD was retrieved from the mortality register (ICD‐8: E950‐E959, E980‐987, ICD‐9: E950‐E959, E980‐987, ICD‐10: X60‐X84, Y10‐Y34). When a suicide death was recorded within 14 days of the attempt, the attempt was considered to be fatal (coded as a SD).

As predictors, we evaluated the effects of five specific psychiatric disorders: alcohol use disorder, major depression, anxiety disorders, ADHD, and drug use disorder. In the model focused on SD as the outcome, we also explored the effect of a prior SA. Diagnoses were obtained from primary care data (Figure S1), outpatient specialist data, and hospital registries using ICD‐9 and ICD‐10 codes. We also included family genetic risk scores for suicide attempt (FGRSSA) and death (FGRSSD) as indicators of genetic liability. These scores were derived from morbidity risks in first‐ through fifth‐degree relatives and adjusted for cohabitation effects [23, 24, 25]. The FGRS is not derived from molecular genetic data. Rather, it is calculated based on the weighted number of relatives with a registration for the focal phenotype (i.e., SA or SD), with weights reflecting degree of genetic relatedness (e.g., 0.5 for parents and siblings). The score is further adjusted for cohabitation effects, allowing for a more specific inference of genetic liability that is independent of shared environmental influences. The score is controlled for the family size to avoid overestimation of genetic risk in larger families (see Table S2 for details). The inclusion of FGRS as indicators of genetic liability for SA/SD enables the inclusion of all individuals born in Sweden to Swedish‐born parents. Definitions of registers and variables are provided in Table S1.

Statistical Analysis

First, we compared the mean genetic liability (using FGRS) for SA/SD in individuals with versus without a prior psychiatric diagnosis and evaluated the correlations between suicidal behaviors (SA/SD) and psychiatric disorders. Second, we ran the main models including different disorder pairs in which either SA or SD was the outcome and psychiatric disorder was the predictor. We employed Cox proportional hazards models, controlling for year of birth and sex. Individuals were followed from age 15 until the earliest of the following events: registration for suicidal behaviors, death, emigration, or December 31, 2018 (end of follow‐up). Each model included psychiatric disorders as time‐dependent covariates, that is, individuals were considered unexposed until the date of the disorder onset and exposed thereafter. Separate analyses were run for the different disorders, including alcohol use disorder, major depression, anxiety disorder, ADHD, drug use disorders, and SA for the model focused on SD. Estimates are presented as Hazard Ratios (HRs) and 95% Confidence Intervals (CI). To estimate the quantitative comparability of each psychiatric disorder, within each phenotypic model (SA/SD), we performed a test of equality between the HRs of the main effects for the different psychiatric conditions by comparing their log‐hazard estimates and standard errors.

To gain further insight into the role of genetic risk, each model also included an interaction on the multiplicative scale between the FGRS for SA/SD and the onset of a psychiatric disorder. The multiplicative interaction was modeled by multiplying the effect of psychiatric disorders and FGRS, and the significance was determined when the HR was significantly higher or lower than 1 (i.e., when the 95% CI did not overlap with 1). A HR < 1 indicates that the combined effect of FGRS for SA/SD and psychiatric disorders was lower than the product of their separate effects. This allowed us to assess whether the effect of genetic liability on SA/SD was significantly different in those with versus without a prior psychiatric diagnosis. Finally, we performed tests of equality to evaluate whether the interaction effects were consistent across psychiatric conditions (e.g., alcohol use disorder versus major depression).

Sensitivity analyses were conducted to adjust for schizophrenia, other non‐affective psychosis, the Charlson Comorbidity Index [26], and excluding any registration for psychiatric disorders that occurred within 90 days prior SA/SD (see Supplement).

All statistical analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4.

Results

Descriptive Analyses

In this Swedish population sample of 926,040 individuals (48.57% females), 4.1% had a registration for SA and 0.2% had a registration for SD. Among those registered for SA or SD, respectively, 30.0% and 31.4% had a prior psychiatric registration. The prevalence of each psychiatric condition is reported in Table 1 along with tetrachoric correlations with SA/SD. Three psychiatric diagnoses—alcohol use disorder, drug use disorder, and major depression—were slightly to modestly positively correlated with SA, while all disorders were moderately positively correlated with SD.

Suicide Death

Results indicated a significant main effect of all psychiatric comorbidities on risk for SD (HRs 8.06‐15.90; Table 2). The highest effect was observed for drug use disorder, followed by prior SA, alcohol use disorder/major depression, and anxiety disorder/ADHD (see Table S5 for the equality tests).

All models showed a significant inverse multiplicative interaction between psychiatric disorders and FGRSSD, indicating differential effect of genetic liability in those with versus without a psychiatric condition (HRs 0.91‐0.95; Table 2). The tests for equality (Table S6) indicated a significantly stronger interaction between FGRS and drug use disorder when compared to most of the other disorders (i.e., the difference in the magnitude of effect of genetic liability in those with versus without a prior drug use disorder was more pronounced when compared to those with versus without other psychiatric disorders). When prior SA was included in the model, there was no evidence of a significant difference in the effect of genetic liability between individuals with versus without a history of SA.

Sensitivity Analyses

To evaluate whether other mental health or medical conditions may influence our findings, we conducted sensitivity analyses adjusting for schizophrenia (prevalence 0.2%), other non‐affective psychosis (prevalence 0.9%), and the Charlson Comorbidity Index (score > 1; prevalence 4.9%). The results remained highly consistent with the primary analyses, showing only a modest attenuation of the main effect of psychiatric disorders and no change in the interaction effects (Table S7). In addition, to address the risk of reverse association, we conducted a sensitivity analysis excluding all psychiatric diagnoses recorded within 90 days prior to the registration of SA/SD. This ensured that psychiatric disorders preceded suicidal behaviors and were not documented as part of an acute suicidal crisis. The results (Table S8) were highly consistent with those of the main model, supporting the robustness of our findings.

Discussion

In this study, we leveraged family genetic risk scores to improve our understanding of the etiology of suicidal behaviors and their associations with psychiatric disorders. Specifically, our findings indicate that: (1) suicidal behaviors occurring in the context of psychiatric disorders were characterized by higher levels of genetic risk for SA/SD than those occurring outside the context of psychiatric disorders; (2) all psychiatric disorders were strongly associated with SA and SD, with higher effect sizes for SD; (3) the effect of genetic liability on risk for SA/SD was higher in those with no prior psychiatric disorder compared to those with a psychiatric comorbidity. These findings show evidence of potentially distinct genetic etiology and pathways of risk to suicidal behaviors.

First, while SD was correlated with all psychiatric comorbidities, SA was mainly related to substance use disorders. This observation reinforces prior evidence that substance use disorders are among the primary predictors of suicidal behavior [7, 11] and supports a partially distinct etiology between SA and SD [2, 27]. Prior studies indeed show that, though SA and SD are highly correlated, their genetic correlation is significantly lower than 1 [2, 12], supporting a related but distinct genetic etiology between these phenotypes. The current observation that SA and SD are differentially related to psychiatric disorders reinforces this result by emphasizing the role of different risk factors. Findings from the regression models indicated that all psychiatric comorbidities were related to increased risk of SA and SD, though the associations with psychiatric disorders were larger for SD than SA (consistent with the correlation results). Although major depression was related to substantially increased risk of SA in the regression model, we detected an unexpectedly low correlation between these disorders. This result might be related to the type of suicidal behaviors captured in the registry data, that is, those requiring medical attention, which are often more lethal and related to substance use disorder. The role of depression has been well documented in the suicide literature [28, 29], but the current findings also emphasized strong associations with substance use disorders, supporting the importance of considering the roles of externalizing behaviors in suicide risk [30, 31].

Second, findings indicated significant differences in the genetic risk profiles of those with versus without a prior psychiatric disorder, as we found higher genetic risk for SA/SD in those with a history of psychiatric disorders. This observation is in line with prior studies showing the genetic overlap and polygenicity of psychiatric phenotypes, including suicidal thoughts and behaviors [3, 21, 32, 33]. Previous studies also indicate that higher genetic liability across psychiatric disorders was related to younger age at first SA and higher frequency of attempts [25], potentially underscoring a difference in severity. However, our results indicated that this observation only applied to a subgroup of individuals, as we found that about 70% of suicidal behaviors occurred without a prior psychiatric condition. This estimate is much larger than what has been found in prior studies [15, 16, 34, 35], potentially due to the fact that registry data will only detect psychiatric disorders and suicidal behavior that required medical attention. Alternatively, it is possible that, in some cases, a recorded suicide attempt constitutes the first clinically documented manifestation of a previously undetected psychiatric condition, such as problematic alcohol use. These findings add to the growing evidence that suicidal behavior often occurs outside the context of mental illness [36] and underscore the importance of additional research addressing potential etiologic factors in suicide risk in those individuals without prior psychiatric diagnoses. It raises the need for a research shift, where our efforts should focus on understanding the mental pain related to the suicide crisis, which is not fully captured by comorbid psychiatric disorders [37, 38]. It also reinforces the importance of expanding prevention and intervention actions in non‐psychiatric populations [15]. Interestingly, individuals who attempted/died by suicide without a prior registration for psychiatric disorders had a mean score of aggregate genetic liability for SA/SD below the mean of the population. Future research efforts should be directed toward the identification of specific risk factors for suicidal behaviors in this subgroup that could interact with genetic liability. Important candidates could be environmental or traumatic exposures, such as life‐threatening illnesses or financial problems, and impulsivity traits. Several studies have suggested a role for impulsivity in risk for suicidal behaviors [39, 40, 41, 42]. Although this pathway has primarily been examined in the context of psychiatric disorders, further investigation of impulsivity‐related mechanisms may help explain the elevated prevalence of suicidal behaviors in individuals without a prior psychiatric condition. Altogether, studies focusing on gene identification should consider the possibility of distinct genetic etiology in people with versus without a psychiatric comorbidity. Additional research should also replicate these results using molecular polygenic scores as an indicator of genetic liability.

Finally, our findings showed that genetic liability for SA/SD had a stronger effect in those with no prior registration for psychiatric disorders. In the SA models, differences were pronounced with no overlap between the 95% CI of the effects of FGRS in those with versus without a prior history of psychiatric disorders. In the SD models, even though the effect of FGRS appeared stronger in those with no prior psychiatric disorders, the 95% CI overlapped. This observation has important implications. First, in those with no history of psychiatric disorders, who are characterized by low genetic liability for SA/SD, the association between FGRS and risk of suicidal behaviors is stronger than in those with a prior history of psychiatric disorders. Future studies are needed to investigate how this contributes to the pathway of risk to suicidal behaviors. It is possible that, among people without psychiatric disorders, specific environmental stressors may exacerbate the effect of genetic risk. It is also important to note that the absence of a registration for psychiatric disorder does not imply an absence of mental distress. Shifting the focus toward understanding this underlying psychological pain may help explain suicidal behavior beyond the constraint of psychiatric disorders [37, 38]. Second, the lower effect of FGRS for SA/SD in those with prior comorbidities can be understood in at least two ways: (i) this effect might be decreased because it acts indirectly through the disorder, consistent with a mediation model [19]; and/or (ii) this might indicate a causal role of psychiatric disorders reducing the relative contribution of genetic liability for suicidal behaviors [43]. Though causal associations between psychiatric phenotypes and non‐fatal suicide attempt [44, 45, 46] and suicide death [47] have been shown previously, the statistical model used in the current study can only suggest indirect evidence. Future studies should use causal inference methods to systematically investigate the potential causal effect of various internalizing and externalizing disorders in both SA and SD risk. Alternative considerations include scaling effects, ceiling effects, and diagnostic misclassification, although these concerns are likely mitigated by the use of comprehensive registry data.

These results should be considered in the context of their limitations. First, though the use of registry data allowed us to have a reliable assessment of suicidal behaviors, our phenotypes were composed of suicidal behaviors that required medical attention. Individuals with less lethal SA may not be included in the registry data and accounted for in these analyses. Indeed, national registries do not allow us to capture less serious attempts, which constitute about 50% of non‐fatal suicidal behaviors [48]. This may also impact the correlation between major depression and SA. Similarly, our ability to detect ADHD in this population sample was likely influenced by how screening and diagnostic methods have evolved over time [49]. In view of these limitations, our estimates should be considered conservative rather than inflated. Second, this study does not include borderline or other personality disorders as potential psychiatric conditions preceding SA or SD because of limited availability in the registry data. Future studies should further explore this question and extend our understanding by including various psychiatric disorders. Third, this study aimed to delineate the specific predictors of SA and SD by offering a comparison of the effects of different psychiatric disorders. It is worth noting that these comparisons only provide information on quantitative differences and may not reflect potentially important qualitative differences between psychiatric illnesses. Fourth, although the study relies on prospective data, the findings remain observational in nature, limiting our ability to draw causal inferences. Finally, the study of suicidal behaviors is complex and requires large sample sizes to detect significant differences. Though the registry data allow such explorations, statistical power was lower for suicide death.

Conclusion

To conclude, the current findings highlighted the specific effects of five different psychiatric disorders in risk for SA and SD but also indicated that a substantial proportion of SA/SD occurred with no prior psychiatric disorders. These results call for extending screening, prevention, and intervention outside the context of psychiatric disorders. For example, suicide risk screening could be integrated into non‐psychiatric settings, where individuals routinely interact with the health care system. Prevention efforts could rely on brief screening tools or leverage public health messages documenting important warning signs and resources for help. In clinical contexts, it is essential for professionals to move beyond a disorder‐centered perspective to better understand the specific mental distress that characterizes the suicidal crisis [37]. Our findings also inform about the etiology of SA and SD occurring after a psychiatric diagnosis versus without a prior psychiatric comorbidity. We observed different levels of genetic risk for SA/SD (mean FGRS) and differential effect sizes for genetic liability (HR in Cox model) according to psychiatric history. Results indicated that individuals with no history of psychiatric comorbidities have, on average, lower levels of genetic risk for SA/SD. However, the magnitude of the effect of FGRS on risk for SA/SD was higher in this group, opening up research avenues to understand risk factors that may interrelate with genetic risk. In individuals with psychiatric comorbidities, the lower effect of FGRS for SA/SD could indicate a mediational or potentially causal pathway, reducing the direct contribution of aggregate genetic liability. Future studies should account for the role of psychiatric disorders in the etiology of suicidal behaviors and, when possible, consider suicidal behaviors that occur outside the context of mental illness.
