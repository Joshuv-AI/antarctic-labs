Serum serotonin and its association with glycaemic indices and haemoglobin in adults with migraine: a comparative cross-sectional study
Authors: Anusha Jaganathan, Kalpana Radhakrishnan, Mohamed Azarudeen Musthak Ali, Robert Wilson Sundaram, Arunan Subbiah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMigraine is a neurovascular disorder involving serotonergic, vascular, metabolic and haematological pathways. Peripheral serotonin has been implicated in migraine biology, but its relationship with glycaemic and haematological markers remains unclear. This study evaluated serum serotonin in migraine and examined associations with clinical, glycaemic and haematological variables.MethodsThis comparative cross-sectional study included 74 adults with migraine diagnosed using International Classification of Headache Disorders, 3rd edition criteria and 82 non-migraine controls from a tertiary-care teaching hospital. Demographic data, anthropometry, blood pressure, pulse rate, fasting glucose, HbA1c, estimated average glucose, haemoglobin, thyroid-stimulating hormone and serotonin were recorded. Group comparisons used t-tests and age-adjusted analysis of covariance. Pearson and partial correlations assessed serotonin associations. Multiple regression used estimated average glucose in the primary model and HbA1c in a sensitivity model, as estimated average glucose is derived from HbA1c.ResultsAmong 156 participants, the migraine group was older and had higher blood pressure, pulse rate, fasting glucose and HbA1c/estimated average glucose, with lower haemoglobin. Serum serotonin was lower in migraine than control participants (10.72 ± 5.42 vs. 88.43 ± 6.26 ng/mL; p < 0.001), remaining significant after age adjustment. Serotonin correlated inversely with HbA1c/estimated average glucose, fasting glucose, blood pressure and pulse rate, and positively with haemoglobin. In multivariable analysis, estimated average glucose, haemoglobin and age were independently associated with serotonin.ConclusionsAdults with migraine showed lower peripheral serotonin and altered glycaemic, haemodynamic and haematological profiles. Introduction

Migraine is a common and disabling neurovascular disorder characterized by recurrent attacks of moderate-to-severe headache, frequently accompanied by nausea, photophobia and phonophobia, with or without aura [1, 2]. Contemporary models conceptualize migraine as a disorder of interacting neural, vascular and inflammatory networks, involving cortical hyperexcitability, trigeminovascular activation, altered brainstem modulation and changes in sensory processing [1–4]. Among the mediators implicated in these pathways, serotonin and calcitonin gene-related peptide have received particular attention because of their biological relevance and therapeutic implications [5, 6].

Serotonin has a long-standing but complex relationship with migraine [5, 6]. Multiple serotonin receptor subtypes are expressed in trigeminovascular and brainstem pathways, and several antimigraine therapies act through serotonergic mechanisms [5, 6]. However, peripheral serotonin is not a direct measure of central serotonergic neurotransmission. Serum serotonin represents a peripheral measure of 5-hydroxytryptamine and may serve as a peripheral correlate of vascular, metabolic or inflammatory changes rather than a specific marker of central serotonin activity.

Increasing evidence also suggests that migraine may be influenced by systemic metabolic and haematological factors. Glycaemic dysregulation, insulin resistance, obesity and altered energy metabolism have been linked to migraine susceptibility and severity in previous studies [7, 8]. Serotonin is also involved in metabolic regulation, including body-weight regulation, insulin secretion and glycaemic control [9, 10]. Similarly, lower haemoglobin, iron deficiency and nutritional factors may contribute to headache burden through impaired oxygen delivery, altered monoamine synthesis and changes in cortical excitability [11, 12]. These observations support the possibility that peripheral serotonergic variation in migraine may coexist with systemic metabolic and haematological changes.

Despite these developments, relatively few studies have examined serum serotonin together with routine glycaemic indices and haemoglobin in the same migraine cohort. Such routinely available markers may help contextualize peripheral serotonergic changes in adults with migraine, although they cannot establish causality in a cross-sectional design. Therefore, this study aimed to compare serum serotonin levels between adults with migraine and non-migraine controls and to explore the associations of serum serotonin with glycaemic indices, haemodynamic variables and haemoglobin.

Methods

Study design and setting

This was a comparative cross-sectional study conducted in a tertiary-care teaching hospital. Adults attending neurology and general outpatient clinics were screened and allocated to migraine or non-migraine control groups based on clinical assessment. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of SRM Medical College Hospital and Research Centre.

Participants and eligibility criteria

Adults aged 18–60 years were eligible for inclusion. The migraine group included participants with a diagnosis of migraine according to the International Classification of Headache Disorders, 3rd edition criteria, confirmed by a neurologist after clinical evaluation [13, 14]. The control group included adults without a history of recurrent primary or secondary headache disorders. The control group is described as non-migraine controls rather than age-matched controls because the final sample showed a significant age difference between groups.

The exclusion criteria were known diabetes mellitus or chronic kidney disease; thyroid dysfunction under treatment; anaemia or other haematological disorders already receiving therapy; chronic inflammatory or autoimmune disease; pregnancy or lactation; current use of drugs known to alter serotonin signalling, including antidepressants, antipsychotics or serotonergic analgesics; acute infection; recent surgery; or hospitalization within the preceding 4 weeks. Participants receiving major serotonin-modifying drugs were excluded. Detailed information on acute migraine medication use, preventive migraine therapy, antihypertensive medication and other drugs that may influence serotonin, blood pressure or pulse rate was not uniformly recorded; this was treated as residual confounding in interpretation.

Migraine clinical characterization

Migraine diagnosis was confirmed according to ICHD-3 criteria by a neurologist. The available clinical records captured the diagnosis of migraine but did not uniformly capture migraine subtype, aura status, chronic versus episodic classification, monthly headache days, attack frequency, attack severity, migraine duration, acute/preventive medication exposure or ictal/interictal sampling phase. The manuscript therefore avoids phenotype-specific conclusions. The availability of these migraine-characterization variables is summarized in Supplementary Table S1.

Clinical and anthropometric assessment

Demographic data, anthropometry and clinical variables were recorded using a structured pro forma. Height and weight were measured using calibrated equipment, and body mass index was calculated as kg/m². Blood pressure was measured in the sitting position after at least 5 min of rest using a validated automated device, consistent with office blood-pressure measurement recommendations [15]. Three readings were obtained at 1-2-minute intervals, and the average was used for analysis. Pulse rate was recorded at the time of blood-pressure measurement.

Blood sampling and laboratory measurements

Venous blood samples were collected from all participants between 08:00 and 10:00 h after an overnight fast of 8–10 h. Therefore, the glucose variable is reported as fasting blood glucose throughout the manuscript. Samples were processed in an ISO-accredited clinical laboratory according to standard operating procedures and internal quality-control protocols.

Fasting blood glucose and thyroid-stimulating hormone were measured using an automated chemistry analyser. Haemoglobin was measured using an automated haematology analyser. HbA1c was determined by high-performance liquid chromatography. Estimated average glucose was calculated using the American Diabetes Association/ADAG formula: . Because eAG is a positive deterministic linear transformation of HbA1c, correlation coefficients between serum serotonin and HbA1c are mathematically identical to those between serum serotonin and eAG. Therefore, HbA1c and eAG were interpreted as equivalent glycaemic indices for correlation-based analyses and were not treated as independent predictors in the same regression model.

Serum was separated and stored at -20 °C until serotonin analysis. Serum 5-hydroxytryptamine concentrations were quantified using a commercially available human serotonin/5-HT enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay kit based on a competitive immunoassay format, following the manufacturer’s protocol. Standards, quality-control material and participant samples were run in duplicate, absorbance was read according to the kit protocol, and concentrations were calculated from the standard curve and reported in ng/mL after applying the relevant dilution factor. Laboratory personnel were blinded to group allocation. The serotonin units and group values reported in this manuscript were verified against the original assay calculation sheets.

Data management and statistical analysis

Data were entered into a password-protected participant-level database and analysed using IBM SPSS Statistics, version 27. All inferential analyses reported in this manuscript, including Pearson correlations, partial correlations, age-adjusted analysis of covariance and multivariable regression, were performed from the participant-level dataset. No inferential analysis was computed from aggregated summary statistics. Where an earlier descriptive output had exported dispersion as standard error of the mean, SD = SEM × √n was used only to express descriptive values as mean ± standard deviation for manuscript presentation; this display conversion was not used to create participant-level observations or to calculate correlations, ANCOVA or regression results.

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test and visual inspection of histograms. Between-group comparisons were initially performed using independent-samples t-tests. Because age differed significantly between groups, age-adjusted analysis of covariance was applied with migraine status as the fixed factor and age as the covariate. Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated to assess associations between serum serotonin and clinical-biochemical variables. Partial correlations were performed after adjustment for pulse rate.

Multiple linear regression was used to identify independent predictors of serum serotonin, following general reporting principles for regression analysis in medical research [16]. Because estimated average glucose is mathematically derived from HbA1c, eAG and HbA1c were not entered together in the primary model. The primary model included nine predictors: age, BMI, fasting blood glucose, estimated average glucose, haemoglobin, thyroid-stimulating hormone, systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure and pulse rate. A sensitivity model replaced estimated average glucose with HbA1c. Model assumptions were assessed using residual plots, tolerance, variance inflation factor and Durbin-Watson statistics. Model fit was reported using R², adjusted R², F statistic and overall model p-value. A two-tailed p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

In this comparative cross-sectional study, adults with migraine showed lower serum serotonin levels and differences in selected glycaemic, haemodynamic and haematological variables compared with non-migraine controls. Serum serotonin was inversely correlated with HbA1c/eAG, fasting blood glucose, blood pressure and pulse rate, and positively correlated with haemoglobin. In the multivariable model, estimated average glucose, haemoglobin and age were independently associated with serum serotonin. These findings suggest that peripheral serotonergic variation in migraine may coexist with systemic metabolic and haematological differences; however, the cross-sectional design does not allow causal or mechanistic conclusions.

The observed lower serum serotonin levels in participants with migraine are consistent with the long-recognized involvement of serotonergic pathways in migraine biology [17, 18]. Serotonin receptors are expressed in trigeminovascular and brainstem pathways, and serotonergic mechanisms remain clinically relevant through the therapeutic actions of triptans, ditans and related agents [5, 6, 18]. Alterations across tryptophan-serotonin-related pathways have also been discussed in migraine-related mechanisms [19]. Nevertheless, serum serotonin requires cautious interpretation. Peripheral 5-hydroxytryptamine is a serum-based peripheral measure and may not directly reflect central serotonergic neurotransmission; therefore, the present findings represent peripheral serotonergic associations rather than direct evidence of central serotonin depletion [20, 21].

The magnitude of the between-group serum serotonin difference was large. Although the units and group values were checked against the original assay calculation sheets, a peripheral biomarker separation of this degree should be interpreted cautiously and replicated in independent cohorts, ideally using detailed assay reporting, external quality control and complementary analytical platforms such as liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry or platelet-poor plasma serotonin measurement. Accordingly, the present manuscript avoids presenting serum serotonin as a diagnostic biomarker and interprets the result as an exploratory group difference requiring validation.

The associations between serotonin and glycaemic indices are biologically plausible but exploratory. Serotonin participates in peripheral metabolic regulation, including glucose handling and insulin secretion [9, 10], while migraine has been linked to altered energy metabolism, insulin resistance and metabolic comorbidity [7, 8, 22, 23]. In the present study, HbA1c/eAG showed the strongest inverse glycaemic association with serum serotonin. Because estimated average glucose is derived from HbA1c, the two markers were interpreted as mathematically equivalent glycaemic indicators rather than independent biological constructs in correlation analyses or in the same primary regression model.

The positive association between haemoglobin and serotonin may reflect an interaction between haematological status and headache biology. Lower haemoglobin may contribute to headache susceptibility through reduced oxygen delivery, altered monoamine synthesis or nutritional factors, as suggested by previous work linking iron-deficiency anaemia and chronic headache disorders [11, 12]. This interpretation remains speculative because iron indices, ferritin, transferrin saturation, vitamin B12, folate and dietary intake were not measured. Although participants receiving treatment for known anaemia were excluded, undiagnosed iron deficiency or nutritional deficiency may have contributed to the observed haemoglobin difference.

The migraine group was older than the control group. This age pattern may reflect the hospital-based tertiary-care recruitment setting, in which adults with persistent, recurrent or complicated headache presentations may be more likely to seek specialist evaluation. It may also reflect coexistence of metabolic or haemodynamic comorbidities in adults attending neurology services. Therefore, the age distribution in this sample does not represent population prevalence patterns of migraine, which commonly peak in younger and middle adult age groups. Age was retained as an adjustment variable, and the lower serum serotonin level in the migraine group remained significant after age adjustment.

This study has several limitations. First, the single-centre cross-sectional design prevents causal inference and limits generalizability. Second, migraine phenotyping was limited; data on aura status, disease duration, monthly headache days, attack frequency, headache severity, chronic versus episodic migraine status and sampling during ictal or interictal phase were not uniformly available, although such clinical characterization is important for interpreting migraine biomarker studies [1, 13, 14]. Third, serum serotonin was measured at a single time point, and temporal fluctuations across migraine phases could not be assessed. Fourth, total serum serotonin was measured rather than platelet-poor plasma serotonin, central serotonin metabolites, tryptophan-kynurenine metabolites, CGRP or inflammatory markers. Fifth, unmeasured confounders including diet, physical activity, sleep, menstrual status, psychiatric symptoms, inflammatory phenotype, iron indices and nutritional status may have influenced the results. Sixth, although major serotonin-modifying drug exposure was excluded, detailed acute migraine medication use, preventive migraine therapy, antihypertensive medication and other drugs that could influence serotonin, blood pressure or pulse rate were not uniformly recorded. Finally, the unusually large serum serotonin group difference and the variability in anthropometric measures require confirmation in independent samples with full assay documentation and prospectively harmonized data capture.

Overall, the findings are exploratory and hypothesis-generating. They support further investigation of peripheral serotonin in relation to metabolic and haematological markers in migraine, but they do not establish serotonin as a diagnostic biomarker or prove a mechanistic pathway. Future multicentre longitudinal studies with age-matched controls, detailed migraine phenotyping, ictal/interictal sampling, medication documentation and broader biomarker panels are warranted to validate these observations.

Conclusions

Adults with migraine showed lower serum serotonin levels and differences in glycaemic, haemodynamic and haematological markers compared with non-migraine controls. Serum serotonin was associated with estimated average glucose, haemoglobin and age in the multivariable model. Because estimated average glucose is a mathematical transformation of HbA1c, these two glycaemic indices were interpreted as equivalent measures rather than independent predictors in the same model. Because of the cross-sectional design, age imbalance, single-time-point serotonin measurement, limited migraine phenotyping and the need for independent verification of the large serum serotonin difference, these findings are best interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating. Further longitudinal, multicentre studies are required to clarify whether peripheral serotonin is consistently associated with metabolic and haematological profiles in migraine.
