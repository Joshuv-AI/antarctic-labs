An Information-Theoretic Analysis of Threshold Group Testing
Remco van der Hofstad, Noela Müller, Connor Riddlesden

Abstract
We study the Threshold Group Testing (TGT) problem in the noiseless and non-adaptive setting, where the objective is to exactly recover a sparse binary vector from pooled tests, using as few tests as possible. In TGT, each test applied to a subset of items returns a positive outcome if the number of 1 1 ’s (‘ defective items ’) in that subset meets or exceeds a specified threshold, and has a negative outcome otherwise. We investigate how the complexity of TGT compares to that of Classical Group Testing (CGT), corresponding to the special case of the threshold equal to one, and analyse the impact of increasing the threshold on the required number of tests. Our main contribution is the derivation of a sharp information-theoretic phase transition at c inf TGT ​ k ​ log ⁡ ( n / k ) c_{\mathrm{inf}}^{\mathrm{TGT}}k\log(n/k) (non-adaptive) tests for TGT within the constant-column test design. The threshold constant c inf TGT c_{\mathrm{inf}}^{\mathrm{TGT}} is expressed as a function of the prevalence of defectives and the threshold value. Our upper bound is derived under an analytic assumption, and we verify that this assumption is satisfied for a threshold value of 2 2 . The value of c inf TGT c_{\mathrm{inf}}^{\mathrm{TGT}} reveals that TGT on the constant-column design has the same information-theoretic behaviour as CGT in the low‑prevalence regime. Yet, strikingly, at higher prevalences, the threshold leads to a significant reduction in the number of tests. On the other hand, we provide evidence that when the asymptotic proportion of defective items is positive, TGT actually becomes strictly harder than CGT (excluding trivial reductions).

1.2
Main Result and Motivating Question

Explicit results for both information theoretic bounds, and algorithms to match, have been found for classical group testing (
t=1

[
4
]
.
Indeed, there are several ways to

We mention two such reductions here.

Reduction via Dummy Items.

This idea assumes that a small set of

V_{\mathrm{dummy}}

{\boldsymbol{G}}
that guarantees with high probability (w.h.p.) exact recovery of
k
defectives using binary tests also for TGT: By

{\boldsymbol{G}}

{\boldsymbol{G}}

Here, and below, we say that a sequence of events

as

From a more practical perspective, this approach requires the dummy variables to be added prior to conducting the measurements, as tests cannot be updated deterministically given the test outcomes:
Since the exact number of defectives in each test is unknown, there is no reliable way to determine when a test outcome would change from negative to positive. In CGT on the other hand, adding dummy items with known status is safe because the test outcome can be updated deterministically. As a result, dummy items can only be used under restrictive conditions.

Reduction via Duplication.

This idea assumes that each item may be included multiple times in each test.
If this is the case, again, we may use any pooling scheme

In

We now turn our attention to TGT in the

In this section, we assume an i.i.d. prior on

independently with fixed and known probability
\alpha\in(0,1)

Here, Aldridge

Theorem 1.5

(
[

]
)
.

Consider non-adaptive binary group testing with an i.i.d. prior where each of the

Taken together, our comparison of TGT to CGT reveals the following qualitative difference between the sublinear and the linear regime:
In the

In contrast, in the linear regime, based on our results, TGT appears to be more challenging than CGT, and varying the threshold does not lead to improvements (without resorting to the obvious reductions).

1.3
Previous Work

The worst-case version of the threshold group testing problem, in which the goal is to identify the ground truth with probability exactly equal to one, has been studied since the work of Damaschke from 2006
[
17
]
. For constant
t
, Damaschke gives an initial information-theoretic upper bound on the number of tests as
O(k\log n)
for the adaptive two-round setting
[
17
]
in the sublinear regime and of
O(n)
tests for the adaptive two-round setting in the linear regime.
In the sublinear setting, De Marco, Jurdziński, Kowalski, Różański and Stachowiak
[
18
]
provide an upper bound of order
O(k^{2}\log(n/k))

for the non-adaptive case and constant
t
. Neither Damaschke nor De Marco et al. consider the computational complexity. Most recently, Bui et al.
[
7
]
provide the first known efficient algorithms for the problem, covering a range of thresholds and numbers of stages.

In this paper, we focus on the average-case setting where the number of defective items, or an upper bound on it, is assumed to be known. Key results include
[
9
,
31
]
. The first results by Chan et al. address an error probability of less than
\varepsilon

[
9
]
. They propose both a one-round and two-round procedure, using
(4\mathrm{e}^{8}\log 2/\pi^{2})\log(1/\varepsilon)k\sqrt{t}\log n+O\left(\log(1/\varepsilon)k\sqrt{t}\right)
and
16\mathrm{e}^{2}k\log n+O\left(\log(1/\varepsilon)k\right)
tests respectively, with decoding complexity of
O\left(n\log n+n\log(1/\varepsilon)\right)
. The results of Reisizadeh et al.
[
31
]
provide a single-round, non-adaptive algorithm requiring
O\left(k\sqrt{t}\log^{3}n\right)
tests, but the decoder requires a precomputed look-up matrix of size

look-up matrix indexed by all

possible defective sets.

Applications of Threshold Group Testing.

The applications of TGT pertain to the case in which an output only occurs when a threshold of the number of items displaying a certain characteristic is met. One such example is learning interpretable scorecards, which can be used in clinical prediction rules such as estimating the risk of a stroke
[
20
,
28
]
.
Chen and Fu
[
11
]
discuss the equivalence between TGT and graph search problems, where finding a hidden graph

involves taking a collection of edges and determining whether the associated subgraph contains at least

. TGT can also be used to identify triggers for reactive jamming attacks in multi-radio wireless sensors networks
[
32
]
. This concept of multiple-packet receptions extends the use of TGT into engineering and practice in energy detectors
[
10
,
21
,
22
]
.

There exist further generalisations of TGT, the first again being proposed by Damaschke, in which the concentration of the defective items in each test plays an important role
[

]
. This problem of
concentration group testing
seems to be very natural, for instance, the standard PCR tests for COVID required a lower limit of 100 copies of viral RNA per millilitre of the sample to provide a positive test result
[

]
. Elsewhere, Tsybakov
[
34
]
produced a model more closely related to
quantitative group testing
(QGT), in which again a threshold
t
is imposed, but if we let the exact number of defectives in the pool be
r
, then the output of a test will be
r
if

, and
t
otherwise. This problem is commonly known as

and has been studied previously
[
8
,
10
,
22
]
.

1.4
Discussion and Outlook

While classical group testing (
t=1
) relies on the fact that any negative test conclusively identifies all items as non-defective, threshold group testing introduces
ambiguity
: an item may consistently be hidden among other defectives in tests. Our results demonstrate that increasing the threshold
t
fundamentally alters the identifiability landscape. Importantly, Theorem
1.3
shows that, at least from an information-theoretic perspective, threshold group testing can actually require fewer tests than classical group testing in certain regimes. Here, the achievability result in Theorem
1.3
(b) holds unconditionally for
t=2
, and for
t\geq 3
under Conjecture
1.2
, which ensures that the relevant objective function does not exhibit irregularities that would break the first-moment analysis. The conjecture is strongly supported by numerical evidence. However, as the number of defectives transitions to the linear regime, Theorem
1.6
demonstrates that, in the absence of individual testing, the opposite is true, and more tests seem to be required for threshold group testing.

We emphasise that our conclusions are mainly based upon the restriction to test designs that do not employ multi-edges (even though we theoretically allow multi-edges in the sublinear constant-column design, in the relevant scaling of
m
and
\Delta
, there will be few of them). This restriction is crucial since allowing
structured
multi-edges can fundamentally alter the problem. For example, as explained in Section
1.2
, a simple reduction via duplication can reduce TGT to CGT in all regimes. Similar uses of structured multi-edges also fundamentally change other group testing problems: An example is quantitative group testing, where using such a method can technically allow for a solution
using a single test
(see
[
24
, Remark 1.3]
).

1.4.1
Sublinear Regime

We derive a sharp information-theoretic phase transition at
c_{\mathrm{inf}}^{\mathrm{TGT}}k\log(n/k)
(non-adaptive) tests
for TGT within the constant-column test design.
Our analysis quantifies how the required number of tests depends on the population size
n
, the prevalence
\theta
, and the threshold parameter
t
. In order to establish a sharp information-theoretic phase transition in the classical group testing problem, Coja-Oghlan et al.
[
13
]
drew inspiration from random constraint satisfaction problems. We employ similar techniques to account for the phenomenon of
disguised
defectives, which represents a central challenge unique to the threshold setting.

Proof Strategy.

The proof strategy of Theorem
1.3
for the large values of
\theta
follows the approach developed by Coja-Oghlan et al.
[
13
]
for the classical group testing problem. Following careful updates and adaptations of the proof strategy, we have generalised it for any constant
t
. For every result we adapt from
[
13
]
, we cite the corresponding one at the beginning. As
[
13
]
, our approach adapts moment bounds and expansion arguments from the random CSP literature regarding the rigidity of the solutions to manage these subtleties, ensuring uniqueness of the solution with high probability. A crucial element of the analysis is handling the so-called
lottery phenomenon
, where rare configurations allow certain defectives to remain insufficiently constrained
[
2
,
30
]
.

Theorem
1.3
is derived for constant-column test designs
\boldsymbol{G}
.
This limitation parallels the classical group testing setting, where the result for fixed designs was first established in
[
13
]
and later extended to arbitrary designs in
[
14
]
. We aimed to replicate this extension in our threshold setting, and for the most part the necessary adaptations were feasible. However, a key obstacle arises in
[
14
, Claim 3.11 (equation (3.14))]
, which relies on the FKG inequality. In TGT, the presence of both increasing and decreasing events relating to disguised items violates the conditions required for applying the FKG inequality. Note that in Section
5
, when dealing with the linear regime, the issue with the FKG inequality arises again. However, in this setting, we were not aiming for the exact constant and work with restrictions on the item and test degrees, so that a suitable weakening of the appropriate events is sufficient to obtain Theorem
1.6
.

Nevertheless, we conjecture that our bound in Theorem
1.3
represents the correct information-theoretic threshold even for arbitrary test designs, since the underlying heuristic argument is that enough tests are needed to eliminate disguised items. Establishing this rigorously remains an open problem.

1.4.2
Linear Regime

Theorem
1.6
establishes that when the number of defectives scales linearly in
n
,
t\geq 2
, and under the restrictions that items are not contained in too many tests, and tests do not contain too many items, the information-theoretic lower bound on tests is at least of order
n\log n
. We conjecture that this is, in fact, the correct result for arbitrary test designs without multi-edges, but have unfortunately been unable to show this.
This highlights a fundamental difference from the sublinear case, where substantial savings over classical group testing are achievable.

Moreover, in TGT without multi-edges, there is no immediate analogue of individual testing (a test that gives definite information with probability one). Consequently the change from the optimality of group to individual testing that appears in classical group testing does not arise in the same manner for
t\geq 2
. In classical group testing, the change naturally occurs when the number of tests required to ensure that each defective item is contained in at least one pivotal test exceeds the cost of individual testing. It is therefore natural to conjecture that, if a phase transition occurs in the linear regime for TGT from impossibility to achievability, it
is governed by the requirement that all defectives be covered by pivotal tests. However, due to the previously noted limitations in applying the FKG inequality, we have been unable to rigorously establish such a characterisation.

1.4.3
Future Directions

Several avenues remain open for further research, the most immediate being the verification of Conjecture
1.2
and the proof of a matching information-theoretic lower bound for arbitrary test designs in the sublinear regime to establish a sharp threshold for arbitrary test designs. Extending our bounds to
noisy
threshold group testing models would be of significant practical interest, while developing efficient non-adaptive algorithms that approach the theoretical limits described here is another promising direction.

A further challenge is to establish the existence of a similar phase transition in the linear regime, if mulit-edges are excluded.
Our results point towards the following compelling picture: in sparse regimes, threshold group testing can even outperform the classical setting, whereas in dense regimes, no asymptotic savings are possible. Clarifying the nature of this transition and constructing matching designs remain key directions for future work.

2.2.1
Repeated Inclusions

Our analysis is based upon designs with non-repeated inclusions, both for the sublinear and the linear case, even though we allow for repetitions in the sublinear case. However, in our settings, there will be few tests containing the same item twice, and drawing with replacement somewhat simplifies the proof, even though the same statements could be formulated in the setting of drawing without replacement. Whenever an argument relies on the fact that each item is contained in a certain number of tests, and multiplicities may create complications, we will explicitly argue that these multiplicities do not affect our arguments.

2.4
Uniqueness and Recovery

We study exact recovery of
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
from the measurement
\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}
and the pooling scheme
\mathbf{G}
. The key quantity governing recovery is the number of vectors consistent with the observations:
Let
S_{k}(\mathbf{G},\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}})
be the set of all vectors
\sigma
with Hamming weight
k
that give rise to the test result
\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}
on the pooling scheme
\mathbf{G}
. Further, denote
Z_{k}(\mathbf{G},\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}})=\left|{S_{k}(\mathbf{G},\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}})}\right|
. Proposition
2.10
shows that the posterior distribution of
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
given
\mathbf{G}
and
\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}
is the uniform distribution on
S_{k}(\mathbf{G},\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}})
.

Proposition 2.10

(
[
13
, Proposition II.1]
)
.

For all
\tau\in\{0,1\}^{\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}}
,

{\mathbb{P}}\left({\boldsymbol{\sigma}=\tau\mid\mathbf{G},\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}}\right)=\frac{\boldsymbol{1}\{\tau\in S_{k}(\mathbf{G},\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}})\}}{Z_{k}(\mathbf{G},\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}})}.

As a consequence, the information-theoretically optimal inference algorithm cannot do better than outputting a uniform sample of
S_{k}(\mathbf{G},\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}})
, and we obtain the following corollary.

Corollary 2.11

(
[
13
, Corollary II.2]
)
.

(1)

If
Z_{k}(\mathbf{G},\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}})=\omega(1)
w.h.p., then for any algorithm
\mathcal{A}
,

{\mathbb{P}}\left({\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{G},\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}},k)=\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\right)=o(1).

(2)

If
Z_{k}(\mathbf{G},\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}})=1
w.h.p., then there is an algorithm
\mathcal{A}
such that

{\mathbb{P}}\left({\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{G},\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}},k)=\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\right)=1-o(1).

2.5
Scaling of
\Delta

In this section, we analyse how the choice of the design parameter
\Delta
affects the balance between positive and negative tests.
Heuristically, having items appearing in too few tests yields mostly negative outcomes, and having items appearing in too many tests yields mostly positive outcomes; in either scenario, the observed test results are uninformative, preventing recovery of the ground truth. Recall the random variable
Z_{k}(\mathbf{G},\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}})
from the previous section.
We now justify that for
m=ck\log\frac{n}{k}
,
c>0
, only constant-order choices of
d
may lead to
Z_{k}(\mathbf{G},\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}})\not=\omega(1)
w.h.p..
The proof of the following lemma is similar to the proof of
[
13
, Lemma II.6]
.

Lemma 2.12
.

1.

If
m=ck\log(n/k)
and
\Delta=o\left({\log(n/k)}\right)
, then
Z_{k}(\mathbf{G},\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}})=\omega(1)
w.h.p.

2.

If
m=ck\log(n/k)
and
\Delta=\omega\left({\log(n/k)}\right)
, then
Z_{k}(\mathbf{G},\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}})=\omega(1)
w.h.p.

Proof.

We first consider the case
\Delta=\omega(\log(n/k))
and use a similar argument as in Lemma
2.9
to study the number of negative tests
{\boldsymbol{m}}_{<t}
. As before, we introduce the
auxiliary quantity

{\boldsymbol{m}}_{<t}^{\prime}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\boldsymbol{1}\{\boldsymbol{X}_{i}\leq t-1\}.

Then

\mathbb{E}[{\boldsymbol{m}}_{<t}^{\prime}\mid\boldsymbol{\Gamma}]=\sum_{i=1}^{m}{\mathbb{P}}\left({{\rm Bin}(\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{i},k/n)\leq t-1\mid\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}\right).

Moreover, for
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}
satisfying (
12
),

\mathbb{E}\left[{{\rm Bin}(\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{i},k/n)\mid\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}\right]=\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{i}k/n=\omega(1).

The Chernoff bound now further implies that for
n
large enough,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left({{\rm Bin}(\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{i},k/n)\leq t-1\mid\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}\right)\leq\exp\left({-\frac{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{i}k}{8n}}\right).

(19)

Summing over
i
, and still assuming that
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}
satisfies (
12
),

\mathbb{E}[{\boldsymbol{m}}_{<t}^{\prime}\mid\boldsymbol{\Gamma}]=\sum_{i=1}^{m}{\mathbb{P}}\left({{\rm Bin}(\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{i},k/n)\leq t-1\mid\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}\right)\leq m\exp\left({-\tfrac{k}{8n}\min_{i}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{i}}\right)=o(m).

An application of the Chernoff bound shows that
{\boldsymbol{m}}_{<t}^{\prime}
is concentrated around its mean. Again, the transition from
{\boldsymbol{m}}_{<t}^{\prime}
to
{\boldsymbol{m}}_{<t}
follows the same argument as (
18
) in Lemma
2.9
, using that
{\mathbb{P}}\left({{\mathcal{E}}|\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}\right)^{-1}=O\left({\sqrt{\Delta k}}\right)=O\left({n^{1+\theta}}\right)
for any choice of
\Delta
. Finally, by Remark
2.7
,
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}
satisfies (
12
) with sufficiently high probability, and we obtain that

{\boldsymbol{m}}_{<t}=o(m)

with probability
1-o(n^{-2})
.
Therefore w.h.p., almost all tests are positive. The rest of the proof now follows as in
[
13
, Lemma II.6]
by observing that w.h.p. over the choice of
{\boldsymbol{G}}
, asymptotically most configurations
\sigma\in\{0,1\}^{n}
give rise to at most
o(m)
negative tests. Going through all such ‘bad’ configurations, there will be at most

n\binom{m}{o(m)}=o(\binom{n}{k})
ones that share their test outcomes with at most
n-1
other configurations. Consequently,
Z_{k}({\boldsymbol{G}},\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}})\geq n
w.h.p. over the choice of
{\boldsymbol{G}}
and
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
.

The case
\Delta=o(\log(n/k))
on the other hand is an immediate consequence of
[
13
, Lemma II.6]
, which states that
{\boldsymbol{m}}_{\geq 1}=o(m)
. Since
{\boldsymbol{m}}_{\geq t}\leq{\boldsymbol{m}}_{\geq 1}
, also
{\boldsymbol{m}}_{\geq t}=o(m)
. Interchanging the positive and negative tests in the previous counting argument gives the claim for
\Delta=o(\log(n/k))
.
∎

2.6
Disguised Items in the Constant Column Design

Our arguments revolve around the notion of
disguised items
- those items whose labels, given
\boldsymbol{G},\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}
, can be changed without changing
\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}
. We now provide the corresponding definitions.

Definition 2.13

(Pivotal Tests)
.

Let
a_{j}
be a test and
x_{i}\in\partial a_{j}
. We say that
a_{j}
is a
pivotal test for item
x_{i}
if

\sum_{y\in\partial a_{j}\setminus\{x_{i}\}}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{y}=t-1.

Here, the notation
\partial a_{j}\setminus\{x_{i}\}
refers to the removal of all occurrences of item
x_{i}
from the multiset
\partial a_{j}
.

This notion accounts for the fact that changing the label of
x_{i}
would change the results
\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{j}
of all its pivotal tests.

Definition 2.14

(Disguised Items)
.

We say that item
x_{i}
is
disguised
if
x_{i}
appears exactly once in each
a_{j}\in\partial x_{i}
and
there exists no
a_{j}\in\partial x_{i}
which is pivotal for
x_{i}
. The set of all disguised items is denoted by
\boldsymbol{V}^{+}
.

Remark 2.15
.

For simplicity of analysis, in Definition
2.14
, we disregard items that appear more than once within a test. Consequently, our notion of disguised items is a
lower bound
on the actual number of items whose labels could be changed without affecting the test results.

2.7
Conditions Under Which Items Are Disguised

We continue with a study on the effect of
c
and
d
on the number of defective and non-defective disguised items.
Recall that
\boldsymbol{V}^{+}
denotes the set of all disguised items, and further define

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{V}_{0}^{+}=\{x_{i}\in\boldsymbol{V}^{+}\mid\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{x_{i}}=0\}\quad\quad\textrm{and}\quad\quad\boldsymbol{V}_{1}^{+}=\boldsymbol{V}^{+}\setminus\boldsymbol{V}_{0}^{+}.

(20)

The following Proposition is similar in spirit to
[
13
, Proposition II.3]
.

Proposition 2.16
.

Let
c,d=\Theta(1)
. Then, the following statements hold w.h.p.:

(i)

If
k\left({1-(d/c)^{t-1}\exp(-d/c)/(t-1)!}\right)^{\Delta}\geq n^{\Omega(1)}
, then
\left|{\boldsymbol{V}_{1}^{+}}\right|=n^{\Omega(1)}
.

(ii)

If
k\left({1-(d/c)^{t-1}\exp(-d/c)/(t-1)!}\right)^{\Delta}=o(1)
,   then
\left|{\boldsymbol{V}_{1}^{+}}\right|=o(1)
.

(iii)

If
k\left({1-(d/c)^{t-1}\exp(-d/c)/(t-1)!}\right)^{\Delta}\geq n^{\Omega(1)}
, then
\left|{\boldsymbol{V}_{0}^{+}}\right|=n^{\Omega(1)}
.

(iv)

Fix
d>0
and let
c_{2}^{*}(d,\theta)=\inf\{c>0:c\geq c_{2}(d/c,\theta)\}
.
If
c<c_{2}^{*}(d,\theta)
,
then
\left|{\boldsymbol{V}_{0}^{+}}\right|,\left|{\boldsymbol{V}_{1}^{+}}\right|=n^{\Omega(1)}
.

(v)

Suppose that
c>c_{\mathrm{inf}}^{\mathrm{TGT}}(n,\theta,t)
and
d=r^{*}(\theta)c
. Then
\left|{\boldsymbol{V}_{1}^{+}}\right|=o(1)
.

Proof.

Proof of (i):

Recall from Definition
2.14
that item
x_{i}
is
disguised
if it is included exactly once in each of its incident tests
a_{j}\in\partial x_{i}
and none of them is pivotal for
x_{i}
.
To estimate
|\boldsymbol{V}_{1}^{+}|
, we first work without the multiplicity restriction.
Let

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{U}_{1}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathds{1}\left\{{\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{i}=1,\forall a\in\partial x_{i}:\boldsymbol{Y}_{a}\not=t}\right\}=\sum_{x\in\boldsymbol{V}_{1}}\boldsymbol{1}\left\{\sum_{a\in\partial x}\boldsymbol{1}\{\boldsymbol{Y}_{a}=t\}=0\right\}

be the number of defective items that do not participate in any test with exactly
t
defective items.
Since every disguised defective item is counted by the corresponding indicator in
\boldsymbol{U}_{1}
,

\boldsymbol{U}_{1}
provides an upper bound on the number
of disguised defectives.

In the present test design, every item has fixed degree
\Delta=d\log(n/k)
.
Given
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}
and
\boldsymbol{m}_{t}
, for an item to be in
\boldsymbol{U}_{1}
, it must choose its
\Delta
edges from the other
k\Delta-t\boldsymbol{m}_{t}
which are not incident to a pivotal test.
By Lemma
2.9
, with probability
1-o(n^{-7})
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{U}_{1}\mid\boldsymbol{\Gamma},\boldsymbol{m}_{t}]

\displaystyle=k\binom{k\Delta-t\boldsymbol{m}_{t}}{\Delta}\binom{k\Delta}{\Delta}^{-1}=k\frac{(k\Delta-t\boldsymbol{m}_{t})_{{\Delta}}}{(k\Delta)_{{\Delta}}}

\displaystyle=\left({1+n^{-\Omega(1)}}\right)k(1-t\boldsymbol{m}_{t}/k\Delta)^{\Delta}

\displaystyle=\left({1+n^{-\Omega(1)}}\right)k\left({1-\frac{(d/c)^{t-1}}{(t-1)!}\exp(-d/c)}\right)^{\Delta}.

(21)

Similarly, w.h.p.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{U}_{1}^{2}\mid\boldsymbol{\Gamma},\boldsymbol{m}_{t}]

\displaystyle=k\binom{k\Delta-t\boldsymbol{m}_{t}}{\Delta}\binom{k\Delta}{\Delta}^{-1}+k(k-1)\binom{k\Delta-t\boldsymbol{m}_{t}}{2\Delta}\binom{k\Delta}{2\Delta}^{-1}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{U}_{1}\mid\boldsymbol{\Gamma},\boldsymbol{m}_{t}]+\left({1+n^{-\Omega(1)}}\right)\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{U}_{1}\mid\boldsymbol{\Gamma},\boldsymbol{m}_{t}]^{2}.

In the present case, we assume that
k\left({1-(d/c)^{t-1}\exp(-d/c)/(t-1)!}\right)^{\Delta}\geq n^{\Omega(1)}
. Therefore, by Chebyshev’s inequality, w.h.p.

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{U}_{1}

\displaystyle\in\left[{\left({1\pm n^{-\Omega(1)}}\right)k\left({1-\frac{1}{(t-1)!}\left({\frac{d}{c}}\right)^{t-1}\exp(-d/c)}\right)^{\Delta}}\right].

(22)

Thus, (
22
) implies there exists
\kappa>0
such that
\boldsymbol{U}_{1}\geq n^{\kappa}
w.h.p.

Let now
\boldsymbol{R}_{1}
be the number of defective items that are contained in at least one test with multiplicity greater than one such that
\boldsymbol{U}_{1}\geq\left|{\boldsymbol{V}_{1}^{+}}\right|\geq\boldsymbol{U}_{1}-\boldsymbol{R}_{1}
.
The probability that an item appears in a specific test at least twice is upper-bounded by
(\Delta/m)^{2}
.
Taking the union bound over all defective items and all tests yields

\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{R}_{1}]\leq km\left({\frac{\Delta}{m}}\right)^{2}=O(\log n).

By Markov’s inequality,

{\mathbb{P}}\left({\boldsymbol{R}_{1}\geq\tfrac{1}{2}n^{\kappa}}\right)\leq\frac{2\,\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{R}_{1}]}{n^{\kappa}}=o(1).

Hence, w.h.p. we simultaneously have

\boldsymbol{U}_{1}\geq n^{\kappa}
and
\boldsymbol{R}_{1}\leq\tfrac{1}{2}n^{\kappa}
, and therefore

\left|{\boldsymbol{V}_{1}^{+}}\right|\geq\boldsymbol{U}_{1}-\boldsymbol{R}_{1}\geq\tfrac{1}{2}n^{\kappa}=n^{\Omega(1)}\quad\text{w.h.p.}

Proof of (ii):

Let
\boldsymbol{U}_{1}
be as in
part (i) such that
\boldsymbol{U}_{1}\geq\left|{\boldsymbol{V}_{1}^{+}}\right|
. If
k\left({1-\tfrac{(d/c)^{t-1}}{(t-1)!}\exp(-d/c)}\right)^{\Delta}=o(1)
,
\left|{\boldsymbol{V}_{1}^{+}}\right|=o(1)
w.h.p. follows from (
21
) and Markov’s inequality.

Proof of (iii):

The proof of (iii) follows an almost identical argument to the proof of (i), but with the adaptation that we first study the tests with exactly
t-1
defective items. Therefore, we relegate this proof to Appendix
B
.

Proof of (iv):

Fix
d>0
. Then

\displaystyle k\left({1-(d/c)^{t-1}\exp(-d/c)/(t-1)!}\right)^{\Delta}=n^{\theta+(1-\theta)d\log\left({(1-\exp(-d/c)\frac{(d/c)^{t-1}}{(t-1)!}}\right)}.

We will show that for
c<c_{2}^{*}(d,\theta)
the exponent is positive, whereby the claim follows from parts (i) and (iii). For this, observe that

\displaystyle\theta+(1-\theta)d\log\left({(1-\exp(-d/c)\frac{(d/c)^{t-1}}{(t-1)!}}\right)>0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad c<c_{2}(d/c,\theta).

By definition of
c_{2}^{*}(d,\theta)
, for
c<c_{2}^{*}(d,\theta)
, the right hand side is satisfied, so that the claim follows.

Proof of (v):

Suppose that
c>c_{\mathrm{inf}}^{\mathrm{TGT}}(n,\theta,t)
and
d=r^{*}(\theta)c
. Then

k\left({1-\frac{\left({\frac{d}{c}}\right)^{t-1}\exp(-d/c)}{(t-1)!}}\right)^{d\log(n/k)}=o(1)

is implied by
\theta+(1-\theta)r^{*}c\log(1-{\mathbb{P}}\left({{\rm Po}(r^{*})=t-1}\right))<0
, which is satisfied since
c>c_{\mathrm{inf}}^{\mathrm{TGT}}
.
By part (ii), we conclude that
\left|{\boldsymbol{V}_{1}^{+}}\right|=o(1)
. ∎

3
Information-Theoretic Lower Bound

We first outline the derivation of Theorem
1.3
(a),
asserting that w.h.p. the ground truth
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
cannot be uniquely identified when
m<(1-\varepsilon)c_{\mathrm{inf}}^{\mathrm{TGT}}k\log\frac{n}{k}
.

Proposition 3.1

(Theorem
1.3
(a))
.

If
m<(1-\varepsilon)c_{\mathrm{inf}}^{\mathrm{TGT}}k\log\frac{n}{k}
, then there does not exist any algorithm that, given
{\boldsymbol{G}},\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}},k
, outputs
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
with a non-vanishing probability.

The form (
1
) of
c_{\mathrm{inf}}^{\mathrm{TGT}}
as a maximum over two different expressions already hints at two different mechanisms being at work. In this sense, we establish the information-theoretic lower bound by two different considerations.

Section
3.1
employs a rather classical information-theoretic argument
[
4
]
, but adapted to the constant-column design.
Section
3.2
on the other hand builds on ideas of Coja-Oghlan et al.
[
13
]
:
We use Proposition
2.16
to show that if the number of tests
m
is less than
c_{\mathrm{inf}}^{\mathrm{TGT}}k\log\frac{n}{k}
, both disguised defectives and disguised non-defectives occur abundantly w.h.p. We will use these items to construct assignments that are consistent with the test results, but different from
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
.
The two parts together yield our information-theoretic lower bound,
as concluded in Section
3.3
.

3.1
Information-Theoretic Lower Bound for Constant-Column Design

We begin by modifying a standard counting argument to our
\Delta
-regular test setting.
While the general strategy is well-known (see e.g.
[
4
]
), the application to our test design appears to be new.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\theta\in(0,1)
,
d>0
,
{\boldsymbol{G}}(n,m,\Delta)
be the item-regular test design with
\Delta=d\log(n/k)
and
c_{1}^{*}=c_{1}^{*}(d)=\inf\left\{{c>0:c\geq c_{1}(d/c)}\right\}
. Then for any
\varepsilon>0
it holds true that if

\displaystyle m\leq\left({1-\varepsilon}\right)c_{1}^{*}(d)k\log\frac{n}{k},

(23)

then every function
\mathcal{A}_{{\boldsymbol{G}}}:\{0,1\}^{m}\to\{0,1\}^{n}
has
{\mathbb{P}}\left({\mathcal{A}_{{\boldsymbol{G}}}({\boldsymbol{G}},\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}})=\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\right)=o(1)
.

Proof.

W.l.o.g. assume that
c_{1}^{*}(d)<\infty
and that
m=\left({1-\varepsilon}\right)c_{1}^{*}(d)k\log\frac{n}{k}
.
Let the random variables
\boldsymbol{Y}=(\boldsymbol{Y}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{Y}_{m})
and
\boldsymbol{X}=(\boldsymbol{X}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{X}_{m})
be defined as in Lemma
2.8
. As the test results can be read off from the numbers of defective items per test, we can write
\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}=f(\boldsymbol{Y})
for an appropriate function
f
. Let
\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\prime}:=f(\boldsymbol{X})
for the same function
f
. Thus Lemma
2.8
implies that
\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\prime}\mid{\mathcal{E}}
given
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}
. Hence, for any
A\subseteq\{0,1\}^{m}
,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\in A\mid\boldsymbol{\Gamma})={\mathbb{P}}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\prime}\in A\mid\boldsymbol{\Gamma},{\mathcal{E}})=\frac{{\mathbb{P}}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\prime}\in A,{\mathcal{E}}\mid\boldsymbol{\Gamma})}{{\mathbb{P}}({\mathcal{E}}\mid\boldsymbol{\Gamma})}\leq\frac{{\mathbb{P}}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\prime}\in A\mid\boldsymbol{\Gamma})}{{\mathbb{P}}({\mathcal{E}}\mid\boldsymbol{\Gamma})}.

(24)

Using
{\mathbb{P}}({\mathcal{E}}\mid\boldsymbol{\Gamma})=\Omega(1/\sqrt{\Delta k})
, we obtain

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\in A\mid\boldsymbol{\Gamma})\leq O(\sqrt{\Delta k}){\mathbb{P}}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\prime}\in A\mid\boldsymbol{\Gamma}).

(25)

Conditioned on
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}
, the random variables
\boldsymbol{X}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{X}_{m}
are independent, hence

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\prime}=\hat{\sigma}\mid\boldsymbol{\Gamma})=\prod_{i=1}^{m}{\mathbb{P}}\left({\boldsymbol{1}\{\boldsymbol{X}_{i}<t\}=\hat{\sigma}_{i}\mid\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{i}}\right),\qquad\hat{\sigma}\in\{0,1\}^{m}.

(26)

Conditionally on
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}
, let
\boldsymbol{Z}=(\boldsymbol{Z}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{Z}_{m})
be a random vector with independent
{\rm Be}(\boldsymbol{p}_{i})
components, where

\boldsymbol{p}_{i}={\mathbb{P}}\left({{\rm Bin}(\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{i},k/n)\leq t-1\mid\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{i}}\right).

Then

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\prime}=\hat{\sigma}\mid\boldsymbol{\Gamma})=\prod_{i=1}^{m}{\mathbb{P}}\left({\boldsymbol{Z}_{i}=\hat{\sigma}_{i}\mid\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{i}}\right).

(27)

Let
p_{\boldsymbol{Z}_{i}|\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{i}}(\cdot)={\mathbb{P}}\left({\boldsymbol{Z}_{i}=\cdot\mid\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{i}}\right)
denote the p.m.f. of
\boldsymbol{Z}_{i}
conditioned on
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{i}
. Then for each outcome of
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}
,
(-\log p_{\boldsymbol{Z}_{i}|\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{i}}(\boldsymbol{Z}_{i}))_{i\in[m]}
is a sequence of independent random variables, where

-\log p_{\boldsymbol{Z}_{i}|\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{i}}(\boldsymbol{Z}_{i})
takes values in an interval of length
\boldsymbol{L}_{i}=|\log(\boldsymbol{p}_{i}/(1-\boldsymbol{p}_{i}))|
.
Hoeffding’s inequality thus yields

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\!\left({\left|{\mathbb{E}\!\left[{\sum_{i=1}^{m}\log p_{\boldsymbol{Z}_{i}|\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{i}}(\boldsymbol{Z}_{i})\Big|\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}\right]-\sum_{i=1}^{m}\log p_{\boldsymbol{Z}_{i}|\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{i}}(\boldsymbol{Z}_{i})}\right|>\varepsilon^{\prime}m\Big|\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}\right)\leq 2\exp\left({-\frac{2(\varepsilon^{\prime})^{2}m^{2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{m}\boldsymbol{L}_{i}^{2}}}\right).

Moreover,
\sum_{i=1}^{m}\boldsymbol{L}_{i}^{2}=O(m)
for each
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}
satisfying (
12
), so that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left({\left|{\mathbb{E}\left[{\sum_{i=1}^{m}\log p_{\boldsymbol{Z}_{i}|\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{i}}(\boldsymbol{Z}_{i})\Big|\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}\right]-\sum_{i=1}^{m}\log p_{\boldsymbol{Z}_{i}|\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{i}}(\boldsymbol{Z}_{i})}\right|>\varepsilon^{\prime}m\Big|\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}\right)\leq\exp\left({-\Omega(m)}\right).

(28)

Next, set

p={\mathbb{P}}\left({{\rm Po}(d/c_{1}^{*}(d))\leq t-1}\right).

Then, by the triangle inequality and mean value theorem,

\displaystyle\left|{\mathbb{E}\left[{\sum_{i=1}^{m}-\log p_{\boldsymbol{Z}_{i}|\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{i}}(\boldsymbol{Z}_{i})\Big|\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}\right]-mH(p)}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left|{H(\boldsymbol{p}_{i})-H(p)}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left|{H^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i})\cdot\left({\boldsymbol{p}_{i}-p}\right)}\right|,

(29)

where
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i}\in\left({\boldsymbol{p}_{i}\wedge p,\boldsymbol{p}_{i}\vee p}\right)
.
By
[
26
, Theorem 2.10]
, for fixed
\Gamma
, the total variation distance between a binomial and Poisson variable is upper bounded by

\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\left({{\rm Bin}(\Gamma,k/n),{\rm Po}(\Gamma k/n)}\right)\leq\Gamma(k/n)^{2}.

Again, it follows from (
3.1
) that for
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}
satisfying (
12
)

\displaystyle\left|{\mathbb{E}\left[{\sum_{i=1}^{m}-\log p_{\boldsymbol{Z}_{i}|\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{i}}(\boldsymbol{Z}_{i})\Big|\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}\right]-mH(p)}\right|=O\left({m\cdot\frac{\Delta n}{m}\cdot\left({\frac{k}{n}}\right)^{2}}\right)=o(m).

(30)

Equations (
28
) and (
30
) together imply that for
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}
satisfying (
12
) and any
\varepsilon^{\prime}>0
,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left({\left|{\sum_{i=1}^{m}-\log p_{\boldsymbol{Z}_{i}|\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{i}}(\boldsymbol{Z}_{i})-mH(p)}\right|>\varepsilon^{\prime}m\Big|\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}\right)\leq\exp\left({-\Omega(m)}\right).

(31)

Finally, let
\mathcal{A}_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{\Gamma})=\left\{{\hat{\sigma}\in\{0,1\}^{m}:\left|{\sum_{i=1}^{m}-\log p_{\boldsymbol{Z}_{i}|\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{i}}(\hat{\sigma})-mH(p)}\right|\leq\varepsilon^{\prime}m}\right\}
.
As for all
\hat{\sigma}\in\mathcal{A}_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{\Gamma})
,
{\mathbb{P}}\left({\boldsymbol{Z}=\hat{\sigma}|\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}\right)\geq\exp(-m(H(p)-\varepsilon^{\prime}))
, we obtain that, for all
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}
,

\displaystyle|\mathcal{A}_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{\Gamma})|\leq\exp\left({m(H(p)+\varepsilon^{\prime})}\right).

(32)

Substituting
m=(1-\varepsilon)c_{1}^{*}(d)k\log(n/k)
and using the definition of
c_{1}^{*}(d)
and continuity, we have
c_{1}^{*}(d)H(p)=c_{1}^{*}(d)/c_{1}(d/c_{1}^{*}(d))=1
. Choosing
\varepsilon^{\prime}=\varepsilon/(2c_{1}^{*}(d))
,
we obtain

\displaystyle m(H(p)+\varepsilon^{\prime})=(1-\varepsilon)c_{1}^{*}(d)k\log(n/k)\left({H(p)+\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2c_{1}^{*}(d)}}\right)\leq(1-\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2})k\log(n/k).

(33)

Hence for
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}
satisfying (
12
), w.h.p.
\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\prime}
takes at most
\exp((1-\varepsilon/2)k\log(n/k))
distinct values. Thanks to (
25
), this transfers to
\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}
.

Finally, fix
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}
that satisfies (
12
), which is independent of
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
. By Lemma
A.5
, the number of potential values of the ground truth
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
is
\binom{n}{k}=\exp(k\log(n/k)(1+o(1)))
. By the pigeonhole principle, w.h.p.  there exists a subset of
\{0,1\}^{n}
of size at least
\exp\left({(\varepsilon/2)k\log(n/k)(1-o(1))}\right)
that yields the same test results.
Therefore, as any inference rule
\mathcal{A}_{{\boldsymbol{G}}}
can only be correct on at most one of these values,

{\mathbb{P}}(\mathcal{A}_{{\boldsymbol{G}}}({\boldsymbol{G}},\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}})=\boldsymbol{\sigma})\leq\exp(-(\varepsilon/2)k\log(n/k)(1-o(1)))+o(1)=o(1).\qed

3.2
Disguised Items

For the second part of the lower bound, we inspect the threshold upon which the item-regular pooling scheme
{\boldsymbol{G}}(n,m,\Delta)
displays disguised items.
An argument of this form also appears in
[

, Corollary IV.2]
:

Corollary 3.3
.

Let
\theta\in(0,1)
,
d>0
,
{\boldsymbol{G}}(n,m,\Delta)
be the item-regular test design with
\Delta=d\log(n/k)
and
c_{2}^{*}=c_{2}^{*}(d,\theta)=\inf\left\{{c>0:c\geq c_{2}(d/c,\theta)}\right\}
. Then for any
\varepsilon>0
it holds true that if

\displaystyle m\leq\left({1-\varepsilon}\right)c_{2}^{*}(d)k\log\frac{n}{k},

(34)

every function
\mathcal{A}_{{\boldsymbol{G}}}:\{0,1\}^{m}\to\{0,1\}^{n}
has
{\mathbb{P}}\left({\mathcal{A}_{{\boldsymbol{G}}}({\boldsymbol{G}},\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}})=\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\right)=o(1)
.

Proof.

Suppose that
m
is as in the statement of the corollary, such that
c
from (
11
) satisfies
c<c_{2}(d/c,\theta)
. By Proposition
2.16
(
iv
), w.h.p.,
|\boldsymbol{V}_{0}^{+}|,|\boldsymbol{V}_{1}^{+}|=n^{\Omega(1)}
. The existence of large sets
\boldsymbol{V}_{0}^{+}
and
\boldsymbol{V}_{1}^{+}
guarantees that, starting from the ground truth
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
, we can select one item from each set and swap their labels to obtain a competing solution
\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\prime}
that produces the same test results
\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}
under the pooling scheme
\boldsymbol{G}
: For any
x_{i}\in\boldsymbol{V}_{0}^{+}
and
x_{j}\in\boldsymbol{V}_{1}^{+}
, the assignment
\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\prime}
defined by interchanging the labels of
x_{i}
and
x_{j}
(i.e., setting
\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\prime}_{x_{i}}=1
,
\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\prime}_{x_{j}}=0
and
\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\prime}_{x_{\ell}}=\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{x_{\ell}}
for

) has Hamming weight
k
, and yields the same test outcomes as

.
Hence,

Z_{k}({\boldsymbol{G}},\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}})\geq|\boldsymbol{V}_{0}^{+}\times\boldsymbol{V}_{1}^{+}|=n^{\Omega(1)}\quad w.h.p.\qed

Proof of Proposition
3.1

Let
{\boldsymbol{G}}={\boldsymbol{G}}(n,m,\Delta)
be the constant-column test design with item-degree
\Delta
. By Lemma
2.12
, it is sufficient to restrict to
\Delta
of the form

for

. For fixed

imply that no inference procedure recovers the ground truth correctly with non-vanishing probability if

We now address the achievability result from Theorem

(b), the derivation of the information-theoretic upper bound.
Our objective is to prove that for a certain choice of
\Delta
,
Z_{k}({\boldsymbol{G}},\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}})=1
w.h.p., indicating that
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
is the sole assignment compatible with the observed test outcomes.

Proposition 4.1
.

Fix
0<\theta<1
,
t\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
and
\varepsilon>0
and recall the unique optimiser
r^{*}
of (
1
). Assume that either
t=2
or that
t\geq 3
and Conjecture
1.2
holds. Then for any number of tests
m=ck\log(n/k)
with
c\geq(1+\varepsilon)c_{\mathrm{inf}}^{\mathrm{TGT}}(n,\theta,t)

{\boldsymbol{G}}(n,m,\Delta)
with

that, given
{\boldsymbol{G}},\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}},k=n^{\theta}

Let us now outline the philosophy behind the proof of Proposition
4.1
.
We define the
overlap

\displaystyle\langle\sigma,\tau\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{1}\left\{{\sigma_{x_{i}}=\tau_{x_{i}}=1}\right\}

(35)

between two assignments

\displaystyle Z_{k,\ell}(\mathbf{G},\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}})=\left|{\{\sigma\in S_{k}(\mathbf{G},\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}):\langle\boldsymbol{\sigma},\sigma\rangle=\ell\}}\right|,\qquad\ell\in\{0,\dots,k\},

this yields the decomposition

\displaystyle Z_{k}(\mathbf{G},\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}})=\sum_{\ell=0}^{k}Z_{k,\ell}(\mathbf{G},\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}).

Our proof of Proposition

is structured in two parts, which are similar in spirit to
[
13
, Propositions III.1 and III.2]
.

0<\theta<1
,

and
\varepsilon>0

r^{*}
of (

). Suppose that

with

and consider the constant column pooling scheme
{\boldsymbol{G}}(n,m,\Delta)
with

(a)

If

for all

.

(b)

for all

Proposition 4.3

(No Large Overlap w.h.p.)
.

Fix
0<\theta<1
,

and
\varepsilon>0

r^{*}
of (

). Suppose that
m=ck\log(n/k)
with

with

(b).

In Section
4.1
, we prove Lemma
4.2
by a moment calculation to establish that, w.h.p., there are no competing solutions exhibiting low overlap with

. Subsequently, in Section

, we prove Proposition

The strategy in
[

, Section III]
for classical group testing serves as a direct inspiration for our approach. Building upon this, we extend the methodology to the threshold group testing setting, introducing several important modifications. These include new expressions involving total variation distance between the probability distributions that allow us to handle more general test outcomes.

4.1
Small Overlap - Truncated First Moment

In this section, we prove Lemma
4.2
.
We begin by studying the first moment of
Z_{k,\ell}({\boldsymbol{G}},\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}})
, first defined in (
36
), for the regime when the overlap
\ell
is relatively small. We first establish the following upper bound, that will be analysed later.

Lemma 4.4
.

Given
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}
, let
(\boldsymbol{B}_{i}(\ell))_{i\in[m]}
be a sequence of independent random variables with
\boldsymbol{B}_{i}(\ell)\sim{\rm Bin}(\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{i},\ell/n)
and let
(\boldsymbol{M}^{(z)}_{i}(\ell))_{i\in[m],z\in\{0,\ldots,t-1\}}
be a sequence of independent random variables with

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{M}^{(z)}_{i}(\ell)

\displaystyle=\left({\boldsymbol{M}^{(z),1}_{i}(\ell),\boldsymbol{M}^{(z),2}_{i}(\ell),\boldsymbol{M}^{(z),3}_{i}(\ell)}\right)

\displaystyle\sim{\rm Mult}\left({\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{i}-z;\frac{k-\ell}{n-\ell},\frac{k-\ell}{n-\ell},\frac{n-2k+\ell}{n-\ell}}\right).

Finally, let

\mathcal{P}=\{i\in[m]:\boldsymbol{B}_{i}(k)\geq t\}
and
\mathcal{N}=[m]\setminus\mathcal{P}
.
For
i\in[m]
, let
q_{i}:={\mathbb{P}}\left({\boldsymbol{B}_{i}(k)\leq t-1|\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}\right)
, and let

\displaystyle p_{i,\ell,\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{z=0}^{t-1}{\mathbb{P}}\left({\boldsymbol{B}_{i}(\ell)=z|\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}\right){\mathbb{P}}\left({\boldsymbol{M}^{(z),1}_{i}\leq t-1-z,\boldsymbol{M}^{(z),2}_{i}\leq t-1-z|\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}\right).

(37)

Let the events
\mathcal{M},\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
be defined as
\mathcal{M}:=\left\{{\boldsymbol{m}_{\geq t}\in\left[{\left({1\pm n^{-\Omega(1)}}\right)m{\mathbb{P}}\left({{\rm Po}(d/c)\geq t}\right)}\right]}\right\}
and
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}:=\left\{{\left|{\mathcal{P}}\right|\in\left[{\left({1\pm n^{-\Omega(1)}}\right)m{\mathbb{P}}\left({{\rm Po}(d/c)\geq t}\right)}\right]}\right\}
.
Then for any
0\leq\ell\leq(1-1/\log{n})k
.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[{\boldsymbol{1}_{\mathcal{M}}Z_{k,\ell}(\mathbf{G},\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}})\mid\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\quad

\displaystyle O\left({\left({\Delta k}\right)^{3/2}}\right)\binom{k}{\ell}\binom{n-k}{k-\ell}\mathbb{E}\left[{\boldsymbol{1}_{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}}\prod_{i\in\mathcal{N}}\left({\frac{p_{i,\ell,\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}}{q_{i}}}\right)\prod_{i\in\mathcal{P}}\left({\frac{1-2q_{i}+p_{i,\ell,\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}}{1-q_{i}}}\right)|\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}\right].

(38)

Proof.

The aim of the lemma is to characterise assignments
\sigma
that have Hamming weight
k
and overlap exactly
\ell
with
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
. Even though
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
is random and
\sigma
depends on
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
, due to symmetry we may assume that
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{x_{i}}=\boldsymbol{1}\{i\leq k\}=\boldsymbol{1}_{[k]}
and
\sigma_{x_{i}}=\boldsymbol{1}\{i\leq\ell\}+\boldsymbol{1}\{k<i\leq 2k-\ell\}
. Then for this choice of
\sigma
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[{\boldsymbol{1}_{\mathcal{M}}Z_{k,\ell}(\mathbf{G},\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}})\mid\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}\right]=\binom{k}{\ell}\binom{n-k}{k-\ell}{\mathbb{P}}\left({\mathcal{M},\forall a\in[m]:\hat{\sigma}_{a}=(\hat{\boldsymbol{1}}_{[k]})_{a}|\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}\right).

(39)

We will use a balls and bins argument, where we think of each test
a_{i}
as a bin of capacity
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{i}
, and of each clone
(x_{i},h)
,
h\in[\Delta]
, of an item as a ball labelled
(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{x_{i}},\sigma_{x_{i}})\in\{0,1\}^{2}
.
Creating the graph
{\boldsymbol{G}}
is then equal in distribution to matching the
\Delta n
balls uniformly into the bins given their capacities.
For
i\in[m]
and
j\in[\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{i}]
, we let
\boldsymbol{A}_{i,j}=(\boldsymbol{A}_{i,j,1},\boldsymbol{A}_{i,j,2})\in\{0,1\}^{2}
be the label of the
j
th ball that ends up in bin number
i
. Observe that we have
\ell\Delta
balls with label
(1,1)
,
(k-\ell)\Delta
balls with label
(1,0)
,
(k-\ell)\Delta
balls with label
(0,1)
and
(n-2k+\ell)\Delta
balls with label
(0,0)
. Moreover,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left({\mathcal{M},\forall a\in[m]:\hat{\sigma}_{a}=(\hat{\boldsymbol{1}}_{[k]})_{a}|\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left({\mathcal{M},\forall i\in[m]:\boldsymbol{1}\left\{{\sum_{i=1}^{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{i}}\boldsymbol{A}_{i,j,1}\leq t-1}\right\}=\boldsymbol{1}\left\{{\sum_{i=1}^{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{i}}\boldsymbol{A}_{i,j,2}\leq t-1}\right\}|\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}\right).

(40)

To facilitate the analysis of the experiment, we introduce a new set of i.i.d. random variables
(\boldsymbol{A}_{i,j}^{\prime})_{(i,j)\in\bigcup_{a=1}^{m}\{a\}\times[\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{a}]}=((\boldsymbol{A}_{i,j,1}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{A}_{i,j,2}^{\prime}))_{(i,j)\in\bigcup_{a=1}^{m}\{a\}\times[\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{a}]}
with distribution

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\!\left({\boldsymbol{A}_{i,j}^{\prime}=(1,1)}\right)

\displaystyle=\ell/n,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\!\left({\boldsymbol{A}_{i,j}^{\prime}=(0,1)}\right)

\displaystyle={\mathbb{P}}\!\left({\boldsymbol{A}_{i,j}^{\prime}=(1,0)}\right)=(k-\ell)/n,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\!\left({\boldsymbol{A}_{i,j}^{\prime}=(0,0)}\right)

\displaystyle=(n-2k+\ell)/n

for all
i,j
.
Moreover, let
\mathcal{T}
be the event that

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{i}}\boldsymbol{1}\left\{{\boldsymbol{A}_{i,j}^{\prime}=(1,1)}\right\}

\displaystyle=\ell\Delta,\qquad\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{i}}\boldsymbol{1}\left\{{\boldsymbol{A}_{i,j}^{\prime}=(0,0)}\right\}=(n-2k+\ell)\Delta,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{i}}\boldsymbol{1}\left\{{\boldsymbol{A}_{i,j}^{\prime}=(1,0)}\right\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{i}}\boldsymbol{1}\left\{{\boldsymbol{A}_{i,j}^{\prime}=(0,1)}\right\}=(k-\ell)\Delta.

(41)

\mathcal{T}
describes the event that the total counts of each ball type in the independent model match the original counts,
and given
\mathcal{T}
, the vector
\boldsymbol{A}^{\prime}=(\boldsymbol{A}_{i,j}^{\prime})_{i,j}
is distributed identically to
\boldsymbol{A}=(\boldsymbol{A}_{i,j})_{i,j}
given
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}
. For
u\in\{1,2\}
let
(\hat{\boldsymbol{A}}^{\prime})^{(u)}\in\{0,1\}^{m}
be the vector defined by the ‘test results’ of the
u
th coordinate in the independent model. That is, for
i\in[m]
, set
(\hat{\boldsymbol{A}}^{\prime})^{(u)}_{i}=\boldsymbol{1}\left\{{\sum_{i=1}^{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{i}}\boldsymbol{A}_{i,j,u}^{\prime}\leq t-1}\right\}
. Moreover, let
\mathcal{P}=\left\{{i\in[m]:(\hat{\boldsymbol{A}}^{\prime})^{(1)}_{i}=1}\right\}
,
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}:=\left\{{\left|{\mathcal{P}}\right|\in\left[{\left({1\pm n^{-\Omega(1)}}\right)m{\mathbb{P}}\left({{\rm Po}(d/c)\geq t}\right)}\right]}\right\}
and
\mathcal{N}=[m]\setminus\mathcal{P}
. Then

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left({\mathcal{M},\forall i\in[m]:\boldsymbol{1}\left\{{\sum_{i=1}^{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{i}}\boldsymbol{A}_{i,j,1}\leq t-1}\right\}=\boldsymbol{1}\left\{{\sum_{i=1}^{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{i}}\boldsymbol{A}_{i,j,2}\leq t-1}\right\}|\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left({\mathcal{M}^{\prime},\forall i\in[m]:\boldsymbol{1}\left\{{\sum_{i=1}^{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{i}}\boldsymbol{A}_{i,j,1}^{\prime}\leq t-1}\right\}=\boldsymbol{1}\left\{{\sum_{i=1}^{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{i}}\boldsymbol{A}_{i,j,2}^{\prime}\leq t-1}\right\}|\boldsymbol{\Gamma},\mathcal{T}}\right).

(42)

Since
\mathcal{T}
is the event that the sums of independent indicator variables precisely match their expected values, its probability is estimated using a Local Central Limit Theorem (LCLT).
An application of Stirling’s formula, which underpins the LCLT in this context, yields

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left({\mathcal{T}|\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}\right)=\Omega((\Delta k)^{-3/2}).

(43)

Now set

\mathcal{S}:=\left\{{\forall i\in[m]:\boldsymbol{1}\left\{{\sum_{i=1}^{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{i}}\boldsymbol{A}_{i,j,1}^{\prime}\leq t-1}\right\}=\boldsymbol{1}\left\{{\sum_{i=1}^{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{i}}\boldsymbol{A}_{i,j,2}^{\prime}\leq t-1}\right\}}\right\}.

Thus, by Bayes’ Theorem and (
43
),

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left({\mathcal{S}\cap\mathcal{M}^{\prime}|\boldsymbol{\Gamma},\mathcal{T}}\right)=\frac{{\mathbb{P}}\left({\mathcal{S}\cap\mathcal{T}\cap\mathcal{M}^{\prime}|\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}\right)}{{\mathbb{P}}\left({\mathcal{T}|\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}\right)}\leq O((\Delta k)^{3/2}){\mathbb{P}}\left({\mathcal{S}\cap\mathcal{M}^{\prime}|\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}\right),

(44)

and it remains to upper bound
{\mathbb{P}}\left({\mathcal{S}\cap\mathcal{M}^{\prime}|\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}\right)
.
Observe that in the auxiliary model, without conditioning on

4.2
Large Overlap

A simple first-moment calculation is insufficient when the overlap
\ell
is close to
k
. The basic calculation relies on the assumption that the probability of a false configuration producing the same set of test results is exceedingly low. However, it overlooks the fact that many tests are already explained by the overlapping true defectives, reducing the number of genuinely independent constraints. This reduction fuels the so-called lottery phenomenon, where rare, atypical configurations artificially inflate the expected number of solutions. To overcome this, we require a more careful analysis that guarantees every defective participates in enough informative tests, a technique first applied in group testing by Coja-Oghlan et al.
[
13
]
.

Outline of the proof.

The proof of Proposition
4.3
is organised into two main parts. Firstly, in Proposition
4.8
, we define the event
{\mathcal{R}}
as the event that every defective item is included in a sufficient number of pivotal tests; writing
m=ck\log(n/k)

c
. Subsequently, in Lemma
4.9

conditioned on the event
{\mathcal{R}}
, which addresses the so-called lottery phenomenon. To achieve this, we bound the expectation by considering the event that tests with quantitative outcomes
t-1
and
t
remain unchanged. Finally, in the proof of Proposition
4.3
, we combine these results to show that the truncated expectation is of order
o(1)
for any
c>0
, implying that the only constraint on
c
arises from the event
{\mathcal{R}}
.

Recall that

denotes the number of times item

appears in test

. We begin with the following proposition, which, as

13

{\mathcal{R}}
and its constraint on

4.4
Recovery without Prior Knowledge of the Number of Defectives

In the previous sections, the algorithm has assumed exact knowledge of the number of defective items
k
. In practical settings, however, it may be challenging to determine this number with certainty. Fortunately, it suffices to have an upper bound
k_{\mathrm{max}}\geq k
, which is enough to ensure correct recovery.

The key observation is that, with high probability, there exists no label vector
\sigma
that is compatible with the observed test results and has Hamming weight strictly smaller than
k_{\max}
. This implies that the true set of defectives forms the
minimal
set consistent with the positive tests. Leveraging this idea, the problem can be reformulated in combinatorial terms as a
minimum
t
-cover in a hypergraph
problem.

Concretely, we represent all
n
items as vertices in a hypergraph, with each positive test
a_{i}
corresponding to a hyperedge connecting the items it contains. Each hyperedge enforces the threshold constraint that at least
t
of its vertices must be selected, reflecting the number of defectives required to produce a positive outcome. In this representation, the true defective set
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
forms a valid
t
-cover of the hypergraph, that is, a subset of vertices satisfying the threshold requirement for every positive test.

Furthermore, w.h.p., this is the
unique

t
-cover of size
k
, and no smaller
t
-cover exists. Consequently, inferring
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
reduces to solving this minimum
t
-cover problem, without needing prior knowledge of
k
, beyond a crude upper bound.

We emphasise that, although knowledge of
k
is not required for decoding, the number of tests
m
is typically designed with respect to
\theta
. In the absence of exact knowledge of
k
, this scaling must instead be based on the upper bound
k_{\mathrm{max}}
.

This approach was first conjectured by Aldridge et al. in
[
3
]
and proved by Coja-Oghlan et al. in
[
13
]
, which established a rigorous foundation for classical group testing without requiring knowledge of the total number of defectives. The following proposition extends it to the threshold group testing setting:

Let
Z_{k,k^{\prime}}^{\prime}({\boldsymbol{G}},\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}})
denote the number of vectors
\tau
of Hamming weight
k^{\prime}<k
that produce the same test outcomes
\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}
under
{\boldsymbol{G}}
as the ground-truth
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
with Hamming weight
k
.

Proposition 4.10

(
[
13
, Lemma III.5]
)

Let
\varepsilon>0
and
0<\theta<1
and assume that
m>(1+\varepsilon)c_{\mathrm{inf}}^{\mathrm{TGT}}k\log(n/k)

Then

\gamma\in[1-1/\log n,1-1/k]
. We then want to estimate the number of possible disguised non-defective items available at level
k^{\prime}
.
The issue we have is that if we flip two disguised items that share a test then it could result in a violation of the test results. Therefore, we will conduct a second moment calculation on the presence of an independent set of a prescribed size existing.

Once again all the positive tests will have been satisfied by the original
k^{\prime}
defectives and thus we need to show that
(1-\gamma)k
in the
k\log n
items of
\boldsymbol{V}_{0}^{+}
can be flipped without turning a negative test to positive. To do this we need to show the existence of a subset
F\subseteq\boldsymbol{V}_{0}^{+}
of size
(1-\gamma)k
such that no two items in
F
share a test. We work on the testing scheme described by the sequence of random variables
(\boldsymbol{X}_{j})_{j\in[m]}
, first discussed in Section
2.3
.

Let
\boldsymbol{F}
be the random variable that counts the number of possible subsets
F
, then by Lemma
2.6
and using total variation distance, with probability at least
1-o(n^{-2})
the expected value will be

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{F}]=

\displaystyle\binom{k\log n}{(1-\gamma)k}\prod_{i=1}^{m}{\mathbb{P}}\left({{\rm Bin}(\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{i},(1-\gamma)k/n)\leq 1}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\binom{k\log n}{(1-\gamma)k}\left({{\mathbb{P}}({\rm Po}(a^{*}(1-\gamma))\leq 1)+O(k/n)}\right)^{m}.

Similarly, we can study the second moment by partitioning it in terms of the overlap between two potential sets as previously used in Section
4.1
. We again use
\ell
to represent the overlap between the two potential sets, and thus let us define two random variables for the distribution of the shared and distinct edges

\boldsymbol{S}_{i,\ell}\sim{\rm Bin}\left({\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{i},\frac{\ell}{n}}\right)\qquad\textrm{and}\qquad\boldsymbol{D}_{\boldsymbol{S}_{i,\ell}}\sim{\rm Bin}\left({\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{i}-\boldsymbol{S}_{i,\ell},\frac{(1-\gamma)k-\ell}{n-\ell}}\right).

Using Lemma
2.6
and total variation distance, with probability at least
1-o(n^{-2})
the second moment will be

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[{\boldsymbol{F}^{2}}\right]=

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=0}^{(1-\gamma)k}\binom{k\log n}{(1-\gamma)k}\binom{(1-\gamma)k}{\ell}\binom{k\log n-(1-\gamma)k}{(1-\gamma)k-\ell}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\times\prod_{i=1}^{m}\left({\sum_{j=0}^{\min\{\ell,1\}}{\mathbb{P}}\left({\boldsymbol{S}_{i,\ell}=j}\right){\mathbb{P}}\left({\boldsymbol{D}_{\boldsymbol{S}_{i,\ell}}\leq 1-j}\right)^{2}}\right).

(77)

Note that (
77
) is strictly decreasing asymptotically as the overlap
\ell
grows and the dominant term will therefore correspond to
\ell=0
, and is given by

\binom{k\log n}{(1-\gamma)k}\binom{k\log n-(1-\gamma)k}{(1-\gamma)k}\prod_{i=1}^{m}{\mathbb{P}}\left({{\rm Bin}(\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{i},(1-\gamma)k/n)\leq 1}\right)^{2}.

Hence, for
\gamma\in[1-1/\log n,1-1/k]
and assuming

c<\tfrac{1}{a^{*}}\left({-\log\!\left(1-\tfrac{(a^{*})^{t-1}\mathrm{e}^{-a^{*}}}{(t-1)!}\right)}\right)^{-1},

the second moment method will give
\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{F}]^{2}/\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{F}^{2}]=1-o(1)
, and by the second-moment method we can conclude that there exists at least one subset
F
of size
(1-\gamma)k
satisfying the correct conditions and have found our contradiction. ∎

Lemma 4.14

(Contained case)
.

Suppose
m>(1+\varepsilon)c_{\mathrm{inf}}^{\mathrm{TGT}}k\log(n/k)
.
With high probability, there is no satisfying label vector of Hamming weight

,

Proof.

5
Linear Number of Defectives

In Section
5.1
, we prove Theorem
1.6
on the linear regime of TGT with i.i.d. defectives. Finally, in Section
5.2
, we present a simple, ‘genuinely TGT’ pooling scheme, and corresponding efficient inference algorithm, that requires
O(n\log n)
tests.

5.1
More than Linear Number of Tests Required: Proof of Theorem
1.6

Recall that we assume that each item is defective independently with probability
\alpha\in(0,1)
and we work under the following assumption on the test design:

Assumption 1
.

Assume that
\boldsymbol{G}
is a test design on
n
items such that the degree of each item is at most
\log^{4}n
, and the degree of each test is at most
M
.

Throughout this section, we follow the strategy used to derive the information-theoretic lower bound for noisy linear CGT in Hintze et al.
[
25
]
. This yields a
\log n
-improvement over an application of the naive counting bound, and allows us to consider reasonably general pooling schemes.

Hintze et al.
[
25
]
do not have a corresponding assumption on the test degrees, as they can remove items of large degree from any test design
G
while making the inference problem for the remaining items only easier. This makes use of the fact that in CGT, all tests that are adjacent a defective item do not contain information about their remaining items in the first place. On the other hand, non-defective items can always safely be removed. In our more general setting however, tests adjacent to defective items actually do contain information about their remaining items.

5.1.1
Optimal Estimators

Once again, our aim is to show that
any
estimator fails w.h.p. unless supplied with a sufficient number of tests, which can first be reduced to considering the Maximum A Posteriori (MAP) estimator.

Definition 5.1

(MAP Estimator)
.

For any test design
G
and any observed outcomes
\hat{\sigma}\in\left\{{0,1}\right\}^{m}
,
the
MAP estimator

\tilde{\sigma}_{{\rm MAP}}(G,\hat{\sigma})=\tilde{\sigma}_{{\rm MAP}}^{G,\hat{\sigma}}
of
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
is defined as

\displaystyle\tilde{\sigma}_{{\rm MAP}}^{G,\hat{\sigma}}=\arg\max_{\tilde{\sigma}\in\left\{{0,1}\right\}^{n}}{\mathbb{P}}\left({\boldsymbol{\sigma}=\tilde{\sigma}\mid G,\hat{\sigma}}\right),

(78)

where, in the event of a non-unique
\arg\max
, we select any
\tilde{\sigma}
containing the greatest number of zeros.

Indeed, the MAP estimator is the optimal estimator for exact recovery:

Fact 5.2

(
[
1
, Section 3.2]
)
.

For any test design
G
and any estimator
\mathcal{A}:G\times\left\{{0,1}\right\}^{m}\to\left\{{0,1}\right\}^{n}
, the estimator
f
performs no better than the
MAP
estimator, i.e.,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left({\boldsymbol{\sigma}=\tilde{\sigma}_{{\rm MAP}}^{G,\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}}}\right)\geq{\mathbb{P}}\left({\boldsymbol{\sigma}=\mathcal{A}(G,\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}})}\right).

Thus, in the following, it is sufficient to consider the performance of the MAP estimator. We will compare it to a (non-practical) algorithm that has oracle-access to additional information about the ground truth, which we call the
genie
estimator:

Definition 5.3

(Genie estimator)
.

Given a test design
G
and the observed results
\hat{\sigma}
, with
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{-i}
the ground truth for every other item except
x_{i}
, the
genie-based estimator
(or just
genie estimator
) is given by

\displaystyle\tilde{\sigma}_{{\rm gen}}^{G,\hat{\sigma}}(i)=\tilde{\sigma}_{{\rm gen}}^{G,\hat{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{-i}}(i)=\arg\max_{s\in\{0,1\}}{\mathbb{P}}\left({\boldsymbol{\sigma}(i)=s\mid G,\hat{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{-i}}\right).

(79)

If both
0
and
1
maximise
{\mathbb{P}}\left({\boldsymbol{\sigma}(i)=s\mid G,\hat{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{-i}}\right)
, then we set
\tilde{\sigma}_{{\rm gen}}^{\boldsymbol{G},\hat{\sigma}}(i)=0
.

Indeed, given that the genie estimator has access to additional information, it is not surprising that is performs even better than the MAP estimator:

Lemma 5.4

(
[
25
, Lemma 4.2]
)
.

For every test design
G
,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left({\boldsymbol{\sigma}=\tilde{\sigma}_{{\rm gen}}^{G,\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}}}\right)\geq{\mathbb{P}}\left({\boldsymbol{\sigma}=\tilde{\sigma}_{{\rm MAP}}^{G,\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}}}\right).

We next characterise the genie estimator in our setting of TGT without noise. For this, recall the notion of a
pivotal test
from Definition
2.13
. For
i\in[n]
, let
g_{i}=g_{i}(G,\boldsymbol{\sigma})
denote the number of pivotal tests involving item
x_{i}
. If the labels of all items except of
x_{i}
are known, and
x_{i}
does not participate in any pivotal test, then its label can be
0
or
1
without changing the test outcomes. In this case, the genie estimator cannot do better than guess the most prevalent status. On the other hand, if
x_{i}
has a pivotal test, then the status of
i
determines the outcome of that pivotal test and thus can be read off from its outcome. Let

and
g_{i}^{-}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})
be the number of positive and negative pivotal tests for item
i
, respectively. Observing that non-defective items cannot participate in positive pivotal tests, and defective items cannot participate in negative pivotal tests, we have just argued the following:

Claim 1
.

For any test design
G
and
i\in[n]
, the genie estimator equals

\displaystyle\tilde{\sigma}_{{\rm gen}}^{G,\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}}(i)=\begin{cases}0&\quad\textup{if $g^{+}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})=0$ and $g^{-}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})>0$ or if $g^{+}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})=g^{-}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})=0$ and $\alpha\leq\frac{1}{2}$},\\
1&\quad\textup{if $g^{-}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})=0$ and $g^{+}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})>0$ or if $g^{+}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})=g^{-}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})=0$ and $\alpha>\frac{1}{2}$}.\end{cases}

Claim

immediately implies the following claim:

Claim 2

Fix any test design
G

\alpha\leq\tfrac{1}{2}

\displaystyle\mathds{1}\left\{{\tilde{\sigma}_{\mathrm{gen}}^{G,\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}}(i)\neq\boldsymbol{\sigma}(i)}\right\}\geq\mathds{1}\left\{{\boldsymbol{g}_{i}^{+}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})=0,\boldsymbol{\sigma}(i)=1}\right\}.

(80)

If
\alpha>\tfrac{1}{2}
, then

\displaystyle\mathds{1}\left\{{\tilde{\sigma}_{\mathrm{gen}}^{G,\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}}(i)\neq\boldsymbol{\sigma}(i)}\right\}\geq\mathds{1}\left\{{\boldsymbol{g}_{i}^{-}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})=0,\boldsymbol{\sigma}(i)=0}\right\}.

(81)

Observe that any test that has degree less than

Given a test design

It is clear that the modified test set-up is not harder than the original one:

Claim 3

Using Lemma

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left({\boldsymbol{\sigma}=\tilde{\sigma}_{\mathrm{gen}}^{G^{\prime},\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^{\prime}}}\right)={\mathbb{P}}\left({\boldsymbol{\sigma}=\tilde{\sigma}_{\mathrm{gen}}^{G,\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}}}\right)\geq{\mathbb{P}}\left({\boldsymbol{\sigma}=\tilde{\sigma}_{\mathrm{MAP}}^{G,\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}}}\right).

(82)
