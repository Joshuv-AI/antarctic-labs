Flatness and Generalization: Learning Multi-Index Models with Homogeneous Neural Networks
Harsh Vardhan, Hossein Taheri, Arya Mazumdar

Abstract
A common heuristic used to explain the generalization of first-order gradient methods on non-convex neural networks is that “flat interpolators generalize well” ( 30 ; 33 ) , where flatness can be measured by the trace of the Hessian of the empirical loss. However, ( 22 ) showed that any interpolator can be made sharper or flatter using the symmetry of the network that can change flatness while keeping the population and empirical losses unchanged. This result makes the earlier heuristic statement vacuous. In this paper, we show that for learning an unknown multi-index model with 2 2 -layer non-convex homogeneous neural networks, there is a connection between flatness and generalization, despite the existence of symmetries. This connection pertains to the “flattest” interpolators, i.e., the interpolators that have orderwise minimum flatness among all interpolators. First, we show that there exists a natural class of non-generalizing interpolators whose flatness cannot be made closer to the flattest possible, even using symmetries. Second, we show that for data generated by a sum of single-index models, if the approximation error and label noise are low, any flattest interpolator achieves small population loss, i.e., the flattest interpolators always generalize. This establishes a direct link between flatness and generalization which applies to a large class of activations and realistic data distributions.

Introduction

In machine learning, overparameterization refers to the paradigm where there are more model parameters than available data samples, such as in modern implementations of neural networks (NN). In this paradigm, the global minimizers of empirical (training) loss can interpolate, or perfectly fit the training data. However, due to the high-dimensional and non-convex landscape of the empirical loss, there are often “bad" interpolators, i.e., interpolators that have large population (test) loss
(
69
;
45
)
. Surprisingly, stochastic gradient methods on these overparametrized models usually converge to “good" interpolators, which generalize well. This empirical observation has not yet been rigorously justified, but one possible explanation for it, from
(
30
)
initially, is the following:
stochastic gradient methods converge to flat interpolators of the empirical loss and flat interpolators of empirical loss always generalize
. In this paper, sharpness of an interpolator is the trace of the Hessian of the empirical loss. Flat interpolators have small trace. In this work, we will try to theoretically justify the second part of this explanation, which states that flat interpolators generalize.

Several existing works have tried to justify the link between flatness and generalization. Experimentally, flatness is strongly correlated to generalization
(
32
;
33
)
and algorithms that explicitly minimize some notion of flatness generalize better
(
33
;
14
;
24
)
. However, there is also evidence refuting this claim.
4
shows that the correlation between flatness and generalization depends on the choice of model and
63
;
55
construct flat interpolators that do not generalize.

Notably, an important setting where this claim can be refuted is in the presence of “symmetries”, i.e., transformations of model weights that do not change input-output relationship. The following well-known result from
22
summarizes the impact of such symmetries on flatness.

Proposition 1

(Symmetries for ReLU Neural Networks
(
22
)
(see Def
1
))
.

There exist symmetries that rescale the layer weights of a NN such that the model output remains the same for a given input (thus, empirical and population losses remain same), however, the flatness changes.

In particular,
22
use this property to show that one can make any interpolator sharper using symmetry, which implies that good interpolators can be sharp. Using symmetry, as in Prop.
1
, one can also make any interpolator flatter. This contradicts the claim “flat interpolators always generalize", as we can make bad interpolators flat. But, how flat can we make these bad interpolators? Can we make bad interpolators the flattest interpolators, i.e., those with the minimum trace of Hessian among all interpolators? Or is there a fundamental barrier that prohibits the symmetries from making certain bad interpolators achieve the minimum flatness?
A necessary condition for this claim to be true is to show there are bad interpolator
that cannot be made the flattest interpolators in the presence of symmetry. This is the first question we consider in this paper:

Q1
: Are there bad interpolators that cannot be made the flattest interpolators using symmetries ?

If the answer to this question is no, then there is a class of bad interpolators that can be made flattest, and there is no link between flatness and generalization in the presence of symmetry. However, if the answer to this question is yes, then symmetry cannot arbitrarily decrease the flatness of every interpolator. Therefore, the set of flattest interpolators does not contain a certain class of bad interpolators. But, when does the set of flattest interpolators does not contain any bad interpolator? This forms our second question, and a
sufficient
condition for our claim.

Q2
: Do all flattest interpolators generalize?

We consider the feature-learning setting to answer these two questions. In our setting, the true labels come from a multi-index model, and we use a
2
-layer neural network with homogeneous non-linear activations to learn it. This is a popular regression problem, where the labels depend only on a few directions (
m^{\star}\ll d
) of the features, specified by the matrix
\Theta^{\star}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m^{\star}\times d}
, via an unknown link function
\sigma^{\star}

2

m

\sigma:{\mathbb{R}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
and the weight vector

Even in this simplified setting, the answer to the questions posed earlier is highly non-trivial. This setup i) has both good and bad interpolators, ii) it is non-linear and non-convex, iii) it has a symmetry that can change flatness without changing population loss, however, iv) gradient-based methods provably generalize for the given sample complexity
(

1.1
Main Contributions

Our main contributions are the answers to our two questions posed earlier.

Non-generalizing Interpolators cannot be Flattest.

Our first contribution is a positive answer to
Q1

Informal Theorem 1

(Thms.
1
-
2
)
.

We characterize a set of bad interpolators in Def.
2
, such that for an interpolator

in this set, its flatness is at least

The class of bad interpolators considered above have their inner-layer weights,

Flattest Interpolators Generalize.

Our second contribution is a positive answer to
Q2

Informal Theorem 2

(Thm.
3
)
.

Under low approx. error and low label noise, an interpolator

that achieves the minimum flatness up to constants, i.e.,
\mathrm{Tr}(\nabla^{2}F_{S}({{\mathbf{w}}}))\asymp\Upsilon^{\star}

F({{\mathbf{w}}})\lesssim n^{-\min\{\frac{1}{2},\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2}\}}

\epsilon_{2}>0

4

\epsilon_{1}

Our current analysis covers both single-index and sum of single-index models and most homogeneous activations like ReLU, LeakyReLU or quadratic activation. Therefore, for our setting of learning a multi-index model with

(

;

(

1.2
Comparison to Related Works

In this section, we compare our results with the most relevant existing works and defer a more comprehensive comparison to App
A
. The closest result to ours is
(
21

. They show that for Quadratic activations (

Two counter-examples where there are bad interpolators that are flattest are provided in
(

)
for smooth convex optimization, and in
(
63
)
for learning a

-layer NN with bias for the XOR problem, a classification variant of our multi-index models. These examples are not contradictory to our findings, as both these counter-examples have specific

. In particular,

(

)
. Moreover, the counter-example in
(
55
)
uses handcrafted loss functions that do not correspond to real tasks like regression or classification on a real data distribution. Additionally, our proofs and results in all theorems for

-layer networks with bias, as used in
(

)
, for Gaussian features. In this case, the flattest interpolator with bias still corresponds to the case of

3
Bad Interpolators of Multi-Index Models are Not Flattest

In this section, we answer
Q1
, thus proving Inf. Thm.
1
. The proof involves two steps, the first being a bound on the minimum flatness of an interpolator (Thm
1
) and the second being a lower bound on the flatness of bad interpolators (Thm.
2
). We first define a natural class of bad interpolators for multi-index data distributions.

Definition 2

(
(\rho^{\star},\rho^{\prime},\kappa)
-Bad Interpolator)
.

For
\rho^{\star},\rho^{\prime}\in[0,\frac{1}{2}]
, and a constant
\kappa\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
, we say that an interpolator
{{\mathbf{w}}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m(d+1)}
is
(\rho^{\star},\rho^{\prime},\kappa)
-bad if,

1.

\forall j\in[m],\quad\theta_{j}\in{\mathbb{S}}^{d-1},\quad\left\langle{\theta_{j}},{\theta_{j^{\prime}}}\right\rangle\leq\rho^{\prime},\forall j\neq j^{\prime}\in[m]
.

2.

F({{\mathbf{w}}})-F^{\star}\geq\kappa(\zeta^{2}+\psi^{\star})
.

3.

\left|{\left\langle{{{\mathbf{v}}}_{1}},{{{\mathbf{v}}}_{2}}\right\rangle}\right|\leq\rho^{\star},\quad\forall\;{{\mathbf{v}}}_{1}\in\mathrm{span}(\{\theta_{j}\}_{j\in[m]})\cap{\mathbb{S}}^{d-1},\forall\;{{\mathbf{v}}}_{2}\in\mathrm{span}(\{\theta_{j}^{\star}]_{j\in[m^{\star}]})\cap{\mathbb{S}}^{d-1}
.

Let us understand each condition in the above definition. The first condition says that inner-layer weights are unit-norm and well-separated. The separation ensures that
{{\mathbf{w}}}
can provably interpolate (cf. Lemma
7
). The second condition ensures that the population loss is large, by a constant
\kappa
for this interpolator, so that it is indeed a bad interpolator. The third condition requires any unit vector in the span of the inner-layer weights,
\mathrm{span}(\{\theta_{j}\}_{j\in[m]})
, to be misaligned with all vectors in the span of true directions,
\mathrm{span}(\{\theta_{j}^{\star}\}_{j\in[m]})
. A consequence of this condition is that each layer weight is also misaligned with the true direction, i.e.,
\left|{\left\langle{\theta_{j}},{\theta_{j^{\prime}}^{\star}}\right\rangle}\right|\leq\rho^{\star},\forall j\in[m],j^{\prime}\in[m^{\star}]

To obtain a lower bound on

for an interpolator

that generates the labels

Assumption 2

(Link Function)
.

For Gaussian random vector,
{{\mathbf{b}}}\sim{\mathcal{N}}(\mu,\varsigma)

\displaystyle\textbf{Mean}:\|\underset{{{\mathbf{b}}}}{{\mathbb{E}}}\left[{{\sigma^{\star}({{\mathbf{b}}})}}\right]\|_{2}=\Theta\left({\left\|{\mu}\right\|_{2}^{c}+\left\|{\varsigma}\right\|_{F}^{\frac{c}{2}}}\right),\quad\quad\textbf{Variance}:\underset{{{\mathbf{b}}}}{\text{Var}}\left[{{\sigma^{\star}({{\mathbf{b}}})}}\right]=\Omega\left({\left\|{\varsigma}\right\|_{F}\left\|{\mu}\right\|_{2}^{2c-2}+\left\|{\varsigma}\right\|_{F}^{c}}\right),

\displaystyle\textbf{Tails}:\sigma^{\star}({{\mathbf{b}}})\text{ is a }(\frac{c}{2},{\mathcal{O}}(\left\|{\mu}\right\|_{2}^{c}+\left\|{\varsigma}\right\|_{F}^{\frac{c}{2}}))\text{-Sub-Weibull random vector.}

Note that a
(\varrho,K)

{\mathcal{O}}(Kk^{\varrho})

Assumption 3

(Width and Inner-Layer Dependence)
.

Let
C\leq m\leq C^{\prime}n

\bar{\Phi}=\frac{1}{n}{\mathbb{E}}\left[{{({{\mathbf{U}}}-{\mathbb{E}}\left[{{{{\mathbf{U}}}}}\right])^{\top}({{\mathbf{U}}}-{\mathbb{E}}\left[{{{{\mathbf{U}}}}}\right])}}\right]

Note that the above assumption ensures that the width of the network is only a constant times larger than

Suppose Assump.

Note that

Flattest Interpolators of Sum of Single Index Models Generalize

In this section, we will answer

Proposition 2

(Necessary and Sufficient Condition for Flattest Interpolator)
.

Suppose Assump.
1
,
2
and
3

{{\mathbf{w}}}
with

This condition on

Assumption 4

(Low Approximation Error and Label Noise)
.

For some constants,
\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2}>0
,

•

Approx. Error:
there exists a
2
-layer NN of width
\breve{m}\geq 1
, with unit norm inner-layer weights
\{\breve{\theta}_{j}\}_{j\in[\breve{m}]}

\breve{{{\mathbf{a}}}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\breve{m}},\left\|{\breve{{{\mathbf{a}}}}}\right\|_{\infty}\leq(2\breve{m})^{-1}\sqrt{\psi^{\star}(\phi^{\star})^{-1}}

\forall{{\mathbf{x}}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}

•

Label Noise:

\zeta={\mathcal{O}}(m^{-\frac{1}{2}}n^{-\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon_{2}})

This assumption forces the link function,

2
-layer network of width
\breve{m}
, activation
\sigma

While the label noise requirement might seem restrictive, as it implies the SNR,

\displaystyle\left\|{{{\mathbf{a}}}}\right\|_{\infty}\leq m^{-1}\left({\sqrt{\psi^{\star}(\phi^{\star})^{-1}}+\gamma n^{-\min\{\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2}\}}}\right),\quad\text{ where }\quad\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2}>0,\gamma={\mathcal{O}}(1)

We focus on the generalization of flattest interpolators in Theorem

\displaystyle 2F({{\mathbf{w}}})=\zeta^{2}+\psi^{\star}+\sum_{j^{\prime}=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}a_{j}a_{j^{\prime}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[{{\sigma(\left\langle{\theta_{j}},{{{\mathbf{x}}}}\right\rangle)\sigma(\left\langle{\theta_{j^{\prime}}},{{{\mathbf{x}}}}\right\rangle))}}\right]-2\sum_{j=1}^{m}a_{j}{\mathbb{E}}\left[{{\sigma^{\star}(\Theta^{\star}{{\mathbf{x}}})\sigma(\left\langle{\theta_{j}},{{{\mathbf{x}}}}\right\rangle)}}\right].

Note that the population loss
F({{\mathbf{w}}})
is a quadratic function in
{{\mathbf{a}}}
, with its coefficients depending on the inner-layer weights. The flattest interpolator imposes no conditions on inner-layer weights, only a condition on
\left\|{{{\mathbf{a}}}}\right\|_{\infty}

{{\mathbf{U}}}{{\mathbf{a}}}={{\mathbf{y}}}

First, consider the quadratic term in Eq. (

a_{j}\leq\left\|{{{\mathbf{a}}}}\right\|_{\infty}
and the terms of
\sigma

\phi^{\star}

{{\mathbf{a}}}

, we can see that for LeakyReLU and Quadratic activation, if the minimum flatness after rescaling,
\min_{{{\mathbf{a}}}}\Upsilon({{\mathbf{w}}})
, is low, then population loss is also low. However, extremely high correlation with the true direction, i.e., large
\rho^{\star}
forces large
\left\|{{{\mathbf{a}}}}\right\|_{\infty}
to ensure interpolation. Thus, large
\rho^{\star}
does not achieve the minimum flatness and, in turn, has a high population loss. This phenomenon results in a poor connection between flatness and generalization for ReLU activation, where very small
\rho^{\star}
can easily interpolate with small
\left\|{{{\mathbf{a}}}}\right\|_{\infty}
, but as
\rho^{\star}
is small, its population loss is large. For single-index models, this is a result of poor conditioning of activation, thus violating Assump.
3
.

Sum of Single-Index Models.

For the sum of single-index models, the connection between flatness and generalization is much stronger. Further, there is also a connection between the correlation
\rho^{\star}
and flatness. From Fig
3
, this connection holds for all activations, even ReLU, where it seemed to be absent for single-index models. If we increase
\rho^{\star}
, the correlation to the true direction, the population loss decreases, and the minimum flatness
\min_{{{\mathbf{a}}}}\Upsilon({{\mathbf{w}}})
also decreases. This stronger connection is precisely because multi-index models with diverse features can interpolate much better for a large
\rho^{\star}
than single-index models, as they can still satisfy Assump.
3
.

Special Cases.

For the special case of learning a linear function using ReLU in Fig
4(a)
, although this is a single-index model, it behaves similarly to our results for multi-index models, with a strong connection between the correlation, population loss and flatness. For a linear link function with linear activation in Fig
4(b)
, it appears that most interpolators obtain the same flatness. While their population losses can be different, all these population losses are very small, even for different
\rho^{\star}
. This is because for linear activation, the outer-layer
{{\mathbf{a}}}
weights can be selected so that
\sum_{j=1}^{m}a_{j}\theta_{j}
is parallel to
\theta^{\star}
. Therefore, for linear link function and linear activation, empirically flattest interpolators seem to generalize, however the theoretical justification in
(
26
)
is still loose. For multi-index model in Fig
4(c)
, note that a
2
-layer network with bounded outer-layer weights cannot approximate it
(
47
)
. Therefore, the approximation error
\Delta
is always large and thus, there is no connection between flatness and generalization, as the flattest solution seems to have a high population loss.

We conclude our paper with a summary of our main contributions, promising extensions and limitations in the next Section.

7
Conclusion

In this paper, we have shown that despite symmetry that can change the flatness of an interpolator, for multi-index data, some bad interpolators are not the flattest interpolators. Further, if labels are generated as a sum of single-index models with low approximation error and low label noise, flattest interpolators generalize. Therefore, the original claim of “flat interpolators generalize" might be false with symmetry, but “flattest interpolators still generalize". This provides a connection between flatness and generalization which was unknown for general noisy data distributions in the presence of symmetry with Homogeneous NNs.

We conclude our paper with a discussion of its limitations that form promising directions for future work:

Higher label noise:
A large label noise makes the flatness of every interpolator the same as that of a bad interpolator in Thm.
2
, as the label
{{\mathbf{y}}}
effectively has a large component independent of activation. A more precise analysis of
\Upsilon({{\mathbf{w}}})
in terms of the signal and noise terms (
\psi^{\star}
and
\zeta^{2}
) should ideally permit a larger label noise as long as SNR also grows with
n
. To extend our results to both constant
\zeta^{2}
and constant SNR, which is the case for gradient-based methods on single-index and sum of single-index data
(
49
;
48
)
, we need to consider weights beyond interpolators,
F_{S}({{\mathbf{w}}})=0
, to approximate interpolators,
F_{S}({{\mathbf{w}}})\lesssim\zeta^{2}
, as none of the gradient methods converge to interpolators in finite steps.

Size of the set of flattest interpolators:
All our results (Thms.
1
,
2
and
3
) hold for a single model with high probability. To extend these results to all models in a given set, for instance that of flattest interpolators, we need to quantify their sizes for a high probability union bound. Unfortunately, there are no known tight estimates for these sizes, and the only loose estimate that we have is the size of set of all model weights.

Broader necessary and sufficient conditions:
While our setting is more general than that of existing works
(
21
)
, it is still focused on a particular form of symmetry, a particular class of multi-index data, and a particular NN architecture. In light of negative results from
55
;
63
, there is still hope for discovering a more general set of necessary and sufficient conditions for flattest interpolators to generalize, of which our problem setup is a special instance.

Algorithms that converge to flattest interpolators:
Our work does not provide any algorithms that can converge to these flattest interpolators. Existing optimizers like SAM
(
24
;
35
)
and its variants, or (S)GD with large step-sizes
(
15
)
, attempt to reduce flatness; however, these are not theoretically guaranteed to reach the flattest interpolators
(
55
;
63
)
except in specific models
(
64
)
.

\Upsilon({{\mathbf{w}}})
: Proof of Lem.
1

Our proof heavily relies on the expression of
\Upsilon({{\mathbf{w}}})
from Lem.
1
. We first provide a proof for this Lem. by using the following two Lems.

Lemma 3
.

For any interpolator
{{\mathbf{w}}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m(d+1)}
with unit norm inner-layer weights,

\displaystyle\Upsilon({{\mathbf{w}}})=\min_{\hat{{{\mathbf{w}}}}\in{\mathcal{W}}_{\mathrm{rescale}({{\mathbf{w}}})}}\mathrm{Tr}(\nabla^{2}F_{S}(\hat{{{\mathbf{w}}}}))=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left|{a_{j}}\right|^{\frac{2c}{c+1}}B(\theta_{j}),

where

\displaystyle B(\theta_{j})=(c^{-\frac{c}{c+1}}+c^{\frac{1}{c+1}})B_{1}^{\frac{1}{c+1}}(\theta_{j})B_{2}^{\frac{c}{c+1}}(\theta_{j}).

Here,
B_{1},B_{2}:{\mathbb{S}}^{d-1}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
are functions given by
B_{1}(\theta)\coloneq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma^{2}(\left\langle{\theta},{{{\mathbf{x}}}_{i}}\right\rangle)
and
B_{2}(\theta)\coloneq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\sigma^{\prime})^{2}(\left\langle{\theta},{{{\mathbf{x}}}_{i}}\right\rangle)\left\|{{{\mathbf{x}}}_{i}}\right\|_{2}^{2}
.

Lemma 4

(Bounds on
B(\theta)
)
.

For Piece-wise Polynomial Activation
\sigma
(Assump.
1
), from Prop.
3
, with probability
1-6\delta
, for a set
\{\theta_{j}\}_{j\in[m]}
, with
\theta_{j}\in{\mathbb{S}}^{d-1},\forall j\in[m]
,

\displaystyle\max_{j\in[m]}B(\theta_{j})\lesssim d^{\frac{c}{c+1}},\quad\min_{j\in[m]}B(\theta_{j})\gtrsim d^{\frac{c}{c+1}},\implies B(\theta_{j})\asymp d^{\frac{c}{c+1}},\forall j\in[m]

Using Lem.
3
and
4
, we can show that with probability
1-6\delta
, each
B(\theta_{j})\asymp d^{\frac{c}{c+1}}
, therefore,

\displaystyle\Upsilon({{\mathbf{w}}})\asymp d^{\frac{c}{c+1}}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left|{a_{j}}\right|^{\frac{2c}{c+1}}=d^{\frac{c}{c+1}}\left\|{{{\mathbf{a}}}}\right\|_{\frac{2c}{c+1}}^{\frac{2c}{c+1}}.

This completes the proof of Lem.
1
. The proof of the intermediate Lem.
4
is deferred to App.
D.3
. We provide a proof for Lem.
3
as it extends the flatness beyond ReLU (
c=1
in
63
) and quadratic activations (
c=2
in
21
) to any piece-wise Polynomial activations.

Proof of Lem.
3
.

To find the flattest empirical minimizer in
{\mathcal{W}}_{\text{rescale}}({{\mathbf{w}}})
for an empirical minimizer
{{\mathbf{w}}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, we need to solve the optimization problem,

\displaystyle\min_{\{\alpha_{j}\}_{j\in[m]},\alpha_{j}>0}\frac{1}{n}\underset{i\in[n],j\in[m]}{\sum\sum}(\alpha_{j}^{2c}\sigma^{2}(\left\langle{\theta_{j}},{{{\mathbf{x}}}_{i}}\right\rangle)+\alpha_{j}^{-2}a_{j}^{2}(\sigma^{\prime})^{2}(\left\langle{\theta_{j}},{{{\mathbf{x}}}_{i}}\right\rangle)\left\|{{{\mathbf{x}}}_{i}}\right\|_{2}^{2})

\displaystyle=\min_{\{\alpha_{j}\}_{j\in[m]},\alpha_{j}>0}\sum_{j\in[m]}\alpha_{j}^{2c}B_{1}(\theta_{j})+\alpha_{j}^{-2}a_{j}^{2}B_{2}(\theta_{j}).

Here,
B_{1}(\theta)\coloneq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in[n]}\sigma^{2}(\left\langle{\theta},{{{\mathbf{x}}}_{i}}\right\rangle)
, and
B_{2}(\theta)\coloneq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in[n]}(\sigma^{\prime})^{2}(\left\langle{\theta},{{{\mathbf{x}}}_{i}}\right\rangle)\left\|{{{\mathbf{x}}}_{i}}\right\|_{2}^{2}
, for any
\theta\in{\mathbb{S}}^{d-1}
. Note that
B_{1},B_{2}\geq 0
,
\forall\theta\in{\mathbb{S}}^{d-1}
. As the objective is separable in
\alpha_{j}
, we can minimize each function of
\alpha_{j}
individually.

Consider the objective function
Q:{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\setminus\{0\}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
. Then,

\displaystyle Q(\alpha_{j})

\displaystyle=B_{1}(\theta_{j})\alpha_{j}^{2c}+a_{j}^{2}\alpha_{j}^{-2}B_{2}(\theta_{j})

\displaystyle\nabla Q(\alpha_{j})

\displaystyle=2cB_{1}(\theta_{j})\alpha_{j}^{2c-1}-2a_{j}^{2}B_{2}(\theta_{j})\alpha_{j}^{-3}

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}Q(\alpha_{j})

\displaystyle=2c(2c-1)B_{1}(\theta_{j})\alpha_{j}^{2c-2}+6a_{j}^{2}B_{2}(\theta_{j})\alpha_{j}^{-4}.

Note that
\nabla^{2}Q(\alpha_{j})>0,\forall\alpha_{j}>0
, so the function is minimized at
\alpha_{j}
where
\nabla Q(\alpha_{j})=0
. This value corresponds to

\displaystyle\alpha_{j}=\left(\frac{a_{j}^{2}B_{2}(\theta_{j})}{cB_{1}(\theta_{j})}\right)^{\frac{1}{2c+2}}.

Therefore, the flatness of the flattest minima obtained by purely rescaling
{{\mathbf{a}}}
and
\theta_{j}
is given by,

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in[m]}\left|{a_{j}}\right|^{\frac{2c}{c+1}}B(\theta_{j}),\text{ where }B(\theta_{j})\coloneq(c^{-\frac{c}{c+1}}+c^{\frac{1}{c+1}})B_{1}^{\frac{1}{c+1}}(\theta_{j})B_{2}^{\frac{c}{c+1}}(\theta_{j}).

(4)

∎

C.2
Lower bounds on
\Upsilon({{\mathbf{w}}})
: Proof of Thm.
1

Lemma 5

(Lower bound on
\Upsilon({{\mathbf{w}}})
)
.

If the conditions of Lem.
2
hold, for an interpolator
{{\mathbf{w}}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m(d+1)}
, with unit-norm inner-layer weights,
\{\theta_{j}\}_{j\in[m]}
, with probability
1-6\delta
,

\displaystyle\Upsilon({{\mathbf{w}}})

\displaystyle\gtrsim d^{\frac{c}{c+1}}\left({\frac{\left\|{{{\mathbf{a}}}_{\min,\ell_{2}}}\right\|_{2}^{2}}{\left\|{{{\mathbf{a}}}_{\min,\ell_{2}}}\right\|_{\frac{2c}{c-1}}}}\right)^{\frac{2c}{c+1}},

\displaystyle\text{ where, }{{\mathbf{a}}}_{\min,\ell_{2}}

\displaystyle={{\mathbf{U}}}^{\top}\widetilde{{{\mathbf{U}}}}{{\mathbf{y}}}=\min_{{{\mathbf{a}}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m},{{\mathbf{U}}}{{\mathbf{a}}}={{\mathbf{y}}}}\left\|{{{\mathbf{a}}}}\right\|_{2}.

If
\frac{\left\|{{{\mathbf{a}}}_{\min,\ell_{2}}}\right\|_{\infty}}{\left\|{{{\mathbf{a}}}_{\min,\ell_{2}}}\right\|_{2}}={\mathcal{O}}(\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}})
,

\displaystyle\Upsilon({{\mathbf{w}}})

\displaystyle\gtrsim d^{\frac{c}{c+1}}m^{\frac{1}{c+1}}\left\|{{{\mathbf{a}}}_{\min,\ell_{2}}}\right\|_{2}^{\frac{2c}{c+1}}.

Lemma 6

(Lower bound on
\left\|{{{\mathbf{a}}}_{\min,\ell_{2}}}\right\|_{2}
)
.

If
\zeta^{2}=o(1)
, and the conditions of Lem.
2
hold, then with probability
1-4\delta
,

\displaystyle\left\|{{{\mathbf{a}}}_{\min,\ell_{2}}}\right\|_{2}\gtrsim\sigma_{\max}^{-1}({{\mathbf{U}}})\sqrt{n}\gtrsim\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}.

Further, for
\left\|{{{\mathbf{a}}}_{\min,\ell_{2}}}\right\|_{2}\asymp\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}
,
\frac{\left\|{{{\mathbf{a}}}_{\min,\ell_{2}}}\right\|_{\infty}}{\left\|{{{\mathbf{a}}}_{\min,\ell_{2}}}\right\|_{2}}={\mathcal{O}}(\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}})
.

From Lems.
5
and
6
, for any interpolator, with probability
1-10\delta
, we have,

\displaystyle\Upsilon({{\mathbf{w}}})\gtrsim d^{\frac{c}{c+1}}m^{-\frac{1}{c+1}}\left\|{{{\mathbf{a}}}_{\min,\ell_{2}}}\right\|_{2}^{\frac{2c}{c+1}}\gtrsim d^{\frac{c}{c+1}}m^{-\frac{1}{c+1}}\left({\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}}\right)^{\frac{2c}{c+1}}=d^{\frac{c}{c+1}}m^{-\frac{c-1}{c+1}}=\Upsilon^{\star}.

This proves Thm.
1
. In the remainder of this Section, we provide the proofs for Lems.
5
and
6
.

Proof of Lem.
5
.

We find a lower bound on
\Upsilon({{\mathbf{w}}})
for a fixed set of inner-layer weights
\{\theta_{j}\}_{j\in[m]}
.
Note that
{{\mathbf{w}}}
is an interpolator, so,

\displaystyle h({{\mathbf{w}}},{{\mathbf{x}}}_{i})=y_{i},\forall i\in[n]

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{j}\sigma(\left\langle{\theta_{j}},{{{\mathbf{x}}}_{i}}\right\rangle)=y_{i}

\displaystyle({{\mathbf{U}}}{{\mathbf{a}}})_{i}=y_{i}

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle{{\mathbf{U}}}{{\mathbf{a}}}={{\mathbf{y}}}.

From Lem.
1
, a lower bound on flatness in terms of only inner-layer weights requires minimizing
\Upsilon({{\mathbf{w}}})
in terms of
{{\mathbf{a}}}
. Note that this is a constrained minimization, as interpolation forces the linear constraint,
{{\mathbf{U}}}{{\mathbf{a}}}={{\mathbf{y}}}
. The following statement holds with probability
1-9\delta
,

\displaystyle\Upsilon({{\mathbf{w}}})\geq\min_{{{\mathbf{a}}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m},{{\mathbf{U}}}{{\mathbf{a}}}={{\mathbf{y}}}}\gtrsim d^{\frac{c}{c+1}}\min_{{{\mathbf{a}}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m},{{\mathbf{U}}}{{\mathbf{a}}}={{\mathbf{y}}}}\left\|{{{\mathbf{a}}}}\right\|_{\frac{2c}{c+1}}^{\frac{2c}{c+1}}.

Note that the above optimization in
{{\mathbf{a}}}
minimizes a convex
\ell_{p}
norm, with a linear equality constraint. Therefore, strong duality holds
(
9
, Chapter 5)
, and we can use duality to find a lower bound on this objective. Using dual variables
{{\mathbf{r}}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
for the interpolation constraint
{{\mathbf{U}}}{{\mathbf{a}}}={{\mathbf{y}}}
, the primal problem has the following form.

\displaystyle\min_{{{\mathbf{a}}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}\max_{{{\mathbf{r}}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\left\|{{{\mathbf{a}}}}\right\|_{{\frac{2c}{c+1}}}^{\frac{2c}{c+1}}+\left\langle{{{\mathbf{r}}}},{{{\mathbf{U}}}{{\mathbf{a}}}-{{\mathbf{y}}}}\right\rangle.

(5)

As strong duality holds, the above optimal primal objective is equal to its optimal dual objective. Its dual problem is given by the following.

\displaystyle\max_{{{\mathbf{r}}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\min_{{{\mathbf{a}}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}\left\|{{{\mathbf{a}}}}\right\|_{{\frac{2c}{c+1}}}^{\frac{2c}{c+1}}+\left\langle{{{\mathbf{r}}}},{{{\mathbf{U}}}{{\mathbf{a}}}-{{\mathbf{y}}}}\right\rangle

(6)

We first solve the inner unconstrained minimization in terms of
{{\mathbf{a}}}
. Note that the Lagrangian is a convex function, therefore, we can minimize it by setting its gradient to
0
. We consider
2
cases, when
c=1
and
c>1
, to handle the
\ell_{1}
norm and arbitrary
\ell_{p}
norms for
p>1
separately.
We first find the lower bound for
c>1
.

Case I :
c>1
.

By setting the first derivative of the Langrangian in Eq (
6
) to
0
for
c>1
, we obtain,
\forall j\in[m]
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{sign}(a_{j})\frac{2c}{c+1}\left|{a_{j}}\right|^{\frac{c-1}{c+1}}+\sum_{i\in[n]}r_{i}{{\mathbf{U}}}_{i,j}=0,

\displaystyle\left|{a_{j}}\right|=\left(-\mathrm{sign}(a_{j})\frac{(c+1)\sum_{i\in[n]}r_{i}{{\mathbf{U}}}_{i,j}}{2c}\right)^{\frac{c+1}{c-1}}.

Plugging this value into Eq (
6
), the Langrangian becomes,

\displaystyle\left\|{{{\mathbf{a}}}}\right\|_{{\frac{2c}{c+1}}}^{\frac{2c}{c+1}}+\left\langle{{{\mathbf{r}}}},{{{\mathbf{U}}}{{\mathbf{a}}}-{{\mathbf{y}}}}\right\rangle=-\widetilde{c}\sum_{j\in[m]}\left|{\sum_{i\in[n]}r_{i}{{\mathbf{U}}}_{i,j}}\right|^{\frac{2c}{c-1}}-\left\langle{{{\mathbf{r}}}},{{{\mathbf{y}}}}\right\rangle=-\widetilde{c}\left\|{{{\mathbf{U}}}^{\top}{{\mathbf{r}}}}\right\|_{\frac{2c}{c-1}}^{\frac{2c}{c-1}}-\left\langle{{{\mathbf{r}}}},{{{\mathbf{y}}}}\right\rangle.

(7)

Here
\widetilde{c}=(c-1)(c+1)^{\frac{c+1}{c-1}}(2c)^{-\frac{2c}{c-1}}
. The above function is a concave objective in
{{\mathbf{r}}}
as it is negative of an
\ell_{p}
norm of
{{\mathbf{r}}}
with
p=\frac{2c}{c-1}
. As
p=\frac{2c}{c-1}\geq 2
, the
p
-norm is convex, and the negative of a convex function is concave.

To maximize Eq (
7
) with respect to
{{\mathbf{r}}}
, we can set the first derivative with respect to
{{\mathbf{r}}}
to
0
. However, computing a closed-form solution to
{{\mathbf{r}}}
is not feasible for any arbitrary
c>1
. Instead, we find a lower bound to Eq (
7
). As the original dual problem, Eq (
6
) maximizes the objective in Eq (
7
) in terms of
{{\mathbf{r}}}
, if we plug in any value of
{{\mathbf{r}}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, it should be a lower bound Eq (
6
). We plug in
{{\mathbf{r}}}=-\beta({{\mathbf{U}}}{{\mathbf{U}}})^{-1}{{\mathbf{y}}}
in Eq (
7
) for some
\beta>0
.

\displaystyle\max_{{{\mathbf{r}}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}-\widetilde{c}\left\|{{{\mathbf{U}}}^{\top}\widetilde{{{\mathbf{U}}}}{{\mathbf{y}}}}\right\|_{\frac{2c}{c-1}}^{\frac{2c}{c-1}}-\left\langle{{{\mathbf{r}}}},{{{\mathbf{y}}}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\geq\max_{\beta\in{\mathbb{R}}}-\widetilde{c}\beta^{\frac{2c}{c-1}}\left\|{{{\mathbf{U}}}^{\top}\widetilde{{{\mathbf{U}}}}{{\mathbf{y}}}}\right\|_{\frac{2c}{c-1}}^{\frac{2c}{c-1}}+\beta\left\|{\widetilde{{{\mathbf{U}}}}^{\frac{1}{2}}{{\mathbf{y}}}}\right\|_{2}^{2}

To obtain a tight lower bound, we can now maximize this lower bound in terms of
\beta
by setting the first derivative with respect to
\beta
to
0
, as this is a concave function
\beta
. This gives us the optimal value of
\beta^{\star}
as the following.

\displaystyle\beta^{\star}=\left({\frac{(c-1)\left\|{\widetilde{{{\mathbf{U}}}}^{\frac{1}{2}}{{\mathbf{y}}}}\right\|_{2}^{2}}{2c\widetilde{c}\left\|{{{\mathbf{U}}}^{\top}\widetilde{{{\mathbf{U}}}}{{\mathbf{y}}}}\right\|_{\frac{2c}{c-1}}^{\frac{2c}{c-1}}}}\right)^{\frac{c-1}{c+1}}.

This is a concave function as its hessian with respect to
\beta
is
-\widetilde{c}\frac{2c(c+1)}{(c-1)}\beta^{\frac{2}{c-1}}\left\|{{{\mathbf{U}}}^{\top}\widetilde{{{\mathbf{U}}}}{{\mathbf{y}}}}\right\|_{2}
, hence it is maximized at this value of
\beta^{\star}
. Plugging in this value of
\beta^{\star}
and ignoring the constant terms of
c
, we obtain the following lower bound on flatness for inner layer weights
\{\theta_{j}\}_{j\in[m]}
.

\displaystyle\min_{{{\mathbf{a}}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m},{{\mathbf{U}}}{{\mathbf{a}}}={{\mathbf{y}}}}\Upsilon({{\mathbf{w}}})\gtrsim d^{\frac{c}{c+1}}\left({\frac{\left\|{\widetilde{{{\mathbf{U}}}}^{\frac{1}{2}}{{\mathbf{y}}}}\right\|_{2}^{2}}{\left\|{{{\mathbf{U}}}^{\top}\widetilde{{{\mathbf{U}}}}{{\mathbf{y}}}}\right\|_{\frac{2c}{c-1}}}}\right)^{\frac{2c}{c+1}}

(8)

Case II :
c=1
.

We need to minimize
\ell_{1}
norm of weights subject to the constraint
{{\mathbf{U}}}{{\mathbf{a}}}={{\mathbf{y}}}
.
The dual problem takes the following form,

\displaystyle\max_{{{\mathbf{r}}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\min_{{{\mathbf{a}}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\sum_{j\in[m]}\left|{a_{j}}\right|(1-\mathrm{sign}(a_{j})({{\mathbf{U}}}^{\top}{{\mathbf{r}}})_{j})-\left\langle{{{\mathbf{r}}}},{{{\mathbf{y}}}}\right\rangle.

If
\exists j\in[m]
such that
\left|{({{\mathbf{U}}}^{\top}{{\mathbf{r}}})_{j}}\right|>1
, then each
\left|{a_{j}}\right|
is multiplied by a negative term, thus it can be minimized by setting
\left|{a_{j}}\right|\to\infty
, and the objective would be
-\infty
making the problem infeasible. If
\left\|{{{\mathbf{U}}}^{\top}{{\mathbf{r}}}}\right\|_{\infty}\leq 1,\forall j\in[m]
, then, the coefficient of each
\left|{a}\right|_{j}
is non-negative. This is minimized for
{{\mathbf{a}}}={\mathbf{0}}
. Therefore, solving the inner minimization in terms of
{{\mathbf{a}}}
gives us the following bound.

\displaystyle\max_{{{\mathbf{r}}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}-\left\langle{{{\mathbf{r}}}},{{{\mathbf{y}}}}\right\rangle,\quad\text{ s.t. }\quad\left\|{{{\mathbf{U}}}^{\top}{{\mathbf{r}}}}\right\|_{\infty}\leq 1

We can again convert this to a
1
-dimensional optimization problem by setting
{{\mathbf{r}}}=-\beta\widetilde{{{\mathbf{U}}}}{{\mathbf{y}}}
for some
\beta>0
. From the constraint, we obtain
\beta\leq\left\|{{{\mathbf{U}}}^{\top}\widetilde{{{\mathbf{U}}}}{{\mathbf{y}}}}\right\|_{\infty}^{-1}
. Therefore, a lower bound on this function is
\frac{\left\|{\widetilde{{{\mathbf{U}}}}^{\frac{1}{2}}{{\mathbf{y}}}}\right\|_{2}^{2}}{\left\|{{{\mathbf{U}}}^{\top}\widetilde{{{\mathbf{U}}}}{{\mathbf{y}}}}\right\|_{\infty}}
, which can also be obtained by taking the limit of
c\to 1
for Eq (
8
).

Final Bound.

We now try to find the lower bound on RHS of Eq (
8
). To do this, we consider the minimum
\ell_{2}
norm interpolator of
{{\mathbf{a}}}
defined as,

\displaystyle{{\mathbf{a}}}_{\min,\ell_{2}}=\mathop{\arg\min}_{{{\mathbf{a}}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m},{{\mathbf{U}}}{{\mathbf{a}}}={{\mathbf{y}}}}\left\|{{{\mathbf{a}}}}\right\|_{2}.

If we fix the matrix of activations
{{\mathbf{U}}}
, the constraint
{{\mathbf{U}}}{{\mathbf{a}}}={{\mathbf{y}}}
corresponds to an interpolator of overparametrized linear regression for
{{\mathbf{a}}}
as
m\geq n
. The minimum
\ell_{2}
-norm interpolator
6
for overparmetrized linear regression is obtained by solving the above problem. Further, from Lem.
2
, the
\sigma_{\min}({{\mathbf{U}}})>0
, so
{{\mathbf{U}}}
is full-rank, and therefore,
{{\mathbf{U}}}{{\mathbf{U}}}^{\top}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n}
is invertible. In this case, the minimum
\ell_{2}
-norm interpolator for
{{\mathbf{a}}}
, is given by
6
,

\displaystyle{{\mathbf{a}}}_{\min,\ell_{2}}={{\mathbf{U}}}^{\top}\widetilde{{{\mathbf{U}}}}{{\mathbf{y}}}.

Additionally, the
\ell_{2}
norm of this interpolator is given by,

\displaystyle\left\|{{{\mathbf{a}}}_{\min,\ell_{2}}}\right\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|{{{\mathbf{U}}}^{\top}\widetilde{{{\mathbf{U}}}}{{\mathbf{y}}}}\right\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle={{\mathbf{y}}}^{\top}\widetilde{{{\mathbf{U}}}}{{\mathbf{U}}}{{\mathbf{U}}}^{\top}\widetilde{{{\mathbf{U}}}}

\displaystyle={{\mathbf{y}}}^{\top}\widetilde{{{\mathbf{U}}}}{{\mathbf{y}}}

\displaystyle=\left\|{\widetilde{{{\mathbf{U}}}}^{\frac{1}{2}}{{\mathbf{y}}}}\right\|_{2}^{2}.

Therefore, the RHS of Eq (
8
) takes the following form,

\displaystyle d^{\frac{c}{c+1}}\left({\frac{\left\|{\widetilde{{{\mathbf{U}}}}^{\frac{1}{2}}{{\mathbf{y}}}}\right\|_{2}^{2}}{\left\|{{{\mathbf{U}}}^{\top}\widetilde{{{\mathbf{U}}}}{{\mathbf{y}}}}\right\|_{\frac{2c}{c-1}}}}\right)^{\frac{2c}{c+1}}=d^{\frac{c}{c+1}}\left({\frac{\left\|{{{\mathbf{a}}}_{\min,\ell_{2}}}\right\|_{2}^{2}}{\left\|{{{\mathbf{a}}}_{\min,\ell_{2}}}\right\|_{\frac{2c}{c-1}}}}\right)^{\frac{2c}{c+1}}.

If
\frac{\left\|{{{\mathbf{a}}}_{\min,\ell_{2}}}\right\|_{\infty}}{\left\|{{{\mathbf{a}}}_{\min,\ell_{2}}}\right\|_{2}}={\mathcal{O}}(\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}})
, then, by using Riesz-Thorin Interpolation Theorem
(
59
)
, we have,

\displaystyle\left\|{{{\mathbf{a}}}_{\min,\ell_{2}}}\right\|_{\frac{2c}{c-1}}\leq\left\|{{{\mathbf{a}}}_{\min,\ell_{2}}}\right\|_{2}^{\frac{c-1}{c}}\left\|{{{\mathbf{a}}}_{\min,\ell_{2}}}\right\|_{2}^{\frac{1}{c}}\leq m^{-\frac{1}{2c}}\left\|{{{\mathbf{a}}}_{\min,\ell_{2}}}\right\|_{2}.

Plugging this in, we obtain the required final expression.

\displaystyle\min_{{{\mathbf{a}}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m},{{\mathbf{U}}}{{\mathbf{a}}}={{\mathbf{y}}}}\Upsilon({{\mathbf{w}}})=d^{\frac{c}{c+1}}\left({\frac{\left\|{{{\mathbf{a}}}_{\min,\ell_{2}}}\right\|_{2}^{2}}{\left\|{{{\mathbf{a}}}_{\min,\ell_{2}}}\right\|_{\frac{2c}{c-1}}}}\right)^{\frac{2c}{c+1}}\asymp d^{\frac{c}{c+1}}m^{\frac{1}{c+1}}\left\|{{{\mathbf{a}}}_{\min,\ell_{2}}}\right\|_{2}^{\frac{2c}{c+1}}.

This completes the proof.
∎

Proof of Lem.
6
.

Note that, by Cauchy-Schwarz,

\displaystyle\left\|{{{\mathbf{a}}}_{\min,\ell_{2}}}\right\|_{2}^{2}={{\mathbf{y}}}^{\top}\widetilde{{{\mathbf{U}}}}{{\mathbf{y}}}\geq\lambda_{\max}(\widetilde{{{\mathbf{U}}}})\left\|{{{\mathbf{y}}}}\right\|_{2}^{2}=\sigma_{\max}^{-2}({{\mathbf{U}}})\left\|{{{\mathbf{y}}}}\right\|^{2}

From Lem.
2
, with probability,
1-\delta
,
\sigma_{\max}^{-2}({{\mathbf{U}}})\gtrsim\frac{1}{mn}
.

Therefore, to bound
\left\|{{{\mathbf{a}}}_{\min,\ell_{2}}}\right\|
, we need to bound
\left\|{{{\mathbf{y}}}}\right\|_{2}^{2}
. Decomposing it into the signal and noise terms
{{\mathbf{y}}}^{\star}
and
{{\mathbf{n}}}
respectively, we obtain,

\displaystyle\left\|{{{\mathbf{y}}}}\right\|_{2}^{2}=\left\|{{{\mathbf{y}}}^{\star}+{{\mathbf{n}}}}\right\|_{2}^{2}=\left\|{{{\mathbf{y}}}^{\star}}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\left\|{{{\mathbf{n}}}}\right\|_{2}^{2}+2\left\langle{{{\mathbf{n}}}},{{{\mathbf{y}}}^{\star}}\right\rangle.

(9)

We will bound each of the
3
terms in the above equation.
For the first term
\left\|{{{\mathbf{y}}}^{\star}}\right\|_{2}^{2}
. Note that it is the
\ell_{2}
norm of an
n
-dimensional each. Each coordinate of
{{\mathbf{y}}}^{\star}
is an independent random variable
\sigma^{\star}(\Theta^{\star}{{\mathbf{x}}}_{i})
. As
{{\mathbf{x}}}_{i}
is Gaussian and
\Theta^{\star}
is an orthonormal matrix,
\Theta^{\star}{{\mathbf{x}}}_{i}\sim{\mathcal{N}}({\mathbf{0}},{\mathbb{I}}_{m^{\star}})
. Further, from Assump.
2
,
\sigma^{\star}(\Theta^{\star}{{\mathbf{x}}}_{i})
is a
(\frac{c}{2},{\mathcal{O}}(1))
- Sub-Weibull random variable, as
\left\|{{\mathbb{I}}_{m^{\star}}}\right\|_{F}=\sqrt{m^{\star}}={\mathcal{O}}(1)
, and
{\mathbb{E}}\left[{{(\sigma^{\star})^{2}(\Theta^{\star}{{\mathbf{x}}}_{i})}}\right]=\psi^{\star}=\Theta(1)
. Further,
\left\|{{{\mathbf{y}}}^{\star}}\right\|_{2}^{2}
is a quadratic form of Sub-Weibull random variables, so from Lem.
16
, with probability
1-\delta
,

\displaystyle\left\|{{{\mathbf{y}}}^{\star}}\right\|_{2}^{2}\asymp n.

As for the second term which is an inner product, using Sub-Gaussian concentration
(
62
, Chapter 2)
for
{{\mathbf{n}}}
with probability
1-\delta
,

\displaystyle 2\left\langle{{{\mathbf{n}}}},{{{\mathbf{y}}}^{\star}}\right\rangle\geq-2\zeta\left\|{{{\mathbf{y}}}^{\star}}\right\|_{2}\sqrt{2\log(1/\delta)}\gtrsim-\zeta\sqrt{n}.

For the third term
\left\|{{{\mathbf{n}}}}\right\|_{2}^{2}
, which is a quadratic form of the gaussian random variables
\{\xi_{i}\}_{i\in[n]}
, by Hanson-Wright
(
53
, Thm. 1)
, with probability
1-\delta
, we have,

\displaystyle\left\|{{{\mathbf{n}}}}\right\|_{2}^{2}\geq\zeta^{2}n-\zeta^{2}\nu_{1}\max\{\sqrt{n\log(2/\delta),\log(2/\delta)}\}\gtrsim n\zeta^{2}.

Plugging these bounds into Eq (
9
), along with the bounds on
\sigma_{\max}({{\mathbf{U}}})
, with probability,
1-4\delta
, we have,

\displaystyle\left\|{{{\mathbf{a}}}_{\min,\ell_{2}}}\right\|_{2}^{2}={{\mathbf{y}}}^{\top}\widetilde{{{\mathbf{U}}}}{{\mathbf{y}}}\gtrsim\frac{1+\zeta^{2}}{m}-\frac{\zeta}{m\sqrt{n}}\gtrsim\frac{1}{m}.

Note that
\zeta=o(1)
.

To prove the condition between,
\ell_{2}
and
\ell_{\infty}
norms of
{{\mathbf{a}}}_{\min,\ell_{2}}
. Note that
\sigma_{\max}({{\mathbf{U}}})
corresponds to the all-ones singular vector. Therefore, to achieve the lower bound on
\left\|{{{\mathbf{a}}}_{\min,\ell_{2}}}\right\|_{2}\asymp\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}
, we need
{{\mathbf{a}}}_{\min,\ell_{2}}
to be almost parallel to
{\mathbf{1}}_{m}
. Since,
\left\|{{\mathbf{1}}_{m}}\right\|_{\infty}=\frac{\left\|{{\mathbf{1}}_{m}}\right\|_{2}}{\sqrt{m}}
, any vector almost parallel to
{\mathbf{1}}_{m}
also satisfies the required condition.This completes the proof.

∎

C.3
Flatness of Bad Interpolators: Proof of Thm.
2

In this section, we provide lower bounds on flatness after rescaling,

Existence of Bad Interpolators.

In Def.

Proof of Thm.
5
.

From App.
B
, the population risk is given by,

\displaystyle F({{\mathbf{w}}})=\frac{1}{2}\left({\zeta^{2}+\psi^{\star}+{{\mathbf{a}}}^{\top}\Phi{{\mathbf{a}}}-2\left\langle{{{\mathbf{a}}}},{\widetilde{\Psi}}\right\rangle}\right)

We need an upper bound on
F({{\mathbf{w}}})
under the constraint

\left\|{{{\mathbf{a}}}}\right\|_{\infty}\leq m^{-1}\left({\sqrt{\frac{\psi^{\star}}{\phi^{\star}}}+\gamma n^{-\epsilon}}\right)
.

We first bound the quadratic term in
{{\mathbf{a}}}
.

\displaystyle{{\mathbf{a}}}^{\top}\Phi{{\mathbf{a}}}\leq

\displaystyle\left\|{{{\mathbf{a}}}}\right\|_{2}^{2}\lambda_{\max}(\Phi)\leq\left\|{{{\mathbf{a}}}}\right\|_{2}^{2}m\phi^{\star}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle m^{2}\left\|{{{\mathbf{a}}}}\right\|_{\infty}^{2}\phi^{\star}=\frac{m^{2}\psi^{\star}(1+3\gamma n^{-\epsilon})}{m^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\psi^{\star}(1+3\gamma n^{-\epsilon})

Since each element of the symmetric matrix
\Phi\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times m}
is
\leq\phi^{\star}
, its maximum eigenvalue is
m\phi^{\star}
.

We now need to maximize the linear term
-\left\langle{{{\mathbf{a}}}},{\widetilde{\Psi}}\right\rangle
in terms of
{{\mathbf{a}}}
under the constraint on
\left\|{{{\mathbf{a}}}}\right\|_{\infty}
, and interpolation (
{{\mathbf{U}}}{{\mathbf{a}}}={{\mathbf{y}}}
). This is equivalent to minimizing the negative of this linear term under the same constraints.

Therefore, we need to find a valid lower bound on the following objective,

\displaystyle\min_{{{\mathbf{a}}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}\left\langle{{{\mathbf{a}}}},{\widetilde{\Psi}}\right\rangle\quad\text{ s.t. }\left\|{{{\mathbf{a}}}}\right\|_{\infty}\leq m^{-1}\left({\sqrt{\frac{\psi^{\star}}{\phi^{\star}}}+\gamma n^{-\epsilon}}\right)\quad\text{ and }{{\mathbf{U}}}{{\mathbf{a}}}={{\mathbf{y}}}

Note that this is a linear objective with convex and linear constraints, hence strong duality holds for this problem. Using lagrange multipliers
{{\mathbf{r}}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
and
\bar{r}\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
for the equality and
\ell_{\infty}
norm constraints respectively, the primal objective is given by,

\displaystyle\min_{{{\mathbf{a}}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\max_{{{\mathbf{r}}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n},\bar{r}\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\left\langle{{{\mathbf{a}}}},{\widetilde{\Psi}}\right\rangle+\bar{r}\left({\left\|{{{\mathbf{a}}}}\right\|_{\infty}-m^{-1}\left({\sqrt{\frac{\psi^{\star}}{\phi^{\star}}}+\gamma n^{-\epsilon}}\right)}\right)+\left\langle{{{\mathbf{r}}}},{{{\mathbf{U}}}{{\mathbf{a}}}-{{\mathbf{y}}}}\right\rangle

The dual of this problem is given by,

\displaystyle\max_{{{\mathbf{r}}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n},\bar{r}\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\min_{{{\mathbf{a}}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\left\langle{{{\mathbf{a}}}},{\widetilde{\Psi}}\right\rangle+\bar{r}\left({\left\|{{{\mathbf{a}}}}\right\|_{\infty}-m^{-1}\left({\sqrt{\frac{\psi^{\star}}{\phi^{\star}}}+\gamma n^{-\epsilon}}\right)}\right)+\left\langle{{{\mathbf{r}}}},{{{\mathbf{U}}}{{\mathbf{a}}}-{{\mathbf{y}}}}\right\rangle

Since the objective function is a convex function in
{{\mathbf{a}}}
, we can minimize it.
Collecting the terms of only
{{\mathbf{a}}}
, we need to minimize,

\displaystyle\left\langle{{{\mathbf{a}}}},{\widetilde{\Psi}+{{\mathbf{U}}}^{\top}{{\mathbf{r}}}}\right\rangle+\bar{r}\left\|{{{\mathbf{a}}}}\right\|_{\infty}\geq\left\|{{{\mathbf{a}}}}\right\|_{\infty}\left({\bar{r}-\left\|{\widetilde{\Psi}+{{\mathbf{U}}}^{\top}{{\mathbf{r}}}}\right\|_{1}}\right)

If the term multiplied by
\left\|{{{\mathbf{a}}}}\right\|_{\infty}
is
<0
, then we can set
\left\|{{{\mathbf{a}}}}\right\|_{\infty}\to\infty
, so that the dual problem is not feasible. For it to be feasible, we need the coefficient of
\left\|{{{\mathbf{a}}}}\right\|_{\infty}
to be non-negative, in which case we setting
\left\|{{{\mathbf{a}}}}\right\|_{\infty}=0
minimizes the problem in terms of
{{\mathbf{a}}}
.
This requires,
\bar{r}\geq\left\|{\widetilde{\Psi}+{{\mathbf{U}}}^{\top}{{\mathbf{r}}}}\right\|_{1}
.
Therefore, the dual problem becomes,

\displaystyle\max_{{{\mathbf{r}}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n},\bar{r}\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}-\left\langle{{{\mathbf{r}}}},{{{\mathbf{y}}}}\right\rangle-\bar{r}m^{-1}\left({\sqrt{\frac{\psi^{\star}}{\phi^{\star}}}+\gamma n^{-\epsilon}}\right),\quad\text{ s.t. }\bar{r}\geq\left\|{\widetilde{\Psi}+{{\mathbf{U}}}^{\top}{{\mathbf{r}}}}\right\|_{1}

By strong duality, the maximum of this linear objective has the same value as the minimum of the primal objective.This problem again does not have a closed-form solution. If we plug in a value of
{{\mathbf{r}}}
and
\bar{r}
, we get a lower bound to the optimal dual objective and thus also a lower bound to optimal primal objective.

We set
{{\mathbf{r}}}=-\frac{{{\mathbf{y}}}}{n}
and
\bar{r}=\left\|{\widetilde{\Psi}+{{\mathbf{U}}}^{\top}{{\mathbf{r}}}}\right\|_{1}
. The lower bound on the objective is ,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\left\|{{{\mathbf{y}}}}\right\|_{2}^{2}-\frac{\left\|{\widetilde{\Psi}-\frac{1}{n}{{\mathbf{U}}}^{\top}{{\mathbf{y}}}}\right\|_{1}}{m}\left({\sqrt{\frac{\psi^{\star}}{\phi^{\star}}}+\gamma n^{-\epsilon}}\right)

We need a lower bound on
\left\|{{{\mathbf{y}}}}\right\|_{2}^{2}
and an upper bound on
\left\|{\widetilde{\Psi}+{{\mathbf{U}}}^{\top}{{\mathbf{r}}}}\right\|_{1}
.

Lower bound on
\left\|{{{\mathbf{y}}}}\right\|_{2}^{2}

Note that
\left\|{{{\mathbf{y}}}}\right\|_{2}^{2}=\left\|{{{\mathbf{y}}}^{\star}+{{\mathbf{n}}}}\right\|_{2}^{2}
. Conditioned on
{{\mathbf{y}}}^{\star}
,
{{\mathbf{y}}}\sim{\mathcal{N}}({{\mathbf{y}}}^{\star},\zeta^{2}{\mathbb{I}}_{n})
. Therefore, using Hanson-Wright
(
53
, Thm. 1)
, conditioned on

, with probability
1-\delta

\displaystyle\left\|{{{\mathbf{y}}}}\right\|_{2}^{2}\geq\left\|{{{\mathbf{y}}}^{\star}}\right\|_{2}^{2}+n\zeta^{2}-n\zeta^{2}\sqrt{\log(2/\delta)}\geq\left\|{{{\mathbf{y}}}^{\star}}\right\|_{2}^{2}

For

.
Without conditioning, the probability of error is
\underset{{{\mathbf{y}}}^{\star}}{{\mathbb{E}}}\left[{{\delta\,|\,{{\mathbf{y}}}^{\star}}}\right]=\delta
.

Since
\left\|{{{\mathbf{y}}}^{\star}}\right\|_{2}^{2}
is a quadratic form of independent
(\frac{c}{2},{\mathcal{O}}(1))
-Sub-Weibull random variables, using Hanson-Wright (Lem.
16
), with probability
1-\delta
,

\displaystyle\left\|{{{\mathbf{y}}}^{\star}}\right\|_{2}^{2}\gtrsim n\psi^{\star}-\sqrt{n\log(2/\delta)}

Upper bound on
\left\|{\widetilde{\Psi}-\frac{1}{n}{{\mathbf{U}}}^{\top}{{\mathbf{y}}}}\right\|_{1}

Let’s consider the
j^{th}
coordinate of this vector using the definition of
\widetilde{\Psi}
.

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[{{\sigma^{\star}(\Theta^{\star}{{\mathbf{x}}})\sigma(\left\langle{\theta_{j}},{{{\mathbf{x}}}}\right\rangle)}}\right]-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma(\left\langle{\theta_{j}},{{{\mathbf{x}}}_{i}}\right\rangle)(\sigma^{\star}(\Theta^{\star}{{\mathbf{x}}}_{i})+\xi_{i})

\displaystyle=\underset{I_{j}}{\underbrace{{\mathbb{E}}\left[{{\sigma^{\star}(\Theta^{\star}{{\mathbf{x}}})\sigma(\left\langle{\theta_{j}},{{{\mathbf{x}}}}\right\rangle)}}\right]-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma(\left\langle{\theta_{j}},{{{\mathbf{x}}}_{i}}\right\rangle)\sigma^{\star}(\Theta^{\star}{{\mathbf{x}}}_{i})}}+\underset{II_{j}}{\underbrace{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma(\left\langle{\theta_{j}},{{{\mathbf{x}}}_{i}}\right\rangle)\xi_{i}}}.

This is the difference between the empirical mean and the expectation of a random variable
\sigma(\left\langle{\theta_{j}},{{{\mathbf{x}}}_{i}}\right\rangle)(\sigma^{\star}(\Theta^{\star}{{\mathbf{x}}}_{i})+\xi_{i})
. We bound the two terms separately.

Bound on
I_{j}

Note that
\sigma(\left\langle{\theta_{j}},{{{\mathbf{x}}}_{i}}\right\rangle)\sigma^{\star}(\Theta^{\star}{{\mathbf{x}}}_{i})
is a product of two
(\frac{c}{2},{\mathcal{O}}(1))
-Sub-Weibull random variables. Therefore, independent of their correlation, this is a
(c,{\mathcal{O}}(1))
-Sub-Weibull random variable (Lem.
14
).

Using Lem.
15
, with probability
1-\delta
,

\displaystyle I_{j}\lesssim\sqrt{\frac{\log(2m/\delta)}{n}},\quad\forall j\in[m]

The term
\log(2m/\delta)
comes due to a union bound over all
j\in[m]
with probability of error
\delta/m
for each
j\in[m]
.

Bound on

Conditioning on
\{{{\mathbf{x}}}_{i}\}_{i\in[n]}

, with probability

\displaystyle II_{j}\leq\zeta\frac{\sqrt{\log(2m/\delta)\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma^{2}(\left\langle{\theta_{j}},{{{\mathbf{x}}}_{i}}\right\rangle)}}{n}

, giving the term of

.

Without conditioning, the probability of error is also
\delta
for this bound.

Note that

This provides the following bound on

\displaystyle II_{j}\lesssim\frac{\zeta}{\sqrt{n}}\lesssim\frac{1}{n}

For the final step we use

\displaystyle\left\|{\widetilde{\Psi}-\frac{1}{n}{{\mathbf{U}}}^{\top}{{\mathbf{y}}}}\right\|_{1}\leq

\displaystyle\lesssim

D.5.1

For
Single-Index
models defined in Section

\forall{{\mathbf{x}}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}

\displaystyle h({{\mathbf{w}}},{{\mathbf{x}}})=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{1}{m}\sigma(\left\langle{\theta^{\star}},{{{\mathbf{x}}}}\right\rangle)=y.

Therefore, this is indeed an interpolator, and it is good, as
F_{S}({{\mathbf{w}}})=F({{\mathbf{w}}})=0

{{\mathbf{a}}}
into Lem.

.

\Upsilon({{\mathbf{w}}})\asymp d^{\frac{c}{c+1}}m^{-\frac{c-1}{c+1}}\asymp\Upsilon^{\star}

D.5.2
Proof of Prop.
2

For the sufficient condition, i.e.,
\left\|{{{\mathbf{a}}}}\right\|_{\infty}\implies\Upsilon({{\mathbf{w}}})\asymp\Upsilon^{\star}
, we find an upper bound on
\left\|{{{\mathbf{a}}}}\right\|_{\frac{2c}{c+1}}^{\frac{2c}{c+1}}
. Note that,

\displaystyle\left\|{{{\mathbf{a}}}}\right\|_{\frac{2c}{c+1}}^{\frac{2c}{c+1}}\lesssim mm^{-\frac{2c}{c+1}}=m^{-\frac{c-1}{c+1}}.

Combining this with Lem.
1
, we prove the sufficient condition with high probability.

For the necessary condition, we need to show

\displaystyle\Upsilon({{\mathbf{w}}})\asymp\Upsilon^{\star}\implies\left\|{{{\mathbf{a}}}}\right\|_{\frac{2c}{c+1}}^{\frac{2c}{c+1}}\asymp m^{-\frac{c-1}{c+1}}.

For an interpolator
{{\mathbf{w}}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m(d+1)}
with unit-norm inner-layer weights, we have
{{\mathbf{U}}}{{\mathbf{a}}}={{\mathbf{y}}}
. Therefore, the outer-layer weights satisfy the following with high probability.

\displaystyle\left\|{{{\mathbf{a}}}}\right\|_{2}\geq\left\|{{{\mathbf{a}}}_{\min,\ell_{2}}}\right\|_{2}\gtrsim\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}.

Here, we use Lem.
6
, which holds for all interpolators.

We will show that a
{{\mathbf{w}}}
that is flattest and an interpolator is not possible unless
\left\|{{{\mathbf{a}}}}\right\|_{\infty}={\mathcal{O}}(m^{-1})
.
By contradiction, assume that there is a subset of coordinates
{\mathcal{Q}}\subset[m]
of size
\left|{{\mathcal{Q}}}\right|=q
such that
\left|{a_{j}}\right|\asymp m^{-b},\forall j\in{\mathcal{Q}}
for some
b>1
, and for all coordinates
j\notin{\mathcal{Q}}
,
\left|{a_{j}}\right|\leq{\mathcal{O}}(m^{-1})
. Note that
\left\|{{{\mathbf{a}}}}\right\|_{\infty}=m^{-b}
. If
q=\Theta(m)
, then
{{\mathbf{a}}}
is the flattest interpolator, i.e.,
\left\|{{{\mathbf{a}}}}\right\|_{\frac{2c}{c+1}}^{\frac{2c}{c+1}}\lesssim m^{-\frac{c-1}{c+1}}
, iff
b=1
. Therefore, we assume that
q=o(m)
. Since,
n=\Theta(m)
,
q=o(n)
.

Now, note that the vector
{{\mathbf{a}}}
is close to a
q
-sparse vector. Let
{{\mathbf{a}}}={{\mathbf{a}}}_{{\mathcal{Q}}}+{{\mathbf{a}}}_{{\mathcal{Q}}^{c}}
be its decomposition into two vectors with different magnitude of coordinates. Note thar
{{\mathbf{a}}}_{{\mathcal{Q}}}
is
q
-sparse. To ensure interpolation, we require
{{\mathbf{U}}}{{\mathbf{a}}}={{\mathbf{y}}}
, or
{{\mathbf{U}}}{{\mathbf{a}}}_{{\mathcal{Q}}}={{\mathbf{y}}}-{{\mathbf{U}}}{{\mathbf{a}}}_{{\mathcal{Q}}^{c}}=\widetilde{{{\mathbf{y}}}}
. Additionally, note that
\widetilde{{{\mathbf{y}}}}
is an
n
-dimensional vector, and
{{\mathbf{a}}}_{{\mathcal{Q}}}
is a
q
-sparse vector. We will show that with high probability, we cannot interpolate. For a fixed
{{\mathbf{a}}}_{{\mathcal{Q}}}
that is
q
-sparse,

\displaystyle\Pr{{{\mathbf{U}}}{{\mathbf{a}}}_{{\mathcal{Q}}}-\widetilde{{{\mathbf{y}}}}=0}\leq\vartheta^{n}.

Note that
\widetilde{{{\mathbf{y}}}}
has each coordinate independent, and
{{\mathbf{U}}}
also has independent rows. Further, each of these variables has non-zero mean, and a continuous distribution, so
\vartheta
is their probability density function at
0
. Due to independence, we multiply it
n
times.

Now, taking a union bound over set of
q
sparse-vectors with each coordinate of magnitude
m^{-b}
, we obtain,

\displaystyle\Pr{{{\mathbf{U}}}{{\mathbf{a}}}_{{\mathcal{Q}}}-\widetilde{{{\mathbf{y}}}}=0}\leq\exp(-n\log(1/\vartheta)+q\log(m/q)).

Since,
q=o(n)
, this probability is bounded by
\exp(-\widetilde{C}^{\prime}n)
for some constant
\widetilde{C}^{\prime}>0
. Therefore, for any
b<1
, with high probability, if
{{\mathbf{w}}}
is flattest, it will not interpolate. This provides a contradiction and thus
b\geq 1
, which provides us with the necessary condition.

D.6
Flattest minima with inner-layer bias

One of the settings in
(
63
)
uses a bias
\{b_{j}\}_{j\in[m]}
with the inner layer weights. This increases the dimension of the weights, as
{{\mathbf{w}}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m(d+2)}
, and the output of the network is
h({{\mathbf{w}}},{{\mathbf{x}}})=\sum_{j=1}^{m}a_{j}\sigma(\left\langle{\theta_{j}},{{{\mathbf{x}}}}\right\rangle+b_{j}),\forall{{\mathbf{x}}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. The only change that this causes in Lem.
1
, is to replace the terms of
\left\langle{\theta_{j}},{{{\mathbf{x}}}}\right\rangle
in
B(\theta_{j})
by
\left\langle{\theta_{j}},{{{\mathbf{x}}}}\right\rangle+b_{j}
. From Section
D.2.1
,
\sigma,\sigma^{2},\sigma^{\prime}
are still
(\varrho,K)
-Sub-Weibull, but now their means, variances and Orlicz norm
K
has increased by a term proportional to
\left|{b_{j}}\right|^{c}
. Note that this holds for activations that do not have any piece being
0
, i.e.,
c^{\prime}\neq 0,c^{\prime\prime}\neq 0
in Assump.
1
. Therefore, to minimize flatness, the optimal choice is to set
b_{j}=0
. Further, even for the bounds on
\left\|{{{\mathbf{a}}}}\right\|_{\frac{2c}{c+1}}^{\frac{2c}{c+1}}

1
,
4
,
2
, all the Sub-Weibull random variables have an additional term of
\left|{b_{j}}\right|

. Therefore, for our setting even with inner-layer bias, the flattest minima are achieved when this inner layer bias is set to

.
