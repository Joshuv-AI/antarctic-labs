Abstract
. As large language models (LLMs) evolve from single-user assistants to active participants in civic and workplace deliberation, evaluating their effects on collective decision making becomes a governance challenge. We present two empirical studies (N=879) of real-time, text-based group deliberation in an incentive-compatible charity allocation task with real financial stakes ($7,200 USD). Groups of three allocate a donation budget under varying LLM facilitation conditions: Study 1 (N=204) compares three frontier models; Study 2 (N=675) compares facilitator strategies against a no-facilitation baseline. Across both studies, LLM facilitation did not significantly improve group consensus in either study, yet participants consistently preferred facilitated discussion. We additionally identify two governance-relevant risks. First, algorithmic steering : facilitators shifted select charity-level allocations by up to 5.5 percentage points—directly affecting the final charitable payout—even when aggregate agreement metrics remained unchanged. Second, an illusion of inclusion : participants cited inclusivity as their primary reason for preferring LLM facilitators, yet neither survey nor transcript-based measures of participation equity improved. Notably, participants reported greater trust in the process under the same conditions where facilitators exerted directional influence on outcomes. Together, these findings show that in AI-mediated group deliberation, perceived procedural improvement can coexist with measurable steering and unchanged participation inequality, motivating evaluation practices that treat collective outcomes, interaction dynamics, and participant perceptions as distinct governance targets.

1.
Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used in social and collective settings to summarize discussions, coordinate decisions, and facilitate deliberation in civic and workplace contexts
(
Tessler et al. 2024
;
Li et al. 2025
;
Konya et al. 2025
;
Napolitan Institute and Jigsaw 2025
)
. This shift raises governance-relevant challenges that are largely absent in one-on-one interactions: in group settings, values and preferences can diverge across participants, and facilitation may affect not only whether groups converge, but whose views are surfaced, how disagreement is handled, and whether the process feels legitimate.

Prior work has introduced LLM-based facilitation and mediation strategies, but evaluations often emphasize either downstream outcomes or subjective experience, without jointly examining conversational dynamics
(
Buçinca et al. 2020
;
Tan et al. 2024
;
Qian et al. 2025c
)
.
Recent improvements in model latency and conversational capabilities have made it possible to evaluate LLM facilitation in real-time group discussions at scale
. This enables the study of synchronous, time-boxed interactions—scenarios such as team meetings or community town halls—that move beyond LLMs’ proven capabilities in post-hoc summarization and asynchronous mediation.

We contribute two empirical studies (
N=879
) of real-time, text-based group deliberation in an incentive-compatible charity allocation task. In groups of three, participants help to allocate a real donation budget (total $7200 USD) across nine charities. Each group completes three rounds of deliberation, with a different facilitator treatment presented in randomized order. In Study 1 (N=204), we vary the
facilitator model
, comparing three frontier models (
Gemini 2.5 Flash, Claude 4.5 Haiku, and GPT-5 mini
) under a constant, minimally specified prompt. In Study 2 (N=675), we hold the model fixed (Gemini) and vary the
facilitator strategy
, comparing a summarization-centric facilitator and a principles-guided facilitator against a no-facilitator baseline. Ultimately, this design enables a multi-dimensional evaluation of real-time group-AI interaction, pairing actual financial stakes with granular measures of dialogue, consensus, and multi-user preferences.

Our primary outcome is group-level consensus; we also evaluate how facilitation shapes decision distributions, conversational dynamics, and participant perceptions. Across both studies, facilitation by LLM agents does not significantly improve aggregate consensus, yet participants prefer facilitation over the no-facilitator baseline. At the same time, facilitators shift
what
groups decide: we observe topic-specific steering in allocations even when aggregate consensus is unchanged. Participants report greater perceived inclusion and coordination with LLMs, though transcript-based measures show largely unchanged participation inequality and a slight reduction in human discussion. Finally, facilitator preference is participant- and context-dependent: participants who report higher investment in the outcomes prefer facilitation at higher rates.

Together, this work demonstrates that LLM facilitation in real-time decision making cannot be evaluated through a single outcome measure alone. We find that while real-time, LLM-based facilitation doesn’t provide material improvements to group consensus, participants consistently perceive the facilitated process as better. Furthermore, facilitation can fundamentally alter how decisions are made and distributed.
These findings motivate evaluation and deployment practices for multi-user AI systems that treat outcomes, processes, and perceptions as distinct governance targets.

1.1.
Related Work

As LLMs are increasingly used in multi-user contexts in roles such as facilitators, mediators, and moderators, it is important to evaluate how they can affect group dynamics and collective decision-making.

LLM facilitation and evaluation in applied settings.

Prior work shows that LLMs as facilitators can affect group outcomes in settings such as debate, mediation, and coordination
(
The Verge 2025
;
Konya et al. 2025
;
Li et al. 2025
;
Qian et al. 2025a
)
,
but these evaluations often emphasize either downstream outcomes, such as agreement or task completion, or perceptual outcomes, such as satisfaction and preference
(
SwayBeta 2025
)
. The literature on human-computer interaction shows that user satisfaction can be a poor proxy for downstream reasoning quality or task performance
(
Buçinca et al. 2020
;
Qian and Wexler 2024
)
, and finds that LLM facilitation may not reliably improve group performance
(
Alsobay et al. 2025
)
.
We build upon this by evaluating LLM facilitation across multiple dimensions, including a primary consensus outcome, and secondary conversational dynamics, decision distributions, and participant perceptions.

Comparisons against human baselines.

Studies comparing LLM vs. human facilitators show that participants may rate LLM-generated interventions favorably on measures such as perceived performance, information quality, or understandability
(
Koçak et al. 2025
;
Tan et al. 2024
;
Kim et al. 2025
)
.
However, these studies evaluate isolated facilitator utterances, constrained turn-taking, or domain-specific disputes rather than free-form group deliberation with downstream task outcomes
(
Tan et al. 2024
)
.
By comparing multiple LLM facilitation regimes in live, synchronous group interaction, we evaluate not only participant judgments, but also consensus change, allocation shifts, and conversational dynamics.

Facilitation strategies.

Prior work on LLM-based facilitation include summarization-based mediation
(
Tessler et al. 2024
)
, principles-guided prompting, consensus-oriented refinement, and adversarial or devil’s-advocate interventions
(
Pitre et al. 2025
;
Chen et al. 2025
;
Chiang et al. 2024
;
Govers et al. 2024
;
Matsumura et al. 2025
;
Papachristou et al. 2025
)
.
These findings suggest that facilitation strategy matters, particularly in asynchronous coordination and simulated settings. Leveraging advances in LLMs’ capabilities for synchronous communication, we now have the opportunity to evaluate these facilitation methods in real-time experimental conditions.

Failure modes in facilitation and group dynamics
.

Literature on generative models identifies failure modes in generative facilitation, including hallucination, overconfidence, sycophancy, and value-laden “neutrality”
(
Choi et al. 2025
;
Taubenfeld et al. 2024
;
Garcia et al. 2024
;
Kasirzadeh and Gabriel 2022
)
. Constitutional and principle-based methods have been proposed to mitigate these risks by iteratively refining system behavior
(
Bai et al. 2022
;
Petridis et al. 2024
;
Maiya et al. 2025
;
Thakur et al. 2025
)
.

Separate literature on group decision-making identifies recurring process failures in coordination tasks
(
Janssens et al. 2022
;
Karpowitz et al. 2012
)
, including premature consensus
(
Janis 1982
)
, biased information sharing
(
Stasser and Titus 1985
)
, and unequal participation
(
Woolley et al. 2010
;
Siu 2017
)
, which may be exacerbated in online settings
(
Small et al. 2023
)
. Guided by this literature, we develop a principles-based facilitator to address such breakdowns: model-side risks such as overconfidence and value-laden neutrality, and interaction-level failures such as weak justification, self-contained reasoning, and premature consensus.

Empirical gap.

Taken together, prior work provides a strong foundation for understanding LLM mediation in simulated environments, asynchronous tasks, and primarily through the lens of individual user satisfaction.
Building upon this foundation, our paper studies real-time, LLM-based facilitation in synchronous human group deliberation, leveraging low-latency frontier models that make live facilitation increasingly feasible at scale. This allows us to evaluate not only whether facilitation is preferred, but how it shapes agreement, decision distributions, participation, and perceived inclusion as the interaction unfolds.

Our contribution is synthesizing these outcome, process, and perception measures together in a controlled human study of live AI-mediated deliberation.

Research Questions

To characterize how LLM facilitation affects group decision-making, we study the following:

•

RQ1 (Consensus outcomes):
How does LLM facilitation affect groups’ consensus formation on a collective allocation task?

•

RQ2 (Individual preferences):
How do participants evaluate different facilitation styles and models, independent of outcome improvements?

•

RQ3 (Interaction dynamics):
How does LLM facilitation shape conversational structure and participation patterns in group deliberation?

In addition to these pre-specified research questions, we report exploratory analyses of
decision distributions
across charities, which revealed evidence of algorithmic steering under facilitation.

Experiment design

We chose
charity allocations
as the domain for the consensus-seeking task; in the task, participants help to allocate a real $7,200 donation budget across nine charities spanning diverse causes (e.g., humanitarian relief, crisis response, global health, and environmental conservation).

3.1.
Experiment notation and
consensus measures

In the studies, participants are randomly assigned to groups of three and complete three rounds of deliberation.
In each round
i\in\{1,2,3\}
, the group allocates a unit budget across three charities

C_{i}=\{c_{i0},c_{i1},c_{i2}\}
sampled without replacement from the set of nine. Each round involves 1) an individual, initial pre-discussion
allocation across the three charities, 2) a 5-minute group discussion with team members, and 3) an individual, post-discussion allocation (stages shown in Appendix Figure
7
).

Allocations.

Let
s\in\{0,1\}
denote the pre-discussion (0) or post-discussion (1) stage.
In each allocation, participants distribute a
unit budget
normalized to 1 across the three charities in the round;
a_{p,s,i}(c_{ij})\in[0,1]
denotes the share of a unit budget (summing to 1) that participant
p
allocates to charity
c_{ij}
in round
i
at stage
s
. We denote all of a participant p’s allocations as the vector
A_{p,s,i}
.

Consensus.

For each round
i
and stage
s
, we compute Krippendorff’s
\alpha
(interval metric)
(
Krippendorff 2004
)

across the three participants as the consensus score
\alpha_{s,i}
, treating each charity
j\in\{0,1,2\}
as a unit and the
assigned share
a_{p,s,i}(c_{ij})
as the interval-valued judgment.
3
3

3

For robustness, alternative consensus and influence metrics are reported in Appendix
G
, including Euclidean distance and standard deviation.
For ease of interpretation, all
\alpha
scores are scaled and shifted by
\alpha=(3\cdot 100\alpha+100)/4
, and clamped to the range [0, 100], to represent a 0–100 metric.

The change in group-level consensus score pre- and post- discussion is then
\Delta\alpha_{i}=\alpha_{1,i}-\alpha_{0,i}
.

We treat group-level change in consensus,
\Delta\alpha_{i}
, as the primary outcome throughout the paper. Analyses of steering effects and conversational dynamics are secondary and, where noted, exploratory.

3.2.
Treatment design

In both studies, groups complete three rounds. Each round consists of an initial allocation, a group discussion stage, and a final allocation. Each round’s discussion stage is conducted under one of three facilitator treatment conditions, with condition order randomized within groups.

Timing considerations.
Each round spans approximately 15 minutes, with the live group discussion capped at five minutes. While this short window is operationally efficient for a multi-round experiment, it also targets an important and under-explored class of "micro-deliberation" settings, such as workplace stand-up meetings or rapid committee votes. Modern work and civic settings frequently rely on these brief, synchronous interactions, yet they rarely receive structured facilitation because human intervention is cost-prohibitive at this scale. Real-time LLMs offer a scalable alternative, and this design lets us test their capacity to mitigate process failures within highly compressed decision-making windows.

Human facilitator baseline.
Our study design focuses on no-facilitator and LLM-facilitator conditions. While a human-facilitator baseline would be informative in theory, it is largely cost-prohibitive in practice for the short-horizon interactions we study. Human professional facilitators are rarely deployed for five-minute meetings, making automated interventions a unique logistical opportunity; even a marginally effective LLM facilitator would offer a substantial improvement over the status quo of no facilitation at all. Methodologically, holding the facilitator fixed also eliminates facilitator-to-facilitator variation, allowing us to compare model and strategy effects in tightly matched conditions across a much larger sample than would be feasible with human facilitation.

After providing informed consent, they were assigned to live 3-person groups with random, pseudonymous, animal-based identifiers (e.g. ‘Lion‘, ‘Goose‘). Incentives and payment.

Participants were compensated at $15 USD/hour. To promote incentive compatibility, we informed participants that greater within-group consensus would increase the weight placed on their group’s final allocation in determining the experiment’s real charitable payout. Before the rounds began, a comprehension check verified that they understood the key incentive: greater agreement within the group increased the influence of their group’s final allocation on the real charitable payout.

Sample size rationale.

RQ1 (consensus) is defined at the group level, so the independent unit is the 3-person group.

4.3.
RQ3: Interaction dynamics

Conversation structure.

Table
1
reports the number of turns and total words per round for humans and LLMs. Groups engaged in substantial interaction within the five-minute discussion window. In the no-LLM baseline, groups exchanged an average of 15.0 human turns per round—roughly 3 unique messages per minute. Under facilitation, participants contributed fewer turns (approximately 10–14 per round), but this was offset by multiple facilitator interventions; the principles-based facilitator, for instance, responded about once per minute (Table
1
).

LLM facilitation increases total message volume relative to the no-LLM baseline, driven by additional LLM messages rather than greater human participation. Notably, in RQ2 we found that participants prefer Gemini and GPT facilitation over Haiku. Here, we find that Gemini and GPT are also more verbose—contributing longer messages at higher volume—which in turn leaves less room for human messages on average. Haiku, by contrast, is less verbose and its groups exhibit slightly higher human participation, yet participants preferred it the least.

5.
Discussion

Preference and collective outcomes can diverge under facilitation.

Across both studies, AI facilitation did not significantly improve group consensus (
\Delta\alpha
), yet participants consistently preferred facilitated discussions over unfacilitated ones.
This extends single-user HCI findings that user perceptions can diverge from downstream performance
(
Buçinca et al. 2020
;
Qian and Wexler 2024
)
.
In multi-user settings, the divergence is more consequential: a facilitator can be experienced as useful or fair without improving collective outcomes, and different participants may value different features of the process.
Many deployed LLMs are developed and selected using human preference signals
(
Ouyang et al. 2022
;
Bai et al. 2022
;
Maiya et al. 2025
)
.
Our results suggest that in group settings, those signals reward facilitators that feel effective while leaving collective outcomes unchanged — and that this preference may partly reflect novelty rather than sustained value. Confirming whether it persists over time requires longitudinal research. For governance, this implies that high user-satisfaction scores are insufficient, and often misleading, proxies for the safety and efficacy of conversational AI systems.

Algorithmic steering: facilitation can shift decisions without changing consensus

Even when overall agreement (
\Delta\alpha
) does not measurably improve, facilitation can reshape what groups ultimately decide.
We observe charity-specific shifts in allocations under LLM facilitation (e.g., reduced allocations to Global Fund for Women). Qualitatively, facilitators—particularly the summarization agents—restated participant-proposed splits, which can normalize outlier suggestions in high-variance topics. This shows that while facilitation may not affect outcome measures (e.g.
\alpha
), it can still exert directional influence. Deliberative quality depends not only on whether groups converge, but also on how decisions were surfaced, normalized, and negotiated
(
Small et al. 2023
)
; our results provide empirical evidence that these distributional effects can arise even when a primary agreement metric remains unchanged. This raises an accountability concern: a facilitator presented as neutral can redirect real resources through its influence on dialogue, even when core metrics suggest no effect. In this experiment, the
-5.5
percentage-point shift in Global Fund for Women allocations under the Principles facilitator translated directly into the final charitable payout (Table
4
), demonstrating that algorithmic steering has financial consequences even in a controlled study.

Illusion of inclusion: perceived inclusion does not imply quantitative gains to inclusivity.

Participants frequently described facilitators as improving inclusivity, but we observe no improvement in conversational shares and a modest displacement of human turns when a facilitator is present
(
Few et al. 2007
)
.
This echoes work on procedural justice and deliberation: people care deeply about voice, acknowledgment, and respectful treatment, yet inequality in who speaks and who is influential persists even in settings designed to be fair
(
Tyler 2003
;
Siu 2017
;
Karpowitz et al. 2012
)
.
Our findings indicate that preference-tuned LLM facilitators improve
perceived
inclusiveness—through acknowledgment, summaries, and directed responses—without materially equalizing participation. This is a sociotechnical risk: facilitators can provide the conversational texture of an inclusive process while leaving the underlying distribution of voice unchanged.
Future work should pair perception-based measures of inclusion with direct measures of conversational share, influence, and uptake. Combined with the steering effects reported above, this creates a compound risk: participants report greater trust in the process under exactly the conditions where facilitators exert the most directional influence on outcomes.

Facilitation preferences are context-dependent.

Preferences are not uniform: participants who report greater investment in the outcome are less likely to prefer the no-facilitator baseline and more likely to prefer the more dynamic
Principles
-style facilitator.
This is consistent with prior work showing that mediation style shapes both how the mediator is perceived and how much support participants want from the process
(
Govers et al. 2024
)
.
Comparing facilitated and unfacilitated conditions, interventions that help under low-coordination or higher-stakes conditions can feel intrusive when groups are already aligned. Facilitation policies should be evaluated not only on average preference, but on their effectiveness across varying group dynamics.

Implications for evaluation and deployment.

Taken together, these results argue for collective evaluations that separate (i) collective outcomes (e.g.,
\Delta\alpha
), (ii) decision distributions across tasks (e.g. topic-level shifts and steering), (iii) procedural dynamics (e.g. participation balance and conversational displacement), and (iv) perception (e.g. preference and felt inclusion). We caution against deploying facilitators and other sociotechnical systems based primarily on preference measures: in our study, we show that preference signals may not align with outcome measures in group settings. This is especially relevant for workplace facilitation tools and citizen deliberation platforms, where legitimacy and equity depend on procedural dynamics (who speaks, what gets discussed, and how consensus is reached); in these settings, preference-outcome mismatches can amplify idiosyncratic views, enable strategic gaming of participating LLM agents, or shift facilitator behavior toward satisfying organizers rather than the participating group
(
Tyler 2003
;
Small et al. 2023
)
.

These findings extend prior work on AI assistance and facilitation by showing that real-time group settings require a broader evaluative lens, as facilitators may be experienced as effective while remaining imperfectly aligned with group-level outcomes and deliberative processes.

6.
Conclusion

As LLMs move from single-user assistance to collective settings, evaluating how they affect collective outcomes and social dynamics is critical.
In collective settings, facilitators may shape not only whether groups converge, but also what they decide, how the discussion unfolds, and how the process is experienced.
We study this question in a large-scale charity-allocation task with two experiments that vary either the facilitator model (Study 1) or the facilitator strategy (Study 2), allowing us to measure consensus change, decision distributions, interaction dynamics, and participant perceptions within the same setting.

Across both studies, LLM facilitation did not produce significant changes in consensus (
\Delta\alpha
),
our primary outcome
, yet participants consistently found this process preferable over the no-facilitator baseline and differentiated between models and facilitation styles. At the same time, facilitation changed the
shape
of deliberation: it modestly reduced human discussion, left participation inequality largely unchanged, and produced charity-specific steering effects even when aggregate agreement remained unchanged.
These findings show that in group settings, perceived procedural improvement and directional influence can coexist with null aggregate outcome effects.

Taken together, our results emphasize that building agentic systems to improve group dynamics should not focus solely on outcome measures or preference measures.
Instead, AI-mediated group dynamics should be assessed across multiple dimensions, including collective outcomes, decision distributions, procedural dynamics, and participant perceptions. This is especially important for workplace facilitation tools, deliberation platforms, and other multi-user systems, where legitimacy depends not only on what a group decides, but on how consensus is reached and whose voices shape it.
LLMs as real-time facilitators are a scalable mechanism for structuring group deliberation—one that demands evaluation, governance, and deployment practices calibrated to collective decision-making, not individual assistance.

7.
Limitations

Our results are specific to a controlled charity-allocation task with small groups, short synchronous discussions, and relatively lightweight facilitator prompts. These design choices let us compare facilitation conditions cleanly, but also narrow the range of settings to which the findings directly apply.

Lack of a human facilitator baseline.

We compare LLM-facilitator and no-facilitator conditions, absent a human
facilitator baseline. Accordingly, our results identify differences
across LLM conditions and relative to no facilitation, but do not establish whether
LLM facilitation reproduces, improves on, or falls short of human facilitation. We
note that the short-form deliberation settings we study (e.g., workplace standups,
rapid committee votes) rarely employ human facilitators due to cost, making LLM
facilitation a comparison against the realistic status quo of no facilitation at all.
Holding the facilitator fixed also eliminates facilitator-to-facilitator variation,
enabling tighter treatment comparisons at larger sample sizes than human facilitation
would permit. Direct human–LLM comparisons, particularly on process quality, steering,
and preference–outcome divergence, remain an important direction for future work.

Discussion duration and time to consensus.

The five-minute discussion cap targets micro-deliberation settings but precludes
measuring time-to-consensus as an independent variable and limits the depth of
deliberation groups can achieve. Future work should use longer or open-ended discussion
windows to evaluate whether the effects observed here—algorithmic steering, illusion
of inclusion, null
\Delta\alpha
—persist, attenuate, or amplify over time.

High baseline agreement and low-conflict structure.

Participants had access to the same charity information, faced limited strategic
incentives, and often started from high levels of agreement (median post-discussion

\alpha_{1}=100
). This likely constrained the room for facilitation to improve
consensus. Facilitation may have larger effects in settings with information
asymmetries, role-differentiated expertise, or genuine conflicts of interest, where
surfacing hidden constraints and reconciling competing accounts is central to the task.

Facilitator design.

The facilitators in both studies were intentionally lightweight and not tuned to the
population or task. More heavily optimized facilitators—or those trained via RLHF on
group-level outcomes rather than individual preference—may produce different consensus
effects and alter the preference–outcome divergence observed here.

8.2.
Ethical Considerations

Research ethics.

This experiment was conducted via an institutional IRB-approved protocol. For more discussion on recruitment and compensation, refer to Section
3.6
. Additionally, the full $7200 was disbursed to the charities disclosed according to the actual consensus weights provided by participants.
Participants consented to the study, and were notified whenever an LLM agent was present in the experiment. Participants interacted under anonymity, being provided pseudonymous animal avatars for the discussion stage.

Broader sociotechnical impact.

The sociotechnical risks discussed in this study (a false sense of inclusion, and algorithmic steering), demonstrate how LLMs can exert directional influence over real world decisions. We urge researchers, lawmakers, and engineers to be very mindful of these risks, and the myriad other risks and limitations posed by LLM systems, and to carefully audit all outcomes in a system interfacing with multi-user deliberation rather than solely relying on metrics like user satisfaction.

4. Principles-Based Facilitator Prompt

This facilitator is provided with a lookup table of common conversational failure modes and their associated “solutions” - common strategies associated with each conversational failure mode. It uses the following schema.

Principles-Based Facilitator System Prompt

You are a neutral facilitator supporting a group discussion about how to allocate donations. Participants are anonymous animal avatars. Your job is to help them achieve consensus through addressing failure modes in the discussion. You do not lead, persuade, or introduce ideas.

## When to speak

Intervene only when observing one of the failure modes below:

### LowEffortOrLowEngagement

* Symptoms: minimal participation, one-word answers, low / apathetic group energy
* Intervention strategy: spark brief reasoning or values without pressure

### OffTopicDrift

* Symptoms: drifting into side chat or adjacent topics, forgetting the goal.
* Intervention strategy: let small tangents breathe, then gently anchor back to decision-making.

### UnevenParticipation

* Symptoms: one or two people dominate while the third stays quiet.
* Intervention strategy: wait a bit; if the pattern continues, gently open space.

### NoJustificationOrPrematureConsensus

* Symptoms: the group appears to agree quickly without explaining why; decisions settle fast to avoid friction.
* Intervention strategy: gently surface one reason, confirm real alignment, or invite a light alternative check.

### BinaryStuck

* Symptoms: the group locks into two preferred splits (e.g., 40/30 vs. 30/40), treats it as an either-or choice.
* Intervention strategy: highlight the spectrum, invite small-step thinking, and connect to shared goals.

### SelfContainedReasoningOnly

* Symptoms: participants share reasoning but do not engage with each other; parallel monologues.
* Intervention strategy: invite building on or reacting to each other’s ideas.

## Step rules
1. Identify the most likely observedFailureMode.
2. Decide shouldRespond. This is true only if a failure mode is active and there is a high-leverage response. If unsure, stay silent.
3. Update the response with your intervention. Be concise (1-3 sentences) and neutral.

F.2.
Model Selection and Parameters

We used each model with its default sampling settings, with temperature set to 1.0 for Gemini, GPT, and Claude and no extended “thinking” or reasoning modes where available. We chose these settings to approximate a realistic deployment regime for real-time conversational agents: heavier temperature tuning might alter response style, and extended reasoning modes may introduce substantial latency.

Selection criteria:
We selected Gemini 2.5 Flash, Claude 4.5 Haiku, and GPT-5 mini because they were representative API-accessible frontier conversational models that, at the time of study, offered a practical combination of low latency, deployment stability, and real-time usability for synchronous experiments. Our goal in Study 1 was not to provide a comprehensive benchmark of all available LLM families, but to compare a small set of broadly comparable, deployable facilitators under realistic interaction constraints. We prioritized models that could be integrated into the same experimental setup with similar operational assumptions.

The checkpoints for each model version are dated as follows:

•

Gemini 2.5 Flash (6/17/25)

•

Claude 4.5 Haiku (10/15/25)

•

GPT-5 mini (8/07/25)

We limited the study to three models because adding more would have substantially increased the number of within-group treatments and extended the session beyond a feasible duration. We also did not include open-weight models, since doing so would have introduced a different deployment stack, including self-hosting and inference-pipeline choices, which would have made comparisons less apples-to-apples. Evaluating open-weight alternatives under matched real-time conditions remains an important direction for future work.

We investigated a range of metrics for group and individual consensus outcomes. We analyzed the change in allocations pre and post-discussion and derive the following metrics for group consensus:

(1)

Group Euclidean Distance:
How much the allocations of the group move towards each other as measured by Euclidean distance.

(2)

Group Krippendorff Alpha:
The change in aggregate of Krippendorff’s Alpha, normalized between 0 and 100.

(3)

Group Standard Deviation Change:
The change in the standard deviation of the participant’s allocations pre- and post-discussion.

We also derive the following measures for individual influence:

(1)

Individual Convergence:
How much each individual moved towards the mean of the group allocation, as measured by Euclidean distance.

(2)

Individual Self-Change:
The total amount of movement of individuals in each group as measured by the difference in
\Delta_{\alpha}
(direction-agnostic).

We include alternative consensus and influence metrics in Table
6
because different metrics capture different aspects of agreement and movement. Some of these metrics (e.g. group Krippendorff’s, group SD, individual convergence) yield similar qualitative patterns, especially for the summarization treatment, while others (e.g. Euclidean distance, individual self-change) are more sensitive to smoothing or outlier behavior. In the main text, we focus on group-level change in Krippendorff’s
\alpha
, since it provides a natural summary of agreement for small groups with interval-valued responses and heterogeneous disagreement
(
Krippendorff 2004
)
. The appendix metrics are intended as robustness checks rather than competing primary outcomes.
