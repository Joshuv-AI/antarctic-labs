Abstract
Energy Directors (EDs), once considered necessary for ultrasonic welding of polymers and polymer
composites, introduce limitations including mold cost, flash formation, and restrictions on weld size and
configuration that often require high-precision machining. This study demonstrates a hybrid manufacturing
and joining approach integrating additive manufacturing and compression molding (AMCM) to produce
thermoplastic composite plates, followed by continuous linear ultrasonic welding without the use of EDs.
30 wt.% short glass fiber-reinforced polypropylene thermoplastic composite plates were manufactured
using the AMCM process and subsequently joined using a high-speed linear ultrasonic welding system.
The effects of ultrasonic welding amplitude and probe travel speed were systematically investigated, and
interfacial bond-lines were characterized using a hierarchical approach. Non-destructive evaluation (NDE)
using ultrasonic C-scan analysis was conducted in parallel, revealing that weld quality is inversely
dependent on ultrasonic vibration amplitude. Detailed bond characterization using high-resolution optical
microscopy and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) showed intimate interfacial fusion with no observable
discontinuities across multiple combinations of amplitude and probe travel speed. Lap shear testing further
demonstrated that several welded joints achieved failure loads exceeding 2000 N, indicating load-bearing
capacity comparable to the base material. Overall, this work establishes a scalable methodology for directly
joining large thermoplastic composite components using continuous linear ultrasonic welding without EDs,
enabling improved manufacturability and supporting rapid adoption in transportation applications.

1. Introduction
The objective of this CRADA is to advance a robot-agnostic ultrasonic welding system for high-rate joining
and shaping of thermoplastic composites in automotive applications by developing application-specific
hardware, establishing optimized processing parameters, and integrating in-weld sensing and data
acquisition capabilities. This effort aims to enable reliable joining of similar and/or dissimilar fiber-
reinforced polymer composites while generating comprehensive process–structure–property datasets to
support improved weld quality, system performance, and future data-driven manufacturing approaches,
thereby advancing the technology from TRL 3 to TRL 6.

2. Materials and Methods

2.1 GFPP material and AMCM of GFPP

Glass fiber reinforced (30% short glass fiber) H1030 polypropylene from Sabic was used to produce GFPP
panels. To fabricate these panels, pellets were fed from a dryer into a strangpresse Model-30 extruder having
temperature settings of 200 °C, 220 °c, and 240 °c across the three Heating zones and nozzle. The material
was 3D-deposited using a unidirectional toolpath printing into a 457.2 × 304.8 mm mold to establish the
initial plate geometry. As shown in Figure 1, following the 3D printing, compression molding was
performed under a uniform pressure of 150 tons for 180 s to consolidate the material and reduce porosity.
This process produced plates with fibers highly aligned in the print direction, reduced porosity and
improved surface quality.

2.2 Ultrasonic Welding (UW) Machine Details, Welding Process and Welding Parameters

The polypropylene glass fiber-reinforced (PP-GF) composite plates used for welding measured 200 × 170
mm with a nominal thickness of 4.0 mm. Welding was performed using an ultrasonic robotic end-effector
(sonotrode) operating at 20 kHz and equipped with a 25.4 mm × 12.7 mm horn face (Agile Ultrasonic Inc.).
The end-effector incorporates pneumatic hold-down cylinders and integrated position sensors to ensure

                                                                                                Page |3

consistent normal force application and stable horn-to-material contact during scanning. For each weld, the
robot positioned the sonotrode at the programmed start location, engaged the hold-down cylinders, activated
ultrasonic excitation, and executed a continuous scan weld along a 25.4 mm overlap region. Active cooling
was applied throughout the process to maintain thermal stability and limit excessive softening within the
weld zone. Further details, including the ultrasonic welding schematic and welded plates, are presented in
Figure 2.

A structured design-of-experiments (DoE) framework was implemented to establish the processing window
for ultrasonic welding of GFPP composites. The study examined four ultrasonic amplitude settings (60%,

between 500 and 1000 mm/min. A constant normal force of approximately 500 N was applied throughout
all experiments under active cooling conditions. The full experimental matrix used for weld fabrication is

and corresponding weld formation behavior.

After fabrication, the welded plates underwent visual and tactile examination to evaluate joint integrity. All
specimens maintained structural continuity upon removal from the fixture, indicating successful formation
of continuous GFPP–GFPP joints without the incorporation of energy directors. This outcome confirms
that adequate localized heating and interfacial fusion were achieved during the ultrasonic welding process.
The top and bottom surfaces of the additively manufactured and compression-molded GFPP plates
following linear ultrasonic welding are presented in Figures 2d and 2e, respectively. Initial surface
inspection revealed that certain samples exhibited only minor surface deformation, with very limited
evidence of polymer squeeze-out beneath the ultrasonic tool imprint, suggesting effective confinement of
thermal energy to the interface region. The backside surfaces showed no observable distortion, confirming
that the lower adherend retained its structural integrity throughout the welding process.

2.3 Non-Destructive Testing Method

Phased Array Ultrasonic Testing (PAUT) was employed to perform C-scan based non-destructive
evaluation (NDE) of the welded panels. The measurements were conducted using an OmniScan-SX-PA
system (Evident Inc.) equipped with a 64-element linear array transducer (5L64-SNW1-0L) operating at a
center frequency of 5 MHz and a scan resolution of 1 mm (Figure 3a). This transducer configuration was
selected to enhance signal-to-noise performance while maintaining adequate spatial resolution under
attenuative conditions. The system operates in an immersion pulse-echo mode, generating longitudinal
waves at an approximate velocity of 3000 m/s, with reflected signals acquired and processed through
OmniScan software.
Given the application of ultrasonic welding for large-scale GFPP composite structures, non-destructive
evaluation is essential for initial assessment of weld quality. In this study, C-scan imaging using PAUT was
utilized to provide rapid spatial mapping of weld integrity. This technique enables planar visualization of
internal features, supporting defect detection, sizing, and localization. In addition, color-mapped amplitude

                                                                                                Page |5

responses allow identification of regions with impedance variations associated with voids, discontinuities,
or other interfacial defects.

 array ultrasonic testing (PAUT); and (b) C-scan amplitude map of ultrasonically welded regions.

The quality of the welded regions was assessed based on impedance mismatch reflections, which were used
to infer relative defect density, as shown in Figure 3b. Regions exhibiting higher color intensity (yellow to
red) correspond to increased impedance mismatch, indicating reduced weld quality. These areas are
associated with void formation or incomplete interfacial fusion, reflecting insufficient consolidation during
the ultrasonic welding process. Based on the observed impedance response, samples W750,60, W1000,60,
W1000,70, and W1000,90 exhibit comparatively lower weld quality, with the relative severity of impedance
mismatch following the trend: W1000,60 > W750,60 > W1000,90 > W1000,70. In this context, higher impedance
mismatch intensity corresponds to lower weld integrity.

2.4 Mechanical Testing, Hardness Evaluation, and Weld Performance

Vickers hardness (HV) measurements across the welded region were performed using a LECO (LM110
series) AMH 55 automatic hardness indenter with a 200 N load, 500 µm inter-indent spacing, and a dwell
time of 10 seconds. Lap shear test (LST) specimens were prepared using waterjet machining in accordance
with the dimensions shown in Figure 4a. End tabs of equivalent thickness to the base material were applied
to ensure symmetric load transfer relative to the bond-line. Lap shear testing was conducted using an Instron
universal testing frame equipped with pneumatically controlled flat wedge grips at a quasi-static
displacement rate of 0.50 in/min, following ASTM D5868. For each condition, three specimens were tested,
and the average peak load at failure was reported.
Figure 4b summarizes the lap shear testing results, highlighting peak load at failure across all welding
conditions. The results indicate two distinct failure regimes separated by an approximate threshold of 2000
N. Welds exhibiting peak loads above this threshold consistently demonstrated base material (BM) failure,
also referred to as plate failure, while welds below this threshold exhibited interfacial failure (IF), or weld
failure.
For low and moderate welding speeds (500 mm/min and 750 mm/min), peak loads exceeding 2000 N were
consistently achieved across all amplitude conditions, indicating BM failure. These results suggest that
these welding speeds provide sufficient energy input and residence time to enable effective melting, flow,
and consolidation of the polypropylene matrix at the interface, resulting in strong weld formation. At a
welding speed of 750 mm/min, high peak loads were observed across all amplitudes except at the lowest
amplitude (60%), indicating a threshold ultrasonic energy input between 60% and 70% amplitude required
for complete interfacial fusion.
At higher welding speeds (1000 mm/min), peak loads were generally below 2000 N, except for the highest
amplitude condition (80%). This behavior indicates insufficient energy input due to reduced residence time,
limiting heat generation and resulting in incomplete melting and weaker interfacial bonding.
Representative force–displacement curves for both BM and IF failure modes are shown in Figure 4c. The
results indicate pronounced nonlinear deformation behavior associated with the viscoelastic nature of the
polymer matrix, which reduces the effective peak load at failure, particularly for samples exhibiting BM
failure. In contrast, samples exhibiting interfacial failure show reduced nonlinearity and lower load-bearing.
capacity.

Figure 5 presents the variation in microhardness across the bonded interface for weld condition W1000,90,

profile follows an approximate inverse Gaussian trend, showing lower hardness values of ~15 HV within
the weld region and higher values of ~20 HV in the surrounding base material. A closer inspection of the
indentation morphology (Figures 5b and 5c) highlights a strong dependence between local hardness and
deformation response. Indents located in the base material, away from the weld zone, produce relatively

                                                                                                   Page |8

smaller impressions, consistent with a stiffer, more constrained fiber network. Conversely, indentations
near the weld interface generate larger impressions, reflecting reduced resistance to deformation and a
softened polymer matrix due to localized structural changes. Overall, the results indicate that ultrasonic
welding leads to localized microstructural rearrangement and matrix re-solidification at the interface,
forming a mechanically softened region within the composite.

 Figure 5: Hardness evaluation across the weld interface (weld condition W1000,90): (a) Vickers hardness
 profile across the weld cross-section; (b) macroscopic view of hardness indentations; and (c–e)

The observed increase in deformation and corresponding reduction in hardness near the welded interface is
attributed to localized polymer melting and re-solidification during ultrasonic welding. This process leads
to partial relaxation of molecular orientation and reduced fiber constraint within the composite. Such
softening behavior has been reported in ultrasonic and friction-based welding of thermoplastic composites,
where interfacial heating results in localized reductions in crystallinity and matrix stiffness. The resulting
hardness gradient reflects the presence of a thermo-mechanically affected zone across the interface, where
ultrasonic energy input induces microstructural and mechanical heterogeneity within the fiber–matrix
system.

2.4 Bond Quality Characterization Methods

Cross-sectional specimens of the welded panels were prepared via waterjet cutting, followed by sequential
polishing using silicon carbide abrasive papers, diamond suspensions, and alumina slurries ranging from

                                                                                                 Page |9

320 grit to 0.05 µm particle size. Both as-cut (unpolished) and polished surfaces were examined using a
Keyence VHX-5000 series optical microscope.
High-resolution microstructural evaluation of the bond-line, fiber deformation, and void morphology was
carried out using a Zeiss Gemini 450 scanning electron microscope (SEM). Imaging was performed in both
secondary electron (SE) and backscattered electron (BSE) modes at accelerating voltages of 10–20 keV
and beam currents between 10–15 nA. A multiscale (hierarchical) examination of the weld line was
conducted to assess the influence of ultrasonic welding parameters on interfacial development and weld
formation in GFPP thermoplastic composite (TPC) plates. The corresponding results are presented in
Figures 6–8. At the macroscopic level (Figure 6), the combined effects of tool travel speed and displacement
amplitude are evident in the resulting weld morphology. Figure 6a illustrates the cross-section for weld
condition W750,60, where the heat-affected zone (HAZ), indicated by a yellow dashed line, corresponds to
the region influenced by direct sonotrode contact with the upper adherend. Within this region, the weld-
fusion zone responsible for interfacial bonding between the adherends is clearly observed, while non-
bonded regions appear near the outer edges of the HAZ, where spacers or cushioning elements are typically
required during ultrasonic welding. Localized material extrusion is observed near the terminal edge of the
upper adherend (green arrow), suggesting localized overheating and excessive ultrasonic energy input for
this condition. In addition, discontinuities and unfused regions are present within the weld-fusion zone,
indicating insufficient energy delivery to achieve complete melting and resolidification at the faying
interface under the W750,60 condition.

 weld condition W750,60 with various zones and weld formation morphologies, and (b) illustration of the

Additional comparisons can be drawn from the cross-sectional features shown in Figure 6b. The formation
of the bond-line between the upper and lower GFPP plates is strongly influenced by the combined effect of
tool travel speed and ultrasonic amplitude, both of which determine the effective energy input during
welding. At the lowest travel speed of 500 mm/min, continuous and well-consolidated bond-lines are
achieved for amplitudes of 60%, 70%, and 80%, with only limited evidence of macroscopic voiding or
cavity formation. In contrast, increasing the amplitude to 90% (W500,90) results in pronounced void
formation along the weld line, suggesting localized overheating and gas evolution within the polypropylene
matrix due to excessive interfacial temperature rise.
By comparison, higher travel speeds of 750 mm/min and 1000 mm/min lead to discontinuous weld
structures characterized by macroscopic voids and unfused regions (e.g., W750,60–80 and W1000,60–90). These
features indicate insufficient thermal energy delivery and inadequate interfacial melting, preventing full
consolidation of the adherends.

A further observation from Figure 6 concerns deformation of the upper adherend. Conditions such as
W500,70–90 and W750,70–90 exhibit permanent surface damage, indicative of over-processing. This
degradation adversely affects surface integrity and reduces suitability for structural applications. It is
attributed to excessive energy input, which increases local temperature, promotes fiber agitation, and
induces polymer flow. Consequently, matrix squeeze-out occurs from the weld zone, resulting in partial
fiber exposure prior to resolidification.
Among all tested conditions, W500,60 provides the most favorable overall weld quality, exhibiting a
continuous bond-line with no evident base material damage. Conversely, W500,90 shows severe degradation
of the upper adherend, extensive matrix squeeze-out, bond-line distortion, and void formation. These two
conditions were therefore selected for detailed multiscale characterization using optical microscopy and
SEM, as presented in Figures 7 and 8.
Figure 7 presents optical and SEM micrographs of the W500,60 cross-section, including the weld edge regions
(Figures 7b and 7d) and the central weld region (Figure 7c). The weld zone is largely free from voids,
delamination, or interfacial distortion. However, a localized unwelded region of approximately 1.5 mm
(kissing bond) is observed near the weld extremities (Figures 7b and 7d). This is attributed to the presence
of edge cushioning and reduced normal pressure at the tool boundaries, which promotes outward heat
dissipation and limits bonding at the edges.
Higher magnification SEM observations (Figures 7e and 7f) confirm fiber redistribution within the upper
adherend, while the lower adherend remains largely unchanged. At further magnification (Figures 7g and
7h), pronounced polymer interdiffusion across the interface is evident, which is responsible for the
formation of a continuous and well-consolidated weld-fusion zone under the W500,60 condition.

Weld condition W500,90 exhibits extensive damage across both the upper and lower adherends within the
weld zone and along the weld line, as shown in Figure 8. Significant material extrusion is observed on the
top adherend surface, leading to macroscopic cavities and localized material loss. These features are
indicative of excessive ultrasonic energy input, which results in complete melting of the GFPP composite

                                                                                                   P a g e | 13

(melting point ~167.64°C) and uncontrolled fiber redistribution driven by melt flow and pressure-induced
squeeze-out.
A higher magnification optical view taken near the extremity of the weld zone (Figure 8b), adjacent to the
top adherend base material, reveals a well-bonded, void-free interface in regions away from the kissing
bond area. However, the bond-line within the central tool region (Figure 8e) and at the opposite weld edge
near the bottom adherend (Figure 8f) shows clear evidence of void formation, material expulsion, and
marcelling-like deformation features.
These observations are further corroborated by SEM analysis (Figures 8g and 8h). Near the kissing bond
region (Figure 8g), the interface contains relatively small voids on the order of 15–20 µm, with limited fiber
rearrangement, suggesting localized matrix flow without substantial disruption of the fiber network. In
contrast, the central weld region (Figure 8h) exhibits a higher void density with sizes ranging from 30–50
µm along the weld interface. This behavior is consistent with excessive energy input beyond optimal
welding conditions, promoting matrix degradation and possible gas evolution due to partial thermal
decomposition. The resulting gaseous species become trapped within the molten polymer, forming larger
void structures upon solidification.
In addition, progressive fiber reorientation is evident in both adherends, with more pronounced effects in
the upper plate compared to the lower one (Figures 8e, 8f, and 8h). This suggests that excessive ultrasonic
energy intensifies polymer flow, disrupts the original fiber architecture, and leads to fiber redistribution and
reorientation during the melting and resolidification stages of weld formation.

Figure 9 shows representative images of base material (BM), or substrate, failure following lap shear testing
for weld condition W750,60. At the macroscopic scale (Figure 9a), the fracture surface exhibits a highly
irregular and tortuous crack path, indicating crack deviation around reinforcing fibers and significant energy
dissipation during failure.
At higher magnification (Figures 9b and 9c), pronounced fiber pull-out and fiber bridging mechanisms are
evident across the fracture surface. These micromechanical features enhance resistance to crack growth
under lap shear loading by promoting additional energy absorption during failure.
Overall, the observed failure mode confirms that, under this welding condition, the joint strength exceeds
the inherent load-bearing capacity of the short-fiber glass fiber-reinforced polypropylene (GFPP)
composite, resulting in failure occurring within the base material rather than at the weld interface.

Figure 10 presents the faying surfaces of the top and bottom adherends for weld samples that failed in an
interfacial (IF) mode. Across the examined weld conditions (W750,60, W1000,60, and W1000,60), a clear phase-
segregated surface morphology is observed, characterized by alternating light (grey-to-white) and dark
(dark-to-black) regions, as shown in Figures 10a–10c. The contrast between these regions reflects the
formation of a heterogeneous interfacial structure during ultrasonic heating and subsequent resolidification.
The lighter zones correspond to areas where localized melting, polymer flow, and chain interdiffusion
occurred, indicating effective bonding regions (“bonded areas”). In contrast, the darker zones represent
regions with limited melting and poor wetting, corresponding to “non-bonded areas” with weak or absent
interfacial adhesion. This spatial heterogeneity is attributed to the highly localized and transient nature of
ultrasonic energy deposition, where frictional and viscoelastic heating is concentrated at discrete interfacial
regions rather than being uniformly distributed. Under suboptimal processing conditions, this leads to

uneven energy coupling, resulting in irregular morphology and discontinuous bonding along the weld
interface.

3. Conclusions
This study demonstrated the ability of using Agile Ultrasonics continuous linear ultrasonic welding machine
to join AMCM part made from glass fiber–reinforced polypropylene (GFPP) composite without the use of
conventional energy directors. The results showed that welding parameters, particularly tool travel speed
and ultrasonic amplitude, have a significant influence on weld quality, microstructure, and mechanical
performance, with lower travel speeds (500–750 mm/min) and moderate amplitudes (60–80%) producing
continuous bond lines, minimal defects, and lap shear strengths that resulted in base material failure. Non-
destructive evaluation using phased array ultrasonic testing showed good agreement with optical
microscopy, SEM, hardness measurements, and mechanical testing, confirming that optimized processing
conditions promote intimate interfacial fusion with minimal void formation. Preliminary welding trials on
PEEK/carbon fiber laminates further demonstrated the potential applicability of the process to higher-
performance thermoplastic composites. Overall, these findings establish continuous linear ultrasonic
welding as a promising, scalable, and high-rate joining technology for advanced thermoplastic composite
structures, supporting its future implementation in lightweight transportation manufacturing and providing
a foundation for further process optimization, in-process monitoring, and evaluation of long-term structural
performance
