Beyond Inference-Only Deployment: Comparing Weight-Based Consolidation Against Cascading Compaction
Simon Dennis, Kevin Shabahang, Hao Guo, Rivaan Patil

Abstract
Major LLM platforms deploy models in an inference-only configuration: the model serves requests but never updates per-user weights. Users must repeatedly re-teach preferences, corrections, and project context, and context-based workarounds consume context-window space and degrade under cascading compaction. We evaluate an alternative: nightly consolidation of interaction knowledge into model weights via reflection, synthesis, and Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) fine-tuning on a single consumer GPU. Across ten realistic software development conversations ( n = 10 n=10 , 1,146 1{,}146 test questions across three memory types), three cycles of cascading compaction retain 36.8 ± 3.0 % 36.8\pm 3.0\% of knowledge (between an 11.8 % 11.8\% no-context floor and a 90.1 % 90.1\% full-context ceiling), while consolidation retains 80.4 ± 1.3 % 80.4\pm 1.3\% —a 43.6 43.6 pp gain (paired t ⁡ ( 9 ) = 14.8 t(9)=14.8 , p < 0.001 p<0.001 ) that more than doubles what compaction preserves, with the largest gains on procedural corrections ( 36.3 % → 74.6 % 36.3\%\!\to\!74.6\% ) and episodic project facts ( 31.5 % → 78.2 % 31.5\%\!\to\!78.2\% ). As a methodological aside, mean per-token validation cross-entropy is negatively correlated with LLM-judged accuracy ( r = − 0.51 r=-0.51 ) while median per-token validation cross-entropy tracks accuracy almost exactly ( r = + 0.99 r=+0.99 ): under evaluators that tolerate surface-form variation, the mean is misleading and a heavy-tail-robust statistic is the faithful signal. Persistent personalization requires moving beyond inference-only deployment toward architectures that consolidate knowledge into weights.



1
Introduction

Large language models have transformed how we interact with AI systems, but they share a fundamental limitation: once deployed, they do not learn from their interactions with individual users. Consider a software developer who uses an LLM assistant daily. Over weeks of collaboration, they repeatedly tell the assistant to use snake_case for function names, to prefer pathlib over os.path, and that their project uses a specific technology stack. Despite these repeated instructions, nothing is transferred to the model’s weights. Some platforms carry forward summaries or compacted context between sessions, but this information competes for finite context window space and degrades under repeated compaction—it is a workaround, not learning.

The practical consequences are significant. Corrections and preferences do not accumulate—the model makes the same mistakes in new conversations and cannot adapt to individual users. Most critically,
over months of collaboration, the volume of relevant project context far exceeds what any context window can hold
. Architecture decisions, past debugging sessions, team conventions, and user preferences accumulate continuously, but the context window does not grow to match. Context-based workarounds—retrieval-augmented generation
(
Lewis et al. 2020
)
and external memory stores
(
Zhong et al. 2024
;
Chhikara et al. 2025
)
—can defer this problem but cannot solve it.

Biological brains face a similar challenge, and Complementary Learning Systems (CLS) theory
(
McClelland et al. 1995
)
offers a useful analogy. The brain uses a fast system (hippocampus) for rapid encoding of new experiences and a slow system (neocortex) for persistent knowledge, with consolidation transferring information from the fast to the slow system
(
Kumaran et al. 2016
;
Sun et al. 2023
)
. In LLMs, attention over the context window plays the fast-encoding role while model weights serve as persistent storage. The analogy is particularly strong for linear attention models, which implement Hebbian learning similar to that proposed for hippocampal area CA3
(
Rolls 2013
)
. Fine-tuning a model on its own interaction data is well-studied in continual learning
(
Kirkpatrick et al. 2017
;
Parisi et al. 2019
)
, and recent work has begun to explore weight-based personalization at deployment time
(
Tan et al. 2024
)
; nonetheless, production assistants overwhelmingly leave user weights frozen and rely on context-based memory.

We propose a three-stage consolidation pipeline—reflection, synthesis, training (Figure
1
, §
2.2
)—built around two design choices: LoRA-based architectural isolation
(
Hu et al. 2021
)
, which lets new information be assimilated into the model’s weights quickly while keeping the base model’s pre-trained capabilities intact, and the use of an off-the-shelf pretrained LLM as the synthetic-data generator—a pattern established by Self-Instruct
(
Wang et al. 2023b
)
. The rehearsal data is a synthetic set of short user–assistant conversations of the kind the user is likely to have on each topic in future sessions, rather than verbatim transcripts or bare fact statements.

The envisioned deployment mirrors the biological sleep–wake cycle: during the day, the model operates normally, accumulating new facts, corrections, and preferences in its context window so that it can respond immediately to user input. Overnight, the consolidation pipeline processes the day’s conversations—reflecting on what was learned, synthesizing training data, and fine-tuning the model’s weights. The next morning, the context is cleared and the model begins fresh, but with yesterday’s knowledge now encoded in its parameters rather than consuming context space. Over weeks and months of this cycle, the model’s weights become an increasingly rich representation of the user’s preferences, project history, and working patterns, while the context window remains free for the current conversation.

Whether this pipeline is preferable to keeping recent context summarised in-window is an empirical question. We evaluate consolidation against cascading compaction—the strategy currently deployed in coding assistants—across ten long software-development sessions, with three contributions:

1.

We apply the standard taxonomy of human memory—procedural, semantic, and episodic—to the LLM setting, and design a matched experiment comparing consolidation against cascading context compaction.

2.

We provide empirical evidence that cascading compaction—the dominant memory management strategy in deployed LLM coding assistants—retains only
36.8\%
of knowledge after three cycles, while consolidation into model weights preserves
80.4\%
, more than doubling retention.

3.

We demonstrate a graded pattern across memory types: semantic memories are best preserved under both compaction and consolidation, while procedural corrections and episodic project facts suffer more severe compaction losses that consolidation substantially mitigates.

2
Method

Our method consists of two components: a taxonomy of memory types that organises what is extracted from each conversation (§
2.1
), and a three-stage pipeline that turns each extraction into a consolidated LoRA adapter (§
2.2
).

2.1
Memory Taxonomy

Our taxonomy takes inspiration from the standard distinction in cognitive psychology between procedural memory (knowing
how
) and declarative memory (knowing
that
), with declarative memory subdivided into semantic (general knowledge) and episodic (event-specific) components
(
Squire 1980
)
. We adapt this three-way split to the personalization setting, where each type maps onto a functionally distinct kind of information that should be extracted and trained on differently:

Procedural memories
encode lessons learned from the model’s mistakes during conversations. When the model encounters a bug, diagnoses it, and applies a fix, the goal of procedural consolidation is for the model to execute the correct approach the first time in future encounters—avoiding the error entirely rather than merely recalling that it occurred.

Semantic memories.
Standard semantic memory refers to general world knowledge; in the personalization setting, the semantic knowledge that matters is instead
self-referential
: stable facts about the user—their preferences, conventions, and ways of working—that generalize across contexts but are meaningful only with respect to a particular individual.
Conway and Pleydell-Pearce 2000
place this kind of material in the self-memory system as autobiographical semantic knowledge, distinct both from episodic recollections of specific events and from general world facts. Concretely these take the form of meta-preferences about how the user wants the model to work—response length and level of detail, workflow expectations (running tests before declaring a change done, confirming before destructive operations), and how the model should handle uncertainty or disagreement.

Episodic memories
capture project-specific history—events, decisions, and facts unique to the particular deployment context.

2.2
Pipeline

Consolidation proceeds in three stages, applied uniformly across all three memory types.

The reflection stage
analyzes conversation transcripts to extract structured memories. This is analogous to the initial encoding that occurs in the hippocampus—identifying what information from an experience should be retained. For each conversation in the log we run three independent extraction passes with Claude Sonnet 4, classifying each memory by type and extracting the core content in a concise, testable form (see the middle column of Table
1
); the three passes are unioned and deduplicated by fact name, which surfaces different framings of the same underlying knowledge and is what produces the redundancy that the synthesis stage then amplifies.

The synthesis stage
turns each extracted fact into a set of synthetic user–assistant conversations of the kind the user is likely to have on that topic in future sessions. This functions as paraphrastic rehearsal: presenting the same content in many surface forms is empirically more effective than presenting a single canonical form
(
Allen-Zhu and Li 2024
)
; see §
5
for discussion. For every fact, regardless of memory type, we prompt the same model (Claude Sonnet 4) to generate 20 such two-message conversations, varying question style (direct, how-to, recall, scenario, negation, task-oriented) and answer length and tone. Combined with the three-pass extraction, each underlying concept ends up covered by roughly
40
–
60
paraphrased conversations on average; the total training set is approximately
18{,}000
conversations per source conversation (Appendix
A
). Framing varies by memory type: semantic facts as conventions and stable preferences, procedural facts as lessons learned (with the assistant demonstrating the correct approach directly, without first making the error), and episodic facts as project history and specific configurations. The same two-message format is used throughout to match the format of the test questions at evaluation time.

The training stage
fine-tunes the base model on the synthesized training data via LoRA
(
Hu et al. 2021
)
(rank
r=16
,
\alpha=32
) applied to the attention projections of Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct
(
Qwen Team 2024
)
. Each training example is a two-message user–assistant exchange, formatted with the base model’s chat template and fed as a single sequence to the default supervised-fine-tuning loss. Remaining hyperparameters (learning rate, batch size, training time, etc.) are given in Appendix
C
.

3
Evaluation

3.1
Experimental Design

We designed a matched experiment where both conditions—compaction and consolidation—operate on identical conversational data, enabling direct comparison.

Conversation Generation

We generated ten realistic software development conversations, each approximately 50,000–65,000 tokens long, covering diverse project scenarios: a CLI developer tool, a data pipeline system, an ML training framework, a monitoring dashboard, a web frontend, a game development project, DevOps infrastructure-as-code, scientific computing, a security scanner, and a distributed message queue. Each conversation naturally contains a mix of all three memory types: coding conventions expressed by the user, debugging episodes where errors are diagnosed and fixed, and project-specific architectural decisions and technical facts. Conversations were generated using Claude Sonnet 4, following realistic multi-turn development patterns including code generation, debugging, refactoring, and design discussion. See Appendix
B
for a sample.

Test Extraction

For each conversation, we extracted testable facts using an LLM judge (Claude Sonnet 4). The extraction identified specific, verifiable knowledge across all three memory types: semantic rules (e.g., “uses Google-style docstrings”), procedural facts (e.g., “
hmac.new()
doesn’t exist, use
hmac.digest()
”), and episodic facts (e.g., “the CLI tool is called
devtool
”). This yielded
1{,}146
total test questions across the ten conversations (
115\pm 9
per conversation): 184 semantic, 154 procedural, and 808 episodic tests. Appendix
A
(Table
3
) gives the per-conversation breakdown of test counts, extracted facts, and training examples.

Compaction Protocol

We simulated the cascading compaction that occurs in deployed LLM coding assistants (e.g., Claude Code) when conversations exceed context limits. At each cycle:

1.

The current context is summarized at a 6:1 ratio, producing a summary of approximately
1/6
the original token count—the compression factor reported for Claude Code’s automatic compaction
(
Reliable Data
Engineering 2026
)
.

2.

A
continuation
—a fresh
\sim
60,000-token conversation of new simulated work on the same project—is generated in the context of this summary and appended. A continuation is not a re-telling of the original; it introduces new debugging episodes, new design decisions, and new coding patterns that the project accumulates over continued use, so each cycle both compresses prior content and dilutes it with fresh content.

3.

The model is tested on the
original
conversation’s facts, using the summary plus continuation as the system prompt.

This protocol runs for three cycles, producing three measurement points: Cycle 1 (first compaction + continuation), Cycle 2 (second compaction + continuation), and Cycle 3 (third compaction + continuation). The test questions remain constant across all cycles, measuring degradation of the original knowledge as it is progressively compressed and diluted by new content.

Consolidation Protocol

For each conversation, we run the full consolidation pipeline:

1.

Extract facts from all four conversations (original + three continuations) via reflection, running three independent extraction passes and taking their union to reduce the variance of any single extraction pass (per-conversation fact counts in Table
3
).

2.

Synthesize training data from extracted facts (approximately 20 diverse question-answer paraphrases per fact; per-conversation example counts in Table
3
).

3.

Train a LoRA adapter on the synthesized data (8 epochs, selected from an epoch sweep; see Figure
2
). All ten runs were trained on NVIDIA A100-40GB GPUs, with checkpoints saved every epoch.

4.

Evaluate the consolidated model on the same test questions used for compaction.

The consolidated model receives no context at test time—it must answer from knowledge encoded in weights alone. This provides a direct comparison: compaction retains knowledge in (compressed) context, consolidation retains knowledge in weights.

Evaluation

The model under test generates an answer to each test question via greedy decoding (temperature 0, up to 512 new tokens). The system prompt is condition-specific: the full-context ceiling sees the original conversation, each compaction cycle sees the cycle’s summary plus continuation, the consolidated and no-context conditions do not receive a system prompt. In every case the test question is delivered as the user turn of the chat template. The answer is then judged by Claude Sonnet 4 (also temperature 0), which receives the question, the expected answer, and the model’s response and decides whether the response contains the expected information.

3.2
Results

Training Dynamics and Choice of Epoch Count

To choose an appropriate number of epochs, we trained each LoRA adapter for twelve epochs while saving a checkpoint at the end of every epoch and evaluating all twelve against the test questions. Figure
2
summarizes the result. LLM-judged task fail rate drops steeply through the first several epochs and reaches its minimum of
19.6\%
at epoch 8, after which it fluctuates within noise. We therefore adopt
8 epochs
as the canonical training schedule; all main-table results (Table
2
) are reported at this checkpoint.

The same figure also illustrates a subtlety worth describing. The standard training-time validation signal—
mean
per-token cross-entropy on the held-out test questions—shows a U-shape: it reaches a minimum at epoch 3 and then rises monotonically to about twice its minimum value by epoch 12, the classical overfitting signature. Yet fail rate keeps improving past epoch 3, reaching its minimum at epoch 8 rather than worsening. The discrepancy is an artefact of averaging a heavy-tailed distribution of token losses. The
median
per-token validation CE decreases monotonically from
0.23
nats at epoch 1 to effectively zero by epoch 12, i.e. the typical token is being learned better at every step. The apparent overfitting in the mean comes from a small fraction of tokens where the model effectively assigns near-zero probability to the correct token (
90
th-percentile token CE roughly triples from
4.8
to
11.9
nats across training, the latter close to
\log(\text{vocab size})\approx 11.93
). Those heavy-tail tokens dominate the mean but are tolerated by the LLM judge—which follows its instructions to be “lenient on exact wording but strict on factual content.” Across epochs, median token CE correlates with fail rate at
r=+0.994
(Pearson), while mean token CE correlates at
r=-0.506
. The broader methodological note is that when a downstream evaluator is tolerant to surface-form variation, mean per-token validation loss can be actively misleading about task-level progress, and a heavy-tail-robust statistic such as the median is a more faithful training-time signal.

A Graded Pattern Across Memory Types

The three memory types respond differently to both compaction and consolidation:

•

Semantic knowledge
is most resilient to compaction (
62.0\%
at Cycle 3) and nearly fully preserved by consolidation (
94.3\%
). Meta-preferences about how the user wants the model to work are abstract and recurrent—summarizers tend to preserve explicit directives, and fine-tuning effectively encodes them.

•

Procedural corrections
retain only
36.3\%
under compaction. Consolidation recovers most of this loss, preserving
74.6\%
—a 38pp improvement. Procedural memories encode specific error-fix pairs (e.g., “
hmac.new()
doesn’t exist, use
hmac.digest()
”), concrete details that summarizers are unlikely to prioritize.

•

Episodic facts
are most vulnerable to compaction (
31.5\%
at Cycle 3). Consolidation preserves
78.2\%
—more than double the compaction result. Project-specific details (architecture decisions, configuration values, API designs) are precisely what summarizers deprioritize when space pressure forces triage, but they can be encoded as question-answer associations in weights.

Why Does Compaction Fail?

Each 6:1 compaction cycle discards approximately
83\%
of the context’s information, and because the continuation conversation is of similar length to the original, the surviving
\sim
17% must compete with entirely new content for the model’s attention. Lossy compression also compounds: each cycle discards information that cannot be recovered, so after three cycles the original conversation’s facts must survive repeated compression while being diluted by three full conversations of new content.

Why Does Consolidation Help?

Consolidation sidesteps the compaction problem entirely. Knowledge encoded in model weights is invariant to context pressure: it costs zero tokens at inference time, and—unlike the one-shot, position-bound storage of a summary—it is folded into the network through interleaved rehearsal of many paraphrased forms of each fact. New consolidation events can still overwrite earlier weights, but each fact has been distributed across many parameters during training, so any one update perturbs each fact only marginally—in contrast to a summary, where the previous version is discarded outright when the next compaction runs. The consolidation pipeline extracts facts from all available conversations (original + continuations), synthesizes diverse training examples, and trains the model to recall this knowledge in response to direct questions.

4
Discussion

Our results demonstrate a fundamental limitation of context-based memory management. Cascading compaction—the approach used by deployed assistants when conversations exceed context limits—loses 53 percentage points of knowledge within three cycles. This is not a pathological case: the 6:1 compaction ratio mirrors real-world behavior in tools like Claude Code, and three compaction cycles correspond to only a few additional continuation conversations on top of the original. Users of context-only assistants are
guaranteed
to lose the majority of accumulated knowledge over extended interactions.

Both compaction and consolidation produce a graded pattern across memory types. Semantic knowledge (coding conventions, tool choices) survives compaction reasonably well (
62.0\%
) and is nearly fully preserved by consolidation (
94.3\%
). Procedural corrections and episodic facts suffer severe compaction losses (
36.3\%
and
31.5\%
) but are substantially recovered by consolidation (
74.6\%
and
78.2\%
). The pattern is thus roughly semantic
>
procedural
\approx
episodic for compaction, with the consolidation advantage being largest for the memory types that compaction leaves most impoverished. We note that this compaction ordering does not recapitulate the classical biological dissociation, in which procedural memory is the most preserved type in amnesia—consistent with procedural memory in the brain depending on separate subcortical systems rather than the hippocampal-to-neocortical pathway that our consolidation pipeline emulates.

Deployment Economics

A natural objection to consolidation is compute cost. Two design choices make it practical in absolute terms. First, the expensive steps—fact extraction and training-data synthesis—are
one-time
per consolidation event (e.g., nightly), not per query, and operate on a bounded set of text (one conversation plus its continuations). Second, because Figure
2
shows pass rate peaks at epoch 8, we can use an 8-epoch schedule without sacrificing quality. At that budget, training fits comfortably on widely available hardware: we measured approximately
1.5 hours
per conversation on a single NVIDIA A100-40GB and
8.2 hours
on a commodity NVIDIA RTX 5090—a natural fit for overnight consolidation on user-owned hardware, mirroring the sleep–wake cycle that motivates CLS theory. In cloud terms the full pipeline (Claude-based synthesis plus LoRA training on a rented A100) is on the order of $5 per user-night; with user-owned compute the marginal cost falls to synthesis API calls alone.

Limitations

(1) We evaluate on software-development conversations only; whether the same retention pattern holds in other domains is untested. (2) Fact extraction and synthesis use an external LLM API; moving them on-device would further reduce platform cost but is not validated here. (3) We do not measure general-knowledge or general-coding benchmarks such as MMLU or HumanEval, since consolidation deliberately redirects representational capacity toward user-specific context.

Broader Impacts

The deployment we advocate has the happy property that
privacy is an advantage, not a trade-off
: because an 8-epoch run fits overnight on a consumer-grade GPU, consolidation can run on user-owned hardware, keeping accumulated knowledge in the user’s own weights rather than in a platform-hosted store—reducing data-leakage attack surface, loosening subscription-tied lock-in, and cutting per-query context cost. On the risk side, consolidated adapters inherit the standard concerns of fine-tuning pretrained models: biases or errors in the day’s conversations can be encoded in weights, and whether consolidation perturbs base-model refusal or safety behaviour has not been measured, motivating a standard-benchmarks audit of each consolidated adapter in production deployments.

Related Work

Continual learning.
The problem of teaching a model new information without erasing what it already knows has long been studied as continual learning, and methods fall into three broad families.
Regularization
approaches keep each new training step from drifting too far from the parameters that mattered for previous tasks; the canonical example is Elastic Weight Consolidation, which penalises changes to weights identified as important by a Fisher-information estimate
(
Kirkpatrick et al. 2017
;
Zenke et al. 2017
)
.
Replay
approaches mix examples from prior tasks (real or generated) into each new training batch so the model rehearses old skills while learning new ones
(
Buzzega et al. 2020
)
; our pipeline falls into this family, using LLM-generated paraphrases as the rehearsal stream.
Architectural-isolation
approaches dedicate disjoint parameter groups to different tasks—adding new modules over time
(
Rusu et al. 2016
)
or freezing portions of the network
(
Mallya and Lazebnik 2018
)
—so new learning cannot overwrite old by construction. The same toolbox is now being applied to large pretrained language models, where catastrophic forgetting of the base model’s general capabilities is a central concern
(
Wang et al. 2025
)
.

Closest precedents.
Three recent papers share ingredients with our pipeline. The most direct is
OPPU

(
Tan et al. 2024
)
, which proposes per-user LoRA adapters as an alternative to context-based personalization—the same approach we take. OPPU evaluates on
LaMP

(
Salemi et al. 2024
)
, a benchmark for personalized text classification and generation (e.g. predicting which paper title a particular user would write next), and trains each user’s adapter directly on that user’s raw interaction history. We differ in two ways: we extract a structured fact inventory from each conversation rather than fine-tune on raw transcripts, and we synthesize many paraphrased question–answer rehearsal examples per fact rather than using the original interactions verbatim.
Physics of Language Models 3.1

(
Allen-Zhu and Li 2024
)
supplies the empirical justification for that paraphrasing step. In a controlled study of pretraining-time fact memorization, they show that models trained on a single canonical statement of a fact memorize it but fail to extract it on simple queries phrased differently, while models trained on many surface paraphrases of the same fact extract it reliably; we extrapolate this finding from pretraining to per-user fine-tuning. Finally,
O-LoRA

(
Wang et al. 2023a
)
constrains each new LoRA adapter to lie in a subspace orthogonal to all previously trained task adapters, giving capability-protection by construction; our pipeline relies instead on the more limited form of isolation that LoRA already provides (the base weights are not touched and the adapter’s update is low rank).

6
Conclusion

Across ten realistic software-development conversations, cascading context compaction retains
36.8\%
of knowledge after three cycles (against an
11.8\%
no-context floor and a
90.1\%
full-context ceiling), while a nightly LoRA consolidation step reaches
80.4\%
with no context at test time, recovering
87\%
of the floor-to-ceiling gap for the cost of an overnight training run on a consumer GPU. As conversational history accumulates the consolidation curve stays essentially flat at
80
–
82\%
while the compaction curve continues to fall, so the gap between the two strategies grows with the length of the user’s ongoing relationship with the assistant. Along the way we note a methodological point: median per-token validation CE tracks LLM-judged accuracy (
r=+0.99
) while mean CE correlates the
wrong
way (
r=-0.51
). Treating every conversation as the model’s first is an engineering choice. Practitioners building long-running personalized assistants should replace cascading compaction with nightly weight-based consolidation: it costs less per query, retains more knowledge, and the gap only widens the longer the assistant is in use.
