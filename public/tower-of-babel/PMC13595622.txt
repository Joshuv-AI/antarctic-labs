Performance of cognitive screening tools in detecting possible dementia among older males in custody in Canada: a pilot study
Authors: Bryce E Stoliker, Krista Lagimodiere, Christie Tetreault, Lisa M Jewell, Olajide Adelugba, Susan Chalmers, Mansfield Mela, Megan O’Connell, Lindsay Reddick, Travis Boone, Shelley Peacock, Megan Surkan, Arlene Kent-Wilkinson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOlder individuals represent a growing demographic in correctional institutions worldwide, many of whom are living with complex health and social needs. One area of concern is that older people in custody are at increased risk of developing, or are currently living with, neurocognitive issues such as dementia. This pilot study examined the preliminary diagnostic performance of several cognitive screening tools for detecting suspected cases of dementia when compared with a formal diagnostic assessment among older people in custody at a multi-level forensic psychiatric treatment centre in Saskatchewan, Canada.ResultsData collection included administering cognitive screening tools to older people in custody (n = 28) and a formal diagnostic assessment for dementia by a geriatrician (n = 18). Findings revealed that (a) cognitive screening tools varied in the identification of possible dementia, with point estimates ranging from 21% (Canadian Indigenous Cognitive Assessment; CICA) to 43% (Community Screening Instrument for Dementia; CSI ‘D’), whereas 33% of participants were screened positive for cognitive impairment (Mini-Mental State Exam; MMSE); (b) approximately 39% were diagnosed with dementia and 17% with mild cognitive impairment; and, (c) certain screening tools (CICA and MMSE) perform better in classifying cases of dementia.ConclusionsPreliminary evidence suggests variability in the diagnostic performance of the assessed cognitive screening tools. The CICA and MMSE show promise in discriminating older males in custody in a forensic psychiatric setting with dementia from those without.Keywords: Dementia, Cognitive impairment, Cognitive screening tools, Prison, Older people in custody

Background

Across many countries, older and aging individuals represent a growing demographic in correctional institutions (Brooke et al., 2020; Forsyth et al., 2020; Gaston, 2018; Stoliker et al., 2022; Treacy et al., 2024). In Canada, the most recent estimates suggest that individuals aged 50 and older accounted for one-quarter of the overall population in federal custody (McKendy et al., 2019). Factors that account for the increasing proportion of older people in custody include overall trends in population aging and increased life expectancy, more crimes and arrests later in life, convictions for historical offences, better healthcare within prison systems, and strict sentencing policies and the imposition of longer sentences (Widra, 2023). This demographic shift has placed considerable pressure on correctional systems, as they are faced with the challenge of managing the complex health and social needs of older individuals within settings that are not designed for an aging population. One area of concern is that older people in custody are at increased risk of developing (or are currently living with) neurocognitive issues, such as dementia, especially as modifiable and non-modifiable correlates of cognitive and functional decline (Livingston et al., 2024)1 are highly prevalent among incarcerated populations (Baidawi et al., 2011; Brooke et al., 2020; Combalbert et al., 2018; du Toit et al., 2019; Fazel et al., 2001; Gaston, 2018; Maschi et al., 2012; Peacock et al., 2019; Skarupski et al., 2018; Stoliker et al., 2022; Treacy et al., 2024; Williams et al., 2012).

Given the increasing number of older and aging people in custody, combined with an elevated vulnerability for neurocognitive issues, international research has sought to estimate the prevalence of dementia and cognitive impairment in custodial populations to better understand the nature and extent of the problem (Iloabachie et al., 2025). Estimates from these studies suggest that anywhere between 1 to 70% of older people in custody may be living with dementia or cognitive impairment depending on study methodology, correctional context, and population characteristics (Ahalt et al., 2018; Combalbert et al., 2018; Forsyth et al., 2020, 2025; Perez et al., 2021; Stoliker et al., 2022; Verhülsdonk et al., 2021, 2023). Specifically, in a nationally representative study of 869 older people in custody in England and Wales, Forsyth et al. (2025) found the prevalence of dementia to be 7%, with the highest rates among those aged 70 years and older (11%), followed by those aged 50–59 (6.1%), and those aged 60–69 (5%). Moreover, efforts have also extended to identifying care pathways for older people in custody living with these health problems, as well as determining the training needs of correctional staff to support older people in custody (Forsyth et al., 2020; MacRae et al., 2024; Stoliker et al., 2022; Treacy et al., 2024). There is, therefore, a recognized need to incorporate cognitive screening tools into correctional healthcare policy and practice (Brooke et al., 2020; Peacock et al., 2019), as early detection is a critical first step toward implementing effective strategies for managing individuals with cognitive impairment and dementia in the prison setting (Forsyth et al., 2020; Gaston, 2018; Treacy et al., 2024).

Though past studies have adopted various instruments—with or without modification for the correctional setting—to enhance the epidemiological data on cognitive impairment and dementia among older people in custody (Iloabachie et al., 2025), some have argued that current cognitive screening tools and practices may not be suitable for identifying possible cases of dementia among custodial populations (Brooke et al., 2020). Specifically, cognitive screening tools have been developed and validated within community-based populations and, therefore, it is unclear whether specialized tools and practices may be required for the custodial setting (Iloabachie et al., 2025). Apart from two studies that aimed to validate instruments to screen for dementia and cognitive impairment within custodial populations (Forsyth et al., 2020; Modini et al., 2025), research has yet to identify a specific tool, or set of tools, most appropriate for this population. In addition, no studies have incorporated a formal diagnosis as part of the cognitive screening and assessment process to test the efficacy of cognitive screening tools in detecting possible dementia (i.e., diagnostic accuracy). This presents a significant gap and opportunity to build evidence on the diagnostic performance of cognitive screening tools within the correctional context. Specifically, research into dementia among older people in custody has been limited as it relates to the identification of instruments that can optimally distinguish the presence or absence of possible dementia or cognitive impairment (Brooke et al., 2020; Iloabachie et al., 2025).

Against this background, this pilot study aimed to assess the diagnostic performance of several cognitive screening tools in detecting suspected cases of dementia when compared with a formal diagnostic assessment (i.e., diagnosis by a geriatrician). The following research questions guided this study: (1) What is the prevalence of dementia and cognitive impairment among a sample of older people in custody? (2) Which cognitive screening tools are best able to distinguish the presence or absence of possible dementia? The goal of this investigation was to provide preliminary evidence regarding the efficacy of instruments used to screen for dementia within custodial settings, as well as to inform full scale studies.

Methods

Setting

The study was conducted at a multi-level custodial forensic psychiatric treatment centre in Saskatchewan (a prairie province located in western Canada with a population of approximately 1.2 million people). Specifically, the treatment centre is operated by Correctional Service Canada (CSC) and is designated for federally sentenced men and women (i.e., those serving custodial sentences of two or more years) requiring psychiatric assessment, treatment, stabilization, and rehabilitation while in custody. Therefore, the facility population comprises those living with one or more mental disorders. The treatment centre has approximately 345 correctional and health staff members and a capacity of 204 beds for forensic patients spread across five secure units, including one unit dedicated for women (20 beds) and one specialized unit for psychogeriatric patients (24 beds). It also includes a hospital unit (9 beds) for acute medical cases. Most patients are men and are referred from other federal correctional facilities within the surrounding region. Notably, the study population is unique in that individuals at this treatment centre have a higher level of psychiatric needs as compared with other custodial populations (Adelugba et al., 2015). The study facility also has a high proportion of Indigenous people in custody, which coincides with national-level trends. For instance, while representing 5% of the Canadian adult population, Indigenous Peoples are overrepresented in Canada’s correctional system (Department of Justice Canada, 2023), with the latest data suggesting Indigenous adults accounted for approximately one-third of all adult admissions to federal custody (Statistics Canada, 2024).

Measures

Cognitive screening tools

Three cognitive screening tools were chosen for this study, which included the Community Screening Instrument for Dementia (CSI ‘D’), the Canadian Indigenous Cognitive Assessment (CICA), and the Mini-Mental State Exam (MMSE). These tools were chosen for three primary reasons, including (1) previous use in research involving custodial populations (e.g., MMSE, CSI ‘D’), (2) specifically designed to mitigate cultural and educational biases (e.g., CSI ‘D’, CICA), and/or (3) development within the Canadian context (e.g., CSI ‘D’, CICA). While the MMSE may already be used in the provision of healthcare services across Canada’s federal institutions, the CICA and CSI ‘D’ have not been adopted as standard screening instruments for dementia and were only implemented as part of this research study. Other commonly used instruments were not included due to limited time and resources, as well as the primary interest in assessing tools designed in Canada that are relevant for Indigenous and non-Indigenous populations.

Community Screening Instrument for Dementia (CSI 'D')

The CSI ‘D’, used to flag those with possible dementia, was originally developed within the Canadian context and validated in diverse community-based populations (Hall et al., 1993, 1996, 2000; Hendrie et al., 1995; Unverzagt et al., 1999) and has previously been modified for use within custodial populations (Stoliker et al., 2022). The current study adopted this modified version of the CSI ‘D’, which includes a participant interview and informant (e.g., caregiver) questionnaire. The participant interview consists of 33 scored items on: memory (4), abstract thinking (4), higher cortical function (10), praxis (5), and orientation to time and place (10). Scores on these items are used to calculate a Cognitive Score, ranging from 0 to 33 (with a higher score indicative of better cognitive and functional capacity). The informant questionnaire consists of 23 scored items on: memory and cognition (11), activities of daily living (7), and miscellaneous problems (5). Scores on these items are used to calculate an Informant Score, ranging from 0 to 30 (with a lower score indicative of better cognitive and functional capacity). The Cognitive Score and Informant Score are combined to create a Discriminant Score, signifying risk for dementia (Hall et al., 1996, 2000). The Cognitive Score can also be used in the absence of an Informant Score. A separate scoring protocol for the Discriminant Score is used for Indigenous and other racialized persons (0.461839—[0.012164 * Cognitive Score] + [0.045880 * Informant Score]) versus non-Indigenous and non-racialized persons (0.564786—[0.015019 * Cognitive Score] + [0.044918 * Informant Score]) to mitigate potential educational and cultural biases (Hall et al., 2000, p. 526). Participants were placed into the following categories based on the Discriminant Score (DS) or Cognitive Score (CS): good performance = DS < 0.120 or CS only > 29.5; intermediate performance = DS 0.120–0.183 or CS only > 28.5 ≤ 29.5; poor performance = DS ≥ 0.184 or CS only ≤ 28.5. Those with poor performance are flagged for possible dementia (Hall et al., 1996, p. 136). With respect to psychometric properties of the CSI ‘D’, Hall et al. (2000) found it demonstrated good diagnostic accuracy for dementia detection across culturally distinct community-based populations, with area under the curve (AUC) values for the Cognitive Score ranging from 0.74 to 0.93 and values for the Discriminant Score ranging from 0.82 to 0.97. A brief version of the CSI ‘D’ (combined informant and cognitive score) demonstrated sensitivity between 85.1% to 100% and specificity between 85.2% to 100% across culturally distinct community-based populations (Prince et al., 2011). The psychometric properties of this instrument have not been previously investigated within custodial populations.

Canadian Indigenous Cognitive Assessment (CICA)

The CICA is also used to flag those with possible dementia and was originally developed and validated within Indigenous community-based populations in Canada (Jacklin et al., 2020). While the CICA was designed and validated for use with Indigenous populations, it has been used with a variety of settler and Indigenous Peoples with excellent overall accuracy statistics (O’Connell et al., 2022). For example, in a community-based sample of older First Nations men and women, Walker et al. (2021) reported the CICA demonstrated excellent reliability (intra-class coefficient = 0.95) and good diagnostic accuracy for detecting probable dementia, with an AUC value of 0.98 (sensitivity = 100% and specificity = 85%). The psychometric properties of this instrument have not been previously investigated within custodial populations. The current study used the standard version of the CICA, which had not yet been used with people in custody. The CICA contains 37 scored items on: orientation to time and place (3), recognition and naming (6), registration (3), verbal comprehension (3), verbal fluency (1), recall (3), visual naming (5), executive function (1), free recall (5), cued recall (5), and praxis (2). Scores on the CICA range from 0 to 39 (higher scores indicate better cognitive function), with a score of 34 or less indicative of possible dementia (Walker et al., 2021).

Mini-Mental State Exam (MMSE)

The MMSE is used to assess cognitive function and impairment (Folstein et al., 1975) and has been used (without modification) in several previous studies investigating dementia and cognitive impairment among older and aging people in custody (Combalbert et al., 2018; Verhülsdonk et al., 2021, 2023). The MMSE has also been recommended for screening for dementia (Verhülsdonk et al., 2023). This measure contains 11 scored items on: orientation to time and place (2), registration (1), attention and calculation (1), recall (1), and language (6). Scores on the MMSE range from 0 to 30 (higher scores indicate better cognitive function), with a score below 24 favoured for classifying cognitive impairment in community-based dementia studies (Mitchell, 2009). With respect to psychometric properties of the MMSE, a meta-analysis by Mitchell (2009) found it demonstrated moderate diagnostic accuracy with better performance as a “rule-out” screening tool (i.e., identifying true negative cases). Specifically, in discriminating individuals with dementia versus healthy controls in high-prevalence settings (e.g., memory clinics), pooled sensitivity and specificity was 77.0% and 91.2%, respectively, whereas in low-prevalence settings (e.g., community) pooled sensitivity and specificity was 83.3% and 86.6%, respectively. Despite being used in past research into dementia in custodial settings (Combalbert et al., 2018; Verhülsdonk et al., 2021, 2023), the psychometric properties of this instrument have not been assessed in this context.

Data collection

A multi-stage approach was adopted for data collection, which spanned between February and July 2024. First, an up-to-date list of all older individuals residing in the facility was extracted from the site’s data management system. Consistent with past research, older people in custody were defined as any non-Indigenous individual aged 50 years and older or any Indigenous individual aged 45 years and older (Baidawi et al., 2011; Iloabachie et al., 2025; Merkt et al., 2020; Stoliker et al., 2022).2 These age cut-offs are primarily based on assumptions of “accelerated aging” among incarcerated populations (i.e., the onset of physiological illnesses earlier in the lifespan), as well as the need to recognize racial and ethnic disparities in health (Stoliker et al., 2022). This served as the inclusion criterion for the study. Prospective participants were excluded if they had any medical conditions that would affect their ability to fully participate in the study, lacked the capacity to consent, or posed a safety risk to the research team as determined by one of the site’s forensic psychiatrists (OA).3 There was a total of 50 older individuals residing in the facility at the beginning of the study period.

Second, two research team members (BS and AK-W) visited the facility to meet with each individual to explain the purpose of the study and determine interest in participating. A total of 28 individuals agreed to participate in the study and, at that time, informed written consent was obtained after reading the consent form to each participant. Participants were also provided a copy of the consent form (which described the study and outlined all ethical considerations as per standard protocol for research involving humans), and refresher consent was obtained during any subsequent data collection activities. The remainder of the prospective participants (n = 22) were not included as they declined participation, were unable to participate due to medical reasons, or were going to be transferred/discharged prior to data collection.

Third, two research team members (BS and AK-W) and three graduate research assistants attended the site to administer the CSI ‘D’ participant interview and CICA to the 28 study participants. Prior to data collection, research team members received a one-day training session on the administration of the CSI ‘D’ and CICA by authors with past experience and/or knowledge of the tools (BS and MO). These instruments were administered to participants consecutively in one sitting; however, the order of administration was alternated between each interview to mitigate biases due to practice effects. The CSI ‘D’ also contains an informant questionnaire and, therefore, correctional healthcare professionals (i.e., nurses, social workers) who worked closely with the 28 study participants were asked to complete this component of the screening tool following the participant interview.

Fourth, the geriatrician (KL) visited the site to complete the final diagnostic assessment.4 Of the initial 28 participants, 18 completed the final diagnostic assessment (and MMSE). The remaining 10 were not included due to refusal, medical conditions, or transfer/discharge. The diagnostic process was informed by the Comprehensive Geriatric Assessment (CGA), which is the criterion standard for identifying and managing health-related problems in older adults (Briggs et al., 2022; Stuck et al., 1993; Williamson et al., 1964). In brief, the diagnostic process consisted of an in-person visit by the geriatrician (KL) at the study facility to complete a systematic evaluation during face-to-face appointments. Prior to appointments, the geriatrician reviewed participants’ medical profile across multiple domains (e.g., cognition, psychiatric history, nutrition, functional capability, sleep, social history, existing medical conditions, medications, blood panels, physical examinations, brain imaging), which was made available through the correctional system’s electronic medical records database. A nurse manager at the facility (SC) oversaw the collation of medical and background information (completed by nurses) for the geriatrician’s review, which followed a standardized template to ensure consistent and comprehensive information across all participants. As part of the face-to-face appointments, the geriatrician evaluated participants across multiple domains in accordance with the CGA (e.g., physical health, functional status, cognitive function, mental health, socioenvironmental considerations). This evaluation, along with the criterion standard of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (5th ed.), was used to determine a dementia diagnosis. Based on the overall assessment, the geriatrician provided a probable diagnosis of normal cognition, mild cognitive impairment, or dementia. A probable diagnosis was provided because, in some cases, collateral information was not available, or information was missing regarding participants’ medical profile.

Notably, the MMSE (Folstein et al., 1975) and Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) (Nasreddine et al., 2005) are standard components of the CGA and evidence from these screening tools is used to inform a dementia diagnostic assessment. Therefore, the MMSE was not used in isolation in this study, as scores from this assessment also informed the geriatrician’s overall clinical impression. However, the geriatrician did not administer the MMSE (and was blind to performances on the CICA and CSI ‘D’). As part of the diagnostic process, nurses at the facility collated information on participants’ medical profile, as well as administered the MMSE. The clock drawing test from the MoCA (Nasreddine et al., 2005) was used clinically and qualitatively to inform the overall diagnostic assessment. This was administered alongside the MMSE by the nurses prior to the geriatrician’s visit at the site for diagnostic assessments. It is important to note, however, that dementia diagnosis is clinical, based on expert opinion, and not solely based on evidence from cognitive screening tools. Evidence from cognitive screening tools, such as the MMSE and MoCA, serves as only one piece of information used to diagnose dementia (i.e., they are not used in isolation for a diagnostic assessment).

Finally, information was also collected on select individual characteristics based on self-report and corroboration with data derived from facility records. This included age, gender, Indigenous status, and education. All participants were English-speaking.

Ethical and operational approval

Our study design and procedures received ethics approval by the University of Saskatchewan's Behavioural Research Ethics Board (Beh 4169) on September 17, 2023, and operational approval by CSC Research Branch on October 5, 2023. This study was conducted in accordance with the Tri-Council Policy Statement on Ethical Conduct for Research Involving Humans (Canadian Institutes of Health Research et al., 2022). As per CSC policy, participants did not receive any form of benefits or compensation for taking part in this study or from obtaining a dementia diagnosis.

Statistical analysis

Data were cleaned and analyzed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 29. Scores were first calculated to transform the cognitive screening tools into summative scales and, subsequently, the abovementioned cut-off scores for each instrument were used to categorize participants according to performance level (i.e., possible dementia or cognitive impairment). Descriptive analyses were performed to describe sample characteristics (Table 1) separately for the full set of participants who were initially screened using the CSI ‘D’ and CICA (n = 28) and the subset who completed the diagnostic assessment and MMSE (n = 18). Descriptive statistics, internal consistency reliability, and correlations were also computed for the cognitive screening tools (Table 2). Sample prevalence estimates were calculated for performance on the cognitive screening tools (based on the above cut-off scores) and the outcome of the diagnostic assessment (Table 3). Analyses were also run to compare Indigenous and non-Indigenous participants according to outcomes on the cognitive screening tools and diagnostic assessment using Pearson chi-square test (Table 4). Finally, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analyses were conducted to assess the diagnostic performance of the cognitive screening tools. For ROC curve analyses, the outcome was categorized as probable diagnosis of dementia (positive outcome) versus normal cognition or mild cognitive impairment (referent category). The ROC analysis also computes optimal cut-off values for determining the presence or absence of an outcome of interest. This is valuable considering this type of analysis has yet to be done within custodial populations and, therefore, could provide insight into whether cut-off scores should be adjusted when administering these cognitive screening tools to similar populations. Given the nature of this study (i.e., pilot and exploratory) and small sample size for assessing diagnostic performance (n = 18), ROC curve analyses only provide preliminary estimates. Collectively, findings from all analyses are preliminary and should be interpreted with caution. There were no missing data and, with respect to hypothesis testing, probability values <.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Descriptive statistics

Table 1 presents sample characteristics by study stage. Of the initial 28 participants, the average age was approximately 59 years (SD = 9.44), all identified as men, the average level of education was approximately grade 9 (SD = 2.58), and 61% identified as Indigenous. Additional analyses revealed there were no statistically significant differences on sociodemographic characteristics, or performance on cognitive screening tools (i.e., CSI ‘D’ and CICA), between the subset of 18 who completed the study and the 10 participants who discontinued their participation after the initial screening. Table 2 presents summary statistics for the cognitive screening tools. Mean scores for the CICA (M = 35.32, SD = 2.84) and CSI ‘D’ Cognitive Score (M = 29.52, SD = 2.96) suggest the sample generally scored above the cut-off point, however, average scores on the MMSE (M = 23.55, SD = 5.10) were slightly below the cut-off point. All instruments showed acceptable internal consistency reliability, with Cronbach’s alpha coefficients ranging from.73 to.87. A Pearson correlation analysis indicated the CSI ‘D’ Cognitive Score, CICA, and MMSE were all positively and significantly associated.

Sample prevalence estimates by screening and diagnosis

Table 3 presents sample prevalence estimates according to performance on the cognitive screening tools, as well as the outcome of the final diagnostic assessment. Results show that approximately 21% (95% CI [6,36]) and 43% (95% CI [24,61]) were flagged with possible dementia using the CICA and CSI ‘D’, respectively, whereas 33% (95% CI [11, 55]) were flagged with cognitive impairment using the MMSE. Based on the final diagnostic assessment, approximately 39% (95% CI [16,61]) were diagnosed with probable dementia, 17% (95% CI [0,34]) with mild cognitive impairment, and 44% (95% CI [21,67]) displayed normal cognition.

With respect to Indigenous and non-Indigenous participants’ performance on the cognitive screening tools (Table 4), results show that a higher proportion of Indigenous participants screened positive for possible dementia on the CICA (35% vs. 0%) and CSI ‘D’ (53% vs. 27%), as well as cognitive impairment on the MMSE (36% vs. 29%). However, group-based comparisons suggest the only statistically significant difference between Indigenous and non-Indigenous participants was found for the CICA. With respect to Indigenous and non-Indigenous participants’ results from the final diagnostic assessment (Table 4), a higher proportion of non-Indigenous participants were diagnosed with dementia (57% vs. 27%) whereas a higher proportion of Indigenous participants were diagnosed with mild cognitive impairment (27% vs. 0%). Group-based comparisons suggest these differences were not statistically significant.

Diagnostic performance of cognitive screening tools

Figures 1 and 2 present results from the ROC curve analyses. The preliminary diagnostic performance of the CICA, CSI ‘D’ Cognitive Score and Discriminant Score, and MMSE were assessed against the classification of probable diagnosis of dementia (n = 7) versus normal cognition or mild cognitive impairment (n = 11). The CICA (AUC =.766, SE =.113, 95% CI [.546,.987], p =.018) and MMSE (AUC =.792, SE =.111, 95% CI [.575, 1.00], p =.008) significantly classified cases of probable dementia. Area under the curve (AUC) values for these instruments suggest classification performance above chance levels as the AUC exceeded 0.50. Although the estimated AUC values were 0.76 (CICA) and 0.79 (MMSE), confidence intervals were wide, indicating substantial uncertainty in the discrimination estimate. The CSI ‘D’ Cognitive Score (AUC =.636, SE =.134, 95% CI [.374,.899], p =.308) and Discriminant Score (AUC =.714, SE =.141, 95% CI [.437,.991], p =.129) did not significantly classify cases of probable dementia. Although the estimated AUC values were 0.63 (CSI ‘D’ Cognitive Score) and 0.71 (CSI ‘D’ Discriminant Score), confidence intervals suggest these tests perform no better than random prediction. Sensitivity and specificity were also calculated for the CICA (sensitivity =.285, specificity =.818), MMSE (sensitivity =.571, specificity =.818), CSI ‘D’ Cognitive Score (sensitivity =.428, specificity =.727), and CSI ‘D’ Discriminant Score (sensitivity =.714, specificity =.363). These diagnostic indices suggest that specificity exceeded sensitivity for all measures except the CSI ‘D’ Discriminant Score.

Diagnostic Performance of the CICA, CSI ‘D’ Cognitive Score, and MMSE

Diagnostic Performance of the CSI ‘D’ Discriminant Score

ROC analyses also provided a potential range of cut-off values according to sensitivity and specificity. Given the importance of early detection of dementia, we emphasized higher sensitivity in our assessment of optimal cut-off scores for probable dementia. Accordingly, an optimal cut-off value for a positive classification of probable dementia would be ≤ 35 for the CICA (sensitivity =.714, specificity =.727) and ≤ 26 for the MMSE (sensitivity =1.00, specificity =.636). An optimal cut-off value would be less than or equal to 30.29 for the CSI ‘D’ Cognitive Score (sensitivity =.571, specificity =.727), and greater than or equal to 0.2812 for the CSI ‘D’ Discriminant Score (sensitivity =.714, specificity =.909).

Discussion

Several studies have utilized cognitive screening tools to build epidemiological data on the extent of dementia and cognitive impairment among older and aging people in custody (Ahalt et al., 2018; Combalbert et al., 2018; Forsyth et al., 2020, 2025; Modini et al., 2025; Perez et al., 2021; Stoliker et al., 2022; Verhülsdonk et al., 2021, 2023), as well as assessed the diagnostic performance of certain instruments (Forsyth et al., 2020; Modini et al., 2025). This pilot study is one of the first to explore the preliminary diagnostic performance of several cognitive screening tools within a custodial population when compared with a formal diagnostic assessment. Similar extant research had only relied upon retrospective diagnostic information (Forsyth et al., 2020; Modini et al., 2025).

Interpretation of key findings

In the present study, cognitive screening tools varied with respect to the estimation of possible dementia (based on pre-determined cut-off scores), with point estimates ranging from 21% (CICA) to 43% (CSI ‘D’). Thirty-three percent of participants were screened positive for cognitive impairment (MMSE). This is consistent with previous research in that screening tools point to a high percentage of older people in custody with impaired cognition, but also that there can be variability in prevalence estimates across instruments used within the same study (Combalbert et al., 2018; Stoliker et al., 2022; Verhülsdonk et al., 2021, 2023). Related to the former point, health screens tend to be overinclusive to ensure people who require specialized services are not screened out (Trevethan, 2017) and, therefore, diagnoses may be more conservative. According to the diagnostic assessment, approximately 39% and 17% were given a probable diagnosis of dementia or mild cognitive impairment, respectively (44% with normal cognition).

Though findings are preliminary, diagnostic indices suggest the CSI ‘D’ and MMSE may be more inclusive in screening for probable dementia, whereas the CICA may be less inclusive in this regard, as evidenced by the CICA showing the lowest sensitivity (28.5%). Most measures also showed higher specificity relative to sensitivity, with exception to the CSI ‘D’ Discriminant Score (for which sensitivity exceeded specificity). This suggests these measures perform better in correctly identifying cases without probable dementia and, therefore, may have greater utility as “rule-out” screening tools. A meta-analysis on studies using the MMSE in community-based settings similarly reported that specificity exceeded sensitivity when discriminating individuals with dementia versus healthy controls (Mitchell, 2009). When compared with community-based studies using the CICA (Walker et al., 2021) and CSI ‘D’ (Prince et al., 2011), these instruments underperformed on sensitivity and specificity in the present study. Due to the exploratory nature of this study and small sample size, only tentative claims can be made regarding the ability of these measures to accurately discriminate the presence or absence of probable dementia based on the above diagnostic indices (i.e., sensitivity and specificity). Future studies with adequate sample size and power should verify these findings.

Furthermore, when comparing participants according to Indigenous or non-Indigenous status, a higher proportion of Indigenous persons were screened with possible dementia (CICA and CSI ‘D’) and cognitive impairment (MMSE), whereas a higher proportion non-Indigenous persons received a probable diagnosis of dementia. Despite the substantive differences in cognitive performance between Indigenous and non-Indigenous persons, these differences were not statistically significant with respect to the CSI ‘D’, MMSE, and diagnostic assessment. The only exception was performance on the CICA, where Indigenous persons were statistically significantly more likely to screen positive for possible dementia as compared with non-Indigenous persons. Notably, the CICA only identified Indigenous persons with possible dementia. Though this finding was unexpected given the tool has been used with both settler and Indigenous populations (O’Connell et al., 2022), it does align with the fact that it was specifically designed and validated for use with Indigenous Peoples (Walker et al., 2021). Irrespective of these findings, it is important to acknowledge factors that may influence the efficacy of cognitive screening tools for use with Indigenous people in custody. First, risk assessments that are not developed specifically for Indigenous Peoples tend to “over-classify” this group (Gutierrez et al., 2016; Olver et al., 2024). Second, certain health and social factors may be more common among Indigenous persons (Perdacher et al., 2019), which could influence performance on cognitive screening tools. Third, while two of the three tools do account for cultural and educational biases through their design and scoring protocols (i.e., CICA and CSI ‘D’), the scoring procedures (e.g., cut-off scores) used in the current study may not be sensitive to the current population (Perez et al., 2021). Future validation studies should further investigate the efficacy of cognitive screening tools for use with Indigenous people in custody.

Most importantly, our pilot study extends beyond descriptive analyses by investigating the preliminary diagnostic performance of three cognitive screening tools. This aligns with the recognized need to establish suitable cognitive screening tools and practices for identifying possible cases of dementia among custodial populations (Brooke et al., 2020) as a first step toward establishing supports for people in custody living with dementia. Indeed, past research has sought to validate certain screening tools for routine use in prisons to aid in the classification of older people in custody with possible dementia or cognitive impairment. Specifically, Forsyth and colleagues (2020) found that the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (Nasreddine et al., 2005) was more effective than the six-item cognitive impairment test (Brooke & Bullock, 1999) for screening older people in custody in England and Wales. Modini et al. (2025) found that the Addenbrookes Cognitive Examination – Third Revision (ACE-III) and Mini Addenbrookes Cognitive Examination (M-ACE) demonstrated utility as potential tools for use in incarcerated populations. Specifically, both tools acceptably discriminated those with suspected dementia from those without among 114 males in custody in Victoria, Australia.

Though the current study only included a select number of cognitive screening tools, AUC values suggest that the CICA (AUC =.766, 95% CI [.546,.987]) and MMSE (AUC =.792, 95% CI [.575, 1.00]) significantly distinguished cases of probable dementia from those with normal cognition or mild cognitive impairment above chance levels. However, wide confidence intervals and low sensitivity raise uncertainty regarding the diagnostic accuracy of these instruments. The CSI ‘D’ (including both the Cognitive Score and Discriminant Score) performed with no better than random prediction in classifying cases of probable dementia. Comparatively, these instruments performed relatively poorly in discriminating positive and negative cases when assessed against large-scale validation studies conducted in community-based settings (Hall et al., 2000; Mitchell, 2009; Prince et al., 2011; Walker et al., 2021), as these studies reported moderate to good diagnostic performance for the CICA, MMSE, and CSI ‘D’. While this could suggest the need to develop cognitive screening tools specifically designed for a custodial setting (Iloabachie et al., 2025), it is plausible that these findings were due to the study design (i.e., pilot study with a small sample size). Nevertheless, there is preliminary evidence to suggest that certain tools (i.e., CICA and MMSE) perform better in identifying cases of possible dementia within a custodial sample, which aligns with the findings from Forsyth and colleagues (2020). One plausible explanation for the variability in the diagnostic performance of these cognitive screening tools could be the slight differences in their design and measurement. Specifically, certain tools may consist of constructs and items that are better weighted to detect elements of cognitive and functional capacity linked to dementia in a custodial population. Future research should investigate whether (a) the diagnostic performance of these cognitive screening tools improves in a large-scale study and (b) any observed differences in diagnostic performance is linked to differences in the design and measurement of these instruments.

Implications for research and practice

While all findings are preliminary and should be interpreted with caution pending follow-up validation studies, several implications may be outlined to inform practice and research into the selection of cognitive screening tools for custodial settings. The CICA and MMSE were the only instruments that were able to significantly classify cases of probable dementia above chance levels and, thus, may be more effective than the CSI ‘D’ in screening for possible dementia within custodial populations. However, a significantly higher proportion of Indigenous persons screened positive on the CICA and, what is more, only Indigenous persons were flagged with possible dementia on this instrument. This suggests the CICA may only be appropriate for screening for possible dementia among Indigenous custodial populations. This aligns with the construct validity of this tool as it was specifically designed for Indigenous populations in Canada (Jacklin et al., 2020; Walker et al., 2021). Taken together, it is likely that separate cognitive screening tools will need to be adopted for Indigenous and non-Indigenous people in custody to effectively screen for possible dementia, especially in Canada where there is an over-representation of Indigenous Peoples in custodial settings (Statistics Canada, 2024). For instance, the CICA shows promise as a potential cognitive screening tool for Indigenous people in custody. Other cognitive screening tools, such as the MMSE, may be most appropriate for non-Indigenous persons. Nevertheless, further investigation is needed to clarify whether these or other instruments can effectively identify possible dementia in both Indigenous and non-Indigenous custodial populations.

Considering this was a pilot study with a small sample size that only assessed three possible cognitive screening tools, there is not enough evidence to suggest which instrument(s) would be most appropriate for the custodial context. Other commonly used instruments were not included in this study, such as the DemTect (Kalbe et al., 2004), Addenbrookes Cognitive Examination – Third Revision (Hsieh et al., 2013), 6-item cognitive impairment test (Brooke & Bullock, 1999), and Montreal Cognitive Assessment (Nasreddine et al., 2005). More evidence on the diagnostic performance of these and other cognitive screening tools within the custodial setting is needed prior to recommending the clinical application of an instrument. Relatedly, this study involved a unique population of federally sentenced forensic psychiatric inpatients with a high level of psychiatric needs and, therefore, the screening tools that may show promise in this setting and population (e.g., CICA and MMSE) could differ from other custodial populations. This warrants further investigation into the efficacy of cognitive screening tools across sub-populations of people in custody. There is also the potential need to consider a specialized cognitive screening tool for custodial populations, as existing instruments have primarily been developed and validated within community-based populations (Iloabachie et al., 2025). This warrants further investigation into whether specialized tools and practices may be more effective for custodial settings (Brooke et al., 2020; Iloabachie et al., 2025). In addition to diagnostic performance, it is also importance to consider the clinical utility (e.g., administration, scoring, etc.) when selecting cognitive screening tools for the custodial setting. The CICA and MMSE are relatively brief, require little time, are easy to administer, and consist of a straightforward scoring protocol. Conversely, the CSI ‘D’ contains many more items, requires both a participant interview and informant questionnaire, requires more time to administer, and consists of a complex scoring protocol. These extraneous factors are relevant to cognitive assessment in custodial settings, as time and resources are sparce in these contexts.

Irrespective of the cognitive screening tool(s) selected for the custodial setting, it is important to define appropriate cut-points for classifying possible cases of dementia and cognitive impairment. Cut-points for the CICA, MMSE, and CSI ‘D’ could potentially be optimized for custodial populations; particularly, those similar to the current study. Drawing from the current preliminary evidence for diagnostic performance, optimal cut-points for positive classification of dementia would be ≤ 35 for the CICA. This would result in a true positive rate of 71.4% and a true negative rate of 72.7%. An optimal cut-point for the MMSE would be ≤ 26. This would result in a true positive rate of 100% and a true negative rate of 63.6%. While estimates may be unreliable for the CSI ‘D’, a cut-point for the Cognitive Score would be less than or equal to 30.29 (true positive rate of 57.1% and true negative rate of 72.7%), whereas for the Discriminant Score a cut-point would be greater than or equal to 0.2812 (true positive rate of 71.4% and true negative rate of 90.9%).

Since these instruments are health screeners, it is arguably more acceptable to have greater inclusivity (i.e., higher cut-points) to increase the chances that those in need are able to receive further clinical assessment and specialized health services (Trevethan, 2017). A potential drawback, however, is that this would be more costly for correctional systems due to the expenses associated with conducting clinical assessments for those screened at risk for dementia. In populations with high levels of comorbidities, including older and aging people in custody, increased sensitivity of health screeners may result in a greater number of referrals for clinical assessment where cognitive impairment is not attributable to dementia (e.g., it may be related to psychiatric comorbidities, substance use, medication, other physiological illness or disease). This could place strain on correctional healthcare services with limited capacity for specialist services, as well as limit access to timely assessment and care pathways for older people in custody (e.g., waitlists). Potential misclassification due to increased sensitivity of health screeners could also influence how individuals are understood and managed within custodial settings.

Strengths and limitations

A major strength of this study lies within the fact it was one of the first to investigate the preliminary performance of several cognitive screening tools in classifying cases of dementia when compared with a formal diagnostic assessment, in a forensic psychiatric custodial population. However, findings should also be interpreted in light of several limitations.

First, the low response rate and small sample size may have introduced biases to the data. Nearly half (44%) of prospective participants were not included in the study and, among the 28 older people in custody who initially began the study, approximately 35% did not complete the diagnostic assessment. Thus, it is unclear whether and how the analytic sample differed from those who were not included in the study or dropped out after the first stage of data collection. This may have affected reliability and increased errors in estimates. In addition, a smaller sample size limits statistical power and variability, introducing measurement error for ROC curve analyses and, thus, restricts any claims regarding the diagnostic performance of the cognitive screening tools as estimates may be unstable. Findings from ROC curve analyses are therefore preliminary and may only inform future adequately powered validation studies; though findings suggest possible clinical utility, replication is required. While the number of cases were limited for ROC curve analyses, it is the most appropriate statistical test to assess the performance of cognitive screening tools with respect to distinguishing cases with or without a probable diagnosis of dementia. In addition, the study was conducted in a forensic psychiatric treatment centre, representing a setting and population that is difficult to access, which provides further justification for a small sample size. It should be recognized that, given this was a pilot study with a small sample and limited statistical power, all findings are tentative and should be interpreted with caution. Future research should consider including a larger sample of older people in custody to investigate the current research objectives, verify our findings, and validate these (or similar) cognitive screening tools.

Second, the current sample only included older men in custody as one woman met the age criterion for the study but was ruled out due to medical reasons; thus, findings cannot be generalized to older women in custody. Future research should address this gap, as little is known about gender differences in this line of research (Forsyth et al., 2025). Third, while this study included three instruments, it could not include the full spectrum of cognitive screening tools that exist and have previously been used within custodial populations (Ahalt et al., 2018; Combalbert et al., 2018; Forsyth et al., 2020, 2025; Perez et al., 2021; Stoliker et al., 2022; Verhülsdonk et al., 2021, 2023). Notably, we omitted the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (Nasreddine et al., 2005) which, alongside the Mini-Mental State Exam (Folstein et al., 1975), is a standard component of the Comprehensive Geriatric Assessment (Briggs et al., 2022; Stuck et al., 1993; Williamson et al., 1964). The decision to omit this tool was due to the low levels of formal education (and potential learning disabilities) among the study sample and, therefore, the MoCA may not have been appropriate as it was validated with people with higher formal education. Other tools, including the ones assessed in the current study, could also be influenced by education. This should be further investigated as it was beyond the scope of this study to directly examine diagnostic performance of cognitive screening tools across educational levels. Future research should apply similar methodology used in this study to provide additional evidence on the performance of these and other instruments in classifying cases of dementia, particularly within the context of specific sub-groups (e.g., gender, education, age, race/ethnicity).

Fourth, the study facility is a unique and specialized setting, which houses individuals with serious and complex mental health needs; thus, it is not comparable to other mainstream correctional settings. This further limits the generalizability of findings from this study. Similar studies should be conducted to build evidence on the performance of cognitive screening tools within diverse custodial populations. Fifth, the current study did not directly examine or statistically control for the confounding effects of comorbidities, such as psychiatric diagnoses. This presents a significant limitation given the characteristics of the study sample (i.e., forensic psychiatric patients) and the fact that psychiatric conditions could influence cognitive performance, as well as mask or resemble symptoms of dementia. Sixth, the CSI ‘D’ informant questionnaire completed by correctional healthcare workers (i.e., nurses, social workers) relied upon retrospective self-reports, which is vulnerable to biased recall and social desirability. Seventh, the cognitive screening tools generally measure the same constructs and, therefore, results may include biases due to practice effects; though, the research team attempted to mitigate these biases by alternating the order of administration of the screening tools between each interview. Eighth, there is potential bias in assessing the diagnostic performance of a cognitive screening tool that also informed part of the dementia diagnosis (i.e., MMSE). However, this tool was administered by a nurse at the facility and only formed a small portion of all the information needed and used by the geriatrician to make a diagnosis.

Finally, it is important to acknowledge the practical challenges associated with conducting a dementia assessment and diagnosis within the study population. Specifically, there was limited ability to access family collateral history (e.g., pre-incarceration cognitive and functional status), few participants had brain imaging available, and there was an absence of documentation on functional abilities within the institution. These factors limited the support for the final clinical diagnosis. In addition, a low formal education, high burden of psychiatric illness, neurocognitive deficits (e.g., linked to developmental delay, learning disability, Fetal Alcohol Spectrum Disorder, traumatic brain injury, etc.), and other confounding factors (e.g., use of psychotropic medications) in this population could influence the reliability and interpretation of test scores as well as increase the difficulty in determining a diagnosis given a lower baseline level of function. Notably, clinical information compiled and used to inform the final dementia diagnosis was not formally collected and analyzed as part of current analyses. Therefore, we could not provide detail on the level of missingness for clinical information within the study sample. Overall, given the complexity of completing a dementia assessment and diagnosis within a correctional institution, studies should specifically examine the diagnostic process in these settings to inform best practice.

Conclusion

With the continued increase in the number of older and aging people in custody, combined with an elevated risk for neurocognitive disorders (e.g., dementia), there is a need to improve the provision of care for this population. Routine cognitive assessments should be integrated into correctional healthcare policy and practice, as early detection is important for developing effective strategies to manage individuals with cognitive impairment and dementia in the prison setting (Forsyth et al., 2020; Gaston, 2018; Treacy et al., 2024). However, it is still unclear whether current cognitive screening tools and practices are suitable for identifying possible cases of dementia and cognitive impairment within custodial populations (Brooke et al., 2020). This pilot study has contributed to limited evidence on the preliminary effectiveness of cognitive screening tools within custodial populations, suggesting some instruments perform better in classifying cases of dementia and that adjustments according to cut-points should be considered for optimal performance. It is critical to continue building evidence on the efficacy of (modified and non-modified) cognitive screening tools across diverse custodial populations which, in turn, will help improve care pathways for older people in custody living with dementia or cognitive impairment (MacRae et al., 2024).
