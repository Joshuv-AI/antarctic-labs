A Self-Evolving Agentic Framework for Metasurface Inverse Design
Yi Huang *,, Bowen Zheng, Yunxi Dong, Hong Tang, Huan Zhao, S. M. Rakibul Hasan Shawon, Hualiang Zhang, #,

Abstract
Metasurface inverse design can realize complex optical functionality, but turning a target response into executable optimization code still requires substantial expertise in computational electromagnetics and solver-specific software engineering. We present a self-evolving agentic framework that addresses this barrier by coupling a coding agent, explicit skill artifacts, and a deterministic physics-based evaluator. Rather than updating model weights, the framework updates workflow guidance based on solver-grounded rollout feedback, while keeping the base model and differentiable solver fixed. On a benchmark spanning multiple metasurface inverse-design task types, evolved skills raise in-distribution task success from 38% to 74%, increase criteria pass fraction from 0.510 to 0.870, and reduce average attempts from 4.10 to 2.30. On held-out task families, binary success changes only marginally, but improvements in best margin, error composition, and agent behavior indicate partial transfer of workflow knowledge. These results suggest a practical path toward more autonomous and accessible metasurface inverse-design workflows.

Introduction

Metasurface design is increasingly cast as an inverse problem, in which a target optical response is specified and the corresponding structure is sought
[
12
,
20
]
. This paradigm reflects the growing demand for broadband, multifunctional, and highly integrated meta-optical devices, whose associated design spaces are high-dimensional, strongly coupled, and resistant to intuition-guided exploration, particularly under freeform parameterizations
[
1
,
22
,
23
,
14
,
7
,
11
]
. In parallel, deep-learning-assisted methods have broadened the computational design landscape for metasurfaces and flat optics
[
2
,
6
,
3
]
. Consequently, intuition-driven geometry tuning and parameter sweeps are no longer adequate for the efficient identification of high-performance structures, and computational inverse-design workflows have become central to metasurface research
[
12
,
20
,
14
,
9
,
8
,
11
]
. Yet the growing centrality of inverse design has not translated into commensurate ease of automation. Converting a desired optical response into an executable design workflow still requires specialized knowledge of computational electromagnetics together with the software-engineering expertise needed for solver-specific workflow implementation and maintenance
[
20
,
14
]
. These barriers often confine metasurface inverse-design workflows to relatively narrow, task-specific design problems, making them difficult to extend to more complex functionalities. The same limitations also make broader multiphysics applications harder to realize, thereby restricting further interdisciplinary exploration and development.

In practice, the difficulty lies in turning a target response into a working inverse-design program. To run an inverse-design task, the researcher must turn a target response into a working program: choose a parameterization, define the objective and constraints, decide how to initialize and optimize the variables, and verify the solver outputs
[
20
,
14
]
. Recent progress in
large language model (LLM)
-based coding, tool use, and natural-language interaction makes part of this workflow easier. Early studies in nanophotonic inverse design mostly used
LLMs
as surrogate predictors or natural-language interfaces to existing tasks
[
19
,
15
,
13
,
26
]
. More recent systems instead use the model to write code, call tools, and assemble optimization loops around established computational engines
[
16
,
18
]
. Our recent
Model Context Protocol (MCP)
-based framework showed that this workflow can be grounded in standardized solver access without compromising mathematical rigor
[
10
]
. Even so, these systems are still short-horizon: they can complete a task-specific workflow, but they do not retain solver-specific tactics in a form that later tasks can reuse
[
16
,
18
,
10
]
.

Addressing that limitation requires a workflow that can keep and reuse what worked on earlier tasks. In this work, drawing on the bi-level view of
meta context engineering (MCE)

[
25
]
, we evolve context-level artifacts rather than model weights. The learned object is a set of explicit skill files revised from rollout feedback. This keeps adaptation outside the base model and avoids the data demands and forgetting risks associated with fine-tuning
[
4
,
5
,
17
]
. A separate deterministic evaluator scores each candidate with physical simulation rather than model self-assessment, so the model, the solver, and the scoring rule remain fixed throughout the study
[
21
,
24
]
. We therefore ask two concrete questions: whether context-level skill evolution improves task success and search efficiency in physics-based metasurface inverse design, and whether the resulting guidance transfers at all to held-out task families. To our knowledge, this is the first study to examine context-level skill evolution in agentic metasurface inverse-design workflows.

In the reference implementation, the coding agent is instantiated as a single
LLM
with generic file and shell tools, while solver usage patterns, code templates, and repair heuristics are supplied through the skill files. More generally, however, the framework only assumes a coding agent that maps the task specification and current skill set to a candidate optimization program. This coding agent may therefore be realized by a single agent or by a multi-agent system without changing the evaluator or the outer skill-evolution loop. Its responsibility is limited to program synthesis and revision; execution, scoring, and stopping decisions remain outside the coding agent.

A deterministic evaluation pipeline, separate from the coding agent, executes each generated candidate program and evaluates the resulting design against fixed physical criteria. Both retry levels follow a Ralph-style generate, execute, feedback, and revise pattern
[
21
]
. Within one outer round, inner attempts revise the same candidate program while retaining local contextual feedback, and the highest-ranked evaluated candidate from that round is retained. Across outer rounds, the coding-agent session is reset, and only the best prior candidate together with a compact summary is carried forward. Evaluated candidates are ranked lexicographically by
CPF
and then
BM
, with earlier candidates preferred under ties; if no candidate reaches physical evaluation in any round, the task is recorded as an execution failure. The procedure terminates as soon as any attempt reaches
SG

=1
. Because evaluation is performed outside the coding agent by a fixed procedure, the evaluation signal cannot be modified by the model itself.

2.2
Problem Formulation and Evaluation Metrics

Each design task specifies a target optical response
\mathbf{y}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
(e.g., a transmission or reflection spectrum), from which a set of
K_{i}
task-specific physical criteria
\{g_{i,j}\}_{j=1}^{K_{i}}
is derived.
The objective is to find design parameters
\mathbf{p}_{i}\in\Omega_{i}
such that the electromagnetic response computed by the forward solver
\mathcal{F}
satisfies all criteria simultaneously:

g_{i,j}\!\bigl(\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{p}_{i})\bigr)\geq 0,\quad j=1,\ldots,K_{i},

(2)

where
\mathcal{F}:\Omega_{i}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
maps design parameters to the simulated optical response using the
TorchRDIT
backend, and
g_{i,j}\!\bigl(\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{p}_{i})\bigr)
denotes the scalar margin for criterion
j
of task
i
. For inequality criteria, the margin is the signed distance to the threshold; for target-matching criteria, it is the tolerance minus the deviation from the target. A nonnegative margin indicates that the criterion is satisfied, whereas a negative margin indicates violation.

We report five dataset-level metrics for evaluating design performance.
Success execution (SE)
measures the fraction of tasks for which the generated program executes successfully and the solver returns a valid simulation result:

\text{SE}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\llbracket\,\text{code}_{i}\ \text{executes}\;\wedge\;\text{solver}_{i}\ \text{completes}\,\rrbracket.

(3)

SG
is the primary metric. For each task, we define a binary success indicator

\text{SG}_{i}=\llbracket\,\forall\,j\in\{1,\ldots,K_{i}\}:\ g_{i,j}\!\bigl(\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{p}_{i})\bigr)\geq 0\,\rrbracket,

(4)

and report the dataset-level success rate as

\text{SG}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\text{SG}_{i}.

(5)

By definition,
\text{SG}\leq\text{SE}
; the gap
\text{SE}-\text{SG}
captures tasks that execute successfully but fail to meet the physics requirements.
CPF
provides a finer-grained view by measuring the fraction of satisfied criteria for each task:

\text{CPF}_{i}=\frac{1}{K_{i}}\sum_{j=1}^{K_{i}}\llbracket\,g_{i,j}\!\bigl(\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{p}_{i})\bigr)\geq 0\,\rrbracket,

(6)

and report the dataset-level average as

\text{CPF}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\text{CPF}_{i}.

(7)

BM
quantifies the normalized margin of the least satisfied criterion. For each task
i
, we normalize the raw criterion margin by a criterion-specific scale,

\hat{m}_{i,j}=\frac{g_{i,j}\!\bigl(\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{p}_{i})\bigr)}{c_{i,j}},\qquad c_{i,j}=\begin{cases}\tau_{i,j},&\text{for target-matching criteria},\\
|t_{i,j}|,&\text{for inequality criteria with }t_{i,j}\neq 0,\\
1,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(8)

where
t_{i,j}
and
\tau_{i,j}
denote the target and tolerance of criterion
(i,j)
, respectively. We then define the task-level best margin as

\text{BM}_{i}=\min_{j}\,\hat{m}_{i,j},

(9)

and report the dataset-level average as

\text{BM}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\text{BM}_{i}.

(10)

A positive
BM
indicates that all criteria are met with margin to spare, while a negative value reveals the magnitude of the worst violation. Finally,
Attempts
denotes the mean number of retry attempts used per task in Algorithm
1
.

3
Experiments

3.1
Experimental Setup

We evaluate the framework with two complementary protocols. The
in-distribution (IID)
split asks whether evolved skills help on tasks drawn from template families represented during training. The
out-of-distribution (OOD)
split asks whether the same guidance helps on families withheld during skill evolution. This separation lets us distinguish within-family improvement from transfer across family changes.

All benchmark tasks are derived from six primary metasurface design template families (G1–G6) together with one auxiliary family (
G_{\mathrm{aux}}
) used for split balancing, where each family groups closely related task templates with shared physical structure and optimization objectives. These families have known feasible solution structures, ensuring that each instance corresponds to a physically meaningful inverse-design problem rather than an unconstrained code-generation exercise. Each task specifies a target optical response together with a set of physical criteria, as defined in Section
2.2
. The experiments use fixed 50/15/50 train/validation/test partitions for both the
IID
and
OOD
settings. During skill evolution, each iteration samples a training batch of 20 tasks, while the full validation split of 15 tasks is used for skill selection and the independent test split of 50 tasks is reserved for final reporting.
A split-by-split summary of the finalized IID and OOD partitions is given in Appendix
S2
, and representative task specifications for each template family are provided in Appendix
S3
. Because validation and test are assigned to different held-out families and the test split spans multiple physical regimes, the OOD benchmark should be read as heterogeneous family-heldout transfer rather than as a uniform extrapolation test.

In this work, both the meta-agent and the coding agent use Claude Sonnet 4.6. Skill evolution is performed for four iterations. In each iteration, the current skill set is applied to a batch of training tasks to produce rollout feedback, after which the meta-agent updates the skills based on the observed outcomes. Candidate skill sets are selected using the held-out validation split. For each setting, we evaluate the baseline agent initialized with a starter initial skill on the fixed 50-task test split before skill evolution and re-evaluate the selected post-training skill set on the same held-out test split after training. Thus, the training and validation splits are used exclusively for skill refinement and model selection, while both baseline and post-training results are measured on the same test dataset. Iteration-wise training and validation dynamics are summarized in Appendix
S4
.

Read together, they show not only whether more tasks are solved, but also whether they are solved earlier and with fewer retries.

On IID, all three views indicate a cleaner search process. The Pass@K curve shifts upward at small
K

Taken together, the evolved skills more often produce workable optimization code on the first few tries and spend fewer retries on avoidable dead ends.

On OOD, the figure is much flatter. This matches Table

SG
=92%) with only 1.60 attempts on average. With so little headroom, the small changes in Pass@K, transitions, and attempts do not suggest that the evolved skills are opening many new solution paths on unseen families. Instead, they point to weaker, task-dependent transfer, which is consistent with the stronger movement in
BM
than in
SG

3.4
Error Analysis

Figure 3:
Error-type composition across training rounds of skill evolution. Panel (a) shows IID and panel (b) shows OOD. Infrastructure failures, such as connection errors and timeouts, are excluded so that the figure reflects code-level errors more directly tied to skill quality.

In Figure
3
(a), IID errors drop quickly after the first plotted round. Tensor-indexing and API-misuse failures disappear once they have been observed and written back into the skill set, which suggests that these mistakes are comparatively easy to correct through explicit procedural guidance. The trajectory is not monotonic, however: one intermediate round shows a spike in gradient-related failures. This indicates that some newly adopted optimization patterns can still break the computational graph before later rounds repair them.

In Figure
3
(b), the OOD error mix changes, but less decisively than in IID. API-misuse errors dominate the first round and then largely disappear, which suggests that solver-usage patterns and execution hygiene transfer across held-out families. What remains is mostly missing-code or no-solution behavior, indicating that the harder problem is no longer low-level API correctness but producing complete solution programs for unfamiliar tasks. The transferred skills therefore seem more effective at preventing obvious implementation mistakes than at supplying the higher-level design strategy needed to finish every held-out task. Definitions of the error categories are provided in Appendix

4
Discussion and Conclusion

Overall, this paper argues that a central bottleneck in metasurface inverse design is not the absence of a physics solver, but the difficulty of repeatedly turning task specifications into working optimization code. The proposed self-evolving framework addresses that bottleneck by keeping the coding agent, solver, and evaluator fixed while evolving explicit skill files from solver-grounded rollout feedback. In this formulation, the adapted object is the workflow guidance itself rather than the model weights.

The strongest gains appear on tasks aligned with the training families. Under IID evaluation, skill evolution raises
SG
from 38% to 74%, increases
CPF
from 0.510 to 0.870, reduces mean attempts from 4.10 to 2.30, and suppresses several recurring procedural errors. On held-out families, the effect is narrower: binary success changes little, but
BM
, error composition, tool-use traces, and cost all indicate partial transfer of solver-specific workflow knowledge. We therefore interpret the method as improving workflow reliability much more clearly than it establishes broad family-independent generalization.

The broader implication is practical. In this setting,
LLMs
are most useful as programmable interfaces around reliable computational engines, not as replacements for the solver itself. Coupled to a deterministic evaluator, explicit skill files turn solver-grounded feedback into inspectable, revisable, and reusable workflow knowledge. That makes the framework relevant not only for improving task reliability, but also for lowering the combined computational-electromagnetics and software-engineering barrier that still limits accessibility, cross-disciplinary use, and extension to more complex or multiphysics design problems. At the same time, the present evidence is bounded by one solver stack, one benchmark design, and only modest OOD gains. Larger benchmarks, additional solver backends, and harder cross-regime or multiphysics transfer tests are the next natural targets.

Across sampled IID and OOD runs, the evolved
SKILL.md
files consistently combine four kinds of guidance: methodology-level workflow rules, solver API and execution contracts, optimization and task-type-specific recipes, and failure-avoidance or context-engineering heuristics. Skill evolution therefore updates reusable textual guidance while keeping the base model, evaluator, physical solver, and held-out test split fixed. In this sense, this work studies the evolution of reusable context artifacts rather than the adaptation of model weights.

Appendix S2
Dataset Split Summary

Rather than enumerating implementation-specific template identifiers, this appendix describes the benchmark used in this work in terms of abstract task families. A task instance is written as

\tau=(\xi,\mathcal{X},\mathcal{C}),

where
\xi
denotes the fixed physical context, including wavelength, incidence condition, material setting, and lattice parameters,
\mathcal{X}
denotes the design space, and
\mathcal{C}
denotes the evaluation criteria. The benchmark is organized around six primary families
G_{1},\dots,G_{6}
, plus one auxiliary family
G_{\mathrm{aux}}
used only for split balancing. For transmissive phase-target tasks, the criteria take the form

T(\lambda_{0};x)\geq\tau_{T},\qquad d_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\!\left(\phi(\lambda_{0};x),\phi^{\star}\right)\leq\varepsilon_{\phi},

where
d_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}
denotes cyclic phase distance. Reflective tasks use one or two constraints on
R
, evaluated across wavelength, incidence angle, or polarization depending on the family.

Under IID, the partitions stay close because they come from sibling templates with related objective structure and similar physical settings, even though the exact task instances differ across splits. Under OOD, the train, validation, and test splits use disjoint task families, so transfer must cross changes in both objective structure and physical regime rather than merely unseen parameter samples. Table
S3
reports the train and validation metrics of the candidate skill sets considered during skill evolution. The starter-skill baseline and post-train rows are test-set evaluations before and after evolution. The intermediate rows summarize the train and validation outcomes observed in each round. Validation metrics provide the selection signal, while training metrics reflect adaptation on the sampled rollout batch.

The table is not a strict adjacent-iteration lineage. As described in the main text, each new round starts from the best previously selected skill set according to validation performance. The trajectory therefore shows successive rounds under best-prior carry-forward. The post-train row is evaluated on the fixed held-out test split rather than on the validation split used for selection, which makes it directly comparable to the starter-skill baseline row.

The table mirrors the main results. Under IID, validation SG reaches 100% early and training CPF rises substantially, which is consistent with rapid improvement within seen template families. Under OOD, validation behavior is less stable across rounds and the final test SG stays close to an already strong baseline. This is why the main text treats OOD effects as weaker and reads them mainly through continuous metrics rather than binary success alone.
