Evolution of percutaneous tension band constructs: biological fixation strategies in contemporary patellar fracture management
Authors: Wei Li, Mei Zhou, Yang Li, Hui Zhang, Quankui Zhuang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThis article aims to review the progress of minimally invasive tension band techniques in the treatment of patellar fractures, summarize the operational points, clinical outcomes, and advantages and disadvantages of various techniques, and provide a reference for clinicians.MethodsA literature search was conducted in PubMed, Cochrane Library, and China National Knowledge Infrastructure databases up to December 2023. Search terms included “patella fracture” or “patellar fracture” combined with “minimally invasive,” “percutaneous,” “tension band,” or “arthroscopic.” Clinical studies on minimally invasive tension band treatment for patellar fractures were included.ResultsMinimally invasive tension band techniques mainly include simple tension band technique, K-wire tension band technique, cannulated screw tension band technique, and non-absorbable suture technique. Based on the use of arthroscopic assistance, they can be categorized into arthroscopically assisted and non-arthroscopically assisted minimally invasive techniques. Compared with traditional open surgery, minimally invasive tension band techniques have significant advantages in operative time, hospital stay, postoperative pain, joint function recovery, and cosmesis. However, issues such as the need for secondary implant removal surgery and the controversial necessity of arthroscopic assistance remain unresolved.ConclusionMinimally invasive tension band techniques for patellar fractures may offer potential advantages including reduced soft tissue trauma, earlier postoperative mobilization, and improved cosmetic outcomes. However, the current evidence base is limited by a predominance of small, retrospective studies with heterogeneous techniques and short-term follow-up. While these approaches represent a promising direction, high-quality comparative studies with long-term outcomes are needed to establish definitive superiority over conventional open techniques.Keywords: Patella fracture, Minimally invasive technique, Arthroscopically assisted technique, Non-arthroscopically assisted technique, Tension band technique

Introduction

The patella, considered the largest sesamoid bone in the human body, is located in the front of the knee joint and plays a crucial role in the mechanism of knee extension [1]. Patella fracture constitute a relatively frequent skeletal injury, accounting for around 1% of all fractures [2]. Patella fractures can occur due to various mechanisms, including direct trauma from an anterior blunt force, indirect trauma from avulsion due to excessive tensile stress, or a combination of these trauma mechanisms [3]. Transverse fractures occurring in the middle third of the patella are the most common type, accounting for roughly 50–80% of all patella fractures [4].

It is well known that the majority of patella fractures do not require surgical treatment.Specialists have widely agreed that patella fractures with more than 2 to 3 mm of articular step-off or displacements between 1 and 4 mm require surgical treatment [5, 6]. And patella fractures without displacement have been considered suitable for conservative treatment.

In recent years, epidemiological studies have shown that surgical treatment for patella fractures is becoming increasingly popular [3]. Due to its alignment with the biomechanical characteristics of the patella, the tension band technique has long been considered the gold standard for the treatment of patella fractures and is widely used in clinical practice [7].The approaches for implementing the tension band technique mainly include open reduction internal fixation and closed reduction internal fixation, with open reduction internal fixation still being the predominant method [8].However, advancements in medical technology and materials in recent years have led to an increasing acceptance of closed reduction internal fixation for patella fractures, known as the minimally invasive tension band technique. This article provides a comprehensive review of the application of the minimally invasive tension band technique in treating patella fractures.

Methods

This is a narrative review conducted in accordance with the SANRA (Scale for the Assessment of Narrative Review Articles) reporting guidelines.

Search strategy

A literature search was performed in PubMed, Cochrane Library, and China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI) databases from inception to December 2023 to identify studies on minimally invasive tension band techniques for patellar fractures. The search strategies were as follows:

Pubmed: ((((minimally invasive) OR (percutaneous)) OR (tension band)) OR (arthroscopic)) AND ((patella fracture AND (1854:2023[pdat])) OR (patellar fracture AND (1854:2023[pdat])))

Cochrane Library (Cochrane Reviews): (Patella Fracture OR Patellar Fracture) AND (Tension Band OR Minimally Invasive OR Percutaneous OR arthroscopic).

CNKI (English articles): (SU=’patella fracture’ OR SU=’patellar fracture’) AND (SU=’minimally invasive’ OR SU=’percutaneous’ OR SU=’tension band’ OR SU=’arthroscopic’ OR SU=’cerclage’ OR SU=’K-wire’ OR SU=’cannulated screw’ OR SU=’suture’).

CNKI (Chinese articles, supplementary): (SU=’髌骨骨折’) AND (SU=’微创’ OR SU=’经皮’ OR SU=’张力带’ OR SU=’关节镜’ OR SU=’环扎’ OR SU=’克氏针’ OR SU=’空心钉’ OR SU=’缝线’).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Two reviewers (WL and MZ) independently screened these records based on the inclusion criteria. Disagreements were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer (QKZ). The inclusion criteria were: (1) studies on patellar fractures; (2) closed reduction internal fixation techniques; (3) English or Chinese language. The exclusion criteria were: (1) patellar dislocation; (2) non-patellar fractures; (3) pediatric patellar fractures; (4) animal studies; (5) reviews; (6) full text unavailable; (7) open reduction internal fixation; (8) non-tension band techniques.

Study selection

English-language search

The initial English-language search yielded 867 records: PubMed (n = 530), Cochrane Library (n = 12), and CNKI (n = 325, English articles only). After removing 240 duplicate records, 627 articles remained for title and abstract screening.

Two reviewers (WL and MZ) independently screened the titles and abstracts based on the inclusion criteria. Disagreements were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer (QKZ). Of the 627 articles screened, 591 were excluded as irrelevant to minimally invasive tension band techniques or not meeting inclusion criteria.

The remaining 36 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility and all met the inclusion criteria. These 36 studies formed the core evidence base for this review [9–44].

Chinese-language search

The Chinese-language search in CNKI yielded 3,439 records. These records were not intended to form part of the core evidence base for this review, as the review focuses on peer-reviewed English-language literature that is internationally accessible and subject to standardized peer-review processes. Instead, a limited number of Chinese-language studies were selectively reviewed to provide contextual background and to identify technical variations that may not be captured in the English literature. These studies are cited as supplementary references where relevant [13, 18, 19, 33], but were not subjected to the same systematic screening process applied to the English-language studies.

Supplementary references

To provide background context on patellar fracture epidemiology, biomechanical principles of tension band fixation, and conventional open surgical approaches, landmark studies and authoritative references were identified through snowballing (reference lists of included articles) and the authors’ expert knowledge. These supplementary references are also indicated in the text.

Non-arthroscopically assisted minimally invasive techniques

Current minimally invasive tension band techniques for treating patella fractures primarily encompass tension band technique, kirschner wire tension band technique, cannulated screws, and non-absorbable suture techniques.

Simple tension band technique

In 1983, Leung and colleagues first detailed the process of treating patella fractures using the percutaneous 8-figure tension band technique [45] (Fig. 1). Outcomes from five case studies underscored several benefits: shorter operation times, the possibility of early postoperative mobilization, and aesthetically favorable results, suggesting that this technique might be a superior option for managing patella fractures. Additionally, they provided crucial guidance on various aspects of the technique. Notably, they established the indications for using this method, applicable primarily to undisplaced fractures or those with a displacement gap of less than 0.5 cm. Moreover, while hemarthrosis can be addressed by other methods before and after surgery, the focus on minimally invasive techniques highlights their potential for less invasive and more effective fracture management.Later, Biyani et al. applied the same percutaneous eight-figure tension band technique to treat 26 patients with minimally displaced patella fractures, achieving satisfactory outcomes. They further suggested that postoperative plaster immobilization was not necessary, indicating a more streamlined and potentially quicker recovery process for patients [31].

Simple tension band technique (percutaneous figure-8 tension band technique). A: A cannulated introducer needle is advanced through the quadriceps tendon, allowing a stainless steel wire to be threaded through its lumen and exit the contralateral soft tissue. B-C: Using the introducer needle as a conduit, the wire is sequentially passed anterior to the patellar ligament, forming the superior loop of the figure-8 configuration. The needle is then redirected to complete the formation of the inferior loop. D: The introducer needle is advanced through the inferior portion of the patellar ligament. Both wire ends are retrieved through this needle channel, establishing a secure tension band anchor point. E: The wire ends are manually tensioned to achieve anatomical reduction, then securely twisted and crimped to maintain compressive force across the fracture site

Ma and colleagues reported on 107 cases of patella fractures, including transverse, upper or lower pole, and comminuted fractures, treated with percutaneous cerclage wire [30] (Figs. 2, 3 and 4). The majority of cases showed excellent or good outcomes. The study suggests that comminuted patella fractures can also be effectively treated with the percutaneous cerclage technique. Additionally, the research indicates that in cases where the patella ligament tear is not repaired during surgery, good knee joint function can still be achieved postoperatively [32, 35, 37, 38]. Clinical comparative studies have shown that percutaneous cerclage tension band can shorten hospital stay, promote bone healing, and reduce pain [33, 39]. In response to the issues of wire breakage and loosening, some researchers attempted to use titanium cable cerclage in the treatment of 21 cases of comminuted fractures [46]. The results showed that the titanium cable had superior tensile strength and fatigue resistance compared to wires. Matsuo achieved percutaneous minimally invasive treatment of patella fractures using titanium cable cerclage [34]. Later, Zhai and colleagues also achieved good results by using percutaneous double steel wire cerclage technique to treat elderly type C patella fractures [37]. One study has shown that percutaneous titanium cable cerclage combined with an 8-figure steel wire minimally invasive treatment for patella fractures had significant advantages over open internal fixation surgery in terms of postoperative complications and knee joint function [39].

Kirschner wire tension band technique

In order to overcome the issue of insufficient strength of tension band technique in maintaining patella stability, scholars have adopted Kirschner wire tension band technique to treat patella fractures. The method has been effective in achieving stability at the fracture site [47, 48]. Therefore, the Kirschner wire tension band technique has also become a classic surgical procedure for treating patella fractures. Luna-Pizarro and colleagues utilized specialized auxiliary instruments to achieve minimally invasive treatment of patella fractures using Kirschner wire tension band technique [40] (Fig. 5). Postoperatively, it was found that in terms of surgical time, postoperative pain, and joint function, this approach was superior to open surgery. Subsequent studies have also confirmed that percutaneous Kirschner wire tension band technique has significant advantages in the treatment of patella fractures [20, 36, 41–43].

K-wire tension band technique (percutaneous k-wire stainless steel wire figure-8 tension band technique). A: After closed reduction of the fracture, two parallel K-wires are inserted for percutaneous fixation to stabilize the fracture ends. B: A cannulated introducer needle is percutaneously passed beneath the two K-wires, and the steel wire is threaded through the introducer to the contralateral side. C, D: Using the cannulated introducer, the steel wire is passed in front of the patellar ligament to form a figure-8 configuration. E: The steel wire is passed through the distal ends of the two parallel K-wires via the cannulated introducer. F: Compression is applied, and the ends are securely tied

Cannulated screws

Kirschner wire tension band technique still has its shortcomings, including poor reduction, loosening of internal fixation, skin irritation, and delayed postoperative functional exercises [49]. To address these drawbacks, some scholars have attempted to use two cannulated screws for transverse patella fractures [50]. Research showed that compared to conventional tension band techniques, cannulated screws titanium cable tension band technique has significant advantages in terms of functional recovery, degree of reduction, and other aspects [51]. Cho J-H et al. successfully treated 30 cases of patella fractures using percutaneous cannulated screws steel wire tension band [22] (Fig. 6). There have been reports of using minimally invasive cannulated screw tension band techniques to treat patella fractures [17–21, 23–27].

Cannulated screw tension band technique. A: After closed reduction of the fracture, two parallel guide wires are used for percutaneous fixation to stabilize the fracture ends. B: A cannulated screw of adjustable length is screwed in along the guide wires. C: At the other end of the guide wire, the cannulated screw cap is screwed in along the guide wire. D: The guide wires are removed, and using a specific cannulated screwdriver, the steel wire is implanted into the cannulated screw. E: At the other end of the cannulated screw, using the specific cannulated screwdriver, the steel wire is passed through the skin

Non-absorbable suture techniques

The major drawback of metal implants is the need for a second surgery to remove the implant. In addition, internal implants for patella fractures may also cause skin irritation [52]. To address this issue, related studies have shown that non-absorbable sutures can effectively stabilize patella fractures, serving as a potential alternative to metal internal implants [53–55]. Some scholars have achieved satisfactory results in treating patella fractures using minimally invasive techniques with percutaneous non-absorbable sutures and cannulated screw [28]. Other studies have also demonstrated that non-absorbable sutures can substitute for metal implants [28, 29].

Arthroscopically assisted minimally invasive techniques

Arthroscopy assisted minimally invasive techniques in treating patella fractures enable direct visualization of the reduction of the patella articular surface. According to the different types of minimally invasive tension band fixation, arthroscopic techniques have been used to assist in various minimally invasive treatments of patella fractures, including cannulated screws [12, 56], cannulated screws combined with tension bands [10, 11, 13, 14], Kirschner combined with circumferential wire loop [9, 15], Kirschner wire tension band [27], and others [16]. Arthroscopic assisted minimally invasive techniques for the treatment of patella fractures offers the following advantages: removal of intra-articular hematomas, clearance and debridement of loose fragments, and visualization of the articular surface for reduction.

Discussion

Minimally invasive treatment of patella fractures is an inevitable trend of the times

Numerous studies have shown that minimally invasive treatment of patella fractures has been achieved in most types of fractures, which better meets the needs of modern individuals [37]. Compared to open reduction internal fixation for patella fractures, related studies have shown that minimally invasive treatment offers significant advantages in terms of surgical time, postoperative aesthetics, time from injury to surgery, postoperative knee joint adhesions, interference with blood supply, and postoperative hospital stay [25, 26, 40, 55, 57] (Table 1). However, it should be noted that these comparisons are derived primarily from small, non-randomized studies, and the magnitude of differences may vary across techniques and fracture types.

Surgical indications are expanding

With the improvement of people’s quality of life and the accelerated pace of life, the rapid recovery of postoperative life and work after patella fracture surgery has become an important goal for patients. With continuous improvement of minimally invasive treatment methods for patella fractures, this goal is gradually being achieved. Patella fractures that were previously considered suitable for conservative treatment are now becoming indications for surgery due to the development of minimally invasive patella techniques. Some scholars have already used minimally invasive techniques to treat non-displaced or mildly displaced patella fractures [45].

Limitations of minimally invasion treatment for patella fractures

The key challenge of minimally invasive tension band techniques lies in how to improve and innovate current techniques for the treatment of patella fractures. Although current minimally invasive techniques for patella fractures offer numerous advantages such as shorter operative times, reduced hospitalization, lower complication rates, earlier mobilization, and improved aesthetics [22, 30, 34, 45], there are still some unresolved issues that need to be addressed.

Metal internal fixation materials used in treating patella fractures, such as K-wires, stainless steel wires, titanium cables, and cannulated screws, often necessitates a second surgery for hardware removal in many patients. Additionally, some patients may experience pain and irritation due to the presence of metallic fixation devices.Therefore, non-metallic materials may become the primary materials for treating patella fractures in the future.Some previous studies have shown that patients with patella fractures have been successfully treated with open reduction internal fixation using bioabsorbable cannulated lag screws with or without non-metal wire suture [58, 59]. Further statistical analysis and biomechanical evidence are needed to support the feasibility of using non-metallic materials for patella fracture treatment.

Moreover, based on the arthroscopic-assisted technique mentioned above, it is understood that arthroscopy for the treatment of patella fractures plays roles such as evacuating intra-articular hematomas, removing and debriding free bone or cartilage fragments, and visualizing the reduction of the articular surface [60]. In fact, there is a controversial issue regarding the clinical significance of arthroscopy for the minimally invasive treatment of displaced patella fractures.Firstly, the evacuation of intra-articular hematomas through arthroscopic means may not be essential, as some studies have shown that hematomas can be effectively drained through a stab incision without the need for arthroscopic intervention [12]. Secondly, During surgery, arthroscopic techniques cannot fully replace a C-arm. Arthroscopy can only assess the condition of the patella joint surface and cannot evaluate the overall reduction of the fracture or determine if the position of internal fixation devices is appropriate [11, 12, 14]. Thirdly, concerns about radiation exposure during arthroscopic procedures are also highlighted. While some may argue that arthroscopy can potentially reduce radiation exposure, there is a lack of concrete evidence supporting this claim. Additionally, successful outcomes have been reported in patella fracture cases treated using non-arthroscopic minimally invasive techniques, indicating that arthroscopy may not always be essential for achieving good results [22, 34]. In the future, more evidences about detailed roles of arthroscopic in the treatment of patella fracture will be needed.

The learning curve for arthroscopically assisted patellar fixation is steeper than for percutaneous techniques without arthroscopy, as it requires proficiency in both arthroscopic visualization and percutaneous instrumentation. The additional equipment requirements—including arthroscopic tower, camera system, fluid management, and specialized instruments—increase procedural costs and may limit accessibility in resource-constrained settings. To date, no prospective comparative studies have directly evaluated whether the added complexity and cost of arthroscopic assistance translate into improved clinical outcomes compared with non-arthroscopic percutaneous techniques.

Economic and Training Resource Considerations

The widespread adoption of minimally invasive tension band techniques also faces challenges related to economics and training resources. Specialized instruments such as cannulated screw systems and arthroscopic equipment increase medical costs and may be difficult to popularize in resource-limited settings. Furthermore, percutaneous techniques require a certain learning curve, and the training needs of young surgeons deserve attention. Future research should include cost-effectiveness analyses to evaluate the feasibility of different minimally invasive techniques in various healthcare settings and develop simulators for training in minimally invasive patellar fracture procedures.

Critical appraisal of the evidence

A critical examination of the included studies reveals important limitations that temper the strength of conclusions that can be drawn regarding minimally invasive tension band techniques for patellar fractures. As summarized in Table 2, the majority of included studies are retrospective case series (Level 4 evidence), with only a few randomized controlled trials and retrospective cohort studies (Levels 2 and 3). The predominance of low-level evidence has several implications. First, estimates of complication rates and functional outcomes may be subject to selection bias and reporting bias. Second, the lack of comparative effectiveness data limits the ability to determine which minimally invasive technique is superior for specific fracture patterns. Third, the absence of long-term follow-up in most studies leaves questions about hardware-related complications, osteoarthritis progression, and reoperation rates unanswered. These limitations do not invalidate the clinical utility of minimally invasive techniques, but they underscore the need for cautious interpretation of the available literature. Readers should recognize that current evidence supports feasibility and short-term safety rather than definitive superiority over open techniques.

Conclusion

Patella fractures are a common injury resulting from direct and indirect trauma during physical activities. The minimally invasive tension band technique is becoming widely accepted because it does not disrupt the vascular supply around the patella and allows for early postoperative rehabilitation. However, there are still some limitations with the current technique. Therefore, future efforts should prioritize the conduct of well-designed multicenter prospective studies and randomized controlled trials to provide higher-level evidence on the comparative effectiveness, safety, and long-term outcomes of minimally invasive tension band techniques. Until such evidence becomes available, the role of these techniques should be considered complementary rather than replacement to established open surgical approaches.
