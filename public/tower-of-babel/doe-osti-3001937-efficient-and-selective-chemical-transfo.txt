 Abstract
 The acceleration of chemical reaction rates and the increased product selectivity in microdroplets
 compared to that in bulk solutions has become a topic of increasing interest that has been
 extensively characterized by electrospray ionization mass spectrometry (ESI-MS). However, the
 sources of this acceleration and the detailed relationships between droplet properties and
 resulting reaction rate acceleration are still under debate. Moreover, droplet properties are
 governed by multiple interrelated experimental parameters, i.e., electrospray voltage, solution
 flow rate, etc., which makes it difficult and time-consuming to explore this diverse parameter
 space using traditional manual experimental or computational approaches. In this work, we
 developed an automated experimental platform integrating reactions in controlled charged
 microdroplet environments with ESI-MS characterization and sequential hybrid Bayesian
 modeling, as well as an optimal experimental design framework, to achieve multidimensional
 parameter optimization for higher reaction turnover rates, based on a model reaction of
 tetraethylenepentamine (TEPA) with carbon dioxide. With the current platform, we have achieved
 automated scans with a range of electrospray voltages and solution flow rates, and determined
 and optimized parameter settings to achieve increased reaction turnovers. We have also linked
 this platform to the underlying properties of droplets via a hybrid model incorporating physics,
 high-level theoretical calculations, and machine learning (ML) approaches. The autonomous
 platform is broadly applicable to a range of chemical reactions relevant to DOE’s mission in
 chemical separations, catalysis, and materials synthesis.

Abstract ii

 Summary
 This project developed an experimental microdroplet reaction platform integrating ESI-MS
 characterization, autonomous multidimensional parameter exploration, and optimization of
 targeted reaction turnover rates utilizing a physics-informed ML algorithm. In this work, we chose
 a model reaction of TEPA with CO2 and first characterized it through ESI-MS and determined the
 reaction turnover rate by calculating the relative intensities of unreacted TEPA and TEPA-CO2
 complexes from mass spectra obtained at a wide range of experimental conditions. Hundreds of
 mass spectra were obtained across the selected parameter space with various electrospray
 voltages and solution flow rates using an automated experimental protocol and data acquisition
 method. At each parameter setting, a scan of various distances between the electrospray capillary
 emitter and the mass spectrometer inlet was performed to obtain real-time kinetic data on
 reactions between solvated amines and gaseous CO2 at the droplet interface.

 A hybrid model incorporating physical/chemical properties and ML components was first
 trained using Bayesian inference to calibrate the physics model to find values for the kinetic rate
 constants and the heteroskedastic error, which were consistent with the acquired data. The model
 was then further refined by solving a maximum likelihood estimation on Gaussian process
 hyperparameters. The final hybrid model was used in a multi-objective optimization to find
 optimized experimental parameters that simultaneously maximize TEPA-CO2 formation and
 minimize the standard deviation of the heteroskedastic observational noise. A Pareto optimal
 solution was used to balance these two competing factors. In summary, we demonstrated the
 application of Bayesian hybrid modeling in ESI-MS characterization, parameter estimation, and
 optimal experimental design of chemical reactions in highly charged and confined nanodroplets.
 This platform is a unique capability that may be used to study other DOE-relevant chemical
 reactions at the droplet-gas interface relevant to industrial separations and catalytic
 transformations.

1.0 Introduction
 ESI-MS is a powerful analytical tool for determining the molecular masses and chemical
structures of gaseous ions, ranging from isolated species like amino acids to nanoscale particles
(Prabhu et al. 2023). Beyond simple molecular weight information, ESI-MS is also capable of
studying chemical reactions in situ, providing insight into reaction kinetics (Schröder 2012;
Banerjee and Mazumdar 2012; Mehara and Roithová 2020). Numerous recent studies suggest
that the rate of chemical reactions may be dramatically accelerated in microdroplets generated
by ESI compared to the same reactions taking place in conventional bulk solutions (J.K. Lee et
al. 2015; Z. Wei et al. 2020; Wilson and Prophet 2024). Such phenomena have seen potential
applications in the energy sciences, for example, in the absorption of carbon dioxide (CO2). Amine
solutions have been investigated for large-scale industrial CO2 capture at point sources, yet these
processes remain energy intensive (113 kWh per mt CO2 removed) and require high pressures
to compress gaseous CO2 (150 bar) (D. Wei, Junge, and Beller 2021). In comparison, reactions
between amines and CO2 are found to be much faster and spontaneous in microdroplets
generated by ESI (Huang, Wei, and Cooks 2021). In these unique reaction environments, the
turnover rate is related to droplet properties that are in turn governed by well-established
experimental variables, such as the electrospray voltage, solvent flow rate, etc. (Rovelli et al.
2020). However, understanding the complex interplay between these interrelated droplet
properties and experimental variables is challenging and time-consuming to acheive using
traditional manual experimental or computational approaches. Therefore, optimization of the
experimental turnover rates has largely been accomplished through time-, labor-, and resource-
intensive manual experimentation (Volk et al. 2023).
 There has been substantial recent research aimed at developing kinetic models and
software for studying droplet-based chemistry. For example, a kinetic model for gas-droplet
multiphase uptake and surface/bulk reaction kinetics under atmospheric conditions was
developed (Wilson, Prophet, and Willis 2022). This model includes complex interfacial and bulk
partitioning kinetics as well as stochastic reaction-diffusion phenomena. Additionally, there are
general-purpose simulation tools such as Kinetiscope (Madhavan et al. 2024; Deal et al. 2024)
which have be used to simulate complex interfacial kinetics and reaction-diffusion via discrete
stochastic events. While Kinetiscope is well-suited for exploring the forward problem, i.e.,
simulating the outcomes of a prescribed reaction network under varying conditions, it does not
address the inverse problem: given experimental observations, what are the most plausible kinetic
parameters or mechanisms? In contrast, the hybrid kinetic modeling and Bayesian inference
approach developed in this project is explicitly designed for solving inverse problems. We
combine mechanistic ordinary differential equation (ODE) models of reaction-diffusion kinetics
with parametric and data-driven corrections to capture previously unmodeled dynamics and utilize
experimental data to quantify uncertainty in kinetic rate parameters. This novel allows us to
identify probable values for rate constants and test mechanistic scientific hypotheses in a
statistical framework with built-in uncertainty quantification. As such, we present a new platform
combining automated experimentation, driven by a physics/chemistry-based ML model, and
mechanistic theoretical/computational work to probe the relationship between CO2 capture and
droplet properties in an efficient and reliable fashion (Figure 1). The multivariate nature of the
droplet reactivity problem is well-suited to autonomous experimentation and control. The physics-
based model employed here also yields chemical insight into the processes occurring in
microdroplets.

2.0 Methods
2.1 Experimental Characterization of Reactions in Microdroplets
 ESI-MS mass spectra of the reactions of TEPA dissolved in microdroplets with gaseous
CO2 were recorded using a Thermo LTQ Orbitrap XL mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher
Scientific, San Jose, CA) operated in the positive ion mode. Droplets containing TEPA molecules
were generated by spraying 100 µM methanolic solutions of TEPA (Sigma-Aldrich) through a
custom capillaruy (fused silica Molex capillaries, 50 µm inner diameter) mounted on a home-built
three-axis adjustable stage to ensure precise distances to the mass spectrometer inlet. The ESI
process was carried out without the conventional neuralizer gas. Instead an enclosure purged
with CO2 (OXARC) was assembled and used to induce reactions between solvated TEPA and
gaseous CO2. The resulting mass spectra were collected over a mass-to-charge ratio of m/z =
100 – 300 with the following settings: capillary temperature, 175 °C; s-lens RF level, 68 %; RF
amplitude, 400 V. These instrument settings were found to produce sufficient abundances of the
reactant and product ions without inducing fragmentation. For model training, we scanned a batch
of mass spectra with 20 combinations of source voltages and liquid flow rates that were generated
through a Latin hypercube sampling protocol (Table 1). At each parameter setting, a set of 12
mass spectra were acquired with controlled distances between the ESI emitter and mass
spectrometer inlet in the range of 0.01 – 0.12 inches in intervals of 0.01 inch. As the droplets are
formed at the ESI emitter and the reaction ends following desolvation at the mass spectrometer
inelt, changing the distance between these components provides direct insight into time-
dependent reactions in evaporating microdroplets.

2.2 Theoretical Modeling of Reaction Thermodynamics and Rate
 Constants
 Density functional theory (DFT) calculations were employed to calculate the energies of
reactions between amine carbon capture solvents and CO2. Due to the broad configurational
space for the amines studied, the Confab utility (O'Boyle, Vandermeersch, et al.) as implemented
in OpenBabel (O'Boyle, Banck, et al.) was employed to generate a diverse set of input structures.
All resulting structures were optimized using the B3LYP functional (Becke ; C.T. Lee, Yang, and
Parr 1988) and the 6-31G** basis set (Francl et al. 1982; Hehre, Ditchfie.R, and Pople 1972).
Canonical tautomers of the amine and carbamate structures resulting from CO2 absorption were
optimized in the gas phase, while zwitterionic forms were optimized with methanol universal
solvation model (SMDO) solvent (Marenich, Cramer, and Truhlar) to prevent collapse back to the
canonical form. Frequency and solvation energy calculations were calculated using the same
level of theory. Electronic energies were obtained from a large basis set calculation which
employed the same functional and a triple zeta 6-311++G** basis set (Krishnan et al. 1980; Clark
et al. 1983). Transition states were calculated in the same manner, while allowing a single
negative frequency whose normal mode led to the formation of the product. These transition
states were further validated using intrinsic reaction coordinate calculations to ensure the
products and reactants were connected on the potential energy surface via the calculated
transition state.

2.3 Hybrid Model Development
 A Bayesian hybrid modeling approach, inspired by the seminal work of Kennedy and O’
Hagan (Kennedy and O'Hagan 2001), was applied to model the time-varying observable

 3

quantities in the microdroplet ESI-MS system. The hybrid modeling framework combines first-
principles models with data-driven terms calibrated to experimental observations. Such an
approach has been successfully applied in a broad array of applications, including computational
fluid dynamics (Tagade, Jeong, and Choi 2013), chemical kinetics (Sargsyan, Najm, and Ghanem
2015; Bui et al. 2022), building energy (Kim and Park 2016), and pharmacodynamic models
(Isenberg et al. 2024). To the best of our knowledge, the present work represents the first effort
applying Bayesian hybrid modeling to a microdroplet reaction process analyzed by ESI-MS.

Figure 2. Workflow for the training and optimization of a Bayesian hybrid model for microdroplet
reaction processes. Bold-font symbols represent vectors.

 The overall model-training approach is sequential, as seen in Figure 2. First, the physics-
only model containing just the kinetic model with control-dependent (i.e., heteroskedastic) error
is calibrated via Bayesian inference on experimental data. In this notation of Figure 1, yobs(t; ξ)
are the experimentally observable quantities (in this case, the mole fraction of TEPA-CO2
product), ξ ∈ Ξ represent the experimental controls within respective operational limits (here,
we take ESI voltage V and solution flow rate F as the independent controls), ysim(t; θ) is the
simulated observable from a first-principles model, θ represent the uncertain parameters of the
first-principles model (the kinetic rate constants of reactions k1 and k2), and ϵ ∼ N(0, σ(ξ; s) is the
observational heteroskedastic error with calibration parameters, s. In this first-step calibration,
DFT estimations for reaction rate constants are incorporated as prior chemical/physical
information. The posterior estimates for the kinetic rate constants and parameters of the
heteroskedastic error model are then fixed at their expected values in the maximum-likelihood
estimation of a Gaussian process discrepancy function trained to model residual error, rij, at each
time, i, given the experimental control setting, j. Once trained, this hybrid model for TEPA-CO2 is
used in multi-objective optimization to identify the optimal control setting, ξ∗, which maximizes the
signal-to-noise ratio and chemical reactivity (product yield). Given the optimal control, new
experimental data are collected to simultaneously improve the model and the experimental
outcomes (reaction turnover).

 4

 The sequential hybrid model training approach used here is similar to that presented by
Eugene et al. (Eugene, Gao, and Dowling 2020). The model calibration process is conducted
sequentially to decouple the identification of the physical rate constants from the identification
parameters of the data-driven model. It is expected that the initial calibration of the physics-only
model will improve the identifiability of physical parameters, k1 and k2, by removing compensatory
effects caused by the presence of the discrepancy function. Our approach differs as we assume
a parametric heteroskedastic error likelihood for simplicity. Additionally, we adapt and apply this
Bayesian hybrid methodology in a control setting to a real-world, operational experiment with
noisy measurements and limited data as opposed to well-behaved artificial simulated
experiments. Ultimately, the hybrid modeling approach leads to additional insights regarding the
nature of microdroplet reactions and the relationship between ESI parameters and resulting
reaction turnover rate. Details on the derivation of the physical and hybrid models are provided in
the following subsections.

 The amine system model reaction considered in this work is a solution of TEPA with the
chemical formula of (NH2CH2CH2NHCH2CH2)2NH. This amine has three secondary and two

this reaction network, we derive the complete dynamic model for the droplet system in Appendix

Beauchamp 2010). To model the system described in Section 2.3, we utilize this approach to
capture both evaporation and reaction dynamics in multi-component charged droplets generated
by ESI. This model provides a mechanistic framework for the size, composition, and charge
evolution of charged droplets in flight between the electrospray emitter and mass spectrometer
inlet while under an applied electric field. This system of coupled nonlinear ODEs jointly models
the evaporation dynamics, compositional evolution, and reaction kinetics of charged
microdroplets. This model represents the kinetics of an ionized droplet under reaction conditions,
tracking the rate of change of droplet radius r, mole fractions xi of reactants and products, and
surface temperature, Tsurf, over time. System-specific chemical data, such as the reaction rate
constants and heat of reaction, are computed using DFT. The ODE model provides simulation
data ysim(t; θ ) and can be easily integrated and calibrated in the Bayesian framework. In the
derivation of the dynamic simulation model, several approximations and simplifications were
made. First, the kinetic model used in this work assumes that the TEPA with CO2 reaction occurs
homogeneously in the bulk droplet even though previous studies suggest that interfacial effects
and surface-limited reactions sometimes dominate in microdroplets. Second, in the present study,
it is assumed that there are no Rayleigh discharge events leading to the fission of larger droplets
into smaller progeny droplets during the simulation time horizon. Additionally, constant, though
uncertain, forward and reverse rate constants k1 and k2 are assumed, although it has been shown
that the reaction rates in ionized droplets are dependent on conditions such as electrospray

 5

voltage and solution flow rate. Finally, because the CO2 reactant is present in excess, we treat
the reaction as pseudo-first order with respect to the concentration of TEPA.

2.3.2 Hybrid Model

 The first-principles kinetic model described in Section 2.3.1 does not have the ability to
capture the relationship between the experimental controls and the predicted outputs. It is well
known that experimental parameters, i.e., V and F, etc., impact the rate of reaction for reagents
in charged droplets (Rovelli et al. 2020). However, an exact empirical relationship between these
control variables and the rate of reaction has yet to be derived and is likely to be highly system-
dependent. Therefore, the kinetic model in Equations 12 – 24 does not fully represent the
underlying ESI process for an evaporating droplet. This discrepancy leads to disagreement
between the observed and simulated mole fractions of the TEPA-CO2 product under different
experimental controls. To account for these discrepancies, we utilize a Bayesian hybrid model
structure as proposed in Kennedy and O ’ Hagan (Kennedy and O'Hagan 2001). Here, the
uncertain parameters of the physics model with parametric heteroskedastic probability are first
calibrated to obtain informative prior estimates. Then, a time-invariant data-driven discrepancy
function is trained over the control variables on the residual error between the posterior physics
model and the observations. Here, the control variables are the solution flow rate F ∈ R+ and
electrospray voltage V ∈ R+. The form of the hybrid model for predicting observables

2.3.3 Multi-Objective Optimization

 In practical operation of the ESI-MS experiment, it is desirable to select operating
conditions that both maximize the CO2 uptake by the amine molecules in the droplets, measured
by the mole fraction of TEPA-CO2 product, while also maximizing the signal-to-noise ratio of the
measured data. However, there is a known trade-off between these two objectives for the present
application. Specifically, the conditions most conducive to large reaction rates in droplets also
result in low signal-to-noise ratios. Therefore, to identify the optimal operating conditions for
droplet reactions analyzed by ESI-MS, both objectives must be optimized simultaneously. This
problem can thus be cast as a multi-objective optimization problem with two objective functions:
maximizing the final mole fraction of the TEPA-CO2 product at the final time point of the
experiment, tf , as predicted by the posterior hybrid model in Section 3.3, and minimizing the
standard deviation of the experimental noise, σ(F, V), as defined in Equation 3. Given equal
weighting of the two objectives, the optimization problem can be specified as in Equation 4.

other solution is strictly better in every possible way. This means that the Pareto optimal solution
cannot be improved along one objective without sacrificing performance in another. The Pareto
set is defined as the set of decision variable values where no other decision dominates. And the
Pareto front is the set of objective values corresponding to the Pareto set.

3.0 Results and Discussion
3.1 ESI-MS Characterization of Reactions in Microdroplets

Figure 3. Representative mass spectra with (a) and without (b) the presence of CO2 and the
identification of the reaction products.

 Figure 3 exhibits the general pattern of mass spectra obtained in positive ion mode from
the reaction of TEPA in microdroplets with gaseous CO2. Spectra obtained with and without the
presence of CO2 are both dominated by a peak with an m/z value of 190, attributed to the
protonated TEPA ion (denoted [TEPA]+ here), representing the unreacted TEPA in this reaction.
With the presence of CO2, though some changes in relative intensities of peaks in the m/z range
of 210 – 220 were seen, the major difference was the appearance of a new peak with an m/z of
234, indicating the addition of one CO2 molecule to the protonated TEPA. This species was thus
easily identified as the reaction product, [TEPA-CO2]+.

 For each parameter setting, we took a one-minute data acquisition to minimize system
errors. The mole fraction of each component was then calculated based on the averaged intensity
of selected species normalized against the total ion intensity. This approach ensured accurate
representation of the reaction turnover by accounting for overall changes in ion intensity resulting
from different experimental conditions. In addition, distance scans were carried out at each
parameter setting (V, F). According to previous studies, the droplets generated through ESI travel
roughly at a constant velocity of 100 m/s, and the reaction only occurs during the travel from the

 9

emitter capillary tip to the mass spectrometer inlet while immediately quenching upon entering
the instrument (Huang, Wei, and Cooks 2021). Through such distance scans using a calibrated
micrometer, we thereby recorded mole fractions representing reaction turnover rates at specific
reaction times up to ~30 μs.

Student-t prior values with three degrees of freedom and a wide scale parameter of σ = 5. This
incorporates our theoretical DFT estimates while allowing heavy-tailed support across many
orders of magnitude. The broad prior values allow the experimentally observed data to dominate
if the bulk solution DFT kinetic rate values are inconsistent with the dynamics observed in
microdroplets.

 3.3 Complete Hybrid Model with Informative Priors
 The posterior probability distributions for log k1, log k2, and s obtained in Section 3.2 are
 used as informative priors to train the hybrid model in Equation 1–2 with a known heteroskedastic
 error defined in Equation 3. The posterior distribution means, estimated in Section 3.2, are taken
 as the values for k1, k2, s0, . . . , s5 in the training of the hybrid model.

 To quantify the relative improvement in predictive accuracy between the physics-only
 posterior model and trained hybrid model, we compute the average negative log predictive density
 (NLPD) under the control settings for F and V in the test set in Table 1. The NLPD is used to
 evaluate the predictive performance of a probabilistic model. It quantifies how well the posterior
 (i.e., trained) model predicts on the unseen test data while accounting for both the accuracy of
 the mean predictions and the uncertainty of the predictions (i.e., the predictive distribution). In
 general, the more negative the value of average NLPD, the better the accuracy and generalization
 of the model. For the physics-only posterior model, the NLPD is -1.62 on the training data and
 -1.40 on the testing data. For the hybrid posterior model, the NLPD is -1.72 on the training data
 and -1.76 on the testing data. The fact that the NLPD on the training and testing data are similar
 in magnitude in both cases indicates that there is likely minimal overfitting. The lower NLPD in the
 hybrid model compared to the physics-only model may be understood by the better posterior
 mean fits and narrower confidence intervals, as seen in all (F,V)-pair test instances in the posterior
 predictive plots in Figure 5. This result shows that the full hybrid model outperforms the physics-
 only version in prediction accuracy and confidence, in part due to the informative prior information
 provided by the physics-only model, and also due to the added flexibility imparted by the addition
 of the discrepancy function.

4.0 Conclusions and Outlook
 We developed a sequential hybrid Bayesian modeling approach for an automated ESI-
MS microdroplet reactor platform with an amine-based solvent undergoing reaction with carbon
dioxide. The hybrid model incorporates a novel physics/chemistry-informed model formulation for
the reaction and mass transfer kinetics, DFT kinetic rate constants, and real experimental
measurements of relative reactant and product concentrations. Heteroskedastic error is handled
via a simple parametric model, while residuals between the physical model and the experimental
data are captured by a flexible Gaussian process model. The first step of the model training is to
use Bayesian inference to calibrate the physics model to find values for the kinetic rate constants
and heteroskedastic error consistent with the observed data, given the kinetic model. Using these
posterior estimates for key parameters and residuals, the next step of the model training involves
solving a maximum likelihood estimation on Gaussian process hyperparameters. The final hybrid
model is used in a multi-objective optimization to find control settings for ESI flow rate and voltage,
which simultaneously maximize TEPA-CO2 production and minimize the standard deviation of the
heteroskedastic observational noise. These two objectives are in direct conflict with each other,
as increasing TEPA-CO2 leads to noisy measurements in practice due to the limitations of the
instrument, such as ionization efficiency and detector sensitivity. Therefore, we identify a Pareto
optimal solution wherein the competing objectives are balanced. Overall, this demonstrates how
a Bayesian hybrid modeling approach advances goals in microdroplet chemistry. The platform
and modeling approach developed in this project constitute a novel capability for understanding
and controlling the acceleration of reactions in confined droplet environments relevant to chemical
separations and catalysis.
