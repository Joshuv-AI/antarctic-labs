Comparison of proton vs. photon radiotherapy for liver cancer: a meta-analysis
Authors: Hongjie He, Yuanyuan Yan, Yanming Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo systematically evaluate the efficacy and safety of proton beam radiotherapy (PBT) compared with photon radiotherapy in the treatment of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), thereby providing evidence-based guidance for clinical decision-making in radiotherapy modality selection.MethodsA systematic search of PubMed, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, and EMBASE databases was conducted to identify clinical studies comparing PBT with photon radiotherapy for HCC. Study quality was assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS), and the certainty of evidence was evaluated using the GRADE system. Meta-analysis was performed using Review Manager 5.4. The primary outcomes assessed were overall survival (OS), radiation-induced liver injury (RILD), and non-classical RILD.ResultsFour retrospective cohort studies were included. Meta-analysis results indicated that the PBT group had a hazard ratio for OS of 0.45 (95% CI: 0.34–0.60, P < 0.00001). However, moderate heterogeneity was observed for this outcome (I² = 48%), accompanied by significant publication bias, resulting in a low certainty of evidence. Conversely, PBT significantly reduced the risk of RILD (OR = 0.26, 95% CI: 0.15–0.46, P < 0.00001) and non-classical RILD (OR = 0.27, 95% CI: 0.15–0.47, P < 0.00001). Both outcomes demonstrated no heterogeneity (I² = 0%) and no significant publication bias, yielding a moderate certainty of evidence. The number needed to treat to prevent one case of liver injury was approximately five.ConclusionCompared with photon radiotherapy, PBT offers a clear advantage in reducing the risk of RILD and non-classical RILD in patients with HCC. However, the potential benefit of PBT in improving OS remains inconclusive due to limitations related to study design and publication bias, resulting in a low level of evidence. Clinical selection of radiotherapy modalities should be individualized based on patients’ liver function reserve, tumor characteristics, and medical conditions. Large-scale, multicenter prospective studies are warranted to further validate the long-term survival benefits of PBT.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12876-026-05050-7.

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is one of the most prevalent malignancies globally, with its incidence and mortality rates ranking among the highest for all cancers [1, 2]. For patients with unresectable, locally advanced, or recurrent HCC, radiotherapy serves as a crucial local treatment modality [3, 4]. Conventional photon radiotherapy, utilizing techniques such as intensity-modulated radiation therapy (IMRT), volumetric modulated arc therapy (VMAT), and stereotactic body radiation therapy (SBRT), enables precise dose delivery. However, due to the physical characteristics of photon beams, radiation-induced damage to the surrounding normal liver tissue is inevitable [5–7]. Adverse events such as radiation-induced liver injury (RILD) not only limit the escalation of the radiation dose but may also compromise patient tolerance and quality of life, representing a core bottleneck in the application of photon radiotherapy for HCC [8, 9]. Proton beam radiotherapy (PBT), leveraging the unique Bragg peak physical property, achieves high-dose coverage of the tumor target while significantly reducing the radiation exposure to the normal liver and adjacent organs-at-risk. Theoretically, this offers advantages in enhancing treatment efficacy and reducing radiotherapy-related adverse events. Consequently, PBT has been progressively integrated into the clinical management of HCC in recent years [10–12].

Numerous clinical studies have compared the efficacy and safety of PBT versus photon radiotherapy for HCC; however, the existing evidence predominantly comprises single-center, small-sample retrospective cohort studies. A systematic review and meta-analysis by Qi et al. in 2015 evaluated the clinical value of charged-particle therapy compared with photon therapy for HCC [13]. That study aggregated data from 70 non-comparative, single-arm observational studies, combining proton and carbon ion radiotherapy under the umbrella of charged-particle therapy for the overall analysis. The findings indicated that charged-particle therapy significantly improved 1-, 3-, and 5-year overall survival rates and demonstrated superior outcomes in reducing high-grade acute and late radiation toxicities compared with conventional photon radiotherapy. No significant difference in efficacy was observed compared with photon SBRT. This research established a crucial evidence base for subsequent clinical investigation of particle therapy in HCC. However, that analysis combined proton and carbon ion radiotherapy, did not isolate the clinical effect of PBT alone, and exclusively included non-comparative single-arm studies. More recently, Bae et al. (2024) published a systematic review and meta-analysis on PBT for HCC, pooling data from 22 studies (mostly single-arm) and reporting favorable local control and low toxicity. However, that study did not directly compare PBT with photon radiotherapy [11].

A significant gap remains regarding the absence of large-sample, high-quality systematic syntheses on whether PBT can significantly improve overall survival (OS) or reduce the incidence of RILD in HCC patients. In this context, the present study aims to conduct a systematic review and meta-analysis, comprehensively integrating available clinical data to evaluate the clinical value of PBT compared with photon radiotherapy for HCC. The findings are intended to supplement the evidence base in the field of HCC radiotherapy from an evidence-based medicine perspective and provide a reference for clinicians formulating individualized radiotherapy plans for patients with HCC.

Research objectives and questions

This study aims to systematically search for and synthesize data from clinical studies comparing PBT and photon radiotherapy for HCC. Through meta-analysis, it seeks to quantitatively evaluate the efficacy and safety of these two radiotherapy modalities, thereby providing evidence-based guidance for the clinical selection of radiotherapy regimens for HCC.

Methods

Literature screening and data extraction

Eligibility criteria

This review strictly adhered to the PICOS framework to define the inclusion and exclusion criteria and to establish rules for study categorization, as detailed below:

(1) Inclusion Criteria

Study Type: Published randomized controlled trials (RCTs), prospective or retrospective cohort studies comparing PBT with photon radiotherapy for the treatment of primary liver cancer.

Population: Patients diagnosed with HCC via histopathology or radiological imaging, irrespective of tumor stage, liver function grade, or prior treatment history.

Intervention: The experimental group received PBT, while the control group received any form of photon radiotherapy.

Outcomes: At least one of the following core outcomes was reported: OS, RILD, or other treatment-related adverse events.

Language and Publication Status: Peer-reviewed literature published in the English language, containing complete and extractable study data.

(2) Exclusion Criteria

Studies not focused on HCC.

Study types including case-control studies, cross-sectional studies, case reports, reviews, expert consensus, animal experiments, or in vitro cell studies.

Duplicate publications.

Studies from which valid data could not be extracted or which had incomplete data.

Non-English language publications and unpublished grey literature.

(3) Study Categorization

Studies meeting the inclusion criteria were categorized according to study design (RCT/cohort study), intervention details, sample size, and type of outcome measure to facilitate subsequent quantitative synthesis (meta-analysis) or qualitative descriptive analysis.

Information sources

To identify eligible clinical studies, a computerized systematic search was conducted across four core English-language databases. The final search across all databases was performed on March 12, 2026. Two reviewers independently performed the search, and the results were cross-checked; disagreements were resolved through discussion. The specific databases searched included PubMed, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, and EMBASE. Additionally, following the initial literature screening, the reference lists of included studies and relevant systematic reviews/meta-analyses were manually screened to supplement the search and identify any potentially eligible studies that might have been missed. No restrictions on language or publication type were imposed during the search process; literature selection was based solely on the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria to minimize the risk of publication bias and literature omission.

Search strategy

Two reviewers independently executed the searches in all databases. The final search date was uniformly set as March 12, 2026, with the language restricted to English. The search strategy combined the following Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms and keywords: hepatocellular carcinoma, HCC, primary liver cancer, proton beam therapy, PBT, proton radiotherapy, photon radiotherapy, intensity-modulated radiotherapy, IMRT, volumetric modulated arc therapy, VMAT, stereotactic body radiation therapy, SBRT, three-dimensional conformal radiotherapy, 3D-CRT, overall survival, OS, progression-free survival, PFS, radiation-induced liver disease, RILD, adverse event.

Detailed search strategies, including the specific search strings and field codes for each database, are provided in Table S1. Furthermore, we manually searched the reference lists of included studies and relevant review articles to identify any potentially eligible studies missed by the electronic database searches. This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [14]. The meta-analysis protocol was registered with the Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) and adhered strictly to the PRISMA-P guidelines.

Study selection process

Two reviewers, both trained in systematic review methodology, independently and blindly performed the study selection, applying the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria in a stepwise manner. Initially, all records retrieved from the database searches were imported into EndNote 21 software for duplicate removal, yielding a set of unique records. Both reviewers independently screened the titles and abstracts of all these unique records, rapidly excluding those clearly irrelevant based on study type, population, or intervention. Records with insufficient information for immediate judgment were retained for full-text review. Following the initial screening, the two reviewers compared their results; discrepancies were resolved by consensus through discussion and re-examination of the records. If consensus could not be reached, a third senior researcher with extensive experience in HCC ablation research made the final decision. Subsequently, both reviewers independently obtained the full texts of all potentially relevant records and re-evaluated them against the inclusion/exclusion criteria to definitively determine eligibility. Disagreements at this stage were resolved using the same process as in the initial screening. The automated tool used during selection was EndNote 21, primarily for aggregating search results and identifying/removing duplicate records to reduce manual workload.

Data collection process

Two reviewers trained in methodology independently extracted data from the full texts of all included studies. Both reviewers extracted data from every study in its entirety, without task division or splitting. Data collected encompassed basic study information, baseline characteristics, intervention details, outcome measures, and methodological quality items. EndNote 21 was used to manage the included studies, facilitating full-text access. A standardized data extraction sheet was created in Microsoft Excel for unified data entry and storage. Upon completion of extraction by both reviewers, the results were cross-checked. Discrepancies in extracted data were resolved by jointly consulting the original article; if an agreement could not be reached, a third senior researcher adjudicated. For studies with missing critical data, a designated reviewer attempted to contact the corresponding author via email for verification, allowing a reasonable waiting period for response. If no feedback was received, the data point was marked as missing, and no imputation was performed.

Data items

This review collected data on three predefined core outcome measures. All available measurements and corresponding follow-up time point data reported for these three outcomes within each study were extracted comprehensively, without selective reporting.

OS: Defined as the time from initiation of radiotherapy to death from any cause or the last follow-up. The reported hazard ratio (HR) with its 95% confidence interval (CI), or survival data at specific time points, were extracted.

RILD: Defined as liver-specific toxicity occurring after radiotherapy. The number of events and the incidence rate were extracted.

Non-classical RILD: Defined as liver injury characterized by persistent abnormal liver function tests after radiotherapy, in the absence of ascites or hepatic encephalopathy. The number of events and the incidence rate were extracted.

Simultaneously, study-related variables were collected, including the first author’s name, publication year, study design, and the number of patients in each group. Patient baseline characteristics collected included age, sex, Child-Pugh grade, number of tumor lesions, maximum tumor diameter, and whether the patient was treatment-naïve. Intervention-related information collected included the specific radiotherapy technique used (proton/photon), total radiation dose and fractionation schedule, and dose constraints applied to the normal liver. Follow-up duration and completeness of follow-up were also recorded.

Bias and evidence quality assessment

Risk of bias in individual studies

The Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) was used to assess the methodological quality of the included cohort studies. This scale comprises nine evaluation items across three core domains, assessing the following: (a) Representativeness of the exposed cohort. (b) Selection of the non-exposed cohort. (c) Ascertainment of exposure. (d) Demonstration that the outcome of interest was not present at the start of the study. (e) Comparability of cohorts based on the study design or analysis (control for key confounding factors). (f) Assessment of outcome. (g) Was follow-up long enough for outcomes to occur? (h) Adequacy of follow-up of cohorts. The total score was the sum of scores for each item, with higher scores indicating higher study quality. The maximum total score is 9. Subsequently, studies were categorized into risk of bias levels based on their total score, where a higher score suggests better methodological quality and a lower risk of bias.

After the assessment, results were cross-checked. For items with inconsistent ratings, the reviewers resolved the issue by jointly reviewing the original article and discussing it. If discrepancies persisted, a third senior researcher made the final decision. EndNote 21 was used to manage the literature during the assessment process for easy access to full texts. A standardized evaluation form was created in Microsoft Excel to record the judgment and rationale for each bias item. No other automated assessment tools were used.

Reporting bias

This study assessed the risk of publication bias and outcome reporting bias using multiple methods. For each core outcome, funnel plots were generated for qualitative visual assessment, examining the symmetry of the distribution of effect estimates to preliminarily judge potential bias from unpublished negative results. Additionally, Egger’s linear regression test was performed as a quantitative analysis to test the statistical significance of funnel plot asymmetry, further evaluating the potential presence of publication bias.

Certainty of evidence

The internationally accepted GRADE (Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluations) system was used to assess the certainty of evidence for each outcome. Two reviewers, both trained in systematic review methodology, independently rated the evidence level for all core outcomes. Following the rating, the results were cross-checked. Disagreements were resolved through discussion and re-examination of the original articles; if consensus could not be reached, a third senior researcher made the final decision.

Effect measures and synthesis methods

Effect measures

A hierarchical approach to effect measures was adopted based on the outcome type, encompassing both relative and absolute effect metrics:

For the time-to-event outcome OS, the HR with its 95% CI was the primary effect measure, reflecting the difference in survival risk over time between the two groups. If a study did not directly report the HR, data were extracted from survival curves, and the HR was estimated using the method described by Tierney et al.

For dichotomous outcomes such as RILD and non-classical RILD, the odds ratio (OR), relative risk (RR), risk difference (RD), and number needed to treat (NNT) were presented. OR reflects the difference in the odds of an event, is suitable for retrospective studies, and can approximate RR when event rates are low. RR directly represents the ratio of event rates between groups and is more interpretable for prospective studies. RD represents the absolute difference in event rates between groups; the NNT (calculated as 1/RD) derived from RD provides an assessment of the clinical benefit of the intervention, indicating the number of patients needed to treat to prevent one adverse outcome. A smaller NNT suggests a more substantial clinical benefit. Priority was given to extracting OR/RR directly reported in the studies. If only raw event counts were provided, the corresponding relative effect measures were calculated first, and RD and NNT were subsequently calculated to comprehensively present both the relative effect and the absolute clinical benefit of the intervention.

Statistical analysis

First, studies were selected based on the predefined PICOS criteria. Subsequently, the intervention characteristics (e.g., radiotherapy technique, dose regimen), outcome definitions, data completeness of each included study were compared against the pre-specified synthesis groups (PBT vs. photon radiotherapy). Only studies where the interventions and outcome definitions were highly consistent with the synthesis objectives and from which data could be completely extracted were included in the quantitative synthesis. If substantial heterogeneity in interventions or outcomes was present across studies, only a qualitative description was performed.

For missing or incomplete summary statistics, we first attempted to contact the original authors to obtain supplementary data. If unsuccessful, studies were included in the qualitative analysis only if the missing data constituted < 10% of the relevant information and their absence was not deemed to affect the core conclusions; otherwise, they were excluded from the quantitative synthesis. For continuous variables reported only as medians and interquartile ranges, the method by Hozo et al. was used to convert them to means and standard deviations for meta-analysis suitability. For different effect measure types (e.g., RR and OR), approximate conversions were performed under the assumption of a low event rate (< 10%) to ensure uniformity of effect measures across studies.

A summary table was prepared to present individual study and synthesized results, including study characteristics, effect estimates with 95% CIs, and study weights. For visualization, forest plots were generated to display individual study effects and the pooled estimate, and funnel plots combined with Egger’s test were used to assess publication bias.

Statistical heterogeneity was identified and quantified using the Q test and I² statistic, guiding the selection of the meta-analysis model:

When the Q test P-value was ≥ 0.10 and I² <50%, indicating low heterogeneity among studies, a fixed-effects model (Inverse Variance method) was used to pool effect estimates.

When the Q test P-value was < 0.10 and I² ≥50%, indicating moderate-to-high heterogeneity, sources of heterogeneity were explored. The subsequent synthesis approach was determined based on the findings of this exploration.

All quantitative syntheses and basic heterogeneity testing were performed using Review Manager 5.4. Stata 17.0 was used for subsequent in-depth heterogeneity analyses and publication bias assessments.

In cases where moderate-to-high heterogeneity (I² ≥50%) was encountered, subgroup analyses and meta-regression were planned to explore potential sources:

Subgroup Analysis: Studies were grouped according to factors such as study design, specific radiotherapy technique, patient baseline characteristics, and median follow-up duration. Effect estimates were compared across subgroups to identify key sources of heterogeneity.

Meta-regression: Continuous variables such as sample size were included as covariates in meta-regression models to quantitatively assess their influence on the effect estimate and their contribution to heterogeneity.

To evaluate the robustness of the pooled results, sensitivity analyses were performed:

Repeating the meta-analysis after omitting one study at a time to observe the stability of the results.

Re-analyzing the data after excluding studies assessed as having low methodological quality.

For outcomes where publication bias was suspected, the trim-and-fill method was applied to adjust the pooled estimate and assess the potential impact of such bias.

Results

Study selection results

The literature screening process, conducted in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 statement, is illustrated in Fig. 1. The initial search yielded 6,506 records. After removing duplicates, the remaining records underwent title and abstract screening. Full texts were retrieved for 641 records for detailed evaluation. Following full-text assessment against the eligibility criteria, studies were excluded for reasons such as ineligible study design or study population. Ultimately, 4 studies met all inclusion criteria and were included in the review.

Characteristics of included studies

A total of four retrospective studies comparing proton beam therapy (PBT) and photon radiotherapy for hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) were included in this meta-analysis. This cohort comprised two multicenter and two single-center studies. Variations in sample sizes and follow-up durations were observed across the study groups. The enrolled patients were predominantly male and presented with Child-Pugh class A liver function. Regarding tumor characteristics, the study groups were generally comparable in terms of disease stage, tumor size, tumor number, and the presence of macrovascular invasion. The tumors were predominantly intermediate to advanced stage and medium to large in size, with a higher proportion exhibiting negative macrovascular invasion. In terms of treatment planning, the PBT groups frequently employed individualized dose-fractionation schedules based on tumor anatomy, resulting in generally higher biologically effective doses. Conversely, the photon radiotherapy groups primarily utilized intensity-modulated radiotherapy (IMRT) and volumetric modulated arc therapy (VMAT), with some cases incorporating stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT). Photon doses were individually tailored according to tumor characteristics and normal liver tissue constraints. Consequently, distinct technical approaches and dose planning strategies were evident between the two modalities (Tables 1, 2 and 3).

Results of risk of bias in individual studies

The risk of bias for the four included retrospective cohort studies was evaluated using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS). The total possible score ranges from 0 to 9, with scores of 7–9 defined as high-quality. The assessment revealed that all included studies were of high methodological quality (Table 4). The studies consistently performed well across core domains, including the appropriateness of the non-exposed cohort selection, the objectivity of exposure ascertainment, the absence of the outcome at baseline, control for key confounders, the reliability of outcome assessment, and adequacy of follow-up. Minor discrepancies were observed only in terms of cohort representativeness, adjustment for additional confounders, or the duration of follow-up. Overall, the included studies demonstrated a low risk of bias and high quality, thereby providing a robust evidence base for the subsequent meta-analysis.

Results of individual studies and synthesis

Overall survival (OS)

A total of four studies were included in the analysis of OS, employing a fixed-effects model for data synthesis. Each individual study demonstrated that the PBT group had a reduced risk of death compared to the photon radiotherapy group, with hazard ratios (HRs) ranging from 0.16 to 0.56, and their 95% confidence intervals (CIs) excluding the null value of 1 (Fig. 2). The pooled effect size indicated that PBT significantly improved OS in patients with HCC (pooled HR = 0.45; 95% CI: 0.34, 0.60; overall effect Z = 5.47, P < 0.00001). Heterogeneity testing suggested moderate heterogeneity among the studies (χ²=5.74, df = 3, P = 0.12, I²=48%), which was not statistically significant. The HRs for all individual studies and the pooled estimate were consistently less than 1, uniformly indicating that PBT significantly reduces the risk of death compared to photon radiotherapy, with a stable and definitive direction of effect. Sensitivity analysis, performed by sequentially omitting each study, showed that the direction of the pooled HR remained unchanged, consistently supporting the survival benefit of PBT. Notably, the heterogeneity decreased substantially to I²=0% after excluding Hsieh et al. (2024), suggesting this study was the primary source of heterogeneity. The overall results were therefore robust and reliable [17]. As a sensitivity analysis, a random-effects model was also applied, yielding a pooled HR of 0.42 (95% CI: 0.28–0.64; P < 0.001; Tau²=0.08). The direction and significance of the effect remained unchanged, supporting the robustness of the findings.

Due to the limited number of included studies (n = 4), the planned multivariate meta-regression and subgroup analyses—including those by BCLC stage, MVI status, ALBI grade, photon modality (SBRT/IMRT/VMAT), PBT dose level, and tumor size—could not be performed. Meta-regression with fewer than ten studies carries a high risk of unreliable results, including substantially inflated false-positive rates when heterogeneity is present and a high risk of model overfitting when the number of studies per covariate is low [19]. None of the studies adjusted for microvascular invasion (MVI), satellite nodules, or tumor capsule integrity. Only two studies, Cheng et al. (2020) and Sanford et al. (2019) reported and adjusted for ALBI grade. BCLC stage subgroups were not used in any study (one study used AJCC stage instead) [15, 18]. The absence of adjustment for these established prognostic factors represents a significant source of potential bias for the OS outcome.

Radiation-induced liver disease (RILD)

A fixed-effects model was employed to synthesize the data on RILD. Each individual study demonstrated that the PBT group significantly reduced the risk of RILD compared to the photon radiotherapy group, with odds ratios (ORs) ranging from 0.22 to 0.31 and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) excluding the null value of 1 (Fig. 3). The pooled effect size indicated that PBT significantly reduced the risk of RILD, with a summary OR of 0.26 (95% CI: 0.15, 0.46) and an overall effect test showing Z = 4.68 (P < 0.00001), a highly statistically significant difference. The heterogeneity test suggested no heterogeneity among the studies (χ²=0.27, df = 2, P = 0.88, I²=0%). Sensitivity analysis, performed by sequentially omitting each individual study, revealed that the direction of the pooled effect remained unchanged, consistently supporting that PBT reduces the risk of RILD. Further pooled analyses using risk ratio (RR) and risk difference (RD) yielded results of RR = 0.34 (95% CI: 0.21, 0.54) and RD=-0.21 (95% CI: -0.30, -0.13), respectively, consistent with the direction of the pooled OR. The corresponding number needed to treat (NNT) was approximately 5, suggesting that for every five patients with hepatocellular carcinoma treated, using PBT instead of photon radiotherapy could prevent one additional case of RILD.

Non-classical RILD

A fixed-effects model was employed to synthesize the data for non-classical RILD. Each individual study demonstrated that the PBT group significantly reduced the risk of non-classical RILD compared to the photon radiotherapy group, with odds ratios (ORs) ranging from 0.22 to 0.33, and all 95% confidence intervals (CIs) excluding the null value of 1 (Fig. 4). The pooled effect size indicated that PBT significantly lowered the incidence of non-classical RILD (summary OR = 0.27, 95% CI: 0.15 to 0.47), with an overall effect test showing a highly statistically significant difference (Z = 4.51, P < 0.00001). Heterogeneity testing revealed no evidence of heterogeneity among the included studies (χ²=0.42, df = 2, P = 0.81, I²=0%). Sensitivity analysis, performed by sequentially omitting each study, demonstrated that the direction of the pooled effect remained unchanged, consistently favoring PBT for reducing the risk of non-classical RILD. Furthermore, pooled risk ratios (RR) and risk differences (RD) yielded results consistent with the OR analysis: RR = 0.34 (95% CI: 0.21 to 0.56) and RD=-0.20 (95% CI: -0.28 to -0.12). Consequently, the corresponding number needed to treat (NNT) was approximately 5, suggesting that for every five patients treated for liver cancer, replacing photon radiotherapy with PBT could prevent one additional case of non-classical RILD.

Other clinical outcomes

PFS was reported only in Hsieh et al. (2024), with a median PFS of 7.4 months in the proton group versus 3.3 months in the photon group (HR = 0.569, 95% CI: 0.406–0.797, P = 0.001) [17]. Local control rate (LCR) was reported in two studies: Hsieh et al. (2024) reported 2-year LCR of 88.5% for protons versus 33.8% for photons [17]; Sanford et al. (2019) reported 2-year local control of 93% for protons versus 90% for photons, with no significant difference (HR = 0.74, P = 0.67) [18]. Due to the limited number of studies and missing hazard ratio data, formal meta-analysis could not be performed for these outcomes. Disease-free survival (DFS), late toxicity (grade ≥ 3 occurring > 6 months post-treatment), quality of life (QoL), and hospital stay were not reported in any of the included studies.

Results of reporting bias

The funnel plot for OS, with SE(log[HR]) plotted against HR, showed that most effect sizes clustered in the region of HR < 1. However, the distribution of studies was asymmetrical, with small-sample studies leaning towards the left side, indicating potential small-study effects or publication bias (Fig. 5). Consistently, Egger’s regression test revealed a significant small-study effect (beta1 = -5.42, SE = 2.385, z = -2.27, P = 0.0230), leading to the rejection of the null hypothesis of no small-study effects. This finding suggests that the pooled OS effect size may be overestimated, potentially due to publication bias favoring positive findings from smaller studies, a limitation that warrants emphasis in the review. Sensitivity analyses indicated that the direction of the pooled HR remained unchanged after sequentially omitting individual studies. Notably, heterogeneity substantially decreased to I² = 0% upon exclusion of the Hsieh 2024 study, identifying it as the primary source of heterogeneity.

For RILD, the funnel plot demonstrated a generally symmetric distribution of study points around the region of OR < 1, with all studies falling within the 95% confidence limits, suggesting no obvious asymmetry or publication bias. Furthermore, Egger’s test did not reject the null hypothesis of no small-study effects (beta1 = -2.09, SE = 4.137, z = -0.50, P = 0.6139), indicating no significant small-study effects or publication bias. However, it is important to note that this analysis included only three studies; consequently, the statistical power of the publication bias test was limited, warranting caution in result interpretation. Based on the available evidence, no significant bias signal was detected.

Regarding non-classical RILD, the funnel plot showed a relatively symmetric distribution of effect sizes from the three included studies. Correspondingly, Egger’s test indicated no significant small-study effects (beta1 = -2.94, SE = 4.741, z = -0.62, P = 0.5359), failing to reject the null hypothesis. This suggests an absence of significant small-study effects or publication bias. Nevertheless, this analysis was based on only three studies, which limits the statistical power of the test and necessitates cautious interpretation of the findings.

However, due to the small number of studies (n≤4), these tests have limited statistical power, thus, the results should be interpreted with caution.

Results of certainty of evidence

In this study, the GRADE approach was employed to assess the certainty of evidence for core outcomes comparing proton beam therapy (PBT) with photon radiotherapy in the treatment of hepatocellular carcinoma. As all included studies were retrospective cohort designs, the initial evidence level was set as moderate. The assessment indicated that the certainty of evidence for overall survival (OS) was ultimately downgraded to low due to moderate heterogeneity (I² = 48%) and significant publication bias (Egger’s test, P < 0.05). This suggests that the conclusion that PBT improves OS in patients with hepatocellular carcinoma has limited credibility, and the effect size may be overestimated, necessitating further validation through large-scale, high-quality prospective studies. Conversely, for radiation-induced liver disease (RILD) and Non-classical RILD, no downgrading factors were identified across all domains. Consequently, the certainty of evidence was deemed moderate, indicating that the conclusion that PBT reduces the risk of RILD and Non-classical RILD in these patients is relatively reliable and holds clinical value. Overall, the evidence supporting PBT in reducing radiotherapy-related liver injury is more robust, whereas the evidence base for improving long-term survival requires further strengthening. Therefore, clinical conclusions should be interpreted with caution, considering the level of evidence (Table 5).

Discussion

This systematic review integrated data from four retrospective cohort studies comparing PBT and photon radiotherapy for hepatocellular carcinoma. The meta-analysis revealed that PBT may reduce the risk of mortality in liver cancer patients and significantly decrease the risks of RILD and non-classical RILD. The NNT to prevent one case of liver injury was approximately five. Heterogeneity testing indicated moderate heterogeneity for the OS outcome, while no statistically significant heterogeneity was observed for liver injury-related outcomes. Publication bias assessment and GRADE evaluation suggested a significant small-study effect for OS, resulting in low certainty of evidence. In contrast, no significant publication bias was detected for RILD and non-classical RILD, and the certainty of evidence was moderate. These findings preliminarily suggest potential therapeutic and safety benefits of PBT in liver radiotherapy; however, conclusions regarding survival benefits are constrained by study design and bias, warranting cautious interpretation considering the evidence’s limitations.

The potential benefits of PBT in liver radiotherapy are fundamentally attributable to its physical dosimetric characteristics, which differ from those of photon radiotherapy. Although photon radiotherapy has benefited from technological advancements that optimize dose delivery, the exponential attenuation of photon beams results in continuous radiation exposure to normal liver tissue along the beam path before reaching the tumor target. Consequently, controlling the radiation dose delivered to normal liver tissue remains challenging [20, 21]. In contrast, the Bragg peak characteristic of PBT reduces the mean dose and the volume of normal liver tissue receiving high radiation doses. This advantage was consistently demonstrated across the studies included in this analysis; the PBT groups were frequently able to administer higher biologically effective doses without being overly constrained by normal liver tissue dose limits. This dosimetric optimization not only directly reduces the risk of liver injury but also establishes a foundation for effective tumor control [22, 23]. The trend toward improved survival observed in the PBT group in this study is therefore likely associated with enhanced tumor control from higher doses and improved patient tolerance to treatment due to reduced liver injury.

The trend toward a survival benefit with PBT observed in this study offers some clinical reference value; however, significant limitations in the evidence preclude definitive conclusions. The pooled results demonstrated a consistent direction of effect, with all four included studies suggesting a survival benefit for PBT. However, critical appraisal of the primary studies revealed that none of them adjusted for key prognostic confounders including MVI, satellite nodules, or tumor capsule integrity. Only two studies adjusted for ALBI grade, and none used BCLC stage subgroups (one study used AJCC stage instead). Given the well-established prognostic value of these factors in HCC radiotherapy, the observed OS benefit for PBT may be substantially confounded. Therefore, the OS finding should be interpreted as a potential trend rather than a definitive survival advantage. Nevertheless, the presence of moderate heterogeneity and significant publication bias substantially diminishes the confidence in this conclusion. The moderate heterogeneity in the OS outcome was primarily attributable to the study by Hsieh et al. (2024) [17]. This single-center retrospective analysis featured a larger sample size and longer follow-up in the PBT arm, and its reported hazard ratio was lower than those of other studies. This discrepancy may be associated with differences in patient baseline characteristics or a higher degree of individualization in the radiation dose protocols, reflecting clinical heterogeneity across the included studies. A sensitivity analysis using a random-effects model produced a pooled HR of 0.42 (95% CI: 0.28–0.64), which remained consistent with the primary fixed-effects estimate (HR = 0.45, 95% CI: 0.34–0.60), indicating that the conclusion was not sensitive to the choice of model. Furthermore, asymmetry in the funnel plot and a significant Egger’s test result suggested the presence of publication bias. This indicates that the published literature may be biased towards positive findings, potentially excluding unpublished negative studies and thus overestimating the survival effect of PBT. Moreover, all included studies were retrospective cohort designs. Although each employed propensity score matching to control for key confounders such as age, liver function grade, and tumor stage, inherent selection biases could not be entirely eliminated. The absence of published randomized controlled trials (RCTs) comparing these modalities globally remains a critical factor limiting the strength of evidence for the survival benefit of PBT. Therefore, the current evidence only supports a trend toward survival benefit with PBT for liver cancer patients; confirmation of a true improvement in survival requires validation through higher-quality studies.

Compared to the survival outcomes, the conclusion that PBT significantly reduces the risks of RILD and non-classical RILD is more robust and holds clearer clinical relevance. No statistical heterogeneity was observed for either liver injury outcome, with a high degree of consistency in effect sizes across the included studies. Funnel plot analysis and Egger’s test revealed no significant publication bias, leading to a GRADE assessment of moderate certainty of evidence. Clinically, RILD is the primary dose-limiting toxicity in liver radiotherapy. Although classical RILD has a relatively low incidence, it is associated with high mortality. Non-classical RILD is more common, typically manifesting as persistent abnormalities in liver function tests. While often asymptomatic, it can reduce patient tolerance to treatment, hindering the subsequent administration of comprehensive therapies such as targeted therapy, immunotherapy, or antiviral treatment, thereby indirectly impacting long-term survival [24]. This study quantified the NNT with PBT to prevent liver injury as approximately five. For liver cancer patients with adequate hepatic functional reserve and large tumor burdens requiring high-dose radiotherapy, substituting PBT for photon therapy could effectively reduce the risk of liver injury with a relatively low treatment cost [25]. This advantage is particularly significant given that the patient population in the included studies predominantly consisted of those with intermediate-advanced stage hepatocellular carcinoma and Child-Pugh A liver function. Furthermore, this study reinforces that non-classical RILD, as the most common toxicity in liver radiotherapy, represents a key target for the safety advantage of PBT, suggesting its routine inclusion in the safety assessment framework for liver radiotherapy.

The findings of this study align well with and complement the existing evidence base for liver radiotherapy, while also addressing certain gaps in previous research. By synthesizing data from clinical studies, this meta-analysis provides a quantitative assessment of the benefits of PBT, offering clinicians an evidence-based reference. Furthermore, through GRADE methodology, this study elucidates the differing levels of evidence across outcomes, confirming the reliable safety advantage of PBT while objectively highlighting the limitations in the evidence for its survival benefit. The physical characteristics of PBT underpin its well-supported safety profile, whereas its therapeutic efficacy requires confirmation through additional high-quality research.

Although all included studies were retrospective cohort designs deemed high-quality by NOS scores, inherent characteristics of the primary research impose several limitations that affect the strength and generalizability of the findings. First, inherent design flaws exist. The absence of RCT data means that despite controlling for key confounders like age, liver function grade, and tumor stage via propensity score matching, selection bias and information bias cannot be completely ruled out. Unmeasured confounders, such as nutritional status, subsequent comprehensive treatment regimens, and treatment adherence, may have also potentially influenced the outcomes. Second, sample characteristics present significant limitations. The total sample sizes for both the PBT and photon radiotherapy groups across the included studies were less than 300 patients. Small individual study sample sizes increase the risk of bias in effect estimates. Furthermore, all studies originated from China (Taiwan) and the United States, lacking data from diverse geographical regions. Variations in regional healthcare standards, etiological factors of liver cancer, and clinical practices regarding radiotherapy techniques may limit the generalizability of the results. Third, inconsistencies in follow-up reporting were evident. The median follow-up durations varied considerably across studies, with the shortest being only 4 months. For malignancies like liver cancer, such short follow-up periods may inadequately capture long-term therapeutic effects. One study did not clearly report the follow-up duration, precluding stratified analyses, and differences in follow-up completeness could affect the accuracy of outcome ascertainment. Fourth, heterogeneity in radiotherapy protocols and data reporting exists. Variations in dose-fractionation schedules for PBT and specific techniques used for photon radiotherapy across different studies may dilute or amplify the true effect size. Additionally, secondary criteria for defining RILD were not entirely uniform, and some secondary outcomes were not reported, limiting the scope for in-depth subgroup analyses and data synthesis. Fifth, critical baseline data were missing. Core baseline characteristics, including ALBI grade, alpha-fetoprotein levels, and tumor differentiation grade, were missing to varying extents in the included studies. This prevented subgroup analyses based on these factors, hindering the identification of patient populations most likely to benefit from PBT and a more comprehensive exploration of potential sources of heterogeneity.

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted strictly following PRISMA guidelines, employing measures such as independent dual performance and cross-checking to minimize bias. Nevertheless, certain limitations inherent to the review process are unavoidable. First, inherent design flaws exist. The absence of RCT data means that despite controlling for some baseline factors via propensity score matching, selection bias and information bias cannot be completely ruled out. Critically, none of the four included studies adjusted for MVI, satellite nodules, or tumor capsule integrity—all established independent prognostic factors for HCC radiotherapy. Only two studies adjusted for ALBI grade, and no study used BCLC stage subgroups for adjustment. These unadjusted confounders are likely to bias the OS estimates in favor of PBT. Furthermore, other unmeasured confounders such as nutritional status and subsequent treatment regimens may also have influenced outcomes. Due to the limited number of included studies (n = 4), we could not perform multivariate meta-regression or subgroup analyses to adjust for these factors, which represents a major methodological constraint. Second, the literature search strategy was restricted. Searches were limited to four core databases (PubMed, Web of Science, etc.), and grey literature, such as conference abstracts and dissertations, was not sought. This may have resulted in the omission of relevant studies, potentially exacerbating publication bias. Second, the power to detect publication bias was limited. The analysis for OS included only four studies, and the analyses for RILD and non-classical RILD included only three. The small number of studies limits the statistical power of funnel plot asymmetry and Egger’s test; thus, even though no significant bias signals were detected, potential publication bias or small-study effects cannot be definitively excluded. Furthermore, the limited number of studies precluded meaningful correction for effect sizes in outcomes with suspected publication bias. Fourth, in-depth analyses were constrained. Due to limitations in the data reported by the primary studies, detailed information on potential sources of heterogeneity, such as operator experience, specific tumor anatomical location, and normal liver tissue dose constraints, was unavailable. This prevented more detailed subgroup analyses or meta-regression, restricting the ability to pinpoint specific causes of heterogeneity beyond identifying its main source. Fifth, potential bias may arise from the choice of effect synthesis model. While the selection of a fixed-effect model based on I² values adheres to statistical conventions, the included studies are observational, and clinical heterogeneity is inherently present. A fixed-effect model may underestimate the variability between studies, potentially leading to artificially narrow 95% confidence intervals and an increased probability of finding statistically significant results. Sixth, subjective bias in quality assessment is possible. Although widely used tools like the NOS scale and GRADE system were employed following international standards, certain NOS items, such as representativeness of the exposed cohort and adequacy of follow-up, involve subjective judgment. Despite dual, independent assessment and cross-checking, this subjectivity cannot be entirely eliminated. Additionally, the reviewers were not blinded to the authors or institutions of the included studies during selection, data extraction, or quality assessment, which could introduce potential bias. Seventh, our analysis was limited to OS, RILD, and non-classical RILD due to data availability. Key clinically relevant outcomes including PFS, DFS, late toxicity (grade ≥ 3 occurring beyond 6 months), QoL, and hospital stay were not reported in the primary studies. The absence of these endpoints restricts a comprehensive evaluation of PBT’s overall clinical benefit and limits the applicability of our findings for clinical decision-making, particularly regarding patient selection and shared decision-making.

Regarding clinical practice, based on the demonstrated benefit of PBT in reducing RILD, its use may be considered for liver cancer patients with adequate hepatic functional reserve who require high-dose radiotherapy, provided resources and access permit. Furthermore, incorporating non-classical RILD into routine toxicity monitoring with standardized diagnostic criteria is advisable. Given the low certainty of evidence for a survival benefit with PBT, clinical decision-making should be individualized, integrating patient tumor characteristics and economic considerations. For patients with small tumor burdens where photon radiotherapy can easily meet normal tissue constraints, conventional photon therapy remains a reasonable option, avoiding the indiscriminate promotion of PBT.

Concerning health policy, the demonstrated safety benefits of PBT in liver radiotherapy support the strategic allocation of proton therapy resources. Equipment deployment could be prioritized in major medical centers within regions bearing a high burden of liver cancer. Concurrently, strengthening technical training for radiation oncologists and medical physicists is essential to enhance the standardization of clinical application. For potential beneficiary populations of PBT, gradually refining relevant health insurance reimbursement policies could help alleviate the financial burden on patients and improve access to this technology. Simultaneously, promoting the development of standardized clinical guidelines for PBT in liver cancer, including unified dose-fractionation schedules and toxicity assessment criteria, would help reduce heterogeneity in clinical practice across different centers.

Addressing the limitations of the current evidence base necessitates prioritizing large-scale, multicenter prospective cohort studies or RCTs in the field of liver radiotherapy to further validate the long-term survival effects of PBT. Concurrently, research efforts should focus on conducting subgroup analyses based on patient characteristics such as liver function grade, tumor stage, and anatomical location to precisely identify the populations most likely to derive benefit. Facilitating cross-regional collaborative research with standardized outcome reporting and adjudication criteria will enhance the generalizability of future findings. Furthermore, exploring combination strategies integrating PBT with other treatment modalities, such as targeted therapy, immunotherapy, and antiviral agents, could uncover its potential value within comprehensive liver cancer treatment paradigms.

Conclusion

This systematic review and meta-analysis found that, compared to photon radiotherapy, proton beam therapy for hepatocellular carcinoma significantly reduces the risks of RILD and non-classical RILD, demonstrating a clear safety advantage. Regarding overall survival, PBT shows a potential trend towards benefit, but this finding is based on low-certainty evidence due to the retrospective design, lack of adjustment for key prognostic confounders (including MVI, satellite nodules, tumor capsule integrity, and BCLC stage subgroups; only ALBI was adjusted in two studies), and publication bias. Therefore, no definitive conclusion on OS improvement can be drawn.
