U-Shaped Association Between Serum Vitamin B12 and All-Cause Mortality in Adults With Obesity in the United States: A Prospective Cohort Study
Authors: Jie Wan, Yuanyuan Li, Yuxin Huang, Lurong Yu, Limei Liu, Bin Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractsBackgroundDespite the health benefits of vitamin B12, the potential association between vitamin B12 and all-cause mortality in populations with obesity remains understudied. Objective: To dissect this intrinsic association in adults with obesity and identify potential threshold effects. Methods: Data from 1999 to 2006 and 2011 to 2014 were retrieved from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey database. Three models were developed by adjusting for demographic characteristics, clinical factors, and comorbidities, and the relationship between vitamin B12 and all-cause mortality was further analyzed using Cox proportional hazards modelling, restricted cubic spline, survival curve analysis, and threshold effect analysis.ResultsAmong 8301 participants with 1347 all-cause mortality over median follow-up of 9.08 years, the lowest groups Q1, Q2 and the highest group Q5 showed significantly increased all-cause mortality risk, although the P for trend was nonsignificant. The unweighted threshold effect analysis showed an inflection point of 354 pmol/L for serum vitamin B12 levels. Below this point, lower B12 levels were associated with lower all-cause mortality, whereas above the threshold, higher vitamin B12 levels were associated with high all-cause mortality.ConclusionsAll-cause mortality in the population with obesity showed an approximately U-shaped association with serum vitamin B12 levels. Maintaining serum B12 levels within 300 to 500 pmol/L is associated with a lower mortality risk. Owing to its observational design, causality cannot be inferred. Dynamic monitoring of vitamin B12 levels may help identify individuals at increased risk; however, further studies are needed to establish causality.Key words: All-cause mortality, NHANES, Obesity, Serum vitamin B12, Threshold effect

Introduction

Vitamin B12, also called cobalamin, is an essential nutrient that the body cannot synthesize on its own and must be obtained through dietary sources (eg, dairy, eggs, and meat), supplements, or injections.1,2 As a key cofactor for methionine synthase and a coenzyme for methyl-malonyl coenzyme A, vitamin B12 plays a central role in homocysteine (Hcy) metabolism and the tricarboxylic acid cycle, thereby influencing energy metabolism and redox balance.2,3

Obesity is a global health issue closely associated with insulin resistance, hypertension, type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease (CVD), and other metabolic syndromes.4, 5, 6 Serum B12 levels in individuals with obesity are significantly lower than those in individuals with normal weight,5 whereas Hcy levels are significantly elevated (P < 0.05)7 suggesting that the metabolic environment specific to obesity may alter B12 homeostasis and physiological function. Although definitive clinical evidence is lacking, the use of vitamin B12 supplements continues to be common in the United States.8 However, the reported average American vitamin B12 intake is nearly double the recommended daily level,9 and some multivitamins, energy drinks, and supplements contain over 1000 times the recommended daily intake2,10 raising concerns regarding their potential harm.11

Most existing studies on the associations between serum vitamin B12 and all-cause or cause-specific mortality have been conducted in the general population.1,12, 13, 14 Evidence specific to individuals with obesity is even more limited.15, 16, 17 However, directly extrapolating findings from the general population to individuals with obesity may be inappropriate, as obesity is often associated with chronic low-grade inflammation, alterations in the gut microbiota, and insulin resistance18—factors that may influence the absorption, transport, and metabolism of vitamin B12.19 The relationship between vitamin B12 and mortality in the population with obesity needs to be specifically evaluated.

Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the association between serum vitamin B12 levels and all-cause mortality in adults with obesity using National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) data (1999–2006 and 2011–2014). We employed Cox proportional hazards models, restricted cubic splines, and threshold effect analysis to determine the safe range of vitamin B12 concentrations, thereby providing a reference for clinical monitoring and personalized nutritional interventions in patients with obesity. Unlike previous NHANES-based studies that focused on the general populations, we specifically examined adults with obesity and used quintiles to capture nonlinear relationships.

Materials and Methods

Study population

The NHANES, managed by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), is a systematic, ongoing program that monitors the health and nutritional status of the U.S. civilian population across all age groups through population-based surveys. Each participant underwent physical measurements and dietary assessment. Physical measurements included blood pressure, weight, and height, along with biochemical measurements of cholesterol, glucose, insulin, and C-reactive protein (CRP) levels in blood samples. Dietary assessment was performed using questionnaires to estimate the nutrient content of the food consumed by the participants during the survey period, as well as health- and disease-related questionnaires.20 The NHANES protocol has approved by the Research Ethics Review Board of the National Center for Health Statistics. Written informed consent was obtained from each participant, and the dataset is now publicly available.15

We extracted relevant data from 1999 to 2006 and from 2011 to 2014 from the NHANES and included adults with obesity with complete serum B12 levels and all-cause mortality data.

Definition of obesity

The World Health Organization (WHO) characterizes obesity as abnormal accumulation or excessive deposition of fat, which can have an impact on health. In this study, obesity was recognized using the WHO criteria, where obesity was defined as a body mass index (BMI) of >30 kg/m² [BMI = body weight (kg) ÷ height² (m²)].7,21,22

Outcomes

This cohort study identified all-cause mortality as the primary assessment endpoint. The National Center for Health Statistics used a probabilistic matching algorithm to obtain mortality data by correlating NHANES-related records with the National Death Index.23

Covariates

Covariate data were preselected based on the risk factors in the study. Continuous variables consisted of age (years), family poverty income ratio, BMI (kg/m2), and laboratory data such as alanine aminotransferase (U/L), aspartate aminotransferase (U/L), creatinine (µmol/L), serum glucose (mmol/L), cholesterol (mmol/L), CRP (mg/dL), hemoglobin (g/dL), serum folate (nmol/L), glycohemoglobin (%), systolic blood pressure (SBP, mm Hg), and diastolic blood pressure (mm Hg), and so on. Categorical variables included sex, race/Hispanic origin, educational status, smoking status, and health status (hyperlipidemia, CVD, diabetes, hypertension, liver disease, and cancer). Hyperlipidemia was defined by total cholesterol levels ≥200 mg/dL, triglyceride levels ≥150 mg/dL, high-density lipoprotein (HDL) levels <40 mg/dL in men, HDL levels <50 mg/dL in women, and low-density lipoprotein levels ≥130 mg/dL. Additionally, those who indicated that they were using medications to lower cholesterol levels were also categorized as having hyperlipidaemic.24,25 The diagnostic codes for CVD were I00 to I09, I11, I13, I20 to I51, I60 to I69. Data on diabetes mellitus, liver disease, hypertension, and cancer were obtained from the patients’ self-reports.

Statistical analysis

Serum vitamin B12 levels were categorized into quintiles (Q1–Q5), and each group was statistically analyzed.

Three models were established to account for confounding variables in the study, considering the physiological nature and covariates selected in previous studies as follow: (1) model 1: unadjusted; (2) model 2: adjusted for age, sex, ethnicity, education status, smoking status, and family poverty income ratio; and (3) model 3: also adjusted for serum glucose, cholesterol, hemoglobin, glycohemoglobin, hyperlipidemia, CVD, diabetes, hypertension, liver disease, cancer, and vitamin B12 supplementation based on the model 2. The Cox proportional hazards model and restricted cubic spline were utilized in model 3 to investigate the nonlinear relationship between serum vitamin B12 levels and the probability of all-cause mortality.26 Kaplan–Meier curves were created to assess the differences in survival rates among the five subgroups. To analyze the association between serum vitamin B12 levels and all-cause mortality and to examine whether the effects were age-, sex-, or race-specific, we performed stratified analyses with the middle group set as the reference group. To investigate the potential threshold effect of serum vitamin B12 on all‑cause mortality, we used a two-piecewise linear regression model within the Cox proportional hazards framework. The model assumes a single inflection point (turning point, K) that divides the vitamin B12 levels into two segments, allowing different slopes on each side. The optimal inflection point was identified using a grid search algorithm to maximize model likelihood. We then performed a log-likelihood ratio test to compare the two-piecewise model with a standard linear model (one‑slope model). A significant P value indicated that the two-piecewise model fit the data better, confirming a nonlinear relationship. The 95% confidence interval (CI) for the inflection point was estimated using bootstrap resampling.

Dummy variables were used to indicate missing covariate values when >1% of the continuous variables were missing. Considering the complex stratified probability sampling design of the NHANES database, we performed a weighted analysis utilizing the weights advised by the NHANES database.27 Continuous variables are presented as weighted means ± standard errors to account for the complex survey design, and categorical variables are expressed as percentages (%). The P value was calculated using the Kruskal–Wallis test. Statistical significance was determined using a two-tailed P value <0.05. R version 4.4.1 and EmpowerStats (www.empowerstats.com, X&Y Solutions, Inc, Boston, Massachusetts) were used for all the statistical analyses.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the study participants

A total of 61,405 participants from the NHANES 1999 to 2006 and 2011 to 2014 were excluded based on the following criteria: age <18 years (n = 26,804), BMI < 30 kg/m2 (n = 24,044), with missing (n = 2158) or abnormal serum vitamin B12 data (>1500 pg/mL, n = 93), and those with missing data on all-cause mortality (n = 5). Ultimately, 8301 participants were included in this study (Figure 1).

The population with obesity was categorized into (1) Q1 for serum vitamin B12 concentration within 13.30 to 239.11 pmol/L, 1650 individuals; (2) Q2 for serum vitamin B12 concentration within 239.85 to 307.75 pmol/L, 1656 individuals; (3) Q3 for serum vitamin B12 concentration within 308.48 to 383.76 pmol/L, 1670 individuals; (4) Q4 for serum vitamin B12 concentration within 383.80 to 499.60 pmol/L, 1663 individuals; and (5) Q5 for serum vitamin B12 concentration within 499.63 to 1497.40 pmol/L, 1662 individuals. Significant differences in age, sex, smoking status, and BMI between the groups were observed across the vitamin B12 concentration strata. However, no statistically significant differences in education, family poverty income ratio, cholesterol, CRP, SBP, or hyperlipidemia were observed between groups.

Vitamin B12 and all-cause mortality

Table 2 presents the findings of the stratified analysis demonstrating the relationship between serum vitamin B12 levels and all-cause mortality. The median follow-up duration was 9.08 years with an interquartile range of 6.67 to 15.42 years. Using the third group (Q3, intermediate vitamin B12 level) as the reference, the lowest group (Q1) (hazard ratio [HR] = 1.30; 95% CI: 1.05–1.61; P = 0.016) and the second group (Q2) (HR = 1.23; 95% CI: 1.01–1.50; P = 0.037) were associated with a significantly increased risk of all-cause mortality. The highest group (Q5) was associated with a 28% increased risk (HR = 1.28; 95% CI: 1.03–1.60; P = 0.027). The fourth group (Q4) showed a nonsignificant increase (HR = 1.17; 95% CI: 0.93–1.47; P = 0.171). The P value for the trend was not significant (P = 0.140), indicating a nonlinear relationship. Detailed information regarding the number of at-risk participants and the distribution of censoring during the follow-up period is provided in Supplementary Figure 1. To further characterize the dose–response relationship and enhance clinical interpretability, we additionally categorized serum vitamin B12 levels into clinically relevant cutoffs (148, 148–221, 222–500, and 500 pmol/L). A weighted analysis using clinical cutoffs is shown in Supplementary Table 1.

Figure 2 shows the differences in all-cause mortality in populations with obesity with different serum vitamin B12 concentrations. Individuals in the middle vitamin B12 groups (specifically, the Q3 and Q4 quantiles) exhibited the highest probability of survival over time, followed by those in the low (Q1 and Q2) and high (Q5) groups. The survival curves for the lowest (Q1) and highest (Q5) B12 groups were approximately coincident throughout the follow-up period, with both groups showing lower survival rates than the middle groups. The differences in the curves became more pronounced with longer follow-up, especially after >100 months. This pattern supports a U-shaped association, with the middle B12 groups (Q3 and Q4) showing the best survival rates.

Threshold effect analysis

Table 3 shows the threshold effects of serum vitamin B12 levels on mortality. This analysis was performed without weighting the surveys. The weighted results are shown in Supplementary Table 2. In the fully adjusted model 3, the inflection point (K) was 354 pmol/L. Below this threshold, low vitamin B12 levels are associated with a reduced risk of all-cause mortality (HR = 0.91,95% CI: 0.83–1.00, P = 0.040). Above this threshold, high vitamin B12 levels were associated with an increased risk of all-cause mortality, although the effect size was very small (HR = 1.04, 95% CI: 1.00–1.08, P = 0.046). The likelihood ratio test confirmed a significant nonlinear relationship (P = 0.019 in model 3).

Discussion

In this large prospective cohort study of adults with obesity in the United States, we observed an approximately U-shaped association between serum vitamin B12 levels and all-cause mortality after a median follow-up of 9.08 years. The lowest mortality risk was observed at intermediate levels (300–500 pmol/L), with an inflection point at 354 pmol/L. Given the observational design, all associations should be interpreted as correlational, and reverse causation cannot be fully ruled out.

Previous studies of association between vitamin B12 levels and mortality have yielded inconsistent results. In line with our U-shaped observation, a study in patients with metabolic-dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease has reported that low B12 was associated with higher mortality, whereas high B12 levels showed no significant association.12 Another large cohort study has also reported that compared with patients with intermediate levels of vitamin B12, patients with B12-overdose and B12-deficient had a higher mortality rate.28 However, some studies have reported different conclusions. A cohort of older Chinese individuals has demonstrated a J-shaped relationship,1 and a Danish study has indicated that elevated plasma vitamin B12 levels were associated with an increased risk of developing cancer.14 These discrepancies may be attributable to differences in study populations, follow-up durations, and adjustments for confounders.

In addition, the threshold effect analysis in model 3 revealed that below the inflection point, vitamin B12 level was inversely associated with all-cause mortality; above the threshold, a weakly positive association was observed. Thus, the high serum B12 levels we observed may be the result of the individual’s underlying health conditions,29 such as liver dysfunction,30,31 infectious diseases, and cancer,14 rather than a direct contributor to the increased mortality risk. In individuals with obesity, various factors can contribute to low or deficient vitamin B12 levels, including malabsorption due to gastrointestinal dysfunction,2 inadequate dietary intake (especially for those with low-fiber diets or vegetarians32), excessive alcohol consumption,33 and the use of medications, such as metformin.2,34

Vitamin B12 is an essential cofactor in one‑carbon metabolism, DNA synthesis, and fatty acid oxidation. In individuals with obesity, changes in the gut microbiota,4,35 chronic inflammation,36 and the use of medications (such as metformin) may affect vitamin B12 status. Although B12 deficiency may potentially promote obesity-related complications through pathways such as elevated Hcy levels,7,37 impaired mitochondrial energy metabolism,5 and DNA methylation,36 these mechanisms remain unclear. Owing to the observational nature of this study, causality could not be established. Further basic and clinical research is needed to elucidate the specific mechanisms by which vitamin B12 influences mortality risk in individuals with obesity.

Despite the risks associated with both deficiency and excess, maintaining serum B12 levels within 300 to 500 pmol/L may be associated with lower mortality in adults with obesity. Clinicians should exercise caution when prescribing vitamin B12 supplements without a confirmed deficiency. Moreover, a cohort study has indicated that vitamin B12 supplementation is associated with an increased risk of hip fractures in postmenopausal women.38 Nevertheless, individual differences should be considered, including genetic predisposition and lifestyle factors that may impact nutrient metabolism and utilization when reaching this conclusion.39

This retrospective analysis of a large prospective cohort study based on the NHANES database has several advantages. First, survey weights were applied to ensure national representation. Second, we performed threshold effect analysis to determine the optimal serum vitamin B12 concentration. This information can guide clinicians in recommending vitamin B12 supplementation, monitoring patient health, and preventing diseases related to vitamin B12 imbalances. Finally, we established multiple models that were adjusted for confounding variables to reduce bias stemming from demographic and clinical features of the participants.

However, our study has some limitations. First, reverse causation cannot be ruled out, and elevated serum B12 levels may result from underlying diseases (eg, liver disease, renal dysfunction, or occult malignancy) rather than directly causing increased mortality. Owing to the observational design, causality cannot be established. Second, a single baseline measurement of B12 was used, with a median follow-up of 9 years. B12 levels may change over time owing to diet, supplement use, or disease progression. This nondifferential misclassification typically biases the HRs toward the null. Third, previous studies have shown that using BMI alone to define obesity has its limitations,40 and abdominal obesity and vitamin B12 levels have a significant association.41 However, although the NHANES includes waist circumference data, this study did not incorporate waist circumference or other measures of abdominal obesity. Finally, the findings may not be generalizable to populations without obesity or other ethnic/geographic groups. Therefore, serum vitamin B12 levels being not the only biomarkers should be considered, although widely used, they lack sensitivity and specificity in diagnosing vitamin B12 deficiency,42 and measuring plasma total Hcy concentrations or serum holotranscobalamin concentrations39,42 could provide additional insights into blood micronutrient levels.43

Conclusion

In adults with obesity, some risks associated with either too high or too low serum vitamin B12 concentrations were identified. Serum vitamin B12 levels showed an approximately U-shaped association with all-cause mortality, with the lowest all-cause mortality observed at 300 to 500 pmol/L and an inflection point at 354 pmol/L. These findings provide a reference for clinicians to dynamically monitor serum vitamin B12 levels in patients with obesity. However, because this was an observational study with a single B12 measurement and the potential for reverse causation, causality could not be determined. The observed associations do not imply that maintaining B12 levels within 300 to 500 pmol/L directly reduces the mortality risk. Further mechanistic and interventional studies are required to validate these causal relationships.
