Prognostic Value of Skin Lesion Characteristics in Subcutaneous Panniculitis‐Like T‐Cell Lymphoma: A Retrospective Cohort Study Assessing the Applicability of T Category of the TNM Classification for Primary Cutaneous Lymphomas Other Than Mycosis Fungoides and Sézary Syndrome
Authors: Myoung Eun Choi, Joon Min Jung, Soo Hyun Jeon, Chong Hyun Won, Sung Eun Chang, Mi Woo Lee, Woo Jin Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundSubcutaneous panniculitis‐like T‐cell lymphoma (SPTL) is a rare form of cutaneous lymphoma characterized by neoplastic T‐cell infiltration of subcutaneous tissue, clinically mimicking panniculitis. This study aimed to evaluate the applicability and prognostic value of the T component of the Tumor–Node–Metastasis (TNM) classification system, as well as the prognostic significance of skin lesion characteristics in patients with SPTL.MethodsClinical characteristics and survival outcomes of patients with SPTL treated at a Korean academic medical center were retrospectively reviewed to assess the prognostic significance of TNM T staging and skin lesion features.ResultsAmong 43 patients with SPTL, nine had concurrent hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH). Higher T categories were associated with an increased likelihood of HLH. Although overall survival (OS) did not differ significantly across T categories, higher T stage and presence of lipoatrophy correlated with poorer relapse‐free survival (RFS). Of all clinical variables, only HLH was significantly associated with reduced OS. Decreased RFS was associated with lipoatrophy, HLH, and visceral or nodal involvement. Multivariable analysis identified T3 stage and HLH as independent prognostic factors of RFS.ConclusionsPatients with SPTL presenting with extensive skin involvement (T3) have a higher risk of disease relapses and are more likely to develop HLH. Keywords: prognosis, subcutaneous panniculitis‐like T‐cell lymphoma, T category of the TNM classification

Introduction

Subcutaneous panniculitis‐like T‐cell lymphoma (SPTL) is a rare cutaneous lymphoma characterized by infiltration of subcutaneous adipose tissue by neoplastic cytotoxic T cells, clinically mimicking panniculitis. In the World Health Organization–European Organization for Research and Treatment of Cancer classification of primary cutaneous lymphomas, SPTL is restricted to tumors with an αβ T‐cell receptor (TCR) phenotype. However, tumors with a γδ TCR phenotype are categorized separately as cutaneous γδ T‐cell lymphoma (CGD‐TCL) [1]. SPTL typically consists of CD8+ cytotoxic T cells expressing αβ TCRs and exhibits a CD4−, CD8+, and CD56− immunophenotype, with disease largely confined to the subcutaneous tissue. In contrast, CGD‐TCL (formally termed SPTL‐GD) is characterized by a CD4−, CD8−, CD56+ phenotype and is associated with a poorer prognosis [2].

Approximately 20% of patients with SPTL develop hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH) [3], a frequently fatal syndrome characterized by uncontrolled inflammation, hypercytokinemia, and multiorgan failure [4]. Prognosis is strongly influenced by HLH status, with SPTL showing a generally favorable outcome [2] (5‐year overall survival [OS] rate > 80%) unless complicated by HLH, which reduces the 5‐year OS to 46% [2]. Beyond HLH and HAVCR2 mutations [5, 6], few prognostic factors have been identified in SPTL. In particular, the prognostic significance of skin lesion characteristics remains poorly understood.

A Tumor–Node–Metastasis (TNM) classification system was introduced in 2007 for primary cutaneous lymphomas, excluding mycosis fungoides (MF) and Sézary syndrome (SS), to enable standardized anatomical assessment of disease extent [7]. However, to our knowledge, its relevance to SPTL has not been previously evaluated. This study, therefore, investigated the prognostic relevance and clinical applicability of the T category of the TNM classification system and skin lesion characteristics in patients with SPTL.

Materials and Methods

Following approval by the institutional review board (IRB No. 2022–0832), the Asan Medical Center database was retrospectively reviewed to identify cases of cutaneous SPTL confirmed by skin biopsy between January 1998 and June 2024. Pathological diagnoses were established based on the following immunophenotypic criteria: CD3 positivity, expression of CD8 and cytotoxic granule‐associated proteins (such as granzyme B and T‐cell intracellular antigen‐1 [TIA‐1]), and absence of Epstein–Barr virus (EBV). Primary cutaneous lymphoma was defined as a disease confined to the skin at initial staging, regardless of subsequent systemic involvement.

Clinical data were extracted from medical records, including age, sex, lesion location and type, histopathologic findings, computed tomography findings, bone marrow findings, presence of B symptoms, treatment modalities, follow‐up outcomes, relapse occurrence and site, and survival status. Prognostic factors were evaluated using clinical data obtained at initial staging. Disease extent was assessed using the proposed TNM classification system for primary cutaneous lymphomas, excluding MF and SS (Table S1) [7]. Because SPTL is confined to the skin at diagnosis, only T categories were evaluated. OS was defined as the time from diagnosis to death from any cause or last follow‐up. Relapse‐free survival (RFS) was defined as the interval from treatment initiation to relapse (defined as the appearance of new skin lesions) or the last follow‐up.

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize baseline characteristics. Subgroups were compared using the χ 2 test for categorical variables, and either the Student's t‐test or the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables. Missing data were handled using a complete‐case approach, and no imputation was performed. The extent of missingness was assessed for all variables and was considered minimal. Survival analyses were conducted using the Kaplan–Meier method, with subgroup comparisons performed using the log‐rank test. The primary endpoint was death or last follow‐up. Variables with statistical significance in the univariable analysis were subsequently included in the multivariable Cox proportional hazards model. All statistical analyses were performed using Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) software, version 22.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). A two‐sided p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Table 1 summarizes the clinical features of 43 patients (22 men and 21 women). The mean age was 38 years (range, 7–74 years). Multiple lesions were observed in 38 patients (88.4%), affecting the upper extremities in 27 (63%), lower extremities in 25 (58%), trunk in 22 (51%), and face in 13 (30%). Most patients (39, 91%) presented predominantly with subcutaneous nodules, whereas plaques and cellulitis‐like swelling were observed in eight (19%) and seven (16%) patients, respectively (Figure 1). No lesions exhibited ulceration. Lipoatrophy occurred in six patients (14%), involving the face in one case and the limbs in the remaining five. Initial staging revealed no extracutaneous involvement; however, during disease progression, lymph node involvement was identified in six patients (14%) and visceral infiltration in three (7%) in advanced stages. Bone marrow biopsy was performed at initial diagnosis in seven patients, and no evidence of lymphoma involvement was identified in any of these cases.

HLH developed in nine patients (21%). B symptoms—including fever and night sweats, with or without weight loss—were reported in 28 patients (65%). Autoimmune disorders were present in five patients, comprising two cases of Sjögren's syndrome, two cases of lupus erythematosus, and one case of type 1 diabetes mellitus. Laboratory abnormalities included leukopenia and elevated liver enzymes in 16 patients (37%), anemia in 15 (35%), and elevated lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) levels in 30 (70%). Antinuclear antibodies were positive in two patients.

Treatment regimens included cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, vincristine, and prednisone (CHOP) chemotherapy, non‐CHOP chemotherapy, immunosuppressive therapy, and symptomatic management (n = 2). Immunosuppressive therapy was the most commonly administered treatment, given to 21 patients (49%). The regimens consisted of systemic corticosteroids alone or in combination with other immunosuppressive agents, including cyclosporine (n = 16) and methotrexate (n = 3). Cyclosporine‐based therapy was the most frequently used approach, administered either as monotherapy or in combination with corticosteroids. Chemotherapy was administered to 20 patients (47%). CHOP‐based regimens were used in 16 patients, with a median of 4 cycles (range, 3–7), whereas non‐CHOP regimens were administered to 4 patients, with a median of 4 cycles (range, 3–8).

The mean follow‐up duration was 53 months (median, 61 months; range, 1–144 months). The 5‐year OS rate was 85.5%. Median OS and RFS were 285.6 ± 19.0 months and 87.4 ± 18.3 months, respectively. Overall, 32 patients (74.4%) achieved complete response (CR), defined as complete clearance of skin lesions, while seven patients (16.3%) achieved partial response (PR). The remaining 4 patients (9.3%) had stable or progressive disease. During a follow‐up, 30 of 43 patients (69.8%) experienced disease relapse. Among patients who achieved CR, relapse occurred in 23 patients (71.9%), with a median time to relapse of 45 months (range, 36–86). When stratified by treatment modality, CR was achieved in 71.4% (15/21) of patients receiving immunosuppressive therapy, compared to 70% (14/20) in those treated with chemotherapy.

Hemophagocytic Lymphohistiocytosis

Among patients who developed HLH, the median time from initial symptom onset to HLH diagnosis was 2.8 months (range, 1–6 months). B symptoms were present in 9 patients (100%), elevated LDH levels in seven patients (78%), leukopenia in six patients (67%), anemia in seven patients (78%), abnormal liver function tests in 6 patients (67%), and hepatosplenomegaly (44%). Among the nine patients with HLH, bone marrow biopsy was performed in five patients. Although hemophagocytosis was observed in these cases, no bone marrow involvement by lymphoma was detected. Treatment for HLH included systemic corticosteroids (n = 9), cyclosporine‐based regimens (n = 4), and/or chemotherapy (n = 2), depending on disease severity and physician discretion.

Impact of Higher T Category on HLH Occurrence and Survival Outcomes

Table 2 summarizes the clinical characteristics, T‐category distribution at initial diagnosis, and corresponding OS and RFS in patients with SPTL. According to the TNM classification at initial diagnosis, five patients (12%) had a solitary lesion (T1), five (12%) had regional skin lesions (T2), and 33 (77%) presented with generalized lesions (T3) at initial diagnosis. Only 2 of 30 patients with relapse—both initially classified as T1 or T2—demonstrated an increase in T stage at recurrence of skin lesions. Although rates of visceral and lymph node involvement during disease progression did not differ significantly across T categories, patients with higher T classification at initial diagnosis were more likely to develop HLH. The frequency of facial involvement and lipoatrophy was similar across T stages. While OS was not substantially affected by T classification, patients with lower T categories demonstrated significantly better RFS than those with higher T classification (Table 2, Figure 2).

Prognostic Value of Other Clinical Factors

The impact of age, sex, facial involvement, lipoatrophy, HLH, autoimmune disorders, and lymph node or visceral involvement on survival outcomes was assessed (Table 3). Facial involvement and lipoatrophy were not associated with HLH occurrence. Among all clinical variables, only HLH was significantly associated with reduced OS (Figure 2). Decreased RFS was associated with lipoatrophy, HLH, and visceral or nodal involvement (Figure 2). Neither facial involvement, autoimmune disorders, nor patient age had a significant impact on prognosis. Additionally, OS and RFS did not differ markedly between patients receiving chemotherapy and those treated with immunosuppressive therapy. Multivariable analysis identified HLH and T3 stage as independent predictors of poor RFS (Table 3).

Histological Findings

Biopsy specimens from all 43 patients revealed dense subcutaneous infiltrates of atypical lymphocytes in a lobular panniculitis pattern (Table S2). All cases exhibited rimming of fat lobules by atypical lymphocytes. Cytologically, infiltrates were predominantly small‐ to medium‐sized atypical lymphocytes, admixed with small lymphocytes, histiocytes, and occasional plasma cells. Karyorrhexis was variably present, along with macrophages containing nuclear debris, resulting in the typical “bean‐bag” appearance. Fat necrosis was observed in eight patients, and angiocentric infiltration by atypical lymphocytes occurred in 10 cases; however, angiodestructive patterns were absent. Immunophenotyping demonstrated positivity for CD3, CD8, and TCR βF1 and negativity for CD4 and CD56 in all tested cases. Cytotoxic markers, including TIA‐1 and granzyme B, were consistently positive. In situ hybridization for EBV was negative in all eight patients tested. Among 31 patients tested for TCR gene rearrangement, 23 demonstrated clonal TCR gamma gene rearrangements. Ki‐67 expression and TCR monoclonality were not significantly associated with disease extent, OS, or RFS (Table 2, Table 3).

Discussion

SPTL is a rare subtype of cutaneous T‐cell lymphoma, characterized by neoplastic α/β T cells infiltrating the subcutaneous tissue in a lobular pattern. The prognosis is generally favorable, with a reported 5‐year cumulative survival rate of approximately 82% [2].

In this study, we analyzed the clinicopathological characteristics of 43 patients with SPTL and assessed the prognostic significance of skin lesion features, including the T category. Clinically, SPTL typically presents as multiple subcutaneous, erythematous, painless plaques or nodules on the extremities and trunk [8]. Ulceration is uncommon, unlike in primary cutaneous γδ T‐cell lymphoma. In our cohort, subcutaneous nodules were the most frequent skin manifestation (observed in 91% of patients). These lesions were distributed almost equally across the upper and lower extremities, which contrasts with previous studies from Europe [9], China [10], and Japan [11], where lesions predominantly involved the lower limbs. The sex distribution in our cohort was approximately equal, differing from earlier studies reporting a female predominance. The median age at diagnosis was 38.1 years, consistent with prior reports citing a median age of 31–36 years [2, 11, 12].

In 2008, Willemze et al. reported on 63 cases of α/β SPTL compared with 20 cases of γδ SPTL from the EORTC Cutaneous Lymphoma Group Workshop. At that time, doxorubicin‐based multi‐agent chemotherapy was the standard first‐line therapy in most centers. However, their findings indicated that αβ SPTL without HLH had a favorable prognosis, challenging the routine use of multi‐agent chemotherapy as first‐line therapy [2].

In our cohort, HLH developed in 21% of patients. Willemze et al. reported a 17% incidence, with a high mortality rate of 63% [2], while Michonneau et al. observed HLH in 37% of patients but found it was not a significant prognostic factor [12]. A review of 16 published Japanese cases reported higher incidences of B symptoms (81%) and HLH (45%) [11]. In our study, age, B symptoms, and skin lesion characteristics (including T category, lipoatrophy, and facial involvement) were not associated with OS. In contrast, HLH was significantly associated with poor OS and was a predictor of skin lesion recurrence.

SPTL, although primarily involving subcutaneous tissue, frequently exhibits systemic manifestations such as fever, cytopenias, and elevated inflammatory markers, particularly in cases complicated by HLH, underscoring its nature as a systemic immune dysregulatory disorder rather than a purely cutaneous lymphoma. In parallel, imaging modalities including magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and positron emission tomography/computed tomography (PET/CT) have been increasingly recognized as valuable tools for assessing disease burden and metabolic activity, enabling detection of both cutaneous and extracutaneous involvement and providing complementary information to histopathologic evaluation [13, 14]. These findings support a multidisciplinary framework in which clinical, radiologic, and immunopathologic features collectively inform disease assessment and may help explain the link between extensive skin involvement and systemic hyperinflammatory complications such as HLH.

A recent study identified germline HAVCR2 gene mutations in 59% of familial SPTL cases [5]. HAVCR2, also known as T‐cell immunoglobulin and mucin‐domain containing‐3, is an immune checkpoint receptor that regulates inflammation by promoting T‐cell exhaustion and suppressing T‐cell activity [15]. Germline HAVCR2 mutations are strongly associated with increased susceptibility to SPTL and frequently co‐occur with severe HLH [6]. Patients with HLH and HAVCR2 mutations tend to be younger than those without these features [16, 17, 18]. Nonetheless, even in pediatric cases of SPTL with HLH, treatment outcomes are typically favorable [18]. Within the SPTL‐HLH subgroup, older age is associated with poorer prognosis compared with younger patients [16].

In our analysis, the T category of the TNM classification applied to SPTL and provided valuable prognostic insights. Patients with higher T categories at initial diagnosis were more likely to develop HLH than those with lower T categories. Specifically, the T3 category (generalized skin involvement) was associated with significantly poorer RFS compared with the T1 (solitary skin lesion) and T2 (regional skin involvement) categories. The association between greater skin extent and the development of HLH in SPTL likely reflects increased systemic immune activation driven by tumor burden. Extensive cutaneous involvement may indicate a higher load of activated cytotoxic T cells producing proinflammatory cytokines such as interferon‐γ, tumor necrosis factor‐α, and interleukin‐6, which promote macrophage activation and sustain a feed‐forward inflammatory loop [19, 20, 21]. Accordingly, extensive skin involvement may not only serve as a marker of disease burden but also contribute directly to the cytokine‐driven pathogenesis of HLH. Although SPTL generally has a favorable prognosis, it is characterized by a high rate of skin lesion recurrence, complicating decisions regarding the optimal duration of immunosuppressive therapy. Currently, no reliable predictors of relapse have been established. In our study, however, the T3 category and lipoatrophy were associated with poorer RFS, suggesting that patients with these risk factors may benefit from prolonged drug therapy.

Notably, HLH was observed exclusively in patients with T3 disease, suggesting a close relationship between advanced skin involvement and systemic hyperinflammatory status. This overlap raises the possibility of collinearity between T stage and HLH, which should be taken into account when interpreting multivariate analyses. In this regard, HLH may represent a more direct and clinically relevant prognostic factor than T staging, as it reflects systemic disease activity rather than the extent of cutaneous involvement alone.

SPTL can occasionally present with facial swelling, including periorbital edema or diffuse facial involvement, as reported in prior studies [22, 23]. Other cutaneous lymphomas may also involve the face [24], and in MF, head and neck involvement correlates with higher T stage and worse prognosis [25]. Based on these findings, we evaluated whether facial involvement in SPTL was associated with T category or clinical outcomes. Facial involvement, however, was not significantly associated with disease extent, HLH development, or OS.

In the present study, survival outcomes did not differ considerably between patients treated with chemotherapy and those receiving immunosuppressive therapy. Due to the lack of a standardized therapeutic protocol for SPTL, treatment approaches were heterogeneous. Polychemotherapy is commonly employed in clinical practice, but reported complete remission rates vary widely, ranging from 0% to 67% [10, 26, 27, 28]. In patients with indolent disease and no evidence of HLH, immunosuppressive therapy is typically effective. Corticosteroids, either alone or combined with agents such as cyclosporine, methotrexate, or hydroxychloroquine, have been shown to induce durable remission in many cases. Long‐term disease control can be achieved using systemic corticosteroids or combination regimens involving corticosteroids and immunosuppressants, such as cyclosporine A or low‐dose methotrexate. In such patients, multi‐agent chemotherapy should no longer be considered a first‐line option [8]. However, in cases of progressive, relapsed, or refractory disease, systemic chemotherapy, such as the CHOP regimen, may be necessary. In refractory or relapsing HLH, hematopoietic stem cell transplantation may be indicated [10, 26, 27, 28]. Treatment heterogeneity represents a potential source of confounding; however, in our cohort, treatment modality was not significantly associated with RFS in univariable analysis. Nonetheless, given the retrospective design and limited sample size, residual confounding cannot be completely excluded.

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size was relatively small, particularly within certain subgroups such as T1 and T2 disease, which may limit the statistical power of survival and multivariable analyses. As a result, some estimates, especially in the multivariate models, were associated with wide confidence intervals and therefore should be interpreted with caution. Second, given these limitations, the results of the multivariable analysis should be considered exploratory rather than definitive. The potential for overfitting and residual confounding cannot be excluded. Third, the generalizability of our findings may be limited, as this was a retrospective study conducted at a limited number of centers, and treatment approaches were not standardized. In addition, T1 and T2 disease were grouped together for analysis due to the small number of patients in each category, which precluded meaningful separate statistical comparisons. However, this approach may have obscured potential differences between these subgroups. Further studies with larger cohorts are warranted to validate our findings.

In summary, HLH remains the sole factor significantly associated with poor OS in SPTL. The extent of skin involvement, as reflected by the T category, may help predict the risk of HLH and disease recurrence. Furthermore, the presence of HLH or lipoatrophy may serve as a predictor of lesion relapse. These clinical indicators should guide decisions regarding the optimal duration and intensity of treatment in SPTL management.
