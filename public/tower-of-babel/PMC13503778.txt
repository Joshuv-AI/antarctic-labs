An examination of the associations between AUDIT scores, person-level differences in daily location changes, and stress among college students
Authors: Mason L. Hatwan, Noah N. Emery, Surabhi Swaminath, Mahsa Mojallal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Daily fluctuations in location changes and stress may influence health outcomes, yet their interrelation and links to alcohol-related behaviors are not well characterized in college populations. To assess dynamic associations between location changes and stress and determine whether baseline Alcohol use symptomology predict these patterns. Undergraduates (N = 185; Mage = 18.47; 75% female) from the Colorado Ecological Momentary Assessment Project reported daily locations visited and stress. Dynamic Structural Equation Modeling estimated autoregressive and cross-lagged effects, with baseline Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test (AUDIT) scores as a predictor. Stress demonstrated significant autoregressive stability (p < 0.05); location changes did not. Cross-lagged effects were non-significant, but post hoc reciprocal paths between stress and location changes were significant (p < 0.05). Higher baseline AUDIT scores predicted greater average location changes across the sampling period (p < 0.05), but not stress. The present findings suggest that variability in alcohol use symptomology and related behaviors may be meaningfully associated with individual differences in stress and mobility patterns among young adult college students. Incorporating location change metrics into behavioral health research may inform interventions aimed at stress regulation and alcohol risk reduction in young adults. More broadly, these findings highlight the importance of considering contextual and structural influences, such as academic schedules and daily routines, when interpreting patterns of student mobility, stress management, and alcohol-related behaviors.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-51408-y.

Introduction

Human behavior unfolds within dynamic environmental contexts that shape psychological states and health-related behaviors. The environments individuals occupy and the ways they move through them can influence exposure to stressors, opportunities for social interaction, and engagement in health-risk behaviors1,9,29. Among young adults, alcohol use is particularly embedded within environmental and social contexts, making college populations an important setting in which to examine how daily movement across environments relates to stress experiences and alcohol-related harm26,45. Movement across daily environments may influence alcohol-related behaviors by increasing or decreasing exposure to contexts in which alcohol consumption occurs, such as parties, bars, or peer gatherings. Understanding how patterns of daily environmental engagement relate to stress processes and alcohol-related risk may therefore provide insight into behavioral mechanisms contributing to alcohol-related consequences, especially among those who experience greater alcohol use disorder symptomology during the transition to early adulthood.

Extant literature underscores the importance of real-time data in understanding and addressing the dynamic nature of the human experience31,34,42. Traditional psychological research methods, like cross-sectional surveys and retrospective self-report assessments, are often limited in their ability to capture the dynamic nature of individuals’ daily experiences34. These approaches require participants to recall behaviors and emotional states over extended periods, which may obscure within-person variability and temporal relationships among psychological processes31,42. In this context, ecological momentary assessment (EMA) effectively addresses these limitations by collecting repeated assessments of individuals’ experiences in real-time, thereby providing a more temporally precise picture of daily behavior and psychological states34. EMA approaches have been widely used to examine alcohol use, stress, and contextual influences on behavior, and they are particularly useful for studying behavioral processes that fluctuate across daily contexts.

Recent advances in ambulatory assessment have further expanded the capacity to examine how individuals interact with their environments by incorporating mobile sensing technologies. Smartphones and wearable devices now allow researchers to capture behavioral indicators such as physical movement and geographic location in real time, providing insight into how mobility patterns relate to psychological functioning and health behaviors27,33. For example, geolocation technologies such as global positioning systems (GPS) and geofencing have been used to identify environmental contexts associated with alcohol consumption and other behaviors. Wray and colleagues (2019) [ ]used smartphone-based geofencing to examine how entry into specific environmental contexts was associated with alcohol use and sexual behavior among gay and bisexual men, demonstrating how location-based data can provide insight into behavior occurring in naturalistic settings.

Despite these advances, continuous geolocation tracking introduces methodological and ethical challenges. Collecting precise geographic coordinates can raise concerns about participant privacy and autonomy, particularly when research involves behaviors that may carry social stigma or legal implications32. Participants may respond to these concerns by disabling location services, masking their location data, or otherwise modifying their behavior, which can compromise data validity. These concerns may be particularly relevant in studies involving alcohol-related behaviors among college students, where some participants may be under the legal drinking age and therefore hesitant to disclose information about locations associated with alcohol consumption. As a result, researchers have begun exploring alternative approaches that capture meaningful aspects of mobility while minimizing the need for continuous geolocation tracking.

One such approach involves deriving mobility indicators from self-reported location context data collected through ecological momentary assessment. Rather than recording precise geographic coordinates, participants report their current location category (e.g., home, school, work, party, etc.), allowing researchers to estimate patterns of movement across meaningful environments while preserving participant privacy. Aggregating these reports across the day can produce a behavioral mobility metric reflecting the number of distinct location changes individuals experience. This approach provides a parsimonious indicator or environmental engagement without requiring passive GPS monitoring and may serve as a privacy-preserving method for examining daily mobility patterns within behavioral research.

Daily mobility patterns may represent an important behavioral indicator of environmental engagement and exposure to contexts associated with alcohol use and stress. For example, individuals who move across a greater number of environments throughout the day may encounter more social opportunities where alcohol consumption occurs26. Mobility patterns may also reflect behavioral responses to stress, as individuals may increase movement due to obligations and daily demands or reduce mobility when coping with distress. From this perspective, daily mobility patterns may represent a behavioral phenotype associated with alcohol-related risk, reflecting differences in environmental engagement, social exposure, and routine variability that my increase opportunities for alcohol use or exposure to drinking contexts.

Stress processes represent another important factor in understanding alcohol-related risk. Stress has long been implicated in the initiation and maintenance of substance use behaviors and may influence alcohol consumption through both psychological and physiological mechanisms35,36. Among college students, stress is common during the early years of university enrollment and is associated with a range of behavioral and emotional outcomes25. Intensive longitudinal methods such as EMA provide a unique opportunity to examine how stress fluctuates across time and how these fluctuations relate to patterns of daily behavioral dynamics that may contribute to alcohol-related risk behaviors.

The present study builds on these methodological and theoretical developments by examining daily mobility patterns operationalized as the number of distinct location changes reported across momentary EMA assessments. Using intensive longitudinal data collected from undergraduate students, we investigate how daily mobility patterns relate to dynamic stress processes and whether differences in alcohol use symptomology prospectively associate with these individual-level behavioral dynamics. This work makes three contributions to the literature. First, we introduce a privacy-preserving mobility indicator derived from EMA location context reports that captures behavioral movement across environments without requiring continuous GPS tracking. Second, we examine whether this mobility metric provides meaningful behavioral information by testing its dynamic associations with daily stress processes. Third, we evaluate whether alcohol use symptomology, indexed by baseline Alcohol Use Disorder Identification Test (AUDIT) scores, is associated with individual differences in these mobility and stress dynamics across the EMA sampling period. Rather than assessing momentary alcohol consumption1, the present study examines whether individuals with greater alcohol use symptomology at the start of the sampling period exhibit distinct stress reports and behavioral mobility patterns that may reflect differences in environmental exposure and daily engagement with alcohol-related contexts.

To address these aims, we estimated a dynamic structural equation model (DSEM) examining temporal, cross-lagged, and reciprocal relationships between daily stress and location changes across a 14-day sampling period. At the within-person level, we tested whether previous-day stress and location changes predicted subsequent-day levels of these variables along with cross-lagged and reciprocal reports. At the between-person level, we examined whether baseline AUDIT scores predicted individual differences in mean levels, temporal stability, and variability in stress and mobility patterns. By examining these relationships, the current study evaluates the utility of a novel EMA-derived mobility indicator while advancing understanding of how daily behavioral patterns relate to stress processes and alcohol-related risk in young adults.

Methods

The current study tested a DSEM of daily stress, location changes, and baseline Alcohol Use Identification Test (AUDIT) reports. The study followed the two-level N > 1 DSEM framework outlined by McNeish and Hamaker22. In the cross-lagged panel model of stress and location changes, the study estimated six random effects with two additional effects added post-hoc, reflecting individual differences in the time course of day-to-day stress exposure and location changes. Two additional contemporaneous paths were added to the cross-lagged panel model post-hoc. The reciprocal cross-lagged panel model is further discussed in the results section.

This study is the first to examine the reciprocal cross-lagged panel model of location changes and daily stress. In this study, our hypotheses were tested using daily counts of location changes, calculated from the location context variable to measure the number of distinct places visited, along with daily stress, autoregressions (i.e., inertia, temporal stability), and innovation variances (i.e., volatility). The goal of this research is to provide evidence for a new approach to measuring and evaluating individual-level location changes, and to explore the prospective association of the AUDIT on the eight random effects of interest within the reciprocal cross-lagged panel model.

The study makes six hypotheses, four at the within-person level: H1) Previous-day location changes is hypothesized to be positively associated with next-day location changes; H2) Previous-day stress is hypothesized to be positively associated with next-day stress; H3) Previous-day location changes is hypothesized to be inversely associated with next-day stress; H4) Previous-day stress is hypothesized to be positively associated with next-day location changes; and two at the between-person level: H5) Baseline AUDIT scores are hypothesized to be positively associated with stress, positively with stress inertia, and inversely with stress innovation; H6) Baseline AUDIT scores are hypothesized to be positively associated with person-mean location changes, inversely with location change inertia, and positively with location change innovation. The hypothesized between-person effects were tested with the additive covariates of age and biological sex.

Participants

The initial participant pool consisted of 340 undergraduate college students enrolled at a four-year university in Colorado. The analytic sample was restricted to participants aged 18–20 years. This age range was selected to focus on individuals below the legal drinking age in the United States, a population for whom alcohol-related behaviors may be more sensitive to disclose in real-time drinking assessments. By restricting the sample to this age range, we intentionally offer a more informative setting for evaluating the utility of the privacy-preserving EMA-derived mobility metric examined in the present study. Persons who scored zero on the AUDIT were also included in the analyzed sample to further improve the evaluative utility of the mobility metric. Of the original sample, 182 students with demographic data (72% women, Mage = 18.75, SD = 1.23) met the inclusion criteria. Regarding demographics, 79.12% of the analyzed sample identified as White (n = 144), 4.95% as Asian (n = 9), 4.4% as Black (n = 8), 8.28% as Multiracial (n = 15), ~ 1% as Native American (n = 1), and ~ 3% as another non-listed race (n = 5). Roughly 20% of the sample identified as having Hispanic or Latino/Latina ethnicity (n = 36).

Procedure

The study was approved by the Colorado State University Institutional Review Board (#2888). All procedures were conducted in accordance with relevant ethical guidelines and regulations. Informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to their participation. The present study is a secondary analysis of data collected as part of a broader EMA study examining daily experiences among undergraduate students. Although momentary alcohol consumption was assessed as part of the EMA protocol, these data were not included in the present analysis due to extreme skew, a high proportion of zero reports, and limited within-person variability within the analytic sample, which reduced their suitability for modeling time-dynamic processes. Participants were initially recruited for the study using the university research pool. Participants attended an in-person enrollment session where they completed an informed consent document and baseline assessments. Additionally, participants received training on utilizing the EMA program after downloading it to their mobile devices. The training entailed discussions about the EMA application functions, timing of assessments, and survey questions. The EMA protocol was delivered using mEMA (ilumivu, 2021; https://ilumivu.com), a multi-platform smartphone application (iOS and Android) developed by ilumivu Inc.

The EMA study employed a single measurement burst design, wherein participants received four random momentary surveys and a single morning survey daily across their 14-day enrollment period. Morning surveys were available between 9:30 a.m. and noon. The four momentary surveys were randomly dispersed within 3.5-hour blocks between 10:00 a.m. and midnight. Participants were informed they could skip surveys when they would otherwise be unavailable (e.g., sleeping) or if taking the survey would be dangerous (e.g., driving).

Participants received random prompts for the surveys and were given three reminder prompts at 7-minute intervals if the survey had not yet been taken. After 30 min, the survey would expire. Participants were awarded research credit in their undergraduate psychology courses for participating.

The sampling schedule resulted in approximately three-hour lags between assessments. This schedule was chosen to increase the likelihood of survey completion and to avoid overburdening. Current time-dynamic researchers postulate that the observed effects in autoregressive models potentially vary depending on the specified time lags7,11,16. While this study is the first to assess the day-to-day stress-location changes dynamic, the results in the current study are specific to five possible daily surveys aggregated at the day-level (i.e., day-to-day time lag).

Measures

Baseline measures

Demographic Information. Information was collected on participant age, sex assigned at birth, race, and ethnicity.

Alcohol Use Symptomology. The Alcohol Use Identification Test (AUDIT;4 was used to identify participants’ baseline alcohol use symptomology. The AUDIT is a 10-item assessment that measures three domains of alcohol consumption: hazardous alcohol use (frequency of drinking, heavy drinking, and typical quantity), dependence symptoms (impaired control over drinking, increased salience of drinking, and morning drinking), and harmful alcohol use (guilt after drinking, blackouts, alcohol-related injuries, and others concerns about drinking). Participants were asked to respond to these questions regarding their past-year alcohol consumption, and items were scored on a 5-point scale ranging from 0 to 4, with a minimum total raw score of 0 and a maximum of 40.

Experience sampling measures

Location changes. During assessment windows, participants were asked to indicate their current location (i.e., work, school, home, bar, party, restaurant, recreational place, in transit, or none of the above). The current study computed day-level scores using momentary reports of the current location at the time of the survey signal to develop the location changes variable37. Scores on this location change variable range from 0 (no distinct changes in location) to 4 (four distinct changes in location). To address concerns related to missing data, all responses on the location context variable were screened by person, by day, by signal, and by time of day. If a person completed one survey signal indicating “work” but missed the next one “missing” but, completed the last three indicating “home,” “work,” and “school,” the missing survey signal would be filled in with the previous item response “work,” resulting in a score of three distinct changes in location for the day (i.e., response1: work, response2: work, response3: home, response4: work, response5: school). Therefore, if a person responded to one survey signal indicating they were located at “home” but missed the following four signals the missing responses would be filled in with the previous response “home,” indicating no distinct changes in location for that day (i.e., response1: home, response2: home, response3: home, response4: home, response5: home).

To address the concern of data missingness, participants with location change scores of zero (i.e., no distinct changes in locations by day) were further reviewed. Participants who completed three or more of the four possible random signals and were found to have location change scores of zero were retained in the analysis; if participants were found to have two or more ‘missing’ survey signal responses out of the four possible random signals, they were removed from the analysis. In the present study, the approach used to carry forward responses within a day (i.e., aggregating momentary reports to derive day-level indicators) was not intended to impute missing data in the traditional sense, but rather to construct a summary representation of daily behavior from available EMA observations. This approach is commonly used in intensive longitudinal research when deriving day-level variables from momentary data34. Given we are unable to identify where or whether participants changed locations during the missing survey signals, we believe filling in two or more of the missing signals with the previous response and retaining such observations could skew the distribution of the location change variable, leading to an improperly specified model. Further, clarification for our rational is detailed in the results sections below. The location change variable was coded using Stata 17.0. The code used to generate the location change variable from the location context item is provided in Appendix I.

Stress. During random assessments, participants were asked to rate their momentary stress over the last 30 min (i.e., How STRESSED have you felt in the last 30 min? ). Responses were recorded using an 11-point scale ranging from 0 (not at all) to 10 (extremely). This approach has been employed in other EMA studies examining daily stress18, supporting the validity of this item construct as an appropriate measure of stress37. These momentary ratings were then aggregated into day-level averages.

Analysis plan

To test the hypothesized effects of daily location changes and stress reports on baseline AUDIT scores, a DSEM was estimated in Mplus version 8.8 using the Bayes estimator3; Muthén & Muthén, 2022). The analytic approach for testing a two-level DSEM for N > 1 outlined by McNeish and Hamaker22 was followed. The data were defined as a two-level structure with days (level 1 [L1]; within-person) nested within persons (level 2 [L2]; between-person). DSEM deconstructs the observed variables for person i at observation t into person means and temporal divergence from the mean.

At level 1 (i.e., within-person), daily stress and location change variables at time t are regressed on their preceding daily levels at t-1, representing a day-to-day lag (see Fig. 1). These paths represent the autoregressive paths, φss, and φmm. Further, daily stress at time t is regressed on the daily location changes at time t-1, and daily location changes at time t were regressed onto daily stress at time t-1. These represent the cross-lagged effects, φsm, and φms. Two contemporaneous effects were added post hoc; daily stress at time t is regressed on daily location changes at time t, and daily location changes at time t were regressed on daily stress at time t, representing the reciprocal effects in the panel model28. These contemporaneous effects represent the paths φrecsm and φrecms, respectively. The eight within-person effects were estimated as random effects to increase detectable variations in the strengths of associations at the between-person level. Further, the random residual variances and covariances were estimated. In a model such as this, all paths are estimated simultaneously, and each path coefficient represents a semi-partial estimate.

The DSEM estimated ten random effects from the EMA data (two intercepts (µST, µMO), two autoregressive effects (φss, φmm), two contemporaneous effects (φrecsm, φrecms), two cross-lagged effects (φsm, φms), and two innovation variances (estimated as the logVs at L2 (log(πST), log(πMO)). The AUDIT variable, age variable, and sex variable were exogenous, with paths to eight of the ten random effects at L2. Again, the two contemporaneous effects were added to the DSEM post hoc to increase the identification of the within-person model and were not predicted by any exogenous variables at the between-person level.

A significant positive direct effect from baseline AUDIT scores (L2) to daily stress intercept (µST) and a significant positive direct effect from baseline AUDIT (L2) scores to location changes intercept (µMO) will partially confirm both hypotheses (H5 and H6). Significant positive direct effects from baseline AUDIT scores (L2) to stress innovation variance (log(πST)) and increased stress inertia (φss) will support the hypothesis (H5); AUDIT scores will be positively associated with higher stress levels, more stress inertia, and more stress volatility. Additionally, significant positive direct effects from baseline AUDIT scores (L2) on location changes inertia (φmm) and location changes innovation variance (log(πMO)) will support the hypothesis (H6); AUDIT scores will be positively associated with greater location changes, more location changes inertia, and more location changes volatility, fully confirming both hypotheses (H5 and H6). Although not hypothesized, significant direct effects to either cross-lagged effect (φsm, φms) will be indicative of a moderating effect of AUDIT scores (L2) on the cross-lagged effects of previous day stress to next day location changes (φsm) and on previous day location changes to next day stress (φms).

Statistical power

Power simulation models used to estimate sample size and effect size in two-level models with hierarchically structured data suggest that samples of ~ 30 at lower levels (within; L1) and samples of ~ 200 at higher levels (between; L2) are sufficient for finding medium and large effects of ~ 0.082,30. With a targeted power level ≥ 0.80; to detect medium (e.g., 0.30) intraclass correlation, at least 30 within-person observations and 200 participants are needed to detect within-person effects of 0.08 or greater, between-person effects of 0.20 or greater, and cross-level effects of 0.21 or greater2,30. In the current sample, participants could complete up to 14 total L1 observations, and the final L2 sample would consist of 185 participants (182 with full demographic data).

Based on the power simulation recommendations outlined by Schultzberg and Muthén30 and Arend and Schäfer2, the current study sample is within an adequate range for power recommendations to detect medium and large effects. Additionally, the proposed model included between-persons covariates such as age and biological sex, which will account for additional residual variance and ultimately lead to an increase in power.

Results

This section discusses the estimation of the two-level DSEM for N > 1 model. To ensure a thorough evaluation, we executed the hypothesized model configuration four times, employing progressively increasing iterations: initially at 1,000, followed by 5,000, next with 10,000, and culminating at 50,000 iterations. This method allowed us to observe the stability and convergence behaviors of the models under extensive testing conditions. Overall, the final model with 50,000 iterations produced a potential scale reduction (PSR) value of 1.002, indicating good convergence in the model.

In the present study, momentary location context EMA reports were aggregated to the day level (requiring at least three of four completed random surveys per day) to aggregate daily location changes indicators. This approach was not intended to impute missing data in the traditional sense, but rather to derive day-level representations from available observations. Aggregation of momentary data to form daily variables is a common practice in intensive longitudinal research when the primary focus is on day-to-day dynamics rather than moment-to-moment fluctuations34. By aggregating observed data with each day, we aimed to preserve the integrity of the behavioral signals while maintaining a consistent temporal structure for analysis. Additionally, given sample size and complexity of the DSEM framework, this approach provided a more parsimonious and statistically stable alternative to model-based imputation.

Descriptive statistics

The final analyzed sample was 185 (Mage = 18.47), and response compliance with surveys was 84.1%. On average, participants spent 13.69 (SD = 0.99) days in the study and could complete up to 56 within-person observations (L1) across the 14-day protocol. Across the EMA sampling period, 73.60% of observations occurred on weekdays (Monday-Friday) and 26.40% occurred on weekends (Saturday-Sunday). Examination of EMA location context frequencies indicated that the majority of reports occurred at home (54.71% and school (30.22%), whereas explicitly alcohol-related contexts (i.e., bar [0.30%] and party [0.11%]) were rarely endorsed. This distribution suggests that the location-change metric in the present study, likely reflets variability (distinct location changes) in routine daily environments rather than direct exposure to alcohol-specific contexts2. Broken down by risk levels (Zone I, II, III, IV) on the AUDIT, 51% of participants were in Zone I (0–7; abstinence or low-risk drinking), 41% were in Zone II (8–15; alcohol use in excess and likely minor negative consequences), 6% were in Zone III (16–19; harmful and hazardous drinking and possible dependence), and nearly 3% were in Zone IV (20–40; severe Alcohol use symptomology and likely dependence). The distribution is comparable to historical long-term self-reports in large populations21,24. Descriptive statistics for study variables are presented in Table 2. Location changes exhibited a small inverse correlation with biological sex and a small positive correlation with subject mean stress. All other variables exhibited non-significant correlations. The location changes and stress variables were assumed to vary across persons and days; therefore, the intraclass correlations were calculated for each variable. The intraclass correlation for the location changes variable (i.e., distinct changes in location between momentary surveys) was 0.14. This means that 14% of the variance in the location changes variable was observed at the between-person level (L2), and 86% of the variance was at the within-person level (L1). Dynamic structural equation model

We estimated the dynamic structural equation model (DSEM) in Mplus 8.8 with the Bayes estimator (see Fig. 1) with assessments of stress and location changes across 14 days from 185 persons (see Table 2); the final analysis was conducted with 2,755 observations. To test the hypothesized associations between the daily stress and location change variables and baseline AUDIT reports, a DSEM was estimated, where simultaneously, at the within-person level (L1), daily stress (t) and location changes (t) had direct paths to the previous day’s daily stress (t-1) and location changes (t-1) to estimate the two autoregressive effects (φss, φmm). Additionally, two cross-lagged paths from daily stress (t) and location changes (t) to the previous day’s daily location changes (t-1) and stress (t-1), to estimate the two cross-lagged effects (φsm, φms). Two contemporaneous direct paths (φrecsm, φrecms) were added post hoc: one direct path from the same-day location changes (t) to same-day stress (t) and one from same-day stress (t) to same-day location changes (t).

In total, 10 within-person random effects were created during the within-person (L1) estimation, and 8 within-person random effects were used in the between-person (L2) model estimation: two intercepts (µST, µMO), two autoregressive effects (φss, φmm), two cross-lagged effects (φsm, φms), and two innovation variances (estimated as the logVs at L2 (log(πST), log(πMO)). Within-person standardized random effects, posterior distribution standard deviation, and 95% credibility estimates are presented in Table 1. Next, the eight within-person random effects from level 1 were taken up to the between-person level (L2), and all random effects had direct paths to the baseline AUDIT scores, age, and sex variables.

In line with our hypothesis, a within-person (L1) significant moderate positive autoregressive effect for stress (φss) was observed. Contrary to our hypotheses, the location change (φmm) autoregressive effect and the two cross-lagged effects were not significant. The two contemporaneous effects (φrecsm, φrecms) of same-day location changes (t) to same-day stress (t; φrecsm) and same-day stress (t) to same-day location changes (t; φrecms) exhibited significant, small inverse associations. Discussion

The current study applied advanced statistical modeling to explore the prospective association of AUDIT scores with individual differences in day-to-day location changes and stress. We employ intensive longitudinal data from a sample of undergraduate college students to examine these aims. This is the first study to explore these dynamics and use a novel location change variable aggregated using the self-report location context item included in the EMA battery. Consistent with our hypothesis (H2), we observed a significant autoregressive stress effect (φss; i.e., inertia) wherein previous-day stress (t-1) was positively associated with next-day stress (t). The inertia parameter estimates the likelihood of staying in the previously reported state from one moment to the next3. In other words, this suggests participants endorsed a higher carryover effect of stress from one day to the next (i.e., stress inertia). Consistent with our hypothesis (H6), AUDIT scores were positively associated with person-mean location changes (µMO). This finding suggests that individuals with higher scores on the AUDIT also had a higher average number of distinct locations visited across days in the study. This association should not be interpreted as directional or causal, as AUDIT scores reflect current alcohol use disorder symptomology and may capture relatively stable patterns of alcohol-related behavior that persist during the EMA sampling period. The observed relationship may therefore reflect broader contextual or lifestyle factors (e.g., social engagement, environmental exposure, or daily routines) rather than alcohol-related processes alone.

Additionally, we found that mean location changes (µMO) were inversely associated with age, in that individuals identified closer to the legal drinking age in the U.S.A. exhibited lower person-mean location changes across the sampling period. Concerning biological sex, we observed inverse associations with stress inertia (φss), person-mean stress (µST), and stress innovation variance (log(πST); i.e., reactivity). In this context, innovation variance is an estimation of the unobserved heterogeneity thought to comprise the construct3. These findings suggest that in the current sample, males may exhibit less stress carryover from one day to the next. In addition, their stress appears to be less erratic and unpredictable (i.e., less volatile). The primary hypotheses and main findings are further discussed below in the context of current literature.

Within-person effects

Autoregressive effects

Location change. Autoregression, also described as inertia in behavioral and psychological sciences, refers to a person’s propensity to retain a previously reported state from one moment to the next3. Location changes, operationalized here as the number of changes in distinct locations across the daily sampling window, were expected to exhibit a positive autoregressive effect from one day to the next. Contrary to our hypothesis (H1), the autoregressive effect of location change (φmm) was non-significant. This indicates that location change patterns, as operationalized by the frequency of distinct locations visited day-to-day across the sampling period, did not demonstrate a consistent carryover effect from one day to the next. This finding may reflect the unique behavioral variability in young adult college students, whose daily routines are influenced by diverse factors such as biological differences, academic schedules, social interactions, and external environmental constraints25. Future research could explore location changes at different time scales, using different measurement techniques (i.e., accelerometer, wearable devices, GPS), as well as examining the contextual moderators of location change patterns (i.e., employment status, enrolled credit hours, etc.) to understand these null findings.

Stress. As predicted (H2), stress exhibited a significant positive autoregressive effect (φss), providing evidence of stress inertia. This result underscores the temporal stability of stress experiences and highlights how elevated stress levels can persist across days. Such findings align with existing literature suggesting that individuals prone to experiencing stress may have difficulty disengaging from stressful events, resulting in prolonged emotional reactivity12, as well as destabilized emotional inertia from continual stress exposure, as evidenced in the affective science literature on negative affect19. This phenomenon has implications for interventions targeting distress tolerance and emotion regulation, as breaking this stress carryover cycle could mitigate the deleterious effects of chronic stress while simultaneously increasing emotional inertia.

Cross-lagged effects

The hypothesized cross-lagged effect of previous day location changes t-1 on subsequent day stress t (φsm) was not supported. While this result contradicts the hypothesized effect (H3), it provides further insight into the novel location change construct and its relationship with stress25. The absence of the hypothesized (H3) cross-lagged effect of location changes t-1 on stress t (φsm) could be evidence that location changes alone may not confer protection against stress across days. Additionally, hypothesis H4, the cross-lagged effect of previous day stress t-1 on subsequent day location changes t (φms), was non-significant.

The null findings of hypotheses H3 and H4 might reflect the high degree of structured variability in location change behaviors within this population (e.g., running late, class beginning soon, having to get moving regardless of how stressed the student feels), potentially masking any systematic relationships. Therefore, it may be possible that location changes, as measured in the current study, may not capture the quality or intentionality of movement (e.g., purposeful versus aimless activity), which could be more critical in mediating stress levels in college students. Future studies could benefit from integrating affective measures as they relate to location changes and qualitative location changes assessments to distinguish between perceived purposefulness (e.g., Rate the PURPOSEFULNESS of your most recent period of movement; Movement was done with a subjective goal in mind) and perceived aimlessness (e.g., Rate the AIMLESSNESS of your most recent period of movement; Movement was done without a subjective goal in mind).

Contemporaneous effects

Adding contemporaneous effects post hoc revealed significant reciprocal relationships between stress and location changes occurring on the same day. Specifically, same-day location changes t had a significant negative contemporaneous effect on same-day stress t (φrecsm), and same-day location changes t exhibited a significant negative contemporaneous effect on same-day location changes t (φrecms). These findings contrast the non-significant cross-lagged effects observed between these variables across days, suggesting that while stress and location changes may not influence each other across days, they are closely intertwined within the same day.

This contemporaneous relationship could reflect the immediate and situational interactions between stress and location changes. For instance, heightened stress levels may prompt individuals to limit their location changes on the same day, choosing to stay in familiar or less demanding environments as a stress management strategy. Conversely, location changes—whether through social activities, academic commitments, or other contexts—may temporarily relieve stress by offering a distraction or a resolution of a stressor (e.g., completing the midterm exam). This contemporaneous bidirectional association underscores the immediate, situational nature of the relationship, as opposed to a longer-term, lagged influence.

In summary, the absence of significant cross-lagged effects between previous-day stress (t-1) and subsequent-day location changes (t; φsm) and previous-day location changes (t-1) and subsequent-day stress (t; φms) suggests that their association may not extend consistently from one day to the next. This finding may reflect the high degree of structured variability in the academic schedules of college students, whose daily schedules are shaped by dynamic, predictable, and unpredictable factors such as stable academic schedules (e.g., highly routinized class schedules depending on the day), social engagements (e.g., attending study sessions, parties, sporting events, etc.), and external responsibilities (e.g., employment vs. unemployment, etc.).

Such structured variability could mask enduring patterns, as immediate stress or location change decisions may be overridden by the unique circumstances of each day. Additionally, the daily lag used in this study may not align with the natural timescale at which these variables are related; stress and location changes may exert influence within shorter, more immediate periods, as captured by the significant contemporaneous effects (φrecsm, φrecms). Another potential explanation lies in the context-specific nature of stress and location changes. For instance, stress caused by an immediate deadline, like an upcoming academic exam, might restrict location changes for the day (i.e., studying all day in one location) but have little relevance for the following day once the stressor is resolved.

Between-person effects

Baseline AUDIT reports were not significantly associated with person-mean stress levels (µST), stress inertia (φss), or stress reactivity (log(πST). These null results contradict hypothesis H5, indicating that AUDIT scores in this sample did not prospectively associate with the stress outcome. The observed null finding is consistent with existing literature linking alcohol consumption to psychological distress and physiological stress responses35. Specifically, acute alcohol intake has been observed to stimulate cortisol production in low or naïve drinkers13,14,23, whereas in populations of alcohol-dependent drinkers, including heavy and binge drinkers, increases in basal cortisol levels and reduced cortisol response to acute alcohol exposure have been observed5,17,40,43,44. These patterns suggest that habitual heavy drinking alters the biological stress response35,36, potentially contributing to higher person-mean stress observed in those with higher AUDIT scores. Given that the current study did not exclude self-reported abstinent participants from the analysis, the lack of a significant relationship between stress and AUDIT may be a byproduct of group-level heterogeneity. This null finding highlights the complexity of the relationship between alcohol use and stress processes, emphasizing the need for nuanced models that consider additional moderating or mediating variables and are more homogeneous.

Consistent with hypothesis H6, AUDIT scores were positively associated with person-mean location changes (µMO), indicating that higher AUDIT scores at baseline were prospectively associated with greater day-to-day location changes over the EMA sampling period. However, there were no significant associations observed between AUDIT reports and location change inertia (φmm) or location change innovation (log(πMO)). These findings may suggest that while individuals with higher AUDIT reports exhibit more daily location changes, on average, their location changes are not consistent across the sampling period. To help further explain these findings, a significant between-person level (L2) correlation and residual intercept will be briefly discussed.

In the current study, a very strong positive correlation (r=0.84) between person-mean location changes (µMO) and the location changes innovation parameter (log(πMO) was observed. The innovation parameter estimates the unobserved heterogeneity thought to comprise the construct3 and is interpreted as the volatility or reactivity from the influence of the unobserved factors. Further, the location change innovation parameter (log(πMO) was observed to have a significant, small intercept value (b = 0.07) at the between-person level (L2), indicating a small proportion of the residual variance (i.e., unobserved heterogeneity) in the location change variable was observed at the between-person level (L2). This suggests a significant majority of the variance is observed at the within-person level (L1).

Therefore, we speculate that a very strong positive correlation and a large amount of within-person (L1) residual variance may indicate an unobserved idiographic environmental effect. This alludes to the unobserved interaction between the within-person and between-person factors that are contextually the same between individuals (e.g., enrollment in college courses) while holding significant idiographic variability (e.g., different majors, course meeting times, enrolled credit hours, goal-directed behaviors, etc.), requiring deliberate movement (e.g., attending classes or skipping class) that may be inconsistent across days (e.g., meeting Tuesday, Thursday vs. meeting Monday, Wednesday, Friday). Providing further evidence supporting our interpretation of a possible environmental effect influenced by the structured variability in college students’ academic schedules.

Therefore, location changes, as measured in the current study, may not capture the scale at which distinct carry-over effects (i.e., inertia) or reactivity (i.e., innovation) occur in part due to the extreme ideographic nature of the structured variability of college students’ academic schedules. This aligns and contends with previous research linking alcohol use as a motivator of increased social and environmental engagement20,26,46, calling for further exploration of the veiled motivations, social influence, and contextual factors driving both alcohol-related behaviors and location change patterns10,38.

Demographic effects

The analyses also revealed significant demographic effects. Older participants exhibited lower person-mean location changes (µMO), suggesting potential habituation of routine or decline in exploratory behaviors as individuals become more comfortable within the sampling environment (i.e., acculturating to the college environment). Additionally, males demonstrated lower stress inertia (φss) and reactivity (log(πST) compared to females, indicating potential temporal sex differences in stress processing. This finding adds to the extant literature examining sex differences in college students’ stress experiences and preferred coping strategies6,15,39,41.

Strengths and limitations

The current study did not include day-level contextual covariates (e.g., day-of-week or daily alcohol consumption), which limits the ability to disentangle whether observed mobility patterns reflect general behavioral tendencies or context-specific factors such as weekend social activity or dinking occasions, an important direction for future research. The current study did not differentiate between stressors (e.g., educational, social, financial, etc.). This was done purposefully to assess participants’ general stress without excluding any possible stressors. By asking participants to rate their general stress levels over the previous 30 min during each momentary survey, we were able to capture a more reliable stress report when aggregated at the day-level. This approach provides the most substantial general stress assessment but limits the specificity of identifying idiographic stress contexts. While single-item measures of stress have been used in prior EMA research18, the reliability of such items remains an area of novel development and inquiry8,37.

The study did not test the subjective experience or emotional outcomes associated with location changes, which could influence its relationship with stress. The sample characteristics (college students vs. clinical inpatient populations) and restricted age range (18–20 versus 18-n) may not capture variations in alcohol use and stress relationships across broader contextual and developmental stages. The study did not assess whether location changes were directly linked to moving into an environment where alcohol consumption could occur (e.g., visiting bars, parties, certain days of the week, and linking contextual factors to reports of alcoholic drinks consumed at that location). Finally, it is worth noting that the distinct location-change metric used in the current study, although potentially more privacy-preserving than GPS-based approaches, remains a more accessible, less costly, less burdensome, and less invasive method of measuring mobility that is nonetheless subject to the limitations inherent in self-report data.

Implications

The present findings suggest that variability in alcohol use symptomology and related behaviors may be meaningfully associated with individual differences in stress and mobility patterns among young adult college students. However, given the correlational nature of these results, these patterns should be interpreted as reflecting potential behavioral and contextual associations rather than actionable targets for intervention. One implication of this work is methodological: mobility indicators derived from EMA location context reports may offer a feasible, privacy-preserving approach to capturing behavioral patterns in naturalistic settings. Future research is needed to determine whether such indicators reliably reflect meaningful aspects of environmental engagement and whether they provide incremental value equal to or beyond GPS monitoring.

More broadly, these findings highlight the importance of considering contextual and structural influences, such as academic schedules and daily routines, when interpreting patterns of student mobility, stress management, and alcohol-related behaviors. Although the current study does not directly examine these factors, future research integrating mobility patterns with contextual variables (e.g., day-of-week, social context, momentary alcohol use) will be critical for clarifying these relationships and determining the extent to which observed patterns reflect general behavioral tendencies versus context-specific influences.

Conclusion

This study offers valuable insights into the dynamic relationships between daily stress, location changes, and AUDIT reports among U.S. college students. The significant finding of stress inertia (φss) highlights how unresolved stress persists across days, emphasizing further investigation into the contextual factors of persistent stress in the early stages of college. The observed association between higher AUDIT reports and increased average location changes (µMO) suggests that younger college students with higher average alcohol use symptomology and related behaviors tend to frequent more locations, reflecting potential associations between alcohol-related behaviors, social context, and environmental factors. Limitations such as no experimental manipulation, sample characteristics, a narrow age range, and the lack of qualitative context for location changes highlight growth edges for future research. For example, using longer (e.g., momentary reports) or more densely sampled time series (e.g., survey bursts) may benefit from idiographic modeling approaches (e.g., GIMME frameworks) that estimate person-specific dynamic relationships between mobility patterns and stress, allowing for a more precise characterization of individual-level behavioral processes.
