From imaging clue to diagnosis: Pseudomonas putida pneumonia with the reversed halo sign-navigating diagnosis and treatment
Authors: Yaqi Wang, Yuan Zhang, Kangkang Liu, Keke Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A 69-year-old female was admitted with a four-day history of unexplained fever. Lung examination revealed coarse breath sounds, and laboratory tests showed elevated inflammatory markers. She had no identifiable environmental or occupational exposure, and initial screening for common respiratory pathogens was negative. Chest computed tomography (CT) scan demonstrated patchy ground-glass opacities and reticular shadows distributed along the bronchovascular bundles in the right upper lobe and dorsal segments of both lower lobes, with lesions showing the reversed halo sign (RHS). Initial empirical antibiotic therapy was ineffective. Subsequent bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) culture yielded heavy growth of Pseudomonas putida, with negative fungal and tuberculosis nucleic acid testing. Based on antimicrobial susceptibility testing, the treatment regimen was adjusted to a combination of ceftazidime and amikacin. Following this targeted therapy, the patient's symptomatic improvement occurred while imaging abnormalities persisted, indicating a clinical-radiological dissociation. The patient was discharged after a 15-day hospital stay. This case highlights the following: (1) In community-acquired pneumonia with atypical imaging features (such as the RHS) that is refractory to conventional therapy, the differential diagnosis should be expanded to include environmental opportunistic pathogens like P. putida; (2) The RHS is a non-specific imaging finding, and its diagnostic significance must be interpreted in conjunction with lesion distribution, dynamic changes, and the complete clinical context; (3) Obtaining microbiological evidence through minimally invasive techniques such as BAL is crucial for shifting from empirical to precise targeted therapy, thereby improving patient prognosis.

Introduction

The reversed halo sign (RHS) is a distinctive but non‑specific imaging feature on chest computed tomography (CT) [1]. In 2008, the Fleischner Society defined it as a central ground-glass opacity surrounded by a complete or incomplete solid ring [2]. Initially considered characteristic of cryptogenic organizing pneumonia, this sign has since been identified in various infectious (e.g., invasive fungal diseases) and non-infectious pulmonary conditions. Pseudomonas putida is a Gram-negative bacillus widely distributed in the environment [3], [4] and an occasional human commensal. It is a relatively rare clinical pathogen, primarily causing infections in immunocompromised hosts. Reports of pneumonia caused by P. putida are scarce, and its imaging features remain poorly defined. We present a case of P. putida pneumonia in an immunocompetent patient, focusing on the CT imaging findings and their evolution, including the RHS. Based on this experience, we discuss the challenges and insights in diagnosing and managing such atypical pneumonia.

Case presentation

A 69-year-old female patient presented with a 4-day history of fever of unknown origin. Her body temperature had peaked at 40°C, accompanied by chills, anorexia, and generalized weakness. She reported mild shortness of breath, which worsened with brisk walking or climbing stairs, but denied cough, sputum production, hemoptysis, chest pain, weight loss, night sweats, nausea, vomiting, or urinary symptoms. Self‑administered oral medication provided no significant relief. A chest X-ray at a local municipal hospital revealed infiltrates in the right upper lobe, suggestive of pneumonia, with tuberculosis included in the differential diagnosis. Due to persistent symptoms and ineffective treatment, she was referred to the Affiliated Hospital of Gansu Medical College for further evaluation and management. She had no recent hospitalization, no outpatient infusion therapy, and no dental procedures within 2 weeks prior to symptom onset. She denied using any home humidifiers, air conditioning systems, or engaging in gardening activities. No occupational or environmental exposure to soil, contaminated water, or organic dust was identified. Although no definitive environmental source was identified, we postulate that her advanced age (69 years) may have contributed to age-related immunosenescence or mucosal barrier impairment, potentially increasing susceptibility to opportunistic environmental pathogens such as P. putida.

On admission, her axillary temperature was 38.0°C. The patient was conscious and alert. Physical examination revealed coarse breath sounds in both lungs without rales. Other system examinations were unremarkable. Laboratory tests showed a normal white blood cell count (6.8 × 10⁹/L), but elevated erythrocyte sedimentation rate (89 mm/h) and C-reactive protein (30.78 mg/L). Oropharyngeal swab multiplex polymerase chain reaction testing was negative for influenza A/B virus, respiratory syncytial virus, adenovirus, human rhinovirus, and Mycoplasma pneumoniae. Serum autoantibody panels (including anti-SSA, anti-SSB, dsDNA, nucleosome, histone, ribosomal P protein, and anti-mitochondrial M2) were all negative. Tests for HIV and syphilis were also negative. A preliminary diagnosis of non-severe community-acquired pneumonia was made, and empirical antibiotic therapy with intravenous ceftazidime (1.5 g every 12 h) was initiated.

Day 2 of hospitalization, a non-contrast chest CT scan demonstrated multiple patchy ground-glass opacities and reticular shadows distributed along the bronchovascular bundles in the right upper lobe and dorsal segments of both lower lobes, with lesions presenting as RHS (Fig. 1). On the same day, flexible bronchoscopy with bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) was performed. BAL fluid was sent for microbiological studies, including bacterial culture, fungal culture, and tuberculosis nucleic acid testing. On day 4, levofloxacin (0.5 g daily) was added. Discussion

P. putida is predominantly found in natural environments and serves as an opportunistic and invasive pathogen. It often colonizes damp inanimate surfaces in healthcare settings and patient mucosal sites, leading to healthcare‑associated infections. The bacterium may also enter sterile body cavities through invasive medical procedures or breaches in the skin or mucosal barriers [5]. Predisposing factors for such infections include immunosuppression, transfusion of contaminated blood products, and catheter‑related bloodstream infections [4]. P. putida can cause a variety of clinical manifestations, such as respiratory tract infections, skin and mucosal infections, central nervous system infections, and bloodstream infections [6]. A literature review of 28 patients with P. putida infections reported that 24 (85.7%) were immunocompromised, and catheter‑related bloodstream infection accounted for 92.9% of cases, with 2 patients (9.5%) concurrently having pneumonia [5]. Currently, cases of pulmonary infection due to P. putida are rarely reported, and there is a lack of sufficient clinical imaging data to characterize its radiological manifestations.

The patient in this case was a middle‑aged to elderly female whose main clinical presentation was fever, without symptoms such as cough or sputum production. Infections with this bacterium are typically observed in immunocompromised patients who have undergone invasive procedures or have underlying comorbidities [3]. However, the patient in this case had neither a history of relevant invasive interventions nor underlying diseases, suggesting that P. putida may also infect the general population. Therefore, clinicians should enhance their awareness of this pathogen and ensure accurate differential diagnosis to prevent missed or misdiagnosis.

The serial CT scans in this case revealed several imaging characteristics of P. putida pneumonia: (1) Lobe predilection: All lung lobes could be involved, with the most severe involvement in the right upper lobe. (2) Distribution: Lesions followed bronchovascular bundles. (3) Morphology and density: In the early stage, ground-glass opacities, linear opacities, and patchy opacities predominated, presenting as an RHS. The central ground‑glass area of the RHS showed heterogeneous density, and its peripheral wall appeared irregular with multiple elongated linear opacities. The lesions exhibited a fused and reticular pattern, without calcification. (4) Extrapulmonary findings: There was no bilateral hilar or mediastinal lymphadenopathy, nor pleural or pericardial effusion. (5) Evolution: Early manifestations consisted of ground‑glass opacities, linear opacities accompanied by the RHS. As the disease progressed, the lesion extent expanded, with a reduction in ground-glass opacities, an increase in linear opacities, and resolution of the RHS. Following this targeted therapy, the patient's symptomatic improvement occurred while imaging abnormalities persisted. This clinical–radiological dissociation may be attributed, in part, to the release of endotoxin by P. putida[7], [8], which sustains the infectious process and contributes to lesion expansion during disease progression.

The dynamic imaging progression in this case provides a reference for understanding the natural course of P. putida pneumonia. The early presentation of the RHS warrants particular attention. In this case, its occurrence in P. putida infection suggests that the infection may have originated from a central focus and spread peripherally. The central area might have sustained relatively less damage due to factors such as blood flow, ventilation, or immune cell aggregation, whereas the leading edge of peripheral spread exhibited the most intense inflammatory and host defense responses. Dynamic CT revealed gradual enlargement of the lesions with increasing marginal density, consistent with this pathological progression. Lung injury caused by P. putida differs from the diffuse consolidation typically seen in Streptococcus pneumoniae or Klebsiella pneumoniae pneumonia [9], [10], presenting instead as a more localized, "target-like" inflammatory reaction. Following initial tissue damage, the host repair response may become overactivated, promoting the proliferation of fibroblasts and inflammatory cells in the peripheral zone and forming a band of granulation tissue. Consequently, on imaging, the central area is dominated by infectious inflammation (manifesting as ground-glass opacity), while the peripheral region is characterized by reparative organization (manifesting as consolidation), together constituting the RHS. The central ground‑glass opacity of the RHS often corresponds to alveolar septal inflammation, cellular infiltration, or hemorrhage in the central region, whereas the surrounding ring‑shaped consolidation typically reflects organizing pneumonia, dense cellular infiltration, or granulation tissue within the alveolar ducts [11], [12]. However, in RHS caused by P. putida, the central ground-glass opacity may not represent homogeneous edema but rather a mixture of incompletely necrotic inflammatory areas, hemorrhage, and residual lung tissue [13]. The nodules observed within may represent early microabscesses or necrotic foci, while linear opacities may correspond to residual thickened interlobular septa, bronchovascular bundles, or local fibrous proliferation. As an opportunistic pathogen, P. putida may, through its virulence factors and lipopolysaccharides, induce a intense local inflammatory and organizing response that is predominantly bronchocentric in distribution. The infection often spreads along the bronchial tree, forming inflammatory foci centered on the bronchioles. This pattern can lead to multifocal, patchy lesions. The RHS is more frequently observed in cryptogenic and secondary organizing pneumonia [14], particularly among immunocompetent patients. In immunocompetent hosts, the immune response to atypical pathogens may be more “vigorous” and “organized,” tending to form localized granulomatous or organizing pathological changes, partially resembling those seen in tuberculosis or fungal infections [15], which might partly explain the overlapping imaging features. Although there is currently a lack of direct histopathological evidence to support the above hypotheses, and the appearance of the RHS in this case of P. putida pneumonia may be coincidental, this finding suggests that the RHS is not a “specific” sign for infectious diseases, and its underlying pathophysiological mechanisms may overlap among different pathogens.

The RHS has a broad differential diagnosis, encompassing organizing pneumonia, granulomatous diseases (e.g., tuberculosis, sarcoidosis), fungal and viral infections, and even malignancies [15], [16]. Given its non-specific nature, the RHS cannot be interpreted in isolation; accurate differentiation requires integrating multiple dimensions beyond the sign itself. Morphologically, the characteristics of the RHS offer initial clues: the central area may show ground‑glass attenuation, nodules, or reticulation, while the rim may be smooth or nodular [17]. For instance, in organizing pneumonia, the internal ground‑glass opacity of the RHS tends to be homogeneous [18], and the peripheral solid component is typically uniform and smooth [19]. In organizing pneumonia, the RHS is more frequently observed in the lower lobes, often located in subpleural and peribronchovascular regions [20], with recurrence and migration being characteristic features [21]. In contrast, a nodular RHS is suggestive of granulomatous diseases [22], notably in tuberculosis [23] and sarcoidosis [24]. In tuberculosis, the RHS may be single or multiple, with the peripheral rim and central ground‑glass area often containing multiple tiny nodules [23], [25], while sarcoidosis often accompanies such findings with bilateral hilar lymphadenopathy and systemic symptoms [21], [24]. Clinically, individuals diagnosed with sarcoidosis frequently present with a range of systemic manifestations. These can include fatigue and cough, sometimes with skin or ocular involvement, and may have supportive laboratory findings such as elevated angiotensin‑converting enzyme levels. In viral pneumonia, the central ground‑glass opacity reflects inflammatory changes triggered by viral injury, whereas the surrounding consolidation suggests the host immune response, with lesions commonly located in the right lower lobe [26]. Specifically in COVID‑19, the RHS typically appears during the late or recovery phase and may indicate the development of post‑inflammatory fibrosis [27], [28], often accompanied by laboratory findings such as lymphopenia. A distinctive feature of the RHS in fungal infections is its central region, which frequently contains a network of irregular, crisscrossing lines indicative of necrotic debris. This is typically encircled by a peripheral zone of consolidation, representing either active inflammation or evolving fibrosis [15]. The rim thickness often exceeds 1 cm, and pleural effusion is common; lesions tend to involve the upper lobes, particularly in immunocompromised or diabetic patients [29]. Neoplastic causes, though rare, should be considered; In the context of primary lung malignancies, the RHS may occasionally be observed. Here, the central ground‑glass component often correlates with areas of tumor necrosis. The surrounding rim, typically exhibiting irregular or spiculated contours, raises strong suspicion for malignancy, especially when findings such as regional lymphadenopathy or satellite metastatic nodules are present. In immunocompromised patients with hematologic malignancies, the presence of RHS may be accompanied by multiple nodules (more than 10) and pleural effusion [15]. The likelihood of an RHS representing lung cancer increases in patients with pertinent risk factors (e.g., heavy smoking, unexplained weight loss) and a subacute or chronic clinical presentation [30].

Taken together, although the RHS has a broad differential diagnosis, integrating imaging features with host factors and microbiological evidence can guide reasonable clinical judgment. In our case, the lesions lacked typical features of organizing pneumonia (no migratory pattern or subpleural predominance); fungal infection was less likely (the patient was immunocompetent, and the central area of the RHS did not exhibit the irregular crisscrossing linear opacities indicative of necrotic debris typically seen in fungal infections); tuberculosis was not the primary consideration (no miliary nodules within or around the RHS, and no relevant exposure history); and malignancy was poorly supported (the irregular margins were characterized by long spiculations rather than short fine spicules, with no mediastinal or hilar lymphadenopathy). More importantly, P. putida was isolated in significant quantities from BAL culture, and the patient responded dramatically to susceptibility-guided targeted therapy after empirical treatment failed. Integrating the imaging characteristics, host status, and microbiological findings, the diagnosis of P. putida pneumonia was firmly established. In this context, the RHS represented an imaging manifestation of an infectious process rather than a specific etiological indicator.

P. putida infections can have variable outcomes. While it is often regarded as a low‑virulence pathogen, and some studies report high cure rates (e.g., 92.9% in one series of 28 patients) [4], other evidence suggests that invasive pulmonary infections may carry significant risk, with one retrospective analysis reporting a 30-day all-cause mortality as high as 39% (7/18) [7]. This elevated mortality may be linked to a higher proportion of pulmonary infections in the cohort, indicating that invasive lung involvement could be an independent prognostic risk factor. Furthermore, the therapeutic challenge is compounded by the potential for multidrug resistance, particularly in hospital‑acquired strains, which may exhibit high resistance rates even to potent β‑lactams and carbapenems, a profile strongly associated with poor outcomes [7]. Therefore, prompt etiological diagnosis, susceptibility‑guided therapy, and active source control are paramount. In our patient, the transition from failed empirical therapy to a targeted regimen based on BAL culture and susceptibility testing was pivotal for recovery. This case underscores that even for pathogens considered low-virulence, timely microbiological guidance is essential to navigate variable outcomes and resistance patterns, ultimately reinforcing the core value of precision anti‑infective strategies in clinical practice.

Conclusions

For patients with community-acquired pneumonia exhibiting atypical imaging signs like the RHS and poor response to initial therapy, clinicians should consider a broad etiological differential, including environmental opportunists like P. putida, even in immunocompetent hosts. The RHS is a non-specific finding; its interpretation requires correlation with imaging details, clinical context, and serial changes. Definitive management hinges on obtaining timely microbiological evidence. When empirical treatment fails, early use of minimally invasive diagnostics to establish etiology is crucial for switching to targeted therapy, ultimately improving outcomes and avoiding mismanagement.
