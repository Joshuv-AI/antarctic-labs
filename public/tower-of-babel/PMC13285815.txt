Three Decades of Hantavirus Management in Chile: Lessons for Europe
Authors: Nicole Le Corre, Constanza Martínez-Valdebenito, Carolina Henriquez, Jenniffer Angulo, Marco Acuña, Cecilia Vial, Pablo A. Vial, Gregory Mertz, Marcela Ferrés
Published: 2026

For more than three decades, the scientific and medical community in the Southern Cone of the Americas has faced a challenging zoonotic infectious disease: the Andes hantavirus (ANDV). The first cases were recognized in the south of Argentina in 1995 and in Aysen, Chile in 1997. This outbreak in Aysen led to public alarm after three family clusters occurred, affecting young men, workers, spouses, and children.1 They lived in isolated rural areas, and some died before receiving medical support. Nothing was known about hantaviruses present in Chile, and physicians lacked experience caring for patients.

The recent ANDV outbreak on the MV Hondius reminded us of the dramatic Aysen outbreak in 1997 and early stages of the COVID-19 pandemic. As such, the lessons learned in Chile and Argentina over the last three decades may be useful in non-endemic regions facing imported Andes virus cases.

ANDV infection has a fatality rate of 20–30%, yet low disease incidence limits the feasibility of large clinical trials.2 Our local progress has relied on centralized epidemiological surveillance, including notifiable disease and case or cluster epidemiological investigation. In 2017, a national consensus on management, where centers request immediate transfer to a center with ECMO (extracorporeal membrane oxygenation) availability in addition to requesting specific virologic analysis, led to improvement of patient outcomes in Chile. Following the confirmation of cases, health authorities initiate an epidemiological investigation and conduct contact follow-up.

In Chile, a placebo-controlled trial of high-dose methylprednisolone showed no benefit, whereas an open trial of convalescent immune plasma suggested a survival benefit.3,4 Concurrently, external and national grants supported research on ecology and reservoirs, epidemiological issues, laboratory diagnosis and efforts to identify the factors responsible for the development of severe hantavirus cardiopulmonary syndrome. We have supported training of ECMO specialists, allowing us to have well-prepared and equipped centers throughout the country that ensure equal access to treatment.

Cases most often occur in rural areas that are geographically isolated from high-complexity hospitals or diagnostic centers. To counter this, we have trained local healthcare workers, at the undergraduate level and above, to identify early warning signs and epidemiological risk factors. Early identification, facilitated by decentralized training, remains the most effective tool to narrow this diagnostic gap. As survival rates rise, we must address the long-term follow-up of survivors, with a focus on pulmonary function, mental health, and social reintegration of those recovering from such a severe acute illness.5

The epidemiological profile of ANDV is distinct from other hantaviruses.2 A higher proportion of ANDV cases occur in clusters (approximately one third), and there is a higher proportion of pediatric cases (approximately 15%). Furthermore, person-to-person transmission has been confirmed, forcing us to look for interpersonal transmission whenever a cluster is present.6,7

To evaluate risk factors person-to-person transmission, we conducted a prospective study of household contacts.8 We found that the risk of infection in a sexual partner was ten times higher than for other household contacts. Increased risk was also present in those sleeping in the same bed or room. In the same study, we detected viral RNA up to 15 days before the onset of symptoms or detection of antibodies. We subsequently identified viral genome and infectious virus in different body fluids, providing clues to modes of transmission.9

By implementing prospective follow-up of close contacts with the use of RT-qPCR in buffy coat, we have identified cases in the pre-symptomatic stage, leading to early hospitalization and often a better outcome. These findings highlight the potential value of active monitoring strategies in high-risk contacts during outbreak settings. However, we still need to develop improved risk stratification to focus public health resources on those most likely to develop the disease.

As we move forward, the low incidence of the disease makes the collaboration essential to conduct multicenter trials for new drugs, management strategies and development of vaccines. However, we must remember that the primary risk is the environment. The rise of ecotourism has increased human–zoonotic interactions. Prevention should focus on risk communication, rodent surveillance and the deployment of rapid diagnostic tests in high-risk locations. Avoiding the primary contact from the rodent reservoir is the main way to prevent a human transmission chain. Lessons from the Chilean experience with Andes virus relevant for Europe and non-endemic regions.

In conclusion, our decades of experience with the Andes virus have taught us that while the pathogen is lethal and its context neglected, public health strategies, including education, risk quantification, early detection, and collaborative intensive care, can be successful. We share this experience not just as a historical record, but also as a practical framework for current and future zoonotic outbreaks in an increasingly interconnected world.
