Ultralimits of Sobolev maps and stability of Dehn functions Thanks: Both authors were supported by Swiss National Science Foundation grant 212867. T.I. was also supported by the Research Council of Finland, project number 332671.
Toni Ikonen, Stefan Wenger

Abstract
. We show that the ultralimit of a bounded sequence of Lipschitz maps into pointed metric spaces extends naturally to p p -bounded sequences of Sobolev maps and that this ultralimit for Sobolev maps enjoys desirable properties. We use this to prove the stability of Dehn functions under ultraconvergence of pointed length spaces, thus resolving a problem posed by several researchers in the field. As an application, we obtain a simpler proof of a recent result of Stadler–Wenger, previously proved in the locally compact case by Lytchak–Wenger, characterizing spaces of curvature bounded above by κ \kappa via an isoperimetric inequality for curves.

1.
Introduction and main results

1.1.
Background

Limits of metric spaces and mappings are important in many areas of geometry and analysis. In the case of spaces, the (pointed) Gromov–Hausdorff convergence, introduced by Gromov in
[
Gro81
]
, has wide applicability for example in geometric group theory, in the theory of Ricci limit spaces, in the theory of synthetic curvature lower bounds, in Riemannian/subRiemannian and metric geometry and in geometric analysis
[
Pan83
,
Pet90
,
GPW90
,
CC97
,
CC00a
,
CC00b
,
Stu06a
,
Stu06b
,
Gro07
,
Wen08
,
LV09
,
BNS22
,
Bat22
,
BPS25
]
. However, the required assumptions often do not hold in the generality necessary for applications in geometric group theory, for spaces with curvature bounded above in the sense of Alexandrov, and for diameter-volume bounded sequences of closed Riemannian manifolds. A more flexible notion of convergence is given by the ultralimit
X_{\omega}
of a sequence of pointed metric spaces
X_{m}=(X_{m},d_{m},p_{m})
with respect to some non-principal ultrafilter
\omega
on
\mathbb{N}
. This notion, first introduced by van den Dries and Wilkie
[
vdDW84
]
, has played a key role in geometric group theory and the theory of curvature upper bounds, in particular, in the study of asymptotic cones of groups and spaces
[
Gro93
,
KL95
,
KL97
,
KKL98
,
Dru02
,
KSTT05
,
DS05
]
. We refer to
Section

2.4
for the definitions of ultralimits.

Similar flexibility is desirable in the context of mappings
\varphi_{m}\colon(X_{m},d_{m},p_{m})\to(Y_{m},d_{m},q_{m})
. This is achieved as follows in the Lipschitz setting. We say that
(\varphi_{m})
is
Lipschitz bounded
if

(1.1)

\sup_{m\in\mathbb{N}}\left(d_{m}(q_{m},\varphi_{m}(p_{m}))+\LIP(\varphi_{m})\right)<\infty,

where
\LIP(\varphi)
is the Lipschitz constant. Any such sequence has a
pointwise ultralimit

\displaystyle\varphi_{\omega}\colon X_{\omega}\to Y_{\omega},\quad\text{where $\varphi_{\omega}(\lim\nolimits_{\omega}x_{m})=\lim\nolimits_{\omega}\varphi_{m}(x_{m})$,}

which is again Lipschitz; see
Section

2.5
. This flexibility is already useful in the case of a constant sequence
X_{m}=X
in which case we obtain a pointwise ultralimit
X\to Y_{\omega}
by precomposing
\varphi_{\omega}
with the canonical isometric embedding of
X
into the
ultracompletion

X_{\omega}
.

In many problems in calculus of variations and geometric group theory, Lipschitz maps form too rigid of a class. Therefore it is desirable to extend the ultralimit construction to more general classes of mappings such as Sobolev maps. In fact, there exists a robust metric valued Sobolev theory on Euclidean domains or more general metric spaces; see e.g.
[
KS93
,
Haj96
,
Res97
,
HKST01
,
HKST15
]
. These have become prominent in various fields including analysis in metric spaces, metric geometry, and geometric group theory
[
GS92
,
Jos94
,
SW01
,
DMV11
,
LW17a
,
LW17b
,
Raj17
,
LW18a
,
LW18b
,
LW20
,
DM21
,
NR22
,
NR23
,
MW25
]
.

We focus on
W^{1,p}
-Sobolev maps
(u_{m}\colon\Omega\to X_{m})
, where
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a bounded Lipschitz domain,
p\in(1,\infty)
, and
(X_{m})
is a sequence of
complete
pointed metric spaces. We say that a sequence of
W^{1,p}
-Sobolev maps
(u_{m}\colon\Omega\to X_{m})
is
p
-bounded
if

(1.2)

\sup_{m\in\mathbb{N}}\left(\int_{\Omega}d^{p}_{m}(p_{m},u_{m}(z))\,dz+E_{+}^{p}(u_{m})\right)<\infty,

where
E_{+}^{p}(u)
is the energy of
u
; we refer to
Section

3
for definitions and notation related to Sobolev maps.

In the present manuscript, we construct an ultralimit for every
p
-bounded sequence. We show that it has many natural properties and use it to establish a stability property for the Dehn function under ultralimits. Roughly speaking, Dehn functions measure how difficult it is to fill in closed Lipschitz curves of a given length by discs. They are closely related to the isoperimetric inequality of the ambient space and they have been widely studied in many different settings; see e.g.
[
Res68
,
Gro93
,
GHR03
,
You13
,
LW18b
,
Sta21
,
SW25
]
. The question on the stability of Dehn functions has only been addressed in special cases in
[
Wen11b
,
Wen19
,
LWY20
,
SW25
]
. We also provide further applications.

1.2.
Ultralimits of Sobolev maps

Our first theorem shows that we can extend the ultralimit construction to the Sobolev setting. For the statement, let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a bounded Lipschitz domain and
p\in(1,\infty)
. Let
\omega
be a non-principal ultrafilter on
\mathbb{N}
.

Theorem 1.1
.

There is a unique map sending a
p
-bounded sequence
(u_{m}\colon\Omega\to X_{m})
to a Sobolev map
u_{\omega}\in W^{1,p}(\Omega,X_{\omega})
with the following properties:

(1)

If the sequence
(u_{m})
is Lipschitz bounded, then
u_{\omega}
agrees with the pointwise ultralimit of
(u_{m})
almost everywhere.

(2)

If
(\phi_{m}\colon X_{m}\to Y_{m})
is Lipschitz bounded and
\phi_{\omega}\colon X_{\omega}\to Y_{\omega}
the pointwise ultralimit, then the sequence
(v_{m}=\phi_{m}\circ u_{m})
is
p
-bounded and
v_{\omega}=\phi_{\omega}\circ u_{\omega}
almost everywhere.

(3)

We have

\|d_{\omega}(u_{\omega},v_{\omega})\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}=\lim\nolimits_{\omega}\|d_{m}(u_{m},v_{m})\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}

for
p
-bounded
(u_{m}\colon\Omega\to X_{m})
and
(v_{m}\colon\Omega\to X_{m})
.

Uniqueness in
Theorem

1.1
is understood in the sense that if another assignment
(u_{m})\mapsto\widetilde{u}_{\omega}
satisfies axioms (
1
)-(
3
), then
u_{\omega}=\widetilde{u}_{\omega}
almost everywhere for every
p
-bounded
(u_{m})
. This allows us to talk about
the ultralimit (with respect to
\omega
)
, which moreover has the following desirable properties for calculus of variations.

Theorem 1.2
.

The ultralimit from
Theorem

1.1
satisfies the following properties.

(4)

For every
p
-bounded
(u_{m})
and every lower semicontinuous energy, it holds that

\lim\nolimits_{\omega}E^{p}(u_{m})\geq E^{p}(u_{\omega}).

(5)

For every
p
-bounded
(u_{m})
with
p\geq n
and every lower semi-continuous volume

\lim\nolimits_{\omega}\mathrm{Vol}(u_{m})\geq\mathrm{Vol}(u_{\omega}).

(6)

The traces of
p
-bounded sequences
(u_{m}\colon\Omega\to X_{m}),(v_{m}\colon\Omega\to X_{m})
satisfy

\displaystyle\|d_{\omega}(\trace(u_{\omega}),\trace(v_{\omega}))\|_{L^{p}(\partial\Omega)}=\lim\nolimits_{\omega}\|d_{m}(\trace(u_{m}),\trace(v_{m}))\|_{L^{p}(\partial\Omega)}.

(7)

If the traces
\trace(u_{m})\colon\partial\Omega\to X_{m}
have continuous representatives
(\gamma_{m})
that are equi-continuous and equi-bounded, then the pointwise ultralimit
\gamma_{\omega}
is a continuous representative of the trace of
u_{\omega}
.

In the paper
[
GW20
]
, Guo and the second author were able to define an ultralimit of a suitable subsequence of a given
p
-bounded sequence. Passing to a subsequence, however, often poses a problem, since the resulting map takes values in the ultralimit of the
subsequence
of spaces instead of the ultralimit of the
full sequence
of spaces.

Our main interest in the ultralimit construction arises from the stability of Dehn functions under ultralimits.

1.3.
Stability of Dehn functions

In order to state our stability results, we define the Sobolev filling area of a Lipschitz curve
\gamma\colon\mathbb{S}^{1}\to X
by

\displaystyle\mathrm{FillArea}_{X}(\gamma)\coloneqq\inf\{\mathrm{Area}(v)\colon v\in W^{1,2}(\mathbb{D},X),\,\trace(v)=\gamma\},

where
\mathbb{D}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
is the Euclidean unit disk and
\mathbb{S}^{1}
its boundary. See
Section

3.5
for the definition of parametrized Hausdorff area
\mathrm{Area}(v)
. Here we simply mention that if
v
is injective and Lipschitz, then
\mathrm{Area}(v)
is equal to the two-dimensional Hausdorff measure of the image. Moreover, in case
v\in W^{1,2}(\mathbb{D},X)
and
X
is Riemannian,
\mathrm{Area}(v)
is obtained by integrating the Jacobian of the weak differential of
v
. The (Sobolev–)Dehn function of
X
is

\displaystyle\delta_{X}(r)=\sup\{\mathrm{FillArea}_{X}(\gamma)\colon\text{ $\gamma\colon\mathbb{S}^{1}\to X$ is Lipschitz and $\ell(\gamma)\leq r$}\}

for
r>0
, where
\ell(\gamma)
denotes the length of
\gamma
.

It was recently proved in a paper by Lytchak, the second author, and Young
[
LWY20
]
that in the setting of sequences of proper length spaces, a local quadratic isoperimetric inequality is stable under ultralimits. It was then asked by Stadler and the second author whether this remains true in the non-proper setting and for general Dehn functions; see
[
SW25
, Problem 1]
. The following theorem resolves the problem positively.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
r_{0}\in(0,\infty]
and let
\delta\colon(0,r_{0})\to(0,\infty)
be continuous from the right. If
(X_{m},d_{m},p_{m})
is a sequence of complete pointed length spaces with

\displaystyle\delta_{X_{m}}(r)\leq\delta(r)\quad\text{for every $r\in(0,r_{0})$},

then the ultralimit
X_{\omega}
satisfies

\displaystyle\delta_{X_{\omega}}(r)\leq\delta(r)\quad\text{for every $r\in(0,r_{0})$.}

A coarse version of
Theorem

1.3
will be proved in
Theorem

7.2
.

There is an analogous definition of Lipschitz–Dehn function using Lipschitz maps instead of Sobolev maps. However, we cannot expect stability properties for the Lipschitz–Dehn function since a bound on the growth does not give control on the Lipschitz constants of the fillings.

We next highlight key applications of the stability.

1.4.
Applications

Our study of Dehn functions is partly motivated by the recent
analytic
characterization of
\mathrm{CAT}(\kappa)
spaces — complete metric spaces with curvature bounded above by
\kappa
in the sense of Alexandrov. Let
M^{2}_{\kappa}
be the complete simply connected surface of constant sectional curvature
\kappa
and let
D_{\kappa}
be its diameter; when
\kappa\neq 0
,
M^{2}_{\kappa}
is isometric to a rescaled sphere or a hyperbolic plane. Let
\delta_{\kappa}
be the Dehn function of
M^{2}_{\kappa}
, see
[
SW25
, Section 4.1]
for a precise formula. When
\kappa=0
, it holds that
\delta_{0}(r)=r^{2}/4\pi
for
r>0
but always
\lim_{r\to 0^{+}}\delta_{\kappa}(r)/r^{2}=1/(4\pi)
. We recall that if
X
is
\mathrm{CAT}(\kappa)
, then the Dehn function of
X
is bounded from above by
\delta_{\kappa}
by Reshetnyak’s majorization principle
[
Res68
]
. The converse is known in proper length spaces by a theorem due to Lytchak and the second author
[
LW18b
]
. More recently, Stadler and the second author
[
SW25
]
extended this to the non-proper setting as follows.

Theorem 1.4

(
[
SW25
, Theorem A]
)
.

Let
\kappa\in\mathbb{R}
and
r_{0}\in(0,2D_{\kappa}]
. Let
X
be a complete length space and suppose that
\delta_{X}(r)\leq\delta_{\kappa}(r)
for
r\in(0,r_{0})
. Then every closed ball of radius
r_{0}/4
of
X
is geodesic and
\mathrm{CAT}(\kappa)
. Moreover, if
r_{0}=2D_{\kappa}
, then
X
is a
\mathrm{CAT}(\kappa)
space.

The general strategy of
[
SW25
]
is similar to that of
[
LW18b
]
. However, it involves a delicate analysis of the intrinsic structure of a solution to a Plateau problem in the ultracompletion of
X
. Using the stability result,
Theorem

1.3
, we can give a proof that is considerably simpler by employing the strategy of
[
LW18b
]
directly.

Our second application concerns Gromov hyperbolic spaces. In
[
Gro87
]
, Gromov famously showed that spaces with a subquadratic isoperimetric inequality are Gromov hyperbolic and thus have a linear isoperimetric inequality. In fact, it suffices to have a quadratic isoperimetric inequality with a sufficiently small constant. The second author proved a sharp version of Gromov’s result in
[
Wen08
]
using the theory of integral currents. We establish the following version using our stability results.

Theorem 1.5
.

Suppose that
X
is a complete geodesic metric space with

\displaystyle\limsup_{r\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\delta_{X}(r)}{r^{2}}<\frac{1}{4\pi}.

Then
X
is Gromov hyperbolic.

In the setting of proper geodesic metric spaces, a proof of
Theorem

1.5
was given in
[
LWY20
]
. Our proof parallels this proof and relies, in addition, on the stability of Dehn functions,
Theorem

1.3
, or rather the coarse version,
Theorem

7.2
.

1.5.
Outlines of the proofs and additional results

We begin by highlighting some of the ideas in the proof of the ultralimit theorems.
The central notion we introduce and study in this context is that of a Lusin–Lipschitz admissible sequence, cf.
Section

5
. Roughly speaking, this is a sequence of Lipschitz maps
f^{k}
from
\Omega
into a metric space
X
which agree on larger and larger subsets of
\Omega
, eventually exhausting
\Omega
in measure. Lusin–Lipschitz admissible sequences have natural limits because, by definition,
(f^{k}(z))
is eventually constant for almost every
z\in\Omega
. Now, let
(u_{m})
be a
p
-bounded sequence of Sobolev maps
u_{m}\colon\Omega\to X_{m}
as in
Theorem

1.1
. We first embed
X_{m}
isometrically into a larger space
X^{\prime}_{m}
with suitable Lipschitz extension properties. This allows us to show that each
u_{m}
is the limit of a Lusin–Lipschitz admissible sequence
(u_{m}^{k})
of maps
u_{m}^{k}\colon\Omega\to X^{\prime}_{m}
in such a way that, for each
k
, the sequence
(u_{m}^{k})
is Lipschitz bounded. In particular, for each
k
, the pointwise ultralimit
u_{\omega}^{k}
is a Lipschitz map into the ultralimit
X^{\prime}_{\omega}
of the pointed sequence
(X^{\prime}_{m})
. The crucial point we prove and use in
Section

6
is that the sequence
(u_{\omega}^{k})
is again Lusin–Lipschitz admissible and, for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
u_{\omega}^{k}
maps a large subset of
\Omega
into
X_{\omega}
. This enables us to define its limit
u_{\omega}
and to show that
u_{\omega}
has image in the subset
X_{\omega}
of
X^{\prime}_{\omega}
, after possibly modifying
u_{\omega}
on a negligible subset. The map
u_{\omega}
is our candidate for the ultralimit of the
p
-bounded sequence
(u_{m})
.

In order to prove that
u_{\omega}
is Sobolev and has the properties stated in
Theorems

1.1
and
1.2
, we use a
geometric realization
of the ultralimit. Namely, we establish the following theorem akin to Gromov’s embedding theorem
[
Gro81
, Section 6]
.

Theorem 1.6
.

Let
(Y^{k})_{k\in I}
be a countable collection of separable metric spaces and
(X_{m},d_{m},p_{m})
a sequence of complete pointed metric spaces. Let
f_{m}^{k}\colon Y^{k}\to X_{m}
be continuous maps for
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
k\in I
, and suppose that the sequence
(f_{m}^{k})
is equi-continuous and equi-bounded for each
k\in I
. Then:

(1)

there exist a complete metric space
Z
, a subsequence
(m_{l})
, and isometric embeddings
\iota_{l}\colon X_{m_{l}}\to Z
such that, for each
k\in I
, the sequence
(\iota_{l}\circ f_{m_{l}}^{k})
converges to a continuous map
f_{\infty}^{k}\colon Y^{k}\to Z
pointwise everywhere and uniformly on compact sets;

(2)

there exists an isometric embedding
\eta\colon C\to X_{\omega}
, where
C=\overline{\bigcup_{k\in I}f_{\infty}^{k}(Y^{k})}
, such that

f_{\omega}^{k}=\eta\circ f_{\infty}^{k}

for every
k\in I
.

Moreover, given subsets
\mathbb{N}\supset\mathcal{F}_{1}\supset\mathcal{F}_{2}\supset\dots
satisfying
\omega(\mathcal{F}_{m})=1
for all
m
, the subsequence
(m_{l})
can be chosen so that
m_{l}\in\mathcal{F}_{N_{l}}
for a strictly increasing sequence
(N_{l})
.

The flexibility provided by this result allows the independent study of the properties stated in
Theorems

1.1
and
1.2
.

Next, we formulate a theorem underlying the stability of Dehn functions, which can be understood as lower semicontinuity of filling areas under ultralimits.

Theorem 1.7
.

Suppose that
(X_{m},d_{m},p_{m})
is a sequence of complete pointed length spaces and
X_{\omega}
the ultralimit. If
(\gamma_{m}\colon\mathbb{S}^{1}\to X_{m})
is a Lipschitz bounded sequence with
\gamma=\lim\nolimits_{\omega}\gamma_{m}
and
\lim\nolimits_{\omega}\mathrm{FillArea}_{X_{m}}(\gamma_{m})<\infty
, then there exists
u\in W^{1,2}(\mathbb{D},X_{\omega})
with

\displaystyle\trace(u)=\gamma\quad\text{and}\quad\mathrm{Area}(u)\leq\lim\nolimits_{\omega}\mathrm{FillArea}_{X_{m}}(\gamma_{m}).

The theorem implies, in particular, that

\mathrm{FillArea}_{X_{\omega}}(\gamma)\leq\lim\nolimits_{\omega}\mathrm{FillArea}_{X_{m}}(\gamma_{m}).

The proof builds upon the ultralimit construction of Sobolev maps and uses, in addition, a mapping cylinder construction similar to
[
Sta21
,
Cre22
]
. A crucial point which we exploit is that the mapping cylinder construction is robust under taking ultralimits.

1.6.
Final remark and structure of the paper

In convex geometry there are several natural definitions
\mu
of area in two-dimensional normed spaces, among which the Holmes–Thompson area
\mu^{ht}
, the Benson or Gromov
\text{mass}^{*}
area
\mu^{*}
, Ivanov’s inscribed Riemannian area
\mu^{i}
, as well as the Hausdorff or Benson-Hausdorff area
\mu^{bh}
; see
[
APT04
]
and
Section

3.6
below. Every definition
\mu
of area gives rise to a parametrized area
\mathrm{Area}^{\mu}(u)
for Sobolev maps with values in a metric space, and the parametrized Hausdorff area
\mathrm{Area}(u)
used in the subsections above is the one corresponding to the Benson-Hausdorff definition
\mu^{bh}
. For simplicity, we stated
Theorems

1.3
,
1.4
,
1.5
and
1.7
for the parametrized Hausdorff area but they can be generalized to other definitions of area as well. Indeed,
Theorems

1.3
and
1.7
hold for any lower semi-continuous area; see

7.1
and
7.2
.
Theorem

1.4
extends to
\mu^{*}
and

. In fact, the statement holds for any lower semicontinuous area for which the isoperimetric constant is
1/(4\pi)
and inner product spaces are the only spaces realizing the isoperimetric constant. The latter assumption is necessary since the conclusion of the theorem fails for the Holmes–Thompson area already in normed planes and
\kappa=0
.
Finally, we also formulate a version of
Theorem

1.5
for all lower semi-continuous area
\mu
, cf.
Theorem

8.1
, where the constant

needs to be replaced by the

of the

).

2.
Preliminaries

We give a quick overview of our basic notation. We need the notion of a
pointed metric space
which is a triple
(X,d_{X},p_{X})
such that
d_{X}\colon X\times X\rightarrow[0,\infty)
is a distance on
X
and
p_{X}\in X
is a point. The open and closed metric balls centered at
y\in X
and of radius
r>0
are denoted by
B_{X}(y,r)
and
\overline{B}_{X}(y,r)
, respectively. Typically it will be clear from the context which space the ball is being considered in and in such a case we drop the subscript
X
. We use the same convention for the distance as well. We will be mainly working with
complete
metric spaces. Given a non-empty set
A\subset X
in a metric space,
\overline{A}
will denote the
closure
of
A
.

Lipschitz mappings between metric spaces play an important role for us so we recall the definition. Given a pair of metric spaces
X
and
Y
, a map
f\colon X\rightarrow Y
is
Lipschitz
if

\displaystyle\sup_{x\neq x^{\prime}}\frac{d(f(x),f(x^{\prime}))}{d(x,x^{\prime})}<\infty.

The supremum is the
Lipschitz constant
of
f
and we denote it by
\LIP(f)
. We say that
f
is
L
-Lipschitz if
\LIP(f)\leq L
. We say that
f
is
L
-bi-Lipschitz
if

\displaystyle L^{-1}d(x,y)\leq d(f(x),f(y))\leq Ld(x,y)\quad\text{for every $x,y\in X$.}

If
L=1
, we say that
f
is
isometric
or an
isometric embedding
.

2.1.
Curves and length distances

A
curve
is a continuous map
\gamma\colon I\rightarrow X
where
I
is an interval or the
unit circle

\mathbb{S}^{1}
. If
I
is an interval, then the length of
\gamma
is defined by

\displaystyle\ell(\gamma)=\sup\left\{\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}d(\gamma(t_{i}),\gamma(t_{i+1}))\colon t_{i}\in I\,\text{and}\,t_{0}<t_{1}<\dots<t_{k}\right\}

and an analogous definition applies in the case
I=\mathbb{S}^{1}
. A
Jordan curve

\Gamma
is the image of a topological embedding
\gamma\colon\mathbb{S}^{1}\rightarrow X
. A curve
\gamma\colon\mathbb{S}^{1}\rightarrow X
is a
weakly monotone parametrization of
\Gamma
if it a uniform limit of homeomorphisms
\mathbb{S}^{1}\rightarrow\Gamma
.

A metric space
X
is a
length space
if, for every
x,y\in X
,

\displaystyle d(x,y)=\inf\{\ell(\gamma)\colon\gamma\colon[a,b]\to X,\gamma(a)=x,\gamma(b)=y\}.

A curve
\gamma\colon[a,b]\to X
is a
geodesic
if
d(\gamma(a),\gamma(b))=\ell(\gamma)
, and a metric space
X
is
geodesic
if every pair
x,y\in X
can be joined with a geodesic.

2.2.
Hausdorff measures

We consider the
n
-dimensional Hausdorff measure on a metric space
X
. We recall that for a set
A\subset X
we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{n}(A)=\sup_{\delta>0}\mathcal{H}^{n}_{\delta}(A),

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{n}_{\delta}(A)=\frac{\omega_{n}}{2^{n}}\inf\left\{\sum_{i}\diam(E_{i})^{n}\colon\text{ $A\subset\bigcup E_{i}$ and $\diam E_{i}<\delta$ for $i\in\mathbb{N}$ }\right\}.

Here
\omega_{n}
is the Lebesgue measure of the
n
-dimensional Euclidean unit ball. This normalization guarantees, in particular, that
\mathcal{H}^{n}
coincides with the
n
-dimensional Lebesgue measure
\mathscr{L}^{n}
in the
n
-dimensional Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

2.3.
Non-principal ultrafilters

We review standard definitions related to a
non-principal ultrafilter

\omega
on
\mathbb{N}
. First, recall that a non-principal ultrafilter is a finitely additive measure
\omega\colon\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{N})\rightarrow\{0,1\}
on the power set
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{N})
of the natural numbers
\mathbb{N}
giving zero measure to finite sets and measure one to
\mathbb{N}
.

Given a bounded sequence
(r_{m})
of real numbers, we say that
t\in\mathbb{R}
is the
ultralimit
if

\omega\left(\left\{m\in\mathbb{N}\colon|r_{m}-t|<\varepsilon\right\}\right)=1\quad\text{for every $\varepsilon>0$.}

The ultralimit of a bounded sequence always exists and is unique, justifying the notation
\lim_{\omega}r_{m}
. For bounded sequences in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
we similarly may take componentwise ultralimits. This allows us to take ultralimits of a sequence
(E_{m})
of non-empty subsets of
\Omega
when
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is bounded: We denote

\displaystyle E_{\omega}=\{\lim\nolimits_{\omega}x_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\colon\text{$x_{m}\in E_{m}$ for every $m\in\mathbb{N}$}\}

and call
E_{\omega}
the ultralimit of
(E_{m})
.

We will later need the following semicontinuity result for the measure under ultralimits.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
(E_{m})
be a sequence of non-empty measurable subsets of a bounded open set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then the ultralimit
E_{\omega}
is a non-empty compact subset of
\overline{\Omega}
and satisfies

\mathscr{L}^{n}(\Omega\cap E_{\omega})\geq\lim\nolimits_{\omega}\mathscr{L}^{n}(E_{m}).

Proof.

Clearly
E_{\omega}
is a non-empty compact subset of
\overline{\Omega}
so it suffices to prove the measure lower bound for
\Omega\cap E_{\omega}
.
Observe that the sequence of characteristic functions
g_{m}=\chi_{E_{m}}
forms a bounded sequence in
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and as
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is the dual of
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, there exists
g_{\omega}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
such that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}hg_{\omega}\,dz=\lim\nolimits_{\omega}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}hg_{m}\,dz\quad\text{for every $h\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})$.}

In particular, if we apply this equality to nonnegative
h
, it immediately follows that
0\leq g_{\omega}\leq 1
almost everywhere. We conclude that

\displaystyle\mathscr{L}^{n}(\Omega\cap\left\{g_{\omega}>0\right\})\geq\lim\nolimits_{\omega}\int_{\Omega}g_{m}\,dz=\lim\nolimits_{\omega}\mathscr{L}^{n}(E_{m}).

If
x\in\Omega\cap\left\{g_{\omega}>0\right\}
is a Lebesgue density point, we claim, in fact, that
x\in E_{\omega}
. This will finish the proof. Let

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{k}=\{m\in\mathbb{N}\colon\mathscr{L}^{n}(E_{m}\cap B(x,k^{-1}))>0\}\cap[k,\infty).

It holds that
\mathcal{G}_{k}\supset\mathcal{G}_{k+1}
and
\omega(\mathcal{G}_{k})=1
for
k\in\mathbb{N}
by construction. To finish, let
x_{m}\in E_{m}
for
m\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\mathcal{G}_{1}
and
x_{m}\in E_{m}\cap B(x,k^{-1})
if
m\in\mathcal{G}_{k}\setminus\mathcal{G}_{k+1}
. Since
x=\lim\nolimits_{\omega}x_{m}
, the proof is complete.
∎

2.4.
Ultralimits of spaces

In this subsection, we consider a sequence
(X_{m},d_{m},p_{m})
of pointed metric spaces. A sequence
(x_{m})
, for
x_{m}\in X_{m}
, is
bounded
if

\displaystyle\sup_{m\in\mathbb{N}}d_{m}(x_{m},p_{m})<\infty.

We say that two bounded sequences
(x_{m})
and
(y_{m})
are
equivalent
if

\lim\nolimits_{\omega}d_{m}(x_{m},y_{m})=0.

The equivalence class of a bounded sequence
(x_{m})
is denoted by
[x_{m}]
and the collection of such equivalence classes we denote by
X_{\omega}
. We refer to
[x_{m}]
as the
ultralimit
of a bounded sequence
(x_{m})
. We at times use the notation
\lim\nolimits_{\omega}x_{m}
to refer to
[x_{m}]
as well.

Observe that

d_{\omega}([x_{m}],[y_{m}])\coloneqq\lim\nolimits_{\omega}d_{m}(x_{m},y_{m})

defines a distance function on
X_{\omega}
. We also denote
p_{\omega}\coloneqq[p_{m}]
. We say that the triple
(X_{\omega},d_{\omega},p_{\omega})
is the
\omega
-ultralimit
, or the
ultralimit
, of the pointed metric spaces
(X_{m},d_{m},p_{m})
. We often use the shorthand notation
X_{\omega}
to refer to
(X_{\omega},d_{\omega},p_{\omega})
. We note that
X_{\omega}
is
always
complete. Moreover, if
(X_{m},d_{m},p_{m})
are length spaces, then
X_{\omega}
is geodesic. When we consider the constant sequence
(X,d,p)
, the ultralimit
X_{\omega}
is called an
ultracompletion
of
X
. In this case, we have the canonical isometric embedding
X\xhookrightarrow{}X_{\omega}
where
x\mapsto[x]
.

In case
(X_{m},d_{m},p_{m})=(X,\lambda_{m}d,p_{m})
for
\lambda_{m}\rightarrow 0^{+}
, the ultralimit
X_{\omega}
is called an
asymptotic cone
of
X
. If instead
p_{m}=p
and
\lambda_{m}\rightarrow\infty
, then
X_{\omega}
is a
tangent (cone)
of
X
(at the point
p
).

We note that if pointed metric spaces
(X_{m},d_{m},p_{m})
converge to a proper
(Z,d,p)
in pointed Gromov–Hausdorff topology, then the ultralimit
X_{\omega}
is isometric to
(Z,d,p)
, cf.
[
AKP24
, Theorem 5.16]
.

2.5.
Ultralimits of mappings

We also consider ultralimits of mappings. Consider sequences of pointed metric spaces
(X_{m},d_{m},p_{m})
and
(Y_{m},d_{m},q_{m})
.

(1)

A sequence
(f_{m}\colon(X_{m},d_{m},p_{m})\to(Y_{m},d_{m},q_{m}))
is
equi-continuous on bounded sets
if for every
R>0
and
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
\delta>0
such that
d_{m}(x,y)<\delta
implies
d_{m}(f_{m}(x),f_{m}(y))<\varepsilon
whenever
x,y\in B_{X_{m}}(p_{m},R)
and
m\in\mathbb{N}
. In case we may take
R=\infty
, the sequence is
equi-continuous
.

(2)

A sequence
(f_{m}\colon(X_{m},d_{m},p_{m})\to(Y_{m},d_{m},q_{m}))
is
equi-bounded on bounded sets
if for every
R>0
, there exists
\lambda>0
such that
f_{m}(\overline{B}_{X_{m}}(p_{m},R))\subset\overline{B}_{Y_{m}}(q_{m},\lambda)
for every
m\in\mathbb{N}
. In case we may take
R=\infty
, the sequence is
equi-bounded
.

Now, in case a sequence
(f_{m}\colon(X_{m},d_{m},p_{m})\to(Y_{m},d_{m},q_{m}))
is equi-continuous and equi-bounded on bounded sets, there exists a
pointwise ultralimit

f_{\omega}\colon X_{\omega}\to Y_{\omega}
defined by
f_{\omega}(\lim_{\omega}x_{m})=\lim_{\omega}f_{m}(x_{m})
(or equivalently
f_{\omega}([x_{m}])=[f_{m}(x_{m})]
). The construction of
f_{\omega}
implies that
f_{\omega}
maps bounded sets to bounded sets and it has the same modulus of continuity as
(f_{m})
. Moreover, if
(X_{m},d_{m},p_{m})=(X,d,p)
, then
f_{\omega}(x)=[f_{m}(x)]
holds for
x\in X
under the canonical identification
X\subset X_{\omega}
given by
x\mapsto[x]
.

2.6.
Injective metric spaces

We will need injective metric spaces in the sequel. Recall that a metric space
Y
is
injective
if for every subset of a metric space
E\subset Z
, any
1
-Lipschitz map
E\to Y
extends to a
1
-Lipschitz map
Z\to Y
. In particular, every injective metric space is complete. Also recall that via the Kuratowski embedding, every metric space
X
embeds into the injective Banach space
\ell^{\infty}(X)
isometrically; here
\ell^{\infty}(X)
is the space of bounded functions
X\to\mathbb{R}
equipped with the supremum norm. The injectivity of
\ell^{\infty}(X)
is easy to see using componentwise McShane extension. Recall that the
\lambda
-Lipschitz McShane extension
\hat{f}\colon Z\to\mathbb{R}
of a
\lambda
-Lipschitz
f\colon Z\to\mathbb{R}
is defined by
\hat{f}(z)=\inf\{f(y)+\lambda d(y,z)\colon y\in E\}
. Then the truncated McShane extension
\bar{f}\colon Z\to\mathbb{R}
, where
\bar{f}(z)=\max\{\sup_{y\in E}|f(y)|,\hat{f}(z)\}
, leads to a
\lambda
-Lipschitz extension
\bar{f}\colon Z\to\mathbb{R}
with
\sup_{z\in Z}|\bar{f}(z)|=\sup_{z\in E}|f(z)|\in[0,\infty]
. This implies the standard fact that any closed ball in any injective space is injective.

An
injective hull

Y
of a metric space
X
is an injective metric space equipped with an isometric embedding
e\colon X\to Y
that is minimal in an appropriate sense among injective spaces containing
X
. Injective hulls were constructed by Isbell in
[
Isb64
]
.

3.
Metric valued Sobolev mappings

We briefly review metric valued Sobolev theory for domains in Euclidean spaces. There exist several equivalent definitions and we refer to the monograph
[
HKST15
]
and the recent manuscripts
[
LW17a
,
CE24
]
for details. See also
[
KS93
,
Haj96
,
Sha00
,
HKST01
]
. We will use the approach due to Reshetnyak
[
Res97
]
based on post composition by Lipschitz functions.

3.1.
Sobolev spaces due to Reshetnyak

Let
X
be a complete metric space,
p\in(1,\infty)
and let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a bounded open set. We denote by
L^{p}(\Omega,X)
the set of measurable and essentially separably valued
u\colon\Omega\to X
such that
d(u,p_{X})\in L^{p}(\Omega)
for some (and thus any)
p_{X}\in X
, the classical Lebesgue space of
p
-integrable functions. The distance on
L^{p}(\Omega,X)
is defined by

\displaystyle\|d(u,v)\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}=\left(\int_{\Omega}(d(u,v))^{p}\,dz\right)^{\frac{1}{p}},\quad\text{for $u,v\in L^{p}(\Omega,X)$.}

Since
X
is complete, the space
L^{p}(\Omega,X)
becomes a complete metric space.

A map
u\in L^{p}(\Omega,X)
belongs to the Sobolev space
u\in W^{1,p}(\Omega,X)
if there exists an
L^{p}(\Omega)
-integrable
\rho\colon\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that for every
1
-Lipschitz
\varphi\colon X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, it holds that
x\mapsto\varphi\circ u(x)
belongs to
W^{1,p}(\Omega)
in the classical sense and the distributional gradient satisfies
|\nabla(\varphi\circ u)|\leq\rho
almost everywhere. The
Reshetnyak
p
-energy
of
u\in W^{1,p}(\Omega,X)
is defined by

\displaystyle E_{+}^{p}(u)=\inf\left\{\|\rho\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}^{p}\colon\rho\text{ is as above}\right\}.

The
p
-energy is extended as
\infty
to all of
L^{p}(\Omega,X)
after which
E_{+}^{p}\colon L^{p}(\Omega,X)\rightarrow[0,\infty]
becomes lower semi-continuous with respect to
L^{p}(\Omega,X)
-convergence. In case
E_{+}^{p}(u)<\infty
, standard arguments show that the infimum above is a minimum and the minimizer is unique almost everywhere. We denote the minimizer by
|\nabla u|
. For real-valued functions,
|\nabla u|
is the modulus of the weak differential.

3.2.
Lower semi-continuous energies

In the sequel, we consider lower semi-continuous energies. We say that an energy functional
E^{p}
is
lower semi-continuous
if for every complete metric space
X
and bounded Lipschitz domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, any
p
-bounded
(u_{m}\colon\Omega\to X)
with
\lim_{m\to\infty}\|d(u_{m},u)\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}=0
satisfies

\displaystyle\liminf_{m\to\infty}E^{p}(u_{m})\geq E^{p}(u).

The standard energies such as the Korevaar–Schoen energy, the Reshetnayk energy, and the Hajłasz energy are lower semi-continuous in this sense, cf.
[
LW17a
, Section 5]
. We also recall that the Newton–Sobolev energy coincides with the Reshetnyak energy on all domains
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and all of these approaches define comparable energies for bounded Lipschitz domains
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, cf.
[
HKST15
, Theorems 6.1.17 and 7.1.20 and Section 10]
.

3.3.
Hajłasz gradients

When
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a bounded Lipschitz domain, Reshetnyak’s definition of a Sobolev map above is equivalent to that of a Hajłasz–Sobolev map (see
[
Haj96
]
or
[
HKST15
, Section 10]
) and thus, for every
u\in W^{1,p}(\Omega,X)
, there exists a nonnegative function
g\in L^{p}(\Omega)
and a negligible set
N\subset\Omega
such that

(3.1)

\displaystyle d(u(x),u(y))\leq(g(x)+g(y))d(x,y)\quad\text{for every $x,y\in\Omega\setminus N$.}

We call
g
a
Hajłasz gradient
of
u
. For convenience sake, we will assume that our Hajłasz gradients are set to infinity in the set where (
3.1
) would fail otherwise. Thus (
3.1
) holds for
every
pair
x,y\in\Omega
. It holds that for
u\in W^{1,p}(\Omega,X)
, there exists a Hajłasz gradient
g
of
u
satisfying

(3.2)

\|g\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}^{p}\leq CE^{p}_{+}(u)\quad\text{for every $u\in W^{1,p}(\Omega,X)$}

for a constant
C
depending only on
\Omega
and
p
.

3.4.
Trace theory

When
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a bounded Lipschitz domain, the
trace
of
u\in W^{1,p}(\Omega,X)
is a mapping
h\in L^{p}(\partial\Omega,X)
, the definition of
L^{p}(\partial\Omega,X)
being analogous to
L^{p}(\Omega,X)
above, such that

\limsup_{r\rightarrow 0^{+}}\mathchoice{\mathop{\kern 1.99997pt\vrule width=6.00006pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.49997pt\kern-8.00003pt\intop}\nolimits_{\kern-3.68748ptB(x,r)\cap\Omega}}{\mathop{\kern 1.00006pt\vrule width=5.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.59996pt\kern-6.00006pt\intop}\nolimits_{B(x,r)\cap\Omega}}{\mathop{\kern 1.00006pt\vrule width=5.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.59996pt\kern-6.00006pt\intop}\nolimits_{B(x,r)\cap\Omega}}{\mathop{\kern 1.00006pt\vrule width=5.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.59996pt\kern-6.00006pt\intop}\nolimits_{B(x,r)\cap\Omega}}d(u(z),h(x))\,dz=0\quad\text{for $\mathcal{H}^{n-1}$-a.e. $x\in\partial\Omega$,}

where
\mathchoice{\mathop{\kern 1.99997pt\vrule width=6.00006pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.49997pt\kern-8.00003pt\intop}\nolimits_{\kern-3.68748ptE}}{\mathop{\kern 1.00006pt\vrule width=5.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.59996pt\kern-6.00006pt\intop}\nolimits_{E}}{\mathop{\kern 1.00006pt\vrule width=5.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.59996pt\kern-6.00006pt\intop}\nolimits_{E}}{\mathop{\kern 1.00006pt\vrule width=5.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.59996pt\kern-6.00006pt\intop}\nolimits_{E}}h\,dz
refers to the integral average over a set
E
. It is clear that if
h
exists, it is unique and thus we use the notation
\mathrm{tr}(u)=h
. The trace above is equivalent to the Korevaar–Schoen definition
[
KS93
, Section 1.12]
, cf.
[
GHWX22
, Section 3]
. We state some basic properties of traces. We note that the uniqueness implies the composition rules:
\Phi\circ\trace(u)(x)=\trace(\Phi\circ u)(x)
and
d(\trace(u)(x),\trace(v)(x))=\trace(d(u,v))(x)
for almost every
x\in\partial\Omega
for
u,v\in W^{1,p}(\Omega,X)
and Lipschitz
\Phi\colon X\to Y
. The trace
\trace\colon W^{1,p}(\Omega,X)\to L^{p}(\partial\Omega,X)
for
X=(X,d,p_{X})
is bounded in the sense that

\|d(\trace(u),p_{X})\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\leq C\left(\|d(u,p_{X})\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}^{p}+E_{+}^{p}(u)\right)^{1/p}

for some
C=C(p,\Omega)
. Finally,

\lim_{m\to\infty}\|d_{m}(\trace(u_{m}),\trace(v_{m}))\|_{L^{p}(\partial\Omega)}=0

if
\lim_{m\to\infty}\|d_{m}(u_{m},v_{m})\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}=0
and
(u_{m}\colon\Omega\to X_{m})
and
(v_{m}\colon\Omega\to X_{m})
are
p
-bounded. This follows from the
p
-boundedness of
(d(u_{m},v_{m}))
and weak continuity of
\trace\colon W^{1,p}(\Omega)\to L^{p}(\partial\Omega)
.

3.5.
The Hausdorff volume and the area formula

We next define a notion of parametrized Hausdorff volume for Sobolev mappings. To this end, let
u\in W^{1,p}(\Omega,X)
for
p\in(1,\infty)
. Such a
u
has an
approximate metric derivative
at almost every
z\in\Omega
in the following sense; see
[
Kar07
,
LW17a
]
. There exists a unique seminorm in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, denoted
\mathrm{md}_{z}(u)
, such that

\displaystyle\mathrm{ap}\lim_{z^{\prime}\rightarrow z}\frac{d(u(z),u(z^{\prime}))-\mathrm{md}_{z}(u)(z^{\prime}-z)}{|z^{\prime}-z|}=0

where
|\cdot|
is the Euclidean norm and
\mathrm{ap}\lim
refers to the approximate limit; see
[
Fed69
, 2.9.11]
.

Definition 3.1
.

When
p\in[n,\infty)
and
u\in W^{1,p}(\Omega,X)
, the parametrized Hausdorff volume of a map
u\in W^{1,p}(\Omega,X)
is

\displaystyle\mathrm{Vol}(u)=\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{J}(\mathrm{md}_{z}(u))\,dz,

where the Jacobian
\mathbf{J}(s)
of a seminorm
s
is the
n
-dimensional Hausdorff measure of the Euclidean unit cube in
(\mathbb{R}^{n},s)
if
s
is a norm and zero otherwise. When the ambient dimension
n
is two, we instead use the notation
\mathrm{Area}(u)
and refer to it as parametrized Hausdorff area.

Recall that the
n
-dimensional Hausdorff measure is normalized so that the
n
-dimensional Hausdorff measure of the standard Euclidean norm and Lebesgue measure coincide. Thus an equivalent definition of the Jacobian is
\omega_{n}/\mathscr{L}^{n}(\{s\leq 1\})
where
\omega_{n}
is the Lebesgue measure of the
n
-dimensional ball and
\{s\leq 1\}
is the unit ball defined by the norm
s
.

If
u\in W^{1,p}(\Omega,X)
satisfies Lusin’s Property (
N
), e.g. when
p>n

[
Vod00
]
, and thus sends sets of Lebesgue measure zero to sets of Hausdorff
n
-dimensional measure zero, then

\displaystyle\mathrm{Vol}(u)=\int_{X}\sharp(\left\{z\colon u(z)=x\right\})\,d\mathcal{H}^{n}(x)

by the area formula
[
Kar07
, Theorem 3.2]
. If
u
is also injective, it holds that
\mathrm{Vol}(u)=\mathcal{H}^{n}(u(\Omega))
. Nevertheless, even if
p=n
, there exists a set
N\subset\Omega
of Lebesgue measure zero such that
u|_{\Omega\setminus N}
satisfies Lusin’s Condition (
N
) and

(3.3)

\displaystyle\int_{E}\mathbf{J}(\mathrm{md}_{z}(u))\,dz=\int_{X\setminus u(N)}\sharp(\left\{z\in E\colon u(z)=x\right\})\,d\mathcal{H}^{n}(x)

for Lebesgue measurable
E\subset\Omega
. It follows from (
3.3
) that
u|_{\{\mathbf{J}(\mathrm{md}(u))>0\}\setminus N}
satisfies Lusin’s Conditions
(N)
and
(N^{-1})
.

3.6.
Lower semi-continuous volumes

Important examples of volumes arise in convex geometry
[
APT04
]
: a
volume
is a function
\mu
which assigns to every
n
-dimensional normed space
V
a norm
\mu_{V}
on the exterior power
\Lambda^{n}V
in such a way that
\mu_{V}
is induced by the Lebesgue measure if
V
is Euclidean and such that for every linear
1
-Lipschitz map
T\colon V\to W
between
n
-dimensional normed spaces, the induced map
T_{*}\colon\Lambda^{n}V\to\Lambda^{n}W
is
1
-Lipschitz. Following
[
LW17a
]
, we define the Jacobian
\mathbf{J}^{\mu}
for a seminorm
s
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
by

\mathbf{J}^{\mu}_{n}(s)=\left\{\begin{split}&\mu_{(\mathbb{R}^{n},s)}(e_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge e_{n}),\quad&&\text{if $s$ is a norm}\\
&0,\quad&&\text{if $s$ is a seminorm,}\end{split}\right.

where
e_{1},\dots,e_{n}
is the standard basis in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The associated parametrized volume of a Sobolev map
u\in W^{1,p}(\Omega,X)
, for
p\geq n
, is

\displaystyle\mathrm{Vol}^{\mu}(u)\coloneqq\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{J}^{\mu}_{n}(\mathrm{md}_{z}(u))\,dz.

When
n=2
, we instead write
\mathrm{Area}^{\mu}(u)
and
\mathbf{J}^{\mu}
.

We say that a volume functional
\mathrm{Vol}
is
lower semi-continuous
if for
p\in[n,\infty]
and every complete metric space
X
, any bounded domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and any
p
-bounded sequence
(u_{m}\colon\Omega\to X)
with
\lim_{m\to\infty}\|d(u_{m},u)\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}=0
, it holds that

\displaystyle\liminf_{m\to\infty}\mathrm{Vol}(u_{m})\geq\mathrm{Vol}(u).

Note that the lower semicontinuity for
p>n
follows from the lower semicontinuity in the case
p=n
.

The parametrized volume
\mathrm{Vol}^{\mu}
is lower semicontinuous if (and only if)
\mu
induces quasi-convex
n
-volume densities; see
[
LW17a
, Definition 2.6 and Corollary 5.8]
. We call
\mu

lower semi-continuous
if
\mathrm{Vol}^{\mu}
is lower semi-continuous in the sense above. When
n=2
, we instead use the term
area
. In particular, the parametrized Hausdorff area is lower semi-continuous by
[
BI12
, Theorem 1]
.

We give additional comments on the areas and refer to
[
LW17b
, Section 2.4]
for further discussion. We will later need another notion of area, called the
Riemann inscribed area
(due to Ivanov
[
Iva08
]
): The
\mu^{i}
-Jacobian of a norm
s
on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
satisfies

\displaystyle\mathbf{J}^{\mu^{i}}(s)=\frac{\pi}{\mathscr{L}^{2}(L)}

where
\mathscr{L}^{2}(L)
is the Lebesgue measure of the ellipse of maximal area contained inside the unit ball
\{s\leq 1\}
. That is, the Lebesgue area of the
John ellipse
of
\{s\leq 1\}
. We note that

\displaystyle\frac{\pi}{4}\mathbf{J}^{\mu^{i}}(s)\leq\mathbf{J}(s)\leq\mathbf{J}^{\mu^{i}}(s)\quad\text{for every seminorm in $s$.}

In fact, the right-hand side is an equality if and only if
s
is not a norm or it is induced by an inner product.

In addition to
\mu^{i}
, other prominent lower semi-continuous areas include the Holmes–Thompson area
\mu^{ht}
, Benson’s area
\mu^{*}
(also known as Gromov’s
\text{mass}^{*}
), and the Busemann–Hausdorff area
\mu^{bh}
. The latter one defines the same Jacobian as the parametrized Hausdorff area. The main inequalities are
\mu^{bh}\geq\mu^{ht}
and
\mu^{*}\geq\mu^{ht}
both of which are strict outside inner product spaces. We remark that the Jacobians of every notion of area
\mu
satisfies
2^{-1}\mathbf{J}^{\mu^{i}}\leq\mathbf{J}^{\mu}\leq\mathbf{J}^{\mu^{i}}
.

Proof of

We recall the statement of the embedding theorem. Below
\omega

\mathbb{N}
.
See
1.6

Before proceeding with the proof, we fix notation. We lose no generality in supposing that
I=\mathbb{N}
. Let
(\varepsilon_{N})
be a decreasing sequence of positive numbers with

, and let

be a countable dense set for

. Let also

be the ultralimit of

and let

(f_{m}^{k}\colon Y^{k}\to X_{m})

.

The construction of the space

[

. However, to guarantee compatibility with the ultralimit requires further care. To begin the proof, we will consider a decreasing family of sets
\mathcal{G}_{N}
with
\omega(\mathcal{G}_{N})=1
for

with the following requirements:

if and only if

(1)

for

\displaystyle|d_{m}(f_{m}^{k}(y_{j}^{k}),f_{m}^{k^{\prime}}(y_{j^{\prime}}^{k^{\prime}}))-d_{\omega}(f_{\omega}^{k}(y_{j}^{k}),f_{\omega}^{k^{\prime}}(y_{j^{\prime}}^{k^{\prime}}))|<\varepsilon_{N}.

N\leq m
.

We have
\mathcal{G}_{N+1}\subset\mathcal{G}_{N}
for

and

\omega

We now proceed with the construction of the sequence
(m_{l})
. To this end, for each

, let

be the largest integer such that

. Next, let
m_{1}
be an element in
\mathcal{G}_{1}
and let
N_{1}\coloneqq N(m_{1})
. Suppose that
m_{l-1}
and
N_{l-1}
have been defined for some

and then consider any element
m_{l}\in\mathcal{G}_{m_{l-1}+1}
, and denote
N_{l}=N(m_{l})
. It follows that
(N_{l})
and
(m_{l})
are strictly increasing and
N_{l}\leq m_{l}
.

We are ready to construct the space
Z
via a direct limit construction. To this end, let
Z^{1}=X_{m_{1}}
and let
d^{1}\coloneqq d_{m_{1}}
. Suppose that
Z^{l-1}
and
d^{l-1}
have been defined for some

. Next, consider the disjoint union

and define a distance

as follows. In

and on

d_{m_{l}}

where the minimum is taken over

and

\displaystyle|d_{m_{l}}(f_{m_{l}}^{k}(y^{k}_{j}),f_{m_{l}}(y^{k^{\prime}}_{j^{\prime}}))-d_{m_{l-1}}(f_{m_{l-1}}^{k}(y^{k}_{j}),f_{m_{l-1}}(y^{k^{\prime}}_{j^{\prime}}))|<(\varepsilon_{N_{l-1}}+\varepsilon_{N_{l}})

when

easily implies the triangle inequality for

. It follows that

is an actual distance on

with the desired properties. Next, we define

as the completion of the direct limit of the spaces

and we denote the distance on

by

for simplicity. We also suppress the notation for the isometric embeddings

.

Let

. We claim that for

, the sequence

is a Cauchy sequence, whose limit we denote by

. This defines implicitly a map

. We first consider the case

for some

. To this end, let

such that

. When

, we have that

\displaystyle d(f_{m_{l}}^{k}(y_{j}^{k}),f_{m_{l+1}}^{k}(y_{j}^{k}))=(\varepsilon_{N_{l}}+\varepsilon_{N_{l+1}})

\displaystyle\sum_{l=l_{1}}^{l_{2}}d(f_{m_{l}}^{k}(y_{j}^{k}),f_{m_{l+1}}^{k}(y_{j}^{k}))\leq 2\sum_{l=N_{l_{1}}}^{\infty}\varepsilon_{l}\quad\text{for $l_{2}\geq l_{1}\geq l_{0}$}.

Next, given

\displaystyle d(f_{\infty}^{k}(y_{j}^{k}),f_{\infty}^{k^{\prime}}(y_{j^{\prime}}^{k^{\prime}}))=\lim_{l\rightarrow\infty}d(f_{m_{l}}^{k}(y_{j}^{k}),f_{m_{l}}^{k^{\prime}}(y_{j^{\prime}}^{k^{\prime}}))=d_{\omega}(f_{\omega}^{k}(y_{j}^{k}),f_{\omega}^{k^{\prime}}(y_{j^{\prime}}^{k^{\prime}})).

\displaystyle d(f_{\infty}^{k}(y),f_{\infty}^{k^{\prime}}(y^{\prime}))=d_{\omega}(f_{\omega}^{k}(y),f_{\omega}^{k^{\prime}}(y^{\prime}))\quad\text{for $(y,y^{\prime})\in Y^{k}\times Y^{k^{\prime}}$ and $k,k^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}$.}

such that

for

. For each

implies that the pointwise convergence of

is as above, we may replace

.

5.
Lusin–Lipschitz admissible sequences

In this section, we study quantitative Lusin–Lipschitz admissible sequences, defined below. They will be a crucial ingredient in the proofs of the ultralimit theorems. Let
X
be a complete metric space and
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
a bounded Lipschitz domain.

Definition 5.1
.

A sequence of Lipschitz maps
f^{k}\colon\Omega\to X
is called Lusin–Lipschitz admissible if there exist non-empty measurable subsets
E^{1}\subset E^{2}\subset\dots\subset\Omega
such that
\mathscr{L}^{n}(\Omega\setminus E^{k})\to 0
as
k
tends to infinity and
f^{k+1}|_{E^{k}}=f^{k}|_{E^{k}}
for every
k
.

If
\lambda_{k},c_{k}>0
with
c_{k}\to 0
are such that
f^{k}
is
\lambda_{k}
-Lipschitz and
\mathscr{L}^{n}(\Omega\setminus E^{k})\leq c_{k}
for every
k
, we call
\lambda_{k}
and
c_{k}
the parameters of the Lusin–Lipschitz admissible sequence
(f^{k})
. Clearly, for almost every
z\in\Omega
, the sequence
(f^{k}(z))
is eventually constant and, in particular, the pointwise limit
f(z)=\lim_{k\to\infty}f^{k}(z)
is an almost everywhere defined measurable and essentially separably valued map from
\Omega
to
X
. This observation will be at the basis of the ultralimit construction.

The following proposition shows that a Lusin–Lipschitz admissible sequence
(f^{k})
converges to the pointwise limit
f
in
L^{p}
quantitatively in the parameters
\lambda_{k}
and
c_{k}
.

Proposition 5.2
.

Given
C,p>1
there exist
C^{\prime},\delta>0
with the following properties. For every Lusin–Lipschitz admissible sequence of maps
f^{k}\colon\Omega\to X
with parameters
\lambda_{k}=C\cdot 2^{k}
and
c_{k}=\mathscr{L}^{n}(\Omega)\cdot 2^{-kp}
and limit map
f
, the real-valued function
h_{k}=d(f,f^{k})
satisfies

\|h_{k}\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\leq 2^{-k\delta}C^{\prime}

for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

The proof will show that
\delta
depends on
p
and
n
and
C^{\prime}
depends on
p
,
n
,
C
, and
\Omega
.

Proof.

Let
C,p>1
and for
k\in\mathbb{N}
set
\lambda_{k}=C\cdot 2^{k}
and
c_{k}=\mathscr{L}^{n}(\Omega)\cdot 2^{-kp}
. Suppose
(f^{k})
is a Lusin–Lipschitz admissible sequence of maps
f^{k}\colon\Omega\to X
with parameters
\lambda_{k}
and
c_{k}
. That is,
f^{k}
is
\lambda_{k}
-Lipschitz and there exist non-empty measurable subsets
E^{1}\subset E^{2}\subset\dots\subset\Omega
such that
\mathscr{L}^{n}(\Omega\setminus E^{k})\leq c_{k}
and
f^{k+1}|_{E^{k}}=f^{k}|_{E^{k}}
for every
k
. The limit
f
of the sequence
(f^{k})
satisfies
f|_{E^{k}}=f^{k}|_{E^{k}}
for every
k
.

Fix
k\in\mathbb{N}
and abbreviate
h=h_{k}
. We claim that
h\in W^{1,q}(\Omega)
with Sobolev norm

\|h\|_{W^{1,q}(\Omega)}\leq 2^{\left(1-\frac{p}{q}\right)k}D

for every
1<q<p
, where
D
depends on
p
,
q
,
C
,
n
, and
\diam(\Omega)
. For this, observe first that
h|_{E^{k}}=0
and, for
i>k
, we have

h|_{E^{i}}=d(f^{i},f^{k})|_{E^{i}}\leq(\lambda_{i}+\lambda_{k})\cdot\diam(\Omega)\leq 2^{i+1}C\cdot\diam(\Omega).

From this, we deduce that

\begin{split}\int_{\Omega}h^{q}(z)\,dz&=\sum_{i=k+1}^{\infty}\int_{E^{i}\setminus E^{i-1}}h^{q}(z)\,dz\\
&\leq(2C\diam(\Omega))^{q}\cdot\sum_{i=k+1}^{\infty}2^{qi}\cdot\mathscr{L}^{n}(\Omega\setminus E^{i-1})\\
&\leq 2^{(q-p)k}C_{0}\end{split}

for some constant
C_{0}
depending only on
p,q,C
,
n
, and
\diam(\Omega)
. Now, for
l\geq k
, consider the Lipschitz function
g_{l}=d(f^{l},f^{k})
. Since

|g_{l}-h_{k}|=|d(f^{l},f^{k})-d(f^{k},f)|\leq h_{l},

it follows from the above that
g_{l}
converges to
h_{k}
in
L^{q}
. Notice that
g_{l}|_{E^{k}}=0
and
g_{l}
is
2\lambda_{i}
-Lipschitz on
E^{i}
for every
i\geq l
. In particular,
|\nabla g_{l}|=0
almost everywhere on
E^{k}
and
|\nabla g_{l}|\leq 2\lambda_{i}
almost everywhere on
E^{i}
. Therefore, we obtain almost as above

\int_{\Omega}|\nabla g_{l}|^{q}\,dz\leq(2C)^{q}\sum_{i=k+1}^{\infty}2^{qi}\mathscr{L}^{n}(\Omega\setminus E^{i-1})\leq 2^{(q-p)k}C_{1}

for some constant
C_{1}
only depending on
p
,
q
,
n
, and
C
. Thus, the
L^{q}
-norm of
|\nabla g_{l}|
is bounded independently of
l
and since
g_{l}
converges in
L^{q}
to
h
, it follows that
h\in W^{1,q}(\Omega)
and

\int_{\Omega}|\nabla h|^{q}\,dz\leq 2^{(q-p)k}C_{1}.

This yields the claim.

Finally, consider
1<q<\min\{p,n\}
such that
\frac{qn}{n-q}>p
. By the Sobolev embedding theorem and Hölder’s inequality, there exists a constant
D^{\prime}
only depending on
p,q,n
, and
\Omega
such that

\|h\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\leq D^{\prime}\|h\|_{W^{1,q}(\Omega)}\leq 2^{\left(1-\frac{p}{q}\right)k}D^{\prime}D.

This concludes the proof with
\delta=\frac{p}{q}-1
and
C^{\prime}=D^{\prime}D
.
∎

Proposition 5.3
.

Given
p>1
and
M\geq 1
, there exists
C\geq 1
, depending only on
M
,
\Omega
and
p
, with the following property. Let
X
be an injective metric space,
p_{X}\in X
, and let
f\in W^{1,p}(\Omega,X)
satisfy

\int_{\Omega}d^{p}(f(z),p_{X})\,dz+E_{+}^{p}(f)\leq M.

Then
f
is the limit of a Lusin–Lipschitz admissible sequence
(f^{k})
with parameters
\lambda_{k}=C\cdot 2^{k}
and
c_{k}=\mathscr{L}^{n}(\Omega)\cdot 2^{-kp}
and moreover satisfying
f^{k}(\Omega)\subset\overline{B}_{X}(p_{X},\lambda_{k})
for every
k
.

The condition that
X
be an injective metric space can be weakened. It is enough to assume that
X
is Lipschitz
(n-1)
-connected; see e.g.
[
LS05
]
or
[
HS14
]
for related extension constructions. In that case, the constant
C
also depends on the data of the Lipschitz connectivity.

Proof.

Let
p_{X}
and
f
be as in the statement of the proposition. Consider a Hajłasz gradient
g
of
f
satisfying

\displaystyle\|g\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}^{p}\leq C^{\prime}E^{p}_{+}(g).

Recall that we may choose
C^{\prime}\geq 1
to depend only on
\Omega
and
p
by (
3.2
).

Consider the sublevel sets

\displaystyle A_{k}

\displaystyle=\left\{g\leq 2^{k}\right\}\quad\text{and}\quad B_{k}=\left\{d(f,p_{X})\leq 2^{k}\right\}

for
k\geq 1
. We deduce from Chebyshev inequality that

\displaystyle\mathscr{L}^{n}(\Omega\setminus A_{k})

\displaystyle\leq C^{\prime}\frac{E^{p}_{+}(u)}{2^{kp}}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathscr{L}^{n}(\Omega\setminus B_{k})\leq\frac{\|d(f,p_{X})\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}^{p}}{2^{kp}}.

Let
k_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
be so large that
C^{\prime}M/2^{k_{0}p}<\mathscr{L}^{n}(\Omega)
. Then, for
k\geq 1
, the set
E^{k}=A_{k+k_{0}}\cap B_{k+k_{0}}
satisfies

\displaystyle\mathscr{L}^{n}(\Omega\setminus E^{k})\leq\frac{C^{\prime}M}{2^{k_{0}p}}\frac{1}{2^{kp}}<\frac{\mathscr{L}^{n}(\Omega)}{2^{kp}}

and, in particular,
E^{k}
is non-empty. Moreover, by (
3.1
), the restriction
f|_{E^{k}}
is
C2^{k}
-Lipschitz with
C=2^{k_{0}+1}
, and
f(E^{k})\subset\overline{B}_{X}(p_{X},C2^{k})
.

Now as
X
is injective, we may apply the injectivity of
\overline{B}_{X}(p_{X},C2^{k})
to find a
\lambda_{k}
-Lipschitz extension
f^{k}
of
f|_{E^{k}}
satisfying
f^{k}(\Omega)\subset\overline{B}_{X}(p_{X},C2^{k})
. The sequence
(f^{k})
has the desired properties.
∎

Below
\omega

Lemma 6.2

The ultralimit

belongs to

.

Given a lower semi-continuous notion of an energy and volume (if

The claim follows.
∎

The next lemma implies that the map

Lemma 6.3
.

If

and ultralimit

and

\|d_{\omega}(\trace(u_{\omega}),\trace(v_{\omega}))\|_{L^{p}(\partial\Omega)}=\lim\nolimits_{\omega}\|d_{m}(\trace(u_{m}),\trace(v_{m}))\|_{L^{p}(\partial\Omega)}.

This immediately implies the following key properties.

If
(u_{m})
is Lipschitz bounded, then

agrees (almost everywhere) with the pointwise ultralimit. Moreover, if

is a Lipschitz bounded sequence of maps

The first part is immediate from

has an extension

Proof of

.

The proof uses similar ideas as the one of

. Indeed, by

1.6
, there exist a complete metric space

7.1.
Proof of
Theorem

1.7

We formulate the following general version of
Theorem

1.7
.

Theorem 7.1
.

Let
\mu
be a lower semi-continuous area. Suppose that
(X_{m},d_{m},p_{m})
is a sequence of complete pointed length spaces and
X_{\omega}
the ultralimit. If
(\gamma_{m}\colon\mathbb{S}^{1}\to X_{m})
is a Lipschitz bounded sequence with
\gamma=\lim\nolimits_{\omega}\gamma_{m}
and
\lim\nolimits_{\omega}\mathrm{FillArea}^{\mu}_{X_{m}}(\gamma_{m})<\infty
, then there exists

with

\displaystyle\trace(u)=\gamma\quad\text{and}\quad\mathrm{Area}^{\mu}(u)\leq\lim\nolimits_{\omega}\mathrm{FillArea}^{\mu}_{X_{m}}(\gamma_{m}).

Proof.

Let
L>\lim\nolimits_{\omega}\LIP(\gamma_{m})
. We consider the subset
\mathcal{G}\subset\mathbb{N}
defined by the properties
d(p_{m},\gamma_{m}(\mathbb{S}^{1}))\leq 1+d(p_{\omega},\gamma(\mathbb{S}^{1}))
,
\mathrm{FillArea}^{\mu}(\gamma_{m})\leq 1+\lim\nolimits_{\omega}\mathrm{FillArea}^{\mu}(\gamma_{m})
, and
\LIP(\gamma_{m})\leq L
. It holds that
\omega(\mathcal{G})=1
. For

, we may assume that
X_{m}
is a singleton.

Consider
u_{m}\in W^{1,2}(\mathbb{D},X_{m})
such that
\mathrm{tr}(u_{m})=\gamma_{m}
, and

(7.1)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Area}^{\mu}(u_{m})\leq\mathrm{FillArea}^{\mu}_{X_{m}}(\gamma_{m})+\frac{1}{m}.

We lift the sequence
(u_{m})
to the
mapping cylinders

Y_{m}
of
\gamma_{m}
next. To this end, equip the cylinder
Z=[0,3L]\times\mathbb{S}^{1}(L)\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
with the intrinsic length distance. Let
Y_{m}
be the quotient space obtained by gluing
Z
to
X_{m}
such that
\gamma_{m}(s)
is identified with
(0,Ls)
for each

and equip

with the quotient metric.

Observe that by the choice of

, the natural inclusion

is isometric and the natural map
\iota_{m}\colon Z\rightarrow Y_{m}
is
1
-Lipschitz and its restriction to
(0,3L]\times\mathbb{S}^{1}(L)
is injective and a local isometry; in fact, the restriction of
\iota_{m}
to the
L
-neighbourhood

of the top

is an isometric embedding. Also, the projection map
P_{m}\colon Y_{m}\to X_{m}
fixing

pointwise and sending

to
\gamma_{m}(L^{-1}t)
for

is
1
-Lipschitz.

By the above, we may consider
u_{m}
as an element of
W^{1,2}(\mathbb{D},Y_{m})
. By standard gluing properties of Sobolev maps
[
LW16
, Section 2.2]
, we obtain a
W^{1,2}(\mathbb{D},Y_{m})
-mapping

v_{m}(z)=\left\{\begin{split}&u_{m}(2z),\quad&&\text{if $|z|<2^{-1},$}\\
&\left(3L(2|z|-1),L\frac{z}{|z|}\right),\quad&&\text{if $2^{-1}\leq|z|<1$.}\end{split}\right.

It holds that

\displaystyle\mathrm{Area}^{\mu}(v_{m})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Area}^{\mu}(u_{m})+6\pi L^{2}.

By the metric Morrey’s
\varepsilon
-conformality lemma
[
FW20
]
, there exists a diffeomorphism up to the boundary
\phi_{m}\colon\mathbb{D}\rightarrow\mathbb{D}
such that

satisfies

\displaystyle E_{+}^{2}(h_{m})\leq\frac{1}{m}+C_{\mu}\mathrm{Area}^{\mu}(v_{m}),

where
C_{\mu}
is a constant depending only on
\mu
. In particular, after identifying the top
\Gamma_{m}\coloneqq\iota_{m}(T)\subset Y_{m}
isometrically with

through

, we may suppose that the 3-point condition

\displaystyle\trace(h_{m})(p_{j})=(3L,Lp_{j})\quad\text{for $j\in\{1,2,3\}$ and $m\in\mathbb{N}$}

holds for a fixed triple
\{p_{1},p_{2},p_{3}\}
from

. In particular, the traces

form an equi-continuous family by the Courant–Lebesgue lemma, cf.
[
LW17a
, Lemma 7.3]
.

It holds that
(h_{m})
is
2

[

, Lemma 4.11]
) and that

\mathrm{Area}^{\mu}(P_{m}\circ h_{m})=\mathrm{Area}^{\mu}(P_{m}\circ v_{m})=\mathrm{Area}^{\mu}(u_{m}).

The second equality follows from the area formula (
3.3
).

Next, we apply the ultralimit theorem,

1.1
, yielding an ultralimit

of
(h_{m})
. Consider the ultralimit

of the projections
(P_{m})
. It holds that

is an ultralimit of

by

1.1
. Moreover, using

\displaystyle\mathrm{Area}^{\mu}(P_{\omega}\circ h_{\omega})\leq\lim\nolimits_{\omega}\mathrm{Area}^{\mu}(P_{m}\circ h_{m})\leq\lim\nolimits_{\omega}\mathrm{FillArea}^{\mu}_{X_{m}}(\gamma_{m})

by (

form an equi-continuous sequence, the ultralimit

of the traces is a weakly monotone parametrization of

While

satisfies the correct area bound (

8.1.
Gromov hyperbolicity

We formulate and prove the following general version of
Theorem

1.5
. Recall that a complete geodesic metric space
X
is Gromov hyperbolic if and only if all its asymptotic cones are metric trees, cf.
[
Dru02
, Proposition 3.1.1]
.

Theorem 8.1
.

Suppose that
\mu
is a lower semi-continuous area and
X
is a complete geodesic space with

\displaystyle\limsup_{r\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\delta_{X}^{\mu}(r)}{r^{2}}<C_{\mu}.

Then every asymptotic cone
X_{\omega}
of
X
is a metric tree. That is,
X
is Gromov hyperbolic.

The constant
C_{\mu}
is defined by

(8.1)

\displaystyle C_{\mu}\coloneqq\inf\{\mu_{V}(\mathbb{I}_{V})/\ell^{2}(\partial\mathbb{I}_{V})\},

where the infimum is taken over two-dimensional normed planes
V
where
\mathbb{I}_{V}\subset V
is an
isoperimetric set
. Namely, the set whose
\mu
area is the largest among sets of a fixed perimeter. For instance, for the Holmes–Thompson, Gromov’s
\text{mass}^{*}
, the Busemann–Hausdorff, and Ivanov’s inscribed Riemannian area, it holds that
C_{\mu}=1/(4\pi)

[
APT04
]
. Thus
Theorem

8.1
implies
Theorem

1.5
as claimed.

For the proof of
Theorem

8.1
, we need a lemma.

Lemma 8.2
.

Suppose that
\mu
is a lower semi-continuous area and
X
is a complete length space with

\displaystyle\limsup_{r\rightarrow 0}\frac{\delta_{X}^{\mu}(r)}{r^{2}}<C_{\mu}.

Then
\mathrm{Area}^{\mu}(u)=0
for every
u\in W^{1,2}(\mathbb{D},X)
.

Proof.

In case there exists
u\in W^{1,2}(\mathbb{D},X)
that satisfies
\mathrm{Area}^{\mu}(u)>0
, then the set where the approximate metric differential of
u
is a norm has positive measure. Then a tangent
X_{\omega}
of
X
contains a two-dimensional normed space
V\subset X_{\omega}
by a standard argument, cf.
[
LW17a
, Proposition 11.2]
. The isoperimetric set
\mathbb{I}_{V}\subset V
of perimeter one satisfies

\displaystyle C_{\mu}\leq\mu_{V}(\mathbb{I}_{V}).

Let
Z
be the injective hull of
V
and
f\colon X_{\omega}\to Z

1
-Lipschitz such that
f|_{V}\colon V\to V
is the identity. Let
c\colon\mathbb{S}^{1}\to\partial\mathbb{I}_{V}
be a constant speed parametrization. Since
\mu
is a lower semi-continuous area, it follows that

\displaystyle\mu_{V}(\mathbb{I}_{V})=\mathrm{FillArea}_{V}^{\mu}(c)=\mathrm{FillArea}_{Z}^{\mu}(f\circ c),

so the
1
-Lipschitz property of
f
implies

\displaystyle C_{\mu}\leq\mathrm{FillArea}_{X_{\omega}}^{\mu}(c).

By the stability result,
Theorem

7.2
, we deduce a contradiction

\displaystyle C_{\mu}\leq\mathrm{FillArea}_{X_{\omega}}(c)\leq\left(\limsup_{r\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{\delta^{\mu}_{X}(r)}{r^{2}}\right)<C_{\mu}.

∎

For the proof of
Theorem

8.1
and below, we will use the following definition: given a Jordan curve
\Gamma\subset X
, we define
\Lambda(\Gamma,X)
to be the set of all
u\in W^{1,2}(\mathbb{D},X)
whose trace has a continuous representative which is a weakly monotone parametrization of
\Gamma
.

Proof of
Theorem

8.1
.

The aim is to prove that every asymptotic cone
Y
of
X
is a metric tree. For this, it suffices to prove that there are no rectifiable Jordan curves
\Gamma\subset Y
. By the stability result,
Theorem

7.2
, every asymptotic cone
Y
of
X
satisfies

\displaystyle\delta_{Y}^{\mu}(r)\leq(1-\varepsilon)C_{\mu}r^{2}\quad\text{for some $\varepsilon\in(0,1)$ and every $r>0$.}

Now suppose that
\Gamma\subset Y
were a rectifiable Jordan curve of length
L>0
. First
\mathrm{Area}(u)=0
for each
u\in\Lambda(\Gamma,Y)
by
Lemma

8.3
. Then, using metric Morrey’s
\varepsilon
-conformality, we find a sequence
u_{m}\in\Lambda(\Gamma,Y)
satisfying a fixed 3-point condition and
E^{2}_{+}(u_{m})\leq 1/m
. By Courant–Lebesgue, the sequence
(\trace(u_{m}))
is equi-continuous, so, for the ultracompletion
Y_{\omega}
, the ultralimit
u_{\omega}\colon\Omega\to Y_{\omega}
of
(u_{m})
belongs to
\Lambda(\Gamma,Y_{\omega})
and has zero energy, so it must be essentially constant. This is a contradiction because
u_{\omega}\in\Lambda(\Gamma,Y_{\omega})
.
∎

8.2.
Comparison geometry

We prove some preliminary results before the proof of

1.4
.

Lemma 8.3
.

Suppose that
\mu
is a lower semi-continuous area and that the inner product normed planes are the unique normed planes realizing the isoperimetric constant

. If

is a complete length space with

(8.2)

\displaystyle\limsup_{r\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{\delta_{X}^{\mu}(r)}{r^{2}}\leq C_{\mu},

then

has property (ET). In particular, every

satisfies

\mathrm{Area}^{\mu}(u)=\mathrm{Area}^{\mu_{i}}(u)=\mathrm{Area}(u).

Property (ET), namely that of having

, was introduced in
[

]
.

Proof.

In case

does not have Property (ET), by definition, there exists
u\in W^{1,2}(\mathbb{D},X)
for which the set where the approximate metric differential of

is not an inner product norm has positive measure. Then a tangent of

contains a normed plane whose norm is not induced by an inner product. This leads to a contradiction as in the proof of
Lemma

8.2
.
∎

Before presenting the proof of
Theorem

1.4
, we solve the Plateau problem in ultracompletions. To make this precise, we define further notation. The
filling area
of a Jordan curve
\Gamma\subset X
with respect to an area
\mu
is

\mathrm{FillArea}^{\mu}_{X}(\Gamma)=\inf\left\{\mathrm{Area}^{\mu}(u)\colon u\in\Lambda(\Gamma,X)\right\}.

As in the case of Lipschitz curves, when
\mu
is omitted, we refer to the parametrized Hausdorff area.

Proposition 8.4
.

Let
\kappa\in\mathbb{R}
and
r_{0}\in(0,2D_{\kappa}]
. Let
X
be a complete length space and suppose that
\delta_{X}(r)\leq\delta_{\kappa}(r)
for
r\in(0,r_{0})
. If a rectifiable Jordan curve
\Gamma\subset X
has length strictly less than
r_{0}
, then there exists a solution for the Plateau problem for
\Gamma
in the ultracompletion
X_{\omega}
. That is, there exists
u_{\omega}\in\Lambda(\Gamma,X_{\omega})
satisfying

\displaystyle E^{2}_{+}(u_{\omega})=\mathrm{Area}(u_{\omega})=\mathrm{FillArea}_{X_{\omega}}(\Gamma).

Proof.

By the stability,
Theorem

1.3
, the ultracompletion
X_{\omega}
satisfies

(8.3)

\displaystyle\delta_{X_{\omega}}(r)\leq\delta_{\kappa}(r)\quad\text{for $r\in(0,r_{0})$.}

Consider a Jordan curve
\Gamma\subset X
with length strictly less than
r_{0}
. Let
c\colon\mathbb{S}^{1}\to\Gamma
be a constant speed parametrization. Given (
8.3
), we obtain

\displaystyle\mathrm{FillArea}_{X_{\omega}}(\Gamma)=\mathrm{FillArea}_{X_{\omega}}(c)

from
[
LW18a
, Lemma 4.8]
. Furthermore,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Area}(v)=\mathrm{Area}^{\mu_{i}}(v)\quad\text{and}\quad\mathrm{FillArea}^{\mu_{i}}_{X_{\omega}}(c)=\mathrm{FillArea}_{X_{\omega}}(c)

whenever
v\in W^{1,2}(\mathbb{D},X_{\omega})
by
Lemma

8.3
.

By
[
SW25
, Proposition 4.1]
(cf. the proof of
[
LWY20
, Proposition 3.5]
), there exists a sequence of complete length spaces
(Y_{m})
such that
X\hookrightarrow Y_{m}
isometrically and the following holds: First, the
m^{-1}
-neighbourhood of
X
in
Y_{m}
is
Y_{m}
. Second,
Y_{m}
is
L
-Lipschitz
1
-connected up to scale
\frac{1}{Lm}
. Third,

\displaystyle\delta^{\mu_{i}}_{Y_{m}}(r)\leq\delta_{\kappa}(r)+Lr^{2}\quad\text{for $r\in(0,r_{0})$}

and that

\displaystyle\delta^{\mu_{i}}_{Y_{m}}(r)\leq\delta_{\kappa}(r)+\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}r^{2}\quad\text{for $r\in\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}},r_{0}\right)$,}

where
L
is a universal constant.

By metric Morrey’s
\varepsilon
-conformality, there exists
u_{m}\in\Lambda(\Gamma,Y_{m})
so that

\displaystyle E^{2}_{+}(u_{m})\leq\mathrm{FillArea}^{\mu_{i}}_{Y_{m}}(\Gamma)+\frac{1}{m}

and
\trace(u_{m})
satisfies a fixed
3
-point condition. By Courant–Lebesgue, the family
(\trace(u_{m}))
is equi-continuous.

Let
X_{\omega}
be an ultralimit
(Y_{m},d_{m},p)
for
p\in X\subset Y_{m}
. Notice that
X_{\omega}
is isometric to the ultracompletion of
X
. The ultralimit
u_{\omega}\in W^{1,2}(\mathbb{D},X_{\omega})
of
(u_{m})
has the following key properties. First, the trace
\trace(u_{\omega})
is an ultralimit of
(\trace(u_{m}))
and thus
u_{\omega}\in\Lambda(\Gamma,X_{\omega})
. Second,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Area}(u_{\omega})=\mathrm{Area}^{\mu_{i}}(u_{\omega})\leq E^{2}_{+}(u_{\omega})\leq\lim\nolimits_{\omega}E^{2}_{+}(u_{m})

and

\displaystyle\lim\nolimits_{\omega}E^{2}_{+}(u_{m})\leq\lim\nolimits_{\omega}\mathrm{FillArea}^{\mu_{i}}_{Y_{m}}(\Gamma).

Third, the lifting result,
[
Wen19
, Theorem 5.1]
, extends to

\displaystyle\lim\nolimits_{\omega}\mathrm{FillArea}^{\mu_{i}}_{Y_{m}}(c)\leq\mathrm{FillArea}^{\mu_{i}}_{X_{\omega}}(c)

with minor modifications. Finally, since
\mathrm{FillArea}^{\mu_{i}}_{Y_{m}}(\Gamma)=\mathrm{FillArea}^{\mu_{i}}_{Y_{m}}(c)
by
[
LW18a
, Lemma 4.8]
, we have

\displaystyle\lim\nolimits_{\omega}\mathrm{FillArea}^{\mu_{i}}_{Y_{m}}(\Gamma)\leq\mathrm{FillArea}_{X_{\omega}}(\Gamma)\leq\mathrm{Area}(u_{\omega}).

The claim follows from the chains of inequalities.
∎

Before the proof of
Theorem

1.4
, we recall the definition of upper curvature bounds on metric spaces. A
geodesic triangle

\Delta\subset X
is a union of three geodesics
\gamma_{1}
,
\gamma_{2}
, and
\gamma_{3}
, joining the
vertices

p_{1}
to
p_{2}
,
p_{2}
to
p_{3}
, and
p_{3}
to
p_{1}
, respectively. The
perimeter
of
\Delta
is
\ell(\gamma_{1})+\ell(\gamma_{2})+\ell(\gamma_{3})
.

Given
\kappa\in\mathbb{R}
, a geodesic triangle
\Delta
with perimeter
<2D_{\kappa}
satisfies the
\mathrm{CAT}(\kappa)
comparison if, given a comparison triangle
\Delta^{\prime}\subset M^{2}_{\kappa}
, the
comparison map

\Delta^{\prime}\to\Delta
, sending sides to sides isometrically, is
1
-Lipschitz. We say that a metric space
X
is
\mathrm{CAT}(\kappa)
if every geodesic triangle with perimeter
<2D_{\kappa}
satisfies the
\mathrm{CAT}(\kappa)
comparison and every
x,y\in X
with
d(x,y)<D_{\kappa}
can be joined with a geodesic; see
[
BH99
]
(and
[
AKP24
]
) for further background. A
Jordan triangle
is a geodesic triangle so that the sides overlap only at their end points. In particular, a Jordan triangle is homeomorphic to
\mathbb{S}^{1}
.

Proof of
Theorem

1.4
.

We first prove the following claim: Let
X
be a complete length space with
\delta_{X}(r)\leq\delta_{\kappa}(r)
for
r\in(0,r_{0})
. Then any geodesic triangle
\Delta\subset X
with perimeter
<r_{0}
satisfies the
\mathrm{CAT}(\kappa)
comparison.

Arguing as in
[
LW18b
, Lemma 3.1]
, it suffices to consider a Jordan triangle
\Delta
with perimeter
<r_{0}
. Consider the ultracompletion
Y=X_{\omega}
. By the stability,
Theorem

1.3
, the ultracompletion satisfies

(8.4)

\displaystyle\delta_{Y}(r)\leq\delta_{\kappa}(r)\quad\text{for $r\in(0,r_{0})$.}

Let
u\in\Lambda(\Delta,Y)
be a solution to the Plateau problem obtained from
Proposition

8.4
. It holds by (
8.4
) that
u
has a continuous representative up to the boundary, cf.
[
LW17a
, Theorem 9.1]
. Moreover,
u
factors through an intrinsic disk
Z
: by
[
LW18a
, Theorem 1.1]
, there exists a geodesic space
Z
homeomorphic to
\overline{\mathbb{D}}
, a continuous and monotone
P_{u}\colon\overline{\mathbb{D}}\to Z
, and
1
-Lipschitz
\overline{u}\colon Z\to X_{\omega}
satisfying
u=\overline{u}\circ P_{u}
, and that the lengths of
u\circ\gamma
and
P_{u}\circ\gamma
are equal for every rectifiable
\gamma
. Hence it suffices to verify the comparison estimate for
\partial Z
. We claim that

(8.5)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{2}(\Omega)\leq\delta_{\kappa}(\ell(\partial\Omega))\quad\text{for Jordan domains $\Omega\subset Z$ with $\ell(\partial\Omega)<2D_{\kappa}$.}

To this end, when
\ell(\partial\Omega)<r_{0}
, inequality (
8.5
) follows from (
8.4
) by the proof of
[
LW18a
, Theorem 8.2]
with minor modifications. If
r_{0}\leq\ell(\partial\Omega)<2D_{\kappa}
instead, we observe
\mathcal{H}^{2}(\Omega)\leq\mathcal{H}^{2}(Z)\leq\delta_{\kappa}(\ell(\partial Z))\leq\delta_{\kappa}(\ell(\partial\Omega))
. Then, by the proof of
[
LW20
, Theorem 1.4]
, we conclude
\delta_{Z}(r)\leq\delta_{\kappa}(r)
for
r\in(0,2D_{\kappa})
. Thus, by
[
LW18b
, Theorem 1.4]
,
Z
is
\mathrm{CAT}(\kappa)
. The proof of the claim is complete.

To finish, we first assume that
X
is geodesic. If
r_{0}=2D_{\kappa}
,
X
is
\mathrm{CAT}(\kappa)
by the claim on geodesic triangles. If
r_{0}<2D_{\kappa}
, we proceed as follows. For all
x,y\in X
with
d(x,y)<r_{0}/2
, the claim on geodesic triangles implies that the geodesic
\gamma_{xy}
joining
x
to
y
is unique and moreover depends continuously on the end points. Furthermore, if
x,y
are contained in an open ball
B=B_{X}(x_{0},r_{0}/4)
, then
\gamma_{xy}
lies in
B
. In particular,
B
is geodesic and locally
\mathrm{CAT}(\kappa)
. By
[
AKP24
, Radial Lemma, Lemma 9.52]
,
B
is
\mathrm{CAT}(\kappa)
and it is not difficult to see that the closed ball
\overline{B}
, which coincides with the closure of
B
, is geodesic and
\mathrm{CAT}(\kappa)
, as claimed.

It remains to consider the case when
X
is merely a complete length space. Then its ultracompletion
Y
is geodesic and satisfies the conclusions of the previous steps. Since
X
is a complete length space, it easily follows that for any
x,y\in X
with
d(x,y)<r_{0}/2
, the unique geodesic
\gamma_{xy}
in
Y
is, in fact, contained in
X
. In case
r_{0}=2D_{\kappa}
, the proof is complete. If
r_{0}<2D_{\kappa}
, then any closed ball
\overline{B}_{X}(x_{0},r_{0}/4)
is a geodesic subset of the
\mathrm{CAT}(\kappa)
space
\overline{B}_{Y}(x_{0},r_{0}/4)
. Thus
\overline{B}_{X}(x_{0},r_{0}/4)
is
\mathrm{CAT}(\kappa)
.
∎

Remark 8.5
.

Proposition

8.4
, and thus
Theorem

1.4
, clearly holds if the Busemann–Hausdorff area is replaced by any lower semi-continuous area
\mu
for which the inner product normed planes are the unique normed planes realizing the isoperimetric constant
C_{\mu}=1/(4\pi)
. This is immediate from
Lemma

8.3
.
