Foundational Analysis Of The Solvability Complexity Index: The Weihrauch-SCI Intermediate Hierarchy And A Koopman Operator Example
Christopher Sorg 1

Abstract
. The Solvability Complexity Index (SCI) provides an abstract notion of “computing” a target map Ξ \Xi from finitely many oracle evaluations Λ ⊆ ℂ \Lambda\subseteq\mathbb{C} via finite-height towers of pointwise limits. We first give a foundational analysis of what this extensional framework does and does not determine. We show that the SCI separation “axiom” is equivalent to a factorization of Ξ \Xi through the full evaluation table, and we isolate the minimal logical role of Λ \Lambda as an information interface. To connect the SCI to Type-2 computability and Weihrauch reducibility, we give an effective enrichment for countable Λ \Lambda by viewing the evaluation table image I Λ ⊆ ℂ ℕ I_{\Lambda}\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{\mathbb{N}} as a represented space and factoring Ξ \Xi as Ξ ^ \widehat{\Xi} . We then define the Weihrauch-SCI rank of a problem as the least number of iterated limit-oracles needed to compute it in the Weihrauch sense, i.e. the least k k such that Ξ ^ ≤ W lim ( k ) \widehat{\Xi}\leq_{W}\lim^{(k)} , and prove well-posedness and representation invariance of this rank. A central negative result is that the unrestricted type- G G SCI model (arbitrary post-processing of finite oracle transcripts) is generally not comparable to Weihrauch/Type-2 complexity: finite-query factorizations collapse type- G G height, and analytic (non-Borel) decision problems yield examples with SCI G = 0 \mathrm{SCI}_{G}=0 but infinite Weihrauch-SCI rank. To recover a robust bridge, we introduce an intermediate SCI hierarchy by restricting the admissible base-level post-processing to regularity classes (continuous/Borel/Baire) and, optionally, to fixed-query versus adaptive-query policies. We prove that these restrictions form genuine hierarchies, and we establish comparison theorems showing what each restriction logically enforces (e.g. Borel towers compute only Borel targets; continuous-base towers yield finite Baire class). As an application, we reinterpret the SCI classification of Koopman spectral approximation problems as an instance of the intermediate hierarchy.

1.
Introduction

The
Solvability Complexity Index
(SCI) was introduced as a classification tool for
mathematical tasks that are not naturally captured by a single-shot notion of computation,
but instead admit (or require)
towers of algorithms
: procedures whose outputs converge
to the desired value only after one or several limiting operations
[
Han11
,
BACH+15
,
CH23
]
.
This perspective has proved particularly fruitful in computational spectral theory, where
the existence (or impossibility) of algorithms can depend delicately on the permitted input
interface and on the allowed post-processing of oracle information
[
Han11
,
CH23
]
.

At the same time, the SCI is intentionally
extensional
: at its core, one fixes an input
class
\Omega
, a target map
\Xi:\Omega\to\mathcal{M}
for a metric space
\mathcal{M}
, and an evaluation family
\Lambda
(an
information interface), and defines what it means for a tower to compute
\Xi
from finitely
many evaluation values (possibly iterated through limits). Here, extensional is not the set-theoretic axiom of extensionality,
the corresponding SCI-definitions just don’t fix (on purpose) any syntax-level notation of computation yet.
This abstraction is a strength: it allows one to compare disparate numerical and information
models under a single roof but it also raises a foundational question that motivates the
present work:

Q:
When does the SCI define a computability model?

A
“computability model”
is not merely a set of input-output tasks; it is a stable notion of

morphism
closed under composition and basic data operations, so that procedures can be
combined, relativized, and compared without ambiguity.
In Type-2 computable analysis this stability is enforced by
representations
(names) and

realizers
: maps between represented spaces form a robust category in which computable
operations are automatically continuous at the level induced by the representations

[
Wei00
,
BGP21
]
.
Weihrauch reducibility is meaningful precisely because it lives inside such a compositional
setting: it compares problems by uniform pre-/post-processing around a single oracle call,
and the resulting degree structures support a rich algebra of computational content

[
BG11
,
BGP21
]
.

In contrast, the purely extensional SCI definition leaves open what counts as admissible
post-processing on finitely many oracle values.
Different interpretations of this interface can lead to genuinely different
“heights”
for the
same extensional task.
A classical example of such divergence is the gap between
atomic
real-number interfaces
(which allow exact comparisons) and
name-based
interfaces (Type-2), where computability
is necessarily continuous
[
Wei00
,
NP18
,
BCSS98
]
.
This paper therefore takes the stance that statements of the form
“
\Xi
has SCI height
k
”

should be read as
conditional
on an explicit package of premises describing
(i) the information interface, and (ii) the closure/regularity properties
demanded of the admissible post-processing.

There is an instructive parallel here to a familiar point in logic.
On the proof-theoretic side, theoremhood (
“tautology”
) is defined relative to a chosen
deductive calculus (axioms and inference rules); on the semantic side, validity is defined
relative to a chosen account of consequence (e.g. Tarski’s model-theoretic criterion)

[
Tar02
,
S+98
]
.
Carnap famously emphasized that different formal frameworks may be adopted for different
purposes (
“tolerance”
), while Quine challenged sharp analytic/synthetic boundaries and
thus any overly rigid separation between
“truth by meaning”
and
“truth by fact”

[
Car14
,
Qui00
]
.
In the contemporary literature, these tensions also motivate more pluralist readings of
logical consequence
[
BR00
]
, as well as detailed debates about what Tarski’s
definition does and does not capture
[
Etc90
]
.
The moral for the present context is not that the SCI is purely philosophical, but that
the
informativeness of any classification depends on making its background commitments explicit
.
Our goal is to do this in a way that stays mathematically sharp while remaining neutral with
respect to foundational preferences.

2.
The Purely Extensional Standard (Precised) SCI Definition

We are interested in measuring the
“algorithmic complexity”
of mathematical spectral approximation problems in the sense of the
Solvability Complexity Index
(SCI), which measures the minimal number of nested limits required by any algorithm to solve a given (infinite-dimensional) problem
[
Han11
,
BACH+15
,
BAHNS15
,
CH22
]
. The SCI framework has revealed sharp boundaries for spectral computations of (unbounded) operators. Some spectral problems are provably not solvable by any single-limit procedure (hence admit no global error control), yet become solvable by towers of algorithms of finite height or may not be solvable for a finite height at all, in the latter the SCI being therefore infinity, see e.g.
[
CMS24
, Theorem B.1]
or
[
Sor26
, Theorem 4.9, Theorem 4.14]
. We start by stating the pure extensional SCI definition and enrich it later for Weihrauch comparisons, where it is logical necessary.

Notation:
In this work, we will always use the notation
\mathbb{N}:=\{1,2,3,\dots\}
and
\mathbb{N}_{0}:=\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
.

Definition 2.1

(Metric space)
.

A
metric space
is a pair
(\mathcal{M},d)
where
\mathcal{M}
is a set and the map

d:\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{M}\to[0,\infty)
satisfies
\forall x,y,z\in\mathcal{M}
:
(i)
d(x,y)=0\Leftrightarrow x=y
;
(ii)
d(x,y)=d(y,x)
;
(iii)
d(x,z)\leq d(x,y)+d(y,z)
.

Definition 2.2

(Complex-valued evaluation set)
.

Let
\Omega
be a set. Write
\mathbb{C}^{\Omega}:=\{f:\Omega\to\mathbb{C}\}
.
An
evaluation set
on
\Omega
is a subset
\Lambda\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{\Omega}
.
Equivalently,
\Lambda
is a family of maps
f:\Omega\to\mathbb{C}
.

Remark 2.3

(Non-
\mathbb{C}
-valued
“natural”
evaluations)
.

Many evaluations are not
\mathbb{C}
-valued.
To fit
definition

2.2

literally
, one fixes once and for all an injective encoding

\iota:V\hookrightarrow\mathbb{C}
of the value set
V
(or into
\mathbb{C}^{m}
and then into
\mathbb{C}
),
and replaces each
g:\Omega\to V
by
\iota\circ g:\Omega\to\mathbb{C}
.
This does not change the
information content
of the oracle family as long as
\iota
is injective.

The now following SCI definitions are based on the definitions in
[
CMS24
, Appendix A]
but slightly precised for our purposes in this work.

Definition 2.4

(Computational problem; consistency
“axiom”
)
.

A
computational problem
is a quadruple

\mathcal{P}=\bigl(\Xi,\Omega,(\mathcal{M},d),\Lambda\bigr),

where

•

\Omega
is a set (the
primary set
, i.e. input class);

•

(\mathcal{M},d)
is a metric space in the sense of
definition

2.1
;

•

\Xi:\Omega\to\mathcal{M}
is a function (the
problem function
);

•

\Lambda\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{\Omega}
is an evaluation set in the sense of
definition

2.2
.

We impose the
consistency
“axiom”
:

(2.1)

\forall A,B\in\Omega,\quad\Xi(A)\neq\Xi(B)\ \Longrightarrow\ \exists f\in\Lambda\ \text{s.t. }f(A)\neq f(B).

Remark 2.5

(Why (
2.1
) is not a set-theoretic axiom)
.

Fix a background foundation, e.g.
\mathsf{ZFC}
.
In the strict logical sense, an
axiom of
\mathsf{ZFC}
is a
sentence

(i.e. a closed formula) in the language of set theory that belongs to the axiom list
(of
\mathsf{ZFC}
, or an axiom scheme thereof).
By contrast, (
2.1
) is a
predicate on the parameters

\mathcal{P}=\bigl(\Xi,\Omega,(\mathcal{M},d),\Lambda\bigr)
; it is therefore not an axiom of the ambient
set theory, but a
structural well-posedness condition
imposed on the objects under study.

More formally, consider the universal closure of the consistency condition:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cons}^{\mathrm{all}}:\quad\forall\Xi,\Omega,\mathcal{M},d,\Lambda\;\Bigl(\bigl[\Xi:\Omega\to\mathcal{M}\ \wedge\ d\ \text{is a metric on }\mathcal{M}\ \wedge\ \Lambda\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{\Omega}\bigr]

\displaystyle\rightarrow\forall A,B\in\Omega\,\bigl(\Xi(A)\neq\Xi(B)\rightarrow\exists f\in\Lambda\,(f(A)\neq f(B))\bigr)\Bigr).

Then
\mathsf{ZFC}
proves
\neg\mathrm{Cons}^{\mathrm{all}}
by an explicit counterexample:
take
\Omega:=\{0,1\}
,
\mathcal{M}:=\{0,1\}
with the discrete metric,
let
\Xi:=\mathrm{id}_{\Omega}
, and let
\Lambda:=\{f\}
where
f:\Omega\to\mathbb{C}
is constant.
Then
\Xi(0)\neq\Xi(1)
but
f(0)=f(1)
, so (
2.1
) fails.

In particular, (
2.1
) cannot be
equivalent
(in the usual proof-theoretic
sense
“
\mathsf{ZFC}\vdash\varphi\leftrightarrow\psi
”
, where
\varphi,\,\psi
are formulas) to any axiom (or axiom scheme instance)

\varphi
of
\mathsf{ZFC}
: if
\mathsf{ZFC}\vdash\varphi\leftrightarrow\mathrm{Cons}^{\mathrm{all}}
,
then since
\mathsf{ZFC}\vdash\varphi
one would obtain
\mathsf{ZFC}\vdash\mathrm{Cons}^{\mathrm{all}}
,
contradicting
\mathsf{ZFC}\vdash\neg\mathrm{Cons}^{\mathrm{all}}
above.

Thus the term
“axiom”
in (
2.1
) is only meant in the informal sense of a standing

assumption/restriction on admissible problems
. We have retained the term nonetheless, as it is often found that way in the SCI-literature.

For any set
X
, we will write in the following

[X]^{<\omega}:=\{E\subseteq X:E\text{ is finite}\}.

Definition 2.6

(General algorithm)
.

Let
\mathcal{P}=\bigl(\Xi,\Omega,(\mathcal{M},d),\Lambda\bigr)
be a computational problem.
A
general algorithm
for
\mathcal{P}
is a pair
(\Gamma,\Lambda_{\Gamma})
where

\Gamma:\Omega\to\mathcal{M}\quad\text{and}\quad\Lambda_{\Gamma}:\Omega\to[\Lambda]^{<\omega}

assigns to each
A\in\Omega
a finite nonempty set of queries
\Lambda_{\Gamma}(A)\subseteq\Lambda

such that for all
A,B\in\Omega
:

(1)

(
Finite-information dependence
): If
f(B)=f(A)
for all
f\in\Lambda_{\Gamma}(A)
,
then
\Gamma(B)=\Gamma(A)
.

(2)

(
Stability of the query set
): If
f(B)=f(A)
for all
f\in\Lambda_{\Gamma}(A)
,
then
\Lambda_{\Gamma}(B)=\Lambda_{\Gamma}(A)
.

Definition 2.7

(Query policy; fixed vs. adaptive querying)
.

Let
\mathcal{P}=\bigl(\Xi,\Omega,(\mathcal{M},d),\Lambda\bigr)
be an SCI computational problem and let
(\Gamma,\Lambda_{\Gamma})

be a general algorithm for
\mathcal{P}
in the sense of
definition

2.6
.

The map
\Lambda_{\Gamma}:\Omega\to[\Lambda]^{<\omega}
is called the

query policy
of the algorithm.

(1)

We call
(\Gamma,\Lambda_{\Gamma})

fixed-query
if there exists a finite set

Q\subseteq\Lambda
such that
\Lambda_{\Gamma}(A)=Q
for all
A\in\Omega
.

(2)

Otherwise we call
(\Gamma,\Lambda_{\Gamma})

adaptive-query
.
Equivalently, it is adaptive-query iff there exist
A,B\in\Omega
with

\Lambda_{\Gamma}(A)\neq\Lambda_{\Gamma}(B)
.

Remark 2.8

(Extensional meaning of adaptivity)
.

Definition

2.6
is the extensional footprint of adaptive oracle access: the set of
queries used on input
A
is determined solely by the answers to
those
queries.
Formally, for all
A,B\in\Omega
,

\Bigl(\forall f\in\Lambda_{\Gamma}(A)\;\;f(B)=f(A)\Bigr)\ \Longrightarrow\ \Bigl(\Lambda_{\Gamma}(B)=\Lambda_{\Gamma}(A)\ \wedge\ \Gamma(B)=\Gamma(A)\Bigr).

Thus two inputs that induce the same query-answer transcript on the queried evaluations
force identical algorithmic behaviour (same queries, same output).

Remark 2.9

(Suppressing the query policy)
.

When only the output map matters, we will (ab)use notation and write
\Gamma
for the
pair
(\Gamma,\Lambda_{\Gamma})
, keeping
\Lambda_{\Gamma}
implicit; when needed we
write
\Lambda_{\Gamma}(A)
explicitly.
This avoids ambiguity later when towers are written only in terms of their output maps.

Definition 2.10

(Tower of algorithms)
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. A
tower of algorithms of height
k
for

\mathcal{P}=\bigl(\Xi,\Omega,(\mathcal{M},d),\Lambda\bigr)
consists of:

•

if
k=0
: a single general algorithm
(\Gamma,\Lambda_{\Gamma})
such that

\Gamma(A)=\Xi(A)
for all
A\in\Omega
;

•

if
k\in\mathbb{N}
: a family of general algorithms

\Gamma_{n_{k}},\ \Gamma_{n_{k},n_{k-1}},\ \dots,\ \Gamma_{n_{k},\dots,n_{1}}:\Omega\to\mathcal{M}\qquad(n_{1},\dots,n_{k}\in\mathbb{N})

such that for every
A\in\Omega
the iterated limit equalities

\Xi(A)=\lim_{n_{k}\to\infty}\Gamma_{n_{k}}(A),\quad\Gamma_{n_{k}}(A)=\lim_{n_{k-1}\to\infty}\Gamma_{n_{k},n_{k-1}}(A),\quad\dots,\quad\Gamma_{n_{k},\dots,n_{2}}(A)=\lim_{n_{1}\to\infty}\Gamma_{n_{k},\dots,n_{1}}(A).

hold in the metric
d
(and similarly for all inner levels).

Now we state first the tower definitions as in most of the SCI-literature.

Definition 2.11

(Tower types: general (G) vs. arithmetic (A))
.

One may restrict the
base maps

\Gamma_{n_{k},\ldots,n_{1}}
in a tower to define different tower types
\alpha
.

•

General towers (
\alpha=G
):
no restriction beyond
definition

2.10
.

•

Arithmetic towers (
\alpha=A
):
A map
\Gamma:\Omega\to\mathcal{M}
is called an
arithmetic algorithm
if

\Gamma
is a general algorithm in the sense of
definition

2.6
and, moreover,
for every
A\in\Omega
the value
\Gamma(A)
is obtained from the finite tuple

\bigl(f(A)\bigr)_{f\in\Lambda_{\Gamma}(A)}
by a finite computation using only

arithmetic operations
on
\mathbb{C}
. We call a tower of algorithms in the sense of
definition

2.10
an
arithmetic tower
,
if each base map
\Gamma_{n_{k},\ldots,n_{1}}
is an arithmetic algorithm.

We note that for the purposes of this work
definition

2.11
is
not
precise enough. First
“finite computation”
is not a mathematical object unless one chooses a machine model (or equivalent syntax);
“comparisons”
over
\mathbb{C}
are underspecified and for general
\mathcal{M}
it is unclear what it means to
“output”
an element of
\mathcal{M}
. The most natural fix we could think of is a BSS-specification, which we will precise now. This precision is in line with the more precise SCI-definitions provided e.g. in
[
Col20
, §2]
or in
[
BACH+15
, §6]
.

Definition 2.12

(
\mathbb{R}^{\star}
(finite real tuples))
.

Let

\mathbb{R}^{\star}\ :=\ \bigsqcup_{m\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{R}^{m}

be the disjoint union of all finite-dimensional Euclidean spaces.

Definition 2.13

(Finite output interface)
.

Let
(\mathcal{M},d)
be a metric space.
A
finite output interface
for
\mathcal{M}
is a fixed (possibly partial) map

\delta^{\mathrm{fin}}_{\mathcal{M}}:\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{\star}\to\mathcal{M}.

Elements
u\in\mathrm{dom}(\delta^{\mathrm{fin}}_{\mathcal{M}})
are called
finite codes
for

\delta^{\mathrm{fin}}_{\mathcal{M}}(u)\in\mathcal{M}
.

Definition 2.14

(Evaluation oracle for a countable evaluation set)
.

Assume
\Lambda
is countable and fix an enumeration
\Lambda=(f_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
.
For each
A\in\Omega
define the
evaluation oracle

\mathcal{O}_{A}:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R}^{2},\qquad\mathcal{O}_{A}(i):=\bigl(\operatorname{Re}(f_{i}(A)),\operatorname{Im}(f_{i}(A))\bigr).

Definition 2.15

(Oracle BSS machine (parameter-free; partial QUERY))
.

Fix an enumeration
\Lambda=(f_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
and the associated oracle maps

\mathcal{O}_{A}:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
from
definition

2.14
.
An
oracle BSS machine over
(\mathbb{R},+,-,\cdot,/,<)
in the sense of
[
BCSS98
]

without real parameters
is a BSS machine whose only built-in constants are

0
and
1
(hence all rational constants are computable inside the machine),
equipped with an additional instruction

\mathrm{QUERY}:\ \text{read }i\text{ from a designated register and write }\mathcal{O}_{A}(i)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\text{ into designated registers.}

The instruction
\mathrm{QUERY}
is
partial
: it is defined only if the designated
register contains a natural number
i\in\mathbb{N}
(encoded as the real
i
); otherwise the run
is undefined (equivalently: the machine diverges).

A run
halts
if it reaches a halting state after finitely many steps; upon halting it
outputs a finite tuple
u\in\mathbb{R}^{\star}
(encoded in the standard BSS way).

This is the formal replacement for
“finite computation using arithmetic operations and comparisons”
.

Definition 2.16

(BSS-arithmetic algorithm)
.

Let
\mathcal{P}=\bigl(\Xi,\Omega,(\mathcal{M},d),\Lambda\bigr)
be a computational problem.
Assume:

(1)

\Lambda
is countable with a fixed enumeration
\Lambda=(f_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
;

(2)

\mathcal{M}
is equipped with a fixed finite output interface

\delta^{\mathrm{fin}}_{\mathcal{M}}
as in
definition

2.13
.

A map
\Gamma:\Omega\to\mathcal{M}
is called a
BSS-arithmetic algorithm

if there exists an oracle BSS machine
\mathbb{M}
in the sense of
definition

2.15

such that for every
A\in\Omega
the run of
\mathbb{M}
with oracle
\mathcal{O}_{A}

halts and outputs some
u\in\mathrm{dom}(\delta^{\mathrm{fin}}_{\mathcal{M}})
with

\delta^{\mathrm{fin}}_{\mathcal{M}}(u)=\Gamma(A).

We require
\mathbb{M}
to be parameter-free in the sense of
definition

2.15
.

Now we can give a formal precise version of
definition

2.11
.

(Tower types: general (G) vs. arithmetic (A))
.

One may restrict the

\Gamma_{n_{k},\ldots,n_{1}}
in a tower to define different

\alpha
.

•

General towers (

):
no restriction beyond
definition

.

Definition 2.18

(SCI)

Let
\alpha\in\{G,A\}

(Why extra structure is logically necessary)
.

,
2.6
,

and

are

3.
The Minimal Logical Role Of The Evaluation Set
\Lambda

Definition 3.1

(Full evaluation table and
\Lambda
-indistinguishability)
.

For each
f\in\Lambda
let
D_{f}
denote its codomain. Define the
full evaluation table map

\mathrm{Ev}_{\Lambda}:\Omega\to\prod_{f\in\Lambda}D_{f},\qquad\mathrm{Ev}_{\Lambda}(A):=(f(A))_{f\in\Lambda}.

(If all
D_{f}=\mathbb{C}
, then
\prod_{f\in\Lambda}D_{f}=\mathbb{C}^{\Lambda}
.)

Define an equivalence relation
\sim_{\Lambda}
on
\Omega
by

A\sim_{\Lambda}B\quad:\Longleftrightarrow\quad\forall f\in\Lambda,\ f(A)=f(B).

Theorem 3.2

(Consistency
\Leftrightarrow
factorization through the evaluation table)
.

Let
\mathcal{P}=\bigl(\Xi,\Omega,(\mathcal{M},d),\Lambda\bigr)
.
The following are equivalent:

(1)

(Consistency)
\Xi(A)\neq\Xi(B)\Rightarrow\exists f\in\Lambda:\ f(A)\neq f(B)
.

(2)

(Extensionality)
A\sim_{\Lambda}B\Rightarrow\Xi(A)=\Xi(B)
.

(3)

(Factorization) There exists a unique map

\Phi:\mathrm{Ev}_{\Lambda}(\Omega)\to\mathcal{M}

such that
\Xi=\Phi\circ\mathrm{Ev}_{\Lambda}
.

Proof.

(1)
\Leftrightarrow
(2) is contrapositive.
Assume (2). Define
\Phi
on
\mathrm{Ev}_{\Lambda}(\Omega)
by

\Phi(\mathrm{Ev}_{\Lambda}(A)):=\Xi(A)
.
This is well-defined: if
\mathrm{Ev}_{\Lambda}(A)=\mathrm{Ev}_{\Lambda}(B)
then
A\sim_{\Lambda}B
, hence
\Xi(A)=\Xi(B)
.
Then
\Xi=\Phi\circ\mathrm{Ev}_{\Lambda}
by construction.
Uniqueness holds because
\mathrm{Ev}_{\Lambda}(\Omega)
is the image.
Conversely, (3) implies (2) because equality of evaluation tables forces equality of
\Phi
-images.
∎

Corollary 3.3

(Necessity of consistency)
.

If consistency fails (i.e. there exist
A,B\in\Omega
with
A\sim_{\Lambda}B
but
\Xi(A)\neq\Xi(B)
),
then there is no tower of general algorithms (of any finite height) computing
\Xi
from
\Lambda
.
Hence
\mathrm{SCI}_{G}(\mathcal{P})=\infty
.

Proof.

Let
\{\Gamma_{n_{k},\dots,n_{1}}\}
be any proposed tower.
Each base algorithm
\Gamma_{n_{k},\dots,n_{1}}
depends only on finitely many evaluations, so if
A\sim_{\Lambda}B
then in particular

f(A)=f(B)
for all
f\in\Lambda_{\Gamma_{n_{k},\dots,n_{1}}}(A)
.
By
definition

2.6
(1),
\Gamma_{n_{k},\dots,n_{1}}(A)=\Gamma_{n_{k},\dots,n_{1}}(B)

for all indices, hence every iterated limit agrees at
A
and
B
, contradicting
\Xi(A)\neq\Xi(B)
.
∎

Remark 3.4

(Why
“minimum structure”
on
\Lambda
becomes problem-dependent)
.

Consistency is the
minimal logical
requirement for
\Xi
to be determined by
\Lambda
at all.
But for
lower bounds
in the SCI hierarchy, one needs an additional
“adversary flexibility”
property (or something logically equivalent):
for a fixed input
F_{0}
, and for the (countable) set of all points queried by the tower at
F_{0}
,
one must be able to modify
F
away from those points while staying in
\Omega
and changing
\Xi(F)
.
This property depends heavily on
(\Omega,\Lambda)
and on the functional
\Xi
. For a deeper and more precise understanding of this, we refer the curious reader to e.g.
[
Sor25
, Counterexample 5.7, Counterexample 5.11, Lemma 5.13]
.

4.
The Topology Induced By An Evaluation Set
\Lambda

From category theory it is well known that the category of compactly generated Hausdorff spaces and continuous maps
\mathbf{CGHaus}
is cartesian closed (A category
\mathcal{C}
is
cartesian closed
if it has finite products and
Y^{X}
exists for all objects
X,Y\in\mathcal{C}
.). For a solution map
S:C(X,X)\to\mathcal{M}
this is equivalent to equip
C(X,X)
with the compact-open topology (will be defined below). The evaluation set, that is mostly used (at least as a minimum requirement) in the SCI-literature, is the set of point-evaluations. If therefore for point-evaluations we have
\Omega\subseteq C(X,X)
, do general algorithms behave equivalent nicely? We will shortly discuss this point in this section.

Definition 4.1

(Initial (information) topology induced by
\Lambda
)
.

Let
\Omega
be a set and
\Lambda
a family of maps
f:\Omega\to Y_{f}
into topological spaces
Y_{f}
.
The
initial topology

\tau_{\Lambda}
on
\Omega
induced by
\Lambda
is the coarsest topology on
\Omega

making every
f\in\Lambda
continuous.
Equivalently,
\tau_{\Lambda}
is generated by the subbasic sets
f^{-1}(U)
with
f\in\Lambda
and
U\subseteq Y_{f}
open.

One might expect that point-evaluations will naturally (so in the initial topology sense) lead to the topology of pointwise convergence and indeed

Proposition 4.2

(Point-evaluation
\Lambda
induces the topology of pointwise convergence)
.

Let
X
be a topological space and let
\Omega:=C(X,X)
as a set.
Let
\Lambda_{\mathrm{pt}}=\{\mathrm{ev}_{x}:\Omega\to X\mid x\in X\}
.
Then the initial topology
\tau_{\Lambda_{\mathrm{pt}}}
equals the topology of pointwise convergence,
i.e. the subspace topology inherited from the product space
X^{X}
with the product topology.

Proof.

The product topology on
X^{X}
is, by definition, the initial topology induced by the coordinate projections

\pi_{x}:X^{X}\to X
,
\pi_{x}(F)=F(x)
.
Restricting to the subset
C(X,X)\subseteq X^{X}
yields the initial topology on
C(X,X)
induced by
\mathrm{ev}_{x}
.
∎

For topological spaces
X,Y
, we will write
C(X,Y)_{\mathrm{pt}}
for
C(X,Y)
equipped with
the topology of pointwise convergence, i.e. the subspace topology inherited from
Y^{X}

with the product topology.

Now we want to answer the question from the beginning of this section, defining the

Definition 4.3

(Compact-open topology
[
Fox45
,
AD51
,
EH01
]
)
.

Let
X,Y
be topological spaces and let
C(X,Y)
denote the set of continuous maps
X\to Y
.
For each compact
K\subseteq X
and open
U\subseteq Y
, define

[K,U]:=\{f\in C(X,Y):f[K]\subseteq U\}.

The
compact-open topology
on
C(X,Y)
is the topology generated by the subbasic sets
[K,U]
.
We denote the resulting space by
C(X,Y)_{co}
.

Proposition 4.4

(Point evaluations are continuous in the compact-open topology)
.

For each
x\in X
, the point evaluation map

\mathrm{ev}_{x}:C(X,Y)_{co}\to Y,\qquad\mathrm{ev}_{x}(f):=f(x),

is continuous.

Proof.

Let
U\subseteq Y
be open. Then

\mathrm{ev}_{x}^{-1}(U)=\{f\in C(X,Y):f(x)\in U\}=[\{x\},U],

and
\{x\}
is compact, hence
[\{x\},U]
is subbasic open in the compact-open topology.
∎

Proposition 4.5

(Compact-open is at least as fine as pointwise)
.

For any topological spaces
X,Y
, the identity map

\mathrm{id}:C(X,Y)_{co}\to C(X,Y)_{\mathrm{pt}}

is continuous (so
\tau_{\mathrm{pt}}\subseteq\tau_{co}
).

Proof.

By
proposition

4.4
, each
\mathrm{ev}_{x}
is continuous on
C(X,Y)_{co}
,
hence
\tau_{co}
is a topology that makes all point evaluations continuous.
Since
\tau_{\mathrm{pt}}
is the
coarsest
such topology, we have
\tau_{\mathrm{pt}}\subseteq\tau_{co}
.
∎

Remark 4.6

(When can pointwise and compact-open coincide?)
.

If
X
is discrete, then every compact
K\subseteq X
is finite, so the compact-open subbasic sets

[K,U]
impose only finitely many point constraints; hence
\tau_{co}=\tau_{\mathrm{pt}}
.
Outside such special situations,
\tau_{co}
is strictly stronger.

In SCI terms: using only finitely many point evaluations as the basic information
captures
pointwise
information. To obtain
uniform
(compact-open) control from
point samples, one typically needs additional structure/information (e.g. a known modulus of continuity,
equicontinuity, Lipschitz bounds, etc.), which can be modeled either by restricting the input class
or by enriching
\Lambda
(or the representation) beyond raw point evaluations. This is exactly the logical reason, why the considered
\Omega
-subclasses analyzed in e.g.
[
CMS24
,
Sor25
]
involved knowledge about properties like a modulus of continuity.

Proposition 4.7

(Failure of joint evaluation in the pointwise topology)
.

Assume
X
is a compact metric space with a non-isolated point
x_{0}
, and let
Y=\mathbb{R}
.
Then the joint evaluation map

\mathrm{ev}:C(X,\mathbb{R})_{\mathrm{pt}}\times X\to\mathbb{R},\qquad(f,x)\mapsto f(x),

is not continuous.

Proof.

Let
f_{0}\equiv 0
and fix
x_{0}
non-isolated. Let
U:=(-1,1)
, so
\mathrm{ev}(f_{0},x_{0})=0\in U
.
Assume
\mathrm{ev}
is continuous at
(f_{0},x_{0})
. Then there exist neighborhoods

N
of
f_{0}
in
C(X,\mathbb{R})_{\mathrm{pt}}
and
V
of
x_{0}
in
X
such that

N\times V\subseteq\mathrm{ev}^{-1}(U)
.

A basic neighborhood
N
of
f_{0}
in the pointwise topology is determined by finitely many points

x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\in X
and open intervals
U_{i}\ni 0
such that

N=\bigcap_{i=1}^{n}\{f\in C(X,\mathbb{R}):f(x_{i})\in U_{i}\}.

Since
X
is metric (hence Hausdorff), we can shrink
V
to avoid all
x_{i}\neq x_{0}
.
Because
x_{0}
is non-isolated, pick
x\in V
with
x\neq x_{0}
and
x\neq x_{i}
for all
i
.

Choose
\delta>0
so small that the closed ball
\overline{B}(x,\delta)
is disjoint from
\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}
.
Define a continuous bump
b:X\to[0,2]
with
b(x)=2
and
b\equiv 0
on
\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}

(e.g.
b(y)=2\cdot\max\{0,1-d(y,x)/\delta\}
).
Then
b\in N
(since
b(x_{i})=0\in U_{i}
), but
\mathrm{ev}(b,x)=b(x)=2\notin U
,
so
(b,x)\in N\times V
but
(b,x)\notin\mathrm{ev}^{-1}(U)
. Contradiction.
∎

Corollary 4.8

(Pointwise topology does not yield exponentials)
.

Under the hypotheses of
proposition

4.7
,
C(X,\mathbb{R})_{\mathrm{pt}}
cannot be
an exponential object
\mathbb{R}^{X}
in
\mathbf{Top}
, because any exponential requires a continuous joint evaluation map.

Remark 4.9
.

For compact metric
X
, the compact-open topology on
C(X,Y)
coincides with uniform convergence
(for metric
Y
). Thus compact-open/uniform does support exponentials in
\mathbf{Top}
.
But the oracle-induced topology from point evaluations is the strictly weaker pointwise topology,
and this is exactly where the exponential property breaks (joint evaluation fails).

5.
Effective Enrichment: Represented Spaces Induced By Countable
\Lambda

We recall some basic definitions from Weihrauch theory.

Definition 5.1

(Represented space)
.

A
represented space
is a pair
X=(|X|,\delta_{X})
where

\delta_{X}:\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}\to|X|
is a partial surjection.
A
p\in\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}
with
\delta_{X}(p)=x
is called a
name
of
x
.

Definition 5.2

(Standard representations for
\mathbb{C}
and countable products)
.

Fix a computable dense sequence

Q:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{C}.

Let
\delta_{\mathbb{C}}
be the standard Cauchy representation of
\mathbb{C}
induced by
the usual metric and the dense sequence
Q
.
(Equivalently, any Cauchy representation induced by the usual metric and a computable
dense sequence; any two such Cauchy representations are computably isomorphic.)

For a sequence of represented spaces
(D_{n},\delta_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, define the product
representation
\delta_{\prod D_{n}}
on
\prod_{n}D_{n}
via any fixed computable coding
of sequences of names into one name. In particular, this yields the standard product
representation
\delta_{\mathbb{C}^{\mathbb{N}}}
on
\mathbb{C}^{\mathbb{N}}
.

Definition 5.3

(Countable evaluation set and evaluation-table map)
.

Let
\Lambda\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{\Omega}
be
countable
. Fix once and for all

\Lambda=\{f_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}},\qquad f_{n}:\Omega\to\mathbb{C}.

Define the
evaluation-table map

\mathrm{Ev}_{\Lambda}:\Omega\to\mathbb{C}^{\mathbb{N}},\qquad\mathrm{Ev}_{\Lambda}(A):=(f_{n}(A))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}.

Define the
\Lambda
-information set

\mathcal{I}_{\Lambda}:=\mathrm{Ev}_{\Lambda}(\Omega)\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{\mathbb{N}}.

Equip
\mathbb{C}^{\mathbb{N}}
with the product representation
\delta_{\mathbb{C}^{\mathbb{N}}}
in the sense of
definition

5.2
,
and equip
\mathcal{I}_{\Lambda}
with the induced subspace representation, denoted
\delta_{\mathcal{I}_{\Lambda}}
.

Lemma 5.4

(Consistency
\Rightarrow

\Xi
factors through
\mathcal{I}_{\Lambda}
)
.

Assume the consistency
“axiom”
holds for
\mathcal{P}=\bigl(\Xi,\Omega,(\mathcal{M},d),\Lambda\bigr)
and that

\Lambda=\{f_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is countable. Let
\mathrm{Ev}_{\Lambda}:\Omega\to\mathbb{C}^{\mathbb{N}}

be the evaluation-table map and
I_{\Lambda}:=\mathrm{Ev}_{\Lambda}(\Omega)
.
Then there is a unique map
\widehat{\Xi}:I_{\Lambda}\to\mathcal{M}
such that

\widehat{\Xi}(\mathrm{Ev}_{\Lambda}(A))=\Xi(A)
for all
A\in\Omega
.

Proof.

This is up to notation the exact same statement as
theorem

3.2
(3), still we give again a proof here for completeness. Define
\widehat{\Xi}:I_{\Lambda}\to\mathcal{M}
by

\widehat{\Xi}(x):=\Xi(A)\quad\text{for any }A\in\Omega\text{ with }\mathrm{Ev}_{\Lambda}(A)=x.

We must show this is well-defined. Suppose
A,B\in\Omega
satisfy

\mathrm{Ev}_{\Lambda}(A)=\mathrm{Ev}_{\Lambda}(B)
. Then
f_{n}(A)=f_{n}(B)
for every
n
,
hence
f(A)=f(B)
for every
f\in\Lambda
. By the consistency
“axiom”
, this implies

\Xi(A)=\Xi(B)
. Therefore
\widehat{\Xi}
is well-defined.

By construction,
\widehat{\Xi}(\mathrm{Ev}_{\Lambda}(A))=\Xi(A)
for all
A
.
Uniqueness follows because
\mathrm{Ev}_{\Lambda}:\Omega\to I_{\Lambda}
is surjective.
∎

Remark 5.5

(Enumeration-(in)dependence)
.

Let
(f_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(g_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be
bijections

\mathbb{N}\to\Lambda
(i.e. enumerations without repetition) of the same countable set
\Lambda
.
Then there exists a (unique) permutation
\pi:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
such that
g_{n}=f_{\pi(n)}
for all
n
.

The induced coordinate permutation

P_{\pi}:\mathbb{C}^{\mathbb{N}}\to\mathbb{C}^{\mathbb{N}},\qquad P_{\pi}\bigl((z_{n})_{n}\bigr):=(z_{\pi(n)})_{n}

is always a homeomorphism (for the product topology). Moreover, with respect to the
standard product representation on
\mathbb{C}^{\mathbb{N}}
, the map
P_{\pi}
is computable (and hence
a computable isomorphism of represented spaces) iff
\pi
is a computable
function.

Consequently,
\mathcal{I}_{\Lambda}\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{\mathbb{N}}
obtained from two enumerations are
always homeomorphic, but they are computably isomorphic only under computable reindexings.

5.1.
Weihrauch Reducibility, Iterated Limits, And The SCI-Rank

Definition 5.6

(Pairing/tupling function)
.

Fix once and for all a computable bijection

\langle\cdot\rangle_{k}:\mathbb{N}^{k+1}\to\mathbb{N}

for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
(coding
(n_{1},\dots,n_{k},m)
into one natural number).

Definition 5.7

(The limit operator
\operatorname{lim}
on Baire space)
.

Fix a computable bijection
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle:\mathbb{N}^{2}\to\mathbb{N}
.
For
p\in\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}
write
p_{n}(k):=p(\langle n,k\rangle)
.
Define
\operatorname{lim}:\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}\to\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}
by

p\in\mathrm{dom}(\operatorname{lim})\;:\Longleftrightarrow\;(\forall k\in\mathbb{N})\;\text{the sequence }n\mapsto p_{n}(k)\text{ is eventually constant},

and then

(\operatorname{lim}(p))(k):=\lim_{n\to\infty}p_{n}(k).

Definition 5.8

(Iterates of
\operatorname{lim}
)
.

Define
\operatorname{lim}^{(0)}:=\mathrm{id}_{\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}}
and
\operatorname{lim}^{(n+1)}:=\operatorname{lim}\circ\operatorname{lim}^{(n)}
for
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.

Definition 5.9

(Realizer)
.

Let
f:\subseteq X\rightrightarrows Y
be a multi-valued map between represented spaces

X=(|X|,\delta_{X})
and
Y=(|Y|,\delta_{Y})
. A partial map

F:\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}\to\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}

realizes
f
, written
F\vdash f
, if for every
p\in\mathrm{dom}(\delta_{X})
with

\delta_{X}(p)\in\mathrm{dom}(f)
one has
\delta_{Y}(F(p))\in f(\delta_{X}(p))
.

Definition 5.10

(Weihrauch reducibility on represented spaces)
.

Let
f:\subseteq X\rightrightarrows Y
and
g:\subseteq Z\rightrightarrows W

be multivalued functions on represented spaces.

•

We say
f
is
Weihrauch reducible
to
g
and write
f\leq_{\mathrm{W}}g

if there exist computable partial functions
H,K:\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}\to\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}
such that
for every realizer
G\vdash g
, the function

p\ \mapsto\ K\bigl(\langle p,G(H(p))\rangle\bigr)

is a realizer of
f
.

•

We say
f
is
strongly Weihrauch reducible
to
g
and write

f\leq_{\mathrm{sW}}g
if there exist computable
H,K
such that for every
G\vdash g
,

p\ \mapsto\ K\bigl(G(H(p))\bigr)

realizes
f
(so the post-processor
K
does
not
see the original input name
p
).

We write
f\equiv_{\mathrm{W}}g
if
f\leq_{\mathrm{W}}g
and
g\leq_{\mathrm{W}}f
.

We will also use the notation of a
“tightening”
, which is defined as follows: For mulitvalued functions
f,g:\subseteq X\rightrightarrows Y
on represented spaces
X,Y
, we call
f\preceq g
a tightening of
g
by
f
iff
(\mathrm{dom}(g)\subseteq\mathrm{dom}(f)\,\land\,(\forall x\in\mathrm{dom}(g))f(x)\subseteq g(x))
.

Definition 5.11

(SCI-rank on the Weihrauch preorder)
.

For any (multi-valued)
f
between represented spaces, define its
Weihrauch-SCI rank

\mathrm{rank}_{\mathrm{W}}(f)\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\cup\{\infty\}

by

\mathrm{rank}_{\mathrm{W}}(f):=\begin{cases}\min\{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}:\ f\leq_{\mathrm{W}}\operatorname{lim}^{(k)}\},&\text{if this set is nonempty,}\\
\infty,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Theorem 5.12

(Existence and well-posedness of
\mathrm{rank}_{\mathrm{W}}
)
.

For every (multi-valued)
f
between represented spaces,
\mathrm{rank}_{\mathrm{W}}(f)
is well-defined.
Moreover:

(1)

If
f\equiv_{\mathrm{W}}g
, then
\mathrm{rank}_{\mathrm{W}}(f)=\mathrm{rank}_{\mathrm{W}}(g)
(so it descends to Weihrauch degrees).

(2)

If
f\leq_{\mathrm{W}}g
, then
\mathrm{rank}_{\mathrm{W}}(f)\leq\mathrm{rank}_{\mathrm{W}}(g)
(order-preserving).

Proof.

Well-definedness:
Let
S_{f}:=\{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}:\ f\leq_{\mathrm{W}}\operatorname{lim}^{(k)}\}
.
If
S_{f}=\mathchar 31\relax
, define
\mathrm{rank}_{\mathrm{W}}(f):=\infty
.
If
S_{f}\neq\mathchar 31\relax
, then
S_{f}\subseteq\mathbb{N}_{0}
has a least element by well-ordering of
\mathbb{N}_{0}
; define
\mathrm{rank}_{\mathrm{W}}(f)
to be this minimum.

(1):
If
f\equiv_{\mathrm{W}}g
, then
f\leq_{\mathrm{W}}g
and
g\leq_{\mathrm{W}}f
.
Hence
S_{g}\subseteq S_{f}
and
S_{f}\subseteq S_{g}
, so
S_{f}=S_{g}
and the minima (or emptiness) coincide.

(2):
If
f\leq_{\mathrm{W}}g
and
k\in S_{g}
, then
g\leq_{\mathrm{W}}\operatorname{lim}^{(k)}
.
By transitivity of
\leq_{\mathrm{W}}
,
f\leq_{\mathrm{W}}\operatorname{lim}^{(k)}
, so
k\in S_{f}
.
Thus
S_{g}\subseteq S_{f}
, hence
\min S_{f}\leq\min S_{g}
in the nonempty case, and the
\infty
case is immediate.
∎

Remark 5.13

(This is the
“rank along the iterated-limit chain”
)
.

The map
\mathrm{rank}_{\mathrm{W}}
is a canonical order-preserving map from the Weihrauch preorder to the chain
(\mathbb{N}_{0}\cup\{\infty\},\leq)
induced by the increasing family

\operatorname{lim}^{(0)}\leq_{\mathrm{W}}\operatorname{lim}^{(1)}\leq_{\mathrm{W}}\operatorname{lim}^{(2)}\leq_{\mathrm{W}}\cdots
.
No further SCI-specific structure is needed for
theorem

5.12
.

5.2.
Connecting
\mathrm{rank}_{\mathrm{W}}
Back To SCI Towers (Type-2 SCI)

Definition 5.14

(Type-2 SCI for a represented-space problem)
.

Let
f:\subseteq X\rightrightarrows Y
be a (multi-valued) function between represented spaces.
Define the
Type-2 SCI
of
f
by

\mathrm{SCI}_{\mathrm{TTE}}(f):=\mathrm{rank}_{\mathrm{W}}(f).

Remark 5.15

(Why this is the correct
“effective”
SCI notion)
.

In the Type-2 setting, the fundamental discontinuity operator is
\operatorname{lim}
.
The equality
\mathrm{SCI}_{\mathrm{TTE}}(f)\leq k\iff f\leq_{\mathrm{W}}\operatorname{lim}^{(k)}

is exactly the content of
definition

5.14
; it is commonly recorded as a theorem/observation in the literature, see e.g.,
[
NP18
, Observation 43]
- which we will discuss further below - but the
logical well-posedness
is already captured by
theorem

5.12
.

Definition 5.16

(Weihrauch-rank of a countable-
\Lambda
SCI problem)
.

Let
\mathcal{P}=\bigl(\Xi,\Omega,(\mathcal{M},d),\Lambda\bigr)
be an SCI computational problem in the sense of
definition

2.4
-
definition

2.18

with
countable

\Lambda
in the sense of
definition

5.3
.
Assume additionally that
\mathcal{M}
is equipped with a fixed representation
\delta_{\mathcal{M}}

(e.g. the Cauchy representation induced by
(\mathcal{M},d)
if
\mathcal{M}
is separable and complete).

Let
\widehat{\Xi}:\mathcal{I}_{\Lambda}\to\mathcal{M}
be the factor map from
lemma

5.4
,
viewed as a (single-valued) function between represented spaces

(\mathcal{I}_{\Lambda},\delta_{\mathcal{I}_{\Lambda}})
and
(\mathcal{M},\delta_{\mathcal{M}})
.

Define the
Weihrauch-SCI rank
of the SCI problem
\mathcal{P}
by

\mathrm{rank}_{\mathrm{W}}(\mathcal{P}):=\mathrm{rank}_{\mathrm{W}}(\widehat{\Xi}).

Theorem 5.17

(Existence / well-posedness of
\mathrm{rank}_{\mathrm{W}}(\mathcal{P})
)
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
satisfy the hypotheses of
definition

5.16
.
Then
\mathrm{rank}_{\mathrm{W}}(\mathcal{P})
exists and is independent of

(1)

the chosen enumeration of the countable set
\Lambda
, up to computable permutations
of
\mathbb{N}
(i.e. up to computable reindexings of coordinates);

(2)

the choice of standard Cauchy representation of
\mathbb{C}

(with respect to the usual metric and a computable dense sequence);

(3)

the choice of representation of
\mathcal{M}
within its computable isomorphism class
(i.e. replacing
\delta_{\mathcal{M}}
by a computably equivalent representation).

Proof.

Existence is immediate from
theorem

5.12
applied to the represented-space map
\widehat{\Xi}
.
Independence under computable reindexings follows from
remark

5.5
and invariance
of
\mathrm{rank}_{\mathrm{W}}
under computable isomorphisms.
Independence of standard admissible representations can be proven similarly: changing to a computably isomorphic representation
does not change the Weihrauch degree of
\widehat{\Xi}
, hence not its rank.
∎

Remark 5.18

(Scope and limitations)
.

Theorem

5.12
and
theorem

5.17
give a fully precise, foundational
existence and well-posedness
theory for an SCI-rank compatible with Weihrauch theory. But one has to be careful: This is an
effective
refinement of SCI.
The original SCI definitions (cf.
definition

2.4
-
definition

2.18
) are not, by themselves,
statements about computability in the Type-2 sense; they must be enriched with representations (and typically
with
\Delta_{1}
-style access to evaluation values, see e.g. the discussion around inexact information in
[
Sor25
]
)
to connect to Weihrauch degrees.

Remark 5.19

(Classical status of
\mathrm{rank}_{\mathrm{W}}
and
\mathrm{SCI}_{\mathrm{TTE}}
)
.

In Weihrauch theory, the limit operator
\lim
is a central benchmark degree.
It characterizes limit computability:
f\leq_{\mathrm{W}}\lim\iff f
is limit computable.
Moreover, reducibility to iterates of
\lim
corresponds to effective Borel measurability levels,
and
\lim
enjoys strong algebraic properties (cylinder, strong parallelizability, etc.).

Remark 5.20

(Relation to SCI type
A
and type
G
in the SCI literature)
.

In the SCI framework, type
A
towers impose effectivity restrictions at the base level,
whereas type
G
towers are (typically) purely information-based and allow arbitrary
post-processing of finitely many oracle values without measurability/continuity constraints.

Therefore,
\mathrm{SCI}_{G}
does
not
automatically coincide with
\mathrm{SCI}_{\mathrm{TTE}}
.
For type
A
, one often expects compatibility with
\mathrm{SCI}_{\mathrm{TTE}}
in analytic settings,
but this requires that the evaluation device provides Type-2 names/approximations of oracle values
and that the base computations avoid non-computable primitives (e.g. exact real comparisons).

Definition 5.21

(Cylinder (strong form))
.

For a problem
g:\subseteq Z\rightrightarrows W
, let

\mathrm{id}\times g:\subseteq X\times Z\rightrightarrows X\times W,\qquad(\mathrm{id}\times g)(x,z):=\{(x,w):w\in g(z)\}.

We call
g
a (strong) cylinder if

\mathrm{id}\times g\leq_{sW}g.

Definition 5.22

(Transparency (operational form on Baire))
.

A problem
g
is
transparent
if for every computable functional

T:\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}\to\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}
there exists a computable functional

S:\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}\to\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}
such that

g\circ S\preceq T\circ g.

Lemma 5.23

(
\operatorname{lim}^{(n)}
is a transparent cylinder)
.

For every
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, the problem
\operatorname{lim}^{(n)}
is a transparent cylinder.

Proof.

This result is very classical, still we give a short proof here. We argue by induction on
n
.

Base case
n=0
:
By definition,
\operatorname{lim}^{(0)}=\mathrm{id}_{\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}}
.
The identity is a cylinder since
\mathrm{id}\times\mathrm{id}\leq_{\mathrm{sW}}\mathrm{id}

via computable pairing/unpairing. It is transparent because for any computable
h
we can take

h^{\prime}:=h
and obtain
h\circ\mathrm{id}=\mathrm{id}\circ h^{\prime}
.

Inductive step:
Assume
\operatorname{lim}^{(n)}
is a transparent cylinder. Since
\operatorname{lim}
itself is a
transparent cylinder, and the class of transparent cylinders is closed under composition,
it follows that
\operatorname{lim}\circ\operatorname{lim}^{(n)}=\operatorname{lim}^{(n+1)}
is a transparent cylinder.

Thus
\operatorname{lim}^{(n)}
is a transparent cylinder for all
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.
∎

Lemma 5.24

(Normal form for reductions to a single-valued transparent cylinder)
.

Let
g
be a single-valued transparent cylinder and let
f
be any problem.
Then

f\leq_{\mathrm{W}}g\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\exists\text{ computable }K:\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}\to\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}\text{ such that }g\circ K\preceq f.

Proof.

(
\Rightarrow
):

Assume
f\leq_{W}g
. Since
g
is a cylinder, we may assume without loss
of generality that the reduction is strong, i.e.
f\leq_{sW}g
. Hence there exist
computable functionals
H_{0},T
such that

T\circ g\circ H_{0}\preceq f.

By transparency of
g
, there exists a computable functional
S
such that

g\circ S\preceq T\circ g.

Therefore

g\circ S\circ H_{0}\preceq T\circ g\circ H_{0}\preceq f,

so we can take
K:=S\circ H_{0}
.

(
\Leftarrow
):

Suppose
g\circ K\preceq f
for some computable
K
. Then

\mathrm{dom}(f)\subseteq\mathrm{dom}(g\circ K)\quad\text{and}\quad(g\circ K)(x)\subseteq f(x)

for all
x\in\mathrm{dom}(f)
. Hence the reduction is witnessed by the pre-processor
K
and
the identity post-processor.
∎

We will now generalize and precise
[
NP18
, Observation 43, Theorem 44]
, as Neumann-Pauly worked just in the special reell-valued case.

Definition 5.25

(TTE tower of height
n
for a problem)
.

Let
f:\subseteq X\rightrightarrows Y
be a problem between represented spaces.
We say that
f
has a
TTE tower of height
n
if

f\ \leq_{\mathrm{W}}\ \operatorname{lim}^{(n)}.

Theorem 5.26

(Pure-tower normal form)
.

Let
f
be any problem between represented spaces. Then for every
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}

f\leq_{\mathrm{W}}\operatorname{lim}^{(n)}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\exists\ \text{computable }K:\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}\to\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}\ \text{ such that }\operatorname{lim}^{(n)}\circ K\preceq f.

In particular, the definition
\mathrm{SCI}_{\mathrm{TTE}}(f)=\mathrm{rank}_{\mathrm{W}}(f)
coincides with the least height of a
“computable
n
-limit normal form”
for
f
.

Proof.

(
\Rightarrow
):
By
lemma

5.23
,
\operatorname{lim}^{(n)}
is a single-valued
transparent cylinder. Hence
lemma

5.24
yields a computable
K
such that

\operatorname{lim}^{(n)}\circ K\preceq f.

(
\Leftarrow
):
If
\operatorname{lim}^{(n)}\circ K\preceq f
for some computable
K
, then

f\leq_{W}\operatorname{lim}^{(n)}
is witnessed by taking the Weihrauch pre-processor to be
K

and the post-processor to be the identity.
∎

For completeness we also state the Neumann-Pauly results adapted to our notation. But first we recall a few basic definitions from classical computability theory.

Definition 5.27

(BSS-computability (minimal interface))
.

A (partial) function
F:\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{\star}\to\mathbb{R}^{\star}
is
BSS-computable
if it is computed by
a Blum–Shub–Smale machine over
(\mathbb{R},+,-,\cdot,/,<)
in the usual sense.

Definition 5.28

(
n
-tower on
\mathbb{R}^{\star}
and
\mathrm{SCI}_{\mathrm{BSS}}
)
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{\star}\to\mathbb{R}^{\star}
be (single-valued).
An
n
-tower
for
f
is a function

F:\mathbb{N}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{\star}\to\mathbb{R}^{\star}

such that for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{\star}
the iterated limit exists (in the unique
\mathbb{R}^{k}
-component where the
values eventually live) and equals
f(x)
, i.e.

f(x)=\lim_{i_{1}\to\infty}\cdots\lim_{i_{n}\to\infty}F(i_{1},\dots,i_{n},x).

Define
\mathrm{SCI}_{\mathrm{BSS}}(f)
as the least
n
such that there exists a
BSS-computable

n
-tower for
f
.
Define
\mathrm{SCI}_{\mathrm{TTE}}^{\mathbb{R}^{\star}}(f)
as the least
n
such that there exists a
TTE-computable

n
-tower for
f
(with respect to the usual Cauchy representation of reals).

Theorem 5.29

(Neumann-Pauly: comparison of
\mathrm{SCI}_{\mathrm{BSS}}
and
\mathrm{SCI}_{\mathrm{TTE}}
on
\mathbb{R}^{\star}
)
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{\star}\to\mathbb{R}^{\star}
.

(1)

For every
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,

\mathrm{SCI}_{\mathrm{TTE}}^{\mathbb{R}^{\star}}(f)\leq n\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad f\leq_{\mathrm{W}}\operatorname{lim}^{(n)}.

(2)

If
\mathrm{SCI}_{\mathrm{TTE}}^{\mathbb{R}^{\star}}(f)\geq 2
or
\mathrm{SCI}_{\mathrm{BSS}}(f)\geq 2
, then

\mathrm{SCI}_{\mathrm{TTE}}^{\mathbb{R}^{\star}}(f)=\mathrm{SCI}_{\mathrm{BSS}}(f).

Proof.

This is exactly the work in
[
NP18
, Definition 42, Observation 43, Theorem 44]
.
∎

Remark 5.30

(Why the models can differ at height
0
or
1
)
.

Exact comparisons in the BSS model can decide discontinuous predicates instantly (height
0
), while Type-2 computability cannot. A standard example is the sign function

\mathrm{sgn}:\mathbb{R}\to\{-1,0,1\}
: it is BSS-computable (one comparison to
0
), but not TTE-computable. However it is limit-computable (one limit suffices), so it typically lands at height
1
in the TTE/Weihrauch sense. Neumann-Pauly show that such discrepancies disappear once the SCI is at least
2
.

6.
A-Case

Definition 6.1

(SCI computational problem (abstract))
.

A
computational problem
(in the SCI sense) is a quadruple

\mathcal{P}=(\Xi,\Omega,(\mathcal{M},d),\Lambda)

where
\Omega
is an input set,
(\mathcal{M},d)
a metric space,
\Xi:\Omega\to\mathcal{M}
a target
function, and
\Lambda
a family of evaluation maps
\lambda:\Omega\to\mathbb{C}
.
Assume again the
consistency/separation
“axiom”
:

\Xi(A)\neq\Xi(B)\ \Longrightarrow\ \exists\lambda\in\Lambda:\ \lambda(A)\neq\lambda(B).

Definition 6.2

(Countable evaluation representation induced by
\Lambda
)
.

Assume
\Lambda
is countable and fix an enumeration
\Lambda=\{\lambda_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
.
Define the
evaluation embedding

\mathrm{ev}_{\Lambda}:\Omega\to\mathbb{C}^{\mathbb{N}},\qquad\mathrm{ev}_{\Lambda}(A):=\big(\lambda_{i}(A)\big)_{i\in\mathbb{N}}.

Fix any representation
\delta_{\mathbb{C}^{\mathbb{N}}}
of
\mathbb{C}^{\mathbb{N}}
(e.g. the product of Cauchy representations).
Define a represented space

\Omega_{\Lambda}:=\big(\mathrm{ev}_{\Lambda}(\Omega),\ \delta_{\Omega_{\Lambda}}\big),\qquad\delta_{\Omega_{\Lambda}}:=\delta_{\mathbb{C}^{\mathbb{N}}}\upharpoonright\mathrm{ev}_{\Lambda}(\Omega).

By the consistency
“axiom”
,
\Xi
factors uniquely through
\mathrm{ev}_{\Lambda}
:
there exists a unique
\widehat{\Xi}:\mathrm{ev}_{\Lambda}(\Omega)\to\mathcal{M}
with

\Xi=\widehat{\Xi}\circ\mathrm{ev}_{\Lambda}
.

Definition 6.3

(Effective (A-case) towers as TTE towers)
.

Assume
\mathcal{M}
is equipped with a representation
\delta_{\mathcal{M}}
compatible with
d

(e.g. Cauchy representation if
(\mathcal{M},d)
is complete and separable).
We define the
effective A-case SCI
of
\mathcal{P}
as the TTE/Weihrauch SCI of the induced map:

\mathrm{SCI}_{A,\mathrm{TTE}}(\mathcal{P})\;:=\;\mathrm{SCI}_{\mathrm{TTE}}(\widehat{\Xi}:\Omega_{\Lambda}\to\mathcal{M}).

Theorem 6.4

(A-case
\Longleftrightarrow
Weihrauch rank, once a representation is fixed)
.

With the notation above,

\mathrm{SCI}_{A,\mathrm{TTE}}(\mathcal{P})\;=\;\mathrm{rank}_{\mathrm{W}}(\widehat{\Xi}).

In particular, after fixing a representation of the evaluation data, the A-case SCI is
exactly the least number of iterated
\lim
-oracles needed to compute
\Xi
in the Type-2 sense.

Proof.

This is immediate from
definition

5.14
.
∎

Remark 6.5

(BSS vs. Type-2 readings of
“arithmetic towers”
)
.

The SCI-literature often leaves implicit what kind of access one has to real oracle
values. Two standard readings are: (i) If the interface supplies

Type-2 names/approximations
of each oracle value and the base post-processing uses
only Type-2 computable primitives, then the natural
notion is the name-based arithmetic height
\mathrm{SCI}_{A,\mathrm{TTE}}
from

definition

6.3
. In particular, any explicit arithmetic tower built from Type-2
computable operations witnesses an upper bound on
\mathrm{SCI}_{A,\mathrm{TTE}}
.

(ii) If the interface supplies
exact real/complex constants
and admits exact
comparisons as primitive operations (an atomic real interface), then the natural
formalization is the oracle-BSS model from
definition

2.16
, and the
corresponding arithmetic height is
\mathrm{SCI}^{\mathrm{BSS}}_{A}
(cf.
remark

2.19
).

7.
G-Case

7.1.
Type-G SCI (Unrestricted Post-Processing)

We shortly recall the
G
-case definition of the SCI.

Definition 7.1

(Type-G towers and
\mathrm{SCI}_{G}
)
.

Let
\mathcal{P}=(\Xi,\Omega,(\mathcal{M},d),\Lambda)
be an SCI computational problem with general algorithms.
A
type-G tower
is a tower whose base maps are merely
general algorithms
,
with no further restrictions (this is
“
\alpha=G
”
).

Define
\mathrm{SCI}_{G}(\mathcal{P})
as the least height of a type-G tower computing
\Xi
, or
\infty
if none exists.

Remark 7.2

(What type-G does
not
assume)
.

Type-G imposes
no
regularity requirement on the base-level post-processing of queried oracle values:
no computability, no continuity, no Borel measurability. Some further discussion about that can be found e.g. in
[
Sor26
, §1.6]
.

7.2.
Purely Logical Consequences Of The Type-G Setting

Definition 7.3

(Finite-query factorisation)
.

Let
\mathcal{P}=(\Xi,\Omega,(\mathcal{M},d),\Lambda)
.
We say that
\Xi
admits a
finite-query factorisation through
\Lambda
if there exist

f_{1},\dots,f_{m}\in\Lambda
and a map
G:\operatorname{im}(f_{1},\dots,f_{m})\to\mathcal{M}
such that

\Xi(A)=G\bigl(f_{1}(A),\dots,f_{m}(A)\bigr)\qquad\forall A\in\Omega,

where
\operatorname{im}(f_{1},\dots,f_{m})\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{m}
is the range of
A\mapsto(f_{1}(A),\dots,f_{m}(A))
.

Theorem 7.4

(Finite-query factorisations force
\mathrm{SCI}_{G}=0

[
Sor26
, Appendix C.2, Theorem C.5]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{P}=\bigl(\Xi,\Omega,(\mathcal{M},d),\Lambda\bigr)
be an SCI computational problem. Suppose there exist

f_{1},\dots,f_{m}\in\Lambda
and a function
G:\mathbb{C}^{m}\to\mathcal{M}
such that

\Xi(A)=G\bigl(f_{1}(A),\dots,f_{m}(A)\bigr)\quad\text{for all }A\in\Omega,

i.e.
\Xi
admits a finite-query factorisation through
\Lambda
.
Then
\mathrm{SCI}_{G}(\mathcal{P})=0
.

Proof.

This is already proven in
[
Sor26
, Appendix C.2, Theorem C.5]
, still we give a proof here for completeness. Define
\Gamma:\Omega\to\mathcal{M}
by
\Gamma(A):=G(f_{1}(A),\dots,f_{m}(A))
and define the query policy

\Lambda_{\Gamma}(A):=\{f_{1},\dots,f_{m}\}
for all
A\in\Omega
.

Then
(\Gamma,\Lambda_{\Gamma})
is a general algorithm: if
B
agrees with
A
on all queries in

\Lambda_{\Gamma}(A)
, then
f_{i}(B)=f_{i}(A)
for all
i
, hence
\Gamma(B)=\Gamma(A)
, and clearly also

\Lambda_{\Gamma}(B)=\Lambda_{\Gamma}(A)
because the query set is constant.

Moreover
\Gamma=\Xi
by construction, so
\Xi
is computed by a height-
0
tower (i.e. a single
general algorithm). Therefore
\mathrm{SCI}_{G}(\mathcal{P})=0
.
∎

Remark 7.5

(Core logical point)
.

Theorem

7.4
is
pure logic
: once base post-processing is unrestricted,

any
map
G
on the finite transcript is allowed.
This is the reason why descriptive-set/Type-2 lower-bound methods do not automatically imply type-G lower bounds,
as stressed in
[
Sor26
, Appendix C.2]
.

7.3.
Monotonicity In Tower Type: G Vs. A

The following theorem was also proven in
[
Sor26
, Appendix C.3, Lemma C.7]
but we state and prove it here again to adjust it to our new precised SCI notation and to resolve possible concerns about the notation that was used there and is meant here.

Theorem 7.6

(Monotonicity in tower type)
.

For every SCI computational problem
\mathcal{P}
for which
\mathrm{SCI}^{\mathrm{BSS}}_{A}(\mathcal{P})

is defined, we have

\mathrm{SCI}_{G}(\mathcal{P})\ \leq\ \mathrm{SCI}^{\mathrm{BSS}}_{A}(\mathcal{P}).

Proof.

Let
k:=\mathrm{SCI}^{\mathrm{BSS}}_{A}(\mathcal{P})
and fix a height-
k
arithmetic tower
witnessing this value. Each base map of this tower is a BSS-arithmetic algorithm
in the sense of
definition

2.16
, hence, in particular, it depends on only finitely many oracle
values and is therefore a general algorithm in the sense of
definition

2.6
.
Thus the same tower is admissible as a type-
G
tower, witnessing

\mathrm{SCI}_{G}(\mathcal{P})\leq k
.
∎

7.4.
Why Type-G Is Not Captured By Weihrauch/TTE In General

Lemma 7.7

(Finite
\mathrm{rank}_{\mathrm{W}}
implies Borel regularity)
.

Let
f:\subseteq X\to Y
be single-valued between represented spaces and assume

f\leq_{\mathrm{W}}\operatorname{lim}^{(n)}
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.
Then
f
is Borel-measurable with respect to the represented-space induced topologies.
In particular, if
Y=\{0,1\}
is discrete, then
f^{-1}(\{1\})
is a Borel subset of
X
.

Proof.

If
n=0
, then
\operatorname{lim}^{(0)}=\mathrm{id}
and
f\leq_{\mathrm{W}}\mathrm{id}
implies that
f
is computable,
hence continuous, hence Borel measurable.

Now assume
n\in\mathbb{N}
. By the characterization theorem for the
\operatorname{lim}
-hierarchy (c.f.
[
BGP21
, Thm. 6.5]
),

f\leq_{\mathrm{W}}\operatorname{lim}^{(n)}
implies that
f
is effectively
\Sigma^{0}_{n+1}
-measurable, and in particular
Borel measurable.
∎

Theorem 7.8

(Type-G can have
\mathrm{SCI}_{G}=0
while
\mathrm{SCI}_{\mathrm{TTE}}=\infty
)
.

Assume a standard represented space structure on
X=\mathbb{C}
(e.g. Cauchy representation),
and let
Y=\{0,1\}
be discrete.
Let
A\subseteq\mathbb{C}
be a fixed analytic-complete (hence non-Borel) set.
Define the single-valued map

f:\mathbb{C}\to\{0,1\},\qquad f(z):=\mathbf{1}_{A}(z).

Consider the SCI computational problem

\mathcal{P}=(\Xi,\Omega,(\mathcal{M},d),\Lambda)\quad\text{with}\quad\Omega=\mathbb{C},\ \mathcal{M}=\{0,1\},\ \Xi=f,\ \Lambda=\{\mathrm{id}_{\mathbb{C}}\}.

Then

(1)

\mathrm{SCI}_{G}(\mathcal{P})=0
;

(2)

\mathrm{SCI}_{\mathrm{TTE}}(f)=\infty
(equivalently,
\mathrm{rank}_{\mathrm{W}}(f)=\infty
).

Proof.

(1):
Since
f=\mathbf{1}_{A}\circ\mathrm{id}_{\mathbb{C}}
, this is a finite-query factorisation
through the single evaluation
\mathrm{id}_{\mathbb{C}}\in\Lambda
with post-processing
G=\mathbf{1}_{A}
.
Thus
\mathrm{SCI}_{G}(\mathcal{P})=0
by
theorem

7.4
.

(2):
Suppose for contradiction that
f\leq_{\mathrm{W}}\operatorname{lim}^{(n)}
for some
n
.
Then by
lemma

7.7
, the set
A=f^{-1}(\{1\})
must be Borel,
contradicting the choice of
A
. Hence
f\not\leq_{\mathrm{W}}\operatorname{lim}^{(n)}
for all
n
, so
\mathrm{SCI}_{\mathrm{TTE}}(f)=\infty
.
∎

Corollary 7.9

(No general equivalence (nor implication) between type-G SCI and Weihrauch ranks)
.

There is no function

h:\mathbb{N}_{0}\cup\{\infty\}\to\mathbb{N}_{0}\cup\{\infty\}

such that, uniformly over all SCI computational problems
\mathcal{P}
(even with countable
\Lambda
),

\mathrm{SCI}_{G}(\mathcal{P})\leq n\Longrightarrow\mathrm{SCI}_{\mathrm{TTE}}(\widehat{\Xi})\leq h(n)

or

\mathrm{SCI}_{\mathrm{TTE}}(\widehat{\Xi})\leq n\Longrightarrow\mathrm{SCI}_{G}(\mathcal{P})\leq h(n).

Proof.

The first failure is witnessed by
theorem

7.8
(take
n=0
).
The second direction fails in general because
\mathrm{SCI}_{\mathrm{TTE}}
is defined only after fixing
a represented-space structure (names), whereas
\mathrm{SCI}_{G}
is a purely extensional
finite-oracle-value model; without compatibility assumptions between these two input interfaces, no implication can be derived uniformly.
∎

7.5.
How To Recover A Weihrauch-Style Bridge: Add Regularity At The Base

We capture here the same idea as in
[
Sor26
]
, namely equipping
\Omega
with a Polish topology. We will construct here a first solution for a ”meta-bridge”, being a special case of the full intermediate hierarchy later.

Definition 7.10

(Borel-restricted base algorithms (decision case))
.

Assume that
(\Omega,\tau)
is Polish and that
\mathcal{M}=\{0,1\}
carries the discrete topology.
A general algorithm
\Gamma:\Omega\to\{0,1\}
is called
Borel-restricted
if there exist

m\in\mathbb{N}
, a finite list
f_{1},\dots,f_{m}\in\Lambda
, and a Borel-measurable map

G:\mathbb{C}^{m}\to\{0,1\}
(with
\mathbb{C}^{m}
in the usual Polish topology) such that

\Gamma(A)=G\bigl(f_{1}(A),\dots,f_{m}(A)\bigr)\qquad\forall A\in\Omega,

and each
f_{i}:(\Omega,\tau)\to\mathbb{C}
is Borel-measurable.

Lemma 7.11

(Borel base
\Rightarrow
Borel acceptance)
.

In the setting of
definition

7.10
, the acceptance set
\Gamma^{-1}(\{1\})
of a Borel-restricted base algorithm is Borel in
(\Omega,\tau)
.

Proof.

By hypothesis,
\Gamma=G\circ(f_{1},\dots,f_{m})
with
G
Borel and each
f_{j}
Borel.
Thus
(f_{1},\dots,f_{m}):\Omega\to\mathbb{C}^{m}
is Borel measurable, and

\Gamma^{-1}(\{1\})=(f_{1},\dots,f_{m})^{-1}(G^{-1}(\{1\}))
is Borel.
∎

Theorem 7.12

(Borel barrier for finite towers of decision problems
[
Sor26
, Appendix C.1]
)
.

Let
\Xi=\mathbf{1}_{A}:\Omega\to\{0,1\}
be a decision problem on a Polish space
(\Omega,\tau)
.
Assume there exists a finite-height tower computing
\Xi
such that

•

every base algorithm has Borel acceptance set (e.g. the base algorithms are Borel-restricted);

•

each limit in the tower is a pointwise limit along
\mathbb{N}
(as in the SCI definition).

Then
A
is Borel in
(\Omega,\tau)
.

Consequently, if
A
is analytic-complete (hence non-Borel), then no such Borel-restricted finite tower exists.

Remark 7.13

(What premise is missing in unrestricted type-G)
.

Theorem

7.12
explains precisely what is missing to connect type-G lower bounds to
Weihrauch/Type-2 methods:
one needs a
regularity constraint
(Borel/continuous/effective) on base post-processing.
Unrestricted type-G drops this premise, so non-Borel (even analytic-complete) acceptance sets do not
imply
\mathrm{SCI}_{G}=\infty
;
theorem

7.4
and
theorem

7.8

exhibit the failure mechanism. This is the logical content of
[
Sor26
, Appendix C.2]
.

8.
The Intermediate Hierarchy

The last section is the inspiration point for our full intermediate hierarchy now. But first of all we sharply precise what we mean by a hierarchy.

Definition 8.1

(Preorder)
.

A
preorder
is a pair
(I,\preceq)
where
I
is a set and
\preceq
is a relation on
I

that is reflexive and transitive.

Definition 8.2

(Hierarchy indexed by a preorder)
.

Let
U
be a set (the
“universe”
) and let
(I,\preceq)
be a preorder.
A family of subclasses
(\mathcal{H}_{i})_{i\in I}
with
\mathcal{H}_{i}\subseteq U
is called an

(I,\preceq)
-hierarchy on
U
if

i\preceq j\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\mathcal{H}_{i}\subseteq\mathcal{H}_{j}.

Definition 8.3

(Regularity classes on
\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}
)
.

Let
\mathcal{R}
be a class of partial maps
F:\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}\to\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}
.
We consider the following standard classes (all w.r.t. the usual product topology on
\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}
):

•

\mathsf{Comp}
: the (Type-2) computable partial maps.

•

\mathsf{Cont}
: the continuous partial maps.

•

\mathsf{Bor}
: the Borel-measurable partial maps (measurable w.r.t. the Borel
\sigma
-algebra on
\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}
).

•

\mathsf{Baire}
: the Baire-measurable partial maps (measurable w.r.t. the
\sigma
-algebra generated by the
zero sets / equivalently by continuous real-valued maps).

We order such classes by inclusion:
\mathcal{R}\subseteq\mathcal{S}
.

Proposition 8.4

(Inclusion chain)
.

One has

\mathsf{Comp}\subseteq\mathsf{Cont}\subseteq\mathsf{Bor}\subseteq\mathsf{Baire}.

Proof.

Let
(\Omega,\tau)
and
(\mathcal{M},d)
be Polish spaces.

(
\mathsf{Comp}\subseteq\mathsf{Cont}
):

Every Type-2 computable function between represented spaces is continuous with respect
to the induced topologies; in particular, every computable realizer is continuous on

\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}
, hence the induced map on points is continuous.

(
\mathsf{Cont}\subseteq\mathsf{Bor}
):

If
\Gamma:(\Omega,\tau)\to(\mathcal{M},d)
is continuous, then for every open set
U\subseteq\mathcal{M}
,
the preimage
\Gamma^{-1}(U)
is open in
\Omega
, hence Borel. Thus
\Gamma
is Borel
measurable.

(
\mathsf{Bor}\subseteq\mathsf{Baire}
):

On a Polish space, every Borel set has the Baire property. Therefore, if
\Gamma
is
Borel measurable, then for every open
U\subseteq\mathcal{M}
, the set
\Gamma^{-1}(U)
is Borel
hence has the Baire property, which is exactly Baire measurability.
∎

8.1.
Continuous And Borel-Weihrauch Reducibilities

8.1.1.
Parametrized Weihrauch Reducibilities

Definition 8.5

(
\mathcal{R}
-Weihrauch reducibility)
.

Let
f:\subseteq X\rightrightarrows Y
and
g:\subseteq U\rightrightarrows V
be problems between represented spaces.
Let
\mathcal{R}
be a class of partial maps
\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}\to\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}
.
We write

f\leq_{\mathrm{W}}^{\mathcal{R}}g

if there exist
H,K\in\mathcal{R}
such that for every realizer
G\vdash g
,
the map

p\mapsto K\bigl(\langle p,G(H(p))\rangle\bigr)

is a realizer of
f
. (Here
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle:\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}\times\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}\to\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}
is any fixed computable pairing.)

Remark 8.6

(Standard special cases)
.

•

\leq_{\mathrm{W}}^{\mathsf{Comp}}
is the usual (computable) Weihrauch reducibility.

•

\leq_{\mathrm{W}}^{\mathsf{Cont}}
is the standard
continuous Weihrauch reducibility
: Weihrauch reducibility has a continuous counterpart obtained by replacing the reduction functions
H,K
by continuous ones, see e.g.
[
BG11
]
.

Proposition 8.7

(
\leq_{\mathrm{W}}^{\mathcal{R}}
is a preorder and monotone in
\mathcal{R}
)
.

Fix
\mathcal{R}
that is closed under composition and contains all computable pairing/projection maps on
\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}
.
Then
\leq_{\mathrm{W}}^{\mathcal{R}}
is a preorder.
Moreover, if
\mathcal{R}\subseteq\mathcal{S}
, then

f\leq_{\mathrm{W}}^{\mathcal{R}}g\ \Longrightarrow\ f\leq_{\mathrm{W}}^{\mathcal{S}}g.

Proof.

Reflexivity is witnessed by taking
H(p)=p
and
K(\langle p,q\rangle)=q
, which are computable (hence in
\mathcal{R}
).
Transitivity is obtained by composing witnesses; closure of
\mathcal{R}
under composition and pairing ensures
the composed witnesses remain in
\mathcal{R}
.
Monotonicity in
\mathcal{R}
is immediate: any witnesses in
\mathcal{R}
are also witnesses in
\mathcal{S}
.
∎

Definition 8.8

(Continuous and Borel Weihrauch reducibilities)
.

Define

\leq_{\mathrm{W}}^{\mathrm{cont}}:=\leq_{\mathrm{W}}^{\mathsf{Cont}},\qquad\leq_{\mathrm{W}}^{\mathrm{Bor}}:=\leq_{\mathrm{W}}^{\mathsf{Bor}}.

Remark 8.9

(Oracle-relative view of
\leq_{\mathrm{W}}^{\mathrm{cont}}
)
.

A common equivalent presentation is:
f\leq_{\mathrm{W}}^{\mathrm{cont}}g
iff there exists an oracle

p\in\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}
such that
f\leq_{\mathrm{W}}^{p}g
(i.e. the reduction functionals are computable relative to
p
).
This is exactly the viewpoint used when Brattka-Gherardi-Pauly relativize
[
BGP21
, Theorem 6.5]
and characterize the non-effective Borel hierarchy via existence of an oracle.

8.2.

8.2.1.

Definition 8.10

(Topologized SCI problem)
.

A
topologized SCI computational problem
is a tuple

\mathcal{P}_{\tau}=(\Xi,\Omega,(\mathcal{M},d),\Lambda,\tau)

where
(\Xi,\Omega,(\mathcal{M},d),\Lambda)
is a computational problem in the SCI sense, and
\tau
is a topology on

.
We write
\mathcal{B}(\Omega)
for the Borel
\sigma
-algebra of
(\Omega,\tau)
and
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{M})
for the Borel
\sigma
-algebra of the metric space
(\mathcal{M},d)
.

Remark 8.11

(Why the extra structure is necessary)
.

Already in
[
Sor26
, Appendix C.1]
it was stressed that to even state
“
A\subseteq\Omega
is Borel/analytic/complete”
one must equip
\Omega
with a Polish topology (or at least a standard Borel structure). Thus any attempt to import descriptive-set lower bounds into SCI necessarily fixes such structure.

8.2.2.
Regularity-Restricted Base Algorithms And Towers

Let

\mathfrak{R}_{\mathrm{top}}:=\{\mathsf{Cont},\mathsf{Bor},\mathsf{Baire}\}.

For a topological space
X
and a metric space
Y
, define

\mathrm{Reg}_{\mathsf{Cont}}(X,Y):=\{h:X\to Y:h\text{ is continuous}\},

\mathrm{Reg}_{\mathsf{Bor}}(X,Y):=\{h:X\to Y:h\text{ is Borel measurable}\},

\mathrm{Reg}_{\mathsf{Baire}}(X,Y):=\{h:X\to Y:h\text{ is Baire measurable}\}.

We order the labels in
\mathfrak{R}_{\mathrm{top}}
by

r\sqsubseteq_{\mathrm{top}}s\quad:\Longleftrightarrow\quad\mathrm{Reg}_{r}(X,Y)\subseteq\mathrm{Reg}_{s}(X,Y)

for every topological space
X
and every metric space
Y
.
Equivalently,

\mathsf{Cont}\sqsubseteq_{\mathrm{top}}\mathsf{Bor}\sqsubseteq_{\mathrm{top}}\mathsf{Baire}.

Therefore in
section

8.2
-
section

9
we use
r,s\in\mathfrak{R}_{\mathrm{top}}
for topological regularity labels, other than we did in
section

8.1
, where we reserved calligraphic letters
\mathcal{R},\mathcal{S}
for classes of
realizers
on
\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}
.

Definition 8.12

(Fixed-query
(\Lambda,r)
-base algorithms)
.

Let
\mathcal{P}_{\tau}=(\Xi,\Omega,(\mathcal{M},d),\Lambda,\tau)
be a topologized SCI computational problem and let

r\in\mathfrak{R}_{\mathrm{top}}
.
A general algorithm
\Gamma:\Omega\to\mathcal{M}
is called
fixed-query
(\Lambda,r)
-regular
if there exist
f_{1},\dots,f_{m}\in\Lambda
and a map
G:\mathbb{C}^{m}\to\mathcal{M}
with
G\in\mathrm{Reg}_{r}(\mathbb{C}^{m},\mathcal{M})
such that

\Gamma(A)=G\bigl(f_{1}(A),\dots,f_{m}(A)\bigr)\quad\forall A\in\Omega,

and each
f_{i}:(\Omega,\tau)\to\mathbb{C}
belongs to
\mathrm{Reg}_{r}(\Omega,\mathbb{C})
.

In the special case
r=\mathsf{Bor}
and
\mathcal{M}=\{0,1\}
with discrete topology, this definition reduces to
definition

7.10
.

Definition 8.13

(
r
-general algorithms and
r
-towers)
.

Let
\mathcal{P}_{\tau}=(\Xi,\Omega,(\mathcal{M},d),\Lambda,\tau)
be a topologized SCI computational problem,
and let
r\in\mathfrak{R}_{\mathrm{top}}
.

An
r
-general algorithm
is a general algorithm
\Gamma:\Omega\to\mathcal{M}
such that

\Gamma\in\mathrm{Reg}_{r}(\Omega,\mathcal{M}).

Here, unlike
definition

8.12
, the finite query set may depend on the input!

A
height-
k

r
-tower
is a height-
k
SCI tower
whose base maps
\Gamma_{n_{k},\dots,n_{1}}:\Omega\to\mathcal{M}
are
r
-general algorithms.

The intuition here is: there are two distinct ways to enforce regularity at the base level.

Definition

8.12
restricts the
post-processing
from finitely many queried oracle values
(fixed-query form), while
definition

8.13
restricts the
entire
base algorithm as a map

(\Omega,\tau)\to(\mathcal{M},d)
(allowing adaptive querying but controlling descriptive complexity).
The restricted heights
\mathrm{SCI}_{G,r}
quantify how many iterated limits remain
once this regularity is enforced.

Definition 8.14

(Restricted SCI values)
.

For
r\in\mathfrak{R}_{\mathrm{top}}
define

\mathrm{SCI}_{G,r}(\mathcal{P}_{\tau})\;:=\;\min\Bigl\{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}:\text{there exists a height-}k\text{ $r$-tower computing }\Xi\Bigr\},

with value
\infty
if the set is empty.

8.2.3.
The Hierarchy Property

Theorem 8.15

(Restricted SCI models form a hierarchy in the regularity parameter)
.

Let
\mathcal{P}_{\tau}
be a fixed topologized SCI problem.
If
r\sqsubseteq_{\mathrm{top}}s
,
then

\mathrm{SCI}_{G,s}(\mathcal{P}_{\tau})\ \leq\ \mathrm{SCI}_{G,r}(\mathcal{P}_{\tau}).

In particular,

\mathrm{SCI}_{G,\mathsf{Baire}}(\mathcal{P}_{\tau})\ \leq\ \mathrm{SCI}_{G,\mathsf{Bor}}(\mathcal{P}_{\tau})\ \leq\ \mathrm{SCI}_{G,\mathsf{Cont}}(\mathcal{P}_{\tau}).

Proof.

Any height-
k

r
-tower is in particular a height-
k

s
-tower whenever

r\sqsubseteq_{\mathrm{top}}s
. The displayed chain follows from

\mathsf{Cont}\sqsubseteq_{\mathrm{top}}\mathsf{Bor}\sqsubseteq_{\mathrm{top}}\mathsf{Baire}.

∎

Theorem 8.16

(A two-parameter hierarchy: regularity and height)
.

Fix a universe
U
of topologized SCI computational problems.
For each pair
(r,k)
with
r\in\mathfrak{R}_{\mathrm{top}}
and
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, define

\mathcal{H}_{(r,k)}:=\{\ \mathcal{P}_{\tau}\in U\ :\ \mathrm{SCI}_{G,r}(\mathcal{P}_{\tau})\leq k\ \}.

Equip the index set

I:=\mathfrak{R}_{\mathrm{top}}\times\mathbb{N}_{0}

with the preorder

(r,k)\preceq(s,\ell)\quad:\Longleftrightarrow\quad r\sqsubseteq_{\mathrm{top}}s\text{ and }k\leq\ell.

Then
(\mathcal{H}_{(r,k)})_{(r,k)\in I}
is an
(I,\preceq)
-hierarchy in the sense of
definition

8.2
.

Proof.

If
(r,k)\preceq(s,\ell)
, then any
r
-tower of height
\leq k

is also an
s
-tower and has height
\leq\ell
. Hence
\mathcal{H}_{(r,k)}\subseteq\mathcal{H}_{(s,\ell)}
.
∎

Definition 8.17

(Optional refinement: add a query-policy parameter)
.

Let
\mathcal{Q}:=\{\mathrm{fix},\mathrm{ad}\}
.
For
q=\mathrm{fix}
use fixed-query
(\Lambda,r)
-regular base maps in the sense of
definition

8.12
;
for
q=\mathrm{ad}
use
r
-general base maps in the sense of
definition

8.13
.

Define the corresponding base-map class

\mathsf{Base}(q,r):=\begin{cases}\{\text{fixed-query $(\Lambda,r)$-regular base maps}\},&q=\mathrm{fix},\\
\{\text{$r$-general base maps}\},&q=\mathrm{ad}.\end{cases}

Define
\mathrm{SCI}^{q,r}_{G}(\mathcal{P}_{\tau})
as the least tower height for
\mathcal{P}_{\tau}
with base maps in
\mathsf{Base}(q,r)
.

Definition 8.18

(Index preorder for the
(q,r)
-refinement)
.

Let

I:=\mathcal{Q}\times\mathfrak{R}_{\mathrm{top}}\times\mathbb{N}_{0}.

For
i=(q,r,k)
and
j=(q^{\prime},r^{\prime},k^{\prime})
in
I
, define

i\preceq j\;:\Longleftrightarrow\;\mathsf{Base}(q,r)\subseteq\mathsf{Base}(q^{\prime},r^{\prime})\ \text{ and }\ k\leq k^{\prime}.

Then
(I,\preceq)
is a preorder.

Proposition 8.19

(Hierarchy property)
.

Fix a universe
U
of topologized SCI problems.
For each index
i=(q,r,k)\in I
define

\mathcal{H}_{i}\;:=\;\bigl\{\mathcal{P}_{\tau}\in U:\mathrm{SCI}^{q,r}_{G}(\mathcal{P}_{\tau})\leq k\bigr\}.

Then the family
(\mathcal{H}_{i})_{i\in I}
is an
(I,\preceq)
-hierarchy on
U
in the sense of

definition

8.2
.

Proof.

Let
i=(q,r,k)\preceq j=(q^{\prime},r^{\prime},k^{\prime})
and let
\mathcal{P}_{\tau}\in\mathcal{H}_{i}
.
Then
\mathrm{SCI}^{q,r}_{G}(\mathcal{P}_{\tau})\leq k
, so there exists a tower of height
\leq k

computing
\mathcal{P}_{\tau}
whose base maps lie in
\mathsf{Base}(q,r)
.
Since
\mathsf{Base}(q,r)\subseteq\mathsf{Base}(q^{\prime},r^{\prime})
and
k\leq k^{\prime}
,
the same tower is admissible for
(q^{\prime},r^{\prime})
and has height
\leq k^{\prime}
.
Hence
\mathrm{SCI}^{q^{\prime},r^{\prime}}_{G}(\mathcal{P}_{\tau})\leq k^{\prime}
, i.e.
\mathcal{P}_{\tau}\in\mathcal{H}_{j}
.
Therefore
\mathcal{H}_{i}\subseteq\mathcal{H}_{j}
.
∎

.

]

8.3.
Comparison Theorems: What Each Restriction Buys You

8.3.1.
Borel-Restricted Towers

Theorem 8.21

(Borel towers compute only Borel targets
[
Sor26
, Theorem C.3]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{P}_{\tau}
be a topologized SCI problem with
\tau
Polish.
If there exists a height-
k
Borel tower computing
\Xi
in the sense of
definition

8.13
with
r=\mathsf{Bor}
,
then
\Xi:(\Omega,\tau)\to(\mathcal{M},d)
is Borel measurable.
In particular, for a decision problem
\mathcal{M}=\{0,1\}
(discrete), the acceptance set is Borel.

Remark 8.22

(Why this recovers standard lower-bound methods)
.

In
[
Sor26
]
it is explained that in any SCI model in which base maps are required to be
“effective”
(TTE/recursive/Borel, etc.), the base maps are typically at least Borel; hence
theorem

8.21
yields an obstruction:
if the acceptance set is non-Borel (e.g. analytic-complete), then no finite-height such tower can decide it.
This is the formal (hidden) backbone behind many descriptive-set lower bounds in the arithmetic/effective SCI literature.

8.3.2.
Continuous Base: Baire-Class Height

Definition 8.23

(Baire classes (finite levels))
.

Let
X
be a topological space and
Y
a metric space.
Define inductively

•

\mathsf{BC}_{0}(X,Y)
is the class of continuous maps
X\to Y
.

•

\mathsf{BC}_{n+1}(X,Y)
is the class of pointwise limits of sequences of maps in
\mathsf{BC}_{n}(X,Y)
.

Theorem 8.24

(Continuous-base towers yield finite Baire class)
.

Let
\mathcal{P}_{\tau}
be a topologized SCI problem.
If
\Xi
is computed by a height-
k

\mathsf{Cont}
-tower in the sense of
definition

8.13
with
r=\mathsf{Cont}
,
then
\Xi\in\mathsf{BC}_{k}(\Omega,\mathcal{M})
. If moreover
(\Omega,\tau)
is Polish, then
\Xi
is Borel measurable.

Proof.

We argue by induction on
k
.

For
k=0
,
\Xi
is a continuous general algorithm, hence
\Xi\in\mathsf{BC}_{0}
by
definition

8.23
.

Assume the claim for
k-1
and let
\Xi
be computed by a height-
k

\mathsf{Cont}
-tower.
Unwinding the tower definition, there exist maps
\Gamma_{n_{k}}:\Omega\to\mathcal{M}
with

\Xi(\omega)=\lim_{n_{k}\to\infty}\Gamma_{n_{k}}(\omega)\quad\text{for all }\omega\in\Omega,

and each
\Gamma_{n_{k}}
is itself computed by a height-
(k-1)

\mathsf{Cont}
-tower (the inner limits).
By the induction hypothesis, each
\Gamma_{n_{k}}\in\mathsf{BC}_{k-1}(\Omega,\mathcal{M})
.
Hence
\Xi
is a pointwise limit of a sequence in
\mathsf{BC}_{k-1}
, i.e.
\Xi\in\mathsf{BC}_{k}
.

If moreover
(\Omega,\tau)
is Polish, then every finite Baire class map is Borel measurable,
hence
\Xi
is Borel measurable.
∎

8.3.3.
Continuous Weihrauch To
\operatorname{lim}^{(n)}
And Borel Levels

Theorem 8.25

(Continuous reducibility to
\operatorname{lim}^{(n)}
characterizes Borel measurability level)
.

Let
f
be a problem between represented spaces and let
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

(1)

f\leq_{\mathrm{W}}\operatorname{lim}^{(n)}
if and only if
f
is effectively
\Sigma^{0}_{n+1}
-measurable.

(2)

For an oracle
p\in\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}
,
f\leq_{\mathrm{W}}^{\,p}\operatorname{lim}^{(n)}
if and only if

f
is
\Sigma^{0}_{n+1}
-measurable relative to

.

(3)

Equivalently,
f\leq_{\mathrm{W}}^{\mathrm{cont}}\operatorname{lim}^{(n)}
if and only if
f
is (non-effective)

\Sigma^{0}_{n+1}
-measurable.

At level
n=0
one has

f\leq_{\mathrm{W}}\operatorname{lim}^{(0)}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad f\leq_{\mathrm{W}}\mathrm{id}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad f\text{ is computable},

and

f\leq_{\mathrm{W}}^{\mathrm{cont}}\operatorname{lim}^{(0)}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad f\leq_{\mathrm{W}}^{\mathrm{cont}}\mathrm{id}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad f\text{ is continuous}.

Proof.

The case
n\!=\!0
is immediate from
\operatorname{lim}^{(0)}=\mathrm{id}
. (1)-(2) is exactly
[
BGP21
, Theorem 6.5]
, together with its standard oracle relativization.
(3) follows from the well-known equivalence between continuous Weihrauch reducibility and
computable reducibility relative to some oracle.
∎

In summary we can now say

•

The

side is robust:
“height

forces (at most) Baire class

(

), and continuous Weihrauch reducibility to

characterizes exactly the corresponding

-measurability level (

).

•

The

), hence non-Borel acceptance sets block

•

In contrast, the unrestricted type-

and thus descriptive-set hardness alone does not imply any

(see also
[

, Theorem C.5, Corollary C.6]
).

Thus the

is mathematically concrete: it is the two-parameter hierarchy in

, and its interaction with Weihrauch theory is governed by the measurability characterizations around

(cf.
[

). For the

.

(Point-evaluation oracle as an evaluation set)

Let

.

We separate the three concrete families of deepest-level base maps from

Case 2:
\Omega_{X}^{m}

Case 3:

Outlook

In this paper - especially explicit in
appendix

A
- we reason in the usual classical metatheory (first-order classical logic with a
standard set-theoretic background, e.g.
\mathsf{ZFC}
). In that calculus,
the phrase
“premise (P) is logically necessary for desiderata (D1)-(D3)”
in

theorem

A.22
is shorthand for the
provability
claims

\mathsf{ZFC}\ \vdash\ (D1\wedge D2\wedge D3)\ \rightarrow\ (P1\wedge P2\wedge P3\wedge P4),

together with the pointwise consequences
\mathsf{ZFC}\vdash(D1\wedge D2\wedge D3)\to Pi
for
i=1,\ldots,4

witnessed by the explicit references in the proof. Likewise, the
“sufficiency”
clause is the
provability statement

\mathsf{ZFC}\ \vdash\ (P1\wedge P2\wedge P3\wedge P4\wedge P5)\ \rightarrow\ (D1\wedge D2).

Of course, what counts as a
logical
consequence depends on the chosen background
consequence relation and axioms;
theorem

A.22
is therefore a minimality claim

relative to the present metatheory
.

A reverse-mathematical analysis makes this dependence explicit by fixing a base theory

T
(typically
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}
) and internalizing the discussion as a
scheme over codes

for the relevant objects; see
[
Sim09
]
for a possible general framework.
Concretely, one restricts attention to instances of the SCI where the input/output types
are presented as represented spaces whose representations are coded by sets of naturals, and
where realizers/reductions are coded by indices or by accepted codes
for continuous/Borel functionals. Under such a translation:

•

(P1) becomes the requirement that representations (or admissible equivalence classes of
representations) are part of the data, so that
“
\mathcal{R}
-computable/realizable”
is a

definite
predicate on codes (cf.
definition

A.1
(
Well-definedness
)).

•

(P2) becomes the requirement that the real/complex interface is name-based (e.g. Cauchy
names), not atomic exact comparison; this is precisely what makes computability imply
topological continuity on the induced representation-topologies (cf. the discontinuity obstruction in
corollary

A.10
).

•

(P3)-(P4) become closure axioms on the code-class for allowable pre-/post-processing,
ensuring that the induced reducibilities and height notions are stable under identity,
composition, and basic data operations (pairing/projections), i.e. that they genuinely define
preorders/hierarchies rather than ad hoc relations.

For type-A, after fixing representations/names and restricting admissible base
post-processing to a class
\mathcal{R}
, one obtains equality between pure tower
height and iterated-
\operatorname{lim}
rank precisely when the chosen
\mathcal{R}
satisfies the
corresponding
\operatorname{lim}
-normal-form property.

With this perspective,
theorem

A.22
suggests the following reverse-mathematics
questions, which are
not
settled in the present work:

(1)

For computability-based choices of
\mathcal{R}
(e.g.
\mathcal{R}=\mathsf{Comp}
or

\mathcal{R}=\mathsf{FMC}
), determine the weakest subsystem
T
in which the full package

(D1\wedge D2\wedge D3)\ \rightarrow\ (P1\wedge P2\wedge P3\wedge P4)

can be formalized and proved.

(2)

For higher regularity choices of
\mathcal{R}
(e.g.
\mathcal{R}=\mathsf{Cont}
or
\mathcal{R}=\mathsf{Bor}
),
pin down how the answer depends on the chosen
coding
of continuous/Borel functionals and on
the background axioms needed to develop the corresponding closure/measurability theory inside
T

(e.g. potential needs for
\mathsf{WKL}_{0}
,
\mathsf{ACA}_{0}
, or
\mathsf{ATR}_{0}
-type principles).

In short:
theorem

A.22
is a
\mathsf{ZFC}
-level minimality result, but it naturally opens the door to a
fine-grained calibration of its
foundational strength
(and of the SCI-Weihrauch comparison theorems)
within the reverse-mathematics program.

(2)

(

for all

(3)

(

To have everything compact here, we also recall again, what a representation and realizer is.

Definition A.2

(Represented space and realizer)
.

A
represented space
is
X=(|X|,\delta_{X})
where
\delta_{X}:\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}\to|X|
is a partial surjection.
A partial function

realizes

(written

\delta_{Y}(F(p))=f(\delta_{X}(p))\quad\text{for all }p\in\mathrm{dom}(\delta_{X})\text{ with }\delta_{X}(p)\in\mathrm{dom}(f).

Definition A.3

(Representation-induced topology)
.

Given
(X,\delta_{X})
, define a topology
\tau_{\delta_{X}}
on
|X|
by declaring

open iff

\delta_{X}^{-1}(U)\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}
is open (in the product topology on
\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}
).

Now we first recall a well known but for our purpose very crucial lemma.

Lemma A.4

(Type-2 computable implies being continuous)
.

Let
f:|X|\to|Y|
be Type-2 computable between represented spaces.
Then
f:(|X|,\tau_{\delta_{X}})\to(|Y|,\tau_{\delta_{Y}})
is continuous.

Proof.

Let
U\subseteq|Y|
be open. Then
\delta_{Y}^{-1}(U)
is open in
\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}
.
Let
F\vdash f
be a computable realizer; any computable
F
is continuous on
\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}
.
Hence
F^{-1}(\delta_{Y}^{-1}(U))
is open in
\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}
.
But
F^{-1}(\delta_{Y}^{-1}(U))=\delta_{X}^{-1}(f^{-1}(U))
by the realizer condition.
Thus
\delta_{X}^{-1}(f^{-1}(U))
is open, i.e.
f^{-1}(U)
is open in
\tau_{\delta_{X}}
.
∎

By this lemma, we will now show, that the
\mathrm{SCI}_{\mathrm{A}}
necessarily has to fix a representation.

Theorem A.5

(Representation dependence:
any
function can be made computable)
.

Let
Y=(|Y|,\delta_{Y})
be any represented space and let
f:|X|\to|Y|
be any function on a bare set
|X|
.
Assume
|X|
has some representation
\delta_{X}:\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}\to|X|
(not necessarily admissible).
Then there exists another representation
\delta_{X}^{f}:\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}\to|X|
such that
f:(|X|,\delta_{X}^{f})\to(|Y|,\delta_{Y})
is Type-2 computable.

Proof.

Fix a computable pairing
\langle\,\cdot,\cdot\,\rangle:\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}\times\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}\to\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}
with computable projections
\pi_{0},\pi_{1}
.
Define
\delta_{X}^{f}:\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}\to|X|
by

\delta_{X}^{f}(\langle\,\cdot,\cdot\,\rangle(p,q)):=\delta_{X}(p)\quad\text{provided that }\delta_{Y}(q)=f(\delta_{X}(p)).

This is a partial surjection because for each
x\in|X|
choose any name
p
with
\delta_{X}(p)=x

and any name
q
with
\delta_{Y}(q)=f(x)
, then
\delta_{X}^{f}(\langle\,\cdot,\cdot\,\rangle(p,q))=x
.

Now define
F:\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}\to\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}
by
F(r):=\pi_{1}(r)
.
Then for any

we have
\delta_{Y}(F(r))=\delta_{Y}(q)=f(\delta_{X}(p))=f(\delta_{X}^{f}(r))
.
Hence
F\vdash f
, and
F
is computable. Thus
f
is computable w.r.t.
\delta_{X}^{f}
.
∎

Corollary A.6

(Logical necessity of fixing representations)
.

Any notion of
“computable function on
|X|
”
that does not fix a representation (or at least an equivalence
class of representations) is ill-posed: by
theorem

A.5
every function can be made
computable by a change of representation.
Therefore, specifying representations of inputs and outputs is a logically necessary premise for calling a framework a computability model in the Type-2 sense.

Remark A.7

(This is the first minimality premise)
.

Corollary

is a purely formal obstruction: without representations there is no canonical

.
This is why any comparison of SCI-

to Weihrauch theory
must
first pass to represented spaces
(e.g. via the countable evaluation-table representation).

A.1.

Definition A.8

(Atomic real interface (BSS-style primitive))

(Atomic comparison violates Type-2 continuity constraints)

Let

(2)

\mathrm{sgn}

Proof.

.

(2):

, any Type-2 computable map

∎

(Second minimality premise: to compare to Weihrauch, reals must be given by names)

(cf.

(Connection to SCI

.

This is exactly the semantic difference behind the BSS vs. TTE gap at low heights firstly recognized in

.

.

Proof.

Pairing/projections and constant maps are computable, hence in

constant in

.

We also recall from the intermediate hierarchy

Appendix B
Kihara’s Piecewise-Continuity Reducibilities And Their Relation To The Intermediate SCI Hierarchy

B.1.
What Kihara Does: Parametrized Reducibilities By Regularity Classes

First we recall Kihara’s definition of uniformization.

Definition B.1

(Multifunctions and uniformizations
[
Kih17
, §2.1]
)
.

Let
X,Y
be topological spaces. A
(partial) multi-valued function
is a set
F\subseteq X\times Y
,
written
F:\subseteq X\rightrightarrows Y
, with

F(x):=\{y\in Y:(x,y)\in F\},\qquad\mathrm{dom}(F):=\{x\in X:F(x)\neq\mathchar 31\relax\}.

A
uniformization
(or
selection
) of
F
is a function
\psi:\mathrm{dom}(F)\to Y
such that
\psi(x)\in F(x)
for all
x\in\mathrm{dom}(F)
.

Definition B.2

(
(\mathcal{K},\mathcal{H})
-Weihrauch reducibility
[
Kih17
, §2.2]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{K},\mathcal{H}
be classes of functions between appropriate spaces.
For multifunctions
F:X_{0}\rightrightarrows Y_{0}
and
G:X_{1}\rightrightarrows Y_{1}

we say that

F\leq_{\mathrm{W}}^{(\mathcal{K},\mathcal{H})}G

if there exist

h:X_{0}\to X_{1}\text{ in }\mathcal{H},\qquad k:X_{0}\times Y_{1}\to Y_{0}\text{ in }\mathcal{K},

such that for every uniformization
\psi
of
G
, the map

x\mapsto k\bigl(x,\psi(h(x))\bigr)

is a uniformization of
F
. Equivalently

(\forall x\in X_{0})(\forall y\in Y_{1})\ \bigl(y\in G(h(x))\Rightarrow k(x,y)\in F(x)\bigr).

Remark B.3

(The
“same idea”
as the intermediate SCI hierarchy)
.

In the intermediate hierarchy one fixes a topological regularity label
r\in\mathfrak{R}_{\mathrm{top}}

and restricts the admissible base-level post-processing to maps lying in the corresponding
class
\mathrm{Reg}_{r}
.

Kihara does essentially the analogous move
on the reduction level
:

\mathcal{K}
is the admissible post-processing class
k(\cdot,\cdot)
, and

\mathcal{H}
is the admissible pre-processing class
h(\cdot)
.
Thus the family
\leq_{\mathrm{W}}^{(\mathcal{K},\mathcal{H})}
is an explicit
regularity-parametrized hierarchy of reducibilities.

Proposition B.4

(Monotonicity in the regularity parameters)
.

If
\mathcal{K}\subseteq\mathcal{K}^{\prime}
and
\mathcal{H}\subseteq\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
then

F\leq_{\mathrm{W}}^{(\mathcal{K},\mathcal{H})}G\ \Longrightarrow\ F\leq_{\mathrm{W}}^{(\mathcal{K}^{\prime},\mathcal{H}^{\prime})}G.

Proof.

Immediate: any witnesses
h\in\mathcal{H}
and
k\in\mathcal{K}
are also in

\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
and

B.4.

\Longleftrightarrow

)
.

This is exactly

(Restricted

(This is the precise conceptual connection)

This is exactly the kind of move one wants for an intermediate SCI hierarchy:

B.5.
A Major Difference: Closure/Transitivity Issues

Proposition B.13

(Why closure under composition matters
[
Kih17
, §2.3]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{K}
be a class of single-valued functions on a space
X
.
Define the induced function-reducibility

f\leq_{\mathcal{K}}g\iff(\exists\theta\in\mathcal{K})\ \ f=g\circ\theta.

If
\mathcal{K}
is closed under composition and contains
\mathrm{id}_{X}
, then
\leq_{\mathcal{K}}
is transitive.
If
\mathcal{K}
is not closed under composition, then
\leq_{\mathcal{K}}
need not be transitive.

Proof.

If
\mathcal{K}
is closed under composition:

f=g\circ\theta_{1}
and
g=h\circ\theta_{2}
implies
f=h\circ(\theta_{2}\circ\theta_{1})
with
\theta_{2}\circ\theta_{1}\in\mathcal{K}
.

If
\mathcal{K}
is not closed under composition, pick
\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\in\mathcal{K}
with

\theta_{2}\circ\theta_{1}\notin\mathcal{K}
.
Let
h=\mathrm{id}_{X}
,
g=\theta_{2}
, and
f=\theta_{2}\circ\theta_{1}
.
Then
f\leq_{\mathcal{K}}g
(via
\theta_{1}
) and
g\leq_{\mathcal{K}}h
(via
\theta_{2}
),
but
f\not\leq_{\mathcal{K}}h
since this would require
f\in\mathcal{K}
.
∎

Remark B.14

(Difference to SCI towers)
.

This closure issue is explicitly noted by Kihara in
[
Kih17
, §2.3, Weihrauch Reduction.]
:
for a fixed labeled tree
T
, the class of
T
-piecewise continuous functions
need not be closed under composition, so the induced
T
-reducibilities may fail transitivity,
and one has to pass to suitable closures/collections (veins, transitive closures, etc.)
to recover stable degree theories.

By contrast, in SCI theory one typically wants a well-defined height notion stable under
algorithmic composition; any intermediate SCI restriction indexed by a topological label
r\in\mathfrak{R}_{\mathrm{top}}

must therefore be chosen so that the corresponding admissible base-map classes have the
relevant closure properties (composition, pairing, etc.) if one wants a clean preorder/hierarchy.

B.6.
Another Difference: SCI Has An Explicit Information Model

Remark B.15

(Evaluation sets vs. full-information topology)
.

SCI starts from a quadruple
(\Omega,\Lambda,\Xi,\mathcal{M})
:
algorithms can read only finitely many values
f(A)
with
f\in\Lambda
.
Kihara’s framework, in contrast, is formulated directly on spaces
X
and functions/relations

F\subseteq X\times Y
; the reducibility notion is about allowable regularity of maps
h
and
k
,
not about a chosen information interface
\Lambda
.

Therefore

•

Kihara’s hierarchy is a
regularity hierarchy of reductions
on a fixed topological input space.

•

The intermediate SCI hierarchy is a
regularity hierarchy of algorithms
relative to a chosen
information interface
\Lambda
.

This difference matters: changing the evaluation set
\Lambda
in
\mathcal{P}
changes the induced topology/representation on
\Omega
,
hence changes what
“continuous post-processing”
even means in SCI-computational problems.

B.7.
A Further SCI-Adjacent Point: Flowcharts As Computation Models

Remark B.16

(Flowcharts and BSS-style computation
[
Kih17
, Remark 2.12]
)
.

Kihara remarks that if one drops well-foundedness and uses labeled directed graphs,
one obtains a natural model of computation by flowcharts with noncomputable tests,
and explicitly connects this to BSS machines with
\Pi^{0}_{1}
tests.
This is directly adjacent to SCI
G
-style
“general”
algorithms (unrestricted post-processing),
and suggests that the right intermediate SCI restrictions may be formulated in terms of

(tree/graph ranks), not only Borel/Baire measurability. We leave this point open for further future research.

Appendix C
A Minimal
“Algorithmic Regularity”
Envelope For SCI Base Maps

C.1.
Why
“A-Towers”
Should Not Be Conflated With
\mathsf{Comp}

In the SCI literature, a type-
A
base algorithm is allowed to use
comparisons
on oracle-provided real/complex values (and equality tests, if the model permits) as primitive operations.
Such branching is
not
Type-2 computable in general (hence not in
\mathsf{Comp}
), because Type-2 computation is necessarily continuous.
Therefore, the right
“regularity class”
for many concrete
A
-base maps is typically
\mathsf{Bor}
(often even very low Borel rank), not
\mathsf{Comp}
.
This is exactly the same phenomenon as for standard benchmark maps such as
\mathrm{LPO}
: it is Borel but discontinuous (hence not Type-2 computable; to be more precise: it is so called
“
1
-weakly discontinuous”
, proven in
[
Uft25
, Lemma 3.2(i)]
). Cf. the use of LPO/LLPO as discontinuity benchmarks in computable analysis/Weihrauch theory
[
BG11
,
BGP21
]
.

We provide now a general lemma, that is classical but very crucial here.

Lemma C.1

(Borelness of finite arithmetic decision trees)
.

Let
Y
be a Polish space, and let
r_{1},\dots,r_{m}:Y\to\mathbb{R}
be continuous.
Consider a partial function
F:\subseteq Y\to D
into a finite discrete set
D

obtained by a finite decision tree whose internal nodes test predicates of the form

r_{i}(y)<q
or
r_{i}(y)\leq q
with
q\in\mathbb{Q}
, and whose leaves output constants in
D
.
Then
F
is Borel measurable.
Moreover, each fiber
F^{-1}({d})
is in the finite Boolean algebra generated by sets of the form

{y:\ r_{i}(y)<q}
and
{y:\ r_{i}(y)\leq q}
, hence is a
\Delta^{0}_{2}
-Borel set.

Proof.

For each rational
q
, the set
{y:\ r_{i}(y)<q}
is open and
{y:\ r_{i}(y)\leq q}
is closed by continuity of
r_{i}
.
A finite decision tree produces each fiber as a finite Boolean combination of these open/closed sets.
Finite Boolean combinations of open (equivalently, of open and closed) sets are Borel, in fact
\Delta^{0}_{2}
.
Since
D
is discrete and finite, Borelness of all fibers is equivalent to Borel measurability of
F
.
∎

Remark C.2

(What fails for
\mathsf{Cont}
)
.

Even if all
r_{i}
are continuous, a branching test
r_{i}(y)<q
produces a discontinuity at any boundary point
y_{0}
with
r_{i}(y_{0})=q
at which the decision is not locally constant.
Thus
lemma

C.1
gives a robust default:
Borel
is the minimal regularity one can assert for
“finite arithmetic + comparisons”
base maps without extra structure.

C.2.3.
Are The A-Case Base Maps In
\mathsf{Comp}
?

Theorem C.6

(
A

.

Fix indices
(n_{2},n_{1},n_{0},z)
. Take the arithmetic residual

F\ \mapsto\ h_{n_{2},n_{1},n_{0}}(z,F).

This is a finite arithmetic/comparison expression in finitely many oracle values and fixed rational tables, hence defines a Borel map (indeed a very low-level Borel map) on any Polish presentation of the input space.

However, because comparisons on real quantities are discontinuous in general, such base maps are not guaranteed to lie in
\mathsf{Comp}
(Type-2 computable maps), unless one strengthens the model/representation so that the relevant comparisons become decidable in a continuous way (which is impossible for standard real representations).

Proof.

In
[
Sor25
, Theorem 5.27, Step 1]
, the arithmetic residual

F\mapsto h_{n_{2},n_{1},n_{0}}(z;F)

is built from finitely many quantities of the form

F\mapsto F(x_{P}),\qquad F\mapsto\varphi_{j}(F(x_{P})),

together with fixed constants
\varphi_{j}(x_{P})
,
\mathrm{Meas}_{n_{0}}(P)
, and the
arithmetic approximation
\mathrm{Pow}_{p,n_{0}}
.

The map

F\mapsto F(x_{P})

is continuous on
(\Omega,\tau_{\infty})
, while

is Borel in the Haar-type setting. Hence every scalar subexpression occurring in the
finite decision process for

is Borel. Finite arithmetic
combinations of Borel functions are Borel, finite minima preserve Borelness, and every
branch test of the form

r(F)<q\quad\text{or}\quad r(F)\leq q

with

Borel has a Borel acceptance set. Therefore

F\mapsto h_{n_{2},n_{1},n_{0}}(z;F)

is Borel.

For the second claim, any Type-2 computable map must be continuous. The arithmetic
base maps in
[
Sor25
]
use genuine comparison branching at threshold surfaces, and
such branching is not forced to be continuous for standard real representations. Hence
these base maps are not guaranteed to lie in
\mathsf{Comp}
.
∎

C.3.
Easy
“Fixes”
To Increase Regularity - And What They Cost

Proposition C.7

(Fix 1 for the finite-minimum residual schemes)
.

Consider a scalar residual of the form

h(F)=\min_{c\in C}R_{c}(F)

with finite coefficient set
C
, where each
R_{c}(F)
is assembled from finitely many
quantities
\psi(F(x_{P}))
by arithmetic operations.

If
\psi
is discontinuous, then

need not be

\psi
is replaced by a continuous dictionary
\theta
, then every

is continuous and hence

is continuous.

Proof.

Each evaluation map

F\mapsto F(x_{P})

is continuous. Therefore

F\mapsto\psi(F(x_{P}))

is continuous if and only if
\psi
is continuous. Finite arithmetic combinations preserve
continuity, and a finite minimum of continuous functions is continuous. This proves the
continuous case.

For the discontinuous case, choose an input
F_{0}
and a sampled point

at which

\psi
has a jump. Then a perturbation of
F(x_{P})
across that jump changes

, and therefore

, discontinuously.
∎

This proposition is exactly the motivation in
[
Sor25
]
for the switch away from a discontinuous Haar-type construction when uniform Lipschitz/quadrature control is required.

.

Assume the scalar residuals

are continuous.
Then the map

defined by the strict threshold condition

is always Borel.
In general it cannot be forced to be continuous without changing either:

•

the output representation/topology on compact sets, or

the hard thresholding step (e.g. replacing it by a continuous “soft” output such as the full residual field

).

Proof.

This follows directly from
theorem

.
∎

(What

Appendix D
Feasible Real RAMs Are Not A Drop-In Replacement For The BSS-Based Model

It is natural to ask whether
definition

2.13
-
definition

2.18
should be based on the
feasible real random access machines (FRAM) of
[
BH98
]
rather than on oracle
BSS machines. The answer is
no
in the literal form used here, and the
reason is structural.

First difference: the input model:

The present BSS-reading is an
oracle model
: for an SCI problem

\mathcal{P}=(\Xi,\Omega,(\mathcal{M},d),\Lambda)
with countable

\Lambda=(f_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
, an input
A\in\Omega
is accessed through the
exact evaluation oracle

\mathcal{O}_{A}:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R}^{2},\qquad\mathcal{O}_{A}(i):=\bigl(\operatorname{Re}(f_{i}(A)),\operatorname{Im}(f_{i}(A))\bigr).

Thus the machine model is tailored to countably many evaluations and to the SCI
information interface
\Lambda
.
By contrast, a FRAM in the sense of
[
BH98
]
is a finite
flowchart machine
\mathbb{M}=(X,Y,F)
whose input and output spaces are finite products
of
\mathbb{N}
and
\mathbb{R}
. No countable oracle interface occurs in their
basic definition. Therefore, if one wants a faithful SCI-version of FRAMs, one
must
first add
an oracle layer to the Brattka-Hertling model; this is
extra structure not present in
[
BH98
]
.

Second difference: exact atomic tests versus finite-precision tests:

In
definition

2.15
-
definition

2.16
the machine has the exact BSS order predicate
<
on
real registers. Hence branching may depend on exact atomic comparisons of real numbers.
A FRAM is built differently: exact tests
=
and
<
are available only
on natural-number registers, while on real-number registers the exact order test
is replaced by a finite-precision predicate
<_{k}
with uncertainty

1/(k+1)
, and exact integer-part extraction is replaced by a finite-precision
staircase operation
[\cdot]_{k}
. Concretely, Brattka-Hertling define

(x<_{k}y)\Rightarrow\begin{cases}\mathrm{TRUE}&\Longleftrightarrow x<y,\\
\mathrm{FALSE}&\Longleftrightarrow x>y-\frac{1}{k+1},\end{cases}

so the two cases are intentionally
not disjoint
; similarly,

[x]_{k}
is generally multi-valued near integer boundaries.
Thus FRAMs deliberately remove the exact atomic branching that is built into the
oracle-BSS reading.

Third difference: functional semantics versus relation semantics:

In the present BSS-reading a machine run is deterministic once the oracle is
fixed, and a base algorithm is a single-valued exact map

\Gamma\colon\Omega\to\mathcal{M}
.
A FRAM, however, has an extended semantics: because the finite-precision
test and the finite-precision staircase are multi-valued, one input may induce
several computation paths. Accordingly Brattka-Hertling define the semantics
of a RAM by a relation

R_{M}\subseteq X\times Y,

not by a single-valued function.

Fourth difference: exact output decoding versus approximate output
semantics:

Definition

2.13
uses a fixed
exact
finite output interface

\delta_{\mathcal{M}}^{\mathrm{fin}}\colon\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{\ast}\to\mathcal{M},

and
definition

2.16
requires that the machine halt with a finite tuple

u\in\operatorname{dom}(\delta_{\mathcal{M}}^{\mathrm{fin}})
satisfying

\delta_{\mathcal{M}}^{\mathrm{fin}}(u)=\Gamma(A)
.
Brattka-Hertling do
not
use this kind of exact decoding. Instead, a
relation
R\subseteq(X\times\mathbb{N})\times Y
is said to approximate a
function
f\colon X\to Y
if every output
y\in R(x,n)
satisfies

d_{Y}(f(x),y)<2^{-n}.

Thus the FRAM analogue of
\delta_{\mathcal{M}}^{\mathrm{fin}}
is not a function

\mathbb{R}^{\ast}\to\mathcal{M}
, but a precision-indexed approximation interface.
A faithful SCI-FRAM replacement would therefore require a relation of the form

\delta_{\mathcal{M}}^{\mathrm{app}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{\ast}\times\mathbb{N}\times\mathcal{M},

where
(u,k,x)\in\delta_{\mathcal{M}}^{\mathrm{app}}
is read as:

“the finite register output
u
is accepted as a
2^{-k}
-approximation of

x
”
.

For targets
\mathcal{M}\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{a}\times\mathbb{R}^{b}
with the max metric one may
set

\displaystyle(u,k,x)\in\delta_{\mathcal{M}}^{\mathrm{app}}\iff

\displaystyle\Bigl(\text{the }\mathbb{N}\text{-coordinates of }u\text{ and }x\text{ agree exactly}\Bigr)\ \land\ \Bigl(\text{the }\mathbb{R}\text{-coordinates satisfy }\|u-x\|_{\infty}<2^{-k}\Bigr).

For a general metric target
(\mathcal{M},d)
, however, such a relation is
additional
structure
; unlike
\delta_{\mathcal{M}}^{\mathrm{fin}}
, it is not supplied by the metric
alone. If one wants Weihrauch comparability, the honest replacement is even
stronger: one must pass from the exact finite decoder
\delta_{\mathcal{M}}^{\mathrm{fin}}

to a genuine represented-space structure

\delta_{\mathcal{M}}\colon\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}\to\mathcal{M}
, as in
section

6
and
appendix

A
.

In particular, the present BSS-reading and the FRAM model address

different
notions of low-level computation:

Definition

2.15
-
definition

2.18
formalize an
atomic exact
SCI-reading, whereas
Brattka-Hertling formalize an
approximative, finite-precision
RAM model.
The latter is therefore much closer in spirit to the Type-2/TTE side than to the
atomic BSS side.

Proposition D.1

(FRAM is strictly different from the atomic BSS-reading at height
0
)
.

Equip
\{-1,0,1\}
with the subspace representation inherited from
\mathbb{R}
.
Then, for

\operatorname{sgn}:\mathbb{R}\to\{-1,0,1\}\subseteq\mathbb{R},

one has

(1)

\operatorname{sgn}
is computable in the exact atomic BSS-reading;

(2)

\operatorname{sgn}
is not computable by FRAMs in the sense of
[
BH98
]
.

Consequently, replacing
definition

2.15
-
definition

2.18
by FRAMs changes the
height-
0
computational content of the theory.

Proof.

(1):
the machine performs the exact tests
x<0
,
x=0
, and
x>0
and
outputs
-1
,
0
, or
1
accordingly.

(2):

[
BH98
, Theorem 12]
states that their FRAM model matches
the computational power of Turing machines on real numbers.
Hence a FRAM computation of
\operatorname{sgn}
would induce a Type-2 /
Turing computation of
\operatorname{sgn}
with respect to the signed-digit
representation of the reals used in
[
BH98
]
.
But every Type-2 computable map from
\mathbb{R}
to a discrete space is
continuous, whereas
\operatorname{sgn}
is discontinuous at
0
.
This is impossible. Therefore
\operatorname{sgn}
is not FRAM
computable.
∎

Remark D.2

(Why
appendix

A
does
not
collapse under an FRAM replacement)
.

Although FRAMs are much closer to the Type-2/TTE side than the atomic
BSS-reading,
appendix

A
does not become tautological if one replaces BSS by FRAM.
The reason is that Brattka-Hertling prove in
[
BH98
, Theorem 12]
an equivalence between machine/time
models on finite products of
\mathbb{N}
and
\mathbb{R}
endowed with standard
real-number representations.
Appendix

A
, by contrast, proves a much more
abstract statement:
it identifies a
minimal package of premises
under which an SCI-interface
induces a genuine represented-space computability model and under which the
pure
\mathcal{R}
-
\operatorname{lim}
tower height agrees with an
\mathcal{R}
-Weihrauch rank.
Even in an FRAM-based reading one still has to provide

(1)

fixed represented-space interfaces for the SCI information space and the
output space;

(2)

a class
\mathcal{R}\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}\to\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}
of
admissible realizers;

(3)

adequacy of
\mathcal{R}
(pairing, projections, constants, closure under
composition);

(4)

the pure
\operatorname{lim}
normal-form premise

(\ast_{\mathcal{R},k})
used in
theorem

A.21
.

[
BH98
, Theorem 12]
does not provide these represented-space
premises for arbitrary SCI problems with countable evaluation families.
Thus
appendix

A
remains a genuine theorem package, not a mere change of
notation.

Appendix E
Comparison Of The BSS-Based SCI And Hansen’s Original Definition

There are three precise differences between
definition

2.10
and the original
SCI definition in
[
Han11
, Definition 3.1]
.

(1)

Hyperspace versus metric target:

Hansen’s target space is the hyperspace of closed subsets of
\mathbb{C}

(which Hansen also denotes by
\Omega
), and convergence is then defined separately
by local Hausdorff convergence on compact balls.
By contrast,
definition

2.10
is formulated for a fixed metric target
(\mathcal{M},d)
.
Thus, in order to compare Hansen’s formulation with
definition

2.10
, one must first
choose a concrete metric presentation of Hansen’s hyperspace limit notion.

(2)

Index order:

Hansen writes the tower indices as

\Gamma_{n_{1}},\Gamma_{n_{1},n_{2}},\ldots,\Gamma_{n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}},

whereas
definition

2.10
uses the opposite order

\Gamma_{n_{k}},\Gamma_{n_{k},n_{k-1}},\ldots,\Gamma_{n_{k},\ldots,n_{1}}.

This difference is purely notational.

(3)

Where the finite-information requirement is imposed:

This is the essential point: In
[
Han11
, Definition 3.1]
, only the
deepest
estimator

\Gamma_{n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}}
is required to be evaluable from finitely many matrix entries
by finitely many arithmetic operations and radicals.
The outer maps

\Gamma_{n_{1}},\Gamma_{n_{1},n_{2}},\ldots,\Gamma_{n_{1},\ldots,n_{k-1}}

are merely arbitrary maps on the full matrix table, constrained only by the successive
limit equalities.

By contrast, in
definition

2.10
every level of the tower is itself a general algorithm
in the sense of
definition

2.6
.
Hence the present paper imposes the finite-information condition at
all
levels,
not only at the bottom level.

Consequently,
[
Han11
, Definition 3.1]
agrees with
definition

2.10
at height
0
, and,
after the obvious identification of the unique level with the deepest estimator,
also at height
1
.
The first genuine difference appears at height
k\geq 2
. We will prove this now.

Proposition E.1

(The weakening in Hansen’s Definition is strict for heights
k\geq 2
)
.

For every
k\geq 2
,
[
Han11
, Definition 3.1]
is strictly weaker than
definition

2.10
.
Already for
k=2
there exists a height-
2
Hansen tower whose intermediate layer is
not a general algorithm in the sense of
definition

2.6
.

Proof.

It suffices to treat the case
k=2
.
Let

X:=\Bigl\{x=(x_{n,m})_{n,m\in\mathbb{N}}\in\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}^{2}}:\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}2^{-m}x_{n,m}\text{ exists}\Bigr\},

let
(\mathcal{M},d)=([0,1],|\cdot|)
, and let

\Lambda:=\{\pi_{n,m}:X\to\{0,1\}\mid\pi_{n,m}(x)=x_{n,m}\}.

Define

\Gamma_{n}(x):=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}2^{-m}x_{n,m},\qquad\Gamma_{n,k}(x):=\sum_{m=1}^{k}2^{-m}x_{n,m},\qquad\Xi(x):=\lim_{n\to\infty}\Gamma_{n}(x).

For every fixed pair
(n,k)
, the map
\Gamma_{n,k}
depends only on the finitely many
coordinates
\pi_{n,1},\ldots,\pi_{n,k}
and is obtained from them by finitely many
arithmetic operations.
Hence
(\Gamma_{n},\Gamma_{n,k})
is a height-
2
tower in the sense of
[
Han11
, Definition 3.1]
.

However,
\Gamma_{n}
is not a general algorithm in the sense of
definition

2.6
.
Indeed, suppose
(\Gamma_{n},\Lambda_{\Gamma_{n}})
were such a general algorithm.
Fix
x\equiv 0
.
Then
\Lambda_{\Gamma_{n}}(x)\subseteq\Lambda
is finite, so there exists
m_{0}\in\mathbb{N}

with
\pi_{n,m_{0}}\notin\Lambda_{\Gamma_{n}}(x)
.
Let
y\in X
be given by

y_{n,m_{0}}=1,\qquad y_{r,s}=0\quad\text{for all }(r,s)\neq(n,m_{0}).

Then

\pi(y)=\pi(x)\qquad\text{for all }\pi\in\Lambda_{\Gamma_{n}}(x),

but

\Gamma_{n}(y)-\Gamma_{n}(x)=2^{-m_{0}}\neq 0.

This contradicts finite-information dependence in
definition

2.6
.
Hence
\Gamma_{n}
is not a general algorithm, and the tower is not admissible under

definition

2.10
.

For larger
k
, one simply keeps all additional lower indices dummy.
∎

We give now a proof that
[
Han11
, Definition 3.1]
is additionally a logical problem for
definition

A.20
, namely: Hansen’s deepest-only clause does not force the existence of the uniform preprocessor
K
from
definition

A.20
.

Definition E.2

(Represented weak Hansen package)
.

Fix a countable-
\Lambda
SCI computational problem

\mathcal{P}=\bigl(\Xi,\Omega,(\mathcal{M},d),\Lambda\bigr)

with fixed represented information space
(I_{\Lambda},\delta_{I_{\Lambda}})
and a fixed
representation
\delta_{\mathcal{M}}
of
\mathcal{M}
, as in
definition

A.20
.
Let
\mathcal{R}\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}\to\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}
be a class of realizers.
A
represented weak Hansen
\mathcal{R}
-tower of height
k\in\mathbb{N}
means a family of
maps

\Gamma_{n_{k}},\ \Gamma_{n_{k},n_{k-1}},\ \dots,\ \Gamma_{n_{k},\dots,n_{1}}\colon\Omega\to\mathcal{M}\qquad(n_{1},\dots,n_{k}\in\mathbb{N})

such that

(1)

the same pointwise iterated-limit equalities as in
definition

2.10
hold, i.e.

\Xi(A)=\lim_{n_{k}\to\infty}\Gamma_{n_{k}}(A),\qquad\Gamma_{n_{k}}(A)=\lim_{n_{k-1}\to\infty}\Gamma_{n_{k},n_{k-1}}(A),

and so on down to depth
k
;

(2)

only the deepest maps
\Gamma_{n_{k},\dots,n_{1}}
are required to be

\mathcal{R}
-realizable general algorithms (equivalently: after fixing names, each deepest map has an
\mathcal{R}
-realizer and satisfies
definition

2.6
); no condition is imposed on the intermediate maps

\Gamma_{n_{k}},\ \Gamma_{n_{k},n_{k-1}},\ \dots,\ \Gamma_{n_{k},\dots,n_{2}}.

This is the literal represented-space repair of
[
Han11
, Definition 3.1]
obtained by keeping Hansen’s deepest-estimator clause and fixing only the extra data required by
appendix

A
(representations, names, and a class
\mathcal{R}
of realizers).

Proposition E.3

(The deepest-only Hansen clause does not imply a pure
\mathcal{R}
-
\operatorname{lim}
witness)
.

Fix
\mathcal{R}=\mathrm{Comp}
. There exists a countable-
\Lambda
SCI computational problem

\mathcal{P}=\bigl(\Xi,\Omega,(\mathcal{M},d),\Lambda\bigr)

with fixed represented information space and fixed represented output space such that

(1)

\mathcal{P}
admits a represented weak Hansen
\mathrm{Comp}
-tower of height
2
in the sense of
definition

E.2
;

(2)

every deepest estimator
\Gamma_{n,m}
is in fact a fixed-query computable general
algorithm;

(3)

every intermediate estimator
\Gamma_{n}
fails to be a general algorithm in the
sense of
definition

2.6
;

(4)

nevertheless there is no computable function

K\colon\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}\to\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}

with

\operatorname{lim}^{(2)}\circ K\preceq\widehat{\Xi}

in the sense of
definition

A.20
.

In plain words, even after fixing the represented-space data required in
appendix

A
,
Hansen’s deepest estimator clause is still too weak to yield Weihrauch comparability. It does not imply the existence of the uniform preprocessor
K
required in
definition

A.20
and
theorem

A.21
.

Proof.

We build and verify a counterexample explicitly in six steps.

Step 1: the represented input space:

Let

\Omega:=\Bigl\{x\in 2^{\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}_{0}}:(\forall j\in\mathbb{N}_{0})\ \lim_{n\to\infty}x(n,j)\text{ exists in }\{0,1\}\Bigr\}.

Equip
\Omega
with the subspace representation inherited from the Cantor space

2^{\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}_{0}}
.
Let

\Lambda:=\{\pi_{n,j}:\Omega\to\{0,1\}\subseteq\mathbb{C}\mid n\in\mathbb{N},\ j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\},\qquad\pi_{n,j}(x):=x(n,j).

Thus the induced represented information space is the standard name space for the
countable table of bits of
x
. Via this coordinate interface, the factor space
I_{\Lambda}
identifies canonically with
\Omega
itself; in the sequel we silently use this identification when writing
\widehat{\Xi}
.

Step 2: the represented output space:

Let
\mathcal{C}:=2^{\mathbb{N}_{0}}
be the Cantor space with its standard representation and let

\mathcal{M}:=\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{C}

with the usual product representation and any compatible product metric.
Write
0^{\omega}\in\mathcal{C}
for the all-zero sequence.

Now consider the set

S:=\Bigl\{u\in\mathcal{C}:\exists q\in\operatorname{dom}(\operatorname{lim}^{(2)})\cap\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}\text{ computable with }\delta_{\mathcal{M}}(\operatorname{lim}^{(2)}(q))=(u,v)\text{ for some }v\in\mathcal{C}\Bigr\}.

There are only countably many computable
q
, hence
S
is countable.
Choose once and for all some

p\in\mathcal{C}\setminus S.

For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
define the finite-prefix approximation

p_{n}(j):=\begin{cases}p(j),&j<n,\\
0,&j\geq n,\end{cases}\qquad(j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}),

and for
n,m\in\mathbb{N}
define

p_{n,m}:=p_{\min\{n,m\}}.

Then each
p_{n}
and each
p_{n,m}
is a computable point of Cantor space (its first
finitely many bits are hard-coded into the program, and all later bits are
0
), and

\lim_{m\to\infty}p_{n,m}=p_{n},\qquad\lim_{n\to\infty}p_{n}=p

in the Cantor space.

Step 3: the target map and the weak Hansen family:

Define

L\colon\Omega\to\mathcal{C},\qquad L(x)(j):=\lim_{n\to\infty}x(n,j)\qquad(j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}),

which is well-defined by the definition of
\Omega
. For every
n,m\in\mathbb{N}
define

R_{n}(x)(j):=x(n,j),\qquad R_{n,m}(x)(j):=\begin{cases}x(n,j),&j<m,\\
0,&j\geq m.\end{cases}

Now set

\Gamma_{n}(x):=(p_{n},R_{n}(x)),\qquad\Gamma_{n,m}(x):=(p_{n,m},R_{n,m}(x)),\qquad\Xi(x):=(p,L(x)).

We claim that these maps form a represented weak Hansen
\mathrm{Comp}
-tower of
height
2
.

First, for each fixed
n
and
x\in\Omega
we have

\lim_{m\to\infty}\Gamma_{n,m}(x)=\Gamma_{n}(x).

Indeed, the first coordinate satisfies
p_{n,m}\to p_{n}
and the second coordinate
satisfies
R_{n,m}(x)\to R_{n}(x)
, because the truncations of a binary sequence converge to
that sequence in the product metric on the Cantor space.
Second, for each
x\in\Omega
we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}\Gamma_{n}(x)=\Xi(x).

Again the first coordinate satisfies
p_{n}\to p
, while the second coordinate satisfies

R_{n}(x)\to L(x)
since for every fixed coordinate
j
the sequence
n\mapsto x(n,j)
is
eventually constant and therefore the convergence is coordinatewise on every finite
prefix, hence in the Cantor metric.
Thus the required nested limit equalities hold.

Step 4: the deepest estimators are fixed-query computable general algorithms:

Fix
n,m
. The map
\Gamma_{n,m}
depends only on the finitely many coordinates

\pi_{n,0},\pi_{n,1},\dots,\pi_{n,m-1}.

Indeed, the first component
p_{n,m}
is constant, and the second component

R_{n,m}(x)
only uses the first
m
entries of the
n
-th row of
x
.
Hence
\Gamma_{n,m}
is a fixed-query general algorithm in the sense of

definition

2.6
, witnessed by the constant query policy

\Lambda_{\Gamma_{n,m}}(x):=\{\pi_{n,0},\dots,\pi_{n,m-1}\}.

Moreover
\Gamma_{n,m}
is computable: from a name of
x
one can compute the queried
bits
x(n,0),\dots,x(n,m-1)
, write down the computable constant
p_{n,m}
, and output a
name of the pair
(p_{n,m},R_{n,m}(x))
.
Therefore every deepest estimator is a fixed-query computable general algorithm.

Step 5: the intermediate estimators are not general algorithms:

Fix
n\in\mathbb{N}
. We show that
\Gamma_{n}
is not a general algorithm.
Suppose, towards a contradiction, that some finite query policy

\Lambda_{\Gamma_{n}}(x)
witnesses
definition

2.6
for
\Gamma_{n}
on some input

x\in\Omega
.
Because
\Lambda_{\Gamma_{n}}(x)
is finite, there exists
j_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that the
coordinate query
\pi_{n,j_{0}}
does not belong to
\Lambda_{\Gamma_{n}}(x)
.
Define
y\in\Omega
by flipping exactly that one bit in the
n
-th row, i.e.

y(i,j):=\begin{cases}1-x(n,j_{0}),&(i,j)=(n,j_{0}),\\
x(i,j),&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Then
x
and
y
agree on all queries in
\Lambda_{\Gamma_{n}}(x)
, but

\Gamma_{n}(x)=(p_{n},R_{n}(x))\neq(p_{n},R_{n}(y))=\Gamma_{n}(y)

because the second coordinate differs at position
j_{0}
.
This contradicts the finite-information dependence clause in
definition

2.6
.
Hence
\Gamma_{n}
is not a general algorithm. Since
n
was arbitrary, no intermediate layer belongs to
definition

2.10
.

Step 6: no computable
K
as in
definition

A.20
exists:

Assume for contradiction that there exists a computable functional

K\colon\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}\to\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}

such that

\operatorname{lim}^{(2)}\circ K\preceq\widehat{\Xi}.

Let
x^{(0)}\in\Omega
be the computable zero matrix, and set

\widehat{x}^{(0)}:=\mathrm{Ev}_{\Lambda}(x^{(0)})\in I_{\Lambda}.

Let

r^{(0)}\in\mathrm{dom}(\delta_{I_{\Lambda}})

be a computable name of
\widehat{x}^{(0)}
, i.e.

\delta_{I_{\Lambda}}(r^{(0)})=\widehat{x}^{(0)}.

Since
K
is computable, the sequence

q:=K(r^{(0)})

is computable. By the equivalent formulation in
definition

A.20
, the assumption

\operatorname{lim}^{(2)}\circ K\preceq\widehat{\Xi}

implies that
q\in\mathrm{dom}(\operatorname{lim}^{(2)})
and

\delta_{\mathcal{M}}(\operatorname{lim}^{(2)}(q))=\widehat{\Xi}(\widehat{x}^{(0)}).

By the canonical identification
I_{\Lambda}\cong\Omega
from Step 1,

\widehat{\Xi}(\widehat{x}^{(0)})=\Xi(x^{(0)})=(p,0^{\omega}).

Hence

\delta_{\mathcal{M}}(\operatorname{lim}^{(2)}(q))=(p,0^{\omega}).

Therefore, by the definition of
S
in Step 2, we have
p\in S
, contradicting

p\in\mathcal{C}\setminus S.

This contradiction shows that no such computable
K
exists.
∎
