 Model short description

 o WaveDenoiser is a deep learning model designed to reduce noise from seismograms in the time domain.

 Model description

 o WaveDenoiser was trained using both earthquake signals and pure noise waveforms from the Stanford
 Earthquake Dataset (STEAD [1]). The model achieved a median signal-to-noise ratio (SNR)
 improvement of 8 dB. Because the model uses seismograms as direct input, the model is five times faster
 than an existing model that uses spectral representations of seismograms as input [2].

 Model type

 o UNet

 Inputs and outputs

 o Data—Time series (ground motion) data were used for this model.

 o Input—This model was trained with a three-channel time series. Each channel contained 5,700 data points
 with a sampling rate of 100 Hz or 57 s of seismogram.

 o Output—The output is a batch of denoised three-channel seismograms of the same length.

 2. INTENDED USES

The WaveDenoiser model is designed to reduce noise from three-component seismic data sampled at 100 Hz. The
model was trained using earthquake data at local distances (0–350 km) and may not perform well for earthquake
data at larger distances or for nonearthquake sources.

 Intended use

 o The model can be applied to three-component seismic data recorded by seismic stations at local distances
 and can reduce common noise.

 Primary intended users

 o The model will be used primarily by data analysts working with seismic data.

 2

 Out-of-scope use cases

 o Applying the model to data other than seismic time series, earthquake data at distances larger than 350 km
 from the source, and nonearthquake sources would be considered out of scope.

 4. BIAS, RISKS, AND LIMITATIONS

The model may have a less-than-optimal performance for earthquake data outside of a source–receiver distance
range of 10–110 km and an earthquake magnitude range of 0–4.5 because of biases in the training data.

 Known training data biases

 3

 o The source–receiver distance and earthquake magnitude of the training data are not uniformly distributed.
 Most earthquake data have a source–receiver distance between 10 and 110 km and an earthquake
 magnitude between 0 and 4.5. Transfer learning has been successfully applied to adapt a pretrained model
 for different scales [3]. The research team suggests fine-tuning the WaveDenoiser model for out-of-
 distribution earthquake data.

 Biased prediction examples

 o NA

 Model limitations

 o The model was trained for a sampling rate of 100 Hz and may not perform ideally for other sampling
 rates.

 Trust calibration maturity model score [4]

 o Performance: Level 2 (low confidence, tested on broad benchmark). The performance of the model has
 been evaluated.

 o Bias and robustness: Level 2 (some consideration). This model was pretrained solely on data from the
 STEAD dataset, which is not representative of all seismic source types expected in a real-world
 application scenario. Therefore, the statistical patterns that this model learned will not apply equally well
 to previously unseen source types and to data of rare sources.

 o Transparency: Level 3 (useful mental model). Model weights are accessible, and intermediate results
 can be visualized.

 o Safety and security: Level 1 (unknown). The system is designed to be deployed locally. User inputs
 cannot be accessed by others.

 o Usability: Level 2 (basic). The user interface exists. Example data and script are provided. The user
 document is included.

 5. TRAINING DETAILS

5.1 TRAINING DATA

This study used STEAD to train and evaluate this model because STEAD is among the best benchmark datasets
available for local to regional data. STEAD is a global dataset with over 1 million 60 s long seismic waveforms
that originated from approximately 450,000 earthquakes and background noise captured by more than 2,500
seismic stations. Each waveform in STEAD was attached with metadata such as earthquake locations, station
locations, and signal arrival times when available.

This study divided the waveforms into three sets: train, development, and test. The test set consisted exclusively
of seismograms that originated from earthquakes in the Oklahoma region or that were recorded by stations in the
same area. The Oklahoma region was selected for the test set because of the considerable number of earthquake
signals available. The remaining seismograms were distributed between the train and development sets.
Earthquake seismograms and noise waveforms were randomly apportioned so that 95% of waveforms belong to
the train set. The train, development, and test sets comprised approximately 950,000, 50,000, and 30,000
earthquake seismograms, respectively, as well as 220,000, 11,000, and 2,000 noise waveforms, respectively.

 4

5.2 TRAINING PROCEDURE

This study focused on training a deep learning model to reduce noise in seismic signals within the time domain.
Pure noise waveforms were combined with earthquake signals to generate noisy signals. The resulting noisy
signals, along with their corresponding clean signals (original seismograms), served as input-target pairs for
model training. The team followed data augmentation approaches detailed in Chai et al. (2025) [5]. To enhance
dataset diversity, 21 randomly selected and amplitude-scaled noise waveforms were added to each clean signal.
Additionally, a random time shift was applied to each noisy signal, further enriching data diversity. The models
processed three-component seismograms that were 57 s long, sampled at a rate of 100 Hz, and outputted a
denoised three-component seismogram. The development set was used to determine the appropriate model size
and training parameters. The optimal model was selected on the basis of achieving the highest median SNR
improvement on the development set. The training process employed a learning rate of 10−6, had a batch size of
256, and included 50 training epochs using the Adam optimizer.

 6. EVALUATION DETAILS

6.1 EVALUATION DATA

The test set, extracted from STEAD [1], is composed solely of seismograms originating from earthquakes in the
Oklahoma region to avoid data leakage and pure noise waveforms recorded by stations in the same area. The
model’s processing speed is significantly influenced by the hardware used and the efficiency of data loading
processes. The system used for this test took approximately 3 min to evaluate the entire test set, which consists of
around 164,000 seismograms. (See Section 7 for hardware specifications.)

6.2 EVALUATION PROCEDURE

The team used SNR improvements to quantify the model performance. SNR improvements were calculated as the
difference in SNR between the denoised signals produced by the WaveDenoiser model and the corresponding
noisy inputs. The SNRs were computed following the methodology detailed in Mousavi et al. [1]. WaveDenoiser
achieved a median SNR improvement of 7.7 dB and an average SNR improvement of 8.3 ± 5.8 dB.
