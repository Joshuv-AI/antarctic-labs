 ABSTRACT

Tristructural-isotropic (TRISO) fuel particles consist of uranium oxide or uranium oxide/uranium carbide
(UCO) kernels coated with three layers of pyrolytic carbon (PyC) and one layer of silicon carbide. The
Department of Energy Office of Nuclear Energy’s (DOE-NE) Advanced Gas Reactor Fuel Development
and Qualification (AGR) Program has led TRISO fuel fabrication and performance testing in the United
States and demonstrated exceptional performance for UCO TRISO under a prescribed operational
envelop. The AGR UCO TRISO fuel will be used for near-term deployment of TRISO-based reactors.
However, for some novel reactor applications, an expanded operational envelope is targeted and will
require the use of coated particle fuels with different kernel sizes and compositions, as well as different
coating layers, thicknesses, and compositions. For example, zirconium carbide (ZrC) is a candidate
material for advanced coated particle fuels. In TRISO-coated particle fuel concepts, ZrC could be used
not only as a SiC replacement for very-high-temperature applications or extended service conditions but
also as a getter to limit the release of fission products from the kernel, as shown in historical UO2* TRISO
fuel forms. ZrC has been traditionally deposited using halogen-based precursors such as ZrCl4 and
methane; however, the formation of HCl as a subproduct limits its use for direct coatings on uranium-
bearing kernels. Therefore, it is desirable to explore the use of halogen-free precursors for ZrC deposition,
which could allow for direct deposition on kernel material, thus avoiding the need for protective PyC
layers.

In this work, we introduce a new process that makes use of additive manufacturing and particle image
velocimetry to rapidly prototype, test, and select new components to fluidize a wide range of particles
with different sizes and densities. It was shown that by using the new process described herein, efficient
optimization of coating parameters could be obtained limiting development time and avoiding a “trial-
and-error” approach to developing new coating architectures.

ZrC kernel getter coatings were deposited using ZrCl4+CH4 at 1,500 °C with an intermediate PyC layer,
similar to the UO2* previously reported in the literature. While the use of zirconium acetylacetonate
(ZrAcAc) as a metal–organic precursor was explored to deposit ZrCx coatings at 800°C directly on a
kernel surrogate. Both precursors were used in a fluidized bed chemical vapor deposition system using
tungsten carbide kernels with a 500 µm diameter as the substrate. These metal–organic and halogen-based
precursor coatings were characterized by scanning electron microscopy and energy-dispersive x-ray
spectroscopy. This work demonstrated the possibility of producing good quality PyC/ZrC using the
traditional route and the viability of using ZrAcAc as a precursor for ZrCx deposition.

 1. INTRODUCTION

Tristructural isotropic coated particle fuel technology was re-established domestically through the
Department of Energy Office of Nuclear Energy’s (DOE-NE) Advanced Gas Reactor Fuel Development
and Qualification (AGR) Program now under the DOE-NE’s Advanced Fuels Campaign (AFC).
Traditional TRISO fuel particles consist of a uranium-bearing kernel (uranium oxide or a mixture of
uranium oxide/uranium carbide) between 350–500 µm in diameter, coated with a low-density pyrolytic
carbon (PyC) layer called a buffer, a high-density inner pyrolytic carbon (IPyC) layer, a silicon carbide
(SiC) layer, and a dense outer pyrolytic carbon (OPyC) layer [1]. The AGR program has successfully
demonstrated excellent TRISO fuel performance for uranium oxide/uranium carbide TRISO fuel at
conditions relevant to high temperature gas-cooled reactor (HTGR) operations where near-term
deployment of TRISO-based reactors will focus on AGR pedigree TRISO fuel. Although originally
designed for HTGRs, TRISO fuel has been selected for other future small modular and microreactor
designs for terrestrial use and space exploration [2, 3]. In some cases, the expanded envelope of operating
conditions and core configurations targeted by these new applications would benefit from coated particle
fuel designs with deviations from modern TRISO fuel. Potential particle designs may include a wide
variety of kernel compositions (oxides, carbides, or nitrides), kernel sizes (200–800 µm), and differences
in the number of coating layers, coating thicknesses, and coating compositions (e.g., PyC, SiC, ZrC, W,
ZrN). To fabricate these new configurations, not only will it be necessary to modify the deposition
conditions of these coatings, but in many cases the use of new chemical precursors and the fabrication of
new fluidized bed chemical vapor deposition (FBCVD) components will be required to ensure optimal
fluidization and coating of these new materials.

For the past 50 years, FBCVD has been used in the nuclear industry for the production of TRISO-coated
fuel particles [1, 4]. In this deposition system, hundreds of thousands to millions of submillimeter
diameter nuclear fuel kernels are fluidized and coated with ceramics by transitioning the particle bed from
a solid granular to a dynamic liquid-like state as gas passes through [5]. Normally, the fabrication of new
coated particle fuels is achieved through the traditional trial-and-error approach, which is slow and
capital-intensive as the CVD coater and the selection of the deposition conditions require continuous
iterative adjustments to account for any variation in the variables mentioned above. Therefore, as
particulate fuels are becoming attractive for other reactor designs, it is necessary to rethink the traditional
experimentation process on fuel fabrication to considerably reduce the time and effort required to
accommodate every possible variation in the characteristics of the FBCVD coater and the deposition
process for each type of coating and substrate.

Furthermore, as new fuels are expected to be used for prolonged periods of time or at higher operation
temperatures, the use of other ceramic coatings has been proposed. An example of a possible new
configuration is the use of ZrC as a replacement for SiC but also as a getter to control the diffusion and
release of fission products [6]. ZrC as well as SiC are generally produced via the use of halogen-based
precursors, such as zirconium tetrachloride (ZrCl4) and methyltrichlorosilane (CH3SiCl3) [7]. The
challenge with the use of this type of chemical is the release of HCl as a subproduct, its diffusion through
the coating layer, and its reaction with the uranium kernel, which results in uranium dispersion. The
formation of HCl during the fabrication of coatings using halogen-based precursors restricts the thickness
of the IPyC and limits the deposition of ceramic coatings directly on the kernels. Therefore, the use of
other chemical precursors could allow for the fabrication of other coated particle fuels with loosened fuel
specifications and deposition of getters directly on the kernel [6].

An initial focus of iterative improvement to TRISO fuel, under the DOE-NE AFC Next Generation Fuels
(NGF) TRISO effort, was on establishing ZrC coatings on fuel kernels to control fission product release

 1

during operation. Controlling fission product release from kernels is of specific interest to microreactor
concepts which target fuel lifetimes up to 20 years [2]. The AGR pedigree TRISO fuel has shown
exceptional performance at the targeted operational envelope for HTGR operations (e.g., burnup, particle
power, fast fluence, temperature, uranium loading) and will be leveraged for all near-term deployment of
TRISO-based reactors. However, longer fuel lifetimes targeted by microreactors may be limited by
fission product attack, identified as a primary failure under the AGR program, and supported by designs
which incorporate fission product getters.

The inclusion of an additional ZrC layer to kernels, known as UO2* with a protective PyC layer followed
by a ZrC layer deposited on the kernel, has an established precedent for showing improved fission
product retention compared to UO2 and UCO kernel TRISO [6]. While, the UO2* design may provide
performance benefits regarding fission product control, the historic design adds two additional layers.
Additional layers add complexity and cost to quality assurance and quality control rigor, limiting the
potential feasibility of a UO2*-like TRISO design. To attempt to realize the benefits of UO2* while
limiting the addition of new coating layers, new ZrC layer deposition approaches that can be directly
applied to a uranium bearing kernel were pursued as halogen-based approaches require a protective PyC
layer due to the production of HCl which degrades the kernels during deposition.

In this effort, previously established methodology for coating kernels with novel architectures outside of
those used for the AGR program were implemented to replicate UO2* architectures while new coating
approaches to afford direct deposition of ZrC layers on uranium-bearing kernels using a metalorganic
precursor were explored avoiding the requirement of a protective PyC layer. The new methodology
development was supported by the U.S. Department of Defense Operational Energy Capability
Improvement Fund. The prior established novel coating development methodology includes a new
process to develop coater components for FBCVD and particle image velocimetry (PIV) to rapidly
prototype and test new components by quantifying particle movement in the bed. Previous results are
replicated to show that improvements in particle movement—such as particle velocity, frequency, and
dead time—identified in a cold fluidization rig lead to higher-quality coatings in high-deposition
processes. Specific to the NGF TRISO effort, we demonstrate the capability, using the novel coating
methodology, to produce WC/PyC/ZrC coatings such as those produced in historical UO2* fuel [6] and
explore the use of metal–organic (MO) precursors, in particular zirconium acetylacetonate (which
subproducts are CH4 and CxHyOz compounds), for the deposition of ZrC.

 2. METHODOLOGY

2.1 EQUIPMENT

2.1.1 Cold Fluidization

A cold fluidization rig was constructed to visualize particle movement on the wall. A glass cone with a
5 cm inner diameter and a 60° angle was used for particle movement visualization. In the process
developed, the gas distributors were fabricated using a 3D printer (Raise3D Pro3). In total, 9 nozzles were
3D-printed, and the number of holes and hole orientation varied. A modular system of interchangeable
nozzles, cones and tubes was preferred to facilitate the design, fabrication and testing of a larger number
of components. A high-speed camera (Fastec IL5, RDI Technologies) at 430 fps with a shutter speed of
125 µs was used to record particle movement.

2.2 MATERIALS

Argon (ultra-high purity, Airgas), methane (ultra-high purity, Airgas), acetylene (ultra-high purity,
Airgas), propylene (ultra-high purity, Airgas), hydrogen (ultra-high purity, Airgas), chlorine (5% Cl2
balanced Ar, Airgas), zirconium granules (American Elements, 99.9%, with a nominal size of 1–3 mm),
and zirconium(IV) acetylacetonate (Zr(C5H7O2)4, 97% Sigma-Aldrich) were used without further
treatment. WC kernels (WC-YSZ, Cospheric) with diameters of 500 µm were used as substrates.

2.3 METHODS

2.3.1 Cold Fluidization

WC particles of 60 g were fluidized using 7 slm for each nozzle tested (Figure 1). PIV was used to
quantify particle movement using high-intensity white LED panels in a forward scattering configuration
as the illumination source. Images captured the dynamics of the particles at the glass cone front wall. The
camera was located perpendicular to the imaging plane, and in situ calibration was performed. The
calibration procedure included the placement of a planar target printed with a grid pattern. The calibration
target was placed at the glass cone wall and the central planes to account for local optical conditions.
Images were captured using full-resolution color frames 2560 × 2048 (12 bits) with an equivalent real-
world imaging viewing area of 76.8 × 61.4 mm. An area of approximately 10 × 10 mm at the middle of
the bed was selected for quantification of the particle movement. Image pre-processing was performed on
the raw images to enhance the accuracy of the particle detection algorithm. This process included the
conversion of the color RGB frames to grayscale images and the subtraction of the digital image average
from a set of 430 frames to single frames in the set. Image binarization was achieved using a local
moments threshold algorithm, and particle tracking was estimated using the 2 × 3-point sub-pixel
estimator with a two-pass overlapped window of 64 × 32 using the TSI INSIGHT 2D-PIV software. No
vector interpolation was used for single-pair velocity calculations, but ensemble-averaged vector fields
were produced to calculate averaged velocity estimations.

2.3.2 FBCVD of PyC and ZrC

WC particles of 60 g were used as substrates. Argon, acetylene, and a mixture of acetylene/propylene
with 70 and 30 vol.%, respectively, were used as gas precursors for the deposition of the low-density and
high-density PyC. The general deposition conditions for these layers are shown in Table 1.

2.3.3 Characterization

Coatings were assessed by surface imaging and analysis of cross-sections when feasible. For cross-section

polishing pads starting from 9 µm down to 1 µm diamond suspension. Coatings were imaged using

 3. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

3.1 IMPROVEMENTS TO FLUIDIZATION

This section highlights the previously established methodology to systematically optimize fluidization
supported by the U.S. Department of Defense Operational Energy Capability Improvement Fund. Lessons
learned from this approach were applied to NGF TRISO activities to support target ZrC layer depositions
avoiding the use of trial-and-error to optimize fluidization setup.

Figure 5 shows an example of the images and information that can be extracted from PIV. By visualizing
particle movement at the wall for each gas distributor configuration, it is possible to obtain relevant data
such as maximum velocity, frequency, and waiting period (time at which particles are static) at each flow
studied. Although more data can be extracted from these measurements, the present work focuses only on
the maximum particle velocities and frequencies of the bed, since both are parameters that have typically
been used to quantify particle fluidization in the past and are critical to bed dynamics. The analysis was
focused on the movement of the particles at the wall because it is in this region that particles are expected
to move the slowest. It is expected that improvements in particle movement in this region should be
reflected in higher particle movement in the entire bed.

Figure 5. Particle image velocimetry analysis of a spouted-bed fluidized bed and examples of the information
 that can be extracted. Image on the right shows a snapshot of the vectors (intensity and direction) of the particle
 movement in the bed as generated by the PIV software.

Figure 6 shows the particle velocity profiles of two gas distributors. The standard gas distributor was
observed to have the slowest movement and the highest frequency, but it also showed regions where
particles had relatively low movement. The multi-hole gas distributor had a higher velocity and lower
frequency but no waiting periods. These changes show the considerable increments in particle velocities
that can be achieved by improving the gas distributor configuration.

Figure 7 shows an SEM image of WC particles coated with PyC using a single-hole gas distributor that
was previously used to successfully coat UCO kernels under the AGR Program. It was observed that the
batch contained uncoated particles (arrows indicate abnormal particles in Figure 7). Around 30% of the
particles in the batch were uncoated. Conversely, when the gas distributor was used, all the particles were
coated (Figure 8). This gas distributor was further used to coat UN kernels, generating a buffer layer
coating with a coating thickness of 18.00 ± 3.52 µm. This demonstrates that the improvements observed
on WC successfully translated to uniform coatings on the UN kernels. Figure 8b shows the UN-coated
particles with uniform buffer and IPyC layers, demonstrating the improvements achieved on coating
uniformity by selecting the adequate gas distributor configuration.

Moreover, because the single-hole nozzle (nozzle 1) had been used in the past to successfully coat
350 µm UCO kernels, our results also highlight that a single FBCVD system is not the most effective
means of coating any type of kernel, as variations in size, density, shape, and even surface roughness
could have an important impact on its fluidization and the system’s capacity to coat them. Nevertheless,
this work demonstrates that despite these challenges, it is possible to use cold fluidization rigs, additive
manufacturing, and PIV for rapid prototyping, testing, and selection of new FBCVD components that
could be used to effectively coat new kernels.

3.2 FBCVD OF ZRC

The experience gained from the cold fluidization tests as well as the development of the multi-hole gas
distributor was used to efficiently fluidize and coat WC particles with larger particle sizes to support
development of ZrC coated kernels for fission product control. Figure 9 shows an example of the UO2*
surrogate particles produced using ZrCl4 as a gas precursor and WC kernels as substrates. Appropriate
fluidization and uniform coatings were achieved without the need to perform multiple coating runs.
Figure 9 depicts the control that can be achieved in the deposition of ZrC coatings through the use of the
parallel chlorinator system (Figure 3), where Zr chips are used to produce in situ ZrCl4. The use of this
parallel gas delivery system allows better control of reactive gases because Ar/H2/CH4 and ZrCl4/Ar have
different paths, thereby reducing the premature decomposition and reaction of the precursor gases.
Another advantage of the system developed is its modularity. The system also allows the use of a wide
variety of cones and nozzle configurations; the chlorinator can be added as a standalone component or
permanently attached to the base of the nozzle.

 8

Figure 9 shows that it was possible to replicate the coating configuration previously reported, with a thin
PyC and a dense and uniform ZrC layer [6]. As previously stated, despite the quality of the ZrC coatings
that can be achieved using ZrCl4, the use of this precursor limits its deposition directly to the kernel. It is
for this reason that a PyC diffusion barrier is included. Nevertheless, it would be desirable to develop a
process that could allow the deposition of ZrC directly on the kernel, reducing the complexity of the fuel
system and allowing the fabrication of coated particle fuels with wider coating thickness configurations.

 Figure 9. WC coated with PyC and ZrC, using ZrCl4+CH4.

Figure 10 shows the SEM micrographs of WC/PyC and WC/PyC/ZrCx coated particles using zirconium
acetylacetonate [Zr(C5H7O2)4]. For this application it was decided to coat MO-ZrCx on PyC due to the
difficulty of characterizing this layer on top of a WC kernel, which also contained yttria-stabilized
zirconia. Although the use of a PyC layer is not necessary for this precursor because it does not produce
HCl or any other acid, the use of a PyC facilitated the identification of the ZrCx layer.

Multiple unsuccessful tests were performed to deposit ZrC using ZrAcAc. In these initial experiments, the
evaporation temperature was controlled using the temperature reading from the heating mantle. The
temperature for evaporation was set at 160°C. However, regardless of the coating run, carrier gas flow, or
deposition temperature, no coating was deposited. These results suggested that most likely no MO
precursor was being delivered into the CVD chamber. For this reason, a thermocouple was introduced
inside the bubbler to measure the actual evaporation temperature. Table 2 shows the differences observed
between the temperature of the heating mantle and the temperature inside the bubbler. The table shows a
large difference between the set temperature of the heating mantle and the actual temperature inside the
bubbler. It also shows that no coatings were obtained in the initial tests since the set temperature of
160°C reflected a temperature of 96°C inside the bubbler, a value too low to generate any ZrAcAc in the
gas phase. Using the information in Table 2, it was decided that a set temperature of at least 225°C at the
heating mantle was needed to achieve a temperature of at least 150°C, which is required to evaporate
ZrAcAc.

Figure 10 shows the ZrCx deposited using an evaporation temperature of 150°C (measured inside the
bubbler) after two hours of deposition (MO-ZrC1). The EDS spectrum shows the presence of Zr and C,
thus suggesting the formation of ZrCx; however, it was also possible to detect W. The presence of W

Since WC also contained Zr, it was necessary to change the deposition conditions to deposit a thicker

necessary to ensure adequate evaporation of the ZrAcAc precursor.

 Figure 10. SEM micrograph and EDS spectra of MO-ZrC1.

Figure 11 shows the surface of PyC coated WC (used as substrate) and ZrC coated PyC/WC particles.
The PyC coated particle was used to more easily identify and characterize the presence of ZrCx. The
micrographs show clear differences in the surface roughness of the PyC and ZrCx coated particles,
suggesting the deposition of a ceramic coating, for sample MO-ZrC2. For this sample the evaporation
temperature was increased to 170°C for a total of 3 hours to promote the formation of a thicker coating.
Figure 12 shows the EDS spectra, confirming the presence of Zr on the surface of this coating. This result
confirmed that ZrCx was successfully produced. Nevertheless, it is expected that the coating produced
contained a high concentration of carbon, which is a feature generally observed for MO precursors in
CVD. Furthermore, even though the ZrC in Figure 11 and Figure 12 was deposited for 5 hours, the
coating was speculated to be only a few micrometers thick, showing that the use of a MO precursor was
less efficient compared to ZrCl4. This is partially due to the significant difference in deposition
temperature used (1,500°C for ZrCl4 and 1,000°C for ZrAcAc) and that the process for the use of MO
was not optimized. Nevertheless, Figures 11 and 12 demonstrate that the use of MO precursor for the
deposition of ZrC is promising.

 4. SUMMARY AND FUTURE WORK

This work investigated the challenges associated with the fabrication of coated particle fuels with
configurations that vary from modern TRISO designs. The use of different kernels and kernel sizes will
influence the fluidization behavior, which will require in many instances the optimization of the coating
process. To accelerate this process, a new method, that combines additive manufacturing and PIV to
rapidly prototype, test, and select new gas distributors, was applied to develop ZrC coatings on kernel
surrogates. It was shown that PIV can be used to quantify particle movement at the wall of the bed and
that these measurements can be used to guide the design and selection of new gas distributors. The
fabrication of UO2* coated particle fuel, where a ZrC layer is used as a getter, was re-reestablished. Two
routes to fabricate ZrC were explored: the use of a halogen- and an MO-based precursor. The use of
ZrCl4, although efficient, cannot be used directly on uranium-bearing kernels and requires the use of a
PyC protective layer, introducing a more complex structure to the fuel. The use of an MO precursor can
be used directly on the kernel. The work presented herein showed that ZrAcAc could be used as ZrCx
precursor because it was used to successfully deposit a ZrCx coating though additional development is
required to produce optimal coatings. The results also showed the complexity of its use: it is less efficient
and produces coatings with a high carbon concentration. Nevertheless, this work demonstrates that the use
of MO precursors could help to develop coated particle fuels with more flexible architectures.

The following activities could be pursued based on the results of this project.

 - Optimize ZrC deposition by ZrAcAc. Study the impact of deposition temperature, evaporation
 temperature, and hydrogen concentration on the microstructure and composition of ZrC.
 - Explore the use of other MO precursors to improve efficiency and yield.
 - Combine both fabrication routes: an initial seal layer of ZrC using MO precursors and grow the
 rest of the ZrC coating using a traditional ZrCl4 route.

Following successful fabrication, consideration for irradiation performance testing would be warranted.
