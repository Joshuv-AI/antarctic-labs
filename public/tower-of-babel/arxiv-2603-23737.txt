Risk-Aware Linear-Quadratic Regulation with Temporally Coupled States footnoteinfo
Chuanning Wei, Kin Fung Li, Dionysis Kalogerias, Margaret P. Chapman ∗ , {}^{\ast\text{,}}

Abstract
We formulate and solve a discrete-time linear-quadratic regulation (LQR) problem in a finite horizon that penalizes temporal variability and stochastic variability of the state trajectory. Our approach enables the user to strike a balance between regulating the state and reducing temporal variability, with explicit sensitivity to risk. We achieve this by extending a risk measure called predictive variance to a setting with temporally coupled states. Numerical examples demonstrate the effect of temporal coupling in both risk-aware and risk-neutral control settings. Particularly, we observe that explicitly penalizing temporal variability alone can also reduce stochastic variability.

Stochastic control; Risk-aware optimal control; Temporally coupled states; Linear systems.

Introduction

In state regulation of a control system, the behavior of the state trajectory can differ as a result of design choices.
In particular, one may prefer trajectories that vary slowly over time, if abrupt changes in the state are undesirable.
The design of slowly varying, continuous-time trajectories (small
|\dot{x}|
) has been studied in
[
20
,
19
,
13
]
, among many others,
whereas this brief paper concerns the design of slowly varying, discrete-time trajectories (small
|x_{t}-x_{t-1}|
). The goal to design slowly varying trajectories arises naturally in areas such as robotics and mechatronics
[
15
,
2
,
16
]
,
building climate control
[
30
,
42
]
, and even medicine
[
31

. For example, the path of a robot can be designed to be short by penalizing the distance between subsequent states
[

Additionally, in building climate control, occupants were found to prefer raising the temperatures of their air conditioners gradually rather than abruptly, which incentivizes energy savings
[

[
31

Motivated by such examples and viewing real-world uncertainties from a probabilistic perspective, we study a stochastic controller synthesis problem that rewards slowly varying trajectories. The setting of interest concerns standard linear systems, but we evaluate their performance in a manner that deviates from a classical stochastic viewpoint.

Classical stochastic optimal control summarizes uncertain performance on average and thus is not designed to manage rare events
[

,
29
,
46
,
41
,

minimizes the expectation of the LQR cost
\mathcal{J}\coloneqq x_{N}^{\top}Qx_{N}+\sum_{t=0}^{N-1}x_{t}^{\top}Qx_{t}+u_{t}^{\top}Ru_{t}
and so lacks explicit sensitivity
to the stochastic variability of
\mathcal{J}

risk measures
quantify a probability distribution in various ways, e.g., the mean of the upper tail, the spread relative to some value,

Reducing stochastic variability in system performance can be dated back to the minimal cost variance (MCV) problem studied by Sain in 1966, which minimizes the variance of an LQR cost subject to an expected cost constraint
[
38
]
.
Another classical example is the linear-exponential-quadratic-Gaussian (LEQG) problem
[
48
]
. This problem minimizes the objective

-2\theta^{-1}\log\mathbb{E}(\exp(-\theta\mathcal{J}/2))
with a risk-aversion parameter
\theta<0
,
which approximates a mean-variance objective locally
[
48
]
. LEQG is closely related to other problems in control theory. There is a correspondence between LEQG and a mixed
\mathcal{H}_{2}
/
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}
control problem
[
18
,
49
]
.
Also, both the MCV problem and the LEQG problem (under a series expansion of the LEQG objective) can be viewed as special cases of controlling the statistics of the distribution of an LQR cost
[
37
,
26
]
.
However, the Gaussian noise assumptions in
[
48
,
26
]

imply that these controllers are not designed to handle more general noise distributions that may be skewed or heavy-tailed.

A modern approach for managing stochastic variability in linear system performance is to incorporate the
predictive variance
measure into an optimal control problem
[
44
]
. This measure assesses the variability of the state energy
x_{t}^{\top}Qx_{t}
relative to the predicted average state energy at time
t

[
44
]
. It takes the form of
\mathbb{E}((x_{t}^{\top}Qx_{t}-\mathbb{E}(x_{t}^{\top}Qx_{t}|h_{t-1}))^{2})
, where
h_{t-1}
contains historical information
before time
t

[
44
]
. Predictive variance is an attractive risk measure for fully observable linear systems with additive noise for several reasons. First, the noise need not be Gaussian
[
44
]
.
Second, an optimal controller that is affine state-feedback and depends on the skewness of the noise can be computed in closed-form via a Riccati recursion
[
44
]
.
Third, predictive variance assesses risk in an interpretable way in terms of stochastic variability in a dynamic setting
[
44
]
. This type of risk assessment is a natural choice when variability above and below a benchmark (the predicted average state energy in this case) should be penalized evenly
[
41
,
46
]
. Also, note that using variance as a measure of risk has a rich history in financial portfolio management; e.g., see
[
14
]
.
The predictive variance approach has been extended to the infinite-horizon case
[
50
]
, partially observable systems
[
43
]
, interconnected systems
[
33
,
47
]
, and model predictive control
[
12
]
, showing its applicability to various LQ control problems. In the current work, we connect predictive variance to the aim of rewarding state trajectories that vary gradually over time. To achieve this, first we extend
both the state energy and the predictive variance to be defined for a portion of the state trajectory, where the involved states are said to be
temporally coupled
.
Then, we propose and solve an optimal control problem where the risk measure is the predictive variance for temporally coupled states.

Temporal coupling reflects the behavior of a stochastic control system
over a time period
(of stages) rather than at a single stage.
We refer to behavior over such a period as trajectory-wise behavior.
Practical examples include
the path length traversed by a robot
[
16
]
,
the maximum rate of power drawn from an electric grid
[
25
]
,
and a system’s maximum distance outside a safe region
[
10
]
. In contrast, stage-wise behavior, like the robot’s current location or the instantaneous rate of power, only indicate behavior briefly in time.
Trajectory-wise behavior can be formally specified by a
non-Markovian
objective functional, where the cost incurred at some stage is a functional of states at different times
[
4
]
.

Now, consider the problem of optimizing a non-Markovian objective for a control system at hand.
While dynamic programming (DP) is a standard approach for deriving optimal controllers, the system model in its given form may not be conducive to a direct application of DP. However, it may be valid to apply DP by first suitably augmenting the state with additional information
[
25
]
.
Deterministic and stochastic DP with state augmentation has been studied in
[
25
]
, where the authors developed examples involving the optimization of a maximum cost.
State augmentation is also useful for solving some risk-aware optimal control problems; examples include problems whose objectives are defined in terms of the conditional value-at-risk (CVaR) measure
[
6
,
10
]

and expected utility measures
[
7
]
.
2
2

2

The CVaR represents an expected value in a given fraction of the worst cases
[
40
]
. An expected utility generalizes the exponential utility
[
7
]
.

Applying DP to an augmented system increases numerical complexity
[
25
]
,
and the user must decide whether such complexity is acceptable for a given application.
While this brief paper does not specifically address
the numerical complexity challenge,
we use an augmented state with a flexible dimension
n_{t}\leq n(\mathbf{k}+1)
, where
n
is the original state dimension but

\mathbf{k}
can be adjusted. Our solution is based on a Riccati recursion, and the dimension of the inverted matrix does not depend on
\mathbf{k}
.

Contribution.

We formulate and solve a nonstandard stochastic LQR problem in a risk-aware manner, i.e., with explicit sensitivity to rarer outcomes in addition to what is expected.
The problem deviates from a standard regulation problem in that it allows the user to encode a preference for slowly varying state trajectories along with state regulation, for example, by penalizing both
(x_{t}-x_{t-1})^{\top}Q(x_{t}-x_{t-1})
and
x_{t}^{\top}Qx_{t}
.
We generalize this penalty using a convex quadratic cost that contains historical states over a period, a cost called the
sequential state energy
, and those states are said to be
temporally coupled
.
We quantify risk by extending predictive variance
[
44
,
43
]
,
a measure of stochastic variability, to be defined for the sequential state energy. The extended predictive variance is a
trajectory-wise
risk concept instead of a stage-wise one. That is, in general, the risk over a period consisting of distinct stages is not immediately separable into the risks over these stages, and such a separation may even be impossible.
Thus, our problem differs from risk-aware control problems that concern stage-wise risk concepts, such as those in
[
36
,
17
,
44
,
39
,
45
,
5
,
24
,
43
,
32
]
.
Since the (extended) predictive variance is defined via conditional expectation,
we derive an analytical expression for it. This expression also turns out to be convex quadratic in the temporally coupled states (Lemmas
1
–
2
).
This nontrivial discovery allows us to reformulate our nonstandard problem (Problem
1
) into a form that enjoys a well-known solution.
An optimal controller can be found analytically via a Riccati recursion
and is affine in a recent state history (Theorem
1
, Corollary
1
).
Numerical examples illustrate tradeoffs that can arise from different parameter choices, and demonstrate the effect of temporal coupling in both risk-aware and risk-neutral control settings.

2.1
Sequential State Energy and its Predictive Variance

As motivated in Section

(3)

Since

Risk-Aware Optimal Control Problem

Using the predictive variance

Under Assumption (A2), consider

\displaystyle\;\inf_{\mathbf{u}}

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{\lambda}(\mathbf{u})

\displaystyle\mathrm{dynamics}~\eqref{linsys},\;x_{0}=\mathbf{x}_{0}\mathrm{\;a.s.},

\textstyle\mathcal{J}_{\lambda}(\mathbf{u})\coloneqq\mathbb{E}\left(z_{N}+\sum_{t=0}^{N-1}z_{t}+u_{t}^{\top}Ru_{t}\right)+\lambda\mathbb{E}\left(\sum_{t=1}^{N}\Delta_{t}^{2}\right).

(5)

Under Assumption (A2),

\displaystyle\;\mathrm{subject\;to}

4
Risk-Aware Optimal Controller Synthesis

Here, we show that an optimal controller can be computed via a Riccati recursion and only requires the truncated state history
\eta_{t}
instead of the full history
h_{t}
. This is achieved by reformulating the system dynamics and the control constraints, using
\eta_{t}
as an augmented state.

Despite the dynamics (
1
) being Markovian (in the original state
x_{t}
),
dynamic programming cannot be applied directly to Problem
2
in its current form because
c_{t}
is a function of
\eta_{t}
rather than
x_{t}
alone.
To circumvent this issue, we express the dynamics (
1
) in terms of
\eta_{t}
, regarding
\eta_{t}
as a
\mathbb{R}^{n_{t}}
-valued random augmented state vector. For every
t\in\{0,\dots,N-1\}
, we define

\displaystyle A_{t}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\begin{cases}\begin{bmatrix}A&0_{n\times n\mathbf{k}_{t}}\end{bmatrix}&\text{if }\mathbf{k}_{t}\geq 1,\\
A&\text{if }\mathbf{k}_{t}=0,\end{cases}\quad\text{for }\mathbf{k}\in\{0,\dots,N\},

\displaystyle D_{t}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\begin{cases}\begin{bmatrix}I_{n\mathbf{k}}&0_{n\mathbf{k}\times n}\end{bmatrix}&\text{if }t\geq\mathbf{k},\\
I_{n(t+1)}&\text{if }t<\mathbf{k},\end{cases}\quad\text{for }\mathbf{k}\in\{1,\dots,N\}.

Observe that
A_{t}
satisfies the relation
A_{t}\eta_{t}=Ax_{t}
, and if
t\geq\mathbf{k}
, then
D_{t}\eta_{t}
equals
\eta_{t}
without the earliest state
x_{t-\mathbf{k}}
. Then, the augmented dynamics are

\eta_{t+1}=f_{t}(\eta_{t},u_{t},w_{t}),\quad t\in\{0,\dots,N-1\},

(11)

where we define
f_{t}:\mathbb{R}^{n_{t}}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n_{t+1}}
by

f_{t}(\eta,u,w)\coloneqq\tilde{A}_{t}\eta+\tilde{B}_{t}u+\tilde{C}_{t}w

and the matrices by

\tilde{A}_{t}\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}A_{t}\\
D_{t}\end{bmatrix},\;\tilde{B}_{t}\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}B\\
0_{n\mathbf{k}_{t+1}\times m}\end{bmatrix},\;\tilde{C}_{t}\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}I_{n}\\
0_{n\mathbf{k}_{t+1}\times n}\end{bmatrix}

if
\mathbf{k}\in\{1,\dots,N\}
,
whereas
\tilde{A}_{t}\coloneqq A
,
\tilde{B}_{t}\coloneqq B
, and
\tilde{C}_{t}\coloneqq I_{n}
if
\mathbf{k}=0
.
The augmented dynamics (
11
) are Markovian (in
\eta_{t}
) and linear. However, unlike a standard LQR set-up, the state space
\mathbb{R}^{n_{t}}
of (
11
) is time-varying.
For convenience, we define
\mathcal{G}_{t}\coloneqq\sigma(g_{t})
with
g_{t}\coloneqq[\eta_{0}^{\top},u_{0}^{\top},\dots,\eta_{t-1}^{\top},u_{t-1}^{\top},\eta_{t}^{\top}]^{\top}
if
t\in\{1,\dots,N\}
and
g_{0}\coloneqq\eta_{0}
, analogous to the definitions of
\mathcal{F}_{t}
and
h_{t}
, respectively.
As a final step in the reformulation,
we pose the following problem
and show its connection to
Problems
1
–
2
(see Theorem
1
):

Problem 3

Under Assumption (A2), consider the optimal control problem (each
c_{t}
is defined in (
10
)):

\displaystyle\inf_{\mathbf{u}}

\displaystyle\textstyle\mathbb{E}\left(c_{N}(\eta_{N})+\sum_{t=0}^{N-1}c_{t}(\eta_{t},u_{t})\right)

\displaystyle\;\mathrm{subject\;to}

\displaystyle\mathrm{dynamics}~\eqref{augsys},\;\eta_{0}=\mathbf{x}_{0}\mathrm{\;a.s.},

\displaystyle u_{t}\in\mathcal{L}^{2}(\mathcal{G}_{t}),\;t\in\{0,\ldots,N-1\}.

Theorem 1

If
\mathbf{u}^{*}
is optimal for Problem
1
,
2
, or
3
, then
\mathbf{u}^{*}
is optimal for the remaining two problems.

Proof.

We already explained the above statement regarding Problems
1
and
2
in Section
3
. Now, since the objectives of Problems
2
and
3
are identical, it suffices to confirm that their constraints are equivalent.
We showed that the original dynamics (
1
) can be lifted to the augmented dynamics (
11
) previously in Section
4
. Conversely, (
11
) can be reduced to (
1
) by considering the first
n
entries of
\eta_{t+1}
. Problems
2
and
3
have the same initial condition
\eta_{0}=x_{0}=\mathbf{x}_{0}
a.s. To see that their control constraints are equivalent, first observe that
g_{t}=h_{t}
if
\mathbf{k}=0
. Otherwise, if
\mathbf{k}\geq 1
, then
g_{t}
and
h_{t}
are still measurable maps of each other,
leading to
u_{t}\in\mathcal{L}^{2}(\mathcal{F}_{t})
if and only if
u_{t}\in\mathcal{L}^{2}(\mathcal{G}_{t})
.
∎

Overall, we have reformulated the original nonstandard problem (Problem
1
) into a problem (Problem
3
) that enjoys a standard form, and more specifically, a standard dynamic-programming-based solution.

Corollary 1

Let all conditions and definitions pertaining to Problem
3
hold. Define the functions
J_{t}
on
\mathbb{R}^{n_{t}}
by
J_{N}\coloneqq c_{N}
, and for each
t=N-1,\dots,0
,

J_{t}(\eta)\coloneqq\inf_{u\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}\Big(c_{t}(\eta,u)+\mathbb{E}(J_{t+1}(f_{t}(\eta,u,w_{t})))\Big).

(12)

Then,
J_{t}
is convex quadratic for every
t\in\{0,\ldots,N\}
and an affine function
\mu_{t}^{*}:\mathbb{R}^{n_{t}}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
attains the infimum in (
12
) for every
t\in\{0,\ldots,N-1\}
. In addition,
\mathbf{u}^{*}\coloneqq(u_{0}^{*},\ldots,u_{N-1}^{*})
, where
u_{t}^{*}\coloneqq\mu_{t}^{*}\circ\eta_{t}
, is an optimal controller for Problem
3
, and the optimal value of Problem
3
is
\mathcal{J}_{\lambda}^{*}(\mathbf{x}_{0})=J_{0}(\mathbf{x}_{0})
. Particularly, for every
t\in\{0,\ldots,N-1\}
and
\eta\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{t}}
, we have

J_{t}(\eta)=\eta^{\top}P_{t}\eta+q_{t}^{\top}\eta+r_{t}\quad\text{and}\quad\mu_{t}^{*}(\eta)=K_{t}\eta+\kappa_{t},

where
P_{t}\in\mathcal{S}_{n_{t}}
,
q_{t}
,
r_{t}
,
K_{t}
, and
\kappa_{t}
satisfy the following Riccati recursion:

\displaystyle P_{t}

\displaystyle\coloneqq-\tilde{A}_{t}^{\top}P_{t+1}\tilde{B}_{t}(\tilde{B}_{t}^{\top}P_{t+1}\tilde{B}_{t}+R)^{-1}\tilde{B}_{t}^{\top}P_{t+1}\tilde{A}_{t}

\displaystyle+\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda,t}+\tilde{A}_{t}^{\top}P_{t+1}\tilde{A}_{t}

\displaystyle q_{t}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\lambda\zeta_{t}+(\tilde{A}_{t}+\tilde{B}_{t}K_{t})^{\top}(q_{t+1}+2P_{t+1}\tilde{C}_{t}\bar{w})

\displaystyle r_{t}

\displaystyle\coloneqq r_{t+1}+\mathrm{tr}((\Sigma+\bar{w}\bar{w}^{\top})\tilde{C}_{t}^{\top}P_{t+1}\tilde{C}_{t})+q_{t+1}^{\top}\tilde{C}_{t}\bar{w}

\displaystyle-\kappa_{t}^{\top}(\tilde{B}_{t}^{\top}P_{t+1}\tilde{B}_{t}+R)\kappa_{t}

\displaystyle K_{t}

\displaystyle\coloneqq-(\tilde{B}_{t}^{\top}P_{t+1}\tilde{B}_{t}+R)^{-1}\tilde{B}_{t}^{\top}P_{t+1}\tilde{A}_{t}

\displaystyle\kappa_{t}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\textstyle-\frac{1}{2}(\tilde{B}_{t}^{\top}P_{t+1}\tilde{B}_{t}+R)^{-1}\tilde{B}_{t}^{\top}(q_{t+1}+2P_{t+1}\tilde{C}_{t}\bar{w})

with
P_{N}\coloneqq\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda,N}
,
q_{N}\coloneqq\lambda\zeta_{N}
, and
r_{N}\coloneqq 0
.

Proof.

The result follows from dynamic programming, e.g., see
[
21
, Th. 3.2.1]
and
[
28
, Ch. 6, Th. 2.15]
, where the model is nonstationary
[
21
, p. 32]
because the state space
\mathbb{R}^{n_{t}}
, the dynamics map
f_{t}
, and the stage cost function
c_{t}
depend on time. The optimal values of Problems
2
and
3
are equal (proof of Theorem
1
); the optimal value of Problem
3
is
J_{0}(\mathbf{x}_{0})
; and we denoted the optimal value of Problem
2
by
\mathcal{J}_{\lambda}^{*}(\mathbf{x}_{0})
. Hence,
\mathcal{J}_{\lambda}^{*}(\mathbf{x}_{0})=J_{0}(\mathbf{x}_{0})
. ∎

Let us comment on the structure of
\mathbf{u}^{*}
.
First, at each time
t
, the control

is an affine feedback of the truncated state history
\eta_{t}
, which contains at most
\mathbf{k}+1
recent states,
instead of the full history.
Second,
\tilde{B}_{t}^{\top}P_{t+1}\tilde{B}_{t}+R\in\mathcal{S}_{m}^{+}
is the only matrix being inverted in the recursion, and its dimension does not depend on
\mathbf{k}
.
Third, if
\mathbf{k}=0
, then
\mathbf{u}^{*}

simplifies to the predictive variance controllers (without temporal coupling) in
[
44
, Th. 2]
and
[
43
, Th. 5]
.
If furthermore
\lambda=0
and
\bar{w}=0_{n\times 1}
, then

becomes the linear state-feedback controller for standard stochastic LQR.
While enjoying a familiar analytical form,
\mathbf{u}^{*}
in the case of
\mathbf{k}\geq 1
allows the user to consider the aims of state regulation and temporal variability reduction simultaneously, as illustrated next.

6
Conclusion

This work presents a trajectory-wise risk concept that, when incorporated into an optimal control problem for a stochastic linear system, admits an elegant closed-form solution.
The derived controller
\mathbf{u}^{*}
can be viewed as one that shapes a stochastic state trajectory,
penalizing variability of the trajectory over time and also in a probabilistic sense. In the case of
\lambda>0
,
\mathbf{u}^{*}
admits an explicit risk-aware interpretation, while this benefit is lost in the case of
\lambda=0
.
Nonetheless, we found experimentally that
\mathbf{u}^{*}
with large
\mathbf{k}
and
\lambda=0
can result in risk-aware system behavior (see
\theta_{8}
in Fig.
1
(c)). Future research can examine further connections between temporal coupling and risk, possibly in other systems. Temporal or trajectory-wise risk concepts have potential utility in other stochastic control problems, such as those involving systems with data-based trajectory-wise representations
and systems with time delays. We hope the results presented here inspire other notions of risk and associated controllers that can be computed efficiently.
