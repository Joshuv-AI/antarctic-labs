Separating intrinsic from extrinsic stomatal control in different soils
Authors: Ibrahim Bourbia, Luke Yates, Andrea Carminati, Timothy J Brodribb
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Summary The regulation of stomata in response to environmental changes is one of the most dynamic and important behaviours in vascular plants, playing a central role in determining gas exchange and vulnerability to drought. Yet whether this regulation is determined by intrinsic species physiology or by soil hydraulic properties or a combination of both remains unsolved.Here, we use a long‐term dataset of needle water potential (Ψneedle) measurements to compare stomatal regulation in a hardy conifer, Callitris rhomboidea, growing at two sites with contrasting soil textures: sand and clay‐loam.Inferred stomatal sensitivity to water potential differed between soil types when expressed against soil water potential but exhibited a universal relationship to Ψneedle. This observation aligned with the predictions from an optimal soil water extraction model, indicating that distinct optimal water extraction in the two soil types could be achieved through a common relationship between stomatal conductance and Ψneedle.Our results suggest that the primary driver of stomatal response to soil and atmospheric dryness lies in a common response to leaf water potential. This work reconciles competing ideas of stomatal control, indicating how the appearance of soil‐specific behaviour is in fact the expected consequence of a single, optimal physiological behaviour across soil types. Characterising the variation in species‐specific stomatal response to leaf water potential will enable comparisons of water extraction behaviour between species in different soils. Keywords: plant water potential, soil hydraulics, soil moisture/drought, soil texture, soil type, stomatal behavior, vapour pressure deficit (VPD)

Introduction

Stomata dynamically regulate transpirational water loss and carbon uptake by adjusting their aperture in response to environmental stimuli, playing a key role in determining terrestrial productivity and the water cycle (Hetherington & Woodward, 2003; Schlesinger & Jasechko, 2014). By controlling transpiration, stomata also play a central role in maintaining plant water potential within functional limits, preventing it from dropping to levels that could cause xylem cavitation and plant damage under conditions of water stress (Brodribb et al., 2017; Buckley, 2019). When light is not limiting, low soil moisture and high vapour pressure deficit (VPD) or a combination of both lead to stomatal closure (Grossiord et al., 2020; Mencuccini et al., 2024). Yet, it remains unclear whether stomatal responses to these factors are determined by conservative species‐specific physiology or whether they can differ across soils. Clarifying this distinction is critical as it underpins how we characterise and predict species' stomatal regulation across different soil types.

Behavioural differences in stomatal responses to VPD and soil moisture across different species have been recognised as an important axis of variation in ecological and water use strategies (Klein, 2014; Gleason et al., 2025). However, recent studies suggest that such behaviour can also vary with soil texture even within the same species, challenging the concept of species‐specific regulation (Cai et al., 2021; Koehler et al., 2024; Wankmüller et al., 2024). The idea that soil modulates stomatal behaviour originated from early hypotheses, suggesting that roots sense drying soil and produce abscisic acid (ABA) signalling stomata to close (Gollan et al., 1986; Zhang & Davies, 1990; Khalil & Grace, 1993). However, this explanation has since been challenged by numerous studies, showing that stomata respond to ABA produced at the leaf level (McAdam et al., 2016; Buckley, 2019). Yet recent studies have shifted attention back to the soil, proposing the decline in soil hydraulic conductivity (K soil) as the main trigger of stomatal closure under water stress (Carminati & Javaux, 2020; Wankmüller et al., 2024). The dynamics of Ksoil during drying is largely influenced by soil texture, which can vary greatly across landscapes (Delval et al., 2025). Coarse soils lose more moisture and conductivity for a given decrease in soil water potential (Ψsoil) than fine soils (Cai et al., 2021). Hence to sustain the same rate of transpiration, plant water potential is expected to decline more steeply in coarse‐textured soils in response to declining Ψsoil compared with fine‐textured soils, potentially requiring earlier and stronger stomatal regulation in the former to avoid the early drop in K soil in sand, which could exacerbate declines in plant water potential to damaging levels (Carminati & Javaux, 2020; Wankmüller & Carminati, 2022). Consistent with this expectation, several pot studies have reported that, within the same species, stomata close at less negative Ψsoil in coarse‐textured soils compared with fine‐textured soils (Cai et al., 2021; Koehler et al., 2022). This implies that the regulatory behaviour of stomata is not conserved within species but can be plastic and soil‐dependent. This creates uncertainties in characterising species‐specific stomatal behaviour and predicting water use across edaphically heterogeneous landscapes and undermines the meaningfulness of interspecies comparisons. However, while expressing stomatal behaviour as a function of Ψsoil can highlight differences across soil types within the same species, there is no clear stomata mechanism linking Ψsoil directly with stomatal aperture (Carminati & Javaux, 2020; Cai et al., 2021; Wankmüller et al., 2024). Recent studies suggest that stomata respond to both Ψsoil and VPD by sensing the resultant changes in water potential induced at the leaf level (Ψleaf) (Rodriguez‐Dominguez et al., 2016; Buckley, 2017, 2019). If stomatal behaviour is directly governed by Ψleaf and its sensitivity is determined by species‐specific physiological constraints instead of soil hydraulic limitation, one would expect uniform species‐specific responses to Ψleaf across hydraulically differing soil textures. This has been suggested by a recent study across diverse species (Carminati et al., 2026), yet this remains to be tested within a species across contrasting soil types.

In this study, we examine the influence of soil texture on whole tree stomatal response to needle water potential (Ψneedle) in a single species. Our hypothesis is that individuals growing in sand and clay‐loam will have distinctive stomatal responses to Ψsoil across soil types. However, due to the likelihood of a direct mechanistic link between internal plant water potential and the stomatal aperture, this soil‐specific behaviour will be reconciled by a single response function between stomatal conductance and Ψneedle. To test this hypothesis, we collected a fine‐scale time series (15‐ to 30‐min intervals) of in situ Ψneedle (and corresponding Ψsoil) monitored with optical dendrometry in the conifer species Callitris rhomboidea across 1 to 4 highly variable growing seasons grown at two field sites with distinct soil textures: sandy and loam‐clay soils. We then use the soil‐needle water potential difference (ΔΨ) and detailed knowledge on the above‐ and belowground hydraulics (Bourbia et al., 2021, 2022, 2025) to infer and compare patterns of stomatal regulation in response to Ψsoil and Ψneedle across the two sites.

Materials and Methods

The experiment was carried out at two locations with contrasting soil types located on the southeast of Tasmania, Australia: Pelverata (43°03′03.5″ S 147°06′15.8″ E, 250 m elevation), an inland site characterised by a 50 cm deep clay‐loamy soil, and Dodges Ferry (42°51′13.4″ S 147°36′59.7″ E, 8 m elevation), a coastal site with quartz sand to a depth of >2 m. Both locations are characterised by cool, wet winters and relatively mild summers with episodic rainfall events and maximum daily air temperature ranging between 22 and 30°C.

Continuous monitoring of Ψneedle

Needle water potential (Ψneedle) was continuously monitored at both sites on trees of hardy conifer Callitris rhomboidea R. Br. Ex A. Rich (Cupressaceae) using estimates derived from optical dendrometers (Bourbia et al., 2025). At the sandy site, continuous monitoring was carried out on three trees ranging from 12 to 18 yr (5 m tall) over one growing season (from September 2024 to May 2025). At the clay‐loamy site, Ψneedle was monitored in four 12‐yr‐old trees (5 m tall) across five consecutive growing seasons (2020–2025), with the 2025 season overlapping the measurement period at the sandy site, allowing for direct comparison of stomatal behaviour between the two different sites Supporting Information (Dataset S1). The monitored trees were separated by an average distance of 50 m at the clay‐loamy site and 5–10 m at the sandy site.

In each tree, an optical dendrometer was attached to a determinate nongrowing branchlet (< 3 mm in diameter) to continuously monitor its width changes at 15‐ to 30‐min intervals. Branchlet width was calibrated in each individual tree against Ψneedle measured periodically (every 15–20 d) during the study period with a Scholander pressure chamber (PMS Instruments, Corvallis, OR, USA). These measurements were performed on neighbouring nontranspiring leaves that were wrapped in a damp paper towel and aluminium foil for at least 1 h before measurements. The covered leaves were then excised with a razor blade and placed in a sealable bag with moist paper towels. They were then put in a dark box, transported to the laboratory and measured within 1 h after the leaves were collected. Plant‐specific relationships between branchlet width and measured Ψneedle using the pressure bomb were established and used to determine Ψneedle dynamics during the experimental period (Fig. S1).

Ψneedle measurements were paired with site‐level air temperature and humidity that were monitored continuously at 15‐ to 30‐min intervals throughout each season using an SHT31 weatherproof temperature and humidity sensor installed within the mid‐canopy of one of the trees to capture the microclimate surrounding the transpiring leaves. The functional photosynthetic units (green branchlets with needles of C. rhomboidea) are highly dissected, resulting in a small boundary layer around them. This means that the VPD measured within the canopy should closely correspond to the actual VPD at the leaf level. These measurements were used to calculate VPD (Buck, 1981). Rainfall data were obtained from the Grove weather station (Station ID: 94220) for the clay‐loamy site, and the Hobart airport station (Station ID: 094250) for the sandy site. Both stations are maintained by the Australian Bureau of Meteorology and are located within c. 6–7 km of the study sites.

At both sites, the trees were not always monitored for the same duration or concurrently, but their monitoring periods overlapped for most of the study period (Fig. 1). We view the asynchronous monitoring as an opportunity to test the temporal consistency of stomatal behaviour.

For the clay‐loamy site, we also included an additional 4 yr of data in the analysis (from 2020 to 2024) (Bourbia et al., 2025) to ensure overlap in rainfall, VPD and Ψneedle between the two sites.

Comparing stomatal responses to Ψsoil and VPD between sandy and clay‐loamy sites

Whole plant stomatal behaviour (g c) in response to Ψsoil and VPD between sand and clay‐loamy sites was characterised in terms of soil‐to‐needle water potential difference ΔΨ (=Ψsoil–Ψneedle) in response to variation in VPD and Ψsoil using the combined Darcy–Fick laws under steady‐state conditions (Eqn 4) (Whitehead, 1998). Applying these laws requires near steady‐state water flux and water potential, meaning that capacitance effects must be negligible. Here, we used steady‐state maximum daytime ΔΨ to satisfy this condition (see ‘Model diagnostics’ in the Materials and Methods section). To characterise the regulatory behaviour of ΔΨ in response to changes in VPD and Ψsoil, we constructed a model of ΔΨ using a single steady‐state maximum daytime ΔΨ to represent each day, along with its corresponding VPD and Ψsoil. We chose steady‐state daily maximum ΔΨ to avoid the potential confounding effect of capacitance on diurnal Ψneedle dynamics that can occur under nonsteady‐state conditions of Ψneedle, which would otherwise violate the steady‐state assumptions of fluxes and water potential required for applying the combined Darcy–Fick laws in Eqn 4. Steady state refers here to conditions where Ψneedle reached a minimum value during daytime and remained relatively constant (< 5% change) for at least 1 h. In each individual tree, maximum daily ΔΨ was calculated as the difference between minimum mean daily Ψneedle (most negative value) averaged over a 2‐h period (using 4–8 Ψneedle datapoints) and its corresponding daytime Ψsoil. The corresponding daytime Ψsoil throughout the study period for each individual tree was determined using linear interpolation between the optically derived mean predawn Ψneedle values (maximum value of Ψneedle before sunrise averaged over 2 h). Under conditions of high nocturnal VPD, predawn Ψneedle does not represent Ψsoil due to nocturnal transpiration (Sellin, 1999; Kavanagh et al., 2007; Kangur et al., 2020). For this reason, Ψneedle values used for interpolating daytime Ψsoil in each individual tree throughout the study period were selected on the nights where humidity remained above 95% (VPD < 0.05 kPa) throughout the whole night (between midnight and before sunrise). Under such conditions, night‐time transpiration was found to be negligible in this species (Bourbia & Brodribb, 2024), hence predawn Ψneedle was assumed to have been given sufficient time to establish equilibrium with Ψsoil in the rooting zone.

Model diagnostics

Models were fitted using the R package brms (Bürkner, 2017) for full Bayesian inference using Hamiltonian Monte Carlo sampling methods (4 chains, 2000 iterations each) (Notes S1). Model convergence was assessed via improved R̂ statistics (Vehtari et al., 2021) and visual chain inspection. Model performance and comparisons were evaluated using approximate leave‐one‐out (LOO) cross‐validation to estimate R 2 and expected log predictive density (ELPD) (Table S1) respectively (Vehtari et al., 2017). Additional diagnostics included residual plots against VPD and Ψsoil, and posterior‐predictive checks (Table S2). Details of the data and code structure are provided in Table S3.

Our statistical model performed well, explaining 83% (R 2 = 0.826, 0.816 and 0.834; 95% CI) of the variation in ΔΨ across both soil types, conditional on the plant‐level random effects. The model's LOO R 2 is 0.817 (0.791 and 0.842; 95% CI), which is only slightly lower than the ordinary R 2, strongly suggesting the model is not overfit and has excellent ability to generalise to new observations for the observed plants. We also computed the marginal R 2 (R 2 marginal = 0.609, 0.395 and 0.730, 95% CI), which estimates the variance explained by the fitted model for a new unobserved plant. The generality of the model across the two sites is supported by the lack of pattern with respect to plant when the residuals are plotted against both VPD and Ψsoil (Fig. S2). In addition to the large percentage of variance explained, the validity of the model was demonstrated by the close correspondence between the observed and predicted data and the excellent calibration of the CIs (e.g. 94.06% of the data lie within the 95% CI; Fig. S3), as well as the model convergence diagnostic R̂ (all values were < 1.003 where values must be below 1.01 for convergence; Table S2).

K s‐p was set constant across the entire range of Ψneedle experienced by the trees in both soil types. (North & Nobel, 1991; Rodriguez‐Dominguez & Brodribb, 2020; Bourbia et al., 2021). The potential effects of soil‐type differences in how K s–p declines with decreasing Ψneedle during soil drying on predicted g c are discussed in the Results and Discussion section.

Inferring g c from Eqn 1 has been validated by comparison with independent gravimetric measurements in several species, including C. rhomboidea (Bourbia et al., 2022, 2023). We normalised g c within each plant by its maximum across the range of Ψneedle. We then extracted the upper envelope of the normalised g c across Ψneedle by binning Ψneedle into consecutive, nonoverlapping 0.3‐MPa intervals (0 to −2.5 MPa) and, within each bin, retaining the upper 5% of g c values per plant. This removed lower g c values that may have been affected by energy limitation or changes in leaf osmotic potential during drying. To these data, we modelled g c as a function of Ψneedle using a sigmoidal response function. We fitted two candidate models, each based on a three‐parameter sigmoidal function of the form g c = a/(1 + (Ψneedle/b) c ). The first model estimated separate values of a, b and c for each soil type, while the second model estimated common values across both soils (Notes S2). To compare models, and thereby test the hypothesis of a common vs soil‐specific relationship between g c, and Ψneedle, we used LOO cross‐validation as the basis for information‐theoretic model selection (Table S4) (Vehtari et al., 2017; Yates et al., 2023). We accounted for model selection uncertainty by computing the mean and the SE of the differences in the estimated model scores (Fig. S4; Table S5). These nonlinear models were fitted using the R package brms, with model convergence assessed using the diagnostics described in the previous section and reported in the Notes S2 and S3.

Results and Discussion

At both sites, the monitored trees exhibited strong variation in diurnal Ψneedle and experienced a wide range of VPD and Ψsoil (predawn Ψneedle) that was associated with seasonal variation in VPD, episodic soil water deficit and rainfall events (Fig. 1). Throughout the study period (September 2024–May 2025), both sand and clay‐loam trees experienced similar variations in maximum daytime VPD, with mean daytime air temperature and VPD ranging from 0.34 to 4.09 kPa, respectively. However, the sandy site received 50% (269.4 mm) less rainfall than the loamy site (521 mm). As a result, Ψneedle of the trees in the sandy soil declined more frequently and reached lower values, with minima c. −5 MPa, compared to the loamy site where declines were less frequent and did not fall below −4 MPa. Variation was also observed within each site, as individual trees differed in the minimum Ψneedle experienced during the season, likely due to variability in root depths between trees. Note that for the clay‐loamy site, we also included an additional 4 yr of data (from 2020 to 2024) (Bourbia et al., 2025), to ensure complete overlap in monthly rainfall and Ψneedle between the two soil types.

The regulation of ΔΨ (Eqn 4) exhibited both conserved and divergent features across the two soil types. A conserved aspect of the response was the similar sensitivity of ΔΨ to VPD in both sand and clay‐loam trees under wet conditions (Fig. 2) (Ψsoil close to 0 MPa). In both soil types, at a given Ψsoil, ΔΨ initially increased with VPD due to declining Ψneedle before reaching an asymptote (θ 0), with 95% of the asymptotic value was attained at VPD of 1.84 kPa (1.64 and 2.04; 95% credible interval (CI)) in clay soil and 1.59 kPa (1.39 and 1.81; 95% CI) in sandy soil, indicating that beyoond these VPD thresholds Ψneedle remained relatively constant in both soil types. The 95% CI for the difference between soils in the VPD required to reach 95% of the asymptotic ΔΨ value included zero (−0.54 to 0.04), indicating no credible evidence of a difference in the VPD response between sandy and clay soils. This asymptotic pattern reflects progressive stomatal closure with increasing VPD, which is consistent with stomatal models whereby stomatal closure at high VPD prevents large excursions in transpiration and leaf water potential that could lead to tissue damage (Novick et al., 2016; Grossiord et al., 2020; Brodribb et al., 2021). No credible difference in λ (rate at which ΔΨ reaches the asymptote) (Fig. 3) was observed between soil types. From a hydraulic perspective, the lack of soil influence on VPD response in the two hydraulically contrasting soil textures in the wet range is somewhat expected, as the hydraulic conductivity of both soils when wet is large compared to plant hydraulic conductance and, therefore, not expected to limit water flow to the plant (Draye et al., 2010). Therefore, the VPD response is likely governed by intrinsic factors, pointing to a conserved internal mechanism controlling stomatal behaviour in this species. However, as Ψsoil declined due to soil drying, stomatal sensitivity to VPD appeared to diverge between the two soil types (Fig. 2). In clay‐loam, the asymptotic relationship between ΔΨ and VPD initially remained constant with the initial decline in Ψsoil as shown by the constancy of the asymptote θ (Fig. 4), suggesting an initial insensitivity of stomata to declining Ψsoil. Such insensitivity might be attributed to a gradual shift in the osmotic potential of mesophyll cells to more negative values during progressive soil drying, a behaviour that has been observed in many species exposed to gradual or frequent drying in the field and glasshouse experiments (Premachandra et al., 1992; Cardoso et al., 2020; Herrera et al., 2022). However, once the Ψsoil dropped below a mean threshold of −0.92 MPa (−1.10 and −0.76; 95% CI) in the clay‐loamy soil (Figs 2, 3, 4), stomata became highly sensitive to Ψsoil, closing more in response to high VPD, as shown by the gradual decline in θ at Ψsoil below this threshold (Fig. 4). This collapse in ΔΨ mirrors the commonly observed strong reduction in sap flux in response to VPD in dry soils driven by strong stomatal closure (Dumberger et al., 2025). This also suggests a limit to further osmotic adjustment beyond this soil hydration threshold. In sand, however, θ collapsed immediately after Ψsoil began to decline, yielding a substantially less negative threshold of −0.13 MPa (−0.24 and −0.01; 95% CI), indicating strong stomatal control from the early stages of soil drying. The absence of a plateau in θ in response to declining Ψsoil in sandy soil (Fig. 4) suggests an absence of progressive osmotic adjustment during soil drying. However, under wet soil conditions (Ψneedle close to 0 MPa), θ was credibly higher in sand than in clay (1.35 MPa in sand vs 0.8 MPa in clay; Fig. 3), meaning that stomata in sandy soil closed at a more negative Ψneedle than in clay in wet soils. This suggests that trees at the sandy site may already be osmotically pre‐adjusted even Ψsoil close to 0 MPa. A possible explanation is that trees growing in sandy soils experience daily reductions in soil hydraulic conductance, which can occur at Ψsoil close to 0 MPa (Wankmüller et al., 2024). This would impose hydraulic limitation for a given transpiration demand and could lead to recurrent short‐term diurnal water deficits, thereby promoting baseline osmotic adjustment. By contrast, trees growing at the clay‐loamy site would have less incentive to maintain osmotic adjustment under wet conditions, because soil hydraulic conductance is not limiting. Instead, osmotic adjustment in clay appears to develop only during soil drying, as indicated by the constancy of θ over a range of declining Ψsoil. A further distinction between the two soil types is the rate (α) of θ collapse below the Ψsoil threshold, which was greater in sand than in clay‐loam (Figs 3 and 4), reflecting faster stomatal closure as Ψsoil declined in sand compared with clay‐loam.

The differences in Ψsoil thresholds between the two soil types appear to coincide with the different points at which soil hydraulic conductance drops rapidly and becomes highly limiting for water extraction by plants (Carminati & Javaux, 2020; Wankmüller et al., 2024). This finding aligns with pot studies reporting that stomata close at less negative Ψsoil in sandy compared with clay‐loamy soils (Cai et al., 2021; Koehler et al., 2022; Wankmüller et al., 2024), attributing this early closure to the avoidance of early and steep declines in soil hydraulic conductance in sandy soils during the onset of drying. The initial conclusion from our findings might be that stomatal regulation is not determined by species‐specific physiology but strongly modulated by soil texture. While this makes soil moisture a useful predictor of transpiration, it also means that species‐specific stomatal behaviour cannot be reliably characterised without accounting for soil texture and raises doubts about whether species‐specific regulation can be meaningfully defined at all.

However, the closure of stomata in response to increasing VPD implied in the wet range in both soil types (Ψsoil close to 0 MPa), as shown by the asymptotic relationship between ΔΨ and VPD (Fig. 2), challenges the soil limitation hypothesis. Under well‐hydrated conditions, hydraulic conductance of both sandy and clay‐loamy soils is several orders of magnitude higher than plant hydraulic conductance and does not limit water supply to the plant and therefore cannot explain stomatal closure at high VPD. This implies that stomatal regulation can occur independently of soil hydraulic limitation, supporting the idea of an internal driven control mechanism mediated by leaf water potential. If stomatal regulation was primarily governed by local changes in leaf water potential, then uniform behaviour would be expected within species if responses were expressed relative to leaf water potential, irrespective of soil texture. Indeed, when we expressed the implied stomatal behaviour in relation to Ψneedle, this revealed a similar behaviour across the two hydraulically contrasting soil types (the model with common parameters was preferred over the model with soil‐specific parameters (ΔELPD = −4.1, se(ΔELPD) = 1.8) (Fig. 5)). This supports the conclusion that stomatal regulation is not triggered by changes in soil hydraulic conductivity as previously suggested (Carminati & Javaux, 2020; Abdalla et al., 2021; Cai et al., 2021; Wankmüller et al., 2024) but is instead controlled by stomatal sensitivity to leaf water potential, which is determined by inherent plant physiological constraints. Such a conserved relationship between stomata and leaf water potential within species implies that species can be characterised and compared. This then provides a robust framework for predicting and comparing plant water use and climate sensitivity across contrasting environments.

It is worth noting that g c predicted from Eqn 7 was derived assuming a constant K s–p. During soil drying, however, K s–p can decline, which would violate this assumption. In this species, K s–p has been shown to remain stable in wet soils under diurnal changes in transpiration and during early soil drying (Bourbia et al., 2021, 2022; Bourbia & Brodribb, 2024), declining only when K soil becomes limiting. Previous studies have shown that substantial stomatal closure during soil drying occurs concomitantly with declines in K s–p (Rodriguez‐Dominguez & Brodribb, 2020; Abdalla et al., 2021; Bourbia et al., 2021). Because stomata in both soil types appear to begin closing at a similar Ψneedle (Fig. 5), any decline in K s–p would also be expected to begin at a similar Ψneedle in both soil types. Therefore, according to Eqn 7, any decline in K s–p below the Ψneedle at which stomatal closure begins during soil drying would primarily steepen the already strong decline in gc with decreasing Ψneedle. However, K s–p may decline to different extents relative to Ψneedle beyond this point in the two different soil types, which could introduce soil‐type differences in the slope of the g c decline in response to declining Ψneedle. Direct measurements of K s–p dynamics during soil drying would be required to quantify this effect.

Our observations of distinct stomatal behaviour in the two hydraulically different soil textures align very well with the predictions from the optimal soil water extraction model for each soil type (Carminati & Javaux, 2020) (Fig. 6). According to this model, to optimally extract water from the soil during drying while avoiding the cost related to excessive declines in Ψneedle due to declines in soil hydraulic conductance, transpiration in sandy soil is predicted to decline at less negative Ψsoil than in clay‐loam. Such divergence is attributed to early and steep declines in hydraulic conductance in sandy compared with clay loamy soils during drying. However, using the same model, we found that this distinct optimum soil water extraction across the two soil types within the same species can also be achieved by a conserved relationship between stomatal aperture and Ψneedle (Fig. 6), further supporting the idea that stomatal regulation is governed by conserved intrinsic plant controls, independent of soil hydraulic properties.

This indicates that distinct soil‐specific patterns of water extraction during soil drying can be achieved through a common stomatal–needle water potential relationship across soil types. For the simulations, we used average woody plant parameters from the literature (Wankmüller et al., 2024). We set the water‐potential gradient needed to sustain maximum transpiration (Emax) at maximum plant hydraulic conductance (K plant‐max) as ΔΨ = E max/K plant‐max = 0.8 MPa when the soil was wet (Ψsoil ≈ 0 MPa). Maximum transpiration per root area (E max/A root) was set to 10−7 cm s−1, with A root being equal to the total root surface active in water uptake. The plant water potential threshold at which the stem hydraulic conductance is reduced to 50% was set to −2.5 MPa based on reported values of xylem embolism in woody species. All plant model parameters were kept consistent across soil types, aside from the soil hydraulic properties that varied with soil texture.

Conclusion

Together, our results show that apparently distinct patterns of stomatal behaviour in different soil types can be explained by a common stomatal response to leaf water potential. Hence, characterising the g c‐Ψneedle relationship should allow comparison of species' water‐extraction behaviour across different environments without needing to account for soil texture, which can vary dramatically among sites. Incorporating this relationship into vegetation models could therefore reduce reliance on detailed soil parameterisation when predicting plant water use.
