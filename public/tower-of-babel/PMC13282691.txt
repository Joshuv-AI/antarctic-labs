Characterizing the Compound Extreme Temperature and PM2.5 Pollution Events: A Multi‐Country Analysis
Authors: Deyang Chen, Mustafa Zahid, Minghao Qiu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe increasing frequency of climate extreme events and persistent air pollution challenges pose compound environmental risks, yet the spatiotemporal patterns and trends of compound extreme temperature‐air pollution events remain poorly understood globally. In this paper, we quantify the temporal trends and spatial distributions of compound extreme temperature‐PM2.5 pollution events across 10,067 monitor locations in 43 countries spanning 2003–2023. We find that compound extreme heat and PM2.5 pollution (heat + PM2.5) events decreased in most countries in our sample (35/43), while compound extreme cold and PM2.5 pollution (cold + PM2.5) events showed diverse trends across different nations. Despite the increase in global temperature, cold + PM2.5 events still represent a substantial component of the compound extreme temperature and PM2.5 pollution events. Sensitivity analyses using alternative thresholds and MERRA‐2 PM2.5 data sets yield generally consistent results. The spatial heterogeneity in the compound events is linked to the diverse temperature‐PM2.5 relationships, for example, we find higher PM2.5 concentrations during cold conditions in China, India, and Europe, while the USA and Australia show higher PM2.5 concentrations during hot conditions. Concentrated populations in high‐frequency regions further amplify the exposure burden, with India, Pakistan, and China exhibiting disproportionately higher exposure levels relative to the compound events. Given the likely health impacts of these compound events, our findings suggest that future policies should target temperature‐dependent emission activities that will amplify pollution under extreme temperature conditions.Keywords: extreme temperature, PM2.5 , compound events, health effect, climate change, air quality

Introduction

Extreme temperature and ambient air pollution are two leading environmental risk factors for morbidity and mortality worldwide (Ebi et al., 2021; T. Li et al., 2018; Matthews et al., 2025). Combined ambient and indoor air pollution is estimated to have caused 8.1 million deaths globally in 2021, making it the second leading risk factor for global mortality, while non‐optimal temperatures (including both heat and cold conditions) contribute to 1.9 million deaths (Global Burden of Disease, 2024). Most studies examine the trends, distributions, and health impacts of extreme temperature and air pollution in isolation (S. J. Brown et al., 2008; Lee et al., 2014; S. Li et al., 2023; T. Li et al., 2018). However, these two issues are closely interconnected (Castro et al., 2024; Roberts, 2004), with bidirectional interactions that can, at times, contribute to the occurrence of these extreme events (Kalisa et al., 2018). Their co‐occurrence can generate synergistic effects that amplify health risks beyond what would be expected from individual exposures alone (Castro et al., 2024; Roberts, 2004; Zhou et al., 2023). Characterizing global patterns of these compound events is essential for assessing their potential health consequences and informing integrated solutions.

Previous research has investigated the compound events of extreme temperatures and air pollution at various spatiotemporal scales. Among the different air pollutants, existing studies have predominantly focused on ozone pollution and its occurrence with extreme heat events (Schnell & Prather, 2017; Su et al., 2025; S. Wang et al., 2025; Y. Wang et al., 2025), given the well‐established temperature dependence of ozone formation (Coates et al., 2016; Im et al., 2011; Stathopoulou et al., 2008). However, PM2.5 (atmospheric particulate matter with aerodynamic diameter ≤2.5 μm) remains the leading cause of mortality from air pollution (Burnett et al., 2018; Chowdhury et al., 2022; T. Li et al., 2018), responsible for approximately 8 times more deaths globally than ozone (Health Effects Institute, 2024). Therefore, quantifying the characteristics of compound extreme temperature‐PM2.5 pollution events is a key research gap considering their potential health damages. While extreme heat events have generally increased across many regions due to human activities (S. J. Brown et al., 2008; Fischer et al., 2021), there exists substantial regional heterogeneity in trends of extreme cold conditions (J. Guan et al., 2022; Y. Guan et al., 2021). In the case of PM2.5, some countries have experienced rising concentrations driven by urbanization and industrialization (Sahoo & Sethi, 2022; Yun & Zhao, 2022), whereas others have seen declines due to environmental regulations (Yu et al., 2022; Zou, 2021) and energy transitions (Pereira et al., 2021). Given these divergent and spatially heterogeneous trends in both extreme temperature and PM2.5, the occurrence and distribution of compound extreme temperature‐PM2.5 pollution events remain largely unquantified at the global scale.

Compound extreme temperature‐PM2.5 pollution events can arise from their two‐way interactions associated with multiple mechanisms. First, extreme temperature increases energy consumption and fossil fuel combustion, thereby elevating emissions of PM2.5 precursors (e.g., Volatile Organic Compounds (VOCs), SO2, NO x ), leading to elevated PM2.5 concentrations (Auffhammer et al., 2017; Wei et al., 2022). Second, higher temperatures increase wildfire risks, releasing large amounts of PM2.5 into the atmosphere (Burke et al., 2023; Childs et al., 2022). Third, extreme heat often coincides with stagnant meteorological conditions, such as high pressure, weak winds, and a shallow boundary layer, which inhibit the dispersion of pollutants and cause PM2.5 to accumulate near the surface (Nasong et al., 2025). In turn, PM2.5 can influence temperature through radiative and dynamic feedbacks (S.‐L. Chen et al., 2021; J. Li et al., 2020; X. Wang et al., 2024). Together, these processes highlight the complex and reciprocal nature of temperature–PM2.5 interactions.

Several existing studies have examined the trends of compound extreme temperature and PM2.5 pollution events. For instance, Z.‐Y. Chen et al. (2025) analyzed the trends of such events across 35 European countries from 2003 to 2023. Schnell and Prather (2017) investigated the co‐occurrence characteristics of PM2.5 and extreme temperatures in USA. Gao et al. (2024) projected future changes in compound extreme heat and PM2.5 pollution events in China and India. Huang et al. (2025) characterized the global evolution of compound hot‐and‐polluted episodes (HPEs) and quantified their associated PM2.5‐related premature mortality. Nduka et al. (2022) analyzed long‐term trends of HPEs in the Pearl River Delta and quantified their associated air pollution–related mortality. While existing research on compound extreme temperature–PM2.5 events has provided valuable insights, the body of literature remains limited, and several critical research gaps persist. First, most existing research focuses on individual countries or specific regions (Z.‐Y. Chen et al., 2025; Gao et al., 2024; Schnell & Prather, 2017), lacking a comprehensive global analysis. Second, existing research mainly concentrates on heat extremes (Gao et al., 2024; Huang et al., 2025; Nduka et al., 2022; Su et al., 2025), while compound events involving extreme cold and air pollution remain largely unexplored. Finally, most studies rely on numerical modeling rather than ground‐based observations. Numerical models are often limited by incomplete process representations, coarse resolution, and uncertainty in simulating extreme conditions. These limitations can lead to significant uncertainties, particularly when observational constraints are scarce. Leveraging high‐quality observational data sets is essential for improving the characterization of compound temperature–PM2.5 events.

In this study, we comprehensively assess the frequency and characteristics of compound extreme temperature‐PM2.5 pollution events globally from 2003 to 2023. PM2.5 concentration data is derived from a global network of ground‐based monitoring stations across 43 countries. Compound events are defined as days when both extreme temperature and PM2.5 pollution conditions are met, and they are categorized into compound extreme cold and PM2.5 pollution (cold + PM2.5) events, and compound extreme heat and PM2.5 pollution (heat + PM2.5) events. Our analysis quantifies the spatiotemporal trends of extreme temperatures, PM2.5 pollution, and their compound events at the global scale. We further conduct sensitivity analyses using PM2.5 concentrations derived from the Modern‐Era Retrospective Analysis for Research and Applications, Version 2 (MERRA‐2) reanalysis data set. In addition, we further estimate the population exposure to compound temperature–PM2.5 events, indicating the potential health risk associated with these events. To shed light on the driving factors of the compound events, we examine the PM2.5 concentration distributions across different temperature conditions and the temperature‐PM2.5 relationship across different countries. The structure of this paper is as follows. Section 2 describes the data and methods. Section 3 provides the main results. The conclusions and discussion are given in Section 4.

Methods and Data Sources

Data Sources

In this study, we use daily temperature and PM2.5 concentration data from a global network of 10,067 ground‐based monitoring stations. Figure S1 in Supporting Information S1 shows the distribution of the monitoring stations, which are located in 43 countries across the globe. Our monitoring station data set includes 19,990,888 observations in total. Since the EU, India, China, and the USA have the highest station density and thus provide the most statistically representative samples, our analysis focuses primarily on these four regions. To explore broader patterns of compound extreme temperature‐PM2.5 pollution events, a separate analysis using all monitoring locations is also conducted. We acknowledge that the spatial distribution of surface air quality monitors is highly uneven across the world, thus our results can only reflect the pattern in these sampled regions. Nevertheless, we believe it is important to characterize the compound events using in situ measurements first, before extending our research to reanalysis PM2.5 data sets with better spatial coverage. As temperature is often used as an input variable in those modeling pipelines, this leads to potential concerns that the temperature‐PM2.5 relationships are mechanical in these data sets.

PM2.5 observations were derived from the United States Environmental Protection Agency (US EPA), European Environment Agency (EEA), China National Environmental Monitoring Center (CNEMC), Open Air Quality (OpenAQ), and other publicly available databases. To ensure data quality, we apply several filtering criteria. Stations are included only if they report PM2.5 concentrations for at least 300 days in a given year, and at least 3 years of coverage are retained for analysis. Outliers and extreme values beyond 99.9th quantile are identified and removed based on statistical thresholds. In addition, duplicate records, negative concentrations, and values showing unrealistic temporal spikes or flatlines are excluded.

To assess the robustness of our results to the choice of PM2.5 data source, we also conduct the key analyses using the MERRA‐2 aerosol reanalysis product. We aggregated the hourly concentrations to derive daily mean PM2.5 values. The native MERRA‐2 resolution (0.5° × 0.625°) was regridded to 0.5° × 0.5° using bilinear interpolation.

Population data were obtained from the LandScan Global data set developed by the Oak Ridge National Laboratory, which provides annual gridded population estimates at ∼1 km resolution. Data for 2003–2023 were aggregated to a 0.5° resolution by summing grid‐level population counts to match the spatial resolution of the gridded PM2.5 and temperature data sets. We use the 2m temperature data from the ERA5 hourly reanalysis data set (2003–2023). This data set provides gridded temperature estimates at a spatial resolution of 0.25° × 0.25°. We derive daily temperatures by aggregating the hourly data at each station's geographic location. For the analysis using MERRA‐2 PM2.5 data, the temperature data were aggregated to a 0.5° spatial resolution for consistency.

Definition of Compound Extreme Temperature and PM2.5 Pollution Events

We define extreme temperature using a grid‐based, percentile‐threshold approach (Ridder et al., 2022; J. Wang et al., 2021). Daily minimum and maximum temperatures during 2003–2023 were extracted at each grid across the study domain. Extreme heat and cold events are defined using the 95th and 5th percentiles of the historical (2003–2023) temperature distribution, with stricter thresholds (99th and 1st percentiles) used to ensure the robustness of the results to alternative extreme temperature definitions. These percentile thresholds were computed individually for each grid based on the full 21‐year reference period. To link these gridded temperature thresholds to observed data, we spatially match the coordinates of national air quality monitoring stations (PM2.5 monitoring stations) to the nearest grid. A given station‐day is classified as an extreme cold (or heat) day if the observed daily minimum (or maximum) temperature falls below (for cold) or exceeds (for heat) the corresponding percentile threshold for that grid.

To define PM2.5 pollution events, we use the 24‐hr threshold of 35 μg/m3 and 15 μg/m3 to test the sensitivity of our results. The 35 μg/m3 threshold aligns with key standards such as the U.S. EPA's National Ambient Air Quality Standards (NAAQS), China's Class‐1 ambient air quality standard, and the World Health Organization's Interim Target‐3 (IT‐3). The alternative threshold of 15 μg/m3 is selected as it corresponds to the latest, more stringent 2021 WHO Global Air Quality Guideline. A compound event day is defined as any day that simultaneously meets the criteria for both an extreme temperature event and a PM2.5 pollution event.

Time Trend Analysis

For each monitoring station, the annual frequency of compound events was calculated as the ratio of compound event days to the total number of sampling days in that year. To obtain national‐level estimates, we compute the arithmetic mean of these station‐level ratios across all stations within each country. Finally, to standardize the metric, the resulting proportions were multiplied by 365 days, yielding the estimated average number of compound event days per year. To estimate the long‐term trend in compound event frequency for each station or country, we regress the annual number of events against time using an ordinary least squares (OLS) model.

We analyze PM2.5 concentrations under different temperature conditions using two approaches. First, we compare daily PM2.5 concentrations across five temperature intervals (<0°C, 0–10°C, 10–20°C, 20–30°C, and >30°C). Besides, we examine the temporal trends of annual mean PM2.5 concentrations under three temperature conditions: extreme cold days (below the 5th percentile), non‐extreme temperature days (5th–95th percentile), and extreme heat days (above the 95th percentile).

Results

Patterns of Extreme Temperature and PM2.5 Events

Figure 1 presents the temporal trends of extreme temperature events and PM2.5 pollution events across different countries (Figures 1a–1c), along with the annual average PM2.5 concentrations at monitoring stations (Figure 1d). The temporal trends of extreme temperature events and PM2.5 pollution show distinct regional patterns over the study period. For extreme cold events, China and India display increasing trends in station‐averaged frequency, with annual growth rates of 0.25%/yr and 0.15%/yr, respectively. In contrast, the USA and EU demonstrate declining trends in extreme cold event occurrence throughout the sample period. However, the trend estimates are not significant during the sample period (p > 0.05). The frequency of extreme heat events is increasing in most regions, and the trend is statistically significant in the EU and the USA (p < 0.05), but not in China. EU demonstrates the highest growth rate for extreme heat events, with an 0.28% increase each year. As shown in Figures S2 and S3 in Supporting Information S1, the frequency of extreme temperature events (defined by grid‐specific historical 5th/95th percentiles) is not mechanically linked to local mean temperature, as each threshold reflects the station's own climatic distribution.

We observe significant improvements in PM2.5 pollution across all four regions, though at varying rates. China shows the steepest decline in PM2.5‐pollution days among all regions, indicating marked progress in air pollution control. India shows a more gradual decline in PM2.5 pollution levels, while the USA and EU maintain steady but modest decreasing trends from their relatively low baseline concentrations. It is important to note that the country‐level results presented above are averages derived from the monitoring stations and do not necessarily represent the nation as a whole. The trends of individual extreme cold events, extreme heat events, and PM2.5 pollution events in our study are consistent with previous findings (S. J. Patterns of Compound Extreme Temperature‐PM2.5 Events

Figure 2 presents the annual mean frequency of compound events calculated using ground‐based observations and MERRA‐2 PM2.5 data. India experiences the highest average frequency of cold + PM2.5 events (3.6% in monitoring station data), while the USA has the lowest (0.1% in monitoring station data). In addition, Central and North China are also hotspots for compound cold + PM2.5 events. Winter cold outbreaks in this region are typically accompanied by stable atmospheric stratification, suppressed boundary layer heights, and temperature inversions that inhibit pollutant dispersion (Jin et al., 2022; Zhong et al., 2018). Additionally, the topographic barrier of the Taihang and Yanshan Mountains traps pollutants within the North China Plain (Liao et al., 2023; RenHe et al., 2014), while intensive winter heating emissions further elevate PM2.5 concentrations during cold episodes (Song et al., 2023). For both cold + PM2.5 and heat + PM2.5 compound events, MERRA‐2 and station‐based analyses show broad agreement in identifying the spatial hotspots of high compound event frequency (Figures 2a–2f). When sampling is conducted over the same spatial domain, the trends derived from monitoring station data and MERRA‐2 estimates are broadly consistent; however, the trends may change when the spatial coverage is expanded (Figure S8 in Supporting Information S1). Notably, the gridded MERRA‐2 analysis (Figures 2c and 2f) captures elevated compound event frequencies in regions with limited ground‐based monitoring coverage, including southern Australia and the Amazon‐Cerrado transition zone in western and central Brazil, both regions prone to concurrent wildfire‐driven PM2.5 pollution and extreme heat events (B. Chen et al., 2024; Ye et al., 2022).

However, systematic differences exist between the two PM2.5 data sources (Figure S6 in Supporting Information S1 for a comparison of the PM2.5 data and Figure S7 in Supporting Information S1 for a comparison of the compound events). At the station level, MERRA‐2 tends to underestimate compound event frequencies relative to station observations in China and India. The agreement is strongest for India (r = 0.796–0.830), where the high baseline frequency provides a robust signal‐to‐noise ratio. In contrast, MERRA‐2 shows low correlation with station‐derived cold + PM2.5 frequencies in the USA (r = 0.148) and EU (r = 0.079), largely because compound event frequencies in these regions are extremely low (<1%), making the comparison sensitive to small absolute differences. These regional discrepancies are consistent with the known limitations of MERRA‐2 in capturing fine‐scale PM2.5 variability in areas with complex emission source distributions and strong meteorological gradients (Buchard et al., 2016; Hammer et al., 2020).

We find that heat + PM2.5 events are more frequent than cold + PM2.5 events when defined with a lower PM2.5 threshold, whereas they are less frequent than cold + PM2.5 events under a high PM2.5 threshold. This pattern is robust and holds consistent for the aggregate of all stations (Figure S9 in Supporting Information S1). Using the high PM2.5 threshold (≥35 μg/m3) and the 95th/5th extreme temperature definition (Figure S9a in Supporting Information S1), the cold + PM2.5 median frequency (0.15%) is 3 times higher than that of heat + PM2.5 events (0.05%). The differing mechanisms of pollution formation potentially explain this threshold‐dependent contrast. Hot and stagnant conditions can favor the accumulation of PM2.5 and may enhance secondary aerosol formation. In contrast, cold, stable conditions trap pollutants and are coupled with increased primary emissions from heating, allowing PM2.5 concentrations to accumulate and spike to much higher levels (Green et al., 2015; Yan et al., 2022).

Conversely, the frequency of cold + PM2.5 events increases in the USA and EU while it decreases in India and China. Overall, these results suggest that the key findings of this study are robust to the choice of PM2.5 data source and spatial coverage.

At the national level (Figures 3c and 3f), India records the highest total exposure intensity for both cold + PM2.5 (∼12.5 billion person‐days/year) and heat + PM2.5 events (∼15.8 billion person‐days/year), followed by China with approximately 6.4 and 8.3 billion person‐days/year for cold and heat events, respectively. For both countries, heat + PM2.5 exposure exceeds cold + PM2.5 exposure, consistent with the higher event frequencies observed for heat‐based compound events in these regions.

The population‐weighted exposure days analysis (Figures 3b and 3e) provides a standardized per‐capita metric of individual‐level exposure burden. Pakistan exhibits the highest values, with residents experiencing an average of about 11 and 15 days per person per year exposed to cold + PM2.5 and heat + PM2.5 events, respectively. Pakistan, India and China consistently rank among the top countries, indicating that these nations bear a disproportionate share of the global compound exposure burden.

PM2.5 Levels Under Different Temperature Conditions

We analyze the differences in frequency and trends of heat + PM2.5 and cold + PM2.5 events by examining PM2.5 under different temperature conditions. Figure S11 in Supporting Information S1 shows the average PM2.5 concentrations in winter (December–February in the Northern Hemisphere; June–August in the Southern Hemisphere) and summer (June–August in the Northern Hemisphere; December–February in the Southern Hemisphere), and we find that PM2.5 concentrations are higher in winter at most global stations. However, this association may reflect a combination of factors such as seasonal emissions, atmospheric stability, and boundary layer dynamics, rather than temperature effects alone. We further explore the PM2.5 concentration distributions under different temperatures across various countries (Figure 4a). We find that PM2.5 concentrations are higher under low‐temperature conditions in China, India, and the EU. The USA exhibits higher PM2.5 concentrations in high‐temperature days, which may be attributed to more frequent wildfire activities during hot weather (Hu et al., 2025). Australia, another wildfire hotspot, similarly shows higher PM2.5 concentrations under high‐temperature conditions, which validates our mechanistic hypothesis regarding the temperature dependence of PM2.5 in the USA (Figure S12 in Supporting Information S1). These distributions exhibit consistent trends across the two PM2.5 data sets. Beyond differences in emission sources, certain atmospheric chemical processes can also explain the concentration differences between low‐ and high‐temperature conditions. At lower temperatures, the gas‐particle partitioning of semi‐volatile species favors the particulate phase, particularly for ammonium nitrate (NH4NO3), which thermodynamically dissociates into gaseous ammonia (NH3) and nitric acid (HNO3) at higher temperatures (M. Wang et al., 2020). Conversely, elevated temperatures enhance photochemical oxidation of VOCs and nitrogen oxides (NO x ), promoting secondary organic aerosol formation, which can subsequently increase PM2.5 levels through oxidation pathways (Hallquist et al., 2009; Srivastava et al., 2022). However, higher temperatures are also associated with deeper boundary layer heights, which dilute surface‐level pollutant concentrations and may offset the enhanced chemical production (Q. Li et al., 2021). Overall, differences in PM2.5 concentration levels reflect variations in both emission sources and atmospheric chemical processes.

Figure 4b shows long‐term trends in annual average PM2.5 concentrations under extreme cold, extreme heat, and non‐extreme conditions. China achieved consistent and significant declines across all temperature categories (−2.6 to −2.9 μg/m3/yr), indicating that emission controls have been broadly effective across multiple sources and weather conditions. Nevertheless, concentrations remain highest during cold extremes due to winter heating emissions and stagnant air (H. Wu et al., 2015), and lowest during heat events when vertical mixing disperses pollutants and heating demand is minimal. India shows sharper seasonal contrasts: cold extremes register the highest pollution due to biomass burning, household fuels, and poor dispersion (Lan et al., 2022). Although PM2.5 has seen the largest decline (−8.7 μg/m3/yr) during extreme cold conditions, absolute levels remain high. Hot and non‐extreme conditions have lower levels and moderate declines, suggesting partial success in reducing non‐winter emissions. The USA maintains uniformly low concentrations, with slight increases during heat extremes linked to secondary aerosol formation, higher electricity demand for cooling, and wildfire smoke during heatwaves and droughts (Burke et al., 2021, 2023). The EU displays minimal variation across temperature categories, with heat extremes and non‐extreme days showing nearly identical concentrations, while PM2.5 on extreme cold days displays greater interannual variability.

Discussions and Conclusions

This study reveals five key findings regarding compound extreme temperature‐PM2.5 events: (a) Cold + PM2.5 events and Heat + PM2.5 events are both prevalent globally, with strong spatial heterogeneity in their frequency and trend. Overall, cold + PM2.5 events have a similar frequency to Heat + PM2.5 events, despite the global increase in heat extremes. (b) Both cold + PM2.5 and heat + PM2.5 are decreasing in most of the stations in our sample, driven by the declines in PM2.5 concentrations. The frequency of heat + PM2.5 events decreased across all four regions, while cold + PM2.5 events showed diverse trends in different nations. This finding remains robust across different definitions for the compound events. (c) We find that higher PM2.5 concentrations tend to occur under cold conditions in regions like China, India, and Europe, whereas hot conditions are associated with higher PM2.5 in countries such as the U.S. and Australia—likely reflecting varying local pollution sources and meteorological factors. (d) In countries with marked declines in PM2.5 concentration, we observe somewhat heterogeneous trends in the PM2.5 changes across different temperature conditions (e.g., PM2.5 concentrations declined more during cold extremes in India relative to other temperature conditions), suggesting that changes in PM2.5 sources may shift the dynamics of the compound events. (e) Pakistan, India, and China have the highest population‐weighted mean exposure days to compound events, and high population density amplifies the absolute exposure burden in countries such as India and China.

Mechanisms behind the observed compound temperature–PM2.5 events likely differ between cold and hot conditions. Under cold conditions, elevated PM2.5 concentrations may be driven by increased emissions from heating (Liu et al., 2016; H. Wu et al., 2015) and combustion sources (Yin et al., 2024), coupled with meteorological factors such as shallow boundary layers (Yan et al., 2022), and enhanced atmospheric stability (Green et al., 2015), both of which hinder pollutant dispersion and favor accumulation. These factors contribute to the higher frequency and increasing trend of cold + PM2.5 events. In contrast, under hot conditions, PM2.5 levels may rise due to stagnant weather and enhanced photochemical activity. High temperatures can increase the emissions and atmospheric reactivity of VOCs (W. Wu et al., 2024), leading to the formation of secondary organic aerosols and thereby elevating PM2.5 concentrations (Wei et al., 2022). Additionally, extreme heat can increase wildfire risk (P. T. Brown et al., 2023), which serve as a major source of PM2.5 during hot periods in fire‐prone regions (Burke et al., 2023). Further research is needed to disentangle the relative contributions of emissions, meteorology, and atmospheric chemistry in shaping these compound events under different thermal regimes.

Previous studies defined compound events based on simultaneous exceedance of temperature and pollutant percentile thresholds (Huang et al., 2025; Nduka et al., 2022; Rosenthal et al., 2022), while others adopted absolute thresholds such as specific temperature cutoffs combined with air quality guideline values (Weiss, 2025). Among existing studies, Huang et al. (2025) represents the most comprehensive and closely related work to our analysis, providing a global assessment of hot‐and‐polluted episodes from 1990 to 2019 using MERRA‐2 PM2.5 data and ERA5 temperature fields at 0.5° resolution. Their findings of increasing HPE frequency globally and identification of India and China as the most vulnerable countries provide important foundations for understanding compound event dynamics. Our study complements their work by extending temporal coverage to 2023, employing ground‐based observations and MERRA‐2 data set, and including cold + PM2.5 events alongside hot + PM2.5 combinations. The declining trends observed in our study for certain regions likely reflect recent air pollution control policies that postdate much of their study period. Our spatial exposure analysis corroborates their health estimates, with India and China identified as the highest‐burden countries in both studies.

Our research addresses critical knowledge gaps by providing a multi‐country level assessment of temperature‐PM2.5 compound events across 43 countries over 2003–2023. Our historical analysis also sheds light on the different mechanisms and drivers for cold + PM2.5 and heat + PM2.5 events across different regions, with important implications for projecting future environmental health risks and developing evidence‐based adaptation strategies. Our global assessment of the compound temperature‐PM2.5 events can also support the development of exposure data sets of these compound events and empirically assess their health impacts.

Our research has several limitations which point to important areas for future work. The uneven distribution of air quality monitoring stations may introduce representation biases, particularly in regions with sparse observational coverage. To address this limitation, we incorporated reanalysis‐based PM2.5 data (MERRA‐2), which provides spatially continuous coverage and helps reduce sampling biases associated with uneven monitoring networks. However, reanalysis products are subject to uncertainties arising from model assumptions and data assimilation processes, which may bias PM2.5 estimates in certain regions. In addition to the co‐occurrence approach used in this study, compound events can also be defined based on statistical dependence between variables or spatially compounding events (Leonard et al., 2014). We also acknowledge that the use of ERA5 reanalysis data may introduce uncertainty in measuring extreme temperature, as these temperature fields are derived from model‐based assimilation systems and may not fully capture local‐scale variability.

Despite the overall decline in PM2.5, we find that the compound extreme temperature and PM2.5 events remain an important issue in many countries around the world. The trends in cold + PM2.5 and heat + PM2.5 events highlight critical policy directions for compound risk management. Low temperatures pose greater health risks in cold regions like the EU, while high temperatures are more harmful in hot regions like India (S. Wang et al., 2025; Y. Wang et al., 2025). For example, improving heating efficiency in the EU can reduce PM2.5 emissions during cold periods, lowering winter health risks. In India, a combined heat and PM2.5 warning system can help limit outdoor exposure during summer, protecting vulnerable groups. Given the temperature‐dependent nature of PM2.5, countries that experience a higher frequency of compound temperature‐PM2.5 pollution events, should further strengthen emission regulations to protect public health.
