Introduction

We consider the following
N
-component system

-u^{\prime\prime}_{i}-2(\sum_{j=1}^{N}u^{2}_{j})u_{i}=\mu_{i}u_{i}\ \,\ \hbox{in }\ {\mathbb{R}},\ \ i=1,2,\cdots,N,

(1.1)

where
N\geq 2
,
\mu_{1}\leq\mu_{2}\leq\cdots\leq\mu_{N}<0
, and

u_{i}\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
. The systems of coupled nonlinear Schrödinger equations arise in several
physical and mathematical contexts, including multi-component
Bose–Einstein condensates, nonlinear optics, and effective models of
many-particle quantum systems, see e.g.

[
16
,
20
,
21
,
27
]
. In such models, different component
interacts with each other through a common nonlinear density, which often
create the complicated structures absent in a single scalar equation. In the
elliptic PDE theory, much attention has been devoted to the existence, uniqueness
multiplicity and other qualitative properties of standing waves and
normalized solutions for these systems, see

[
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
,
18
,
25
,
26
]
and the references therein. These works also provide
generally variational backgrounds of the coupled systems. Instead of studying a
particular state, in this paper we aim at a complete classification on all finite-energy
solutions of the one-dimensional system (
1.1
).

A more specific motivation of studying (
1.1
) comes from the nonlinear
Schrödinger equations of orthonormal functions, which are naturally related
to the effective descriptions of fermionic systems and Lieb–Thirring
inequalities (cf.
[
7
,
8
,
10
]
). See also
[
14
,
17
]
for the classical
Lieb–Thirring (L-T) inequality. The finite-rank version of L-T inequality
is concerned with the estimates of the form

\sum_{j=1}^{N}|\lambda_{j}(-\Delta-V)|^{\gamma}\leq L_{\gamma,d}^{(N)}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}V_{+}^{\gamma+d/2}\,dx,

(1.2)

where
\lambda_{j}(-\Delta-V)
denotes the min-max levels of the
Schrödinger operator
-\Delta-V
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. The optimizer problem of this
finite-rank inequality was studied in details by Frank-Gontier-Lewin

[
7
]
. In particular, Frank-Gontier-Lewin showed in
[
7
]
that the optimizers of (
1.2
) satisfy
an Euler-Lagrange equation, which is a system of coupled nonlinear
Schrödinger equations.

For the particular case where
d=1
and the
exponent
\gamma=3/2
, the optimizer of (
1.2
) satisfies the cubic system (
1.1
), after a simple normalization
of the eigenfunctions, and the corresponding finite-rank optimizing potential is precisely the
KdV
N
-soliton potential, which is reflectionless (cf.
[
7
]
).
Thus, a classification on the solutions for (
1.1
) gives an
eigenfunction-level description of the bound-state profiles associated with
the soliton potential. This solitonic point of view is already visible in the low-dimensional
classifications. More precisely, when
N=2
, the classification on the solutions of (
1.1
) can be
expressed (cf.
[
8
]
) through explicit formulas. When
N=3
, Guo-Luo-Wei

[
11
]
recently obtained the complete classification and nondegeneracy of solutions for (
1.1
).
We remark that the above mentioned classifications in
[
8
,
11
]
make full use of Hirota-type bilinear
methods, together with algebraic constants of
motion. These arguments also lead to some conjectures in
[
11
]
on the structure of
solutions for (
1.1
) in the general case
N>3
.
However, it should be emphasized that it is nontrivial to extend the cases
N=2,3
to the general case
N\geq 2
. Actually, one can note that the proofs for the cases
N=2,3
heavily rely on the identities whose explicit
forms become increasingly complicated as the number
N
increases.
In particular, a direct use of the conserved quantities and a componentwise
Hirota expansion result in the more involved analysis of algebraic structures for (
1.1
).
Thus, even though the formulas suggested by the cases
N=2,3
exhibit a clear determinant pattern, a uniform verification and a complete classification for the general case
N\geq 2
require a more systematic approach.

The main purpose of the present paper is to provide such a uniform approach to the complete classification and nondegeneracy of solutions for (
1.1
). We shall prove that
all
H^{1}(\mathbb{R})^{N}
solutions of (
1.1
) can be completely
classified for arbitrary
N\geq 2
. In particular, when the spectral parameters
\mu_{1}<\mu_{2}<\cdots<\mu_{N}<0
are
pairwise distinct, each solution with nontrivial components is shown to be
given by an explicit determinant formula. When some spectral parameters of
\mu_{1}\leq\mu_{2}\leq\cdots\leq\mu_{N}<0

coincide with each other, it however does not produce genuinely new scalar profiles, in the sense that the system just reduces to the distinct-parameter case by grouping equal spectral values, and the
only additional freedom is the natural rotation inside each spectral block.
On the other hand, we shall further prove the nondegeneracy of the classified solutions for (
1.1
), and their exact

L^{2}
-mass identities are also derived in terms of
\mu_{1}\leq\mu_{2}\leq\cdots\leq\mu_{N}<0
. In particular, our results settle some conjectures of
[
8
,
11
]
on the complete classification and nondegeneracy of solutions for (
1.1
) in the general case
N\geq 2
.

1.1
Main results

In this subsection, we shall introduce the main results of the present paper. We begin with the case of pairwise distinct spectral parameters, where the
full determinant structure is already visible. Since identically zero
components can be essentially removed, it is enough to state the following theorem for
solutions whose components are all nontrivial.

Theorem 1.1
.

For
\mu_{1}<\mu_{2}<\cdots<\mu_{N}<0
, suppose
u=(u_{1},u_{2},\cdots,u_{N})
is a solution of (
1.1
) in
(H^{1}({\mathbb{R}})\setminus\{0\})^{N}
. Then there exists a vector
a=(a_{1},a_{2},\cdots,a_{N})\in({\mathbb{R}}\setminus\{0\})^{N}
such that
u
can be expressed exactly as

u_{i}(x)=\frac{a_{i}e^{\eta_{i}x}\det(I+B^{(i)}M(x))}{\det(I+M(x))},\ \ i=1,2,\cdots,N,

(1.3)

where

M(x):=\bigl(m_{jk}(x)\bigr)_{1\leq j,k\leq N},\ \ m_{jk}(x):=\frac{a_{j}a_{k}}{2\sqrt{\eta_{j}\eta_{k}}(\eta_{j}+\eta_{k})}e^{(\eta_{j}+\eta_{k})x},

(1.4)

and for each
i=1,2,\cdots,N
,
\eta_{i}=\sqrt{-\mu_{i}}>0
, and

B^{(i)}:=\operatorname{diag}\Big(\frac{\eta_{i}-\eta_{1}}{\eta_{i}+\eta_{1}},\frac{\eta_{i}-\eta_{2}}{\eta_{i}+\eta_{2}},\dots,\frac{\eta_{i}-\eta_{N}}{\eta_{i}+\eta_{N}}\Big).

(1.5)

We remark that the parameters
a_{1},\cdots,a_{N}
of Theorem
1.1
are not
auxiliary constants introduced by the determinant ansatz. They are precisely
the leading coefficients of the solution
u
at
-\infty
. Indeed, since

M(x)\to 0
as
x\to-\infty
, the formula (
1.3
) gives that

u_{i}(x)=a_{i}e^{\eta_{i}x}+o(e^{\eta_{i}x})\ \ \mbox{as}\ \ x\to-\infty,\ \ i=1,\cdots,N.

Thus, Theorem
1.1
gives a one-to-one parametrization of all
solutions with prescribed nonzero left asymptotic data. Moreover, translations and independent sign changes of Theorem
1.1
are encoded directly in
the parameters. If
U^{a}=(U_{1}^{a},\cdots,U_{N}^{a})
denotes the solution corresponding to some vector
a
, then

U^{a}(x+t)=U^{a(t)}(x),\ \ a_{i}(t)=a_{i}e^{\eta_{i}t},

and for
\sigma_{i}\in\{\pm 1\}
,

U^{(\sigma_{1}a_{1},\cdots,\sigma_{N}a_{N})}_{i}=\sigma_{i}U^{a}_{i}.

Consequently, the determinant formula of Theorem
1.1
describes the full solution manifold of (
1.1
),
including its natural translation and sign symmetries.

Denote

V_{pot}^{a}(x):=2\sum_{j=1}^{N}(U_{j}^{a}(x))^{2},\ \ H_{a}:=-\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}-V_{pot}^{a}(x)\ \ \,\mbox{in}\,\ L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}).

Then each component
U_{i}^{a}
of the solution
U^{a}=(U_{1}^{a},\cdots,U_{N}^{a})
for (
1.1
) is an eigenfunction of
H_{a}
with the
eigenvalue
\mu_{i}
:

H_{a}U_{i}^{a}=\mu_{i}U_{i}^{a}\ \,\ \mbox{in}\ \ {\mathbb{R}},\ \ i=1,\cdots,N.

In particular, when

\mu_{1}<\mu_{2}<\cdots<\mu_{N}<0,

the determinant formula shows that
U_{1}^{a}
has a fixed sign on

\mathbb{R}
. Hence,
U_{1}^{a}
is a ground state of
H_{a}
, and
\mu_{1}
is the
first eigenvalue of
H_{a}
.
Since
H_{a}
is self-adjoint, we note that the eigenfunctions corresponding to
distinct eigenvalues are orthogonal in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, which implies that

\int_{\mathbb{R}}U_{i}^{a}U_{j}^{a}\,dx=0,\ \ i\neq j.

The exact
L^{2}-
mass identity of Theorem
1.5
below further gives that

\|U_{i}^{a}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}=2\eta_{i}=\sqrt{-\mu_{i}}>0,\ \ i=1,\cdots,N.

Thus, we have

\sum_{i=1}^{N}|\mu_{i}|^{1/2}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\eta_{i}=\frac{1}{4}\int_{\mathbb{R}}V_{pot}^{a}(x)\,dx.

This spectral viewpoint explains the relevance of the classification for (
1.1
) to
the finite-rank Lieb-Thirring inequality studied in
Frank-Gontier-Lewin
[
8
]
.

We now sketch the proof of Theorem
1.1
.
For any
N
, the main point of proving Theorem
1.1
is to verify the determinant ansatz
without expanding it one component by one component. By a determinant-ratio
identity, the formula of Theorem
1.1
can be rewritten as

U_{i}^{a}=\sqrt{2\eta_{i}}\,q_{i},\ \ q=(q_{1},\cdots,q_{N})=(I+M)^{-1}v,

where
v
is a suitable vector. The verification is then reduced to the claim
that
q
satisfies a closed second-order differential system. This can
be checked uniformly in
N
by exploiting the special Cauchy-type
structure of
M(x)
in (
1.4
).

To finish the proof of Theorem
1.1
, it then remains to prove that no other
H^{1}
-solutions exist. For an arbitrary
solution
u
, each component equation is an
L^{1}
-perturbation of

y^{\prime\prime}-\eta_{i}^{2}y=0
near
-\infty
. The
H^{1}
-condition excludes the
growing mode
e^{-\eta_{i}x}
as
x\to-\infty
, and hence it determines a unique vector

a=(a_{1},\cdots,a_{N})
from the asymptotic behavior

u_{i}(x)=a_{i}e^{\eta_{i}x}+o(e^{\eta_{i}x})\ \ \mbox{as}\ \ x\to-\infty.

Comparing
u
with the determinant solution
U^{a}
associated with the
same asymptotic data, an asymptotic uniqueness principle then gives

u\equiv U^{a}
on a left half-line, and the usual uniqueness theorem of
ODE systems then yields
u\equiv U^{a}
on
\mathbb{R}
. We refer to Sections 2 and 3 for the detailed proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Based on the classification of Theorem
1.1
, we next describe the linearized
structure of solutions for (
1.1
).
For any fixed vector

a=(a_{1},\cdots,a_{N})\in(\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\})^{N}
, let

U^{a}=(U_{1}^{a},\cdots,U_{N}^{a})

(1.6)

be the determinant solution given in Theorem
1.1
. Direct computations then yield that

U_{i}^{a}(x)=a_{i}e^{\eta_{i}x}+o(e^{\eta_{i}x})\ \ \mbox{as}\ \ x\to-\infty.

The linearized operator at
U^{a}
is defined as

(\mathcal{L}_{U^{a}}\phi)_{i}:=-\phi_{i}^{\prime\prime}-\mu_{i}\phi_{i}-2\left(\sum_{j=1}^{N}(U_{j}^{a})^{2}\right)\phi_{i}-4U_{i}^{a}\sum_{j=1}^{N}U_{j}^{a}\phi_{j},\ \ i=1,\cdots,N.

(1.7)

The natural kernel directions of (
1.7
) are obtained by differentiating
the family with respect to the parameters
a_{1},\cdots,a_{N}
, and the
following nondegeneracy theorem shows that these are the only
H^{1}
-kernel directions.

Theorem 1.2
.

For
\mu_{1}<\mu_{2}<\cdots<\mu_{N}<0
, let
U^{a}
be the
solution given by Theorem
1.1
for some vector

a=(a_{1},\cdots,a_{N})\in(\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\})^{N}
.
Then we have
\dim\ker_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R})^{N}}\mathcal{L}_{U^{a}}=N
, and

\ker_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R})^{N}}\mathcal{L}_{U^{a}}=\operatorname{span}\left\{\partial_{a_{1}}U^{a},\cdots,\partial_{a_{N}}U^{a}\right\},

where
\mathcal{L}_{U^{a}}
is defined by (
1.7
).

We now pass from the case of pairwise distinct spectral parameters to the general case
of repeated spectral values.
Roughly speaking, we can illustrate that these repeated parameters do not however create new scalar profiles. More precisely, we shall prove that after grouping
equal spectral values, all components in the same spectral block are
multiples of one common profile, with only the natural rotational freedom
inside that block.
Let

\mu_{1}<\mu_{2}<\cdots<\mu_{K}<0

be the distinct spectral values, which have their multiplicities

k_{1},\cdots,k_{K}
, so that
k_{1}+\cdots+k_{K}=N
.
Set

N_{\alpha}:=k_{1}+\cdots+k_{\alpha},\ \ N_{0}:=0,\ \ I_{\alpha}:=\{N_{\alpha-1}+1,\cdots,N_{\alpha}\}.

(1.8)

Let
\nu_{1},\cdots,\nu_{N}
be the full list of the spectral parameters counted with their
multiplicities, namely

\nu_{i}=\mu_{\alpha},\ \ i\in I_{\alpha},\quad\alpha=1,\cdots,K.

(1.9)

For the reduced distinct-root system

V_{\alpha}^{\prime\prime}+2\left(\sum_{\beta=1}^{K}V_{\beta}^{2}\right)V_{\alpha}+\mu_{\alpha}V_{\alpha}=0,\ \ \alpha=1,\cdots,K,

(1.10)

let

V^{a}=(V_{1}^{a},\cdots,V_{K}^{a}),\ \ a=(a_{1},\cdots,a_{K})\in(\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\})^{K},

be the determinant family given by Theorem
1.1
.

For

n^{(\alpha)}=(n_{i}^{(\alpha)})_{i\in I_{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{S}^{k_{\alpha}-1},\ \ \alpha=1,\cdots,K,

namely,

\sum_{i\in I_{\alpha}}(n_{i}^{(\alpha)})^{2}=1,

define

U_{i}^{a,n}(x)=n_{i}^{(\alpha)}V_{\alpha}^{a}(x),\ \ i\in I_{\alpha},\ \ \alpha=1,\cdots,K.

(1.11)

We denote the extended family by

\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{multi}}:=\left\{U^{a,n}\text{ defined by }\eqref{eq:multi-lift}:\,a\in(\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\})^{K},\ n^{(\alpha)}\in\mathbb{S}^{k_{\alpha}-1}\right\}.

(1.12)

We shall derive the following property.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
I_{\alpha}
and
\nu_{1},\cdots,\nu_{N}
be defined by (
1.8
) and (
1.9
), respectively, and assume
u=(u_{1},\cdots,u_{N})\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R})^{N}

is a solution of

u_{i}^{\prime\prime}+2\left(\sum_{j=1}^{N}u_{j}^{2}\right)u_{i}+\nu_{i}u_{i}=0\ \ in\,\ {\mathbb{R}},\ \ i=1,\cdots,N.

(1.13)

If
\sum_{i\in I_{\alpha}}u_{i}^{2}\not\equiv 0
holds for every

\alpha=1,\cdots,K
, then
u\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{multi}}
, where

\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{multi}}
is given by (
1.12
).

Remark 1.1
.

One can note that Theorems
1.1
and

1.3
give a complete classification
on
H^{1}(\mathbb{R})^{N}
solutions of (
1.1
) for any

N\geq 2
. In particular, in the case of pairwise distinct spectral
parameters, Theorem
1.1
proves the explicit formula conjectured in

[
11
, Conjecture 5.1]
.

We next describe the nondegeneracy of the extended family

\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{multi}}
for the general case
\nu_{1}\leq\nu_{2}\leq\cdots\leq\nu_{N}<0
. Let

U^{a,n}\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{multi}}

be given by (
1.11
), namely

U^{a,n}_{i}(x)=n_{i}^{(\alpha)}V_{\alpha}^{a}(x),\ \ i\in I_{\alpha},\quad\alpha=1,\cdots,K.

(1.14)

Since
n^{(\alpha)}\in\mathbb{S}^{k_{\alpha}-1}
, we have

S^{a}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{N}(U^{a,n}_{\ell})^{2}=\sum_{\beta=1}^{K}(V_{\beta}^{a})^{2}.

(1.15)

The linearized operator at
U^{a,n}
is defined by

(\mathcal{L}_{U^{a,n}}\phi)_{i}:=-\phi_{i}^{\prime\prime}-\nu_{i}\phi_{i}-2S^{a}\phi_{i}-4U^{a,n}_{i}\sum_{\ell=1}^{N}U^{a,n}_{\ell}\phi_{\ell}\ \ \,\mbox{in}\,\ {\mathbb{R}},\ \ i=1,\cdots,N.

(1.16)

In view of (
1.9
), this can equivalently be written as

(\mathcal{L}_{U^{a,n}}\phi)_{i}=-\phi_{i}^{\prime\prime}-\mu_{\alpha}\phi_{i}-2S^{a}\phi_{i}-4U^{a,n}_{i}\sum_{\ell=1}^{N}U^{a,n}_{\ell}\phi_{\ell}\ \ \,\mbox{in}\,\ {\mathbb{R}},\ \ i\in I_{\alpha}.

(1.17)

The linearized operator at the reduced solution
V^{a}
is defined by

(\mathcal{L}_{V^{a}}p)_{\alpha}:=-p_{\alpha}^{\prime\prime}-\mu_{\alpha}p_{\alpha}-2S^{a}p_{\alpha}-4V_{\alpha}^{a}\sum_{\beta=1}^{K}V_{\beta}^{a}p_{\beta}\ \ \,\mbox{in}\,\ {\mathbb{R}},\ \ \alpha=1,\cdots,K.

(1.18)

Applying Theorem
1.2

to the reduced distinct-root system (
1.10
), we have

\ker_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R})^{K}}\mathcal{L}_{V^{a}}=\operatorname{span}\left\{\partial_{a_{1}}V^{a},\cdots,\partial_{a_{K}}V^{a}\right\}.

(1.19)

The nondegeneracy in the repeated-parameter case has two types
of kernel directions. The first type comes from changing the reduced
parameters
a_{\gamma}
. The second type comes from rotating the unit
vectors
n^{(\alpha)}
inside the spectral blocks.
The tangent space
T_{U^{a,n}}\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{multi}}
is defined as the span of the
following directions. Firstly, set for
\gamma=1,\cdots,K
,

\Phi_{i}^{(\gamma)}=n_{i}^{(\alpha)}\partial_{a_{\gamma}}V_{\alpha}^{a},\ \ i\in I_{\alpha},\quad\alpha=1,\cdots,K.

(1.20)

Secondly, for each
\alpha=1,\cdots,K
and each tangent vector

\xi
to the unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{k_{\alpha}-1}
at
n^{(\alpha)}
, namely

\xi=(\xi_{i})_{i\in I_{\alpha}}\in T_{n^{(\alpha)}}\mathbb{S}^{k_{\alpha}-1}=\left\{\zeta\in\mathbb{R}^{k_{\alpha}}:\quad\zeta\cdot n^{(\alpha)}=0\right\},

set

\Psi_{i}^{(\alpha,\xi)}=\begin{cases}\xi_{i}V_{\alpha}^{a},&i\in I_{\alpha},\\
0,&i\notin I_{\alpha}.\end{cases}

(1.21)

Thus

T_{U^{a,n}}\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{multi}}=\operatorname{span}\left\{\Phi^{(\gamma)},\ \Psi^{(\alpha,\xi)}:\gamma=1,\cdots,K,\ \alpha=1,\cdots,K,\ \xi\cdot n^{(\alpha)}=0\right\}.

(1.22)

Together with Theorem
1.2
, the following theorem addresses the nondegeneracy for the general case
\nu_{1}\leq\cdots\leq\nu_{N}<0
.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
U^{a,n}\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{multi}}
and
\mathcal{L}_{U^{a,n}}
be given by
(
1.14
) and
(
1.16
), respectively.
Then we have

\dim\ker_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R})^{N}}\mathcal{L}_{U^{a,n}}=N
and

\ker_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R})^{N}}\mathcal{L}_{U^{a,n}}=T_{U^{a,n}}\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{multi}},

where
T_{U^{a,n}}\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{multi}}
is given by (
1.22
).

We finally derive the following exact
L^{2}
-mass identity of solutions for (
1.1
), whose proof is based on an algebraic identity generated by the constants of motion, rather than on the explicit determinant formula of solutions for (
1.1
).

Theorem 1.5
.

Under the same assumptions of
Theorem
1.3
, any solution
u=(u_{1},\cdots,u_{N})
of (
1.1
) satisfies for every

\alpha=1,\cdots,K
,

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\sum_{i\in I_{\alpha}}u_{i}^{2}(x)\,dx=2\sqrt{|\mu_{\alpha}|}>0,

(1.23)

where the set
I_{\alpha}
is defined by (
1.8
).

The proof of Theorem
1.5
yields the
following pointwise algebraic identity: Under the same assumptions of
Theorem
1.3
, we have for every

\alpha=1,\cdots,K
,

\sum_{j\in I_{\alpha}}\left[(u_{j}^{\prime})^{2}+\left(\sum_{l=1}^{N}u_{l}^{2}+\mu_{\alpha}\right)u_{j}^{2}\right]+\sum_{\beta\neq\alpha}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\in I_{\alpha}\\
l\in I_{\beta}\end{subarray}}\frac{(u_{j}^{\prime}u_{l}-u_{j}u_{l}^{\prime})^{2}}{\mu_{\beta}-\mu_{\alpha}}=0.

This identity is derived from the spectral polynomial identity of
Lemma
5.2
in Section 5.

Remark 1.2
.

Theorem
1.5
also gives a simple description of the
componentwise normalized solutions for (
1.1
). Indeed, if a solution

u\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{multi}}
of (
1.1
) is written as

u_{i}=n_{i}^{(\alpha)}V_{\alpha}^{a},\ \ i\in I_{\alpha},\quad\alpha=1,\cdots,K,

then Theorem
1.5
gives that

\int_{\mathbb{R}}u_{i}^{2}\,dx=\bigl(n_{i}^{(\alpha)}\bigr)^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}(V_{\alpha}^{a})^{2}\,dx=2\eta_{\alpha}\bigl(n_{i}^{(\alpha)}\bigr)^{2}.

Hence
u=(u_{1},\cdots,u_{N})
satisfies

\int_{\mathbb{R}}u_{i}^{2}\,dx=1,\ \ i=1,\cdots,N,

if and only if

\eta_{\alpha}=\sqrt{|\mu_{\alpha}|}=\frac{k_{\alpha}}{2}\quad\text{and}\quad\bigl(n_{i}^{(\alpha)}\bigr)^{2}=\frac{1}{k_{\alpha}},\ \ i\in I_{\alpha},\quad\alpha=1,\cdots,K.

Remark 1.3
.

As a consequence of Theorems
1.3
and

1.5
, the system (
1.1
) does not admits any orthonormal solution, whose components
are pairwise orthogonal and have the same nonzero
L^{2}
-norm. This addresses the nonexistence of orthonormal solutions for (
1.1
), which was conjectured by Frank-Gontier-Lewin
[
8
, page 1793]
and
Guo-Luo-Wei
[
11
, Conjecture 5.2]
for the general case
N\geq 2
.

This paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we construct a determinant family and verify the equations in the distinct-parameter case. Section 3 is devoted to the proofs of Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
on the classification and nondegeneracy of solutions for (
1.1
) in the distinct-parameter case. In Section 4, we prove Theorems
1.3
and
1.4
on the classification and nondegeneracy results in the case of repeated spectral parameters. Theorem
1.5
is finally proved in Section 5 by deriving an algebraic identity and the exact
L^{2}
-mass formulas.

2
Construction of Determinant Solutions

In this section, we verify that the explicit determinant formula stated in
Theorem
1.1
satisfies (
1.1
). Our argument is divided into two
parts. In Subsection 2.1, we rewrite the explicit formula (
1.3
) for

u=(u_{1},u_{2},\cdots,u_{N})
in terms of an auxiliary vector
q
. In
Subsection 2.2, we use this vector formulation to verify the differential
equation. The key point is that the auxiliary vector
q
satisfies a closed second-order
system, which is equivalent to (
1.1
) after a simple rescaling.

We now introduce the notation used in the determinant formula. Throughout this section, we assume that

\mu_{1}<\mu_{2}<\cdots<\mu_{N}<0\quad\hbox{and}\quad\eta_{i}=\sqrt{-\mu_{i}},\quad i=1,2,\cdots,N.

Fixing

a=(a_{1},\cdots,a_{N})\in(\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\})^{N},

we recall that

m_{jk}(x)=\frac{a_{j}a_{k}}{2\sqrt{\eta_{j}\eta_{k}}(\eta_{j}+\eta_{k})}e^{(\eta_{j}+\eta_{k})x},\ \ M(x)=(m_{jk}(x))_{1\leq j,k\leq N},

and

B^{(i)}=\operatorname{diag}\left(\frac{\eta_{i}-\eta_{1}}{\eta_{i}+\eta_{1}},\cdots,\frac{\eta_{i}-\eta_{N}}{\eta_{i}+\eta_{N}}\right).

Define

f(x):=\det\bigl(I+M(x)\bigr)

(2.1)

and

g_{i}(x):=a_{i}e^{\eta_{i}x}\det\bigl(I+B^{(i)}M(x)\bigr),\ \ i=1,\cdots,N,

(2.2)

such that

u_{i}(x)=\frac{g_{i}}{f}\quad\hbox{for}\,\ i=1,2,\cdots,N.

We shall derive the following precise verification.

Proposition 2.1
.

For

\mu_{1}<\mu_{2}<\cdots<\mu_{N}<0
and
\eta_{i}=\sqrt{-\mu_{i}}
, let
a=(a_{1},\cdots,a_{N})\in({\mathbb{R}}\setminus\{0\})^{N}
, and define
f
and
g_{i}

by (
2.1
) and (
2.2
), respectively. Then

u_{i}(x):=\frac{g_{i}(x)}{f(x)},\ \ i=1,\cdots,N,

defines a classical solution
u=(u_{1},\cdots,u_{N})\in\big(H^{1}({\mathbb{R}})\setminus\{0\}\big)^{N}
of (
1.1
).

The proof of Proposition
2.1
is divided into two steps. We first establish in Subsection 2.1 a determinant
ratio identity which rewrites
g_{i}/f
in terms of
q
. We then verify in Subsection 2.2
that this vector
q
satisfies the closed differential system equivalent
to (
1.1
).

2.1
A determinant ratio formula

We begin with the introduction of an auxiliary vector.
Set

v(x):=\left(v_{1}(x),\cdots,v_{N}(x)\right)^{T},\ \ v_{j}(x):=\frac{a_{j}}{\sqrt{2\eta_{j}}}e^{\eta_{j}x}.

(2.3)

Then
M(x)=V(x)CV(x)
, where

V(x):=\operatorname{diag}(v_{1}(x),\cdots,v_{N}(x)),\ \ C:=\left(\frac{1}{\eta_{j}+\eta_{k}}\right)_{1\leq j,k\leq N}.

(2.4)

We first observe that
I+M(x)
is invertible for every
x\in\mathbb{R}
.
Indeed,
note that

\frac{1}{\eta_{j}+\eta_{k}}=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\eta_{j}t}e^{-\eta_{k}t}\,dt,

and thus
C
is the Gram matrix of the functions

e^{-\eta_{j}t}
in
L^{2}(0,\infty)
. Since the numbers

\eta_{j}
are distinct, these functions are linearly independent, and
therefore
C
is positive definite. Since
V(x)
is invertible,
M(x)=V(x)CV(x)
is positive definite by
congruence. In particular,
I+M(x)
is positive definite and hence
invertible for every
x\in\mathbb{R}
.

Since
I+M(x)
is invertible, define

q(x):=(I+M(x))^{-1}v(x),

(2.5)

We now prove the following lemma.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
f(x)
,
g_{i}(x)
and
q(x)
be defined by
(
2.1
), (
2.2
), and (
2.5
). Set
u_{i}(x):=\frac{g_{i}(x)}{f(x)}
, then for every

i=1,\cdots,N
,

u_{i}(x)=\sqrt{2\eta_{i}}\,q_{i}(x),

where
q_{i}
denotes the
i
-th component of
q
.

Proof.

For any
i\in\{1,\cdots,N\}
, we prove the identity by Cramer’s rule.
For simplicity, we suppress the variable
x
in the following computation.
Set

A:=I+M.

Let
A_{i}
be the matrix obtained from
A
by replacing its
i
-th
column by
v
. Since
q=A^{-1}v
, Cramer’s rule gives that

q_{i}=\frac{\det A_{i}}{\det A}.

(2.6)

Thus, it remains to identify the numerator
\det A_{i}
. We claim that

\det A_{i}=v_{i}\det\bigl(I+B^{(i)}M\bigr).

(2.7)

Once this is proved, the desired conclusion follows immediately from the facts that

f=\det A
and

a_{i}e^{\eta_{i}x}=\sqrt{2\eta_{i}}\,v_{i}(x).

We now prove (
2.7
). Write

\beta_{j}^{(i)}:=\frac{\eta_{i}-\eta_{j}}{\eta_{i}+\eta_{j}},\ \ B^{(i)}=\operatorname{diag}\bigl(\beta_{1}^{(i)},\cdots,\beta_{N}^{(i)}\bigr).

For any
N\times N
matrix
K
and any subset

I\subset\{1,\cdots,N\}
, let
K[I]
denote the principal submatrix of

K
indexed by
I
, where the elements of
I
are always taken in their
natural increasing order. We also use the convention

\det K[\emptyset]=1
. Then we have the standard principal minor expansion (see, e.g.,
[
13
, (1.2.13)]
):

\det(I+K)=\sum_{I\subset\{1,\cdots,N\}}\det K[I].

Applying this identity to
K=B^{(i)}M
, and using the fact that

B^{(i)}
is diagonal, we obtain that

\det\bigl(I+B^{(i)}M\bigr)=\sum_{I\subset\{1,\cdots,N\}\setminus\{i\}}\left(\prod_{j\in I}\beta_{j}^{(i)}\right)\det M[I].

(2.8)

Indeed, all terms with
i\in I
vanish because
\beta_{i}^{(i)}=0
.
Since

M_{kj}=\frac{v_{k}v_{j}}{\eta_{k}+\eta_{j}},

we have

M[I]=\operatorname{diag}(v_{j})_{j\in I}\left(\frac{1}{\eta_{k}+\eta_{j}}\right)_{k,j\in I}\operatorname{diag}(v_{j})_{j\in I}.

Hence

\det M[I]=\left(\prod_{j\in I}v_{j}^{2}\right)\det\left(\frac{1}{\eta_{k}+\eta_{j}}\right)_{k,j\in I}.

(2.9)

Combining (
2.8
) and
(
2.9
), we obtain that

\det\bigl(I+B^{(i)}M\bigr)=\sum_{I\subset\{1,\cdots,N\}\setminus\{i\}}\left(\prod_{j\in I}\beta_{j}^{(i)}\right)\left(\prod_{j\in I}v_{j}^{2}\right)\det\left(\frac{1}{\eta_{k}+\eta_{j}}\right)_{k,j\in I}.

(2.10)

We next expand
\det A_{i}
by the multilinearity of the determinant with
respect to the columns. For each
j\neq i
, the
j
-th column of
A_{i}

is

e_{j}+M_{\cdot j},

where
e_{j}
is the
j
-th column of the identity matrix and

M_{\cdot j}
is the
j
-th column of
M
. The
i
-th column of

A_{i}
is
v
. Thus, in the multilinear expansion,
we choose either
e_{j}
or
M_{\cdot j}
for each
j\neq i
. Denote by

I\subset\{1,\cdots,N\}\setminus\{i\}
the set of indices for which

M_{\cdot j}
is chosen. Then

\det A_{i}=\sum_{I\subset\{1,\cdots,N\}\setminus\{i\}}\Delta_{I},

(2.11)

where
\Delta_{I}
is the determinant of the matrix whose
j
-th column is

M_{\cdot j}
for
j\in I
, is
e_{j}
for

j\notin I\cup\{i\}
, and is
v
for
j=i
.

To compute
\Delta_{I}
, set

S:=I\cup\{i\}.

For
j\notin S
, the
j
-th column is the unit vector
e_{j}
. Hence this column has only one nonzero entry, namely the entry
1
in the
j
-th row. Expanding the determinant successively along these unit columns, we may delete all rows and columns with labels
j\notin S
. Therefore
\Delta_{I}
is equal to the determinant of the matrix obtained by keeping only the rows and columns whose labels belong to
S
. In this reduced matrix, the column with label
i
is
(v_{k})_{k\in S}
, while the column with label
j\in I
is
(M_{kj})_{k\in S}
.

We then move the row and the column with label
i
simultaneously to the
first position. The row permutation and the column permutation have the same
sign, so their signs cancel. Thus the determinant is unchanged. We then obtain that

\Delta_{I}=\det\begin{pmatrix}v_{i}&(M_{ij})_{j\in I}\\
(v_{k})_{k\in I}&(M_{kj})_{k,j\in I}\end{pmatrix}.

(2.12)

Using
M_{kj}=v_{k}v_{j}/(\eta_{k}+\eta_{j})
, we factor
v_{k}
from each row
with label
k\in S
, and
v_{j}
from each column with label
j\in I
.
This gives that

\Delta_{I}=v_{i}\left(\prod_{j\in I}v_{j}^{2}\right)\det\begin{pmatrix}1&\left(\frac{1}{\eta_{i}+\eta_{j}}\right)_{j\in I}\\
1&\left(\frac{1}{\eta_{k}+\eta_{j}}\right)_{k,j\in I}\end{pmatrix}.

(2.13)

In the last determinant, subtract the first row from each of the remaining
rows and then expand along the first column. This gives

\det\begin{pmatrix}1&\left(\frac{1}{\eta_{i}+\eta_{j}}\right)_{j\in I}\\
1&\left(\frac{1}{\eta_{k}+\eta_{j}}\right)_{k,j\in I}\end{pmatrix}=\det\left(\frac{1}{\eta_{k}+\eta_{j}}-\frac{1}{\eta_{i}+\eta_{j}}\right)_{k,j\in I}.

Moreover, the matrix on the right-hand side admits the factorization

\left(\frac{1}{\eta_{k}+\eta_{j}}-\frac{1}{\eta_{i}+\eta_{j}}\right)_{k,j\in I}=\operatorname{diag}(\eta_{i}-\eta_{k})_{k\in I}\left(\frac{1}{\eta_{k}+\eta_{j}}\right)_{k,j\in I}\operatorname{diag}\left(\frac{1}{\eta_{i}+\eta_{j}}\right)_{j\in I}.

We thus conclude from above that

\Delta_{I}=v_{i}\left(\prod_{j\in I}\frac{\eta_{i}-\eta_{j}}{\eta_{i}+\eta_{j}}\right)\left(\prod_{j\in I}v_{j}^{2}\right)\det\left(\frac{1}{\eta_{k}+\eta_{j}}\right)_{k,j\in I}.

(2.14)

Substituting (
2.14
) into (
2.11
), we get that

\det A_{i}=\sum_{I\subset\{1,\cdots,N\}\setminus\{i\}}v_{i}\left(\prod_{j\in I}\beta_{j}^{(i)}\right)\left(\prod_{j\in I}v_{j}^{2}\right)\det\left(\frac{1}{\eta_{k}+\eta_{j}}\right)_{k,j\in I}.

Combining this with (
2.10
) gives
(
2.7
). Finally, since
f=\det A
, it follows from (
2.6
) and
(
2.7
) that

q_{i}(x)=\frac{v_{i}(x)\det(I+B^{(i)}M(x))}{f(x)}.

Since
a_{i}e^{\eta_{i}x}=\sqrt{2\eta_{i}}\,v_{i}(x)
, the desired identity follows.
This completes the proof.
∎

2.2
Verification of the determinant family

In this Subsection we shall complete the proof of Proposition
2.1
.
By Lemma
2.2
, the determinant ratios are reduced to
the vector

q=(I+M)^{-1}v,\ \ u_{i}=\sqrt{2\eta_{i}}\,q_{i}.

It remains to show that
q
satisfies the closed second-order system
equivalent to (
1.1
).

Proof of Proposition
2.1
.

By Lemma
2.2
, the determinant solution satisfies

u_{i}=\sqrt{2\eta_{i}}\,q_{i}.

Thus the proof reduces to deriving a closed equation for
q
.
Let

\Lambda=\operatorname{diag}(\eta_{1},\dots,\eta_{N}).

By (
2.3
) and (
1.4
), we have

m_{jk}(x)=\frac{v_{j}(x)v_{k}(x)}{\eta_{j}+\eta_{k}},

and hence

M^{\prime}(x)=v(x)v(x)^{T},\ \ v^{\prime}(x)=\Lambda v(x).

Recall that

A(x)=I+M(x)\quad\hbox{and}\quad q(x)=A(x)^{-1}v(x).

Set

s:=v^{\top}q,\ \ r:=q^{\top}\Lambda q.

To prove Proposition
2.1
, it suffices to show that

Aq^{\prime\prime}=A(\Lambda^{2}q-4rq)

(2.15)

holds true.
Indeed, since
A=I+M
is invertible, if (
2.15
) holds true, then we have

q^{\prime\prime}=\Lambda^{2}q-4rq.

(2.16)

Since
\mu_{i}=-\eta_{i}^{2}
and

\sum_{j=1}^{N}u_{j}^{2}=2\sum_{j=1}^{N}\eta_{j}q_{j}^{2}=2r,

the equation

q_{i}^{\prime\prime}-\eta_{i}^{2}q_{i}+4rq_{i}=0

is exactly equivalent to (
1.1
).
Thus,
u=(u_{1},u_{2},\cdots,u_{N})=(\sqrt{2\eta_{1}}q_{1},\sqrt{2\eta_{2}}q_{2},\cdots,\sqrt{2\eta_{N}}q_{N})
satisfies (
1.1
).

We next prove (
2.15
) as follows.

Step 1. Computation of
Aq^{\prime\prime}
.

We claim that

Aq^{\prime\prime}=\Lambda^{2}v-s\Lambda v-3rv+\frac{s^{2}}{2}v.

(2.17)

Indeed, since

Aq=v,

we differentiate both sides of the identity to obtain that

Aq^{\prime}+A^{\prime}q=v^{\prime}.

Since
A^{\prime}=vv^{\top}
and
v^{\prime}=\Lambda v
, while

A^{\prime}q=vv^{\top}q=v(v^{\top}q)=(v^{\top}q)v=sv,

we obtain that

Aq^{\prime}=(\Lambda-sI)v.

(2.18)

Differentiating (
2.18
) yields that

Aq^{\prime\prime}+A^{\prime}q^{\prime}=(\Lambda-sI)^{\prime}v+(\Lambda-sI)v^{\prime}=-s^{\prime}v+(\Lambda-sI)\Lambda v.

Since
A^{\prime}=vv^{\top}
, this becomes

Aq^{\prime\prime}+v(v^{\top}q^{\prime})=\Lambda^{2}v-s\Lambda v-s^{\prime}v.

(2.19)

Next, we compute
v^{\top}q^{\prime}
and
s^{\prime}
. Since

v=Aq=(I+M)q=q+Mq,

we have

q^{\top}\Lambda v=q^{\top}\Lambda q+q^{\top}\Lambda Mq=r+q^{\top}\Lambda Mq.

Using
m_{jk}=v_{j}v_{k}/(\eta_{j}+\eta_{k})
and the fact that
the scalar

q^{\top}\Lambda Mq
satisfies

q^{\top}\Lambda Mq=(q^{\top}\Lambda Mq)^{\top}=q^{\top}M\Lambda q,

we get

2q^{\top}\Lambda Mq=q^{\top}(\Lambda M+M\Lambda)q=\sum_{j,k=1}^{N}v_{j}q_{j}v_{k}q_{k}=s^{2}.

Hence

q^{\top}\Lambda v=r+\frac{s^{2}}{2}.

(2.20)

Multiplying (
2.18
) by
q^{\top}
from the left and
using
q^{\top}A=v^{\top}
, we obtain that

v^{\top}q^{\prime}=q^{\top}(\Lambda-sI)v=q^{\top}\Lambda v-s^{2}.

By (
2.20
), this gives that

v^{\top}q^{\prime}=r-\frac{s^{2}}{2}.

(2.21)

Finally, differentiating
s=v^{\top}q
, we have

s^{\prime}=(v^{\top}q)^{\prime}=(v^{\prime})^{\top}q+v^{\top}q^{\prime}=v^{\top}\Lambda q+v^{\top}q^{\prime}=q^{\top}\Lambda v+v^{\top}q^{\prime}.

We get from (
2.20
) and (
2.21
) that

s^{\prime}=2r.

(2.22)

Substituting (
2.22
) and (
2.21
) into
(
2.19
), it yields that

Aq^{\prime\prime}=\Lambda^{2}v-s\Lambda v-s^{\prime}v-v(v^{\top}q^{\prime})=\Lambda^{2}v-s\Lambda v-3rv+\frac{s^{2}}{2}v,

which proves (
2.17
).

Step 2: Computation of
A(\Lambda^{2}q-4rq)
.

We claim that

A(\Lambda^{2}q-4rq)=\Lambda^{2}v-s\Lambda v-3rv+\frac{s^{2}}{2}v.

(2.23)

Indeed, since
Aq=v
,

A(\Lambda^{2}q-4rq)=A\Lambda^{2}q-4rv,

and
A=I+M
, we have

\displaystyle A\Lambda^{2}q=\Lambda^{2}q+M\Lambda^{2}q

\displaystyle=\Lambda^{2}(v-Mq)+M\Lambda^{2}q

\displaystyle=\Lambda^{2}v-(\Lambda^{2}M-M\Lambda^{2})q

For the commutator term, its
i
-th component is

\bigl((\Lambda^{2}M-M\Lambda^{2})q\bigr)_{i}=\sum_{k=1}^{N}(\eta_{i}^{2}-\eta_{k}^{2})\frac{v_{i}v_{k}}{\eta_{i}+\eta_{k}}q_{k}=\sum_{k=1}^{N}(\eta_{i}-\eta_{k})v_{i}v_{k}q_{k}.

Hence

\bigl((\Lambda^{2}M-M\Lambda^{2})q\bigr)_{i}=\eta_{i}s\,v_{i}-v_{i}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\eta_{k}v_{k}q_{k}.

Since

\sum_{k=1}^{N}\eta_{k}v_{k}q_{k}=q^{\top}\Lambda v=r+\frac{s^{2}}{2},

we conclude that

(\Lambda^{2}M-M\Lambda^{2})q=s\Lambda v-\left(r+\frac{s^{2}}{2}\right)v.

Thus,

A\Lambda^{2}q=\Lambda^{2}v-s\Lambda v+\left(r+\frac{s^{2}}{2}\right)v,

and hence (
2.23
) holds true.

We now conclude from (
2.17
) and (
2.23
) that (
2.15
) holds true. This completes the proof.
∎

3
Solutions for the Distinct-Parameter Case

In this section, we prove Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
on the classification and nondegeneracy results
in the case where the spectral parameters are distinct. Our proofs are given by comparing solutions through their leading asymptotic
data at
-\infty
. We first show that every

H^{1}
-solution has a well-defined leading coefficient at
-\infty
.
These coefficients are then used as the parameters of the determinant
solution constructed in Section 2. A left-uniqueness principle finally
shows that two solutions with the same leading left asymptotics must
coincide with each other.

3.1
Left asymptotics and asymptotic uniqueness

In this subsection, we mainly prove the following scalar ODE lemma to identify the leading asymptotic
behavior of each component
u_{i}
of a solution

u=(u_{1},\cdots,u_{N})
for (
1.1
) as
x\to-\infty
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\eta>0
,

q\in C((-\infty,R))\cap L^{1}(-\infty,R)
, and

h\in C((-\infty,R))
. Suppose

y\in H^{1}(-\infty,R)\cap C^{2}(-\infty,R)
solves

y^{\prime\prime}-\eta^{2}y=q(x)y+h(x)\quad\text{on }(-\infty,R),

(3.1)

and assume that there exists
\sigma>0
such that

h(x)=O(e^{(\eta+\sigma)x})\quad\text{as }x\to-\infty.

Then there exists a constant
a\in\mathbb{R}
such that

y(x)=ae^{\eta x}+o(e^{\eta x}),\ \ y^{\prime}(x)=\eta ae^{\eta x}+o(e^{\eta x})\quad\text{as }x\to-\infty.

Moreover, if
h\equiv 0
and
a=0
, then

y\equiv 0\quad\text{on }(-\infty,R).

Lemma
3.1
is applied separately to each component, in order to extract
its leading coefficient at
-\infty
. More precisely, for an

H^{1}
-solution of

y^{\prime\prime}-\eta^{2}y=q(x)y+h(x),\ \ q\in L^{1}(-\infty,R),\ \ h(x)=O(e^{(\eta+\sigma)x})\quad\text{as }\ x\to-\infty,

Lemma
3.1
gives a well-defined leading coefficient
a
in the
asymptotic expansion

y(x)=ae^{\eta x}+o(e^{\eta x}),\ \ y^{\prime}(x)=\eta ae^{\eta x}+o(e^{\eta x})\quad\text{as }\ x\to-\infty.

In the homogeneous case
h\equiv 0
, this coefficient can vanish only
for the trivial solution. This is the basic tool of extracting the
left asymptotic coefficients
a_{i}^{-}
of the components
u_{i}
.

Proof of Lemma
3.1
.

The proof is divided into three steps. Firstly, we construct a fundamental system

\{\varphi,\psi\}
for the homogeneous equation

w^{\prime\prime}-\eta^{2}w=q(x)w,

(3.2)

such that
\varphi
has the decaying asymptotic behavior
e^{\eta x}
, while

\psi
has the growing asymptotic behavior
e^{-\eta x}
as

x\to-\infty
. Secondly, we use variation of constants to write

y=\alpha\varphi+\beta\psi+p,

where
p
is a particular solution produced by
h
. Finally, the decay
assumption on
h
implies that
p
and
p^{\prime}
are both of lower order than

e^{\eta x}
. Therefore, the condition
y\in H^{1}(-\infty,R)
excludes the
growing mode
\psi
and the desired asymptotic estimate is obtained.

Step 1: Construction of two fundamental solutions for the homogeneous equation (
3.2
).

Choose
R_{0}<R
sufficiently negative so that

\frac{1}{2\eta}\|q\|_{L^{1}(-\infty,R_{0})}<1.

(3.3)

We first construct a decaying solution of the homogeneous equation
(
3.2
) in
(-\infty,R_{0})
. To obtain a solution with the leading behavior

e^{\eta x}
as
x\to-\infty
, we write

\varphi(x)=e^{\eta x}u(x).

Then
\varphi
solves (
3.2
) with the leading behavior

e^{\eta x}
as
x\to-\infty
, if

u^{\prime\prime}+2\eta u^{\prime}=q(x)u,\ \ u(x)\to 1,\quad u^{\prime}(x)\to 0\quad\text{as }x\to-\infty.

(3.4)

For a bounded continuous solution
u
,
u
solves (
3.4
) if

u
satisfies the following integral equation:

u(x)=1+\frac{1}{2\eta}\int_{-\infty}^{x}\bigl(1-e^{-2\eta(x-s)}\bigr)q(s)u(s)\,ds,\ \ x<R_{0}.

(3.5)

Indeed, if
u
satisfies (
3.5
), then
qu\in L^{1}(-\infty,R_{0})
, and hence the integral term tends to zero as
x\to-\infty
.
Thus,
u(x)\to 1
as
x\to-\infty
.
Differentiating (
3.5
) gives that

u^{\prime}(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{x}e^{-2\eta(x-s)}q(s)u(s)\,ds,

which implies that
u^{\prime}(x)\to 0
as
x\to-\infty
. Differentiating once more yields that

u^{\prime\prime}=q(x)u-2\eta u^{\prime},

and hence
u^{\prime\prime}+2\eta u^{\prime}=q(x)u
.

We next prove the existence of a solution to the integral equation
(
3.5
). Set
X=C_{b}((-\infty,R_{0}])
, equipped with the supremum norm. The
right-hand side of (
3.5
) defines a map
T:X\to X
.
Moreover, for any
u_{1},u_{2}\in X
,

\|Tu_{1}-Tu_{2}\|_{L^{\infty}(-\infty,R_{0})}\leq\frac{1}{2\eta}\|q\|_{L^{1}(-\infty,R_{0})}\|u_{1}-u_{2}\|_{L^{\infty}(-\infty,R_{0})}.

By (
3.3
),
T
is a contraction, which implies that
(
3.5
) has a unique bounded continuous solution

u\in C_{b}((-\infty,R_{0}])
.
By the argument following (
3.5
), this solution
u
satisfies (
3.4
). Applying the standard one-dimensional regularity of ODE, we have

u\in C^{2}(-\infty,R_{0})
.
Thus,

\varphi=e^{\eta x}u
is a
C^{2}
-solution of the homogeneous equation
(
3.2
) and satisfies

\varphi(x)=e^{\eta x}(1+o(1)),\ \ \varphi^{\prime}(x)=\eta e^{\eta x}(1+o(1))\quad\text{as }x\to-\infty.

(3.6)

In view of (
3.6
), by decreasing
R_{0}
further if necessary,
we may assume that

\varphi(x)\neq 0\quad\text{on }(-\infty,R_{0}).

We next construct the growing homogeneous solution by reduction of order:

\psi(x):=2\eta\,\varphi(x)\int_{x}^{R_{0}}\frac{dt}{\varphi(t)^{2}},\ \ x<R_{0}.

(3.7)

Then
\psi
also solves the homogeneous equation. Moreover,

W(\varphi,\psi):=\varphi\psi^{\prime}-\varphi^{\prime}\psi=-2\eta.

Thus
\varphi
and
\psi
form a fundamental system on

(-\infty,R_{0})
. Note from (
3.6
) that

\frac{1}{\varphi(t)^{2}}=e^{-2\eta t}(1+o(1))\quad\text{as }t\to-\infty.

Hence

\int_{x}^{R_{0}}\frac{dt}{\varphi(t)^{2}}=\frac{1}{2\eta}e^{-2\eta x}(1+o(1))\quad\text{as }x\to-\infty.

It follows from (
3.7
) that

\psi(x)=e^{-\eta x}(1+o(1))\quad\text{as }x\to-\infty.

Differentiating (
3.7
), we get that

\psi^{\prime}=2\eta\varphi^{\prime}\int_{x}^{R_{0}}\frac{dt}{\varphi(t)^{2}}-\frac{2\eta}{\varphi}.

Using (
3.6
) again, we obtain that

\psi(x)=e^{-\eta x}(1+o(1)),\ \ \psi^{\prime}(x)=-\eta e^{-\eta x}(1+o(1))\quad\text{as }x\to-\infty.

(3.8)

Step 2: A variation-of-constants representation for (
3.1
).

We now treat the inhomogeneous term. Define

I_{1}(x):=\int_{-\infty}^{x}\psi(s)h(s)\,ds,\ \ I_{2}(x):=\int_{-\infty}^{x}\varphi(s)h(s)\,ds.

These integrals are well defined. Indeed, by (
3.6
),
(
3.8
), and the assumption on
h
,

\psi(s)h(s)=O(e^{\sigma s}),\ \ \varphi(s)h(s)=O(e^{(2\eta+\sigma)s})\quad\text{as }s\to-\infty.

Set

p(x):=\frac{1}{2\eta}\varphi(x)I_{1}(x)-\frac{1}{2\eta}\psi(x)I_{2}(x).

(3.9)

Since

I_{1}^{\prime}=\psi h,\ \ I_{2}^{\prime}=\varphi h,\ \ W(\varphi,\psi)=-2\eta,

a direct computation gives that

p^{\prime\prime}-(\eta^{2}+q(x))p=h(x).

Thus,
p
is a particular solution of (
3.1
). Hence,

y-p
solves the homogeneous equation, and there exist constants

\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
such that

y(x)=\alpha\varphi(x)+\beta\psi(x)+p(x),\ \ x<R_{0}.

(3.10)

Step 3: The asymptotic estimates for
H^{1}(-\infty,R)
solutions.

The above estimates yield that

I_{1}(x)=O(e^{\sigma x}),\ \ I_{2}(x)=O(e^{(2\eta+\sigma)x})\quad\text{as }x\to-\infty,

which imply that

\varphi(x)I_{1}(x)=O(e^{(\eta+\sigma)x}),\ \ \psi(x)I_{2}(x)=O(e^{(\eta+\sigma)x})\quad\text{as }x\to-\infty.

Consequently,

p(x)=O(e^{(\eta+\sigma)x})=o(e^{\eta x})\quad\text{as }x\to-\infty.

(3.11)

Differentiating (
3.9
), since the two terms involving
h(x)

cancel, we have

p^{\prime}(x)=\frac{1}{2\eta}\varphi^{\prime}(x)I_{1}(x)-\frac{1}{2\eta}\psi^{\prime}(x)I_{2}(x).

By the estimates
of
I_{1},I_{2}
, we get from (
3.6
) and (
3.8
) that

p^{\prime}(x)=O(e^{(\eta+\sigma)x})=o(e^{\eta x})\quad\text{as }x\to-\infty.

(3.12)

Applying (
3.6
), (
3.8
), and
(
3.11
), we get from (
3.10
) that

y(x)=\alpha e^{\eta x}(1+o(1))+\beta e^{-\eta x}(1+o(1))+o(e^{\eta x})\quad\text{as }\ x\to-\infty.

Since
y\in H^{1}(-\infty,R)
, we have
y\in L^{2}(-\infty,R)
.
The function
e^{-\eta x}
is not in
L^{2}(-\infty,R_{0})
, whereas

\varphi\in L^{2}(-\infty,R_{0}),\ \ p=o(e^{\eta x})\quad\text{as }\ x\to-\infty.

Thus
\beta=0
and

y(x)=\alpha e^{\eta x}+o(e^{\eta x})\quad\text{as }\ x\to-\infty.

Applying (
3.10
) with
\beta=0
, we obtain from
(
3.6
) and (
3.12
) that

y^{\prime}(x)=\alpha\varphi^{\prime}(x)+p^{\prime}(x)=\eta\alpha e^{\eta x}+o(e^{\eta x})\quad\text{as }\ x\to-\infty.

Taking
a:=\alpha
, we obtain the desired asymptotics.

It remains to prove the last assertion in the homogeneous case. Assume

h\equiv 0
, so that

I_{1}=I_{2}\equiv 0,\ \ p\equiv 0.

Hence

y(x)=\alpha\varphi(x)+\beta\psi(x),\ \ x<R_{0}.

As before, it follows from
y\in L^{2}(-\infty,R_{0})
that
\beta=0
. Additionally, if

a=0
, then
\alpha=0
, and hence

y\equiv 0\quad\text{on }(-\infty,R_{0}).

Since
y
solves a second-order linear ordinary differential equation with
continuous coefficients, the uniqueness of Cauchy problem implies that

y\equiv 0\quad\text{on }(-\infty,R).

The proof is complete.
∎

Applying the preceding scalar lemma to each component, we obtain the following left asymptotic behavior for
H^{1}(\mathbb{R})^{N}
-solutions of (
1.1
).

Lemma 3.2
.

For
\mu_{i}=-\eta_{i}^{2}<0
,
i=1,\cdots,N
, assume

u=(u_{1},\cdots,u_{N})\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R})^{N}

solves

u_{i}^{\prime\prime}+2\left(\sum_{j=1}^{N}u_{j}^{2}\right)u_{i}=\eta_{i}^{2}u_{i},\ \ i=1,\cdots,N.

Then for each
i=1,\cdots,N
, there exists a constant

a_{i}^{-}\in\mathbb{R}
such that

u_{i}(x)=a_{i}^{-}e^{\eta_{i}x}+o(e^{\eta_{i}x}),\quad u_{i}^{\prime}(x)=\eta_{i}a_{i}^{-}e^{\eta_{i}x}+o(e^{\eta_{i}x})\ \ x\to-\infty.

Moreover, if
u_{i}\not\equiv 0
, then

a_{i}^{-}\neq 0
.

Proof.

Since
u\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R})^{N}
, we have

2\sum_{j=1}^{N}u_{j}^{2}(x)\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
.
For each fixed
i
, the equation of
u_{i}
can be rewritten as

u_{i}^{\prime\prime}-\eta_{i}^{2}u_{i}=q_{i}(x)u_{i},\ \ q_{i}(x):=-2\sum_{j=1}^{N}u_{j}^{2}(x)\in C(\mathbb{R})\cap L^{1}(\mathbb{R}).

By standard one-dimensional regularity of ODE system, we have

u\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R})^{N}
. Applying Lemma
3.1
to
y=u_{i}
,

\eta=\eta_{i}
and
q=q_{i}
, we obtain that

u_{i}(x)=a_{i}^{-}e^{\eta_{i}x}+o(e^{\eta_{i}x}),\quad u_{i}^{\prime}(x)=\eta_{i}a_{i}^{-}e^{\eta_{i}x}+o(e^{\eta_{i}x}),\ \ x\to-\infty.

If
a_{i}^{-}=0
, then the last assertion of
Lemma
3.1
gives that

u_{i}\equiv 0\quad\text{on }(-\infty,R)

holds
for any fixed
R\in\mathbb{R}
. Hence,
u_{i}\equiv 0
on

\mathbb{R}
. Therefore, if
u_{i}\not\equiv 0
, it must have

a_{i}^{-}\neq 0
.
∎

If a
solution of a diagonal second-order system is smaller than its natural
decaying modes at
-\infty
, and if the source term is quadratically
small on the left, then the following left-uniqueness principle further gives that the solution must vanish on a sufficiently far
left half-line.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
\eta_{i}>0
, and
set
\eta_{*}:=\min_{1\leq i\leq N}\eta_{i}
,
i=1,\cdots,N
.
Assume

z=(z_{1},\cdots,z_{N})\in H^{1}(-\infty,R_{0})^{N}\cap C^{2}(-\infty,R_{0})^{N}

satisfies for
i=1,\cdots,N
,

z_{i}^{\prime\prime}-\eta_{i}^{2}z_{i}=F_{i}(x)\quad\text{in }(-\infty,R_{0}),\ \ z_{i}(x)=o(e^{\eta_{i}x})\quad\text{as }x\to-\infty,

where
F_{i}\in C((-\infty,R_{0}))
denotes the right-hand side associated
with
z
. If there exists a constant
C>0
such that for any
x<R_{0}
,

|F_{i}(x)|\leq Ce^{(\eta_{i}+2\eta_{*})x}\max_{1\leq j\leq N}e^{-\eta_{j}x}|z_{j}(x)|,\ \ i=1,\cdots,N,

(3.13)

then there exists
R<R_{0}
such that
z\equiv 0
on
(-\infty,R)
.

Proof.

Define for
R<R_{0}
,

W_{R}:=\max_{1\leq j\leq N}\sup_{x\leq R}e^{-\eta_{j}x}|z_{j}(x)|.

By the assumption
z_{j}(x)=o(e^{\eta_{j}x})
as
x\to-\infty
,
we obtain that
W_{R}<\infty
.
Fix
R<R_{0}
and define

Y_{i}(x):=\frac{1}{\eta_{i}}\int_{-\infty}^{x}\sinh\bigl(\eta_{i}(x-t)\bigr)F_{i}(t)\,dt,\ \ x\leq R.

By the definition of
W_{R}
, we obtain from (
3.13
) that

|F_{i}(x)|\leq CW_{R}e^{(\eta_{i}+2\eta_{*})x},\,\ x\leq R.

(3.14)

Hence the above integral
is well-defined for
x\leq R
. Moreover, differentiating the above
integration gives that

Y_{i}^{\prime\prime}-\eta_{i}^{2}Y_{i}=F_{i}.

Thus,

h_{i}:=z_{i}-Y_{i}

satisfies the homogeneous equation

h_{i}^{\prime\prime}-\eta_{i}^{2}h_{i}=0\quad\text{on }\ (-\infty,R].

Hence,

h_{i}(x)=\alpha_{i}e^{\eta_{i}x}+\beta_{i}e^{-\eta_{i}x}.

Since
z_{i},Y_{i}\in H^{1}(-\infty,R)
, we have

h_{i}\in H^{1}(-\infty,R)
. This excludes the mode
e^{-\eta_{i}x}
, and hence
\beta_{i}=0
.
On the other hand, we obtain from (
3.14
) that

\displaystyle|Y_{i}(x)|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{CW_{R}}{2\eta_{i}}\int_{-\infty}^{x}e^{\eta_{i}(x-t)}e^{(\eta_{i}+2\eta_{*})t}\,dt

\displaystyle\leq CW_{R}e^{\eta_{i}x}e^{2\eta_{*}x}=o(e^{\eta_{i}x})\quad\text{as }x\to-\infty.

By the assumption

z_{i}(x)=o(e^{\eta_{i}x})

as
x\to-\infty
,
we then have

h_{i}(x)=o(e^{\eta_{i}x})
as
x\to-\infty
.
Since
h_{i}(x)=\alpha_{i}e^{\eta_{i}x}
, we get that
\alpha_{i}=0
.

We obtain from above that

z_{i}(x)=Y_{i}(x)=\frac{1}{\eta_{i}}\int_{-\infty}^{x}\sinh\bigl(\eta_{i}(x-t)\bigr)F_{i}(t)\,dt,\ \ x\leq R.

Since

\sinh\bigl(\eta_{i}(x-t)\bigr)\leq\frac{1}{2}e^{\eta_{i}(x-t)},\ \ t\leq x,

we obtain from (
3.14
) that

\displaystyle|z_{i}(x)|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C}{2\eta_{i}}W_{R}\int_{-\infty}^{x}e^{\eta_{i}(x-t)}e^{(\eta_{i}+2\eta_{*})t}\,dt

\displaystyle=\frac{C}{2\eta_{i}}W_{R}e^{\eta_{i}x}\int_{-\infty}^{x}e^{2\eta_{*}t}\,dt

\displaystyle=\frac{C}{4\eta_{i}\eta_{*}}W_{R}e^{\eta_{i}x}e^{2\eta_{*}x}.

Since
\eta_{i}\geq\eta_{*}
, there exists a constant
\Theta:=\frac{C}{4\eta_{*}^{2}}>0
,
independent of
R
, such that

e^{-\eta_{i}x}|z_{i}(x)|\leq\Theta W_{R}e^{2\eta_{*}x}\leq\Theta W_{R}e^{2\eta_{*}R},\ \ x\leq R.

Taking the supremum over
x\leq R
and then the maximum over
i
,
we get that

W_{R}\leq\Theta e^{2\eta_{*}R}W_{R}.

Choosing
R\ll-1
such that

\Theta e^{2\eta_{*}R}<1,

we thus conclude that
W_{R}=0
. Hence

z_{i}(x)=0,\ \ x\leq R,\quad i=1,\cdots,N,

which completes the proof.
∎

3.2
Classification in the distinct-parameter case

In this subsection, we prove Theorem
1.1
on the classification of solutions for (
1.1
) in the distinct-parameter case. For any

H^{1}
-solution
u\in(H^{1}({\mathbb{R}})\setminus\{0\})^{N}
, roughly speaking, the previous lemmas give its leading coefficients at

-\infty
. We shall use these coefficients as the parameters of the
determinant solution constructed in Section 2, and then apply the
left-uniqueness principle to identify the two solutions.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Set

\eta_{i}:=\sqrt{-\mu_{i}}>0,\ \ i=1,\cdots,N,

so that the system (
1.1
) can be rewritten as

u_{i}^{\prime\prime}+2\left(\sum_{j=1}^{N}u_{j}^{2}\right)u_{i}=\eta_{i}^{2}u_{i},\ \ i=1,\cdots,N.

By Lemma
3.2
, there exist constants

a_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
such that for each
i=1,\cdots,N
,

u_{i}(x)=a_{i}e^{\eta_{i}x}+o(e^{\eta_{i}x})\quad\text{as }x\to-\infty.

Since
u_{i}\not\equiv 0
, the last assertion of
Lemma
3.2
gives that

a_{i}\neq 0,\ \ i=1,\cdots,N.

Let
a=(a_{1},\cdots,a_{N})
be the vector of leading coefficients obtained
above, and denote
U^{a}=(U_{1}^{a},\cdots,U_{N}^{a})
the determinant solution
with parameter
a
, namely

U_{i}^{a}(x)=\frac{a_{i}e^{\eta_{i}x}\det(I+B^{(i)}M(x))}{\det(I+M(x))},\ \ i=1,\cdots,N.

By Proposition
2.1
,
U^{a}
is a

(H^{1}(\mathbb{R})\setminus\{0\})^{N}
solution of the system (
1.1
). Moreover, since

M(x)\to 0
as
x\to-\infty
, we have

U_{i}^{a}(x)=a_{i}e^{\eta_{i}x}+o(e^{\eta_{i}x})\quad\text{as }\ x\to-\infty.

It remains to prove that
u\equiv U^{a}
. Set

w_{i}:=u_{i}-U_{i}^{a},\ \ S_{u}:=\sum_{j=1}^{N}u_{j}^{2},\ \ S_{a}:=\sum_{j=1}^{N}(U_{j}^{a})^{2}.

Then

w_{i}^{\prime\prime}-\eta_{i}^{2}w_{i}=-2\left(S_{u}u_{i}-S_{a}U_{i}^{a}\right)=:F_{i}.

Since
u
and
U^{a}
have the same left asymptotic data, we have

w_{i}(x)=o(e^{\eta_{i}x})\quad\text{as }x\to-\infty.

Set

\eta_{*}:=\min_{1\leq j\leq N}\eta_{j}.

By the above asymptotics, there exist
R_{0}<0
and
C_{0}>0

such that for all
x\leq R_{0}
,

|u_{i}(x)|+|U_{i}^{a}(x)|\leq C_{0}e^{\eta_{i}x},\ \ i=1,\cdots,N.

Define

Z(x):=\max_{1\leq j\leq N}e^{-\eta_{j}x}|w_{j}(x)|,\ \ x\leq R_{0},

so that

|w_{j}(x)|\leq Z(x)e^{\eta_{j}x},\ \ x\leq R_{0}.

Using

S_{u}u_{i}-S_{a}U_{i}^{a}=S_{u}w_{i}+(S_{u}-S_{a})U_{i}^{a},\ \ S_{u}-S_{a}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}(u_{j}+U_{j}^{a})w_{j},

we obtain that

\displaystyle|F_{i}(x)|

\displaystyle\leq 2S_{u}(x)|w_{i}(x)|+2|U_{i}^{a}(x)|\sum_{j=1}^{N}|u_{j}(x)+U_{j}^{a}(x)|\,|w_{j}(x)|

\displaystyle\leq Ce^{(\eta_{i}+2\eta_{*})x}Z(x),\ \ x\leq R_{0},

where
C>0
is independent of
x
. Thus,
w
satisfies the
assumptions of Lemma
3.3
, which implies that

w\equiv 0\ \ \text{on a sufficiently far left half-line}.

In particular, we have for some
R<R_{0}
,

u_{i}(R)=U_{i}^{a}(R),\ \ u_{i}^{\prime}(R)=(U_{i}^{a})^{\prime}(R),\ \ i=1,\cdots,N.

By the uniqueness of solutions for ODE systems, we obtain that

u\equiv U^{a}\quad\text{on }\ \mathbb{R}.

This proves Theorem
1.1
.
∎

3.3
Nondegeneracy in the distinct-parameter case

In this subsection, we prove Theorem
1.2
on the
nondegeneracy of the determinant family in the distinct-parameter case.
It is clear that the tangent directions obtained by differentiating the
determinant family belong to the kernel. To prove that these are the only
H^{1}
-kernel
directions, we again use the left asymptotics and the left-uniqueness
principle established above.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

For each
k=1,\cdots,N
, differentiating the identity

(U_{i}^{a})^{\prime\prime}+2\left(\sum_{j=1}^{N}(U_{j}^{a})^{2}\right)U_{i}^{a}=\eta_{i}^{2}U_{i}^{a},\ \ i=1,\cdots,N,

with respect to
a_{k}
, we obtain that

\mathcal{L}_{U^{a}}(\partial_{a_{k}}U^{a})=0,

where
\mathcal{L}_{U^{a}}
is given by

(\mathcal{L}_{U^{a}}\phi)_{i}:=-\phi_{i}^{\prime\prime}+\eta_{i}^{2}\phi_{i}-2S^{a}\phi_{i}-4U_{i}^{a}\sum_{j=1}^{N}U_{j}^{a}\phi_{j},\ \ i=1,\cdots,N.

Here
S^{a}=\left(\sum_{j=1}^{N}(U_{j}^{a})^{2}\right)
.
Furthermore, direct computations yield that
\partial_{a_{k}}U^{a}\in H^{1}({\mathbb{R}})^{N}
holds for
a_{k}\neq 0
. Thus, we have for every
a\in(\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\})^{N}
,

\operatorname{span}\left\{\partial_{a_{1}}U^{a},\cdots,\partial_{a_{N}}U^{a}\right\}\subset\ker_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R})^{N}}\mathcal{L}_{U^{a}}.

Conversely, we have for

\phi=(\phi_{1},\cdots,\phi_{N})\in\ker_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R})^{N}}\mathcal{L}_{U^{a}}
,

\phi_{i}^{\prime\prime}-\eta_{i}^{2}\phi_{i}=-2S^{a}\phi_{i}-4U_{i}^{a}\sum_{j=1}^{N}U_{j}^{a}\phi_{j},\ \ i=1,\cdots,N.

Since
U_{i}^{a}(x)=O(e^{\eta_{i}x})
as
x\to-\infty
, we obtain that

S^{a}\in L^{1}(-\infty,R_{0})
. Moreover, the one-dimensional Sobolev
embedding gives that

\phi\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})^{N}
.

For each
i
, rewrite the equation as

\phi_{i}^{\prime\prime}-\eta_{i}^{2}\phi_{i}=-2S^{a}\phi_{i}+h_{i}(x),

where

h_{i}(x):=-4U_{i}^{a}(x)\sum_{j=1}^{N}U_{j}^{a}(x)\phi_{j}(x).

Set

\eta_{*}:=\min_{1\leq j\leq N}\eta_{j},

so that

|h_{i}(x)|\leq C|U_{i}^{a}(x)|\sum_{j=1}^{N}|U_{j}^{a}(x)|\leq Ce^{(\eta_{i}+\eta_{*})x}\quad\text{as }x\to-\infty.

In particular,

h_{i}(x)=o(e^{\eta_{i}x})
as
x\to-\infty
.
Applying Lemma
3.1
, we obtain that there exists a constant

b_{i}^{-}\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\phi_{i}(x)=b_{i}^{-}e^{\eta_{i}x}+o(e^{\eta_{i}x})\quad\text{as }x\to-\infty.

On the other hand, the determinant formula of
U^{a}
gives that

\partial_{a_{k}}U_{i}^{a}(x)=\delta_{ik}e^{\eta_{i}x}+o(e^{\eta_{i}x})\quad\text{as }\ x\to-\infty.

Define

\psi:=\sum_{k=1}^{N}b_{k}^{-}\partial_{a_{k}}U^{a},

so that
\mathcal{L}_{U^{a}}\psi=0

and

\psi_{i}(x)=b_{i}^{-}e^{\eta_{i}x}+o(e^{\eta_{i}x})\quad\text{as }\ x\to-\infty.

Hence

z:=\phi-\psi

satisfies

\mathcal{L}_{U^{a}}z=0
,
and

z_{i}(x)=o(e^{\eta_{i}x})\ \ \text{as }\ x\to-\infty,\ \ i=1,\cdots,N.

Moreover,
z(x)
satisfies

z_{i}^{\prime\prime}-\eta_{i}^{2}z_{i}=-2S^{a}z_{i}-4U_{i}^{a}\sum_{j=1}^{N}U_{j}^{a}z_{j}=:F_{i}(x).

Set

Z(x):=\max_{1\leq j\leq N}e^{-\eta_{j}x}|z_{j}(x)|,\ \ x\leq R_{0}.

Since
U_{i}^{a}(x)=O(e^{\eta_{i}x})
as
x\to-\infty
, after decreasing

R_{0}
if necessary, we have

|F_{i}(x)|\leq Ce^{(\eta_{i}+2\eta_{*})x}Z(x),\ \ x\leq R_{0}.

Thus,
z
satisfies the assumptions of
Lemma
3.3
. This gives that there exists
R<R_{0}
such that

z\equiv 0\quad\text{on }\ (-\infty,R).

In particular,

z_{i}(R)=z_{i}^{\prime}(R)=0,\ \ i=1,\cdots,N.

Since
z
satisfies the linearized ODE system with continuous
coefficients, the uniqueness theorem of ODE systems
implies that

z\equiv 0
on
{\mathbb{R}}
.
Thus,

\phi=\sum_{k=1}^{N}b_{k}^{-}\partial_{a_{k}}U^{a},

which proves that

\ker_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R})^{N}}\mathcal{L}_{U^{a}}\subset\operatorname{span}\left\{\partial_{a_{1}}U^{a},\cdots,\partial_{a_{N}}U^{a}\right\}.

Combining above two inclusions, we obtain that

\ker_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R})^{N}}\mathcal{L}_{U^{a}}=\operatorname{span}\left\{\partial_{a_{1}}U^{a},\cdots,\partial_{a_{N}}U^{a}\right\}.

Finally, since

\partial_{a_{k}}U_{i}^{a}(x)=\delta_{ik}e^{\eta_{i}x}+o(e^{\eta_{i}x})\quad\text{as }x\to-\infty,

the vector-valued functions

\partial_{a_{1}}U^{a},\cdots,\partial_{a_{N}}U^{a}

are linearly independent. Hence

\dim\ker_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R})^{N}}\mathcal{L}_{U^{a}}=N,

which completes the proof of Theorem
1.2
.
∎

4
Classification and Nondegeneracy with Repeated Spectral Parameters

This section is concerned with the classification and nondegeneracy of
H^{1}(\mathbb{R})^{N}
-solutions for (
1.1
), in the case where the spectral parameters are allowed to have multiplicities. In Subsection
4.1
, we shall prove Theorem
1.3
on the classification of solutions for (
1.13
). In Subsection
4.2
, we prove the nondegeneracy of Theorem
1.4
for the linearized operator
\mathcal{L}_{U^{a,n}}
defined in (
1.16
).

Let

\mu_{1}<\cdots<\mu_{K}<0

be the distinct spectral parameters, and denote
k_{\alpha}
to be the
multiplicity of
\mu_{\alpha}
, so that

k_{1}+\cdots+k_{K}=N.

Set

N_{0}:=0,\ \ N_{\alpha}:=k_{1}+\cdots+k_{\alpha},\ \ I_{\alpha}:=\{N_{\alpha-1}+1,\cdots,N_{\alpha}\}.

The full list of spectral parameters counted with multiplicity is denoted
by
\nu_{1},\cdots,\nu_{N}
and

\nu_{i}=\mu_{\alpha},\ \ i\in I_{\alpha}.

For
\alpha=1,2,\cdots,K
, we also write

\mu_{\alpha}=-\eta_{\alpha}^{2}<0
.

The key observation of this section lies in the fact that repeated spectral parameters do not produce new
scalar profiles. Instead, the components in the same spectral block are
locked together and differ only by constant coefficients. Hence, each
nontrivial block
I_{\alpha}
reduces to a single scalar profile

V_{\alpha}
, and the collection
V=(V_{1},\cdots,V_{K})
solves the reduced
distinct-parameter system (
1.10
). Therefore, the
classification in the repeated-parameter case follows from the
distinct-parameter classification of Theorem
1.1
, together with the
internal rotational freedom inside each block.

4.1
Classification with repeated spectral parameters

The purpose of this subsection is to prove
Theorem
1.3
, namely the complete
classification of
H^{1}(\mathbb{R})^{N}
-solutions for
(
1.13
) with repeated spectral parameters.

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

Let

S=\sum_{\ell=1}^{N}u_{\ell}^{2},

and fix
\alpha\in\{1,\cdots,K\}
. It then yields from (
1.9
) that for any
i,j\in I_{\alpha}
,

\nu_{i}=\nu_{j}=\mu_{\alpha}.

Hence, both
u_{i}
and
u_{j}
solve the same linear equation

y^{\prime\prime}+2Sy+\mu_{\alpha}y=0.

Define

W_{ij}:=u_{i}^{\prime}u_{j}-u_{i}u_{j}^{\prime},

so that
W_{ij}^{\prime}=u_{i}^{\prime\prime}u_{j}-u_{i}u_{j}^{\prime\prime}=0
.
Thus,
W_{ij}
is constant on
\mathbb{R}
. Moreover, since

u_{i},u_{j}\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
, we have

u_{i}^{\prime}u_{j},\ u_{i}u_{j}^{\prime}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}),

which implies that
W_{ij}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
. Since a constant function belonging to

L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
must be zero, we have

W_{ij}\equiv 0\ \ \hbox{for any }i,j\in I_{\alpha}.

Since
\sum_{i\in I_{\alpha}}u_{i}^{2}(x)\not\equiv 0
, there exists

i_{\alpha}\in I_{\alpha}
such that

u_{i_{\alpha}}\not\equiv 0
.
For any
i\in I_{\alpha}
, choose
x_{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}
such that

u_{i_{\alpha}}(x_{\alpha})\neq 0
, and set

c_{i}:=\frac{u_{i}(x_{\alpha})}{u_{i_{\alpha}}(x_{\alpha})}.

Define

w_{i}:=u_{i}-c_{i}u_{i_{\alpha}},

so that
w_{i}
satisfies the same linear equation

w_{i}^{\prime\prime}+2Sw_{i}+\mu_{\alpha}w_{i}=0.

Moreover, we have
w_{i}(x_{\alpha})=0
.
Since
W_{ii_{\alpha}}\equiv 0
, we also have

u_{i}^{\prime}(x_{\alpha})u_{i_{\alpha}}(x_{\alpha})-u_{i}(x_{\alpha})u_{i_{\alpha}}^{\prime}(x_{\alpha})=0,

which gives that
w_{i}^{\prime}(x_{\alpha})=0
.
By the uniqueness of second-order linear ODE, we obtain that

w_{i}\equiv 0.

We thus have

u_{i}=c_{i}u_{i_{\alpha}},\ \ i\in I_{\alpha}.

Set

d_{\alpha}:=\left(\sum_{i\in I_{\alpha}}c_{i}^{2}\right)^{1/2}>0,\ \ V_{\alpha}:=d_{\alpha}u_{i_{\alpha}},\ \ n_{i}^{(\alpha)}:=\frac{c_{i}}{d_{\alpha}},\quad i\in I_{\alpha}.

Then

n^{(\alpha)}=(n_{i}^{(\alpha)})_{i\in I_{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{S}^{k_{\alpha}-1},

and

u_{i}=n_{i}^{(\alpha)}V_{\alpha},\ \ i\in I_{\alpha}.

Consequently,

\sum_{i\in I_{\alpha}}u_{i}^{2}=V_{\alpha}^{2},

and hence

S=\sum_{\beta=1}^{K}V_{\beta}^{2}.

Substitute
u_{i}=n_{i}^{(\alpha)}V_{\alpha}

into the equation of
u_{i}
, and choose
i=i_{\alpha}
so that

n_{i_{\alpha}}^{(\alpha)}\neq 0
. We then obtain that

V_{\alpha}^{\prime\prime}+2\left(\sum_{\beta=1}^{K}V_{\beta}^{2}\right)V_{\alpha}+\mu_{\alpha}V_{\alpha}=0,\ \ \alpha=1,\cdots,K.

Thus

V=(V_{1},\cdots,V_{K})

is an
H^{1}(\mathbb{R})^{K}
solution of the reduced distinct-root system
(
1.10
). Since
\sum_{i\in I_{\alpha}}u_{i}^{2}(x)\not\equiv 0
, we have

V_{\alpha}\not\equiv 0
.

Applying Theorem
1.1
to the reduced system
(
1.10
), it yields that there exists

a=(a_{1},\cdots,a_{K})\in(\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\})^{K}

such that

V=V^{a}.

Therefore

u_{i}=n_{i}^{(\alpha)}V_{\alpha}^{a},\ \ i\in I_{\alpha},\quad\alpha=1,\cdots,K.

The definition of
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{multi}}
in
(
1.12
) then gives that

u\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{multi}},

and we are done.
∎

4.2
Nondegeneracy with repeated spectral parameters

In this subsection, we prove the nondegeneracy of Theorem
1.4
. The argument follows the same reduction principle as in the classification theorem, except that it is now at the linearized level. The component of a kernel element parallel to
n^{(\alpha)}
is governed by the reduced
K
-component linearized operator, while the orthogonal component inside each block is generated exactly by rotations of
n^{(\alpha)}
.

Proof of Theorem
1.4
.

Let

U^{a,n}\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{multi}}
be given by

U_{i}^{a,n}(x)=n_{i}^{(\alpha)}V_{\alpha}^{a}(x),\ \ i\in I_{\alpha},\quad\alpha=1,\cdots,K,

where
V^{a}=(V_{1}^{a},\cdots,V_{K}^{a})
is the reduced solution with distinct
spectral parameters and

n^{(\alpha)}=(n_{i}^{(\alpha)})_{i\in I_{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{S}^{k_{\alpha}-1}
. Since
|n^{(\alpha)}|=1
, we have

S^{a}(x):=\sum_{\ell=1}^{N}(U_{\ell}^{a,n})^{2}=\sum_{\beta=1}^{K}(V_{\beta}^{a})^{2}.

The linearized operator at
U^{a,n}
is

(\mathcal{L}_{U^{a,n}}\phi)_{i}=-\phi_{i}^{\prime\prime}-\nu_{i}\phi_{i}-2S^{a}\phi_{i}-4U_{i}^{a,n}\sum_{\ell=1}^{N}U_{\ell}^{a,n}\phi_{\ell},\ \ i=1,\cdots,N.

We also recall the tangent directions of the manifold

\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{multi}}
at
U^{a,n}
. The directions generated by
the reduced parameters are

\Phi^{(\gamma)}=(\Phi_{1}^{(\gamma)},\cdots,\Phi_{N}^{(\gamma)}),\ \ \hbox{where}\,\ \Phi_{i}^{(\gamma)}=n_{i}^{(\alpha)}\partial_{a_{\gamma}}V_{\alpha}^{a},\ \ i\in I_{\alpha},\quad\gamma=1,\cdots,K.

The rotational directions inside the block
I_{\alpha}
are

\Psi^{(\alpha,\xi)}=(\Psi_{1}^{(\alpha,\xi)},\cdots,\Psi_{N}^{(\alpha,\xi)}),\ \ \hbox{where}\,\ \Psi_{i}^{(\alpha,\xi)}=\begin{cases}\xi_{i}V_{\alpha}^{a},&i\in I_{\alpha},\\
0,&i\notin I_{\alpha},\end{cases}\ \ \xi\in T_{n^{(\alpha)}}\mathbb{S}^{k_{\alpha}-1},

where

T_{n^{(\alpha)}}\mathbb{S}^{k_{\alpha}-1}=\left\{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{k_{\alpha}}:\ \xi\cdot n^{(\alpha)}=0\right\}.

Thus,
T_{U^{a,n}}\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{multi}}
is the span of all

\Phi^{(\gamma)}
and
\Psi^{(\alpha,\xi)}
.

We now prove that

T_{U^{a,n}}\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{multi}}\subset\ker_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R})^{N}}\mathcal{L}_{U^{a,n}}.

Indeed, the directions
\Phi^{(\gamma)}
are obtained by differentiating
the family
U^{a,n}
with respect to the reduced parameters
a_{\gamma}
.
Since
U^{a,n}
solves the full
N
-component system for all admissible

a
, we have

\mathcal{L}_{U^{a,n}}\Phi^{(\gamma)}=0,\ \ \gamma=1,\cdots,K.

Similarly, fix
\alpha
and let

\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{k_{\alpha}}
satisfy

\xi\cdot n^{(\alpha)}=0
.
Then there exists a smooth curve

n_{\varepsilon}^{(\alpha)}\in\mathbb{S}^{k_{\alpha}-1}

such that

n_{0}^{(\alpha)}=n^{(\alpha)},\ \ \frac{d}{d\varepsilon}n_{\varepsilon}^{(\alpha)}\Big|_{\varepsilon=0}=\xi.

Define the perturbed solution by

U_{i}^{\varepsilon}=\begin{cases}n_{\varepsilon,i}^{(\alpha)}V_{\alpha}^{a},&i\in I_{\alpha},\\
U_{i}^{a,n},&i\notin I_{\alpha}.\end{cases}

Since the system is invariant under orthogonal
rotations inside each block
I_{\alpha}
,

U^{\varepsilon}
remains a solution for all

\varepsilon
.
Differentiating the equation with respect to

\varepsilon
at
\varepsilon=0
, it
yields that

\mathcal{L}_{U^{a,n}}\Psi^{(\alpha,\xi)}=0.

This gives that

T_{U^{a,n}}\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{multi}}\subset\ker_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R})^{N}}\mathcal{L}_{U^{a,n}}.

It remains to prove the reverse inclusion. Let

\phi=(\phi_{1},\cdots,\phi_{N})\in\ker_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R})^{N}}\mathcal{L}_{U^{a,n}}.

For each block
I_{\alpha}
, decompose
\phi
into its component parallel
to
n^{(\alpha)}
and its orthogonal component. Define

p_{\alpha}:=\sum_{i\in I_{\alpha}}n_{i}^{(\alpha)}\phi_{i},\quad r_{i}:=\phi_{i}-n_{i}^{(\alpha)}p_{\alpha},\ \ i\in I_{\alpha},

then

\phi_{i}=n_{i}^{(\alpha)}p_{\alpha}+r_{i}
for
i\in I_{\alpha}
,
and

\sum_{i\in I_{\alpha}}n_{i}^{(\alpha)}r_{i}=0.

Multiplying the
i
-th equation by
n_{i}^{(\alpha)}
and summing over

i\in I_{\alpha}
, we obtain that

-\left(\sum_{i\in I_{\alpha}}n_{i}^{(\alpha)}\phi_{i}\right)^{\prime\prime}-\mu_{\alpha}\sum_{i\in I_{\alpha}}n_{i}^{(\alpha)}\phi_{i}-2S^{a}\sum_{i\in I_{\alpha}}n_{i}^{(\alpha)}\phi_{i}-4\left(\sum_{\ell=1}^{N}U_{\ell}^{a,n}\phi_{\ell}\right)\sum_{i\in I_{\alpha}}n_{i}^{(\alpha)}U_{i}^{a,n}=0.

Since

\sum_{i\in I_{\alpha}}n_{i}^{(\alpha)}\phi_{i}=p_{\alpha}
,

\sum_{i\in I_{\alpha}}n_{i}^{(\alpha)}U_{i}^{a,n}=V_{\alpha}^{a}\sum_{i\in I_{\alpha}}(n_{i}^{(\alpha)})^{2}=V_{\alpha}^{a},

and

\sum_{\ell=1}^{N}U_{\ell}^{a,n}\phi_{\ell}=\sum_{\beta=1}^{K}V_{\beta}^{a}p_{\beta},

we get that

-p_{\alpha}^{\prime\prime}-\mu_{\alpha}p_{\alpha}-2S^{a}p_{\alpha}-4V_{\alpha}^{a}\sum_{\beta=1}^{K}V_{\beta}^{a}p_{\beta}=0.

Hence,
p=(p_{1},\cdots,p_{K})
belongs to

\ker_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R})^{K}}\mathcal{L}_{V^{a}}
.
By the nondegeneracy of the reduced solution in Theorem
1.2
, we have

\ker_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R})^{K}}\mathcal{L}_{V^{a}}=\operatorname{span}\left\{\partial_{a_{1}}V^{a},\cdots,\partial_{a_{K}}V^{a}\right\},

and hence there exist constants
c_{1},\cdots,c_{K}
such that

p=\sum_{\gamma=1}^{K}c_{\gamma}\partial_{a_{\gamma}}V^{a}.

We now identify the orthogonal part. Recall that
r_{i}=\phi_{i}-n_{i}^{(\alpha)}p_{\alpha}
. For
i\in I_{\alpha}
, subtract

n_{i}^{(\alpha)}
times the equation of
p_{\alpha}
from the
i
-th
equation of
\phi_{i}
, and use

U_{i}^{a,n}=n_{i}^{(\alpha)}V_{\alpha}^{a},\ \ \sum_{\ell=1}^{N}U_{\ell}^{a,n}\phi_{\ell}=\sum_{\beta=1}^{K}V_{\beta}^{a}p_{\beta}.

It yields that

-r_{i}^{\prime\prime}-\mu_{\alpha}r_{i}-2S^{a}r_{i}=0,\ \ i\in I_{\alpha}.

Therefore, both
r_{i}
and
V_{\alpha}^{a}
solve the same linear equation

-y^{\prime\prime}-\mu_{\alpha}y-2S^{a}y=0,

their Wronskian

W_{i}^{(\alpha)}:=r_{i}(V_{\alpha}^{a})^{\prime}-r_{i}^{\prime}V_{\alpha}^{a}

is constant, due to the fact that
(W_{i}^{(\alpha)})^{\prime}=0
. Since
r_{i},V_{\alpha}^{a}\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
, we have

r_{i}(V^{a}_{\alpha})^{\prime},\ r_{i}^{\prime}V_{\alpha}^{a}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}).

Hence
W_{i}^{(\alpha)}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
, which gives that

W_{i}^{(\alpha)}\equiv 0.

Since
V^{a}_{\alpha}\not\equiv 0
, choose
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
such that

V^{a}_{\alpha}(x_{0})\neq 0.

Set

d_{i}^{(\alpha)}:=\frac{r_{i}(x_{0})}{V^{a}_{\alpha}(x_{0})},

so that

r_{i}(x_{0})=d_{i}^{(\alpha)}V^{a}_{\alpha}(x_{0}).

Moreover, since
W_{i}^{(\alpha)}\equiv 0
that
W_{i}^{(\alpha)}(x_{0})=0
, we have

r_{i}^{\prime}(x_{0})=d_{i}^{(\alpha)}(V^{a}_{\alpha})^{\prime}(x_{0}).

Since
r_{i}
and
d_{i}^{(\alpha)}V^{a}_{\alpha}
solve the same second-order
linear equation, the uniqueness gives that

r_{i}=d_{i}^{(\alpha)}V^{a}_{\alpha},\ \ i\in I_{\alpha}.

The orthogonality condition thus gives that

\sum_{i\in I_{\alpha}}n_{i}^{(\alpha)}r_{i}=0,

which therefore implies that

\sum_{i\in I_{\alpha}}n_{i}^{(\alpha)}d_{i}^{(\alpha)}=0,

i.e.,

d^{(\alpha)}\cdot n^{(\alpha)}=0.

By the parallel and orthogonal parts, we now obtain that for
i\in I_{\alpha}
,

\phi_{i}=n_{i}^{(\alpha)}\sum_{\gamma=1}^{K}c_{\gamma}\partial_{a_{\gamma}}V^{a}_{\alpha}+d_{i}^{(\alpha)}V^{a}_{\alpha},

where

d^{(\alpha)}\cdot n^{(\alpha)}=0.

Thus,
\phi
is a linear combination of the parameter directions

\Phi^{(\gamma)}
and the rotation directions

\Psi^{(\alpha,\xi)}
. Hence

\ker_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R})^{N}}\mathcal{L}_{U^{a,n}}\subset T_{U^{a,n}}\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{multi}}.

Together with the first inclusion, this proves that

\ker_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R})^{N}}\mathcal{L}_{U^{a,n}}=T_{U^{a,n}}\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{multi}}.

Finally, the parameter directions are independent, due to the nondegeneracy
of the reduced solution and the local parametrization
a\mapsto V^{a}
.
The rotation directions in the block
I_{\alpha}
form the tangent space

T_{n^{(\alpha)}}\mathbb{S}^{k_{\alpha}-1}
, and hence contribute to

k_{\alpha}-1
independent directions. These two types of directions are
independent, because they correspond respectively to the parallel and
orthogonal components in each block. We thus have

\dim T_{U^{a,n}}\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{multi}}=K+\sum_{\alpha=1}^{K}(k_{\alpha}-1)=N,

and the proof is therefore complete.
∎

5
L^{2}
-Mass Formula of Solutions

In this section, we shall prove Theorem
1.5
in two steps. In Subsection 5.1 we first derive a pointwise algebraic identity of the components belonging to the same spectral block. We then use this identity to establish the exact
L^{2}
-mass formula of the classified solutions. The derivation of the algebraic identity relies on the constants of motion. After rewriting these constants in a polynomial form, we then evaluate the resulting polynomial identity at suitable spectral values. This gives the desired identity in the distinct-parameter case, while the repeated-parameter case follows by reducing each block of equal parameters to a single effective component.

5.1
An Algebraic identity

In this subsection we first prove the following algebraic identity, which is used in the next subsection to derive the exact
L^{2}
-mass formula.

Proposition 5.1
.

Under the same assumptions of
Theorem
1.3
, we have for any
\alpha=1,\cdots,K
,

\sum_{j\in I_{\alpha}}\left[(u_{j}^{\prime})^{2}+\big(\sum_{l=1}^{N}u_{l}^{2}+\mu_{\alpha}\big)u_{j}^{2}\right]+\sum_{\beta\neq\alpha}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\in I_{\alpha}\\
l\in I_{\beta}\end{subarray}}\frac{(u_{j}^{\prime}u_{l}-u_{j}u_{l}^{\prime})^{2}}{\mu_{\beta}-\mu_{\alpha}}=0.

(5.1)

We remark that the constants of motion for (
1.1
) were established in

[
11
]
. For convenience, we record
them in their generating polynomial form. More precisely, when the left-hand
side of (
5.2
) below is expanded as a
polynomial in
\lambda
, it has degree at most
N-1
, and for each

k=1,\cdots,N
, the coefficient of
\lambda^{N-k}
is exactly the

k
-th constant of motion in
[
11
, Lemma 5.1]
. In order to prove Proposition
5.1
,
we first prove the following constants of motion in a simplified approach.

Lemma 5.2
.

For
\mu_{1}\leq\mu_{2}\leq\cdots\leq\mu_{N}<0
, let
u=(u_{1},\cdots,u_{N})\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R})^{N}
be a solution of
(
1.1
), namely

-u_{j}^{\prime\prime}-2Su_{j}=\mu_{j}u_{j},\ \ S=\sum_{i=1}^{N}u_{i}^{2},

and set

W_{jl}:=u_{j}^{\prime}u_{l}-u_{j}u_{l}^{\prime}
for
1\leq j,l\leq N
. Then the following polynomial identity holds

\begin{split}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left[(u_{j}^{\prime})^{2}+(S+\mu_{j})u_{j}^{2}\right]\prod_{i\neq j}(\lambda+\mu_{i})+\sum_{1\leq j<l\leq N}W_{jl}^{2}\prod_{i\neq j,l}(\lambda+\mu_{i})\equiv 0\ \ \text{in}\ \ {\mathbb{R}}\end{split}

(5.2)

as a polynomial identity in
\lambda
.

Proof.

Set for
j=1,\cdots,N
,

P_{j}:=(u_{j}^{\prime})^{2}+(S+\mu_{j})u_{j}^{2},

and define for

\lambda\notin\{-\mu_{1},\cdots,-\mu_{N}\}
,

\mathcal{C}(\lambda,x):=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{P_{j}}{\lambda+\mu_{j}}+\sum_{1\leq j<l\leq N}\frac{W_{jl}^{2}}{(\lambda+\mu_{j})(\lambda+\mu_{l})}.

We claim that
\mathcal{C}(\lambda,x)
is independent of
x
.

In order to prove the above claim, it suffices to show that
\partial_{x}\mathcal{C}(\lambda,x)\equiv 0
. Note that

\begin{split}\partial_{x}\mathcal{C}(\lambda,x)&=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{P_{j}^{\prime}}{\lambda+\mu_{j}}+\sum_{1\leq j<l\leq N}\frac{2W_{jl}W_{jl}^{\prime}}{(\lambda+\mu_{j})(\lambda+\mu_{l})}.\end{split}

We next compute
P_{j}^{\prime}
as follows. Since

u_{j}^{\prime\prime}=-(2S+\mu_{j})u_{j},

we obtain that

\begin{split}P_{j}^{\prime}=2u_{j}^{\prime}u_{j}^{\prime\prime}+S^{\prime}u_{j}^{2}+2(S+\mu_{j})u_{j}u_{j}^{\prime}=S^{\prime}u_{j}^{2}-2Su_{j}u_{j}^{\prime}.\end{split}

Because
S^{\prime}=2\sum_{m=1}^{N}u_{m}u_{m}^{\prime}
, we have

P_{j}^{\prime}=-2\sum_{m=1}^{N}u_{j}u_{m}W_{jm},

On the other hand, we obtain that

W_{jl}^{\prime}=u_{j}^{\prime\prime}u_{l}-u_{j}u_{l}^{\prime\prime}=(\mu_{l}-\mu_{j})u_{j}u_{l}.

For each pair
j<l
, the coefficient of
u_{j}u_{l}W_{jl}
coming from
\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{P_{j}^{\prime}}{\lambda+\mu_{j}}
satisfies

-\,\frac{2u_{j}u_{l}W_{jl}}{\lambda+\mu_{j}}+\frac{2u_{j}u_{l}W_{jl}}{\lambda+\mu_{l}}=\frac{2(\mu_{j}-\mu_{l})u_{j}u_{l}W_{jl}}{(\lambda+\mu_{j})(\lambda+\mu_{l})}.

The contribution coming from
\sum_{1\leq j<l\leq N}\frac{2W_{jl}W_{jl}^{\prime}}{(\lambda+\mu_{j})(\lambda+\mu_{l})}
also satisfies

\frac{2(\mu_{l}-\mu_{j})u_{j}u_{l}W_{jl}}{(\lambda+\mu_{j})(\lambda+\mu_{l})}.

Hence,

\partial_{x}\mathcal{C}(\lambda,x)=0
, and it proves the claim that
\mathcal{C}(\lambda,x)
is a constant.

On the other hand, since
u\in H^{1}({\mathbb{R}})^{N}
, we have
\mathcal{C}(\lambda,x)\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}})
. Since
\mathcal{C}(\lambda,x)
is a constant in
x
, it must have

\mathcal{C}(\lambda,x)\equiv 0.

Multiplying this identity by

\prod_{i=1}^{N}(\lambda+\mu_{i})
, it
gives that (
5.2
) holds for every

\lambda\notin\{-\mu_{1},\cdots,-\mu_{N}\}
. Since the left-hand side is a
polynomial in
\lambda
, the identity (
5.2
) holds for all
\lambda
.
∎

Lemma 5.3
.

Under the assumptions of Lemma
5.2
, assume

\mu_{1}<\mu_{2}<\cdots<\mu_{N}<0.

Then we have for each
j=1,2,\cdots,N
,

(u_{j}^{\prime})^{2}+(S+\mu_{j})u_{j}^{2}+\sum_{l\neq j}\frac{(u_{j}^{\prime}u_{l}-u_{j}u_{l}^{\prime})^{2}}{\mu_{l}-\mu_{j}}=0\ \ \text{in }\ \mathbb{R}.

(5.3)

Proof.

By Lemma
5.2
, we have

\begin{split}\sum_{r=1}^{N}\left[(u_{r}^{\prime})^{2}+(S+\mu_{r})u_{r}^{2}\right]\prod_{i\neq r}(\lambda+\mu_{i})+\sum_{1\leq r<s\leq N}W_{rs}^{2}\prod_{i\neq r,s}(\lambda+\mu_{i})\equiv 0.\end{split}

Fix
j\in\{1,2,\cdots,N\}
. Since the numbers

\mu_{1},\cdots,\mu_{N}
are pairwise distinct, we may evaluate this
polynomial identity at
\lambda=-\mu_{j}
. Then all terms in the
first sum vanish except the term with
r=j
, and all terms in the
second sum vanish except those involving the index
j
. This gives that

\left[(u_{j}^{\prime})^{2}+(S+\mu_{j})u_{j}^{2}\right]\prod_{i\neq j}(\mu_{i}-\mu_{j})+\sum_{l\neq j}(u_{j}^{\prime}u_{l}-u_{j}u_{l}^{\prime})^{2}\prod_{i\neq j,l}(\mu_{i}-\mu_{j})=0.

It then follows that

(u_{j}^{\prime})^{2}+(S+\mu_{j})u_{j}^{2}+\sum_{l\neq j}\frac{(u_{j}^{\prime}u_{l}-u_{j}u_{l}^{\prime})^{2}}{\mu_{l}-\mu_{j}}=0.

This proves (
5.3
).
∎

Applying the above two lemmas, we are now ready to prove Proposition
5.1
.

Proof of Proposition
5.1
.

By Theorem
1.3
, we have

u\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{multi}}
. Hence there exist

a=(a_{1},\cdots,a_{K})\in(\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\})^{K}\,\ \hbox{and}\,\ n^{(\alpha)}=(n_{j}^{(\alpha)})_{j\in I_{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{S}^{k_{\alpha}-1},\ \ \alpha=1,\cdots,K,

such that

u_{j}(x)=n_{j}^{(\alpha)}V_{\alpha}^{a}(x)
holds for
j\in I_{\alpha}
.
For simplicity, write
V_{\alpha}=V_{\alpha}^{a}
. Since

\sum_{j\in I_{\alpha}}\bigl(n_{j}^{(\alpha)}\bigr)^{2}=1,

we have

\sum_{j\in I_{\alpha}}u_{j}^{2}=V_{\alpha}^{2},\ \ \sum_{j\in I_{\alpha}}(u_{j}^{\prime})^{2}=(V_{\alpha}^{\prime})^{2}.

Consequently,

S=\sum_{j=1}^{N}u_{j}^{2}=\sum_{\gamma=1}^{K}V_{\gamma}^{2}.

Moreover, we have for
\alpha\neq\beta
,
j\in I_{\alpha}
, and

l\in I_{\beta}
,

u_{j}^{\prime}u_{l}-u_{j}u_{l}^{\prime}=n_{j}^{(\alpha)}n_{l}^{(\beta)}\bigl(V_{\alpha}^{\prime}V_{\beta}-V_{\alpha}V_{\beta}^{\prime}\bigr).

We then have

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\in I_{\alpha}\\
l\in I_{\beta}\end{subarray}}(u_{j}^{\prime}u_{l}-u_{j}u_{l}^{\prime})^{2}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{j\in I_{\alpha}}\bigl(n_{j}^{(\alpha)}\bigr)^{2}\right)\left(\sum_{l\in I_{\beta}}\bigl(n_{l}^{(\beta)}\bigr)^{2}\right)\bigl(V_{\alpha}^{\prime}V_{\beta}-V_{\alpha}V_{\beta}^{\prime}\bigr)^{2}

\displaystyle=\bigl(V_{\alpha}^{\prime}V_{\beta}-V_{\alpha}V_{\beta}^{\prime}\bigr)^{2}.

Substituting these identities into the left-hand side of
(
5.1
), we obtain that

\mbox{LHS of \eqref{eq:algebraic-identity}}=(V_{\alpha}^{\prime})^{2}+\left(\sum_{\gamma=1}^{K}V_{\gamma}^{2}+\mu_{\alpha}\right)V_{\alpha}^{2}+\sum_{\beta\neq\alpha}\frac{\bigl(V_{\alpha}^{\prime}V_{\beta}-V_{\alpha}V_{\beta}^{\prime}\bigr)^{2}}{\mu_{\beta}-\mu_{\alpha}}.

The reduced profile

V=(V_{1},\cdots,V_{K})

solves the distinct-root system

V_{\alpha}^{\prime\prime}+2\left(\sum_{\gamma=1}^{K}V_{\gamma}^{2}\right)V_{\alpha}+\mu_{\alpha}V_{\alpha}=0,\ \ \alpha=1,\cdots,K.

Applying Lemma
5.3
to this

K
-component distinct-root system, we then obtain that

\mbox{LHS of \eqref{eq:algebraic-identity}}=(V_{\alpha}^{\prime})^{2}+\left(\sum_{\gamma=1}^{K}V_{\gamma}^{2}+\mu_{\alpha}\right)V_{\alpha}^{2}+\sum_{\beta\neq\alpha}\frac{\bigl(V_{\alpha}^{\prime}V_{\beta}-V_{\alpha}V_{\beta}^{\prime}\bigr)^{2}}{\mu_{\beta}-\mu_{\alpha}}=0,

and we are done.
∎

5.2
The
L^{2}
-mass formula

In this subsection, we discuss the exact
L^{2}
-mass formula of Theorem
1.5
. We remark that the pointwise algebraic identities proved in the previous subsection allow us to express the mass of each spectral block in terms of the corresponding spectral parameter.

Proof of Theorem
1.5
.

Recall that

S=\sum_{i=1}^{N}u_{i}^{2}
.
For each

\alpha=1,\cdots,K
, Theorem
1.3
shows that there exist

n^{(\alpha)}=(n_{i}^{(\alpha)})_{i\in I_{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{S}^{k_{\alpha}-1}
and a nontrivial function
v_{\alpha}\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R})\cap C^{2}(\mathbb{R})
such that

u_{i}=n_{i}^{(\alpha)}v_{\alpha},\ \ i\in I_{\alpha}.

Hence
S=\sum_{\gamma=1}^{K}v_{\gamma}^{2}

and

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\sum_{i\in I_{\alpha}}u_{i}^{2}\,dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}}v_{\alpha}^{2}\,dx\neq 0.

It next suffices to prove that

\int_{\mathbb{R}}v_{\alpha}^{2}\,dx=2\eta_{\alpha}.

The reduced functions
v_{\alpha}
satisfy

v_{\alpha}^{\prime\prime}+(2S+\mu_{\alpha})v_{\alpha}=0,\ \ \alpha=1,\cdots,K.

(5.4)

Fix
\alpha
and define for
\beta\neq\alpha
,

\delta_{\alpha\beta}:=\mu_{\beta}-\mu_{\alpha},\ \ W_{\alpha\beta}:=v_{\alpha}^{\prime}v_{\beta}-v_{\alpha}v_{\beta}^{\prime}.

We then derive from (
5.4
) that

W_{\alpha\beta}^{\prime}=\delta_{\alpha\beta}v_{\alpha}v_{\beta}.

(5.5)

Moreover, Proposition
5.1
gives the reduced identity

(v_{\alpha}^{\prime})^{2}+(S+\mu_{\alpha})v_{\alpha}^{2}+\sum_{\beta\neq\alpha}\frac{W_{\alpha\beta}^{2}}{\delta_{\alpha\beta}}=0.

(5.6)

Define on the open set
\{v_{\alpha}\neq 0\}
,

Q_{\alpha}:=\frac{v_{\alpha}^{\prime}}{v_{\alpha}}+\sum_{\beta\neq\alpha}\frac{W_{\alpha\beta}v_{\beta}}{\delta_{\alpha\beta}v_{\alpha}}.

We first compute
Q_{\alpha}^{\prime}
on the open set
\{v_{\alpha}\neq 0\}
: it follows from
(
5.4
) and (
5.6
) that

\displaystyle\left(\frac{v_{\alpha}^{\prime}}{v_{\alpha}}\right)^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\frac{v_{\alpha}^{\prime\prime}}{v_{\alpha}}-\frac{(v_{\alpha}^{\prime})^{2}}{v_{\alpha}^{2}}

\displaystyle=-(2S+\mu_{\alpha})-\frac{(v_{\alpha}^{\prime})^{2}}{v_{\alpha}^{2}}

\displaystyle=-S+\sum_{\beta\neq\alpha}\frac{W_{\alpha\beta}^{2}}{\delta_{\alpha\beta}v_{\alpha}^{2}}.

We also derive from (
5.5
) that

\displaystyle\left(\frac{W_{\alpha\beta}v_{\beta}}{\delta_{\alpha\beta}v_{\alpha}}\right)^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\frac{W_{\alpha\beta}^{\prime}v_{\beta}+W_{\alpha\beta}v_{\beta}^{\prime}}{\delta_{\alpha\beta}v_{\alpha}}-\frac{W_{\alpha\beta}v_{\beta}v_{\alpha}^{\prime}}{\delta_{\alpha\beta}v_{\alpha}^{2}}

\displaystyle=v_{\beta}^{2}+\frac{W_{\alpha\beta}}{\delta_{\alpha\beta}v_{\alpha}^{2}}\left(v_{\alpha}v_{\beta}^{\prime}-v_{\alpha}^{\prime}v_{\beta}\right)

\displaystyle=v_{\beta}^{2}-\frac{W_{\alpha\beta}^{2}}{\delta_{\alpha\beta}v_{\alpha}^{2}}.

Summing over
\beta\neq\alpha
, it then yields that

Q_{\alpha}^{\prime}=-v_{\alpha}^{2}\ \ \text{on }\{v_{\alpha}\neq 0\}.

(5.7)

It remains to justify that
Q_{\alpha}
can be extended across the zeros of

v_{\alpha}
. Write

Q_{\alpha}=\frac{P_{\alpha}}{v_{\alpha}},\ \ P_{\alpha}:=v_{\alpha}^{\prime}+\sum_{\beta\neq\alpha}\frac{W_{\alpha\beta}v_{\beta}}{\delta_{\alpha\beta}},

and let
x_{0}
be a zero of
v_{\alpha}
. Since
v_{\alpha}
is a nontrivial
solution of the linear equation (
5.4
), we have

v_{\alpha}^{\prime}(x_{0})\neq 0
. Hence,
x_{0}
is a simple zero of
v_{\alpha}
.
On the other hand, evaluating (
5.6
) at
x_{0}

and using

W_{\alpha\beta}(x_{0})=v_{\alpha}^{\prime}(x_{0})v_{\beta}(x_{0}),

we obtain that

0=(v_{\alpha}^{\prime}(x_{0}))^{2}\Big(1+\sum_{\beta\neq\alpha}\frac{v_{\beta}(x_{0})^{2}}{\delta_{\alpha\beta}}\Big).

Since
v_{\alpha}^{\prime}(x_{0})\neq 0
, this implies that

1+\sum_{\beta\neq\alpha}\frac{v_{\beta}(x_{0})^{2}}{\delta_{\alpha\beta}}=0.

Consequently,

P_{\alpha}(x_{0})=v_{\alpha}^{\prime}(x_{0})\left(1+\sum_{\beta\neq\alpha}\frac{v_{\beta}(x_{0})^{2}}{\delta_{\alpha\beta}}\right)=0.

Thus, both
P_{\alpha}
and
v_{\alpha}
vanish at
x_{0}
, while

v_{\alpha}
has a simple zero at
x_{0}
. So
P_{\alpha}/v_{\alpha}
has a
finite limit at
x_{0}
. We extend
Q_{\alpha}
to
x_{0}
by this limit.
Since
P_{\alpha}
and
v_{\alpha}
are
C^{1}
, this extension is continuous.

Extending
Q_{\alpha}
by the above removable limits, we obtain a continuous
function on
\mathbb{R}
. Since the zeros of
v_{\alpha}
are isolated,
there are only finitely many zeros in any compact interval. Hence, integrating
(
5.7
) on each nodal interval and summing them, the interior boundary
terms cancel by the continuity of
Q_{\alpha}
. We thus have for any

R_{1}<R_{2}
,

\int_{R_{1}}^{R_{2}}v_{\alpha}^{2}\,dx=Q_{\alpha}(R_{1})-Q_{\alpha}(R_{2}).

(5.8)

Finally, we derive from Lemma
3.1
that there exist nonzero constants

c_{\gamma}^{-}
and
c_{\gamma}^{+}
such that for every

\gamma=1,\cdots,K
,

v_{\gamma}(x)=c_{\gamma}^{-}e^{\eta_{\gamma}x}+o(e^{\eta_{\gamma}x}),\ \ v_{\gamma}^{\prime}(x)=\eta_{\gamma}c_{\gamma}^{-}e^{\eta_{\gamma}x}+o(e^{\eta_{\gamma}x})\quad\text{as }\ x\to-\infty,

and

v_{\gamma}(x)=c_{\gamma}^{+}e^{-\eta_{\gamma}x}+o(e^{-\eta_{\gamma}x}),\ \ v_{\gamma}^{\prime}(x)=-\eta_{\gamma}c_{\gamma}^{+}e^{-\eta_{\gamma}x}+o(e^{-\eta_{\gamma}x})\quad\text{as }\ x\to+\infty,

where
\eta_{\gamma}=\sqrt{|\mu_{\gamma}|}
for
\gamma=1,2,\cdots,K
.
The right-hand asymptotics follow by applying
Lemma
3.1
to the reflected function

\widetilde{v}_{\gamma}(x):=v_{\gamma}(-x)
. The corresponding leading
coefficient cannot vanish, since otherwise Lemma
3.1
would
imply that
v_{\gamma}
vanishes on a right half-line, and hence

v_{\gamma}\equiv 0
by the uniqueness of ODE.
We hence have

\frac{v_{\alpha}^{\prime}}{v_{\alpha}}\to\eta_{\alpha}\quad\text{as }\ x\to-\infty,\ \ \frac{v_{\alpha}^{\prime}}{v_{\alpha}}\to-\eta_{\alpha}\quad\text{as }\ x\to+\infty.

Moreover, for every
\beta\neq\alpha
,

\frac{W_{\alpha\beta}v_{\beta}}{v_{\alpha}}=O(e^{2\eta_{\beta}x})\quad\text{as }\ x\to-\infty,

and

\frac{W_{\alpha\beta}v_{\beta}}{v_{\alpha}}=O(e^{-2\eta_{\beta}x})\quad\text{as }\ x\to+\infty.

These yield that

Q_{\alpha}(x)\to\eta_{\alpha}\quad\text{as }\ x\to-\infty,\ \ Q_{\alpha}(x)\to-\eta_{\alpha}\quad\text{as }\ x\to+\infty.

Setting
R_{1}\to-\infty
and
R_{2}\to+\infty
in
(
5.8
), we conclude that

\int_{\mathbb{R}}v_{\alpha}^{2}\,dx=\eta_{\alpha}-(-\eta_{\alpha})=2\eta_{\alpha}=2\sqrt{|\mu_{\alpha}|}.

Consequently, we have for every
\alpha=1,2,\cdots,K
,

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\sum_{i\in I_{\alpha}}u_{i}^{2}\,dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}}v_{\alpha}^{2}\,dx=2\eta_{\alpha}=2\sqrt{|\mu_{\alpha}|}.

This therefore completes the proof of Theorem
1.5
.
