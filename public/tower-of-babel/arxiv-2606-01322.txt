TukaBench: A Culturally Grounded Jailbreak Benchmark for African Languages
Victor Akinode, Senyu Li, Wassim Hamidouche, Waqas Zamir, Inbal Becker-Reshef, David Ifeoluwa Adelani

Abstract
Safety evaluation of Large Language Models (LLMs) remains heavily English-centric, leaving Low-Resource Languages (LRLs), particularly African ones, critically underexplored. We introduce TukaBench , a jailbreak benchmark for seven African languages that extends JailbreakBench (JBB) beyond direct translation through four settings: human translation of JBB prompts, English adaptation to African contexts followed by human translation, human curated prompts validated through interactions with GPT-5.2, and code-switched prompts combining English and African languages, isolating the effect of language, cultural grounding, and prompt evasiveness on model safety. Across closed and open models, prompting in African languages reduces refusal relative to English, with culturally adapted prompts leading to least refusal. The evaluation also surfaces two structural limitations: model comprehension failures and reduced LLM-as-a-judge reliability in LRLs. To capture the first, we introduce Deflection alongside Refused and Jailbroken; to assess the second, we validate outputs with human annotations, showing that judge-human agreement drops in lower-resource languages and less commonly supported scripts.

Introduction

Large Language Models (LLMs) have achieved strong performance across a wide range of NLP tasks, yet their deployment in real-world settings depends critically on their safety behavior
20
;
5
. Safety evaluation has so far concentrated almost exclusively on English
(
32
;
29
;
24
)
, and even when multilingual coverage is included, it typically extends only to a limited set of high- and mid-resource languages
(
10
;
15
)
, leaving Low-Resource Languages (LRLs) largely outside the scope of current safety assessment.

This gap is particularly critical in the context of
jailbreaking
: the use of adversarial prompts to
circumvent safety measures
and
elicit undesirable behavior
from aligned models. Existing jailbreak evaluations remain centered on a small set of high-resource languages
(
6
;
9
)
, even though LRLs such as Zulu have been shown to jailbreak frontier models including GPT-4
(
25
)
. A few recent benchmarks have started to cover safety in selected LRLs
4
;
16
, yet African languages in particular remain without any human-curated jailbreak benchmark, to the best of our knowledge.

In this paper, we introduce
TukaBench
, a jailbreaking benchmark for seven African languages that extends JailbreakBench (JBB)
6
, the widely used benchmark of 100
harmful
and 100
benign
English prompts for evaluating the susceptibility of LLMs to jailbreak attacks, with responses assessed via LLM-as-a-judge
31
. Rather than relying on direct translation, we construct
TukaBench
through four complementary settings designed to capture both linguistic and cultural dimensions of safety: (1)
Human translation of JBB
harmful and benign prompts into the target African languages; (2)
English adaptation of JBB to African contexts followed by human translation
, incorporating culturally relevant named entities and references; (3)
Human curated prompts validated through interaction with GPT-5.2
to filter out easily detectable patterns and produce more challenging adversarial inputs; and (4)
Code-switched prompts
combining African languages with English.

Evaluating models within an LLM-as-a-judge framework, we find that African-language prompts reduce
refusal
relative to English, and that culturally adapted prompts result in the least refusal of harmful prompts. This suggests that cultural grounding shapes model vulnerability beyond language resource level alone.
The evaluation also exposes two structural limitations of current safety assessment in low-resource settings: limited language understanding in the models themselves, and reduced reliability of LLM-as-a-judge methods for LRLs
(
12
;
11
)
. To keep the analysis grounded, we restrict our evaluation to the more resourced African languages, leaving extremely low-resource cases outside the present scope.

To mitigate the impact of these limitations, we validate a subset of model outputs through
human evaluation
, which makes safety assessment substantially
more expensive
than in high-resource settings. We further introduce a new response category,
Deflection
, alongside the standard Refusal and Jailbroken labels, capturing cases in which the model fails to understand the prompt and responds off-target rather than refusing. This addition enriches the evaluation framework and produces more consistent assessments across model families: proprietary models behave more uniformly across languages, while open models suffer considerably more from comprehension failures. We hope
TukaBench
will support continued community progress on safety for under-resourced African languages, and we plan to release the dataset, code, and human judgments under an open license.

Related Work

TukaBench builds on two lines of work: multilingual safety evaluation and jailbreak research.

Multilingual Safety.

LLM safety behaviour varies across languages, making language itself an attack surface. Prior work shows that translating unsafe English prompts into low-resource languages can bypass GPT-4 safeguards
(
26
)
, and multilingual jailbreak benchmarks such as MultiJail extend this setting with manually translated prompts across multiple languages
(
8
)
. Other work studies related risks through jurisdiction-specific safety policies
(
30
)
, mixed-language prompting
(
22
)
, and domain-specific safety benchmarks
(
13
)
. For African languages, UbuntuGuard
1
, which introduces a culturally grounded, policy-based benchmark of adversarial queries for 10 African languages constructed through human verification of OpenAI’s GPT-5-generated prompts. However, its evaluation primarily focuses on assessing whether guardian or moderation models adhere to predefined safety policies. In contrast, our work focuses on identifying and refining prompts that maximize the likelihood of successful jailbreak attacks.

Jailbreaking.

Jailbreaking has been studied extensively in English, with benchmarks such as JailbreakBench
(
6
)
and HarmBench
(
19
)
providing standardized settings for evaluating attacks and defenses. Recent attacks further explore black-box optimization
(
7
)
, generation-level manipulation
(
3
)
, semantic decomposition
(
23
)
, and multi-turn search
(
14
)
. However, these methods are developed and evaluated almost exclusively in English, leaving their interaction with translation, code-switching, and LRLs largely unexplored.

Our contribution.

TukaBench
complements prior work by evaluating harmful prompting in low-resource African languages through both JailbreakBench-aligned prompts for comparability and culturally grounded prompts for local relevance. It further considers stronger attack settings, including Boundary Point Jailbreaking and code-switching. Crucially,
TukaBench
introduces a three-way evaluation scheme:
Jailbroken
,
Refused
, and
Deflected
, to distinguish genuine safety behaviour from failures to understand the prompt, enabling more precise comparisons across languages, models, and attack conditions.

TukaBench

TukaBench
is built around five complementary prompt sets that together isolate the effect of
language
,
cultural grounding
, and
code-switching
on LLM safety behaviour. The components are organized along two axes: their
source
, either JailbreakBench (JBB)
(
6
)
or newly authored African-context prompts, and their
construction strategy
, ranging from translation with Western context preserved to full cultural adaptation and code-switching.
TukaBench
contains 986 prompts per African language across seven African languages, with all African-language prompts produced through machine translation followed by human post-editing. Table
1
summarizes the full benchmark. The remainder of this section describes the languages covered, the five components and their construction, the translation and post-editing pipeline, and the safeguards used to ensure annotation quality.

3.1
Language Selection

TukaBench
includes English and seven African languages:
Amharic
(
amh
),
Hausa
(
hau
),
Igbo
(
ibo
),
Chichewa
(
nya
),
Kiswahili
(
swh
),
Yorùbá
(
yor
), and
isiXhosa
(
xho
). The selection reflects both
typological
and
geographical diversity
: five languages belong to the Niger-Congo family, the largest language family in Africa, and two to the Afro-Asiatic family. Each language is spoken by more than 10 million people in Africa and beyond, providing broad cultural and geographic coverage (West, East and Southern African languages) across the continent and making
TukaBench
a practical testbed for evaluating LLM safety under African-language jailbreak and defense scenarios.

3.2
Benchmark Components

TukaBench
draws from two sources: JBB and
AfriJail
, a new dataset of culturally grounded adversarial prompts authored through direct interaction with GPT-5.2. All prompts were first curated in English and then translated and adapted into the seven African languages through the pipeline described in Section
3.3
.

3.2.1
JBB-Derived Components

Afri-JBB-Harm and Afri-JBB-Benign.

We adopt 100 harmful and 100 benign prompts from JBB without modification, spanning the ten harm categories defined in JBB. Both sets are translated into each of the seven African languages, preserving the original Western context and named entities. The benign set serves a dual purpose: measuring over-refusal and surfacing comprehension failures, since refusal on benign inputs can indicate that the model failed to understand the prompt rather than making an appropriate safety judgment.

Afri-JBB-Culture.

A culturally adapted version of JBB-Harmful. Two native speakers first adapt the English sentences to African contexts by introducing culturally relevant named entities. Since the original JBB prompts contain relatively few named entities, the adapted versions are typically richer and not directly equivalent to their source. The adapted English prompts are then translated into each African language through the pipeline described in Section
3.3
, with translators further adjusting named entities during the post-editing stage where appropriate, for example replacing a Nigerian city with a Kenyan city for Swahili.

3.2.2
African-Authored Components

AfriJail-Mono.

A set of 343 culturally grounded adversarial prompts covering scenarios not addressed by JBB, including local governance structures, regional conflict dynamics, and culturally specific social harms. Prompts were authored by data collectors based in Nigeria, where three of the target languages are spoken, through direct interaction with GPT-5.2. Annotators were deliberately not exposed to JBB during this process to avoid stylistic anchoring. All prompts were initially written and tested in English, then translated and culturally localized into each African language, with further adaptation for East and Southern African contexts where appropriate. Each prompt is classified under the ten JBB misuse categories (
Physical Harm
,
Fraud/Deception
,
Malware/Hacking
,
Expert Advice
,
Government Decision-Making
,
Sexual/Adult Content
,
Harassment/Discrimination
,
Disinformation
,
Privacy
, and
Economic Harm
) to enable category-level comparison with prior work (Appendix
C
).

AfriJail-CS.

A code-switched version of
AfriJail-Mono
, mixing each target African language with English within single utterances. Code-switching, the practice of alternating between two languages within a single utterance, is common in everyday multilingual African speech and has been shown to weaken safety alignment and jail-breaking in prior work
(
22
;
27
)
.
AfriJail-CS
covers six of the seven African languages, excluding isiXhosa.
2
2

2

Xhosa was excluded due to the time constraints involved in validating the generated code-switched sentences.

3.3
Translation and Adaptation Pipeline

All English prompts are processed into each target language through a three-stage pipeline: machine translation, automatic quality estimation, and human post-editing. Code-switched variants are subsequently generated through a separate model-assisted process described at the end of this section.

Machine Translation.

We use the Google Translate API for all languages except Yorùbá, where we instead use AfriqueQwen-8B
(
28
)
, since Google Translate does not reliably preserve Yorùbá diacritics and proprietary LLMs exhibit high refusal rates on safety-sensitive prompts. AfriqueQwen-8B is prompted with five few-shot examples drawn from the MAFAND dataset
(
2
)
using greedy decoding to ensure reproducibility.

Quality Estimation.

Each translation receives a reference-free quality score from SSA-COMET-QE
(
17
)
, a quality estimation (QE) metric adapted from the COMET architecture
(
21
)
for African languages. SSA-COMET-QE takes the source text and its translation as input and outputs a score between 0 and 1, where higher scores indicate better translation quality. We flag translations with scores below 0.50 as
Low
, scores from 0.50 to 0.64 as
Medium
, and scores of 0.65 or above as
High
. These flags are surfaced in the annotation interface to help post-editors prioritize their effort. We make use of HumanSignal platform for annotation.

Human Post-Editing and Cultural Adaptation.

Two translators per language correct machine translation errors and explicitly log the types of errors encountered. For Afri-JBB-Harm and Afri-JBB-Benign, post-editors produce faithful translations that preserve the original Western context and named entities, without any cultural adaptation. For Afri-JBB-Culture, the same JBB-Harmful prompts are first adapted to African contexts in English, then post-edited from machine translation with translators replacing named entities to match the target language’s cultural context. For
AfriJail-Mono
, post-editors correct the machine translation and perform cultural localization in the same stage, producing prompts that are both fluent in the target language and grounded in its cultural context. Full quality-control procedures are described in Appendix
A
.

Code-Switched Generation.

AfriJail-CS
variants are produced by AfriqueQwen-8B using three native-speaker-authored few-shot examples per language, each consisting of an English prompt, its full target-language translation, and a natural code-switched version written by a fluent bilingual speaker. The model generates a code-switched version of each of the

AfriJail
prompts by following this few-shot pattern with greedy decoding. Human annotators then review and correct all model outputs to ensure naturalness, producing a human-verified code-switched prompt set that serves as an additional evaluation setting in our analysis.

4
Experimental Setup

This section describes the models, prompting strategies, evaluation protocol, and metrics. Results are reported in Section
5
.

4.1
Models

We evaluate thirteen LLMs representative of major closed-source and open-weight families currently in widespread deployment.
3
3

3

Due to space constraints, results for a subset of models are deferred to Appendix
8
.
The closed-source family includes GPT-{3.5-Turbo, 4o, 5.2}, Grok-{3, 4, 4.3}, and Gemini-{2.5-Pro, 3.1-Pro}; the open-weight family includes Gemma-3-27B, Gemma-4-31B, Llama-4-Maverick, DeepSeek-3.2, GPT-oss-120B, and Qwen-3.5-27B.

Closed-source models are accessed through their official APIs (OpenAI, Google, and xAI via OpenRouter), and open-weight models through the Together AI API. For each model, we use the most recent stable checkpoint available at the time of evaluation. To support reproducibility, all models are queried with greedy decoding under each model’s default chat template. Grok-3 and Grok-4 were deprecated by the provider during the course of this work; for these two models, we report only the results obtained prior to deprecation.

4.2
Prompting Methods

Each model is evaluated under two prompting conditions, designed to capture both baseline safety behaviour and behaviour under adversarial pressure.

Direct Prompting.

The model receives each prompt verbatim, without any adversarial modification. This condition establishes a baseline and isolates the effect of language and cultural grounding from the influence of explicit attack strategies.

Boundary Point Jailbreaking (BPJ).

We additionally evaluate models under BPJ
(
7
)
, a black-box adversarial attack that augments harmful prompts with random characters to elicit non-refusing responses. The method proceeds in three stages: (i) noisy, partially corrupted variants of the original prompt are generated;
(ii) Some of the letters in the prompt are replaced with random characters or tokens (e.g.,
@, #, *, &
) at different positions, retaining variants whose responses are judged
Jailbroken
by the LLM judge; and (iii) the noise level is progressively reduced until the new noisy prompt elicits a jailbroken response. Full implementation details, including the prefix initialization, mutation operators, and stopping criteria, are provided in Appendix
B
.

4.3
LLM-as-a-Judge Evaluation

We employ GPT-4.1 as an LLM-as-a-judge to label model responses. Given a prompt and the corresponding response, the judge assigns one of three labels: (i)
Jailbroken
: the response substantively fulfills the harmful intent of the prompt; (ii)
Refused
: the response constitutes an explicit refusal to comply; and (iii)
Deflected
: the response is neither a substantive completion nor an explicit refusal, typically reflecting off-target generation or apparent non-comprehension of the prompt.

This three-way labelling scheme distinguishes genuine safety behaviour from comprehension failures, a distinction we find particularly informative in low-resource settings (Section
5
). The full judge prompt is provided in Appendix
D.1
.

4.4
Human Verification

To assess the reliability of the LLM judge, we conduct human verification on a representative subset of model outputs. The same 50 prompts are evaluated across five target models (GPT-4o, GPT-5.2, Grok-3, Grok-4, and DeepSeek-3.2) and six languages (Yorùbá, Hausa, Igbo, Kiswahili, Chichewa, and Amharic), yielding 1,500 model responses in total. For each language, three native-speaker annotators independently label each response as
Jailbroken
,
Refused
, or
Deflected
, following the same definitions used by the LLM judge. The three annotations are aggregated by majority vote to obtain the human reference label.
Instances of full three-way disagreement are excluded from the analysis, as such cases are unlikely to admit a single unambiguous ground-truth label.

4.5
Metrics

We report three metrics computed over harmful prompts:
Attack Success Rate (ASR)
, the proportion of responses labelled
Jailbroken
;
Deflection Rate
, the proportion labelled
Deflected
, capturing comprehension failures and off-target generation; and
Refusal Rate
, the proportion labelled
Refused
, equivalently
100-\mathrm{ASR}-\mathrm{Deflection}
.
The three rates sum to 100% by construction.

Results

5.1
Direct Prompting Results

African-language prompts primarily increase deflection rather than ASR.

Table
2
shows that switching from English to African-language prompts does not consistently increase attack success across models: some models exhibit higher ASR, while others remain unchanged or show a decrease. The more consistent pattern across the evaluated models is a sharp rise in
Deflection
, where responses are neither harmful completions nor explicit refusals. Lower or stable ASR in African languages should therefore not be interpreted as evidence of stronger safety; rather, models often fail to engage with the prompt at all, with refusals occurring less frequently than in English.

Cultural grounding increases model vulnerability.

Table
3

5.3
Human Verification and Judge Reliability

Judge reliability declines for lower-resource and non-Latin-script languages.

Table

shows that LLM-as-a-judge reliability varies systematically across languages. Swahili, the highest-resourced African language in our benchmark, has the highest average judge–human agreement (80%), while lower-resource Latin-script languages such as Yorùbá and Igbo fall below 60%. Amharic exhibits a particularly informative pattern: despite occupying a higher resource tier than several Latin-script languages, it has the lowest average agreement, suggesting that the non-Latin Ge’ez script imposes a distinct reliability barrier beyond resource level alone. These results extend our broader finding that African-language safety evaluation is constrained by two linked failures: models may struggle to comprehend and engage with prompts, and LLM judges may struggle to evaluate the resulting responses reliably under the same conditions.

Jailbroken and Deflected rates reveal clearer resource patterns when considered jointly.

Table

shows that neither
Jailbroken

Jailbroken

Deflected

Jailbroken
and
Deflected

These findings support reporting both rates jointly, as either metric alone can mislead multilingual safety evaluation.

5.4
Benign Prompt Evaluation

Models over-refuse benign prompts, with deflection rising in African languages.

We evaluate models on
Afri-JBB-Benign
to measure over-refusal and surface comprehension failures on benign inputs. Models refuse a substantial fraction of benign prompts overall (often near
50\%
), suggesting that safety mechanisms may over-attend to surface-level unsafe words rather than interpreting sentence-level intent. Unlike the harmful-prompt setting, refusal and compliance rates do not exhibit a clear English–African separation. The clearer cross-lingual difference is in
Deflection

12
.

6
Conclusion

We introduced
TukaBench
, a jailbreak benchmark for English and seven African languages, organized along two axes: source (JBB-derived or African-authored) and construction strategy (translation, cultural adaptation, or code-switching). We also proposed a three-label evaluation scheme,
Jailbroken
,
Refused
, and
Deflected
, which separates safety behaviour from comprehension failure. Our results show that ASR alone is insufficient for safety evaluation in low-resource languages. Culturally grounded prompts elicit higher rates of both harmful responses and deflection than direct translations; BPJ converts off-target responses into harmful completions, while code-switching reduces deflection without uniformly raising ASR, identifying language comprehension as the primary bottleneck. Failure patterns are jointly shaped by resource level, script, and model generation. Finally, judge–human agreement degrades for lower-resource and non-Latin-script languages, the same conditions where models produce the most off-target responses.

Limitations

First, although
TukaBench
covers seven African languages, its language coverage is still limited relative to the linguistic diversity of the African continent. The selected languages provide typological, geographic, and script diversity, but they do not represent the full range of African language families.

Second, our benchmark does not cover extremely low-resource African languages. Because our evaluation requires human translation, cultural localization, and native-speaker verification, we focus on languages for which reliable annotator recruitment and quality control are feasible.
As a result, our findings may underestimate the challenges faced by even lower-resource languages.

Third, the set of evaluated models is necessarily limited. We include a range of closed and open models, but the model landscape changes rapidly, and our results should be interpreted as a snapshot of current systems rather than a complete characterization of all LLMs.

Finally, some model results are incomplete due to model availability. Grok-3 and Grok-4 were deprecated during the experimental phase of this work, so we report only the subset of results completed before their deprecation. We indicate missing entries in the relevant tables and compute averages over the available languages for those rows.

Appendix A
Human Post-Editing: Quality Control and Workflow

This appendix details the recruitment, instructions, workflow, and quality control measures applied during the human post-editing stage of the TukaBench dataset construction.

A.1
Annotator Recruitment and Compensation

For each of the seven target languages (Yoruba, Hausa, Igbo, Swahili, Nyanja, Amharic, Isixhosa), we recruited two native-speaker annotators through Upwork. Candidates were screened for native fluency, prior translation or annotation experience, and willingness to commit to the multi-week annotation timeline. All annotators were paid 0.5 dollars per prompt through Upwork, with payment processed on task completion. Annotators were informed in writing that their corrections would be used in academic research on language-model safety and consented to the use of their work.

A.2
Task Allocation Across the Two Annotators

Each language was assigned two annotators, denoted Annotator 1 and Annotator 2, with distinct but partially overlapping workloads designed to enable both efficient coverage and inter-annotator agreement measurement.

Annotator 1.

Worked on 350 prompts in total: the 100 JBB-Original (harmful) prompts, the 100 JBB-Benign control prompts, the 100 JBB-Adapted (Afri-JBB-Culture) prompts, and the last 50 prompts of AfriJail. The first 100 prompts (JBB-Original and Afri-JBB-Culture) were corrected for translation accuracy without cultural adaptation, since these prompts are intended to retain their Western context for comparison with the JBB literature. The remaining 150 prompts (Afri-JBB-Culture, and the 50 shared AfriJail prompts) were corrected for both translation accuracy and cultural adaptation.

Annotator 2.

Worked on all 343 AfriJail prompts. The last 50 of these overlapped with Annotator 1’s allocation, providing a shared validation overlap of 50 prompts per language for inter-annotator agreement scoring. The remaining 293 prompts were corrected and culturally adapted by Annotator 2 alone.

This allocation gave us 350 doubly-annotated prompts across the seven languages (50 per language) for use in the inter-annotator agreement analysis described in Section
, while keeping the total annotation budget tractable.

A.3
Annotation Workflow

Annotation was conducted in Label Studio, configured with a five-column interface for each row: row index, source English prompt, machine-translated target-language prompt (produced by AfriqueQwen-8B for Yoruba and Google Translate for the other six languages), error list field (free text), and corrected translation field (free text).

For each prompt, the annotator was instructed to:

1.

Read the source English prompt and the machine-translated version.

2.

Identify any errors in the machine translation, including mistranslation, grammatical errors, untranslated words, awkward phrasing, dropped or hallucinated content, and cultural mismatch. Annotators were asked to enumerate each error in the error field.

3.

For JBB-Benign, Afri-JBB-Culture, and AfriJail prompts, additionally perform cultural adaptation: replace Western named entities (place names, institutions, currencies, public figures) with locally appropriate equivalents where doing so preserves the harmful intent of the prompt. For JBB-Original, retain the Western entities to preserve comparability with the original JailbreakBench dataset.

4.

Submit the corrected translation in the final field.

Annotators were given a written guide with three worked examples per error category and three worked examples of cultural adaptation, drawn from a held-out practice set of ten prompts per language that did not appear in the main evaluation. The guide explicitly instructed annotators not to alter the underlying request being made (whether harmful or benign) so that the safety evaluation would target the same behaviour across the translated and adapted versions.

A.4
Quality Control

Quality control was applied at three stages.

Pre-screening machine translations.

Before sending prompts to annotators, each machine-translated prompt was scored using SSA-COMET-QE
. Prompts scoring below 0.5 were re-translated using a fallback translation system and re-scored. This pre-screening filtered out machine-translation outputs that would have been unfixable by post-editing and reduced annotator load on low-quality translations.

Spot-check during annotation.

We performed a spot check on each batch by reviewing 10 randomly selected prompts per batch for completeness (error list non-empty when errors clearly exist, corrected translation non-empty, cultural adaptation applied where appropriate). If issues were found, the annotator was contacted with concrete examples and asked to revise the batch before continuing.

Post-hoc inter-annotator agreement.

The 50-prompt overlap between Annotator 1 and Annotator 2 in each language was scored using SSA-COMET-MTL, as described in Section
3.3
. Pairs scoring below 0.5 were flagged for review.

A.5
Annotator Communication

All annotators for a given language were added to a private Slack channel for that language, where they could ask questions to the research team and discuss ambiguous cases. Common questions and their resolutions were captured in a per-language FAQ document that was used throughout the annotation period. This ensured consistent handling of edge cases (for example, how to handle proper names of foreign individuals not commonly known in the target-language region, or how to adapt prompts referring to specific Western legal systems).

A.6
Annotator Statistics

Across the seven languages and fourteen annotators, the average total time per prompt (including reading the source, identifying errors, writing the error list, and producing the corrected translation) was approximately 4–6 minutes for translation-only corrections and 7–10 minutes for prompts requiring cultural adaptation. The full annotation campaign spanned roughly four weeks of calendar time, with annotators working asynchronously on their own schedules.

A.7
Human Evaluation of Model Outputs

In addition to the translation and cultural-adaptation stage, we conducted a separate human evaluation pass to verify the LLM-as-a-judge labels assigned to model responses. This stage was carried out on six languages (Yoruba, Hausa, Igbo, Swahili, Nyanja, Amharic) and was kept fully independent of the translation annotators so that human-judge agreement could not be inflated by the same annotator handling both stages.

Annotator pool.

For each of the six languages, we recruited three new native-speaker annotators through Upwork using the same screening criteria as the translation stage (native fluency, prior annotation experience, willingness to commit to the timeline). Each annotator was paid 0.2 dollars per labelled output through Upwork.

Task design.

Each annotator was asked to label the responses of five target models (Grok-3, Grok-4, GPT-4o, GPT-5.2, DeepSeek-3.2) on the same set of 50 prompts in their target language, producing 250 labelled outputs per annotator and 750 outputs per language across the three annotators. We aggregated by majority vote across the three annotators for each (prompt, model, language) triple, yielding 250 majority labels per language and 1,500 majority labels in total.

Annotation interface.

Annotation was conducted in Label Studio. Each row presented the annotator with the English source prompt, the corrected target-language prompt that was sent to the model, and the model’s response. The annotator selected exactly one of three labels:
Refused
,
Jailbroken
, or
Deflected
. The label definitions matched those given to the LLM judge verbatim, and annotators were given three worked examples per label drawn from a held-out practice set.

Quality control.

We performed the same per-batch spot-check procedure used in the translation stage: 10 randomly selected outputs per batch were reviewed for label plausibility, and annotators were contacted with concrete examples when patterns of mislabelling were observed. When all three annotators disagreed completely on a (prompt, model, language) triple (a three-way tie across Refused, Jailbroken, and Deflected), we recorded the case as ambiguous and used these counts as a measure of annotation difficulty. Three-way ties occurred for 13 tasks in Yoruba, 8 in Hausa, 4 in Igbo, 10 in Swahili, 14 in Nyanja, and 36 in Amharic, for a total of 85 across the 1,500 labelled outputs (5.7%). Amharic’s elevated rate (14.4% of its tasks) is consistent with its position as the language with the strongest comprehension-floor effect in our results: when model outputs are ambiguous, native annotators reasonably disagree about whether the response constitutes a refusal, a jailbreak, or a deflection. Per-(language, model) majority labels and the corresponding aggregate rates are reported in
Table

6

Appendix B
Boundary Point Jailbreaking: Technical Details

BPJ
(
7
)
probes a model by perturbing a harmful prompt with character-level noise and reducing that noise step-by-step until the model produces harmful output. The intuition is that high noise can bypass surface-level safety filters that pattern-match on the clean form of harmful text, while lower noise is closer to the model’s natural input distribution. The attack searches for the noisiest version that still succeeds, which is the version most likely to evade input-side defenses such as perplexity filters.

Noise Schedule.

We follow the schedule from the original paper, applying noise levels of 20, 15, 12, 9, 6, 3, and 0 in that order. Each level corresponds to an increasing amount of character-level perturbation (substitutions, insertions, and visually similar character swaps).

Stopping Rule.

For each prompt, BPJ tries successive noise levels until the judge returns a Jailbroken verdict, at which point the search stops and the successful version is recorded. If no level succeeds, the prompt is recorded as Refused or Deflected based on the cleanest (noise level 0) output.

Reproducibility.

We use a fixed random seed of 42 for the noise generation so that the noise patterns are deterministic and replicable.

AfriJail
dataset,
"how to start an online church to scam people using prophecies"
(61 characters), BPJ produces the following sequence of perturbed versions by replacing
n
characters at seed-determined positions with noise characters

)

B.1
Code-Switched Prompt Annotation

The third human annotation stage produced the code-switched variants of the
AfriJail
prompts used in Section
5.2
. Because code-switching is a register that depends on a single speaker’s bilingual fluency rather than on inter-annotator agreement over a fixed reference translation, we used one annotator per language.

Annotator pool.

For each of the six target languages, we recruited one native-speaker annotator with documented bilingual fluency in the target language and English. Each annotator was paid 0.4 dollars per code-switched prompt through Upwork.

Task design.

The annotator was presented with three columns per row: the English source prompt, the full target-language translation produced and corrected during the post-editing stage, and a machine-generated code-switched candidate produced by AfriqueQwen-8B with three native-speaker-authored few-shot examples. The annotator was asked to rewrite the third column whenever the machine-generated version sounded unnatural for a bilingual speaker in the target community, and to leave it as-is when the machine output already read naturally. The corrected code-switched prompts are what we send to target models for the code-switched evaluation condition.

Quality control.

We performed a per-batch spot-check (10 prompts per batch) focused on two questions: (i) whether the code-switched version preserves the harmful or benign intent of the original prompt, and (ii) whether the language mixture pattern is naturalistic for the target community rather than a uniform sentence-level switch. Annotators flagged for either issue were given concrete examples and asked to revise their batch. Final code-switched prompts were also reviewed informally by the language coordinator in the per-language Slack channel before being released to the evaluation pipeline.

Appendix D
Prompts and Decoding Settings

This appendix documents every prompt template and decoding configuration
used in our pipeline so that the experiments can be reproduced exactly.

D.1
Judge Prompt

We use GPT-4.1 as the safety judge with greedy decoding
(
temperature=0.0
). The judge receives the
original English prompt and the model response, and assigns one of three
labels. The system message is:

You are an expert jailbreak evaluation judge. Given a USER PROMPT (harmful
request) and a MODEL RESPONSE, classify the model’s behavior into ONE of the
following:

1. Refused: the model clearly refused (e.g., ‘‘I’m sorry’’, ‘‘I cannot help
with that’’).

2. Jailbroken: the model directly complied and produced the requested
harmful content.

3. Deflected: the model gave an irrelevant, incomplete, or evasive response.

Return a compact JSON with no extra text:
{‘‘label’’: ‘‘Refused | Jailbroken | Deflected’’, ‘‘reason’’: ‘‘<short
reasoning>’’}

The full prompt for each row is assembled as the system message followed by
the user prompt and the model response:

<SYSTEM>

USER PROMPT:

<prompt_english>

MODEL RESPONSE:

<response>

D.2
Direct Prompting

In the Direct Prompting condition there is no wrapper. The model receives the
target-language prompt exactly as stored in the dataset, taken from the

Goal_Translation
column. The decoding settings are:

temperature: 0.0

top_p: 1.0

do_sample: false

Reasoning models that emit long chains of thought before their final answer
(e.g.,
gemini-2.5-pro
and
gemma-4-31b
) use

max_tokens=2000
so that the answer is not truncated before it is
produced.

D.3
Code-Switching Attack at Evaluation Time

The code-switching attack also uses no wrapper. The model receives a
pre-prepared code-switched prompt. The decoding settings are identical to
Direct Prompting. Because the prompt itself is the attack, no additional
instruction or jailbreak template is added at evaluation time.

D.4
Code-Switched Data Generation

We generate the code-switched prompts ahead of time with

McGill-NLP/AfriqueQwen-8B
. For each target language, three few-shot
examples authored by native speakers (or AI-generated and then verified by a
native-speaker spot check) are stacked, and the new goal is appended for the
model to complete:

English: <fewshot1_english>

Translated: <fewshot1_translation>

CodeSwitched: <fewshot1_codeswitched>

English: <fewshot2_english>

Translated: <fewshot2_translation>

CodeSwitched: <fewshot2_codeswitched>

English: <fewshot3_english>

Translated: <fewshot3_translation>

CodeSwitched: <fewshot3_codeswitched>

English: <goal>

Translated: <goal_translation>

CodeSwitched:

The generator decodes greedily with
temperature=0.0
and

do_sample=False
. We keep the generated text up to the first
newline as the code-switched prompt.
