NAD+ modulates mitochondrial vulnerability in MERTK-associated models of retinitis pigmentosa
Authors: Lujia Feng, Yuwen Wen, Ting Zhang, Xiaohua Zhuo, Yantao Wei, Lixian Liu, Zhaohao Huang, Guoming Zhang, Lu Chen, Yong Du, Dong Fang, Wangting Li, Linbin Zhou, Ling Zhao, Yingting Zhu, Jia Liang, Zhiquan Li, Xiaojing Song, Caibin Deng, Xingxing Mao, Xiaofeng Hou, Wenru Su, Wei Chi, Yehong Zhuo, Shaochong Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRetinitis pigmentosa (RP) is the most common inherited retinal degenerative disease leading to blindness. RP is characterized by progressive loss of photoreceptors and retinal pigment epithelium (RPE), leading to retinal degeneration. The mechanisms that initiate RP and drive retinal vulnerability are poorly understood, and new strategies for preventing and treating RP are urgently needed. Although mitochondrial dysfunction initiates many neurodegenerative diseases, the contribution of mitochondrial dysfunction to RP is unclear. Single-cell RNA sequencing, transmission electron microscopy, and enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays revealed that photoreceptor and RPE cells have abnormal mitochondria in rats with RP. Nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+) metabolism decreased in rats with RP, increasing the vulnerability to disease-related insults. Similar experimental results were observed in a Mer tyrosine kinase receptor (MERTK)-associated RP primary human RPE cell model. Electroretinography, immunofluorescence, and fundus photography revealed that oral administration of the NAD+ precursor nicotinamide mononucleotide (NMN) protected rats with RP from retinal degeneration. Single-cell RNA sequencing, siRNA targeting, and Adeno-associated virus applications demonstrated that NMN elicits therapeutic effects via the glyceraldehyde-phosphate dehydrogenase-mitochondria pathway. These results indicate that mitochondrial abnormalities may be drivers of RP, and NMN elicits therapeutic effects on RP.Subject terms: Mechanisms of disease, Retinal diseases, Transcription

Introduction

Retinitis pigmentosa (RP) is a group of complex, multifactorial, degenerative retinal diseases characterized by the loss of photoreceptors and retinal pigment epithelium (RPE), leading to blindness1,2. RP is one of the most common retinal degeneration diseases worldwide, affecting more than 2.5 million people3. Currently, no effective prevention or therapies for this devastating disease are clinically available. Over the past several decades, studies have demonstrated that mutations in more than 100 genes are associated with RP2,4,5. Although damage to photoreceptors and RPE cells contributes to RP, the specific mechanisms that render photoreceptors and RPE cells more vulnerable to damage in patients with RP have not been identified4,6. Therefore, investigations into the pathophysiological mechanisms of RP are urgently needed to develop prevention strategies and treatments for this destructive disease.

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) are involved in the pathogenesis of RP7. Mitochondrial damage generates ROS, which may trigger mitochondrial dysfunction and amplify ROS production8. Mitochondria perform a variety of tasks, including the generation of adenosine triphosphate (ATP), Ca2+ buffering, and epigenetic signaling9. Mitochondrial dysfunction is a crucial factor in a variety of neurodegenerative diseases. Impaired mitochondria and energy metabolism disorders are significant features of aging and age-related neurodegeneration, including Alzheimer’s disease10 and Parkinson’s disease11. The retina contains abundant mitochondria, which support normal retinal physiological functions; mitochondria are involved in phototransduction in RPE cells and mediate photoelectric signal transduction in retinal neuroepithelial cells. Retinas are exposed to a high-oxygen environment, which is exacerbated by the loss of rods in RP; these conditions create a hyperoxic environment that is hostile to the remaining cells12. Mitochondrial dysfunction may be involved in retinal degeneration in RP5,13, and augmenting the mitochondrial damage defense systems may protect the retina in RP and other inherited retinal dystrophies.

Nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+) is a coenzyme for dehydrogenase enzymes that perform reduction-oxidation (redox) reactions, connecting glycolysis and the citric acid cycle to oxidative phosphorylation via the electron transport chain (ETC). Thus, NAD+ homeostasis plays a crucial role in maintaining mitochondrial function. Previous neurodegeneration studies suggest that perturbations in NAD+ homeostasis contribute to retinal neurodegeneration. In the Wds mouse model, a chimeric protein capable of enhancing NMNAT1 enzymatic activity and maintaining NAD+ homeostasis significantly delayed the progression of degenerative lesions induced by neuronal axonal injury; this chimeric protein contributed to the repair and growth of neuronal axons14. Glaucoma-prone mice exhibited retinal NAD+ deficiency with age, leading to mitochondrial abnormalities and, ultimately, retinal neuronal dysfunction; the retinal neuronal dysfunction was attenuated by supplementation with the NAD+ precursor nicotinamide (vitamin B3)15. Photoreceptors are highly specialized neurons that may rely on NAD+ homeostasis for survival and function16. However, the contribution of the NAD+-mitochondria pathway to the development of RP and dysfunction in RPE cells and photoreceptors is unclear and is the focus of our current research.

The Royal College of Surgeons (RCS) rat is a spontaneously occurring RP model. RCS rats develop RP due to a specific mutation in the MER tyrosine kinase (MERTK) gene17. The MERTK receptor is predominantly expressed in RPE cells of the rat retina, localized to the apical membrane surface. The MERTK receptor mediates phagocytosis of photoreceptor outer segment (POS) discs. Thus, MERTK plays an essential role in maintaining retinal physiological function18. The gene mutation in RCS rats causes functional defects in the encoded MERTK receptor, leading to impaired phagocytic clearance of POS discs by RPE cells. This phagocytic dysfunction triggers RP pathogenesis19. Notably, this MERTK mutation has been identified in human patients with RP17, highlighting the significant translational value of this animal model.

In this study, retina cells from RP rats (RCS rats) were analyzed using single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq). In addition, dysregulation of mitochondrial functions and metabolism, accompanied by decreased NAD levels in RPE and photoreceptor cells in the early stages of retinal degeneration, was verified. Oral administration of the NAD+ precursor nicotinamide mononucleotide (NMN) protected RP rats against retinal degeneration. The therapeutic effect of NMN on RP via the glyceraldehyde-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH)-mitochondria pathway was also verified. Taken together, our data indicate that mitochondrial dysfunction may be the driver of RP, and NMN has a therapeutic effect on RP, laying a strong foundation for the future development of treatments for RP.

Results

Mitochondrial dysfunction in photoreceptors and RPE cells is associated with progressive retinal damage in RP

The retina transcriptomes from RCS rats with a congenital mutation in MERTK, the human orthologue of the RCS rat retinal dystrophy gene17, and RDY rats (the control group for RCS rats) were analyzed using scRNA-seq (Fig. 1a). Single-cell suspensions were converted to barcoded scRNA-seq libraries using the 10X Genomics platform, and 17,759 cells were reserved for further analysis. Cells were clustered into 11 cell types based on their canonical lineage markers (Fig. 1b, Fig. S1a, b, and Table 1). Molecular changes in photoreceptor and RPE cells precede retinal degeneration in RP. Therefore, the genetic and functional transformation in both cell types is important. Our data revealed that RCS rats exhibited a marked decreasing trend in photoreceptors and VECs of retinas, with a particularly pronounced reduction in rod photoreceptors (from 74.72% to 25.28%) and cone photoreceptors (from 65.55% to 34.45%) (Fig. S1c, d). The differentially expressed genes (DEGs) are shown in Table S1. Subsequent Gene Ontology (GO) analysis revealed enriched pathways related to mitochondrial dysfunction in the two damaged cell types (RPE and photoreceptors), indicating that mitochondrial dysfunction may be the main pathological change in RP (Fig. 1c).

Mitochondrial respiratory complex gene analysis demonstrated mitochondrial dysfunction in the RPE and photoreceptor cells from RCS rats at postnatal day 51 (P51) (Fig. 1d, Table 2). Other retinal nerve cells, such as amacrine cells, also exhibited mitochondrial dysfunction in RP (Fig. S2a). A DEG analysis was conducted to compare photoreceptor cell and RPE gene expression between the RCS and RDY groups (Table 3). The pathway analysis of DEGs revealed that oxidative phosphorylation, the citric acid (TCA) cycle, respiratory electron transport in photoreceptor cells, and regulation of actin filament bundle and response to ATP were enriched in RPE from RCS rats, and phototransduction-related functions were downregulated in photoreceptor cells and RPE from RCS rats (Fig. 1e, g and Fig. S2b–d). This finding may be attributable to the higher number of mitochondria in cone photoreceptors, which confers greater resistance to oxidative stress than that observed in rod cells under equivalent stress conditions. This observation further supports that rod photoreceptors likely undergo earlier degeneration than cones during RP pathogenesis. These results suggest that mitochondria-related functions and metabolism may be impaired in the RPE and photoreceptor cells during the early stages of retinal degeneration in RCS rats.

To verify the mitochondrial dysfunction in RP, MERTK was knocked down in human primary retinal pigment epithelial (HsRPE) cells. The purity of HsRPE cells was determined by photographing the original cells, which contain brown pigment particles (Fig. S3a), and exhibited the characteristic polygonal morphology of RPE cells (Fig. S3b). Additionally, the HsRPE cells were stained with RPE65, BEST1, MERTK, OTX1, and PMEL17 (the hallmark proteins of the RPE20,21) (Fig. S3c). MERTK was silenced in HsRPE cells via transfection with MERTK small interfering RNA (siRNA). Real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) and western blot analysis showed that MERTK siRNA significantly downregulated MERTK expression in the HsRPE cells (Fig. S4a–c). Consistent with the results of the in vivo experiments, RNA sequencing of HsRPE cells showed that mitochondrial dysfunction-related pathways were abnormally upregulated in the HsRPE cells in the MERTK siRNA group (HsRPE model group) compared with the negative control group (Fig. S4d).

A previous study showed that dysfunctional POS disc phagocytosis in RPE cells contributes to RP in RCS rats22. Consistent with this previous study, RPE cells from RCS rats but not control rats exhibited reduced phagocytic ability towards POS discs (Fig. S4e). For the in vitro assay, we isolated porcine POS, conjugated them with FITC, and performed a phagocytosis assay to evaluate HsRPE phagocytic function. The results showed that the number of FITC-POS particles engulfed by HsRPE cells in the MERTK siRNA group was significantly reduced compared with the negative control groups, indicating decreased phagocytosis of HsRPE cells in the MERTK-associated RP model (Fig. S4f, g).

Mitochondrial dysfunction in the retina of RCS rats

To verify the results of RNA-seq, we examined mitochondrial structure in the RPE cells and photoreceptors of RCS and RDY rats at postnatal days 14 (P14), 21 (P21), 28 (P28), 51 (P51), and 70 (P70). Electron microscopy revealed that RCS rats exhibited mitochondrial vacuolization in RPE cells as early as P14 (Fig. 2a), while photoreceptors showed abnormal mitochondrial vacuolization at P21 (Fig. 2b). As the disease progressed from P14 to P70, mitochondrial vacuolization intensified, vacuolar changes also developed in the endoplasmic reticulum and Golgi apparatus of both RPE and photoreceptor cells (Fig. 2a, b). In RPE cells, vacuoles containing membranous debris appeared from P14 onward, and by P70, numerous lysosome-like vacuolar structures had formed (Fig. 2a). For the in vitro assay, in contrast to control HsRPE cells (NC siRNA), HsRPE cells transfected with MERTK siRNA also exhibited reduced cristae volume (Fig. 2c). To further investigate retinal mitochondrial function in RCS and RDY rats, we assessed energy metabolism (via adenosine triphosphate [ATP] levels and oxygen consumption rate [OCR]), mitochondrial membrane potential (using the JC-1 fluorescent dye), and ROS levels (using dihydroethidium). The results showed an age-dependent progression of mitochondrial dysfunction in RCS rats from P14 to P70, characterized by reduced ATP production (Fig. 2d), decreased OCR (Fig. 2e), an increased proportion of depolarized mitochondria (Fig. 2f), and elevated ROS (Fig. 2g). The total retinas and RPE cells from RCS rats also exhibited increased cytochrome c (CYCS) levels (Fig. 2h, j, k, l) from P14 to P70, indicating mitochondrial damage23, and increased levels of eukaryotic translation initiation factor 2 (EIF2), a key regulator of redox homeostasis and cellular adaptations to stress24 (Fig. 2i, j, m, n). These findings demonstrate that mitochondrial damage progressively worsens alongside retinal degeneration in RCS rats and that mitochondrial impairment in RPE cells precedes that in photoreceptor cells.

To investigate whether similar mitochondrial dysfunction exists in other RP models, we conducted experiments using the PDE6B mutation-associated RP model (Rd10 mice, P30) and performed single-cell RNA sequencing on their retinas. Unsupervised single-cell RNA sequencing of wild-type (C57BL/6 J, C57) and Rd10 retinas resolved 12 major retinal classes and reproduced canonical marker expression (Fig. S5a, b). Rd10 showed contraction of photoreceptor lineages, with rods most affected and cones least affected. A modest reduction in RPE representation was also observed (Fig. S5c). Volcano plots revealed widespread transcriptional remodeling in rods, cones, and the RPE (Fig. S5d, f). Across photoreceptors, phototransduction genes were broadly downregulated (Gnat1, Gngt1, Nrl, and Cnga1), whereas inflammatory and apoptotic programs were upregulated (Junb, Fos, and Hmgb1). In the RPE, metabolic gene expression was decreased (Ldha, Gapdh, and Aldoa), whereas that of immune-response genes increased (Junb, Fos). GO analysis indicated that rods exhibited enhanced stress and cell-death programs, including death-receptor, MAPK, and receptor tyrosine kinase signaling, oxidative-stress responses, and apoptosis, together with declines in phototransduction, outer-segment organization, visual perception, and neurotransmission (Fig. S5g, j). Cones showed a similar pattern, with enrichment of stress and programmed cell-death pathways and reductions in phototransduction, cone differentiation, synaptic-receptor signaling, calcium handling, nicotinamide-nucleotide metabolism, and ATP metabolic processes (Fig. S5h, k). The RPE displayed selective elevation of cellular stress and apoptotic signaling and decreased expression of pathways supporting phagocytic recognition and maturation, cytoskeleton-dependent intracellular transport, mitochondrial transport along microtubules, neurotransmitter transport, and ADP catabolism, indicating impaired trafficking and phagosome processing (Fig. S5i, l). Furthermore, we employed TEM to examine the mitochondrial ultrastructure in the photoreceptor and RPE cells of Rd10 mice. Despite the onset of retinal degeneration in Rd10 mice, the mitochondrial morphology in both photoreceptor and RPE cells showed no significant alterations compared with the control group (Fig. S5m, n).

Our results reveal that not all forms of RP are accompanied by mitochondrial dysfunction at disease onset, nor is mitochondrial impairment necessarily a universal driver of disease progression across all RP models. However, in the RCS rat model, mitochondrial perturbations are the most common changes occurring within the RPE and photoreceptor cells in the early stages of retinal degeneration in RCS rats.

Retinal NAD+ levels are decreased in RP rats

Based on these results, we assessed the metabolites in the retinas of RCS and RDY rats. Mitochondrial energy metabolism depends on nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+), an essential cofactor for respiratory and TCA cycle enzymes24,25. Based on pathway analysis of upregulated DEGs, NADH-relevant pathways were enriched in photoreceptor and RPE cells (Fig. 3a). Consistent with the in vivo results, RNA sequencing showed that NAD +-related pathways were significantly upregulated in HsRPE models compared to the negative control groups (Fig. 3b). NAD+ and GSH, which are central to healthy mitochondrial metabolism and protection from oxidative stress, were also significantly decreased in RCS rats compared with RDY rats (Fig. 3c, e). A similar decrease was observed in HsRPE cells transfected with MERTK siRNA compared with that in control cells (Fig. 3d, f). To further investigate potential disruptions in NAD+ metabolism, we quantified key upstream and downstream metabolites—nicotinamide (NAM), nicotinamide mononucleotide (NMN), and nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADP). The results revealed a progressive decline in the levels of NAM, NMN, and NADP with advancing age in RCS rats (Fig. 3g–i). These decreases are expected to sensitize the retina to disease-related metabolic stress and mitochondrial dysfunction15,26, and suggest that metabolic stress levels are higher in the retinas of RCS rats compared with controls. To investigate the cause of the decline in NAD+ and its metabolites, we examined the expression of key NAD+-synthesizing enzymes and key NAD+-consuming molecules in the retinas of RCS rats. The expression of NAD+-synthesizing enzymes NMN adenylyltransferase 1–3 (Nmnat1–3, which synthesize NAD from NMN) was not suppressed (Fig. S6b–d). In contrast, the expression of nicotinamide phosphoribosyltransferase (Nampt, which converts NAM to NMN), the rate-limiting enzyme in NAD+ biosynthesis, was significantly downregulated (Fig. S6a). Furthermore, none of the major NAD+-consuming molecules—poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase 1 (Parp1), cluster of differentiation 38 (CD38), or Sirtuins 1–7 (SIRT 1–7)—showed significant upregulation (Fig. S6e–m). Therefore, these findings suggest that the observed reduction in NAD+ levels may be attributed to the co-occurrence of NAM deficiency and suppressed Nampt expression.

Previous studies support a role for NAD+ in neuroprotection. Recent studies have revealed key roles for NAD+ and related metabolites in neuronal adaptation to physiological stressors and neurodegenerative diseases, such as Alzheimer’s, Parkinson’s, and Huntington’s diseases, and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis27–30. Our results indicate that RCS rats experience mitochondrial stress and metabolite depletion, supporting a model in which declines in NAD+ and GSH levels in the retina render RCS rats vulnerable to RP. Thus, increasing NAD+ levels may protect RCS rats from RP by decreasing the probability of metabolic/energetic failure, making the photoreceptor and RPE cells more resilient to mitochondrial dysfunction stress. In the retinas of RCS rats, despite NAM deficiency coupled with suppressed Nampt expression, the expression of downstream NAD+-synthesizing enzymes Nmnat1–3 remains unaffected. Therefore, it can be inferred that, mechanistically, exogenous NMN supplementation could bypass the Nampt-mediated rate-limiting step and effectively restore NAD+ levels. Moreover, oral supplementation with NMN rectified disturbances in NAD+ metabolism in several animal models of degenerative diseases31–33.

NMN supplementation prevents RP development in rats

To determine the effects of NMN, we administered NMN (1000 mg/kg/d) intragastrically to RCS rats from P21 (when most eyes start developing RP34) for 1, 2, 3, and 4 weeks (All animals were euthanized on P51, which is a representative stage for assessing retina degeneration in this RP model35). The decline in NAD+ and GSH levels was prevented in RCS rats after 4 weeks of NMN administration (Fig. 4a, b). To investigate whether NMN supplementation improves mitochondrial function, we assessed ATP levels and OCR, mitochondrial membrane potential, and ROS. The results showed that 4 weeks of NMN administration enhanced ATP production (Fig. 4c), OCR (Fig. 4d), and mitochondrial membrane potential (Fig. 4e) in the retinas of RCS rats, while reducing ROS levels (Fig. 4f). Fundus photography and optical coherence tomography (OCT) were performed at 1, 2, 3, and 4 weeks after NMN intervention to evaluate retinal degeneration in the superior, inferior, temporal, and nasal quadrants. The results indicated that prolonged NMN administration was associated with a progressively stronger attenuation of retinal thinning, as evidenced by preserved total retinal thickness and outer nuclear layer (time-dependentin RCS rats (Fig. 4g–k). Consistent with the OCT findings, H&E staining confirmed that NMN treatment exerted a time-dependent therapeutic effect, with enhanced attenuation of retinal degeneration observed in both the total retina and the ONL at various distances from the optic disc (Fig. 4l–n). Electroretinography (ERG) assessments under both photopic and scotopic conditions across multiple stimulus intensities demonstrated that prolonged NMN administration progressively prevented the decline in a- and b-wave amplitudes, indicating a protective effect of NMN on visual function (Fig. 4o–r). In addition, 4 weeks of NMN administration prevented retinal thinning and photoreceptor cell loss in RCS rats (Fig. 4s–w). EM revealed that 4 weeks of NMN administration inhibited the formation of dysfunctional mitochondria with abnormal cristae (Fig. 4w) and increased phagocytosis in RPE cells from RCS rats (Fig. 4x). These results highlight the unexpected potency of NMN in decreasing metabolic disruption in RP and suggest that NMN can protect photoreceptor and RPE cells from the pathogenic process in RP. Therefore, NMN has great potential for the treatment of RP. However, human studies are required to verify our findings.

NMN increased the phagocytosis of RPE cells

Consistent with the results of our in vitro experiments, 1 mM NMN attenuated the decreased cell viability induced by transfection with MERTK siRNA (Fig. 5a); therefore, 1 mM NMN was applied in the subsequent experiments. NMN treatment prevented the decline in NAD and GSH levels (Fig. 5b, c) and the increase in CYCS and EIF2 levels induced by transfection with MERTK siRNA in HsRPE cells (Fig. 5d–f). Moreover, NMN treatment inhibited the formation of dysfunctional mitochondria with abnormal cristae (Fig. 5g). NMN prevented the decreases in cofilin (CFL), p-cofilin (p-CFL), and F-actin (ACTF) expression (three essential phagocytic proteins) induced by transfection with MERTK siRNA (Fig. 5h–k) and reconstructed the arrangement of F-actin, which was destroyed in the MERTK-associated RP model (Fig. 5l). NMN treatment attenuated the reduction in phagocytosis in the HsRPE cells transfected with MERTK siRNA (Fig. 5m, n). Together, these results indicate that oral supplementation with NMN rescues retinal degeneration in RPE cells.

NMN prevents RP development by suppressing mitochondrial dysfunction via the NAD+/GAPDH pathway

To fully understand the effects of NMN treatment, scRNA-seq was performed in photoreceptor cells and the RPE cells from RCS rats after NMN administration. Among the widely regulated genes, we identified LOC108351137 (glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase, Gapdh), which was upregulated in both photoreceptor cells and the RPE after NMN treatment (Fig. 6a). NMN effectively rescued and upregulated the visual and sensory functions of photoreceptor cells during phototransduction (Fig. 6b). The scRNA-seq results were verified with western blotting and immunofluorescence by detecting the expression of GAPDH. The results showed the expression of GAPDH decreased in the RPE cells and the whole retina from RCS rats (Fig. 6c–g), but NMN treatment significantly enhanced GAPDH expression (Fig. 6h, i). GAPDH plays an essential role in metabolism by catalyzing the conversion of D-glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate to 1,3-diphosphoglycerate using NAD+ as a cofactor36,37.

Ongoing stress reduces GAPDH expression, and decreases in GAPDH levels may increase vulnerability to retinal degeneration. GAPDH activity is inhibited in the brains of patients with Alzheimer’s disease and varies with stress38,39. Changes in GAPDH activity may contribute to individual differences in vulnerability to retinal degeneration40,41. To verify the therapeutic effect of NMN on RP via the GAPDH pathway, Gapdh adeno-associated viruses (Gapdh-AAV) or Gapdh siRNA were injected intravitreally to up- or down-regulate the expression of GAPDH in the retinas of rats. We first evaluated the biosafety of intravitreal Gapdh siRNA and Gapdh-AAV injection. The histopathological analysis of liver and kidney tissues one month post-injection revealed no significant pathological alterations in either treatment group compared with the normal control group (Fig. S7a). Furthermore, complete blood count, blood biochemistry, and electrolyte tests showed no remarkable abnormalities in the Gapdh siRNA and Gapdh-AAV injected groups (Tables S2–4). Fluorescence analysis revealed a progressive increase in signal intensity from 1 to 4 weeks post-Gapdh-AAV injection. Expression intensity peaked at week 4, followed by a decline at week 5 (Fig. S7b, c). Immunofluorescence staining for GAPDH expression revealed that at 4 weeks post-injection, compared with the negative control group, the Gapdh siRNA group showed a significant reduction in GAPDH levels in the retina, whereas the Gapdh-AAV group exhibited a marked increase (Fig. S7d, e). To investigate whether Gapdh-AAV treatment improves mitochondrial function, we assessed ATP levels and OCR, mitochondrial membrane potential, and ROS. The results showed that Gapdh-AAV enhanced ATP production (Fig. S7f), OCR (Fig. S7g), and mitochondrial membrane potential (Fig. S7h) in the retinas of RCS rats, while reducing ROS levels (Fig. S7i). Fundus photography and OCT revealed a ruddier retina in RCS rats. Furthermore, retinal atrophy was inhibited after NMN or Gapdh-AAV treatment; however, Gapdh siRNA inhibited the protective effects of NMN in the superior, inferior, temporal, and nasal quadrants of the whole retina and the ONL (Fig. 6j–n). Consistent with the OCT findings, H&E staining showed both NMN and Gapdh-AAV treatment prevented photoreceptor cell loss and retinal thinning, indicative of RP, but Gapdh siRNA blocked the therapeutic effects of NMN and enhanced the retinal degeneration observed in both the total retina and the ONL at various distances from the optic disc (Fig. 6o–q). Furthermore, ERG assessments under both photopic and scotopic conditions across multiple stimulus intensities demonstrated that NMN and Gapdh-AAV treatments conferred protective effects on visual function. Both NMN and Gapdh-AAV administration effectively prevented the reduction of a- and b-wave amplitudes, whereas Gapdh siRNA abolished the therapeutic benefits of NMN (Fig. 6r–u). Both NMN and Gapdh-AAV treatment rescued the formation of dysfunctional mitochondria with abnormal cristae in the photoreceptor and RPE cells of the retina, but Gapdh siRNA treatment prevented the therapeutic effects of NMN (Fig. 6v). More importantly, both NMN and Gapdh-AAV treatment increased the phagocytosis of POS by RPE cells, but Gapdh siRNA treatment reversed the effects of NMN on phagocytosis (Fig. 6w). Overall, these results suggest that the therapeutic effects of NMN in RCS rats are mediated by the GAPDH pathway.

To understand the mechanism of NMN treatment, the expression of GAPDH was up-regulated or down-regulated in HsRPE cells using Gapdh siRNA or Gapdh lentivirus (Gapdh-Le), respectively, in the MERTK-associated RP model (Fig. S8a–c). The transmission EM results showed that both NMN and Gapdh-Le treatment prevented the formation of dysfunctional mitochondria with abnormal cristae in the MERTK siRNA-treated group. In contrast, down-regulating GAPDH with Gapdh siRNA attenuated the effects of NMN on mitochondria (Fig. S8d). Both NMN and Gapdh-Le treatment improved phagocytic activity (Fig. S8e, f) and increased CFL, p-CFL, and ACTF expression in HsRPE cells transfected with MERTK siRNA (Fig. S8g–j). In contrast, the downregulation of GAPDH by Gapdh siRNA in the NMN-treated group prevented the NMN-enhanced phagocytic activity and attenuated the increased expression of CFL, p-CFL, and ACTF induced by NMN and Gapdh-Le. These results indicate that GAPDH treatment increased phagocytosis in RPE cells.

In conclusion, we showed that dietary supplementation with NMN significantly reduces retinal vulnerability to RP by supporting mitochondrial health and metabolism (Fig. 7). We believe that combining NMN (and/or GAPDH gene therapy) with previously established medications may protect against RP. Our results provide the molecular and metabolic link between increased retinal vulnerability and RP. Thus, the results are of critical importance for the treatment of RP and possibly other retinal degenerative diseases.

Discussion

RP, a consequence of multiple molecular interactions, developed complex strategies to cope with environmental stress. Mitochondria are often activated in response to retinal stresses to maintain normal cellular function. NAD+ plays an important role in the mitochondria and the retina. A growing number of studies have demonstrated that mitochondrial dysfunction is involved in RP5,13. However, to date, no reports have shown that NAD+ is involved in RP or which mitochondria-related pathway is dysfunctional during the progression of RP. Our study focused on the molecular mechanisms of the NAD+-mitochondria pathway related to GAPDH expression in the development of RP. We detected abnormal mitochondrial metabolic pathways, respiratory complexes, and NAD+-related metabolic pathways in RPE and photoreceptor cells in the early stage of RP. Mitochondrial structure was destroyed, and inflammation-related CYCS and EIF2 proteins were highly expressed in the RPE of RP rats. Surprisingly, NMN treatment prevented mitochondrial and phagocytocytic dysfunction in RPE cells and prevented retinal degeneration in RCS rats. Importantly, scRNA sequencing analysis after NMN treatment revealed that NMN rescued the visual function of RCS rats by restoring the expression of GAPDH protein in RPE and photoreceptor cells (Fig. 6).

Healthy mitochondria in RPE and photoreceptor cells are essential for maintaining physiological function. Mitochondria produce ATP via oxidative phosphorylation via the transfer of electrons between multi-subunit complexes (I to IV) of the ETC coupled with the reduction of molecular oxygen. Oxygen consumption in photoreceptors and RPE cells is high due to their high ATP demand, and photoreceptors use oxidative phosphorylation and glycolysis to produce ATP42. Phagocytosis is one of the daily functions of RPE cells and predisposes these cells to altered mitochondrial redox states and increased mtDNA damage. The abnormal structure or function of mitochondria in photoreceptor cells and RPE cells may lead to the development of RP. The mtDNA ATPase6 gene (m.8993 T > G) is one of the mutations contributing to RP. This mutation results in the replacement of the highly conserved Leu156 with Arg (Leu156Arg) of the ATPase6-encoded proton channel, resulting in increased electron leakage, ROS production, and oxidative damage to cell structures43. Autosomal dominant RP is associated with the generally controlled nonrepressed kinase; proteomic and bioinformatics analyses identified 49 proteins that were associated with mitochondria as generally controlled nonrepressed kinase-dependent44. Studies in murine photoreceptor cells demonstrated that protecting mitochondria from damage reduced photoreceptor cell death in cells and organ cultures45. We show for the first time that the structure and function of mitochondria in the photoreceptor cells and RPE cells of RCS rats are destroyed at an early stage, and these changes are accompanied by abnormalities in the mitochondrial respiratory complex signaling pathway. These results indicate that mitochondrial damage may be the initiator of retinal degeneration.

NAD+ is an essential coenzyme for maintaining mitochondrial function. NAD provides oxidoreductive power for ATP generation in the mitochondria and plays a pivotal role in preserving redox balance within cells. Thus, decreased NAD+ levels lead to ATP failure, disruption of intracellular redox balance, and dysfunction of proteins that use NAD+ as a coenzyme. NAD+ plays an important role in the pathogenesis of neurodegenerative diseases, and abnormalities in the NAD+ signaling pathway may be involved in RP. Hartong. et al. demonstrated hereditary neurodegeneration of rod and cone photoreceptors in the retina of mice. The affected mice were homozygotes for loss-of-function mutations in the IDH3B gene encoding the beta subunit of NAD-specific isocitrate dehydrogenase46. Jaliffa et al. demonstrated that rd10 mice with retinal degeneration harbored changes in Sirt 1, a NAD(+)-dependent deacetylase. The pathologic pattern of SIRT 1 immunoreactivity correlated with the start of retinal degeneration in rd10 mice47. Ozaki demonstrated that activation of Sterile alpha and Toll/interleukin-1 receptor motif–containing 1 causes cell death, contributing to photoreceptor cell degeneration. Cell death was induced by mitochondrial decoupler carbonyl cyanide m-chlorophenyl hydrazone and NAD+ destruction because endogenous Sterile alpha and Toll/interleukin-1 receptor motif–containing 1 consumed NAD+ in photoreceptor cells48. However, whether NAD+ can regulate mitochondrial function in rats with RP is unclear.

Consistent with previous studies, we demonstrated that NAD+ levels were decreased in the retinas of RCS rats. The molecular mechanisms of decreased NAD+ are still unclear. Nevertheless, according to previous studies, impaired phagocytosis of POS by RPE cells leads to elevated ROS levels in both the RPE and photoreceptor cells during RP pathogenesis49. Increased ROS disrupts NAD+ metabolism, resulting in reduced NAD+ levels50. The decline in NAD+ disrupts the intracellular redox balance, leading to decreased mitochondrial membrane potential, reduced ATP production, and structural damage to the mitochondria51. Although cone photoreceptors contain more mitochondria than rods, rod cells also contain substantial mitochondria; thus, NMN reduction significantly impacts mitochondrial function in both rod and cone photoreceptors. Additionally, previous studies postulated that NAD+ deficiency is driven by a combination of (i) increased activity of NAD-consuming enzymes, especially PARP1 and CD38, and (ii) compromised NAD+ biosynthesis, which is primarily caused by decreased NAMPT-mediated NAD+ salvage and a decrease in the activity of the mitochondrial enzyme NMNAT352. In our study, upstream and downstream metabolites of NAD+, including NAM, NMN, and NADP, were significantly downregulated with advancing age in RCS rats. The expression of NAD+-synthesizing enzymes Nmnat1–3 was not suppressed. In contrast, the expression of Nampt was significantly downregulated. Furthermore, none of the major NAD+-consuming molecules—Parp1, CD38, or SIRT 1–7—showed significant upregulation. These findings suggest that a combination of NAM deficiency and suppressed Nampt expression may underlie the observed reduction in NAD+ levels. NAD+ levels can be boosted by inhibition of NAD+ consumption and stimulation of NAD biosynthesis. The therapeutic potential of the NAD+ precursors nicotinamide riboside (NR) and NMN have been explored in a variety of clinical trials. In contrast to niacin, which induces a painful flushing reaction, neither NMN nor NR causes adverse effects53, even after a single oral dose of 0.5 g of NMN. In our study, despite NAM deficiency coupled with suppressed Nampt expression, the expression of downstream NAD+-synthesizing enzymes Nmnat1–3 remained unaffected. Thus, it can be inferred that mechanistically, exogenous NMN supplementation could bypass the Nampt-mediated rate-limiting step and effectively restore NAD+ levels. Therefore, we treated RCS rats with MNM to elevate the NAD+ levels in the retina. NMN supplementation was found to restore mitochondrial morphology and markedly increase ATP levels.

Previous studies have indicated that MERTK mutations impair POS phagocytosis by RPE cells, which is the fundamental cause of RP in RCS rats49. Phagocytosis is a dynamic process that requires continuous cytoskeletal remodeling within cells to facilitate POS binding, engulfment, and internalization—all of which depend on mitochondrial energy supply. Insufficient energy supply can impair phagocytic efficiency54. Therefore, mitochondrial dysfunction can lead to impaired phagocytic activity. Both our in vivo and in vitro studies demonstrated that MERTK mutations cause mitochondrial structural damage and dysfunction in RPE cells, resulting in a significant reduction in ATP production. Surprisingly, NMN supplementation led to enhanced RPE phagocytosis of POS. Given these findings, we hypothesize that NMN may rescue RPE phagocytic function by repairing mitochondrial morphology and improving mitochondrial function. Most importantly, NMN protected RP rats against retinal degeneration.

GAPDH, a well-known housekeeping protein, is a phosphorylation enzyme in the glycolytic pathway that converts glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate to 1,3-bisphosphoglycerate and produces NADH. NADH is involved in the generation of ATP via the ETC in the mitochondria. GAPDH is involved in many processes, including glycolysis, translational silencing, transcriptional regulation of specific genes, and nitric oxide sensing. The precise involvement of GAPDH in these different roles is unclear, but it may involve specific posttranslational protein modifications. However, the modification site and molecular consequences are not known. Previous studies demonstrated that GAPDH plays a significant role in mitochondrial damage induced by NAD+ deficiency. NAD+ deficiency may induce mitochondrial damage via two mechanisms. (1) NAD+ prevents glycolytic inhibition at the GAPDH step. NAD+ and NADH are crucial coenzymes for GAPDH activity, and depletion of NAD+ in the cytoplasm can lead to glycolytic blockade. Glycolytic blockade limits the supply of pyruvate, which is essential for driving the mitochondrial TCA cycle. (2) Hypoxia-induced NAD+ depletion may lead to increased levels of Bnip3 in mitochondria, leading to mitochondrial alterations. Additionally, Malhotra et al. demonstrated that NAD+ is an effector in the formation and reactions of GAPDH55. The downregulation of GAPDH expression plays a significant role in the progression of various retinal diseases. For example, the expression of GAPDH is decreased in the retina after ischemia and reperfusion injury, and overexpression of GAPDH prevents ischemia and reperfusion injury-induced oxidative stress and ATP reduction by inhibiting necrosis and DNA damage56. During hyperglycemia, GAPDH in retinal microvascular cells is inhibited by covalent modifications, leading to the activation of multiple pathways that contribute to the pathogenesis of diabetic retinopathy57. At the moment of the extinction of the ERG response, GAPDH activity is suppressed in the retina in a rabbit model of RP58. Consistent with previous studies, our data showed that GAPDH was down-regulated in the retina of RCS rats and was the only gene upregulated in both photoreceptor cells and RPE cells after NMN treatment. Surprisingly, the downregulation of GAPDH expression blocked the therapeutic effects of NMN in RCS rats, and upregulating the expression of GAPDH by Gapdh-AAV protected RP rats against retinal degeneration. These results indicate that NMN elicits therapeutic effects in RCS rats by increasing the expression and activity of GAPDH.

Our study has several limitations due to the focus on the MERTK mutation RP model. Although we included the PDE6B mutation RP model (rd10 mice), our investigations using this model are preliminary. Since the experimental design was confined to single-cell RNA sequencing of retinal tissues at the typical onset stage (P30), without including earlier or later time points, the comprehensiveness of our conclusions may be limited. Consequently, our research does not encompass all RP-causing mutations. To address this, future studies should incorporate additional models (e.g., RHO or USH2A mutations) to systematically evaluate the universality of mitochondrial dysfunction and the therapeutic potential of NMN-based strategies across diverse RP genetic backgrounds.

In summary, our results demonstrate, for the first time, that mitochondrial abnormalities may be the drivers of RP, and NMN has therapeutic effects on RP. Moreover, our findings highlight the NAD+/GAPDH signaling pathway as a promising therapeutic target for treating RP. These findings open the perspectives concerning the relationship between NAD+, mitochondria, and RP, and provide the framework for future studies regarding the molecular mechanisms underlying the pathogenesis of RP.

Methods

Animal strains and experimental procedures

All animal breeding and experimental procedures were performed in accordance with the Association for Research for Vision and Ophthalmology Statement for the Use of Animals in Ophthalmic Research. The rat study was approved by the animal ethics committee of Zhongshan Ophthalmic Center, Sun Yat-sen University (Guangzhou, China; approval No. ID:2020-127). RCS rats (rdy+p+) and RDY rats (rdy+p-) were obtained from the Third Military Medical University (Chongqing, China). The mouse study was approved by the animal ethics committee of Shenzhen Eye Hospital, Shenzhen Eye Medical Center, Southern Medical University (Shenzhen, China; approval No. ID:2025-0108). The Rd10 and C57BL/6 J mice were generously provided by Professor Xiaohe Yan (Shenzhen Eye Hospital, Shenzhen Eye Medical Center, Southern Medical University). The rats and mice were housed under the following conditions: room temperature 23 ± 2 °C; relative humidity 60 ± 10%, a 12 h light/dark cycle, and food and water available ad libitum59. RCS rats were treated with NMN in drinking water from day (d) 21 (the early stage of retinal degeneration). NMN (Sigma Aldrich, CAS No. 1094-61-7, 1000 mg/kg/d) was dissolved in regular drinking water. Control rats received equal amounts of water with no NMN.

Isolation of retinas for single-cell RNA sequencing

The rats (P51) and mice (P30) were euthanized, and the eyes were enucleated and immediately placed in ice-cold HBSS. The retinas were dissected from the eyes. For scRNA-seq analysis, the neurosensory retina of one rat/mouse was combined with the RPE layers of eight rats/mice. Each group contained two samples (n = 2), collectively comprising the retinal neuroepithelial layer from two rats/mice and the RPE layer from 16 rats/mice. After adding 1 ml of papain (3 units/mL) from the Single-cell extraction kit (Worthington Biochemical), the retinas were incubated for 10 min at 37 °C, followed by centrifugation for 5 min at 300 × g. The supernatant was removed, and fresh papain was added to the precipitate and incubated for 10 min at 37 °C. After centrifuging at 300×g for 5 min, 600 μL of a solution containing 2.7 mL EBSS, 300 μL ovomucoid inhibitor, 150 μL DNase, and 1 mL of coid inhibitor was added to the retina precipitate. The mixture was centrifuged for 5 min at 70 × g. Subsequently, 1 mL of pre-cooled RPMI 1640 medium was added to the precipitate, and the precipitate was filtered (40 μm filter). The resulting supernatant was a retinal single-cell suspension with a uniform cell size, cell mass, and fragments < 5%. The Countess®II Automated Cell Counter was used to determine cell viability and cell count. Samples with viability ≥ 85% and cell concentration ≥ 1 × 106 cells/mL were collected for scRNA-seq.

Single-cell RNA sequencing

Single-cell suspensions from retina samples were converted into barcoded scRNA-seq libraries using the Chromium Single Cell 5 library, Gel Bead, and Multiplex Kit, and the Chip Kit (10x Genomics, Pleasanton, CA, USA). The scRNA libraries were prepared according to the manufacturer’s instructions using a Chromium Single Cell 5 v2 Reagent kit (cat. No. 120237; 10x Genomics). Quality checks were performed using FastQC software. Sequencing data were processed using Cell Ranger 5.0.1 software. The R package Seurat 4.1.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) was used for data integration and preliminary collation60. Quality control was performed using a filter criteria of 200–8000 genes and < 20% of mitochondrial genes. Differential expression analysis was performed using the Wilcoxon test, implemented in the “FindAllMarkers” function of the Seurat R package, for each cell type. DEGs between the two groups were identified using the “FindMarkers” function in Seurat, with the following criteria: log2 fold change > 0.25, adjusted P < 0.05, and > 5% of cells in either test group.

Gene functional annotation

The Gene Ontology (GO) analysis of DEGs was performed using the Metascape webtool61. Among the top 50 enriched GO terms, we selectively curated RCS-related or rescue-DEG GO terms or pathways using the ggplot2 R package62. Individual cells were assessed for gene expression of biological functions via scoring based on the mean normalized expression of relevant genes.

Isolation of human RPE cells

Human donor eyes were obtained from the Guangdong Eye Bank in accordance with the 2013 Declaration of Helsinki. Written consent was obtained from the donor or the donor’s family for the use of the eyes in medical research. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Zhongshan Eye Center, Sun Yat-sen University (Guangzhou, China; approval No. ID: 2021KYPJ187). Human primary RPE (HsRPE) cells were separated from human donor eyes as previously described in ref. 63. All HsRPE cells were cultured in DMEM containing 4.5 g/L glucose, supplemented with 1% penicillin/streptomycin and 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), in a humidified incubator in 5% CO2 at 37 °C.

Transmission electron microscopy

Rats (P14, P21, P28, P35, P51, and P70) and mice (P30) were euthanized, and the eyes were enucleated and fixed in Smith–Rudt (0.8% PFA and 1.2% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M phosphate buffer). The cornea and lens were dissected, and the remaining posterior eye cup was post-fixed in 2% aqueous osmium tetroxide and dehydrated in ethyl alcohol. The samples were infiltrated and flat-mounted in Embed 812/Araldite resin (Electron Microscopy Sciences) to allow en-face sectioning. The blocks were polymerized at 60 °C for 48 h, and 90 nm microtome sections were cut (Leica UC7, Leica) onto 300-mesh copper grids. Grids were stained with 1% aqueous uranyl acetate/Reynold’s lead citrate and viewed using a transmission electron microscope (Tecnai G2 Spirit, FEI Czech Ltd.).

HsRPE cells were treated with NC siRNA and MERTK siRNA (20 nM) for 48 hours before being collected and fixed with 2% paraformaldehyde-2.5% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M phosphate buffer (Sigma-Aldrich), pH 7.3, at 4 °C overnight. After incubation, the cells were washed thrice with phosphate buffer and post-fixed in 1% osmium tetroxide (Structure Probe) for 30 min. The samples were dehydrated and stained with 1% aqueous uranyl acetate (Structure Probe). The structures of the mitochondria were observed under an H-7500 transmission electron microscope (Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan) operated at 80 kV.

Real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction

Total mRNA was extracted from the retinas of rats and HsRPE samples, which were treated with NC siRNA and MERTK siRNA for 24 h using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen)64. The cDNAs were generated via in vitro reverse transcription using mRNA as the template. Target gene expression was quantified by RT-PCR using specific primers (RiboBio, Guangdong, China). The primer sequences for RT-PCR are listed in Table S5. RT-PCR was performed using an ABI Prism 7900 HT Sequence Detection System (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA) with a Taqman Gene Expression Master Mix (Applied Biosystems). All data are presented as fold changes compared to the control after normalizing to β-actin or 18S.

RNA sequencing of HsRPE cells

Total HsRPE cell RNA was extracted from each group using the RNeasy Mini Kit (QIAgen), per the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA concentration and purity were determined using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer. RNA integrity was assessed with the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer System (RNA Integrity Number > 8.0 required for all samples). Sequencing libraries were constructed from 1 μg of high-quality total RNA per sample using the Illumina Stranded mRNA Prep Kit following poly-A selection. The library preparation process involved mRNA purification and fragmentation, cDNA synthesis, adapter ligation, and PCR amplification. Final library quality was verified using the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer High Sensitivity DNA Kit. Qualified libraries were sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform with 150 bp paired-end reads, generating approximately 40 million reads per sample.

Transcriptome analysis of HsRPE cells

Following sequencing, the initial data quality of HsRPE was evaluated with FASTQC (v0.11.5). Adaptor sequences and low-quality bases were removed using Trimmomatic (v0.39). The cleaned reads were then aligned to the hg19 human reference genome with STAR, and transcript abundance was quantified in TPM units via RSEM. Differential expression analysis was conducted with the DESeq2 package, applying thresholds of |log₂FC| ≥ 1 and FDR < 0.05. Finally, GO enrichment was assessed using Metascape with significance cutoffs of p < 0.01 and q < 0.05.

Western blot analysis

Western blotting was performed on rat retinas or HsRPE samples treated with NC siRNA and MERTK siRNA with or without NMN for 48 h, as previously described in ref. 65. Briefly, rat retinas or human RPE cells were homogenized in complete lysis buffer (Cell Signaling Technology), followed by centrifugation at 18,760 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The protein concentrations were determined using the Bicinchoninic Acid Protein Assay kit (Beyotime Biotechnology). Protein samples (30 µg) were separated by SDS-PAGE (Beyotime Biotechnology) and transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride membranes (Millipore). Membranes were blocked (5% nonfat dried milk and Tris-buffered saline Tween 20; both from Cell Signaling Technology) for 1 h at room temperature. The membranes were incubated at 4 °C overnight with primary antibodies, followed by incubation with the corresponding secondary antibodies. Table S6 shows the list of antibodies. Antibody binding was evaluated using chemiluminescence (Millipore), followed by exposure to an X-ray camera obscura (Guangdong Yuehua Medical Equipment Factory, Guangdong, China). Subsequently, the film was removed and soaked in developer for 5 s before the immunoreactive bands were visualized under red light. Band detection was conducted within the linear range, and three independent technical replicates were performed for each blot. The uncropped and unprocessed scans of blots have been provided in the Source Data file.

Immunofluorescence analysis

After euthanizing the rats, their eyes were enucleated and placed in 4% PFA overnight. The eyes were cryoprotected in 30% sucrose overnight, frozen in optimal cutting temperature compound, and cryosectioned at 4 µm. The slides were warmed to room temperature and permeabilized with 0.1% Triton-X for 15 min, blocked with 2% bovine serum albumin in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and stained with primary antibodies overnight at room temperature (antibody list in Table S6). After primary antibody incubation, retinal sections were washed five times with PBS and stained with secondary antibody for 4 h at RT. Retinal sections were washed five times with PBS, stained with DAPI for 15 min, mounted with fluoromount, coverslipped, and sealed with nail polish. Retinal sections were imaged on a Nikon AX confocal microscope for higher-resolution images.

Immunofluorescence analysis was conducted on HsRPE cells treated with NC siRNA and MERTK siRNA with or without NMN for 48 h, as previously described in ref. 66. After fixing the cells, the cover glasses were incubated with primary antibodies (Table S6) at 4 °C overnight, followed by incubation with the corresponding secondary antibodies. The nuclei were stained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) for 5 min. All images were recorded using a digital camera connected to a Nikon AX confocal microscope.

Transient transfection with siRNAs

The siRNAs for MERTK and GAPDH were designed and synthesized by RiboBio Inc. (Guangzhou, China). The MERTK and GAPDH siRNA sequences are listed in Table S7. Transient transfections of siRNAs for human MERTK (20 nM) and GAPDH (20 nM) were conducted using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX for 24 or 48 h (Invitrogen, New York, NY, United States), according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

Phagocytosis assay

Phagocytosis assays were performed as previously described in refs. 67,68. Porcine POS were isolated from fresh eyes under dim red light, purified by differential centrifugation and sucrose gradient, and labeled with FITC (Thermo, 46425). After quenching and resuspension, confluent HsRPE cells were challenged with FITC-POS for 6 h at 37 °C. Parallel assays using Nile red-labeled microspheres (2 μm) were conducted under identical conditions. Following incubation, the cells were washed and stained with DAPI. Phagocytic activity was quantified by measuring internalized fluorescence using confocal microscopy.

NAD+, GSH, NAM, NMN, and NADP assays

Metabolic phenotyping was performed to quantify GSH (Thermo Fisher Scientific; 2S9W7), NAD+ (Thermo Fisher Scientific; 4A4R8), NAM (MyBiosource, MBS7269618), NMN (Shanghai Fantai Biotechnology Co., Ltd. FT-PD8189S), and NADP (Shanghai Fantai Biotechnology Co., Ltd. FT-PD7563S) in retinas. HsRPE cells were dissociated into cell lysates and assayed following the manufacturer’s instructions. The results were calculated according to the standard curve generated using standards from the kits. The final metabolite concentrations for each sample were normalized to the total protein concentration measured with the Bradford assay.

Retinal photography

Fundus color photography was performed after pupillary dilation with 0.5% tropicamide (Jinyao) using a retinal camera (Micron IV Retinal Imaging Microscope). Eyes were covered with carboxymethyl cellulose and placed under a stereomicroscope. The fundus was photographed with a digital camera (Phoenix Research Labs, USA).

Optical coherence tomography (OCT) evaluation

An intraperitoneal injection of 0.6% pentobarbital sodium was administered to anesthetize the rats (100 µL/10 g). The rats’ pupils were dilated using tropicamide ophthalmic drops, and the corneas were lubricated with hypromellose gel. OCT (Heidelberg Engineering, Heidelberg, Germany) was performed on both eyes to investigate structural changes. Heidelberg Eye Explorer software was used to compute retinal thickness and volume.

Electroretinography

Briefly, after 12 h of dark adaptation under dim red light, rats were anesthetized using 0.1% pentobarbital. The pupils were dilated with 0.5% tropicamide (Jinyao), and body temperature was maintained at 37 °C with a heating pad (Gaoshiyuanwang Technologies Co., Ltd., Beijing, China). Oxybuprocaine hydrochloride (for corneal anesthesia) and tropicamide phenylephrine (for pupil dilation) (Santen Pharmaceuticals, Tokyo, Japan) were applied, and the eyes were kept moist using physiological saline. ERGs (RetiMINER System, IRC Medical Equipment, Chongqing, China) were recorded from each eye using a gold loop electrode on the corneal surface, maintained with carboxymethyl cellulose. A reference electrode was attached to the shaved skin on the head, and a ground electrode was clipped to the rat’s tail. Initially, scotopic ERG was conducted using ascending light intensities ranging from 0.001 to 10 cd·s/m2. A 10 min light adaptation was conducted using a background light intensity of 30 cd·s/m2. Photopic ERG was subsequently conducted at luminance levels of 1.0, 3.0, and 10 cd·s/m2. Ten sweeps were performed for each light stimulus. The pre- and post-trigger times were set to 50 and 300 ms, respectively, with a sampling frequency of 2000 Hz. For both scotopic and photopic recordings, the amplitude of the a-wave was determined by subtracting the baseline from the trough, while the b-wave amplitude was defined as the measurement from the trough of the a-wave to the peak of the b-wave.

Hematoxylin-eosin (HE) assay

After sacrifice, rat eyes were removed, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, and embedded in paraffin. Sections (4 µm thick) of eyes were cut along the vertical meridian through the optic nerve head. After dewaxing with xylene, sections were dehydrated with anhydrous ethanol and stained with Hematoxylin-Eosin (Sigma-Aldrich). Retinal thickness was measured under a light microscope (Axioplan2; Carl Zeiss Meditec, Inc., Jena, Germany). Upper retina thickness was measured 1000 μm away from the optic nerve. Retinal thickness measurements were averaged within each group to provide a single value for statistical analysis.

Cell counting Kit-8 assay

A Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK8) (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) was used to measure cell viability. HsRPE cells were cultured at a density of 7 × 103 cells/well in 96-well plates after transfection with MERTK siRNA with or without NMN treatment (1 mM) for 48 h. The CCK8 assay was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and the absorption values at 480 nm were evaluated.

Oxygen consumption rate (OCR) examination

HsRPE cells were assessed OCR using a Seahorse XF24 analyzer. The data were normalized to cell numbers, and metabolic parameters were automatically calculated by Seahorse software.

Adenosine triphosphate (ATP) production analysis

Mitochondrial ATP levels were evaluated with the ATP Determination Kit (A22066, Invitrogen) following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Mitochondrial membrane potential assay

The mitochondrial membrane potential was measured using a 5,5’,6,6’-Tetrachloro-1,1’,3,3’-tetraethylbenzimidazolylcarbocyanine iodide (JC-1) mitochondria staining kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, C2006). In brief, cells of retina were incubated with JC-1 staining solution (5 μg/mL) for 20 min at 37 °C. After incubation, the cells were centrifuged at 600 x g for 3 min. The mitochondrial membrane potential was determined using fluorometry. For JC-1 monomers and aggregates, fluorescence was measured using a multifunctional microplate reader (Molecular Devices, Sunnyvale, CA, USA).

Measurement of reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels

Retina or RPE cells were dissociated into single cells using 0.05% trypsin EDTA. ROS levels were measured using 2’−7’-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA; Beyotime Biotechnology, S0033). In brief, HsRPE cells were incubated with 10 μM of DCFH-DA for 30 min at 37 °C. After incubation, excess DCFH-DA was removed by washing with PBS three times. Finally, the fluorescence signals were measured using a multifunctional microplate reader (Molecular Devices, Sunnyvale, CA, USA).

Gene therapy in rats

For Gapdh siRNA treatment, intravitreal injections were administered to 21-day-old rats, as described previously. Tropicamide phenylephrine (Santen Pharmaceutical, Osaka, Japan) and oxybuprocaine hydrochloride (Santen) eye drops were administered to dilate the pupils. The needle was inserted ~1 mm behind the corneal limbus, avoiding the crystalline lens, and 2 µL of Gapdh siRNA was quickly injected. The intravitreal injection was administered twice a week for 4 weeks. During the siRNA treatment period, the rats were treated with NMN (1000 vmg/kg/d), which was dissolved in drinking water. The rats were euthanized at the age of 51 d for subsequent analysis.

For virally delivered gene therapy, pupils were dilated (as above) and intravitreally injected with 2.0 µL (1 × 1210 gc/mL) AAV2.2 rat Gapdh under a CMV promoter with a GFP reporter. Rats were injected at 21 d of age and euthanized at 51 d for subsequent analysis.

Transfection and expression of GAPDH-Le

GAPDH expression was upregulated in HsRPE cells using lentiviral vectors. The recombined plasmid with Gapdh (NM_002046) in pCDH-GFP+Puro-3xFlag was used to produce the lentivirus. The RGC cells were transfected with lentivirus. After 72 h, 293 T cells were collected. The concentrated virus was extracted by centrifugation, and the viral titer was detected. HsRPE cells were inoculated in 6-well plates. After reaching 30% confluence, 50 μL of virus was added (titer: 2 × 108 TU/mL). After 48 h of culture, expression of the target gene in the lentivirus was observed by measuring the green fluorescent protein, and the mRNA and protein levels were measured. The GAPDH lentiviral vector sequences are listed in Table S8.

Statistics and reproducibility

GraphPad Prism Software (version 10.4.2) was employed for data analysis and presentation. The data is presented as the mean values accompanied by their standard deviation (mean ± SD) of at least three independent trials. For statistical analysis, either an unpaired, two-tailed, multiple Student’s t test, one-way, or two-way ANOVA was chosen, depending on the experimental design. The statistical significance was indicated using the following notation for interpretation: P > 0.05 = n.s. = not significant.
