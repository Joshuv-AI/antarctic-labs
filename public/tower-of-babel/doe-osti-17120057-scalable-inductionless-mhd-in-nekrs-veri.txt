 Summary
The fusion blanket is a crucial engineering component with several roles. It breeds tritium to close
the fuel cycle, converts fusion energy into usable heat, and shields the components behind it from
radiation. The primary working fluid is a liquid metal. Because it is electrically conducting and the
reactor field reaches several tesla, the flow is governed by magnetohydrodynamic (MHD) effects. A
full blanket calculation is therefore multiphysics, coupling magnetohydrodynamics, turbulence, heat
transfer, tritium transport, and neutronics. It is also multiscale, ranging from the thin Hartmann
and side-wall layers to transitional flow between different channel configurations and regimes.
 These demands shape the choice of solver. We develop the MHD solver in NekRS, a GPU-
accelerated spectral element code for incompressible flow, which brings portable performance and
a mature solver stack. High-order discretization suits the multiscale problem, since it preserves
accuracy across the full range of scales. We demonstrate this at an ultra-high Hartmann number of
Ha = 106 , where the wall layer is resolved by only a few grid points. The strong magnetic field also
restricts the timestep, and treating the Lorentz damping term implicitly raises the maximum stable
value by a factor of five in the MHD-dominated regime.
 The solver is then verified and validated against benchmark configurations, examined in order
of increasing geometric and physical complexity. Hartmann flow between parallel plates verifies the
spatial discretization, showing exponential p-convergence and the expected algebraic h-convergence
rates up to Ha = 106 , where the interaction number reaches N = 1010 . The Hunt II duct validates
the treatment of conducting walls, where resolving the solid domain recovers the thick-wall solution
of Sloan and Smith rather than the thin-wall solution of Hunt, with relative errors of O(10−6 ) in
velocity and O(10−7 ) in electric potential. A square duct with conducting walls reproduces the
asymptotic pressure-drop correlation, and a circular pipe exiting a fringing magnetic field reproduces
published velocity and pressure distributions. Beyond the steady cases, NekRS resolves the physical
fluctuation and captures the three-dimensional transitional flow, with a growth rate matching the
prediction of linear stability theory.
 Early strong scaling results on Frontier and Aurora sustain 80% efficiency down to 8 to 10M
grid points per rank with MHD enabled, against 5 to 6M without it. The shift is expected, since the
electric potential is solved over the larger conjugate domain and adds coarse grid solves. Holding
the same order of efficiency under that additional work indicates the solver stack absorbs the MHD
components without a structural penalty.

2 Numerical Methods
2.1 Weak Formulation and Discretization

The Poisson equation for the inductionless solver is obtained from charge conservation, ∇ · j = 0.
For all test functions ψ ∈ H01 (Ω), the weak form is

                               0 = (ψ, ∇ · j)Ω = (ψ, j · n)∂Ω − (∇ψ, j)Ω ,                            (9)

where the second equality follows from integration by parts over Ω. Since the normal current is
continuous across material interfaces, [[j · n]] = 0, the same expression is obtained by applying inte-
gration by parts elementwise and then assembling the contributions. Substituting the inductionless
Ohm’s law gives

                           (∇ψ, σ ∗ ∇ϕ)Ω = (∇ψ, σ ∗ u × B0 )Ω − (ψ, j · n)∂Ω .                      (10)

This weak-divergence form reduces the regularity required for the u × B0 term.
 In NekRS, the incompressible Navier–Stokes equations are advanced using a velocity–pressure
splitting scheme [1, 2], in which body forces are treated explicitly via temporal extrapolation.
Therefore, the Lorentz force is lagged in time and evaluated from the velocity available at the
beginning of each timestep.
 Given (un−1 , pn−1 , ϕn−1 ), the current density jn−1 is computed from (7), and the Lorentz force
is formed as f n−1 = N jn−1 × B0 . The Navier–Stokes equations are then advanced from tn−1 to tn
using the extrapolated forcing f ∗ = EXT(f n−1 , f n−2 , . . .). After the velocity update, the potential
equation (10) is solved for ϕn using un . The scheme is summarized in Algorithm 1.

Algorithm 1 Temporal scheme for the inductionless model
 1: Initialize u0 and ϕ0
 2: for n = 1, 2, . . . do
 3: Compute jn−1 from (7) and set f n−1 ← jn−1 × B0
 4: Advance (u, p) from tn−1 to tn using the splitting scheme [1, 2], with extrapolated body
 force f ∗ , obtaining un and pn
 5: Solve (10) for ϕn using un
 6: end for

 Both the velocity, pressure and the electric potential are discretized with the spectral element
method (SEM) [3, 4, 5]. The domain is partitioned into E curvilinear hexahedral elements, and
within each element every field is expanded in tensor-product Lagrange polynomials of degree N on
the Gauss–Lobatto–Legendre (GLL) nodes. Integrals are evaluated by GLL quadrature on those
same nodes, which renders the mass matrix diagonal. Derivatives are applied as one-dimensional
matrix products along each coordinate direction through the chain rule. Both operations therefore
reduce to elementwise tensor contractions, and sum factorization evaluates the SEM Laplacian on
n ≈ EN 3 grid points in O(nN ) operations with O(n) memory references. Continuity across element
interfaces is enforced by direct stiffness summation, which couples the elements in C 0 .
 The resulting discretization is spectrally accurate for smooth solutions, so the error decays

 2

exponentially in N , and it adds no artificial viscosity. Its dissipation and dispersion errors also decay
exponentially for resolved wavenumbers [6, 4]. These properties are what allow the thin Hartmann
and Shercliff layers to be resolved by raising the polynomial degree on a fixed mesh.
 Three linear systems are solved each timestep, namely the pressure Poisson equation, the
velocity Helmholtz equations, and the potential Poisson equation (10). The two Poisson solves
dominate the cost. Their Green’s functions couple every point in the domain to every other, so the
solution cannot be advanced by local work alone and the solves are communication bound at scale.
The velocity Helmholtz systems are local by comparison and converge in one or two iterations for
the timestep sizes used here.
 NekRS provides a broad set of Krylov solvers and preconditioners for these systems, since no
single combination is best across the range of geometries and operating conditions encountered in
practice. Conjugate gradient and GMRES are both available, and the preconditioner may be built
either from p-multigrid with a choice of smoother and Chebyshev acceleration [7] or from a low
order finite element discretization of the same operator, with the resulting coarse problem solved by
algebraic multigrid [8]. The pressure solve additionally projects onto a space of previous solutions
to form the initial guess [9], which reduces the iteration count at the cost of storing a small number
of prior vectors.
 The potential solve reuses this infrastructure with two differences. It is posed on the conjugate
fluid–solid domain rather than on the fluid alone, so the system is larger. Its coefficient σ ∗ is also
variable, jumping by the conductivity ratio across the fluid–solid interface, whereas the pressure
operator has constant coefficients. A variable coefficient operator constrains the preconditioner,
since the jump must be represented on every level of the hierarchy rather than averaged away. The
options needed for this case are in place and are used throughout the results reported here, and
extending the remaining ones to variable coefficients is left for future work.

2.2 Interface and Boundary Conditions

For the liquid-metal (LM) region, continuity of the normal current, [[n · j]] = 0, together with the
no-slip condition in the solid, us = 0, gives [[n·(σ ∗ ∇ϕ)]] = 0. Neglecting surface charge accumulation
also gives continuity of the electric potential, [[ϕ]] = 0. The resulting boundary contribution is
(ψ, j · n)∂Ω and has the following cases, also collected in Table 1.

 • LM–insulator.
 An insulating wall cannot sustain electric current, so continuity of the normal current gives
 n · jLM = 0. With no slip at the wall, Ohm’s law reduces to a homogeneous Neumann condition
 for ϕ, and the boundary term vanishes.

 • LM–perfect conductor.
 In the perfect-conductor limit, the electric field vanishes in the solid, so the potential is constant.
 If the conductor is grounded, this gives the homogeneous Dirichlet condition ϕLM = 0. The
 test function then vanishes on the boundary, eliminating the boundary term.

 • LM–finite conductor.
 For a finite-conductivity solid, both ϕ and j · n are continuous across the interface. If the
 solid-side potential is prescribed, these relations may be used to impose an equivalent Dirichlet

 3

 or Neumann condition on the LM. In the general case, the computational domain includes
 both the LM and the conducting solid. The SEM discretization enforces C 0 continuity of
 ϕ strongly and j · n = σ ∗ ∇ϕ · n continuity weakly, while the problem is closed by an outer
 insulating or perfectly conducting boundary condition. Thus, LM–solid interfaces are treated
 in the same manner as internal spectral element boundaries. This is the configuration shown
 in Figure 1.

 • LM–inflow/outflow
 For a free-electrode condition, j · n = 0 is imposed at the inlet or outlet. Since the boundary
 term is written directly in terms of j · n, this contribution vanishes.

 • Periodic condition
 Without an imposed voltage drop, the electric potential is taken to be periodic. With an
 imposed voltage drop, one may decompose the potential into a known particular part and a
 periodic correction, then solve for the periodic correction.

2.3 Implicit Treatment

At large Hartmann number the Lorentz force is much stronger than the advective term, and an
explicit treatment restricts the timestep to ∆tmax = O(Ha−2 ). Following Khodak et al. [10], we
expand the Lorentz force using the vector triple product,

                     N (j × B0 ) = N (−∇ϕ) × B0 − u∥B0 ∥2 + B0 (u · B0 ) ,
  
 (11)

and treat the damping term −N u∥B0 ∥2 implicitly. Of the three terms this is the only one available,
since the other two involve ∇ϕ and the component of u along B0 , neither of which is a multiple
of the unknown un alone. The damping term is linear in u and negative semidefinite, so moving
it to the left-hand side adds N ∥B0 ∥2 to the diagonal of the velocity operator and improves its
conditioning. The two remaining terms stay explicit and enter the extrapolated forcing f ∗ . The
resulting Helmholtz problem for each velocity component reads

where β0 is the leading BDF coefficient and gn collects the extrapolated forcing together with the
previous timestep contributions.
 The treatment is consistent with the velocity–pressure splitting scheme only when ∥B0 ∥ is
constant in space. The splitting scheme requires that the operator applied to un preserve the
divergence constraint, so that the pressure solve remains the only mechanism enforcing ∇ · un = 0.
Applying the product rule to the implicit term gives

                            ∇ · u∥B0 ∥2 = ∥B0 ∥2 ∇ · u + u · ∇∥B0 ∥2 ,
 
 (13)

whose first term vanishes for a divergence-free velocity. For constant ∥B0 ∥ the second term vanishes
as well, so the implicit term acts as a scalar shift of the Helmholtz operator and introduces no
additional source in the pressure Poisson equation, leaving the divergence-error structure of the
splitting scheme unchanged [1, 2]. For a spatially varying ∥B0 ∥ the second term survives, and
treating the damping term implicitly would feed a spurious divergence source into the pressure
equation. Two options remain in that case. The term may be left explicit, which recovers the
O(Ha−2 ) restriction, or a constant portion of ∥B0 ∥2 may be treated implicitly with the remainder
left explicit. The latter keeps the implicit coefficient piecewise constant, so the second term still
vanishes within each element, and it recovers part of the timestep gain wherever the field is close to
uniform.
 Among the cases in this report, the uniform field configurations satisfy the constant ∥B0 ∥
requirement and use the implicit treatment. The fringing field cases do not, and are advanced
explicitly. The gain in stable timestep is measured in Section 3.

3.1.3 Mesh Placement

The exact solution depends only on the y coordinate and is therefore essentially one-dimensional.
We use uniform meshes in the periodic x and z directions and focus on element placement in y.
As discussed in Section 3.1.2, the Hartmann-layer thickness ranges approximately from 2.3/Ha to
6.9/Ha. Prior knowledge of this thickness can therefore guide the placement of element boundaries
near the wall.

 We consider four mesh strategies, each using Ey = 14 elements in the y direction. The element-

at wall distance 1/Ha. The second adds two more near-wall elements, spanning [1/Ha, 3/Ha] and
[3/Ha, 10/Ha]. These first two strategies assume only that the layer thickness is O(1/Ha), without
using a known proportionality constant. The third strategy places the first element at the wall
distance 2.3/Ha, corresponding to the location where the normalized velocity reaches 0.9, and
distributes the remaining boundaries geometrically. The final strategy uses a mapping based on the

wall, with the smallest wall-normal spacing set to 1/Ha.
 Figure 5 shows the resulting element distributions against the layer thicknesses. Only m3
and m4 place more than one boundary inside [1/Ha, 6.9/Ha], and m4 concentrates six of its seven
half-domain boundaries there, leaving the core coarse. The same pattern holds at both Ha = 103
and 105 , since every strategy is defined relative to 1/Ha.
 As shown in Figure 6, all four meshes exhibit exponential convergence with increasing polynomial
order, although their error levels differ substantially. In particular, the relatively small change from
m1 to m2 reduces the error at N = 9 by approximately four orders of magnitude, indicating that
placing element boundaries within the relevant Hartmann-layer thickness is critical. Although 1/Ha
lies inside the boundary layer, the solution exhibits its strongest curvature near 2.3/Ha. Overall,
m4 achieves the highest accuracy because of its aggressive, approximately exponential refinement
toward the wall.

3.2 Hunt II Configuration

Hunt [17] gives analytic solutions for MHD flow in a rectangular duct under two wall configurations.
The first has perfectly conducting Hartmann walls with arbitrary conducting side walls, and the
second has arbitrary conducting Hartmann walls with insulating side walls. The second is the more
widely used and is known as the Hunt II case. Both derivations invoke the thin-wall assumption,
which replaces the conducting wall by a Robin boundary condition.
 Sloan and Smith [18] extend the result by solving the fluid and solid domains together, enclosed
by an outer insulating wall. This thick-wall solution removes the thin-wall approximation. It
reduces to Hunt’s formulation as the wall thickness approaches zero, and it matches the two limits
of vanishing and infinite wall conductivity, the former recovering Shercliff’s solution [19].
 The difference between the two formulations measures the influence of the wall. Because NekRS
solves the electric potential in the solid domain, it recovers the thick-wall solution directly. Figure 10
shows the configuration and the side-wall jets that form under a strong field.

Figure 10: Schematic of the Hunt II configuration. The side walls are insulating (white), whereas
the Hartmann walls have arbitrary electrical conductivity (gray). Under appropriate conditions,
strong side-wall jets form. The velocity profile shown here is the scaled streamwise velocity for
c = ∞ and Ha = 200.

3.2.2 NekRS Results

Because NekRS solves the electric potential over the conjugate fluid–solid domain, it should reproduce

3.4 Linear Stability

The validation cases above are steady and unidirectional by construction. Blanket conditions are
not, and the transition to three-dimensional flow limits how far a steady laminar calculation can be
trusted. This section records how that transition first appeared in our own runs, places it against
the literature, and states what has been verified so far.

3.4.1 Observation

The behavior first appeared in the Thanh1 duct, a conducting duct under a uniform applied field at
reactor relevant parameters. This case has no known closed form solution, but Walker [22] gives an
 3c
asymptotic relation at high Ha in which the pressure gradient follows |∂p/∂x| = 3c+4   ρU B 2 . As
shown in Figure 21, the NekRS results follow that line from Ha = 1075 to 6453, spanning applied
fields of one to six tesla.

 Running the case further toward steady state, however, the solution departs from its steady
trajectory and develops three-dimensional structure. The streamwise pressure gradient is largely
unaffected and continues to follow the asymptotic relation. The instability therefore redistributes
the flow without changing the integral pressure drop, so a pressure based check alone would not
reveal it.

3.4.2 Numerical Origin Ruled Out

Because a spurious oscillation can mimic a physical instability, we checked the usual numerical
causes before accepting the result. Tightening the solver tolerance, refining the mesh, and reducing
the timestep all leave the fluctuation intact, so it is not a discretization or convergence artifact.
 Two further tests identify it as a genuinely three-dimensional mode. At N = 1 the instability
does not appear, which is consistent with a low-order discretization damping the mode rather than
with the mode being absent. Zeroing the spanwise velocity components at every timestep, which
forces the flow to remain unidirectional, also recovers a stable steady solution. The instability
therefore requires the spanwise degrees of freedom that the enforced unidirectional flow removes,
and it is not an artifact of the solver.
 Figure 22 shows the time history at Ha = 200 for two wall thicknesses. The temporal derivatives
are estimated as the difference between consecutive steps. Both quantities decay by three to four
orders of magnitude as the flow approaches the unidirectional state and reach a minimum near
t = 35. From there to about t = 50 the signal grows exponentially, spanning roughly three orders
of magnitude on a straight log-linear segment. This window is the linear phase, in which the
perturbation is small enough that the dynamics are governed by the linearized operator, so the
slope measures the growth rate of the least stable eigenmode predicted by linear stability theory.
Beyond t ≈ 50 the amplitude is no longer small, nonlinear effects take over, and the signal departs
from the exponential and saturates.
 The thicker wall grows at a slightly higher rate through the linear phase and saturates about
an order of magnitude higher. A more conducting path through the wall therefore destabilizes
the flow more strongly, which is consistent with the trend of critical Reynolds number with wall
conductance reported by Priede [23]. Since the target configuration has both a thicker wall and a
higher Hartmann number than this test, it sits further above the critical Reynolds number than the
Ha = 200 case shown here.

3.4.3 Comparison with the Literature

The phenomenon is documented. Reed and Picologlou [24] measured strong velocity fluctuations
near the sidewall in the ALEX experiment at Ha = 5200 and c = 0.07, over a Reynolds number
range that corresponds to Re ∈ [1250, 5000] in the present convention, with a nearly periodic time
signal. Kinet et al. [25] simulated the inductionless system at Ha = 200 and Re ∈ [2000, 10000],
finding instability above Re ≈ 3700, and traced the vortices to the breakdown of the sidewall jet.
A series of linear stability analyses by Priede [26, 27, 23] solved the eigenvalue problem about the
unidirectional base flow and reported the critical Reynolds number as a function of Ha for several
wall types and conductance ratios.
 The mechanism is attributed to the scaling of the sidewall jet. As Ha increases the jet narrows
as O(Ha−1/2 ) while its peak velocity grows as O(Ha1/2 ), so the shear across the layer strengthens
on both counts. Above a critical Reynolds number the layer can no longer sustain that shear and it
breaks down. Figure 23 shows the streamwise velocity in this state. The jet separates from the wall,
rolls up into vortices, and feeds three-dimensional motion into the core. The structures repeat at
a regular spacing in both space and time, which is the signature of a single dominant eigenmode
rather than of broadband turbulence. The same mechanism and the same qualitative structure are
reported by Kinet et al. [25].

Figure 23: Streamwise velocity in the x–z midplane at Ha = 200, Re = 5000, c = 0.5, and tw = 0.1.
The sidewall jets separate from the wall and break down into the core, leaving low velocity regions
behind them at a regular streamwise spacing.
 Linear theory is unusually well suited to this regime. In the inductionless model the Lorentz
force is linear in the velocity, and its magnitude relative to advection is set by the interaction
number N = Ha2 /Re. At blanket conditions N is large, so the linear damping term dominates
the quadratic advective term and the governing operator is close to linear. The growth of a small
perturbation is then governed by the linearized problem over a wide amplitude range, and the
critical Reynolds number predicted by eigenvalue analysis is a meaningful threshold.
 Comparing these studies requires care with the definitions, since the reported Reynolds numbers
do not share a common scaling. Every source bases the Hartmann number on the half height L,
so no conversion is needed there. Two of them scale the Reynolds number differently, with Reed
normalizing by the full height 2L and Priede using the peak velocity of the sidewall jet rather than
the mean. Table 5 gives the value each study reports, together with its conversion to a common
scaling based on L and the mean velocity. On that common basis the target condition lies above
every reported threshold for instability, by a factor of three or more in each case, so the comparison

3.4.4 Verification against Linear Theory

The published analyses and reference simulations are all based on a thin wall, whereas our solver
resolves the conducting solid. A companion report [28] carries out the linear stability analysis for
the conjugate fluid–solid problem and compares it against NekRS in detail. We summarize the
outcome here.
 For Hunt flow over the fluid domain alone, the computed eigenvalues reproduce those of
Priede [26], and the growth rate measured from the NekRS simulation converges to the analysis
under increasing polynomial order and mesh refinement. For the Thanh configuration with the solid
domain resolved, the measured growth rate again matches the analysis at Ha = 10, 100, and 200, so
including the conducting wall does not degrade the agreement. At Ha = 200 the critical Reynolds
number from the eigensolver agrees with the thin-wall result of Priede [23], the NekRS growth rate
matches the corresponding eigenmode, and the unforced simulation develops the instability on its
own in agreement with Kinet [25].
 NekRS therefore captures this instability and reproduces its growth rate. The transition is a
physical feature of the conjugate problem rather than a numerical artifact, and it bounds the regime
in which the steady validation cases of the preceding sections remain representative.

4 Performance
A key requirement for the MHD solver is that it not degrade the performance of the existing code.
We measure this on the ITER water-cooled lead–lithium (WCLL) test blanket module on Frontier
and Aurora, comparing otherwise identical runs with the MHD components enabled and disabled.
The difference isolates the cost of the potential solve and the Lorentz force evaluation, since every
other term is common to both.
 Figure 24 shows strong scaling for a mesh of E = 257M elements at N = 7, or n = 88.4 billion
grid points. Both machines provide 64 GB of memory per GPU tile, which sets the largest problem
that fits on one rank. Without MHD the solver reaches approximately 27M grid points per rank,
and enabling MHD reduces this to about 22M because the potential field and its solver workspace
must also be stored.
 Parallel efficiency degrades as the problem is spread thinner, which is expected due to a higher
communication-to-computation ratio. With MHD disabled the solver holds roughly 80% efficiency
down to 5 to 6M grid points per rank, and enabling MHD shifts that threshold to 8 to 10M points
per rank. The electric potential accounts for the shift. It is an additional elliptic solve carried over
the conjugate domain, so it contributes its own coarse grid work and its own global communication
while adding comparatively little local arithmetic. A calculation with MHD therefore needs a larger
local problem to stay in the efficient regime. The cost per step follows the same pattern, with MHD
raising the time per step by roughly a factor of 1.5 to 2 at fixed loading on both machines. This is
a modest penalty for adding a global elliptic solve to every timestep, and it reflects the potential
equation reusing the same preconditioner infrastructure as the pressure solve.

5 Conclusion
The inductionless MHD solver in NekRS has been verified against exact solutions and validated
against experiment and reference simulations across the benchmark cases above. The Hartmann
case confirms exponential p-convergence and the expected O(hN ) and O(hN +1 ) spatial rates up
to Ha = 106 , where the interaction number reaches N = 1010 and the wall layer is resolved by
a handful of elements. The Hunt II case shows that resolving the conducting solid recovers the
thick-wall solution of Sloan and Smith rather than the thin-wall approximation, with relative errors
of O(10−6 ) in velocity and O(10−7 ) in electric potential. The Thanh1 and Thanh3 cases reproduce
the asymptotic pressure-drop scaling and the reference velocity and pressure distributions under a
fringing field.
 The transition to three-dimensional flow bounds the regime in which these steady cases remain
representative. It is a physical feature of the conjugate problem, it occurs within the parameter
range of interest for blanket conditions, and NekRS captures it with growth rates matching linear
stability theory. Steady laminar calculations above the critical Reynolds number should therefore
be interpreted with that limit in mind.
 The solver carries this additional physics without a performance penalty that would restrict its

 30

use. Treating the Lorentz damping term implicitly raises the stable timestep by a factor of five in
the MHD-dominated regime, which more than offsets the factor of about two in per-step cost that
the potential solve adds. Enabling MHD shifts the threshold for efficient strong scaling upward by a
comparable factor, so a blanket calculation with MHD should be sized for a larger local problem
than the same calculation without it.
 This validation campaign is ongoing. The cases reported here cover spatial convergence, wall
conductivity and thickness, curved geometry, and a non-uniform applied field, which are the features
that govern a steady laminar blanket flow. Two directions extend beyond it. The linear stability
results establish the onset of three-dimensional motion, but the transitional and turbulent states
beyond onset are not yet validated, with the Ting–Walker vortex one configuration where reference
data exist. Thermal coupling is the other, since buoyancy and temperature dependent properties
alter the flow that the electromagnetic forces act on. On the solver side, extending the remaining
preconditioner options to variable coefficients is left for future work.
