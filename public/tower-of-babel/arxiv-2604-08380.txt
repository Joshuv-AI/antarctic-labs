Sufficiency and Petz recovery for positive maps
Lauritz van Luijk, Henrik Wilming

Abstract
We study the interconversion of families of quantum states (“statistical experiments”) via positive, trace-preserving (PTP) maps and clarify its mathematical structure in terms of minimal sufficient Jordan algebras, which can be seen to generalize the Koashi-Imoto decomposition to the PTP setting. In particular, we show that Neyman-Pearson tests generate the minimal sufficient Jordan algebra, and hence also the minimal sufficient *-algebra corresponding to the Koashi-Imoto decomposition. As applications, we show that a) equality in the data-processing inequality for the relative entropy or the α \alpha - z z quantum Rényi divergence implies the existence of a recovery map also in the PTP case and b) that two dichotomies can be interconverted by PTP maps if and only if they can be interconverted by decomposable, trace-preserving maps. We thoroughly review the necessary mathematical background on Jordan algebras. As a step beyond the finite-dimensional case, we also prove Frenkel’s formula for approximately finite-dimensional von Neumann algebras.

1.2
Bayes sufficiency and the minimal sufficient algebra

We now connect (C)PTP-equivalence to binary hypothesis testing.
Recall that the optimal tests for binary hypothesis testing with priors

One may wonder whether there is a connection between the Neyman-Pearson tests and minimal sufficient (J)*-algebras.
The following theorem clarifies this. To state it we denote by

Theorem C

(cp.

)

Let

\displaystyle K_{(\rho,\sigma)}\subset J_{(\rho,\sigma)}\subset A_{(\rho,\sigma)}

and both the minimal sufficient J*-algebra and the minimal sufficient *-algebra are generated by the Neyman-Pearson tests:

\displaystyle J_{(\rho,\sigma)}=\mathrm{\textup{J*-}alg}(K_{(\rho,\sigma)}),\quad A_{(\rho,\sigma)}=\mathrm{\textup{*-}alg}(K_{(\rho,\sigma)}).

(1.15)

In fact the statement generalizes to arbitrary faithful statistical experiments

in the following sense.
If we define

, and set

\displaystyle K_{(\rho_{\theta})}=\lin\{[\rho_{\theta}>t\omega]\}_{t>0,\theta\in\Theta}+{\mathbb{C}}\cdot\text{1},

then we also have

\displaystyle J_{(\rho_{\theta})}=\mathrm{\textup{J*-}alg}(K_{(\rho_{\theta})}),\quad A_{(\rho_{\theta})}=\mathrm{\textup{*-}alg}(K_{(\rho_{\theta})}).

(1.17)

Part of the proof of

such that

1.4
Beyond finite dimensions

Our proofs are restricted to finite dimensions, but we believe that most of our results extend to the setting of von Neumann algebras. In fact, we believe that they generalize to the setting of JW*-algebras, the Jordan analog of von Neumann algebras
[
44
]
.
In particular, it will be interesting to prove the sufficiency result for the quantum relative entropy in this setting.
We plan to revisit this problem in a future publication.
A core ingredient for our proof is Frenkel’s integral formula, which has not yet been generalized to von Neuman algebras.
The Hockey stick divergence
E_{t}(\omega\|\varphi)
naturally extends to the von Neumann algebraic setting. In
Appendix

Proposition G

(see
Proposition

C.1
)
.

Let

be an approximately finite-dimensional von Neumann algebra, and let
\omega,\varphi
be normal states on
M

Then

D(\omega\|\varphi)=\int_{1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{t}E_{t}(\omega\|\varphi)+\frac{1}{t^{2}}E_{t}(\varphi\|\omega)\right)dt.

(1.28)

In the case
M=L({\mathcal{H}})
with
\dim{\mathcal{H}}=\infty
, the statement was shown in
[
45
]
, albeit with a more complicated proof.
A proof of Frenkel’s integral formula for states on general semifinite von Neumann algebras will appear in upcoming work
[
46
]
.

1.5
Discussion and Outlook

Our results support the idea
[
8
,
13
]
that CPTP maps do not provide the right mathematical framework for thinking about sufficiency and distinguishability of quantum states, even though they certainly provide the right framework for talking about the possible physical processes in quantum theory.

There are obvious questions left open by our results apart from those mentioned above already.
Most importantly, in the CPTP-case it is known that
approximate
equality in the data-processing inequality implies
approximate
recoverability
[
47
,
48
,
49
,
50
,
51
,
52
]
, which can be quantified in terms of the fidelity
F(\rho,\sigma)=\lVert\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}\sigma^{\frac{1}{2}}\rVert_{1}
.
Since the fidelity can be computed on the level of the minimal sufficient J*-algebra (see
Eq.

1.26
for
\alpha=z=\frac{1}{2}
), it is only reasonable to expect that the approximate recovery result also generalizes to the PTP setting.

It is known that every sufficient *-algebra admits a
(\rho,\sigma)
-preserving conditional expectation.
We do not know whether the corresponding statement holds for sufficient J*-algebras.

We have shown that the minimal sufficient J*-algebras of dichotomies are 2-generated and that every 2-generated J*-factor is minimal sufficient for some dichotomy.
We believe that the latter statement should hold for general J*-algebras.

2.1
Basics of J*-algebras

If
a_{1},\ldots a_{n}
are operators on a Hilbert space
{\mathcal{H}}
, we denote their symmetrized product by

\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\}=\frac{1}{2}\big(a_{1}a_{2}\cdots a_{n}+a_{n}a_{n-1}\cdots a_{1}\big)

(2.1)

While this notation is easy to confuse with that for sets, in the following, it will be clear from the context what is meant.
In the case
n=2
, this gives (half) the anti-commutator, better known as the
Jordan product
,

\{a,b\}=\frac{1}{2}(ab+ba).

The Jordan product is commutative, but not associative.
It is fully determined by squares in the sense that
6
6

6

This follows from adding the equations
(a+b)^{2}=a^{2}+ab+ba+b^{2}
and
-(a-b)^{2}=-a^{2}+ab+ba-b^{2}
.

\{a,b\}=\frac{1}{4}\big((a+b)^{2}-(a-b)^{2}\big).

(2.2)

Definition 2.1
.

An
operator system
on a Hilbert space
{\mathcal{H}}
is a complex subspace
J\subset L({\mathcal{H}})
, which is is *-invariant and contains the identity.
A
J*-algebra
is an operator system that is closed under Jordan products.
7
7

7

Our notion of a J*-algebra agrees with that of a JC*-algebra or a JW*-algebra on a finite-dimensional Hilbert space (in general, JC*- and JW*-algebra differ in their topological properties similar to C*- and W*-algebras, but the difference disappear in finite-dimensions)
[
44
]
.

Thus, a unital *-invariant subspace
J
is a J*-algebra if and only if it is closed under squares.
In fact, it suffices to check squares of hermitian elements.
Clearly, every *-algebra
A
on
{\mathcal{H}}
is a J*-algebra on
{\mathcal{H}}
.
In particular, this includes
L({\mathcal{H}})
.
At the end of this subsection, we give examples of J*-algebras that are not *-algebras.

The structure of a J*-algebra is encoded in its hermitian part.
An operator system
J\subset L({\mathcal{H}})
is a J*-algebra if and only if its hermitian part
J_{h}=\{x\in J:x=x^{*}\}
is a Jordan algebra of hermitian operators, i.e., a unital real vector space of hermitian operators that is closed under the Jordan product.
8
8

8

In operator algebra lingo, a Jordan algebra of hermitian operators on a Hilbert space is a concretely represented JC- or a JW-algebra
[
44
]
.

If
a=a^{*}
is a hermitian element in a J*-algeba
J
, then
a^{n}\in J
for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
Indeed, this follows inductively because
a^{n}=\{a^{n-1},a\}
and
a\in J
.
Therefore, J*-algebras are closed under the functional calculus.
If
a=\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}p_{i}
is the spectral decomposition, then
f(a)=\sum_{i}f(\lambda_{i})p_{i}
is in
J
for all functions
f:\Sp(a)\to{\mathbb{C}}
.
In particular, the spectral projections lie in
J
.
Thus, a J*-algebra is spanned by its projections

J=\lin\{p\in J\ :\ p^{2}=p=p^{*}\}

(2.3)

A J*-algebra
J
on a Hilbert space
{\mathcal{H}}
inherits a positive cone
J^{+}=\{a\in J:a\geq 0\}
from the positive semi-definite order on
L({\mathcal{H}})
.
It can be characterized algebraically via

J^{+}=\{a^{2}\ :\ a=a^{*}\in J\}.

(2.4)

Moreover, the positive cone
J^{+}
is self-dual with respect to the trace: If
a\in J
, then
9
9

9

To see this, note
\mathrm{Re}(a),\mathrm{Im}(a)\in J
.
If
\mathrm{Re}(a)=\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}p_{i}
is the spectral decomposition, then
0\leq\tr(ap_{i})=\tr(\mathrm{Re}(a)p_{i})=\lambda_{i}
shows
\mathrm{Re}(a)\geq 0
.
A similar argument shows
\mathrm{Im}(a)=0
.

a\in J^{+}\qquad\iff\qquad\tr(ab)\geq 0\quad\forall b\in J^{+}.

(2.5)

If
S\subset L({\mathcal{H}})
is a collection of hermitian operators, we denote by
\mathrm{\textup{J*-}alg}(S)
the linear hull of the identity and elements that can be written as nested Jordan products of elements of
S
.
It can be checked that
\mathrm{\textup{J*-}alg}(S)
is a J*-algebra. In fact, it is the smallest J*-algebra containing
S
:

\mathrm{\textup{J*-}alg}(S)=\bigcap\,\{J\subset L({\mathcal{H}})\ \text{J*-algebra containing $S$.}\}.

(2.6)

If
S=\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\}
is a finite set, we write
\mathrm{\textup{J*-}alg}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})
as a short hand.

A unital linear map
T:J_{1}\to J_{2}
between J*-algebras is a
J*-homomorphism
if it is unital, preserves the adjoint, and the Jordan product.
A J*-homomorphism is necessarily positive.
By (
2.2
), to check whether a hermitian-preserving map
T
is a J*-homomorphism, it suffices to check that

T(a^{2})=T(a)^{2}
for all
a\in J
.
A
J*-isomorphism
is a bijective J*-homomorphism (the inverse is automatically a J*-homomorphism).
We write
J_{1}\mathrel{\cong_{\mathrm{\textup{J*}}}}J_{2}
if a J*-isomorphism
T:J_{1}\to J_{2}
.

A useful identity is the
Jordan triple product
formula, which expresses the symmetrized triple product in terms of Jordan products:

\{\{a,b\},c\}+\{\{b,c\},a\}-\{\{a,c\},b\}=\frac{1}{2}(abc+cba)=\{a,b,c\},\qquad a,b,c\in L({\mathcal{H}}).

(2.7)

This shows that J*-algebras are closed under the triple product.
In particular, a J*-algebra
J
contains the product
aba=\{a,b,a\}
for all
a,b\in J
.
There exist no corresponding formulae that express symmetrized products of four or more operators in terms of the Jordan product.
A J*-algebra
J\subset L({\mathcal{H}})
that is closed under higher-order symmetrized products is called
reversible
.
Reversible J*-algebras will play a crucial role in this project.

Next, we consider two classes of examples of J*-algebras.
We will return to these examples on several occasions.

(fixed-points of involutions)
.

Let

.

Consider a family

, which are pairwise anti-commuting, i.e., satisfy

.
Then

(2.12)

is a J*-algebra. J*-algebras of this form are called

(see

Let us consider an explicit example on the

.
Denoting the Pauli matrices on

2.2
Multiplicative properties of positive maps

In this section we discuss multiplicative properties of positive maps.
We say that a UP map
T
admits a faithful invariant state if
T^{*}\sigma=\sigma
for some faithful state
\sigma
.

In contrast to completely positive, unital maps, general UP maps do not fulfill the Kadison-Schwartz inequality. However, they fulfill the Jordan-Schwartz inequality:

Lemma 2.4

(Jordan-Schwartz inequality
[
53
, Lem. 7.3]
)
.

Let
T:L({\mathcal{H}})\to L({\mathcal{K}})
be a unital, positive map. Then

\{Ta^{*},Ta\}\leq T\{a^{*},a\},\qquad a\in L({\mathcal{H}}).

(2.15)

As in the case of a completely positive map, we collect the elements that saturate the Jordan-Schwarz in the
multiplicative domain
:

Definition 2.5

(
[
54
]
)
.

The
multiplicative domain
11
11

11

In the literature, the term multiplicative domain is often reserved for UCP maps. The multiplicative domain of a UP map is called the
“definite set”
in
[
54
,
55
]
.

\mathrm{MD}(T)
of a UP map
T:L({\mathcal{H}})\to L({\mathcal{K}})
is defined as

\mathrm{MD}(T)=\big\{a\in L({\mathcal{H}})\ :\ \{Ta^{*},Ta\}=T\{a^{*},a\}\,\big\}.

(2.16)

If
T:L({\mathcal{H}})\to L({\mathcal{K}})
is a UCP map, then
\mathrm{MD}(T)
coincides with the usual multiplicative domain
12
12

12

By the Scwartz inequality,
T\{a^{*},a\}=\{Ta^{*},Ta\}

(see

Proposition 2.6

(

Let

If

2.3
The KMS inner product and Woronowicz’s maximum principle

It will be useful to consider UP maps as operators on a Hilbert space.

Consider a faithful state
\sigma
on
{\mathcal{H}}
. Then we can turn
L({\mathcal{H}})
into a Hilbert-space using the KMS inner product
14
14

14

Note that
\langle a,b\rangle_{\sigma}=\langle\Omega_{\sigma}|\bar{a}\otimes b|\Omega_{\sigma}\rangle
, where
\Omega_{\sigma}
is the canonical purification of
\sigma
on
\bar{\mathcal{H}}\otimes{\mathcal{H}}
.

\displaystyle\langle a,b\rangle_{\sigma}=\tr(\sigma^{\frac{1}{2}}a^{*}\sigma^{\frac{1}{2}}b).

(2.22)

The KMS inner product is useful because it respects the order interval of the reference state
\sigma
.
To see this, we define
\Gamma_{\sigma}=\sigma^{\frac{1}{2}}({\,\cdot\,})\sigma^{\frac{1}{2}}:L({\mathcal{H}})\to L({\mathcal{H}})
.
Then
\Gamma_{\sigma}
is an isomorphism between the order intervals
[0,\sigma]_{L({\mathcal{H}})}
and
[0,\text{1}]_{L({\mathcal{H}})}
, where
[0,a]_{L({\mathcal{H}})}=\{b\in L({\mathcal{H}}):0\leq b\leq a\}
, and we have

\langle a,b\rangle_{\sigma}=\tr(a^{*}\Gamma_{\sigma}(b))=\tr(\Gamma_{\sigma}(a^{*})b),\qquad a,b\in L({\mathcal{H}}).

(2.23)

From the above discussion, it is clear that
\langle a,b\rangle_{\sigma}\geq 0
whenever
a,b\geq 0
.
Woronowicz’s maximum principle shows that the above properties uniquely single out
\langle a,b\rangle_{\sigma}
:

Theorem 2.10

(
[
59
, Thms. 1.1 & 1.2]
)
.

Let
s:L({\mathcal{H}})\times L({\mathcal{H}})\to{\mathbb{C}}
be a sesquilinear form such that (i)
s(a,a)\geq 0
for all
a\in L({\mathcal{H}})
, (ii) if
a,b\geq 0
, then
s(a,b)\geq 0
, and (iii)
s(\text{1},a)=\tr(\sigma a)
. Then

\displaystyle s(a,a)\leq\langle a,a\rangle_{\sigma},\qquad a\in L({\mathcal{H}}).

(2.24)

If, in addition, for every
\omega\in[0,\sigma]
, there is an
a\in L({\mathcal{H}})
with
\tr(\omega b)=s(a,b)
for all
b\in L({\mathcal{H}})
, then
s(a,b)=\langle a,b\rangle_{\sigma}
.

We note that Woronowicz’s theorem holds in much more generality than stated here (see also
[
60
]
).
A direct consequence is that UP maps are contractions relative to the KMS inner product:

Corollary 2.11
.

Let
\sigma,\hat{\sigma}
be faithful states on
{\mathcal{H}}
and
\hat{\mathcal{H}}
, respectively, and
T:L(\hat{\mathcal{H}})\to L({\mathcal{H}})
be a UP map such that
T^{*}\sigma=\hat{\sigma}
.
Then

\displaystyle\langle Ta,Ta\rangle_{\sigma}\leq\langle a,a\rangle_{\hat{\sigma}},\qquad a\in L(\hat{\mathcal{H}}).

(2.25)

Proof.

Set
s(a,b)=\langle Ta,Tb\rangle_{\sigma}
. Then the properties required for
Theorem

2.10

\displaystyle E\{a,b\}=\{Ea,b\},\quad E(bab)=bE(a)b,\quad a\in L({\mathcal{H}}),\ b\in J.

(2.26)

If

Example 2.13

The following lemma follows an argument by Haagerup and Størmer

Lemma 2.15
.

Let
\sigma
be a faithful state,
J\subset L({\mathcal{H}})
a J*-algebra with
\sigma
-preserving conditional expectation

\langle Fa,b\rangle_{\sigma}=\langle a,Fb\rangle_{\sigma},\qquad a,b\in L({\mathcal{H}}).

(2.31)

Proof.

By
Corollary

2.11

\langle Fa,Fa\rangle_{\sigma}\leq\langle a,a\rangle_{F^{*}\sigma}=\langle a,a\rangle_{\sigma}
,
a\in L({\mathcal{H}})

Thus

is a contraction for the KMS inner product.
But any idempotent contraction on a Hilbert space is hermitian.
15
15

15

We check that the range of

is orthogonal to its null space, which shows the claim since

is idempotent. Let
a=Fa

and

Since

is a contraction, we have
\lVert a\rVert_{\sigma}=\lVert F(a-\lambda b)\rVert_{\sigma}\leq\lVert a-\lambda b\rVert_{\sigma}

\lVert a\rVert_{\sigma}^{2}:=\langle a,a\rangle_{\sigma}

\lambda=\langle b,a\rangle_{\sigma}/\langle b,b\rangle_{\sigma}

\displaystyle\lVert a\rVert_{\sigma}^{2}\leq\lVert a-\tfrac{\langle b,a\rangle_{\sigma}}{\langle b,b\rangle_{\sigma}}b\rVert_{\sigma}^{2}=\lVert a\rVert_{\sigma}^{2}-\tfrac{|\langle a,b\rangle_{\sigma}|^{2}}{\langle b,b\rangle_{\sigma}}.

∎

Using this result, we can express a state-preserving conditional expectation in terms of the trace-preserving conditional expectation. The formula below was also noted by Jenčová

Corollary 2.16

Let
\sigma

\displaystyle F(a)=(E\sigma)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\,E(\sigma^{\frac{1}{2}}a\sigma^{\frac{1}{2}})\,(E\sigma)^{-\frac{1}{2}},

(2.32)

where
E:L({\mathcal{H}})\to J
is the trace-preserving conditional expectation.

Proof.

We first show
\langle a,b\rangle_{E\sigma}=\langle a,b\rangle_{\sigma}
for all

By polarization, it suffices to show this for

\displaystyle\langle a,a\rangle_{\sigma}=\langle Ea,Ea\rangle_{\sigma}\leq\langle a,a\rangle_{E\sigma}=\langle Fa,Fa\rangle_{E\sigma}\leq\langle a,a\rangle_{F^{*}E\sigma}=\langle a,a\rangle_{\sigma}.

Thus, equality holds everywhere, and we may conclude

for all

Using that

for

\displaystyle\langle Fa,b\rangle_{E\sigma}=\langle EFa,b\rangle_{E\sigma}=\langle Fa,Eb\rangle_{E\sigma}=\langle Fa,Eb\rangle_{\sigma}=\langle a,Eb\rangle_{\sigma},\qquad a,b\in L({\mathcal{H}}).

But

\displaystyle\langle Fa,b\rangle_{E\sigma}=\tr((E\sigma)^{\frac{1}{2}}F(a)(E\sigma)^{\frac{1}{2}}b)\quad\quad\text{and}\quad\quad\langle a,Eb\rangle_{\sigma}=\tr(E(\sigma^{\frac{1}{2}}a\sigma^{\frac{1}{2}})b).

was arbitrary, we find

If a conditional expectation

A technical lemma that we will need later shows that the range of a faithful conditional expectation, in fact, coincides with the multiplicative domain:

Lemma 2.17

Let

It is clear that

2.5
Structure theory of abstract J*-algebras

So far, we have considered J*-algebras as collections of operators on a given Hilbert space
{\mathcal{H}}
.
It is sometimes better to regard J*-algebras as abstract objects and to regard the action on a given Hilbert space as a representation.
We make the following non-standard definition:
17
17

17

Our notion of an abstract J*-algebra is known in the mathematics literature as a finite-dimensional JC*- or JW*-algebra (cp.
Footnote

7
).
JC*- and JW*-algebras are a special case of the more general notion of JB*- or JBW*-algebra, which do not necessarily admit representations as operators on a Hilbert space
[
44
]
.

Definition 2.20
.

We define an
abstract J*-algebra

as a finite-dimensional *-vector space
J
, equipped with a map
\{{\,\cdot\,},{\,\cdot\,}\}:J\times J\to J
, and a special element
1=1^{*}\in J
, such that there exists a Hilbert space
{\mathcal{H}}
and an injective *-preserving linear map
\pi:J\to L({\mathcal{H}})
, which is unital, i.e., satisfies
\pi(1)=\text{1}
, and takes the Jordan product on
J
to the Jordan product of
L({\mathcal{H}})
, i.e.,

\pi(\{a,b\})=\{\pi(a),\pi(b)\},\qquad a,b\in L({\mathcal{H}}).

(2.38)

A tuple
(\pi,{\mathcal{H}})
with these properties is called a
(J*-)representation
.

If
(\pi,{\mathcal{H}})
is a representation of an abstract J*-algebra
J
, the range
\pi(J)
is a J*-algebra on
{\mathcal{H}}
.
Thus, the Jordan product
\{{\,\cdot\,},{\,\cdot\,}\}
on an abstract J*-algebra satisfies all algebraic properties enjoyed by the Jordan product on
L({\mathcal{H}})
.
If
({\mathcal{H}}_{i},\pi_{i})
,
i=1,2
, are J*-representation of an abstract J*-algebra
J
, then
\pi_{1}\circ\pi_{2}^{-1}
is an J*-isomorphism between the J*-algebras
\pi_{i}(J)\subset L({\mathcal{H}}_{i})
,
i=1,2
(note that the
\pi_{j}
are injective).
Consequently, all properties of J*-algebras that are preserved by J*-isomorphisms can be understood as properties of abstract J*-algebras.

Every finite-dimensional *-algebra
A
is an abstract J*-algebra in the obvious way, and every concrete J*-algebra
J\subset L({\mathcal{H}})
on a Hilbert space
{\mathcal{H}}
is an abstract J*-algebra by taking
\pi
as the embedding
J\hookrightarrow L({\mathcal{H}})
.
A simple example of a J*-representation of the *-algebra
L({\mathbb{C}}^{n})
, which is not a *-representation, is the following:

Example 2.21
.

The map
a\mapsto a\oplus a^{t}
is a J*-representation of
L({\mathbb{C}}^{n})
on
{\mathbb{C}}^{2n}={\mathbb{C}}^{n}\oplus{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
.

It makes sense to speak of J*-homomorphisms between abstract J*-algebras.
More generally, UP maps between abstract J*-algebras make sense because the latter have a well-defined positive cone
J^{+}
(cp. (
2.4
)) and unit element

We will often drop the adjective

in cases where the distinction between abstract and concretely represented J*-algebras is either irrelevant or clear from the context.
In the following, we discuss the structure of abstract J*-algebras.
We begin by discussing the center and direct sum decomposition into so-called J*-factors. Then, we describe the classification of J*-factors.

The center

of an abstract J*-algebra

The following Lemma summarizes several characterizations of central elements for a representation

Next, we shall consider a canonical way to embed an abstract J*-algebra into a (finite-dimensional) *-algebra:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces J}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces B}

2.6
Representations of J*-algebras

In the previous subsection, we considered properties of J*-algebras that are independent of the chosen representation.
We also need to understand representation-dependent properties.
In the following we describe part of the representation theory of J*-algebras.

As is well-known, any (finite-dimensional) *-algebra
A
is *-isomorphic to
A\cong\oplus_{j}L({\mathcal{H}}_{j})
and any *-representation of
A
on a finite-dimensional Hilbert space is of the form

{\mathcal{H}}\cong\oplus_{j}{\mathcal{H}}_{j}\otimes{\mathcal{L}}_{j},\quad A\cong\oplus_{j}L({\mathcal{H}}_{j})\otimes\text{1}.

(2.44)

Thus, a *-representation is, up to unitary equivalence, determined by the multiplicities
(l_{j})
,
l_{j}=\dim{\mathcal{L}}_{j}
.
We will see that the corresponding statement for J*-algebras is false.
For instance, the defining representation of

on
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
is not unitarily equivalent to the J*-representation

on
{\mathbb{C}}^{2n}
(see

In this case, the non-equivalence of the two J*-representations is clear from the different Hilbert space dimensions.
A more useful invariant that we can associate with a J*-representation

is the isomorphism class of the *-algebra
\mathrm{\textup{*-}alg}(\pi(J))
generated by the representation.
This invariant is insensitive to changes in the multiplicity of the representations.

Let us consider a J*-representation
(\pi,{\mathcal{H}})
of a J*-algebra
J
.
Let

be the direct sum decomposition into J*-factors.
Let

be the unit of

as an element of

Then

is a family of projections on

with

, and

is evidently a direct sum of J*-representations

, where
{\mathcal{H}}_{i}=\pi(p_{i}){\mathcal{H}}

Therefore, to understand J*-representations of general J*-algebras, it is sufficient to understand those of J*-factors.

For our later discussions of various quantum divergences, we develop a minimal amount of

Let

For

For

Moreover, for

and

\displaystyle\tr((\Gamma_{\sigma}^{\frac{1}{q}}a)^{*}(\Gamma_{\sigma}^{\frac{1}{p}}b))=\langle a,b\rangle_{\sigma}.

(2.61)

The conditional expectation

3
Faithful statistical experiments

We begin by introducing some terminology regarding statistical experiments.
A
statistical experiment

(\rho_{\theta})_{\theta\in\Theta}
is simply a collection of states on a Hilbert space
{\mathcal{H}}
. Here
\Theta
is some index set, which we assume to be finite (purely for simplicity, all our results can be adapted to the case where
\Theta
is a general measure space).
The interpretation of
\Theta
is as a space of outcomes of an experiment which results in the preparation of state
\rho_{\theta}
. The experiment is a
dichotomy
if
|\Theta|=2
.
We will typically denote dichotomies as
(\rho,\sigma)
.

We say that two statistical experiments
(\rho_{\theta})
and
(\sigma_{\theta})
with the same outcome space
\Theta
, but on possibly different Hilbert spaces, are
PTP-equivalent
, written
(\rho_{\theta})\overset{\text{\tiny PTP}}{\longleftrightarrow}(\sigma_{\theta})
, if there exists UP maps
S,T
such that

\displaystyle T^{*}\rho_{\theta}=\sigma_{\theta},\quad S^{*}\sigma_{\theta}=\rho_{\theta},\qquad\theta\in\Theta.

(3.1)

We also say that the two experiments can be
interconverted
via PTP maps.
Similarly, they are CPTP-equivalent if they can be interconverted by CPTP maps. In
[
13
]
, it was shown that CPTP-equivalence can be determined from the Koashi-Imoto decomposition
[
29
]
of a statistical experiment.

Suppose
(\rho_{\theta})\overset{\text{\tiny PTP}}{\longleftrightarrow}(\sigma_{\theta})
with interconverting maps
T
and
S
.
Then, by linearity, we have

\displaystyle S^{*}T^{*}\rho=\rho,\quad\rho\in\mathrm{conv}\{\rho_{\theta}\}_{\theta\in\Theta},\quad T^{*}S^{*}\sigma=\sigma,\quad\sigma\in\mathrm{conv}\{\sigma_{\theta}\}_{\theta\in\Theta},

(3.2)

where
“
\mathrm{conv}
”
denotes the convex hull.
This shows that PTP-equivalence is really a question about the convex hull of a statistical experiment.

In particular, we can find an enumeration of the extremal points
\rho_{\theta_{i}}
,
i\in I
, of
\mathrm{conv}\{\rho_{\theta}\}
and the extremal points
\sigma_{\theta_{i}}
,
i\in I
of
\mathrm{conv}\{\sigma_{\theta}\}
such that

\displaystyle T^{*}\rho_{\theta_{i}}=\sigma_{\theta_{i}},\quad S^{*}\sigma_{\theta_{i}}=\rho_{\theta_{i}},\quad i\in I.

(3.3)

Set
\overline{\rho_{\Theta}}=\frac{1}{|I|}\sum_{i\in I}\rho_{\theta i}
.
Since

\displaystyle\rho_{\theta_{|I|}}=n\overline{\rho_{\Theta}}-\sum_{i=1}^{|I|-1}\rho_{\theta i},

(3.4)

it follows that
(\rho_{\theta})_{\theta\in\Theta}
and
(\sigma_{\theta})_{\theta\in\Theta}
are PTP-equivalent if and only if

\displaystyle(\rho_{\theta_{1}},\ldots,\rho_{\theta_{|I|-1}},\overline{\rho_{\Theta}})\ \overset{\text{\tiny PTP}}{\longleftrightarrow}\ (\sigma_{\theta_{1}},\ldots,\sigma_{\theta_{|I|-1}},\overline{\sigma_{\theta}}).

(3.5)

Note that
\rho_{\theta_{i}}\ll\overline{\rho_{\Theta}}
.
Hence, for questions regarding PTP-equivalence, one can, without loss of generality, assume that any statistical experiment contains a state whose support contains the support of all the other states.

Definition 3.1
.

We call
(\rho_{\theta})_{\theta\in\Theta}

faithful
if
for any
a\in L({\mathcal{H}})
,
\tr(\rho_{\theta}a^{*}a)=0
for all
\theta\in\Theta
implies
a=0
.

Let us define the support of a statistical experiment as

\displaystyle\supp\,(\rho_{\theta})_{\theta\in\Theta}=\bigvee_{\theta\in\Theta}\supp\rho_{\theta},

(3.6)

where, for a collection
p_{i}
,
i\in I
, of projections, the projection
\vee_{i}p_{i}
is the projection onto the linear subspace spanned by the ranges of the projections
p_{i}
.

Lemma 3.2
.

The following are equivalent:

1.

The statistical experiment
(\rho_{\theta})
is faithful;

2.

\supp(\rho_{\theta})_{\theta\in\Theta}=\text{1}
;

3.

\mathrm{conv}\{\rho_{\theta}\}_{\theta\in\Theta}
contains a faithful state;

4.

The state
\sum_{\theta}\mu(\theta)\rho_{\theta}
is faithful for any faithful probability measure
\mu
on
\Theta
.

Proof.

Immediate.
∎

The following lemma shows that for questions regarding PTP-equivalence, we can always assume faithfulness.

Lemma 3.3
.

Any statistical experiment
(\rho_{\theta})
is CPTP-equivalent to the faithful statistical experiment resulting from restricting
(\rho_{\theta})
to the subspace
{\mathcal{K}}=\supp(\overline{\rho_{\Theta}}){\mathcal{H}}
.

Proof.

Write
s=\supp(\overline{\rho_{\theta}})
and denote by
\iota:L({\mathcal{K}})\to L({\mathcal{H}})
the natural embedding, so that
\iota(\text{1})=s
. Then
T(x)=\iota(x)+(\text{1}-s)\tr(\sigma\iota(x))
for
x\in L({\mathcal{K}})
and
S(y)=\iota^{-1}(sys)
for
y\in L({\mathcal{H}})
define UP maps that interconvert the two statistical experiments. Both maps are clearly UCP maps.
∎

Combined with the discussion above, we conclude that we can always assume that a statistical experiment contains a faithful state.

4
CPTP and PTP sufficiency

Sufficiency encodes the idea that a strict subset of all possible measurements already encodes all the relevant information of a statistical experiment
(\rho_{\theta})_{\theta\in\Theta}
.
As discussed in the introduction, there are different ways to formalize this notion.
If
T
is a
(\rho_{\theta})
-preserving UCP map on
L({\mathcal{H}})
, i.e., a UCP map such that

T^{*}\rho_{\theta}=\rho_{\theta},\qquad\theta\in\Theta,

(4.1)

then, for every observable
x\in L({\mathcal{H}})
and
\theta\in\Theta
, the observable
Tx
has the same expectation value as
x
:

\tr(\rho_{\theta}\,Tx)=\tr(T^{*}\rho_{\theta}\,x)=\tr(\rho_{\theta}x),\qquad\theta\in\Theta.

Thus, if one only looks at expectation values, it is sufficient to consider observables in the range of
T
.

The range
K=\mathrm{Ran}(T)
of a UP map
T
is an
operator system
(see
Definition

2.1
).
An operator system is linearly spanned by the effect observables it contains, i.e.,
K=\lin\,[0,\text{1}]_{K}
, where
[0,\text{1}]_{K}=\{x\in K:0\leq x\leq\text{1}\}
.
We make the following definition:

Definition 4.1
.

An operator system
K\subset L({\mathcal{H}})
is
PTP-sufficient
(resp.
CPTP-sufficient
) for a statistical experiment
(\rho_{\theta})
if there exists a
(\rho_{\theta})
-preserving UP (resp. UCP) map
T
on
L({\mathcal{H}})
whose range is contained in
K
.

We will later consider a third notion of sufficiency for operator systems, defined in terms of Bayesian hypothesis testing.
Clearly, CPTP-sufficiency implies PTP-sufficiency.
Unsurprisingly, the converse is false in general.
However, as we will discuss shortly, the two notions become equivalent if
K
is a *-algebra
[
63
]
.

Before we continue our discussion of sufficiency, let us recall that any statistical experiment is equivalent to a faithful one via CPTP maps.
Hence, we can make the following assumption:

Assumption
.

Throughout this section, we assume (without loss of generality) that statistical experiments are faithful.

If
A\subset L({\mathcal{H}})
is a *-algebra, our notion of CPTP-sufficiency agrees with the notion of sufficiency studied by Petz and others
[
31
,
32
,
26
,
27
,
28
]
.
Petz proved that among all CPTP-sufficient *-algebras, there is a (necessarily unique) CPTP-sufficient *-algebra
A_{(\rho_{\theta})}
that is contained in all other CPTP-sufficient *-algebras.
We refer to
A_{(\rho_{\theta})}
as the
minimal sufficient *-algebra
.
It is given by

A_{(\rho_{\theta})}\,=\bigcap\,\big\{\,\mathrm{Fix}(T)\ :\ \text{$T$ is a $(\rho_{\theta})$-preserving UCP map on $L({\mathcal{H}})$}\,\big\}.

(4.2)

It is clear from (
4.2
) that
A_{(\rho_{\theta})}
is not only minimal among CPTP-sufficient *-algebras, but also among all CPTP-sufficient operator systems.
If
T:L({\mathcal{H}})\to A_{(\rho_{\theta})}
is a
(\rho_{\theta})
-preserving UCP map, then (
4.2
) implies that
T
restricts to the identity on
A_{(\rho_{\theta})}
, so that
T
must be a conditional expectation onto.
21
21

21

Such a conditional expectation is necessarily unique (see
Corollary

2.16
).

Thus, there exists a
(\rho_{\theta})
-preserving conditional expectation onto
A_{(\rho_{\theta})}
, which is the unique
(\rho_{\theta})
-preserving UCP map into
A_{(\rho_{\theta})}
.
Let us summarize the above discussion:

\begin{gathered}\textit{There is a minimal CPTP-sufficient operator system, it is a *-algebra,}\\
\textit{and it admits a state-preserving conditional expectation}.\end{gathered}

(4.3)

For the PTP case, we will establish the following Theorem, which is essentially contained in the works
[
74
,
63
]
of A. Łuczak, and partly contained in the Bachelor thesis of O. Skodda
[
75
]
:

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
(\rho_{\theta})_{\theta\in\Theta}
be a faithful statistical experiment. Then:

1.

There is a minimal PTP-sufficient operator system
J_{(\rho_{\theta})}
.
It is the J*-algebra given by

\displaystyle J_{(\rho_{\theta})}\,=\bigcap\,\big\{\mathrm{Fix}(T)\ :\ \text{$T$ is a $(\rho_{\theta})$-preserving UP map on $L({\mathcal{H}})$}\,\big\}.

(4.4)

where the intersection is over
(\rho_{\theta})
-preserving UP maps
T:L({\mathcal{H}})\to L({\mathcal{H}})

2.

There is a unique
(\rho_{\theta})
-preserving UP map
F
of
L({\mathcal{H}})
into
J_{(\rho_{\theta})}
, which is a conditional expectation onto
J_{(\rho_{\theta})}
.

3.

The *-algebra generated by
J_{(\rho_{\theta})}
is the minimal sufficient one, i.e.,

\mathrm{\textup{*-}alg}(J_{(\rho_{\theta})})=A_{(\rho_{\theta})}

(4.5)

Since the statements in the theorem are not explicit in
[
74
,
63
]

(Łuczak’s work is concerned with W*-algebras, Jordan algebras only appear as a proving tool), we provide a self-contained proof adapted to our setting below.

Before we come to the proof, let us discuss some important consequences.
Based on

\begin{gathered}\textit{There is a minimal PTP-sufficient operator system, it is a J*-algebra,}\\
\textit{and it admits a state-preserving conditional expectation}.\end{gathered}

We know that minimal (C)PTP-sufficient operator systems exist.
Thus, in principle, we have a complete understanding of (C)PTP-sufficiency: An operator system is (C)PTP-sufficient if and only if it contains the minimal (C)PTP-sufficient one.
Hence,

A *-algebra

Thus, we can and will, in the following, drop the prefix CPTP when discussing sufficient *-algebras.

Remark 4.8

The Koashi-Imoto theorem

As for *-algebras, J*-algebras have a direct sum of simple J*-factors (J*-algebras without non-trivial J*-ideals).

Therefore, for every

{\mathcal{H}}=\oplus_{k}\oplus_{j\in I(k)}{\mathcal{H}}_{j}\otimes{\mathcal{K}}_{j},\quad J_{(\rho_{\theta})}=\oplus_{k}J_{k},\quad\mathrm{\text{*-}alg}(J_{k})=\oplus_{j\in I(k)}L({\mathcal{H}}_{j})\otimes\text{1}_{{\mathcal{K}}_{j}}.

Looking at the representation theory of finite-dimensional Jordan algebras

8
Sufficiency and Bayesian hypothesis testing

Suppose a quantum system is known to be either in the quantum state
\rho
or in the state
\sigma
.
To distinguish the two cases, one performs a binary measurement
(x,\text{1}-x)
, where
x
(resp.
\text{1}-x
) is associated with
\rho
(resp.
\sigma
).
There are for possible cases, two error cases (mistaking
\rho
for
\sigma
or
\sigma
for
\rho
) and two success cases (correctly returning
\rho
or correctly returning
\sigma
).
If
(\rho,\sigma)
occur (or are believed to occur) with probabilities
(p,1-p)
with
p\in(0,1)
, the success probability is

p\tr(\rho x)+(1-p)\tr(\sigma(\text{1}-x))=(1-p)+p\tr((\rho-\tfrac{1-p}{p}\sigma)x))

(8.1)

Maximizing the success probability over all possible measurements, we obtain

P_{\text{succ.},p}(\rho,\sigma)=1-p+p\tr((\rho-\tfrac{1-p}{p}\sigma)^{+})=p+(1-p)\tr((\sigma-\tfrac{p}{1-p}\rho)^{+}),

(8.2)

where the second equality follows from (writing
t=(1-p)/p
)

pt+p\tr((\rho-t\sigma)^{+})=p\left(t+t\tr((\sigma-\tfrac{1}{t}\rho)^{-})\right)=p\left(1+t(\tr(\sigma-\tfrac{1}{t}\rho)^{+})\right)=p+pt\tr((\sigma-\tfrac{1}{t}\rho)^{+}).

Let
s=\supp(\rho,\sigma)
be the support projection of the dichotomy
(\rho,\sigma)
. Then for any test
x

\displaystyle\tr((\rho-t\sigma)x)=\tr(s(\rho-t\sigma)sx)=\tr((\rho-t\sigma)sxs).

(8.3)

Therefore, tests can always be restricted to the subspace
{\mathcal{K}}=\supp(\rho,\sigma)
without affecting the success probability. This means that Bayesian hypothesis is only concerned with the subspace
{\mathcal{K}}
.
In other words, we can assume without loss of generality that all dichotomies are faithful.

In the following, we will express the success probability using the
Hockey stick divergence
, defined as
[
37
,
38
]

E_{t}(\rho\|\sigma)=\max_{0\leq x\leq 1}\tr((\rho-t\sigma)x)-(1-t)^{+}=\tr((\rho-t\sigma)^{+})-(1-t)^{+},\qquad t\geq 0.

(8.4)

The term
(1-t)^{+}=\max\{1-t,0\}
in the definition ensures that
E_{t}(\rho\|\rho)=0
for all
t\geq 0
.

Using the Hockey stick divergence, the success probability takes the form

P_{\text{succ.},p}(\rho,\sigma)=\begin{cases}(1-p)+pE_{(1-p)/p}(\rho\|\sigma)),&\text{if }p\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\\
p+pE_{(1-p)/p}(\rho\|\sigma),&\text{if }p\geq\tfrac{1}{2}.\end{cases}

(8.5)

Since
E_{t}
fulfills

E_{t}(\rho\|\sigma)=tE_{1/t}(\sigma\|\rho)

[
38
]
, the apparent asymmetry between
\rho
and
\sigma
is only superficial.
Evidently, the Hockey stick divergences and the success probability fulfill the data-processing inequality: If
T:L({\mathcal{K}})\to L({\mathcal{H}})
is a UP map, we have

\displaystyle E_{t}(T^{*}\rho\|T^{*}\sigma)-E_{t}(\rho\|\sigma)=\max_{0\leq T(x)\leq\text{1}}\tr((\rho-t\sigma)T(x))-\max_{0\leq x\leq\text{1}}\tr((\rho-t\sigma)x)\leq 0.

(8.6)

) because we consider operator systems, which are unital by definition.
For the proof of

, we need the following Lemma, essentially taken from
[

:

Lemma 8.4
.

For any pair of hermitian operators
a,b\in L({\mathcal{H}})
, the map
t\mapsto[a>tb]
is right continuous, while
t\mapsto[a\geq tb]
left continuous.
The two functions have finitely many (at most
\dim({\mathcal{H}})
) points of discontinuity and coincide at all other points

[a>t\sigma]=[a\geq tb].

(8.12)

Proof.

As noted in the proof of
[
36
, Lem. 2.1]
, the spectral decomposition

a-tb=\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}(t)|v_{i}(t)\rangle\langle v_{i}(t)|,\quad t\in{\mathbb{R}},

(8.13)

with analytic eigenpairs
(\lambda_{i}(t),v_{i}(t))
has the property that each eigenvalue function
\lambda_{i}(t)
is monotonically decreasing in
t
.
Let
\bullet
denote either
“
>
”
or
“
\geq
”
.
We have

[a\bullet tb]=\sum_{i}\chi_{\bullet}(\lambda_{i}(t))\,|v_{i}(t)\rangle\langle v_{i}(t)|,\qquad t>0,

(8.14)

where
\chi_{>}
and
\chi_{\geq}
are the characteristic function of
(0,\infty)
and
[0,\infty)
, respectively.
Since
\chi_{>}
is left continuous and the
\lambda_{i}(t)
are continuous and monotonically decreasing,
[a>tb]
is right continuous in
t
.
The left continuity of
[a\geq tb]
follows analogously.
The points of discontinuity are the
t
values for which (at least) one of the eigenvalue functions
\lambda_{i}(t)
is zero.
Away from points of discontinuity, the two cases coincide.
∎

Proof of
Proposition

.

Clearly,
K
is Bayes-sufficient if
[\rho>t\sigma]\in K
for all
t>0
. We show the converse:
If

is sufficient,

implies that, for each

, there is an operator

with

In the remainder of this section, we study how the minimal Bayes-sufficient operator system

It is clear that, for an operator system

.

be a faithful dichotomy on a Hilbert space

.

8.3
and

.

be a faithful dichotomy on
{\mathcal{H}}

.

In fact, the following corollary shows that the minimal sufficient J*-algebra of any statistical experiment is generated by Neyman-Pearson tests.

\displaystyle=\mathrm{\textup{J*-}alg}((E\rho_{\theta})_{\theta})=\mathrm{\textup{J*-}alg}((E\rho_{\theta})_{\theta},E\omega)=\mathrm{\textup{J*-}alg}\big(\cup_{\theta}\mathrm{\textup{J*-}alg}(E\rho_{\theta},E\omega)\big)

Remark 9.2

\displaystyle=d_{\hat{\rho}|\hat{\sigma}}.

and the result would follow from

Remark 9.3
.

We note a, maybe non-obvious, formula for

that may be of independent interest.
Given a faithful state

\displaystyle\varphi_{\sigma}(a)=\int_{0}^{1}\sigma^{s-\frac{1}{2}}a\sigma^{\frac{1}{2}-s}\,ds=\int_{0}^{1}\{\sigma^{s-\frac{1}{2}},a,\sigma^{\frac{1}{2}-s}\}\,ds.

Consider the logarithmic derivative

\displaystyle L_{\sigma}(\rho):=\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{\log(\sigma+t\rho)-\log(\sigma)}{t}=\int_{0}^{\infty}[\rho>t\sigma]\,dt,

(9.5)

We thus have

\displaystyle d_{\rho|\sigma}

\displaystyle=\sigma^{-\frac{1}{2}}\rho\sigma^{-\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=\sigma^{-\frac{1}{2}}\big(L^{-1}_{\sigma}(L_{\sigma}(\rho))\big)\sigma^{-\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=\varphi_{\sigma}(L_{\sigma}(\rho))

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\varphi_{\sigma}([\rho>t\sigma])\,dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{1}\{\sigma^{s-\frac{1}{2}},[\rho>t\sigma],\sigma^{\frac{1}{2}-s}\}\,ds\,dt.

Since

onto

and
\sigma
with

and

in the formulae above.

and

A.1
Jordan symmetries vs unitary symmetries

An (anti-)unitary symmetry of a dichotomy
(\rho,\sigma)
on a Hilbert space
{\mathcal{H}}
is an (anti-)unitary operator
u

{\mathcal{H}}
such that

u\rho u^{*}=\rho,\qquad u\sigma u^{*}=\sigma.

(A.1)

A unitary symmetry is trivial if
u\propto 1
is a scalar.
The absence of non-trivial unitary symmetries is easily characterized:

Lemma A.1
.

Let
(\rho,\sigma)
be a faithful dichotomy.
The following are equivalent:

1.

(\rho,\sigma)
only have trivial unitary symmetries;

2.

L({\mathcal{H}})
is the minimal sufficient *-algebra;

3.

the Koashi-Imoto decomposition
{\mathcal{H}}=\oplus_{j}{\mathcal{K}}_{j}\otimes{\mathcal{L}}_{j}
consists of a single block
{\mathcal{K}}
with trivial multiplicity, i.e.,
\dim{\mathcal{L}}=1
.

4.

the pair
(\rho,\sigma)
is irreducible, i.e., all jointly invariant subspaces are trivial.

Proof.

1

\Leftrightarrow

4
follows from the standard fact that a pair
(a,b)
of hermitian operators on a finite-dimensional Hilbert space is irreducible if and only if the only unitaries commuting with both
a
and
b
are the trivial ones.

2

\Leftrightarrow

3
is explained in
Remark

4.8
.
It follows from the Koashi-Imoto decomposition (see
Remark

4.8
) that the group of unitary symmetries of
(\rho,\sigma)
is the unitary group of the commutant of the minimal sufficient *-algebra
A_{(\rho,\sigma)}
.
This unitary group is trivial if and only if
A_{(\rho,\sigma)}=L({\mathcal{H}})
. This shows
1

\Leftrightarrow

2
, which finishes the proof.
∎

Appendix B
2-generated J*-algebras

Proposition B.1
.

An abstract J*-algebra
J
is 2-generated if and only if it is J*-isomorphic to a direct sum of the following J*-factors:

•

L({\mathbb{C}}^{d})
,
d\geq 1
;

•

L({\mathbb{C}}^{d})^{t}
,
d\geq 2
;

•

L({\mathbb{C}}^{2d})^{\beta}
,
d\geq 4
.

We see that the class of 2-generated J*-algebras is almost the same as the class of universally reversible J*-algebras, except that the latter also allows the J*-factor
L({\mathbb{C}}^{2d})^{\beta}
with
d=3
.
It is known that 2-generated J*-algebras are reversible in any representation
[
44
, Cor. 2.3.8]
and, hence, universally reversible.

We say that a real Jordan algebra is 2-generated if it is generated by two of its elements and the identity.

Lemma B.2
.

The Jordan algebra
\mathrm{Sym}_{d}({\mathbb{R}})
of symmetric real matrices
d\times d
is generated by two elements for all
d\geq 1
.

Proof.

We let
e_{l,m}
with
l,m\in\{0,\ldots,d-1\}
denote the standard matrix units.
We set
f_{m,n}=e_{m,n}+e_{n,m}
,
m,n=0,\ldots,d-1
, where
e_{m,n}
denote the standard matrix units, and note the following identity

\{f_{l,m},f_{m,n}\}=\frac{1}{2}f_{l,n},\quad\text{if $l,m,n$ are distinct}.

(B.1)

Clearly,
\mathrm{Sym}_{d}({\mathbb{R}})=\lin\{f_{l,m}\}_{l,m}
.
We set

a=\sum_{l=0}^{d-1}l\,e_{l,l},\qquad b=\sum_{l=0}^{d-2}f_{l,l+1}

(B.2)

and claim that
(a,b)
generate
\mathrm{Sym}_{d}({\mathbb{R}})
.
Let
J
be the Jordan algebra generated by
a
and
b
.
Clearly
a
and
b
are symmetric, so that
J\subset\mathrm{Sym}_{d}({\mathbb{R}})
.
We have
e_{l,l}=\frac{1}{2}f_{l,l}\in J
,
l=0,\ldots,d-1
, because these are the spectral projections of
a
.
We have to show
f_{l,m}\in J
for
l<m<d
.
We have

\displaystyle\{e_{l,l},b,e_{l+1,l+1}\}

\displaystyle=f_{l,l+1}.

Thus, we have
f_{l,l+1}\in J
for
0\leq l\leq d-2
.
For
d=2
, this finishes the proof. Assume now
d>2
.
By (
B.1
), we have

f_{l,l+2}=2\{f_{l,l+1},f_{l+1,l+2}\}\in J.

(B.3)

Iterating (the next step would be
f_{l,l+3}=2\{f_{l,l+1},f_{l+1,l+2}\}\in J
) shows
f_{l,m}\in\mathrm{\textup{J*-}alg}(a,b)
for all
l<m<d
. This finishes the proof.
∎

Lemma B.3
.

For
d\geq 4
, the Jordan algebra
\mathrm{Herm}_{d}(\mathbb{H})
of hermitian quaternionic
3\times 3
matrices is 2-generated.

Proof.

For a quaternion
q\in\mathbb{H}
, we define

f_{l,m}(q)=qe_{l,m}+\bar{q}e_{m,l}.

(B.4)

Note that
f_{l,m}(1)
is the matrix
f_{l,m}
in the proof of
Lemma

B.2
.
The relation (
B.1
) generalizes to

\{f_{l,m}(q),f_{m,n}(p)\}=\frac{1}{2}f_{l,n}(qp)\quad\text{if $l,m,n$ are distinct},

(B.5)

where
p,q\in\mathbb{H}
.
Then
\mathrm{Herm}_{d}(\mathbb{H})
is spanned by
f_{l,m}(1)
,
f_{l,m}(i)
,
f_{l,m}(j)
and
f_{l,m}(k)
.
Let
J
be the Jordan algebra generated by the matrices

a=\sum_{l=0}^{d-1}l\,e_{l,l},\qquad b=\sum_{l=0}^{d-2}f_{l,l+1}+f_{0,2}(i)+f_{1,3}(j)

(B.6)

As in the proof of
Lemma

B.2
, we have
f_{l,m}(1)\in J
for all
l,m
.
In particular,
e_{l,l}\in J
for all
l
.
Moreover, we have

f_{0,2}(i)=2\{e_{0,0},b,e_{2,2}\}\in J,\qquad f_{1,3}(j)=2\{e_{1,1},b,e_{3,3}\}\in J.

(B.7)

We use (
B.7
) and (
B.5
) to obtain

f_{l,m}(i)=4\{f_{l,0}(1),\{f_{0,2}(i),f_{2,m}(1)\}\}\in J.

(B.8)

Analogously, we get
f_{l,m}(j)\in J
.
To see

, pick some

B.5

∎

Lemma B.5
.

The Jordan algebra
H_{3}(\mathbb{H})
of hermitian quaternionic
3\times 3
matrices is not 2-generated.

Proof.

We show that the Jordan algebra
J
generated by an arbitrary pair
a,b\in H_{3}(\mathbb{H})
and the identity matrix is always contained in an isomorphic copy of the hermitian complex matrices.
Indeed, by the spectral theorem for quaternionic matrices
[
83
]
, there is a unitary quaternionic matrix
u\in M_{3}(\mathbb{H})
diagonalizing
a
, i.e.,
u
is such that
uau^{*}=\mathrm{diag}(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3})
for
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3}\in{\mathbb{R}}
.
Set
a^{\prime}=uau^{*}

b^{\prime}=ubu^{*}
.
Note that the map
u({\,\cdot\,})u^{*}
is a Jordan isomorphism on
H_{3}(\mathbb{H})
.
Let

b^{\prime}=\begin{pmatrix}\beta_{1}&x&y\\
\bar{x}&\beta_{2}&z\\
\bar{y}&\bar{z}&\beta_{3}\end{pmatrix},\qquad\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\beta_{3}\in{\mathbb{R}},\ x,y,z\in\mathbb{H}.

(B.10)

We set
v=\mathrm{diag}(1,\lvert x\rvert^{-1}x,\lvert y\rvert^{-1}y)
(setting the ratio to 1 if
x
or
y
is zero).
Then
va^{\prime}v^{*}=a^{\prime}
because
a^{\prime}
is diagonal and real-valued and

vb^{\prime}v^{*}=\begin{pmatrix}\beta_{1}&\lvert x\rvert&\lvert y\rvert\\
\lvert x\rvert&\beta_{2}&q\\
\lvert y\rvert&\bar{q}&\beta_{3}\end{pmatrix},\qquad q:=\lvert x\rvert^{-1}\lvert y\rvert^{-1}xz\bar{y}.

(B.11)

We now pick an imaginary unit
s
, i.e., a quaternion with
s^{2}=-1
, such that
q\in{\mathbb{C}}_{s}={\mathbb{R}}+s{\mathbb{R}}
.
By construction, both
vb^{\prime}v^{*}
and
va^{\prime}v^{*}=a^{\prime}
are
{\mathbb{C}}_{s}
-valued matrices.
The hermitian
{\mathbb{C}}_{s}
-valued matrices
H_{3}({\mathbb{C}}_{s})
are closed under the Jordan product (they are a Jordan-isomorphic copy of the complex hermitian matrices).
Therefore,
J
is contained in
u^{*}v^{*}H_{3}({\mathbb{C}}_{s})uv
, which is Jordan isomorphic to
H_{3}({\mathbb{C}}_{s})
, which is Jordan isomorphic to
H_{3}({\mathbb{C}})=L({\mathbb{C}}^{3})_{h}
.
∎

Lemma B.6
.

Let
J_{1},\ldots J_{n}
be abstract J*-algebras, then
\oplus_{k}J_{k}
is 2-generated if and only if each
J_{k}
is 2-generated.

Proof.

Let
e_{k}\in\oplus_{k}J_{k}
be the central projection corresponding to the unit
1\in J_{k}
.
If
\oplus_{k}J_{k}
is generated by the unit and a pair of hermitian elements
a,b
, then each
J_{k}
is generated by the unit and
a_{k}=e_{k}ae_{k}
,
b_{k}=e_{k}be_{k}
.
Conversely, assume that
J_{k}
is generated by
(e_{k},a_{k},b_{k})
for each
k
.
Pick weights
\lambda_{k}>0
such that the spectra of the elements
\lambda_{k}a_{k}
,
k=1,\ldots,n
, are distinct and the spectra of the elements
\lambda_{k}b_{k}
,
k=1,\ldots,n
, are distinct.
Then, for each
k
, the elements
a_{k}
and
b_{k}
(now regarded as elements of the direct sum) can be obtained via the functional calculus from
a=\oplus_{l}a_{l}
and
b=\oplus_{k}b_{k}
, respectively. Therefore, the J*-algebra generated by
(1,a,b)
contains the direct sum of the J*-algebras generated by
(e_{k},a_{k},b_{k})
, which is
J
.
∎

Appendix C
Proof of Frenkel’s integral formula for approximately finite-dimensional von Neumann algebras

For normal states
\omega,\varphi
on a von Neumann algebra
M
, the Hockey stick divergence is defined as

E_{t}(\omega\|\varphi)=(\omega-t\varphi)^{+}(1)=\frac{1}{2}\big(\lVert\omega-t\phi\rVert+1-t\big),\qquad t>1,

(C.1)

where
\chi^{+}
denotes the positive part of a normal linear functional
\chi\in M_{*}
.

Proposition C.1
.

Let
M
be an approximately finite-dimensional von Neumann algebra and let
\omega,\varphi
be normal states on
M
.
Then

D(\omega\|\varphi)=\int_{1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{t}E_{t}(\omega\|\varphi)+\frac{1}{t^{2}}E_{t}(\varphi\|\omega)\right)dt.

(C.2)

In the particular case
M=L({\mathcal{H}})
with
\dim{\mathcal{H}}=\infty
, the statement was shown in
[
45
]
, albeit with a more complicated proof.

Proof.

Frenkel showed his formula for density operators on finite dimensional Hilbert spaces
[
25
]
.
Algebraically phrased, this means that the formula holds for states on matrix algebras.
By standard procedure, it extends to direct sums of matrix algebras
M\cong L({\mathbb{C}}^{n})
.
Since both sides of (
C.2
) are invariant under *-isomorphism, the formula holds for all states on finite-dimensional unital *-algebras.

If
M
is approximately finite-dimensional, there is an increasing net
(M_{\alpha})_{\alpha}
of finite-dimensional *-subalgebras
M_{\alpha}\subset M
with

M=\overline{\bigcup_{\alpha}\,M_{\alpha}}^{\mathrm{uw}}.

(C.3)

We define projective nets of states
(\omega_{\alpha})_{\alpha}
,
(\varphi_{\alpha})_{\alpha}
via
\omega_{\alpha}=\omega|_{M_{\alpha}}
,
\varphi_{\alpha}=\varphi|_{M_{\alpha}}
.
By the approximation property of the relative entropy
[
26
, Cor. II.5.12]
, we have

D(\omega\|\varphi)=\lim_{\alpha}D(\omega_{\alpha}\|\varphi_{\alpha}).

(C.4)

Using
\omega_{\alpha}=\omega_{\beta}|_{M_{\alpha}}
,
\varphi_{\alpha}=\varphi_{\beta}|_{M_{\alpha}}
for
\beta>\alpha
and the monotonicity (data processing inequality) of the hockey stick divergence, we see that

\frac{1}{t}E_{\alpha}(\omega_{\alpha}\|\varphi_{\alpha})+\frac{1}{t^{2}}E_{t}(\varphi_{\alpha}\|\omega_{\alpha})\qquad\text{is non-decreasing in $\alpha$.}

(C.5)

The density of
\bigcup_{\alpha}M_{\alpha}
in
M
implies

E_{t}(\omega\|\varphi)=\frac{1}{2}(\lVert\omega-t\phi\rVert-1-t)=\lim_{\alpha}\frac{1}{2}(\lVert\omega_{\alpha}-t\phi_{\alpha}\rVert-1-t)=\lim_{\alpha}E_{t}(\omega_{\alpha}\|\varphi_{\alpha}).

(C.6)

Using the monotone convergence theorem, Frenkel’s integral formula for states on finite-dimensional unital *-algebras, and equations (
C.4
), (
C.5
), and (
C.6
), we find

\displaystyle\int_{1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{t}E_{t}(\omega\|\varphi)+\frac{1}{t^{2}}E_{t}(\varphi\|\omega)\right)dt

\displaystyle=\int_{1}^{\infty}\lim_{\alpha}\left(\frac{1}{t}E_{t}(\omega_{\alpha}\|\varphi_{\alpha})+\frac{1}{t^{2}}E_{t}(\varphi\|\omega)\right)dt

\displaystyle=\lim_{\alpha}\int_{1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{t}E_{t}(\omega_{\alpha}\|\varphi_{\alpha})+\frac{1}{t^{2}}E_{t}(\varphi\|\omega)\right)dt

\displaystyle=\lim_{\alpha}D(\omega_{\alpha}\|\varphi_{\alpha})=D(\omega\|\varphi).

∎
