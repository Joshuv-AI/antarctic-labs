Proton pump inhibitor use and risk of dementia in a population-based cohort study
Authors: Hsing-Hui Liu, Tzu-I Chen, Yong-Chen Chen, Vinchi Wang, Ping-Keung Yip, Fu-Jen Lee, Wan-Lun Hsu, Yi-Chen Lan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Understanding the effect of long-term medication exposure in middle-aged to older adults is crucial for dementia prevention. Proton pump inhibitors (PPIs), widely used for gastrointestinal conditions, have shown inconsistent associations with dementia risk. We conducted a retrospective cohort study using the Taiwan Adult Preventive Health Services and National Health Insurance databases. The study included 2,444,999 adults aged ≥ 40 years without baseline dementia. Incident dementia and cumulative PPI use, calculated as cumulative defined daily doses, were tracked. Multivariate Cox proportional hazards models estimated hazard ratios (HRs), adjusting for age, sex, BMI, lifestyle factors, and comorbidities like hypertension and diabetes. During follow-up, 137,126 participants (5.6%) developed dementia (mean age: 72.7 years; 58.6% females). PPI use was associated with an increased dementia risk in a dose-response manner (HR 1.66; 95% CI, 1.64–1.69 for the high-exposure category). The strongest associations were observed in the younger cohort: individuals aged < 65 years with high-exposure exhibited a significantly higher risk (HR 1.97; 95% CI, 1.89–2.06) compared to older groups. PPI use is associated with an elevated, dose-dependent risk of all-cause dementia in this observational cohort. While this association attenuates with advancing age, potential causal mechanisms remain to be fully elucidated.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-55683-7.

Introduction

Proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) are a widely prescribed medication. By blocking the enzyme H+, K+ ATPase, PPIs reduce stomach acid secretion, effectively relieving symptoms of acid-related disorders1. In Taiwan, PPIs like omeprazole were introduced the late 1980’s, and became popular in the 1990s2. Over the past two decades, there has been a significant increase in global consumption of PPIs, especially among individuals aged 40 years and older3,4. Approximately half of prescriptions lack valid indications for treatment5. PPI intake has raised concerns of serious side effects such as dementia, stroke, osteoarthritis, respiratory infections, and type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), with some exhibiting dose-response relationships6–8.

The interaction between PPIs and dementia has been a particularly controversial topic9,10. PPIs like omeprazole show a blood-to-brain coefficient of distribution of 0.15, which suggests that omeprazole is able to penetrate the blood–brain barrier11. PPIs can selectively inhibit the enzyme responsible for the biosynthesis of acetylcholine, potentially inhibiting neuronal signaling in the brain12. Studies also highlighted concerns about PPI-induced deficiencies in micronutrients that have neuroprotective properties13,14. Further research inspired by the gut-brain axis found that long-term PPI use may alter the composition of gut microbiota15–17.

Dementia notably impacts patients’ quality of life, social interactions, caregiving responsibilities, and professional activities18. Most research on PPIs and dementia has focused on individuals over 75 years old19,20. Pourhadi et al. (2023) found that dementia risk increases with longer PPI exposure, particularly among younger individuals (ages of 60 and 69)21. This association diminishes as the age at diagnosis increases. The influence of medication exposure prior to the onset of dementia on cognitive decline, is an area requiring further exploration.

This nationwide, population-based cohort study investigates the relationship between PPI use and dementia risk in individuals aged 40 and above. We also assess how varying levels of PPI exposure impact dementia risk across different age groups. Given the significant role of middle-aged and older adults in society, understanding the dose-response relationship between PPI use and dementia risk is essential for promoting healthy aging22,23.

Methods

Study setting and data source

We conducted a retrospective cohort study using three databases from Taiwan’s Ministry of Health and Welfare: Adult Preventive Healthcare Services Database (APHSD), National Health Insurance Research Database (NHIRD), and Cause of Death Database. These databases have been extensively utilized and validated in numerous previous studies24,25. Data were collected on residents aged 40 and above who participated in the adult preventive health program between 2012 and 2013. By linking the databases through identifiable information, we determined baseline cumulative PPI consumption three years prior to study entry, which served as the exposure indicator. We then conducted a longitudinal study on individuals without a prior history of dementia to investigate the association between baseline PPI exposure levels and the subsequent incidence of dementia. Dementia diagnoses were confirmed using inpatient and outpatient diagnostic codes from 2015 to 2020 from the NHIRD. We included 2,444,999 eligible participants.

The APHSD in Taiwan offers health exams every three years for individuals aged 40 to 64, and annually for those aged 65 and above. In 2012, 33.3% of seniors utilized this program. This program aids in early disease detection and management, thus improving health outcomes and reducing costs. The NHIRD covers over 99% of Taiwan’s population (approximately 23 million people). It includes comprehensive medical and pharmacy claims, diagnostic codes (ICD-9 before 2016 and ICD-10 after 2016), and medication prescriptions. The linked datasets ensured confidentiality while allowing the ability to follow up patient baseline health conditions and disease outcomes. This study was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board of Fu Jen Catholic University (IRB No. C112180). Due to the retrospective nature of the study, the Institutional Review Board of Fu Jen Catholic University waived the need of obtaining informed consent. All methods were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations.

Ascertainment of outcomes

The primary outcome was incident all-cause dementia, identified via NHIRD records through December 31, 2020. We identified the study cohort from the 2012–2013 APHSD and excluded individuals with a previous dementia diagnosis, age < 40 years, or incomplete records. To minimize protopathic bias, we further excluded participants diagnosed with dementia within a three-year lag period after study entry.

We opted for all-cause dementia rather than specific subtype analysis to maximize cohort representativeness and prevent selection bias. This decision aligns with clinical coding practices in the NHIRD, where non-specific dementia codes are predominantly utilized during the initial diagnostic phase. Dementia was defined using ICD-9-(290.0-290.4, 294.x, 331.0-331.9, 331.0-331.2, and 332.0) and ICD-10-CM codes (F01.x-F03.x, G30.x-G31.x, and G20.x) Diagnoses were confirmed by at least two outpatient visits within two years or one inpatient admission to ensure diagnostic validity. The date of the first valid diagnosis served as the event occurrence point. Participants were followed from study entry until the first dementia diagnosis, death, or December 31, 2020, whichever occurred first.

Exposure assessment

To standardize PPI consumption across different agents, we utilized the Defined Daily Dose (DDD) as established by the WHO according to the Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical (ATC) classification system. Prescription data were collected for three years prior to study entry, focusing on the following PPIs: omeprazole (A02BC01), pantoprazole (A02BC02), lansoprazole (A02BC03), rabeprazole (A02BC04), esomeprazole (A02BC05), and dexlansoprazole (A02BC06). By aggregating all prescriptions within the A02BC class into a standardized cumulative DDD (cDDD), we effectively accounted for medication switching between different agents, as the cDDD represents the total pharmacological burden. This methodology is well-established in pharmacoepidemiological research26,27.

cDDD was obtained from inpatient and outpatient pharmacy databases, included comprehensive information on dosage, prescription dates, and the total number of pills dispensed (Supplemental Table 1). Prescription refill records were utilized as a proxy for medication exposure status. However, over-the-counter (OTC) purchases were not captured in these clinical records. We assumed that cumulative exposure to these drugs contributed to the long-term risk of all-cause dementia. Due to the right-skewed distribution of PPI consumption, participants were categorized into no, low, and high exposure groups based on the median cDDD of users. Furthermore, for age group analyses, we divided participants into those aged 65 years and older and those younger than 65 years.

Potential confounders

We included multiple confounders from the APHSD by distinguishing between self reported behaviors and clinical measurements. Demographic variables consisted of age and sex. Health related behaviors, including smoking and alcohol consumption, were obtained from self reported questionnaires reflecting the six-month period preceding the health exam. Physical activity and depressive tendencies were assessed based on the two weeks prior to the assessment. Clinical variables measured during the health exams included BMI, total cholesterol, triglycerides, blood pressure, and fasting glucose. Cutoff values for BMI and lipids adhered to standards from Taiwan’s Health Promotion Administration. Hypertension was defined as blood pressure greater than 130/80 mmHg. Type 2 diabetes mellitus was defined as fasting glucose ≥ 126 mg/dL (7.0 mmol/L) or non-fasting glucose ≥ 200 mg/dL (11.1 mmol/L) according to WHO guidelines.

To enhance diagnostic sensitivity, we combined blood test data with historical medical diagnoses to identify hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and type 2 diabetes mellitus. Data from the 2020 APHSD indicated that 24% had abnormal cholesterol, 21.8% had high blood pressure, and 9.8% had abnormal blood sugar levels28. This approach allowed for more sensitive detection of time-varying health conditions compared to relying solely on historical medical diagnoses. Detailed definitions for comorbidities, resources and cutoffs are provided (Supplemental Table 2).

Statistical analysis

This study utilized means ± SD or total counts and percentages for descriptive analyses of the baseline characteristics, comorbidities, and PPIs usage. We used cause-specific Cox proportional hazards models to calculate the incidence rates of all-cause dementia using univariate and multivariate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). In the multivariate model, we adjusted for baseline age, sex, smoking, alcohol consumption, exercise, depressive tendency, BMI, hyperlipidemia, hypertension, and T2DM. We examined collinearity among variables, followed by subgroup and sensitivity analyses. Statistical significance was determined with two-tailed tests using a threshold of P < .05. Statistical analyses were performed using SAS statistical software (version 9.4 for Windows; SAS Institute, Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

Subgroup and sensitivity analyses

To assess the robustness of the association between PPI use and dementia, we conducted subgroup analyses by stratifying the study population based on age, sex, health-related behaviors, comorbidities, and healthcare utilization rates. Additionally, we performed sensitivity analyses to ensure the stability of our results. We employed a 5-year lag time approach to reduce potential protopathic bias and conducted a further sensitivity analysis restricted to patients who met NHIRD reimbursement criteria for cholinesterase inhibitors (ChEI). In Taiwan, ChEI use serves as a proxy for common dementia subtype like AD and Parkinson’s disease dementia (PDD), such prescriptions require specialist confirmation via objective cognitive testing (MMSE or CDR scores) and brain imaging, minimizing potential diagnostic misclassification.

Results

We identified 2,801,898 individuals above 40 years old at the start of the 2012–2013 study period (Fig. 1). We excluded 356,899 during quality-control filtering; 164,546 had incomplete data, 103,246 had a pre-existing dementia diagnosis, and 89,107 received a dementia diagnosis within the 3 years lag time. In the PPI-exposed cohort, 370,973 participants (15.2%) received PPI prescriptions during the three-year exposure window (Supplemental Tables 3 and Table 4). The cDDD showed a mean [SD] of 101.3 [134.2] and a median of 65. Notably, older populations exhibited both a higher prevalence of PPI exposure and higher cumulative usage.

We observed a clear dose-response relationship with higher PPI exposure (cDDD > 65) was associated with higher incidence of dementia (1.66 [95% CI, 1.64–1.69]; P < .001) (Table 2). Although absolute incidence was higher in the elderly, the relative risk contrast was most pronounced in participants under 65 years; in this group, the magnitude of association for high exposure reached a hazard ratio of 1.97 (95% CI, 1.89–2.06).

In subgroup analyses, we conducted multivariable stratified analyses (Figs. 2 and 3). The adjusted hazard ratios (aHRs) for PPI use among all subgroups were above 1.0 with P-values < 0.001, indicating a higher risk of dementia with PPI exposure regardless of subgroup. Notably, the association between PPI exposure and dementia risk was less pronounced with increasing age. Furthermore, a subgroup analysis was specifically conducted for patients prescribed cholinesterase inhibitors (Supplementary Table 5). The observed association was further validated through several sensitivity analyses, including the application of extended lag periods for the overall cohort (Fig. 4) and for individuals receiving cholinesterase inhibitors (Supplementary Table 6).

Discussion

In this population-based cohort study of individuals above 40 years, PPI use was associated with an increased rate of all-cause dementia, even after adjustment. We observed a dose-response relationship, with the highest aHRs in the 40–59 age group at entry, decreasing in older groups. These findings remained consistent, even under strict definition of patients who received cholinesterase inhibitors under the NHI.

Dementia significantly impacts individuals, families, and the economy, with global costs exceeding 1 trillion USD annually29. By 2050, dementia cases are projected to reach 152 million30. In this study, the incidence rates of dementia were 7.4 per 1,000 person-years overall, 1.8 for those under 65, and 17.8 for those over 65, similar to another study from Taiwan31. Compared to larger cohorts, Pourhadi et al. (2024) reported an incidence rate of 4.8 per 1,000 person-years in a Danish cohort, while Zhang et al. (2022) found rates of 1.06 per 1000 person-years among PPI users and 0.51 per 1,000 person-years among non-users in the UK, both of which were lower than the incidence rate in Taiwan21,32. In a systematic analysis for the Global Burden of Dementia, Nichols et al. (2019) reported a moderate decline in age-standardized dementia-associated mortality rates in Taiwan but a significant increase in prevalence compared to other nations33. While variations in healthcare feasibility and demographic compositions across regions may influence diagnostic rates18, the overarching trend suggests that Taiwan has an expanding population of individuals with dementia who are experiencing extended survival34.

The trend underscores the need to identify risk factors and implement preventive measures to reduce the incidence of dementia. Livingston et al. (2024) notes that addressing modifiable risk factors can reduce the incidence of dementia by 45%. Key modifiable risk factors include excessive alcohol consumption, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, smoking, obesity, depression, physical inactivity, and diabetes, which have been addressed in this analysis18. In this study, dementia patients tended to exhibit depressive tendency, hypertension, elevated fasting glucose and T2DM.

Recent observational studies indicated an increased rate of dementia associated with the use of PPIs35–38. The observed weakening of the dose-response relationship with advancing age aligns with previous age-stratified studies19,23,39. Systematic reviews have concluded no association or no publication bias10,20,40. When examining these studies, we observed high heterogeneity in methodological design, especially regarding the definition of PPI exposure. Many focused on patients over 75 and lacked age-stratified analyses. Focusing on individuals above 75 may exclude the most biologically relevant exposure window, since age already plays a dominant role in dementia progression, and it may be hard to avoid competing causes of death.

Aligning with our findings, Pourhadi et al. (2024) reported a greater relative risk of dementia among younger age strata exposed to PPIs21. Friesen et al. (2022) dismissed the pronounced association in their youngest cohort as ‘noise,’ suggesting that high PPI use in younger patients acts as a proxy for poorer baseline health rather than a true biological signal of PPI41. Several biological mechanisms might support this age-dependent impact. For instance, PPI-induced vitamin B12 deficiency may exert a more significant impact on younger users due to longer cumulative exposure and the absence of comorbidity-driven masking42. Recent evidence also suggests that younger individuals may be more susceptible to PPI-induced disruptions in brain microstructure, as characterized by higher mean diffusivity and poorer cognitive performance in long-term users43. Conversely, the weaker association observed in older adults may be influenced by surveillance bias; for example, dementia evaluations in nursing homes are often deemed superfluous due to patient frailty, leading to underdiagnosis44. Furthermore, such associations in the elderly typically diminish or disappear after rigorous adjustment for confounders20,45.

The progression of dementia can take several years, with prodrome symptoms often becoming noticeable around 5–10 years after disease onset46–48. Therefore, it is crucial to establish appropriate lag windows between exposure and outcome to minimize protopathic bias48,49. Ahn et al. (2023) reviewed nine studies and found no clear evidence linking PPI use to dementia. Notably, there were inconsistent lag time in the studies. Considerable heterogeneity in lag time can affect the validity of study results. Also, when no lag time is included, the PPI exposure window could fall well within the dementia prodromal period and is not a biologically relevant period.

Whether PPI exposure initiates dementia or accelerates its progression in at-risk individuals remains unclear. For instance, APOE ε4, a major genetic risk factor for young-onset dementia, may synergistically amplify this risk42. Zhang et al. (2022) reported that the association between PPI use and dementia increased significantly among ε4 heterozygotes32. Further research is needed to determine whether these stronger associations are driven by genetic factors or other pathophysiological pathways, and to clarify discrepancies in PPI-related dementia subtypes among younger populations.

It’s also plausible that dementia manifesting at different ages could be considered distinct diseases with varying characteristics and risk factors50,51. Individuals with unhealthy lifestyles including current smoking, alcohol consumption, physical inactivity, and obesity had a higher risk of early-onset dementia52,53. It was interesting that in this study, the dementia group had a lower prevalence of alcohol consumption and smoking. We cannot exclude the possibility of statistical bias due to competing causes of death.

This study has several strengths. The sample size represents one of the largest cohorts among similar studies and is among the few dementia-related studies utilizing national health examination databases. Its external validity can be applied to other related adult health examination research. We accounted for several self-reported lifestyle-related health factors, allowing for a comprehensive analysis of the relationship between PPI use and dementia.

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, unrecorded OTC PPI use and the inability to fully verify medication adherence from pharmacy claims may lead to non-differential misclassification, likely biasing our results toward the null. Second, unmeasured confounding remains possible due to the lack of APOE ε4 and family history data. Third, we did not categorize dementia subtypes or implement a new-user design, alternative approaches that could yield different etiological perspectives. Finally, we did not model the competing risk of mortality, restricting our present analyses strictly to cause-specific hazard modeling. Future studies incorporating competing risk methods may help refine these findings.

Conclusions

This observational study suggests a dose-response association between PPI use and a higher risk of incident dementia, which was notably observed among middle-aged adults. The association attenuates as age increases, suggesting that middle age may be a crucial window for dementia-related exposures, our findings cannot establish causality. These results highlight the need for clinical caution regarding unnecessary PPI prescriptions, especially in middle-aged populations. Future studies are warranted to clarify underlying mechanisms and explore age-specific dementia subtypes associated with PPI use.
