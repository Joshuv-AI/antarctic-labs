Ischemic injury and liver graft congestion increase post-transplant interleukin-6 and tumor necrosis factor-alpha in hepatocellular carcinoma
Authors: Young Ju Oh, Gyu-Seong Choi, Jongman Kim, Jinsoo Rhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLiver transplantation (LT) is an essential treatment for patients with end-stage liver disease (ESLD), including hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). Post-transplant complications such as venous congestion and tumor recurrence remain challenging, and recent studies suggest that ischemia-reperfusion injury (IRI) and the resulting inflammatory response play a crucial role in these complications. This study was undertaken to investigate the relationship between early post-operative cytokine profiles and the development of venous congestion and tumor recurrence in LT recipients with HCC. Forty-two patients with HCC who underwent living donor liver transplantation (LDLT) were prospectively studied. Data on liver chemistry, venous outflow reconstruction, plasma cytokine levels (IL-6, TNF-α, IL-10, MMP-1, and HGF), and tumor characteristics were collected. Venous congestion volume was assessed using CT scans, and plasma cytokines were measured multiple times post-LDLT. Elevated IL-6 and TNF-α levels were associated with early liver graft regeneration. Patients with ≥ 15% hepatic congestion tended to show elevated levels of these cytokines. A weak association was observed between ischemic events, congestion, and TNF-α levels. HCC recurrence was associated with higher IL-6 levels. IL-6 and TNF-α play a critical role in early graft regeneration. Venous congestion may be associated with altered cytokine levels post-transplantation. Introduction

Liver transplantation (LT) is essential for patients with end-stage liver disease, including hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), the most common primary liver malignancy1. However, post-transplant complications such as venous congestion and tumor recurrence remain significant challenges2–4. Recent studies suggest that ischemia-reperfusion injury (IRI) and the resulting inflammatory response play crucial roles in these complications2, and it has been suggested that IRI triggers the release of proinflammatory cytokines that promote tumor growth and metastasis2,4. Furthermore, evidence indicates that venous congestion of a liver graft exacerbates IRI and contributes to HCC recurrence1,5.

Patients with ESLD often exhibit a dysregulated cytokine profile characterized by a pro- to anti-inflammatory cytokine imbalance. This type of imbalance can persist after LT and might contribute to various post-transplant complications6. Studies have shown that alterations in cytokine levels are associated with both venous congestion and tumor recurrence in LT recipients6–8. For example, elevated levels of IL-6 and TNF-α have been linked to increased risk of HCC recurrence after LT6,7,9,10. Thus, understanding the complex interplay of cytokines in the post-transplant setting appears to be crucial for optimizing patient outcomes and preventing complications.

This study aimed to investigate the relationship between early post-operative cytokine profiles and the development of venous congestion and tumor recurrence in LT recipients with HCC. Specifically, we hypothesized that specific cytokine patterns may predict these complications and guide post-transplant management. By documenting the effects of cytokines on post-transplant outcomes, this study contributes to improving the long-term survival of LT recipients with HCC.

Materials and methods

Study population

Forty-two patients with HCC who underwent LT at Samsung Medical Center (SMC), Seoul, Korea, from November 2020 to December 2022 were enrolled in this prospective study before LDLT. Exclusion criteria were: (i) non-HCC liver disease and (ii) death or graft failure within one month of transplantation.

Data collection

Recipient data, including demographics, etiology of liver disease, Model for End-Stage Liver Disease (MELD) scores, types and numbers of pre-LT locoregional therapies, and alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) levels at the time of LT were collected. Surgical data regarding venous outflow reconstruction were obtained, and pathological data, such as microvascular invasion, portal invasion, tumor size, and number, were documented. Post-LT follow-up data included the details of complications that occurred within 30 days. Clinical data and laboratory data, including liver chemistry values recorded up to post-operative day (POD) 14, were extracted from medical records. All patients received standard immunosuppressive therapy consisting of tacrolimus-based regimens with perioperative steroid administration. Measurement of plasma

As illustrated in Fig. 1A, plasma cytokines, including IL-6, IL-10, matrix metalloproteinases (MMP)-1, tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), and hepatocyte growth factor (HGF), were measured pre-LT and on POD 1, 3, 7, and 14. These time points were selected to reflect early post-reperfusion inflammatory responses. Plasma samples were obtained by centrifuging blood at 4000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C and stored at -80 °C until required for analysis. Cytokine concentrations were measured using a Human Premixed Multi-Analyte Kit (Koma Biotech, Seoul, Korea), according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and are expressed in pg/mL. All measurements were performed by investigators blinded to clinical outcomes and statistical analyses.

Venous congestion volume measurement

We assessed venous outflow congestion in liver grafts two weeks post-LT. CT scans were reviewed in three phases: arterial, portal, and delayed. Hounsfield units (HU) were measured to identify enhancement differences across liver segments. Liver congestion volume was quantified using post-LT CT scans on POD 14 using Mimics software (Materialise, Leuven, Belgium) (Fig. 1B).

Statistical analysis

Liver chemistry profiles and cytokine levels before and after LT were compared between patient groups. Data of patients with a congestion area ≥ 15% or an ischemic event were compared with those of patients who did not. Patients were stratified into four groups by congestion area (≥ 15%) and an ischemic event. The ≥ 15% threshold for defining significant congestion was selected based on prior volumetric assessments and its clinical relevance in distinguishing meaningful graft congestion, and was used as a pragmatic cutoff rather than a strict biological threshold. The four groups were defined as follows: (1) patients with a normal clinical course (the normal group) (n = 29, 69.0%), (2) patients with ≥ 15% hepatic congestion (the hepatic congestion group) (n = 5, 11.9%), (3) patients who experienced an ischemic event (the ischemic event group) (n = 4, 9.5%), and (4) patients with ≥ 15% hepatic congestion and an ischemic event (the hepatic congestion plus ischemic event group) (n = 4, 9.5%). In addition, patients who experienced or did not experience recurrence were also compared.

Continuous variables are presented as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs), and categorical variables are expressed as counts and percentages. Due to the non-normal distribution and variability of cytokine levels, non-parametric tests were used. The Mann-Whitney U test was used to analyze continuous variables, and Fisher’s exact test was used to compare categorical variables. The analysis was performed using SPSS software version 22 (IBM Corporation; Armonk, NY, USA), and statistical significance was accepted for P values < 0.05. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Samsung Medical Center (IRB No. 2020-06-165), and all participants provided informed consent.

Intraoperative and post-operative data

Table 2 summarizes intraoperative and post-operative data. One-third of the patients did not undergo middle hepatic vein (MHV) reconstruction, and 3 patients (7.1%) underwent inferior hepatic vein (IHV) reconstruction. Occlusion was observed in 41.4% of the 29 patients with reconstructed veins. Twenty-three patients (54.8%) demonstrated partial congestion, while 2 (4.8%) exhibited partial ischemia. Nine patients (21.4%) had at least 15% congestion. Eight patients (19%) experienced a major ischemic event postoperatively. These events included hepatic artery complications (n = 2), portal vein complications (n = 3), and reoperation due to bleeding (n = 3). Twenty-nine patients (69.0%) had normal parenchyma and no ischemic event based on recategorized radiological findings and post-operative events. Only 5 patients (11.9%) had congestion ≥ 15%, 4 patients (9.5%) experienced an ischemic event without radiological congestion, 4 experienced an ischemic event and radiological congestion ≥ 15%, and 4 experienced HCC recurrence during the 1-year follow-up period.

Post-operative laboratory values

An abrupt decline in laboratory values was observed until POD 2, and this was followed by a more gradual decrease. AST peaked on the day of LT and then gradually decreased. Conversely, ALT peaked on POD 1 and gradually declined (Fig. 1C). Similarly, IL-6, IL-10, and HGF plasma levels peaked on POD 1 and gradually declined. MMP-1 levels also peaked on POD 1, but the subsequent decrease was more gradual over follow-up (p > 0.05). On the other hand, TNF-α levels decreased until POD 3 and then increased on POD 7 (Fig. 1D).

Comparison of laboratory values in the four stratified groups

Post-LT liver chemistry results for the TB, AST, and ALT levels of patients with ≥ 15% congestion with or without an ischemic event were not significantly different during follow-up. However, AST levels tended to be higher on POD 1 in patients with ≥ 15% congestion or an ischemic event compared to those without ischemic events, although this difference did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 2A).

Impact of ischemic events and congestion on post-operative liver chemistry and cytokine levels. (A) Changes in mean levels of total bilirubin, AST, and ALT from pre-LT to POD 7 (B) Changes in mean plasma levels of IL-6, IL-10, MMP-1, TNF-α, and HGF from pre-LT to POD 14.

Regarding cytokine levels (Fig. 2B), IL-6 levels were higher on POD 3 and 7 in patients with an ischemic event or congestion ≥ 15% than in the normal group, and IL-6 summation values were significantly different. TNF-α levels were elevated in patients with an ischemic event or congestion ≥ 15% on POD 3 and 7, and TNF-α maximum and summation values were also higher in these patients than in the normal group. IL-10 and HGF levels were not significantly different in patients with an ischemic event or congestion ≥ 15% or patients without an ischemic event with ≤ 15% congestion. On the other hand, although the summation value of MMP-1 was significantly higher for patients with an ischemic event or congestion ≥ 15% than for patients without an ischemic event with ≤ 15% congestion, pre-LT values were higher, while post-LT values were similar.

Comparisons of liver chemistry profiles and cytokine levels

The liver chemistry profiles of patients following LT were investigated by comparing values in the four stratified groups. The data indicated that from POD 1 to 3, AST and ALT values were elevated to the greatest extent in the hepatic congestion plus ischemic event group (Fig. 3A).

Impact of congestion and ischemic events on postoperative liver function and inflammatory response. (A) Changes in mean levels of total bilirubin, AST, and ALT from pre-LT to POD 7 (B) Changes in mean plasma levels of IL-6, IL-10, MMP-1, TNF-α, and HGF from pre-LT to POD 14.

Upon analyzing cytokine levels in four patient groups after LT, no significant differences were observed between IL-6, IL-10, MMP-1, or HGF plasma levels. Nevertheless, TNF-α levels were notably higher in patients in the hepatic congestion and the hepatic congestion plus ischemic event groups than in the normal group (Fig. 3B). These results suggest that hepatic congestion may be associated with increased TNF-α levels following transplantation.

Tumor recurrence

Four of the forty-two patients experienced tumor recurrence, and all were beyond Milan criteria. The levels of AFP (3.5 ng/mL, IQR 2.5–10.7 vs. 36.9 ng/mL, IQR 20.0-77.9, P = 0.011) and PIVKA-II (26.5 mAU/L, IQR 21.0-54.3 vs. 5449 mAU/L, IQR 351-14777, P < 0.001) were significantly higher in those that experienced recurrence (Table 3). Median congested volume was non-significantly higher in the recurrence group (12.5 cm3, IQR 0-130.8 vs. 89.5 cm3, IQR 19.8-127.8, P = 0.685). Post-operative complication rates were similar in those who experienced or did not experience recurrence (47.4% vs. 50%, P = N/S).

Explanted liver pathology findings revealed significant differences between these two patient groups. The median number of tumors was significantly higher in the recurrence group than in the no-recurrence group (a median 8 tumors (IQR 5–19) versus a median of 1 tumor (IQR 1–3); p < 0.001). Similarly, median tumor size was significantly larger in the recurrence group (8.4 cm (IQR 3.7–12.9) versus 1.6 cm (IQR 1.1–2.9); p = 0.004). All 4 patients in the recurrence group exhibited microvascular invasion as compared to 36.8% (14 of 38) in the no-recurrence group, and this difference was significant (p = 0.027). No significant intergroup difference was observed for portal invasion, intrahepatic metastasis, or cirrhosis.

Liver chemistry markers of patients who experienced tumor recurrence post-LT and those who did not were also compared (Fig. 4A). No significant intergroup difference in the daily levels of TB, AST, or ALT was observed over the initial 7 post-operative days, suggesting that tumor recurrence did not meaningfully affect these specific liver function parameters within the early post-transplant period.

Impact of tumor recurrence on postoperative liver chemistry and cytokine levels. (A) Changes in mean levels of total bilirubin, AST, and ALT from pre-LT to POD 7 (B) Changes in mean plasma levels of IL-6, IL-10, MMP-1, TNF-α, and HGF from pre-LT to POD 14.

However, a comparison of plasma cytokine levels in patients with and without tumor recurrence post-LT revealed significant differences. IL-6 levels were notably higher in the recurrence group on POD 1, while TNF-α levels were significantly elevated on POD 3 and 7. Maximum and summation values of IL-6 and TNF-α were significantly higher in the recurrence group, suggesting an enhanced inflammatory response associated with tumor recurrence. Conversely, IL-10 levels were similar in the two groups, and MMP-1 and HGF levels were not well correlated with tumor recurrence over the 1-year post-operative period (Fig. 4B).

Tumor recurrence among beyond Milan patients (N = 12)

Pre-transplant predictors and post-transplant outcomes of patients with and without tumor recurrence beyond Milan criteria showed significant differences (Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2). The recurrence group had notably higher pre-transplant levels of PIVKA-II (5449 mAU/L, IQR 351-14777 vs. 67.5 mAU/L, IQR 22–254, P = 0.028), a greater median number of tumors (8.4, IQR 3.7–12.9 vs. 2.0, IQR 1.1–4.8, P = 0.109), and a higher median congested volume (89.5 cm3, IQR 19.8-127.8 vs. 0 cm3, IQR 0-40.5), as determined by 2-week post-transplant CT (Supplementary table).

Supplementary Fig. 1 compares the liver chemistry parameters of LT recipients with or without tumor recurrence beyond the Milan criteria. No significant difference was observed in daily TB and AST levels between these two groups during the week following transplantation, suggesting that tumor recurrence did not have a discernible impact on these liver function indicators during the immediate post-operative period. A comparison of the plasma cytokine levels post-LT of patients beyond the Milan criteria with or without tumor recurrence showed that IL-6 levels were significantly higher in the recurrence group on POD 1 (Supplementary Fig. 2). Additionally, maximum and summation values of IL-6 levels revealed a significant increase in the recurrence group. However, the levels of other cytokines, including IL-10 and TNF-α, in these two groups were not significantly different during the 14-day post-operative period. MMP-1 and HGF levels varied over this period but were not associated with tumor recurrence.

Discussion

This study investigated relationships between venous outflow congestion, IRI, and early post-operative cytokine profiles in LDLT recipients with HCC. Our primary objective was to identify potential predictors of tumor recurrence and contribute to the optimization of post-transplant outcomes by elucidating the dynamic interplay of cytokines during early graft regeneration. Understanding the complex interplay between these factors is crucial for improving the long-term outcomes of LDLT recipients with HCC, as venous congestion and IRI can significantly impact graft function and survival.

Our findings revealed a potential association between IL-6 and TNF-α levels and liver graft venous congestion. This finding can be interpreted in terms of its relevance to liver regeneration. Notably, elevated levels of these cytokines were observed in patients with parenchymal congestion, even in the absence of significant changes in liver function tests. This observation aligns with previous research regarding the crucial roles of IL-6 and TNF-α during liver regeneration6,11 and further emphasizes their importance for promoting hepatocyte proliferation and survival after transplantation. IL-6 and TNF-α are key mediators of inflammatory response and play pivotal roles in orchestrating the complex cascade of events that lead to liver regeneration. IL-6 is primarily produced by Kupffer cells and hepatocytes, stimulates hepatocyte proliferation, and protects against apoptosis. TNF-α is also produced by Kupffer cells, initiates inflammatory response, and triggers the release of other cytokines involved in liver regeneration11. Moreover, the delicate balance between proinflammatory and anti-inflammatory cytokines is essential for successful liver regeneration and graft function.

However, our findings contrast with those of Murtha et al., who observed a direct correlation between cytokine elevations and liver enzyme abnormalities12. This discrepancy might be attributed to differences in study design, patient demographics, and the timings of cytokine and liver function tests12. It is plausible that the complex interplay between cytokines and liver enzymes is influenced by various factors, including IRI severity, the extent of congestion, and individual patient characteristics13. Further research is needed to elucidate the precise mechanisms underlying these discrepancies and to determine the optimal timing for cytokine and liver function testing in the post-transplant setting. Additionally, elucidation of the specific roles of different cytokine subtypes and their interactions with other inflammatory mediators may provide a more comprehensive understanding of the complexities of inflammatory response following LDLT.

Interestingly, we found that congestion ≥ 15% was significantly associated with increased plasma levels of inflammatory cytokines, particularly TNF-α. The ≥ 15% threshold was selected as a pragmatic definition of clinically meaningful congestion based on volumetric assessment and should not be interpreted as a strict biological cutoff. These findings suggest that venous congestion may be associated with enhanced inflammatory responses after LDLT, potentially through exacerbation of ischemia-reperfusion injury. However, a causal relationship cannot be established based on the current data. Venous congestion may also be influenced by surgical factors, including middle hepatic vein (MHV) reconstruction and venous outflow patency, which were not fully accounted for in this study. IL-6 and TNF-α may reflect both physiological liver regeneration and pathological inflammatory responses, as well as nonspecific surgical stress. Therefore, it is difficult to distinguish these processes based solely on cytokine levels in the present study. To assess congestion accurately, we utilized 3-D software to measure congested volumes. This method offers advantages over conventional 2-D assessments, which may underestimate the true extent of congestion.

Animal studies have demonstrated the crucial roles of IL-6 and TNF-α during liver regeneration. Hepatocytes from IL-6 deficient mice exhibited impaired proliferation and increased mortality after partial hepatectomy14,15. Furthermore, liver regeneration defects in TNFR1 knockout mice were rescued by IL-6 injection15,16, suggesting that the primary role of TNF-α in liver regeneration involves upregulating IL-6. Although ischemic events also contribute to the elevation of cytokine levels, their effect appeared less pronounced than that of congestion. Nevertheless, the present findings should be interpreted cautiously, as cytokine elevation may also reflect baseline tumor biology, pre-existing inflammatory status, or perioperative factors rather than congestion or ischemia-reperfusion injury alone. This observation underscores the importance of meticulous surgical techniques to minimize congestion and optimize venous outflow, as emphasized in previous studies5. However, the current study does not establish that reducing congestion directly decreases cytokine activation or tumor recurrence risk. By minimizing congestion, surgeons may potentially mitigate inflammatory response, promote early graft regeneration, and ultimately improve long-term outcomes. Furthermore, the development of novel surgical techniques and strategies to reduce congestion and IRI is required to improve graft survival and patient outcomes.

Regarding tumor recurrence, our study was limited by the short follow-up period and the small number of recurrences. The median follow-up duration of approximately 14 months may be insufficient to fully capture long-term recurrence patterns after LT. Although patients with recurrence exhibited higher IL-6 levels, this finding is exploratory in nature and should be interpreted with caution due to the limited number of events and insufficient statistical power. The observed differences in cytokine levels between groups do not provide sufficient evidence to support a definitive association with tumor recurrence. Importantly, elevated cytokine levels observed in the recurrence group may also reflect underlying tumor biology rather than solely post-transplant congestion or ischemia-reperfusion injury. Patients with recurrence demonstrated higher preoperative TNF-α levels, and IL-6 elevation was observed from POD 1, whereas differences in hepatic congestion became more apparent later in the postoperative course. Therefore, the observed cytokine differences may partially reflect pre-existing inflammatory conditions associated with tumor burden or aggressive tumor characteristics. Larger, prospective studies with longer follow-up durations are needed to validate our findings and further elucidate the complex interplay between cytokines, IRI, and tumor recurrence after LDLT. It is conceivable that specific cytokine patterns might serve as early predictors of tumor recurrence, potentially guiding post-transplant surveillance and treatment strategies. Additionally, investigations of the roles of cytokines in the tumor microenvironment and their interactions with tumor cells might provide valuable insights into the mechanisms responsible for tumor recurrence and identify potential therapeutic targets. Conclusions

Despite these limitations, our study provides valuable insights into dynamic cytokine response during the early post-transplant period and highlights the potential impact of congestion and IRI on graft regeneration and long-term outcomes. Monitoring IL-6 and TNF-α levels may help identify patients at risk of complications and guide personalized post-transplant management strategies. Future research should focus on elucidating the downstream effects of cytokine alterations on the inflammatory cascade and explore the potential of cytokine-targeted therapies to improve long-term outcomes after LDLT. Moreover, further investigations of the mechanisms whereby congestion and IRI influence cytokine production and subsequent downstream signaling pathways may reveal novel therapeutic targets for preventing complications and improving graft survival.

Ethical statement

The authors are accountable for all aspects of the work in ensuring that questions related to the accuracy or integrity of any part of the work are appropriately investigated and resolved. This study was performed according to the Declaration of Helsinki (as revised in 2013) and the Declaration of Istanbul. Informed consent was obtained from all patients for the use of their data for research purposes. All living donor liver transplantations included in this study were performed at Samsung Medical Center. All grafts were obtained from living donors who voluntarily donated in accordance with institutional guidelines and national regulations. No organs or tissues were procured from prisoners or other vulnerable populations.

Institutional review board

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Samsung Medical Center (IRB No. 2020-06-165), and all participants provided informed consent.
