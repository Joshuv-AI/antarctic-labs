EphB4/EphrinB2 signaling regulates pericyte-like function of DPSCs in angiogenesis via transcriptional factor SRF
Authors: Shulan Lin, Fan Yang, Zhaoming Wu, Jialin Zhong, Yuchen Zhang, Junqing Liu, Jun Kang, Chengfei Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDuring angiogenesis, pericytes (PCs) drive vascular stabilization and maturation, a process largely regulated by the transcription factor serum response factor (SRF). Dental pulp stem cells (DPSCs) possess the potential to acquire PC-like properties and regulate angiogenesis. While EphB4/EphrinB2 signaling is reported to be involved in DPSC-mediated angiogenesis, its role in modulating PC-like function remains unexplored. Therefore, this study aims to investigate how EphB4/EphrinB2 signaling regulates the PC-like properties of DPSCs.MethodsDPSCs were transfected with lentivirus to knock down or overexpress EphB4. Cell proliferation was assessed by cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) assay and Ki-67 staining, and cell motility was evaluated using the Boyden chamber assay. To examine the PC-like function of DPSCs, the expression of contractile markers, including alpha-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA), calponin1, and smooth muscle 22-alpha (SM22-α), was analyzed by reverse transcription-quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR), Western blot, and immunofluorescence staining, along with collagen contraction assay. Transcriptome profiling was performed to explore the potential downstream factor. The association between EphB4/EphrinB2 signaling and SRF was determined by Western blot and further verified using small interfering RNA (siRNA), followed by collagen contraction assay. Functional PC behavior of DPSCs was evaluated in a three-dimensional (3D) spheroid sprouting model with human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs).ResultsEphB4 knockdown in DPSCs suppressed the expression of contractile markers and impaired contractility, whereas its overexpression enhanced both. RNA sequencing results revealed an enrichment of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) involved in smooth muscle contraction and Ephrin pathway after EPHB4 silencing. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) showed significant enrichment of smooth muscle contraction, angiogenesis, and neovascularisation-related genes among downregulated genes in EphB4-deficient DPSCs. Moreover, transcription factor binding site (TFBS) of SRF was significantly downregulated. SRF expression closely correlated with EphB4 levels, and SRF silencing abolished EphB4-induced functional enhancement of DPSCs, suggesting SRF as a downstream effector of EphB4/EphrinB2 signaling. 3D spheroid sprouting assay confirmed that EphB4-deficient and SRF-silenced DPSCs failed to restrain HUVEC sprouting due to the impaired PC-like function of DPSCs.ConclusionsThis study elucidates a novel EphB4/EphrinB2/SRF signaling axis that is pivotal in regulating DPSCs into functional PC-like cells during angiogenesis.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13287-026-05107-0.

Background

Angiogenesis, the process of forming new blood vessels from existing ones, is an intricate process involving coordinated interactions among different cells, exchange of various molecules, and crosstalk of multiple signaling pathways. It plays a key role in tissue graft survival and the success of tissue regeneration [1]. The process comprises several key steps: angiogenic activation by growth factors, basement membrane degradation and PC detachment, formation of sprouts by endothelial cell (EC) proliferation and migration, development of new microvascular network, and vessel maturation through new basement membrane formation and PC reattachment [2, 3]. It is noted that multiple signaling pathways, including EphB4/EphrinB2, vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF)/vascular endothelial growth factor receptor (VEGFR), transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β)/Smad, platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF)/platelet-derived growth factor receptor (PDGFR), and angiopoietin 1 (Ang 1)/Tie2, have been identified as critical regulators orchestrating the sophisticated process of angiogenesis to generate a robust, mature, and functional vessel network [4–6].

PCs are perivascular cells that wrap around the ECs in microvessels [7]. By providing structural support and regulating endothelial behavior, PCs play an essential role in maintaining the homeostasis of vasculatures [8]. Functionally, PCs modulate vessel permeability, regulate blood flow, and actively participate in both physiological angiogenesis and pathological vascular remodeling and repair [9]. A hallmark of functional PCs is the robust expression of contractile markers, including α-SMA, calponin 1, and SM22-α. Accordingly, perturbations in PC function, represented by downregulation of contractile markers or diminished PC coverage of the endothelium, are sufficient to compromise vessel morphology and barrier integrity, precipitating vascular dysfunction [10, 11]. At the transcriptional level, SRF has emerged as a key regulator of PC contraction and differentiation [12]. SRF binds to the DNA motif known as the CArG box, which is enriched in the promoter and enhancer regions of contraction-related genes, thereby driving their expression [13–16]. Notably, conditional knockout of the Srf gene in mural cells results in PC deficiency in mouse vessels and leads to vascular dilation and leakage [12, 17], highlighting the indispensable contribution of SRF to PC differentiation.

DPSCs are a population of cells isolated from the human permanent tooth pulp. Sharing key characteristics with mesenchymal stem cells, DPSCs are considered to be a promising cell source for regenerative medicine due to their accessibility and multipotency [18]. They can be easily obtained from extracted human third molars, which are typically discarded as medical waste. DPSCs have been shown to differentiate into adipocytes, neural cells [19], osteoblasts, endotheliocytes [20], and chondrocytes [21]. Recent studies have highlighted their potential in promoting postnatal angiogenesis, either by secreting proangiogenic factors such as VEGF, PDGF, and fibroblast growth factor (FGF) to facilitate vascular network formation [22, 23], or by acting as PC-like cells to stabilize the newly formed vessels [24]. Our previous studies, along with those of others, demonstrated that DPSCs interact with ECs by initiating angiogenesis via VEGF secretion, thereby promoting EC proliferation, migration, and vessel formation. Subsequently, reciprocal signaling between ECs and DPSCs drives the latter toward a PC-like phenotype, as indicated by the upregulation of PC markers, contributing to stabilization of the newly formed vessels and vascular integrity in vitro [25–27].

As aforementioned, the EphB4/EphrinB2 signaling pathway is essential for angiogenesis, vascular morphogenesis, and arteriovenous differentiation [28]. The receptor tyrosine kinase EphB4 and its membrane-bound ligand EphrinB2 mediate cell-cell interactions that regulate various aspects of cell behavior. Inactivation of either Ephb4 or Efnb2 in mice results in early embryonic lethality due to arrested vascular development at the primitive capillary plexus stage, highlighting the essential role of the EphB4/EphrinB2 pathway in vessel maturation [29, 30]. Likewise, the blockade of EphrinB2 has been shown to significantly reduce vessel density and impair PC recruitment in a Kaposi sarcoma model [31]. In line with these findings, our previous work demonstrated that the EphB4/EphrinB2 pathway plays a pivotal role in governing the capacity of DPSCs to promote sprouting angiogenesis [4]. Nevertheless, although the involvement of EphB4/EphrinB2 signaling in angiogenesis has been extensively characterized, its contribution to the PC-like function of DPSCs remains largely unexplored, and the underlying molecular mechanisms have not been adequately illustrated [32–35].

To address this research gap, the present study aims to investigate the role of EphB4/EphrinB2 signaling in modulating the PC-like properties of DPSCs. Given the central role of SRF in orchestrating PC differentiation and function, we further seek to explore whether SRF operates as a downstream effector of EphB4/EphrinB2 signaling.

Methods

Cell culture

DPSCs and HUVECs, purchased from Lonza (Basel, Switzerland) and ScienCell (ScienCell Research Laboratories, CA, USA) respectively, were grown in a 37 °C 5% CO2 incubator. DPSCs were grown in alpha-modified Eagle’s medium (α-MEM, Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Sigma-Aldrich, MO, USA), and cells between passages 7 and 9 were used for experiments. HUVECs were cultured in endothelial growth medium-2 bulletkit (Lonza, MD, USA) and used at cell passage 5 to 7.

EphB4 modulation

Lentiviral particles carrying small hairpin RNA (shRNA) and open reading frame (ORF) clones targeting EphB4 and corresponding controls were purchased from Santa Cruz Biotechnology (CA, USA) and GeneCopoeia (MD, USA). DPSCs were transfected with viral particles according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

CCK-8 assay

DPSCs were seeded in 96-well plates and grown for 1, 3, 5, and 7 days. At each time point, 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent (Dojindo Molecular Technologies, MD, USA) was added to each well containing 100 µL of medium and incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm by SpectraMax® M2 microplate reader (Molecular Devices, CA, USA).

Ki-67 staining

The Ki-67 staining assay was performed as instructed (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Briefly, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) and blocked at room temperature before staining with antibodies. DAPI (Beyotime) was used as the nuclear counterstain. Images were acquired from at least 5 randomly selected fields per sample using a fluorescence microscope (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany).

RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted using RNAeasy™ animal RNA isolation kit from Beyotime (Shanghai, China). Reverse transcription cDNA was synthesized using a reverse transcription kit (Takara Biotechnology, CA, USA). Real-time PCR was performed using a SYBR Premix Ex Taq Kit (Takara Biotechnology) and an ABI PRISM7500 real-time PCR system (Life Technologies, CA, USA). The sequences of primers were provided in the supplements (Supplementary Table 1).

Western blot

Cell cultures were lysed with radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and protein was quantified using the bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Equal amounts of protein were loaded and resolved on sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) gels before transferring onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (Sigma-Aldrich), which were then blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA). Membranes were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1 h. Blots were detected using enhanced chemiluminescence substrate (Advansta, CA, USA). Quantification was then performed with ImageJ software (Bethesda, MD, USA). Antibodies used were listed in the supplements (Supplementary Table 2).

Immunofluorescence staining

Samples were fixed with 4% PFA for 30 min, followed by blocking with 5% BSA containing 0.2% Triton-X100 for 1 h at room temperature. After incubating with primary antibodies, samples were washed with PBS at least 3 times before adding secondary antibodies. DAPI (Beyotime) was used as the nuclear counterstain. Images were acquired using ZEISS LSM 900 microscope (ZEISS, Oberkochen, Germany). Reagents used were detailed in the supplements (Supplementary Table 2).

Collagen contraction assay

The collagen contraction assay was performed as described before [5]. In brief, collagen type I, medium 199 (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and NaOH (1 M) were mixed on ice at a ratio of 8:1:1 to make the collagen working solution. Next, 0.6 mL of collagen working solution was mixed with 1.2 mL of DPSC suspension (2 × 105 cells/mL). 500 µL of mixture was immediately transferred into 24-well plates and kept in a 37 °C incubator for 30 min before adding 100 µL of α-MEM to each well. After 48 h, collagen gel area was measured and analyzed using ImageJ (Bethesda).

Boyden chamber assay

To perform the Boyden chamber assay, 150 µL of DPSCs (2 × 105 cells/mL) suspended in serum-free medium was seeded into the 24-well transwell inserts. α-MEM containing 10% FBS was added to the lower chamber. After incubation for 24 h, the cells were fixed with 4% PFA and stained with crystal violet. Cells on the interior side of the inserts were removed by swabs, and remaining cells on the exterior surface were imaged using a light microscope (Nikon, Shinagawa-ku, Tokyo). At least 3 randomly selected fields were imaged for later analysis.

Transfection of siRNA

SRF small-interfering RNA (siSRF) and control small-interfering RNA (siNC) were purchased from GenePharma (Shanghai, China). Cells were transfected with siRNA following the manufacturer’s instructions.

3D spheroid sprouting assay

HUVECs and DPSCs were resuspended at a density of 8 × 104 cells/mL in α-MEM containing 20% Methocel (Sigma-Aldrich) and mixed at 1:1 ratio. Cell suspension drops were then placed on non-adherent plastic plates at a volume of 25 µL. Plates were placed upside-down and incubated for 24 h at 37 °C to let the cells aggregate. The next day, the spheroids were collected and resuspended. Thrombin at a final concentration of 2U/mL was added to the suspension. The fibrinogen solution was prepared at 5 mg/mL and mixed with the suspension at 1:1 ratio. 500 µL of mixture was transferred into each 24-well plate immediately. At least 10 randomly selected spheroids per group were captured at 12 h and 24 h using a microscope (Nikon), and cumulative sprouting length at 24 h was measured with ImageJ software (Bethesda).

RNA sequencing

Total RNA was extracted using the TRIzol Reagent (Life Technologies). Only samples with qualified purity, concentration, and integrity were used for later experiments. cDNA libraries were generated using NEBNext UltraTM RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (NEB, USA). After cluster generation, the library preparations were sequenced on an Illumina platform and paired-end reads were generated. DEGs were identified using the criteria of Fold Change ≥ 1.5 and FDR < 0.05. The enrichment of TFBS was analyzed using ToppGene online tool. Reactome enrichment and GSEA of DEGs were performed.

Statistical analysis

Each experiment was repeated at least 3 times independently. Data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). For statistical analysis, student’s t-test was utilized for comparisons between 2 groups, while one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s post hoc test was performed for multi-group comparisons. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS software (IBM, NY, USA). P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Altered EphB4/EphrinB2 signaling affects cell motility of DPSCs

Successful construction of sham knockdown and sham overexpression DPSCs (shNC, orfNC), EphB4-deficient and EphB4-overexpressed DPSCs (shEFB4, orfEFB4) was verified at both gene and protein levels (Fig. 1A and C). To determine EphB4 modulation on cell biological behavior, cell proliferation and movement were evaluated. Cell proliferation was assessed using CCK-8 assays and Ki-67 immunostaining. CCK-8 analysis revealed no significant differences in OD450 values between shNC and shEFB4 across the evaluated time points (Fig. 1B). Aligning with that, the percentage of Ki-67-positive cells remained comparable between shNC and shEFB4, both approximating 70%, suggesting that hampered EphB4 expression did not affect cell proliferation (Fig. 1Ei, iii). Meanwhile, the Boyden chamber assay showed that EphB4 deficiency significantly diminished DPSC motility (Fig. 1Fi, iii). In contrast, EphB4 overexpression enhanced cell migration (Fig. 1Fii, iv) without exerting significant effects on proliferation (Fig. 1D, 1Eii, iv). These results indicate that EphB4/EphrinB2 signaling modulates the migratory capacity of DPSCs but not their proliferative activity.

EphB4/EphrinB2 signaling regulates PC-like function of DPSCs

We further explored the regulatory roles of EphB4/EphrinB2 in the PC-like function of DPSCs. Western blot and RT-qPCR showed that PC markers, such as α-SMA (ACTA2), calponin 1 (CNN1), and SM22-α (TAGLN), were significantly downregulated in EphB4-deficient DPSCs (Fig. 2A, Supplementary Fig. 1), indicating diminished PC-like properties. Immunofluorescence staining further confirmed these results, with decreased fluorescence intensity of α-SMA, calponin 1, and SM22-α in shEFB4 (Fig. 2B). Conversely, overexpression of EphB4 led to a marked upregulation of these markers at both mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 3A, Supplementary Fig. 2). Consistent with this, immunofluorescence signal intensity for the PC markers was stronger in orfEFB4 compared to orfNC (Fig. 3B).

RNA-sequencing suggests SRF as a downstream factor of EphB4/EphrinB2 signaling

To explore the potential downstream factor of EphB4/EphrinB2 signaling in regulating the PC-like properties of DPSCs, RNA-sequencing was performed. The volcano plot (Fig. 4A) depicted the DEGs between shNC and shEFB4, among which ID3, ANGPTL4, ADAMTS9, FBXO41, MYOCD, ACTG2, XKR5, SLC38A5, ATP6V1C2, and IRAG1 were the most significantly downregulated genes, while the most significant upregulated genes included PPL, INMT, ERV3-1-ZNF117, LAMP5, HTR1F, UNC13D, STAB1, ESAM, SLC2A5, and ZNF660-ZNF197. The result also showed that DEGs were significantly enriched in pathways related to Ephrin signaling and smooth muscle contraction (Fig. 4B). GSEA further revealed that the genes involved in smooth muscle contraction (Fig. 4C), angiogenesis (Fig. 4D), and neovascularisation (Fig. 4E) signaling pathways were altered. To understand how EPHB4 knockdown may lead to gene expression changes, we performed enrichment analysis on the TFBS within the promoters of down-regulated genes using ToppFun [36]. The result showed that a significant enrichment of the TFBS of SRF (Fig. 4F), the key transcription factor regulating PC contraction, suggesting that EphB4 may regulate PC-like properties of DPSC through SRF.

EphB4/EphrinB2 signaling manages the PC-like function of DPSCs via SRF

Since a strong association between the expression level of EphB4 and SRF was observed by Western blot (Fig. 5A), we investigated the functional contribution of SRF in the EphB4/EphrinB2-regulated PC-like properties of DPSCs. siRNA was employed to knock down SRF in DPSCs. siSRF resulted in a marked reduction in the expression of PC markers including α-SMA, calponin 1, and SM22-α (Fig. 5B). Collagen contraction assay revealed significantly reduced gel contraction in siSRF compared to the siNC, indicative of impaired PC-like contractile function (Fig. 5C). Importantly, siSRF counteracted the upregulation of α-SMA, calponin 1, and SM22-α induced by EphB4 overexpression (Fig. 5D). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that the EphB4/EphrinB2 signaling pathway modulates the PC-like function of DPSCs through an SRF-dependent mechanism.

Impaired EphB4/EphrinB2 signaling diminishes PC-like ability of DPSCs via SRF in a 3D spheroid sprouting model

To assess the functional roles of EphB4/EphrinB2 signaling in the PC-like properties of DPSCs during sprouting angiogenesis, DPSCs with modulated expression of EphB4 or SRF were co-cultured with HUVECs in a 3D spheroid sprouting model. After 12 h, HUVECs co-cultured with shEFB4 had already initiated fibrin matrix invasion and sprout formation, whereas such responses were barely observed in shNC (Fig. 6Ai). By 24 h, endothelial sprouts were present in both groups; however, quantitative analysis revealed a statistically significant increase in cumulative sprout length in shEFB4 compared with shNC (Fig. 6Aii, iii). A consistent pattern was observed when HUVECs were co-cultured with SRF-silenced DPSCs, which produced markedly more sprouts than the siNC at both time points, as confirmed both visually and quantitatively (Fig. 6B). Taken together, these results indicate that silencing either EphB4 or its downstream effector SRF in DPSCs impairs their PC-like ability to restrain and stabilize HUVECs in sprouting. These findings suggest that disruption of EphB4/EphrinB2/SRF axis in DPSCs compromised their PC-like potential during sprouting angiogenesis in vitro.

Discussion

Tissue engineering has emerged as a promising strategy for treating extensive tissue defects in the oral and maxillofacial region, where conventional tissue graft transplantation holds the drawbacks of donor-site morbidity, insufficient graft volume, and unpredictable resorption. However, a major challenge in engineering viable tissue constructs is the timely and adequate establishment of blood supply. Without a functional vascular network, implanted constructs could go through hypoxia and necrosis due to the shortage of nutrient exchange, waste removal, and oxygen [37, 38]. Consequently, strategies promoting rapid and stable vessel formation are indispensable for translating tissue engineering from the bench to the clinic.

Angiogenesis is the formation of blood vessels from existing vasculature, a process that depends not only on the formation of endothelial tubes but also on the maturation and stabilization of these nascent vessels [2]. PCs provide essential structural support, regulate vascular permeability, and secrete endothelial survival factors to stabilize the adjacent vessels. Endothelial tubes that lack PC coverage are structurally fragile, excessively permeable, and prone to rapid regression following implantation [39]. This is precisely where mesenchymal stem cell (MSC) populations capable of differentiating into functional PCs become invaluable in oral and maxillofacial defect repair, as they can simultaneously serve as angiogenic components of a tissue-engineered construct, generating mature, PC-stabilized vessels that are capable of sustaining long-term perfusion in the challenging environment. DPSCs are stem cells derived from dental pulp that have received significant attention owing to their advantages over other MSCs. Commonly, DPSCs are harvested from extracted wisdom teeth, supernumerary teeth, or teeth extracted for orthodontic reasons. The collection process itself can be part of a routine dental procedure, meaning there will be no additional surgical procedures, and thus the invasiveness is low. There is evidence showing that DPSCs have a faster proliferation rate in comparison with stem cells derived from bone marrow and adipose tissue, which is beneficial in tissue engineering [40, 41]. As for the developmental origin, unlike most MSCs that are mesoderm-derived, DPSCs derive from neural crest, where most of the craniofacial structures originate [42]. This is particularly significant for oral and maxillofacial applications, which means DPSCs are already “programmed” with a tissue memory that favors craniofacial regeneration.

EphB4 and its transmembrane ligand, EphrinB2, have emerged as a paramount regulator of angiogenesis. This pathway actively regulates the sprouting, migration, and proliferation of ECs. Furthermore, this signaling axis is increasingly recognized for its vital role in stem cell-mediated angiogenesis. During post-natal angiogenesis, MSC populations, such as DPSCs, are known to secrete pro-angiogenic factors and function as PCs that directly interact with surrounding ECs. The EphB4/EphrinB2 cascade is instrumental in these processes. A previous study has revealed that EphB4/EphrinB2 pathway mediates the VEGF secretion by DPSCs and therefore modulates endothelial sprouting [4]. In this study, we seek to explore the potential of EphB4/EphrinB2 signaling in regulating PC-like function of DPSCs. Understanding the precise regulatory mechanism is critical for advancing therapeutic strategies targeting vascularization in regenerative therapies.

Our findings demonstrated that altering EphB4 expression in DPSCs had no significant effect on cell proliferation but markedly affected cell motility. Specifically, interference with EphB4/EphrinB2 signaling impaired cellular migration, while its activation enhanced motility. These results align with previous reports showing that the activation of EphB4/EphrinB2 signaling promotes the migratory behavior of different cells [43, 44]. Beyond cell movement, the PC-like characteristics of DPSCs were profoundly affected by EphB4 expression. PCs are characterized by high expression of contractile markers such as α-SMA, calponin 1, and SM22-α, which are crucial for maintaining vascular contractility and stability, features indicating a “differentiated” phenotype [45]. Decreased expression of these markers has been associated with impaired vessel contraction, vascular tone dysregulation, and structural instability [46]. Loss-of-function experiments in mice revealed a significant reduction in systolic blood pressure and a 1.6-fold increase in vascular permeability, highlighting their indispensable role in maintaining vessel stability and integrity [47–49]. In this study, EphB4 deficiency in DPSCs caused a significant downregulation of α-SMA, calponin 1, and SM22-α, whereas EphB4 overexpression resulted in the opposite effect. Since cell contraction would exert mechanical force on surrounding environment, it can be visualized using collagen contraction assay. By embedding cells in collagen matrix, cell contraction could pull the collagen fibers, causing the gel to shrink in area and therefore reflecting the contractile force generated by the cell population. In accordance with our molecular findings, this functional assessment confirmed that DPSC contractility was correlated with EphB4 expression levels. Compared with shNC, shEFB4 exhibited weaker contractility, as the collagen gel area was prominently larger at 48 h. In contrast, the collagen gel shrinkage increased significantly in orfEFB4, suggesting reinforced cell contraction. Collectively, these results indicated EphB4/EphrinB2 signaling modulates the PC-like function of DPSCs.

Transcriptomic analysis also supported the above-mentioned observations. RNA-sequencing results showed an enrichment of genes in smooth muscle contraction that are downregulated following EPHB4 knockdown. Given the shared molecular features and properties between PCs and smooth muscle cells (SMCs), the findings likely reflected their overlapping contractile gene programs rather than distinct lineage identities. Meanwhile, GSEA revealed gene sets regarding angiogenesis and neovascularisation were downregulated in EphB4-deficient DPSCs, which was later assessed by sprouting assay. Interestingly, we noticed that the TFBS of SRF was significantly enriched in down-regulated genes. As SRF has been well documented in orchestrating the transcription of contractile markers and in vascular SMC biology [12, 46, 50], we hypothesized that it may also be the key factor in regulating PC-like function of DPSCs following EphB4 modulation. Therefore, we first investigated the involvement of SRF by evaluating its expression in both EphB4-deficient and EphB4-overexpressed DPSCs by Western blot, which showed that the SRF level accompanied EphB4 changes. The role of SRF was further validated by silencing SRF using siRNA. As expected, the silence of SRF not only diminished the protein levels of PC markers, but the PC-like function of DPSCs. Meanwhile, SRF silence also counteracted the elevation of α-SMA, calponin 1, and SM22-α by EphB4 overexpression, confirming its role as a downstream effector of EphB4/EphrinB2 signaling in modulating the PC-like phenotype in DPSCs.

3D spheroid sprouting assay, in comparison with the conventional two-dimensional (2D) tube formation assay, provides a more physiologically relevant model of angiogenesis, as it can recapitulate several key steps of this process, including EC sprouting, lumen formation, branching, and anastomosis [51–53]. In this study, the functional assessment using a 3D HUVEC-DPSC co-culture spheroid sprouting model further supported our molecular findings. This model allowed direct evaluation of the PC-like function of DPSCs in supporting and modulating endothelial behavior. Previous studies have revealed that PCs can inhibit the proliferation and migration of ECs by providing structural support and paracrine signals, thereby ensuring the organization and stability of the newly-formed vessels [54, 55]. Consistent with these reports, we found that HUVECs co-cultured with EphB4-deficient DPSCs had longer sprouts, indicating a loss of PC-like ability to restrain and stabilize HUVECs in sprouting, a hallmark of impaired PC function. Moreover, the silence of SRF also hampered PC-like function of DPSCs and resulted in longer HUVEC sprouts, confirming its role as a downstream factor for EphB4/EphrinB2 signaling during sprouting angiogenesis.

Taken together, this study identifies a critical mechanistic link between EphB4/EphrinB2 signaling and SRF-mediated transcriptional control in directing the PC-like function of DPSCs in angiogenesis. These findings deepen our understanding of vascular niche regulation and offer translational potential for developing strategies to promote the formation of stable and functional blood vessels in the regeneration of oral maxillofacial defects. Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although SRF was identified as a downstream mediator, the precise upstream events connecting EphB4/EphrinB2 signaling cascade to SRF activation remain unclear and highly warrant further investigation. Second, considering that our study was primarily conducted in vitro, the intricate cell–cell and cell–matrix interactions, and dynamic angiogenic microenvironment present in vivo may not be fully recapitulated. Consequently, it remains to be determined whether the PC-like behavior observed in vitro translates into functional vessel stabilization and long-term perfusion within oral and maxillofacial defects in a living organism. Future studies employing relevant in vivo models, such as DPSC-laden vascularized scaffolds implanted in calvarial, alveolar bone, or dental pulp regeneration models, together with intravital imaging and lineage-tracing approaches, will be essential to validate these findings, evaluate the long-term integration and functionality of DPSC-derived PCs, and assess their therapeutic applicability.

Despite these limitations, the present in vitro data establish a solid mechanistic foundation and provide a compelling rationale for pursuing such preclinical investigations, ultimately paving the way toward clinically translatable, vascularized tissue-engineering strategies for treating oral and maxillofacial defects.

Conclusions

In this study, we investigated the role of EphB4/EphrinB2 signaling in regulating the PC-like function of DPSCs. Our findings revealed that EphB4 positively regulated the PC-like properties of DPSCs: its knockdown reduced the expression of PC markers (α-SMA, calponin 1, and SM22-α) and impaired contractility, whereas EphB4 overexpression produced the opposite effect. RNA-sequencing showed that downregulated genes were significantly enriched in smooth muscle contraction, angiogenesis, and neovascularisation gene sets following EPHB4 silencing. Meanwhile, the TFBS of SRF was decreased prominently. Later investigations confirmed that SRF closely mirrored the expression of EphB4. The silence of SRF mimicked the changes found in EphB4-deficient DPSCs, which also abolished the EphB4-induced enhancement of PC-like function in EphB4-overexpressed DPSCs, confirming its role as a key downstream effector. Consistently, HUVECs co-cultured with EphB4-deficient or SRF-silenced DPSCs both exhibited longer sprouts in the 3D spheroid model, reflecting compromised vessel-stabilizing capacity.

Collectively, these findings establish a novel EphB4/EphrinB2/SRF regulatory axis that drives DPSC differentiation toward PC-like phenotype. This work refines our understanding of vascular niche dynamics in angiogenesis and provides a mechanistic foundation for harnessing DPSCs in vascularized tissue engineering for oral and maxillofacial regeneration.
