Hybrid Microbial-Enzymatic Electrosynthesis for De Novo Decanoic Acid Production from CO2
Authors: Yongze Wang, Wenjin Dong, Meijing Wei, Su Song, Zhiqing Huang, Yaojie Liu, Shelley D. Minteer, Hui Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The steady increase in atmospheric CO2 levels is a key driver of anthropogenic climate change, prompting widespread global concern. In this context, the sustainable conversion of CO2 into value-added chemicals represents an attractive route toward carbon-neutral manufacturing and green chemistry. Here, we developed a proof-of-concept hybrid microbial-enzymatic electrosynthesis system that combines microbial electrosynthesis (MES) and enzymatic electrosynthesis (EES) to convert CO2 into decanoic acid under ambient conditions, using electrical energy. In the MES module, CO2 was converted to acetate via the Wood–Ljungdahl pathway of Clostridium ljungdahlii, using H2 produced by electrochemical water splitting as the electron donor. In the downstream EES module, acetate was transformed into acetyl-CoA, which was then elongated to decanoic acid through an in vitro reversed β-oxidation (rBOX) pathway driven by bioelectrocatalytic NADH regeneration. To verify the functional coupling between the two modules, 13C isotopic labeling was employed to trace carbon flow from CO2 to acetate and further to decanoic acid, confirming that MES-derived carbon served directly as the precursor for downstream chain elongation in the EES module. Under optimized conditions, the hybrid MES-EES system produced 0.80 mM decanoic acid with 81.3% specificity. These results demonstrate the successful hybrid of MES and EES, enabling the de novo bioelectrosynthesis of decanoic acid from CO2 and presenting a promising approach for coupling CO2 resource utilization with renewable electrical energy.

Introduction

The continuous rise in atmospheric CO2 levels is a major contributor to anthropogenic climate change, prompting widespread global concern. Consequently, reducing CO2 emissions and ultimately achieving carbon neutrality have become pressing challenges for modern society. The efficient conversion of CO2 into fuels and value-added chemicals, thereby realizing its resource utilization, offers a promising pathway toward a carbon-neutral economy. , However, the chemical inertness and thermodynamic constraints limit the spontaneous conversion of CO2, necessitating external energy to drive the conversion process. , In recent years, significant advances have been made in the catalytic conversion of CO2 into simple and activated C1 and C2 products, such as carbon monoxide, formate, ethylene, acetate, and ethanol, using renewable electricity or solar energy as the driving force. ,

Despite these advancements, achieving deep conversion of CO2 into complex products remains a critical challenge due to the high energy barrier to C–C coupling and intricate multiproton/electron-transfer pathways. The direct transformation of CO2 into long-chain, structurally complex products with high energy densities remains rare using conventional electrocatalytic and photocatalytic approaches. , To address this issue, a hybrid strategy has been proposed that combines chemical catalysis, such as electrocatalysis, photocatalysis, and thermocatalysis, with subsequent biosynthesis to achieve deep CO2 conversion by capitalizing on the complementary strengths of each approach. − In this paradigm, known as the “reduction-first” strategy, CO2 is initially reduced into simple and reactive C1 or C2 intermediates through chemical methods powered by electrical or solar energy. These intermediates serve as feedstocks for subsequent biosynthetic processes, which further transform them into a variety of C2+ compounds featuring complex structures, high energy densities, and excellent product selectivity. −

Decanoic acid, a typical medium-chain fatty acid with a 10-carbon backbone, has broad industrial applications, including as an antimicrobial agent, food additive, rubber-processing aid, and lubricant thickener. , Additionally, its methyl and ethyl esters exhibit high energy densities due to their elevated carbon-to-oxygen ratios, making decanoic acid a promising “drop-in” biofuel or energy-rich chemical feedstock. Although decanoic acid can be derived from petroleum or plant-based sources, petroleum routes have not become economically dominant, whereas plant extraction remains limited by low productivity and competition with food resources. , These constraints motivate the development of alternative routes for producing decanoic acid from renewable, nonedible carbon sources. In this context, the de novo synthesis of fatty acids, including decanoic acid, from CO2, a significant greenhouse gas emitted by industrial and automotive activities, represents an attractive strategy for both carbon utilization and renewable chemical manufacturing. The third-generation (3G) biosynthesis employs bioconversion systems to convert CO2 and renewable energy sources, such as solar energy and sustainably generated green electricity, into fuels and chemicals. , This approach not only substantially reduces feedstock processing costs but also minimizes risks to food security. Using 3G biosynthetic technologies to convert CO2 into decanoic acid and other fatty acids offers a promising approach to efficient CO2 fixation, sustainable production of medium-chain fatty acids, and high-density energy storage in both volumetric and gravimetric terms.

Bioelectrosynthesis is a practical approach that employs biocatalysts to synthesize target compounds using electrical energy. By combining the strengths of both biosynthesis and electrosynthesis, this strategy offers enhanced effectiveness and sustainability. Accordingly, the conversion of CO2 to decanoic acid through bioelectrosynthesis represents a promising implementation of 3G biosynthetic technology. Microbial electrosynthesis (MES) uses electroactive microorganisms to convert electrical energy and simple carbon sources, like CO2, into valuable chemicals and fuels. In this setup, microbial catalysts accept electrons from cathodes to drive the reduction of CO2. When methanogenesis is inhibited, acetate typically emerges as the dominant product. Although several studies have demonstrated the production of medium-chain fatty acids from CO2 in MES, the most extended carbon chain length reported thus far is C6, and product specificity remains low. − The efficient and selective production of high-value, energy-dense compounds with longer carbon chains, such as decanoic acid, remains a challenge in the development of MES. Enzymatic electrosynthesis (EES), particularly multienzyme electrosynthesis, leverages the synergistic action of multiple enzymes to catalyze complex reactions. This technology offers a promising approach to synthesizing value-added chemicals using external electrical energy, delivering high specificity and selectivity while minimizing side reactions. Here, it is envisioned that a hybrid MES with EES could provide a positive strategy that combines the strengths of microbial metabolism and multienzyme catalysis to achieve the de novo synthesis of decanoic acid from CO2 with high specificity. In such a system, the MES module would first reduce CO2 to activated C2 short-chain fatty acids, such as acetate. And the EES module would then use acetate as a feedstock for downstream carbon chain elongation to produce decanoic acid. The entire process operates under mild conditions and is powered by the renewable electrical energy, which is ultimately stored in the C–C bonds formed through the polymerization of C1 building blocks.

In this study, we developed a proof-of-concept hybrid system that functionally couples MES and EES for CO2-to-decanoic acid conversion. In the MES module, CO2 was first reduced to acetate via the Wood–Ljungdahl pathway (WLP) in wild-type Clostridium ljungdahlii DSM 13528, using H2 produced via the hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) in electrochemical water splitting. In the downstream EES module, the acetate produced was then converted to decanoic acid with high specificity via acetyl-CoA and a reverse β-oxidation (rBOX) pathway driven by bioelectrochemical NADH regeneration. The EES module includes an acetyl-CoA synthetase (ACS, EC 6.2.1.1) from Escherichia coli, a polyphosphate-nucleotide phosphotransferase (PPK, EC 2.7.4.1) from Erysipelotrichaceae bacterium, a β-keto thiolase B (BktB, EC 2.3.1.16) from Cupriavidus necator H16, a fatty acid oxidation complex subunit α (FadB, EC 1.1.1.35) from E. coli, a trans-2-enoyl-CoA reductase (TER, EC 1.3.1.9) from Treponema denticola, an acyl-CoA thioesterase (YdiI, EC 3.1.2.20) from Cronobacter turicensis, and a diaphorase (DI, EC 1.7.1.17) from Geobacillus stearothermophilus (Figure S1). The entire conversion process is powered by electrical energy under mild conditions, achieving both deep CO2 conversion and electrochemical energy storage.

Results and Discussion

Design of a Hybrid Microbial-Enzymatic Electrosynthesis System for Decanoic Acid Production from CO2

As illustrated in Figure , the proposed CO2-to-decanoic acid route was organized into two sequentially coupled functional modules. Module I was designed to convert CO2 into acetate via MES, while Module II upgraded the MES-derived acetate into decanoic acid via EES. In Module I, water oxidation (OER) at the anode generated protons and electrons required for cathodic reduction. Specifically, an H-type dual-chamber cell was employed, in which C. ljungdahlii in the cathodic chamber was separated from the anodic chamber by a Nafion proton exchange membrane (PEM). Protons generated by water splitting at the anode passed through the PEM into the cathodic chamber, where they were reduced to H2 at the cathode surface via HER. Then, C. ljungdahlii utilized hydrogenases to oxidize the in situ generated H2, producing reducing equivalents such as NADH, NADPH, and reduced ferredoxin. These reducing equivalents drove the WLP, enabling anaerobic CO2 fixation into acetate. −

Module II was designed to selectively convert the acetate generated in Module I into decanoic acid through an electroenzymatic cascade. In this module, ACS catalyzed the ATP-dependent activation of acetate to form acetyl-CoA, a central metabolic intermediate that served as the C2 building block for downstream carbon-chain elongation. The required ATP is regenerated in situ by PPK. The resulting acetyl-CoA entered an in vitro rBOX cycle involving BktB, FadB, and TER, thereby enabling stepwise chain elongation. The elongated intermediate, decanoyl-CoA, was then selectively hydrolyzed by YdiI to release decanoic acid and free CoA, the latter being recycled by ACS for continuous acetyl-CoA regeneration. Because the rBOX cycle requires a sustained supply of reducing equivalents, NADH regeneration was incorporated into the EES module through DI, using electrons supplied by the cathode and mediated by reduced methyl viologen (MV+•). In this way, the overall hybrid design linked two sequential carbon-upgrading steps: Module I converted C1 CO2 into C2 acetate, and Module II further extended the carbon chain from C2 to C10, yielding decanoic acid. Thus, this system was designed as a functionally integrated yet physically separated proof-of-concept platform for coupling electricity-driven CO2 fixation with downstream enzymatic carbon-chain elongation under ambient conditions.

Module I: CO2-to-Acetate Conversion through MES

Module I consisted of a MES system that converted CO2 into acetate, transforming chemically inert C1 feedstock into an activated C2 intermediate using C. ljungdahlii cells as the biocatalyst (Figure ). Previous studies have shown that C. ljungdahlii can grow lithoautotrophically via the WLP, using H2 as the energy source and CO2 as the electron acceptor, with acetate as the primary metabolic product. , In this study, H2 was generated through an electrochemical water-splitting system and HER. The resulting H2 likely served as the major molecular electron carrier that could be directly utilized by C. ljungdahlii in situ to drive CO2 reduction to acetate in the cathodic chamber (Figure a). Additionally, the continuous supply of H2 and CO2 helped maintain strict anaerobic conditions in the cathodic chamber, which are essential for the growth and activity of C. ljungdahlii (Figure b).

To validate the roles of CO2 and HER, we assessed C. ljungdahlii growth and acetate production under different conditions. As shown in Figure S2a, the cell density of C. ljungdahlii increased consistently over time under CO2 bubbling with HER, and the OD600 value reached a maximum at 72 h. The negative control experiments were conducted on the same conditions as the MES system but without CO2 or electricity (i.e., no HER). Under these conditions, no detectable growth of C. ljungdahlii was observed, highlighting the essential roles of CO2 as the carbon source and HER-derived H2 as the electron donor in supporting cell growth. The accumulation of acetate followed a similar trend (Figures b and S3). Under CO2 bubbling with HER, the acetate concentration steadily increased over time and reached a maximum of 5.59 mM (titer: 335 mg·L–1) at 72 h, corresponding to an acetate production rate up to 84 mg·L–1·d–1. A small amount of ethanol (0.36 mM) was also produced as a byproduct (Figure S4). In the control groups, no significant accumulation of acetate was observed. As a result, any significant rise in C. ljungdahlii growth or acetate accumulation originates from the provided CO2 and H2 derived from HER. Overall, these results support an H2-mediated CO2-fixation process in the MES module, although minor direct or surface-associated electron transfer cannot be completely excluded. Additionally, a change of approximately 0.25 V in the onset potential over 96 h demonstrates the stability of the MES system (Figure S5). As shown in Figure S2b, the apparent Faradaic efficiency for acetate gradually decreased during MES operation, indicating that electron utilization progressively shifted toward biomass growth, cell maintenance, and other competing sinks rather than net acetate formation.

To further clarify the cathodic process, the pH of the catholyte and cathodic electrochemical behavior were examined. During constant-current MES operation, the catholyte pH increased rapidly from 5.8 to 6.6 within the first 12 h and then remained nearly stable, with only a slight increase to 6.8 during prolonged operation (Figure S6a), which is consistent with proton consumption at the cathode during HER. The cathodic linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) measurements showed clear electrochemical responses in the HER region (Figure S6b), indicating that the carbon felt cathode itself was capable of sustaining hydrogen evolution under the present conditions. Moreover, the difference between the biotic and abiotic LSV curves suggests that the presence of C. ljungdahlii altered the cathodic polarization behavior during MES operation. Although the bulk catholyte remained mildly acidic, proton transport through PEM was still necessary to replenish protons consumed locally at the cathode/electrolyte interface and to maintain charge balance during sustained electrolysis. Therefore, water oxidation in the anodic chamber served as the ultimate proton source at the system level, even though the catholyte itself was not proton-free.

Accordingly, to elucidate the anodic reactions, the electrochemical and chemical processes occurring in the anolyte were investigated in detail (Figure S7a). At the anode, the presence of chloride ions in the anolyte gives rise to competing anodic reactions, such that OER and chloride oxidation (ClER) occur in parallel, generating O2 and Cl2, respectively. As shown in Figure S7b, the anolyte pH decreased rapidly at the initial stage and then remained at a low value, indicating substantial proton generation at the anode. Together with the continuous gas evolution observed during operation (Figure S7c), these results are consistent with anodic water oxidation serving as a major proton-generating process that sustains cathodic HER and, in turn, H2-mediated CO2 fixation in the MES module. Although chlorine-related oxidation also occurred, as indicated by the detection of ClO– (Figure S7d–f), no adverse effects on microbial activity were observed, as evidenced by sustained growth of C. ljungdahlii and continuous acetate production. This can be attributed to the effective separation of anodic and cathodic compartments by the proton exchange membrane, which prevented the crossover of chlorine-derived species into the cathode chamber.

Taking together, these findings demonstrate that the MES module effectively converted CO2 into acetate, with H2-mediated MES likely serving as the dominant pathway under the present conditions, while also revealing that both cathodic and anodic processes were more complex than typically assumed. Future optimization of the MES module should therefore target both sides of the electrochemical system. On the anodic side, electrolyte composition and electrode materials should be optimized to suppress ClER and improve anodic selectivity. On the cathodic side, enhanced electrode materials, improved H2 capture and interfacial electron transfer, and metabolic engineering of intracellular acetate synthesis pathways are expected to further increase acetate accumulation and apparent Faradaic efficiency. At the same time, direct H2 quantification and more rigorous characterization of possible direct electron transfer will be required in future studies to fully resolve the electron-transfer mechanism.

Subsequently, to further confirm the carbon origin of the acetate product, isotopic labeling experiments were performed using 13CO2 and NaH13CO3 as carbon sources under low-organic-carbon culture conditions (see SI). GC-MS analysis showed that the generated acetate contained mixtures of 13C and 12C, with the 13C-acetate isotopologues dominated by almost entirely labeled 13CH3–13COO– (Figure S8). The 13C/12C acetate ratio was approximately 74:26 (Figure c). These results confirm that acetate carbon predominantly originates from the supplied 13CO2 and NaH13CO3, with a smaller portion of 12C likely coming from the yeast extract in the culture medium and cellular carbon reserves.

Previous studies have demonstrated that MES systems combining electrochemical water splitting–HER with CO2-fixing microorganisms can provide a feasible route for converting electricity, water, and CO2 into reduced carbon products. Although H2 exhibits low solubility and limited mass transfer in aqueous systems, it remains an effective molecular redox carrier for linking electrochemical and microbial processes. − As summarized in Table S1, the present MES module remains modest in acetate titer, productivity, and apparent Faradaic efficiency relative to several optimized literature systems. This difference is partly attributable to the intentionally low level of organic nutrients in the catholyte, which was used to minimize interference from non-CO2 carbon sources, as well as to the use of unmodified anodic and cathodic electrodes in the present proof-of-concept setup. Accordingly, the MES module in this study primarily served as an upstream proof-of-concept acetate-supplying interface for downstream EES upgrading.

Optimizing the Enzymatic Pathway for Converting Acetate to Decanoic Acid

Acetate generated from CO2 via MES in Module I must be further converted into acetyl-CoA, which serves as the C2 extender unit for carbon chain elongation and decanoic acid production through the rBOX pathway. This activation step is catalyzed by ACS and requires ATP as an energy source, making efficient ATP supply and regeneration essential for sustained pathway activity. To identify an effective phosphate donor for PPK-catalyzed ATP regeneration, three inorganic polyphosphatessodium tripolyphosphate (P3), sodium tetraphosphate (P4), and sodium hexametaphosphate (P6)were evaluated as representative short- to medium-chain phosphate donors. Among them, P6 gave the best performance and significantly outperformed P3 and P4 in supporting acetyl-CoA formation and downstream decanoic acid production, indicating that sustained ATP regeneration was important for maintaining flux through the cascade (Figures S9–12). In addition to decanoic acid, small amounts of octanoic and dodecanoic acids were also detected. The formation of these C8 and C12 byproducts is consistent with the iterative nature of the rBOX pathway, in which premature hydrolysis of shorter-chain acyl-CoA intermediates or further elongation beyond the C10 intermediate can occur. These results further highlight the need for pathway-level optimization to improve selectivity toward decanoic acid.

In the in vitro rBOX system, ACS and BktB functioned as critical bottlenecks, regulating the supply of acetyl-CoA and the elongation of carbon chains, respectively. Additionally, YdiI is a key enzyme that catalyzes the hydrolysis of decanoyl-CoA, releasing free decanoic acid as the final product. YdiI also played a role in controlling the final product’s carbon chain length, thereby influencing the specificity of decanoic acid production. , To establish a functional benchmark for the in vitro rBOX cascade and improve the efficiency and specificity, enzyme loading optimization was first performed under cofactor-sufficient conditions using 5 mM acetate (final concentration) generated from MES as the substrate. As shown in Figure a, increasing the ACS loading concentration increased decanoic acid production, consistent with improved acetyl-CoA supply to the downstream elongation pathway. With the addition of 37.5 μM ACS, 0.18 mM decanoic acid was produced after a 30 min reaction, with a product specificity of approximately 72%. The optimal loading concentration of BktB, in the presence of 37.5 μM ACS, was 15 μM, yielding 0.35 mM decanoic acid and a specificity of 76% after 30 min (Figure b). However, further increases in BktB concentration led to a decrease in both product accumulation and specificity. This reduction is likely due to excessive BktB activity, which favors the polymerization of acetyl-CoA into short-chain intermediates, rather than facilitating the elongation required for decanoic acid synthesis. For YdiI, the optimal enzyme loading was 2 μM, yielding 0.45 mM decanoic acid with 82% specificity (Figure c), indicating that the terminal hydrolysis step strongly affected product release and final chain-length distribution. To further examine whether additional rBOX enzymes could influence pathway performance, FadB and TER were also subjected to concentration-gradient optimization. As shown in Figure d, FadB loading affected decanoic acid accumulation, with the highest titer obtained at 30 μM. Lower or higher FadB concentrations both resulted in reduced decanoic acid formation, whereas product specificity remained relatively stable. Similarly, TER loading influenced pathway output (Figure e), and decanoic acid production increased with TER concentration up to 30 μM, above which no substantial further improvement was observed. NADH concentration also significantly influenced decanoic acid production, as the operation of the rBOX cycle depends on an adequate supply of reducing equivalents (Figure f). The highest decanoic acid production under these benchmark conditions was obtained at 30 mM NADH. We emphasize that this high-NADH condition was not intended to represent a practical production scenario, but rather to define the intrinsic behavior of the cascade under nonlimiting reducing conditions and to provide a reference state for subsequent evaluation of the electrochemically driven EES system. Following the optimization of ACS, BktB, YdiI, and NADH concentrations, we further monitored the time-course production of decanoic acid, octanoic acid, and dodecanoic acid. After 1 h of reaction, the maximum concentration of decanoic acid reached 0.81 mM with a maintained specificity of 82%, accompanied by the formation of 0.12 mM octanoic acid and 0.07 mM dodecanoic acid (Figure a). The overall conversion of 5 mM acetate was approximately 99% (Figure b). These results define a cofactor-sufficient benchmark for selective C10 formation and provide a pathway-level reference for interpreting the performance of the downstream electroenzymatic system.

Module II: Acetate-to-Decanoic Acid Conversion via EES

The rBOX cycle requires a continuous and sufficient supply of NADH, as the synthesis of one molecule of decanoic acid consumes five molecules of acetate and eight molecules of NADH. Therefore, establishing an efficient NADH regeneration system is critical to avoid the need for high concentrations of costly NADH. A commonly used approach for NADH regeneration in biosynthesis is enzyme-coupled regeneration, in which a regeneration enzyme catalyzes the reduction of NAD+ to NADH using a sacrificial substrate. In contrast, bioelectrocatalytic NADH regeneration offers a more sustainable alternative by directly utilizing electricity as the electron source, thereby eliminating the need for sacrificial substrates and avoiding byproduct formation. , In this study, the free MV2+ was employed as an electron mediator to shuttle electrons from the cathode to DI, which then catalyzed the reduction of NAD+ to regenerate NADH. Cyclic voltammetry (CV) analysis was used to evaluate the electrochemical behavior of MV2+/DI/NAD+ in the EES system. As shown in Figure S13, MV2+ showed a characteristic redox couple, with the oxidized MV2+ to reduced MV occurring at approximately −0.73 V vs SCE. After addition of DI and NAD+, a more obvious catalytic wave was observed in the same potential region. This result demonstrated that the electrons mediated by MV2+ were utilized by DI and finally transferred to NAD+ to realize the recycling of NADH. To ensure effective operation of the NADH-regeneration module, the DI concentration was first optimized. As shown in Figure S14, the highest decanoic acid production was obtained at 60 μM DI and increasing the DI concentration did not improve product formation. Further, the working potential of the EES system was optimized to maintain a high current density for high conversion rates. As shown in Figure , the optimal working potential in the presence of 1 mM NAD+ was −0.80 V vs SCE, corresponding to an overpotential of approximately 70 mV for the reduction of MV2+.

The electron transfer from the cathode to NAD+, mediated by MV2+ and catalyzed by DI, was further confirmed by chronoamperometry conducted at −0.80 V vs SCE (Figure a). Upon the injection of 1 mM NAD+, a pronounced reductive current response was observed in the presence of both DI and MV2+, which gradually decreased over time. This current response was absent in the control group without DI and MV2+, indicating that the observed current originated from the DI-catalyzed reduction of NAD+, which consumes both reduced MV+• and electrons from the cathode. Moreover, the operation of the rBOX cycle further consumed MV+•, thereby sustaining electron transfer and maintaining a stable electrochemical environment for NADH regeneration. Consistent with this result, decanoic acid formation was detected only in the presence of both DI and MV2+ (Figure S15), further supporting the functionality of the mediated NADH regeneration pathway. As shown in Figure b, the concentration of decanoic acid produced from acetate via EES steadily increased over time, reaching a maximum of 0.80 mM after 3 h of reaction. During this period, 0.09 mM octanoic acid and 0.10 mM dodecanoic acid were also detected, resulting in a product specificity of 81.3% for decanoic acid. The overall conversion rate from 5 mM acetate to total fatty acids, including decanoic acid, octanoic acid, and dodecanoic acid, reached 98.8% (Figure S16a). The Faradaic efficiency of the EES process was evaluated over time. As shown in Figure S16b, the Faradaic efficiency for decanoic acid synthesis progressively increased, peaking at approximately 68.6% after 3 h. Similarly, the Faradaic efficiency for the total fatty acid production reached a maximum of approximately 82.4% at 3 h, indicating efficient electron utilization during the peak reaction period. The slight decline thereafter likely resulted from nonideal coupling between NADH regeneration and the enzymatic cascade, together with other minor nonproductive electron losses. To further probe cofactor-regeneration dynamics in real time, NADH formation was monitored under different electroenzymatic conditions (Figure S17). In the absence of the rBOX enzymes, the MV2+/DI/NAD+ system under applied potential rapidly accumulated NADH and reached a plateau, directly supporting that the cathode-MV2+/MV+•-DI pathway could regenerate NADH from NAD+. In the complete EES system containing the rBOX cascade, NADH accumulated more gradually at a later stage, reflecting the net balance between electroenzymatic NADH regeneration and continuous NADH consumption by downstream chain elongation. In contrast, only negligible NADH formation was observed in the absence of MV2+, DI, or applied potential, indicating that all three components were required for effective electricity-driven NADH regeneration. These results provide a direct kinetic profile of cofactor regeneration and further support functional coupling between electroenzymatic NADH supply and acetate upgrading in the EES module.

It is worth noting that the rate of decanoic acid production in the EES system with 1 mM NAD+ was lower than that observed in the enzymatic system supplied with 30 mM NADH directly. This comparison indicates that the EES system retained the intrinsic catalytic capability of the rBOX cascade, but its apparent productivity was limited under electricity-driven conditions. This difference is most likely due to the limited rate of electroenzymatic NADH regeneration catalyzed by MV2+ and DI. In addition, mass-transfer mismatch between the NADH regeneration step and the downstream enzymatic chain-elongation reactions may further constrain overall performance. Therefore, although the working potential has been optimized, further improvement of the adaptability of the electrochemical system architecture and the biocatalytic components remains essential. Future improvements should focus on increasing the rate of NADH regeneration, enhancing electron delivery and utilization, and optimizing reactor architecture and biocatalyst stability to improve the overall performance of the electroenzymatic system.

To further evaluate the operational stability of the EES module, the reaction was further extended to 24 h under repeated acetate feeding (Figure S18). As shown in Figure S18a, decanoic acid continued to accumulate over time and reached approximately 2.56 mM after 24 h, while the specificity of decanoic acid remained relatively stable at around 80%, indicating that the cascade retained selective C10-forming capability during prolonged operation. The FE of the fed-batch EES system was also monitored over time (Figure S18b). The FE for total fatty acid production remained at a relatively high level throughout the major part of the operation, while the FE for decanoic acid showed a modest decline at later stages. Meanwhile, the decanoic acid accumulation observed after extended operation was lower than the value expected, indicating that the EES system retained catalytic function but underwent partial activity decay. In parallel, amperometric i-t analysis (Figure S18c) showed that the cathodic current could be reactivated after each acetate addition, confirming that the electroenzymatic NADH regeneration module remained functional over multiple cycles. confirming that the electroenzymatic NADH-regeneration module remained functional over multiple feeding cycles. However, the peak current gradually decreased upon successive acetate additions, indicating progressive activity decay with time. To further probe the origin of this decay, time-dependent CV measurements were carried out during the 24 h fed-batch process (Figure S19). The mediator-associated catalytic response remained observable throughout the operation, indicating that MV2+ retained electrochemical activity in the complex EES milieu. At the same time, the progressive attenuation of the CV response suggests partial loss of overall electroenzymatic activity during prolonged operation, likely associated with enzyme deactivation, mediator instability, or changes in the reaction environment. Taken together, these results show that the integrated EES module remained operational during extended fed-batch operation, while its long-term performance was limited by gradual activity loss. Future work should focus on enzyme robustness, mediator stability, and electroenzymatic coupling. In particular, replacing free mediators with spatially confined or electrode-anchored redox polymers represents a promising strategy to improve mediator stability, facilitate product separation, and enable mediator recycling.

Similarly, to further trace carbon incorporation during decanoic acid biosynthesis and verify the functional coupling between the MES and EES modules, 13C isotopic labeling EES experiment was carried out. The mixture of isotopically labeled and unlabeled acetate generated in the 13C-labeled MES experiment was used as the substrate without purification or replacement. As shown in Figure S20, LC-MS isotopologue profiling of the resulting fatty acids demonstrated the incorporation of 13C into octanoic acid, decanoic acid, and dodecanoic acid, providing direct evidence that carbon from 13CO2 was elongated via the rBOX cycle to form decanoic acid. The 13C labeling pattern of the products (Figure c) revealed that the proportions of 13C atoms incorporated into decanoic acid, octanoic acid, and dodecanoic acid were approximately 73.59%, 73.91%, and 72.91%, respectively, which are essentially consistent with the labeling pattern of the acetate produced by MES. These results confirm a continuous and functionally integrated CO2-to-acetate-to-decanoic acid pathway, demonstrating that carbon fixed during upstream MES serves as a direct precursor for chain elongation in the downstream EES module.

Conclusion

In this study, we developed an experimentally validated hybrid CO2-to-C10 bioconversion system by hybrid MES and EES, enabling the selective conversion of chemically inert CO2 into decanoic acid under mild conditions, driven by electrical energy. In the MES module, CO2 was activated through an electrochemical water splitting system, in which HER-generated H2 acted as the electron carrier to drive the Wood–Ljungdahl pathway in C. ljungdahlii, producing acetate as the key linking intermediate. In the subsequent EES module, acetate was converted into acetyl-CoA within ATP regeneration and used as the C2 extender unit for the rBOX cycle, while MV2+/DI-mediated electron transfer enabled NADH regeneration, ultimately achieving the selective synthesis of decanoic acid. Although the current product titers remain modest, this work establishes a proof-of-concept route for coupling electricity-driven CO2 fixation with downstream enzymatic carbon-chain elongation. Future improvements in electrode materials, reactor configuration, mass transfer, enzyme stability, and cofactor regeneration are expected to enhance overall system performance. This hybrid system represents a sustainable and environmentally friendly method for chemical manufacturing from CO2, H2O and renewable electricity. More broadly, the strategy demonstrated here provides a modular framework for extending electricity-driven CO2 conversion toward other acetyl-CoA-derived products. Furthermore, by leveraging microbial metabolic engineering in MES and the practical design of multienzyme cascades in EES, the system is expected to enable on-demand synthesis of diverse target compounds powered by clean electricity.
