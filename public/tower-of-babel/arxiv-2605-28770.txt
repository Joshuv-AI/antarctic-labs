Cutoff profiles for conjugacy invariant random walks on symmetric groups
Lucas Teyssier

Abstract
We prove asymptotic equivalents for finite-level representations of symmetric groups, that is, for Young diagrams having all but finitely many boxes on their first row. We deduce that random walks on symmetric groups generated by conjugacy classes with a macroscopic number of fixed points have a Poissonian cutoff profile. We also prove that the random involution walk exhibits cutoff and find its cutoff profile. Finally, we obtain numerics for the random transposition walk on a deck of 52 cards, giving concrete estimates on the question that originally motivated Diaconis and Shahshahani.

Introduction

1.1
Some history

Let
n\geq 2
and let
{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
be the symmetric group of index
n
. Given a probability measure
\mu
on
{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
, we refer to the discrete time Markov chain with transition probabilities
P(\sigma,\tau)=\mu(\sigma^{-1}\tau)
for
\sigma,\tau\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
as the
random walk driven by
\mu
or
\mu
-walk
.
Denote the uniform measure on a finite set
S
by
\textup{Unif}_{S}
, and the conjugacy class of transpositions of
{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
by
{\mathcal{T}}
.

The
\textup{Unif}_{{\mathcal{T}}}
-walk (with laziness
1/n
) is a historically important model known as the random transposition shuffle.
In terms of card shuffling, this corresponds to picking at each step two cards independently and uniformly at random, and swapping them. The initial problem that emerged at the Bell Laboratories in the 1970’s was to understand how many random transpositions are needed to mix a deck of 52 cards. We refer to the conversation between Aldous and Diaconis
[
Ald13
]
for a more detailed history of the problem.

After initial attempts relying purely on probability failed, Diaconis and Shahshahani
[
DS81
]
solved this problem (asymptotically) using representation theory. They proved that a phase transition, called cutoff, occurs around
t_{n}:=\frac{1}{2}n\ln n
steps. Essentially, what they proved is that for any
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, denoting the distribution of the walk after
t
steps by
\mu_{t}
, we have

\textup{d}_{\textup{TV}}\left(\mu_{\left\lfloor(1-\varepsilon)t_{n}\right\rfloor},\textup{Unif}_{{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}}\right)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}1\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ and }\hskip 10.00002pt\textup{d}_{\textup{TV}}\left(\mu_{\left\lceil(1+\varepsilon)t_{n}\right\rceil},\textup{Unif}_{{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}}\right)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0,

(1.1)

where the total variation distance between two probability measures
\mu
and
\nu
on a finite set
S
is given by
\textup{d}_{\textup{TV}}(\mu,\nu)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x\in S}|\mu(x)-\nu(x)|
.

This led to several refinements and generalizations, and in particular to
\textup{Unif}_{{\mathcal{C}}}
-walks, where
{\mathcal{C}}
is a conjugacy class:

[
Rou00
,
BSZ11
]

for conjugacy classes with small support,

[
Hou16
,
BŞ19
]
for conjugacy classes of support size
o(n)
,
[
Lul96
,
LS08
,
TT24
]
for fixed point free permutations,
[
Roi96
,
MSP07
]
for general
L^{2}
bounds (that are however weaker than cutoff). Recently, in a companion paper, we proved the
L^{2}
cutoff for all conjugacy classes with
\Theta(n)
fixed points
[
OTTT25
]
. The cutoff result of Diaconis and Shahshahani was also generalized to several models of non-uniform transpositions
[
Dia88a
,
Lac16b
,
BCMR21
,
NY24
,
AN25
]
.

What happens within the phase transition is also well understood for transpositions. In
[
Tey20
]
, we found the

: for any

, we have

\textup{d}_{\textup{TV}}\left(\mu_{\left\lfloor t_{n}(1+a/\ln n)\right\rfloor},\textup{Unif}_{{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}}\right)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}\textup{d}_{\textup{TV}}\left(\textup{Poiss}(1),\textup{Poiss}\left(1+e^{-a}\right)\right),

(1.2)

where

The convergence in (

in
[

]
, and to models that are not conjugacy invariant, such as star transpositions
[
Nes24
]
. Intuitively, the profile involves Poisson laws because the last
observable
to be mixed is the number of fixed points.
This intuition was recently made rigorous for transpositions
[
JS26
]
, and generalized to decks with repeated cards
[
Che26
]
. The separation profile for transpositions (and for other models) was also recently found
[
FN26
]
.

Cutoff profiles have also been found in several other contexts: for instance for the hypercube
[
DGM90
]
, the riffle shuffle
[
BD92
]
, repeated averages
[
CDSZ22
]
, the symmetric and asymmetric simple exclusion processes
[
Lac16a
,
BN22
]
. Many other cutoff profiles have been found in the last few years. Recent accounts of the literature on cutoff profiles can be found in
[
OTS26
,
Tey26
]
.

Main results

The main novelty of the paper consists of Theorems

Denote the set of conjugacy classes of

For

The next statement shows that they furthermore have a Poissonian cutoff profile, as conjectured (for conjugacy classes with

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\delta>0
.
For each

, let
{\mathcal{C}}^{(n)}\in{\textup{Conj}}^{*}({\mathfrak{S}}_{n})
. Assume that

Let

and

be a sequence of integers such that

(1.4)

Given an even integer
n\geq 2
, the random involution walk on
{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
with parameter

is the walk driven by the measure

\textup{RI}_{n,p}=\sum_{s=0}^{n/2}\binom{n/2}{s}(1-p)^{s}p^{n/2-s}\textup{Inv}_{n,s},

where

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
p\in(0,1)
.
Let
a\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Let
S\subset{\mathbb{N}}
and
(t_{n})_{n\in S}
be a sequence of integers such that
t_{n}=\frac{\ln n+a+o(1)}{\ln(1/p)}
.
As
S\ni n\to\infty
we have

\textup{d}_{\textup{TV}}\left(\left(\textup{RI}_{n,p}\right)^{*t_{n}},\textup{Unif}_{{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}}\right)\to\textup{d}_{\textup{TV}}\left(\textup{Poiss}\left(1+e^{-a}\right),\textup{Poiss}(1)\right).

(1.6)

Finally, we give estimates on the random transposition walk for
n=52
.
They rely on computers only for elementary computations. This illustrates that using representation theory can give very precise results not only asymptotically, but also for small values of

, and gives concrete estimates on the question at the origin of the study of the cutoff phenomenon.

Proposition 1.3
.

Let
n=52
. Recall that
{\mathcal{T}}
denotes the conjugacy class of transpositions. Write
\text{t}_{\textup{mix}}(\varepsilon)=\min\left\{t\geq 0\mid\textup{d}_{\textup{TV}}(\textup{Unif}_{{\mathcal{T}}}^{*t},\textup{Unif}_{{\mathfrak{E}}({{\mathcal{T}}},t)})\leq\varepsilon\right\}
for

. We have

(a)

;

(b)

;

(c)

.

Remark 1.1
.

Laziness has a significant impact for small values of
n
for the original lazy walk (the
\left(\frac{1}{n}+\frac{n-1}{n}\textup{Unif}_{{\mathcal{T}}}\right)
-walk (started at the identity permutation
Id
), which we denote by

).

For

, the sign of permutations is not fully mixed yet, and one can even bet on

being even.
More precisely, the number of times

.

1.3
Obtaining cutoff profiles with eigen-analysis

There are different techniques to obtain cutoff profiles, using spectral analysis, probabilistic arguments, or a bit of both. For walks that have lots of symmetries, it is often possible to obtain detailed spectral information. Then to obtain the cutoff profile it is sufficient to have the following three ingredients:

(1)

precise-enough bounds on eigenvalues and their multiplicities;

(2)

precise-enough Taylor expansions on the eigenvalues that
asymptotically contribute
;

(3)

identities that relate eigenvectors corresponding to eigenvalues that
asymptotically contribute
.

In this paper, we focus on the
\textup{Unif}_{{\mathcal{C}}}
-walks, where
{\mathcal{C}}
is a conjugacy class of
{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
with
\Theta(n)
fixed points (or a mixture of such classes for random involutions). For these walks, (1) was obtained in
[
OTTT25
]
and (3) was obtained in
[
Tey20
]
. The last piece needed to prove Theorem
1.1
is therefore to prove asymptotic equivalents for some of the eigenvalues, i.e. to prove (2).

Let us give more context on methods leading to (1), (2), and (3), and how (2) differs from (1) and (3). This paragraph assumes some knowledge of representation theory.

For (1), one needs to obtain precise enough uniform bounds on characters (i.e. on eigenvalues). There is no known way to do this using only the Murnaghan–Nakayama rule. In
[
OTTT25
]
we relied on the Naruse hook length formula (see
[
MPP18
]
), and had to introduce deep triple decompositions of Young diagrams to quantify which parts of the diagrams contribute to characters and which parts contribute to dimensions (i.e. to multiplicities of eigenvalues).
This combinatorial technique was designed for
high frequencies
, which are the most delicate to control despite not contributing asymptotically. The counting arguments worked well for conjugacy classes with, say,
\varepsilon n
to
(1-\varepsilon)n
fixed points, and some probabilistic bootstrapping was needed to transfer the bounds to conjugacy classes with support
o(n)
.

For (3), we explained in
[
Tey20
, Section 2]
how to filter out
low frequencies
(the ones which asymptotically contribute) and obtained, mostly via the Murnaghan–Nakayama rule, a character identity
[
Tey20
, Lemma 4.3]
which tells how some weighted sums of characters (i.e. eigenvectors) at the same
level
asymptotically compensate (for most permutations). We thank Christian Krattenthaler for identifying the polynomials from this identity to be Poisson–Charlier polynomials.

For (2) which is the goal of the present paper, we will prove asymptotic equivalents of characters for low frequencies in Theorem
2.3
. This also relies on the Murnaghan–Nakayama rule but in a different way: we study characters individually and we count how many ribbon tableaux of a Young diagram there are (the ribbons are also peeled in a different order compared to what was done for (3)), depending on the number of boxes over the first row which are covered by ribbons of length at least 2. We also complement these equivalents with bounds, such as Theorem
2.5
, which can be proved by the same technique but are useful for other problems. We discuss some of these other potential applications at the end of Section
2.2
.

We assume familiarity with the representation theory of symmetric groups and refer to

]

Given a Young diagram

, we denote the length of its

.

We write

•

•

•

We often emphasize the dependence on a parameter by writing it as an index. For example, if

.

2
Asymptotics of finite-level characters

2.1
General statements

We introduce a new quantity to better capture the contribution of the cycle structure of permutations to characters: for
\sigma\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
we set

M(\sigma)=\max_{i\geq 1}f_{i}(\sigma)^{1/i};

(2.1)

and the rescaled version

J(\sigma)=\frac{\ln M(\sigma)}{\ln n}=\max_{i\geq 1}\frac{1}{i}\frac{\ln f_{i}(\sigma)}{\ln n}.

(2.2)

Recall that
\textup{ch}^{\lambda}
denotes the character and
\chi^{\lambda}=\textup{ch}^{\lambda}/d_{\lambda}
is the renormalized character. The
level
, which we denote by
r(\lambda)
, of a representation (or equivalently of a Young diagram)
\lambda\vdash n
is the number of boxes outside of its first row, that is,
r(\lambda)=n-\lambda_{1}
. Intuitively, representations with a low level correspond to low frequencies in terms of Fourier analysis.

We prove the following general bound on finite-level characters of symmetric groups.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
r\geq 0
. As
n\to\infty
, uniformly over all
\lambda\vdash n
such that
\lambda_{1}=n-r
and every
\sigma\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
, we have

\left|\textup{ch}^{\lambda}(\sigma)\right|\lesssim_{r}M(\sigma)^{r};

(2.3)

and equivalently,

\left|\textup{ch}^{\lambda}(\sigma)\right|\lesssim_{r}d_{\lambda}^{J(\sigma)}.

(2.4)

Remark 2.1
.

Larsen and Shalev
[
LS08
]
introduced an orbit growth exponent
E(\sigma)
and proved a uniform bound
\left|\textup{ch}^{\lambda}(\sigma)\right|\leq d_{\lambda}^{E(\sigma)+o(1)}
, which we improved to
\left|\textup{ch}^{\lambda}(\sigma)\right|\leq d_{\lambda}^{E(\sigma)+O(1/\ln n)}
in
[
TT24
]
. For representations of level
r
, this bound can be rewritten as

\left|\textup{ch}^{\lambda}(\sigma)\right|\lesssim_{r}d_{\lambda}^{E(\sigma)}.

(2.5)

The quantity
E(\sigma)
is optimal for
pure
cycle structures, that is if
\sigma\sim m^{n/m}
for some
m
. In this case we have
J(\sigma)=E(\sigma)
. However
E(\sigma)
fails to give the correct exponent for hybrid complex structures.
For example, omitting integer parts, if
\sigma
is an involution with
\sqrt{n}
fixed points, that is, if
\sigma\sim(1^{\sqrt{n}},2^{(n-\sqrt{n})/2})
,
E(\sigma)=3/4+o(1)
while
J(\sigma)=1/2
.

We conjecture that the sharpest exponential character bound, uniform over all
\lambda\in\widehat{{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}}
and all
\sigma\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
, should be written in terms of
J(\sigma)
, with an error term that is so small that it does not affect the applications to mixing times.

Remark 2.2
.

The main result of
[
OTTT25
]
is a character bound that is uniform over all
\lambda
and all
\sigma
with
\Theta(n)
fixed points, and that can be rewritten as
\left|\textup{ch}^{\lambda}(\sigma)\right|\leq d_{\lambda}^{J(\sigma)}
. However the bound for permutations with
o(n)
fixed points are currently weaker than this.

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
r\geq 1
. As
n\to\infty
, uniformly over all diagrams
\lambda\vdash n
with
n-\lambda_{1}=r
, and all permutations
\sigma\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
with
f_{1}(\sigma)\geq\sqrt{n}
, we have, denoting
f=f_{1}(\sigma)
and
k=n-f
,

\chi^{\lambda}(\sigma)=\left(\frac{f}{n}\right)^{r}\left(1+O_{r}\left(\frac{k}{f^{2}}\right)\right).

(2.6)

Remark 2.3
.

The case of [
k
-cycles with
k\leq n/3
] of Theorem
2.2
is known as
[
NOT22
, Corollary 3.4]
, and the case of finite
k
-cycles was reproved independently in
[
Ful24
, Lemma 4.3]
, both with the same the error term. Both results above rely on the character estimates of
[
Hou16
]
, which rely on complex analysis.

Remark 2.4
.

Let
n\geq 2
,
\sigma\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
and
\lambda\vdash n
. Then
\chi^{\lambda^{\prime}}(\sigma)={\textup{sgn}}(\sigma)\chi^{\lambda}(\sigma)
, where
\lambda^{\prime}
is the conjugate of
\lambda
. Therefore Theorem
2.2
also applies up to a factor
{\textup{sgn}}(\sigma)
to diagrams with
\lambda_{1}^{\prime}=n-O(1)
.

2.2
Statements for applications

The following consequences of Theorems
2.1
and
2.2
will be useful in practice. Corollary

is a generalization of
[

, Lemma 4.4]
(which is implicit in
[

]
). It follows from Theorem

, which itself follows from Theorem

. Theorem

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
r\geq 0
. For each
n\geq 1
, let
\sigma_{n}\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
.
Assume that
f_{1}(\sigma_{n})/\sqrt{n}\to\infty
. Then as

, uniformly over all
\lambda\vdash n
such that
\lambda_{1}=n-r
, we have

\chi^{\lambda}(\sigma_{n})=\left(f_{1}(\sigma_{n})/n\right)^{r+o_{r}(1/\ln n)}.

(2.7)

Corollary 2.4
.

Let
r\geq 0
. Let

. For each
n\geq 1
, let
\sigma_{n}\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
. Let
(t_{n})
be a sequence of positive real numbers such that

.
Assume that
f_{1}(\sigma_{n})/\sqrt{n}\to\infty
. Then as
n\to\infty
, uniformly over all
\lambda\vdash n
such that
\lambda_{1}=n-r
, we have

d_{\lambda}\chi^{\lambda}(\sigma_{n})^{t_{n}}\to\frac{e^{-rc}}{r!}d_{\lambda^{*}},

(2.8)

where
\lambda^{*}=(\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3},\ldots)\vdash r
is the diagram

minus its first row.

Theorem 2.5
.

Let
r\geq 0
. For each
n\geq 1
, let
\sigma_{n}\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
.
Assume that
f_{1}(\sigma_{n})=o(\sqrt{n})
and
f_{2}(\sigma_{n})=o(n)
. Then as
n\to\infty
, uniformly over all
\lambda\vdash n
such that
\lambda_{1}=n-r
, we have

\chi^{\lambda}(\sigma_{n})=o_{r}(n^{-r/2}).

(2.9)

Corollary 2.6
.

Let
r\geq 0
. For each
n\geq 1
, let
\sigma_{n}\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
.
Assume that
f_{1}(\sigma_{n})=o(\sqrt{n})
and

. Then as

, uniformly over all
\lambda\vdash n
such that
\lambda_{1}=n-r
, we have

(2.10)

Remark 2.5
.

It is not hard to check that the assumptions “
f_{1}(\sigma_{n})/\sqrt{n}
” and “
f_{1}(\sigma_{n})=o(\sqrt{n})
and

” in the results above are sharp, by looking at diagrams with two rows, i.e. diagrams

.

In this paper we only use Theorem

, and only consider applications to cutoff profiles.

Asymptotic equivalents of characters are also useful on their own (even if one is not able to bound characters uniformly) to obtain information on statistics of random permutations. Fulman
[

]
(and independently Nestoridi and Yan
[

]

. This was generalized by Arcona
[
Arc26
]
, who obtained limiting results for structure of small cycles for a product of
\Theta(n)
transpositions of
{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
. Corollary
2.4
may be used to generalize these results to conjugacy classes with
\omega(\sqrt{n})
fixed points. On the other hand, the small cycle structure was proved to be close to that of a uniform permutation for some products of two conjugacy invariant random permutations in
[
KM20
]
and in
[
BCP19
]
. We believe that combining Arcona’s arguments with Theorem
2.5
would enable recovering these results.

2.3
The Murnaghan–Nakayama rule

Our proofs rely on the Murnaghan–Nakayama rule (see for instance
[
Mé17
, Theorem 3.10]
) which is an explicit combinatorial formula that enables computing characters.

We refer the reader unfamiliar with the Murnaghan–Nakayama rule (and the related notions of skew partition, ribbon, ribbon tableau, height) to a companion paper
[
Tey25
, Section 3.1]
, which explains it with a fair amount of details and illustrations.

We use the following notation:

•

d_{\lambda\backslash\mu}
for the number of standard tableaux of a skew partition
\lambda\backslash\mu
;

•

{\textup{RT}}(\lambda,\alpha)
for the set of ribbon tableaux of shape
\lambda
and weight
\alpha
;

•

{\textup{RT}}(\lambda)
for the set of all ribbon tableaux of shape
\lambda
(and of any weight);

•

0pt(\rho)
for the height of a ribbon
\rho
;

•

0pt(T)=\sum_{\rho\in T}0pt(\rho)
for the height of a ribbon tableau
T
.

The Murnaghan–Nakayama rule can then be stated as follows.
If
n\geq 1
,
\lambda\vdash n
, and
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
, writing
\alpha
for the cycle lengths of
\sigma
(in any order), we have

\textup{ch}^{\lambda}(\sigma)=\sum_{T\in{\textup{RT}}(\lambda,\alpha)}(-1)^{0pt(T)}.

(MN)

2.4
Estimates on dimensions of representations

There are many bounds on dimensions of irreducible representations in the literature. We will use the following one, which follows shortly from the hook length formula. Recall that given a Young diagram
\lambda\vdash n
, we write
r(\lambda)=n-\lambda_{1}
.

Lemma 2.7

(
[
Tey20
, Proposition 3.2]
)
.

Let
r\geq 0
. As
n\to\infty
, uniformly over all
\lambda\vdash n
such that
r(\lambda)=r
, we have

d_{\lambda}=\binom{n}{r}d_{\lambda^{*}}\left(1-\frac{r}{n}+O_{r}\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\right)\right).

(2.11)

We also prove a second order expansion on descending factorials.
Given two integers
0\leq r\leq n
we write
n^{\downarrow r}=r!\binom{n}{r}=n(n-1)\cdots(n-r+1)
. For
m\geq 0
denote the
m
-th triangular number by
T(m)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}i=\frac{m(m+1)}{2}
.

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
r\geq 1
. As
n\to\infty
we have

n^{\downarrow r}=n^{r}\left(1-\frac{T(r-1)}{n}+O_{r}\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\right)\right).

(2.12)

Proof.

Expanding the product (which has finitely many terms), and using that
r=O_{r}(1)
, we get

\frac{n^{\downarrow r}}{n^{r}}=\prod_{i=0}^{r-1}\left(1-\frac{i}{n}\right)=1-\frac{\sum_{i=0}^{r-1}i}{n}+O_{r}\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\right)=1-\frac{T(r-1)}{n}+O_{r}\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\right).\qed

(2.13)

We deduce the following.

Lemma 2.9
.

Let
r,K,f
be three integers such that and
0\leq K\leq r\leq f/2
. As
n\to\infty
, uniformly over all
\lambda\vdash n
such that
r(\lambda)=r
and all
\mu\vdash f
such that
\mu\subset\lambda
and
r(\mu)=r-K
, we have

\frac{d_{\mu}}{d_{\lambda}}\asymp_{r}\left(\frac{f}{n}\right)^{r}f^{-K};

(2.14)

and moreover, if
K=0
(that is, if
\mu^{*}=\lambda^{*}
), we have, denoting
k=n-f
,

\frac{d_{\mu}}{d_{\lambda}}=\left(\frac{f}{n}\right)^{r}\left(1-T(r)\frac{k}{fn}+O_{r}\left(\frac{1}{f^{2}}\right)\right).

(2.15)

Proof.

Uniformly over all
\lambda
and
\mu
as above, by Lemma
2.7
we have
d_{\lambda}\asymp_{r}\binom{n}{r}\asymp_{r}n^{r}
and
d_{\mu}\asymp_{r}\binom{f}{r-K}\asymp_{r}f^{r-K}
, so
\frac{d_{\mu}}{d_{\lambda}}\asymp_{r}\left(\frac{f}{n}\right)^{r}f^{-K}
, which proves the first point. For the second point, assuming that
K=0
, we have
d_{\lambda^{*}}=d_{\mu^{*}}
, so by Lemma
2.7
we have

\frac{d_{\mu}}{d_{\lambda}}=\frac{\binom{f}{r}}{\binom{n}{r}}\left(1-r\left(\frac{1}{f}-\frac{1}{n}\right)+O_{r}\left(\frac{1}{f^{2}}\right)\right).

(2.16)

Moreover, by Lemma
2.13
, we have

\frac{\binom{f}{r}}{\binom{n}{r}}=\left(\frac{f}{n}\right)^{r}\left(1-T(r-1)\left(\frac{1}{f}-\frac{1}{n}\right)+O_{r}\left(\frac{1}{f^{2}}\right)\right).

(2.17)

The result follows, since
T(r-1)+r=T(r)
and
\frac{1}{f}-\frac{1}{n}=\frac{k}{fn}
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
2.2
.

Denote the cycle structure of
\sigma
written in weakly increasing order by
\alpha
. First, the assumption
f\geq\sqrt{n}
ensures that
f\geq G(\sigma)
, i.e. that
M(\sigma)=f
. Also, we have

G(\sigma)=\max_{i\geq 2}f_{i}(\sigma)^{1/i}\leq\max_{i\geq 2}f_{i}(\sigma)^{1/2}\leq k^{1/2}.

(2.24)

Therefore by Lemma
2.11
we have

\sum_{K=2}^{r}|S^{\prime}(\lambda,\alpha,K)|\lesssim_{r}\sum_{K=2}^{r}f^{r-K}G(\sigma)^{K}=\sum_{K=2}^{r}f^{r}\left(\frac{k^{1/2}}{f}\right)^{K}\lesssim_{r}f^{r}\left(\frac{k^{1/2}}{f}\right)^{2}=f^{r}\frac{k}{f^{2}}.

(2.25)

Moreover, since
f\geq 2r
,
{\textup{RT}}(\lambda,\alpha)
is the disjoint union of the
S^{\prime}(\lambda,\alpha,K)
for
K\in\left\{0,2,3,\ldots,r\right\}
, and by Lemma
2.11
again, the
d_{(\lambda_{1}-k,\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3},\ldots)}
elements of
S^{\prime}(\lambda,\alpha,0)
have height 0, so

\sum_{T\in S^{\prime}(\lambda,\alpha,0)}(-1)^{0pt(T)}=d_{(\lambda_{1}-k,\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3},\ldots)},

(2.26)

and by the triangle inequality and recalling that
d_{\mu}\asymp_{r}f^{r}
we have

\left|\sum_{K=2}^{r}\sum_{T\in S^{\prime}(\lambda,\alpha,K)}(-1)^{0pt(T)}\right|\leq\sum_{K=2}^{r}|S^{\prime}(\lambda,\alpha,K)|\lesssim_{r}f^{r}\frac{k}{f^{2}}\lesssim_{r}d_{(\lambda_{1}-k,\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3},\ldots)}\frac{k}{f^{2}}.

(2.27)

We deduce from the Murnaghan–Nakayama rule that

\begin{split}\textup{ch}^{\lambda}(\sigma)&=\sum_{T\in S^{\prime}(\lambda,\alpha,0)}(-1)^{0pt(T)}+\sum_{K=2}^{r}\sum_{T\in S^{\prime}(\lambda,\alpha,K)}(-1)^{0pt(T)}\\
&=d_{(\lambda_{1}-k,\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3},\ldots)}+d_{(\lambda_{1}-k,\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3},\ldots)}O_{r}\left(\frac{k}{f^{2}}\right).\end{split}

(2.28)

Dividing both sides by
d_{\lambda}
gives

\chi^{\lambda}(\sigma)=\frac{d_{(\lambda_{1}-k,\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3},\ldots)}}{d_{\lambda}}\left(1+O_{r}\left(\frac{k}{f^{2}}\right)\right).

(2.29)

The result follows, since
\frac{d_{(\lambda_{1}-k,\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3},\ldots)}}{d_{\lambda}}=\left(\frac{f}{n}\right)^{r}\left(1+O_{r}\left(\frac{k}{f^{2}}\right)\right)
by the second part of Lemma
2.9
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
2.3
.

Denote
f=f_{1}(\sigma_{n})
and
k=n-f
. By Theorem
2.2
, we have

\chi^{\lambda}(\sigma)=(f/n)^{r}(1+O_{r}(k/f^{2}))=(f/n)^{r}e^{O_{r}(k/f^{2})}=(f/n)^{r+O_{r}\left(\frac{k}{f^{2}\ln(n/f)}\right)}.

(2.30)

If
f\leq n^{2/3}
then
\frac{k}{f^{2}\ln(n/f)}\asymp\frac{n}{f^{2}\ln n}=o(1/\ln n)
since
f/\sqrt{n}\to\infty
by assumption. If
f>n^{2/3}
then, using that
\ln(n/f)=-\ln(1-k/n)\geq k/n
, we have
\frac{k}{f^{2}\ln(n/f)}\leq\frac{n}{f^{2}}\leq\frac{n}{n^{4/3}}=o(1/\ln n)
.
This concludes the proof.
∎

Proof of Corollary
2.4
.

By Lemma
2.7
we have
d_{\lambda}\sim\frac{n^{r}}{r!}d_{\lambda^{*}}
. By Theorem
2.3
, we have

\chi^{\lambda}(\sigma)^{\frac{(\ln n)+c+o(1)}{\ln(n/f_{1}(\sigma))}}=e^{-r((\ln n)+c+o(1))}\sim n^{-r}e^{-rc}.

(2.31)

Multiplying the two estimates gives the desired result.
∎

3.1
Generalized approximation and comparison lemmas

The approximation lemma
[
Tey20
, Lemma 2.1]
enabled filtering
low frequencies
to obtain the profile for transpositions, and the comparison lemma for cutoff profiles
[
Nes24
, Lemma 1.4]
enabled obtaining the profile of a (sequence of) irreducible Markov chain from another one, given that they have similar spectral properties.
In this paper we want to apply the comparison of profiles to chains may be periodic. These variants also work for time-inhomogeneous chains, for example if we multiply by permutations uniform a different conjugacy class at each step. This naturally appears for some models of random maps, which are coded by a product of a fixed point free permutation and another permutation, see for instance
[
Gam06
,
BCP19
]
.

We therefore have to prove a variant of these lemmas. Note however that this section does not contain any new argument, and is here only for completeness.

In what follows the set of all irreducible representations of a finite group
G
is denoted by
\widehat{G}
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
G
be a finite group. Let
H
be a subset of
G
,
A\subset B\subset\widehat{G}
, and let
(u_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in\widehat{G}}
be a family of complex numbers. Then

\left|\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{g\in H}\left|\sum_{\alpha\in B}d_{\alpha}u_{\alpha}\overline{\textup{ch}^{\alpha}(g)}\right|-\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{g\in H}\left|\sum_{\alpha\in A}d_{\alpha}u_{\alpha}\overline{\textup{ch}^{\alpha}(g)}\right|\right|\leq\sum_{\alpha\in{B\backslash A}}d_{\alpha}|u_{\alpha}|.

(3.1)

Proof.

By triangle inequalities, the left hand side of (
3.1
) is upper bounded by

\begin{split}\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{g\in H}\sum_{\alpha\in B\backslash A}d_{\alpha}|u_{\alpha}|\left|\textup{ch}^{\alpha}(g)\right|=\sum_{\alpha\in B\backslash A}d_{\alpha}|u_{\alpha}|\left(\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{g\in H}\left|\textup{ch}^{\alpha}(g)\right|\right).\end{split}

(3.2)

This concludes the proof, since by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality and orthonormality of characters we have

\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{g\in H}\left|\textup{ch}^{\alpha}(g)\right|\leq\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{g\in G}\left|\textup{ch}^{\alpha}(g)\right|\leq\frac{1}{|G|}\sqrt{|G|\sum_{g\in G}\left|\textup{ch}^{\alpha}(g)\right|^{2}}=\frac{1}{|G|}\sqrt{|G|\cdot|G|}=1.\qed

(3.3)

Taking
A=\emptyset
in Lemma
3.1
, we obtain the following.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
G
be a finite group. Let
H\subset G
,
B\subset\widehat{G}
, and
(u_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in\widehat{G}}
be a family of complex numbers.
Then

\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{g\in H}\left|\sum_{\alpha\in B}d_{\alpha}u_{\alpha}\overline{\textup{ch}^{\alpha}(g)}\right|\leq\sum_{\alpha\in B}d_{\alpha}|u_{\alpha}|.

(3.4)

Now let us compare these quantities for different families
(u_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in\widehat{G}}
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
G
be a finite group. Let
H\subset G
,
B\subset\widehat{G}
, and
(u_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in\widehat{G}},(v_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in\widehat{G}}
be two families of complex numbers.
Then

\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{g\in H}\left|\left|\sum_{\alpha\in B}d_{\alpha}u_{\alpha}\overline{\textup{ch}^{\alpha}(g)}\right|-\left|\sum_{\alpha\in B}d_{\alpha}v_{\alpha}\overline{\textup{ch}^{\alpha}(g)}\right|\right|\leq\sum_{\alpha\in B}d_{\alpha}|u_{\alpha}-v_{\alpha}|.

(3.5)

Proof.

By the triangle inequality, the left hand side of (
3.5
) is upper bounded by

\begin{split}\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{g\in H}\left|\sum_{\alpha\in B}d_{\alpha}u_{\alpha}\overline{\textup{ch}^{\alpha}(g)}-\sum_{\alpha\in B}d_{\alpha}v_{\alpha}\overline{\textup{ch}^{\alpha}(g)}\right|=\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{g\in H}\left|\sum_{\alpha\in B}d_{\alpha}(u_{\alpha}-v_{\alpha})\overline{\textup{ch}^{\alpha}(g)}\right|\end{split}

(3.6)

Applying Lemma
3.2
to the family

concludes the proof.
∎

3.2
Random transpositions in coset distance

In the random transposition (RT) model on
{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
introduced by Diaconis and Shahshahani
[
DS81
]
, the two hands pick cards independently, so the probability that they are the same is
1/n
, and the model has inherent laziness
1/n
, which makes the walk aperiodic. This walk is driven by the measure
\mu_{{\textup{RT}}}=\frac{1}{n}\delta_{\textup{Id}}+\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right)\textup{Unif}_{{\mathcal{T}}}
, where we recall that
{\mathcal{T}}={\mathcal{T}}^{(n)}\subset{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
is the conjugacy class of transpositions.

The pure transposition walk (pRT) is the walk driven by
\mu_{{\textup{pRT}}}=\textup{Unif}_{{\mathcal{T}}}
. This corresponds to taking at each step two distinct cards and swapping them.

The eigenvalues of pRT are exactly the characters
\chi^{\lambda}({\mathcal{T}})
(for
\lambda\vdash n
), and therefore have good symmetry properties: we have
\chi^{\lambda^{\prime}}({\mathcal{T}})=-\chi^{\lambda}({\mathcal{T}})
, where we
\lambda^{\prime}
is the conjugate diagram of
\lambda
.
On the other hand, the eigenvalues of RT are
\frac{1}{n}+\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right)\chi^{\lambda}(\tau)
, and laziness then removes some symmetry for the eigenvalues. While it affects the eigenvalues that are close to 1 (that is, for
\lambda
such that
\lambda_{1}=n-O(1)
) only by
O(1/n^{2})
, which is negligible, it affects the eigenvalues close to
-1
(that is, for
\lambda
such that
\lambda_{1}^{\prime}=n-O(1)
) by
2/n+O(1/n^{2})
, which makes all these representations asymptotically negligible close to the mixing time in the distance to stationarity.

The two models however have the same asymptotic behaviour. The number of fixed points of a permutation taken uniformly in either
{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
, or
{\mathfrak{A}}_{n}
, or
{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}\backslash{\mathfrak{A}}_{n}
, is asymptotically distributed as
\textup{Poiss}(1)
. On a computational level, if
\lambda
is such that
\lambda_{1}=n-O(1)
, the contribution of
\lambda
for RT is (asymptotically) the same as the contribution of
\lambda
and
\lambda^{\prime}
combined for pRT, and
\lambda^{\prime}
doesn’t contribute to RT. All other estimates are exactly the same. Making these minor adaptations in the proof of
[
Tey20
]
immediately gives the following for pRT.

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
a\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Let
(t_{n})
be a sequence of integers such that
t_{n}=\frac{1}{2}n(\ln n+a+o(1))
. Then as
n\to\infty
, we have

\textup{d}_{\textup{TV}}(\textup{Unif}_{{\mathcal{T}}^{(n)}}^{*t_{n}},\textup{Unif}_{{\mathfrak{E}}({{\mathcal{T}}^{(n)}},t_{n})})\to\textup{d}_{\textup{TV}}\left(\textup{Poiss}\left(1+e^{-a}\right),\textup{Poiss}(1)\right).

(3.7)

4.1.4
Cutoff and cutoff profile for random involutions

The goal of this section is to prove Theorem
1.2
. In this section we omit the dependence on
p\in(0,1)
from asymptotic notation, and write for example
O(\cdot)
for
O_{p}(\cdot)
.

4.1.5
Some intuition

Let
p\in(0,1)
. Let
n
be a large even integer. Recall from (
1.5
) that the random involution walk on
{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
with parameter
p
is the walk driven by the measure

\textup{RI}_{n,p}=\sum_{s=0}^{n/2}\binom{n/2}{s}(1-p)^{s}p^{n/2-s}\textup{Inv}_{n,s},

(4.18)

where
\textup{Inv}_{n,s}
is the uniform measure on permutations of
{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
with cycle type
(2^{s},1^{n-2s})
.
By definition this is a class function, and the resulting Markov chain is aperiodic.

Let us first present some intuition for this model. When one picks a permutation according to
\textup{RI}_{n,p}
, one picks a conjugacy invariant permutation
\sigma
whose support
{\textup{supp}}(\sigma)
follows approximately a normal law of average
(1-p)n
and standard deviation
\Theta(\sqrt{n})
, so
{\textup{supp}}(\sigma)
is
very
concentrated around
(1-p)n
. To prove a cutoff it would be enough that typically
{\textup{supp}}(\sigma)=(1-p)n(1+o(1))
, and to prove the profile we need that typically
{\textup{supp}}(\sigma)=(1-p)n(1+o(1/\ln n))
.

To write this rigorously we couple random involutions with a truncated version of random involutions, for which the support is
always
in an interval around
(1-p)n
, and then estimate the eigenvalues by averaging, which gives essentially the same estimates as for conjugacy classes.

4.1.6
Coupling with truncated random involutions (TRI)

First we define a truncated binomial distribution. Denote the probability measure
\textup{Bin}(n/2,1-p)
by
\varphi
, set

m=n^{4/5},

(4.19)

and let

I=I_{n,p}=[(1-p)n/2-m,(1-p)n/2+m]\cap{\mathbb{Z}}.

(4.20)

For
k\in I
, set

\varphi_{\textup{trunc}}(k)=\frac{\varphi(k)}{\varphi(I)}.

(4.21)

In words,
\varphi_{\textup{trunc}}
is a rescaled truncation of
\varphi
on a small interval of size
\asymp m=n^{4/5}
close to its average.
We have
\mathrm{Var}(\varphi)\asymp n
so
1-\varphi(I)=O(n/m^{2})=O(n^{-3/5})
by the Bienaymé–Chebyshev inequality. (Much better bounds can be proved but this is not necessary here.)
It follows that

\textup{d}_{\textup{TV}}(\varphi,\varphi_{\textup{trunc}})=O(n^{-3/5}).

(4.22)

Let
M\sim\varphi
and
N\sim\varphi_{\textup{trunc}}
. Let
\sigma
(resp.
\tau
) be a uniform involution with support
2M
(resp.
2N
). The law of
\sigma
is
\textup{RI}_{n,p}
, and the law of
\tau
is

\textup{TRI}_{n,p}=\frac{1}{\varphi(I_{n,p})}\sum_{s\in I_{n,p}}\binom{n/2}{s}(1-p)^{s}p^{n/2-s}\textup{Inv}_{n,s},

(4.23)

By construction, we have
\textup{d}_{\textup{TV}}(\textup{RI}_{n,p},\textup{TRI}_{n,p})\leq\textup{d}_{\textup{TV}}(\varphi,\varphi_{\textup{trunc}})
, so using (
4.22
) we obtain

\textup{d}_{\textup{TV}}(\textup{RI}_{n,p},\textup{TRI}_{n,p})=O(n^{-3/5}).

(4.24)

A union bound immediately implies the following.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
p\in(0,1)
. As
n\to\infty
, uniformly over all
0\leq t\leq 10\log_{1/p}n
, we have

\textup{d}_{\textup{TV}}(\textup{RI}_{n,p}^{*t},\textup{TRI}_{n,p}^{*t})=O\left(\frac{\ln n}{n^{3/5}}\right)=o(1).

(4.25)

As a result, the asymptotics for the total variation distance to stationarity for
\textup{RI}_{n,p}
and
\textup{TRI}_{n,p}
are the same, and we only need to study the second one.

4.1.7
Proof of Theorem

As we will see, the proof of Theorem

), as done in

Proof of Theorem

Let
(s_{n})_{n\in S}
be a sequence of integers such that
s_{n}=\frac{1}{2}n((\ln n)+a+o(1))
.

By conjugacy invariance, the eigenvalues of the
\textup{TRI}_{n,p}
-walk are for
\lambda\vdash n
,

\alpha_{\lambda}:={\mathbb{E}}_{\tau\sim\textup{TRI}_{n,p}}\left[\chi^{\lambda}(\tau)\right]=\frac{1}{\varphi(I_{n,p})}\sum_{s\in I_{n,p}}\binom{n/2}{s}(1-p)^{s}p^{n/2-s}\chi^{\lambda}(2^{s},1^{n-2s}).

(4.26)

Let
{\textup{T}}_{n}=\frac{1}{n}+\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right)\textup{Unif}_{{\mathcal{T}}_{n}}
be the driving measure of the transposition walk (with laziness
1/n
). Denote the eigenvalues of the
{\textup{T}}_{n}
-walk by, for
\lambda\vdash n
,
\beta_{\lambda}:=\frac{1}{n}+\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right)\chi^{\lambda}(2,1^{n-2})
.

By Lemma
4.3
and applying Lemma
3.3
with

B=\widehat{{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}}^{*}=\widehat{{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}}\backslash\left\{[n]\right\}
,
u_{\lambda}=\alpha_{\lambda}^{t_{n}}
, and
v_{\lambda}=\beta_{\lambda}^{s_{n}}
, we obtain

\begin{split}&2\left|\textup{d}_{\textup{TV}}(\textup{RI}_{n,p}^{*t_{n}},\textup{Unif}_{{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}})-\textup{d}_{\textup{TV}}({\textup{T}}_{n}^{*s_{n}},\textup{Unif}_{{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}})\right|+o(1)\\
=\;&2\left|\textup{d}_{\textup{TV}}(\textup{TRI}_{n,p}^{*t_{n}},\textup{Unif}_{{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}})-\textup{d}_{\textup{TV}}({\textup{T}}_{n}^{*s_{n}},\textup{Unif}_{{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}})\right|\leq\sum_{\lambda\in\widehat{{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}}^{*}}d_{\lambda}\left|\alpha_{\lambda}^{t_{n}}-\beta_{\lambda}^{s_{n}}\right|.\end{split}

(4.27)

Let
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, and let
R\geq 1
be an integer.
By
[
OTTT25
, Theorem 5.8]
, we have

d_{\lambda}\left|\alpha_{\lambda}\right|^{t_{n}}\leq\max_{\sigma\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}{\;:\;}|f_{1}(\sigma)-pn|\leq m}d_{\lambda}\left|\chi^{\lambda}(\sigma)\right|^{t_{n}}\leq d_{\lambda}^{(a-\zeta(R)+o(1))/\ln n},

(4.28)

so we obtain, exactly as in the proof of Theorem
1.1
, that

\sum_{\lambda\in\widehat{{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}}^{*}{\;:\;}r_{\textup{sym}}(\lambda)>R}d_{\lambda}\left|\alpha_{\lambda}\right|^{t_{n}}\leq\sum_{\lambda\in\widehat{{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}}^{*}{\;:\;}r_{\textup{sym}}(\lambda)>R}d_{\lambda}^{(a-\zeta(R)+o(1))/\ln n}.

(4.29)

Therefore, by the triangle inequality and using also
[
Tey20
, Lemma 4.1]
to bound the sum for lazy transpositions, there exists
R_{0}
such that for all
n
large enough we have

\begin{split}&\sum_{\lambda\in\widehat{{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}}^{*}{\;:\;}r_{\textup{sym}}(\lambda)>R_{0}}d_{\lambda}\left|\alpha_{\lambda}^{t_{n}}-\beta_{\lambda}^{s_{n}}\right|\\
\leq\;&\sum_{\lambda\in\widehat{{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}}^{*}{\;:\;}r_{\textup{sym}}(\lambda)>R_{0}}d_{\lambda}\left|\alpha_{\lambda}\right|^{t_{n}}+\sum_{\lambda\in\widehat{{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}}^{*}{\;:\;}r_{\textup{sym}}(\lambda)>R_{0}}d_{\lambda}\left|\beta_{\lambda}\right|^{s_{n}}\\
\leq\;&\varepsilon.\end{split}

(4.30)

Now denote

A^{\prime}(R_{0}):=\left\{\lambda\in\widehat{{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}}\mid 1\leq r(\lambda)\leq R_{0}\right\}=\left\{\lambda\in\widehat{{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}}\mid 1\leq n-\lambda_{1}\leq R_{0}\right\},

(4.31)

and

A^{\prime\prime}(R_{0}):=\left\{\lambda\in\widehat{{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}}\mid 1\leq r(\lambda)\leq R_{0}\right\}=\left\{\lambda\in\widehat{{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}}\mid 0\leq n-\lambda_{1}^{\prime}\leq R_{0}\right\}.

(4.32)

Averaging Theorem
2.2
we obtain that uniformly over
\lambda\in A^{\prime}(R_{0})
, we have

\alpha_{\lambda}\sim p^{r(\lambda)},

(4.33)

and uniformly over
\lambda\in A^{\prime\prime}(R_{0})
, we have

\alpha_{\lambda}=(1/2+o(1))p^{r(\lambda)}-(1/2+o(1))p^{r(\lambda)}=o(1),

(4.34)

since a permutation picked according to
\textup{TRI}_{n,p}
has probability
1/2+o(1)
to be even so there are sign compensations (see Remark
2.4
).
It is also known from
[
Tey20
]
that
\sum_{\lambda\in A^{\prime\prime}(R_{0})}d_{\lambda}\beta_{\lambda}^{s_{n}}=o(1)
.
We deduce that

\sum_{\lambda\in A^{\prime\prime}(R_{0})}d_{\lambda}\left|\alpha_{\lambda}^{t_{n}}-\beta_{\lambda}^{s_{n}}\right|=o(1)

(4.35)

and

\begin{split}\sum_{\lambda\in A^{\prime}(R_{0})}d_{\lambda}\left|\alpha_{\lambda}^{t_{n}}-\beta_{\lambda}^{s_{n}}\right|&=\sum_{\lambda\in A^{\prime}(R_{0})}d_{\lambda}\left|\frac{e^{-ar+o(1)}}{n^{r}}-\frac{e^{-ar+o(1)}}{n^{r}}\right|\\
&=\sum_{\lambda\in A^{\prime}(R_{0})}d_{\lambda}o(n^{-r})=o(1).\end{split}

(4.36)

Putting everything together, and since
\varepsilon
was arbitrary, we obtain

\left|\textup{d}_{\textup{TV}}(\textup{RI}_{n,p}^{*t_{n}},\textup{Unif}_{{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}})-\textup{d}_{\textup{TV}}({\textup{T}}_{n}^{*s_{n}},\textup{Unif}_{{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}})\right|=o(1).

(4.37)

Plugging
[
Tey20
, Theorem 1.1]
into this concludes the proof.
∎

5
Numerics for transpositions on a deck of 52 cards

Let
n\geq 2
and
t\geq 0
. For
r\geq 1
, denote

M_{r}^{(n)}(t)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{E}({\mathcal{T}},t)}\left|\sum_{\lambda\in\widehat{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}^{**}{\;:\;}1\leq r_{\textup{sym}}(\lambda)\leq r}\frac{d_{\lambda}}{n!}\chi^{\lambda}({\mathcal{T}})^{t}\textup{ch}^{\lambda}(\sigma)\right|.

(5.1)

and

E_{r}^{(n)}(t)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\lambda\in\widehat{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}^{**}{\;:\;}r_{\textup{sym}}(\lambda)=r}d_{\lambda}\left|\chi^{\lambda}({\mathcal{T}})\right|^{t}.

(5.2)

Denote also
\textup{d}^{(n)}(t)=\textup{d}_{\textup{TV}}(\textup{Unif}_{{\mathcal{T}}}^{*t},\textup{Unif}_{\mathfrak{E}({\mathcal{T}},t)})
.
Then by (
4.2
) and Lemma
3.1
, we have

\left|\textup{d}^{(n)}(t)-M_{1}^{(n)}(t)\right|\leq\sum_{r\geq 2}E_{r}^{(n)}(t).

(5.3)

We will first bound the error terms
\sum_{r\geq 2}E_{r}^{(n)}(t)
and estimate the main term
M_{1}^{(n)}(t)
for any value of
n
and
t
, and then provide numerics for
n=52
.

5.1
Bound on the contribution of most representations

We will use the following classical eigenvalue estimate.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
n\geq 2
be even. Let
\lambda\vdash n
. The following holds.

(a)

If
1\leq r_{\textup{sym}}(\lambda)\leq n/2
, then
|\chi^{\lambda}({\mathcal{T}})|\leq 1-\frac{2r_{\textup{sym}}(\lambda)(n-r_{\textup{sym}}(\lambda)+1)}{n(n-1)}
.

(b)

If
r_{\textup{sym}}(\lambda)\geq n/2
, then
|\chi^{\lambda}({\mathcal{T}})|\leq 1/2
.

Proof.

This follows from
[
Dia88b
, Lemma 3.D.2]
and symmetry. (We emphasize that in
[
Dia88b
]
, the notation
r(\lambda)
means
\chi^{\lambda}({\mathcal{T}})
, while for us
r(\lambda)=n-\lambda_{1}
.)
∎

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
n\geq 2
. Let
r\geq 1
. We have

\sum_{\lambda\vdash n{\;:\;}r(\lambda)=r}d_{\lambda}\leq n^{r}.

(5.4)

Proof.

For
\lambda\vdash n
such that
r(\lambda)=r
, we have
d_{\lambda}\leq\binom{n}{r}d_{\lambda^{*}}\leq n^{r}\frac{d_{\lambda^{*}}}{r!}\leq n^{r}\frac{(d_{\lambda^{*}})^{2}}{r!}
. The result then follows from the identity
\sum_{\mu\vdash r}d_{\mu}^{2}=r!
.
∎

Denote by
p(n)
the number of integer partitions of
n
.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
n\geq 2
. For any
t\geq 0
, we have

\sum_{r\geq 2}E_{r}^{(n)}(t)\leq\left(\sum_{r=2}^{\left\lfloor(n-1)/2\right\rfloor}n^{r}\left(1-\frac{2r(n-r+1)}{n(n-1)}\right)^{t}\right)+\frac{\sqrt{p(n)n!}}{2^{t+1}}.

(5.5)

Proof.

By symmetry, Lemma
5.2
, and Lemma
5.1
(a), we have

\begin{split}\sum_{r=2}^{\left\lfloor(n-1)/2\right\rfloor}E_{r}^{(n)}(t)&\leq\sum_{r=2}^{\left\lfloor(n-1)/2\right\rfloor}n^{r}\left(1-\frac{2r(n-r+1)}{n(n-1)}\right)^{t}.\end{split}

(5.6)

By Lemma
5.1
(b), the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, and using that
\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}d_{\lambda}^{2}=n!
, we obtain

\frac{1}{2}\sum_{r\geq\left\lfloor(n+1)/2\right\rfloor}\sum_{\lambda\vdash n{\;:\;}r_{\textup{sym}}(\lambda)=r}d_{\lambda}|\chi^{\lambda}({\mathcal{T}})|^{t}\leq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}d_{\lambda}(1/2)^{t}\leq(1/2)^{t+1}\sqrt{p(n)n!}.

(5.7)

The result follows.
∎

5.2
Contribution of representations with (symmetrized) level 1

Let us now study the contribution of the representations
\lambda
with
r_{\textup{sym}}(\lambda)=1
. For these representations, we have to make an estimate which is both a lower and an upper bound.

We first show that the probability that a random permutation picked in
{\mathfrak{A}}_{n}
or
{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}\backslash{\mathfrak{A}}_{n}
has no fixed point is extremely close to
1/e
.

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
n\geq 2
. Let
X_{n}^{\textup{even}}\sim\textup{Unif}_{{\mathfrak{A}}_{n}}
and
X_{n}^{\textup{odd}}\sim\textup{Unif}_{{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}\backslash{\mathfrak{A}}_{n}}
. Then

\left|{\mathbb{P}}(f_{1}(X_{n}^{\textup{even}})=0)-1/e\right|\leq\frac{1}{(n-1)!}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ and }\hskip 10.00002pt\left|{\mathbb{P}}(f_{1}(X_{n}^{\textup{odd}})=0)-1/e\right|\leq\frac{1}{(n-1)!}.

(5.8)

Proof.

Denote the number of derangements (i.e. fixed point free permutations) of
{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
by
D_{n}
. Denote the number of even and odd derangements of
{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
(i.e. of derangements that are in
{\mathfrak{A}}_{n}
or
{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}\backslash{\mathfrak{A}}_{n}
) by
D_{n}^{\textup{even}}
and
D_{n}^{\textup{odd}}
.
It is standard that

D_{n}^{\textup{even}}+D_{n}^{\textup{odd}}=D_{n}=n!\sum_{i=0}^{n}\frac{(-1)^{i}}{i!}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ and }\hskip 10.00002ptD_{n}^{\textup{even}}-D_{n}^{\textup{odd}}=(-1)^{n-1}(n-1).

(5.9)

(
D_{n}^{\textup{even}}-D_{n}^{\textup{odd}}
is the determinant of the
n
by
n
matrix with 0’s on the diagonal and 1’s elsewhere.)
Hence we have

{\mathbb{P}}(f_{1}(X_{n}^{\textup{even}})=0)=\frac{2}{n!}D_{n}^{\textup{even}}=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\frac{(-1)^{i}}{i!}+\frac{(-1)^{n-1}(n-1)}{n!}

(5.10)

and therefore

\left|{\mathbb{P}}(f_{1}(X_{n}^{\textup{even}})=0)-1/e\right|=\left|{\mathbb{P}}(f_{1}(X_{n}^{\textup{even}})=0)-\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{i}}{i!}\right|\leq\left|\sum_{i=n+1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{i}}{i!}\right|+\frac{(n-1)}{n!},

(5.11)

so we finally obtain

\left|{\mathbb{P}}(f_{1}(X_{n}^{\textup{even}})=0)-1/e\right|\leq\frac{1}{(n+1)!}+\frac{(n-1)(n+1)}{(n+1)!}=\frac{n^{2}}{(n+1)!}\leq\frac{1}{(n-1)!}.

(5.12)

The same arguments also give
{\mathbb{P}}(f_{1}(X_{n}^{\textup{odd}})=0)\leq 1/(n-1)!
.
∎

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
n\geq 3
and
t\geq 0
. Then

\left|M_{1}^{(n)}(t)-\frac{(n-1)}{e}\left(\frac{n-3}{n-1}\right)^{t}\right|\leq\frac{1}{(n-2)!}.

(5.13)

Proof.

For
\sigma\in{\mathfrak{E}}({\mathcal{T}},t)
and any
\lambda\in\widehat{{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}}
, we have
\chi^{\lambda^{\prime}}({\mathcal{T}})^{t}\textup{ch}^{\lambda^{\prime}}(\sigma)=\chi^{\lambda}({\mathcal{T}})^{t}\textup{ch}^{\lambda}(\sigma)
and
d_{\lambda^{\prime}}=d_{\lambda}
. Therefore the representations
(n-1,1)
and
(2,1^{n-2})
have the same contribution, which cancels the factor
1/2
. Using that
d_{(n-1,1)}=n-1
and
\textup{ch}^{(n-1,1)}(\sigma)=f_{1}(\sigma)-1
for any
\sigma
, we deduce that

\begin{split}M_{1}^{(n)}(t)=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{E}({\mathcal{T}},t)}\left|\frac{(n-1)}{n!}\chi^{\lambda}({\mathcal{T}})^{t}(f_{1}(\sigma)-1)\right|=\frac{n-1}{2}|\chi^{\lambda}({\mathcal{T}})|^{t}\frac{2}{n!}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{E}({\mathcal{T}},t)}|f_{1}(\sigma)-1|.\end{split}

(5.14)

Moreover, we have, letting
X\sim\textup{Unif}_{\mathfrak{E}({\mathcal{T}},t)}
, we have
{\mathbb{E}}[f_{1}(X)]=1
(this uses that
n\geq 3
) so

\frac{2}{n!}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{E}({\mathcal{T}},t)}|f_{1}(\sigma)-1|=2{\mathbb{P}}(f_{1}(X)=0).

(5.15)

We therefore obtain

\left|M_{1}^{(n)}(t)-\frac{(n-1)}{e}\left|\chi^{\lambda}({\mathcal{T}})\right|^{t}\right|=(n-1)\left|\chi^{\lambda}({\mathcal{T}})\right|^{t}\left|{\mathbb{P}}(f_{1}(X)=0)-1/e\right|.

(5.16)

Applying Lemma
5.4
and using that
\left|\chi^{\lambda}({\mathcal{T}})\right|=\frac{n-3}{n-1}\leq 1
concludes the proof.
∎

5.3
General approximation

Proposition 5.6
.

Let
n\geq 3
and
t\geq 0
. Then

\left|\textup{d}^{(n)}(t)-\frac{(n-1)}{e}\left(\frac{n-3}{n-1}\right)^{t}\right|\leq\varepsilon^{(n)}(t),

(5.17)

where

\varepsilon^{(n)}(t)=\left(\sum_{r=2}^{\left\lfloor(n-1)/2\right\rfloor}n^{r}\left(1-\frac{2r(n-r+1)}{n(n-1)}\right)^{t}\right)+\frac{\sqrt{p(n)n!}}{2^{t+1}}+\frac{1}{(n-2)!}.

(5.18)

Proof.

This follows from (
5.3
), Lemmas
5.3
and
5.5
, and the triangle inequality.
∎

Lemma 5.9
.

Let

(a)
