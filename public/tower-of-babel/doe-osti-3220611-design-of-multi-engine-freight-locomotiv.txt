EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
GTI Energy advances practical, low‑emission technologies for the US transportation sector. In
collaboration with OptiFuel Systems, GTI Energy completed the full technical design of a
modular, hybrid freight locomotive fueled by renewable natural gas. This design is capable of
achieving near‑zero emissions while meeting the stringent performance requirements of
long‑haul rail service.
The design of the locomotive integrates ten natural gas engines operating in parallel to deliver
continuous power suitable for duty cycles required by the industry and is capable of meeting US
Environmental Protection Agency emission requirements. The advanced powertrain enables
clean, fast engine starts, zero‑emission maintenance shop moves, and regenerative braking, thus
ensuring cross-country regulatory compliance. Onboard renewable natural gas storage is
configured for safety, serviceability, and adequate range.
The project successfully completed systems engineering and component selection, as well as
plans for the locomotive production and testing. The starter generator system progressed
through design and environmental testing. Component vendors across propulsion, traction,
energy storage, and controls specializations were selected, establishing the supply chain
foundation. A full‑scale engine module mockup was fabricated and publicly demonstrated.
Although the technical development met its objectives, the project did not move into the
production and test phases, as the designated engine supplier withdrew its specific engine from
the application. However, the completed work provides a comprehensive, validated blueprint for
next‑generation natural gas locomotive platforms. It establishes a robust technical basis for
future rail innovation efforts across Class I, II, and III operators, demonstrating that modular
multi‑engine hybrid architectures can simultaneously achieve operational cost savings,
performance, streamlined maintenance, and deep emissions reductions.

INTRODUCTION
The Department of Energy (DOE) has long encouraged wider use of alternative fuels in the rail
industry, leading locomotive manufacturers to focus first on engine improvements.

Background
In the last decade, rail equipment manufacturers have been introducing natural gas (NG)
solutions for multi-engine rail service to meet federal-based Tier 3 emission standards. Such
hybrid locomotives were thought to be particularly effective for the needs of the freight
industry, combining a train’s propulsion system with its onboard energy storage and allowing it
to recover braking energy, assist the engine during high-load conditions, and operate more
efficiently. While these initial steps did not immediately lead to transformative operational cost
savings or widespread industry adoption, they provided valuable experience and opportunities
for further innovation.
To continue the effort, GTI Energy, in collaboration with OptiFuel Systems, LLC, and partners in
the rail industry, set out to conduct a field demonstration of a multi-engine 4,300 horsepower,
Tier 5-compliant hybrid line-haul locomotive. This commercially viable locomotive would be
capable of operating on renewable natural gas (RNG) for the following purposes:
• Lower emissions — nitrogen oxides (NOx), particulate matter (PM), carbon monoxide (CO),
 hydrocarbons (HC)
• Reduced fuel consumption
• Improved efficiency on grades and stop-and-go routes
• Less brake wear due to regenerative braking
As one of the project’s goals was to reduce emissions, targets were set relative to the current
and proposed standards at the onset of the project (Table 1) and according to the Code of
Federal Regulations (1).

Project objectives
The main goal of this project was to develop and demonstrate a near-zero, Tier 5-compliant
RNG hybrid line-haul locomotive using commercially available technologies and a multi-engine
hybrid architecture. The project focused on the following technical objectives:
 1. Demonstrate that commercially available and reliable components can be integrated into
 a cost-effective, near zero emission, Tier 5 capable locomotive platform — validating that
 existing engine, traction, electrical, and fuel storage systems could meet performance
 and regulatory requirements.

 Page 7

 2. Demonstrate the application of the on-road, 100% RNG, near-zero emission engine in
 off-road markets — confirming that the engine could be adapted for rail and other
 heavy duty applications.
 3. Quantify potential improvements in system energy efficiency and fuel consumption
 enabled by a multi-engine hybrid architecture — including expected 20-40% fuel
 consumption reductions through using the latest engine technology, optimized engine
 loading, and regenerative braking.
 4. Demonstrate fuel cost, criteria pollutant, and greenhouse gas emission reductions for
 Class I, II, and III railroads operating CNG and RNG locomotives — requiring field
 validation under representative duty cycles.*
 5. Collect operational durability and reliability data in freight rail service — supporting long
 term adoption, maintenance planning, and commercialization.*
Note* With the unexpected withdrawal of the engine supplier, Cummins, the engine was no
longer available, ending the project before the Transportation Technology Center at the US
Department of Transportation could conduct testing and field demonstration. Only Objectives
1–3 are covered in this report; Objectives 4 and 5 could not be met due to the shortened
timeline.

TECHNICAL APPROACH
The locomotive design describes a modular quick-disconnect platform built on an SD90-class
frame. It features two SD70 radial‑steering trucks and integrates all major propulsion and energy
subsystems, including traction inverters, traction motor and gearbox combos, modular power
control, fuel storage, and battery.
Propulsion is provided by ten engines and their paired starter generators, forming the
distributed power that feeds the volts direct current (VDC) link and enables traction horsepower
(hp) with continuous engine output. Batteries and fuel storage are placed above and below
deck, and the locomotive is ready for an optional use of an external RNG/H₂ tender for extended
range missions.
To support system reliability and maintainability, the locomotive uses International Organization
for Standardization (ISO) corner modular construction across all major components, allowing
forklift‑level removal and replacement. A deck‑integrated clean‑air plenum provides filtered air
to traction motors, power controls, electronics, and battery chillers, while centralized wiring
tunnels reduce heavy‑cable length and simplify integration. Altogether, these components are
configured as in Figure 2.

To support rapid installation, removal, and secure interconnection of all large subsystems, the
platform employs ISO corner‑lock interfaces at every major module station. This approach
standardizes the way engine pods, RNG storage units, the battery system, and power
control/electronics packages are attached to the deck, enabling quick‑disconnect handling
during assembly and maintenance while also allowing modules to lock into adjacent structures
for stability and crash survivability when the consist is in service.

Propulsion system
The propulsion system consists of ten modular engine — generator power units that convert
RNG fuel into electrical power for traction and auxiliary loads. Each unit integrates a Cummins
X15N natural gas engine, a water‑cooled permanent‑magnet starter/generator, and a dedicated
thermal‑management package into a single ISO corner quick‑disconnect module. This modular
propulsion architecture enables redundancy, rapid replacement, and high availability while
meeting the continuous‑power requirements of line‑haul duty cycles.

Engine modules
The engine power modules supply the primary energy for traction and auxiliary loads. Ten gas
engines (Figure 3) operate in parallel, each packaged with a mechanically coupled 512 kilowatt
(kW) — approximately 600 hp — water‑cooled permanent‑magnet starter/generator inside a
single ISO corner quick‑disconnect module. This modular approach provides redundancy,
enables forklift‑level removal and replacement, and supports high availability in line‑haul duty
cycles.

Permanent-magnet starter generator
Each of the ten engines power modules couples to a water‑cooled permanent‑magnet starter
generator with a nominal continuous rating of 512 kW (≈ 680 hp), as shown in Figure 5. The
machine is engine‑mounted, uses surface‑mounted magnets with sleeve for robustness, and is
packaged to fit the X15N pod envelope. Thin inter‑pod walls direct radiator airflow, while ISO
corner ties at the top and bottom of adjacent pods maintain structural alignment and

This system provides bidirectional electromechanical conversion in each engine module starter
mode for rapid, low‑emission cranking and generator mode for continuous, high‑efficiency

Cooling and airflow for propulsion pods
Thermal management for the propulsion modules is provided by an integrated three‑stack
radiator assembly in each pod, consisting of engine‑block cooling, charge‑air cooling, and
starter/generator liquid cooling. The relocation of the catalyst downward and aft within the
module created the necessary overhead clearance for the expanded radiator system, which was
enlarged by approximately 20% to improve thermal margin during high‑notch,
continuous‑power operation. The radiator pack is designed for top‑down insertion and removal,
allowing maintenance crews to replace or service the cooling system without disturbing adjacent
propulsion modules or requiring major disassembly.
Airflow through each propulsion pod is managed by thin inter‑pod walls that maintain
directional flow across the radiator stacks and prevent recirculation between neighboring
modules. This ensures that each engine receives a consistent supply of cooling air, even when
the locomotive operates under high thermal load or in restricted‑ventilation conditions. The
module framing and ISO corner structure maintain pod alignment to preserve airflow geometry,
while the under‑deck relocation of the battery modules and chillers helps protect the overhead
service envelope and reduces obstructed flow paths within the engine bay.

Distributed power and control system
The locomotive employs a distributed electrical architecture that integrates the modular power
control system (MPCS) units, the high-voltage direct current (HVDC) link, the alternating‑current
(AC) traction interface, the lithium iron phosphate (LFP) battery interface, the auxiliary power
distribution interface, and the locomotive‑level controller area network (CAN) bus.
OptiFuel coordinated with Cummins, BAE Systems, TMV Control Systems, Dynamic E‑Flow,
Construcciones y Auxiliar de Ferrocarriles, Mosebach, and New York Air Brake to finalize the
integrated electrical architecture and equipment placements for the line‑haul locomotive.

Modular Power Control System
The locomotive employs five high‑power MPCS units. Each MPCS manages two X15N engine-
generator sets, performing AC to DC (direct current) conversion, regulating the HVDC bus in the
600–800 V DC range, and coordinating battery charge/discharge. Functional slices within each
cabinet handle generator rectification, DC link regulation, battery interface logic, protection, and
communications. This arrangement scales with module count and enables degradation control
— a single MPCS event can be isolated while the remaining units continue to support the DC
link.
To reduce mass, losses, and installation complexity associated with large‑gauge conductors, the
MPCS cabinets are relocated into the central wiring tunnel beneath the deck walkway. From this
corridor, cable runs to the ten engine-generator pods are short and direct; the same corridor
provides protected access for maintenance and improves fore‑aft mass balance by centralizing
converter weight.
Each MPCS monitors generator phase currents and temperatures, DC link voltage, and battery
interface states. Over‑voltage, over‑current, over‑temperature, and ground‑fault conditions

 Page 16

trigger local protective action and publish events on the CAN bus. Power‑flow commands
(torque requests to generators, battery charge limits, DC link setpoints) execute at sub‑second
cadence to keep the DC link stable through throttle steps, dynamic‑braking transitions, and
auxiliary‑load changes.
All electrical modules — MPCS units, traction electronics, battery‑management systems, and
auxiliary converters — communicate over the locomotive‑level CAN bus, which carries control
commands, interlocks, protection states, and diagnostics across the distributed architecture.
Message priorities ensure deterministic delivery for safety‑critical actions and torque/charge
requests, while faults and derates are broadcast with contextual data (temperatures, voltages,
currents, isolation states) to enable rapid triage and localized fault isolation. Versioned
configuration blocks and counters support maintenance tracking and post‑event analysis, and
centralized access points in the wiring tunnel reduce harness length, streamline service, and
provide consistent tool‑based interrogation of all nodes.

High-Voltage DC Link
The latest per‑module electronics schematic shows that each MPCS controls two X15N engine-
generator sets and converts their three‑phase AC output to the 600–800 V DC high‑voltage link.
This HVDC link is the common bus for traction propulsion, auxiliary conversion, and battery
charge/discharge. MPCS regulation and distributed bus capacitance keep the link stable against
rapid load changes — for example, throttle steps, dynamic‑braking transitions, and
auxiliary‑load swings.
From the 600–800 V DC link, the Auxiliary Power Distribution system generates the locomotive
service buses — 12 V DC, 24 V DC, 28 V DC, 72 V DC, 120 V AC, and 440 V AC — which supply
compressors, fans, sensors, valves, pumps, chillers, control electronics, HVAC, electronic brakes,
traction blowers, multiple‑unit train control, and the engine shutdown/start (ESS) system.
Consolidating all auxiliary conversion at the HVDC bus minimizes high‑current taps, shortens
cable runs to major auxiliary clusters, simplifies protection, and standardizes diagnostics across
hotel/support loads.
High-voltage taps, bus protection points, and isolation hardware are routed within the central
wiring tunnel to minimize exposure and streamline maintenance — segmentation allows
individual converters or loads to be serviced without de‑energizing the entire bus.

LFP Battery System
The battery system uses two CATL strings, each string composed of two D02 modules plus one
D05 module (four modules total), providing 380 kW‑hr at 1C (714.83 V nominal per string) with
4,000‑cycle LFP cells. Both strings are installed in removable battery bays on the deck and are
shock‑isolated for durability and serviceability. Each string includes a battery‑management
system and battery thermal‑management system with an integrated cooler and heater.
Electrically, the strings connect directly to the 600–800 V DC link and are supervised over the
locomotive CAN bus. Controls manage charge limits, contactor states, thermal derates, and fault
reporting so the packs remain within safe operating limits while stabilizing the DC link. In
service, the batteries provide 380 kW continuous at 1C and 760 kW peak for 60 s, supporting

 Page 17

power‑assist at higher notches, regenerative‑braking energy recovery, and low‑emission engine
starts via the starter/generator path.

AC Traction System
The AC traction solution comprises six inverters driving six traction motor/gearbox
combinations. Because each of the six AC traction motors with gearboxes can produce ~750 hp
of tractive effort — ~4,500 hp total on rail — and the locomotive also requires ~400 hp for
auxiliary loads, the propulsion baseline was increased to ten 510 hp X15N power modules
(5,100 hp continuous) to ensure margin at high notches. In addition, the design includes
~380 kW (~500 hp) of LFP battery assist to support transients and regeneration. The layout of
the system is shown in Figure 7.

Internal layout (left) and cabinet enclosure (right)

The primary function of the system is precise power control to the traction motors. Driver
modules command the alternator field(s) and regulate inverter output via CAN bus torque
commands. The CPU main software handles communications with locomotive control
equipment and generates high‑level torque references, which the DSP board converts to
low‑level modulation commands at ~40 µs cycles for optimal traction motor control. Braking
chopper control regulates DC‑bus voltage during electric braking and protects against sudden
voltage surges. The anti‑slip/slide system detects and corrects wheel slip under traction and
provides wheel‑slide protection during braking to optimize adhesion.

The AC traction system is integrated with a Mosebach dynamic‑brake resistor grid, used when
regenerative absorption is limited or unavailable, ensuring controlled electrical braking and
bus‑voltage management.

Storage system for renewable natural gas
This system provides on‑board, crashworthy RNG storage for line‑haul duty — with safe
refueling, isolation, pressure management, and roof‑directed venting — while preserving a deck
walk‑through and minimizing high‑pressure line length to the engine modules.
OptiFuel developed high‑pressure gaseous‑fuel storage with Federal Railroad Administration
(FRA) concurrence for locomotives. Gen‑1 (80″) achieved the first FRA concurrence for 5,000‑psi
CNG on four Indiana Harbor Belt Railroad switchers. Gen‑2 (80″) RNG/H₂ 5,000‑psi storage for
Sierra Northern Railway was delivered in early October 2024 — lessons learned reduced
manufacturing and assembly cost and informed the line‑haul Gen‑3 design (Figure 8).

Completed engineering design
This section presents the design of the hybrid locomotive, which features a 10‑engine modular
system engineered to meet critical horsepower requirements while achieving near‑zero
emissions. The locomotive also incorporates a 380 kilowatt-hour (kWh) battery to optimize
starting efficiency and enable regenerative braking. Each engine is integrated within a dedicated,
removable module, and the full-scale engine module mockup was displayed at the 2024
American Short Line and Regional Railroad Association (ASLRRA) conference. Test generators
were used in dynamometer evaluation. The finalized architecture is now available for future
applications.

Locomotive capabilities and specifications

an SD90‑class frame with six powered axles — delivering 4,500 hp on‑rail traction, 5,100 hp

spend long periods in high notches. Ten stoichiometric NG engine modules, each coupled to a
water‑cooled permanent‑magnet starter/generator, feed a 600–800 VDC bus that powers the AC
traction package with six inverters and six motor/gearbox combos; auxiliaries (12/24/28/72 VDC

Duty cycle and power requirements
The US Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) line‑haul duty cycle specifies the percentage of
total operating time a locomotive spends at each throttle notch, and it serves as the basis for
calculating continuous horsepower, energy consumption, and criteria emissions. Analysis of this
duty cycle shows that line‑haul locomotives must sustain high continuous power for long

 Page 21

periods, with at least 25 percent of total operating hours occurring in Notches 5 through 8
during cross‑country service. Delivering the required 4,500 horsepower of tractive effort plus
300 to 500 horsepower of auxiliary load for hours at a time exceeds the practical capability of
battery‑electric or hydrogen fuel‑cell hybrid systems, whose energy density and
continuous‑power limits prevent them from supporting real‑world U.S. line‑haul freight
operations.
Implementation of the EPA‑mandated engine shutdown/start (ESS) system further increases the
demand for sustained power. ESS reduces idle time by approximately 80 percent, shifting those
hours into active power notches and increasing the fraction of time spent in Notches 5 through
8 to roughly 35 percent of total locomotive runtime. Under this updated distribution, the
locomotive must be capable of delivering full continuous traction and auxiliary power for
extended periods without performance degradation.
The design satisfies the updated EPA line‑haul duty cycle, accommodates the ESS‑driven
operating profile, and ensures the locomotive can deliver reliable, sustained performance under
heavy‑freight conditions. Table 7 summarizes the line‑haul duty cycle percentages and
associated horsepower requirements with and without ESS.

Table 7 demonstrates that the ten 510‑hp RNG engine modules collectively provide 5,100 hp of
continuous power, which is required to move heavy long‑haul freight trains across mountain
grades. For context, EPA switcher duty cycles are heavily weighted toward idle and low‑power
notches, whereas line‑haul requires sustained high‑notch operation - a difference that explains
why low‑power hybrid approaches suitable for switchers cannot meet line‑haul requirements.
In addition to the engine modules, the locomotive incorporates 380 kW (500 hp) of battery
capacity, which serves three key functions:
• Elimination of transient NOx and PM emissions. The batteries power the starter generator
 system to bring each engine up to higher RPM before fueling begins, avoiding transient
 combustion during cranking. This allows every X15N engine to enter directly into
 steady‑state operation.
• Extension of locomotive range through regenerative braking. Captured braking energy
 reduces the demand on the ten internal combustion engines, decreasing time spent in
 high‑power notches 5–8.
• Zero‑emission maneuvering for maintenance operations. The battery system enables moving
 the locomotive in and out of service buildings without operating the internal combustion
 engines.
Based on the ESS‑adjusted duty cycle, each engine module operates approximately 2,281 hours
per year, or 55% of total locomotive runtime. The resulting per‑engine load factor is 41%,
corresponding to an average output of 211 hp for each 510‑hp engine.

Heavy‑duty stoichiometric NG engines equipped with advanced three-way catalysts (TWCs)
have repeatedly demonstrated ultra‑low NOx on regulated cycles — around 0.02 g/bhp‑hr and
lower when catalyst light‑off is fast and fuel‑air control is tight — which translates well to rail
generator service that minimizes transients and holds the catalyst in its optimal window. These
same systems oxidize particulates and non‑methane hydrocarbons effectively, supporting the
0.00 PM and 0.00 NMHC near‑zero goals shown in the table.
CO remains the most sensitive pollutant to catalyst sizing and mixture control — the project
target of 0.60 g/bhp‑hr is feasible for stoichiometric NG and TWC and is more stringent than the
federal Tier‑4 line‑haul CO standard of 1.5 g/bhp‑hr, preserving compliance margin while
aligning with real‑world durability needs. For context, Tier‑4 limits are NOx 1.3, PM 0.03, CO 1.5
g/bhp‑hr; the proposed NG‑TWC architecture is what enables the near‑zero NOx/PM/NMHC
values the table uses for goals.

a controlled RPM band and the TWC at high conversion, battery assist enables clean high‑RPM
starts and regenerative braking, and ESS shifts time into powered notches that the multi‑engine

DISCUSSION
The project concluded with a complete and validated technical design of a modular, multi-
engine natural gas line-haul locomotive, showcased at full scale at the American Short Line and
Regional Railroad Association.
Due to unanticipated changes in industry market conditions, however, the key technology
supplier, Cummins, withdrew from its prior commitment to supply the X15N rail engine. Hence,
the project will not proceed to commercial production as planned.
Despite this setback, the work demonstrated that commercially available engines, generators,
traction equipment, batteries, and storage units could be integrated into an affordable natural
gas based platform. By quantifying emissions reductions, energy efficiency gains, and
operational advantages, the design provides a solid foundation for future natural gas
locomotive programs across Class I, II, and III railroads.
