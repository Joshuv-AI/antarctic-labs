Circumventing the wettability issue of heterogeneous metal catalysts for solvent-free organic transformations
Authors: Chaofeng Zhu, Xiaomeng Dou, Lixiang Qian, Tianxiang Chen, Wenying Li, Carlo Marini, Eduardo Villalobos-Portillo, Liang Zhang, Lichen Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Substituting homogeneous metal catalysts with recyclable heterogeneous metal catalysts has long been a goal in catalysis. However, most approaches yield heterogeneous metal catalysts that often exhibit relatively low stability and diminished catalytic activity under practical reaction conditions. Herein, we present an inorganic Rh catalyst obtained by confining subnanometer Rh clusters within a self-pillared zeolite structure. This catalyst design not only stabilizes the subnanometer Rh active sites but also facilitates efficient mass transport properties during solvent-free olefin hydroformylation reaction. Owing to the unique electronic properties of the Rh clusters and the optimal morphology of the zeolite support, the optimal catalyst achieves over 1,100,000 total turnovers across a broad substrate range under solvent-free conditions. This strategy can be extended to other metals confined within self-pillared zeolite, offering a general approach to circumvent the wettability issue of heterogeneous metal catalysts for solvent-free organic transformations.

Introduction

Substituting homogeneous metal catalysts with recyclable heterogeneous metal catalysts has long been a goal in catalysis. For example, owing to the involvement of the expensive Rh complex and organic ligands in industrial olefin hydroformylation, substituting conventional homogeneous Rh catalysts has long been a challenge in the largest homogeneous catalytic process in the current chemical industry1–4. As described in Fig. 1a, although different types of supported metal catalysts have been developed for heterogeneous olefin hydroformylation reactions, their overall performances are still substantially below those of the classic homogeneous metal catalysts5–11. For instance, these great efforts are reflected in the immobilization of isolated single atoms or clusters on N-doped carbon matrices, the use of d-metal/p-block-element (e.g., Rh2P and Rh17S15) to modulate the geometric and electronic properties of Rh species, and the encapsulation of metal complexes within metal-organic frameworks10,12–15.Fig. 1Significance and challenges in olefin hydroformylation reaction under solvent-free conditions.a Illustrations of typical heterogeneous hydroformylation catalysts, including the immobilization of metal complexes, metal complexes anchored in polymers, metal entities (from isolated metal atoms to clusters and nanoparticles) supported on solid carriers, and subnanometer metal clusters confined in porous materials (zeolites or metal-organic frameworks). b Two different routes for the olefin hydroformylation reaction, depending on the involvement of solvent. From a practical standpoint, heterogeneous metal catalysts should compete with conventional homogeneous metal catalysts under industrially relevant conditions16,17. For instance, in most current industrial hydroformylation processes, homogeneous metal catalysts are preferred for handling highly concentrated reaction feedstocks (i.e., with a low solvent volume), thereby maximizing production efficiency in batch or semi-batch reactors (Fig. 1b)18. In the concentrated reaction medium, the metal sites are exposed and coordinated by concentrated oxygenate products, resulting in the site blockage19–22. Moreover, heavy oxygenate by-products produced from the self-condensation of the concentrated aldehyde products are also detrimental to the homogeneous metal complexes23. In this regard, though the homogeneous systems have been optimized for decades, it is still challenging to maintain high activity/selectivity of the metal complex under solvent-free reaction conditions.

When performing the olefin hydroformylation reaction with heterogeneous metal catalysts under solvent-free conditions, the catalyst’s performance is significantly compromised due to the undesired adsorption of products/byproducts on the solid carriers, leading to severe catalyst deactivation due to site blocking (Fig. 1c). Furthermore, leaching and aggregation of metal active sites are exacerbated by the presence of concentrated reactants/products, leading to very poor dispersion of catalyst particles in the reaction mixture. By encapsulating metal active sites within porous carriers (e.g., polymers, metal-organic frameworks, or zeolites), the stability and recyclability of metal species can be substantially improved5,24–28. Recently, Rh species confined within zeolites have exhibited superior performance under dilute solution conditions8,28. However, in these cases, site blocking by concentrated reactants/products will pose a significant challenge, leading to rapid catalyst deactivation. Consequently, most previous studies on heterogeneous hydroformylation catalysts have been conducted under dilute conditions (typically <10 vol% in solvent)29. Notably, the abovementioned issues are not only encountered in olefin hydroformylation reaction, but also are critical factors that preclude the application of heterogeneous metal catalysts for organic transformations.

In this work, we have developed an inorganic Rh catalyst by confining subnanometer Rh clusters into self-pillared zeolite support (Rh@SPZ catalyst) to balance the stabilization, accessibility of the Rh sites, and the wettability of catalyst particles for solvent-free olefin hydroformylation reaction (Fig. 1d). Owing to the unique electronic properties of Rh clusters and the optimal morphology of zeolite support, the Rh@SPZ catalyst can achieve up to >300,000 turnover (TON) for a broad scope of olefin substrates in a single catalytic run and >1,170,000 TON for consecutive runs under solvent-free conditions. This strategy can be potentially extended to other noble metals (Pt, Ir, Pd, etc.) confined within self-pillared zeolite for solvent-free organic transformations, offering a general approach to sustainable organic transformations with heterogeneous metal catalysts.

Results

Catalyst synthesis and structural characterizations

The subnanometer Rh catalysts confined in pure-silica self-pillared zeolite (Rh@SPZ) were prepared by a one-pot synthesis approach, in which a mixture of the zeolite precursors, organic structure-directing agent, and Rh precursor was subjected to a hydrothermal crystallization treatment (see experimental details in “Methods” section)30. Afterward, the as-synthesized Rh@SPZ samples were subjected to a calcination treatment in the air to remove the organics and a subsequent reduction by H2, resulting in the formation of subnanometer Rh species in SPZ zeolite. The high-angle annular dark-field scanning electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) and atomic force microscopy images reveal that the SPZ material is composed of thin zeolite nanosheets with a thickness of 2–5 nm, assembled into a three-dimensional self-pillared morphology (Fig. 2a–c and Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2). For comparison, we have prepared reference Rh@MFI and Rh@MEL samples by one-pot synthesis, in which subnanometer Rh clusters are encapsulated in pure-silica MFI and MEL zeolite crystallites with regular morphologies (Fig. 2d–i)31,32. The Rh@MFI sample consists of prism-shaped zeolite crystallites, while the Rh@MEL sample consists of MEL mesocrystals made by rod-shaped crystallites, both of which exhibit much larger crystallite size than the Rh@SPZ sample (Supplementary Figs. 3–5).Fig. 2Structure and morphology of the subnanometer Rh clusters in different zeolite materials.a–c 0.35Rh@SPZ, d–f 0.35Rh@MFI and g–i 0.35Rh@MEL. XRD patterns of the SPZ zeolite sample indicate the presence of both MFI- and MEL-type zeolite structures in the SPZ zeolite, consistent with the structure-directing preference of tetrabutylammonium hydroxide (Supplementary Figs. 6–9). The XRD patterns also infer a relatively lower crystallinity of zeolite structure compared to conventional MFI and MEL zeolite materials, consistent with the solid-state 29Si NMR spectra (Supplementary Fig. 10). Additionally, the unique morphology of SPZ material compared to MFI and MEL materials is also inferred by other characterization techniques, such as argon adsorption-desorption isotherms (Supplementary Fig. 11 and Supplementary Table 1)33,34. Due to the high surface area exposure, a large number of external silanol groups are observed in the SPZ material compared to the conventional MFI and MEL zeolites (Supplementary Figs. 12 and 13)35. This surface property feature could facilitate the dispersion of SPZ material in an organic medium containing concentrated aldehyde products, a scenario that occurs during the solvent-free olefin hydroformylation reaction27,36,37.

Using high-resolution HAADF-STEM imaging, we visualize subnanometer Rh species across different Rh@SPZ samples. As shown in Fig. 2c and Supplementary Figs. 14 and 15, the 0.35Rh@SPZ sample with a Rh loading of 0.35 wt% contains Rh clusters of 0.4–0.7 nm with an average particle size of ~0.5 nm. The size distributions of Rh species in the final Rh@SPZ samples can be modulated via the Rh loading. By lowering the Rh loading to 0.15 wt%, the average particle size decreases to ~0.4 nm (Supplementary Fig. 16). Regarding the Rh@SPZ samples with Rh loading of <0.05 wt%, the number of subnanometer Rh clusters is greatly diminished (Supplementary Figs. 17 and 18). In particular, we can barely observe Rh clusters in the 0.015Rh@SPZ sample, implying that Rh species may predominantly exist as atomically dispersed Rh species. The location of the subnanometer Rh species is determined by combining high-resolution HAADF-STEM and integrated differential phase contrast (iDPC)-STEM imaging techniques38. As displayed in Fig. 3a–p, the subnanometer Rh clusters are found to be primarily confined in the 10MR channels of the pentasil zeolite structure (MFI- or MEL-type zeolite), regardless of the Rh loading (Supplementary Figs. 19–22)39. Catalytic tests for solvent-free olefin hydroformylation

Solvent-free hydroformylation of styrene is employed as a model reaction to test the performance of the Rh@SPZ samples. The initial reaction rates measured under kinetically relevant conditions are normalized by the number of Rh atoms in the supported Rh catalysts, which serves as the basis for comparing activities among different materials. As shown in Fig. 5a, the 0.015Rh@SPZ sample exhibits a slightly higher initial reaction rate than the homogeneous HRh(CO)(PPh3)3 catalyst. As the Rh loading is increased to 0.035 wt%, the 0.035Rh@SPZ sample exhibits a 2.5-fold initial reaction rate of the HRh(CO)(PPh3)3 catalyst, suggesting that incorporating subnanometer Rh species into SPZ support can surpass the benchmark homogeneous Rh catalyst under challenging reaction conditions. Interestingly, as the Rh loading increases further, the specific initial reaction rates decline. Following the above hypothesis, we conduct a set of control experiments to clarify the optimal atomicity of Rh sites in Rh@SPZ catalysts. The pre-treatments of the Rh-zeolite catalysts can control the dispersion of Rh species. The 0.035Rh@SPZ-Air and 0.015Rh@SPZ-Air samples were prepared by calcination in air, resulting in predominantly atomically dispersed Rh species on SPZ zeolite (Supplementary Figs. 32–36). In comparison with the reduced counterparts containing subnanometer Rh clusters (0.035Rh@SPZ and 0.015Rh@SPZ samples), samples derived from calcination in air present lower initial activities, implying that atomically Rh species are probably less active than the Rh clusters (Supplementary Fig. 37). By correlating the size distributions and initial reaction rates of different Rh@SPZ catalysts, we tentatively assign the optimal particle size for hydroformylation of styrene to be Rh clusters of 0.3–0.5 nm, which probably contain 3–5 atoms48.

The catalytic performance of the Rh@SPZ catalysts can be optimized by altering the parameters of the one-pot synthesis procedure. As shown in Supplementary Figs. 38–72, when tuning the contents of alkali promoter (K+) or organic ligand (ethylene diamine) in the synthesis mixture, the dispersion of Rh species and the morphology of the SPZ zeolite support will be modified, which further affects the catalytic performances in hydroformylation of styrene31. These results demonstrate that both the structural features of the Rh active species and the microenvironment that hosts the Rh sites are essential for achieving high activities in solvent-free olefin hydroformylation reaction32.

Rh-zeolite catalysts have been reported as active catalysts for olefin hydroformylation reaction conditions in prior works, among which the Rh-zeolite catalysts were usually tested in the presence of large quantities of organic solvents. Herein, we compare the Rh@SPZ samples with the reference Rh@MFI and Rh@MEL catalysts, in which subnanometer Rh species are confined within MFI and MEL zeolites. Structural characterizations of these catalysts suggest that the dispersion of Rh species is not significantly affected by the type of zeolite support. The subnanometer Rh species in the 0.035Rh@MEL and 0.035Rh@MFI catalysts are also located within the 10MR channels, resulting in a similar surrounding environment as the 0.035Rh@SPZ catalyst (Supplementary Figs. 73 and 74). The 0.035Rh@SPZ catalyst shows nearly one order of magnitude higher initial reaction rate than the 0.035Rh@MEL and 0.035Rh@MFI catalysts for the hydroformylation of styrene (Fig. 5a). The superior performance of Rh catalysts confined in SPZ is also reflected in the comparison between the catalysts with high Rh loadings (0.35Rh@SPZ and 0.35Rh@MFI catalysts) (Supplementary Fig. 75). From a practical standpoint, loading a relatively high number of subnanometer Rh sites into SPZ zeolite, which exhibits high exposure to olefin reactants and maximizes Rh-site utilization, enables high productivity within a limited reaction time and reactor volume49.

Given that the reactant and product molecules in the hydroformylation of styrene are close to the pore dimension of 10MR channels in MFI and MEL zeolites50,51, the discrepancy in mass transport properties may cause the profound differences among the Rh-zeolite samples as implied by the very short diffusion distance from the Rh active sites to the reactant in the Rh@SPZ materials according to our modelling results (Supplementary Figs. 76 and 77). This conjecture is supported by the diffusion coefficients of styrene within different zeolite catalysts measured by dynamic vapor adsorption tests (Fig. 4h, i). Moreover, the 0.035Rh@SPZ catalyst exhibits significantly improved wettability and dispersion compared to the 0.035Rh@MEL and 0.035Rh@MFI catalysts in neat styrene, as indicated by the smaller sizes of zeolite particle aggregates measured by dynamic light scattering (DLS) (Fig. 4g and Supplementary Figs. 78–81)52,53. The excellent dispersion of 0.035Rh@SPZ catalyst within the reaction mixture can be maintained throughout the entire reaction period, but the other two Rh-zeolite catalysts suffer severe aggregation of the zeolite crystallites in the solvent-free reaction medium (Supplementary Figs. 82–85).

Furthermore, we have prepared a series of heterogeneous reference Rh catalysts by immobilizing Rh active sites on conventional open-structure oxide carriers, which are reported to be efficient for olefin hydroformylation6,7. As shown in Supplementary Figs. 86–100, Rh species are nearly atomically dispersed on these supports due to the very low Rh loading (<0.05 wt%). When testing under the same conditions as the 0.035Rh@SPZ catalyst, the oxide-supported Rh catalysts show considerably lower activities for solvent-free hydroformylation of styrene, regardless of the pre-treatment conditions (Supplementary Figs. 101–103). These results not only confirm the superior performance of Rh clusters over isolated Rh atoms but also demonstrate the remarkable advantage of using SPZ material as the host of subnanometer Rh clusters over the conventional oxide carriers. As shown in Fig. 5e, compared to reported heterogeneous and homogeneous Rh catalysts, the 0.035Rh@SPZ catalyst exhibits significantly higher turnover numbers in a single catalytic run under solvent-free conditions. Besides its high activity in converting olefin reactants, the Rh@SPZ catalyst maintains selectivity to aldehyde products at very high levels (Fig. 5f).

Theoretical calculations

To elucidate the mechanism of styrene hydroformylation and identify the optimal catalyst configuration, we performed density functional theory (DFT) calculations on three distinct active sites encapsulated in the MEL zeolite framework: a single-atom (Rh1@Z model), a small cluster (Rh3@Z model), and a sub-nanocluster (Rh7@Z model). The calculated Gibbs free energy profiles presented in Fig. 6a outline the seven key elementary steps in the styrene hydroformylation reaction. A comparison of the energy profiles for the three models reveals that their pathways are energetically similar before the CO insertion step (S3 → S4). A clear divergence occurs because the reaction energy on the Rh7@Z model is significantly higher than that on the Rh1@Z and Rh3@Z models for the CO insertion step. Another notable difference is observed during the H2 dissociation step, where we observe an increase in energy with Rh1@Z and a decrease in energy on Rh3@Z and Rh7@Z models. This discrepancy suggests that H2 dissociation is less favorable on the Rh1@Z model compared to Rh sites with higher atomicity. While thermodynamics provides insight into intermediates’ stability, the overall catalytic performance is dictated by the energy barrier of the rate-determining step. As presented in Fig. 6b, the CO insertion step (TS3-4) carries the highest barrier for Rh1@Z (1.58 eV) and Rh7@Z (1.60 eV), while the second H insertion step (TS5-6) is highest for Rh3@Z (0.87 eV). The Rh3@Z model exhibits the lowest maximum barrier among the three models, consistent with its highest catalytic activity. These results demonstrate that the Rh3 cluster could be the most kinetically favorable site for the styrene hydroformylation reaction compared to the single-atom Rh site and Rh clusters with relatively high atomicity.

To understand the origin of the size-dependent activity of subnanometer Rh species in styrene hydroformylation reaction, we further analyze the C–C bond formation in the transition state of the CO insertion step by quantifying the covalent character of the bonds using Crystal Orbital Hamilton Population (–COHP) analysis (Fig. 6c). The integrated -COHP (ICOHP) value for the C–C bond on the Rh3 site is −8.94 eV, which is more negative than the values for the Rh1 and Rh7 sites (−8.46 eV for both). The Bader charge analysis shows that the Rh1 site carries a high positive charge of +0.89 e, which should be less favorable for the CO insertion reaction compared to the more moderate charge of +0.53 e on the reaction Rh site in the Rh3@Z model (Fig. 6d–f)54,55.

The charge density difference plot for Rh3 illustrates that significant charge redistribution occurs not only at the primary Rh site for the hydroformylation reaction but also involves an adjacent Rh atom (Fig. 6e). From an electronic perspective, the neighboring Rh atom facilitates CO insertion by stabilizing reaction intermediates and preventing the primary Rh site from being fully coordinated. As shown in Fig. 6f, the initial distance between the reacting carbon atoms in CO and ethylbenzene species on the Rh7 cluster is 3.72 Å, which is much larger than that on the Rh3 cluster (2.90 Å). Such a discrepancy in geometric structures will cause a substantial energy barrier towards the formation of the transition state in the CO insertion step in the styrene hydroformylation reaction. In summary, the Rh3 cluster confined in zeolite channels emerges as the optimal structure because it balances favorable electronic and geometric features for the styrene hydroformylation reaction.

Scope study and stability test

The excellent performance of the 0.035Rh@SPZ catalyst for olefin hydroformylation under solvent-free conditions is further evaluated across a broad range of industrially relevant olefin feedstocks. As shown in Fig. 7, when dealing with linear α-olefin reactants (Compounds 1–8), the 0.035Rh@SPZ catalyst can give high conversion (>90%) and high selectivity to aldehydes (≥95%). Due to the low loading of Rh, the turnover numbers can reach above 300,000 for a variety of linear α-olefins. In comparative experiments under identical conditions, the 0.035Rh@MFI and 0.035Rh@MEL catalysts exhibit lower aldehyde product yields and lower TONs (Fig. 5c). These results demonstrate the superior performance of the 0.035Rh@SPZ catalyst over conventional zeolite-confined Rh catalysts under solvent-free conditions. Detailed comparison between 0.035Rh@SPZ and 0.035Rh@MFI catalyst for hydroformylation of 1-decene suggests that the Rh clusters confined in SPZ material exhibit much higher selectivity to aldehyde products, while the Rh clusters in the conventional MFI zeolite give a considerable yield of isomerized decenes (Supplementary Fig. 105). Besides linear olefins, we have further tested the hydroformylation of other olefin feedstocks with larger kinetic diameters than linear α-olefins, which are usually challenging to deal with because of their slow diffusion rates along the microporous zeolite channels51. For example, in the case of aromatic-type olefins, the catalyst can also give high TONs (at the magnitude of 105) as the hydroformylation of styrene, which should be ascribed to the high intrinsic activity and accessibility of Rh clusters confined in SPZ structure. In the case of linear internal olefins or internal aromatic olefins, the 0.035Rh@SPZ catalyst can also achieve very high TONs and high aldehyde selectivities. The greatly extended substrate scope is further reflected by the high TONs achieved with cycloolefins and naphthalene derivatives. For comparison, we have tested the performance of 0.035Rh@MFI and 0.035Rh@MEL, which have similar Rh loadings as the 0.035Rh@SPZ catalyst, in the hydroformylation of 2-vinylnaphthalene. The two catalysts exhibit negligible activity due to the unfavorable diffusion of 2-vinylnaphthalene through the 10MR channels (Supplementary Figs. 106 and 107). Additionally, we have studied the tolerance of the 0.035Rh@SPZ catalyst to different functional groups in the olefin substrates. The catalytic results show that the 0.035Rh@SPZ catalyst can deal with olefins comprising substituted groups such as –Cl, –Br, –NO2, –OH, –COOH, and –OCH3 for hydroformylation reactions under solvent-free conditions, demonstrating the potential application of subnanometer Rh clusters confined in SPZ zeolite as a general and sustainable heterogeneous olefin hydroformylation catalyst under solvent-free or highly concentrated conditions. To the best of our knowledge, the 0.035Rh@SPZ catalyst exhibits the broadest level of substrate scope among the heterogeneous metal catalysts for olefin hydroformylation reaction3.

In addition to its high intrinsic activity originating from the unique electronic properties of Rh clusters confined in zeolite channels, the advantage of the 0.035Rh@SPZ catalyst over the classic homogeneous Rh catalyst is reflected by its facile separation from the reaction mixture and reusability of the noble Rh species. As displayed in Fig. 5d, the 0.035Rh@SPZ catalyst can be reused for at least four consecutive reaction cycles, accumulating a total turnover number of over 1,170,000 for the solvent-free hydroformylation of styrene, which is the highest value reported to date for an inorganic solid Rh catalyst in olefin hydroformylation reactions. The heterogeneous nature of the catalytic process with the 0.035Rh@SPZ catalyst is supported by the hot filtration experiment (Supplementary Table 7) and by the preservation of Rh content in the recycled catalyst (Supplementary Table 8). Under the same reaction conditions, the 0.035Rh/SPZ sample prepared by the conventional impregnation method suffers considerable deactivation during the consecutive recycling tests, which is probably caused by the leaching and/or agglomeration of surface Rh species under the harsh reaction conditions, as implied by the hot-filtration testing results (Supplementary Table 9). The leaching of Rh species under solvent-free conditions has also been observed with atomically dispersed Rh species on CeO2, which could be caused by the lack of sufficient confinement effects on Rh by the open-structure CeO2 surface (Supplementary Table 10). The robustness of the subnanometer Rh clusters in the 0.035Rh@SPZ catalyst is further confirmed by the electron microscopy imaging technique as well as the in situ CO-IR spectroscopy, which shows the stabilization of subnanometer Rh clusters in the 10MR zeolite channels (Supplementary Figs. 108–116).

Discussion

In summary, we have developed a new strategy to circumvent the wettability issue of heterogeneous metal catalysts in solvent-free organic transformations, yielding excellent performance in solvent-free olefin hydroformylation. This strategy can bridge homogeneous and heterogeneous catalysis by confining isolated subnanometer metal sites within a zeolite matrix for various organic transformations, thereby enabling the simultaneous achievement of high reactivity, facile recycling, and post-reaction separation of noble metal catalysts under industrially relevant conditions56. Moreover, the potential extension of the SPZ confinement strategy to other subnanometer noble metal catalysts (Pd, Pt, Ir, etc.) for a broad range of catalytic reactions. Consequently, by substituting traditional homogeneous metal catalysts with metal-SPZ catalysts, the sustainability of the entire reaction processes can be greatly improved.

Synthesis of Rh@SPZ zeolite with varying Rh contents

Firstly, a tetrabutylammonium hydroxide (TBAOH) solution was prepared by mixing 4.5 g of TBAOH (40 wt% aqueous solution), 27 mg of KOH, and 3.11 g of distilled water at room temperature. After stirring for 1 h, 4.8 g of TEOS was added dropwise into the TBAOH solution with vigorous stirring. Then, the mixture was hydrolysed at room temperature for 6 h under stirring (500 r.p.m.). Various amounts of RhCl3 aqueous solution (0.4 mol·L−1) and 150 μL of ethylenediamine were mixed to obtain a Rh-ethylenediamine complex solution, which was then added to the TEOS-TBAOH-water mixture while stirring for 30 min. The amount of RhCl3 aqueous solution added was adjusted to tune the Rh loading in the final Rh@SPZ materials. The resultant yellow solution was then transferred to a Teflon-lined autoclave and heated in an electric oven at 115 °C for 96 h under static conditions. The loadings of Rh in the final Rh@SPZ materials range from ~0.015 to ~0.35 wt%, depending on the amount of RhCl3 solution added. After the hydrothermal process, the solid product was isolated by centrifugation, washed with distilled water and acetone, and then dried at 60 °C for 12 h. The solid sample was then calcined in a quartz tube furnace with flowing air (20 mL min−1) at 560 °C for 8 h, followed by reduction with H2 (5% H2 in argon) at 600 °C for 2 h.

General procedure of Rh-zeolite catalyzed solvent-free hydroformylation of styrene

In a representative procedure, a 12 mL screw-cap vial equipped with a magnetic stirring bar was charged with Rh-zeolite and styrene (25 mmol). Subsequently, 100 μL of n-hexane was added to the product mixture, which was then analyzed by gas chromatography (Agilent 8890, equipped with HP-5 column).

Computational details

All calculations were performed using the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP 6.3.2)57,58. The projector augmented-wave method was used, employing the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof functional within the generalized gradient approximation59,60. Van der Waals corrections were included using Grimme’s DFT-D3 method61. A plane-wave kinetic energy cutoff of 450 eV and a Gamma-point-only (1 × 1 × 1) k-mesh were used for all calculations. The electronic self-consistent loop was converged to 10−8 eV.

The stable intermediates (S1–S6) were optimized using the conjugate-gradient algorithm. All structures were fully relaxed until the residual forces on each atom were less than 0.025 eV/Å. Transition states for the CO insertion step were located using the Lanczos method as implemented in the VASP-VTST code62. Vibrational frequencies were calculated for all species. These frequencies were then processed using the VASPkit code, which applied thermal corrections, including the zero-point energy (ZPE), to obtain the Gibbs free energies at 353 K at atmospheric pressure63.

To analyze the bonding characteristics of the forming C–C bond in the transition state, Crystal Orbital Hamilton Population (–COHP) analysis was performed using the LOBSTER package64,65. Bader charges were calculated using the code developed by the Henkelman group to analyze the electronic properties66. The charge density difference plots were generated to visualize charge redistribution and were plotted using VESTA with an isosurface level set to 0.01 e/Å3 67.

During the frequency calculations for the adsorbed intermediates (S1–S6), all Si and O atoms of the zeolite framework were held fixed in their optimized positions to suppress the low-frequency collective modes arising from the extended framework, which would otherwise introduce spurious contributions to the vibrational entropy within the harmonic oscillator approximation.

Point-scattering method

The crystal geometry (MFI octagonal prism or MEL/SPZ cuboid) is defined in a 3D Cartesian coordinate system based on crystallographic parameters. Rh7 cluster numbers are determined from metal loading and cluster size, and points representing clusters are randomly distributed within the crystal volume under a minimum distance constraint to avoid overlap. For each point, the shortest distance to the relevant crystal surfaces is calculated to represent the diffusion path along straight channels. The average diffusion distance is computed, and multiple scattering rounds are performed to obtain statistically reliable results.
