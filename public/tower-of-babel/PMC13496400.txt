What Transesophageal Echocardiography Reveals About Intraoperative Iatrogenic Aortic Dissection
Authors: Kazumasa Orihashi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntraoperative iatrogenic aortic dissection is a rare but potentially catastrophic complication of cardiovascular surgery. Although transoesophageal echocardiography (TEE) is considered a key diagnostic tool, its practical value and limitations are not fully appreciated. From the perspective of a TEE observer with more than 30 years of experience, only 7 cases of intraoperative iatrogenic aortic dissection were encountered. Review of these cases yielded 3 practical observations: conventional monitoring modalities may fail to detect developing dissection; the mere presence of a TEE probe does not guarantee recognition; and some forms of aortic wall injury may challenge conventional interpretations. Representative cases demonstrated that dissection can progress without immediate changes in haemodynamic variables or surgical findings, and that clinically important pathology may extend beyond the visible operative field. Additional observations suggested that intraoperative aortic wall injury may present with diverse morphologic appearances depending on the involved layer of the aortic wall. These experiences support considering proactive TEE surveillance during initiation of arterial perfusion and suggest that recognition of otherwise occult pathology may facilitate timely intervention and improve situational awareness during cardiovascular surgery.

Intraoperative iatrogenic aortic dissection during cardiovascular surgery is rare, with a reported incidence of 0.06%-0.23%, but remains a potentially catastrophic complication associated with mortality rates ranging from 6.7% to 47.6%.1 Similar to spontaneous aortic dissection, rupture and malperfusion are the principal causes of adverse outcomes, and the above wide range likely reflects differences in case mix, timing of recognition, extent of dissection, and development of rupture or malperfusion. Because symptoms are unavailable under general anaesthesia, prompt recognition, interruption of disease progression, and accurate assessment of the resulting pathology are essential for minimizing mortality and permanent sequelae.

From the perspective of a transoesophageal echocardiography (TEE) observer with more than 30 years of experience, only 7 cases of intraoperative iatrogenic aortic dissection were encountered. Despite their rarity, these experiences provided 3 practical insights: (1) conventional monitoring modalities have important limitations in detecting intraoperative dissection, (2) simply using TEE does not guarantee recognition, and (3) TEE may reveal unusual forms of aortic wall injury that challenge conventional interpretations. The representative cases particularly reinforced the first 2 insights while illustrating the third, less-appreciated concept. This retrospective review of surgical, echocardiographic, and operative video records routinely archived for quality improvement and patient safety purposes was approved by the institutional review board (Approval No. ERB-112313), and the requirement for individual informed consent was waived using an opt-out process.

TEE can visualize intrathoracic abnormalities in real time and facilitate prompt intervention.2,3 Wu et al1 reported that 38.7% of intraoperative dissections were initially recognized by TEE, whereas 51.6% were first noticed from surgical findings. Two previously reported cases of dissection involving the aortic arch and descending thoracic aorta clearly illustrate the first 2 observations.4,5 While dissection developed immediately after initiation of arterial perfusion, no abnormality was recognized by the surgeons, anaesthesiologists, or perfusionists, who were surprised when immediate cessation of arterial perfusion was recommended based on TEE findings. Although the subsequent course remains speculative, it is likely that several minutes would have elapsed before haemorrhage or malperfusion produced detectable changes in vital signs, cerebral oximetry, or laboratory parameters.

An important lesson from these cases is that even when a TEE probe is in place, dissection may not be detected unless the downstream region of the aorta is monitored during initiation of perfusion. Although intraoperative dissection is encountered only once every several years, these experiences suggest that continuous surveillance of the downstream aorta for approximately 1 minute after initiation of perfusion may be worthwhile. Such vigilance may have contributed to favourable outcomes in all 7 cases. Once dissection or unusual aortic wall injury is suspected, TEE assessment should focus on: (1) rupture or haematoma extension into the pleural, pericardial, or mediastinal space, (2) coronary, cerebral, or visceral malperfusion, and (3) the need for alternative arterial perfusion.

The next 2 cases highlight a different limitation.

An 81-year-old man underwent total arch replacement for an aortic arch aneurysm. Arterial perfusion through the ascending aorta was initiated uneventfully. However, 5 minutes later, the TEE observer incidentally noticed a rapidly expanding subadventitial haematoma propagating retrogradely from the arterial cannulation site (Video S1). Although the event was visible on monitors shared by the operative team, the surgeons were focused on cannulation for retrograde cardioplegia, while the anaesthesiologists were occupied with anaesthetic management and haemodynamic monitoring, and nobody else recognized the developing haematoma. Immediate TEE assessment demonstrated expansion of a false lumen, compression of the true lumen, and retrograde extension towards the sinotubular junction without coronary involvement (Figure 1A and B). Epiaortic ultrasonography confirmed the diagnosis, and TEE excluded extension into the aortic arch and supra-aortic branches. After multidisciplinary discussion, cooling was continued under careful TEE surveillance. Once ventricular fibrillation occurred during systemic cooling, the aorta was cross-clamped, cardiac arrest was achieved using retrograde cardioplegia, and the planned operation proceeded successfully. Surgical inspection subsequently confirmed dissection originating near the cannulation site (Figure 1C). The postoperative course was uneventful.

A second case involved a 79-year-old man undergoing emergency coronary artery bypass grafting for unstable angina. After induction of anaesthesia, TEE revealed more severe aortic regurgitation than had been appreciated preoperatively, and aortic valve replacement was added to the surgical plan. Cardiopulmonary bypass was established uneventfully through the ascending aorta. During cardiac displacement for coronary inspection, a subadventitial haematoma gradually appeared around the cannulation site and progressively expanded throughout the ascending aorta (Video S2). Because localized subadventitial haematoma is not uncommon, the finding could initially have been dismissed as a more extensive form of an otherwise familiar phenomenon. However, because aortic wall injury could influence subsequent proximal graft anastomoses, TEE was performed. Circumferential thickening of the ascending and arch aortic walls was observed (Figure 1D and E), together with newly developed haematoma surrounding the descending thoracic aorta (Figure 1F). The findings were immediately communicated to the surgical team. Pulsatile bleeding subsequently developed from disruption of the adventitia near the cannulation site (Video S2). Arterial perfusion was therefore converted to the femoral artery, the ascending aortic cannula was removed, and haemostatic repair was performed. Expansion of the haematoma ceased thereafter. When the aorta was opened, no typical intimal flap was identified. Instead, the adventitia had separated from the media. Descending aortic haematoma remained stable. Although temporary extracorporeal membrane oxygenation support was required postoperatively, the patient recovered without major sequelae. While the definition of “aortic dissection” remains debatable, this case showed that subadventitial bleeding occurred behind the surgical field, potentially penetrating into the pleural cavity. Importantly, the surgically visible bleeding represented only the accessible portion of a more extensive aortic wall injury, which may be recognized by means of TEE if it is timely applied for this purpose. This concept—that the visible event may not represent the entire pathological process—should be emphasized in surgical education.

A related observation was reported previously in a patient with chronic descending aortic dissection, in whom a new and more superficial dissection developed within the intimal side of the wall (Video S3).5 Watke et al6 described a similar appearance as “pseudo-dissection,” attributing it to an interface between crystalloid prime and blood. However, during routine TEE surveillance at initiation of femoral arterial perfusion, we have consistently observed only poorly defined mixing patterns rather than a distinct interface (Video S4). Furthermore, in the present case, the false lumen rapidly occupied the entire aortic lumen within seconds and disappeared completely after perfusion was stopped. Had TEE not been performed at that precise moment, branch vessel occlusion and transient ischaemic injury might have occurred without leaving any detectable postoperative imaging findings. This observation raises the possibility that at least some findings previously dismissed as a perfusate-blood interface may represent real but transient subintimal dissection, particularly when a thin flap forms a false lumen that dynamically collapses after cessation of perfusion (Figure 2).

These experiences suggest a simple but important practical message. In addition to detecting classical dissection, TEE may reveal otherwise occult forms of aortic wall injury that extend beyond the surgical field. During initiation of arterial perfusion, the downstream aorta should be actively visualized by TEE to confirm the absence of newly developing dissection despite the rarity of the event. In antegrade perfusion, dissection tends to propagate rapidly, whereas in femoral perfusion the retrograde direction of flow may delay appearance of abnormalities for several seconds.5 Once retrograde flow and crystalloid prime become visible without evidence of dissection, the likelihood of dissection appears low. However, because delayed dissections have been reported even tens of minutes after initiation of femoral perfusion,7 repeated surveillance after the initial assessment may also be advisable.

This report reflects the experience of a single TEE observer and includes only a limited number of cases. Although definitive conclusions cannot be drawn, these observations support considering proactive TEE surveillance for recognizing otherwise occult manifestations of intraoperative aortic wall injury. Outcomes cannot be attributed to this surveillance approach because of the uncontrolled nature of this small case series. In addition, the 30-year, single-observer retrospective design introduces potential selection and recall bias.
