The use of 3-dimensional (3D) printing in teaching musculoskeletal oncology for medical undergraduates
Authors: Qinxiang Shant Sin, Hun Yi Koh, Kumaran Rasappan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionThe approach to integrating relevant anatomy in the medical curriculum has been debated for many years. Current literature has explored the broad impact of 3D printing in medical education. However, there is little evidence on 3D printing for the teaching of musculoskeletal oncology (MSO). This is a self-controlled case series (SCCS) study that aims to analyse the effectiveness of 3D printed models in MSO in enhancing the learning experience, engagement and understanding of clinical and surgical anatomy for medical students.MethodA cross-sectional cohort study involving 75 clinical year medical students across 3 years from 2 medical schools that rotated through a single teaching hospital’s orthopaedic department. Participants first viewed a set of computed-tomography (CT) images of a large pelvic osteosarcoma from a free open-source database, the Cancer Genome Atlas Sarcoma Collection (TCGA-SARC). A standardised 10-minute pre-intervention questionnaire which comprised 15 questions categorised by: 4 questions in ‘anatomical knowledge’, 7 questions in ‘spatial awareness’, 4 questions in ‘surgical planning and complications’, was administered to assess the baseline knowledge in their interpretation of the pathology via CT images only. Next, a 3D-printed model of the pelvic osteosarcoma, which included colour-coded adjacent structures, was provided as an adjunct to answer the same questionnaire. This concluded with a 5-point Likert scale feedback survey to gauge their perspectives and experience. ResultsThe mean scores comparing their pre- and post-intervention assessment questionnaire increased by +1.34 from 8.15 (SD = 1.85) to 9.49 (SD = 1.7) (p < 0.001). The final year students had the greatest improvement of +1.54 from 8.00 (SD = 1.89) to 9.54 (SD = 1.59) (p = 0.004). There was no significant difference between the scores amongst the 2 medical schools. 91% of students agreed that the 3D model had helped them further their understanding of the anatomy of the sarcoma and 87% would want 3D printing models to augment their learning in anatomy. Baseline weaker students demonstrated significantly greater improvement in scores compared with baseline stronger students (mean difference +2.04 vs +0.30, p < 0.001).Conclusion3D printing is an effective teaching adjunct for musculoskeletal oncology surgical anatomy for medical undergraduates and could be used to enhance their understanding and learning experience. 3D models could be integrated in the teaching curriculum of surgical anatomy for undergraduate students.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12909-026-10023-1.

Introduction

Anatomy is a difficult yet fundamental knowledge and a core curriculum in medical education that lays the foundation for safe surgical and medical practice [1]. With good anatomical understanding, students and practitioners can better relate pathology with the relevant anatomy, thereby improving their clinical acumen [2, 3]. However, the ability to learn and understand, followed by the translation of this knowledge into a clinical application may be demanding for undergraduate students [4]. Classically, cadaveric dissection has been the cornerstone of anatomy teaching and a preferred learning resource amongst students [5, 6]. However, they are less desirable due to practical and logistical reasons such as, difficulty in identifying structures, difficulty acquiring specimens, costs and the odor of formalin [7]. Alternatives to this include the use of anatomy videos, electronic resources, plastinated specimens and 3-dimensional (3D) models [5].

Musculoskeletal tumours can occur in almost any anatomic site such as surrounding organs, soft tissue structures/vessels in various pathologies [8, 9] and anatomical appreciation of the tumours can be complex. Although there have been studies exploring its use on surgical planning, there is little evidence on 3D printing for teaching surgical anatomy [10–12] in musculoskeletal oncology. Considering an up-and-coming trend and the broad impact of 3D printing, we have created a self-controlled case series (SCCS) with the aim to analyse the effectiveness of a comprehensive 3D printed model in musculoskeletal oncology pathology in enhancing the learning experience, engagement and understanding of clinical/surgical anatomy for medical students.

Materials and methods

This was a cross-sectional cohort study. A prospective power calculation was done prior to recruitment to ensure validity of sample size which yielded a participant number of at least 50. We managed to recruit 75 undergraduate medical students. The students were spread across 3 clinical years (3rd to 5th year) from 2 medical schools. One medical school utilises cadeveric dissection and the other uses plastinated structures and virtual anatomy software as their respective key educational components in the anatomy curriculum. These students rotated through a single teaching hospital’s orthopaedic department. Domain Specific Review Board (DSRB) approval was obtained from the institution’s National Health Group (NHG) prior to commencement of the project.

Participants first viewed a patients set of computed tomography images of a large pelvic osteosarcoma from a free open-source database, the Cancer Genome Atlas Sarcoma Collection (TCGA-SARC) Refer to Fig. 1 for the open-source CT images.

Segmentation and 3D printing preparation

The CT images in DICOM format were viewed and segmented using Materialise Mimics software (Materialise NV, Leuven, Belgium) to create a surface rendered model of the anatomy by a trained biomedical engineer. The model was reviewed and verified by a consultant physician. The digital model was exported via the Standard Triangle Language (STL) file format and prepared for 3D printing in Materialise 3-matic software (Materialise NV, Leuven, Belgium) by splitting it into various parts and designing connections for final assembly. This was necessary to fit the available build size of the 3D printers and for convenience of transporting the model. The individual pieces were then exported to various 3D printing slicer software for 3D printing (see Table 1). Segmentation and processing of the parts took about a full working day for the trained biomedical engineer.

3D printing and assembly

The individual pieces of the model were printed on various 3D printers and various materials at the local institution (see Table 1). The materials chosen included rigid filaments such as PLA (polylactic acid) and PETG (Polyethylene Terephthalate Glycol), elastic materials (Stratasys Elastico) and multicolour materials (Vero series, Stratasys) in order to optimise the accuracy of the model with respect to accurately displaying the anatomical relationships. The various pieces were printed in parallel across 3 different 3D printers to save time for a total of approximately 30 h of print time.

Once printing was completed all the constituent parts were post processed to remove support structures and assembled, taking approximately 6 h.

This 3D model displays the oncological pathology, surrounding soft tissue such as the relevant muscles, great vessels, ureters, kidneys etc. as well as the bony structures. Different anatomical structures were colour coded respectively to provide a clear anatomical relationship as seen in Figs. 2, 3 and 4. Moreover, various structures were made removable to allow participants to appreciate the spatial anatomy.

The students were recruited between the duration of March 2023 - September 2023 in various group sizes (range of 12–18 students per session) and each session was conducted in a tutorial room. A standardised 10-minute pre-intervention questionnaire comprising 15 questions categorised by: 4 questions in ‘anatomical knowledge and characteristics’, 7 questions in ‘spatial anatomy and 3D spatial awareness’, 4 questions in ‘surgical planning and complications’, was administered to assess the participants’ baseline knowledge in their interpretation of the pathology via CT images only. The minimum possible score was 0 and the maximum possible score was 15. These categories were used to compartmentalise our questions based on their anatomical understanding, imaging interpretation skills and clinical surgical application. These questions were set with reference to published literature [13–16]. The papers reflected a recurring theme of testing anatomical knowledge by identifying key structures involved in the pathology and asked questions on the potential challenges and complications relevant to the anatomy and possible intervention required. Next, the 3D-printed model of the pelvic osteosarcoma was provided as an adjunct tool to answer the same questionnaire again. This was considered the post-intervention assessment questionnaire. Lastly, participants were asked to complete a feedback survey consisting of six 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree) questions. The questions were framed on four main factors of motivation, barriers, learning enhancers and discouragers. This survey was used to gauge their perspectives and experience of the effectiveness of the 3D model. During the whole conduct, each student had individual access to the CT images via their personal electronic device (phone, tablet or laptop). These images were shared just prior to the commencement of the survey. There was only 1 3D model that was provided to the group which the students could access anytime during the test period. The students were not allowed to discuss with other participants at any point in time during the conduct and were strictly given 10 min to complete each set of questionnaires.

Statistical analysis

The data was compiled, and statistical analysis was executed via Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS), version (23), (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY) and Microsoft Excel, version (16), (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA). A 2-tailed t-test statistical analysis was conducted to compare the relative mean scores and standard deviations across the whole cohort and amongst the respective clinical years between their pre- and post-intervention assessment questionnaires. Cohen’s d was calculated to analyse the effect size of the 3 respective domains of the survey. The cohort was further subdivided into baseline weaker students (pre-intervention assessment score ≤ 8) against baseline stronger students (pre-intervention assessment test score ≥ 9) and compared the respective impact based on their score differences. It is assumed that the sample mean follows a normal distribution.

Results

There were 51 and 24 undergraduate students from each medical school respectively. There were 44 year 3s (M3s), 7 year 4s (M4s), 24 year 5s (M5s) in total. The students were assumed to have learnt basic CT interpretation and anatomy knowledge based on their pre-clinical medical curriculum. A considerable improvement in post-intervention assessment results is shown in Fig. 5. The mean scores for the entire cohort of students comparing their pre- and post-intervention assessment questionnaire increased by + 1.34 from 8.15 (SD = 1.85) to 9.49 (SD = 1.7) (p 0.001). The lowest and highest pre-intervention assessment scores were 4/15 and 12/15 respectively. The M5s had the greatest improvement of + 1.54 from 8.00 (SD = 1.89) to 9.54 (SD = 1.59) (p = 0.004). Notably, M3s had the highest pre- and post-intervention assessment scores with an increase of + 1.25 from 8.41 (SD = 1.69) to 9.66 (SD = 1.67) (p = 0.001). There was no significant difference comparing the scores of the students between the 2 medical schools pre-intervention assessment (p = 0.094) and post-intervention assessment (p = 0.791).

In the analysis of respective domains, the increase in mean total correct answers per question for the domain of “Surgical utility/planning and complications” had a statistically significant increase of + 6.50 from 26.75 (SD = 22.07) to 33.25 (SD = 21.23) (p = 0.030). Though the rest of the domain analysis was not statistically significant, the highest mean total correct answers in the pre- intervention assessment and post-intervention assessment per question was in the domain of “Spatial anatomy and 3D spatial awareness” with a pre- intervention assessment mean of 48.57 (SD = 17.38) and post-intervention assessment mean score of 55.71 (SD = 19.24) (p = 0.084). Next, the domain of “Anatomical knowledge and characteristic” had the largest increase in the mean total correct answers from the pre- intervention assessment to post-intervention assessment scores of + 9.50 from 37.75 (SD = 17.82) to 47.25 (SD = 15.13). In terms of effect size, the domain of “Anatomical knowledge and characteristic” provided the largest effect size of moderate (Cohen’s d = 0.57, p = 0.229, 95% CI 0.24–0.90). The second and third domain provided a small-moderate (Cohen’s d = 0.39, p = 0.084, 95% CI 0.07–0.72) and small effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.30, p = 0.030, 95% CI 0.02–0.62) respectively. However, only the effect in domain 3 was statistically significant. Collectively, the effect size of our study produced at Cohen’s d calculation of 0.76 (p < 0.001, 95% CI: 0.43–1.09), suggesting a statistically significant moderate-large effect size. In the analysis of the score differences of the baseline weaker and stronger groups, students in the baseline weaker group (pre-intervention assessment score ≤ 8) demonstrated a greater increase in scores compared with students in the baseline stronger group (pre-intervention assessment score ≥ 9). The mean increase in score was 2.04 (SD = 1.51) in the baseline weaker group and 0.30 (SD = 1.90) in the baseline stronger group (p = 0.001), suggesting that students with lower baseline performance benefited more from the introduction of the 3D Model. Although our data was not formally analysed qualitatively, we noticed some recurring themes in the comments. Some notable comments include; “Good experience, colour coding made the model clearer, good that we could detach structures”, “The 3D model does help in visualisation, but the CT images are much more comprehensive, hence they should be used in conjunction”, “3D model was much more engaging & vivid in reflecting the pathology. Ultimately, the results display quantitative evidence that the 3D model effectively enhances the teaching of surgical anatomy in this aspect of musculoskeletal oncology.

Discussion

Anatomy education remains a difficult yet fundamental knowledge in every clinician’s journey. With the attained knowledge, it is an essential skill to correlate with every unique patient specific pathology. Classically, the use of cadaveric models has been the gold-standard educational tool [6, 17]. With the advent of technology, 3D printing is a contentious but potentially effective alternative to substitute the resource intensive cadaveric model and augment our clinical practice, be it in surgical education, pre-operative planning and even surgical related procedures [6, 10, 11, 18–20] Currently, there are numerous studies investigating the use of 3D printed models to train residents, with a few exploring the effectiveness of 3D models in the teaching of anatomy in medical schools [15]. Our study delved into the potential of 3D printed models to augment the learning experience of undergraduate students in musculoskeletal oncology.

Participants had considered the use of 3D models as more “engaging” and “comprehensive” than the CT images. With respect to anatomy education, there has been evidence supporting the use of 3D printing over 2D images as well as an adjunct to cadaveric dissection [18]. The tactile and visual nature of these models promote interactive learning which increases their engagement. A study by Mogali et al. supported this finding, where a multi-coloured, multi-material 3D model was used to teach upper limb anatomy. It was deemed as a valuable adjunct in teaching anatomy in comparison to wet cadaveric or plastinated prosections [13]. The increased engagement may reflect a stronger teaching utility that 3D models can provide in improving a student’s learning environment to better understand the anatomy in comparison to other methods. This preferred perception of a positive learning environment could have a beneficial impact on medical students’ academic and professional development. Considering that basic anatomy education and visualisation is addressed by the utilisation of cadaveric courses or plasticine models in the respective schools, the use of 3D models can potentially be more applicable for complex cases.

With respect to clinical care, 3D printed models have generally established an impact in various stages of patient care and medical education. The models provide the visual anatomical extent of a pathology which can be used as a pre-operative planning or simulation. Intra-operatively, 3D printing has aided in the development of surgical tools to accurately guide the excision or management of a pathology such as soft tissue sarcoma, bone or spinal tumours [10, 11, 16, 21]. Post-operatively, it aids in defining the bone defect to plan for prosthetic use if applicable [11]. Evidently, the 3D-printed models enhance the visualisation of sophisticated pathologies and the intricate spatial relationships with the surrounding structures to execute complicated patient specific surgical procedures.

In musculoskeletal oncology, previous studies have used 3D models to teach pathologies such as soft tissue sarcomas of the thigh and bone tumours [11, 22]. The latter study solely produced a 3D model displaying the bone pathology itself without neighbouring important structures to a group of 32 first year medical students who had no prior education towards bone tumours [11]. The former study produced full-colour 3D models displaying the pathology, surrounding vessels and muscles. These models aided in enhancing final year medical students in their understanding of the surgical anatomy as well as preoperative planning and intraoperative navigation amongst the surgical team involved [12]. To strengthen the current literature our study was targeted at not only producing a comprehensive full-colour 3D model involving the pathology itself, but also the important surrounding vessels, muscles and organs. Moreover, to supplement the learning, we have also incorporated the clinical skill of CT interpretation which can enhance the students’ learning not only in their understanding of the surgical anatomy but a general background of their radiology knowledge [23]. Ultimately, our current study has involved the largest demographic of medical students spread over 3 clinical years, explored the use of a very comprehensive 3D model and tapped onto radiology knowledge as well. Our results show that there is a significant improvement, particularly in the ‘Anatomical knowledge and characteristics’ domain based on our test score analysis. Moreover, in a subgroup analysis, our 3D model revealed a greater improvement in test scores among lower-performing students. This suggest that the 3D model confers a particular benefit to this cohort. has provided a visual-spatial supplementary resource to address the questionnaire. This may be attributed to the model’s intention to provide a visual-spatial learning aid, thereby accommodating students with a predominantly visual learning style [24]. These findings align with the intended purpose of the 3D model — to enhance visualisation of the relevant pathology, reinforce anatomical comprehension, and develop spatial awareness. Collectively, the results provide quantitative evidence supporting the educational value of 3D models in teaching musculoskeletal oncology, contributing to the growing body of literature on technology-enhanced anatomical education.

To further interrogate our findings, we evaluated year-specific interval changes across the three clinical cohorts. M5 students demonstrated the largest gain, with a mean increase of 1.54 (from 8.00 ± 1.89 to 9.54 ± 1.59; p = 0.004). In contrast, M3 students achieved the highest absolute scores at both time points, improving by 1.25 (from 8.41 ± 1.69 to 9.66 ± 1.67; p = 0.001). These observed differences are likely reflective of variation in curricular exposure and knowledge retention across training stages. M3 students, being closest to formal anatomy training in M1 and M2, likely retained stronger foundational knowledge in orthopaedic anatomy. In comparison, M4 students typically lack a dedicated orthopaedic rotation, with exposure limited to an optional elective posting during their respective elective block within the respective school curriculums. Although M5 students re-engage with orthopaedic surgery during a formal clinical posting, this phase is predominantly focused on applied clinical practice and preparation to transition as a house officer, with relatively less emphasis on fundamental anatomical concepts.

In terms of the technical aspect of 3D production, it can be argued that 3D printing incurs an initial large capital as it requires a computer system, professional 3D printer and manpower services [19]. With the advent of technology and benefits from the economies of scale, the production costs have been decreasing coupled with improved integration and accuracy and reduced construction time. As such, 3D printing remains in good stead to be a cost-effective educational tool [22]. Moreover, in comparison to the gold standard use with cadaveric dissection, we avoid the resource intensive process to obtain the cadaver, the strict storage requirements as well as the ethical issues surrounding its usage [3]. Though there has been a lack of literature showing a direct comparison between the 2 methods in teaching surgical pathologies, 3D printing can potentially be a practical alternative in anatomy education in musculoskeletal oncology.

Furthermore, in terms of the software specific use, our institution used various commercial software in the preparation of the model. However, other open-source software may also be used to lower the resource burden. In particular, Mimics Materialise may be easily substituted with the popular open-source segmentation software 3D slicer (https://www.slicer.org/, Brigham and Women’s Hospital) in nonclinical use [25]. Materialise 3-matic may also be substituted by open-source software Autodesk Meshmixer (San Rafael, CA, Autodesk ) or Blender (Blender Foundation, Amsterdam, Netherlands) in preparation of the 3D model.

Additionally, the Stratasys Polyjet 3D printer used in this work is a relatively expensive piece of equipment that may not be readily available to all institutions; however since this work was done with newer low-cost 3D printers capable of multicolour/multimaterial 3D printing such as the Bambulabs X1, Prusa XL, or the Snapmaker U1, this has lowered the prospective cost substantially. Elastic filaments such as Thermoplastic Polyurethane (TPU) can be used to substitute for various elastic polyjet materials of higher cost although careful consideration of the print parameters will be needed for success.

Considering the advancement in technology, we would also like to acknowledge Virtual Reality (VR) as another potential alternative adjunct for anatomy education. Zhao et al. conducted a meta-analysis of controlled studies to assess the utility of VR in teaching medical anatomy. The consensus was that VR improves spatial understanding and learner engagement compared with traditional methods. However, the study is constrained by the absence of standardised outcome measures and significant inter-study heterogeneity [26]. In another study, Hammouda et al., created and tested their own VR tool for their own anatomy education which included virtual 3D anatomical models. The study showed positive receptiveness towards the benefit that VR can provide in terms of precise visualisation and spatial awareness coupled with the integration of CT/MRI images, which suggests an innovative initiative towards improving medical education [27].

Future insights

Moving forward, we can explore stronger collaboration with medical engineering and innovation to fine tune the manufacturing process to strive towards accuracy and cost-effectiveness. Moreover, we can perform longitudinal studies to further assess outcomes and evaluate knowledge retention amongst the participants. With these studies, we can build upon the current evidence to create more comprehensive structures to better replicate the complete anatomical pathology of interest and improve our understanding in terms of spatial relationships as well as its respective identification when viewing radiological 2D imaging. This can be done in conjunction with longitudinal studies to evaluate its long-term impact such as knowledge retention. Moreover, these studies can establish a controlled environment to directly compare 3D models against other teaching methods for the same content such as the use of plasticine models or cadaveric bodies. In the technical manufacturing process, we can experiment with various materials to better replicate the tactile quality and physical properties of the essential structures to improve pre-operative planning [28].

To build upon the survey feedback, we can further assess the use of combining radiological modalities, such as MRI and CT imaging, with 3D models to develop a more holistic approach in teaching surgical anatomy. 2D imaging may provide more detailed information on the fine vasculature and detailed anatomy of the pathology. Whereas the 3D model can provide a better visualisation of the overall spatial alignment. As such, future studies can seek to evaluate a more cohesive approach in integrating the skill of radiological interpretation to co-relate the precise 2D imaging details with the overall spatial visualisation that 3D models can provide. Our findings suggest that 3D models can play a part to augment the understanding and application of clinical anatomy. This can be integrated into the current pre-clinical anatomy curriculum involving problem-based learning. In so doing, this better facilitates the transference of clinical knowledge from didactic teaching and apply to clinical cases to improve the retention & conceptual understanding in anatomy [29].

Limitations

The results have shown strong evidence in supporting the use of 3D printed models in teaching musculoskeletal oncology. However, we have to acknowledge the limitations that were present in this study. Firstly, we were unable to standardise the pre-clinical year anatomy training as the undergraduates were recruited from 2 different medical schools with 2 different anatomy teaching curriculums as explained earlier. An analysis was done of the scores between the 2 schools, however the results were statistically insignificant. Ultimately, the main aim of our study was not to test the pre-intervention test scores amongst the different schools but on the interval change in questionnaire score. This interval change reflects how each participant, irrespective of previous anatomy curriculum, had improved scores on their post-intervention assessment questionnaire. We also acknowledge the variable of learning effect on the interval change of the questionnaire score considering that it is the participants’ second attempt at the same questionnaire.

In terms of the technical aspect of our survey, our survey instrument was not validated, and the difficulty level was not piloted prior. As this is the first study of our institution with a limited sample size, we took reference from the recurrent themes from prior studies to develop the survey with the included domains. Our survey was further verified by a musculoskeletal oncology consultant. By building upon the evidence from this study, future studies can seek to validate their respective survey and scale up to include a wider cohort of students to better determine the difficulty levels.

Lastly, the study is a SCCS, hence it lacks a separate control group within this study. We acknowledge that the exposure to the intervention of the 3D model is short, thus it may not significantly impact knowledge retention. However, the primary objective of the present study was to evaluate the immediate impact of the 3D model on students’ spatial understanding and initial comprehension of pathological anatomy, rather than long-term retention of knowledge. As such, the study was designed to have a pre-intervention assessment to assess the student’s baseline knowledge and a post-intervention assessment within the same setting to assess the short- term educational outcomes following exposure to the model as accurately as possible.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our current study findings provide quantitative evidence to support the current positive evidence of 3D models as a teaching adjunct in Musculoskeletal Oncology. The results from our paper can be practically implemented in pre-clinical anatomy teaching curriculum such as integrating 3D models with clinical cases in a problem-based teaching session. Moreover, this could be extrapolated to post-graduate level where residents can use 3D models to aid in pre-op planning, for which however, more evidence is required. This study has shown the effectiveness of a more comprehensive 3D printed model in musculoskeletal oncology pathology in enhancing the learning experience, engagement, particularly in the domain of ‘Anatomical knowledge and characteristics’ and for students with baseline weaker knowledge understanding.
