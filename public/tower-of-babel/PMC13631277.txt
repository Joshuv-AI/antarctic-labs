Efficient recovery of zebrafish maternal-zygotic mutants from in vivo expanded and cryopreserved germline stem cells
Authors: Yaqing Wang, Rui Qu, Xiaosi Wang, Yongkang Hao, Jiajie Deng, Houpeng Wang, Yonghua Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Long-term preservation of genetic lines is crucial for animal research. Current methods heavily rely on cryopreservation of haploid sperm, whereas diploid germline stem cells (GSCs) may provide a superior alternative. Here, using zebrafish, we establish an integrated platform combining in vivo expansion of GSCs (eGSCs), cryopreservation and direct genetic recovery. The eGSCs are amplified in cyp11a2-/- mutants and exhibit a total number of about 300-fold higher than conventional GSCs after cryopreservation and thawing. The eGSCs achieve a success rate of 82% after transplantation, compared with less than 1% for conventional GSCs. Furthermore, we develop the GSC-deficient nanos2-/- mutant as an ideal host for eGSC transplantation, enabling efficient production of both sperm and oocytes within a single maturation period. This allows rapid recovery of maternal-zygotic mutants directly in the F1 generation. Overall, our strategy provides a robust platform for preserving and restoring zebrafish homozygous mutants, opening new opportunities for zebrafish research.

Introduction

Zebrafish (Danio rerio) has emerged as a pivotal vertebrate model for developmental and biomedical research, due to its transparent embryos, ease of genetic manipulation, well-annotated genome, and over 85% genetic homology to humans1,2. During the past decades, the establishment of numerous mutant and transgenic lines has greatly advanced our understanding of vertebrate genetics3. To safeguard these invaluable genetic resources, sperm cryopreservation has been widely employed by major zebrafish resource centers, including China Zebrafish Resource Center (CZRC, China), Zebrafish International Resource Center (ZIRC, USA), and European Zebrafish Resource Center (EZRC, Germany)4,5.

However, sperm cryopreservation preserves only the haploid paternal genome and presents several limitations: limited sperm yield per male, the need for large-scale screening, and the inefficient recovery of homozygous mutants—only 25% in single-gene and 1.56% in triple-gene mutants of F2 individuals. Furthermore, reconstruction of maternal-zygotic (MZ) mutants for genes with maternal contributions, such as nanog (nanog homeobox) and pou5f3 (POU domain, class 5, transcription factor 3), typically requires two generations of breeding, extending recovery times to at least two generation periods of six months6,7. These constraints underscore the need for diploid-level germline preservation strategies that can enable rapid and complete strain reconstruction.

Currently, oocyte and embryo cryopreservation remain technically challenging in zebrafish, primarily due to their high yolk content and low permeability to cryoprotectants. In contrast, cryopreservation of germline stem cells (GSCs) combined with transplantation offers a promising alternative for preserving diploid genomes and reproductive capacity8,9. GSCs can be obtained from juvenile or adult individuals and are relatively accessible, which possess stable self-renewal capacity and the ability to differentiate into either oocytes or sperm, making them an important target for cryopreservation10–12.

Cryopreservation and subsequent transplantation techniques for testicular tissue cryopreservation including GSCs have already been successfully established in mammals, such as mice and rabbits13,14, and have been explored for preserving the genetic resources of endangered fish species, including Acipenser sinensis, Polyodon spathula, and Tokyo bitterling15–17. Notably, significant progress has been made in aquaculture species like Cyprinus carpio, rainbow trout, and Plecoglossus altivelis18–20, but challenges remain in model organisms such as zebrafish21. Two major limitations continue to hinder the broader application of GSC cryopreservation and transplantation: (1) low concentration and viability of cryopreserved GSC, resulting in suboptimal transplantation efficiency, and (2) the lack of suitable host models capable of producing donor-derived oocytes22.

Our previous work demonstrated that zebrafish blastula cells can be reprogrammed into induced primordial germ cells (iPGCs), effectively increasing PGCs availability22. However, scalable production of functional GSCs, particularly after cryopreservation, remains an unresolved challenge. We further identified that cyp11a2−/− mutants, which exhibit impaired steroidogenic somatic cell function, maintain GSCs in an undifferentiated state and promote their accumulation23. This observation raises a fundamental question: is GSC self-renewal regulated by the somatic microenvironment? Moreover, can cyp11a2−/− mutants serve as in vivo expander of allogeneic GSCs?

Host selection is another major bottleneck in germline transplantation. Effective sterilization of host germ cells is essential to ensure donor germline exclusivity24,25. Thus, endogenous germ cells of host were eliminated commonly by dead end antisense morpholino (dnd1_MO) injection at 1-cell stage, germline-deficient mutants, and triploid induction to improve donor cell engraftment15,21,26–28. However, zebrafish lack sex chromosomes, elimination of endogenous germ cells early in development typically results in all-male phenotypes, preventing female microenvironment establishment and donor-derived egg formation21,26. A critical challenge is selecting an optimal host that can ensure both efficient colonization and the production of functional donor-derived sperm and oocytes.

In this study, we established a comprehensive zebrafish GSC preservation system integrating in vivo expansion of GSCs, cryopreservation of expanded GSCs(eGSCs), and GSCT transplantation. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first demonstration of robust expansion of allogeneic GSCs in cyp11a2−/− hosts. Using nanos2−/− mutants as hosts, we achieved the production of both donor-derived sperm and eggs, including the reconstruction of 100% MZnanog embryos directly in the F1 generation within three months—far faster than conventional sperm cryopreservation approaches. Beyond accelerating conservation, this strategy offers a powerful tool to generate zygotic, maternal-zygotic lethal, and complex multi-gene mutants, opening new avenues for genetic research and biomedical innovation.

Results

Allogeneic testicular GSCs undergo robust in vivo expansion in cyp11a2−/− hosts

To establish an efficient strategy for zebrafish testicular GSC (also known as spermatogonial stem cell, SSC) preservation, we compared two widely used cryopreservation approaches: direct freezing of isolated GSCs and cryopreservation of intact testicular tissue (TT) (Fig. 1A). Viability assays demonstrated that GSCs preserved within intact testes (thawed TT group) exhibited markedly higher post-thaw survival and live cell concentration than isolated GSCs, although both were lower than fresh controls (Figs. 1B and S1A, B). These results indicate that the intact testicular microenvironment provides a more favorable context for GSC preservation than dissociated cells. To improve recovery efficiency, we next optimized the cryoprotectant composition. Supplementation with 100 mM trehalose in L-15 medium further enhanced viability, identifying this formulation as optimal (Fig. S1C). Despite these improvements, cryopreserved GSCs yielded only ~30 ± 4 transplantable hosts per donor (success rate: 0.71 ± 1.42%), substantially lower than that achieved with freshly isolated GSCs (Fig. 1C, D). To address this limitation, we explored whether cyp11a2–/– mutants, previously reported to maintain germ cells in an undifferentiated state23, could serve as in vivo expanders of allogeneic GSCs. GSCs isolated from Tg(ddx4: EGFP) donors were transplanted into cyp11a2−/− hosts, and donor cell dynamics were monitored longitudinally. Donor-derived EGFP⁺ cells successfully colonized the genital ridge by 3 days post-transplantation (dpt) and established early gonadal structures by 30 dpt (Fig. 1E). At 4 months post-transplantation (mpt), EGFP-positive testes were detected in 21.99 ± 3.13% of WT hosts and in 24.87 ± 2.09% of cyp11a2−/− hosts (Figs. 1F and S1D). Notably, testes in cyp11a2−/− hosts were markedly enlarged (Fig. 1F, G), suggesting enhanced donor GSC expansion. To assess donor cell fate, dual immunofluorescence for Ddx4 (strong positivity indicating GSCs) and Sycp3 (meiotic marker) was performed. In WT hosts, most donor-derived germ cells co-expressed Ddx4 and Sycp3, indicating meiotic entry. In contrast, testes from cyp11a2−/− hosts predominantly contained strong Ddx4-positive, Sycp3-negative cells, indicating retention in the undifferentiated GSC state (Fig. 1H). Quantitative analyses confirmed a significantly higher proportion of GSCs and GSC contents per fish in cyp11a2−/− hosts compared to WT (Fig. 1I, J). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that cyp11a2−/− mutants provide a supportive microenvironment that maintains transplanted allogeneic GSCs in a self-renewing state, resulting in substantial in vivo expansion (Fig. 1K).

To evaluate whether the expanded population retained GSC identity, immunostaining was performed using an antibody against Nanos2, a conserved germline stem cell marker. The host_cyp11a2−/− group exhibited a striking enrichment of Nanos2-positive cells, reaching 257.2 ± 13.5 × 10³ compared with only 0.78 ± 0.20 × 10³ cells in controls—a ~ 344-fold increase (Fig. 2D–F). These results confirmed that the cyp11a2−/− environment selectively supported the self-renewal and accumulation of undifferentiated GSCs. Following cryopreservation, GSCs derived from cyp11a2−/−-expanded testes demonstrated robust colonization after transplantation into dnd1_MO hosts. EGFP fluorescence revealed extensive donor-derived cell distribution across the genital ridges at both 3 and 30 dpt, substantially exceeding the signal observed in WT-derived controls. Quantitatively, GSCs from the host_cyp11a2−/− group supported transplantation into 670 ± 60 hosts per donor—approximately 27-fold higher than controls (25 ± 4)—and achieved a higher transplantation success rate of 82.0 ± 3.0% versus 0.95 ± 1.65% (Fig. 2G–I).

All dnd1_MO hosts matured as functional males with semen volumes comparable to WT fish (Fig. 2J). Moreover, immunofluorescence confirmed co-localization of EGFP and Ddx4 signals in testes, indicating that thawed donor-derived GSCs had undergone complete spermatogenic differentiation (Fig. 2K). Importantly, donor-derived sperm were fully functional: when host males were crossed with WT females, EGFP-positive progeny were obtained with normal fertilization rates (Fig. 2L, M), confirming successful germline transmission. Taken together, these results demonstrate that allogeneic GSCs expanded in cyp11a2–/– hosts markedly enhance transplantation efficiency post-cryopreservation.

The nanos2−/− hosts enable efficient generation of donor-derived functional oocytes

Although GSC expansion significantly improved transplantation efficiency after cryopreservation, dnd1_MO host zebrafish failed to produce donor-derived oocytes, as previously described29. In dnd1 morphants, all germ cells are ablated before 24 hpf, which leads to exclusive male development30 and thus prevents the formation of a female gonadal environment to support oocyte differentiation. In contrast, nanos2−/− mutants retain a normal number of germ cells during early-to-mid larval stages, allowing female development31. We found that nanos2−/− females could undergo one to two rounds of oogenesis shortly after sexual maturation, but rapidly became completely germ cell-depleted, with no oogonia detected according to Ddx4 immunofluorescence (Fig. S3A–F). These observations indicate that nanos2−/− mutants are capable of establishing a stable ovarian somatic environment during early development. This early-stage ovarian somatic microenvironment, together with the subsequent loss of endogenous germ cells after early oogenesis, provides a favorable niche for transplanted GSCs to differentiate into functional donor-derived oocytes. Furthermore, germ cells are also completely absent in male nanos2−/− mutants (Fig. S3G, H). We therefore hypothesized that nanos2−/− mutants could serve as efficient hosts for GSC transplantation to reconstitute both male and female gametes.

To test this hypothesis, GSCs isolated from Tg(ddx4: EGFP) donors, previously expanded in cyp11a2−/− host, were transplanted into 4 dpf nanos2−/− larvae without dnd1_MO injection, and the engraftment and subsequent development were examined (Fig. 3A). At 3 dpt, donor-derived GSCs aggregated along the genital ridge, and the EGFP fluorescence signal progressively expanded at 15 and 30 dpt, and by 45 and 80 dpt, both male and female hosts were identified (Fig. 3B). In contrast to dnd1_MO hosts, which developed exclusively as males, nanos2−/− hosts yielded 35.43 ± 2.08% females (Fig. 3C). At 90 dpt, strong EGFP fluorescence was detected throughout the ovaries of nanos2−/− female hosts, indicating efficient colonization and differentiation of donor-derived GSCs (Fig. 3D). In WT host ovaries, only scattered EGFP-positive germ cells were observed (Fig. 3D, E). In the ovaries of nanos2−/− females, all oocytes exhibited co-localized green (donor-derived EGFP) and red (Ddx4) signals after one spawning event, confirming complete donor origin (Fig. 3F, G). To verify gamete functionality, nanos2−/− females were naturally mated with WT males, and fluorescence analysis of F1 progeny was conducted at 5 dpf (Fig. 3H). During the first spawning event (83 dpt), 33.70 ± 3.57% of embryos exhibited germline-specific EGFP fluorescence, consistent with the spatial expression pattern of Tg(ddx4: EGFP) (Fig. 3I–K), confirming the donor-derived origin of the oocytes. Strikingly, from the second spawning onward, all embryos were EGFP-positive, and this pattern persisted in subsequent weekly spawnings (Fig. 3I–K). Fertility assays revealed that nanos2−/− female hosts exhibited spawning capacity and fertilization rates comparable to those of WT controls (Fig. 3L, M). In contrast, WT female hosts produced very few donor-derived embryos, underscoring the superior suitability of nanos2−/− females as transplantation hosts (Fig. 3I–K). Moreover, nanos2−/− male hosts were able to produce functional donor-derived sperm from thawed donor GSCs, which successfully generated offspring (Fig. S3I–L).

Together, these findings demonstrate that nanos2−/− mutants can serve as an effective dual-sex transplantation model that supports the continuous production of functional donor-derived gametes, without the need for dnd1_MO–mediated germ cell ablation. This system provides a robust and broadly applicable strategy for accelerating the generation of maternal and MZ mutants from cryopreserved GSCs.

Rapid recovery of MZnanog embryos via eGSC cryopreservation and transplantation

To evaluate the broader utility of GSC-based cryopreservation and transplantation, we applied this strategy to nanog, a classical maternal-effect gene. It has been reported that zygotic mutants (Znanog) develop normally, whereas MZnanog exhibit severe early embryonic defects6. The experimental protocol consisted of the expansion and cryopreservation of nanog-/- GSCs, followed by the rapid recovery of MZnanog embryos (Fig. 4A). Transplantation of nanog-/- GSCs into cyp11a2-/- hosts produced enlarged donor-derived testes (Figs. 4B, C and S4A), which retained high viability and live cell concentration following 6 months of cryopreservation (Fig. S4B, C). When the eGSCs transplanted into nanos2−/− hosts, both the number of transplantable hosts and the transplantation success rate were markedly increased compared with WT controls (Fig. S4E–G). Crosses between male and female nanos2−/− hosts yielded progeny were designated MZnanog_eGSCT (Fig. 4A). These embryos consistently displayed the characteristic developmental defects of MZnanog mutants, which were partially rescued by nanog mRNA injection (Fig. 4F, G). Importantly, both sexes of nanos2−/− hosts generated fertile donor-derived gametes across multiple spawnings (Fig. S4H, I).

It is well established that Nanog plays a critical role in the maternal-zygotic transition (MZT), facilitating the activation of zygotic genes and the clearance of maternal genes. To further validate the molecular authenticity of the MZnanog_eGSCT phenotype obtained using this method, transcriptomic analysis was performed on 4 hpf (hour post fertilization) MZnanog_eGSCT embryos (eGSCT for short), with WT and MZnanog serving as controls. Venn diagram analysis revealed that 4,903 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were shared with conventional MZnanog embryos relative to WT, highlighting a similar gene expression pattern (Fig. 4H). Hierarchical clustering further emphasized the close similarity in gene expression patterns between eGSCT and MZnanog, particularly in genes associated with stem cell maintenance and organ differentiation (Fig. 4I). When compared to WT, nanog expression was found to be significantly reduced in both MZnanog and eGSCT embryos. Additionally, the expression levels of zygotic genes such as dharma, mxxt2, mir-430, bmp2b, gsc, and wnt8a were downregulated in both MZnanog and eGSCT, indicating that zygotic gene activation had failed (Fig. 4J). In contrast, maternal genes such as lin28a, nanos1, sox2, sox19b, kfl4, and gys1 were upregulated, suggesting that maternal gene clearance was incomplete (Fig. 4K). KEGG pathway enrichment showed extensive overlap between eGSCT vs WT and MZnanog vs WT, including stem cell pluripotency and axon guidance pathways (Fig. S4J, K).

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that GSC expansion, cryopreservation, and transplantation enable faithful reconstruction of MZnanog mutants, recapitulating both the phenotypic and transcriptomic features of maternal-zygotic deficiency in the F1 generation. Compared with traditional sperm cryopreservation, which requires at least three generations (6 months) to regenerate MZ mutants, integration of eGSC cryopreservation and transplantation reduces the regeneration time by half.

Discussion

In this study, we established an integrated system for GSC in vivo expansion, cryopreservation, and transplantation in zebrafish, enabling the simultaneous recovery of both donor-derived sperm and oocytes from cryopreserved eGSCs (Fig. 5). To our knowledge, this is the first demonstration of successful in vivo expansion of allogeneic GSCs in vertebrates. One major innovation of this work is the use of cyp11a2−/− mutants as in vivo expander of GSCs (Fig. 5). These mutants provide a stable and undifferentiated testicular niche that promotes robust self-renewal of testicular GSCs, previously known as spermatogonial stem cells (SSCs), thereby overcoming a key bottleneck in GSC cryopreservation—insufficient viable cells post-thawing for transplantation32. While previous studies explored the in vitro GSC expansion in several fish species, maintaining long-term self-renewal without differentiation has proven difficult to achieve32–35. Efforts to reconstruct the microenvironment necessary for GSCs survival in vitro by incorporating serum, gonadal somatic cells, etc., have also fallen short of preserving their undifferentiated characteristics while achieving numerical expansion36–38. Consistent with recent findings23, we confirmed that cyp11a2−/− mutants naturally preserve GSCs in an undifferentiated state and, importantly, support the in vivo expansion of allogeneic GSCs. This resulted in nearly a 300-fold increase in viable GSCs after thawing, providing a scalable approach to overcome the quantitative bottleneck of GSC-based cryopreservation. Furthermore, with immune tolerance modification of cyp11a2−/− mutants, combined with the strategy of engineering Tregs-mediated immune tolerance via foxp3a overexpression recently reported39, this system is expected to be utilized for GSC expansion across fish species in the future.

Host selection proved equally critical for the success of GSC transplantation. While retaining endogenous germ cells provides a supportive gonadal microenvironment, competition for germ niches typically limits donor cell colonization25. This is consistent with our results observed in the WT hosts, which endogenous germ cells were not eliminated (Fig. 4D). One widely used strategy to enhance donor cell engraftment is the use of germ cell-deficient hosts, generated by approaches such as dnd1_MO morpholino injection or germline-deficient mutants, which eliminate endogenous germ cells and thereby provide vacant niches for donor cells, and improved the GSC transplantation success rate26,40. The transplantation efficiency in this study was lower than previously reported21, likely because fewer donor cells were transplanted per host, as donor cells in this study were derived from a single fish, whereas previous studies likely used multiple fish. A distinct strategy involves the use of triploid hosts, which are typically induced by retention of the second polar body after fertilization, results in sterility due to disrupted meiosis caused by an odd chromosome number27. In several fish species possessing sex chromosomes, including salmonids, goldfish, and grass puffer T. alboplumbeus, such strategies have enabled the successful production of donor-derived eggs through germ cell transplantation, demonstrating that undifferentiated testicular germ cells can differentiate into oocytes when transplanted into a female gonadal microenvironment18,19,27,41–43. However, in zebrafish, elimination of endogenous germ cells early in development typically results in all-male phenotypes, preventing the establishment of a female gonadal environment required for oogenesis26,29. In this context, nanos2−/− mutants offer a unique advantage. Previous studies have highlighted the critical role of germ cells, particularly oocytes, in female sex determination and the maintenance of the female state throughout development44–47. nanos2 is a conserved germline stem cell gene, which is crucial for the self-renewal of reproductive stem cells40,47–49. It has been reported that the loss of GSCs in nanos2−/− females leads to a failure of ovary regeneration and eventual sex reversal to males31. This result is consistent with our findings; although these fish lose oocytes over time, they transiently retain a female microenvironment during early development. We exploited this transient window to support GSC-derived oogenesis, demonstrating that donor GSCs can differentiate into functional oocytes as endogenous oocytes are lost. This mechanism effectively maintains the female fate and enables stable donor-derived egg production. Using nanos2−/− mutants as hosts, we successfully obtained functional donor-derived eggs and sperm in the F1 offspring within 3 months. The nanos2−/− host model therefore provides a robust and reproducible system for generating donor-derived oocytes in zebrafish, a long-standing challenge in teleost germline surrogacy research.

Beyond improving transplantation outcomes, this system markedly accelerates the reconstruction of MZ mutant lines. In this study, using nanos2−/− hosts, 100% MZnanog embryos were recovered in the F1 offspring. A direct comparison between traditional sperm cryopreservation and GSC-based cryopreservation (Fig. 5) further highlights the practical advantages of our approach. Unlike sperm cryopreservation, which necessitates sequential F1–F2–F3 breeding to restore MZ mutants (Fig. 5, lower left), GSC-based methods direct F₁ reconstruction while preserving diploid genetic integrity of donor fish. When thawed GSCs are transplanted into nanos2−/− hosts, donor-derived gametes contribute directly to both paternal and maternal lineages, enabling immediate production of 100% MZ mutants in the first generation (F1) (Fig. 5, lower right). This innovation shortens the recovery period to approximately three months, representing a more direct and efficient route for the restoration of complex genotypes.

Preservation of GSCs represents a key strategy for safeguarding genetic resources in vertebrates. Previous studies have reported cryopreservation of testicular tissue in fish has made significant progress18–20. In species possessing defined sex chromosomes, such as semelparous salmon, rainbow trout (Oncorhynchus mykiss), and medaka (Oryzias latipes), germ cells including GSCs have been shown to differentiate into sperm or eggs depending on the sex of the host, indicating their intrinsic bidirectional differentiation potential19,41,42,50. In the present study, we demonstrate that cryopreserved testicular GSCs are capable of generating both functional sperm and oocytes after transplantation into hosts of opposite sexes. These findings confirm the bidirectional developmental potential of testicular GSC and suggest that the germ cell population previously referred to as SSC should be more accurately defined as testicular GSC. Importantly, this GSC–based preservation system provides a conceptual and technical framework for the long-term conservation of genetic resources, including those from animal models and endangered species.

In conclusion, this study establishes a versatile framework for GSC-based germline preservation and genetic resource regeneration in zebrafish. By integrating GSC in vivo expansion, cryopreservation, and transplantation, this system enables diploid-level conservation and rapid recovery of complex genotypes. This system not only advances zebrafish genetic resource management but also can be extended to diverse teleost species, offering broad applications in aquaculture, biodiversity conservation, and biomedicine. This integrative strategy bridges fundamental germline biology and applied conservation technology, paving the way for next-generation germline engineering across vertebrates.

Experimental section

Zebrafish maintenance

The zebrafish used in this study were maintained at the China Zebrafish Resource Center (CZRC) under standard laboratory conditions (14-h light/10-h dark cycle, 28.5 °C). All wild-type, mutant, and transgenic zebrafish lines used in this study are on the AB background. The cytochrome P450 family 11 subfamily A member 2 (cyp11a2)ihb317/ihb317 line was genotyped using high-resolution melting (HRM) analysis; the nanog homeobox (nanog)ihb98/ihb98, and nanos homolog 2 (nanos2)zf3087/zf3087 lines were genotyped by direct Sanger sequencing; and positive individuals of the Tg(ddx4:EGFP)zf45Tg (where ddx4 is DEAD-box helicase 4) transgenic line were identified by fluorescence screening as reported previously6,23,31,51. The primers used for genotyping all mutant lines are listed in Supplementary Table S1. Before experiments, zebrafish were anesthetized with 0.2 mg/mL tricaine methane sulfonate (MS-222; Sigma) in aquatic water. All animal experiments involving zebrafish were reviewed and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Institute of Hydrobiology, Chinese Academy of Sciences (protocol number: IHB/LL/2022051). The experiments were performed in accordance with the approved protocols, and we have complied with all relevant ethical regulations for animal use.

Cryopreservation and thaw of zebrafish germ cells

To establish an optimized cryopreservation protocol, both testicular tissue fragments and isolated GSCs enriched by Percoll density gradient centrifugation were subjected to 6-month cryopreservation before viability assessment. Briefly, intact testes from adult zebrafish (n = 3) were collected, rinsed in L-15 medium, dissected into ~5 × 5 × 5 mm fragments, and transferred into cryogenic vials containing 500 µL cryopreservation medium with permeating cryoprotectants. Samples were pre-cooled on ice for 0.5–1 h, gradually cooled to −80 °C in a programmed freezing container overnight, and subsequently stored in liquid nitrogen. For isolated GSCs cryopreservation, dissociated testicular cells were enriched via Percoll gradient centrifugation, counted with a Neubauer chamber, and aliquoted (1 × 105 cells in 500 µL cryoprotectant) prior to freezing, following the same cooling and storage protocol. Thawing was performed in a 32 °C water bath for 2 min, followed by dilution in L-15 medium supplemented with 10% FBS to remove cryoprotectants. Subsequently, testicular tissues were then dissociated and GSCs were purified with or without Percoll enrichment as described previously29.

Assessment of testicular cells viability

During the cryopreservation protocol optimization, the enrichment of testicular cells and GSCs from one fish were performed as reported previously23. Briefly, testes were dissociated in 2 mL L-15 medium containing 0.25% trypsin (Invitrogen), 0.26 U liberase, 0.05% DNase I at 28 °C for 2–3 h with gentle shaking. The reaction was terminated by adding 10% FBS (v/v), filtering through 40 μm cell strainers (258369, NEST, China). GSCs or testicular cells were resuspended in L-15 medium. Cell viability was evaluated using acridine orange/propidium iodide (AO/PI) staining.

GSC and eGSC transplantation (GSCT and eGSCT)

For transplantation, donor cells from one fish enriched by Percoll density gradient centrifugation were designated as GSC transplantation (GSCT), while expanded GSC (eGSC) without Percoll density gradient centrifugation were designated as eGSC transplantation (eGSCT)23. Donor cells were labeled with CellTracker™ CM-DiI (C7000, Invitrogen) unless derived from Tg(ddx4: EGFP) fish, where fluorescence enabled direct tracking. Meanwhile, in addition to nanos2−/−, other transplant hosts, including WT and cyp11a2−/−, 100 nM dead end 1 (dnd1) antisense morpholino (dnd1_MO) oligonucleotide (5’-GCTGGGCATCCATGTCTCCGACCAT-3’) was injected into host embryos at the 1-cell stage to deplete endogenous PGCs according to previous report23,52. At 4 days post-fertilization (dpf), donor cells were transplanted into the genital ridge of hosts under a stereomicroscope. At 3 days post-transplantation (dpt), hosts with no detectable fluorescence were excluded, and the remaining larvae were reared to adulthood for fertility analysis.

Immunofluorescence assay

Immunostaining was performed on dissociated testicular cells and gonadal sections. For immunofluorescence staining, the following antibodies were used at a dilution of 1:500: anti-Vasa, anti-Nanos2, anti-Sycp3, and anti-Pcna. All antibodies were generated against zebrafish antigens and purified using antigen-affinity chromatography by our lab. The effectiveness and specificity of these antibodies have been fully described in a previous study53. Primary antibodies rabbit anti-Nanos2 (1:500, Home-made (HuaBio), RRID: AB_2895084; http://antibodyregistry.org/AB_2895084) has been previously applied in our published work23. Antirabbit Alexa Fluor 594 was used as secondary antibody (Molecular Probes, RRID: AB_141637; https://antibodyregistry.org/AB_141637) at a dilution of 1:1000. After fixation in 4% PFA and washing, samples were blocked in PBST (0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS, pH 7.4) containing 1% BSA and 0.1% DMSO for 1–3 h at room temperature (RT). Antibodies incubation was performed overnight at 4 °C, followed by DAPI (Beyotime, China) counterstaining for 1 h at RT. Samples were mounted with VECTASHIELD® and imaged using a Leica SP8 confocal microscope (SP8, Leica) with an LD C-Apo 40×/NA 1.1 water objective. A series of sections were obtained from at least 3 individual zebrafish for image analysis.

Evaluation fertile transplanted adults and F1 progeny tests

Transplanted adult hosts were naturally mated with WT zebrafish to evaluate spawning and fertilization rates. For progeny derived from Tg(ddx4: EGFP) donors, EGFP fluorescence localization in F1 embryos was examined at 5 dpf using a fluorescence stereomicroscope (Axio Zoom.V16, Zeiss). For progeny derived from nanog−/− donors, embryonic phenotypes were assessed at 6 hpf.

RNA sequencing and analysis

Total RNA was isolated from WT and transplanted zebrafish embryos at 4 hpf using RNA isolater Total RNA Extraction Reagent (Vazyme, China). RNA integrity was assessed using Nanodrop, Qubit, and Labchip GX. For each sample, 50 ng RNA was used for library preparation with the Universal V6 RNA-seq Library Prep Kit for Illumina (Vazyme, China). Sequencing was performed on an Illumina NextSeq 500 platform generating 150-bp paired-end reads. Reads were aligned to the zebrafish genome (GRCz11), quantified with featureCounts (v1.6.0), and analyzed using DESeq2 (v1.22.1). Differentially expressed genes were defined as |log2FC| ≥ 1.0 and adjusted P < 0.05. GO enrichment of DEGs was performed with ClusterProfiler (v4.0).

Histological analysis

For surgical procedures, adult zebrafish were anesthetized by immersion in 0.2 mg/mL MS-222 solution. For euthanasia, adult zebrafish were euthanized by immersion in a lethal dose of 2 mg/mL MS-222 solution, in accordance with institutional animal care and use committee guidelines. Ovarian tissues were dissected from WT and transplanted zebrafish at 3 months post-fertilization (3 mpf). Tissues were fixed overnight in 4% PFA dehydrated, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 4 µm thickness. Sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and imaged under an Olympus BX53 microscope.

Data analysis

All data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) from at least 3 independent biological replicates. Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism (version 9.0, GraphPad Software, USA). Differences between two groups were analyzed using an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test, while comparisons among multiple groups were evaluated using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Comparisons between two groups were made using an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test, while one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for multi-group comparisons. Statistical significance was defined as follows: *: P < 0.05, **: P < 0.01, and ***: P < 0.001, ****: P < 0.0001, ns: no significant difference. All graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism.

Statistics and reproducibility

All quantitative data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) from independent biological replicates. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (GraphPad, USA). Comparisons between two groups were performed using an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test, and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for multi-group comparisons. Significance thresholds were defined as *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, and ****P < 0.0001. All experiments were performed at least twice to ensure reproducibility.
