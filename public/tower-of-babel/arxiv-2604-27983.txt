Abstract
In this paper, we consider the Santa Claus problem in the 𝖢𝖮𝖭𝖦𝖤𝖲𝖳 \mathsf{CONGEST} model. This NP-hard problem can be modeled as a bipartite graph of children and gifts where an edge indicates that a child desires a gift. Notably, each gift can have a different value. The goal is to assign the gifts to the children such that the least happy child is as happy as possible. Even though this is a well-studied problem in the sequential setting, we obtain the first results the distributed setting. In particular, we show that the complexity of computing an 𝒪 ⁡ ( log ⁡ n / log ⁡ log ​ n ) \mathcal{O}(\log n/\log\log n) -approximation is Θ ^ ​ ( n + D ) \hat{\Theta}(\sqrt{n}+D) rounds, where our Ω ~ ​ ( n + D ) \widetilde{\Omega}(\sqrt{n}+D) -round lower bound is even stronger and holds for any approximation. This lower bound is in stark contrast to related allocation tasks like maximum matching and load balancing, where there exist poly ⁡ log ⁡ n \operatorname{\text{{\rm poly}}}\log n -round approximation algorithms. Since Santa Claus is a max-min allocation problem, there are many more global dependencies. Our lower bound construction shows that the non-existence of short augmenting paths does not guarantee a good approximation, thereby ruling out many techniques used for matching and related problems. Instead, our algorithm exploits that Santa Claus, despite its non-linear objective function, can be modeled as a linear program (LP). In contrast to maximum matching, it is not a packing (or covering) LP, because it has both packing and covering constraints. We provide the first 𝖢𝖮𝖭𝖦𝖤𝖲𝖳 \mathsf{CONGEST} solver that solves mixed packing and covering LPs in 𝒪 ⁡ ( D ⋅ poly ⁡ ( log ⁡ n , ε − 1 ) ) \mathcal{O}(D\cdot\operatorname{\text{{\rm poly}}}(\log n,\varepsilon^{-1})) rounds. Based on a fractional solution obtained by the solver, we obtain an integral solution via an involved global rounding approach. The LP solver, as well as the rounding technique we present, may both be of independent interest for the distributed community and further our knowledge on the complexity of approximation in distributed optimization.

1.1
Our Contribution

Notably, Santa Claus has not yet been studied in a distributed setting.
In this paper, we consider the

Distributed Hardness.

Santa Claus is a max-min fair allocation problem where the max-min property creates global dependencies that require considerable amount of communication.
This is reflected by the fact that computing a multiplicative approximation, i. e. an integral solution that is only a multiplicative factor worse than the optimum, is already hard.
More formally, we show the following lower bound.

Note that in the integral solution, each gift is either assigned to one child or not at all.

Linear Programming.

As the max-min objective is technically non-linear, we start by explaining why the Santa Claus problem can be modeled as an LP.
By a standard binary search framework, the following decision variant of Santa Claus is equivalent to an
\alpha
-approximation for the Santa Claus problem for some

Given a threshold
T\geq 0

T/\alpha
or determine that the optimal value of the Santa Claus Problem is less than

2

Intuitively, the threshold

can be thought of as the optimal fractional value found by binary search.
Throughout, we assume that

is known.
The big advantage of the decision variant is that it can be formulated as an LP as we explain in more detail in
Section

1.2.1

However, formulating Santa Claus as an LP can only be advantageous if we can actually solve the respective LP (formulation)
efficiently
in the distributed setting.
This is not the case for all classes of LPs, as the fastest general distributed LP solver takes

However, significant changes are necessary to make the sequential algorithm suitable for the distributed setting which we point out throughout the following explanation.

Throughout, we consider the Santa Claus problem in form of the bipartite graph

\{c,g\}\in E

1.2.2
Efficiently Eliminating Cycles in Gift Graph via Global Rounding

A crucial, reoccurring subroutine in the computation of our integral gift assignment in
Section

1.2.3
, i. e. our
\alpha
-approximation, is the efficient elimination of cycles in subgraphs of
G
via global rounding.
For the sake of simplicity, we explain our algorithm only for the general gift graph
G
that is restricted to its fractional edges.
Our goal is to reduce
G
to a forest.
In the sequential setting, the algorithm is simple:
For all even cycles
C
in the bipartite graph
G
, partition
C
into two disjoint matchings
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
and decrease the edge weight in
C_{1}
at the same rate as the edge weights in
C_{2}
increase.
Eventually, at least one edge weight becomes integral and is removed.
We repeat this process until there are no more cycles left in
G
.

However, a naive implementation of this sequential procedure is prohibitively expensive. Since each cycle might be of length
\mathcal{O}(n)
and we are only guaranteed to remove one edge in each iteration, this leads to a total runtime of
\mathcal{O}(nm)
rounds—a time in which one can trivially solve any problem in
\mathsf{CONGEST}
by gathering the whole instance in a node.
Therefore, our
\mathsf{CONGEST}
algorithm is substantially more involved.
Based on a cycle cover algorithm
[
67
,
75
]
, we design a cycle rounding algorithm that finds and eliminates many disjoint short cycles in parallel and takes
\hat{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{n}+D)
rounds.
The high-level idea of our approach to eliminating cycles is to repeat the following steps restricted to the graph with only fractional edges:

1.

Compute a
low diameter decomposition
, obtaining clusters with small diameter and
m/10
inter-cluster edges.

2.

Within each cluster, carefully extract a Eulerian subgraphs using
T
-joins
.

3.

Compute
short cycle covers
: every edge appears in at least one cycle of length
n^{o(1)}
.

4.

Select a maximal set of disjoint cycles using an MIS algorithm on a virtual graph.

5.

Round all cycles in parallel such that at least one edge in each cycle becomes integral.

We show that each iteration makes a
1/n^{o(1)}
fraction of the remaining edges integral. Then we repeat all steps on all remaining fractional edges.
After
\log n\cdot n^{o(1)}
iterations, the graph
G
becomes a tree.

A priori, we cannot guarantee any progress once the graph becomes very sparse, that is, when there are
n-1+n/10
edges left. In this case, the number of inter-cluster edges can be as large as
n/10
such that the remainder is already a tree and no further edges are rounded.
In order to avoid this and make our algorithm efficient, we apply the two following tricks:
First, we simplify our graph to
G^{\prime}
by repeatedly removing all vertices of degree one.
Second, we contract all long paths.
Together, this results in a graph
G^{\prime\prime}
where the average degree is
>2
. This means that
(1-\varepsilon)|E(G^{\prime\prime})|\geq|V(G^{\prime\prime})|-1
, and we can still make progress.
Since
G^{\prime\prime}
is not a subgraph pf the communication graph
G
(but also contains contractions), each ‘round’ on
G^{\prime\prime}
takes several rounds on
G
. We show that we can simulate each round on
G^{\prime\prime}
with
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{n}+D)
rounds on
G
. Multiplying this overhead with the number of rounds per iteration and the number of iterations gives
\widehat{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{n}+D)
.

1.2.3
Computing an Integral Gift Assignment

Based on the feasible fractional solution
(x,y)
to
\mathrm{SantaLP}(T/2)
, we explain how to compute an integral gift assignment
(X,Z)
such that each child receives a value of
T/\alpha
.
Our algorithm can be characterized by the following three steps where each step takes
\widetilde{O}(\sqrt{n}+D)
rounds in
\mathsf{CONGEST}
.

1.

Clustering of Big Gifts.

With the goal to compute multiple clusters of big gifts, we use our cycle rounding algorithm to compute a fractional solution
(x^{\prime},y)
that corresponds to an acyclic subgraph
G_{x^{\prime}}\subseteq G_{x}
.
However, the resulting forest
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
does not yet have all properties we require to compute an integral assignment for all gifts.
Thus, as a next step, we compute a fractional solution
(x^{*},y)
where
G_{x^{*}}\subseteq G_{x^{\prime}}
is a sub-forest
\mathcal{T}^{*}\subseteq\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
of children and big gifts with the
crucial
property that for each tree
T\in\mathcal{T}^{\ast}
we can always find one child in
T
to be satisfied in small gifts while all remaining children receive one big gift.
Recall that any child is satisfied by one big gift.

2.

Randomized Selection of Small Gifts.

For each tree
\mathcal{T}^{*}
, we randomly select
exactly one
child to receive only small gifts and propagate the choice to all other nodes in
\mathcal{T}^{*}
.
Importantly, the random selection happens independently and in parallel for all
\mathcal{T}^{*}
such that all respective decisions whether a small gift is assigned to a tree are in fact
independent
.
Using a concentration and union bound, each small gift is w. h. p. (fractionally) assigned to at most
\beta=\mathcal{O}(\log n/\log\log n)
trees and exactly one child within each tree.
After downscaling the solution by
\beta
, each gift is fractionally assigned to at most one child and each selected child receives a value of
T/\beta
.
Notably, the random selection directly implies the integral big gift assignment
X
, i. e. we simply root all
\mathcal{T}^{*}
in their respectively chosen children and assign the big gifts to their respective child node using the crucial property.

Naively, rooting a tree takes diameter rounds. Since we consider the big gift forest
G_{x^{*}}
, each
\mathcal{T}^{*}
can have diameter
n
. By using broadcast primitives on a compressed virtual graph, we show how to root all
\mathcal{T}^{*}
in
G_{x^{*}}
in parallel in
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{n}+D)
rounds.

3.

Integral Assignment of Gifts.
Lastly, we consider the subgraph of small gifts and randomly selected children with the goal to compute an integral assignment of small gifts.
Using our cycle rounding algorithm as a subroutine again, we first eliminate all cycles in our small gift graph—this requires a careful adaption of the edge weights such that the rounding takes both the fractional solution and the values of the small gifts into account.
In a second step, we round our fractional small gift assignment to an integral assignment
Z
such that each gift is assigned to at most one child and each child receives a value of
T/\alpha
where
\alpha=2\beta
.
The high-level idea of the rounding is as follows:
For a fixed child
c\in\mathcal{C}
that receives small gifts, let
k_{c}
denote the (fractional) number of small gifts it receives in the fractional solution.
Then we round it such that each child receives
\mathopen{}\lfloor k_{c}\rfloor\mathclose{}
small gifts and thus loses at most one small gift.
In the worst case, this is the most valuable gift of value at most
T/\alpha
and each child receives a value of at least
T/\beta-\max_{s\in\mathcal{S}}v_{s}=T/\alpha
.

1.2.4
Lower Bound

We prove
Theorem

1
via a reduction from set-disjointness in the two-party model to the Santa Claus problem in
\mathsf{CONGEST}
. In particular, we construct a family of lower bound graphs where deciding whether Santa Claus admits a solution of size
1
or
0
corresponds to the decision whether a certain set-disjointness instance is a “yes”- or a “no”-instance. Observe that any multiplicative approximation is also able to distinguish between a
1
- and a
0
-instance. Combining our reduction with the restricted bandwidth of our lower bound graphs and the long-standing lower bound of
\Omega({n})
for set-disjointness in the two-party communication model
[
71
]
, we obtain the claimed lower bound.

This raises the important question why we follow the first approach of

The answer is two-fold.

Bansal and Sviridenko
[

also show how to extend the approach followed in this paper to obtain a

approximation via the sequential
Lovász Local Lemma (LLL)
.
Improving upon these ideas with iterative applications of LLL, Feige

6

At the time, it was a non-constructive result that was later shown to be constructive by a result of

As the applications of distributed LLL either requires bounds on the number of small gifts that are adjacent to a big gift cluster (and vice versa) or subsampling of configurations of the configuration LP, it is highly unclear if this approach can be successfully transferred to the distributed setting.

Any sequential algorithm of
[
2
,
26
,
29
,
27
]
is inherently
global

On a high level, they rely on polynomially many iterations of finding augmenting paths in the gift graph that improve upon the current gift assignment.
Intuitively, one can hope that that all augmenting paths are short as long as we are far away from an optimal solution, because this would allow us to augment many paths in parallel.
However, the proof of our

Even if we are far away from an optimal solution, the augmenting paths can still be of length

This is also the reason why the constant integrality gap of
\mathrm{SantaLP}(T)
does not automatically imply a constant approximation, as our approximation factor can only be as good as the guarantee that we maintain during our rounding procedure.
It remains an open question whether a different approach that does not rely on the augmenting paths leads to a better approximation factor for our
\mathsf{CONGEST}
algorithm.

A more general version of Santa Claus, also called

1.3.2
Distributed Rounding

On a high-level, our algorithm computes a fractional solution to Santa Claus and then rounds it to an integral solution. Although distributed rounding is well-studied, the challenge we face are distinct from existing work.
Mainly following one recipe, previous work on local rounding in distributed algorithms can be described as follows: Start with a suitable fractional solution—usually an uniform distribution over all possible output labels—of the problem. Next, define a linear objective function
\Phi=\sum_{v\in V}\Phi_{v}
.
Then pick a set
S
of vertices such that changing the fractional labels of any vertex
v\in S
does not affect
\Phi_{w}
for any
w\in S\setminus\{v\}
.
Round the fractional labels in
S
to integral labels and remove
S
from the graph.
Repeat this procedure until the graph is empty.
This produces a partial solution for a constant fraction of vertices in the graph.
Hence, recursing on the remaining subgraph for
\mathcal{O}(\log n)
iterations solves the problem on the entire graph.
This general method is responsible for many recent breakthroughs in the
\mathsf{LOCAL}
model; see
[
35
,
44
,
40
,
32
,
41
,
42
]
.
This approach relies on a few key assumptions that are violated for Santa Claus: First of all, it assumes that any valid partial solution can always be extended to a proper solution of the same objective value.
Secondly, it assumes that it is always possible to round a fractional solution without loss in objective value.
This is clearly impossible for Santa Claus, as illustrated by the following simple instance:
There are two children and one gift of value
1
desired by both children. The optimal fractional solution has value
1/2
but any integral assignment has value
0
.

For global problems, rounding is less common. The only rounding algorithm in
\mathsf{CONGEST}
we are aware of is for flow and takes
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{m}+D)
rounds
[
37
]
. The important subroutine of finding a Eulerian tour has since been improved to
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{n}+D)
rounds
[
75
]
which can be seen as the starting point of the cycle rounding detailed in
Section

2.1.1
. However, we cannot use their algorithm, as our input graph does not have the structure required by
[
37
,
75
]
.

1.4
Future Directions

In some sense, the cycle rounding subroutine of
Section

2.1.1
can be seen as a sparsification procedure: it significantly reduces the number of fractional edges while maintaining the fact that it is a feasible fractional solution.
To some extend, this rounding technique is content-oblivious: it is rounding the values of a fractional solution of a linear program.
Further research is required to find out under which circumstances such a sparsification can be used in other optimization problems. For Santa Claus, it already raises the question whether this sparsification can be run directly on the entire fractional solution—thereby considerably simplifying our algorithm. It turns out that this is not possible, which requires some care to see.
Such a sparsification step essentially removes any edges that attain weight 0 through rounding. However, an optimal integral solution does not need to be a subset of the non-zero fractional edges (see
Example

43
in
Appendix

A
).
Importantly, when we apply this sparsification step as a subroutine in our algorithm, we
diligently
guarantee that there exists a good integral solution that is a subset of the fractional edges.
We leave as an open question which other optimization problems can be approached in a similar manner.

Proof of the Cycle Rounding Lemma

Putting all the pieces together, we finally prove our cycle rounding algorithm of

Proof of
Lemma

4
.

We describe an iterative process, such that in each iteration, a
1/n^{o(1)}
-fraction of the remaining edges (minus some forest) becomes integral. After
\log n\cdot n^{o(1)}
iterations, all edges apart from some remaining forest become integral.
See
Algorithm

Each iteration consists of two steps, which we detail below. We make our progress in the second step, where we need to compute a
T
-join (
Lemma

9
) and a cycle cover (
Lemma

11
). Both procedures have a round complexity that depends on the diameter of
G
. So we have a first step that removes tree-like parts of the graph and computes a low diameter decomposition on the remainder, giving the graph on which we make progress low diameter.

1. Simplifying the graph.

Our goal is to remove any part of the remaining graph that cannot be covered by a cycle. In other words, we remove the tree-like part of the graph.
More precisely, we give an efficient implementation of the algorithm that iteratively removes all edges that connect to a vertex of degree 1. Let
F
denote these edges.
We find this set
F
by the Rake & Compress procedure of
Lemma

5
, tailored to this application.
We run
\mathcal{O}(\log n)
iterations of the following algorithm.

•

Rake:
Remove all edges incident to a vertex of degree one.

•

Compress:
Contract all paths of degree two vertices of length at least two into a single edge.

Formally, we apply the Rake & Compress procedure in
Lemma

5
with the forest
F
as defined above. This seems contradictory: we apply
Lemma

5
with
F
as input to find
F
. However, it is crucial here that
Lemma

5
does not require that all the edges in
F
know that they are in
F
: only the leaves need to be aware of this at any moment in time. Hence, we can perform each Rake operation in 1 round. As detailed in
Lemma

5
, we can perform each Compress operation in
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{n}+D)
rounds. Note that for this step, we compress
all
paths of degree two vertices, not only the paths within the tree. This is necessary, since a priori it is not possible to know which paths are part of the tree. However, this does not incur more rounds:
Lemma

45
shows that we can contract such paths with at most
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{n}+D)
overhead.

At the end of this process, we consider the original (uncompressed) graph without all removed edges.
This is a graph without degree
1
vertices. However, we can still have many degree
2
vertices. We now run one iteration of Compress, but contract slightly less aggressively:

•

Compress’:
Contract all paths of degree two vertices of an odd number of edges into a single edge. Replace the weight of this edge by the minimum weight on the path.

The reason to only contract odd number of edges, is that the graph now remains bipartite. We perform Compress’ in
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{n}+D)
rounds, again using
Lemma

45
, which also ensures that, when round a contracted edge, we can forward the weight change to all edges on the path in
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{n}+D)
rounds.

Number of edges left.

After
\mathcal{O}(\log n)
iterations of (i) and (ii), we have reduced to a graph with fewer nodes and edges. We denote this graph by

with

nodes and

We claim that
m^{\prime}\geq\tfrac{6n^{\prime}}{5}
. This can be seen as follows.
Note that after a Compress operation, no two degree 2 nodes can be adjacent.
If there are at least
\tfrac{3n^{\prime}}{5}
nodes with degree

, all non-adjacent, then we have

\tfrac{3n^{\prime}}{5}
nodes with degree

, then we have at least
\tfrac{2n^{\prime}}{5}

.

that appear on these cycles. That is, at each iteration, the ratio between the number of edges we cover and the number of edges that the covering cycle removed is at least
1/dc
.

This means that we have a set of disjoint cycles of length
n^{o(1)}
each, covering at least
(m^{\prime}-\tfrac{m^{\prime}}{10}-|F|)/n^{o(1)}
edges in total, where the
-m^{\prime}/10
term comes from the inter-cluster edges of the low diameter decomposition and the
-|F|
from the
T
-join.
We can round a cycle by traversing it on the virtual graph in
n^{o(1)}
rounds. Since the cycles are edge-disjoint, we can round all cycles in parallel.
Again by by
Lemma

45
, this takes
n^{o(1)}\cdot\widehat{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{n}+D)
rounds on the communication network. Since each cycle makes at least one edge integral and each cycles has length
n^{o(1)}
, this makes at least
(\tfrac{9}{10}m^{\prime}-|F|)/n^{o(1)}\cdot\tfrac{1}{n^{o(1)}}=(\tfrac{9}{10}m^{\prime}-|F|)/n^{o(1)}
edges integral.

Iterative improvement.

We now repeat Steps 1 and 2, each time starting with all remaining fractional edges of
G=(V,E)
– so all pruning and contraction steps are undone and the
T
-join forest
F
is added back in to the graph.

If we would omit Step 1, the algorithm does not guarantee to make progress when
m^{\prime}=O(n)
, since then
\tfrac{9}{10}m^{\prime}-|F|
could be 0, and no further edges are rounded. However, by Step 1, we have
m^{\prime}\geq\tfrac{6n^{\prime}}{5}
, or equivalently
n^{\prime}\leq\tfrac{5}{6}m^{\prime}
and hence
\tfrac{9}{10}m^{\prime}-|F|\geq\tfrac{9}{10}m^{\prime}-\frac{5}{6}=\tfrac{1}{15}m^{\prime}
. Hence we always remove a
\tfrac{1}{15}/n^{o(1)}=1/n^{o(1)}
-factor of the edges that are not part of the
T
-join forest
F
. The edges that survive this iteration are the edges in
F
, together with all edges on the cycles that did not become integral.
Thus, the number of fractional edges decreases to
m^{\prime\prime}\leq|F|+(1-1/n^{o(1)})(m^{\prime}-|F|)
, or equivalently
m^{\prime\prime}-|F|\leq(1-1/n^{o(1)})(m^{\prime}-|F|)
. Hence, we claim that
m^{\prime}=|F|
after
\log n\cdot n^{o(1)}
iterations and thus we are only left with a forest of fractional edges.
∎

2.2
Linear Programming Formulation

Let
\OPT
denote the optimal value of the Santa Claus problem.
By a standard binary search framework, the following decision variant of the Santa Claus problem is equivalent to an
\alpha
-approximation algorithm for some
\alpha>1
:
Given a threshold
T\geq 0
, either compute a solution of value
T/\alpha
or determine that
\OPT<T
.
Intuitively, the threshold
T
can be thought of as the optimal value that was found by binary search.
Throughout, we assume that
T
is known and partition the set of gifts into
big gifts

\mathcal{B}:=\{g\in\mathcal{G}:v_{g}\geq T/\alpha\}
and
small gifts

\mathcal{S}:=\{g\in\mathcal{G}:v_{g}<T/\alpha\}
.
Originally introduced by
[
61
]
, this distinction has become the standard in the literature on Santa Claus.
Importantly, as we are showing an
\alpha
-approximation, any child that receives one big gift is satisfied and does
not
require any more gifts.
Due to that insight, we implicitly assume that all big gifts have a value of
T
for the rest of the section.

In order to compute a fractional solution, we use the following non-trivial LP of Davies, Rothvoss and Zhang
[
29
]
(this expanded version was first used in
[
72
]
) that we refer to as
\mathrm{SantaLP}(T)
throughout.

\displaystyle\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}v_{s}\cdot z_{cs}

\displaystyle\geq T\cdot\left(1-\sum_{b\in\mathcal{B}}x_{cb}\right)

\displaystyle\forall c\in\mathcal{C}

(1)

\displaystyle z_{cs}

\displaystyle\leq 1-\sum_{b\in\mathcal{B}}x_{cb}

\displaystyle\forall c\in\mathcal{C},s\in\mathcal{S}

(2)

\displaystyle\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}x_{cb}

\displaystyle\leq 1

\displaystyle\forall b\in\mathcal{B}

(3)

\displaystyle\sum_{b\in\mathcal{B}}x_{cb}

\displaystyle\leq 1

\displaystyle\forall c\in\mathcal{C}

(4)

\displaystyle\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}z_{cs}

\displaystyle\leq 1

\displaystyle\forall s\in\mathcal{S}

(5)

\displaystyle x_{cb},z_{cs}

\displaystyle\geq 0

\displaystyle\forall c\in\mathcal{C},b\in\mathcal{B},s\in\mathcal{S}

(6)

During the following explanation of the constraints, it is helpful to think of an integral solution:
Constraints (
3
) and (
4
) ensure that each big gift
b
is assigned to at most one child
c
and vice versa.
If child
c
is assigned a big gift
b
, then
z_{cs}=0
and
c
does not receive any small gifts as enforced by Constraint (
2
).
Otherwise, we have
z_{cs}\leq 1
and
c
is compensated in small gifts only.
The same argument applies to Constraint (
1
), where the right-hand side is zero if
c
is assigned a big gift or
T
if it is not.
In the latter case, child
c
receives at least a value of
T
in small gifts.
Constraint (
5
) guarantees that each small gift is assigned at most one time.

Furthermore, Constraints (
1
) and (
2
) enforce the fundamental property that any child
c\in\mathcal{C}
that receives small gifts with
z_{c}>0^{\lvert\mathcal{S}\rvert}
is always assigned more than
\alpha
many gifts.
More formally, let
\mathcal{S}_{c}\subseteq\mathcal{S}
denote the set of small gifts that
c
receives.
By definition of the small gifts and Constraints (
2
) and (
1
) follows that

\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}_{c}}v_{s}\cdot z_{cs}<\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}_{c}}T/\alpha\cdot\left(1-\sum_{b\in\mathcal{B}}x_{cb}\right)=\frac{\lvert\mathcal{S}_{c}\rvert}{\alpha}\cdot T\cdot\left(1-\sum_{b\in\mathcal{B}}x_{cb}\right)\leq\frac{\lvert\mathcal{S}_{c}\rvert}{\alpha}\cdot\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}_{c}}v_{s}\cdot z_{cs}.

Consequently, any child
c
with
z_{c}>0^{\lvert\mathcal{S}\rvert}
is assigned strictly more than
\alpha
many small gifts.
Thus, it is always possible to scale the small gift assignment of
c
to be of value
T
, i. e. we set

z^{\prime}_{c}=\frac{1}{1-\sum_{b\in\mathcal{B}}x_{cb}}\cdot z_{c}\leq 1^{\lvert\mathcal{S}\rvert}

such that
\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}_{c}}v_{s}\cdot z^{\prime}_{cs}\geq T
.
While
z^{\prime}
is not necessarily a feasible solution to
\mathrm{SantaLP}(T)
anymore, we use this property to compute our approximation.

Given a fractional solution
(x,y)\in[0,1]^{\lvert\mathcal{C}\rvert\cdot\lvert\mathcal{G}\rvert}
to
\mathrm{SantaLP}(T)
, the vectors
x\in[0,1]^{\lvert\mathcal{C}\rvert\cdot\lvert\mathcal{B}\rvert}
and
y\in[0,1]^{\lvert\mathcal{C}\rvert\cdot\lvert\mathcal{S}\rvert}
denote the assignments for the big and small gifts, respectively.
In addition, let
G_{x}=(\mathcal{C}\cup\mathcal{B},E)
denote the big gift graph between children
c\in\mathcal{C}
and big gifts
b\in\mathcal{B}
where an edge
\{c,b\}\in E
of weight
x_{cb}
is present if
x_{cb}>0
.
Similarly, we define the small gift graph
G_{y}=(\mathcal{C}\cup\mathcal{S},E^{\prime})
for
c\in\mathcal{C}
and
s\in\mathcal{S}
where each edge
\{c,s\}\in E^{\prime}
of weight
y_{cs}
is present if
y_{cs}>0
.

2.3
Computing an Integral Gift Assignment

Starting from an optimal fractional solution
(x,y)
to
\mathrm{SantaLP}(T)
, we show how to compute an integral gift assignment

such that each child receives a value of

throughout this section.

2.3.1
Big Gift Clustering

As a first step, we transform an optimal fractional solution

to

Similar to Bansal and Sviridenko

Recall that any child is satisfied with receiving one big gift of value

We start by showing that the result of removing cycles from

Corollary 12

(Acyclic Big Gift Graph)
.

such that the corresponding big gift graph

Proof.

Applying

4
to

results in a sparse subgraph

in
\widehat{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{n}+D)
rounds of
\mathsf{CONGEST}
.
From property
(2)
follows that
\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}x^{\prime}_{cb}\leq 1
and
\sum_{b\in\mathcal{B}}x^{\prime}_{cb}\leq 1
.
Thus, the Constraints (

) and (

) are still satisfied. Since

In the following, we compute an integral small gift assignment

computed in

First, we show that we can randomly pick exactly one child

We use the following Chernoff bound from

to bound the number of times that one small gift is fractionally assigned to a child.

Lemma 14

(Chernoff Bound)
.

Let
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\in[0,1]
be independent random variables with means
p_{1},\dots,p_{n}

\mu=\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}
and let

. Then, for any

with

\mathbb{P}[X\geq r\mu]\leq\mathcal{O}(1/r)^{\Omega(r\mu)}.

The most important insight to the following lemma is that the random selection happens independently and in parallel for all
\mathcal{T}^{*}
such that all respective decisions whether a small gift is assigned to a tree are in fact

More formally, we show the following lemma.

Lemma 15

.

Let
(x^{*},y)

In

\mathsf{CONGEST}

z_{cs}\leq y_{cs}\cdot T/V_{\mathcal{S}}(c)\leq T/V_{\mathcal{S}}(c)\cdot\left(1-\sum_{b\in\mathcal{B}}x^{\ast}_{cb}\right)\leq\frac{\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}v_{s}\cdot y_{cs}}{V_{\mathcal{S}}(c)}=1.

(8)

Further, it holds that
\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}v_{s}z_{cs}=T
for all children
c\in\mathcal{C}_{s}
.

As a last step, we show that the random choice of
z
(fractionally) assigns each small gift to at most
\beta
many children with high probability.
For each small gift
s\in\mathcal{S}
and cluster pair
i
, define the random variable
Z_{is}\in[0,1]
by
Z_{is}:=z_{c_{i}s}
.
For a fixed gift
s\in S
, the
p
many random variables
\{Z_{is}:i\in[p]\}
are independent, because the random selection of children
c_{i}
happens independently in all
p
clusters.
For each small gift
s\in\mathcal{S}
, we have that

\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i\in[p]}Z_{is}\right]=\sum_{i\in[p]}\mathbb{E}\left[Z_{is}\right]=\sum_{i\in[p]}\sum_{c\in C_{i}}p_{c}\cdot z_{cs}.

Recall that
p_{c}=V_{s}(c)/V_{s}(C_{i})
. Then we get that

\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i\in[p]}Z_{is}\right]=\sum_{i\in[p]}\sum_{c\in C_{i}}p_{c}\cdot z_{cs}=\sum_{i\in[p]}T\cdot\frac{\sum_{c\in C_{i}}y_{cs}}{V_{\mathcal{S}}(C_{i})}\leq 2\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}y_{cs}\leq 2,

(9)

where the last inequality follows from Constraint (
5
) of
\mathrm{SantaLP}(T)
.
Let
\beta=C\cdot\log n/\log\log n
for a sufficiently large constant
C
, then using
Lemma

14
it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\sum_{i\in[p]}Z_{is}>\beta\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left[\sum_{i\in[p]}Z_{is}>\beta/2\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i\in[p]}Z_{is}\right]\right]\leq\mathcal{O}(2/\beta)^{\Omega(\beta)}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}(\log\log n/\log n)^{\Omega(\log n/\log\log n)}=2^{-\Omega(\log n)}.

Further, we have that

\sum_{i\in[p]}Z_{is}=\sum_{i\in[p]}z_{c_{i}s}=\sum_{c\in C}z_{cs}.

Using a union bound over all small gifts, we see that each small gift is w. h. p. fractionally assigned at most
\beta
times.
As a last step, each child
c\in\mathcal{C}_{s}
locally scales down their small gift assignment by
\beta
such that
z_{c}\in[0,1]^{\mathcal{S}}
.
Consequently, each child receives w. h. p. a total value of
T/\beta
and each small gift is w. h. p. fractionally assigned at most once.
∎

Intuitively, the parameter
\beta
measures the distance to the optimal solution where each small gift is assigned at most once. Therefore, it is closely related to our approximation factor by
\alpha=4\beta
.
As the last step, we round
z
to an integral assignment
Z
of small gifts, such that no gift is assigned to multiple children.
The high-level idea of the rounding can be described as follows:
For a fixed child
c\in\mathcal{C}_{s}
, let
k_{c}=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}z_{cs}
denote the (fractional) number of small gifts
c
receives in solution
z
.
Next we show how to round this fractional solution such that each child receives
\mathopen{}\lfloor k_{c}\rfloor\mathclose{}
small gifts.
Hence, each child loses the value of at most one small gift.
In the worst case, this is the most valuable gift of value
\max_{s\in\mathcal{S}}v_{s}
and each child receives a value of at least
T/\beta-\max_{s\in\mathcal{S}}v_{s}
.
More formally, we show the following lemma.

Lemma 16

(Integral Assignment)
.

Let
z
be the fractional small gift assignment in
Lemma

15
.
In
\widehat{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{n}+D)
rounds, we can compute an integral small gift assignment
Z
such that w. h. p.

\forall c\in\mathcal{C}_{s}:\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}v_{s}\cdot Z_{cs}\geq\frac{T}{\beta}-\max_{s\in S}v_{s}.

(10)

Proof.

We prove the lemma in two steps: First we remove all cycles in
G_{z}
to obtain the forest
G_{z^{\prime}}\subseteq G_{z}
, then we integrally assign the gifts for each tree of
G_{z^{\prime}}
such that each child loses at most one gift.
Our algorithm consists of two parts, first we remove cycles and then we round the remaining forest.

Algorithm Part 1.

Lemma

4
shows how to remove cycles in a weighted graph. We cannot apply this lemma directly to
G_{z}
, since we have two types of weights: we have the fractional weights on the edges
z_{cs}
, but we also have the weights of the small gifts
v_{s}
. We apply
Lemma

4
to
G_{z}
with an auxiliary weight function
w
and two small adjustment. First, we describe this weight function.
In order to ensure that the cycle removal maintains the total value of
T/\beta
for each child, we set the edge weights to
w_{cs}:=z_{cs}\cdot v_{s}
for all
c\in\mathcal{C}_{s}
and
s\in\mathcal{S}
.
These weights are no longer in
[0,1]
as in
Lemma

4
. We give the two adjustments to the algorithm. First, to round each cycle, we compute
w:=\min\{w_{cs},v_{s}-w_{cs}\}
and alternatingly change the edge weights to
w_{cs}^{\prime}=w_{cs}\pm w
. At the same time we update
z^{\prime}_{cs}=w^{\prime}_{cs}/v_{s}
for all
c\in\mathcal{C}_{s}
and
s\in\mathcal{S}
.
By the choice of
w
, at least one edge in each cycle becomes integral with
z_{cs}^{\prime}\in\{0,1\}
while we have
z_{cs}^{\prime}\in[0,1]
for all other edges.
This leads us to the second adjustment: we remove an edge
\{c,s\}
from
G_{z}
if
w_{cs}\in\{0,v_{s}\}
, which corresponds to
z_{cs}\in\{0,1\}
being integral. Note that removing an edge
z_{cs}=1
means that the small gift
s
is permanently assigned to child
c
and
z_{cs}=0
means the child forgoes on the gift.

These two adjustment do not affect correctness or the running time in
Lemma

4
, hence we eliminate cycles from
G_{z}
in
\widehat{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{n}+D)
rounds. Finally, we obtain the edge weights in
G_{z^{\prime}}
by scaling
z_{cs}^{\prime}=w_{cs}/v_{s}
for all
c\in\mathcal{C}_{s}
and
s\in\mathcal{S}
. We are left with the acyclic graph
G_{z^{\prime}}
.

Algorithm Part 2.

Next, we show how to round the remaining forest
G_{z^{\prime}}
.
For each tree
\mathcal{T}
in
G_{z^{\prime}}
, we permanently assign small gifts
s\in\mathcal{S}
to children
c\in\mathcal{C}_{s}
starting from the leaves.
We root
\mathcal{T}
at an arbitrary child
c\in\mathcal{T}
. Then, each gift is assigned to its parent in the tree. Since each child has at most one parent in
\mathcal{T}
, it loses at most
\max_{s\in S}v_{s}
in value and thus
Equation

10
holds.

Round Complexity.

A naive distributed implementation of this algorithm takes diameter(
\mathcal{T}
) rounds, which could be as big as
n-1
. Next we show how to implement this algorithm in
\widehat{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{n}+D)
rounds.

The main observation hereto is that on a path, all nodes are determined by what happens on the endpoints. This can be seen as follows: consider a path that ends in a degree 1 vertex. If this is a gift, then all odd edges on the path are rounded up to 1 and all even edges are deleted. If the last vertex is a child, then all odd edges are deleted and all even edges are rounded up to 1. Hence, each edge only needs to know 1) is the endpoint of the path a gift or a child and 2) are there an odd or even number of edges till this endpoint.
Hence, all paths can be contracted and unrolled once the endpoints are known.
We perform this with a version of
Rake
and
Compress
; see
Lemma

5
:

•

Rake:
Decide on all edges incident to a vertex of degree one.

•

Compress:
Contract all paths into a single edge, storing on the edge whether this contains an odd or even number of vertices.

By the same proof as
Lemma

5
, the graph is empty after
\mathcal{O}(\log n)
iterations and each iteration can be performed in
\widehat{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{n}+D)
rounds.
∎

We conclude with the following lemma that shows how to efficiently compute an integral big gift assignment
X
.

Lemma 17
.

Let
x^{*}
be the fractional big gift assignment in
Lemma

13
and
Z
the integral small gift assignment in
Lemma

16
.
In
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{n}+D)
rounds of
\mathsf{CONGEST}
, we can compute a big gift assignment
X
for all
c\in\mathcal{C}
with
Z_{c}=0^{\lvert\mathcal{S}\rvert}
.

Proof.

In
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{n}+D)
rounds, we root all trees
\mathcal{T}^{*}
in parallel at their respective children
c^{*}
with
Z_{c^{*}}\neq 0^{\lvert\mathcal{S}\rvert}
using
Lemma

5
.
By
Lemma

13
, each big gift in
\mathcal{T}^{*}
has degree at most
2
.
Thus, we integrally assign each child its parent big gift.
Hence, we computed a big gift assignment
X
in
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{n}+D)
rounds.
∎

2.4
Proof of the Approximation Factor

We conclude the previous subsections with the proof of
Theorem

2
.

Proof of
Theorem

2
.

Let
\beta=

\mathcal{O}(\log n/\log\log n)
be as in
Lemma

15
and
\alpha=4\beta
.
Throughout this proof, we explicitly state which

is used in the LP formulation of Santa Claus by writing
\mathrm{SantaLP}_{\alpha}(T)

We compute an
\alpha

First, we determine a value

\mathrm{SantaLP}_{\alpha}(T)
for different values of
T
.
Recall that
\OPT
denotes the optimal (integral) value of the Santa Claus problem.
Second, we carefully round an approximate solution
(x,y)
into an integral solution
(X,Z)
that is an
\mathcal{O}(\log n/\log\log n)
-approximation.

Find Optimal Value and Fractional Solution.
Let
\varepsilon=1/2
and

denote the total sum of gift values.
In
\mathcal{O}(D)
rounds, we elect a leader that coordinates the binary search.
The goal of our binary search is to find a value

such that
\mathrm{SantaLP}_{\alpha/2}(T/2)
is feasible and

.
Let

This is trivially satisfied in the beginning, as

.

In each of the following steps, the leader communicates the next value

to all other nodes in
\mathcal{O}(D)
rounds.
Based on
T
and
\alpha
, all gift nodes can locally distinguish whether they belong to the big gifts
\mathcal{B}:=\{g\in\mathcal{G}:v_{g}\geq T/\alpha\}
or the small gifts

.
Observe that the gift size distinction is the same for both

and
\mathrm{SantaLP}_{\alpha/2}(T/2)

.
Next, we try to compute a feasible fractional solution

to

29
.
Depending on the outcome, the leader adjusts the search interval to

if

is feasible and
[a,T]
otherwise.
Once our search interval contains a single value, we have determined our value

Note that by

, our solution
(x,y)
only undershoots the covering Constraint
(\ref{eq:lp-objective-value})
such that for all

, it holds that

\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}v_{s}\cdot y_{cs}\geq\frac{T}{2}\cdot\left(1-\sum_{b\in\mathcal{B}}x_{cb}\right).

Since binary search takes at most
\mathcal{O}(\log V)
steps, where each step takes

, we can find

and a feasible fractional solution

to
\mathrm{SantaLP}_{\alpha/2}(T/2)

\mathsf{CONGEST}
.

Round to Integral Solution.

Based on
(x,y)
, we compute a series of feasible fractional solutions to
\mathrm{SantaLP}_{\alpha/2}(T/2)
where all transformations take
\widehat{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{n}+D)

With

12
, we obtain a fractional solution

such that

is acyclic.
Using

, we compute a fractional solution

where

is a forest of highly structured trees

.
Based on

15
to compute a fractional small gift assignment

such that all children in
\mathcal{C}_{s}
receive w. h. p. a total value of

in small gifts.
As a last step, with

16
, we obtain an integral small gift assignment

where each child

receives w. h. p. at least a value of

\frac{T}{2\beta}-\max_{s\in\mathcal{S}}v_{s}\geq\frac{2T}{\alpha}-\frac{T}{\alpha}=\frac{T}{\alpha},

where the inequality follows from the definition of small gifts
\mathcal{S}
and
\alpha
.
By

17
, we compute an integral big gift assignment

where each child receives exactly one big gift of value at least

.
Hence, we computed w. h. p. an

-approximation in

rounds of
\mathsf{CONGEST}

∎

3
Lower Bounds

The goal of this section is to prove the hardness result of the Santa Claus problem in the
\mathsf{CONGEST}
model. In fact, we show a lower bound of
\widetilde{\Omega}(\sqrt{n}+D)
rounds to decide whether a solution of non-zero value exists. Additionally, this result implies that any (multiplicative) approximation to the integral Santa Claus problem also obeys this lower bound. A simpler version of the
\mathsf{CONGEST}
construction also shows a diameter lower bound for the same problem in the
\mathsf{LOCAL}
model.
Moreover, we show that solving the fractional version of the Santa Claus problem exactly also requires at least diameter rounds in the distributed setting.

See
1

We construct a family of graphs
SC_{n}
with
\mathcal{O}(n)
nodes and
\mathcal{O}(\log n)
diameter, such that solving the Santa Claus problem on
SC_{n}
is as hard as solving the set-disjointness problem on sets of size
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{n})
in the two-party model.
Our construction is motivated by the construction of Peleg and Rubinovich
[
68
]
.

Construction.

The construction we use for the lower bound is a graph consisting of
\sqrt{n}
disjoint paths of length
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{n})
, where each path is its own Santa Claus instance with value
0
or
1
depending on the input for Alice and Bob. To reduce the diameter of the graph, we add a binary tree of height
\mathcal{O}(\log n)
on top of the paths, allowing for shortcuts between the endpoints of the graph. This yields a graph of diameter
\mathcal{O}(\log n)
, and finally, we show that the bandwidth of the binary tree is so small that the graph still needs
\widetilde{\Omega}(\sqrt{n}+D)
rounds to solve the Santa Claus problem on all paths.

Formally, let
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\sqrt{n}\in\mathbb{N}
. The graph
SC_{n}
consists of
\sqrt{n}
disjoint paths
P_{i}
of length
2\sqrt{n}-1
and are alternating child-gift paths, where each gift has a value of
1
, which are connected by a full binary tree
T
with
\sqrt{n}
child leaves
\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{\sqrt{n}}
. Each leaf
\ell_{j}
connects to the
j
th gift
g_{j}^{i}
of every path
P_{i}
. To ensure that we are considering a Santa Claus instance, the levels of the tree
T
are alternating between children and gifts, e.g., the parents of the leaves are gifts. In particular, we want the binary tree not to interfere with the solution of the paths, nor change the overall optimal solution size. To ensure that, we also add a private gift
p_{c}
to each gift child
c^{T}\in V(T)
. Formally, we have
\sqrt{n}
copies
P_{1},\dots,P_{\sqrt{n}}
of the path
\mathcal{P}
:

•

V(\mathcal{P})=\{c_{j}\mid j=1,\dots,\sqrt{n}\}\cup\{g_{j}\mid j=1,\dots,\sqrt{n}-1\}
,

•

E(\mathcal{P})=\{\{c_{j},g_{j}\}\mid j=1,\dots,\sqrt{n}\}\cup\{\{g_{j},c_{(j+1)}\}\mid j=1,\dots,\sqrt{n}-1\}
,

and we denote gifts and children of the path
P_{i}
as
c_{j}^{i}
, and
g_{j}^{i}
respectively.

Figure 1:
The lower bound graph
SC_{n}
. Boxes are gifts, and circles are children.

Finally, since we want to reduce the Santa Claus instance to a set-disjointness problem in the two-party model, we explain how the input for Alice and Bob influences the construction. Both Alice and Bob receive a
\sqrt{n}
-bit binary string,
a_{1}a_{2}\dots a_{\sqrt{n}}
and
b_{1}b_{2}\dots b_{\sqrt{n}}
. Alice and Bob are both child nodes and are connected to
\sqrt{n}+1
many gifts. One of those gifts is a private gift
p^{A}
(resp.
p^{B})
that is used to make Alice (resp. Bob) happy, i.e., they have exclusive access to that gift. The remaining
\sqrt{n}
gifts
g_{i}^{A}
(resp.
g_{i}^{B}
),
i=1,\dots,\sqrt{n}
, are connected to Alice (resp. Bob) and the leftmost child of the path
P_{i}

c_{1}^{i}
(resp. rightmost child
c_{\sqrt{n}}^{i}
).
The values of
g_{i}^{A}
and
(g_{i}^{B})
is given by
a_{i}
and
b_{i}
, respectively.

Remark 18
.

The graph
SC_{n}
has
\mathcal{O}(n)
nodes and diameter
D=\mathcal{O}(\log n)
.

Lemma 19
.

Any distributed algorithm that decides whether there exists a solution of value
1
in the graph
SC_{n}
also solves the set-disjointness problem for

\displaystyle A=\{i\mid a_{i}=0\},\quad B=\{i\mid b_{i}=0\}.

Proof.

If
A\cap B=\emptyset
, then for every
i\in[\sqrt{n}]
at least one of
a_{i}
or
b_{i}
is
1
. Without loss of generality assume that
a_{i}=1
, then we can assign the gift
g_{i}^{A}
with value
a_{i}=1
to
c_{1}^{i}
and for the rest of the
i
th path assign
g_{j}^{i}
to
c_{(j+1)}^{i}
, and thus every child
c_{j}^{i}
gets assigned one gift of value
1
. The children in the binary tree, as well as Alice and Bob, get assigned their private gifts of value
1
, and thus also get assigned at least one gift each.

On the other hand, if
A\cap B\neq\emptyset
, there exists at least one index
i
such that
a_{i}=b_{i}=0
. Thus, the
i
th path contains
\sqrt{n}
children, but only
\sqrt{n}-1
gifts of value
1
, and since each child is only adjacent to the gifts of that path, at least one child cannot get assigned any gift. Thus, the value of this instance is
0
.
∎

It is well known that the set-disjointness problem defined in
Lemma

19
needs
\Omega(\sqrt{n})
bits of communication, since the bit strings are of length
\sqrt{n}

[
71
]
.
Thus, due to

\Omega(\sqrt{n})
bits to solve the given Santa Claus instance.

To bound the number of bits Alice and Bob can communicate in
T
rounds on the

[

]
.

Definition 20
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph with two distinguished nodes
a,b
(Alice and Bob). A moving cut is an assignment
\ell:E\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
. We say
\ell
has capacity
C=\sum_{e\in E}\ell(e)
and distance
T
if
d_{1+\ell}(a,b)\geq T
, where

denotes the shortest path distance from
v
to
w
when each edge has cost

.

Lemma 21
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph where each edge carries
k
bits of information per round, and let
a,b\in V
be distinguished nodes (Alice and Bob). Suppose
\ell:E\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
is a moving-cut of capacity

and distance

between

and

. Then any distributed protocol that completes in at most

Proof.

This is also motivated by
[
69

, and a proof of the exact statement can be found in
[

]
.
∎

Finally, to prove that any set-disjointness protocol on the graphs
SC_{n}
, and hence the Santa Claus problem, must run for at least
\widetilde{\Omega}(\sqrt{n})
rounds, we construct a moving cut of small capacity, but large distance:

Lemma 22
.

For every
\gamma>0
and every
n\in\mathbb{N}
there exists a moving cut
\ell_{n}
in
SC_{n}
with

\displaystyle capacity(\ell_{n})=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\gamma}\sqrt{n}\log n\right),\quad distance(\ell_{n})=\Omega\left(\frac{1}{\gamma}\sqrt{n}\right).

Proof.

Set
\ell(e)=0
for all edges that are not part of the binary tree. Also set
\ell(e)=0
for all edges between children and their private gifts in the binary tree. Let the height of a tree node be the distance to the leaves, i.e., the level of the leaves is
0
, and the level of their parents is
1
, and the level of the root is
\log(\sqrt{n})=\mathcal{O}(\log n)
. Assign to the parent edge of each node with height
h
the value
\ell(e)=\lfloor\frac{2^{h}}{\gamma}\rfloor
. Since there are at most
\sqrt{n}/2^{h}
nodes at height
h
, the total capacity of the moving cut
\ell
is at most

\displaystyle\sum_{h=0}^{\mathcal{O}(\log n)}\frac{\sqrt{n}}{2^{h}}\cdot\frac{2^{h}}{\gamma}=\sum_{h=0}^{\mathcal{O}(\log n)}\frac{\sqrt{n}}{\gamma}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\gamma}\sqrt{n}\log n\right).

To check that any path from Alice to Bob is at least of length
\Omega\left(\frac{\sqrt{n}}{\gamma}\right)
in the
1+\ell
distance, we observe that if we shortcut
2k
edges of a path
P_{i}
via the tree, we have to use a path in
T
that uses at least one node of height
\lceil\log k\rceil
. Thus, a path of length
2k
(in one of the
P_{i}
’s) can be replaced with a path in the tree of
(1+\ell)
distance at least

\sum_{h=1}^{\lceil\log k\rceil}2\cdot\frac{2^{h-1}}{\gamma}=\sum_{h=1}^{\lceil\log k\rceil}\frac{2^{h}}{\gamma}\geq k/\gamma.\qed

Lemma 23
.

In the
SC_{n}
graph, any distributed algorithm that uses

-bit messages per edge per round to decide whether there is a solution to the Santa Claus problem of value at least
1
requires
\widetilde{\Omega}(\sqrt{n}/k)

Proof.

By
Lemma

19
, any distributed algorithm that decides whether there exists a solution to the Santa Claus problem of value at least
1
also solves set-disjointness for

\displaystyle A=\{i\mid a_{i}=0\},\quad B=\{i\mid b_{i}=0\}.

From
[
71
]
we know that any distributed protocol solving this set-disjointness instance (of size
\Theta(\sqrt{n})
) requires
\Omega(\sqrt{n})
bits of communication between Alice and Bob. By
Lemma

, with
\gamma=c\cdot k\cdot\log n
(for a sufficiently large constant

), we know that there exists a moving cut
\ell_{n}
with

\displaystyle capacity(\ell_{n})=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\sqrt{n}}{ck}\right),\quad distance\left(\ell_{n}\right)=\Omega\left(\frac{\sqrt{n}}{ck\log n}\right).

By

, in

Secondly, we show that even the fractional relaxation of the Santa Claus problem is already hard to solve in the distributed setting via a reduction from the fractional perfect matching problem.

Lemma 26

\displaystyle\quad\sum_{e\in\delta(v)}x_{e}\leq 1

\displaystyle\forall v\in V,

In general, the feasibility problem and the optimization problem are closely related.
In the sequential setting, it is easy to use the feasibility problem combined with a binary search framework to obtain an approximation for the optimization problem.
In the distributed setting, this technique already implies a runtime of

Definition 27

(Mixed Packing-Covering LPs)
.

For given matrices
\mathbf{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{p}\times m},\mathbf{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{c}\times m}

\displaystyle\min\{\lambda\mid\mathbf{P}\mathbf{x}\leq\lambda\mathbf{p},\mathbf{C}\mathbf{x}\geq\mathbf{c},\mathbf{x}\geq 0\},

(11)

where
\mathbf{P},\mathbf{C},\mathbf{p},\mathbf{c}
all have non-negative entries.

In particular, we consider a bipartite graph
G=(V,E)
with

, where

has a node for each covering, and each packing constraint, and
|X|=m
has a node for each variable. We always connect a variable node

with a packing constraint node

or a covering constraint node

th row

For our application to the Santa Claus problem in

Remark 28
.

For given matrices
\mathbf{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{p}\times m},\mathbf{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{c}\times m}
and matching vectors
\mathbf{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{p}},\mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{c}}

\displaystyle\max\{\gamma\mid\mathbf{P}\mathbf{x}\leq\mathbf{p},\mathbf{C}\mathbf{x}\geq\gamma\mathbf{c},\mathbf{x}\geq 0\},

In particular given a solution

Lemma 44
.

There is a

Rake and Compress.

For our algorithm, we often need to run subroutines on subgraphs of our communication network. These subgraphs might contain long paths and thus have a much higher diameter compared to our original communication network.

We will use two simple operations inspired by Miller & Reif
