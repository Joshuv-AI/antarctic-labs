Abstract
. In this paper, we study two-dimensional steady solitary gravity waves propagating along the surface of a fluid of finite depth. In particular, we can deal with general vorticity distributions and overhanging wave profiles. By conformal mappings, we reformulate the problem into an overdetermined elliptic system coupled with an elliptic boundary value problem in a fixed strip domain. To avoid imposing extra constraints on vorticity function, we further reformulate the problem into the form of an abstract operator. Based on the formulations, the existence of small-amplitude solitary waves is proved by the center manifold reduction method, while the large-amplitude waves are obtained based on the analytic global bifurcation theorem.

1.
Introduction

The study of water waves has a long history and the rigorous mathematical analysis can be traced back
to the times of 18th century. In particular, Stokes
[
45
]
studied the water wave problem in infinite
depth and conjectured that there is a branch of nontrivial periodic waves bifurcating from a half-space and
limiting to an extrme. Gerstner
[
27
]
found an explicit solution in the Lagrangian setting for deep water gravity waves.
Although the exact solutions obtained by Gerstner are applicable to rotational water waves, most results at that time are
related to irrotational settings, which correspond to that the curl of the velocity field vanishes. For
irrotational water waves, a big advantage is that we can reformulate the problem into a problem for a harmonic
function in a fixed domain. In
[
26
]
, Friedrichs and Hyers proved the existence of solitary waves
in exponentially weighted spaces, based on an application of inverse function theorem. Mielke
[
42
]

developed a spatial dynamical approach, involving looking for a center manifold for quasilinear partial differential
equations, to the irrotational steady waves. For large-amplitude irrotational
water waves, the first rigorous result was proved by Amick and Toland in
[
5
]
, in which they used a
conformal mapping approach and construct a connected set of solitary waves by taking the limit of a sequence of
approximate problems with better compactness properties. The limiting wave is singular and has a stagnation point
with
120^{\rm o}
angle at the crest
[
4
]
, which thus solved the famous Stokes conjecture.

Compared with irrotational water waves, rotational waves are more commonly observed in nature. Rotational water
waves are the waves with vorticity, which is the hallmark of underlying non-uniform currents. The study of rotational
waves is relatively latter than that of irrotational settings. In
[
46
]
, Ter-Krikorov used the methods
of
[
26
]
to prove the first existence result of small-amplitude waves. Groves and Wahlén used a
centre-manifold technique in
[
30
]
to reduce the system of solitary water waves with an arbitrary distribution
of vorticity to a locally equivalent Hamiltonian system with one degree of freedom and then obtained the existence
result. Hur proved the symmetry property for rotational solitary waves in
[
35
]
. For large-amplitude
rotational waves, the first rigorous mathematical result was provided by Constantin and Strauss
[
16
]
,
in which they used an appropriate hodograph change of variable devised by Dubeil-Jacotin
[
23
]
to transform the problem into an equivalent form of a quasilinear
elliptic equation in a fixed rectangular domain, and then use the method of bifurcation theory
[
14
]

to construct a global connected set of periodic solutions. Wheeler in
[
53
]
provided the first construction of exact solitary waves of large amplitude with an arbitrary distribution of vorticity, and the results can be regarded as a generalization of the classical result of Amick and Toland. We refer to the monograph
[
15
]
and the papers
[
6
,
21
,
33
]
for more results on rotational periodic water waves. Results on rotational solitary water waves in the absence of critical layers can be found in
[
11
,
34
,
44
,
54
,
55
]
.
Note that all results mentioned above exclude the possibility of critical layers and overhanging wave profiles.

During the last decade, one more interesting and more challenging problem in the theory of steady water waves is to prove the existence of waves with critical layers where the horizontal fluid velocity in the moving frame vanishes, or the existence of overhanging waves which means that the free surface is not the graph of a function. Such waves occur even in the case of non-zero constant vorticity. In
[
49
]
, Wahlén constructed for the first time small-amplitude periodic waves with critical layers for the flows with non-constant vorticity, but the waves are not overhanging. A break-through work was done by Constantin, Strauss and Varvaruca
[
18
]
, in which
they constructed large-amplitude periodic waves with non-zero constant vorticity and the waves can be overturning. Hur and Wheeler obtained in
[
36
]
the overturning periodic waves for the flows with constant vorticity and weak gravity, by perturbing a family of explicit solutions with zero gravity. However, the study of solitary overhanging waves is more difficult than that of the periodic counterparts, because the unboundedness of the surface leads to compactness issues. Based on the analytic global bifurcation theory, Haziot and Wheeler in
[
32
]
constructed continuous curves of large-amplitude solutions, allowing for both internal stagnation points and overhanging waves profiles. The proof relies on a novel reformulation of the problem as an elliptic system for two scalar functions in a fixed domain, one describing the conformal map of the fluid region and the other the flow beneath the wave. The first construction of overhanging solitary gravity water waves having the approximate form of a disk joined to a strip by a thin neck was proved in
[
22
]
very recently by assuming that the vorticity is constant and the dimensionless gravitational constant
g>0
is sufficiently small. Note that the method developed in
[
22
]
can be applied to other overdetermined elliptic problems.
See
[
1
,
10
,
19
,
20
,
31
,
38
,
39
,
47
,
50
,
51
]
for more results on rotational water waves in the presence of critical layers or overhanging waves.

In this paper, we continue this topic and aim to construct the existence of large-amplitude solitary gravity waves which can be overhanging. Different from
[
36
]
, we consider the settings allowing for general vorticity distributions.
To achieve the goal, we need to overcome two principal difficulties: the lack of compactness stemming from the unbounded domain, and the added complexity arising from a general vorticity distribution. To resolve these issues, we adopt a new functional formulation and apply a singular bifurcation argument introduced by Chen, Walsh and Wheeler in
[
11
,
12
]
.
The rest part of this paper is organized as follows. In section 2, we present the governing equations of our problem and reformulate the problem into an equivalent system in term of conformal variables. Then we further reformulate the problem into an operator form which is strongly related to an elliptic system. In section 3, We present the linearization of the operator along the trivial solution and prove the functional properties of the linearized operator. In section 4, we construct the small-amplitude solitary water waves by a recently derived center manifold reduction theorem. In section 5, we give the velocity in the conformal variables, and prove the solutions satisfying a monotonicity property which is referred to as the nodal property. In section 6, we construct an invariant known as the flow force, and apply its invariance to show the non-existence of bore solution, which implies the compactness properties. Finally in section 7, we construct large-amplitude solution and prove our main result.

2.
Governing equations and reformulations

In this section, we present the governing equations of our problem and give the equivalent reformulations in term of conformal mapping.

2.1.
Governing equations

Let
\Omega
be an unbounded fluid domain in the
(X,Y)
-plane, bounded below by the flat bed
Y=0
and above by the free surface
\mathcal{S}
. We assume that the fluid domain
\Omega

\mathcal{R}:=\{(x,y)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}:0<y<d\}.

The upper and lower boundaries of
\mathcal{R}
are denoted by

\Gamma:=\{(x,y)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}:y=d\}\quad\mathrm{and}\quad B:=\{(x,y)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}:y=0\}.

In this frame, we are working with the following two-dimensional Eulerian governing equations in terms of the velocity
(U,V)
and pressure

\displaystyle U_{X}+V_{Y}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\mathrm{in~}\Omega,

(2.1b)

\displaystyle UU_{X}+VU_{y}

\displaystyle=-P_{X}

\displaystyle\mathrm{in~}\Omega,

(2.1c)

\displaystyle UV_{X}+VV_{Y}

\displaystyle=-P_{Y}-g

\displaystyle\mathrm{in~}\Omega,

coupled with the kinematic boundary conditions

(
2.1
d)

\displaystyle U\eta_{x}-V\xi_{y}

\displaystyle=0\quad

\displaystyle\mathrm{on~}\mathcal{S},

(
2.1
e)

\displaystyle V

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\mathrm{on~}Y=0,

and the dynamic boundary

(
2.1
f)

P=P_{\mathrm{atm}}\quad\mathrm{on~}\mathcal{S},

here
P_{\mathrm{atm}}
is the constant atmospheric pressure and
g>0
is the gravity acceleration.
We focus on the solitary waves, which are solutions to (
2.1
) satisfying the asymptotic conditions

(
2.1

\eta(x,d)\to d,\quad\xi(x,d)\to\pm\infty\quad\mathrm{as~}x\to\pm\infty,

and

(

V(X,Y)\to 0,\quad U(X,Y)\to U(Y)\quad\mathrm{as~}X\to\pm\infty

is the shear flow at
X=\pm\infty

For the convenience of our analysis, we introduce a one-parameter family of shear flows

U(Y)=FU^{*}(Y),

where

is the Froude number to denote a dimensionless wave speed, given by

F^{2}=\frac{U^{2}(d)}{gd},

and

Due to the condition (

defined as

\Psi_{X}=-V,\quad\Psi_{Y}=U.

The kinematic boundary conditions (

\Psi

\Psi
such that

\Psi=m~\mathrm{on~}\mathcal{S}\quad\mathrm{and}\quad\Psi=0~\mathrm{on~}Y=0,

where

is some constant to denote the relative mass flux.

Let us define the vorticity function as

\mathrm{curl}(U,V)=U_{Y}-V_{X}
.
Taking the curl of (
2.1b
) and (
2.1c
), we can see that there exists a vorticity function such that

\Delta\Psi=-\gamma(\Psi).

Moreover, it follows from the Bernoulli’s law that

P+\frac{1}{2}|\nabla\Psi|^{2}+gY+G(\Psi)=\mathrm{const}\quad\mathrm{in~}\Omega,

where
G(s)=\int_{0}^{s}\gamma(t)dt.
To sum up, in the
(X,Y)-
plane,
\Psi
satisfies the following overdetermined problem

(2.2a)

\displaystyle\Delta\Psi

\displaystyle=-\gamma(\Psi)

\displaystyle\mathrm{in~}\Omega,

(2.2b)

\displaystyle\Psi

\displaystyle=m

\displaystyle\mathrm{on~}\mathcal{S},

(2.2c)

\displaystyle\Psi

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\mathrm{on~}Y=0,

(2.2d)

\displaystyle|\nabla\Psi|^{2}+2g(Y-d)

\displaystyle=Q

\displaystyle\mathrm{on~}\mathcal{S},

where

A solitary wave solution to (

\Psi_{X}\to 0,\quad\Psi_{Y}\to FU^{*}(Y)\quad\mathrm{as~}X\to\pm\infty.

The total head

and the mass flux

can be given in terms of the Froude number by

Q=F^{2}gd\quad\mathrm{and}\quad m=F\int_{0}^{d}U^{*}(Y)dY.

The vorticity function
\gamma
is given in terms of

and

\gamma(s)=-FU^{*}_{Y}(Y)\quad\mathrm{with~~~}s=F\int_{0}^{Y}U^{*}(t)dt.

In the following, we require that the general vorticity function

such that

(2.3)

By introducing the following dimensionless variables,

we obtain the final equations of the problem (for convenience, we drop the tilde)

(2.4a)

\displaystyle\Delta\Psi

\displaystyle=-\gamma(\Psi)

\displaystyle\mathrm{in~}\Omega,

(2.4b)

\displaystyle\Psi

\displaystyle=1

\displaystyle\mathrm{on~}\mathcal{S},

(2.4c)

\displaystyle\Psi

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\mathrm{on~}Y=0,

(2.4d)

\displaystyle|\nabla\Psi|^{2}+2\alpha(Y-1)

\displaystyle=\mu

Conformal mapping

From now on, we use the dimensionless notations but the same labels as before to denote the domain

\mathcal{R}:=\{(x,y)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}:0<y<1\},\quad\Gamma:=\{(x,y)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}:y=1\},\quad B:=\{(x,y)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}:y=0\}.

By the chain principle and the Cauchy-Riemann equation, equations (

3.
Linearization and Functional Analytic Formulation

(3.2)

\begin{cases}\Delta\mathcal{A}_{w}(\phi,w,\alpha)\dot{w}=-2\gamma(\phi+\psi_{\mathrm{triv}})\left\langle(0,1)+\nabla\zeta_{[w]}\big|_{\Gamma},\nabla\zeta_{[\dot{w}]}\big|_{\Gamma}\right\rangle\quad&\mathrm{in~}\mathcal{R},\\
\mathcal{A}_{w}(\phi,w,\alpha)\dot{w}=0&\mathrm{on~}\Gamma\cup B,\end{cases}

and

(3.3)

\begin{cases}\Delta\mathcal{A}_{\phi}(\phi,w,\alpha)\dot{\phi}=-\gamma^{\prime}(\phi+\psi_{\mathrm{triv}})\left((0,1)+\nabla\zeta_{[w]}\big|_{\Gamma}\right)^{2}\dot{\phi}\quad&\mathrm{in~}\mathcal{R},\\
\mathcal{A}_{\phi}(\phi,w,\alpha)\dot{\phi}=0&\mathrm{on~}\Gamma\cup B.\end{cases}

In particular, for
\phi=0
and
w=0
, it follows from (
3.2
)-(
3.3
) that
\mathcal{A}_{w}^{0}\dot{w}
and
\mathcal{A}_{\phi}^{0}\dot{\phi}
are the unique solutions of

\begin{cases}\Delta\mathcal{A}_{w}^{0}\dot{w}=-2\gamma(\psi_{\mathrm{triv}})\partial_{y}\zeta_{[\dot{w}]}\quad&\mathrm{in~}\mathcal{R},\\
\mathcal{A}_{w}^{0}\dot{w}=0&\mathrm{on~}\Gamma\cup B,\end{cases}

and

\begin{cases}\Delta\mathcal{A}_{\phi}^{0}\dot{\phi}=-\gamma^{\prime}(\psi_{\mathrm{triv}})\dot{\phi}\quad&\mathrm{in~}\mathcal{R},\\
\mathcal{A}_{\phi}^{0}\dot{\phi}=0&\mathrm{on~}\Gamma\cup B.\end{cases}

Obviously, it follows from (
3.1
) that

(3.4)

\displaystyle\partial_{(\phi,w)}\mathcal{F}_{1}(0,0,\alpha)(\dot{\phi},\dot{w})=

\displaystyle\dot{\phi}-\mathcal{A}_{\phi}^{0}\dot{\phi}-\mathcal{A}_{w}^{0}\dot{w},

\displaystyle\partial_{(\phi,w)}\mathcal{F}_{2}(0,0,\alpha)(\dot{\phi},\dot{w})=

\displaystyle\psi_{\mathrm{triv},y}(1)\left(\mathrm{\partial}_{y}(\mathcal{A}_{\phi}^{0}\dot{\phi}+\mathcal{A}_{w}^{0}\dot{w})\big|_{\Gamma}\right)+\alpha\dot{w}-\mu\partial_{y}\zeta_{[\dot{w}]}\big|_{\Gamma}.

Before studying the functional properties of the linearized operator
\mathcal{F}_{(\phi,w)}
, let us introduce an isomorphism to facilitate the subsequent analysis. Such an isomorphism is the so-called
\mathcal{T}
-isomorphism. See
[
3
,
24
,
40
]
.
To do this, we introduce the spaces

\tilde{\mathcal{X}}:=\{\theta\in C^{3+\beta}(\overline{\mathcal{R}})\cap C_{0}^{2}(\mathcal{R}):\theta|_{\Gamma}\in C^{2+\beta}(\Gamma),~\theta=0~\mathrm{on~}B\}

and

\tilde{\mathcal{X}}_{b}:=\{\theta\in C_{b}^{3+\beta}(\overline{\mathcal{R}}):\theta|_{\Gamma}\in C_{b}^{2+\beta}(\Gamma),~\theta=0~\mathrm{on~}B\}

with the norm

\|\theta\|_{\tilde{\mathcal{X}}_{b}}=\|\theta\|_{C_{b}^{3+\beta}(\overline{\mathcal{R}})}+\|\theta|_{\Gamma}\|_{C_{b}^{2+\beta}(\Gamma)}.

where the
\theta
is called the “good unknown” in
[
3
]
.

Define the linear isomorphism
\mathcal{T}:\tilde{\mathcal{X}}\to\mathcal{X}
by

(3.5)

\mathcal{T}\theta=\left(\theta-\frac{\psi_{\mathrm{triv},y}}{\psi_{\mathrm{triv},y}(1)}\zeta_{[\theta|_{\Gamma}]},-\frac{\theta|_{\Gamma}}{\psi_{\mathrm{triv},y}(1)}\right),

which has the inverse given by

(3.6)

[\mathcal{T}]^{-1}(\phi,w)=\phi-\psi_{\mathrm{triv},y}\zeta_{[w]}.

Lemma 3.1
.

For all
\theta\in\tilde{\mathcal{X}},
it holds that

\mathcal{A}^{0}_{w}(\theta|_{\Gamma})+\mathcal{A}^{0}_{\phi}(\psi_{\mathrm{triv},y}\zeta_{[\theta|_{\Gamma}]})=(\psi_{\mathrm{triv},y}-\psi_{\mathrm{triv},y}(1))\zeta_{[\theta|_{\Gamma}]}.

Proof.

Let
f:=\mathcal{A}^{0}_{w}(\theta|_{\Gamma})+\mathcal{A}^{0}_{\phi}(\psi_{\mathrm{triv},y}\zeta_{[\theta|_{\Gamma}]})-(\psi_{\mathrm{triv},y}-\psi_{\mathrm{triv},y}(1))\zeta_{[\theta|_{\Gamma}]}
. Then

\displaystyle\Delta f

\displaystyle=-2\gamma(\psi_{\mathrm{triv}})\partial_{y}\zeta_{[\theta|_{\Gamma}]}-\gamma^{\prime}(\psi_{\mathrm{triv}})\psi_{\mathrm{triv},y}\zeta_{[\theta|_{\Gamma}]}-\psi_{\mathrm{triv},yyy}\zeta_{[\theta|_{\Gamma}]}-2\psi_{\mathrm{triv},yy}\partial_{y}\zeta_{[\theta|_{\Gamma}]}

\displaystyle=-2\gamma(\psi_{\mathrm{triv}})\partial_{y}\zeta_{[\theta|_{\Gamma}]}-\gamma^{\prime}(\psi_{\mathrm{triv}})\psi_{\mathrm{triv},y}\zeta_{[\theta|_{\Gamma}]}+\gamma^{\prime}(\psi_{\mathrm{triv}})\psi_{\mathrm{triv},y}\zeta_{[\theta|_{\Gamma}]}+2\gamma(\psi_{\mathrm{triv}})\partial_{y}\zeta_{[\theta|_{\Gamma}]}

\displaystyle=0

in
\mathcal{R}
and vanishes at the top and bottom. By Theorem
B.1
, we can deduce that
f\equiv 0
.
∎

Now we introduce the operators
\mathcal{L}(\phi,w,\alpha)
and
\mathcal{L}^{0}
, which are defined by

\mathcal{L}(\phi,w,\alpha)=\mathcal{F}_{(\phi,w)}(\phi,w,\alpha)\circ\mathcal{T},

and

\mathcal{L}^{0}:=\mathcal{L}(0,0,\alpha)=\mathcal{F}_{(\phi,w)}(0,0,\alpha)\circ\mathcal{T}=(\mathcal{L}_{1}^{0},\mathcal{L}_{2}^{0}),

It follows from (
3.4
), (
3.5
) and Lemma
3.1
that

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{0}_{1}\theta=\theta-(\mathcal{A}_{\phi}^{0}\theta+\zeta_{[\theta|_{\Gamma}]}),

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{0}_{2}\theta=\left(\psi_{\mathrm{triv},y}(1)\partial_{y}\mathcal{A}_{\phi}^{0}\theta-\left(\frac{\alpha}{\psi_{\mathrm{triv},y}(1)}-\gamma(1)\right)\theta+\frac{\mu}{\psi_{\mathrm{triv},y}(1)}\partial_{y}\zeta_{[\theta|_{\Gamma}]}\right)\bigg|_{\Gamma}.

Note that
\mathcal{L}_{1}^{0}\theta
is the unique solution of the problem

(3.7)

\begin{cases}\Delta[\mathcal{L}_{1}^{0}\theta]=\Delta\theta+\gamma^{\prime}(\psi_{\mathrm{triv}})\theta\quad&\mathrm{in~}\mathcal{R},\\
\mathcal{L}_{1}^{0}\theta=0&\mathrm{on~}\Gamma\cup B,\end{cases}

and
\mathcal{L}_{2}^{0}\theta
can be rewritten as

(3.8)

\mathcal{L}_{2}^{0}\theta=\left(\psi_{\mathrm{triv},y}(1)\partial_{y}(\theta-\mathcal{L}_{1}^{0}\theta-\zeta_{[\theta|_{\Gamma}]})-\left(\frac{\alpha}{\psi_{\mathrm{triv},y}(1)}-\gamma(1)\right)\theta+\frac{\mu}{\psi_{\mathrm{triv},y}(1)}\partial_{y}\zeta_{[\theta|_{\Gamma}]}\right)\bigg|_{\Gamma}.

To study the kernel of
\mathcal{F}
, it sufficies to study the kernel of
\mathcal{L}^{0}
.
Besides,
\mathcal{L}^{0}\theta=0
if and only if
\theta
satisfies

\begin{cases}\Delta\theta+\gamma^{\prime}(\psi_{\mathrm{triv}})\theta=0\quad\mathrm{in~}\mathcal{R},\\
\left(\psi_{\mathrm{triv},y}(1)\partial_{y}\theta-\left(\frac{\alpha}{\psi_{\mathrm{triv},y}(1)}-\gamma(1)\right)\theta+\left(\frac{\mu}{\psi_{\mathrm{triv},y}(1)}-\psi_{\mathrm{triv},y}(1)\right)\partial_{y}\zeta_{[\theta|_{\Gamma}]}\right)\bigg|_{\Gamma}=0.\end{cases}

It follows from (
2.8c
) that

\displaystyle\frac{\mu}{\psi_{\mathrm{triv},y}(1)}-\psi_{\mathrm{triv},y}(1)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mu-\psi_{\mathrm{triv},y}^{2}(1)}{\psi_{\mathrm{triv},y}(1)}=0.

Thus we have deduced the following boundary value problem

(3.9)

\begin{cases}\Delta\theta+\gamma^{\prime}(\psi_{\mathrm{triv}})\theta=0\quad&\mathrm{in~}\mathcal{R},\\
\partial_{y}\theta-\tilde{\alpha}\theta=0,&\mathrm{on~}\Gamma,\\
\theta=0,&\mathrm{on~}B,\end{cases}

where

\tilde{\alpha}=-\frac{\gamma(1)}{\psi_{\mathrm{triv},y}(1)}+\frac{\alpha}{\psi_{\mathrm{triv},y}^{2}(1)}.

Lemma 3.2
.

For
(\phi,w,\alpha)\in\mathcal{U}
, the linearized operator
\partial_{(\phi,w)}\mathcal{F}(\phi,w,\alpha):\mathcal{X}_{b}\to\mathcal{Y}_{b}
and the operator
\mathcal{L}(\phi,w,\alpha):\tilde{\mathcal{X}}_{b}\to\mathcal{Y}_{b}
have the following estimates

(3.10)

\|(\dot{\phi},\dot{w})\|_{\mathcal{X}_{b}}\leq C\bigg(\|\partial_{(\phi,w)}\mathcal{F}(\phi,w,\alpha)(\dot{\phi},\dot{w})\|_{\mathcal{Y}_{b}}+\|\mathcal{A}_{\phi}(\phi,w,\alpha)\dot{\phi}+\mathcal{A}_{w}(\phi,w,\alpha)\dot{w}\|_{C_{b}^{0}(\overline{\mathcal{R}})}\bigg)

and

(3.11)

\|\theta\|_{\tilde{\mathcal{X}}_{b}}\leq C\bigg(\|\mathcal{L}(\phi,w,\alpha)\theta\|_{\mathcal{Y}_{b}}+\|(\mathcal{A}_{\phi}(\phi,w,\alpha)\circ\mathcal{T})\theta+(\mathcal{A}_{w}(\phi,w,\alpha)\circ\mathcal{T})\theta\|_{C^{0}_{b}(\mathcal{R})}\bigg),

where the constant
C
relies on
\alpha
, the norm of
\phi,w
, the minor constant
\sigma(w,\alpha)
and the norm of vorticity function.

Proof.

In the following proof, the constant
C
will vary from line to line.
Due to (
3.1a
), we have

(3.12)

\|\dot{\phi}\|_{C_{b}^{3+\beta}(\mathcal{R})}\leq\|\partial_{(\phi,w)}\mathcal{F}_{1}(\phi,w,\alpha)(\dot{\phi},\dot{w})\|_{C_{b}^{3+\beta}(\mathcal{R})}+\|\mathcal{A}_{\phi}(\phi,w,\alpha)\dot{\phi}+\mathcal{A}_{w}(\phi,w,\alpha)\dot{w}\|_{C_{b}^{3+\beta}(\mathcal{R})}.

Moreover, by (
3.1b
) and the definition of the set
\mathcal{U}
, we have

(3.13)

\displaystyle\|\dot{w}\|_{C_{b}^{2+\beta}(\Gamma)}\leq

\displaystyle C(\sigma^{-1}(w,\alpha))\bigg(\bigg\|\partial_{(\phi,w)}\mathcal{F}_{2}(\phi,w,\alpha)(\dot{\phi},\dot{w})\bigg\|_{C_{b}^{2+\beta}(\Gamma)}

\displaystyle+\bigg\|\mathcal{A}_{\phi}(\phi,w,\alpha)\dot{\phi}+\mathcal{A}_{w}(\phi,w,\alpha)\dot{w}\bigg\|_{C_{b}^{3+\beta}(\Gamma)}\bigg).

By (
3.2
) and (
3.3
), we notice that
\mathcal{A}_{\phi}(\phi,w,\alpha)\dot{\phi}+\mathcal{A}_{w}(\phi,w,\alpha)\dot{w}
satisfied the following linear elliptic PDE

\begin{cases}\begin{split}\Delta[\mathcal{A}_{w}(\phi,w,\alpha)\dot{w}+\mathcal{A}_{\phi}(\phi,w,\alpha)\dot{\phi}]=&-2\gamma(\phi+\psi_{\mathrm{triv}})\left\langle(0,1)+\nabla\zeta_{[w]}\big|_{\Gamma},\nabla\zeta_{[\dot{w}]}\big|_{\Gamma}\right\rangle\\
&-\gamma^{\prime}(\phi+\psi_{\mathrm{triv}})\left((0,1)+\nabla\zeta_{[w]}\big|_{\Gamma}\right)^{2}\dot{\phi}\quad\mathrm{in~}\mathcal{R},\end{split}\\
\mathcal{A}_{w}(\phi,w,\alpha)\dot{w}+\mathcal{A}_{\phi}(\phi,w,\alpha)\dot{\phi}=0\quad\mathrm{on~}\Gamma\cup B,\end{cases}

coupled with the boundary condition (
3.1b
). By the (
2.15
) and the definition of the set
\mathcal{U}
in (
2.13
), we can deduce that

\bigg|\partial_{y}\mathcal{A}(\phi,w,\alpha)\bigg|_{\Gamma}+\psi_{\mathrm{triv},y}(1)\bigg|^{2}\geq\sigma(w,\alpha)>0,

which implies the uniform obliqueness condition holds. Applying the Schauder estimates in Lemma
C.1
yields

(3.14)

\|\mathcal{A}_{w}(\phi,w,\alpha)\dot{w}+\mathcal{A}_{\phi}(\phi,w,\alpha)\dot{\phi}\|_{C_{b}^{3+\beta}(\overline{\mathcal{R}})}\leq C(\|\mathcal{A}_{w}(\phi,w,\alpha)\dot{w}+\mathcal{A}_{\phi}(\phi,w,\alpha)\dot{\phi}\|_{C^{0}_{b}(\overline{\mathcal{R}})}),

where the constant
C=C\left(\|\gamma\|_{C^{1+1}(\mathbb{R})},\|(\phi,w)\|_{\mathcal{X}_{b}}\right)
.
Then, we can deduce from (
3.13
)-(
3.14
) that

(3.15)

\displaystyle\|\dot{w}\|_{C_{b}^{2+\beta}(\Gamma)}

\displaystyle\leq C(\|\partial_{(\phi,w)}\mathcal{F}_{2}(\phi,w,\alpha)(\dot{\phi},\dot{w})\|_{C_{b}^{2+\beta}(\Gamma)}+\|\mathcal{A}_{w}(\phi,w,\alpha)\dot{w}+\mathcal{A}_{\phi}(\phi,w,\alpha)\dot{\phi}\|_{C^{0}_{b}(\overline{\mathcal{R}})}),

where the constant
C=C\left(\sigma(w,\alpha),\|\gamma\|_{C^{1+1}(\mathbb{R})},\|(\phi,w)\|_{\mathcal{X}_{b}}\right)
.
Thus, the inequality (
3.10
) is established via (
3.12
) and (
3.15
).

Moreover, by (
3.6
), we can deduce that

\|\theta\|_{C^{3+\beta}(\mathcal{R})}\leq C(\|\dot{\phi}\|_{C^{3+\beta}(\mathcal{R})}),\quad\|\theta\|_{C^{2+\beta}(\Gamma)}\leq C(\|\dot{w}\|_{C^{2+\beta}(\Gamma)}),

for some constant
C>0
depending only on

. Consequently, the inequality (
3.11
) holds.
∎

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
\alpha_{\mathrm{cr}}
be given in
(
3.18
)
. Then the operator
\mathcal{L}^{0}:\tilde{\mathcal{X}}_{b}\to\mathcal{Y}_{b}
has a trivial kernel for any
\alpha<\alpha_{\mathrm{cr}}
.

Proof.

Let
\theta(x,y)=u(x)v(y)
be the solution of problem (
3.9
). Then we obtain the following self-adjoint Sturm-Liouville problem

(3.16a)

\displaystyle-v_{yy}-\gamma^{\prime}(\psi_{\mathrm{triv}})v=\nu v,

(3.16b)

\displaystyle v_{y}(1)=\tilde{\alpha}v(1),

(3.16c)

\displaystyle v(0)=0.

It follows from the standard spectrum theory that problem (
3.16
) admits a countable
number of simple eigenvalues
\nu_{0}<\nu_{1}<\cdots
with
\nu_{n}\to\infty
.

First we show that
\nu=0
is an eigenvalue of (
3.16
) if and only if

\tilde{\alpha}=\tilde{\alpha}_{\mathrm{cr}}=-\frac{\gamma(1)}{\psi_{\mathrm{triv},y}(1)}+\frac{1}{\psi_{\mathrm{triv},y}^{2}(1)\int_{0}^{1}\frac{dy}{\psi_{\mathrm{triv},y}^{2}(y)}}.

Indeed, for
\nu=0
, differentiating (
2.7
) with respect to
y
, we have that
v_{1}=\psi_{\mathrm{triv},y}(y)
is a non-trivial solution. By the Liouville’s formulation, the general solution of (
3.16a
) with (
3.16c
) is

(3.17)

v(y)=C\psi_{\mathrm{triv},y}(y)\int_{0}^{y}\frac{dt}{\psi_{\mathrm{triv},y}^{2}(t)}

with
C
being an arbitrary constant.
It is easy to see that (
3.16b
) is satisfied if and only if
\tilde{\alpha}=\tilde{\alpha}_{\mathrm{cr}}
. Besides, the critical value
has the following relation

(3.18)

\alpha_{\mathrm{cr}}=\tilde{\alpha}\psi_{\mathrm{triv},y}^{2}(1)+\gamma(1)\psi_{\mathrm{triv},y}(1)=\frac{1}{\int_{0}^{1}\frac{dy}{\psi_{\mathrm{triv},y}^{2}(y)}}.

Moreover, one readily see that the eigenspace for
\nu=0
is two-dimensional and generated by
\varphi_{0}(y)
and
x\varphi_{0}(y)
, where
\varphi_{0}(y)
is the eigenfunction with
\nu_{0}=0
, that is,

(3.19)

\mathrm{Ker~}(\mathcal{L}(0,0,\alpha_{\mathrm{cr}}))=\text{span}\{\varphi_{0}(y),x\varphi_{0}(y)\},

where the eigenfunction
\varphi_{0}(y)
(depending on the vorticity function
\gamma(\psi)
) corresponding to
\nu_{0}=0
differs from the general solution
v
in (
3.17
) only by a constant factor, which is determined by the choice of the nontrivial solution
v_{1}
. By the classical Sturm-Liouville theory, we can choose
\varphi_{0}(y)>0
for
y\in(0,1]
.

Next, we know that the eigenvalue of (
3.16
) can be characterized by the following minimization problem

(3.20)

\nu_{0}(\tilde{\alpha})=\inf_{v\in H^{1}(0,1),v(0)=0,v\not\equiv 0}\frac{\int_{0}^{1}v_{y}^{2}-\gamma^{\prime}(\psi_{\mathrm{triv}})v^{2}dy-\tilde{\alpha}v^{2}(1)}{\int_{0}^{1}v^{2}dy}.

Differentiating (
3.20
) with respect to
\tilde{\alpha}
, we can easily see that
\nu_{0}(\tilde{\alpha})
is strictly decreasing in
\tilde{\alpha}
. Besides, we can also deduce that
\nu_{0}(\tilde{\alpha})>0
as
\tilde{\alpha}<\tilde{\alpha}_{\mathrm{cr}}
.

Multiplying the first equation of (
3.9
) by
\theta\in\tilde{\mathcal{X}}_{b}
and integrating by parts, we can obtain

(3.21)

\int_{\mathcal{R}}|\nabla\theta|^{2}dxdy=\tilde{\alpha}\int_{\Gamma}\theta^{2}dx+\int_{\mathcal{R}}\gamma^{\prime}(\psi_{\mathrm{triv}})\theta^{2}dxdy.

For any fixed
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
, it follows from (
3.20
) that

(3.22)

\int_{0}^{1}(\partial_{y}\theta)^{2}-\gamma^{\prime}(\psi_{\mathrm{triv}})\theta^{2}(y)dy-\tilde{\alpha}\theta^{2}(x,1)\geq\nu_{0}(\tilde{\alpha})\int_{0}^{1}\theta^{2}dy.

Now integrating (
3.22
) with respect to
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
, we have

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{0}^{1}(\partial_{y}\theta)^{2}-\gamma^{\prime}(\psi_{\mathrm{triv}})\theta^{2}(y)dydx-\tilde{\alpha}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\theta^{2}(x,1)dx\geq\nu_{0}(\tilde{\alpha})\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\int_{0}^{1}\theta^{2}dydx.

Using (
3.21
), we obtain that

-\int_{\mathcal{R}}(\partial_{x}\theta)^{2}dxdy\geq\nu_{0}(\tilde{\alpha})\int_{\mathcal{R}}\theta^{2}dxdy.

Combining the monotonicity of
\nu_{0}(\tilde{\alpha})
, we see that equation (
3.9
) admits only the trivial solution for
\tilde{\alpha}<\tilde{\alpha}_{\mathrm{cr}}
, which is just
\alpha<\alpha_{\mathrm{cr}}
.
∎

Example 3.5
.

When the vorticity function is linear, that is

Lemma 3.6
.

For
(\phi,w,\alpha)\in\mathcal{U}
, the operator
\mathcal{L}(\phi,w,\alpha)
is locally proper both as a map
\tilde{\mathcal{X}}_{b}\to\mathcal{Y}_{b}

\tilde{\mathcal{X}}\to\mathcal{Y}

\mathcal{F}_{(\phi,w)}(\phi,w,\alpha)

\mathcal{X}_{b}\to\mathcal{Y}_{b}

By the translation argument and the Lemma
C.1
, on can show that
\mathcal{L}(\phi,w,\alpha):\tilde{\mathcal{X}}_{b}\to\mathcal{Y}_{b}

has a trivial kernel
[
48
]
. By Lemma
3.3
, we can deduce that
\mathcal{L}(\phi,w,\alpha):\tilde{\mathcal{X}}_{b}\to\mathcal{Y}_{b}

Let
\{\dot{\theta}_{n}\}\subset\tilde{\mathcal{X}}

\{\mathcal{L}(\phi,w,\alpha)\dot{\theta}_{n}\}
is a convergent sequence in

\mathcal{Y}_{b}

\mathcal{X}
to
\mathcal{Y}
.
∎

Lemma 3.7
.

For
(\phi,w,\alpha)\in\mathcal{U}
, the operator
\mathcal{L}^{0}
is invertible both as a map
\tilde{\mathcal{X}}_{b}\to\mathcal{Y}_{b}
and as a map
\tilde{\mathcal{X}}\to\mathcal{Y}
. Meanwhile, the operator
\mathcal{F}_{(\phi,w)}(0,0,\alpha)

\mathcal{X}_{b}\to\mathcal{Y}_{b}

\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}

Proof.

\theta\in\tilde{\mathcal{X}}_{b}
,

and

.
Finally, since
\mathcal{T}
is an isomorphism, the operator
\mathcal{F}_{(\phi,w)}(0,0,\alpha)
is consequently invertible both from
\mathcal{X}_{b}
to
\mathcal{Y}_{b}
and from

Example 4.3

6.

In this section, we study an important invariant which is the so-called flow force in
[

,
8
,
13
]
.
In our case, the flow force is
given as

(6.1)

S=\int_{x=\mathrm{const}}(P-P_{\mathrm{atm}}+U^{2})dY-UVdX,

where the integral is taken over a vertical cross section of the fluid and the second term comes from the divergence form of the horizontal component (

) of the momentum equation,

(U^{2}+P-P_{\mathrm{atm}})_{X}+(UV)_{Y}=0.

Lemma 6.1
.

Assume that
(\psi,\eta,\alpha)
solves (
2.11
). Then the flow force (

(6.2)

Proof.

Note that in the nondimensionless variables, the Bernoulli law is equivalent to

P+\frac{1}{2}(U^{2}+V^{2})+\alpha(\eta-1)-G(\Psi)=\frac{\mu}{2}+P_{\mathrm{atm}}-G(1),

where

.
In the conformal variables, we have the form (
6.2
).
Differentiating
S
with respect to
x
, we obtain

(6.3)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dx}S(x;\eta,\psi,\alpha)=\int_{0}^{1}

\displaystyle\bigg(uu_{x}-vv_{x}-\alpha\eta_{x}+G(\psi)\psi_{x}\bigg)\eta_{y}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\bigg(\frac{1}{2}(u^{2}-v^{2})-\alpha(\eta-1)+(G(\psi)-G(1))+\frac{\mu}{2}\bigg)\eta_{xy}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle u_{x}v\eta_{x}+uv_{x}\eta_{x}-uv\eta_{yy}dy.

By integrating by parts to the second term and the last term, we can calculate that the right hand side of (

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}

\displaystyle\bigg(uu_{x}-vv_{x}-\alpha\eta_{x}+\gamma(\psi)\psi_{x}\bigg)\eta_{y}-\int_{0}^{1}(uu_{y}-vv_{y}-\alpha\eta_{y}+\gamma(\psi)\psi_{y})\eta_{x}

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{1}(vu_{x}\eta_{x}+uv_{x}\eta_{x}+(uv)_{y}\eta_{y})dy+\bigg[\bigg(\frac{1}{2}(u^{2}-v^{2})-\alpha(\eta-1)\eta_{x}+\frac{\mu}{2}\bigg)\eta_{x}-uv\eta_{y}\bigg]\bigg|_{\Gamma}

by (
5.2c
)-(
5.2d
), we can see that the last term vanishes. Now we show that the integral term vanishes as well. By direct calculation and appling (
5.2a
)
-(
5.2b
) and (

) , we find that

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle u_{x}\eta_{x}-vv_{x}\eta_{y}-uu_{y}\eta_{x}+vv_{y}\eta_{x}+u_{x}v\eta_{x}+uv_{x}\eta_{x}+\eta_{y}(u_{y}v+uv_{y})-\gamma(\psi)v|\nabla\eta|^{2}

\displaystyle=u\gamma(\psi)\eta_{y}\eta_{x}-u\gamma(\psi)\eta_{y}\eta_{x}+\gamma(\psi)v|\nabla\eta|^{2}-\gamma(\psi)v|\nabla\eta|^{2}=0,

which shows that

\frac{d}{dx}S(x;\eta,\psi,\alpha)=0.

∎

Since we are working the an unbounded domain, the compact embeddings between the Hölder spaces do not make sense. However, we shall see that, for monotone waves, the only way to lose compactness is through the existence of a bore.

A bore is a solution

of (

with

(6.4)

\hat{\eta}_{\mathrm{triv}}(y;d)=dy,

and
\hat{\psi}_{\mathrm{triv}}(y;d)
satisfies the following problem

(6.5)

\displaystyle\hat{\psi}_{\mathrm{triv},yy}=-\gamma(\hat{\psi}_{\mathrm{triv}})d^{2}\quad

\displaystyle\mathrm{in~}(0,1),

\displaystyle\hat{\psi}_{\mathrm{triv}}=1\quad

\displaystyle\mathrm{on~}y=1,

\displaystyle\hat{\psi}_{\mathrm{triv}}=0\quad

\displaystyle\mathrm{on~}y=0,

\displaystyle\hat{\psi}_{\mathrm{triv},y}^{2}(1)=(\mu-2\alpha(d-1))d^{2}\quad

\displaystyle\mathrm{on~}y=1.

Note that

(6.6)

\hat{\psi}_{\mathrm{triv}}(y;d)=\hat{\psi}_{\mathrm{triv}}(dy;1),

)-(

Moreover,
\hat{S}(\hat{d}):=S(x,\hat{\psi}_{\mathrm{triv}}(\cdot,\hat{d}),\hat{\eta}_{\mathrm{triv}}(\cdot,\hat{d})).

Lemma

\hat{S}(d_{-})=\hat{S}(d_{+})=\hat{S}(1).

The total head

∎

Note that
\hat{Q}(d_{*})=\hat{Q}(1)

\frac{\hat{Q(1)}}{2}\Big(d_{*}-\frac{1}{d_{*}}\Big)=\alpha(d_{*}^{2}-d_{*}).

The function
\hat{S}(d)
satisfies

if and only if

Proof.

Note that

(6.9)

\int_{0}^{d}\frac{1}{2}\psi_{\mathrm{triv}}^{2}(y;1)+(G(\psi_{\mathrm{triv}}(y;1))-G(1))dy.

Differentiating
\hat{S}(d)
with respect to

7.3
). By the symmetry and monotonicity, the infimum in the first term is attained at the crest
x=0
, where

\mu-\frac{2\mu}{F^{2}(s)}\frac{\eta(s)-d}{d}=\frac{\mu U^{2}}{F^{2}gd}>0,

this first term vanishes
precisely when the fluid is approaching stagnation
U=V=0
at the crest. The second term in (
7.3
) provides a simple measure of the non-degeneracy of the conformal mapping. The third term vanishes only when the Froude number
F

tends to infinity. Finally, the last term vanishes only as
F
tends to critical value
F_{\mathrm{cr}}
.

Appendix B
Maximum Principles

We refer to
[
25
,
28
,
43
]
for the following maximum principles for elliptic PDEs.
Let
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
be a connected, open set (possibly unbounded), consider the the second-order operator

L=\Delta+c(x),

where
c\in C^{0}(\bar{\Omega})
. Let
u\in C^{2}(\Omega)\cap C^{0}(\bar{\Omega})
be a classical solution of
Lu=0
in
\Omega
.

Theorem B.1
.

(
Strong maximum principle
)
Suppose
u
attains a maximum on
\bar{\Omega}
at a point in the interior of
\Omega
. If
c\leq 0
in
\Omega
or if
\sup_{\Omega}u=0
, then
u
is a constant function.

Theorem B.2
.

(
Hopf boundary lemma
)
Suppose that
u
attains a maximum on
\bar{\Omega}
at a point
x^{*}\in\partial\Omega
for which there exists an open ball
B\subset\Omega
such that
\overline{B}\cap\partial\Omega=\{x^{*}\}
. Assume either
c\leq 0
in
\Omega
or else
\sup_{B}u=0
. Then
u
is a constant function or

\nu\cdot\nabla u(x^{*})>0,

where
\nu
is the outward unit normal to
\Omega
at
x^{*}
.

Theorem B.3
.

(
Serrin edge point lemma
)
Let
x^{*}\in\partial\Omega
be an edge point in the sense that near
x^{*}
it consists of two transversally intersecting
C^{2}
hypersurfaces
\{\gamma(x)=0\}
and
\{\sigma(x)=0\}
. Suppose that
\gamma,\sigma<0
in
\Omega
. If
u\in C^{2}(\bar{\Omega})
,
u>0
,
u(x^{*})=0
. Assume for a bluntness function
B
, for the edge
\{\gamma=0\}\cap\{\sigma=0\}
related to operator
L
, satisfies

B(x^{*})=0\quad\mathrm{and}\quad\partial_{\tau}B(x^{*})=0,

for every differential
\partial_{\tau}
tangential to
\{\gamma=0\}\cap\{\sigma=0\}
. Then for any unit vector
s
outward from
\Omega
at

, either
\partial_{s}u(x^{*})<0
or

.
