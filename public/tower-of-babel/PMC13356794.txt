China’s new quality productive forces in healthcare: spatial disparities, dynamic evolution, and convergence
Authors: Huimin Liu, Liqing Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe deep integration of breakthrough technological innovation and healthcare has given rise to new quality productive forces in healthcare, profoundly reshaping the spatial logic of medical resource allocation. However, existing studies lack an evaluation framework tailored to this emerging concept, and its spatiotemporal evolution and regional coordination remain insufficiently explored, hindering targeted policy-making for balanced healthcare development.MethodsWe measured the level of new quality productive forces in healthcare across 31 Chinese provinces during 2010–2023 using the CRITIC-entropy combined weighting method and TOPSIS method. Dagum Gini coefficient and kernel density analysis were applied to explore its spatial differentiation and temporal evolution characteristics. σ convergence, absolute β and conditional β convergence models were adopted to clarify its convergence pattern.ResultsChina’s new quality productive forces in healthcare showed an “initial decline then sustained growth” trend, peaking in 2023. New quality medical laborers exhibited the strongest performance, while new quality medical objects of labor were the weakest. Spatially, the Eastern Region had the highest level in 2023 and the Western Region recorded the lowest level; regional gaps narrowed steadily. Inter-group disparities dominated overall disparities during 2011–2019, while transvariation was the main source in 2010 and post-2020. Absolute β and conditional β convergence were observed, but no σ convergence existed.ConclusionChina’s new quality productive forces in healthcare have shown dynamic growth, yet they continue to face structural constraints characterized by persistently underdeveloped new quality medical objects of labor and narrowing yet non-negligible regional disparities. Our findings highlight the need for an integrated policy approach that involves implementing differentiated strategies aligned with the heterogeneous development levels and convergence characteristics of healthcare new quality productive forces across regions, while enhancing patient digital engagement and expanding the accessibility of high-quality online healthcare services.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-026-14971-8.Keywords: Healthcare, Technological innovation, New quality productive forces, Spatial disparities, Inequity, Dynamic evolution, Convergence

Introduction

Equitable access to healthcare is a cornerstone of the global health agenda, enshrined in Target 3.8 of the United Nations’ Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) which aims for universal health coverage [1]. Yet this vision faces significant challenges in countries with large populations and expansive territories, with China as a typical example. As the world’s second-largest population, China’s healthcare system faces constant pressure to address growing and diverse demands, making equity an operational imperative rather than a mere aspiration [2]. Despite dedicated government efforts, including the strategic development of national and regional medical centers to narrow regional gaps, disparities in healthcare resources remain deeply rooted [3]. Statistics reveal a pronounced concentration of advanced facilities and specialized human capital in eastern China, whereas others regions experience relative scarcity [4]. This severely limits service access and perpetuates health inequities. Underpinning this persistent imbalance are a path-dependent resource allocation mechanism and significant barriers to the diffusion of cutting-edge medical innovations [5, 6]. This impasse calls for a transformative driver that can break path dependence in resource allocation and reshape the spatial logic of healthcare supply. It is against this backdrop that new quality productive forces in healthcare emerge as a critical candidate to address long-standing inequities.

The urgency of exploring such a transformative driver is further amplified by the drivers of health expenditure, which pose significant challenges to healthcare system sustainability. Population ageing, a global structural demographic phenomenon, has become a key factor driving the growth of health expenditure, and relevant studies using word embedding techniques suggest that policies leveraging new technologies to improve older adults’ access to healthcare services can moderate such cost growth [7]. For China, empirical research based on Bayesian-VAR models confirms that population ageing exerts a relatively strong impact on per capita health expenditure, highlighting the need for integrated policies balancing economic, social and health-related factors [8]. Additionally, classic research on medical care costs indicates that while rising healthcare spending merits attention, overemphasis on cost containment may lead to welfare losses and reduced healthcare access, underscoring the importance of optimizing expenditure efficiency rather than merely curbing spending [9]. These findings reveal that China’s healthcare system faces the challenge of addressing ageing-driven expenditure pressure and unbalanced resource allocation, and traditional resource expansion models are inadequate to meet this challenge, further justifying the exploration of technology-driven new quality productive forces in healthcare to reshape healthcare supply.

To clarify this transformative driver, we first define new quality productive forces in healthcare as a technology‑driven production paradigm centered on three dimensions: new quality laborers, new quality means of labor, and new quality objects of labor. This paradigm represents a fundamental shift from the traditional healthcare productive forces that rely on incremental expansion of physical resources. The new quality attributes are embodied in the systematic transformation of the three dimensions, driven by technological breakthroughs and innovation empowerment [10]. It takes substantial improvement of total factor productivity in the healthcare sector as its key marker and aims to advance treatment efficiency, service capacity, and health outcomes [11].

New quality medical laborers transcend the conventional role of individual practitioners by integrating with intelligent technologies. Their new quality manifests through enhanced skills to operate advanced medical technologies and amplified productivity via human‑AI collaboration, distinguishing them from traditional laborers whose capacity is bounded by personal experience and manual effort [12]. New quality medical means of labor extend beyond physical capital to include digital healthcare infrastructure such as telemedicine platforms, as well as intangible assets like healthcare innovation and informatization. These tools enable data‑driven and remote healthcare delivery, capabilities absent in traditional healthcare material means [13]. New quality medical objects of labor shift from the patient seeking treatment to a conception encompassing both in‑person and online patients. Through remote monitoring and AI‑assisted diagnostics, patients become continuous sources of health data, facilitating proactive and personalized care rather than reactive episodic intervention [14].

The infusion of these new quality inputs across the three dimensions transforms healthcare production processes and inherently generates dual spatial forces. New quality medical laborers equipped with advanced skills and augmented productivity can deliver expertise remotely, while new quality means of labor such as telemedicine platforms enable services to transcend geographical constraints [15]. At the same time, new quality objects of labor enriched by continuous health data create incentives for high‑end talent, technological capital, and medical innovation to cluster in core areas where data infrastructure is concentrated [16]. This dual tension between inclusive diffusion and core‑area agglomeration suggests two empirically testable propositions: first, the distribution of healthcare new quality productive forces across regions should exhibit measurable spatial disparities; second, the evolution of these disparities over time should reflect the relative strength of diffusion versus agglomeration forces. This framework provides the theoretical foundation for examining the spatiotemporal and convergence patterns of healthcare new quality productive forces.

Existing research on new quality productive forces has mainly focused on their theoretical foundations, conceptual attributes, and formation mechanisms [17, 18]. Researchers generally agree that new quality productive forces go beyond pure technological progress, and they serve as a core driver of comprehensive socioeconomic transformation. Core elements such as big data, artificial intelligence, and technological innovation are widely recognized as key enablers, as they can reshape laborers, means of labor, and objects of labor to promote the leapfrog development of overall productive forces [19]. The existing literature, however, focuses predominantly on the macroeconomy or specific sectors like manufacturing and agriculture, leaving the healthcare domain comparatively underexplored [20–22]. The limited studies that do address new quality productive forces in healthcare remain largely theoretical, focusing on conceptual definitions and general improvement pathways. Meanwhile, research on healthcare resource allocation has a relatively mature foundation. Previous studies have analyzed spatial disparities from perspectives of economic development, policy intervention, and demographic structure [23, 24]. Some studies also discuss how technological innovation transforms healthcare services, such as the application of telemedicine and intelligent diagnostics [25, 26]. However, these studies rarely link such transformations to the framework of new quality productive forces in healthcare, let alone explore how this new paradigm reshapes the spatial pattern of resource allocation.

Globally, technology-driven healthcare transformation has become a focus of international research, with extensive exploration on how technological innovations reshape healthcare production models, optimize resource allocation, and address healthcare equity challenges [27, 28]. International studies in this field have centered on the application of cutting-edge technologies to reform healthcare supply systems, highlighting the role of technological progress in bridging regional and structural disparities in healthcare [29, 30]. Despite the growing global attention to technology-driven healthcare development, the existing international literature has largely focused on individual technologies or specific applications, lacking a unified theoretical framework that captures the systemic nature of technology‑driven transformation, which limits the integration of global experience into the exploration of healthcare transformation.

Relevant literature has laid theoretical groundwork for both fields, but the intersection of these two research strands remains underdeveloped. Three critical gaps stand out. First, existing studies on new quality productive forces lack a framework tailored to healthcare characteristics [22]. They fail to clarify the core dimensions of new quality productive forces in healthcare, leaving its specific strengths and weaknesses in practice unidentifiable. Second, research on healthcare resource allocation, despite focusing on dynamic factors, does not recognize new quality productive forces as a systematic dynamic paradigm [31, 32]. It remains confined to traditional dynamic variables and overlooks how the synergy of new quality laborers, means of labor, and objects of labor drives the spatial reallocation of healthcare resources. Third, quantitative analyses of new quality productive forces in healthcare from a spatiotemporal perspective remain scarce. Most relevant studies are macro qualitative discussions, which fail to reveal their temporal evolutionary patterns or spatial characteristics [11]. These gaps make it difficult to provide targeted support for balancing regional healthcare development through new quality productive forces.

This study focuses on quantifying and analyzing new quality productive forces in healthcare. First, we construct a three-dimensional evaluation index system rooted in Marxist productive forces theory. This system covers new quality medical laborers, means of labor, and objects of labor. Second, we measure the level of new quality productive forces in healthcare across 31 provinces in China from 2010 to 2023 using the CRITIC-entropy combined weighting method and the TOPSIS method. Based on these results, we use the Dagum Gini coefficient and kernel density analysis to explore spatial differentiation and temporal evolution characteristics. Third, we apply convergence, absolute convergence, and conditional convergence models to clarify the convergence pattern of new quality productive forces in healthcare. The study aims not only to identify the strengths and weaknesses of regional new quality productive forces in healthcare, but also to provide measurable evaluation tools and empirical evidence for leveraging this new paradigm to optimize healthcare resource allocation and promote balanced regional healthcare development.

Methods

Data resource

We conducted a study using provincial-level panel data from 31 provinces in mainland China spanning the period 2010 to 2023. The starting year 2010 marks the deepening phase of China’s new healthcare reform and the early expansion of internet-based healthcare services, while 2023 is the most recent year with complete provincial level data [33, 34]. The 31 provinces represent the full set of standard administrative units in mainland China, allowing a comprehensive assessment of regional heterogeneity without sampling bias. We sourced data on physicians, medical institutions, and patients from the China Health Statistics Yearbook and supporting provincial health statistics yearbooks, which are compiled by official health authorities. R&D-related metrics in the high-tech medical sector, including personnel inputs, expenditure, and patent counts, were obtained from the China High-Tech Industry Statistics Yearbook. We obtained information on Internet pharmaceutical projects and innovative pharmaceutical enterprises from the Ministry of Industry and Information Technology of China. Data on Internet hospitals and healthcare informatization were collected from the National Health Commission, respective provincial health commissions, and the Institute of Asclepius Hospital Management. Additionally, we extracted provincial-level aggregated data on online healthcare services from Haodf.com, a leading professional online healthcare platform in China. We calculated the provincial annual average value of online patients’ healthcare service indicators from all registered hospitals in each province, to form panel data fully consistent with other provincial-level variables. The platform data achieves coverage of 31 provinces throughout the study period, with stable coverage scope. For a small number of missing observations in the sample, we used the linear interpolation method to fill in the missing data.

Setting

We categorized 31 provinces in mainland China into four regions, namely Eastern, Central, Western, and Northeastern China, to examine the spatial heterogeneity and dynamic evolution of new quality productive forces in healthcare. The division strictly adheres to national regional development planning and takes into account differences in economic development level and healthcare system capacity [35]. The Eastern region includes Beijing, Tianjin, Hebei, Shanghai, Jiangsu, Zhejiang, Fujian, Shandong, Guangdong, and Hainan. The Central region comprises Shanxi, Anhui, Jiangxi, Henan, Hubei, and Hunan. The Western region covers Inner Mongolia, Chongqing, Guangxi, Sichuan, Guizhou, Yunnan, Tibet, Shaanxi, Gansu, Qinghai, Ningxia, and Xinjiang. The Northeastern region encompasses Liaoning, Jilin, and Heilongjiang.

Measurement

The construction of the evaluation index system for new quality productive forces in healthcare is built on a robust conceptual framework. This framework integrates the Marxist theory of productive forces, which offers a fundamental theoretical foundation for understanding the components and evolution of productive forces [22]. It further incorporates the global health priorities outlined in the United Nations’ SDG 3, namely Good Health and Well-Being, and aligns with the strategic directives of the Healthy China Initiative, a national strategy launched in 2016 designed to improve population health, strengthen health systems, and advance high‑quality healthcare development [1, 36]. This integrated approach ensures that the measurement framework is not only theoretically sound but also reflects both internationally recognized objectives and context-specific national health governance priorities.

This conceptual framework guides indicator selection through three core criteria. First is scientific rigor, which requires each indicator to reflect the core attributes of new quality productive forces in healthcare. Second is operability, which prioritizes indicators with data accessible from official sources to ensure measurement feasibility at the provincial level. Third is systematic completeness, which mandates that the selected indicators collectively capture the multi-dimensional nature of the concept, forming a coherent and comprehensive evaluation system.

This study measures new quality productive forces in healthcare, which takes new quality medical laborers, means of labor, and objects of labor as core components [10]. A unified and consistent classification criterion is established for the indicator system based on the theoretical boundary of each element. The new quality medical laborers focus on the active subject of healthcare production activities. The new quality medical means of labor focuses on the carriers and tools used in healthcare production activities. The new quality medical objects of labor focus on the action objects of healthcare production activities. All indicators are classified in strict accordance with this unified criterion to ensure logical and theoretical consistency of the evaluation system.

New quality medical laborers serve as the active subject of medical production activities and the key agents of new quality productive forces in healthcare, with their ability to apply cutting-edge technologies defining the new quality of this group. This dimension focuses exclusively on the labor input, professional capacity and service efficiency of human participants in the innovative healthcare production, which forms a clear theoretical boundary with other dimensions. To capture this key characteristic, we select indicators reflecting their professional competence and innovative potential, which align with the conceptual definition of new quality laborers that focuses on high-skilled, technology-adaptive talents [37]. We also incorporate indicators of doctors’ online and offline clinical work volumes. These indicators enable the quantification of labor productivity through objective operational data [38]. It also fits the study’s focus on measurable technology-driven elements, as workload metrics reflect how technological integration translates to tangible efficiency gains for new quality medical laborers.

New quality medical means of labor serve as carriers and tools for healthcare production activities, encompass material and intangible categories, both of which embody the technology-driven core of the conceptual definition. This dimension focuses exclusively on the material facilities, technical achievements and digital foundations that support medical service delivery, which is clearly distinguished from the human subject of production and the service object of production in theoretical connotation. For material means of labor, we select indicators that balance traditional medical infrastructure and digital medical infrastructure. Traditional infrastructure indicators capture basic resource support, a prerequisite for innovation, while digital infrastructure indicators, covering digital service capabilities, data governance support, and technical facility synergy, directly reflect the technological advancement [39, 40]. For intangible means of labor, we select two indicator types. The first reflects healthcare technological innovation, including innovation entities, R&D investment, and achievement transformation [41]. This maps to the conceptual requirement of technological breakthroughs driving leapfrog development for new quality productive forces. The second focuses on healthcare informatization, covering information standardization, digital application, and smart construction [42]. It corresponds to the data-driven service transformation of new quality means of labor, ensuring indicators quantify how informatization optimizes medical workflows.

New quality medical objects of labor serve as the action objects of healthcare production activities, centering on patients, including those accessing offline healthcare services and online patients [43]. This dimension focuses exclusively on the service recipients and the final outcomes of medical labor, and captures the full chain of effects of new quality healthcare productive forces acting upon patients, covering both the process of service matching and the outcome of value realization [10, 11]. This aligns with the technology-driven core of the conceptual definition as technological innovation has reshaped how patients engage with medical services. We structure this dimension into a process layer and an outcome layer. We select indicators reflecting patients’ utilization of both online and offline healthcare services, including average visits to online hospital, annual outpatient visits per capita beyond internationally benchmarked reference ranges, and annual hospitalization rate beyond internationally benchmarked reference ranges [44, 45]. The reference range is specified as the 95% confidence interval of the average values of the corresponding indicators across OECD member states [46, 47]. The indicator value is calculated as the absolute deviation of the provincial observed value from the reference range. Imbalanced healthcare utilization beyond the reasonable reference range directly reflects the mismatch between healthcare resources and patient needs, as well as inefficient resource allocation, which is a manifestation of insufficient development of healthcare new quality productive forces. These three indicators constitute the process layer, capturing the matching efficiency between medical resources and patient needs under technology empowerment and also reflecting the coverage effectiveness of technology-enabled healthcare services, both of which are key attributes of new quality medical objects of labor. We also incorporate indicators such as patients’ online ratings and mortality rates. These indicators constitute the outcome layer. Patients’ online ratings reflect their subjective experience of technology-enabled services, while mortality rates reflect the objective effects of clinical diagnosis and treatment supported by new quality productive forces [48, 49]. Together with new quality medical laborers and means of labor as the input, this dimension covers the process and outcome, forming a logically complete framework.

Statistical analysis

First, we assigned weights to the indicators using the CRITIC-entropy combined weighting method rooted in the multi-criteria decision-making theoretical framework [50]. This method integrates conflicts, correlations, and information quantity among multi-dimensional indicators, enhancing the rationality of weight allocation [51]. We also employed the TOPSIS method to quantify the relative proximity of each province to the ideal state, establishing an analytical framework for evaluating new quality productive forces in healthcare across regions [52].

Second, we adopted the Dagum Gini coefficient to identify the sources of regional inequality in healthcare new quality productive forces. This method is selected for three advantages aligned with the research design: it decomposes total regional inequality into three mutually exclusive components, namely inter-group disparity, intra-group disparity, and transvariation, enabling a complete dissection of the driving forces of regional gaps; it addresses the limitations of traditional measures by accurately quantifying the contribution of cross-regional sample overlap to total inequality, avoiding biased estimation of regional disparities; it has no strict distributional assumptions for sample data, ensuring the reliability and robustness of results for our provincial panel data.

Third, we applied the kernel density estimation method to explore the dynamic distribution characteristics of new quality productive forces in healthcare across regions. As a non-parametric estimation method, the kernel density estimation does not require presetting the theoretical distribution form of the data, and fits continuous probability density curves purely based on sample data, which avoids the model specification bias of traditional parametric methods and restores the true distribution characteristics of the data more accurately [53]. We can observe how the distribution evolves along four key dimensions by comparing curves across different time points. The distribution position reflects whether the overall level of new quality productive forces increases or decreases over time. The main peak shape and curve width indicate whether regional disparities are widening or narrowing. The tail of curve reveals the presence of extreme values where certain provinces perform substantially better or worse than others. The number of peaks signals the extent of polarization. These features provide a comprehensive picture of the dynamic evolution of healthcare new quality productive forces.

Fourth, we employed the convergence and convergence models to analyze whether convergence exists in new quality productive forces in healthcare. We used the coefficient of variation to measure convergence [54].

Results

The level of new quality productive forces in healthcare

New quality productive forces in healthcare across provinces exhibited either fluctuating or steady growth between 2010 and 2023. We adopted a tier classification method based on mean and standard deviation to identify the development tiers of healthcare new quality productive forces across provinces. This method can directly quantify the relative position of each province against the national average benchmark, aligning with the goal of distinguishing development tiers relative to the national overall level, which cannot be achieved by the rank-based interquartile range method [56, 57]. Provinces were categorized into three tiers based on the mean score (M = 0.318) and standard deviation (SD = 0.068) in 2023. The ±0.5 standard deviation threshold is set to ensure balanced sample distribution across tiers and clear inter-tier differentiation. Tier 1 comprises provinces with scores exceeding the mean plus 0.5 standard deviations. Tier 2 includes those with scores within 0.5 standard deviations above or below the mean. Tier 3 consists of provinces whose scores fall below the mean minus 0.5 standard deviations. The results indicate that seven provinces belong to Tier 1: Shanghai, Beijing, Zhejiang, Guangdong, Shandong, Jiangsu, and Sichuan. Ten provinces are classified as Tier 2, including Tianjin, Hubei, Heilongjiang, Hunan, Henan, Chongqing, Liaoning, Hebei, Shaanxi, and Anhui. The remaining fourteen provinces, such as Fujian, Shanxi, and Yunnan, are classified as Tier 3.

Spatial disparities

We decomposed the overall disparity of new quality productive forces in healthcare, as illustrated in Fig. 4. Transvariation made the largest contribution to the overall disparity in 2010, accounting for 39.88% of total inequality. During 2011–2019, inter-group disparity became the dominant source, with its contribution share peaking at 63.01% in 2018. A significant structural shift occurred in 2020: the contribution of inter-group disparity dropped sharply to 30.80%, while the contribution of transvariation surged to 41.49%, re-establishing transvariation as the dominant source of overall disparity. After 2020, the contribution of inter-group disparity continued to decline, with transvariation remaining the primary source of national disparities, reaching 62.30% in 2023.

Decomposition of disparities of new quality productive forces in China

Figure 5 presents the intra-group disparities of new quality productive forces in healthcare across China as a whole and its four regions. In 2023, the intra-group disparity in China was 0.12, which was significantly higher than that in 2010. The Eastern Region recorded an average intra-group disparity of 0.13 from 2010 to 2023, ranking first among the four regions. The Western Region had an average intra-group disparity of 0.09, ranking second and slightly higher than those in the Central and Northeastern Regions.

Intra-group disparities of new quality productive forces in healthcare

Figure 6 shows the inter-group disparities of new quality productive forces in healthcare across the four regions. In 2010, the inter-group disparities among all regions were broadly comparable, all below 0.05. They first exhibited a fluctuating upward trend, subsequently followed by a fluctuating downward trend. In 2023, the inter-group disparities rose to approximately 0.10, which was higher than the level in 2010. From 2010 to 2023, the average disparities in new quality productive forces in healthcare between the Eastern Region and other regions were comparatively high, consistently ranking among the top three with broadly consistent trends.

Temporal evolution

We generated three-dimensional kernel density curves of new quality productive forces in healthcare, as shown in Fig. 7. For China, the kernel density curve shifted leftward during 2010–2014 and rightward after 2014, indicating an initial decline followed by a subsequent growth trend in new quality productive forces in healthcare. This is consistent with findings in Fig. 3. The height of the main peak decreased, while the width of the curve followed a “rise-fall-rise” trend, indicating that the degree of dispersion first widened, then narrowed, and subsequently widened again. The curve exhibited a right tail, indicating that the level of new quality productive forces in healthcare in some provinces was far higher than that in other provinces. It was unimodal with no significant polarization.

Robustness check

We conducted a sensitivity test based on indicator reclassification to verify the robustness of our key findings. We reassigned the full time equivalent of R&D personnel from the new quality laborers dimension to the new quality means of labor dimension. We then recalculated the healthcare new quality productive forces with the same method used in the baseline analysis. We compared the new index and corresponding empirical results with the baseline findings to test the stability of our conclusions. The result indicates that the provincial ranking of healthcare new quality productive forces remains almost unchanged after indicator reclassification. We also tested the consistency between the adjusted index and the baseline index. The Spearman rank correlation coefficient between the two indices is 0.962, and it is statistically significant at the 1% level. The measurement of the index shows high stability. We then examined all empirical analyses based on the adjusted index. For the analysis of spatial disparities, the pattern of regional difference decomposition are consistent with the baseline results. For the temporal evolution analysis, the distribution shape, displacement trend and polarization characteristics reflected by the kernel density estimation are highly consistent with the baseline findings. For the convergence analysis, the overall trend of convergence, and the sign and significance of the key coefficients in both absolute convergence and conditional convergence models are consistent with the baseline results. All conclusions of the study do not change substantially after the adjustment of indicator classification. This sensitivity test confirms that our key findings are not sensitive to the reasonable adjustment of indicator classification. The evaluation system and empirical results of the study have strong robustness.

For the platform-based indicators in the objects of labor dimension, inter-provincial gaps in internet penetration may lead to two types of measurement bias: one is the measurement bias for online patients’ visit, which may systematically underestimate the online healthcare utilization level of provinces with low internet penetration; the other is the sample selection bias for online patients’ ratings, which may reduce the inter-provincial comparability of subjective service quality data. We supplemented the robustness test by excluding all platform-based indicators to verify the stability of conclusions. We re-estimated the healthcare new quality productive forces index by excluding all platform-based indicators, and the key findings including provincial ranking, spatial disparity pattern, temporal evolution characteristics and convergence results remain consistent with the baseline.

Discussion

We measured new quality productive forces in healthcare across 31 provinces in China from 2010 to 2023. The results indicate two key findings: First, China’s overall new quality productive forces in healthcare in 2023 were higher than those in 2010, showing a trend of initial decline followed by sustained growth. Second, among the three dimensions of new quality productive forces in healthcare, China achieved the strongest performance in new quality medical laborers, while new quality medical objects of labor remained the weakest.

In terms of the long-term overall trend, our finding that China’s new quality productive forces in healthcare showed an overall upward pattern aligns with previous evidence [10], which also observed a continuous increase. However, our study further identifies a “first decline then rise” phase in the trend, which differs from their observation of continuous growth. This discrepancy may stem from the longer timeframe of our study, which allows us to capture fluctuations not included in their shorter one. Furthermore, the “decline-then-growth” trend is closely associated with the phased focus of China’s new healthcare reform. The reform, initially launched in 2009, prioritized establishing a nationwide basic healthcare system to improve public access to affordable healthcare services [58, 59]. In the early stage, most healthcare resources were allocated to expanding basic service coverage rather than technological innovation. This led to insufficient investment in healthcare technology, alongside limited optimization of resource allocation efficiency and personnel management structures [35, 60], which explains the initial decline in new quality productive forces in healthcare. From 2015 onwards, the deepening reform drove new quality productive forces in healthcare growth. The government promoted the “Internet + Healthcare” model, a policy framework introduced in 2015 to encourage the integration of internet technologies such as telemedicine, online consultation, and digital health management into healthcare service delivery. This policy was further facilitated by the widespread adoption of 4 G networks across China, which laid the foundational digital infrastructure for its implementation. Meanwhile, long-term physician training programs began to deliver outcomes, driving the development of new quality factors in healthcare [34].

For new quality medical laborers specifically, the government has placed strong emphasis on their role, implementing policies such as the 12th and 13th Five-Year Health Plans and key tasks for medical reform. These policies increased funding for medical universities, strengthened the professional technical skills and efficiency of medical laborers, thereby promoting the development of new quality medical laborers [61]. In contrast, the new quality medical objects of labor is the weakest dimension in healthcare new quality productive forces, with significant spatial heterogeneity. Our quantitative test confirms that regional differences in online healthcare service development are highly consistent with the spatial pattern of this dimension. The Spearman rank correlation between provincial average online patient visits and the score of new quality medical objects of labor is 0.83, which is statistically significant at the 1% level. Provinces with higher dimension’s scores, such as Beijing and Zhejiang, have significantly higher online medical service utilization, while provinces with lower scores show persistent lags in online service penetration. The weak overall performance and regional heterogeneity of this dimension are fundamentally shaped by institutional design constraints. First, while the national basic medical insurance system covers a wide range of offline services, reimbursement policies for online consultations remain limited and vary across provinces, reducing patient willingness to adopt digital channels. Second, the high access threshold for internet hospital filing limits the popularization of online medical services in central and western provinces with weak grassroots medical resources. Third, the uneven supply of digital literacy public services across regions further exacerbates the gap in residents’ ability to access and use online medical services. Addressing these institutional constraints will be essential for improving the new quality medical objects of labor.

Spatial analysis of new quality productive forces in healthcare across China’s four regions yields several key findings. The Eastern Region had the highest level of new quality productive forces in healthcare in 2023. The average disparities between the Eastern Region and the other three regions remained substantial. In addition, both disparities between the Eastern Region and others, and the overall intra-group disparity in China have narrowed steadily since 2016. This narrowing period coincided with the sustained growth of new quality productive forces in healthcare.

The Eastern Region’s dominant performance in new quality productive forces in healthcare stems from its economic strength and concentrated innovative healthcare resources, which align with previous findings [62]. The narrowing of disparities after 2016 can be attributed to several factors. The dynamic synergy among new quality medical laborers, means of labor, and objects of labor jointly drives the development of new quality healthcare productive forces [63], while also reshaping the regional healthcare resource allocation. First, new quality medical laborers with enhanced digital proficiency transcend geographical constraints. They deliver remote high-quality healthcare services, reducing the mobility barriers of medical laborers previously constrained by traditional geographical space [64]. Second, digital healthcare infrastructure, such as telehealth platforms and 5 G-enabled medical systems, breaks down geographical barriers to resource flow. It facilitates the penetration of technology-driven healthcare resources and high-quality healthcare services into underdeveloped healthcare regions. Third, growing patient demand for online healthcare services drives a shift in resource allocation models. This shift moves from a static layout centered on physical medical institutions in developed regions to an adaptive model oriented to patients’ dynamic needs. It further reconfigures the spatial balance between the supply and demand of healthcare resources, as highlighted by previous evidence [65].

This study contributes to the broader literature on technology-driven spatial allocation of healthcare resources [66, 67]. Our finding that regional disparities in new quality productive forces in healthcare narrow as the overall level grows aligns with some prior research [68]. Previous findings similarly indicated that telehealth can improve access to healthcare services for remote populations through technology and is beneficial for reducing regional healthcare disparities [69]. However, some studies report inconsistent results [70]. The conflicting findings in existing literature arise from the dual spatial tension between inclusive diffusion and core-area agglomeration of the elements of new quality productive forces in healthcare, as discussed in previous research [15, 16]. On the one hand, agglomeration effects can widen disparities. High-skilled medical laborers who are proficient in cutting-edge technologies are highly dependent on technological resources. They tend to cluster in healthcare technology-intensive areas, exacerbating disparities in the spatial distribution of new quality medical laborers [71]. Meanwhile, healthcare technological innovation and informatization amplify the competitive advantages of core regions through a self-reinforcing effect. This further widens the gap between healthcare technology-intensive regions and less developed counterparts. On the other hand, inclusive diffusion effects—a key force in China’s post-2016 era—can narrow disparities. Digital tools such as digital health and online consultations, coupled with policy-driven resource sharing mechanisms such as national telemedicine networks, mitigate the agglomeration-driven disparities [72]. The final direction of regional disparities depends on which force dominates. This dominance is further shaped by a country’s healthcare system and government intervention. For instance, China’s “Internet + Healthcare” policies launched after 2015 strengthened the inclusive diffusion effect, which underpins our finding of narrowing regional disparities.

The evolution of spatial disparities further reveals the dynamic characteristics of new quality productive forces in healthcare. Transvariation was the primary source of national disparities in 2010 and after 2020. Inter-group disparity dominated during 2011–2019. This shift reflects changing spatial patterns of new quality productive forces in healthcare. Before 2020, substantial inter-regional gaps drove overall disparities. The post-2020 resurgence of transvariation is closely linked to the COVID-19 pandemic. During this period, the Chinese government issued multiple directives to formalize and expand telemedicine and cross-regional resource allocation [73]. For instance, the National Health Commission of China issued a formal notice in 2020 to launch national inter-provincial remote consultation for severe and critical COVID-19 patients, establishing a standardized institutional framework for cross-regional medical resource mobilization and medical technology diffusion. A 2020 nationwide survey by the Chinese Hospital Association, covering 1,014 hospitals across China, documented that 75.15% of surveyed hospitals carried out professional remote consultation during the pandemic, reflecting the rapid nationwide uptake of inter-provincial telemedicine collaboration driven by pandemic response requirements. This process accelerated the cross-regional flow of medical technologies and services, intensifying the overlap and intersection of new quality productive forces in healthcare across regions and thereby elevating the contribution of transvariation. This process intensified the overlap and intersection of new quality productive forces in healthcare across regions, thereby elevating the contribution of transvariation.

Convergence analysis yields another key finding. No convergence exists for new quality productive forces in healthcare, either at the national level or within the four regions. However, both absolute convergence and conditional convergence are present. These findings are not contradictory but reflect distinct aspects of the convergence process. convergence indicates a conditional catch-up effect, meaning provinces with lower initial levels of new quality productive forces experience faster growth rates than their higher initial-level counterparts. This confirms a catch-up effect among less developed regions. However, convergence requires not only that lagging provinces grow faster but also that their growth advantage is sufficiently large to overcome the initial absolute gap. When leading regions continue to grow positively, even a faster growth rate among lagging regions may be insufficient to narrow the absolute gap, particularly when initial disparities are substantial. This economic mechanism explains why relative catch-up can coexist with widening absolute gaps. The lack of convergence suggests that persistent structural differences across regions—including gaps in medical resource endowments, technological innovation capabilities, and digital infrastructure—keep the absolute gap in healthcare new quality productive forces from narrowing [74]. The overall pattern reflects relative catch-up amid persistent absolute divergence for less developed provinces.

The empirical results can be interpreted through the dual spatial tension framework, where inclusive diffusion and core‑area agglomeration operate simultaneously. The decomposition of spatial disparities reveals the shifting balance between these two forces. During 2011 to 2019, inter‑group disparity dominated, consistent with agglomeration effects where the Eastern region’s concentration of talent, capital, and infrastructure sustained its lead. The structural shift after 2020, when transvariation surged to become the primary source of inequality, aligns with intensified diffusion effects. This period coincided with the COVID‑19 pandemic, during which telemedicine platforms and cross‑provincial resource mobilization accelerated the flow of technologies and services across regions, increasing distributional overlap. The kernel density results further illustrate how these two forces shape regional patterns. In the Eastern region, the unimodal distribution without tailing reflects stable agglomeration, where provinces within this region develop relatively evenly around a high mean. In contrast, the emerging right tail in the central region after 2019 suggests diffusion effects reaching these areas, enabling some provinces to achieve higher levels and creating a more dispersed distribution. The convergence analysis captures the dual tension at the aggregate level. The presence of convergence indicates that lower‑initial‑level provinces experience faster growth, reflecting diffusion effects that enable catch‑up. However, the absence of convergence shows that absolute gaps persist, as agglomeration forces maintain the leading region’s continued growth. These patterns confirm that the evolution of healthcare new quality productive forces is shaped by the interplay of diffusion and agglomeration, producing relative catch‑up within persistent absolute divergence.

Our provincial-level analysis cannot directly capture intra-provincial urban-rural disparities, yet the findings offer indirect evidence that advancements remain concentrated within metropolitan innovation hubs. The consistently higher levels observed in the eastern region, particularly in provinces with major metropolitan centers, suggest that provincial aggregates may be driven by core cities, while rural areas lag behind. This pattern is likely reinforced by the uneven distribution of digital infrastructure, the concentration of skilled medical talent in urban centers, and policies that initially prioritize innovation hubs. Such uneven distribution restricts the inclusive advancement of healthcare new quality productive forces and weakens the spillover benefits of technological progress to rural populations. Although the convergence findings indicate cross-provincial catch-up, this does not guarantee equitable benefits for rural areas within catching-up provinces. Policymakers should consider targeted investments in rural digital infrastructure, workforce training, and service delivery models tailored to rural contexts to ensure that technological advancements do not inadvertently deepen existing urban-rural divides.

This study explores the spatiotemporal characteristics and convergence of new quality productive forces in healthcare, yielding actionable empirical insights for addressing unbalanced regional healthcare development in China and optimizing healthcare resource allocation. Specifically, differentiated and region-tailored policies should be formulated according to heterogeneous development levels and convergence speeds across regions. For the Eastern Region, which maintains a dominant position with the highest levels, policies should continue to consolidate its overall advantages, enhance innovation-driven development, and serve as a key engine for cross-regional cooperation and technology spillover. The Northeastern region exhibits the fastest convergence speed, indicating strong catch‑up potential despite its current moderate level. Policies should focus on sustaining this catch-up momentum by strengthening regional medical innovation hubs, retaining skilled medical talent, and strengthening institutional linkages with the Eastern region to ensure that rapid growth translates into sustained advancement. The Central region shows moderate convergence speed with medium development levels. Policy interventions should target accelerating this progress through expanding digital infrastructure deployment, fostering local innovation capacity, and creating incentive mechanisms for technology adoption across healthcare facilities. The Western region has the slowest convergence speed and the lowest baseline. It requires foundational support, including prioritized investment in digital health infrastructure, workforce training programs to enhance digital competencies among healthcare professionals, and tailored approaches to address geographic and economic constraints that impede technology diffusion. The post-2020 surge in transvariation also highlights the value of cross-regional medical resource mobility, which can guide policy design for inter-provincial healthcare collaboration.

This study also holds substantial relevance for other countries, particularly low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) grappling with regional healthcare disparities and inadequate resource distribution. The three-dimensional evaluation framework and the insights into convergence analysis in this study can serve as a reference for LMICs to design contextually appropriate strategies. These nations can replicate the approach of leveraging technological innovation for healthcare advancement to bridge gaps, rather than relying solely on expanding traditional medical resource inputs. Additionally, the exploration of convergence patterns enriches global discourse on healthcare system optimization. It provides theoretical and empirical support for international efforts to achieve universal health coverage and promote equitable healthcare development, which collectively constitute a core objective of global health governance.

This study makes three key contributions to the existing literature. Previous studies on new quality productive forces have constructed evaluation indicators from dimensions such as technology, industry, and environment [37, 75]. These frameworks emphasize the external manifestations of new driving forces but have not explored the internal structural logic of new quality productive forces. Against this backdrop, this study establishes a novel three-dimensional evaluation system based on the Marxist theory of productive forces, selecting new quality medical laborers, means of labor, and objects of labor as core dimensions. This design grounds the evaluation in the essential elements of productive forces, facilitating the clear identification of the strengths and weaknesses of new quality productive forces in healthcare [20]. Furthermore, the evaluation index system aligns with both domestic and international health governance objectives. It deepens the application of new quality productive forces theory in healthcare, breaking free from the universal frameworks of previous studies and providing a new theoretical perspective to understand how technological innovations reshape healthcare productive forces. Finally, this study analyzed the spatiotemporal evolution and convergence of new quality productive forces in healthcare, revealing their spatial differentiation characteristics. This supplements existing research on regional healthcare balance in the context of new quality productive forces and provides new empirical evidence for policies targeting optimized regional healthcare resource allocation and balanced development.

This study is not without its limitations. First, it focused on the measurement of new quality productive forces in healthcare using provincial level data. While this level of analysis captures broad regional patterns, provincial aggregates may obscure internal disparities within provinces, particularly the gaps between urban and rural areas. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted as reflecting provincial average trends rather than conditions at sub-provincial levels. Future research could use municipal or county level data to capture fine grained spatial differences and conduct urban rural stratified analysis. Second, while the evaluation index system is grounded in the Marxist theory of productive forces, it may not fully encompass all critical dimensions of new quality productive forces in healthcare. The connotation of healthcare new quality productive forces is still in the dynamic expansion stage. Consequently, our measurement of new quality productive forces should be understood as an assessment within the current framework rather than a comprehensive or definitive evaluation. Future studies could expanded optimize the index system combined with the latest development practice of healthcare new quality productive forces. Third, although this study identifies the convergence trend of healthcare new quality productive forces, it does not deeply explore the specific driving mechanisms or quantify the relative contributions of determinants. The conditional convergence model includes a standard set of control variables, yet does not fully incorporate factors such as policy intensity, cross-provincial medical collaboration, and digital infrastructure, due to data availability constraints and the difficulty of consistent measurement across provinces. In addition, given our emphasis on cross-regional spillovers, the current specification does not explicitly account for spatial dependence, which may influence convergence patterns. Consequently, the internal logic underlying the observed convergence remains insufficiently clarified. Future research may adopt spatial econometric models and mediating effect analysis to incorporate spatial spillovers, include more structural determinants, and disentangle the driving forces of convergence.

Conclusions

Our findings confirm two key patterns. From a temporal perspective, China’s new quality productive forces in healthcare showed an initial decline followed by sustained growth, with new quality medical laborers performing best and new quality medical objects of labor weakest. From a spatial perspective, the Eastern Region has maintained a dominant position, with regional gaps narrowing steadily. Transvariation has become the primary source of disparities since 2020, and absolute and conditional convergence were observed across regions. This research enriches the theoretical application of new quality productive forces in the healthcare sector and provides an operable evaluation framework. It also offers empirical evidence for optimizing regional healthcare resource allocation, with insights that can inform similar practices in low- and middle-income countries. Policies should prioritize two key actions. Policy implications should be framed around two key insights. First, the uneven spatial distribution and varying convergence speeds across regions call for differentiated strategies rather than uniform measures. The Eastern region, as the leader, should play a central role in facilitating cross‑regional technology diffusion. Regions with faster convergence, such as the Northeast, require sustained support to maintain their catch‑up momentum, while slower‑converging regions, particularly the West, need foundational investments in digital infrastructure and capacity building to overcome structural disadvantages. Second, the persistently weak performance of new quality medical objects of labor across all regions highlights the need to enhance patient digital engagement and expand the accessibility of online healthcare services. These targeted strategies can help translate technological advancements into more balanced and inclusive healthcare development.
