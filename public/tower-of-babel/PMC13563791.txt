tRNA methylation: functional insights and epitranscriptomic regulation
Authors: Saihao Wang, Yan Wang, Ting Zhao, Binbin Ma, Siyu Chen, Lei Chen, Lili Niu, Ye Zhao, Mailin Gan, Li Zhu, Linyuan Shen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstracttRNAs, one of the most conserved and abundant RNAs, are central components of protein synthesis, transferring genetic information from DNA to proteins through a precise base-pairing mechanism. Post-transcriptional modifications of tRNAs by tRNA modifying enzymes are essential for maintaining their normal physiological functions, including methylation, isomerization and glycosylation. tRNA methylation, particularly 1-methyladenosine (m1A), 5-methylcytidine (m5C), and 7-methylguanosine (m7G), are among the most abundant and diverse types of post-transcriptional modifications of tRNA, which promote the stability of tRNA secondary and tertiary structures and allow for proper translation. In addition, tRNA methylation affects the production and function of tsRNA (tRNA-derived small RNA), small fragments of RNA that further regulate gene expression and protein synthesis. In our review, we discuss the relevant biological functions of tRNA methylation, including tRNA stability, protein translation, and tsRNA biogenesis.Keywords: tRNA methylation, tRNA stability, Translational regulation, tsRNA, Disease, Epigenetic regulation

Introduction

There is a wide variety of tRNAs. For each amino acid, multiple tRNA species often exist, including isoacceptor tRNAs that recognize different codons for the same amino acid and isodecoder tRNAs that share the same anticodon but differ in body sequence [1]. Although tRNAs are best known for their essential role as adaptor molecules in protein synthesis, they also regulate gene expression through multiple mechanisms, including modulating translation efficiency, influencing codon usage bias, and serving as precursors for tsRNAs. They are also among the most extensively modified RNA species [2]. Post-transcriptional modifications are chemically defined as alterations to standard nucleosides, introduced through post-transcriptional processing of the four canonical nucleosides. To date, over 150 different RNA modifications have been identified across all domains of life [3]. Among all RNA classes, tRNAs harbor more than 100 distinct types of modifications, with this number still growing, making them one of the most extensively modified molecules [4]. These modifications are essential for several aspects of tRNA function, including decoding the genetic code and stabilizing the tertiary structure of the tRNA [5]. It has been reported that each human tRNA contains an average of 13 modification sites [6].

Post-transcriptional modifications are critical for tRNA function, influencing processes from structural stability to the accuracy and efficiency of protein synthesis [7]. While tRNA modifications are distributed throughout the molecule, certain regions, particularly the anticodon loop (notably at positions 34 and 37) and the tRNA core region (including the D-loop, TΨC-loop, and variable arm), are especially enriched and functionally critical [8]. Within these regions, they play pivotal roles in ensuring accurate codon recognition and in maintaining the structural integrity essential for translational activity [9].

Among the many tRNA modifications, methylation is the most prevalent, with more than 30 distinct methylated nucleosides identified at various positions across different tRNAs [10]. These modifications are catalyzed by specific methyltransferases that utilize S-adenosylmethionine (SAM) as a methyl donor, including m1A, m5C, and m7G [2]. Methylation of tRNAs affect their stability and contributes to the fine-tuning of both translation initiation and elongation [11]. Here, we have summarized the common tRNA methylation sites on tRNA (Fig. 1). Furthermore, tsRNAs, produced by regulated tRNA cleavage, have emerged as a significant focus of recent research. tRNA modifications are intimately linked to the synthesis of tsRNAs. Previous reviews have provided detailed summaries of the roles and mechanisms of action of tsRNAs [12, 13].

Historically, tRNAs were extensively studied for their canonical role as adaptor molecules that decode mRNA codons and deliver amino acids during protein synthesis, including investigations into their structure, biochemistry, and modification profiles. However, recent advances in epitranscriptomics, including high-throughput sequencing technologies and improved RNA modification mapping methods, have spurred a surge of studies on tRNA modifications, particularly tRNA methylation, bringing tRNAs back into the spotlight as prominent research subjects [14, 15]. Here, we summarize the three main types of tRNA methylation and their associated enzyme classes and outline how they function.

tRNA methylation

tRNA methylation primarily occurs at specific positions within the anticodon loop, TΨC loop, D loop, and variable arm, where it fine‑tunes tRNA structure and decoding [8]. Most of these reactions are catalyzed by SAM dependent methyltransferases [2]. Although many tRNA methylations are constitutively installed during maturation and relatively stable, certain modifications can dynamically respond to environmental cues; for example, oxidative stress or nutrient deprivation alters the activity of specific tRNA methyltransferases, leading to transient changes in methylation levels that modulate translation efficiency [16]. Thus, tRNA methylation can be dynamically regulated to maintain cellular homeostasis [5].

This review focuses on three modifications, namely m1A, m5C, and m7G, because they are the most prevalent and best‑characterized in higher eukaryotes, have well‑defined enzymatic machineries (TRMT6/61A, NSUN2/DNMT2, and METTL1/WDR4, respectively), and play critical roles in tRNA stability, translation regulation, and tsRNA biogenesis and function. Other methylated nucleosides (e.g., m2C, m2G, m5U) are briefly discussed in Sect. Other base methylations in tRNA. Here, we provide an overview of the enzymes catalyzing those three modifications and their functions (Fig. 2).

m1A modification

The m1A modification is present in different tRNA species, with m1A58 in the TΨC loop being a highly conserved and functionally important modification in eukaryotic cytosolic tRNAs [17]. During methylation, binding of the tRNA to the m1A58 methyltransferase induces transient structural rearrangements that temporarily separate the D and TΨC loops, exposing A58 for modification [18]. The introduction of a positive charge by m1A methylation can influence tRNA structure and its interactions with binding partners [11].

In the cytoplasm, the TRMT6-TRMT61A complex catalyzes m1A58 modification, with TRMT6 serving as the tRNA-binding subunit and TRMT61A as the catalytic component [19]. m1A14 has been identified only in mammalian tRNAPhe [20, 21], and its methyltransferase remains to be fully characterized. Unlike these cytosolic modifications, m1A9 is primarily found in mitochondrial tRNAs.

Human mitochondria harbor several m1A sites. m1A58 is present only in mt-tRNALeu(UUR) and is catalyzed by TRMT61B [22, 23]. m1A9 is widely distributed across 14 mitochondrial tRNAs and is introduced by the TRMT10C/SDR5C1 methyltransferase complex, in which TRMT10C serves as the catalytic core [24, 25]. In addition, m1A16 is specific to human mt-tRNAArg [26].

Removal of m1A modifications is mediated by Fe(II)/α-ketoglutarate-dependent dioxygenases of the ALKB family. ALKBH1 demethylates m1A on multiple tRNAs, including tRNAGlu(CUC), tRNAHis(GUG), and tRNALys(UUU/CUU), among others [11]. Knockdown of ALKBH1 increases m1A levels on most tRNAs, with some exceptions (e.g., tRNAPhe(GAA) and tRNASer(UCA)) [11]. ALKBH1 also acts on mitochondrial tRNAs, affecting m1A16 of mt-tRNAArg and m1A58 of mt-tRNALeu [27]. Notably, ALKBH1 can additionally oxidize m5C34 to f5C in mitochondrial tRNA, suggesting broader substrate versatility [28].

ALKBH3 also possesses m1A demethylase activity toward selected tRNAs, such as tRNAGly(GCC), tRNAHis(GUG), and tRNAiMet(CAU) [29, 30]. The fat mass and obesity-associated protein (FTO) has been reported to demethylate m1A on several tRNAs (e.g., tRNAGlu(CUC), tRNAHis(GUG), tRNAGly(GCC)) [31]; however, FTO is primarily known for its role in m6A demethylation of mRNA, and its contribution to tRNA m1A demethylation appears context‑dependent and requires further validation.

In mitochondria, ALKBH7 acts as an RNA demethylase that removes m1A from the tRNAmt-Leu1 precursor within nascent polycistronic RNAs, thereby preventing premature RNA processing and maintaining normal mitochondrial tRNA levels and energy metabolism [32] (Fig. 2).

m5C modification

The m5C modification is widely distributed across various RNA species, and in tRNAs it occurs at specific positions, primarily within the anticodon loop and the variable arm. In eukaryotes, the main enzymes catalyzing m5C methylation of tRNA belong to two families: the TRDMT1 (also known as DNMT2) and the NSUN family (NSUN2, NSUN3, and NSUN6) [33].

Although initially thought to be a DNA methyltransferase, DNMT2 was later shown to function as an RNA m5C methyltransferase, introducing m5C at position 38 of the anticodon loop in specific tRNAs, including tRNAAsp(GUC), tRNAVal(AAC), and tRNAGly(GCC) [34, 35]. This modification requires prior modifications at the wobble position (position 34), such as queuosine (Q) or inosine, indicating cross-talk between different tRNA modification events [36, 37]. A regulatory mechanism involving intermolecular disulfide bonds has been proposed to modulate DNMT2 activity, though this remains an area of active investigation [38].

The NSUN family members exhibit distinct substrate specificities and subcellular localizations. NSUN2 is the major m5C methyltransferase in the nucleus and cytoplasm, responsible for modifying approximately 80% of cellular tRNAs [39]. It introduces m5C at position 34 of the anticodon loop (in pre-tRNALeu(CAA), which requires an intron) and at positions 48–50 of the variable loop [39, 40]. NSUN2 also localizes to mitochondria, where it methylates positions 34, 48, 49, and 50 of multiple mitochondrial tRNAs, thereby supporting mitochondrial translation [41]. In contrast, NSUN3 is exclusively mitochondrial and catalyzes m5C at position 34 of mt-tRNAMet [42]. This m5C34 can be further oxidized by ALKBH1 to generate 5-hydroxymethylcytidine (hm5C) and then 5-formylcytidine (f5C), which expands codon recognition [39, 42]. NSUN6 is localized in the cytoplasm and requires the presence of a CCA tail at the 3′ end for activity, indicating that it acts at a late stage of tRNA biogenesis. It has narrow substrate specificity, methylating cytosine at position 72 of tRNACys(GCA) and tRNAThr(TGT) [33, 43]. Structural studies have shown that NSUN6 recognizes its substrates through its PUA domain [44].

Notably, knockdown of NSUN2 leads to increased m5C levels on some tRNAs, which is attributed to a compensatory upregulation of DNMT2 mediated methylation, suggesting that these two enzymes work together to maintain m⁵C homeostasis [45, 46].

In both cytoplasm and mitochondria, m5C residues installed by NSUN2 or NSUN3 can be further oxidized by ALKBH1 to generate hm5C and f5C [39, 42]. For example, in mitochondrial tRNAMet, NSUN3-mediated m5C34 is converted to f5C34 by ALKBH1, enabling the tRNA to recognize both AUG and AUA codons during mitochondrial translation [42].

m7G modification

The m7G modification is present in mRNA, tRNA, and rRNA; however, the m7G cap at the 5′ end of mRNA is catalyzed by a distinct set of enzymes (e.g., RNMT) and serves different functions from the internal m7G46 modification in tRNA discussed here [47]. In tRNA, the m7G modification typically occurs at position 46 of the variable arm. The enzymes that catalyze the m7G46 modification are METTL1 and WDR4 [48]. In this complex, METTL1 is responsible for catalyzing the methylation of guanine, while its regulatory or scaffold subunit WDR4 helps stabilize the methyltransferase complex [49] (Table 1).

The m7G modification is introduced during tRNA maturation [48] and contributes to stabilizing the three-dimensional core of tRNA, where m7G46 participates in tertiary interactions rather than forming a conventional base pair [48]. The molecular mechanism by which the METTL1-WDR4 complex catalyzes the m7G modification has been elucidated. This complex recognizes specific regions of tRNA through shape and charge complementarity. Upon binding to the cofactor SAM or SAH, it releases the G46 site and inserts it into the active site through a series of conformational changes, thereby completing the methylation reaction [51]. m7G on tRNA is a conserved modification that regulates steady-state tRNA levels and thus influences cell growth [52, 53]. Recent studies have revealed widespread m7G methylation in mammalian tRNAs [54–56]. These evidence suggest that METTL1/WDR4-mediated tRNA m7G modifications play important roles in regulating cell fate decisions.

Functions of tRNA methylation

RNA post-transcriptional modifications are key functional determinants of tRNA maturation. Hypomodified pre-tRNA and mature tRNA are more prone to degradation [53], indicating a major role of modifications in protecting tRNA from specific decay pathways [57]. Modifications impact tRNA structure by altering base hydrophobicity, pairing, stacking, and charge stability, depending on their type and location [58, 59]. Those in the tRNA core (such as the D-loop or T-loop) can modulate or, in some cases, disrupt Watson-Crick base pairing [60].Methylation, in particular, is critical for structural stability [59]. tRNA modifications fine-tune structure and codon interactions to influence translation [61]. Beyond their canonical role in translation, tRNAs are a major source of regulatory small non-coding RNAs known as tRNA-derived small RNAs (tsRNAs) [62]. These fragments are generated by cleavage at specific sites via enzymes such as Angiogenin (ANG) and Dicer [63, 64], and are broadly categorized based on biogenesis into tRNA halves (tiRNAs; 31–40 nt, cleaved at the anticodon loop) and tRNA-derived fragments (tRFs; 14–30 nt, originating from various regions of mature or precursor tRNAs). tRFs can be further subdivided into subtypes including tRF-3s and tRF-5s, which are derived from the 3’ and 5’ ends of mature tRNAs, respectively.

Importantly, tsRNAs are not merely random degradation products; their biogenesis and regulatory functions are tightly linked to tRNA modifications, which endow them with precise sequences and specific biological roles [12, 65]. Consequently, these fragments have emerged as critical regulators in diverse biological processes, ranging from gene expression to intergenerational inheritance [12, 13]. Together, these findings underscore the functional significance of RNA methylation beyond its role in maintaining tRNA stability, highlighting its involvement in the epitranscriptomic regulation mediated by tsRNAs.

Functional overview of tRNA m1A methylation

Facilitate proper tRNA folding

The m1A58 modification is a highly conserved feature of eukaryotic initiator tRNAs, critical for maintaining structural integrity and stability [66]. The introduction of a positive charge by m1A58 reinforces tertiary structure without disrupting the reverse A58-T54 base pair, thereby enhancing tRNA stability, preventing cleavage, and suppressing tsRNA generation [67]. Demethylation of m1A58 by ALKBH1 impairs proper folding of tRNAiMet, leading to destabilization and degradation [11]. Additionally, the m1A58 modification in tRNATyr(GUA) is intron-dependent; intron loss abrogates this modification, causing tRNA instability and activating the integrated stress response [68]. However, not all tRNAs rely on m1A58 for stability, as exemplified by mt-tRNALeu(UUR) [69].

Beyond position 58, m1A at other sites (e.g., m1A9) also induces conformational changes, such as disrupting the A9-U64 Watson-Crick pair to facilitate the transition from a stretched hairpin to the functional cloverleaf conformation [50, 70, 71]. Even when global structure is unaltered, m1A modifications fine-tune local tRNA dynamics; for instance, the m1A9 equilibrium in mitochondrial tRNALys facilitates proper D-loop assembly [71, 72]. Furthermore, the methyltransferase SDR5C1, which generates m1G9 and m1A9, is a component of human mitochondrial RNase P, underscoring the mechanistic link between tRNA modification and processing [24].

Modulate protein translation efficiency

m1A modification enhances translation efficiency, as m1A-methylated tRNAs are preferentially incorporated into polysomes to facilitate elongation [11]. The TRMT6/TRMT61A complex, which catalyzes m1A58, promotes translation of specific mRNAs (e.g., PPARγ, TSC1, and histone mRNAs) in various cell types, thereby regulating self-renewal, proliferation, and cell cycle progression [73–75]. Mitochondrial m1A modifications, such as m1A58 in mt-tRNALys, similarly enhance the synthesis of mitochondrial-encoded proteins [69].

Demethylases add another layer of regulation: ALKBH1 removes m1A58 from tRNAiMet, impairing initiation, and disrupts eEF1α binding to certain tRNAs, thereby impeding elongation [11, 31]. ALKBH3 also targets m1A58 but promotes protein synthesis in cancer cells; its knockdown increases tRNA m1A levels and suppresses translation [29]. While both demethylases act on m1A58, their downstream effects diverge: ALKBH3-demethylated tRNAs are prone to ANG cleavage, generating tsRNAs that facilitate ribosome assembly and inhibit apoptosis, whereas ALKBH1 primarily affects tRNAiMet folding and initiation [11, 76]. Thus, m1A modifications modulate translation through context-dependent mechanisms influenced by modification sites, specific enzymes, and cellular processes.

Orchestrate the biogenesis and functional coordination of tsRNAs

Reduced tRNA modification levels frequently promote tsRNA biogenesis, which is linked to cancers, neurodegenerative diseases, and metabolic disorders [77]. tsRNA generation is often co-regulated by multiple modifications; for example, tRNAGlu and tRNAAsp display low m1A58 levels yet yield relatively few tRF-3 fragments(3’-derived tRNA fragments, 14–30 nt; see [12]for nomenclature), suggesting that other T-loop modifications also influence tRF-3 biogenesis [78, 79]. In cancer cells, ALKBH3 binds tRNA and promotes ANG-dependent tsRNA production [76]. Under various cellular stress conditions, tRNAs undergo extensive cleavage, but only a small proportion of resulting tsRNAs carry m1A modifications, supporting a role for demethylation in facilitating tRNA fragmentation [67].

Beyond biogenesis, m1A modifications directly affect tsRNA function: TRMT6/61A knockdown reduces m1A at position 4 in the seed sequence of tRF-3b (a tRF-3 subtype), impairing base pairing with target mRNAs such as MBTPS1 and CREB3L2 and reducing silencing efficiency [79]. Notably, tRNA methyltransferases can promote tsRNA generation independently of their catalytic activity. The TRMT6–TRMT61A complex binds tRNA and enhances anticodon loop accessibility, facilitating ANG recognition and cleavage to produce tiRNAs [18, 80]. In aging murine hematopoietic stem cells, elevated TRMT6–TRMT61A expression acts as a scaffold that recruits tRNA molecules, promoting ANG-mediated cleavage without increasing m1A levels, a methylation-independent role in tsRNA biogenesis [81] (Fig. 3).

Functional panorama of tRNA m5C methylation

Promote tRNA structure stabilization

In tRNA, the m5C modification is introduced at multiple positions depending on the specific methyltransferase, cellular context, and tRNA species (i.e., whether the tRNA contains a cytosine at the given position in its primary structure). These include position 34 of the anticodon loop (by NSUN2 in the cytoplasm or NSUN3 in mitochondria), position 38 (by DNMT2), positions 48–50 in the variable loop (by NSUN2), and position 72 near the acceptor stem (by NSUN6) [33, 39, 40]. Growing evidence suggests that m5C plays a crucial role in promoting tRNA structural stability and regulating protein synthesis [46]. By adding m5C, enzymes such as NSUN2 and DNMT2 stabilize the structure of some mature tRNAs and protect them from rapid degradation [46]. For instance, in Drosophila and mice, tRNAAsp is shielded from ribonuclease cleavage during the heat shock response [82].

The stabilization of tRNA tertiary structure by m5C results from a combination of local base stacking, hydrogen bonding, and global folding effects, rather than from a single chemical property of the modification [59]. Specifically, m5C48 contributes to the stability of the “Levitt pair” formed between C48 and G15 by enhancing base hydrophobicity and promoting base stacking, thereby supporting the formation of tRNA’s tertiary structure [39, 59]. In addition, m5C modifications can facilitate the binding of Mg2+ to tRNA [83], which is essential for maintaining its tertiary architecture; however, magnesium coordination is primarily determined by the overall folding of the molecule, and modifications play an auxiliary role in stabilizing that structure [84]. The m5C modification of tRNA mediated by NSUN2 prevents rapid degradation of tRNA, thereby maintaining normal protein synthesis [85, 86].

However, not all losses of tRNA modifications significantly impact tRNA stability. Knockout of NSUN2 resulted in undetectable levels of m5C in mitochondrial tRNAs, yet the absence of this modification did not substantially affect mammalian mitochondrial function, suggesting that the lack of m5C in the variable loop region alone is not sufficient to destabilize tRNA [87].

Fine-tune codon-anticodon wobble interactions

Modifications within the tRNA anticodon loop are highly conserved and essential for maintaining translational fidelity. Defects in these modifications, particularly at the wobble position, can disrupt codon-anticodon pairing and compromise decoding accuracy [88]. For instance, m5C modifications fine-tune codon–anticodon interactions to regulate both the efficiency and accuracy of translation [89].

In mitochondria, the m5C/f5C modification at position 34 of mt-tRNAMet, catalyzed by NSUN3, expands its codon recognition capability. The oxidized form, f5C34, base-pairs with both adenine and guanine, enabling efficient recognition of both AUA and AUG codons. This flexibility enhances mitochondrial translation initiation and elongation [42]. Furthermore, mitochondrial initiation factor IF-3mt cooperates with the m5C/f5C modification to facilitate efficient initiation at AUA codons, a non-AUG start codon, strategically relaxing the fidelity of start codon selection for leaderless mRNAs [90].

In the nuclear context, NSUN2-mediated m5C modification influences translation in a tissue-specific manner. Its knockdown in mouse neurons selectively depletes tRNAGly m5C, impairing the translation of glycine-rich proteins involved in glutamatergic signaling [46, 91]. In triple-negative breast cancer, NSUN2 installs m⁵C in tRNAVal(CAC), promoting the codon frequency-dependent translation of glycolysis-related genes and thereby supporting tumor cell proliferation and invasion [92].

Additionally, DNMT2-mediated m5C38 in tRNAAsp promotes its aminoacylation by aspartyl-tRNA synthetase, favoring the translation of poly-Asp-containing proteins [93]. Conversely, in the absence of DNMT2, loss of m5C38 on tRNAAsp impairs its ability to discriminate near-cognate codons for aspartate and glutamate, leading to amino acid misincorporation, reduced translational fidelity, and aberrant polypeptide synthesis [94]. Notably, modifications near the amino acid acceptor arm can influence tRNA charging, with hypermodified tRNAs often being preferentially aminoacylated, a mechanism that further safeguards translational fidelity [95].

Modulate cellular stress response

Oxidative stress arises from an imbalance between reactive oxygen species (ROS) production and elimination, leading to accumulation of superoxide, hydrogen peroxide, and hydroxyl radicals [96]. While ROS function as signaling molecules under physiological conditions, their excess can inflict severe damage on cells and tissues, contributing to cardiovascular, immune, and neurodegenerative disorders [97–99].

The m5C RNA modification plays a key role in cellular stress adaptation. Using NAIL-MS, dynamic tRNA methylation changes have been observed under chemical stress, linking tRNA modifications to stress responses [100]. The methyltransferase NSUN2 acts as both a tRNA stabilizer and a sensor of external stress. Under oxidative conditions, NSUN2 activity is inhibited, reducing specific tRNA methylation and modulating protein synthesis rates, ultimately contributing to neurodevelopmental defects [101].

In colon cancer, hypoxic stress upregulates NSUN2, which methylates C34 of tRNAArg, protecting it from cleavage. This downregulates tRFArg(CCG) and tRFArg(CCU), diminishes their binding to TRIB1 mRNA, and promotes cancer migration and invasion [102]. The recruitment of DNMT2 and NSUN2 to stress granules further underscores the importance of m5C methylation in stress regulation [9, 103].

NSUN3, another RNA cytosine methyltransferase, influences embryonic stem cell differentiation and stress response. Although NSUN3 loss does not alter basal ROS, it significantly reduces mitochondrial ROS under oxidative stress, suggesting that NSUN3 regulates oxidative stress adaptation and differentiation partly through mitochondrial ROS production [104].

Stabilize tsRNA production and inheritance

Emerging evidence has revealed that small RNAs derived from tRNAs can serve as carriers of epigenetic information across generations. This phenomenon, termed tsRNA inheritance, refers to the ability of tsRNAs to mediate the transmission of acquired traits from parents to offspring [105]. Unlike canonical small RNAs, whose functions are largely dictated by their sequences, tsRNAs rely intimately on their post-transcriptional modifications for inheritance capacity [106].

It is important to distinguish between the general roles of m5C modification in regulating tsRNA biogenesis and stability within a single generation and their specific function in mediating intergenerational inheritance. Within a generation, several studies have demonstrated that m5C modifications influence tsRNA production and stability in a cell-autonomous manner. For instance, deletion of NSUN2 and DNMT2 leads to hypomethylation of m5C on tRNA, which recruits nucleases to cleave tRNA, resulting in the accumulation of tsRNAs [19, 62, 107]. Under oxidative stress conditions, NSUN2 activity is effectively inhibited, reducing m5C methylation on tRNA, decreasing tRNA stability, and promoting ANG-mediated cleavage into tsRNA fragments. Additionally, TET2 catalyzes the conversion of m5C to hm5C on tRNA, thereby regulating tsRNA biogenesis: this leads to down-regulation of 3′ tRFs and up-regulation of 5′ tRFs [108].

Beyond these roles within a generation, m5C modifications are critically involved in the intergenerational inheritance of acquired traits. DNMT2-mediated m5C methylation promotes the stability of tsRNAGly [62, 109]. More importantly, m5C-modified tsRNAs play a crucial role in the epigenetic inheritance of paternal metabolic disorders. Deletion of DNMT2 reduces m5C abundance on tsRNAs, decreases their stability, and prevents sperm tsRNAs from transmitting high-fat-diet-induced metabolic disorders to offspring [35]. Furthermore, m5C modification at the C38 site significantly alters the secondary structure of tsRNAs, enhancing their resistance to ribonuclease degradation. This modification expands the biological functions of tsRNAs beyond their linear sequence [35]. In models of intergenerational transmission of paternal diet-induced metabolic disorders, microinjection of synthesized unmodified tsRNAs failed to recapitulate the phenotypes produced by endogenous tsRNAs, further demonstrating that modifications are essential for tsRNA function in inheritance [106] (Fig. 4).

Mechanism of action of m5C methyltransferases. The m5C modification not only promotes the intrinsic stability of tRNAs and broadens codon recognition (e.g., from AUG to include AUA) during translation, but the m5C methylation of tRNA-derived small RNAs enhances their resistance to ribonuclease degradation by altering secondary structure or by recruiting protective factors. Conversely, hypomodified tRNAs are not only prone to degradation or cleavage into tsRNAs, but also fail to accurately distinguish near-cognate codons, resulting in amino acid misincorporation and impaired translational fidelity

Importantly, the regulation of tRNA stability and fragmentation by m5C does not occur in isolation. Other tRNA modifications, such as queuosine (Q) at the wobble position, also influence tRNA structure, translational fidelity, and susceptibility to cleavage, illustrating functional interactions between different modification pathways [36, 37]. Moreover, loss of certain ribose 2′-O-methylations (Nm) can perturb RNA interference pathways and promote tRNA fragmentation, leading to neuronal phenotypes [110, 111]. While a comprehensive discussion of these cross-talk mechanisms is beyond the scope of this review, acknowledging their existence places the role of m5C methylation in a broader and more balanced context.

Functional panorama of tRNA m7G methylation

Roles of m7G methylation in cancer and cell cycle regulation

METTL1 is responsible for catalyzing the m7G modification at position 46 of tRNA [112]. This modification is essential for efficient translation of certain mRNAs, especially those enriched in codons preferentially recognized by m7G-modified tRNAs [113]. Deletion of METTL1 reduces m7G levels on tRNAs and impairs codon recognition during translation, thereby decreasing translation efficiency of specific mRNAs [114, 115].

In certain cancers, METTL1 acts as an oncogene by catalyzing m7G modification of a single tRNA, Arg-TCT-4-1. This modification stabilizes the tRNA and enhances the translation of mRNAs enriched in the cognate AGA codon, including many critical cell-cycle regulators. The subsequent accelerated G1/S transition and uncontrolled proliferation directly fuel oncogenic transformation [116]. In esophageal squamous cell carcinoma, targeted inhibition of METTL1 or WDR4 reduces m7G-modified tRNA expression, leading to excessive autophagy and cell death [117]. In nasopharyngeal carcinoma, METTL1 enhances WNT/β-catenin signaling by increasing translation efficiency of relevant mRNAs [118]. Conversely, in breast cancer, METTL1 overexpression increases m7G modification on tRNAs, facilitating translation of GADD45A and RB1 mRNA – negative cell-cycle regulators – thereby inhibiting excessive tumor cell proliferation [119].

Beyond cancer, m7G tRNA modification also controls normal cell cycle progression. Knockdown of METTL1 in embryonic stem cells decreases translation efficiency of cell-cycle-related mRNAs and increases the proportion of cells in G2 phase, indicating a defect in G2/M transition [120]. Overexpression of METTL1/WDR4 accelerates S-to-G2 progression and faster re-entry into G1 after mitosis, while METTL1 knockdown increases G1-phase cells and reduces S-phase cells, suggesting a role in G1/S transition [116]. Together, these findings highlight that m7G modification regulates cell cycle progression both in normal and cancerous contexts.

Regulate tsRNA biogenesis

As discussed above, reduced tRNA modification levels can promote tsRNA biogenesis. In prostate cancer, METTL1‑mediated m7G methylation prevents the cleavage of tRNA into small non‑coding RNA fragments. Specific knockdown of METTL1 results in the up‑regulation of 5′‑terminal oligo‑guanine‑rich tsRNA fragments derived from tRNACys and tRNAAla, which inhibit protein synthesis by substituting the translational regulator PAPB1 [121]. Similarly, in acute myeloid leukemia cells, METTL1 deletion leads to decreased abundance of several tRNAs and increased levels of tsRNAs [122].

Notably m7G not only inhibits tRNA cleavage and reduces tsRNA production but also promotes the formation of specific tsRNAs. Experiments showed that tsRNAs extracted from serum (with protein binders removed) exhibited greater stability than chemically synthesized tsRNAs when re-incubated in serum, implying that the enhanced stability may derive from intrinsic features of native tsRNAs, such as nucleotide modifications, although a definitive causal role of modifications remains to be established [123]. In colorectal cancer, METTL1‑mediated m7G modification promoted the up‑regulation of tsRNAGly(GCC) while enhancing its stability, partially explaining the elevated levels of this tsRNA. Further studies revealed that tsRNAGly(GCC) inhibits STAT6 transcriptional activity by directly targeting SPIB, thereby regulating the JAK1/STAT6 signaling pathway and promoting colorectal cancer development and drug resistance [124]. Similarly, METTL1‑mediated m7G modification also can upregulate mt‑tRF3b‑LeuTAA expression. This tsRNA promotes SUMOylation of Sirt3 (a post-translational modification by SUMO1) by targeting SENP1, which inhibits Sirt3 function and hinders PINK1/Parkin‑mediated mitochondrial autophagy [125].

Additionally, overexpression of METTL1 increased the levels of m⁷G‑modified tiRNALys(TTT) (referred to as mtiRL; tiRNAs are tRNA halves, and alternative naming conventions such as ‘tiRL’ are used in some studies [12]). Importantly, overexpression of mtiRL containing m7G modifications, but not unmodified synthetic tiRL, even in the absence of METTL1, promotes the interaction between Annexin A2 (a protein involved in cell growth and signaling) and YES1 (a proto-oncogenic tyrosine kinase) by directly binding to Annexin A2. This interaction induces phosphorylation at the Tyr24 residue of Annexin A2, a modification that has been associated with enhanced tumor cell proliferation and migration [126]. Furthermore, METTL1 catalyzes the generation of m7G‑modified tiRNA, which in turn drives the assembly of stress particles [127]. Together, these findings highlight the critical role of m7G modification in tsRNA generation and its diverse biological functions (Fig. 5).

Other base methylations in tRNA

In addition to the extensively studied m1A, m5C, and m7G modifications described above, tRNAs contain several other base methylations that occur at specific positions and are catalyzed by distinct methyltransferases. These modifications generally contribute to tRNA folding, decoding accuracy, and translational regulation, albeit with varying degrees of impact.

The 3-methylcytidine (m3C) modification is found at multiple positions. At position 32 of the anticodon loop, m3C is catalyzed by different methyltransferases: METTL2A/METTL2B act on tRNAThr and tRNAArg, METTL6 on tRNASer, and METTL8 primarily on mitochondrial tRNAThr and tRNASer [78, 128]. Additionally, m3C can appear at the third nucleotide after position 47 in the variable loop of certain tRNAs (e.g., tRNASer, tRNALeu) and at position 20 of the D‑loop of tRNAMet, though the enzymes responsible for these sites remain unidentified [129]. The m3C32 modification does not appear to substantially affect tRNA stability but may fine‑tune conformational dynamics; its impact on translation is relatively minor, as loss of the DALRD3 (a cofactor required for METTL2A/METTL2B-mediated m3C32 modification) cofactor does not markedly inhibit translation [130, 131]. In mitochondria, deletion of METTL8 does not affect aminoacylation or ribosome binding of mt‑tRNAThr/Ser [130, 132].

5-Methyluridine (m5U) is one of the most abundant base modifications in cytoplasmic tRNA. TRMT2A specifically methylates uridine at position 54, achieving its substrate specificity through structural recognition of the target site [133]. In the absence of TRMT2A, translation fidelity is significantly reduced, detected as increased mistranslation at a glutamine codon in a reporter assay, while overall protein synthesis levels remain unchanged. Witzenberger et al. attribute this uncoupling of efficiency from fidelity to the possibility that the loss of TRMT2A-mediated m5U54 modification affects decoding accuracy only at specific codons (e.g., CAA/G for glutamine) without globally perturbing translation elongation or ribosome biogenesis, thereby allowing normal protein output despite elevated error rates at certain sites [133]. In mitochondria, TRMT2B is responsible for m⁵U modification in both tRNA and rRNA, demonstrating that a single methyltransferase can act on multiple RNA species within the same organelle [134].

N2-methylguanosine (m2G) and N2,N2-dimethylguanosine (m22G) modifications are introduced by several enzymes. THUMPD3, in complex with the cofactor TRMT112, functions as a tRNA‑binding subunit required for m2G methylation at positions 6 and 7 of some mature tRNAs; the complex recognizes the 3′‑CCA sequence to achieve specificity [135]. Knockdown of THUMPD3 results in impaired overall protein synthesis and slower cell growth [135]. TRMT11, also requiring TRMT112 as a cofactor, catalyzes m2G10 modification, which is thought to contribute to tRNA stability [136]. In contrast, TRMT1 independently catalyzes both m2G and m22G at position G26 of cytoplasmic and mitochondrial tRNAs by recognizing specific base pairings in the D‑loop, thereby preventing aberrant tRNA folding and maintaining structural rigidity [137].

1-methylguanosine (m1G) at position 9 is catalyzed by TRMT10A [138]. This modification, together with m1A9, helps establish proper cloverleaf folding by disrupting an early Watson‑Crick pair. Collectively, these diverse base methylations ensure the functional integrity of tRNAs and enhance the efficiency and fidelity of translation [11, 138].

Conclusions and perspectives

In conclusion, tRNAs are a highly abundant non-coding RNAs whose functions, including codon recognition, reading frame maintenance, and decoding accuracy, are influenced by diverse chemical modifications [139]. These modifications are essential for tRNA stability and translation efficiency, as exemplified by anticodon loop modifications that facilitate codon-anticodon interactions [140]. tsRNAs derived from tRNA cleavage also participate in gene silencing, ribosome biogenesis, and intergenerational inheritance [13]. However, many questions remain unanswered: For example, the enzymes responsible for m3C modification at specific sites (e.g., position 47 in tRNASer/tRNALeu and position 20 in tRNAMet) are still unidentified [141]. Furthermore, the temporal and spatial order of modification addition during tRNA maturation, as well as the synergy among modifying enzymes, remains poorly understood [43].

A key emerging concept is that tRNAs modifications function within interconnected networks rather than independently. The addition of one modification can influence others, as seen with DNMT2 requiring prior wobble modifications for m5C38 addition [36, 37]. Hierarchical modification during maturation and modification‑dependent folding checkpoints ensure proper tRNA function. These ideas represent major frontiers in the field, illustrating how different modifications cooperate to maintain tRNA structure and function.

Dysregulated tRNA modifications directly or indirectly affect protein translation [142] and are critical for tsRNA production and function. However, technical challenges hinder progress: modified nucleotides can interfere with reverse transcription, cause cDNA synthesis errors, bias library preparation, and impede accurate mapping [143]. Understanding how various modifications cooperate will provide deeper insights into regulation of translation and gene expression, and continued research promises to reveal novel regulatory mechanisms underscoring the importance of tRNA modifications in fundamental biology.
