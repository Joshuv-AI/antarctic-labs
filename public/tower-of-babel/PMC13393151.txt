Immediate and single-stage facial reanimation after radical parotidectomy and oncologic facial nerve sacrifice: A systematic review
Authors: Charbel Saad, Thadeu Rangel, Juan Pablo Menendez, Gregory Nicolas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRadical parotidectomy with facial nerve sacrifice produces major functional and aesthetic deficits. Immediate or single-stage facial nerve reconstruction and facial reanimation may restore symmetry, ocular protection, oral competence, and dynamic movement while avoiding the limitations of delayed intervention. This review synthesizes the available evidence on reconstructive strategies and outcomes in this setting.MethodsA systematic review was performed of studies evaluating immediate, concurrent, or single-stage facial nerve reconstruction or facial reanimation at the time of radical parotidectomy or oncologic facial nerve sacrifice. PubMed, Embase, Scopus, and Web of Science were searched from database inception through April 2026. Eligible studies were original clinical series reporting postoperative functional outcomes, patient-reported outcomes, complications, or revision procedures. Data were synthesized descriptively because of heterogeneity in techniques and outcome reporting.ResultsNineteen studies comprising 277 patients met inclusion criteria. Reconstructive strategies included cable or interpositional nerve grafting, concurrent masseteric nerve transfer, vascularized nerve grafting, temporalis-based dynamic reanimation, free functional muscle transfer, and composite flap-based reconstruction. Across studies, immediate reconstruction was associated with restoration of resting symmetry, oral competence, and ocular protection, while dynamic approaches more often reported smile recovery and oral commissure movement. Interpositional cable grafting resulted in a postoperative recovery in the House–Brackmann III range. Reported complication rates were acceptable, with low donor-site morbidity and uncommon flap loss. Limited radiotherapy-specific analyses suggested that postoperative radiotherapy did not uniformly preclude functional recovery after immediate reconstruction.ConclusionsImmediate or single-stage facial nerve reconstruction and facial reanimation after radical parotidectomy appear feasible across a range of oncologic defects and are associated with reported postoperative restoration of facial tone, symmetry, oral competence, ocular protection, and, in selected series, dynamic facial movement. However, the current evidence is limited by retrospective design, small sample sizes, and heterogeneous outcome reporting. Larger comparative studies using standardized functional and patient-reported outcome measures are needed to better define the optimal reconstructive strategy.

Introduction

Parotid malignancies are rare but surgically consequential tumors of the head and neck because of their intimate relationship to the facial nerve.1,2 When the nerve is not infiltrated, preservation is preferred whenever oncologically safe; however, when the nerve is involved by tumor, the affected segment may need to be resected as part of radical parotidectomy, and primary reconstruction or reanimation is considered the most effective means of restoring long-term facial function and appearance.1,3

The deficits that follow facial nerve sacrifice after radical parotidectomy extend well beyond loss of neural continuity alone. Patients may be left with immediate hemifacial paralysis, lagophthalmos with risk of exposure keratopathy, oral incompetence, impaired speech and eating, and substantial psychosocial distress related to facial asymmetry and loss of expression.4,5 These functional problems coexist with major soft-tissue and contour defects created by parotid ablation, skin resection, or more extensive oncologic extirpation, making reconstruction after radical parotidectomy a simultaneous problem of restoring both form and function.4, 5, 6

For this reason, immediate or single-stage facial nerve reconstruction and facial reanimation have become increasingly attractive. Reconstruction performed at the time of tumor ablation may shorten the duration of paralysis, preserve the opportunity for reinnervation before prolonged denervation-related muscle atrophy occurs, and restore static symmetry, ocular protection, and oral competence without requiring a delayed second-stage pathway that may be less feasible in patients requiring adjuvant therapy.7,8 Recent studies suggest that immediate reconstruction can provide meaningful recovery even when postoperative radiotherapy is required.8, 9, 10

Despite growing interest in immediate reconstruction, the literature remains difficult to apply in a unified way. Most published studies are small, single-center series centered on one technical approach, such as cable grafting, concurrent masseteric nerve transfer, temporalis-based reanimation, vascularized nerve grafts, or composite flap-based reconstruction.11, 12, 13, 14, 15 In contemporary practice, technique selection is further shaped by oncologic variables such as perineural invasion, proximal and distal stump availability, extent of soft-tissue loss, prior treatment, and anticipated adjuvant therapy, which makes direct comparison across studies challenging.3,11

Accordingly, this systematic review synthesized the available evidence on immediate or single-stage facial nerve reconstruction and facial reanimation performed during the same operation as radical parotidectomy or oncologic facial nerve sacrifice. The review encompassed facial nerve reconstruction, dynamic reanimation, and composite reconstructions with soft-tissue restoration, with the aim of summarizing reconstructive strategies, functional outcomes, and complications in this challenging oncologic setting.

Methods

Study design

This systematic review was performed in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 statement.16 The review was designed to evaluate immediate or single-stage facial nerve reconstruction and facial reanimation performed in the setting of radical parotidectomy or oncologic facial nerve sacrifice, with emphasis on reconstructive strategies, functional outcomes, and postoperative complications.

Information sources and search strategy

A systematic search was performed in PubMed, Embase, Scopus, and Web of Science from database inception through April 2026. Database-specific subject headings and free-text keywords were used to capture studies involving parotidectomy, facial nerve sacrifice, immediate reconstruction, facial reanimation, nerve grafting, nerve transfer, temporalis-based reanimation, and free functional or flap-based reconstruction. Complete search strategies can be found in Supplementary Appendix S1. Reference lists of relevant studies were also reviewed to identify additional eligible articles.

Eligibility criteria

Studies were eligible if they included patients undergoing radical or total parotidectomy, or parotid-region oncologic resection with facial nerve sacrifice, followed by immediate, concurrent, or single-stage facial rehabilitation performed during the same operation as tumor ablation. Eligible interventions included facial nerve reconstruction (eg, interpositional or cable grafting and vascularized nerve grafting), dynamic facial reanimation (eg, nerve transfer, temporalis-based reanimation, and free functional muscle transfer), and composite reconstructions in which facial nerve reconstruction or reanimation was a defined component. Studies were required to report postoperative outcomes related to facial function, facial symmetry, ocular protection, oral competence, patient-reported outcomes, complications, or revision procedures. Cases without direct neural reconstruction were eligible if immediate dynamic reanimation was performed and postoperative functional outcomes were reported.

Retrospective and prospective clinical case series were eligible. Studies were excluded if they focused exclusively on delayed reconstruction, secondary facial reanimation, benign parotid disease, non-oncologic facial paralysis, or non-parotid populations in which the relevant oncologic subgroup could not be separately extracted. Studies limited to static-only procedures were excluded, as were reviews, technical notes, conference abstracts without full text, and single-case reports.

Study selection

All records were imported into Covidence systematic review software (Veritas Health Innovation, Melbourne, Australia) for deduplication and screening. Title and abstract screening was performed independently by two reviewers, followed by independent full-text review of potentially eligible articles by the same two reviewers. Disagreements at either stage were resolved through discussion and consensus; when consensus could not be reached, a third reviewer served as the tiebreaker.

Data extraction

Data were extracted on study characteristics, patient and tumor characteristics, reconstructive technique, adjuvant treatment, functional outcomes, and complications. Study-level variables included author, year, country, study design, sample size, study period, and follow-up duration. Patient- and disease-level variables included age, sex, pathology, preoperative facial nerve status, extent of parotidectomy, concurrent neck dissection, recurrent or salvage setting, and use of postoperative radiotherapy. Reconstruction-specific variables included the type of facial nerve reconstruction or reanimation, donor nerve or motor source, use of vascularized versus nonvascularized grafts, soft-tissue flap reconstruction, static suspension procedures, and ocular adjuncts. Extracted outcomes included facial nerve recovery, dynamic smile or commissural movement, static symmetry, ocular outcomes, oral competence, complications, and revision procedures.

Data synthesis

A quantitative meta-analysis was not performed because of substantial heterogeneity in reconstructive strategy, defect characteristics, patient selection, radiation exposure, and functional outcome reporting. Instead, findings were synthesized descriptively. Included studies were grouped by reconstructive approach, including immediate cable or interpositional grafting, vascularized nerve grafting, combined nerve grafting with nerve transfer, temporalis-based dynamic reanimation, and free functional muscle or composite flap reconstruction.

Outcomes were summarized across four main domains: facial motor recovery, static facial symmetry and oral competence, ocular function, and complications or revision burden. When studies reported House-Brackmann grades, these were extracted directly as reported. Methodological quality was assessed using the JBI Critical Appraisal Checklist for Case Series as all included studies were ultimately uncontrolled clinical case series without a comparator group.17

Results

Study selection

The search identified 318 records (Embase, n = 102; Scopus, n = 92; PubMed, n = 68; Web of Science, n = 56). After removal of 168 duplicates (166 by Covidence and 2 manually), 150 records underwent title and abstract screening, of which 123 were excluded. Twenty-seven reports were sought for retrieval, 1 was not retrieved, and 26 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility. Seven full-text articles were excluded for the following reasons: foreign language (n = 5), wrong outcomes (n = 1), and wrong study design (n = 1). Nineteen studies comprising 277 patients met the eligibility criteria and were included in the qualitative synthesis. Patient, tumor, and reconstructive characteristics

Included cohorts comprised primary salivary malignancies, metastatic cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma, carcinoma ex pleomorphic adenoma, adenoid cystic carcinoma, high-grade adenocarcinoma, leiomyosarcoma, soft-tissue sarcoma, recurrent pleomorphic adenoma, and recurrent or salvage disease. Previously irradiated and salvage settings were represented in some series.9,10 Patient age varied widely, although many cohorts involved middle-aged or older adults; one series focused on an elderly cohort treated with immediate lengthening temporalis myoplasty.18 Preoperative facial weakness, when reported, was associated with advanced disease, recurrence, or direct nerve involvement, while neck dissection and postoperative radiotherapy were common in many cohorts.5,6,8, 9, 10,18, 19, 20

Reconstructive strategies were heterogeneous. Immediate interpositional or cable nerve grafting was the most common approach, using sural, greater auricular, medial antebrachial cutaneous, superficial radial, thoracodorsal, and vascularized motor nerve grafts. Several studies used vascularized nerve grafting, including scapulodorsal vascularized nerve flaps and grafts based on the lateral femoral circumflex vascular system.12,13,21 Dynamic strategies included orthodromic temporalis tendon transfer, lengthening temporalis myoplasty, selective reinnervation, concurrent masseteric nerve transfer, and free functional muscle transfer using gracilis- or vastus lateralis-based constructs.4,7,14,18,22 In more complex defects, neural reconstruction was combined with simultaneous restoration of volume and contour using ALT, PAP-gracilis, scapulodorsal, latissimus dorsi, or vastus lateralis-based flaps, often with adjunctive fascia lata suspension, lip suspension, brow lift, eyelid procedures, or temporary tarsorrhaphy.4, 5, 6,11,13,18,22 These patient, tumor, treatment, and reconstructive characteristics are detailed in Tables 2 and 3.Table 2Patient, tumor, and treatment characteristics.Table 2 dummy alt textStudyYearAge, yearsSex, male/femalePredominant pathology or tumor profilePreoperative facial functionNeck dissectionAdjuvant treatmentVaughan et al.1993Mean 51.6 (range, 14–82)9/5Mixed malignant/recurrent salivary-parotid cohort including melanoma, SCC, recurrent MEC, NHL, ACC, recurrent pleomorphic adenoma, CXPA, acinic cell carcinomaWeakness: 5/147/14 (RND 5, FND 2)RT: 10/14; Functional outcomes

Functional outcomes were reported heterogeneously. House-Brackmann outcomes were reported in 10 studies.4,7,9,12,18,19,22, 23, 24, 25 All 19 studies reported some degree of postoperative facial functional outcome after immediate reconstruction. In large grafting series, recovery was observed in 18 of 23 evaluable patients in one study and postoperative House-Brackmann grade III was achieved in 25 of 32 patients in another, including similar outcomes in irradiated salvage and adjuvant radiotherapy settings; a prospective interpositional grafting series reported House-Brackmann grades II-III in 11 of 22 patients.8,9,19 Smaller studies using thoracodorsal nerve grafting, recipient-bed preparation, and vascularized nerve flap reconstruction also reported functional recovery within a similar range.12,23,25

Dynamic facial movement or smile-related outcomes were reported in 8 studies.4, 5, 6, 7,11,14,18,22 Oral commissure motion returned within 2–7 months after concurrent masseteric nerve transfer and cable grafting, selective reinnervation improved 9 of 10 patients to modified House-Brackmann grade III and Terzis grade 4–5, and algorithm-based masseteric-to-facial neurorrhaphy showed favorable eFACE smile outcomes.7,11,14 Volitional gracilis activation was observed in 6 of 10 PAP-gracilis cases, while orthodromic temporalis tendon transfer-based series consistently reported favorable dynamic smile restoration and oral competence.4, 5, 6

Ocular outcomes were specifically reported in 9 studies.6,10,11,13,18,20,22, 23, 24 ALT- and temporalis-based reconstructions achieved resting symmetry, oral competence, dynamic corner-of-mouth movement, and full eye closure in small series, while a large vascularized nerve flap series reported satisfactory volumetric restoration in 93% of cases.4,6,13 Ocular outcomes were more variably reported, but studies incorporating tarsorrhaphy, eyelid lipofilling, or upper-face support generally described acceptable corneal protection and eyelid closure.11,18,20

Three studies also examined the effect of radiotherapy. 8, 9, 10 Immediate sural nerve grafting showed no significant differences in blink recovery, commissural excursion, or time to first movement between patients treated with surgery alone and those receiving postoperative brachytherapy and external beam radiotherapy.10 Similar conclusions were reported in a larger long-term grafting series and in an MABC graft series including both previously irradiated salvage cases and patients receiving postoperative adjuvant radiotherapy.8,9 Taken together, these findings suggest that immediate reconstruction remains feasible and potentially beneficial even in irradiated or salvage settings.

Discussion

Immediate facial nerve reconstruction at the time of radical parotidectomy appears feasible across a broad spectrum of oncologic defects. The available literature suggests that successful reconstruction in this setting extends beyond restoration of neural continuity alone. Earlier series focused primarily on interpositional grafting and regional reanimation, whereas more contemporary studies increasingly combine neural reconstruction with dynamic reanimation, soft-tissue restoration, contour correction, and periocular support. This shift is conceptually important because radical parotidectomy rarely creates an isolated nerve defect. Instead, it more often results in a composite deficit involving facial movement, resting symmetry, periocular competence, oral competence, and soft-tissue volume.

Facial nerve management remains fundamentally oncologic, but the decision to preserve or sacrifice the nerve is not always straightforward. Pretreatment facial weakness has long been regarded as a marker of neural invasion and poor prognosis, yet this relationship is imperfect.3,26,27 Park et al. reported that some resected nerves in patients with pretreatment facial weakness showed no histopathologic neural invasion, whereas Carlson et al. demonstrated that occult proximal spread may involve the intratemporal facial nerve despite preserved preoperative function and that positive proximal margins are associated with worse locoregional control.26,27 Thielker et al. similarly noted that some patients with normal preoperative facial function nevertheless required radical surgery because tumor infiltration was identified intraoperatively.3 For reconstructive planning, these findings underscore that preoperative facial function alone does not reliably define the extent of neural disease or the feasibility of preservation, and that intraoperative findings often determine whether immediate grafting, nerve transfer, or non-neural reanimation strategies are required.

Immediate reconstruction retains value even when complete functional recovery is uncommon. Several graft-dominant series reported only partial recovery in a substantial proportion of patients and a considerable burden of secondary procedures, particularly eyelid procedures and static revisions.8, 9, 10,19 However, even partial recovery may still be clinically meaningful by restoring resting tone, improving symmetry, and enhancing oral competence, while also creating a better substrate for later refinement. Supporting this idea, Thielker et al. (2021) showed that long-term facial dysfunction remains common after primary parotid cancer surgery and suggested that facial reanimation may still be underutilized.28 Earlier reconstructive literature reached a similar conclusion. Sobol et al. (1990) emphasized the value of early facial reanimation after extirpative parotid and temporal bone surgery in reducing functional, cosmetic, and psychological morbidity.29 Taken together, these findings suggest that immediate reconstruction may not restore normal facial function in every patient, but it may still meaningfully improve the postoperative baseline.

A further observation from this review is that multimodal strategies may offer advantages over grafting alone in selected patients. Studies incorporating concurrent masseteric nerve transfer, selective reinnervation, orthodromic temporalis tendon transfer, or free functional muscle transfer generally reported more favorable smile outcomes and stronger oral competence than graft-only approaches.4,5,7,11,14,18 This observation should be interpreted cautiously, however, because these data are derived from small, noncomparative series with substantial selection bias and heterogeneity in defect severity, stump availability, salvage status, and adjuvant treatment. In long defects, irradiated fields, or salvage settings, interpositional grafting may restore continuity without producing strong or coordinated facial excursion. In such settings, addition of a reliable motor source or tendon-based reanimation may help address deficits in dynamic movement, oral competence, and symmetry within the same operation.

Patients undergoing radical parotidectomy with immediate facial nerve reconstruction have been shown to achieve broadly comparable overall quality of life to more limited parotidectomy cohorts, although deficits in speech, eye symptoms, and eating-related function may persist.30 This pattern is consistent with the included reconstructive series, in which resting symmetry, oral competence, and overall facial balance were often improved, whereas periocular symptoms and oral-phase function remained more difficult to normalize.

Soft-tissue restoration deserves particular emphasis. In several included studies, flap-based reconstruction appeared to provide more than simple volume replacement. It restored contour, improved the recipient bed, separated the skin flap from deeper structures, and likely improved the durability of reconstruction in patients receiving adjuvant treatment.5,6,13,24 This may also have implications for late parotid bed morbidity, although the available evidence in the radical oncologic setting remains limited. Roh (2023), in a conservative parotidectomy cohort, showed that Frey syndrome was significantly more common when exposed parotid tissue and fascia were left unclosed than when the bed was closed, and that sialocele was likewise more frequent in the non-closure group.31 Although this evidence is indirect and derives from a different surgical population rather than radical parotidectomy with immediate facial reanimation, it supports the broader principle that barrier restoration at the parotid bed may have functional consequences beyond appearance alone. In the radical setting, soft-tissue reconstruction should therefore be considered an integral component of functional rehabilitation rather than a secondary aesthetic measure.

The effect of radiotherapy warrants careful interpretation. Although anticipated postoperative radiotherapy is often viewed as a factor that may weaken the rationale for immediate nerve repair because of concerns regarding wound healing and long-term facial recovery, the available reconstructive literature does not support abandoning immediate reconstruction on this basis alone. Useful recovery has been reported after immediate grafting in both adjuvant and previously irradiated salvage settings, even though postoperative radiotherapy has also been identified as an independent risk factor for permanent facial dysfunction.8, 9, 10, 28, 30

This review must also be interpreted in light of significant limitations. Nearly all included studies were retrospective case series. Exclusion of five non-English full-text studies may have introduced language bias, which is particularly relevant in this rare and highly specialized field, where important reconstructive innovations may be reported by non-English-speaking centers. Functional outcomes were assessed with heterogeneous instruments, at variable postoperative intervals, and with inconsistent reporting of revision burden. The methodological appraisal showed that case identification and clinical reporting were generally adequate, but consecutive inclusion and complete inclusion were frequently unclear, particularly in older retrospective series. Because the evidence consists almost entirely of small, heterogeneous, uncontrolled case series, this review remains primarily descriptive and cannot support meaningful comparisons between reconstructive techniques or treatment strategies. This also complicates attempts to isolate the effect of reconstructive strategy from tumor extent, preoperative facial function, salvage status, prior treatment, and adjuvant therapy.

Conclusions

Immediate facial nerve reconstruction and facial reanimation at the time of radical parotidectomy appear feasible across a broad range of oncologic defects and are associated with at least partial functional benefit in many reported series. The available literature increasingly supports reconstructive strategies that address the full functional and structural consequences of radical parotidectomy rather than focusing on nerve continuity alone. Reconstructive planning should prioritize ocular protection, resting symmetry, oral competence, dynamic reanimation when feasible, and durable soft-tissue coverage. Better comparative studies with standardized functional and patient-reported outcome reporting are needed to refine procedure selection and optimize reconstruction in this setting.
