The impacts of public hospital compensation reform on the health workforce compensation and its structure: quasi-experimental evidence in China
Authors: Yaxin Zhao, Zixuan Peng, Dan Cao, Rashed Nawaz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe Chinese government piloted the public hospital’s compensation system reform in 2017. It is important for policymakers to understand how this reform has affected the health workforce’s compensation and its structure. We evaluated the impact of the reform on health workers’ compensation levels and structure.MethodsThis nationwide study used panel data from the 2016–2017 China Health Workforce Compensation Reform Survey, covering 102 public hospitals and 111,204 health workers across 15 provinces. A difference-in-differences approach combined with propensity score matching was employed to estimate the reform’s impact on compensation and its structure. Primary outcomes were the percentages of base salary and merit salary in total compensation, secondary outcomes included total, base, and merit salary.ResultsFollowing the reform, the percentages of base salary and merit salary increased by 3.54% and 0.53%, respectively. Total compensation, annual base salary, and annual merit salary increased by 1%, 13%, and 5%, respectively. Compensation levels and structure were associated with the average social wages of urban workers, consumer price index, hospital levels, education, nature of employment, qualifications and departments were found to be associated with compensation and its structure. General hospitals showed the largest increase in the percentage of base salary, while specialized hospitals had greater growth in merit salary. The reform also contributed to an increased performance salary structure for contract and short-tenure staff, although it did not significantly improve their total compensation.ConclusionRPHCS has helped reshape salary structures for the health workforce, but has not yet to fully achieved the equity goal of “equal pay for equal work.” Policymakers should refine performance evaluation systems and extend base salary protections to vulnerable groups to promote equitable compensation outcome.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-026-14850-2.

Background

The reform of public hospital compensation systems is regarded as a key policy lever for improving health system performance [1]. The health workforce compensation system influences not only medical students’ career decisions and health workers’ retention, but also the quality of health services [2–7]. Compensation is essential for sustaining health workforce’s livelihood [8]. Some scholars also argue that the structure of compensation is critical for motivating health workforce [9].

The dominant modes of health workforce’s payment include fee-for-service (FFS), capitation, salary-only, and value-based [10–13]. In the U.S., the health workforces in public hospitals are paid under a federal salary system, while in the U.K., a nationally unified base salary mechanism applied through collective wage negotiation [14]. Compensation in developed countries is generally higher than that in developing countries, and base salary constitutes the major component. [15–17] In contrast, inadequate salaries and unreasonable compensation structure in developing countries such as China have reduced health workforce’s motivation and retention [18, 19]. Only a small proportion of high school graduates chose to major in medicine, and many talented doctors consider leaving their profession due to low compensation [20, 21]. Studies in Peru and Africa countries have emphasized the urgency for policymakers to retain health workforce through salary increases and compensation system reforms [22–24].

In China, health workforce are paid under a position-based performance payment system. Compensation consists of: (1) a base salary determined by education, professional title, and length of service; (2) a merit salary based on workload medical income, treatment effect, and service quantity, etc.; and (3) allowances. Prior to the reform, China’s public hospital physicians practiced under a compensation system plagued by systemic limitations. Total salary expenditure was strictly controlled and not permitted to exceed a prescribed ceiling. Rigid salary caps suppressed incomes that were disconnected from clinical value, while internal distribution often prioritized seniority over performance and tied earnings to drug sales and procedures, creating perverse incentives. Furthermore, a stark divide in pay and benefits between salaried and contract staff undermined equity and motivation. These critical flaws collectively justified the launch of the Registered Public Hospital Compensation System (RPHCS) aimed at instilling performance-based and equitable compensation [24].

In response, the government carried out the “Two Permissions” policy, which outlined the direction for reforming health worker remuneration. First, nonprofit hospitals were allowed to exceed the prevailing salary cap for public institutions. Second, they were allowed to use medical service revenues—after deducting costs and allocated funds—primarily for staff incentives. This meant an elevation of the salary ceiling and an expansion of performance-based pay, options that were unavailable before the reform. Subsequently, in January 2017, the Chinese government launched the Guiding Opinions on the Pilot Reform of Public Hospital’s Compensation System (RPHCS), with the goal of mobilizing health workforce’s enthusiasm and enhancing the public welfare nature of public hospitals.

Numerous studies have examined health worker compensation across countries, factors influencing pay, gender-based inequities, and the effects of payment models on behavior [25–30]. Previous research on the relationship between RPHCS and health workforce’s compensation has been limited by narrow outcome measures (e.g., focusing only on salary), data quality issues (e.g., reliance on self-reported surveys from single provinces), and methodological constraints (e.g., use of logistic regression without causal rigor) [31]. McCoy et al.’s survey in six sub-Saharan African countries found that base salaries accounted for only one-quarter to one-third of public-sector health workers’ total income, with complex allowances, private practice and informal payments necessary to make ends meet-highlighting how low base pay undermines morale and retention [30]. The American College of Physicians’ position paper notes that although women physicians in the U.S. have gained representation, they still face compensation inequities, limited leadership opportunities, and other barriers—and provides suggestions to realize pay equity [25]. Thus, whether compensation reform can positively affect compensation levels and structure in China remains a pertinent research question.

This article contributes to the literature in four ways. First, it provides rigorous causal evidence on the relationship between RPHCS and hospital staff’s compensation and its structure using difference-in-difference, enriching previous studies on payment system impact. Second, it use actual administrative data covering 15 provinces, offering more accurate evidence than questionnaire-based studies. Third, it estimates the net effect of the reform on pay equity. Finally, the policy implications drawn can inform health workforce compensation policy in other developing countries.

Methods

Conceptual framework

Based on an analysis of provincial reform plans, we summarized the core interventions of RPHCS, which were broadly consistent across provinces. These included: (1) encouraging pilot hospitals to adjust base salary standards and optimize compensation structure—many pilot areas accordingly raised base salary benchmarks; (2) allowing hospitals to reasonably determine total compensation, including permitting them to exceed public institution salary ceilings and use operational surpluses, thereby raising salary caps and expanding performance-based funding sources; [25] some regions set the total merit salary at 2–3 times the local average social wage; (3) promoting annual compensation systems for hospital executives—93.6% of pilot areas explicitly proposed or explored annual salary systems for top managers, typically set at 2–5 times the average staff salary; and (4) allowing pilot hospitals autonomy in determining the ratio between base and merit salary within the approved merit pay pool—78.6% of hospitals exercised this autonomy, also setting night shift standards. Reforms also encouraged tilting performance pay toward high-risk departments, raising pay for short-tenure staff, and promoting equal pay for salaried and contract workers. Based on this, we developed a conceptual framework and hypothesized the net effects of RPHCS on compensation and its structure (see Fig. 1). The following hypothesis were made:

H1

RPHCS increased thepercentage of annual base salary in total compensation.

H2

RPHCS increased total compensation, annual base, and annual merit salary.

H3

RPHCS increased compensation for health workforce with short tenure.

H4

RPHCS increased compensation for contracted staff.

Study design

We employed a quasi-experimental design combining propensity score matching (PSM) with difference-in-differences (DID) analysis, which has strength in estimating the causal impacts of health policy [32]. Treatment assignment was determined by actual reform implementation status in 2017: hospitals that officially launched the reform were classified as the treatment group (Group = 1), while those that had not implemented it by the end of 2017 served as controls (Group = 0) [33]. The pre- and post-intervention periods were defined as 2016 (T = 0) and 2017 (T = 1), respectively. This study used de-identified administrative data and did not require ethics approval or informed consent.

Data sources and sample

Data were drawn from the 2016–2017 China Health Workforce Compensation Reform Survey, conducted by the China National Health Development Research Centre. The survey targeted the first batch of RPHCS pilot hospitals and collected hospital- and staff-level information through questionnaires completed by hospital human resources departments. Hospital-level variables included reform launch time, revenue, subsidies, and personnel expenditures. Individual-level data covered demographics, education, department, and detailed compensation components. Provincial indicators were obtained from the China Statistical Yearbook [16, 34]. The initial sample included 115,128 health workers from 171 hospitals in 2016, and 106,318 workers from 160 hospitals in 2017. After PSM, the final analytic sample consisted of 111,204 workers from 102 hospitals across 15 provinces. The distribution of study sites and the number of health workers deployed is depicted in appendix 1.

Variables

Primary outcomes were the percentages of base and merit salary in total compensation. The percentage of annual base salary calculated as annual base salary/total annual compensation)×100%. The percentage of annual merit salary calculated as annual merit salary/total annual compensation)×100%. Secondary outcomes included absolute values of total compensation, annual merit salary, and annual base salary were included as the secondary outcome variables. Total compensation include position-based salary, seniority-based salary, performance-related payments, allowances, ad other salary. Position-based salary and seniority-based salary both are integral parts of the annual base salary. Independent variables included group (reform status), time period, and their interaction. Covariates covered provincial- (average social wages of urban workers, per capita GDP, number of health technical personnel per 1,000 population, population aged 65 and over, and consumer price index (CPI), hospital- (type and level of hospitals), and individual-level characteristics (education, qualifications, nature of employment, length of service, departments, job type, and positions) (see appendix 2).

Statistical analysis

Difference-in-differences combined with the propensity score matching method (PSM -DID) were employed to estimate the net reform effects [35–38]. Since we could only access one-year pre-treatment data, the PSM and the random selection test were used to address the potential sample selection bias, to create balanced groups [39–42]. We employed PSM approach to create balanced treatment and control group using 2016 data via a logit model incorporating education, qualification, nature of employment, departments, length of service, job type and position [43]. Nearest-neighbor matching with a matching ration 1:1 was used to obtain matched samples. Random selection tests and baseline comparisons of provincial- and hospital-level indicators confirmed group comparability and supported the parallel trends assumption.Finally, the model included individual and time fixed effects [44, 45]. We also conducted subgroup analyses by employment type and length of service, focusing on salaried versus contract staff-key groups for assessing the reform’s “equal pay for equal work” objective-and health workers with ≤ 10 years of service, reflecting the reform’s emphasis on early-career support. Treatment effects were considered robust if effect direction and statistical significance remained consistent with the main analysis. We also conducted robustness checks through altering the outcome variables—replacing percentages with natural logarithms and converting logarithmic compensation back to absolute values, redefining base salary as fixed salary (comprising basic salary plus allowances), excluding samples from Ningxia Autonomous Region, and removing control variables at the provincial level. All analyses were performed using Stata 15, with two-sided tests and a significance level of p < 0.05.

Results

Descriptive analysis

Table A2 presents the health workforce characteristics for the treatment group and the control group after matching. A total of 55 602 health workforce were surveyed in the pre-treatment period. Most were employed in tertiary hospitals (73.6% treatment and 64.6% control; respectively), general hospitals (92.6% and 70.5%; respectively), and internal medicine departments (25.6% and 24.9%; respectively). The majority had junior college education and below (40.8% and 51.4%; respectively), held registered nurses (48.5% and 48.9%; respectively), were salaried staff (52.2% and 56.3%; respectively), and occupied technical positions (91.9% and 94.0%; respectively). Average length of service was 12.35 years in the treatment group and 13.85 years in the control group. Approximately 3% of health workforce were excluded due to missing or anomalous salary data. There are no significant differences between final sample and data before cleaning (see appendix 4).

As shown in Appendix 5, the percentage of annual base salary increased to 34.1% in 2017, and the percentage of annual merit salary increase to 49.3% in 2017. The percentage of merit salary was higher than that of base salary. Total compensation, annual base salary, and annual merit salary increased to CNY 94 496.5, CNY 28,063.2, and CNY 50 358.8, respectively. Figure 2 compare the compensation and its structure in the treatment and control group before and after policy intervention. Both groups increases in all compensation primary and secondary measures from 2016 to 2017. Moreover, the difference of all compensation measures before and after policy intervention in the treatment group were larger compared to the control group.

Results of the random selection test

The differences in per capita GDP, number of health technical personnel per 1,000 population, population aged 65 and over, and CPI between the two groups were insignificant, verifying baseline comparability at the provincial-level (see appendix 6). The differences in total hospital revenue, medical care revenue, financial subsidy, personnel expenditures, number of health workers, number of visits, inpatient amount, and health workforce are responsible for the number of visits per day between the two groups were insignificant, indicating the randomness of hospital-level sample selection at baseline (see appendix 6).

Results of the PSM-DID analysis

The matching results between the two groups were shown in appendix 7. All covariates exhibited standardized biases below 5% after matching, indicating good matching quality. There was substantial overlap between the two groups after matching, indicating that the common support requirement to implement PSM was satisfied. Balance tests showed statistically significant differences in control variables between the two groups before matching, while these differences were no longer statistically different after matching, meeting the balancing assumption (see appendix 8).

Table 1 reports that the percentage of annual base salary and annual merit salary raised by 3.54% and 0.53% compared with the control group after RPHCS, with a stronger effect on the percentage of annual base salary. Therefore, our hypothesis 1 has been validated. Total compensation, annual base salary, and annual merit salary in medical personnels significantly increased by 1%, 13%, and 5% following the implementation of RPHCS, respectively. Therefore, our hypothesis 2 has been supported. Several factors were associated with compensation and its structure, including average social wages of urban workers, number of health technical personnel per 1,000 population, CPI, hospital levels, education, nature of employment, qualifications and departments.

Subgroup analysis and sensitivity analysis

Table 2 shows the heterogeneous effects of RPHCS on health workforce across hospital levels and types.In secondary hospital, the percentage of merit salary decreased 1.78% and merit salary decreased 15% following the implementation of RPHCS. Results for tertiary hospital were almost consistent with the main analysis, implying that our findings were robust. For different type of hospital, general hospitals showed the most pronounced increase in base salary proportion compared to Chinese medicine and specialized hospitals. Conversely, specialized hospitals exhibited the largest growth in both the amount and proportion of merit salary relative to general and Chinese medicine hospitals. Table 3 shows the effects of RPHCS on health workforce with the different nature of employment and length of service. Salaried staff experienced greater improvements in base salary proportion, total compensation, and base salary amount than contract staff following the reform. However, contract staff saw significant increases in performance-related components, with an 8% rise in merit salary and a 0.98% increase in its proportion. Among health workers with more than 10 years of service, the percentage of merit salary declined by 0.66%, while the base salary proportion rose most noticeably. Effects for staff with ≤ 10 years of service aligned with main results. Notably, the reform had no significant impact on total compensation for contract staff or those with ≤ 10 years of service, indicating only partial support for Hypotheses 3 and 4. Robustness checks—including alternative variable specifications, sample exclusions, and model adjustments—yielded results consistent with the main analysis, confirming the reliability of our findings (see appendix 9).

Discussion

Our study used a nationwide longitudinal sample from China to examine the effects of RPHCS. Our findings suggest that RPHCS may have contributed to an increase in both the percentage of base salary and merit salary, with a more pronounced effect on base salary. Moreover, RPHCS may have contributed to an increase in the total compensation, annual base salary, and annual merit salary. Heterogeneous findings suggest that general hospitals had the largest significant increase in base salary percentage, whereas specialized hospitals had greater growth in merit salary. However, the reform did not significantly improve total compensation for contracted staff or health workforce with a short length of service, though it did enhance the performance-related salary structure for these groups. Additionally, compensation were also influenced by the average social wages of urban workers, number of health technical personnel per 1,000 population, CPI, hospital levels, education, nature of employment, qualifications, and departments.

To the best of our knowledge, an important contribution of our research is that we empirically evaluate the impact of RPHCS on salary structure using a difference-in-differences design with national longitudinal data. The observed shift toward the percentage of base salary and merit salary aligns with the reform’s objectives of strengthening public hospital welfare and reducing provider-induced demand. This aligns with local reform practices: some regions have explored annual and negotiated salary systems, adjusted public hospital base salary standards, and expanded performance pay scope—consistent with a descriptive study in Sichuan Province, where fixed and merit salary growth rates in reform group (4.7% and 2.8%) were higher than that in the non-reform group (2.2% and − 4.5%) [46]. Similarly, a study in Zhejiang Province showed health workforce base salary percentage rose from 6.4% to 10.6% post-policy, further validating our findings [47].

Another interesting finding is that the greater growth in base salary relative to merit salary—while merit pay remained the dominant component—is contrary to the findings in developed countries [48]. The unequal compensation structure with lower fixed pay and higher incentive pay is the main cause of provider-induced demand [49, 50]. A study conducted in the US emphasized that a small percentage of performance pay in total compensation to reinforce the hospital’s core value of “the patient’s’ needs first” [51]. Additionally, in developing countries like Russia, most health workers prioritized fixed salary increases for income stability [52]—a demand mirrored in China’s context, underscoring the urgency for the government to further raise base salary percentages to align with global equity and stability goals. The findings is consistent with a survey in Nigeria, which shown that health workforce receiving PBF payments reported to be ‘woken up’ by performance bonuses [53]. Another studies in Africa also suggested that additional duty hours allowance scheme (ADHA) increased health workforce’ compensation, and nearly half (46%) of this were ADHA scheme payments [54].

Our analysis revealed a critical insight into the nature of the RPHCS reform: while annual base and merit salaries increased significantly by 13% and 5%, respectively, the growth in total compensation was marginal at 1%. The average total salary, average base salary and average merit salary in 2016 were CNY 91 417.2, 24534.3, and 47142.1, it meant increase to approximately CNY 92335.9, 27931.7, and 49558.5 annually following RPHCS. This suggests that RPHCS primarily reshaped the composition of compensation rather than generating substantial overall income growth. A survey in Russia found that the compensation of health workforce increased by 19% after public hospitals were allowed to pay merit bonuses, which was higher than the 1% growth in total compensation attributed to the reform [55]. The modest aggregate increase may be explained by the reform’s focus on structural adjustment via the “Two Permissions” policy, which raised salary caps and recalibrated base and performance standards within existing fiscal constraints.

Another critical finding is the uneven distribution of reform benefits across different staff groups, which contradicts RPHCS’s “equal pay for equal work” objective. Salaried staff gained more in base salary percentage, total compensation, and base salary than contracted staff; yet RPHCS had no significant effect on contracted staff and short-time staff’s total compensation. This mirrors pay gaps between different physicians: in England, junior doctors historically faced pay inequities, prompting policy interventions like guaranteed 2% annual pay rises and targeted contract negotiations to address “equal pay for equal work.” [56–58] In USA, there are pay gaps between independent physicians and hospital-owned practices [59] For China, the root cause likely lies in two mechanisms: first, contracted staff are often excluded from local government’s base salary adjustment policies, which primarily target salaried roles; second, performance evaluation systems in most hospitals still tie merit pay to seniority rather than role-specific workload or quality, leaving short-tenure staff at a disadvantage. This inequity not only undermines reform credibility, but also risks exacerbating staff turnover among contracted and junior workers. The rise in performance pay for contract and junior staff indicates that the reform has begun to tilt incentive mechanisms toward previously marginalized groups, aligning with the “equal pay for equal work” principle to some extent. The finding is in contrast to the study in England and Wales, there are inequities for groups of doctors (like different length of services) to get The National Clinical Excellence Awards, who work for full time are more likely to self-nominate [60]. However, the fact that these gains were insufficient to raise their overall total compensation highlights persistent institutional constraints. This suggests that while the reform has made progress in introducing performance elements for vulnerable groups, further adjustments are needed to ensure that these incentives translate into meaningful improvements in overall income equity.

Heterogeneity across hospital types further reflects policy adaptation to institutional contexts. General hospitals had the largest base salary percentage growth, while specialized hospitals saw larger merit salary increases—both consistent with RPHCS guidelines that “compensation adjustments should align with hospital functions.” General hospitals, as primary providers of basic care, benefit more from base salary increases, while specialized hospitals leverage merit pay (via the “Two Permissions” surplus allocation) to reward specialized skills. Secondary hospital’ slight merit salary decline (1.78%) is not a policy failure but a result of their limited medical service surpluses (compared to tertiary hospitals), which restricts merit pay funding—highlighting the need for additional fiscal support to ensure equitable reform outcomes across hospital levels. Consistent with evidence of unequal pay from Zimbabwe’s P4P programme, where bonuses ultimately favoured already better-resourced facilities because the largest share of rewards hinged on service volume [61].

In line with previous research, our study found compensation was strongly associated with hospital levels, education, nature of employment, and departments [62]. As in the current evaluation, differences in department choice and job types continue to be important in explaining the gap. We found that surgical, pediatrics obstetrics and gynecology, and emergency departments had higher annual merit salary than internal medicine, which is in line with the measure of the reform that the distribution of performance salary should be skewed toward high-risk departments. While there was no significant difference in total salary between departments, in contrast to a study that compared annual salary across specialties in the US, in which the salaries in internal medicine and pediatrics combined (−$24.36), family medicine (−$23.70), and internal medicine (−$24.27) were significantly lower for the general surgery [63].

Several limitations of our study warranted recognition. First, we were unable to access data on gender and productivity of health workforce; hence, our study may suffer from the omitted variable bias. Second, although this research used a nationwide sample, it did not cover all provinces in China. Third, 35% hospitals of treatment group implemented the policy in mid-2017, which may underestimate the effects of the policy. Fourth, we were unable to observe the long-term impact of reform due to the data limitation. Finally, although we used matching and random selection tests to balance the treatment and control groups, a limitation lies in the inability to fully verify the parallel trend assumption due to only having one year of pre-intervention data, which prevents ruling out pre-existing diverging trends between groups and may weaken the credibility of our causal inference. Lastly, we acknowledge that our study cannot explore the differential effects of variations in reform intensity or specific measures, and the design cannot definitively attribute causal effects to the annual salary system alone.

Conclusions

RPHCS may contribute to reshape salary structures for the health workforce, which have some vital policy implications for China and developing countries in implementing the payment system reform. A key finding is the differential impact of the reform: while it increased both base and merit salary components overall, the effect was disproportionately stronger for base salary—particularly in general hospitals and among salaried staff. Critically, the reform did not significantly improve total compensation for contracted and short-tenure health workers, despite increasing their performance-related pay proportion. This indicates that the RPHCS, in its current implementation, has yet to fully achieve its equity objective of “equal pay for equal work,” as institutional biases linked to employment nature and seniority appear to persist. Policymakers should thus prioritize refining performance evaluation systems and extending base salary protections to vulnerable groups to ensure equitable compensation outcomes.
