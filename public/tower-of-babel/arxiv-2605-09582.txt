Abstract
. For κ \kappa a regular uncountable cardinal, the higher Baire and Cantor spaces κ κ {}^{\kappa}\kappa and 2 κ {}^{\kappa}2 (endowed with the < κ {<}\kappa -box topology) have been relatively well-studied, but less is known about the case where κ \kappa is singular. We will consider several spaces of functions and box topologies that could serve as higher Baire and Cantor spaces for singular cardinals. The ultimate focus of the article lies in studying cardinal characteristics of the ideal of κ \kappa -meagre subsets of these spaces.

Much less studied is the higher Baire space
{}^{\mu}\mu
where we assume
\mu
is a singular cardinal. In this article, we make a contribution to close this gap by providing a systematic study of the topology of product spaces that involve a singular cardinal. It is
a priori
unclear what constitutes a higher Baire space for a singular cardinal: even if we just restrict ourselves to spaces of functions between two cardinals with some natural generalisation of the product topology (as we do in this article), there are various choices to be made.

Apart from the topological goal of studying higher singular product spaces themselves, we will study the cardinal charactersitics of the (higher) meagre ideal associated to each space. Indeed, the choice of space is significant, and does consistently lead to different cardinal characteristics.

2.
Topological considerations

Before we can study the higher Baire and Cantor spaces for singular cardinals, we have to properly define what these spaces are. When we generalise the classical context of
\omega
to the higher context of a singular cardinal
\mu
, we first have to consider what property of
\omega
we are trying to generalise: should we replace
\omega
by
\mu
or by its cofinality
\kappa
? The choice depends on whether we want to interpret
\omega
as a cardinality or as a cofinality. Exhausting all such choices in the generalisation of
{}^{\omega}\omega
and
{}^{\omega}2
, we end up with four sets of functions
{}^{\mu}\mu
,
{}^{\mu}\kappa
,
{}^{\mu}2
and
{}^{\kappa}\mu
as potential underlying sets for the higher Baire and Cantor spaces.

The ubiquitous choice for a topology on
{}^{\omega}\omega
(and
{}^{\omega}2
) is the product topology. Remember that a subbase for the product topology is given by cylinder sets of the form
C_{n,m}=\mathopen{}\left\{f\in{}^{\omega}\omega\penalty\ \middle|\penalty\ f(n)=m\right\}\mathclose{}
. To obtain a base, we close this subbase under finite intersections. The topologies we will consider in the higher context will have bases given by intersections of cylinder sets as well, but we have to choose how to generalise the notion of a
finite
intersection. The concept of “finiteness”—the natural choice of smallness with respect to
\omega
—can be understood in three ways: in terms of cardinality (finite cardinalities are strictly smaller than
\omega
), cofinality (finite sets in
\omega
are bounded), or a combination of both (the cardinality of a finite set is too small to be cofinal). For a regular cardinal, these three interpretations coincide, but for a singular cardinal they do not, and hence we will consider three topologies.

2.1.
Definition of the topological spaces

Let us, for the purpose of giving the definition of the topologies, fix our set of functions as the set
{}^{\delta}\rho
. That is,
\delta
is the domain and
\rho
is the range. Let
s\colon\delta\rightharpoonup\rho
be a partial functions from
\delta
to
\rho
, then we define the
cone
of
s
(relative to
{}^{\delta}\rho
) as:

\mathopen{}\left[s\right]\mathclose{}=\mathopen{}\left\{f\in{}^{\delta}\rho\penalty\ \middle|\penalty\ s\subseteq f\right\}\mathclose{}.

Clearly, each cone
[s]
is an intersection of cylinder sets in
{}^{\delta}\rho
; precisely,
[s]=\bigcap_{\alpha\in\mathrm{dom}(s)}C_{\alpha,s(\alpha)}
. Each of the topologies we consider has a base consisting of cones of a specific family of partial functions.

The
{<}\mu
-box topology
on
{}^{\delta}\rho
is generated by sets that are the intersection of
{<}\mu
-many cylinder sets. Equivalently, a base for the
{<}\mu
-box topology is given by
\mathopen{}\left\{\mathopen{}\left[s\right]\mathclose{}\penalty\ \middle|\penalty\ s\in\mathrm{Fn}_{\mu}(\delta,\rho)\right\}\mathclose{}
. In much the same way, we define the
{<}\kappa
-box topology
as being generated by intersections of
{<}\kappa
-many cylinder sets; equivalently, the base is given by
\mathopen{}\left\{[s]\penalty\ \middle|\penalty\ s\in\mathrm{Fn}_{\kappa}(\delta,\rho)\right\}\mathclose{}
. Finally, the
bounded topology
is generated by cones
[s]
for
s\colon\delta\rightharpoonup\rho
partial functions with a domain bounded in
\delta
; equivalently, the family of basic open sets is given by
\mathopen{}\left\{\mathopen{}\left[s\right]\mathclose{}\penalty\ \middle|\penalty\ s\in{}^{<\delta}\rho\right\}\mathclose{}

Assuming that
\mathrm{cf}(\delta)=\kappa

{<}\kappa
-box topology is the coarsest of the three, whereas the
{<}\mu
-box topology is the finest of the three. Moreover,
\delta<\mu
implies that the
{<}\mu
-box topology is discrete, and
\delta=\kappa
(which is regular) implies that the
{<}\kappa
-box and bounded topologies coincide. We will write
({}^{\delta}\rho,\kappa)
,
({}^{\delta}\rho,\mathrm{bd})
and
({}^{\delta}\rho,\mu)
for the space
{}^{\delta}\rho
equipped with, respectively, the
{<}\kappa
-box, bounded, and
{<}\mu
-box topologies. Similarly, we will abbreviate these topologies by “
\kappa
”, “
\mathrm{bd}
” and “
\mu
” in various places, e.g., in subscripts.

To summarise, we have four sets of functions—
{}^{\mu}\mu
,
{}^{\mu}\kappa

{}^{\mu}2
and
{}^{\kappa}\mu
—and three topologies—bounded,
{<}\kappa

consisting of one of the sets of functions

2.3.
The bounded topology spaces

For the spaces with the bounded topology, we will show that any of them is consistently homeomorphic to any other, and this consistency depends on whether the spaces have equal weight. We also repeat that the

Proof.

3.
Category and its additivity and covering numbers

Let us first review the classical notion of Baire category.
Let
X
be a topological space. A subset
N\subseteq X
is
nowhere dense
if every nonempty open
U
has a nonempty open
V\subseteq U
such that
N\cap V=\varnothing
, or equivalently if the interior of the closure of
N
is empty. In particular, each nowhere dense is contained in a closed nowhere dense set, and the complement of a closed nowhere dense set is open dense. A set is called
meagre
(or
of first category
) if it is the countable union of nowhere dense sets, and otherwise it is called
nonmeagre
(or
of second category
). The complement of a meagre set is called
comeagre
. The (classical) Baire category theorem states that, in any completely metrisable space, the countable intersection of open dense sets is dense. In other words, the Baire category theorem is equivalent to all comeagre sets being dense.

When the Baire category theorem (and with it the notion of category) are generalised to the higher context, we may often prove a stronger theorem, in which countable intersections are replaced by intersections of a larger family of open dense sets. For a cardinal
\lambda
, let us call a set
\lambda
-meagre
if it is the union of
\lambda
-many nowhere dense sets. Exactly, if we consider the higher Baire space
{}^{\lambda}\lambda
where
\lambda
is regular, then the intersection of
\lambda
-many open dense subsets of
{}^{\lambda}\lambda
is dense, or equivalently every
\lambda
-comeagre subset of
{}^{\lambda}\lambda
is dense. We will see that in the higher context for a singular cardinal
\mu
, the cofinality
\mathrm{cf}(\mu)=\kappa
plays the role of
\lambda
. We first establish that
\kappa
-meagre is the right notion to obtain something strictly more general than nowhere denseness. The proof of the following fact is elementary.

Fact 15
.

Let
(X,\tau)
be a space considered in
14
. Then
\kappa
is the least cardinal such that there exists a family of
\kappa
-many nowhere dense subsets of
X
whose union is dense.∎

One way to describe the statement of the above fact, is that the nowhere dense ideal of
(X,\tau)
is not
\kappa
-additive
. This leads us to the definition of four cardinal characteristics associated with an ideal
\cal I
on a space
X

Definition 16
.

Let
\cal I\subseteq\cal P(X)
be a proper ideal on
X
, then we define the following cardinal characteristics.

(1)

The
additivity number

\mathrm{add}(\cal I)
is the least size of a set
A\subseteq\cal I

\bigcup A\notin\cal I
.

(2)

The
covering number

\mathrm{cov}(\cal I)
is the least size of a set

\bigcup C=X

(3)

The

\mathrm{non}(\cal I)
is the least size of a set

,

(4)

The

It is well-known as well as easy to see that

For the space

Classically, no treatment of the cardinal characteristics of the meagre ideal would be complete without mentioning the bounding and dominating numbers. This section is added mostly for the sake of completeness, and because we will need dominating numbers in

We are primarily interested in domination on the sets of functions
{}^{\kappa}\kappa

{}^{\kappa}\mu

{}^{\mu}\kappa

(1)

\delta

\nu

(3)

6.
Cofinality numbers

In the context of higher Baire spaces for regular cardinals (i.e.,
{}^{\kappa}\kappa
) it was shown in
[
5
]
that
2^{\kappa}<\mathrm{cof}({\cal M}_{({}^{\kappa}\kappa,\kappa)})
is consistent, and in fact that
2^{<\kappa}<\mathrm{cof}({\cal M}_{({}^{\kappa}\kappa,\kappa)})
holds for every regular
\kappa
. The proof relies on a lower bound derived from a specific dominating number, and on closer examination it becomes clear that antichains of size
2^{<\kappa}
in the topology on
{}^{\kappa}\kappa
form the key to this lower bound. The method of proof can be generalised to give lower bounds for
\mathrm{cof}({\cal M}_{(X,\tau)})
for our spaces
(X,\tau)
with
\tau
either the
{<}\mu
-box topology or the bounded topology. We will use the notation from
Section

5
to describe the relevant dominating numbers.

Theorem 43
.

(1)

{\mathfrak{d}}_{({}^{\nu}\kappa,\mathrm{all})}\leq\mathrm{cof}({\cal M}_{({}^{\kappa}\mu,\kappa)})
for
\nu=\mu^{<\kappa}
.

(2)

{\mathfrak{d}}_{({}^{\nu}\kappa,\mathrm{all})}\leq\mathrm{cof}({\cal M}_{({}^{\mu}\mu,\mathrm{bd})})
for
\nu=\mu^{<\mu}

The proof structure is the same in both case, so we will discuss the general structure first. For both

of size
\nu

\mathopen{}\left\langle\sigma_{\gamma}\penalty\ \middle|\penalty\ \gamma\in\nu\right\rangle\mathclose{}
of
\cal S

Let
X\in\mathopen{}\left\{{}^{\kappa}\mu,{}^{\mu}\mu\right\}\mathclose{}
be the relevant space and suppose
\cal F\subseteq{\cal M}_{(X,\mathrm{bd})}
has cardinality
\mathopen{}\left|\cal F\right|\mathclose{}<{\mathfrak{d}}_{({}^{\nu}\kappa,\mathrm{all})}
, then we we will define for each

a set of functions

with
\mathopen{}\left|D_{A}\right|\mathclose{}\leq\nu

\nu<{\mathfrak{d}}_{({}^{\nu}\kappa,\mathrm{all})}

\mathopen{}\left|\bigcup_{A\in\cal F}D_{A}\right|\mathclose{}\leq\mathopen{}\left|\cal F\right|\mathclose{}\cdot\nu<{\mathfrak{d}}_{({}^{\nu}\kappa,\mathrm{all})}

g\in{}^{\nu}\kappa
such that
g\nleq_{\mathrm{all}}f
for any

. We then use

to define a set

that is nowhere dense in

\mathrm{cof}({\cal M}_{(X,\mathrm{bd})})

(I)

(II)

Construct

(III)

Show that
