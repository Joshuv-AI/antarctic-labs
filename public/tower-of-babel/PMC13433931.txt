Epithelial cell extrusion underlies starvation-induced cell loss in a sea anemone
Authors: Inés Fournon-Berodia, Noah Bruderer, Lionel Christiaen, Patrick R. H. Steinmetz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Epithelia likely predate the last common animal ancestor, yet the evolutionary origin and nutritional regulation of epithelial remodelling remain poorly understood. Here, we show that extensive, starvation-induced cell loss in the sea anemone Nematostella vectensis is associated with epidermal cell extrusion. This process involves formation of a rosette-like arrangement in which an apoptotic, extruding cell is surrounded by a phospho-ERK1/2-positive ring of cells, accompanied by basal translocation of adherens junction components. Combining chemical perturbations with computational quantification of extrusion and cell density, we show that apoptosis is necessary but not sufficient for rosette formation, and that ERK1/2 signalling limits epidermal extrusion density. Furthermore, we find increased extrusion activity during starvation, and potential nutrient recycling via phagocytosis of extruded cells. Together, our findings indicate that epithelial cell extrusion has physiological roles in sea anemones and that its key hallmarks are likely evolutionarily ancient, predating the last common cnidarian-bilaterian ancestor.

Introduction

Epithelia are a key evolutionary novelty of animals found throughout metazoans, including sponges, where their existence has long been debated1,2. They form a protective barrier and allow the development of structurally distinct tissues and organs. Key molecular components of epithelia, such as the Catenin/Cadherin complex at adherens junctions (AJ) (Fig. 1A, B) or the Integrin-based cell-matrix junction components, are widely conserved across nearly all major metazoan groups (Fig. 1D)3. Together, these findings led to the commonly accepted hypothesis that epithelia predate the last common metazoan ancestor4. Epithelia undergo a constant cell turnover that is essential for their renewal and integrity, and for responding to environmental insults, such as wounding. An imbalance in this turnover can have dramatic effects leading to over-proliferation or reduced cell loss, which are prime causes of many cancer types5,6. Over the last decade, studies in mammals7, zebrafish8, nematodes9 and flies10 have uncovered epithelial cell extrusion as a tightly regulated process to remove single epithelial cells without compromising the structural integrity of the epithelium. So far, it has been mainly studied in the context of development, during morphogenesis and as response to mechanical tissue stress, but little is known about its regulation by metabolic factors such as nutrient availability. In vertebrates and flies, cells surrounding the extruding cell adopt a characteristic ‘rosette’ formation, accompanied by actomyosin and AJ remodelling in both extruding and rosette cells (Fig. 1A–C). While rosette formation is a hallmark of epithelial cell extrusion, it can also occur during morphogenetic cell rearrangements11. During extrusion, the central cell typically undergoes Caspase-mediated apoptosis. However, it remains unclear whether apoptosis triggers extrusion or occurs as a secondary consequence, for example through anoikis, where apoptosis is initiated by loss of basal membrane contact12. Contraction of the supracellular ring-like actomyosin system in rosette and central cells leads to apical, basal or lateral extrusion13,14. A hallmark of extrusion in flies and vertebrates is the activity of extracellular signal-regulated kinases (ERK) pathway signalling in single or multiple rows of rosette cells, depending on the species and tissues15–17. ERK signalling is currently thought to mediate extrusion in a non-cell autonomous fashion and protect the surrounding epithelium against pro-apoptotic signals from the extruding cell15,16. A major cause of cell extrusion is epithelial crowding, but how cells are selected for extrusion remains poorly understood10,18,19.

In many bilaterian animals, nutrient availability greatly affects tissue homeostasis, as shown by starvation-induced atrophy of the intestinal epithelium in mice20, snakes21, and insects22,23. In extreme cases, starvation leads to whole-body shrinkage that is rare among vertebrates (e.g., fish, amphibians, reptiles)24–26, but common among planarians27, acoels28 and non-bilaterian taxa, including sea anemones29,30 (Fig. 1D). However, the environmental control of body and tissue plasticity remains poorly understood on cellular and molecular levels. In Drosophila, starvation induces shrinkage of the midgut epithelium by apoptosis- and cell extrusion-mediated cell loss23,31. Similarly, starvation-induced shrinkage in planarians is mediated by basal cell extrusion in the epidermis, although the underlying cellular and molecular mechanisms remain unclear32,33.

Recently, the sea anemone Nematostella vectensis has emerged as a powerful model system for studying the molecular and cellular basis of body plasticity. As is typical for cnidarians, Nematostella is composed of only two epithelial cell layers: the outer epidermis and inner gastrodermis34. Nematostella juveniles survive months of starvation and lose >50% of their total cell count over three weeks, starting as soon as 2 days after the last meal30,35. Such extensive cell loss necessitates large-scale epithelial rearrangement, making this system uniquely suited to study how environmental cues regulate epithelial remodelling. Here, we show that nutritional fluctuations trigger cell extrusion in Nematostella, revealing an ancient epithelial cell elimination mechanism that likely predates the cnidarian–bilaterian split.

Results

Cell extrusion rosettes in the Nematostella epidermis

We aimed to monitor epithelial cell extrusion in Nematostella vectensis (Nv) by searching for rosette-like cell arrangements. We visualised cell outlines in the epidermis of the body column by immunolabelling three AJ components: NvCadherin136 (Fig. 1E, E’), F-actin (Fig. 1F, F’) and a β-Catenin-mOrange2 fusion protein in a newly generated transgenic knock-in line (Fig. 1G, G’ and Supplementary Fig. 1B). Throughout this study, we focused on the body column epidermis of juvenile polyps, which is particularly accessible to in vivo confocal microscopy (Supplementary Fig. 1B, C). In all three labelling assays, we observed rosette-like cell arrangements in the Nematostella epidermis (Fig. 1E–G’). Because rosettes can also occur during morphogenetic cell rearrangements11, we established in vivo confocal time-lapse imaging for the Nematostella juvenile epidermis to visualise and characterise cell extrusion events (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Fig. 1B). These recordings confirmed progressive constriction and eventual disappearance of the central cell, accompanied by a shift of the intracellular β-Catenin-mOrange2 and F-actin from the apical cortex towards the basal side of the cell (Fig. 2D1–I6 and Supplementary Fig. 2C1–G3). Based on these observations, we propose four stages of epithelial extrusion in the Nematostella epidermis: (i) a ‘pre-rosette’ stage (Fig. 2D1–I1), in which the future extruding cell is indistinguishable from its neighbours; (ii) an ‘early rosette’ stage (Fig. 2D2–I2 and Supplementary Fig. 2C1–G1), marked by the initiation of rosette formation and central cell constriction; (iii) a ‘mid-rosette’ stage (Fig. 2D3–I3 and Supplementary Fig. 2C2–G2), during which β-Catenin and F-actin become apparent basally within a clearly defined rosette; and (iv) a ‘late rosette’ stage (Fig. 2D4–I6 and Supplementary Fig. 2C3–G3), characterised by a basal ring or dot of β-Catenin or F-actin and culminating in extrusion. In vivo, the entire process took ~12 min (11:33 ± 2:41 min, n = 13 extrusion events) from the early rosette stage initiation to complete extrusion (Supplementary Fig. 3B), without clear differences in duration between fed and starved animals (Supplementary Fig. 3B, Supplementary Data 1). This is much shorter than the hours-long extrusion process reported for the Drosophila gut epithelium31 and similar in duration to extrusion in the zebrafish periderm37 and in the Drosophila pupal notum, leg, and abdominal epithelia38–41.

To get insights into the direction of cell extrusion, we studied the location of nuclei within the epithelium. Live imaging of β-Catenin-mOrange2 combined with nuclear staining revealed cases of apical cell extrusion, where the nucleus of an extruding cell appeared apically in the plane of the adherens junction (Supplementary Fig. 3A–A” and Supplementary Movie 2). The pronounced nuclear DNA stain is consistent with genomic DNA condensation, one of several hallmarks of cell death (Supplementary Fig. 3C–C”). We also found cases where highly fragmented nuclei, indicative of late apoptosis, were located near the basal side of rosette cells (Supplementary Fig. 3D–E’). These may have resulted from basal or lateral extrusion events followed by phagocytosis into rosette cells (see also later Results sections). Our observations indicate that epithelial cell extrusion in the Nematostella juvenile epidermis occurs primarily apically, but occasional basal or lateral extrusion events remain possible.

Rosette and cell density dynamics in fed and starved polyps

Our findings suggest that epidermal cell extrusions contribute to the substantial cell loss reported during starvation-induced body shrinkage30. We therefore set out to characterise the dynamics of extrusion during feeding and starvation. To quantify epidermal rosettes in a reproducible, high-throughput manner, we developed an image segmentation and rosette prediction pipeline to analyse large numbers of maximum intensity projection (MIP) in vivo confocal 2D images of the β-catenin-mOrange2-labelled epidermis in transgenic juvenile polyps (Supplementary Fig. 4; Methods and Supplementary Notes). Using these images, we segmented individual epidermal cells with a fine-tuned Cellpose model to automatically predict cell outlines42. This semi-automated approach accelerated quantification by ~5-fold compared with manual rosette identification (Supplementary Fig. 4, Methods and Supplementary Notes). In Nematostella juveniles, cell proliferation rates are strongly feeding-dependent30,35. Strict control of feeding history is therefore required to control proliferation and, consequently, cell density, which influences cell extrusion rates in both vertebrates and flies10,43. We therefore sampled polyps either during daily feeding or following a standardised starvation/refeeding protocol consisting of a 1-hour refeeding pulse after 5 days of prior starvation (T5ds; sampled before refeeding) (Fig. 3A, B). This induces a controlled and relatively synchronised proliferative response in >50% of all cells within 48 h, before returning to baseline during continued starvation35. Notably, a 1-h refeeding pulse is sufficient to elicit a full proliferative response, comparable to 1 day of ad libitum refeeding35. This protocol thus ensures controlled feeding history across animals and precise control over the onset and duration of starvation.

Using cell segmentation masks, we quantified epidermal cell area as a proxy for tissue density. We found a bimodal distribution of cell area measurements into two populations (Supplementary Fig. 5A): (i) ~25% small cells of ~6.3 µm2 (95% CI = 6.2–6.4)), likely including stinging cells (i.e., cnidocytes) and sensory neurons; and (ii) ~75% larger cells measuring ~27 µm2 (95% CI = 26.2–27.9), likely consisting predominantly of epidermal cells (Supplementary Fig. 5B, C). The population of small cells showed no significant changes in mean cell area after feeding or during starvation, consistently remaining within a narrow range (6.1–6.8 µm²) (Supplementary Fig. 5D–G). We thus focussed our analysis on changes in the population of larger epidermal cells (Fig. 3C, D). Within this population, mean cell areas did not significantly differ between any time points over three days of daily feeding (Fig. 3C), between T5ds and 5 days post-refeeding (dpr) or between 10 and 20 dpr (Fig. 3D). However, we found a significant ~20% increase in mean cell area between 5 and 10 dpr (Tukey-adjusted p < 0.001), from ~26 µm² (95% CI: 24.4–27.6) to ~30 µm² (95% CI: 28.6–32.4) (Fig. 3D). Notably, cell areas at 10 and 20 dpr (~31 µm², 95% CI: 29.4–34.0) are also significantly higher (~27% adjusted p-value < 0.001) compared to daily fed samples (~25 µm², 95% CI: 23.3–26.8). Together, this data shows that cell density is similar between daily fed, T5ds and over the first 5 days after refeeding at T5ds, but that the epidermis becomes significantly less dense during prolonged starvation.

Across all timeseries datasets (Fig. 3A, B), epidermal rosettes typically comprised an average of 6.0 ± 1.8 cells, which is nearly identical to the number reported in the mammalian gut (~6 cells)44. We then quantified epithelial rosette density, corresponding to the number of rosettes per 1000 large cells in the body column epidermis. In daily fed polyps, we detected ~5‰ epidermal rosettes (Fig. 3E; 95% CI: 3.4–6.9). Refeeding at T5ds led to a significant, ~2.5-fold increase in mean rosette density from ~6‰ at T5ds (sampled before refeeding; 95% CI: 1.3–10.6) to ~15‰ at 4 days post-refeeding (dpr) (95% CI: 10.2–19.9; Tukey-adjusted p < 0.001) (Fig. 3F). Between 4 and 10 dpr, mean rosette density decreased significantly by ~30% to intermediate levels of ~10‰ (95% CI: 5.2–15.2; Tukey-adjusted p = 0.033) (Fig. 3F). Notably, rosette density at 10 and 20 dpr (~10‰; 95% CI: 8.1–13.3) remained ~2-fold higher compared to daily fed animals (~5‰, 95% CI: 2.6–8.2) or to T5ds (~6‰; 95% CI: 1.3–10.6) (Fig. 3E, F and Supplementary Data 4). Refeeding for 1 h after 20 days of starvation led to a ~40% decline in mean rosette density to ~6‰ within 24 h, reflecting the approximate levels in daily fed polyps or at T5ds (Fig. 3F) (Tukey-adjusted p = 0.0592, 95% CI: 1.3–11.5). This suggests that refeeding at 20 dpr had the opposite effect on extrusion dynamics compared to refeeding at T5ds (Fig. 3F). In summary, our results reveal dynamic changes in extrusion activity across feeding and starvation conditions that are not solely determined by tissue density.

ERK1/2 signalling regulates rosette density

To gain mechanistic insights into cell extrusion in Nematostella and enable molecular comparisons with bilaterians, we studied the role of ERK signalling during extrusion (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Figs. 6, 7). In vertebrates and flies, the ERK pathway is induced in rosette cells, coordinates the spatiotemporal dynamics of extrusion and protects the surrounding epithelium from pro-apoptotic signals15,16. We tested whether ERK activity in rosettes is evolutionary conserved in Nematostella by performing immunolabelling of phosphorylated ERK1/2 (pERK1/2; Fig. 4A–D” and Supplementary Fig. 6A–C”’) using a commercial antibody previously validated in Hydra45. Using this antibody, we found pERK1/2+ cell clusters broadly co-localising with early to late-stage rosettes throughout the epidermis (Fig. 4A–D”’). Manual quantification showed that ~89% of all rosettes identified by F-actin labelling were pERK1/2+ (n = 78/88; 95% CI: 80-94%; Fig. 4B’, C’, D’ and and Supplementary Data 1). These observations support a strong association between ERK1/2 signalling activity and rosette formation beyond early stages. To assess the function of ERK during cell extrusion, we used the small molecule U0126 inhibitor, which was previously validated in Nematostella larvae as an inhibitor of the mitogen-activated extracellular signal-regulated kinase (MEK) that directly phosphorylates ERK1/246. We confirmed that a 2-h treatment with 20 µM U0126 effectively decreased pERK levels below the detection limit also in the juvenile epidermis (Supplementary Fig. 7A–B’). Under these short-term experimental conditions, we found that MEK inhibition at 5 dpr, when cell and rosette densities are high, caused a significant increase in rosette density compared to the 0.5% DMSO-treated control (+88% change, Tukey-adjusted p-value = 0.0027) (Fig. 4E). At 20 dpr, when cell and rosette densities have declined, MEK inhibition led only to a mild, non-significant increase in mean rosette density (by ~14%; p = 0.57) (Fig. 4E). In contrast to rosette density, the mean cell area of large epidermal cells was not significantly different between U0126- and DMSO-treated polyps at 5 or 20 dpr (Supplementary Fig. 7C). The U0126-induced increase in rosette density is thus not indirectly caused by changes in tissue density. Our findings indicate that ERK signalling regulates cell extrusion density independently of tissue density in the Nematostella epidermis. Together, our results highlight ERK signalling activity in rosette cells as an evolutionary conserved feature of cell extrusion between Nematostella, flies and vertebrates.

Caspase-mediated apoptosis is required for cell extrusion

To test whether apoptosis drives the cell death observed during Nematostella cell extrusion, we assayed morphological and molecular markers that have been associated with different stages of apoptosis in bilaterians. We first studied p38 mitogen-activated protein kinase (p38 MAPK) signalling, which has been linked to apoptosis in Drosophila, planarians and mammals47–49. Using a phospho-specific p38 antibody (p-p38) previously validated in the cnidarian Hydra to detect p38 activity45, we found that p-p38+ cells often exhibited condensed or fragmented nuclei, indicative of late apoptotic states (Supplementary Fig. 6A–A”). Co-staining with the pERK1/2 antibody further confirmed that at least some p-p38+ cells were surrounded by pERK1/2+ cells within an extrusion rosette (Supplementary Fig. 6B–C”’). To capture earlier stages of apoptosis, we used an antibody detecting the conserved Caspase cleavage substrate motif (CCS) that becomes exposed upon Caspase-catalysed proteolysis50,51. After aligning fixed, F-actin-stained rosettes along the proposed staging system, we found CCS+ central rosette cells throughout all stages starting at pre- or early rosette stage (Fig. 5A–C”’). At the earliest stage, CCS+ cells were of comparable size to their neighbours, making it difficult to determine if rosette constriction had already been initiated (i.e., early rosette) or not (i.e., pre-rosette) (Fig. 5A–A”’). At late rosette stages, we observed that some extruded CCS+ cells were retained at the epithelial surface (Fig. 5F–F”). These observations prompted us to study the role of apoptosis during cell extrusion using Staurosporine, which induces apoptosis by non-selective inhibition of protein kinases52,53. We confirmed that 0.1 µM, 0.5 µM and 1 µM Staurosporine consistently induced apoptosis in the Nematostella body column epidermis in a concentration-dependent manner (Supplementary Fig. 8A–D’). Incubating polyps at 0.5 µM Staurosporine for 2 h predominantly induced apoptosis in single, spatially dispersed cells, allowing us to test whether isolated apoptotic cells can induce rosette formation (Supplementary Fig. 8C, C’). This treatment led to no significant changes in rosette density compared to 0.5% DMSO-treated controls at 5 dpr or 20 dpr (Fig. 5D). In contrast, Staurosporine caused a decrease in mean cell area at both 5 dpr (model adjusted p-value = 0.09) or 20 dpr (model adjusted p-value = 0.043) that may have resulted from cell loss by apoptosis (Supplementary Fig. 8G). Notably, Staurosporine treatment induced a broad increase in ERK activity across the epidermis, indicative of a global stress response15 (Fig. 5E–F’). Under these conditions, the lack of increased extrusion indicates that apoptosis per se is not sufficient to initiate epidermal cell extrusion in Nematostella.

To further explore whether apoptosis is essential for the initiation or progression of cell extrusion in Nematostella, we used the pan-Caspase inhibitor Q-VD-OPh to test whether blocking apoptosis affects rosette density54,55. Using confocal imaging, we confirmed that incubation in 100 µM Q-VD-OPh over 2 h robustly decreased epidermal CCS immunolabelling compared to 2% DMSO-treated controls (Supplementary Fig. 8H–I’). Under the same conditions, we found the inhibition of apoptosis suppressed rosette density at both 5 dpr (−20%, model-adjusted p-value = 0.17) and 20 dpr (−31%, model-adjusted p-value = 0.018) (Fig. 5E). Cell density, however, was not significantly affected between Q-VD-OPh and DMSO controls at any timepoint (Supplementary Fig. 8J). Together, our results suggest that Caspase-mediated apoptosis is necessary but not sufficient to trigger epithelial cell extrusion in the Nematostella epidermis.

Nutrient recycling by phagocytosis of extruding cells?

During late rosette stages, we occasionally observed CCS+ intracellular vesicles of 1–2 µm located within rosette cells (Fig. 5G–G”’ white arrowheads). These observations indicate efferocytosis, the engulfment and phagocytosis of apoptotic material by neighbouring rosette cells. It also intriguingly suggests that cell extrusion contributes to nutrient recycling during starvation. To test this assumption, we studied Target Of Rapamycin (TOR) signalling activity, a nutrient-sensitive regulator of anabolic processes (e.g., protein translation) conserved across fungi and animals56. Active TOR signalling was detected by immunolabelling of phosphorylated ribosomal S6 protein (pRPS6), a widely conserved TOR signalling readout previously validated in Nematostella35,57. As expected, we found broad epidermal pRPS6 staining in juvenile polyps at 24 h after feeding (Fig. 6A–A”). To test whether TOR signalling is required for rosette formation, we assessed the effect of AZD8055, a small molecule inhibitor of TOR complexes 1 and 2, on extrusion density35,58,59 (Supplementary Fig. 9A–D). We found that incubating juvenile polyps in 1 µM AZD8055 for 2 h strongly decreased epidermal pRPS6 levels (Supplementary Fig. 9A–B’) but did not significantly affect rosette or tissue densities compared to 0.5% DMSO controls (Supplementary Fig. 9C, D). Altogether, these data indicate that activation of TOR signalling is downstream of rosette formation and may result from nutrient recycling.

Discussion

Epithelia are found across all animal taxa, yet the evolutionary origins of their plasticity and regulation by metabolic cues remain poorly understood. In this study, we used the epidermis of the sea anemone Nematostella vectensis as a tractable system to investigate the cellular mechanisms underlying starvation-induced cell loss and body shrinkage. Juvenile polyps lose more than half of their cells within three weeks of starvation, whereas refeeding triggers a pronounced proliferative burst from previously quiescent cells30,35. These observations prompted us to investigate how the epithelium accommodates such extensive cell loss without compromising epithelial integrity. In vertebrates and Drosophila, epithelial cell extrusion plays a central role in maintaining epithelial homeostasis and integrity31,60. Using live imaging of a transgenic knock-in reporter line expressing the β-Catenin-mOrange2 fusion protein to visualise AJs, we discovered and characterised epithelial cell extrusion in the Nematostella epidermis.

Together with F-actin labelling and immunostaining of Nematostella Cadherin-1 and pERK1/2, we found that extrusion involves the formation of pERK1/2+ rosette cells, basal translocation of AJ-associated F-actin and β-Catenin-mOrange2, and predominantly apical extrusion of a central apoptotic cell. In vertebrates and flies, a cell-autonomous contractile F-actin ring in the extruding cell and a supracellular F-actin ring undergoing apical-basal translocation in surrounding cells are considered hallmarks of the extrusion process7,13. However, comparisons between Drosophila and different vertebrate systems revealed a substantial diversity in the timing, direction and coordination of ring contraction and AJ remodelling40,61–63. In Nematostella, our current methods did not allow us to resolve whether the translocating F-actin and β-Catenin rings reside primarily in the central cell, in neighbouring rosette cells or in both. Nonetheless, our data suggest that translocating AJs may actively contribute to extrusion: the basal shift of both F-actin and β-catenin during constriction, together with dynamic interactions involving Cadherin-1, likely maintains the epithelial seal while pushing the extruding cell apically (Fig. 7). Extrusion directionality also shows substantial variation across animals, developmental stages and tissues64. Vertebrate epithelia generally shed cells apically7,8, whereas delamination in the Drosophila larval epidermis typically occurs basally65. However, exceptions such as the apical extrusion in the fly gut31 and bidirectional extrusion in the Xenopus embryonic ectoderm66 highlight that the direction of extrusion is also context-dependent. In Nematostella, we predominantly observed apical extrusion events, although basal or lateral extrusion may also occur but be more difficult to detect in our imaging setup.

Another molecular hallmark of extrusion in flies and vertebrates is the wave-like activation of the ERK pathway in surrounding rosette cells. During extrusion, ERK1/2 signalling coordinates the spatial distribution of rosettes and provides a pro-survival signal that protects the surrounding epithelium from initiating additional apoptotic extrusion events15,16,67. In Nematostella, we observed consistently high levels of pERK1/2 in surrounding rosette cells from early stages through extrusion, whereas pERK1/2 remained undetectable in central cells. In some cases, weaker pERK1/2 signal extended beyond the rosette into adjacent cells. Whether this variable spatial extent reflects different phases of a potential wave-like ERK1/2 phosphorylation15 or a pulse-like signalling pattern16 will require future in vivo imaging studies using ERK activity sensors68,69.

Pharmacological inhibition of ERK1/2 phosphorylation using the MEK inhibitor U0126 increased rosette density, supporting a role for ERK1/2 in spatially restricting extrusion events. However, because our analyses relied on single time-point measurements, we cannot exclude the possibility that MEK inhibition prolonged the duration of extrusion events, which would have also increased apparent rosette density. Developing a segmentation and tracking pipeline for time-lapse imaging, similar to those developed for Drosophila pupal notum or wing disc16,70, would enable quantitative analysis of extrusion dynamics and help resolve this question. We also observed that increased rosette density was not accompanied by a change in cell density. This is unlikely to be explained by compensatory proliferation, as ERK inhibition typically reduces cell proliferation rates in Nematostella71 and other systems72. Instead, the 2-hour incubation, intended to capture acute effects while minimising secondary effects, may have been too short to result in a measurable decrease in cell density despite increased cell loss.

In vertebrates and flies, the extruding cell consistently undergoes apoptosis, but the causal relationship between the Caspase-mediated apoptosis initiation and cell extrusion initiation remains debated. For example, effector Caspase activation precedes and is required for extrusion in the Drosophila pupal notum10,73, whereas in C. elegans, extrusion can occur independently of Caspases9. In Nematostella, the central rosette cells consistently exhibited condensed or fragmented nuclei, activated p38 MAPK signalling and high levels of cleaved Caspase substrate (CCS) immunolabelling. Phospho-p38 has been associated with apoptotic cells in Hydra, planarians and mice45,48,49, and CCS likewise marks apoptotic cells in human cancer cell lines and in Nematostella50,51. Because CCS+ cells appear already during early, and possibly even pre-rosette stages, Caspase-mediated apoptosis in Nematostella likely precedes extrusion initiation, consistent with observations in the Drosophila pupal notum10. This timing argues against a major role for anoikis, in which loss of basal membrane attachment induces apoptosis12.

Experimentally inducing apoptosis with Staurosporine, which produced predominantly isolated apoptotic events in the context of a global stress response, did not alter extrusion density, indicating that apoptosis alone is not sufficient to trigger cell extrusion. This contrasts with mammalian cell lines, where apoptosis induction increases the frequency of extrusion7,74, and suggests that cell loss in Nematostella may also occur through alternative, rosette-independent expulsion mechanisms (e.g., by T1 or T2 transition remodelling75). It also remains possible that Staurosporine-induced apoptosis accelerates extrusion, which would not be captured by our single time-point recordings.

Conversely, inhibiting Caspases in Nematostella using Q-VD-OPh reduced extrusion density, consistent with findings in fly and mammalian systems74,76. As with U0126 treatment, the 2-hour Q-VD-OPh incubation may have been too short for these changes to translate into measurable differences in cell density. Altogether, our results indicate that epidermal extrusion events in Nematostella are tightly associated with Caspase-mediated apoptosis, which appears necessary but not sufficient for extrusion initiation.

The extensive loss of cells – and thus nutritive biomass – during starvation could be partially mitigated by local nutrient recycling. Indeed, we observed intracellular, ~1–2 µm-sized vesicles containing nuclear fragments derived from CSS+ apoptotic cells within rosette cells, consistent with efferocytosis-mediated recycling of extruded material14,77. After prolonged starvation, epidermal TOR signalling became largely restricted to late-stage rosette cells, the majority of which (71%) contained such nuclear debris. However, TOR inhibition by AZD8055 did not affect rosette density, indicating that TOR activity is not required for extrusion. Together, these observations are consistent with a model in which efferocytosis of apoptotic material contributes to local nutrient recycling during prolonged starvation and may activate TOR signalling in rosette cells. On a cellular level, similar processes have been described in planarians, where extruded epidermal cells are engulfed and digested by intestinal phagocytes as part of ongoing tissue turnover32,33, and in Hydra, where starvation-induced cell loss is accompanied by efferocytosis of Caspase-3+ apoptotic cells in the epidermis78,79.

Although extrusion has been studied primarily in the contexts of development and disease, its role in feeding-dependent tissue or body plasticity remains poorly understood. Starvation-induced intestinal shrinkage has been documented in snakes, mice and Drosophila, but a dependence on extrusion-driven cell loss has only been demonstrated for Drosophila midgut shrinkage23,31. We therefore developed a machine-learning-based pipeline to quantify extrusion dynamics in Nematostella and assess their potential contribution to starvation-induced tissue remodelling.

Our results reveal a complex relationship between cell extrusion, proliferation and tissue density in the Nematostella juvenile epidermis. Although extrusion remained low during daily feeding despite relatively high tissue density (Fig. 7G), a marked increase in extrusion occurred following refeeding after five days of starvation (T5ds; Fig. 7H). The absence of a preceding increase in tissue density argues against a simple density-driven mechanism. Instead, we propose that increased extrusion reflects a transient mismatch between proliferation and the tissue’s capacity to accommodate newly added cells. One possible explanation is that rapid, refeeding-induced proliferation is not matched by extracellular matrix production, potentially due to limited nutrient availability after a short refeeding pulse. In contrast, low extrusion at T5ds, when proliferation is low and tissue density remains comparable to fed animals, may reflect a state in which tissue accommodation is not limiting. It is also possible that extrusion transiently increased prior to T5ds, consistent with previous observations of elevated cell loss during this period30,35. During prolonged starvation (Fig. 7I), extrusion persists at intermediate levels despite reduced tissue density and low proliferation30,35, suggesting that additional, potentially metabolic or energetic factors contribute to extrusion in this regime, as recently reported in mammalian systems80. Together, these observations suggest that extrusion dynamics are shaped by multiple interacting factors, including proliferation dynamics and metabolic state, rather than tissue density alone.

Overall, we conclude that cell extrusion in the Nematostella epidermis shares key molecular and morphological hallmarks with extrusion in Drosophila and vertebrates, including rosette formation, translocation of AJ components, active ERK signalling in rosette cells and apoptosis induction in the extruding cell7,15,16,64. These similarities strongly support the view that epithelial cell extrusion represents an evolutionary conserved mechanism of epithelial remodelling, dating back at least to the last common ancestor of cnidarians and bilaterians. This interpretation is consistent with previously described forms of epithelial cell shedding in other cnidarians, such as continuous shedding of cnidocytes at Hydra tentacle tips81,82, or the loss of symbiont-containing gastrodermal cells during coral bleaching83,84. Comparable epithelial cell loss was also observed in other non-bilaterian taxa, including expulsion of apoptotic cells during sponge metamorphosis85 and from choanocyte chambers86,87, and radiation-induced cell shedding in the placozoan Trichoplax adhaerens88. Notably, placozoans, sponges and ctenophores also undergo starvation-induced shrinkage, raising the possibility that epithelial extrusion-like processes contribute to cell loss and nutrient recycling across non-bilaterian lineages89–91. However, because these processes have not yet been resolved at the molecular and cellular levels, it remains unclear whether they represent bona fide extrusion or distinct modes of epithelial cell removal. Future studies will be required to determine whether epithelial extrusion represents not only a conserved mechanism of tissue remodelling but also an evolutionarily ancient strategy for nutrient-dependent tissue plasticity.

Methods

Nematostella breeding and husbandry

Nematostella vectensis specimens originate from CH6 females and CH2 males collected at Rhode River, MA92. Adult males and females are kept separately in the dark at 18 °C in ~16 ppt filtered seawater (Nematostella medium, NM) and fed with fresh Artemia nauplii five times per week, with a daily partial water exchange and a monthly full cleaning of the culture boxes. Spawning is induced every three weeks triggered by temperature increase (25 °C) and a 12-h light cycle overnight93. Embryos are maintained at 25 °C, and feeding with Artemia nauplii begins approximately 7–10 days post-fertilization. Sex was not determined in juveniles; therefore, all data shown include both males and females. All experiments were performed on juvenile polyps of ∼5 mm in length, approximately 1 month after fertilisation

Immunofluorescence

Juvenile polyps were relaxed in 0.1 M MgCl₂ in Nematostella Medium (NM) and then transferred to a plate containing 3.7% formaldehyde/ 0.5% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) in NM (“killing solution”). They were then carefully transferred into a fixation solution of 1× Phosphate-Buffered Saline (PBS)/ 3.7% formaldehyde/ 0.5% DMSO/ 0.1% Tween-20 and fixed overnight at 4 °C. Following fixation, samples were thoroughly washed with 1× PBS/ 0.1% Tween-20 and stored at 4 °C for a maximum of 10 days. Samples were then blocked in 1× PBS/ 5% normal goat serum (NGS)/ 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA)/ 0.2% Triton X-100 for two hours at room temperature. Primary antibody incubation was performed in 5% NGS/ 1% BSA/ 0.2% Triton X-100 overnight at 4 °C. Primary antibody dilutions were used as follows: 1:200 for Cleaved Caspase Substrate, 1:50 for phospho-p38 conjugate, 1:200 for phospho-S6 ribosomal, 1:200 for phospho-ERK1/2, 1:200 for phospho-p38 and 1:500 for Cadherin1 (see Supplementary Table 1 for commercial catalogue numbers). Following overnight incubation, tissues were washed in 1× PBS/ 0.2% Triton X-100 and then blocked in 1× PBS/ 5% NGS/ 0.2% Triton X-100 for 30 minutes at room temperature. Hoechst nuclear staining (1:10,000 dilution) (ThermoFisher) and Phalloidin F-actin staining (1:50, dilution) and secondary antibodies (1:1000 dilution) (see Supplementary Table 1 for commercial catalogue numbers) incubated in 1× PBS/ 5% NGS/ 0.2% Triton X-100 overnight at 4 °C. Finally, samples were thoroughly washed in 1× PBS/ 0.2% Triton X-100, cleared in glycerol and mounted on slides (Electron Microscopy Sciences, 63418-11) in 80% glycerol. Coverslips (Menzel-Gläser, 18 × 18 mm) were sealed with clear nail polish. Exceptionally, rabbit anti-phospho-RPS6 was blocked for three nights35. For the Cadherin1 antibody, tissues were instead fixed for 1 h at 4 °C with Lavdovsky’s fixative (3.7% formaldehyde/ 50% ethanol/ 4% acetic acid)36. For double immunolabelling, polyps were incubated with phospho-p38 Alexa Fluor 594 conjugate at a 1:50 dilution along with the phospho-ERK1/2 antibody at a 1:100 dilution in blocking buffer at 4 °C overnight. The protocol then proceeded as usual, except that no secondary antibody was necessary for the phospho-p38 Alexa Fluor 594 conjugate45. Tissue staining and immunohistochemistry have been performed on different feeding states, including early post-feeding (1-2, 5 days starved), as well as late starvation (20 days starved). In the manuscript, exemplary images represent the results found across multiple feeding states unless a significant different result was found between fed and starved states.

Drug treatments

All pharmacological inhibitors were prepared from 100% stocks and diluted in NM to their working concentrations: AZD-8055 from 10 mM stock to 0.1 µM35, Staurosporine from 500 µM to 0.5 μM; U0126 from 10 mM to 20 μM, and QV-D-OPh from 10 mM to 100 μM. All treatments had additional DMSO added to a final concentration of 0.5% in NM, and the negative controls included 0.5% DMSO to match treatments (see Supplementary Table 1 for commercial catalogue numbers), except for QV-D-OPh which was used at 1% DMSO for live imaging. For all drug treatments, polyps were incubated in drug solution for 2 h at 25 °C standard culturing temperature prior to imaging. This incubation time was chosen as a compromise between allowing sufficient drug penetration and effective perturbation while minimising potential secondary effects. Because individual extrusion events occur on a timescale of ~10–15 min, the aim was to capture acute rather than long-term or potentially irreversible effects. For live imaging, animals were relaxed by some drops of 1 M MgCl2 added 30 min before drug removal, followed by transfer into fresh NM with 0.1 M MgCl2 followed by mounting into in Ibidi µ-Slide VI 0.4 (Cat. # 80606) and confocal imaging.

Transmitted light and confocal imaging

Immunofluorescence samples were imaged using an Olympus FV3000 confocal microscope (standard PMT detectors, 63× silicon-immersion objective). Live juvenile polyps were relaxed in 0.1 M MgCl₂ in NM, mounted on an Ibidi µ-Slide VI 0.4 (Cat. # 80606) and also imaged on the Olympus FV3000 confocal microscope. Confocal room temperature was at a range between 20 and 22 °C, and therefore live imaging was performed at that temperature. Images and confocal stacks were processed, cropped, and adjusted for levels and colour balance using ImageJ/Fiji.

CRISPR/Cas9-mediated generation of β-Catenin-mOrange2 knock-in line

The used protocols were adapted from previously established protocols for the generation of CRISPR/Cas9-mediated knock-in lines in Nematostella vectensis94,95. A single guide RNA (gRNA) was designed with a predicted cut site 14 bp upstream of the Stop codon of the Nematostella β-catenin gene (Nvec200_v1.10389.1.p1) using CRISPOR96. The gRNA template was generated by annealing and elongating an invariant T7 promotor/tracrRNA-encoding and a sequence-specific oligos97 (ThermoFisher, desalted, see Supplementary Table 2 for sequences). In vitro transcription of the gRNAs was performed using a T7 MegaScript Transcription Kit (ThermoFisher), followed by ammonium chloride precipitation and dilution in nuclease-free water to a final concentration of 1.5 μg/μl. The donor DNA fragment for homology-mediated repair (HDR) consisted of the 3’-end of the β-catenin gene open reading frame (ORF) cloned in-frame with a (GGGGS)2 linker and the mOrange2 protein-coding sequence. The construct also contained 994 bp long ‘left’ (upstream) and 936 bp long ‘right’ (downstream) homology arms (see Supplementary Table 2 for sequences). This entire construct was cloned into a pJet1.2 plasmid backbone (ThermoFisher) using Gibson Assembly Master Mix (NEB)98. The donor DNA fragment was PCR-amplified using biotin-labelled oligos (ThermoFisher; desalted, see Supplementary Table 2 for sequences) flanking the donor fragment to enhance integration efficiency99. Cas9-mediated knock-in injections were carried out by modifying existing protocols for CRISPR/Cas9-mediated mutagenesis, using 0.75 μg/μl TrueCut Cas9 protein v.2 (Thermofisher), 75 ng/μl of each guide RNA, 70 ng/μl column-purified donor DNA, and a modified injection buffer containing 220 mM KCl100. Approximately 1600 zygotes were injected, and the resulting juvenile polyps displaying mOr2+ signal were selected and raised to sexual maturity. At the adult stage, polyps exhibiting mOrange2 signal in the developing gametes were chosen as founder animals and outcrossed with wild-type animals to generate the F1 generation. The successful integration of the linker-mOrange2 fragment knock-in was validated at the transcript level by cDNA synthesis from total RNA extracted from a mix of larvae tissue resulting from a cross of two heterozygous F1 animals. Using this cDNA, regions spanning the transition between the 3’ UTR and mOrange2-encoding region, and between the mOrange2-encoding region and the 3’ end of the β-catenin ORF were amplified using oligos outside the homology arm regions (see Supplementary Table 2). Sanger sequencing of PCR fragments following gel elution and column purification confirmed the flawless, in-frame integration of the linker-mOrange2 fragment into the locus. The successful, flawless genomic integration was further validated at the genomic level by extracting genomic DNA from mixed tentacle clips of several homozygous F2 animals from the same founder F1 animals. Based on this genomic DNA, the transitions between the 3’-end of the β-catenin ORF and the (GGGGS)2 linker/mOr2 and between and the 3’ end of the mOr2 ORF and the 3’UTR of the β-catenin gene were amplified using oligos outside the homology arm regions (see Supplementary Table 2 for sequences). Gel elution and Sanger sequencing confirmed successful integration.

Image acquisition and experimental design

Data acquisition was performed using a confocal microscope. For each β-catenin-mOrange2 transgenic juvenile Nematostella vectensis polyp, two to three sets of image stacks were acquired, focusing on the epidermal cell layer. Approximately 10–15 animals per treatment condition or timepoint were imaged to ensure sufficient statistical power. In the starvation experiment, animals were initially starved for five days following continuous daily feeding (T5ds) to control for their feeding history. They were sampled at T5ds or refed for 1 h and subsequently sampled at the following timepoints: 1 day post-refeeding (dpr) through to 5 dpr, 10 dpr, and 20 dpr. At 20 dpr timepoint, animals were fed again for 1 h and sampled twenty-four hours later to obtain a final timepoint (+24 h). This starvation experiment was conducted with two biological replicates.

For the drug treatments, animals were starved for 5 days (T5ds) fed for 1 h and then starved for either 5 dpr or 20 dpr before being treated with one of four compounds: AZD8055, U0126, QV-D-OPh, or Staurosporine (see details above for Drug treatments). Each treatment condition had 3 biological replicates and corresponding DMSO controls. In total, this resulted in two timepoints (5 and 20 dpr), four inhibitors, four respective controls, and three independent replicates per condition, a total of 937 images. As with the starvation data, two to three image stacks were acquired per animal, with approximately 10–15 animals imaged per treatment or control group.

Juvenile polyps used for the daily fed dataset were maintained under the normal daily feeding regime used for general Nematostella vectensis husbandry. Animals were fed daily each morning and then cleaned in the early afternoon to remove residual food and waste before imaging. Imaging was performed at the same time of the day (14.00–16.00) as all other experiments (starvation and drug) to standardise conditions across all samples. For each of the three consecutive days, a different biological batch of animals was used, providing three independent replicates. This resulted in three separate datasets representing animals under daily feeding conditions. As with the starvation and drug experiments, two to three image stacks were acquired per animal, with approximately 10–15 animals imaged per biological replicate.

Image curation and cell segmentation pipeline

Following image acquisition, the image stacks were manually curated to select the most appropriate z-planes, specifically those where the surface of the epidermis was best visualised. Image z-stacks were then edited into 2D maximum intensity projections with the use of ImageJ/Fiji101 to improve the coverage of the epithelium especially in cases of capturing areas in which the epidermis was not entirely flat while also improving the focus of the image. These curated images were then subjected to initial segmentation using Cellpose software (https://www.cellpose.org)42. For the time-series dataset, approximately 250 of 500 images were manually corrected after initial segmentation to refine cell boundaries and eliminate segmentation errors. These manually corrected masks were then used to train a custom Cellpose Cyto3 base model, which improved performance and was subsequently used to segment the remainder of the time-series dataset as well as the inhibitor dataset. Post-segmentation processing included computational quality control steps to remove background artefacts, identify unsegmented regions, and classify cells based on size. Segmented objects below a defined pixel area threshold were assumed to be “oversegmented” or background noise and were merged with neighbouring cells. Conversely, objects with abnormally large areas were excluded as background or segmentation artefacts (see further details in Supplementary Notes). The resulting processed masks were used for further analysis.

Rosette annotation and prediction

For automatic rosette segmentation, we trained an Attention U-Net deep learning model102 on a manually annotated (i.e. human-in-the-loop workflow) subset of 268 time-series images. To prioritise multicellular geometric features over intensity cues, the model was trained exclusively on two geometric inputs derived from cell segmentations: binary cell boundary maps and morphologically dilated boundaries rather than raw fluorescence. Model performance was monitored on a 20% held-out validation set using Dice coefficient. Following inference, all predictions underwent manual review and correction. We quantified the method’s efficiency by calculating the speed-up factor of this model-assisted workflow compared to two baselines: manual identification on segmented images and manual identification on raw data (see further details in Supplementary Notes). To ensure reproducibility and facilitate scalable processing across all datasets, the entire analysis workflow—from segmentation to quantification—was orchestrated using a custom pipeline with managed software environments (see further details in Supplementary Notes).

Statistical analysis and graphical representation of data

Data visualization and analysis were performed using R103 (v. 4.5.2 (2025-10-31), https://www.r-project.org/, see Supplementary Notes for packages used). All statistical tests were two-sided. Timepoint and treatment condition were treated as fixed effects, with replicate and animal identity included as random effects where appropriate; effect sizes were estimated as differences in Tukey-adjusted means with 95% confidence intervals. Cell area measurements exhibited a robust bimodal distribution consisting of two populations of small and large cells (see Zenodo public repository for raw data files 1, 3, and 5–8; 10.5281/zenodo.19726756. To statistically define these classes, a Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM) with two components was fitted to each image (see Supplementary Notes for details and public repository at Zenodo for raw data files 2, 4, 9–12; 10.5281/zenodo.19726756). Cells were classified as small or large based on Bayesian posterior probabilities. Across all datasets, the proportions of cell classes were stable (~25% small, ~75% large), and the size of the small-cell population remained invariant across treatments and timepoints. As a result, small cells were excluded from analyses of rosette density. Rosette density was calculated as the number of rosettes per 1000 large cells per image. For the rosette-density timeseries and daily fed datasets, temporal changes were assessed using linear mixed-effects models to account for replicate variability and to obtain model-adjusted means and p-values (see Supplementary Data 2, 5). Cell area dynamics were analysed separately for small and large cells. Cell area was also modelled using linear mixed-effects models from which estimated marginal means (EMMs) and Tukey-adjusted pairwise comparisons were extracted (see Supplementary Data 3 and 6). P-values from multiple comparisons were adjusted using Tukey’s method where applicable. Additional details on mixed-model fitting are provided in Supplementary Notes. For drug experiments, treated vs. control comparisons were assessed using Wilcoxon rank-sum tests and Kruskal–Wallis tests, supplemented with rank-transformed mixed models (see further details in Supplementary Notes and Supplementary Data 7–14). All reported means in the main text, p-values, and percentage changes refer to EMM-adjusted results. Rosette density and cell area were visualised using boxplots and violin plots, respectively. For manual/visual quantifications (see Supplementary Data 1), confidence intervals for proportions reported were calculated using Wilson’s method. Figures were exported as vector graphics (PDF), and final layouts were refined in Adobe Illustrator.
