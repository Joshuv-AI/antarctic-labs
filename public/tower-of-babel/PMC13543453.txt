Autologous pericardial conduit reconstruction of an inadvertently transected left superior pulmonary vein during thoracoscopic surgery: A case report and literature review
Authors: Wei Zhu, Huanqi Mo, Kan Wang, Yuan Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMajor vascular complications during video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) are uncommon, and complete transection of a non-target pulmonary vein by an endoscopic stapler is exceedingly rare. When venous drainage of a preserved lobe is abruptly interrupted, rapid pulmonary congestion and life-threatening complications may ensue. Optimal management is particularly challenging when direct reconstruction is not technically feasible and additional lung resection is poorly tolerated.Case presentationA 76-year-old man with left lower lobe lung cancer and moderate-to-severe obstructive ventilatory dysfunction underwent thoracoscopic resection, during which the left superior pulmonary vein was inadvertently completely transected. The left upper lobe became progressively congested. Because the proximal stump was only 3–5 mm long and located adjacent to the left atrium, direct reanastomosis was not possible. Additional left upper lobectomy, combined with the planned lower lobe resection, would have effectively amounted to left pneumonectomy. After conversion to thoracotomy, an autologous pericardial patch was fashioned into a tubular conduit and used to reconstruct the transected vein. The planned lobectomy was modified to extended S7 + 8 segmentectomy to preserve pulmonary function. Postoperative imaging showed no obvious residual congestion and satisfactory perfusion of the remaining left lung; however, conduit patency was not directly confirmed radiologically but was inferred from these favorable imaging findings and the absence of clinical signs of venous obstruction. Follow-up CT at approximately 3 months demonstrated significant resolution of pulmonary exudative lesions with no evidence of recurrent venous congestion.ConclusionsAutologous pericardial conduit reconstruction may provide an effective salvage strategy for inadvertent pulmonary vein transection during VATS when standard repair is not feasible. Combined with parenchymal-sparing resection, this approach may be particularly valuable in patients with limited pulmonary reserve. Introduction

Lung cancer ranks among the leading malignancies worldwide in terms of both incidence and mortality [1]. Surgical resection remains the most important curative treatment modality for early- and intermediate-stage non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), with lobectomy combined with systematic lymph node dissection being the standard procedure. With the widespread adoption of minimally invasive surgical approaches, video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) has become the mainstream technique, and endoscopic staplers are routinely used for transection of pulmonary vessels and bronchi. However, the confined operating space, two-dimensional visual field, and restricted instrument angulation inherent to thoracoscopic surgery render the risk of vascular injury non-negligible. The reported incidence of major intraoperative vascular complications during VATS anatomic resection ranges from approximately 1.5% to 6.3% [2–4]. Types of vascular injury include vessel wall laceration, hemorrhage, and inadvertent injury to non-target vessels, among which complete transection of a non-target pulmonary vein by an endoscopic stapler is exceptionally rare. As pulmonary veins drain directly into the left atrium, complete transection immediately obstructs venous return from the preserved lobe, leading to acute pulmonary congestion and hemorrhagic pulmonary infarction, with potentially severe postoperative complications such as intractable hemoptysis [5, 6]. To date, only Hashimoto et al. [5], have reported a case involving accidental pulmonary vein transection during segmentectomy, in which direct reanastomosis was performed. However, no prior report has described the use of a complete tubular autologous pericardial conduit to bridge and reconstruct a fully transected pulmonary vein when direct repair was not feasible.

Autologous pericardium, by virtue of its ready availability, excellent tissue biocompatibility and favorable biomechanical properties, has been extensively utilized in cardiovascular surgery for valve repair, vascular patch augmentation, and conduit reconstruction [7–9]. In thoracic surgery, autologous pericardium has also been employed for patch repair of the pulmonary artery and superior vena cava [10]. Nevertheless, its use as a complete tubular vascular substitute for bridging reconstruction of a fully transected pulmonary vein has not been previously reported. Herein, we present the case of a 76-year-old male patient with preoperative moderate-to-severe obstructive ventilatory dysfunction who underwent thoracoscopic resection of left lower lobe lung cancer, during which the left superior pulmonary vein was inadvertently transected completely by an endoscopic stapler. Following conversion to open thoracotomy, the pulmonary vein was successfully reconstructed using an autologous pericardial conduit. We discuss this case in the context of the existing literature, with the aim of providing a reference for the management of similar clinical scenarios.

Case presentation

Patient information and preoperative data

A 76-year-old man was admitted to a local hospital after a left lower lobe pulmonary lesion was detected on routine examination. He had a long history of smoking and emphysema. Pulmonary function testing demonstrated moderate-to-severe obstructive ventilatory dysfunction. Preoperative chest CT performed on January 15, 2026, revealed an irregular cystic lesion with a surrounding solid nodule in the anteromedial basal segment of the left lower lobe, measuring approximately 31 mm × 27 mm, highly suspicious for malignancy. The left superior pulmonary vein showed no anatomic abnormality on preoperative imaging. Cardiac, hepatic, and renal function tests were normal, and distant metastasis was excluded. After multidisciplinary discussion, thoracoscopic resection of the left lower lobe lung cancer was planned.

Operative course

On January 21, 2026, the patient underwent thoracoscopic resection of the left lower lobe at the local hospital. During stapler division of the hilar vessels, the left superior pulmonary vein was inadvertently completely transected. Intraoperative inspection showed progressive dark-red discoloration of the left upper lobe, indicating impaired venous drainage and evolving pulmonary congestion. Thoracic surgery specialists from our institution were urgently called in for emergency assistance. Systemic heparin (3000 IU intravenously) was administered immediately upon recognition of the vein transection. Thoracic surgery specialists from our institution were urgently consulted and dispatched to the local hospital for emergency assistance.

After comprehensive assessment, it was concluded that, given the patient’s moderate-to-severe obstructive ventilatory dysfunction, proceeding directly to left upper lobectomy would effectively result in a left pneumonectomy, with a very high likelihood of postoperative ventilator dependence. Conversely, leaving the congested left upper lobe untreated would result in severe hemoptysis and other life-threatening complications. Therefore, reconstruction of the left superior pulmonary vein to restore venous drainage was considered the most appropriate strategy. Concurrently, the planned left lower lobectomy was modified to an extended combined anatomical S7 + 8 segmentectomy, thereby preserving as much left lung parenchyma as possible while maintaining complete tumor resection and providing a functional reserve should the reconstructed venous conduit become stenotic or occluded in the future.

The incision was extended and the operation was converted to open thoracotomy. After opening the pericardium, exploration confirmed that the left superior pulmonary vein had been completely transected by the stapler. The proximal stump was extremely short, approximately 3–5 mm in length, and was located immediately adjacent to the left atrial orifice. Because direct end-to-end anastomosis was technically prohibitive and would have entailed excessive tension, reconstruction with an autologous pericardial conduit was performed. A longitudinal incision was made in the lateral pericardium, and an approximately 4 cm×4 cm autologous pericardial patch was harvested. The patch was fashioned into a tubular graft approximately 4.0 cm in length, matching the diameter of the superior pulmonary vein. Atraumatic vascular clamps were applied to both the proximal and distal stumps, the staple lines at the margins were excised, and the autologous pericardial conduit was anastomosed end-to-end to the proximal (atrial-side) and distal stumps of the transected left superior pulmonary vein using 5 − 0 Prolene sutures. The total ischemia time of the left upper lobe—from the moment of inadvertent transection to restoration of blood flow upon clamp release—was approximately 180 min. Upon release of the clamps, the conduit filled well with satisfactory blood flow assessed visually (direct observation of conduit distension and color change of the upper lobe from dusky red to pink; no intraoperative Doppler was available), and the left upper lobe showed good expansion, indicating restoration of venous return. Subsequently, an extended left lower lobe combined anatomical S7 + 8 segmentectomy was performed (the tumor was located in the anteromedial basal segment, and extended segmentectomy ensured a margin of more than 2 cm from the tumor edge), along with systematic lymph node dissection (Fig. 1).

Postoperative recovery and anticoagulation

Postoperative anticoagulation protocol: Low-molecular-weight heparin (enoxaparin 0.4 mL subcutaneously every 12 h) was initiated on postoperative day 1 and continued until discharge. The patient was then transitioned to oral anticoagulation with rivaroxaban 15 mg twice daily, planned for at least 3 months, with reassessment based on follow-up imaging. Because no established anticoagulation protocol exists for autologous pericardial pulmonary venous conduits, the anticoagulation regimen was selected empirically based on institutional experience with vascular reconstructions.

A plain chest CT on postoperative day 2 (January 23, 2026) demonstrated no obvious congestion in the remaining left lung. Contrast-enhanced chest CT on postoperative day 7 (January 28, 2026) showed that the autologous pericardial conduit itself was not clearly delineated on the enhanced images; however, areas of consolidation within the remaining left lung demonstrated marked homogeneous enhancement, indicating satisfactory pulmonary parenchymal perfusion and indirectly confirming patency of the reconstructed left superior pulmonary venous outflow. The patient recovered uneventfully, with no hemoptysis or signs of pulmonary venous congestion, and was discharged after chest tube removal.

Follow-up plain chest CT on April 30, 2026 (approximately 3 months postoperatively) showed significant absorption of bilateral pulmonary exudative lesions compared with the immediate postoperative CT. Importantly, no new consolidation, congestion, or hemorrhagic change was observed in the remnant left lung, and the patient remained free of hemoptysis or other symptoms of pulmonary venous congestion.

Discussion

Mechanisms and consequences of inadvertent pulmonary vein transection by endoscopic stapler during VATS

Vascular injury during thoracoscopic lobectomy represents one of the most urgent intraoperative complications. Compared with open thoracotomy, VATS has inherent limitations, including restricted exposure, limited instrument freedom, all of which contribute to an increased risk of vascular injury. Chen et al. [2] retrospectively analyzed 1064 VATS anatomic pulmonary resection cases and found that the incidence of intraoperative unexpected hemorrhage was 6.30%, with pulmonary artery injury being the most common (72%), followed by pulmonary vein injury (10%); suture repair was the most frequently employed hemostatic technique (64%). Tomoyasu et al. [4] reviewed 1,098 cases of thoracoscopic lung cancer resection and reported a pulmonary artery bleeding rate of 1.5%, half of which required emergency conversion to thoracotomy. In a systematic review and meta-analysis of 20 studies involving 72,932 VATS anatomic pulmonary resections, Power et al. [11] found a median conversion rate of 9.6%, with vascular injury or bleeding being the most common reason for conversion (27.9%). Notably, the vascular injuries described in the aforementioned literature predominantly involved partial vessel wall lacerations or hemorrhage; complete transection of a non-target pulmonary vein by an endoscopic stapler was exceedingly rare.

In the present case, the left superior pulmonary vein was inadvertently transected while dividing the left lower lobe vessels. Contributing factors likely included: the confined anatomic space of the left hilum, the close proximity of the upper and lower pulmonary veins, difficulty of anatomic identification in a two-dimensional thoracoscopic field, and possible tumor- or inflammation-related adhesions that may have obscured tissue planes. Surgeon experience is also a recognized risk factor; the literature suggests that vascular injury risk during VATS is closely related to surgical volume and the learning curve [2, 3].

Once venous drainage of the left upper lobe is completely obstructed, pulmonary congestion develops rapidly. Pulmonary capillary pressure rises sharply, resulting in alveolar hemorrhage and hemorrhagic pulmonary infarction. This case highlights that when irreversible devices such as staplers are used during VATS, meticulous anatomic identification is mandatory, and prompt conversion to thoracotomy should be undertaken whenever adequate exposure cannot be ensured [11].

Therapeutic decision-making: Why reconstruction was the preferred strategy

After complete transection of the left superior pulmonary vein, three potential management strategies may be considered:

reconstruct the transected vein;

reconstruct the transected vein;

perform an additional left upper lobectomy to remove the congested lung;

perform an additional left upper lobectomy to remove the congested lung;

leave the congested left upper lobe in situ without intervention.

leave the congested left upper lobe in situ without intervention.

The third option is clearly unacceptable, as the congested lung tissue would serve as a persistent source of postoperative hemoptysis, with potential secondary infection and empyema. The second option—additional left upper lobectomy—may represent a straightforward solution in certain circumstances. However, in the present case, preoperative pulmonary function testing indicated markedly diminished pulmonary reserve. Proceeding with the planned left lower lobectomy combined with additional left upper lobectomy would be equivalent to a left pneumonectomy. Meta-analytic evidence supports that parenchymal-sparing procedures, whenever technically feasible, are associated with lower operative mortality and better long-term outcomes than pneumonectomy [12–14]. For this patient, the combination of poor baseline pulmonary function and the physiologic burden of pneumonectomy would have made postoperative ventilator dependence highly likely. Therefore, reconstruction of the left superior pulmonary vein was considered the most appropriate strategy in this clinical context.

Additionally, the surgical team modified the planned left lower lobectomy to an extended combined anatomical S7 + 8 segmentectomy. The rationale was that the long-term patency of an autologous pericardial conduit remains uncertain. Should postoperative conduit stenosis or occlusion occur, the left upper lobe would once again be at risk of congestion. If the entire left lower lobe had already been removed, subsequent resection of the left upper lobe would be tantamount to a left pneumonectomy. Conversely, preserving segments S6, S9, and S10 meant that even if a secondary upper lobectomy became necessary, some left lower lobe function would remain. Thus, the decision to de-escalate from lobectomy to segmentectomy served as a functional safeguard reflecting a lung-preserving philosophy under conditions of compounded risk. From an oncologic perspective, the tumor was located in S7 + 8, and extended segmentectomy achieved complete resection with adequate margins while systematic lymph node dissection preserved oncologic rigor.

Novelty and technical considerations of autologous pericardial conduit reconstruction

To our knowledge, this is the first reported case in which autologous pericardium was fashioned into a complete tubular conduit to bridge and reconstruct a fully transected pulmonary vein. A literature search was performed in PubMed and Embase using the terms “pulmonary vein reconstruction”, “pulmonary vein injury”, “autologous pericardium”, and “pericardial conduit” to verify the novelty of this approach. Previous applications of autologous pericardium in thoracic vascular surgery have been limited to patch repair of partial defects in the pulmonary artery or superior vena cava [10, 15], or conduit reconstruction of the pulmonary artery [16]. The central novelty of the present approach lies in extending the role of autologous pericardium from patch repair to complete tubular venous substitution, thereby overcoming the critical challenge posed by the extremely short proximal stump that precluded all standard repair options.

When pulmonary vascular injury occurs, standard repair options include direct suture repair for small vascular wall injuries, side-clamp repair for partial tears, direct end-to-end anastomosis when the stumps are sufficiently long and tension-free, and patch reconstruction for partial defects [15]. In the present case, the completely transected vein with an extremely short proximal stump (approximately 3–5 mm) immediately adjacent to the left atrial orifice rendered all of these methods impracticable.

Several technical points merit emphasis. First, prompt conversion to thoracotomy was essential for adequate working space and visualization. Second, harvesting and preparation of the pericardium required selection of an area of appropriate thickness with minimal adherent fat to ensure uniform conduit construction. Third, appropriate sizing of the conduit was critical—the diameter needed to match that of the superior pulmonary vein to avoid stenosis from an excessively narrow conduit or turbulent flow and thrombus formation from an excessively wide conduit. Fourth, the proximal anastomosis within the pericardial cavity required precise continuous 5 − 0 Prolene suture to achieve a smooth, watertight anastomosis. Fifth, autologous pericardium, as native tissue, offers excellent biocompatibility, eliminates the risk of immunologic rejection, and may have theoretically favorable properties for endothelialization compared with synthetic grafts.

The total ischemia time of the left upper lobe was approximately 180 min, substantially exceeding the 30–60 min typically reported during sleeve resection. This was unavoidable given the need for emergency consultation, specialist team transit, and meticulous reconstruction. Despite this prolonged venous obstruction, the left upper lobe showed no irreversible ischemic injury or hemorrhagic infarction at 3-month follow-up. This tolerance may be attributed to preserved bronchial arterial inflow providing partial tissue oxygenation and to immediate systemic heparinization mitigating microvascular thrombosis within the congested lobe. Nevertheless, the safe upper limit of pulmonary venous ischemia duration remains poorly defined, and this single favorable outcome should not be generalized.

Current applications of autologous pericardium in vascular reconstruction

Autologous pericardium has a long history of use in thoracic surgery. Rendina and colleagues [15] systematically described techniques for pulmonary artery sleeve resection and reconstruction, including the feasibility of using autologous tissue for vascular repair. D’Andrilli et al. [10] reported the use of glutaraldehyde-preserved autologous pericardium for patch reconstruction of the pulmonary artery and superior vena cava, demonstrating the safety and efficacy of this material. Matsutani et al. [16] reported the use of a pericardial conduit for pulmonary artery reconstruction using surgical stapling, demonstrating the feasibility of tubular pericardial grafts in pulmonary vasculature. However, no prior report has described a completely tubular autologous pericardial conduit used to bridge a fully transected pulmonary vein. The successful outcome in the present case suggests that autologous pericardium can function not only as a patch but also as a tube graft for urgent bridging reconstruction in the low-pressure pulmonary venous system. Pulmonary venous circulation is a low-pressure system, which reduces the mechanical demands on the graft material and may make autologous pericardium a suitable option in this setting.

Postoperative assessment and follow-up

It is noteworthy that the autologous pericardial conduit itself could not be clearly visualized on contrast-enhanced CT. This may be attributable to several factors: the limited density difference between pericardial tissue and adjacent structures; the relatively small conduit caliber; its partial course within the pericardial cavity; and the fact that conventional contrast-enhanced CT acquisition was not specifically optimized for the pulmonary venous phase or thin-slice evaluation of such a small conduit. Nevertheless, the residual left lung parenchyma showed clear and homogeneous enhancement without imaging evidence of ongoing venous congestion, indirectly supporting adequate pulmonary venous drainage through the reconstructed pathway. Furthermore, the absence of hemoptysis or other symptoms of pulmonary venous congestion throughout the postoperative course supports the clinical success of the reconstruction. The 3-month follow-up plain chest CT (April 30, 2026) further corroborated these findings, demonstrating significant resolution of bilateral pulmonary exudative lesions with no evidence of recurrent congestion. In future follow-up, dedicated CT angiography with pulmonary venous phase imaging and thin-slice reconstruction is planned to provide more direct assessment of conduit morphology and patency.

Limitations and future perspectives

This report describes a single case and has several important limitations. First, although serial imaging up to approximately three months postoperatively showed no evidence of recurrent venous congestion, follow-up remains relatively short, and the mid- and long-term patency of the autologous pericardial conduit still requires continued imaging surveillance, including assessment for stenosis, thrombosis, or degenerative changes. The risk of late stenosis due to neointimal hyperplasia, thrombosis due to the non-endothelialized luminal surface, and unknown remodeling behavior of pericardial tissue in the venous position must all be acknowledged. Second, no direct intraoperative flow measurement (e.g., Doppler ultrasonography or transit-time flow measurement) was available to quantitatively confirm conduit patency; assessment relied on visual inspection of conduit distension and color change of the upper lobe. Third, the inability of routine postoperative contrast-enhanced CT to clearly delineate the conduit indicates that conventional CT has limitations in evaluating such small reconstructed channels; targeted CT angiography with venous phase optimization should be performed during follow-up. Fourth, because this was an emergency intraoperative salvage procedure, no preplanned comparison with alternative repair strategies was possible. Fifth, the success of this approach may in part be related to the low-pressure hemodynamic environment of the pulmonary venous system, and generalizability to other clinical scenarios remains uncertain. Finally, although postoperative anticoagulation was administered, the optimal anticoagulation or antiplatelet regimen for pericardial venous conduits has not been established, and further investigation is warranted.

Conclusion

This report describes a rare complication of inadvertent transection of the left superior pulmonary vein by an endoscopic stapler during thoracoscopic lobectomy and presents an innovative salvage strategy—bridging reconstruction using a tubular conduit fashioned from autologous pericardium. To our knowledge, this represents the first reported use of a complete tubular autologous pericardial conduit for pulmonary vein reconstruction. It should be emphasized that conduit patency was not directly confirmed radiologically but inferred from satisfactory pulmonary perfusion and the absence of clinical signs of venous obstruction; dedicated CT angiography with pulmonary venous-phase imaging during longer-term follow-up is therefore warranted. This case offers a novel salvage option for unexpected vascular injury during VATS and highlights the value of combining vascular reconstruction with de-escalation to segmentectomy in patients with limited pulmonary reserve who cannot tolerate more extensive resection.
