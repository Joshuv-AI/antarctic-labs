Bifenthrin exacerbates ulcerative colitis via immunotoxicity: network toxicology and experimental validation reveal novel therapeutic targets
Authors: Lingxiao Wang, Weixing Zhang, Yifan Kang, Yaoping Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Bifenthrin (BF) is a widely used pyrethroid insecticide and is recognized as an endocrine-disrupting chemical (EDC). Accumulating evidence indicates that long-term exposure to BF can induce a variety of adverse health outcomes. However, its potential role in the pathogenesis of ulcerative colitis (UC) remains elusive. In this study, we integrated data from multiple databases—including the Comparative Toxicogenomics Database (CTD), TargetNet, GeneCards, SwissTargetPrediction, and STITCH—to predict potential molecular targets of BF. UC-associated genes were compiled from GeneCards, Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man (OMIM), DisGeNET, Therapeutic Target Database (TTD), and DrugBank. Candidate targets were identified by intersecting predicted BF targets with UC-related genes, followed by functional enrichment analysis using DAVID. The STRING database and Cytoscape software were employed to construct protein-protein interaction (PPI) networks and screen for hub genes. A diagnostic model based on these hub genes was established and validated using the GSE47908 and GSE13367 datasets. Additionally, immune infiltration analysis was performed to compare the UC group with controls, identifying immune cell types significantly associated with the target genes. Molecular docking simulations via AutoDock Vina were conducted to evaluate the binding affinities between BF and the five hub proteins. Functional analysis revealed that the candidate genes were primarily enriched in pathways related to oxidative stress and inflammatory responses. Five core hub genes—BCL2, TP53, TNF, IL6, and PTGS2—were consistently identified across all analytical platforms. In vitro experiments demonstrated that BF exposure significantly exacerbated colonic inflammation by downregulating anti-inflammatory/apoptotic genes (BCL2, TP53) and upregulating pro-inflammatory markers (IL6, TNF, PTGS2). Collectively, our findings suggest that bifenthrin may promote colorectal inflammation by dysregulating key genes and modulation of the pro-inflammatory immune microenvironment, thus providing novel insights into the environmental etiology of UC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40360-026-01173-5.

Introduction

Ulcerative colitis (UC) is a chronic inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) characterized by diffuse, non-specific inflammation of the intestinal mucosa, whose pathogenesis involves complex crosstalk between immune and inflammatory pathways [1]. Its clinical manifestations—such as abdominal pain, diarrhea, and mucopurulent bloody stool—are persistent and recurrent due to the chronic nature and high relapse rate of the disease, rendering complete remission difficult to achieve [2, 3]. The risk of complications, including colorectal cancer, increases with disease duration and extent; patients with long-standing, extensive colonic involvement have a cumulative incidence of up to 3% [4]. Disease progression is driven by the multifactorial interplay of genetic predisposition, immune dysregulation, intestinal barrier dysfunction, environmental exposures, and psychosocial factors [5]. Current therapeutic strategies primarily aim to control inflammation, modulate immune responses, and relieve symptoms with agents including aminosalicylates, corticosteroids, and immunosuppressants [6]. However, these treatments are often limited by significant adverse effects and suboptimal response rates in a substantial proportion of patients, highlighting the urgent need to investigate the environmental etiologies of UC for the development of safer and more effective preventive and therapeutic strategies.

Among environmental risk contributors, the widespread use of synthetic pesticides has raised increasing public health concerns [7–9]. Pyrethroid insecticides, particularly bifenthrin (BF), are extensively used in agricultural and urban pest control due to their reportedly low mammalian toxicity [10, 11]. As a Type I pyrethroid, BF is widely used in crop protection and the control of vector-borne diseases [12]. Due to its low water solubility, low volatility, and strong adsorption to soil and sediment, BF displays significant environmental persistence and bioaccumulation potential [13]. Human exposure to BF occurs primarily through dietary intake, inhalation, and dermal contact [14]. Accumulating evidence has linked BF exposure to multiple toxicological effects in mammals, including neurotoxicity [15], immunotoxicity [16], reproductive toxicity [17, 18], hepatotoxicity [19], and potential nephrotoxicity [20]. Notably, BF has been demonstrated to induce oxidative stress, disrupt intracellular calcium homeostasis, and activate the MAPK signaling pathway, ultimately leading to apoptosis in bovine mammary epithelial cells [21]. These biological processes—oxidative stress, inflammation, and cellular dysfunction—are core mechanisms underlying in UC pathogenesis.

Despite the established immunomodulatory effects of environmental toxins and the pro-inflammatory properties of BF, the specific role of BF in triggering or exacerbating UC remains poorly defined. Conventional toxicological models are often insufficient to unraveling the multi-target, systemic effects of environmental chemicals. To fill this research gap, our study integrates network toxicology, bioinformatic analysis, and experimental validation to systematically elucidate the immunotoxicological mechanisms of BF in the context of colorectal inflammation. Among these approaches, network toxicology, as an efficient, systematic, and scalable computational toxicology paradigm, has successfully elucidated the complex mechanisms of environment- and drug-related diseases in multiple studies, including: clarifying the core targets and pathways of tetracycline-induced acute pancreatitis and renal injury [9, 22]; dissecting the neuroimmune regulatory axis underlying levofloxacin-induced cognitive impairment and epileptic seizures [23]; and identifying the molecular network through which potassium sorbate induces renal injury by disrupting intercellular communication in renal tubular epithelial cells [7]. This approach integrates target prediction, PPI network construction, hub gene screening, and functional enrichment and pathway mapping analyses, enabling the comprehensive characterization of the systemic toxic effects of environmental chemicals and the precise identification of potential therapeutic targets and early warning biomarkers. Our study advances this field by identifying BF as a specific environmental risk factor for UC, rather than just a broad-spectrum toxicant. By integrating network toxicology with experimental validation, we show that BF dysregulates a specific set of genes (BCL2, TP53, IL6, TNF, PTGS2) that regulate apoptosis and pro-inflammatory signaling—core processes in UC pathogenesis. This work provides a mechanism-based framework for associating pesticide exposure with UC onset, facilitating future research into environmental risk stratification and targeted prevention strategies.

Methods

Identification of BF-associated genes

To identify potential target genes of BF, we retrieved data from five comprehensive databases: the Comparative Toxicogenomics Database (CTD), TargetNet, GeneCards, SwissTargetPrediction, and STITCH. We first compiled the initial gene lists from these sources and then conducted data integration by eliminating duplicate entries, ultimately obtaining a refined, unified set of target genes. To ensure the reliability of gene and protein interaction data, only genes with a confidence score of at least 80 in the CTD were included, as such scores indicate direct or indirect associations with BF. A probability score of 0.7 or higher was applied in TargetNet and SwissTargetPrediction to prioritize potential human targets. In the GeneCards database, a relevance score of 20 or above was used as the threshold for screening genes associated with BF and UC. Additionally, protein-chemical interactions in the STITCH database were filtered by a combined score of 0.7 or greater. The combination of these five databases avoids the limitations of single-database prediction and ensures the comprehensiveness and accuracy of BF target gene identification.

Collection of UC targets

The search was conducted for the terms “ulcerative colitis” in GeneCards, OMIM, DisGeNET, TTD, and Drugbank [24, 25]. For OMIM, DisGeNET, TTD, and DrugBank, only genes directly implicated as causal factors or therapeutic targets in UC were selected, while those with indirect or putative associations were excluded. The acquired target data were merged, and duplicate targets were removed to obtain UC-related targets.

Approach to identifying hub genes

UC-related genes were compared with BF-associated genes to identify common candidate genes. The identified overlapping genes were imported into the STRING database to build a protein-protein interaction (PPI) network. Then, hub genes were screened using three algorithms from the CytoHubba plugin: Maximal Clique Centrality (MCC), Maximum Neighborhood Component (MNC), and Degree. MCC prioritizes genes in the core clique of the PPI network and has high accuracy for identifying disease-related hub genes; MNC focuses on the number of adjacent nodes and is suitable for screening genes with broad regulatory effects in inflammatory pathways; Degree is the most classic algorithm for identifying hub genes with the highest connectivity in the PPI network.

Functional enrichment analysis

The overlapping genes were analyzed for Gene Ontology (GO) terms and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways using the DAVID database. Results were visualized using the ggplot2 package in R. GO enrichment analysis was performed across the three main categories: biological process (BP), cellular component (CC), and molecular function (MF). Significant terms were defined by an adjusted P-value < 0.05 and a minimum of five genes per term; the top five terms from each category were selected based on significance levels. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was conducted with a threshold of adjusted P-value < 0.05 and at least three genes per pathway. The top five pathways, ranked by the lowest adjusted P-value, were selected for visualization.

Validation of the overlapping genes

All datasets utilized in this study, including bulk RNA-seq data, were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database. We validated the candidate targets using the GSE47908 and GSE13367 datasets. Differential expression analysis was conducted on the both datasets with the following thresholds: |log2FoldChange| > 1 and adjusted P-value < 0.05. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were generated to validate the diagnostic value of the target genes in the validation set.

Immune infiltration and correlation analysis

We employed the CIBERSORT algorithm, an in silico computational approach, to quantify the relative proportions of 22 immune cell subtypes in control and UC samples. The Wilcoxon test and Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression analysis were used to screen for immune cells with significant infiltration differences between the two groups (P-value < 0.05), identifying 9 and 5 cell types, respectively. The intersection of these two sets retained 4 immune cells types as the final significantly dysregulated infiltrating cells. The Wilcoxon test was implemented via wilcox.test in R, and LASSO regression was performed with the glmnet package. The corrplot, vioplot, ggplot2, and glmnet packages were used for result visualization. Additionally, Spearman correlation analysis was conducted to investigate the relationships between the five hub genes and immune cell infiltration profiles.

Construction and evaluation of the predictive model

A logistic regression model was constructed in the validation set based on four selected hub genes using the glm function in R, and a nomogram was generated to estimate UC risk. Model performance was assessed through ROC curves, decision curve analysis (DCA), and calibration curves. Additionally, five gene combinations were generated to construct additional logistic regression models; area under the curve (AUC) values were calculated using the glm and pROC packages in R, with all models validated in the same validation set.

Molecular docking

The three-dimensional structure of BF was retrieved from PubChem (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). Relevant protein structures were obtained from the RCSB PDB database and processed with PyMOL, with solvent molecules and non-essential components removed. For docking preparation, hydrogen atoms were added to proteins, and the ligand’s torsional angles were optimized using AutoDock Tools. Protein-ligand interactions were assessed with AutoDock Vina 1.5.7 [23], and binding modes were evaluated based on docking scores. Molecular interactions were further analyzed and visualized using PyMOL.

Cell culture

The Caco-2 and THP-1 cell lines were purchased from Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). Caco-2 cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. THP-1 cells were maintained in RPMI-1640 medium containing 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. All cell cultures were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO₂.

Cell proliferation

Caco-2 cells were seeded at a density of 2.0 × 10⁵ cells per well in Transwell chambers. Cell differentiation and monolayer integrity were monitored by measuring transepithelial electrical resistance (TEER). Only chambers with a stable TEER value of ≥ 300 Ω·cm² were used for subsequent experiments. Simultaneously, THP-1 cells were seeded at the same density in 12-well plates and treated with 100 ng/mL phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA) for 48 h to induce differentiation into adherent macrophage-like cells. After induction, all adherent cells exhibited typical macrophage morphology (pseudopodia extension, increased cell volume, firm adhesion) under a light microscope. Non-adherent suspended cells were completely removed during medium replacement. The two cell systems were then co-cultured and incubated at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO₂ atmosphere for 24 h. Subsequently, cells were assigned to six experimental groups: control group, LPS group (1 µg/mL LPS), and four BF treatment groups (1 µM, 10 µM, 50 µM, 100 µM, and 200 µM). Following treatment, 10 µL of Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) solution (Shanghai Beitan Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) was added to each well, and plates were incubated for an additional 2 h. Optical density (OD) was then measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader to assess cell viability. The concentrations used in this in vitro study were selected to elicit clear biological responses within a limited timeframe—a standard approach in mechanistic exploratory research. Nevertheless, the identified molecular pathways may remain relevant under conditions of low-concentration and prolonged BF exposure.

Real-time fluorescent quantitative PCR (qRT-PCR)

Real-time fluorescent quantitative PCR (qRT-PCR) analysis was conducted using 2X M5 HiPer SYBR qPCR Premix. Reactions were run on an Mx3005P real-time PCR system. Relative gene expression levels were normalized to glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH), and relative fold changes were calculated using the ΔCt method. Primer sequences are listed in Supplemental Table 1.

Cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA)

RIPA buffer was added to pretreated cells to induce lysis. Samples were first normalized based on total protein concentration, measured using a protein assay kit (Beyotime, Jiangsu, China). A PCR system (Eppendorf, Germany) was then set up, and samples were subjected to denaturation at gradient temperatures for 8 min. Finally, the interaction between BF and the target protein was assessed via Western blot analysis.

Determination of oxidative stress indicators

Cells were collected as pellets by centrifugation and gently washed twice with pre-cooled PBS. Total superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity, reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels, and glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px) activity were measured using the Beyotime Total SOD Activity Assay Kit, ROS Assay Kit, and GSH-Px Ratio Assay Kit, respectively. All detection results were normalized to total protein concentration (determined via the BCA method) to ensure data comparability and quantitative accuracy.

Western blot analysis

Cell lysates were collected using RIPA buffer with 0.1% PMSF (BOSTER Biological Technology; Wuhan, China). Protein samples were separated by SDS-PAGE on 10% gradient gel and transferred onto 0.45 μm PVDF membranes. The expression of target protein was normalized to GAPDH protein(loading control). Primary antibodies used were respectively BCL2, TP53, IL6, TNF and PTGS2(Proteintech Biological Technology; Wuhan, China). The antibodies used in this study were listed in Supplemental Table 2.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses and visualizations were performed in R software (version 4.2.2). Differential expression analysis was conducted using the limma package. The Wilcoxon rank-sum test was applied to compare immune cell infiltration between groups. Data visualization was performed with the ggplot2 package. Results are presented as the means ± standard error of the mean (SEM), and multiple group comparisons were analyzed using one-way ANOVA followed by the Student-Newman-Keuls test. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Identification of hub genes and enrichment analysis, identification of hub genes

Integrated analysis of five databases identified 263 BF-related targets. A total of 1143 UC-related differentially expressed genes were retrieved from GeneCards, OMIM, DisGeNET, the Therapeutic Target Database, and DrugBank. Intersection of BF-associated targets and UC-related genes yielded 93 overlapping genes (Fig. 1A). GO enrichment analysis revealed that these 93 genes were primarily enriched in biological processes including response to oxidative stress, inflammatory response and metal ion binding (Fig. 1B). While KEGG pathway analysis indicated enrichment in pathways such as PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, MAPK signaling pathway as well as TNF signaling pathway and apoptosis (Fig. 1C). The 93 candidate genes were analyzed using the STRING database to construct a PPI net work. Three different network metrics to rank the importance of 93 candidate genes, including MCC, MNC and Degree. The top ten genes identified by each metric were selected (Fig. 1D-F), and an intersection analysis was performed. As a result, six common hub genes were identified: BCL2, TP53, TNF, IL6, AKT1 and PTGS2.

Verification of gene expression and immune infiltration ssGSEA analysis

In the validation set, BCL2, TP53, TNF, IL6 and PTGS2 showed significant differential expression between UC and control groups (P-value < 0.05), but no significant difference in AKT1 expression was observed (Fig. 2A). ROC curve analysis demonstrated the diagnostic value of each hub gene: BCL2 (AUC = 0.677), TP53 (AUC = 0.723), IL6 (AUC = 0.671), TNF (AUC = 0.653), PTGS2 (AUC = 0.655). In contrast, AKT1 had low diagnostic value (Fig. 2B). To ensure analytical rigor and consistency throughout the study, AKT1 was excluded from the final hub gene list. This decision was based on its lack of differential expression and poor diagnostic performance in our validation datasets. Consequently, the subsequent predictive model was constructed using the remaining five hub genes. Subsequently, the correlation with 22 immune cells in each sample is given in the form of a heat map (Fig. 2C). Two different algorithms, Wilcoxon test and LASSO regression, were used to distinguish significant differences in immune cell infiltration in Control and UC. The results of the Wilcoxon test were shown in the violin plot (Fig. 2D), which presented 9 immune cells with P-value < 0.05; the LASSO regression results (Figs. 2E, F) showed that 5 immune cells were included. The 4 immune cells common to both methods were extracted, namely Eosinophils, iDC, Macrophages and Neutrophil.

The correlation between immune cell infiltration analysis and five hub genes

Spearman correlation analysis was performed to investigate the relationship between the five hub genes and the four significantly dysregulated immune cells, revealing consistent association patterns in UC patients (Fig. 3A). Specifically, TNF showed negative correlations with Eosinophils but a positive correlation with iDC (P < 0.001, Fig. 3B, D). IL6 was negatively correlated with Eosinophils and positively correlated with Neutrophil (P < 0.001, Fig. 3C, F). PTGS2 was positively correlated with Neutrophil (P < 0.001, Fig. 3E).

Molecular docking of BF with five proteins

Molecular docking simulations were performed to assess the binding affinity between BF and the five hub proteins (BCL2, TP53, TNF, IL6 and PTGS2). A binding energy of < − 7 kcal/mol was considered strong affinity, − 7 to − 4 kcal/mol moderate, and > − 4 kcal/mol weak or negligible binding [26]. The results indicated that BF exhibited strong to moderate binding affinity with all five targets: BCL2 (− 8.9 kcal/mol), TP53(− 8.9 kcal/mol), TNF (− 6.2 kcal/mol), IL6 (− 8.5 kcal/mol), and PTGS2 (− 5.3 kcal/mol). Bifenthrin regulates the expression of UC targets

A CCK-8 assay was first performed to evaluate the cytotoxicity of BF in the Caco-2/THP-1 co-culture system. The results showed that the concentration of bifenthrin below 10 µM was safe for cells, while there was significant cytotoxicity at concentrations above 50 µM, and the toxicity became increasingly apparent as the concentration increased (Fig. 6A). Based on this, 50 µM and 100 µM BF were selected as non-cytotoxic concentrations for subsequent molecular experiments. RT-qPCR and Western blot analyses were used to detect the mRNA and protein expression of the five hub genes in the co-culture system (Figs. 6B-G). BCL2 and TP53 mRNA and protein levels were downregulated with increasing BF concentration, while IL6, TNF, and PTGS2 were upregulated in a concentration-dependent manner. In contrast to a previous study by Wang et al. [22], we observed TP53 downregulation; this discrepancy may be attributed to differences in the cell model used and requires further validation. CETSA confirmed direct binding between BF and the five hub proteins: BF treatment increased the thermal stability of endogenous hub proteins in Caco-2 cells (Fig. 6H), indicating specific protein-ligand interactions. Additionally, BF treatment inhibited SOD and GSH-Px activity, increased intracellular ROS accumulation, and exacerbated oxidative stress in the co-culture system (Figs. 6I-K).

Discussion

This study systematically investigated the immunotoxicological mechanisms by which BF exacerbates UC using an integrated approach of network toxicology and in vitro experimentation. Functional enrichment analysis revealed that the shared target genes between BF and UC were significantly enriched in pathways associated with oxidative stress and inflammatory response—findings consistent with previous studies [27–29] and further confirming these processes as core mechanisms of UC pathogenesis. By combining gene expression analysis in validation cohorts, immune infiltration assessment, and molecular docking simulations, we identified five core hub genes critically involved in BF-induced UC development. Notably, BF exposure downregulated BCL2 and TP53 and upregulated pro-inflammatory genes (TNF, IL6, PTGS2), suggesting BF exacerbates UC by disrupting the balance between apoptotic signaling and pro-inflammatory responses.

GO and KEGG pathway analyses indicated that BF-associated targets are primarily involved in oxidative stress and inflammation-related pathways, aligning with previous reports that BF induces organ injury via oxidative stress and inflammatory cascades [30, 31]. El Safadi et al. [30] reported BF-induced liver injury through dysregulation of the Nrf2/Keap1 and NF-κB pathways, and Piao et al. [31] demonstrated BF triggers developmental inflammation in zebrafish embryos. Our in vitro results extend these findings by showing BF promotes intestinal ROS accumulation and activates oxidative stress in a colonic co-culture system, which is a key initiator of UC-associated intestinal mucosal damage. Collectively, these data support the hypothesis that BF impairs intestinal homeostasis by inducing chronic oxidative stress and inflammation.

UC is characterized by dysregulation of both innate and adaptive immune responses [32–34], and this study is the first to link BF exposure to immune dysregulation in UC. Prior studies have shown that functional alterations in colonic CD4+ T cells and regulatory T cells drive immune imbalance in UC [35], and our results suggest BF may compromise colonic mucosal barrier integrity through multiple pathways, thereby promoting abnormal immune cell infiltration and activation. Immune infiltration analysis revealed significant downregulation of eosinophils and iDC and upregulation of macrophages and neutrophils in UC which changes consistent with abnormal BCL2 and TP53 expression. BCL2 and TP53 dysregulation disrupts intestinal epithelial cell apoptosis, leading to barrier dysfunction; this creates a structural basis for immune cell infiltration, induces iDC polarization to mature DCs [36], and further promotes macrophage and neutrophil recruitment. Barrier damage releases danger-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) that activate lamina propria macrophages [33], triggering the NF-κB pathway and upregulating TNF and IL6—forming a positive feedback loop of barrier damage, immune infiltration and inflammatory amplification that exacerbates UC [37, 38].

Integrated analysis revealed significant correlations between TNF/IL6 expression and eosinophil/neutrophil infiltration, suggesting these genes and immune cells may serve as potential biomarkers and therapeutic targets for UC. This finding is supported by Tsai et al. [39], who showed elevated IL6 and TNFα expression is associated with increased neutrophil infiltration and severe DSS-induced colitis. Abnormal hub gene expression drives the recruitment and activation of inflammatory immune cells, and these activated cells further upregulate pro-inflammatory gene expression via paracrine signaling which forming a pro-inflammatory feedback loop that perpetuates intestinal inflammation in UC.

Molecular docking analysis demonstrated strong binding affinity between BF and BCL2, TP53, and IL6, and in vitro experiments validated these interactions: BF exposure dysregulated hub gene expression in a concentration-dependent manner, exacerbated oxidative stress, and promoted pro-inflammatory responses. These results are consistent with previous studies showing BF induces immunotoxicity via oxidative stress and inflammatory pathways [20, 40–42]. Wang et al. [20] reported BF exacerbates LPS-induced inflammation in murine macrophages, and Gargouri et al. [40] demonstrated BF triggers neuroinflammation through NF-κB activation. Both of which align with our findings of BF-induced colonic inflammation via pro-inflammatory gene upregulation.

This study has several limitations that warrant consideration. First, it lacks in vivo validation; the in vitro BF concentrations used are higher than human environmental exposure levels, a common limitation of mechanistic in vitro research. However, the molecular pathways identified herein provide a critical mechanistic hypothesis for subsequent in vivo studies and human-relevant organoid model validation. Second, the study did not investigate the effects of BF isomers, which may have different toxicological effects [20]. Future research will include animal models of UC with environmentally relevant BF exposure, patient cohorts with varying BF exposure levels, isomer-specific toxicity assays, and human intestinal organoid models to recapitulate the epithelial-immune microenvironment and validate BF-driven inflammatory mechanisms in a physiologically authentic context [43]. Additionally, further studies will explore the potential therapeutic targets identified in this study to develop strategies for preventing BF-associated UC.

Notably, this study moves beyond general pesticide toxicity research by identifying BF as a specific environmental risk factor for UC via a distinct molecular signature (BCL2/TP53 downregulation and TNF/IL6/PTGS2 upregulation). This signature mirrors the immune-apoptotic imbalance characteristic of UC, establishing a direct link between pesticide exposure and UC pathogenesis. This work bridges environmental toxicology and clinical gastroenterology, providing novel insights into the environmental etiology of UC and identifying potential targets for environmental risk stratification and targeted intervention.

Conclusion

This research combines network toxicology and in vitro experiments to elucidate the mechanisms by which BF exacerbates intestinal inflammation, providing cellular-level insights for comprehensive safety assessment. Through comprehensive analysis, this approach not only improves the accuracy of toxicity prediction but also deepens our understanding of the pathways through which BF exerts its toxic effects and its possible environmental impacts. The results provide new perspectives on the immunotoxicological effects of BF and identify potential molecular targets that could aid in the future development of strategies for preventing and treating BF-induced ulcerative colitis.
