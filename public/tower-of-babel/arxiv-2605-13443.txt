Imaging-formulation-based numerical speckle reduction for optical coherence tomography Journal: opticajournal Publication type: Research Article
Xibo Wang, 1 Shuichi Makita, 1 Nobuhisa Tateno, 1 Suzuyo Komeda, 1 Cunyou Bao, 1 Atsuko Furukawa, 2 Satoshi Matsusaka, 2 Makoto Kobayashi, 3 and Yoshiaki Yasuno 1,*

Abstract
Speckle is an intrinsic pattern in optical coherence tomography (OCT) that obscures fine image features and degrades effective resolution. In this study, we propose a numerical speckle reduction method based on the dispersed scatterer model and the imaging formulation of OCT. Utilizing the shifted-complex-conjugate-product, the proposed method digitally modulates speckle patterns by shifting the complex en face OCT signal and averaging the resulting real-part images. This approach allows for effective speckle suppression using a single volumetric acquisition without additional hardware modifications. OCT point spread function phantom measurement demonstrated lateral resolution preservation of the proposed method. We validated the method using a custom-built full-field swept-source OCT system on human breast adenocarcinoma spheroids and a zebrafish eye. Quantitative evaluations using the contrast-to-noise ratio and equivalent number of looks demonstrated that the proposed method significantly outperforms conventional frame-averaging techniques. The speckle-reduced images revealed microstructures previously obscured by speckle, such as necrotic regions in spheroids, while preserving the original image sharpness and resolution.



1
Introduction

Recent improvements in cell culture techniques have enabled three-dimensional (3D) cell culture models such as organoids and spheroids
[
1
,
2
]
, which have been widely adopted in biomedical and life science research.
Optical coherence tomography (OCT)
[
3
]
is expected to be suitable to non-invasively investigate such thick 3D cell cultures.
However, the lateral resolution of OCT is conventionally configured to be relatively low, such as around 5 to ten few micrometers, due to the due to the trade-off between lateral resolution and depth of focus (DOF).

Full-field swept-source OCT (FF-SSOCT)
[
4
,
5
,
6
]
is a variant of OCT that uses a wavelength-sweeping light source and an area camera.
Since FF-SSOCT does not have confocal pinhole, it is free from confocal signal decay
[
7
]
.
In addition, computational (i.e., holographic) refocusing enables diffraction limit resolution over the depth range much wider than the DOF
[
6
]
.
The absence of confocal pinhole and the computational refocusing resolve the resolution-DOF trade-off, and hence, FF-SSOCT can achieve micrometer-scale lateral resolution while maintaining millimeter-scale imaging depth
[
7
,
6
]
.

FF-SSOCT enables fast volumetric imaging with high lateral resolution, and hence it well-suites for 3D imaging of thick
in vitro
samples, such as tumor spheroids.
However, the coherent nature of OCT inevitably causes the speckle
[
8
,
9
]
.
Speckle is a granular pattern that obscures fine sample structure and hence reduces the effective resolution.
Therefore, effective speckle reduction is essential for microscopic OCT imaging.

There are several speckle reduction methods, but each of them has its own limitations.
For example, the multi-frame averaging method
[
10
,
11
,
12
]
is a widely used and very effective.
However, it requires a large number of OCT images acquired at the same position, and hence, it requires a long acquisition time.
The angle-compounding method
[
13
,
14
]
is based on averaging of multiple images acquired from several directions.
Although it works well, it requires a major hardware modification.
The wavelength-compounding method
[
15
,
16
]
is based on the averaging of OCT images acquired at different wavelength bands.
Typically, the multi-band OCT images were generated by windowed Fourier transform of a spectral interference signal, and hence it causes the reduction of axial resolution.
The polarization diversity method
[
17
]
, which is based on averaging of OCT images obtained with different polarization states, is free from this resolution reduction.
However, there are only two independent states of polarization in principle, it causes the only two fully de-correlated speckle patterns at most, and it limits the maximum efficacy of speckle reduction.

While the above-mentioned methods are based on modified acquisition protocol or hard-ware extensions (so we call it as “hardware-based methods”), there are some methods which solely based on image processing (i.e, software-based methods).
Although the software-based methods are successful, there are still some limitations.
For example, digital filter-based methods
[
18
,
19
,
20
]
can be suffered from resolution degradation, expensive computation, or structural distortion.
The non-local-mean method effectively suppresses speckle as keeping the perceptually important image features, but is computationally expensive
[
21
,
22
]
.
Neural network-based methods
[
23
,
24
,
25
,
26
,
27
]
require large datasets for training.
In addition, both of the hardware- and software-based methods were designed without a clear analytical model (i.e., the mathematical definition) of speckle.

Recently, Tomita
et al.
proposed an analytic signal-formation model of OCT
[
28
]
.
In this formulation, an OCT image is represented as the summation of two mathematically defined components: an “incoherent OCT image” and “speckle.”
The incoherent OCT image is expressed as a convolution between a real-and-positive point spread function (PSF) and the sample structure, and is an ideal speckle-free image.
In contrast, the speckle arises from the interactions among scattered light from different scatterers and is described as an integral term in this formulation.
Although this speckle term is complicated, it provides a clear mathematical definition of OCT speckle and offers a theoretical basis for manipulating speckle independently from the incoherent image.

In this paper, we exploit this formulation and derive a fully numerical speckle reduction method that does not require a specific hardware extension.
This method is based on a model-based numerical complex-signal processing which can modulate the speckle patterns after image acquisition while keeps the incoherence image component unchanged.
We generate multiple OCT images with different speckle realizations, and these images are averaged to suppress the speckle.
The imaging model suggests that this operation does not sacrifice the OCT resolution.
Since this method uses only one OCT image, its image-acquisition time is short.
In addition, since this method uses only simple and definitive numerical computation, it requires only a short computation time.
We implement this method on FF-SSOCT.
Since FF-SSOCT does not require mechanical lateral scanning, it has high transversal phase stability and is suitable for our complex-signal processing-based speckle reduction.
The resolution preservation nature of this method is experimentally demonstrated by measuring an established scatterer phantom.
This method is also applied to biological samples including tumor-spheroid and zebrafish OCT, and its performance is demonstrated subjectively by image observation and also objectively using quantitative image-based metrics.

Theory

2.1
Image-processing flow

Fig.
1
illustrates the overall processing flow of the proposed speckle reduction method.
Prior to the speckle reduction, we first correct the defocus of the complex OCT by computational refocusing [Step-(i) of Fig.
1
].
The details of this computational refocusing method can be found elsewhere
[
6
]
.

Figure 1
:
Schematic diagram of the proposed speckle reduction processing.
The complex OCT is refocused (i), duplicated and mutually-shifted (ii).
One of the duplicated images is complex-conjugated and multiplied with the other image (iii) to yield a “shifted-complex conjugate (SCCP)” image.
Subsequently, the real part of SCCP (real SCCP) is computed (iv).
This process is iterated for several shift directions and amounts (v), and the resulting real SCCP images are averaged (vi) to obtained the speckle-reduced image.

After computational refocusing, an
en face
complex OCT image is extracted from the volume.
This image is duplicated, and they are mutually digitally shifted (Step-(ii) in the figure).
The product of one of the images and the complex conjugate of the other image is computed as depicted as Step-(iii) in the figure.
Here we denote this product as “shifted-complex-conjugate product (SCCP).”
Subsequently, the real part of the SCCP (denoted as “real SCCP”) is computed (Step-(iv)).
As to be shown in the next section (Section
2.2
), the real SCCP consists two components; an incoherence image of the sample and speckle-related components.
For simplicity, we denote this speckle-related component as speckle.

This process is iterated for several shift directions and amounts (Step-(v)).
It results in multiple real SCCP images with the same incoherent image component but with different speckle realizations (see the next section for theoretical details).
These real SCCP images are finally averaged (Step-(vi)), and the speckle-reduced image is obtained.

For the image observation, the speckle-reduced image is displayed in a logarithmic (dB) scale after taking its absolute value.
It is noteworthy that both the standard OCT intensity and the real SCCP are the product of a complex OCT signal and a complex-conjugate OCT signal.
And hence they have the same signal dimension, and it makes the dB-scale display reasonable.

2.2
Theory

2.2.1
Model of complex OCT signal

The theory of our speckle reduction method starts from the OCT image formation model presented by Tomita
et al.
[
28
]
.

In this model, the sample to be measured is represented as the spatially slowly varying refractive index distribution in which scatterers are spatially randomly dispersed.
With this sample representation, the complex OCT signal at a particular depth in the OCT volume is expressed as follows

s(\bm{x}^{\prime})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}B(\bm{x}_{i})e^{i\phi_{i}}P_{c}(\bm{x}^{\prime}-\bm{x}_{i}),

(1)

where
\bm{x}^{\prime}=(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
is the two-dimensional lateral position in the image, and
\bm{x}_{i}=(x_{i},y_{i})
is the lateral position of the
i
-th scatterer in the sample.
Here the magnification between the sample and the image is assumed to be 1 without losing the generality.

N
is the number of all scatterers in the sample,
B(\bm{x}_{i})
is the scattering potential distribution of the sample, and
P_{c}
is the complex PSF of the OCT.

\phi_{i}
is the phase offset caused by the depth position of the
i
-th scatterer, and it is practically unpredictable and random.
In total, the complex OCT signal is the weighted summation of scattered light from each scatterer, with the PSF serving as the weighting function.
Note that our method is based on
en face
image manipulation, and hence, here we focus on the
en face
formulation.

The original Tomita’s formulation is for point-scanning OCT, but the complex PSF of FF-SSOCT is not identical to that of point-scanning OCT.
For point-scanning OCT the PSF is defined by both the illumination and collection optics.
On the other hand, the PSF of SS-FFOCT is governed only by the collection optics because the sample is illuminated by a flood plane wave (namely, the pupil function of the illumination is a delta function).
As shown in Refs.
[
7
,
6
]
in details, the lateral PSF of FF-SSOCT is

P_{c}(\bm{x},z;z_{0})=\frac{w_{0}}{w(z;z_{0})}\exp\left[{-\frac{\bm{x}^{2}}{w^{2}(z;z_{0})}}\right]\exp\left[-i\left\{nk_{0}(z-z_{0})+\frac{nk_{0}\bm{x}^{2}}{2R(z;z_{0})}-\psi(z;z_{0})\right\}\right],

(2)

where
z_{0}
is the depth position of the focus,

w(z;z_{0})
is the radius of the probe beam at depth
z
,

w_{0}=w(z=z_{0};z_{0})
is the collection beam radius at the in-focus depth (i.e., a virtual beam spot on a sample by assuming a point source is at a camera plane),
and
n
is the refractive index of the sample around the position of interest.

k_{0}
is the center wavenumber of the light source.
The second term in the second exponential represents the quadratic phase induced by defocus, and
R(z;z_{0})
is the phase curvature induced by the defocus.

\bm{x}^{2}
represents
\bm{x}\cdot\bm{x}
.
Similarly, hereafter, the squared vector represents the inner product of a vector with itself.
In the third term of the second exponential,
\psi(z;z_{0})
represents the Gouy phase.
The the defocus-induced phase curvature
R
is given by

R(z;z_{0})={\left({z-z_{0}}\right)}{\left[{1+{\left\{{\frac{n^{2}\,k_{0}\,w_{0}^{2}}{2(z-z_{0})}}\right\}}^{2}}\right]}.

(3)

By specifying the depth position
z
, Eq. (
2
) can be substituted into Eq. (
1
).

2.2.2
Shifted-complex-conjugate product (SCCP) and real SCCP

By following the same mathematical flow with Ref.
[
28
]
but with the complex PSF of Eq. (
2
), the analytic representation of SCCP of SS-FFOCT is derived as

\begin{split}&\mathrm{SCCP}=s\!\left(\bm{x}^{\prime}+\frac{\Delta\bm{x}}{2}\right)s^{*}\!\left(\bm{x}^{\prime}-\frac{\Delta\bm{x}}{2}\right)={}\,{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\frac{w_{0}^{2}}{w^{2}}\exp\!\left(-\frac{\Delta\bm{x}^{2}}{2w^{2}}\right)\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}D(\bm{x})\,B^{2}(\bm{x})}\\
&\qquad\qquad{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\times\exp\!\left[-\frac{2}{w^{2}}(\bm{x}^{\prime}-\bm{x})^{2}\right]\exp\!i\!\left[-\frac{nk_{0}}{R}\,\Delta\bm{x}\cdot(\bm{x}^{\prime}-\bm{x})\right]\,\bm{\mathrm{d}}\bm{x}}\\
&\quad\qquad+\,{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\frac{w_{0}^{2}}{w^{2}}\exp\!\left(-\frac{\Delta\bm{x}^{2}}{2w^{2}}\right)\iint_{-\infty}^{\infty}D^{\prime}(\bm{x}_{p},\bm{x}_{q})\,B(\bm{x}_{p})B(\bm{x}_{q})}\\
&\qquad\qquad{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\times\exp\!\left[-\frac{1}{w^{2}}(\bm{x}_{p}-\bm{x}_{q})\!\cdot\!\left(\frac{\bm{x}_{p}-\bm{x}_{q}}{2}-\Delta\bm{x}\right)\right]e^{i\phi_{pq}}\exp\!\left[-\frac{2}{w^{2}}\!\left(\bm{x}^{\prime}-\frac{\bm{x}_{p}+\bm{x}_{q}}{2}\right)^{\!2}\right]}\\
&\qquad\qquad{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\times\exp\!i\!\left[\frac{nk_{0}}{R}(\bm{x}_{p}-\bm{x}_{q}-\Delta\bm{x})\!\cdot\!\left(\bm{x}^{\prime}-\frac{\bm{x}_{p}+\bm{x}_{q}}{2}\right)\right]\,\bm{\mathrm{d}}\bm{x}_{p}\,\bm{\mathrm{d}}\bm{x}_{q}}\\
&\quad\qquad-\ \text{the first integral of R.H.S.,}\end{split}

(4)

where R.H.S. means “the right-hand side.”
Here
D
is the scatterer density distribution, and
D^{\prime}
is a cross-density of the scatterers that is a spatial function closely related to
D
.

\phi_{pq}
is a practically random phase due to the depth positions of scatterers
p
and
q
.
See Ref.
[
28
]
for more details about these quantities.

\Delta\bm{x}
is the lateral two dimensional shift digitally introduced after the data acquisition (Step-(ii) in Fig.
1
).
Notably, when the shift is zero, the SCCP becomes identical to the conventional OCT intensity image.
Here the blue part of the equation correspond to the incoherent image component, while the red part correspond to speckle.

The real SCCP is obtained by taking the real part of this SCCP as

\begin{split}&\mathrm{Re}\left[s\!\left(\bm{x}^{\prime}+\frac{\Delta\bm{x}}{2}\right)s^{*}\!\left(\bm{x}^{\prime}-\frac{\Delta\bm{x}}{2}\right)\right]={}\,{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\frac{w_{0}^{2}}{w^{2}}\exp\!\left(-\frac{\Delta\bm{x}^{2}}{2w^{2}}\right)\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}D(\bm{x})\,B^{2}(\bm{x})}\\
&\quad\qquad{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\times\exp\!\left[-\frac{2}{w^{2}}(\bm{x}^{\prime}-\bm{x})^{2}\right]\cos\!\left[-\frac{nk_{0}}{R}\Delta\bm{x}\cdot(\bm{x}^{\prime}-\bm{x})\right]\,\bm{\mathrm{d}}\bm{x}}\\
&\qquad+\,{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\frac{w_{0}^{2}}{w^{2}}\exp\!\left(-\frac{\Delta\bm{x}^{2}}{2w^{2}}\right)\iint_{-\infty}^{\infty}D^{\prime}(\bm{x}_{p},\bm{x}_{q})\,B(\bm{x}_{p})B(\bm{x}_{q})}\\
&\qquad\quad{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\times\exp\!\left[-\frac{1}{w^{2}}(\bm{x}_{p}-\bm{x}_{q})\!\cdot\!\left(\frac{\bm{x}_{p}-\bm{x}_{q}}{2}-\Delta\bm{x}\right)\right]\exp\!\left[-\frac{2}{w^{2}}\!\left(\bm{x}^{\prime}-\frac{\bm{x}_{p}+\bm{x}_{q}}{2}\right)^{\!2}\right]}\\
&\qquad\quad{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\times\cos\!\left[\phi_{pq}+\frac{nk_{0}}{R}\,(\bm{x}_{p}-\bm{x}_{q}-\Delta\bm{x})\!\cdot\!\left(x^{\prime}-\frac{\bm{x}_{p}+\bm{x}_{q}}{2}\right)\right]\,\bm{\mathrm{d}}\bm{x}_{p}\,\bm{\mathrm{d}}\bm{x}_{q}}\\
&\qquad{}-{}\ \text{the first integral of R.H.S.}\end{split}

(5)

Here we used the fact that
D
(the scatterer density distribution),
D^{\prime}
(cross-density distribution of scatters),
B^{2}
(the squared scattering potential) take real and positive values.
Only the complex components of Eq. (
4
) are the exponential functions consisting of imaginary number (
i
), and it became the cosine functions according Euler’s formula.

2.2.3
Speckle modulation and reduction

By computationally correcting the defocus (Step-(i) of Fig.
1
),
w
in Eq. (
5
) becomes
w_{0}
and the phase curvature
R
approaches infinity.
Thus, the real SCCP is simplified to

\begin{array}[]{l}\mathrm{Re}\!\left[s\!\left(\bm{x}^{\prime}+\dfrac{\Delta\bm{x}}{2}\right)s^{*}\!\left(\bm{x}^{\prime}-\dfrac{\Delta\bm{x}}{2}\right)\right]=\\
\qquad\qquad{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\displaystyle\exp\!\left(-\frac{\Delta\bm{x}^{2}}{2w_{0}^{2}}\right)\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}D(\bm{x})B^{2}(\bm{x})\exp\!\left[-\frac{2}{w_{0}^{2}}(\bm{x}^{\prime}-\bm{x})^{2}\right]\bm{\mathrm{d}}\bm{x}}\\
\qquad\qquad{}+{}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\displaystyle\exp\!\left(-\frac{\Delta\bm{x}^{2}}{2w_{0}^{2}}\right)\iint_{-\infty}^{\infty}D^{\prime}(\bm{x}_{p},\bm{x}_{q})B(\bm{x}_{p})B(\bm{x}_{q})\exp\!\left[-\frac{1}{w_{0}^{2}}(\bm{x}_{p}-\bm{x}_{q})\cdot\left(\frac{\bm{x}_{p}-\bm{x}_{q}}{2}-\Delta\bm{x}\right)\right]}\\
\qquad\qquad\quad{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\displaystyle{}\times{}\exp\!\left[-\frac{2}{w_{0}^{2}}\left(\bm{x}^{\prime}-\frac{\bm{x}_{p}+\bm{x}_{q}}{2}\right)^{2}\right]\cos(\phi_{pq})\,\bm{\mathrm{d}}\bm{x}_{p}\,\bm{\mathrm{d}}\bm{x}_{q}}\\
\qquad\qquad{}-{}\text{the first integral of R.H.S.}\end{array}

(6)

As we have mentioned, the blue part of the equation corresponds to the incoherence image.
Namely, the integral represents the convolution of
D(\bm{x})B^{2}(\bm{x})
and
\exp\left[-2(\bm{x})/w_{0}^{2}\right]
.
The former correspond to the scattering-strength distribution of the sample, and it can be considered as the “sample structure” measured by OCT.
On the other hand, the latter is a real positive Gaussian function having the same size with the lateral intensity PSF of OCT.
Since both of them are real and positive, this blue part corresponds to the incoherence image of the sample.
Note that this blue part is insensitive to the digital shift (
\Delta\bm{x}
) except for the overall scaling factor, i.e., the exponential coefficient left to the integral.
And hence, this blue part is analogous for any shift values.

On the other hand, the red part that corresponds to speckle has
\Delta\bm{x}
within the integral, and hence, is modulated by changing
\Delta\bm{x}
.

In summary, Eq. (
6
) implies that the speckle pattern of real-SCCP image can be altered by changing the digital shift even after the OCT acquisition (i.e., physical measurement), while the same operation keeps the incoherent image component unchanged.
Finally, the speckle can be reduced by averaging the multiple real-SCCP images (Step-(vi) of Fig.
1
).

Implementation and study protocol

3.1
FF-SSOCT system

A custom-built FF-SSOCT
[
6
]
was used to validate our speckle reduction method.
The light source is a wavelength-sweeping light source with a center wavelength of 840 nm and a sweeping range of 75 nm (BS-840-1-HP, Superlum, Ireland).
The probe beam illuminates the sample as a collimated plane wave where the beam diameter and the power on the sample is 2.34 mm and 5.23 mW, respectively.
The sample is images on a high-speed area camera (FASTCAM Mini AX100, Photron, Japan) through a microscope objective (NA = 0.3, effective focal length = 18 mm, RMS10X-PF, Olympus, Japan) and a tube lens (
f=750\,mm
).
The back scattered probe beam is overlapped with a reference beam and forms an interference signal on the camera.

The in-focus lateral resolution at the full-width at half-maximum (FWHM) intensity is 1.4
\muup
m (in air), and the axial resolution is 6.5
\muup
m (FWHM intensity in air).
The camera pixel size is 20
\muup
m, which corresponds to 0.48
\muup
m on the sample and is almost 1/3 of the in-focus lateral resolution.
The DOF is 16.0
\muup
m.
The sensor resolution is
1024\times 1024
pixels, which results in the maximum lateral field of view (FOV) is 0.49 mm
\times
0.49 mm.
The camera operates at at 4,000 frames/s and a single OCT volume is reconstructed from 1,000 spectral interference signals.
And hence, the volumetric measurement speed is 4 volumes/s.

After the OCT volume is reconstructed, the defocus is computationally corrected throughout the whole imaging depth by phase-only spatial-frequency filter.

More details of the OCT system and the computational refocusing are described elsewhere
[
6
]
.

Implementation of speckle reduction method

), the speckle reduction method generates multiple real SCCP images with several shifts (i.e, several shift amounts and directions) and average them.
These shifts are defined as follows.

3.3
Protocol for optimization and performance evaluation

3.3.1
Samples

To quantitatively evaluate the impact of our speckle reduction method on the lateral resolution, a sparse-scatterer phantom (OCT point spread function phantom, OCTPSF01, Edmund Optics, NJ) was measured.
The phantom consists of a solid medium with a refractive index of approximately 1.5 and a thickness of 10 mm, in which FeO nano-particles, which are smaller than 1
\muup
m, are sparsely suspended.
The randomly distributed scatterers generate isolated bright spots in OCT images, which enables quantitative assessment of the lateral resolution.

To validate the speckle-reduction effect of the present method in biomedical samples,
in vitro
cancer spheroids and an fixed adult zebrafish were measured.
The spheroids were formed by seeding 1,000 MCF-7 (human breast adenocarcinoma cell line) cells.
To include variety of samples, we used three spheroids with different cultivation protocols.
Namely, two of them were cultured without anti-cancer drug administration, where one of them was cultured for 8 days and the other was cultured for 10 days.
The other one was cultured for 8 days with 10
\muup
M of doxorubicin (DOX, anticancer drug), and the drug was applied on day 5 of the cultivation.

The zebrafish sample is a 11-month-old wild-type zebrafish.
The fish has been euthanized and fixed using a 4% Paraformaldehyde solution, then has stored in a phosphate-buffered saline solution.

3.4
Image evaluation metrics

To quantitatively evaluate the speckle-reduction performance, two widely used image metrics including the contrast-to-noise ratio (CNR) and equivalent number of looks (ENL) are used.

The CNR represents the contrast of simple structures in the image and is defined as

\mathrm{CNR}=10\log_{10}\left(\frac{\left|\mu_{1}-\mu_{2}\right|}{\sqrt{\sigma_{1}^{2}+\sigma_{2}^{2}}}\right),

(7)

where
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
represent the mean values of speckle-reduced image of the high-intensity region (region of interest 1, ROI1) and the low-intensity region (ROI2), respectively, and
\sigma_{1}^{2}
and
\sigma_{2}^{2}
are the variances within the ROI1 and ROI2, respectively.

The ENL represents the smoothness of the image within a homogeneous region and is defined as

\mathrm{ENL}=\frac{\mu^{2}}{\sigma^{2}},

(8)

where
\mu
denotes the mean signal within the selected ROI and
\sigma^{2}
denotes the corresponding variance.

5
Discussion

5.1
Image processing speed

We measured the image processing time of the proposed speckle reduction method.
The computational cost of both the speckle reduction algorithm and the overall post-processing pipeline was evaluated using a
1024\times 1024\times 512
-pixel OCT volume.

For the proposed speckle reduction process, as in processing Step-(ii) to Step-(vii) in Fig.
1
, approximately 0.55 s is required to process a single
en face
image.
When applied to a single
en face
OCT image, the processing time is approximately 0.55 seconds per frame.

The complete imaging and post-processing workflow consists of several sequential steps.
First, the OCT interference signal is acquired by the camera, including spectral detection and data storage.
With our FF-SSOCT system, the acquisition of a single OCT volume takes approximately 0.25 seconds, while saving the acquired data requires about 16 seconds.

After acquisition, the OCT signal is reconstructed through spectral rescaling followed by a Fourier transform, with a computation time of approximately 94.5 seconds per volume.
High-frequency noise is then removed using a binary spatial frequency filter, which requires approximately 104.7 seconds per volume.
Subsequently, computational refocusing is applied to correct defocus, which constitutes the most time-consuming step in the post-processing pipeline.
The refocusing process requires approximately 1151.9 seconds per volume, excluding manual parameter selection.

It should be noted that all post-processing procedures were implemented in a Jupyter-based Python environment;
therefore, the reported computation times are not optimized.
Despite its high computational cost, refocusing is necessary in FF-SSOCT imaging because the depth of field is limited to approximately 16
\muup
m.

In the study to determine the optimal parameters in Section
4.2.1
, up-sampling was applied to achieve sub-pixel shifts of OCT images.
However, based on the experimental results, the optimal despeckling protocol requires a maximum lateral shift of 8 pixels with a step size of 1 pixel, indicating that up-sampling is not required for practical applications.

Overall, the total post-processing time per OCT volume is approximately 1632.7 seconds.
As shown in Table
2
, the proposed speckle reduction method uses approximately 17.3% of the overall post-processing time and increases the total post-processing time by only about 20.8%.
Considering its effective speckle suppression performance and the absence of additional hardware requirements, the proposed method introduces a modest computational overhead and is practical for FF-SSOCT image quality enhancement using post-acquisition signal processing.

5.2
Comparison with other methods

As discussed in Section
1
, most of hardware-based speckle reduction methods rely on averaging multiple uncorrelated speckle patterns.
In this section, we compare our SCCP-based speckle reduction method with representative speckle reduction techniques in terms of acquisition strategy and the required number of frames.

Frame averaging is one of the most widely used speckle reduction methods in clinical OCT imaging.
Here, we first compared the performance of our SCCP-based speckle reduction method with the frame-averaging approach using the
en face
image of the same spheroid sample as in Fig.
6
but at a different depth.
In this study, due to camera memory limitations, only 32 OCT volumes can be captured in one acquisition of our FF-SSOCT.
We apply the frame-averaging approach using only 32 frames, which is fewer than the 50 or 60 frames reported in the literature.
We generated the SCCP-based speckle-reduced image with optimal parameters.
Fig.
8
presents a visual comparison among the original image, the 32-frame averaged speckle-reduced image, and the SCCP-based speckle-reduced image.
Quantitative evaluations using the CNR and ENL are summarized in Table
3
.

As shown in Fig.

, the SCCP-based speckle reduction method reveals clearer microstructural features compared with conventional frame averaging, and the appearance of speckle is more effectively suppressed.
In contrast, although frame averaging reduces speckle to some extent, speckle remains evident.

The quantitative results in Table

The SCCP-based method achieves a higher CNR than both the original image and the 32-frame averaged image, indicating enhanced image contrast.
In addition, the ENL values in ROI 1 and ROI 2 are improved using the proposed method and higher than 32-frame averaged image, demonstrating effective speckle suppression in relatively homogeneous regions.
These results suggest that the proposed speckle reduction method demonstrates superior performance in suppressing speckle while preserving structural information, and outperforms conventional frame averaging in
in vitro
imaging.

In addition to the speckle reduction performance, the processing time of 32-frame averaging takes much longer than the SCCP-based method, while some published methods even require more frames to average.
Götzinger
et al.
[
10
]
presented a frame averaging method that achieves effective speckle suppression using 60 OCT frames to average, while Alonso-Caneiro
[
11
]
used 50 frames.
It should also be noted that the frame averaging method is primarily designed for B-scan imaging and is not well-suited for FF-SSOCT.
In FF-SSOCT, a large volumetric dataset is acquired in each frame, which leads to excessive data size and increased demands on camera memory when conventional frame averaging is applied.

In addition, frame averaging is mainly designed for
in vivo
samples.
On the other hand,
in vitro
samples are generally more stable than
in vivo
samples, such as the retina, so the correlation between the speckle patterns of different frames is relatively high.
This causes the effectiveness of the frame averaging method to be low.
In comparison, our SCCP-based method achieves superior speckle reduction performance.

In conclusion, the results demonstrate that the proposed SCCP-based speckle reduction method outperforms conventional frame averaging in FF-SSOCT, achieving better speckle suppression and contrast enhancement in significantly less time.

Additionally, several other hardware-based speckle reduction techniques have also been published.
Desjardins
et al.
[
14
]
demonstrated an angular compounding method requiring a single acquisition with approximately 400 frames.
Zhao
et al.
[
13
]
presented a multi-angle compounding approach using 11 illumination angles and 20 B-scans per angle.
Polarization diversity methods can achieve speckle reduction within a single acquisition,
while Pircher
et al.
[
15
]
proposed a wavelength-compounding technique using two light sources.
Despite their effectiveness, these methods require specialized optical hardware and system modifications, making them incompatible with most OCT systems, including our FF-SSOCT system.
Furthermore, angle compounding methods acquire a large amount of data, and polarization diversity methods theoretically improve speckle contrast by only a factor of
\sqrt{2}
.
Sub-wavelength-band compounding methods, such as the method proposed by Zhao
et al.
[
16
]
, achieve speckle reduction at the cost of reduced axial resolution, which limits its applicability in high-resolution OCT imaging.
In contrast, our SCCP-based method does not require additional hardware modifications, only requires one acquisition, and preserves axial resolution, making it particularly suitable for the FF-SSOCT system.

6
Conclusion

In this paper, an imaging-model-based numerical speckle reduction method for FF-SSOCT is presented.
PSF phantom experiments confirm that the proposed method preserves lateral resolution.
Experiments conducted on spheroids and a zebrafish eye demonstrate improvements in image contrast and smoothness, as quantitatively evaluated using the CNR and the ENL.
In addition, subjective visual observations confirm effective speckle suppression, with microstructures clearly revealed.

Owing to its simple implementation, fast processing speed, resolution preservation, and remarkable speckle reduction performance,
our proposed speckle reduction method is both practical and effective for improving the image quality of FF-SSOCT using only post-acquisition signal processing.
