HBV breakthrough infection in individuals born to mothers with HBV infection: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Yuchen Pan, Zhifang Jia, Yangyu Zhang, Yanhua Wu, Jing Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDespite effective immunoprophylaxis, hepatitis B vaccine breakthrough infection (VBI) can still occur among individuals born to mothers with HBV infection. This study synthesized evidence on the incidence of post-vaccination HBV infection in this high-risk population.MethodsA systematic literature search was conducted in PubMed, Embase, Scopus, China National Knowledge Infrastructure and Wanfang Chinese databases for articles evaluating VBI in individuals born to mothers with HBV infection. The VBI was characterized by seroconversion to HBsAg and/or anti-HBc positivity at or after 2 years of age, following initial post-immunoprophylaxis HBsAg negativity. The incidence of VBI was pooled after quality assessment. The review followed PRISMA 2020 guidelines and was registered in PROSPERO (CRD420251058273).ResultsAmong 40 included studies, 37 provided HBsAg data (4,776 individuals; 24,434 person-years) and 29 reported anti-HBc (6,190 individuals; 16,663 person-years). The pooled cumulative incidence of HBsAg positivity was 0.24% (95% CI: 0.01–0.66%) and the incidence rate was 2.19 per 1,000 person-years (95% CI: 1.01-4.74). Anti-HBc positivity was higher, with a pooled cumulative positivity of 4.57% (95% CI: 2.18-7.66%) and an incidence rate of 19.69 (95% CI: 6.05-64.01) per 1,000 person-years. Subgroup analyses showed comparable HBsAg positivity between children born to mothers who were HBeAg-positive and HBeAg-negative (0.38% vs. 0.34%), while anti-HBc positivity differed markedly (6.81% vs. 0.64%). Consistently lower infection rates were observed among children who received booster immunization (HBsAg: 0.00% vs. 0.34%; anti-HBc: 0.37% vs. 3.88%), higher vaccine doses (10 µg vs. 5 µg: HBsAg 0.32% vs. 0.84%; anti-HBc 3.84% vs. 11.12%), or yeast-derived recombinant vaccines (HBsAg: 0.16% vs. 0.95%; anti-HBc: 4.50% vs. 7.95%), whereas anti-HBc positivity was higher among children older than 5 years than among those aged ≤ 5 years (9.61% vs. 2.38%).ConclusionsHBsAg positivity after successful neonatal immunoprophylaxis was rare, but the higher anti-HBc positivity suggests cumulative HBV exposure in this population, particularly among children born to HBeAg-positive mothers. Sustaining long-term protection through optimized vaccine strategies, such as timely boosters or higher postnatal dose regimens, may further reduce VBI risk in high-risk population and warrants further evaluation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28286-0.Keywords: HBV, Vaccine breakthrough infection, Postnatal immunoprophylaxis, Meta-analysis

Introduction

Hepatitis B virus (HBV) remains a major global public health threat. Approximately 254 million people live with chronic hepatitis B (CHB) worldwide, at risk of severe sequelae including liver cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma, leading to 1.1 million annual deaths [1]. Despite sustained efforts to reduce the global burden of HBV infection via prevention and therapy, 1.23 million new cases were still reported in 2022 [2]. Vertical transmission is the predominant route of HBV infection. Reducing the global burden of HBV therefore requires effective interruption of vertical transmission. Since 1992, the World Health Organization (WHO) has recommended the inclusion of hepatitis B vaccination in the Expanded Programme on Immunization (EPI), with administration of the first dose of Hepatitis B vaccine (HepB) at birth to prevent perinatal transmission [3]. In several countries, hepatitis B immunoglobulin (HBIG) has also been incorporated into national prevention programs for the infants born to mothers living with HBV [2]. The prevalence of chronic HBV infection among children under 5 years has declined markedly, reaching just under 1% globally in 2019, with more recent regional estimates reporting ≤ 0.1% in the Region of the Americas in 2022 and 0.30% in China in 2020 [4–6].

It is believed that standard hepatitis B vaccination provides long-term protective immunity [7]. However, vaccine breakthrough infections (VBI) have been reported among some vaccinated individuals [8]. For children born to mothers with HBV infection, close maternal contact during infancy and early childhood may pose a risk of infection after successful interruption of vertical transmission. This risk may increase over time as vaccine-induced anti-HBs levels wane, potentially leading to reduced protection against HBV infection. Previous studies have demonstrated that 10 years after completing the primary hepatitis B vaccination, the positivity rate of hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) is 0% and the positivity rate of antibody to hepatitis B core antigen (anti-HBc) is 1.8% in the general population [9]. One study followed children born to mothers with HBV infection who completed a full postnatal vaccination course until 5 years of age, reporting HBsAg and anti-HBc positivity of 1.5% and 5.3%, respectively [10]. However, data on the incidence of VBI in this high-risk population remain limited. HBV infection during infancy and childhood carries a substantially higher risk of chronicity compared with infection in adulthood [4]; therefore, the prevalence patterns of VBI among these high-risk children warrant urgent investigation.

This meta-analysis primarily assesses the incidence of vaccine breakthrough infection among children born to mothers living with HBV infection, as well as across different subgroups. These findings could provide data to address the current gap in understanding the burden of VBI in this population and offer evidence to inform the optimization of prevention and control strategies, consistent with the targets outlined in the WHO Interim Guidance for Country Validation of Viral Hepatitis Elimination.

Materials and methods

Search strategy and selection criteria

This meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with PRISMA guidelines and registered the study protocol in PROSPERO, CRD420251058273. A systematic literature search was conducted in PubMed, Embase, Scopus, China National Knowledge Infrastructure and Wanfang Chinese databases to identify all relevant studies until April 15, 2025. The search terms used included “hepatitis B virus” and “breakthrough infection” (the full search strategy can be found in Supplementary Appendix S1). References in the eligible articles were cross-checked by hand for additional literature.

The following standards were applied exclusively for selecting studies: (1) study participants born to mothers living with HBV infection; (2) participants received postnatal HBV prophylaxis and tested negative for HBsAg at previous followed up timepoint (before VBI assessment); (3) VBI assessment (including HBsAg and/or anti-HBc test) was performed when the participants were aged 2 years or older. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) systematic reviews, case reports/series, and editorials; (2) duplicate reports from the same population at the same time point; and (3) duplicate records. Two authors independently screened all titles and abstracts, followed by full text. Any discrepancies were resolved through re-evaluation and group discussion.

Outcomes

VBI was evaluated using two serological outcomes: either HBsAg positivity or anti-HBc positivity at or after 2 years of age following documented post-immunoprophylaxis HBsAg negativity. HBsAg positivity was considered evidence of overt HBV breakthrough infection, whereas anti-HBc positivity was interpreted as a broader marker of HBV exposure-related serological breakthrough, including natural HBV exposure, subclinical infection, or resolved infection.

Data extraction and quality assessment

Two authors independently extracted data from the included studies, and discrepancies were resolved through discussion. Data, including study details (author, year of publication, study setting, study region, sample size), participant demographics (maternal hepatitis B e antigen (HBeAg) status, postnatal HepB dose and type, and booster immunization), timing of post-vaccination serologic testing, the age of subsequent HBV marker testing (conducted at or after 2 years of age), and the HBV markers tested.

Risk of bias assessment was undertaken utilizing a modified adaptation of the tool developed by Hoy et al. [11]. Details are presented in Supplementary Table S1. Each assessment item was scored as 0 (yes, low risk) or 1 (no, high risk). Based on the summative score (range: 0–9), studies were classified as having a low (0–1), moderate [2–5], or high [6–9] risk of bias.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using R version 4.4.0. Pooled estimates were calculated separately according to the HBV markers (HBsAg or anti-HBc) and the type of data (cumulative incidence or incidence rate) reported. Given the expected clinical and methodological heterogeneity across studies, random-effects models were used for pooled analyses. For cumulative incidence, meta-analyses of proportions were conducted using the DerSimonian and Laird random-effects model with the Freeman-Tukey double arcsine transformation to stabilize the variance of proportions. The Hartung-Knapp adjustment was applied to provide more conservative 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Incidence-rate analyses were restricted to studies with serial follow-up assessments or sufficient information to estimate person-time. For studies with multiple follow-up time points, person-time was estimated using interval-specific numbers at risk and the corresponding interval length. Events were counted as newly detected positive results during follow-up whenever possible to avoid repeated counting across multiple time points. Meta-analyses of incidence rates were performed using log-transformed incidence rates under a random-effects model with restricted maximum likelihood estimation of between-study heterogeneity and Hartung-Knapp adjustment for CIs. The results were expressed as cases per 1,000 person-years. Between-study heterogeneity was assessed using I². Subgroup analyses were conducted according to maternal HBeAg status, age at VBI assessment, booster immunization, postnatal HepB dose, and vaccine type of primary immunization. Several sensitivity analyses were performed to assess the robustness of the findings. First, a sensitivity analysis was restricted to studies reporting both HBsAg and anti-HBc outcomes in the same study population. Second, a best-worst case sensitivity analysis was conducted by reassigning studies with unclear subgroup classification to either comparison group to examine the stability of pooled estimates. Third, leave-one-out sensitivity analyses were performed to assess the influence of individual studies on the pooled estimates. In addition, a post hoc sensitivity analysis excluding studies with HBsAg positivity above 1% was conducted to evaluate the contribution of higher-estimate studies to heterogeneity. Potential publication bias was evaluated using funnel plots and Egger’s test, and a two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

The initial literature search identified 5,233 potentially eligible articles (Fig. 1). During title and abstract screening, 3,436 records were excluded primarily because they were in vitro/basic experimental studies, review articles, or studies focusing on antiviral treatment or clinical management of hepatitis B. After full-text review, 40 studies met the inclusion criteria [10, 12–50], including 37 for the HBsAg analysis and 29 for the anti-HBc analysis. Among these, 35 were cohort studies, 4 were cross-sectional studies, and 1 was a randomized controlled trial. Geographically, 29 studies were from Asia, 8 from Europe, 2 from North America, and 1 from Oceania. The characteristics of the included studies are summarized in Table 1. The risk of bias assessment is presented in Supplementary Table S2, indicating that the included studies were of moderate to high quality.

Thirty-seven studies, including 25 studies (4,776 individuals) for cumulative incidence of infection and 12 studies (24,434 person-years of follow-up) for incidence rate, provided data on HBsAg positivity. As shown in the Fig. 2A, the pooled cumulative incidence of infection was 0.24% (95% CI: 0.01%-0.66%) under a random-effects model, with considerable heterogeneity (I² = 70.1%). The pooled incidence rate was 2.19 (95% CI: 1.01-4.74) per 1,000 person-years, showing moderate heterogeneity (I² = 63.9%, Fig. 2B).

Pooled incidence estimates based on HBsAg: A pooled cumulative incidence and B pooled incidence rate

A total of 29 studies reported data on anti-HBc positivity (with HBsAg negative), involving 24 studies (6,190 individuals) for cumulative positivity and 5 studies (16,663 person-years) for incidence rate. Under the random-effects model, the pooled cumulative positivity and incidence rate were 4.57% (95% CI: 2.18%-7.66%) and 19.69 (95% CI: 6.05-64.01) per 1,000 person-years, respectively, with substantial heterogeneity (I² = 91.9% and 94.2%, respectively) (Fig. 3A and B).

Pooled incidence estimates based on anti-HBc: A pooled cumulative positivity and B pooled rate

Due to the limited number of studies reporting incidence rate, subgroup analyses were conducted only among studies reporting cumulative incidence of infection (Table 2). For HBsAg, the cumulative incidence was comparable between individuals born to mothers who were HBeAg-positive and HBeAg-negative (0.38%, 95% CI: 0.00-0.92% vs. 0.34%, 95% CI: 0.00-0.82%). In contrast, a more pronounced difference was observed for anti-HBc, with higher positivity rates in individuals born to mothers who were HBeAg-positive (6.81%, 95% CI: 2.08%-13.23%) than in those born to mothers who were HBeAg-negative (0.64%, 95% CI: 0.08%-1.54%). Individuals who received booster immunization showed a lower cumulative incidence compared with those without boosters (0.00%, 95% CI: 0.00-0.07% vs. 0.34%, 95% CI: 0.02%-0.93% for HBsAg; 0.37%, 95% CI: 0.00-1.61% vs. 3.88%, 95% CI: 1.70%-6.76% for anti-HBc). Similarly, vaccination with a 10 µg HepB was associated with lower cumulative incidence compared with the 5 µg (0.32%, 95% CI: 0.00-1.13% vs. 0.84%, 95% CI: 0.00-2.91% for HBsAg; 3.84%, 95% CI: 2.02%-6.12% vs. 11.12%, 95% CI: 0.03%-33.80% for anti-HBc). By age, the cumulative incidence of HBsAg was similar between children aged 2-5 years (0.36%, 95% CI: 0.00-1.21%) and those older than 5 years (0.26%, 95% CI: 0.00-1.03%), whereas anti-HBc positivity was more common in the older group (9.61%, 95% CI: 3.54%-17.98%) than in those aged 2-5 years (2.38%, 95% CI: 0.80%-4.60%). When stratified by vaccine type of primary immunization, the cumulative incidences of both HBsAg and anti-HBc positivity were lower in individuals immunized with yeast-derived recombinant vaccines (HBsAg: 0.16%, 95% CI: 0.00-0.78%; anti-HBc: 4.50%, 95% CI: 2.81%-6.51%) than in those receiving plasma-derived vaccines (HBsAg: 0.95%, 95% CI: 0.12%-2.29%; anti-HBc: 7.95%, 95% CI: 0.00-32.74%).

In the sensitivity analysis restricted to studies reporting both HBsAg and anti-HBc outcomes in the same study population, HBsAg positivity remained low, with a pooled estimate of 0.18% (95% CI: 0.00-0.67%; I² = 75.0%), whereas anti-HBc positivity remained relatively higher, with a pooled estimate of 5.14% (95% CI: 2.15-9.15%; I² = 93.2%) (Supplementary Figure S1). To assess the robustness of the findings, a best-worst case sensitivity analysis was conducted by assigning studies with unclear subgroup classification to the corresponding subgroups under two extreme allocation scenarios, followed by re-estimation of the pooled effects (Supplementary Table S3). In general, the pooled estimates for most subgroups showed limited changes across scenarios, and the overall trends remained broadly consistent with those in the primary analyses. Scenario-dependent variations in relative differences were observed in several subgroups, mainly including maternal HBeAg status (HBsAg), postnatal hepatitis B vaccine dose (HBsAg), age at VBI assessment (HBsAg), and vaccine type of primary immunization (anti-HBc). To further assess the influence of individual studies on the pooled estimates, leave-one-out sensitivity analyses were performed. The pooled estimates were generally stable for HBsAg cumulative incidence, HBsAg incidence rate, and the cumulative positivity of anti-HBc, suggesting that these findings were not driven by any single study. Although some variation was observed for the pooled rate of anti-HBc positivity likely due to the small number of contributing studies, the estimates remained consistently higher than those for HBsAg positivity. (Supplementary Figure S2). In addition, a post hoc sensitivity analysis excluding studies with HBsAg positivity above 1% showed that pooled HBsAg positivity remained close to zero and heterogeneity decreased markedly to I² = 0.0%, suggesting that these higher-incidence studies contributed substantially to heterogeneity in the primary HBsAg analysis.

Although the funnel plots showed mild visual asymmetry (Supplementary Figure S3), the Egger’s test results did not provide clear evidence that publication bias had materially affected the findings (HBsAg: P = 0.228 for cumulative incidence and P = 0.290 for incidence rate; anti-HBc: P = 0.265 for cumulative positivity and P = 0.082 for rate).

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis provide an overview of HBV vaccine breakthrough infection rates following immunoprophylaxis in individuals born to mothers living with HBV infection. Breakthrough infections occurred after immunoprophylaxis, with rare HBsAg positivity but relatively higher anti-HBc positivity. Because studies reported either cumulative positivity or incidence rates, these measures were synthesized separately. The broadly consistent estimates obtained from these two analytical approaches support the robustness of our findings. The subgroup analyses also revealed lower pooled estimates of HBV serological breakthrough markers among individuals who received higher postnatal vaccine doses, booster immunization, or yeast-derived recombinant vaccines, whereas anti-HBc positivity tended to increase with longer follow-up duration.

With the implementation of the standard HepB vaccination series combined with HBIG administration in high-risk neonates, vertical transmission of HBV has been substantially reduced in clinical practice [28]. However, because household exposure may persist when mothers are living with HBV infection, concerns remain regarding subsequent horizontal transmission in this high-risk group. In the present meta-analysis, HBsAg positivity remained uncommon among children who had completed postnatal immunoprophylaxis and were initially HBsAg-negative, with a pooled cumulative incidence of 0.24% and a pooled incidence rate of 2.19 per 1,000 person-years. These findings support the sustained protective value of neonatal immunoprophylaxis and highlight the importance of maintaining complete vaccination among infants born to mothers living with HBV infection. However, several studies reported HBsAg positivity above 1% and contributed to heterogeneity. These studies varied in immunoprophylaxis period, vaccine type or dose, follow-up duration, maternal risk profile, and study design. Although no single factor fully explained these higher estimates, such contextual differences should be considered when interpreting pooled HBsAg findings. Therefore, low overt HBsAg positivity does not fully exclude residual HBV exposure under current immunization strategies, which is further reflected by the anti-HBc findings discussed below. Anti-HBc positivity was higher than HBsAg positivity, with a pooled cumulative positivity of 4.57% and a pooled rate of 19.69 per 1,000 person-years. An HBsAg-negative and anti-HBc–positive profile may reflect previous exposure with viral clearance, subclinical infection, or occult HBV infection (OBI), which has been reported in vaccinated children born to mothers with HBV infection despite appropriate immunoprophylaxis [51, 52]. Because baseline anti-HBc status and HBV DNA testing were often unavailable, we could not distinguish resolved early exposure, later horizontal exposure, or true OBI. Nevertheless, the higher anti-HBc positivity suggests that residual HBV exposure may occur even when overt HBsAg positivity remains rare, highlighting the value of anti-HBc as a marker for detecting subclinical or cumulative viral contact in this high-risk population.

In subgroup analyses, several patterns emerged for HBsAg and anti-HBc positivity. The cumulative incidence of HBsAg positivity was comparable between children born to mothers with and without HBeAg positivity, indicating that subsequent overt HBsAg positivity remained uncommon during follow-up in this initially HBsAg-negative population. Nevertheless, maternal HBeAg status remains clinically relevant. HBeAg positivity is a recognized marker of active viral replication and is often accompanied by high maternal HBV DNA levels, which are key determinants of perinatal transmission risk and immunoprophylaxis failure [53, 54]. Children born to mothers with HBeAg positivity may therefore have experienced greater viral exposure around delivery or early life, which could later be reflected by anti-HBc positivity even without persistent HBsAg positivity. The higher anti-HBc positivity among children born to mothers with HBeAg positivity may therefore indicate greater cumulative HBV exposure pressure. In light of the interpretation of anti-HBc described above, this pattern may reflect transient or subclinical viral contact rather than overt persistent infection. Thus, maternal HBeAg positivity should be regarded as a marker of increased cumulative HBV exposure risk, supporting continued post-vaccination monitoring in this high-risk subgroup.

Booster immunization was associated with lower pooled estimates of HBsAg and anti-HBc positivity in the subgroup analyses. For HBsAg positivity, this finding is biologically plausible, as a booster dose can restore or increase anti-HBs titers and may help maintain humoral protection when vaccine-induced antibodies decline over time [55, 56]. The interpretation of anti-HBc positivity, however, warrants greater caution. Booster vaccination mainly enhances anti-HBs responses and is not expected to have a direct biological effect on anti-HBc seroconversion, which reflects natural HBV exposure. Therefore, the lower anti-HBc positivity observed among booster recipients should not be interpreted simply as a direct effect of booster vaccination, as it may also be influenced by differences in study populations, follow-up intensity, healthcare access, birth cohort, and background HBV exposure risk. According to the WHO, booster immunization is not routinely recommended for the general population, but the potential benefits of boosters in individuals with declining or undetectable anti-HBs levels have been evaluated [57]. Therefore, in this high-risk population, the present findings should not be interpreted as supporting routine booster vaccination for all children, but rather as highlighting the potential value of continued serological surveillance and targeted booster strategies for those with waning or undetectable anti-HBs.

Lower pooled estimates of HBV serological outcomes were observed among individuals receiving higher postnatal HepB doses. The difference between 10 µg and 5 µg HepB appeared more pronounced for anti-HBc positivity than for HBsAg positivity, while HBsAg positivity remained low in both dose groups. From a public health perspective, this distinction is important because HBsAg positivity more directly reflects overt or persistent infection, whereas anti-HBc positivity provides broader serological evidence of HBV exposure, subclinical infection, or resolved infection. Therefore, higher-dose regimens may be more relevant to reducing serological evidence of residual HBV exposure than to further lowering overt HBsAg positivity, which was already rare under both dose strategies. This pattern may be partly related to differences in vaccine-induced anti-HBs responses, as higher and more sustained antibody levels could contribute to protection against low-level HBV exposure or serological breakthrough. This interpretation is biologically plausible, as previous evidence suggests that higher antigen doses can enhance hepatitis B vaccine immunogenicity and improve anti-HBs responses after primary immunization [58–60]. However, dose-related differences, particularly for anti-HBc positivity, should also be interpreted cautiously because vaccine dose was not randomly assigned and may have been influenced by study context and exposure background. Therefore, in this high-risk population, higher-dose regimens may have potential protective value, but their independent effect should be further evaluated in prospective studies with comparable programmatic contexts.

Age appeared to have a different association with the two serological markers. The cumulative incidence of HBsAg positivity was similar between children aged > 5 years and those aged 2-5 years, indicating that overt HBsAg positivity remained uncommon beyond early childhood. In contrast, anti-HBc positivity was higher among older children (9.61% vs. 2.38%), which may reflect cumulative subclinical exposure over time as vaccine-induced antibody levels decline. This pattern suggests that transient HBV exposure may become more detectable with longer follow-up, whereas persistent HBsAg positivity remains rare. These findings support the durability of protection afforded by early immunization, while also highlighting the potential value of ongoing serologic surveillance in school-aged children and adolescents to detect silent exposures and inform targeted booster strategies in high-risk settings.

Individuals who received yeast-derived recombinant vaccines showed lower cumulative incidences of both HBsAg and anti-HBc positivity than those immunized with plasma-derived vaccines. This finding is consistent with evidence that recombinant vaccines provide standardized HBsAg antigen production and can induce reliable anti-HBs responses, which may contribute to protection against persistent HBsAg positivity after low-level HBV exposure [61–63]. However, given that vaccine type was closely related to vaccination era and prevention-program context, the independent contribution of vaccine formulation should be interpreted cautiously.

Overall, these subgroup findings should be interpreted as observational associations rather than causal effects. Several subgroup factors were closely intertwined with vaccination period and prevention-program context. For booster immunization, among the studies included in the anti-HBc subgroup analysis, 6 of 8 studies in the booster group were published after 2010, compared with 13 of 20 studies in the non-booster group, suggesting that part of the observed difference may reflect later birth cohorts, more regular serological follow-up, improved healthcare access, strengthened HBV prevention programs, and lower background transmission rather than booster vaccination alone. Similarly, higher-dose HepB regimens were not randomly assigned but often reflected local clinical protocols, regional immunization policies, and health system capacity; studies using higher doses also tended to come from relatively later periods, although this pattern was not absolute. Vaccine type was also strongly linked to era; as noted above, plasma-derived vaccines were mainly used in earlier immunization programs, whereas yeast-derived recombinant vaccines became common during periods when HBIG use, maternal screening, viral-load assessment, and neonatal immunoprophylaxis practices were more standardized. These contextual factors are particularly relevant for anti-HBc positivity, which is sensitive to cumulative exposure history, follow-up duration, and testing frequency. Therefore, the lower pooled estimates observed in booster, higher-dose, or recombinant-vaccine subgroups may reflect both potential biological or immunological advantages and broader improvements in HBV prevention systems over time.

Substantial heterogeneity was observed across several outcomes. Although sensitivity analyses suggested that the main pooled estimates were generally not driven by any single study, pooled results should still be interpreted in light of cross-study differences in population characteristics, immunoprophylaxis strategies, follow-up design, and exposure background. This issue is particularly relevant to incidence-rate analyses, which included fewer studies and were more sensitive to differences in follow-up duration, loss to follow-up, testing frequency, and person-time estimation. Because incidence-rate analyses included few studies with incomplete covariate reporting, formal subgroup or meta-regression analyses for these outcomes were not considered reliable; these estimates should therefore be interpreted cautiously.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, most included studies did not assess OBI or systematically report baseline anti-HBc status; therefore, anti-HBc positivity during follow-up should be interpreted as a marker of cumulative HBV exposure rather than definite incident infection. Second, the geographic distribution of included studies was highly uneven, with most studies conducted in Asia, no data from Africa, and very limited evidence from the Americas and Oceania, which may limit generalizability to underrepresented regions with different HBV epidemiology and prevention resources. Third, incidence-rate estimates were affected by heterogeneity in follow-up duration, testing frequency, and person-time estimation because serological markers were usually assessed only at scheduled visits. Finally, subgroup comparisons were mainly based on indirect comparisons across observational studies, and residual confounding from study period, exposure background, immunization strategy, healthcare access, and follow-up intensity may have influenced the observed associations, especially for anti-HBc-related outcomes.

Conclusions

In this meta-analysis of individuals born to mothers living with HBV infection who received immunoprophylaxis, HBV vaccine breakthrough infection remained uncommon, indicating the sustained effectiveness of current prevention strategies. Lower infection estimates among children receiving booster immunization or higher HepB doses may be consistent with stronger vaccine-induced protection, although these observational findings require further evaluation in prospective studies. Collectively, these results suggest the sustained effectiveness of neonatal HBV immunization while highlighting potential areas for targeted optimization. Strengthening post-vaccination monitoring and considering tailored booster or higher-dose strategies for high-risk subgroups may further reduce breakthrough infections and advance progress toward HBV elimination goals by 2030.
