Cardiovascular peptide markers are associated with age-related macular degeneration in the Bialystok PLUS general population cohort
Authors: Agnieszka Budnik, Jakub Tuchliński, Łukasz Lisowski, Magdalena Michnowska-Kobylińska, Diana Anna Dmuchowska, Małgorzata Chlabicz, Anna Szpakowicz, Marlena Dubatówka, Marcin Kondraciuk, Karol Kamiński, Joanna Konopińska
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAge-related macular degeneration (AMD) and cardiovascular disease (CVD) share numerous risk factors; however, protein biomarkers for AMD are lacking. We investigated whether circulating cardiovascular peptide biomarkers are associated with AMD in the population-based Białystok PLUS cohort. This cross-sectional analysis included 699 participants aged ≥ 50 years (AMD + = 93; AMD⁻ = 606) examined between 2018 and 2023. AMD was graded from fundus photos with the use of the Wisconsin and modified International Classification systems. Ninety-two cardiovascular proteins were quantified in serum with the Olink Target Cardiovascular III panel. Age-adjusted linear or logistic regressions assessed biomarker-AMD associations, and receiver-operating-characteristic (ROC) curves evaluated discriminative performance. After adjustment, AMD+ participants exhibited lower galectin-4 (β = − 0.15, p = 0.043) and TNF-receptor-superfamily-member-10 C (TNFRSF10C) (β = − 0.17, p = 0.037) concentrations and higher von Willebrand factor (vWF) levels (β = 0.22, p = 0.036) versus AMD⁻ individuals. Galectin-4, TNFRSF10C, and vWF predicted AMD with areas under the ROC curve of 0.613 (95% confidence interval [CI] 0.516–0.709), 0.606 (0.520–0.692), and 0.594 (0.500–0.687), respectively. Optimal cut-offs were 4.05 NPX for galectin-4, 5.09 NPX for TNFRSF10C, and 8.03 NPX for vWF, yielding sensitivities/specificities of 57%/63%, 58%/62% and 55%/63%, respectively. Elevated vWF and reduced galectin-4 and TNFRSF10C are independently associated with AMD, suggesting overlapping vascular, inflammatory and apoptotic pathways with CVD. Introduction

Age-related macular degeneration (AMD) is a degenerative, multifactorial disease with modifiable (smoking, diet, and lack of physical activity) and non-modifiable (increasing age, family history, and ethnicity) risk factors.1 It is estimated to be present in 8.69% of the global population, affecting 196 million people in 2020, projected to reach 288 million by 2040.2 Despite advances in treatment, AMD is the most common cause of irreversible vision loss in population aged > 55 years in high-income countries.3.

Cardiovascular diseases (CVDs), including coronary heart disease (CHD) and cerebrovascular disease (stroke), are the leading causes of morbidity and premature death worldwide, especially in developing countries. According to the Global Burden of Cardiovascular Disease and Risk Factors, CVD’s overall prevalence increased from 271 million in 1990 to 523 million in 2019.4 Moreover, CVDs reported for 27.8% of the total deaths in 2021 in Western Europe.5 Common risk factors mimic those of AMD, including aging, obesity, physical inactivity, smoking, dyslipidemia, diabetes mellitus (DM), hypertension, and genetic predisposition. Several risk factors associated with CVD have been implicated in AMD, suggesting overlapping pathophysiology between conditions.

Given these overlapping risk factors and potentially shared mechanisms, we explored whether insights from CVD research, particularly regarding protein biomarkers, could guide the search for reliable biomarkers for AMD. Currently, no protein biomarker specific to AMD can assist in its diagnosis. Detecting AMD at an early stage with such a marker would facilitate further investigations into early treatment strategies for at-risk patients. Therefore, our study sought to identify novel cardiovascular biomarkers associated with AMD.

Data were obtained from the Białystok PLUS Study, a fully randomized prospective cohort of individuals aged 20–79 years. The present analysis focused on participants aged ≥ 50 years, aligning with AMD diagnostic criteria. We investigated circulating protein biomarkers in relation to AMD status to identify markers that could aid diagnosis, support risk evaluation, and elucidate AMD pathogenesis. By examining a range of cardiovascular proteins, we also assessed links between cardiovascular health and AMD, thereby providing insight into the disease’s systemic aspects. These findings could enhance AMD risk assessment, facilitate early diagnosis, and eventually inform new therapeutic strategies.

Materials and methods

Study design

The Białystok PLUS Study is a prospective, population-based cohort investigating the risk factors and prevalence of cardiovascular, neurological, musculoskeletal, psychiatric, ophthalmic, and endocrine diseases among residents of Białystok (Northeastern Poland). A random sample of individuals aged 20–79 years was drawn from the general population. The precise study’s aims and methodology were presented elsewhere6. For the present analysis, we included Białystok PLUS participants aged 50–79 years at the time of the survey at the study center. Data were gathered from November 2018 and June 2023; with a total of 2149 individuals examined. Consistent with AMD diagnostic criteria, the minimum age threshold of 50 years yielded 1035 eligible participants. Fundus photography analysis excluded 163 individuals owing to lens opacity, and 35 were lost to follow-up. Participants were not consecutive clinical cases; they were randomly selected from the general population registry, with no selection based on clinical presentation, referral pattern, or temporal sequence of attendance.

Diagnosis of AMD

Fundus photos were achieved on all study participants without previous pharmacological mydriasis, using a 35° color digital fundus camera (Canon CR-2 PLUS AF; Canon U.S.A., Inc., New York, USA). Images were classified according to the Wisconsin AMD grading system7 and the modified International Classification System8 by properly trained ophthalmologists (ŁL, MMK). The eyes of each individual were separately assessed and categorized, and those with more severe stages were qualified for further analysis. Analogous to other population-based study,9 we included both early and late AMD cases in the analysis as a single AMD-positive group, in accordance with the predefined study design. Stratification by AMD stage was not part of the original analytical plan. Moreover, we investigated the presence of AMD-specific lesions, such as retinal pigmentary alterations, large drusen (≥ 125 μm), and large drusen area (≥ 331.820 µm2),10 as a additional outcome variable. The patients were allocated to one of two groups based on the presence (AMD+ group) or absence (AMD- group).

Other characteristics and methods

Anthropometric measurements were obtained using certified equipment and performed in a standard manner. Body weight and height were recorded with participants barefoot and wearing underwear. Body mass index was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height (m2). Fasting blood samples were collected from all participants to assess serum concentrations of biochemical parameters.

Data collection

Data were obtained through standardized health examinations conducted by trained staff in specially equipped examination centers. Meticulous medical history was sought from each participant using questionnaires. Blood pressure was measured in seated position after a minimum 5-min rest, using the oscillometric Omron MG Comfort device (Omron Healthcare Co., Ltd., Muko, Kyoto, Japan).11,12

Biochemical parameters

Peripheral intravenous fasting blood samples were collected at the time of visit in the morning after an 8-h fast. Next, the samples were immediately centrifuged and stored at − 80 °C until analysis. Total cholesterol, triglyceride, and creatinine levels were determined by the enzymatic colorimetric method using a Cobas c111 from ROCHE. High-density lipoprotein cholesterol and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol levels were determined using the homogeneous enzymatic colorimetric method on a Cobas c111 from ROCHE ( Basel, Switzerland). Fasting glucose and 120-min glucose in the oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) were measured using the enzymatic reference method with hexokinase on a Cobas c111 from ROCHE. Fasting insulin and 120-min insulin during the OGTT were quantified by electrochemiluminescence immunoassay (ECLIA) on a Cobas e411 analyzer (Roche). Hemoglobin A1c was measured by ion-exchange high-performance liquid chromatography on a D-10 system (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). N-terminal pro-brain natriuretic peptide and high-sensitivity troponin T concentrations were also determined by ECLIA on the Cobas e411.

Proteomic analysis

Our participants’ serum samples were stored in a biobank at -80 °C. The samples were gradually warmed to 4 °C to thaw and prepare for transport to the Olink Proteomics Laboratory (Uppsala, Sweden), where analyses were performed. We analyzed 92 protein biomarkers in the Target 96 Cardiovascular III panel (catalog number 95611). The panel comprises proteins involved in cardiovascular regulation, inflammation, endothelial function, apoptosis, and metabolic pathways. The complete list of analyzed proteins is provided in the Supplementary Material. The protein screening was established using the Proximity Extension Assay (PEA) method and expressed in normalized protein expression (NPX) relative units on a log2 scale. Biomarkers were excluded from analysis when > 25% of samples fell below the study’s LOD and the biomarker failed to differentiate between groups.

Trial registration and ethical issues

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee on Medical University of Bialystok (approval number: R-I-002/108/2016). Written informed consent for attending the study and for publication was obtained from all participants of the study. ClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: NCT03197363. The study has been performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was carried out using R software, version 4.2.1. (R Core Team [2022] R: Language and environment for statistical computing by R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Descriptive statistics comprised counts (percentage) for nominal variables and mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (quartile 1; quartile 3) for continuous variables, according to distribution normality. Group comparisons employed Fisher’s exact or χ2 tests for nominal data and t-tests or Mann–Whitney U tests for continuous data, as appropriate. Normality was assessed via the Shapiro–Wilk test and by examining skewness and kurtosis. Differences between groups, expressed as mean/median values (AMD+ minus AMD⁻) or odds ratios (ORs) for AMD+ versus AMD-, were reported with 9% confidence intervals (CIs). All tests were two-tailed with α = 0.05. Linear and logistic regressions adjusted for age evaluated associations between individual parameters and AMD presence. Receiver-operating-characteristic (ROC) curves were generated for variables significant in the regression models, and cut-off points were derived using the Youden index.

There were no significant between-group differences in sex, body mass index, education level, past cataract surgery, presence of CHD, hypertension, DM, smoking, or smoking time.

Discussion

In this population-based study, three circulating proteins—von Willebrand factor (vWF), galectin-4 (LGALS4), and tumor necrosis factor receptor superfamily member 10 C (TNFRSF10C)—were associated with AMD status. Elevated vWF levels are consistent with previous reports linking endothelial dysfunction and vascular alterations with AMD development and progression. In contrast, LGALS4 has not previously been described in AMD cohorts and may reflect mechanisms related to cell–cell adhesion, membrane organization, and inflammatory signaling involved in choroidal neovascularization. TNFRSF10C, a decoy receptor for TRAIL, suggests a potential contribution of apoptotic regulation to retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) survival. Importantly, these associations persisted after adjustment for age. Among the three proteins, TNFRSF10C demonstrated the most consistent discriminative signal (AUC 0.606) and carries the strongest biological plausibility in AMD pathogenesis given its direct role in modulating TRAIL-induced apoptosis of RPE cells. Galectin-4 and vWF, while statistically significant after age adjustment, showed more modest AUC values (0.613 and 0.594, respectively) with wide confidence intervals, and should be regarded as exploratory associations requiring replication. All three proteins should currently be considered candidate markers rather than clinically applicable diagnostic biomarkers.

Several pathomechanisms underlie AMD, including lipofuscinogenesis—the accumulation of lipofuscin within RPE cells,13 drusogenesis (deposition of insoluble drusen),14 chronic inflammation,15 and choroidal neovascularization.16 Several molecular factors participate in these pathological processes. Here, proteins with statistically significant adjusted p-values (padj) included LGALS4, vWF, and TNFRSF10C. Cohort studies analyzing factors and markers significantly associated with AMD confirmed the association of elevated blood vWF levels with AMD occurrence.17–19 Nonetheless, no cohort study highlighted an association between AMD and changes in LGALS4 and TNFRSF10C levels. Many cohort studies investigating AMD have primarily focused on lifestyle factors, environmental exposures, mortality outcomes, or genetic polymorphisms rather than circulating blood-based protein markers.

vWF is a glycoprotein predominantly involved in blood coagulation. It is produced by endothelial cells and megakaryocytes and is present in plasma in free form or complexed with factor VIII, in platelet granules, and on the endothelial cell surface.20 vWF mediates platelet adhesion to injury sites by binding specifically to platelet glycoproteins and extracellular matrix components.21 Notably, the association between vWF and AMD is well established,22–24 with vWF correlating with both the stage and progression of AMD, making it a potential biomarker for the disease.22,23 Other studies also highlighted associations between VEGF, hemorheological parameters, endothelial dysfunction, and AMD, suggesting these markers may collectively reflect processes described by Virchow’s triad.24 Furthermore, research on Japanese patients with AMD demonstrated that intravitreal injection of aflibercept, a VEGF inhibitor, significantly reduced vWF levels, correlating with clinical improvement, particularly in vitreous and subretinal hemorrhages.23

LGALS4 is a member of the carbohydrate-binding lectin family. It is synthesized as a cytosolic protein and is often found in insoluble complexes. In adults, its expression is restricted primarily to the digestive tract. LGALS4 localizes to adherens junctions and lipid rafts in microvillus membranes, suggesting its role in stabilizing cell-cell contacts and membrane domains, and functions as a cross-linker that recognizes specific sugar moieties on the cell surface. It participates in cell adhesion, protein translocation, wound healing, inflammatory responses in the intestine, and cancer progression.25 Importantly, reduced LGALS4 expression has been observed in rheumatoid arthritis,26 inflammatory bowel disease,27 various cancers, including colorectal, prostate, and skin malignancies.28 In epithelial cancer cell lines, galectin-4 is upregulated and soluble in highly differentiated, polarized monolayers; however, it is absent in poorly differentiated lines. These alterations may be relevant to the early stages of tumorigenesis in the gastrointestinal epithelia. Although LGALS4 has not been directly implicated in AMD, several studies have investigated its role in retinal and choroidal neovascularization. LGALS4 remains relatively understudied in the context of ocular diseases. However, several studies investigating other members of the galectin family (including galectin-1, galectin-3, galectin-8, and galectin-9) have demonstrated their involvement in VEGF signaling, angiogenesis, and inflammatory responses, processes that are highly relevant to AMD pathology. 29,30

TNFRSF10C, also known as Decoy Receptor 1 or TRAIL-R3, is a member of the tumor necrosis factor receptor family. It contains a TRAIL-binding extracellular domain and a transmembrane domain but lacks a cytoplasmic death domain, rendering it incapable of inducing apoptosis. Instead, TNFRSF10C acts as a decoy receptor by competing for TRAIL binding, thereby inhibiting TRAIL-induced apoptosis and protecting cells from inappropriate programmed cell death.31 Decreased levels of TNFRSF10C are detected in cancers, such as colorectal cancer,32 pancreatic cancer,33 prostate cancer,34 and non-small cell lung cancer,35 systemic lupus erythematosus,36 and Alzheimer’s disease.37 Although TNFRSF10C has been implicated in AMD pathogenesis,38–40 its role remains incompletely characterized. In AMD, the receptor’s anti-apoptotic function may be pivotal for RPE survival. Because RPE dysfunction is central to disease progression, preserving the balance between pro- and anti-apoptotic cues is essential for retinal integrity. Direct investigations of TNFRSF10C in RPE cells are scarce; nonetheless, related receptors such as TNFRSF10A have been associated with RPE degeneration, supporting a regulatory contribution by TNFRSF10C.41 A review on the association of TRAIL with eye disease observed a significant association of another protein in this family, TNFRSF10A (TNFRSF10-LOC389641 rs13278062), with an increased AMD risk.42,43 Another study found a reduced level of TNFRSF10C, which is thought to act as a dominant-negative receptor. The authors suggest that the low levels of TNFRSF10C in these patients may increase the amount of TRAIL interacting with the proapoptotic receptors (TNFRSF10A and TNFRSF10B), resulting in enhanced TRAIL apoptosis of photoreceptors and retinal pigment epithelial cells.43,44 Furthermore, TNFRSF10C expression can be regulated by factors such as p53 and epigenetic modifications, including promoter hypermethylation, which impacts its ability to inhibit apoptosis.45,46 In AMD, where oxidative stress and chronic inflammation are key pathology drivers, TNFRSF10C expression modulation may influence RPE cell survival and disease progression. Further investigation of the TRAIL-TNFRSF10C axis in retinal cells may provide novel insights into AMD pathogenesis and therapeutic opportunities targeting apoptotic regulation.47

The identification of circulating proteins associated with AMD raises the prospect of blood-based risk stratification complementing or preceding fundus photography-based screening—particularly in primary care or cardiology settings where retinal imaging may not be routinely available. A serum panel incorporating TNFRSF10C, vWF, and galectin-4 could help identify individuals at elevated AMD risk warranting early ophthalmic referral, and given the shared pathophysiology with CVD, may also support integrated cardiovascular risk management. At present, however, the modest discriminative performance (AUC ≈ 0.6) precludes standalone diagnostic use; their value lies in enriching biomarker panels and generating mechanistic hypotheses. Regarding anti-VEGF therapies, intravitreal aflibercept has already been shown to reduce plasma vWF in AMD patients,23 and newer agents such as faricimab—with its dual VEGF-A and angiopoietin-2 blockade—may more potently suppress endothelial activation and normalize vWF levels. No data currently exist on the effect of anti-VEGF treatment on circulating TNFRSF10C or galectin-4; future clinical trials should therefore monitor these proteins as pharmacodynamic endpoints to validate their pathophysiological relevance and potential utility in treatment response monitoring.

Limitations

This study presents some limitations. Although the analyses are embedded within a prospective cohort, they rely on a single time-point assessment (2018–2023), precluding conclusions regarding temporality or causality between circulating proteins and AMD onset. AMD cases were analyzed as a single group in line with the predefined study design and to ensure sufficient statistical power in this population-based cohort. Consequently, stage-specific analyses (early vs. late AMD) were beyond the scope of the present study. However, such analyses are clinically relevant and should be addressed in future investigations with larger sample sizes. In addition, all participants were urban residents of Bialystok, Poland, aged ≥ 50 years, limiting generalizability to other ethnicities, rural settings, or distinct environmental or genetic backgrounds. Fundus photographs were captured without pharmacologic dilation, a practice that may diminish image quality and sensitivity to subtle drusen or pigmentary alterations, thereby risking misclassification of early AMD. Furthermore, the discriminative performance of the identified biomarkers was modest (AUC ≈ 0.6), indicating limited diagnostic utility at this stage and underscoring the need for validation in larger, independent cohorts. Moreover, no formal correction for multiple testing (e.g., Bonferroni or Benjamini–Hochberg procedure) was applied across the 92 proteins analyzed. This reflects the exploratory, hypothesis-generating nature of the study; however, it increases the risk of false-positive findings, and the identified associations should be interpreted accordingly. Nevertheless, the present study was designed primarily to identify blood-based biomarkers of AMD and to refine risk stratification for individuals susceptible to vision loss.

Conclusions

The present findings carry several clinical implications. First, they identify candidate biomarkers whose inclusion in future risk-stratification algorithms should be considered. Furthermore, the observed associations between LGALS4 and TNFRSF10C encourage mechanistic investigations of RPE dysfunction, oxidative-stress responses, and inflammatory signaling in AMD. Probing the TRAIL–TNFRSF10C axis and galectin-mediated pathways may ultimately yield novel strategies for modulating angiogenesis and apoptosis in this disease.

In summary, the study not only reaffirms the established vascular contributions to AMD pathogenesis but also identifies two novel protein associations, thereby laying the foundation for enriched biomarker panels and targeted interventions to mitigate vision loss in at-risk populations. However, given the modest discriminatory performance observed, these proteins should currently be considered exploratory markers that warrant further validation in larger and independent cohorts.
