Abstract
Turbulence in protoplanetary disks plays a crucial role in the evolution of disk structures and the planet formation process therein. However, the strength of the turbulence remains unclear in young, embedded disks surrounded by infalling envelopes. In this paper, we present the first direct measurement of the nonthermal velocity dispersion within the embedded disk around HL Tau, which possesses a dusty disk with multiple rings and gap structures but is still associated with infalling gas flows from an envelope. Using ALMA archival data of the H 2 ​ CO \text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{CO} emission, we measured the local line width through a parametric model fitting that accounts for the contribution of Keplerian shear motion. After subtracting the thermal component, the nonthermal velocity dispersion is ∼ 0.15 ​ km ​ s − 1 \sim\!0.15~\mathrm{km\ s^{-1}} on average over radii of 80 ​ – ​ 180 ​ au 80\mbox{--}180~\mathrm{au} , and it slightly increases with radius. The estimated nonthermal motions correspond to a turbulent mach number of ℳ ∼ 0.4 \mathcal{M}\!\sim\!0.4 or a viscous α \alpha value of α ∼ 0.16 \alpha\!\sim\!0.16 , assuming that it is entirely caused by turbulence and α ∼ ℳ 2 \alpha\!\sim\!\mathcal{M}^{2} . Our analysis also suggests that the H 2 ​ CO \text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{CO} emission traces near the disk midplane ( z ≲ 0.1 ​ R z\lesssim 0.1R ). Turbulence driven by the gravitational instability or infall from the envelope most naturally explains the large nonthermal motions, considering the large disk mass and associated infalling streamers. The strong turbulence measured in the outer disk, in contrast to the vertically settled inner dusty disk, suggests a pronounced radial variation in the turbulence strength and/or an anisotropic nature of the turbulence within the disk.

I
Introduction

Protoplanetary disks are expected to be turbulent due to the magneto-rotational instability or hydrodynamic instabilities
(
G. Lesur et al. 2023
)
. The strength of turbulence is a crucial parameter in planet formation models. The evolution of solid particles within disks depends on the turbulence strength
(
T. Birnstiel et al. 2016
)
. Planetesimal formation via the streaming instability requires the concentration of dust grains that have settled toward the disk midplane, whereas strong turbulent mixing inhibits this process
(
J. Dra̧żkowska & C. P. Dullemond 2018
)
. The turbulence in disks does not only affect dynamics of solid particles, but also influence chemical processes within disks
(
K. Furuya & Y. Aikawa 2014
)
. Therefore, constraining strength of turbulence and its driving mechanisms in disks is crucial for understanding of disk evolution and planet formation processes.

Turbulence in protoplanetary disks is described by the dimensionless parameter
\alpha
in the context of the viscous
\alpha
-disk model
(
N. I. Shakura & R. A. Sunyaev 1973
)
. In this framework, the kinetic viscosity
\nu
, as a product of turbulence, is expressed with
\alpha
as
\nu=\alpha c_{\mathrm{s}}H
, where
c_{\mathrm{s}}
is the isothermal sound speed, and
H
is the gas pressure scale height. Although deriving
\alpha
directly from observations is difficult, there are a few ways to infer
\alpha
through a conversion from observed quantities
(
G. P. Rosotti 2023
)
.

Several studies have observationally investigated the strength of turbulence on scales of
\sim\!30
–
500~\mathrm{au}
in disks in Class
\mathrm{II}
sources (or T Tauri stars;
t_{\mathrm{age}}\!\!\sim\!1
–
5~\mathrm{Myr}
). The most direct way is to measure the turbulent line width with molecular line observations. Direct measurements of nonthermal broadening of line emission result in a typical upper limit of
\sim\!5\mbox{--}10\%
of the isothermal sound speed in four disks
(
A. M. Hughes et al. 2011
;
K. M. Flaherty et al. 2017
;
K. M. Flaherty et al. 2018
;
K. Flaherty et al. 2020
;
R. Teague et al. 2016
;
R. Teague et al. 2018b
, e.g.,)
, and detections of strong turbulence (
v_{\mathrm{turb}}\sim 0.2\mbox{--}0.7~c_{\mathrm{s}}
) in two disks of DM Tau and IM Lup
(
S. Guilloteau et al. 2012
;
K. Flaherty et al. 2020
;
T. Paneque-Carreño et al. 2024
;
K. Flaherty et al. 2024
;
C. Hardiman et al. 2026
)
. These quantities can be equated to the dimensionless parameter
\alpha
assuming
\alpha\sim\mathcal{M}^{2}=(\delta v_{\mathrm{turb}}/c_{\mathrm{s}}/\sqrt{2})^{2}

(
C. Pinte et al. 2022
)
, although it should be noted that this conversion is not necessarily always valid
(
G. Lesur et al. 2023
)
. These estimated turbulent velocities correspond to
\alpha\lesssim 10^{-2}
–
10^{-3}
in the weak turbulence case and
\alpha\sim 0.1
in the strong turbulence case.

The vertical thickness of the dusty disks has also been used as an indirect way to probe the strength of turbulence via dust diffusion
(
C. Pinte et al. 2016
)
. With this method, dust scale heights of
H_{\mathrm{d}}/R<0.05
much smaller than typical gas pressure scale heights were suggested in the Class
\mathrm{II}
disk of Oph 163131, which correspond to
\alpha\!\sim\!10^{-4}
or less
(
M. Villenave et al. 2022
)
. With similar methods, the dust scale heights were estimated in the disk of HD 163296, and moderate to strong turbulence of
\alpha\lesssim 10^{-3}
and
\alpha\gtrsim 10^{-2}
was inferred with a radial variation
(
S. Ohashi & A. Kataoka 2019
;
K. Doi & A. Kataoka 2021
;
K. Doi & A. Kataoka 2023
, e.g.,)
. More recent studies expand these analyses to a sample of Class
\mathrm{II}
disks, reporting that turbulence is typically
\alpha\lesssim 10^{-4}\mbox{--}10^{-3}
in outer disks
(
M. Villenave et al. 2025
;
H. Jiang et al. 2025
)
.

While these previous studies offer strong constraints on the strength of turbulence in Class
\mathrm{II}
disks, the turbulence strength in even younger disks around Class 0 or I protostars (
t_{\mathrm{age}}\!\!\lesssim\!0.1
–
0.5~\mathrm{Myr}
) remains unclear. Recent discoveries of substructures in these young disks hint at planet formation at this early evolutionary stage
(
P. D. Sheehan et al. 2020
;
N. Ohashi et al. 2023
, e.g.,)
, emphasizing the importance of the disks around protostars as the site of planet formation. Therefore, it is crucial to measure turbulence in such protostellar disks.

HL Tau is a nearby protostar located in the Taurus molecular cloud
(
P. A. B. Galli et al. 2018
,
d\!\sim\!147~\mathrm{pc}
;)
. It has been considered as a Class I/
\mathrm{II}
source, i.e., being at a late accretion phase, and is still embedded in an infalling envelope
(
C. Mullin et al. 2024
)
. This source also exhibits a strong molecular jet and outflow
(
S. Cabrit et al. 1996
;
M. Takami et al. 2007
)
, and is associated with anisotropic inflows connected to the disk on a scale of
\sim\!300~\mathrm{au}
, as seen in the
\text{HCO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{+}}
(3–2) emission
(
H.-W. Yen et al. 2019a
)
. HL Tau possesses a dusty disk showing clear multiple gap and ring structures
(
ALMA. Partnership et al. 2015
)
.
A previous study conducted by
C. Pinte et al. 2016
found that the dust scale height is as small as
H_{\mathrm{d}}/R<0.01
and estimated the strength of turbulence within the dusty rings to be
\alpha\!\sim\!3\times 10^{-4}
. More recently,
H. Yang et al. 2025
used the polarized dust emission and derived similar small dust scale heights at radii of
\sim\!40\mbox{--}120~\mathrm{au}
.

In this work, we evaluate the strength of turbulence in the outer disk (
r\gtrsim 80~\mathrm{au}
) of HL Tau using ALMA archival data of the
\mathrm{H_{2}CO}
line through measurements of the nonthermal line broadening. The two methods using the dust scale height and the nonthermal motion are complementary because the dust emission is generally sensitive to the inner disk while the molecular line can trace the outer portion of the disk.
Measurements of the nonthermal velocity dispersion in embedded disks are challenging due to small gaseous disk size and central stellar mass, and contamination of infalling envelopes and molecular outflows. However, the relatively large disk with a moderate inclination angle of
i\!\sim\!47^{\circ}

(
ALMA. Partnership et al. 2015
)
and its large protostellar mass make HL Tau a suitable target for the first precise measurement of nonthermal velocity dispersions in embedded disks. Previous observations revealed spatial and velocity structures of several molecular lines in HL Tau
(
A. Garufi et al. 2021
;
A. Garufi et al. 2022
)
. In these observations, the
\mathrm{H_{2}CO}
line is found to isolate the disk from the infalling envelope, while other molecular lines, such as CO,
\mathrm{HCO^{+}}
and CS, trace the envelope or outflow. By utilizing the
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{CO}
emission from the HL Tau disk, we demonstrates the capability of measurements of the nonthermal velocity dispersion toward protostellar disks with a reasonable selection of the molecular tracer.

This paper is structured as follows. Section
II
presents the data reduction process and observational data. Section
III
describes the parametric model fitting approach, and then Section
IV
shows the results of the model fitting. Section
V
presents an analysis to estimate the emission height. Section
VI
discusses the interpretation of the results, including potential sources of uncertainties and possible physical mechanisms that can explain the observed nonthermal velocity dispersions. Section
VII
summarizes the conclusions of this paper. Additionally, Appendix discusses the accuracy and robustness of the model fitting approach.

II.1
Data Reduction

We analyzed ALMA archival data of the
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{CO}
(
3_{1,2}\mbox{--}2_{1,1}
) line (225.697775 GHz; CDMS) of HL Tau (2018.1.01037.S; PI: L. Podio)
(
A. Garufi et al. 2021
;
A. Garufi et al. 2022
)
. The details of the observations were presented in
A. Garufi et al. 2021
. Observational setup is briefly summarized below. The data were taken in Frequency Division Mode (FDM) during 2018 October 28 to November 3. The 1.2 mm continuum emission was observed within a spectral window with a bandwidth of
1.875~\mathrm{GHz}
and spectral resolution of
0.976~\mathrm{MHz}
. The
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{CO}
(
3_{1,2}\mbox{--}2_{1,1}
) line was observed in a spectral window with a bandwidth of
58.6~\mathrm{MHz}
and a channel spacing of
122~\mathrm{kHz}
, down to which the original channel spacing was reduced with a channel averaging factor of two. This correlator setup resulted in a velocity resolution of
\sim\!0.187~\mathrm{km\ s^{-1}}
at the frequency of the
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{CO}
line. The data were calibrated with the standard ALMA pipeline calibration with Common Astronomy Software Application
(
J. P. McMullin et al. 2007
, CASA;)
version 5.4.0. The bandpass and amplitude calibrations were performed with the quasar J0423
-
0120, and the phase calibration was conducted with the quasar J0510
-
1800. We performed self-calibration on the phase with the continuum emission. The self-calibration process was iterated four times with decreasing solution intervals of
solint = inf
,
inf
,
90s
and
30s
from the first to last iterations. All scans were averaged in the first round, but not in the subsequent iterations. Then, the solutions were applied to the
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{CO}
line data.

In addition, the self-calibrated
\text{HCO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{+}}
(
3\mbox{--}2
) line data (267.557633 GHz) presented by
H.-W. Yen et al. 2019a
were revisited for comparison. The details of the observations and data calibration process, including the self-calibration, were described in
H.-W. Yen et al. 2019a
. For brief summary, the data were calibrated with the pipeline calibration with CASA version 4.7.0. Self-calibration on the phase was performed for the continuum data, and then the solution was applied to the line data.

We performed imaging using the
tclean
task in CASA version 6.5.2. Briggs weighting with a robust parameter of 0.5 was applied to both
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{CO}
and
\text{HCO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{+}}
data. The resulting angular resolutions of the
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{CO}
and
\text{HCO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{+}}
image cubes are
0\farcs 325\times 0\farcs 275~(-1.8^{\circ})
and
0\farcs 095\times 0\farcs 088~(20.2^{\circ})
, and their rms noise levels are
1.68~\mathrm{mJy\ beam^{-1}}
and
2.03~\mathrm{mJy\ beam^{-1}}
, respectively.
The channel width of the
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{CO}
and
\text{HCO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{+}}
images was set to
0.163~\mathrm{km\ s^{-1}}
, which was the finest spacing allowed for the
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{CO}
data.

III
Parametric Modeling

The microscopic nonthermal gas motion can be derived from the line widths of the observed spectra. The total line width of the observed
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{CO}
spectrum at a given spatial coordinate is, however, dominated by Keplerian shear motion due to the limited spatial resolution of the data and small disk size. Hence, we employed a parametric model fitting approach to measure the nonthermal velocity dispersion taking into account the Keplerian shear motion. In this method, we fitted velocity channel maps of the
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{CO}
emission with a parametric disk model to constrain the physical parameters that determine the velocity structures of the emission. The local line width, including both thermal and nonthermal broadening, was parameterized as a power-law function of radius in the model assuming no vertical variations. The temperature structure of the HL Tau disk has been well characterized in previous studies
(
S. Okuzumi et al. 2016
;
H.-W. Yen et al. 2019a
)
. Hence, we evaluated the thermal component using the temperature profiles constrained in previous work. Then, the nonthermal velocity dispersion was derived by subtracting the thermal component from the local line width that was determined by the parametric model fitting.

In this subsection, we describe the disk model that is used to fit the observational data. We employ the standard viscous disk model
(
N. I. Shakura & R. A. Sunyaev 1973
)
. A set of formulae is given in cylindrical coordinates
(R,z,\phi)
. The surface density of the
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{CO}
molecule is expressed as follows assuming a constant molecular abundance throughout the disk:

\displaystyle N_{\mathrm{g}}(R)=N_{\mathrm{g,c}}\left(\frac{R}{R_{\mathrm{g,c}}}\right)^{-\gamma_{\mathrm{g}}}\exp\left\{-\left(\frac{R}{R_{\mathrm{g,c}}}\right)^{2-\gamma_{\mathrm{g}}}\right\}.

(1)

The vertical structure of the gas is assumed to follow the hydrostatic equilibrium:

\displaystyle\rho_{\mathrm{g}}(R)=\frac{\Sigma_{\mathrm{g}}(R)}{\sqrt{2\pi}H}\exp\left\{-\frac{z^{2}}{2H^{2}}\right\}.

(2)

Here,
H\equiv c_{\mathrm{s}}/\Omega
is the gas pressure scale height, where
c_{\mathrm{s}}
is the isothermal sound speed and
\Omega
is the angular velocity. The isothermal sound speed is defined as
c_{\mathrm{s}}=\sqrt{k_{\mathrm{b}}T/\mu m_{\mathrm{H}}}
with the Boltzmann constant
k_{\mathrm{b}}
, the gas temperature
T
, the mean molecular weight
\mu=2.37
and the weight of the hydrogen atom
m_{\mathrm{H}}
. The gas pressure scale height is calculated with the gas temperature and rotational velocity field described below. As found in Equation (
2
),
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{CO}
molecules are assumed to be distributed near the disk midplane, since the velocity channel maps suggest the emission arises from layers close to the disk midplane. However, we note that contributions from higher altitudes are also accounted for in the model, as the modeled disk has a vertical thickness. The gas kinetic temperature is assumed to follow a power-law function:

\displaystyle T_{\mathrm{g}}(R)=T_{\mathrm{g,0}}\left(\frac{R}{100~\mathrm{au}}\right)^{-q_{\mathrm{g}}}.

(3)

The gas disk is assumed to be vertically isothermal. Then, the gas kinetic temperature
T_{\mathrm{g}}
is adopted for the excitation temperature
T_{\mathrm{ex}}
of the
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{CO}
emission assuming the local thermal equilibrium (LTE).

The model includes a geometrically thin dust layer to take into account the effect of subtraction of the optically thick dust continuum. We model the dust layer separately from the gaseous component to avoid assumptions about the gas-to-dust mass ratio and molecular abundance, both of which are poorly constrained. The radial profile of the optical depth of the dust layer is described as

\displaystyle\tau_{\nu,\mathrm{d}}=\tau_{\mathrm{d,c}}\left(\frac{R}{R_{\mathrm{d,c}}}\right)^{-\gamma_{\mathrm{d}}}\exp\left\{-\left(\frac{R}{R_{\mathrm{d,c}}}\right)^{2-\gamma_{\mathrm{d}}}\right\}.

(4)

This form is motivated by the viscous disk model and an assumption of a constant dust absorption coefficient across the disk, leading to
\tau_{\nu,\mathrm{d}}\propto\Sigma_{\mathrm{d}}
. The dust temperature profile is assumed to be the same form as that for gas but is modeled independently with
T_{\mathrm{d,0}}
and
q_{\mathrm{d}}
. For simplicity, the dust layer is assumed to have smooth density and temperature profiles, ignoring the ring and gap structures that are known in HL Tau, which is sufficient for the current purpose of including the effect of the subtraction of the dust continuum emission at the current spatial resolution.

The velocity field of the gas is assumed to follow Keplerian motion including the vertical height:

\displaystyle\frac{v_{\phi}^{2}}{R}=\frac{GM_{\ast}R}{(R^{2}+z^{2})^{3/2}},

(5)

where
M_{\ast}
is the protostellar mass,
G
is the gravitational constant, and
v_{\phi}
is the rotational velocity. No systemic motions in the radial or vertical directions are adopted. The line-of-sight (LOS) velocity is calculated based on the projection of the rotational velocity onto the plane of the sky (POS) as

\displaystyle V_{\mathrm{LOS}}=v_{\phi}\cos\phi\sin i,

(6)

where
i
is the inclination angle of the disk. Then, rotation by the position angle (PA;
\theta_{\mathrm{PA}}
) and a shift of the disk center from the image center
(\delta x_{0},\delta y_{0})
are applied on POS coordinates.

The line profile function at each position is calculated as

\displaystyle\psi(v)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}\Delta V}\exp\left\{{-\frac{(V_{\mathrm{LOS}}-v)^{2}}{\Delta V^{2}}}\right\},

(7)

where
\Delta V
is the local line width and
\mathrm{FWHM}=2\sqrt{\ln 2}\Delta V
, considering the Doppler line broadening. The local line width is modeled with a power-law function of radius

\displaystyle\Delta V(R)=\Delta V_{0}\left(\frac{R}{100~\mathrm{au}}\right)^{l}.

(8)

This local line width accounts for all sources of line broadening except for the Keplerian shearing and is independent of the assumed gas temperature in the model. This allows us to later assess uncertainties in the gas temperature and thermal line broadening that cannot be fully considered in the simple parametric model. All the model parameters are summarized in Table
1
.

III.2
Radiative Transfer with Channel-based Three-layer Approximation

In an inclined disk with finite thickness, gas located at different radii can lie along a common LOS, leading to velocity differences that contribute to line broadening. This velocity shear due to the thickness of the disk is more significant at smaller radii and can be negligible when the radial extent of the disk is large
(
T. Paneque-Carreño et al. 2024
)
. However, given the small disk size of HL Tau, the contribution of this velocity shear to the line broadening needs to be considered.

Although the radiative transfer calculation is required to account for the effects of disk thickness, solving the full radiative transfer is computationally expensive. To enable efficient exploration of a wide parameter space while accounting for disk thickness, we introduced the channel-based three-layer approximation, as illustrated in Figure
3
. First, the gas along a given LOS is divided into cells, each of which corresponds to a distinct velocity channel bin. Because the velocity ranges of these cells do not overlap, they can be treated independently in the radiative transfer calculation. The column density of each cell is then computed by integrating the gas density along the LOS, approximating each cell by a thin layer in every velocity channel.
The gas temperature of the layer is computed as the density-weighted mean temperature. This procedure is applied separately to gas located in front of and behind the dust layer. In this framework, line broadening due to the disk thickness is naturally incorporated, as velocity differences arising from the vertical structure are mapped into distinct velocity channels. Simultaneously, this approximation allows us to solve the radiative transfer analytically by considering two gas layers ahead and behind the geometrically thin dust layer at each velocity channel.

The optical depth of the molecular line of the gas layers is calculated assuming LTE.
At each frequency and spatial coordinate, the radiative transfer equation is written as

\displaystyle I_{\mathrm{\nu,line}}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})=

\displaystyle B_{\nu}(T_{\mathrm{bg}})e^{-\tau_{\nu,\mathrm{gr}}-\tau_{\mathrm{\nu,d}}-\tau_{\mathrm{\nu,gf}}}

\displaystyle+B_{\nu}(T_{\mathrm{gr}})\left(1-e^{-\tau_{\mathrm{\nu,gr}}}\right)e^{-\tau_{\mathrm{\nu,d}}-\tau_{\mathrm{\nu,gf}}}

\displaystyle+B_{\nu}(T_{\mathrm{d}})\left(1-e^{-\tau_{\mathrm{\nu,d}}}\right)e^{-\tau_{\mathrm{\nu,gf}}}

\displaystyle+B_{\nu}(T_{\mathrm{gf}})\left(1-e^{-\tau_{\mathrm{\nu,gf}}}\right)

\displaystyle-B_{\nu}(T_{\mathrm{bg}}).

(9)

Here, (
x^{\prime}
,
y^{\prime}
) is the POS coordinates. The subscriptions, d, gr and gf, denote quantities for dust, gas on the rear side of the dust layer, and gas on the fore side of the dust layer, respectively. The background temperature
T_{\mathrm{bg}}
is assumed to be
2.73~\mathrm{K}
. The line emission after continuum subtraction is calculated with

\displaystyle\Delta I_{\mathrm{\nu,line}}=I_{\mathrm{\nu,line}}-I_{\mathrm{\nu,dust}},

(10)

where the dust continuum emission is given by

\displaystyle I_{\mathrm{\nu,dust}}=\left\{B_{\nu}(T_{\mathrm{d}})-B_{\nu}(T_{\mathrm{bg}})\right\}\left(1-e^{-\tau_{\mathrm{\nu,d}}}\right).

(11)

In the signal processing for ALMA data, Hanning smoothing is applied in the lag domain prior to channel averaging, resulting in a spectral resolution slightly coarser than the channel spacing. To mimic this process, the model channel bin is first set to be three times finer than a FWHM of a smoothing function. Then, a Gaussian function with FWHM of
0.16~\mathrm{km\ s^{-1}}
, equivalent to the width of the smoothing function of the data, is convolved after solving the radiative transfer. Finally, the model channel bins are averaged down to the image channel spacing, and a Gaussian beam of the same size as that of the data is convolved.

III.3
Fitting Data

We fitted the observational data with the model using the Marcov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) method. The fitting was performed in two steps. First, we fitted the continuum emission obtained from line-free channels with a geometrically thin dusty layer model to determine the dust optical depth
\tau_{\mathrm{d,c}}
. The other parameters of the dusty disk are fixed based on previous studies.
We adopted parameters for the dust surface density distribution
(R_{\mathrm{c,d}},\gamma_{\mathrm{d}})=(84.2~\mathrm{au},-0.2)
from
W. Kwon et al. 2015
, which used the same viscous disk model, with correction for the updated distance. We used the dust temperature profile
T_{\mathrm{d}}(R)=22.5(R/100~\mathrm{au})^{-0.57}~\mathrm{K}
derived in
S. Okuzumi et al. 2016
. Then, we fitted the channel maps of the
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{CO}
emission with the model described above using the derived
\tau_{\mathrm{d,c}}
and the other parameters of the dust layer. We allowed the parameters of the gas surface density and temperature profiles to vary during the fitting to match the intensity of the model and observed image cubes. However, the derived gas surface density and temperature profile would not be very reliable because only a single rotational transition is used in the fitting. Therefore, uncertainties of the gas temperature and thermal line broadening were discussed separately.

Several velocity channels were excluded from the fitting procedure. The
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{CO}
emission exhibits negative intensities near the disk center in the velocity range of
5.92\mbox{--}7.71~\mathrm{km\ s^{-1}}
, as noted in Section
II.2
. These channels were therefore excluded, as they are likely affected by absorption from the foreground envelope. In addition, a careful inspection of the observed spectra indicates that channels between
8.86~\mathrm{km\ s^{-1}}
and
10.32~\mathrm{km\ s^{-1}}
are likely contaminated by emission associated with an infalling streamer, which is discussed in more detail in Section
IV
. Hence, these velocity channels were also excluded from the fitting. We note that the line velocity dispersion can still be reasonably constrained despite the exclusion of some velocity channels, since the spatial extent of the emission in individual channel maps retains information on the line broadening
(
S. Guilloteau et al. 2012
;
K. M. Flaherty et al. 2015
;
K. Flaherty et al. 2020
)
.

The MCMC sampling was performed using the Python package
emcee

(
D. Foreman-Mackey et al. 2013
)
with 26 walkers and 2500 steps, with the first 2000 steps discarded as burn-in. All fixed and best-fit parameters with their prior ranges in the fitting for the
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{CO}
emission are summarized in Table
1
.

III.4
Measurement of Nonthermal Velocity Dispersion

The nonthermal velocity dispersion was derived by subtracting the thermal component from the local line width
\Delta V
, which was constrained with the model fitting, using a relationship of

\displaystyle\Delta V=\sqrt{v_{\mathrm{th}}^{2}+v_{\mathrm{nth}}^{2}},

(12)

where
v_{\mathrm{th}}
and
v_{\mathrm{nth}}
are the thermal and nonthermal velocity dispersions, respectively. The thermal component is defined as

\displaystyle v_{\mathrm{th}}=\sqrt{\frac{2kT}{m_{\mathrm{mol}}m_{\mathrm{H}}}},

(13)

where
m_{\mathrm{mol}}
is the molecular weight.

As noted earlier, the gas temperature inferred from the current model fitting is not well constrained, as only a single transition is used. Therefore, to estimate the thermal component, we adopted radial temperature profiles derived in previous observational studies.
S. Okuzumi et al. 2016
derived a dust midplane temperature profile of
T(R)=22.5(R/100~\mathrm{au})^{-0.57}~\mathrm{K}
using optically thick dust continuum emission.
H.-W. Yen et al. 2019a
performed an LTE analysis of the
\text{HCO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{+}}

J=3\mbox{--}2
and
J=1\mbox{--}0
transitions, finding the former line to be optically thick and deriving a kinetic temperature profile of
T_{\mathrm{k}}(R)=28.1(R/100~\mathrm{au})^{-0.48}~\mathrm{K}
. Because the
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{CO}
emission is expected to trace a height between the disk midplane and the more optically thick
\text{HCO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{+}}
emitting layer, we adopted these dust- and
\text{HCO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{+}}
-based temperature profiles as lower and upper bounds on the gas temperature, respectively, considering the typical disk thermal structure that monotonically increases with increasing heights. The upper limit temperature is also comparable to the temperature estimated by
O. M. Guerra-Alvarado et al. 2024
using the optically thick 0.45 mm dust continuum emission, which traces small dust grains that are well coupled to gas and thus could arise from a slightly elevated layer.

C. Carrasco-González et al. 2019
reported a high dust temperature profile of
T(R)=47(R/100)^{-0.5}
based on multiwavelength dust continuum analyses. This temperature is substantially higher than other estimates of the dust temperature and also exceeds the gas temperature inferred from the
\text{HCO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{+}}
emission. Such a discrepancy may arise from a high dust albedo assumed in their modeling. This dust temperature profile requires a vertical temperature inversion because the gas temperature estimated with the
\text{HCO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{+}}
emission, which presumably traces a higher altitude,
is lower. Furthermore, two dimensional temperature measurements of Class
\mathrm{II}
disks using optically thick molecular lines suggest that disk temperatures are typically below
30~\mathrm{K}
at radii of
R=100\mbox{--}200~\mathrm{au}
and heights of
z/R\leq 0.1

(
C. J. Law et al. 2021
)
, corresponding to the region predominantly traced by the
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{CO}
emission (see Section
V
for a quantitative discussion of the
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{CO}
emission height). This also supports a characteristic temperature range of
\!\sim\!20\mbox{--}30~\mathrm{K}
at
R=100~\mathrm{au}
. For these reasons, we do not adopt the temperature profile of
C. Carrasco-González et al. 2019
when deriving the nonthermal velocity dispersion. The impact of adopting this higher temperature profile is discussed separately in Section
VI.1
.

Emission Height

Figure 9:
Representative channel maps of the
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{CO}

,

and

(color-shaded area). The solid curves indicate the isovelocity curves.

Molecular distributions within disks are usually vertically stratified
(
C. J. Law et al. 2021
)
. The height of the emitting layer of the
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{CO}
emission provides crucial information about the origin of the nonthermal velocity dispersion. The emission surface can be inferred from the horse-shoe-like shape of the line emission from a Keplerian disk in velocity channel maps
(
C. Pinte et al. 2018
)
. Qualitatively, the morphology of the
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{CO}
emission, which appears to follow a single isovelocity curve in the individual channel maps, suggests that the emission originates from layers close to the disk midplane, as noted in Section
II.2
. In this section, the emission height of the
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{CO}
emission is more quantitatively estimated by comparing expected isovelocity areas and the observed velocity channel maps.

We computed Keplerian isovelocity areas for three representative emission heights of
z/R=0.1,0.2
and 0.3, assuming a protostellar mass of
2.34~M_{\odot}
, an inclination angle of
45^{\circ}
, and a channel spacing of
0.163~\mathrm{km\ s^{-1}}
. Then, we convolved the isovelocity areas with a Gaussian beam assuming a uniform intensity distribution within the isovelocity area. The maximum disk radius is set to
R=180~\mathrm{au}
so that the extent of the beam-convolved intensity distributions is comparable to that of the observed emission.

Figure
9
compares the calculated isovelocity areas at different heights of
z/R=0.1
,
0.2
, and
0.3
to the observed channel maps. The color shaded area corresponds to regions where the intensity after beam convolution is more than
3\sigma
assuming the peak S/N is 15 in each velocity channel, which matches the observed
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{CO}
emission. The model with
z/R=0.1
is consistent with the observed velocity channel maps. In contrast, the model with
z/R=0.2
exhibits slightly larger emitting area than the
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{CO}
emission. At the velocity of
4.94~\mathrm{km\ s^{-1}}
, the cavity of the horse-shoe structure in the model is more filled than in the observations. In addition, the model emission extends beyond the observed emission in the vertical direction at velocities of
5.43
and
5.92~\mathrm{km\ s^{-1}}
. We note that geometrically thin emitting layers are assumed in these simple calculations. Considering the thickness of each layer and further line broadening due to the thermal and nonthermal motions, the emitting area of the model with
z/R=0.2
would become even broader. The model with
z/R=0.3
covers much wider areas, and deviates from the shape of the observed emission more clearly. These comparisons suggest that the
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{CO}
emission originates primarily from heights of
z/R\lesssim 0.1
.

A previous study has shown that less abundant molecules tend to trace deeper layers close to the disk midplane, based on analyses of CO isotopologues
(
C. J. Law et al. 2021
)
. More recently,
T. Paneque-Carreño et al. 2023
reported that
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{CO}
emission typically arises from disk heights of
z/R\lesssim 0.1
, based on a sample of several Class
\mathrm{II}
sources. These observational results also support our inference that the
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{CO}
emission originates from
z/R\lesssim 0.1
.

We note that the increase in emission height due to optical depth and consequent line broadening due to the elevated layers are inherently accounted for in our model through the radiative transfer calculation. The line broadening arising from elevated layers and that from velocity dispersion exhibit different spatial dependencies. Because our fitting utilizes the full channel maps, this difference helps break the degeneracy between the two effects, allowing the local line width to be robustly constrained.

VI
Discussion

VI.1
Uncertainties

The uncertainty of the nonthermal velocity dispersion is estimated to be
\sim\!30~\mathrm{m\ s^{-1}}
based on statistical errors of
\Delta V
and uncertainties in the gas temperature, which are derived from a temperature range of the outer disk of HL Tau reported in previous studies, as discussed in Section
III.4
.
C. Carrasco-González et al. 2019
derived a significantly higher dust temperature than the temperature range suggested in other studies. When adopting their high dust temperature profile, thermal velocities are calculated to be
\sim\!0.14\mbox{--}0.17~\mathrm{km\ s^{-1}}
at radii of
80\mbox{--}180~\mathrm{au}
, and consequently the nonthermal velocity decreases to
0.10\mbox{--}0.14~\mathrm{km\ s^{-1}}
. Nevertheless, the nonthermal component of
\sim\!0.2c_{\mathrm{s}}
is still required, where
c_{\mathrm{s}}
also increases to
\sim\!0.5~\mathrm{km\ s^{-1}}
as the temperature increases.

In addition to these uncertainties, another possible uncertainty is the systemic error inherited from our modeling approach. To examine the accuracy of the current model fitting approach, we applied the method to mock observational data generated using full radiative transfer models with known turbulent velocities. We fitted the mock data with and without channel masking, which restricts the fit to only a subset of the velocity channels, to assess the robustness of the fitting approach and impact of the channel masking separately. In addition, we also tested how the
uv
sampling and imaging with CLEAN affect the fitting result with an ALMA simulation. Details of these tests are presented in Appendix
A
, and the results of measurements of the nonthermal velocity dispersions using the mock data are provided in Figure
11
in the appendix. In these experiments, which adopt the same density structure of the disk, the nonthermal velocity is recovered to within an accuracy of
\sim\!20~\mathrm{m\ s^{-1}}
when
v_{\mathrm{nth}}=0.3\mbox{--}0.5~c_{\mathrm{s}}
, regardless of the
uv
sampling and channel masking. Hence, the measured nonthermal velocity dispersion of
0.6~c_{\mathrm{s}}
appears robust against these uncertainties.

VI.2
Origin of the large nonthermal velocity dispersion

Our analysis suggests large nonthermal velocity dispersions of
v_{\mathrm{nth}}\!\sim\!0.6~c_{\mathrm{s}}
near the midplane of the outer disk of HL Tau. In this section, we discuss the physical origin of the large nonthermal velocity dispersions.

VI.2.1
Turbulence

Turbulence contributes to the line broadening, as we attempted to measure it through this work. Assuming that the measured nonthermal velocity dispersion is purely due to turbulence, we obtain a turbulent Mach number
\mathcal{M}\!\sim\!0.4
in the outer disk of HL Tau. This can be further translated into the dimensionless parameter
\alpha\!\sim\!0.16
via
\alpha\!\sim\!\mathcal{M}^{2}
. Several mechanisms have been proposed as the origin of turbulence in protoplanetary disks
(
W. Lyra & O. M. Umurhan 2019
;
G. Lesur et al. 2023
)
such as the magnetorotaional instability
(
S. A. Balbus & J. F. Hawley 1991
, MRI; )
, vertical share instability
(
R. P. Nelson et al. 2013
;
M.-K. Lin & A. N. Youdin 2015
;
Y. Fukuhara et al. 2021
, VSI; )
and the gravitational instability
(
K. M. Kratter & G. Lodato 2016
, GI; )
. Infall is also a possible driving source of turbulence, as the HL Tau disk is associated with an infalling envelope and streamers
(
G. Lesur et al. 2015
;
P. Hennebelle et al. 2017
;
A. Kuznetsova et al. 2022
)
.

Local numerical simulations of MRI show that the MRI-driven turbulence can be as strong as
\mathcal{M}\!\sim\!0.4
or
\alpha\!\sim\!0.1
at high disk altitudes (
z/R>0.2
), but is typically much smaller than
0.1~c_{\mathrm{s}}
near the disk midplane (
z/R<0.1
)
(
S. Okuzumi & S. Hirose 2011
;
J. B. Simon et al. 2018
)
. The MRI can drive a strong turbulence of
\mathcal{M}\!\sim\!0.4
even near the disk midplane when the magnetic field is strongly coupled with gas in a condition nearly ideal MHD
(
X.-N. Bai & J. M. Stone 2011
;
S. Okuzumi & S. Hirose 2011
;
J. B. Simon et al. 2018
)
. However, such a condition is usually not expected in the midplane of protoplanetary disks at a large radius of
\sim\!100~\mathrm{au}
because of the low ionization degree of the disk gas.
Similarly, numerical simulations suggest that VSI drives turbulence of
\sim\!0.1~\mathrm{km\ s^{-1}}
, or
\mathcal{M}\!\sim\!0.3
at most
(
M. Flock et al. 2020
;
G. Lesur et al. 2025
)
, which is smaller than the value suggested by this work. Hence, it would be difficult to explain the observed large velocity dispersion with MRI or VSI turbulence.

GI possibly explains the large velocity dispersion. GI drives turbulence as strong as
v_{\mathrm{turb}}\!\sim\!0.2\mbox{--}0.6~c_{\mathrm{s}}
near the disk midplane
(
J.-M. Shi & E. Chiang 2014
;
A. Riols et al. 2020
)
, which matches the measured nonthermal velocity. Indeed, previous observational work suggests that the HL Tau disk is subject to GI.
The criterion whether GI occurs or not is often assessed with the Toomre
Q
parameter
(
A. Toomre 1964
)
:

\displaystyle Q=\frac{c_{\mathrm{s}}\kappa}{\pi G\Sigma},

(14)

where
\Sigma
is the gas surface density, and
\kappa
is the epicyclic frequency that is equal to the angular velocity
\Omega
in Keplerian disks.
Based on observations in the dust continuum emission, previous studies estimated the Toomre
Q
parameter to be larger than 2 at inner radii but close to 2 near the edge of the dusty disk, inferring that the outer disk may be GI unstable
(
E. Akiyama et al. 2016
;
Y. Liu et al. 2017
)
. A more robust measurement of the disk mass using an optically thin
\hphantom{{}^{\text{13}}_{\text{}}}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}{}^{\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{13}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{13}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{13}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{13}$}}}\kern 0.0pt\text{C}\hphantom{{}^{\text{17}}_{\text{}}}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{17}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{17}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{17}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{17}$}}}\kern 0.0pt\text{O}
emission suggests
Q<1.7
at radii of
50\mbox{--}100~\mathrm{au}
and that the outer disk of HL Tau is gravitationally unstable
(
A. S. Booth & J. D. Ilee 2020
)
.

The Toomre
Q
parameter generally decreases with radius, given the radial dependence of typical temperature and surface density profiles of protoplanetary disks, and Keplerian angular velocity. Consequently, it is naturally expected that a disk becomes GI unstable toward outer radii. This tendency also aligns with the fact that turbulence is as weak as
\alpha\leq 10^{-4}
in the inner disk of HL Tau
(
C. Pinte et al. 2016
;
H. Yang et al. 2025
)
, i.e., implying that the disk is gravitationally stable in the inner (low
\alpha
) region and unstable in the outer (high
\alpha
) region.

The possibility that the outer disk is gravitationally unstable is further supported by the expectation that the disk is in an increasing phase of disk mass.
The mass infall rate from the envelope to the disk in HL Tau has been estimated to be
2.2\times 10^{-6}~M_{\odot}\ \mathrm{yr}^{-1}
based on an infalling arc structure on
1000~\mathrm{au}
scale, detected in the
\text{C}\hphantom{{}^{\text{18}}_{\text{}}}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{18}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{18}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{18}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{18}$}}}\kern 0.0pt\text{O}
emission
(
H.-W. Yen et al. 2017
;
H.-W. Yen et al. 2019b
)
. The mass accretion rate onto the protostar is estimated to be
1.6\times 10^{-7}~M_{\odot}\ \mathrm{yr}^{-1}
based on a spectroscopy
(
R. J. White & L. A. Hillenbrand 2004
)
, which is smaller than the mass infall rate by an order of magnitude. Hence, the disk should grow on a timescale of
0.1~\mathrm{Myr}
to reach a GI unstable regime,
M_{\mathrm{disk}}/M_{\ast}\!\sim\!0.1
.

The mass accretion rate need not be spatially uniform across the disk, particularly when substructures are formed by pressure maxima that traps material. If the outer disk is gravitationally unstable, it is expected to sustain a higher mass accretion rate than the inner regions. In the viscous disk frame work, the mass accretion rate can be expressed as

\displaystyle\dot{M}_{\mathrm{acc}}=3\pi\Sigma\alpha\frac{c_{\mathrm{s}}^{2}}{\Omega}.

(15)

The surface density at the characteristic radius
\Sigma_{\mathrm{c}}
can be written with the disk mass as

\displaystyle\Sigma_{\mathrm{c}}=\frac{(2-\gamma)M_{\mathrm{disk}}}{2\pi R_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}}.

(16)

The mass accretion rate at the characteristic radius can be estimated using these equations.
We adopt
R_{\mathrm{c}}=100~\mathrm{au}
,
\gamma=-0.7
and
T=25~\mathrm{K}
from our best-fit model and temperature estimates by previous work.
Assuming
\alpha\!\sim\!0.1
and
M_{\mathrm{disk}}=0.2~M_{\odot}

(
A. S. Booth & J. D. Ilee 2020
)
, we obtain
\dot{M}_{\mathrm{acc}}\!\sim\!3.3\times 10^{-6}~M_{\odot}\ \mathrm{yr}^{-1}
. This value is broadly consistent with mass accretion rates driven by angular momentum transport via GI-induced spiral structures in numerical simulations at evolutionary stages comparable to the Class I phase
(
E. I. Vorobyov & S. Basu 2007
)
, and also with the observationally estimated value of the mass infall rate from the envelope.

Gravitational instability is a self-regulating process: the development of spiral arms and the subsequent generation of (self-gravitating) turbulence drive mass accretion, which reduces the disk surface density and thereby stabilizes the disk. As a result, the instability (and turbulence) is suppressed on a timescale of the local orbital period, and renewed mass supply is required for the instability to grow again and maintain the turbulence.
For HL Tau, the orbital timescale at
R=100~\mathrm{au}
is
t_{\mathrm{rot}}=2\pi\sqrt{R^{3}/(GM_{\ast})}\!\sim\!650~\mathrm{yr}
assuming the protostellar mass of
2.34~M_{\odot}
. Combined with the mass accretion rate estimated above, this orbital timescale implies a mass of
\sim\!2~M_{\mathrm{Jup}}
is removed from the outer disk before the spirals decay and the outer disk temporarily returns a GI-stable state. A comparable mass infall rate from the envelope onto the disk, estimated to be
2.2\times 10^{-6}~M_{\odot}\ \mathrm{yr}^{-1}
, suggests that the depleted mass can be replenished on a timescale of
\sim\!1000~\mathrm{yr}
, allowing the disk to become a GI-unstable phase again. This recurrence timescale is much shorter than the typical lifetime of Class I objects
(
N. J. Evans et al. 2009
)
, which would make such states naturally observable.

Infall from the envelope onto the disk and its dynamical perturbation may be another possible origin of strong turbulence. Hydrodynamical simulations show that accretion shock between the disk and infalling gas generates spiral density waves, leading to non-negligible Reynolds stress
\alpha
throughout the disk
(
G. Lesur et al. 2015
;
P. Hennebelle et al. 2017
;
A. Kuznetsova et al. 2022
)
. The Reynolds stress induced by such spiral waves can be as strong as
\alpha~\!\sim\!0.1\mbox{--}1
near the edge of the disk and propagate to smaller radii decreasing down to
\alpha\!\sim\!10^{-3}\mbox{--}10^{-4}

(
G. Lesur et al. 2015
;
P. Hennebelle et al. 2017
)
. These high
\alpha
values in the outer disk and a increasing radial trend are consistent with the results of our analysis. These theoretical studies assume disk masses and mass infall rates on the orders of
10^{-2}~M_{\odot}
and
10^{-7}~M_{\odot}\ \mathrm{yr}^{-1}
, respectively, both of which are an order of magnitude smaller than those estimated for HL Tau. Nevertheless, when considering the ratio of the mass infall rate to the disk mass (
\dot{M_{\mathrm{inf}}}/M_{\mathrm{disk}}
), the parameters explored in the numerical simulations are comparable to the values for HL Tau. Hence, the infalling gas could induce density perturbations of similar magnitude to those seen in the simulations and potentially drive turbulence of
\alpha\!\sim\!0.1
in the outer disk of HL Tau.

A caveat of both GI- and infall-driven turbulence scenarios is that no clear spiral structures have been identified in the HL Tau disk, even though they are expected to drive the strong velocity perturbations and angular momentum transport. One possible explanation is that dust grains have already drifted inward, and spiral structures at outer radii may not be detectable in the dust continuum. Velocity perturbations associated with GI-induced spirals are typically confined to spatial scales of
\lesssim 10~\mathrm{au}
at a radius of
\sim\!100~\mathrm{au}

(
C. Hall et al. 2020
;
J. Sai et al. 2025
;
M. Barraza-Alfaro et al. 2025
)
. The current angular resolution of the
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{CO}
data is therefore insufficient to resolve such features, even if they are present. Future observations at high angular resolutions in molecular lines would shed light on the presence or absence of spiral structures in the outer disk.

VI.2.2
Non-turbulence origin

One may wonder whether the disk wind contributes to the line broadening rather than turbulence, as the bipolar outflow has been observed in HL Tau
(
S. Cabrit et al. 1996
;
M. Takami et al. 2007
)
. The velocity structures of the
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{CO}
emission mostly follow Keplerian rotation of the disk and do not show any features of outflowing gas motions, as seen in Section
II.2
and Section
IV
. The base of the disk wind could contaminate and contribute to the total line width. However, the base of the disk wind is typically located at the FUV ionized surface at
z/H\gtrsim 5

(
X.-N. Bai & J. M. Stone 2013
;
O. Gressel et al. 2015
;
G. Lesur et al. 2023
)
,
which is much higher than the layer traced by the
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{CO}
emission. Furthermore, it is expected that the gas density at the wind base is too low to contribute the line emission. Hence, it is unlikely that the disk wind is responsible for the observed nonthermal velocity dispersion.

Radial gas flows within the disk plane are naturally expected in the accretion disk, which could contribute to the line broadening but is typically negligible
(
C. Pinte et al. 2022
)
. In the frame work of the viscous disk model
(
N. I. Shakura & R. A. Sunyaev 1973
)
, the radial accretion velocity within the disk is expressed as

\displaystyle v_{R}=\frac{3}{2}\alpha\left(\frac{H}{R}\right)^{2}v_{\mathrm{Kep}},

(17)

where
v_{\mathrm{Kep}}
is the Keplerian velocity. For HL Tau, the Keplerian velocity is
\sim\!4.6~\mathrm{km\ s^{-1}}
at a radius of
100~\mathrm{au}
. Even assuming a highly turbulent case of
\alpha\!\sim\!0.1
, the accretion velocity at a radius of
100~\mathrm{au}
is estimated to be
v_{R}\!\sim\!7~\mathrm{m\ s^{-1}}
with the typical aspect ratio of the disk of
H/R\!\sim\!0.1
, which is much smaller than the suggested nonthermal velocity dispersion.

As the HL Tau disk possesses multiple rings in the dusty disk and possibly in the gas disk
(
ALMA. Partnership et al. 2015
;
H.-W. Yen et al. 2019a
)
, non-Keplerian motion may be induced by the change in the pressure gradient around gaps and contribute to the line broadening
(
R. Teague et al. 2018a
)
. The non-Keplerian motions caused by the pressure gradient term are typically a few percent of the projected Keplerian speed
(
R. Teague et al. 2018a
;
C. Pinte et al. 2022
)
. In the case of HL Tau, the velocity of
2\mbox{--}3\%
of the projected Keplerian at outer gap locations of
R=80~\mathrm{au}
and
100~\mathrm{au}

(
I. W. Stephens et al. 2023
)
is
\sim\!0.06\mbox{--}0.1~\mathrm{km\ s^{-1}}
, which is insufficient to explain the observed nonthermal line broadening alone. Furthermore, in the HL Tau disk, gaps possibly causing these velocity deviations have been confirmed only at radii smaller than
100~\mathrm{au}
, while the
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{CO}
emission extends up to a radius of
\sim\!200~\mathrm{au}
. Hence, our measurements that is more sensitive to the outer disk would not be significantly affected by these possible non-Keplerian motions associated with the known gaps.
