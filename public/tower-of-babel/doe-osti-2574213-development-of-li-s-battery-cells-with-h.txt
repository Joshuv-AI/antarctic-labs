Executive Summary
Lithium–sulfur (Li–S) batteries are considered one of the most promising next-generation
energy storage technologies for electric vehicles, owing to their high energy density (up
to three times greater than current lithium-ion batteries) and the low cost of sulfur
(approximately 100 times cheaper than conventional cobalt oxide). However, several
challenges hinder their commercial viability, including the electronically and ionically
insulating nature of sulfur, polysulfide dissolution, and lithium dendrite growth. These
issues significantly limit battery energy density and cycle life and must be addressed to
enable successful commercialization.
Current strategies involving porous carbon structures, additives, and electrocatalyst
engineering have not fully resolved the polysulfide dissolution problem. While polysulfide-
free cathode materials offer a promising solution, they often suffer from low sulfur content,
leading to reduced discharge capacity and sluggish reaction kinetics.
To overcome these limitations, we have developed novel soluble-polysulfide-free sulfur
cathode active materials with a high sulfur content (>50 wt%). By integrating these
cathodes with functional binders, optimized electrolyte formulations, and refined electrode
fabrication techniques, we demonstrate Li–S batteries with both high energy density and
long cycle life. This advancement brings the realization of low-cost, high-energy Li–S
batteries significantly closer to practical application.

Project Background
The Li–S battery, based on reversible lithiation/delithiation of sulfur (16Li + S8 → Li2Sn +
8Li2S), is believed to be a next-generation high-energy power source for electric vehicle
applications due to its high theoretical energy density (specific energy density of 2450 Wh
kg–1 and volumetric energy density of 2899 Wh L–1 for the cathode, 3 times that of the
traditional Li-ion battery cathode) and the low cost of sulfur. Despite the numerous
advantages, practical application of Li–S battery technology is encountering several
challenges for both sulfur cathode and lithium anode. First, the electric and ionic insulating
nature of sulfur makes it difficult to obtain high capacity at useful charge/discharge rates.
It requires conductive additives during the fabrication of electrodes to access large
portions of the sulfur which reduces the practical energy density. Second, the lithium
polysulfide (Li2Sx, x= 4 ~ 8) can dissolve in the liquid electrolyte and be reduced on the
anode surface causing multiple issues during cell cycling, such as shuttling effect, self-
discharge, and continuous capacity fading. Finally, electrolyte depletion due to growth of
Li dendrites and electrolyte reaction with the Li anode shall lead to poor cycle life. Among
these challenges, the key challenge is polysulfide dissolution and migration.
Consequently, to achieve reversible high-energy-density Li–S batteries, the primary task
is to address the issue of polysulfide dissolution and migration. To this end, novel designs
for the Li–S battery’s cathode composition and structure, electrolyte, and anode material

have been developed. The main strategies in the literature are as follows: 1) Development
of advanced carbon materials to facilitate electron transport and trap lithium polysulfide
within sulfur cathode by physisorption and chemisorption; 2) Introduction of other atoms,
small molecules, or polymers into sulfur network through covalent bonding to alter the
solubility of polysulfides or even eliminate their generation; 3) Design of functional
polymer binders to regulate the behavior of polysulfide as well as improve ionic/electronic
transportation; 4) Design of electrolyte composition with different solvents and additives
to alter polysulfide solubility and sulfur chemistry; 5) Design of functional lithium anode
protection layer to inhibit the reaction between lithium metal and polysulfide and facilitate
uniform lithium deposition.
Most of these strategies, however, cannot fully eliminate the issues of polysulfide
dissolution or will limit the sulfur content (< 50 wt%) in the cathode. As the polysulfide can
still dissolve in the electrolyte, the accumulation of insulating Li2S is inevitable, causing
deposition-induced effective surface area loss and loss of ionic/electronic conductive
pathways. As a result, the porosity of the sulfur cathode cannot be reduced to less than
40% without sacrificing cathode capacity. The large porosity of the cathode will, in turn,
result in a high electrolyte/sulfur (E/S) ratio to maintain efficient ionic transport and restrict
the practical energy density of the cell.
An alternative strategy is to develop sulfur cathodes without polysulfide generation, like
sulfurized polyacrylonitrile (SPAN). However, the sulfur content in the SPAN cathode is
usually limited (< 40 wt%), and the packing density of the cathode is typically low, which
leads to lower gravimetric and volumetric energy densities. As a result, Li–S batteries with
energy density > 400 Wh kg–1, lean electrolyte, limited thin Li metal anode (low N/P ratio),
and cycle life of over 300 cycles have not been demonstrated yet.
In brief, the most challenging issue is still polysulfide dissolution and its shutting
phenomena. It is crucial to develop a new soluble-polysulfide-free sulfur cathode with high
sulfur content (> 50 wt%). With this polysulfide-free cathode, accompanied by the
development of functional binders, optimization of electrolyte compositions, and electrode
fabrication, and a protected Li metal anode, advanced Li–S batteries with high energy
density and long cycle life are achievable.

Project Objectives & Accomplishments
This project aims to develop a new soluble-polysulfide-free sulfur cathode with a high
sulfur content (> 50 wt%) and high discharge specific capacity (> 700 mAh g–1, based on
the weight of the whole cathode). This project will also demonstrate the performance of
the sulfur electrode at high electrode capacity (> 7 mAh cm–2), low N/P ratio (< 2) and low
E/S ratio (< 2.5 μL mg–1), and demonstrate prototype 0.5 Ah Li–S pouch cells with
predicted energy density of 400 Wh kg–1 and 80% capacity retention for over 300 cycles
using conventional electrolyte and lithium metal anodes with a protective layer developed
from PI’s group.

Specifically, approaches to realize the project objectives include the following:
1) Development of novel sulfur composite active materials with tunable sulfur content,
 good ionic conductivity upon lithiation, and no generation of soluble polysulfide.
2) Development of novel functional polymer binders with the ability to generate
 electroosmosis to enhance Li-ion transport and eliminate residual Li polysulfide to
 ensure formation of the polysulfide-free electrode during cycling.
3) Diagnostics and characterization of the proposed design based on electrochemical

4) Theoretical simulation to understand the mechanism of the polysulfide-free sulfur
 composite active materials in improving Li–S batteries.
5) Development of Li–S cells using these advanced polysulfide-free cathodes, functional
 binder, and optimization of electrode, electrolytes, and cell design techniques.

Summary of Project Activities
1) SPZ-derived sulfur composite active materials
In this project, we firstly developed a sustainable, sulfurized inorganic–organic hybrid
polymer (S-HYB) network as sulfur cathodes (Figure 1) that enable a unique insertion
conversion reaction for high-capacity, soluble-polysulfide-free Li–S batteries. The material,
hybridizing the inorganic sulfurized polyphosphazene (SPZ) that has high sulfur content
and organic polymers (such as polyacrylonitrile (PAN), pitch, or nitrile rubber), on the one
hand, allows the easy formation of conjugated, sp2-carbon frameworks for electron
transport. On the other hand, the SPZ backbone consisting of alternating phosphorus (P)
and nitrogen (N) atoms offers abundant P–S sites to graft sulfur chains, and adjacent N
sites for on-site adsorption of lithiated sulfur species. This designed S-HYB structure,
wherein the extensive sulfur chains are bonded within the interpenetrating inorganic–
organic polymer network by covalent P–S and C–S tethers and atomic adsorption from N
sites, would unlock high-capacity sulfur conversions without soluble polysulfides.

Figure 1. Schematic of the proposed S-HYB, where sulfur chains are immobilized by
multiple covalent tethers and the Li2S discharge products are regulated by on-site atomic
adsorption and gathered within the inorganic–organic S-HYB.
By introducing the inorganic SPZ, the S-HYB exhibited a higher sulfur content (~62 wt%)
than the sulfurized homopolymer without SPZ (S-HOM, ~51 wt%), as determined by
elemental analysis, which aligned with the thermogravimetric analysis (TGA), showing an
increased weight loss below 300 °C in the S-HYB (Figure 2a). As revealed by density
functional theory (DFT) calculations, this enhanced sulfur content is caused by the

To study the (de)lithiation behaviors of S-HYBs, we used an in situ bias transmission
electron microscopy (TEM) technique through collaboration with Pacific Northwest
National Laboratory (Figure 3a). The pristine S-HYB particle showed homogeneous
distribution of S, P, N, and C (Figure 3b) with an amorphous feature (Figure 3c). Time
series in situ TEM images and their corresponding inverse fast Fourier transform (FFT)
patterns revealed a clear trend of Li2S nanocrystalline formation on lithiation (Figure 3d,e)
and its reversible conversion to amorphous species on delithiation (Figure 3f) in S-HYBs.
In dark contrast, during the first lithiation, amorphous domains were first observed (8.37 s)
and attributed to the first cycle formation. Then, dispersed nanocrystalline Li2S gradually
formed (from 38.34 s; Figure 3d), wherein the presence of irregular and continuously
changing domains suggested that the sulfur species were not entirely nucleated or
crystallized but highly gathered within the interpenetrating inorganic–organic polymer
network. At the end of lithiation (Figure 3e), we observed a homogeneous, nanocrystalline
Li2S network, consisting of interconnected, elongated Li2S domains.
We found that the rapid Li ion insertion process in S-HYB drove this thorough conversion
from bonded sulfur chains to Li2S network. Importantly, on subsequent delithiation, the
nanocrystalline Li2S in S-HYBs fully converted back to amorphous species (Figure 3f).
During recurring lithiation and delithiation, neither notable volume expansion (around ±1%
during lithiation and delithiation) nor microstructural collapse was detected. These results
indicated that our designed S-HYB initiated a unique insertion conversion reaction,
distinguished from conventional sulfur conversions in the fast Li ion insertion and volume-
stable feature as opposed to sluggish kinetics or drastic volume change during S8/Li2S

Furthermore, we studied the atomic structure and evolution of electronic states of S-HYB
during charge/discharge through collaboration with Brookhaven National Laboratory
using soft X-ray absorption spectroscopy (sXAS) (Figure 4a,b). Besides the cathode–
electrolyte interphase formation (2,157 eV) after the first discharge observed in P K-edge
spectra, the peaks at 2,152 eV representing P–S 1s transition to P–S t2* (the antibonding
 8

molecular orbital with t2 symmetry) state from the P–S sites in S-HYB, were nearly
overlapped among pristine, discharged and charged S-HYBs during the first cycling
(Figure 4a). This pointed to the stable presence of P–S covalent tethers during
electrochemical lithiation and delithiation. In contrast, S = C π* (2,468.5 eV) and S–C σ*
(2,472.5 eV) peaks (the asterisks after π and σ represents antibonding of molecular
orbitals) exhibited irreversibly decreased abundance after the first discharge–charge
processes (Figure 4b), indicating the release of unbonded sulfur species from C–S sites
during the first discharge, probably correlated with their chemical environments.
Given the predominantly amorphous nature of S-HYB, we further used a synchrotron-
based pair distribution function (PDF) (Figure 4c–e), which is not constrained by
crystallinity, to specify the sulfur conversion pathway. A clear trend of sulfur dimer (S–S at
~2.10 Å) consumption on discharge and re-formation on charge existed in S-HYB,
wherein the re-formed S–S showed a slightly reduced bond length after the first charge
(Figure 4c). We attribute this reduction in bond length (from ~2.09 to 2.07 Å) to the
translocation of S–S from C–S to P–S sites within S-HYBs, based on the observation that
S–S bonds have a length of ~2.10 Å when bonded at C–S sites, compared to ~2.06 Å at
P–S sites (Figure 4d). It is noteworthy that the translocation of S–S from C–S to P–S sites
on the first charge was also coincident with the irreversible loss of C–S bonds and
formation of sulfur trimers (-S–S–S-, ~3.25 Å; Figure 4c). Based on these results, we
deduced that the P–S sites can dynamically re-bond the irreversibly released sulfur
species from C–S sites to form P–Sx–S (x ≥ 2) containing S–S–S (Figure 4f) during cell
cycling, thus accounting for the soluble-polysulfide-free nature of S-HYBs. Notably, sulfur
conversion involving prolonged sulfur chains (P–Sx–S, x ≥ 2) bridged between the SPZ
and organic polymers in S-HYBs exhibited high reversibility over 50 cycles (Figure 4e).
Homogeneous sulfur conversions at the nanoscale were evidenced by TEM (Figure 4g),
in line with the stable cycling performance of high-capacity S-HYB-based cells.

After studying the redox behaviors of the materials, we conducted electrochemical testing
of the S-HYB-based Li–S batteries in the carbonate-based liquid electrolyte. S-HYB-
based cells exhibited a single discharge plateau after the first cycle of activation,
delivering both higher specific capacity (903.2 mAh g−1; Figure 5a) and improved capacity
retention (87.8% after 300 cycles; Figure 5b) than control S-HOM-based cells. The
observed high sulfur use (87%) in this case indicated that the S-HYB could unlock the
high capacity of the high sulfur content without forming soluble polysulfides.
Differential capacity (dQ/dV) analysis was performed to show the electrochemical origin
of S-HYBs. The sulfur conversion was highly reversible in S-HYBs because the
dQ/dV curves were nearly overlapped for over 200 cycles (Figure 5c). In comparison, in
the control S-HOMs, a newly emerged oxidation peak at ~2.3 V was observed within 50
cycles (Figure 5d). This oxidation reaction was primarily attributed to the oxidation of
unbonded sulfur species like soluble polysulfides (inset in Figure 5c). As increasing
unbonded sulfur species generated and evolved into soluble polysulfides in S-HOMs, we
observed increased voltage fading and reduced capacity due to side reactions with the
carbonate solvent (Figure 5d). Collectively, the sulfur inventory was maintained
exclusively in a bonded manner within S-HYBs (inset in Figure 5c), in contrast to S-HOMs
(inset in Figure 5d) and conventional sulfur cathodes, where dispersion of unbonded
sulfur species occurred during cycling.
When the inorganic SPZ was incorporated, the S-HYB exhibited enhanced Li+ transport
kinetics during sulfur conversion. An Li+ diffusion coefficient (DLi) derived using the
Randles–Ševčík equation of 4.0 × 10−8 cm2 s−1 was determined for the S-HYBs (Figure
5e); it was 48% higher than that of S-HOMs (2.7 × 10−8 cm2 s−1). S-HYBs also showed
constantly lower overpotential than control S-HOMs at varying states of discharge
measured using galvanostatic intermittent titration (GITT) (Figure 5f), further manifesting
its enhanced Li+ migration and rate capability (Figure 5g).

Considering the extensive recycling opportunities of pitch from waste paving materials
and nitrile rubber from scrap tires, we assessed the applicability of our renewable hybrid
polymer network strategy by synthesizing S-HYBs based on inorganic SPZ and the
organic polymers of pitch or nitrile rubber. Each type of S-HYB exhibited both improved
specific capacity and capacity retention through cycling compared to their control S-HOM
samples (Figure 6b). This highlights the broad applicability of our strategy, that is,
hybridizing inorganic and organic polymers into an interpenetrating network to form a
dynamically and covalently (re)bonded framework that invokes a reversible insertion

along the electronically and ionically conductive framework backbones to complete the
conversion without notable volume change, paving the way for developing cost-effective

2) Sulfur-rich polymer-based active material
In addition to the SPZ-derived sulfur composite active material, we developed a sulfur-
rich organic polymer. This sulfur-containing polymer (denoted as SCP-DPC) possesses
a high density of C−S bonding sites, an increased number of nitrogen atoms that enhance

To evaluate the electrochemical performance of the SCP-DPC, we assembled coin cells
with a previously optimized ether-based localized high-concentration electrolyte. During
the initial activation cycle, both SCP-DPC and SCP-SNP demonstrate a charge/discharge
voltage profile with a single plateau at ~1.9 V, indicating a solid-to-solid conversion without
polysulfide shuttling (Figure 8b,c). Notably, despite the initial capacity loss that is
commonly observed in S-PAN-based cathode materials, SCP-SNP experiences
continuous capacity degradation from the second cycle to the tenth cycle, while the SCP-
DPC maintains significantly higher capacity retention (%). This is probably attributed to
the introduction of sulfurized TAA in SCP-DPC, which promotes the formation of a more
stable CEI and prevents further sulfur loss. After stabilization, the control SCP-SNP
showed a relatively lower discharge specific capacity of 619 mAh g–1 (based on active
material) at 0.2 A g–1 (Figure 8a). In contrast, benefiting from the introduction of TAA, the
SCP-DPC delivers an enhanced and highly reversible specific capacity of 766.6 mAh g–
1, along with a capacity retention of 85.9% after 300 cycles, pointing to a 24%

enhancement in discharge capacity. This high sulfur utilization forms a foundation to fully
unlock the high capacity of the LiS batteries without forming soluble polysulfides.
The reversible electrochemical redox reactions in SCP-DPC were then dissected by the
differential capacity (dQ/dV) analysis (Figure 8d). The SCP-DPC exhibits highly reversible
redox chemistry since its dQ/dV curves were nearly overlapped for the 300 cycles, with
well-maintained peaks corresponding to –SSx–Li reduction (at ≈2.1 V, in discharge), Li2S
reduction (at ≈1.8 V, in discharge), and Li2S oxidation (at ≈2.2 V, in charge). In stark
contrast, the dQ/dV curve of the control SCP-SNP cathode (Figure 8e) shows a rapid
increase in polarization, with an average shift of 0.1 V observed across all three peaks
previously mentioned. It is noteworthy that a new peak at around 2.4 V was observed in
discharge, indicating an increased generation of unbonded sulfur species. In line with the
dQ/dV analysis, the GITT measurement of the SCP-DPC cathode also showed constantly
lower overpotential than the control SCP-SNP cathode at varying states of discharge
(Figure 8g,h), further confirming its enhanced Li+ transport kinetics. These improved
kinetics contribute to the enhanced rate capability of the SCP-DPC cathode (Figure 8f)
over a wide range of current density from 0.1 to 5.0 A g–1. Specifically, the SCP-DPC
showed higher discharge capacity and better capacity retention, especially at the highest
5.0 A g–1 rate tested, as compared to the control SCP-SNP cathode. Notably, the
discharge capacity fully recovered when the current density was switched back from 5.0
to 0.1 A g–1.

coin cells were disassembled after 300 cycles for SEM-EDS and XPS analysis. SEM/EDS

the SCP-DPC cathode. Notably, the cathode surface appears smooth and free of visible
cracks, with a uniform distribution of carbon, nitrogen, and sulfur elements, indicating the
absence of aggregation. The sulfur EDS mapping shows a sulfur-rich signal concentrated
in the cross-section, with only sparse signals on the surface. This suggests that the
majority of the active material remains embedded within the bulk cathode. The limited
surface sulfur may result from side reactions between the cathode and electrolyte during

The design of SCP-DPC also proved effective in enhancing sulfur utilization and cycling
stability under practical conditions involving a high-loading cathode. As shown in Figure
9d, both SCP-DPC and SCP-SNP cathodes were tested at a sulfur loading of 6 mg cm–
2, paired with a limited lithium anode (N/P ratio = 1.74) and lean electrolyte conditions

(E/S ratio = 3). The SCP-DPC cathode demonstrated superior cycling stability, while the
control SCP-SNP cathode exhibited a more pronounced capacity fade, particularly after
70 cycles. Moreover, a single-layer SCP-DPC pouch cell tested under the same stringent
conditions achieved comparable performance to the high-loading coin cells, delivering an
areal capacity of 4.5 mAh cm–2. This performance surpasses that of several previously
reported Li–S pouch cells operating under similar constraints.

Conclusions

 17

Final Technical Report The Pennsylvania State University

In conclusion, we have successfully achieved the objectives outlined for Budget Periods
1–3, with the following key accomplishments:
(1) We synthesized soluble-polysulfide-free sulfur composite active materials, particularly
the SPZ-derived composite, which exhibit high sulfur content, excellent cycling stability,
and superior electrochemical performance.
(2) The electrochemical (de)lithiation mechanisms and ion transport behaviors were
thoroughly investigated using advanced spectroscopic, morphological, and
electrochemical characterization techniques.
(3) High-performance Li–S pouch cells were fabricated with cathode areal loadings
exceeding 5 mAh cm–2, a limited lithium metal anode (~2× excess), and a lean carbonate
electrolyte (E/S ratio = 2.5). These cells demonstrated outstanding capacity retention of
84.9% after 150 cycles, with a projected energy density approaching 300 Wh kg–1, placing
them among the best-performing Li–S batteries reported under comparable conditions.
