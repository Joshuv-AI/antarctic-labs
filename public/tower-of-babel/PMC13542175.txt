Synthesis and characterization of electrochemically polymerized indole on screen-printed Ag-conductive transparency sheet for enzymatic biofuel cell applications
Authors: Maha Khan, Inamuddin, Tariq Altalhi, Arwa Alrooqi, Narcis Duteanu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA flexible, lightweight and low-cost enzymatic bioanode was developed using a screen-printed silver conductive transparency sheet for enzymatic biofuel cell (EBFC) applications. In this work, indole was electrochemically polymerized directly onto the conductive substrate to form a polyindole (PIn) matrix capable of simultaneously entrapping glucose oxidase (GOx) and redox mediator vitamin K3 (VK3). The study integrates a disposable transparency-sheet platform with an electroactive PIn network that promotes efficient enzyme immobilization and enhanced electron transfer for glucose bioelectrocatalysis. The synergistic interaction between PIn, VK3 and GOx significantly improved charge-transfer kinetics and stabilized the bioelectrocatalytic interface, resulting in enhanced electrochemical performance. The fabricated PIn/VK3/GOx bioanode exhibited a current density of 1.18 mA cm− 2 in 40 mM glucose solution, demonstrating efficient glucose-dependent electrocatalytic activity. The conductive PIn framework facilitated rapid electron transport between the buried active sites of GOx and the electrode surface, while VK3 acted as an efficient and biocompatible electron shuttle. The bioanode also displayed good electrical stability, semiconducting behavior, and favorable electrochemical characteristics, highlighting its suitability for flexible and wearable bioelectronic applications.Keywords: Redox mediator, Conducting polymer, Entrapment, Flexible electronics, Four-probe electrical conductivity measurement technique, Screen-printed electrodeSubject terms: Chemistry, Energy science and technology, Materials science

Introduction

The 21 st century has observed a tremendous increase in human population, resulting in severe global issues such as exceeding pollution levels, uncontrolled emission of greenhouse gases, alarming rise in the temperature due to global warming and gradual decline in non-renewable sources of energy such as fossil fuel, coal, natural gas, etc. These problems necessitate rapid advancements and breakthroughs in science and technology to meet the demands of the ever-growing population on Earth. The need to explore alternative clean and sustainable energy sources is greater than ever. Fuel cells are futuristic devices that convert the chemical energy of renewable fuels into electricity through electrochemical reactions1. The stored chemical energy of a fuel is converted into electrical current through electro-oxidation of fuels at the anode with high efficiency and net-zero carbon emissions, resulting in clean energy2. The fuel cell offers a variety of applications in transportation, electronics3, energy storage devices4, portable and stationary power plants5. Fuel cells are of different types depending on the operating temperature, electrolyte used, and potential applications, i.e., solid oxide fuel cells, alkaline fuel cells, proton exchange membrane fuel cells, molten carbonate fuel cells, phosphoric acid fuel cells and biofuel cells6. Biofuel cells offer a plethora of advantages, such as use of biological catalysts (Microbes and enzymes) instead of expensive noble metal catalysts such as Pt, Au, Ag, as compared to conventional fuel cells7. Biofuel cells are of two major types, mainly microbial and enzymatic biofuel cells. The applications of microbial fuel cells are limited due to their low current output8, conversion efficiency and potential health risks associated with their use9. However, enzymatic biofuel cells (EBFCs) are highly substrate (Fuel) specific for a wide variety of abundantly available fuels from biological sources such as glucose, fructose, sucrose and alcohols10. The potential for miniaturization, ambient operational temperature and neutral pH conditions make them more compatible to use in room-temperature devices11 such as implantable and wearable medical devices12, portable electronics, cochlear implants, smart textiles, self-powered biosensors, and health monitoring devices. The electrochemical output in enzymatic biofuel cells depends on material type and the architecture of the electrode13. These are critical factors in determining the electrode’s stability, electron transfer kinetics, enzymatic activity, current and energy density.

In recent years, screen-printed electrodes have gained considerable attention as promising electrochemical platforms owing to their low fabrication cost, disposability, portability, and compatibility with large-scale manufacturing processes14. Screen-printing technology enables the precise deposition of conductive inks onto flexible substrates such as paper, plastic films, and transparency sheets, resulting in lightweight and miniaturized electrochemical devices with excellent reproducibility. Recent advances in nano-engineered paper-based electrochemical electronics have demonstrated that these electrodes can be readily modified with nanomaterials, conductive polymers, mediators, and biocatalysts to significantly enhance electron-transfer kinetics, sensitivity, and operational stability. These unique characteristics make printed electrodes highly attractive for enzymatic biofuel cell applications, where efficient enzyme immobilization, rapid charge transfer, mechanical flexibility, and low-cost fabrication are essential requirements15. Consequently, the integration of conductive polymer-based bioelectrodes with screen-printed platforms represents an effective strategy for developing sustainable, scalable, and high-performance bioenergy devices.

Paper-based electrodes, in particular, are a promising technology offering unique advantages such as flexibility, disposability, lightweight, and cost-effectiveness, finding applications in a wide variety of small-scale devices ranging from point-of-care diagnostic medical devices to epidermal electronics and sensors16. However, the widespread commercialization of paper-based EBFCs remains a challenge due to limitations such as low electrical conductivity and power output, enzyme leaching and instability, limiting the generated current output. The integration of conducting polymeric supports like polyaniline, polypyrrole, polyindole, polythiophene and poly(3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene) into the paper-based biofuel cell framework can sufficiently increase its electrical conductivity and provide a matrix for sufficient immobilization of enzymes, enhancing overall performance17. Conducting polymers are increasingly employed as supporting material in biofuel cell applications18 because their conjugated structures offer high electrical conductivity, facile synthesis, and exceptional chemical stability19. Polyindole, in particular, has attracted the attention of researchers due to the ease of its chemical and electrochemical polymerization, good electrical conductivity20, low toxicity, optimum redox activity, high thermal stability, and low rate of degradation21. Therefore, polyindole is a potential material for supercapacitors22, solar cells, diodes, biomedicines, drug delivery systems, sensors and biosensors23. In view of the advantages associated with polyindole, this study reports the electrochemical polymerization of indole on a screen-printed transparency with the simultaneous entrapment of glucose oxidase (GOx) and biocompatible mediator vitamin K3 (VK3) in its matrix. Electropolymerization is a particularly attractive technique for the fabrication of bioelectrodes because it enables the direct deposition of conductive polymer films onto electrode surfaces with precise control over thickness, morphology and electrochemical properties24. Moreover, the process facilitates the simultaneous entrapment of enzymes, redox mediators, and other functional materials within the growing polymer matrix, resulting in enhanced immobilization efficiency and improved electron-transfer kinetics. The strong adherence of electropolymerized films to the electrode surface minimizes material loss and contributes to superior operational stability25. Hence, the use of PIn as a conductive support offers two major advantages: it enhances electron transfer between surface of the electrode and the hidden active sites of the enzyme, providing an appropriate conducting support for efficient immobilization of GOx, maximizing stability and performance of the constructed anode.

As the enzyme entrapped within PIn matrix undergoes oxidation, electrons are released, which need to flow to the surface of the electrode. Two major types of electron transfer processes are observed: direct electron transfer (DET) and mediated electron transfer (MET)26. The electrons produced from fuel oxidation are transferred directly from redox-active enzymatic sites to the anode surface in DET. The limitation associated with DET-based EBFCs is that the functional sites of enzymes are deeply buried in non-conducting proteinaceous shells, making charge transfer inefficient. To overcome this, suitable redox mediators such as thionine, methylene blue, methylene green, phenanthrolines, and quinones in MET can enhance electron transfer by minimizing the electron tunneling distance from the active sites of the enzyme to the electrode. Tsuruoka et al.27 reported using poly(methylene green) mediator for glucose dehydrogenase (GDH) based catalysis, generating a modest redox current of 3 mA cm− 2. Even though MET produces higher power output in EBFCs than DET, the challenges associated with their use include potential toxicity24 and leakage due to improper immobilization. The successful entrapment of vitamin K3 as a non-toxic, environment-friendly and biocompatible mediator28 in the PIn/VK3/GOx anode, not only facilitates swift electron transfer but also ensures suitable incorporation of this anode into wearable and implantable electrochemical technology. The fabrication of PIn/VK3/GOx EBFC electrode with electropolymerized indole on a light-weight, flexible transparency sheet, along with entrapped enzyme and mediator complex, opens doors to the realm of cost-effective, sustainable, green sensing and biosensing technology. Materials and methods

Chemicals

Silver conductive ink (S-020, 75–85 wt%) was procured from Thermo Scientific Chemicals, USA. Acetonitrile (99.8% pure, HPLC grade) was purchased from Merck, India. Glucose oxidase (GOx) from Aspergillus niger (Activity: 100,000–150,000 units g⁻¹ of protein), vitamin K₃ (98% pure), indole (99% pure), lithium perchlorate trihydrate (99% pure), D-(+)-glucose anhydrous (99.5% pure), acetone (99% pure), ethanol (99.9% pure), buffer solution (pH 5.0) and phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) were obtained from Central Drug House (CDH), India. The experiments were conducted using double-distilled water. All chemicals were of analytical reagent grade and were used as received without further purification.

Instrumentation

Electrochemical characterization was conducted on a PC-operated potentiostat/galvanostat (PGSTAT 302 N Autolab, Switzerland). A standard three-electrode cell consisting of the working Ag-ink screen-printed transparency sheet (5×1 cm2) electrode, an Ag/AgCl reference electrode and a counter platinum electrode was connected to the potentiostat during electrochemical experiments. Screen-printing of the disposable sheet was performed using a PreAsion Printing Press. DC conductivity measurement of the electrochemically synthesized PIn was performed on a four-in-line probe electrical conductivity measuring instrument for semiconductors (SES Instruments, Roorkee, India). Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was done on a Carl Zeiss Model Supra 55 with the sample stationed on a copper grid. XRD and TGA were performed on Rigaku (SmartLab) and DTG-60 H (Shimadzu), respectively. Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy was implemented in the range of 500 to 4000 cm− 1 on a Nicolet iS50 FT-IR (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Electrochemical polymerization of indole

1 M of indole monomers in 50 mL of acetonitrile were magnetically stirred for 30 min until a homogenous solution was obtained. To this monomeric solution, pre-optimized 0.1 M lithium perchlorate as a dopant was added and magnetically stirred. For effective entrapment of enzyme and mediator into the polymeric lattice, different quantities of VK3 (5, 10, 15 mg mL− 1) and GOx (10.0 mg mL− 1 in pH 5.0 buffer solution) (2, 4, 6 µL) were carefully added into the indole monomers and the resulting cyclic voltammograms obtained were studied to examine the best electrochemical output. It was observed that the bioanode with 10 mg mL− 1 (VK3) and 4 µL (GOx) provided the best output.

The working electrode was a 5 cm2 rectangular strip of Ag-screen printed transparency sheet. The three electrodes were placed in the standard cell containing an indole solution, along with optimized enzyme and mediator concentrations, and connected to the potentiostat to initiate polymerization. Cyclic voltammetry was performed over the potential range of -0.8 to 0.8 V at a scan rate of 100 mV s− 1. The electropolymerized Ag screen-printed sheet of PIn/VK3/GOx was then rinsed repeatedly with acetone and distilled water and left in the oven for drying at 35 °C for six hours. Before electrochemical measurements, the fabricated bioanode was stored at 4 °C to preserve the biological activity of the immobilized enzyme and prevent its denaturation.

Electrical conductivity of polyindole

Electrical conductivity tests were performed on polyindole using the DC four-probe method of conductivity measurement for semiconductors. To minimize protonic conductivity, the sample must be thoroughly dried before use29. Electropolymerized indole, after drying, was scraped off from the surface of the screen-printed transparency sheet. 250 mg of PIn was ground to a fine powder using a mortar and pestle. The powdered PIn was then transferred into a pellet-press die set, consisting of two die pellets, a base plate and a load-bearing rod. The die set assembly was placed in a hydraulic pellet press machine at 60 KN for 15 min to ensure the formation of a mechanically stable PIn pellet of appropriate thickness. The obtained PIn pellet was placed in the four-probe assembly. The probes were tightly screwed on the center of the pellet, producing a gentle pressure to avoid damaging the sample. In a four-probe experiment, the floating potential across two inner probes is studied as current passes through the two other probes. To study the effect of temperature on conductivity, the four-probe assembly was placed in the oven, and the supply was switched on. As the temperature increased gradually, the resulting current and potential were recorded. The electrical conductivity of PIn pellet was determined by isothermal, cyclic, and stability tests.

Thermogravimetric analysis

Figure 5 shows the thermal stability analysis of the electropolymerized PIn/VK3 biocomposite. The analysis shows a step-wise weight loss with an initial loss of 13.3% at 100 °C, indicating the possible elimination of water molecules and adsorbed gases trapped within the polymeric matrix31. A 7.69% weight loss was due to the removal of dopant ions and unreacted monomers up to a temperature of 250 °C32. Thermal degradation of carbon-containing residues in the polymeric backbone begins at around 450 °C, ultimately resulting in a carbon-black mass due to the formation of oxides of the sample up to 800 °C33.

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy analysis

Figure 6 displays the FTIR spectrum of electrochemically synthesized PIn. A sharp and broad band observed at 3410 cm− 1 is attributed to N-H stretching vibration, confirming the synthesis of PIn34. The peak at 1575 cm− 1 is assigned to the stretching vibrations of N-H bonds, proving that the nitrogen atom isn’t involved in the process of electropolymerization35 and only the 2- and 3-positions of the indole monomer are available for polymerization. The peak at 740 cm− 1 is assigned to the out-of-plane C-H bond deformation of the benzene ring. The peak at 2935 cm− 1 is due to the C-H bonds present in the indole lattice. The peaks at 1450 and 1624 cm− 1 indicate the C-N and C-C benzenoid ring stretching in PIn36. The peak at around 1100 cm− 1 indicates the C = N stretching vibration.

X-ray diffraction analysis

Figure 8 displays the X-ray diffraction analysis of PIn/VK3, performed at a scan rate of 10 degrees min− 1. The diffraction profile exhibits a broad diffraction peak centered at 2θ = 21.22°, indicating the predominantly amorphous nature of the synthesized polyindole (PIn) matrix. The appearance of broad and diffused peaks suggests the absence of long-range structural ordering within the polymeric backbone, which is characteristic of conjugated conducting polymers synthesized through electrochemical polymerization. Similar broad diffraction behavior for PIn has been previously reported in literature and attributed to disordered chain packing, irregular intermolecular arrangement, and partial π–π stacking interactions within the polymer framework37.

XRD spectrum of PIn/VK3.

The amorphous nature of PIn can also be associated with the random orientation of polymeric chains formed during oxidative electropolymerization, which restricts the formation of highly ordered crystalline domains. Conducting polymer polyindole generally exhibits semi-crystalline or amorphous characteristics due to structural defects, chain entanglement, and localized conjugation along the polymer backbone. The broadening of the diffraction peak additionally confirms the nanostructured nature of the prepared material38.

Electrochemical characterization of anode

The electrochemical performance of PIn/VK3/GOx bioanode was investigated using cyclic voltammetry in pH 7.4 PBS in a potential window of - 0.8 to 0.8 V at 100 mV s− 1 in N2-purged atmosphere. Figure 9, curve (a) displays the voltammogram of a bare screen-printed Ag ink electrode, which exhibited minimal current density, no catalytic turnover, and no significant redox peaks. The CV of electropolymerized PIn in the curve (b) shows the characteristic oxidation and reduction peaks of the polymeric backbone and a low current output of 0.17 mA cm− 2. The anodic and cathodic peaks suggest a reversible or quasi-reversible redox reaction39. Curve (c) of PIn/VK3 shows the addition of mediator vitamin K3 into the PIn matrix, significantly increasing the current output to 0.48 mA cm− 2. VK3 facilitates efficient electron shuttling and lowers the activation energy for oxidation, shifting the anodic peak to a more negative potential, indicating easier oxidation. Similarly, the cathodic peak shifts to a more positive potential, indicating improved charge transfer kinetics and easier reduction. Curve (d) of PIn/VK3/GOx displayed an electrochemical current output of 0.8 mA cm− 2, indicating improved electron transfer kinetics between the active sites of GOx and the electrode with the help of VK3. PIn provides a suitable electron-rich conducting matrix for the immobilization of GOx, stabilizing the electrode. As the polymeric network grows electrochemically on the Ag screen-printed substrate, GOx molecules become physically confined within the developing porous and electroactive PIn framework. Simultaneously, intermolecular interactions such as hydrogen bonding, π–π interactions, and electrostatic attractions between the functional groups of GOx and the conjugated PIn backbone contribute to enhanced enzyme retention and stabilization within the matrix40. The conductive nature of PIn provides an electron-rich microenvironment that facilitates electrical communication between the deeply embedded redox-active centers of GOx and the electrode surface. In addition, the incorporated vitamin K3 mediator acts as an efficient electron shuttle, minimizing electron tunneling limitations typically associated with direct electron transfer in enzyme-based systems. The observed enhancement in current density, stable redox behaviour, and glucose-dependent bioelectrocatalytic response collectively confirm the successful immobilization and preservation of enzymatic activity within the PIn matrix. Thus, efficient shuttling of electrons along with the electrical communication provided by PIn, improves the overall electrochemical performance of the bioanode.

CVs of bare electrode, PIn, PIn/VK3, PIn/VK3/GOx in PBS (pH 7.4) at 100 mV s− 1.

The bioelectrocatalytic performance of PIn/VK3/GOx was studied in 0 and 40 mM of glucose solutions. Figure 10 shows an appreciable increase in the redox peaks and current output from 0.8 to 1.18 mA cm− 2 on glucose addition into the electrolyte. The increase is attributed to the substrate-specific functioning of GOx, which converts glucose present in the electrolyte into gluconolactone, releasing electrons. The electrons are efficiently transferred to the electrode surface via the electrochemical mediator VK3, increasing the current density. Successful immobilization of GOx and VK3 within the entrapping PIn matrix also enhanced the bioelectrocatalytic activity of the anode, as it prevents enzymatic leaching and stabilizes the biocomposite to prolong its lifetime. The constructed anode is thus sensitive to glucose in its environment and utilizes it to generate electricity in a GOx-dependent manner41. The rise in current density with increasing glucose concentrations suggests the potential application of PIn/VK3/GOx anode in glucose detection technologies.

CVs of PIn/VK3/GOx in the absence and presence (40 mM) of glucose solution at 100 mV s− 1.

The fabricated PIn/VK3/GOx bioanode delivered a maximum current density of 1.18 mA cm− 2 in 40 mM glucose solution. Although current density is lower than certain nanocomposite-based EBFC systems reported in literature, the present study emphasizes the development of a lightweight, flexible, low-cost, and disposable screen-printed transparency-sheet platform with facile fabrication and biocompatible architecture. For instance, Inamuddin et al. reported a zinc oxide/PIn-MWCNTs/ferritin/GOx bioanode with a current density of 4.9 mA cm− 2, attributed to the synergistic contribution of ZnO nanoparticles and MWCNTs providing significantly higher surface area and conductivity42. In another study, three-dimensional graphene-based GOx bioanodes have demonstrated significantly high-power densities due to the exceptional conductivity and porous architecture of graphene frameworks43. However, graphene fabrication, functionalization, and integration into electrodes require advanced processing techniques and substantially increase the overall fabrication cost. This study, in contrast, offers several practical advantages. The developed bioanode employs a commercially available transparency sheet as a lightweight, flexible, disposable, and low-cost substrate, eliminating the need for costly carbon nanomaterials, graphene derivatives, or noble-metal-based architectures. The observed electrochemical performance, together with the simplicity of fabrication, mechanical flexibility, and use of a biocompatible vitamin K3 mediator, highlights the potential of the developed bioanode for wearable and low-cost bioelectronic applications. Linear sweep voltammetry

The electrocatalytic sensing response of the PIn/VK3/GOx bioanode was investigated using the linear sweep voltammetry technique over the range of 0–50 mM glucose. Figure 12 (a) reveals that the PIn/VK3/GOx bioanode generated a current output of 1.18 mA cm− 2 from the electrocatalytic oxidation of 40 mM glucose. Initially, the generated anodic current increases linearly with increasing glucose concentration, confirming the catalytic performance of GOx in the substrate-specific generation of gluconolactone from glucose, delivering electrons. These electrons travel through the circuit, generating current. These observations indicate the dependency of the anodic current produced on the presence of glucose and confirm the catalytic ability of the prepared biocomposite. After reaching a saturation limit in 40 mM glucose solution, the redox current density gradually declines, and stagnancy in the anodic output is observed. The reaction thus follows saturation kinetics, wherein a stagnant value of the oxidation current is obtained with an increase in glucose above optimum concentrations51. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy

The Nyquist plots were analyzed in terms of charge transfer resistance (Rct), interfacial electron-transfer characteristics, and diffusion-controlled processes occurring at the electrode/electrolyte interface of PIn/VK3/GOx bioanode. The Nyquist plot contains an imaginary Z” and a real Z’ impedance axis, which provide an account of the capacitance and resistance of the prepared biocomposite, respectively. In the low frequency region, the straight-line region suggests that electrochemical processes are governed by mass transfer, while the semi-circular region in high frequency indicates that the process is charge transfer driven at the electrode-electrolyte interface52. Figure 13 (a) displays the EIS plots of PIn, PIn/VK3 and PIn/VK3/GOx. A higher inclination of PIn and PIn/VK3 towards the imaginary impedance and the comparatively smaller semicircle observed indicate lower charge-transfer resistance and faster electron-transfer kinetics due to the conductive nature of the polyindole matrix and the electron-shuttling role of vitamin K3. Upon immobilization of GOx, the diameter of the semicircle increased, suggesting an increase in interfacial resistance caused by the incorporation of the non-conductive proteinaceous enzyme layer within the conductive polymer framework. This increase in Rct provides indirect evidence for the successful immobilization of GOx onto the PIn matrix. Enzyme and mediator entrapped PIn show a semi-circle with a large diameter, indicating an increased overall resistance introduced by the successful immobilization of the enzyme and electrochemical mediator within the conductive PIn matrix.

(a) EIS spectra of PIn, PIn/VK3 and PIn/VK3/GOx in pH 7.4 phosphate buffer solution and (b) Equivalent Randles’ circuit diagram.

The inclined linear region observed in the low-frequency domain corresponds to Warburg impedance, indicating diffusion-controlled ion transport and electrolyte penetration within the porous polymeric network. The combination of charge-transfer and diffusion behavior confirms that the electrochemical processes are governed by both interfacial electron transfer and mass transport phenomena. The enhanced electrochemical response observed in cyclic voltammetry and glucose oxidation studies, despite the increase in Rct after enzyme immobilization, demonstrates that the conductive PIn/VK3 framework effectively compensates for enzymatic resistance by facilitating efficient electron mediation between GOx and the electrode surface. Stability

The stability of the fabricated PIn/VK₃/GOx bioanode was evaluated by cyclic voltammetry over 20 days. The bioelectrode was stored at 4 °C, and its electrochemical response was recorded at an interval of 5 days in glucose solution. As shown in Fig. 14, the voltammograms retained their characteristic shape throughout the storage period, indicating that the bioelectrocatalytic activity of the immobilized glucose oxidase and the electron-transfer capability of the composite matrix remained largely preserved. The outermost voltammogram, corresponding to the freshly prepared bioanode, exhibited an anodic peak current density of 1.18 mA cm− 2. With increasing storage duration, a gradual decrease in the peak current density was observed, reaching approximately 0.92 mA cm− 2 after 20 days. Despite this reduction, the bioanode retained nearly 75% of its initial electrochemical response, demonstrating appreciable storage stability and resistance to performance degradation.

Cyclic voltammograms of PIn/VK₃/GOx electrode in glucose over a period of 20 days.

The observed stability can be attributed to the robust polyindole matrix, which provides a conductive and mechanically stable environment for enzyme immobilization, thereby minimizing enzyme leaching during prolonged storage. Furthermore, vitamin K₃ acts as an efficient redox mediator, facilitating electron transfer between the active sites of glucose oxidase and the electrode surface. The synergistic interaction between PIn and VK₃, together with the porous structure and large electroactive surface area of the composite, enables effective retention of the biocatalyst and preservation of its activity over time. These results confirm that the PIn/VK₃/GOx bioanode exhibits good long-term electrochemical durability and is a promising candidate for enzymatic biofuel cell applications that require extended operational stability. Conclusion

A flexible, low-cost, disposable PIn/VK3/GOx anode was fabricated on an Ag-ink screen-printed transparency sheet for enzymatic biofuel cell applications. The bioanode exhibited a current output of 1.18 mA cm− 2 in a 40 mM glucose solution. Electrochemically polymerized indole on the transparency sheet, along with entrapped VK3 and GOx, furnished an appreciable electrical connection between the hidden active sites of enzyme GOx and the electrode surface. The mediator, VK3, also enhanced the electrochemical output by supplying electrons from the buried active sites of enzymes to the electrode. Optimum electrochemical performance of the bioanode proves the effective entrapment of GOx and VK3 within the PIn lattice. PIn/VK3/GOx EBFC thus proved to be catalytically efficient, stable and cost-effective, portraying remarkable bioelectrocatalytic performance on a screen-printed flexible, disposable, readily available, and cheap transparency sheet utilized as an electrode. Future work may involve improving the performance of EBFCs for their widespread commercialization, focusing on enzyme stability, high power output and operational lifetimes. Applications of flexible sheet-based electrodes in wearable and implantable EBFCs can also be explored.
