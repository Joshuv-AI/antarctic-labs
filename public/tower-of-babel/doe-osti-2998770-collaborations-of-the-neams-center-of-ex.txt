 Executive Summary

The mission of the NEAMS Center of Excellence for Thermal-fluids Applications in Nuclear Energy
is to advance the goals of the NEAMS program by providing leadership, best practices, research, and
support and training for computational thermal hydraulics. In particular NEAMS tools are being
developed to ultimately span the entire range of length- and time-scales required for a comprehensive
reactor design and safety analysis. The focus of The Center is on the use, best practices, and
deployment to stakeholders of the thermal hydraulic codes.
 The Center of Excellence, originally launched in 2018, has as its key goal to serve as a “front
door” to industry. As part of this goal, The Center is in its sixth year of a program to engage
in collaborative efforts between the laboratories and industry with the objective of stimulating
cooperation and increasing the adoption of thermal hydraulics tools developed under NEAMS by
the industry at large. With an overwhelmingly positive response from participants, previous short-
term projects have led to more in-depth collaborations.
 In FY25, the collaboration with Kairos Power LLC focuses on a study of thermally driven
natural convection in pebble-bed systems. This project targets two distinct configurations:

  1. Canister Pebble Bed System – Used pebbles are stored in a canister and cooled by natural
 convection of air or another gas. The pebbles continue to generate residual heat, while the
 canister wall is actively cooled.

  2. Reduced-Diameter Hermes-Height Core – In this configuration, molten salt rises through
 the core as the heated pebbles induce buoyancy-driven flow. An active cooler, modeled as a
 heat sink, drives the salt downward through a downcomer channel separated from the bed.

 The primary objective is to evaluate the heat-transfer coefficients resulting from thermally
driven natural convection or circulation. This work is still in progress; the present report sum-
marizes current status and preliminary findings. Final results will be presented in next year’s
report.

1 Introduction

1.1 The NEAMS Center of Excellence

A primary goal of the Department of Energy’s Nuclear Energy Advanced Modeling and Simulation
(NEAMS) program is to develop the next generation of simulations tools to support the design
and deployment of commercial nuclear power. The mission of the NEAMS Center of Excellence
for Thermal-fluids Applications in Nuclear Energy is to advance this goal by providing leadership,
best practices, research, and support and training for computational thermal-fluids applications in
nuclear energy. This is accomplished by leveraging expertise in the NEAMS tools to solve challenging
problems in fluid flow and heat transfer. The NEAMS tools provide simulation capabilities covering
a range of temporal and spatial scales and the Center connects stakeholders in industry with the
expertise to use these tools to their fullest. This addresses a pressing need, facilitating advanced
reactor development and commercialization.

 Building on the success of previous years, The Center continues to serve as an avenue of
engagement for industry with a low barrier to entry, serving as a true “front door”. The Center
strives to make the full suite of NEAMS TH tools available to industry stakeholders. This is
accomplished through center meetings, training, and more directly through short-term collaboration
projects. We note that these short term projects are not aimed at providing a funding venue
and cannot be used to address problems requiring the use of protected intellectual property (IP).
These collaborations are intended to serve as a launching point for a more in-depth collaboration,
leading to a partnership for a larger proposal, such as through GAIN or other funding opportunity
announcements (FOAs), which can involve the use of protected IP. The potential paths of industrial
customers are outlined in the diagram below, reflecting the different levels of commitment from the
industry partner.

1.2 Collaboration with Kairos Power, LLC

In FY25 we collaborated with Kairos Power, LLC to investigate thermally driven natural convection
in pebble-bed systems. Kairos Power LLC is developing the Kairos Power Fluoride-Cooled High-
Temperature Reactor (KP-FHR), an advanced reactor concept that employs TRISO fuel in pebble
form with a low-pressure fluoride salt coolant [1]. Figure 1.2 presents the system configuration of
the KP-FHR integrated with the steam cycle, while

 To support the accelerated deployment of the KP-FHR, our prior collaboration focused on flow
and heat transfer in random pebble beds [2]. High-fidelity computational fluid dynamics (CFD)
simulations were used to generate turbulence and heat transfer data of exceptional quality. These
data sets were then leveraged to inform and validate reduced-order approaches, such as empirical
heat transfer correlations for use in systems analysis codes.

 The fundamental innovation of FHR technology lies in the unique combination of TRISO
particle fuel with a chemically inert, optically transparent fluoride salt coolant, FLiBe (Li-7 enriched
Li2 BeF4 ). Additionally, the KP-FHR offers several inherent advantages: a low-pressure primary
boundary, high thermal efficiency, and fully passive, robust safety systems. Collectively, these
features enable the KP-FHR to deliver a safe, reliable, and cost-competitive reactor technology that
can directly compete with natural gas–fired combined-cycle power plants.

 Figure 1.2: KP-FHR system configuration with steam cycle [3]

 In this collaboration, instead of the study the forced flow and mixed flow condition, those has
already be done by previous work [2, 4], focused on thermally driven natural convection in pebble
beds. The proposed work focuses on two distinct configurations:

  1. Canister Pebble Bed System — Used pebbles are stored in a canister and cooled by
 natural convection of air or another gas. The pebbles continue to generate residual heat,

 2

 while the canister wall is actively cooled. Natural convection induced by the thermal gradient
 removes heat from the pebbles to the wall, and thermal radiation also contributes to the
 overall heat transfer. For convenience, ”canister” will be used to refer this pebble bed.

 2. Reduced-Diameter Hermes-Height Core — In this configuration, molten salt rises
 through the core as the heated pebbles create buoyancy-driven flow. An active cooler, modeled
 as a heat sink, drives the salt downward through a downcomer channel separated from the
 bed. The pebble bed height matches that of the Hermes test reactor [5, 4] to approximate the
 total pressure head, but the bed diameter is reduced due to limit computational cost. Again,
 ”core” will be used to refer this pebble bed.

 The primary objective of this study is to evaluate the heat-transfer coefficients associated with
thermally driven natural convection, circulation, and thermal radiation. A secondary objective is to
generate benchmark data for the validation of porous-media models. Since the project began late
in the fiscal year, the work remains in progress. This report summarizes the current status and
preliminary findings, while final results will be presented in the next report.

2 Numerical method

2.1 NekRS

In this work, we leveraged the High-fidelity Computational Fluid Dynamic code NekRS [6], which
is developed by Argonne National Laboratory, based on the Spectral Element Method (SEM) [7].
The SEM is a subclass of Galerkin methods or weighted residual methods. It is non-dissipative and
delivers low numerical dispersion. The foundational idea is to minimize the error of the numerical
computation in the energy norm over a chosen space of piecewise polynomials. Overall, the method
presents key similarities and a common theoretical framework with the finite element method (FEM),
but it typically achieves the same accuracy with fewer degrees of freedom as the FEM is constrained
to low polynomial orders due to computational cost [8]. In NekRS, Lagrange polynomials are used
as basis functions on Gauss-Lobatto-Legendre quadrature [7, 9, 10] to solve the incompressible
Navier-Stokes equations.

 NekRS is originally developed as the GPU variant of Nek5000 [9] , to leverage the computational
power of GPU. The kernels in NekRS started as an early fork from libParanumal [11] and were
tailored and expanded to meet the specific requirements of large-scale turbulent flow applications
in complex domains. NekRS provides access to the standard Nek5000 interface and features (e.g.,
conjugate heat transfer), which allows users to leverage existing application-specific source code and
data files on GPU-based platforms. The GPU kernels are written in the portable Open Concurrent
Compute Abstraction (OCCA) library [12] to abstract between different parallel languages such
as OpenCL, CUDA, and HIP. OCCA allows developers to implement the parallel kernel code in a
slightly decorated C++ language, OKL (Occa Kernal Language). At runtime, the user can specify
which parallel programming model to target, after which OCCA translates the OKL source code into
the desired target language and Just-In-Time (JIT) compiles kernels for the user’s target hardware
architecture. In the recent application of NekRS, we have successfully simulated an entire pebble

bed core [13] and SMR core [14, 15]. More recently, a study, performed by Yuan et al [4, 2], used
NekRS to simulate the flow and heat transfer in a Hermes-size pebble bed, in forced and mixed

does not only proves the feasibility, and also provide high fidelity data for heat transfer correlations’
validation.

3 Meshing for random pebble bed

3.1 Voronoi cell approach for pure hexahedral meshing

NekRS and Nek5000 employ the high-order spectral element method (SEM) with tensor-product
bases defined on a reference cube. This formulation is naturally suited for hexahedral elements in
three dimensions and quadrilateral elements in two dimensions. As a result, the performance and
robustness of NekRS rely on the use of hex-only SEM with tensor-product bases.

 Despite these advantages, generating a purely hexahedral mesh for complex geometries remains
a significant challenge. For example, previous efforts to construct hexahedral meshes for randomly
packed pebble beds have utilized the robust tet-to-hex method [19, 20]. In this approach, a pure
tetrahedral mesh is first generated for the complex geometry, and each tetrahedral element is then
subdivided into four hexahedral elements. Although effective, this tet-to-hex procedure results in
prohibitively large meshes, typically requiring on the order of 5,000 elements per pebble. Such
excessive element counts severely restrict the applicability of the method to larger pebble-bed
systems.

 7

 Alternatively, the Voronoi cell approach [21] is employed as an effective strategy to reduce the
element count. To understand this method, it is useful to first recall the mathematical concept of a
Voronoi diagram, which partitions a plane into regions associated with a given set of objects. In
the simplest case, these objects are finitely many points in the plane, referred to as seeds, sites, or
generators. Each seed defines a corresponding region, known as a Voronoi cell, which consists of all
points closer to that seed than to any other. Thus, the Voronoi decomposition provides a natural
way to divide space into well-defined, non-overlapping regions.

 When this concept is extended to three dimensions, a Voronoi cell becomes a polyhedron
surrounding a seed. This polyhedron is bounded by polygonal faces, which can be further tessellated
into quadrilaterals. To create a surface-conforming mesh, these quadrilaterals are projected onto
the pebble surface, as illustrated in Figure 3.1. In this way, the Voronoi framework provides a
systematic procedure for generating high-quality meshes around individual pebbles.

 One of the key advantages of this approach is its ability to reduce mesh sizes dramatically.
Compared to the tet-to-hex approach, which typically requires around 5,000 elements per pebble,
the Voronoi-based method reduces the requirement to as few as 300 elements per pebble [21]. This
significant reduction not only decreases computational cost but also makes large-scale simulations
more feasible. For example, Figure 3.2 presents a turbulent flow field in a randomly packed pebble
bed containing 1,568 pebbles, where the mesh was generated using the Voronoi cell approach.

 To ensure both accuracy and stability in the simulations presented here, the mesh size is
maintained at approximately 1,600 elements per pebble. This resolution provides sufficient fidelity
to capture pebble-to-pebble contacts and to resolve near-surface flow features. Finally, it is worth
noting that the robustness and reliability of this Voronoi-based meshing strategy have been confirmed
in numerous recent studies [4, 17, 22, 23], further underscoring its suitability for complex pebble-bed
reactor applications.

3.2 Approximation of contacts

The handling of contacts is a critical aspect of mesh generation. In general, any CFD mesh that
contains zero-area or zero-volume elements introduces singularities, which cannot be resolved by
the solver. As a result, some form of compromise must be introduced during mesh construction to
ensure numerical stability.

 This issue becomes particularly important in the simulation of random pebble beds, where
numerous pebble-to-pebble and pebble-to-wall contacts occur throughout the domain. In reality,
when two pebbles—or a pebble and a wall—come into contact, the contact area increases slightly
due to deformation. However, because graphite has a very large Young’s modulus, this deformation
is extremely small. From a numerical perspective, these vanishingly small features are impossible to
represent accurately, which makes direct meshing of the true geometry infeasible.

 To address this challenge, the literature typically offers two main strategies. One common
approach is to artificially adjust pebble diameters: either increasing them to enlarge the contact
area or decreasing them to eliminate contact altogether. While straightforward, this method alters
the local porosity, which in turn produces non-negligible changes in pressure drop and heat transfer
predictions. An alternative approach, which avoids these drawbacks, is to introduce fillets or
chamfers at contact regions. These geometric modifications prevent zero-area contact while exerting
only a minimal influence on porosity.

 In the present work, for the generation of pure hexahedral meshes suitable for NekRS, we adopt

 9

the chamfer-based approach to simplify both pebble-to-pebble and pebble-to-wall contacts. Two
parameters are required to define the chamfers. The first parameter is the chamfer diameter (Dch ),
which can be visualized as the diameter of a small cylinder inserted to bridge two pebbles (or a
pebble and a wall). The second parameter is the chamfer detection distance (Dde ). Whenever the
center-to-center distance between two pebbles falls below this threshold, a chamfer is introduced
between them. Similarly, when the center of a pebble lies within half of the detection distance from
a wall, a chamfer is added at that interface as well.

 Figure 3.3 illustrates the concept of pebble-to-pebble contact with a chamfer and the definitions
of Dch and Dde . For all meshes used in this report, the parameters are set to Dch = Dpebble /10 and
Dde = 1.025 Dpebble .

5 Conclusion and future work

In this year, we initiated a collaboration with Kairos Power LLC to investigate thermally induced
natural convection in pebble-bed systems. Two configurations are studied. The first is a canister
containing used pebbles that continue to generate residual heat. As the canister wall is cooled,
natural convection develops due to the imposed temperature gradient and buoyancy effects. The
second configuration is a reduced-diameter, Hermes-height pebble bed. With active cooling applied
at the side-top region, cold salt flows downward through the downcomer channel, which is separated
from the bed. Both cases have been executed, and preliminary results have been obtained.

 For future work, in addition to extending the study to a broader set of boundary conditions,
we plan to incorporate the following missing physics into the simulations. First, the P1 thermal
radiation model will be included to evaluate the impact of thermal radiation heat transfer. Second,
conjugate heat transfer will be considered, particularly in the canister case, where solid conduction
is a dominant mechanism under the present flow regime.

 From these simulations, data such as pebble-to-fluid and fluid-to-wall heat transfer coefficients
will be extracted. The role of thermal radiation will also be quantified by analyzing the ratio
of radiative heat flux to conductive/convective heat flux. The final results of this work will be
documented in the next report.
