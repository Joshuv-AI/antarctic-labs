Mediating Effects of Fear of Movement and Self-Efficacy Between Illness Perception and Health-Related Quality of Life in Pulmonary Hypertension
Authors: Juanjuan Xiong, Yiru Zhai, Aiyun Yu, Liting Wang, Zeqi Zheng, Qiong Yan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThe purpose of this study was to investigate the mediating effects of fear of movement and self-efficacy on the relationship between illness perception and health-related quality of life (HRQOL) in patients with pulmonary hypertension (PH).Patients and MethodsFrom July 2025 to February 2026, patients with PH were enrolled from the outpatient clinics or wards at two campuses of a tertiary hospital in Nanchang, China, using a convenience sampling method. For each patient, a range of data, including sociodemographic and clinical information, and validated scales assessed for illness perception, self-efficacy, fear of movement, and HRQOL were collated to examine the interaction. The mediation analysis was carried out with the PROCESS macro in SPSS.Results185 patients with PH were investigated with structured questionnaires. The mediation analysis showed that illness perception had a significant total effect on HRQOL. Additionally, the significant indirect effects of illness perception on HRQOL were mediated by fear of movement respectively, accounting for 16.4% and 8.7% of the total effect. We also observed that fear of movement and self-efficacy sequentially mediated the relationship between illness perception and HRQOL, which accounted for 4.6% of the total effect.ConclusionFear of movement and self-efficacy were multiple mediators of the relationship between illness perception and HRQOL among patients with PH. This highlights the importance of fear of movement and self-efficacy. Thus, potential clinical interventions targeted for strengthening self-care efficacy and relieving fear of movement may be beneficial for the improvement of HRQOL.Keywords: pulmonary hypertension, quality of life, fear of movement, self-efficacy, illness perception, mediation effect

Introduction

Pulmonary hypertension (PH) is defined as a complex clinicopathological syndrome characterized by progressive elevation of pulmonary arterial pressure resulting from increased pulmonary vascular resistance.1 This hemodynamic derangement imposes a sustained pressure overload on the right ventricle, precipitating maladaptive structural remodeling and functional impairment. The pathophysiological cascade manifests clinically as a progressive reduction in exercise tolerance and a markedly increased risk of right ventricular failure—the principal driver of morbidity and mortality in advanced disease stages. Substantial advances in targeted therapies have significantly improved the prognosis, primarily by delaying disease progression, contributing to demonstrable improvements in long-term survival.2 However, PH remains associated with profound functional impairment, leading to debilitating symptomatology (such as dyspnea, fatigue, and exertional chest pain), and causing severe deterioration in quality of life.3

Health-related quality of life (HRQOL) is defined as an individual’s subjective perception of life status within the context of culture and value systems, which is related to personal goals, expectations, and standards.4 HRQOL in the populations with PH is consistently reported to be among the lowest of all chronic cardiopulmonary diseases, with no evidence of improvement over time.5 Previous research has indicated that a series of potential factors, including illness perception, functional classification, exercise performance, and symptom burden may all have an impact on HRQOL.6 Given that HRQOL is inherently defined through the cognitive process of self-evaluation, its impact extends beyond physical symptoms to include emotional distress, emotional distress, burden of disease management, and functional dependency.7 Currently, optimizing HRQOL has been established as a therapeutic objective alongside improving survival and hemodynamic parameters in existing PH management guidelines. Therefore, to enable healthcare professionals to deliver personalized behavioral health interventions tailored to the individual needs of patients with PH, it is essential to understand their daily living conditions and the interrelationships among key influencing factors.

Background

Fear of movement is defined as an excessive and irrational fear of physical activity and movement due to a feeling of vulnerability to painful injury or re-injury, which is highly prevalent in chronic cardiopulmonary diseases.8 Studies have shown that fear of movement leads to negative consequences such as reduced exercise adherence and diminished quality of life in patients.9,10 For individuals with PH, the experience of dyspnea and exertion-induced discomfort may serve as powerful negative stimuli that deplete psychological resources, leading to the development of extreme negative perceptions of physical activity and subsequent avoidance of any activity.7 This creates a vicious cycle of deconditioning, worsened symptom burden, and further HRQOL decline.

Notably, self-efficacy is an individual’s belief in their capability to organize and execute courses of action required to manage prospective situations, which is a cornerstone of successful chronic disease management.11 Patients with PH will face numerous self-management challenges, including medication adherence, symptom monitoring, oxygen therapy management, and activity pacing.12 Therefore, self-efficacy for symptom management and maintaining daily activities is crucial for adherence to complex treatment regimens and engagement in safe physical activity. Self-efficacy beliefs may serve as a foundational resource that enables patients to navigate these challenges effectively, whereas fear of movement represents a more specific barrier related to physical activity engagement. Liu et al13 has found that the effect of fatigue on physical activity among kidney transplant recipients is partially mediated by fear of movement and physical self-efficacy. Meanwhile, study report has also indicated that fear of movement and low self-efficacy are important barriers in the management of cardiovascular risk.14 These two factors are likely interrelated that high fear of movement may directly erode an individual’s confidence in their ability to perform tasks, while strong self-efficacy may buffer against the development of debilitating fear.

As the most important clinical endpoint indicator for patients with PH, HRQOL is influenced by various factors such as demographics, physiology, and psychology. Evidence indicates that illness perception can directly influence HRQOL, and this association is not restricted to cardiovascular disease15,16 but rather generalizes to various other chronic conditions, such as hematology, epilepsy, cancer and inflammatory bowel disease.17–20 Zhang et al21 has also confirmed that coping strategy and psychological status can play multiple mediating roles in this correlation relationship. Simultaneously, Huang et al22 has also found that the direct effect was mediated by negative emotion (eg depression) in patients with heart failure. However, there is no previous study focusing on the mediating effects of variables on illness perception and HRQOL in PH. It can be reasonably assumed that fear of movement and self-efficacy may mediate the relationship between illness perception and HRQOL among patients with PH.

The Common-Sense Model of Self-Regulation (CSM) was constructed by Leventhal et al in the 1980s based on disease management, with illness perception as its core concept, to explain how individuals understand and respond to health threats.23 The model is structured around four elements: disease threat, illness representation, coping strategies, and outcome evaluation. The core purpose is to assist patients in effectively managing their health and psychological states, thereby facilitating the recovery process and alleviating negative consequences. Drawing on the aforementioned theory, this study aimed to examine whether illness perception (as a cognitive appraisal of disease threat) exerts a direct and indirect effect on HRQOL, and whether fear of movement (as an emotional representation) and self-efficacy (as a coping strategy) serve as multiple mediators.

The study aims to investigate the mediating effects of fear of movement and self -efficacy on the relationship between illness perception and quality of life in patients with pulmonary hypertension (PH).

Based on the theoretical framework, the study proposes the following hypotheses:

(1)Fear of movement mediates the relationship between illness perception and HRQOL;

Fear of movement mediates the relationship between illness perception and HRQOL;

(2)Self-efficacy mediates the relationship between illness perception and HRQOL;

Self-efficacy mediates the relationship between illness perception and HRQOL;

(3)Fear of movement and self-efficacy sequentially mediate the relationship between illness perception and HRQOL.

Materials and Methods

Design

This study was designed as a cross-sectional study using a convenient sampling approach to investigate the relationships among fear of movement, illness perception, self-efficacy, and quality of life in patients with PH. The reporting of this study adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines to ensure transparency.

Study Setting and Sampling

From July 2025 to February 2026, patients with PH were enrolled from the outpatient clinics or wards at two campuses of a tertiary hospital in Nanchang, China, using a convenience sampling method. The required sample size was calculated a priori using G*Power software (version 3.1). With a moderate effect size (f2= 0.15), an alpha level of 0.05, a statistical power of 0.95, and 18 predictors, the multiple regression analysis required a minimum of 120 participants. Accounting for a potential 15% invalid response rate, the target sample size was increased to 138. Ultimately, a total of 185 patients with PH were recruited, which was deemed adequate for the study.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Adults aged ≥18 years; (2) Clinically diagnosed with PH in accordance with the 2021 Chinese Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Pulmonary Hypertension,24 with the diagnosis confirmed by right heart catheterization; (3) Consciousness and an adequate level of comprehension and communication abilities; (4) provided written informed consent and voluntarily participated in the study. Patients were excluded if they had a history of psychiatric diagnosis, complicated with severe physical disease (liver and kidney damage, hematological system diseases) or malignant tumor, or had been involved in other studies.

Instrument with Validity and Reliability/Data Source

The information gathered pertains to various sociodemographic factors such as gender, age, educational, marital status, and regular exercise status prior to hospitalization. The clinical information consisted of PH duration, World Health Organization (WHO) functional class, 6-minute walk distance (6MWD), and number of PH exacerbations in the past year. All the information was either self-reported by the patients or abstracted from medical records.

The Tampa Scale for Kinesiophobia Heart (TSK-SV Heart) is the most widely utilized tool to evaluate the level of fear of movement in cardiac patients, which has been adapted and translated into the Chinese version by Lei et al.25 The scale comprises four dimensions with a total of 17 items. Each item is scored on a four-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 4 (strongly agree). The total score ranges from 17 to 68 points, with higher scores reflecting more severe kinesiophobia. A cut-off value of 37 points indicates a clinically significant level of fear of movement. The Chinese version has demonstrated adequate reliability and validity, with a Cronbach’s α of 0.859 and test–retest reliability of 0.792.

The Brief Illness Perception Questionnaire (BIPQ) was developed by Broadbent et al,26 and was translated and adapted into Chinese by Mei et al.27 The 9-item questionnaire is structured to evaluate three domains: illness-related cognitions, emotional impact, and illness comprehension. The item 9 is an open-ended question and is not included in the total score calculation. Each item is scored scale from 0 to 10 points, yielding a total score ranging from 0 to 80, with higher scores indicating more negative perceptions of the illness. The Chinese version of the scale showed good internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s α of 0.77 and a split-half reliability coefficient of 0.81.

Pulmonary Hypertension Assessment of Symptoms and Impact-10 Items (EmPHasis-10) was jointly developed by the UK Pulmonary Hypertension Association and the University of Manchester in 2013 to determine the impact of PH on patients’ quality of life. This scale is a unidimensional instrument, comprising 10 items with each scored from 0 to 5 points, resulting in a total possible score of 50. Higher scores indicate more severe impairment of health-related quality of life. The scale has been widely adopted internationally and was translated and validated into the Chinese version, demonstrating good reliability and validity (Cronbach’s α= 0.91).28

The Heart Health Self-efficacy and Self-management (HH-SESM) scale was proposed by Dr. Mares et al to assess the levels of self-management and self-efficacy in patients with heart disease.29 This scale consists of 12 items and comprises two subscales, self-efficacy and self-management, which share the same items for scoring. The scale assesses patients across several health behavior dimensions, including physical activity, dietary habits, medication adherence, psychosocial status, weight management, and smoking behavior. Each of the subscales is scored independently using a 4-point Likert scale (from 1 to 4 points). A higher score indicates a higher level of self-efficacy. The reliability and validity of this scale have been validated in coronary heart disease populations.30 The Cronbach’s α for the self-efficacy subscale was 0.773, and the test–retest reliability was 0.761.

Data Collection and Data Analysis

The data collection team was comprised of three head nurses and four nurses. All the researchers received a standardized training prior to the survey. The purpose of the study, along with its anonymity and confidentiality protocols, was explained to the patients by the investigators. Patients were provided with a series of informed consent forms to sign prior to their enrollment. Trained investigators distributed paper-and-pencil questionnaires on-site and provided standardized instructions to patients. When patients were unable to complete the questionnaire independently due to physical limitations or literacy issues, investigators read the questions aloud and recorded the patients’ responses verbatim. Completed questionnaires were collected upon completion and checked for completeness.

Date analysis was performed using SPSS 27.0 software. The threshold for statistical significance was defined as a two-tailed p-value of less than 0.05. Prior to conducting inferential analyses, data normality was assessed using P-P plots and Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests. Descriptive statistics included means and standard deviations for continuous variables, and frequencies with percentages for categorical variables. Group differences across sociodemographic and clinical characteristics were analyzed with independent t-tests or one-way ANOVA, followed by Bonferroni-corrected post hoc comparisons for multiple group analyses. Bivariate correlations between illness perception, fear of movement, self-efficacy, and quality of life were investigated using the Pearson correlation coefficient. A multiple mediation analysis was conducted using Model 6 of the SPSS PROCESS macro, employing bias-corrected bootstrapping with 5000 resamples while controlling for covariates that had shown statistical significance in prior analyses. Results were presented in the form of regression coefficients (β) along with 95% confidence intervals (CI) for the direct, indirect, and total effects. The mediating effect was considered significant when the 95% CI did not contain zero.

Ethical Considerations

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University (Approval number: IIT [2024] Clinical Ethics Review No.792).

Multiple Mediation Analysis

The mediation effects of fear of movement and self-efficacy were shown in Figure 1 and Table 3. Significant covariates in the univariate analyses were controlled in the mediation model. The mediation analysis showed that the total effect of illness perception (β = 0.608, 95% CI: 0.511, 0.705) on HRQOL was significant. We identified that illness perception had a significant total indirect effect on HRQOL (β=0.181, 95% CI: 0.090, 0.272) and accounting for 29.8% of the total effect. Additionally, the significant indirect effects of illness perception on HRQOL were mediated by fear of movement (β = 0.100, 95% CI: 0.028, 0.176) and self-efficacy (β = 0.053, 95% CI: 0.018, 0.098), respectively, accounting for 16.4% and 8.7% of the total effect. Furthermore, we also observed that fear of movement and self-efficacy sequentially mediated the relationship between illness perception and HRQOL (β = 0.028, 95% CI: 0.008, 0.052), which accounted for 4.6% of the total effect. Thus, the above results suggested that fear of movement and self-efficacy partially mediated the relationship between illness perception and HRQOL and the accuracy of hypotheses were verified.

Discussion

The cross-sectional study investigated the mediating roles of fear of movement and self-efficacy in the relationship between illness perception and HRQOL in patients with PH. The findings provide empirical support for the proposed mediation model, elucidating that fear of movement and self-efficacy serve as critical psychological determinants through both the sequential and independent pathways. Overall, the results were consistent with a serial mediation pathway, wherein negative illness perceptions were associated with greater fear of movement, which in turn was related to lower self-efficacy, and this pathway was further associated with poorer HRQOL. These results advance our understanding of the psychosocial processes underlying HRQOL impairment in PH and have important implications for clinical practice.

Our findings demonstrated that a significant indirect effect of illness perception on HRQOL through fear of movement. This finding is consistent with previous research, confirming that kinesiophobia serves as a psychological mechanism linking patients’ illness cognitions to their daily life experiences.31 This cognitive appraisal process is consistent with the theory of CSM, which posits that illness perception may directly determine emotional responses and coping behaviors, with maladaptive beliefs about a health threat giving rise to specific fears and avoidance cognitions.32 Patients with more negative illness perceptions, including beliefs about serious consequences, a chronic timeline, or lack of personal control, tend to develop greater fear of movement-related symptoms. As documented in previous research, an international survey found that 63% of PH patients who reported adverse events during exercise were more likely to experience ongoing exercise concerns and anxiety.33 In this qualitative research, fear, frustration, and uncertainty have been identified as prominent and recurrent barriers to exercise engagement among this population. Moreover, our findings are consistent with the fear-avoidance model applied in patients with cardioembolic stroke and chronic pain.34,35 In this model, threatening illness perceptions is associated with fear of movement, which in turn prompts patients to reduce physical activity. When fear leads to activity avoidance, patients enter a vicious cycle: reduced activity leads to deconditioning, which lowers the threshold for symptom provocation, which in turn reinforces fear beliefs. This cycle ultimately manifests as declines in physical function, role participation, and overall well-being, which has been verified in patients with heart failure.36

Similarly, our findings revealed that illness perception significantly predicted self-efficacy, with more negative illness beliefs associated with lower confidence in the challenges of managing disease-related. As postulated by Social Cognitive Theory, self-efficacy is shaped by how individuals interpret and appraise their experiences, including their understanding of their health condition.37 Negative illness perceptions, especially those regarding low personal control over the condition, may directly undermine patients’ confidence in their ability to manage symptoms, adhere to complex treatment regimens, and maintain valued activities. Self-efficacy emerged as a significant mediator, with its diminution in turn reducing patients’ engagement in proactive behaviors and adaptive coping strategies, which were ultimately associated with poorer HRQOL. Collectively, these findings underscore the critical role of patients’ confidence in their capabilities in disease management as a key determinant of life satisfaction in PH.

Additionally, the study revealed that fear of movement and self-efficacy were serial multiple mediators between illness perception and HRQOL. Our finding of a potential chain mediating effect is consistent with emerging evidence in cardiovascular populations.31 A recent cohort study involving 267 patients undergoing cardiac surgery examined the serial mediating roles of kinesiophobia and sedentary time in the relationship between illness perception and postoperative frailty.38 While the mediators in that study differ from ours, the underlying principle is highly relevant. According to the theory of CSM, threatening illness perceptions trigger a psycho-behavioral cascade that leads to adaptation behavioral changes, ultimately compromising health outcomes.32 Lack of self-efficacy can be regarded as a major barrier to patients’ modification of unhealthy behaviors. Conversely, patients with higher self-efficacy may be better equipped to confront feared activities, preventing the development or maintenance of excessive fear. Moreover, self-efficacy and individual emotional arousal is a mutually interacting process. This bidirectional relationship suggests that fear of movement and self-efficacy are not independent psychological processes but rather interconnected mechanisms that may reinforce each other over time, creating either virtuous or vicious cycles in patients’ adaptation to illness. A randomized controlled trial of an individual remote physiotherapy program for PH patients found that improvements in general self-efficacy accompanied enhanced exercise participation in daily activities, which provides indirect support for our analysis.39

From a treatment perspective, our mediation findings carry important implications for intervention. Accumulating evidence may facilitate the identification of specific therapeutic targets in this population. Cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) and metacognitive therapy have been reported to improve cognitive-behavioral processes and metacognitions, which are psychological mechanisms thought to underlie HRQOL, as well as emotion regulation.40 This appears consistent with findings from a trial of self-help CBT for depression in PH.41 In addition, an 8-week mobile app-based program combining mindfulness-based training and self-management yielded preliminary evidence of improvements in both body awareness and emotional regulation, suggesting potential benefits for symptom management and HRQOL in patients with PH.42 Similarly, qualitative findings from the 8-week web-based multimodal Mindfulness Meditation for PH program revealed that participants perceived the intervention as beneficial for symptom management, with mindfulness practice fostering awareness and acceptance and supporting the integration of skills into daily routines. Improvements in app functionality may further enhance engagement and facilitate gains in depression, symptom burden, and quality of life.43 In a word, cognitive behavioral interventions designed to address maladaptive beliefs may contribute to clinically meaningful improvements in HRQOL.

Limitations

Several limitations of this study must be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences about the relationships among variables. Although our mediation model was theoretically derived, longitudinal studies are needed to confirm the temporal ordering of illness perceptions, psychological mediators, and HRQOL outcomes. Second, the generalizability of our findings is constrained by the exclusive enrollment of Chinese patients. Cultural contexts shape not only self-reported illness perceptions and fear of movement but also receptivity to and effectiveness of behavioral health interventions. Third, the etiological heterogeneity of our sample (comprising all five PH groups) may have introduced variability in illness perceptions, symptom experience, and emotional responses, given their distinct pathophysiologies and clinical prognoses. Therefore, the predominance of research in Group 1 PAH further underscores the need for subgroup-specific investigations. Finally, all data were collected through self-report measures, which may be subject to recall bias and common method variance. Future studies should incorporate objective measures of physical activity and clinical indicators of disease severity.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study indicates that the effect of illness perception on HRQOL in patients with PH is mediated both respectively by fear of movement and self-efficacy, and together by them in serial. These findings suggest that effective PH management requires medical staff to address both the physical aspects of the disease as well as the psychological factors impacting patients’ daily lives. A stepped and integrated approach, encompassing cognitive restructuring, mindfulness-based training, behavioral activation, and targeted psychological support, may serve as an effective strategy to significantly enhance HRQOL. As the paradigm shifts toward more comprehensive care models, recognizing and targeting these interconnected psychological mechanisms will be essential to achieving better clinical outcomes.
