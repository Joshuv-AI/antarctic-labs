Adipocytokine Pathway Dysregulation Characterizes Immune-Related Molecular Subtypes and Candidate Biomarkers in Myocardial Infarction
Authors: Jianxiong Liu, Weiqing Wang, Li Zhen, Dihua Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMyocardial infarction (MI) is a critical cardiovascular event with a complex pathophysiology. This study aimed to investigate the role of the adipocytokine signaling pathway in MI, assess its potential association with diagnostic features, and explore its association with molecular subtypes.MethodsGene expression profiles from the GSE48060 and GSE66360 datasets were normalized and merged. Pathway activity was quantified using single-sample Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (ssGSEA). To prioritize genes of interest, we applied a machine learning approach using the XGBoost algorithm alongside multivariate logistic regression. Introduction

Myocardial infarction (MI) is one of the most critical and life-threatening cardiovascular events, characterized by the interruption of blood flow to a part of the heart, leading to myocardial cell death.1,2 This condition, commonly known as a heart attack, has significant morbidity and mortality rates worldwide.3 Despite advancements in medical interventions and a deeper understanding of its pathophysiology, MI remains a leading cause of death globally.4 The underlying mechanisms of MI are complex, involving multiple biological pathways and molecular interactions.5,6 Among these, the adipocytokine pathway has emerged as a significant player in the pathogenesis of MI, linking metabolic and inflammatory processes to cardiovascular health.7

Recent studies have highlighted the role of adipocytokines, a group of bioactive molecules secreted by adipose tissue, in modulating cardiovascular functions and contributing to the inflammatory milieu post-MI.8 Adipocytokines such as adiponectin, resistin, and leptin have been shown to influence myocardial metabolism, insulin sensitivity, and inflammatory responses, all of which are crucial in the context of MI.9–12 While transcriptomic profiling studies have increasingly applied pathway-based analyses to cardiovascular diseases, the adipocytokine pathway has not been examined with the same depth as immune or metabolic signaling axes in MI. Prior work has either targeted single adipocytokine genes in isolation or has embedded the pathway within broader metabolic screens, without a dedicated focus on how coordinated dysregulation of this specific signaling network relates to patient heterogeneity.13 Moreover, although consensus clustering and immune infiltration deconvolution are now established tools for molecular subtyping in oncology and, more recently, in cardiovascular research,14 their application to stratify MI patients based explicitly on adipocytokine pathway activity remains largely unexplored.

The unresolved question driving this study is whether the aggregate activity of the adipocytokine pathway—rather than the expression of individual genes—can distinguish clinically meaningful MI subgroups and point to candidate markers that reflect underlying disease biology beyond standard cardiac enzymes. We reasoned that a focused analysis of this pathway might capture aspects of the metabolic-inflammatory interface that are not readily apparent from broader transcriptome-wide clustering.

For this analysis, we selected two publicly available MI transcriptomic datasets: GSE48060 and GSE66360. Both datasets were generated on the same microarray platform (Affymetrix GPL570) and consist of peripheral blood samples from MI patients and non-MI controls, which facilitates technical harmonization and minimizes cross-platform variability during data merging. The clinical definitions of MI in both cohorts are based on standard diagnostic criteria, and the sample sources are comparable, making them suitable for an integrated analysis aimed at identifying shared transcriptional signatures. Data Concerning Datasets Associated with MI

To address our research question, after normalizing and merging the raw expression matrices, we employed ssGSEA to evaluate pathway activities. Consensus clustering was then performed specifically on genes annotated to the adipocytokine pathway to define MI subtypes, an approach distinct from prior subtyping efforts that relied on global differentially expressed genes or immune-related gene sets. Further analyses were conducted to explore the biological and immune profiles of these subtypes and to identify genes within the pathway that might serve as exploratory markers for MI.

The primary contribution of this work lies not in the novelty of the individual methods but in their integrated application to a pathway that has received comparatively less systematic attention in MI transcriptomics. By anchoring the subtyping and gene prioritization explicitly within the adipocytokine signaling framework, the study offers a focused perspective on how this specific metabolic-immune interface varies across MI patients. These observations may inform the selection of pathway-centric markers for future, larger-scale validation studies and underscore the potential relevance of pathway-level heterogeneity in cardiovascular risk stratification.

Methods

Data Preprocessing and Batch Effect Correction

Raw microarray data (CEL files) for the MI datasets GSE48060 and GSE66360 were downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database. Preprocessing and normalization of the raw data were performed using the affy package. Background correction, quantile normalization, and probe summarization were conducted using the Robust Multi-array Average (RMA) algorithm. Probe identifiers were mapped to official gene symbols using the annotation file corresponding to the GPL570 platform. For cases where multiple probes mapped to the same gene symbol, the median expression value across all corresponding probes was retained as the representative expression level for that gene. Probes lacking a corresponding gene symbol or mapping to ambiguous or deprecated identifiers were excluded from downstream analyses. Following individual dataset preprocessing, the two expression matrices were merged by common gene symbols. Initial assessment of batch effects was conducted using Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) via the umap R package. To correct for residual non-biological variation between the datasets, we applied the ComBat algorithm from the sva R package, which employs an empirical Bayes framework for batch harmonization. Post-correction UMAP visualization was used to confirm effective mitigation of batch effects.

Gene Expression and Pathway Analysis

To assess alterations in the adipocytokine pathway in MI, we performed single-sample Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (ssGSEA) to compute pathway activity scores for each sample.15 Adipocytokine pathway gene sets were obtained from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB) (https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb). The ssGSEA scores were compared between MI and control groups using the GSVA package, and statistical significance was determined using Wilcoxon rank-sum tests. Differential gene expression analysis was conducted using the limma package, with significance thresholds set at adjusted p-value (FDR) < 0.05. Heatmaps and boxplots were generated using pheatmap and ggplot2 packages, respectively.

Machine Learning Analysis

Gene expression data for the pathway-related genes were extracted from the MI dataset. The XGBoost model was implemented using the xgboost R package. Hyperparameter tuning was performed using a grid search approach combined with 10-fold cross-validation on the training set. The parameters optimized included: maximum tree depth (range: 3–6), learning rate (eta; range: 0.01–0.3), subsample ratio (range: 0.5–1.0), and column sampling ratio by tree (range: 0.5–1.0). The final model was trained using the optimal hyperparameter combination and subsequently evaluated on the held-out test set to assess generalizability. Feature importance was calculated based on the gain metric, which quantifies the average improvement in accuracy contributed by a feature across all splits in which it is used. Genes were ranked according to their importance scores, and those with non-zero gain were retained as candidate contributors. In parallel, multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed using the glm function with a binomial family in R. To address potential multicollinearity among predictor variables, variance inflation factors (VIFs) were calculated using the car package, and variables with VIF values exceeding 5 were considered indicative of problematic collinearity and were excluded from the final model. The event-to-variable ratio was assessed to ensure model stability; with 80 MI cases and 71 controls in the merged cohort, the model was restricted to a maximum of five predictor variables to maintain a ratio exceeding 10 events per variable, consistent with recommended guidelines for logistic regression. Variable selection was performed using a stepwise approach based on the Akaike information criterion (AIC). Odds ratios and corresponding 95% confidence intervals were calculated, and statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. The intersection of genes identified by both XGBoost and logistic regression was visualized using a Venn diagram generated with the VennDiagram R package.

Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) Curve Analysis

ROC curve analysis was employed to evaluate the discriminatory capacity of the identified candidate genes within the merged microarray cohort. ROC curves were generated using the pROC package. The expression values of IRS2 and SOCS3 from the combined GSE48060 and GSE66360 dataset were used as predictor variables, with the binary disease status (MI versus non-MI control) serving as the response variable. The area under the curve (AUC) was computed to quantify the overall diagnostic performance of each gene.

Clinical Sample Collection and Quantitative Real-Time PCR (qRT-PCR) Validation

Peripheral blood samples were collected from 6 patients diagnosed with MI and 6 healthy controls. The clinical characteristics of the participants are shown in Table S1. All participants gave written informed consent prior to enrollment, and the study protocol was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Hubei Provincial Hospital of Traditional Chinese Medicine (approval number: HBZY2025-36). Total RNA was extracted from the blood samples using TRIzol™ reagent (Invitrogen, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA concentration and purity were assessed using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA); samples with an A260/A280 ratio between 1.8 and 2.0 were used for subsequent analysis. For cDNA synthesis, 1 µg of total RNA from each sample was reverse transcribed into first-strand cDNA using the First-Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (KeyGen BioTECH, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. qRT-PCR was performed using SYBR Green Premix (TaKaRa, Japan) on an Applied Biosystems real-time PCR system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The relative expression levels of target genes were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method and normalized to an endogenous control gene.

Consensus Clustering for MI Subtype Identification

Unsupervised consensus clustering was performed using the ConsensusClusterPlus package to stratify MI patients based on adipocytokine pathway-related gene expression. The analysis employed hierarchical clustering with Pearson correlation distance and Ward’s linkage, with 1000 resampling iterations and an 80% item resampling ratio. The optimal number of clusters was determined by inspecting the cumulative distribution function (CDF) curves and the delta area plot. Differential expression between subtypes was analyzed using limma package, and results were visualized via volcano plots and heatmaps.

Functional Enrichment Analysis

Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses of differentially expressed genes between MI subtypes C1 and C2 was conducted using the clusterProfiler package. Significantly enriched terms (FDR < 0.05) were visualized using chord diagrams and dot plots.

Immune Cell Infiltration and Immune-Related Pathway Activity Profiling

To characterize the immune microenvironment, we estimated the enrichment levels of 24 immune cell populations using predefined marker gene sets from published literature.16 Single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) implemented in the GSVA R package was employed to compute immune infiltration scores. Group comparisons between C1 and C2 subtypes were conducted using the nonparametric Wilcoxon test, with significance thresholds adjusted for false discovery rate (FDR < 0.05). For immune pathway analysis, curated gene signatures were obtained from the ImmPort repository (https://www.immport.org). Pathway activity quantification was performed via ssGSEA, followed by differential analysis between subtypes using the Wilcoxon test (FDR-corrected p < 0.05). Correlation analyses were conducted to examine relationships between adipocytokine pathway activation, immune cell infiltration, and immune pathway activity. Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients were calculated, with multiple testing adjustments applied using the Benjamini-Hochberg method.

Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA)

Pathway-level differences between MI subtypes C1 and C2 were quantified using Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA), which was performed on hallmark gene sets obtained from MSigDB.17 The GSVA scores were compared between the two subtypes, and significant differences in pathway activity were visualized using boxplot. The analysis was carried out using the GSVA R package, with statistical significance determined by the Wilcoxon test (p < 0.05).

Results

Adipocytokine Pathway Alterations and Gene Expression Analysis in MI

The raw expression matrices from each dataset were normalized and merged using R software. Initially, the combined dataset exhibited a clear batch effect, as illustrated in Figure 1A. The UMAP visualization shows distinct clusters corresponding to the two original datasets (GSE48060 in red and GSE66360 in blue), indicating significant batch-related variances. To mitigate the batch effect, we applied the ComBat function from the “sva” package in R. Post-correction, the UMAP plot (Figure 1B) displays a more homogeneous distribution of samples, with the previous distinct clustering effectively minimized. This suggests that batch effects were substantially removed, allowing for a more accurate downstream analysis of myocardial infarction-related gene expression changes.

As illustrated in Figure 2A, ssGSEA scores revealed a significant elevation in adipocytokine pathway activity in the MI group compared to the control group (p < 0.001). Box plots in Figure 2B demonstrate significantly higher expression levels of genes associated with the adipocytokine pathway in the MI group compared to controls. Notable genes with significant upregulation in MI include ACSL1, ADIPOR1, CD36, IRS2, NFKBIA, NFKBIE, RXRA, SOCS3, STAT3, and TNF (p < 0.001 for all). The heatmap for differentially expressed genes in Figure 2C visually corroborates the findings from the box plots. Strong positive correlations were observed among most of the genes, indicating their coordinated upregulation in the context of MI. In conclusion, these results underscore a pronounced activation of the adipocytokine pathway in MI, accompanied by significant alterations in gene expression and notable gene-gene correlations, potentially implicating this pathway in the pathophysiology of MI.

Identification and Validation of Hub Genes in the Adipocytokine Signaling Pathway

To pinpoint critical regulators within the adipocytokine signaling pathway, we employed a machine learning approach. Using the XGBoost algorithm on the expression profiles of differentially expressed genes within this pathway, we identified four genes—IRS2, SOCS3, ACSL1, and NFKBIE—as the most significant contributors to the classification model (Figure 3A). In parallel, a multivariate logistic regression analysis was conducted to identify independent predictors associated with MI. This analysis revealed six independent genes: ADIPOR1, CD36, IKBKB, IRS2, NFKBIA, and SOCS3 (Table 2). By intersecting the genes derived from both the XGBoost and logistic regression models, we identified an overlap of two genes, IRS2 and SOCS3 (Figure 3B). To evaluate the discriminatory capacity of the identified candidate genes in distinguishing MI samples from non-MI controls within the merged microarray cohort, ROC curve analysis was performed. As shown in Figure S1, both IRS2 and SOCS3 demonstrated moderate to high diagnostic potential. Specifically, SOCS3 exhibited superior performance with an AUC of 0.793, while IRS2 yielded an AUC of 0.733. To validate these findings, we assessed the expression levels of IRS2 and SOCS3 in an independent cohort of clinical blood samples from MI patients using qPCR. Consistent with our bioinformatic predictions, both IRS2 and SOCS3 showed significant differential expression in the MI samples compared to controls, confirming the reliability of our analytical pipeline (Figure 3C).

Subtype Identification Based on Adipocytokine Pathway-Related Genes

In order to identify potential subtypes of MI patients based on adipocytokine pathway-related gene expression, consensus clustering analysis was conducted. Evaluation of the cumulative distribution function (CDF) curves and the relative change in area under the CDF curve indicated that the optimal number of clusters was k = 2 (Figure S2). Application of this clustering approach partitioned the MI samples into two distinct molecular subtypes, designated Cluster 1 (C1) and Cluster 2 (C2) (Figure 4A). Differential expression analysis between the two subtypes (C1 and C2) identified 3666 upregulated genes and 2019 downregulated genes in C1 compared to C2, as depicted in the volcano plot in Figure 4B. A heatmap of the top differentially expressed genes between C1 and C2 is shown in Figure 4C. This heatmap illustrates distinct expression profiles, with certain genes consistently upregulated in C2 subgroup. In summary, the consistent clustering analysis revealed two distinct subtypes of MI patients based on adipocytokine pathway-related gene expression. Subsequent differential expression analysis confirmed significant molecular differences between these subtypes, providing new insights into the heterogeneity of MI.

Enrichment Analysis of Adipocytokine Pathway-Related MI Subtypes

To further characterize the biological differences between the two identified MI subtypes (C1 and C2), we performed enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes. The chord diagram in Figure 5A presents the GO enrichment analysis results for the differentially expressed genes between C1 and C2 subtypes. The analysis identified significant enrichment in several GO terms, including myeloid leukocyte activation, cell activation, immune effector processes, defense response, myeloid leukocyte-mediated immunity, leukocyte-mediated immunity, regulation of immune system processes, exocytosis, response to biotic stimulus, etc. Key enriched pathways include tuberculosis, osteoclast differentiation, leishmaniasis, pathogenic Escherichia coli infection, NF-kappa B signaling pathway, C-type lectin receptor signaling pathway, phagosome, legionellosis, B cell receptor signaling pathway, and Chagas disease (Figure 5B). In summary, the enrichment analysis revealed significant biological pathways and processes that differentiate the two adipocytokine pathway-related MI subtypes, suggesting a role of immune modulation and infection pathways in the pathophysiology of MI.

Analysis of Immune Cell Infiltration and Immune Pathway Activity

To further elucidate the immunological differences between the adipocytokine pathway-related MI subtypes, we performed a comprehensive analysis of immune cell infiltration levels and immune-related pathway activity. As shown in Figure 7A, significant differences were observed for several immune cell types. Subtype C2 exhibited higher infiltration levels for neutrophils, macrophages, iDC, and eosinophils, while subtype C1 showed higher infiltration levels for Th2 cells, T cells, pDC, NK cells, and NK CD56bright cells (* p < 0.05, ** p < 0.01, *** p < 0.001). Figure 7B shows a comparative analysis of immune pathway activity between the two subtypes. Subtype C2 demonstrated higher activation in pathways such as antigen processing and presentation, TNF family members, TCR signaling pathway, natural killer cell cytotoxicity, interleukins receptor, interleukins, chemokine receptors, BCR signaling pathway, and antimicrobials. The correlation heatmap in Figure S3A shows the relationship between adipocytokine pathway activity and various immune cell infiltration levels. Positive correlations were observed between adipocytokine pathway activity and infiltration levels of neutrophils, macrophages, iDC, and eosinophils, while negative correlations were evident for pDC, NK cells, and NK CD56bright cells. Figure S3B presents the correlation heatmap between adipocytokine pathway activity and immune pathway activity levels. There are strong positive correlations observed between adipocytokine pathway activity and antigen processing and presentation, TNF family members, TCR signaling pathway, natural killer cell cytotoxicity, interleukins receptor, chemokine receptors, BCR signaling pathway, and antimicrobials. The analysis revealed significant differences in immune cell infiltration and immune pathway activity between the two MI subtypes, emphasizing the distinct immunological landscapes of C1 and C2. These findings underscore the importance of immune modulation in the pathophysiology of the identified MI subtypes.

Discussion

In this study, we identified significant alterations in the adipocytokine pathway in MI, with substantial upregulation of key genes such as ADIPOR1, CD36, IRS2, and NFKBIA, as shown by ssGSEA scores, box plots, and heatmaps. These observations indicate that the adipocytokine pathway is transcriptionally active in peripheral blood samples from MI patients and are consistent with prior reports linking this signaling axis to cardiovascular inflammation. Using consensus clustering based on adipocytokine pathway-related gene expression, we further delineated two subgroups (C1 and C2) within the MI samples, which exhibited divergent immune cell composition and pathway activity profiles. It should be emphasized that these subtypes were derived from a pathway-focused analysis rather than an unbiased genome-wide approach, and their clinical significance has not been established in this study.

The activation of the adipocytokine pathway in MI is consistent with previous studies that have implicated this pathway in the inflammatory response and cardiovascular diseases. Adipocytokines, including adiponectin and leptin, have been shown to influence a variety of processes in cardiovascular pathology, including endothelial function, inflammation, and lipid metabolism.18,19 Adipocytokine signaling via receptors such as ADIPOR1 and CD36, which were found to be upregulated in our MI samples, has been implicated in the regulation of immune responses and tissue remodeling in MI.7 Specifically, CD36 has been shown to mediate the uptake of oxidized low-density lipoprotein (oxLDL), contributing to the inflammatory cascade in atherosclerosis.20,21 The upregulation of CD36 observed in the MI group is therefore concordant with established mechanisms of lipid-driven inflammation. However, the present data cannot distinguish whether this transcriptional shift contributes causally to MI pathogenesis or represents a secondary response to the ischemic insult.

The identification of two subgroups based on adipocytokine pathway gene expression adds to the growing recognition that MI encompasses molecularly distinct patient subsets. Previous studies have suggested that MI is not a homogeneous disease but rather a complex and multifaceted condition with diverse underlying molecular mechanisms.22 The differences in pathway activity and immune infiltration patterns observed between C1 and C2 support the notion of biological heterogeneity within MI. Nonetheless, because these subgroups were defined using a targeted set of adipocytokine-related genes, they should be regarded as exploratory stratifications that capture variation along this particular signaling axis. Whether these subgroups correspond to differences in clinical presentation, therapeutic responsiveness, or long-term outcomes cannot be determined from the current data and would require correlation with prospectively collected clinical information and interventional studies.

Several features of the data are consistent with a more pronounced inflammatory signature in the C2 subtype. The observed differences in immune cell infiltration between the two subtypes, with subtype C2 showing higher levels of neutrophils, macrophages, and eosinophils, align with a transcriptional profile enriched for pro-inflammatory signaling.23,24 This is consistent with the higher activity of immune-related pathways in subtype C2, including antigen processing and presentation, TNF signaling, and TCR signaling, which are known to be involved in the activation of immune responses in cardiovascular disease.25,26 Subtype C1, in contrast, exhibited higher inferred infiltration of T cells and NK cells, which may reflect a distinct immunological state. However, the functional implications of these inferred differences remain speculative in the absence of direct cellular profiling or functional assays. The upregulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF, which was also found to be elevated in the MI group, further supports the idea that adipocytokines may contribute to inflammation and immune activation in MI.27 Nevertheless, these associations are derived from computational deconvolution of bulk transcriptomic data and should be interpreted with appropriate caution.

The differential pathway activity observed between the two MI subtypes, including enhanced activity in NF-kappa B signaling, P53 signaling, and IL6/JAK/STAT3 signaling in subtype C2, highlights biological processes that vary between the subgroups.28–30 While these pathways are recognized for their involvement in inflammation, cell survival, and apoptosis, the present study does not establish whether the observed transcriptional differences correspond to functional alterations at the protein level or cellular phenotype. The identification of these pathways provides a foundation for future investigations aimed at determining whether pathway-directed interventions might differentially affect MI patient subgroups, but such therapeutic implications remain hypothetical at this stage.

However, there are several limitations to this study. First, the sample size of the clinical validation cohort was limited (n = 6 per group), which restricts the statistical power of the qRT-PCR analysis. The observed differential expression of IRS2 and SOCS3 should therefore be viewed as preliminary confirmation of directionality rather than definitive clinical validation; larger, well-characterized cohorts will be necessary to establish the generalizability of these candidate markers. Second, while the analysis of gene expression and immune cell infiltration provides valuable insights, it is still largely correlative in nature. Mechanistic studies, including in vitro manipulation of IRS2 and SOCS3 in relevant cardiac or immune cell models and in vivo functional assays, are required to determine whether these genes actively contribute to MI pathophysiology or merely reflect downstream transcriptional responses. Third, the molecular subtyping approach employed here was intentionally anchored to adipocytokine pathway-related genes, which represents a hypothesis-driven strategy rather than an unbiased, genome-wide classification. As such, the C1 and C2 subtypes may capture only a subset of the true biological heterogeneity present in MI patients, and these cluster assignments have not been validated in an independent external dataset. Replication of this subtyping framework in additional transcriptomic cohorts will be essential to assess its robustness and reproducibility. Finally, the study focused primarily on the adipocytokine pathway and immune modulation, and future studies could explore the involvement of other molecular pathways in MI subtypes.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study provides exploratory evidence for the involvement of the adipocytokine pathway in the transcriptional landscape of MI. The identification of two subgroups based on adipocytokine pathway-related gene expression underscores the molecular heterogeneity that exists among MI patients and offers a framework for future investigation into pathway-specific variation. However, the clinical relevance of these subgroups, including any potential implications for treatment stratification, remains to be determined through external validation in larger, independent cohorts and correlation with longitudinal clinical outcomes. The genes IRS2 and SOCS3 emerged as candidate markers in this analysis and warrant further study in well-powered validation sets and functional experiments. At present, the findings should be regarded as hypothesis-generating observations that contribute to the broader effort to characterize heterogeneity in MI rather than as established diagnostic or therapeutic conclusions.
