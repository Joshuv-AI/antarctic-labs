 ABSTRACT
 Critical experiments, sometimes referred to zero-power reactors, are crucial
tools in developing and validating nuclear physics predictions and thus an
indispensable capability to ensure criticality safety during all parts of the nuclear
fuel cycle and in reducing uncertainties in reactor physics predictions toward
optimizing nuclear energy production. The System Physics Advanced Reactor
Facility (SPARC) project was recently initiated to enable large-scale criticality
experiments using a horizontal split table machine well suited toward solid core
materials systems (fuel, moderator, reflector). The facility selected for this
mission was a former pool-type research reactor building and thus also well
suited toward a second critical experiment capability able to house full-size light-
water reactor (LWR) fuel bundles. A conceptual design study was undertaken to
review past water tank critical experiments used for LWR physics experiments
and to develop an early engineering design for a new critical assembly tank
(CAT). The work described here shows that a relatively simple CAT concept can
be constructed and deployed in the SPARC facility to meet the urgent demands
for new critical experiments on advanced LWR fuel bundles designs. The
SPARC facility layout is conducive to the receipt and upending of LWR fuel
bundles using existing containers and equipment from the LWR industry. The
facility’s overhead crane can then be used to handle fuel bundles and place them
in a vertical storage rack or in the CAT for critical experiments, both of which fit
within the building’s “open basement” alongside other equipment planned for
SPARC and the horizontal split table. A slightly lower area in the basement can
serve as a large drain tank so that fail-safe valves drain the CAT reactor tank for
safe shutdown. Neutronic configurations were determined where a 3 × 3 array of
fuel bundles can be surrounded by full-length “loose rods” to adjust reactivity so
that critical is achieved when the bundles are fully submerged. Viable
configurations were determined for both pressurized- and boiling-water-reactor-
type fuel bundles. This design concept was used to develop an early planning
basis for establishing the CAT capability alongside the otherwise planned
SPARC project in order to help streamline the process. Recent presidential
executive orders have highlighted the need to achieve power uprates in LWR
plants and the CAT capability will be a crucial element of these initiatives. Based
on the work described herein, it is recommended that an earnest and timely
project begin in order to establish this urgently needed capability.

 1. INTRODUCTION
 Critical assembly machines, sometimes referred to as zero-power reactors, have been crucial to
support the development and validation of nuclear physics predictions since the beginning of nuclear
technology. While many of these facilities existed historically, most have now been decommissioned.
Those facilities that remain operational today are treasured facilities with strong experimental programs,
but none are able to create critical experiments from large arrays of fuels, moderators, and reflectors to
represent reactor-scale core lattices or configurations with high neutron leakage and sensitivity to
intermediate neutron energies. The U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) and Nuclear Regulatory
Commission (NRC) have important goals to enable the safe deployment and expansion of nuclear energy.
The current light-water reactor (LWR) commercial reactor fleet operates using nuclear fuel with <5%
low-enriched uranium (LEU), but many new advanced reactor designs require higher enrichments
between 10% and 20%, referred to as high-assay low-enriched uranium (HALEU). The DOE/NRC
Criticality Safety for Commercial-Scale HALEU Fuel Cycle and Transportation project was established
to fill these data gaps for HALEU criticality safety.
 This project is also supporting a new effort to establish a large critical experiment facility, referred to
as the System Physics Advanced Reactor Critical (SPARC) facility, to establish a large critical
experiment capability with a focus on HALEU criticality safety research. The key feature of the SPARC
facility will be a horizontal split table (HST) able to achieve criticality by bringing two assemblies of core
materials into close proximity with each other with remote-controlled moving platforms. After a robust
feasibility study [1], it was determined that the former Special Power Excursion Reactor Test IV (SPERT-
IV) reactor building will be outfitted to host the HST and become the SPARC facility. This building is in
the Arco desert at the Idaho National Laboratory (INL) and is identified internally as Power Burst Facility
Building 613 (PBF-613).
 The SPARC feasibility study found that a need existed for large-scale water-moderated critical
experiments to support the deployment of advanced LWR fuel assemblies planning to employ uranium
enrichments between 5% and 10%, referred to as LEU+. Like historic HST machines, all of the historic
large water tank critical facilities have been decommissioned, and the capability needs to be reestablished.
The key reason for performing such experiments is to enable the deployment of LEU+ advanced fuel
bundle designs and characterize their spatial fission rate predictions with low uncertainties to enable
increased power output from LWR plants. This need has been strongly highlighted with the urgent
directives of a recent U.S. presidential executive order to uprate and expand LWR power production [2].
Since the SPERT-IV reactor was a pool-type research reactor, the building that housed it is ideally suited
for establishing a large water tank with overhead handling capability for large LWR fuel bundles. Thus,
the Advanced Fuels Campaign, who has overseen DOE-funded LWR fuel research in recent years,
supported the present study to develop a conceptual design for a second critical assembly capability at
SPARC focused on water-moderated reactor needs. This experiment capability is referred to as the critical
assembly tank (CAT).
 1.1. The Need for Water-Moderated Criticality Experiments
 Zero-power reactors play an indispensable role in validating reactor physics calculations. The purpose
of validating neutronics codes is to characterize uncertainties stemming from the nuclear data as well as
from modeling assumptions or simplifications made within the neutron transport code. Historically, low-
power reactors were used for fundamental reactor physics studies, such as measuring criticality of a
system for various configurations (pitch, fuel enrichment, poison types, etc.), as well as detailed neutron
flux characteristics such as neutron spectrum, spatial distribution of reaction rates, and kinetic parameters,

 13

 for example. These critical experiments played a key role during the dawn of the LWR industry, including
 both pressurized-water reactor (PWR) and boiling-water reactor (BWR) designs, using facilities that are
 now decommissioned. At the time, computational models were much simpler than now owing to limited
 computational resources and less mature nuclear data libraries.
 However, it is important to note that experiments performed decades ago are still routinely used by
 the LWR industry for model validation. Indeed, the validation of neutronics codes is an essential
 component of licensing topical reports, which are prepared by vendors and submitted to the U.S. NRC for
 review and approval. Thanks to progress made in computing power, new codes and methods are regularly
 introduced, from finite difference two-dimensional (2D) diffusion codes such as PDQ used in the 1970s
 up to current state-of-the-art lattice physics/nodal core simulators such as CASMO5-SIMULATE5,
 PARAGON2-ANC9, LANCR02/PANACEA, and APOLLO2-ARTEMIS for example, which are
 Studsvik, Westinghouse, Global Nuclear Fuel (GNF), and Framatome’s core physics tools, respectively.
 The validation of these modern codes used for core design and reload analyses of the current fleet still
 heavily rely on legacy critical experiments and integral measurements performed in zero-power reactors
 that were all shutdown some decades ago. Table 1 gives a summary of critical experiments used for the
 validation of lattice physics codes from Westinghouse, Framatome, and Studsvik as listed in publicly
 available versions of licensing topical reports.

 There were two main categories of experimental data from criticality experiments, each serving a
 specific purpose. The first category was the measurement of reactivity (neutron multiplication factor).
 Reactivity measurements that are representative of in-core LWR configurations were intended for
 validating neutron multiplication factor (Keff) calculations. The experiments listed above were used with
 UO2 fuel pins, UO2-PuO2 fuel pins, and various types of neutron poisons (borosilicate glass, B4C,
 gadolinia, erbia, and hafnium, for example) that are used in control rods or as burnable absorbers. Some
 of the critical experiments also investigated in configurations near the reflector (heavy baffle reflector),
 where neutron flux gradients are important. Reactivity measurements have also been performed, which
 are representative of transport casks or a storage rack of fresh assemblies, also intended for validating Keff
 calculations but for out-of-core, criticality safety studies. Reactivity measurements are important for
 validating and quantifying uncertainties in predicted quantities, such as core excess reactivity, control rod
 worth, and burnable poison worth. Similarly, for criticality safety, these experiments were important to
 confidently estimate margins to criticality.
 The second category was a measurement of reaction rates, usually by the gamma scanning of
 individual fuel pins shortly after performing critical experiments. Pin-by-pin fission rate measurements
 were made with the goal of representing in-core LWR assembly power distributions. The purpose of these
 experiments was to validate pin power predictions from lattice physics codes. Gamma scan measurements
 of individual fuel pins provide the spatial distribution of La-140, itself produced by the decay of Ba-140,
 a fission product with a half-life of 12.8 days whose concentration is proportional to fission rates. In most
 situations, experiments were 2D in nature with axially uniform fuel pins. Fission rates were either
 measured at some axial elevation (core mid-plane), or for the entire length, thus giving one data point per
 rod. These experiments were modeled using a 2D lattice physics code, provided that the axial geometric
 buckling has been measured, which is achieved by measuring the axial neutron flux profile. Some
 campaigns aimed at measuring the radial power profile within a fuel pellet via post-irradiation destructive
 techniques.

 16

 Additionally, these experiments performed 238U resonance integral measurements, an important
parameter in core design as 239Pu is bred through neutron capture in 238U. In the past, empirical
correlations were derived from these measurements, with resonance integrals tabulated as a function of
fuel surface and mass. Currently, these data are directly compared to predicted values from lattice physics
codes. These measurements were usually done using cadmium-covered fuel rods to remove thermal
neutrons, and 239Np activity was then measured via gamma scan to provide an estimate of 238U capture.
Some experiments such as those performed in two-region critical experiment or B&W facilities also
measured the radial profile of the 238U resonance integral inside the pellet by cutting the fuel pellet into
different rings, dissolving them, and performing a gamma emission measurement for each of the radial
solutions.
 Fission rate measurements in these low-power reactors play a significant role in validating pin power
predictions made by LWR core simulators. In commercial power plants, in-core neutron instruments that
are periodically inserted for mapping the axial flux distribution in some assemblies or locations are unable
to measure local pin-by-pin power variations. Pin-by-pin fission rates can only be measured via gamma
scans of individual pins. While pin-by-pin gamma scans have been done in the past at commercial power
plants, primarily for BWR fuel assemblies, they require an important investment in experimental setup
given the high dose rates for irradiated fuel, requiring this operation to be done underwater in the spent
fuel pool. Local pin power distributions are important for predicting margins to thermal limits, such as the
departure from nucleate boiling ratio, fuel centerline temperature, peak clad temperature, and linear heat
generation rate for example. In most production core simulators, 3D pin powers are reconstructed from
node-average power distributions typically produced by low-order nodal diffusion calculations and
modulated using pin power form factors from the 2D transport lattice physics calculations. The latter can
be validated using 2D fission rate measurements from critical experiments done in low-power reactors.
 This review summarized in this section shows that the LWR neutronics code validation performed by
the current LWR industry relies heavily on historic experiments performed in facilities that are no longer
operating. The lack of new experiment data for modern designs poses a challenge as there has been a
steady evolution of LWR fuel design and core management over the years driven by a need to enhance
fuel economics, prompting vendors to introduce new fuel designs that showcase incremental variations in
fuel enrichment, neutron poison content, geometry, etc. For example, most of the legacy experiments used
lower 235U enrichment values, which may not be representative of current practices where longer cycles
are economically beneficial to utilities. PWR geometries have evolved from 14 × 14 or 15 × 15 up to 17 ×
17 or 18 × 18 designs. For BWRs, changes are more dramatic with lattice sizes 7 × 7 up to 11 × 11 with
smaller pin diameters, enhanced water channels, part-length rods, axial and radial enrichment zoning, and
burnable poison zonings.
 The current trend of fuel vendors and utilities developing more optimized fuel management strategies,
with fuel enrichment up to 10 wt%, is expected to increase. While there are some criticality experiments
in the ICSBEP database with pins in water with enrichment up to 10 wt%, to the best of the authors’
knowledge there are no pin-by-pin fission rate measurements available for enrichments above 5 wt%.
Higher concentrations of burnable poisons are also needed with higher fissile content, which would also
need to be well characterized, for example for high gadolinia content above 10 wt% or new poison types.
New fuel compositions, such as chromium-coated zirconium alloy cladding and fuel pellets “doped” with
sintered aids such as chromia alumina, are planned for use in LWRs but are not represented in the current
physics experiments validation database. Further advancements such as FeCrAl alloy or SiC/SiC
composition claddings and even dramatic changes in fuel geometry such as the Lightbridge fuel design
will represent dramatic departures from the existing database and will require new critical experiments to
facilitate licensing and allow core designers to access their full value potential in LWRs.

 1.2. Review of Water-Moderated Critical Experiment Facilities
        1.2.1. B&W Facility
 Four critical facilities were licensed and operated by B&W in Lynchburg, VA, from 1957 to the early
1980s [3]. The main purpose was to generate data supporting the B&W nuclear reactor design program.
Some of the most used critical experiments were the so-called B&W-1810 cores [4] for which detailed
information was found. The diameter, height, and wall thickness of the core tank were 152.4, 198.12, and
1.27 cm, respectively. It was contained in a steel tank with a diameter of 274 cm and was supported on a
skirt at about 76.2 cm above the base of the steel tank. The space inside the steel tank and outside the core
tank was occupied by air. The top and bottom eggcrate grid plates in the core tank held the fuel and
absorber rods in a square pitch of 1.6358 cm. A 5.08-cm-thick aluminum base plate supported the fuel
and absorber rods. The aluminum base plate was 2.54 cm above the bottom of the core tank. The core
tank was filled with borated water. The core tank and its drain tank are shown in Figure 1 and Figure 2,
respectively.

 Various core configurations can be achieved, typically representing a 3 × 3 fuel assembly lattice
surrounded by driver fuel pins including a 15 × 15 lattices representative of B&W configuration and a 16
× 16 (Combustion Engineering configuration) with 2 × 2 water holes. Fuel pins with solid or annular
gadolinia pellets were included in the lattices. Criticality was achieved by controlling the water level and
boron concentration—critical boron concentration was the measured quantity for water at a given (fixed)
height. This allowed critical operations at a constant water height. Measurements included gamma scans
performed at the core mid-plane for selected rods, as well as radial power profile and 238U resonance
integral measurements inside solid and annular pellets.
        1.2.2. KRITZ
 KRITZ was a zero-power reactor operated by Studsvik in Sweden in the 1970s [5, 6]. Its unique
characteristic was a pressurized tank allowing for critical experiments at elevated temperatures, up to
250°C with no boiling, providing highly relevant data. Vertical and horizontal cross sections of the
KRITZ reactor are shown in Figure 3 and Figure 4, respectively. The tank dimensions were also set to

        1.2.5. PROTEUS
 The PROTEUS zero-power reactor at the Paul Scherrer Institute in Switzerland could perform reactor
physics experiments over a wide range of reactor concepts. PROTEUS had a large cylindrical central test
location that could accommodate large experiments, surrounded by an annular driver core with graphite
with holes for fuel pins and control rods, as seen in Figure 7 and Figure 8. The annular driver core
provided a way to adjust reactivity. The LWR-PROTEUS program aimed at validating a core analysis
tool for modern BWR assemblies and PWR assemblies with higher burnup and longer operating cycles
[12, 13]. LWR-PROTEUS was the only reactor physics experiment performed using full-size bundles
including spacer grids, lower tie plates, upper tie plates, etc. and not just individual pins. A 3 × 3 array of
BWR and PWR fuel assemblies were loaded inside the test location. Assemblies were encased in a square
tank and filled with water, optionally mixed with heavy water to mimic the fuel-to-moderator ratio found
for voided conditions in BWRs. For some experiments, control blade wings were inserted between BWR
bundles in the water gaps. One main purpose of these experiments was to measure pin-by-pin power
distributions. Since the annular driver core was only 1 m, the tank containing the 3 × 3 bundle array was
vertically lifted up and down to irradiate the pins axially. Pin-by-pin fission rates and 238U capture rates
were measured through gamma scans using an automatic scanning machine, shown in Figure 9. In
addition, radial and azimuthal within-pin reaction rates were made using activation foils made from
copper, gold, and 238U. These foils were placed between fuel pellets in special demountable fuel pins. The
data collected provided the radial and azimuthal distribution of reaction rates within pins themselves.
LWR-PROTEUS had many desirable features that will be replicated for CAT, in particular the capability
to perform criticality experiments using full-size bundles, which requires a large central cavity surrounded
by a driver core (fuel and reactivity control mechanism). Since CAT is only intended for LWRs though, a
solid driver core is not needed and can be made of individual fuel pins inside the tank.

        1.2.6. SPRF/CX
 The Sandia Pulse Reactor Facility Critical Experiments (SPRF/CX) is located at Sandia National

spacer grids for different fuel pin pitches.

 2. CAT CONCEPTUAL DESIGN
 2.1. Mechanical Design
 The CAT is a device that allows LWR-type fuel assemblies to be submerged in water such that the
water level or control elements are manipulated to bring the fissile material, moderator, and cladding
together to achieve a critical or subcritical assembly. Criticality is controlled through the use of water
level, water chemistry, or control elements. The constraints on the CAT ensure that the total assembly can
be brought to critically slowly, safely, and remotely until criticality is achieved.
 As the payload for the CAT, a critical assembly is the physical system for performing critical
experiments. In a critical assembly, the energy produced by fission is insufficient to require auxiliary
cooling, and the power history is such that the inventory of long-lived fission products is insignificant
 The proposed CAT is primarily designed to control reactivity by adjusting the water level in the tank.
The present design will allow for additional control elements to be added depending on the requirements
for the experiment.
 The CAT is a two-tank system composed of a criticality tank and a drain tank. The criticality tank
will house the critical assembly and its associated grid plates and support structure. The drain tank will act
as the catch basin for rapid water removal during a reactor trip event and for water storage and chemistry
adjustments.
 During a critical experiment, water will be transferred from the drain tank and fed into the bottom of
the criticality tank. Fill pump variable speed control will allow for the fine adjustment of water input to
the criticality tank. A motorized flow control drain valve will be used for regulated water removal from
the tank. In combination, these will allow for the precise control of the water level in the tank. Real-time
water level feedback will be provided by level monitoring instruments in both the drain and criticality
tanks.
 The CAT will incorporate two independently controlled butterfly valves as the primary safety
devices. The valves can shut down the critical assembly by draining the water which reduces reactivity
rapidly. The butterfly valves will be configured to a normally open state and rely on facility power to
keep closed. If the facility loses power, the spring return of the valves will automatically open the valves
and drain the tank. The fill pump is sized to fill the tank in approximately 2 hours at maximum capacity.
The safety valves have been sized to allow the tank to drain completely in less than 2 minutes, even if one
of the valves fails to actuate. The safety valve arrangement can be seen in Figure 13.

 27

Figure 13. CAT safety drain valves.

 A recirculation pump and heating element will allow for water temperature adjustments to ensure
each experiment is run at a repeatable water temperature. The heating circuit can be used to preheat the
drain tank to the desired operating temperature prior to an experiment. For fully submerged experiments,
the heating circuit will be capable of maintaining the temperature in the criticality tank by continuously
feeding water into the bottom of the tank and having it return to the drain tank via the overflow.
 A deionized water system will be used to control the conductivity of the water in the CAT. An
optional boron injection and concentration monitoring system could be added for future capabilities. The
process and instrumentation diagram for the CAT can be seen in Figure 14.

Figure 14. CAT process and instrumentation diagram.

 A modular grid plate support system will allow for flexibility in configuring the critical assembly.
The modular grid plate system will support both PWR and BWR fuel bundles. Depending on the
requirements of the experiment, driver fuel pins as well as control drums can be accommodated.
 Co-located next to the CAT will be a fuel storage rack capable of housing the PWR and BWR fuel
bundles as well as driver fuel pins and control elements. This will allow for the efficient transfer of the

 A key capability to enable the CAT mission will be a fuel rod gamma scanning system to quantify the
axial fission rate in selected rods. The scope of this conceptual design work did not include the detailed
mechanical design of a gamma scan system, but a brief description of this strategy is summarized here for
completeness. The foreseen experiment strategy entails brief low-power operations (<100 min, <100 W
reactor power) to activate gamma emitting fission products in fuel assemblies in CAT and then to extract
fuel rods from the centermost fuel assembly so that each rod can undergo axial gamma scanning. The
center fuel assembly would likely require the spacer grids and other hardware to be “loosened” slightly
beforehand so that fuel rods of interest can be extract with low risk for mechanical damage. A section of
rods that represent symmetry in the power distribution of the center fuel assembly would be extracted.
The total number of rods to be scanned depends on the fuel assembly design but would be on the order of
~40 fuel rods for a symmetric slice. These rods will be loaded into a horizontal gamma scanning system
with a high-purity germanium detector and detector shielding to reduce background radiation effects.
Each rod will be axially scanned while being rotated to establish a fuel rod axial power profile. The
system would likely include a magazine or rack system, which can be loaded with a handful of rods to
automatically load or increment them into the scanner path so that a batch of rods can be counted
efficiently between facility shifts to minimize radioisotope decay time between irradiation and counting.

as shown in Figure 18, and INL has experience designing similar systems for various post-irradiation

2.2.2 Preliminary Structural Analysis of CAT
 A preliminary structural analysis of the CAT tank was performed in order to determine the stresses in
the material and anchor bolt reaction forces. The following standards were reviewed and used to the

 34

extent they are applicable: ASCE 7, AAWA D100, API 650, and AISC 370. Chapter 15 of ASCE 7
provides general seismic design requirements for tanks. AAWA D100 provides requirements for water
storage tanks; however, it only covers carbon-steel tanks and does not give direction for stainless steel.
API 650 provides provision for stainless steel; however, the tanks must be uniformly ground supported
(i.e., the tank contents rest directly on the base plate), which the CAT tank is not. AISC 370 provides
general provisions for the design of structural stainless-steel members. Since AAWA D100 and API 650
don’t directly apply, the loads will be calculated to ASCE 7 and capacities to AISC 370.
 The seismic loads on tanks are calculated assuming impulsive and convective components of mass.
For tanks less than 5 ft in diameter, ASCE 7 allows them to be designed as if the entire tank and contents
are one impulsive mass. The CAT, with a 5 ft 8 inches diameter, is slightly outside of these bounds;
however, scoping calculations indicated that the convective component would be less than 10% of the
total mass. Furthermore, convective spectral accelerations are typically lower than impulsive spectral
accelerations given the larger period in a sloshing mode; therefore, the tank is conservatively analyzed as
if the entire mass is a convective component.
 The tank will be treated as an elevated tank in Chapter 15 of ASCE 7 for determining seismic loads.
Guidance being developed for the seismic analysis of stainless-steel structures suggests using the same
response modification factor (R) but scaling the overstrength factor (Ωo) by 1.5. In the case of elevated
tanks, R is 3 and Ωo is 2 per ASCE 7. When increased by a factor of 1.5, Ωo becomes the same value as R
and therefore those factors cancel out in the calculations in load cases used for concrete anchorage. Thus,
the seismic loads on the tank simplify down to the total mass multiplied by the short period spectral
acceleration (SDS) and importance factor (Ie). In this case, SDS is 0.13g and Ie is 1.5 for a total lateral force
coefficient of 0.195g (0.200 g is conservatively used in preliminary calculations).
 A model of the CAT tank was created in Abaqus using shell elements for the tank wall, skirt wall, and
base plate. Various scenarios were evaluated with the skirt and tank walls varying from ¼ inch to ½ inch,
the base plate varying from ½ inch to 1 inch, and the boundary conditions ranging from bottom supported
only to top and bottom supported. The tank will be anchored at the base with 12 1-1/4-inch-diameter post-
installed anchors. The loads applied included the weight of the tank and contents (including fuel array),
hydrostatic pressure from the water, and vertical and lateral seismic loads applied in multiple directions.
The finite element model is shown in Figure 22. The results for all scenarios showed that stresses in the
tank, skirt, and base plate were within acceptable limits for stainless-steel shells.

 35

Figure 22. Abaqus model of CAT.

 The post-installed anchors at the base will be embedded into a 4 ft 7 inch existing pool foundation.
The thickness of the concrete is such that it should be sufficient to support the loads from the tank. The
anchors can be embedded sufficiently such that their capacities will be greater than both the maximum
tension and shear demand. When the tank is top supported, there will not be any tension in the base
anchors due to overturning demands, which will reduce the required anchor embedment depth. Instead,
for the top-supported scenario, lateral forces at the top will be transferred through anchorage into the
operating room floor.
 At the operating floor level, there are existing ¾-inch-diameter cast-in-place anchor bolts spaced at a
maximum of 18 inches on center that are embedded 9 inches into the operating floor. These anchor bolts
anchor an L3 × 3 × 1/4 that was previously used to support a welded grating. This angle can be removed
and the existing anchors reused to anchor the platform atop the tank to the operating floor. The size and
spacing of the anchors are such that this connection is expected to have adequate capacity to resist the
lateral seismic loads from the top of the tank.

 2.3. CAT Operational Strategy
 2.3.1. Assumed Facility Status
 For the purposes of this conceptual design report, it assumed that efforts are either underway or
completed to establish PBF-613 as a DOE Hazard Category 2 nuclear facility to support the SPARC HST
testing activities at the time CAT activities are initiated. It is also assumed that the facility material
balance area (MBA) and criticality control area and associated criticality control rules have been or are in
the process of being established. This would include a baseline development of a Safety Analysis Report
and associated Technical Safety Requirements (TSR), which establishes the DOE-approved HST safety
basis operating envelope. The addition of the CAT capability would require a revision to the SPARC
Safety Analysis Report to include any necessary changes to the TSRs to deconflict operating two different
criticality test capabilities in the same facility.
 2.3.2. Operational Approach
2.3.2.1.      Fuel Receipt
 Upon receiving DOE authorization to conduct CAT critical experiments, operational activities will
start with the receipt of fuel that will be used to operate the CAT. Fuel originating outside of INL will be
shipped to the facility in vendor-provided Department of Transportation–approved transportation
containers utilizing established INL safeguards and security processes. The fuel will enter the facility
through the southern roll-up door. Approved procedures will direct the forklift and crane operations
needed to bring the fuel inside its transportation container into the upper floor of PBF-613. Receipt
inspection activities will ensure that the shipment matches shipping and safeguards documentation. Fuel
will be positioned in the vertical position using either facility- or vendor-provided upender equipment
designed to safely transition the fuel transportation containers between horizontal and vertical
orientations. The fuel will be removed from its transportation container using the crane, handling tools,
and lifting devices and placed in an appropriate storage location in accordance with developed nuclear
material handling controls and approved procedures. After fuel has passed the receipt inspection and is
placed in storage, the shipping package will be loaded back onto the trailer it arrived on to be returned to
the vendor.
2.3.2.2.      Core Loading
 When an approved fuel loading plan is complete and all applicable TSR surveillances have been
completed for core loading, operators will execute the plan to load the fuel into the CAT using the crane
for full PWR or BWR assemblies or the control element position system as required to facilitate other fuel
options if required for the experiment. This core loading plan may also include the reconfiguration or
orientation of fuel elements if required by the experiment prior to being loaded into the core. If an
individual fuel assembly needs to be reconfigured by relocating the pin positions within the assembly to
facilitate testing, this will be accomplished by taking the fuel assembly to the horizontal position in the
upender and then making pin adjustments by hand. Pin laydown and storage will need to be considered in
the planning of these activities if required. Nuclear instrumentation will be available to allow count rate
monitoring to the operators in the facility while performing core loading operations. Additional count rate
and reactivity monitoring will be available from a control room adjacent to the main PBF-613 facility.
2.3.2.3. Criticality Test Operation
 When all fuel and control elements are verified to be properly loaded into the core, all applicable TSR
surveillances will be completed prior to criticality test operations. When all required systems have been
verified operable, reactor operations will commence under the direction of approved procedures.
Individual test plans may be developed to explore specific criticality data objectives for each test. While
each test plan may differ, certain aspects will need to be addressed. These aspects may include water
chemistry, water temperature, tank water level, and estimated critical control element position. The start-

 37

up process will also include ensuring that any building occupation requirements or restrictions are posted
and enforced to ensure personnel safety. Testing protocols and approved operating procedures will be
used to control the CAT control element positioning system and water level from the control room in
order to place the CAT in the required configuration per experimental requirements. Data will be
collected throughout the test and be made available to engineers and vendors to determine test results.
Some tests will require multiple tests runs with minor core adjustments between them to achieve test
objectives.
2.3.2.4.      Core Load Swap
 Following the completion of testing in the CAT, fuel will need to be allowed to dry prior to core
offload operations. A dewatering system that may consist of forced air may be used to ensure no moisture
remains on fuel elements prior to core offload to preclude any additional concern for the potential spread
of contamination. While it is recognized that all fuel currently planned to enter the CAT are either no or
low burnup and therefore are not expected to have any appreciable buildup of daughter products both
prior to and after operation in the CAT, it is considered a prudent approach to ensure that fuel is moisture
free prior to core offload. When fuel is determined ready to be removed from the CAT by the approved
procedure, fuel will be removed and relocated to the fuel storage racks with the crane observing all
nuclear material storage and handling requirements.
2.3.2.5.      Fuel Shipment
 When fuel is no longer needed for the operation of the CAT, operations will work with the vendor to
return fuel elements as they are no longer needed. Operations will receive the shipping package from the
vendor by trailer through the southern roll-up door and place it on the facility upender or vendor-provided
upender if required. The shipping package will then be positioned vertically and opened to receive the
fuel that will be shipped back to the vendor. The fuel will then be lifted from storage using the crane and
associated fuel handling tools per approved procedures and in accordance with MBA controls and placed
into the appropriate shipping package. Then, the fuel will be secured within the shipping package and
lowered to the horizontal position using the appropriate upender. The fuel will be inspected and the
shipping package closed and sealed for transport per safeguards, vendor, and Department of
Transportation requirements. When all appropriate documentation has been reviewed and approved for
the fuel to leave the MBA and the SPARC facility, the shipping package will be loaded onto the transport
trailer by crane and forklift per the approved procedure. The shipper will secure the shipping package on
the trailer for transit, and when all checks have been completed and verified including a final review of all
required shipping paperwork, the fuel will be released for offsite transport.
 2.4. Neutronic Calculations
 Most low-power water-moderated reactors used short fuel pins to define various lattice configurations
mimicking PWR or BWR lattices in an actual commercial core, with sometimes additional pins acting as
driver fuel pins. Short pins (~1–1.5 m) allow for manual handling, can be easily mechanically restrained,
and are sufficient for reaching criticality when submerged even in small numbers, on the order of a few
hundred. Since the pins were axially uniform, these core experiments were essentially 2D, allowing for
easy comparison with 2D lattice physics codes, especially with respect to pin-by-pin power distributions
when the axial buckling is measured. The unique design characteristic of CAT is to accommodate full-
size fuel bundles in a central test location, large enough to accommodate 3 × 3 PWR bundles or 4 × 4
BWR bundles. The core loading will be configured to have a central bundle as representative as possible
of an infinite lattice array and thus decoupled neutronically from control rods or driver fuels located in the
periphery as much as possible. After the critical experiment, individual rods from the central bundle will
then be gamma scanned. Having access to full 3D pin-by-pin power distribution for commercial LWR
bundles paves the way for detailed measurements accounting for axial flux variations due to spacer grids,
part-length rods, or axial enrichment or poison zoning.

 38

 Using the exact fuel assembly geometry will provide valuable insights on LWR core physics codes
accuracy for novel fuel assembly designs. Reactor physics codes—either Monte Carlo or deterministic-
based—have greatly evolved over the years and are now capable of dealing with complex
multidimensional geometries. It is crucial to design experimental capabilities that provide useful
validation data for current and next-generation neutronics codes. The only similar capability existed in the
PROTEUS reactor, which had a large central cavity capable of fitting a 3 × 3 array of full-size LWR
bundles. Its annular driver core was only 1 m in height though, requiring assemblies to be vertically lifted
up and down to activate the fuel rods on the entire axial length. The CAT driver core will cover the full
active length of commercial LWR bundles, ranging from 3.65 to 3.81 m, and will be designed to be
critical with the active fuel region fully submerged to provide detailed pin-by-pin reaction rates via
gamma scanning.
 2.4.1. PWR Configuration
 Publicly available full-size PWR assemblies were taken from [21] representative of standard 17 × 17
PWR fuel. The entire assembly is modeled, with bottom and top nozzles and spacer grids. Some
assemblies have burnable absorbers consisting of borosilicate glass. Enrichment varies from 1.6% to
3.4%, which is in the low range since it was for a fresh core, but provides a conservative estimate for the
tank dimensions and driver fuel requirements. Different assemblies were tried in a 3 × 3 or 5 × 5 core
array, with different boron concentrations. Key findings are summarized below:
 All core configurations will go critical with a partially submerged core, if only water level is used as
 reactivity control with no driver fuel.
 Minimum core configuration is 3 × 3 (9 bundles). Absent driver fuel, a 3 × 3 core may only be critical
 for low boron concentrations depending on the fuel enrichment and burnable poison content of the
 fuel assemblies.
 Minimum tank diameter to accommodate a square 3 × 3 PWR assembly core array is on the order of
 90 cm. The worth of the radial reflector is low, meaning that the diameter of the tank can be kept
 small without penalizing the reactivity of the experiment. There may be an interest from a shielding
 perspective to keep a certain amount of water radially though, as well as to keep enough space for
 holding larger experiment configurations with structural materials or in-core instrument trains.

To have an easily tunable reactivity control mechanism, control elements such as rotating drums are also
needed. Control drums need to have sufficient worth to keep the core subcritical when fully submerged.

drums is that the axial flux shape will not be too disturbed. One drawback is that their worth is low since
they are located on the periphery of the core. To evaluate the feasibility of this option, standard 4.95 wt%
enriched UO2 fuel pins are added with four control drums. Two layouts are tested, one with fuel pins
added using a regular layout around the test location and one using a circular lattice, as shown in Figure

 As a summary, dealing with full-size LWR bundles only in CAT may not offer the level of fine
tuning required for achieving both reactivity and fission rate measurements. While reaction rates may be
measured on the immerged portion and provide useful data for neutronics code validation, this data will
not be available for the full length of the fuel pins. The proposed solution is to have an annular driver core
consisting of fuel pins and some reactivity control device, such as rotating drums, which together would
provide a much easier way to control excess reactivity. Pins used in regular LWR assemblies (3.65– 4 m)
would require special consideration for preserving pitch, likely wire wrapping would be needed. Lastly,
mixing both pins and fuel bundles would require some further consideration for the bottom grid plate
supporting the fuel elements to offset differences in active fuel height.

When modeling a large building such as PBF-613 in a neutron/photon transport code, many modeling

factors following the ANSI/ANS-6.1.1-2020 standard [24]. Following the standard recommendations, the
most conservative effective dose conversion coefficients corresponding to an anteroposterior exposure
geometry were selected. Prompt photons emitted from fission, radiative capture, and inelastic scattering
were modeled. Neutron and photon source rates from a critical core configuration were used as input.
Dose rates are directly proportional to the source rates, which are proportional to the power level. The
dose rates reported here are for 10 W core power. However, the direct measurement of core power is
extremely challenging in a system with low power and no heat removal system, thus significant
uncertainties should be considered in predicting limiting dose rates. Photons resulting from the potential
activation of core and surrounding materials were neglected given the low neutron flux. Neutron and
photon ambient dose rates were estimated inside the building for the basement, main floor reactor hall,

 2.5. Neutron Room Return Evaluation
 One of the key requirements for clean reactor physics experiments is to minimize the so-called
neutron room return, which may otherwise hinder the usability of collected data as code benchmark
information. Not only will it be challenging to model the room surrounding the critical experiment, but
the material composition of the floor or walls (concrete, rebar) is unknown. Thus, there is a preference for

 52

large, open-space areas when hosting zero-power critical piles where neutrons leaking out of the core do
not re-enter the core. In modeling space, this would translate to having to only model the core itself and
setting the outer boundary at the outer surface of the water vessel. Evaluating neutron room return due to
the surrounding walls and floor uses the same neutronics model as used for shielding analyses. As a first
guess, the tank is set at the center of the floor available in the basement and then moved to the corner
closer to two walls which should provide a bounding case in terms of room return. The placement of the
CAT in both locations is shown in Figure 42. The Keff obtained for each of these placements is compared
against a reference “bare” calculation where the model boundary is set at the outer surface of the CAT
vessel, which is the objective in terms of future benchmarking applications. Results shown in Table 6
demonstrate that the impact of neutron room return is low, where differences between the case where the
CAT is modeled inside the room in various locations and the bare CAT model are around 12 pcm, with a
statistical error of around 7-12 pcm, depending on location. This uncertainty would need to be added to
any benchmark evaluation using only the reactor in its model.

 2.6. Implications for I&C and Control Console
 The integration of SAT into the SPARC facility introduces several key changes to the instrumentation
and control (I&C) system when compared to the HST-only configuration. In the HST-only setup, the
Rockwell-based programmable logic controller (PLC) and human-machine interface (HMI) system is
responsible for controlling table motion and monitoring position sensors with an independent SPARC
protection system (SPS). The HST I&C system collects data from neutron detectors, thermocouples, and
environmental sensors. A patch panel near the HST provides connectivity for instrumentation routed to
I&C cabinets located in Room 107. With the addition of the CAT, the I&C system must be expanded to
accommodate new inputs for valve control (e.g., drain and fill operations), water quality sensors
(conductivity and pH), and thermocouples mounted throughout the CAT and its drain tank. Additional
coaxial connections at the top of the CAT will support experiment-specific neutron detectors with a
secondary patch panel for I&C. These changes require the PLC to incorporate new logic for CAT-specific
operations and interlocks to ensure the safe, exclusive operation of either the HST or CAT at any given
time. The PLC must also incorporate interlocks that prevent simultaneous criticality in both the HST and
CAT. For example, the system should inhibit CAT water fill operations if the HST is not fully split and
vice versa.
 The SPS undergoes modifications in the dual-machine configuration. In the HST-only version, the
SPS includes trips from a high power level in four channels of a wide-range neutron monitoring system
(NMS), seismic events, manual scram buttons, and door interlocks. The SPS for the CAT would function
similarly to the HST in that a trip would cause a loss of power to the trip mechanism. For the HST, this
loss would cause the table to split, whereas for the CAT, the trip would actuate the drain valves.
 Since reactor cores on the HST will have significant neutron leakage, the HST will use wall-mounted
neutron detectors. However, the CAT will require detectors mounted in the tank due to water’s ability to
easily attenuate neutrons. With the addition of the CAT, the NMS must now support a second set of
neutron detectors mounted on adjustable supports within the CAT tank. Instead of using separate neutron
channel electronics, the HST and CAT may share the same electronics by utilizing a patch panel in the
SPS cabinet to switch between HST and CAT detectors. The CAT neutron detectors will be mounted to
the core support plate and can be repositioned vertically and radially depending on the core configuration.
For each experiment, the detectors would be recalibrated and the trip setpoint adjusted accordingly.
 The control console, originally designed for single-reactor operation, must be upgraded to support
dual-experiment functionality. In the HST-only configuration, the console provides an HMI for HST
operation, neutron monitoring, and data logging. With the CAT added, the console must now include a
dual-mode interface that allows operators to select between HST and CAT modes. It must also display
and control CAT-specific parameters, such as water level, pumps, valve status, temperature, pH, and
conductivity. The console will need to integrate additional status indicators for the CAT, as well as
provide visibility into interlock status to prevent dual criticality. The I&C system must be expanded to log
CAT-specific data alongside HST data, and operator training and procedures must be updated to reflect
the increased system complexity.

 Audio channels located on the operating floor and control room will be provided for monitoring

 External communication to the INL controls and enterprise networks will be used for long-term data

 2.7. Safety Basis Strategy
 Though adding the CAT to the SPARC facility will change some things about the safety basis, many
things will remain similar. The facility hazard category will be the same, natural phenomena hazard
design category will be the same, and authorization pathway will be the same. There will only be one
safety basis document written for the SPARC facility, not individual safety bases for each critical
assembly. There will be one Nuclear Safety Design Agreement, one Preliminary Documented Safety
Analysis, and one Documented Safety Analysis. In writing the SPARC safety basis, including the CAT,
there will notionally be an extra time investment of 25% to incorporate the added CAT information. This
25% increase accounts for doing additional dose consequence calculations, hazard and accident analyses
for hazards associated with the CAT, and writing relevant information for the CAT in the safety basis.
 Some support analyses for the safety basis may see a similar increase in time investment. These could
include: the criticality safety analysis, the activation analysis, the shielding adequacy analysis, the fire
hazards analysis, the seismic and natural phenomena hazard analysis, and the Structures, Systems, and
Component (SSC) performance analysis.
 Hazard controls and safety SSCs would also differ with the inclusion of the CAT:
 Both the HST and CAT would need at least two independent instruments to indicate the neutron flux
 within their respective assemblies (ASNI/ANS-1-2000).
 Controls would be necessary to prevent both assemblies from going critical at the same time.
 Preferably, these controls would be engineered and not administrative. This could be an interlock that
 would only allow one reactor to operate at a time. The method for these controls is still undecided.

 57

 The CAT itself would involve very few safety SSCs, since most accidents or abnormal operation
 would result in the draining of the CAT, and therefore the removal of the moderator and insertion of
 negative reactivity. The tank is not anticipated to be a safety SSC (but would be Natural Phenomena
 Hazard Design Category 2 to prevent it from falling onto the HST). Potential safety SSCs would
 include the CAT drain valves, NI system, fuel assembly structures, and fuel confinement.
 In summary, the impacts of including the CAT on the safety basis strategy for SPARC are listed
below:
 Increased time commitment for safety basis writing and associated analyses
 Increased number of safety SSCs and hazard controls associated with the CAT.

 High-level project assumptions and risks that were identified during this conceptual design task are:

 environmental assessment.
 Nuclear safety basis documentation will primarily be developed by the SPARC HST project for PBF-
 613 and will include CAT within that documentation.

 any needs by CAT.

 Existing raceways can be reutilized.
 Adequate space is available in PBF-613 to support both the HST and CAT and their respective
 necessary subsystems (e.g., drain tank, gantry).
