Hydrogen peroxide self-supplied nanozyme system for synergistic prostate cancer therapy via ferroptosis/pyroptosis and Ca2+ overload-induced apoptosis
Authors: Sixu Chen, Linnan Yang, Peng Wang, Chenghao Wen, Zhaoxu Shi, Rui Tan, Wenrui Guan, Guowen Jiang, Pengfei Zhang, Chaozhao Liang, Xianwen Wang, Yunjiao Zhang, Guilong Zhang, Li Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFerroptosis, an iron-dependent and non-apoptotic regulated cell death mode, has garnered increasing attention in treating cancers with apoptosis resistance. However, the insufficient hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) supply impedes the sustained generation of highly cytotoxic free radicals, resulting in diminished ferroptosis. In addition, monotherapy with ferroptosis inducers frequently shows limited efficacy in cancer management. Thus, promotion of ferroptosis by increasing H2O2 supply, and the development of targeted combination approaches that co-activate multiple cell death pathways are essential to maximize the tumoricidal effects. Herein, we developed a multifunctional targeted nanozyme system pPB-Fe3O4@CaO2-BzATP, in which the pPB targeting cyclic peptide endowed the nanozyme system with enhanced prostate tumor-specific targeting ability. Upon tumor cell internalization, calcium peroxide (CaO2) was hydrolyzed under acidic conditions to produce H2O2 and calcium ions (Ca2+), and iron oxide (Fe3O4) nanozyme catalyzed self-supplied H2O2 to generate highly cytotoxic ·OH, thereby inducing prostate cancer cell death via augmented ferroptosis. Meanwhile, released Ca2+ can trigger calcium overload to elicit apoptosis. Moreover, 2‘(3’)-O-(4-benzoylbenzoyl)adenosine 5’-triphosphate (BzATP) activated the P2X7 receptor, which not only enhanced Ca2+ influx to further reinforce calcium overload but also promoted efflux of potassium ions (K+), followed by nucleotide-binding oligomerization domain (NOD)-like receptor pyrin domain containing 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome assembly and ultimately pyroptosis induction. Both in vitro and in vivo studies verified that pPB-Fe3O4@CaO2-BzATP nanozyme system significantly enhanced the tumoricidal efficacy with negligible side effects. Overall, we propose a novel and promising strategy to combat castration-resistant prostate cancer by synergistically integrating ferroptosis, pyroptosis and calcium overload-induced apoptosis into a unified therapeutic modality.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12951-026-04744-0.Keywords: Nanozyme, Prostate cancer, Ferroptosis, Pyroptosis, Calcium overload

Introduction

Prostate cancer is recognized as the leading malignancy within the male genitourinary tract, representing the second highest incidence of cancers among men globally [1, 2]. Its occurrence and progression are influenced by multiple factors, such as aging, genetic susceptibility, aberrant cell signaling involving androgen, and environmental exposure to endocrine-disrupting pollutants including bisphenol A [3]. Currently, surgical excision, hormonal therapy, radiation therapy, and chemotherapy are the common treatment regimens for prostate cancer [4–6]. Among these, chemotherapy remains a standard therapeutic option for castration-resistant prostate cancer, whereas its clinical effectiveness is frequently impaired by the development of drug resistance [5]. These therapeutic limitations underscore the urgent need to develop alternative and highly effective strategies for advanced prostate cancer, particularly those capable of bypassing conventional resistance mechanisms through noncanonical cell-death-associated interventions. Meanwhile, owing to their distinctive physicochemical and biological properties, nanomaterials represent a promising class of agents for the treatment of various cancers [7–10]. Recent studies have shown that nanotherapeutic systems could help address drug-resistance-related treatment barriers by improving tumor accumulation and intracellular delivery, bypassing efflux-related mechanisms, modulating tumor microenvironment, and supporting integrated therapeutic designs [11, 12]. Based on these advances, representative nanomaterial-based approaches have been developed, including self-activated DNAzyme-enabled chemo-gene combination therapy [13], tumor microenvironment-responsive prodrug nanoassembly [14], and ferroptosis-oriented nanotherapeutic systems for cancer treatment [15].

Ferroptosis, a regulated cell death modality driven by iron-dependent lipid peroxidation and mediated by Fenton reactions, has garnered increasing attention in treating cancers with apoptosis resistance [16, 17]. In addition, emerging evidence suggests that advanced prostate cancer, particularly castration-resistant disease, exhibits substantial metabolic and redox reprogramming, including dysregulated lipid metabolism and enhanced oxidative stress dependence, which may render prostate cancer more vulnerable to ferroptosis-based interventions [18]. Since ferroptosis is closely associated with reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation, lipid peroxidation, and oxidative membrane damage, catalytic nanomaterials capable of amplifying oxidative stress provide a rational basis for ferroptosis-oriented cancer therapy. Nanozymes are equipped with abundant active sites, multiple active phases, and nanostructures that sustain stability even under extreme conditions, demonstrate enzymatic capabilities that rival or exceed those of natural enzymes, along with unique physicochemical properties [19, 20]. Notably, nanozymes can exert either anti-oxidative or pro-oxidative effects depending on their catalytic characteristics and biological context [21–26]. Anti-oxidative nanozymes have been extensively used for scavenging ROS and alleviating oxidative stress and inflammation, as highlighted by the recent study on Pt-based nanozymes for the treatment of intracerebral hemorrhage [22]. Likewise, hyaluronic acid-guided cerium oxide nanozymes efficiently scavenged excessive ROS through reversible Ce3+/Ce4+ redox cycling, thereby alleviating oxidative stress and suppressing atherosclerosis progression [23]. In contrast, pro-oxidative nanozymes are more suitable for cancer therapy as they can effectively amplify the oxidative damage by catalyzing ROS generation within tumor cells [24–26]. Specifically, iron-based nanozymes are capable of catalyzing the transformation of weakly toxic H2O2 into highly reactive hydroxyl radicals (·OH) by virtue of their peroxidase (POD)-like activity [24]. Consequently, cytotoxic lipid peroxide (LPO) accumulates, thus triggering ferroptosis. However, the inadequate availability of H2O2 within the complex tumor microenvironment often limits the sustained production of ROS, thereby weakening the catalytic efficiency of nanomaterials for the Fenton reaction in cancer cells [27, 28].

Calcium-based nanomaterials have emerged as a promising tool in cancer therapy, owing to their capacity to modulate Ca2+ homeostasis through diverse mechanisms, including Ca2+overload-mediated direct tumor cell killing, microenvironment modulation to suppress tumor growth, as well as the combination of chemotherapy and photothermal therapy to amplify antitumor efficacy [29]. Accordingly, recent studies have explored the combination of Ca2+ with other metal ions for cancer therapy. For instance, Ca/Mn nanomodulators have been developed for ion-interference-enhanced antitumor therapy [30], and ultrasound-responsive Ca/Cu phosphate nanomaterials have been shown to provide an effective strategy for synergistic metal ion release-mediated mitochondrial dysfunction and tumoricidal effects [31]. Among calcium-based materials, CaO2 generally undergoes decomposition to generate H2O2 and liberate free Ca2+ in response to the acidic tumor microenvironment [32]. The released Ca2+ can further induce intracellular Ca2+ overload, which has attracted increasing interest as a potential option for antitumor therapies [33–36]. In particular, accumulating evidence has demonstrated that nanocomposites integrating CaO2 and iron oxide (Fe3O4) can generate substantial amounts of ·OH, thereby eliciting ferroptosis in cancer cells [37, 38]. Furthermore, excessive Ca2+ accumulation within cells triggers mitochondrial damage and facilitates endogenous ROS production, potentiating oxidative stress. This cascade ultimately suppresses glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) expression, thereby enhancing ferroptosis efficiency [39, 40]. Nevertheless, most of these studies focus primarily on directly elevating intracellular Ca2+ levels via calcium-based materials. The regulatory role of membrane calcium channels/pumps in restoring calcium homeostasis is often overlooked. Notably, cancer cells possess intrinsic calcium buffering capacity, largely mediated by membrane calcium channels/pumps [41–43], which may enable cancer cells to rapidly counteract therapeutic Ca2+ overload and restore ionic homeostasis, substantially compromising tumoricidal efficacy. For instance, plasma membrane Ca2+-ATPase actively extrudes excess cytosolic Ca2+ to prevent sustained Ca2+ overload, and thus disrupting this buffering capacity could be essential for activating programmed cell death [44]. Notably, the Ca2+ buffering capacity is also observed in prostate cancer and exhibits disease-specific characteristics. In prostate cancer, calcium signaling networks are frequently remodeled to maintain intracellular calcium homeostasis and limit excessive calcium influx. This remodeling supports tumor cell survival while simultaneously restricting calcium-dependent tumoricidal effects [45]. Thus, modulating calcium channels to disrupt calcium homeostasis and induce Ca2+ overload may represent a particularly relevant and effective strategy for prostate cancer therapy.

At present, BzATP serves as the most widely employed agonist for the P2X7 receptor. In comparison to adenosine triphosphate (ATP), the P2X7 receptor’s endogenous agonist, BzATP shows significantly stronger agonistic activity against P2X7 receptor [46]. Stimulation of P2X7 receptor located on the cell membrane is capable of opening cation channels, boosting Ca2+ influx, and aggravating intracellular Ca2+ accumulation [47].This process can induce cell death through calcium overload. Moreover, activation of P2X7 receptor promotes K+ efflux [48], which has been demonstrated to initiate assembly of the NLRP3 inflammasome [49]. The NLRP3 inflammasome represents a cytosolic multiprotein complex formed by pattern recognition receptors (PRRs), which can recruit and activate the pro-inflammatory protease caspase-1, thus inducing caspase-1-dependent programmed cell death termed pyroptosis [50–52].

Pyroptosis, a new lytic type of programmed cell death regulated by gasdermin family proteins, offers an alternative approach to targeting apoptosis-resistant cancer cells [53–55]. Unlike apoptosis, pyroptosis is marked by cell enlargement and rapid disruption of membrane integrity through pore formation, which ultimately leads to efficient and irreversible cell death [56, 57]. Therefore, precise induction of pyroptosis has great potential for enhancing direct cytotoxicity against cancer cells. For instance, targeted nanoplatforms loaded with chemotherapeutic agents or plasmids encoding the N-terminal domain of gasdermin D (GSDMD-N) can significantly raise tumoricidal efficacy by triggering GSDMD-mediated pyroptosis [58, 59]. Prostate cancer has been shown to harbor an endogenous pathway that converges on the same GSDMD-mediated pyroptotic endpoint, namely the NLRP3/caspase-1/GSDMD signaling axis, wherein activation of this pathway induces pyroptotic cell death and suppresses tumor growth in vivo [60, 61]. Thus, nanomaterial-based platforms enable controlled activation of pyroptosis in cancer cells, representing an innovative and effective strategy to improve therapeutic outcomes of prostate cancer.

To develop a targeted nanoplatform capable of selectively recognizing cancer cells, disrupting Ca2+ homeostasis, inducing intracellular Ca2+ overload, and exerting tumoricidal effects through multiple pathways, we constructed an iron-based nanoplatform composed of Fe3O4, CaO2, BzATP, and a platelet-derived growth factor receptor-β (PDGFR-β)-binding cyclic peptide (designated as pPB). The obtained nanozyme system, fabricated by depositing CaO2 nanoparticles onto Fe3O4 nanoparticles, loading BzATP, and coating with pPB-polyethylene glycol (PEG)-1,2-distearoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine (DSPE), was denoted as pPB-Fe3O4@CaO2-BzATP. Upon administration, pPB-Fe3O4@CaO2-BzATP actively targeted the PDGFR-β, which is overexpressed in prostate cancer, leading to enhanced accumulation within tumor tissues. Subsequently, the acidic lysosomal environment initiated the hydrolysis of Fe3O4@CaO2-BzATP, resulting in the release of Ca2+ and the production of H2O2, while the escaped BzATP activated P2X7 receptor. Of note, hydrolysis of CaO2 combined with P2X7 receptor activation further promoted intracellular Ca2+ accumulation, ultimately leading to augmented Ca2+ overload. The generated H2O2 reacted with Fe3O4-POD nanozyme to produce highly cytotoxic ·OH, which accelerated prostate cancer cell death via ferroptosis. Moreover, P2X7 receptor activation promoted NLRP3 inflammasome assembly, thereby eliciting pyroptosis. Collectively, the pPB-Fe3O4@CaO2-BzATP nanozyme system significantly enhances tumoricidal efficacy both in vitro and in vivo with negligible systemic toxicity. This novel strategy synergistically integrates ferroptosis, pyroptosis, and apoptosis into a unified therapeutic modality, representing a promising approach for effective prostate cancer therapy (Fig. 1).

Materials and methods

Materials

A detailed list including chemical components and biological reagents utilized in this research is presented in Table S1 in the Supporting Information.

Synthesis of Fe3O4, FC, FCB and PFCB nanoconstructs

Synthesis of Fe3O4 nanoparticles

0.5 g of ferric acetylacetonate was dissolved in a mixed solvent consisting of 25 mL ethylene glycol and equal volume of diethylene glycol. The solution was subsequently heated to 80 °C, followed by incubation for 30 min. Next, 1.5 g of sodium acrylate was introduced into the system and allowed to proceed with the reaction for 30 min, after which an additional 2 g of sodium acrylate was added. This mixture was subsequently transferred into an autoclave and held at 200 °C for 24 h. Following natural cooling down to room temperature (RT), the resulting product was harvested via centrifugation for 10 min at 11,000 rpm, and rinsed three consecutive times using anhydrous ethanol as well as deionized water in sequence. Finally, the purified Fe3O4 nanoparticles were re-suspended in 20 mL of deionized water for follow-up experimental procedures.

Synthesis of Fe3O4@CaO2 (FC) nanoparticles

0.1 g CaCl2 and 1 mL deionized water were added into the as-prepared Fe3O4 nanoparticle dispersion (2 mL), and the mixture was ultrasonicated for 10 min. Then, 12 mL of PEG 200 and 0.5 mL of ammonia water were introduced and thoroughly mixed. Subsequently, 0.5 mL of 30% H2O2 solution was infused at a flow rate of 3 mL per hour under stirring at 600 rpm, and this reaction was continued for 30 min. The obtained product was collected via centrifugation for 10 min at 14,000 rpm, rinsed three consecutive times using anhydrous ethanol, then re-dispersed in ethanol. Finally, the FC nanoparticles were magnetically separated and dispersed in 10 mL ethanol.

Synthesis of Fe3O4@CaO2-BzATP (FCB) and pPB-Fe3O4@CaO2-BzATP (PFCB)

1 mL of 10 mg/mL BzATP solution was mixed with equal volume of FC nanoparticle dispersion (2 mg/mL) and subjected to sonication for 25 min. The resulting FCB product was harvested after centrifugation for 10 min at 11,000 rpm. Following this, FCB was dispersed in 2 mL of pPB-PEG-DSPE solution (0.05 mg/mL) under 4 h of stirring and 15 min of sonication. Centrifugation of the mixture was performed for 10 min at 11,000 rpm, the final precipitate (PFCB) was gathered and re-dispersed in 2 mL ethanol before use.

Characterization

The morphological features of the nanoparticles were observed using a transmission electron microscope (TEM, JEM-2100Plus, JEOL, Japan) operated at an accelerating voltage of 120 kV. The crystal structure and composition of the nanoparticles were determined by X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis using the Ultima IV diffractometer (Rigaku, Japan) with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 0.15406 nm). The measurements were conducted at a voltage of 40 kV and a current of 40 mA. Data were collected over a 2θ range of 10 ° to 80 ° with a scanning step of 0.02° and a scanning rate of 2 °/min. The surface elemental composition and chemical states were analyzed by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, K-Alpha spectrometer, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The system was equipped with a monochromatic Al Kα X-ray source (hν = 1486.68 eV) operating at 15 kV and 10 mA. The base pressure in the analysis chamber was maintained below 5 × 10⁻10 Pa. For signal optimization, 5–10 cycles of accumulation were performed. The survey spectra were acquired with a passing energy of 50 eV and an energy step of 0.1 eV. The HRTEM image of the nanoparticles was acquired using a TEM (Talos F200X, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) operated at an accelerating voltage of 200 kV. Elemental mapping and energy-dispersive X-ray (EDX) spectroscopy were performed using a TEM (JEM-F200, JEOL, Japan) to determine the elemental distribution and composition. The hydrodynamic diameters of the nanoparticles were measured using the Zetasizer Nano ZS instrument (Malvern Instruments, UK). Fourier-transform infrared (FT-IR) spectra were recorded on the Nicolet iS20 spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) to identify the functional groups and chemical composition. The released metal ions were quantified using inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) (7800 ICP-MS, Agilent Technologies, USA).

Enzymatic activity measurement

POD-like activity was assessed using a typical 3,3′,5,5′-tetramethylbenzidine (TMB)/methylene blue (MB) oxidation assay. Specifically, acetate buffer (pH 4.0) containing TMB (10 mM)/MB (10 µg/mL) and H2O2 (10 mM) was prepared, followed by the addition of PFCB (Fe: 50, 100, 150 µg/mL). Each final reaction mixture was brought to 1 mL and maintained at 37 °C under light-protected conditions for 10 min, after which the formation of oxidized TMB/MB was quantified by recording the absorbance spectra with a UV‒Vis spectrometer (UV-2600i, Shimadzu, Japan). In addition, PFCB (Fe: 100 µg/mL) was added to acetate buffer (pH 4.0) containing H2O2 solution (50, 100, 150, 200, 250, 300, 350, 400 mM) and TMB (10 mM). UV‒Vis absorption spectra of the solutions at 652 nm were recorded, and the Michaelis‒Menten kinetics of PFCB were determined by plotting the initial reaction velocity against a range of H2O2 concentrations.

Cell lines and culture conditions

Human prostate cancer PC-3 cells, murine prostate cancer RM-1 cells, and human immortalized noncancerous prostate epithelial RWPE-1 cells were maintained in RPMI 1640 + 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM) + 10% FBS, and keratinocyte serum-free medium (K-SFM), respectively, to all of which 1% penicillin-streptomycin was supplemented. All the cells were cultured under the standard conditions (37 °C and 5% CO2) in a humidified incubator.

Cellular uptake determination

A quantitative analysis of cellular uptake was performed using ICP-MS based on intracellular Fe and Ca contents. Following seeding onto 12-well plates at a density of 2 × 105 cells per well, PC-3 cells were incubated overnight under standard conditions. Subsequently, the cells were treated with different formulations and allowed to uptake the nanomaterials for 6 h, 12 h, and 24 h. After incubation, the cells were extensively washed with phosphate buffered saline (PBS) to remove non-internalized nanomaterials and then lysed using cell lysis buffer. The collected cell lysates were digested with 4 mL of aqua regia for 12 h, followed by sonication to ensure complete digestion. Finally, the Fe and Ca contents in each sample were quantified by ICP‒MS with subtraction of the basal cellular contents.

Cell viability assay

Following seeding onto 96-well plates (6 × 103 cells per well), PC-3 cells were incubated overnight. After treatment with Fe3O4, FC, FCB, or PFCB at equivalent Fe concentrations (Fe: 6.25, 12.5, 18.75, 25, 31.25 µg/mL) for 24 h, cell culture supernatants were removed, followed by supplement with fresh culture medium containing methyl thiazolyl tetrazolium (MTT) solution (0.5 mg/mL) and further incubation in the humidified incubator (37 °C and 5% CO2) for 4 h. Subsequently, the produced formazan crystals were dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) (150 µL), followed by determination at 492 nm using the microplate reader (Infinite M Nano, Tecan, Switzerland).

Following seeding onto 96-well plates (6 × 103 cells per well), RWPE-1 cells were incubated overnight. After treatment with Fe3O4, FC, FCB, or PFCB at equivalent Fe concentrations (Fe: 25 µg/mL) for 24 h, cell culture supernatants were removed, followed by supplement with fresh culture medium containing MTT solution (0.5 mg/mL) and further incubation in humidified incubator (37 °C and 5% CO2) for 4 h. Subsequently, the produced formazan crystals were dissolved in DMSO (150 µL), followed by determination at 492 nm using the microplate reader.

Western blot analysis

Following seeding onto 6-well plates (5 × 105 cells per well), PC-3/RWPE-1 cells were incubated overnight and treated with Fe3O4, FC, FCB, or PFCB at equivalent concentrations (Fe: 25 µg/mL) for 24 h. Next, secreted proteins in the culture supernatants were isolated via methanol‒chloroform precipitation. Afterward, cells were harvested and subjected to cell lysis buffer supplemented with protease/phosphatase inhibitor cocktails. The cell lysates were separated by centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the protein concentration of the supernatants was quantified prior to normalization. Subsequently, the secreted proteins isolated from culture supernatants were mixed with 1× sample loading buffer and the protease/phosphatase inhibitor cocktails, while the cell lysate supernatants were mixed with 5× sample loading buffer, both of which were boiled for 10 min. Following separation via SDS‒PAGE, the proteins were electrotransferred onto nitrocellulose (NC) membranes. After being blocked with 5% non-fat milk diluted in Tris-buffered saline with 0.1% Tween 20 (TBST) at RT for 1 h, the NC membranes were probed with corresponding primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. After extensive washing with TBST, secondary antibodies were applied for incubation at RT for 1 h. The signals of protein bands were visualized via enhanced chemiluminescence substrate and the imaging system (5200 Multi, Tanon, China).

Intracellular glutathione (GSH) assay

Following seeding onto 12-well plates (2 × 105 cells per well), PC-3/RWPE-1 cells were incubated overnight and subsequently treated with Fe3O4 , FC, FCB, or PFCB at equivalent Fe concentrations (25 µg/mL) for 24 h. Intracellular total GSH (T-GSH) and oxidized glutathione (GSSG) were measured using a commercial colorimetric assay kit (E-BC-K097-M, Elabscience, China). Briefly, cells were harvested and homogenized with sonication on ice, followed by centrifugation at 10,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. For T-GSH determination, the supernatant was mixed with the reaction working solution. Serially diluted GSH standards were prepared in parallel to generate a standard curve. After incubation, the substrate working solution was added to initiate the enzymatic reaction. For GSSG determination, reduced GSH in the supernatant was first removed by treatment with GSH scavenger auxiliary solution and working solution, followed by the same colorimetric procedure. The absorbance was recorded at 412 nm using a microplate reader. The T-GSH and GSSG concentrations were determined from their respective standard curves. Intracellular reduced GSH content was calculated by subtracting twice the GSSG value from the T-GSH value, and the GSH/GSSG ratio was expressed relative to the untreated control.

Malondialdehyde (MDA) assay

Following seeding onto 12-well plates (2 × 105 cells per well), PC-3/RWPE-1 cells were incubated overnight. Afterward, cells were treated with Fe3O4 , FC, FCB, or PFCB at equivalent concentrations (Fe: 25 µg/mL) for 18 h. Intracellular MDA content was measured using a commercial colorimetric assay kit (E-BC-K025-M, Elabscience, China) based on the thiobarbituric acid method. Briefly, cells were harvested and lysed with sonication on ice, followed by centrifugation at 10,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was sequentially mixed with clarificant, acid reaction buffer, and thiobarbituric acid-containing chromogenic reagent. Serially diluted MDA standards were processed in parallel for quantification. The mixture was heated at 100 °C for 40 min. After cooling to RT, the reaction mixture was centrifuged to remove any precipitate and the supernatant was transferred to a 96-well plate. The absorbance was recorded at 532 nm using the microplate reader. The MDA concentration in each sample was calculated from the MDA standard curve and normalized to the total protein content.

Intracellular glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) activity measurement

Following seeding onto 12-well plates (2 × 105 cells per well), PC-3/RWPE-1 cells were incubated overnight. Afterward, cells were treated with Fe3O4, FC, FCB, or PFCB at equivalent concentrations (Fe: 25 µg/mL) for 24 h. GPX4 enzymatic activity was measured using a commercial colorimetric assay kit (E-BC-K883-M, Elabscience, China). Briefly, cells were harvested and lysed in extract working solution with sonication on ice, followed by centrifugation at 10,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. For each sample, 20 µL of supernatant was dispensed into paired experimental and control wells of a microplate. The experimental wells received 40 µL of reaction buffer, while the control wells received 40 µL of reaction buffer containing GPX4 inhibitor. After incubation at 37 °C for 30 min, 140 µL of reaction working solution was added to all wells, followed by 40 µL of oxidant working solution to initiate the GPX4-coupled reaction. The absorbance at 340 nm was recorded within 15 s (A1) of oxidant addition and again after incubation at RT for 15 min (A2) using a microplate reader. GPX4 activity (U/g protein) was calculated from the absorbance change (ΔA = A1 − A2) of the experimental well relative to the control well, normalized to the total protein content.

Intracellular ROS detection

Following seeding onto 24-well plates (1 × 105 cells per well), PC-3 cells were incubated overnight. Afterward, cells were treated with Fe3O4, FC, FCB, or PFCB at equivalent concentrations (Fe: 25 µg/mL) for 12 h. PC-3 cells were then washed twice with PBS and incubated with 2′,7′-dichlorofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) (10 µM) for 25 min in dark. After incubation, the cells were washed twice with PBS followed by fresh PBS supplement. The images were documented by using the fluorescence microscope (IX73, Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

LPO assay

Following seeding onto 24-well plates (1 × 105 cells per well), PC-3/RWPE-1 cells were incubated overnight. Afterward, cells were treated with Fe3O4, FC, FCB, or PFCB at equivalent concentrations (Fe: 25 µg/mL) for 24 h, followed by washing twice with PBS and incubation with C11-BODIPY581/591 (2 µM) for 30 min in dark. After incubation, the cells were washed twice with PBS followed by fresh PBS supplement. The images were documented by using the fluorescence microscope.

Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) assay

Following seeding onto 12-well plates (2 × 10 5 cells per well), PC-3/RWPE-1 cells were incubated overnight. Afterward, cells were treated with Fe3O4 , FC, FCB, or PFCB at equivalent concentrations (Fe: 25 µg/mL) for 24 h. LDH release into the culture media was measured using a commercial colorimetric assay kit (GK10003, GlpBio, USA). Briefly, culture media were collected, followed by centrifugation at 10,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatants were transferred to a 96-well plate, mixed with the LDH detection working solution, and incubated at RT in the dark. The enzymatic reaction was terminated by the stop solution, and the absorbance was recorded at 450 nm using the microplate reader. The LDH release was expressed as a percentage relative to that of the control group.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Following seeding onto 12-well plates (2 × 105 cells per well), PC-3/RWPE-1 cells were incubated overnight. Afterward, cells were treated with Fe3O4 , FC, FCB, or PFCB at equivalent concentrations (Fe: 25 µg/mL) for 24 h. The concentrations of interleukin-1β (IL-1β) and high mobility group box-1 protein (HMGB-1) in the culture media were determined using commercial ELISA kits (E-HSEL-H0001 and E-EL-H1554, Elabscience, China). Briefly, culture media were collected and centrifuged at 1,000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C. Aliquots (100 µL) of the supernatants were added to antibody-precoated 96-well plates. Serially diluted protein standards were included on the same plate for quantification. The plates were incubated at 37 °C for 1.5 h. After removal of the liquid, 100 µL of biotinylated detection antibody working solution was added to each well and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. The plates were washed three times with wash buffer, followed by the addition of 100 µL of HRP conjugate working solution and incubation at 37 °C for 30 min. After five additional washes, 90 µL of TMB substrate reagent was added and incubated at 37 °C for 15 min in the dark. The reaction was terminated by adding 50 µL of stop solution, and the absorbance was measured at 450 nm using the microplate reader. The concentrations of IL-1β and HMGB-1 were calculated from their respective standard curves.

Apoptosis analysis

Following seeding onto 12-well plates (2 × 105 cells per well), PC-3 cells were incubated overnight. Afterward, cells were treated with Fe3O4, FC, FCB, or PFCB at equivalent concentrations (Fe: 25 µg/mL) for 24 h. Upon termination of incubation process, the cells were harvested, followed by staining with Annexin V and PI. Apoptotic cell population was analyzed via a flow cytometer (DxFLEX, Beckman Coulter, USA).

In vivo tumoricidal effect evaluation

To establish the murine model of prostate cancer in vivo, 8 × 105 RM-1 cells (100 µL sterile PBS-resuspended) underwent subcutaneous injection into the right flank of each male C57BL/6J mouse. When the tumor volume reached approximately 200 mm3, the mice were randomly divided into five groups and intravenously injected with normal saline (NS) or Fe3O4, FC, FCB, and PFCB at equivalent ion concentration (Fe: 10 mg/kg), respectively. Body weights and tumor volumes were measured every 2 days. Ascertainment of tumor morphometric parameters was conducted utilizing precision digital calipers, with tumor volume subsequently computed according to the established equation: volume = (length × width²)/2. Following the endpoint of the experimental protocol, humane euthanasia was performed on the experimental mice on Day 8 starting from the first treatment, after which the tumor tissues were harvested via surgical excision and underwent sequential processing steps: gravimetric assessment, digital documentation via photography, immersion fixation in 4% formaldehyde solution, and subsequent H&E, immunohistochemical (IHC) and terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase mediated dUTP nick end labeling (TUNEL) analyses.

Data analyses

GraphPad Prism (version 10.0) served as the computational tool for the implementation of both data processing operations and statistical analyses. Data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was utilized to determine the differences between groups, and a P-value of less than 0.05 was defined as denoting statistical significance. Survival curves were evaluated using a log-rank (Mantel-Cox) test.

Results

Synthesis and characterization of the pPB-Fe3O4@CaO2-BzATP nanoplatform

Nanozyme-driven ferroptosis holds great promise in cancer therapy, yet the inadequate supply of H2O2 within the tumor microenvironment largely restricts the catalytic efficiency of nanozymes. To resolve this matter, we developed a targeted nanozyme platform pPB-Fe3O4@CaO2-BzATP, which integrated self-supplied H2O2, tumor targeting, and multimodal cell death induction for prostate cancer therapy. First, Fe3O4 nanoparticles were synthesized. TEM images revealed that the Fe3O4 nanoparticles, which were composed of multiple crystals, possessed an average size of approximately 50 nm (Fig. 2A). Subsequently, ultrasmall CaO2 was deposited onto the surface of Fe3O4 to form Fe3O4@CaO2 nanocomposites (designated as FC). TEM images showed numerous ultrasmall particles on the surface of Fe3O4 nanoparticles, indicating successful deposition of CaO2 (Fig. 2B). In addition, high-resolution TEM (HRTEM) images displayed distinct lattice fringes as well as the deposited layer on the core surface (Fig. 2C). Elemental mapping analysis and EDX pattern of FC nanoparticles further confirmed the presence of Fe, Ca, and O elements, with Ca element predominantly distributed at the outer layer of nanocomposites (Fig. 2D, E). The crystal structure of FC nanoparticles was then characterized by XRD, which identified two crystalline phases corresponding to Fe3O4 and CaO2 (JCPDS card nos. 19–0629/03-0865) (Fig. 2F). Moreover, XPS analysis of FC nanoparticles verified the existence of Fe, Ca, and O elements, which was in line with elemental mapping findings. High-resolution XPS spectra of Fe 2p and Ca 2p exhibited characteristic peaks at 725.4 eV, 710.7 eV, and 346.3 eV, which corresponded to Fe2+, Fe3+, and Ca2+, respectively (Fig. 2G, H, S1). To further determine the relative proportions of Fe2+ and Fe3+, the Fe 2p spectrum was deconvoluted using a nonlinear least-squares fitting (NLLSF) method. The results showed that the percentages of Fe2+ and Fe3+ were 31.17% and 68.83%, respectively (Fig. S2). The above mentioned analyses collectively confirmed the successful synthesis of the FC nanoparticles. To construct a tumor-targeted nanoplatform for synergistic therapy of prostate cancer, FC nanoparticles were loaded with BzATP to form Fe3O4@CaO2-BzATP (designated as FCB), followed by modification with pPB-PEG-DSPE polymer, yielding the final nanocomposite pPB-Fe3O4@CaO2-BzATP (designated as PFCB). The modification procedure for PFCB was validated by FT-IR spectroscopy. The FT-IR spectrum of Fe3O4 showed a typical characteristic absorption at 570 cm⁻1, corresponding to the stretching vibration of the Fe–O bond. Compared with bare Fe3O4, FC exhibited new absorption peaks at 1490 cm⁻1 and 1413 cm⁻1, which were assigned to Ca–O-related vibrations in CaO2, verifying the successful deposition of CaO2. In addition, BzATP showed a strong absorption peak at 1384 cm⁻1, attributed to the bending vibration of methyl groups. After BzATP loading, a blue shift of this characteristic absorption from 1384 cm⁻1 to 1423 cm⁻1 was observed in FCB, which might be attributed to hydrogen bonding interactions between BzATP and FC nanoparticles. Notably, the absorption peak of pPB-PEG-DSPE at 1104 cm⁻1, corresponding to the stretching vibration of the C–O bond, was also observed in PFCB. Moreover, the absorption intensity of PFCB at 1104 cm⁻1 was higher than that of FCB, further supporting the successful modification with pPB-PEG-DSPE. Taken together, these FT-IR results confirmed the successful stepwise fabrication of PFCB (Fig. 2I). In addition, the hydrodynamic size of PFCB showed no significant increase compared to that of the FC nanoparticles (Fig. 2J), indicating good stability of the constructed nanoplatforms. To confirm the pH responsiveness of PFCB, the release of Fe and Ca ion was evaluated in phosphate buffer at different pH values representing physiological (pH 7.4), intra-tumoral (pH 6.5), endosomal/lysosomal (pH 5.5) conditions. PFCB showed relatively lower Fe/Ca ion release at pH 7.4, while markedly increased release was observed at pH 6.5 and, more prominently, at pH 5.5, due to the accelerated disintegration of PFCB under acidic condition (Fig. S3A, B).

It is well established that Fe3O4 possesses excellent nanozyme activity, enabling it to exert functions analogous to those of biological enzymes and convert H2O2 into ·OH under acidic conditions. To systematically evaluate the ROS generation capacity of PFCB, a series of in vitro experiments were designed to characterize its enzyme-mimetic activities and ROS production efficiency. Initially, we evaluated the POD-like catalytic activity of PFCB via a colorimetric assay with TMB and MB (Fig. 2K). The ∙OH derived from PFCB-catalyzed H2O2 oxidation converted colorless TMB to its blue form of oxidized TMB (oxTMB), which displayed a characteristic absorbance peak at 652 nm (Fig. 2L). Later on, the capacity to generate ·OH was examined by MB degradation, confirming POD-like catalytic activity of PFCB (Fig. 2M). Eventually, the POD-like activity of PFCB was confirmed by the Michaelis–Menten constant (Km = 179.2 mM) and maximum velocity (Vmax = 6.463 × 10− 3 mM min− 1) (Fig. 2N). As illustrated in Fig. S4, PFCB exhibited potent intratumoral H2O2 catalysis, generating highly cytotoxic ∙OH through its POD-like activities. To further verify the involvement of ∙OH in the PFCB-mediated catalytic process, typical ∙OH scavengers, including DMSO, isopropyl alcohol (IPA), and tert-butyl alcohol (TBA), were introduced to capture ∙OH generated during the catalytic reaction. As shown in Fig. 2O and P, the addition of DMSO, IPA, or TBA markedly suppressed both oxTMB formation and MB degradation, indicating that ∙OH was mainly responsible for the POD-like catalytic activity of PFCB.

PFCB triggers ferroptosis in prostate cancer cells

To confirm the internalization of nanoconstructs in cells, the intracellular Fe and Ca contents were quantitatively measured by ICP‒MS after cells were incubated with different nanoconstructs. As shown in Fig. 3A, the intracellular Fe content gradually increased over time in all Fe-containing groups, with the PFCB group exhibiting the highest Fe accumulation. Similarly, PFCB realized markedly higher intracellular Ca content than FCB and FC (Fig. 3B). These results confirmed that PFCB could be efficiently internalized by prostate cancer cells. The in vitro cytotoxicity of Fe3O4, FC, FCB, and PFCB nanoparticles toward PC-3 prostate cancer cells was determined by MTT assay. In the comparative study at equal concentrations, the cell viability was significantly lower in the groups treated with FC, FCB and PFCB, especially in PFCB group (Fig. 3C-F). To investigate whether PFCB-induced cell death involves ferroptosis, we first measured intracellular GSH levels, which can effectively suppress ferroptosis. As illustrated in Fig. S5A, treatment with FC, FCB or PFCB significantly reduced intracellular GSH levels in PC-3 cells compared to the control group or Fe3O4 -treated group. In contrast, the levels of GSSG, a metabolic product of GSH, increased following FC, FCB or PFCB treatment (Fig. S5B), thereby exhibiting the decreased GSH/GSSG ratio in these three groups, among which the PFCB group exhibited the most pronounced decrease (Fig. 3G). In addition, as shown in Fig. 3H, compared to the other groups, PFCB treatment exhibited the greatest increase in MDA, which is a terminal byproduct of LPO and serves as a key indirect biomarker for ferroptosis. As GPX4 enzymatic activity is critically dependent on GSH availability, and GSH depletion directly impairs GPX4 function, we next examined GPX4 enzymatic activity, which was significantly decreased upon treatment with FC, FCB or PFCB in PC-3 cells, with PFCB showing the strongest effect (Fig. 3I). To further evaluate whether PFCB induced similar ferroptosis-related changes in noncancerous cells, the aforementioned ferroptosis indicators were examined in human prostate epithelial RWPE-1 cells. In contrast to PC-3 cells, no obvious changes of GSH/GSSG ratio, MDA level, and GPX4 activity were observed in RWPE-1 cells after treatment with FC, FCB and PFCB (Fig. 3J-L). In parallel, GPX4 protein expression level was markedly decreased in PC-3 cells after FC, FCB or PFCB treatment, especially in the PFCB group, whereas GPX4 protein abundance remained largely unchanged in RWPE-1 cells after corresponding treatments (Fig. 3M, N), indicating the selective ferroptosis-inducing effect of the nanoconstructs in prostate cancer cells. Later on, we evaluated the intracellular ROS and LPO levels by using the fluorescent probes DCFH-DA (for ROS) and C11-BODIPY581/591 (for LPO), respectively. As illustrated in PC-3 cells, most significant ROS accumulation was observed in the PFCB group (Fig. 3O, S6, S7). Similarly, PFCB group exhibited the highest green fluorescence signal of LPO probe (Fig. 3P, S8). In contrast, C11-BODIPY581/591 staining showed no remarkable enhancement of green fluorescence in RWPE-1 cells after the administration of same duration and concentration of PFCB (Fig. S9), further confirming that PFCB did not induce significant ferroptosis in noncancerous prostate epithelial RWPE-1 cells. Subsequently, acridine orange (AO) staining assay was used to assess ROS-mediated lysosomal damage in PC-3 cells. As shown in Fig. S10, large red-stained spots indicating intact lysosomes were observed in the cytoplasm of the control and Fe3O4 groups, while the gradually weakened red fluorescence signals were captured in the FC and FCB groups. In the PFCB group, the red fluorescence of AO was even absent, suggesting severe lysosomal disruption due to excessive ROS generation. Notably, the cytotoxicity of PFCB could be significantly reversed by classical ferroptosis inhibitors, including iron chelator deferasirox (DFR), GSH and N-acetylcysteine (NAC) in PC-3 cells (Fig. 3Q), further supporting the crucial role of ferroptosis in PFCB-elicited prostate cancer cell death.

PFCB elicits pyroptosis in prostate cancer cells

Given that BzATP possesses potent agonistic activity at the P2X7 receptor, whose activation is closely associated with inflammasome assembly and pyroptotic cell death, we next investigated whether PFCB treatment could induce pyroptosis in prostate cancer cells. As pyroptosis is typically characterized by progressive ballooning, morphological changes were first examined to evaluate pyroptotic features following different treatments. PC-3 cells treated with PBS maintained normal morphology, while cells incubated with FCB and PFCB displayed classic characteristics of pyroptosis, including cell swelling with balloon-like structures (indicated with white arrows) (Fig. 4A). In contrast, no obvious balloon-like swelling structures were observed in each group in noncancerous prostate epithelial RWPE-1 cells after treatment with Fe3O4, FC, FCB, and PFCB (Fig. S11). Meanwhile, in PC-3 cells, a greatest increase in LDH release was detected in the culture supernatants following PFCB exposure (Fig. 4B). In addition, ELISA revealed that FC treatment did not significantly elevate IL-1β or HMGB-1 secretion compared with the control or Fe3O4 groups, whereas both inflammatory mediators were markedly increased following FCB or PFCB treatment (Fig. 4C, D). However, in RWPE-1 cells, no significant changes were detected in LDH, IL-1β, and HMGB-1 release among the groups (Fig. 4E–G), indicating that these nanoconstructs did not induce potent pyroptosis-related inflammatory responses in noncancerous prostate epithelial cells. To further verify the occurrence of pyroptosis, we detected the expression levels of NLRP3, GSDMD, GSDMD-N, pro-caspase-1, pro-IL-1β in cell lysates, and assessed the secretion of cleaved caspase-1 as well as mature IL-1β in culture supernatants via Western blot assay. As shown in Fig. 4H and I, a significant increase of key pyroptosis-related proteins (NLRP3, GSDMD-N, cleaved caspase-1 and mature IL-1β) was documented in the PFCB group, suggesting the activation of the cleaved caspase-1/GSDMD-N-dependent pyroptosis pathway. Conversely, after RWPE-1 cells were treated with Fe3O4, FC, FCB and PFCB, Western blot analysis showed no significant upregulation of essential pyroptosis-related proteins, including NLRP3, GSDMD-N in cell lysates and cleaved caspase-1, mature IL-1β in cell culture supernatants (Fig. 4J). Of note, the cytotoxicity of PFCB was moderately attenuated by classical pyroptosis inhibitor MCC950 (Fig. 4K), further supporting the involvement of pyroptosis in PFCB-induced prostate cancer cell death.

PFCB induces apoptotic cell death via calcium overload

First of all, Fluo-4 AM probe was employed to detect the intracellular Ca2+ levels. Compared with the control group, green fluorescence intensity was markedly enhanced in PC-3 prostate cancer cells treated with FC, FCB and PFCB nanoparticles, with the strongest signal captured after PFCB treatment, confirming the efficient internalization of nanoparticles and BzATP intervention could further enhance intracellular Ca2+ accumulation (Fig. 5A, S12). Based on the above findings, we hypothesized that PFCB released a substantial amount of Ca2+, leading to intracellular Ca2+ overload, which disrupted mitochondrial homeostasis and subsequently induced apoptosis. Therefore, we next employed the JC-1 probe to assess the impact of Ca2+ on mitochondrial function in PC-3 cells. Under normal MMP, JC-1 forms aggregated pattern within the mitochondria, which emits red fluorescence. By contrast, at lower MMP, JC-1 probe exists in a monomeric form that emits green fluorescence. As shown in Fig. 5B, green fluorescence began to increase in FC-treated PC-3 cells and was further enhanced in FCB and PFCB groups, with the most pronounced increase after PFCB treatment, indicating severe mitochondrial damage and loss of membrane potential. Furthermore, the expression levels of proteins associated with apoptosis were examined by Western blot assay. Significant downregulation of Bcl-2 as well as remarkable upregulation of Bax and cleaved caspase-3 were detected in Ca2+-containing nanoconstruct groups (Fig. 5C, D), confirming apoptosis induction under Ca2+ overload conditions. Moreover, compared with FCB treatment, the more potent apoptosis-inducing effect of PFCB suggested a contribution from active targeting motif pPB. To further assess the tumoricidal activity of each nanoconstruct, calcein-AM/PI dual-staining assay was utilized to discriminate between live and dead cells. PI fluorescence intensity (red channel) was notably elevated in FC-, FCB- and PFCB-treated groups, which demonstrated the superior tumoricidal effect of PFCB nanoplatform (Fig. 5E). Subsequently, the in vitro cytotoxicity of Fe3O4, FC, FCB, and PFCB nanoconstructs toward PC-3 cells was assessed via the MTT assay. As depicted in Fig. 5F, cell viability was notably reduced in FC-, FCB- and PFCB-treated groups relative to the control and Fe3O4-treated groups, especially for the PFCB-treated group. In contrast, these nanoconstructs showed relatively limited effects on the viability of noncancerous prostate epithelial RWPE-1 cells after treatment with FC, FCB, and PFCB (Fig. 5G). Together with the distinct ferroptosis- and pyroptosis-related responses, these results suggested that PFCB exhibited selective cytotoxicity toward prostate cancer cells. Furthermore, Annexin V/PI dual-staining assay was utilized to detect apoptotic cell death, demonstrating a marked increase in the apoptotic cell ratio in PFCB-treated group relative to that in the other groups (Fig. 5H). Eventually, calcium chelator (BAPTA-AM) and pan-caspase inhibitor (z-VAD-FMK) could significantly reverse the tumoricidal effect of PFCB (Fig. 5I), further supporting the essential role of Ca2+ overload-mediated apoptosis in PFCB-elicited prostate cancer cell death.

Antitumor efficacy of PFCB in vivo

To evaluate the in vivo tumoricidal effect of PFCB, the RM-1 murine prostate cancer model was established (Fig. 6A). When tumor volume reached approximately 200 mm3, the mice were randomly allocated to the experimental groups below: NS, Fe3O4, FC, FCB and PFCB groups. All animals in the aforementioned groups were injected intravenously (i.v.). The mice in all groups exhibited stable body weights, with no significant alterations observed throughout the treatment period (Fig. 6B). Notably, compared with the NS and Fe3O4 groups, the FC-, FCB- and PFCB-treated groups showed significant tumor growth suppression (Fig. 6C–F). Among these, the PFCB-treated group showed most potent antitumor efficacy, which could be attributed to its ability to actively target prostate cancer and unique capability to simultaneously elicit ferroptosis, pyroptosis and apoptosis. In addition, PFCB treatment also markedly prolonged the survival of tumor-bearing mice. The NS, Fe3O4, and FC groups exhibited 0% survival by Day 10, Day 10, and Day 16, respectively, starting from the first treatment (Day 0), whereas 40% of mice in the FCB group remained alive at Day 20. Notably, the PFCB group maintained 100% survival throughout the observation period, demonstrating its relatively sustained tumor suppression effect (Fig. S13A, B). To further investigate the underlying mechanisms of cell death in vivo, surgically excised tumor tissues were collected and subjected to IHC staining for GPX4 (a ferroptosis-related marker), GSDMD-N (a pyroptosis-related indicator), and cleaved caspase-3 (an apoptosis-related marker). As presented in Fig. 6G, decreased GPX4 and increased cleaved caspase-3 expression levels were observed in the FC-, FCB- and PFCB-treated groups, demonstrating the activation of ferroptosis and apoptosis in vivo. Moreover, elevated GSDMD-N expression in the FCB- and PFCB-treated groups indicated the induction of pyroptosis via additional BzATP loading of the nanoconstructs. Consistent with the aforementioned results, the PFCB-treated group exhibited most pronounced activation of all three cell death pathways, eventually contributing to its superior antitumor effect. Likewise, TUNEL/DAPI staining of the tumor tissues demonstrated enhanced apoptosis in both the FC- and FCB-treated groups, and the most significant increase was documented after PFCB treatment (Fig. 6G). H&E staining further revealed more pronounced cavity-like regions and fragmented morphology in tumor tissues after PFCB treatment (Fig. 6G). Taken together, these results indicated that the PFCB nanoplatform effectively elicited multi-modal cell death in vivo, presenting remarkable tumor tissue damage.

Biocompatibility of PFCB in vivo

Since PFCB nanoplatform has demonstrated excellent performance in suppressing prostate cancer progression through i.v. administration, we first evaluated the biocompatibility of PFCB by measuring the hemolysis rate. Generally, hemolysis leads to disruption of erythrocyte integrity and release of hemoglobin. As shown in Fig. S14, in contrast to the positive control group treated with deionized water, no visible color change was observed following PFCB treatment at each concentration. Moreover, the hemolysis rate of PFCB was calculated to be only 2.13% even at the maximum Fe concentration of 50 µg/mL, which was below 5% threshold for good blood compatibility, confirming its excellent hemocompatibility. In addition, H&E staining of the major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney) after PFCB treatment revealed no significant pathological abnormalities or tissue damage (Fig. S15). Likewise, compared with the NS group, no marked differences were observed in serum biomarkers of liver and renal function. In particular, alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), and creatinine (CREA) remained within the normal ranges (Fig. S16). Collectively, these results indicated that PFCB exhibited excellent biocompatibility in vivo and supported its potential as an ideal nanomedicine for prostate cancer therapy.

Discussion

In this study, we developed a multifunctional targeted nanoplatform PFCB with a Fe3O4-POD nanozyme core, which was decorated with CaO2 and loaded with P2X7 receptor agonist BzATP. The surface was modified with pPB-PEG-DSPE for enhanced prostate tumor-specific targeting ability. Upon cellular internalization, PFCB nanoplatform underwent responsive dissociation in the acidic environment, CaO2 was hydrolyzed by acidification to release Ca2+ and realize H2O2 self-supply continuously. The Fe3O4 nanozyme had POD-like activity, catalyzing H2O2 to generate highly cytotoxic ·OH while simultaneously consuming GSH, disrupting redox balance, and causing the accumulation of toxic LPO to augment ferroptosis. Released Ca2+ accompanied by enhanced Ca2+ influx, which was facilitated by P2X7 receptor activation with BzATP, synergistically triggered Ca2+ overload, leading to mitochondrial dysfunction and caspase-3-dependent apoptosis. Furthermore, BzATP concurrently facilitated K+ efflux, triggering NLRP3 inflammasome assembly and caspase-1 activation, followed by cleavage of the GSDMD protein to elicit pyroptosis. By combining catalytic ROS and LPO generation, Ca2+ overload and GSDMD-N cleavage, PFCB nanozyme system achieved synergistic therapy against prostate cancer, thereby establishing a self-reinforcing intracellular cascade of cell death.

Catalytic therapy based on the POD-like activity of nanozymes has become a powerful cancer treatment strategy. It can specifically induce the accumulation of LPO and trigger ferroptosis to eradicate cancer cells by catalyzing the production of potently cytotoxic ·OH from endogenous H2O2 [24, 62]. However, its therapeutic efficacy is frequently constrained by the scarcity of H2O2 in the complex tumor microenvironment and the robust antioxidant defense machinery (such as high abundance of GSH) within cancer cells, which impairs catalytic activity for the Fenton reaction and thereby may attenuate ferroptotic cell death in these cells [27, 28]. To overcome these bottlenecks, and inspired by the finding that a Cu2₋XSe-based cascade nanoreactor can enable stimulus-triggered burst release and in situ generation of therapeutic species to amplify antitumor efficacy [63], we introduced CaO2 as a built-in H2O2 generator to the Fe3O4-POD nanozyme, which continuously self-supplied catalytic substrate under acidic conditions, and significantly enhanced the efficiency of ROS generation. This strategy not only potently increased intracellular ROS levels but also concurrently depleted GSH and suppressed GPX4 activity, ultimately augmenting ferroptosis through the accumulation of LPO. These findings align with the previous reports, verifying the feasibility of self-supplied H2O2 nanoplatform in enhancing the efficacy of ferroptosis [37, 38].

Furthermore, the normal function of mitochondria is highly dependent on the orderly transport of signaling ions such as Ca2+. Excessive Ca2+ accumulation can lead to MMP decline and reduced ATP synthesis, thereby inducing mitochondrial dysfunction and disrupting cellular homeostasis [33–36]. Although intracellular Ca2+ levels can be elevated via internalization of calcium-based materials, cancer cells are capable of restoring Ca2+ homeostasis through intrinsic buffering capacity, thereby hampering the induction of apoptosis [41–43]. By comparison, the PFCB nanoplatform with dual mechanism, represented by CaO2 decomposition as well as P2X7 receptor activation to activate cation-selective channels for Ca2+ influx, maximized intracellular Ca2+ overload. Our experimental results demonstrated that PFCB-mediated elevation of Ca2+ was accompanied by the significant decrease in MMP, upregulated expression of the pro-apoptotic protein Bax and cleavage of caspase-3, indicating the occurrence of mitochondrial-mediated apoptosis. Of particular note, as a key organelle that regulates the balance of intracellular ROS and GSH, mitochondria under dysfunctional status may further exacerbate oxidative stress and synergize with ferroptosis catalyzed by the Fe3O4-POD nanozyme, consequently enhancing the tumoricidal effect.

As stated above, BzATP can endow the PFCB nanozyme system with the ability to actively regulate ion channels in the cell membrane. Following P2X7 receptor activation, continuous Ca2+ influx and K+ efflux led to intracellular ion imbalance, which in turn activated the NLRP3 inflammasome assembly, triggering caspase-1-dependent GSDMD cleavage for pyroptosis induction [49, 52, 54]. Our findings demonstrated significant upregulation of NLRP3 and GSDMD-N, elevated release of LDH, increased secretion of cleaved caspase-1, IL-1β, and HMGB-1, confirming PFCB-mediated pyroptosis. These findings are in line with previous studies, further clarifying P2X7 receptor acts as a direct upstream regulator that mechanistically couples ionic dyshomeostasis with inflammasome activation and pyroptotic cell death [47].

It is worth emphasizing that the PFCB nanoplatform simultaneously integrated ferroptosis, pyroptosis and apoptosis, providing a novel approach to overcoming tumor heterogeneity and apoptosis resistance. Ferroptosis exploits vulnerabilities in lipid metabolism, pyroptosis is marked by robust inflammation and plasma membrane rupture, and apoptosis mainly relies on the mitochondrial dysfunction. These distinct cell death pathways function synergistically, complementing and reinforcing one another to establish a self-amplifying cytotoxic network. Consistent with this mechanism, experiments conducted both in vitro and in vivo demonstrated that PFCB could significantly inhibit tumor growth, concurrently upregulate the expression of GSDMD-N and cleaved caspase-3, and downregulate GPX4. These findings collectively confirm the potent synergistic antitumor efficacy of the PFCB nanoplatform.

In addition to the potent antitumor efficacy, the selectivity of PFCB toward prostate cancer cells was further supported by parallel experiments in noncancerous prostate epithelial RWPE-1 cells. At the same administered duration and concentration, PFCB did not induce remarkable ferroptosis- and pyroptosis-related alterations in RWPE-1 cells, and the inhibition of viability caused by PFCB in RWPE-1 cells was significantly lower than the pronounced tumoricidal effect observed in PC-3 cells. The differential response may be associated with the distinct characteristics between cancerous and noncancerous prostate epithelial cells. Aggressive prostate cancer cells frequently exhibit remodeled lipid metabolism, enhanced dependence on iron-associated redox homeostasis, and elevated basal oxidative stress, which may collectively reduce their tolerance to oxidative damage and regulate cell death [18, 64].

In terms of biosafety, the surface modification with pPB peptide enables active targeting of prostate cancer cells that overexpress PDGFR-β, thereby enhancing tumor-specific accumulation and therapeutic precision. This targeting strategy, combined with PEGylation, endowed the PFCB nanoplatform with favorable biocompatibility. Recent advances in intelligently engineered nanoencapsulation systems have further highlighted the importance of integrating polymer modification with targeted motif-assisted nanoengineering to improve tumor selectivity, stability, and biosafety of antitumor nanoplatforms [65], which also support the rational design of the present PFCB system. Negligible toxicities were recorded in hemolysis tests, serum biochemical analyses, and histopathological evaluations, indicating that PFCB nanoplatform holds promise for clinical translation.

Conclusion

In summary, we develop an active targeting nanozyme system (pPB-Fe3O4@CaO2-BzATP), which integrates H2O2 self-supply, catalytic amplification, and ion channel regulation to trigger ferroptosis, pyroptosis, and apoptosis for prostate cancer therapy. Benefiting from the combinatorial design, PFCB can be readily adapted to other malignancies by simply replacing the surface targeting ligand, thereby establishing a versatile nanoplatform for precision cancer therapy. Moreover, PFCB-induced pyroptosis, which involves the P2X7 receptor, holds potential to synergize with immune checkpoint inhibitors to elicit systemic antitumor immunity. The selective tumoricidal effect along with the favorable biosafety profile further supports its future clinical translation. Despite the encouraging prospects, the long-term biodegradation and excretion kinetics of the inorganic components (Fe3O4 and CaO2) require further systematic evaluation. Meanwhile, interpatient tumor heterogeneity, particularly the variable expression levels of PDGFR-β and P2X7 receptor, may influence the therapeutic efficacy. Collectively, this work provides an H2O2 self-supplied nanoplatform to selectively combat prostate cancer by integrating enhanced ferroptosis, P2X7 receptor-mediated pyroptosis and Ca2+ overload-induced apoptosis as a promising and unified therapeutic strategy.
