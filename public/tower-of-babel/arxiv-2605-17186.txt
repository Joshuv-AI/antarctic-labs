Introduction

Infinite linear ODE systems arise naturally as Chapman–Kolmogorov forward equations for continuous-time Markov processes.
The canonical example is the birth-death process,

\frac{\mathrm{d}p_{n}(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}=\lambda_{n-1}\,p_{n-1}(t)+\mu_{n+1}\,p_{n+1}(t)-(\lambda_{n}+\mu_{n})\,p_{n}(t),

(1)

with
p_{n}(t)=\mathbb{P}(X_{t}=n)
and birth-death rates
\lambda_{n},\mu_{n}\geq 0
; here
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
is the running count, and we use lowercase letters for count indices and uppercase letters for the corresponding output-window caps throughout.
The hierarchy is bidirectionally coupled: each level
n
exchanges mass with both neighbors
n-1
and
n+1
.
The standard remedy is to truncate at a finite cap
N
(the largest count the user asks to compute), but truncation drops the back-flux from
p_{N+1}
and biases the in-window solution – mildly at
t=0
, and increasingly with time as the bulk of
p
drifts toward the cap.
Even setting truncation aside, naively solving such hierarchies quickly becomes infeasible at scale.
In a telegraph-style model of stochastic gene expression, for instance, a hidden promoter state takes one of
n_{T}
values, with
n_{T}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
denoting the number of hidden states, and is coupled to an unbounded mRNA count
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
capped at
M
.
The matrix-valued hierarchy
\dot{\boldsymbol{P}}_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{T}}
then has joint truncation size
n_{T}(M{+}1)
and an attendant matrix exponential of cubic cost.
The two-species predator-prey master equation with joint state
(x,y)
and per-species caps
N_{X},N_{Y}
carries a dense matrix exponential of cost
(N_{X}N_{Y})^{3}
and becomes computationally prohibitive past joint dimensions of a few thousand.
A formally time-reversed birth-death system has
\lambda<0
and no probabilistic meaning, yet its coefficient ODE still encodes an analytic answer worth computing – so the algorithmic question is not exclusively probabilistic.

Our method builds on the composition identity for the generating function

G(z,t)=K_{t}(z)\,G\bigl(\Phi_{t}(z),0\bigr),

(2)

which expresses the generating function
G(z,t)=\sum_{n}x_{n}(t)\,z^{n}
of any linear-rate hierarchy (i.e. one whose off-diagonal generator entries satisfy
\mathcal{L}_{n+r,n}=\alpha_{r}n+\beta_{r}
for real coefficients
\alpha_{r},\beta_{r}
) in terms of a single-particle characteristic flow
\Phi_{t}
and an immigration multiplier
K_{t}
.
This formula is standard in the branching-process and monomolecular-CME literature – it appears as the Kendall-Harris composition formula for Markov branching with immigration

2.1
Scalar linear-rate closure

2.1.1
Linear-rate hierarchies

The feature of the master equation that produces a first-order PDE in
G(z,t)
– and therefore the closure – is the dependence of the rates on the source index.

Definition 1

(Linear-rate hierarchy)
.

The hierarchy
\dot{x}_{n}=\sum_{m}L_{n,m}\,x_{m}
on
\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
is
linear-rate
if there exist finitely supported real sequences
\{\alpha_{r}\}_{r\geq-1}
and
\{\beta_{r}\}_{r\geq 0}
(with
\beta_{-1}:=0
) such that

L_{n+r,\,n}=\alpha_{r}\,n+\beta_{r},\qquad r\geq-1,\;n\geq 0.

(3)

The
\alpha_{r}\,n
piece is per-particle; the
\beta_{r}
piece is state-independent.

The restriction
r\geq-1
covers all particle-system applications (death is the only
r<0
event in a per-particle generator); for formal or signed instances with deeper negative shifts the same Cauchy-product argument applies but
A
becomes a Laurent polynomial and existence of
\Phi_{t}
on
[0,1]
requires additional hypotheses.
This is the ‘monomolecular’ (chemical kinetics) or ‘linear-rate’ (CME) condition specialised to a single-species count axis; we use ‘linear-rate’ rather than ‘monomolecular’ to emphasise that the closure applies equally to signed or formal-power-series instances where the probabilistic interpretation is absent.

In the Markov case, linear-rate rates are exactly the rate structure forced by particle independence:
n
independent particles each producing change
r
at per-particle rate
\alpha_{r}
collectively produce rate
\alpha_{r}\,n
, with an additional state-independent
\beta_{r}
stream from outside the system.

It is a standard generating-function fact
[
19
]
that under Definition
1
the generating function
G(z,t)=\sum_{n}x_{n}(t)\,z^{n}
satisfies the first-order linear PDE

\partial_{t}G(z,t)=A(z)\,\partial_{z}G(z,t)+B(z)\,G(z,t),

(4)

with
A(z)=\sum_{r}\alpha_{r}\,z^{r+1}
and
B(z)=\sum_{r}\beta_{r}\,z^{r}
.
Equation (
4
) is the algebraic image of the backward equation for the single-ancestor PGF: in the Markov case
\Phi_{t}(z):=\mathbb{E}[z^{X_{t}}\mid X_{0}=1]
solves the characteristic ODE (
5
) as a Kolmogorov backward equation in
z
on
[0,1]
, and
K_{t}(z)
accumulates immigration multiplicatively along the characteristic; outside the Markov case the identification is purely algebraic but the same decomposition holds.
The backward viewpoint is the methodological origin of the closure: the linear-rate condition makes the backward action of the generator on the test function
z^{n}
a polynomial in
z
of bounded degree, which collapses the infinite forward hierarchy into a finite ODE on
\Phi_{t},K_{t}
.
Conversely, if
\partial_{t}G
involves
\partial_{z}^{k}G
with
k\geq 2
, then reading
\partial_{z}^{k}G=\sum_{n}n(n-1)\cdots(n-k+1)\,x_{n}\,z^{n-k}
shows that
\mathcal{L}_{n+r,n}
contains a polynomial factor of degree
\geq 2
in
n
, ruling out Definition
1
.
Hence the linear-rate condition is the rate-structure condition that produces a first-order PDE in
G(z,t)
; the finite-support requirement of Definition
1
additionally restricts
A(z)
and
B(z)
to polynomials, whereas analytic non-polynomial coefficients correspond to non-finite linear-rate rate structures.
Equation (
4
) is the foundation of the closure: a first-order PDE in one space variable integrates by characteristics, which is precisely what §
2.1.2
does.

2.1.2
Composition-multiplier solution

First-order linear PDEs in one space variable integrate by the method of characteristics.
Let
\Phi_{t}(z)
solve the characteristic ODE

\frac{\mathrm{d}\Phi_{t}(z)}{\mathrm{d}t}=A(\Phi_{t}(z)),\qquad\Phi_{0}(z)=z,

(5)

and let the multiplier
K_{t}(z)
solve

\frac{\mathrm{d}K_{t}(z)}{\mathrm{d}t}=B(\Phi_{t}(z))\,K_{t}(z),\qquad K_{0}(z)=1.

(6)

Theorem 2

(Composition-multiplier representation)
.

Let the hierarchy be linear-rate with
\Phi_{t}
and
K_{t}
defined by (
5
)–(
6
).
For any initial
G(\cdot,0)
such that
G(\Phi_{t}(\cdot),0)
is well defined on the relevant domain,

G(z,t)=K_{t}(z)\,G\bigl(\Phi_{t}(z),0\bigr)

(7)

is the unique solution of (
4
).

The initial generating function
G(\cdot,0)
is shifted along the characteristic flow
\Phi_{t}
(itself the single-particle survival map in the Markov case) and then rescaled by the multiplier
K_{t}
, which accumulates the immigration contributions from
B
.
Equation (
7
) converts the open hierarchy (
1
) into a finite computation on
\Phi_{t}
and
K_{t}
alone.

Corollary 3

(Algebraic recursion)
.

Expanding
\Phi_{t}(z)=\sum_{k}\phi_{k}(t)\,z^{k}
and
K_{t}(z)=\sum_{k}\kappa_{k}(t)\,z^{k}
as formal power series in
z
, equations (
5
)–(
6
) reduce to a closed lower-triangular system of ODEs on the coefficient vectors
\{\phi_{k}\},\{\kappa_{k}\}
:

\dot{\phi}_{n}(t)=[z^{n}]A(\Phi_{t}(z)),\qquad\dot{\kappa}_{n}(t)=[z^{n}]\bigl(B(\Phi_{t}(z))\,K_{t}(z)\bigr).

(8)

The right-hand sides are finite Cauchy products of
\{\phi_{0},\ldots,\phi_{n}\}
and
\{\kappa_{0},\ldots,\kappa_{n}\}
;
\dot{\phi}_{n}
depends only on
\{\phi_{k}\}_{k\leq n}
and
\dot{\kappa}_{n}
only on
\{\phi_{k},\kappa_{k}\}_{k\leq n}
, so the output coefficients on
\{0,\ldots,N\}
are obtained without imposing any boundary value at
N+1
.
The canonical specialization is pure birth-death with
\alpha_{1}=\lambda
,
\alpha_{-1}=\mu
,
\beta_{r}\equiv 0
, giving
A(z)=\lambda z^{2}-(\lambda+\mu)z+\mu
and
B\equiv 0
: (
8
) becomes the lower-triangular nonlinear ODE

\dot{p}_{n}(t)=-(\lambda+\mu)\,p_{n}(t)+\mu\,\delta_{n0}+\lambda\sum_{k=0}^{n}p_{k}(t)\,p_{n-k}(t),

(9)

the characteristic ODE (
5
) is the scalar Riccati
\dot{\Phi}=\lambda\Phi^{2}-(\lambda+\mu)\Phi+\mu
with closed-form
p_{0}(t)=(\mu-\mu\,e^{-(\mu-\lambda)t})/(\mu-\lambda\,e^{-(\mu-\lambda)t})
for
\lambda\neq\mu
(critical limit
\mu t/(1+\mu t)
), and the multiplier is trivial.
Writing
\rho(t):=(\lambda/\mu)\,p_{0}(t)
and
p_{1}(t):=(1-p_{0}(t))(1-\rho(t))
yields the geometric tail

p_{n}(t)=p_{1}(t)\,\rho(t)^{n-1},\qquad n\geq 1,

(10)

which is cancellation-free in double precision; heavy-tailed experiments in §
3
use (
10
) in place of any ODE integration (derivation in Appendix
H
).
Contrast (
1
), whose
\mu_{n+1}\,p_{n+1}
term reaches above

and forces a boundary closure
p_{N+1}=0
that biases the in-window solution.

Corollary 4

(Matrix recursion)
.

Suppose the hidden state is augmented by a finite-state index
a\in\{1,\ldots,n_{T}\}
with transitions independent of the count
X_{t}
, and that every hidden state shares the same per-particle death rate (scalar-drift condition).
Then
\Phi_{t}
remains scalar and the multiplier lifts to a matrix-valued
\mathbf{K}_{t}(z)\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{T}\times n_{T}}
solving

\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{K}_{t}(z)}{\mathrm{d}t}=\bigl(\mathbf{A}+\Phi_{t}(z)\,\mathbf{B}\bigr)\,\mathbf{K}_{t}(z),\qquad\mathbf{K}_{0}(z)=\mathbf{I},

(11)

where
\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{T}\times n_{T}}
collect transcript-free and transcript-producing hidden-state transitions.
Expanding
\mathbf{K}_{t}(z)=\sum_{k}\mathbf{K}_{t}^{(k)}\,z^{k}
in formal power series yields a lower-triangular matrix ODE on
\{\mathbf{K}_{t}^{(0)},\ldots,\mathbf{K}_{t}^{(N)}\}
with each block of size
n_{T}\times n_{T}
.
Without scalar drift the characteristic itself is
z
-dependent and the recursion no longer factors through a single
\Phi_{t}
; the construction and the telegraph G/R example are developed in §
2.2
.

2.1.4
Multi-type branching

The framework extends directly to multi-type branching processes, where each particle carries one of
K
types and offspring distributions may depend on the parent type
[
1
,
20
]
.
The state is the count vector
\boldsymbol{n}=(n_{1},\ldots,n_{K})\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{K}
, the PGF is the multivariate

F(\boldsymbol{z},t)=\sum_{\boldsymbol{n}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{K}}p_{\boldsymbol{n}}(t)\,\boldsymbol{z}^{\boldsymbol{n}},\qquad\boldsymbol{z}^{\boldsymbol{n}}:=\prod_{i=1}^{K}z_{i}^{n_{i}},

and the linear-rate condition becomes the multi-index
\mathcal{L}_{\boldsymbol{n}+\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{n}}=\sum_{i=1}^{K}\alpha^{(i)}_{\boldsymbol{r}}\,n_{i}+\beta_{\boldsymbol{r}}
, where
\alpha^{(i)}_{\boldsymbol{r}}
collects per-particle rates for a type-

\boldsymbol{r}
, and
\beta_{\boldsymbol{r}}
is state-independent (type-blind) immigration.

The PGF then satisfies the multivariate first-order linear PDE

\partial_{t}F=\sum_{i=1}^{K}A_{i}(\boldsymbol{z})\,\partial_{z_{i}}F+B(\boldsymbol{z})\,F,

(13)

with
A_{i}(\boldsymbol{z})=\sum_{\boldsymbol{r}}\alpha^{(i)}_{\boldsymbol{r}}\,z_{i}\,\boldsymbol{z}^{\boldsymbol{r}}
and
B(\boldsymbol{z})=\sum_{\boldsymbol{r}}\beta_{\boldsymbol{r}}\,\boldsymbol{z}^{\boldsymbol{r}}
.
The same characteristic-curve mechanism integrates (
13

\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{t}:\mathbb{R}^{K}\to\mathbb{R}^{K}
replaces the scalar
\Phi_{t}
, the multiplier
K_{t}
stays scalar, and the analogue of the composition-multiplier representation (

F(\boldsymbol{z},t)=K_{t}(\boldsymbol{z})\,F_{0}\bigl(\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{t}(\boldsymbol{z})\bigr).

The coefficient system on multi-indices
\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{K}
is closed and lower-triangular in the natural componentwise order on
\boldsymbol{k}
, so a finite output window
\{\boldsymbol{n}:0\leq n_{i}\leq N\}
admits the same in-window-exact integration as Proposition
5

2.2
Matrix-valued hidden states

2.2.1
Telegraph model

A canonical matrix-valued hierarchy is the telegraph model of stochastic gene expression
[
30
,
34
]
: a gene flips between
n_{T}
finite hidden states (promoter on/off, plus optional elongation chain), each state produces mRNA at a state-dependent rate, and every mRNA degrades at rate
\mu
regardless of hidden state.
Let
\boldsymbol{P}_{m}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{T}}
have entries
\boldsymbol{P}_{a,m}(t)=\mathbb{P}(\text{hidden state }a\text{ and }m\text{ mRNAs at time }t)
.
The joint master equation can be written

\frac{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{P}_{m}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\mathbf{A}\,\boldsymbol{P}_{m}+\mathbf{B}\,\boldsymbol{P}_{m-1}+\mu\bigl((m+1)\,\boldsymbol{P}_{m+1}-m\,\boldsymbol{P}_{m}\bigr),\qquad\boldsymbol{P}_{-1}\equiv 0,

(15)

where
\mathbf{A}
collects hidden-state transitions that do not produce a transcript and
\mathbf{B}
collects those that do.
For the ordinary two-state telegraph,
\mathbf{B}
is diagonal (a transcript is produced from the “on” state with no change of hidden state).
For the G/R elongation extension,
\mathbf{B}
may be off-diagonal (a transcript-producing transition simultaneously advances the polymerase).

2.2.2
Matrix-valued generating function

Define the vector-valued generating function
\boldsymbol{Z}(z,t)=\sum_{m}\boldsymbol{P}_{m}(t)\,z^{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{T}}
.
Multiplying (
15
) by
z^{m}
and summing gives the matrix-valued first-order PDE

\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{Z}(z,t)=(\mathbf{A}+z\mathbf{B})\,\boldsymbol{Z}(z,t)+\mu(1-z)\,\partial_{z}\boldsymbol{Z}(z,t).

(16)

The structure is identical to (
4
) except that the inhomogeneity
\mathbf{B}(z)
is now matrix-valued.
The hidden-state transitions and the per-mRNA degradation rate
\mu
are independent of
m
, so Corollary
4
applies with characteristic
\Phi_{t}(z)=1-(1-z)\,e^{-\mu t}
and matrix multiplier
\mathbf{K}_{t}(z)
solving (
11
).
The matrix-valued composition-multiplier representation

\boldsymbol{Z}(z,t)=\mathbf{K}_{t}(z)\,\boldsymbol{Z}_{0}\bigl(\Phi_{t}(z)\bigr)

(17)

delivers
\boldsymbol{Z}(z,t)
once
\mathbf{K}_{t}(z)
has been integrated on the output window.
Expanding
\mathbf{K}_{t}(z)=\sum_{k}\mathbf{K}_{t}^{(k)}\,z^{k}
in formal power series yields a lower-triangular matrix ODE on
\{\mathbf{K}_{t}^{(0)},\ldots,\mathbf{K}_{t}^{(M)}\}
, each block of size
n_{T}\times n_{T}
.
Integrating this system on the chosen output window
\{0,\ldots,M\}
costs
\Theta(S\,M\,n_{T}^{3})
in time and
\Theta(M\,n_{T}^{2})
in memory for
S
effective ODE steps, with no boundary truncation.
Dense Padé on the joint state of dimension
n_{T}(M+1)
costs
\Theta(n_{T}^{3}M^{3})
in time and
\Theta(n_{T}^{2}M^{2})
in memory, so the closure delivers an
M^{2}/S
time advantage and an
M
memory advantage that both grow with the output window.

2.2.3
Scalar drift

When every hidden state shares the same degradation rate
\mu
(
scalar-drift
condition, satisfied by the telegraph and G/R models above), the characteristic flow
\Phi_{t}
is scalar and the matrix multiplier retains a tractable lower-triangular coefficient structure.
When per-state rates differ the characteristic is itself matrix-valued; the matrix-affine-process framework of Cuchiero et al.
[
7
]
applies, which we treat as out of scope.

2.3
Beyond linear-rate: scope and weakly non-affine extension

Quadratic-rate generators such as pure pairwise annihilation
X+X\to\emptyset
produce a backward PDE not in the linear-rate class; the splitting of §
2.4
is the general fallback.
When the non-affine remainder
\mathcal{B}
has a small coupling constant
\varepsilon
, the closure supplies a systematic perturbation expansion whose level-
k
correction is one quadrature of the exactly-closed affine propagator against
\mathcal{B}\,p^{(k-1)}
(derivation and numerical verification in Appendix
D
); for moderate or large
\varepsilon
the splitting of the next section applies.

2.4
Operator splitting against a non-affine remainder

2.4.1
Decomposition

Many physically relevant generators decompose into a linear-rate piece and a non-affine remainder.
The Schlögl bistable model
[
33
]
in chemical kinetics (parameter set of
[
40
]
) has
A+2X\rightleftharpoons 3X
(autocatalytic bimolecular forward and trimolecular backward, both non-affine in
n
) plus
B\rightleftharpoons X
(immigration plus linear death, linear-rate).
The coagulation-branching model with reactions
\emptyset\to X
,
X\to\emptyset
,
X\to 2X
,
X+X\to X
has linear-rate birth-death-immigration plus quadratic pairwise coagulation.
The two-species Lotka-Volterra-style predator-prey model has per-species linear-rate dynamics plus bilinear predation
X+Y\to 2Y
.

In each case we decompose the generator as

\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{A}+\mathcal{B},

(18)

where
\mathcal{A}
is linear-rate (closed in-window by §
2.1
–§
2.2
) and
\mathcal{B}
is the non-affine remainder, integrated on the truncated state space
\{0,\ldots,N\}
by a conventional solver.
Strang’s symmetric composition gives a second-order approximation:

\tilde{p}(t+\Delta t)=e^{(\Delta t/2)\,\mathcal{A}}\,e^{\Delta t\,\mathcal{B}}\,e^{(\Delta t/2)\,\mathcal{A}}\,p(t).

(19)

The total error decomposes into the unprojected Strang commutator term (
C_{\mathrm{split}}T\Delta t^{2}
), the out-of-window tail from the non-affine half (
C_{\mathcal{B}}T\max_{k}\delta^{\mathcal{B},k}
), and the affine pre-projection residue (
C_{\mathcal{A}}T\max_{k}\delta^{\mathrm{pre},k}
, zero for pure-death halves); Appendix
J
records the accounting, and the empirical
\Delta t^{2}
and Richardson
\Delta t^{4}
slopes in Appendix
J
(Figure
8
) and §
3.2
–
3.3
do the evidential work
[
36
,
25
,
13
]
.

2.4.2
Richardson extrapolation

When the window-truncation terms are below the time-discretization error, the leading
\Delta t^{2}
commutator term cancels under Richardson extrapolation of two Strang runs at step counts
K_{s}
and
2K_{s}
:

P^{\mathrm{Rich}}_{K_{s}}(T)=\tfrac{4}{3}\,S_{\Delta t/2}^{2K_{s}}\bigl(p(0)\bigr)-\tfrac{1}{3}\,S_{\Delta t}^{K_{s}}\bigl(p(0)\bigr),

(20)

leaving the expected
\Delta t^{4}
temporal term at roughly
3\times
the wall-clock cost of a single
K_{s}
-step Strang.
Yoshida–Forest 4th-order composition
[
43
]
would have been the textbook alternative, but its three-substep formula uses a negative middle weight
w_{2}=-2^{1/3}/(2-2^{1/3})\approx-1.70
.
For a dissipative half
\mathcal{A}
– such as M/M/
\infty
death, where the propagator with
\Delta t<0
has
p=e^{-\mu\,w_{2}\Delta t}>1
and the binomial-thinning kernel is undefined – the negative substep is inadmissible.
The classical Sheng-Suzuki barrier
[
35
,
37
]
confirms there is no real symmetric splitting of order
\geq 3
with all-positive weights, so Richardson extrapolation is our preferred dissipative-safe lift.

2.4.3
Pure-degradation split for matrix-valued models

The split applies when the matrix-valued generator of §
2.2
has
off-diagonal
production
B
– meaning the count
m
is advanced by a hidden-state transition together with a birth, not by a diagonal
\mathrm{diag}(\boldsymbol{\nu})
in the same hidden state.
The classical two-state Peccoud-Ycart telegraph does not need this split: its
B
is diagonal, the scalar-drift case of §
2.2.3
applies, and the matrix closure of §
2.6.3
is already efficient.
The G/R chain has elongation transitions that couple a hidden-state jump to mRNA production, so
B
is strictly off-diagonal and the matrix closure pays the full
n_{T}
-state cost per step.
We use this split in the numerical experiment of §
3.4
.

Decompose

\mathcal{L}_{A}:\dot{\boldsymbol{P}}_{m}=\mu\bigl((m+1)\boldsymbol{P}_{m+1}-m\boldsymbol{P}_{m}\bigr),\qquad\mathcal{L}_{B}:\dot{\boldsymbol{P}}_{m}=\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{P}_{m}+\mathbf{B}\boldsymbol{P}_{m-1}.

The first half is pure mRNA degradation acting independently in each hidden state – per-state M/M/
\infty
death – advanced in closed form by binomial thinning of each hidden-state row of
P
, with no truncation.
The second half has no death term, so it is lower-triangular in
m
on the chosen output window and integrates by RK4 on the joint
n_{T}\times(M{+}1)
array without imposing a boundary at
m=M{+}1
.
Both halves close.
Strang composition of the two carries only
\Delta t^{2}
commutator error; Richardson extrapolation raises the order to
\Delta t^{4}
at the usual
3\times
cost.
The split is implemented as
purebd_strang_split_solve
and
purebd_strang_richardson_solve
in the reference module
telegraph_closure.jl
in the source repository; in §
3.4
we will refer to these methods as
PureBDStrang
and
PureBDRichardson
.

2.5
Steady-state limit

When stationarity is the target, the same linear-rate first-order PDE in
z
reduces at
\partial_{t}=0
to a first-order matrix ODE in
z
alone, integrable by a Frobenius seed at the regular-singular point
z=1
followed by an RK4 sweep and a contour-FFT extraction of the Taylor coefficients (PGF-FFT, Appendix
O
); this route is cap-free in principle and floating-point-bounded in practice by the
r^{-m}
Cauchy amplification.
For hybrid generators
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{A}+\mathcal{B}
, closure-Strang power iteration on the one-step map
S_{\Delta t}
provides a stationary solver for multi-dimensional product-state-space problems where direct factorization is infeasible.
Matrix-analytic baselines – block-Thomas back-substitution on the block-tridiagonal recurrence (QBD structure
[
28
,
23
]
), sparse LU, and dense SVD – are the standard comparison family; all solve the truncated linear system to machine precision but pay an absorbing-cap bias that grows with
t
.
Analytical stationary PGFs for telegraph-class models and the contour-inversion strategy are treated by Shahrezaei-Swain
[
34
]
, Gorin-Pachter
[
12
]
, and (for the transient case) Veerman et al.
[
39
]
.
The full PGF-FFT derivation, Frobenius recursion, block-Thomas formulation, and three solver-variant comparisons are in Appendix
O
; §
3.5
locates the crossovers empirically.

Discussion

When the generator is purely linear-rate the closure replaces
\Theta((N+1)^{3K})
dense-expm work with
\Theta(SK(N+1)^{2K})
; dense Padé suffices at
K\in\{1,2\}
but is infeasible at
K\geq 3
.
For genuinely non-affine generators, Strang splitting retains the closure advantage on the affine half while paying a
\Delta t^{2}
commutator error, lifted to
\Delta t^{4}
by Richardson extrapolation at
3\times
wall clock.
The advantage is in the count axis – exactness on the coefficient hierarchy – not in the time integrator: adaptive RK45 already extracts most of what the polynomial right-hand side offers (Appendix
E.1
), so any quality stiff RK code works as the inner integrator.

Splitting saves memory (
\Theta(K(N{+}1)^{K})
coefficient state versus
\Theta((N{+}1)^{2K})
for the full generator) and exploits sparsity: the matrix exponential of a sparse generator is generically dense, whereas the closure inner solve inherits the sparsity of
\mathcal{B}
.
Dense Padé is fastest at small system sizes; its cubic cost is overtaken by near-linear splitting as
N
,
(N{+}1)^{K}
, or
n_{T}M
grows.
The closure shifts the dominant error from the boundary feedback of direct truncation to the initial-condition tail, set independently via
M_{0}
.
At
V=500
,
N=5{,}000
closure-Strang is
3.3\times
faster than dense and
6.1\times
faster than Krylov (§
3.2
); at
K=8
,
N\geq 3
it is the only feasible method (§
3.3
); at
K\geq 6
it is the only solver reaching usable stationary accuracy (§
3.5
).

Beyond-scalar-drift matrix-valued models require the matrix-affine-process machinery of Cuchiero et al.
[
7
]
.
Complex-time splitting
[
3
]
reduces the commutator error past
\Delta t^{4}
at the cost of complex arithmetic.
Applications – maximum-likelihood inference from stationary snapshots, spatial or lattice extensions, multi-type spectral asymptotics – are follow-up work.

Appendix A
Proof of Proposition
5
(in-window exactness of the closure)

We restate the assertion: under the linear-rate condition with finite support, the truncated coefficient ODE on
\mathbb{R}^{2(N+1)}
for
\{\phi_{n},\kappa_{n}\}_{n\leq N}
has a unique maximal classical solution and coincides on
[0,T_{\star})
with the formal-power-series coefficients
[z^{n}]\Phi_{t}
and
[z^{n}]K_{t}
of the characteristic flow and multiplier, independently of any value assigned to coefficients above
N
.

The proof has three steps: a lower-triangular polynomial structure for the truncated system, an induction on the level
n
, and a read-off of independence from coefficients above
N
.

Step 1: lower-triangular polynomial structure.

The initial condition
\Phi_{0}(z)=z
gives
\phi_{0}(0)=0
,
\phi_{1}(0)=1
,
\phi_{k}(0)=0
for
k\geq 2
.
For any integer
r\geq 0
,

[z^{n}]\,\Phi_{t}(z)^{r+1}\;=\;\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k_{1}+\cdots+k_{r+1}=n\\
k_{i}\geq 0\end{subarray}}\phi_{k_{1}}(t)\,\cdots\,\phi_{k_{r+1}}(t),

(22)

a finite multinomial of degree
r+1
in
\{\phi_{0}(t),\ldots,\phi_{n}(t)\}
since every index
k_{i}
in a nonzero summand satisfies
0\leq k_{i}\leq n
.
For the death shift
r=-1
,
\Phi_{t}^{0}=1
, so
[z^{n}]\Phi_{t}^{0}=\delta_{n0}
and the same lower-triangular conclusion holds.
Linearity of
[z^{n}](\cdot)
together with the finite-support assumption gives

[z^{n}]\,A(\Phi_{t}(z))\;=\;\sum_{r=r_{\min}}^{r_{\max}}\alpha_{r}\,[z^{n}]\,\Phi_{t}(z)^{r+1},

a polynomial of degree at most
r_{\max}+1
in
\{\phi_{0}(t),\ldots,\phi_{n}(t)\}
.
For
\Psi_{t}(z):=B(\Phi_{t}(z))\,K_{t}(z)
,

[z^{n}]\,\Psi_{t}(z)\;=\;\sum_{j=0}^{n}[z^{n-j}]\,B(\Phi_{t}(z))\,\cdot\,\kappa_{j}(t),

where
[z^{n-j}]\,B(\Phi_{t})
is, by the same reasoning, a polynomial in
\{\phi_{0}(t),\ldots,\phi_{n-j}(t)\}
.
The level-
n
system therefore takes the form
(\dot{\phi}_{n},\dot{\kappa}_{n})=F_{n}(\phi_{0},\ldots,\phi_{n},\kappa_{0},\ldots,\kappa_{n})
with
F_{n}
a polynomial map of total degree at most
r_{\max}+1
, and depends on no level
k>n
.

Step 2: level induction.

Polynomial maps are
C^{\infty}
and therefore locally Lipschitz on compact subsets, so we induct on
n
.

For the base case
n=0
,
(\phi_{0},\kappa_{0})
satisfies a closed two-dimensional polynomial autonomous ODE with initial data
\phi_{0}(0)=0
,
\kappa_{0}(0)=1
; Picard-Lindelöf gives a unique solution on some
[0,T_{0})
with
T_{0}>0
, agreeing with the formal-series coefficients on
[0,T_{\star})
by uniqueness.

For the inductive step, suppose the assertion holds for levels
0,\ldots,n-1
.
Substituting the known levels into the level-
n
component reduces it to a non-autonomous polynomial ODE in
(\phi_{n},\kappa_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
alone, with
C^{\infty}
coefficients on
[0,T_{\star})
.
Picard-Lindelöf gives a unique local solution from
(\delta_{n,1},\delta_{n,0})
.
The formal-series coefficients
\bigl([z^{n}]\Phi_{t},[z^{n}]K_{t}\bigr)
satisfy the same ODE with the same initial data on
[0,T_{\star})
, hence agree with
(\phi_{n},\kappa_{n})
wherever both are defined, completing the induction.

Step 3: independence from
\{\phi_{k},\kappa_{k}\}_{k>N}
.

By Step 1, every
F_{n}
with
n\leq N
depends on no level above
n
, hence on no level above
N
.
The truncated system is therefore a closed dynamical system on
\mathbb{R}^{2(N+1)}
whose trajectories do not see the formal coefficients
\{\phi_{k},\kappa_{k}\}_{k>N}
, and the identification holds for any choice (or non-choice) of those coefficients.
Existence of the maximal classical solution on
[0,T_{N})
with
T_{N}\geq T_{\star}
follows from Step 2 and the standard extension theorem for finite-dimensional polynomial ODEs.
∎

Markov and signed cases

When
\alpha_{r},\beta_{r}\geq 0
for

[
19
]
.
For general finite-support offspring laws –

The single-ancestor PGF

\partial_{t}F=R(B(F)-F)
with offspring PGF
B

14
,
1

Appendix D
Perturbation expansion in the weakly non-affine limit

The closure framework supplies a systematic perturbation expansion when the non-affine remainder has a small coupling constant.
Write
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{A}+\varepsilon\,\mathcal{B}
with
\mathcal{A}
linear-rate, and expand

p_{n}(t)=p^{(0)}_{n}(t)+\varepsilon\,p^{(1)}_{n}(t)+\varepsilon^{2}\,p^{(2)}_{n}(t)+\cdots

(23)

Order-by-order matching in
\varepsilon

and
\dot{p}^{(k)}=\mathcal{A}\,p^{(k)}+\mathcal{B}\,p^{(k-1)}

At each order the equation is linear in

The affine propagator

is the closure of §
2.1
– exact in-window and computable at

The expansion is asymptotic in
\varepsilon
rather than convergent at fixed

\gamma\ll\lambda,\mu

at large

For moderate or strong non-affine couplings, the operator splitting of §
2.4
is the appropriate method; the two are complementary.

Appendix E
Taylor integration of the closure RHS

The linear-rate closure has polynomial right-hand side in the coefficient state, so every time derivative of the closure is itself a polynomial in the state, computable by the same Cauchy-product machinery used for the RHS.
The polynomial structure makes Parker-Sochacki / power-series integration
[
29
,
17
]
a natural fit: each successive differentiation of the closure yields a closed expression for
p^{(k+1)}_{n}
in terms of
\{p^{(j)}_{m}\}_{j\leq k,\,m\leq n}
, with the cross-order coupling supplied by Leibniz on the Cauchy product.

For binary BD, differentiating the Riccati closure once gives
\ddot{p}_{n}=-(\lambda+\mu)\dot{p}_{n}+2\lambda(p\star\dot{p})_{n}
, and the general Leibniz pattern is

p^{(k+1)}_{n}\;=\;-(\lambda+\mu)\,p^{(k)}_{n}+\lambda\sum_{j=0}^{k}\binom{k}{j}\,(p^{(j)}\star p^{(k-j)})_{n},

(25)

with
p^{(j)}\star p^{(k-j)}
the Cauchy product of the
j
-th and
(k-j)
-th derivative sequences, evaluated at the current state.
For general linear-rate generators the closure RHS is polynomial of the maximum offspring degree:
k
-ary fission gives a
k
-fold Cauchy term and the higher time derivatives expand via multinomial Leibniz into
k
-fold Cauchy products of derivative sequences.

The closure mechanism of §

\geq 0

We run a formally non-stochastic instance: M/M/
\infty
with
negative

\nu=-1
,
\mu=1
,

,

Appendix G
Method selection guide

Given a generator, identify the rate structure first, then pick the cheapest method that meets the accuracy target.
The items below are ordered by what to try first, not by generality.

1. Use the closed-form recursion when it exists.

If both the characteristic flow
\Phi_{t}
and the coefficients
\{p_{n}(t)\}
have stable formulas, do not integrate an ODE and do not build a truncated generator.

Example:
binary-fission BD, where the geometric tail gives
p_{n}(t)=p_{1}(t)\,\rho(t)^{n-1}
from a closed-form Riccati for
p_{0}(t)
.

Method:
evaluate the formula directly; cost
\Theta(N)
, no boundary cap, no solver tolerance.

2. Use stationary recursions only for stationary questions.

Some models have a clean recursion after setting
\partial_{t}=0
but not in the transient problem.

Example:
the factorial-moment recursion for the telegraph model becomes
\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{k}=(k\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{T}^{\top})^{-1}\mathrm{diag}(\boldsymbol{\nu})\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{k-1}
only at stationarity
[
2
]
; the transient recursion contains an
\dot{\mathbf{x}}_{n}
term and is not algebraic.

3. Use finite-window closure for linear-rate generators.

If rates have the form
\mathcal{L}_{n+r,n}=\alpha_{r}n+\beta_{r}
, use the closure.
Proposition
5
gives exact coefficients on the requested output window; increasing
N
is then about output resolution, not about moving an artificial boundary farther away.

Examples:
general finite-support offspring branching with
\geq 3
offspring, birth-death-immigration with arbitrary rates, M/M/
\infty
queues with state-independent service.

Cost:

\Theta(S\,N^{2})
for
S
effective right-hand-side evaluations plus
\Theta(N^{2}M_{0})
for the composition kernel.

4. Split only when the affine half is large enough to matter.

For
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{A}+\mathcal{B}
with linear-rate
\mathcal{A}
and non-affine
\mathcal{B}
, Strang splitting is useful when the exact
\mathcal{A}
half removes a real state-space cost.

Examples:
Schlögl bistable (
\mathcal{A}
= immigration plus linear death,
\mathcal{B}
=
2X\rightleftharpoons 3X
); coagulation-branching; two-species predator-prey; G/R elongation with off-diagonal
B
.

Method:
Strang composition with
\mathcal{A}
integrated exactly by the closure and
\mathcal{B}
by truncation; Richardson extrapolation lifts the temporal order from
\Delta t^{2}
to
\Delta t^{4}
at
3\times
cost; Kronecker factorization of
\mathcal{A}
for product state spaces.

Rule of thumb:
use it for multi-species, lattice, matrix-valued hidden-state, or other product-structured problems.

5. Use model-specific exact methods when they exist.

Example:
pure pairwise annihilation
X+X\to\emptyset
admits a closed moment hierarchy of Riccati type and conserved parity in
n
, and a small number of similar non-affine kinetics have model-specific exact solutions in the chemical-kinetics literature; the spatial operator of the backward PDE for quadratic-rate generators is not in Sturm-Liouville form, so this route applies model by model rather than as a general technique.

6. Use perturbation only for a real small parameter.

For
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{A}+\varepsilon\,\mathcal{B}
, perturbation (Appendix
D
) is useful only when
\varepsilon
is small on the scale of the affine rates and the requested accuracy is compatible with a finite asymptotic expansion.

Trade-off:
asymptotic in
\varepsilon
rather than convergent at finite-order-in-
\Delta t
; complementary to splitting.

7. Fall back to standard truncation when none of the above applies.

Method:

Appendix H
Birth-death stable geometric tail derivation

For binary birth-death branching, the extinction probability
p_{0}(t)
solves the scalar Riccati equation

\dot{p}_{0}(t)=-(\lambda+\mu)\left[p_{0}(t)-\frac{\mu}{\lambda+\mu}\right]+\lambda\,p_{0}(t)^{2},

(26)

with
p_{0}(0)=0
.
The two stationary points are
p_{0}^{*}=1
(extinction, attracting in the subcritical regime
\lambda<\mu
) and
p_{0}^{*}=\mu/\lambda
(supercritical).
Solving in closed form and substituting
\rho(t)=(\lambda/\mu)\,p_{0}(t)
and
p_{1}(t)=(1-p_{0}(t))(1-\rho(t))
into the upward recurrence

\dot{p}_{n+1}=\tfrac{1}{(n+1)\mu}\left[(\lambda+\mu)(n-1)\,p_{n}-\lambda(n-1)\,p_{n-1}+\mu\,\delta_{n0}+\lambda\sum_{k=0}^{n}p_{k}\,p_{n-k}\right]

yields the geometric tail
p_{n}(t)=p_{1}(t)\,\rho(t)^{n-1}
after resumming.
The raw upward recurrence is mathematically exact but numerically ill-conditioned in double precision because successive levels subtract nearly cancelling terms; the geometric form is the stable algebraic evaluation used in the experiments, optionally in log scale for very small tail probabilities.

Appendix I
Steady-state forward iteration instability

The natural-looking way to solve the block-tridiagonal stationary recurrence is to parameterize
\boldsymbol{P}_{m}=\mathbf{L}_{m}\,\boldsymbol{P}_{0}
and iterate forward in
m
, then fix
\boldsymbol{P}_{0}
via the mass-conservation constraint
\sum_{m}\mathbf{L}_{m}\boldsymbol{P}_{0}=\boldsymbol{\pi}
.
The forward iteration is unconditionally unstable, irrespective of how well-conditioned the generator is.
The mechanism is the same one that destabilizes forward iteration of scalar three-term recurrences on decaying solutions: solving for
\boldsymbol{P}_{m+1}
amplifies eigenmodes of
-\mathbf{A}/\mu
whose eigenvalues exceed unity, and accumulated roundoff in those modes overruns the exact solution well before any moderate
M
is reached.
Figure
9
(d) documents the catastrophic growth: the naive forward iterate’s
\ell^{1}
error against the reference reaches
\mathcal{O}(10^{2})
by
M\approx 200
, while the block-Thomas error sits at machine precision over the same range.
Block-Thomas eliminates the amplification by propagating boundary
relationships
(
\boldsymbol{P}_{m}=\mathbf{R}_{m}\boldsymbol{P}_{m+1}
) backward in
m
, rather than propagating values forward.

Appendix J
Strang error accounting

This appendix records the decomposition summarized in §
2.4
.
It is not a proof that the projected closure kernels form a finite-dimensional semigroup; in general they do not, because out-of-window mass may later return.
We compare the implemented finite-window method with the unprojected Strang split and then charge the projections separately.

Let
\mathcal{S}_{\Delta t}:=e^{(\Delta t/2)\mathcal{A}}\,e^{\Delta t\mathcal{B}}\,e^{(\Delta t/2)\mathcal{A}}
denote the unprojected Strang map on a weighted
\ell^{1}
space on which the three semigroups are stable over
[0,T]
.
Under the standard Strang hypotheses on this space – bounded trajectory, bounded relevant commutators, and sufficiently small
\Delta t
– the classical estimate gives

\|\Pi_{N}p(T)-\Pi_{N}\mathcal{S}_{\Delta t}^{K_{s}}p(0)\|_{1}\;\leq\;C_{\mathrm{split}}\,T\,\Delta t^{2}.

The implemented step replaces the unprojected middle flow by
\Pi_{N}e^{\Delta tB_{N}}
and applies the affine closure only to the projected in-window input.
The missing contribution from the middle flow is the mass that the unprojected
\mathcal{B}
flow transports above
N
before the next step; with finite-window stability constant
C_{\mathcal{B}}
, its accumulated contribution is bounded by
C_{\mathcal{B}}\,T\,\max_{k\leq K_{s}}\delta^{\mathcal{B},k}
.
The affine half-step is exact on the output window for the input actually supplied, by Proposition
5
; if the unprojected affine half-step sends non-negligible mass above the window before the next projection, the omitted tail can influence later dynamics, contributing
C_{\mathcal{A}}\,T\,\max_{k<K_{s}}\delta^{\mathrm{pre},k}
.
For pure-death affine halves, and more generally for affine halves that do not move mass above the selected window over the computed trajectory, this residue is zero.

Combining the unprojected Strang estimate with the two projection contributions yields the working estimate of §
2.4
.
In the numerical sections we verify the asymptotic
\Delta t^{2}
and Richardson
\Delta t^{4}
rates by step refinement and estimate the window terms by cap refinement or by comparison against independently converged high-cap references.

Appendix O
Block-tridiagonal stationary recurrence and solver variants

This appendix records the block-tridiagonal recurrence and the three numerical solvers compared in Appendix
K
, summarized in the main text in §
2.5
.

Setting
\dot{\boldsymbol{P}}_{m}=0
in the forward equation gives the block-tridiagonal three-term recurrence

\mu\,(m+1)\,\boldsymbol{P}_{m+1}-(\mu m\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{A})\,\boldsymbol{P}_{m}+\mathbf{B}\,\boldsymbol{P}_{m-1}=0

(27)

in
n_{T}\times n_{T}
blocks – a level-dependent QBD system
[
28
,
23
]
in which
m
is the level and the
n_{T}
hidden states are the phases.
The level-dependent diagonal block
-(\mu m\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{A})
prevents matrix-geometric closed-form factorization but leaves the block-tridiagonal structure intact.
Truncating at
m=M
with absorbing
\boldsymbol{P}_{M+1}=0
gives a capped joint generator
\mathcal{L}_{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{T}(M+1)\times n_{T}(M+1)}
that is generically full-rank, and we formulate the capped stationary problem as the augmented system
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{M}\,\mathbf{p}=\mathbf{e}_{n_{T}(M+1)}
, with the last row replaced by
\mathbf{1}^{\top}
to encode
\sum_{a}\sum_{m}\boldsymbol{P}_{a,m}=1
.
The three numerical solvers we compare – sparse LU, dense SVD on
\mathcal{L}_{M}
, and block-Thomas back-substitution – approximate the same augmented problem and agree up to the cap-leakage residual.

Block-Thomas propagates matrices
\mathbf{R}_{m}
satisfying
\boldsymbol{P}_{m}=\mathbf{R}_{m}\,\boldsymbol{P}_{m+1}
,

\mathbf{R}_{0}=-\mu\,\mathbf{A}^{-1},\qquad\mathbf{R}_{m}=-\mu(m+1)\,\bigl(\mathbf{B}\,\mathbf{R}_{m-1}+\mathbf{A}-\mu m\,\mathbf{I}\bigr)^{-1}\quad(1\leq m\leq M-1),

(28)

reducing the system to a single
n_{T}\times n_{T}
block equation
\mathbf{M}_{M}\,\boldsymbol{P}_{M}=0
with
\mathbf{M}_{M}=\mathbf{B}\,\mathbf{R}_{M-1}+\mathbf{A}-\mu M\,\mathbf{I}
;
\boldsymbol{P}_{M}
is recovered as the smallest right singular vector of
\mathbf{M}_{M}
, the rest by back-substitution, and the global vector is renormalized.
The total work is
\mathcal{O}(M\,n_{T}^{3})
, with no fill-in overhead.
Naive forward iteration is unconditionally unstable (Appendix
I
); block-Thomas propagates the stable relationships
\mathbf{R}_{m}
backward in
m
instead.
