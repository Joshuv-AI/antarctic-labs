Regulatory dynamics of molecular determinants in divergent rice varieties under the influence of nano-structured Zinc: an integrative study of OsbZIP genes and their targeting miRNAs
Authors: Shamshadul Haq, Ashutosh Pathak, Pratibha Dwivedi, S L Kothari, Rohit Jain, Sumita Kachhwaha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractZinc (Zn) is an indispensable micronutrient required in various physiological, biochemical, and molecular processes in plants. Zinc Oxide (ZnO), in its nano-structured form (nano-Zn) possesses unique physico-chemical properties that improve its absorption efficiency and exert a better impact on plants as compared to its bulk counterparts. The present study was conducted to investigate molecular response and cooperation between bZIP transcription factors (TFs) and microRNAs (miRs) in divergent rice varieties under nano-Zn imposition. Accordingly, spatio-structural annotations (gene-structures, Cis-elements, conserved motifs, evolutionary and syntenic nature) of OsbZIP TF genes were analyzed with their targeting Osa-miRs. Furthermore, the response of 30 OsbZIPs and 41 corresponding Osa-miRs were assessed between unlike (CSR-30 and PB-1) rice varieties under nano-Zn treatment. In CSR-30 rice, OsbZIP TFs generally showed reduced expression levels, with fold changes ranging from 0.95 to 0.44 (R² = 0.982). Correspondingly, their associated Osa-miRNAs exhibited increased expression, with changes ranging from 1x to 2.5x (R² = 0.947). In PB-1 rice, OsbZIP TFs generally exhibited reduced expression, with fold changes ranging from 0.85 to 0.47 (R² = 0.940). Similarly, their corresponding Osa-miRNAs also showed decreased expression, ranging from 1.05 to 0.30 fold change (R² = 0.940). Notably, distinct expressions were also identified for OsbZIP1, OsbZIP13, Osa-miR1846a, Osa-miR396e, Osa-miR11337a, and Osa-miR5801r, in CSR-30 rice. Additionaly, OsbZIP6, OsbZIP14, OsbZIP23, OsbZIP27, and OsbZIP29 exhibited distinct expression patterns, along with uneven responses of their corresponding Osa-miRNAs, suggesting multilayered combinatorial regulation in PB-1 rice. Therefore, the present study demonstrates the differential and coordinated responses of OsbZIP transcription factors gene family and Osa-miRNAs and also provides insights into key molecular determinants regulating rice growth under the influence of nano-Zn.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-09087-4.Keywords: Oryza sativa, Rice bZIP transcription factor, Rice microRNAs, Nano-Zn, Gene expression, Transcriptional and Post transcriptional regulation

Introduction

Rice (Oryza sativa L.) is an important staple crop consumed by half of the world’s population because it is an excellent source of energy, fibers, and nutrients [1]. Globally, rapidly changing environment and human population explosion have severely impacted agricultural productivity, and threatened food security by reducing crop production (https://openknowledge.fao.org). Rice cultivation is also facing significant challenges from abiotic factors including heavy metal toxicity which exerts major detrimental effect on rice productivity by limiting plant growth [2]. Moreover, abiotic factors affect morphological, biochemical and physiological regimen of the plant, which adversely impact the growth and development and ultimately leads to lesser crop yield [3]. Nowadays, management of agricultural soil is becoming a serious environmental concern due to its exposure to excessive chemical fertilizers, synthetic pesticides and increased anthropogenic activities, which have substantially increased soil contamination and heavy metal toxicity. Crops are vital for the food chain and alongwith the absorption of water and nutrients they also uptake toxic heavy metals from the soil which increases accumulation of heavy metals upto the toxic levels and adversely affects crop yield and quality, while simultaneously posing serious threats to food safety and human health [4, 5]. Following the entry into plant systems, heavy metals show high affinity for enzyme binding sites and alter their confirmation and functional regulation. Consequently, heavy metals disrupt cellular homeostasis, intracellular compartmentalization, biochemical interaction, oxidative balance, membrane integrity, molecular cross linking and genetic modification [4, 6, 7]. These damages severely affect plant physiological, biochemical and molecular functions, thereby inhibiting plant growth and development and ultimately compromising crop productivity and sustainability [5].

Recently, advancements in nanotechnology have revolutionized the agricultural systems by checking the excessive use of chemicals fertilizers and enabling smart delivery of nutrients at the nanoscale. These interventions have significantly improved soil quality and plant health, leading to enhanced crop yields while minimizing environmental risks and promoting human health [8]. Nano materials possess unique physico-chemical characteristics including a precise nanoscale (1–100 nm), high surface-to-volume ratios, elevated surface energy and reactivity, superior mechanical and physical strength, and quantum confinement effects. These distinctive properties render nanoparticles (NPs) functionally superior to their bulk counterparts [9, 10]. In the present study, ZnO was used in its nano-structured form (nano-Zn) because of its unique physiochemical properties and higher absorption efficiency [11]. Zn is and essential trace element required in optimal concentration for normal plant growth and development and is also contributes to improve tolerances against other heavy metals [3]. However, excessive accumulation of Zn can be toxic to plant and trigger stress responses, including growth inhibition, nutrient imbalance and oxidative damage associated with reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation [12, 13]. More importantly, Zn plays crucial role in membrane stability and cellular integrity, osmolytes accumulation, antioxidant activities, maintenance of plant water relationship, nutrient uptake, and enhancement of photosynthetic activity. Further, Zn is ubiquitous and required for various biochemical processes in plants such as carbohydrate, lipid, protein and nucleic acid metabolisms. It also participates in a vast variety of enzymatic reactions, protein regulation, transcriptional regulation, gene expression and cell signaling [14, 15], . Collectively, these physiological and biochemical functions have a significant impact on overall plant vigor, leading to promotion of growth and development, crop productivity and yield [16–18]. Consequently, the use of nano-Zn has been reported in various plant and crop systems for improving growth and productivity through enhanced seed germination, better shoot and root lengths and maintenance of redox homeostasis and boosting stress-responsive gene expression [19–26].

Zn has also been identified as improving antioxidant responses and osmoprotection, thereby enhancing tolerance to abiotic stress in different crops [26, 27]. Moreover, Zn acts as a molecular regulator by modifying the expression of stress-responsive genes and transcription of antioxidant enzymes in hormone-related signaling pathways, including abscisic acid (ABA), cytokinins, and auxins [28–30]. The transcriptomic evidences have also revealed that nano-Zn enables smart nutrient delivery and functions as molecular modulator under transcriptional reprogramming by activating responsive gene expression against different types of abiotic stresses [31–33].

In this context, the present investigation was carried out to evaluate the response and coordination between basic leucine zipper (bZIP) TF and their targeting microRNAs (miRNAs) during growth and development in divergent rice varieties under nano-Zn application. Both TFs and miRNAs are very important molecular modulators which not only are involved in regulation of the target gene but determine the fate of gene action also by switching on/off under various biotic and abiotic stress conditions, as well as evolutionary and conservatory processes [34–38]. Specifically, bZIP TF constitutes a widely distributed protein family and are remarkably conserved in their domain structure among eukaryotes, including plants, animals, human beings and microorganisms [39, 40]. Role of bZIP TFs in various biological processes including cell elongation, organ and tissue differentiation, photo-morphogenesis, somatic embryogenesis, seed maturation and germination, flower development, vascular development is well documented [41–43]. In addition to this, TFs in coordination with miRNAs act as fine tuners of the transcriptional regulatory machinery by modulating gene expression [44]. It has already been reported that most of miRNA targets are TFs [45], however, effect of Zn on the interaction and coordination between miRNAs and bZIP TF in rice are limited [46, 47]. Moreover, reports on regulatory cooperation between TFs and miRNAs are available in various other plant species, e.g., miR164 targets NAC TF family under salt stress to regulate lateral root initiation in Arabidopsis [48], coordination between MYB TFs and miR159d during leaf senescence in maize inbred line [49], and miR156 targets SPL (SQUAMOSA Promoter Binding-Like) TFs in rice, tomato, and maize during floral development [50]. The opposite pattern of coordination between WRKY6 (TF) and miR396 was also reported under high temperature or salicylic acid in sunflower [51]. Hence, the present study provides spatio-structural annotation of OsbZIP TF genes and their corresponding Osa-miRs followed by assessment of their response and cooperation under nano-Zn treatment during early vegetative growth and development in divergent rice (CSR-30 and PB-1) varieties.

Materials and methods

Molecular determinants characterization

Genomic survey, characterization and physiochemical analysis

The seed sequence of bZIP protein domain (PF0170.24) from the Pfam database (http://pfam.xfam.org) was used as reference sequence to identify potential bZIP protein sequences in the Rice Genome Annotation Project database (RGAP) using BLASTP analysis with 1e− 5 (threshold value) [52]. The bZIP domain in the retrieved protein sequences was further reconfirmed using the SMART (https://smart.embl.de/) and HMMER platforms (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/Tools/hmmer/home) [43]. Subsequently, 30 OsbZIP protein sequences were selected based on best cut-off values, including overall E-value and score for the chosen protein sequences, followed by assessment of domain integrity using conditional E-value, independent E-value, domain bit score, and most accurate functional annotation. These selected 30 OsbZIP were designated as OsbZIP1 to OsbZIP30 based on their chromosomal coordinate [53]. Afterward, several physiochemical parameters such as chromosome position, CDS length, protein length, molecular weight (Mw), Isoelectric point (PI) and grand average of hydropathy (GRAVY) were analyzed for the selected OsbZIPs using the ExPASy platform [54].

Homology modeling with structural measures, protein-protein Interaction, and sub-cellular Localization

Protein Homology Modeling was performed for the 30 OsbZIP sequences using SWISS-MODEL (https://www.swissmodel.expasy.org) and best GQME score was used to select the protein model. The PDB file format of proteins models was used for structural assessment using ERRAT score analysis and Ramachandran plot at UCLA platform (https://saves.mbi.ucla.edu/). In addition, the Z-score was determined using PROSA web tool (https://prosa.services.came.sbg.ac.at/prosa.php). The protein-protein interaction (PPI) was carried out using the STRING database (https://string-db.org/) and sub cellular localization of proteins was predicted using the CELLO v.2.5 (http://cello.life.nctu.edu.tw/).

Phylogenetic and Synteny of OsbZIPs

The same bZIP domain was further used as query to search in Arabidopsis thaliana (L.) Heynh.employing the TAIR database (https://www.arabidopsis.org/), Zea mays L. using MaizeGDB database (https://www.maizegdb.org/) and Solanum lycopersicum L. and Sorghum bicolar (L.) Moench using Phytozome v.12 platform (https://phytozome-next.jgi.doe.gov/) through BLASTP analysis with a threshold value of 1e− 5 [52]. The phylogenetic study included model organisms such as rice and Arabidopsis as well as other commonly studied species representing monocot and dicot plant clades [53–55]. Therefore, a set of 72 bZIP proteins from rice, Arabidopsis thaliana, Zea mays, Solanum lycopersicum and Sorghum bicolor were used for phylogenetic analysis using MEGA 7.0 software (https://www.megasoftware.net/). The sequences alignment was performed using built-in ClustalW program with default parameters and the phylogenetic tree was constructed based on the maximum likelihood method with 1000 iterations of bootstrap value using MEGA 7.0 software [56]. Furthermore, collinearity gene analysis was performed among selected monocot and dicot species to map the inter-syntenic relationships between rice and other model and non-model plant species (A. thaliana, S. lycopersicum, S. bicolor and Z. mays). The chromosome FASTA file and GFF3 files were used to construct a dual syntenic map using TBtools [57].

Chromosomal location and gene duplication

The distribution of selected OsbZIP gene paralogs was mapped on the rice chromosomes according to their coordinate information using the MG2C v.2.1 web tool (http://mg2c.iask.in/mg2c_v2.1/). Intra-syntenic analysis among OsbZIPs was performed to identify gene duplication events through Plant Genome Duplication Database (PGDD) (https://chibba.agtec.uga.edu/). The identified duplicated gene pairs were subsequently visualized using a Circos plot function in TBtools Software [57]. Further, the nature of duplication events was calculated by Ka (nonsynonymous substitution), Ks (synonymous) and Ka/Ks ratio for identifying nature of selection pressure of duplicated gene pairs during evolution using TBtools [56, 57].

Conserved protein motifs, Gene structure and Cis-acting elements analysis

The conserved protein motifs were identified using the Multiple Expectation Maximization for Motif Elicitation (MEME) program under Classic mode analysis, with the number of motifs limited to 15 (Additional file 1) and all other parameters set to default values (https://meme-suite.org/). The organization of the gene structure was explored using TBtools [57]. The PlantCARE database was used to identify the Cis-acting regulatory elements in OsbZIP gene promoter region (https://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/plantcare). A 2000 bp upstream region from the transcription start site was used for Cis-acting regulatory elements screening [43, 53] and their visualization was performed by TBtools [57].

Tandem-repetition distribution and Predicted microRNAs Analysis

The distribution of tandem repeats or Simple Sequence Repeats (SSRs) was analyzed in both CDS and genomic sequences of OsbZIP TF genes by microsatellite identification tool (MISA) platform with default parameters at IPK Leibniz Institute, Gatersleben, Germany (https://www.ipk-gatersleben.de/en/). In addition, the prediction of targeting miRNAs against the selected OsbZIP TF gene was performed using psRNATarget (https://www.zhaolab.org/psRNATarget/home). The mature miRNA reference data of rice was obtained from PmiREN2.0 data base (Plant miRNA ENcyclopedia) (https://www.pmiren.com/). All the miRNAs targeting the OsbZIP genes were mapped using Cytoscape software (https://cytoscape.org).

Experiment design and Implementation

Nanomaterial, plant material and treatment

Zinc oxide nanomaterials (nano-Zn) (Type 1; APS ~ 30 nm) with 99% purity were procured from SRL, India (Sisco Research Laboratories Pvt. Ltd.). The physical and functional characterization of nano-Zn was done by Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) (Nova Nano FE-SEM 450) and Fourier-Transform Infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy (Shimadzu IR Affinity-1) (Fig. 1). FTIR analysis of nano-Zn showed several band peaks in the range of 400–600 cm− 1 with a characteristic peak at 490 cm⁻¹ corresponding to Zn-O stretching vibrations. Additional spectral peaks at 3378 cm⁻¹, 2915 cm⁻¹, and 1560 cm⁻¹ represented a stretching of –OH, C–H, and C = C group vibrations, respectively. While SEM assay of nano-Zn was identified by their electron micrograph showing spherical along with hexagonal shapes of crystals and their distribution reflected less agglomeration (Fig. 1). Furthermore, the seeds of CSR-30 (Alkalinity tolerant) and PB-1 (Alkalinity sensitive) rice varieties were procured from Indian Agricultural Research Institute (ICAR-IARI), New Delhi. The dehusked rice seeds were washed with distilled water and then surface sterilization was carried out using 4% sodium hypochlorite (NaClO) for 15 min, followed by several rinses with distilled water to remove residual NaClO. The nano-Zn formulation was prepared in deionized water, and a homogeneous dispersed solution was obtained using ultrasonic system (Labman, India) with an operating frequency of 40 ± 3 kHz. Different concentrations of nano-Zn (50 µg mL-1, 100 µg mL-1, and 200 µg mL-1) were prepared and used for the experiments [20, 58, 59]. The seeds were hydroprimed with each concentration of nano-Zn for 36 h prior to incubation for 24 h on an orbital shaker (Orbitek, Scigenics, India). Thereafter, the seeds were grown in water-agar medium in growth chamber 16/8 h (light/dark) photoperiod, 26±1 °C temperature and irradiance level of 300 µmol m−2s− 1 provided by white LED light (Philips, India) and 60% to 70% relative humidity [60].

Total RNA and miRNA isolations, cDNA Synthesis, primer designing, and qPCR analysis

Twelve-day-old seedlings were used for total RNA and miRNAs extraction from contrasting rice varieties using NucleoSpin miRNA kit (Takara Bio, India). The RNAs purity ratios (A260/A280 and A230/A260) ranged from 1.95 to 2.2 and 2.1 to 2.3 respectively, as measured using Nanodrop spectrophotometer (QualityOne, Thermofisher), and the samples were subsequently used for cDNA synthesis. The extracted total RNAs and miRNAs were used for first-strand cDNA synthesis by PrimeScript 1st strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Takara Bio, India) and Mir-X™ miRNA First-Strand Synthesis Kit (Takara Bio, India) respectively. Primer3 (https://primer3.org/), and BatchPrimer3 (https://wheat.pw.usda.gov/demos/BatchPrimer3/) were used for primer designing and complete primer details are provided in Additional file 2. Initially, the primer efficacy was evaluated through sequence length (18–23 bp), GC content (54–60%), melting temperature (Tm; 59–65 °C), GC clamp presence and the absence of self-annealing and hairpin formation using Primer Stats Suite [61]. Primer specificity was further confirmed by optimizing the annealing temperatures (55 - 62 °C) through gradient thermal cycler (C1000, BioRad, India), and only primers showing a single band were selected on agarose gel electrophoresis. Additionally, mature Osa-miRs sequences were used as forward primers, while mRQ3 was used as the universal reverse primer (Takara Bio, India), and their details are provided in Additional file 3. The OsActin and U6 primers were used as internal controls to normalize the expression of OsbZIPs and Osa-miRs respectively. The consistency of the housekeeping gene (OsActin and U6) was also verified in the rice cDNAs by conventional PCR amplification followed using agarose gel electrophoresis. Subsequently, real-time quantitative PCR was performed on Qiagen rotor gene-q 5-plex system with QuantiNova SYBR Green dye (Qiagen). For OsbZIP genes, the thermal cycler conditions were set as follows: an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 45 cycles of 95 °C for 20 s, 58 to 62 °C for 25 s and finally at 72 °C for 45 s. For Osa-miRs, the thermal cycling conditions were: 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 45 cycles of 95 °C for 5 s, 60 °C for 20 s and 72 °C for 20 s. The three biological replicates and three technical replicates were used in the experiment and expression data were calculated according to 2−(ΔΔCT) method for relative expression analysis [62].

Statistical analysis

The experiment was performed repeatedly, and one-way ANOVA was conducted to compare differences among variables at a significant value α = 0.05 with p < 0.05 considered as statistically significant. Regression analysis was performed to examine relationships among variables and regression equation with R2 (coefficient of determinant or goodness of fit) was obtained using Microsoft Excel (https://www.microsoft.com/en/microsoft-365/excel). After the significant differences obtained among treatment groups by ANOVA, the pairwise comparisons between each group were evaluated by Dunn’s test (post hoc analysis) with Bonferroni adjustment, while P value < 0.05 was considered as statistically significant using PAST software [63]. The χ2 (Kruskal-Wallis) statistics was used to confirm statistically significant difference in expression score between the different treatments. Also, the multivariate analysis was carried out through principal component analysis (PCA) using web based SRPLOT platform (https://www.bioinformatics.com.cn/en).

Results

Identification, characterization and physiochemical analysis

A set 30 OsbZIP TF gene paralogs was used in this study based on the integrity of the bZIP domain integrity, which varied from 48 to 96 amino acids in length with an average of 63 amino acid long domain. Physico-chemical properties including CDS, protein length, protein Mw, PI, and GRAVY index were analyzed for all OsbZIP genes family as shown in Table 1. The CDS length varied from 438 bp (OsbZIP32 and OsbZIP33) to 5295 bp (OsbZIP29) with an average of 967.76 bp. Correspondingly, protein length ranged from 145 (OsbZIP32 and OsbZIP33) to 1764 amino acids (OsbZIP29) with an average of 321.59 amino acids. The molecular weight of proteins fluctuated from 15.49 kDa (OsbZIP24) to 198.10 kDa (OsbZIP29) with an average of 34.67 kDa, while the predicted PI value ranged from 5.05 (OsbZIP6) to 10.25 (OsbZIP28) with an average of 7.59 PI. All identified OsbZIP TF gene family exhibited negative GRAVY score, ranging from − 1.052 (OsbZIP11) to -0.285 (OsbZIP10), indicating a varied level of hydrophilic nature of OsbZIP TF gene family.

Homology modeling and structural assessment

Protein homology modeling for the 30 OsbZIP genes was performed using SWISS PORT database (Fig. 2a). The protein models with best GMQE score were further evaluated for quality assessment using ERRAT, Z-Score, and Ramachandran plot analysis. In the present study, the average GMQE score was 0.65, ranging from 0.46 (OsbZIP27) to 0.81 (OsbZIP29), while the mean ERRAT score was 91.20, ranging from 81.20 (OsbZIP1) to 100 (OsbZIP30). The Z-score ranged from − 12.32 (OsbZIP27) to -1.52 (OsbZIP21), with an average of -4.41. Furthermore, the two-dimensional (2D) spatial arrangement of amino acids in OsbZIP proteins, was examined using φ and ψ dihedral (torsion) angles. The percentage of residues in the favored regions of the Ramachandran plot varied from 64.24% (OsbZIP17) to 97.2% (OsbZIP29), with an average of 75.32% (Table 2).

Structural assessment of OsbZIP TF proteins gene family

Protein-Protein Interaction (PPI) and sub-cellular Localization

The predicted PPI network was analyzed to obtain functional annotation of OsbZIP proteins. The network analysis displayed 34 nodes and 70 interaction edges, with a PPI enrichment p-value of 3.19e− 13 and a mean local clustering coefficient was 0.612 (Fig. 2b). STRING analysis successfully mapped 9 OsbZIP proteins to TGA2.2, ABI5, LG2, RISBZ3/4, TGAL9, bZIP19, and bZIP23. Furthermore, these mapped proteins also showed interactions with the proteins involved in various metabolic pathways and were associated with important cellular processes such as cell growth, differentiation and apoptosis (Additional file 4). Significantly, most of the OsbZIP TF protein members displayed their localization into nucleus but OsbZIP3, OsbZIP9, and OsbZIP14 showed their presence among peroxisome, cytoplasm, and chloroplast respectively beside nucleus. Interestingly, OsbZIP27 protein was found to be unique, as it showed predicted localization in multiple organelles other than the nucleus (Table 2).

Phylogenetic and syntenic analysis

The evolutionary and conservatory nature of the 30 OsbZIP TF proteins (Oryza sativa) was analyzed in comparison with other model and non-model plant species (Arabidopsis thaliana, Zea mays, Solanum lycopersicun and Sorghum bicolor). A total of 72 bZIP protein sequences, including 30 of OsbZIPs, 10 of AtbZIPs, 11 of SlbZIPs, 8 of SbbZIPs, and 13 of ZmbZIPs, were employed to generate a phylogenetic dendrogram. Analysis of Cis-acting elements and tandem repetitions (Simple Sequence Repeats)

The conditional and diverse functions of genes are closely associated with the involvement of Cis-acting regulatory elements (CREs) in their promoter regions, which play a crucial role in regulating gene actions under different conditions. Therefore, the distribution of CREs was analyzed up to 2000 bp upstream from the start codon in the OsbZIP genes sequence. A total of 35 different types of CREs were identified in selected OsbZIP gene family and classified into eight distinct functional categories, namely development-related elements (DREs), defense and stress related elements (DSREs), hormone responsive elements (HREs), light responsive elements (LREs), promoter related element, site-binding related elements, unknown functions and others (Fig. 7). Among these categories, HREs was the most abundant, comprising 10 different motifs, amongst TGACG, CGTCA, ABRE (Abscisic acid responsive elements), GARE (Gibberellin-responsive elements), and P-box motifs showed highest incidence. LREs were also highly represented, with motifs such as TCCC, GATA, G-Box, GT1, Box 4, ATCT, and I-box occurring frequently in the promoter regions. The DSREs category also comprised six different motifs elements, among which ARE (Auxin responsive elements), CCAAT-box, and LTRE (Low-temperature responsive elements) motifs were more frequently observed. Likewise, the DRE category included eight types of motif elements, of which GC, CAT-box and O2-site motifs were more prominently represented (Additional file 6). Therefore, the presence and diversification of one or more CREs are likely associated with the conditional functions of the OsbZIP gene family under various environmental conditions.

Discussion

The present study provides an in-depth molecular perspective on the responses of transcription factors (TFs) and their corresponding microRNAs in divergent rice varieties under ZnO nanoparticles (nano-Zn) exposure. TFs are key regulatory nodes in molecular response networks, playing crucial roles in controlling the expression of gene or genes networks and determining the fate of genes action during various biological processes under abiotic and biotic stress conditions, and across evolutionary and conserved functions [36, 38, 64]. It is also well established that most of miRNA targets are TFs [45] and both TFs and miRNAs serve as important molecular determinants in gene regulatory systems by ensuring proper gene expression at the right time and in the right cellular context for normal physiological and developmental functions [34]. Several studies have highlighted the coordinated regulation between TFs and miRNAs in plant growth and development. For example, the combinatorial regulation involving MYB TFs with Osa-miR159, SPL TF with Osa-miR156 and WRKY TFs with Osa-miR396, has been reported during senescence, floral development and metal toxicity responses in rice, respectively [49, 50, 65]. In addition, participation of OsPCF6 and OsTCP21 TFs with miR319 hve been reported to positively regulate cold tolerance [66] [67]. However, limited information is available on TFs and miRNA-mediated regulation in rice plant under nanoparticle application. For example, selenium nanoparticles have been reported to mitigate drought stress through the coordinated action of Osa-miR172, bZIP TFs, and CRTISO genes, thereby enhancing antioxidant responses, and stimulating secondary metabolism in tomato plants [68]. Similarly, in other plant systems, foliar application of ZnO nanoparticles was reported to regulate seed grain development, carbohydrates remobilization and nutrient transport related genes through the coordinated action of microRNA 171 (miR171), miR156, NAM, and SUT genes in wheat [69]. In this context, the present study has initially characterized OsbZIP TF genes and their corresponding Osa-miRs through detailed spatio-structural analysis. Subsequently, 30 OsbZIP TF genes and their 41 corresponding Osa-miRs were experimentally validated through RT-qPCR analysis in response to nano-Zn treatments in divergent rice varieties (CSR-30 and PB-1).

Structural and functional characterization of molecular determinants

In the present study, 30 OsbZIP TF gene paralogs were characterized, revealing variation in CDS length, amino acid length, Mw, PI and GRAVY values. These observations are consistent with previous reports on bZIPs TF family in various species, including rice [40, 56, 70, 71]. Such divergence in physiochemical properties of the OsbZIPs TF family represents an important molecular signature, reflecting functional diversification among gene family members. The PI values of several OsbZIP proteins were > 7 indicating its alkaline nature and localization as integral membrane protein [72]. In contrast, the negative or relatively low GRAVY values observed for the selected OsbZIPs indicating their hydrophilic nature [73] suggesting a higher likelihood of globular protein characteristics [74]. Furthermore, the structural quality of the predicted OsbZIP protein models was supported by ERRAT scores ranging between 81.20 and 100%, indicating good quality of crystallographic nature for protein model [75] while Z-score ranged from − 12.32 to − 1.52, further supporting acceptable structural quality of protein model [76]. ERRAT and z-score are very important indices for protein model validation as ERRAT evaluates the spatial distribution of amino acids and non-bonded atomic interactions based on comparative statistics [77], while z-score reflects three-dimensional fold recognition and energy distribution within protein structures [78].

The physical and functional associations of the 30 OsbZIPs proteins were explored through PPI analysis in the present study. The PPI network linked OsbZIP proteins with functionally important partners such as LG2, RISBZ3/4, ABI5, TGA2.2, TGAL9, bZIP12, bZIP19, and bZIP23. LG2 is commonly recognized as transcriptional regulator involved in defense-related activity [79], whereas TGA2.2 has vital significance in plant immunity and defense responses [80]. Similarly, TGAL9 (also referred to as bZIP17) is known to participate in defense-related functions in rice [81]. In addition, ABI5 and bZIP12 are key regulators of ABA signaling under abiotic stress conditions in rice [82] The homologs RISBZ 3/4/5 possesses specific Cis-acting DNA binding selectivity toward the 5’-TGAGTCA-3’ sequence and are involved in regulating seed development, seed storage protein metabolism and abiotic responses such as cold and drought in rice [83–85]. Furthermore, bZIP19 and bZIP23 have been implicated in zinc assimilation in rice [86]. Thus, PPI analysis provides valuable insight into the physical and functional roles of OsbZIP proteins and helps to predict their potential involvement in diverse biological processes.

The evolutionary trajectory of selected OsbZIP proteins was evaluated through phylogenetic analysis with AtbZIP, SlbZIP, ZmbZIP and SbbZIP proteins. The resulting dendrogram revealed seven major groups along with some unique paralogs and depicted an uneven distribution of OsbZIP paralogs based on AtbZIP reference genes. Such clustering patterns are important for classifying bZIP homologs and for understanding the expansion, conservation and evolutionary dynamics of the gene family. The dendrogram also illustrated phylogenetic relatedness between OsbZIP sequences and bZIP sequences from other plant species; however, some clusters were composed exclusively of OsbZIP paralogs, suggesting OsbZIP genes may have undergone distinct ecological and evolutionary divergence. These findings are in agreement with earlier studies on bZIP gene family in different plant species [56, 71, 87, 88]. A few OsbZIPs paralogs appeared isolated in the dendrogram and were considered unique, which may reflect the loss and gain of specific sequence regions in OsbZIP gene and could potentially be associated with distinct responses to environmental conditions. In addition, four inter-genomic syntenic maps were constructed between OsbZIP genes and two monocot species (S.bicolor and Z. mays) as well as two dicot species (A. thaliana, and S. lycopersicum). The highest number of collinear genes pairs (48) were identified between O. sativa and Z. mays, followed by 41 pairs between O. sativa and S. bicolor, 8 pairs between O. sativa and S. lycopersicum, and only 2 collinear pairs between O. sativa and A. thaliana. These syntenic relationships indicate the degree of conservation of OsbZIP genes within and across phylogenetic clades and are in accordance with previous reports on collinearity of bZIP genes among model and non-model plant species [54, 56, 87, 89]. Collinearity analysis is highly informative for understanding gene organization and evolutionary dynamics [90] and their comparative genomic organization is particularly valuable for tracing evolutionary trajectories [91]. It also plays a crucial role in identifying gene content variation, phenotypic divergence, and evolutionary relationships among homologous genes that have undergone genome duplication events [92].

Further, the evolutionary trajectory of OsbZIP genes was also examined in the present study. Distinct clustering patterns were identified among the selected OsbZIP TF gene family members, which can be attributed to variations in gene structure organization and conserved motif composition, providing valuable insight into gene evolution and functional diversification within the family [93]. The uneven distribution of OsbZIP gene paralogs across different groups reflects variation in exon (CDS) and intron structural arrangements, serves as an important indicator of evolutionary relationships and divergence within the gene family [94]. In particular, intron patterns play a crucial role in shaping evolutionary trajectories and are also involved in complex regulation of gene function through splicing-dependent and splicing-independent mechanisms, as well as intron-mediated enhancement of mRNA accumulation [95]. Therefore, structural diversification of exon-intron patterns provides important clues for understanding environmental and ecological influences on gene functions. Additionally, the distribution of conserved protein motifs was analyzed in relation to the phylogenetic classification of OsbZIP genes. The number of motifs ranged from 2 to 6 per OsbZIP protein, with motif 1 being uniformly conserved, corresponding to core bZIP domain. Moreover, motif conservation within specific phylogenetic subgroups suggests relatedness and potential involvement in similar physiological processes [87]. Overall, motif distribution patterns offer valuable insight into the functional diversification of the OsbZIP gene family in response to ecological and environmental factors [40, 96].

In the present study, the distribution of the 30 OsbZIP gene paralogs was found to be uneven, with localization near midpoint and terminal regions of chromosome, and their position varied across rice Chrs, reflecting distinct linkage groups. These observations are consistent with previous studies reporting on chromosomal distribution of bZIP genes and their association with different linkage groups among various plant species [55, 71, 89]. The presence of multiple gene copies or paralogs indicates gene duplication events, which provides valuable insights into evolutionary processes, adaptability to new conditions, and responses to changing environmental or selection pressures [97]. Gene duplication also contributes to functional divergence and the generation of novel gene functions through sub-functionalization and neo-functionalization, thereby increasing biological complexity and diversity that contribute to development of advanced molecular systems for environmental adaptation [37]. Accordingly, 11 gene duplication pairs were identified within the 30 OsbZIP genes in the present study, including 10 segmental duplication events and 1 tandem duplication event. Gene duplication plays a crucial role in the acquisition of new functions required to meet cellular demands under changing environment conditions during plant growth and development [98, 99]. Both tandem and segmental duplication events contribute significantly to gene family expansion and functional modification during evolution [100], thereby promoting gene functional diversity [101]. In addition, the Ka/Ks ratio for duplicated OsbZIP gene pairs ranged from 0.18 to 0.48, with an average of 0.26. Since all Ka/Ks values were less than 1, these results suggested that the OsbZIP gene family has predominantly undergone negative or purifying selection. In general, Ka/Ks ratio < 1, =1, and > 1 indicate purifying selection, neutral selection and positive selection, respectively [102]. Therefore, the results of the present work are consistent with previous studies reporting that the bZIP gene family has largely evolved under purifying selection across various plant species [56, 71].

In the present study, a total of 35 different classes of Cis-elements were identified, and their distribution was classified into eight functional regulatory categories. The Cis-acting regularity elements located in the gene promoter region play a crucial role in gene function by specifically interacting with the transcriptional machinery, and their coordinated action helps to shape gene expression under external, developmental, and intrinsic regulatory conditions [103]. The present analysis revealed diverse classes of Cis-elements in the promoter regions of the OsbZIP gene family, highlighting their functional versatility. This diversity is likely associated with the presence of distinct Cis-elements involved in the growth and development, as well as responses to biotic and abiotic stresses. Previous studies have also shown that Cis-elements participate in the regulation of various biological functions, hormonal actions, stress and developmental processes [104, 105]. For instance, Cis-acting elements like TCA-element, TGACG-motif, CGTCA-motif, GARE-motif, P-box and ABRE are play crucial roles in regulating gene expressions under abiotic stresses and hormonal responsive conditions in rice and other plant species [82, 106–108]. In addition, tandem repeats (SSRs) were analyzed in both the genomic and CDS region of OsbZIP gene family in the present study. The genomic sequences portrayed a higher SSR frequency (2.73%) than CDS sequences (0.73%). SSR motifs such as CCT/AGG, CCG/GGC, GGA/TTC, and GAA/TTC were found to be highly distributed among OsbZIP TF gene family, and these motifs may contribute to the formation of unusual DNA folding structures, such as hairpin form, bipartite triplex form, and simple loop conformations, which can significantly influence gene expression and regulatory mechanisms [109]. Moreover, an increased repetition of CCG/CGG motif was observed more in the CDS regions compared to the genomic sequences, indicating a relatively higher GC-content. This may reflect greater gene density and their compactness, earlier replication timing, altered rates of recombination, and may also influence the physical and physiological properties of DNA, as well as its conservation and evolutionary dynamics [110]. Likewise, an increased repetition of CCG/CGG motif was observed more in the CDS regions compared to the genomic sequences, indicating a relatively higher GC-content. This may reflect greater gene density and their compactness, earlier replication timing, altered rates of recombination, and may also influence the physical and physiological properties of DNA, as well as its conservation and evolutionary dynamics [109].

In the present analysis, a total of 167 Osa-miRs corresponding sites were identified and their distribution was uncommon under 60 different classes against OsbZIP TF gene family. The coordination between TFs and miRNAs is crucial for gene regulation, as miRNAs modulate target gene expression at the post-transcriptional level by influencing mRNA stability and translation, thereby altering gene function [34, 111]. They also play decisive role in controlling gene expression during development, ecological adaptation, and evolutionary processes in plants [34, 112]. Importantly, TFs are common targets of miRNAs, and their coordinated interaction plays a key role in fine-tuning of gene expression during plant growth and development under both normal and stress conditions [34, 113, 114]. Previous studies have also reported that miRNAs are involved in diverse cellular and physiological processes, including protein biosynthesis, signal transduction, membrane trafficking, responses to environmental stimuli, and the regulation of plant stress tolerance [115–117].

Expression analysis of OsbZIP genes and their corresponding Osa-miRs

In this study, nano-Zn was applied in a dose dependent manner to evaluate its effects on the growth of unlike rice varieties (CSR-30 and PB-1) grown under identical conditions. CSR-30 rice exhibited a reduction in both shoot and root length, whereas PB-1 rice displayed a negative impact on shoot length but a contrasting response in root length under nano-Zn treatment. To investigate these responses at a deeper molecular level, transcriptional and post-transcriptional regulations were observed at the 12-day seedling stage through expressions analysis of OsbZIP TF genes and their corresponding Osa-miRs under both treated and untreated conditions.

CSR-30 rice variety showed an overall reduction in the expression of the OsbZIP TF gene family along with elevated expression of their corresponding Osa-miRs. These observations suggest that OsbZIP TFs and their corresponding Osa-miRs established an antagonistic regulatory relationship in CSR-30 rice, thereby repressing OsbZIP gene expression [34, 118]. In PB-1 rice variety generally exhibited reduced expressions of both OsbZIP TF genes and their corresponding Osa-miRs under nano-Zn effect. Therefore, these results suggest a more complex multilayered combinatorial regulation, where both OsbZIP TFs and their corresponding Osa-miRs were down regulated under similar nano-Zn exposure [104, 119]. Therefore, the present research highlights differential dominance of transcriptional and post-transcriptional regulation, which may underlie the unrelated growth physiology observed between the two rice varieties in response to nano-Zn treatment.

Interestingly, some OsbZIP paralogs exhibited notable expression responses in both rice varieties under nano-Zn treatment in the present study. In particular, OsbZIP1 (LOC_Os01g46970) and OsbZIP13 (LOC_Os05g36160) displayed up-regulation accompanied by reduced expression of their corresponding Osa-miRs in the CSR-30 rice variety. The observed up-regulation of OsbZIP1 aligns with previous findings related to rice vegetative development [120] and its enhanced expression also agrees with earlier studies conducted under alkaline or saline conditions in different rice varieties [121, 122]. The OsbZIP1, also known as OSBZ8, has been reported to be induced through ABA-inducible genes action via hormonal control under abiotic stress conditions [122, 123]. Similarly, the expression pattern of OsbZIP13 in the present investigation is in accordance with previous studies reporting its involvement under cold stress, drought tolerance and ABA signaling [82, 124, 125]. OsbZIP13 (LOC_Os05g36160) has been documented as Enhanced drought tolerance 1 (Edt1), is considered an important candidate for breeding drought-tolerant rice cultivars [126]. Earlier studies have further shown that OsbZIP13 shares homology with ABF1 (Abscisic acid responsive element-binding factor 1), which is significantly involved in abiotic stress responses and ABA signaling in rice [82, 125]. Also, OsbZIP13 has been reported as a key regulator of gibberellin (GA) homeostasis through GA-inducible OsABF1, thereby influencing plant height, seed germination and repression of the green revolution gene SD1, ultimately reducing the endogenous GA level in rice [127].

Similarly, in the PB-1 rice variety, few gene paralogs like OsbZIP6 (LOC_Os02g07840), OsbZIP14 (LOC_Os05g41070), OsbZIP23 (LOC_Os09g13570), OsbZIP27 (LOC_Os12g06010), and OsbZIP29 (LOC_Os12g37410) were identified as distinctive in the present study, as they exhibited increased expression along with reduced expression of their corresponding Osa-miRs under nano-Zn treatment. For example, OsbZIP6 was characterized as RISBZ4, a member of the bZIP TF family and its expression, along with RISBZ1 and RISBZ3, has previously been reported during seed maturation in rice [84]. It has also been actively expressed in the plant vascular system, where it may facilitate the transport of minerals and other essential nutrients required for plant development [124]. Furthermore, improved expression of OsbZIP14 gene was observed in the present study. This gene paralog, also known as Hd3a Binding Repressor Factor1 (HBF1), has previously been reported to act antagonistically in rice flowering by repressing the expression of Early Heading Date 1 (Ehd1), Heading Date 3a (Hd3a), and Rice Flowering Locus T 1 (RFT1) [128, 129]. OsbZIP14 as acts as a positive regulator of ABA signaling, likely due to the presence of Abscisic Acid-Responsive Elements (ABREs) and G-box motifs in its promoter region, particularly under drought stress conditions [130]. The expression pattern of OsbZIP23 in the present study is also consistent with earlier reports showing its up regulation under salt related studies [106]. In addition, it responds to PEG, ABA and drought treatments, indicating its functional importance in abiotic stress signal transduction [131]. Similarly, the expression pattern of OsbZIP27 in the present study agrees with previous findings in rice, where it was classified as an unusual class of bZIP DNA-binding domain and participate in hetero-dimerization with EmBP-1, thereby interfering with EmBP-1 binding to the ABRE [132]. Likewise, OsbZIP29, also known as OsOBF1 has previously been reported to be involved in abiotic stress signaling, particularly under cold stress in rice [133]. More recently, its increased expression has also been linked to coordinate with Premature Leaf Senescence 4 (PLS4) in the regulation of leaf senescence in rice [134].

Commonly, gene paralogs such as OsbZIP3 (LOC_Os01g64000) and OsbZIP30 (LOC_Os12g40920) showed significantly elevated expression in both the rice varieties in the present case. The expression pattern of OsbZIP3 is in concurrence with previous analysis on alkali-responsive (IAR) genes conducted on contrasting rice cultivars [135]. It has also been reported as positive regulator of ABA signaling and a negative regulator of seed germination, post germination growth, and salt tolerance in rice [136]. OsbZIP3 is known as OsABI5, and its role has been associated with ABA signaling, rice fertility and stress tolerance responsive gene regulation in rice [135, 137]. Similarly, the increased expression of OsbZIP30 observed in the present study is in compliance with previous findings showing that it interacts with another bZIP protein, OsbZIP71, by hetero-dimerization and plays a crucial role in drought and salt tolerance through ABA dependent regulatory pathways in rice [131]. Therefore, these gene paralogs appear to have significant roles in stress and developmental related processes, likely mediated through ABA associated regulation and the coordinated involvement of different Cis-acting regulatory elements [82, 124, 131, 135, 137].

Conclusion

The present study was undertaken to identify key molecular determinants involved in transcriptional and post-transcriptional regulation in response to nano-Zn during early growth and development between divergent rice varieties (CSR-30 and PB-1). Detailed spatio-structural characterization of the OsbZIP TF gene family including physicochemical properties, protein modeling, protein-protein interaction, gene structure, promoter architecture, conserved motifs, tandem repeats and associated miRNAs revealed substantial structural and functional diversification within the gene family. These features indicated evolutionary divergence and functional specialization of OsbZIP genes under different biological contexts. Further, 30 OsbZIP TF genes and their 41 corresponding Osa-miRs were experimentally validated under nano-Zn treatment in both the rice varieties. The results revealed distinct, selective and genotype-dependent expression responses of OsbZIP gene family with their corresponding Osa-miRs indicating differential transcriptional and post-transcriptional regulation under nano-Zn exposure. The altered expression of several OsbZIP genes indicated ABA-related signaling with their possible involvement in modulating early growth physiology under nano-Zn treatment. Overall, the present study provides novel insight into the coordinated yet complex regulatory interaction between OsbZIP TFs and their corresponding miRNAs in rice under nano-Zn exposure. These findings highlight a multilayered molecular regulatory framework that may contribute to differential growth responses in the divergent rice varieties provide a valuable basis for future functional studies and the development of stress-resilient crop improvement strategies under nano-Zn application. However, further validation through field-scale trails, long-term safety assessments and multi-environment trials are required before practical implementation to better understand its broader impacts on crop improvement, human health, crop economics and environmental safety.
