A Symmetry-First Elementary Derivation of the Lorentz Transformation
Tan Nianjun tannianjun@gmail.com

Abstract




Abstract:

We present an elementary, symmetry-first derivation of the Lorentz
transformation together with a methodological clarification of the
linearity step. Starting from the Principle of Relativity, supplemented
by spacetime homogeneity, isotropy, continuity in the event coordinates,
and the absence of a distinguished inertial frame, we first establish
linearity and then determine the general symmetry-constrained form of
inertial-frame transformations. The inverse transformation is written
with parameter
-v
, not as a separate technical theorem, but as the
physically natural expression of the equivalence of inertial frames in
the standard configuration. Transformation consistency is used in
stages: its identity-and-inverse part constrains the remaining
coefficients, while its composition part leads to a one-parameter family
of inertial-frame transformations characterized by a universal constant

R
. The light postulate is introduced only in the final step, where
it selects the physically relevant branch by fixing
R=-c^{2}
, thereby
recovering the Lorentz transformation and identifying
c
as maximal.
The derivation is explicitly expository, making transparent several
steps often compressed in standard treatments, especially the derivation
of linearity, the elimination of transverse cross-terms, and the
algebraic determination of the relative-velocity composition law. A
central methodological point is that once homogeneity has reduced the
problem to an additive transformation law, continuity,
differentiability, and boundedness are equivalent regularity routes to
the same linear class of inertial-frame transformations. In the present
approach, only continuity in the event coordinates is assumed, while
continuity in the velocity parameter emerges a posteriori from the
explicit coefficient formulas.

Keywords:
Special Relativity; Lorentz Transformation;
Elementary Derivation; Symmetry; Pedagogy; Undergraduate Education;
Principle of Relativity

1. Introduction and Literature
Review

The Lorentz transformation, the mathematical cornerstone of special
relativity, has been derived in many ways, depending on which postulates
are taken as primitive and which mathematical tools are used. The aim
here is modest: not to propose a new kinematics, but to give a
symmetry-first derivation that remains at an elementary level while
making explicit several steps often compressed in standard
presentations. A second aim is to clarify a methodological point often
left implicit in such derivations, namely the equivalence of the
standard regularity routes from additivity to linearity once spacetime
homogeneity has been imposed.

Einstein’s original derivation starts from the Principle of Relativity
and the constancy of the speed of light together [1]. A different
line of thought was opened by Ignatowski, who showed that the general
transformation law can be derived from relativity, homogeneity, and
isotropy without initially assuming invariance of the speed of light
[2]. This symmetry-first tradition was later developed along several
lines. For the present paper, Berzi and Gorini are especially relevant
because they make precise how spacetime homogeneity leads to an additive
functional equation and how a weak regularity condition excludes
pathological solutions [3]. Lévy-Leblond emphasized the
group-theoretic structure [4], Pal gave a compact algebraic
derivation [5], and Gannett showed that boundedness conditions can
replace stronger smoothness assumptions in the passage from additivity
to linearity [6]. Moylan [7] later revisited the status of
velocity reciprocity. We use that discussion only in a limited sense: it
helps clarify why later authors isolated reciprocity as a separate
technical issue, but we do not adopt the view that the physical content
of the relativity principle is best represented by allowing the same
relative motion to receive unequal speed magnitudes in the two
reciprocal descriptions. More recent papers have returned to the
explicit derivation of linearity [8] and to a linear-algebraic
presentation of the Lorentz transformation [9].

The present derivation belongs to this symmetry-first line of work, but
differs mainly in organization and level of exposition. Rather than
beginning with the light postulate, we first derive the general
symmetry-constrained kinematics and postpone the empirical
identification of the invariant scale until the final stage. We also
make explicit that, within the additive framework supplied by
homogeneity, continuity, differentiability, and boundedness are
equivalent regularity routes to linearity, and that continuity is needed
only in the event coordinates rather than as an a priori assumption on
the velocity parameter.

Methodologically, we adopt a stronger physical reading of the Principle
of Relativity than the merely formal requirement that admissible
transformations form a common kinematical class. Whether or not one
chooses to subsume it under the Principle of Relativity as a matter of
definition, the absence of a distinguished inertial frame must in any
case be retained as an independent and indispensable physical
requirement. In the present context, this means that two reciprocal
descriptions of one and the same relative motion must remain physically
equivalent. If the direct and reciprocal descriptions were allowed to
assign unequal speed magnitudes to that motion, the discrepancy itself
would provide a criterion for distinguishing the status of the two
frames. From this viewpoint, the appearance of
-v
in the inverse
transformation is not best regarded as an extraneous technical theorem
obtained only after supplementing relativity by additional assumptions
on a velocity-labeling map. Rather, in the standard configuration it is
the natural kinematical expression of inertial-frame equivalence.
Section 2.2 establishes linearity and clarifies the equivalence of
regularity assumptions. Section 2.3 determines the coefficient structure
and the associated one-parameter family of transformations. Section 2.4
uses the light postulate to select the physically relevant branch,
recover the Lorentz transformation, extend the one-dimensional light
postulate to full three-dimensional kinematics, and show that
c
is
maximal. Section 2.5 briefly records the resulting group structure.

2. Derivation Process

2.1 Physical Assumptions

Our derivation rests on the following assumptions.

1.

Spacetime Structure Assumption

Spacetime is homogeneous, isotropic, and continuous.

2.

Principle of Relativity

The laws of physics have the same form in every inertial frame.

3.

No Distinguished Inertial Frame

No inertial reference frame is physically privileged over another.

4.

Transformation Consistency

The inertial transformations include the identity, admit inverses, and
are closed under composition.

5.

Principle of the Constancy of the Speed of Light
(hereafter,
the light postulate)

The speed of light in vacuum,
c
, is the same in all inertial
frames.

2.2.2 Linearity of the
Transformation

Let
T(\mathbf{e},v)
be the transformation between inertial frames
for fixed relative velocity
v
. By the spacetime structure assumption
(Section 2.1),
T(\cdot,v)
is continuous in the event argument

\mathbf{e}
. This continuity will only be used to extend homogeneity
from rational to real scalars.

(a) Independence from the base point

For any event
\mathbf{e}
and displacement
\mathbf{h}
, define

D(\mathbf{e},\mathbf{h},v):=T(\mathbf{e}+\mathbf{h},v)-T(\mathbf{e},v).

Spacetime homogeneity means that, for fixed relative velocity
v
, the
image increment associated with a given displacement
\mathbf{h}

cannot depend on the starting event
\mathbf{e}
, but only on the
displacement itself. Hence
D
is independent of
\mathbf{e}
, so
there exists a function
G
such that

D(\mathbf{e},\mathbf{h},v)=G(\mathbf{h},v).

Therefore,

T(\mathbf{e}+\mathbf{h},v)=T(\mathbf{e},v)+G(\mathbf{h},v).

(1)

(b) Additivity and homogeneity

Let
\mathbf{e}
be arbitrary. Using the above relation twice,

\displaystyle G(\mathbf{h}_{1}+\mathbf{h}_{2},v)

\displaystyle=T(\mathbf{e}+\mathbf{h}_{1}+\mathbf{h}_{2},v)-T(\mathbf{e},v)

\displaystyle=\big[T(\mathbf{e}+\mathbf{h}_{1}+\mathbf{h}_{2},v)-T(\mathbf{e}+\mathbf{h}_{1},v)\big]+\big[T(\mathbf{e}+\mathbf{h}_{1},v)-T(\mathbf{e},v)\big]

\displaystyle=G(\mathbf{h}_{2},v)+G(\mathbf{h}_{1},v).

Hence
G(\cdot,v)
is additive:

G(\mathbf{h}_{1}+\mathbf{h}_{2},v)=G(\mathbf{h}_{1},v)+G(\mathbf{h}_{2},v).

By induction,

G(n\mathbf{h},v)=n\,G(\mathbf{h},v),\qquad n\in\mathbb{Z}.

For rational scalars
\lambda=\frac{m}{n}
,

G(\lambda\mathbf{h},v)=\lambda\,G(\mathbf{h},v).

To extend this to all real
\lambda
, fix any base point

\mathbf{e}_{0}
. As in Berzi and Gorini [3], continuity is used
here to exclude pathological additive solutions and extend rational
homogeneity to all real scalars. Since
T(\cdot,v)
is continuous,

\mathbf{h}\mapsto T(\mathbf{e}_{0}+\mathbf{h},v)

is continuous, hence

G(\mathbf{h},v)=T(\mathbf{e}_{0}+\mathbf{h},v)-T(\mathbf{e}_{0},v)

is continuous in
\mathbf{h}
.

Therefore the rational homogeneity extends to all real
\lambda
:

G(\lambda\mathbf{h},v)=\lambda\,G(\mathbf{h},v),\qquad\lambda\in\mathbb{R}.

Combining additivity and homogeneity,
G(\cdot,v)
is linear in

\mathbf{h}
. Hence there exists a linear map
F(v)
such that

G(\mathbf{h},v)=F(v)\,\mathbf{h},

where
F(v)
is a
4\times 4
matrix depending only on
v
.

(c) Affine and linear form of
T

Setting
\mathbf{e}=\mathbf{0}
in
Eq.~(
1
), we obtain

T(\mathbf{h},v)=T(\mathbf{0},v)+G(\mathbf{h},v).

Since this relation holds for an arbitrary displacement vector

\mathbf{h}
, we may relabel that vector as an arbitrary event vector

\mathbf{e}
. Using
G(\mathbf{e},v)=F(v)\,\mathbf{e}
then gives

T(\mathbf{e},v)=F(v)\,\mathbf{e}+T(\mathbf{0},v),

so
T
is affine in
\mathbf{e}
.

In the standard configuration, the origins coincide, so

T(\mathbf{0},v)=\mathbf{0}
. Therefore,

\mathbf{e}^{\prime}=T(\mathbf{e},v)=F(v)\,\mathbf{e},

with
F(v)=[f_{ij}(v)]_{i,j=1}^{4}
.

2.2.3 Equivalent Regularity Routes to
Linearity

The proof above uses continuity in the event coordinates to extend
rational homogeneity to real homogeneity. This choice is not unique.
Once spacetime homogeneity has reduced the problem to the additive law
in Eq.~(
1
), the standard regularity
assumptions used in the literature all lead to the same linear class.

At the scalar level, the issue is the additive functional equation

f(x+y)=f(x)+f(y)
. In this setting, differentiability implies
linearity, continuity at a single point implies linearity, and
boundedness on a nontrivial interval implies continuity and hence
linearity. These routes appear in different forms in the literature, for
example in Berzi and Gorini [3], Pal [5], and Gannett [6].

Conversely, once linearity has been established, the corresponding
regularity properties follow immediately: linear transformations are
continuous, differentiable, and bounded on bounded sets. Thus, within
the homogeneous additive framework, continuity, differentiability, and
boundedness should be understood not as physically distinct hypotheses,
but as alternative ways of excluding pathological additive solutions.

2.3 Symmetry and Consistency
Constraints

With linearity established and the role of the regularity assumptions
clarified, we now determine the coefficient functions by symmetry and
consistency.

2.3.1 Physical Choice of the Inverse
Velocity

(a) Why the inverse transformation uses

-v

We first fix the velocity parameter appearing in the inverse
transformation. In the standard configuration the two frames are
physically equivalent, their spatial axes are parallel with the same
orientation, and no inertial frame is allowed to acquire a distinguished
status in the reciprocal description of one and the same relative
motion. For that reason, if
K^{\prime}
moves with velocity
v
relative to

K
, we write the inverse transformation with the opposite parameter

-v
:

\mathbf{e}^{\prime}=T(\mathbf{e},v),\qquad\mathbf{e}=T(\mathbf{e}^{\prime},-v).

This is adopted here as a physically direct interpretation of
inertial-frame equivalence rather than as a separate technical theorem.
If one were to assign to the inverse transformation a parameter whose
magnitude differed from
|v|
, then the same relative motion would be
given different speed magnitudes by the two frames. That asymmetry would
itself provide a way to distinguish the status of
K
and
K^{\prime}
,
contrary to the requirement that no inertial frame be physically
privileged. It would also sit uneasily with isotropy, since reversing
the description of the same collinear motion should not alter its
physical content except for the sign convention attached to direction.
In this sense, writing the inverse transformation with
-v
is not
justified by standard configuration alone, but by the physical
requirements adopted in this paper, of which the standard configuration
is the coordinate realization.

(b) Setup Transformation Equations

Accordingly, we write the direct and inverse transformations in the
forms

\mathbf{e}^{\prime}=T(\mathbf{e},v)=F(v)\,\mathbf{e},\quad\mathbf{e}=T(\mathbf{e}^{\prime},-v)=F(-v)\,\mathbf{e}^{\prime}

22
) gives

x=f_{11}(v)[x^{\prime}+vt^{\prime}]=f_{11}(v)[f_{11}(v)(x-vt)+v(f_{41}(v)x+f_{11}(v)t)]=[f_{11}(v)^{2}+vf_{11}(v)f_{41}(v)]x

so the coefficient of
x
must equal unity:

\displaystyle f_{11}(v)^{2}+vf_{41}(v)f_{11}(v)=1

(24)

Eq.~(
24
) gives the
inverse-consistency constraint between
f_{11}(v)
and
f_{41}(v)
;
the composition part of transformation consistency will determine their
explicit functional forms.

(b) Introducing the Universal Constant
R

from Composition

To impose the composition part of transformation consistency, consider
three inertial frames
K
,
K^{\prime}
, and
K^{\prime\prime}
, with
K^{\prime}
moving
with velocity
v
relative to
K
and
K^{\prime\prime}
moving with velocity

u
relative to
K^{\prime}
. Denote the velocity of
K^{\prime\prime}
relative to

K
by
w(u,v)
. The corresponding transformations are

From
K
to
K^{\prime\prime}
:

\displaystyle x^{\prime\prime}

\displaystyle=f_{11}(w)(x-wt)

(25)

\displaystyle t^{\prime\prime}

\displaystyle=f_{41}(w)x+f_{11}(w)t

(26)

From
K
to
K^{\prime}
:

\displaystyle x^{\prime}

\displaystyle=f_{11}(v)(x-vt)

(27)

\displaystyle t^{\prime}

\displaystyle=f_{41}(v)x+f_{11}(v)t

(28)

From
K^{\prime}
to
K^{\prime\prime}
:

\displaystyle x^{\prime\prime}

\displaystyle=f_{11}(u)(x^{\prime}-ut^{\prime})

(29)

\displaystyle t^{\prime\prime}

\displaystyle=f_{41}(u)x^{\prime}+f_{11}(u)t^{\prime}

(30)

Substituting
Eqs.~(
27
) and
(
28
) into
Eqs.~(
29
) and
(
30
) gives the composite transformation:

\displaystyle x^{\prime\prime}

\displaystyle=[f_{11}(u)f_{11}(v)-uf_{11}(u)f_{41}(v)]x-[f_{11}(u)vf_{11}(v)+uf_{11}(u)f_{11}(v)]t

(31)

\displaystyle t^{\prime\prime}

\displaystyle=[f_{41}(u)f_{11}(v)+f_{11}(u)f_{41}(v)]x+[-f_{41}(u)vf_{11}(v)+f_{11}(u)f_{11}(v)]t

(32)

For the composite transformation to agree with the direct
one, corresponding coefficients must match. Matching the coefficient of

x
in Eq.~(
31
) and the coefficient
of
t
in Eq.~(
32
) with the common
coefficient
f_{11}(w)
in
Eqs.~(
25
) and
(
26
) gives

f_{11}(u)f_{11}(v)-uf_{11}(u)f_{41}(v)=-f_{41}(u)vf_{11}(v)+f_{11}(u)f_{11}(v)

which simplifies to

\displaystyle uf_{11}(u)f_{41}(v)=vf_{11}(v)f_{41}(u).

(33)

Eq.~(
33
) holds
for all
u
and
v
. In particular, if
u=0
or
v=0
, then at
least one of the composed transformations is the identity
transformation, and both sides vanish because
f_{41}(0)=0
.

We therefore turn to the case
u\neq 0
and
v\neq 0
, where the
analysis splits into two branches.

If
f_{41}(v)=0
for every nonzero
v
, then together with

f_{41}(0)=0
this gives
f_{41}(v)\equiv 0
.
Eq.~(
24
) then reduces, for
every nonzero
v
, to
f_{11}(v)^{2}=1
. Since
f_{11}(v)>0
by
Eq.~(
14
), it follows that

f_{11}(v)=1
for every nonzero
v
, and the identity transformation
gives
f_{11}(0)=1
. Hence
f_{11}(v)=1
for every
v
. This is
precisely the Galilean branch, which remains possible until the light
postulate is imposed in Section~2.4.

We now turn to the non-Galilean branch. Assume that
f_{41}(v)
is not
identically zero for nonzero velocities. Then there exists some nonzero
velocity
v_{0}
such that
f_{41}(v_{0})\neq 0
. Setting
v=v_{0}
in
Eq.~(
33
) gives

uf_{11}(u)f_{41}(v_{0})=v_{0}f_{11}(v_{0})f_{41}(u).

If
f_{41}(u_{0})=0
for some nonzero
u_{0}
, then substituting

u=u_{0}
into the displayed equation yields

u_{0}f_{11}(u_{0})f_{41}(v_{0})=0,

which is impossible because
u_{0}\neq 0
,
Eq.~(
14
) gives
f_{11}(u_{0})>0
, and

f_{41}(v_{0})\neq 0
. Therefore, in the non-Galilean case,

f_{41}(v)\neq 0,\qquad v\neq 0.

Hence, for every nonzero
u
, we may divide by

uf_{11}(u)\,v_{0}f_{11}(v_{0})
and obtain

\displaystyle\frac{f_{41}(u)}{uf_{11}(u)}=\frac{f_{41}(v_{0})}{v_{0}f_{11}(v_{0})}.

(34)

Thus the ratio on the left is independent of the chosen
nonzero velocity. Denoting its reciprocal by
R
, we obtain, for every
nonzero
v
,

\frac{vf_{11}(v)}{f_{41}(v)}=R,\qquad v\neq 0,

where
R
is a universal constant with the dimension of velocity
squared. In the non-Galilean case this constant satisfies
R\neq 0
,
because for nonzero
v
both
vf_{11}(v)
and
f_{41}(v)
are
nonzero. Thus, for nonzero
v
,

f_{41}(v)=\dfrac{vf_{11}(v)}{R}.

Substituting this relation into
Eq.~(
24
) for nonzero
v

gives

1=f_{11}(v)^{2}\left(1+\frac{v^{2}}{R}\right)

Thus

f_{11}(v)^{2}=\frac{1}{1+\frac{v^{2}}{R}},

Since
f_{11}(v)>0
by Eq.~(
14
),
the positive branch is selected:

\displaystyle f_{11}(v)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\frac{v^{2}}{R}}}

(35)

This formula has been derived for
v\neq 0
, and it extends
consistently to
v=0
, where it gives
f_{11}(0)=1
in agreement
with the identity transformation. For the coefficient function to remain
real, one must require

1+\frac{v^{2}}{R}>0.

Equivalently, if
R>0
this condition holds for every real
v
,
whereas if
R<0
it requires
v^{2}<-R
; the case
R=0
is excluded.
The physically relevant branch will be identified more explicitly in
Section~2.4. Returning now to the ratio relation, we
obtain

\displaystyle f_{41}(v)=\frac{vf_{11}(v)}{R}

(36)

This formula has likewise been derived for
v\neq 0
, and
it is also compatible with
v=0
: using
f_{11}(0)=1
, it yields

f_{41}(0)=0
, in agreement with the identity transformation. At this
stage, the non-Galilean family is fixed up to the universal constant

R
. On its real domain of definition, the coefficient functions are
explicit elementary functions of
v
, and hence continuous there.
Continuity in the velocity parameter is therefore a derived property,
not an additional assumption. The remaining task is to determine how the
relative-velocity parameter itself composes.

(c) Derivation of the Relative-Velocity
Composition Law

With the coefficient functions now determined in terms of
R
, the
remaining task is to determine the composition law for the relative
velocity. The Principle of Relativity requires that the transformation
from
K
to
K^{\prime\prime}
have the same functional form as any other
inertial-frame transformation. Accordingly, we compare the direct
transformation from
K
to
K^{\prime\prime}
, written in the form of
Eqs.~(
25
) and
(
26
) with relative velocity
w(u,v)
, with
the composite transformation obtained by passing through
K^{\prime}
. In
Eq.~(
25
), the coefficient of

x
is
f_{11}(w)
, whereas in
Eq.~(
31
) the coefficient of
x
is

f_{11}(u)f_{11}(v)-uf_{11}(u)f_{41}(v)
. Equating these
coefficients and using Eq.~(
36
)
gives

f_{11}(w)=f_{11}(u)f_{11}(v)(1-\frac{uv}{R})

Next, in Eq.~(
25
) written
as
x^{\prime\prime}=f_{11}(w)x-wf_{11}(w)t
, the coefficient of
t
is

-wf_{11}(w)
, while in Eq.~(
31
)
the coefficient of
t
is

-[f_{11}(u)vf_{11}(v)+uf_{11}(u)f_{11}(v)]
. Equating these
coefficients gives

wf_{11}(w)=f_{11}(u)f_{11}(v)(u+v).

Since
u
and
v
are relative-velocity parameters of
inertial-frame transformations written in the standard configuration,
Eq.~(
14
) gives
f_{11}(u)>0
and

f_{11}(v)>0
. Moreover, the direct transformation from
K
to

K^{\prime\prime}
has the same form with parameter
w
, so
Eq.~(
14
) also gives
f_{11}(w)>0
.
The first relation therefore implies

1-\frac{uv}{R}>0,

since its left-hand side is positive and the prefactor

f_{11}(u)f_{11}(v)
on the right-hand side is also positive. In
particular,
1-\frac{uv}{R}\neq 0
, so we may divide the second
relation by the first and obtain directly

\displaystyle w=\frac{u+v}{1-\frac{uv}{R}}

(37)

Thus the velocity-composition law is fixed algebraically
within the symmetry-constrained family, with no separate continuity
assumption on the composition law. Moreover,
Eq.~(
37
) shows a posteriori
that the velocity-composition law is continuous wherever the denominator
is nonzero. At this stage, however,
Eq.~(
37
) should still be read
as the composition law for inertial-frame relative-velocity parameters.
The corresponding transformation law for the speed of a particle or
signal will be obtained in Section~2.4 by applying the
generalized spacetime transformation to a collinear worldline.

(d) Derivation of the Generalized Spacetime
Transformation

With both the coefficient functions and the velocity-composition law now
determined in terms of
R
, the generalized transformation can be
written explicitly. Its coefficient functions are

\displaystyle\gamma_{R}(v)

\displaystyle=f_{11}(v)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\frac{v^{2}}{R}}}

(38)

\displaystyle f_{41}(v)

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma_{R}(v)v}{R}

(39)

Substituting into Eqs.~(
20
) and
(
21
) yields the generalized inertial-frame
transformation along the
x
-axis:

\displaystyle x^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\gamma_{R}(v)(x-vt)

(40)

\displaystyle t^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\gamma_{R}(v)\left(t+\frac{v}{R}x\right)

(41)

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle=\gamma_{R}(v)(x^{\prime}+vt^{\prime})

(42)

\displaystyle t

\displaystyle=\gamma_{R}(v)\left(t^{\prime}-\frac{v}{R}x^{\prime}\right)

(43)

with
y^{\prime}=y
,
z^{\prime}=z
, and correspondingly
y=y^{\prime}
,

z=z^{\prime}
.

2.4 Introducing the Principle of the Constancy of the Speed
of
Light

We now use the light postulate to fix
R
and thereby select the
physically relevant member of the one-parameter family obtained in
Section~2.3.5. The resulting transformation is then
written in Lorentz form, and the role of
c
in the full
three-dimensional kinematics is clarified.

(a) Applying the Light Postulate

The light postulate first excludes the Galilean branch. In that case the
transformation reduces to
x^{\prime}=x-vt
and
t^{\prime}=t
, so a collinear
particle or signal with worldline
x=ut
in
K
has transformed
speed
u^{\prime}=x^{\prime}/t^{\prime}=u-v
in
K^{\prime}
. Requiring a light signal to have the
same speed
c
in both frames would therefore give
c=c-v
, which is
impossible for nonzero
v
. We therefore turn to the non-Galilean
family.

Let
K^{\prime}
move with velocity
v
relative to
K
along the positive

x
-direction, and consider a particle or signal moving collinearly
with speed
u
in
K
, so that its worldline is
x=ut
. Using
Eqs.~(
40
) and
(
41
), we obtain

x^{\prime}=\gamma_{R}(v)(u-v)t,\qquad t^{\prime}=\gamma_{R}(v)\left(1+\frac{uv}{R}\right)t.

Hence the transformed collinear speed is

u^{\prime}=\frac{x^{\prime}}{t^{\prime}}=\frac{u-v}{1+\frac{uv}{R}}.

Now consider a light signal propagating in the same direction. By the
light postulate, this signal has speed
c
in both frames, so we set

u=c
and
u^{\prime}=c
. This gives

c=\frac{c-v}{1+\frac{cv}{R}}.

Hence

R=-c^{2}.

Accordingly, the one-dimensional composition law becomes

\displaystyle w=\frac{u+v}{1+\frac{uv}{c^{2}}}.

(44)

Thus the physically relevant branch is
R=-c^{2}<0
. Using

f_{11}(v)>0
from Eq.~(
14
), one
obtains

f_{11}(v)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-v^{2}/c^{2}}},

so the coefficient functions remain real and finite precisely for

|v|<c
.

For completeness, the other formal possibilities may be noted only in
passing. The case
R=0
is excluded: it does not define a distinct
branch, but a singular degeneration in which the coefficient functions
and composition law cease to define finite invertible inertial-frame
transformations. If
R>0
, one may write
R=W^{2}
and parametrize

u=W\tan\theta_{1}
,
v=W\tan\theta_{2}
, in which case the composition
law becomes
w=W\tan(\theta_{1}+\theta_{2})
, that is, a trigonometric
angle-addition rule. Because
\tan\theta
has poles, this branch does
not supply a natural global real-velocity domain, and it is not selected
by the light postulate. Retrospectively, the Galilean branch may be
viewed as the formal limit of the non-Galilean family as
R\to\infty
.

(b) Lorentz Form and the Role of
c

Since part (a) has shown that
R=-c^{2}
, the generalized transformation
reduces immediately to the Lorentz transformation:

\displaystyle x^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\gamma(v)(x-vt),

\displaystyle t^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\gamma(v)\left(t-\frac{v}{c^{2}}x\right),

with

\gamma(v)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\frac{v^{2}}{c^{2}}}}.

Together with
y^{\prime}=y
and
z^{\prime}=z
, this is the standard Lorentz
transformation.

In accordance with the inverse-parameter choice adopted in
Section~2.3.1, the inverse Lorentz transformation is
obtained by replacing
v
with
-v
.

To examine the transformation of particle velocities from
K
to

K^{\prime}
, differentiate the direct transformation with respect to
t

and write
u_{x}=dx/dt
,
u_{y}=dy/dt
, and
u_{z}=dz/dt
. For the
transverse component, one has
y^{\prime}=y
, hence
dy^{\prime}=dy=u_{y}\,dt
, while

dt^{\prime}=\gamma(v)\left(dt-\frac{v}{c^{2}}dx\right)=\gamma(v)\left(1-\frac{u_{x}v}{c^{2}}\right)dt.

Therefore,

u_{y}^{\prime}=\frac{dy^{\prime}}{dt^{\prime}}=\frac{u_{y}}{\gamma(v)\left(1-\frac{u_{x}v}{c^{2}}\right)}=\frac{u_{y}\sqrt{1-\frac{v^{2}}{c^{2}}}}{1-\frac{u_{x}v}{c^{2}}}.

Proceeding in the same way for the longitudinal and
z
-components
gives

u_{x}^{\prime}=\frac{u_{x}-v}{1-\frac{u_{x}v}{c^{2}}},\qquad u_{y}^{\prime}=\frac{u_{y}\sqrt{1-\frac{v^{2}}{c^{2}}}}{1-\frac{u_{x}v}{c^{2}}},\qquad u_{z}^{\prime}=\frac{u_{z}\sqrt{1-\frac{v^{2}}{c^{2}}}}{1-\frac{u_{x}v}{c^{2}}}.

Now consider a light ray in frame
K
. If its direction is
arbitrary, then its velocity components satisfy

u_{x}^{2}+u_{y}^{2}+u_{z}^{2}=c^{2}.

Substituting this relation into the velocity-transformation formulas
gives

\displaystyle u_{x}^{\prime 2}+u_{y}^{\prime 2}+u_{z}^{\prime 2}

\displaystyle=\frac{(u_{x}-v)^{2}+\left(1-\frac{v^{2}}{c^{2}}\right)(u_{y}^{2}+u_{z}^{2})}{\left(1-\frac{u_{x}v}{c^{2}}\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{(u_{x}-v)^{2}+\left(1-\frac{v^{2}}{c^{2}}\right)(c^{2}-u_{x}^{2})}{\left(1-\frac{u_{x}v}{c^{2}}\right)^{2}}=c^{2}.

Thus a light ray with speed
c
in
K
also has speed

c
in
K^{\prime}
, regardless of direction. This extends the
one-dimensional light postulate to the full three-dimensional
kinematics.

We now turn to maximality. The transformation between inertial frames is
real only for
|v|<c
, since

\gamma(v)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\frac{v^{2}}{c^{2}}}}

becomes singular at
|v|=c
. For a particle with speed
u
in

K
, where
u^{2}=u_{x}^{2}+u_{y}^{2}+u_{z}^{2}
, one finds

\displaystyle c^{2}-u^{\prime 2}

\displaystyle=c^{2}-(u_{x}^{\prime 2}+u_{y}^{\prime 2}+u_{z}^{\prime 2})=\frac{\left(1-\frac{v^{2}}{c^{2}}\right)(c^{2}-u^{2})}{\left(1-\frac{u_{x}v}{c^{2}}\right)^{2}}.

Therefore, if
u<c
in one inertial frame, then
u^{\prime}<c

in every other inertial frame; and if
u=c
, then
u^{\prime}=c
. Thus
c

is both an invariant speed and the maximal speed.

2.5 Symmetry, Structure, and the Group Nature of
Transformations

Looking back at the argument, the same structural assumptions that
produced the symmetry-constrained family in Section~2.3
also determine the group properties of the Lorentzian branch selected in
Section~2.4. On that branch, the physically allowed
parameter range is
|v|<c
. Within this range, setting
v=0
yields
the identity transformation, reversing the sign of
v
gives the
inverse transformation, and composing two successive inertial-frame
transformations with relative-velocity parameters
u
and
v

produces a single transformation with combined parameter
w
. Closure
follows from Eq.~(
44
), which
maps the interval
(-c,c)
into itself. These properties establish
closure, identity, and inverse; associativity follows from the ordinary
associativity of composition of maps. Hence the set of inertial
transformations along a fixed axis forms a continuous one-parameter
group on the interval
(-c,c)
. With the explicit transformation law
established, this group may be described more precisely as a
one-parameter Lie group acting linearly on spacetime, with parameter

v
and invariant speed
c
.

3. Summary

We have given a staged symmetry-first derivation of the Lorentz
transformation and clarified several methodological points often left
implicit. Homogeneity and continuity in the event coordinates yield
linearity of the transformation, and within that additive framework
continuity, differentiability, and boundedness are equivalent regularity
routes to the same linear class. Thus only continuity in the event
coordinates is assumed, whereas continuity in the velocity parameter is
recovered a posteriori from the explicit coefficient formulas. The
inverse transformation is written with parameter
-v
as the natural
kinematical expression of inertial-frame equivalence in the standard
configuration. Transformation consistency enters in two steps: its
identity-and-inverse part constrains the remaining coefficients, and its
composition part introduces the universal constant
R
while
determining the relative-velocity composition law and the generalized
inertial-frame transformation in terms of
R
. The light postulate
then fixes
R=-c^{2}
, recovering the Lorentz transformation and showing
that
c
is maximal.
