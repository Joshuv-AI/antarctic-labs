Trends and disparities in glycemic control and severe hyperglycemia in U.S. adults with diabetes mellitus before and during COVID-19 pandemic
Authors: Litao Wang, Liuguang Song, Jiaqi Fan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe COVID-19 pandemic disrupted healthcare delivery and individual behaviors in individuals with diabetes. Limited data exist on glycemic control during this period. This study aimed to evaluate trends in glycemic control and severe hyperglycemia among U.S. adults with diagnosed diabetes before and during the COVID-19 pandemic.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort analysis of data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) between pre-pandemic (January 2017-March 2020) and pandemic (August 2021-August 2023) periods. Glycemic control (HbA1c < 7%) and severe hyperglycemia (HbA1c > 10%) were assessed overall and by age, race/ethnicity, and socioeconomic status using logistic regression.ResultsAmong 2528 adults with diabetes, glycemic control remained unchanged between pre- and during the pandemic (59.7% vs. 56.1%, p = 0.29), but severe hyperglycemia increased significantly (5.6% to 8.5%, p = 0.03). Female participants were more likely to achieve glycemic control (adjusted OR [aOR], 1.40; 95% CI, 1.11–1.75), and those with more than high school education had higher glycemic control (aOR, 1.33; 95% CI, 1.06–1.67) and lower severe hyperglycemia (aOR, 0.51; 95% CI, 0.29–0.89). Severe hyperglycemia was least prevalent in adults aged 65 years or older (aOR, 0.27; 95% CI, 0.15–0.50) but more common among Hispanic/Mexican American (aOR, 2.62; 95% CI, 1.53–4.50) and Non-Hispanic Black adults (aOR, 2.80; 95% CI, 1.62–4.84).ConclusionWhile glycemic control remained stable, severe hyperglycemia increased during the COVID-19 pandemic, particularly among Hispanic/Mexican American and Non-Hispanic Black adults. Higher education and older age were associated with better outcomes, emphasizing the need for targeted interventions to address disparities and prevent hyperglycemia.

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus affects over 38 million individuals in the United States and is a leading cause of morbidity and mortality [1]. Glycemic control, measured by glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), is essential to minimize the risk of complications, including cardiovascular disease, nephropathy, and retinopathy. Optimal control is generally defined as HbA1c < 7%, while severe hyperglycemia (HbA1c > 10%) is associated with increased risk of acute and chronic complications [2–4]. Over the past decades, major advances in diabetes technology and management strategies have been achieved [3]. However, little is known regarding recent patterns of glycemic control among patients with diabetes, especially for severe hyperglycemia.

The COVID-19 pandemic introduced unprecedented disruptions to healthcare access and delivery. Restrictions on in-person visits, coupled with shifts to telehealth, lifestyle changes, and increased psychosocial stress, may have significantly impacted diabetes management. Early reports have indicated a potential worsening of chronic disease outcomes, including glycemic control. Similarly, some systematic meta-analyses have demonstrated an increased risk of developing diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) during the COVID-19 pandemic compared to the pre-pandemic era [5, 6]. However, comprehensive data on the impact of the pandemic on diabetes outcomes across diverse populations remain limited. To identify the trends and disparities in glycemic control could inform health policy and public health initiatives to improves outcomes among these patients.

Noteworthily, persons form low-socioeconomic status backgrounds, and racial and ethnic minority patients, might have less assess to technologies that improve the therapeutic safety, such as continuous glucose monitoring, potentially leading to worse glycemic control [7]. However, few studies have examined the effects of these disparities on glycemic control among these patients over time. To understand the population-level diversities is crucial for formulating policies and specific interventions to eliminate inequities among such patients.

Therefore, in this study, we aimed to assess changes in diabetes control and severe hyperglycemia among U.S. adults during the COVID-19 pandemic compared to the pre-pandemic period, using data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES). Additionally, we investigated these outcomes overall and by educational level, as well as race and ethnicity, and found that severe hyperglycemia significantly worsened during the pandemic, particularly among vulnerable subgroups, including racial/ethnic minorities and those of lower socioeconomic status.

Methods

Study design and population

We performed a retrospective cohort analysis using NHANES data from 2017 to 2023. NHANES is a cross-sectional survey designed to assess the health and nutritional status of the U.S. population through household interviews, standardized physical examinations, and laboratory tests. Our analysis included nonpregnant adults aged 20 years or older with a self-reported diagnosis of diabetes mellitus. The National Center for Health Statistics Institutional Review Board approved the study protocols, and all the participants provided written informed consent.

Glycemic control and severe hyperglycemia

HbA1c level was measured using high-performance liquid chromatography, and was calibrated using a previously validated equipercentile equating approach to rectify distribution shifts due to changing laboratory methods [8]. Glycemic control was defined as an HbA1c level below 7% (53 mmol/mol). Severe hyperglycemia was characterized by an HbA1c level exceeding 10% (86 mmol/mol). In sensitivity analyses, we also explored alternative thresholds for glycemic control (HbA1c < 8% [64 mmol/mol]) and severe hyperglycemia (HbA1c > 9% [75 mmol/mol]).

Sociodemographic and other measures

Participants provided information on age, sex (male or female), race and ethnicity (non-Hispanic White, non-Hispanic Black, Asian American, Mexican American, or other), educational attainment (less than high school, high school diploma, or more than high school), and smoking status (current smoker, former smoker, or never smoked). Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared, and participants were categorized into three weight-status groups: normal (BMI < 25 kg/m²), overweight (BMI 25 to < 30 kg/m²), or obese (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m²).

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize baseline characteristics. Proportions were compared using chi-square tests. Multivariable logistic regression models were used to estimate adjusted odds ratios (aORs) for poor glycemic control and severe hyperglycemia during the pandemic compared to the pre-pandemic period, adjusting for potential confounders, including age, sex, race/ethnicity, BMI, and socioeconomic status. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Analyses were performed using STATA 16.0 (StataCorp, College Station, TX).

Patient and public involvement

Patients and/or the public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting, or dissemination plans of this research.

Results

Baseline characteristics of US adults with diabetes

The study population included 2528 adults with diagnosed diabetes (1496 pre-pandemic and 1032 during the pandemic), and the demographic profile was summarized in Table 1. Compared to the adults with diabetes from 2017 to 2020, the current mean age of those from 2021 to 2023 was 59.9 years [95% CI, 59.0-60.8 years] and did not change markedly over time (p = 0.61). Additionally, we didn’t find significant discrepancies in the overall race/ethnic composition (p = 0.43), sex (p = 0.17), educational background (p = 0.49), weight status (p = 0.95) and smoking statues (p = 0.45).

Moreover, there was no significant difference between the two groups in achieving blood pressure control (< 140/90 mmHg), which increased from 72.6% to 77.0% (p = 0.15). Analogously, the proportion of adults achieving blood pressure < 130/80 mmHg increased from 48.9% to 51.6% (p = 0.4), and the percentage of individuals with non-HDL cholesterol < 130 mg/dl remained stable at 59.0% pre-pandemic and 58.8% during the pandemic (p = 0.95). The proportion of individuals achieving control of all three risk factors (HbA1c < 7%, blood pressure < 140/90 mmHg, and non-HDL cholesterol < 130 mg/dl) also remained largely unchanged, increasing slightly from 26.5% to 28.4% (p = 0.63).

Notably, the overall percentage of adults with diabetes who used insulin did not change significantly from 29.0% in 2017–2020 to 31.7% in 2021–2023 (p = 0.29). However, the proportion of individuals with severe hyperglycemia (HbA1c > 10%) increased dramatically from 5.6% to 8.5% (p = 0.03) during the pandemic compared to pre-pandemic period. Trends were similar that the proportion increased from 10.9% to 15.8% (p = 0.01) when using alternate uniform HbA1c cutoffs (HbA1c > 9%).

Adjusted likelihood of glycemic control and severe hyperglycemia

After adjusting for age, gender, race and ethnicity, education, obese status, and smoking status, female adults were more likely to achieve glycemic control (aOR, 1.40; 95% CI, 1.11–1.75) than males. Moreover, participants with more than high school education had higher odds of glycemic control (aOR 1.33, 95% CI, 1.06–1.67) compared to those with less education (Table 3).

Discussion

In the present study, our findings indicated that, from the 2017–2020 to 2021–2023, there was no significant change in the prevalence of glycemic control among U.S. adults with diabetes. However, the COVID-19 pandemic markedly worsened the percentage of patients with severe hyperglycemia compared to the pre-pandemic period, which increased from 5.6% to 8.5%. These results underscored the importance of protection for susceptible patients to reduce the occurrence of serious complications.

Epidemiological evidence from population-based electronic health databases indicated an increase in the incidence of diabetes after COVID-19 pandemic, including the cohort from Germany, the United States and Korea [9–11]. Moreover, several previous studies also demonstrated a significant rise in the levels of HbA1c in patients with diabetes [12, 13]. However, we observed no evidence of a deterioration in the glycemic control during 2021–2023 in comparison with 2017–2020. Consistent with our findings, a recent study showed that the levels of HbA1c were nearly identical during the pandemic compared to the pre-pandemic period (7.16% vs. 7.14%) 14. One explanation for these findings could be the small rise in medication fill rate during the COVID-19 pandemic that might protect against the disruptions in diabetes self-management and hence avoided detrimental effects on glycemic control. Also, the mail-order pharmacies and pharmacy delivery services may play an important role in ensuring patients receive the medications timely [14]. More interestingly, an overall improvement in glycemic control was observed in some studies [15, 16]. The variations in the study designs and differences in included patients and races might explain the discrepancies. Thus, limited by the relatively small sample size and the noninstitutionalized adults enrolled by the NHANES, a largest possible sample size and certain segments of the unincluded population with diabetes should be further investigated to clarify the more precise trends in glycemic control.

The significant increase in poor glycemic control (HbA1c > 9%) and severe hyperglycemia (HbA1c > 10%) highlights the vulnerability of individuals with diabetes during a public health crisis. Several factors might account for these findings. On the one hand, the severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) virus itself could induce hyperglycemia, insulin resistance, and diabetes mellitus decompensation [17]. On the other hand, the observed deterioration in severe hyperglycemia might be consistent with the disruption of healthcare services, changes in patients’ habits and prescription patterns, as well as shifts in pharmacologic therapies [18, 19]. During COVID-19 pandemic, many countries closed the boarders and implemented stay-at home (lockdown) measures to limit virus spread and protect citizens. The lockdown inadvertently restricted patients to visit outpatient clinics, impacted their living and eating habits, limited their physical activities, and heightened their psychosocial stress [20]. Though the advantages of telemedicine during the pandemic [14], a considerable quantity of patients with diabetes developed complications, possibly related to fear and delay in seeking medical help [21]. To ensure successfulness of telemedicine program, health care providers engaged in virtual clinics should communicate with patients remotely via phone call or video calling. However, most clinicians have not used telemedicine as a routine part of their daily work, which might increase the difficulties of blood sugar management for people with lower educational levels, such as the elderly [22]. Additionally, the lockdown could have both short-term or long-term effects on diet quality and physical activity habits [23]. A scoping review showed that the consumption of homemade meals, fruits and vegetables increased but so did intake of comfort food, snacking, number of meals consumed per day, and overall food intake [24]. Another systematic review of 66 studies observed a decline in all physical activity and an increase in sedentary behavior [25]. Finally, a considerable decrease in the average number of weekly antidiabetic drug prescription fills was found during the COVID-19 pandemic in the U.S., which might be attribute to the reduced contact with prescribing clinicians during the pandemic, rationing, previous stockpiling, or loss of insurance [26]. Collectively, the above alterations might contribute to the poor glycemic control among patients with diabetes mellitus during the pandemic.

Notably, the most affected groups were those aged 45–64 years, females, and individuals of lower socioeconomic status. These findings underscore the broader societal impacts of the pandemic, which disproportionately affected underserved populations and exacerbated pre-existing health disparities. The observed disparities can be linked to structural inequities, including reduced healthcare access, lower health literacy, and socioeconomic barriers that limited the ability to adapt to the challenges of diabetes self-management during the pandemic.

Trends in severe hyperglycemia varied considerably across race and ethnicity in the current study. Compared to Non-Hispanic White, Hispanic/Mexican American and non-Hispanic Black adults experienced disproportionately higher odds of severe hyperglycemia. The disparities might be driven partly by discrepancies in socioeconomic resources. Other possible contributors may incorporate the cultural diversity and health beliefs (e.g., fear of needles), differences in health care literacy and racial discrimination [27]. Overall, the increase in severe hyperglycemia, particularly among racial and ethnic minorities, further reflects long-standing healthcare inequities. These findings are indicative of systemic barriers, including limited access to healthcare resources, reduced affordability of diabetes medications, and other social determinants of health that disproportionately impact these populations. Addressing these disparities requires comprehensive strategies that involve both healthcare system reforms and community-level interventions to support diabetes care in vulnerable groups. Therefore, to improve diabetes care among these patients may require culturally tailored interventions. Additionally, the pandemic also altered lifestyle factors crucial to diabetes management, including diet, physical activity, and medication adherence. The closure of gyms, restricted movement, and increased consumption of unhealthy foods likely contributed to worsening glycemic control. Moreover, increased stress and anxiety related to the pandemic may have triggered metabolic changes that further compromised glycemic control. These challenges underscore the importance of integrating psychosocial support into diabetes care, particularly during times of crisis. Last but not least, the shift to telehealth, while beneficial for maintaining continuity of care, may not have fully compensated for the reduction in face-to-face interactions. Many individuals, particularly those from lower socioeconomic backgrounds, may have faced technological barriers to accessing telehealth services. Ensuring equitable access to telehealth and enhancing digital literacy are critical to mitigating disparities in diabetes care in future public health emergencies.

Strengths and limitations

To the best of our knowledge, this was one of the first nationally representative studies attempting to dissect the discrepancy of glycemic control and severe hyperglycemia among U.S. adults during the COVID-19 pandemic compared to the pre-pandemic period using the representative NHANES data, which provides insights into the impact of the pandemic on diabetes outcomes at a population level. Furthermore, the adjustment for multiple confounding factors greatly strengthens the validity of our findings. However, several limitations should also be considered in our study. First, the cross-sectional design of NHANES data precludes causal inferences. Second, self-reported diabetes status may introduce recall bias. Third, the lack of detailed information on healthcare access during the pandemic also limits our ability to fully understand the mechanisms underlying the observed trends. Fourth, the lack of COVID-19 disease severity data may influence the results, and future studies linking NHANES data with hospitalization or disease severity metrics could provide further insights. Fifth, the geographical correlation with COVID-19 prevalence is a potential confounding factor that might influence diabetic outcomes during the pandemic, and future studies merging NHANES data with geographically stratified information (e.g., CDC COVID-19 case rates or hospital admission rates) could provide a more nuanced analysis. Finally, since the insurance has a great influence on the healthcare, the findings may not be fully generalizable to other population such as those with medical insurance or the uninsured. Future research should focus on longitudinal data to establish causal relationships and identify specific intervention strategies to address the disparities highlighted by our study.

Conclusion

Our study demonstrated that the prevalence of glycemic control among U.S. adults with diagnosed diabetes did not significantly change during the COVID-19 pandemic. However, severe hyperglycemia increased significantly, particularly among Hispanic/Mexican American and Non-Hispanic Black adults. Education level and age were significant determinants of glycemic control and hyperglycemia, with those having more than a high school education and adults aged 65 years or older being less likely to experience severe hyperglycemia. These findings highlight ongoing disparities in diabetes management, particularly among racial and ethnic minorities, and emphasize the need for targeted interventions to improve glycemic control and reduce severe hyperglycemia in vulnerable populations.
