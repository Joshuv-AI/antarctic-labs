Long-term metabolically unhealthy aging, its underlying molecular underpinnings, and association with cognitive impairment: a 12.4-year longitudinal cohort study
Authors: Kui Deng, Zheqing Zhang, Ke Zhang, Kejun Zhou, Xinyue Wang, Shize Jia, Dongmei Ru, Zilong Lu, Yan Yan, Fengjie Huang, Tianlu Chen, Ju-Sheng Zheng, Guoxiang Xie, Yujing Huang, Yu-ming Chen

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIndividuals experience heterogeneous aging processes closely associated with metabolism. However, it remains unclear whether older adults undergo different metabolic health-related aging patterns over time. We aimed to investigate the long-term metabolic health-related aging trajectories, their associations with cognitive function, and underlying molecular underpinnings among older adults.MethodsThis study was based on a longitudinal cohort of 1334 middle-aged and elderly Chinese adults (age: 57.5 ± 5.2 years; 30.7% men) with a median follow-up of 12.4 years across four visits. We used the latent class growth mixed model to identify longitudinal metabolic disturbance trajectory groups. We then examined the prospective associations of the identified longitudinal metabolic disturbance subtypes with cognitive function assessed by Addenbrooke’s Cognitive Examination-Revised (ACE-R), and with brain structure measured by magnetic resonance imaging. Leveraging repeated-measured proteome (up to three times; Nproteome = 1334 with 3417 data points) and metabolome data (up to four times; Nmetabolome = 584 with 2330 data points), we explored potential mechanisms underlying the longitudinal metabolic disturbance subtypes.ResultsWe identified two distinct longitudinal metabolic disturbance subtypes, i.e., metabolically unhealthy aging group (MUAG) (N = 270) and metabolically healthy aging group (MHAG) (N = 1064), characterized by differences in 13 metabolic traits and four major metabolic diseases (type 2 diabetes, hypertension, obesity, and metabolic syndrome). MUAG was associated with cognitive impairment and smaller volumes of brain grey matter, thalamus, caudate, hippocampus, and amygdala. The identified longitudinal metabolic disturbance subtypes may involve biological pathways of immune regulation, key enzyme activity regulation, branched-chain amino acid biosynthesis, and citrate cycle. We identified 11 serum proteins and 31 serum metabolites associated with the MUAG, and revealed a protein-metabolite network involving 26 protein-metabolite associations.ConclusionsWe identified two longitudinal metabolic health-related aging subtypes among middle-aged and elderly adults and uncovered their underlying molecular underpinnings. Our findings may help improve health management of aging populations, offer mechanistic insights, and inform new therapeutic targets for metabolically unhealthy aging.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12916-026-05001-9.Keywords: Healthy aging, Metabolic health, Proteomics, Metabolomics, Cognitive function, Brain structure

Background

The global population is shifting towards an aging population due to increasing life expectancy and declining fertility rates [1, 2]. Despite increasing lifespan, the healthspan has not improved accordingly [3]. The aging process varies substantially among individuals, owing to the heterogeneous impacts of aging on the functions of cells, tissues, and organs [4]. Consequently, individuals of the same age may exhibit varying pathophysiological phenotypes and have differing susceptibilities to aging-related diseases. However, it is less clear whether and how individuals present different trajectories of health-related phenotypes during aging. Addressing this research gap could help promote health management and potentially optimize the prevention and treatment strategies in a personalized manner for older adults.

Aging is closely linked with metabolism, and dysregulated metabolism is involved in the mechanisms of several aging hallmarks, including deregulated nutrient-sensing, mitochondrial dysfunction, chronic inflammation, and disabled macroautophagy [4, 5]. Major manifestations of aging-related metabolic dysregulation include central adiposity, sarcopenia, insulin resistance, decreased resting energy expenditure, metabolic syndrome (MetS), and type 2 diabetes (T2D), among others [5, 6]. Metabolic dysregulation has also been linked to cognitive decline and brain aging [7, 8]. Thus, maintaining healthy metabolic regulation is crucial for achieving a long-term healthspan. A significant challenge in the field of aging is defining healthy aging, especially in the context of metabolic multimorbidity, which is highly prevalent among older adults [9]. Recent studies have identified metabolically healthy and unhealthy aging subgroups by incorporating metabolic multimorbidity based on cross-sectional data [10, 11]. However, longitudinal metabolic health-related aging patterns among older individuals with a high prevalence of metabolic multimorbidity, along with their underlying mechanisms, have not yet been investigated. In addition, whether and how long-term metabolic disturbance patterns impact cognitive function remains less clear.

Therefore, this study aimed to identify metabolic disturbance trajectory classes during the aging process, based on a longitudinal cohort of 1334 middle-aged and elderly Chinese adults, with a median follow-up of 12.4 years across four visits. We then evaluated the associations between long-term metabolic disturbance subtypes and cognitive function. Furthermore, we explored proteomic and metabolic underpinnings of the identified longitudinal metabolic disturbance patterns, based on repeated-measured proteome (Nproteome =3417 with ≥ 1 cohort visit) and metabolome data (Nmetabolome =2330 with ≥ 3 cohort visits), aiming to elucidate underlying biological mechanisms and identify potential therapeutic targets for metabolically unhealthy aging.

Methods

Study population and design

This study was based on Guangzhou Nutrition and Health Study (GNHS), which was approved by the Ethics Committee of the School of Public Health at Sun Yat-sen University (approval number: 2018048) and Westlake University (approval number: 20190114ZJS0003), with written informed consent obtained from all participants. Detailed study design and protocol of GNHS have been described previously [12]. In brief, a total of 4048 Chinese participants, aged 40–75 years and residing in urban Guangzhou, China, for at least five years, were recruited between 2008 and 2013 (baseline), and followed up at 2014–2017 (F2), 2018–2019 (F3), and 2020–2023 (F4), respectively. At baseline and three follow-up visits, information on demographics, lifestyles, disease history, and medication use was collected using a structured questionnaire during face-to-face interviews. Fasting blood samples were collected on-site at four cohort visits on the examination day, stored temporarily on ice, and transported to our research laboratory. Serums were prepared per a standardized protocol and stored at -80℃ in 0.5 mL aliquots within 4 h.

In the present study, we included participants with ≥ 3 measures of metabolic health-related metrics, including body mass index (BMI), blood lipids, blood glucose, and blood pressure (BP) (the scoring of these metrics is described in the next section), across four cohort visits, resulting in 1400 participants. After further excluding 66 participants with self-reported cancer at any visits, 1334 individuals remained in our analysis.

Serum proteome data from 1334 participants were measured at baseline, F2, or F3, with 858 participants measured three times, 367 measured twice, and 109 measured once (Nproteome =3417) [13, 14]. The proteome data were further randomly divided into discovery and validation sets (with no participant overlap) because they were measured at different time points with independent sample preparation, mass spectrometry (MS) acquisition, and data preprocessing [13]. The discovery set comprised 1065 participants with 2740 proteome data points (699 participants measured three times, 277 measured twice, and 89 measured once), and the validation set included 269 participants with 677 proteome data points (159 participants measured three times, 90 measured twice, and 20 measured once).

We selected 675 participants with blood samples collected at all four cohort visits for repeated-measured metabolomics profiling, 584 of whom overlapped with the 1334 participants mentioned above. After quality control, 2330 serum metabolome data from 584 participants were obtained, with 578 measured four times and six measured three times. In addition, we included 346 participants with cross-sectional metabolome data previously profiled at a single time point (F2 or F3) [15, 16], which did not overlap with the repeated-measured metabolome data, to validate the results from the latter.

The discovery and validation sets comprised 1291 serum samples from 468 participants and 307 serum samples from 116 participants, respectively, profiled for both proteome and metabolome data. These data were used for proteome-metabolome integration analysis. The study design overview is presented in Fig. 1.

The longitudinal metabolic disturbance subtypes were identified based on 1334 participants with ≥ 3 measures of metabolic health-related metrics across four cohort visits, including body mass index, lipids, glucose, and blood pressure scores constructed according to the American Heart Association guidelines. We examined the associations of metabolic disturbance subtypes with cognitive function and brain structural markers. Furthermore, we constructed the protein-metabolite network associated with the metabolic disturbance subtypes based on repeated-measures and potential causal (determined by Mendelian randomization analysis) associations between proteins and metabolites

Measurement of metabolic health-related metrics, other metabolic traits and diseases, and covariates

We utilized the health factor components of Life’s Essential 8 to evaluate metabolic health and constructed BMI, lipids, glucose, and BP scores, according to the American Heart Association guidelines [17], as shown in Additional file 1: Table S1. Each of these scores ranges from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating better metabolic health. Specifically, BMI was calculated as weight (kg)/height (m)2, where weight and height were measured by trained nurses on-site. Blood lipids component was scored based on non-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (non-HDL-C) (calculated by subtracting HDL-C from total cholesterol [TC]) and use of lipid-lowering medications. Blood glucose component was scored based on T2D status, fasting blood glucose (FBG), and glycated hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) at F2-F4 follow-up visits, and based on T2D status and FBG at baseline, as HbA1c was not measured at baseline in our cohort. Blood pressure component was scored based on systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), and use of antihypertensive medications. The total metabolic health score (ranging from 0 to 100), a comprehensive measure of metabolic health, was calculated as the arithmetic mean of the BMI, lipids, glucose, and BP scores.

HbA1c was measured by high-performance liquid chromatography using Bole D-10 Hemoglobin A1c Program on Bole D-10 Hemoglobin Testing System. Insulin was measured by electrochemiluminescence immunoassay using a Cobas 8000/e602 immunoanalyzer (Roche Diagnostics). FBG, TC, triglycerides (TG), HDL-C, and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) were measured by colorimetric methods using a Cobas 8000 c702 automated analyzer (Roche Diagnostics). Homeostasis model assessment-estimated insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) was calculated based on fasting insulin and FBG [18]. Waist circumference (WC) and hip circumference were measured by trained staff, and the corresponding Waist-to-Hip Ratio (WHR) was calculated. SBP and DBP were measured using an arm digital sphygmomanometer (HEM-7011, OMRON Corporation).

T2D was diagnosed by FBG ≥ 7.0 mmol/L (126 mg/dL), HbA1c ≥ 6.5% (48 mmol/mol), or use of T2D medications [19]. Hypertension was defined as SBP ≥ 140 mmHg, DBP ≥ 90 mmHg, or use of antihypertensive medications [20]. MetS was diagnosed when three or more of the following criteria were met: (1) WC > 90 cm (male) or > 85 cm (female); (2) FBG ≥ 6.1 mmol/L (110 mg/dL) or history of T2D; (3) TG ≥ 1.7 mmol/L (150 mg/dL); (4) HDL-C < 1.04 mmol/L (40 mg/dL); and (5) SBP/DBP ≥ 130/85 mmHg or history of hypertension [21]. Obesity was defined as BMI ≥ 28 [22, 23].

Physical activity intensity was measured by total metabolic equivalent for task (MET) hours/day using a validated physical activity questionnaire with 19 items [24]. We calculated total energy intake based on habitual dietary intakes over the past year using a validated food frequency questionnaire with 79 items [25].

Assessment of cognitive function and brain structure

The cognitive function was assessed by Addenbrooke’s Cognitive Examination-Revised (ACE-R) [26] at F4 (N = 1334), one of the most widely used tools for cognitive testing and dementia screening [27]. ACE-R comprises five cognitive domains with a total score of 100, including attention/orientation (18 points), memory (26 points), fluency (14 points), language (26 points), and visuospatial (16 points). Higher scores indicate better cognitive performance. Dementia and mild cognitive impairment (MCI) were defined based on the ACE-R score, with a score < 73 indicating dementia and 73–79 indicating MCI, according to previous studies using the Chinese version of Addenbrooke’s cognitive examination [28, 29].

Among the included 1334 participants for longitudinal metabolic disturbance subtype identification, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was performed on the brains of 752 participants at F4. Detailed information on MRI acquisition can be found in our previous paper [30]. Briefly, 3D T1-weighted structural brain images were obtained using the magnetization-prepared rapid acquisition gradient echo sequence on a 3.0 T scanner (MAGNETOM Skyra, Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany). Grey matter tissue was first segmented from T1-weighted structural brain images to obtain partial volume estimates, along with intracranial volume. The native grey matter maps were subsequently nonlinearly normalized to the Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI152) standard space and further aligned to a study-specific template generated using the DARTEL module in Statistical Parametric Mapping software (SPM12; The Welcome Department of Imaging Neuroscience, London) to account for local expansion. The resulting normalized and modulated images were smoothed using an isotropic Gaussian kernel of 8 mm. Regional volumetric segmentation was then performed using the automated anatomical labeling (AAL116) atlas as the predefined region-of-interest masks. The structural brain images were processed and analyzed using MATLAB version R2020b (The MathWorks Inc., Natick, MA). In this study, we selected the volumes of grey matter, white matter, and subcortical regions, including thalamus, caudate, putamen, pallidum, hippocampus, and amygdala, as they have been reported to be associated with cognitive function [31, 32]. These brain structural volumetric markers were standardized into Z-scores.

Proteomics profiling and preprocessing

Details of serum proteomics profiling, raw data preprocessing, and quality control can be found in our previous publications [13, 33, 34]. Briefly, the serum samples were randomly divided into 178 and 132 batches (plates) in the discovery and validation set, respectively. Each batch contained two biological replicates and one quality control (QC) sample (pooled serum samples) to ensure the stability of the profiling process. Biological replicates were generated by processing 526 randomly selected serum samples twice. MS-QC samples (pooled serum peptides) were injected before each batch to ensure the instrument was in good condition. Peptides were analyzed by sequential window acquisition of all theoretical mass spectra (SWATH)-MS using TripleTOF 5600 system (SCIEX, CA, USA) coupled to Eksigent NanoLC 400 System (Eksigent, Dublin, CA, USA) [35]. Some randomly selected batches were analyzed 2–3 times as technical replicates. The obtained MS files were then analyzed by DIA-NN (1.8) against a serum spectral library containing 5102 peptides and 819 unique proteins from the Swiss-Prot database of Homo sapiens [35, 36]. Proteins were automatically quantified by DIA-NN, in which protein inference was set to the protein names (from the FASTA file), and the cross-run normalization was specified as ‘RT-dependent’. As proteomic data were obtained from MS-based qualification, the protein values represent relative protein abundances.

To ensure high-quality proteome data, we excluded MS data with protein identifications < 75% of the median value and excluded proteins with missing values in more than 80% of samples. Proteins that were detected in both the discovery and validation sets were retained for downstream analyses, resulting in 411 overlapping proteins. Batch (the smallest unit of samples entering the instrument) effects were removed using autoComBat [13, 37]. Serum samples from the discovery and validation sets were prepared, profiled, and preprocessed separately to ensure the independence of the validation procedure and prevent potential information leakage between the two datasets. As it was not feasible to profile the large number of serum samples (N = 2740) all at once in the discovery set, these samples were profiled at three time points (730, 1285, and 725 samples, respectively; hereafter referred to as the inner sequencing batch). For 288 MS-QC samples, 512 biological replicates, and 5317 technical replicates in the discovery set, the median Pearson correlation coefficient of any two sets of QC samples or replicates was 0.94, 0.97 and 0.96, respectively; for 213 MS-QC samples, 338 biological replicates, and 3432 technical replicates in the validation set, the median Pearson correlation coefficient was 0.93, 0.96, and 0.95, respectively, suggesting high consistency and reproducibility of our proteomics workflow. Proteins with missing values were imputed using half the minimal value in our data (the median [interquartile range] missing rate: 7.4% [0.1%-33.1%]; missing rates for all included proteins shown in Additional File 2: Table S2), given most of the missing values occurred when protein levels were below the detection threshold (1.5e-05 in the discovery set and 9.62e-04 in the validation set) [33, 38–40]. Protein abundances were log-transformed and standardized into Z-scores for all samples across different time points, separately for the discovery and validation sets.

Metabolomics profiling and preprocessing

We applied targeted metabolomics profiling for serum samples using the Q300 Metabolite Assay Kit (Human Metabolomics Institute [HMI], Inc., Shenzhen and Shanghai, China) [41], enabling absolute quantification of > 300 small molecule metabolites covering > 12 biochemical classes and commonly used in recent studies [42–44]. The targeted metabolites (unit: µmol/L) were profiled using ultra-performance liquid chromatography coupled to tandem mass spectrometry (UPLC-MS/MS) (ACQUITY UPLC-Xevo TQ-S, Waters Corp., Milford, MA, USA), according to a published assay protocol [41]. Briefly, serum samples were vortexed vigorously with ice-cold methanol containing internal standards, and the supernatant was collected. Then, the extracts were diluted with ice-cold 50% methanol and centrifuged at 4000 × g, and the resulting supernatant containing internal standards was transferred and sealed prior to UPLC–MS/MS analysis. Metabolomic profiling was performed using a C18 analytical column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.7 µM) maintained at 40 °C. The mobile phase consisted of solvent A (water with 0.1% formic acid) and solvent B (acetonitrile: IPA, 90:10). QC samples were prepared by pooling aliquots from all study samples and injected after every 14 samples to monitor analytical stability. Raw data generated by UPLC-MS/MS were processed using QuanMET software (v2.0) for peak integration, calibration, and metabolite quantification. Metabolites were annotated at Metabolomics Standards Initiative (MSI) level 1 confidence through comparison with the standards of targeted metabolites.

We performed repeated-measured metabolomics profiling (196 metabolites) at Shenzhen Huiyun Medical Laboratory (Shenzhen, China; a subsidiary of HMI, Inc.), whereas the cross-sectional metabolomics profiling had been previously performed at MetaboProfile Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China; a subsidiary of HMI, Inc.) [42]. Metabolites with missing values were imputed by half the minimal value in the non-missing samples (the median [interquartile range] missing rate: 0.0% [0.0%-0.6%]; missing rates for all included metabolites shown in Additional file 2: Table S3). All metabolites were log-transformed and standardized to zero-mean and unit-variance for all samples integrated from different time points, separately for the repeated-measured metabolome data and cross-sectional metabolome data.

Statistical analysis

We used the latent class growth mixed (LCGM) model to identify metabolic disturbance trajectory classes based on the total metabolic health score (see the previous “Measurement of metabolic health-related metrics, other metabolic traits and diseases, and covariates” section) across four cohort visits. In the LCGM model, the total metabolic health score was modeled as a function of age (centered by subtracting the mean age and scaled by dividing by 10 for computational efficiency and to avoid multicollinearity) using a linear mixed model, and individuals were classified into heterogeneous latent subgroups through a multinomial logistic model [45]. The optimal model was selected from the linear, quadratic, and cubic models with 1–6 potential subgroups and determined by the Bayesian information criterion (model fit information shown in Additional file 2: Table S4). Participant assignment was based on the posterior class-membership probabilities calculated using Bayes theorem, which were derived from the likelihood of each participant’s complete longitudinal data across four visits. Each participant was assigned to the subgroup with the highest posterior probability. The LCGM model was implemented using R package lcmm (version: 2.1.0).

To interpret the identified subgroups in terms of metabolic traits and diseases and to better understand their clinical significance, we assessed the differences in the distributions of metabolic traits (BMI, WC, WHR, TC, TG, LDL-C, HDL-C, SBP, DBP, FBG, HbA1c, fasting insulin, and HOMA-IR) and the prevalences of metabolic diseases (T2D, hypertension, obesity, and MetS) between the metabolically unhealthy aging group (MUAG) and metabolically healthy aging group (MHAG) identified by the LCGM model using the Wilcoxon test and Chi-square test, respectively, at each visit. The above analyses were also conducted in subpopulations with T2D, hypertension, obesity, and MetS, respectively.

We then investigated whether traditional metabolic health-related factors (BMI, FBG, TC, TG, HDL-C, LDL-C, SBP, and DBP) could predict MUAG status at baseline, aiming to provide a practical tool to identify MUAG status using routinely measured metabolic indicators at earlier ages, especially in settings where repeated-measured data are not available. The baseline data within the MUAG and MHAG groups were randomly divided into the discovery (70%) and validation (30%) sets. In the discovery set, a logistic regression model was used to construct the prediction model, and its performance was evaluated using the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve and area under the curve (AUC) in the entire validation set and subgroups by age (≥ mean age and < mean age) and sex, as well as among those without T2D, hypertension, obesity, and MetS.

We explored whether MUAG could affect the cognitive function compared with MHAG. We used linear regression to examine the association between metabolic disturbance subtypes and ACE-R scores (measured at F4), adjusted for age, sex, education, income, smoking status, alcohol status, physical activity, and total energy intake. The difference in the proportions of participants with dementia, MCI and normal cognition between MUAG and MHAG was tested by Chi-squared test. The associations between metabolic disturbance subtypes and brain structural volumetric markers were assessed using linear regression, adjusted for the same covariates mentioned above, with additional adjustment for total intracranial volume.

We identified serum proteins that were associated with the metabolic disturbance subtypes based on repeated-measured proteome data. Subsequently, in the discovery set, we explored the associations of the above-identified replicable proteins with metabolic traits using repeated measurements, including BMI, WC, WHR, TC, TG, LDL-C, HDL-C, SBP, DBP, FBG, HbA1c, fasting insulin, and HOMA-IR, using a linear mixed-effects model adjusted for time-varying age, sex, education, income, smoking status, alcohol status, physical activity, total energy intake, and inner sequencing batch. Logistic regression was used to examine the prospective associations between baseline metabolites and incident metabolic diseases, adjusted for age and sex, given the small number of incident metabolic disease cases (NT2D =27; Nhypertension =38; NMetS =42). The associations with incident obesity were not analyzed, as no cases of incident obesity were observed.

The associations obtained from the discovery and validation sets were then pooled by the fixed-effects meta-analysis using R package metafor (version: 4.6-0).

We performed a one-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis using the 2-stage least squares method to test the causal relationships between the above-identified protein-metabolite associations, with age, sex, the first 5 principal components of genetic data, and inner sequencing batch (included in the discovery set, not in the validation set) included as covariates in linear mixed-effects models. Detailed descriptions of the genetic data in our cohort and the genome-wide association analysis of serum proteins can be found in our previous paper [33]. To increase the number of genetic instrumental variables, independent protein quantitative trait loci with a lenient significance threshold of P < 1 × 10− 5 were used, similar to previous MR analyses [49–51]. The one-sample MR analysis was first conducted in the discovery set, and significant associations (FDR < 0.05) were then replicated in the validation set. The protein-metabolite network, incorporating both identified repeated-measures and causal associations, was visualized using Cytoscape [52], in which the betweenness centrality score of each node in the network was calculated. All statistical analyses were conducted in R (version: 4.4.2).

Results

Participant characteristics and longitudinal metabolic disturbance subtypes

Among the included 1334 study participants with four visits, the mean age was 57.5 (standard deviation [SD]: 5.2) years (30.7% men) when they were enrolled in this study (Table 1). Over a median follow-up of 12.4 (range: 8.0–14.7) years, we identified two trajectory groups (LCGM model fit information shown in Additional file 2: Table S4): one group showing relatively persistent metabolic unhealthiness (termed the metabolically unhealthy aging group [MUAG]; N = 270) and another demonstrating relatively persistent metabolic healthiness (termed the metabolically healthy aging group [MHAG]; N = 1064) (Fig. 2A). The fixed effect of age was significantly associated with metabolic health score in MHAG (P < 0.001) but not in MUAG (P = 0.321). The distributions of baseline sociodemographic and lifestyle factors were similar between MUAG and MHAG (Table 1).

Compared with MHAG, MUAG exhibited lower BMI, lipids, glucose, and BP scores at all four visits (except for lipids score at F4; P < 0.05; Fig. 2B). We further interpreted the identified MHAG and MUAG in terms of metabolic traits and diseases to better understand their clinical significance. We found that almost all metabolic traits (except for TC and LDL-C at F4) were differently distributed between MHAG and MUAG at all visits. Specifically, compared with MHAG, MUAG presented higher levels of adiposity indicators (BMI, WC, and WHR), lipids (TG, TC, and LDL-C), blood pressure (SBP and DBP), and blood glucose-related traits (FBG, HbA1c, fasting insulin, and HOMA-IR), as well as lower HDL-C levels (P < 0.05; Fig. 2C-F). Meanwhile, MUAG had higher prevalences of major metabolic diseases, including T2D, hypertension, obesity, and MetS, compared with MHAG at all visits (Fig. 2G).

Subgroup analysis among participants with T2D, MetS, hypertension, or obesity showed that MUAG exhibited lower metabolic health-related metrics (BMI, lipids, glucose, and BP scores), higher levels of several unfavorable metabolic traits (e.g., adiposity-related, lipids-related, and glucose-related traits), lower HDL-C levels, and higher prevalences of other metabolic diseases at most visits, compared with MHAG (P < 0.05; Additional file 1: Fig. S1 for T2D, Additional file 1: Fig. S2 for MetS, Additional file 1: Fig. S3 for hypertension, and Additional file 1: Fig. S4 for obesity). In particular, among participants with T2D, those in MUAG had lower glucose scores at F2-F4, higher FBG levels at F2-F4, and higher HbA1c levels at F2 and F4, compared with those in MHAG (Additional file 1: Fig. S1), suggesting two subgroups of T2D with differing severities. Similarly, for participants with hypertension, MUAG presented lower BP scores at baseline and F2, and higher DBP levels at all four visits than MHAG (Additional file 1: Fig. S3), indicating two subgroups of hypertension.

We then evaluated whether traditional metabolic health-related factors (BMI, FBG, TC, TG, HDL-C, LDL-C, SBP, and DBP) could predict MUAG status at baseline. We randomly divided the baseline data within the MUAG and MHAG groups into discovery (70%) and validation sets (30%), constructed a logistic model including the aforementioned risk factors in the discovery set, and evaluated the prediction performance in the validation set. Traditional metabolic health-related factors could effectively predict MUAG status at baseline among all enrolled participants and the subgroups stratified by age and sex, as well as among those without major metabolic diseases (T2D, hypertension, obesity, and MetS), with AUC ranging from 0.92 to 0.96 (Fig. 2H).

Associations of MUAG with cognitive function and brain structural markers

Given the contribution of metabolic dysfunction to cognitive decline and brain aging [7, 8], we then explored the associations of the longitudinal metabolic disturbance subtypes with cognitive function (N = 1334) and brain structural markers (among participants with brain MRI data; N = 752) measured at F4. Participants in MUAG had lower ACE-R scores than those in MHAG (P < 0.001; Fig. 3A). In addition, MUAG had a higher proportion of participants with dementia and MCI than MHAG (15.5% vs. 9.8% for dementia and MCI; P = 0.011; Fig. 3B). For brain structural markers measured by MRI, we found that MUAG was associated with lower volumes of brain grey matter, thalamus, caudate, hippocampus, and amygdala (FDR < 0.05; Fig. 3C).

Serum proteins associated with metabolic disturbance subtypes

We then investigated the proteomic underpinnings of the identified longitudinal metabolic disturbance subtypes among participants with proteome data (N = 1065 with 2740 measurements across up to 3 visits in the discovery set; and N = 269 with 677 measurements across up to 3 visits in the validation set). In the discovery set, we identified 70 serum proteins with differing abundances between MHAG and MUAG (main effect in the linear mixed-effects model; FDR < 0.05; Fig. 4A). The identified proteins were mainly enriched in biological processes related to immune regulation (complement activation, humoral immune response, and innate immune response) and key enzyme activity regulation (negative regulation of hydrolase activity, negative regulation of endopeptidase activity, regulation of peptidase activity, and negative regulation of catalytic activity) (FDR < 0.05; Fig. 4B). Among the 70 identified differential proteins between MHAG and MUAG, 11 were replicated in the validation set, with apolipoprotein B (APOB), apolipoprotein C2 (APOC2), proteoglycan 4 (PRG4), afamin (AFM), and attractin (ATRN) upregulated, and formimidoyltransferase-cyclodeaminase (FTCD), extracellular calcium-sensing receptor (CASR), leucine rich alpha-2-glycoprotein 1 (LRG1), echinoderm microtubule-associated protein-like 6 (EML6), alpha-1B-glycoprotein (A1BG), and adiponectin (ADIPOQ) downregulated in MUAG compared with MHAG (P < 0.05; Fig. 4C). We did not observe replicable significant interactions across the discovery and validation sets when including the MUAG × age interaction term in the linear mixed-effects model, suggesting the longitudinal stability of our identified associations.

To investigate the metabolic health domains associated with the metabolic disturbance subtype-related proteins (11 validated proteins), we examined their associations with metabolic traits (discovery set: N = 1065 for BMI, TC, TG, LDL-C, HDL-C, SBP, DBP, and FBG, N = 1064 for WC and WHR, N = 1008 for HbA1C, and N = 974 for fasting insulin and HOMA-IR; validation set: N = 269 for BMI, WC, WHR, FBG, TG, SBP and DBP, N = 265 for TC, LDL-C, and HDL-C, N = 234 for HbA1C, and N = 210 for fasting insulin and HOMA-IR) and incident metabolic diseases (among participants with baseline protein measurements; N = 954 for T2D, N = 832 for MetS, N = 719 for hypertension, and N = 915 for obesity in the discovery set; N = 221 for T2D, N = 211 for MetS, N = 192 for hypertension, and N = 213 for obesity in the validation set). We identified 86 associations between the above-validated proteins and metabolic traits in the discovery set (FDR < 0.05), 39 of which were replicable in the validation set (P < 0.05; Fig. 4D). For example, AFM, upregulated in MUAG, showed positive associations with BMI, WC, WHR, TG, blood glucose, and insulin resistance. Conversely, LRG1, downregulated in MUAG, was inversely associated with BMI, WHR, TC, TG, and insulin resistance. Additionally, we identified 19 prospective associations between proteins and incident metabolic diseases (FDR < 0.05), and six of them were replicable in the validation set (P < 0.05; Fig. 4E). For example, AFM, upregulated in MUAG, was positively associated with incident T2D and MetS, while ADIPOQ, downregulated in MUAG, was inversely associated with incident T2D and MetS.

Serum metabolites associated with metabolic disturbance subtypes

Subsequently, we explored the metabolic underpinnings of the longitudinal metabolic disturbance subtypes among participants with repeated-measured metabolome data (N = 584 with 2330 measurements across 3 ~ 4 visits). We identified 31 differential serum metabolites between MHAG and MUAG (main effect in the linear mixed-effects model; FDR < 0.05; Fig. 5A). The identified metabolites were significantly enriched in valine, leucine and isoleucine biosynthesis, citrate cycle, alanine, aspartate and glutamate metabolism, lipoic acid metabolism, and glyoxylate and dicarboxylate metabolism pathways (Fig. 5B). We did not observe significant interactions when including the MUAG × age interaction term in the linear mixed-effects model, suggesting that our identified associations were longitudinally stable. We then validated the identified associations in the cross-sectional metabolome data (N = 346 measured at F2 or F3). Among the 31 above-identified metabolites, 26 were present in the cross-sectional metabolome data, and 17 of them were validated (P < 0.05; Fig. 5C).

To explore which aspects of metabolic health are associated with the above-identified metabolites (31 metabolites identified in the repeated-measured metabolome data) linked to metabolic disturbance subtypes, we evaluated their associations with metabolic traits (N = 584 for all traits in the discovery set; N = 346 for BMI, HbA1C, TC, TG, LDL-C, HDL-C, SBP and DBP, N = 345 for WC, WHR, and FBG, N = 278 for fasting insulin, and N = 277 for HOMA-IR in the validation set) and incident metabolic diseases (among participants with baseline metabolite measurements; N = 563 for T2D, N = 496 for MetS, N = 429 for hypertension, and N = 546 for obesity in the discovery set; N = 321 for T2D, N = 265 for MetS, and N = 211 for hypertension in the validation set, no cases of incident obesity were observed in the validation set). The results above not only identified proteins and metabolites associated with metabolically unhealthy aging, but also provided potential functional insights into how these molecules are linked to specific domains of metabolic dysfunction.

Protein-metabolite network associated with metabolic disturbance subtypes

For the above-identified proteins (11 proteins) and metabolites (31 metabolites) associated with the metabolic disturbance subtypes, we investigated the potential protein-metabolite network by integrating repeated-measured proteome and metabolome data among participants with both data types (N = 468 with 1291 serum samples collected across up to 3 visits in the discovery set; and N = 116 with 307 serum samples collected across up to 3 visits in the validation set). We identified 117 associations between proteins and metabolites in the discovery set (FDR < 0.05), 26 of which were replicable in the validation set (P < 0.05; Fig. 6A). One-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis further identified 19 potential causal protein-metabolite associations from 26 associations in the discovery set (FDR < 0.05), 9 of which were replicated in the validation set (P < 0.05; Fig. 6B).

Discussion

In this longitudinal Chinese cohort with four visits, we identified two distinct metabolic disturbance subtypes, i.e., MHAG and MUAG, characterized by differing distributions of metabolic traits and varying prevalences of metabolic diseases. MUAG was associated with cognitive impairment and smaller volumes of several brain structural markers. We identified serum proteins and metabolites associated with the identified metabolic disturbance subtypes and constructed a protein-metabolite network. To our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate longitudinal metabolic disturbance patterns during aging, evaluate their potential impact on cognitive function and brain structural markers, and unravel their proteomic and metabolic underpinnings.

Our study identified two distinct longitudinal metabolic disturbance patterns during aging, with one pattern (MUAG) being metabolically less healthy than the other (MHAG). While previous studies identified metabolic multimorbidity patterns based on cross-sectional data [10, 11], our study revealed long-term metabolic disturbance trajectories, in the context of multimorbidity, during aging by utilizing a longitudinal cohort with a median 12.4-year follow-up over four visits. MUAG was characterized by higher levels of unfavorable metabolic traits and lower levels of favorable metabolic traits, as well as higher prevalences of major metabolic diseases (T2D, hypertension, obesity, and MetS), compared with MHAG. Notably, among participants with specific metabolic diseases (e.g., T2D and hypertension), those in the MUAG exhibited more severe disease-related metabolic risk factors than those in MHAG, suggesting our identified metabolic disturbance patterns may uncover the underlying subgroups of metabolic diseases. The lifestyle factors, including smoking status, alcohol intake, and physical activity, were not associated with the identified metabolic disturbance subtypes in our cohort (P > 0.05). As metabolic health is a multifactorial phenotype influenced by multiple factors besides lifestyle behaviors, the metabolic disturbance subtypes may be determined by other factors, such as genetics and gut microbiota, two main modulators for the metabolome [51, 53], warranting further investigation. The constructed prediction model, which can efficiently predict MUAG status among participants regardless of age and sex, and even among those without major metabolic diseases, may serve as a screening tool for MUAG diagnosis among middle-aged and elderly adults, especially when repeated-measured data are unavailable. However, given the overlap between variables used for subtype identification and prediction model construction, as well as the use of an internal validation strategy, the prediction performance may be inflated and thus should be interpreted with caution and further independently validated in external longitudinal cohorts. Our identified longitudinal metabolic disturbance subtypes may facilitate health management and personalized intervention of the aging population with differing metabolic health risks.

Our study showed that participants in MUAG exhibited poorer cognitive function and impaired brain structure markers compared with those in MHAG. Aligned with previous studies linking metabolic dysfunction to cognitive decline [54–56], our study demonstrated an association between long-term metabolic dysregulation during aging and cognitive impairment. Furthermore, we found that long-term metabolic unhealthy aging was significantly associated with smaller volumes of grey matter, thalamus, caudate, hippocampus, and amygdala, which have been linked to different stages of Alzheimer’s disease [31]. Thus, our study highlights the importance of maintaining long-term metabolic health during aging for preserving cognitive function and brain health.

Proteomics can help uncover molecular mechanisms underlying the longitudinal metabolic disturbance subtypes and discover potential druggable targets for treating metabolic disorders. Utilizing repeated-measured proteome data, our study identified serum proteins associated with longitudinal metabolic disturbance subtypes. These proteins were primarily involved in regulating immune regulation and key enzyme activity biological processes. Complement activation, the most significantly enriched biological process, has been reported to be linked to insulin resistance, dyslipidemia, and low-grade inflammation, and implicated in the pathophysiology of obesity, diabetes, and cardiovascular diseases (CVD) [57, 58]. The regulation of enzyme activity, including hydrolase activity, endopeptidase activity, and peptidase activity, is fundamental for maintaining metabolic health [59, 60]. AFM, a human plasma vitamin E-binding glycoprotein, has been detrimentally associated with all components of MetS and T2D [61], consistent with our findings. ADIPOQ, the most abundant pleiotropic adipokine, could combat inflammation, increase insulin sensitivity, mitigate atherosclerosis, and improve cardiovascular health and metabolic fitness [62, 63]. Consistently, our study showed that ADIPOQ levels were inversely associated with incident T2D and MetS, and downregulated among participants in MUAG. In addition, we identified two apolipoproteins, APOB and APOC2, which were upregulated in MUAG and were positively associated not only with blood lipids but also with adiposity-related traits and T2D (APOC2, but not APOB, was associated with T2D). Accumulating evidence has highlighted the role of APOB in assessing CVD risk [64–66]. APOC2 has been associated with T2D in the rat model and a small human study (n < 250) [67, 68]. Overall, our proteomic results reveal the potential role of complement activation and enzyme (including hydrolase, endopeptidase, and peptidase) activity regulation in metabolically unhealthy aging. The identified proteins, such as AFM, ADIPOQ, APOC2, and APOB, among others, may serve as potential therapeutic targets for treating aging-related metabolic disorders.

Metabolomics, measuring the substrates, intermedia, or end products of diverse biological processes, is a promising approach for discovering potential metabolic pathways associated with longitudinal metabolic disturbance subtypes [69]. Based on the repeated-measured metabolome data collected across four visits, we identified serum metabolites and metabolic pathways related to longitudinal metabolic disturbance subtypes. Valine, leucine, and isoleucine (branched chain amino acids [BCAA]) biosynthesis, the most enriched KEGG metabolic pathway, has been reported to have a deteriorating effect on aging and metabolic disorders [70, 71]. Our study showed that L-valine and L-leucine were positively associated with incident T2D and MetS and related metabolic traits (negatively associated with HDL-C), consistent with previous studies [70, 72, 73]. In addition, we found that higher concentrations of citrate cycle (TCA cycle) metabolites, including pyruvic acid, isocitric acid, and oxoglutaric acid, were associated with worse metabolic health. In line with our findings, prior studies have reported that higher isocitric acid concentrations are associated with lower odds of longevity and an increased risk of future T2D, CVD, and all-cause mortality [74, 75], while elevated pyruvic acid concentrations have been linked to a higher risk of T2D [75]. Our study also showed that 2-hydroxybutyric acid, the hub metabolite in the protein-metabolite network, was positively associated with incident T2D and MetS, as well as several metabolic health-related traits. Previous studies have similarly linked 2-hydroxybutyric acid to insulin resistance, T2D, and CVD [76, 77], with underlying mechanisms involving increased lipid oxidation and oxidative stress [78]. Taken together, our metabolomic results indicate that BCAA biosynthesis and the TCA cycle may play important roles in the development of metabolically unhealthy aging. The metabolites involved in these two pathways, including L-valine, L-leucine, pyruvic acid, isocitric acid, and oxoglutaric acid, as well as 2-hydroxybutyric acid, warrant further investigation as potential treatment targets for metabolically unhealthy aging.

Our study identified a protein-metabolite network associated with the metabolic disturbance subtypes. To our knowledge, this is the largest study exploring the associations between serum proteins and metabolites based on repeated-measured proteome and metabolome data [79], providing insights into the longitudinal co-variation between proteins and metabolites and their role in metabolic health. Several protein-metabolite associations may be causal, as shown in our MR analysis. In the constructed protein-metabolite network, the hub protein PRG4 was positively associated with blood lipids and insulin resistance, potentially through its influence on several metabolites, primarily amino acids, including BCAA. A previous mouse study demonstrated that Prg4 deficiency could protect against high-fat diet-induced glucose intolerance [80], supporting our observed association between PRG4 and insulin resistance. The hub metabolite 2-hydroxybutyric acid may be causally associated with AFM and ADIPOQ. AFM, ADIPOQ, and 2-hydroxybutyric acid were all associated with T2D and MetS, consistent with previous studies [61, 63, 76, 77]. We also found that AFM may be causally associated with glycine. Our study showed that glycine was inversely associated with BMI, glucose levels, and insulin resistance, findings supported by previous studies [81, 82]. AFM, as mentioned earlier as a harmful protein associated with an increased risk of MetS and T2D, may influence metabolic health by reducing glycine levels. Therefore, our identified protein-metabolite-metabolic trait/disease pathways may provide mechanistic hypotheses for metabolically unhealthy aging, warranting future experimental validations.

The major strength of this study is the longitudinal design with four cohort visits over a median follow-up of 12.4 years, enabling us to capture within-individual changes in metabolic health over time and identify metabolic health-related trajectories during the aging process. The trajectories (MUAG and MHAG) we identified remain relatively stable across follow-ups, suggesting these two subtypes reflect persistent metabolic states rather than changes driven by specific time points. The repeated measurements in our cohort design are critical to confirming the temporal stability of these patterns. Our findings suggest that a relatively long follow-up duration (e.g., over a decade) with multiple measurements is important for studying metabolically unhealthy aging. Besides the longitudinal design with repeated measurements, other strengths of our study include repeated-measured serum proteome (up to three times) and metabolome data (up to four times) and the discovery and validation strategy.

Nevertheless, we acknowledge several limitations of the current study. First, our identified differential metabolites between MUAG and MHAG based on the repeated-measured metabolome data were validated in a cross-sectional metabolome dataset. Ideally, repeated-measured metabolome data would be optimal for validating these identified metabolites. As repeated-measured metabolome data are rare in the field at this stage, our findings warrant further validation in future studies with such data. In addition, the validation rate of differential proteins identified between MHAG and MUAG was relatively low (11 out of 70 were validated), which may be attributable to the separate processing of serum samples in the discovery and validation sets that may introduce measurement noise and reduce reproducibility between the two datasets, as well as the considerably smaller sample size in the validation set (269 vs. 1065), which may limit the statistical power to detect significant associations. Thus, our findings, including the identified MUAG and MHAG subtypes, along with their associations with cognitive function and the associated serum proteins and metabolites in Chinese adults, should be further validated in other large longitudinal cohorts of racially and geographically diverse populations, with repeated-measured blood proteome and metabolome data. Second, although our identified protein-metabolite associations were replicable through internal validation, and some may be causal based on MR analysis, these associations and their links to metabolic traits/diseases should be further validated in external cohort studies or experimental studies. Third, the complete-case design, i.e., including participants with all four visits, may introduce selection bias in our study. Specifically, we found that participants who dropped out in subsequent visits had lower baseline metabolic scores (i.e., worse metabolic health; 69.84 ± 16.41 vs. 73.29 ± 15.15 for participants with and without drop-outs, respectively, P < 0.001 by t test); a greater number of drop-outs was associated with smaller baseline metabolic scores (Ptrend<0.001 by linear regression); and the mean baseline metabolic score of the included samples was slightly higher than that of the full parent samples (73.29 ± 15.15 vs. 71.04 ± 16.06; no statistical test conducted). These results suggest the possibility of selection bias arising from the preferential retention of healthier individuals in our study, which may limit the ability to detect other possible trajectory patterns in our dataset (e.g., “metabolically healthy → metabolically unhealthy” and “metabolically unhealthy → metabolically healthy”), as most participants in our cohort maintained relatively stable metabolic profiles over the follow-up period. However, we have re-run the LCGM model, including participants with at least three visits (the minimum required for the LCGM model), and similar two trajectories were identified, indicating that our results are likely valid. Nevertheless, attrition bias cannot be excluded and may have prevented the identification of additional metabolic aging groups. Fourth, dementia was diagnosed only based on ACE-R in our study, which may lead to false positives or false negatives. A comprehensive examination, including cognitive and neuropsychological tests, neurological evaluation, brain scans, laboratory tests, and psychiatric exam, should be conducted to diagnose dementia in the future.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our study identified two distinct longitudinal metabolic disturbance subtypes, MUAG and MHAG, among Chinese middle-aged and elderly adults. MUAG was associated with worse cognitive function and smaller volumes of brain grey matter, thalamus, caudate, hippocampus, and amygdala. We identified serum proteomic and metabolomic underpinnings of the two subgroups and constructed a protein-metabolite network based on the identified proteins and metabolites. Our study suggests that maintaining long-term metabolic health during aging is important for supporting cognitive function and brain health. In addition, our study may help identify individuals with metabolically unhealthy aging pattern among older adults, thereby facilitating individualized health management in aging populations. The identified proteins and metabolites may offer new insights into the underlying mechanisms and inform the development of novel therapeutic targets for metabolically unhealthy aging.
