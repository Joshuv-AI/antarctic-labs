Coats-like exudative vasculopathy in a patient with Bardet-Biedl syndrome☆
Authors: Eiji Kishi, Tadashi Yokoi, Hitomi Akitomi, Mizuki Asano, Yukiko Kuroda, Takashi Koto, Makoto Inoue
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeWe report a case of Bardet-Biedl syndrome (BBS) complicated by bilateral Coats-like exudative vasculopathy with exudative retinal detachment and neovascular glaucoma (NVG).ObservationsA 10-year-old boy noticed decreased visual acuity and was referred to our clinic. He had a history of retinitis pigmentosa, renal dysfunction, obesity, and a delay in mental development at 4 years of age. Genetic testing confirmed BBS with compound heterozygous variants detected in the BBS2 gene. His visual acuity was light perception in both eyes, and the intraocular pressure (IOP) was 14 mmHg in the right eye and 35 mmHg in the left eye. Fundus examination revealed severe exudative changes in both eyes with vitreous hemorrhage in the left eye. In the right eye, fluorescein angiography showed limited retinal vascular circulation and a nonperfused peripheral area in the equatorial region with multiple sites of neovascularization. Panretinal photocoagulation was performed on the right eye, and vitrectomy was performed on the left eye for the NVG. The left eye had proliferative membranes throughout the periphery. Postoperatively, the IOP of the right eye was elevated accompanied by iris rubeosis. Aflibercept was injected Intravitreally and lens-sparing vitrectomy was performed on the right eye with additional endophotocoagulation. One month later, the visual acuity remained at light perception in both eyes. The IOP was well controlled with topical medication at 18 mmHg for the right eye and 20 mmHg for the left eye.Conclusions and importanceThe Coats-like exudative lesions associated with eyes with the BBS2 gene variants and retinal vascular occlusion likely led to the NVG.

Introduction

Bardet-Biedl syndrome (BBS) is a rare genetic disorder with extensive variations in the signs and symptoms. It is categorized as a syndromic form of retinitis pigmentosa and is characterized by retinal degeneration, obesity, reduced renal function, polydactyly (extra digits on the hands or feet), and numerous other features.1, 2, 3, 4, 5 The signs and symptoms of BBS vary among the affected individuals, and even within the same family. A decrease in the vision is a major characteristic of BBS, and it is often accompanied by early-onset and severe rod-cone dystrophy.1, 2, 3, 4, 5 Difficulties with night vision become apparent by middle childhood, followed by scotomas that develop in the peripheral visual fields.3, 4, 5 With time, these scotomas enlarge and merge, resulting in the loss of peripheral vision and the development of tunnel vision. Most individuals with BBS also develop blurred central vision and poor visual acuity and can become legally blind by adolescence or early adulthood. While there are more than 20 genes associated with BBS, the underlying cause, regardless of gene, is the malfunction of the primary cilia, a key component of cellular communication.4 Thus, BBS is categorized as a ciliopathy or a disorder of the cilia. The causative genes for BBS are related to a dysfunction of the cilia, especially the BBS1 and BBS10 genes. Other causative genes for BBS include BBS2, BBS4, BBS5, BBS7, BBS9, BBS12, ARL6, MKKS, CEP290, MKS1, TRIM32, TTC8, and IFT27, and variants of these genes can also cause retinitis pigmentosa.5,6

Coats-like exudative vasculopathy has been described in eyes with inherited retinal dystrophy, including retinitis pigmentosa and Leber congenital amaurosis that are associated with variants of the CRB1 gene.7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15 The characteristics of these two retinal disorders are believed to overlap pathologically, but the mechanism by which retinal dystrophy leads to Coat-like changes remains undetermined.

We report a case of BSS with Coats-like exudative vasculopathy and severe retinal vascular occlusions. These changes led to NVG that was treated with vitreous surgeries and extensive photocoagulation.

Findings

A 10-year-old boy was examined in our clinic. His parents reported that an earlier physician noted retinitis pigmentosa, renal dysfunction, obesity, and a delay in mental development at 4 years of age. The electroretinograms were non-recordable. Genetic testing was performed as follows. TruSight One (Illumina) panel sequencing targeting 4813 genes associated with Mendelian disorders was performed as a laboratory-developed test at the Kanagawa Children's Medical Center, with a mean depth of coverage of 56.8× per base, and 94.3% of the coding regions were covered by at least 10 reads. Panel sequencing identified possible compound heterozygous variants in BBS2 (NM_031885.4:c.1062C > G p.(Asn354Lys)/NM_031885.4:c.407C > A p.(Ala136Glu)). The allele frequencies were 0.0000007 (1/1,461,890) and 0.000005 (8/1,461,750) in the gnomAD v4.1 exome database, respectively.

Sanger sequencing confirmed that c.407C > A was inherited from the heterozygous mother, whereas c.1062C > G was not detected in the mother. Biallelic inheritance could not be confirmed because the paternal sample was unavailable.

According to the 2015 American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics guidelines for variant interpretation, both variants were classified as “likely pathogenic” (PS1+PM2+PP3+PP4 for c.1062C > G; PM3+PM5+PP3+PP4 for c.407C > A).

The renal dysfunction was being treated by peritoneal dialysis, but otherwise, he was followed by observation alone. One month before presentation at our hospital, the boy noted a decrease in his visual acuity, leading to referral to our hospital.

At our initial examination, his visual acuity was light perception in both eyes, and the intraocular pressure (IOP) was 14 mmHg in the right eye and 35 mmHg in the left eye. Fundus examination revealed severe exudative changes in both eyes with vitreous hemorrhage in the left eye (Fig. 1). Fluorescein angiography showed retinal vascular occlusions and areas of nonperfusion that extended from the equatorial region to the periphery of the right eye. These changes were accompanied by neovascularizations on the optic disc and in multiple peripheral areas. Optical coherence tomographic images showed an exudative retinal detachment in the right eye and peripheral anterior synechia in the left eye (Fig. 2). Photocoagulation of the peripheral retina of the right eye was performed, and vitrectomy was performed on the left eye under general anesthesia. The left eye had proliferative membranes throughout the periphery that made the differentiation of them from the retina difficult. Nevertheless, photocoagulation was applied as extensively as possible to the periphery after lensectomy, and silicon oil was injected to tamponade the retina. The day after the surgery, the right eye developed an elevation of the IOP accompanied by rubeosis of the iris. NVG was diagnosed, and aflibercept was injected intravitreally 3 days after the surgery. After the IOP in the right eye was reduced, a lens-sparing vitrectomy was performed one month later. A posterior vitreous detachment was created during the surgery, and additional photocoagulation was applied to the most peripheral areas. One month postoperatively, the visual acuity in both eyes remained at light perception, and the IOP was 18 mmHg in the right eye and 20 mmHg in the left eye with topical medication (Fig. 3A). A second vitrectomy was performed on the right eye to remove the vitreous hemorrhage and to treat the tractional retinal detachment (Fig. 3B). A lensectomy was required to remove a retrolental fibrin formation, and extensive laser photocoagulation up to the pars plana, and silicone oil tamponade were applied. Under the silicone oil, the retina was reattached in the right eye and partially reattached in the left eye (Fig. 3C and D). The BCVA was hand motion in the right eye and light perception in the left eye at postoperative 8 months, and the IOP was 13 mmHg in the right eye and 14 mmHg in the left eye with topical medication.

Discussion

Coats-like exudative vasculopathy in eyes diagnosed with BSS has been reported to be caused by variants of the CEP290 gene.16,17 Because of this, it was not unexpected that Coats-like retinal changes can occur in BBS2 which is associated with and conjugates with disorders of the cilia. After conducting a literature search in PubMed, Google Scholar, and Scopus using the keywords “Coats-like” and “BBS2” on October 29, 2025, we have not found any prior reports of Coats-like exudative vasculopathy caused by BBS2 variants. The CEP290 gene is related to the location of the ciliary transition zone, and it interacts with nephrocystin-5 which in turn binds BBS2 and other BBSome components that are critical for maintaining the composition and signaling of the ciliary membrane.5 A loss or dysfunction of BBS2 or CEP290 disrupts the BBSome assembly, impairs ciliary entry of the complex, and leads to defective delivery of signaling molecules related to ciliary function, such as Smoothened and Rab8a.18,19 These defects contribute to the pleiotropic clinical manifestations, including retinitis pigmentosa in Bardet-Biedl syndrome. Given the co-function between CEP290 and BBS2, it is reasonable to conclude that BBS2 variants may also lead to a similar Coats-like exudative vasculopathy as observed in BBS patients carrying CEP290 variants. However, the precise trigger for Coats-like exudative vasculopathy remains unknown even in cases of retinitis pigmentosa caused by various gene variants.1, 2, 3, 4, 5.

Although speculative, Coats-like vasculopathy in our patient may arise from endothelial ciliary dysfunction. Endothelial primary cilia in blood vessels function as mechanosensors of fluid shear stress through polycystin-1, and impaired polycystin-1-mediated ciliary mechanosensing may disrupt Ca2+/NO signaling and endothelial homeostasis, thereby contributing to vascular instability.20 Although the supporting data were derived from the developing mouse retina, endothelial cilia have been shown to stabilize newly formed vascular connections under low to intermediate shear stress, enhance responsiveness to BMP9, a vascular quiescence and stabilization signal, and prevent premature, random retinal vessel regression. Supporting the requirement for intact endothelial cilia, endothelial-specific deletion of Ift88 (intraflagellar transport protein 88), a gene required for ciliogenesis, disrupts this protective process.21 Additional evidence from islet endothelial models suggests that primary cilia may also modulate VEGF-A/VEGFR2-dependent angiogenic and barrier signaling.22 Since the BBSome, including BBS2, regulates ciliary membrane-protein trafficking,5 BBS2 dysfunction could plausibly impair endothelial homeostasis, thereby promoting vascular closure, fragility, exudation, and Coats-like vasculopathy.

In our case, fundus examination revealed severe neovascular proliferation due to vascular occlusions which were detected at the initial examination. Both eyes exhibited severe Coats-like exudative changes, and despite photocoagulation, the condition rapidly progressed to NVG. Because Coats-like exudative vasculopathy in retinitis pigmentosa typically presents around puberty following a period of silent progression, it may be possible to control the condition with thorough photocoagulation or combined anti-VEGF therapy without surgical intervention. Because BBS patients invariably show intellectual disability and often visit at a later stage of the disease process with severe reduction of vision, regular examinations searching for possible acute progression is essential. This may prevent the blindness from Coats-like exudative vasculopathy in patients with BBS.

Although the visual prognosis was poor due to the severe retinopathy and NVG, our sequential vitrectomy with extensive laser photocoagulation helped to preserve the eye and possible hand motion or light perception. These preservations are considerably supportive for such BBS patients and their families.23

In the right eye with an attached and posteriorly perfused retina, intensive laser photocoagulation followed by laser treatment after vitrectomy with lensectomy and anti-VEGF injection, led to a retinal reattachment with hand motion vision. In addition, these procedures also kept the IOP under control.

Vitrectomy with lensectomy and laser treatment to the attached periphery retina were the only option for the left eye. Unfortunately, these procedures resulted in a failure to reattach the retina, although the patient still maintained light perception under the silicone oil.

Given these surgical results from both eyes with different degrees of severity, earlier stages of this Coats-like exudative vasculopathy seem to be manageable even though vitrectomy was necessary. Extensive laser photocoagulations including the most peripheral retina, achieved by vitrectomy with lensectomy under the supportive anti-VEGF injections, may result in preserving vision. If severe vascular occlusive lesions coexist, NVG is inevitable and additional procedures like glaucoma-filtering surgery, e.g., Ahmed tube implantation or ciliary body photocoagulation, should be considered.

This study has a limitation of being a study of one case which makes it difficult to draw strong conclusions. However, these cases are rare, and we await other studies to confirm or disagree on the usefulness of our technique to treat similar cases.

Conclusions

Coats-like exudative vasculopathy and retinal vascular occlusion will likely lead to retinal detachment with severe retinal ischemia and NVG in cases of BBS caused by the BBS2 variants. We recommend that these eyes be monitored carefully and be treated with anti-VEGF therapy and extensive laser photocoagulation.
