Biophysical modeling for accurate T cell specificity prediction of viral and tumor antigens
Authors: Zahra S. Ghoreyshi, Noah Tubo, Luca Zammataro, Xizeng Mao, Ho Ngai, Duncheng Wang, Yibin Chen, Qiuming He, Eduardo Cisneros de la Rosa, Shoudan Liang, Priya J. Koppikar, Xingcheng Lin, Jeffrey J. Molldrem, Jason T. George
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
We develop and apply a dual experimental and computational framework to predict antigen specificity of TCR sequences in serial clinical samples. Our model integrates TCR primary sequences with previously reported and in silico-derived TCR-pMHC structural data. We apply this approach in the setting of hematopoietic stem cell transplant, focusing on a collection of HLA-A*02-restricted epitopes, including the Melan-A tumor associated antigen (ELAGIGILTV), Influenza A virus M158-66-derived peptide (GILGFVFTL), and human cytomegalovirus pp65-derived peptide (NLVPMVATV). We demonstrate accurate prediction of specificity for previously uncharacterized donor- and patient-derived TCRs, wherein model performance is enhanced through sequence-based clustering and incorporation of structurally diverse templates. Our results demonstrate that structure-guided learning enables robust specificity prediction from limited training data and can generalize across sequentially obtained patient samples. This framework provides a scalable strategy for TCR specificity prediction with potential applications in immunotherapy, vaccine design, and immune monitoring.

Introduction

The ability of T cell receptors (TCRs) to selectively recognize short antigenic peptides bound to major histocompatibility complex molecules underpins a broad collection of adaptive immune responses in clinical contexts such as infection, cancer, allogeneic immunity, and autoimmunity. Accurate prediction of peptide recognition by diverse TCR repertoires remains a central challenge in computational immunology, with relevance for optimal vaccine design and the development of targeted immunotherapies. Human TCR repertoires are remarkably diverse—each comprising an estimated 108 unique clonotypes drawn from a theoretical space of up to 10191–3—and also capable of self-non-self discrimination within an extensive (~1013) peptide landscape3. This vast combinatorial complexity, therefore, necessitates the development of robust computational frameworks to complement experimental strategies aimed at understanding repertoire-level TCR specificity.

Recent significant advancements have been made in characterizing TCR recognition, driven by both the emergence of high-throughput experimental datasets1,4–8 and concurrent development of advanced computational frameworks that accurately capture TCR–pMHC interactions9–24. Among these computational approaches, structurally informed biophysical models of the TCR–peptide interaction (RACER-m) have emerged as particularly effective frameworks to understand TCR specificity18,19. While these models have demonstrated reasonable accuracy in predicting specificity for a variety of publicly available datasets, their capacity to accurately predict antigen specificity for previously unseen TCR sequences remains unstudied.

In this study, we leverage the RACER-m framework with the goal of distinguishing tumor-specific TCRs from those targeting viral epitopes. The clinical context of allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (allo-HSCT) provides an ideal source of expanded donor- and patient-derived TCR repertoires, which can be efficiently obtained and sequenced from peripheral blood or BM. Following sequencing, TCR specificity was computationally predicted and subsequently validated experimentally through affinity-driven tetramer sorting, then evaluated iteratively. To address the limitation of sparse TCR training data in a discrete ligand space, we incorporated sequences obtained from HSCT-derived repertoires, experimentally validated TCR–pMHC complexes, and additional in silico-derived TCR–pMHC structural models generated using several recent computational approaches, including AlphaFold325. Furthermore, we established peptide-specific binder distribution profiles to more accurately characterize antigen recognition. The integration of structural features and sequence-based clustering via TCRdist22,26 significantly enhanced the model’s ability to resolve the specificity of previously unseen TCRs recognizing the three HLA-A*02:01–restricted epitopes examined in this study. Our specialized approach consistently achieved high predictive accuracy (>80%) across multiple TCR test cohorts. Collectively, our results illustrate the predictive power of structurally informed models. This joint experimental and modeling approach also establishes an iterative strategy for continued model refinement through sequential incorporation of in silico-generated structural data and TCR repertoire data as they become available, which we anticipate will be of broad use in diverse clinical contexts19,27–30.

Results

Overview of the experimental and modeling framework

We developed a joint experimental and computational framework to characterize TCR specificity by integrating high-throughput tetramer-based profiling with a structure-guided biophysical model (Fig. 1). In the experimental component (Fig. 1a), peripheral blood mononuclear cell (PBMC) and bone marrow (BM) samples from healthy donors and patients were processed using DNA-barcoded peptide–MHC tetramers. Individual samples were labeled with unique molecular hashtags, pooled, and stained with a tetramer library encoding multiple antigen specificities. Tetramer-binding CD8+ T cells were isolated and subjected to single-cell sequencing to recover paired TCRα/β sequences together with their associated peptide specificities. This strategy enables scalable identification of antigen-associated TCR clonotypes while preserving the linkage between sequence and specificity at single-cell resolution.Fig. 1Schematic representation of the workflow.a Experimental setup: patient-derived and healthy donor peripheral blood mononuclear cell (PBMC) or bone marrow (BM) samples are labeled with sample-specific hashtags, pooled, and stained with a DNA-barcoded tetramer library, where each tetramer is uniquely encoded by a specific DNA barcode. Building on these experimentally derived datasets, we implemented the RACER-md framework to predict TCR–pMHC binding specificity (Fig. 1b). In the training phase, experimentally solved TCR–pMHC crystal structures, together with a limited set of in silico-generated structural templates, were used to learn residue-level interaction energetics governing TCR recognition. Because the structural diversity of available templates can limit predictive performance for previously unseen TCRs, we further incorporated an iterative update strategy in which additional in silico-derived structural templates are selectively introduced to expand coverage of diverse sequence clusters. For inference, each candidate TCR was evaluated against all peptide targets by threading the sequence onto the most similar structural templates, followed by computation of binding energies within the RACER-md model. These energies were subsequently normalized to generate peptide-specific scores, enabling assignment of antigen specificity through comparative ranking across candidate peptides. This combined framework provides a scalable and structurally grounded approach for predicting TCR specificity across diverse and previously unseen TCR repertoires.

Optimized structural biophysical modeling successfully recovers TCR specificity

To address this imbalance and also include clinically derived cancer- and viral-specific TCRs, we experimentally assessed specificity for a variety of TCRs obtained from HSCT donors and patients. Repeating this approach with inaccurate templates elsewhere yielded two distinct clusters for A2-MART1-ELA, one cluster for A2-FLU-GIL, and two clusters for A2-CMV-NLV, as visualized by hierarchical clustering (Fig. S1). Consequently, we established distinct statistical tests corresponding to each structural cluster based on the identified Z-score distributions. A2-MART1-ELA cases exhibited two empirical distributions with means of μA2-MART1-ELA,1 = 3.2 and μA2-MART1-ELA,2 = 6.8. A2-CMV-NLV cases also displayed two distinct distributions with means of μA2-CMV-NLV,1 = 1.3 and μA2-CMV-NLV,2 = 4.6. In contrast, A2-FLU-GIL cases were characterized by a single distribution with a mean of μA2-FLU-GIL = 3.2. To evaluate the robustness of our results obtained by modeling a single A2-FLU-GIL cluster, we also evaluated a two-family assignment for A2-FLU-GIL. In this setting (9 A2-FLU-GIL cases in the evaluated dataset), the ROC-AUC improved modestly from 0.94 to 0.95, with the minor subgroup exhibiting a mean Z-score of μ = 1.6, indicating limited structural separation between the two putative groups. Because this improvement was minimal and the secondary subgroup lacked clear structural resolution in the training-derived clustering, we retained the single-family A2-FLU-GIL model in the primary analysis. All distributions were normalized to have a standard deviation of σ = 1 (Fig. 2). We integrated these distributions into our model (RACER-md) by assigning each test case to the appropriate structural cluster using sequence-based similarity to the closest template. Because each structural cluster corresponded uniquely to one statistical distribution, we then performed the respective statistical test by calculating the within-cluster percentile rank. The resulting percentile rank thus quantified the relative binding strength of the TCR to each pMHC complex for the three target peptides analyzed.

To evaluate optimized model performance, we predicted the specificity of 118 HLA-A*02–restricted TCRs from an independent PBMC and BM-derived TCR cohort (MDACC_2; Table 1). The ROC curves for MDACC_2 are summarized in Fig. 3, while Table S2 reports the sensitivity, specificity, and diagnostic accuracy. The model demonstrated strong predictive performance across all three peptides, with the highest accuracy for A2-CMV-NLV (0.91), followed by A2-FLU-GIL (0.90) and A2-MART1-ELA (0.85). Sequence similarity-based clustering enables appropriate template selection in testing

Given our model’s initial predictive performance, we next aimed to develop an update policy whereby the specificities of previously obtained donor-derived TCR sequences, once experimentally confirmed, can be incorporated into model training. We thus performed a series of repeated tests of our modeling framework on additional independent datasets. The prior training and experimentally confirmed test data were incorporated into an updated training dataset (comprised of ATLAS, MDACC_1, and MDACC_2 sequences). Two additional independent test datasets (MDACC_3 and MDACC_4) from PBMC and BM-derived TCR cohorts were then used to assess the model’s ability to generalize across diverse TCR repertoires. In all subsequent test cases, any duplicate TCR sequences were removed from predictions.

In our initial test (MDACC_2), a structure-based template selection approach effectively mapped test sequences to an appropriate structure. However, when we applied this same method to additional unseen TCR cohorts (MDACC_3 and MDACC_4), our initial approach failed to maintain predictive accuracy (Figs. S3b and S4b). A careful examination of the inverse mapping (f−1: Y → X) from the test TCR sequences Y to the set of structural templates available in training X revealed sensitivity issues, wherein test sequences mapped to multiple templates in some cases, while in others, similar test sequences were mapped to disparate structures for threading. As a result, we turned to TCRdist22,26, a previously established sequence-based clustering method that leverages detailed sequence similarity metrics to partition TCR sequences into distinct clusters. Specifically, we defined an optimized clustering threshold by selecting the largest TCRdist radius around an antigen-associated (centroid) TCR for which fewer than one in one million (10−6) background TCRs are expected to fall. This threshold was determined by constructing empirical cumulative distribution functions (CDFs) for each candidate centroid TCR using both antigen-associated sequences and a specifically constructed background repertoire comprising V–J gene-matched synthetic sequences and cord blood TCRs. By selecting this threshold, we aimed to balance the capture of closely related antigen-associated TCRs while minimizing the inclusion of nonspecific, background TCR sequences. Although this approach inevitably groups together sequences with subtle structural variations, it strikes a balance between sensitivity (capturing relevant TCR sequences) and specificity (excluding unrelated background sequences).

We performed TCRdist clustering on the updated training set (ATLAS, MDACC_1, and MDACC_2) using CDR3α/β sequences and genetic features (J-gene and V-gene), available from the Immune Epitope Database 34 and germline sequenced MDACC datasets (excluding ATLAS sequences lacking J-gene and V-gene information). This clustering was then used to restrict candidate structural templates to those representing multiple closely related TCR sequences. The application of TCRdist to MDACC_3 and MDACC_4 refined template selection by filtering out singleton clusters. This method improved template selection for A2-MART1-ELA, A2-FLU-GIL, and A2-CMV-NLV, generating a prioritized list of structural templates that accurately reflected the diversity of TCR sequences (Fig. S2).

Using the updated TCRdist criteria for template matching, we then retrained our model and tested using the new test datasets. These predictions were then compared against experimentally determined ground-truth specificity for each TCR, which was also used to assess clustering accuracy within the TCRdist framework. The ROC analysis demonstrated that the model effectively distinguished binder TCRs across different epitope groups. The AUC values for A2-MART1-ELA were 0.89 and 0.80 for MDACC_3 and MDACC_4, respectively. For A2-FLU-GIL, the corresponding values were 0.69 and 0.80, respectively, while for A2-CMV-NLV, the model achieved AUC scores of 0.93 and 0.74, respectively. Notably, the limited number of A2-FLU-GIL cases in MDACC_3 and MDACC_4 may have influenced the lower reliability of the A2-FLU-GIL predictions in these datasets. While TCRdist provides a powerful clustering method based on sequence similarity and does a reasonable overall job in classifying, it can be limited whenever close sequence similarity occurs among TCRs having distinct specificity (i.e., “ambiguous sequence neighborhoods”) (Fig. 5b, d). Overall, structural modeling combined with TCRdist-based template selection can improve classification performance by helping resolve these ambiguous neighborhoods, although the effect varies based on the peptide specificity in question and clinical cohort. Incorporation of in silico-derived TCR–pMHC structures generalizes model predictions to diverse and unseen TCR test sequences

We further evaluated our approach using a final test dataset, comprising additional patient-derived PBMC and BM-derived TCRs that exceeded all previous test sets combined (“MDACC_5,” Table 1). TCR clustering revealed that this test dataset was substantially more diverse and comprised of disparate TCR sequences relative to the previous cases (Fig. S7). We hypothesized that this diversity would limit predictive efficacy in our existing framework and that the inclusion of an informed choice of additional templates would lead to improved model predictions.

To assess this possibility, our overall strategy sought to incorporate informative in silico-generated structural templates. Once identified, each case was a priori experimentally tested to confirm peptide specificity and subsequently excluded from predictive assessment. We then used these confirmed TCR–pMHC systems to create additional in silico-derived structures using AlphaFold325. In each instance, sequence data were available only for CDR3α/β and the corresponding peptide; accordingly, we incorporated the remaining Vα/β sequences from the closest available RACER-md-identified crystal structure along with HLA-A*02:01 to generate synthetic TCR–pMHC structures for these selected cases (Fig. 5a). This step was necessary because AlphaFold3 requires full-length variable region input sequences, but only partial (CDR3) sequence information was available for these cases. To avoid excluding them entirely while also only relying on data available in training, we supplied the missing Vα/Vβ framework from the most similar experimentally solved TCR structure, identified by RACER-md sequence similarity. Importantly, these reconstructed sequences were used only to generate additional in silico-derived structural templates for the training pool and were excluded from the test set, ensuring that independence of testing was preserved. In this way, the synthetic structures enriched the diversity of the training data without reusing complete template information from RACER-md in model evaluation.

We identified a small (n = 15; ~5%) subset of diversified TCRdist-clustered test TCR sequences for the creation of additional in silico-generated structural templates. Specifically, we selected TCRs from clusters containing four or more members and deliberately excluded those from highly dense ones (particularly the two central clusters in Fig. S7). Our rationale for this exclusion was based on the observation that densely populated clusters typically reflect sequences with limited variability and conserved, canonical motifs that are shared across individuals. These high-similarity TCR sequences tend to have conserved structural conformations, and as such, their structural space is likely well-represented by existing crystal structures. By excluding these clusters from our selection, our aim was to focus on less populated, more heterogeneous cases that may harbor under-explored structural configurations16,22. TCRdist sequence-based clustering analysis of this dataset (Fig. 5b) revealed a heterogeneous network of identified TCR chains. These choices were guided by our selection strategy (pink nodes), which aimed to target diverse regions of sequence space for meaningful augmentation of the available structural library accomplished with a small subset of maximally informative sequences. The ROC curve in Fig. 5c illustrates the performance of this selection procedure, yielding ROC-AUC values of 0.72, 0.85, and 0.74 for A2-MART1-ELA, A2-FLU-GIL, and A2-CMV-NLV, respectively. To further contextualize the performance of this approach, we benchmarked RACER-md against alternative state-of-the-art methods on the MDACC_5 dataset (Fig. 5d), demonstrating the improvements in accuracy of our structurally informed framework across the evaluated antigen classes.

A repeat attempt to create these predictions in the absence of additional in silico-generated structures resulted in significant reductions in overall predictive performance (Fig. S5), thereby quantifying the added benefit of utilizing an update strategy requiring minimal additional TCR information. To establish the impact of structure selection criteria on model performance, we compared the density-based selection strategy with a randomized template selection approach, whereby a random subset (10%) of TCRs from the MDACC_5 test set were selected for structure generation. This was followed by predictive assessment on the 90% hold-out cases as a consistency check. We then retrained the RACER-md model using the same training procedure as before while incorporating additional in silico-generated structural templates. The optimized model was subsequently evaluated on each of the test datasets. This strategy effectively resolved duplication between training and testing sets while enriching the training data with structurally informative cases. This random selection was repeated over six iterations to establish confidence intervals on the ROC curves derived from randomized template selection (Fig. 6a). To further assess the influence of these generated structures, we compared mutual Q heat maps for the highest- and lowest-performing selections. In the best-performing heat map (Fig. S9), the A2-MART1-ELA in silico-generated structures form a distinct and nonoverlapping cluster relative to available crystal structures. In contrast, the poorest-performing heat map (Fig. S10) shows that, within the same functional family, the in silico and experimental structures share significant structural similarity, indicating that the additional in silico structures do not effectively expand the model’s diversity of structural families available for predictions. The experimentally confirmed TCRdist-clustered specificities (Fig. 6b) illustrate the relative sequence diversity in MDACC_5. In particular, A2-MART1-ELA exhibits highly diverse TCR clusters, whereas A2-FLU-GIL and A2-CMV-NLV display patterns of limited and dense clusters. This result further reinforces the importance of including meaningful structures into the training and optimization steps to appropriately generalize the modeling frameworks to new test data.

Both of the above approaches did not explicitly take into account the sequence similarity of test cases relative to existing crystal structures. To evaluate the importance of incorporating new templates that are distinct from existing ones on prediction accuracy, we implemented a third template selection approach (Fig. S11a). Initially, we identified clusters that already contained experimental crystal structures (red nodes) and then prioritized TCRs (light blue nodes) from clusters explicitly lacking existing structures for structure generation. We then repeated the same density-dependent selection of (n = 15) ~5% of the MDACC_5 TCRs to a priori confirm their specificity (Fig. S11b). The model was then retrained and tested on the remaining data (Fig. S11c). This approach led to a slight improvement of ROC-AUC for A2-CMV-NLV, as the relative dearth of available experimental structures for this case provided an opportunity for in silico-derived structures to enhance model ability. In contrast, for A2-MART1-ELA and A2-FLU-GIL, the refined selection yielded results similar to those obtained with the (n = 30) random selection strategy and the (n = 15) representative case selection informed by cluster density and size (Figs. 5 and 6), indicating that the structural selections were largely consistent across all strategies.

Lastly, to evaluate the robustness of our approach to any particular choice of in silico structure creation method, we repeated our analysis again, this time utilizing several alternative approaches, including AlphaFold325, AlphaFold Multimer35, and Boltz-136 (Fig. S12). Repeat predictive assessments demonstrated that overall model performances across these methods were comparable, achieving average ROC-AUC scores for the MDACC_5 test set of 0.72, 0.78, and 0.71 (AlphaFold3), 0.74, 0.76, and 0.73 (AlphaFold Multimer), and 0.70, 0.76, and 0.72 (Boltz-1) for the A2-MART1-ELA, A2-FLU-GIL, and A2-CMV-NLV peptides, respectively. These results highlight that the computational strategy of in silico structural augmentation remains largely robust to any particular method of structure generation, provided that sufficient structural diversity and quality are maintained. To evaluate whether RACER-md performance depends on the structural confidence of AlphaFold-derived templates, we examined the relationship between ROC-AUC values and AlphaFold ipTM/pTM scores across augmentation rounds and peptides (Fig. S13). All generated TCR–pMHC complexes exhibited reasonably high confidence scores (>0.7), with similar mean ipTM and pTM values across rounds within a given peptide system. However, ROC-AUC varied substantially between peptides, with A2-CMV-NLV exhibiting consistently lower confidence structural predictions. Model performance on A2-CMV-NLV-specific TCRs exhibits lower AUC values when compared to those of A2-FLU-GIL, but comparable values when compared to A2-MART1-ELA-specific TCRs. It is therefore likely that structure quality differences (weakest in A2-CMV-NLV cases) and TCR diversity (highest in A2-MART1-ELA) both contribute to the observed trends in AUC values. Collectively, these results demonstrate the effectiveness of utilizing a small number of structurally informed templates to enhance model generalization to previously unseen TCRs. Additionally, iterative updating with the inclusion of relevant in silico-derived structural data can effectively augment the training of the RACER-m model, particularly in scenarios where experimental structural data are limited.

Discussion

TCR–pMHC prediction is a formidable task, owing to both the diversity of sequence space and a sparse sampling of that space in any feasible experimental approach. In this study, we aimed to reliably discern TCR specificity against tumor and viral epitopes in the setting of donor-derived hematopoietic stem cell transplant (HSCT). Model applications are wide-ranging and include, in the context of transplant, identifying tumor antigen-specific TCRs from those that expand during viral reactivation. We thus developed a biophysical modeling framework to appropriately cluster and assign TCR specificity, in addition to an update policy for improving the model as continuous additional patient TCR sequences become available and are ultimately validated.

Specificity divergence among TCRs sharing a high degree of CDR3 sequence similarity contributes to the intrinsic difficulty of accurate specificity prediction. In our cohorts, TCRdist-based analysis (e.g., Figs. 4b, d, and 6b) reveals examples of closely related clonotypes that occupy overlapping TCR sequence neighborhoods yet exhibit distinct antigen specificities. These cases complicate TCR specificity identification based on sequence similarity and offer a valuable context for integrating structural information in specificity prediction models. One primary advantage of proceeding with a structure-based approach is the extent to which biophysical interactions can be reliably learned at the level of pairwise TCR–peptide amino acid interactions, which then enable training on a relatively small set of sequences and structures. In this context, patient-derived TCR sequences provide an additional opportunity to refine this approach. Our initial training and predictions benefited from structural clustering to identify antigen-specific TCR clusters corresponding to unique peaks in the distributions of binding interactions, indicating that the RACER-md model performs well in datasets with less diverse TCR repertoires when appropriate structural templates are available. However, test datasets with significant TCR diversity required (1) additional sequence-based clustering and (2) the inclusion of further in silico-derived structures for optimal predictions. The former need arose either from an ill-defined mapping of test sequences to multiple template structures or from poor assignment of closely related test sequences to disparate structural templates.

To address these issues, we incorporated additional structural templates generated by AlphaFold3, which expanded the structural diversity within the training set and ensured better coverage of TCR clusters observed in diverse patient datasets. The TCRdist approach effectively captures sequence variations not apparent from structural similarities alone, particularly when templates are limited or TCR diversity is high. Consequently, by integrating these new templates, model predictions became more robust. We note, however, that the benefit of combining TCRdist assignment with structural modeling is not uniform across cohorts. The integrated pipeline is most helpful when sequence-only clustering yields ambiguous neighborhoods (closely related TCRs having distinct specificity), where structure-derived energetic scoring can provide an additional discriminative signal beyond sequence similarity. In contrast, when epitope-specific groups are already well resolved in sequence space, the incremental benefit of structural scoring may be limited. Moreover, because RACER-md relies on structural inputs, fixed-threshold metrics can degrade when selected templates are suboptimal or insufficiently representative, or when structure prediction introduces modeling noise. We also clarify that, as a consequence of inherent variability in sampled human T cell repertoires, the number of confirmed A2-FLU-GIL binders was limited in MDACC_2-4 (9 cases in MDACC_2, 2 cases in MDACC_3, and 2 cases in MDACC_4). A2-FLU-GIL predictions were thus significantly affected by data sparsity, and so the corresponding AUC values should, therefore, be interpreted with caution. In contrast, the larger set of A2-FLU-GIL binders in MDACC_5 ultimately allowed for a more reliable assessment of model performance. We observed notable improvements in model prediction with the incorporation of in silico-derived templates, with optimal selection yielding a significantly improved ROC curve due to broader coverage of TCR clusters. Notably, our results suggest that the RACER-md framework maintains predictive performance over a variety of structure generation methods, which is reassuring from the standpoint of consistency across structure type or clinical implementation. While we found that structural modeling can enhance discriminative power (as supported by ROC analyses), its precise impact on fixed-threshold metrics depends on template diversity, template quality and representativeness, structural prediction accuracy, and dataset size.

In this work, we benchmarked general-purpose deep learning predictors—AlphaFold3, AlphaFold Multimer, and Boltz-1—because they natively generate complete TCR–pMHC complexes under a unified, end-to-end modeling framework. In contrast, TCR-specific structure prediction tools typically model TCR chains in isolation or rely on multi-stage docking pipelines, which introduce additional user-defined steps that would complicate comparisons of structure quality in downstream biophysical energy modeling. Restricting our analysis to end-to-end complex predictors, therefore, ensured methodological consistency and preserved interpretability within the RACER-md framework. To evaluate the role of uncertainty in AlphaFold-derived structural templates on specificity prediction, we studied the relationship between RACER-md classification accuracy and AlphaFold ipTM/pTM scores across peptides and augmentation rounds and observed no systematic global correlation between structural confidence and predictive performance. Although A2-CMV-NLV templates exhibited moderately lower AlphaFold confidence and correspondingly lower AUC values, overall performance differences were more strongly associated with repertoire diversity and structural heterogeneity than with backbone confidence metrics.

In our analysis of datasets MDACC_2, MDACC_3, and MDACC_4, we initially pursued a strategy of identifying a minimal yet representative subset of structural templates capable of capturing TCR specificity. In contrast, the local diversity of MDACC_5 limited the ability to reliably predict specificity using a fixed set of templates. This finding motivated the inclusion of broader structural coverage to improve model generalizability. While these two strategies contribute opposing effects on the number of available templates, they complement one another by meeting the shared objective of selecting structural templates that best represent the distribution of TCRs within each dataset. Despite this strategy, not all included structures contributed positively to predictive accuracy. Templates exhibiting suboptimal quality or limited representativeness of TCR clusters correlated with poorer performance outcomes. Specifically, we observed an association between poorly performing templates and unfavorable Z-score distributions, suggesting that structural inadequacy—such as inaccurate modeling or poor alignment with real TCR–peptide binding conformations—can distort energetic predictions. These findings emphasize the importance of carefully selecting structural templates to balance coverage and quality, and highlight that both template diversity and accuracy are critical for improving model generalizability across patient cohorts.

In more diverse test cohorts (MDACC_3 and MDACC_4), some TCRs mapped ambiguously to multiple templates, reflecting limited structural coverage and the presence of multiple recognition geometries within a single peptide–MHC context. Although experimental validation confirmed the absence of cross-reactivity between A2-MART1-ELA, A2-FLU-GIL, and A2-CMV-NLV specificities, ambiguous mapping of sequences to structure highlights that distinct structural recognition modes can coexist even for TCRs specific for a single peptide. Further, we observed improvement in model performance when a sufficient number of templates—either randomly selected or guided by sequence-based clustering methods—were used to augment the diversity of available structures. Our framework prioritizes a relatively small set of canonical structural families for stable and interpretable predictions, but it is certainly possible that this choice may under-represent rare alternative recognition geometries, underscoring a general limitation of current TCR–pMHC modeling approaches and the possible need for broader structural coverage in future work.

Despite the above strategy, the A2-CMV-NLV test sets illustrate that performance variation cannot be attributed to sample size alone. Unlike the A2-FLU-GIL cohort, where variability in precision/recall is largely driven by limited sample size, the A2-CMV-NLV cohort contains a comparable number of cases, yet still exhibits reduced precision and accuracy in certain splits. Closer inspection indicates that this decline is associated with template selection and template representativeness: out of the 7 misclassified A2-CMV-NLV cases in test set 3, 4 were inappropriately mapped to structural template 3QDG, and 3 were inappropriately mapped to template 3HG1 as the highest-scoring structural matches. Both 3QDG and 3HG1 correspond to A2-MART1-ELA structural templates, and their selection as best matches for A2-CMV-NLV cases contributed to incorrect classification. Consequently, these cases exhibited less favorable binding-score (Z-score) distributions and poorer predictive performance.

We further acknowledge that these template-related issues were most apparent in MDACC_3 and MDACC_4, where inverse mapping of test TCRs occasionally produced ambiguous assignments, with some sequences mapping to multiple templates or to disparate structures. While the integration of TCRdist substantially reduced these inconsistencies by enforcing sequence-based clustering, it did not completely eliminate the underlying sensitivity to template quality. This highlights that clustering and structural modeling must be jointly optimized, and that incomplete or unrepresentative structural coverage can still limit predictive performance.

Antigen specificity in this study was defined by tetramer binding rather than direct functional assays. Although DNA-barcoded tetramer staining provides a robust readout on peptide–MHC binding, this measure does not always equate to functional activity, such as cytokine production or cytotoxicity. Accordingly, the RACER-md framework was designed to predict TCR binding specificity and not downstream T-cell effector function. Future efforts to incorporating functional validation assays will be important to further evaluate the precise relationship between tetramer binding and ultimate TCR function for the TCRs identified in this study.

Our analysis identified several variations in update procedures whereby additional in silico-derived structures can be incorporated for more accurate future predictions, which were primarily partitioned based on randomized vs. directed choice of additional in silico-derived structural templates selected from diverse sequence clusters. While the strategies explored here offered comparable predictive performance, each case possesses different implications on updating with respect to additional information. For example, randomization, while requiring more upfront specificity data, can be used as an unbiased method for incorporating additional in silico-derived structures in sequence. In contrast, the selection-based approach, while more efficient in requiring minimal TCR specificity, may result in suboptimal template selection relative to future TCR datasets, particularly if the distribution of sequence diversity becomes skewed in favor of a cluster for future test TCRs. In reality, the best choice is likely context-specific and requires further experimental validation supporting the initial findings herein. We remark that our approach, while achieving high predictive accuracy, still required experimental confirmation of a small sub-sampling of test cases. The principal advantage in applying our structural model is that a few carefully selected templates with sufficient diversity can recover accurate predictions on a majority of unseen test cases.

In this study, we focused on three canonical HLA-A*02:01–restricted peptides—A2-MART1-ELA (ELAGIGILTV), Influenza A M158–66 (GILGFVFTL), and A2-CMV-NLV (NLVPMVATV)—which are among the most immunodominant and well-characterized peptides in their respective biological contexts and are highly represented in public structural and repertoire datasets. Alternative epitopes from the same source proteins were not included because they are restricted to non-A*02 alleles, lack experimentally resolved TCR–pMHC structures, or are underrepresented in available datasets. Although HLA-A*02:01 is the most prevalent HLA allele and provides a consistent setting for analysis of allo-HSCT repertoires, variation across other HLA alleles can substantially influence TCR–pMHC docking geometry, binding energetics, and model interpretability. Accordingly, while the RACER-md framework is in principle allele-agnostic, consistent extension to additional HLA alleles will require the inclusion of allele-specific structural templates and training data to ensure robust and biologically meaningful predictions. Our supplementary analysis of HLA-B*07:02–restricted cases further supports the feasibility of extending this framework across HLA alleles, although comprehensive multi-allele validation remains beyond the scope of the present study (Figs. S14–S18). All patient-derived TCRs in MDACC_2–MDACC_5 were experimentally validated to recognize HLA-A*02:01–restricted peptide–HLA complexes. For patient samples, high-resolution HLA genotyping was performed as part of the standard clinical transplant workup, confirming the presence of an HLA-A*02:01 allele. Healthy donors were screened by HLA-A2 antibody staining (phenotypic typing) to confirm expression of the relevant HLA-A2 molecule, and donors contributing HLA-B*07:02–restricted repertoires were additionally confirmed to be B7-positive by fluorescence-based antibody detection. No TCRs restricted to alternative HLA alleles were included in the analysis.

Future studies could explore expanding the RACER-md framework by incorporating a broader range of peptide targets beyond those studied here. This augmented set could include a larger collection of putative tumor-associated antigens. Additionally, the inclusion of minor-histocompatibility differences (SNP polymorphisms) resulting in differences in self and donor-derived antigens could in the future, enable clinicians to add the additional criterion of minimization of GVHD in the optimal selection of a donor TCR repertoire. The integration of sequence-based and structure-based models directly into the training of the energy matrix is one future direction that could provide a more comprehensive approach to capturing the complexity of TCR–pMHC interactions. Another future direction includes leveraging additional molecular features, such as peptide-binding motifs or TCR–peptide docking simulations, to further improve prediction accuracy and extend the applicability of RACER-md to a wider array of antigen-specific immune responses. Our modeling framework in its current form can guide the identification of antigen-specific TCRs, and we anticipate that this approach can in the future, be expanded to a variety of clinical contexts involving TCR antigen specificity prediction.

Methods

All experimental data were obtained in accordance with relevant ethical regulations and approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the University of Texas MD Anderson Cancer Center (healthy donor samples: IRB protocol # 2024-1437; patient samples: IRB protocol # 2021-1013). TCRs were obtained from peripheral blood and BM samples from patients and healthy donors undergoing HSCT that were collected under previously approved protocols. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to sample collection. Participant demographic information, including age and sex, was recorded where available. Sex was determined based on clinical records; sex and gender were not considered as variables in the study design, as the primary objective was to evaluate TCR specificity independent of any demographic factors. No sex- or gender-based stratified analysis was performed. No participants received financial compensation for participation in this study.

Experimental methods

Generation of peptide:HLA-I monomers and tetramers

Peptide-loaded biotinylated HLA monomers were produced via individual refolding reactions containing recombinant HLA-A*02:01, B2M, and a fixed peptides37, or recombinant HLA-A*02:01, B2M, and a UV-exchangeable peptide38. HLA monomers were sourced from the Baylor College of Medicine Tetramer Core and Biolegend. Peptides were synthesized using standard Fmoc-chemistry and purified to >90% via LC-MS (Genscript).

Peptide:HLA-I with the desired peptide specificities were generated via UV-mediated ligand exchange, as described elsewhere38. Briefly, HLA monomer was diluted to 0.1 mg/ml in PBS and mixed 1:1 with 400 μM target peptide in PBS. The mixture was transferred to a polypropylene 96-well plate and exposed to 365 nm UV-C light for 30 min using a CL-3000 UV crosslinker (Analytik Jena), followed by overnight incubation at 4∘ C. The efficiency of UV-exchange reaction was assessed using the LEGEND MAX Flex-T Human Class I Peptide Exchange ELISA kit (Biolegend), which measures the association of biotinylated MHCI and B2M (Biolegend).

Peptide:HLA tetramers were produced by adding streptavidin-conjugates to biotinylated monomers in four serial additions, with 10-min incubations between additions, such that the final stoichiometric ratio of streptavidin to peptide:MHCI was 1:4. Subsequently, any remaining unoccupied biotin-binding sites were quenched by the addition of 50 μM soluble D-biotin. Fluorescent tetramers were made by conjugating HLA monomers with premium-grade phycoerythrin (PE) and/or allophycocyanin (APC) streptavidin conjugates (Invitrogen). DNA-barcoded tetramers were produced by linking monomers to TotalseqC-streptavidin conjugates (Biolegend). Conjugations were carried out on ice.

Tetramer staining and flow cytometry

The experimental approach included an initial cohort (MDACC_1) of TCRs isolated based on their relative abundance in pooled samples of peripheral blood or BM samples obtained from healthy donors. Cells were stained using tetramers bound with one of three HLA-A*02:01–restricted peptides: the A2-MART1-ELA peptide (ELAGIGILTV) derived from the MART-1 (Melan-A) protein, the Influenza A M158−66 peptide (GILGFVFTL) derived from Influenza A virus matrix protein 1, and the A2-CMV-NLV peptide (NLVPMVATV) derived from the UL83 phosphoprotein of human cytomegalovirus. We will refer to these peptide–HLA cases throughout as A2-MART1-ELA, A2-FLU-GIL, and A2-CMV-NLV, respectively. Cells were subsequently enriched by magnetic selection to isolate tetramer-binding populations (Table 3). The enriched cells then underwent approximately 10 days of in vitro expansion, using anti-CD3/CD28/CD2 stimulation supplemented with IL-2. This in vitro expansion step was performed only for the MDACC_1 cohort. PBMCs derived from healthy donors and patients were processed as distinct biological samples and were kept separately prior to initial labeling and staining. In experiments employing sample multiplexing, individual samples were first labeled with unique cell-surface hashtag oligonucleotides (Supplementary Data 1) and only then pooled, enabling unambiguous post hoc assignment of each cell to its original donor or patient source. For downstream computational analyses, MDACC datasets were constructed by combining T cell repertoires from distinct experimental batches as independent cohorts rather than mixed biological samples. Specifically, the aforementioned MDACC_1 comprised healthy donor and patient repertoires from batch 1. Subsequent cohorts included MDACC_2, containing patient-derived repertoires from batch 2, MDACC_3, containing healthy donor and patient repertoires from batch 3, MDACC_4, containing patient repertoires from batch 4, and MDACC_5, containing patient repertoires from batch 5.

Prior to pooling for tetramer staining, cells from different patient samples were stained with unique Totalseq-C format DNA-oligo conjugate Hashtag antibodies (Biolegend), in the presence of 50 nM dasatinib. Cells were thoroughly washed and subsequently stained on ice with 5 μg/ml fluorescent and/or DNA barcoded tetramers for 30 min. Cells were resuspended to 25 M cells/ml in staining buffer (PBS + 2% FBS + 2 mM EDTA) containing 50 nM dasatinib. Cells were stained on ice with 5 μg/ml of fluorescent and/or DNA-barcoded peptide:MHCI tetramers for 30 min. Cells were washed, and subsequently stained with a cocktail of antibodies including CD8a-Buv395 or CD8a-FITC(RPA-T8), CD3e-BV421 (OKT3), Dump-FITC or PECy7 (CD19 (HIB19), CD14 (M5E2), CD16(3G8), and in some experiments CD4 (RPA-T4)), anti-APC (APC003), and anti-PE (PE001) (see Supplementary Note Table S6 for full antibody details). The flow cytometry gating strategy is shown in Supplementary Note Fig. S19.

For sequencing experiments, live, tetramer-positive, dump-negative CD8 T cells were sorted. Cells were immediately run using the 10x Genomics 5' Single Cell Immune Profiling workflow, using the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, the sorted cells were loaded onto a lane of a Chromium Next GEM Chip G (10x Genomics) for GEM production using the Chromium controller. GEM-RT, cDNA amplification, and the following library prep were using the Chromium Single Cell V(D)J Reagent Kits User Guide (v1.1 Chemistry) with Feature Barcoding technology for Cell Surface Protein (RevG) workflow (per 10x Genomics protocols).

Cells exhibiting more than one TCRβ (TRB) chain were observed at low frequency and were evaluated in the context of known technical and biological sources. In experiments employing cell-surface hashtag oligonucleotides (batches 1–5 and S1), doublets arising from the capture of two cells within a single droplet were identified by the presence of multiple hashtags and excluded prior to downstream analysis. While doublets originating from two cells of the same sample cannot be fully eliminated by this strategy, these quality-control steps substantially reduce their frequency. Dual TRB observations, therefore, likely reflect a combination of true biological dual TCR expression, which has been reported at low frequency (approximately 1–7% of T cells)39,40, and residual technical artifacts that cannot be completely excluded in droplet-based single-cell sequencing.

After expansion, cells were stained again with DNA-barcoded tetramers, each antigen specificity uniquely encoded by a distinct combination of two DNA barcodes. In these experiments, DNA-barcoded tetramer libraries contained control tetramers in addition to those corresponding to the peptides of interest. Specifically, each TCR was assessed against five irrelevant peptide–MHC tetramers, and TCRs that were considered exhibited no detectable binding to these control tetramers. Tetramer-positive cells from the initial cohort underwent short-term in vitro expansion prior to sequencing, whereas subsequent cohorts were analyzed directly ex vivo without prior expansion. This multiplexed barcoding strategy enables stringent discrimination between specific and nonspecific interactions while preserving the biological context of antigen-driven T cell expansion. Tetramer-positive cells were pooled, sorted, and subsequently sequenced to identify paired TCR sequences along with their associated DNA barcodes. Cells were assigned antigen specificity based on binding to the correct DNA barcode combination corresponding to the tested peptide panel. A TCR clonotype was defined as antigen-specific only if greater than 90% of cells sharing that clonotype demonstrated the same barcode-defined specificity. We remark that this assignment characterizes specificity with respect to the tested peptides, but does not exclude the possibility of cross-reactivity with other, untested antigens. These TCR sequences thus represent experimentally identified antigen-associated TCRs, rather than functionally confirmed receptors.

All experimental data were generated across five independent experimental batches, each consisting of single-cell tetramer staining, magnetic enrichment (where applicable), cell sorting, library preparation, and sequencing workflows performed on different days. Each batch included five internal irrelevant tetramers to confirm the absence of nonspecific binding and cross-reactivity. Because the analyzed TCRs were derived from distinct clinical peripheral blood or BM samples from different donors and patients, biological variability is captured at the level of independent TCR clonotypes and single-cell tetramer binding events. For each TCR identified, we required >90% tetramer binding consensus amongst all cells sharing TCR sequences.

Illumina sequencing

DNA libraries, including Feature Barcode, TCR, and GEX (gene expression), were quality controlled using TapeStation D5000 high-sensitivity tape (Agilent). TapeStation bioanalyzer readout was also referenced for average DNA size calculation to determine the library loading for multiplexed sequencing. Concentration of library dsDNA was measured using a Qubit dsDNA Broad Range kit (Life Technologies, cat. Q32850). Libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 S1 reagent kit v1.5 100 cycles (Illumina 20028319) using sequencing cycles configuration of 26 + 8 + 0 + 91 (read 1 + i7 index + i5 index + read 2) for combined assessment of feature barcode library, TCR, and GEX (RNA seq) information. Sequencing depth is 7500 read pairs per cell for the feature barcode library, 7500 read pairs per cell for the TCR library.

Model development

To predict binding affinity between TCR–peptide pairs, we specialized our previously developed RACER-m model, an energy-based biophysical framework designed to quantify TCR–pMHC interactions18,19. RACER-m focuses on the CDR3α and CDR3β regions as the primary mediators of context-specific TCR–peptide binding41. We retrained and optimized the RACER-m model using 66 experimentally derived TCR–pMHC structures as the initial core structural dataset. TCR sequence used in training included 97 binder TCR–peptide pairs from the ATLAS database31, as well as additional TCR–pMHC complexes sourced from MDACC and experimentally confirmed as binders (see Table 1). For each strong-binding TCR–peptide pair, we generated an additional set of 1000 random TCR sequences to serve as nonbinding examples during training. Threading these TCR–peptide pairs through candidate structural templates selected based on sequence similarity enabled the development of an optimized energy matrix for accurately predicting TCR–peptide binding affinities in this clinical context.

To reduce redundancy and mitigate potential train-test leakage arising from closely related clonotypes, TCRs were first clustered using TCRdist based on CDR3α/β sequence similarity. Only a single representative TCR from each cluster was selected for structural modeling and inclusion in the training set. TCRs within the same cluster with high CDR3 sequence similarity (>85% in either CDR3α or CDR3β) were retained, although they comprise a rare minority of cases (<0.90%). We note that such cases can diverge in their specificity and therefore represent an important and challenging aspect of resolving TCR specificity in human repertoires.

Model training and optimization

Following the procedure outlined in refs. 18,19, we trained the energy model to maximize the energy gap, δE/ΔE, between binders and non-binders. For each binder, the average binding energy, 〈Ebinder〉, was computed, along with the mean energy of its corresponding non-binders, 〈Enon-binder〉, and the standard deviation ΔE of the non-binder energies. This approach maximizes the ratio δE/ΔE, where δE = 〈Enon-binder〉 − 〈Ebinder〉, measures the separation between binders and non-binders.

RACER-m achieves this optimization by maximizing the ratio δE/ΔE using an adapted AWSEM force field42, a residue-level protein force field widely used for investigating protein folding and interactions4,42. We utilized the protein-protein interaction component of the force field to compute contact energies specifically at the TCR–peptide interface. Specifically, Cβ atoms were employed for most residues, except for glycine, where Cα atoms were used. This calculation involves interaction weights for residue pairs and distance-based potentials to compute binding energies. For unknown TCRs, RACER-m applies template-based modeling with MODELLER43, matching test sequences to template structures based on composite similarity scores across CDR3α, CDR3β, and peptide regions (Fig. 1).

TCR–pMHC structural modeling

Since RACER-m evaluates binding energies based on contact interactions between peptides and TCRs, it relies on the availability of 3D structures for TCR–pMHC complexes. Obtaining this information for every test case is impractical, particularly when the relevant task involves characterizing specificity for subsets of the T cell repertoire that are distinct from training sequences. To address this limitation, RACER-m19 utilizes MODELLER43 to build 3D models from sequence data, which we adopt in this study. For training, the structural template library consisted of experimentally resolved TCR–pMHC crystal structures together with a limited number of high-confidence in silico complexes generated using AlphaFold3 to expand structural diversity within the template set. Importantly, these structural templates are directly associated with experimentally confirmed antigen specificities, enabling the RACER-md framework to learn residue–residue interaction energetics by contrasting known binder TCR–peptide pairs with generated nonbinding sequences. For unseen TCR sequences, structures were constructed using template-guided homology modeling with MODELLER based on the closest available structural template. While deep-learning predictors, such as AlphaFold3, can produce highly accurate individual structures, they are computationally intensive and not well-suited for modeling many pairwise TCR–pMHC combinations in large TCR repertoires. In contrast, template-based homology modeling provides a scalable and consistent framework for generating large numbers of TCR–pMHC binding predictions while preserving the structural backbone required for RACER-md’s energy calculations. This design aims to achieve predictive performance that reflects generalization of the learned biophysical energy model rather than variability introduced by case-specific structure prediction engines.

For each TCR–pMHC test pair, RACER-m computed Hamming distances separately for the peptide, CDR3α, and CDR3β sequences relative to each of the 66 template structures. Sequence similarity scores were calculated by counting the number of matching amino acids between the target and template sequences. A composite similarity score was then obtained by summing the similarity scores of the CDR3 regions and multiplying by the peptide similarity score. The template with the highest composite similarity score was selected for structure generation using MODELLER (see Fig. 1b).

Similarity-based template correction

To improve the accuracy of template selection in the RACER-m model18, we employed a two-part correction strategy based on structural and sequence similarity measures. The initial RACER-m model used a training set of 66 crystal structures and organized them into clusters using the mutual Q similarity metric32,33. Mutual Q quantifies structural similarity between 3D configurations, allowing for global comparison of two protein structures. In silico structure generation

In the RACER-m model, the relative scarcity of crystal structures was previously shown to limit accurate predictions on structurally disparate TCR–peptide systems19. We will show that this limitation was especially pronounced for patients with diverse TCR clusters recognizing a specific peptide, such as A2-MART1-ELA, where the existing crystal structures were insufficient for accurate predictions. To address this limitation, we utilized AlphaFold325 to generate additional 3D structures from a minority (~10%) of test cases, incorporating these into the training set. Specifically, from the full set \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{\mathcal{D}}}}=\{{d}_{1},{d}_{2},\ldots,{d}_{N}\}$$\end{document}D={d1,d2,…,dN} of N TCRs obtained and sequenced in a given specificity experiment, we randomly select a minority fraction (N/10) for specificity determination and template construction. Following complete experimental validation of our results, we then iterated this same procedure K = 6 times to establish confidence intervals for our results. This approach allowed us to assess the reliability of the generated subsets and identify the best set of structures to augment the training data, thereby improving the model’s predictive performance.

In a few cases where we observed overlap in TCR primary sequences between training and test sets, we used this as an opportunity to generate and evaluate additional in silico-derived structures for the overlapping TCRs that lacked structural information using AlphaFold3. When such sequences appeared in the (randomly selected) training subset, we replaced their original entries with the in silico-derived modeled structures. Predictive performance for these updated models was assessed by evaluating their ability to distinguish binding versus nonbinding cases using ROC curve analysis, repeated over multiple randomized training and test splits to ensure robustness. To comprehensively evaluate any potential effect of utilizing different methodology for in silico template creation on predictive performance, we repeated the above procedure using AlphaFold Multimer35, along with more recent structure prediction methods, including Boltz-136, which leverage large-scale transformer-based architectures and diffusion models designed for biomolecular complex prediction. We then evaluated these models as done above for AlphaFold3.

To generate additional in silico-derived structures for the excluded cases, we required detailed sequence information, including pMHC sequences and TCR variable regions (Vα and Vβ). For the testing cases, sequence data were available only for CDR3α/β and the peptide, with the MHC being identical across all cases (HLA-A*02:01). To obtain the remaining Vα/β sequences, we referred to the best template identified by RACER-m during the primary selection step. These sequences were then used to construct additional in silico-derived 3D structures, which were incorporated into the training set to enhance model applicability. Similarly, for non-CDR regions, we selected consensus sequences derived from curated TCR databases and validated structural templates to ensure proper folding and structural stability. The complete TCR sequences—including both CDR and non-CDR regions—along with the pMHC sequences, were then used to generate additional in silico-derived 3D structures.

TCR–pMHC distribution percentile calculation

The recognition task in this study focuses on identifying TCRs as binders to a single antigen target by learning distinctive biophysical features from training distributions of binders and non-binders. We implemented a new classification method that improved upon our previous approach (RACER-m)19, which originally classified TCR–pMHC pairs as binders based on a global Z-score cutoff. We refer to this distribution-based extension as RACER-md (RACER-m distribution-based), which replaces the global thresholding strategy with a percentile-based classification framework derived from antigen-specific binding distributions. Using RACER-md, we generated Z-score distributions across our training set for three antigens: A2-MART1-ELA, A2-FLU-GIL, and A2-CMV-NLV. For an unknown TCR, we computed Z-scores for each of these antigen pairs and determined the percentile ranking of each Z-score within its respective distribution. The percentile rank serves as a measure of binding potential for each peptide based on its Z-score (Fig. 7a). In this approach, the peptide having the highest percentile ranking is identified as the strongest binder. When the Z-score exceeds the mean, a higher percentile indicates a stronger binding interaction and reinforces its classification as a binder. Conversely, when the Z-score falls below the mean, larger percentile scores correspond to values closer to the mean, signifying that among the available options, this peptide exhibits the highest relative binding potential.
