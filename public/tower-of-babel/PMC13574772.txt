Firecracker-induced air pollution and health burden during Diwali: ground and satellite assessment across 14 cities of The Indo-Gangetic Basin
Authors: Vaishnav Bartaria, Ranjit Kumar, Kamal Kumar, Ashok Jangid
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study examines the impact of Diwali festivities on air quality and public health across 14 cities in the Indo-Gangetic Basin (IGB), integrating ground-based monitoring, satellite data, and meteorological analysis. PM₂.₅ and PM₁₀ levels exceeded national standards by 3–10 times during Diwali relative to CPCB and WHO 24-hour guidelines, respectively. Delhi witnessing a 19% rise in PM₂.₅ during the festival and post-Diwali biomass burning driving PM₂.₅ spikes exceeding 250 µg m⁻³ in Hisar. Gaseous pollutants, including SO₂ and NO₂, surged sharply due to firecracker emissions, while satellite observations revealed pronounced aerosol plumes, particularly over urban and agricultural zones. Meteorological stagnation, marked by low wind speeds, temperature inversions, and high humidity, trapped pollutants, amplifying exposure risks. Health risks escalated significantly, with 80% of cities shifting to ‘severe’ or ‘hazardous’ Health Air Quality Index (HAQI) categories. Cities like Chandigarh demonstrated rapid post-Diwali PM₂.₅ recovery in contrast to cities with persistent elevated concentrations, where unregulated biomass burning and continued firecracker use extended the pollution episode.. Transitioning to sustainable urban practices, such as regionally coordinated bans on biomass burning, promoting “green crackers”, and integrating real-time pollution alerts with public health advisories, is critical. Strengthening green infrastructure, adopting renewable energy, and fostering community-led awareness campaigns can balance cultural traditions with environmental resilience. Aligning these strategies with Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), such as SDG 3 (health) and SDG 11 (sustainable cities) will ensure equitable air quality management, reduce health disparities, and build climate-resilient urban systems in the IGB. This holistic approach underscores the need for policies that prioritize long-term sustainability over short-term gains, safeguarding both public health and ecological balance in one of South Asia’s most vulnerable regions.Keywords: Particulate Matter, Aerosol Optical Depth (AOD), Air Quality Index (AQI), Health Air Quality Index (HAQI), Public Health RiskSubject terms: Climate sciences, Environmental sciences, Environmental social sciences

Introduction

Pollution is one of the most pressing global public health challenges of the 21st century, linked to nearly 9 million premature deaths annually, of which air pollution alone accounts for ~ 7 million deaths, including ~ 4.2 million attributable to fine particulate matter (PM₂.₅)1,2. For regulatory context, the 24-hour ambient air quality standards for particulate matter are 60 µg m⁻³ (PM₂.₅) and 100 µg m⁻³ (PM₁₀) as prescribed by the Central Pollution Control Board (CPCB), while the World Health Organization (WHO) recommends more stringent 24-hour guideline values of 15 µg m⁻³ for PM₂.₅ and 45 µg m⁻³ for PM₁₀. Major cities worldwide, particularly in low and middle income countries, grapple with hazardous air quality exacerbated by rapid urbanization, industrialization, and energy-intensive practices. According to the World Health Organization2, over 90% of urban populations breathe air exceeding PM₂.₅ safety thresholds (5 µg m⁻³ annual mean), with megacities like Delhi (India), Beijing (China), and Lagos (Nigeria) consistently ranking among the most polluted. In South Asia, India bears a disproportionate burden, with its cities accounting for 14 of the world’s 20 most polluted urban centers3. Within India, the air pollution crisis remains severe, with recent estimates indicating that over ~ 2.1 million deaths in 2021 were attributable to ambient and household air pollution exposure, reflecting cardiovascular, respiratory, and other disease outcomes linked to fine particulate matter and other pollutants1,4. The Indo-Gangetic Basin (IGB), a densely populated region spanning northern India and neighbouring countries, is a global pollution hotspot. This region has nearly one-seventh of the world’s population and major industrial hubs (e.g., Kanpur, Lucknow) and megacities (e.g., Delhi, Kolkata), the IGB records annual mean PM₂.₅ concentrations exceeding 110 µg m⁻³ which is 22 times of the WHO guideline5–8. This region’s unique topography, meteorology (low wind speeds, temperature inversions, high humidity), and emissions from agriculture, transport, and biomass burning create a “pollution valley” where contaminants linger for weeks9. Recent satellite analysis identify the IGB as hosting 12 of India’s 15 most polluted cities, with Delhi’s annual PM₂.₅ averaging 98.6 µg m⁻³ in 2022, nearly 20-fold higher than safe limits3,10.

Diwali was traditionally celebrated with diya (oil lamp) lighting, communal gatherings, and other cultural practices that foster social cohesion and well-being. However, in recent decades, the widespread use of fireworks has triggered extreme short-term deterioration in air quality. Firecrackers release a complex mix of pollutants, including fine particulate matter (PM₂.₅), heavy metals, sulfur dioxide, and nitrogen oxides, which rapidly accumulate in the atmosphere under stable meteorological conditions. The sudden spike in these pollutants not only reduces visibility and alters atmospheric chemistry but also intensifies respiratory and cardiovascular health risks, particularly in densely populated urban environments9.

Studies across Indian cities report PM₂.₅ concentrations surging to 500–1,000 µg m⁻³ on Diwali night, approximately 30-60 times the WHO’s 24-hour limit11,12. In the IGB, these spikes are amplified by stagnant post-monsoon weather, trapping pollutants and creating multiday haze episodes13–16. The health implications are severe like exposure to such pollution levels elevates risks of respiratory emergencies (asthma, COPD exacerbations), cardiovascular events, and premature mortality, particularly among children, the elderly, and those with pre-existing conditions13,14.

This study provides a comprehensive multi-city assessment of Diwali-driven air quality degradation across the Indo-Gangetic Basin (IGB), integrating high-resolution satellite data (Aerosol Optical Depth (AOD), Ultraviolet Aerosol Index (UV-AI), Absorbing Aerosol Index (AAI)), ground monitoring datasets from the Central Pollution Control Board (CPCB), and health risk models. By analysing key pollutants; PM₂.₅, PM₁₀, SO₂, NO₂, NH₃, CO, and O₃ across 14 major IGB cities, including megacities (Delhi, Kolkata), industrial hubs (Kanpur, Patna), and religious centres (Varanasi, Ayodhya), we elucidate how urban emissions, geography, and meteorological factors (e.g., temperature inversions, wind speed, humidity) modulate pollution exposure and retention. The study bridges critical gaps in regional air quality dynamics by quantifying spatial and temporal variations in pollution spikes during Diwali, while employing the Health Air Quality Index (HAQI) to assess acute health risks. As India’s urban population is projected to double by 2050, balancing cultural traditions with sustainable practices is critical. This study underscores the need for decentralized air quality management strategies tailored to the IGB’s socio-climatic diversity. By linking transient pollution spikes to mortality and morbidity burdens, it strengthens the case for stricter fireworks regulations, real-time air quality alerts, and healthcare preparedness during high-risk periods, a model applicable to other pollution-prone regions globally.

Materials and methods

Study area and site selection

This study focuses on the Indo-Gangetic Basin (IGB), an extensive and densely populated region spanning multiple states from Punjab in the northwest to West Bengal in the east. The IGB is known for its unique meteorological conditions, significant industrial activity, high vehicular emissions, and agricultural biomass burning, which collectively contribute to poor air quality. For this analysis, fourteen major cities within the IGB viz., Amritsar, Chandigarh, Ludhiana, Hisar, Delhi, Jaipur, Agra, Kanpur, Lucknow, Varanasi, Patna, Muzaffarpur, Kolkata and Agartala, were selected based on their demographic significance, high pollution levels, and availability of comprehensive air quality monitoring data (Fig. 1). Each of these cities encompasses a mix of urban, industrial, and suburban environments, offering a comprehensive representation of Diwali-related pollution dynamics across the Indo-Gangetic Basin (IGB). The study sites (Table 1) were categorized into Megacities (Delhi and Kolkata), Urban centers (Agra, Jaipur, Chandigarh, Kanpur, Lucknow, Varanasi, Patna, Amritsar, Ludhiana, and Agartala), and Semi-urban/agriculture-influenced cities (Hisar and Muzaffarpur) based on population size, urbanization level, dominant emission activities, and regional setting. This classification reflects the spatial heterogeneity in urban development and pollution sources across the Indo-Gangetic Basin.

Data collection

Air pollutant data, including PM2.5, PM10, SO2, NO2, NH3, CO, and O3, were obtained from the Central Pollution Control Board (CPCB) (https://app.cpcbccr.com/ccr/#/caaqm-dashboard-all/caaqm-landing/caaqm-comparison-data), under the Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change (MoEFCC), Government of India. The daily (24-hour) concentrations of particulate matter (PM2.5 and PM10), nitrogen dioxide (NO2), sulfur dioxide (SO2), ammonia (NH3), carbon monoxide (CO), and ozone (O3; 8-hour) were collected from CPCB data portal. Satellite-based active fire detections from NASA FIRMS (VIIRS and MODIS) were also analyzed across the Pre-Diwali, Diwali, and Post-Diwali phases to characterize regional post-monsoon biomass-burning activity and provide contextual background for observed air quality variations. The data collection period spans ten days, from October 27 to November 5, 2024, and is categorized into three phases: Pre-Diwali (October 27–30), Diwali (October 31–November 1), and Post-Diwali (November 2–5). As per the report13,16, this period coincides with the post-monsoon crop-residue burning season in the Indo-Gangetic Plain, which has shifted by approximately 10–15 days in recent years due to groundwater conservation policies affecting paddy transplantation and harvesting schedules, thereby extending biomass-burning activity into late October and early November. In addition to ground-based monitoring, satellite-derived data were incorporated to analyze pollution dispersion and intensity over the IGB. The parameters analyzed include Aerosol Optical Depth (AOD), Absorbing Aerosol Index (AAI), and Ultraviolet Aerosol Index (UV-AI), sourced from MODIS (Moderate Resolution Imaging Spectroradiometer) and Copernicus satellite datasets. Data extraction and processing were carried out using the Google Earth Engine (GEE) platform. Furthermore, meteorological parameters such as temperature at 2 m height, dewpoint temperature, relative humidity, surface pressure, and boundary layer height were also obtained via satellite-based observations using GEE. MODIS Aerosol Optical Depth (AOD) data were obtained from the MODIS Terra platform (Collection 6.1), which has a local overpass time of approximately 10:30 a.m. IST, enabling the capture of aerosol loading during the early boundary layer development phase, before the mixed layer reaches its afternoon maximum depth, and thus reflecting residual nighttime emission accumulation.

Assumptions and limitations of HAQI estimation

The health-risk framework applied in this study assumes a log-linear exposure–response relationship between short-term pollutant concentrations and health risk, consistent with established epidemiological evidence. Population exposure is assumed to be spatially uniform within each city, and intra-urban variability, population mobility, and differential vulnerability across age or health groups are not explicitly resolved. The adopted exposure–response coefficient (β) represents an average acute risk increment derived from India-specific studies and may not fully capture city-specific baseline health conditions or adaptive responses. Moreover, the analysis estimates relative exposure-related risk based on ambient concentration exceedances rather than on direct clinical or hospital admission data. Accordingly, the derived results should be interpreted as comparative indicators of short-term exposure stress, rather than as absolute estimates of morbidity or mortality. Despite these limitations, this approach provides a robust and transparent means of comparing Diwali related short-term health risk across multiple urban settings.

Event-scale slope and temporal trend analysis during Diwali 2024

The Mann–Kendall (MK) test was applied to evaluate trends in pollutant concentrations over time and determine their statistical significance. This analysis was conducted at a 95% confidence level (α = 0.05), where a p-value below 0.05 indicated a statistically significant trend. To further assess the magnitude of these trends, Sen’s slope method was utilized, providing a quantitative measure of the rate of change over time. For a comprehensive discussion on the methodology, refer to Mohammad et al. (2022), Bhattacharyya (2023), and Da Silva et al. (2020)22–25. Furthermore, variations in the Air Quality Index (AQI) during the pre-Diwali, Diwali, and post-Diwali periods across the Indo-Gangetic Basin (IGB) were analyzed. The AQI was calculated using the CPCB AQI Calculator. To examine significant differences in meteorological parameters across the study phases (Pre-, Diwali, and Post-Diwali), firstly data distributions have been checked for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test and for homogeneity of variances using Levene’s test. A one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was then performed at a 95% confidence level (α = 0.05). Where the ANOVA indicated significant differences, Tukey’s HSD test was applied for pairwise comparisons among phases. In addition, Dunnett’s test (with Pre-Diwali as the control) was considered to specifically compare Diwali and Post-Diwali against the baseline (Table 2).

Results and discussion

Air pollutant concentration

Air Pollutant (PM₂.₅, PM₁₀, SO2, NO2, CO, NH3, O3) concentrations across 14 cities in the Indo-Gangetic Basin (IGB) from October 27 to November 5, 2024, covering pre-Diwali (October 27–30), Diwali (October 31-November 1), and post-Diwali (November 2–5) periods (Fig. 2).

Variation of particulates matter (PM2.5, PM10) and gaseous pollutants (SO2, NO2, NH3, CO, O3) during Diwali festival.

PM₂.₅ and PM₁₀ levels during and after Diwali across 14 cities in the Indo-Gangetic Basin (IGB), with the extent of pollution varying based on urbanization, meteorological conditions, and policy enforcement. Pre-Diwali baseline levels were already high in cities like Delhi (185.49 µg m− 3), Amritsar (164.29 µg m− 3), and Hisar (115.36 µg m− 3), mainly due to vehicle emissions, industrial activities, and trans-boundary pollution from crop residue burning in Punjab and Haryana. However, during Diwali, Delhi recorded a 19% increase (220.64 µg m− 3 on Nov 1), and Hisar saw a 34% rise (154.02 µg m− 3). Post-Diwali (Nov 2–5), pollution remained elevated, with some cities (Hisar: 259.98 µg m− 3, Delhi: 233.76 µg m− 3) showing delayed peaks due to meteorological stagnation. Similar trends were observed for PM₁₀, with pre-Diwali levels highest in Delhi (333.66 µg m− 3), Amritsar (253.15 µg m− 3), and Jaipur (233.44 µg m− 3). During Diwali, Amritsar showed a significant increase (396.42 µg m− 3, Nov 1), while Delhi (358.72 µg m− 3) and Kanpur (246.51 µg m− 3) also surpassed safe limits. Post-Diwali, cities like Patna (276.04 µg m− 3) and Hisar (229.3 µg m− 3) exhibited high levels due to dust resuspension and biomass burning, prolonging pollution episodes.

Urban areas (Delhi, Kanpur, Jaipur, Lucknow) exhibited higher post-Diwali PM₂.₅ and PM₁₀ levels, influenced by vehicular congestion, industrial sources, and firecracker emissions. The urban heat island (UHI) effect further modulates pollutant dispersion by altering near-surface thermal structure, particularly during nighttime. Heat retained by urban surfaces reduces nocturnal cooling and suppresses the development of a deeper boundary layer, resulting in a shallow, stable atmosphere that limits vertical mixing. Under such conditions, pollutants emitted during evening and nighttime hours such as those from firecracker activity remain confined near the surface, leading to elevated concentrations despite relatively weak emission durations. The UHI effect and concrete structures trap heat and pollutants, limiting dispersion. Semi-urban and rural locations (Agartala, Hisar, Varanasi) saw lower but still significant increases, mainly due to biomass burning, localized festivities, and pollution transport from nearby urban centers. Hisar, recorded a 54% increase in PM₂.₅ post-Diwali, likely due to stubble burning in adjacent agricultural regions5.

Lower wind speeds (< 2 m s− 1) in Delhi and Kanpur post-Diwali contributed to pollutant accumulation. Conversely, cities like Kolkata, closer to the coast, experienced better dispersion due to stronger winds. Early winter conditions led to temperature inversions at night, trapping pollutants closer to the ground, particularly in North Indian cities (Delhi, Kanpur, Patna). High relative humidity (> 75%) in cities like Agra and Jaipur led to PM hygroscopic growth, increasing particle sizes and worsening pollution severity26.

The relative contributions of firecrackers, crop-residue burning, and other local sources were inferred based on a combination of temporal signatures, spatial coherence, aerosol characteristics, and prevailing meteorological conditions, rather than direct source apportionment. Firecracker-related emissions were identified by abrupt, short-duration nocturnal increases in PM₂.₅, PM₁₀, SO₂, NO₂, AQI, and absorbing aerosol indices (UV-AI and AAI) occurring synchronously across urban sites during Diwali night. In contrast, crop-residue burning influence was inferred from delayed and regionally coherent post-Diwali pollution enhancement, particularly over northwestern IGB cities such as Amritsar, Ludhiana, and Hisar, consistent with contemporaneous agricultural fire activity and sustained high absorbing aerosol signals. Persistent background pollution and gradual pre-Diwali elevations were attributed to local urban sources, including vehicular emissions, industrial activity, residential combustion, and resuspended dust27–29.

PM₂.₅ levels increased by 40–60% within 24 h of Diwali, aligning with studies highlighting Diwali as a pollution amplification event. Delhi and Kanpur, with high traffic density and industrial activities, recorded a consistent rise in PM levels even before Diwali, suggesting a continuous emission burden. The sharp increase in PM₂.₅ in Amritsar and Hisar (40–50% rise post-Diwali) aligns with the stubble burning period in Punjab and Haryana, contributing to long-range pollution transport5.

The recorded pollution levels exceed both CPCB and WHO standards, with severe health implications: PM₂.₅ concentrations post-Diwali in Delhi (233.76 µg m− 3) were nearly 16 times the WHO 24-hour limit (15 µg m− 3), increasing risks of respiratory infections, cardiovascular diseases, and oxidative stress2. Amritsar’s PM₁₀ peak (441.2 µg m− 3) poses risks of lung fibrosis, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and inflammation over prolonged exposure29.

Despite existing policies like the Graded Response Action Plan (GRAP) in Delhi, post-Diwali pollution control remains ineffective, highlighting gaps in enforcement. Cities with partial bans (e.g., Chandigarh, Kolkata) recorded lower post-Diwali PM increases (~ 20–30%), suggesting that stricter enforcement of bans can mitigate extreme pollution spikes.

The impact of Diwali celebrations on gaseous air pollutants, including SO₂, NO₂, NH₃, O₃, and CO, was evident through distinct temporal variations observed before, during, and after the festival across multiple cities30,31. Sulfur dioxide (SO₂) levels remained relatively stable during the pre-Diwali period (27–30 Oct 2024), with the highest concentrations recorded in Hisar (34.12 µg m− 3), Agartala (24.53 µg m− 3), and Varanasi (24.88 µg m− 3), while lower values were observed in Kolkata (5.77 µg m− 3) and Chandigarh (6.63 µg m− 3). However, a significant spike in SO₂ was recorded on Diwali days (31 Oct − 1 Nov), particularly in Delhi (rising from 16.7 µg m− 3 on 30 Oct to 25.02 µg m− 3 on 1 Nov), Jaipur (12.02 to 25.50 µg m− 3), and Amritsar (7.22 to 34.11 µg m− 3), primarily attributed to firecracker emissions releasing sulfur-based compounds. Post-Diwali (2–5 Nov), SO₂ levels exhibited a mixed trend; while Delhi showed a rapid decline (19.82 µg m− 3 on 2 Nov), cities such as Amritsar (47.48 µg m− 3 on 2 Nov) and Hisar (64.43 µg m− 3 on 5 Nov) retained elevated concentrations due to localized pollution accumulation and unfavorable meteorological conditions.

Similarly, nitrogen dioxide (NO₂) levels were moderately high before Diwali, with Delhi recording the highest pre-festival average (52.77 µg m− 3), followed by Jaipur (50.52 µg m− 3 on 30 Oct) and Chandigarh (40.16 µg m− 3), primarily due to vehicular and industrial emissions. A sharp increase in NO₂ levels was recorded during Diwali due to firecracker combustion and increased vehicular activity, with Agra rising from 18.08 µg m− 3 (29 Oct) to 27.30 µg m− 3 (31 Oct), Kanpur from 23.84 µg m− 3 (30 Oct) to 25.63 µg m− 3 (31 Oct), and Delhi peaking at 66.01 µg m− 3 on 5 Nov. While most cities experienced a gradual decline post-Diwali, residual pollution persisted in Delhi (62.56 µg m− 3 on 3 Nov) and Jaipur (49.73 µg m− 3 on 4 Nov), indicating slower dispersion rates. Conversely, cities like Hisar (8.95 µg m− 3) and Agartala (8.46 µg m− 3) showed minor fluctuations, suggesting lesser firecracker-induced pollution due to lower population density.

Ammonia (NH₃) concentrations followed a more complex pattern, with high pre-Diwali values in Kolkata (57.19 µg m− 3 on 27 Oct), Muzaffarpur (42.08 µg m− 3), and Chandigarh (40.44 µg m− 3), attributed to emissions from vehicular exhaust, biomass burning, and agricultural activities. During Diwali, NH₃ levels exhibited regional variations; while Delhi showed a decrease (from 47.61 µg m− 3 on 30 Oct to 37.44 µg m− 3 on 1 Nov) due to its conversion into secondary pollutants like ammonium sulfate, Lucknow experienced an increase (from 23.36 µg m− 3 on 30 Oct to 34.18 µg m− 3 on 1 Nov), possibly due to nitrogen-based firecracker emissions. Post-Diwali, NH₃ levels exhibited mixed trends, with Agartala (31.34 µg m− 3 on 1 Nov to 28.74 µg m− 3 on 2 Nov) and Kolkata (48.98 µg m− 3 on 1 Nov to 64.86 µg m− 3 on 2 Nov) showing an increase due to continued biomass burning, while Amritsar exhibited a slight decrease (36.19 µg m− 3 on 1 Nov to 36.13 µg m− 3 on 4 Nov), suggesting effective dispersion. SO2 and NH3 present in the atmosphere, react, often with water and sunlight and form ammonium sulphate as a secondary aerosol which impacts air quality and health. Ammonia (NH₃), primarily originated from agricultural and livestock activities in the Indo-Gangetic Basin plays important role in the formation of sulphate as ammonium sulphate.

Interestingly, ozone (O₃) levels displayed an inverse trend compared to other pollutants. Pre-Diwali, O₃ concentrations were relatively high in Jaipur (69.74 µg m− 3 on 27 Oct), Delhi (41.14 µg m− 3), Muzaffarpur (50.38 µg m− 3), and Amritsar (50.77 µg m− 3), influenced by photochemical reactions involving NOₓ and volatile organic compounds (VOCs). However, during Diwali, O₃ levels decreased in most cities, with Delhi falling from 41.14 µg m⁻³ (27 Oct) to 30.67 µg m⁻³ on Diwali night (31 Oct), and Jaipur from 69.74 µg m⁻³ (27 Oct) to 42.82 µg m⁻³ (31 Oct), consistent with NOₓ titration (NO + O₃ → NO₂ + O₂). A marginal within-Diwali recovery was observed on 1 Nov (Delhi: 32.17 µg m⁻³; Jaipur: 43.71 µg m⁻³). This drop is attributed to the titration effect, where increased NO emissions from firecrackers react with ambient O₃ to form NO₂, leading to temporary O₃ suppression. However, in cities like Muzaffarpur (55.81 µg m− 3 on 30 Oct), O₃ remained elevated due to the continued presence of VOCs. Post-Diwali, O₃ levels rebounded, with Delhi rising from 42.78 µg m− 3 (3 Nov) to 47.64 µg m− 3 (5 Nov) and Jaipur from 43.71 µg m− 3 (1 Nov) to 83.41 µg m− 3 (5 Nov), indicating a return to photochemical equilibrium as NO₂ levels declined.

These findings collectively highlight the significant air quality deterioration during Diwali, with firecracker emissions causing abrupt spikes in SO₂ (up to 35.11 µg m− 3) and NO₂ (up to 66.01 µg m− 3), while NH₃ exhibited variability due to complex secondary reactions. The sharp decline in O₃ levels during Diwali, followed by a post-festival rebound, underscores the role of NO titration and photochemical recovery in urban atmospheres. Additionally, regional disparities were observed, with northern cities like Delhi, Agra, and Kanpur experiencing more pronounced pollution increases, whereas eastern cities like Kolkata and Agartala showed sustained NH₃ and SO₂ levels due to prolonged biomass burning emissions. These trends align with previous studies confirming that Diwali-related emissions cause short-term air quality degradation, the duration of which depends on meteorological conditions. The study underscores the need for stricter firecracker regulations and urban emission control measures, particularly in high-density areas. Future research should further explore meteorological influences on post-festival pollutant persistence and develop mitigation strategies to minimize short-term exposure risks.

Coarse-to-fine particle size analysis

Coarse-to-fine particle size analysis describes the relative contribution of fine (PM₂.₅) and coarse (PM₁₀–PM₂.₅) particulate matter mass fractions, using the PM₂.₅/PM₁₀ ratio as an indicator of fine-mode dominance (Fig. 3). The PM2.5/PM10 ratio remained relatively moderate across most cities, with values ranging between 0.50 and 0.59 for cities like Agra (0.59), Kanpur (0.58), and Delhi (0.57). These values indicate a balanced distribution of fine and coarse particles, with a slight inclination towards fine-mode dominance in some urban areas, attributed to local emissions from vehicular traffic, industrial activities, and biomass burning, which are characteristic of pre-winter conditions in the IGB region32–34. The lower ratios observed in cities such as Varanasi (0.43) and Ludhiana (0.45) suggest the influence of re-suspended dust and coarser emissions from construction and road dust, which are more prevalent in these regions35. The Diwali phase (October 31–November 1) exhibited a noticeable increase in the PM2.5/PM10 ratio in most cities, primarily driven by the intense firecracker bursting, enhanced vehicular congestion, and additional emissions from festive activities. The highest increase was observed in cities like Agra (0.59) and Kanpur (0.58), indicating the dominance of finer particles due to incomplete combustion processes from firecrackers, which generate ultrafine aerosols rich in black carbon and heavy metals36–40. The relatively lower ratios in cities like Hisar (0.50) and Patna (0.48) suggest the presence of mixed aerosol sources, including a combination of fine-mode and coarse-mode particles from both combustion and local dust re-suspension. The spike in fine-mode dominance aligns with previous studies that reported a sharp increase in PM2.5 levels during Diwali, often exceeding the national air quality standards41–43.

Post-Diwali (November 2–5) trends show a gradual decline in the PM2.5/PM10 ratio across most cities, indicating the dispersion of fine-mode aerosols due to meteorological factors such as increased wind speed and boundary layer height37,44,45. However, in cities like Muzaffarpur (0.48) and Varanasi (0.43), the ratio remained relatively lower, suggesting a shift back to coarse-mode dominance due to the settling of finer particles and the resuspension of road dust and other coarse materials. This post-festival decline is consistent with previous findings that show a rapid reduction in PM2.5 concentrations a few days after Diwali due to atmospheric dilution and reduced anthropogenic activities12,37,43.

The analysis confirms that the Diwali festival significantly elevates the fine particulate fraction in urban air, raising health concerns, particularly for respiratory and cardiovascular ailments27. The persistence of high PM2.5 levels post-Diwali in some regions further suggests the role of meteorology in pollutant dispersion and highlights the need for stricter air quality regulations and public awareness campaigns to mitigate the adverse health impacts of festival-related air pollution.

Synergistic effects of meteorology on air quality

The meteorological analysis presented here focuses on conditions prevailing during the 2024 Diwali period to explain pollutant accumulation and persistence at an event scale, rather than assessing long-term climatological trends. The interplay of meteorological parameters and anthropogenic emissions during Diwali 2024 across the Indo-Gangetic Basin (IGB) underscores the region’s vulnerability to episodic pollution events, driven by post-monsoon atmospheric transitions and festive emissions. A detailed synthesis of city-specific trends, regional contrasts, and pollutant-meteorology coupling reveals critical mechanisms governing air quality degradation and persistence. Figure 4 illustrates the variation in meteorological parameters.

Variation of Meteorological Parameters (Temperature, Surface Pressure, Relative Humidity, Dewpoint temperature, boundary layer Height) during Diwali festival.

The post-monsoon cooling trend (e.g., Delhi: 25.5 °C → 22.9 °C) intensified nocturnal temperature inversions, particularly in northern cities like Delhi and Chandigarh. These inversions stabilized the boundary layer, reducing mixing heights and trapping pollutants near the surface. For instance, Delhi’s boundary layer height (BLH) collapsed to 63.4 m on Diwali night (October 31), coinciding with a PM2.5 spike to 220 µg m− 3, as emissions from fireworks were confined to shallow atmospheric layers46. Concurrently, rising surface pressure (e.g., Kolkata: 1006 → 1011 hPa) suppressed vertical dispersion by compressing the boundary layer, amplifying stagnation. In Kanpur, a 3 hPa pressure rise reduced daytime BLH by ~ 25%, correlating with PM2.5 levels of 173.6 µg m− 347. Based on observed diurnal variability, boundary layer suppression alone reduced the effective mixing depth by more than an order of magnitude between daytime and nighttime (< 100 m), which corresponded to a 20–30% reduction in PM₂.₅ during daytime and rapid nighttime accumulation during Diwali. This indicates that BLH variability was a dominant meteorological control on short-term pollutant concentrations, accounting for a substantial fraction of the observed intra-day PM variability under stagnant conditions.

Relative humidity (RH) exhibited dual roles: in eastern IGB cities like Kolkata (RH > 95%), hygroscopic growth of aerosols increased PM2.5 mass by 15–20% through moisture-induced swelling of sulfate and nitrate particles14,48. Conversely, western cities such as Jaipur (RH: 83–89%) exhibited comparatively weaker aqueous-phase secondary aerosol formation relative to eastern coastal cities, despite elevated humidity. In these regions, secondary aerosols were likely dominated by solid or semi-solid particles. However, during Diwali, O₃ levels decreased in most cities, with Delhi falling from 41.14 µg m⁻³ (27 Oct) to 30.67 µg m⁻³ on Diwali night (31 Oct), and Jaipur from 69.74 µg m⁻³ (27 Oct) to 42.82 µg m⁻³ (31 Oct), consistent with NOₓ titration (NO + O₃ → NO₂ + O₂). A marginal within-Diwali recovery was observed on 1 Nov (Delhi: 32.17 µg m⁻³; Jaipur: 43.71 µg m⁻³) formed through gas-to-particle conversion pathways, while enhanced biomass-burning influence contributed substantially to particulate loading, with PM₁₀ concentrations reaching 335.9 µg m⁻³. Dew point trends further modulated fog and haze development; declining dew point temperatures in Jaipur (15.3 °C → 14.1 °C) reduced the likelihood of persistent fog formation, whereas consistently high dew point temperatures in Kolkata (~ 24 °C) favored sustained haze, enhancing aerosol hygroscopic growth, light scattering, and visibility degradation49.

Delhi emerged as a pollution epicenter during Diwali 2024, driven by its landlocked geography, persistently low nocturnal boundary layer heights (BLH < 100 m), and high anthropogenic emissions. A temperature decline of 2.6 °C strengthened atmospheric inversions, trapping PM2.5 at 220.6 µg m− 3 on Diwali night. Post-festival, PM₂.₅ levels remained elevated (> 200 µg m⁻³ until November 4) due to sluggish boundary layer height (BLH) recovery and enhanced secondary inorganic aerosol formation under stagnant conditions. In contrast, Kolkata’s coastal location moderated pollution spikes despite comparable firework emissions. Higher baseline BLH (~ 100 m) and sea breezes enhanced dispersion, though persistent relative humidity (RH > 95%) amplified PM2.5 mass by 15–20% through hygroscopic growth of sulfate and nitrate aerosols50,51. This interplay of humidity and coastal dynamics limited Kolkata’s peak PM2.5 to 133 µg m− 3, significantly lower than Delhi’s extremes.

Amritsar, situated in the western Indo-Gangetic Basin (IGB), faced distinct challenges. High RH (~ 85%) and cooler temperatures (24.6 °C → 23.1 °C) amplified biomass burning emissions, while minimal secondary aerosol activity allowed coarse particles from fireworks to dominate. PM10 concentrations surged to 441 µg m− 3 on November 2 under a collapsed BLH of 40 m, reflecting the region’s susceptibility to primary particulate pollution. Agartala, despite high RH (> 94%), exhibited resilience due to coastal advection, which accelerated BLH recovery (~ 100 m at night) and dispersed pollutants, limiting PM2.5 to 83 µg m− 3, 50% lower than Delhi’s peak.

Regional contrasts further highlighted the IGB’s spatial heterogeneity. Northern cities like Delhi and Ludhiana, characterized by landlocked stagnation, low nighttime BLH, and calm winds (< 1 m s− 1) (Fig. 5), formed “pollution reservoirs,” with Ludhiana’s PM10 reaching 261.83 µg m− 3, 17 times the WHO guideline. Eastern cities such as Kolkata and Patna, while benefiting from higher baseline BLH (~ 120 m) and maritime influences, grappled with prolonged haze as RH-driven aerosol swelling offset dispersion gains. By November 5, Kolkata’s PM2.5 dropped to 30.5 µg m− 3, underscoring the moderating role of coastal dynamics. Conversely, western IGB cities like Jaipur and Hisar, under drier conditions (RH < 90%), faced elevated PM10 from dust resuspension and biomass burning. Hisar’s PM2.5 spiked to 259 µg m− 3 under low dew points (14.1 °C), emphasizing the region’s vulnerability to combustion-related emissions.

Diurnal BLH cycles critically shaped exposure risks. Daytime solar heating expanded the boundary layer to 1,000–1,500 m (e.g., Delhi: 1,240.6 m at 08:00 on October 27), temporarily lowering PM2.5 by 20–30%. However, nocturnal BLH collapse (< 100 m) nullified these gains, particularly during peak firework activity (20:00–22:00). In Kanpur, BLH plummeted to 15–20 m at 22:00, coinciding with PM2.5 surges to 173.6 µg m− 3. Delhi’s PM2.5 peaked at 220 µg m− 3, aligning with a BLH minimum of 63 m at 20:00 on October 31. These dynamics exacerbated health risks, as acute PM exposure in northern cities exceeded WHO limits by 5–10×, correlating with spikes in hospital admissions for respiratory distress52–54.

To address these challenges, region-specific policies are imperative. In northern IGB, enforcing fireworks bans on days with forecasted BLH < 100 m and integrating real-time BLH/RH data into air quality alerts could reduce acute exposure. Eastern regions should leverage coastal wind forecasts to optimize emission dispersal during high-humidity periods, while all cities must adopt BLH-based emission curbs, restricting industrial and vehicular activity during low-BLH windows (18:00–06:00). Such strategies, tailored to the IGB’s meteorological and geographical diversity, are critical to mitigating the compounding effects of festive emissions and climatic vulnerability.

Statistical relationship between meteorological parameters and air pollutants

The correlation analysis presented in the comprehensive matrix provides valuable insights into the intricate relationships between air pollutants and meteorological parameters (Fig. 6). Understanding these relationships is crucial for assessing air quality variations, identifying major sources of pollution, and evaluating the impact of meteorological factors on pollutant dispersion, transformation, and deposition.

Multivariate correlation analysis between air pollutant and meteorological parameters.

The strong positive correlation between PM2.5 and PM10 (ρ = 0.91) indicates that both pollutants share common sources, such as vehicular emissions, industrial activities, road dust, biomass burning, and construction activities. PM10 includes coarser particles that can settle faster, while PM2.5 remains suspended for longer periods due to its finer size. The high correlation suggests that controlling sources contributing to PM10 emissions can also help in reducing PM2.5 levels, thereby improving overall air quality. Previous studies55–57 have demonstrated that combustion-related activities, particularly during winter and festive events like Diwali, contribute significantly to elevated PM concentrations.

The weak negative correlation of SO2 with NO2 (ρ = -0.08) and weak positive correlations with PM₂.₅ (ρ = 0.25) and PM₁₀ (ρ = 0.16) suggest partially decoupled emission sources, with SO₂ linked to industrial and combustion emissions and NO₂ to vehicular exhaust. SO2 contributes to the formation of sulfate aerosols, which can enhance PM concentrations through secondary aerosol formation30,34. The correlation between NO2 and SO2 (ρ = 0.40) and PM2.5 (ρ = 0.48) highlights its primary sources—vehicular emissions, industrial combustion, and biomass burning. NO2 plays a critical role in secondary aerosol formation and ozone chemistry. Its positive correlation with O3 (ρ=+28−0.22) suggests a complex photochemical interaction where NO2 contributes to ozone formation in the presence of sunlight, but higher NO2 levels may also lead to ozone depletion through titration effects13,58.

The weak correlation of NH3 with PM2.5 (ρ = 0.05) and PM10 (ρ = 0.09) indicates that while ammonia plays a role in forming secondary ammonium aerosols, its concentration may not be a direct driver of PM variations in the dataset analyzed. NH3 primarily originates from agricultural emissions, vehicular exhaust, and waste decomposition.

Ozone shows a weak to moderate correlation with PM2.5 (ρ = 0.28), PM10 (ρ = 0.33), and CO (ρ = 0.13), reflecting its secondary nature and dependence on precursor availability, sunlight, and atmospheric chemistry. Higher O3 levels are typically associated with photochemical smog formation, especially in urban areas with high NOx and VOC emissions59,60. The moderate positive correlation between CO and PM2.5 (ρ = 0.50), PM10 (ρ = 0.49), and NO2 (ρ = 0.52) indicates common sources such as incomplete combustion from vehicular emissions, biomass burning, and industrial activities. CO levels tend to increase during cooler conditions due to lower dispersion rates and stable atmospheric conditions, leading to pollution accumulation near the surface61,62.

Meteorological parameters play a crucial role in determining air pollutant concentrations by influencing their dispersion, chemical transformations, and removal processes. The negative correlation of temperature with PM2.5 (ρ=−0.50), PM10 (ρ=−0.48), and CO (ρ=−0.43) suggests that higher temperatures promote pollutant dispersion by increasing atmospheric turbulence and boundary layer height. During colder months, stable atmospheric conditions, low boundary layer height, and temperature inversions trap pollutants near the surface, leading to higher concentrations63,64. Dewpoint temperature exhibits a strong negative correlation with PM2.5 (ρ=−0.60) and PM10 (ρ=−0.54), indicating that increased moisture may facilitate wet deposition, leading to particle removal. Similarly, relative humidity negatively correlates with PM2.5 (ρ=−0.60) and PM10 (ρ=−0.54), suggesting that higher humidity levels can lead to particle aggregation, making them more susceptible to deposition processes. However, under certain conditions, high humidity can also promote secondary aerosol formation, increasing PM concentrations14,65. The high correlation (ρ = 0.86) between dewpoint temperature and relative humidity reflects the interdependence of these parameters in regulating atmospheric moisture content. Increased humidity can impact pollutant chemistry by influencing gas-to-particle conversion processes and particle growth66,67.

Event-scale slope and temporal trend analysis during Diwali 2024

It is important to clarify that the trend analysis presented here represents short-term, event-scale temporal evolution of pollutant concentrations across the pre-Diwali, Diwali, and post-Diwali phases during 2024, rather than long-term interannual trends. The Mann–Kendall test and Sen’s slope estimator are applied to quantify the direction and magnitude of concentration changes within this defined festival window, enabling assessment of pollution build-up, peak intensity, and post-event persistence under prevailing meteorological conditions. The Mann-Kendall (MK) test and Sen’s Slope analysis (Table 3) revealed spatially heterogeneous trends in PM2.5 and PM10 concentrations across Indo-Gangetic Basin (IGB) cities during Diwali 2024, reflecting the role of anthropogenic, meteorological, and policy-driven factors. Statistically significant increasing trends (p < 0.05) were observed in Delhi (PM2.5 slope = 8.67, p = 0.02; PM10 slope = 12.29, p = 0.01), Kolkata (PM2.5 slope = 6.03, p = 0.04; PM10 slope = 10.94, p = 0.04), and Lucknow (PM2.5 slope = 7.94, p = 0.04; PM10 slope = 16.66, p = 0.02), aligning with studies attributing extreme PM2.5 spikes (> 500 µg m⁻³) during Diwali to firework combustion tracers like potassium, sulfur, and aluminum11. These megacities, characterized by wintertime temperature inversions and stagnant winds14,37, experienced amplified pollution retention, compounded by vehicular emissions and industrial activity. Kanpur and Patna exhibited significant PM10 increases (slopes = 13.38 and 10.76; p = 0.03), likely driven by post-monsoon stubble burning in Punjab and Haryana, which elevates coarse particles regionally13,32, alongside resuspended dust from Kanpur’s unpaved roads. Varanasi’s PM2.5 rise (slope = 3.31, p = 0.03) underscores ritualistic contributions, as oil lamps and firecrackers rich in organic carbon are linked to submicron aerosol surges45. In contrast, Chandigarh’s PM10 exhibited a negligible negative slope (-0.25, p = 1.00), reflecting the success of paved road expansion and mechanized sweeping in curbing resuspended dust96. Jaipur (PM2.5 slope = 2.39, p = 0.10) and Agartala (PM2.5 slope = 3.32, p = 0.21) showed no significant trends, likely due to natural aridity stabilizing coarse particles97 or competing emission sources masking trends. Similarly, Agra (PM2.5 slope = 6.02, p = 0.11), Amritsar (PM2.5 slope = 5.56, p = 0.59), and Hisar (PM2.5 slope = 5.60, p = 0.21) displayed positive but non-significant slopes, potentially reflecting transient agricultural or transport emissions diluted by sporadic wind gusts. Ludhiana (PM2.5 slope = 1.76, p = 0.59) and Muzaffarpur (PM2.5 slope = 0.87, p = 0.72) exhibited minimal trends, suggesting localized variability from mixed agrarian-industrial activities, as observed in Punjab’s small-scale industries34.

The findings underscore the health risks in trend-significant cities, where PM2.5’s deep alveolar penetration exacerbates cardiopulmonary morbidity98,99. Targeted interventions such as firework bans, subsidized crop residue management, and green infrastructure are critical to mitigate synergies between cultural practices, agricultural burning, and winter meteorology. Policy spatialization, informed by these trends, can balance public health imperatives with cultural traditions in the IGB.

Previous multi-year studies over the Indo-Gangetic region report consistent Diwali-related amplification of particulate and gaseous pollutants and, under stagnant meteorological conditions, multi-day persistence of elevated PM₂.₅9,100. Causal analyses in Delhi further show spatial heterogeneity in Diwali impacts at city scale, supporting our interpretation that short-lived fireworks emissions drive nocturnal spikes while regional biomass burning and meteorology control background persistence88.

Conclusion

This study assesses Diwali’s impact on air quality across 14 Indo-Gangetic Basin (IGB) cities, highlighting the interplay of anthropogenic emissions, meteorology, and policy frameworks. Fireworks, vehicular emissions, and crop residue burning triggered severe pollution spikes, with PM₂.₅ and PM₁₀ levels surging by 19–60%. Post-festival stagnation sustained hazardous pollution, peaking at 233.76 µg m− 3 (Delhi) and 259.98 µg m− 3 (Hisar). Gaseous pollutants (SO₂: 64.43 µg m− 3, NO₂: 66.01 µg m− 3) spiked, while O₃ dipped due to NO titration. The Health Air Quality Index (HAQI) soared to 377.5 (“Severely Unhealthy”), driving increased respiratory morbidity risk. Meteorological factors like low wind speeds, inversions, and high humidity prolonged pollution episodes, particularly inland. Coastal cities like Kolkata saw moderated spikes but suffered from aerosol hygroscopic growth. Statistical tests (Mann-Kendall, Sen’s Slope) confirmed significant pollution trends, especially in urban hubs. Despite policies like the Graded Response Action Plan (GRAP), weak enforcement of firecracker bans and lack of regional coordination worsened exposure. Beyond the current Graded Response Action Plan (GRAP), which has shown limited effectiveness in isolation, sustainable solutions for the Indo-Gangetic Basin must emphasize regionally coordinated airshed management, large-scale adoption of crop-residue management technologies such as Happy Seeder, promotion of green crackers, stricter enforcement of firework bans during adverse meteorological conditions, and the dissemination of real-time AQI/HAQI-based public health advisories. Such integrated and multi-sectoral measures can offer long-term and sustainable pathways for air quality improvement in this densely populated region. Partial bans (e.g., Chandigarh) demostrated faster post-episode PM2.5 recovery, underscoring the need for stricter regulations, real-time forecasting, and sustainable crop management. Diwali’s cultural significance is undeniable, but urgent action is needed to mitigate its environmental and health toll. Transitioning to green crackers, enforcing stricter emission controls, and fostering regional cooperation is crucial for cleaner air without compromising tradition. Future research should focus on long-term health impacts and mitigation technologies to ensure sustainable air quality improvements.

Limitations

This study provides a comprehensive multi-city analysis of Diwali-related air pollution in the Indo-Gangetic Basin, some limitations must be acknowledged. First, this study does not capture background seasonal variability. Second, satellite-derived parameters (e.g., AOD, AAI) have inherent retrieval uncertainties under high aerosol loading. Third, the attribution of pollution peaks to fireworks versus crop-residue burning was based on temporal patterns rather than source apportionment, which may introduce uncertainty. Fourth, health impacts were estimated using HAQI-based risk models incorporating India-specific exposure–response coefficients rather than direct hospital records; therefore, the reported health burden should be interpreted as indicative of relative risk changes rather than absolute clinical outcomes. Future work incorporating chemical speciation, fire count data, and long-term epidemiological analysis would help strengthen causal attribution and refine health risk estimates.
