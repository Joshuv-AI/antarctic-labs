High-flow nasal oxygen versus conventional nasal cannula for gastroscopy with sedation in elderly patients at high altitudes: a partially-blinded randomized controlled trial in Tibet, China
Authors: Jianlei Zhang, Ningbo Cheng, Bianbawangdui, Tinley, Huaquan Luo, Tashiyonten, Dou Wang, Jingwen Wang, Xin Yin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn high-altitude environments with hypobaric hypoxia, chronic hypoxia can affect organs. This study aimed to determine whether high-flow nasal oxygen (HFNO) can reduce the incidence of hypoxemia in elderly patients undergoing gastroscopy with sedation at high altitudes.MethodsThis single-center, partially-blinded, prospective randomized trial was conducted in Shigatse, Tibet, China (3800 m above sea level). Elderly patients aged ≥ 60 undergoing gastroscopy with sedation were randomly assigned to either the HFNO group (60 L per minute, 100% oxygen) or the conventional nasal cannula (CNC) group (6 L per minute). The primary endpoint was the incidence of hypoxemia, defined as pulse SpO2 < 90% for > 10 s during sedation.ResultsThe incidence of hypoxemia was significantly lower in the HFNO group than the CNC group (2.8% vs. 32.9%, risk ratio (RR): 0.691; 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.584–0.818, p < 0.001). The median SpO2 after pre-oxygenation was similar between the groups (97.0% [96.0–98.0] vs. 96.0% [95.0–98.0], p = 0.053). However, the median minimum SpO2 during the procedure was significantly higher in the HFNO group compared to the CNC group (96% [95–98] vs. 93% [87–96], p < 0.001). The median duration of hypoxemia and the number of hypoxemia episodes were 0.0 s (0.0–22.0) and 0 (0–1), respectively. In contrast, the values were 0.0 s (0.0–0.0) and 0 (0–0) in the HFNO group (p < 0.001). The median recovery time was shorter in the HFNO group compared to the CNC group (1.0 min [0.5–1.5] vs. 1.5 min [1.0-2.5], p = 0.003).ConclusionsCompared to CNC, HFNO significantly reduced the incidence of hypoxemia during gastroscopy with sedation in elderly patients at high altitudes.Trial registrationChiCTR2400085110; registered on 31 May 2024.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12871-026-03968-9.Keywords: High-flow nasal oxygen, High altitude, Elderly, Gastroscopy, Dedation, Hypoxemia

Introduction

The use of sedation during gastrointestinal endoscopy significantly enhances patient comfort. Propofol, a sedative-hypnotic medication, is commonly used due to its rapid onset, short duration, and quick recovery time. However, sedation-related complications are unavoidable. The incidence of respiratory depression and hypoxemia caused by sedatives during gastroscopy ranges from 13% to 30% [1, 2]. To minimize the risk of hypoxia during sedation, maintaining adequate oxygenation is crucial. High-flow nasal oxygen (HFNO) delivers heated and humidified oxygen at high flow rates (up to 90 L per minute) through a specialized nasal cannula [3]. HFNO has several advantages over conventional nasal cannula (CNC), including providing positive end-expiratory pressure, reducing anatomical dead space, and improving the ventilation/perfusion ratio [4]. Therefore, compared to conventional oxygenation methods, HFNO can effectively reduce the risk of hypoxemia during gastrointestinal endoscopy with sedation [5].

At high altitudes, the significant decrease in the partial pressure of oxygen directly impacts oxygenation, leading to persistent hypobaric hypoxia [6]. Despite a compensatory increase in erythrocytes, the average oxygen saturation of high-altitude residents is around 90% [7, 8]. Long-term exposure to high altitude affects physiological functions, with the most significant change being pulmonary hypertension. The sustained increase in pressure in the pulmonary circulation increases the right ventricular afterload [9]. These cardiopulmonary changes may make patients more susceptible to hypoxemia when their respiratory function is compromised by external factors.

The elderly population is more vulnerable to hypoxemia than younger individuals during procedural sedation and analgesia [10]. In a previous study conducted at low altitudes, we demonstrated that the HFNO system significantly reduced the incidence of hypoxemia compared to CNC in elderly patients undergoing gastroscopy with sedation [11]. However, HFNO has not been widely used during sedation in the Tibet Autonomous Region of China, which has an average altitude of over 4,000 m. To identify a more effective oxygen delivery strategy, we conducted a prospective, randomized controlled trial to compare the efficacy of HFNO and CNC in preventing hypoxemia in elderly patients undergoing gastroscopy with sedation. To our knowledge, this is the first randomized controlled trial to compare HFNO with CNC in elderly Tibetan patients undergoing sedated gastroscopy at high altitude. Prior relevant studies have been performed either at sea level or under general anesthesia; however, none have specifically focused on this vulnerable patient population exposed to hypobaric hypoxia.

Methods

This single-center, partially-blinded, prospective randomized trial was conducted at People’s Hospital of Shigatse City, Tibet, China, located at an average altitude of over 3800 m. The study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the People’s Hospital of Shigatse City (Approval No. 2024RKZRMYY05M001) and written informed consent was obtained from all subjects participating in the trial. The trial was registered prior to patient enrollment at Chinese Clinical Trials Registry (ChiCTR2400085110, Principal investigator: Xin Yin, Date of registration:31 May 2024). The trial took place between May 2024 and October 2024.

Patients

Elderly patients undergoing gastroscopy with sedation were enrolled in the study. Inclusion criteria included: (i) age ≥ 60 years, (ii) Tibetan ethnicity, and (iii) American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status classes I-III. Exclusion criteria included: (i) symptomatic heart failure, (ii) severe arrhythmia with hemodynamic instability, (iii) history of unstable angina pectoris or acute myocardial infarction in the past 6 months, (iv) acute upper respiratory tract infections or other severe respiratory conditions, (v) coagulation disorders, (vi) allergies to propofol, eggs, or soybeans, (vii) emergency procedures, and (viii) a length of stay in Shigatse of less than 3 months, considering insufficient acclimatized to the hypoxic environment.

Study design

Patients were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to either the intervention group (HFNO) or the standard care group (CNC) using computer-generated randomization numbers. Baseline characteristics collected included age, weight, body mass index (BMI), ASA physical status, baseline mean arterial pressure (MAP), baseline heart rate, baseline pulse oximeter saturation (SpO2), comorbidities at admission, breath-holding time (BHT), smoking history, STOP-Bang score, and Mallampati grade. Upon admission to the examination room, vascular access was established. Continuous monitoring of non-invasive MAP, electrocardiography, and SpO2 was performed simultaneously. Patients were positioned in the left lateral recumbent position. In the HFNO group, the devices (AIRVO 2 provided by Fisher & Paykel, Panmure, New Zealand) were used for respiratory support. After preheating, 100% oxygen was administered at 30 L per minute with a temperature of 31 °C. In the CNC group, oxygen was supplied at 6 L per minute via a central oxygen supply system. Both groups received pre-oxygenation for 3 min before sedation to ensure that SpO2 was ≥ 95%. All patients received the same intravenous sedation: fentanyl (0.5 µg·kg − 1) was administered slowly followed by propofol (1 mg·kg-1) until the patient lost consciousness (loss of palpebral reflex and response to stimulation, confirmed by another anesthesia staff). If adequate sedation was not achieved, an additional dose equivalent to half the initial dose was titrated incrementally until adequate sedation was attained. Afterwards, the oxygen flow rate in the HFNO group was increased to 60 L per minute and maintained until the end of the procedure, while it remained unchanged in the CNC group. If MAP dropped below 30% of the baseline value or HR fell below 50 beats per minute, ephedrine (0.05–0.1 mg·kg-1) was administered to maintain hemodynamic stability. The gastroscopy was performed by an experienced endoscopist, with the endoscope inserted through the esophagus into the stomach. A biopsy was taken if suspicious lesions were observed.

In a previous study, we found that severe hypoxemia in the CNC group could be rapidly improved by switching to HFNO when routine jaw thrust and facemask ventilation were ineffective [11]. Based on our clinical experience, elderly patients at high altitudes treated with sedatives were prone to develop severe hypoxemia (SpO2 < 75%) if SpO2 dropped below 90% during the procedure. Therefore, in this study, if the primary endpoint of hypoxemia occurred in the CNC group, the HFNO system was used if jaw thrust and facemask ventilation were insufficient, avoiding the need for endotracheal intubation.

Randomization and blinding

Participants were recruited by an independent anesthesiologist. A statistician generated a 1:1 randomization sequence using SAS software, sealed in sequentially numbered opaque envelopes. In the examination room, an opaque drape shielded both the patient’s head and oxygen delivery device. An independent anesthesia staff opened the envelope to reveal group assignment and then initiated the oxygen delivery device in accordance with group allocation. To avoid interference, this independent anesthesia staff activated the HFNO device for all patients prior to sedation, leaving investigators unable to identify the oxygen therapy type by visual or auditory cues. Blinding of patients was not feasible due to perceptible oxygen flow rates. All data were recorded by sub-investigators. Investigators and statistician remained blinded to group allocation throughout the entire study period.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the incidence of hypoxemia, defined as SpO2 < 90% for > 10 s during sedation. Secondary outcomes included SpO2 after pre-oxygenation, the number of hypoxemia episodes (a new episode was defined as SpO2 < 90% for > 10 s after maintaining a recovery [SpO2 ≥ 90%] for > 30 s), the incidence of severe hypoxemia (defined as SpO2 < 75% for any duration or SpO2 < 90% for ≥ 60 s) [12], duration of hypoxemia (time taken from the onset of hypoxemia to reach SpO2 ≥ 90%), minimum SpO2, recovery time, patient comfort (measured by a numerical rating scale, NRS, from 0 for no discomfort to 10 for maximum imaginable discomfort), airway interventions (e.g., patient stimulation, jaw thrust, facemask ventilation, crossover to HFNO). Sedation-related adverse events were recorded 30 min after recovery from anesthesia including events recorded by tools proposed by the World SIVA International Sedation Task Force [13].

Statistical analysis

The sample size was calculated using PASS (Power Analysis and Sample Size). Version 15.0 software (NCSS, LLC, Kaysville, UT, USA). Sample size was calculated based on a preliminary pilot study conducted at our center (unpublished data), in which the incidence of hypoxemia was 34.5% in the CNC group and 10.3% in the HFNO group. With α = 0.05 and power = 0.9, a minimum sample size of 116 patients (58 per group) was needed. Allowing for a 20% dropout rate, the final sample size was determined to be 73 patients per group. Measurement data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (± s) and compared using an independent t-test if they were normally distributed. Non-normally distributed data were presented as median and interquartile range and compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical data were expressed as the number of events (%) and analyzed using Fisher’s exact test or chi-squared test. The level of significance was set at a two-sided p-value < 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 25.0 (IBM Corporation, NY, USA).

Four patients were excluded due to inadequate gastrointestinal preparation, leaving 71 patients in the HFNO group and 70 in the CNC group for statistical analysis. All patients were of Tibetan ethnicity. Baseline characteristics were generally well balanced between the groups (Table 1). There were no significant differences between the two groups regarding the duration of sedation or the dosages of fentanyl and propofol (Table 1).

Overall, 25 of 141 patients (17.7%) developed hypoxemia. The study achieved the primary endpoint. The incidence of hypoxemia was significantly lower in the HFNO group compared to the CNC group (2.8% vs. 32.9%, RR: 0.691; 95% CI: 0.584–0.818, p < 0.001) (Fig. 2). Severe hypoxemia occurred in 8 of 70 (11.4%) patients in the CNC group, while no occurrence of severe hypoxemia was observed in the HFNO group. Two patients in the CNC group crossed over to the HFNO group after developing severe hypoxemia. Accordingly, both intention-to-treat (ITT) and per-protocol (PP) analyses were performed in this study. Although the median SpO2 after pre-oxygenation was similar between the groups (97.0% [IQR, 96.0–98.0] vs. 96.0% [IQR, 95.0–98.0], p = 0.053), the median minimum SpO2 during the procedure was significantly higher in the HFNO group compared to the CNC group (96% [IQR, 95–98] vs. 93% [IQR, 87–96], p < 0.001). In the CNC group, the median duration of hypoxemia and the median number of hypoxemia episodes were 0.0 s (IQR, 0.0–22.0) and 0 (IQR, 0–1). In contrast, the values were 0.0 s (IQR, 0.0–0.0) and 0 (IQR, 0.0–0.0), respectively, in the HFNO group (p < 0.001) (Table 2).

Discussion

High-altitude residents undergo physiological acclimatization mechanisms to adapt to hypobaric and hypoxic high-altitude environment [9, 14]. While these changes help adapt to sustained hypoxia, they can also lead to chronic high-altitude conditions like chronic mountain sickness (CMS) and high-altitude pulmonary hypertension (PH) [15]. Interestingly, Tibetans, unlike other high-altitude populations, have relatively low hemoglobin concentrations and red blood cell counts [16]. They occasionally experience severe chronic high-altitude conditions [17], likely due to better hypoxia tolerance from their ancestors’ early migration to the plateau. Notably, the oxygen saturation levels of these highlanders are significantly lower than those of residents at sea level [17], consistent with our data (91%, [IQR, 89–92]). Importantly, acclimatization to high altitudes does not eliminate the risk of hypoxia during procedures under sedation. We observed that when oxygen was delivered via CNC during gastroscopy, 28.6% of patients experienced a drop in SpO2 below baseline, despite successful pre-sedation oxygenation.

Due to the inherent hypoxia at high altitudes, any disruption in oxygenation or ventilation during procedures may lead to moderate or severe hypoxemia, posing a serious risk to organ function and compromising patient safety. Jiang et al. reported that 47.2% and 25.0% of high-altitude patients experienced moderate or severe hypoxemia, respectively, during gastrointestinal endoscopy under deep sedation using supplemental oxygen via CNC [18]. In our study, severe hypoxemia occurred in 11.4% of patients. The difference may be attributed to the oxygen flow rate, which was set at 6 L per minute in our study compared to 2 L per minute in Jiang’s study. Secondly, different dosages of anesthetics may also exert an influence. In Jiang’s study, the median dose of propofol was 115 mg (85–150) and that of fentanyl was 500ug (0-750) in CNC group. In contrast, we used relatively low doses of propofol (90 mg, 80–90) and fentanyl (25ug, 25–25), which may result in less respiratory depression. In addition, we speculate that lower proportion of original hypoxia (defined as SpO2 < 90% before oxygenation) in our study compared with Jiang’s (30.5% vs. 34.7%) may also be one of the factors contributing to the differences in the results.

Our study focused on the elderly population, as the issue of an aging society in China is becoming more pronounced. The number of elderly individuals requiring healthcare is increasing. Dong et al. reported a significant positive correlation between altitude and frailty in Chinese older adults, with higher altitudes showing a greater degree of correlation [19]. This indicates that the physical condition of elderly highlanders requires special attention. Respiratory health in the elderly is another critical issue; COPD is significantly associated with being aged ≥ 40 years in populations living at higher altitudes [20]. Thus, the physical weakening associated with aging may increase the risk of anesthesia.

The principal finding of this study is that, at high altitude, compared to CNC (6 L per minute), the HFNO system (60 L per minute) significantly reduced the incidence of hypoxemia in elderly patients undergoing gastroscopy with sedation. Despite similar baseline SpO2 values between the groups, the minimum SpO2 was much higher in the HFNO group than in the CNC group. Additionally, 28.6% of patients in the CNC group required airway intervention due to hypoxemia. The lower partial pressure of oxygen in the ambient air at high altitudes makes it difficult for oxygen to diffuse into pulmonary capillaries. We believe that persistent positive pressure ventilation with 100% oxygen via HFNO significantly improves oxygen diffusion. These results are consistent with our previous data collected at sea level [11], suggesting that HFNO may be an effective method for oxygen delivery regardless of altitude.

In the present study, we found that the recovery time was significantly shorter in the HFNO group compared to the CNC group. We hypothesize that the adequate oxygen supply provided by the HFNO system may positively influence drug metabolism. The use of HFNO not only reduces the workload of anesthesiologists but also enhances patient safety. In our study, four patients experienced severe hypoxemia. Two of these patients did not respond to jaw thrust and mask ventilation with persistent SpO2 < 75% lasting over 60 s, prompting a switch to HFNO therapy. After that, their SpO2 quickly increased and stabilized around 95%. Analyses on both the ITT and PP sets demonstrated consistent statistical differences across all evaluated indicators. It has been reported that airway pressures in the main bronchus, trachea, and pharynx increase with the flow rate generated by HFNO [21]. Indeed, HFNO may help maintain passive oxygenation, increase functional residual capacity, and improve the inspired fraction of oxygen, thereby extending the safe apnea period. Additionally, HFNO provides heated and humidified oxygen, which can alleviate airway dryness and reduce the risk of epistaxis. These may partly explain why we did not observe differences in comfort levels between the groups.

This study expands the application scenarios of HFNO but also has certain limitations. First, residents of high-altitude areas typically have higher hemoglobin (Hb) concentrations than those at sea level, due to erythrocyte proliferation as a compensatory mechanism. We did not collect Hb data, as routine blood tests were not mandatory before gastroscopy. We believe this is the primary limitation of our study. Further research on whether Hb concentration affects oxygen saturation during gastroscopy is warranted. Second, for safety reasons, we did not include elderly patients with severe respiratory diseases which resulted in limited generalizability of the study results. Lastly, evidences from animal study have shown that the clearance of propofol decreases, while peak plasma concentration, half-life, and average residence time increase in high-altitude hypoxic environments [22]. Another study on the frequency of anesthetic overdose at high altitude, guided by mean alveolar concentration, found a 20% incidence of overdose in patients [23]. Therefore, it remains undetermined whether the pharmacokinetics of anesthetics is altered in our study and subsequently exerts an impact on the development of hypoxemia.

In summary, compared to CNC, HFNO reduces the occurrence of hypoxemia in elderly patients undergoing gastroscopy with sedation at high altitudes. In environments of hypobaric hypoxia, the advantages of HFNO suggest it may be a preferred oxygenation strategy.
