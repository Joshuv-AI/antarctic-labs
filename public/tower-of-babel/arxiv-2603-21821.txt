Isogeometric analysis with C 1 C^{1} cubic Powell–Sabin splines
Jan Grošelj, Ada Šadl Praprotnik, Hendrik Speleers

Abstract
In this paper, we consider C 1 {C}^{1} cubic Powell–Sabin splines for the numerical solution of boundary value problems on planar and spatial surface domains. We first review the construction and basic properties of polynomial and rational C 1 {C}^{1} cubic Powell–Sabin spline representations on unstructured triangulations. Then, we discuss how these flexible representations can be exploited to create geometry mappings suited for a precise description of (classes of) surface domains. This is illustrated with several examples. Finally, the obtained domain descriptions are utilized in the isogeometric analysis framework for solving various Poisson and biharmonic problems. It is demonstrated that C 1 {C}^{1} cubic Powell–Sabin splines form a powerful alternative to C 0 {C}^{0} cubic Lagrange elements and bicubic NURBS.

Introduction

Polynomial splines over a triangulation are an extensively studied approximation framework in numerical analysis. Numerous constructions have their origin in the theory of finite elements, where they serve as building blocks in space discretizations for solving partial differential equations (PDEs). In this context, they are traditionally characterized by an interpolation problem such that each polynomial piece over a triangle of the triangulation is determined by local interpolation data
[
3
]
.

Isogeometric analysis is a spin-off of finite element analysis focusing mainly on bridging the gap between simulation and design
[
24
,
5
]
. In design systems, models are commonly parametrized by tensor-product B-splines, i.e., a set of locally supported basis functions that form a convex partition of unity
[
6
]
. The splines expressed in such a basis come equipped with a geometrically intuitive control structure and stable evaluation algorithms. The key concept of isogeometric analysis is to use these basis functions also as an approximation framework to solve PDEs in the isoparametric sense. This means that both, the parametrization of the domain and the numerical solution on the domain, are expressed by the same set of basis functions. For this approach to be computationally effective, a crucial step has been the generalization of standard basis functions to support adaptive refinement strategies; see, e.g.,
[
34
,
13
,
10
]
.

Although finite element analysis and isogeometric analysis are built on the same foundations, the theories have quite ambivalent outlook on the underlying mesh and space discretizations. Isogeometric analysis highlights the role of splines in the approximation process and is focused on the investigation of effects that spline properties, such as high-order smoothness and convex representation, have on the accuracy and stability of the numerical solution of a PDE. On the other hand, finite element analysis is commonly performed on geometries discretized by triangular meshes using only continuous or even discontinuous splines that are considered separately on each element of the partition.

In order to adapt splines over a triangulation to the isogeometric analysis framework, an important step is to establish spline representations in terms of basis functions that mimic the properties of B-splines, in particular the nonnegativity and partition of unity. In a local sense, such a representation is provided by the Bernstein–Bézier
[
12
,
27
]
or simplex spline
[
4
,
33
,
30
,
31
,
32
]
techniques. The transition between local and global context can be carried out by finding a minimal determining set of the chosen smoothness, which identifies the degrees of freedom and presents an alternative to using interpolation functionals. This approach has been successfully tested in applications related to isogeometric analysis; see, e.g.,
[
25
,
50
,
45
,
49
,
51
,
11
]
.

Global B-spline techniques over a triangulation are looking for locally supported basis functions that form a convex partition of unity and have a certain order of smoothness on the entire domain. Such basis functions can be constructed by similar techniques as local basis functions but usually require specific configurations or geometric assumptions that allow for the direct incorporation of smoothness properties into their definition. An example of such basis functions obtained by means of simplex splines are the so-called TCB-splines
[
29
,
46
]
. On the other hand, the Bernstein–Bézier techniques have enabled the development of several B-spline-like representations for classical and non-classical finite elements of Argyris
[
15
,
16
]
, Clough–Tocher
[
42
,
17
]
, and Powell–Sabin
[
9
,
41
,
43
,
22
]
type.

This paper is focused on utilizing
{C}^{1}
cubic Powell–Sabin B-splines in the isogeometric analysis framework. The B-spline representation for Powell–Sabin splines of degree
2
is well-established and widely tested in the context of geometric modeling
[
47
,
48
,
37
,
39
]
and finite element applications
[
35
,
36
,
38
,
40
,
14
]
. The degree
3
offers several options for definition of a suitable spline space
[
2
,
28
,
44
,
18
,
20
]
. Here, we consider the macro-element space introduced in
[
44
]
that has a dimension very similar to
{C}^{0}
cubic Lagrange elements, possesses some attractive
{C}^{2}
super-smoothness properties, and is globally representable by a set of B-spline-like functions. We take advantage of the properties of these functions and generalize the piecewise polynomial space to a vector space spanned by rational B-spline functions.

A rational B-spline representation over an unstructured triangulation provides a versatile approximation framework. In the context of isogeometric analysis it can be used to express, either in exact or approximate way, the geometries that are usually described by tensor-product NURBS. This may gracefully resolve some common issues with NURBS geometries, such as
{C}^{1}
spline parametrization at extraordinary points, irregular local mesh refinements, and restriction to four-sided domains.

In this paper, we develop techniques and present examples of geometry descriptions based on
{C}^{1}
cubic Powell–Sabin B-splines. By studying the properties of the basis functions, we also discuss how to satisfy boundary conditions imposed on a domain. Then, we apply the splines to solve second and fourth order boundary value problems on surface domains with the isogeometric analysis approach. Through numerical examples we demonstrate that these splines perform comparably to or better than
{C}^{0}
cubic Lagrange elements and bicubic NURBS, thereby providing a powerful tool combining the benefits of the two standard frameworks.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section

5

Cubic Powell–Sabin splines

2.1
Powell–Sabin refinement

Let
\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a closed polygonal region with connected boundary
\partial\Theta
. We consider this region as a parametric domain. Moreover, let
\triangle
be a triangulation of
\Theta
represented by a triplet
\triangle=(V,E,T)
, where
V
is the set of vertices,
E
is the set of edges, and
T
is the set of triangles of the triangulation
\triangle
. These sets are more precisely specified as follows.

•

The set
V
consists of points
v_{i}\in\Theta
indexed by
i
in range from
1
to the cardinality
|V|
of
V
.

•

The set
E
consists of edges
e_{l}
indexed by
l
in range from
1
to the cardinality
|E|
of
E
. An edge is the convex hull
[v_{i},v_{j}]\subset\Theta
of two distinct points
v_{i},v_{j}\in V
.

•

The set
T
consists of triangles
t_{m}
indexed by
m
in range from
1
to the cardinality
|T|
of
T
. A triangle is the convex hull
[v_{i},v_{j},v_{k}]\subseteq\Theta
of three noncollinear points
v_{i},v_{j},v_{k}\in V
.

The union of triangles in
T
is
\Theta
, and we impose that the intersection of two distinct triangles from
T
is either empty or corresponds to a common vertex or edge of the two triangles. We also assume that each edge in
E
is an edge of a triangle in
T
, and each vertex in
V
is a vertex of a triangle in
T
. Additionally, we denote by
V^{b}\subseteq V
the set of boundary vertices and by
E^{b}\subseteq E
the set of boundary edges. Namely,
v_{i}\in V
is in
V^{b}
if
v_{i}\in\partial\Theta
, and
e_{l}\in E
is in
E^{b}
if
e_{l}\subset\partial\Theta
.

The Powell–Sabin refinement of
\triangle
is a triangulation
{\triangle}_{\mathrm{PS}}
obtained by splitting each triangle of
T
into six smaller triangles in the following way. Let
t_{m}\in T
. Suppose
e_{l}\in E\setminus E^{b}
, and let
t_{m^{\prime}}\in T
be the triangle sharing the edge
e_{l}
with
t_{m}
. Choose the triangle split points
v_{m}^{t}
and
v_{m^{\prime}}^{t}
in the interior of
t_{m}
and
t_{m^{\prime}}
, respectively, so that the line segment
[v_{m}^{t},v_{m^{\prime}}^{t}]
intersects the interior of the edge
e_{l}
. The point of intersection is denoted by
v_{l}^{e}
and is called an edge split point. If
e_{l}\in E^{b}
, choose the split point
v_{l}^{e}
to be any point in the interior of the edge. The refinement of
t_{m}
into six smaller triangles is obtained by connecting the triangle split point
v_{m}^{t}
to the vertices of
t_{m}
and to the edge split points located on the edges of
t_{m}
.

Applying the refinement process to each triangle of

{\triangle}_{\mathrm{PS}}

, where
{V}_{\mathrm{PS}}
is the set of vertices,
{E}_{\mathrm{PS}}
is the set of edges, and
{T}_{\mathrm{PS}}
is the set of triangles of
{\triangle}_{\mathrm{PS}}
. Each
{t}_{\mathrm{PS}}\in{T}_{\mathrm{PS}}
is of the form
{t}_{\mathrm{PS}}=[v_{i},v_{l}^{e},v_{m}^{t}]
for some indices
i
,
l
,
m
, such that
t_{m}\in T
,
e_{l}\in E
is an edge of
t_{m}
, and
v_{i}\in V
is an endpoint of
e_{l}
.

2.2
Cubic B-spline representation

Let
\mathbb{P}_{3}
denote the space of bivariate polynomials of total degree at most three. For a given triangulation
\triangle
of
\Theta
and a refinement
{\triangle}_{\mathrm{PS}}
of
\triangle
we consider the vector space

\mathbb{S}_{3}({\triangle}_{\mathrm{PS}})=\left\{s\in{C}^{1}(\Theta):\ s|_{{t}_{\mathrm{PS}}}\in\mathbb{P}_{3},\ {t}_{\mathrm{PS}}\in{\triangle}_{\mathrm{PS}}\right\}\cap{C}^{2}\left(\left\{v_{m}^{t}:\ t_{m}\in T\right\}\right)\cap{C}^{2}\left(\left\{v_{l}^{e}:\ e_{l}\in E^{b}\right\}\right),

i.e., the space of cubic splines over
{\triangle}_{\mathrm{PS}}
that are
{C}^{1}
smooth everywhere and
{C}^{2}
smooth at every triangle split point and at every boundary edge split point. Remarkably, these additional
{C}^{2}
super-smoothness conditions imply that every spline in
\mathbb{S}_{3}({\triangle}_{\mathrm{PS}})
is
{C}^{2}
smooth at
[v_{m}^{t},v_{l}^{e})
for any triangle
t_{m}\in T
and any edge
e_{l}\in E
attached to this triangle.

The space
\mathbb{S}_{3}({\triangle}_{\mathrm{PS}})
was introduced in
[
44
]
and has a convenient dimension formula, i.e.,
\dim(\mathbb{S}_{3}({\triangle}_{\mathrm{PS}}))=3|V|+2|E|
. By definition, it contains
\mathbb{P}_{3}
and thereby allows optimal approximation order. Moreover, a spline from
\mathbb{S}_{3}({\triangle}_{\mathrm{PS}})
can be locally characterized and represented by B-spline-like basis functions. Namely,

•

three basis functions
B_{i,r}^{v}:\Theta\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
,
r=1,2,3
, for each
v_{i}\in V
,

•

two basis functions
B_{l,r}^{e}:\Theta\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
,
r=1,2
, for each
e_{l}\in E
.

All basis functions are nonnegative and form a partition of unity. The nonnegativity and shape properties are closely related to a choice of triangles associated with the vertices of
\triangle
, which define a configuration of the basis functions (for an illustration, see Figure
1
). This configuration is discussed in Section
3.2
. Further details on the construction of the basis and its properties are presented in
[
44
,
20
]
. Examples of these basis functions are shown in Figures
3
and
5
.

Thanks to the B-spline-like basis functions, the polynomial framework of
{C}^{1}
cubic Powell–Sabin splines can be readily generalized to a rational one
[
19
]
. For each
v_{i}\in V
and
r\in\left\{1,2,3\right\}
, let us associate with
B_{i,r}^{v}
a weight
w_{i,r}^{v}\in(0,\infty)
, and, for each
e_{l}\in E
and
r\in\left\{1,2\right\}
, let us associate with
B_{l,r}^{e}
a weight
w_{l,r}^{e}\in(0,\infty)
. Moreover, let the spline
W\in\mathbb{S}_{3}({\triangle}_{\mathrm{PS}})
be given by

W=\sum_{i=1}^{|V|}\sum_{r=1}^{3}w_{i,r}^{v}B_{i,r}^{v}+\sum_{l=1}^{|E|}\sum_{r=1}^{2}w_{l,r}^{e}B_{l,r}^{e}.

The spline
W
is positive everywhere. Based on this spline and the weights, we define a set of rational basis functions. Namely,

•

three basis functions
N_{i,r}^{v}:\Theta\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
,
r=1,2,3
, for each
v_{i}\in V
, given by

N_{i,r}^{v}=\frac{w_{i,r}^{v}}{W}B_{i,r}^{v},

•

two basis functions
N_{l,r}^{e}:\Theta\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
,
r=1,2
, for each
e_{l}\in E
, given by

N_{l,r}^{e}=\frac{w_{l,r}^{e}}{W}B_{l,r}^{e}.

By the assumptions on the weights, these functions are well-defined and nonnegative. The functions are rational and piecewise cubic in numerator and denominator. If all
w_{i,r}^{v}
and all
w_{l,r}^{e}
are equal,
N_{i,r}^{v}=B_{i,r}^{v}
and
N_{l,r}^{e}=B_{l,r}^{e}
. For a fixed set of weights
w_{i,r}^{v}
and
w_{l,r}^{e}
, we denote by
\mathbb{S}_{3}^{w}({\triangle}_{\mathrm{PS}})
the vector space spanned by
N_{i,r}^{v}
and
N_{l,r}^{e}
.

Finally, let us note that the gradients of
N_{i,r}^{v}
and
N_{l,r}^{e}
can be computed based on the gradients of
B_{i,r}^{v}
and
B_{l,r}^{e}
in a straightforward manner. Namely,

\nabla N_{i,r}^{v}=\frac{w_{i,r}^{v}}{W}\nabla B_{i,r}^{v}-\frac{N_{i,r}^{v}}{W}\,\nabla W,\qquad\nabla N_{l,r}^{e}=\frac{w_{l,r}^{e}}{W}\nabla B_{l,r}^{e}-\frac{N_{l,r}^{e}}{W}\,\nabla W.

Prelude to approximation

3.1
Geometry mapping

Let
\mathbb{S}_{3}^{w}({\triangle}_{\mathrm{PS}})^{3}=\mathbb{S}_{3}^{w}({\triangle}_{\mathrm{PS}})\times\mathbb{S}_{3}^{w}({\triangle}_{\mathrm{PS}})\times\mathbb{S}_{3}^{w}({\triangle}_{\mathrm{PS}})
. The basis functions
N_{i,r}^{v}
and
N_{l,r}^{e}
provide a flexible tool to define a geometry mapping
F\in\mathbb{S}_{3}^{w}({\triangle}_{\mathrm{PS}})^{3}
such that
\Omega=F(\Theta)\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
is a bounded surface with connected boundary
\partial\Omega=F(\partial\Theta)
. Suppose
F
is a diffeomorphism given by

F=\sum_{i=1}^{|V|}\sum_{r=1}^{3}(x_{i,r}^{v},y_{i,r}^{v},z_{i,r}^{v})N_{i,r}^{v}+\sum_{l=1}^{|E|}\sum_{r=1}^{2}(x_{l,r}^{e},y_{l,r}^{e},z_{l,r}^{e})N_{l,r}^{e}

(1)

for some
x_{i,r}^{v},y_{i,r}^{v},z_{i,r}^{v}\in\mathbb{R}
and
x_{l,r}^{e},y_{l,r}^{e},z_{l,r}^{e}\in\mathbb{R}
. Let
J_{F}:\Theta\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3\times 2}
denote the mapping assigning to each point the Jacobian matrix of
F
, which is assumed to be of full rank. Then, the mapping
K_{F}:\Theta\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
given by
K_{F}={J_{F}}^{T}J_{F}
assigns to each point a symmetric positive definite matrix, and the function
\kappa_{F}:\Theta\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
given by
\kappa_{F}=\sqrt{\det(K_{F})}
is well-defined and positive.

The surface representation in (
1
) is well-suited for freeform modeling. As the basis functions form a convex partition of unity on
\Theta
, one can consider
(x_{i,r}^{v},y_{i,r}^{v},z_{i,r}^{v})
and
(x_{l,r}^{e},y_{l,r}^{e},z_{l,r}^{e})
in (
1
) as control points to modify the surface. Additionally, the weights appearing in
N_{i,r}^{v}
and
N_{l,r}^{e}
can be used to finetune the shape.

In the special case where all
z_{i,r}^{v}
and all
z_{l,r}^{e}
are zero, we can consider
F
as a mapping to
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, i.e.,
F\in\mathbb{S}_{3}^{w}({\triangle}_{\mathrm{PS}})^{2}
, and
\Omega
as a bounded domain in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Under the previous assumptions, the mapping
J_{F}
then assigns to each point from
\Theta
an invertible matrix, and the function
\det(J_{F}):\Theta\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is either positive or negative. Commonly, the former is required for
F
to preserve the orientation.

Often, one describes a planar domain
\Omega
by specifying the parametrization of its boundary in the form of a bijective spline curve
C:\partial\Theta\rightarrow\partial\Omega
and defines
F
on
\Theta
by minimizing a suitable energy functional under the conditions that
F(\partial\Theta)=C(\partial\Theta)=\partial\Omega
and
\det(J_{F})
is positive. This is a sufficient condition for the bijectivity of
F

[
26
]
. The following example is taken from
[
40
]

3.2
Approximation function and boundary conditions

To define an approximation function on a domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
parametrized by a geometry mapping
F\in\mathbb{S}_{3}^{w}({\triangle}_{\mathrm{PS}})^{3}
, we follow the isoparametric approach and set a function
\varphi:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
as
\varphi=s\circ F^{-1}
for some
s\in\mathbb{S}_{3}^{w}({\triangle}_{\mathrm{PS}})
. We consider
s
as

s=\sum_{i=1}^{|V|}\sum_{r=1}^{3}c_{i,r}^{v}N_{i,r}^{v}+\sum_{l=1}^{|E|}\sum_{r=1}^{2}c_{l,r}^{e}N_{l,r}^{e},

(2)

where the coefficients
c_{i,r}^{v}\in\mathbb{R}
and
c_{l,r}^{e}\in\mathbb{R}
are free parameters in the approximation process. Typically, a subset of these coefficients is fixed by boundary conditions. More particularly, in Section
4
we deal with two types of boundary conditions. The first is the standard Dirichlet condition
\varphi=g_{0}
on
\partial\Omega
for some function
g_{0}:\partial\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, which implies

s=g_{0}\circ F\qquad\text{on $\partial\Theta.$}

(3a)

The second is the Dirichlet condition supplemented by
\nabla_{\Omega}\varphi\cdot n_{\partial\Omega}=g_{1}
on
\partial\Omega
for some function
g_{1}:\partial\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, where
\nabla_{\Omega}
is the surface gradient
[
8
]
and
n_{\partial\Omega}:\partial\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3}
is the vector field specifying the outward unit vector normal to the boundary
\partial\Omega
, i.e., the vector collinear to the cross product of the tangent to the boundary and the normal to the surface
\Omega
at the considered point on
\partial\Omega
. In relation to the parametrization
F
of
\Omega
, the surface gradient can be expressed as

\nabla_{\Omega}\varphi\circ F=\nabla_{\Omega}(s\circ F^{-1})\circ F=J_{F}{K_{F}}^{-1}\nabla s.

Moreover, let us denote by
\tau:\partial\Theta\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
the vector field specifying the tangent and by
\nu:\partial\Theta\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
the vector field specifying the outward unit normal to the piecewise linear boundary of
\Theta
. Since
J_{F}\tau
is tangent to the boundary of
\Omega
, there exists a scalar field
\gamma:\partial\Theta\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that
n_{\partial\Omega}\circ F=\gamma J_{F}{K_{F}}^{-1}\nu
. This implies

\nabla s\cdot\gamma{K_{F}}^{-1}\nu=g_{1}\circ F\qquad\text{on $\partial\Theta.$}

(3b)

Thus, in order to identify the coefficients affected by the conditions in (
3
), it is important to have a better understanding of the supports of the basis functions
N_{i,r}^{v}
and
N_{l,r}^{e}
and their gradients.

It is clear from the definition of
N_{i,r}^{v}
and
N_{l,r}^{e}
that their supports match the supports of the corresponding basis functions
B_{i,r}^{v}
and
B_{l,r}^{e}
. Thus, following the construction in
[
44
, Section 3]
, the support of
N_{i,r}^{v}
is contained in the union of triangles of
\triangle
that have the vertex at
v_{i}
(see Figure
3
), and the support of
N_{l,r}^{e}
is contained in the union of triangles of
\triangle
that have the edge
e_{l}
in common (see Figure
5
). Moreover, at a vertex
v_{i}\in V
only the basis functions
N_{i,r}^{v}
have nonzero values and gradients, and on an edge
e_{l}=[v_{i},v_{j}]\in E
only the basis functions
N_{i,r}^{v}
,
N_{j,r}^{v}
, and
N_{l,r}^{e}
may have nonzero values and gradients. If
e_{l}
is a boundary edge, then one of
N_{l,r}^{e}
,
r=1,2
, is zero on
e_{l}
(see Figure
5(b)
), while the values of
N_{i,r}^{v}
and
N_{j,r}^{v}
on
e_{l}
depend on the choice of triangles that define the configuration of
B_{i,r}^{v}
and
B_{l,r}^{e}
.

The configuration of the basis functions is determined by a set of triangles in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, one for each vertex
v_{i}\in V
(see Figure
1
). The triangle
q_{i}=[q_{i,1},q_{i,2},q_{i,3}]\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
associated with
v_{i}
contains the vertex
v_{i}
and, for each
t_{m}=[v_{i},v_{l}^{e},v_{m}^{t}]\in{\triangle}_{\mathrm{PS}}
with a vertex at
v_{i}
, also the points
\frac{2}{3}v_{i}+\frac{1}{3}v_{l}^{e}
and
\frac{2}{3}v_{i}+\frac{1}{3}v_{m}^{t}
. This ensures the nonnegativity of the basis functions
[
44
, Theorem 3]
but also gives some further insight into the values of
B_{i,r}^{v}
on the edge
e_{l}
. From
[
44
, Sections 3.2 and 3.3]
it follows that they are completely specified by two barycentric coordinates with respect to the triangle
q_{i}
, i.e., the barycentric coordinate of the point
v_{i}
and the barycentric coordinate of the point
\frac{2}{3}v_{i}+\frac{1}{3}v_{l}^{e}
, both corresponding to the vertex
q_{i,r}
. In the specific case where the triangle
q_{i}
is positioned in such a way that its edge opposite
q_{i,r}
lies on the line containing the edge
e_{l}
, both these coordinates are equal to zero and hence the restriction of
B_{i,r}^{v}
to
e_{l}
is zero. Due to the constraints on the triangle
q_{i}
this is possible only when
v_{i}
is a vertex on the boundary of
\Theta
. Moreover, if
B_{i,r}^{v}
is to be equal to zero on the entire boundary, the two boundary edges of
\triangle
that meet at
v_{i}
must lie on the same line. In this situation only two of the three basis functions associated with
v_{i}
are nonzero on
\partial\Theta
. In order to minimize the number of coefficients affected by the boundary conditions, we adhere to such positioning of triangles on the boundary. For an illustration, see Figure
1
and compare Figures
3(b)
and
3(c)
.

To summarize, let the set
V^{b}
of boundary vertices of
\triangle
be considered as the disjoint union of
\widehat{V}^{b}
and
\bar{V}^{b}
, where
v_{i}\in V^{b}
belongs to
\widehat{V}^{b}
if the two boundary edges of
\triangle
that meet at
v_{i}
do not lie on the same line, and to
\bar{V}^{b}
otherwise. Then, a suitable configuration of triangles
q_{i}
ensures that the number of nonzero basis functions on
\partial\Theta
is
b_{0}=3|\widehat{V}^{b}|+2|\bar{V}^{b}|+|E^{b}|=4|\widehat{V}^{b}|+3|\bar{V}^{b}|
. The number of basis functions that are nonzero on
\partial\Theta
or have a nonvanishing gradient on
\partial\Theta
is
b_{1}=3|V^{b}|+2|E^{b}|=5|V^{b}|
, regardless of the configuration.

In general, the boundary conditions in (
3
) cannot be satisfied exactly. If the condition (
3a
) is imposed, we utilize the least squares fitting on a dense set of points on
\partial\Theta
to determine the identified
b_{0}
coefficients and compute
s_{0}\in\mathbb{S}_{3}^{w}({\triangle}_{\mathrm{PS}})
that approximates
g_{0}\circ F
. Similarly, if both (
3a
) and (
3b
) are imposed, we determine the identified
b_{1}
coefficients and compute
s_{1}\in\mathbb{S}_{3}^{w}({\triangle}_{\mathrm{PS}})
such that
s_{1}
and
\nabla s_{1}
provide the best least squares fit to
g_{0}\circ F
and
g_{1}\circ F
in the sense of (
3
).

To simplify the notation in the rest of the paper, we rename and reindex the basis functions
N_{i,r}^{v}
and
N_{l,r}^{e}
to
N_{k}
,
k=1,2,\ldots,3|V|+2|E|
, in such a way that when (
3a
) is imposed, the first
n_{0}=3|V|+2|E|-b_{0}
basis functions are zero on
\partial\Theta
, and when (
3a
) and (
3b
) are imposed, the first
n_{1}=3|V|+2|E|-b_{1}
basis functions are zero on
\partial\Theta
and their gradients are zero on
\partial\Theta
as well. Thus (
2
) can be rewritten as

s=s_{d}+\sum_{k=1}^{n_{d}}c_{k}N_{k},\qquad d=0,1,

(4)

and the conditions
c_{k}\in\mathbb{R}
represent the remaining degrees of freedom.

4
Approximation problems

4.1
Poisson problems

Let
\Delta_{\Omega}=\nabla_{\Omega}\cdot\nabla_{\Omega}
be the Laplace–Beltrami operator on the surface domain
\Omega
parametrized by the geometry mapping
F\in\mathbb{S}_{3}^{w}({\triangle}_{\mathrm{PS}})^{3}
. We are interested in finding an approximation
s\in\mathbb{S}_{3}^{w}({\triangle}_{\mathrm{PS}})
to a function
u:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
satisfying

\left\{\begin{aligned} -\Delta_{\Omega}u&=f&&\text{on}\ \Omega\setminus\partial\Omega,\\
u&=g_{0}&&\text{on}\ \partial\Omega,\end{aligned}\right.

(5)

for given functions
f:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and
g_{0}:\partial\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. To this end, we follow
[
7
]
and consider the weak formulation of (
5
) under suitable smoothness conditions on
f
and
g_{0}
, in particular
f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
. After the discretization based on
\mathbb{S}_{3}^{w}({\triangle}_{\mathrm{PS}})
and the pull-back to the parametric domain
\Theta
, we arrive at the conditions

\int_{\Theta}\nabla s\cdot\left({K_{F}}^{-1}\,\nabla N_{j}\right)\,\kappa_{F}\,\mathrm{d}\Theta=\int_{\Theta}(f\circ F)\,N_{j}\,\kappa_{F}\,\mathrm{d}\Theta,\qquad j=1,2,\ldots,n_{0},

(6)

and the boundary conditions (
3a
) imposed on the spline
s
expressed in the form (
4
) for
d=0
. Then, by (
6
), the coefficients of
s
collected in the vector
c=(c_{k})_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{0}}
are the solution of the system of linear equations
Ac=b
, where the entries of the matrix
A=(a_{j,k})_{j,k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{0}\times n_{0}}
are given by

a_{j,k}=\int_{\Theta}\nabla N_{k}\cdot\left({K_{F}}^{-1}\,\nabla N_{j}\right)\,\kappa_{F}\,\mathrm{d}\Theta,

and the entries of the vector
b=(b_{j})_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{0}}
are given by

b_{j}=\int_{\Theta}\left((f\circ F)\,N_{j}-\nabla s_{0}\cdot\left({K_{F}}^{-1}\,\nabla N_{j}\right)\right)\,\kappa_{F}\,\mathrm{d}\Theta.

When
\Omega=\Theta
and
F:\Theta\rightarrow\Omega
is the identity mapping in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, the weak form discretization (
6
) reduces to the standard Ritz–Galerkin method on
\mathbb{S}_{3}({\triangle}_{\mathrm{PS}})
. In the following example we compare this method with the common approach based on cubic Lagrange elements. We note that the cubic Lagrange elements over
\triangle
have a very comparable number of degrees of freedom, i.e.,
n_{0}\approx(|V|-|V^{b}|)+2(|E|-|E^{b}|)+|T|
because
|T|=2|V|-|V^{b}|-2
. The results indicate that the Powell–Sabin elements provide a slight but consistent improvement over the Lagrange elements.

5
Conclusion

In this paper, we utilized
{C}^{1}
cubic Powell–Sabin splines in the isogeometric analysis framework for the numerical solution of various boundary value problems on planar and spatial surface domains. First, the polynomial
{C}^{1}
cubic Powell–Sabin spline space with additional
{C}^{2}
super-smoothness properties from
[
44
]
was reviewed and its rational extension was introduced. Then, both polynomial and rational splines were exploited to define different types of geometry mappings, providing a flexible tool for precise description of (classes of) planar and spatial surfaces. Finally, the obtained surfaces were considered as physical domains in several examples of Poisson and biharmonic problems.

The numerical experiments conducted in this research indicate that
{C}^{1}
cubic Powell–Sabin splines perform comparably to or better than cubic Lagrange elements and bicubic NURBS. Their advantage in comparison to Lagrange elements is that they provide
{C}^{1}
and not just
{C}^{0}
parametrizations. In contrast to tensor product splines, they are not limited to four-sided domains and enable simpler and more versatile domain refinement, which is crucial for efficient approximation. Future work employing higher-degree Powell–Sabin splines
[
43
,
23
]
may further enrich the considered approximation framework.
