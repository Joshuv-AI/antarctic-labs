Approximations of the Mortensen observer using higher order extended Kalman filters
Tobias Breiten ⋆ and Justus Ramme ⋆ and Jesper Schröder †

Abstract
. A polynomial approximation of the minimum energy estimator, also called Mortensen observer, is discussed. The method relies on successive differentiations of an underlying value function and the Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman equation, respectively. By means of neglecting higher order derivatives of the value function along the unknown observer trajectory, a coupled set of nonlinear tensor structured differential equations is derived. In its simplest form, the approach boils down to the well-known extended Kalman filter. Numerical experiments with polynomials up to the order eight illustrate the potential of the new approach and indicate local convergence to the Mortensen observer.



Keywords:
Mortensen observer, Kalman filter, nonlinear state estimation, generalized Lyapunov equations, polynomial approximations

AMS subject classification:15A69, 49L12, 93B53, 93C15

1.
Introduction

This work considers a nonlinear system of the form

(1.1)

\displaystyle\dot{x}(t)

\displaystyle=f(x(t))+Gv(t),\penalty\ t\in(0,T],

\displaystyle x(0)

\displaystyle=x_{0}+\eta,

where
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n},G\in\mathbb{R}^{n,m}
and
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
are known characteristics of the dynamic and
v\in L^{2}(0,T;\mathbb{R}^{m}),\eta\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
denote unknown deterministic disturbances. Given
measurements

(1.2)

y(t)=h(x(t))+\mu(t)

with
h\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{p}
which are subject to deterministic disturbances
\mu\in L^{2}(0,T;\mathbb{R}^{p}),
our goal is to derive a sequence of nonlinear equations which locally approximate the minimum energy estimator, also called Mortensen observer, originally proposed in
[
29
]
. Referring to the latter work, let us consider the Luenberger-type observer

(1.3)

\displaystyle\dot{\widehat{x}}_{\mathrm{M}}(t)

\displaystyle=f(\widehat{x}_{\mathrm{M}}(t))+(\nabla^{2}\mathcal{V}(t,\widehat{x}_{\mathrm{M}}(t)))^{-1}\mathcal{J}_{h}(\widehat{x}_{\mathrm{M}}(t))^{\top}Q(y(t)-h(\widehat{x}_{\mathrm{M}}(t))),\penalty\ t\in(0,T],

\displaystyle\widehat{x}_{\mathrm{M}}(0)

\displaystyle=x_{0},

where
\nabla^{2}\mathcal{V}
and
\mathcal{J}_{h}
denote the Hessian and the Jacobian with respect to the spatial variable, respectively.
The time and space dependent output injection operator is implicitly characterized by the following time-dependent, nonhomogeneous Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman (HJB) equation

(1.4)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathcal{V}(t,\xi)

\displaystyle=-\nabla\mathcal{V}(t,\xi)^{\top}f(\xi)-\frac{1}{2}\|G^{\top}\nabla\mathcal{V}(t,\xi)\|_{R^{-1}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|y(t)-h(\xi)\|_{Q}^{2},

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}(0,\xi)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\|\xi-x_{0}\|_{\Gamma}^{2},

where
\nabla\mathcal{V}
denotes the spatial gradient.
Here,
\mathcal{V}\colon[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is the minimal value function associated with the (backwards running) optimal control problem

(1.5)

\displaystyle\underset{v\in L^{2}(0,t;\mathbb{R}^{m})}{\min}\penalty\ \mathcal{J}(t,\xi;v)=\frac{1}{2}\|x(0)-x_{0}\|_{\Gamma}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\|v(s)\|_{R}^{2}+\alpha\|y(s)-h(x(s))\|_{Q}^{2}\penalty\ \mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\text{s.t. }\dot{x}(s)=f(x(s))+Gv(s),\penalty\ s\in(0,t),\penalty\ x(t)=\xi,

with symmetric, positive definite weighting matrices
\Gamma
,
R
, and
Q
.
Assuming sufficient smoothness of
\mathcal{V}
, in
[
29
]
and later also in
[
15
]
it was shown that if one defines an estimate as

(1.6)

\widehat{x}_{\mathrm{M}}(t)\coloneqq\argmin\limits_{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mathcal{V}(t,\xi),

the dynamical system (
1.3
) characterizes the evolution of
\widehat{x}_{\mathrm{M}}(\cdot)
over
[0,T]
. While there exist research works on the Mortensen observer taking a filtering perspective, see, e.g.,
[
19
,
20
,
12
,
13
]
and
[
28
]
for the discrete-time case, throughout this manuscript, we follow the deterministic viewpoint proposed in the original formulation
[
29
]
. Let us also refer to
[
15
]
and
[
34
]
where detailed derivations based on a deterministic optimal control framework have been presented. Due to the strong assumptions required for rigorously defining (
1.3
), the literature focusing on the theoretical aspects of the Mortensen observer is rather scarce, often assuming the involved nonlinearities to be globally Lipschitz
[
24
]
. More recently, for systems with quadratic nonlinearity, we have shown the local well-posedness of (
1.3
) by employing a sensitivity analysis for the value function in (
1.4
), see
[
11
]
.
It is well known that for linear dynamics and measurements, i.e.,
f(\xi)=A\xi
and
h(\xi)=C\xi
, with matrices
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n,n}
,
C\in\mathbb{R}^{p,n}
the Mortensen observer reduces to the classic Kalman filter.
Hence, the Mortensen observer can be viewed as an energy optimal extension of the Kalman filter to nonlinear systems. Unfortunately, its dependence on
\nabla^{2}\mathcal{V}
makes its numerical realization a highly challenging task. While in principal the value function is available as the solution of (
1.4
), solving such HJB equations is known to suffer from the curse of dimensionality
[
4
]
.

Recently, various approaches tackling this issue were proposed. Let us mention the learning based methods utilizing neural networks
[
6
]
or structured polynomials
[
9
]
. We also point to an algorithmic realization based on max-plus algebra
[
16
]
. Here we take a different route and instead consider numerical approaches based on the local approximation of the value function.
Assuming sufficient regularity of
\mathcal{V}
, one can characterize the Hessian along the observer
\Pi(t)\coloneqq\nabla^{2}\mathcal{V}(t,\widehat{x}_{\mathrm{M}}(t))
via the differential equation

(1.7)

\displaystyle\dot{\Pi}(t)=-\mathcal{J}_{f}(\widehat{x}_{\mathrm{M}}(t))^{\top}\Pi(t)-\Pi(t)\mathcal{J}_{f}(\widehat{x}_{\mathrm{M}}(t))-\Pi(t)GR^{-1}G^{\top}\Pi(t)+\mathcal{J}_{h}(\widehat{x}_{\mathrm{M}}(t))^{\top}Q\mathcal{J}_{h}(\widehat{x}_{\mathrm{M}}(t))

\displaystyle+\mathbf{D}^{(3)}\mathcal{V}(t,\widehat{x}_{\mathrm{M}}(t))\underset{1,1}{*}\Pi(t)^{-1}(\mathcal{J}_{h}(\widehat{x}_{\mathrm{M}}(t))^{\top}Q(y(t)-h(\widehat{x}_{\mathrm{M}}(t)))

\displaystyle-\mathbf{D}^{(2)}h(\widehat{x}_{\mathrm{M}}(t))\underset{1,1}{*}Q(y(t)-h(\widehat{x}_{\mathrm{M}}(t))),

\displaystyle\Pi(0)=\Gamma.

Here
\mathbf{D}^{(3)}\mathcal{V}(t,\xi)\in\mathbb{R}^{n,n,n}
and
\mathbf{D}^{(2)}h(\xi)\in\mathbb{R}^{p,n,n}
denote order three spatial derivatives as tensors and
\underset{1,1}{*}
denotes an appropriate tensor multiplication, cf.
Section

2.1
below for the details.

Equation

1.7
is of interest from different perspectives. On the one hand, if we assume that
\mathbf{D}^{(2)}h
and
\mathbf{D}^{(3)}\mathcal{V}
vanish along
\widehat{x}_{\mathrm{M}}(\cdot),
then (
1.3
) together with (
1.7
) boil down to the well-known extended Kalman filter

(1.8)

\displaystyle\dot{\widehat{x}}_{\mathrm{K}}(t)

\displaystyle=f(\widehat{x}_{\mathrm{K}}(t))+\Sigma(t)\,\mathcal{J}_{h}(\widehat{x}_{\mathrm{K}}(t))^{\top}Q(y(t)-h(\widehat{x}_{\mathrm{K}}(t))),

\displaystyle\widehat{x}_{\mathrm{K}}(t)

\displaystyle=x_{0},

\displaystyle\dot{\Sigma}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{J}_{f}(\widehat{x}_{\mathrm{K}}(t))\,\Sigma(t)+\Sigma(t)\,\mathcal{J}_{f}(\widehat{x}_{\mathrm{K}}(t))^{\top}

\displaystyle-\Sigma(t)\,\mathcal{J}_{h}(\widehat{x}_{\mathrm{K}}(t))^{\top}Q(\mathcal{J}_{h}(\widehat{x}_{\mathrm{K}}(t)))\,\Sigma(t)+GR^{-1}G^{\top},

\displaystyle\Sigma(0)

\displaystyle=\Gamma^{-1},

where we have replaced (
1.7
) by an explicit equation for
\Sigma(t)\coloneqq\nabla^{2}\mathcal{V}(t,\widehat{x}_{\mathrm{K}}(t))^{-1}.
On the other hand, it turns out that for non-vanishing
\mathbf{D}^{(3)}\mathcal{V}
, the second derivative involves the (unknown) third derivative of the value function. In fact, this analogously carries over to all higher order derivatives of
\mathcal{V}
and was already referred to as
closure problem
in
[
29
]
. While this naturally prevents from iteratively computing any of the derivatives of
\mathcal{V}
“exactly”, at the same time it allows for numerical approximations based on the assumption that
\mathbf{D}^{(k)}\mathcal{V}
vanishes and it will be the starting point of our contribution here.
Note that the idea of approximating
\nabla^{2}\mathcal{V}
by means of a Taylor series is not new and has been proposed in
[
25
,
26
]
for discrete-time systems. In fact, using power series based approximations was already suggested in
[
2
]
in the closely related context of optimal feedback stabilization. Here, the literature is quite rich and we exemplarily mention
[
27
]
for a theoretical analysis of Taylor series approximations for analytic control systems,
[
1
,
23
]
for a numerical realization for small and medium scale systems as well as the more recent work
[
5
,
7
,
22
,
14
]
utilizing tensor calculus in the large scale case. Moreover, we further refer to
[
8
]
where polynomial feedback laws have been studied in the context of infinite-dimensional bilinear systems. Despite the overall common goal of locally approximating the value function, it should be pointed out that there is a fundamental conceptual difference that distinguishes the computation of optimal feedback laws from the computation of the Mortensen observer.

In particular, the first class of methods typically considers infinite horizon stabilization problems around an a priori given steady state
x_{s}
, often
x_{s}=0
, and subsequently aims for an approximation
F
of the optimal feedback taking the form

(1.9)

\displaystyle F(x)=-G^{\top}\nabla\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{apr}}(x)\approx-G^{\top}\nabla\mathcal{V}(x),

where the value function is locally approximated using its
k
-th Taylor polynomial, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{apr}}(x)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{V}(x_{s})+\nabla\mathcal{V}(x_{s})^{\top}(x-x_{s})+\frac{1}{2}(x-x_{s})^{\top}\nabla^{2}\mathcal{V}(x_{s})(x-x_{s})

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=3}^{k}\frac{1}{i!}\mathbf{D}^{(k)}\mathcal{V}(x_{s})(x-x_{s})^{k},

where the last summand formally denotes the multiplication of
(x-x_{s})
to each of the
k
sides of the tensor
\mathbf{D}^{(k)}\mathcal{V}(x_{s})
.
These tensors are obtained as solutions of a set of tensor equations where the equation characterizing
\mathbf{D}^{(j)}\mathcal{V}
depends only on
\mathbf{D}^{(i)}\mathcal{V}
,
i\leq j
. Hence they can be solved iteratively and yield exact derivatives. The obtained feedback law is expected to perform well, for initial states
x_{0}
sufficiently close to
x_{s}
. Whenever the closed loop system driven by the feedback
F
visits states
x
far away from
x_{s}
, the approximation quality of the Taylor polynomial may degrade quickly.

The approximation scheme discussed in this work is conceptually different. In fact, assuming knowledge of the third derivative
\mathbf{D}^{(3)}\mathcal{V}(t,\widehat{x}_{\mathrm{M}}(t))
,
equation

1.7
yields the exact optimal observer gain and coupling it with the observer equation (
1.3
) would yield the optimal observer
\widehat{x}_{\mathrm{M}}
. The derivation of the tensor equation characterizing
\mathbf{D}^{(3)}\mathcal{V}(t,\widehat{x}_{\mathrm{M}}(t))
however, corresponds to an expansion of
\mathcal{V}(t,\cdot)
around
\widehat{x}_{\mathrm{M}}(t)
. The time-dependence of the expansion point introduces a coupling such that determining the third derivative requires the forth. As mentioned above, this effect carries over to all higher order derivatives. Hence truncating the
k
-th order derivative implicitly affects the approximation of
\mathbf{D}^{(3)}\mathcal{V}(t,\widehat{x}_{\mathrm{M}}(t))
which enters the Riccati-like equation (
1.7
). Therefore the obtained approximations of derivatives are not in fact coefficients of the associated Taylor polynomial and as of now no results regarding convergence or error bounds are available. On the other hand, the approach is not as sensitive towards the initial state. As pointed out above, an application of the feedback control (
1.9
) to a system with initial state
x_{0}
far away from the equilibrium
x_{s}
would almost certainly fail. The same effect does not occur in the scheme analyzed in this work, as the expansion of the value function is done around the minimizing trajectory
\widehat{x}_{\mathrm{M}}
itself.

Our main results now can be summarized as follows:

•

Utilizing a generalized product rule for the differentiation of two vector-valued functions, in
Lemma

3.7
we derive explicit expressions for arbitrary higher order derivatives of the HJB equation (
1.4
).

•

Assuming a particular order of the derivative of
\mathcal{V}
to vanish along the Mortensen observer, in
Theorem

3.3
we characterize
\widehat{x}_{\mathrm{M}}
by a set of nonlinear and partially structured tensor differential equations. For the general case without a vanishing derivative, the same set of equations subsequently defines an approximation to the Mortensen observer, thereby generalizing the well-known extended Kalman filter, cf.
Remark

3.4
.

•

An implementation of the higher order Kalman filter for two academic examples illustrates the feasibility and effectiveness of the approximation. The application to a low-dimensional chaotic oscillator indicates convergence to the Mortensen observer with increasing orders of the HOEKF. In addition, a realization for a semi-discretized, cubic wave equation demonstrates the effectiveness for systems of larger dimensions. The results are compared to available data-driven methods for the realization of the Mortensen observer, showing that the HOEKF outperforms them in accuracy and computational efficiency.

2.
Some results from tensor calculus

For deriving a sequence
\mathbf{D}^{(k)}\mathcal{V}
locally approximating the Mortensen observer and characterizing the required terms via a coupled set of differential equations, we identify higher order derivatives with multidimensional arrays or tensors. Here, we restrict ourselves to the finite-dimensional case
\mathbb{R}^{n_{1},\dots,n_{d}}
and refer to
[
17
]
for an in-depth treatment of tensor products for general Banach spaces. Most of the presented material is well-known in the context of tensor calculus and our exposition largely follows the ones from
[
35
,
30
]
.

2.1.
Basic notions and preliminaries

We denote by
\mathbf{T}
a
tensor
of order
d
, i.e., a mapping

(2.1)

\mathbf{T}\colon\mathbb{N}_{n_{1}}\times\dots\times\mathbb{N}_{n_{d}}\to\mathbb{R},\quad(i_{1},\dots,i_{d})\mapsto\mathbf{T}[i_{1},\dots,i_{d}].

The set of all such tensors is denoted by

\mathbb{R}^{n_{1},\dots,n_{d}}\coloneqq\left\{\mathbf{T}\mid\mathbf{T}\colon\mathbb{N}_{n_{1}}\times\dots\times\mathbb{N}_{n_{d}}\to\mathbb{R}\right\}.

We refer to
n_{\mu}
as the
\mu
-th dimension
of
\mathbf{T}
and to
d\in\mathbb{N}
as the
order
of
\mathbf{T}
.
In particular, we identify matrices as order-two tensors via
\mathbf{C}[i,j]=C_{ij}
for
C\in\mathbb{R}^{n,m}
.
Given
0<\ell<d
and two tensors
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1},\dots,n_{\ell}},\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\ell+1},\dots,n_{d}}
of order
\ell
and
d-\ell
, respectively, the
tensor product
is defined by

\otimes\colon\mathbb{R}^{n_{1},\dots,n_{\ell}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n_{\ell+1},\dots,n_{d}}\to\mathbb{R}^{n_{1},\dots,n_{d}},\quad\left(\mathbf{A}\otimes\mathbf{B}\right)[i_{1},\dots,i_{d}]\coloneqq\mathbf{A}[i_{1},\dots,i_{\ell}]\mathbf{B}[i_{\ell+1},\dots i_{d}].

Similarly, for the tensor product of two tensor spaces, we set

\mathbb{R}^{n_{1},\dots,n_{\ell}}\otimes\mathbb{R}^{n_{\ell+1},\dots,n_{d}}\coloneqq\vspan\left\{\left(\mathbf{A}\otimes\mathbf{B}\right)\mid\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1},\dots,n_{\ell}},\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\ell+1},\dots,n_{d}}\right\}.

When all the
n_{i}
are equal, i.e.,
n_{1},\dots,n_{d}=n
, we use the shorthand notation
\bigotimes^{d}\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Below, we consider successive differentiations of the HJB equation (
1.4
) for which we utilize a generalized product rule for tensors. For this purpose, it is convenient to make use of appropriate
reshapings
of the underlying multidimensional arrays. Given
d\in\mathbb{N}
and a permutation
\sigma\in S_{d}
, a
reshape
is an isomorphism defined as

\reshape_{\sigma}\colon\mathbb{R}^{n_{1},\dots,n_{d}}\to\mathbb{R}^{n_{\sigma(1)},\dots,n_{\sigma(d)}},\quad\reshape_{\sigma}(\mathbf{A})[i_{1},\dots,i_{d}]=\mathbf{A}[i_{\sigma(1)},\dots,i_{\sigma(d)}].

In the following we shorten the above notation and often simply write
\mathbf{A}^{\sigma}
instead of
\reshape_{\sigma}(\mathbf{A})
.

Remark 2.1
.

Let us note that there are different ways of defining the reshape operator. In particular, the one used in this manuscript differs from the one used in
[
35
,
30
]
which reads

\widetilde{\reshape}_{\sigma}\mathbf{A}[i_{\sigma(1)},\dots,i_{\sigma(d)}]=\mathbf{A}[i_{1},\dots,i_{d}].

In other words, the difference in notation is caused by the implicit use of the inverse permutation and we have the following relation

\reshape_{\sigma}(\mathbf{A})=\widetilde{\reshape}_{\sigma^{-1}}\mathbf{A}.

While the notation introduced in
[
35
,
30
]
corresponds to the way the reshape operation is encoded in MATLAB
®
, our notation allows for a more intuitive way of deriviving the differentiation rules in
Section

2.2
and has been used in a similar context in
[
8
]
.

Besides the tensor product, we also need a generalization of the matrix multiplication, typically referred to as
tensor contraction.

Let
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1},\dots,n_{d}}
and
\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{1},\dots,m_{\ell}}
be two tensors of order
d
and
\ell
respectively. The contraction
\mathbf{A}\underset{\mu,\nu}{*}\mathbf{B}
of the
\mu
-th dimension of
\mathbf{A}
with the
\nu
-th dimension of
\mathbf{B}
is defined entrywise as

\left(\mathbf{A}\underset{\mu,\nu}{*}\mathbf{B}\right)[i_{1},\dots,i_{\mu-1},i_{\mu+1},\dots,i_{d},j_{1},\dots,j_{\nu-1},j_{\nu+1},\dots,j_{\ell}]\\
\coloneqq\sum_{k=1}^{n_{\mu}}\mathbf{A}[i_{1},\dots,i_{\mu-1},k,i_{\mu+1},\dots,i_{d}]\mathbf{B}[j_{1},\dots,j_{\nu-1},k,j_{\nu+1},\dots,j_{\ell}].

For the contraction of two tensors to be well-defined, it is required that
n_{\mu}=m_{\nu}
.
The resulting tensor
\mathbf{A}\underset{\mu,\nu}{*}\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1},\dots,n_{\mu-1},n_{\mu+1},\dots,n_{d},m_{1},\dots,m_{\nu-1},n_{\nu+1},m_{\ell}}
is of order
d+\ell-2
.

Remark 2.2
.

For fixed dimensions, the contraction operation is associative. However, one downside of the introduced notation is that whenever the order of operations is switched, the dimensions in the contractions need to be adjusted.
As an example consider
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1},n_{2},n_{3}}
,
\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{2},n_{2},p}
and
\mathbf{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{1},m_{2},m_{3}}
. Then it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}\underset{2,1}{*}(\mathbf{B}\underset{1,2}{*}\mathbf{C})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n_{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{m_{2}}\mathbf{A}[i_{1},j,i_{2}]\mathbf{B}[k,j,i_{3}]\mathbf{C}[i_{4},k,i_{5}]=\left(\mathbf{A}\underset{2,2}{*}\mathbf{B}\right)\underset{3,2}{*}\mathbf{C}.

Note that the term
(\mathbf{A}*_{2,1}\mathbf{B})*_{1,2}\mathbf{C}
is not even well-defined since in general
n_{1}\neq m_{2}
. Thus, whenever we switch the order of operation we need to also adjust the syntax.

When deriving the observer equations, we often need to contract a tensor and a matrix followed by applying a certain permutation. Thus, for
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1},\dots,n_{d}}
and
\mathbf{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mu},m}
we define

\displaystyle\left(\mathbf{A}\times_{\mu}\mathbf{C}\right)[i_{1},\dots,i_{k}]\coloneqq

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n_{\mu}}\mathbf{A}[i_{1},\dots,i_{\mu-1},k,i_{\mu+1},\dots,i_{d}]\mathbf{C}[k,i_{\mu}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\mathbf{A}\underset{\mu,1}{*}\mathbf{C}\right)^{(1,\dots,\mu-1,\mu+1,\dots,d,\mu)}.

Here, we contract the
\mu
-th dimension of
\mathbf{A}
with the first dimension of
\mathbf{C}
and then perform a reshape operation to restore the order of the dimensions of
\mathbf{A}
and substitute the contracted dimension with the remaining dimension of
\mathbf{C}
.

2.2.
Differentiation rules for tensors

In this subsection, we recall some basics on the derivatives of vector-valued functions and identify these derivatives with tensors. We also state a generalized product rule that already has been utilized in
[
8
]
.

First, recall that the
k
-th derivatives of a multivariable function
u\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
can be identified with a
k
-linear map
D^{k}u\colon(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{\times k}\to\mathbb{R}
.

Due to the natural connection between tensors and multilinear mappings, given
u\in C^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R})
and
g\in C^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{m})
, we define

\displaystyle\mathbf{D}^{(k)}u(\xi)[j_{1},\dots,j_{k}]\coloneqq\partial_{j_{k},\dots,j_{1}}u(\xi)\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbf{D}^{(k)}g(\xi)[i,j_{1},\dots,j_{k}]\coloneqq\partial_{j_{k},\dots,j_{1}}g_{i}(\xi).

This way we have that

D^{k}u(\xi)(e_{j_{1}},\dots,e_{j_{k}})=\partial_{j_{k},\dots,j_{1}}u(\xi)=\mathbf{D}^{(k)}u(\xi)[j_{1},\dots,j_{k}],

where
D^{k}u(v_{1},\dots,v_{k})
is the
k
-th directional derivative of
u
, first with respect to
v_{1}
and last with respect to
v_{k}
and
e_{j}
denotes the
j
-th unit vector.

Remark 2.3
.

(i)

Note that for fixed
i
, we have that

\mathbf{D}^{(k)}g(\xi)[i,j_{1},\dots,j_{k}]=\mathbf{D}^{(k)}g_{i}(\xi)[j_{1},\dots,j_{k}].

(ii)

Similar to
equation

1.7
, we can identify
\mathbf{D}^{(1)}u
,
\mathbf{D}^{(2)}u
and
\mathbf{D}^{(1)}g
with the gradient
\nabla u
, Hessian
\nabla^{2}u
and Jacobian
\mathcal{J}_{g}
, respectively. Technically,
\mathbf{D}^{(2)}u
corresponds to the transpose of the Hessian, due to the ordering of its entries. However, since we assumed continuous differentiability, by the theorem of Schwarz the Hessian is symmetric and hence the identification with
\mathbf{D}^{(2)}u
is justified.

Next, we recall a generalized product rule that can be found in, e.g.,
[
18
, Proposition 5]
or
[
8
, Lemma 12]
. For this purpose, let us define the set of permutation
S_{i,j}\subset S_{m}
as follows.

S_{i,j}\coloneqq\left\{\sigma\in S_{i+j}\mid\sigma(1)<\dots<\sigma(i)\text{ and }\sigma(i+1)<\dots<\sigma(i+j)\right\}.

Note that
|S_{i,j}|=\binom{i+j}{i}
.
We then have the following result.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
u,v\in C^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R})
. Then it holds that

\partial_{j_{1},\dots,j_{k}}(uv)=\sum_{i=0}^{k}\sum_{\sigma\in S_{i,k-i}}(\partial_{j_{\sigma(1)},\dots,j_{\sigma(i)}}u)(\partial_{j_{\sigma(i+1)},\dots,j_{\sigma(k)}}v).

The proof can be found in
[
8
]
in the language of multilinear forms.
Introducing the operator

\Sym_{i,j}(\mathbf{T})=\sum_{\sigma\in S_{i,j}}\mathbf{T}^{\sigma}

for
\mathbf{T}\in\bigotimes^{i+j}\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
we can write the derivative of
uv
as

(2.2)

\mathbf{D}^{(k)}(uv)=\sum_{i=0}^{k}\Sym_{i,k-i}\left(\mathbf{D}^{(i)}u\otimes\mathbf{D}^{(k-i)}v\right).

Remark 2.5
.

Note that in
[
8
]
the operator
\Sym_{i,j}(\mathbf{T})
is further multiplied by
\binom{i+j}{i}^{-1}
to represent the symmetric part of the tensor
\mathbf{T}
. We choose to omit the scaling factor in the definition of
\Sym_{i,j}
, since it cancels out in the equations (
2.2
) and (
2.3
).

Later we need to differentiate the contraction of two vector-valued functions. The previous product rule can easily be generalized to this setting.

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
u,g\in C^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{m})
, then it holds that

(2.3)

\mathbf{D}^{(k)}(u^{\top}g)=\sum_{i=0}^{k}\Sym_{i,k-i}\left(\mathbf{D}^{(i)}u\underset{1,1}{*}\mathbf{D}^{(k-i)}g\right).

Proof.

Note that differentiation as well as the operator
\Sym_{i,j}
are linear. By the first part of
Remark

2.3
, it holds that

\mathbf{D}^{(k)}g(\xi)[i,j_{1},\dots,j_{k}]=\mathbf{D}^{(k)}g_{i}(\xi)[j_{1},\dots,j_{k}].

Thus we get that

\displaystyle\mathbf{D}^{(k)}(u^{\top}g)=

\displaystyle\;\mathbf{D}^{(k)}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{m}g_{j}u_{j}\right)=\sum_{j=0}^{m}\mathbf{D}^{(k)}\left(g_{j}u_{j}\right).

Now using
equation

2.2
we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbf{D}^{(k)}(u^{\top}g)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{m}\sum_{i=0}^{k}\Sym_{i,k-i}\left(\mathbf{D}^{(i)}u_{j}\otimes\mathbf{D}^{(k-i)}g_{j}\right)=\sum_{i=0}^{k}\Sym_{i,k-i}\left(\mathbf{D}^{(i)}u\underset{1,1}{*}\mathbf{D}^{(k-i)}g\right).

∎

3.
Higher order extended Kalman filters

Using the results from
Lemma

2.6
, in this section, we derive a sequence of approximations to the Mortensen observer by successively taking derivatives of the HJB equation (
1.4
) and assuming the derivative of a certain order to vanish. Let us demonstrate the main idea by exemplarily considering a second order approximation of the Mortensen observer. For this purpose, assume that we construct an approximation
\widehat{x}(t)\approx\widehat{x}_{\mathrm{M}}(t)
by making the ansatz
\mathbf{D}^{(3)}\mathcal{V}(\cdot,\widehat{x}(\cdot))=0
. Denoting
P(t)=\nabla^{2}\mathcal{V}(t,\widehat{x}(t))
, equations (
1.3
) and (
1.7
) completely describe the resulting observer as

(3.1)

\displaystyle\dot{\widehat{x}}(t)

\displaystyle=f(\widehat{x}(t))+P(t)^{-1}\mathcal{J}_{h}(\widehat{x}(t))^{\top}Q(y(t)-h(\widehat{x}(t))),

\displaystyle\dot{P}(t)

\displaystyle=-\mathcal{J}_{f}(\widehat{x}(t))^{\top}P(t)-P(t)\mathcal{J}_{f}(\widehat{x}(t))-P(t)GR^{-1}G^{\top}P(t)

\displaystyle+\mathcal{J}_{h}(\widehat{x}(t))^{\top}Q\mathcal{J}_{h}(\widehat{x}(t))-\mathbf{D}^{(2)}h(\widehat{x}(t))\underset{1,1}{*}Q(y(t)-h(\widehat{x}(t))),

\displaystyle\widehat{x}(0)

\displaystyle=x_{0}\quad\text{and}\quad P(0)=\Gamma.

As we can see from (
1.8
) and its discussion, the observer
\widehat{x}
is in spirit similar to the extended Kalman filter and only differs in the term corresponding to the second derivative of
h
. Note that in order to define this observer we need to assume
f
and
h
to be
C^{2}
-regular and
\mathcal{V}
to be
C^{3}
-regular.

Analogously, as we have done for the second order approximation, let us now approximate the Mortensen observer by considering all derivatives of the value function of order up to some fixed
k\geq 3
, which is fixed for the remainder of the section. Hence, we neglect all terms containing
\mathbf{D}^{(\ell)}\mathcal{V}(t,\xi)
for
\ell=k
. We will see that the derivatives of order larger than two are characterized by generalized differential Lyapunov equations. Differentiating the HJB equation requires the following assumptions.

Assumption 3.1
.

(i)

The functions
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
h\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{p}
are
k
-times continuously differentiable.

(ii)

For all
t\in[0,T]
the value function
\mathcal{V}(t,\cdot)
is
(k+1)
-times continuously differentiable.

(iii)

The value function and its spatial derivatives are
C^{1}
in time and the order of derivatives can be exchanged, i.e., for
\ell\leq k
it holds

\partial_{t}\mathbf{D}^{(\ell)}\mathcal{V}(\cdot,\widehat{x}_{\mathrm{M}}(\cdot))=\mathbf{D}^{(\ell)}\partial_{t}\mathcal{V}(\cdot,\widehat{x}_{\mathrm{M}}(\cdot))
.

Two additional assumptions are required to ensure that the Mortensen observer is well-defined and satisfies
equation

1.3
.

Assumption 3.2
.

(i)

For every
t\in[0,T]
the Mortensen observer
\widehat{x}_{\mathrm{M}}(t)
is well-defined as the unique minimizer of the value function
\mathcal{V}(t,\cdot)
.

(ii)

For all
t\in[0,T]
the Hessian
\nabla^{2}\mathcal{V}(t,\widehat{x}_{\mathrm{M}}(t))
is a regular matrix.

Note that, while the assumptions on the regularity of the value function may seem restrictive, we point out that for quadratic dynamics with linear measurements the value function
\mathcal{V}
has been shown to be locally smooth with a positive definite Hessian
[
11
]
.
Under these assumption we state the main theoretical result of this work.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
3.1
and
3.2
hold and additionally assume
\mathbf{D}^{(k+1)}\mathcal{V}(\cdot,\widehat{x}_{\mathrm{M}}(\cdot))=0
.
Then
\widehat{x}_{\mathrm{M}}
is characterized as the solution
\widehat{x}
of

(3.2)

\displaystyle\dot{\widehat{x}}(t)=

\displaystyle f(\widehat{x}(t))+\mathbf{P}_{2}(t)^{-1}(\mathcal{J}_{h}(\widehat{x}(t)))^{\top}(y(t)-h(\widehat{x}(t))),

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{P}}_{2}(t)=

\displaystyle-\mathcal{J}_{f}(\widehat{x}(t))^{\top}\mathbf{P}_{2}(t)-\mathbf{P}_{2}(t)\mathcal{J}_{f}(\widehat{x}(t))-\mathbf{P}_{2}(t)GR^{-1}G^{\top}\mathbf{P}_{2}(t)-\mathbf{R}_{2}(t)

\displaystyle-\mathbf{P}_{3}(t)\underset{1,1}{*}\left(\mathbf{P}_{2}(t)^{-1}\left(\mathcal{J}_{h}(\widehat{x}(t))^{\top}Q\left(y(t)-h(\widehat{x}(t))\right)\right)\right),

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{P}}_{j}(t)=

\displaystyle-\sum_{i=1}^{j}\mathbf{P}_{j}(t)\times_{i}\left(\mathcal{J}_{f}(\widehat{x}(t))+GR^{-1}G^{\top}\mathbf{P}_{2}(t)\right)-\mathbf{R}_{j}(t)

\displaystyle-\mathbf{P}_{j+1}(t)\underset{1,1}{*}\left(\mathbf{P}_{2}(t)^{-1}\left(\mathcal{J}_{h}(\widehat{x}(t))^{\top}Q\left(y(t)-h(\widehat{x}(t))\right)\right)\right),

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{P}}_{k}(t)=

\displaystyle-\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathbf{P}_{k}(t)\times_{i}\left(\mathcal{J}_{f}(\widehat{x}(t))+GR^{-1}G^{\top}\mathbf{P}_{2}(t)\right)-\mathbf{R}_{k}(t),

with the initial conditions

\widehat{x}(0)=x_{0},\qquad\mathbf{P}_{2}(0)=\Gamma\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbf{P}_{j}(0)=0\quad\text{for all }j=3,\dots,k

where

\mathbf{R}_{\ell}
is given by

(3.3)

\displaystyle\mathbf{R}_{\ell}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell-2}\Sym_{i,\ell-i}\left(\mathbf{P}_{i+1}(t)\underset{1,1}{*}\mathbf{D}^{(\ell-i)}f(\widehat{x}(t))\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=2}^{\ell-2}\Sym_{i,\ell-i}\left(\mathbf{P}_{i+1}(t)\underset{1,1}{*}\left(GR^{-1}G^{\top}\underset{2,1}{*}\mathbf{P}_{\ell-i+1}(t)\right)\right)

\displaystyle+\mathbf{D}^{(\ell)}h(\widehat{x}(t))\underset{1,1}{*}Q(y(t)-\widehat{x}(t))

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{\ell-1}\Sym_{i,\ell-i}\left(\mathbf{D}^{(i)}h(\widehat{x}(t))\underset{1,1}{*}\left(Q\underset{2,1}{*}\mathbf{D}^{(\ell-i)}h(\widehat{x}(t))\right)\right)

for
\ell=2,\dots,k
.

Before proving the theorem some remarks are in order.

Remark 3.4
.

The assumption of a vanishing
(k+1)
order derivative
\mathbf{D}^{(k+1)}\mathcal{V}(\cdot,\widehat{x}_{\mathrm{M}}(\cdot))
can generally not be expected to hold. Hence, in a general setting, the trajectory
\widehat{x}
obtained from (
3.2
) is to be understood as an approximation rather than an analytic representation of the Mortensen observer
\widehat{x}_{\mathrm{M}}
.
We call the resulting observer the
higher order extended Kalman filter of order
k
or short
HOEKF
-
k
.

Motivated by the generalized Lyapunov equations in
[
8
]
we refer to the equations for
\mathbf{P}_{j}
and
\mathbf{P}_{k}
as
generalized differential Lyapunov equations
.
The higher order extended Kalman filter defined by (
3.2
)-(
3.3
) consists of a feedback equation, a Riccati-like equation and
k-2
generalized differential Lyapunov equations, all of which are coupled.
Note that, when applied to linear systems, i.e.,
f(\xi)=A\xi
and
h(\xi)=C\xi
, for all
k\geq 2
the HOEKF-
k
reduces to the Kalman filter

(3.4)

\displaystyle\dot{\widehat{x}}_{\mathrm{K}}(t)

\displaystyle=A\widehat{x}_{\mathrm{K}}(t)+\Sigma(t)C^{\top}Q(y(t)-C\widehat{x}_{\mathrm{K}}(t)),\qquad\qquad

\displaystyle\widehat{x}_{\mathrm{K}}(0)=x_{0},

\displaystyle\dot{\Sigma}(t)

\displaystyle=A\Sigma(t)+\Sigma(t)A^{\top}-\Sigma(t)C^{\top}QC\Sigma(t)+GR^{-1}G^{\top},\qquad

\displaystyle\Sigma(0)=\Gamma^{-1}.

This can be seen by observing that for linear systems it holds
\mathbf{R}_{3}(t)=0
and

\mathbf{R}_{j}(t)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=2}^{j-2}\Sym_{i,j-i}\left(\mathbf{P}_{i+1}(t)\underset{1,1}{*}\left(GG^{\top}\underset{2,1}{*}\mathbf{P}_{j-i+1}(t)\right)\right),\qquad j\geq 4.

Consequently, a solution
(\widehat{x},(\mathbf{P}_{j})_{j=1}^{k})
to (
3.2
) is given by
\widehat{x}=\widehat{x}_{\mathrm{K}}
,
\mathbf{P}_{2}=\Sigma^{-1}
, and
\mathbf{P}_{j}=0
,
j\geq 3
, with
\widehat{x}_{\mathrm{K}}
and
\Sigma
solving (
3.4
).

Remark 3.5

(Existence of solutions)
.

At first glance, the structure of the generalized differential Lyapunov equations seems quite appealing. Indeed, it is similar to the equations appearing in
[
8
]
for which the authors show existence of solutions. In the present setting, the coupling and the matrix inverse introduce additional challenges prohibiting an analogous treatment. The analytical investigation regarding well-posedness of the observer hence requires a novel approach and is left for future work.

Remark 3.6
.

There are numerous ways to modify the previous considerations.
E.g., in
[
15
]
, the author considers the cost functional

\tilde{J}(t,\xi,v)=\phi(x(0))+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\left\|v(s)\right\|^{2}+\alpha\left\|\tilde{y}(s)-Cx(s)\right\|^{2}\penalty\ \mathrm{d}s.

Using this cost functional, we end up with different initial conditions for the equations in (
3.2
). In particular, the corresponding initial condition to the HJB equation (
1.4
) would take the form
\mathcal{V}(0,\xi)=\phi(\xi)
. Hence, by assuming sufficient smoothness of
\phi
, the initial conditions of the
\mathbf{P}_{i}
would take the form
\mathbf{P}_{i}(0)=\mathbf{D}^{(i)}\phi(x_{0})
for
i\geq 2
.

The remainder of this section is concerned with the proof of
Theorem

3.3
.
As a first step we differentiate both sides of the HJB equation (
1.4
) with respect to
\xi
.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
3.1
hold. Then for all
3\leq j\leq k
it holds

(3.5)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathbf{D}^{(j)}\mathcal{V}(t,\xi)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i=0}^{j}\Sym_{i,j-i}\left(\mathbf{D}^{(i+1)}\mathcal{V}(t,\xi)\underset{1,1}{*}\mathbf{D}^{(j-i)}f(\xi)\right)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=0}^{j}\Sym_{i,j-i}\left(\mathbf{D}^{(i+1)}\mathcal{V}(t,\xi)\underset{1,1}{*}\left(GR^{-1}G^{\top}\underset{2,1}{*}\mathbf{D}^{(j-i+1)}\mathcal{V}(t,\xi)\right)\right)

\displaystyle-\mathbf{D}^{(j)}h(\xi)\underset{1,1}{*}Qy(t)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=0}^{j}\Sym_{i,j-i}\left(\mathbf{D}^{(i)}h(\xi)\underset{1,1}{*}\left(Q\underset{2,1}{*}\mathbf{D}^{(j-i)}h(\xi)\right)\right).

Proof.

We differentiate the three summands of (
1.4
) separately using
Lemma

2.6
.
For the first term, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbf{D}^{(j)}\left(\nabla\mathcal{V}(t,\xi)^{\top}f(\xi)\right)=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{j}\Sym_{i,j-i}\left(\mathbf{D}^{(i)}\left(\nabla\mathcal{V}(t,\xi)\right)\underset{1,1}{*}\mathbf{D}^{(j-i)}f(\xi)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{j}\Sym_{i,j-i}\left(\mathbf{D}^{(i+1)}\mathcal{V}(t,\xi)\underset{1,1}{*}\mathbf{D}^{(j-i)}f(\xi)\right).

Similarly, for the second term, we see that

\displaystyle\mathbf{D}^{(j)}\bigl(\|G^{\top}\nabla\mathcal{V}(t,\xi)\|_{R^{-1}}^{2}\bigr)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{D}^{(j)}\bigl(\nabla\mathcal{V}(t,\xi)^{\top}(GR^{-1}G^{\top}\nabla\mathcal{V}(t,\xi))\bigr)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{j}\Sym_{i,j-i}\left(\mathbf{D}^{(i)}\left(\nabla\mathcal{V}(t,\xi)\right)\underset{1,1}{*}\mathbf{D}^{(j-i)}\left(GR^{-1}G^{\top}\nabla\mathcal{V}(t,\xi)\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{j}\Sym_{i,j-i}\left(\mathbf{D}^{(i+1)}\mathcal{V}(t,\xi)\underset{1,1}{*}\left(GR^{-1}G^{\top}\underset{2,1}{*}\mathbf{D}^{(j-i+1)}\mathcal{V}(t,\xi)\right)\right).

Finally differentiating the third term, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbf{D}^{(j)}\left(\|y(t)-h(\xi)\|_{Q}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{D}^{(j)}\left(y(t)^{T}Qy(t)-2h(\xi)^{\top}Qy(t)+h(\xi)^{\top}Qh(\xi)\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{D}^{(j)}\left(y(t)^{T}Qy(t)\right)-2\mathbf{D}^{(j)}\left(h(\xi)^{\top}Qy(t)\right)+\mathbf{D}^{(j)}\left(h(\xi)^{\top}Qh(\xi)\right)

\displaystyle=-2\mathbf{D}^{(j)}h(\xi)\underset{1,1}{*}Qy(t)

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{i=0}^{j}\Sym_{i,j-i}\left(\mathbf{D}^{(i)}h(\xi)\underset{1,1}{*}\left(Q\underset{2,1}{*}\mathbf{D}^{(k-i)}h(\xi)\right)\right).

∎

We further require the following two technical lemmas.

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
\mathbf{A}\in\bigotimes^{j+1}\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\mathbf{B}\in\bigotimes^{\ell+1}\mathbb{R}^{n}
be symmetric, and let
\Gamma=\Gamma^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n,n}
be a symmetric matrix. Then it holds that

(3.6)

\Sym_{j,\ell}\left(\mathbf{A}\underset{1,1}{*}\left(\Gamma\underset{2,1}{*}\mathbf{B}\right)\right)=\Sym_{\ell,j}\left(\mathbf{B}\underset{1,1}{*}\left(\Gamma\underset{2,1}{*}\mathbf{A}\right)\right).

Proof.

We rewrite the first expression as

\displaystyle\Sym_{j,\ell}\left(\mathbf{A}\underset{1,1}{*}\left(\Gamma\underset{2,1}{*}\mathbf{B}\right)\right)[i_{1},\dots,i_{j+\ell}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma\in S_{j,\ell}}\sum_{s=1}^{n}\mathbf{A}[s,i_{\sigma(1)},\dots,i_{\sigma(j)}]\sum_{r=1}^{n}\Gamma_{sr}\mathbf{B}[r,i_{\sigma(j+1)},\dots,i_{\sigma(j+\ell)}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma\in S_{j,\ell}}\sum_{r=1}^{n}\mathbf{B}[r,i_{\sigma(j+1)},\dots,i_{\sigma(j+\ell)}]\sum_{s=1}^{n}\Gamma_{sr}\mathbf{A}[s,i_{\sigma(1)},\dots,i_{\sigma(j)}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma\in S_{j,\ell}}\left(\mathbf{B}\underset{1,1}{*}\left(\Gamma\underset{2,1}{*}\mathbf{A}\right)\right)[i_{\sigma(j+1)},\dots,i_{\sigma(j+\ell)},i_{\sigma(1)},\dots,i_{\sigma(j)}].

Defining the permutation
\tau
as

(\tau(1),\dots,\tau(j+\ell))=(j+1,\dots,j+\ell,1,\dots,j),

it remains to show that

S_{\ell,j}=\left\{\sigma\circ\tau\mid\sigma\in S_{j,\ell}\right\}.

To that end let
\tilde{\sigma}=\sigma\circ\tau\in\left\{\sigma\circ\tau\mid\sigma\in S_{j,\ell}\right\}
. It then follows

\displaystyle\tilde{\sigma}(1)

\displaystyle=\sigma(\tau(1))=\sigma(j+1)<\dots<\sigma(j+\ell)=\sigma(\tau(\ell))=\tilde{\sigma}(\ell)

\displaystyle\tilde{\sigma}(\ell+1)

\displaystyle=\sigma(\tau(\ell+1))=\sigma(1)<\dots<\sigma(j)=\sigma(\tau(j+\ell))=\tilde{\sigma}(j+\ell)

and therefore
\tilde{\sigma}\in S_{\ell,j}
. Note that also
|S_{j,\ell}|=|S_{\ell,j}|<\infty
and hence, equality between the two sets of permutations follows.
∎

Lemma 3.9
.

Let
\mathbf{A}\in\bigotimes^{\ell}\mathbb{R}^{n}
be symmetric and
\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{n,n}
. Then it holds that

\Sym_{\ell-1,1}\left(\mathbf{A}\underset{1,1}{*}\mathbf{B}\right)=\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}\mathbf{A}\times_{j}\mathbf{B}.

Proof.

First note that

S_{\ell-1,1}=\left\{(1,\dots,\ell),(1,3,\dots,\ell,2),\dots,(1,\dots,\ell-2,\ell,\ell-1)\right\}.

By symmetry of
\mathbf{A}
we have
\mathbf{A}*_{1,1}\mathbf{B}=\mathbf{A}*_{j,1}\mathbf{B}
for all
j=1,\dots\ell
and by writing out the product, it follows

\displaystyle\Sym_{\ell-1,1}\left(\mathbf{A}\underset{1,1}{*}\mathbf{B}\right)[i_{1},\dots,i_{\ell}]=

\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma\in S_{\ell-1,1}}\left(\mathbf{A}\underset{1,1}{*}\mathbf{B}\right)[i_{\sigma(1)},\dots,i_{\sigma(\ell)}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}\left(\mathbf{A}\underset{1,1}{*}\mathbf{B}\right)[i_{1},\dots,i_{j-1},i_{j+1},\dots,i_{\ell},i_{j}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}\left(\mathbf{A}\times_{j}\mathbf{B}\right)[i_{1},\dots,i_{\ell}],

which concludes the proof.
∎

With these results at hand we are in a position to prove the main result of this work.

Proof of
Theorem

3.3

Proof.

We denote
\mathbf{P}_{2}(t)=\nabla^{2}\mathcal{V}(t,\widehat{x}_{\mathrm{M}}(t))
and for
j=3,\dots,k+1
we denote
\mathbf{P}_{j}(t)\coloneqq\mathbf{D}^{(j)}\mathcal{V}(t,\widehat{x}_{\mathrm{M}}(t))
.
With
3.1
and
3.2
, a straightforward extension of the arguments presented in
[
6
, Sec. 2.1]
shows that
\widehat{x}_{\mathrm{M}}
satisfies the observer equation

(3.7)

\dot{\widehat{x}}_{\mathrm{M}}(t)=f(\widehat{x}_{\mathrm{M}}(t))+\mathbf{P}_{2}(t)^{-1}(\mathcal{J}_{h}(\widehat{x}_{\mathrm{M}}(t)))^{\top}(y(t)-h(\widehat{x}_{\mathrm{M}}(t))).

Taking two
\xi
derivatives on both sides of the HJB equation (
1.4
) and evaluating along
\xi=\widehat{x}_{\mathrm{M}}(t)
yields the announced differential matrix Riccati-type equation characterizing
\mathbf{P}_{2}
. Here, some terms vanish due to the fact that
\nabla\mathcal{V}(t,\widehat{x}_{\mathrm{M}}(t))=0
. To see this, note that by definition
\widehat{x}_{\mathrm{M}}(t)
minimizes
\mathcal{V}(t,\cdot)
.
Now, for
j=3,\dots,k
the chain rule yields

\dot{\mathbf{P}}_{j}(t)=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left[\mathbf{D}^{(j)}\mathcal{V}(t,\widehat{x}_{\mathrm{M}}(t))\right]=\partial_{t}\mathbf{D}^{(j)}\mathcal{V}(t,\widehat{x}_{\mathrm{M}}(t))+\mathbf{D}^{(j+1)}\mathcal{V}(t,\widehat{x}_{\mathrm{M}}(t))\underset{1,1}{*}\dot{\widehat{x}}_{\mathrm{M}}(t).

Utilizing
Lemma

3.8
, (
3.7
), and
\nabla\mathcal{V}(t,\widehat{x}_{\mathrm{M}}(t))=0
yields

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{P}}_{j}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{P}_{j+1}(t)\underset{1,1}{*}\left(\mathbf{P}_{2}(t)^{-1}\left(\mathcal{J}_{h}(\widehat{x}_{\mathrm{M}}(t))^{\top}Q\left(y(t)-h(\widehat{x}_{\mathrm{M}}(t))\right)\right)\right)

\displaystyle\qquad-\Sym_{j-1,1}\left(\mathbf{P}_{j}(t)\underset{1,1}{*}\left(\mathbf{D}^{(1)}f(\widehat{x}_{\mathrm{M}}(t))+GR^{-1}G^{\top}\underset{2,1}{*}\mathbf{P}_{2}(t)\right)\right)

\displaystyle\qquad-\sum_{i=1}^{j-2}\Sym_{i,j-i}\left(\mathbf{P}_{i+1}(t)\underset{1,1}{*}\mathbf{D}^{(j-i)}f(\widehat{x}_{\mathrm{M}}(t))\right)

\displaystyle\qquad-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=2}^{j-2}\Sym_{i,j-i}\left(\mathbf{P}_{i+1}(t)\underset{1,1}{*}\left(GR^{-1}G^{\top}\underset{2,1}{*}\mathbf{P}_{j-i+1}(t)\right)\right)

\displaystyle\qquad-\mathbf{D}^{(j)}h(\widehat{x}_{\mathrm{M}}(t))\underset{1,1}{*}Q(y(t)-h(\widehat{x}_{\mathrm{M}}(t))

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}\Sym_{i,j-i}\left(\mathbf{D}^{(i)}h(\widehat{x}_{\mathrm{M}}(t))\underset{1,1}{*}\left(Q\underset{2,1}{*}\mathbf{D}^{(j-i)}h(\widehat{x}_{\mathrm{M}}(t))\right)\right).

Finally, with
Lemma

3.9
together with (
3.7
), we obtain the asserted tensor equations.
The initial conditions again follow directly from the initial condition of the HJB equation (
1.4
).
∎

4.
Numerical experiments

To illustrate the analytical results and to showcase the improvements of higher order observers compared to the extended Kalman filter we implement the presented scheme for two examples, namely a low-dimensional chaotic system and a semilinear hyperbolic partial differential equation discretized in the spatial variables. We note that while these experiments justify the concept and motivate further research, the design of a sophisticated algorithm taking into account the structure of the equations (
3.2
) and scaling to systems of larger dimensions will be addressed in future work.
The MATLAB
®
code relies on the tensor toolbox
[
3
]
and is available in
[
10
]
.
All experiments were run in MATLAB
®
R2024b. The (higher order) extended Kalman filters are realized using the MATLAB
®
routine
ode45
with relative tolerance
10^{-6}
and absolute tolerance
10^{-8}
.
The results are compared to approximations of the Mortensen observer subsequently denoted as
\widehat{x}_{\mathrm{M}}
. For the low-dimensional example this approximation is obtained via an integration of the observer equation (
1.3
) using a BDF4 scheme. Evaluations of the Hessian
\nabla^{2}\mathcal{V}
are realized by treating the associated open-loop problem using gradient descent and subsequently solving a matrix Riccati equation, cf.
[

, for the details.
This approach is not feasible for the system of dimension twelve resulting from the spatial discretization of the PDE. Instead, an approximation of the Mortensen observer is obtained using the scheme proposed in

[
9
]
, see
Section

4.2.2
for the details, and
[
33
]
for the MATLAB
®
code.

4.1.
Duffing oscillator

The system under consideration in the first example is a Duffing oscillator, which in state-space form reads

(4.1)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\begin{bmatrix}x_{1}(t)\\
x_{2}(t)\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=A\begin{bmatrix}x_{1}(t)\\
x_{2}(t)\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}0\\
-\beta\,x_{1}(t)^{3}\end{bmatrix}+G\,v(t),\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \begin{bmatrix}x_{1}(0)\\
x_{2}(0)\end{bmatrix}=x_{0}+\eta,

\displaystyle y(t)

\displaystyle=C\begin{bmatrix}x_{1}(t)\\
x_{2}(t)\end{bmatrix}+\mu(t),

where

A=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
-\lambda&-\delta\end{bmatrix},\quad G=\begin{bmatrix}0\\
1\end{bmatrix},\quad\text{and}\quad C=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\end{bmatrix}.

The parameters for the system and the disturbances for the construction of the output

are set to

\lambda=-1,\penalty\ \beta=1,\penalty\ \delta=\tfrac{3}{10},\penalty\ v(t)=\gamma\cos(\omega t),\penalty\ \gamma=\tfrac{1}{2},\penalty\ \omega=\tfrac{12}{10}.

This setting is known to cause chaotic behavior
[
21
,
6
]
and results in a clear discrepancy between the extended Kalman filter and the Mortensen observer
[
9
]
.
The disturbance in the output is set to

\mu(t)=\tfrac{1}{20}\sin(2\pi t)

and for the initial state and its disturbance we set

x_{0}=\begin{bmatrix}0&0\end{bmatrix}^{\top}
and
\eta=\begin{bmatrix}-1.216&0.493\end{bmatrix}^{\top}
.
Note that this combination of a chaotic system with challenging disturbances makes the state estimation an extremely difficult task. In fact, no observer without properly tuned weighting matrices can be expected to yield satisfactory reconstructions of the state. In our experiments we consider the time horizon
[0,T]=[0,10]

4.2.
Semi-discretized cubic wave equation

In the second experiment we consider the defocusing, cubic wave equation given by

\displaystyle\partial_{tt}{u}(t,x)+{u}^{3}(t,x)

\displaystyle=\Delta{u}(t,x)+\nu(t,x)\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\

\displaystyle(t,x)\in(0,T)\times\Omega,

\displaystyle{u}(0,x)

\displaystyle=u_{1}(x)+\eta_{1}(x)\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\

\displaystyle x\in\Omega,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}{u}(0,x)

\displaystyle=u_{2}(x)+\eta_{2}(x)\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\

\displaystyle x\in\Omega,

with spatial domain
\Omega=(0,1)\times(0,1)\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
, given initial data
u_{1}\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
and
u_{2}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
, homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions, and unknown disturbances
\eta_{1}\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
,
\eta_{2}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
, and
\nu\in L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
. The output is modeled as

y(t)=\mathcal{C}\begin{bmatrix}u(t)\\
\partial_{t}u(t)\end{bmatrix}+\mu(t)\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ t\in(0,T),

with a bounded, linear operator
\mathcal{C}\colon H^{1}(\Omega)\times L^{2}(\Omega)\to\mathbb{R}^{p}
,
p\in\mathbb{N}
, and the observation error
\mu\in L^{2}(0,T;\mathbb{R}^{p})
.
In
[
32
]
we studied the Mortensen observer for an equation of this type with a three-dimensional domain
\Omega
. Specifically, we reformulated the system in first order form using mild solutions, derived the associated observer equation and showed local well-posedness of the estimator.
For the sake of completeness we repeat the critical concepts here. The first order form reads

(4.2)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\begin{bmatrix}w_{1}(t)\\
w_{2}(t)\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{A}\begin{bmatrix}w_{1}(t)\\
w_{2}(t)\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}0\\
-w_{1}^{3}(t)\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}0\\
\nu(t)\end{bmatrix}\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ t\in(0,T),\quad\begin{bmatrix}w_{1}(0)\\
w_{2}(0)\end{bmatrix}=w_{0}+\eta,

\displaystyle y(t)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{C}\begin{bmatrix}w_{1}(t)\\
w_{2}(t)\end{bmatrix}+\mu(t)\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ t\in(0,T),

where
\mathcal{A}=\begin{bmatrix}0&\mathrm{Id}\\
\Delta&0\end{bmatrix}
denotes the unbounded wave operator in the energy space
\mathcal{E}=H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\times L^{2}(\Omega)
and we set
w_{0}=[u_{1},u_{2}]\in\mathcal{E}
and
\eta=[\eta_{1},\eta_{2}]\in\mathcal{E}
.
The observation operator
\mathcal{C}
used in this specific experiment is constructed as follows.
We assume access to time-continuous measurements of the wave’s displacement
w_{1}
stemming from
p\in\mathbb{N}
sensors placed in the domain
\Omega
and denote the position of the
i
-th sensor by
(\nu_{1}^{i},\nu_{2}^{i})\in\Omega
. Since
w_{1}(t,\cdot)\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
might not be continuous, point evaluations are not available. Instead, we model each sensor to average the displacement on a square of length
2\ell>0
around
(\nu_{1}^{i},\nu_{2}^{i})
and arrive at the output operator

\mathcal{C}\begin{bmatrix}w_{1}\\
w_{2}\end{bmatrix}=\frac{1}{4\ell^{2}}\begin{bmatrix}\int_{S^{1}}w_{1}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\penalty\ \dots\penalty\ \int_{S^{p}}w_{1}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\end{bmatrix}^{\top},

where
S^{i}=(\nu_{1}^{i}-\ell,\nu_{1}^{i}+\ell)\times(\nu_{2}^{i}-\ell,\nu_{2}^{i}+\ell)
denotes the square of length
2\ell
around the
i
-th sensor.

The extension of the Mortensen observer to the wave equation proposed in
[
32
]
is based on the value function
\mathcal{V}(t,\Xi)
associated with the control problem

(4.3)

\displaystyle\min J(w,\nu;t,y_{\mathrm{abs}})

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\|w(0)-w_{0}\|_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\|\nu(s)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|y(s)-\mathcal{C}w(s)\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\text{subject to}\penalty\penalty\penalty\penalty\penalty\penalty\penalty\penalty\penalty\penalty

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}w(s)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{A}w(s)+\begin{bmatrix}0\\
-w_{1}^{3}(s)\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}0\\
\nu(s)\end{bmatrix}\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ s\in(0,t),

\displaystyle w(t)

\displaystyle=\Xi,

defined on infinite-dimensional Hilbert spaces. We note that the three terms in the cost function
J
appear in their respective Hilbert space norms. This is to be understood as the infinite-dimensional extension of using Euclidean norms, i.e., setting the weights
\Gamma
,
R
, and
Q
as identities.

4.2.1.
Discretization in space

We briefly describe the spatial discretization based on spectral-like elements. For a more detailed presentation we refer to the thesis
[
31
]
.
For
i,j\in\mathbb{N}
we define the basis function

\varphi_{i,j}\colon\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R},\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \varphi_{i,j}(x,y)=\sin(\pi ix)\,\sin(\pi jy),

and obtain the basis
\left\{\varphi_{i,j}\mid i,j\in\mathbb{N}\right\}
that is orthogonal both in
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
and
L^{2}(\Omega)
.
For any time-dependent trajectory of states
w(t,x_{1},x_{2})=\begin{bmatrix}w_{1}(t,x_{1},x_{2})\\
w_{2}(t,x_{1},x_{2})\end{bmatrix}
we have

(4.4)

w_{1}(t,x_{1},x_{2})=\sum_{i,j=1}^{\infty}z_{1,i,j}(t)\varphi_{i,j}(x_{1},x_{2}),\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ w_{2}(t,x_{1},x_{2})=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}z_{2,i,j}(t)\varphi_{i,j}(x_{1},x_{2}),

with appropriate, time-dependent coordinates
z_{1,i,j}
and
z_{2,i,j}
. We obtain the spatial discretization by choosing an integer
K\geq 2
and truncating the basis to

\mathcal{B}=\left\{\varphi_{i,j}\colon i+j\leq K\right\},

containing
N=\frac{K(K-1)}{2}
basis elements.
Inserting the approximation of
w_{1}
and
w_{2}
obtained by truncating (
4.4
) using the constructed basis (and analogous approximations of
\nu
,
w_{0}
, and
\eta
) into the control problem (
4.3
) yields a finite-dimensional approximation where the states are characterized by the coordinates
z\colon(0,T)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2N},\penalty\ z(\cdot)=\begin{bmatrix}z_{1}(\cdot)\\
z_{2}(\cdot)\end{bmatrix}
.
By algebraic manipulations and a state space transformation one arrives at the equivalent formulation

(4.5)

\displaystyle\min\mathcal{J}(z,v;t,y)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\|z(0)-\mathcal{T}z_{0}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\|v(s)\|^{2}+\|y(s)-Cz(s)\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s,

\displaystyle\text{subject to}\penalty\penalty\penalty\penalty\penalty\penalty\penalty\penalty\penalty

\displaystyle\dot{z}

\displaystyle=Az+\begin{bmatrix}0\\
M^{-\tfrac{1}{2}}g(M^{-\tfrac{1}{2}}z_{1})\end{bmatrix}+Bv,

\displaystyle z(t)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{T}\xi,

where
z_{0}
,
\xi
, and
v
correspond to the truncated coordinates of
w_{0}
,
\Xi
, and
\nu
, respectively. The system matrices are given as

(4.6)

A=\begin{bmatrix}0&S^{\tfrac{1}{2}}M^{-\tfrac{1}{2}}\\
-M^{-\tfrac{1}{2}}S^{\tfrac{1}{2}}&0\end{bmatrix},\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ B=\begin{bmatrix}0\\
\mathrm{Id}\end{bmatrix},\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ C=\tilde{C}\,T^{-1},

where
\tilde{C}=\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{C}_{1}&0\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{p,2N}
is defined via

\tilde{C}_{1}=\frac{1}{4\ell^{2}}\begin{bmatrix}\int_{S^{i}}\varphi_{k}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\end{bmatrix}_{i=1,k=1}^{i=p,k=N},

and

M=\left[(\varphi_{i},\varphi_{j})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\right]_{i,j=1}^{N},\quad S=\left[(\varphi_{i},\varphi_{j})_{H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}\right]_{i,j=1}^{N}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{T}=\begin{bmatrix}S^{\tfrac{1}{2}}&0\\
0&M^{\tfrac{1}{2}}\end{bmatrix}.

The nonlinearity is

g(z)=g\left(\begin{bmatrix}z_{1}\\
z_{2}\end{bmatrix}\right)=\left[\left((\sum_{k=1}^{N}z_{1,k}\varphi_{k})^{3},\varphi_{i}\right)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\right]_{i=1}^{N}.

4.2.2.
Realization of the estimators

We realize the approximations of the Mortensen observer for the semi-discretized system associated with the control problem (
4.5
) of dimension
2N
. The system matrices
(A,B,C)
are given in (
4.6
) and the cost functional
J
, see (
4.3
), in combination with our chosen discretization scheme implies that the weights
\Gamma
,
R
, and
Q
are given by identities of appropriate sizes, cf. (
4.5
). The higher order extended Kalman filters are then realized as described in the beginning of
Section

4
.

As mentioned above the dimension of the system prohibits a realization of the Mortensen observer by means of the approach that was applied for the first example. Instead, we construct a data-driven approximation
\mathcal{V}_{p}
of the associated value function using a slightly adjusted version of the scheme proposed in
[
9
]
. The estimators are then realized either by solving the observer equation using
\nabla^{2}\mathcal{V}_{p}^{-1}
or by minimizing
\mathcal{V}_{p}(t,\cdot)
.
We begin by pointing out the minor adjustments. First, the gradient descent scheme for solving open-loop control problems to generate the data set is altered. More precisely, we omit the line search applied in
[
9
, Alg. 3.1]
and instead set the step size directly via Barzilai-Borwein.
The second adjustment is concerned with fitting a polynomial to the data set. Denoting the evaluation of the polynomial basis functions in the sampling points by
H
, the vector containing the data points by
V
, and the size of the polynomial basis by
N_{p}
it reads

\min_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{p}}}\|H\,\theta-V\|_{2}^{2}.

Here the large number of samples and basis functions required for a sufficiently accurate approximation of the value function lead to a dense matrix
H
of enormous dimension. Hence, due to memory limitations,
a direct solve is no longer feasible.
Instead we solve

\min_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{p}}}\|H^{\top}H\,\theta-H^{\top}V\|_{2}^{2}

using the MATLAB
®
, backslash routine, i.e.,
\theta=(H^{\top}H)\backslash(H^{\top}V)
, where
H^{\top}H
and
H^{\top}V
are constructed in a block-wise, iterative fashion, sidestepping the memory issue.

We turn to the specifics of the implementation and note that the gradient descent scheme terminates when reaching a relative tolerance of
10^{-6}
.
The associated state equations and adjoint equations are solved by an application of a BDF4 scheme using 501 time discretization points. The nonlinearities are treated by a Newton scheme with absolute tolerance
10^{-12}
. Since the Riccati equations yielding evaluations of the Hessians depend on the previously realized optimal states and adjoints, we integrate the DREs using a BDF4 scheme using the same time nodes. The implicit time steps are realized by the MATLAB
®
routine icare. Only for the four initial time steps a Newton scheme is employed, where the absolute tolerance is set to
10^{-8}
. The first initial control for the gradient descent is set to zero and the second one is obtained by performing the first step of the gradient descent with fixed step size
10^{-4}
.
With an approximation of the value function at hand the observer equation is integrated using a BDF4 scheme with
10^{3}
discretization points and a Newton scheme scheme with absolute tolerance
10^{-8}
. Also, the approximated value function is minimized using a gradient descent scheme with relative and absolute tolerance
10^{-6}
.

5.
Conclusion & Outlook

By means of successive differentiations of the HJB equation, we derived a constitutive set of coupled vector, matrix and tensor differential equations generalizing the classical extended Kalman filter. From a theoretical perspective, the approach is justified by assuming that a higher order derivative of the minimum energy value function is negligible in the vicinity of the unknown Mortensen observer trajectory. The provided numerical experiment shows a considerable improvement of our approach over the extended Kalman filter in the sense of (numerically) converging to the exact Mortensen observer when higher order polynomials are considered.

While the method appears promising both in terms of required numerical effort as well as approximately solving the minimum energy estimation problem, many direct and related research questions remain open. First of all, we have not discussed existence and uniqueness of a solution to
equation

3.2
. Even if this can be shown, the results should only be expected to be of a local nature as already our numerical experiments indicate a strong dependence of the involved parameters and the degree of the considered polynomials. On the other hand, a theoretical study of
equation

3.2
could further open up a way to analytically explain the frequently reported success of the extended Kalman filter. From a numerical perspective, the next steps should evolve around a more efficient implementation for solving
equation

3.2
, a natural starting point being the recently developed tensor methods for Taylor series based feedback stabilization from
[
22
,
14
]
.
