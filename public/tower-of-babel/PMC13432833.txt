Clinical characteristics of ALK-positive NSCLC patients treated with sequential ALK-TKI therapies in a single-centre
Authors: Kohei Fujita, Takuma Imakita, Naoki Fujimoto, Saiki Yoshimura, Shogo Toyama, Takanori Ito, Issei Oi, Osamu Kanai, Yuki Yamamoto, Kiminobu Tanizawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAlthough sequential treatment with ALK-TKIs for ALK-positive lung cancer is commonly practiced in clinical settings, there are few reports on the actual treatment choices, efficacy and safety.MethodsThis retrospective study evaluated the treatment sequence, progression-free survival (PFS), overall survival (OS), and adverse events (AEs) for the patients who received two or more ALK-TKIs.ResultsEleven patients were included in the study. The median age was 59 years, and five patients (45.5%) were female. The number of ALK-TKIs administered was two in five patients, three in five patients, and four in one patient. The drugs used were Alectinib in 10 patients, Lorlatinib in eight, Brigatinib in six, Ceritinib in three, and Crizotinib in two. Six patients (54.5%) had a history of treatment with cytotoxic chemotherapy in addition to ALK-TKIs. The most common first-line drug was Alectinib, followed by Brigatinib and Crizotinib. The median PFS for each treatment line was 23 months for the first line, 17 months for the second, 14 months for the third, and 5 months for the fourth. The median OS from 1st ALK-TKI was 58 months. Discontinuation of ALK-TKI due to adverse events occurred in six of the total treatment cases, and five of these (83.3%) were attributed to Brigatinib.ConclusionSequential treatment with different types of ALK-TKIs demonstrated a certain degree of efficacy. However, the incidence of discontinuation due to adverse events was higher for Brigatinib than for other ALK-TKIs.

Introduction

Lung cancer, including tracheal and bronchial cancer, was the world’s most commonly diagnosed cancer and the leading cause of cancer death in 2022. There were 2.48 million new cases and 1.82 million deaths worldwide. Asia bore the greatest burden, accounting for over 63% of cases and deaths. Marked sex and regional differences were seen, with especially high incidence in some parts of Europe and Asia. The findings underline the urgent need for stronger tobacco control, earlier diagnosis, and better access to treatment globally [1]. The anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK) fusion gene is a potent oncogene found in 5–8% of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) patients [2]. For patients with this fusion gene, ALK tyrosine kinase inhibitors (ALK-TKIs) have demonstrated remarkable antitumor effects and have become the cornerstone of treatment in recent years. ALK-TKIs have been developed sequentially across generations, with the aim of achieving greater efficacy and overcoming resistance [3–5].

ALK-TKIs are categorized into first, second, and third generations based on their development timeline and differential effectiveness against resistance mutations. A major challenge of ALK-TKI therapy is the acquired drug resistance that emerges over time. This resistance is associated with secondary mutations in the ALK gene itself and the activation of alternative signaling pathways [6].

In cases of acquired resistance to ALK-TKIs, genetic analysis frequently identifies secondary mutations within the ALK gene. The specific type of mutation can guide the selection of subsequent therapeutic agents. Consequently, sequential therapy, which involves the successive use of first, second, and third-generation ALK-TKIs in response to the emergence of resistance, is considered crucial for prolonging patient survival. However, there is a paucity of data from real-world clinical practice regarding sequential treatment with multiple ALK-TKIs.

This study examined the clinical characteristics, efficacy, and safety of sequential ALK-TKI therapy in a single-center cohort.

Patients and methods

This study was conducted in the National Hospital Organization Kyoto Medical Center, Kyoto, Japan between April 2016 and March 2026.

We collected the clinical data of the ALK-positive patients who received multiple ALK-TKI therapies.

At the time of writing this paper, five ALK-TKIs; crizotinib, alectinib, ceritinib, lorlatinib, and brigatinib, have been approved in Japan. This study reviewed patients with ALK-positive NSCLC who had received any of these five ALK-TKIs. Treatment lines were counted as follows: only ALK-TKI therapy was counted as a treatment line, while administration of cytotoxic anticancer drugs was not included in the line count. Progression-free survival (PFS) was defined as the time from treatment initiation to disease progression or death from any cause. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (A) a diagnosis of ALK-positive NSCLC; (B) treatment with two or more ALK-TKIs; and (C) availability of clinical course data in the electronic medical records.The exclusion criteria were as follows: (A) treatment with one or fewer ALK-TKIs; and (B) inability to confirm the clinical course from the electronic medical records.

The dose of each ALK-TKI was increased or reduced in accordance with the respective prescribing information. PFS was defined as the time from treatment initiation to disease progression or death from any cause. Patients without these events were censored at the last follow-up. The Kaplan–Meier method was used to estimate survival curves and calculate median PFS with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

Results

During the study period, 19 ALK-positive NSCLC patients received ALK-TKI treatment. Regarding the diagnostic method for ALK-positive cases: from 2014 to 2019, we used a two-stage screening process involving IHC and FISH. From 2019 to 2026, we have been conducting multiplex testing using next generation sequence (NGS). The test adopted at our hospital is the Oncomine Dx Target Test. Of these, 11 patients received multiple ALK-TKI treatments and were included in the current analysis. Characteristics of the patients are shown in Table 1. The mean age was 57 years, and five patients (45.5%) were female. Regarding metastatic patterns at baseline, four patients (36.4%) had brain metastases, which is a common site of involvement in ALK-positive NSCLC and a critical consideration for treatment selection given the importance of central nervous system (CNS) penetration of ALK-TKIs. Three patients (27.3%) presented with pleural effusion, one (9.1%) had bone metastases, and one (9.1%) had liver metastases. ALK diagnosis was performed using immunohistochemistry (IHC) and fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) in seven patients, and using NGS in four patients. PD-L1 tumor proportion score (TPS) was available in six patients; one had TPS <1%, four had TPS of 1–49%, and one had TPS ≥50%. PD-L1 expression was unknown in five patients. Regarding the administration of cytotoxic chemotherapy, two patients received two courses of CBDCA plus PTX as concurrent chemoradiotherapy before first-line ALK-TKI treatment. In addition, three patients received platinum doublet therapy after sequential ALK-TKI treatment. No patients received cytotoxic chemotherapy during the sequence of ALK-TKI treatments. Regarding radiotherapy for CNS metastases, three patients underwent stereotactic radiotherapy and one patient underwent whole-brain radiotherapy. In addition, one patient received palliative radiotherapy to the thoracic spine.

Table 3 shows the adverse events. The grade of adverse events is reported in accordance with the CTCAE version5.0. Adverse events associated with ALK-TKIs were generally uncommon. Hyperlipidemia was most frequent with Lorlatinib (4 cases), whereas nausea was most common with Brigatinib (3 cases). Diarrhea, edema and CK elevation were otherwise limited to isolated cases. Early onset pulmonary event was observed in one patient treated with Brigatinib. Discontinuation of ALK-TKI due to adverse events occurred in six of the total treatment cases, and five of these (83.3%) were attributed to Brigatinib, representing a notably higher rate of treatment discontinuation compared to other ALK-TKIs. This finding was consistent across both first-line and subsequent-line uses of this drug.

Discussion

This retrospective, single-center study evaluated the clinical characteristics, treatment patterns, efficacy, and safety of sequential ALK-TKI therapy in 11 ALK-positive NSCLC patients. Our findings demonstrate that sequential use of multiple ALK-TKIs can provide sustained antitumor activity across several lines of treatment, as evidenced by a median OS of 58 months from the initiation of the first ALK-TKI. The median PFS of 23 months observed in the first-line setting in our cohort is broadly consistent with real-world data, though somewhat shorter than the results of pivotal phase III trials [3]. The ALEX trial demonstrated a median investigator-assessed PFS of 34.8 months with alectinib compared with 10.9 months with crizotinib in treatment-naive ALK-positive NSCLC patients [3]. Similarly, the J-ALEX trial, conducted exclusively in Japanese patients, showed a significantly prolonged PFS with alectinib over crizotinib (median PFS not reached vs. 10.2 months at interim analysis) [8]. The modest discrepancy between our real-world results and the pivotal trial data may be attributable to the heterogeneity of the patient population, differences in performance status, the presence of more advanced disease stages, and the inclusion of a broader spectrum of first-line agents beyond alectinib alone in our cohort (alectinib n = 6, brigatinib n = 3, crizotinib n = 2).

The median PFS values in the second (17 months) and third lines (14 months) are notable given that patients reaching these lines represent a selected population that has developed resistance to prior ALK-TKIs. The ALTA-1L trial demonstrated a median PFS of 24.0 months with first-line brigatinib versus 11.0 months with crizotinib [4]; however, the second- and third-line efficacy of subsequent TKIs after brigatinib failure has been less extensively studied in prospective settings.

Lorlatinib was the most commonly used agent in later lines, being administered to 8 of 11 patients (3 in 2nd line, 4 in 3rd line, 1 in 4th line). As a third-generation ALK-TKI with broad activity against virtually all known ALK resistance mutations – including the G1202R compound mutations that confer resistance to second-generation agents – lorlatinib represents a key component of sequential treatment strategies [9]. The phase 2 study by Solomon et al. demonstrated an overall response rate (ORR) of approximately 47% and a median PFS of 5.5 months in patients who had received ≥1 second-generation ALK-TKI, with improved outcomes in patients harboring ALK resistance mutations [10]. In our cohort, the median PFS of 14 months in the third line – where lorlatinib was used in 4 patients – compares favorably with published data and may reflect patient selection and the sequential availability of molecular testing in our clinical practice. The first-line CROWN trial established lorlatinib’s superiority over crizotinib (median PFS not reached vs. 9.3 months) [5], but the role of lorlatinib as a salvage therapy remains essential in patients who progress after second-generation ALK-TKIs.

A notable finding in this study was the high rate of treatment discontinuation attributable to brigatinib. Among the six treatment discontinuations due to adverse events across all lines, five (83.3%) were associated with brigatinib use. This finding warrants careful consideration, as brigatinib is associated with a well-recognized class of early-onset pulmonary events (EOPEs) that are unique to this agent. Ng et al. reported that EOPEs occurred in approximately 4.5% of patients at the standard 90 mg starting dose across clinical trials, with median onset within 2 days of initiation [11]. The step-up dosing strategy (starting at 90 mg for 7 days before escalating to 180 mg) was introduced specifically to mitigate this risk. However, it is possible that some patients in our cohort received brigatinib without optimal implementation of this dose escalation regimen, or that patients who had received prior ALK-TKIs had sensitized lung parenchyma, predisposing them to pulmonary toxicity. Beyond EOPEs, brigatinib is associated with other adverse events including hypertension, peripheral neuropathy, and increased creatine phosphokinase levels, which may collectively contribute to the higher discontinuation rate observed in our study. These findings underscore the importance of close monitoring, particularly during the first week of brigatinib initiation, and reinforce the need for individualized toxicity management strategies.

The emergence of acquired resistance to ALK-TKIs is driven by multiple mechanisms, which broadly fall into two categories: ALK-dependent resistance (secondary ALK mutations) and ALK-independent resistance (bypass signaling pathway activation). Gainor et al. performed comprehensive molecular profiling of post-ALK-TKI progression biopsies and demonstrated that each generation of ALK inhibitor is associated with a distinct spectrum of resistance mutations [9]. For example, the G1202R mutation – which is resistant to most second-generation agents – is sensitive to Lorlatinib, providing the mechanistic rationale for sequential Lorlatinib use after second-generation ALK-TKI failure [9]. In ALK-independent resistance models, a bypass mechanism driven by EGFR phosphorylation (activation) has been found, and it has been shown that combined ALK inhibition plus EGFR inhibition may be effective in preclinical settings [12]. In addition, in Lorlatinib resistance, it has been shown that multiple bypass mechanisms—such as epithelial–mesenchymal transition and loss of NF2 function—may coexist [13]. In our cohort, the frequent use of Lorlatinib as a subsequent therapy after Alectinib or Brigatinib is consistent with this molecular framework, though formal molecular resistance profiling data were not uniformly available due to the retrospective nature of the study. The integration of liquid biopsy or repeat tissue biopsy at progression to identify ALK resistance mutations could further optimize sequential treatment selection in future clinical practice.

The median OS of 58 months from initiation of the first ALK-TKI is clinically significant and compares favorably with earlier data from the pre-second-generation ALK-TKI era. Real-world studies have reported median OS exceeding 40–50 months in ALK-positive NSCLC patients treated with sequential ALK-TKIs, reflecting the cumulative benefit of multiple lines of active targeted therapy [14]. The availability of at least three effective generations of ALK-TKIs – combined with increasingly sophisticated resistance profiling – has transformed ALK-positive NSCLC into a disease in which long-term survival is achievable even in the metastatic setting. The OS data from our cohort lend further support to this paradigm.

Four of the 11 patients (36.4%) in our cohort had brain metastases at baseline, underscoring the clinical importance of intracranial disease control in ALK-positive NSCLC. Second-generation ALK-TKIs, particularly alectinib, have demonstrated robust intracranial activity in both clinical trial and real-world settings. In the ALEX trial, alectinib significantly reduced the risk of CNS progression compared with crizotinib (CNS objective response rate 59% vs. 26% in patients with baseline CNS lesions) [3]. Lorlatinib also exhibits exceptional CNS penetration due to its macrocyclic structure, with high intracranial ORR reported in pretreated patients [10]. The sequential use of these agents in our cohort likely contributed to sustained intracranial disease control, which may be reflected in the observed OS benefit.

Existing ALK-TKI clinical trials have employed differing diagnostic methods (IHC, FISH, NGS, etc.), and discordance between test results has been shown to correlate with poorer prognosis. Furthermore, ALK-positive lung cancer is a heterogeneous disease: early co-occurring mutations such as TP53, or the presence of particular EML4-ALK variants, have been reported to confer intrinsic resistance to ALK-TKIs and to increase the risk of early progression [15].

Urbanska et al. propose risk stratification based on these clinical and molecular biological factors [15]. For the “low-risk group” – patients without co-occurring mutations or brain metastases and with negative ctDNA – sequential therapy starting with a second-generation TKI is suggested to be useful from the standpoint of avoiding overtreatment. For the “high-risk group,” which includes patients with early co-occurring mutations or brain metastases, initial treatment with the most potent third-generation agent, lorlatinib, or combination therapy, among other approaches, may be considered. Further accumulation of real-world clinical data will be needed to refine future treatment strategies.

Limitations

This study has several important limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the retrospective, single-center design and small sample size limit the statistical power and generalizability of our findings. The small cohort precludes meaningful subgroup analyses and multivariate survival analyses. Second, the heterogeneity of treatment sequences across patients – reflecting real-world clinical decision-making rather than a standardized protocol – makes it difficult to attribute efficacy outcomes to specific treatment regimens. Third, in this study cases presented here, we did not perform a re-biopsy to look for ALK-TKI resistance mutations, for the following reasons: First, Japanese healthcare is under a national insurance system, which currently does not cover the testing of ALK resistance mutations. Second, testing for these resistance mutations is not commercially available; it can only be performed at a limited number of research institutions. Consequently, it is challenging for community hospitals like ours to routinely test for resistance mutations in all patients. Therefore, while our discussion is based on current research findings, we realize it was unclear whether we had identified a resistance mutation and selected the sequential therapy accordingly in our patient. Fourth, the median OS of 58 months, while promising, may be subject to lead-time bias and the influence of subsequent therapies beyond ALK-TKIs, including cytotoxic chemotherapy received by 45.5% of patients. Finally, due to the retrospective nature of the study, adverse event documentation may be incomplete and subject to reporting bias. Despite these limitations, our single-center real-world experience adds to the growing body of literature supporting the clinical utility of sequential ALK-TKI therapy.

Conclusions

In conclusion, sequential ALK-TKI therapy provides meaningful clinical benefit in ALK-positive NSCLC, with a median OS of 58 months from first-line treatment. Lorlatinib plays a critical role as a later-line salvage therapy with activity across multiple resistance mutations. The high rate of treatment discontinuation associated with Brigatinib underscores the importance of careful patient monitoring and individualized toxicity management. Future studies incorporating systematic molecular profiling at disease progression will be essential to further refine sequential treatment strategies and maximize long-term outcomes in this patient population.
