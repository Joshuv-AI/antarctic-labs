Neuroleptic malignant syndrome in a cervical spinal injury patient: a case report
Authors: Binayak Deb, Yudhyavir Singh, Shuchi Smita, Sreehari N
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Neuroleptic malignant syndrome (NMS) is a rare, idiosyncratic, and potentially fatal complication associated with the abrupt withdrawal of dopaminergic agents or the use of antidopaminergic medications that affect the hypothalamus and basal ganglia. Its hallmark features include severe hyperthermia, altered consciousness, skeletal muscle rigidity, and autonomic instability. We present the case of a young patient with a cervical spine injury who was admitted to the trauma intensive care unit (ICU). The patient developed a persistent high-grade fever that did not respond to standard interventions, which was ultimately attributed to NMS resulting from the administration of neuroleptic medications. Because of a high degree of clinical suspicion and the implementation of prompt therapeutic measures, the patient achieved a favorable clinical outcome. Early diagnosis and timely intervention are crucial in NMS due to its rapid clinical progression, particularly as the use of dopaminergic antagonists becomes more prevalent in ICU settings. This case highlights the diagnostic challenges of managing NMS alongside spinal trauma in the ICU, as NMS often mimics common postoperative and ICU-related complications, necessitating heightened clinical vigilance.

INTRODUCTION

Since the first description of neuroleptic malignant syndrome (NMS) by Delay and Deniker [1] in 1968, numerous case reports have emerged, particularly over the past two decades, paralleling the increasing use of neuroleptics to manage agitation in the intensive care unit (ICU). Although studies estimate an incidence of 0.01% to 0.02% [2], the true incidence is likely higher due to the syndrome’s nonspecific clinical presentation and broad differential diagnoses. Reported mortality rates for NMS range from 10% to 30% [3,4]. However, data regarding the incidence, prevalence, and mortality of NMS among critically ill trauma patients in the ICU remain limited. Its hallmark features include severe hyperthermia, altered consciousness, skeletal muscle rigidity, and autonomic instability. As a diagnosis of exclusion, NMS requires a high index of clinical suspicion to ensure prompt recognition and treatment.

CASE REPORT

A 22-year-old woman with no known comorbidities presented after a fall from approximately 3 m. Her primary survey was unremarkable except for limb weakness. In the upper limbs, she exhibited bilateral finger flexion with a power of 3/5, while the lower limbs demonstrated flaccid paralysis with a power of 0/5 and lax anal tone, findings suggestive of an incomplete cervical spinal cord injury. The secondary survey and radiologic evaluation revealed a teardrop fracture at C6–C7 with retropulsion of the posterior vertebral body, resulting in significant spinal canal narrowing (minimum diameter of 3 mm). Additional findings included a small right-sided hemothorax (7 mm), a minor contusion in the right lower lobe, and a fracture of the right eighth rib, with no other solid organ injuries identified.

On the third day of admission, she underwent anterior cervical discectomy and fusion under general anesthesia and was subsequently transferred to the ICU for mechanical ventilation. Her postoperative course was uneventful for the first 5 days, except for a low-grade fever and poor tolerance of enteral feeding. Metoclopramide (10 mg) injection was initiated for postoperative ileus, starting once daily and gradually increased to three times daily. Although the ileus showed signs of improvement, she continued to experience persistent high-grade fever spikes ranging from 38.3 to 40 °C. These febrile episodes were unresponsive to antipyretics, both active and passive cooling measures, and empirical antibiotic therapy.

By postoperative day 7, the patient became increasingly delirious, exhibiting incoherent speech and combative behavior that interfered with routine care. A diagnosis of ICU delirium was made, and quetiapine (25 mg) twice daily was initiated. Over the subsequent 5 days, her condition deteriorated further, characterized by persistent fever spikes reaching 41.7 °C, orofacial tremors, and pronounced muscle rigidity in the upper limbs. She required vasopressor support and transitioned from hyperactive to hypoactive delirium. Examination of the surgical site revealed a healthy wound, and cervical radiographs ruled out implant-related infection. Meanwhile, a comprehensive sepsis workup, including blood, urine, bronchoalveolar lavage, and cerebrospinal fluid studies, was negative, with a serum procalcitonin level of 0.54 ng/mL.

Suspecting a drug-induced fever, all potential causative agents, including metoclopramide and quetiapine, were discontinued, and serum creatine phosphokinase (CPK) levels were obtained to evaluate for NMS. Serum CPK was markedly elevated at 3,530 U/L (normal range, 26–140 U/L). Based on the clinical findings and laboratory results, a trial of bromocriptine (2.5 mg, three times daily) was initiated. By the third day of therapy, the patient demonstrated substantial clinical improvement, prompting an increase to the full therapeutic dose of 5 mg three times daily. Over the following days, her fever resolved completely, her sensorium improved to a Glasgow Coma Scale score of 15, and she became fully oriented to time, place, and person. Tremors and muscle rigidity resolved, allowing her to resume oral feeding and participate in physiotherapy. A repeat CPK measurement showed a reduction to 1,648 U/L. She was transferred from the ICU to the general ward, and after several days of observation, with a continued downward trend in CPK (final value, 316 U/L), she was discharged from the hospital. DISCUSSION

The exact cause of NMS remains a topic of debate. One theory suggests that blocking central dopamine receptors in the hypothalamus triggers dysautonomia, including hyperthermia [5]. Alternatively, some studies propose that NMS may result from a severe, dysregulated immune response to neuroleptic drugs. Supporting this hypothesis, laboratory findings in NMS often reveal leukocytosis and elevated transaminases, lactate dehydrogenase, and alkaline phosphatase levels. Additionally, Anglin et al. [6] observed a significant decline in serum iron levels in patients with NMS, further supporting the hypothesis of a dysregulated acute-phase response.

While most cases have been reported after the use of highly potent first-generation antipsychotic agents such as haloperidol and fluphenazine, increasing numbers of NMS cases have also been associated with low-potency antipsychotics, including risperidone, olanzapine, and quetiapine, as well as selective antidopaminergic antiemetics such as metoclopramide, promethazine, and levosulpiride. A distinctive feature of NMS is that its occurrence is not correlated with the dosage or duration of drug exposure, further suggesting a possible immunologic mechanism [7]. Reported risk factors include acute medical conditions (such as trauma, surgery, or infection), hypovolemia, agitation, use of physical restraints, substance abuse, preexisting psychiatric disorders, and rapid upward titration of neuroleptics to achieve therapeutic serum levels [8].

The array of signs, symptoms, and associated laboratory findings in NMS encompasses a broad differential diagnosis, including sepsis, encephalitis, toxic encephalopathy, heat stroke, acute-phase responses to trauma, and serotonin syndrome. In this case, the patient exhibited a progressive sequence of symptoms: altered consciousness, severe hyperpyrexia, muscle rigidity, and ultimately, hemodynamic instability. The differential limb weakness (lower limbs more than upper limbs) due to incomplete cervical spinal cord injury further complicated the assessment of muscle rigidity, one of the hallmark signs of NMS. Several diagnostic criteria for NMS exist, but the guidelines established by the American Psychiatric Association in 1994 remain the most widely used [9].

Once NMS is suspected, management requires the prompt discontinuation of the offending neuroleptic agent, aggressive hydration to prevent rhabdomyolysis and renal injury, correction of electrolyte imbalances, and strict temperature control. Active or passive cooling and antipyretic therapy are essential, as the high-grade fever associated with NMS can cause lasting neurological sequelae. Specific pharmacologic therapies include bromocriptine, a selective dopaminergic (D2) receptor agonist, and dantrolene, a ryanodine receptor antagonist that reduces excitation-contraction coupling in skeletal muscle. Most NMS cases resolve completely within 2 weeks (typically within 7–11 days) if recognized and treated early [10]. Although dantrolene is not widely available in India, bromocriptine therapy has proven effective in shortening the duration and improving outcomes in NMS.

The use of antidopaminergic agents in the ICU is increasing, particularly metoclopramide for ileus and neuroleptics such as haloperidol and quetiapine for managing agitated delirium. The Society of Critical Care Medicine’s sedation guidelines recommend haloperidol as the preferred agent for delirium management in critically ill patients (grade C recommendation). In such clinical scenarios, ICU physicians must maintain a high index of suspicion for NMS and initiate timely management once diagnosed.

This case underscores both the diagnostic challenge and the clinical urgency of managing NMS in the context of spinal trauma within the ICU. The overlap between NMS manifestations and common postoperative or ICU complications necessitates heightened awareness among clinicians. Prompt recognition and intervention are critical given the rapid and potentially fulminant progression of NMS. As the use of dopaminergic antagonists becomes increasingly common in intensive care settings, vigilance for this life-threatening but treatable syndrome is essential to ensure favorable patient outcomes.
