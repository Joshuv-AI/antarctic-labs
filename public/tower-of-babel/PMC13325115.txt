Therapeutic Potential of Extracellular Vesicles Derived from Sheep Placenta in the Treatment of Skin Disorders
Authors: Guiyang He, Ruijie Fan, Jie Zhao, Juanjuan He, Zhongtian Shi, Peiyu Liu, Heng Zhang, Chong Wu, Meng Jia, Xudong Ao, Junqing Liang, Fang Lu, Bing Liu, Ke Zen, Can Zhang, Shaochang Jia, Yongbin Liu, Yaoxin Lu, Jiangfeng He, Hongwei Liang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Skin disorders such as chronic wounds, inflammation, and premature aging remain difficult to treat due to limited therapies and poor regeneration. Extracellular vesicles (EVs) are emerging as promising cell-free therapeutics, but those from human placenta or stem cells face ethical, cost, and scalability barriers. Here, we isolated and characterized sheep placenta-derived EVs (spEVs), an ethical and sustainable alternative. SpEVs exhibited a bilayer structure (150 nm) and expressed typical exosomal markers. Their RNA and protein cargos were enriched in regenerative and anti-inflammatory pathways. Functionally, spEVs promoted keratinocyte proliferation, migration, and angiogenesis, and improved psoriasis-like, UVB-induced, and diabetic wounds in mice. This study identifies sheep placenta as a scalable and ethical source of EVs with promising preclinical potential for skin regeneration.

Introduction

Skin disorders are among the most prevalent health conditions worldwide and impose considerable clinical and socioeconomic burdens. − According to the Global Burden of Disease Study, skin and subcutaneous diseases affect hundreds of millions of people annually, ranking among the top causes of nonfatal disease burden. − Chronic wounds, burns, ulcers, and inflammatory dermatoses not only compromise the structural and functional integrity of the skin but also lead to pain, infection, delayed healing, scarring, and reduced quality of life. − Current treatment strategies, including topical drugs, dressings, surgical grafting, and advanced biologics, provide only partial efficacy and are often associated with high costs, risk of infection, and limited ability to achieve complete regeneration. − These challenges highlight the urgent need for novel therapeutic approaches that can accelerate wound closure, restore skin function, and minimize adverse outcomes.

Extracellular vesicles (EVs) participate in essential physiological activities, including cell-to-cell communication and tissue repair. Among them, exosomes represent a distinct subtype, typically 30–200 nm in diameter, and serve as carriers of diverse biomolecules, such as proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids. Their molecular cargo is determined by the parent cell that generates them. By acting as molecular messengers, exosomes deliver these bioactive components to recipient cells, where they can regulate gene expression, signaling pathways, immune activity, and regenerative processes. Because they circulate through body fluidsincluding blood, saliva, and urinethey can reach remote tissues and exert systemic effects. Owing to their ability to transfer biological information and support repair mechanisms, exosomes are considered promising candidates for therapeutic strategies targeting skin disorders. , Recent studies have shown that EVs derived from human placenta or cultured mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) enhance keratinocyte and fibroblast proliferation, promote cell migration, stimulate angiogenesis, and suppress inflammation, thereby accelerating wound closure and reducing scar formation. − Nevertheless, the clinical use of placenta- or MSC-derived EVs remains limited by restricted tissue sources, ethical challenges, high production costs, and difficulties in achieving large-scale manufacturing and quality control. , On the one hand, placenta-derived EVs raise ethical concerns and carry the risk of transmitting infections. Human placental tissues may harbor latent viruses or unknown pathogens, creating potential risks of contamination and disease transmission. In addition, the processes of placenta collection, preservation, and transportation are logistically challenging and require strict biosafety measures, which further hinder clinical translation. On the other hand, EVs obtained from cultured MSCs rely on large-scale in vitro expansion of stem cells. This process requires serum-free media, sterile facilities compliant with good manufacturing practice (GMP) standards, and highly specialized equipment. These requirements significantly increase production costs and pose difficulties in ensuring reproducibility and consistency across batches. Furthermore, large-scale MSC culture is labor intensive, time-consuming, and often limited by donor variability, which restricts the feasibility of widespread clinical use. Taken together, while human placenta- and MSC-derived EVs have demonstrated strong regenerative and anti-inflammatory effects in preclinical studies, their high cost, biosafety risks, and production barriers underscore the urgent need to explore alternative, safer, and more economical EV sources for the treatment of skin disorders. More recently, increasing attention has been directed toward EVs derived from nonhuman or natural sources, including plant-derived and animal-derived EVs, which exhibit advantages such as low immunogenicity, intrinsic bioactivity, and scalability for large-scale production. − These developments highlight the growing interest in alternative EV sources beyond traditional human-derived systems. −

Sheep placenta is frequently discarded after birth under the assumption that it is waste material, which can contribute to environmental concerns. Importantly, sheep proteins and RNAs share a high degree of homology with their human counterparts, suggesting that they may perform comparable biological functions in the human body. , Consequently, EVs derived from sheep placenta are likely to exhibit similar therapeutic potential to those obtained from human placenta. Indeed, sheep placental extracts have long been used in traditional medicine and cosmetic formulations for the management of skin disorders. − This evidence positions the sheep placenta as a promising yet largely underutilized alternative source of EVs to replace human placenta- or MSC-derived EVs for treating skin disorders. Exploiting sheep placenta–derived EVs could therefore provide a unique opportunity to couple regenerative benefits with practical advantages such as lower production costs, ethical acceptability, scalability, and sustainable “waste-to-resource” conversion. Moreover, this strategy has the potential to generate additional economic value for sheep farming while simultaneously mitigating environmental impacts.

The present study aimed to develop a novel method for isolating EVs from sheep placenta, characterize their proteins and nucleic acid cargo, and evaluate their therapeutic potential for skin disorders in vitro and in vivo. Specifically, we assessed their effects on keratinocyte proliferation, migration, extracellular matrix synthesis, and inflammatory modulation, as well as their ability to promote wound closure and re-epithelialization in a murine skin injury model. To our knowledge, this is the first systematic investigation of sheep placenta–derived EVs in skin disorders, providing new insights into their potential as a scalable, cost-effective, and ethically favorable cell-free therapeutic strategy for skin regeneration.

Materials and Methods

Isolation of EVs from Sheep Placenta

Placental tissues were collected from sheep at the Inner Mongolia Academy of Agricultural and Animal Husbandry Sciences. Within 60 min following delivery, the maternal portion of the placenta was carefully dissected, and the upper segment was removed. The remaining tissue was chopped into small fragments and homogenized. EVs were subsequently separated from the homogenized suspension through a series of differential centrifugation steps. Specifically, centrifugation was performed at 300g for 10 min, followed by centrifugation at 2000g for 20 min and 10,000g for 40 min at 4 °C to sequentially eliminate intact cells, cellular debris, and larger vesicles. The supernatant was then passed through a sterile 0.22 μm filter (Millipore) and subjected to ultracentrifugation at 120,000g for 70 min at 4 °C. The resulting pellet, enriched with exosomes, was resuspended in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and stored at −80 °C until further use.

Proteomic Profiling of Sheep Placenta-Derived EVs

Sheep placenta-derived EVs were lysed and disrupted by ultrasonication, after which their proteins were resolved via SDS–PAGE. Protein bands, visualized by colloidal Coomassie blue staining, were excised and digested with trypsin in the gel. The peptides produced were analyzed via liquid chromatography coupled with tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS). Mass spectrometric measurements were acquired on a Q Exactive Plus Orbitrap instrument (Thermo Fisher Scientific) equipped with a nanoelectrospray ionization source. Peptide separation was achieved on a C18 reversed-phase HPLC column (500 mm × 75 μm ID) with a linear gradient of acetonitrile in 0.1% formic acid ranging from 2.4% to 24% over 120 min at 300 nL/min. Precursor ion spectra (m/z 400–2000) were recorded in the Orbitrap at 70,000 resolution (at m/z 400) via internal calibration. The top 15 most intense precursor ions were sequentially selected for fragmentation through higher-energy collisional dissociation (HCD). The MS/MS data sets were processed and interpreted via the Sequest Sorcerer platform (Sagen-N Research, San Jose, CA, USA).

RNA Sequencing of Sheep Placenta-Derived EVs

Total RNA was isolated with TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) following the manufacturer’s protocol. One microliter of RNA eluate was used to quantify small RNA fractions with the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer and its Small RNA Assay (Agilent Technologies). Small RNA libraries were generated with the CleanTag Small RNA Library Preparation Kit (TriLink) according to the supplier’s instructions. The purified libraries were quantified via high-sensitivity DNA reagents and DNA chips (Agilent Technologies). Libraries were then pooled, and fragments ranging from 140–300 bp were excised on an 8% TBE gel (Invitrogen, Life Technologies). The excised libraries were requantified with Agilent High Sensitivity DNA 1000 ScreenTape and reagents, as well as the TailorMix HT1 qPCR assay (SeqMatic). Sequencing was performed on an Illumina NextSeq 500/550 platform via a high-output v2 kit (single-end SR75). Bioinformatic assessment of RNA composition and abundance was conducted via the exosome RNA analysis workflow provided by the Maverix Biomics platform (55).

HaCaT Cell Culture and Functional Assays

HaCaT keratinocytes were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% exosome-depleted fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, USA; cat no. A2720801) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. The cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator containing 5% CO2. Recombinant interleukin-17A (IL-17A; MedChemExpress, USA) was applied at a final concentration of 200 μg/L. UVB irradiation at 313 nm was optimized to a dose of 50 mJ/cm2/day. Cell proliferation was measured via the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; Bestbio, Shanghai, China). Briefly, HaCaT cells were seeded in 96-well plates at 5 × 103 cells per well and incubated overnight. The cells were then treated with 100 μg/mL placenta-derived EVs for 24 h before the addition of CCK-8 reagent, which was incubated in the dark for 1 h at 37 °C. The absorbance was recorded at 450 nm via a microplate reader (Infinite M200, Tecan, Austria). Cell cycle analysis was conducted via a Cell Cycle and Apoptosis Analysis Kit (C1052, Beyotime, China) on a BECKMAN COULTER CytoFLEX S flow cytometer. For the wound-healing (scratch) assay, HaCaT cells were seeded into 24-well plates until confluence, scratched with a sterile 200 μL pipet tip, rinsed with PBS, and incubated in DMEM containing either 20% FBS (positive control) or 100 μg/mL placenta-derived EVs. Migration into the wound area was monitored after 24 h.

Tube Formation Assay

Human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs; KeyGEN BioTECH) were maintained in F12K medium (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS. To assess angiogenesis, 50 μL of Matrigel (Corning, USA) was dispensed into each well of a 96-well plate and allowed to polymerize. HUVECs (10,000 per well) were seeded with or without 100 μg/mL EVs and incubated for 6 h. Tubular structures were observed under a microscope, and three random fields per well were imaged and quantified. Each experiment was independently repeated at least three times to ensure reproducibility.

Nanoparticle Tracking Analysis (NTA)

The particle size and concentration were determined via a NanoSight NS300 instrument (Malvern, UK) fitted with a 488 nm laser and an sCMOS camera. Measurements were acquired at 25 °C with the following settings: camera level 12, acquisition time 30 s, and detection threshold 7. Three independent recordings were captured, and the data were processed via NanoSight software (v2.3).

Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM)

To evaluate vesicle morphology, EVs were fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde and 5% bovine serum albumin, followed by postfixation in 2% osmium tetroxide. The samples were dehydrated through graded acetone–ethanol steps and embedded in epoxy resin (SPI Inc., Westchester, PA, USA). Ultrathin sections (80–90 nm) were stained with uranyl acetate (5%, 15 min) and lead citrate (0.1%, 5 min). Images were captured on a Hitachi 7500 transmission electron microscope.

Western Blotting

Proteins from EVs and cells were extracted via RIPA buffer (P0013B, Beyotime) supplemented with PMSF (ST506, Beyotime) and protease inhibitors (P1046, Beyotime). Equal protein amounts were resolved by SDS–PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore, USA). The membranes were blocked with 5% nonfat milk for 1 h and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against ALIX (Abcam, ab117600), CD9 (Proteintech, 20597-1-AP), TSG101 (Proteintech, 28283-1-AP), CD63 (Santa Cruz, sc-5275), GAPDH (Proteintech, 60004-1-Ig), S100A7, PCNA, Cyclin D1, STAT3, phospho-STAT3, and Collagen I (Proteintech). The antibody dilution was 1:1000. After being washed in TBST, the membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated goat antimouse (Santa Cruz, sc-2005) or goat antirabbit IgG (Santa Cruz, sc-2004; dilution 1:2000) for 1 h at room temperature. The protein bands were visualized via enhanced chemiluminescence reagents (Millipore, USA). The full uncropped Blots images of Western blotting were provided in Figure S1.

EV Uptake Assay

EVs were labeled with DiI (Beyotime, C1991S) for 6 h, reisolated by differential centrifugation, and coincubated with HaCaT cells for an additional 6 h. After nuclear counterstaining with DAPI, the uptake of labeled vesicles was visualized via confocal microscopy (Olympus FV3000).

RT–qPCR Analysis

RNA was extracted with TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA) and dissolved in DEPC-treated water. The concentration and purity were assessed on a OneDrop-2000 spectrophotometer (NanoDrop Technologies). Reverse transcription and quantitative PCR were carried out with HiScript III All-in-One RT SuperMix (Vazyme Biotech, R333-01) and Taq Pro Universal SYBR Master Mix (Vazyme Biotech, Q712-02). Assays were performed in 96-well plates.

In Vivo Skin Penetration Study

The dorsal skin of eight-week-old male C57BL/6J mice was shaved using an electric clipper. To prepare fluorescently labeled vesicles, spEVs were resuspended and incubated with the lipophilic dye DiI (Beyotime, C1991S) at room temperature for 30–40 min in the dark. The labeled suspension was then transferred into ultracentrifuge tubes and centrifuged at 120,000g at 4 °C for 70 min to remove excess dye. The pellet was resuspended in sterile PBS to obtain DiI-labeled spEVs. The labeled spEVs were topically applied to the exposed dorsal skin at a dose of 50 μg per mouse. Mice were maintained under normal conditions in the dark and sacrificed at 6, 12, and 24 h after administration to evaluate the time-dependent skin penetration of spEVs. Prior to tissue collection, any residual material on the skin surface was thoroughly removed by multiple washes with PBS. Skin samples were harvested from the treated areas, embedded in optimal cutting temperature (OCT) compound (Tissue-Tek, Sakura Finetek, USA), and stored at −80 °C. Cryosections (6 μm thick) were prepared using a cryostat microtome (CM1860, Leica, Germany). After equilibration to room temperature, sections were washed with PBS 2–3 times and incubated with Hoechst nuclear stain for 15 min in the dark, followed by additional PBS washes. Finally, sections were mounted with antifade mounting medium and imaged using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Olympus FV3000). Fluorescence intensity across different skin layers was quantitatively analyzed using ImageJ.

Animal Models of Skin Disorders

Mice were randomly assigned to experimental groups using a randomization procedure. All outcome assessments, including wound measurement and histological analysis, were performed in a blinded manner to minimize experimental bias. In all in vivo experiments, spEVs were administered at a dose of 50 μg per mouse per day, unless otherwise specified. Each experimental group included n = 3 independent biological replicates (individual mice).

For imiquimod (IMQ)-induced psoriasis-like model, eight-week-old male C57BL/6J mice (n = 3 per group) were shaved on the dorsal skin and randomly assigned to a normal control group (NC) and an IMQ-induced psoriasis model group (IMQ). To ensure objectivity, group allocation as well as subsequent severity scoring and histological analyses were performed in a blinded manner. Mice in the IMQ group received a daily topical application of 62.5 mg of 5% imiquimod cream (Med Shine) on the dorsal skin for 7 consecutive days to induce psoriasis-like dermatitis, while the NC group received no treatment. Disease severity was evaluated using the Psoriasis Area and Severity Index (PASI), and a total score >8 was considered successful model establishment. Successfully modeled mice were then randomly divided into a treatment group (spEV IMQ) and a vehicle control group (spss IMQ). The treatment group received daily topical administration of 50 μg spEVs per mouse, whereas the control group received an equal volume of PBS. PASI scores were recorded before treatment (day 0) and on days 3 and 7 post-treatment. After each administration, the treated skin area was covered with sterile Vaseline gauze to prevent licking. After the final assessment, mice were sacrificed, and dorsal skin tissues were collected for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and Masson’s trichrome staining for histopathological analysis.

For UVB-induced skin injury model, male C57BL/6J mice (n = 3 per group) were randomly divided into a normal control group (NC) and a UVB injury group. The NC group received no treatment, while the UVB group was exposed to ultraviolet radiation (wavelength: 313 nm; dose: 180 mJ/cm2; light source: YiXian PL-S 9W/2P) once daily for 5 consecutive days to establish an acute photodamage model. During irradiation, nonexposed areas were covered with aluminum foil for protection. After injury induction, the UVB group was further randomly assigned to a treatment group (spEV-UVB) and a vehicle control group (spss-UVB). The treatment group received daily topical application of 50 μg spEVs to the irradiated area, while the control group received an equal volume of vehicle. The treated area was covered with sterile Vaseline gauze after each administration. On days 0, 4, 8, and 12 postinjury, images of the dorsal skin were analyzed using ImageJ to quantify the percentage of the lesion area (including erythema and scaling) relative to the total dorsal skin area. At the end of the experiment, skin samples were collected for histological examination.

For full-thickness skin defect model, eight-week-old male diabetic db/db mice (n = 3 per group) were anesthetized, and a circular full-thickness skin wound (5 mm in diameter, extending to the panniculus carnosus) was created on the dorsal surface using a biopsy punch (TopBioSolutions). Mice were randomly assigned to three groups: diabetic control (NC-DB), spEV-treated (spEV-FSDM), and vehicle control (spss-FSDM). Blinding was applied to personnel performing wound evaluation. The treatment group received daily topical administration of 50 μg spEVs per mouse (evenly distributed between two wounds), while the control group received an equal volume of vehicle. The applied dose corresponded to approximately 2.5 μg/mm2 wound area. After each administration, wounds were covered with sterile Vaseline gauze and breathable adhesive tape. Digital images of the wounds were captured daily, and wound diameters were measured using ImageJ to calculate wound closure rates. All mice were sacrificed on day 14, and wound tissues along with surrounding skin were harvested for H&E and Masson’s trichrome staining to evaluate re-epithelialization, granulation tissue formation, and collagen deposition.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). All experiments were conducted using independent biological replicates, unless otherwise specified. The sample size (n = 3) was selected based on commonly accepted standards for exploratory preclinical studies while minimizing animal use in accordance with ethical guidelines. Although no formal power calculation was performed prior to the study, the observed effect sizes were consistent across independent experiments. Future studies will include larger sample sizes and formal power analyses to further validate these findings. Prior to statistical testing, data distribution and homogeneity of variance were assessed to ensure the suitability of parametric tests. Student’s t-test or one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple-comparison test was applied when assumptions were met. Exact p-values were reported where applicable, and a p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Confidence intervals (CIs) were considered during data interpretation to provide an estimate of variability and effect size.

Results

Characterization of EVs Derived from the Sheep Placenta

EVs were isolated from sheep placenta via differential ultracentrifugation (Figure A) and characterized via transmission electron microscopy (TEM), nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA), and Western blotting. TEM revealed a typical bilayer membrane structure with an average diameter of approximately 150 nm (Figure B,C). Western blotting confirmed the expression of canonical exosomal markers including CD9, ALIX, TSG101, and CD63, but lacked the endoplasmic reticulum-associated protein Calnexin, GRP94, GM130 and Laminb1 (Figure D).

Analysis of RNA and Protein Cargo in Sheep Placenta-Derived EVs

Proteomic and high-throughput RNA sequencing were performed to analyze the RNA and protein contents of placenta-derived EVs. The majority of EV-associated RNAs were ∼1000 nt in length (Figure A and Table S1) and consisted primarily of protein-coding mRNAs, ncRNAs, and lncRNAs, which together accounted for over 90% of the total RNA population (Figure B). The 20 most abundant transcripts included TMSB10, IGF2, PEG3, RPL4, PEG10, ACTG1, LOC101112509 (placenta-expressed transcript 1 protein), RRBP1, RPS24, EIF4G2, IQGAP1, LOC121818498 (collagen alpha-1­(I) chain-like), EIF3A, LOC121818524 (collagen alpha-1­(I) chain-like), TPT1, HSP90B1, LOC132659099 (lncRNA), LOC132659100 (lncRNA), LOC114117038 (snRNA; U1 spliceosomal RNA), and LOC121818276 (transcribed pseudogene; cytochrome c oxidase subunit 3-like) (Figure C). Functional annotation via human GO and KEGG databases suggested that these RNAs were involved primarily in pathways related to regeneration and inflammation suppression, including adherens junction, tight junction, Hippo signaling, Rap1 signaling, ErbB signaling, EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitor resistance, FoxO signaling, and HIF-1 signaling (Figure D,E). Proteomic analysis revealed a total of 7085 proteins within placenta-derived EVs (Table S2). Among them, the most abundant were HAS1, SRSF1, VIM, KRT25, HBBK, ALB, ACTR2, BCAM, ZFP36, and MIF. Like those associated with the RNA cargo, the results of the GO and KEGG enrichment analyses revealed that these proteins were enriched in signaling pathways associated with wound healing, tissue regeneration, and anti-inflammatory responses, including HIF-1 signaling, VEGF signaling, cell cycle regulation, and TGF-β activation (Figure G,H).

Effects of Sheep Placenta-Derived EVs on Cell Proliferation, Migration, and Angiogenesis

SpEVs contain abundant RNAs and proteins with regenerative and anti-inflammatory properties, including IGF2, PEG3, HAS1, SRSF1, and VIM. We hypothesized that spEVs may suppress skin inflammation, accelerate wound healing, and promote regeneration. To assess uptake, DiI-labeled spEVs were incubated with HaCaT keratinocytes for 6 h. Confocal microscopy revealed strong cytoplasmic localization of the EVs, indicating efficient internalization (Figure A). Functionally, treatment with spEVs (10 μg/mL) significantly enhanced HaCaT proliferation compared with that of the vehicle controls, as determined by a CCK-8 assay (Figure B). Scratch assays demonstrated that spEVs markedly promoted HaCaT migration after 16 h (Figure C). Flow cytometry analysis revealed that spEV treatment increased the proportion of cells in S phase, suggesting accelerated cell cycle progression (Figure D). Furthermore, spEVs promoted angiogenesis in vitro, as evidenced by enhanced tubular network formation in HUVECs cultured on Geltrex compared with control HUVECs (Figure E).

Anti-Inflammatory and Protective Effects of spEVs on Keratinocytes

To evaluate the anti-inflammatory potential of spEVs, we established in vitro models of skin inflammation and damage. HaCaT cells were stimulated with IL-17a (200 μg/L) for 24 h to induce an inflammatory phenotype, followed by coculture with spEVs. Western blotting revealed that, compared with IL-17a alone, spEVs reduced STAT3 and phosphorylated STAT3 expression (Figure A). Additionally, spEVs significantly decreased S100A protein and mRNA levels in IL-17a-stimulated HaCaT cells (Figure A,B). To model UVB-induced skin damage, HaCaT cells were irradiated with 313 nm UVB at 50 mJ/cm2 daily for 3 days. This treatment markedly reduced PCNA and Cyclin D1 expression. Coculture with spEVs restored both proteins to near-normal levels, indicating protective effects against UVB-induced cellular senescence (Figure C).

Therapeutic Effects of spEVs in Mouse Models of Inflammatory Skin Injury

A psoriasis-like mouse model was established via daily topical application of 5% imiquimod cream to the shaved backs of C57BL/6J mice (Figure A). Mice with a PASI score ≥8 were randomized into spEV-treated (spEV-IMQ) and vehicle-treated (spss-IMQ) groups, with untreated mice serving as normal controls (NCs). Compared with vehicle treatment, spEV treatment significantly reduced the PASI score (Figure B). Histological analysis revealed that spEVs markedly decreased epidermal thickness and inflammatory cell infiltration, restoring skin architecture to near-normal levels (Figure C). In the psoriasis model, spEVs reduced pathological hyperproliferation, as evidenced by decreased epidermal thickness and normalization of proliferation markers. Western blotting further confirmed that spEVs suppressed STAT3/P-STAT3 and S100A7 expression in psoriatic lesions (Figure D). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that spEVs alleviate inflammation and ameliorate psoriatic pathology.

spEVs Promote Repair in a UVB-Induced Skin Injury Model

A UVB-induced skin damage model was established in C57BL/6J mice via 313 nm UVB irradiation (Figure A). Compared with vehicle treatment, spEV administration significantly reduced the relative wound area and accelerated healing (Figure B). Histological examination revealed severe dermal and epidermal injury in vehicle-treated mice, whereas spEV treatment restored the skin structure to near-normal levels (Figure C). Western blotting confirmed that spEVs rescued UVB-suppressed expression of PCNA and Cyclin D1 (Figure D), further supporting their regenerative effect on skin injury.

spEVs Enhance Wound Healing in Diabetic Mice

To evaluate the regenerative potential of spEVs under diabetic conditions, full-thickness skin defects were induced in DB/DB mice via a 5 mm biopsy punch (Figure A). The mice were randomized into spEV-treated (spEV–FSDM) and vehicle-treated (spss–FSDM) groups, with untreated mice serving as controls. Daily monitoring revealed that, compared with vehicle, spEVs markedly accelerated wound closure (Figure B). Histological analysis revealed that spEVs restored the collagen content to levels comparable to those of healthy controls (Figure C). Moreover, spEV treatment significantly increased Cyclin D1 and type I collagen expression (Figure D). These results indicate that spEVs promote skin regeneration even under diabetic pathological conditions.

Discussion

Skin disorders, including chronic wounds, burns, diabetic ulcers, and inflammatory dermatoses, are highly prevalent worldwide and impose substantial public health and socioeconomic burdens. According to the Global Burden of Disease studies, skin and subcutaneous diseases rank among the leading causes of nonfatal morbidity, significantly reducing quality of life and functional capacity. Patients face complications such as pain, recurrent infection, fibrosis, scarring, and long-term disability. Conventional therapeutic strategies range from topical medications (anti-inflammatory drugs, antimicrobials, and growth factors) to advanced dressings, surgical interventions, and, more recently, biologics, cell-based therapies, and tissue-engineered scaffolds. Although effective in selected patients, these interventions often fail in cases of extensive skin loss or chronic wounds, particularly in diabetic or ischemic settings. Infections remain a persistent challenge, but surgical grafts are hindered by donor-site morbidity and immune rejection. High costs, limited donor availability, batch heterogeneity, and biosafety risks further constrain advanced therapies based on human placenta, cord blood, or stem cell–derived exosomes. ,

Exosomes and other EVs have emerged as acellular mediators of intercellular communication and are capable of transferring RNAs, proteins, and lipids to recipient cells. In skin repair, mesenchymal stem cell–derived exosomes (MSC-exos) accelerate wound closure by promoting keratinocyte and fibroblast proliferation, enhancing angiogenesis, and modulating immune responses. Placental EVs show similar potential, underscoring the regenerative potential of vesicle-based therapies. − Compared with intact cell therapies, exosomes offer reduced immunogenicity, easier handling, and lower tumorigenic risk. However, clinical translation remains hindered by limited supply, high manufacturing costs, interbatch variability, and biosafety concerns. These challenges highlight the need for alternative EV sources with scalability and reduced ethical constraints.

In agricultural practice, sheep placenta is typically discarded as waste. Repurposing this material as a source of therapeutic EVs provides a sustainable, cost-effective alternative with fewer ethical barriers. While crude sheep placenta extracts have long been used in cosmetics and folk medicine for skin rejuvenation, they suffer from poor stability and imprecise targeting. , In contrast, spEVs protect their molecular cargo within lipid bilayers, enabling efficient uptake by recipient skin cells and improved bioactivity. In this study, we isolated and characterized spEVs and demonstrated their ability to promote keratinocyte proliferation and migration, protect against UVB-induced injury, stimulate angiogenesis, and accelerate wound closure in murine models of psoriasis-like inflammation, photoaging, and diabetic wounds. These findings suggest that spEVs can act through multiple mechanisms relevant to tissue repair. RNA sequencing and proteomic profiling revealed that spEVs contain a repertoire of functional molecules, including TMSB10, IGF2, PEG3, RPL4, PEG10, ACTG1, HAS1, VIM, and MIF. IGF2 is a potent mitogen that promotes proliferation, survival, and extracellular matrix deposition while suppressing proinflammatory signaling. TMSB10 regulates actin cytoskeleton dynamics and immune cell migration, linking inflammation to tissue remodeling. PEG3 influences apoptosis and stress responses, potentially modulating survival during injury repair. HAS1 and VIM contribute to extracellular matrix remodeling and cell motility, whereas MIF serves as a pleiotropic cytokine at the intersection of inflammation and angiogenesis. These components may act synergistically to regulate macrophage polarization, suppress STAT3/S100A7-driven inflammatory cascades, stimulate epithelial proliferation via the PI3K/AKT and ERK pathways, and promote angiogenesis through VEGF/HIF-1 signaling.

While our KEGG and GO enrichment analyses revealed that spEV cargos are associated with signaling pathways such as ErbB, EGFR, PI3K–Akt, Wnt, mTOR, and VEGF, it is important to note that these pathways are not only central to tissue regeneration and wound healing but are also classically implicated in oncogenic processes. This raises a potential concern regarding unintended activation of tumor-promoting signaling. However, the biological effects of these pathways are highly context-dependent. In the setting of tissue injury, their activation is typically transient and tightly regulated, contributing to controlled cell proliferation, angiogenesis, and repair rather than malignant transformation. Consistent with this, in our in vivo modelsincluding psoriasis-like inflammation, UVB-induced injury, and diabetic woundswe did not observe abnormal tissue overgrowth, dysplasia, or tumor formation following spEV treatment. We acknowledge that the relatively small sample size represents a limitation of this study, and larger-scale studies are required to confirm these findings.

Moreover, accumulating evidence from extracellular vesicle–based therapies, particularly those derived from mesenchymal stem cells or placental tissues, indicates that activation of these pathways is generally associated with regenerative outcomes rather than sustained oncogenic signaling. Although our results did not reveal any signs of abnormal tissue growth or tumor formation in the current experimental time frame, comprehensive safety evaluation remains essential prior to clinical translation. Future studies should include long-term tumorigenicity assessments, in which animals are monitored over extended periods following repeated administration of spEVs to detect any potential neoplastic changes. In addition, given the xenogeneic origin of spEVs, immunogenicity should be systematically evaluated, including analysis of inflammatory cytokine profiles, immune cell activation, and histopathological examination of major organs. Furthermore, biodistribution and clearance studies using labeled EVs will be critical to determine their in vivo fate, tissue accumulation, and potential off-target effects. Finally, dose-escalation and toxicity studies are necessary to define the therapeutic window and ensure safety at clinically relevant doses. These investigations will be essential for advancing spEVs toward safe and effective clinical application.

A central question is whether EVs derived from sheep can exert conserved bioactivity in human tissues. Given the high sequence conservation of core signaling proteins and the successful uptake of spEVs by human keratinocytes in vitro, cross-species functionality appears plausible. Similar xenogeneic EV applications have been reported in rodent models, lending further support. Nonetheless, rigorous safety testing, biodistribution studies, and immunogenicity assessments are needed before clinical translation. Moving forward, the optimization of delivery systems will be critical. Biomaterial platforms such as hydrogels, microneedle patches, and electrospun scaffolds may enhance the retention and controlled release of spEVs. Engineering approaches to enrich specific cargos or functionalize vesicle surfaces could further improve therapeutic precision. Additionally, large-animal studies and GMP-compliant scale-up pipelines are necessary to establish safety, efficacy, and regulatory feasibility.

Conclusion

This study establishes sheep placental extracellular vesicles (spEVs) as a promising and sustainable therapeutic platform for inflammatory and degenerative skin disorders. By valorizing agricultural byproducts into high-value biomedical resources, spEVs offer a cost-effective, scalable, and ethically advantageous alternative for exosome-based therapies. Functionally, spEVs demonstrated consistent therapeutic efficacy across multiple experimental models, including psoriasis-like inflammation, UVB-induced skin injury, and diabetic wound healing, highlighting their broad regenerative and immunomodulatory potential. Despite these encouraging findings, the current evidence is primarily derived from in vitro systems and murine models, which may not fully recapitulate human skin physiology and immune complexity. Species-specific differences could influence therapeutic responses and limit direct clinical translation. Therefore, future investigations should focus on large-animal studies, comprehensive pharmacokinetic and biodistribution profiling, and rigorously designed clinical trials to validate safety, efficacy, and translational feasibility. Collectively, our work provides a conceptual and practical framework for transforming biological waste into clinically relevant nanotherapeutics, paving the way for next-generation regenerative strategies in dermatology.
