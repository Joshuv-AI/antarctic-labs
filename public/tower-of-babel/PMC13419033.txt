Enhancing patient safety: factors influencing critical care nurses’ intravenous sedation practices in mechanically ventilated patients
Authors: Mohamed Elsayed Ahmed Allawy, Heba AlKotb Mohamed, Rania Abdel Khalik Gouda, Ohood Ali Alkaabi, Hadeer Hussien Soliman Khalil, Mona Hassan Ibrahim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatient safety is a major priority in intensive care units (ICUs), particularly among mechanically ventilated patients receiving intravenous sedation. Although sedative agents are essential for maintaining comfort and ventilator synchrony, inappropriate sedation practices may contribute to adverse clinical outcomes. Understanding the factors influencing nurses’ sedation practices is therefore essential to promote safe and effective patient care. This study aimed to explore the factors influencing critical care nurses’ intravenous sedation practices and examine their relationship with patient safety outcomes.MethodsA cross-sectional descriptive exploratory study was conducted among 105 nurses working in critical care units. Participants were recruited using a consecutive sampling technique and were directly involved in caring for mechanically ventilated patients receiving intravenous sedation. Data were collected using the Nurses’ Profile Questionnaire and the Nurse Sedation Practices Scale (NSPS). Descriptive statistics and multiple regression analysis were performed to identify predictors of nurses’ sedation practices. Statistical significance was considered at p < 0.05.ResultsSedation orders and goals achieved the highest mean score (81.5%), followed by sedation practices (74.2%) and attitudes toward sedation (72.4%). Subjective norms showed moderate scores (67.6%), while perceived behavioral control demonstrated lower scores (60.1%). Intention to sedate recorded the lowest score (41.1%), suggesting a preference for individualized sedation approaches. Multiple regression analysis identified subjective norms, perceived behavioral control, and intention to sedate as significant predictors of nurses’ sedation practices. Subjective norms were the strongest predictor (B = 0.328, p < 0.001), followed by perceived behavioral control (B = 0.210, p = 0.002). Intention to sedate showed a significant negative association with sedation practices (B = −0.139, p = 0.010).ConclusionImproving sedation practices among critical care nurses requires strengthening perceived behavioral control and promoting patient-centered sedation approaches to enhance patient safety outcomes in mechanically ventilated patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12912-026-05093-x.Keywords: Critical care nursing, Intensive care units, Intravenous sedation, Mechanical ventilation, Patient safety, Sedation practices

Introduction

Patient safety is an essential concern in intensive care units (ICUs), especially for those patients undergoing mechanical ventilation who need constant observation and sophisticated treatment. Intravenous sedation is a vital aspect of critical care management as it helps to maintain comfort in patients, reduce anxiety, help to synchrony the patient with the ventilator and minimise physiologic stress responses. But, the inappropriate sedation management may have an impact on patient safety and result in severe complications such as delirium, accidental extubation, prolonged mechanical ventilation, increased length of stay in the intensive care unit (ICU), and increased morbidity and mortality [1, 2].

While mechanical ventilation can be life-saving, it often requires the use of sedative drugs for patient tolerability and safety [3]. Over or undersedation have been linked to poor clinical outcomes. Too much sedation can make it harder to wean from the vent, keep patients in the ICU, and lead to complications, while not enough can lead to agitation, a patient’s self-extubation, and physiological instability [4, 5]. Therefore, it is crucial to maintain optimal levels of sedation to create optimal outcomes for patients and safe critical care practice.

IV sedation management is a critical role for the critical care nurse since these nurses will be directly involved in the assessment, administration, monitoring, and titration of sedative medications throughout their shift. Advanced clinical judgment and decision-making are essential for ensuring patient safety; their responsibilities involve this [6]. It has been shown in the past that nurses’ knowledge, clinical competence, experience and decision making can have a significant effect on sedation management. However, there are still a number of reported areas for improvement in relation to knowledge and practice of sedation related nursing care [7].

Knowledge, competence, and experience, along with patient agitation, pain, level of illness, and responsiveness to treatment, and organization effects including staffing, workload, training, protocol availability, and managerial support can all influence sedation practices [8]. When caring for patients with sedation, nurses also take into account the recommendations supported by evidence and their clinical judgment, as well as their own experiences [9].

Although evidence-based guidelines and standardized sedation-assessment tools are available, their consistent application in clinical practice remains challenging. Variations in sedation practices have been attributed to inadequate training, inconsistent protocol use, staffing shortages, heavy workload, and limited organizational support [10]. Evidence from Egypt indicates deficiencies in critical care nurses’ knowledge and practices related to intravenous sedation [6]. Similarly, studies conducted in Saudi Arabia have identified gaps in nurses’ sedation knowledge, limited use of standardized assessment scales, and insufficient availability or implementation of written protocols. These studies emphasized the importance of continuing education, performance feedback, multidisciplinary teamwork, and organizational support in improving sedation practices [11, 12]. However, most previous studies have examined individual aspects of sedation management, such as nurses’ knowledge, clinical competence, protocol adherence, or selected patient outcomes, rather than comprehensively evaluating the interacting factors that shape nurses’ sedation practices.

Consequently, there is not much evidence to be found about the association between nurse related factors, patient related factors and organization related factors and the use of intravenous sedation by critical care nurses in Egyptian ICUs. The findings of the previous studies are also less easy to incorporate due to the variation of determinants and clinical contexts across the studies investigated. Although nurses’ knowledge and experience are important factors in their sedation decisions, the clinical state of the patient and the environment in which care is delivered are also important factors that should be assessed. Hence, the purpose of the present study was to assess the nurse related, patient related, and organization related factors that were related to the use of intravenous sedation for mechanically ventilated patients by critical care nurses. The presence of these factors could lead to specific educational and organizational interventions, reinforce evidence-based sedation practices, and enhance patient safety in Egyptian ICUs.

Justification of the problem

Previous research on IV sedation has tended to focus on specific patient outcomes, nurses’ knowledge and competence, protocol compliance, or other individual elements of IV sedation. Little is known about the interaction of these three types of factors with relation to nurses’ sedation practices, therefore. This is especially significant in Egypt, as the existing evidence indicates that there is a lack of knowledge and practice among critical care nurses regarding IV sedation [6]. The clinical and organizational factors that underlie these deficiencies, however, have not been studied in detail.

This is due to variations in the levels of staffing, educational preparation and clinical autonomy of nurses in the different healthcare systems, workload, availability of sedation protocols, continuing education, managerial support and institutional resources in the Egyptian ICUs. Accordingly, the present study aims to conduct a comprehensive and context-specific evaluation of the nurse-, patient-, and organization-related factors related to critical care nurses’ sedation of patients who are mechanically ventilated with intravenous sedation. The study will not only examine these determinants together, but also examine them as a whole, which is expected to provide new evidence to support targeted education, competency development, protocol implementation, resource allocation, and organizational policy development to enhance sedation practices, patient safety, and quality of critical care.

Aim of the study

This study aimed to identify the factors influencing critical care nurses’ intravenous sedation practices in mechanically ventilated patients and to examine the relationships among behavioral, organizational, and clinical determinants shaping these practices.

Methods

Study design and sampling strategy

The cross-sectional descriptive exploratory design was utilized to examine the factors that impact the practices of intravenous sedation use among critical care nurses who provide care to mechanically ventilated patients. This design was suitable because it enabled the evaluation of various individual and organizational factors and how these factors relate to sedation practices at a given time [13]. A consecutive sampling method was used to select eligible nurses from the Intensive Care Unit (ICU), and Cardiac Care Unit (CCU) at Suez Canal University Hospital.

To maximize representativeness and minimize selection bias, all eligible nurses working across morning, evening, and night shifts during the data-collection period were invited to participate using the same eligibility criteria and recruitment procedures. The sample size was calculated using the formula Dawson & Trapp [14]: n= ((Zα/2)2 ×P(1−P)) /d2 where Zα/2 = 1.96, p = 0.50, and d = 0.10. A 10% margin of error was selected as a pragmatic level of precision because of the exploratory nature of the study, the finite accessible population, and the available recruitment resources.With no local data, the most conservative estimate was obtained using p = 0.50. The minimum sample size was 96 nurses, and 10% was added to cover potential non-response to get to a total of 105 nurses. It was also found that the sample size was adequate for multiple regression as proposed by Li & Liu [15] which suggested that at least 5–10 participants per study variable are needed. The study participants were nurses from the ICU and CCU. The sampling frame comprised 130 eligible nurses assigned exclusively to the ICU and CCU across all rotating shifts. Of these, 105 participated, yielding a response rate of 80.8%. Although this approach increased coverage of the accessible nursing population, consecutive sampling and voluntary participation may have introduced selection bias. Therefore, the findings may be generalized primarily to critical care nurses working in similar Egyptian hospital settings. The study participants were identified by staffing records and recruited on the job, after the nurse was provided with information about the study and gave written informed consent. All participants had to be registered nurses, who had at least six months of experience working in a critical care environment with patients who required mechanical ventilation, and had direct experience with administering intravenous sedation. The six-month threshold was selected to ensure that participants had sufficient exposure to critical care routines, mechanically ventilated patients, intravenous sedation administration, patient monitoring, and sedation-related clinical decision-making. Nurses were excluded from the study if they held an administrative role or were a trainee, had less than 6 months of experience or refused participation.

Study setting

The study was conducted at Suez Canal University Hospital, a major tertiary teaching and referral hospital located in Ismailia, Egypt. The hospital was selected because it is a major tertiary teaching and referral center that routinely manages critically ill mechanically ventilated patients and reflects critical care practice within the region. The study was carried out within its critical care services, including the Cardiac Care Unit (CCU) and the Intensive Care Unit (ICU), which manage a high volume of critically ill mechanically ventilated patients requiring intravenous sedation. The CCU, situated on the first floor, consists of 12 beds, whereas the ICU, located on the second floor, contains 20 beds. The sampling frame comprised 130 eligible nurses assigned exclusively to the ICU and CCU across all rotating shifts. The usual nurse-to-patient ratio was 1:2 in the ICU and 1:3 in the CCU. No additional units or hospitals contributed participants. Both units provide continuous 24-hour care for critically ill patients. Data collection was carried out over a six-month period from January 1, 2025, to June 30, 2025.

Conceptual framework

The study variables were selected and organized on the basis of the theory of planned behavior. Attitudes reflected nurses’ beliefs and evaluations regarding intravenous sedation. Professional expectations, sedation orders and goals, and clinical communication are examples of subjective norms. Perceived behavioral control was perceived ability of the nurses to deliver safe sedation in spite of clinical and organizational limitations. Intention towards routine sedation was defined as willingness to sedate all mechanically ventilated patients routinely and sedation practices were a behavioral outcome. The study assumed that these TPB constructs would be significantly related to the sedation practices. The relationships between the constructs were assessed using correlation analysis, and the independent contribution of the constructs to sedation practices after controlling for demographic and occupational factors, using multiple regression. Data collection tools

Two tools were used for data collection in this study.

Tool I: Nurses’ profile questionnaire

A structured questionnaire was developed by the researchers after reviewing relevant literature related to critical care nursing and sedation management. The questionnaire was reviewed by a panel of five experts in medical-surgical and critical care nursing to assess content validity and clarity prior to data collection.

Tool II: Nurse sedation practices scale (NSPS)

The NSPS was not modified, but the original English version developed by Guttormson et al. [16] was used. English was the language used in the study setting as it is the official language of the nursing profession, thus no translation was needed. The NSPS subscales aligned with the study goals, evaluating factors related to nurses’ intravenous sedation practices at the individual, social, organizational, and practice levels. For descriptive purposes, mean scores < 3 were considered low, scores of 3–<4 moderate, and scores ≥ 4 high. Content validity of the tool was determined by five expert nurses in medical-surgical and critical care nursing. The items were judged by the experts on their relevance, clarity and comprehensiveness. The overall Content Validity Index (CVI) was 0.89, which is satisfactory. Cronbach alpha coefficient was used to determine the internal consistency reliability. The current study revealed good reliability for the NSPS with Cronbach alpha coefficients of 0.79 to 0.87 for the subscales. The Cronbach’s alpha for the total scale was satisfactory (0.85). Because the intention to sedate variable was measured with a single item, as originally designed in the NSPS, internal consistency reliability could not be applied to this variable.

Data collection

A social desirability bias was minimized by informing participants that their answers would be anonymous, confidential, and only for research purposes. Questionnaires were filled out in a confidential manner and were sent back in an envelope. The data collection period was from 1/01/2025 to 30/06/2025. All nurses during the day and night shifts were approached and were eligible. Of the 130 eligible nurses approached, 105 consented to participate and completed the questionnaires, yielding an overall response rate of 80.8%. Questionnaires with high rates of missing information (more than 10%) were not analyzed, and minor missing responses were dealt with in line with the scoring instructions of the specific instruments used.

Pilot study

A preliminary study was made with 10 nurses, constituting about 10% of the total number of nurses to be studied. The size was deemed suitable for evaluation of questionnaire clarity, understanding, completion time and administration but not for statistical estimation or reliability testing [17]. The pilot participants were part of the target population but not included in the main study. Minor wording clarifications were provided in light of their feedback, and no changes were needed to the recruitment process or to the data collection process.

Ethical considerations

Identifiers were used instead of participant names to assure confidentiality when using questionnaires. Completed questionnaires were kept in a locked cabinet, and electronic data files were kept on a password-protected computer which was accessible only to the research team. No physical, psychological or professional risks were anticipated from the study. There were no direct benefits, but the results could help enhance sedation management practices, nursing education and patient safety in critical care.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 26. Prior to analysis, the collected data were checked for completeness, coded, and entered into the statistical software according to the study objectives. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize demographic characteristics and study variables. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages, whereas continuous variables and domain scores were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Composite percentage scores were calculated to determine the relative levels of sedation-related domains. Prior to inferential analyses, assumptions of normality, linearity, and homoscedasticity were assessed and found to be satisfactory. Therefore, parametric statistical tests were considered appropriate. Pearson’s correlation coefficient was used to examine the relationships among attitudes toward sedation, subjective norms, perceived behavioral control, sedation orders and goals, intention to sedate, and sedation practices. Multiple linear regression analysis was performed to identify significant predictors of nurses’ sedation practices. Model fitness was evaluated using the coefficient of determination (R2), adjusted R2, predicted R2, F-statistic, p-value, and standard error of the estimate. Multicollinearity among independent variables was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF), with acceptable values indicating the absence of significant collinearity. Regression coefficients were reported with their corresponding 95% confidence intervals. Statistical significance was established at p < 0.05. In addition, graphical visualizations, including radar charts and ellipse correlation matrix plots, were generated using Python software to facilitate visual interpretation of the relationships among study variables.

Results

Sample characteristics

the findings show that nearly one-third of the studied nurses were aged 25 to less than 28 years (31.4%), followed by those aged 28 to less than 31 years (27.6%) and 22 to less than 25 years (26.7%), with a mean age of 26.09 ± 3.29 years. Regarding education, more than half of the nurses had technical nursing education (53.3%), while 30.2% had a bachelor’s degree. Most nurses were working in the ICU (80.0%), compared with 20.0% in the CCU. Concerning critical care unit experience, 41.0% had less than three years of experience, followed by 31.4% who had 3 to less than 6 years. Finally, the majority of nurses had not attended training courses (84.8%), while only 15.2% had attended courses.

Table 1 presents the descriptive statistics of factors influencing sedation practices among critical care nurses. Among the assessed domains, sedation orders and goals demonstrated the highest mean score (4.07 ± 0.77) and composite percentage (81.5%), indicating strong alignment with clinical guidance and physician–nurse communication. This was followed by sedation practices (74.2%) and attitudes toward sedation (72.4%), reflecting generally positive clinical awareness and beliefs. Subjective norms showed a moderate level (67.6%), suggesting a notable influence of social and professional environment on practice. In contrast, perceived behavioral control scored relatively lower (60.1%), indicating potential constraints related to workload or organizational factors. Notably, intention to sedate had the lowest score (41.1%), highlighting a reduced tendency toward routine sedation and suggesting a preference for individualized patient management. Overall, the findings indicate that clinical and organizational factors outweigh individual intention in shaping sedation practices.

Figure 3 reveals important interrelationships among the factors influencing nurses’ administration of sedative medications. Positive and significant correlations were observed between attitudes and sedation orders/goals (r = 0.453, p < 0.001), attitudes and sedation practices (r = 0.301, p = 0.002), and between subjective norms and sedation practices (r = 0.499, p < 0.001), indicating that favorable attitudes and normative influences are closely linked to better sedation practices. Similarly, sedation orders and goals were positively correlated with both sedation practices (r = 0.447, p < 0.001) and attitudes (r = 0.453, p < 0.001), underscoring the role of clear orders and collaborative goal-setting in shaping practice. In contrast, intention to sedate all mechanically ventilated patients was negatively correlated with attitudes (r = −0.304, p = 0.002), subjective norms (r = −0.245, p = 0.012), sedation orders/goals (r = −0.381, p < 0.001), and sedation practices (r = −0.411, p < 0.001), suggesting that greater professional judgment, communication, and practice awareness reduce reliance on universal sedation. Perceived behavioral control showed no significant associations with most domains, reflecting its limited influence in this context. Collectively, the findings highlight that nurses’ practices are strongly shaped by attitudes, peer influence, and collaborative sedation orders, while intention for routine sedation tends to decrease as professional factors strengthen.

Discussion

This study extends current knowledge regarding intravenous sedation management by demonstrating that sedation practices among critical care nurses are influenced more strongly by subjective norms and perceived behavioral control than by individual intention alone. While previous studies have identified sedation management as a multifactorial process, the present findings provide context-specific evidence from Egyptian critical care settings and support the application of the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) in explaining sedation-related decision-making. The results suggest that organizational culture, teamwork, and workplace conditions play a central role in shaping nurses’ sedation practices beyond the influence of clinical protocols alone. Unlike previous studies conducted primarily in Western healthcare systems, the present study provides evidence from an Egyptian university hospital and demonstrates that organizational and social influences may play a particularly important role in shaping sedation practices within resource-constrained critical care environments.

Characteristics of the study population show that many of the nurses were just beginning their careers and had very little formal training in sedation management. This may shed some light on the reliance on physician-directed sedation orders and clinical objectives structure reported in the study. The practice of effective communication and targeted strategies with regards to sedation appears to improve practice. This result aligns with Stollings et al. [18] who spoke about the importance of collaboration and shared decision-making in sedation management. The high percentage of newly licensed nurses and the lack of formal training in sedation may have contributed to greater dependence on orders from physicians and team communication in this context. It is important to note that collaborative influences were strongly observed in this Egyptian ICU context, which may be attributed to the variations in training opportunities, nursing autonomy, staffing and protocol availability.

Subjective norms emerged as the strongest predictor of sedation practices, highlighting the influence of professional expectations, peer behavior, and ICU culture. This finding is consistent with evidence that critical care practice is shaped by team structure and organizational culture [19, 20]. In the present setting, limited formal training and reliance on multidisciplinary decisions may have strengthened the effect of professional norms. In healthcare systems with greater nursing autonomy and established nurse-led sedation protocols, individual clinical judgment may play a larger role.

Perceived behavioral control was the other predictor of sedation practices, reflecting nurses’ confidence and competence in handling sedation in their work place. This result could be attributed to workload, staffing patterns, resource availability, training and organizational support. The nurse/patient ratio of 1:2 in the ICU and 1:3 in the CCU may impact staff’s ability to perform ongoing assessment and titration of sedation. As noted by Kalliopi et al. [21], structural and environmental barriers can impact the ability of nurses to have control over care, but this impact may be more significant in resource-limited settings where there is less staffing and less continuing education.

These findings may also reflect broader healthcare system challenges, including workforce shortages, increasing patient acuity, limited continuing education opportunities, and constraints in ICU resource allocation. Consequently, organizational leadership and policy-level support are essential for creating practice environments that facilitate evidence-based sedation management.

The decreased reliance on routine sedation practices noticed in this study may mark a shift toward a more individualized and patient-centered approach. Evidence-based practice guidelines recommend the use of lighter sedation, as well as sedation practices that target analgesia and avoid deep sedation (to avoid potential complications like delirium and extended mechanical ventilation) [22–24]. Therefore, the observed low intention to sedate may reflect a greater emphasis on individualized sedation management rather than routine sedation administration. It should be noted that the intention item within the NSPS reflects intention toward routine sedation practices; consequently, lower intention scores may not necessarily indicate poorer practice but may instead reflect a preference for patient-centered and evidence-based sedation management. However, alternative explanations, including uncertainty in sedation decision-making, limited autonomy, or variability in training and experience, cannot be excluded.

The relationships identified among the study variables validate the hypothesis that well-developed sedation practices are associated with a positive attitude toward the profession, availability of sound clinical practice guidelines, and good collaboration across professional boundaries. Conversely, reduced reliance on routine sedation practices appears to be associated with improved professional awareness and clinical reasoning. The findings highlight the complexity involved in sedation management and the need for continuous assessment and decision-making. Contrary to routine clinical tasks, it is an ongoing process.

In terms of patient safety, the two most important predictors found in this study, subjective norms and perceived behavioral control, have direct implications for the care of sedation among the nurses who participated in this study. This high subjective norm score indicates that nurses’ sedation assessment, monitoring, and titration practices may rely significantly on the expectations and practices of their physicians, peers, and entire ICU team. The risk of variable sedation decisions, delayed sedative dose adjustment and under or oversedation, could then be heightened due to inconsistent team practices or unclear professional expectations. Also, lower perceptions of behavioral control could hinder nurses’ capacity to conduct frequent assessments of sedation and titration, especially in the nurse-to-patient ratios observed (1:2 in the ICU, 1:3 in the CCU) and in the observed sample with minimal formal sedation training. In this study population, these conditions may make these patients more susceptible to agitation, accidental self-extubation, delirium, longer mechanical ventilation and longer stay in the ICU [25]. The results suggest therefore that failure to enhance patient safety can be achieved by increasing individual intention alone, but it is also necessary to have consistent multidisciplinary expectations, sufficient staffing, available protocols, competency training, and sufficient clinical authority and organizational resources to facilitate safe sedation decisions.

The results show that the sedation practices in question are more influenced by the organizational context, the presence (or absence) of cohesive teamwork, and the perception of control than by individual intentions. Improvement of safety in this regard is the most complex. This is a multidimensional problem that requires the implementation of the above-mentioned systematic approach to education and training, strengthening of interprofessional collaboration, improvement of working conditions and establishment of a positive safety culture in the ICU. These findings may also reflect broader healthcare system challenges, including staffing shortages, high patient acuity, limited continuing education opportunities, and resource constraints within critical care environments. Therefore, leadership support and organizational investment are essential to facilitate evidence-based sedation management. In addition, the application of interdisciplinary and nurse-led sedation management initiatives is likely to promote the implementation of evidence-based practices and positively affect patients’ conditions [26–28].

There are some limitations to be noted. Firstly, the cross-sectional design makes it impossible to draw causal inferences between study variables. Secondly, consecutive sampling may have led to selection bias and/or to non-representativeness of samples. Third, because the study was performed in one university hospital, the results may not be applicable to other health care facilities. Fourth, self-reported questionnaires were used to collect data, and may have included recall and social desirability bias. Lastly, although acceptable psychometric characteristics were found in the study instruments, the reliability in some subscales was moderate and could have affected the precision of the measurements. The results should be interpreted in light with these limitations. Moreover, the sample may not be representative of other healthcare environments and findings may not be generalizable to such environments because of the use of a single university hospital for the consecutive sampling.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that organizational, interpersonal and behavioral factors influence the practices of providing intravenous sedation to critical care nurses. Subjective norms and perceived behavioral control were more predictive than individual intention, indicating the importance of collaboration, professional norms, staffing, work environment, and nurses’ perceived control over practice. The results offer critical care context-specific evidence and further extend the use of the Theory of Planned Behavior, as they show how social and organizational factors affect nurses’ sedation-related behaviors. The findings are relevant to nursing leadership and policy and support the implementation of structured sedation competency training, standardized sedation assessment and titration protocols, regular multidisciplinary sedation rounds, and adequate staffing arrangements. Routine audits of sedation documentation, protocol adherence, and related safety outcomes should also be incorporated into continuous quality-improvement initiatives to strengthen evidence-based sedation practices and improve the safety and outcomes of mechanically ventilated patients.
