Process-separated cascade catalysis for highly efficient alkane-to-aromatic conversion
Authors: Jianhua Cai, Hui Xiao, Qian Wang, Junyao Fu, Pengli Yang, Jiaxuan Zhu, Kaige Tian, Donglong Fu, Chunlei Pei, Zhi-Jian Zhao, Xinbin Ma, Sai Chen, Jinlong Gong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Bifunctional catalysis is pivotal to industrial heterogeneous catalytic processes, yet its performance is limited by the kinetic entanglement among different elementary steps. In the conversion of light alkanes to aromatics, a promising non-naphtha route to produce benzene, toluene and xylene (BTX) that examples this challenging, this paper describes a process-separated cascade catalysis (PSCC) for efficient BTX synthesis, prioritizing kinetic decoupling over spatial intimacy of active sites in bifunctional systems. We employ a spatially decoupled metal-zeolite catalyst, achieving >95% propane conversion and 82.3% aromatic selectivity with near-exclusive BTX formation at 550 °C during continuous reaction-regeneration cycles. In situ spectroscopies and kinetics analysis reveal that PSCC decouples kinetics of alkane dehydrogenation to olefine intermediates (ethene, propene) in the first stage and synchronizes this process with oligomerization and aromatization reactions in the second stage. This synchronization affords the rate matching of two stage, thus minimizes cracking by-products and enhances BTX production substantially. Combined with high catalytic stability and technoeconomic assessment, this PSCC strategy represents a robust pathway in alkane (C2–C4)-to-BTX conversion and beyond bifunctional catalysis processes.

Introduction

Bifunctional catalysis lies at the heart of numerous heterogeneous catalytic processes pivotal to the chemical industry, from alkane upgrading1–3, CO2 hydrogenation4,5, syngas conversion6–8, plastic upcycling9,10, etc. For over six decades, the prevailing “intimacy criterion” introduced by Weisz has guided catalyst design with the principle of “the closer, the better” for positioning distinct active sites11. Yet when different elementary reactions proceed on spatially proximal active sites, mismatched reaction rates and unregulated intermediate diffusion trigger rampant side reactions and rapid catalyst deactivation via coke deposition. Simply minimizing distance often fails to address a more fundamental challenge in coordinating the transport of reactive intermediates and, critically, matching the intrinsic rates of the individual catalytic steps.

Light alkane dehydro-aromatization containing metal sites next to acid sites represents a prime example of this challenge12–14. While metal-zeolite bifunctional catalysts (e.g., Ga/ZSM-515,16 and Zn/ZSM-517,18) have been developed for this reaction, their performance is hampered by the aforementioned kinetic entanglement: sluggish alkane dehydrogenation on metal sites and fast acid-catalyzed oligomerization/aromatization on zeolite acid sites19,20, when paired in close proximity, result in low alkane conversion, poor aromatic selectivity, and weak catalytic durability21–23. Efforts to address these issues have largely focused on fine-tuning the physical proximity of metal and acid sites within the traditional intimacy criterion framework, failing to resolve the core kinetic mismatch that underpins the problem6,8,24. Even state-of-the-art catalysts exhibit limited propane conversions and aromatic selectivity in the ranges of 25–67% and 29–69%, respectively, and rapid coke deactivation depending on reaction conditions.

The aforementioned Ga/ZSM-5 and Zn/ZSM-5 are representative conventional tandem bifunctional catalysts. Their design strictly adheres to the classic “intimacy criterion”, with metal dehydrogenation and acid aromatization sites fabricated in nanoscale proximity within a single zeolite framework. Although such a configuration facilitates short-range diffusion of olefin intermediates, it inevitably causes severe kinetic entanglement between consecutive reaction steps. The strongly coupled active sites preclude independent modulation of intrinsic reaction kinetics, and simply adjusting inter-site distance cannot remedy the fundamental rate mismatch. In comparison, dual-bed and physical separation catalysis represent another prevailing strategy. They segregate dehydrogenation and aromatization functions at the macroscopic millimeter scale, which fully decouples the two reaction pathways and allows flexible optimization of individual reaction conditions. Nevertheless, this layout imposes substantial long-range mass transfer constraints. Olefin intermediates require complete desorption, inter-bed diffusion and re-adsorption, making accurate kinetic matching between the two steps hardly achievable.

Here, we address the kinetic entanglement by proposing process-separated cascade catalysis (PSCC) for efficient light alkane-to-aromatic conversion. (Fig. 1) We employ a spatially decoupled catalyst containing a PtZn intermetallic alloy (IMA) positioned at the pore mouth to selectively dehydrogenate propane to propene, followed by a second zeolite catalyst (Na-ZnZSM-5) that facilitates propene diffusion and subsequent aromatization into BTX in a rate-matched manner. The reaction achieves near-complete selectivity toward BTX among aromatics, with >95% propane conversion and 82.3% aromatics selectivity at 550 °C, outperforming conventional direct propane aromatization. In situ spectroscopies and kinetics analysis reveal that this PSCC strategy decouples the kinetics of alkane dehydrogenation to olefinic intermediates in the first stage and synchronizes this process with oligomerization and aromatization reactions in the second stage. This synchronization prioritizes kinetic decoupling over spatial intimacy of active sites, which minimizes cracking by-products and enhances BTX production substantially. Results

Design of spatially decoupled metal-on-zeolite catalysts

The proposed process separated cascade relies on the precise design of spatially decoupled catalysts. As detailed in the experimental section, this catalyst preparation involved sequential steps, including initial ion-exchange with Na+ to modulate the Brønsted acid sites (BAS) density of ZSM-5 (NaZ5), followed by the introduction of Zn2+ species to create Lewis acid sites (LAS) (ZnNaZ5) and impregnation of the PtZn compound (PtZn-on-ZnNaZ5) at the pore mouth.

X-Ray Diffraction (XRD) patterns (Supplementary Fig. 1) confirmed the characteristic Mobil-type Five (MFI) topology of all samples, indicating an undisturbed zeolite framework throughout the stepwise modifications. ICP-OES elemental analysis quantifies the actual loadings of Na, Zn, and Pt over the modified zeolites (Supplementary Table 1). As shown by NH3-temperature programmed desorption (TPD) in Supplementary Fig. 2, the introduction of Na+ and Zn2+ ions modulates the strength of the strong acid sites. Quantitative analysis of pyridine-adsorption IR spectra (Py-IR) reveals that the parent ZSM-5 possesses a high BAS density (525.2 μmol·g−1) and minimal LAS (10.7 μmol·g−1), giving a BAS/LAS ratio of 49.2 (Supplementary Table 2). After sequential Na+ and Zn2+ modification, the resulting PtZn-on-ZnNaZ5 catalyst exhibits a BAS density of 444.1 μmol·g−1 alongside a markedly increased LAS density of 350.4 μmol·g−1, lowering the BAS/LAS ratio to 1.3. Further increasing Na⁺ exchange progressively reduces BAS to 252.4 μmol·g−1 while raising LAS to 491.8 μmol·g−1, ultimately driving the BAS/LAS ratio down to 0.5. This approach enables a continuous shift from Brønsted-acid to Lewis-acid dominated character in a controlled manner. Ultraviolet-visible (UV-Vis) spectroscopy, shown in Fig. 2a, indicated that the modified ZnNaZ5 exhibited absorption bands at around 210 and 280 nm, which are characteristic of isolated, framework-associated [Zn(OH)]+ species25. Impregnation and loading of the PtZn compound at the pore mouth are to spatially isolate the Pt from the zeolite crystallites. As shown in Fig. 2d, e, aberration-corrected high-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (AC-HAADF-STEM) images revealed that the metal nanoparticles were uniformly dispersed at the zeolite crystal edges, specifically at the pore mouths and external surfaces with a narrow particle size distribution centered at 1-2 nm (Supplementary Fig. 4). The clear lattice fringes observed in the nanoparticles exhibited an interplanar spacing of ~0.286 nm, which can be assigned to the (110) plane of the ordered PtZn intermetallic alloy (IMA) (Supplementary Fig. 5). In situ CO-Diffuse Reflectance Infrared Fourier Transform Spectroscopy (DRIFTS) reveals a single sharp band at 2054 cm−1 corresponding to linearly adsorbed CO on the reduced PtZn-in-zeolite and PtZn-on-zeolite catalysts (Fig. 2b). The identical frequency confirms that the PtZn IMA possesses the similar electronic states. This band is markedly blue-shifted compared to that on metallic Pt (~2076 cm−1) and aligns well with reported values for PtZn IMAs26. All spectra were recorded under low coverage (20 min Ar purge), where dipole-dipole coupling is negligible.(Supplementary Fig. 6) This spectroscopic signature provides compelling evidence for the formation of the electron-deficient Pt sites within the PtZn IMA structure, which are known to be highly selective for dehydrogenation.

The pore-mouth distribution of PtZn IMAs was further verified using a bulky probe molecule technique. Collidine (2,4,6-trimethylpyridine), with its kinetic diameter exceeding the ZSM-5 pore opening, selectively probes externally exposed active sites. In-situ Collidine-Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR) analysis displayed the well-defined absorption features at 1597 cm−1 and 1446 cm−1 (Fig. 2c), signatures exclusively attributed to collidine interacting with PtZn alloy sites. It is noted that the kinetic diameter of the Pt(NH3)42+ (~0.48 nm) is comparable with the ZSM-5 pore size (~0.55 nm) and thus the diffusion of Pt(NH3)42+ ions into the micropores during the impregnation process could be limited, leading to the preferential deposition of Pt nanoparticles on the external surface and pore-mouth regions (denoted as PtZn-on-ZnZ5), corresponding to the scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM) and secondary electron (SE) images where a small fraction of nanoparticles was located inside the ZSM-5 crystals (Fig. 2d, Supplementary Figs. 7–9). In contrast, the kinetically smaller PtCl62- (~0.41 nm) can readily diffuse into the channels, resulting in the distribution of most Pt species inside the zeolite crystallites (denoted as PtZn-in-ZnZ5), evidenced by the absence of Collidine adsorption features at 1597 cm−1 and 1446 cm−1 in FTIR and STEM images (Fig. 2e, Supplementary Figs. 10–12).

The spatial distribution of metal and acid sites is expected to influence the catalytic sequence and product selectivity. As shown in Fig. 2f, PtZn-on-ZnZ5 achieves 82.3% aromatic selectivity with near-exclusive (95.1%) BTX formation (Supplementary Fig. 13), yielding the highest aromatic-to-CH4 ratio (5.8) under the tested conditions (Supplementary Fig. 14). In contrast, for catalysts such as Ga/ZSM-5 (GaZ5) and PtZn-in-ZnZ5, both metal and acid functions are confined within the ZSM-5 micropores, resulting in lower BTX selectivity of 67.7% and 65.5% with higher methane and ethane selectivities of over 9.9% and 16.7%, respectively, at comparable propane conversion (Fig. 2f, Supplementary Figs. 15, 16). This observation indicates that the spatial proximity between metal and acid sites seems to influence the product formation and distribution significantly.

Intimacy-dependent aromatic selectivity and formation rates

From thermodynamic estimations, propane dehydrogenation is endothermic, whereas propene oligomerization and cyclization are exothermic, endowing the reaction with inherent heat coupling (Supplementary Fig. 17)27. The coupling of propane dehydrogenation and propene aromatization additionally consumes the primary propene intermediate, which shifts the thermodynamic equilibrium in favor of propane conversion according to Le Chatelier’s principle.

As displayed in Fig. 3b, the PtZn/SiO2 intermetallic alloy catalyst is highly selective for on-purpose propane dehydrogenation with propene selectivity over 96.2%; however, it yields negligible aromatics due to the absence of Brønsted acid sites inside zeolites, indicating its monofunctional dehydrogenation character. In contrast, ZnZ5 suffers from insufficient dehydrogenation capability with wide product distribution, although it contains both dehydrogenation and BAS functions. This limitation results in pronounced cracking on BAS, as reflected in a high C1 and C2 selectivity of 56.6% and low aromatic selectivity (23.6%). We then design and demonstrate the propane dehydro-aromatization at the different cascade modes (Fig. 3a). The distance between PtZn IMA and ZnZ5 was estimated from the millimeter-scale to the nanoscale by a Monte Carlo method (Supplementary Fig. 19, Supplementary Table 3). As displayed in Fig. 3a, when the PtZn IMA sites were placed in closer proximity to the Zn-modified ZSM-5 acid sites progressing from a dual-bed configuration (M1) in the reactor, physically mixed granules (M2), intimately mixed powders (M3), to impregnated catalyst (PtZn-on-ZnZ5, M4; PtZn-in-ZnZ5, M5), the propane conversion increased from 32.3% to 87.6% steadily (Supplementary Fig. 20). This intimacy evolution drives a clear product shift (Fig. 3b) that selectivity toward light olefins (C2= and C3=) declines from 50.8% (ZnZ5 alone) to below 14.5% (M4) as the intimacy between the sites increased, indicating that the olefin intermediates are increasingly channeled into the subsequent aromatization pathway. To exclude conversion-dependent selectivity variations, we further compared the product distribution of all catalysts at a similar propane conversion (Supplementary Fig. 21). Consistent with the trends observed in Fig. 3b, increasing intimacy between PtZn and acid sites led to a monotonic reduction in light olefin selectivity and a corresponding increase in BTX selectivity, confirming that the evolution in product distribution is an intrinsic function of site proximity rather than conversion level. In parallel, aromatic yield increases progressively from 23.6% to 67.9%, and aromatics formation rate increases from 2.4 mmol·gcat−1·h−1 to 24.8 mmol·gcat−1·h−1 (Fig. 3c), confirming that shortening the diffusion path between dehydrogenation and acid functions contributes to the tandem sequence. It is noted that methane selectivity drops sharply from 21.0% to 3.8%, attributable to the directed and selective dehydrogenation on PtZn IMA sites, which effectively suppresses alkane cracking on Brønsted acid sites when the two functions are in physical distance. However, further decreasing the distance between metal sites and acid sites to an atomic scale, where Pt is located inside zeolite (PtZn-in-ZnZ5, M5), it presented a decreased aromatic selectivity (59.3%) and formation rate (16.7 mmol·gcat−1·h−1) (Fig. 3c). This seems contrast to Weisz’s intimacy criterion, which is often interpreted as “the closer the better”. From a kinetic standpoint, such ‘too intimate’ contact of functional sites facilitates the kinetically preferable molecular cracking of propane and hydrogen transfer on the BAS28–30, which favors the formation of non-selective small alkanes (e.g., C1, C2). This suggests that the kinetic proximity, instead of physical distance between metal and acid sites, governs the product selectivity and reaction efficiency.

The performance of the spatially decoupled PtZn-on-ZnZ5 catalyst was further investigated and benchmarked against a commercial GaZ5 analog, representative of industrial applications. As shown in Fig. 3d, GaZ5 catalysts featuring Ga inside the zeolite exhibited higher selectivity toward by-products, light alkanes (methane, etc.) of 9.6%, due to the excessive cracking and hydrogen transfer on adjacent metal and strong acid sites. This results in the quick deactivation with a deactivation rate of 0.55 h−1 evidenced by the formation of more graphitic coke depositions revealed by thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) and O2-temperature programmed oxidation (TPO) (Supplementary Fig. 22). In comparison, PtZn-on-ZnZ5 achieved a higher aromatic selectivity of 82.3% (BTX selectivity, 78.3%) and lower methane selectivity of 2.2% at a lower deactivation rate of 0.10 h−1 compared to commercial GaZ5 analog and the state-of-the-art catalysts (Supplementary Fig. 23, Supplementary Table 4). The enhanced stability is likely due to the reduced coke formation, a result of the shorter diffusion path for aromatic products and the suppression of heavy carbonaceous species formation via hydrogen transfer reactions31. Moreover, the PtZn-on-zeolite catalyst demonstrated excellent reaction-regenerative stability (Fig. 3d, Supplementary Fig. S24). After a simple calcination in air to remove carbon deposits, the catalytic activity and selectivity were almost fully restored over 95 reaction-regeneration cycles with a total time-on-stream of 430 h, underscoring its robust nature and potential for long-term industrial use. Further HAADF-STEM and CO-DRIFTS of the cycled catalyst confirm that the PtZn intermetallic alloy maintains its structural integrity and electronic properties after repeated reaction–regeneration cycles. (Supplementary Figs. 25, 26). ICP-OES results (Supplementary Table 1) further reveal only a slight loss of metallic components for the regenerated catalyst relative to the fresh one, and such minor metal leaching exerts a negligible influence on the catalytic performance. To further validate its stability under non-equilibrium conditions (far from thermodynamic conversion limits), we performed an additional 100 h cyclic stability test at a higher WHSV (WHSVpropane = 20 h−1) over 26 consecutive reaction-regeneration cycles. (Supplementary Fig. 27) Notably, the PtZn-on-ZnNaZ5 catalyst retained excellent performance throughout this test, further confirming its structural and functional robustness under varied reaction conditions.

Kinetic decoupling beyond nanoscale intimacy

A thorough analysis of the reaction kinetics was then performed to decouple the elementary steps with the elimination of internal and external diffusion (Supplementary Fig. 28, further details regarding the derivations are available in the Supporting Information). As illustrated in Fig. 4a, the reaction order for propane for all samples approximated 1.0 except for PtZn/SiO2, which exclusively produces C3H6 without aromatics in the absence of BAS. This first-order behavior points to monomolecular propane activation as the dominant pathway, excluding significant contributions from bimolecular routes such as propane cracking or cross-reactions between propane molecules under the applied conditions. However, a notable divergence emerged in the hydrogen pressure dependent order (Fig. 4b). For ZnZ5, the H2 reaction order of –0.1 indicates the rate-determining step (RDS) closely associated with the initial C–H bond activation. For GaZ5, the H2 reaction order of –0.4 suggests a transitional case where the RDS remains closely associated with the initial C–H bond activation, yet already exhibits sensitivity to hydrogen pressure. In comparison, PtZn-on-ZnZ5 shows a more negative H2 reaction order of –0.8. The observed increase in negative reaction orders reflects a kinetic transition wherein the RDS shifts toward a later, deeper dehydrogenation step. The quantitative comparison between the dehydrogenation rate constant (kDe) and olefin aromatization rate constant (kAro) using lumped kinetic modeling32, as delineated in Fig. 4d, Supplementary Fig. 29. As summarized in Supplementary Tables 5–13, the conventional ZnZ5 and GaZ5 catalysts show significantly lower kDe value (0.25 and 4.08 kPa−0.5·mmol·g−1·h−1 respectively) relative to kAro (9.87 and 97.20 kPa−2·mmol·g−1·h−1 respectively), establishing the initial propane dehydrogenation as the dominant kinetic bottleneck. This substantial mismatch leads to inadequate propene supply, forcing the system to operate under aromatization-limited conditions. While the introduction of PtZn significantly enhanced the dehydrogenation rate constant (kDe = 9.07 kPa−0.5·mmol·g−1·h−1), a discernible kinetic gap persisted relative to the aromatization rate constant (kAro = 16.43 kPa−2·mmol·g−1·h−1) in ZSM-5. By implementing sequential Na+ ion-exchange and Zn2+ modification, the resulting PtZn-on-ZnNaZ5 system achieved rate matching (kDe = 10.52 kPa−0.5·mmol·g−1·h−1, kAro = 11.12 kPa−2·mmol·g−1·h−1), which minimizes intermediate accumulation and promotes the desired sequential pathway, as evidenced by the suppressed methane selectivity and enhanced BTX yield compared to its unmodified counterpart. Such evolution in the kDe/kAro value underscores the rate matching of the dehydrogenation-aromatization step by process separated cascade catalysis over PtZn-on-zeolite catalysts.

This separation of catalytic process in dehydrogenation-aromatization is visualized in Fig. 4e where dehydrogenation and aromatization sites are spatially decoupled yet interconnected in a hierarchical architecture. Figure 4f depicts a conventional tandem system that the two elementary reactions proceed synchronously that intermediate conversion initiates instantly upon formation, with the relative concentrations of reactant, intermediate and product dictated by the kDe/kAro ratio. In contrast, the process separated dehydrogenation-aromatization cascade, as shown in Fig. 4g is characterized by strict sequential reaction progression that the second-step aromatization is initiated until the first-step dehydrogenation is complete, rather than proceeding synchronously. This stepwise pattern enables independent operation of each reaction, resolving the kinetic mismatch inherent to conventional catalysts that exhibit low kDe in ZnZ5 and GaZ5 catalysts. This underpins the enhanced BTX selectivity and stability on PtZn-on-zeolite catalysts versus counterparts.

The interaction of intermediate with acid sites was further probed by C3H6-temperature programmed desorption (TPD) (Fig. 5b, Supplementary Fig. 32). PtZn/SiO2 exhibits the lowest propylene desorption temperature, a feature that contributes to its high propylene selectivity by rapid olefin desorption prior to its subsequent reactions. For ZnZ5, GaZ5, and PtZn-on-ZnNaZ5, benzene and toluene that are originated from aromatization of adsorbed propylene on BAS, show similar desorption peak temperatures, suggesting comparable intrinsic aromatization activities. However, methane and ethane signals emerge at significantly lower temperatures on ZnZ5 (184.0 °C and 170.3 °C) and GaZ5 (185.7 °C and 184.8 °C) than those on PtZn-on-ZnNaZ5 (293.5 °C and 296.0 °C), indicating the occurrence of more dominant hydrogenolysis and cracking side reactions over ZnZ5 and GaZ5 catalysts with a closer distance between metal and zeolites.

In situ FTIR spectroscopy was further employed to track the evolution of surface species during the reaction. When propane was introduced to the PtZn-on-ZnNaZ5 catalyst (Fig. 5c, Supplementary Fig. 33a), the initial spectra displayed characteristic vibrational bands between 1610 and 1680 cm−133, attributable to C = C stretching vibrations in olefinic intermediates. As the reaction continued, the intensity of these olefin-related bands decreased progressively. However, this phenomenon is not pronounced in GaZ5, suggesting limited involvement of the dehydrogenation-first pathway (Supplementary Fig. 33b). Concurrently, new bands appeared near 1600 and 1500 cm−133, consistent with aromatic ring stretching modes. This sequential spectral transformation offers direct evidence for the conversion of olefinic intermediates into aromatic products. It is noted that a transient band observed at ~1559 cm−1 can be assigned to cyclic carbenium ions, such as cyclopentenyl cations, which have been proposed as pivotal intermediates in the zeolite-mediated aromatization mechanism34.

Further insights into cyclic intermediates were obtained through in situ gas chromatography-mass spectrometer (GC-MS) analysis of the reactor effluent, where cyclopentene was identified (Fig. 5d). This detection supports the involvement of five-membered ring species in the reaction network, with cyclopentenyl cations likely serving as precursors consistent with the FTIR observations35. The functional importance of these species was underscored by a pulsed experiment with cyclopentene (Fig. 5e), which demonstrated its efficient conversion to aromatic hydrocarbons. Corresponding control tests rule out gas-phase reactions and highlight the critical role of the PtZn-acid synergy in cyclopentene transformation. (Supplementary Figs. 34, 35) More significantly, co-feeding cyclopentene with propane induced a pronounced increase of aromatic selectivity to 79.2% compared to 58.2% with propane alone and 54.2% with cyclopentene alone (Fig. 5f). This non-linear enhancement establishes cyclopentene-type species as essential reactive intermediates in the dominant aromatization pathway, likely serving as precursors for ring expansion to six-membered aromatic compounds.

Together, these mechanical investigations revealed that propane dehydrogenation proceeds on the PtZn IMA positioned at the pore mouth, which facilitates an ordered reaction sequence that propane is first selectively converted to olefins, and the generated olefins subsequently undergo aromatization within the zeolite channels via the cyclopentenyl cation intermediates (Fig. 5g, Supplementary Fig. 36). This well-defined spatial decoupling is critical for achieving high selectivity and yield of aromatics.

Practical application viability and technoeconomic analysis

The design of the process separated dehydrogenation-aromatization cascade demonstrates the versatility of light alkanes beyond propane. As summarized in Fig. 6a, Supplementary Fig. 37 and Supplementary Table 14 for ethane, a combined selectivity of 92.7% toward aromatics and ethylene was achieved over this catalyst, highlighting its potential for the simultaneous production of aromatics and ethylene. In the case of n-butane aromatization, the process demonstrates a high aromatic selectivity of 82.1%. Importantly, the selectivity to methane remains very low at 2.8%. Technoeconomic analysis (TEA) was further carried out to evaluate the commercial feasibility of this process, separated dehydrogenation-aromatization cascade (PSDAC) route and benchmarked against conventional naphtha reforming and the GaZ5-based Cyclar process (Supplementary Figs. 38, 39)36,37. Detailed calculations for all systems are documented in the Supporting Information. Heat integration is realized by recycling the waste heat from catalyst regeneration to preheat feedstock and supply reaction heat, reducing energy consumption. In terms of regeneration layout, the fixed-bed cyclic operation mode is compatible with industrial scaling, enabling uninterrupted production through reactor switching during regeneration, ensuring technical feasibility for industrial application. For environmental impact assessment, we quantified the CO2 footprint of each process (Fig. 6b, Supplementary Tables 15, 16), which comprises both direct emissions (from on-stream reactions and coke combustion) and indirect emissions (associated with electricity and steam consumption). Naphtha reforming showed the highest indirect CO2 emissions (0.99 kg-CO2 eq per kg-BTX), stemming from its elevated energy requirements and relatively low BTX yield. In contrast, the process separated dehydrogenation-aromatization cascade (PSDAC) achieved a lower total CO2 emission (0.65 kg-CO2 eq per kg-BTX) due to its superior BTX selectivity and yield despite the similar direct emissions (from fuel combustion) with the Cyclar process. The LCB (Levelized cost of BTX) (Fig. 6c, Supplementary Tables 17–20) analysis reflects the cost competitiveness of the proposed process, separated dehydrogenation-aromatization cascade process (this work) had the lowest LCB, which correlates with its highest net present value (NPV; Fig. 6d), a critical indicator of long-term economic viability. Sensitivity analysis further demonstrates that the process maintains strong NPV performance under typical market fluctuations, including variations in feedstock price (±5%) and BTX product price (±5%). For illustrative purposes, we also evaluated a plausible range of carbon pricing scenarios (25–50 $/t-CO2), which are known to be highly policy-dependent and regionally variable, to assess their potential impact on process economics. The results confirm that the PSDAC process remains economically competitive even under these scenarios, highlighting its inherent resilience. These findings demonstrate that the process separated cascade is not only broadly applicable for upgrading light alkanes to aromatics but also stands out as a commercially viable and environmentally advantageous route.

Discussion

In summary, this work overcame the persistent selectivity limitations of bifunctional catalysis during BTX synthesis by a process separated dehydrogenation-aromatization cascade. Through the design of PtZn-on-zeolite catalysts, we demonstrated that the spatial decoupling strategy prevents kinetic entanglement between propane dehydrogenation and propene aromatization, achieving >95% propane conversion and 82.3% aromatic selectivity with near-exclusive BTX formation. The rate matching between dehydrogenation and aromatization steps effectively suppresses cracking by-products and reduces coke formation, leading to superior catalyst stability. The approach was further validated across C2–C4 alkanes and shows promising techno-economic potential compared to conventional routes. These contributions challenge the long-standing “intimacy criterion” in bifunctional catalyst design, demonstrating that optimal catalysis requires not only physical proximity but also kinetic decoupling of sequential steps. This work establishes process separated cascade catalysis as a guiding principle for the rational design of catalytic systems toward complex reactions.

Methods

Preparation of catalysts

Synthesis of NaZ5-x supports

The parent ZSM-5 zeolite with a SiO2/Al2O3 ratio of 25 was commercially obtained from Nankai Catalyst Co., Ltd. and used as the starting material. To regulate the concentration of Brønsted acid sites, the proton form of ZSM-5 was subjected to ion-exchange with sodium ions. In a typical procedure, 5 g of the parent ZSM-5 was dispersed in 100 mL of an aqueous NaNO3 solution. The suspension was stirred at 80 °C for 2 h. Subsequently, the solid product was recovered by centrifugation and washed thoroughly with deionized water three times to remove residual nitrate ions. The obtained solid product was dried overnight at 100 °C, followed by calcination in static air at 560 °C for 5 h. The resulting zeolites are designated NaZ5-x, where x is the nominal mass fraction of Na (wt%) in the ion exchange feed, set by the concentration of the Na-containing exchange solution, rather than the actual Na content measured in the final zeolite product.

Synthesis of ZnNaZ5-x catalysts

The Zn-modified zeolite was prepared via a liquid-phase ion-exchange method using the synthesized NaZ5-x or ZSM-5 as the parent material. In a standard synthesis, 5.0 g of NaZ5-x or ZSM-5 was dispersed in 25 mL of deionized water. Subsequently, 250 μL of a zinc nitrate solution (Aladdin, 99%, 0.1 g/mL) was added. The mixture was stirred continuously at 80 °C for 5 h and dried overnight at 100 °C. The resulting powder is denoted as ZnNa5-x or ZnZ5.

Synthesis of PtZn-ZnNaZ5-x catalysts

The PtZn intermetallic nanoparticles were loaded onto the ZnNaZ5-x support via the incipient wetness impregnation method, following a procedure adapted from the work of Chen et al. with modifications1.

Synthesis of PtZn-on-ZnNaZ5-x catalysts

The precursor solution was typically prepared by dissolving Pt(NH3)4(NO3)2 (Chemart (Tianjin) Chemical Technology Co., Ltd, 99.9%) and Zn(NO3)2·6H2O (Aladdin (China) Chemical Co., Ltd, 98.0%) in deionized water to achieve nominal loadings of 0.5 wt% Pt and a Pt/Zn molar ratio of 1:1. This mixed precursor solution was then added dropwise to the ZnNaZ5-x support. The impregnated solid was aged at room temperature for 2 h, followed by drying overnight in an oven at 100 °C. The final catalyst was obtained by calcination at 600 °C and 10% H2/Ar atmosphere for 1 h with a ramp rate of 10 °C min−1. This series of catalysts is denoted as PtZn-on-ZnNaZ5-x. (Unless otherwise specified, the catalyst designated simply as PtZn-on-ZnNaZ5 refers to the sample PtZn-on-ZnNaZ5−1.2.)

Synthesis of PtZn-in-ZnNaZ5-x catalysts

PtZn-in-ZnNaZ5-x catalysts were prepared via the same preparation procedures as above, except that H2PtCl6·6H2O (Chemart (Tianjin) Chemical Technology Co., Ltd, 99.9%) was utilized as the platinum precursor. This series of catalysts is denoted as PtZn-in-ZnNaZ5-x.

Synthesis of GaZ5 catalysts

The ion exchange method was utilized to synthesize GaZ5 (mimic Cyclar from UOP) catalysts to compare our PtZn-on-ZnNaZ5-x to the commercialized catalysts. In a typical procedure, a GaNO3·9H2O (Beijing InnoChem Science & Technology Co., Ltd., 99.9%) aqueous solution was prepared with a concentration corresponding to a nominal Ga loading of 2.0 wt%. The parent H-ZSM-5 support was dispersed in this solution, and the mixture was stirred at 80 °C for 5 h. The solid product was then dried overnight at 100 °C. Finally, the material was calcined in static air at 560 °C for 5 h. The resulting catalyst is denoted as GaZ5.

Physical and chemical characterization of redox catalysts

X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were recorded on a Bruker D8-Focus Advance diffractometer using Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5418 Å, 30 kV, 15 mA). The data were collected in the 2θ range of 5° to 50° with a step size of 0.02° and a scanning speed of 5° min−1. The crystallinity of the ZSM-5 phase was evaluated by comparing the intensity of the characteristic peaks with that of a reference sample.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM), high-resolution TEM (HRTEM), and high-angle annular dark-field scanning TEM (HAADF-STEM) were carried out on a JEM-F200 microscope operating at 200 kV. The sample powder was dispersed in deionized water by ultrasonic and deposited on a cooper grid coated with an ultrathin holey carbon film. Particle size distributions were obtained by measuring over 200 individual particles from multiple images.

The CO-diffuse reflectance infrared Fourier transform spectroscopy (CO-DRIFTS) of the catalysts was obtained by a Nicolet 6700 spectrometer with a stainless-steel cell connected to a gas-dosing and evacuation system. 30 mg of the catalyst was compressed into an in situ cell equipped with ZnSe windows. Prior to CO adsorption, the sample was reduced in H2 with a flow rate of 30 mL min−1 at 600 °C for 1.5 h. After that, the catalysts were purged under a flow of Ar (20 mL min−1) for 0.5 h to remove the chemisorbed other impurity species and then cooled to 30 °C under He to collect the background spectrum. Then the catalysts were exposed to a flow of a gas mixture of CO 3 mL min−1, Ar 20 mL min−1 at 30 °C for 0.5 h to adsorb CO, and then purging Ar (20 mL min−1) for 20 min. The IR spectra were collected with a resolution of 8 cm−1.

Ultraviolet–visible spectroscopy (UV-Vis) spectra were collected on a Shimadzu UV-3600i Plus spectrophotometer equipped with an integrating sphere. Al2O3 was used as a reflectance standard. The spectra were recorded in the range of 200–800 nm to identify the coordination environment of metal species.

Collidine-Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (Coll-FTIR) experiments were performed to probe the location of metal species at the zeolite pore mouth, following established procedures2. The catalyst was pressed into a self-supporting disk and activated in the IR cell. It was then exposed to a basic molecular probe (Collidine, 2,4,6-Trimethylpyridine, Aladdin (China) Chemical Co., Ltd, 99.0%), which is too bulky to enter the MFI pores. The difference in the IR bands before and after adsorption indicated the presence of active sites on the external surface.

Temperature-programmed experiments were carried out in a U-type quartz tube with a Micromeritics AutoChem 2920 apparatus connected to a mass spectrometer (Hiden HPR-20). For O2-TPO, 100 mg of samples after a 4 h test at 550 °C was heated at 300 °C for 1 h and cooled down to 50 °C in a flow of N2 (50 mL min−1) and then heated in a stream of 10 vol% O2/He (30 mL min−1) with 10 °C min−1 up to 800 °C. For C3H8 or C3H6 TPSR, 100 mg of catalyst was pre-reduced in 10% H2/Ar at 600 °C. After cooling to 100 °C in He, the feed gas (5% C3H8 or C3H6 in He) was introduced. The temperature was then ramped to 650 °C at a rate of 10 °C min−1. For C3H6-TPD experiments, 100 mg of sample was first reduced in a flowing 10 vol% H2/Ar at 600 °C for 1 h. Then, the sample was cooled down to 50 °C, and the gas was switched to flowing Ar to flush the apparatus. Subsequently, the adsorption of C3H6 was achieved by the introduction of flowing C3H6 for 45 min, and then the system was purged with flowing He for 30 min. After that, the sample was raised at a rate of 10 °C min−1 up to 600 °C in flowing He. For C6H6-TPD experiments, 100 mg of sample was first reduced in a flowing 10 vol% H2/Ar at 600 °C for 1 h. Then, the sample was cooled down to 50 °C, and the gas was switched to flowing Ar to flush the apparatus. Subsequently, the adsorption of C6H6 was achieved by pulse C6H6 steam 30 times, and then the system was purged with flowing He for 30 min. After that, the sample was raised at a rate of 10 °C min−1 up to 600 °C in flowing He. The output products (C7H8, C6H6, C3H8, C3H6, CH4, and H2, m/z equals 97, 78, 29, 41, 16, and 2, respectively) for both TPSR and TPD were measured via a mass spectrometer (MS) instantaneously.

In-situ GC-MS test was integrated with the catalytic reactor for real-time analysis of reaction intermediates and products. The effluent from the reactor was directly transferred via a heated transfer line (maintained at 150 °C) to an online gas chromatograph mass spectrometer (GCMS-QP2020 NX). Separation was achieved using a HP-PONA capillary column.

Cyclopentene cofeeding experiment was conducted in a microreactor system at 550 °C. The catalyst (100 mg) was first pre-treated in 10% H2/N2 flow. Pulses of pure cyclopentene (0.5 mL per pulse) were then injected into the N2 carrier gas. The products from each pulse were analyzed by an online GC to quantify whether cyclopentene compounds are aromatization intermediates.

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was performed on a Setaram Themys apparatus to assess the amount of coke deposited on spent catalysts. 20 mg of the spent catalyst was placed in an alumina crucible. The analysis was conducted under a controlled atmosphere following a two-step protocol: First, the sample was heated from room temperature to 300 °C at a ramp rate of 10 °C min−1 under a 100 mL min−1 N2 flow and held at this temperature for 60 min to remove moisture and weakly adsorbed species. After cooling back to room temperature under N2, the atmosphere was switched to 100 mL min−1 synthetic air. In the second step, the temperature was ramped from room temperature to 850 °C at a rate of 10 °C min−1. The weight loss observed during this second combustion step in air, typically in the temperature range of 300–650 °C, was attributed to the oxidation of carbonaceous deposits (coke). The coke content of the spent catalyst was calculated based on this mass loss.

Catalyst evaluation

The catalytic performance of the synthesized catalysts for propane dehydro-aromatization was evaluated in a continuous flow, fixed-bed reactor under atmospheric pressure.

The evaluation was conducted in a quartz tubular reactor (Inner Diameter: 8 mm). The catalyst (20–40 mesh) was loaded into the isothermal zone of the reactor and sandwiched between two layers of quartz wool. The reaction temperature was monitored and controlled by a K-type thermocouple placed in direct contact with the catalyst bed.

Prior to the reaction, the catalyst was reduced in a 10% H2/Ar flow (50 mL min−1) by ramping the temperature from room temperature to 600 °C at a rate of 10 °C min−1 and holding for 1 h. After activation, the reactor was purged with N2 and cooled to the desired reaction temperature (typically 550 °C). The reaction was initiated by introducing the feed gas, which consisted of propane (Air Liquide, 99.9%) diluted with N2 (C3H8/N2 vol/vol = 1/9). The total gas flow rate was adjusted to achieve a weight hourly space velocity (WHSV) of propane in the range of 2.0 to 4.0 h−1.

The effluent from the reactor was analyzed online by a Shimadzu GC-2014C system equipped with one thermal conductivity detector (TCD, TDX-01 column, 2 m × 1/8 inch × 1 μm) and two flame ionization detectors (FIDs, HP-Q-BOND column, 30 m × 0.32 mm × 10 μm; Stabliwax column, 30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.5 μm).
