Capturing LLM Capabilities via Evidence-Calibrated Query Clustering
Fangzhou Wu, Sandeep Silwal, Qiuyi (Richard) Zhang

Abstract
Query clustering organizes queries into groups that reflect shared latent capability demands, enabling capability-aware LLM evaluation. Existing clustering methods, which primarily rely on semantic taxonomies or embeddings, often fail to capture such latent capability requirements due to a misalignment between surface-level semantics and actual model performance. We propose ECC , an algorithm that calibrates prior semantic embeddings using limited posterior model comparisons to bridge the gap between surface-level semantics and latent capability requirements. ECC characterizes each cluster through a capability profile parameterized by a Bradley-Terry model and uses trainable mixture weights to accommodate queries with mixed capability demands, jointly learning a flexible, capability-aware clustering structure that supports query-specific inference of LLM capabilities. Extensive quantitative and qualitative evaluations demonstrate that ECC significantly improves LLM capability ranking quality, outperforming human-labeled and embedding-based baselines by an average of 17.64 and 18.02 percentage points, respectively, and proves effective in downstream tasks such as query routing. Our code is available at https://github.com/fzwark/ECC .

Introduction

Large Language Models trained on extensive corpora exhibit broad domain knowledge and diverse capabilities, as evaluated by standard benchmarks
[
1
,
42
]
.
Since different LLMs excel in different tasks (i.e.
query-dependent
), accurately assessing their strengths is essential for reliable deployment in domain-specific and personalized settings
[
12
,
32
]
.
However, coarsely curated datasets, or groups of queries, often yield noisy evidence that is insufficient for characterizing latent LLM capabilities, especially when queries within a group differ widely in their capability demands.
This motivates clustering queries into subsets that share similar underlying capability demands, each corresponding to a distinct capability profile, which can enable a capability-aware LLM evaluation
[
48
,
15
]
.

Standard query “clustering” methods typically rely either on static and heuristic human-labeled semantic taxonomies, such as “Mathematics” or “Chemistry”
[
54
,
13
]
, or on unsupervised grouping based on semantic embeddings
[
23
,
29
]
.
While intuitive,
such surface-level semantic clusters often fail to capture the true latent capability requirements of queries
(Figure
1
):
(i) A single label (e.g., “Mathematics”) may encompass queries requiring vastly different levels of underlying capabilities, from rote recall to multi-step deductive derivation, or even a combination of distinct capability requirements.
(ii) Conversely, queries requiring the same underlying capabilities, such as multi-step deductive derivation, may be scattered across different semantic clusters due to superficial topical differences.
This misalignment with the true capability distributions can limit, or even degrade, the generalizability of model capability estimates to unseen queries (Figure
2
), highlighting the insufficiency of prior semantic signals.
Although recent works leverage “LLM-as-a-judge”
[
55
]
to extract explicit skill labels from model responses and build capability structures
[
30
,
44
]
, the resulting partitions of queries are typically hard and discrete, and thus do not naturally accommodate queries that require a complex mixture of capabilities.
Furthermore, these approaches rely on exhaustively evaluating each query across numerous models to determine cluster assignments, which incurs substantial computational cost with LLM-based judges.

These fundamental limitations motivate us to propose
E
vidence-
C
alibrated
C
lustering
(
ECC
), a novel algorithm that efficiently learns underlying capability-aware cluster structure by directly incorporating evidence of model performance, which we term
posterior evidence
, into the clustering process.
Rather than relying solely on
prior
semantic embeddings,
ECC
incorporates limited pairwise model comparisons over query responses as
sample-efficient
signals of LLM capabilities, characterizing each cluster with a capability profile parameterized by a Bradley-Terry model
[
5
]
optimized over these comparisons.
To accommodate queries requiring mixed capabilities,
ECC
introduces trainable
soft responsibilities
to weight the contribution of query embeddings and associated model comparisons across clusters, quantifying how strongly each query aligns with the capabilities represented by each cluster.
To reconcile prior semantics with posterior evidence,
ECC
jointly optimizes cluster centroids, capability profiles, and soft responsibilities through alternating updates.

For unseen queries,
ECC
employs
probe-informed mixture inference
, which leverages a single probe signal (i.e., a single pairwise model comparison) alongside the query embedding to derive a query-specific, capability-aware ranking over LLMs (see
Section

2
).

We validate the effectiveness of
ECC
through extensive quantitative experiments across diverse benchmarks, demonstrating average improvements in ranking quality on unseen queries of
17.64
percentage points (pp) over human-labeled clustering and
18.02
pp over embedding-only clustering.
We analyze the clusterings qualitatively to confirm that incorporating our posterior evidence yields interpretable capability-aware clusters, separating semantically similar queries with divergent capability needs and merging those with aligned needs.
Finally, we apply

to two representative downstream tasks:
(1) guided optimal query routing, where it improves the response quality of routed queries by
16.6%
on average over embedding-only clustering, and
(2) sample-efficient new model ranking, where it achieves
16.3

In summary, our contributions are:

1.

We propose
ECC
, a novel query clustering algorithm that integrates posterior model comparisons with prior embeddings to overcome the misalignment between surface-level semantics and latent capability requirements.

2.

By characterizing each cluster with a capability profile parameterized by a Bradley-Terry model,
ECC
enables sample-efficient estimation of relative model strengths from limited pairwise comparisons.

3.

ECC
captures the mixed capability requirements of queries by introducing trainable responsibilities, yielding a flexible, capability-aware clustering structure that enables query-specific inference of LLM capabilities.

4.

Extensive quantitative and qualitative evaluations show that
ECC
produces more capability-coherent clusters and significantly improves capability-aware ranking quality, outperforming baselines in two downstream tasks.

1.2
Bradley-Terry Model for LLM Ranking.

The Bradley-Terry (BT) model is a widely used framework for estimating latent strength scores of items from pairwise comparison data
[
5
]
.
In the context of LLM evaluation, it has recently been adopted to rank a set of language models from limited
pairwise comparisons over their responses to given queries
[
7
]
.
Formally, let
\mathcal{Q}
denote a set of queries and let there be
M
different LLMs. For each query
q\in\mathcal{Q}
, we assume access to a set of pairwise model comparisons
\mathcal{N}_{q}=\{(i,j,y)\}
, where
i,j\in[M]
refer to two LLMs
m_{i}
and
m_{j}
, and
y\in\{0,1\}
denotes the comparison outcome over their responses to query
q
, with
y=1
indicating that
m_{j}
is preferred over
m_{i}
, and
y=0
otherwise.
In the BT model, the probability that
m_{j}
is preferred over
m_{i}
is defined using the logistic function
\sigma(\cdot)
:

P_{\bm{\theta}}(m_{j}\succ m_{i})=\sigma(\theta_{j}-\theta_{i})=\frac{1}{1+e^{-(\theta_{j}-\theta_{i})}},

(1)

where
\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
denotes the vector of latent strengths for the
M
models.
To learn
\bm{\theta}
, we minimize the BT model loss (neg. log-likelihood) over all pairwise comparisons across all queries:

\ell(\bm{\theta}):=-\sum_{q\in\mathcal{Q}}\sum_{(i,j,y)\in\mathcal{N}_{q}}\log P(y\mid i,j;\bm{\theta})
,
where the log-probability
\log P(y\mid i,j;\bm{\theta})
is defined as
y\log\sigma(\theta_{j}-\theta_{i})+(1-y)\log\sigma(\theta_{i}-\theta_{j}).

Capability-Aware Ranking Quality.

We quantify the quality of a learned model capability ranking
\bm{\theta}
for a query
q
by how well it explains the observed pairwise comparison outcomes, operationalized via the per-query BT loss:

\ell_{\text{comp}}(q;\bm{\theta})=-\frac{1}{|\mathcal{N}_{q}|}\sum_{(i,j,y)\in\mathcal{N}_{q}}\log P(y\mid i,j;\bm{\theta}).

(2)

Lower values of
\ell_{\text{comp}}(q;\bm{\theta})
indicate a better fit to the observed evidence and thus higher ranking quality.
Crucially, this metric is probability-aware: it evaluates not only the ordinal consistency of the ranking with observed comparisons but also the predictive confidence
[
11
]
.

2
Methodology

The main intuition behind
E
vidence-
C
alibrated
C
lustering
(
ECC
) stems from a key limitation in existing query clustering methods that only rely on prior semantic embeddings: posterior evidence with informative model capability signals, such as pairwise model comparisons, is only used after clustering.
This creates a fundamental gap: capability evidence cannot influence cluster formation, resulting in semantically coherent clusters that may still be misaligned with latent capability demands.

ECC
instead
evaluates first, then clusters
, where it first collects comparison evidence on unclustered queries and then uses it to calibrate and reshape the embedding-based semantic structure.
Concretely, given an input number of clusters
K
, for each query
q\in\mathcal{Q}
,
ECC
jointly leverages its semantic embedding
\bm{x}_{q}
and its associated pairwise comparisons
\mathcal{N}_{q}
to learn a flexible latent capability clustering that enables reliable query-specific capability inference of LLMs.

System Parameters
\bm{\Theta}
and Responsibilities
\mathcal{P}
.

To achieve this,
ECC
characterizes each specific cluster
k\in[K]
using two complementary components:
(i) a
semantic centroid

\bm{c}_{k}
, capturing the prior semantic signals in the embedding space,
and (ii) a
capability profile

\bm{\theta}^{(k)}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
parameterizing a BT model that represents the posterior capability strengths of the
M
LLMs on queries associated with the cluster.
Together, these define the overall
system parameters
:
\bm{\Theta}=\{\bm{\theta}^{(k)},\bm{c}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
.

Since a single query may involve multiple capability demands,
ECC
adopts soft assignments rather than hard clustering by introducing a set of
responsibilities

\mathcal{P}=\{r_{qk}\}
.
For each query
q
, the responsibilities satisfy
r_{qk}\geq 0
and
\sum_{k=1}^{K}r_{qk}=1
, where
r_{qk}
measures how strongly
q
aligns with the capability profile represented by cluster
k
.
Together, these cluster-level profiles define a
fine-grained capability structure
: each cluster serves as a basic capability unit, and each query forms its own capability representation by softly combining these units through its responsibilities.

Alternating Optimization.

Unlike purely embedding-based clustering methods (e.g., K-Means
[
27
]
) that update only semantic centroids,
ECC
follows a two-stage alternating optimization procedure, where it iteratively updates (i) the system parameters
\bm{\Theta}
and (ii) the soft responsibilities
\mathcal{P}
, allowing both semantic priors and posterior evidence to jointly shape the resulting clusters.

Optimization Objective
.
For each query
q
and cluster
k
, we define a
combined loss
:

\ell(q,k;\bm{\Theta}):=\ell_{\text{comp}}(q;\bm{\theta}^{(k)})+\lambda\ell_{\text{emb}}(q;\bm{c}_{k}),

(3)

where
\ell_{\text{comp}}(q;\bm{\theta}^{(k)})
measures how well the capability profile of cluster
k
fits the pairwise comparisons of
q
(see
Equation

2
), and
\ell_{\text{emb}}(q;\bm{c}_{k}):=-\bm{x}_{q}^{\top}\bm{c}_{k}
measures the semantic mismatch between the query embedding and the cluster centroid.
The trade-off parameter
\lambda
balances the influence of the posterior evidence and the prior embedding signal. Instead of directly minimizing
Equation

3
via hard assignments that allocate each query to a single cluster,
ECC
adopts a soft optimization approach.
It jointly optimizes the system parameters
\bm{\Theta}
and soft responsibilities
\mathcal{P}
by minimizing the following
entropy-regularized expected loss
:

\mathcal{L}({\mathcal{P}},\bm{\Theta}):=\sum_{q\in\mathcal{Q}}\sum_{k=1}^{K}r_{qk}{\ell(q,k;\bm{\Theta})}-\mathcal{T}\sum_{q\in\mathcal{Q}}H(\bm{r}_{q}),

(4)

where
H(\bm{r}_{q})=-\sum_{k=1}^{K}r_{qk}\log r_{qk}
is the Shannon entropy of the assignment distribution, and temperature
\mathcal{T}>0
controls the strength of the entropy regularization.

\mathcal{P}

\min_{\bm{\Theta}}\sum_{q,k}r_{qk}\ell(q,k;\bm{\Theta})

For each cluster

\bm{c}_{k}

\bm{\theta}^{(k)}

\bm{\theta}^{(k)}=\operatorname*{\argmin}_{\bm{\theta}}\sum_{q\in\mathcal{Q}}{r_{qk}}\ell_{\text{comp}}(q;\bm{\theta}).

The responsibilities enable each query to inform multiple clusters to varying degrees, which is essential for modeling queries that require multiple capabilities.

Stage II: Updating
\mathcal{P}
given
\bm{\Theta}

In the second stage,
ECC
updates
\mathcal{P}
while keeping the current system parameters
\bm{\Theta}

\bm{r}_{q}

r_{qk}\leftarrow\frac{\exp\left(-\ell(q,k;\bm{\Theta})/\mathcal{T}\right)}{\sum_{j=1}^{K}\exp\left(-\ell(q,j;\bm{\Theta})/\mathcal{T}\right)}.

(5)

Convergence
.

ECC
terminates at iteration
t
when the responsibilities and centroids stabilize: the average KL divergence between successive responsibilities
\frac{1}{|\mathcal{Q}|}\sum_{q}\mathrm{KL}(\bm{r}_{q}^{(t)}\|\bm{r}_{q}^{(t-1)})
and the centroid shift
\max_{k}\|\bm{c}_{k}^{(t)}-\bm{c}_{k}^{(t-1)}\|_{2}
fall below predefined thresholds.

Probe-Informed Mixture Inference.

Since
ECC
learns weighted mixture capability clusters, inferring LLM capabilities for a new query
q
first requires estimating its soft assignment
\bm{r}_{q}
across clusters.
A naive approach is to infer
\bm{r}_{q}
using embeddings alone; however, semantic proximity does not reliably imply capability proximity.
Furthermore, this embedding-only inference ignores the fact that the learned cluster structure is jointly shaped by both embeddings and comparison evidence during clustering, resulting in a signal mismatch between clustering and inference.
To reconcile this mismatch in a lightweight manner, we introduce a single
probe
signal during inference:
for each query, we randomly collect
only one pairwise model comparison
and use it to calibrate
\bm{r}_{q}
via the same entropy-regularized update as in
Equations

3
and
5
, with
\ell_{\text{comp}}
computed on the probe only.
Given the calibrated
\bm{r}_{q}
,
ECC
estimates pairwise preferences using the following weighted mixture:

P(m_{j}\succ m_{i}\mid q)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}r_{qk}\sigma\left(\bm{\theta}^{(k)}_{j}-\bm{\theta}^{(k)}_{i}\right).

(6)

Although it only costs a single comparison, the probe provides high-leverage capability evidence, with its effectiveness empirically demonstrated in
Figure

3
. We prove the following convergence guarantee in Appendix
Appendix

C
:

Theorem 2.1

(Monotone convergence of
ECC
)
.

Assume that the objective
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{P},\bm{\Theta})
is finite at initialization.
Then, the objective values
\{\mathcal{L}^{(t)}\}_{t\geq 0}
produced by
ECC
are monotonically non-increasing:

\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{P}^{(t+1)},\bm{\Theta}^{(t+1)})\leq\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{P}^{(t)},\bm{\Theta}^{(t)})
.
Moreover,
\{\mathcal{L}^{(t)}\}
converges to a finite limit.

Benchmarks.

We evaluate on three widely used multi-LLM benchmarks:
i) SPROUT
[
37
]
,
ii) RouterBench
[
17
]
,
and iii) Open LLM Leaderboard v2
[
10
]
.
The queries in these benchmarks span diverse datasets, including MMLU
[
15
]
, MMLU-Pro
[
48
]
, and MATH
[
16
]
.
For each benchmark, all model responses are scored by the same judge to ensure consistency.
We split queries into clustering/test sets with varying clustering proportions from 20% to 90%, using 80% by default.
For each query, we decompose the full set of response scores into pairwise comparisons between models, using non-tied comparisons by default and considering tie-aware variants in the ablation study.
During clustering, we sample a varying number of model comparisons per query (from 2 to 24), with 7 used by default, and vary the number of clusters from 2 to 100, using 30 as the default.
For each test query, unless otherwise specified, we hold out one comparison as a probe for responsibility inference and evaluate on the remaining comparisons.
We use
bge-base-en-v1.5

[
51
]
for query embeddings, and fix
\lambda=3
and
\mathcal{T}=0.1
unless otherwise specified.

Baselines.

We consider the following clustering baselines:
(i)
Human-labeled clustering
, which applies hard cluster partitions using the standard human-annotated taxonomies already provided by the benchmarks;
(ii)
Embedding-only clustering (Emb-only)
, which uses only semantic embeddings for clustering, following the same soft update rule as
ECC
.
Both baselines represent prior semantic clustering structures.
We additionally consider a variant of
ECC
,
Comparison-only (Comp-only)
, which uses only comparison signals by setting
\lambda=0
.
For Emb-only, Comp-only, and
ECC
, we use the same mixture ranking inference in
Section

2
for a fair comparison.
We also include Prompt-to-Leaderboard (P2L)
[
11
]
as a non-clustering baseline.

Inference Signals.

Given the learned clusters, we consider three inference signals for estimating the LLM ranking for a new query:
(i)
Emb-infer
, which only uses the embedding signal of the query;
(ii)
Probe-infer
, which only uses a probe model comparison of the query;
(iii)
Emb+Probe-infer
, which combines both signals.
Emb+Probe-infer is used as the default unless stated otherwise.

Metrics.

We use two main metrics to evaluate different clustering methods:

(1) Clustering performance.

We evaluate the effectiveness of a capability clustering structure via its impact on
capability-aware ranking quality
over unseen queries.
Specifically, we report the
ranking quality gains
, defined as the average reduction in per-query BT loss (
Equation

2
) on pair-wise model comparisons from unseen queries, relative to a single global BT model trained without clustering.

(2) Hard partition structure analysis.

To evaluate the structural properties of the hard cluster partitions (obtained by assigning each query to the cluster with the largest responsibility), we report
Kendall-
\tau
to measure the consistency among the BT profiles across clusters, as well as
Adjusted Rand Index (ARI)
and
Normalized Mutual Information (NMI)
to quantify the agreement between two different partitions.
Additional experimental details are in
Appendix

.

3.1
Main Results

We compare different clustering methods across three inference signals, evaluating their performance on unseen queries with diverse capability demands, and address the following questions.

RQ1: How does
ECC
compare to human-labeled clustering?

We evaluate on three datasets with complete human-defined taxonomies:
(i) MMLU with 57 subject categories,
(ii) MMLU-Pro with 14 domain labels, and
(iii) MATH with 7 problem types.
For human-labeled clustering, we directly assign each query to its benchmark-provided ground-truth category and compute capability-aware ranking using the BT model trained within that category; thus, no additional test-time probe is needed for assignment.
For
ECC
and embedding-only clustering, we use Emb+Probe-infer to compute query-to-cluster responsibilities and induce mixture rankings.
For fairness, we set the number of clusters for
ECC
and embedding-only clustering to match the taxonomy granularity of each dataset.
As shown in Figure
2
,
ECC
consistently achieves the highest capability-aware ranking quality gains on all three datasets, improving over human-labeled clustering by
17.64
pp and over embedding-only clustering by
17.99
pp on average.
Notably, despite using ground-truth semantic categories, human-defined taxonomies do not reliably reflect the underlying capability structure, yielding only marginal gains over the global BT model, and even degrading quality on MMLU.
These results underscore the limitations of both human-labeled and embedding-only clustering, and demonstrate that
ECC
effectively learns cluster structures aligned with the latent capability demands of queries, enabling more reliable capability inference for unseen queries.

RQ2: How does the choice of clustering signals affect capability-aware ranking quality?

To evaluate the impact of clustering signals, we compare
ECC
, embedding-only, and comparison-only clustering across benchmarks.
As shown in Figure
3
, on SPROUT,
ECC
consistently achieves the best ranking quality across inference signals (additional results for other benchmarks are provided in
Figure

7
).
Compared to embedding-only clustering, Table
1
shows that with
K{=}30
,
ECC
improves ranking quality gains by an average of
15.40
pp under Probe-infer, and
18.02
pp under Emb+Probe-infer across benchmarks.
Even when inference relies solely on embeddings (Emb-infer),
ECC
still outperforms embedding-only clustering.
The results highlight the limitations of relying on prior embeddings alone in clustering and the advantages of incorporating posterior comparison evidence during clustering.
Notably, comparison-only clustering performs well only when probe signals are available, but degrades significantly under Emb-infer.
This sensitivity reveals a signal mismatch: clusters learned purely from comparison evidence do not provide a reliable capability inference when the inference signal relies on embeddings alone.
By integrating embeddings with comparison evidence,
ECC
produces a capability-aligned clustering structure that remains robust across inference signals, and outperforms comparison-only clustering under full-signal inference.
These results suggest that embeddings serve as a
complementary
semantic prior to comparison evidence, enabling more accurate and robust alignment with diverse query capability demands.

RQ3: How does the choice of inference signals affect capability-aware ranking quality?

Holding the clustering method fixed and varying the inference signal, Figure

4
Conclusion and Limitations

We present
ECC
, which calibrates prior semantic embeddings using limited posterior model comparisons to bridge the gap between surface-level semantics and latent capability alignment.
By characterizing clusters via capability profiles and introducing trainable responsibilities,
ECC
accommodates queries with mixed capability demands, yielding a flexible, capability-aware clustering structure for query-specific LLM capability inference.
We validate
ECC
through extensive quantitative and qualitative evaluations, demonstrating significant improvements in ranking quality and confirming its effectiveness in downstream tasks.

Our paper uses text-only queries and does not evaluate performance on multimodal inputs.
However,
ECC
makes no assumptions about the embedding modality and can, in principle, accommodate multimodal query embeddings without changing its main clustering and inference procedures.
We also cluster queries using only the standalone text of each query, without considering potential conversational context or historical context.
Extending our method to context-aware settings is an interesting direction for future work.

Appendix A
Experimental Details

Benchmarks.

We use three widely used benchmarks:
i) SPROUT
[
37
]
, which covers 13 LLMs evaluated on a range of curated prompts;
ii) RouterBench
[
17
]
, which includes the responses from 11 different LLMs over diverse domain-specific queries;
iii) Open LLM Leaderboard v2
[
10
]
, collects the queries and corresponding responses from 16 LLMs.
These benchmarks span multiple datasets from diverse sources, including MMLU
[
15
]
, MMLU-Pro
[
48
]
, and MATH
[
16
]
.
For each benchmark, all model responses are scored by the same powerful LLM-based evaluator to ensure consistency.
We split the queries into clustering and evaluation sets with varying proportions (ranging from 20% to 90% for clustering), using a default split of 80% for clustering and 20% for evaluation.
For each query, we decompose the full set of response scores into pairwise comparisons between models, using non-tied comparisons by default and considering tie-aware variants in the ablation study.
During clustering, we sample a varying number of comparison pairs (from 2 to 24), with 7 pairs per query used by default.
We vary the number of clusters from 2 to 100, using 30 as the default.
For each test query, unless otherwise specified, we hold out one model comparison as a probe for responsibility inference and evaluate on the remaining comparisons.
In the main setting, query embeddings are generated using
bge-base-en-v1.5

[
51
]
.
We additionally evaluate
SFR-Embedding-2_R

[
28
]
and

text-embedding-3-large

[
34
]
.
For both embedding and response generation, we use only the user query rather than the full input with the system prompt, avoiding contamination from additional system instructions and ensuring that embeddings reflect the query semantics.
Unless otherwise specified, we fix
\lambda
as 3 and
\mathcal{T}
as 0.1 for
ECC
.
For convergence, we set the threshold for the average KL divergence between successive responsibilities to
10^{-3}
, and the threshold for centroid stability to
10^{-4}
.

Baselines.

We consider two baseline clustering methods:
(i)
Human-labeled clustering
, which applies hard cluster assignments based on static, predefined taxonomies from human annotations;
(ii)
Embedding-only clustering
, which uses only the semantic embeddings of queries for clustering and follows the same soft-assignment update style as
ECC
.
Both baselines represent prior semantic clustering structures.
We additionally consider a variant of
ECC
,
Comparison-only (Comp-only)
, which follows the same optimization procedure but excludes the embedding signal by setting
\lambda=0
.
For human-labeled clustering, we directly use the ground-truth labels as the cluster assignments for each query.
Since each benchmark aggregates multiple datasets, some of which lack human annotations, we restrict our comparison to representative datasets that are internally consistent, drawn from the same source, and fully labeled.
Specifically, we use three such datasets: (i) MMLU, (ii) MMLU-Pro, and (iii) MATH, all of which contain human-defined labels for every query.
For embedding-only clustering, we iteratively compute responsibilities via a temperature-controlled softmax over the embedding-centroid similarities, and update each centroid as the posterior-weighted mean
\bm{c}_{k}\leftarrow\mathrm{norm}\left(\sum_{q}r_{qk}\bm{x}_{q}/\sum_{q}r_{qk}\right)
.
Furthermore, we use the same temperature parameter
\mathcal{T}
as in
ECC
, and terminate the process when the maximum centroid shift falls below a fixed threshold.
When comparing clustering methods (Emb-only, Comp-only, and
ECC
), we use the same mixture ranking inference procedure as described in
Section

2
for a fair comparison.

Additionally, we consider Prompt-to-Leaderboard (P2L)
[
11
]
, which trains an LLM-based predictor on queries and their pairwise model comparisons to predict query-conditioned BT coefficients and induce per-query leaderboards.
We implement P2L following the training objective and protocol described in
[
11
]
.
For each benchmark, we train
Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct

[
43
]
using 4 NVIDIA H200 GPUs, with a learning rate of
8\times 10^{-6}
and batch size of 4.
We use the same set of training queries as for other baselines, with each query associated with 7 pairwise comparisons, and evaluate on the same held-out queries and comparison pairs.

Inference Signals.

Given the learned clusters, we consider three inference signals for estimating the LLM capability ranking for a new query:
(i)
Emb-infer
, which only uses the embedding signal of the query;
(ii)
Probe-infer
, which only uses a probe model comparison of the query;
(iii)
Emb+Probe-infer
, which combines both signals.
We use the Emb+Probe-infer signal as the default inference method, unless otherwise specified.
Notably, all three inference settings follow the same posterior-weighted mixture ranking procedure described in
Section

2
, where they differ only in the information used to infer the query-specific responsibilities.

Metrics.

We use two main metrics to evaluate different clustering methods:

(1) Clustering performance.

We evaluate the effectiveness of a capability clustering structure via its impact on
capability-aware ranking quality
over unseen queries.
Specifically, we report the
ranking quality gains
, defined as the average reduction in per-query BT loss (
Equation

2
) on pair-wise model comparisons from unseen queries, relative to a single global BT model trained without clustering.
A larger improvement indicates better alignment between the clustering structure and the underlying capability distribution.

(2) Hard partition structure analysis.

To evaluate the structural properties of the hard cluster partitions (obtained by assigning each query to the cluster with the highest responsibility), we report
Kendall-
\tau
to measure the consistency among the BT profiles across different clusters, indicating whether the clustering leads to coherent and distinct capability orderings, as well as
Adjusted Rand Index (ARI)
and
Normalized Mutual Information (NMI)
to quantify the agreement between two different query partitions.
We also report the ranking quality gap, as the average absolute difference between per-query BT losses under two rankings evaluated over the same queries.

B.1
RQ4: How do different clustering methods trade off within-cluster consistency and across-cluster separation in their induced hard partitions?

We first evaluate the within-cluster consistency of the induced hard partitions across clustering methods.
Specifically, for each hard cluster, we randomly split its queries into 5 folds, fit an independent BT model using only the queries in each fold, and measure agreement between the resulting BT profiles (rankings) by averaging pairwise Kendall-
\tau
over all fold pairs.
We repeat this procedure 10 times with different random splits and report an overall within-cluster consistency score by averaging across clusters.
As shown in Figure
8
, (induced hard) clusters trained with comparison signals (comparison-only clustering and
ECC
) exhibit consistently higher within-cluster profile consistency across all benchmarks than embedding-only clustering.
In contrast, embedding-only clustering yields lower within-cluster consistency, suggesting that the resulting clusters are less capability-coherent and tend to mix queries with heterogeneous capability demands, thereby making the cluster-specific BT profiles less stable across query subsets.

To further analyze cluster structure and separability, we compute two cross-cluster measures.
First, for each
K
, we compute Kendall-
\tau
between the BT profiles of every pair of hard clusters, and report the mean as the inter-cluster BT profile similarity with the shaded region showing the 25th–75th percentiles of these pairwise similarities.
Second, for each target hard cluster, we compute a cross-cluster ranking quality gap by evaluating queries of that cluster under (i) BT models trained on all other clusters (and averaging the resulting ranking qualities) and (ii) the BT model trained on the same cluster.
The gap is the difference between these two qualities, with larger values indicating better separation.
For each
K
, we report the mean gap across clusters, with the shaded region showing the 25th–75th percentiles of the gaps across clusters.
Figure
4
shows that embedding-only clustering produces substantially higher inter-cluster BT profile similarity and smaller ranking quality gaps across
K
, indicating a weak separation, where queries that require similar underlying capabilities may be scattered across multiple clusters, with different clusters end up inducing similar BT profiles, thus providing limited improvement in distinguishing and inferring capability-specific performance across LLMs.
In contrast, comparison-only clustering and
ECC
yield both lower inter-cluster similarity and larger ranking quality gaps across all benchmarks, suggesting that the induced partitions are better separated in capability space.

Overall, these results underscore the importance of incorporating posterior model-comparison evidence during clustering, which helps calibrate the obtained cluster structure to be more coherent within clusters and more separable across clusters, better reflecting the underlying capability distribution across queries.

B.10
Sample-Efficient New Model Ranking

One important downstream application is to efficiently place a newly arrived model into an existing model ranking under a limited comparison budget.
We evaluate sample efficiency by measuring how accurately each clustering method ranks a held-out new model as a function of the number of queried comparisons (one new-model comparison per selected query).
Concretely, each clustering method first learns query clusters using only pairwise comparisons among the existing (old) models.
Given the learned clusters, we actively acquire comparisons involving the new model: for each selected query, we use its cluster responsibilities to select the median-ranked old model under the corresponding capability-aware ranking and query the pairwise comparison between the new model and this opponent.
After collecting a fixed budget of such comparisons, we keep the clusters learned from old-model comparisons fixed and update only the BT models by incorporating the acquired new-model comparisons.
We then evaluate on held-out new-model comparisons, and report the ranking quality gains relative to a single global BT model trained with the same old-model comparisons and the same new-model comparison budget but without clustering.

We vary the new-model comparison budget from 10 to 100 and treat each model in turn as the new model, while limiting the number of comparisons among the old models to at most 3 per query.
We report the median ranking quality gains over new-model runs on three benchmarks.
As shown in Figure
20
,
ECC
achieves substantial ranking quality gains under small budgets, consistently outperforming embedding-only clustering, and is comparable to comparison-only clustering, demonstrating its superior effectiveness in leveraging limited comparison evidence for new-model ranking.
Notably, ranking quality gains remain relatively stable as the budget increases across all methods.
With a 100-comparison budget,
ECC
achieves
16.3
pp higher new-model ranking quality than embedding-only clustering on average.
This is expected: additional comparisons benefit both the global BT model and clustering methods, thus the relative advantage persists.

\displaystyle=\sum_{q\in\mathcal{Q}}\frac{r_{qk}}{|\mathcal{N}_{q}|}\sum_{(i,j,y)\in\mathcal{N}_{q}}-\log P(y\mid i,j;\bm{\theta}^{(k)})

Appendix D
Discussion

D.1
Reducing Tied Comparisons

In practice, tied comparisons can arise when two models perform similarly or when the evaluator is uncertain.
Since ties provide weaker discriminative evidence than strict wins or losses and may reflect evaluator noise
[
55
]
, including them can dilute capability-aware signals.
Tie frequency can be reduced by using stronger evaluators or stricter judge prompts that reserve the tie option for cases where two responses are genuinely indistinguishable.

D.2
Evaluation Judges

Human judgments are an important source of preference signals in LLM evaluation
[
7
]
.
In practice, however, they are often collected from multiple annotators with heterogeneous preferences and varying reliability, which can introduce noise and systematic bias into pairwise comparisons.
Large-scale human evaluation is also substantially more expensive than automated evaluation.
Therefore, for each benchmark, we use its designated LLM-based judge or fixed evaluation protocol to obtain consistent pairwise signals within that benchmark and avoid annotator-dependent variability.

D.3
Evaluation Efficiency of
ECC

ECC
is lightweight and does not require dense compute (e.g., GPU training
[
11
]
).
Its optimization alternates between (i) updating cluster-specific BT capability profiles and (ii) updating soft responsibilities, both of which are efficient CPU operations.
In our main setting with
K=30
,
ECC
converges within 32 iterations on SPROUT and RouterBench, and within 29 iterations on Open LLM Leaderboard.
Furthermore, fitting BT models from limited pairwise comparisons is substantially cheaper than training additional neural components with gradient-based optimization.
The main efficiency benefit of
ECC
lies in reducing evaluation cost.
Whereas some existing methods rely on exhaustively judging responses from all models for every query,
ECC
only requires a small subset of model outputs and a limited number of pairwise comparisons per query.
As shown in
Figure

11
, using around three comparisons per query already yields strong clustering performance.
While
ECC
may require one probe signal for an unseen query (i.e., generating two model responses and performing one pairwise comparison), this overhead is
O(1)
per query and does not scale with the number of candidate LLMs (
M
).
Moreover, since the probe signal is robust to the choice of probing models (Table
2
), this probe can be obtained using lightweight models when evaluation cost is a concern.
Thus,
ECC
provides an evidence-efficient approach to LLM capability evaluation compared with methods that require substantially more model outputs or pairwise judgments.

Appendix E
Extended Related Work

Capability-Aware Clustering for LLM Evaluation.

Recent work has moved beyond static, human-defined taxonomies and curated benchmark groupings
[
45
,
52
,
24
,
40
,
3
]
toward inducing latent capability structure from evaluation data for more fine-grained analysis
[
44
,
30
,
49
,
26
,
31
]
.

44
introduces SkillVerse, a diagnosis framework that utilizes an “LLM-as-a-Judge” to critique responses and organize them into a hierarchical skill dendrogram, revealing granular capabilities often missed by human labeling.

30
extracts latent task attributes, such as subtasks and domains, from model evaluation data, and uses them as signals to inform unsupervised clustering of prompts.
On a related vein,
49
proposes KCluster, which leverages an LLM-induced question congruity metric to cluster assessment items into latent knowledge components, yielding clusters that predict student and model performance more accurately than expert-defined categories.
However, these methods typically induce capability structure through
explicit
skill or attribute representations for diagnosis or performance prediction.
In contrast,
ECC
does not rely on explicit skill extraction; instead, it directly integrates limited posterior evidence from pairwise model comparisons with prior semantic embeddings to learn
soft
capability-aligned clusters tailored to LLM ranking over diverse queries.
Furthermore, whereas prior work often yields discrete partitions that support group-level analysis once the grouping is fixed,
ECC
learns soft capability-aligned clusters that support
query-conditional
inference over LLM capabilities, enabling stronger adaptation and generalization under limited comparison evidence.

Sample-Efficient LLM Evaluation.

Modern LLM evaluation is costly as a comprehensive assessment requires scoring all LLMs over thousands of benchmark records
[
22
,
7
]
.
To reduce evaluation cost, recent work has developed sample-efficient methods based on adaptive item selection, benchmark compression, and comparison-based ranking
[
22
,
20
,
7
,
38
,
39
]
.
For instance, Chatbot Arena
[
7
]
collects crowdsourced pairwise preferences and fits Bradley-Terry models
[
5
]
to produce scalable global leaderboards without exhaustively evaluating every model on every prompt.
In parallel, Item Response Theory (IRT) provides a principled measurement framework for improving sample efficiency
[
4
]
.

39
proposed IRT-Router, which models latent model ability and query difficulty to support cost-effective multi-LLM routing, improving over semantic-routing baselines.
ATLAS
[
22
]
uses IRT-based adaptive testing (e.g., Fisher-information-guided item selection) to estimate model ability accurately with far fewer benchmark questions, while Metabench
[
20
]
leverages redundancy in large-scale leaderboard data to distill sparse benchmarks that preserve score estimates.
Furthermore, Arena-Lite
[
38
]
improves ranking reliability under limited budgets by organizing LLM-judge comparisons in a tournament structure, reducing the number of required comparisons.
However, most prior work primarily targets sample-efficient estimation of
global
model scores or leaderboards, which is a single ranking shared across all queries.
In contrast, we target
capability-aware
ranking under heterogeneous query demands:
ECC
learns latent capability clusters and performs per-query posterior inference to produce a
query-specific
mixture ranking over LLMs.
This enables fine-grained adaptation across queries while remaining effective under limited comparison budgets.

Query Routing
.
Recent work studies how to match heterogeneous queries to the most suitable model under quality-cost constraints.
A common line of approaches invokes multiple models for each query, either by ensembling or reranking candidate responses
[

,

or by cascading models from cheaper to more expensive ones until a stopping criterion is met

,

,

]
.
For instance, LLM-Blender
[
18
]
combines and reranks responses from multiple LLMs to produce a final output, and Frugal-GPT
[
6
]
sequentially queries models in increasing capability to reduce cost while maintaining quality.
Another line trains a router on historical or auxiliary data to estimate the performance and cost of each query and select the optimal LLM accordingly
[
33
,
37
,
17
,
8
,
25
,
50
,
36
,
9
,
14
,
47
,
41
,

]
.
For example, RouterLLM
[
33
]
trains a lightweight BERT model on preference-labeled data to improve selection accuracy, and CARROT
[
37
]
learns predictors of both quality and cost to support flexible trade-offs.
RadialRouter
[
19
]
proposes a lightweight Transformer backbone, RadialFormer, to model query-LLM relationships for routing, while GraphRouter
[
9
]
uses a heterogeneous graph to capture interactions between query requirements and model capabilities.
These routing methods typically learn a per-query selector, whereas
ECC
learns a capability-aware clustering structure and outputs a query-specific mixture ranking over models, with routing as a special case (selecting the top-ranked model) while enabling broader capability-aware evaluation tasks.
One recent work
[

]
introduces Prompt-to-Leaderboard (P2L), which trains an LLM-based predictor on human-annotated pairwise comparisons to directly estimate query-conditioned BT coefficients, thereby inducing a per-query LLM leaderboard.
In contrast,
ECC
differs fundamentally in both goal and methodology. P2L focuses on a specific application that learns an amortized black-box predictor for per-query leaderboard generation (e.g., for query routing), without modeling any interpretable latent capability structure across queries.

ECC
, on the other hand, aims to uncover a fundamental capability-aware clustering structure that reveals how queries relate based on underlying capability demands – a structure that is both interpretable and broadly applicable to multiple downstream tasks, including query routing and new model ranking.
Furthermore, P2L requires costly model training, whereas
ECC
is training-free and efficient, and executable on commodity hardware such as CPUs.

Appendix F
Broader Impact

This paper advances machine learning by proposing a clustering framework that enables more fine-grained, capability-aware LLM ranking across queries with diverse capability requirements.
As a methodological contribution, it introduces limited direct societal risk.
By providing more reliable LLM rankings, it can help users choose models that better match their needs, improving downstream application outcomes for a broader range of users.
