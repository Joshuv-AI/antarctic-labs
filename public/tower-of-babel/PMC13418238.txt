Dual spectroscopy technique for vapor-phase chlorides and iodine detection in nuclear systems
Authors: Rachelle Austin, Garrett LeCroy, Ruchi Gakhar, Robin Roper, Jessica Meiers, Ammon Williams
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The need for efficient, real-time monitoring of vapor-phase fission products in nuclear systems is critical for advancing nuclear technologies. This study explores a dual-spectroscopy technique, combining ultraviolet-visible (UV-vis) absorbance and laser-induced breakdown spectroscopy (LIBS), to detect and quantify vapor-phase chlorides and molecular iodine under high-temperature conditions. Using a custom-built high-temperature optical cell, we successfully identified and quantified iodine (I2), niobium pentachloride (NbCl5), molybdenum pentachloride (MoCl5), and zirconium tetrachloride (ZrCl4) in both pure and mixed states. Our results demonstrate the robustness of this dual-spectroscopy approach for real-time chemical and elemental monitoring, supporting its application in molten salt reactors (MSRs) and other advanced nuclear technologies. This work establishes a technique for improved safeguards and material accountancy in nuclear systems.

Introduction

As the global energy sector seeks sustainable, low-carbon solutions, advanced nuclear technologies gain renewed attention for their potential to provide reliable power with minimal environmental impact. Central to this vision is the development of efficient, proliferation-resistant fuel cycle strategies that can reduce long-term waste and enhance resource utilization. Recycling approaches, such as aqueous and pyroprocessing methods, play a crucial role in closing the fuel cycle by recovering valuable materials and minimizing waste. The chloride-based volatility (CBV) process is an emerging method for separating uranium from used nuclear fuel (UNF), offering a solvent-free, thermally driven pathway for actinide recovery by leveraging the differential volatility of metal chlorides at elevated temperatures to selectively volatilize uranium and other actinides, potentially reducing repository burdens by an order of magnitude.1 This approach holds promise for advanced nuclear fuel cycle strategies, as well as on-line monitoring of molten-salt reactor (MSR) off-gas systems, which manage the release of volatile fission products and other gases such as iodine generated during reactor operation.

Optical diagnostics such as ultraviolet-visible (UV-vis) absorption spectroscopy and laser-induced breakdown spectroscopy (LIBS) can provide real-time, non-destructive chemical monitoring of vapor-phase species. UV-vis provides molecular-level information through electronic transitions while LIBS enables rapid elemental analysis via plasma-emission spectra.2–5 Both techniques are well-suited for in situ or in-line deployment in harsh environments, including high-temperature corrosive-gas streams typical of MSR systems.6,7 These techniques can be extended to other advanced nuclear technologies requiring robust, real-time chemical monitoring of vapor-phase species, such as high-temperature gas-cooled reactors (HTGRs), sodium-cooled fast reactors (SFRs), and MSR off-gas systems.3 Indeed, the successful use of LIBS to characterize MSR off-gas streams has already been demonstrated.6,8–10 Additionally, the combined vapor-phase UV-vis and LIBS technique can be adapted to real-time monitoring of vapor-phase compounds for applications beyond nuclear technology: environmental monitoring, combustion processes, exhaust-emissions monitoring, and fuel formulations optimization in engines and propulsion systems for aerospace engineering.

We previously developed a high-temperature optical cell capable of integrating UV-vis and LIBS measurements into a single, modular platform for vapor-phase analysis.11 This preliminary design enabled alternating absorption and emission spectroscopy measurements at temperatures up to 450 °C and was used to characterize both organic and inorganic vapours, including metal chlorides relevant to nuclear applications. However, this cell was prone to leaks and air ingression, complicating the species chemistry and spectroscopy measurements. Additionally, the cell windows clouded quickly, limiting the amount of time measurements could be acquired. As part of that preliminary work, a window cover gas approach was explored for near-room temperature systems (<100 °C) and was shown to mitigate these challenges. However, this concept was not fully refined or implemented for high-temperature applications at that time. In this work, we redesigned the optical cell to eliminate air ingression and leaks, incorporated window cover gas systems, and built in the capability for simultaneous optical and emission spectroscopy measurements.

With this new optical cell design, we present the first simultaneous UV-vis and LIBS measurements of vapor-phase iodine and transition metal chlorides and their mixtures under CBV-relevant conditions. Three surrogate metal chlorides (niobium pentachloride [NbCl5], zirconium tetrachloride [ZrCl4], and molybdenum pentachloride [MoCl5]) as well as molecular iodine were selected based on their volatility and relevance to actinide chemistry in chloride-based separations. We report calibration curves for individual species and evaluate the performance of multivariate chemometric models using partial least-squares (PLS) regression for quantitative analysis.12,13 Our results demonstrate that this dual spectroscopy approach enables robust identification and quantification of chloride species in complex vapor streams, supporting its application as an online monitoring tool for MSRs and advanced fuel cycle processes. While this study does not attempt to replicate full molten-salt reactor off-gas chemistries, it focuses on demonstrating the measurement capability necessary for real-time monitoring. The combined LIBS/UV-vis vapor system is designed to establish the foundational diagnostics that can later be extended to fissile-bearing or proliferation-sensitive species in future safeguarded experimental campaigns.

Experimental

Optical cell design

All vapor-phase measurements were conducted using a custom-built high-temperature optical cell designed for simultaneous UV-vis absorption and LIBS emission spectroscopy; a prior iteration of the cell was previously published.11 Here, we report an improved and novel cell design that overcomes stability and measurement limitations of the previous design. The cell was constructed of 316 stainless-steel, operated at temperatures up to 450 °C, and included quartz windows (Thorlabs, VPW42) for optical access, a heated sample inlet, and a sheath gas system to protect optical surfaces. The cell geometry is shown in Fig. 1a. The cell used 5 mm-thick fused-silica optical windows with diameters of 1.5 inches and graphoil seals (Curtiss-Wright) around the windows. The optical pathlength was 15 cm. The optical cell was wrapped in heat tape and insulated to ensure consistent thermal conditions along the vapor pathway. The optical cell was oriented vertically with the LIBS window positioned upwards, while the UV-vis absorption measurements occurred through the two horizontal windows. The windows were kept clear by continuously purging nitrogen as a sheath gas with a flow rate of around 100 ml min−1, divided between the three windows in the optical cell.

(a) 3D visualization of the optical cell showcasing the measurement technique. (b) 2D schematic of the experiment showing the cyclone assembly, sheath gas, sample chamber, and furnace.

Connected to the optical cell inlet was a sampling chamber system, connected via a high-temperature valve (Swagelok, SS-3NBS4-G) and stainless-steel tubing. The purpose of the valve was to isolate the sample from air between the glovebox and connection to the optical cell. The valve portion was heat wrapped, heavily insulated, and sandwiched between the sample furnace and the heated optical cell. In a typical experiment, the sample chamber was loaded with salt in the glovebox, isolated via valves, transferred out of the glovebox, placed in a tube furnace, and finally connected to the optical cell. Argon gas was connected to the inlet side of the sample chamber and used as a carrier gas for the sample with typical flow rates between 0.8 and 0.88 L min−1. The vapor cell and sample chamber were inspected before and after each run. No visible pitting or material degradation was observed under the chloride-vapor conditions employed, and LIBS spectra showed no Fe, Cr, or Ni emission features that would indicate potential corrosion of the stainless-steel components.

Cover gas system and CFD

The cover gas system operates as a cyclone assembly, where nitrogen is introduced to an annular manifold (Fig. 1b) and then introduced into the optical cell through three angled holes that create a twisting flow pattern of gas away from the optical windows. This gas design was refined using computational fluid dynamics (CFD). CFD was performed using Siemens Simcenter STAR-CCM+ software to determine the flow rate needed to keep Ar away from the window ports. The boundary conditions of the simulation included adiabatic walls, a window temperature of 450 °C, Ar inlet flow rate of 0.8 L min−1, and the initial condition of being filled with nitrogen gas (N2). During these simulations, the N2 cover gas flow rates were optimized to reduce mixing effects of the vapor phase while maintaining protection of the optical windows. Results indicated N2 flows between 50 and 100 ml min−1 were sufficient to protect the windows while not diluting the sample gas significantly. N2 flow rates of 50 ml min−1 and above resulted in less than 0.13 mass fraction of Ar at the window surface (Fig. 2a), and this is visualized as the blue shading in Fig. 2b.

CFD results. Mass-flow rates are assumed to be distributed equally for each window. (a) 2D heat map showing the mass fraction of Ar spatially in the cell. (b) The total flow rate (in LPM) required for the vapor cell to sufficiently protect the windows.

Additionally, a gas flow study was conducted to verify the relationship between gas flow rates and vapor concentration, the results of which are provided in the SI Section S6. Gas flows were monitored using a pressure sensor in the sample chamber. By varying Ar and N2 flow rates and measuring the absorbance data for NbCl5 in the vapor phase, we confirmed that increased gas flow dilutes the vapor stream from the sample chamber and results in a decrease of signal intensity.

Materials and methods

Pure chloride salt samples of 0.991 g NbCl5, 1.061 g ZrCl4, 1.032 g MoCl5, and 0.500 g MoCl5 were measured, and testing was conducted for individual species in the vapor optical cell. The sample chamber was filled with the salt sample in an argon-filled glovebox. The sample chamber was then transferred out of the glovebox and attached to the optical chamber and argon inlet. It was placed inside the tube furnace. The sample chamber unit included a pressure sensor that allowed the set up to be tested for leaks each time the sample chamber was attached. Nitrogen flow was used as a sheath gas, and the optical chamber was heated to 400 °C at a ramp rate of 3 °C min−1. After the optical cell reached temperature, the sample chamber was heated to different temperatures depending on the sample matrix and the desired conditions. The setup remained the same as the furnace reached different temperatures and all tests for each sample were completed. With this setup and operation, the temperature in the optical cell, where measurements occurred in NbCl5, MoCl5, and ZrCl4, was always at 400 °C, regardless of the sample chamber temperature. Changes in the sample chamber temperatures was the driver for changing the amount of analyte in the gas phase at any given moment. After both chambers had cooled to room temperature, the sample chamber was removed, and the argon gas was placed on the sample inlet to prevent room air from entering the optical chamber. The remnants of the sample were collected from the sample chamber and various filters and traps. The sample chamber, filters and traps were then rinsed with water, and the sample chamber was dried in an oven at 120 °C overnight in preparation for testing of the next sample.

For the mixed-sample experiments, a ternary mixture containing 0.503 g NbCl5, 0.500 g MoCl5, and 0.186 g ZrCl4 was used. This sample was measured in the optical cell in the same manner as the pure chloride salts, where the temperature was incrementally raised, and the LIBS signal and absorbance spectra were measured. As the boiling points of NbCl5, MoCl5, and ZrCl4 are 248 °C,14 268 °C,15 and 331 °C,16 respectively, we expected to see a signal from each compound in this order.

Testing of I2 vapor monitoring capabilities was performed separately due to the lower sublimation temperature of I2. Solid I2 crystals (0.980 g) were loaded into the external sample chamber. The sample chamber temperature was increased stepwise from 30 to 120 °C to incrementally boil off the sample before passing into the optical cell which was held at 150 °C (all I2 measurements occurred at 150 °C), regardless of the sample chamber temperature.

UV-vis absorbance spectra were collected using a deuterium-halogen UV-vis near-infrared light source (DH-2000-Bal) with a diameter of 11 mm collimated through the optical cell and detected using a fiber-coupled high-resolution spectrometer (Ocean Optics HR6 utilizing a CCD array detector). The integration (measurement) time was 0.036438 seconds.

LIBS measurements were performed using a Q-switched neodymium-doped yttrium-aluminum-garnet laser (Quantel USA, Q-Smart 450) operating at 1064 nm with 9 ns pulse width. The laser beam was expanded from 6 to 18 mm using a Galilean beam expander (f = −75 mm and 200 mm lenses) before it was passed through dichroic mirror that transmitted 1064 nm laser light and reflected lower energies of light. Following the dichroic, the laser light was focused into the vapor stream through the optical window using a 125 mm focal-length lens. Emission spectra were captured using the 125 mm focal-length lens and reflected via the dichroic mirror into a fiber optic and next directed to an Echelle spectrometer (Catalina Scientific, EMU 120/65) with an iXon3-885 electron-multiplying charge-coupled device detector from Andor Technologies. A digital delay generator (Quantum Composer, 9200) was used to gate-delay the spectrometer. Spectra were collected by accumulating 100 laser shots with a gate delay of 6 µs, gate width of 80 µs, and laser energy of 150 mJ. Laser energy was selected based on our previous work, which demonstrated that longer gate delays, corresponding to lower effective plasma temperatures, yield more stable signal-to-background ratio (SBR) and signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) performance for gas-phase LIBS.11

Samples were loaded into the sample cell within an argon glovebox, then removed from the glovebox and connected to the supply gas. Approximately 0.8 L min−1 of Ar gas was flowed through the sample and optical cells to remove any residual oxygen while the optical chamber was ramped up to the operating temperature. Once the optical cell was at temperature, the sample cell was heated during which LIBS and UV-vis measurements were acquired every minute for UV-vis and approximately every 5 minutes for LIBS. The sample furnace was heated incrementally to drive off samples in order of boiling point. Pre- and post-measurement of the sample chamber weight verified that at least 98% mass transfer was achieved for all experiments. There was no indication of deposits in either cell or connective piping, the 2% unaccounted for is thought to have passed through the condenser to the exhaust system.

Calibration approach

To develop UV-vis calibration curves for the pure species, we used a calibration method similar to methods used for inline concentration measurements using UV-vis absorption.17 We correlated the total mass passed through the optical cell over time with the area under the signal vs. time curve. The total area under the curve (AT, arbitrary units min) was determined by integrating the area under the curves shown in Fig. 4b. For this, a numerical approach (trapezoid method18) was used and is detailed in the SI. As the intensity of UV-vis signal is proportional to the concentration of the analyte,19,20 a proportionality constant (k, units of g a.u.−1 min−1) was calculated for each analyte line (eqn (1)) using the total mass of analyte (mT, units of grams) consumed during each test. The k values were then used to calculate the mass of the analyte for each spectrum. Because the initial loadings of the sample are known, and experiments were carried out until all the samples were depleted, the mass flow rate of sample (ṁm) in a single measurement can be determined by dividing the area under a peak for a given measurement (Am) by k (eqn (2)). Using the calculated ṁm per spectrum, the concentration of the vapor in the gas phase was estimated based on the combined Ar and N2 gas flow rate (fg, units of L min−1) and the measurement acquisition time using eqn (3).12ṁm = Am × k3where (units of g mol−1) is the molecular weight of the analyte of interest, R is the ideal gas constant (units of L atm mol−1 K−1), P is the gas pressure in the optical cell (units of atm), and T is the gas temperature in the optical cell (units of Kelvin).

LIBS calibration curves were calculated using the same approach done for the UV-vis absorbance analysis, with the additional step of dividing the peak area by the Ar I 763.51 nm peak area to normalize the data between experiments.21

Chemometric analysis with partial-least squares

All chemometric analysis of LIBS data was performed using open-source packages in the Sci-Kit family of packages in Python.14 Reference concentrations of species for both pure and mixed-species tests were derived from the peak-fitting analysis of LIBS spectra as described in the Calibration approach section. The analysis region for PLS was restricted to the λ = 300–500 nm region. This was chosen to capture the contribution of analyte peak emissions (i.e., Nb, Mo, and Zr) and decrease contributions from argon carrier gas, nitrogen sheath gas, and chlorine emissions. The predictor matrix (X) comprised emission spectra from pure Nb, Mo, and Zr numbering NPureNb = 85, NPureMo = 100, and NPureZr = 91, respectively, and spectra from a mixed analyte run numbering NMixed = 101. This yielded X with NPureNb + NPureMo + NPureZr + NMixed = 377 = NTotal unique spectra. X was then scaled using the robust scaling method where each feature, xf (i.e., emission at a given wavelength λf) was scaled according to eqn (4).4xscaledf = (xf − cf)/sfwhere, xscaledf is the new, scaled feature, cf is the median of the training set at a given λf, and sf is the interquartile range of the training set at a given λf. Two methods of prediction were used in this work. The first method, called the “multiple-element model” hereon, used X to predict all three analyte species concentrations simultaneously, such that a single PLS model can evaluate the full target concentration matrix (Y) of Y = [cNb, cMo, cZr] where cNb, Mo, Zr represents the concentrations of the respective analytes for all 377 spectra and concentrations are 0 for spectra where a species is not present. The second method, called the “single-element model” hereon, uses the same X matrix, but develops three separate PLS models to predict a single column of Y at a time. This yields a unique set of latent variables (LVs) and a unique model for each species. No normalization was applied to the spectra beyond the robust scaling described in eqn (4).

PLS model performances were evaluated through three methods: back-prediction of concentrations based on the full training data set (i.e., the calibration), leave-one-out cross-validation, and 10-fold split cross-validation. All methods were evaluated through the coefficient of determination (R2) of linear fits of data on parity plots and root-mean squared error (RMSE) as evaluated against the reference concentrations of species (see peak-fitting analysis description in Calibration approach). For the single-element models, RMSE for both the calibration (RMSEC) and for the cross-validation approaches (RMSECV) are given by eqn (5).5where, N = NTotal, yi is the reference concentration of sample i, and ŷmodeli represents the prediction of the model trained with all samples for RMSEC, or represents the prediction of the model trained without sample i for the cross-validation approaches.

The RMSE of the multiple-element model was evaluated two ways. The first method combines the RMSE from all target elements into a single value given by RMSEC/CVallmulti in eqn (6).6where yij is the reference concentration of element j in sample i, ŷmodelij is its cross-validated or calibration prediction, N = NTotal is the number of samples, and t = 3 is the number of targets. Because eqn (6) mixes all three analyte errors (in ppm) into one figure, RMSEC/CVallmulti is a summary but is not directly comparable to any single-element RMSE. The second method is essentially the same as shown in eqn (5) and calculates the per-element residual by fixing the second summation in eqn (6) to a single j as shown in eqn (7).7where N = NTotal. RMSEC/CVindividual,jmulti is in ppm for element j alone and is directly comparable to the corresponding single-element model's RMSE, isolating the effect of multi-element versus single-element regression for that analyte.

The number of PLS latent variables (LVs) was selected by 10-fold split cross-validated mean squared error (MSE) using a diminishing-returns rule: starting from one LV and increasing, selection stopped at the first LV count k for which the relative improvement from the next component, (MSEk − MSEk+1)/MSEk, falls below 10% (SI Fig. S16).

Results and discussion

UV-vis absorbance spectroscopy real-time analysis

The absorbance spectrum of pure I2 is shown in Fig. 3a. I2 displays a single, broad electronic transition centered at approximately 520 nm. This transition is assigned to the transition of molecular I2 from its ground state (labelled “X” in molecular spectroscopy) to its second excited state (labelled “B”) such that literature commonly refers to this as a X → B transition22 (Table 1). This transition is overlayed by sharper vibrational excitations shown in Fig. 3b. Importantly, the I2 absorbance band occurs at much longer wavelength than the MoCl5 and NbCl5 features discussed in the following section, providing clear UV-vis spectral separation that enables unambiguous identification of iodine even in the presence of chloride species.

Absorbance peaks were identified for vapor-phase NbCl5 (yellow) and MoCl5 (brown) while ZrCl4 was transparent in the UV-vis range. Fig. 3c shows selected UV-vis spectra of pure NbCl5 and MoCl5. NbCl5 has two characteristic peaks, located at 40 000 cm−1/250 nm and 34 364 cm−1/291 nm, while MoCl5 has three peaks at 38 314 cm−1/261 nm, 28 735 cm−1/348 nm, and 22 124 cm−1/452 nm. These species exist as trigonal bipyramids in their monomeric vapor form with D3h symmetry.23,24Fig. 3d shows a simplified schematic of energy levels for an undistorted trigonal bipyramid structure with D3h symmetry, following the assignments of levels from ref. 16. Optical transitions between d-orbitals are shown as Δ (the lower energy transition) and δ (the higher energy transition), and ligand-to-metal charge transfer (LMCT) transitions are shown in Fig. 3d. Nb5+ has no available d-electrons, and Mo5+ has a single d-electron. For this reason, Nb5+ does not have available d–d absorption transitions and can only show LMCT transitions in the UV region. These LMCT transitions (∼34 480 cm−1/290 nm and 41 840 cm−1/240 nm) are shown in Fig. 3 and listed in Table 1 alongside a comparison to literature. There is likely a third LMCT transition at shorter wavelengths, but the quality of the absorption data below ∼230 nm (∼43 500 cm−1) is not sufficient to resolve any additional peaks. The single d-electron of Mo5+ shows two transitions in the UV and visible region (at ∼21 860 cm−1/458 nm and 28 570 cm−1/350 nm) alongside two observable LMCT transitions in the UV region (at ∼35 700 cm−1/280 nm and 40 810 cm−1/245 nm). Literature regarding these species in the vapor phase is limited, but our spectra are in good agreement with previous reports of NbCl5 vapor23,25 and a more recent report of vapor-phase MoCl5.24 MoCl5 can form various oxychlorides (e.g., MoOCl4 or MoO2Cl2), and these forms are not easily distinguished from MoCl5. MoOCl4 is of particular concern because this impurity has been shown to form readily with even trace oxygen presence.24,26 For the comparison of MoCl5 and MoOCl4/MoO2Cl2, the most salient features are that MoOCl4 lacks the peak at 28 570 cm−1 and displays a mildly blue-shifted lower-energy LMCT peak, and MoO2Cl2 lacks the peaks at both 21 860 and 28 570 cm−1.24,27 This means that there are no peaks that would easily confirm the presence of these oxychlorides in the spectral range measured.

The concentrations versus time for the selected absorbance peaks for I2, MoCl5, and NbCl5 are shown in Fig. 4a. The analyte concentrations in our experiment ranged from zero to approximately 3000 ppm, as expected from the initial loading mass and flow rate. Indeed, from the total masses loaded into the cell, gas flow rate, and total experiment duration, the average concentrations were 961 ppm for the I2 experiment, 1044 ppm for the MoCl5 experiment, and 1506 ppm for the NbCl5 experiment. As seen in Fig. 4a, an I2 signal became observable in the UV-vis spectrum when the sample chamber reached 40 °C and rose gradually as the sample chamber ramped to 120 °C. While holding at 120 °C, the I2 continued to elute until complete sublimation after approximately 120 minutes. By comparison, the higher boiling-point chlorides were ramped to higher temperatures. NbCl5 signal started to increase once the sample chamber was at 150 °C. The signal peaked at 175 °C, at which point it appears the sample was depleting, and the signal decreased. For MoCl5, the signal started increasing at 150 °C and continued to increase as the temperature was raised to 175 °C. While at 175 °C, the signal reached its maximum before decreasing. With the decrease in signal, the temperature of the sample was increased to 270 °C, during which a second increase in signal occurred. We initially hypothesized this observed result could indicate the potential presence of oxyhalide species; however, we observe no change in the absorbance spectrum shape or peaks in the spectra that indicate the presence of oxyhalide species. Fig. S14 shows representative spectra taken from two regimes of MoCl5 signal, and no significant shift or change in absorbance peak shape or positions was seen between the two regimes. Furthermore, SI Fig. S15 shows how relative absorbance values of various features expected of MoCl5 absorbance change with time, and no appreciable change in relative absorbance can be seen with time. If there were significant presence of either MoOCl4 or MoO2Cl2, the relative intensities of the LMCT peak at ∼280 nm (35 700 cm−1) would be expected to increase relative to the ∼351 nm peak (28 570 cm−1).24 Though the presence of oxychloride contaminants is still not easily ruled out from the spectra shown in Fig. 3, this result lends further evidence that oxychloride formation from MoCl5 was at least below the detection limit of our setup. The two regimes of MoCl5 presence may be explained by the presence of MoCl5 dimers. A recent report on MoCl5 dimers showed that solid MoCl5 primarily exists as a dimer, forming the molecular species Mo2Cl10, but thermodynamically, the monomer is favored in vapor phase MoCl5.25 Thus, one possible explanation for the second increase in signal we observe may be that we vaporized two species of the solid (the monomer and the dimer, each with different boiling points) but that both species vaporize to the monomer; thus, only the monomer is observed in the UV-vis absorbance spectra.

From the calibration curves (see SI Section S4 for plots), an estimate can be made on the limits of detection for the select analytes peaks. Here, the equation utilized for the limit of detection (LOD) is given by eqn (8),8where σ is the standard deviations of the normalized peak area at the lowest concentrations of analyte, and m is the slope of the calibration curve. For each calibration curve, the LOD was calculated, and results are shown in Fig. 4c. The estimated LODs were 67 ppm for NbCl5, 91 ppm for MoCl5, and 9 ppm for I2.

We calculated the integrated molar absorptivity (also referred to as the molar oscillator strength)23,24 by plotting the peak area versus molarity for pure NbCl5 and MoCl5 spectra (SI Section S7). The slope of the linear fit to these data is equal to the integrated molar absorptivity (εint) over a given wavelength range (λ1 to λ2) times the path length (b), in accordance with the Beer–Lambert law shown in eqn (9).9where c is the concentration (mol L−1). We report for the first time the integrated molar absorptivity for pure NbCl5 and MoCl5 vapors at 400 °C, which we find to be 1.46 × 105 and 1.60 × 105 nm L mol−1 cm−1, respectively (Fig. 4b). As described by Mayerhöfer et al.,19,20 the molar oscillator strength quantifies the probability of an electronic transition and is directly proportional to the integrated absorbance of a spectral band. Unlike peak absorbance, which may vary due to band broadening or shifting, the integrated molar absorptivity remains linearly dependent on concentration. This linearity arises from sum rules derived from the Kramers–Kronig relations,28 making the molar oscillator strength a robust descriptor of transition intensity. In the context of the vapor-phase NbCl5 and MoCl5 studied in this work, the molar oscillator strength provides a reliable basis for quantifying absorption strength despite potential variations in band shape.

Building on results from the tests on individual components, we measured a ternary mixture containing MoCl5, ZrCl4, and NbCl5. Sample chamber temperatures were chosen to specifically target each compound sequentially, based on boiling point, in the order NbCl5, MoCl5, then ZrCl4. This approach allowed for the observation of each component's behavior at different temperatures. The results are illustrated through plots of the spectra at varying timepoints (Fig. 4b). These plots provide a visual representation of the spectral changes as temperature increased. Initially, when the temperature of the sample chamber reached 150 °C, a signal corresponding to NbCl5 was observed (the dusky-orange region in Fig. 4b). This indicated that NbCl5 had started to vaporize and was present in the vapor stream. As the temperature further increased to 175 °C, the peak at around 280 nm increased significantly (light-orange region in Fig. 4b). Additionally, a slight shoulder was noted on this peak, suggesting a potential overlap between the signals of NbCl5 and MoCl5. This overlap indicates that MoCl5 was also starting to vaporize and contributed to the spectral signal. Upon increasing the temperature to 200 °C, the NbCl5 peak disappeared from the spectra. This disappearance suggested that NbCl5 had fully vaporized and was no longer present in the sample chamber. At this point, the spectra primarily showed the presence of MoCl5, with distinct peaks observed around 275, 360, and 450 nm (green region in Fig. 4b). Further increasing the temperature to between 215 and 250 °C led to the disappearance of the MoCl5 peaks. This qualitatively indicated that MoCl5 had also fully vaporized and was depleted from the sample chamber. As will be discussed in the next section, ZrCl4 became visible in the LIBS spectra, confirming that ZrCl4 was now present in the vapor stream upon the depletion of MoCl5.

The UV-vis spectra of the ternary mixture provided rapid and facile online monitoring of the vapor-phase chlorides for valuable qualitative monitoring of spectral characteristics without the need for post-processing the data. In our system, we successfully employed UV-vis spectroscopy for chemical identification and relative changes in concentration over time. However, quantitative analysis of the mixed chloride salts proved difficult due to absorbance peak convolution, overlap, and broadening in the absorbance spectra of the vapor-phase mixture. As seen in Fig. 3c, NbCl5 and MoCl5 have peaks at 246 and 267 nm, respectively. The peak positions and widths overlap in a way that makes deconvolution and the determination of the concentration of MoCl5 and NbCl5 difficult. The peaks at 293 and 253 nm for NbCl5 and MoCl5, respectively, are also wide, creating spectra that are not consistently resolved. Because the MoCl5 and NbCl5 absorbance features exhibit significant convolution under vapor-phase conditions, UV-vis analysis of mixed chloride experiments was limited to qualitative identification rather than extraction of absolute concentrations.

LIBS reveals quantitative elemental composition

To further identify and quantify elemental composition of the vapor-phase mixtures, we used simultaneous LIBS measurements of the system. The LIBS emission spectrum of the pure I2 sample is presented in Fig. 5a and b, magnified to show select regions with the strongest emission lines observed. Lines were assigned from the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) atomic spectra database (ASD).29 All observed emission lines are shown in Table 2, along with the NIST ASD assignment in spectroscopic notation and energy levels of the transitions. The strongest I emission lines are expected at wavelengths below 210 nm, but these are not observed on our spectrometer and only near-infrared transitions are observed. Notably, the LIBS emission lines used for iodine quantification lie in the 800–900 nm region, which is well separated from the chloride emission features analyzed in this work (discussed in the following paragraph), indicating that the iodine signal would remain spectrally isolated in mixed-vapor conditions. A univariate calibration curve for the LIBS emission peak at 804.37 nm is shown in Fig. 5c. The data shown in Fig. 5c are for the emission peak with the lowest LOD and best R2 of univariate regression, with an LOD of 326 ppm (mol mol−1). Notably, the limit of detection of I2 is above an order of magnitude more sensitive in UV-vis absorbance than in LIBS. This is likely due to multiple factors, including the large absorption coefficient of iodine and the limits of our spectrometer design. However, this example showcases the necessity of both spectroscopy techniques in conjunction to probe across a range of analytes. Low ppm detection of iodine would be insufficient with only LIBS; conversely, the detection of ZrCl4 would not be feasible with only UV-vis. Together, these observations underscore why a dual-spectroscopy approach is essential, as the combined LIBS/UV-vis technique provides multi-species coverage that surpasses the capability of either method used alone.

We observe the same trends of signal versus time that were observed using UV-vis spectroscopy. The NbCl5 signal increased once the sample cell was at 150 °C, and it peaked at 175 °C. The same pattern was observed for all three Nb lines investigated. Mo signal increased at 150 °C and continued to increase up to 200 °C. At 200 °C, the signal reached its maximum before steeply decreasing. With this sudden decrease in signal, the temperature of the sample was increased to 300 °C, at which a second increase in signal occurred. As discussed in the UV-vis absorbance analysis, a possible explanation for the second increase in signal may be that both monomers and dimers are present in the solid MoCl5. At 300 °C, the signal started decreasing as the material in the sample cell was depleted. ZrCl4 signal started increasing linearly around 225 °C. Just after 200 minutes, the sample chamber temperature was ramped up to 300 °C, after which an increase in the signal was observed. After some time at 300 °C, the signal started decreasing as the material in the sample cell was depleted.

LIBS calibration curves were calculated using the same approach done for the UV-vis absorbance analysis. For each calibration curve, the LOD was calculated, and results are shown in Table 3. The LODs were 50 ppm for NbCl5, 46 ppm for MoCl5, 9 ppm for ZrCl4, and 330 ppm for I2. Chemometric analysis using PLS was performed to determine the method's suitability for predicting concentrations from complex, mixed-species spectra. This is important for the future development of vapor-phase LIBS spectroscopy toward application in real MSR or reprocessing off-gas streams, which may contain dozens of volatile species. As outlined in the Methods section, both pure and mixed-species emissions were included in the training dataset to account for plasma-matrix effects in the mixed-species emission data. The training dataset remained the same across models, but two different types of models were developed: (1) one that predicts the concentrations of all species simultaneously (“multiple-element model”), and (2) a group of models that each predict a single species (“single-element models”). All model predictions are compared to the ppm estimates obtained through peak-fitting of the LIBS emission data (see Methods and SI Section S3). The prediction of Nb and Mo concentrations was relatively consistent across the two model methods, and RMSEC was similar to RMSECV for both species, regardless of cross-validation method. Mo displayed RMSECV values of 90–95 ppm and Nb values of 225–230 ppm. Predictive capabilities for Zr were poorer than those for Nb or Mo, regardless of model method. This can be seen qualitatively in Fig. 7 in the scatter of the parity plot. Indeed, R2 values of parity-plot linear fits for Zr lie as low as 0.78 (Table 4). Prediction of Zr did perform marginally better using the multiple-element model, though the difference between the calibration and cross-validation RMSE values is more significant for Zr than for either Nb or Mo. The improvement of Zr prediction with the multiple-element model likely suggests that the joint model is detecting some shared correlations in the Nb or Mo spectra, possibly related to plasma-matrix effects.

The “real” concentration is again based on the ppm estimates obtained through peak-fitting of the LIBS emission data (see Methods section and SI). The PLS predictions of Mo track well with the peak-fit data. Similarly, predictions of Nb perform well, though there is a mild underprediction of concentration at higher ppm levels. This can similarly be seen in Fig. 7, where there is a mild underprediction of ∼200 ppm. Predictions of Zr are interesting, as the multiple-element model underpredicts at lower concentrations and overpredicts at higher concentrations (greater than ∼1500 ppm, see Fig. 7). Conversely, the single-element model overpredicts at lower concentrations and underpredicts at higher concentrations.

The opposing direction of these two trends is notable: the single-element model tends to compress Zr predictions toward the middle of the concentration range, while the multiple-element model shows the reverse pattern, with the spread of predictions widening at the extremes. Given the low R2 for Zr and the limited number of points at the highest concentrations, both observations are treated as qualitative descriptors of the parity-plot behavior rather than evidence of a specific mechanism. The divergence between the two models does suggest, however, that allowing the model to fit all species jointly changes the Zr predictions in a concentration-dependent way relative to the single-element fit.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates the effectiveness of a dual spectroscopic approach for real-time, inline analysis of vapor-phase surrogate chlorides and iodine relevant to chloride-based volatility and MSR applications. Using a custom-designed high-temperature optical cell, we simultaneously collected UV-vis absorption and LIBS emission spectra for pure and mixed vapors of NbCl5, MoCl5, ZrCl4, and I2. LIBS measurements successfully identified multiple emission lines for each element, and these peaks remained well-resolved even in the presence of complex mixtures. UV-vis spectroscopy provided distinct absorption features for NbCl5, MoCl5, and I2 while ZrCl4 exhibited no measurable absorption in the UV-vis range. These spectral characteristics enabled real-time tracking of individual species and compositional changes in the vapor stream.

The custom-designed high-temperature optical cell enables robust identification and quantification of chloride species with low detection limits. The integration of UV-vis and LIBS data into a unified technique further enhances species classification and quantification, demonstrating the value of combining molecular and elemental information for vapor-phase diagnostics, as well as the necessity for both spectroscopic techniques to detect and quantify all analytes of interest. Future efforts will focus on extending this approach to uranium chloride salt systems and exploring integration into process control strategies for MSRs, such as off-gas processes. This work establishes an on-line safeguards and material accountancy technique suitable for the deployment of CBV and similar high-temperature separations in MSR systems, thereby supporting the development of efficient, proliferation-resistant fuel cycle strategies that can significantly reduce repository burdens and contribute to a sustainable energy future.
