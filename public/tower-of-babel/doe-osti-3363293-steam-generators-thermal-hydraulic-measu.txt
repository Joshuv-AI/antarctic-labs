 ABSTRACT
 Steam generators are integral components of both conventional and advanced
reactors utilizing steam power cycles because they influence the power-conversion
performance. Steam-generator designs are highly influenced by the type of reactor
coolant used, which mainly governs the different control, monitoring, and safety
system actuation requirements. Therefore, the present work reviews the thermal
hydraulics instrumentation within steam generators designed for pressurized water
reactors, high-temperature gas-cooled reactors, sodium fast reactors, and molten–
salt reactors. Emphasis is given to measurement techniques that are either unique
or particularly important to specific reactor types. Last, a discussion is provided on
the instrumentation and measured parameters. The work identifies standard
parameters of interest and is expected to aid the development of test beds and novel
measurements techniques.

 1. INTRODUCTION
 Steam generators (SGs) are large heat exchangers used to vaporize feedwater to drive turbines in
nuclear power plants (NPPs) utilizing steam power cycles. Their designs in conventional pressurized water
reactors (PWRs) are highly standardized large shell-and-tube heat exchangers that contain thousands of
tubes. Conventional plants usually contain between two and six such steam generators per reactor unit.
They are an integral part of an NPP and require high-performance and robust design, manufacturing, testing,
and monitoring practices [1]. The performance of the SGs is critical for the overall efficiency of the power
plant [2].
 Around 1,300 steam generators are currently operational within the world’s NPPs, and many more are
under construction [3]. The majority of operational steam generators, as well as the ones currently being
built, are PWR vertical recirculating steam generators (RSGs). However, four major SG designs exist:
(1) PWR vertical RSGs, (2) Canadian deuterium uranium reactor (CANDU) pressurized heavy-water
reactor (PHWR) steam generators, (3) once-through steam generators (OTSGs), and (4) Russian water-
water energetic reactor (WWER) steam generators. These designs differ in various aspects; for instance,
the CANDU RSG design is largely similar to RSGs with differences in size, materials, operating conditions,
and tube-support structures [3]. However, the CANDU designs, unlike American designs, include an
integrated preheater near the feedwater inlet. On the other hand, OTSGs include straight tubes instead of
U-tubes and are smaller because they do not contain moisture separators. Last, the major characteristic of
WWER steam generators is that they contain a horizontal tube bundle and steam collectors.
 On the other hand, considerable work was conducted in the 20th century on high-temperature gas-cooled
reactors (HTGRs), sodium fast reactors (SFRs), and molten salt reactors (MSRs) that utilize steam power
cycles [4]. Many demonstration and prototype plants have been operated throughout the world on these
technologies. SGs for some of these reactors were fundamentally different from PWR SGs due to different
reactor designs, sizes, coolant properties, and safety considerations, which resulted in many distinct steam
generator designs.
 Instruments are placed in SGs for the monitoring and control of system parameters and the actuation of
safety systems [5]. Parameters of interest common for all SG designs are temperatures, pressures, flow
rates, and vibration characteristics while others depend on the particular reactor or SG design [6]. Different
instruments and measurement techniques may be employed to measure these parameters depending on the
SG design. Further, different countries or utility companies may have different approaches, requirements,
or experiences on the use of certain instruments and techniques [7]. In addition, the SG designs’ being
largely from the past century creates difficulties obtaining and compiling information on their
instrumentation due to document availability and the proprietary information.
 For these reasons, this report presents a review of methods of thermal-hydraulic measurement and
instrumentation techniques applicable to nuclear SGs for the purpose of identifying the standard parameters
of interest and for the development of novel techniques and test beds. It should be noted that (1) the
instruments and techniques presented are those used in selected designs because the inclusion of all
available designs was deemed outside of the scope of this work; (2) general information from sources
available to the public are analyzed, without considering any custom modifications or methodologies
particular operators may use, and (3) general instruments associated with the reactor coolant system or
auxiliary systems related to SG operation are not considered since they were deemed outside the scope of
this work.
 2. RECIRCULATING STEAM GENERATORS
 RSGs are described first because they are the most common of the SG designs. A schematic of a PWR
vertical RSG can be seen in Figure 1. The primary fluid flows through the U-tubes, with the inlet and outlet
located at the bottom of the steam generator. The secondary fluid enters the RSG at the feedwater inlet, as

 1

shown, mixing with the fluid that is already recirculating in the shell. It then flows through the downcomer
and up through the tube bundle, where boiling occurs. After the fluid leaves the tube section, it passes
through primary and secondary separators, and the produced steam leaves the RSG at the top of the SG, as
shown in Figure 1. Recirculation of water within the SG can be described as follows: (1) liquid travels
down the downcomer annulus due to hydrostatic pressure head and density differences, (2) the fluid
experiences boiling as it travels through along the tube bundle, (3) the upward flow goes through the
separators, and the separated steam flows out of the SG at the top whereas the liquid recirculates back down
the annulus.
 The secondary-side fluid experiences changes in two-phase heat-transfer modes and flow regimes as it
passes along the tube bundle, as shown in Figure 1. The fluid is initially in a subcooled state as it flows
down the downcomer, but it later experiences nucleate boiling, bubbly flow, churn turbulent flow, and
annular flow. A majority of mass flowing up the tubes is liquid, with a quality around 20–40% [8]; however,
this corresponds to void fractions of around 90%. The pressure losses along the flow path depend on the
flow and boiling conditions.

 2.1. CANDU Steam Generators
 A CANDU SG can be classified as an RSG with an integral preheater, as shown in Figure 2. The
feedwater enters the SG near the tube outlet and perpendicular to the tubes, where the preheater baffles
guide the fluid across the tubes for multiple passes. The fluid then moves along the tubes and boils. As in
an RSG, recirculation takes place within the SG as the separated liquid goes back down the downcomer
after the moisture separators. The flow regimes observed within a CANDU are asymmetrical due to the
presence of the preheater, as shown in Figure 2.

 2.2. Measured Parameters and Instrumentation
 Because they are both RSGs, the measured parameters of interest and the applicable instrumentation
for both standard and CANDU SGs are identical. They can thus be grouped in the same section. It should
be noted that the compiled instruments do not include some feed and steam-system measurements, such as
feedwater and steam-flow rates, pressures, and temperatures that can be valuable status and performance
indicators for an SG.
2.2.1. Temperatures
 Conventional thermocouples (TCs) and/or resistance temperature detectors (RTDs) can be placed at
numerous strategic locations in SGs to measure temperatures. These locations include the primary fluid
inlet and outlet, feedwater inlet, steam outlet, downcomer, and tube and shell walls. Multiple temperature
sensors are commonly placed at certain locations for redundancy or for the use of the signals for multiple
purposes such as monitoring, control, or safety system actuation. Both TCs and RTDs can be of thermowell-
mounted or direct-immersion types, which yield advantages in ease of replacement and response time,
respectively [10]. It should be noted that TCs and RTDs exhibit different accuracy, reliability, and
maintenance characteristics. Thus, the choice of using TCs or RTDs, as well as the number of redundant
sensors, could depend on the measurement purpose.
2.2.2.      Level
 The secondary-side level in a RSG needs to be continuously monitored for use in plant-control and
safety-related functions. It can be measured using level transmitters, which are essentially differential-
pressure transmitters. This approach to level measurement is the most widely used method in the industry
[11]. Multiple level transmitters are required for level control during startup and normal operation, as well
as to trip safety systems [12]. The location of the taps for the level transmitters depends on the use of the

 3

signal output by the level transmitter. It is common for narrow range and wide range-level transmitters to
be employed. The taps of the narrow range-level transmitters are located near the water surface whereas
the wide range-level transmitter taps are located at the extreme ends of the RSG, namely the bottom of the
downcomer and the steam dome [13, 14].
2.2.3.      Pressures
 Pressures on the secondary side of an RSG are commonly measured at the upper-level transmitter
connections near the water level and the feedwater inlet. Their main function is to trip safety systems under
non-normal pressure conditions and to identify asymmetric pressure conditions across the different SGs in
the NPP [12]. On the primary side, the pressure drop across the tubes can be measured for monitoring
purposes and to trip safety systems under low-flow conditions [12]. Additionally, the SG pressure can be
inferred from the main steam-line pressure even if direct pressure measurements through the shell are not
taken [15].
2.2.4. Downcomer Flow Rate
 Measurement of downcomer flow rate is not general practice in conventional SG designs; however, it
may provide certain performance and reliability indicators such as (1) a direct measure of the recirculation
ratio, (2) the identification of such operational changes as steam carryunder, and (3) the investigation of
long-term fouling [2, 16]. Intrusive techniques, such as pitot tubes and boost venturi probes, as well as non-
intrusive ultrasonic transducers, can be employed to determine the downcomer flow velocity [2]. However,
it should be noted that intrusive methods may not be practical because the high temperature and pressure
boundary of the SG must be penetrated.
2.2.5. Vibrations
 Flow-induced vibration of the SG tubes and shell can cause mechanical failure due to such mechanisms
as fatigue, cracking, and fretting wear [17]. Vibration monitoring enables predictive maintenance by
allowing the early detection of structural problems in tubes and tube supports, such that repair measures
can be taken [18]. The detection of compromised structures in turn improves the overall safety and provides
economic benefits. Neutron noise analysis is commonly used for the monitoring of reactor-vessel internals
[19]; however, this method cannot be employed in SGs and other components outside of the reactor vessel
[18]. Thus, mechanical sensors such as strain gauges, accelerometers, and displacement sensors must be
utilized [18, 20, 21].
 3. ONCE-THROUGH STEAM GENERATORS
 OTSGs were developed by Babcock and Wilcox Company (B&W) and contain a single pass of straight
tubes, rather than U-tubes. The operation of an OTSG is shown in Figure 3, where the primary-side fluid
flows through the tubes in the direction of gravity while the secondary-side fluid is on the shell side in the
opposite direction. As can be seen in the figure, the secondary-fluid inlet is located near the middle of the
OTSG, where it is sprayed as subcooled liquid downward and is preheated through direct contact with the
steam aspirated from the tube bundle [22]. The secondary fluid reaches 100% quality at roughly midway
along the tube bundle, after which point it gets superheated to around 30°C [23]. Therefore OTSGs do not
include moisture separators that enable them to be more compact than RSGs [8]. Figure 4 shows the
percentage area that is under the different phase-change heat-transfer mechanisms that exist within the
OTSG. As shown in Figure 4, around 60% of the tube area is under nucleate boiling conditions, which is
the most efficient heat-transfer mode.

 3.1. Measured Parameters and Instrumentation
 Main differences of OTSGs compared to the two RSG designs mentioned can be identified as (1) the
conversion of most water flowing into the shell side at the feedwater inlet into steam (besides the steam
that is aspirated to heat the feedwater to saturation), (2) the production of superheated steam instead of
saturated steam, and (3) the presence of a single pass of tubes instead of U-tubes. These factors may create
major operational differences; however, the instruments involved in these heat exchangers are similar to
those in RSGs. According to B&W, pressure, temperature, and level measurements are taken for OTSGs
[23]. However, B&W documents do not reflect any pressure taps in the shell besides for the level
transmitters [23].
3.1.1. Temperatures
 It is stated that the shell-side temperatures are measured using five thermowell-mounted TCs at roughly
equally spaced locations (~ 2.5 m) along the steam generator. These temperatures are used to monitor the
shell- and tube-side temperature difference, which is a significant concern in OTSGs because large
temperature differences can cause high stresses due to the different thermal-expansion coefficients of the
tube and shell [24]. In addition, two downcomer temperature elements are used to monitor and accurately
determine fluid density for level measurements [23]. Figure 5 shows the axial locations of the shell and
downcomer temperature measurements along the SG with the locations of the feedwater nozzle, aspirating
port, steam outlet nozzle, and the emergency feedwater nozzle. The datum point (0 in.) designates the lower
tubesheet.
3.1.2.      Level
 An OTSG includes three level-measurement ranges by means of differential-pressure transmitters, as
shown in Figure 5; namely, the startup, operating, and full ranges [23]. The startup-range level signals are
used to control feedwater-flow rate when the plant operating power is less than or equal to 15% of the
nominal power [24]. The operating-range level measurement is used under normal operating conditions for
both monitoring and as an input to the plant’s control system (i.e., the integrated control system). Last, the
full-range level signal is used when the SG is shut down and maintained under wet-layup conditions to
minimize corrosion and oxidation [25]. The low- and high-level alarms are tripped by the startup and
operating-range level transmitters, respectively. It should be noted that in integral-economizer OTSGs
(IEOTSGs), only two level measurements are taken, where the full-range level transmitters are used during
normal operation as well, eliminating the need for a separate operating-range level transmitter [24].

 4. WWER STEAM GENERATORS
The main difference between WWER SGs and the aforementioned designs is their horizontal operation;
i.e., both the shell and the tubes are oriented horizontally, as shown in Figure 6. The designs of the WWER-
440 and the WWER-1000 reactors vary mainly in size, tube arrangement, and materials [7]. They contain
a horizontal moisture separator, along with a horizontal steam collector for steam outlet. The primary
coolant enters and leaves the SG from the vertical hot and cold collectors, respectively. Furthermore, unlike
the vertical designs, these SGs contain sludge-blowdown ports due to the water-chemistry employed in the
plants [26]. A discussion of the advantages and disadvantages of horizontal steam generators, based on the
Russian operational experience, can be found in Trunov et al., where the chief advantage is reported to be
a larger liquid inventory that enables smoother transients during startup and accidents [26].

 4.1. Measured Parameters and Instrumentation
 Literature suggests extensive experimental thermal-hydraulic measurements for WWER SGs [28].
However, it is unclear to what degree these measurements reflect those that are normally conducted in
conventional WWER plants. The reported measurements include temperature fields at tube headers, tube
vibrations, steam moisture content, level, as well as local and average void fractions [28]. The exact types
and locations for some of these measurements cannot be found in the available literature in English.
Conventional methods for measuring temperatures (thermocouples or RTDs) and level measurements
(differential-pressure transmitters), are reported [29] along with more-novel techniques, such as weighing
the entire SG to obtain the volume-averaged void fraction [30]. Furthermore, a list of important parameters
needed for model validation was given by Yrjoelae [31].
 5. HTGR STEAM GENERATORS
 HTGRs have a long operational history, starting from 1964. The first was the Dragon Reactor in the
United Kingdom (UK) [32]. Starting with Dragon, the international community has built and tested many
different HTGR designs. These designs employ either a pebble-bed reactor (PBR) or a prismatic-block core,
where the latter is the most common in existing HTGR designs [33]. They mainly use UO2 fuel, with some
designs using tri-structural isotropic (TRISO) particle fuel [34]. Helium and steam are commonly used as
the primary and secondary coolants, respectively. The core outlet temperatures in some HTGRs can reach
up to 950°C, at pressures much lower than those of typical light-water reactors [35]. Furthermore, they are
regarded as high performance (capable of high thermal efficiencies and process heat temperatures) and
technologically mature [36]. Several recent advanced-reactor designs, such as Fast Modular Reactor

 8

(General Atomics) [37], Micro Modular Reactor (Ultra Safe Nuclear Corporation) [38], Holos-Quad
(HolosGen) [39], and Xe-100 (X-Energy) [40], employ HTGR technologies.
 As described in the preceding sections, SG designs for conventional PWRs are relatively standard
across designs. However, because HTGR designs are not yet standardized, SGs need to be developed
considering the requirements for the specific plant [41]. The Peach Bottom (40 MWe) and Fort St. Vrain
(330 MWe) plants were the only two HTGRs operated in the US. Larger commercial HTGRs were proposed
based on the design and operational experiences of these pioneering plants. The present section discusses
SG designs from legacy reactors—i.e., those designed in the past century. It should be noted that there is a
rapid advancement of instrumentation technology and improved safety and monitoring capabilities from
various lessons learned over time. A review of emerging instrumentation technology can be found in Ball
et al. [42].
 5.1. Peach Bottom Unit 1
 The Peach Bottom HTGR prototype plant’s SG design is shown in Figure 7. The plant contained two
such SGs in parallel with the primary coolant, helium, on the shell side, and the secondary coolant, water,
on the tube side. As can be seen from the schematic, the SG contains three sets of U-tube bundles,
functioning as an economizer, evaporator, and superheater moving from right to left. Another novel design
feature is the use of a nozzle composed of concentric tubes that contain the hot helium coming from the
reactor and the cold helium leaving the SG, on the inner and outer sections, respectively. The helium
entering the SG encounters a perforated baffle that ensures uniformity of helium flowing across the tubes.
Next, the helium flow is directed through the tubes via internal baffles, flowing through the superheater,
evaporator, and economizer in that order, before returning to the core. It should be noted that a steam drum
and a recirculation loop exist in order to effectively use the three different tube bundles for their respective
functions, as shown in Figure 8 [41]. The SG design allows the production of superheated steam at around
540°C at 11 MPa pressure.

 5.2. Fort St. Vrain
 Because the Fort St. Vrain design integrated the reactor core, helium recirculators, and SGs within a
prestressed concrete reactor vessel (PCRV), the SG design required a compact form factor while
maintaining a large heat-transfer area [44]. A simplified schematic of the resultant design can be seen in
Figure 9, where the different sections of the SG are shown, along with a diagram of the plant shown in
Figure 10. The primary-side coolant, helium, flows vertically down through the helical tubes and leaves at
the lower plenum, passing through the reheater, superheater, evaporator, and economizer sections.
Feedwater on the secondary side enters the SG outside the PCRV boundary and travels through the many
helical tubes and leaves the SG again at the bottom as superheated steam at 540°C. Later, as can be seen
from the plant schematic, the steam returns to the SG after driving the high-pressure turbine to be reheated
at the helical-reheater tube bundle before it reaches the intermediate pressure turbine.

 5.4. Thorium High-Temperature Reactor (THTR-300)
 THTR-300 was a German PBR, with helium as the primary coolant. It contained six identical SGs
capable of producing steam at 185 bar and 550°C [49]. The SGs were placed around the thermal shield
within the PCRV. They contained three sets of helical-tube bundles, two for high-pressure and one for low-
pressure steam, where the latter is used for reheating [50]. A sectional view of the SGs is shown in
Figure 13, where the helium enters at the bottom and leaves laterally while feedwater enters and leaves
from the top. These were the first nuclear SGs to employ downhill boiling as shown in Figure 14, due to
the design decision related to the direction of the helium flow [49].

 5.5. Measured Parameters and Instrumentation
 Several challenges regarding the implementation of instruments to HTGR systems remain; chief
amongst them is high-temperature operation and steam- or moisture-ingress events. Furthermore, the small
atomic size of helium makes it prone to leakage at connections not made with fusion techniques. Steam
ingress, in particular, is highly significant for designs employing SGs as the secondary side, which is usually
at a much-higher pressure relative to the primary side. This creates the possibility of large amounts of steam
ingress to the primary side in case of a SG tube-rupture accident [52, 53]. Moisture on the primary side
could cause severe disruptions or damage to the reactor because it could cause positive reactivity insertion,
as well as possible damage to graphite support structures and fuel elements.
 Process instrumentation in Peach Bottom and Fort St. Vrain reactors were described by Harrer and
Beckerley [43]. In the Peach Bottom SGs, K-type TCs with stainless steel sheathes and oxide insulation
were used to measure temperature, with the majority of the thermocouples directly welded to piping or
equipment to reduce leakage paths. Furthermore, electrolytic hygrometer moisture detectors were placed at
the helium outlet of each steam generator. Additional hygrometers were placed near the baffles because
they were identified as prone to leakages [43]. Signals from these moisture detectors are used to
automatically isolate leaking loops through a flow controller.
 Instruments in Fort St. Vrain SGs were described by Quade et al. [44] and are more comprehensive,
with measurements of temperature, flow rates, vibratory stresses, and the plateout of radioactive particles
[54]. The instruments are used to verify specific performance metrics such as tube-bundle performance,
tube-to-tube temperature variations, boiling level, etc., as well as vibratory stresses and frequencies during
the precritical flow test and startup operation. They include geminol P-geminol N thermocouples measuring
the feedwater, steam-outlet, steam-subheader, reheat-steam, and cold reheat-steam temperatures at multiple
locations. Thermocouples on the helium side are protected by radiation shields to improve their long-term
reliability. The temperature of the steam side is determined by measuring the tube-wall temperature at

 17

insulated sections of tubing. Temperatures inside the PCRV are taken by sheathed and pressure-tight
thermocouples while temperatures outside the PCRV are measured by standard thermocouples that are
either located in a thermowell or welded. Lead wires penetrate the primary and secondary enclosures
through special brazed plugs without a connector. In addition, strain gauges are used to measure vibratory
stresses during operation. Strain gauges that experience high temperatures are welded directly on the
equipment. Finally, the feedwater flow rate is measured by venturi flow meters. It should be noted that the
steam generators included two sets of instrumentation that are permanent and temporary, where the
temporary instruments cannot be replaced and are only used during startup testing [44].
 Earlier experience on the British designs of gas cooled reactors indicated that flow-induced vibration
of boiler tubing could be a serious problem, leading to tube fretting during operation. There was some
concern during construction that similar problems could arise at Hartlepool and Heysham 1. In order to
ensure that a high standard of information could be obtained during commissioning, several boiler pods at
each station were instrumented with strain gauges and/or accelerometers. The boiler-plant terminal
conditions including feed flows, temperatures, and pressures measured and continuously monitored by the
station data-collection computer system. In addition to these measurements, the superheater-outlet headers
of all boiler-pod units were fitted with thermocouples to measure the temperature of the steam issuing from
individual superheater tail pipes connected to almost all the helices that formed the high-pressure section
of the boiler.
 To ensure adequate monitoring of the conditions of the heating-surface components and provide
suitable data for performance analyses, one boiler-pod unit from each of the Hartlepool and Heysham I
stations were fitted with TCs for measuring the gas and metal temperatures at selected locations on the
boiler-heating surfaces, as shown in Figure 15. Measurements from these specially instrumented pods,
terminal conditions of the other pods—along with performance assessments employing two-dimensional
computer codes—were used to predict the terminal conditions of the non-instrumented pods to ensure that
they operate within the specified design and operational constraints.
 Similarly, several strain gauges were fitted to one boiler pod at each site while accelerometers were
fitted to another pod. In addition, a further set of accelerometers and strain gauges were fitted to a specially
instrumented boiler pod (MA31) on Reactor 2 at Heysham. Furthermore, to provide reliable information
on the feed-flow distribution, special flow-measuring ferrules were installed after boiler re-ferruling in the
feed-inlet headers. The pressure drop across three ferrules fitted to selected tubes connected to the inner,
middle, and outer helices of the high-pressure section were used, after calibration, to calculate the feed-
flow rates in individual tubes.
 The THTR SGs contained several highly loaded components and also employed downhill boiling,
which made it important to know the internal temperatures of both gas and water at critical locations and
to compare them with predictions. Because all six SGs were of identical design, it was decided to instrument
one module only. The internal instrumentation consisted of TCs; early plans to measure stresses and
vibrations as well were abandoned because reliability and effectiveness of accelerometers and strain gauges
were not considered to be commensurate with the involved costs. Sheathed, ungrounded thermocouples
measured gas temperature around the tubes and water or steam temperatures inside the tubes. The location
of the thermocouples was such that the temperature distribution along some selected tubes could be
determined. The results gave information not only about the precision of the thermal sizing, but also on
flow distribution, boundary effects, and flow stability. A detailed account of thermocouple locations,
mounting methods, and the routing of the lead wires can be found in Fricker [55]; however, a schematic of

 6. SFR STEAM GENERATORS
Liquid sodium has been used extensively as a coolant in fast breeder reactors, including EBR-I [56],
Dounreay [57], Fermi Reactor [58], and EBR-II [59]. Sodium as a coolant offers much-higher operating
temperatures and thermal conductivity compared to water or helium; however, its main disadvantage is that
it easily reacts with water or moisture in air. Therefore, SG designs for sodium-cooled reactors must
consider safeguards against possible contact between sodium and water. These measures include the use of
duplex tubes, where a channel filled with helium exists between the sodium and water sides—adding
another layer of protection in case of leakage [60]—as well as various leak-detection methods [61-63].
Another consideration is that the high thermal conductivity of sodium could results in high thermal stresses
in thick-walled components during fast transients [64].
 A comprehensive review of SG designs for sodium-cooled reactors was conducted by Muller and
Schnauder [65]. As is the case for HTGRs, steam generator types and designs vary considerably between
different reactors. Different designs include recirculating systems involving steam drums or OTSGs of
various sizes using different tube shapes and diameters. Due to a large number of different designs, their
thermal-hydraulic instruments are described together instead of allocating separate sections for each. Any
unique instruments that might exist for some designs are also considered. This was deemed appropriate as
the SGs share the same purpose, similar operating conditions, and common safety concerns.
 Although more-conventional designs exist, such as those in EBR-II, the SGs of the Enrico Fermi,
Phenix/Superphenix (France), and Prototype Fast Reactor (PFR, UK) are shown in Figure 17 through
Figure 19. A table summarizing different SG designs from around the globe can be found in Chetal and
Vaidyanathan [64]. As can be seen from Figure 17, the Enrico Fermi reactor SG is a vertical OTSG with
serpentine tubes, producing superheated steam in a single pass [66]. It contains a partial volume of inert gas
in order to stall pressure increase in the event of sodium-water contact, along with a rupture disc to release
reaction products. On the other hand, the Phenix design is particularly unique; each SG contains twelve
serpentine modules (sodium tubes) with each module containing seven water tubes within. Superphenix
SGs, however, have a similar form to a standard OTSG, but include helical tubes and a gas blanket on the
sodium side similar to Fermi design [67]. Last, PFR SGs are composed of three subsystems functioning as
an evaporator, superheater, and reheater, as can be seen in Figure 19. They are integrated to a steam drum
and pump within the plant.

 6.1. Measured Parameters and Instrumentation
 Instrumentation challenges related to sodium-cooled reactor SGs include material-compatibility issues,
high temperatures, and sodium’s high reactivity with water and moisture in air. Conventional methods of
temperature measurements using RTDs and TCs are appropriate. Furthermore, magnetic flow meters for
flow-rate measurements are a simple and reliable way of measuring sodium flow rate. In the context of
sodium-cooled reactor SGs, it is necessary to especially focus on steam-water reactions and sodium-level
measurements.
6.1.1. Sodium-Steam Reaction
 As mentioned previously, sodium-steam reaction is a major concern for SG designs. Therefore,
significant work has been done to investigate measurements techniques that can reliably identify leaks
within a short period. Rapid identification of leaks is crucial for isolation of any affected SGs in order to
prevent damage to the overall system. Leak-identification techniques include chemical methods to detect
reaction products hydrogen or oxygen [61], pressure measurements, and acoustic methods [62, 63].
Multiple devices can also be used, as in Superphenix, where hydrogen detectors were used in conjunction
with a rupture disk [69]. This allowed both the detection of small leaks (<50 g/s) through the hydrogen
detector and fast response to larger leaks (>2,000 g/s) via the rupture disk. It should be noted that other
instruments, such as gas chromatography, and thermal-conductivity measurements can be]used to identify
hydrogen or oxygen within the inert fill gas [43].
 On the other hand, acoustic detection methods are effective for intermediate leaks (50–2,000 g/s) and
rely on (1) sounds emitted by vibrating support structures and sonic velocities at tube-rupture points,
(2) pressure waves created by steam and sodium-steam reactions, (3) stress waves due to rapid changes in

 22

temperature [69]. This method was initially developed for identifying sodium boiling in the steam
generator, but was later extrapolated for sodium-steam reaction applications [70]. Piezoelectric sensors or
accelerometers can be mounted on the shell to obtain signals that are later manipulated using background-
noise information and sophisticated signal-processing techniques to identify leaks [71].
6.1.2.      Level
 Imposing a free sodium level using inert gases is a method used to improve the sensitivity of chemical
leak-detection instruments, such as gas chromatography, and thermal-conductivity methods. Therefore, the
sodium level should be measured to ensure the SG operates as designed. As a reference, the Fermi plant
employed two types of continuous level detectors, special bellow-type pressure detectors and NaK-filled
diaphragm detectors [43, 72]. Both of these detectors measured the pressure difference between the inert-
gas space above the sodium level and at a depth reflecting the desired measurement range. The bellow
detectors are integrated with a pneumatic system to increase response time and transmit pressure over the
SG length. It should be noted that, even though the diaphragm detectors were found to be more accurate,
the bellow detectors were capable of operating for inert-gas pressures below atmospheric. Detailed
descriptions of these instruments can be found in Reicks [72].
Conventional level-measurement techniques, such as differential pressure techniques, are commonly used;
however, the excellent electrical conductivity of sodium also enables the use of conductance/resistance or
inductance techniques. Conductance or resistance probes leverage contact with the sodium coolant in order
to detect its presence at the installed locations [73]. They can be of many shapes and sizes and can use
different circuitry [43]. Conversely, inductance methods do not require direct contact with sodium.
 7. MOLTEN-SALT REACTOR STEAM GENERATORS
 Realized MSR concepts include the Aircraft Reactor Experiment (ARE) [74], and the Molten Salt
Reactor Experiment (MSRE) [75], both conducted by the Oak Ridge National Laboratory. Experience from
these reactors gave rise to other designs such as the Molten Salt Demonstration Reactor [76], the Molten
Salt Breeder Reactor (MSBR) [77], and the Advanced High-Temperature Reactor (AHTR) [78]. Molten
salts as coolants have desirable characteristics because they (1) enable low pressures in the salt loops, (2) do
not have violent exothermic reactions with air or water, as does sodium, (3) have high heat capacities, and
(4) they provide good natural-circulation tendencies [79, 80].
 MSR concepts commonly include the primary loop, an intermediate loop, and a tertiary loop, where
the tertiary loop contains the power-conversion system [81-83]. Out of the past reactor designs mentioned,
MSBR is the only one that includes a supercritical steam-power cycle in which a U-shell, counter-current,
OTSG is used. The design of the MSBR SG is shown in Figure 20. This design was chosen over a
conventional straight shell SG due to a large design temperature difference between the inlet and outlet on
the steam (tube) side [84]. The plant included four identical such SGs for each coolant-salt loop [82]. A
supercritical steam cycle was selected for its efficiency and high temperatures, which are important to
prevent melting on the salt side of the SG.

 23

Figure 20. Conceptual design of the MSBR SGs [77].

 Sabharwall et al. investigated the feasibility of SG concepts for the AHTR in detail [83]. Operating and
design constraints for molten-salt SGs can be outlined as (1) potential solidification of the coolant salt,
diminishing heat transfer, and the potential propagation of the solidification front, (2) corrosion
considerations and the choice of materials compatible with both the coolant salt and steam, (3) tube-rupture
and cracked-header considerations, and (4) thermal and mechanical stresses due to high temperature
gradients. For corrosion issues, although Hastelloy N was identified as showing good corrosion resistance,
hot corrosion, moisture, or oxide impurities, as well as steam on the secondary side, make material
compatibility an important design aspect [83, 85]. Based on a multicriteria decision-making analysis
considering thermal-hydraulic performance, structural performance, and technology readiness, Sabharwall
et al. proposed helical-coil SGs, such as those described for HTGRs in Section 5, and printed-circuit heat
exchangers (PCHE) as a potential solution for the implementation of a steam power cycle to an MSR.
PCHEs were also proposed as a solution recently by He et al. [86].
 7.1. Measured Parameters and Instrumentation
 Operational experience has been obtained for instruments employed in the ARE, MSRE, and MSBR.
These instruments are similar to those used in sodium-cooled reactors because they both operate at high
temperatures, where direct contact with the coolant is normally avoided to prevent corrosion and improve
ease of maintenance. It should be noted that the MSR fuel’s being liquid drastically alters the
instrumentation requirements [87], but this is not applicable to SGs because most designs include an
intermediate loop, as mentioned previously. Important process instruments used in the mentioned reactors
include venturi flowmeters, Inconel-sheathed type-K TCs, diaphragm-type differential pressure
transmitters with NaK impulse lines, bubbler-type level transmitters, conductivity level transmitters,
ultrasonic level transmitters, and pneumatic weighing systems [88].
Specific instruments present in the SG were not identified in literature for the MSBR design. However,
because a U-shell countercurrent once-through design was adopted without the presence of a free coolant
level, it can be deduced that temperature, pressure, and stress-strain measurements are most applicable.

 24

Because thermal stresses due to large temperature gradients were a significant factor in the SG selection
process [84], temperature and stress-strain measurements are of particular importance.
 TC measurements were conducted with type-K TCs in MSRE while type-N TCs were proposed by
Holcomb et al. due to their low drift and decalibration [89]. They also note that sheathing and a thermowell
are necessary, such that they are not grounded to the conductive salt and the sheath is protected from salt
interaction. Furthermore, they propose Au-Pt thermocouples as well, but note that they are not as widely
used in the industry. On the other hand, pressure measurements were demonstrated using pressure
transmitters with NaK-filled impulse lines, as described for sodium-cooled reactors.
 Holcomb et al. investigated the instrumentation necessary for the development of modern MSRs and
determined that, in general, instruments commonly used in high-temperature industrial process plants
should be employed because they are mature and commercially available [89]. However, they proposed
modern instruments that are applicable for consideration that may provide better accuracy or ease of
operation once the technology matures.
 8. DISCUSSION
 Based on the reviewed steam generator designs and instrumentation, it can be deduced that the
parameters of interest are similar due to the components’ common function and operating principles.
Although novel instruments and techniques have been tested in scaled-down models and for experiments
related to advanced reactors, the instruments adapted in operating plants have all used well-established
methods. This is advantageous considering regulatory approvals and personnel operating experience,
especially for sensors critical to the actuation of safety systems. The instrumentation and measurement
techniques identified and reviewed are summarized in Table 1. The methods reviewed include temperature
measurements using TCs and RTDs, level and pressure measurements using pressure transmitters, flow-
rate measurements using pitot tubes and ultrasonic transmitters, vibration measurements using
accelerometers and displacement sensors, as well as moisture detectors. It should be noted that information
on the application of certain SG designs and instruments was not readily available. The most-abundant
information available, both on the SGs themselves and on the instruments employed, were found in legacy
American and Canadian documents.
 Several comments can be made with regard to the particular measurement types. Thermowell-mounted
temperature measurements are used at multiple locations for all reviewed SG types. Thermowell-mounted
temperature sensors are desirable due to their ease of replacement as well as increased reliability because
the shell pressure boundary is not penetrated and the probe is not exposed to coolant. Temperature
measurements are particularly critical for OTSGs and high-temperature reactors due to design limitations
on the allowable temperature difference between shell and tube sides and the inlets and outlets. TC type
should be determined based on the application requirements. Certain applications may require unique
mounting attachments to increase measurement accuracy.
 Level measurements are of crucial importance to proper plant control, monitoring, and the actuation of
safety systems for all SG types containing a free coolant surface or producing superheated steam. The PWR
designs include multiple level-measurement ranges that are used under different operating conditions or for
different purposes. The level is commonly measured using pressure transmitters at multiple pressure taps
along the SG length. Impulse lines filled with NaK or bellow-type detectors can be used for high-
temperature, corrosive, or reactive fluids, where direct contact with the coolant should be avoided.
Furthermore, conductivity or induction-based methods can be employed if the electrical conductivity of the
coolant is sufficiently large. Level measurements must be conditioned based on the measured coolant
temperature in order to accurately compute the coolant density to then infer the level. Reliable level
measurements are imperative to the operation of the plant because it is an important input to plant control
algorithms, especially during off-normal and accident transients. These conditions may include shrink and
swell phenomena during load changes, as well as certain design-basis accidents.

 25

 Pressures are normally measured at pressure taps that may also be used as level taps so that additional
penetration of the pressure boundaries are avoided. For high-temperature fluids, such as liquid metals or
molten salts, NaK-filled impulse lines with diaphragm-type and bellow-type detectors can be used to
prevent freezing, corrosion, or exothermic chemical reactions.
 Flow rates are commonly measured at the feedwater inlet, steam outlet, or other locations in the loop if
the method employed requires boundary penetration. However, for recirculating designs, downcomer flow-
rate measurements are beneficial in multiple ways, including for enabling a direct measurement of the
recirculation ratio. In addition, electrical conductivity of the coolant can enable non-intrusive measurements
using magnetic flow meters.
 Flow-induced vibration or stress-strain measurements are important for the monitoring of SGs with
high flow velocities, bends, and/or large thermal gradients. Vibrations can be caused by turbulence,
instabilities, or general wear and are especially prevalent within U-tubes. Excess vibration can cause
premature tube failure, which can result in costly repairs and plant downtime. Consideration of thermal
stresses is particularly important for SGs with high-temperature fluids: HTGRs, SFRs, and MSRs.

 9. CONCLUSIONS
 Both conventional PWRs and advanced-reactor designs use steam power cycles due to the abundance
of operational experience relative to other cycles. SGs are thus critical components within conventional
PWRs and some advanced-reactor designs because they affect the power-conversion performance.
Instruments are placed at or near SGs for control, monitoring, and the actuation of safety systems. The
present work reviewed thermal-hydraulic instrumentation of various SG designs within different reactor
types. The measured system parameters and instrumentation are reported, focusing on measurements that
are unique to the particular reactor type. It was found that robust methods exist for the measurement of
many fundamental thermal-hydraulic parameters; however, modern techniques should be investigated and
implemented to improve control and monitoring capabilities.
