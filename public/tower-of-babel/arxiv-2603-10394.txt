Moving Phones, Active Peers: Exploring the Effect of Animated Phones as Facilitators in In-Person Group Discussion
Ziqi Pan, Ziqi Liu, Jinhan Zhang, Zeyu Huang, Xiaojuan Ma

Abstract
. In today’s in-person group discussions, smartphones are integrated as intelligent workstations; yet given their co-presence in such face-to-face interactions, whether and how they may enhance people’s behavioral engagement with others remains underexplored. This work investigates how animating personal smartphones to move expressively, without compromising regular functions, can transform them into active embodied facilitators for co-located group interaction. In the four-stranger small-group discussion setting, guided by Tuckman’s group - development theory, we conducted a design workshop (n=12) to identify problematic group-work circumstances and design expressive, attention-efficient animated phone facilitations. Subsequently, we developed AnimaStand , a movement-enabled phone stand that animates phones to deliver group facilitation cues according to conversation dynamics. In a between - subject Wizard - of - Oz study (n=56) with four-stranger group discussions, where everyone’s phone was on an AnimaStand , the facilitations re-engaged inactive members, enhancing group dynamics, task operation performance, and relationships. We finally discuss prospects for more adaptive and generalizable animated personal device facilitation.

1.
Introduction

Smartphones have become vital companions in daily life. In particular, they serve in roles that span from communication devices to mobile productivity workstations
(
110
)
.
As constantly carried items, smartphones effectively engage users with remote collaborators and rich information in the digital space.
Yet in the physical world, given their pervasive co-presence in social life, whether and how smartphones may foster behavioral engagement between users and their co-located peers, like verbal exchanges and non-verbal actions, remains underexplored.
Such interpersonal behavioral engagement is especially important in co-located group work contexts that require sustained collaborative attention and coordination
(
124
)
.
When this engagement drops, for example, when the group falls into pause/silence or some members free-ride with little effort, conversational flow may be disrupted
(
68
)
and groups’ focus on collective goals may be reduced
(
70
;
50
)
. These interactional issues can ultimately impair group effectiveness and rapport
(
115
;
29
;
25
)
.

Previous HCI research has widely explored how co-present devices can facilitate in-person group work, specifically by fostering interpersonal behavioral engagement.
Many studies introduced external third-party artifacts, such as wearables and social robots.
Some focus on mediating relational interactions to catalyze group relationships, utilizing physical interfaces
(
39
;
41
)
and robots
(
107
;
57
;
97
;
122
)
to encourage socializing behaviors like ice-breaking and rapport-building.
Others targeted optimizing collaborative dynamics and consequently enhancing task performance through structuring collaborative behaviors in settings such as meetings, collaborative games, and decision-making. They employed agents or robots to enhance equity in turn-taking and participation
(
64
;
98
)
, also encourage greater involvement
(
81
;
105
)
. Such collaboration process facilitation improved group coordination, thereby enhancing performance
(
95
)
.
Moving beyond introducing new devices with independent identities, another line of work recognized the potential of leveraging intelligent devices that are already co-present with users, notably smartphones.
These studies highlight phones’ sensing, communication, and computational capabilities, equipping them with specialized apps and interfaces (e.g., AR, mobile games)
(
54
;
124
;
87
)
to bolster collaborative social behaviors, thus improving interactional dynamics and relationships.
However, these approaches often depend heavily on the phone’s screen, which may require users to leave regular and ongoing phone usage (e.g., note-taking or messaging) to interact with a separate app.

A question thus arises:
how smartphones could become an embodied facilitator of interpersonal engagement and support co-located groups, while exercising regular productivity support without application switching
?
To answer this question, researchers have explored leveraging smartphones’ modalities beyond the primary on
-
screen interface
(
26
)
, such as sound
(
31
;
32
)
, to support in
-
person group collaboration, typically by enabling multiple people to interact with a shared phone.
Inspired by these prior efforts, in this paper, we propose the design concept to utilize every group member’s own phone, repurposing it from a passive, portable computing tool into an
actively moving device
capable of mediating behavioral engagement in co-located interactions, when necessary, through well-designed modular motions.
This design concept of making phones move actively when needed is both well-motivated and empirically supported.
First, smartphones are already perceived as functional, anthropomorphic, or even ontological extensions of the self
(
84
)
.
Thus, animating them feels less abrupt than introducing new third-party agents; rather, it is more like bringing a previously inert companion to life to better support users’ co-located collaboration.
Second, prior work shows that movements (e.g., proximity changes
(
78
)
, metaphorical actions
(
112
;
69
)
) of smart objects, although not specifically targeting smartphones or group facilitation, can be readily perceived and interpreted as social cues and guidance
(
51
)
.
In this light, phones’ inherent familiarity may allow users to interpret and accept their movement cues even more naturally.
To minimize visual distraction and cognitive load
(
100
;
82
;
65
)
of understanding and following animated phones’ movement facilitations, we argue these movements should be attention-efficient and activated only when necessary.
With careful design of when and how to move, this approach may facilitate group work via augmenting off-screen physical affordances, while preserving the phone’s primary on-screen productivity affordances.

To explore how this design concept of animated phones may facilitate groups across the lifecycle from emergence to dissolution, we conducted our study in a
four
-
stranger roundtable discussion
setting.
To start with, we managed to translate our proposed design concept into a concrete design following the question:
RQ1. When and how should smartphones move to foster interpersonal engagement and facilitate collaboration in co-located groups?

In response to
RQ1
, we first held a
design workshop
(n = 12) grounded in Tuckman’s group development theory
(
108
)
. Through iterative independent and collaborative design activities, participants identified concrete facilitation-needed circumstances, then proposed smartphone behavior designs. Analyzing the resulting designs, we compiled a set of facilitation-needed circumstances with identification rules, and proposed corresponding phone behaviors as facilitation that were expressive and attention
-
efficient.
Next, to realize these movements on phones, we developed
AnimaStand
– a tilted phone stand with two wheels that supports moving, rotating, and signaling with lights. Rather than a standalone agent,
AnimaStand
served purely as an auxiliary phone accessory to animate it.
During group work, when certain problematic circumstances are detected automatically and confirmed manually according to the predefined rules, wizards trigger
AnimaStand
to perform corresponding movements as facilitation.

To investigate how the animated phone facilitations influence group collaboration, and how members’ understandings of such an approach account for these effects, two questions emerge:
RQ2. How does
AnimaStand
influence group collaboration by fostering interpersonal behavioral engagement?
and
RQ3. How do participants perceive and interpret
AnimaStand
and its facilitations?
To answer these questions, we conducted a
between
-
subjects experiment
(n = 56). Groups of four unacquainted participants collaborated to finish an event planning task.
Each participant placed their phone on
AnimaStand
. Seven groups received animated facilitation, while seven control groups did not, with the phone stand staying still. All groups went through Tuckman’s five group-development stages.
Quantitative and behavioral analyses showed that animated facilitation balanced
group interactional dynamics
by re
-
engaging inactive members. Moreover, the facilitations promoted more effective
task operation
procedures, more equal peer contribution, and a trend toward higher
task completion
performance by managing progress via breaking unproductive silences. The perceived
group relationship
was also elevated, largely due to the facilitator’s ability to ease the social atmosphere and resolve conflicts through publicly visible, interpretable movements (
RQ2
). Subjective ratings and interviews further found that participants perceived
AnimaStand
as generally helpful, despite occasional distraction. They made sense of it through a prediction
-
and
-
adaptation process with a transferring strategy, which yielded three distinct interpretive patterns without always matching the design intent (
RQ3
). These results inform future designs of smoother, more adaptive, and more adaptable animated personal devices.

The main contributions of our work to the HCI community are threefold:

•

We empirically explored the design of animated phone behaviors for assisting small-group, in
-
person interactions, grounded in group development, proxemics, and metaphor theories.

•

We revealed the effects of phones embodied with movements on facilitating group interactional dynamics, task operation, and interpersonal relationships.

•

We investigated how group members perceive and interpret animated phone facilitations in ways that may explain their behavioral effects, thereby offering implications for movement
-
enabled personal devices in co
-
located collaboration.

Related Work

2.1.
Small-Group Development: Desired Goals, Challenges, and Facilitation Strategies

Small groups are fundamental collaborative units in human life, enabling members to combine diverse skills and resources toward shared objectives
(
72
)
.
Prior work has extensively examined factors that influence small groups’ effectiveness, including group composition
(
7
)
, cognition
(
118
;
13
)
, and communication
(
34
)
, etc.
Beyond these factor-based perspectives, a family of developmental theories explains how such factors coordinate while groups develop toward effectiveness.
Tuckman’s stage theory is one of the most influential frameworks from such a developmental viewpoint, initially identifying four sequential stages—
Forming
,
Storming
,
Norming
, and
Performing

(
109
)
—and later expanded to include a fifth,
Adjourning

(
108
)
.

Following Tuckman, subsequent work has characterized goals, challenges, and facilitation needs across stages
(
12
;
53
;
1
)
. Overall, although some degree of power or conversational asymmetry can sometimes be functional (e.g., a leader or broker steering information flow)
(
35
;
59
)
, Tuckman-inspired facilitation largely emphasizes role balance and equal voice. In
Forming
, facilitation focuses on rapport building and task/role clarification through orientations, icebreakers, and structured introductions
(
53
;
61
)
. In
Storming
, members negotiate leadership and responsibilities, and facilitation typically centers on conflict mediation and transparent role allocation
(
117
;
96
;
17
;
119
;
12
;
53
;
55
)
.
Norming
aims to build cohesion and interdependence grounded in trust, yet conflicts can recur and regress the group to
Storming

(
46
;
102
;
85
;
18
)
. In
Performing
, groups sustain high efficiency and cohesion, though many never reach it due to unresolved conflicts or fragile norms
(
113
;
103
)
; accordingly,
Norming
is often seen as a prerequisite for
Performing

(
103
)
.
In practice, stages are often blended, and facilitation may address them jointly by promoting balanced participation and interdependence
(
16
)
. Common measures focus on keeping information flow open
(
55
)
and supporting balanced exchange among members
(
44
)
. Finally,
Adjourning
centers on reflecting on achievements and providing closure, with facilitation geared toward farewells and mutual recognition of contributions
(
58
;
2
)
.

We adopt Tuckman’s stage framework to guide our study for three reasons.
First, its stage definitions are highly interpretable, with clear goals that support
designing targeted facilitations
.
Second, it jointly characterizes task activities and group-structure dynamics, offering measurable dimensions for
evaluating facilitation effects
.
Lastly, compared with other developmental theories, such as Gersick’s punctuated
-
equilibrium model
(
33
)
which emphasizes non
-
linear and deadline
-
driven shifts, or McGrath’s TIP (time, interaction and performance) model
(
80
)
that treat development primarily as a function of temporal progression, Tuckman’s stage model occupies a middle ground: it portrays development as non
-
linear yet gradual, with qualitatively distinct stages shaped by more than time alone.
This stage-view makes it well-suited to everyday collaborations without salient deadline shocks, while still accommodating short
-
term groups that pass through several stages within a limited time by tracking qualitative shifts in functioning rather than elapsed time.
However, applying Tuckman’s model to short-term studies requires adaptation. First, in a rather short period, groups may not progress sufficiently through every stage and may show new patterns compared with the previous literature.
For example, the
Adjourning
phase may carry less weight for one-off teams.
Moreover, stage-linked goals and challenges can manifest differently in lab-formed groups.
To address this, we propose to instantiate context-specific challenges and facilitation strategies through a design workshop, resulting in practical animated-phone facilitation designs within an adapted stage framework.

2.3.
Animating Devices with Proxemics and Metaphoric Movement Strategies for Collaborative Interaction Facilitation

Animating objects has been widely studied for enhancing interaction, engagement, and collaboration
(
56
)
. This section outlines the theoretical foundations, namely proxemics and metaphoric movement theories, for designing the behavior of multiple animated personal smartphones as facilitators in small
-
group collaboration, focusing on device
-

-

2.3.1.
Proxemics

Initially introduced by
45
as a social theory describing how people structure interpersonal space, proxemics was later expanded beyond distance zones to a five
-
dimensional concept—distance, identity, location, movement, and orientation (DILMO)—as a design guideline for social interaction in HCI, articulating how people relate to others and their surroundings, including environments and devices
(
60
;
37
;
22
)
.
This perspective has inspired studies on interactive devices that dynamically adjust their spatial relationships with humans as proxemic cues to encourage social and collaborative behaviors.
These studies mainly utilize distance and orientation, given that identity and location configure the context, and movement is often embedded in changes of distance and orientation.
In practice, closer human–device distance and a “toward-and-facing” orientation generally indicate greater willingness to connect and stronger perceived relationships
(
78
)
.
Based on this principle, researchers have shown that orientation control and repositioning can shape social dynamics in human–robot interaction.
For example, studies have oriented robots and controlled gaze to foster inclusive group behavior
(
106
;
19
)
.

36
further developed a humanoid robot as an active moderator that was able to both position and orient itself to engage quieter participants, leading to more balanced turn
-
taking and less subgrouping.
Beyond human-device proxemics, cross-device proxemics also significantly shape human collaboration.
For instance, user-controlled movements of
SocialStool
s helped strangers break the ice
(
39
)
, while the fixed and human-modifiable spatial arrangements of mobile devices and the environment influenced how people shared information
(
38
)
.
Shifting the agency of changing proxemics from human control to autonomous device motions, focusing on autonomous change of the devices’ orientation,
MirrorBot
prompted strangers to converse when mirror-like robots were face
-
to
-
face
(
40
)
.
Building on this, our work explores
how autonomously moving devices drive cross-device proxemics changes in both distance and orientation to influence interpersonal behaviors
.
Particularly, we focus such exploration on personal smartphones.
Recognizing these devices as intimate extensions of the self
(
84
)
, we investigate how their proxemic changes may serve as active social cues to foster collaborative behaviors and facilitate group work.

2.3.2.
Metaphoric Movement

Beyond encoding proxemics cues by dynamically altering phones’ spatial relationships with other entities via simple movements, we also aim to design their motions to be more expressive, enabling them to convey collaboration cues more explicitly when necessary. To this end, we draw on the notion of metaphoric movement.
A metaphor helps us understand something unfamiliar by relating it to something more familiar. According to
69
, metaphor is not merely a linguistic feature but a fundamental structure of thought and experience across multiple modalities.
Prior work has shown that movements can embody metaphors, thereby constituting
metaphoric movements

(
24
;
21
)
. In digital devices such as robots and wearables, these metaphoric movements often follow patterns familiar to humans, making the devices’ behaviors and intentions easier to understand, ultimately increasing acceptance of both the devices and the messages they convey
(
76
)
.
Research on metaphoric movement design spans both individual and group device behaviors. At the individual level, examples include bending robot arms to simulate nodding
(
14
)
, approaching with added shear force as if pushing to attract attention
(
63
)
, or swirling in a dance
-
like manner to attract interaction
(
47
)
. Such movements have been shown to foster positive impressions and increase people’s willingness to interact with digital devices
(
93
)
.
At the group level, coordinated metaphoric formations, such as gathering, mirroring, or weaving in synchrony, have been used to convey togetherness
(
21
;
76
;
63
)
, eliciting trust, positive affect, and a sense of unity
(
62
;
76
)
.
In our study, we propose to design both
individual and group-level metaphoric movements to cope with distinct challenges in different group development stages throughout the collaboration
.
Noting that movement-based facilitations may shift visual attention
(
100
;
82
;
65
)
, we follow the design principle of minimizing attentional demand by using only motions that are essential to express the facilitations’ intention so as to reduce unnecessary attentional load.

3.
Designing Animated Phone Behaviors to Facilitate In-Person Small-Group Collaborative Discussion

To explore when and how smartphones should move to foster interpersonal behavioral engagement and facilitate co-located group collaboration (RQ1), we conducted a design workshop (n=12) in a
four
-
stranger roundtable discussion
setting.
We chose this configuration because newly formed groups of strangers progress through all stages of group development, and a four
-
person group supports both fundamental and more complex interaction dynamics; moreover, circular seating arrangement minimizes predefined territoriality and fosters a socially inviting environment
(
104
)
.
In this setting, participants engaged in two co
-
design activities that identified circumstances requiring facilitation and designed phone movement behaviors to address them.
The resulting designs were later implemented in prototype systems, which informed a follow
-
up study on their behavioral and perceptual effects.

3.1.
Workshop Probes

We designed two types of probes to prompt participants systematically identify problematic circumstances in group discussions and ideate corresponding animated phone movements as facilitations.

3.1.1.
Contextual Probe: Small-Group Development Stages, Challenges, and Goals in the Four-Person Roundtable Discussion Scenario

To help participants systematically identify issues that may hinder collaboration across different stages of group development, we prepared a set of predefined problematic circumstances as contextual probes grounded in Tuckman’s theory
(
108
)
.
First, we reviewed related literature (Sec.
2.1
) and identified desired goals, facilitation-needed circumstances, and facilitation strategies of each stage.
Then, since these descriptions of the facilitation-needed circumstances are often generic, we elaborated them into scenario
-
specific accounts for a four
-
person roundtable by systematically enumerating possible participant configurations (Table
1
).
This set, while based on prior studies and potentially incomplete, served as a contextual probe in the workshop, where participants could add, remove, or refine circumstances.

3.1.2.
Technical Probe: Movable Phone Stand with Controllable Lights Prototype

To exemplify how a phone could be animated on a table, we built an auxiliary accessory, which is a movable phone stand (Fig.

This two

3.2.
Workshop Procedure

3.2.1.
Setup

As in Fig.
2
B, the workshop took place in-person in a room with a round table. Each session replicated a four
-

-

The worksheet was divided into two sections, one for documenting discussion circumstances and one for sketching corresponding design ideas, allowing participants to link situations and facilitation concepts using sticky notes and written drafts. Each also received a phone
-
stand prototype and a cheatsheet of its physical capabilities.

3.2.2.
Participants and Procedure

Approved by IRB, we recruited 12 workshop participants (5 male, 7 female, age
23.8\pm 4.0

Each workshop session lasted for about 120 minutes (compensated 120 HKD) and consisted of a briefing and two activities with a 5-minute break in between.
During the
Briefing

Subsequently, in
Activity 1

3.3.
Data Analysis

Using the workshop recordings and worksheets, two authors conducted a theory
-
driven deductive–inductive analysis of participants’ ideation on problematic group discussion circumstances and animated phone behaviors. We first refined the set of problematic circumstances by discarding contextual probe entries without participant-proposed facilitation designs and merging participant
-
suggested edits from
Activity 1
. For the corresponding phone animation designs from
Activity 2
, we deduplicated ideas and analyzed the resulting concepts through two lenses. First, following
69
, we identified each design’s underlying interaction intention (e.g., expressing closeness, signaling turn
-
taking). Second, using an extended proxemics framework (Sec.
2.3.1
), we coded designs along five socio
-

(
100
;
82
)
, we synthesized designs for each refined circumstance that balance expressiveness and attention economy by assigning only the most relevant phones the minimal actions needed to achieve intended interaction goals.

3.4.
Findings

3.4.1.
Circumstances Require Facilitation and Desired Facilitation Goals Based on Participants’ Feedback

Analyzing participants’ feedback on the circumstance probes, we identified two cross
-
group modifications.
First, all groups recommended
adding “All Members Being Silent”
to both the
Storming
and
Norming–Performing
stages, viewing it as a more urgent concern than leadership competition during the
Storming
stage. Two groups (G1 and G2) further noted that, in the
Norming–Performing
stage, members may also work solely without collaborating. This emphasis may stem from the workshop’s focus on collaboration among strangers rather than more established teams in literatures.
Second, participants recommended
adding “Conflict between Members” also to the
Norming-Performing
stage
, arguing that task
-
focused disagreements are more common than leadership disagreements in a stranger group, and likely to intensify as work progresses.
Participants’ categorization of the problematic circumstances when discussing a consensus modified list of circumstances also indicated a refinement in desired facilitation goals.
In particular, participants (G1, G2, G3) more detailedly articulated two objectives for the
Norming–Performing
stage:
greater engagement from all members; and more balanced participation and interdependence without overt dominance
.
Although expert-led task completion can be effective in some contexts, participants prioritized equal contribution, characterizing effective groups as combining task efficiency with a positive interpersonal climate. This preference was likely shaped by our four-stranger setup, which did not presuppose ability differences, and by the participant pool: many were university students accustomed to assessment contexts (e.g., graded group projects) that emphasize equal contribution.

3.4.2.
Identification of the Problematic Circumstances

Participants also provided insights on how to identify the problematic circumstances.
They generally proposed an approach that combines
conversational features with context-aware interpretation
.
Consistent with our earlier
findings of desired facilitation goal
, they implied participation and interdependence as key indicators of group dynamics in their suggestions of concrete detection cues.
For example, extended speech or prolonged silence could signal excessive or insufficient participation (G1, G3). While frequent two-person turn-taking might indicate interdependence, when combined with contextual cues such as volume level and discussion topic, it can be interpreted as a sign of conflict (G2).
Participants also noted that
while some situations should be detected automatically, others may be better left for users to assess directly
. For instance, it would be better to let users decide whether they lack connection with another member (e.g., upstream/downstream members), based on the task’s progress, rather than relying on automatic detection (G2, G3).
Moreover, participants noted that
some circumstances that often arise when entering a new stage are not necessarily problematic, but still worth supporting
.
For example, in the adjourning stage, when the group is about to disband, although failing to conclude properly may have little impact on group performance as the group no longer exists, ending with a friendly exchange of contact information and goodbyes can still be valuable (G1, G2).

3.4.3.
Movement Design Ideations

The role of the animated phone facilitation differs in different cases.

Participants’ designs assigned two types of roles to the animated phones based on members’ awareness of the collaborative issue and their intent to intervene.
When members want to fix an issue but are socially constrained from addressing it, they designed the phone to act as a
spokesperson
, performing actions on their behalf (e.g., when the ongoing discussion is hard to interrupt, users can manipulate their phones to approach or signal a peer’s phone, discreetly requesting the owner’s attention; G1, G2, G3).
When members are unaware of an issue and do not actively address it, the phones are supposed to serve as a
role model
, demonstrating the desired behavior (e.g., a silent member’s phone standing out and rotating to cue both the owner and others). In this latter case, the phone’s expressive actions redirect attention and are understood then accepted by participants, prompting them to adjust their behavior.

Design animated phone facilitations in modules.

Across groups, participants repeatedly reused similar animations to express similar facilitation intentions, suggesting a modular design approach.
Both the formation of a group of phones and the individual movements could be reused for different circumstances across stages. Group formations like phones gathering and moving synchronously could be leveraged to support cohesion in orientation, leader election, and adjourning. While individual movements could be mapped to human-like behaviors in the same way across circumstances, for instance, blinking lights to indicate speaking, moving to the center and rotating to attract attention, or orienting toward another phone to convey support. For example, in the cases of 1, 2, or 3 members dominating, G1 proposed one design where less active members’ phones move to the center and rotate to attract attention, adjusting only the number of rotating phones to match each case. Such modularity supports coherent, interpretable facilitation with lower cognitive load across circumstances.

Final design of animated phone facilitation behaviors across small-group development stages, showing circumstance types with qualitative identification rules, and facilitation designs with brief descriptions and figure demonstrations.

4.3.
Automated Warning and Facilitation Recommendation Generation for Problematic Circumstances

4.3.1.
Live Diarization and Speech Data Processing

The back
-
end web application first performs live speaker diarization on the audio stream captured during discussions.
Based on all participants’ pre
-
registered voices before each session, we employ the Pyannote toolkit to identify the primary speaker of each second. The diarization did not include the Auto Speech Recognition (ASR)-based content extraction, as ASR accuracy issues could introduce systematic biases.
The diarization output is stored as a growing
n\times 4
matrix, where each column corresponds to a participant and each row indicates their speaking activity at a given second-level timestamp.
For each current timestamp
t
, we apply a sliding
-
window analysis (defined as one
evaluation
) over the preceding 60 seconds
[max(0,t-60s),t]
to compute two conversation feature matrices. These matrices provide a real
-
time quantitative view of participation and interdependence dynamics, which is key to subsequent detection of problematic circumstances.

•

Speech distribution matrix
: a
1\times 4
matrix recording the cumulative speaking time of each participant within the window. An unsupervised k-means clustering algorithm (
k=2
) is then applied to automatically distinguish between dominant and non-dominant speakers based on their relative speaking times.

•

Directed turn
-
taking matrix
: a
4\times 4
matrix in which each entry
(i,j)
records the number of times speaker
i
is followed by speaker
j
, capturing the turn transitions with directions.

We selected a 60
-
second window based on pilot testing and prior work showing that the typical duration per speaker per turn is 15–20 seconds. In four
-
person groups, this 60-second window accommodates roughly one turn per member.

4.3.2.
Warning and Recommendation Generation Algorithm

Leveraging the above metrics, we implemented rule
-
based detection of problematic circumstances as shown in Fig.
5
.
Because workshop participants described how to identify problematic circumstances qualitatively (e.g., ”a certain amount of time”), we established our final time thresholds and parameters by calibrating empirical speaking-turn baselines (15–20 seconds
(
91
)
) through pilot trials.
These detection results serve only as warnings rather than automatic triggers, because they solely rely on conversational dynamics that are inadequate to generate highly responsible decisions.
The final decision to trigger a facilitation is made by a human administrator, considering additional contextual and semantic information. For example, the administrator could delay a facilitation when the situation does not welcome disruptions (e.g., when a participant is writing and blocking the phone’s movement path).

-

-

5.1.
Task Design

We designed a four
-

-

Storming
and
Norming–Performing

Adjourning

Figure 7
.

(2) Task completion procedure;

Aiming to explore more of group-level interaction dynamics, we embedded individual tasks within collaborative group activities to elicit richer and more complex interpersonal interactions. Following this principle, the task comprised three components: (1) electing roles and a leader (
Storming)
, (2) selecting, through discussion, one optimal and one backup plan from four initial proposals, and (3) each participant completing an individual preparation sheet in their role for each selected plan, which together formed a complete proposal (
Norming–Performing
). The execution order, strategy, and methods were entirely participant
-

The task should possibly evoke the problematic circumstances in group work as listed in Fig.
3
, so that facilitations could be triggered and sequentially evaluated. While challenges of
Forming
and
Adjourning
stage naturally occur for our four-stranger new group setting,
Storming
and
Norming–Performing
challenges specifically regarding leadership, conflict, participation, and interdependence should be deliberately induced by the task design. These four challenges were induced through the following features:

•

Leadership competition:
Leaders bore a heavier workload and were eligible for an exclusive bonus (HKD 20 on top of a HKD 100 base payment) for achieving over 85% completion of their departmental sheet for the optimal plan, incentivizing leadership competition.

•

Conflict of interest:
Each member had a private, role
-
based preference for the optimal plan and was eligible for a performance bonus (HKD 20 on top of a HKD 100 base payment) if the group selected their preferred plan, creating goal misalignment.

•

Role differentiation introduced information asymmetry calling for participation
: Once roles (Event, Treasurer, Logistics, or Publicity Officer) were assigned, each participant joined one private Slack channel and received only division
-
specific reference and task sheets, making everyone’s full participation necessary to construct any complete plan.

•

Interdependence:
All departmental sheets required information held exclusively by others, making dyadic information exchange unavoidable.

5.2.3.
Experimental Conditions and Procedure

Upon the arrival of the four participants, the administrators obtained informed consent and requested them to register their voices for live-speech diarization later in the session.

Finally, all participants completed a post

5.3.
Measures

To investigate the effects of
AnimaStand

group interactional dynamics
(RQ2a),

(RQ2b), and
relational climate
(RQ2c). These dimensions draw on Tuckman’s two realms of group development
(
108
)
, namely the interpersonal (the way members acted and related to one another) and task-activity (the content of the action as related to the task) realms. Along each dimension, we first assessed a set of metrics and then conducted behavioral analyses to reveal how
AnimaStand
’s facilitation of interpersonal engagement shaped the measure results.
To explain these effects from a participant’s perspective, we further examined how participants
perceived
(RQ3a) and
interpreted
(RQ3b)
AnimaStand
, primarily through post
-
experiment questionnaires and semi
-

Next, we detail how each dimension is measured.

5.3.1.
RQ2a: Group Interactional Dynamics

Following SYMLOG methodology
(
3
)
, which examines behaviors in overt interaction and nonverbal characteristics, we analyzed verbal interactions quantitatively and nonverbal behaviors qualitatively, using speech diarization data and session recordings, respectively.
First, we calculated the
duration of each group development stage
to provide a foundational overview.
Then, because nonverbal behaviors are hard to quantify and interpret their intent, and task-related interactions in our discussion setting are mostly verbal, we used conversational dynamics for statistical evaluation and nonverbal cues as qualitative evidence.

Quantitative Measures of Conversations

In line with participants’ desired goals from the design workshop of greater involvement with more balanced participation and interdependence, we analysed group-level conversational dynamics during discussion with three statistical measures.

Speech Coverage Rate
(Overall Level of Participation): Proportion of time that is not silent (at least one speaker is speaking) within an observed period.
Formula:
\text{SCR}_{i}=\frac{T_{\text{non-silent}}}{T_{\text{total}}}
,
where
T_{\text{non-silent}}
is the non-silent time and
T_{\text{total}}
is the total duration.
The value ranges from 0 (completely silent) to 1 (continuous speaking).

Normalized Speech Distribution Entropy
(Level of Balanced Participation): Measures the evenness of speaking time distribution across speakers.

H^{*}_{\text{speech}}=\begin{cases}\frac{-\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}\log_{2}p_{i}}{\log_{2}N},&\text{if }\sum_{i=1}^{N}T_{i}>0\\
0,&\text{if no speech occurs}\end{cases}

where
p_{i}=\frac{T_{i}}{\sum_{j=1}^{N}T_{j}}
. The value ranges from 0 (highly uneven
like only one speaker ever talked
or no speech) to 1 (perfectly even,
everyone talked for the same duration
).

Normalized Turn-taking Entropy
(Level of Balanced Interdependence): Quantifies the diversity and balance of turn-taking between speakers.

H^{*}_{\text{turn}}=\begin{cases}\frac{-\sum_{a=1}^{N}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}b=1\\
b\neq a\end{subarray}}^{N}q_{a\to b}\log_{2}q_{a\to b}}{\log_{2}[N(N-1)]},&\text{if total turns}>0\\
0,&\text{if no turns occur}\end{cases}

where
q_{a\to b}=\frac{C_{a\to b}}{\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}^{N}C_{i\to j}}
. The value ranges from 0 (concentrated turns like only between two speakers or no turns) to 1 (fully balanced turns
like a round-robin
).

Qualitative Measures of Behaviors Induced by
AnimaStand

We examine behaviors induced by
AnimaStand
along four features: facilitation type, the actor of the behavior, temporal offset between the reaction and the facilitation trigger, and behavior type (clustered after annotation) with supporting details; the measurement procedure is described later in Sec.
5.4
.

5.3.2.
RQ2b: Task Operation and Performance

Measures for Task Operation

We measured the task operation from a stage-based perspective.
Beyond the basic five stages as in Tuckman’s group development theory
(
108
)
, we further identified three task operation substages during the
Norming-Performing
stage based on pilot observations:

•

Initialization
: After roles are settled and received task materials, members familiarize themselves with their respective information solely without engaging in discussion.

•

Regular Operation
: Beginning from the first discussion between more than two members that breaks in silence, and lasts until the “15 minutes remaining” alert.

•

Countdown Operation
: From the last-15-minute alert until the task completion. The duration may vary slightly due to timing inaccuracies and situational factors (e.g., some groups may report the task has been complete before the deadline).

Building on this stage view, we first measured task operation from a
stage-temporal perspective
. We calculated the duration of each stage and substage, and used both these durations and the transitions between stages to characterize the progression speed.
Then, from a
content perspective
, we identified key task-operation milestones:

•

Information Alignment
: Members exchanged role-specific information to establish a shared understanding

•

Proposal-completion Discussion
: Members focused on discussing one initial proposal at a time and collaboratively elaborating it (as described in Sec.
5.1
), proceeding through the first, second, third, and fourth proposals.

Based on these milestones, we assessed the extent to which the task materials were discussed and the extent to which the task was completed.
Combining the temporal and content perspectives, we further characterized participants’ task
-
operation strategies.

Measures for Task Performance

We evaluated task performance from both objective and subjective perspectives.
Objectively, we calculated
task completion rate
as the proportion of filled blanks in the plan form (Fig.
8(c)
).
Subjectively, we investigated
self-assessed group performance
: (1) confidence in the outcome, and (2) satisfaction with the outcome (both in a 5-point Likert scale); and
self-assessed peer contribution
(Table
2
, Q1–Q2). Additionally, we obtained explanations of impressions of each member’s contributions in the interview (Table.
2
Q5).

RQ2c: Relational Climate

We investigate
AnimaStand

First, the
typical lively moments
triggered by
AnimaStand

Second, we employed
oneness

IOS

We

30
)

Step1:\text{Oneness}_{ij}=\frac{\text{IOS}_{ij}+\text{WeScale}_{ij}}{2},\quad\text{Oneness}_{ij}\in[1,7]

Step2:m_{i}=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}^{N}\ \text{Oneness}_{ij}

Step3:Oneness_{g}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}m_{i}

where:

•

N=4
is the group size.

•

\text{IOS}_{ij}
and
\text{WeScale}_{ij}
are the IOS and We Scale ratings from member
i
to member
j

\text{Oneness}_{ij}
is their average, ranging from 1 to 7.

•

m_{i}
is the minimum oneness score given by member

•

Oneness_{g}
is the group oneness score for group

In the above calculation,

During regular discussions, all groups faced noticeable participation imbalance (e.g., prolonged silence from some members or dominance by a few) with

In our coding, the non-verbal re-engagement only counted those reactions that showed observable attention shifts to the group discussion, such as leaning in to follow ongoing discussions (E3P1).
In the experimental condition, basic
Participation–Balance

In the remaining 18 cases, six were unsuitable for strengthened facilitation (e.g., phones physically obstructed), while the other 12 triggered the strengthened facilitation (active member’s phone moving toward the least active and pulling it toward the center), with nine resulting re-engagement (six verbal, two verbal back-channelling, and one non-verbal).
The re-engagement types and distributions prompted by basic and strengthened facilitation were largely similar.

AnimaStand
’s Inviting Movements Encouraged Salient Verbal Engagement from Both Inactive and Active Members, Balancing Participation and Interdependence

To reveal the mechanism by which these interventions influenced the members and ultimately balanced the groups’ dynamics (as in Fig.
10

Firstly, we discovered that
the moving phones not only prompted their inactive owners, but also other active members
. Inactive members responded directly as the first speaker in 36 instances (31 basic, five strengthened). Alternatively, in 13 other instances (12 basic, one strengthened), an active member noticed the facilitation and explicitly invited the inactive member to join first, then the inactive ones followed.
For example, after E7P3’s phone rotated, E7P4 invited them within 13 seconds (including 8 seconds of phone movement), prompting immediate engagement. Similarly, E6P1 always (5 out of 9 facilitations) initiated talk with the inactive upon noticing such phone behaviors. Such inviting behavior between members created more opportunities for them to release prosocial signs to others, which may have led to enhanced relationships
(
15
)
.

Moreover, regardless of who initiated the speech after the facilitation, we discovered that
when the inactive members rejoined the discussion, they re-balanced both the participation and interdependence
(Fig.
12(a)
).
When re-engaged, they often tried to offer adequate input, thereby increasing their speaking time (the dot size representing speech time grows larger after re-engagement).
Since inactive members also had withheld unique knowledge during their prolonged silence, others often expected more information exchange with them once they were “back-online”.
This often triggered turn-taking between the inactive and other members (more links appear after re-engagement), strengthening and balancing interdependence.
However, in some cases, participation balance did not improve within the next few minutes. We observed that some previously inactive members, once prompted, tended to engage in reactive over-speaking—continuously talking for an extended period after a long silence, as shown in Fig.
12(b)
. This ironically made them the most dominant speaker within the surrounding windows and thus reduced the local participation balance.

’s Facilitations Persisted When Intervention Frequency Dropped During the Countdown Operation

Upon receiving the “last 15 minutes” warning, all groups, experimental or control, expressed surprise at the limited time remaining and accelerated their task progress.
In reaction to the reminder of the time limit, in experiment conditions, speech coverage rate and speech distribution entropy rose significantly (paired-test
p=.01,d=.39

p=.03,d=1.00

4.81\pm 0.95

In contrast, control groups showed no significant improvement in all metrics, even a drop in speech coverage rate (Fig.

This implies that the group momentum for experiment groups improved by the animated phone interventions during the regular operation period may have persisted even with fewer interventions, while control groups did not enjoy such carry-over benefits.
It may be because the participants in the experimental condition already formed the impression that they should “
at least say something, as we have been reminded throughout
”(E5P1).
On the contrary, members in the control groups did not develop such an awareness to be more active, and thus “
As the task proceeded to the last minutes, seeing the growing pile of todos, I felt desperate and did not know what to contribute

According to the calculation of oneness in Sec.

Nonetheless, qualitative interviews or behavioral observations may reveal nuances beyond current measures.
Next, we examine how
AnimaStand
’s facilitation cultivated collective oneness from two perspectives: by positively fostering interpersonal relationships, and by addressing issues that could potentially undermine them.

AnimaStand
’s Synchronous Movements Added Lively Vibe and Emphasized Cohesion, Improving Collective Oneness

Although most of
AnimaStand
facilitations are not designed to elevate atmosphere, many of them also stimulated socializing responses.
In particular, this often happened in facilitations where phones moved in synchronized group formations.
The cohesion indicated by the phones encouraged participants’ more social engagement, which may foster a sense of oneness.
For instance, during
Forming
, participants’ first conversations are often concerning jointly interpreting the intention and metaphor behind the
Icebreaking
facilitation. Similarly, in
Norming-Performing
, as phones moved synchronizedly in the
Silence
-
Breaking
facilitation, participants exhibited a series of notable immediate reactions, including describing the movement, speculating about its intention or purpose, and expressing amusement through laughter or chuckles. These actions helped alleviate the awkward atmosphere created by the prolonged silence. Some even cited this as the most pleasant moment of the session (E1P1&4, E2P2, E3P1, E4P1), highlighting its value as both a progress and social trigger.

AnimaStand
’s Public Intervention Invited Joint Effort to Solve Relational Problems in Some Cases.

While conflict is a well-documented topic in group dynamics research
(
1
)
, only two incidents occurred in our study; both were in the experimental condition and were successfully resolved.
In one, E2P3 and E2P4 argued over a venue for 10+ rounds until the phones approached, blinked, and returned. Their attention shifted to interpreting the movement, and they agreed to follow the opinion of the member whose role related to venue arrangements (E2P3).
In the other, a disagreement between E6P3 and E6P4 triggered the same intervention; E6P1, who was not one of the quarreling, interpreted it as a de
-
escalation cue and proposed a vote to solve the conflict.
These cases suggest that
Conflict-Solving
facilitation, like
Participation-Balance
facilitation, cues both the phones’ owners and others to act. With publicly visible phone movements enabling all peers to intervene and share responsibility for group regulation, these facilitations cultivate the oneness within the group.
Comparatively, private intervention – the
Connection-Tickle
– designed to address the lack of interdependence between two members, was difficult to conceive under the tight time constraints and was applied only twice by the same participant (E1P3): once during the
Norming–Performing
stage and once in the
Adjourning
, purely for amusement.
Although private intervention showed limited practical utility in fast-paced, in-person discussions, it may still ease tension and foster a positive group atmosphere in a lighthearted way.

, indicating generally positive affective impressions. As some participants (E1P3, E2P2, E4P2) echoed, “It is fun to have the phone moving occasionally, while I can still use it.”
However, although distraction received the lowest ratings, it still exceeded neutral, suggesting that distractions presented by
AnimaStand

Regarding expectations for future

6.4.2.
Interpretation of
AnimaStand
(RQ3b)

As reported above, participants were generally able to understand what the
AnimaStand
meant. Next, we analyze how they interpreted its movements throughout the experiment.

Participants Adopted Transferring Strategy when Interpreting Facilitation Movements

The
Storming
stage in our study was generally smooth in both conditions. This may be because participants were strangers and remained courteous despite a leader’s bonus.
In the only two cases where
Leader
-
Election
facilitation was triggered (phones gathered, then pushed the member who had spoken most, with others blinking), the prompted participants both quickly (16s, 18s) guided the election process, though neither chose to become leader (E1P3, E5P4).
Such quick responses likely resulted from their recent exposure to facilitation in the
Forming
stage, enabling them to transfer their understanding, “
I said something because from previous experiences I figured out when it comes out, I should speak
”(E5P4).
This observation partially validates our design choice of modularizing phone behavior design, where shared movement patterns allow participants to reuse interpretive frames for faster, more effective responses.

Participants Interpreted Facilitation Movements Through Prediction-and-Adaptation, Yielding Three Patterns of Interpretation Results

Participants’ sensemaking of
AnimaStand
followed a typical pattern. They first
predicted
movement meanings using prior knowledge. For example, linking phones facing others with displayed names to familiar name tags usage (E7P4).
Over time, participants may
adapt
their responsive behaviors iteratively according to
AnimaStand
’s movements, as E3P1 reflected, “
When I hadn’t talked for a while, my phone moved out; when I spoke, it returned. After seeing this a few times, I knew I should speak when it moves out.
”

Because this predictive–adaptive interpretation is not anchored to an explicit ground truth, participants sometimes assigned meanings to facilitation movements that did not match the design intent. We observed three patterns of interpretation outcomes.
First, some interpretations
aligned with the design intent
, typically enabling expected responses.
However, participants occasionally ignored prompts because they felt other matters were more important or because of cognitive or emotional fatigue. For example, “When it prompts me to speak but I have nothing to say, I feel stressed and lose focus” (E4P2).
Second, some interpretations
diverged from the design intent
.
In our design, we framed facilitation movements at a content-free behavioral level. They were intended to shape interactional dynamics such as switching speakers, managing speech duration, etc.,
Most “misaligned” interpretations instead mapped facilitation to broader task
-
related behaviors with concrete content. For example, some participants interpreted the move
-
out and rotate movements as “share the hidden information” rather than simply “talk more.” (E1P1, E6P4)
This misaligned content-based mapping sometimes still coincidentally led to behaviors that matched our intended effects: when participants were withholding information, they may respond by speaking (as we expected) and sharing it. However, when they were not hiding anything but were simply less engaged, this interpretation could lead to no action.
Finally, some participants
failed to form any clear interpretation
, e.g., “
I don’t know how it operated, it is a bit disruptive
” (E5P1). This results in puzzled gazes and minimal engagement, which is only a reaction toward a visual stimulus.

7.
Discussion

We summarize our key findings as follows:

•

(RQ1)
Design Workshop
: We proposed modular designs for animated phone facilitation in in
-
person group discussions, incorporating instantiated circumstances, rationale for identifying circumstances, and phone
-
movement design strategies.

•

(RQ2)
Effects of
AnimaStand
on group collaboration
: In terms of group interactional dynamics (RQ2a),
AnimaStand
improved engagement and balanced interactional dynamics by inviting inactive members; regarding task operation and performance (RQ2b), it streamlined procedures and promoted a trend toward higher completion by breaking silences; concerning relationships (RQ2c), it elevated perceived group cohesion by promoting socializing interaction and resolving conflicts through publicly visible, interpretable movements.

•

(RQ3)
Participants’ perception and interpretation
AnimaStand
: Participants perceived
AnimaStand
as generally helpful, despite occasional distraction. They made sense of it through a prediction
-
and
-
adaptation process with a transferring strategy, which yielded interpretive patterns that did not always align with the original design intent.

Based on these findings, we first discuss the
AnimaStand
facilitation mechanism in terms of engagement lift and interpretation. Drawing on these discussions, we further propose directions for developing animated personal device facilitation for group collaboration and for repurposing everyday objects as embodied intelligent facilitators of human interaction in the physical world. Finally, we present the limitations and future works of this paper.

7.1.
Engagement Management in Group Collaboration

In designing
AnimaStand
, we explicitly targeted the enhancement of behavioral engagement and implicitly assumed that higher, more equalized engagement would be positively associated with improved group collaboration performance.
In this section, we first examined the potential of this behavioral pathway for fostering overall engagement.
Then, we discussed whether our assumption holds and what may constitute an optimal level of engagement for productive group collaboration, and how facilitation should be configured under different conditions to achieve it.

7.1.1.
Potential of Boosting Behavioral Engagement to Lift Overall Engagement

Behavioral, emotional, and cognitive engagements are highly intercorrelated. Sometimes, the enhancement of one type of engagement may simultaneously improve the other two.
This raises a critical question: what behavioral engagements indicate a concurrent increase in participants’ cognitive and emotional engagement towards group work?
We observed that when participants perform substantive behaviors in response to
AnimaStand
’s cues, like verbally sharing opinions or consulting their own materials in order to answer others’ questions, they actively commit their attention to contribute to group work for a sustained period. Consequently, these
commitments
direct their focus deeper into the task, boosting overall engagement.
However, when users perform passively reactive behaviors, like low-effort follow-up questions or backchannels, they are likely to merely comply with participatory expectations posed by their groupmates and the
AnimaStand
. Such
compliances
sometimes fail to advance the collaborative process and may instead introduce interactional “noise” (as in Sec.
6.1.2
).

Although both active commitment and mere compliance are responses to
AnimaStand
’s cues, the divergence between them may stem from several reasons.
First, users may already be
emotionally or cognitively fatigued
to engage in group work. They were either tired of the task or introverted and reluctant to engage in multi-party collaboration, leading them to offer minimal reactions.
Second, users may perceive a
mismatch between the cognitive engagement demanded by
AnimaStand
’s cue and their current situational needs
.
To stay focused on individual work (e.g., reading materials) rather than the collaborative work, or because the current discussion was too heated for their role as a non-leader member to interrupt, participants may deliberately backchannel in response to
AnimaStand
’s facilitation to convey politeness.
Therefore, to foster active
commitment
that elicits simultaneous behavioral, cognitive, and emotional engagement over mere
compliance
, future facilitations require more adaptive strategies. Such strategies should dynamically align with participants’ momentary readiness and their perceived value of contribution (discussed in Sec.
7.3.1
).

7.1.2.
Configuring Facilitation for Optimal Engagement throughout Group Collaboration

Optimal Engagement Level for Group Collaboration

Previous work has extensively investigated how individual arousal levels may influence their performance. According to Yerkes–Dodson’s law
(
120
)
(as in Fig.
17(a)
), arousal and performance follow an inverted-U relationship: moderate arousal optimizes performance, while excessive arousal becomes counterproductive. Furthermore, this optimal threshold varies by expertise; experts can handle higher arousal and reach a higher performance peak than novices.
Our experimental findings (Sec.
6.1.2
) suggested a parallel dynamic for group-level engagement:
group performance increases with engagement level, but may only up to an optimum.

During the experiment, we noticed that the control groups perceived the spike of engagement in the last countdown task completion substage as “desperate and overwhelming”, indicating they had crossed into an overloaded, counterproductive state.
Moreover, we noted that the
peak performance and its corresponding optimal engagement level varied depending on group maturity
, mirroring the expert-novice divide.
Under comparable high engagement in the
countdown operation
substage, experimental groups collaborated fluently and self-regulated engagement effectively, whereas control groups struggled.
This divergence may partially stem from differences in group maturity.
When entering the
countdown
substage, the experimental groups were more mature as they initiated discussions earlier and experienced more interpersonal coordination throughout earlier substages. Such maturity of collaboration routines and task familiarity may help them to manage themselves better with higher engagement.

In addition, in group collaboration,
equalizing engagement among members does not guarantee improved task performance
.
Though experimental group members were collaborating more fluently with more even peer evaluation, the confidence and satisfaction ratings, as well as the completion rates of the task outcomes, were not significantly higher (Sec.
6.2.2
).
This suggests that, beyond our facilitation strategy that emphasizes equalizing engagement, it may be beneficial to allocate engagement in a more role- and strength-sensitive manner for better task outcomes
(
11
;
6
)
.
In particular, when a specific role or capability is especially critical for a given subtask, the member who holds that role or capability may need to engage more intensely than others. In this way, the strengths of expert-led group work (e.g., efficiency and clarity of direction) can be combined with the benefits of equal engagement (e.g., inclusiveness and shared ownership).

Facilitation Strategies to Configure Optimal Engagement

Situational leadership principles
(
117
)
advocate adapting leadership style to group maturity, giving more autonomy from the leader to the group as the group matures. Building on this view and our earlier discussion of optimal engagement, we argue that animated phone facilitation toward each member of a group should likewise be adjusted situationally, based on both the individual’s role criticality and the group’s maturity.
A mismatch between facilitation style and situational demands can either lead a mature group to become over
-
reliant on facilitation cues to push progress forward (as in Sec.
6.1.1
), with performance might be undermined by underloaded engagement, or cause a newly formed group to become overloaded and exhausted (as in Sec.
6.1.2
).
We therefore propose a
Role Criticality–Group Maturity Model
that specifies four facilitation styles according to different role criticality–group maturity conditions as task progresses (Fig.
17(b)
), in order to guide individual engagement in groups and support sustained peak collaborative performance.
Particularly, we suggest adjusting the frequency and intensity of facilitation movements to realize divergent facilitation styles.

•

Hands-on Coaching
(high criticality, low maturity): stimulate a critical member’s positive engagement through frequent and exaggerated movements.

•

Keep-up Directing
(low criticality, low maturity): keeps a non-critical member on pace with the group through frequent yet less intense cues.

•

Strategic Prompting
(high criticality, high maturity): nudges a capable critical member at key moments with low-frequency but salient movements, emphasizing their role while respecting group autonomy.

•

Subtle Reminding
(low criticality, high maturity): maintains a non-critical member’s awareness with low-frequency and low-intensity movements as reminders, largely granting autonomy to the group.

7.2.
Mechanisms and Pitfalls in Interpreting Animated Facilitation

7.2.1.
Attention Shift toward Interpretation of the Animated Facilitation

Post
-
task questionnaires and observational notes indicated that participants perceived
AnimaStand
, in their role as visual stimuli, to be both distractive and helpful (Sec.
6.4.1
). One possible explanation for this dual effect is an attention shift during sensemaking of the
AnimaStand
’s movements through interpreting and coming up with a response, which can be beneficial or harmful.

On the positive side, momentary shifts in focus occasionally contributed to improved interpersonal relationships and diffused tension in conflicts, such as triggering shared laughter in awkward silence or redirecting quarrels toward commenting on the facilitation (Sec.
6.3
).
However, it is questionable whether this is solely a benefit of facilitator design. It is also possible that positive feedback was affected by a novelty effect, which means that users tend to perceive new technology more positively in the first encounters
(
101
;
88
)
. Longitudinal within
-
participant studies are needed to separate novelty from sustained impact and to refine movement
-
based cues that retain relational benefits without undermining task focus.
On the negative side, two distinct mechanisms may account for the perceived distraction accompanying sensemaking.
First, some participants failed to interpret the movement or spent too long doing so, possibly because the current movements are relatively stiff and insufficiently interpretable, thus demanding more attentional resources. Second, even for those who understood the cues, the movement modality itself is attention
-
grabbing, as also suggested by previous studies
(
65
;
82
)
.
To mitigate these distractions, several participants (E6P4, E3P3) suggested improving the transparency and interpretability of facilitation movements, for example, by providing concise pre
-
task user guidance to reduce learning costs.
To reduce visual distractions from movements, motion designs with the slow-in-slow-out principle
(
94
)
and controlled saliency could be helpful.

7.2.2.
Misalignment Between Intended, Perceived, and Accepted Facilitation

Intended v.s. Perceived

As concluded in Sec.
6.4.2
, participants may map facilitation movements to meanings that are different from the design intent, typically treating them as cues for task
-
related behaviors such as “sharing hidden information”.
Such misalignments are not necessarily “wrong”, instead, they inspired several directions for future facilitation movement design.
First, future designs could
move beyond unified movements to incorporate more owner
-
oriented cues
. The current movements are largely presentational, as phones behave in the same way toward their owners and toward other group members. When a phone moves, it interacts relatively little with its owner, which may shift the owner’s attention to interpret “what the phone is doing” rather than “what the phone wants me to do.” This may help explain why some participants interpreted “talk more” as “share the hidden information”: seeing one’s own phone move out and rotate can be read as the phone enacting the behavior of revealing something to the group, rather than as a prompt for the owner’s general verbal participation in discussion. Increasing owner
-
phone interaction before formal facilitation could strengthen their cohesion, encouraging owners to interpret movements in terms of what they themselves should do.
Second, these task
-
related interpretations suggest that
facilitation movements could carry more task
-
relevant meaning
. For example, when a member’s information is especially important, when they become inactive, their phone could move out for longer or rotate several times, to more clearly signal the importance of their contribution.

Perceived v.s. Accepted

Even when participants perceived a facilitation cue properly, they might still refuse to follow it (Sec.
6.4.2
). Similar to participants’ compliance responses to facilitation (Sec .
7.1.1
), such refusal can occur when participants are fatigued already, or what people want to do is misaligned with what the facilitation suggests.
This refusal may also stem from the
AnimaStand
failing to match participants’ expectations about interpersonal relationships.
In some cases, participants’ personalities simply do not allow them to increase their amount of interaction or deepen their relationships with others.
While most participants were neutral or positive, two resisted—E3P4 preferring to “
connect only with select members
” and E2P3 using phones as “
a shelter from overwhelming collaboration.
” Both initially engaged with
AnimaStand
’s social cues but later became resistant (e.g., ignoring prompts, responding while yawning), suggesting that in
-
context perceptions can shift, but participants’ “built-in” perception norms still constrain how close or intense the facilitated relationship is accepted to become.

7.3.
Directions for Future Development of Animated Facilitation for Group Collaboration: Adaptive and Adaptable

7.3.1.
Adaptive Animated Facilitation from a Combined Standpoint of Both a Participant and an Observer

Our current facilitation is rule
-
based and also relies solely on features observed from a third
-
person perspective. As discussed previously (Sec.
7.1
and
7.2
), to foster participants’ acceptance of the facilitation and to optimize its effects, we argue that a more efficient animated device should apply different facilitations adapted to each group member. Specifically,
adapt decisions (e.g., movement choice) to individual mental states, and adjust style (e.g., frequency, amplitude of the movements) to group maturity and the specific member’s role
.
Furthermore, grounding these adaptive movements directly in the users’ personal and group states would allow the smartphone’s ontological attribute as a “self-extension” to be thoroughly realized in the interaction loop. This shift will further differentiate the animated devices’ role from an invisible, overarching hand regulating the group.
Based on our empirical evidence, we propose to use adaptive mental model construction and macro
-
level adaptive strategy to realize adaptation according to individual states and group progress, respectively. In the following, we outline a potential technical roadmap for realizing such adaptive animated facilitation.

Adaptation to Individual States: Mental Model Construction.

Drawing on
79
’s typology, which conceptualizes groups’ shared mental models in team and task dimensions, we identify two critical mental model elements from our observations:

First,
perceived relationship norms within the group.

Relationship norms include both general perceptions of how close they should be with groupmates shaped by prior experiences, and in
-
context perceptions that evolve during collaboration.
This element appeared in our study as varying comfort levels with connection
-
building.
As suggested in Sec.
7.2.2
, different people may accept different levels of closeness with others. Thus, adaptive facilitation should consider the perception of relationship norms to encourage cooperation within acceptable bounds for better and long-lasting effects.
Second,
perceived task priorities and situational awareness.

This refers to how individuals define their immediate focus, role responsibilities, and readiness to act.
As discussed earlier in Sec.
7.1.1
, these task
-
related perception elements should be integrated into the adaptive framework so that facilitation better matches members’ perceived readiness and the actions they consider feasible.

To build an adaptive mental model that embodies these elements, animated facilitation can start from tuning initial levels individually based on profiling user portraits.
Then, the animated phone may use richer context sensing, which combines screen activity, cameras, and environmental sensors like attached
-
to
-
table sensors, to infer behaviors such as writing, zoning out, or task
-
related phone use
(
73
)
.
These signals, analyzed in temporal windows, can then infer dynamic mental states
(
27
;
49
)
, enabling the animated device to adapt movement choices and styles to each participant.

Adaptation to Group Maturity and Role Criticality: Macro-level Facilitation Strategy.

As suggested in Sec.
7.1.2
, we proposed four styles of facilitation under different role criticality-group maturity conditions. As the task progresses, facilitation with different styles should be sent to different members in order to help the group operate in an optimal way.
To realize such style adaptation, we need to identify which role is currently most critical to the ongoing task, and which level of maturity the group is obtaining.
In practice, analysis of task progress and discussion content via LLM
(
123
)
could match the current challenge with the most suitable member to take the temporary lead, based on past behavior and member profiles.
Moreover, combining such content analysis with conversation dynamics (e.g., balanced participation, interdependence as in Sec.
4.3.2
) and assessments of the alignment among members’ mental models
(
79
;
114
)
can help gauge the group’s maturity level.
Given estimates of each role’s criticality and the group’s maturity, we can then map the current appropriate facilitation style for each member. Concrete design can implement these styles into visible movements by parameterizing movement features such as frequency, intensity, and timing thresholds.
For instance, suppose content analysis identifies member A as best suited to lead. In a newly formed group, A’s device might move to the center and rotate after one minute of A’s silence, while others’ devices only do so after two minutes. In a more mature group, these thresholds could be relaxed–A’s device moves after two minutes and others’ after three–signaling greater trust in the group’s self
-
regulation while still prioritizing the member most suited to take the lead.

7.3.2.
Adaptable and Personalizable Animated Facilitation

Personalization is another key direction proposed by participants.
While phones are personal devices, the current design applies the same movements to all participants, not yet fully reflecting this connection.
Thus, allowing users to customize their phone’s movements could make the phone’s behavior more similar to its owner’s style and strengthen users’ sense of alignment with the phone, which in turn may increase their willingness to accept facilitation (see participants’ quotes in Sec.
6.4.1
).
However, personalized movements should be acceptable not only to the phone owner but also to other group members to effectively support collaboration, so the degree of personalization needs to be bounded. For each problematic situation, the underlying facilitation logic and basic movement pattern should remain consistent. For instance, when a group member is left out, that member’s phone should always move forward to attract attention, regardless of who it is, while the specific way of moving forward (such as trajectory or style) can be customized.
For example, for the basic action of moving forward, one user might favor a swaying gait, while another might choose a slower, steadier pace. When phones gather together, they could express individual character in their movements while remaining sufficiently synchronized to preserve coordination.

7.4.
Directions for Repurposing Objects

Our design of
AnimaStand

repurposed
phones as group facilitators by enabling them to move actively and expressively.
This concept of
Repurposing Everyday Objects
has been widely explored and well-established in HCI
(
89
;
99
)
.
The purpose of an object is shaped by its
affordances
in a given
context
. Its primary purpose is determined by how it enacts its canonical affordances in a canonical context. Previous studies have approached the notion of
repurposing
along three main pathways (Fig.
18
).

Figure 18
.
Pathways to repurpose objects.

First, canonical affordances are enacted in a new context. For example, reusing an old smartphone camera module as a home
-
surveillance camera
(
77
)
.
Second, possible affordances which extended from an object’s physical properties are enacted in its canonical context, such as in affordance
-
based planning: when a chair is used as a surface for placing objects, while a friend is instead invited to sit on a cardboard box
(
83
)
.
Third, possible affordances are enacted in a new context. For instance, using everyday physical objects as controllers in VR environments
(
23
;
75
)
.
Across these three pathways, either the canonical context or the canonical affordance tends to be substantially replaced or overridden.

Our study, in contrast, adopted a different pathway to realize
repurposing
(Fig.
18
, left): we keep the object in its canonical context and augment its canonical affordance in a way that does not interfere with or override it.
Specifically, we keep the smartphone as a mediator in group communication and augment this role, extending it from supporting digital information exchange to facilitating and enriching co
-
located interpersonal interaction.
We argue that this pathway is increasingly significant. As expectations for virtual agents entering physical reality grow with advances in AI, the challenges of embodied intelligence become paramount
(
43
;
90
)
. A key challenge is integrating new technologies into everyday environments with minimal intrusion and cognitive load
(
86
)
.
By augmenting the must-have objects’ role in certain contexts with new functionalities, this repurposing pathway effectively responds to this challenge. Instead of introducing new artifacts, it embodies intelligence in objects that people already use and emotionally relate to. Such experience-grounded embodiment can make agentic behaviors more emotionally legible and acceptable, and reduce the cognitive friction associated with novel robotic forms. Moreover, repurposing objects into additional roles (“
upcycling
”) may strengthen users’ attachment and encourage longer-term use, which in turn supports sustainability
(
10
)
.
This direction also presents key challenges, including preventing conflicts between the augmented functionality and the canonical affordance and making it feel natural rather than confusing, which needs further investigation.

7.5.
Limitation and Future Work

This work has several limitations that inform directions for further exploration.
First, the current facilitation strategy and movement designs are rather rule-based and naive.
We plan to implement the adaptive animated facilitation design as proposed in Sec.
7.3
and evaluate its impact.
In addition, the current study was conducted with short
-
term interactions (about an hour) among small, ad
-
hoc groups of strangers; future work will examine longer
-
term deployments to observe how animated facilitation influences sustained group development.
Moreover, our investigation focused on dyadic facilitation dynamics within a four
-
person discussion group. Whether the findings generalize to larger or more complex configurations remains an open question, and we plan to explore diverse group sizes, task types, and settings to assess the scalability and robustness of this approach.
Finally, this work is an exploratory study, and there is a gap between our prototype’s demonstrated facilitation potential and its acceptance as a real
-
world product. We may develop more advanced stand-alone prototypes and focus more on user experience.

8.
Conclusion

This work explored how animated smartphones can facilitate small
-
group in
-
person collaboration. A design workshop identified facilitation needs across Tuckman’s group stages, informing proxemics
-
and metaphor
-
driven phone movements. We implemented these on
AnimaStand
, a semi
-
automated, movement
-
enabled phone stand, and evaluated them in a between
-
subjects Wizard
-
of
-
Oz study.
Results showed that animated facilitation improved engagement and balanced interactional dynamics by re
-
engaging inactive members. Regarding task operation, it streamlined task procedures, equalized contributions, and had the potential to improve task completion. Perceived group relationships were also elevated.
Subjective ratings and interviews further indicated that
AnimaStand
is generally found helpful, despite occasional distraction. Participants made sense of it through prediction
-
and
-
adaptation with a transferring strategy and yielded three interpretive patterns, highlighting that
AnimaStand
’s effects emerged from the interplay between its publicly visible movements and participants’ interpretations.
This work integrates group development, proxemics, and metaphor theories to design and study animated phone behaviors, showing how movement
-
enabled devices can enhance group dynamics and relationships, informing future designs for adaptive and adaptable animated personal devices, and pathways for repurposing everyday devices.
