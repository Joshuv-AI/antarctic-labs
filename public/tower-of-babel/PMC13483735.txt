Genome-wide association study of never-smoking non-drinking young adults developing oral squamous cell carcinoma
Authors: Guillaume B. Cardin, Alicia Pellerin-Viger, Monique Bernard, Fiona Kessai, Tareck Ayad, Eric Bissada, Louis Guertin, Paul Tabet, Olguta-Ecaterina Gologan, Edith Filion, Phuc Felix Nguyen-Tan, Antoine Desilets, Alexandre Pellan Cheng, Francis Rodier, Houda Bahig, Apostolos Christopoulos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGermline risk variants for oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) in never-smoking non-drinking (NSND) young adults (YA) are poorly characterized and genome-wide association studies focusing on NSND YA OSCC are lacking. This study aims to evaluate if rare germline variants are associated with NSND YA OSCC.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective, single-center Canadian cohort study of patients who underwent primary surgery for OSCC between 2010 and 2024. Genome-wide association analyses were performed for three comparisons: 1- NSND YA (n = 10) vs. other OSCC (n = 150), 2- all-ages NSND (n = 29) vs. ever-smoker and/or drinker (n = 131), and 3- any risk factor YA OSCC (n = 22) vs. age ≥ 45 (n = 138). A polygenic risk score for age at OSCC diagnosis was derived from clumping + thresholding.ResultsWe included 160 patients of European ancestry with a diagnosis of OSCC: 22 YA and 29 NSND patients; 10 patients were both YA and NSND. YA cases predominantly involved the oral tongue. One rare variant, rs191595756, showed a suggestive association with NSND YA OSCC (p = 3.3e-06), but did not reach genome-wide significance. This variant is located in an intron of LINC00944 and in an exon of LINC02824. No rare variant signals were supported in the all-ages NSND and any risk factor YA OSCC. None of the identified variants is reported in ClinVar. An exploratory polygenic risk score composed of 13 index variants was constructed. It is independent of NSND or ever-smoker and/or drinker status. It is also independent of oral tongue or other oral cavity primary cancer site.ConclusionsThis is the first study investigating genome-wide associations in NSND YA OSCC. No single germline variant of genome-wide significance associated with NSND YA OSCC occurrence was found, suggesting a polygenic architecture. Larger, multicentric studies are needed to validate a polygenic risk score specifically predictive of NSND YA OSCC risk.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-026-16342-2.

Background

An increase in the incidence of oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) in young adults (YA), with a predominance of oral tongue cancers, has been reported since the 1980s and has since been confirmed in multiple reports worldwide over recent decades [1–9]. The age threshold defining YA OSCC varies across studies and is most commonly set at 40 or 45 years [10]. While tobacco and alcohol use are frequent in older patients with OSCC, their prevalence and contribution are lower in YA OSCC [10]. This has led to the recognition of a YA never-smoking non-drinking (NSND) OSCC subtype, although underlying causal mechanisms remain elusive.

As young patients are involved, the role of viruses has been extensively studied. Oncogenic viruses are common etiologic factors in several head and neck cancer subsites, but previous sequencing-based efforts to identify viral drivers in YA OSCC tumors have not been unsuccessful [11]. Human papillomavirus (HPV) can sometimes be detected in YA OSCC, but appears unlikely to have an important etiologic role [12–14]. Studies on oral tongue and OSCC looking at HPV directly, and not p16(INK4a) as a surrogate, indicate that its incidence is rare in this disease. A cohort of 32 patients, with 6 cases under 30 years old, showed a frequency of 0/32 of high-risk HPV [15]. Another study looked at 78 oral tongue cancer patients detected HPV in only one case, but in none of the 11 patients under 40 years old [16]. In a cohort of 95 NSND OSCC patients, Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) and Merkel cell polyomavirus (MCV) were also evaluated with EBV only detected in a single case, and with no cases of MCV [17].

A family history of cancer in young relatives has been associated with both YA OSCC and head and neck cancer more generally [10, 18], suggesting that genomic susceptibility could play a role in YA OSCC. From a genetic standpoint, tumor exome analyses of non-smoking OSCC and YA non-smoking OSCC have been published [19, 20], but very little is known about potential germline at-risk variants in those patients. Previous genome-wide association studies have identified OSCC susceptibility loci located on multiple chromosomes [21–24]. However, these analyses largely reflect tobacco- and alcohol-related carcinogenesis and reports testing for age-associated at-risk variants are missing. To date, no polygenic risk score (PRS) predicting age at OSCC diagnosis has been reported.

Evaluating germline at-risk variants associated with YA OSCC – particularly in NSND individuals – remains an important gap in current knowledge. The main objective of this exploratory study is to evaluate, using whole genome sequencing (WGS), whether rare germline variants are associated with NSND YA OSCC. A secondary objective of this study was to construct a PRS predicting age at OSCC diagnosis, to evaluate whether genetic susceptibility patterns differ between NSND and to YA OSCC and ever-smoker and/or drinker (ESD) OSCC. While tumor genomics have been partially explored, germline susceptibility in NSND YA OSCC remains almost completely uncharacterized, justifying an exploratory genome-wide association study on a small study cohort.

Methods

Study design and participants

A total of 160 OSCC patients of European ancestry were included in the first four years of the Marathon of Hope Cancer Centres Network (MOHCCN), a pan-cancer initiative aiming to promote access to WGS and build a clinico-genomic database encompassing 15,000 Canadians with a diagnosis of cancer [25]. The OSCC cohort included patients 18 years or older who underwent surgery at the Centre Hospitalier de l’Université de Montréal (CHUM) between 2010 and 2024, and provided written consent to participate in the institutional head and neck cancer biobank at the CHUM Research Center (CRCHUM). This study and the biobank were approved by CRCHUM’s institutional research ethics board (#21.137 and #09.254).

Demographics and clinical parameters

Demographics and clinical parameters were obtained from clinical records, including sex, age at diagnosis, tobacco use, alcohol use, primary tumor site (oral tongue, floor of mouth, mandibular alveolus, retromolar trigone, buccal mucosa, mucosal lip, hard palate, maxillary alveolus), self-identified genetic ancestry group, and pathologic stage. For tobacco use, patients were categorized as smokers if they were current or former smokers at the time of surgery, with the exception of participants with less than 1 pack-year history. For alcohol use, patients with International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10) Z72.1 (alcohol use) or F10.2 (alcohol dependence) were classified as “drinkers”. Patients under 45 years of age at OSCC diagnosis were classified as YA. Pathologic stage was recorded according to the 8th edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC). Patients from non-European ancestry were excluded to prevent introduction of false positives due to linkage disequilibrium. The majority of included patients were French Canadians.

Germline DNA extraction and sequencing

Peripheral blood samples were obtained at the time of surgery, and peripheral blood mononuclear cells were separated using Ficoll and stored for future analyses. Germline DNA was extracted from peripheral blood mononuclear cells using the PureLink Genomic DNA Mini Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). For one participant, germline DNA was extracted from a negative control lymph node using the AllPrep DNA/RNA FFPE Kit (Qiagen). Nucleic acid quality was assessed using the 4150 TapeStation System (Agilent) prior to library preparation. WGS was performed on germline DNA, following Genome Analysis Toolkit’s (GATK) Best Practices and procedures, with a target of 30X deduplicated coverage per sample, using an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 instrument.

Genotyping

Quality-trimmed and adapter-clipped reads were aligned to the GCA_000001405.15 GRCh38_no_alt_analysis_set.fna human genome assembly using BWA-MEM [26]. Germline variant calls were generated combining Strelka2, VarScan 2, and VarDict calls in one Variant Call Format (VCF) file per participant [27–29]. Single sample VCF files were merged, indexed and filtered to exclude variants with read depth below 25 or genotype quality below 20 using bcftools [30]. The resulting VCF file was converted to a BED/BIM/FAM fileset, including only variants from chromosome 1 to 22 and filtering out multiallelic variants using PLINK version 2.0 [31]. Variants on the X and Y chromosomes were removed as a best practice to prevent confounding by sex. Variants with complete cohort minor allele frequency (MAF) < 0.009 (fewer than 3 occurrences in the full cohort) were also excluded, as they have low statistical power and high false positive rate.

Genome-wide association analysis of NSND YA, all-ages NSND and any risk factor YA OSCC

Association analyses were conducted separately for the NSND YA group, the all-ages NSND group and the YA any risk factor group, using other participants of the cohort as controls and applying Fisher’s exact test in PLINK version 1.9 [32]. Index variants were identified using PLINK’s --clump command with --clump-r2 0.1 and --clump-kb 250. Participant genotypes for variants with p < 1.0e-05 were extracted using PLINK and confirmed, as a quality control step, on the BAM files using IGV version 2.16.2 [33]. Manhattan plots were constructed using the qqman package in R. The All of Us data browser was used on October 21, 2025, to identify the European and the highest reported MAF from every genetic ancestry group of the All of Us cohort [34]. Rare variants were defined as having a highest reported MAF, from every genetic ancestry group of the All of Us cohort, under 0.01. The sample size (n = 10) is very small for the genome-wide association analysis of the NSND YA OSCC group, and the analysis is exploratory for this rare disease group, with a suggestive significance threshold of p < 1.0e-05.

Genome-wide association analysis and PRS for age at OSCC diagnosis

Association analyses with numerical age at diagnosis (in years, rounded to two decimals) was done on the full cohort using Wald test in PLINK version 1.9. Index variants identification, and BAM validation were realized, as described for association analysis. PRS was calculated by clumping + thresholding (C + T) using PLINK’s --score command with the β-value of the regression. PLINK’s --q-score-range command testing addition of variants with p < 1.0e-05, one by one, starting with the lowest p-value, allowed identification of the threshold maximizing R2 [35]. PRS was transformed as “log -(PRS-1)” to test whether the slopes and intercepts of the regression between OSCC subgroups were significantly different, using Prism version 10.2.3. For the overall survival analysis, a multivariate Cox proportional hazards analysis was used to evaluate hazard ratios using JMP. Comparison of PRS distributions for TNM status was evaluated using Welch’s ANOVA test. Since a validation cohort was unavailable, PRS validation was done using permutation analysis, randomly permuting phenotypes 10,000 times, with the empirical p-value calculated using R.

Results

Study cohort

A total of 160 patients with a diagnosis of OSCC were included at CHUM and included in the MOHCCN Canadian pan-cancer cohort, with clinical characteristics detailed in Table S1. All (n = 160; 100%) were of self-identified European ancestry and 22 (14%) were YA (Table 1). Among those YA patients, 19 (86%) were male, 10 (45%) were NSND, and 18 (82%) had an oral tongue primary cancer. For the 138 patients aged ≥ 45 years, 92 (67%) were male, 19 (14%) were NSND, and 61 (44%) had an oral tongue primary cancer. Cancer stage distribution was slightly more aggressive in the ≥ 45 years old group.

Genome-wide association analysis of YA and/or NSND OSCC

The first objective of this study was to evaluate whether rare germline variants are associated with the occurrence of NSND YA OSCC. A genome-wide association analysis comparing NSND YA OSCC patients (n = 10) to other OSCC cases (n = 150), all-ages NSND OSCC patients (n = 29) to ESD OSCC cases (n = 131) and YA OSCC patients (n = 22) to other OSCC cases (n = 138) was performed. These analyses revealed no genome-wide significant hits, and only one rare variant with suggestive significance in the NSND YA analysis.

A total of 655 730 (NSND YA), 646 052 (NSND) and 648,351 (YA) index variants were identified after clumping. Manhattan plots for each differential analysis are shown in Fig. 1, with a red line showing the genome-wide significance threshold (5 × 10− 8) and a blue line showing the suggestive significance threshold (1 × 10− 5). This suggestive significance threshold was used to identify potential rare germline at-risk variants which could be confirmed in larger validation cohorts. Genomic inflation was also evaluated using Q-Q plots for each analysis (Figure S1).

The next step was to assess whether the identified variants were rare variants. To do so, we validated the highest reported MAF from every genetic ancestry group of the All of Us cohort, using a threshold of MAF < 0.01 (Table 2). Identified variants with p < 1.0e-05 for NSND YA OSCC (Table S2) are rs149462127 (chr5:166940729-C-T, intergenic region, nearest gene: ENSG00000298538), rs16946701 (chr15:63255812-G-A, RAB8B intron), rs191595756 (chr12:126719941-A-G, LINC02824 exon and LINC00944 intron), rs113104790 (chr16:74142466-C-T, PSMD7-DT intron), and rs5879963 (chr6:129703003-CA-C, ARHGAP18 intron). Only rs191595756 can be considered a rare variant with the defined threshold. Its genotype distribution in the NSND YA OSCC group is 6 homozygous reference alleles patients, 3 heterozygous patients and 1 homozygous alternate allele patients (MAF = 0.25), while for other OSCC cases it is 149 homozygous reference alleles patients, 1 heterozygous patients and no homozygous alternate allele patients (MAF = 0.0033). The control group distribution is consistent with the European MAF of 0.0035 reported in the All of Us cohort. For rs16946701 and rs113104790, the MAF in the other OSCC group was much lower than the reported European MAF in the All of Us cohort, by factors of 4.5 and 4.2 respectively, suggesting that those variants are false positives. Genomic deflation observed in the Q-Q plot is expected with the small sample size (Figure S1A). None of the identified variants is reported in ClinVar.

PRS for age at OSCC diagnosis

To eliminate the bias of the 45-years-old threshold of the YA group, we chose to perform a genome-wide association analysis with numerical age at diagnosis. We then compared the correlation between PRS and age at diagnosis for the NSND and the ESD subgroups, finding no significant differences. The results of this analysis are exploratory, as the cohort is too small to draw meaningful conclusions. Larger, multicentric studies are needed to validate a polygenic risk score specifically predictive of NSND YA OSCC risk.

A total of 630 739 index variants were identified by clumping. The PRS for age at OSCC diagnosis was calculated. The calculation of R2 following sequential addition of variants with p < 1.0e-05 in the model, starting with the lowest p-value, allowed the identification of the threshold that maximized R2. Using permutation analysis, no permuted test statistics exceeded the observed PRS association using 10,000 permutations (empirical p-value = 9.999e-05). The resulting PRS included the following 13 index variants (Table 3 and Table S5): rs17142701 (chr6:6936428-A-G, intergenic region, nearest gene: RN7SL554P), rs16946701 (chr15:63255812-G-A, RAB8B intron), rs199603591 (chr20:39687424-TTC-T, intergenic region, nearest gene: ENSG00000306887), rs80326268 (chr16:54488136-C-T, intergenic region, nearest gene: ENSG00000286845), rs34440545 (chr7:49593616-CTAT-C, intergenic region, nearest gene: ENSG00000286995), rs79949466 (chr5:37429113-A-G, WDR70, intron), rs116937215 (chr9:13592714-T-A, ENSG00000226197 intron), rs74590769 (chr6:151377741-A-G, ZBTB2 intron), rs75127284 (chr18:34506592-A-C, DTNA intron), rs116985485 (chr7:101536460-G-A, COL26A1 intron), rs568878631 (chr1:67730331-C-T, GNG12 intron), rs12625513 (chr20:62844309-G-A, TCFL5 intron), rs34497370 (chr10:61377055-G-A, intergenic region, nearest gene: ENSG00000305028). From those, rs568878631 is a rare variant. Its genotype distribution in the OSCC cohort is 151 homozygous reference alleles patients, 9 heterozygous patients and no homozygous alternate allele patients (MAF = 0.028). None of the 13 index variants is reported in ClinVar.

Discussion

In this single cohort study, we evaluated associations of germline variants with NSND YA OSCC in patients of European ancestry. We excluded cases of non-European ancestry, as linkage disequilibrium could have introduced false positives in our small cohort. Our results suggest that no single germline variant explains NSND YA OSCC occurrence and no variant with genome-wide significance (p < 5.0e-08) was found.

Our main objective in this study was to identify potential rare variants associated with NSND YA OSCC which would have the potential to be causal factors. To define a variant as rare, we consider variants with a MAF < 0.01 in every genetic ancestry group reported in the All of Us cohort. As our study had only 10 NSND YA OSCC patients, we used a suggestive significance threshold of p < 1.0e-05, as we were aware that we might lack the necessary statistical power to identify potential rare variants with a more stringent genome-wide significance threshold. Indeed, to attain genome-wide significance, a MAF of 0.5 (10/20 alleles) would be needed in the 10 patients NSND YA OSCC test group, for a variant with a MAF of 0.01 (3/300 alleles) in the control group. For a variant with no occurrence (0/300 alleles) in the control group, a MAF of 0.4 (8/20 alleles) would be necessary to attain genome-wide significance. This is hardly realistic and we chose to use a suggestive significance threshold for this exploratory study. We also tested the larger NSND OSCC and YA OSCC groups in which smaller effect sizes could be detected.

Only one of reported variants for NSND YA OSCC patients, rs191595756, has a reported MAF < 0.01 in every ancestry group. Interestingly, this variant is located in an intron of LINC00944 (Long Intergenic Non-Protein Coding RNA 944) and an exon of LINC02824 (Long Intergenic Non-Protein Coding RNA 2824). Multiple long non-coding RNAs have been shown to have oncogenic or tumor suppressive effects in OSCC, regulating multiple pathways such as cell cycle, angiogenesis and apoptosis [36]. LINC00944 has been studied in multiple cancers [37–39]. The LINC00944 gene has been shown to actually encode a small 102-amino acid peptide, which represses transcriptional activity of MYC in vitro in melanoma cells [40]. The importance of MYC in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma is well established, with gene amplification occurring in approximately 12% of cases [41]. LINC00944 has also been shown to be upregulated in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma in comparison to normal adjacent tissues [42]. Overexpression of LINC00944 in the OSCC cell lines SCC-25 and ORL-48T increased cell migration and cell invasion in Transwell assays [42]. LINC00944 was also associated to pro-inflammatory signaling and is upregulated in EBV oral cancer cell lines [42]. The importance of EBV in head and neck cancer is primarily associated with a large increase in the risk of developing nasopharyngeal carcinoma, with EBV-associated OSCC being of rare occurrence [43, 44]. The association between MYC and EBV is well established. MYC regulates the switch between EBV latent and lytic stages [45], and also multiple pathways associated with immune evasion [46]. LINC02824 is a mostly unknown long non-coding RNA, being reported only to be positively associated with the number of regulatory T cells in systemic lupus erythematosus [47].

We also evaluated variants associated with all-ages NSND OSCC and with YA OSCC. No rare variant was found to be associated in both cases, even at the suggestive significance threshold, despite having more cases than in the NSND YA group (respectively 29 and 22, versus only 10 for NSND YA). Interestingly, while three of the five variants reported for the NSND YA group had a highest reported MAF < 0.02, each of the eleven reported variants for the NSND and the YA group were common variants with a highest reported MAF over 0.05.

Our genome-wide association analysis results suggest that no single germline variant explains NSND YA OSCC occurrence and that a polygenic risk score could be more appropriate to evaluate NSND YA OSCC risk. A larger multicentric study could potentially identify a polygenic risk score predictive of NSND YA OSCC risk, as our cohort including only 10 NSND YA OSCC cases is not large enough for an analysis with NSND YA analyzed as a binary trait. However, calculating a PRS score for age at OSCC diagnosis as a numerical variable as an exploratory analysis appeared reasonable. It also allowed to evaluate whether genetic susceptibility to YA OSCC is similar in NSND and ESD OSCC in our cohort.

Our PRS score for age at OSCC diagnosis was shown to be independent of NSND or ESD status. This result is consistent with previous results showing that association analyses considering OSCC as a whole tend to identify variants associated with tobacco and alcohol consumption [22]. Therefore, the NSND YA OSCC group should be evaluated independently, and a larger cohort will be needed to perform this analysis, as our study sample size was too small to calculate a PRS specifically for the NSND YA OSCC group. YA OSCC predominantly involves oral tongue cancers [9], but association of PRS score with age at diagnosis was comparable in oral tongue and other oral cavity cases.

This study has multiple limitations. It was a retrospective, monocentric study with data extracted from clinical records and not from study questionnaires. The cohort includes exclusively patients of European ancestry, with the majority being French Canadians, which limits generalization ad could introduce subtle population stratification in the study. Additionally, the total number of participants was limited for such a study, and no validation cohort was available for the PRS score. A sample size of 160 is small for PRS and threshold optimization increases the risk of overfitting. HPV infection status was not recorded, as it had not been tested for most patients. Previous studies report a low prevalence of HPV in OSCC, particularly in YA cases, but the lack of HPV status data could affect the interpretation of this study. Multi-allelic variants and variants located on the X and Y chromosomes were not evaluated in this study.

Conclusions

The main objective of this study was to evaluate whether rare germline variants are associated with NSND YA OSCC cases. One rare variant, rs191595756, was suggestively associated with NSND YA OSCC, but did not reach genome-wide significance. No variant with p < 5.0e-08 was found. Our findings suggest that no single germline variant explains NSND YA OSCC occurrence. A larger multicentric study is needed to identify a polygenic risk score predictive of NSND YA OSCC risk.
