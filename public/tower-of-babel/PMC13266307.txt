LAT1-mediated amino acid metabolism reprogramming: a novel metabolic vulnerability in recurrent pituitary neuroendocrine tumors
Authors: Zhi-Jie Pei, Gao-Wei Li, Jiang-Hao Yu, Hong-Rui Yang, Yi Fang, Liang-Xue Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTumor recurrence remains a major challenge in the management of pituitary neuroendocrine tumors (PitNETs). This study aimed to investigate recurrence-associated metabolic alterations in PitNETs using an integrated metabolomic approach.MethodsUntargeted gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS)-based metabolomic profiling was performed on tumor tissues from 44 patients with PitNETs as a discovery cohort. Differential metabolites were identified using non-parametric statistical analysis. An independent validation cohort of 40 patients (20 primary and 20 recurrent PitNETs) was used for protein-level validation by multiplex immunofluorescence staining of key amino acid transporters, including L-type amino acid transporter 1 (LAT1) and alanine–serine–cysteine transporter 2 (ASCT2).ResultsMetabolomic analysis revealed distinct metabolic alterations between primary and recurrent PitNETs, characterized by selective enrichment of large neutral amino acids (LNAAs) in recurrent tumors. Pathway enrichment analysis indicated that the altered metabolites were predominantly involved in amino acid-related metabolic pathways. In the validation cohort, multiplex immunofluorescence demonstrated significantly increased LAT1 expression in recurrent PitNETs compared with primary tumors, whereas ASCT2 expression did not differ significantly between groups.ConclusionThese findings indicate that recurrence of PitNETs is associated with selective remodeling of amino acid metabolism, particularly involving LNAAs, accompanied by increased expression of the corresponding transporter LAT1. Together, our results suggest that altered amino acid transport represents an important component of metabolic adaptation in recurrent PitNETs and warrants further investigation.

Introduction

Pituitary neuroendocrine tumors (PitNETs) rank among the most common intracranial tumors, with a population prevalence ranging from 14 to 22% (1). Originating from the anterior pituitary gland, these tumors can lead to a spectrum of clinical manifestations due to excessive hormone secretion and local invasiveness into surrounding structures (2, 3). The latest 2022 World Health Organization classification system provides the core framework for PitNET subtyping, relying on the immunohistochemical detection of pituitary hormones and lineage-specific transcription factors, such as PIT1, TPIT, and SF1 (4).

Despite advances in endoscopic surgical techniques, pharmacological therapy, and radiotherapy, tumor recurrence remains a major challenge in the clinical management of PitNETs, with long-term recurrence rates reported to range from 10 to 30% (5, 6). Recurrent tumors are often characterized by dense adhesions to adjacent structures, leading to reduced rates of gross total resection during repeat surgery. As a result, patients face increased risks of postoperative complications, including visual impairment and permanent hypopituitarism, and may require multiple surgical interventions and lifelong hormone replacement therapy (7). These clinical challenges underscore the need for improved understanding of recurrence-associated biological processes.

Currently, clinicopathological parameters used to assess recurrence risk remain limited. Only a small number of factors, such as elevated Ki-67 index, male sex in lactotroph adenomas, silent corticotroph adenomas, and Crooke’s cell adenomas, have been associated with an increased likelihood of recurrence (4). However, these markers provide limited insight into the underlying molecular mechanisms of tumor recurrence. Although recurrence-associated metabolic alterations have been suggested by recent paired multiomics analyses of primary and recurrent PitNETs, systematic investigation at the metabolome level remains limited (8).

This study employed gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS) to investigate metabolic alterations in primary and recurrent PitNET tissues. Our findings indicate that recurrence is associated with selective remodeling of amino acid metabolism, particularly involving large neutral amino acids (LNAAs). Such metabolic changes are consistent with tumor-associated reprogramming that supports anabolic demands through enhanced nutrient availability and uptake. Given the central role of the L-type amino acid transporter 1 (LAT1/SLC7A5) in LNAA transport, altered amino acid handling may represent an important feature of metabolic adaptation in recurrent PitNETs. These observations provide a metabolic framework for understanding tumor recurrence and highlight amino acid transport as a potential metabolic vulnerability warranting further investigation.

Materials and methods

Study design and patient information

This study was conducted at Department of Neurosurgery, West China Hospital, Sichuan University. In the first phase, patients admitted between May 2025 and September 2025 were enrolled as the metabolomics discovery cohort. This study was based on a consecutive case series. During the study period, a total of 61 consecutive potentially eligible surgical cases were screened. Inclusion criteria were as follows: i) age ≥ 18 years with complete preoperative endocrine evaluation and pituitary magnetic resonance imaging and ii) surgical treatment with postoperative pathological confirmation of pituitary neuroendocrine tumors (PitNETs). The primary group consisted of newly diagnosed patients who had not received radiotherapy or medical therapy before surgery. The recurrent group consisted of patients undergoing repeat surgery for recurrent/progressive PitNETs after prior surgical treatment; previous medical therapy and/or radiotherapy was allowed in this group. Exclusion criteria included the following: i) for patients in the primary group, any radiotherapy or medical therapy before surgery; ii) concomitant pituitary disorders, such as hypophysitis, pituitary hyperplasia, or Rathke’s cleft cyst; iii) the presence of other intracranial lesions; and iv) severe hematologic or systemic diseases. In addition, acute pituitary apoplexy cases with extensive ischemic change were excluded because such specimens were considered unsuitable for representative metabolomic evaluation of viable tumor tissue.

The reasons for exclusion were as follows: refusal of surgery (n = 3), newly diagnosed patients who had already received medical therapy (n = 4), age < 18 years (n = 3), acute pituitary apoplexy (n = 2), intraoperative/postoperative pathological diagnosis of Rathke’s cleft cyst (n = 3), final pathological diagnosis of pituitary hyperplasia (n = 1), and loss of one sample before analytical processing because of handling error (n = 1). After these exclusions, 44 patients were included in the final analytical cohort. The clinicopathological characteristics of the 44 patients in the discovery cohort are summarized in Table 1.

A total of 44 tumor tissue samples were included for GC–MS-based metabolomic profiling to characterize metabolic alterations associated with PitNET recurrence. In the second phase, formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) specimens from 40 patients treated before May 2025 were selected as an independent validation cohort, including 20 primary and 20 recurrence/progression PitNETs, using the same group definitions and general inclusion/exclusion principles. Among the 20 recurrent/progressive PitNETs included in the validation cohort, 6 were classified as regrowth/progression of residual tumor and 14 as true recurrence.

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of West China Hospital, Sichuan University (Approval No. 2023-527). All procedures involving human participants were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Sample collection and metabolite extraction

Tumor tissue samples were collected intraoperatively and immediately rinsed with sterile saline to remove surface blood, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored until analysis.

To minimize pre-analytical bias related to tissue sampling and handling, specimens from both groups were collected according to the same standardized intraoperative protocol. All operations were performed by the same lead surgeon. After sharp opening of the sellar dura, intrasellar tumor tissue was removed in a piecemeal manner using only a metal ring curette or spoon curette. For metabolomic analysis, sampling was targeted as much as feasible to relatively central, viable, and well-perfused tumor tissue. Although perfectly identical ‘central’ sampling cannot be guaranteed in all soft PitNETs, the following principles were strictly applied throughout the study: tissue close to the sellar floor was avoided; tissue adjacent to the cavernous sinus was avoided; tissue removed at the final stage near the diaphragma sellae was avoided; tissue with ischemic necrosis, old hemorrhage, or fresh hemorrhage was not sampled; and only moderately sized, non-fragmented, grossly viable tumor tissue was selected.

Because only metal curettes were used for the metabolomic specimens, no electrocautery, bipolar coagulation, ultrasonic devices, or other energy-based instruments were applied to the sampled tissue, thereby minimizing potential thermal effects. After collection, all specimens were handled by the same assistant using sterile instruments and sterile gauze to gently remove surface contaminants, placed into cryovials, and transferred into a portable liquid-nitrogen container. The interval from intraoperative sampling to liquid-nitrogen preservation was consistently controlled within approximately 5–8 min.

Pathological review was also used to assess specimen representativeness, including hemorrhagic/inflammatory change, necrosis, cystic change, and tumor cellularity. Specimens considered grossly unrepresentative of viable tumor parenchyma were not used for metabolomic analysis. In addition, none of the patients received amino acid-containing intravenous nutritional support before or during surgery, and no patients were instructed to follow a special amino acid- or protein-enriched diet preoperatively.

Approximately 25 mg of frozen tissue was homogenized in 200 μL of water using an automated tissue homogenizer (40 Hz, 3 min).

Methanol (0.8 mL) containing L-norleucine (2.5 μg/mL) was then added as the extraction solvent so that this internal standard entered the workflow before centrifugation and metabolite extraction.

After centrifugation at 4°C and 14,000 g for 15 min, an aliquot of 120 μL supernatant was mixed with 10 μL of L-norvaline (50 μg/mL), and this second internal standard was introduced before drying and derivatization.

The supernatant was dried under a nitrogen stream, and the residue was reconstituted in methoxyamine hydrochloride in pyridine (20 mg/mL, containing n-alkane standards), vortexed briefly, incubated at 37°C for 90 min, and subsequently trimethylsilylated with BSTFA (1% TMCS) at 70°C for 60 min prior to GC–MS analysis.

Quality control (QC) samples were prepared by pooling equal aliquots of supernatants from all samples and processed using the same procedure.

GC–MS analysis

Derivatized samples were analyzed using an Agilent 7890A gas chromatograph coupled to a 5975C mass spectrometer (Agilent Technologies, USA) equipped with an OPTIMA® 5 MS Accent capillary column (30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm). High-purity helium (>99.999%) was used as the carrier gas at a constant flow rate of 1 mL/min.

The oven temperature program was set as follows: initial temperature at 80°C for 4 min, increased to 160°C at 8°C/min and held for 6 min, then raised to 310°C at 15°C/min and held for 4 min. Samples (1 μL) were injected in splitless mode with a solvent delay of 5.4 min. The injector, transfer line, and ion source temperatures were set at 250, 260, and 230°C, respectively. Electron impact ionization was performed at 70 eV, and data were acquired in full-scan mode over an m/z range of 50–600.

Metabolite identification and data processing

GC–MS raw data files were processed using the Automated Mass Spectral Deconvolution and Identification System (AMDIS) for peak deconvolution and alignment. Metabolite identification was performed by matching mass spectra and retention time information against a self-compiled reference library, the Golm Metabolome Database, and the NIST mass spectral library (version 2.0).

Raw data were subjected to peak picking, alignment, deconvolution, and further preprocessing, and the final data matrix was exported as a peak table containing sample identifiers, retention time–mass-to-charge (rt–m/z) variables, and corresponding peak areas.

Approximately 25 mg of frozen tissue was processed for each sample during extraction, which provided upstream standardization of tissue input; no additional post-acquisition tissue-weight normalization was applied.

Peak areas of all detected metabolites were normalized using a multiplicative internal-standard approach rather than TIC/total-area normalization.

Specifically, the raw peak area of each metabolite was divided by the product of the peak areas of the two internal standards, L-norleucine and L-norvaline, obtained from the same GC–MS injection (normalized intensity = area_metabolite/[area_L-norleucine × area_L-norvaline]).

L-norleucine, which was added before extraction, was used to correct for variation in extraction efficiency, whereas L-norvaline, which was added before drying and derivatization, was used to correct for variation in drying/derivatization efficiency. This single formula was applied uniformly to all detected metabolites; no arithmetic or geometric mean of the two internal standards was used, and no metabolite-specific internal standard assignment was performed. Missing values were imputed using one-half of the minimum detected value, and pooled QC samples prepared from all supernatants were used for QC-based LOESS signal drift correction. Before multivariate analysis, the normalized data were mean-centered and unit variance (UV) scaled.

Multiplex immunofluorescence staining and analysis

FFPE sections from the validation cohort were subjected to multiplex immunofluorescence staining following deparaffinization, rehydration, and antigen retrieval. Validated primary antibodies against LAT1 and ASCT2 (Huabio, China) were applied, followed by incubation with appropriate fluorescently labeled secondary antibodies. Stained slides were scanned using an Olympus VS200 whole-slide scanning system (20× objective). At least five non-overlapping fields of view were randomly selected per sample for analysis. Positive cells were quantified manually using the ‘Cell Counter’ plugin in ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, USA). LAT1 expression was calculated as the percentage of LAT1-positive cells relative to total DAPI-positive nuclei in the same field. Group comparisons were performed using appropriate statistical tests, and a P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Statistical and pathway analysis

Univariate statistical analysis was conducted using non-parametric Mann–Whitney U tests. Differential metabolites were screened using a combined criterion of P value <0.05 and |log2 fold change| > 0.58, and raw P values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg method to control the false discovery rate (FDR).

To assess the robustness of key metabolic alterations and account for potential confounding effects of pathological subtypes, a sensitivity analysis was performed by excluding corticotroph adenomas from both the primary and recurrent groups. Differences in selected metabolites were reanalyzed using the Mann–Whitney U test.

Pathway enrichment analysis was conducted based on differential metabolites using the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database. Pathway significance was evaluated using FDR-adjusted P values, and enriched pathways were ranked according to the number of matched metabolites in each pathway.

Differential expression of the LNAA transporter LAT1

We performed pathological validation on FFPE sections from a separate validation cohort of PitNET patients, independent from the cohort used for metabolomic detection. Multiplex immunohistochemistry was employed to assess the expression of LAT1, the transporter for LNAAs, and ASCT2, the transporter for small neutral amino acids. Representative immunofluorescence images are shown in Fig. 4A. LAT1 expression was significantly upregulated in the pathological tissues of the recurrent group, with a positive staining percentage in tumor cells of (78.9 ± 7.2)%, compared to only (7.7 ± 3.2)% in the primary group (P < 0.0001). In contrast, the positive staining percentage for ASCT2 was (37.9 ± 10.4)% in the recurrent group and (32.4 ± 8.1)% in the primary group, showing no significant difference between the two groups. Discussion

Among the differential metabolites identified in our metabolomic analysis, the altered metabolic profile of recurrent PitNETs was predominantly characterized by changes in amino acid-related metabolites, particularly LNAAs, together with a limited number of metabolites involved in central carbon metabolism. These findings suggest that metabolic alterations associated with recurrence are not widespread but instead converge on specific metabolic pathways. This is in line with a recent paired primary–recurrent multiomics study, which identified transcript-level alterations in metabolism-related pathways, including phenylalanine metabolism and valine/leucine/isoleucine biosynthesis (8). While that study provided pathway-level evidence, our results further support recurrence-associated metabolic reprogramming at the metabolite level and link it to increased LAT1 expression.

Tumor cells are known to undergo metabolic reprogramming, most notably the shift toward aerobic glycolysis, commonly referred to as the ‘Warburg effect’ (12). Taken together, the differential metabolites identified in recurrent PitNETs predominantly converged on amino acid–related metabolic pathways, rather than on tumor-specific oncometabolites.

Amino acid-related metabolites constituted a major component of the metabolic alterations observed in recurrent PitNETs. Notably, the most robustly altered metabolites following statistical correction were LNAAs, including leucine, isoleucine, phenylalanine, and tyrosine. LNAAs, which also include valine, tryptophan, methionine, and histidine, are characterized by bulky, hydrophobic or aromatic side chains and a neutral charge at physiological pH (13). LNAAs serve as fundamental building blocks for protein synthesis, and increased availability of these amino acids supports the heightened biosynthetic demands of rapidly proliferating tumor cells. In addition to their structural role, certain LNAAs participate in intracellular signaling pathways. For example, leucine is a well-established activator of the mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) pathway, and acetyl-CoA derived from leucine metabolism has been shown to contribute to mTORC1 activation (14). Activation of mTORC1 promotes tumor cell growth and progression by enhancing protein synthesis and anabolic metabolism (15). Collectively, these observations suggest that altered LNAA metabolism in recurrent PitNETs may provide both the metabolic substrates and signaling inputs required to sustain tumor growth. Furthermore, LNAAs are involved in multiple biosynthetic processes within tumor cells, including fatty acid and nucleotide synthesis, thereby contributing to the metabolic foundation of tumor proliferation (16).

Cellular uptake of LNAAs is primarily mediated by the L-type amino acid transporter 1 (LAT1, also known as SLC7A5) (17). LAT1 is a sodium- and pH-independent transmembrane transporter that functions as a heterodimeric complex with the glycoprotein 4F2hc (CD98, SLC3A2), facilitating the influx of LNAAs such as leucine and phenylalanine in exchange for the efflux of intracellular amino acids, including glutamine (18, 19). LAT1 is highly expressed in cells with sustained amino acid demands, including neurons, glial cells, placental cells, activated T cells, and endothelial cells of the blood–brain barrier (20).

Accumulating evidence indicates that LAT1 expression is markedly upregulated in a wide range of tumor cell lines (21) and human malignancies, including breast, prostate, lung, colorectal, head and neck cancers, and gliomas (22, 23, 24, 25), underscoring its critical role in supporting tumor growth and survival. Functional studies have demonstrated that genetic disruption or knockdown of LAT1 in cancer cells leads to reduced leucine uptake and suppressed cell proliferation, highlighting its importance in sustaining tumor anabolic metabolism (26, 27). Accordingly, LAT1 has emerged as a potential therapeutic target, and pharmacological inhibition of LAT1 has been shown to restrict tumor cell growth by limiting amino acid availability and protein synthesis (24, 28, 29). In the context of recurrent PitNETs, the coordinated upregulation of LNAA-related metabolites observed in our metabolomic analysis, together with the increased expression of LAT1 at the protein level, suggests a concerted metabolic adaptation favoring enhanced amino acid uptake. This adaptation may provide tumor cells with sufficient substrates to support protein synthesis and anabolic signaling pathways, such as leucine-associated mTORC1 activation, thereby contributing to tumor growth and recurrence. Notably, pituitary tumor-specific evidence also supports a role for LAT1 in pituitary tumor biology. In rat GH4 pituitary tumor cells, LAT1 was shown to be the predominant LAT isoform, and pharmacological inhibition of LAT1 with JPH203 suppressed leucine uptake, cell growth, and growth hormone production (30). These findings are conceptually consistent with our results and further support the biological relevance of the LNAA–LAT1 axis in recurrent PitNETs. However, because that study was based on a non-human pituitary tumor cell model, its translational relevance to human PitNETs remains limited and requires further validation (30).

Small neutral amino acids are primarily transported by alanine–serine–cysteine transporter 2 (ASCT2, also known as SLC1A5), a sodium-dependent neutral amino acid transporter responsible for the uptake of glutamine, alanine, serine, threonine, and other neutral amino acids (31). ASCT2-mediated transport relies on the transmembrane sodium gradient and plays an important role in maintaining intracellular amino acid homeostasis. Previous studies have reported that ASCT2 is overexpressed in various malignancies, including prostate cancer, melanoma, colorectal cancer, breast cancer, and leukemia (32, 33), and that elevated ASCT2 expression is associated with enhanced tumor cell proliferation, invasion, metastasis, and poor prognosis (34, 35).

Glutamine transported via ASCT2 serves not only as a nitrogen donor for biosynthetic processes but also contributes to anaplerotic replenishment of the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, thereby supporting malignant cell growth. This metabolic mechanism has also been implicated in pituitary adenomas, as Hu et al. demonstrated that activation of the glutamine/glutamate metabolic axis promotes tumor cell proliferation (36). Accordingly, therapeutic strategies targeting ASCT2-mediated glutamine uptake to disrupt tumor metabolism and induce apoptosis or autophagy have been proposed (32, 37). However, no significant difference in ASCT2 expression was observed between primary and recurrent PitNETs in our cohort. This negative result suggests that recurrence-associated metabolic adaptations in PitNETs may not predominantly rely on ASCT2. Instead, alternative glutamine transport mechanisms may compensate for ASCT2, and therefore, a potential contribution of glutamine transport/metabolism to recurrent PitNET biology cannot be excluded.

These findings suggest that recurrent/progressive PitNETs may adopt a preferential amino acid uptake strategy to sustain anabolic demands and tumor growth. Importantly, none of the six recurrent/progressive cases in the metabolomic discovery cohort had received prior antitumor medical therapy or radiotherapy before repeat surgery, although three patients had received hormone replacement therapy for hypopituitarism. Therefore, the amino acid-related metabolic alterations observed in this limited cohort are unlikely to be primarily driven by prior antitumor treatment exposure. Nevertheless, the present study remains limited by its retrospective design, the small number of recurrent/progressive cases, and the biological heterogeneity within this group, which included both true recurrence and regrowth/progression of residual tumor. Accordingly, the observed metabolic changes should be interpreted cautiously and not attributed solely to recurrence/regrowth biology itself. Further studies are warranted to clarify the functional consequences of LNAA–LAT1 axis activation and its potential therapeutic relevance.

Conclusion

Our study shows that metabolic alterations associated with recurrent/progressive PitNETs are characterized by selective changes in amino acid metabolism, particularly involving LNAAs. By integrating metabolomic analysis with protein-level validation, we found that recurrent/progressive PitNETs showed coordinated enrichment of LNAA-related metabolites together with increased expression of their major transporter, LAT1, at the protein level. These findings suggest the possibility that altered amino acid transport may represent an important component of metabolic adaptation in recurrent/progressive PitNETs.

This study is limited by its retrospective design, the relatively small number of recurrent/progressive cases, and the biological heterogeneity within this group. Although non-parametric statistical methods, sensitivity analyses excluding corticotroph adenomas, and validation strategies were applied to enhance robustness, the findings should still be interpreted cautiously. Although functional validation was not performed, our results highlight the LNAA–LAT1 axis as a pathway of interest in recurrent/progressive PitNETs, warranting further investigation in larger cohorts and mechanistic studies.
