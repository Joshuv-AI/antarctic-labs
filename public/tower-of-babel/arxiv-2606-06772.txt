Generalization in Deep Neural Networks: Minimax Rates for Gradient Methods Corresponding author is Puyu Wang.
Junyu Zhou, Puyu Wang, Yunwen Lei, Marius Kloft, Yiming Ying

Abstract
Understanding the generalization performance of over-parameterized neural networks has become a central topic in deep learning theory. While recent advances, particularly works under the Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK) regime, have shed light on the behavior of shallow architectures, the statistical generalization properties of deep neural networks (DNNs), especially in regression tasks, remain far less understood. In this paper, we make significant progress toward closing this gap by providing a comprehensive generalization analysis of DNNs trained using gradient-based methods. First, we establish, for the first time, a crucial connection between the learning dynamics of a DNN with smooth activation functions trained via gradient-based methods and those of kernel methods, showing that gradient-based methods on over-parameterized DNNs can fully inherit the favorable learning dynamics of their kernel counterparts. Building on this connection and the well-established optimality of kernel methods, we derive the first known minimax-optimal rates for the excess population risk of both gradient descent (GD) and stochastic gradient descent (SGD), under the assumption that network width scales polynomially with the sample size. Our results demonstrate that, with sufficient width, DNNs trained by GD or SGD can achieve generalization performance comparable to kernel-based methods.

Introduction

A central mystery in deep learning is how neural networks, despite being highly non-convex and heavily over-parameterized, are able to achieve near-zero training error while still generalizing well to unseen data. This paradox has sparked a surge of research aimed at understanding the convergence and generalization behavior of neural networks
1
;
2
;
6
;
7
;
15
;
38
;
40
;
49
.

The Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK), introduced by
20
, has become one of a foundational tool for understanding the behavior of training dynamics for neural networks, especially those trained using gradient-based methods such as gradient descent (GD) and stochastic gradient descent (SGD). The core idea here is to linearize the neural network around its random initialization, which enables the evolution of the network during training to be closely approximated by a kernel method associated with the corresponding NTK.
This framework establishes a powerful connection between the evolution of a neural network during training process and the behavior of kernel methods in a reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS) induced by the NTK, allowing insights from the kernel methods to inform our understanding of neural networks.

Following this perspective, the influential work
34
showed that for regression problems, shallow neural networks trained by SGD can achieve generalization performance on par with their kernel counterparts. Specifically, under standard regularity conditions on the regression function and capacity assumptions on the RKHS, they proved that shallow neural networks with smooth activation functions trained using SGD can achieve the minimax-optimal excess risk rate of order
\mathcal{O}(n^{-\frac{2\beta}{2\beta+\gamma}})
for
\beta\in[1/2,1]
. Here,
n
is the sample size,
\beta
is the smoothness of the target function and
\gamma\in[0,1]
is a parameter that measures the capacity of the RKHS. cao2024stochastic improved the required network width from exponential in
34
to polynomial in
n
under the same learning setup.
The work
32
further showed that the optimal excess risk rates can also be achieved by GD with shallow neural networks for any
\beta>0
.
However, these works focus exclusively on shallow neural networks, and the generalization performance of deep neural networks (DNNs) remains an open problem.

The recent work
47
offered valuable insights into the generalization ability of DNNs for regression. They studied deep ReLU networks and provided the uniform convergence of the corresponding NTK. Building on this, they showed that the prediction error of one-pass SGD converges to zero in expectation, though explicit convergence rates were not provided. A critical open question remains:

Can DNNs trained via GD/SGD achieve generalization ability comparable to their kernel-based counterparts?

In this work, we offer a positive answer to this question, thereby substantially advancing the understanding of how GD and SGD generalize in deep neural networks, bridging the gap between shallow and deep architectures.
Our main contribution can be summarized as follows.

•

We establish, for the first time, a crucial connection between the learning dynamic of DNNs trained via GD/SGD and those of kernel methods associated with the
infinite-width
NTK in the corresponding RKHS.
This connection implies that GD/SGD on an over-parameterized DNN can fully inherit the favorable learning dynamics of kernel methods.

•

We provide the first known minimax-optimal excess population risk rates of both GD and SGD for DNNs with smooth activations by leveraging our newly connection and the well-established results for kernel methods, imply GD and SGD can replicate the classical results in the kernel setting under the same assumptions. Specifically, we show that provided the network width
m
scales polynomial on simple size
n
, GD and SGD can achieve the optimal rates
\mathcal{O}(n^{-\frac{2\beta}{2\beta+\gamma}})
when
2\beta+\gamma>1
.
Here,
\beta>0
is the smoothness of the target function and
\gamma\in[0,1]
measures the capacity of the RKHS.

•

Our results are highly flexible and can be readily extended to broader scenarios, including more challenging learning problems (e.g., hard problem with
2\beta+\gamma\leq 1
). By applying our connection between DNNs and kernel methods, we can leverage existing results for kernel methods under various assumptions to provide more general and robust generalization guarantees for DNNs.

Technical Contribution

•

A refined analysis of NTK uniform concentration.
Unlike prior work (e.g.,
15
), which focused solely on the concentration of the last layer over a finite set of data points, we establish concentration results that hold uniformly over the entire input space and across all layers.
Moreover, we improve the uniform convergence rate of NTK from the
\mathcal{O}(1/m^{1/6})
scaling in
47
to
\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{m})
, a sharper rate that is crucial for deriving the minimax-optimal excess risk rates.

•

A novel error decomposition.

Existing error decompositions for shallow neural networks typically rely on controlling the distance between the learning dynamic of kernel gradient methods associated with a finite-width NTK
K^{m}
in its corresponding RKHS and the target function.
Achieving tight control of this term when
\gamma<1
usually requires treating
K^{m}
as a sum of i.i.d. random kernels with mean
K
.
However, this approach does not extend naturally to DNNs.
To overcome this limitation, we propose a new decomposition that directly splits the excess risk into two parts: (i) the distance between the learning dynamics of GD/SGD and those of the kernel GD iteration
g_{T}
associated with the infinite-width NTK
K
, and (ii) the error of
g_{T}
relative to the target function.
This decomposition enables us to derive minimax-optimal results for arbitrary
\gamma\in[0,1]
.

•

Improved control of GD/SGD trajectory.
We provide a more precise analysis of the distance GD/SGD moves away from initialization by employing a carefully designed double induction, resulting in a tighter bound independent of
m
. This refinement allows for a more accurate estimation of the gap between the learning dynamics of DNNs and those of kernel methods.

1.1
Related Work

There exists a substantial body of literature dedicated to understanding the generalization ability of neural networks. We briefly review the most relevant work to ours.

NTK Approach.

A rapidly growing line of work has analyzed over-parameterized neural networks in the NTK regime, yielding generalization bounds for models trained via gradient methods, with significant progress in classification settings.
Under margin separation assumptions,
27
;
21
;
33
derived generalization error bounds for shallow networks with ReLU and smooth activations.

6
;
7
further provided algorithm-dependent misclassification bounds for deep ReLU networks trained by GD and SGD, assuming that the data are separable under certain random feature models
36
.

In contrast, understanding of generalization for regression problems remains limited.
The work
2
;
23
established generalization bounds for GD applied to shallow ReLU networks, under favorable NTK Gram matrix assumptions.
Later,
34
;
32
removed the this assumption and derived minimax-optimal excess risk rates for GD and one-pass SGD, respectively, under smooth activations.

5
improved these results in capacity-independent case by reducing the required network width.
The most related work is
47
, which analyzed the uniform convergence of the NTK for deep ReLU networks at Gaussian initialization. Building on this, they proved that the prediction error of one-pass SGD converges to zero in expectation, though explicit rates were not provided.
Notably, sharp excess population risk bounds for DNNs remain an open challenge.

Uniform Convergence Approach.
Another prominent approach to understanding generalization ability of neural networks is based on uniform convergence. This framework leverages complexity measures such as Rademacher complexity and covering numbers to bound the capacity of the hypothesis class, resulting in algorithm-independent generalization guarantees
3
;
16
;
17
;
30
;
35
. Using this framework, generalization bounds of order
\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{n})
have been obtained by trading off sample fit against hypothesis complexity
2
;
10
;
21
;
25
;
26
.
25
improved population risk bounds to
\mathcal{O}(1/n)
via local Rademacher complexity.
Following this line of work, recent studies
4
;
13
;
14
;
22
established generalization bounds for shallow and deep neural networks trained with GD. In particular,
22
derived a nearly optimal convergence rate of
\mathcal{O}(n^{-\frac{2p}{2p+d}+\epsilon})
for any
\epsilon>0
, for a linear combination of
K_{n}
deep smooth networks, under the assumption that the target function is
(p,C)
-smooth. Here,
K_{n}
is a parameter that depends on both sample size
n
and dimension
d
.

Algorithmic Stability Approach.
More recently, the algorithmic stability framework has emerged as a powerful tool for analyzing generalization in neural networks trained via gradient-based methods. For regression tasks,
38
;
24
;
44
demonstrated that shallow networks with smooth activations trained via GD or SGD can achieve excess risk bounds of
\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{n})
. These results rely on the observation that the empirical risk function is smooth and weakly convex, with convexity parameters decaying with network width.

41
advanced this line of work by establishing a self-bounded weak convexity property for logistic loss under realizable settings, while
40
extended these insights to deeper networks. Very recently,
45
;
46
provided population risk bounds for GD and SGD, respectively, for structured Kolmogorov-Arnold networks via on-averaged argument stability methods.

Problem Formulation

Let
S=\{z_{i}=(\mathbf{x}_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
be a training dataset drawn independently from
\rho
.
Our goal is to learn a predictor
f:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
based on
S
. The performance of
f
can be measured by the expected risk

\mathcal{L}(f):=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{(\mathbf{x},y)\sim\rho}[(y-f(\mathbf{x}))^{2}].

The target function
f_{\rho}:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a global minimizer of
\mathcal{L}(\cdot)
over all measurable functions.
In particular, for the least-squares regression, it is well-known that
f_{\rho}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbb{E}[y|\mathbf{x}]
, where
\mathbb{E}[\cdot|\mathbf{x}]
denotes the conditional expectation given
\mathbf{x}
.

Since
\rho
is unknown, we consider minimizing the empirical risk

\mathcal{L}_{S}(f):=\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_{i}-f(\mathbf{x}_{i}))^{2}.

The generalization performance of a predictor
f
measured in terms of the
excess population risk
is defined by

\varepsilon_{risk}(f)=\mathcal{L}(f)-\mathcal{L}(f_{\rho}),

which can be further expressed as (see
11
for more details)

\varepsilon_{risk}(f)=\frac{1}{2}\|f-f_{\rho}\|_{\rho}^{2}.

parameterized by

Uniform Concentration of the NTK

In this section, we show that the NTK for deep smooth network at Gaussian initialization uniformly concentrates to a deterministic kernel.
Based on this concentration, we will provide a comprehensive generalization analysis for both GD and SGD in Sections
4
.

Given an
L
-th layer neural network
f_{\mathbf{W}}(\mathbf{x})
of form (
1
), we define a positive semi-definite (PSD) kernel
K^{m}:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
at initialization
\mathbf{W}(0)
by
K^{m}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})=\sum\nolimits_{\ell=1}^{L}K^{m,\ell}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})
with
K^{m,\ell}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})=\langle\nabla_{\ell}f_{\mathbf{W}(0)}(\mathbf{x}),\nabla_{\ell}f_{\mathbf{W}(0)}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\rangle
the NTK from the
\ell
-th hidden layer at
\mathbf{W}(0)
.
From Theorem 4.21 in
39
, we know
K^{m}
uniquely defines a RKHS
\mathcal{H}_{m}
given by
\mathcal{H}_{m}=\{f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}\ :\ \exists\ \mathbf{W}\in\mathcal{W}\text{ such that }f(\mathbf{x})=\langle\mathbf{W},\nabla f_{\mathbf{W}(0)}(\mathbf{x})\rangle\},

with norm defined, for any
f\in\mathcal{H}_{m}
, by

\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}_{m}}\!\!\!=\!\inf\{\|\mathbf{W}\|_{2}\!\!:\!\mathbf{W}\!\in\!\mathcal{W}\\
\text{ with }f(\mathbf{x})\!=\!\langle\mathbf{W},\!\nabla f_{\mathbf{W}(0)}\!(\mathbf{x})\rangle\!\}.

The kernel
K^{m}
can be seen as a random feature approximation of the following deterministic NTK
K:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}

K(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})=\sum\nolimits_{\ell=1}^{L}K^{\ell}(x,x^{\prime})

with
K^{\ell}=c_{\sigma}\mathbb{E}[\sigma(U^{\ell-1}(\mathbf{x}))\sigma(U^{\ell-1}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}))]\prod_{h=\ell}^{L}q^{h}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})
.
Here,
q^{\ell}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})=c_{\sigma}\mathbb{E}[\sigma^{\prime}(U^{\ell}(\mathbf{x}))\sigma^{\prime}(U^{\ell}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}))]
, and

\{(U^{\ell}(\mathbf{x}),U^{\ell}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}))\}_{\ell=1}^{L}
are pairs of bivariate normal variables defined iteratively by
(U^{\ell}(\mathbf{x}),U^{\ell}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}))\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma^{\ell-1}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}))
with

\displaystyle\Sigma^{\ell\!-\!1}(\mathbf{x},\!\mathbf{x}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\!\!=\!c_{\sigma}\!\!\begin{pmatrix}\!\!\!\!\!\!\mathbb{E}[\sigma^{2}(U^{\ell\!-\!1}(\mathbf{x}))]\!\!\!\!\!\!\!&&\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\mathbb{E}[\sigma(U^{\ell\!-\!1}(\mathbf{x}))\sigma(U^{\ell\!-\!1}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}))]\\
\mathbb{E}[\sigma(U^{\ell\!-\!1}(\mathbf{x}))\sigma(U^{\ell\!-\!1}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}))]\!\!\!\!\!\!\!&&\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\mathbb{E}[\sigma^{2}(U^{\ell\!-\!1}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}))]\end{pmatrix}

and
\mathbb{E}[\sigma(U^{0}(\mathbf{x}))\sigma(U^{0}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}))]=\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\rangle
.
We note that
\mathbb{E}[\sigma(U^{\ell}(\mathbf{x}))\sigma(U^{\ell}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}))]
and
\mathbb{E}[\sigma^{\prime}(U^{\ell}(\mathbf{x}))\sigma^{\prime}(U^{\ell}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}))]

and

.

4
Optimal Rates for Gradient Methods

We begin by introducing some notations w.r.t the infinite width kernel
K
.
Let
\mathbf{L}:\mathcal{L}^{2}_{\rho_{\mathbf{x}}}\to\mathcal{L}^{2}_{\rho_{\mathbf{x}}}
be the integral operator defined by
\mathbf{L}f=\int_{\mathcal{X}}K(\cdot,\mathbf{x})f(\mathbf{x})d\rho_{\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{x})

Note

has the eigen-decomposition
\mathbf{L}f=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{i}\langle f,\Phi_{i}\rangle_{\mathcal{L}^{2}_{\rho_{\mathbf{x}}}}\Phi_{i}
for any

Here,
\{(\lambda_{i},\Phi_{i})\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
are pairs of eigenvalues
and orthogonal eigenfunctions in
\mathcal{L}^{2}_{\rho_{\mathbf{x}}}
with
\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\ldots\geq 0
, and
\{\Phi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
forms an orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{L}^{2}_{\rho_{\mathbf{x}}}
. For
s\in\mathbb{R}
, we define the power
\mathbf{L}^{s}
as, for any

,
\mathbf{L}^{s}(f)=\sum_{i:\lambda_{i}\neq 0}^{\infty}\lambda_{i}^{s}\langle f,\Phi_{i}\rangle_{\mathcal{L}^{2}_{\rho_{\mathbf{x}}}}\Phi_{i}
.

To analyze the performance of kernel methods, we impose the following standard assumptions also used in
34
;
32
on the capacity of the RKHS and the complexity of the target function
f_{\rho}
.

Assumption 2

(Effective dimension)
.

For some
\gamma\!\in\![0,1]
and
c_{\gamma}\!\geq\!1
, there holds

tr\big(\mathbf{L}(\mathbf{L}+\lambda\mathbf{I})^{-1}\big)=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{\lambda_{i}}{\lambda_{i}+\lambda}\leq c_{\gamma}\lambda^{-\gamma}\text{ for all }\lambda>0.

Here, the parameter
\gamma
is related to the effective dimension of
\mathcal{H}_{K}

8
or the degrees of freedom
48
.
Since

is uniformly bounded (see Eq (
A
) in the appendix), we know
\mathbf{L}
is a trace class operator. Then, Assumption
2
naturally holds with
\gamma=1
, which corresponds to the capacity-independent setting.

Assumption 3

(Source condition)
.

There exist
\beta>0
and
B>0
, such that
\|\mathbf{L}^{-\beta}f_{\rho}\|_{\rho}\leq B
.

Assumption

characterizes the smoothness (or regularity) of
f_{\rho}
. A larger value of
\beta
indicates greater smoothness of
f_{\rho}
, making the assumption correspondingly more stringent.
When
\beta\geq 1/2
, the assumption implies that
f_{\rho}\in\mathcal{H}_{K}
, meaning that there exists at least one minimizer of the population risk within the RKHS
\mathcal{H}_{K}
.

A concrete example.
Suppose the marginal distribution
\rho_{\mathbf{x}}
is a uniform distribution on the unit sphere
S^{d-1}
and
f_{\rho}
is any polynomial. Then, Assumptions
2
and
3
hold with
\gamma=\frac{d-1}{d}
and
\beta=+\infty
(see
34
).

Symmetric initialization of GD and SGD.

Assume
m
is even.
We consider symmetric Gaussian initialization for studying GD and SGD, which are widely used in the theoretical analysis of neural networks

;
34
;
47
.
In particular, we adopt Gaussian initialization for all weights of the first

\mathbf{w}_{r}^{1}(0)\overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{I}_{d})

for all

and

.
Consider the weights of the last and the output layers are initialized additionally using the symmetric weights defined as follows:

\displaystyle\!\mathbf{w}_{r}^{L}\!(0)\!\!\overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\!\sim\!}\!\mathcal{N}\!(0,\mathbf{I}_{m})\textit{ for }r\!\in\!\big\{\!1,\!\ldots\!,\!\frac{m}{2}\!\big\}\textit{ and }\mathbf{w}_{r\!+\!\frac{m}{2}}^{L}\!(0)\!\!=\!\mathbf{w}_{r}^{L}\!(0)

Symmetric initialization is primarily employed to ensure

at initialization, thereby simplifying the analysis.

To study the excess risk rates for GD, we first introduce some useful definitions and notations. Recall that

Regarding the empirical risk

as a functional on the RKHS

\mathcal{H}_{K}
by

\displaystyle g_{k+1}\!=\!g_{k}\!-\!\frac{\eta}{n}\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{n}(g_{k}(\mathbf{x}_{i})-y_{i})K_{\mathbf{x}_{i}}\text{ with $g_{0}=0.$ }

(4)

Let
\mathbf{W}(T)
and

We develop the following novel error decomposition

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{risk}\big(f_{\mathbf{W}(T)}\big)\lesssim\big\|f_{\mathbf{W}(T)}-\mathbf{S}g_{T}\big\|_{\rho}^{2}+\!\big\|\mathbf{S}g_{T}-f_{\rho}\big\|_{\rho}^{2},

where
\mathbf{S}:\mathcal{H}_{K}\xhookrightarrow{}\mathcal{L}^{2}_{\rho_{\mathbf{x}}}

The first term

We assume

Remark 1
.

Different from previous works for shallow neural networks
34
;
32
, which decompose
\varepsilon_{risk}
into two terms,
\|f_{\mathbf{W}(T)}-g_{T}^{m}\|_{\rho}^{2}
and
\|g_{T}^{m}-f_{\rho}\|_{\rho}^{2}
, where
g^{m}_{T}
is GD/SGD associated with the finite-width NTK

within the RKHS
\mathcal{H}_{m}
, we take a different approach.
Specifically, we introduce
g_{T}\in\mathcal{H}_{K}
and directly establish a connection between
f_{\mathbf{W}(T)}
and
g_{T}
in the RKHS
\mathcal{H}_{K}
associated with the infinite-width NTK

.
This new error decomposition enables us to extend minimax-optimal rates for DNNs from the special case of
\gamma=1
to a more general setting with
\gamma\in[0,1]
.
Further details are provided in the technical novelty below.

Define

\mathcal{M}:=\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\sup_{z\in\mathcal{Z}\atop\|\mathbf{W}\!-\!\mathbf{W}(0)\|_{2}\!\leq\!\sqrt{m}}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\big(\big\|\nabla f_{\mathbf{W}}(\mathbf{x})\big\|_{2}^{2}\!+\!\big\|\nabla^{2}f_{\mathbf{W}}(\mathbf{x})\big\|_{op}^{2}\big|f_{\mathbf{W}}(\mathbf{x})\!-\!y\big|\big).

The following proposition provides an estimate of the first error term in (
5
), establishing a key connection, for the first time, between the learning dynamics of DNNs and those in the RKHS
\mathcal{H}_{K}
through the use of over-parameterization. The proof can be found in Appendix C.

Proposition 1
.

Let
\delta\in(0,1)
and
T\in\mathbb{N}
.
Suppose Assumption
1
holds.
Assume that
\eta\leq\big(\mathcal{M}\vee(\|K\|_{\infty}+1)\big)^{-1}
,
1\leq\eta T\leq n\big(9(\mathcal{M}\vee\|K\|_{\infty})\log(3n/\delta)\big)^{-1}
and
m\gtrsim C_{\sigma}^{L}d(\eta T)^{2}\log^{2}({m}/{\delta})
.
With probability at least
1-L^{2}\exp(-C_{\sigma}d\log^{2}(m))-\delta
over initialization
(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{W}(0))
and sampling, it holds that

\|f_{\mathbf{W}(T)}-\mathbf{S}g_{T}\|_{\rho}^{2}\lesssim\frac{C_{\sigma}^{L}d(\eta T)^{4}\log^{2}(m)}{m}.

Proposition
1
shows that over-parameterization prolongs the duration a network remains in the NTK regime, where its learning dynamic can be effectively characterized by kernel GD associated with the infinite-width NTK
K
in
\mathcal{H}_{K}
. Consequently, as long as the network width
m
is sufficiently large, by selecting an appropriate learning rate
\eta
and number of iterations
T
, GD of an over-parameterized DNN can fully inherit the favorable learning dynamic
g_{T}
in
\mathcal{H}_{K}
.

Technical novelty.

As we mentioned in Remark
1
,
the commonly used error decomposition in
34
;
32
requires estimating the term
\|g^{m}_{T}-f_{\rho}\|_{\rho}^{2}
.
A critical step to control this term is to bound
\|g^{m}_{T}-h_{m}\|_{\rho}^{2}
by
n^{-\frac{2\beta}{2\beta+\tilde{\gamma}}}
, where
h^{m}
is either the minimizer of the regularized population risk over
\mathcal{H}_{m}

34
or the GD for the population risk in
\mathcal{H}_{m}

32
, and
\tilde{\gamma}
is its effective dimension.
Achieving minimax-optimal rates thus requires showing that
\mathcal{H}_{m}
and
\mathcal{H}_{K}
share the same effective dimension, i.e.,
\tilde{\gamma}=\gamma
.
For
\gamma=1
, this equivalence holds trivially since the integral operator associated with
K^{m}
is a trace-class operator.
For
\gamma<1
when considering shallow neural networks, it is typically established by treating
K^{m}
as a sum of i.i.d. random kernels with mean
K
(see Proposition B in
34
and Proposition A.18 in
31
).
However, this argument does not extend to deep networks: in DNNs, the gradient
\partial_{\mathbf{w}^{\ell}_{r}}f_{\mathbf{W}(0)}(\mathbf{x})
depends not only on
\mathbf{w}^{\ell}_{r}(0)
in layer
\ell
, but also on weights from all preceding and subsequent layers, introducing intricate dependencies.
This inter-layer coupling renders a direct extension to DNNs significantly more challenging.

To address this, we develop a refined error decomposition (
5
) that avoids directly bounding
\|g^{m}_{T}-h_{m}\|_{\rho}^{2}
. Instead, we focus on
\|g^{m}_{T}-g_{T}\|_{\rho}^{2}
.
The
key finding
enabling this refinement is that the
L^{2}_{\rho}
distance between kernel GD iterates can be controlled by the difference between their kernels. Specifically, for any RKHSs
\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2}
with bounded kernels
K^{1},K^{2}
, letting
g^{1}_{T}
and
g^{2}_{T}
denote the corresponding GD iterates (defined analogously to (
4
)), it holds that

\|g^{1}_{T}-g^{2}_{T}\|_{\rho}\leq\|g^{1}_{T}-g^{2}_{T}\|_{\infty}\lesssim(\eta T)^{2}\|K^{1}-K^{2}\|_{\infty}.

This result implies that kernel GD iterates remain close whenever their kernels are similar.
Consequently,
\|g^{m}_{T}-g_{T}\|_{\rho}^{2}
is controlled by
(\eta T)^{4}\|K^{m}\!-\!K\|_{\infty}^{2}
, and, combined with Theorem
1
, this completes the estimation of the error term for any
\gamma\in[0,1]
.
More details is given in Proposition
21
in the appendix.

By leveraging the well-established optimality for kernel GD in
28
together with Proposition
1

Theorem 2
.

Let
\delta\in(0,1)
and

Suppose Assumptions

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{A}}\big[\varepsilon_{risk}(f_{\mathbf{W}(T)})\big]\lesssim\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{A}}\big[\big\|f_{\mathbf{W}(T)}-\mathbf{S}g_{T}\big\|_{\rho}^{2}\big]+\big\|\mathbf{S}g_{T}-\!f_{\rho}\big\|_{\rho}^{2},

Comparison with existing works.
The most closely related works to ours are
5

34
, which study the generalization performance of one-pass SGD for shallow neural networks with smooth activation functions. In particular,

Limitation.
A potential limitation of the NTK approach lies in its reliance on linear approximations to evaluate network performance during training. In practical applications, local linearity may not always hold, particularly when the number of training iterations is large or the network width is insufficient. Our results depend only on a moderate number of iterations, under which the use of linear approximations is theoretically justified and feasible within our framework. However, in practice, training often involves a larger number of iterations. We therefore recognize the importance of exploring alternative approaches to study the generalization performance of neural networks and view this as an important direction for future research.

5
Conclusion

In this paper, we establish a key connection between the learning dynamics of DNNs and those in the RKHS by leveraging over-parameterization. Building on this connection, we demonstrate that DNNs with smooth activations trained via GD/SGD can achieve the minimax-optimal excess risk rate
\mathcal{O}(n^{-\frac{2\beta}{2\beta+\gamma}})
, provided the network width satisfies
m\gtrsim\text{Poly}(n,d)
. Our results show that GD/SGD with DNNs can attain generalization performance comparable to classical gradient methods in the kernel setting.
Several avenues for future research remain. First, it would be valuable to reduce the exponential dependence on depth
L
in the generalization bounds and the associated width requirement
m
. Second, extending the analysis to other architectures, such as convolutional or residual networks, represents another promising direction.

\displaystyle\frac{\partial f_{\mathbf{W}(0)}(\mathbf{x})}{\partial\mathbf{w}^{\ell}_{r}(0)^{\top}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\mathbf{a}^{\top}o^{L}_{0}(\mathbf{x})}{\partial o^{L}_{0}(\mathbf{x})}\frac{\partial o^{L}_{0}(\mathbf{x})}{\partial o^{L-1}_{0}(\mathbf{x})}\cdots\frac{\partial o^{\ell+1}_{0}(\mathbf{x})}{\partial o^{\ell}_{0}(\mathbf{x})}\frac{\partial o^{\ell}_{0}(\mathbf{x})}{\partial\mathbf{w}^{\ell}_{r}(0)^{\top}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{a}^{\top}\Big[\prod_{h=\ell+1}^{L}\sqrt{\frac{c_{\sigma}}{m}}\mathbf{D}^{h}_{0}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{W}^{h}(0)\Big]\sqrt{\frac{c_{\sigma}}{m}}\mathbf{D}^{\ell}_{0}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{O}^{\ell-1}_{r}(\mathbf{x}),

where
\mathbf{O}^{\ell-1}_{r}(\mathbf{x})
is a matrix such that its
r
-th row is
(o^{\ell-1}_{0}(\mathbf{x}))^{\top}
and other rows are
\mathbf{0}
.
We further define

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}^{\ell}_{L,0}(\mathbf{x})=\bigg(\Big[\prod_{h=\ell+1}^{L}\sqrt{\frac{c_{\sigma}}{m}}\mathbf{D}^{h}_{0}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{W}^{h}(0)\Big]\sqrt{\frac{c_{\sigma}}{m}}\mathbf{D}^{\ell}_{0}(\mathbf{x})\bigg)^{\top}.

Here, we use the conventional notation
\prod_{h=k}^{s}\mathbf{A}_{h}=\mathbf{I}
if
k>s
.

Then, it holds that

\displaystyle\frac{\partial f_{\mathbf{W}(0)}(\mathbf{x})}{\partial\mathbf{W}^{\ell}(0)}=\mathbf{V}^{\ell}_{L,0}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{a}\big(o^{\ell-1}_{0}(\mathbf{x})\big)^{\top}\text{ for all }\ell\in[L].

The above derivative for each layer
\ell
induces a PSD kernel
K^{m,\ell}:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
, which is defined as

\displaystyle K^{m,\ell}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\Big\langle\frac{\partial f_{\mathbf{W}(0)}(\mathbf{x})}{\partial\mathbf{W}^{\ell}(0)},\frac{\partial f_{\mathbf{W}(0)}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})}{\partial\mathbf{W}^{\ell}(0)}\Big\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle o^{\ell-1}_{0}(\mathbf{x}),o^{\ell-1}_{0}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\rangle\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{V}^{\ell}_{L,0}(\mathbf{x})^{\top}\mathbf{V}^{\ell}_{L,0}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{a}.

We will prove later that
K^{m,\ell}
converges to a deterministic NTK
K^{\ell}:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
uniformly as
m\to\infty
, which is defined by

\displaystyle K^{\ell}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})=c_{\sigma}\mathbb{E}[\sigma(U^{\ell-1}(\mathbf{x}))\sigma(U^{\ell-1}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}))]\prod_{h=\ell}^{L}q^{h}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}),

(8)

here
\{(U^{\ell}(\mathbf{x}),U^{\ell}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}))\}_{\ell=1}^{L}
are pairs of bivariate normal variables defined iteratively by
(U^{\ell}(\mathbf{x}),U^{\ell}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}))\!\sim\!\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma^{\ell-1}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}))
with

\displaystyle\Sigma^{\ell\!-\!1}(\mathbf{x},\!\mathbf{x}^{\prime})=c_{\sigma}\left(\begin{aligned} &\mathbb{E}[\sigma^{2}(U^{\ell-1}(\mathbf{x}))]&&\mathbb{E}[\sigma(U^{\ell-1}(\mathbf{x}))\sigma(U^{\ell-1}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}))]\\
\mathbb{E}[&\sigma(U^{\ell-1}(\mathbf{x}))\sigma(U^{\ell-1}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}))]&&\ \ \mathbb{E}[\sigma^{2}(U^{\ell-1}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}))]\end{aligned}\right)\!,

and

\displaystyle\Sigma^{0}(\mathbf{x},\!\mathbf{x}^{\prime})=\left(\begin{aligned} &1&&\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\rangle\\
\langle\mathbf{x}&,\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\rangle&&1\end{aligned}\!\right)

and
\mathbb{E}[\sigma(U^{0}(\mathbf{x}))\sigma(U^{0}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}))]=c_{\sigma}^{-1}\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\rangle
,

q^{\ell}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})=c_{\sigma}\mathbb{E}[\sigma^{\prime}(U^{\ell}(\mathbf{x}))\sigma^{\prime}(U^{\ell}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}))]
.
Note that for all
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}
and
\ell\in[L]
,
\mathbb{E}[\sigma(U^{\ell}(\mathbf{x}))\sigma(U^{\ell}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}))]
and
\mathbb{E}[\sigma^{\prime}(U^{\ell}(\mathbf{x}))\sigma^{\prime}(U^{\ell}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}))]
are deterministic, and does not involve any randomness.

We claim that

\displaystyle c_{\sigma}\mathbb{E}[\sigma^{2}(U^{\ell}(\mathbf{x}))]=1\text{ for all }\ell\in[L-1]\text{ and }\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}.

(9)

We prove this by induction on the layer
\ell
.
Note
U^{1}(\mathbf{x})\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
.
Recall that we define
c_{\sigma}=(\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)}[\sigma^{2}(x)])^{-1}
, it holds that
c_{\sigma}\mathbb{E}[\sigma^{2}(U^{1}(\mathbf{x}))]=1
.
Suppose this claim holds for the layer
\ell
, we will show that it also holds for the layer
\ell+1
. Indeed, the induction assumption implies that
c_{\sigma}\mathbb{E}[\sigma^{2}(U^{\ell}(\mathbf{x}))]=1
.
From the definition of
U^{\ell+1}(\mathbf{x})
we know
U^{\ell+1}(\mathbf{x})\sim\mathcal{N}\big(0,c_{\sigma}\mathbb{E}[\sigma^{2}(U^{\ell}(\mathbf{x})))]\big)=\mathcal{N}(0,1)
.
Then,
c_{\sigma}\mathbb{E}[\sigma^{2}(U^{\ell+1}(\mathbf{x}))]=c_{\sigma}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)}[\sigma^{2}(x)]=1
.

Now, we can show that the NTK
K
is uniformly bounded.
Indeed, for any
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
, we know

\displaystyle K(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\Big[c_{\sigma}\mathbb{E}[\sigma(U^{\ell-1}(\mathbf{x}))\sigma(U^{\ell-1}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}))]\prod_{h=\ell}^{L}c_{\sigma}\mathbb{E}[\sigma^{\prime}(U^{h}(\mathbf{x}))\sigma^{\prime}(U^{h}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}))]\Big]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\Big[\sqrt{c_{\sigma}\mathbb{E}[\sigma^{2}(U^{\ell-1}(\mathbf{x}))]}\sqrt{c_{\sigma}\mathbb{E}[\sigma^{2}(U^{\ell-1}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}))]}\prod_{h=\ell}^{L}c_{\sigma}\mathbb{E}[\sigma^{\prime}(U^{h}(\mathbf{x}))\sigma^{\prime}(U^{h}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}))]\Big]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\prod_{h=\ell}^{L}c_{\sigma}\mathbb{E}[\sigma^{\prime}(U^{h}(\mathbf{x}))\sigma^{\prime}(U^{h}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}))]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}c_{\sigma}B_{\sigma^{\prime}}^{2(L-\ell+1)}\leq C_{\sigma}^{L},

(10)

where in the first inequality we have used Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, in the second inequality we have used (
9
) and in the last inequality we have used
L\leq C^{L}.

implies
1\leq\eta T\leq n\big(9(\mathcal{M}\vee\|K\|_{\infty})\log(12n/\delta)\big)^{-1}

Since

, we can show that
m\gtrsim C_{\sigma}^{L}dn^{\frac{2\beta+4}{2\beta+\gamma}}\log^{2}\big(\frac{dLn}{\delta}\big)
implies the condition
m\gtrsim C_{\sigma}^{L}d\log^{2}({m}/{\delta})
and ensures
\frac{C_{\sigma}^{L}d(\eta T)^{4}\log^{2}(m)}{m}\lesssim C_{\sigma}^{L}n^{-\frac{2\beta}{2\beta+\gamma}}
in a similar way.

By further noting that
m\gtrsim C_{\sigma}^{L}dn^{\frac{2\beta+4}{2\beta+\gamma}}\log^{2}\big(\frac{dLn}{\delta}\big)
and
n\gtrsim\delta^{-1}
imply

Then, from Theorem
2
and (
A

1-\delta
over initialization
(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{W}(0))
and sampling, there holds

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(f_{\mathbf{W}(T)})-\mathcal{L}(f_{\rho})\lesssim C_{\sigma}^{L}n^{-\frac{2\beta}{2\beta+\gamma}}+C_{\sigma}^{L(\beta\vee 1)}\Big(\frac{{\eta T}}{n^{2}}+\frac{(\eta T)^{\gamma}+(\eta T)^{1-2\beta}}{n}\Big)\log^{4}\big(\frac{T}{\delta}\big)+(\eta T)^{-2\beta}.

In addition, since

\eta T\geq 1

Plugging the choice of

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(f_{\mathbf{W}(T)})-\mathcal{L}(f_{\rho})\lesssim C_{\sigma}^{L(\beta\vee 1)}n^{-\frac{2\beta}{2\beta+\gamma}}\log^{4}\big(\frac{n}{\delta}\big).

The proof is complete.

∎
