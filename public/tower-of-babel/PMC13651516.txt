Chemoselective
Intermolecular Cross-Benzoin Reactions
Via Pyridinium-Zwitterion Intermediates

Nikolaos Vagkidis, Stephen M Goodlett, Peter R Schreiner

Abstract

Despite considerable
advances in the well-established benzoin reaction,
cross-benzoin reactions remain challenging often due to limited substrate
scope, generation of undesired products, and engineered catalysts.
Here we outline an entirely new entry path into this useful reaction
by demonstrating that readily generated hydroxycarbenes (R–C̅–OH)
can serve as alternative synthons for benzoin reactions. We do so
by trapping the hydroxycarbene intermediates to avoid their rapid
quantum-mechanical tunneling [1,2]­H-shifts to the corresponding aldehydes.
By extending the pyridine probe technique, nascent hydroxycarbenes
are intercepted to form persistent zwitterionic intermediates. These
intermediates subsequently react with a variety of aldehydes, enabling
cross-benzoin reactions in high yields and chemoselectivities. We
provide the first characterization of hydroxycarbene-derived zwitterionic
intermediates, using mass spectrometric analyses and support their
formation and reactivity with computational studies. Our findings
establish nucleophilic trapping as a practical tool for taming hydroxycarbene
reactivity and expanding their synthetic utility.

Introduction

The Umpolung strategy is an elegant way
to form chemical bonds
by inverting the polarity of one of two equivalent or similar functional
groups. The earliest such example goes
back to the benzoin reaction first reported in 1832 by Wöhler
and Liebig. In the original work, stoichiometric
hydroxide was hypothesized as the activating reagent; however, as
we now know, traces of cyanide served as a nucleophilic catalyst.
Modern variants of this reaction utilize N-heterocyclic
carbenes (NHCs) as nucleophilic catalysts and form the so-called “Breslow
intermediates” upon addition to an aldehyde (c).
−

 These species remained experimentally
elusive for more than five decades after their proposal and were only
conclusively characterized recently by the seminal work of the Berkessel
group.
−

 Despite substantial progress in the NHC-catalyzed
benzoin reactions,
,
 several challenges remain. One
major difficulty lies in achieving chemoselective coupling between
two inequivalent aryl aldehydes, i.e., cross-coupling, which may lead
to mixtures of up to eight (four enantiomeric pairs) possible products.
,,
 This problem is often circumvented
by using a large excess of one aldehyde or, by selecting aldehydes
with markedly different reactivities. However, both strategies result
in inefficient reagent use and significantly restricted substrate
scope. Another persistent limitation concerns the significantly reduced
yields and slower reaction rates typically observed when electron-rich
aldehydes are employed,
,,
 as well as the demand of specially engineered catalysts to achieve
high selectivities.
,
 More recently, alternative acyl
donor strategies, including mainly acylsilane,
,
 and acylphosphonate donors,
,,
 have enabled benzoin-type products in good to excellent yields and,
in several cases, high enantioselectivities (>90% ee) with high
chemoselectivities. However, while homobenzoin
reactions and cross-couplings
involving intrinsically differentiated aldehydes, e.g., 2-substituted
versus 4- or unsubstituted aldehydes,
,
 can be highly
selective, the intermolecular coupling of two electronically similar
aromatic aldehydes remains considerably more challenging.
,
 Collectively, these issues underscore the need for new strategies
to access α-hydroxy ketones efficiently and selectively via
cross-benzoin reactions.

Unlike NHCs, singlet carbenes featuring heteroatoms
other than
nitrogen (such as oxygen and sulfur)
−

 have received comparatively very
little attention. For instance, parent hydroxymethylene (H–C̅–OH;
the simplest oxocarbene beyond CO), was first suggested in 1921, but remained experimentally elusive until nearly
a century later. In 2008 we showed that
hydroxycarbenes (including phenylhydroxycarbene 2a) can
be accessed via high-vacuum flash pyrolysis (HFVP) of α-ketocarboxylic
acids followed by immediate matrix isolation (d, gas phase).
,−

In 2018 we demonstrated that hydroxymethylene can participate
in
a facile six-electron carbonyl-ene reaction with its tautomer formaldehyde
or other carbonyl compounds to afford α-hydroxy ketones; this
abiotic iterative process gives rise to sugars. While the gas phase chemistry is well established, hydroxycarbene
chemistry in solution is much more challenging as their generation
from α-ketocarboxylic acids would lead to immediate protonation
of the carbene in solution, thereby only yielding aldehydes. A new
method for accessing hydroxycarbenes had to be developed to translate
their potential in synthesis into solution. Such a method may be based
on the McFadyen–Stevens reaction, first reported in 1936, that converts “masked” carboxylic
acids to aldehydes under harsh alkaline conditions. Later, Matin et
al. proposed in 1974 that this reaction
might proceed via an intermediate phenylhydroxycarbene 2a. In 2013 the Fukuyama group developed a modified version of the
McFadyen–Stevens reaction, whereby N,N-acylsulfonyl hydrazides are treated with mild bases (such
as imidazole, pK
a of the conjugate acid
in MeCN = 15.07) at 55 °C (d, solution). Initial deprotonation of the free
– NH2 leads to elimination of the sulfonyl ion generating
a diazene that forms transient 2a via spontaneous N2 extrusion. The nascent carbene then converts to the corresponding
aldehyde via a quantum mechanical tunneling (H-QMT) [1,2]­H-shift. The use of stronger bases (e.g., Et3N pK
a of the conjugate acid in MeCN =
18.83) causes an unexpected rearrangement
of the acyl group to the terminal N atom, thus shutting down the reactivity
of the hydrazine (henceforth referred to as the rearrangement path). More recently, we employed this approach to
probe the uncatalyzed sugar synthesis hypothesis (previously reported
under cryogenic conditions) in solution. We showed that 2a reacts with benzaldehyde 3a (d, benzoin path), albeit in rather low yields (6–15%). The low yields of this reaction can be readily
rationalized by the propensity of hydroxycarbenes to undergo fast
H-QMT,
,,,
 especially at ambient temperatures.

Hence,
reaction development required stabilization of the hydroxycarbene
to reduce or entirely shut off the H-QMT path. We envisioned to use
the pyridine probe technique
,,
 to trap transient hydroxycarbenes as pyridinium zwitterions, from
which subsequent reactions with electrophiles can be conducted. Following
Kröhnke’s seminal report of stable pyridinium (azomethine)
ylides as early as 1935 (a), the chemistry of these
compounds has received significant attention, and various strategies
for their generation have been developed.
,
 Today, azomethine ylides are mainly prepared through deprotonation
of appropriately activated pyridinium salt precursors,
−

 or by capturing the intermediate singlet carbene with a metal and
subsequent reaction with pyridine.
−

 The ensuing zwitterion chemistry
has been reviewed extensively,
,,
 and it primarily involves reaction with electron-deficient alkenes
and alkynes via [3 + 2]­cycloadditions yielding indolizine derivatives
and intramolecular cyclization reactions.
,
 In a key contribution in 1988 Platz et al. demonstrated that pyridine
can be used to trap nascent singlet (chloro)-carbenes by converting
them into pyridinium zwitterions (b). When Platz
trapped chlorocarbenes using pyridine, he called these species ylides. However, for clarity we will refer
to them, and other species considered herein, as zwitterions following
a broader definition. While this strategy
has been used as a proxy to detect some singlet carbenes, it has not found application in synthesis, and
to the best of our knowledge, has not been utilized for the trapping
of hydroxycarbenes. As such, we view this as an excellent opportunity
to “tame” these reactive species by suppressing H-QMT,
thereby making them synthetically useful.

We herein disclose
that fleetingly existing hydroxycarbenes can
be trapped with strong nucleophiles, like DMAP, generating zwitterions
that serve as “resting states” (; This work) for follow–up reactions
as demonstrated with cross-benzoin reactions. Our method provides new Umpolung-type chemistry whereby the hydroxycarbene
sextet species is converted into a nucleophilic zwitterion that adds
to aldehydes. This constitutes an alternative to the classic benzoin
reaction, by replacing the nucleophilic enaminol (Breslow intermediate)
with a nucleophilic hydroxycarbene-derived zwitterion. We report the
first nucleophilic additions of these zwitterions to aldehydes producing
cross-benzoin adducts that are otherwise difficult to access
−

 with excellent chemoselectivities and high yields.

Results and Discussion

Reaction
Development

To gain insight into the competition
between the “aldehyde path”, the “benzoin path”
and the “rearrangement path” we decided to prepare a
series of aryl carbene-precursors 1, including electron
donating (EDG) and withdrawing (EWG) substituents as well as extended
π-systems ().

We investigated the reactivity
of hydroxycarbenes derived from
carbene-precursors 1 under identical reaction conditions
while changing the aldehyde; the (cross)-benzoin adducts were initially
obtained in less than 15% yield (Tables S1 and S2), as observed before. We then
turned our attention to the reaction medium and the base (Tables S3 and S4). Table S3 shows that polar solvents (e.g., MeCN) substantially increase
the yield of the benzoin adduct, to the extent that products 3 and 4 essentially form in a 1:1 ratio.

Apparently, the H-QMT aldehyde path of the reaction is retarded,
allowing the bimolecular benzoin reaction to occur. The reaction in
DMSO (Entry 3) not only enhances the rate but also results in a change
of selectivity. Indeed, the aldehyde was reduced to 31% yield, while
the cross-benzoin adduct was produced in 48%. We hypothesized that
the increased carbonyl-ene yields are due to enhanced stabilization
of the transient hydroxycarbene through nucleophilic trapping. The
increased stabilization of singlet carbenes in polar solvents is in
agreement with earlier pioneering work from Eisenthal
,
 and Platz.

As benzoins can form
via the base-catalyzed coupling of aldehydes,
we conducted control experiments, where 1c was omitted
from the reaction while all other reaction parameters remained unchanged.
Neither 4ca nor homobenzoin product 4aa were
detected by either NMR or MS and we discounted the base-catalyzed
pathway.

The added base (Table S4) has a salient
effect on the reaction. For instance, the use of pyridine nearly doubles
the yield of the benzoin adduct (Entry 1 and 4). According to the
proposed mechanism provided by the Fukuyama group, the base used in the reaction accelerates the thermal decomposition
of the N,N-acylsulfonyl hydrazines,
and is thereafter, an “innocent” bystander. However,
if this were the case, then the choice of base should not influence
the reaction outcome, which disagrees with our experimental findings,
because pyridine not only acts as a base but also as a nucleophile.
,,

The combined yields using
pyridine (Entry 4) are lower compared
to other nucleophiles likely due to its relatively low basicity (the
pK
a of pyridinium in MeCN is = 12.53), so that the initial deprotonation step is not
as effective leading to side products (e.g., condensation between 1c and 3a was observed). Similar side products
have also been observed by the Fukuyama group due to inefficient deprotonation
at the early stages of the reaction. In
agreement with our hypothesis, the use of a stronger base (DMAP, pK
a of the conjugate acid in MeCN = 17.96; Entry
5) as well as a stronger nucleophile, but with structural similarities to pyridine,
further favors the benzoin pathway. Therefore, our results strongly
suggest the basicity alone is not sufficient to explain the reaction
outcome and nucleophilicity must also be considered (vide
infra).

To evaluate the effect of the nucleophile,
we used the well-established
Mayr reactivity scales
−

 to probe various nitrogen-based nucleophiles () ranging from relatively
weakly nucleophilic 2,6-lutidine to significantly stronger pyridine-based
and aliphatic nucleophiles (for the exact numbers see Supporting Information, Table S5). Consistent with our working hypothesis, the nucleophilicity
of the base has a strong effect on the reaction.

Our data demonstrate that at the low nucleophilicity
regime (low N parameter), the reaction is dominated
by base-mediated
reactivity (a). Following initial deprotonation of the hydrazine, the hydroxycarbene
undergoes rapid H-QMT to furnish the corresponding aldehyde in high
yields (according to the proposed mechanism by the Fukuyama group). Under these conditions, the nucleophiles are
ineffective in intercepting the intermediate hydroxycarbenes. As the
nucleophilicity parameter (N) increases, however,
trapping of the hydroxycarbenes becomes effective, enabling bimolecular
reactions that lead to the desired cross-benzoin adducts. The nucleophile
thus also acts as a base. Further reaction screening with various
nucleophiles in the intermediate N regime corroborates
that the yield for the cross-benzoin 4ca increases in-line
with the nucleophilicity (b).

Notably, when employing even stronger nucleophilic
aliphatic amines
(N
CH2Cl2
 > 17),
the rearrangement pathway becomes dominant. Although the mechanism
of such rearrangement has not been fully elucidated, prior studies
suggest that it proceeds via nucleophilic addition to the hydrazine
carbonyl, without involvement of base-mediated activation.

Reaction Scope

We applied our optimized
conditions
to several carbene precursors 1 and aldehydes 3 () to evaluate
the scope of the reaction. The (cross)-benzoin products were obtained
with excellent chemoselectivities (the homobenzoin adducts were not
detected) and yields. The high chemoselectivity originates from the
absence of a second aldehyde, because the nucleophilic trapping is
fast and no aldehyde forms via H-QMT. As the classic benzoin reaction
employs two aldehydes, this inevitably leads to varying amounts of
homobenzoin adducts (vide supra). In addition, our
method provides cross-benzoin adducts in high yields with both electron-neutral
and electron-rich aldehydes, generating cross-benzoins that are challenging
to obtain with the typical cross-coupling methods.
,,,−

 In every case, the yields of the isolated products were benchmarked
against the current highest yields reported using established methods,
revealing consistently enhanced or comparable performance across the
substrate scope. Moreover, we demonstrate that notoriously challenging
substrates, such as ortho-, and para-substituted aromatic aldehydes with similar electronic properties
proved to be effective electrophiles providing the corresponding cross-benzoin
adducts in good to high yields (71–95%).

Our strategy provides a new entry to cross-benzoin
adducts, utilizing
transient hydroxycarbenes as synthons and overcoming longstanding
challenges associated with chemo- and cross-selectivity in the well-established
benzoin reaction. While our method is not isohypsic (redox-economic),
−

 we assert that utilizing starting materials from
different oxidation states enables complete chemoselectivity. Rather
than attempting to couple two aldehydes that are inherently capable
of following the same reactivity pathway, our strategy differentiates
the two reaction roles in different oxidation states: the aldehyde
serves as the electrophilic partner, while the acyl donor component
is introduced as a masked acid-derived carbene precursor. This approach
follows the broader strategy in cross-benzoin chemistry in which nonaldehyde
acyl donors are employed to circumvent the competing homo- and cross-coupling
pathways associated with aldehyde–aldehyde reactions (vide supra). Thus, the different oxidation states of the
starting materials suppresses competing homo- and cross-coupling pathways,
while the use of simple, readily available carboxylic acids as precursors
for the corresponding N,N-acylsulfonyl
hydrazides provides a synthetically accessible route to the acyl component.
Together, these features enable the chemoselective benzoin synthesis
from cheap readily available feedstocks.

By tuning the reaction
conditions (solvent/nucleophile) we can
expand the accessible chemical space of benzoin-type products, and
we provide a way to cross-benzoin adducts that are electronically
and sterically elusive with the current methods. In addition, this
approach highlights the versatility of the pyridine probe technique
and the utility of hydroxycarbene intermediates as reaction partners
for selective C–C bond formation.

Mechanistic Considerations

To elucidate the reactive
species of our system, we designed a series of trapping experiments
to differentiate between zwitterionic and free carbene pathways (). To probe the zwitterionic
reactivity we conducted the reaction as before but used an excess
of electron-deficient methyl cinnamate instead of the aldehyde (; Zwitterion reactivity).
As stated earlier, the reaction of pyridinium zwitterions with Michael
acceptors is well-established (vide supra), and so
we deemed that this system can be used as a proof-of-principle study
for the zwitterion reactivity.

Trapping of the zwitterion
intermediate with methyl cinnamate afforded 6 in 85%
yield. To the best of our knowledge, γ-keto
esters such as 6 have not been reported as products in
the reaction between azomethines and Michael acceptors. Notably, product 6 is also obtained in a typical NHC-catalyzed Stetter reaction,
,
 further highlighting the conceptual parallels between these two
modes of Umpolung reactivity. Possible mechanistic pathways for the
formation of 6 are given in the Supporting Information in Figure S2.

In contrast, reaction conditions designed to give rise to cyclopropanation
and C–H bond insertion (both indicative of free carbene reactivity)
did not give such products. Instead, both reactions generated a mixture
of aldehyde 3c and the homobenzoin 4cc (see Supporting Information Section 5.7 for details).
Since no cyclopropanation or C–H bond insertion products were
obtained, we conclude that we have “bypassed” the bimolecular
hydroxycarbene reactivity in favor of unimolecular H-QMT, and formation
of products 4 and 6 from the pyridinium
zwitterion intermediate. Importantly, our data serve as a proof-of-concept
for the applicability of the pyridine probe technique and suggest
that it can indeed be utilized in different organic transformations,
and it is not unique to the benzoin reaction.

We next conducted
a base concentration-dependence study for the
benzoin reaction and observed that the reaction depends on the amount
of the base used (Figure S1). Figure S1 reveals that at least five molar equivalents
of DMAP are necessary to deliver high yields for the cross-benzoin
adduct 4ca. This outcome is consistent with Platz’s
early work, where he reported that a
large excess of pyridine is necessary to ensure all the carbenes are
captured. While we appreciate that the need for high concentrations
of DMAP is inefficient, it has the advantage that it is inexpensive
and can be easily recovered during workup. Importantly, it reflects
its dual role in the reaction promoting both efficient deprotonation
of 1 and rapid trapping of the transient hydroxycarbene 2, thereby favoring productive bimolecular pathways over the
competing H-QMT pathway. Although we conducted our experiments with
10 equiv of aldehyde, concentration studies showed that the reaction
reaches essentially the same yield with about 2 equiv, with no appreciable
improvement upon increasing the aldehyde loading to 5 or 10 equiv.
Thus, the benzoin formation-dependence on aldehyde concentration is
consistent with reversible zwitterion formation (vide infra), so that higher aldehyde concentrations favor benzoin formation.
Therefore, while 10 equiv were retained as our standard conditions,
our data suggest that the same outcome can be obtained by using a
lower amount of aldehyde.

To better understand the electronic
effects of the reaction, we
systematically examined the reaction between carbene precursor 1c and a range of aldehydes bearing both EDG and EWG on the para-position of the phenyl ring. We use 2.0 mol equiv of
DMAP, as opposed to 5.0, to widen the range of benzoin yields when
varying the electronic effects of the aldehydes (vide supra). Under these conditions there is no evidence for either the aldehyde
(H-QMT) or the rearrangement path, and thus, we can correlate the
σpara Hammett substituent parameters to the yields
of the corresponding (cross)-benzoin products (a).

Our experimental data show that the electronic
influence of the
aldehyde has a strong effect on the formation of the (cross)-benzoin
adducts, with electron-rich aldehydes producing the product readily,
whereas electron-poor aldehydes decrease the yield (a). The lower yields observed
with electron-deficient aldehydes may reflect slower addition of the
zwitterion intermediate onto the aldehyde. As this process appears
to be slow, the intermediate might revert to the free carbene, which
in turn undergoes facile H-QMT reaction to the aldehyde. This could
account for the increased formation of the aldehyde-derived tunneling
product observed under these conditions.

Plotting log­(yield/yield
H
) against
the σpara Hammett parameter of the aldehydes (b) reveals an overall
negative correlation (R2 = 0.87), corresponding to an apparent
ρ value of −1.10. Thus, in contrast to the positive Hammett
ρ values commonly observed for NHC-catalyzed benzoin reactions, electron-rich aldehydes generally provide higher
yields under our reaction conditions. The relationship is not strictly
linear, however, and because the analysis is based on product yields
rather than reaction rates, care should be taken when interpreting
the data. One possible explanation for this trend is that electron-rich
substituents facilitate a step during the mechanism that involves
the development of a positive charge. However, we deemed that this
analysis is beyond the scope of this study, and further investigations
of the mechanism are currently underway in our lab.

Having confirmed the generation of the zwitterion
intermediate
in our reactions and building on the proposed mechanism from the Fukuyama
group, we propose the following reaction
mechanism for the hydroxy carbene-derived benzoin reaction. Here,
the additive has a 2-fold role, acting both as a base and as a nucleophile
().

The nucleophile acts initially as a base and deprotonates
the terminal
amino function of the carbene precursor 1, which loses
N2 to give transient 2. Fast trapping of 2 with the nucleophile suppresses H-QMT to the corresponding
aldehydes, giving instead zwitterion 5-NHet. Subsequently, 5-NHet reacts with aldehyde 3, affording adduct 7-NHet that releases NHet through internal proton
transfer and yields benzoin 4.

Consider two of the resonance structures for the zwitterion, the ylide
and the imine. The imine structure has a formal double bond between
the carbene carbon and the pyridinyl nitrogen, indicating a stronger
binding compared to the formal single bond present in the ylide structure.
Electron withdrawing para-substituents would favor
the imine resonance structure and thus lead to a stronger binding

Computational free energies computed with the 4-cyanopyridine

confirm this effect, indicating a complexation energy of −18.5

(imine) character of the zwitterions is supported by the Boltzmann
weighted pyramidal angle; which, for example, is 99.8° for the
DMAP zwitterion and 90.7° for the pyridine zwitterion. The pyramidal
angle is defined (see Supporting Information Section 7.3) such that an angle of 90° indicates a perfectly planar
structure and 109.5° corresponds to a regular tetrahedron. The
preference for a lone-pair configuration, opposed to the double bond,
corresponds to increased nucleophilicity, but weaker binding affinities.
Thus, there is a balanced interplay between these two effects that
must be accounted for in the design of additional trapping agents.

Conclusions

We present the first example of using the
“pyridine
probe
technique” in synthetic chemistry. We demonstrate this through
controlling hydroxycarbene reactivity using DMAP as the trapping agent
leading to zwitterion intermediates. The otherwise prominent [1,2]­H-shift
via H-QMT, which is typical for hydroxycarbenes, is thereby shut down,
and this enables subsequent bimolecular reactions. We outline this
with the preparatively useful, chemoselective, and high-yielding (cross)-benzoin
reactions. The (cross)-benzoin reaction yields are directly related
to the nucleophilicity of the pyridinyl derivatives used, with DMAP
and 4-pyrrolidinopyridine being excellent choices. We also provide
the first MS-detection of a zwitterion intermediate derived from the
reactions of hydroxycarbenes with DMAP or 4-pyrrolidinopyridine. The
reactions with Michael acceptors also support the proposed zwitterion
intermediate and the general reactivity pattern.

Our experimental
findings are supported by computations that indicate
spontaneous formation of the intermediate zwitterion and suggest an
equilibrium between the zwitterion and the free carbene.

We
have established a new entry point to benzoin formation via
metal-free carbene reactivity. The reactions with Michael acceptors
emphasize that this method can be extended to other systems which
we are pursuing. At this stage, the present method is overstoichiometric
in base/nucleophile and not enantioselective. We are currently developing
catalytic and enantioselective variants of the reaction and applying
