Association between total body water and the risk of gastrointestinal symptoms: evidence from NHANES and Mendelian randomization analyses
Authors: Ya-bei Li, Zhong-wen Zheng, Man Zuo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTotal body water (TBW) is an important indicator of fluid homeostasis and is closely related to various metabolic and chronic diseases. However, the relationship between TBW and gastrointestinal symptoms (stomach or intestinal illness, SII) remains unclear. Most previous studies were observational and lacked causal inference. This study, based on data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES, 2001–2018) and combined with two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR), explored the nonlinear relationship and potential causal association between TBW and gastrointestinal symptoms from both epidemiological and genetic perspectives.MethodsUsing cross-sectional data from the NHANES (2001–2018), logistic regression, restricted cubic spline (RCS), and subgroup analyses were performed to explore the association between TBW and gastrointestinal symptoms. A two-sample MR study was subsequently conducted using genome-wide association study (GWAS) summary statistics to determine the causal relationship between body water mass (BWM) and gastrointestinal or abdominal diseases.ResultsFrom the NHANES, 36,043 participants aged ≥ 20 years were included. Baseline analysis revealed a significant difference in TBW between participants with and without gastrointestinal symptoms. Logistic regression confirmed that the association between TBW and gastrointestinal symptoms was robust across models. RCS analysis suggested a potential nonlinear pattern, although formal testing did not support significant nonlinearity (P = 0.551). Within the range of 24.9–38.5 L, TBW was associated with lower odds of gastrointestinal symptoms (odds ratio (OR) < 1), whereas values above 38.5 L were associated with higher odds (OR > 1). In subgroup analysis, the high-TBW group (> 38.5 L) was associated with increased risk, whereas the low-TBW group (< 38.5 L) showed a protective association.ConclusionThere is a potential nonlinear relationship between TBW and the risk of gastrointestinal symptoms. However, as TBW is strongly correlated with body size and adiposity, the associations may be largely driven by obesity-related factors rather than by water balance. MR analysis provided suggestive evidence supporting a causal association between TBW and gastrointestinal or abdominal diseases. Maintaining an appropriate water balance may help reduce gastrointestinal symptoms, but these findings should be interpreted with caution, and further studies incorporating comprehensive body composition measures are needed.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12876-026-05023-w.Keywords: Total body water, Gastrointestinal symptoms, NHANES, Mendelian randomization

Introduction

Gastrointestinal disorders are among the most common conditions worldwide and cause substantial public health and socioeconomic burdens [1]. Nonmalignant upper gastrointestinal diseases, including peptic ulcer disease (PUD), gastritis, duodenitis, and gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD), remain widespread despite advances in diagnosis and treatment strategies. From 1990 to 2019, the number of global disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) decreased overall, but significant regional differences still exist, especially in countries with low social population indices, where population ageing and limited healthcare opportunities continue to increase the disease burden [2]. Functional gastrointestinal diseases (such as irritable bowel syndrome and functional dyspepsia) affect up to 40% of the population globally and often progress to chronic diseases [3]. These disorders have complex pathophysiological features, including dysregulation of the gut–brain axis, mucosal immune changes, microbial community imbalance, and visceral allergic reactions, leading to a significant decline in quality of life and substantial consumption of medical resources [1]. Therefore, identifying modifiable biological factors and systemic mechanisms that may affect the risk and progression of gastrointestinal diseases is urgent.

Total body water (TBW) is a crucial physiological parameter that plays a central role in maintaining cellular function, metabolism, and overall homeostasis [4, 5]. Water imbalance can lead to dehydration or fluid overload, both of which may adversely affect various organs, including the gastrointestinal (GI) system. Adequate hydration is essential for normal gastric peristalsis, nutrient absorption, and digestive enzyme function [6, 7]. Therefore, the state of body water may affect the formation and development of gastrointestinal diseases, including irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) and gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD), which are related to changes in fluid balance [8]. Recent studies have shown that personalized hydration strategies can help control symptoms exacerbated by dehydration, thereby improving fluid balance and overall gastrointestinal health [9]. However, importantly, TBW is strongly correlated with body size, lean mass, and adiposity; thus, associations between TBW and gastrointestinal outcomes may reflect underlying differences in body composition rather than in hydration status alone.

Furthermore, most existing studies on water intake or body water status and gastrointestinal outcomes are observational, which limits the inference of causal relationships because residual confounding factors and reverse causal relationships may be present. Mendelian randomization (MR) is an analytical method that uses genetic variations as instrumental variables to infer causal relationships between exposure factors and outcomes. It provides a powerful approach to overcome these limitations [10, 11]. By combining epidemiological data with genetic evidence, MR can strengthen causal inferences and elucidate the biological rationality of observed associations [12].

Therefore, in this study, we aimed to comprehensively explore the association between TBW and the risk of gastrointestinal symptoms from both epidemiological and genetic perspectives. We used data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES, 2001–2018) and applied logistic regression and restricted cubic spline (RCS) models to examine the relationship between TBW and gastrointestinal symptoms. Based on genome-wide association study (GWAS) summary statistics, we subsequently conducted a two-sample MR analysis to assess the potential causal relationship between body water mass and gastrointestinal or abdominal disease. We explicitly acknowledge that TBW is strongly correlated with body size and adiposity; accordingly, our findings should be interpreted in the context of overall body composition rather than as evidence for a specific effect of water balance.

Materials and methods

Study population

The NHANES database [13] is a national cross-sectional survey representing the noninstitutionalized civilian population of the United States. Data were collected from household interviews and standardized mobile examination centre (MEC) examinations conducted on a two-year cycle. The research protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board for Health Statistics Research. All participants provided written informed consent before data collection. Detailed information on the NHANES protocol is available on the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) website. From 2001–2018, we included respondents aged ≥ 20 years from nine survey cycles to assess the association between gastrointestinal symptoms and TBW (see Fig. 1A) for the flowchart).

Gastrointestinal diseases

Gastrointestinal symptoms were assessed based on the question “Did you have a stomach or intestinal illness?” in the HSQ510 questionnaire. Responses of “Yes” were classified as positive, “No” as negative, and “Refused”, “Do not know”, and missing responses were excluded. We explicitly note that this outcome reflects self-reported gastrointestinal symptoms rather than clinically diagnosed gastrointestinal diseases based on ICD-9/10 codes. Therefore, throughout this manuscript, we refer to this outcome as “gastrointestinal symptoms” or “stomach or intestinal illness (SII)” rather than “gastrointestinal diseases”.

TBW indicator

The TBW indicator used in our study was calculated using validated formulae derived from bioelectrical impedance analysis [14] as follows:

Male: 0.1074*height (cm)-0.09516*age (years)

Male: 0.1074*height (cm)-0.09516*age (years)

Female: +0.1069*height (cm)

Female: +0.1069*height (cm)

These formulae estimate total body water in litres. We acknowledge that TBW is strongly correlated with body weight, height, and body mass index (BMI); thus, the associations observed for TBW may partly reflect general body size effects rather than water balance specifically.

Other covariates

Age was analysed as a continuous variable in years. Race/ethnicity was categorized into five categories: Mexican Americans, other Hispanics, Whites, Blacks, and other races. Household income level was determined using the INDFMPIR index, with a cut-off of < 1 indicating poverty status and ≥ 1 indicating nonpoverty status.

BMI was included as a continuous variable. Educational attainment was stratified into three categories: less than high school, high school graduate or equivalent, and college and above.

Smoking status was determined based on serum cotinine levels; participants with a cotinine concentration > 14 ng/mL were regarded as having a long-term smoking history. Alcohol drinking status was defined as self-reported consumption of at least 12 alcoholic drinks per year, with affirmative responses classified as regular drinkers.

Hypertension was defined in accordance with the 2017 American Heart Association (AHA) guidelines, combined with self-reported physician-diagnosed hypertension and the current use of antihypertensive medications. Diabetes and prediabetes were diagnosed based on the American Diabetes Association (ADA) criteria, defined by any of the following: a glycated haemoglobin concentration > 5.7% (prediabetes) or > 6.5% (diabetes), a fasting plasma glucose concentration > 100 mg/dL (prediabetes) or > 126 mg/dL (diabetes), self-reported physician-diagnosed diabetes, or impaired glucose tolerance [3]. Pulse rhythm was classified as regular or irregular.

The final covariate set included age, sex, race/ethnicity, income status, BMI, educational attainment, smoking status (serum cotinine concentration > 14 ng/mL), alcohol consumption (≥ 12 drinks per year), hypertension (defined by the 2017 AHA criteria or antihypertensive medication use), diabetes (per ADA criteria), and pulse regularity. We noted that several covariates (e.g., BMI derived from measured height and weight) were strongly correlated with total body water (TBW) and may serve as potential confounders or intermediate variables. Given the possibility of overadjustment bias, we constructed statistical models with and without adjustment for BMI for comparative analysis.

Restricted cubic spline analysis

We used RCS analysis to evaluate the shape of the association between TBW and gastrointestinal symptoms. Regression splines are piecewise polynomial functions that require continuity and second-order differentiability at each knot to ensure smoothness. Restricted cubic splines additionally constrain the spline function to be linear in the two tail intervals. We used the rms package in R. Formal testing for nonlinearity was performed using a likelihood ratio test to compare the nonlinear spline model to a linear model. We report both P-overall (testing any association) and P-nonlinear (testing deviation from linearity). Given the absence of strong statistical evidence for nonlinearity (P-nonlinear = 0.551), we interpret the observed patterns cautiously as exploratory.

Subgroup analysis

Subgroup analysis was conducted to explore potential effect heterogeneity. Subgroups were defined by age (< 45, 45–65, > 65 years), sex, BMI (< 25, 25–30, ≥ 30 kg/m2), income (poverty vs. nonpoverty), smoking status, alcohol use status, hypertension status, and diabetes status. Based on the RCS analysis, TBW was dichotomized at 38.537 L (the approximate inflection point) into low (≤ 38.537 L) and high (> 38.537 L) groups. For each subgroup, we estimated the association between TBW (continuous and dichotomized) and gastrointestinal symptoms using multivariable logistic regression. To formally test for heterogeneity across subgroups, we included multiplicative interaction terms between TBW and each subgroup variable in the regression models and reported the interaction P values. We caution against overinterpreting within-subgroup estimates without significant interaction terms.

MR

MR analysis was conducted following the STROBE-MR guidelines (Supplementary Table S9) [15]. This study employed two-sample MR to investigate the potential causal relationship between body water mass (BWM) and gastrointestinal or abdominal disease. MR leverages genetic variants as instrumental variables, exploiting the random allocation of alleles at conception to minimize confounding and reverse causation [16]. The research design is illustrated in Fig. 1B–C.

Data Sources. All GWAS summary statistics were obtained from the OpenGWAS database [17]. For the exposure (BWM), two independent European-ancestry datasets were included: ukb-a-267 and ukb-b-14540. For the outcomes, summary-level data for gastrointestinal or abdominal disease were obtained from ebi-a-GCST90038597 (77,978 cases and 406,620 controls of European ancestry). SNPs were selected at genome-wide significance (P < 5 × 10−⁸) and clumped for linkage disequilibrium (r2 < 0.001, physical distance > 10,000 kb). All datasets are publicly available without additional ethical approval. Detailed dataset characteristics are provided in Supplementary Table S7.

MR Analysis. The primary analysis used the inverse variance weighted (IVW) method, which provides the most efficient estimate under valid instrument assumptions [16]. Four additional methods, MR‒Egger, weighted median, simple mode, and weighted mode, were applied as complementary analyses robust to different patterns of horizontal pleiotropy [18]. Instrument strength was assessed using F statistics, with F > 10 indicating sufficient strength to minimize weak instrument bias [19]. Sensitivity analyses included Cochran’s Q test for heterogeneity, the MR‒Egger intercept test for directional pleiotropy, and leave-one-out analysis [20]. The Steiger directionality test was performed to confirm causal direction, and reverse MR analysis (gastrointestinal disease → BWM) was also conducted. All MR analyses were performed using the TwoSampleMR package in R [17].

Statistical analysis

All the statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.2.2 (https://www.r-project.org/). Continuous variables are presented as medians [interquartile ranges] and were compared using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and were compared using the chi-square test. Survey weights (WTMEC2YR) were applied according to the NHANES guidelines, incorporating strata and primary sampling units for variance estimation. Missing data were handled by complete-case analysis; to evaluate the robustness of the results, we performed a sensitivity analysis by reconducting the analysis after excluding participants with a medical history of heart failure and kidney disease. After exclusion, the direction of the association remained consistent with that of the primary analysis (Supplementary Table S1).

The overall design and analytical framework of this study are illustrated in Fig. 1B–C. This study comprises two complementary analytical components: an observational analysis based on NHANES (2001–2018) cross-sectional data and a two-sample MR analysis based on GWAS summary statistics.

Impact of TBW on gastrointestinal symptoms

Multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed to examine the association between TBW and gastrointestinal symptoms. Three hierarchical regression models were established to verify the robustness of the findings. Model 1 included only TBW as the primary exposure variable. Model 2 was further adjusted for seven covariates: age, sex, household income, BMI, smoking status, hypertension status, and diabetes status. Based on Model 2, Model 3 additionally incorporated race/ethnicity, educational attainment, alcohol consumption, and pulse regularity for full multivariable adjustment.

All regression outcomes are summarized in Table 2. The results revealed that TBW remained an independent influencing factor for gastrointestinal symptoms across Model 1, Model 2, and Model 3. The consistent associations observed across the three adjusted models further confirmed the robustness and stability of the relationship between TBW and gastrointestinal symptom risk. Diuretic use and proton pump inhibitor (PPI) use were included as covariates in the sensitivity analysis. Allometric scaling adjustment was performed by dividing TBW by body weight and multiplying by 0.75 to better distinguish the “body fluid effect” from the “body size effect”. The detailed results are presented in Supplementary Table S2. The sensitivity analysis results support the robustness of the main findings. After participants on relevant medications were excluded, the direction and magnitude of the association between TBW and SII remained consistent with those in the primary analysis; after adjusting for medication covariates, the effect estimate of TBW changed by less than 0.1%, indicating minimal influence. However, the effect direction changed when the standardized TBW (TBW/weight) was used. The discrepancy between the standardized TBW results and the primary analysis may be attributed to a different underlying mechanism linking the TBW/weight ratio to SII compared with the original TBW measure, which warrants further investigation.

RCS analysis

The logistic regression results revealed that the association between TBW and gastrointestinal symptoms remained robust across all the adjusted models. RCS regression was further performed to explore the dose–response shape of this relationship (Fig. 2). The overall association was statistically significant (P-overall < 0.05), whereas no significant nonlinear trend was detected (P-nonlinear = 0.551).

Restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis of the association between total body water (TBW) and gastrointestinal symptoms. Restricted cubic spline (RCS) curves depicting the nonlinear association between total body water (TBW) and the risk of gastrointestinal symptoms. The solid line represents the estimated odds ratio (OR), and the shaded area indicates the 95% confidence interval. Abbreviations: OR, odds ratio; TBW, total body water

In the lower TBW range (< 24.9 L), the OR for gastrointestinal symptoms gradually approached 1 with increasing TBW levels, suggesting a trend towards a null association. Within the intermediate TBW range (24.9–38.5 L), the OR curve initially decreased but then gradually increased, indicating that the risk of gastrointestinal symptoms first decreased but then increased as the TBW increased. Notably, all OR values remained below 1 across this interval, suggesting that TBW acted as a protective factor against gastrointestinal symptoms in this range.

In the higher TBW range (> 38.5 L), elevated TBW was accompanied by a progressive increase in the OR value, suggesting a greater likelihood of gastrointestinal symptom onset; correspondingly, TBW served as a risk factor in this concentration range.

Subgroup analysis

The results of the subgroup analysis are shown in Fig. 3; however, these results were not corrected for multiple comparisons. Among the income groups, the poverty subgroup had an OR > 1 with P < 0.05 for the high-TBW group. In the BMI subgroups, the association appeared more pronounced in normal-weight individuals, although the interaction was not robust to multiple testing correction. When TBW was dichotomized at 38.537 L, the low-TBW group (≤ 38.537 L) had an OR < 1 (suggestive of lower odds), and the high-TBW group (High) (> 38.537 L) had an OR > 1 (suggestive of higher odds). These subgroup findings should be considered hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory.

We performed an interaction test using age groups (< 45, 45–65, and > 65 years). The interaction was significant (P = 0.015), indicating that the association between TBW and SII differed across age groups. In the < 45-year-old group, TBW was significantly and positively associated with SII (OR = 1.012, P = 0.049); in the 45–65-year-old group, TBW was significantly and positively associated with SII (OR = 1.022, P = 0.002), whereas in the > 65-year-old group, no significant association was observed between TBW and SII (OR = 0.992, P = 0.505). The positive association of TBW was significant in young and middle-aged populations but attenuated in the elderly population (Supplementary Table S3).

The subgroup-specific estimates shown in Fig. 3 are derived from the overall subgroup analysis without interaction terms, whereas the age-stratified results reported here and in Supplementary Table S3 are from models incorporating the AgeGroup × TBW interaction term.

Selection of instrumental variables

The number of instrumental variables (SNPs) retained for BWM was 406 for ukb-a-267 and 545 for ukb-b-14540. The proportions of variance in exposure explained (R2) were 0.043 for ukb-a-267 and 0.038 for ukb-b-14540. The F-statistics exceeded 10 for all the instruments (median F = 48.31 for ukb-a-267 and 52.89 for ukb-b-14540), indicating that weak instrument bias is unlikely (Table 3) [18].

MR technical Roadmap

The MR analyses using five different methods (IVW, MR‒Egger, weighted median, simple mode, and weighted mode) are summarized in Table 4 and Fig. 4. Our primary IVW analysis revealed that higher genetically predicted BWM was associated with lower odds of gastrointestinal or abdominal disease (ukb-a-267: OR = 0.978, 95% CI = 0.971–0.986, P < 0.001; ukb-b-14540: OR = 0.973, 95% CI = 0.967–0.980, P < 0.001). Scatter plots (Fig. 5A–B) showed consistent slopes across MR methods. Sensitivity analyses (Cochran’s Q, MR‒Egger intercept, leave-one-out) indicated moderate heterogeneity (I2 = 43.96% for ukb-a-267 and 36.86% for ukb-b-14540; Table 5) but no significant directional pleiotropy (MR‒Egger intercept P = 0.771 and 0.104; Table 6, Fig. 6A–B). Reverse MR analysis (with gastrointestinal disease as the exposure and BWM as the outcome) revealed no significant causal effect (IVW: ukb-a-267, P = 0.104; ukb-b-14540, P = 0.156; Table 7, Fig. 7). For a sensitivity analysis, we also used a GWAS dataset whose outcome definition more closely matched the NHANES symptom-based definition (gastroesophageal reflux disease, ebi-a-GCST90000514). This analysis confirmed a significant causal association between BWM and gastroesophageal reflux disease (Supplementary Tables S4 and S5).

Discussion

Main findings

The present study utilized nationally representative NHANES data spanning 2001–2018, combined with two-sample MR analyses, to evaluate the association between TBW and gastrointestinal symptoms. Although restricted cubic spline regression indicated a potential U-shaped association between TBW and gastrointestinal outcomes, the formal nonlinearity test did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.551). Within the moderate TBW range (approximately 24.9–38.5 L), higher TBW levels were correlated with lower odds of gastrointestinal symptoms, whereas relatively higher or lower TBW values corresponded to an increased symptom burden. Notably, given the tight collinearity between TBW and anthropometric indicators, including body size and adiposity, it remained difficult to isolate the independent effect of water balance from general body composition. MR analysis further suggested a potential causal relationship between body water mass and gastrointestinal or abdominal disease, albeit with a relatively modest effect size (OR ≈ 0.97 per unit increase).

Physiological mechanism

If the observed associations persist after accounting for body composition, several plausible biological pathways may explain the relationship between TBW and gastrointestinal health. Water homeostasis fundamentally regulates digestive juice secretion, nutrient absorption, intestinal peristalsis, and the integrity of the intestinal mucosal barrier [21, 22]. Insufficient TBW, which reflects chronic dehydration, may decelerate intestinal transit, exacerbate constipation, and impair mucosal perfusion, thereby compromising intestinal barrier function [23, 24]. Conversely, excessive TBW, commonly characterized as fluid retention, frequently occurs in patients with heart failure, renal insufficiency, or systemic inflammation. Excess accumulated fluid can induce intestinal wall oedema, disrupt gut microbiota homeostasis, and increase intestinal permeability [25]. Nevertheless, such mechanistic interpretations remain speculative. Considering the strong correlation between TBW and adiposity, a more parsimonious explanation is that obesity, a well-documented risk factor for multiple gastrointestinal symptoms, may primarily drive the observed associations.

Comparison with previous studies

Prior research has focused predominantly on the association between daily water intake and gastrointestinal health, while population-based evidence concerning TBW remains limited. A previous epidemiological study conducted in China reported that long-term tap water consumption was associated with a reduced prevalence of chronic gastrointestinal illnesses [23]. Similarly, a Swedish prospective cohort demonstrated a nonlinear relationship between water intake and gastrointestinal disease risk, with distinct patterns across different water sources [26]. Importantly, TBW is not equivalent to measurable drinking water intake; TBW comprehensively reflects systemic water balance and is profoundly modulated by individual body composition. Consistent with the recognized U-shaped relationship between BMI and multiple metabolic outcomes, the current findings imply that the TBW–gastrointestinal symptom association may be largely mediated by adiposity rather than by hydration status per se [27, 28].

Public health and clinical significance

From a public health perspective, if future well-designed studies validate its independent clinical value, TBW could serve as an accessible and cost-effective biomarker for gastrointestinal health assessment. Maintaining optimal water homeostasis may be a low-cost preventive strategy, particularly for vulnerable populations such as elderly individuals and individuals with chronic metabolic disorders. Nevertheless, considering the inherent collinearity between TBW and body mass indicators, interventions merely targeting fluid balance may fail to reduce gastrointestinal disease burden without concomitant weight management. In clinical practice, TBW monitoring should be incorporated into comprehensive nutritional and metabolic evaluations rather than interpreted as an isolated indicator.

Advantages and limitations

This study has several methodological strengths. The large, nationally representative sample from continuous NHANES cycles enhanced population generalizability. The application of two-sample MR minimized residual confounding and mitigated reverse causality bias. Additionally, RCS regression allowed flexible visualization of the dose‒response relationship between TBW and gastrointestinal symptoms.

Despite these strengths, multiple methodological limitations must be explicitly acknowledged to facilitate rational interpretation of the results.

Outcome ascertainment

Gastrointestinal outcomes in the NHANES were determined based on a single self-reported questionnaire item regarding stomach or intestinal illness rather than standardized ICD-9/10 diagnostic coding. Such self-reported outcomes reflect nonspecific gastrointestinal symptoms instead of clinically confirmed organic diseases. Throughout the manuscript, the terminology has been uniformly revised to strictly conform to this limitation.

Phenotype heterogeneity across datasets

The NHANES assessed self-reported gastrointestinal symptoms, whereas the MR analysis adopted genetically predicted gastrointestinal and abdominal diseases derived from GWAS datasets. The phenotypic inconsistency between the two datasets necessitates cautious interpretation of the triangulated evidence.

Statistical interpretation of nonlinear trends

Although the spline curve visually indicated threshold characteristics, the formal nonlinearity test remained nonsignificant (P = 0.551). The discussion has been revised to avoid overinterpretation of nonsignificant nonlinear patterns and strictly comply with the statistical evidence.

Subgroup analysis

Formal interaction tests and multiple comparison corrections were supplemented in the updated analyses. Most subgroup interactions were statistically nonsignificant; therefore, the subgroup findings should be regarded as exploratory rather than definitive conclusions.

Causal framework and overadjustment bias

TBW was strongly correlated with BMI, body weight, height, and other body composition parameters. Since BMI may act as a mediator rather than a pure confounder, conventional BMI adjustment could introduce overadjustment bias. Furthermore, the NHANES lacks detailed information on fluid-modulating medications (e.g., diuretics and renin–angiotensin system inhibitors) and gastrointestinal symptom-related drugs (e.g., PPIs and NSAIDs), which may constitute unmeasured confounding factors.

Survey design and missing data

All statistical analyses accounted for complex NHANES sampling designs, including strata and primary sampling units across nine survey cycles. A complete-case analytical approach was adopted for participants with missing data, and multiple imputation sensitivity analyses generated consistent outcomes, confirming statistical robustness.

TBW estimation and terminology standardization

TBW values were calculated using validated empirical formulae instead of direct bioelectrical impedance measurements. Terminological inconsistencies between TBW and body water mass (BWM) were eliminated; TBW was uniformly applied to NHANES observational analyses, while BWM was specifically used for MR analyses, with all volume metrics standardized in litres.

MR analytical limitations

The F-statistics of the instrumental variables exceeded the conventional threshold, whereas the explained variance remained relatively low (R2 ≈ 4%). The relaxed threshold for reverse MR analysis (< 5 × 10−⁶) may increase the potential risk of weak instrument bias.

Confounding effect of obesity

This constitutes the most prominent limitation of the present study. The calculated TBW was strongly correlated with body weight and lean mass, indicating that the observed association may essentially reflect the well-established obesity–gastrointestinal disease correlation. Current evidence cannot sufficiently differentiate the independent physiological effect of water balance from body composition. Future studies incorporating comprehensive bioimpedance parameters (e.g., fat mass, fat-free mass, body fat percentage, and standardized TBW proportion) are needed to disentangle these intertwined effects.

Residual confounding

Despite rigorous covariate adjustment, unmeasured confounders, including dietary patterns, physical activity levels, and detailed medication records, could not be completely excluded.

Conclusion and future directions

In summary, the current observational analysis demonstrated a significant association between TBW and gastrointestinal symptoms among the general U.S. adult population. Two-sample MR provided suggestive genetic evidence supporting a potential causal relationship between systemic body water mass and abdominal or gastrointestinal diseases. Nevertheless, the pronounced collinearity between TBW and obesity-related anthropometric indicators precludes the definitive attribution of this association to isolated water balance variation. The observed statistical correlation is plausibly mediated by body size and adiposity, both of which are recognized risk factors for gastrointestinal disorders.

Future research should focus on the following directions: (1) incorporate comprehensive bioimpedance indicators, including fat mass, fat-free mass, TBW percentage, and segmental water distribution, to independently distinguish hydration effects from body composition; (2) adopt clinically validated ICD-coded gastrointestinal diagnoses instead of self-reported symptomatic outcomes; (3) conduct longitudinal cohorts with direct fluid intake monitoring and objective hydration biomarkers; (4) implement controlled hydration intervention trials among populations with documented fluid homeostasis abnormalities; and (5) explore underlying mechanistic pathways involving the gut microbiota, intestinal permeability, and systemic inflammation using experimental models.

Based on existing evidence, public health recommendations should continue to emphasize healthy body weight maintenance and reasonable hydration habits. However, the independent clinical implication of water balance for gastrointestinal health remains inconclusive and requires further rigorous validation.

Data availability

The data used in this study are publicly available and can be accessed through the NHANES website (https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/about-data/index.html) conducted by the U.S CDC. The NHANES variable codes used in this analysis are summarized in Supplementary Table S6, including HSQ510 (stomach or intestinal illness), RIDAGEYR (age), RIAGENDR (gender), BMXWT (weight), and other relevant variables. The genome-wide association study (GWAS) summary statistics used for the MR analysis are available from the open GWAS project (https://opengwas.io/). The following four datasets were used in the Mendelian Randomization analysis: Body water mass: GWAS IDs ukb-a-267 (https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk/datasets/ukb-a-267/) and ukb-b-14540 (https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk/datasets/ukb-b-14540/), both of European ancestry. After quality control and linkage disequilibrium clumping (r² < 0.001), 406 SNPs (ukb-a-267) and 545 SNPs (ukb-b-14540) were retained as instrumental variables, with F-statistics exceeding 10 for all instruments (median F = 48.31 for ukb-a-267, 52.89 for ukb-b-14540). Gastrointestinal or abdominal disease (outcome):GWAS ID ebi-a-GCST90038597 (https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk/datasets/ebi-a-GCST90038597/), comprising 77,978 cases and 406,620 controls of European ancestry. Gastroesophageal reflux disease (sensitivity outcome): GWAS ID ebi-a-GCST90000514 (https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk/datasets/ebi-a-GCST90000514/), used for a sensitivity analysis to align the outcome definition more closely with the NHANES symptom-based assessment. These datasets are publicly accessible and can be downloaded following the respective data access policies. Further information on the data processing methods and analyses used in this study can be obtained from the corresponding author upon request. Given the extensive nature of the details, the complete lists of genetic instrument exposure IDs and the full results of the leave-one-out sensitivity analyses are available as supplementary material accompanying this manuscript (Supplementary Tables S7 and S8). These supplementary tables are an integral part of the study and facilitate a comprehensive assessment of the analyses. Additional methodological details can be obtained from the corresponding author upon request.
