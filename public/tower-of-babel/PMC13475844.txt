Prolonged road transport of a critically ill postcardiac neonate: A systems failure rather than an unavoidable outcome
Authors: Ranganathan Ananthasubramanian, Rajesh Madavathazathil Gopalakrishnan
Published: 2026

Sir,

Interfacility transport of critically ill neonates should be regarded as an extension of intensive care rather than a mere change in location.[1] We describe our experience of prolonged road transport (22 hours) of a postoperative cardiac neonate under extreme environmental and logistical constraints, highlighting systemic vulnerabilities that continue to place such patients at significant risk.

The patient was a 6-day-old neonate who had undergone surgical repair for Tetralogy of Fallot with absent pulmonary valve, including unifocalization. The postoperative course was prolonged and complicated, requiring extended mechanical ventilation, delayed sternal closure, treatment for sepsis, and eventual tracheostomy. After stabilization, the family requested transfer from a tertiary cardiac center to their home state in a mountainous region because of severe financial constraints. As air transport was unaffordable, a long-distance road transfer was undertaken.

Despite pretransport preparation, major challenges emerged soon after departure. Ambient temperatures progressively dropped as night fell, and effective thermal regulation could not be maintained. The infant became profoundly hypothermic, with core temperature reaching a lowest of 33°C and periphery markedly cooler. Monitoring became unreliable, peripheral perfusion deteriorated, and ventilatory support became increasingly difficult to sustain. We transitioned to pressure control ventilation with added HME filters on the transport ventilator. As the child became sick during transport, adrenaline and noradrenaline infusions were commenced as inotropes. Prolonged travel time due to traffic congestion, poor visibility, difficult terrain, and repeated unscheduled halts further compounded physiological stress. On arrival at the destination hospital, delays in admission and lack of immediate preparedness for a critically ill postoperative cardiac neonate resulted in further deterioration. Despite escalation of care, the infant remained profoundly hypothermic, acidotic, and hemodynamically unstable.

This experience reinforces several critical observations. Hypothermia during neonatal transport is not a benign complication; it acts as a major driver of respiratory failure, metabolic derangement, and cardiovascular instability. Long-distance road transport of ventilated neonates in extreme environmental conditions, without dedicated neonatal transport systems, exposes patients to cumulative physiological insults that may outweigh the intended benefits of transfer. Importantly, many of these risks arise from system limitations rather than disease severity alone.

Based on this experience, several pragmatic considerations merit emphasis before undertaking prolonged neonatal road transport, particularly when air transfer is not feasible. Transport should be approached as “intensive care on wheels,” with minimum standards for thermal protection, power backup, suction, and monitoring validated before departure. A brief interdisciplinary discussion involving the referring team, transport personnel, and receiving center, using remote communication where necessary, can help align expectations, anticipate deterioration, and agree on contingency plans. Journey planning should receive the same attention as medical preparation, including route selection, anticipated delays, and mechanisms to reduce avoidable time in transit, such as real-time location sharing and liaison with local traffic authorities when feasible. Identification of intermediate stabilization centers along long routes may provide a safety net if deterioration occurs en route.

In addition, a structured reassessment immediately before departure, with the option to defer or reroute transfer if minimum safety thresholds are not met, may prevent escalation of risk during prolonged journeys. Clear institutional support empowering transport clinicians to halt, divert, or seek intermediate stabilization when safety is compromised is essential, particularly when nonclinical pressures influence decision-making. Mandatory posttransport debriefs of clinical, administrative, and transport services can ensure that such critical events translate into systematic learning rather than isolated experience.

Beyond clinical aspects, this case scenario highlights the ethical distress faced by clinicians when forced to choose between unsafe transport and the socioeconomic realities of families. Adverse outcomes in such circumstances should not be viewed as inevitable. Rather, they underscore the urgent need for standardized neonatal transport protocols, trained transport teams, institutional accountability, and policy-level support to ensure that geography and finances do not determine survival.

This letter is submitted to emphasize that improving neonatal transport safety requires system-level commitment rather than individual improvisation. Without such reforms, clinicians will continue to experience moral distress for outcomes driven primarily by infrastructural and organizational failures.

Safe neonatal transportation must be regarded as a core component of congenital cardiac care and not as an adjunct.[1]
