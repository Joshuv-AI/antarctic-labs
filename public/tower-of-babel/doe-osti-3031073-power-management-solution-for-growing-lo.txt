Executive Summary
Airport operations are expanding, with growing electrical loads that could exceed local grid
capacity limitations. Installing additional capacity to support this load growth can be costly and
time-consuming. The Athena project has developed a load forecast model, Aeroportal, to predict
the future grid capacity that would be needed for these new loads. These models also reveal the
potential for on-site distributed energy resources (DER)—battery energy storage systems (BESS)
and photovoltaics (PV)—and flexible load management to mitigate the need for these upgrades.

This report outlines the development of multiple smart charge management (SCM) load
management solutions—Gold and Platinum with and without DER—that coordinate flexible
loads with DER assets to avoid exceeding grid capacity limitations. The Gold control is a purely
heuristic-based control, while the Platinum control is a forecast-based optimization that requires
detailed information regarding electric vehicle (EV) reservation schedules. Table ES-1 outlines
the summary of results from the testing of each control with a few key conclusions:

 1. The Platinum-based control consistently outperforms the Gold control by improving
 delivered state-of-charge (SOC) metrics.
 2. Each control performs better when paired with DER, as both offer a significant reduction
 in capital investments compared to the uncontrolled capacity upgrade.
 3. The SCM results in some SOC performance compromise to support capacity limits, with
 the Platinum DER control nearly matching the uncontrolled scenario but at a lower cost.
 4. SOC performance compromises highlight the challenges with short dwell times that
 eliminate the option for some EVs to complete their charge session under any scenario.

Introduction
As airports expand services and electrify their operations, their electric loads will continue to
grow. This growth originates from efforts to electrify ground support vehicles, fixed-wing
aircraft, and vehicles charging in passenger parking or at rental car facilities [1, 2, 3]. As these
site loads grow, the costs to supply electricity across the airport will also increase. These costs
may include increases in electricity expenses—through either higher demand charges or variable
energy rate structures—or increases in infrastructure costs through site equipment and grid
upgrades [4]. However, this growth in costs might be mitigated by leveraging any potential load
flexibility or local energy assets. Through appropriately sized on-site power generation and
energy storage, as well as the optimization of flexible charging loads with smart charge
management (SCM), the Athena project has identified a series of solutions to support growing
loads at airports while mitigating the risk of rising electricity costs and avoiding or postponing
grid infrastructure upgrades [3, 4, 5]. This report summarizes the key technological
developments from the Athena project with new tools and resources for airports, as well as the
lab testing and emulation results that validate system performance to de-risk technology
solutions prior to field demonstrations and deployments.

 1

1 Airport Electrification Planning
As airport operations evolve and electrification expands, it is important to forecast electrical
loads across airport sectors and understand their potential impact. This section outlines the
potential load growth at airport rental car centers (RCCs), possible impacts from increasing
electrical demand from charging electric vehicles (EVs), and potential solutions to mitigate
impacts and reduce costs.

1.1 Growing Loads
Among airport operation sectors, RCCs undergoing electrification with the adoption of EVs
might result in outstanding electrical loads due to the volume of vehicles requiring charging upon
return and the traditional rental car business model. For example, in 2024, Los Angeles
International Airport saw approximately 189,000 rental transactions per month, or 6,000 per day
[6]. Moreover, the rental car business model is dictated by minimum vehicle inventory, which
results in a short turnaround time between a vehicle’s return and the next rental pickup, also
defined as “dwell time” [7]. Anecdotal evidence suggests that 25% of vehicles have a dwell time
at the RCC of 1 hour or less.

Under these assumptions, and in advanced electrification scenarios, a significant fraction of the
vehicles returned to the RCC would require charging using high-power DC electric vehicle
supply equipment (EVSE) typically rated at 350 kW, which can charge an EV from 20% to 80%
state of charge (SOC) in 30 minutes or less.

Initial analysis assuming the use of 350-kW EVSE and 10% fleet electrification at Dallas Fort
Worth International Airport—amounting to 367 rental drop-off events per day—estimated peak
electrical loads as high as 5 MW, which could require electrical distribution upgrades amounting
to $13.5 million [3].

1.2 Aeroportal Forecasts
The Athena team developed the Aeroportal to enable airport stakeholders and partners to access
results generated by the National Laboratory of the Rockies’s (NLR’s) high-fidelity optimization
and simulation models within an online, accessible interface [8]. The Aeroportal can be used to
estimate power requirements and cost across different airport transportation sectors, including
RCCs, ground support equipment, transportation network companies, and parking. Users can
interactively select scenario parameters such as expected vehicle demand volume, return and
target SOC, and utility tariffs, and in turn estimate total cost, peak load, and grid stability. In
addition to the grid-only (business-as-usual) scenario, the Aeroportal also reports solutions that
include optimally sized battery energy storage systems (BESS), which considerably decrease
costs and grid upgrade requirements and improve grid stability. The BESS solutions can be
compared with the grid-only solutions to understand the savings produced by this technology
under specific scenario assumptions, which can amount to millions of dollars and tens of
megawatts in peak load reduction. However, further improvements to the battery storage solution
might be obtained through SCM—leveraging temporally flexible loads—to increase savings and
reduce investments in distributed energy resources (DER), which include BESS and solar
photovoltaics (PV).

 2

2 Load Management Solutions
Load growth could increase demand charges, consume energy during peak pricing windows, and
trigger site or grid equipment upgrades. However, coordination between DER assets, load
monitoring, and management can support significant cost savings. The Athena team has
developed tools to forecast load growth, site capacity limitations, utility rates, and EV charging
operations to size DER solutions with load management potential. These resources are designed
to help airports estimate system requirements and ensure new loads are supported by the
appropriate solutions, minimizing costs and optimizing site operation.

2.1 Scoping Generation and Storage Needs
Athena used NLR’s Enabling Distributed Generation Energy Storage (EDGES) model to
generate optimal BESS design solutions presented in the Aeroportal [9]. EDGES employs a bi-
level optimization approach, coupled with high-fidelity simulation and accurate financial
modeling, to identify the DER configuration—BESS and PV—or required grid distribution
upgrades that minimize the project’s life cycle costs [10]. While SCM strategies are not directly
supported by EDGES (the load profile is provided as a fixed time series), DER solutions
obtained with this tool were used to inform the initial configurations to be tested against the site
controllers described in this work and within the emulation environment described in Section
3.2.

2.2 Leveraging Load Flexibility
EV charging loads are based on several factors that are a function of the vehicle and EVSE
hardware—for example, the battery pack temperature and maximum charge voltage and current.
A large fleet of vehicles, if left unmanaged, could quickly exceed the site’s electrical capacity
from the utility. The most common result of this high peak demand is an overloaded transformer
or an overengineered utility connection that is more costly than necessary.

Due to the nature of vehicle dwell time, rental fleets have some flexibility that can be leveraged
by an SCM controller. For example, utilizing several EVSE with diverse power levels such as
350 kW down to 20 kW allows the fleet to prioritize high-demand vehicles at the higher-power
chargers while pushing the lower-demand vehicles to the lower-power chargers. Each vehicle
will hit its target SOC by the time it is scheduled to depart, but not every vehicle needs to charge
at the maximum rate. This allows for a more optimized load profile across the site that could
mitigate costly bills or upgrades as a result of demand charges or overloaded equipment.
Examples of the charging profiles used in this study are shown in Figure 1. Some examples
include a GMC Hummer being charged by the 350-kW EVSE (collected as part of the Next-
Generation Profiles [NextGen Profiles] project [11]) and a Hyundai Ioniq 5 being charged by a
25-kW EVSE. As expected, power draw of the Hummer is much higher and varies more than the
Ioniq [11]. This is due in part to the power limitation of the 25-kW EVSE, so the Ioniq draws a
constant power over a large span of the SOC range. In general, power profile curves like those
shown in Figure 1 are a function of session-specific conditions like ambient temperature and
battery initial SOC. They are also a function of hardware-specific parameters, like EV and EVSE
model, and complex active control decisions made by the EV’s battery management system and
EVSE.

2.3 Site Optimization Solutions
A critical component to coordinate EV charging load flexibility and site DER assets is the
development of an SCM objective function. This SCM objective function must monitor all EV
charging, building loads, and DER to optimize sitewide net demand. The SCM must leverage PV
generation, charge or discharge the energy storage system, and manage EV charging power to
maintain net demand within sitewide capacity limits to mitigate the need for costly upgrades.
There are a few system requirements that must be supported to enable this SCM. Depending on
the site’s capabilities, namely the presence of detailed fleet operation schedules, two control
strategies are described in this report: “Gold” and “Platinum” controls.

2.3.1 Gold Control
The Gold SCM uses a feedback control framework, making decisions solely based on
information received from the hardware. Additionally, each piece of equipment is considered
separately—a limitation usually associated with feedback or heuristic rules—as opposed to
predictive control strategies, creating challenges for optimizing the whole-site performance. Each
of the main parts of the control framework is described next. The main control subtasks are
executed in the following order:

 • Track fleet and facility operations using MQTT messages: EVSE power and SOC, BESS
 power and SOC, and solar and building power.
 • EV-EVSE assignment: Identify incoming EVs and assign them to EVSE.
 • EVSE curtailment: Calculate EVSE power curtailment set points assuming BESS can be
 discharged at maximum power.
 • BESS dispatch: Calculate BESS power set point based on current measurements.
 • Update BESS and EVSE set points by sending MQTT messages.

transfers between them.

EV-EVSE Assignment
EVSE assignment is done using a heuristic rule based on the EV’s arrival SOC. If the EV SOC is
greater than 40% at arrival, it is assigned to a 25-kW charger. Otherwise, if the SOC is lower
than 40%, which typically entails a longer charging time, the EV is assigned to a high-power
350-kW charger. This assignment is made in the context of RCCs, which have distinct areas for
quick turnaround where fast charging is expected to be employed, and longer-term storage areas
where slow charging will be more prevalent.

EVSE currently operating in the same proportion. One key factor is that the Gold SCM standard
does not assume knowledge of the EV’s charge acceptance curve, which makes it challenging to
estimate the EV’s maximum power consumption. While an iterative control algorithm could be
used to estimate the maximum power—for instance, by increasing the power set points slowly over
time and identifying the point where it stops changing—it was deemed out of scope for this effort.

BESS Dispatch
The BESS dispatch adopts a traditional peak-shaving algorithm, which calculates the charge or
discharge power based on the system’s net load and a predefined baseline threshold:

where 𝑃𝑃𝑡𝑡𝐵𝐵𝐵𝐵𝐵𝐵𝐵𝐵 is the power dispatched by the BESS in kilowatts at the current time step 𝑡𝑡, 𝑃𝑃𝑡𝑡𝑛𝑛𝑛𝑛𝑛𝑛 is
the system’s net load at the current time step (building demand plus EVSE demand plus solar

power allowed to be consumed from the grid. Whenever the net load of the system is above the

power draw of the system accounting for on-site generation—for instance, taking the sum of
building and EVSE consumption and solar generation.

which uses two baseline parameters, one for discharging 𝐵𝐵𝑆𝑆 𝐷𝐷𝐷𝐷𝐷𝐷 and one for charging 𝐵𝐵𝑆𝑆 𝐶𝐶𝐶𝐶𝐶𝐶 , and
sets the battery power equal to zero if the net load is between the two baselines. This last option
was implemented for the emulation experiments, ensuring that the BESS only charges when the
site load is low, or when there is excess solar generation.

2.3.2 Platinum Control
The Platinum SCM control leverages a predictive control framework, using forecasts of future
information to take preventive actions and optimize the system’s operation. Expanding on the
Gold SCM capabilities with predictive optimizations requires significantly more details regarding
EV operations. These details include an accurate (in this case perfect) forecast of when each of the
vehicles will be dropped off at the RCC after a rental, their return SOC, and their expected dwell
time before the next rental pickup. In addition to fleet operations, this strategy assumes an
accurate forecast of facility loads and PV generation. Using this information, the controller will
calculate the EVSE set points and net grid demand target in advance. These set points will then be
communicated in real time by the SCM to the EVSE, and will also be used with real-time load
measurements to calculate the BESS dispatch set points in a feedback fashion.

EV-EVSE Assignment
In the Platinum SCM, the EVSE assignment is made by forecasting the vehicle’s dwell time at
arrival, or equivalently its departure time. If the dwell time is long enough to charge with a 25-
kW charger, the EV is assigned to it; otherwise it gets assigned to a 350-kW charger to speed up
the charge. In case there is a waiting queue for the 350-kW chargers (25-kW chargers do not
have a queue), the SCM will sort the available EVSE by lowest queue and then highest vehicle
SOC.

 7

EVSE and BESS Dispatch
The EVSE curtailment and BESS dispatch are achieved by a combination of predictive
(feedforward) control and real-time implementation (feedback control). The predictive control uses
accurate forecasts within a mixed-integer linear programming optimization problem to calculate
the optimal EVSE power set points and target net grid demand required for all vehicles to reach
their target SOC before their next rental and to minimize violations of the distribution grid
capacity. The EVSE set points are referenced in real time, while the target net demand from the

3.1 Testing Methodology
The goals of EVSE testing for this project were to understand hardware performance in nominal
and off-nominal situations, understand what the limits are when scheduling charging, and create
transfer functions of the EVSE charging several EVs. The approach to EVSE characterization is
like that used in the NextGen Profiles project to test 350-kW DC fast chargers so that results
produced by both projects are comparable [11]. This work focused on comparatively low-power
DC fast chargers in the 20- to 30-kW range. The tests performed fall into one of two categories:
EVSE characterization or vehicle characterization.

3.1.1 EVSE Characterization
EVSE characterization tests are explained in detail in Appendix A and include EVSE Nominal
Power Transfer, EVSE High-Utilization Characterization, and Off-Nominal Grid Input. These
tests utilize NLR’s charge control box hardware to emulate the vehicle interface and allow for
more custom charge sessions to be requested of the EVSE than would be possible with an EV.
These results inform the development of CHIL physics models to ensure each CHIL device can
individually emulate a charge session as influenced by the conditions addressed in these
characterization tests.

The vehicle characterization tests are meant to collect transfer functions that include specific
EVSE hardware and EVs for use in the CHIL test environment in addition to vehicle response to

3.3 Results and Transfer Functions
The output of the lab testing effort described above informs the CHIL test by providing EVSE
and vehicle models based on real-world data. This real-world testing data will define physics
models to represent individual EVSE performance in CHIL devices. Hundreds of these CHIL
devices will emulate the charging power of hundreds of EVSE. Although the power transfer
from charging will be emulated, each CHIL device will respond to industry standard control
signals sent from the SCM. This setup will enable CHIL testing to verify SCM performance at
scale and provide a much closer estimate of the actual net power demands of the entire site. This
CHIL environment serves as a stress test for the SCM technology and provides results that will
closely reflect the control capabilities for transition to field demonstrations and implementation.

3.3.1 EVSE Characterization
Characterization of each EVSE listed in Table 1 was performed using the requirements described
in Appendix A. The results included in this section are meant to illustrate the type of results that
were collected but do not include results for every test.

Figure 6 shows results from a 400-VDC nominal power transfer test of the ABB EVSE at
ambient temperature (23°C). For this test, the voltage is held constant, while prescribed steps in
output current are made that cover as much of the operating area at that voltage level as possible.

control. The system is allowed to come to steady state for a period of time before the next step is
made to gather data at each operating point. This test is then repeated for other voltages within
the EVSE’s operating range. This test allows for mapping of metrics like power efficiency over

4 Simulating Control Capabilities
Expanding electrification with flexible charging loads and installing on-site DER for energy
generation and storage presents an opportunity for load management systems to optimize site
operations to reduce energy and infrastructure costs. This optimization is possible with
communication and control systems that monitor and coordinate DER assets and flexible
charging loads with grid and site operations. The complex nature of these objective functions
requires a simulation environment to rapidly test and troubleshoot control capabilities prior to lab
demonstrations and field deployments. The following simulation results highlight the capabilities
of each control to support site planning and cost-benefit analysis.

4.1 Simulation Results
The site controllers presented in Section 2.3 were developed and tested within a simulation
environment that resembled the experimental test bed, except that all objects and interactions
were simulated. We performed sensitivity studies simulating operation under each of the
controllers. Throughout these simulations, we varied the design of the DER—battery storage and
solar PV—and evaluated the controllers’ performance against a baseline. In addition to
quantifying the performance of the controllers, we used the results of these tests to identify
appropriate DER configurations, which were used in the CHIL emulations presented in Section
5. The DER configurations selected for the CHIL tests provide a reasonable balance between the
two objectives to satisfy EV charging needs and mitigate electrical upgrades. While the
controllers were tested assuming different DER configurations, other case study parameters were
consistent across simulations to provide an opportunity for direct comparison:

 • The normalized solar PV generation was fixed. Varying the amount of PV installed
 changed the amount of energy produced but not the normalized power profile.
 • The EV charging sessions (i.e., trips) were also fixed. Each session represents a vehicle
 being dropped off at the RCC and is defined by vehicle type, which in turn dictates
 charge acceptance profile and battery capacity, arrival time, drop-off SOC, and vehicle
 dwell time. The simulation included 750 charge sessions in a 24-hour span.
 • The SOC at which vehicles are given to customers—also referred to as target or pickup
 SOC—was fixed at 80%.
 • The number of charging ports by type was also kept constant across scenarios: 277 EVSE
 at 25 kW and 21 EVSE at 350 kW of charging capacity each. These numbers were
 selected because in an ideal scenario—where all vehicles’ charging requirements and
 dwell times are known in advance—they represent the minimum infrastructure required
 for no queueing at the EVSE.
 • The facility load profile was provided by Dallas Fort Worth International Airport and
 reflects what was measured by the utility meter at the RCC.
 • The grid capacity ceiling, which is the maximum power that can be drawn at the utility
 feeder, was limited to 2 MW, reflecting anecdotal information about the airport’s RCC.

4.1.1 Uncontrolled Case
This is the simplest case from a controller and site design perspective, and is treated as a baseline
against which to compare the more sophisticated site controllers. In this case, the only control
decision is made by the RCC operators based on the vehicles’ return SOC: If the SOC is more
than or equal to 40%, then the vehicle is assigned a 25-kW EVSE. Otherwise, a 350-kW EVSE is

used. Once the vehicle is charging, its charge acceptance curve (CAC) dictates the power
transferred to charge the battery; no dynamic EVSE power set point setting is performed, nor is
any DER deployed. The time series from this scenario of main outputs—facility loads, net power
demand from the grid, and grid capacity limit—are presented in Figure 12, while the aggregated
results are in Table 4.

In this case, around 8% of charging sessions are not completed, meaning 8% of the 750 vehicles
do not reach 80% by their next rental pick, and the average SOC deficit is 13%. This deficit is
due to both the EVSE assignment (certain EVs might be returned with SOC higher that 40% but
have a very short dwell time) and the combination of a short dwell time and high energy
requirement (certain sessions simply cannot be completed, even on a 350-kW EVSE). In addition
to these limitations, a significant portion of the load exceeds the grid capacity ceiling, with the
simulation net peak load amounting to 3.6 MW. To accommodate the peak load above the
current ceiling, on-site distribution upgrades totaling more than $4.2 million might be required.

4.1.4 SCM Platinum, No DER
In this subsection we introduce simulation results using the Platinum SCM strategy, with no

5 Validating System Performance
The simulation results highlight the capabilities of the control objective function. However,
before a system can be deployed, it is critical that software performance operates cohesively with
real-world hardware. In order to stress test the Gold and Platinum management solutions
identified above, the team developed a CHIL/power hardware-in-the-loop (PHIL) test bed to
perform real-time control and optimization tests with detailed physics models to represent real-
world hardware responses, operating alongside actual EVSE hardware. This emulation/test
environment serves as a stress test for the SCM, with full-system control and communication
requirements mirroring those of a field demonstration and site deployment. While the controls
must manage two EVSE in the lab, hundreds of additional CHIL devices scale up the
management and communication requirements to verify at-scale performance. These CHIL end
node devices representing individual assets—including DER and EVSE—receive the same
control signals as the EVSE and emulate the true physics response as characterized in testing to
enable full-scale testing. This setup will mimic the demands of a full rental fleet to de-risk field
demonstrations and deployments by verifying performance at scale.

5.1 Lab Demonstration Test Bed
5.1.1 Test Bed Architecture
The test bed consists of hundreds of Raspberry Pi 4 Model B devices, each operating as an
independent CHIL device to emulate the charging behavior of a specific EV. In a hardware-in-
the-loop setup, real hardware components interact with a simulated environment in real time,
enabling realistic testing without requiring the complete physical system. Based on experimental
hardware test results, a code that models the relationship between the EV state of charge and
charging power for different EV and EVSE types was developed and deployed on the CHIL
devices. For the purposes of this demonstration, this code is referred to as the transfer function.

In addition to active power, the transfer function also calculates reactive power consumption as a
function of SOC, as well as the efficiency curve of the charging equipment. The efficiency curve
is included to account for power losses in the chargers’ converters, thereby improving the
accuracy of the power system model. The energy delivered to the vehicle is calculated within the
transfer function in real time, and SOC updates are derived based on the vehicle’s battery
capacity.

Each CHIL device connects to the Real-Time Digital Simulator (RTDS), a high-performance
computing platform used to model power systems in real time. RTDS simulations are developed
and executed through RSCAD, the companion software environment that provides the interface
for building, running, and monitoring real-time models. Communication between the CHIL
devices and the RTDS is implemented using TCP/IP (Transmission Control Protocol/Internet
Protocol) or UDP (User Datagram Protocol). In parallel, each CHIL device communicates with
the SCM using OCPP. OCPP, developed by the Open Charge Alliance, is an open
communication standard that enables interoperability between EV charging stations and central
management systems from different vendors. In this project, each CHIL device adopts the OCPP

 26

v1.6J protocol to emulate a charging station. The OCPP package used to implement the OCPP
protocol is based on Python programming language. 1

In addition to the CHIL devices, hardware chargers are included in the test bed for PHIL
validation. Two physical chargers are available for this purpose: one rated at 350 kW and another
at 25 kW. Similar to the CHIL devices, the hardware chargers communicate with the SCM via
OCPP v1.6J.

5.1.3 Power System Model
To evaluate the performance of the energy management system, the simulation power model is
built on the IEEE 13-node test feeder. This system consists of 13 buses interconnected by a

 28

combination of overhead and underground cables, a main substation transformer, a step-type
voltage regulator, and an inline transformer. The IEEE 13-node feeder serves as a standard
benchmark for testing and validating power flow analysis methods and optimization algorithms.
The single-line diagram of the model is shown in Figure 18.

In this project, EV charging stations are strategically placed at different nodes within the feeder.
Two types of chargers are considered based on hardware characterization tests performed for this
study: 350-kW and 25-kW units. In addition to the charging infrastructure, the model
incorporates a PV plant and an energy storage system to represent DER. The energy storage
system plays a key role in enhancing grid resilience and mitigating fluctuations in power supply
and demand. Control algorithms governing these resources are implemented within the RSCAD
environment in RTDS hardware-in-the-loop platform.

Converters are modeled as voltage sources equipped with active and reactive power controllers,
with power set points supplied by SCM. The power system simulation is executed with a time
step of 60 𝜇𝜇s, enabling high-fidelity, real-time emulation of dynamic interactions between the
DER, EV charging infrastructure, and grid.

Node 650 in Figure 18 is modeled as the slack bus, with a 4.16-kV voltage and a short circuit
ratio (SCR) of 10, representing a relatively strong grid. SCR is a widely used metric for assessing
grid strength, defined as the ratio of short-circuit power to the rated power of the connected
devices. A higher SCR indicates a stiff, reliable grid with low sensitivity to disturbances and
voltage fluctuations, while grids with SCR values below 3 are typically classified as weak.

5.1.4 Test Flow
EVs arrive at the rental facility according to the predefined schedule. Each arrival triggers the
proximity function and sends the vehicle’s SOC to the SCM. A mapping file containing all
EVSE IDs and their power ratings is accessible to both the SCM and the facility operator. Using
this information, the SCM assigns the EV to a charger and communicates the EVSE ID to the
operator. In making this assignment, the SCM considers multiple factors, including PV
generation, energy storage system SOC, utility prices, and current or forecasted load/generation.

Once the EVSE ID is received by a CHIL device, the charging process begins. The CHIL device
replicates the EV’s power consumption according to its transfer function. During charging, the
CHIL sends OCPP messages (e.g., MeterValues for consumed power and SOC) to the OCPP
server hosted in Node-RED. Simultaneously, it communicates with the RTDS, providing signals
for use within the RSCAD simulation model. The RSCAD model also simulates other sources
and loads in parallel and exchanges data with the SCM in real time.

The charging process ends in either of two cases:

 1. The SOC reaches 80%.
 2. The SCM sends a zero-power set point command to a charger, which the CHIL interprets
 as an instruction to terminate the session.
When the session ends, a StopTransactionReq OCPP message is sent to the OCPP server,
followed by a StatusNotificationReq with an “Available” status. This informs the OCPP server
and the SCM that the charger is now free and ready to serve other vehicles.

as emulated and represented in the CHIL/PHIL test bed above. Unlike the simulation results

protocols. CHIL testing ensures the system will perform at scale, while PHIL testing ensures the
SCM can respond to live operating EVSE hardware. The results below, when compared to
simulation results, provide the confidence this technology is ready for deployment in pilot
demonstrations.

Conclusion
The results of this study highlight grid capacity challenges for airports with expanding electrical
loads. The specific challenges in this study were regarding load increases at airport rental car
facilities due to the adoption of EVs. However, the resulting solutions could support challenges
due to any load increase. If gone unmanaged, these increases in site peak demand would require
significant electrical capacity upgrades—$4.2 million for 1.6 MW of additional capacity.
While the growing loads from EVs create the challenge of a higher peak demand, the nature of
EV charging loads also presents an opportunity. This project developed and tested a site
controller specifically to coordinate DER and flexible charging assets to mitigate peak demand
concerns and avoid costly upgrades. The simulation results display how simple site management
can ensure grid constraints are adhered to and completely avoid infrastructure upgrades.
However, these Gold and Platinum results without DER would extend charging times or result in
vehicles delivered to customers below the desired SOC. These controls are also able to
coordinate with DER assets to further mitigate net peak demand, which would require
significantly less investment than the grid upgrades from the uncontrolled scenario—$2.8 million
and $1.2 million for Gold and Platinum, respectively. These controls do present a significant
improvement in charge session completion compared to uncontrolled and the respective Gold
and Platinum non-DER simulations.
In addition to the development and simulation of these controls, the team also refined their
performance for use in live operations with real-world hardware. This required modifications to
the controllers that addressed EVSE hardware capabilities and industry standard communication
protocol messages. These updates enabled testing in the CHIL/PHIL test bed to verify system
performance at scale. This real-time operation stress test proves the technology could be easily
adapted for field demonstrations with equipment available today.
While the potential cost savings are significant, and the CHIL/PHIL performance suggests the
technology could be deployed in the field, there are some challenges that arose regarding charge
session performance. While all of the results above present portions of charge sessions with an
SOC deficit, it is important to understand how this is caused. The vehicle itineraries that defined
arrival SOC and vehicle dwell period presented many challenges, with a significant portion of
the vehicles arriving with less than 30% SOC and many requiring a quick turnaround of less than
30 minutes. While some of these sessions could not be completed under any circumstance due to
vehicle charge acceptance curve variability, many others leave very little flexibility for
management. Some resolutions to this could include fleet operation revisions that would improve
arrival SOC, extended vehicle dwell periods from reservations, or short delivery delays to ensure
charge sessions have sufficient time to complete.
These findings present exciting opportunities for fleets to address peak demand challenges that
may be increasing utility bills or delaying expanding operations. There are many opportunities
for this technology to be refined for a specific fleet’s needs. These refinements could be tested
and verified using the framework outlined in this report, as well as the capabilities developed and
currently present at NLR. While these results present exciting cost savings, many opportunities
still remain to ensure that expanding electric loads can be supported without costly and time-
consuming upgrade projects.

voltage harmonics that are outside of the hardware’s nominal operating window to observe the
impact on the hardware itself. Table A-3 shows high-level information for the off-nominal tests
including the two operating states, one at maximum power and another at a lower power.
