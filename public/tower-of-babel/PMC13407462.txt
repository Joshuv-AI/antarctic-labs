Multilevel surgical management for severe dysphagia due to lower cranial nerve palsy with multimodal functional assessment: a case report

Introduction

Lower cranial nerve (LCN) palsy following skull base surgery can result in complex dysphagia affecting multiple levels of the swallowing mechanism [1–3]. Although rehabilitation improves function in many cases [3], some patients develop persistent impairment due to multiple dysfunctions such as velopharyngeal insufficiency, reduced pharyngeal contraction, impaired upper esophageal sphincter (UES) relaxation, and glottic insufficiency.

While surgical procedures targeting individual dysfunctions have been reported, management strategies for multilevel dysfunction remain less clearly defined. Furthermore, even within LCN palsy, the number of affected nerves and severity of impairment vary considerably among patients, suggesting that individualized treatment strategies may be necessary.

We report a case of severe dysphagia due to LCN palsy in which a pathophysiology-based multilevel surgical approach resulted in substantial functional improvement.

Case presentation

A 77-year-old woman presented with breathy hoarseness and dysphagia. Laryngoscopy showed right vocal fold paralysis with fixation at the paramedian position. Further evaluation revealed right-sided velopharyngeal insufficiency, pharyngeal paralysis, and atrophy of the right sternocleidomastoid muscle, consistent with multiple right LCN palsies (cranial nerves IX–XI). Her oral intake status was scored as 6 on the Functional Oral Intake Scale (FOIS). Contrast-enhanced magnetic resonance imaging revealed a tumor in the right cerebellopontine angle (Fig. 1A). The neurosurgery team performed tumor resection, and pathology confirmed fibrous meningioma. Postoperatively, her swallowing deteriorated, requiring gastrostomy tube placement. Rehabilitation started on postoperative day (POD) 5. By POD 30, she regained partial oral intake (FOIS 5) with right-neck rotation. The nasogastric tube was removed, and she was discharged. However, despite over six months of rehabilitation, she developed aspiration pneumonia and 10-kg weight loss, prompting her return for further treatment.

Comprehensive investigation of swallowing dysfunction

On re-assessment, the patient showed no signs of tongue atrophy or impaired tongue movement. However, marked atrophy of the left sternocleidomastoid muscle and absence of the right pharyngeal reflex were noted. During phonation, the curtain sign was observed, characterized by leftward retraction of the posterior pharyngeal wall, with more pronounced laryngeal elevation on the left. Her maximum phonation time (MPT) was 2.3 s and mean flow rate (MFR) was 725.9 mL/s, compared with normal values of approximately 14–18 s and 100–200 mL/s, respectively [4]. Endoscopic examination demonstrated right-sided velopharyngeal insufficiency with nasal regurgitation (Fig. 1B). Additionally, a large amount of saliva pooling was observed in the vallecula and bilateral piriform sinuses, along with salivary penetration into the larynx (Fig. 1C), paramedian fixation of the right vocal fold with a vertical height mismatch (Fig. 1D), and impaired right-sided laryngeal sensation. Nevertheless, she could expectorate saliva spontaneously.

Videofluoroscopic swallowing study (VFSS, Supplemental movie 1) showed immediate nasal regurgitation upon swallowing 3 mL of moderately thick liquid (200 mPa·s) (Fig. 1E), with partial esophageal passage and laryngeal penetration (Fig. 1F). Head position changes did not improve function. UES relaxation was impaired, and esophageal peristalsis was absent.

High-resolution manometry (HRM) revealed reduced velopharyngeal and mesopharyngeal pressures, lack of UES relaxation during swallowing, abnormal hypopharyngeal pressure, and absent cervical esophageal contraction (Fig. 2A). Dynamic swallowing CT (Supplemental movie 2) showed right velopharyngeal insufficiency; 14.0 mm anteroposterior velopharyngeal gap at rest was observed (Fig. 2B). Swallowing induced contrast regurgitation into the nasal cavity (Fig. 2C). Right pharyngeal contraction was poor; left-sided contraction displaced the right hypopharynx laterally. Hypopharyngeal volume was 6.80 mL at rest (Fig. 2D) and 2.70 mL at maximum laryngeal elevation (Fig. 2E), indicating insufficient contraction. Based on these findings, a diagnosis of further deterioration of cranial nerves IX–XI was made.

Tailored surgical management

Based on detailed evaluation, the following procedures were considered necessary (Fig. 3): (1) right pharyngeal flap for velopharyngeal insufficiency, (2) right cricopharyngeal myotomy (CPM) for UES dysfunction, (3) right arytenoid adduction for vocal fold paralysis, and (4) right hypopharyngeal pharyngoplasty with reinforcement for impaired pharyngeal contraction. A staged surgical approach was initially proposed, with the pharyngeal flap performed first, followed by procedures (2)–(4). However, the patient preferred a single-stage intervention, thus all procedures were performed in one session. A tracheostomy was performed to ensure perioperative airway safety.

Strategic approach for selection of swallowing improvement surgeries based on identified impairments

Before arytenoid adduction, the thyropharyngeal muscle was separated and preserved laterally. A 1-cm segment of the thyroid ala was excised. After adduction, hypopharyngeal pharyngoplasty was performed by suturing the hypopharyngeal mucosa and thyropharyngeal muscle to the thyroid cartilage (Fig. 4A). As the thyropharyngeal muscle was atrophic and deemed insufficient, a 0.4-mm-thick expanded polytetrafluoroethylene (ePTFE) sheet (Gore-Tex®) was applied to reinforce the reconstructed wall (Fig. 4B).

Intraoperative views. A Hypopharyngeal pharyngoplasty with anterior suspension of the hypopharyngeal mucosa, reinforced by suturing the thyropharyngeal muscle (star) to the thyroid cartilage. B Additional reinforcement using 0.4-mm expanded polytetrafluoroethylene sheet (arrow) over the muscle

CPM was performed only on the right due to esophageal dysmotility and reflux risk. Laryngeal suspension was withheld as elevation was impaired only on one side. A cuffed tracheostomy tube (ID 7.0 mm, OD 9.6 mm) was placed, and parenteral nutrition started via a peripherally inserted central catheter.

Postoperative course and functional outcomes

A cuffed speech-type tube was placed on POD 13, and voice training started. By POD 18, tracheal suctioning decreased. VFSS showed contrast flow (100 mPa·s) without nasal regurgitation using chin-tuck posture (Fig. 5A–B; Supplemental movie 3). Jelly-based oral intake training was then started on POD 18. In addition, the tracheostomy tube was exchanged for a smaller tracheal opening retainer (OD 9.0 mm, length 14.0 mm), which was subsequently fitted with a speaking valve intentionally occluded with tape to simulate full closure. Oral intake was gradually advanced from pureed meals to diced soft food over the following days, completing each meal in about 60 min. As no further tracheal suctioning was needed and stable respiration was confirmed under simulated closure, the tracheostoma was closed surgically on POD 25. By POD 32, the pharyngeal mucosa had epithelialized (Fig. 5C), the flap was confirmed in the right nasopharynx (Fig. 5D), and no nasal regurgitation was observed (Fig. 5E). Glottic closure during phonation was complete (Fig. 5F). MPT increased to 15.2 s and MFR improved to 139.8 mL/s. HRM showed improved velopharyngeal pressure and restored UES relaxation, although mild hypopharyngeal dysfunction persisted (Fig. 6A). She was discharged on POD 33.

At 3 months postoperatively, dynamic swallowing CT and virtual reality showed right velopharyngeal anteroposterior diameter reduced to 6.9 mm, with flap width 7.2 mm (Fig. 6B), without nasal regurgitation (Fig. 6C). Hypopharyngeal wall reinforcement by ePTFE was confirmed (Fig. 6D), and swallowing-phase volume reduced to 1.27 mL (Fig. 6E). At that time, she resumed a regular diet, though with some limitations. At the 16-month follow-up, she maintained stable swallowing function without recurrent aspiration pneumonia.

Discussion

This case highlights the importance of individualized pathophysiology-based surgical planning in complex dysphagia due to LCN palsy. In this patient, multilevel dysfunction persisted despite prolonged rehabilitation, resulting in recurrent aspiration pneumonia and malnutrition, prompting surgical intervention. A single-stage approach, based on the underlying pathophysiology, was selected due to progressive clinical deterioration and patient preference.

Restoration of velopharyngeal closure was essential to re-establish adequate pharyngeal pressure, as bolus propulsion remains insufficient, despite a reduction in UES resistance, in the presence of velopharyngeal insufficiency. In contrast to cases with preserved velopharyngeal function, such as that reported by Maruo et al. [5], in which isolated cricopharyngeal myotomy was effective. Accordingly, the present case required restoration of both pharyngeal pressure generation and bolus outflow. Unilateral pharyngeal paralysis warranted a lateral pharyngeal flap to reinforce the weakened side [6, 7]. Hypopharyngeal pharyngoplasty is also effective in such cases [8, 9], as it improves pharyngeal transit and reduces aspiration risk [10]. This procedure can be performed through the same surgical field as arytenoid adduction. Previous studies have reported the use of polypropylene mesh for pharyngeal wall reinforcement [11]. To our knowledge, this is the first report of ePTFE mesh for this purpose. It was selected as a thin, flexible, and biocompatible material widely used in laryngeal framework surgery, suitable for reinforcing the atrophic pharyngeal constrictor muscles [12, 13]. Nevertheless, the potential for long-term complications should be acknowledged, necessitating careful follow-up.

While advanced modalities such as HRM and dynamic swallowing CT are not required in routine cases, these provided valuable complementary information in this complex case. HRM enabled quantitative assessment of pharyngeal pressure and UES function [10, 14–17], while swallowing CT allowed three-dimensional evaluation of structural dynamics [18–20]. These findings facilitated identification of the distribution and severity of dysfunction and supported more precise surgical planning, highlighting the need for velopharyngeal reconstruction and hypopharyngeal reinforcement.

The favorable postoperative course may be attributed to comprehensive preoperative assessment, a pathophysiology-based multilevel surgical strategy, and structured rehabilitation. Airway safety was ensured by tracheostomy with simulated closure using an occluded speaking valve prior to decannulation. This approach may be considered in patients with multilevel dysfunction and high aspiration risk refractory to conservative treatment, but may not be appropriate in mild or single-level cases or in those deemed medically unfit for extensive surgery. This report is limited by its single-case design. However, favorable functional outcomes persisted at 16-month follow-up, suggesting potential durability of treatment effects over time.

Conclusion

In the present case, comprehensive multimodal swallowing assessment, including laryngoscopy, VFSS, HRM, and swallowing CT, enabled precise evaluation of dysphagia. Based on these findings, tailored surgical interventions resulted in favorable outcomes, with hypopharyngeal reinforcement using ePTFE likely contributing.
