EMBOLISM^ OF THE SPINAL CORD.
By ARCHIBALD W. HARRINGTON, M.D., F.R.F.P.S.G.,
Assistant Physician, Glasgow Royal Infirmary ; Visiting Physician,
Bellahouston Hospital.
In infective endocarditis one is familiar with embolism of
various arteries, such as the splenic, renal, cerebral, &c., but
embolism of the vessels of the spinal cord appears to be very
rare. Consequently the record of the following case is of
interest and importance :?
Agnes B., aged 42 years, a telegraphist, was admitted to
the Glasgow Royal Infirmary on 24th August, 1922, suffering
from loss of power and sensation below the waist of five
days' duration.
On 18th August, while sitting at work, the patient experienced
a sudden dull, dragging pain in the lumbar region, accompanied
by a prickling sensation in the epigastrium. She sat on at her
work, and in about half an hour felt well again, and was quite
well that night and next morning. During the afternoon
of the next day, she was again seized by a similar pain,
and the prickling sensation in the epigastrium was more
pronounced. She felt unwell generally, but managed to
continue at her work. When she came off duty at night,
on going downstairs her left knee " gave way beneath her."
She got on a tramway car with difficulty, and reached her
lodgings. About fifteen minutes after getting home, she
collapsed, falling forward from her chair. She was assisted
into bed, and then found that power and sensation below
the waist were quite gone. Since then she has been confined
to bed, and has had incontinence of urine since 22nd August.
On the night of 21st August she was burned by a hot-water
bottle on the lower abdomen, but felt no pain.

Note on admission.?Patient is a slenderly built woman.
On the lower abdomen is a large burn of the second degree,
quite painless. There is absolute loss of power and sensation
from the costal margin downwards. There is no zone of

and pus. There is incontinence of faeces. The pulse is regular,
and numbers 110 per minute. The heart is not much enlarged,
but there is a well-marked auricular systolic murmur. The
spleen is not palpable. The patient remained very ill,
and her condition precluded detailed examination. Despite
considerable irregular pyrexia, her general condition slightly
improved, but the paralysis was unchanged. On 30th August,
without loss of consciousness, the patient suddenly developed
paralysis of the left side of the face and left arm. Fever
continued, and on 5th September there was some pericarditis.
Blood disappeared from the urine, and pus diminished, but
the albuminuria persisted. The leucocytes on 11th September
numbered 2,500 per c.mm., and a blood culture showed an
abundant growth of a streptococcus of the viridans type.
She gradually sank, and died on 28th September, 1922.
Pathological report (by Dr. J. A. G. Burton).?At the
post-mortem, the pericardium contained a slight excess of
blood-stained serous fluid with evidence of recent pericarditis.
The aortic valve showed old endocarditis with shortening and
thickening of the cusps, the margins of which were adherent.
The mitral valve showed an ulcerative endocarditis obviously
superimposed on a chronic condition. Several of the thickened
chordae tendineae were ruptured and the valve generally showed
gross deformity. The left auricle contained a number of small
firmly adherent white thrombi. The aortic intima was the

These were arranged mainly in pairs and short chains.

The brain, on section, showed marked oedema, with a patch

soft. On removing the dura there was extensive softening
of the whole lumbar enlargement of the cord. On microscopic
examination a small embolus was determined about the level
of the eleventh dorsal segment. Sections cut at this level
showed a central softening of the cord, and this softening
rapidly expanded until about two segments lower there was
complete disintegration of the whole structure. Organisms
similar to those found in the mitral valve were present in
scanty numbers in the neighbourhood of the embolus.
The accompanying low power photographs give a rough
idea of the appearance seen at the different levels. Extreme
chromatolysis was present in the ganglion cells in the neigh-
bourhood of the embolus.
From the foregoing record I think it may reasonably be
inferred that the patient had one, if not two emboli, plugging
her spinal vessels. The embolus, coming from a mitral valve
infected by streptococcus viridans, was itself infected, and
softening over an area greater than that supplied by the
plugged vessel rapidly followed. This explains the ingravescent
nature and completeness of the paraplegia. The infected
embolus set up an acute myelitis, which took a little time to

Very few references to the condition can be found in the
literature. Cases have been described by Von Leyden,5 Weiss,1
and Gowers. In all these the patients were suffering from
mitral disease, and in two of them an autopsy showed the
presence of spinal softenings. Rosenthal and Thiroloix,2 quoted
in Allbutt's " System," in a case of streptococcic vegetative
endocarditis in which an acute paraplegia occurred during the
last few days of life, found that the spinal cord was extensively
softened in the dorsal region, and a culture of streptococcus
was obtained from the lesion. Oppenheim3 describes a similar
case, illustrated by a microphotograph, showing a focus of
infiltration in the cord. Osier4 remarks that "blocking of the
spinal arteries rarely occurs."
This case is also of interest in its relationship to the etiology
of acute myelitis. Bacteria have been repeatedly demonstrated
in the cord in acute myelitis. These organisms find their way
into the cord from the cerebro-spinal fluid by way of the blood
and lymph. Marinesco5 has produced true myelitis by the
injection of micro-organisms (1) into a blood-vessel which
directly supplied the cord; (2) into a blood-vessel distant from
the cord; (3) into a nerve, such as the sciatic nerve; (4) into the
arachnoid cavity. The first method produced poliomyelitis; the
second produced myelitis very rarely; the third meningo-
myelitis; and the fourth meningomyelitis at the seat of
injection. Williamson6 states that it is probable that many
cases described as myelitis, especially when the onset is very
sudden, are really due to spinal softening produced by the
obstruction of blood-vessels. In spinal embolism, whether it
be the capillary spinal embolism described by Von Leyden5 in
ulcerative endocarditis, or embolism of a larger artery as in the
case under consideration, there are the two factors of (1) injury
to the tissue produced by the arterial blocking, and (2) infection.
In such a case the production of an acute myelitis is easy to
understand. The initial lesion in vascular cases may, as
Gowers has said, ultimately disappear in the intense inflamma-
tion which it excites.
