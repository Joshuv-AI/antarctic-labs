How crucial is research philosophy in designing an AI study in medical education?
Authors: Samy A Azer, Jiyoun Song, Anthony P Guerrero
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Research papers are often rejected due to methodological problems, inappropriate tools, and poor alignment between aims, methods, and conclusions. This editorial argues that these problems often stem from neglecting research philosophy when pursuing an idea. Based on the literature and our peer-reviewed experience in medical education and biochemical sciences, we emphasize that clarifying ontological, epistemological, methodological, and axiological assumptions helps select the appropriate paradigm—positivism, post-positivism, interpretivism, critical theory, or pragmatism. To avoid such methodological problems, researchers should clarify their philosophical assumptions, explicitly state the selected paradigm, and ensure that this process is outlined in the methods section or the appendix. This approach increases the rationality, transparency, reproducibility, and defensibility of research, especially in fields like artificial intelligence in healthcare education.

Background

Based on our extensive experience in peer review and editorial work in medical education, biomedical sciences, and clinical medicine, we have consistently observed that weaknesses in research design and methodology are the most common reasons for manuscript rejection. Inappropriate study design, failure to use validated tools, poorly formulated research questions, and misalignment between aims, methods, and conclusions often result in flaws that are difficult to correct [1]. This is analogous to constructing a building without properly assessing site conditions, structural requirements, environmental risks, and regulatory standards.

Recently, the first author was invited as a keynote speaker at the 2nd Artificial Intelligence (AI) in Medical Education Conference 2025 (AIMED), organized by Universiti Kebangsaan Malaysia (UKM), Malaysia. He presented a systematic review on AI-related publications in four leading medical education journals, Academic Medicine, Medical Education, Medical Teacher, and BMC Medical Education (2014–2024), which revealed that AI in medical education is in the early developmental phase. Most publications were commentaries, reviews, perspectives, 12-tips, guides, and editorials, while empirical research comprised a smaller proportion. Interest in AI has increased significantly in 2023–2024, yet contributions were mainly from a limited number of countries, with underrepresentation from North Africa, the Middle East, Southeast Asia, and South America. Therefore, AI in medical education appears to be transitioning from exploratory discourse to empirical implementation, making conceptual and methodological clarity increasingly urgent.

As Guest Editors of two BMC Medical Education special issues on AI, one already published and one ongoing, we have handled numerous submissions and have observed that a primary obstacle to publication is a lack of research design and methodological rigor. Many papers are rejected for weak study designs, poorly structured research questions, inadequate sampling strategies, insufficient tool validity and reliability, and weak theoretical foundations. Most often, deficiencies reflect failure to clearly articulate a research paradigm and ontological or epistemological stance, underscoring the importance of research philosophy in driving quality.

Research design and methodology

Many methodological problems stem from insufficient engagement with research philosophy. Selecting and applying an appropriate paradigm enables researchers to align their work with coherent ontological, epistemological, methodological, and axiological assumptions [2, 3]. Research philosophy concerns how reality is understood (ontology), how knowledge is generated and validated (epistemology), which approaches are suitable for inquiry (methodology), and how values and ethics shape the research process (axiology) [2, 4].

Clarifying these assumptions enables justification of methodological choices, enhances transparency, and ensures logical design throughout the study. It also facilitates interpretation of findings that align with the research question and design, while helping identify potential sources of bias [5, 6]. Paradigm selection—positivism, post-positivism, interpretivism, critical theory, or pragmatism—should be guided by the study’s aims and research question.

Application to AI research

These principles are central to advancing the quality of AI research in medical and healthcare education. As AI studies integrate technology, education, and clinical contexts, a clear philosophical grounding is essential to avoid method-driven approaches and enable research that answers critical questions. Early discussion of research paradigm assumptions among team members will yield innovative ideas that are well thought out, well-studied, and justified on philosophical grounds [5, 7].

The following is an example that could provide further insight into what we mean by applying a research philosophy as the basis for AI studies in medical education and curriculum design and development.

“A researcher decides to examine the readiness of medical interns and their professional identity as they transit into the healthcare workforce. One of the areas central in the study aim is the use of AI-supported patient records and competency in readiness. Now the researchers started to search the literature on this topic to find out what we know and what we do not know. They identified seven papers related to the topic and, through discussion, were able to sharpen the research idea. Then they started discussing the study’s philosophical paradigm and addressing the study in-depth. They present the study’s ontological, epistemological, methodological, and axiological assumptions and summarise their discussion in a table. How would you discuss this part if you were a research team member?”

This study examines medical interns’ professional identity and readiness, especially in relation to competency in AI-supported patient records and examines the philosophical assumptions underlying knowledge emerging from practice, real-world consequences, and problem-solving. A pragmatist paradigm is ideal for such design [8–10]. Why? A pragmatist paradigm enables researchers to study how interns use AI-supported records, the challenges they encounter, and the practices that enhance their clinical performance and readiness. Pragmatism also supports integrating quantitative and qualitative data within a mixed-methods framework, emphasizing what works for interns in preparing their transition into the healthcare workforce. Discussion

The process outlined in Table 1 reflects the project’s deep discussion among the research team and can address different aspects of the study philosophy, study design, and methodological coherence. The team came up with a provisional research question: “What contextual, educational, and organisational factors facilitate or hinder interns’ effective use of AI-supported patient records during transition to practice?” A related question was: “What competencies do medical interns report and demonstrate when using AI-supported patient records in clinical settings?” Researchers should further refine these proto-questions to ensure conceptual precision and alignment with the research.

In a pragmatist paradigm, a mixed-methods design is usually proposed. Qualitative data may include surveys, semi-structured interviews, workflow observations, and reflective notes. Quantitative data may encompass demographic characteristics, readiness and transition scores by supervisors, self-efficacy measures for internship tasks, and validated instruments assessing preparedness for digital clinical practice. Mixed methods allow examination of outcomes and processes, mitigate methodological limitations through complementarity [8, 10], and permit exploration of dimensions such as stress, role negotiation, teamwork, patient-centred orientation, and help-seeking behaviours. Triangulation of methods will enhance validity and integration across data sources for a comprehensive account of readiness and identity formation. A paper on medical education must be grounded in a medical education framework throughout the introduction and discussion sections. Citations should be current, accurate, and aligned with arguments advanced, reflecting scholarship published in leading medical education journals.

Two recent publications from special issues on AI in medical education illustrate diverse paradigmatic and methodological approaches. Busch et al. [13] conducted a global cross-sectional survey across 192 faculties examining AI in medical, dental, and veterinary education [13]. Ergezen Sahin et al. [14] reported a randomized controlled trial evaluating an AI-based physiotherapy educational intervention to enhance clinical reasoning skills [14]. These studies provide valuable opportunities to analyse underlying ontological, epistemological, methodological, and axiological assumptions, and to identify strengths, limitations, and transferable insights relevant to future research design [15].

In conclusion, this editorial is a call for researchers to plan their research on a research philosophy and to select a philosophical paradigm, whether pragmatism, post-pragmatism, interpretivism, or critical theory. Then, discuss related research assumptions and possible questions that can further stimulate the formation of an integrated framework on which you can explore different aspects of the research, ensure alignment, and ensure the ability of the research design to answer the research questions and adequately address the study aims. These preparations will ensure that the research design is transparent, reproducible, and defensible. The editorial provides a practical example to engage readers and highlight the applicability of AI-based research in medical education. Research philosophy will provide a clear ground for the study design and strengthen the methodological justification. Embracing these foundations is crucial to producing high-quality, scholarly work.
