Ideals defining components of two-row Springer fibers
Cristina Sabando-Alvarez, Martha Precup

Abstract
. Springer fibers are subvarieties of the flag variety parameterized by nilpotent matrices. They are central objects of study in geometry representation theory. This paper focuses on two-row Springer fibers, those corresponding to nilpotent matrices with two Jordan blocks. Irreducible components of two-row Springer fibers are in bijection with two-row standard Young tableaux and also with noncrossing matchings. Inspired by the combinatorial commutative algebra of matrix Schubert varieties, we define a polynomial ideal for each noncrossing matching and prove that these ideals define the corresponding components of the Springer fiber. Our proofs leverage geometric descriptions of Springer fibers established by Fung, Stroppel–Webster, Fresse, and Goldwasser–Nadeem–Sun–Tymoczko. Using these ideals to compute examples, we give two conjectural formulas for the cohomology class of each component of a two-row Springer fiber. We apply commutative algebra techniques to prove these conjectures for a specific family of two-row tableaux.



1.
Introduction

Springer fibers are important geometric objects in representation theory. Their cohomology rings carry a natural action of the symmetric group or, more generally, the Weyl group. In this paper, we study Springer fibers in the Type
A
flag variety associated to nilpotent matrices with two Jordan blocks. Utilizing the combinatorics of noncrossing matchings, we identify specific polynomial ideals that define the irreducible components of these Springer fibers.

Let
n
be a positive integer and
\mathcal{F}\ell_{n}(\mathbb{C})
denote the flag variety of nested sequences of subspaces
V_{\bullet}=(\{0\}=V_{0}\subset V_{1}\subset V_{2}\subset\cdots\subset V_{n}=\mathbb{C}^{n})
, where
\dim(V_{i})=i
for all
0\leq i\leq n
. Such a sequence is called a (full) flag. We identify the flag variety
\mathcal{F}\ell(n)
with the quotient space
GL_{n}(\mathbb{C})/B
where
GL_{n}(\mathbb{C})
is the group of
n\times n
invertible matrices and
B\subseteq GL_{n}(\mathbb{C})
is the subgroup of upper triangular matrices. The Springer fiber
\mathcal{B}_{N}
is the collection of flags fixed under the action of an
n\times n
nilpotent matrix
N
, that is,

\mathcal{B}_{N}:=\{V_{\bullet}\in\mathcal{F}\ell_{n}(\mathbb{C})\mid N(V_{i})\subseteq V_{i-1}\}.

The irreducible components of the Springer fiber
\mathcal{B}_{N}
are in bijection with standard Young tableaux whose shape is determined by the Jordan type of
N

[
Spaltenstein1976
,
Vargas79
]
. As such, the study of Springer fibers and their components naturally embody the interplay between tableaux combinatorics and geometry. Springer fibers can be defined in the flag variety associated to any complex reductive group
G
. This paper focuses on the case where
G=GL_{n}(\mathbb{C})
.

Borel’s presentation identifies the cohomology of the flag variety
\mathcal{F}\ell_{n}(\mathbb{C})
as a quotient of the polynomial ring
\mathbb{Z}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
. This identification naturally raises the problem of finding explicit polynomial representatives for the cohomology class
[Y]
of any subvariety
Y\subseteq\mathcal{F}\ell_{n}(\mathbb{C})
. This problem provides the primary motivation for the present work and drives broader research into the subvarieties of the flag variety.

The problem from the previous paragraph is most extensively studied in the context of Schubert varieties. Let
S_{n}
be the symmetric group of permutations on
[n]:=\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
. For each
w\in S_{n}
, let
X_{w}
denote the associated Schubert variety, a subvariety of the flag variety defined by rank conditions imposed by
w
. The cohomology class
[X_{w}]
is represented by the Schubert polynomial
\mathfrak{S}_{w}(\mathbf{x})
, introduced by Lascoux and Schützenberger
[
LascouxSchutzenberger1982
]
. Schubert polynomials possess remarkable combinatorial properties, most notably a well-known expansion in the monomial basis indexed by pipe dreams
[
BergeronBilley1993
]
. In 1992, Fulton
[
Fulton1992
]
characterized the ideals of Schubert varieties using determinantal formulas, now called Schubert determinantal ideals. Knutson and Miller
[
KnutsonMiller2005
]
demonstrated that Schubert polynomials arise as the multidegrees of matrix Schubert varieties. They utilized Gröbner theory to provide a geometric justification for combinatorial pipe dreams.

The success of combinatorial commutative algebra in the Schubert case, particularly the study of Schubert determinantal ideals, motivates the use of commutative algebra to study other subvarieties of the flag variety. A recent survey on Schubert geometry and the related commutative algebra is
[
WooYong2023
]
. Similar techniques were recently used by the second author and Goldin to investigate flat degenerations of Hessenberg varieties
[
GoldinPrecup25
]
, which include Springer fibers as a special case.

In this paper, we set out to develop the combinatorial commutative algebra tools to study components of two-row Springer fibers and their cohomology classes.
Each component of a two-row Springer fiber is indexed by a unique two-row standard tableau
\sigma
, or equivalently, a unique noncrossing matching
\mathcal{C}(\sigma)
. We focus on two-row Springer fibers as they have a concrete geometric description in terms of noncrossing matchings
[
Fung2003
,
StroppelWebster2012
]
.

A noncrossing matching is a planar diagram with vertex set
[n]
consisting of a collection of noncrossing edges (called cups) such that no unmatched vertex lies below a cup and such that no vertex is incident to more than one edge. We say that a noncrossing matching is standard if there are no unmatched vertices. In this paper, we place rays at each unmatched vertex and draw all cups so that they pass above the vertex set (not below); see Figure

.

The matching
\mathcal{C}(\sigma)
associated to a two-row standard tableau
\sigma
is standard if and only if
\sigma
is rectangular. To every standard noncrossing matching
\mathcal{C}(\sigma)
on
[n]
we attach a nesting sequence
S_{\sigma}
that counts, from left to right, the number of cups that have begun in our matching so far. That is, for each
1\leq i\leq n-1
,
S_{\sigma}(i)
is the number of cups with left end point less than or equal to
i
. For example, the nesting sequence for the leftmost standard matching
\mathcal{C}(\sigma)
in Figure

(1,2,2,3,3)
; the nesting sequence for the middle standard matching in Figure

(1,2,3,3,3)
. Using the combinatorial data of the nesting sequence of a standard noncrossing matching we define, in Section
4
below, a polynomial ideal
\mathcal{I}_{\sigma}
in the ring
\mathbb{C}[M_{n}]
of functions on the affine variety
M_{n}
of
n\times n
matrices. Applying the decomposition of Springer fibers induced by the prime decomposition of tableaux, we extend the definition to non-rectangular two-row standard tableau
\sigma
(that is, we extend the definition to noncrossing matchings containing rays).

Defining the ideal
\mathcal{I}_{\sigma}
is our first step to establish a commutative algebra framework for two-row Springer fibers. Our main result is that
\mathcal{I}_{\sigma}
serves as a Springer-theoretic analogue of a Schubert determinantal ideal:

Theorem 1
.

Let
\pi:GL_{n}(\mathbb{C})\to GL_{n}(\mathbb{C})/B\simeq\mathcal{F}\ell_{n}(\mathbb{C})
denote the projection map and let
\ell
be a positive integer with
1\leq\ell\leq\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor
. Let
\sigma
be a standard tableau of shape
(n-\ell,\ell)
and
\mathcal{B}_{\sigma}
be the irreducible component of
\mathcal{B}_{N}
corresponding to
\sigma
. The ideal
\mathcal{I}_{\sigma}\subseteq\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{z}]
is right
B
-invariant with vanishing set
\mathcal{V}(\mathcal{I}_{\sigma})
such that
\pi^{-1}(\mathcal{B}_{\sigma})=\mathcal{V}(\mathcal{I}_{\sigma})\cap GL_{n}(\mathbb{C})
.

The problem of computing the cohomology class of an irreducible component of a Springer fiber was originally posed by Springer
[
Springer1983
]
. Güemes gave a formula for components of hook-type Springer fibers while Graham and Zierau
[
Graham2011
]
gave a formula for components defined using the geometry of
GL_{p}(\mathbb{C})\times GL_{q}(\mathbb{C})
-orbits on the flag variety. More recently, Karp and the second author
[
KarpPrecup25
]
characterized irreducible components of Springer fibers equal to Richardson varieties, which subsume both of these special cases. Spink and Tewari then gave a combinatorial formula for the cohomology class of these “Richardson components”
[
SpinkTewari25
]
.

Leveraging the commutative algebra techniques described in
[
KnutsonMiller2005
,
ITW18
,
MillerSturmfels2005
]
, our main theorem provides a method to compute the cohomology class
[\mathcal{B}_{\sigma}]
for any two-row standard tableau
\sigma
. We generate various examples which suggest two conjectures. Although we state these conjectures below for rectangular two-row tableaux, we discuss in Section
7
how they can be extended to the general setting.

In order to state our first conjecture we introduce a little more notation. Given a cup
(i<j)
in
\mathcal{C}(\sigma)
we define the size
\delta(i<j)
to be the number of cups nested within
(i<j)
, including itself. For example, the leftmost matching in Figure
1
has three cups
(1<6),(2<3),
and
(4<5)
with sizes
\delta(1<6)=3
and
\delta(2<3)=\delta(4<5)=1
.

If
\sigma
is a two-row rectangular tableau,
\mathcal{B}_{\sigma}
is a Richardson variety only when the matching
\mathcal{C}(\sigma)
is a union of cups of size one (see
[
KarpPrecup25
, Remark 3.2(ii)]
). In particular, the components we consider are Richardson varieties only when the noncrossing matching
\mathcal{C}(\sigma)
is a union of rays and size one cups, like the rightmost matching in appearing in Figure
1
.

We can now state our first conjecture, which computes the class of
\mathcal{B}_{\sigma}
as a sum of monomials. The monomials that appear are determined combinatorially by the nesting sequence
S_{\sigma}
and cups with size at least
2
in
\mathcal{C}(\sigma)
.

Conjecture 1
.

Let
\sigma
be a standard tableau of shape
(\ell,\ell)
. The cohomology class of
\mathcal{B}_{\sigma}
in
H^{*}(\mathcal{F}\ell_{n}(\mathbb{C});\mathbb{Z})
is represented by the polynomial

\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\in[n]\\
S_{\sigma}(j)<\ell\end{subarray}}x_{j}^{2(\ell-S_{\sigma}(j))}\cdot\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}(i<j)\\
\delta(i<j)\geq 2\end{subarray}}\left(\sum_{k=i}^{j-1}x_{i}x_{i+1}\cdots\hat{x}_{k}\cdots x_{j-1}\right)

where the product is taken over all cups
(i<j)
with size at least
2
in
\mathcal{C}(\sigma)
.

The Schubert classes
[X_{w}]
for
w\in S_{n}
form a basis for the cohomology of the flag variety. Consequently, our motivating problem can be rephrased as follows: Compute the expansion of the class
[Y]
for a subvariety
Y\subseteq\mathcal{F}\ell_{n}(\mathbb{C})
in the Schubert basis. Our second conjecture computes the Schubert expansion of
[\mathcal{B}_{\sigma}]
using a sequence of divided difference operators; in the statement below
w_{0}:=[n,n-1,\ldots,2,1]
is the longest permutation.

Conjecture 2
.

Let
\sigma
be a standard tableau of shape
(\ell,\ell)
with corresponding cup diagram
\mathcal{C}(\sigma)
. Let
(i_{1}<j_{1}),(i_{2}<j_{2}),\ldots,(i_{\ell}<j_{\ell})
be any total ordering of the cups in
\mathcal{C}(\sigma)
such that when a cup appears, every cup nested inside of that one has already appeared.

Then

[\mathcal{B}_{\sigma}]=\left(\partial_{i_{1}}+\cdots+\partial_{j_{1}-1}\right)\circ\left(\partial_{i_{2}}+\cdots+\partial_{j_{2}-1}\right)\circ\cdots\circ\left(\partial_{i_{\ell}}+\cdots+\partial_{j_{\ell}-1}\right)(\mathfrak{S}_{w_{0}}).

.

Although computational data supports the validity of both conjectures, proving them using combinatorial commutative algebra presents a significant obstacle: in general, the generating set of the ideal
\mathcal{I}_{\sigma}
presented here is not a Gröbner basis.

While noncrossing matchings are purely combinatorial objects, they encode representations of the quantum group
U_{q}(\mathfrak{sl}_{2})

[
CautisKamnitzerMorrison2014
]
. Kuperberg formally defined these matchings as a diagrammatic basis for the
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
-web space, providing a framework to describe morphisms between tensor products of representations
[
TemperleyLieb1971
,
Kuperberg1996
]
. More broadly, the theory of
\mathfrak{sl}_{n}
-webs sits at the intersection of knot theory, representation theory, and combinatorics.

For two-row Springer fibers, the combinatorics of matchings simplifies the task of relating algebraic structures to the geometry of the flag variety. Extending these results to
\mathfrak{sl}_{n}
-webs for
n\geq 3
introduces substantial complexity; however, recent progress by Cummings
[
Cummings2026
]
addresses this problem for two-column tableaux. It remains a question for future research to determine the extent to which
\mathfrak{sl}_{n}
-webs capture the underlying geometry of three-row Springer fibers and more general cases.

This paper is structured as follows. In Section
2
we review noncrossing matchings and two-row standard tableaux, establishing the bijection between them. We then define the prime decomposition of a standard tableau and demonstrate how it corresponds to the decomposition of its associated matching. Finally, we summarize key results regarding the geometry of Springer fibers in the two-row case. Section
3
proves lemmas we use later: we introduce statistics to study standard noncrossing matchings, study rank conditions imposed by certain determinantal equations, and define an explicit map to decompose components of two-row Springer fibers based on the prime decomposition of their associated tableaux. In Section
4
we introduce the ideal
\mathcal{I}_{\sigma}
, provide numerous examples, and state our main result, Theorem
1
.
The proof of Theorem
1
is divided into two parts: In Section
5
we prove the ideal
\mathcal{I}_{\sigma}
is right
\mathcal{B}
-invariant and in Section
6
we study the vanishing set
\mathcal{V}(\mathcal{I}_{\sigma})
. Section
7
establishes the two conjectures for expressing the classes of the components
\mathcal{B}_{\sigma}
and proves both for the one big cup case.

Let

[n]:=\{1,2,\ldots,n\}

\lambda_{i}\geq\lambda_{i+1}

2.1.
Noncrossing matchings and standard two-row tableaux

Let
\lambda
be a partition of

Young diagram
of shape
\lambda
is an array of
n

\lambda_{i}
in the
i

standard tableau of shape
\lambda
is a filling
\sigma
of the Young diagram of
\lambda
with the numbers
1,2,\ldots,n
so that each appears exactly once and so that entries strictly increase across rows from left to right and down columns from top to bottom. Given a standard tableau
\sigma
of
\lambda
we set
|\sigma|:=\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}
. When
\lambda=(n-\ell,\ell)
we say that
\sigma
is a
standard two-row tableau
and let
\mathrm{SYT}(n-\ell,\ell)
denote the set of all two-row tableau of shape
(n-\ell,\ell)
.

A
noncrossing matching
on
[n]
is a planar diagram consisting of:

•

n

•

\ell
edges, called
cups
, drawn so that all cups pass above (not below) vertices, and

•

n-2\ell

rays
directed upward

such that each vertex is attached to exactly one cup or ray and no cup or ray crosses any other. We say that a noncrossing matching is

There is a bijection between standard two-row tableaux and noncrossing matchings; see, for example,
[

2.3.
Springer fibers and two-row Springer fibers

In this section we give a brief introduction to Springer fibers, highlighting results needed for our work. For a more detailed review, we refer the reader to
[
TymoczkoSF
,
Jantzen2004
]
.

A
complete flag in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
is a nested sequences of subspaces of
\mathbb{C}^{n}
,

V_{\bullet}=(V_{1}\subset V_{2}\subset\cdots\subset V_{n}=\mathbb{C}^{n}),

such that
\dim V_{i}=i
for
1\leq i\leq n
. The collection of all complete flags is the
flag variety
, denoted here by
\mathcal{F}\ell_{n}(\mathbb{C})
. It is a smooth projective variety of dimension
{n\choose 2}
. We identify
\mathcal{F}\ell_{n}(\mathbb{C})
with the homogeneous space
GL_{n}(\mathbb{C})/B
where
B
is the subgroup of
GL_{n}(\mathbb{C})
of upper triangular matrices. Specifically, given a matrix
g\in GL_{n}(\mathbb{C})
we obtain a flag
V_{\bullet}
by setting
V_{i}
equal to the span of the first
i
columns of
g
.

Let
N
be a
n\times n
nilpotent complex matrix. The
Springer fiber

\mathcal{B}_{N}
is defined to be

\mathcal{B}_{N}:=\{V_{\bullet}\in\mathcal{F}\ell_{n}(\mathbb{C})\mid N\left(V_{i}\right)\subseteq V_{i-1}~\text{for all}~1\leq i\leq n\}.

The irreducible components of the Springer fiber
\mathcal{B}_{N}
are in bijection with standard Young tableaux corresponding to the partition type of
N

[
Spaltenstein1976
,
Vargas79
]
. Given a standard tableau
\sigma
, we let
\mathcal{B}_{\sigma}
denote the corresponding irreducible component of
\mathcal{B}_{N}
under this bijection.

Fresse showed each component
\mathcal{B}_{\sigma}
of the Springer fiber respects concatenation of tableau
\sigma
in the following sense
[
Fresse2011
, Proposition 7.1]

Proposition 2.8

(Fresse)
.

Given standard tableaux
\sigma
and
\omega
, recall that
\sigma\circ\omega
denotes the standard tableau obtained by concatenation. Then
\mathcal{B}_{\sigma\circ\omega}\simeq\mathcal{B}_{\sigma}\times\mathcal{B}_{\omega}
.

This result reduces the study of the geometry of the component
\mathcal{B}_{\sigma\circ\omega}
to that of
\mathcal{B}_{\sigma}
and
\mathcal{B}_{\omega}
. We use it later to reduce our arguments to the case where
\sigma
is a prime two-row tableau, that is,
\mathcal{C}(\sigma)
is a standard matching.

When
N
has two Jordan blocks we say that
\mathcal{B}_{N}
is a
two-row Springer fiber
. Given a tableau
\sigma\in\mathrm{SYT}(n-\ell,\ell)
, Fung
[
Fung2003
, Theorem 5.2]
gave a simple description of each component
\mathcal{B}_{\sigma}
using the noncrossing matching
\mathcal{C}(\sigma)
. To state the result, we introduce some terminology. If
i
is the endpoint of a ray, we let

c(i)=\#\left\{\text{cups }(i^{\prime}<j^{\prime})\text{ in }\mathcal{C}(\sigma)\mid j^{\prime}<i\right\}

denote the number of cups that appear before the ray. Similarly, we let
r(i)
be the number of rays that have appeared up through index
i
, including the ray with endpoint
i
. Notice that
i=r(i)+c(i)
. The following is Fung’s description of the irreducible component
\mathcal{B}_{\sigma}

[
Fung2003
]
(statement adapted from
[
StroppelWebster2012
, Proposition 7]
).

Proposition 2.9

(Fung)
.

Let
N
be a nilpotent matrix with Jordan type
(n-\ell,\ell)
and
\sigma\in\mathrm{SYT}(n-\ell,\ell)
. Let
V_{\bullet}
be a flag in the Springer fiber
\mathcal{B}_{N}
. Then
V_{\bullet}\in\mathcal{B}_{\sigma}
if and only if

(1)

for each cup
(i<j)
in
\mathcal{C}(\sigma)
,
N^{\delta(i<j)}\left(V_{j}\right)=V_{i-1}
and

(2)

for each ray with end point
i
,
V_{i}=N^{-c(i)}\left(\operatorname{im}N^{n-\ell-r(i)}\right)
.

Remark 2.10
.

Suppose
N
is a nilpotent matrix of Jordan type
\lambda=(n-\ell,\ell)
and suppose
N
is in Jordan canonical form with block sizes decreasing. That is, on the standard basis
\{e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{n}\}
of
\mathbb{C}^{n}
the matrix
N
acts by

e_{n-\ell}\mapsto e_{n-\ell-1}\mapsto\cdots\mapsto e_{1}\mapsto 0\quad\text{ and }\quad e_{n}\mapsto e_{n-1}\mapsto\cdots\mapsto e_{n-\ell+1}\mapsto 0.

Consider the condition of Proposition
2.9
(2) with respect to this choice of basis. Note that there are precisely
n-2\ell
rays in
\mathcal{C}(\sigma)
and thus
r(i)\leq n-2\ell
so
n-\ell-r(i)\geq\ell
. In particular, we get that

\operatorname{im}N^{n-\ell-r(i)}=\mathbb{C}\{e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{r(i)}\}

and

N^{-c(i)}\left(\operatorname{im}N^{n-\ell-r(i)}\right)=\mathbb{C}\{e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{r(i)+c(i)}\}\oplus\mathbb{C}\{e_{n-\ell+1},\ldots,e_{n-\ell+c(i)}\}.

This is a subspace of dimension
i=r(i)+2c(i)
. Thus, Proposition
2.9
(2) is precisely the condition that
V_{i}
is the coordinate subspace spanned by the standard basis vectors
e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{r(i)+c(i)}
and
e_{n-\ell+1},\ldots,e_{n-\ell+c(i)}
.

3.2.
A linear algebra lemma on rank conditions

In this section we prove a lemma regarding the rank of a matrix satisfying certain vanishing conditions. This result will give a technical foundation for our choice of generators of our ideals
\mathcal{I}_{\sigma}
in later sections.

Lemma 3.9
.

Let
m\geq 3
be an odd integer and
b=\frac{1}{2}(m+1)
. Let
\binom{[m]}{b}
and
\binom{[m]}{b-1}
denote the set of all subsets of
[m]
of cardinality
b
and
b-1
, respectively. Let
M
denote the incidence matrix with rows indexed by
\binom{[m]}{b-1}
and column by
\binom{[m]}{b}
defined by

M_{Q,R}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1&\text{ if $Q\subseteq R$}\\
0&\text{ otherwise}\end{array}\right.

for each
Q\in\binom{[m]}{b-1}
and
R\in\binom{[m]}{b}
.
Then the matrix
M
is invertible.

Proof.

Since
2b=m+1
we have in particular that
b=m-b+1
and
m-b=b-1
so

\binom{m}{b}=\frac{m!}{b!(m-b)!}=\frac{m!}{(m-b+1)!(b-1)!}=\binom{m}{b-1}.

In particular, the matrix
M
is square. For
k=0,1,\ldots,b-1
we define rational numbers
q_{k}\in\mathbb{Q}
by

(3.4)

\displaystyle q_{b-1}=\frac{1}{b}\;\text{ and }\;q_{k}=-\frac{(b-k-1)q_{k+1}}{k+1}\;\text{ for all $k=0,\ldots,b-2$}.

For example, if
m=9
then
b=5
and the reader can confirm that
q_{4}=\frac{1}{5}
,
q_{3}=-\frac{1}{20}
,
q_{2}=\frac{1}{30}
,
q_{1}=-\frac{1}{20}
, and
q_{0}=\frac{1}{5}
.

Define an
\binom{m}{b}\times\binom{m}{b}
matrix
N
with rows indexed by elements of
\binom{[m]}{b}
and columns by elements of
\binom{[m]}{b-1}
by the rule

N_{R,Q}=q_{\#(Q\cap R)}

for each
Q\in\binom{[m]}{b-1}
and
R\in\binom{[m]}{b}
. In other words, the value of each entry of
N
is uniquely determined by the cardinality of the intersection of
Q
with
R
. We’ll prove
MN
is the identity matrix.

Let
Q,T\in\binom{[m]}{b-1}
.
By definition of the matrix
M
we have

(3.5)

\displaystyle(MN)_{Q,T}=\sum_{R\in\binom{[m]}{b}}M_{Q,R}N_{R,T}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}R\in\binom{[m]}{b}\\
Q\subset R\end{subarray}}N_{R,T}.

There are precisely
b
many subsets
R\in\binom{[m]}{b}
containing
Q
, so the sum above has
b
many terms.

Suppose
T=Q
. When
Q\subset R
we have
N_{R,Q}=q_{b-1}
and the sum (
3.5
) becomes

(MN)_{Q,Q}=b\cdot q_{b-1}=b\cdot\frac{1}{b}=1.

Next suppose that
T\neq Q
. Let
k=\#(Q\cap T)
. Given any subset
R\in\binom{[m]}{b}
with
Q\subset R
we have either
\#(T\cap R)=k
or
\#(T\cap R)=k+1
. The first case occurs when
R=Q\sqcup\{\ell\}
for some
\ell\notin T\cup Q
. There are
m-2(b-1)+k=k+1
many possible such
\ell
, so there are
k+1
many subsets
R\in\binom{[m]}{b}
such that
Q\subset R
and
\#(T\cap R)=k
. The second case of
\#(T\cap R)=k+1
occurs when
R=Q\sqcup\{\ell\}
for some
\ell\in T\setminus Q
. There are
b-1-k
many possible values of
\ell
, so there are
b-1-k
many subsets
R\in\binom{[m]}{b}
such that
Q\subset R
and
\#(T\cap R)=k
. Thus in this case, the sum (
3.5
) becomes

(MN)_{Q,T}=(k+1)q_{k}+(b-1-k)q_{k+1}.

Our assumption that
T\neq Q
implies
k\leq b-2
. Using the definition of
q_{k}
from (
3.4
) gives us

(k+1)q_{k}+(b-1-k)q_{k+1}=-(k+1)\frac{(b-k-1)q_{k+1}}{k+1}+(b-1-k)q_{k+1}=0

as desired. Thus,
M
is invertible.
∎

Lemma 3.13
.

Let
a,k\geq 2
be a positive integers with
k\geq a+1
and let
A
be
2a\times k
matrix such that

(3.7)

\sum_{k=1}^{a}\mathsf{p}_{\{k,2a-k+1\},\{c_{1},c_{2}\}}(A)=0\quad\textup{ for all }\quad 1\leq c_{1}<c_{2}\leq k.

Then
\operatorname{rk}(A)\leq a
, that is, all
(a+1)\times(a+1)
-minors of
A
are zero.

Proof.

Up to permuting the rows of
A
, the assumptions from (
3.7
) are equivalent to:

(3.8)

\sum_{k=1}^{a}\mathsf{p}_{\{2k-1,2k\},\{c_{1},c_{2}\}}(A)=0\textup{ for all }1\leq c_{1}<c_{2}\leq k.

Since exchanging rows does not change the rank, it suffices to prove
\operatorname{rk}(A)\leq a
under the assumptions of (
3.8
).

We will use the generalized Laplace expansion of
A
to show that if (
3.7
) holds, then all
(a+1)\times(a+1)
minors of
A
vanish.
Without loss of generality, we may assume that
k=a+1
. Otherwise, we may choose a subset of
a+1
columns of
A
and use the argument below.

We group the rows of
A
into sets of pairs of the form
\{2k-1,2k\}
with
k=1,\ldots,a
. Any subset
R\subset[2a]
of cardinality
a+1
must contain at least one such pair. We classify the subsets of cardinality
a+1
in
[2a]
according to the “unpaired” indices they contain.

Let
U
be a subset of
[2a]
such that

(3.9)

\displaystyle\{2k-1,2k\}\nsubseteq U\;\text{ for all }\;k=1,\ldots,a.

We use the notation ‘
U
’ because this will be the set of “unpaired” indices within a subset of
[2a]
of cardinality
a+1
. Note that
U
could be the empty set. If
U
is indeed the subset of unpaired indices in the subset
R\subset[2a]
of cardinality
\#R=a+1
, then
\#R-\#U\geq 2
is even and the condition
\#U+(\#R-\#U)=a+1
implies that
\#U
has the same parity as
a+1
and
\#U\leq a-1
. For example, if
a=3
with pairs
\{1,2\},\,\{3,4\},\,\{5,6\}
the subsets
U
that arise in the way are either the empty set or any of the two element subsets of
[2a]=[6]
that do not consist of a pair. No one element subset of
[6]
can occur as the set of unpaired elements of a subset of
[6]
of cardinality
a+1=4
because the cardinality has the wrong parity.

Given a subset
U
satisfying (
3.9
) such that
\#U
has the same parity as
a+1
and
\#U\leq a-1
we consider the collection
\mathcal{S}_{U}
of all subsets
R\subseteq[2a]
of cardinality
a+1
such that
U\subseteq R
and

k\in R\backslash U\Rightarrow\begin{cases}k-1\in R,&\text{if $k$ is even }\\
k+1\in R,&\text{if $k$ is odd. }\end{cases}

In other words,
\mathcal{S}_{U}
is the set of subsets of
[2a]
of cardinality
a+1
such that
R\setminus U
is a union of pairs. Each
R\in\mathcal{S}_{U}
contains exactly
b:=\frac{1}{2}(a+1-\#U)
pairs and there are precisely
\binom{a-\#U}{b}
elements in
\mathcal{S}_{U}
. To complete the proof, it suffices to show that
\mathsf{p}_{R,[a+1]}(A)=0
for all
R\in\mathcal{S}_{U}
and
U\subset[2a]
such that (
3.9
) holds,
\#U
has the same parity as
a+1
, and
\#U\leq a-1
.

Fix a
U
satisfying the relevant hypotheses and set

P=\{k\in[a]\mid\{2k-1,2k\}\cap U=\emptyset\}.

In other words
P
is the indexing set of all pairs
\{2k-1,2k\}
that do not contain any element of
U
, so
P
has cardinality
a-\#U
. Recall that
b
is the number of pairs in any
R\in\mathcal{S}_{U}
, that is
2b=a+1-\#U
, and thus
\#P=a-\#U=2b-1
is odd.
The set
P
keeps track of the possible pairs, each of the form
\{2k-1,2k\}
with
k\in P
, that we can add to the set
U
in order to obtain an element of
\mathcal{S}_{U}
.

Given a subset
Q\subset P
of cardinality
b-1
, let
Q^{\prime}
be the disjoint union

Q^{\prime}:=\{2k-1,2k\mid k\in Q\}\sqcup U

of the
b-1
pairs indexed by elements of
Q
and
U
. In order to obtain an element of
\mathcal{S}_{U}
we must add one more pair to the set. Note that we may add any pair
\{2r-1,2r\}
for
r\in P\setminus Q
. Consider such a subset
R=Q^{\prime}\sqcup\{2r-1,2r\}\in\mathcal{S}_{U}
. Applying the generalized Laplace expansion with
I=\{2r-1,2r\}
to the determinant
\mathsf{p}_{R,[a+1]}
we have

(3.10)

\displaystyle\mathsf{p}_{R,[a+1]}(A)=\mathsf{p}_{\left(Q^{\prime}\sqcup\{2r-1,2r\}\right),[a+1]}(A)=\sum_{S\in\binom{[a+1]}{2}}(-1)^{m(I,S)}\mathsf{p}_{\{2r-1,2r\},S}(A)\,\mathsf{p}_{Q^{\prime},[a+1]\setminus S}(A),

where we may take
m(I,S)=1+\sum_{s\in S}s
since the sum of elements in
I
is odd.
Summing over all
R
we obtain by adding a pair to
Q^{\prime}
we have,

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}R\in\mathcal{S}_{U}\\
Q^{\prime}\subseteq R\end{subarray}}\mathsf{p}_{R,[a+1]}(A)

\displaystyle=\sum_{r\in P\setminus Q}\mathsf{p}_{\{2r-1,2r\}\sqcup Q^{\prime},[a+1]}(A)

\displaystyle=\sum_{r=1}^{a}\mathsf{p}_{\{2r-1,2r\}\sqcup Q^{\prime},[a+1]}(A)\quad\text{ since $\mathsf{p}_{\{2r-1,2r\}\sqcup Q^{\prime}}(A)=0$ for all $r\notin P\setminus Q$ }

\displaystyle=\sum_{r=1}^{a}\left(\sum_{S\in\binom{[a+1]}{2}}(-1)^{m(I,S)}\mathsf{p}_{\{2r-1,2r\},S}(A)\mathsf{p}_{Q^{\prime},[a+1]\setminus S}(A)\right)\quad\text{ by~\eqref{eqn.genlaplace}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{S\in\binom{[a+1]}{2}}\left(\left(\sum_{r=1}^{a}\mathsf{p}_{\{2r-1,2r\},S}(A)\right)(-1)^{m(I,S)}\mathsf{p}_{Q^{\prime},[a+1]\setminus S}(A)\right)

\displaystyle=0\quad\quad\text{by \eqref{vanmins1}.}

In particular, for each subset
Q
of
P
with cardinality
b-1
we have

(3.11)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}R\in\mathcal{S}_{U}\\
Q^{\prime}\subseteq R\end{subarray}}\mathsf{p}_{R,[a+1]}\left(A\right)=0.

Thus, the column vector
\vec{p}_{U}:=\left(\mathsf{p}_{R,[a+1]}\left(A\right)\right)_{R\in\mathcal{S}_{U}}
with entries indexed by elements of
\mathcal{S}_{U}
satisfies the matrix equation

M\vec{p}_{U}=\vec{0}

where
M
is the invertible incidence matrix as in Lemma
3.9
with
m=\#P
(which is odd) defined by

M_{Q,R}:=\begin{cases}1&\text{ if }Q^{\prime}\subseteq R\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

for all
Q\in\binom{P}{b-1}
and
R\in\mathcal{S}_{U}
(we may identify
R
with a subset in
\binom{P}{b}
associating
R
to the index set of the pairs in
R\setminus U
).
Since
M
is invertible by Lemma
3.9
, we conclude
\vec{p}_{U}=\vec{0}
and thus
\mathsf{p}_{R,[a+1]}=0
for all
R\in\mathcal{S}_{U}
, as desired.
∎

\mu:=(\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\ldots,\mu_{k})
is a composition of
n
and we let
t_{i}=\mu_{1}+\cdots+\mu_{i-1}+\mu_{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,k
be the
i
-th partial sum of
\mu
. Since
\sigma
has two-rows, we have by Corollary
2.6
that either
\mu_{i}=1
(which occurs when
t_{i}
is the endpoint of a ray in
\mathcal{C}(\sigma)
) or
\mu_{i}
is even (which occurs when the subdiagram supported on the vertices
t_{i-1}+1,t_{i-1}+2,\ldots,t_{i}
is a connected standard noncrossing matching in
\mathcal{C}(\sigma)
). When
\mu_{i}\geq 2
we call the vertices
\{t_{i-1}+1,t_{i-1}+2,\ldots,t_{i}\}
a

. We say that the set
\{t_{i-1}+1,t_{i-1}+2,\ldots,t_{i-1}+\frac{\mu_{i}}{2}\}
is the

of the block and that
\{t_{i-1}+\frac{\mu_{i}}{2}+1,t_{i-1}+\frac{\mu_{i}}{2}+2,\ldots,t_{i}\}
is the

. For example, in the cup diagram from Example

{7,8,9,10}
form a block with first half
\{7,8\}
and second half
\{9,10\}
.

Definition 3.14
.

Let
\tau_{\mu}\in S_{n}
be the permutation with one-line notation that lists (in increasing order) all endpoints of rays and all indices in the first half of every block determined by the prime decomposition of
\sigma
, followed by all indices appearing in the second half of any block (in increasing order).

If
\sigma
and
\mathcal{C}(\sigma)
are as in Example
2.14
, then
\mu_{1}=\mu_{4}=4
and
\mu_{2}=\mu_{3}=1
so
\tau_{\mu}=[1,2,5,6,7,8,3,4,9,10]
.
Note that the permutation
\tau_{\mu}
is uniquely determined by the composition
\mu
defined by the size of each prime factor of
\sigma
, and not on the prime factors themselves. For example, any prime tableau
\sigma
of shape
(\ell,\ell)
will have
\tau_{\mu}
equal to the identity.

Recall that for all
i\in[n]
,
c(i)
is the number of completed cups that appear at or before vertex
i
in
\mathcal{C}(\sigma)
and
r(i)
is the number of rays that appear at or before vertex
i
in
\mathcal{C}(\sigma)
.

Lemma 3.15
.

Suppose
\sigma
is a standard tableau of shape
(n-\ell,\ell)
with prime decomposition
\sigma=\sigma_{1}\circ\sigma_{2}\circ\cdots\circ\sigma_{k}
. Let
\mu_{i}=|\sigma_{i}|
and
t_{i}=\mu_{1}+\mu_{2}+\cdots+\mu_{i}
for all
1\leq i\leq k
. The permutation
\tau_{\mu}
of Definition
3.14
is given explicitly by the following formulas.

(1)

If
t=t_{i}
is the endpoint of a ray in
\mathcal{C}(\sigma)
, then

\tau_{\mu}^{-1}(t)=r(t)+c(t).

(2)

If
\mu_{i}\geq 2
is even and
t=t_{i-1}+j
for some
1\leq j\leq\frac{\mu_{i}}{2}
occurs in the first half of the block
\{t_{i-1}+1,t_{i-1}+2,\ldots,t_{i}\}
in
\mathcal{C}(\sigma)
, then

\tau_{\mu}^{-1}(t)=r(t_{i-1})+c(t_{i-1})+j.

(3)

If
\mu_{i}\geq 2
is even and
t=t_{i-1}+\frac{\mu_{i}}{2}+j
for some
1\leq j\leq\frac{\mu_{i}}{2}
occurs in the second half of the block
\{t_{i-1}+1,t_{i-1}+2,\ldots,t_{i}\}
in
\mathcal{C}(\sigma)
, then

\tau_{\mu}^{-1}(t)=n-\ell+c(t_{i-1})+j.

Proof.

Suppose
\mu_{i}\geq 2
. The number of entries in the first half of the block
\{t_{i-1}+1,t_{i-1}+2,\ldots,t_{i}\}
(which is, of course, also the number entries in the second half of the
i
-th block) is
\frac{\mu_{i}}{2}
. The key fact used in the proof below is that, since the subdiagram of
\mathcal{C}(\sigma)
on the
\mu_{i}
vertices
\{t_{i-1}+1,t_{i-1}+2,\ldots,t_{i}\}
is the standard matching
\mathcal{C}(\sigma_{i})
, there are
\frac{\mu_{i}}{2}
cups in
\mathcal{C}(\sigma_{i})
.

By definition, the first
n-\ell
entries of the one-line notation for the permutation
\tau_{\mu}
list, in order, all endpoints of rays and all indices in the first half of every block.

Suppose
\mu_{i}=1
and
t=t_{i}
is the endpoint of a ray. The number
r(t_{i-1})+c(t_{i-1})
is equal to the number of rays and complete cups that have already appeared, which is precisely the number of earlier rays plus the number of indices in the first half of the earlier blocks. The fact that
t
is the endpoint of a ray implies
r(t)+c(t)=r(t_{i-1})+c(t_{i-1})+1
, and the formula in (1) follows.

Next, suppose
\mu_{i}\geq 2
and
t=t_{i-1}+j
is the
j
-th entry in the first half of the corresponding block. The position of
t
in the one-line notation of
\tau_{\mu}
is exactly the total number of completed cups and rays appearing before the start of the block plus
j
. Since the total number of completed cups and rays appearing before block
i
is
r(t_{i-1})+c(t_{i-1})
, the formula from (2) follows.

By definition, the last
\ell
entries of the one-line notation for the permutation
\tau_{\mu}
list, in order, all the indices in the second half of every block.
Suppose
\mu_{i}\geq 2
and
t=t_{i-1}+\frac{\mu_{i}}{2}+j
is the
j
-th entry in the second half of the corresponding block. The sum total number of entries in the second half of every earlier block is given by the number of completed cups appearing before block
i
, which is
c(t_{i-1})
. Thus, the position of
t
in the one-line notation of
\tau_{\mu}
is equal to
n-\ell+c(t_{i-1})+j
, which proves (3).
∎

For a composition
\mu=(\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\ldots,\mu_{k})
we consider the inclusion map

(3.12)

\displaystyle\iota:GL_{\mu_{1}}(\mathbb{C})\times GL_{\mu_{2}}(\mathbb{C})\times\cdots\times GL_{\mu_{k}}(\mathbb{C})\hookrightarrow GL_{n}(\mathbb{C})

defined by embedding each
GL_{\mu_{i}}(\mathbb{C})
as a diagonal block of
GL_{n}(\mathbb{C})
. The inclusion map induces a closed embedding of flag varieties,
\bar{\iota}:\mathcal{F}\ell_{\mu_{1}}(\mathbb{C})\times\mathcal{F}\ell_{\mu_{2}}(\mathbb{C})\times\cdots\times\mathcal{F}\ell_{\mu_{k}}(\mathbb{C})\hookrightarrow\mathcal{F}\ell_{n}(\mathbb{C})
.

Lemma 3.17
.

Let
\sigma=\sigma_{1}\circ\cdots\circ\sigma_{k}
be the prime decomposition of
\sigma
and
\iota
denote the inclusion map from (
3.12
) above. Then

\iota(F_{\sigma_{1}},F_{\sigma_{2}},\ldots,F_{\sigma_{k}})=\dot{\tau}_{\mu}F_{\sigma}.

In particular,
\iota
induces an isomorphism
\mathcal{B}_{\sigma_{1}}\times\mathcal{B}_{\sigma_{2}}\times\cdots\times\mathcal{B}_{\sigma_{k}}\simeq\mathcal{B}_{\sigma}
.

Proof.

Denote the
t
-th column of a matrix
A\in F_{\sigma}
by
v_{t}
; recall that the vector
v_{t}
is defined explicitly using
\mathcal{C}(\sigma)
in Definition
2.13
. The product
\dot{\tau}_{\mu}A
has columns
\dot{\tau}_{\mu}(v_{1}),\dot{\tau}_{\mu}(v_{2}),\ldots,\dot{\tau}_{\mu}(v_{n})
. We compute
\dot{\tau}_{\mu}(v_{t})
for each
t
by considering cases: either
t
is the endpoint of a ray, the right endpoint of a cup, or the left endpoint of a cup in
\mathcal{C}(\sigma)
.

When
t
is the endpoint of a ray,
v_{t}=e_{r(t)+c(t)}
and Lemma
3.15
(1) yields
\dot{\tau}_{\mu}(v_{t})=e_{\tau_{\mu}(r(t)+c(t))}=e_{t}
.

When
t
is the right endpoint of a cup, we also have
v_{t}=e_{r(t)+c(t)}
. Since
t
is incident to a cup, it must appear in some block of
\sigma
. Say
t
is in the block
\{t_{i-1}+1,t_{i-1}+2,\ldots,t_{i}\}
for some
i
with
\mu_{i}\geq 2
. This means the positive integer
t-t_{i-1}
is an entry of the prime factor
\sigma_{i}
of
\sigma
. Since
r(t)+c(t)
counts the total number of complete cups and rays that appear up through vertex
t
, we have that
r(t)+c(t)=r(t_{i-1})+c(t_{i-1})+c_{i}(t-s_{i-1})
where
c_{i}
denotes the cup count for the diagram
\mathcal{C}(\sigma_{i})
of the prime factor
\sigma_{i}
. We know the connected component of the matching
\mathcal{C}(\sigma)
on the block
\{t_{i-1}+1,t_{i-1}+2,\ldots,t_{i}\}
is the standard matching associated to
\sigma_{i}
and has exactly
\frac{\mu_{i}}{2}
many cups, so
c_{i}(t-t_{i-1})\leq\frac{\mu_{i}}{2}
. Thus,
t_{i-1}+c_{i}(t-t_{i-1})
is in the first half of the block and applying Lemma
3.15
(2) with
j=c_{i}(t-t_{i-1})
yields

\tau_{\mu}(r(t)+c(t))=\tau_{\mu}(r(t_{i-1})+c(t_{i-1})+c_{i}(t-t_{i-1}))=t_{i-1}+c_{i}(t-t_{i-1}).

We conclude
\dot{\tau}_{\mu}(v_{t})=e_{t_{i-1}+c_{i}(t-t_{i-1})}
. Note, in particular, that the only nonzero entries of the column
\dot{\tau}_{\mu}(v_{t})
for
t
in the block
\{t_{i-1}+1,t_{i-1}+2,\ldots,t_{i}\}
are in rows indexed by the same set.

When
t
is the left endpoint of a cup, we denote the right endpoint by
\Sigma(t)
and let
\alpha=\alpha(t<\Sigma(t))
. By Definition
2.13
we have

v_{t}=a_{t}e_{r(t)+c(t)+1}+a_{t_{1}}e_{r(t)+c(t)+2}+\cdots+a_{t_{\alpha}}e_{r(t)+c(t)+\alpha+1}+e_{n-\ell+c(t)+\alpha+1}.

First, consider the indices of the form
r(t)+c(t)+b
for
1\leq b\leq\alpha+1
. As above, since
t
is adjacent to a cup it appears in a block of
\sigma
. Say
t
is in the block
\{t_{i-1}+1,t_{i-1}+2,\ldots,t_{i}\}
for some
i
with
\mu_{i}\geq 2
. Letting
c_{i}
denote the cup count on
\mathcal{C}(\sigma_{i})
, we have

r(t)+c(t)+b=r(t_{i-1})+c(t_{i-1})+c_{i}(t-s_{i-1})+b.

Because the numbers
c_{i}(t-t_{i-1})
and
\alpha=\alpha(t<\Sigma(t))
count distinct sets of cups in
\mathcal{C}(\sigma_{i})
and since neither of these numbers counts the cup
(t<\Sigma(t))
we have that
c_{i}(t-t_{i-1})+b\leq c_{i}(t-t_{i-1})+\alpha+1\leq\frac{\mu_{i}}{2}
. In particular, the number
t_{i-1}+c_{i}(t-t_{i-1})+b
must be in the first half of the block. Applying Lemma
3.15
(2) with
j=c_{i}(t-t_{i-1})+b
yields

\tau_{\mu}(r(t)+c(t)+b)=\tau_{\mu}(r(t_{i-1})+c(t_{i-1})+c_{i}(t-t_{i-1})+b)=t_{i-1}+c_{i}(t-t_{i-1})+b.

Next, consider the index
n-\ell+c(t)+\alpha+1=n-\ell+c(t_{i-1})+c_{i}(t-t_{i-1})+\alpha+1
. We showed above that
c_{i}(t-t_{i-1})+\alpha+1\leq\frac{\mu_{i}}{2}
. Thus
t_{i-1}+\frac{\mu_{i}}{2}+c_{i}(t-t_{i-1})+\alpha+1
appears in the second half of the block and applying Lemma
3.15
(3) with
j=c_{i}(t-t_{i-1}+\alpha+1)
,

\displaystyle\tau_{\mu}(n-\ell+c(t)+\alpha+1)

\displaystyle=\tau_{\mu}(n-\ell+c(t_{i-1})+c_{i}(t-t_{i-1})+\alpha+1)

\displaystyle=t_{i-1}+\frac{\mu_{i}}{2}+c_{i}(t-t_{i-1})+\alpha+1.

In summary, we have proved that

\displaystyle\dot{\tau}_{\mu}(v_{t})

\displaystyle=a_{t}e_{t_{i-1}+c_{i}(t-t_{i})+1}+a_{t_{1}}e_{t_{i-1}+c_{i}(t-t_{i})+2}+\cdots

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad+a_{t_{\alpha}}e_{t_{i-1}+c_{i}(t-t_{i})+\alpha+1}+e_{t_{i-1}+\frac{\mu_{i}}{2}+c_{i}(t-t_{i-1})+\alpha+1}.

Once again, we observe that the only nonzero entries of the column
\dot{\tau}_{\mu}(v_{t})
for
t
in the block
\{t_{i-1}+1,t_{i-1}+2,\ldots,t_{i}\}
are only in rows indexed by the same set.

Consider the matrix
\dot{\tau}_{\mu}A
. Our computations above show the matrix is block diagonal. If
t
is the endpoint of a cup, then it corresponds to a
1\times 1
block equal to
[1]
. When
\mu_{i}\geq 2
is even, then the diagonal block of
A
corresponding to the rows and columns indexed by
\{t_{i-1}+1,t_{i-1}+2,\ldots,t_{i}\}
is the
\mu_{i}\times\mu_{i}
matrix with columns equal to
v_{j}=e_{c_{i}(j)}
when
j
is the right endpoint of a cup, and if
j
is the left endpoint of a cup then,

v_{j}=a_{t}e_{c_{i}(j)+1}+a_{t_{1}}e_{c_{i}(j)+2}+\cdots+a_{t_{\alpha}}e_{c_{i}(j)+\alpha+1}+e_{\frac{\mu_{i}}{2}+c_{i}(j)+\alpha+1}

where
j=t-t_{i-1}
. We have
\alpha=\alpha(t,\Sigma(t))=\alpha(j,\Sigma(j))
since the number of cups nested above
(t,\Sigma(t))
in
\mathcal{C}(\sigma)
and the number of cups nested above
(j,\Sigma(j))
in
\mathcal{C}(\sigma_{i})
are equal. Note that we can write

v_{j}=(0^{c_{i}(j)},a_{t},a_{t_{1}},\ldots,a_{\alpha},0^{\frac{\mu_{i}}{2}},1,0^{\mu_{i}-c_{i}(j)-\alpha-1}).

Comparing with Definition
2.13
for the prime tableau
\sigma_{i}
, we conclude the diagonal submatrix corresponding to the rows and columns indexed by
\{t_{i-1}+1,t_{i-1}+2,\ldots,t_{i}\}
is a matrix representative for the flags in
F_{\sigma_{i}}
and the proof of the first statement of the lemma is complete.

The second statement follows immediately from Theorem
2.17
, since

\mathcal{B}_{\sigma}\simeq\dot{\tau}_{\mu}\mathcal{B}_{\sigma}=\overline{\dot{\tau}_{\mu}F_{\sigma}}\simeq\overline{F_{\sigma_{1}}}\times\overline{F_{\sigma_{2}}}\times\cdots\times\overline{F_{\sigma_{k}}}=\mathcal{B}_{\sigma_{1}}\times\mathcal{B}_{\sigma_{2}}\times\cdots\times\mathcal{B}_{\sigma_{k}}

This completes the proof.
∎

4.3.
Statement of the main theorem

With the definition of the ideal
\mathcal{I}_{\sigma}
in hand, we are ready to state the main theorem of this paper.
Let
N
be a nilpotent matrix of Jordan type
\lambda=(n-\ell,\ell)
and suppose
N
is in Jordan canonical form; see (
2.1
).
We consider the action of the subgroup
B
of upper triangular matrices in
GL_{n}(\mathbb{C})
on
\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{z}]
induced by right multiplication. Let
\pi:GL_{n}(\mathbb{C})\to GL_{n}(\mathbb{C})/B
denote the projection map. The main theorem of this paper is:

Theorem 4.13
.

Let
\ell
be a positive integer with
1\leq\ell\leq\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor
and
\sigma\in\mathrm{SYT}(n-\ell,\ell)
. The ideal
\mathcal{I}_{\sigma}
is right
B
-invariant and
\pi^{-1}(\mathcal{B}_{\sigma})=\mathcal{V}(\mathcal{I}_{\sigma})\cap GL_{n}(\mathbb{C})
.

This is a restatement of Theorem
1
from the Introduction. The proof consists of two main parts. We prove
B
-invariance in Section
5
. We then study the vanishing set of
\mathcal{I}_{\sigma}
in Section
6
.

5.
B
-invariance of
\mathcal{I}_{\sigma}

In this section, we take our first step in the proof of Theorem
4.13
. We show that
\mathcal{I}_{\sigma}
is invariant under the action of
B
on
\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{z}]
induced by right multiplication of matrices.

Recall that
B=TU\subset GL_{n}(\mathbb{C})
where
T
is the subgroup of diagonal matrices and
U
is generated by the matrices

(5.1)

\displaystyle b_{q,r}=I_{n}+cE_{q,r},\;1\leq q<r\leq n,\,\text{ and }\,c\in\mathbb{C}

where
E_{q,r}
is the
n\times n
elementary matrix with unique nonzero entry equal to
1
in row
q
and column
r
.
The group
B
acts on
M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
by right multiplication and there is an induced action on
\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{z}]
. This action multiplies columns by any nonzero scalar and adds any scalar multiple of a column to ‘later’ ones. For example, the action of the matrix from (
5.1
) on the
r
-th column is

\begin{bmatrix}z_{1,r}\\
z_{2,r}\\
\vdots\\
z_{n,r}\end{bmatrix}\cdot b_{q,r}=\begin{bmatrix}z_{1,r}+cz_{1,q}\\
z_{2,r}+cz_{2,q}\\
\vdots\\
z_{n,r}+cz_{n,q}\end{bmatrix}.

We begin with a lemma which simplifies our computations later.

Lemma 5.1
.

For any positive integer
a\geq 2
and set of indeterminates
\{v_{i,j}^{k}\mid i,j\in\{1,2\}\text{ and }0\leq k\leq a\}
, we have

(5.2)

\displaystyle v_{1,1}^{0}v_{2,2}^{0}+\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{a-1}\begin{vmatrix}v_{1,1}^{k}&v_{1,2}^{k}\\
v_{2,1}^{k}&v_{2,2}^{k}\end{vmatrix}-v_{2,1}^{a}v_{1,2}^{a}=\sum_{k=0}^{a-1}\begin{vmatrix}v_{1,1}^{k}&v_{1,2}^{k+1}\\
v_{2,1}^{k+1}&v_{2,2}^{k}\end{vmatrix}.

Proof.

We use induction on
a
to show the identity (
5.2
). When
a=2
,

\displaystyle v_{1,1}^{0}v_{2,2}^{0}+\begin{vmatrix}v_{1,1}^{1}&v_{1,2}^{1}\\
v_{2,1}^{1}&v_{2,2}^{1}\end{vmatrix}-v_{1,2}^{2}v_{2,2}^{2}

\displaystyle=v_{1,1}^{0}v_{2,2}^{0}+v_{1,1}^{1}v_{2,2}^{1}-v_{1,2}^{1}v_{2,1}^{1}-v_{1,2}^{2}v_{2,1}^{2}

\displaystyle=\begin{vmatrix}v_{1,1}^{0}&v_{1,2}^{1}\\
v_{2,1}^{1}&v_{2,2}^{0}\end{vmatrix}+\begin{vmatrix}v_{1,1}^{1}&v_{1,2}^{2}\\
v_{2,1}^{2}&v_{2,2}^{1}\end{vmatrix}.

Now suppose the identity (
5.2
) holds for some
a
with
a\geq 2
. Then,

\displaystyle v_{1,1}^{0}v_{2,2}^{0}+

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{a}\begin{vmatrix}v_{1,1}^{k}&v_{1,2}^{k}\\
v_{2,1}^{k}&v_{2,2}^{k}\end{vmatrix}-v_{1,2}^{a+1}v_{2,1}^{a+1}

\displaystyle=v_{1,1}^{0}v_{2,2}^{0}+\sum_{k=1}^{a-1}\begin{vmatrix}v_{1,1}^{k}&v_{1,2}^{k}\\
v_{2,1}^{k}&v_{2,2}^{k}\end{vmatrix}+v_{1,1}^{a}v_{2,2}^{a}-v_{1,2}^{a}v_{2,1}^{a}-v_{2,1}^{a+1}v_{1,2}^{a+1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{a-1}\begin{vmatrix}v_{1,1}^{k}&v_{1,2}^{k+1}\\
v_{2,1}^{k+1}&v_{2,2}^{k}\end{vmatrix}+v_{1,1}^{a}v_{2,2}^{a}-v_{1,2}^{a+1}v_{2,1}^{a+1}\quad\text{ (by the induction hypothesis)}

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{a}\begin{vmatrix}v_{1,1}^{k}&v_{1,2}^{k+1}\\
v_{2,1}^{k+1}&v_{2,2}^{k}\end{vmatrix}.

Thus, the identity (
5.2
) holds by induction.
∎

The next lemma reduces the proof of
B
-invariance to the rectangular case.

Lemma 5.2
.

Suppose
\mathcal{I}_{\sigma}
is
B
-invariant for all
\sigma\in\mathrm{SYT}(\ell,\ell)
. Then
\mathcal{I}_{\sigma}
is
B
-invariant for all
\sigma\in\mathrm{SYT}(n-\ell,\ell)
.

Proof.

Let
\sigma=\sigma_{1}\circ\sigma_{2}\circ\cdots\circ\sigma_{k}
be the prime decomposition of
\sigma
. Recall that
\mu_{i}=|\sigma_{i}|
and
t_{i}=\mu_{1}+\cdots+\mu_{i}
, with the convention that
t_{0}=0
. As the action of
\tau_{\mu}
on
M_{n}
by left multiplication by
\dot{\tau}_{\mu}
permutes rows, but not columns, it suffices to prove that the ideal
\mathcal{I}_{\sigma}^{\prime}=\mathcal{K}_{\mu}+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathcal{I}_{\sigma_{i}}^{\prime}
from Definition
4.8
is right
B
-invariant, where

\mathcal{K}_{\mu}:=\left<z_{i,j}\mid t_{p-1}+1\leq j\leq t_{p}<i\,\text{ for all }\,p=1,\ldots,k-1\right>.

A matrix belongs to
\mathcal{V}(\mathcal{K}_{\mu})
if and only if it is block upper-triangular with blocks of size
\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\ldots,\mu_{k}
. Right multiplication by any upper-triangular matrix preserves this block upper-triangular form, so
\mathcal{K}_{\mu}
is
B
-invariant.

Consider the decomposition
B=B^{\prime}U^{\prime}
where
B^{\prime}
is the subgroup of
B
of block diagonal matrices with blocks of sizes
\mu_{1}
,
\mu_{2},\ldots,\mu_{k}
and
U^{\prime}
is the subgroup of strictly block upper triangular matrices relative to the same block sizes.
In other words,
B^{\prime}
is the Borel subgroup of the Levi subgroup
\iota(GL_{\mu_{1}}(\mathbb{C})\times\cdots\times GL_{\mu_{k}}(\mathbb{C}))
and
U^{\prime}
is the unipotent radical of the parabolic subgroup of
GL_{n}(\mathbb{C})
corresponding to this Levi subgroup.

For each

\mu_{i}\geq 2
, the ideal
\mathcal{I}_{\sigma_{i}}^{\prime}
is the image of the ideal
\mathcal{I}_{\sigma_{i}}
corresponding to the rectangular tableau
\sigma_{i}
under the map which shifts every index by

. Thus each

is an ideal in the polynomial ring with indeterminates

where

\{t_{i-1}+1,t_{i-1}+2,\ldots,t_{i}\}
.
Each

. Right multiplication by

only affects entries of

in the strictly upper triangular blocks. Thus,

acts trivially on

-invariant (where

) implies

-invariant. We conclude

is right

-invariant, as desired.
∎

Proposition 5.3

Next we argue invariance under the action from (2). We treat the cases of generators in the ideals
\mathcal{J}_{\sigma}

The following lemma is a straightforward application of the rank-nullity theorem.

Lemma 6.4
.

Suppose
T:\mathbb{C}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}^{n}
is a linear transformation and
A
and
B
are subspaces of
\mathbb{C}^{n}
such that
B\subseteq A
and
T(A)\subseteq B
. If

\dim A=\dim B+\dim\,\operatorname{ker}(T)

then

(i)

T(A)=B
and

(ii)

A=T^{-1}(B)
.

The next two results use the linear algebraic statements of Lemma
6.4
to obtain two corollaries of Proposition
6.3
. Recall that
F_{i}=\mathbb{C}\{a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{i}\}
denotes the subspace spanned by the first
i
columns of
A
.

Corollary 6.5
.

Suppose
\sigma
is a rectangular tableau and
A\in\operatorname{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{C})\cap\mathcal{V}(\mathcal{I}_{\sigma})
.
For each cup
(i<j)
of
\mathcal{C}(\sigma)
with size
\delta(i<j)\geq 2
,
\dim N^{m(i)}\left(F_{i}\right)=\alpha(i<j)+1
and

N^{-m(i)}\left(N^{m(i)}\left(F_{i}\right)\right)=F_{i}.

Proof.

We claim
i=\dim F_{i}=\dim N^{m(i)}\left(F_{i}\right)+2\,m(i)
. Given this claim, the second part of the corollary follows from Lemma
6.4
(
ii
) with
A=F_{i}
,
B=N^{m(i)}\left(F_{i}\right)
, and
T=N^{m(i)}
since
\dim\operatorname{ker}N^{m(i)}=2\,m(i)
.

By Lemma
3.7
we have

(6.14)

\displaystyle i-2\,m(i)=i-2S_{\sigma}(i)+2\alpha(i<j)+2=\alpha(i<j)+1.

We know
N^{m(i)}\left(F_{i}\right)
is a subspace of the column space of the extended cup matrix
\widetilde{A}_{(i<j)}
. By Proposition
6.3
,
\operatorname{rk}\widetilde{A}_{(i<j)}\leq\alpha(i<j)+1
and thus
\dim N^{m(i)}\left(F_{i}\right)\leq\alpha(i<j)+1
. On the other hand, since
\dim F_{i}=i
and
\dim\operatorname{ker}N^{m(i)}=2m(i)
we have

\displaystyle\dim N^{m(i)}\left(F_{i}\right)

\displaystyle\geq i-2m(i)=\alpha(i<j)+1

where the last equality follows from (
6.14
). This proves

(6.15)

\displaystyle\dim N^{m(i)}\left(F_{i}\right)=\alpha(i<j)+1,

which proves the first part of the corollary.
The claim follows from (
6.14
) and (
6.15
), and proves the second part.

,
\dim N^{m(k^{*})}\left(F_{k^{*}}\right)=\alpha(k^{*}<\Sigma(k^{*}))+1
and therefore (
6.16
) tells us

(6.17)

N^{m(k^{*},k)}(a_{k})\in N^{m(k^{*})}\left(F_{k^{*}}\right).

Notice that

\displaystyle m(k^{*},k)

\displaystyle=(m(k^{*},k)-m(k^{*}))+m(k^{*})=S_{\sigma}(k)-S_{\sigma}(k^{*})+m(k^{*}).

Using this, (
6.17
) becomes

\displaystyle N^{m(k^{*})}\left(N^{S_{\sigma}(k)-S_{\sigma}(k^{*})}(a_{k})\right)\in N^{m(k^{*})}(F_{k^{*}})

\displaystyle\quad\quad\Rightarrow N^{S_{\sigma}(k)-S_{\sigma}(k^{*})}(a_{k})\in N^{-m(k^{*})}\left(N^{m(k^{*})}(F_{k^{*}})\right)\quad\text{(by definition of preimage)}

(6.18)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\Rightarrow N^{S_{\sigma}(k)-S_{\sigma}(k^{*})}(a_{k})\in F_{k^{*}}\quad\text{(by Corollary~\ref{cor.preimage}).}

If
k
is the left endpoint of a cup and
(k^{*}<\Sigma(k^{*}))
is the unique cup directly above
(k<\Sigma(k))
then

S_{\sigma}(k)-S_{\sigma}(k^{*})=\frac{1}{2}\left(k-k^{*}+1\right)

by Lemma
3.4
. Now (
6.1
) yields statement (i).

On the other hand, if
k
is the right endpoint of a cup
(\Sigma^{-1}(k)<k)
and
(k^{*}<\Sigma(k^{*}))
is the unique cup directly above it then combining Lemmas
3.4
and
3.3
gives us

\displaystyle S_{\sigma}(k)-S_{\sigma}(k^{*})

\displaystyle=S_{\sigma}(k)-S_{\sigma}(\Sigma^{-1}(k))+S_{\sigma}(\Sigma^{-1}(k))-S_{\sigma}(k^{*})

\displaystyle=\delta(\Sigma^{-1}(k)<k)-1+\frac{1}{2}\left(\Sigma^{-1}(k)-k^{*}+1\right)

\displaystyle=\delta(\Sigma^{-1}(k)<k)+\frac{1}{2}\left(\Sigma^{-1}(k)-k^{*}-1\right).

Now (
6.1
) implies statement (ii).
∎

Our next proposition combines the previous two corollaries to prove our key technical result: any invertible matrix in
\mathcal{V}(\mathcal{I}_{\sigma})
satisfies the conditions defining
\mathcal{B}_{\sigma}
from Fung’s Proposition
2.9
.

Proposition 6.7
.

Let
\sigma
be a prime standard tableau of shape
(\ell,\ell)
and suppose
A\in\mathcal{V}(\mathcal{I}_{\sigma})\cap GL_{n}(\mathbb{C})
. Let
F_{\bullet}
be the flag represented by
A
, so
F_{i}=\mathbb{C}\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{i}\}
for all
i=1,\ldots,n
where
a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n}
denote the columns of
A
. For each cup
(i<j)
in
\mathcal{C}(\sigma)
we have:

(i)

N\left(a_{k}\right)\in F_{k-1}
for all
k\leq j
and

(ii)

N^{\delta(i<j)}\left(F_{j}\right)=F_{i-1}
.

Proof.

Notice that
F_{i-1}\subseteq F_{j}
and

\displaystyle\dim F_{i-1}+\dim\operatorname{ker}N^{\delta(i<j)}

\displaystyle=(i-1)+2\delta(i<j)

\displaystyle=(i-1)+(j-i+1)=j=\dim F_{j},

Thus to prove (
ii
) it suffices, by Lemma
6.4
, to show that
N^{\delta(i<j)}(F_{j})\subseteq F_{i-1}
. Lemma
6.4
also implies that if
N^{\delta(i<j)}(F_{j})\subseteq F_{i-1}
, then

(6.19)

\displaystyle F_{j}=N^{-\delta(i<j)}(F_{i-1}).

In particular, we get that (
6.19
) holds whenever (
ii
) does, which is useful in the proof below.

We proceed by induction on
j
, that is, by induction on the right endpoint of the cup
(i<j)
. Our base case is the cup
(i<j)
such that
j
is minimal. Any such cup must have size
\delta(i<j)=1
, so
i=j-1
.
The assumption that
j
is minimal also implies that every
k
with
1\leq k\leq i=j-1
is the left endpoint of a cup and the unique cup above each such cup
(k<\Sigma(k))
is
(k-1<\Sigma(k-1))
. In particular, by Corollary
6.6
(
i
) we have

(6.20)

\displaystyle N^{\frac{1}{2}(k-k^{*}+1)}(a_{k})\in F_{k^{*}}\Rightarrow N(a_{k})\in F_{k-1}\quad\text{ for all $1\leq k\leq i=j-1$.}

The cup directly above
(j-1,j)
is
(j-2<\Sigma(j-2))
. Thus Corollary
6.6
(
ii
) implies

(6.21)

\displaystyle N^{\frac{1}{2}((j-1)-(j-2)-1)+\delta(j-1<j)}(a_{j})\in F_{j-2}\Rightarrow N(a_{j})\in F_{j-2}.

This also implies
N(a_{j})\in F_{j-1}
since
F_{j-2}\subset F_{j-1}
. Together with (
6.20
) this gives us
N(a_{k})\in F_{k-1}
for all
k\leq j
, proving (
i
) in the base case. We also have
N(F_{j-1})\subseteq F_{i-1}=F_{j-2}
by (
6.20
) and
N(a_{j})\in F_{j-2}
by (
6.21
) and thus
N(F_{j})\subseteq F_{j-2}
, proving (
ii
) in this case. This concludes the proof of the base case.

Now suppose
(i<j)
is an arbitrary cup in
\mathcal{C}(\sigma)
and that statements (
i
) and (
ii
) hold for all cups
(i^{\prime}<j^{\prime})
such that
j^{\prime}<j
. Assume that
j<n
. (We address the case
j=n
, which is much more straightforward, below.) Since
\sigma
is prime, we may assume that there exists a cup
(i^{*}<j^{*})
directly above
(i<j)
.

We use the induction hypothesis to prove three auxiliary claims:

•

Claim 1:
If
1<k\leq i
is the left endpoint of a cup and
(k^{*}<\Sigma(k^{*}))
is the unique cup directly above the cup
(k<\Sigma(k))
, then
N^{\frac{1}{2}(k-k^{*}-1)}(F_{k-1})=F_{k^{*}}
and
F_{k-1}=N^{-\frac{1}{2}(k-k^{*}-1)}(F_{k^{*}})
.

•

Claim 2:

N^{\delta(i<j)-1}(F_{j-1})=F_{i}
and
F_{j-1}=N^{-\delta(i<j)+1}(F_{i})
.

•

Claim 3:

N^{\delta(i<j)}(a_{j})\in F_{i-1}
.

Proof of Claim 1:
Any cup that appears between
k^{*}
and
k
is of the form
(k^{\prime}<\Sigma(k^{\prime}))
where
k^{*}<k^{\prime}<\Sigma(k^{\prime})<k
. In particular, the induction hypothesis applies to the cup
(k^{\prime}<\Sigma(k^{\prime}))
and there are
\frac{1}{2}(k-k^{*}-1)
cups with this property. Let
(i_{1}<j_{1}),(i_{2}<j_{2}),\cdots,(i_{t}<j_{t})
be the sequence of cups such that
(k^{*}<\Sigma(k^{*}))
the unique cup directly above each one and such that
i_{1}=k^{*}+1
,
i_{r}=j_{r-1}+1
for all
2\leq r\leq t
, and
j_{t}=k-1
. By the induction hypothesis applied using statement (
ii
) to each such cup we have

(6.22)

N^{\delta(i_{r}<j_{r})}(F_{j_{r}})=F_{i_{r}-1}\quad\text{ for all $1\leq r\leq t$}.

Since there are
\frac{1}{2}(k-k^{*}-1)
total cups between
k^{*}
and
k
,

\delta(i_{1}<j_{1})+\delta(i_{2}<j_{2})+\cdots+\delta(i_{t}<j_{t})=\frac{1}{2}(k-k^{*}-1)

and thus (
6.22
) gives us the first equality of Claim 1. The second follows by applying Lemma
6.4
(
ii
).

Proof of Claim 2:
If
\delta(i<j)=1
then the statement holds trivially, so we may assume
\delta(i<j)\geq 2
. Let
(i_{1}<j_{1}),(i_{2}<j_{2}),\cdots,(i_{t}<j_{t})
be the sequence of cups nested inside the cup
(i<j)
, with no other cup between them. That is,
i_{1}=i+1
,
i_{r}=j_{r-1}+1
for all
2\leq r\leq t
, and
j_{t}=j-1
.
Applying the induction hypothesis with statement (
ii
) to each such cup we have

(6.23)

\displaystyle N^{\delta(i_{r}<j_{r})}(F_{j_{r}})=F_{i_{r}-1}\;\text{ for all }1\leq r\leq t.

Since

\delta(i<j)-1=\delta(i_{1}<j_{1})+\delta(i_{2}<j_{2})+\cdots+\delta(i_{t}<j_{t}),

we obtain the first equality of Claim 2 from (
6.23
). The second follows by Lemma
6.4
(
ii
).

Proof of Claim 3:
Recall that
(i^{*}<j^{*})
denotes the cup immediately above the cup
(i<j)
.
Since
j
is the right endpoint of a cup with left endpoint
i
, by Corollary
6.6
(
ii
) applied with
(\Sigma^{-1}(k)<k)=(i<j)
and
(k^{*}<\Sigma(k^{*}))=(i^{*}<j^{*})
we have

N^{\frac{1}{2}(i-i^{*}-1)+\delta(i<j)}(a_{j})\in F_{i^{*}}\ .

By definition of the preimage and Claim 1 applied with
k=i
we get

\displaystyle N^{\delta(i<j)}(a_{j})\in N^{-\frac{1}{2}(i-i^{*}-1)}\left(F_{i^{*}}\right)=F_{i-1},

as desired.

We can now prove the induction step for the cup
(i<j)
with
j<n
:

Proof of (
i
) for the cup
(i<j)
:

Let
m<j
be the largest right endpoint of a cup that appears before
j
. By the induction hypothesis applied to
(\Sigma^{-1}(m)<m)
,
N(a_{k})\in F_{k-1}
for all
1\leq k\leq m
. To complete the proof of (
i
), it suffices to prove that
N(a_{k})\in F_{k-1}
for all
k
such that
m+1\leq k\leq j
.

First, assume
\delta(i<j)=1
, so
(i<j)
has no other cups nested inside it. Thus,
m<i=j-1
and each
k
with
m+1\leq k\leq i=j-1
is the left endpoint of a cup, by maximality of
m
. By Corollary
6.6
(
i
) we get that

\displaystyle N^{\frac{1}{2}(k-k^{*}+1)}(a_{k})\in F_{k^{*}}\;\text{ for all }\;m+1\leq k\leq i=j-1

where
(k^{*}<\Sigma(k^{*}))
is the cup directly above the cup with left endpoint
k
. Thus for all
k
with
m+1\leq k\leq j-1
we have

\displaystyle N^{\frac{1}{2}(k-k^{*}-1)}\left(N(a_{k})\right)\in F_{k^{*}}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow N(a_{k})\in N^{-\frac{1}{2}(k-k^{*}-1)}(F_{k^{*}})\quad\text{(by definition of preimage) }

\displaystyle\Rightarrow N(a_{k})\in F_{k-1}\quad\text{ (by Claim~1).}

Now it only remains to show that
N(a_{j})\in F_{j-1}
, but this follows immediately from Claim 3 since
\delta(i<j)=1
and
F_{i-1}=F_{j-2}\subseteq F_{j-1}
. Thus,
N(a_{k})\in F_{k-1}
for all
k\leq j
whenever
(i<j)
has size
1
.

Now assume
\delta(i<j)\geq 2
. There must be at least one cup nested inside of
(i<j)
, in particular,
j-1
must be the right endpoint of such a cup. Thus
m=j-1
in this case and to complete the proof of (
i
) we need only prove that
N(a_{j})\in F_{j-1}
. Since
F_{i-1}\subseteq F_{i}
we have

\displaystyle N^{\delta(i<j)}(a_{j})\in F_{i}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow N^{\delta(i<j)-1}\left(N(a_{j})\right)\in F_{i}\quad\text{ (by Claim~3)}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow N(a_{j})\in N^{-\delta(i<j)+1}(F_{i})\quad\text{ (by definition of preimage) }

\displaystyle\Rightarrow N(a_{j})\in F_{j-1}\quad\text{ (by Claim~2). }

We have now established that
N(a_{k})\in F_{k-1}
for all
k\leq j
, proving statement (
i
) for the cup
(i<j)
.

Proof of (
ii
) for the cup
(i<j)
:

By statement (
i
) for the cup
(i<j)
, we have
N\left(F_{i}\right)\subseteq F_{i-1}
. Now Claim 2 yields

N^{\delta(i<j)}\left(F_{j-1}\right)=N\left(F_{i}\right)\subseteq F_{i-1}.

Thus,
N^{\delta(i<j)}(F_{j})\subseteq F_{i-1}
follows immediately from
N^{\delta(i<j)}(a_{j})\in F_{i-1}
, which is true by Claim 3. Thus statement (
ii
) holds for the cup
(i<j)
and our proof of the induction step for

is complete.

Finally, we prove the induction step for the cup
(i<j)
with
j=n
. Since
\sigma
is prime, we have
(i<j)=(1<n)
. Now
n-1
must be the right endpoint of a cup, and
N(a_{k})\in F_{k-1}
for all
k\leq n-1
by the induction hypothesis. This implies
F_{n-1}
is an
N
-stable hyperplane. Because
N
is nilpotent, we conclude
N(a_{n})\in F_{n-1}
. Thus, statement (
i
) holds for the cup
(1<n)
. Since
\delta(1<n)=\ell
and
N^{\ell}=0
we also have
N^{\ell}(F_{n})=\{0\}=F_{0}
, proving (
ii
) is also true in this case.

The proof is now complete.
∎

We can now prove that any matrix
A
with
A\in\mathcal{V}(\mathcal{I}_{\sigma})\cap GL_{n}(\mathbb{C})
represents a flag in the component
\mathcal{B}_{\sigma}
.

Corollary 6.8
.

Let
\pi:GL_{n}(\mathbb{C})\to GL_{n}(\mathbb{C})/B
denote the projection map from the group of
n\times n
invertible matrices to the flag variety,
\sigma
be a standard tableau of shape
(n-\ell,\ell)
, and
\mathcal{I}_{\sigma}
be the ideal associated to
\sigma
. Then

\mathcal{V}(\mathcal{I}_{\sigma})\cap GL_{n}(\mathbb{C})\subseteq\pi^{-1}(\mathcal{B}_{\sigma})

where
\mathcal{B}_{\sigma}
denotes the irreducible component of the Springer fiber
\mathcal{B}_{N}
indexed by
\sigma
.

Proof.

By Lemma
6.1
, it suffices to prove the statement when
\sigma
is prime. Consider the flag
F_{\bullet}
represented by a matrix
A\in\mathcal{V}(\mathcal{I}_{\sigma})\cap GL_{n}(\mathbb{C})
. Statement (
i
) of Proposition
6.7
implies
N(F_{i})\subseteq F_{i-1}
for all
i
, so
\pi(A)\in\mathcal{B}_{N}
. Statement (
ii
) of Proposition
6.7
and Proposition
2.9
together imply
\pi(A)\in\mathcal{B}_{\sigma}
.
∎

6.2.
The containment
\pi^{-1}(\mathcal{B}_{\sigma})\subseteq\mathcal{V}(\mathcal{I}_{\sigma})

The results in the previous subsection show that if
A
is an invertible matrix in
\mathcal{V}(\mathcal{I}_{\sigma})
then
A\in\pi^{-1}(\mathcal{B}_{\sigma})
. We now show that the reverse containment holds as well. The next two results focus on the case where
\sigma
is a rectangular tableau.

Lemma 6.9
.

For all
\sigma\in\mathrm{SYT}(\ell,\ell)
, the Springer–Schubert cell
F_{\sigma}
is contained in
\mathcal{V}(\mathcal{J}_{\sigma})
, where
\mathcal{J}_{\sigma}
is the monomial ideal from Definition
4.1
.

Proof.

Recall that
z_{i,j},z_{i+\ell,j}\in\mathcal{J}_{\sigma}
if and only if
S_{\sigma}(j)<\ell
and

S_{\sigma}(j)+1\leq i\leq\ell.

Let
A\in F_{\sigma}
and given
p\in[n]
, let
v_{p}
denote the
p
-th column of
A
. Assume
S_{\sigma}(p)<\ell
and let
i
be such that
S_{\sigma}(p)+1\leq i\leq\ell
. We aim to prove the
i
and
i+\ell
entries of
v_{p}
are
0
.

Suppose
p
is the left end point of a cup
(p<\Sigma(p))
in
\mathcal{C}(\sigma)
and let
\alpha=\alpha(p,\Sigma(p))
. By definition, the entries of
v_{p}
in rows
r
for
1\leq r\leq c(p)
and
c(p)+\alpha+2\leq r\leq c(p)+\alpha+\ell
and
c(p)+\alpha+\ell+2\leq r\leq n
are all
0
. By Lemma
3.6
,

(6.24)

\displaystyle S_{\sigma}(p)=c(p)+\delta(p<\Sigma(p))+\alpha

so

i\geq S_{\sigma}(p)+1\Rightarrow i\geq c(i)+\delta(p<\Sigma(p))+\alpha+1\Rightarrow i\geq c(i)+\alpha+2

where the last implication follows from the fact that
\delta(p<\Sigma(p))\geq 1
. We also get that
i+\ell\geq c(i)+\alpha+\ell+2
. Using the description of the zeros in
v_{p}
above, it follows that the entries of
v_{p}
in rows
i
and
i+\ell
are both
0
.

Now suppose
p
is the right end point of a cup in
\mathcal{C}(\sigma)
. Then
v_{p}=e_{c(p)}
so the only nonzero entry of
v_{p}
occurs in position
c(p)
. Note that
c(p)\leq\ell
. Thus, to argue the

and

entries of

are

, it suffices to show that

. This is obviously true by (
6.24
) above.

We have shown the
(i,j)
and
(i+\ell,j)
entries of

are

whenever

, proving

.
∎

Proposition 6.10
.

\sigma\in\mathrm{SYT}(\ell,\ell)
and suppose
(i<j)
is a cup of size

in
\mathcal{C}(\sigma)
. The Springer–Schubert cell

We will show that the evaluation of the generators of the ideal

at any matrix

in

is zero. For each

, let

denote the

-th column of the matrix

. We consider the matrix

from (

) on the matrix

. The columns of

Suppose

with

) the

\displaystyle=c(\Sigma^{-1}(p))+\delta(\Sigma^{-1}(p)<p)+\alpha(\Sigma^{-1}(p)<p)-\alpha(i<j)+t

\displaystyle=c(p)+\left[\alpha(\Sigma^{-1}(p)<p)-\alpha(i<j)\right]+t

, we know that

. Since

, the entry of

in row

is
0

Recall that for every cup

For each standard tableau

Proof.

If

.

7.
Conjectures on the cohomology class of
\mathcal{B}_{\sigma}

In this section, we use the ideal
\mathcal{I}_{\sigma}
to conjecture two formulas for the cohomology class
[\mathcal{B}_{\sigma}]
of the component
\mathcal{B}_{\sigma}
whenever
\sigma
has two rows. We prove our conjectures for a particular family of noncrossing matchings (the “one big cup” family) and corresponding tableaux.

7.1.
Cohomology classes via combinatorial commutative algebra

Recall that for each subvariety
Y
of the flag variety
\mathcal{F}\ell_{n}(\mathbb{C})
, there is a well defined cohomology class
[Y]\in H^{*}(\mathcal{F}\ell_{n}(\mathbb{C});\mathbb{Z})
. Let
I^{+}
be the ideal in
\mathbb{Z}[x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}]
generated by constant-free symmetric polynomials.
The Borel presentation
[
Borel
]
:

H^{*}(\mathcal{F}\ell_{n}(\mathbb{C});\mathbb{Z})\simeq\mathbb{Z}[x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}]/I^{+}

allows us to identify the class
[Y]
as a polynomial in
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
, defined uniquely up to the quotient by
I^{+}
(see also
[
Manivel
,
BilleyGaoPawlowski25
]
).

Let
\sigma\in\mathrm{SYT}(n-\ell,\ell)
. We can use the ideal
\mathcal{I}_{\sigma}
to compute the cohomology class of each irreducible component
\mathcal{B}_{\sigma}
. To do this, we leverage combinatorial commutative algebra in the polynomial ring
\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{z}]
using techniques of Knutson–Miller
[
KnutsonMiller2005
]
. Define a
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
-grading on the polynomial ring
\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{z}]
by
\operatorname{deg}\left(z_{ij}\right)=e_{j}
, where
e_{j}
is the
j
-th coordinate vector in
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
. By construction, the ideal
\mathcal{I}_{\sigma}
defined in Section
4
is homogeneous with respect to this grading. This means that we can associate a graded Hilbert series to the scheme
\operatorname{Spec}\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{z}]/\mathcal{I}_{\sigma}
. For a formal definition of the graded Hilbert series we refer the reader to
[
MillerSturmfels2005
, §8.1, 8.2]
.

The graded Hilbert series determines the
multidegree

\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{z}]/\mathcal{I}_{\sigma};\mathbf{x})
of
\operatorname{Spec}\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{z}]/\mathcal{I}_{\sigma}
. The multidegree is a polynomial in
\mathbb{Z}[\mathbf{x}]=\mathbb{Z}\left[x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{d}\right]
of degree
\dim\left(\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{z}]/\mathcal{I}_{\sigma}\right)
, defined using a particular shift and truncation of the graded Hilbert series. A precise definition can be found in
[
MillerSturmfels2005
, §8.5]
. The following lemma tells us that the multidegree of
\operatorname{Spec}\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{z}]/\mathcal{I}_{\sigma}
determines the cohomology class
[\mathcal{B}_{\sigma}]
. Recall that
\pi:GL_{n}(\mathbb{C})\rightarrow GL_{n}(\mathbb{C})/B\simeq\mathcal{F}\ell_{n}(\mathbb{C})
denotes the natural projection. The following is
[
KnutsonMiller2005
, Corollary 2.3.1]
; see also
[
ITW18
, Proposition 3]
.

Lemma 7.1

(Knutson–Miller)
.

Let
Y\subseteq\mathcal{F}\ell_{n}(\mathbb{C})
be a closed scheme, and let
\tilde{Y}
be a closed subscheme of the affine scheme
M_{n}=\operatorname{Spec}\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{z}]
of
n\times n
matrices such that
\tilde{Y}\cap GL_{n}(\mathbb{C})=\pi^{-1}(Y)
. Then the cohomology class
[Y]
in
H^{*}(\mathcal{F}\ell_{n}(\mathbb{C});\mathbb{Z})
is represented by the multidegree
\mathcal{C}(\tilde{Y};\mathbf{x})
with respect to the grading
\operatorname{deg}\left(z_{ij}\right)=e_{j}
.

In the next section, we apply this lemma with
Y=\mathcal{B}_{\sigma}
and
\tilde{Y}=\operatorname{Spec}\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{z}]/\mathcal{I}_{\sigma}
. Lemma
7.1
applies because Theorem
4.13
shows that for every two-row tableaux
\sigma
, the associated ideal
\mathcal{I}_{\sigma}
is such that
\pi^{-1}(\mathcal{B}_{\sigma})=\mathcal{V}(\mathcal{I}_{\sigma})\cap GL_{n}(\mathbb{C})
. As a result, we can compute
[\mathcal{B}_{\sigma}]
for two-row tableaux to using commutative algebra. The data so obtained motivates two conjectural formulas, which appear in the next section.

The Schubert polynomials, defined by Lascoux and Schützenberger
[
LascouxSchutzenberger1982
]
, form a basis for the polynomial ring

The polynomial
\mathfrak{S}_{w}(\mathbf{x})

\mathfrak{S}_{w}(\mathbf{x})=\begin{cases}x_{1}^{n-1}x_{2}^{n-2}\cdots x_{n-2}^{2}x_{n-1}&\text{ if }w=w_{0}\\
\partial_{i}\mathfrak{S}_{ws_{i}}(\mathbf{x})&\text{ if }w(i)<w(i+1),\end{cases}

where

is the longest permutation,
s_{i}
is the simple reflection exchanging
i
and

\partial_{i}
acts on

by

\partial_{i}f(\mathbf{x})=\frac{f(\mathbf{x})-s_{i}f(\mathbf{x})}{x_{i}-x_{i+1}}.

Note that
\partial_{i}^{2}=0
and
\partial_{i}\partial_{j}=\partial_{j}\partial_{i}
for all
i
and
j
with
|i-j|\geq 2
.
