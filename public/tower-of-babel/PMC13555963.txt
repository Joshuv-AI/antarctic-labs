BehaviorDiff: a VAE–diffusion framework for AI-generated synthetic behavioral data in procrastination prediction for sports instruction
Authors: Qiang Fang, Yan Li, Yue Yin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccurate prediction of procrastination remains a critical challenge in sports instruction, where behavioral data are often limited and constrained by privacy concerns. This study investigates whether AI-generated synthetic data can approximate real-world behavioral patterns to predict procrastination under controlled experimental conditions. A three-stage framework was developed. First, a multimodal dataset (MAP-487) was constructed from 487 university athletes, integrating IMU-derived physiological signals, training adherence records, and psychological self-reports. Second, a hybrid Variational Autoencoder–Diffusion model (BehaviorDiff) was trained to generate synthetic sequences that preserve key statistical and temporal characteristics of the original data. Third, identical BiLSTM–Transformer models were trained and evaluated on real and synthetic datasets using consistent preprocessing, feature space, and train–test splits to ensure comparability. Results show that models trained on synthetic data achieve performance comparable to those trained on real data (accuracy: 0.94 vs 0.91), with the observed difference not reaching statistical significance (p = 0.072). Additional analyses, including correlation-based validation, indicate that synthetic data preserves essential behavioral relationships and predictive structure. The observed performance differences are interpreted as a consequence of smoothing and regularization effects introduced during data generation rather than an increase in informational content. The findings suggest that synthetic data can serve as a privacy-preserving and structurally consistent complement to real-world data for behavioral modeling in sports instruction. However, conclusions are limited to controlled experimental settings, and further validation across independent datasets is required to establish generalizability.Keywords: AI-generated data, Procrastination prediction, Sports instruction, BiLSTM, Diffusion models, Self-regulation, Synthetic behavioral data, IMU time series, Educational technology

Introduction

Procrastination is defined as the voluntary delay of intended actions despite awareness of potential negative consequences. While extensively studied in psychology, it remains underexplored in sports instruction and coaching analytics, where behavioral consistency is critical for performance outcomes [1]. In athletic learning environments, procrastination manifests as irregular practice patterns, delayed task completion, and reduced engagement in structured training cycles, which can negatively affect performance stability, injury recovery, and psychological readiness [2, 3]. Despite its relevance, the quantitative modeling of procrastination in sports contexts remains limited, primarily due to challenges in collecting large-scale, privacy-compliant, and longitudinal behavioral data [4]. Recent advances in Artificial Intelligence (AI) have demonstrated strong potential for modeling complex behavioral dynamics from multimodal data streams [5]. For instance, prior studies have applied deep learning to IMU-based time-series data to capture latent patterns related to fatigue and performance variability. Similarly, research on AI-assisted learning environments has identified relationships between chatbot usage and procrastination tendencies, as well as the role of AI-guided self-regulation in mitigating delayed task engagement [6]. Collectively, these studies establish that procrastination can be represented as a measurable temporal and behavioral process. However, two major limitations persist: (i) the scarcity of large-scale, privacy-preserving behavioral datasets in sports environments, and (ii) the presence of noise and imbalance in real-world data, which can limit model generalizability [7, 8].

Synthetic data generation has emerged as a potential solution to these challenges. Techniques such as Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs), Variational Autoencoders (VAEs), and diffusion models enable the creation of artificial datasets that preserve statistical dependencies and temporal structure while avoiding privacy risks [9, 10]. Although prior work has explored both procrastination modeling and synthetic data generation independently, there is limited research examining whether synthetic behavioral data can reliably preserve complex temporal patterns associated with procrastination while maintaining predictive consistency in downstream tasks. To address this gap, this study proposes a unified framework that integrates generative modeling (VAE and diffusion) with sequence-based prediction (BiLSTM–Transformer) for procrastination modeling in sports instruction. The primary objective is to evaluate whether synthetic data can approximate real-world behavioral dynamics under controlled experimental conditions, with a focus on fidelity, stability, and predictive alignment. Unlike prior studies, this work explicitly compares models trained on real and synthetic datasets using identical experimental settings, providing a structured assessment of synthetic data as a privacy-preserving complement rather than a replacement for real-world data.

This study proposes the effectiveness of AI-generated synthetic data for predicting procrastination behavior in sports instruction under a controlled experimental framework. Specifically, it compares models trained on synthetic behavioral data with those trained on real-world datasets using multiple evaluation criteria, including predictive accuracy, calibration, and interpretability. To guide this comparison, the study defines a practical performance criterion: models trained on synthetic data are expected to achieve accuracy within an absolute deviation of 5 percentage points (Δ ≤ 0.05) relative to models trained on real data. This threshold is used as a heuristic benchmark for comparability rather than a formal non-inferiority margin. Accordingly, the analysis is interpreted as a descriptive assessment of performance alignment rather than a statistical equivalence test. The evaluation is conducted using a BiLSTM–Transformer hybrid model trained on real and synthetic datasets with identical feature space, preprocessing, and experimental conditions. The study addresses three key research questions: (i) to what extent AI-generated synthetic data can replicate the temporal and behavioral dynamics of procrastination in sports settings; (ii) how predictive models trained on synthetic data compare to those trained on real data in terms of accuracy, calibration, and interpretability; and (iii) the implications of using synthetic data as a privacy-preserving complement for behavioral analytics in sports instruction.

Literature review and theoretical foundations

The use of AI in sports science, educational behavior, and synthetic data provides important insights into the modeling of human performance and procrastination. The field of sports analytics relies on AI and sensor-based data collection. In this regard, a study depicted by [11] presents a deep learning framework for predicting fatigue and stamina from IMU-based multivariate time series. It used triaxial acceleration, angular velocity, and additional physiological variables to capture moment-by-moment variations in athlete performance. This work is relevant because of the structural properties between fatigue prediction and procrastination prediction. Both involve temporal drift, gradual behavioral destabilization, and changes in task engagement across time. Evaluation shows that AI models can extract latent behavioral patterns from motion signals and infer athletes' hidden mental and physical states. Another important contribution is highlighted in [12], which combines results from various AI applications in endurance sports. It highlights that AI systems are effective at integrating multimodal data, including continuous glucose monitoring and genetic data. Additionally, it pointed out challenges such as low model generalizability due to the similarity between training and sensor data, and reliability issues with the sensors. This implies the necessity of increasingly varied datasets and more extensive external validation. In addition to these data-based solutions, the research presented in [13] focuses on the hardware component of the system and investigates AI-optimized energy materials used in sporting gear. These studies provide a basis for using temporal modeling techniques to predict motivational delay in sports instruction.

While most research on procrastination focuses on academic settings, it also provides important insights for sports learning. Schei et al. [14] conducted a scoping review of how students use AI chatbots during learning. They found that students who interacted frequently with AI tools postponed tasks more often and showed weaker self-regulation. These students also demonstrated inconsistent learning rhythms and longer gaps between attempts at tasks. This pattern closely relates to procrastination behavior in sports instruction, where learners avoid practice sessions, delay processing feedback, and disengage after failed attempts. The current study incorporates variables such as session delay, interaction intervals, and feedback latency to mirror the behavioral markers identified in this review. Procrastination is strongly linked to self-regulation, which affects how learners manage their emotions, control their impulses, and remain committed during sports instruction and training tasks. Wu et al. [15] explored this aspect and showed that various AI-guided learning aids, such as ChatGPT-based interventions, are used to improve students' self-monitoring skills and reduce their tendency to delay tasks. Evaluation highlights the importance of support signals, including prompt feedback, structured guidance, and adaptive pacing, in influencing tendencies toward procrastination. This relationship guides the design of input features in the current study. Variables such as self-regulation score, consistency of practice sessions, and the rate of feedback response were modeled based on the identified mechanisms. Their work provides a psychological and structural foundation for predicting procrastination behaviors using machine learning architectures.

Synthetic data generation is used to model sensitive behaviors while protecting privacy. A study by Balog and Zhai [16] discusses user simulation using different AI agents. It involves creating AI agents that mimic human users to generate synthetic data and evaluate AI systems in a controlled manner. This approach has implications for developing and testing interactive AI technologies across various fields. The use of synthetic data is not limited to education. The authors in [17] addressed the challenge of creating training data for AI in autonomous driving. It involves generating and automatically labeling images from 3D virtual scenarios. It uses the UrbanSyn dataset to systematically generate variations of synthetic images to test how specific factors in the rendering pipeline affect the performance of a semantic segmentation model.

Several recent studies inform key methodological aspects of data handling, labeling, and learning in complex environments. For example, the deep learning-based active learning approach in [18] reduces annotation effort by iteratively selecting high-confidence samples. At the same time, the EADN model in [19] applies CNN–LSTM architectures to capture spatiotemporal patterns in sequential data. Similarly, the work in [20] integrates synthetic data generation with attention mechanisms to address data scarcity, and [21] proposes a quality-aware embedding framework for evaluating data fidelity using structured ranking. While these studies demonstrate the effectiveness of deep learning, synthetic data, and temporal modeling, they primarily focus on improving classification performance, reducing labeling cost, or enhancing data quality in generic settings. They do not explicitly investigate whether synthetic data can preserve behavioral realism, temporal dynamics, and predictive consistency in human-centered applications such as procrastination modeling. Furthermore, this study focuses on the behavioral validity of synthetic data. It evaluates whether generated sequences can replicate temporal patterns, psychological dynamics, and predictive relationships observed in real athlete behavior. This perspective extends beyond performance optimization and positions synthetic data as a structured, testable proxy for real behavioral processes, which form the core motivation and novelty of this work.

These studies collectively demonstrate that synthetic data can capture complex behavioral patterns, including variations in motivation, delay cycles, and engagement dynamics, providing a useful foundation for modeling human behavior. However, several important gaps remain. First, existing research in sports analytics predominantly focuses on physical performance indicators such as fatigue and stamina, with limited attention to procrastination as a behavioral outcome. Second, studies investigating AI and procrastination are largely confined to educational or general learning contexts and do not incorporate sports-specific environments or sensor-based multimodal data. Third, there is limited work evaluating whether AI-generated synthetic behavioral data can reliably preserve procrastination-related temporal patterns and support downstream predictive modeling. In particular, direct comparisons between models trained on real and synthetic behavioral datasets under identical controlled conditions remain largely unexplored. To address these gaps, this study conducts a structured evaluation of synthetic data for procrastination prediction in sports instruction. By analyzing real and synthetic datasets using the same feature space, preprocessing pipeline, and model architecture, the study examines whether synthetic data can approximate real behavioral patterns and achieve comparable predictive performance. The findings are interpreted within a controlled experimental setting, positioning synthetic data as a privacy-preserving complement rather than a replacement for real-world data.

System model and problem formulation

The proposed study is structured as a multi-stage pipeline designed to enable a controlled comparison between real and synthetic behavioral data. The workflow consists of five main components: (i) real dataset construction, (ii) synthetic data generation, (iii) predictive model training, (iv) baseline model comparison, and (v) performance evaluation. In the first stage, real-world behavioral data are collected from athletes and organized into a structured multivariate time-series dataset. Each sample corresponds to a single athlete represented as a fixed-length temporal sequence. In the second stage, a synthetic dataset of identical structure and dimensionality is generated using the proposed generative framework. This ensures that both datasets share the same feature space and temporal resolution. In the third stage, predictive models based on a BiLSTM–Transformer architecture are trained independently on the real and synthetic datasets under identical experimental conditions, including preprocessing, feature representation, and train–test splits. In parallel, multiple baseline models are implemented using the same input features and evaluation protocol to ensure fair comparison. In the final stage, all models are evaluated using a consistent set of performance metrics, including accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, correlation, and calibration error. Statistical comparisons are conducted across repeated runs to assess performance variability and alignment between real-trained and synthetic-trained models. Figure 1 illustrates the overall framework. The pipeline begins with real data acquisition and synthetic data generation, followed by feature extraction and sequence modeling. Both datasets are processed through an identical learning pipeline to ensure that observed performance differences arise from the data source rather than variations in model design or preprocessing. The resulting predictions are analyzed to evaluate the extent to which synthetic data can approximate real-world behavioral dynamics under controlled experimental conditions.

Major components of the research study

All components receive the same feature space, preprocessing steps, and the same train–test splits. This ensures that the difference in performance is due to the data source (i.e., real vs. synthetic) and is not dependent on changes in model design. These components are described in detail below.

Real dataset collection

The real dataset used in this study was collected from 487 university athletes participating in structured sports instruction programs. The cohort represents diverse demographic and training profiles, including variations in age, gender, sport type, and training level, which provide contextual variability in physical load, motivation, and behavioral tendencies relevant to the analysis of procrastination. Procrastination labels were generated using a structured rule-based procedure applied at the athlete-sequence level. Each athlete is represented by a single multivariate time series spanning a 12-week training program, consisting of 84 time steps (sessions), and is assigned one binary label indicating procrastination behavior. Participants were included if they (i) were enrolled in a full-time university sports program, (ii) completed at least 85% of scheduled training sessions, and (iii) provided valid IMU sensor recordings for at least 80 out of 84 sessions. Athletes with incomplete sensor logs or missing psychological check-ins for more than four weeks were excluded. Only participants with complete records were retained, resulting in a final dataset of 487 athletes with no mid-study dropouts. The final label distribution consists of 214 procrastination cases (43.9%) and 273 non-procrastination cases (56.1%), indicating a moderately balanced dataset. The labeling procedure combines three independent behavioral indicators: submission delay, training adherence, and weekly self-regulation score, as summarized in Table 2. Submission delay captures the extent of delay in uploading assigned tasks via the digital platform; training adherence reflects consistency in completing scheduled training sessions recorded through the IMU band; and the self-regulation score is derived from weekly psychological check-ins that assess focus, impulse control, and task management. For each athlete, these indicators were aggregated over the full observation period. A binary procrastination label was assigned only when at least two of the three criteria met predefined thresholds (Table 2). This multi-criteria rule reduces sensitivity to noise and prevents isolated abnormal observations from determining the final label, thereby improving robustness and consistency in label assignment.

In addition to behavioral and psychological signals, this study incorporates a physiological workload indicator to better interpret athlete behavior under varying training intensity. The Acute Chronic Workload Ratio (ACWR) is included as a contextual measure to capture short-term workload spikes relative to longer-term training exposure. ACWR is computed as the ratio of short-term (acute) to longer-term (chronic) workload, using rolling averages. Specifically, the acute workload is defined as the total workload accumulated over a 7-day window. This threshold is not arbitrarily selected; it is supported by prior findings in sports science, which show that values above 1.5 are commonly associated with increased fatigue or injury risk. In this study, the threshold serves as an interpretive reference rather than a strict decision boundary. Importantly, ACWR is not used as a direct input feature in the predictive model. Instead, it is applied as a post-hoc analytical signal to examine whether predicted procrastination patterns align with periods of increased physiological stress. Analysis of real dataset

Synthetic dataset generation

After constructing the real dataset, a synthetic dataset was generated to match the original data in terms of structure, dimensionality, and temporal resolution. The aim was to produce artificial sequences that preserve statistical patterns and behavioral dynamics while preventing any linkage to individual participants [28, 29]. This enables a controlled comparison between models trained on real and synthetic data. The synthetic data were generated using a hybrid framework combining a Variational Autoencoder (VAE) and a diffusion-based model (BehaviorDiff). The VAE captures the global latent structure of behavioral sequences, while the diffusion model refines temporal dynamics through iterative noise modeling and reconstruction [30, 31]. These components operate sequentially to balance structural representation and temporal realism. Synthetic samples replicate the real dataset format, where a multivariate sequence of 84 time steps with identical feature definitions represents each athlete. The generative models produce only feature sequences; labels are assigned separately using the same rule-based procedure applied to the real dataset. A sequence is labeled as procrastination if at least two of the three criteria, submission delay, training adherence, and self-regulation score, meet predefined thresholds. To quantitatively measure the similarity between real and synthetic data distributions, Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD) is used [35, 36]. It is a nonparametric statistical metric that assesses whether two datasets are drawn from the same underlying distribution. It compares their mean embeddings in a reproducing kernel Hilbert space. Unlike simple distance measures, MMD captures higher-order distributional differences and is widely used for validating generative models.

A threshold of 0.03 ensures that synthetic sequences are statistically indistinguishable from real athlete behavior. Furthermore, to assess the risk of re-identification and memorization, a privacy audit was conducted using two standard descriptive tests. First, a distance-to-nearest-neighbor (DNN) analysis was performed by computing the minimum Euclidean distance between each synthetic sequence and all real sequences in the normalized feature space. The resulting distribution showed no near-zero distances, indicating that no synthetic sample closely replicates an original individual trajectory.

Second, a membership inference risk test was performed by training a binary classifier to distinguish whether a given sample originated from the real or synthetic dataset. The classifier achieved an AUC of 0.53, close to random guessing (0.5), suggesting that synthetic samples are statistically indistinguishable from real ones and do not reveal membership information. These results provide empirical evidence that the generator does not memorize training examples. Reliability analysis and calibration error

The statistical analysis in this study was designed to compare the predictive performance of models trained on real and synthetic datasets within a controlled, reproducible framework. All experiments were conducted with 10 independent repeated runs, each using a different random 80/20 train–test split at the athlete level. Performance metrics were computed exclusively on the held-out test set and summarized as mean and standard deviation across runs. To evaluate differences in model performance, a paired t-test was applied to the metric values obtained across the repeated runs, comparing real-trained and synthetic-trained versions of the same model. This approach accounts for variability introduced by different data splits while maintaining a consistent comparison framework. In addition, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used as a nonparametric alternative to validate robustness without assuming normality of the performance differences. To control for multiple comparisons across evaluation metrics, the Bonferroni correction was applied to adjust the significance threshold. Effect size was estimated using Cohen's d, providing a measure of the magnitude of differences independent of sample size. Since the study is not designed as a formal non-inferiority or equivalence test, results are interpreted cautiously. In cases where statistical significance is not achieved (e.g., p > 0.05), observed differences are reported as numerical variations rather than evidence of superiority. The statistical comparison figure provides a descriptive summary (mean ± standard deviation), while formal hypothesis testing results are reported in the text. Discussion

The evaluation of the proposed study delivers a clear and surprising answer to the central question of this study regarding procrastination. The answer is yes: synthetic data can replicate real procrastination dynamics. The real-world validation presented in this study is conducted as an observational analysis rather than a controlled experimental trial. The data were collected from multiple cohorts of university athletes participating in structured sports instruction programs over 12 weeks. No explicit intervention or randomized group assignment was performed. Instead, all participants followed their standard training routines, and the proposed model was applied retrospectively to analyze behavioral patterns. The cohorts referenced in this study represent naturally occurring groups rather than experimentally controlled populations. As such, no formal control or treatment groups were defined, and no baseline matching or stratified allocation was conducted. The analysis, therefore, focuses on descriptive comparisons of behavioral trends rather than causal inference. These endpoints are designed to assess whether the model produces reliable and interpretable predictions under real-world conditions. Metrics such as injury reduction or performance improvement are not directly measured outcomes of this study and are not used as primary endpoints. All findings in this section are interpreted as descriptive evidence of applicability, rather than proof of real-world effectiveness. The absence of controlled experimental design, randomization, and baseline comparability limits the ability to draw causal conclusions. Future work will extend this analysis using controlled longitudinal studies with predefined endpoints and intervention protocols.

The proposed study provides a clear answer to the central question regarding procrastination modeling. The results indicate that synthetic data can replicate real procrastination dynamics and achieve predictive performance comparable to that of real data. In some cases, it produces smoother and more stable learning curves across Precision, Recall, F1-score, and Accuracy. This effect is attributed to smoothing and regularization introduced during data generation, rather than improved informational content. This happened because synthetic sequences captured sharper boundaries between normal delay and true procrastination [37, 38]. It also reduces false alarms that commonly arise in naturally noisy human behavior. Real data came with emotional drift, inconsistent motivation, and irregular task engagement [39]. Synthetic data distilled these fluctuations into structured behavioral signals, enabling the model to learn faster. Values of the indicators are shown in Table 7.

Although the synthetic-trained model exhibits slightly higher numerical performance on certain metrics, this should not be interpreted as indicating that synthetic data contains more informative content than real data. Since the synthetic dataset is generated entirely from the real data, it cannot exceed the informational capacity of its source. The observed difference is more plausibly explained by the smoothing effect of the generative model, which reduces measurement noise and extreme fluctuations in the original signals. As a result, the synthetic data may act as a regularized approximation of the real distribution, leading to improved generalization under controlled experimental conditions. This behavior does not imply superior representational power, but rather reflects the stabilizing properties of the generator. This study is based on several assumptions. First, it assumes that the MAP-487 dataset is representative of athlete behavioral patterns during structured training programs. Second, it assumes that the generative models (VAEs and diffusion models) can capture and reproduce the underlying statistical and temporal structure of the real data. Third, the rule-based labeling strategy is assumed to provide a reasonable approximation of procrastination behavior, although it may not capture all psychological nuances.

The study also has several limitations. All experiments are conducted on a single dataset, and evaluation is performed in a closed-loop setting without external validation, which limits generalizability. The proposed pipeline combines multiple components, but no ablation study is conducted to isolate their individual contributions. In addition, while synthetic data is evaluated for statistical similarity and predictive consistency, no formal privacy risk assessment (e.g., re-identification analysis) is performed. Finally, the use of rule-based labels may introduce simplifications that do not fully reflect the real-world complexity of behavior. These limitations define the scope of the current work and highlight directions for future research.

A key limitation of this study is that all experiments are conducted on a single dataset (MAP-487) using repeated random splits. The evaluation remains within a closed-loop setting, where both real and synthetic data share the same underlying distribution. As a result, the findings primarily demonstrate internal consistency and structural fidelity rather than cross-domain generalizability. External validation on independent datasets is required to assess the robustness of the proposed framework under different populations, sensing conditions, and behavioral contexts. Another limitation of this study is the absence of an ablation analysis to quantify the individual contribution of each component in the proposed pipeline. While the combined VAE–diffusion framework is designed to capture both structural and temporal aspects of the data, it remains unclear whether simpler configurations (e.g., VAE-only generation) could achieve comparable performance. Future work will evaluate reduced variants of the model to assess component-level impact.

Conclusion

This study examined whether AI-generated synthetic data can support the prediction of procrastination behavior in sports instruction. The results show that models trained on synthetic data achieve comparable performance to those trained on real data across multiple metrics, including accuracy, F1-score, calibration error, and correlation analysis. The strong alignment between real-trained and synthetic-trained prediction curves indicates that synthetic data preserves key temporal and behavioral patterns. Statistical testing further confirms that the observed differences are not significant (p > 0.05), suggesting that synthetic data can serve as a reliable approximation under controlled conditions. The main strength of this study lies in its comprehensive evaluation framework, which combines predictive performance, temporal consistency, and calibration analysis to assess the quality of synthetic data. In addition, the use of generative models enables privacy-aware data utilization in sensitive behavioral contexts. However, the study has several limitations, including reliance on a single dataset, evaluation within a closed-loop setting, and the absence of ablation analysis to isolate component contributions. These findings suggest that synthetic data can be effectively used as a complementary tool for behavioral modeling when real data is limited or sensitive, particularly in applications such as sports instruction analytics and digital learning environments. However, the results should be interpreted within the current experimental setup, and further validation is required for broader generalization. Future work will focus on extending the framework to multimodal behavioral synthesis, incorporating physiological, motion, and interaction data for richer modeling. In addition, domain-adaptive generation techniques will be explored to improve generalization across different environments. These directions will support the development of scalable, privacy-aware, and behaviorally robust data-driven systems.
