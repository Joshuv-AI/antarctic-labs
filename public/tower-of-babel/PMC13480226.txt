Leadless Pacemaker Dysfunction From Interference by a Retained Transvenous Pacemaker With Active Minute Ventilation Sensor
Authors: Faisal Naffa’, Binaya Basyal, Cyrus Hadadi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLeadless pacemakers reduce complications of transvenous leads. However, interactions between leadless pacemakers and retained transvenous cardiac implantable electronic devices remain an underrecognized issue.Case SummaryA 72-year-old woman with complete heart block underwent dual-chamber pacemaker implantation in 2004 and generator changes and right ventricular lead revision in 2013 for fracture. In 2024, due to lead noise during isometric exercise and concern for lead integrity, leadless pacemaker was implanted without extracting existing system per patient preference. Postimplant the patient reported fatigue and exertional dyspnea initially attributed to COVID-19 or chronotropic incompetence. Device interrogation showed only 9% implant-to-implant (i2i) communication suggesting impaired atrioventricular synchrony. Further evaluation revealed interference from a retained Boston Scientific pacemaker with an active minute ventilation sensor. Deactivation of the sensor restored i2i communication and resolved symptoms.DiscussionTo our knowledge, this is the first reported case of atrioventricular asynchrony specifically resulting from impaired i2i communication in a leadless pacemaker due to interference from a minute ventilation sensor.Take-Home MessageComprehensive pre-implantation assessment of cardiac devices is essential to prevent device-device interactions and ensure safe leadless pacemaker function.

History of Presentation

A 72-year-old woman with a history of complete heart block underwent dual-chamber pacemaker implantation in 2004, followed by generator replacements in 2011, 2013, and 2020. In August 2013, she developed a right ventricular (RV) lead fracture, prompting urgent new RV lead implantation with generator replacement; lead extraction was not performed because it was deemed high risk.Take-Home Messages•Retained transvenous pacing systems with active minute ventilation sensors may contribute to unintended electrical interaction within the intracardiac environment, potentially disrupting implant-to-implant communication in leadless pacemaker systems.•Comprehensive pre-implantation evaluation of all implanted devices and their programmable sensing features is essential to prevent device-device interaction and ensure safe leadless pacemaker function.

Past Medical History

Past medical history included complete heart block, dual-chamber pacemaker (2004), RV lead fracture (2013), multiple generator replacements, coronary artery disease status post percutaneous coronary intervention to the right coronary artery, mitral regurgitation, Hodgkin lymphoma status post radiation therapy, history of multinodular goiter resection.

Investigations

Device interrogation revealed only 9% implant-to-implant (i2i) communication, raising concerns about atrioventricular (AV) synchrony loss (Figure 2). Management

Initial management focused on symptom attribution to chronotropic incompetence and postviral fatigue. However, device interrogation raised concern for impaired AV synchrony. Outcome and Follow-Up

Following deactivation of the MV sensor, i2i communication improved significantly, restoring AV synchrony. The patient reported complete resolution of fatigue and exertional dyspnea on follow-up.

Discussion

Leadless pacemakers were developed to overcome complications associated with transvenous leads, such as fractures, infections, and venous obstruction.1,2 However, interactions between leadless pacemakers and retained transvenous cardiac implantable electronic devices remain an underrecognized issue.

MV sensors are designed to adjust pacing rates based on respiratory activity to improve exercise tolerance.3 These sensors function by generating subthreshold impedance-based signals between the generator and lead to measure transthoracic impedance. This electrical activity may be conducted through surrounding tissues, including the intracardiac blood pool, where it can manifest as low-amplitude electrical noise detectable by intracardiac devices.

The Abbott Aveir system relies on i2i communication using subthreshold electrical pulses transmitted through the intracardiac blood pool to maintain AV synchrony.4 This signaling is inherently sensitive to competing electrical noise. Factors influencing i2i performance include device positioning, patient anatomy, and the presence of interfering electrical signals from other implanted systems.5

In this case, the patient's symptoms of fatigue and dyspnea, initially attributed to postviral illness and presumed chronotropic incompetence, were later linked to impaired AV synchrony. The retained pacemaker with an active MV sensor likely generated recurrent impedance signals that introduced competing electrical activity within the intracardiac environment, thereby disrupting i2i signal fidelity and AV synchrony.

To our knowledge, this is the first reported case of AV asynchrony specifically resulting from impaired i2i communication in a leadless pacemaker due to interference from an MV sensor. Although prior reports have documented challenges related to fractured leads with active sensors, primarily affecting pacing function, this case uniquely highlights disruption of device-to-device communication. For example, Sugiura et al6 described a case in which a lead fracture led to undersensing and reduced pacing efficacy due to electrical noise from an MV sensor.6

This case emphasizes the importance of structured preprocedural assessment before leadless pacemaker implantation in patients with retained transvenous systems. A practical checklist should include the following; 1) identification of all retained generators and leads, 2) confirmation of active device programming and rate-responsive features; 3) deactivation of MV sensing in Boston Scientific systems when present; 4) consideration of generator extraction when clinically feasible, and 5) postimplant interrogation for device-device interaction.

Importantly, preprocedural deactivation of the MV sensor was not performed in this case. In retrospect, this may have contributed to the observed device interaction and serves as an important reminder to incorporate this step into routine pre-implant evaluation.

Conclusions

This case highlights a previously underrecognized interaction between a dual-chamber leadless pacemaker system and a retained transvenous pacemaker with active MV sensing resulting in impaired i2i communication and AV asynchrony.
