Integrated carbon metabolism-transcriptomics-proteomics reveals physiological and molecular mechanisms underlying sour jujube resistance to witches’ broom disease
Authors: Chaohui Wang, Yuping Yan, Zikang Lu, Hongjie Li, Huigai Sun, Lin Pei, Ye Zhang, Xiangrui Fu, Jiahao Hou, Pei He, Hui Guo, Yongxing Song, Xian Gu, Xiaowei Han, Donglai Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPhytoplasma-mediated witches’ broom disease (WBD) causes abnormal growth and inhibits fruit formation in sour jujube trees, causing significant economic damage. Research on the pathogenic mechanisms of WBD and the disease resistance mechanisms of sour jujube tree remains poorly understood. In this study, these mechanisms were investigated using healthy resistant jujube branches that had been grafted onto WBD-infected trees and grown for seven years. Leaves were collected from both healthy resistant branches (KB) and WBD-affected branches (ZFB) to analyze differences in plant physiological indicators, carbon metabolic processes, and transcriptional and protein regulatory mechanisms. The results indicated that, compared with the ZFB group, the KB group had significantly greater levels of photosynthetic indicators, including the transpiration rate, net photosynthetic rate, stomatal conductance, and photosynthetically active radiation. Nutrient absorption and metabolic indicators, such as soluble proteins, were also higher in KB than in ZFB, whereas soluble sugar and starch concentrations were lower. Carbon metabolism profiling revealed that the KB group showed increased fatty acid synthesis for sustained energy production, whereas the ZFB group presented disrupted central carbon flux through an impaired TCA cycle, glycolysis, and pentose phosphate pathways. In addition, ferredoxin (Fd) was identified as a key resistance determinant in jujube trees against WBD phytoplasma. Genes and proteins associated with the photosynthesis pathway exhibited elevated expression levels in KB leaves, particularly those encoding Fd. High Fd expression enhances NADPH and ATP production, accelerates the Calvin cycle, and promotes carbohydrate synthesis. This enhancement supports various biological pathways, including the assimilation of carbon and nitrogen, chlorophyll metabolism, and the synthesis of photosynthetic pigments and fatty acids. In summary, our study reveals that an intact photosynthetic system and its associated metabolism protect resistant branches against WBD, providing a theoretical basis for breeding resistant cultivars.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-09218-x.Keywords: Witches’ broom disease, Phytoplasmas, Disease-resistant varieties, Photosynthetic pathway, Ferredoxin

Introduction

Phytoplasma-associated diseases pose a severe threat to global agricultural productivity, with documented infections in more than 1,000 plant species across diverse cropping systems [1–4]. As economically devastating pathogens, phytoplasmas can trigger catastrophic yield losses—reaching up to 100%—in fruit, vegetable, and cereal crops [5]. Over the years, numerous phytoplasmas have been linked to emerging plant diseases across the globe [6]. Among these, witches’ broom disease (WBD) is a highly destructive phytoplasma-induced disease caused by subgroup 16SrV-B ‘Candidatus Phytoplasma ziziphi’ (‘Ca. P. ziziphi’), which belongs to the elm yellows (16SrV) group of phytoplasmas [7, 8]. Sour jujube (Ziziphus jujuba var. spinosa), a wild relative of common jujube, also known as mountain or wild jujube, belongs to the Rhamnaceae family and the genus Ziziphus. As a highly representative fruit tree species, sour jujube is recognized for its high economic value and ecological benefits. Various parts of the plant have great potential for use in food and healthcare products [9–15]. In addition, sour jujube trees can thrive in barren environments such as mountains and slopes, playing a crucial role in preventing soil erosion [16]. WBD-phytoplasma-infected jujube trees typically exhibit symptoms such as witches’ broom, phyllody (leaf-like flowers), yellowing, little leaf, and stunting, which eventually lead to severe decline and loss of fruiting capacity [17, 18]. Jujube trees die within a few years after being infected with WBD, causing severe losses to the jujube industry [19, 20]. A major obstacle to understanding WBD pathogenesis and developing effective control strategies is the inability to culture phytoplasmas in pure form on artificial media [21]. Consequently, a comprehensive solution to WBD remains unavailable [22]. Thus, studying the pathogenic mechanism, key influencing factors, and host resistance response from the perspective of infected plants and cultivating new disease-resistant lines has become an important breakthrough in improving disease management.

The response of host plants to phytoplasma infection is complex and multifaceted. Infected leaves often lead to reduced stomatal opening, impaired water transport and photosynthesis, elevated sugar and starch accumulation [23]. Additionally, phytoplasma infection adversely affects photosynthetic activity by increasing antioxidant enzyme levels while reducing chlorophyll content, total soluble sugars, and auxin concentrations [24–26]. The imbalance of hormone homeostasis, especially the dysregulation of auxin, jasmonic acid, gibberellin and related signaling pathways, interferes with the normal development and defense responses of jujube trees and plays a key role in symptom formation [27, 28]. Moreover, the biosynthesis of secondary metabolites, such as terpenes, indole alkaloids, and phenylpropanoids, may be disrupted [29]. Despite these observations, the underlying molecular regulatory mechanisms of these physiological changes, especially the molecular basis by which resistant varieties maintain normal physiological functions, remain poorly studied.

Omics technologies have provided powerful tools for dissecting plant-pathogen interactions. Among them, transcriptomics is the earliest and most productive field in the omics research of WBD-infected. The earliest related studies demonstrated that the significant down-regulation of photosynthesis-related genes was associated with the yellowing symptoms of leaves after infection with WBD [30]. However, gene expression levels cannot fully represent the functional state of proteins, nor can they reveal the actual abundance of metabolites. Therefore, the integration of multiple omics has become the latest trend in WBD research. In 2017, Wei et al. systematically analyzed the multi-level regulation during phytoplasma infection through the combined analysis of transcriptomics, proteomics, and plant hormone levels [19]. In 2019, Wang et al. revealed the core regulatory role of jasmonic acid-related metabolism in the recovery process of witches’ broom disease treated with tetracycline through the combined analysis of iTRAQ proteomics and RNA-seq transcriptomics [28]. Moreover, integrative transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses conducted previously have clarified the molecular underpinnings of jujube’s response to phytoplasma infection, revealing key regulatory modules, including the ZjWRKY40-zju-miR157 module, that confer resistance to phytoplasmas in jujube [31].

Although there are reports on the omics analysis of the down-regulation of photosynthesis-related genes in jujube trees infected with WBD, there are still some deficiencies: the key regulatory nodes maintaining the photosynthesis of resistant varieties have not been identified, and the transcriptome, proteome and metabolome data have not been integrated to reveal the multi-level metabolic reprogramming. In this study, healthy branches of certified sour jujube accessions with documented resistance traits were grafted onto phytoplasma-infected rootstocks and maintained for seven years under field conditions. By integrating phenotypic, photosynthetic, antioxidant enzyme activity, carbon metabolomic, transcriptomic, and proteomic data, the long-term responses of resistant and susceptible varieties to phytoplasma infection were systematically compared, with the objective of (1) identifying key molecular nodes that sustain the integrity of the photosynthetic electron transport chain in resistant plants; (2) elucidating carbon metabolic reprogramming pathways in the resistant cultivar; and (3) constructing an integrated mechanistic model of resistance. This study advances beyond previous transcriptome-only research by providing a multi-omics–based characterization of the Fd-centered mechanism that enables resistant jujube trees to resist phytoplasma infection and maintain photosynthetic stability. This work provides new insights and a theoretical foundation for understanding WBD pathogenesis, tapping into the innate resistance potential of jujube, and supporting the sustainable development of sour jujube cultivation.

Materials and methods

Study sites and plant materials

The sour jujube tree affected by WBD was selected from Xindu District, Xingtai City, Hebei Province (37.11°N, 114.22°E). In 2017, branches of a resistant jujube cultivar were grafted onto the lateral branches of symptomatic wild jujube trees using the cleft grafting method. Over seven years of continuous observation, the resistant scions grafted onto jujube trees infected with witches’ broom grew healthily throughout, showing no symptoms of the disease and producing normal flowers and fruits. In contrast, the branches of the mother plant persistently exhibited symptoms of WBD, characterized by densely clustered small leaves and a lack of flowering and fruiting, whereas the tree itself did not die (Fig. 1). Given that graft union healing was completed long ago and no incompatibility was observed, the short-term disturbances caused by grafting had already subsided. Therefore, the metabolic and physiological differences between the resistant and susceptible varieties should be attributed to genotype-specific resistance traits and long-term adaptive responses to phytoplasma exposure, rather than residual effects of the grafting procedure. During the seven-year period, three independent grafting experiments were conducted in different years. In each experiment, scions of the resistant cultivar were grafted onto separate sour jujube rootstocks exhibiting WBD symptoms. Currently, all the grafted branches are growing well. In late September 2024, during the mature developmental stage, leaf samples (n = 6; three biological branches per branch type) free from other pests, diseases, or yellowing symptoms and exhibiting vigorous growth were collected in the morning from WBD-symptomatic branches (rootstock-derived) and healthy resistant branches (scion-derived) of the grafted tree, which has been maintained for seven years since initial grafting. All samples were derived from a single long-term-monitored tree to ensure consistency in grafting history and genetic background. The collected samples were first rinsed with sterile distilled water, gently dried, separately packed and wrapped in aluminum foil, rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen, and then stored in a −80 °C ultra-low temperature refrigerator.

Detection of witches’ broom phytoplasma

We conducted PCR and nested PCR to determine the presence of phytoplasma in the sample genomic DNA. Genomic DNA was extracted and used as a template, with the common primers P1/P7 and R16mF2/R16mR1 for phytoplasma to amplify the 16S rRNA gene [32]. PCR was carried out in a 25µL reaction system; 1µL DNA, 0.5µL of forward and reverse primer, 12.5µL 2 × Taq PCR Master Mix, and 10.5µL ddH2O. The reactions were carried out for 30 cycles with the conditions were as follows. 94 °C for 3 min, 94 °C for 30 s, 55 °C for 30 s, 72 °C for 1 min, followed by a final extension for 10 min at 72 °C. Products for both PCR types were detected on 1% agarose gel electrophoresis. Positive detection was carried out under a 120V, 100mA horizontal current. The target band was excised from the gel and sent to Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China for Sanger sequencing. Sequence alignment was performed based on the Sanger sequencing results.

Morphological and microscopic observations

Morphological characterization of the experimental jujube trees was performed using a Nikon D7200 digital camera to capture representative images of leaves, branches, and whole-plant architecture. For scanning electron microscopy (SEM), leaf tissue blocks (< 3 mm2) were excised from both WBD-affected and resistant branches, immediately fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde (0.1 M phosphate buffer, pH 7.2–7.4), dehydrated through an ascending ethanol series, subjected to critical-point drying (Quorum K850), sputter-coated with gold (HITACHI MC1000), and imaged using a HITACHI Regulus 8100 SEM. For transmission electron microscopy (TEM), samples were fixed sequentially in 2.5% glutaraldehyde (0.1 M phosphate buffer, pH 7.2–7.4) followed by 1% osmium tetroxide (0.1 M phosphate buffer), dehydrated in graded ethanol, embedded in epoxy resin, ultrathin-sectioned (50 nm) using a Leica EM UC7 ultramicrotome, and double-stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate. TEM imaging was conducted on a HITACHI HT7800 transmission electron microscope. For quantitative ultrastructural analysis, six randomly selected fields of view per sample were acquired; organelle dimensions and densities were measured using Image-Pro Plus 6.0 software. Statistical significance between groups was determined using an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test.

Determination of plant physiological indices

Determination of photosynthetic characteristic indicators

Photosynthetic parameters were measured under clear, windless conditions, with a light intensity of 1600 μmol·m−2·s−1. Between 9:00 and 10:00 AM, mature, fully expanded leaves exhibiting healthy and uniform growth at the apical portion of upper branches were selected for measurement using a TP-3051D portable photosynthesis system (LI-COR, Lincoln, Nebraska, USA). Five biological replicates were performed for each group. The primary measurement indicators included the net photosynthetic rate (Pn), stomatal conductance (Gs), transpiration rate (Tr), photosynthetically active radiation (PAR), and intercellular CO2 concentration (Ci) in two groups of anatomically matched leaves.

Determination of the chlorophyll content

Two groups of leaves exhibiting optimal growth conditions were selected. Fresh leaf tissue (excluding leaf veins) was accurately weighed at 0.2 g per sample, and this measurement was performed in quintuplicate for each group. The samples were soaked in 95% ethanol and incubated in the dark for 48 h until decolorization was complete. The absorbance values were subsequently measured at wavelengths of 665 nm, 649 nm, and 470 nm. The chlorophyll content was then calculated from these absorbance values.

Determination of nonstructural carbohydrate content, soluble protein content, and flavonoid content in leaves

Leaf starch content was quantified using the perchloric acid hydrolysis method, and soluble sugar content was determined using the anthrone—sulfuric acid colorimetric assay. Nonstructural carbohydrate (NSC) content in leaves was calculated as the sum of soluble sugar and starch concentrations. The soluble protein content was measured via the Coomassie Brilliant Blue G-250 method. Total flavonoid concentration was measured via the NaNO2-Al(NO3)3-NaOH colorimetric method. The absorbance was measured via a UV-75 UV‒visible spectrophotometer (Shimadzu Enterprise Management, Kyoto, Japan) to establish standard curves for calculating the concentration of each indicator. Reagent kits were obtained from Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology, Beijing, China. Measurements were performed in quintuplicate for each group.

Determination of enzyme activities

Fresh leaves (1 g) were weighed and ground in liquid nitrogen. The resulting powder was homogenized in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; 0.01 mol/L, pH 7.2–7.4) at a ratio of 10% (w/v). The homogenate was centrifuged at 5000 r/min for 15 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected for analysis. Commercially available assay kits were used to quantify oxidative stress markers and antioxidant enzyme activities, including peroxidase (POD) and reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels. Additionally, photosynthesis-related enzyme activities—including pyruvate phosphate dikinase (PPDK) and ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate carboxylase/oxygenase (Rubisco)—were determined. All reagent kits were sourced from Wuhan Saipei Biotechnology, Wuhan, China and Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology, Beijing, China. Measurements were performed in quintuplicate for each group.

Metabolomic, transcriptomic and proteomic sequencing analyses

Plant materials and treatments

The collected samples were taken out from the −80 °C refrigerator for transcriptome, proteome and metabolome analysis. Each treatment group included at least three biological replicates. Total RNA, protein, and metabolite extracts were prepared using validated, method-specific protocols prior to sequencing, which was performed by Shanghai Majorbio Bio-pharm Technology, Shanghai, China.

Metabolome profiling

Metabolites were extracted from the samples, and targeted standards for carbon metabolism were prepared. LC–MS/MS analysis was performed on an ExionLC AD system coupled with a QTRAP® 6500 + triple quadrupole linear ion trap mass spectrometer (SCIEX, USA) at Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology. Detailed protocols for sample preparation, instrument parameters, and data processing were adapted from previously published studies [33].

Raw data were imported into Sciex OS software for automatic ion fragment identification and integration, followed by manual inspection. Metabolite concentrations were calculated based on linear regression of standard curves. Sample distribution patterns were visualized through Principal Component Analysis (PCA). Metabolites with significant abundance variations were screened based on Variable Importance in Projection (VIP) scores obtained from Partial Least Squares Discriminant Analysis (PLS-DA). Multiple testing correction of P-values was performed using the Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) procedure. Hierarchical clustering was implemented in Python to group metabolites with similar expression profiles. Functional pathway annotation of differential metabolites was conducted through mapping against the KEGG database to elucidate their biological roles.

Transcriptome sequencing and DEG analysis

RNA purification, reverse transcription, library preparation, sequencing were performed as previously described [34]. Sequencing libraries were constructed on Illumina platforms according to standard protocols. Raw paired-end reads were processed and quality-filtered using fastp with default settings. High-quality reads were subsequently mapped to the reference genome in a strand-specific manner using HISAT2. Transcript assembly was performed with StringTie employing a reference-guided strategy.

Gene expression levels were quantified as transcripts per million (TPM), and transcript abundances were estimated using RSEM. Sample distribution patterns were visualized by PCA. Differential gene expression analysis between groups was performed using DESeq2. Functional annotation of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) was conducted via Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses. Significantly enriched terms were identified at a Bonferroni-corrected P-value threshold of ≤ 0.05 against the transcriptomic background; GO enrichment analysis was performed with Goatools, and KEGG enrichment analysis was conducted using KOBAS.

TMT proteomic sequencing and DEP analysis

Total protein extraction, TMT labeling, and LC–MS/MS analysis were performed as previously described [35, 36], with minor modifications. Proteins were extracted using urea lysis buffer (8 M urea, 1% SDS) supplemented with protease inhibitors, and protein concentrations were determined using a BCA assay kit (Thermo, USA). Digestion was performed with trypsin (1: 50, w/w) at 37 °C overnight. The resulting peptides were labeled with TMT reagents (No. A44522, Thermo Fisher, USA) and fractionated by high-pH reversed-phase chromatography on an ACQUITY UPLC BEH C18 column (1.7 µm, 2.1 mm × 150 mm; Waters, USA).

Two-dimensional analysis was performed via liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry (Evosep One combined with an Orbitrap Exploris 480 mass spectrometer) according to the standard protocols of Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology (Shanghai, China). The instrument was configured for data-dependent acquisition, alternating between survey scans and tandem mass spectra. Full MS scans (m/z 350–1500) were recorded at a resolving power of 60,000 (at m/z 200), while MS/MS events were executed using higher-energy collisional dissociation at a resolution of 15,000. A 30-s exclusion window was applied to prevent redundant sequencing of precursor ions.

RAW data files were analyzed using Proteome Discoverer (v2.4) with the following parameters: precursor mass tolerance 20 ppm, fragment mass tolerance 0.02 Da, peptide FDR ≤ 1%, and ≥ 1 unique peptide required for protein identification. PCA was used to present the distribution pattern of samples. Differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) were screened using the criteria FC ≥ 2 or FC ≤ 0.83. GO functional annotation and KEGG functional enrichment analysis of DEPs were performed using Goatools and Python, respectively. Subcellular localization of target proteins was performed using MultiLoc2 software.

Combined analysis of the transcriptome, TMT proteome, and metabolome

The differentially expressed genes and proteins with Pearson correlation coefficients > 0.80 and P < 0.05 in each pathway were visualized to analyze the regulatory effect of gene expression on protein expression. KEGG pathway and GSEA enrichment analyses were conducted by combining the transcriptome and proteome, with a focus on the correlation and regulatory mechanisms of differentially expressed genes and proteins in pathways related to photosynthesis. Metabolites from the metabolome were integrated for upstream and downstream joint analysis.

Metabolomic validation

The method for extracting organic acids from samples was as follows: leaf samples were removed from a −80 °C freezer and immediately ground with liquid nitrogen. We weighed 0.1 g of the resulting powder and dissolved it in 10 mL of a 0.1% potassium dihydrogen phosphate solution. The mixture was allowed to soak for 4 h and then extracted via ultrasonication for 30 min. The mixture was filtered, the filtrate was centrifuged at 3500 rpm for 10 min, and the supernatant was passed through a 0.45 µm filter membrane. The filtrate was transferred to a brown vial and stored at 4 °C. An HPLC instrument (Agilent 1260, USA) equipped with a C18 column (Angela Eclipse Plus C18, 4.6 mm × 250 mm) was used for analysis. The detection method was adapted from previous literature on organic acid content determination [37]. Standard samples of fumaric acid, citric acid, succinic acid, a-ketoglutaric acid, and malic acid were used for quantification via the external standard method. Fructose and glucose were determined according to the DNS colorimetric method [38]. All analyses were repeated five times.

Reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) analysis

The plant materials utilized for RT-qPCR were identical to those used for transcriptomics and iTRAQ quantitative proteomics. Total RNA was extracted from two sets of jujube samples via a column-type total RNA extraction and purification kit. The concentration and purity of the RNA were determined via an ultramicro spectrophotometer (A260 nm/A280 nm = 1.8–2.1; A260 nm/A230 nm > 1.8), ensuring high purity suitable for subsequent experiments. cDNA was synthesized via the TransGen Biotech PerfectStart Uni RT-qPCR Kit, which is a two-step fluorescence quantitative PCR kit.

On the basis of the results obtained from the transcriptomic analysis, DEGs within the photosynthesis signaling pathway were selected for further investigation. The primer sequences were designed via Primer3Plus (https://www.primer3plus.com) on the basis of the gene sequence information. Actin gene was used as a reference gene for data normalization. The primer sequences are detailed in Table S1.

RT-qPCR was conducted to assess the relative expression levels of these genes across both sets. Relative quantification was performed via the 2⁻ΔΔCt method to analyze differences in gene expression levels. The reaction was carried out via a CFX96 Touch instrument with the following temperature cycling parameters: 94 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of 94 °C for 5 s and 60 °C for 30 s. Data analysis was performed via the software provided with the CFX96 Touch instrument to obtain Ct values for each reaction.

Statistical analysis

Photosynthetic characteristics, physiological indicators, and enzyme activity data were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) in IBM SPSS Statistics 25.0; statistical significance between groups was assessed using independent-samples t-tests at a threshold of P < 0.05. In the figures, significant differences between the two groups were indicated by asterisks: *, P < 0.05; **, P < 0.01; ***, P < 0.001; ns, not significant. Graphical representations of the statistical results were generated using GraphPad Prism 8. Source data are provided in the Supplementary Data file.

Results

Comparison of the photosynthetic physiological characteristics of KB and ZFB leaves infected with WBD

Due to the grafting of disease-resistant rootstocks, the same plant shows obvious growth heterogeneity (Fig. 1A). The diseased condition of the jujube tree is manifested as shortened and slender internodes that give rise to clusters of abnormal branches. The leaves produced under these pathological conditions are clustered, diminutive, and chlorotic, and fail to initiate flowering or fruiting; moreover, the affected branches exhibit complete spine loss (Fig. 1B-D). In contrast, the grafted resistant branches did not display small leaf clusters; instead, they exhibited a tortuous growth pattern as they extended. The smaller branches might appear either alone or in clusters of 2–7 individual shoots, with spines and flowers at their tips, ultimately leading to fruit production (Fig. 1F-H). A comparative analysis of both groups revealed distinct differences in leaf morphology: the diseased leaves were lanceolate in shape, small and yellowish, thin and tender, with upward-curled edges (Fig. 1E). Conversely, the leaves of the grafted branches were oval or ovoid shape, with colors ranging from dark green to light green; these leaves are paper-like in texture, thick and glossy (Fig. 1I). Compared with the leaves of the mother plants, the leaves of the grafted branches tended to abscise earlier (Fig. 1A). PCR and nested PCR analysis revealed that no phytoplasma was detected in the normally growing resistant branches, whereas phytoplasma was detected in the mother plants exhibiting jujube witches’ broom symptoms (Fig. 2). BLAST analysis against the NCBI nucleotide database revealed a 99% sequence identity between the obtained amplicon and the reference 16S rRNA sequence of Candidatus Phytoplasma ziziphi (commonly associated with jujube witches’ broom disease) (GenBank: DQ919060.1). For comparative purposes, the normally growing resistant cultivar was designated as Group KB, and the phytoplasma-infected susceptible cultivar was designated as Group ZFB.

The ultrastructure of the mature leaves was analyzed by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) (Fig. 3). The SEM results revealed that the epidermal cells of the leaves from the ZFB group were predominantly elongated, whereas those from the resistant KB group exhibited a hexagonal shape. Additionally, the stomatal density in the KB group leaves was greater than that in the ZFB group leaves, although the sizes of the stomata in both groups were comparable. Furthermore, the central vascular bundle within the leaf veins of the ZFB group leaves was smaller than that within the KB leaves. In contrast, KB demonstrated greater thickness in both the upper and lower epidermis, as well as a denser arrangement of palisade and sponge tissues.

In ZFB leaves, mesophyll cells were loosely arranged, whereas in KB leaves, they were densely packed and regularly organized. Moreover, KB mesophyll cells were significantly larger than those in ZFB leaves. Each mesophyll cell contained approximately 5–6 chloroplasts, which were elliptical and distributed along the cell periphery. Chloroplasts in KB leaves were considerably larger than those in ZFB leaves. Furthermore, chloroplasts from KB leaves contained more plastid globules than those from ZFB leaves, which contained only a few dark plastid globules. In addition, starch grains in the mesophyll cells of the KB group were relatively small, whereas those in the ZFB group were relatively large.

Comparison of the photosynthetic characteristics of KB and ZFB leaves infected with WBD

The photosynthetic parameters, including the Pn, Tr, Gs, and PAR, of the KB leaves were significantly greater than those of the ZFB leaves (P < 0.001) (Fig. 4). The difference in Ci between the two groups was not significant, indicates that the difference in photosynthetic capacity between the two groups is not attributable to stomatal limitation.

Comparison of various physiological indices in the leaves of the KB and ZFB groups infected with WBD

Significant differences in metabolic products and various photosynthetic and oxidative enzyme activities were detected between KB and ZFB leaves (Fig. 5). Specifically, KB leaf tissues presented significantly higher levels of soluble proteins and photosynthesis-related enzymes such as PPDK. Rubisco levels were comparable between the two groups. Conversely, the soluble sugar, starch and flavonoid contents were markedly elevated in ZFB leaves relative to KB leaves.

Differentially abundant metabolites were identified via VIP values derived from partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA). A total of 43 metabolites were detected in samples from the KB and ZFB groups, with 33 metabolites showing significant differences under screening conditions (VIP-PLS-DA ≥ 1 and P < 0.05) (Fig. 6B and Table S2). To further analyze the abundance distribution of these metabolites, we performed hierarchical clustering on metabolites from KB and ZFB leaf tissues (Fig. 6C). This analysis revealed 10 clusters, indicating different metabolic patterns between the two groups. Significant differences in metabolite abundance were observed in clusters 1, 2, 3, 5, 7 and 4 between the KB and ZFB treatments. Specifically, cluster 4 contained higher levels of fatty acid metabolites in the KB group than in the ZFB group. These metabolites included ethylmalonic acid, methylmalonic acid, malonic acid. These results suggest that these metabolites may increase the energy sources necessary for sustaining life activities in the KB group.

In clusters 1, 2, 3, 5, and 7, the abundances of metabolites involved in several metabolic pathways were significantly lower in the KB group than in the ZFB group. These pathways mainly involved intermediates of the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, including citric acid, 2-ketoglutaric acid, and cis-aconitic acid. Additionally, intermediates involved in glycolysis and gluconeogenesis, such as glucose, glucose 6-phosphate, gluconic acid, glyceric acid, and glyoxylate, were also lower in the KB group. Furthermore, intermediates from the pentose phosphate pathway, including glucosamine 6-phosphate and adenosine 5’-monophosphate, showed lower abundances in the KB group. These differences in metabolic trends indicate distinct physiological states between the two groups. Analysis of differentially abundant metabolites identified key metabolites that were significantly up- or down-regulated (Fig. 6D). These metabolites were mapped to KEGG pathways, revealing that alterations in metabolite levels significantly affected key pathways, including the pentose phosphate pathway, pyruvate metabolism, and nicotinate and nicotinamide metabolism (Fig. 6E). These changes may contribute to the phenotypic differences observed between the two groups. Five key organic acids and carbohydrate metabolites were quantitatively validated using HPLC and DNS colorimetric assays. The validation results (Fig. 6F–H) were consistent with the metabolomic data.

Discussion

Disease-resistant varieties maintain the integrity of the photosynthetic core

Witches’ broom disease (WBD) causes damage through a series of cascade reactions, starting with the disintegration of the photosynthetic apparatus and ultimately leading to systemic energy metabolism dysfunction. The key distinction between the KB and ZFB lines lies in their ability to maintain the integrity of the photosynthetic core and its downstream metabolic network. At the structural level, KB leaves exhibit characterized by larger vascular bundles, tightly arranged mesophyll tissues, and more complete chloroplast development. Previous studies have shown that damage to midrib tissues and small vascular bundles results in a fragmented leaf phenotype, reduced cortical cell diameter, and decreased photosynthetic rate [39]. This is demonstrated by the significant inhibition of key photosynthetic parameters such as the Pn, Gs, PAR, and Tr. Although intercellular CO2 concentration (Ci) may remain unchanged under saturating environmental conditions, the coordinated inhibition of multiple photosynthetic parameters indicates fundamental impairments in both photochemical reactions and carbon fixation [40].

A sharp decrease in the contents of soluble proteins and chlorophyll was observed in WBD-infected leaves. The soluble protein content is an important indicator of leaf senescence and overall photosynthetic capacity [41], and it remains high in the KB group, indicating that this group has greater photosynthetic capacity and leaf health status. In addition, the decrease in chlorophyll content and damage to the chloroplast structure directly weaken the efficiency of light energy absorption and reduce the energy available for light reactions [24, 42]. This finding is consistent with the widely reported phenomenon in cross-species studies: phytoplasma infection can significantly disrupt the phloem transport function, leading to abnormal accumulation of carbohydrates (particularly soluble sugars and starch) in source leaves. For instance, a significant increase in leaf soluble sugar and starch content has been consistently observed in phytoplasma-infected plants, including periwinkle (Catharanthus roseus) and tobacco (Nicotiana tabacum), coconut palms afflicted with lethal yellowing disease [43, 44], grapevine (Vitis vinifera) infected with Flavescence dorée and Bois noir phytoplasmas, papaya (Carica papaya) infected with “Candidatus Phytoplasma australiense”, and Mexican lime (Citrus aurantifolia) infected with “Candidatus Phytoplasma aurantifolia” [45]. Transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses have further confirmed the upregulation of starch and sucrose metabolic pathways in phytoplasma-infected Paulownia (Paulownia spp.), sugarcane (Saccharum officinarum), and jujuba (Ziziphus jujuba) [46, 47]. Collectively, these findings support the hypothesis that phytoplasma colonization of sieve tube elements triggers callose deposition and sieve plate occlusion, thereby impeding the long-distance phloem transport of photosynthates to sink organs. This disruption leads to feedback accumulation of photosynthetic products in source leaves, ultimately exacerbating physiological imbalances and symptom progression. Furthermore, PPDK activity was markedly higher in the KB group than in the ZFB group. As a key enzyme of the C4 carbon-concentrating mechanism, PPDK converts pyruvate to phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP) and is involved in nitrogen metabolism, energy balance, and stress responses [48]. Comparable Rubisco activities between the two groups suggest that total Rubisco catalytic capacity was preserved. Thus, the photosynthetic advantage of the KB group may be associated with PPDK-mediated carbon metabolic reprogramming.

ROS levels and redox balance: active defense in disease-resistant varieties and ineffective consumption in disease-susceptible varieties

In this study, compared with the ZFB group, the KB group exhibited significantly lower flavonoid content, significantly higher ROS levels, and a slightly higher POD activity. Although this pattern may appear contradictory at first glance, it reflects two distinct metabolic and defense states. In the ZFB group, substantial flavonoid accumulation represents a passive pathological response. Based on phytoplasma detection results, we infer that phytoplasma in the KB group was effectively resisted prior to cellular entry, leading to downregulation of defense metabolism to avoid energy waste. As products of the phenylpropanoid pathway, flavonoid synthesis is induced by pathogen infection [49]. However, despite high flavonoid accumulation in the ZFB group, phytoplasma colonization was not prevented—indicating that this accumulation constitutes “ineffective consumption,” likely resulting from phytoplasma-mediated hijacking of host metabolism, aberrant carbon flux, or misactivation of defense signaling pathways. In contrast, the KB group showed significantly reduced flavonoid content, indicating that phenylpropanoid metabolism is not nonspecifically activated, thereby avoiding unnecessary energy expenditure. Moreover, in this study, the basal ROS level in leaves of the resistant jujube was approximately 20 ng/g FW—within the normal physiological range. ROS primarily functions as a signaling molecule regulating normal growth and development. The antioxidant system promptly eliminates excess ROS, thereby maintaining cellular redox homeostasis and preventing oxidative damage. Furthermore, the relatively low ROS levels in the ZFB group suggest failure to initiate a key oxidative burst, possibly due to suppression of NADPH oxidase activity by phytoplasma effectors, resulting in impairment of this core innate immune event [50]. The modest increase in POD activity reflects fine-tuned redox homeostasis regulation in KB: eliminating excess ROS to prevent oxidative damage while retaining sufficient H2O2 to sustain signaling functions [51, 52].

In conclusion, disease resistance in the KB group does not rely on physical or chemical barriers conferred by flavonoids. Instead, it likely achieves effective inhibition of phytoplasma through a moderate and controllable oxidative burst and maintenance of stable, orderly redox balance. In contrast, massive flavonoid accumulation in the ZFB group represents an ineffective stress response: its ROS level falls below the normal physiological range; the oxidative burst fails to initiate; redox homeostasis collapses; and ultimately, the entire defense system fails.

Analysis and interpretation of the combined results of transcriptome and proteome

The combined analysis of the transcriptome and proteome revealed significant common annotations in UDP-glucuronosyltransferase (UGT) activity between the KB group and the ZFB group. Compared with the ZFB group, the expression in the KB group showed a downward trend. Excessive UGT activity glycosylates hormone signaling molecules (e.g., salicylic acid), thereby inhibiting defense signaling pathways. Downregulating UGT expression maintains active hormone homeostasis, ensuring efficient defense signal transmission [53]. We hypothesize that phytoplasma effector proteins may specifically induce UGT expression, steering host metabolism toward exploitable ‘glycosylation product traps’ [54]. In contrast, disease-resistant varieties may recognize phytoplasma effectors via resistance proteins, activating immune responses while suppressing the expression of susceptibility genes such as UGT [55]. Furthermore, the accumulation of flavonoid compounds and their upstream/downstream pathways in ZFB represents a passive pathological phenotype resulting from the combined effects of phytoplasma metabolic hijacking, host signaling collapse, and disrupted carbon metabolism [56]. This accumulation fails to suppress the pathogen while consuming energy and exacerbating oxidative damage, constituting a non-active defense mechanism.

Ferredoxin is a key metabolic hub for disease resistance

This study revealed the coordinated upregulation of PSII, PSI, and cytochrome b6f components in the KB group during WBD infection. This enhanced photosynthetic capacity increases ATP/NADPH, promotes carbon fixation and synthetic metabolism [57, 58]. Gene set enrichment indicates that ferredoxin (Fd) is a key metabolic center that can effectively allocate PSI derived electrons to NADPH and promote nitrogen assimilation and fatty acid desaturation [59, 60]. Research has shown that the expression of photosynthesis-related and chlorophyll-related genes, including ferredoxin (Fd), progressively decreases as WBD phytoplasma infection advances [61]. This downregulation of key photosynthetic electron transport chain components suppresses photosynthetic activity in infected leaves at later stages of infection, representing a primary mechanism underlying chlorosis in jujube trees. Fd in plants can be divided into four subtypes: Fd(I), photosynthetic type (leaf type); Fd(II), Non photosynthetic type (root type); FdC1(III), which has a shorter extension at the C-end [62, 63]; and FdC2(IV), which has a longer extension at the C-end. Photosynthetic Fd, as the most abundant subtype, mainly occurs in tissues with photosynthetic ability and has low redox potential. Studies have shown that the photosynthetic ferredoxins PfFd(I) and PfFd(II) play important roles in Paulownia’s response to Paulownia witches’ broom phytoplasma. The effector protein pawb3, secreted by the phytoplasma associated with Paulownia witches’ broom, directly targets and interacts with the PfFd(II) protein. By hijacking this central ferredoxin hub, the pathogen precisely cuts off the host plant’s energy supply, implicating PfFd(II) in resistance against Paulownia witches' broom. [64]. Similarly, infection of Arabidopsis thaliana with the tomato pathogenic variant DC3000 of Pseudomonas syringae can inhibit Fd(II) expression and reduce plant resistance to pathogens [65]. The knockout study of Fd(II) further confirmed its role in plant defense. These results indicate that Fd expression is closely related to the ability of plants to resist pathogenic microorganisms. Low Fd expression can impair photosynthesis and metabolic processes, leading to yellowing of leaves, fruit deformities, and overall weakened plant growth. Therefore, maintaining high Fd expression levels may be necessary for breeding disease-resistant plant lines. Fd functions as a key resistance determinant against WBD phytoplasma in jujube trees, where its high expression in the resistant cultivar sustains photosynthetic electron transport and provides essential energy for defense, thereby preventing phytoplasma colonization.

Targeted metabolomics reveals central carbon metabolic reprogramming

Targeted metabolomics analysis revealed significant differences in central carbon metabolism between the KB group and the ZFB group. Central carbon metabolism (CCM) comprises glycolysis (EMP), the TCA cycle, and the pentose phosphate pathway (PPP). EMP breaks down glucose into pyruvate, generating ATP and NADH; pyruvate then enters the mitochondria and is converted into acetyl-CoA to enter the TCA cycle. The TCA cycle is the final metabolic pathway for three major nutrients (carbohydrates, lipids, and amino acids) and serves as a hub for the interconversion of various organic compounds. The PPP uses glucose-6-phosphate as a substrate to produce NADPH (for antioxidant defense and anabolic metabolism) and ribose-5-phosphate (for nucleotide synthesis). These three pathways achieve dynamic coordination of carbon flow through the sharing of intermediate metabolites, which is particularly important for plant growth and development [66]. This study found that the abundance of TCA cycle intermediates (citric acid, 2-ketoglutaric acid, and cis-aconitic acid)—were significantly lower in the KB group than in the ZFB group, whereas these metabolites accumulated in the ZFB group. Combined with the previous physiological index measurements, this difference indicates that in the KB group, carbon skeletons were efficiently used for downstream energy production and the synthesis of defense precursors (such as α-ketoglutaric acid for amino acid and chlorophyll synthesis) [67]; while in the ZFB group, the accumulation of TCA cycle intermediates reflects the obstruction of carbon flow and low metabolic utilization efficiency, which is consistent with the fact that the phloem is blocked by phytoplasma, and photosynthetic products cannot be transported out in time, causing glucose, fructose, and other soluble sugars to accumulate in the leaves, triggering a negative feedback mechanism that inhibits photosynthesis-related gene expression in a high-sugar environment [60]. Similarly, the intermediate products of glycolysis/gluconeogenesis (glucose, glucose 6-phosphate, gluconic acid, glyceric acid, etc.) were lower in the KB group, indicating that sugars in the KB group were rapidly consumed or redirected to other metabolic pathways; while the increase of these products in the ZFB group further confirmed the excess carbon input and utilization obstacles. In transgenic tobacco, an increase in leaf sugar content by 2–3 times can reduce the transcriptional abundance of psbA [68]. This accumulation effect leads to negative feedback regulation, resulting in insufficient raw materials for the TCA cycle, a reduction in intermediate products, and a decrease in the synthesis of glutamic acid, chlorophyll, and catalase [69]. The resulting energy shortage and metabolic stagnation directly lead to typical growth retardation and fruit abortion in infected trees, seriously affecting the normal growth and fruit quality of jujube trees [61]. Additionally, the intermediate products of PPP (glucosamine 6-phosphate and adenosine 5’-monophosphate) were also lower in the KB group, suggesting that the KB group may have reduced its reliance on PPP or rapidly utilized its products (such as NADPH for oxidative bursts). Notably, the metabolites related to fatty acid metabolism (ethyl malonate, methyl malonate, and malonic acid) were significantly elevated in the KB group. Changes in the content of fatty acid metabolic components can lead to various phenotypes, such as chlorosis, leaf malformation, reduced fertility, and dwarfism [70], severely hindering embryo development and reducing seed fatty acid content. In summary, the KB group maintained an efficient carbon metabolic flow (low accumulation of TCA intermediate products, rapid consumption of sugars, and moderate utilization of PPP), avoiding the carbon stagnation, metabolite accumulation, and feedback inhibition observed in the ZFB group. This ensured the smooth operation of energy supply and defense metabolism. These findings at the metabolomic level provide direct evidence that Fd-mediated photosynthetic electron transport maintains downstream carbon metabolic reprogramming.

Integration mechanism model: photosynthesis-defense metabolic circuit centered on ferredoxin

To elucidate whether the observed phenomenon represents an intrinsic mechanism of disease resistance rather than metabolic variation, we constructed a detailed mechanistic diagram (Fig. 10). In KB group, highly expressed Fd efficiently channels electrons from Photosystem I to NADP⁺, generating abundant NADPH that sustains robust CO2 fixation while simultaneously fueling downstream metabolic networks. This contrasts sharply with infected varieties, where carbon flux stagnation leads to starch accumulation and metabolic sequestration. In contrast, in resistant varieties, the phytoplasma is not infected and the priority is given to rapid carbon turnover: fixed sugars are promptly glycolyzed to pyruvate, which is swiftly converted to acetyl-CoA. This key intermediate is then strategically partitioned-a fraction enters the TCA cycle to power bioenergetic demands, while another is diverted toward fatty acid synthesis for membrane repair and defense signaling lipids. Critically, the TCA cycle intermediate α-ketoglutarate serves as a metabolic hub, rapidly assimilating ammonia into glutamate, which subsequently feeds into amino acid pools, protein synthesis, chlorophyll biosynthesis, the photoprotective pigments, and catalase production. The overarching resistance mechanism thus ensures carbon allocation prioritizes growth and defense rather than pathogen exploitation, thereby preserving photosynthetic efficiency and sustaining plant vigor. On the basis of these findings, we propose an integrated model in which ferredoxin acts as a key coordinator of disease resistance. In infected plants, the collapse of this system-which begins with structural damage and is exacerbated by low Fd expression-leads to a vicious cycle of sugar feedback, oxidative damage, and metabolic paralysis. Therefore, our study not only advances understanding of the pathogenic mechanism of WBD but also revealed that the photosynthetic mechanism centered on Fd is the key factor determining disease outcomes. These findings provide a foundation for developing WBD-resistant sour jujube varieties through molecular breeding.

Research limitations and prospects

Finally, although this study has revealed the key molecular mechanisms by which disease-resistant jujube varieties maintain photosynthetic and carbon metabolic homeostasis through multi-omics integration analysis, there are still limitations. In this study, we selected a diseased jujube tree, which was of special value in that it provided a “naturally occurring, long-term stable infection state” that is difficult to obtain in conventional inoculation experiments. To ensure the rigor of the experiment, we removed weeds and understory plants to minimize the risk of secondary infection from surrounding symptomatic trees or insect vectors. However, the single sample limits the assessment of overall variability and lacks biological replicates at the individual tree level. Therefore, the identified key resistance markers—particularly the high expression of ferredoxin—need to be verified in independent resistant germplasm resources and larger-scale populations. Secondly, this study is observational and based on correlation; there is still a lack of direct evidence linking the increase in Fd expression level to plant systemic disease resistance. Functional validation through gene transformation (such as overexpression in jujube or model plants or using CRISPR-Cas9 technology to knockout Fd) or transient expression systems is crucial for establishing a causal relationship. Thirdly, the molecular interactions between the Fd-mediated photosynthetic electron flow and specific plant cell wall effectors (such as secreted proteins that may target Fd) have not been studied. Finally, this study mainly focuses on leaf metabolism; systematic studies on phloem transport, sink-source dynamics, and long-distance signal transduction will further clarify how resistant branches maintain normal growth under continuous pathogen pressure. Future research should address these limitations not only to consolidate the role of Fd as a key resistance hub but also to promote the development of molecular markers for breeding jujube varieties resistant to WBD.

Conclusion

This study elucidates that the fundamental difference between the healthy resistant (KB) and diseased (ZFB) jujube trees lies in their ability to maintain the integrity of the photosynthetic core structure and the stability of their downstream metabolic network. In the photosystem, resistant cultivars maintain high Fd expression, ensuring efficient photosynthetic electron transfer and NADPH supply that energize carbon fixation and defense metabolite biosynthesis. Rapid carbon flux diverts acetyl-CoA into both the TCA cycle and fatty acid synthesis, while α-ketoglutarate drives amino acid and chlorophyll formation, thereby establishing a photo-defense metabolic loop. This strategy avoids the accumulation of energy substances, oxidative damage, and metabolic paralysis observed in susceptible genotypes, ultimately achieving a synergistic enhancement of disease resistance and growth potential. These findings establish a theoretical framework for WBD management and provide molecular targets for breeding resistant jujube cultivars.
