Changes in primary care provider behaviors after a brief educational intervention about opioid prescribing in older adults
Authors: Alëna A. Balasanova, Hongmei Wang, Jungyoon Kim, Jihyun Ma, Rebecca Hiebert, Mary Jo Spurgin, Jane Potter, Alfred L. Fisher
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThere is limited literature on educational interventions for primary care clinicians (PCC) addressing the unique considerations in opioid prescribing for older adult patients.ObjectiveWe aimed to evaluate self-reported knowledge, confidence, and behavior change by PCC and the degree to which respondents followed through with their intentions to implement specific clinical strategies after receiving a brief educational intervention about opioid prescribing in older adults.DesignAnalysis of pre-, post-, and one-month follow-up surveys of PCC.ParticipantsPCC working within our institution’s 13 primary care clinics.InterventionsThe brief educational training was conducted over 20 months and focused on limited benefits and unique harms of chronic opioid use in older adults, opioid use disorder screening, and harm reduction strategies.Main measuresThe surveys assessed perceived knowledge and confidence in non-opioid medication options and opioid safety strategies before and after the intervention and potential use of strategies to address opioid use in their clinical practice. The one-month follow-up survey asked if participants had started implementing opioid safety strategies with patients. We also collected information on age, gender, race, ethnicity, and health professions discipline.Key resultsSixty-five PCC received the intervention and completed the immediate post-training survey. Of these, 24 also completed the one-month follow-up survey. We found increased perceived knowledge of the effectiveness of long-term opioids for chronic pain and increased confidence in suggesting non-opioid treatment options among all participants after the training. Post-training, all but one clinician planned to implement at least one of the taught opioid safety strategies. ConclusionsOur findings show that a brief opioid-focused educational intervention for practicing PCC is associated with increased perceived knowledge and confidence immediately after the intervention and a high likelihood of implementing some of the taught strategies one month later. Background

Although opioid prescriptions for non-cancer pain decreased by nearly 50% between 2012 and 2022, this reduction in prescribing was not associated with a reduction in drug-related mortality with the 12-month period ending in December 2022 marking the highest estimate on record for drug overdose deaths at 109,680, with many of these deaths being attributed to illicit synthetic opioids. [1, 2] The impact of exposure to opioid education among practicing healthcare providers is well-studied and has been associated with decreased rates of opioid prescribing [3]. Current trends suggest that a reduction in opioid prescribing alone is likely not sufficient to stem the tide of opioid related mortality. While opioid prescriptions have decreased in recent years, this has not clearly led to a reduction in drug opioid deaths as well as harm from opioids. Harms from opioids include cognitive decline with long-term use, increased risk of fractures in older adults as well as increased risk of cardiovascular events and motor vehicle accidents. [4, 5] As a result, there remains a need for opioid educational efforts that could help provider teams to identify and take steps to protect individuals in groups at higher risk of harm, which is a broader aim than preventing overdose alone. Targeted education on mitigating potential risks of opioids unique to individual patient populations may be one way to help.

Older adults (65 years and older) have unique considerations when it comes to opioids due to the physiological effects of aging and metabolism changes as well as chronic medical conditions requiring concurrent medications that may interact with opioids [6]. During our work we appreciated that there is limited literature on educational interventions for primary care clinicians specifically addressing the unique considerations in opioid prescribing for the older adult patient population. Consequently, there was the need to develop a new educational intervention to fill this gap. Because educational interventions on other topics for primary care clinicians have been shown to be efficacious [7], we felt that developing a similar intervention specifically about opioid use in older adults could also be helpful. Throughout this study, when we refer to opioid prescribing in older adults, we are referring to opioid prescribing for non-cancer pain. The objectives of our intervention were to educate participants on the prevalence of opioid prescriptions in older adults, describe the unique potential harms associated with opioid use in older adults, and offer practical strategies to reduce opioid related harm in the older adult patient population.

The Health Resources and Services Administration (HRSA) Geriatrics Workforce Enhancement Program (GWEP) aims to improve the health of older adults through healthcare workforce integration of geriatrics and primary care [8]. One of the goals of the GWEP is to enhance opioid education for healthcare providers and future health professionals. To achieve this goal, we developed and implemented an optional, brief educational intervention on opioid safety in older adults for practicing primary care clinicians and health professions students. 8 The current study aims to evaluate self-assessed knowledge, confidence, and prescribing behavior change by practicing clinicians after receiving the educational intervention, as opposed to our initial examination of changes in self-reported knowledge and confidence of health professions students and trainees [8]. While our previous study collected data from trainees (students, residents, fellows) and not practicing clinicians (attending physicians, practicing physician assistants and nurse practitioners), the current study focuses exclusively on practicing clinicians and not trainees.

We hypothesized that our brief educational intervention on the unique considerations of opioid use in older adults delivered to practicing primary care clinicians would result in increased knowledge and confidence about opioid use among clinicians as well as changes in clinicians’ prescribing and patient care practices one month after receiving the educational intervention.

Methods

The need for ethics approval and consent to participate was waived by the Institutional Review Board at the University of Nebraska Medical Center. This project was deemed not to constitute medical research involving human subjects by the Institutional Review Board at the University of Nebraska Medical Center and therefore the Helsinki Declaration for Medical Research Involving Human Subjects is not applicable.

A brief, 20-minute educational training was provided to health care practitioners in 13 primary care clinics at a large Midwestern academic medical center from January 2020 to June 2023. The training was optional though was recommended by clinic medical directors. The educational intervention focused on the limited evidence of benefit and unique harms of chronic opioid use in older adults, ways to screen for opioid use disorder, and strategies to reduce harms of opioid use particularly for older adults [8]. Education sessions were delivered either in person or synchronously using videoconferencing software. The sessions were recorded and video links for asynchronous viewing of the educational intervention were sent to clinicians unable to attend the scheduled trainings.

At the end of each training session, we asked participants to complete a post-training survey on paper or via an online link. Two additional weekly reminder emails were sent to participants who did not complete the initial post-training survey. A follow-up survey was sent to all participants one month after the training session to assess changes in clinical practices and prescribing behaviors. The surveys utilized in this study were developed specifically for this project and have not been published elsewhere. The surveys were adapted from the previously validated retrospective pretest survey developed by Allen and Nimon (2007) [9]. This instrument was selected because this retrospective approach reduces response-shift bias and provides a more accurate assessment of participants’ perceived changes following the intervention. Please see Supplementary files for more information on the surveys.

The initial post training survey assessed perceived knowledge and confidence with non-opioid medication options and opioid safety strategies before (pre-intervention) and after the session (post-intervention) on a Likert scale of 1 = Very Low to 5 = Very High; and potential use of strategies to address opioid use in their clinical practice. The one-month follow-up survey asked if the training participants had started to implement opioid safety strategies with their patients. We also collected demographic information such as age, gender, race and ethnicity, and health professions discipline. The participants provided both pre- and post- scores on perceived knowledge and confidence during the post-session survey and participants were matched on pre/post scores.

We used descriptive statistics for demographic characteristics and the planned and actual use of opioid safety strategies. We assessed pre- and post-training perceived knowledge and confidence levels and the pre- and -post changes in these measures using the Wilcoxon Signed Rank Test. Physicians and advanced practice providers (physician assistants and nurse practitioners) were aggregated in our analysis because these providers have the same prescribing privileges as physicians in our state. All analyses were performed using SPSS version 29.0.1.1.

Results

Healthcare practitioners from 13 primary care clinics completed a total of 176 participant sessions. Of them, 65 responses (37%) from 57 unique participants of the initial post-training survey were collected – this included 50 individuals who completed a single survey, 6 individuals who completed two, and one individual who completed three surveys. Of the 57 participants who responded to the initial post-training survey, 24 (42%) responded to the one-month follow-up survey. Each of the 13 primary care clinics received one session and two clinics had a repeat session a year later due to staff turnover. A total of 15 sessions were conducted.

Of the 57 practicing primary care clinicians who attended an educational session at least one time during the study period and completed the post-training survey, 54 participants (83.1%) also provided demographic information. Among these 54 participants, the disciplines represented were Medicine (medical doctors or doctors of osteopathic medicine) (63%), Physician Assistants (7.4%), Nursing (nurse practitioners) (27.8%), and Pharmacy (pharmacists) (1.9%). There were no trainees or resident physicians, and all respondents were independently practicing clinicians. Additional demographic data such as race/ethnicity distributions are listed in Table 1.

The post-training survey results showed a statistically significant increase in participant perceived knowledge in the ability to prescribe medications safely to an older person (pre-education mean score 3.51, post-education mean score 4.18, p < .0001 by Wilcoxon signed-rank test), perceived knowledge of the effectiveness of long-term opioids for chronic pain (pre-education mean score 3.63, post-education mean score 4.26, p < .0001) and confidence in suggesting “non-opioid treatment options” for older adults (pre-education mean score 3.78, post-education mean score 4.38, p < .0001) (Table 2). Given the inclusion of individuals who completed more than a single survey, we were concerned about the influence of these responses on the group results. But the results were largely unchanged, with significant increases in all three measures of perceived knowledge and confidence remaining at p < .001 level even after excluding the eight survey responses from repeat attendees.

The initial post-training survey queried participants’ intention to apply strategies to address opioid use in their patients with the question “As a result of participating in this session, which strategy(ies) would you use to reduce opioid prescribing for older adults?” with 6 different strategies listed, allowing participants to select all that apply. Suggesting non-opioid treatments was the most frequently selected strategy (80% of participants) followed by educating patients about opioid risks (78.5%), reviewing the prescription drug monitoring program (PDMP) for other providers prescribing opioids (69.2%), prescribing opioids for short durations (69.2%), offering treatment for opioid use disorder (61.5%), and one participant selected none of the strategies (Table 3). Among the 18 participants who were shown the additional option,” Discuss Approaches with Patients to Reduce Opioid Use”, that wasadded partway through the study, 11 (61.1%) indicated they would discuss approaches with patients to reduce opioid use (Table 3).

Of the 24 participants who completed both the initial post-training survey and the one-month follow-up survey, 19 participants reported in the initial survey that they would educate patients about opioid risks as a strategy to address opioid use. Of these 19 participants, 14 (74%) reported that they had implemented this strategy at the 1-month follow-up. Of the 18 participants who identified ‘suggesting non-opioid treatments’ as an intended strategy, 14 (78%) reported implementing this strategy at 1-month follow-up. Of the 20 participants who intended to prescribe opioids for short durations, 8 (40%) reported implementing this strategy at the 1-month follow-up. Of the 20 participants who identified reviewing the PDMP as an intended strategy, 6 (30%) reported implementing this strategy at the 1-month follow-up. Only 25% of participants intending to offer treatment for opioid use disorder reported implementing that strategy at the 1-month follow-up (Table 4). We also found that of the subset of participants offered the option of “Discuss Approaches with Patients to Reduce Opioid Use”, 2 participants intended to implement, and 2 (100%) had done so at the 1-month follow-up (Table 4).

We found that certain opioid safety strategies were self-reported as being implemented at the one-month follow-up more frequently than others (Table 4). Only 25% of those who planned to offer treatment for opioid use disorder reported having done so at one month follow-up, as compared to 100% of those who planned to discuss approaches to reduce opioid use, 78% of those who planned to suggest non-opioid treatments to patients and 74% who planned to educate patients about opioid risks. We did not have valuable feedback provided in the open-ended item about barriers to help us postulate why certain strategies were implemented more than others. While we do not know why certain strategies were implemented more than others, we speculate that clinicians did not feel sufficiently knowledgeable or confident with the strategies that were less frequently self-reported as being implemented. This suggests that the clinicians in our study may benefit from enhanced education on those strategies including potential situations in which to practice them to increase mastery and the potential likelihood they would be implemented in real-world practice. The least chosen strategy (intention to apply) was “Offer treatment for opioid use disorder.” As a result, it is possible that practitioners felt ill-equipped to initiate and carry out such discussions or found connecting individuals with treatment options difficult. Given the wide variety of patients seen by primary care clinicians, it is also possible that one month was an insufficient time frame in which to implement certain strategies as there may not have been patients presenting to clinic for whom those strategies would have been appropriate during that particular month.

Discussion

To our knowledge, there were no other educational sessions on opioid prescribing for older adults in the recent past within our healthcare system. Compared with prior to the educational intervention, we found an increase in perceived knowledge of the effectiveness of long-term opioids for chronic pain and confidence in suggesting non-opioid treatment options among all participants after the training. This is encouraging given the multidisciplinary nature of modern-day primary care practice, as it suggests that instituting brief educational interventions on other topics may also be potentially impactful for multidisciplinary clinicians in the primary care setting, particularly those from medicine, physician assistant studies, pharmacy, and nursing.

There is no literature specifically evaluating primary care clinician perceived knowledge and confidence in addressing opioid use in older adults following a brief educational intervention, and therefore direct comparisons to prior work cannot be made. However, there are several similarly themed studies on educational interventions on topics relevant to older adult patients in primary care. As part of another GWEP initiative, Lally and colleagues (2019) instituted several hour-long sessions for multidisciplinary primary care clinicians on geriatric and palliative care principles with a focus on advanced care planning and assessing for frailty and functional decline. Although they found an increase in participants’ knowledge and confidence on basic geriatric assessment and goals of care, the educational intervention was not brief, and the study did not evaluate whether the self-reported increases in knowledge and confidence resulted in actual behavior changes [10]. Jennings and colleagues (2019) also found increased knowledge and confidence among 54 multidisciplinary primary care clinicians following a three-hour workshop on supporting patients with dementia. However, the educational intervention was lengthy and behavioral outcomes were likewise not assessed [11].

A pilot Veteran’s Health Administration study [12] evaluating the impact of a 70-minute web-based educational session on recognizing and addressing post-traumatic stress disorder for primary care clinicians was not specific to the older adult patient population but did have a one-month follow-up assessing retention of knowledge and utilization of skills taught in the training. At the initial post-test, “90% of participants anticipated being able to apply the training to their clinical practices and at the 30-day follow-up evaluation, 47% reported having done so.”12 However, at 70 minutes, the web-based educational intervention was not brief, and it was not synchronous to allow for individualized questions. In our study, which is dissimilar, all but one participant indicated planning to implement at least one of the taught opioid safety strategies in their practice, and, depending on the strategy, between 25%-78% of participants reported having implemented the planned strategy at one-month follow-up.

The literature also describes several forms of previous educational interventions about opioids that were not focused on the older adult patient. McCracken et al. describe an audit and feedback intervention in which primary care providers were mailed educational materials about opioid prescribing, however there was no resultant change in provider rates of prescribing opioid analgesics [13]. A subsequent study by Narayan et al. details the delivery of an educational webinar to primary care providers who were asked to answer multiple choice knowledge questions before and after the webinar [14]. This strategy yielded mixed results, but the webinar format was overall seen as more favorable than the mailed intervention alone. A follow-up qualitative study of interviews with participants attending the webinars revealed that one likely barrier to successful implementation of the training was individual provider familiarity and experiences with patients with opioid use disorder [15]. A key difference between our study and past approaches was our direct involvement of an addiction psychiatry specialist in the development and delivery of the educational intervention as well as the inclusion of educational content specifically addressing opioid use disorder. We also focused on smaller groups and utilized videoconferencing software that allowed for the intervention to be delivered despite any external influences such as public health emergencies.

Of note, it is unknown whether opioid prescribing policies deferentially impact older adult patients. It is possible that more lenient opioid prescribing policies could potentially discount alternate pain management strategies while more restrictive policies have the potential to reduce access even in settings where use could be safe and effective. We are unable to speculate further on this in the absence of published data.

The current work demonstrates that a brief educational intervention increased the perceived knowledge of the effectiveness of long-term opioids for chronic pain and confidence in suggesting non-opioid treatment options in all providers who responded immediately following the training delivery. Another important finding was the high percentage of self-reported implementation of strategies at the one-month follow-up among participants who indicated planning to use the strategies in the first post-intervention survey. A key strength of our study is its novelty because, to our knowledge, it is the first assessment of primary care clinician self-reported behaviors after a brief educational intervention tailored specifically around opioid use in older adults. While the scant existing literature on educational interventions for primary care clinicians about other topics concerning older adults focus on self-reported knowledge and confidence, our study expands on this by evaluating self-reported use of the safety strategies that were the focus of the intervention one month after the educational intervention. Clinician self-reported behavior changes suggest that a brief educational intervention such as ours could have real-world implications for patient care. Another strength of our work is the high rate of participation among the primary care clinics of our healthcare system. Participating clinics were recruited from our healthcare system’s primary care clinics in the Omaha area that opted to partner with our division for participation in the Nebraska Geriatrics Workforce Enhancement project. The high rate of participation of these clinics in the GWEP suggests that our participants included all of our healthcare system’s primary clinics in the Omaha area at the time of the project.

A limitation of our study is the relatively low response rate particularly at the one-month follow-up and that we may have missed a large population of people who did not make changes nor feel their knowledge or confidence improved so they declined to fill out the survey (i.e. the only people who responded were the ones who found the curriculum helpful). We also were limited in our ability to understand more about self-selected implementation plans and barriers by not including open-ended questions in our surveys.

Exploratory analysis indicated that at one-month follow-up, those who completed the survey did not differ from those not returning the survey except that men were somewhat more likely to complete the one-month follow-up survey. Because most providers at each clinic only attended a single session, we were unable to establish a dose-dependent relationship between number of sessions attended and changes in perceived knowledge/practice. Additionally, completing the survey was optional. We did not have information about the non-participants in the post-training survey for comparison purposes. We appreciate that the optional nature of the survey is a limitation of our work. Further, the lack of a 1-month follow up Likert scale is also a limitation, though this was not included because it was not the primary aim of the study. Lastly, we used the post-session evaluation survey to ask participants for pre-session and post-session knowledge and confidence which could contribute to potential bias and is a limitation. The limitations of self-reported data including social desirability bias, the lack of specific objective measures of knowledge or behavior change, and the small amount of time for respondents to incorporate changes to their practice (e.g. perhaps a great proportion of those who said they were going to incorporate changes to their practice simply did not have the opportunity to do so). Notably, our study did not focus on patient outcomes but rather clinician perceptions of knowledge, confidence, and intended behavior change.

Conclusion

In sum, opioids can pose unique risks for older adults and healthcare providers generally receive limited instruction on safe opioid prescribing specifically in this patient population. Our findings show that a brief educational intervention for multidisciplinary primary care clinicians may be associated with increased knowledge and confidence immediately after the intervention and a high likelihood of implementing some of the taught strategies one month later. Replication with a larger sample size and further research is needed to evaluate specific attributes of the training that are more effective than others, and a better understanding of why certain strategies may be implemented more often than others.
