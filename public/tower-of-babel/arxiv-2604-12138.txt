Aditya Agrawal, Alwarappan Nakkiran, Darshan Fofadiya, Alex Karlsson, Harsha Aduri

Abstract
. Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) systems have transformed how Large Language Models access external knowledge, but we find that current implementations exhibit a bias toward factual, objective content, as evidenced by existing benchmarks and datasets that prioritize objective retrieval. This factual bias—treating opinions and diverse perspectives as noise rather than information to be synthesized—limits RAG systems in real-world scenarios involving subjective content, from social media discussions to product reviews and policy debates. Beyond technical limitations, this bias poses risks to transparent and accountable AI: echo chamber effects that amplify dominant viewpoints, systematic underrepresentation of minority voices, and potential opinion manipulation through biased information synthesis. We formalize this limitation through the lens of uncertainty: factual queries involve epistemic uncertainty reducible through evidence, while opinion queries involve aleatoric uncertainty reflecting genuine heterogeneity in human perspectives. This distinction implies that factual RAG should minimize posterior entropy, whereas opinion-aware RAG must preserve it. Building on this theoretical foundation, we present an Opinion-Aware RAG architecture featuring LLM-based opinion extraction, entity-linked opinion graphs, and opinion-enriched document indexing. We evaluate our approach on e-commerce seller forum data, comparing an Opinion-Enriched knowledge base against a traditional baseline. Experiments demonstrate substantial improvements in retrieval diversity: +26.8% sentiment diversity, +42.7% entity match rate, and +31.6% author demographic coverage on entity-matched documents. Our results provide empirical evidence that treating subjectivity as a first-class citizen yields measurably more representative retrieval—a first step toward opinion-aware RAG systems. Future work includes custom retrievers optimized for our formalized opinion-aware loss function and end-to-end learning approaches that jointly optimize retrieval and generation for distributional fidelity.



1.
Introduction

Retrieval-Augmented Generation has become an important method for making Large Language Models more reliable and factually grounded
[
28
,
20
]
. By conditioning generation on external evidence retrieved at inference time, RAG systems have shown success in reducing hallucinations and improving performance across knowledge-intensive tasks
[
40
,
43
]
. Recent evaluations have confirmed RAG’s effectiveness in enabling accurate, up-to-date, and verifiable text generation across diverse applications
[
18
]
. This approach has enabled LLMs to access current information, provide transparent sourcing, and ground their outputs in verifiable evidence.

However, beneath this success lies a fundamental assumption that has largely gone unexamined: that knowledge bases should contain factually correct, objective information. This assumption manifests across multiple levels of the RAG ecosystem—from the datasets used for evaluation, to the retrieval mechanisms optimized for factual recall, to the generation objectives that prioritize consistency with ground truth answers. While this factual orientation has driven advances in question answering and knowledge synthesis, it leaves the handling of inherently subjective content—which pervades real-world information landscapes—as an unexamined, non-systematic capability rather than an explicit design goal.

Consider the disparity between how current systems handle these two queries:
“What is the corporate tax rate in the United States?”
versus
“What do small business owners think about the current tax policy?”
The first query has a definitive, factual answer that existing RAG systems excel at retrieving and presenting. The second query requires synthesizing diverse, potentially conflicting opinions from multiple sources. While some large language models demonstrate emergent opinion synthesis capabilities—potentially drawing from patterns learned during training (see Appendix
C
for an example from contemporary LLMs)—systematic RAG architectures designed explicitly for transparent, reproducible, and evaluable opinion synthesis remain largely underexplored. The difference between accidental capability and intentional design becomes important when deploying systems in applications where understanding the methodology, sources, and potential biases of opinion synthesis is essential.

This limitation becomes particularly pronounced in domains where understanding human opinion is crucial. Despite growing enterprise adoption across customer service, knowledge management, and domain-specific applications
[
71
,
59
,
16
]
, RAG systems remain optimized primarily for factual accuracy, creating a systematic gap in their ability to handle the subjective, opinion-rich content prevalent in market research, policy analysis, social listening, customer feedback synthesis, and personalized content generation. The timing is critical: as RAG deployments expand beyond traditional knowledge bases into social media, customer forums, and user-generated content
[
44
,
8
]
, the mismatch between factual-oriented architectures and opinion-rich data sources risks creating echo chambers that exclude minority perspectives and present artificial consensus where genuine disagreement exists. In these contexts, the goal is not to find
the
truth, but to understand and represent
multiple truths
as expressed through human experiences, preferences, and judgments.

Addressing this gap requires rethinking how systems handle different information types. Humans employ distinct cognitive mechanisms when processing factual versus socially-transmitted information
[
5
,
47
]
—understanding not just
what
is said but
who
says it and
why
. Current RAG systems lack these mechanisms, and approaches to “robustness” primarily focus on filtering contradictory information
[
7
,
68
]
, treating opinions as defects rather than valuable information to be synthesized.

This paper introduces
Opinion-Aware RAG
, a framework that explicitly models and leverages subjectivity throughout the retrieval-generation pipeline. Rather than seeking consensus or filtering disagreement, opinion-aware systems embrace the complexity of human discourse, providing users with nuanced understanding of how different groups, individuals, or contexts shape perspectives on topics of interest.

Our contributions are: (1) A novel theoretical framework formalizing opinion-aware retrieval through the lens of epistemic vs. aleatoric uncertainty (2) An Opinion-Aware RAG architecture—the first systematic approach to making retrieval systems opinion-aware—featuring LLM-based opinion extraction and entity-linked opinion graphs; (3) Empirical evaluation on e-commerce forum data demonstrating +26.8% sentiment diversity and +42.7% entity match rate, showing that opinion enrichment yields measurably more representative retrieval than semantic similarity alone; and (4) An evaluation framework measuring opinion coverage, perspective diversity, and representation fairness. While this paper presents a complete system—from theoretical framework to empirical validation—we view our most important contribution as drawing systematic attention to opinion-aware RAG as a research priority.

2.2.
Emergent Synthesis and Evaluation Blind Spots

Some LLMs demonstrate emergent opinion synthesis capabilities—responding to queries like
“What do small business owners think about current tax policy?”
with nuanced summaries capturing diverse viewpoints, acknowledging tensions, and presenting balanced perspectives rather than seeking single “correct” answers (see Appendix
C
). However, when responses sound balanced, it remains unclear whether this reflects genuine diversity in retrieved content or the model’s learned tendency—often attributed to RLHF—to present information evenhandedly regardless of input
[
46
]
. Without transparency into retrieval, users cannot distinguish substantive balance from stylistic hedging. This raises fundamental questions:

Retrieval Opacity.
How do retrieval systems translate opinion queries into search strategies that surface diverse perspectives rather than just semantically similar content? Current embedding models, trained primarily on factual content, may embed opposing opinions similarly if they discuss the same topics—making it difficult to distinguish “Tax cuts help small businesses” from “Tax cuts primarily benefit large corporations.” Retrieval systems provide no transparency into which perspectives were considered versus systematically excluded.

Synthesis Opacity.
Even when diverse opinions are retrieved, users cannot understand how conflicting perspectives were weighted or what constitutes “balanced representation.” Unlike factual retrieval where citation suffices, opinion synthesis requires understanding how conflicting viewpoints are reconciled.

The limitations of current evaluation approaches become apparent when examining how traditional RAG metrics can assign high scores to obviously biased outputs. Recent work on bias amplification
[
57
,
70
,
14
]
demonstrates that RAG systems can inherit and amplify biases present in their retrieval corpora—for instance, leadership discussions that overrepresent masculine-coded traits like “assertiveness” and “aggressive persistence” while underrepresenting collaborative qualities. If a knowledge base contains such skewed content, a query like
“What leadership qualities are most important for startup CEOs?”
would retrieve and synthesize this bias into the response (see Appendix
C.2
). Such a response could score excellently on traditional metrics—high ROUGE-L from lexical overlap with retrieved sources, strong context relevance from semantic alignment, high answer faithfulness from accurately reflecting the retrieval set—yet systematically underrepresent certain perspectives. This illustrates how RAG systems can produce outputs that are simultaneously high-quality by traditional measures and biased by human judgment.

Recent RAG research frames subjectivity as a problem to be solved rather than information to be leveraged. RAGuard
[

Work on argument-based retrieval has begun exploring how systems can handle argumentative content. RAFTS
[

synthesizes contrastive arguments for fact verification, explicitly retrieving both supporting and refuting evidence, though it focuses on claim credibility rather than synthesizing diverse perspectives. Other work explores argument mining

—even state-of-the-art models perform only slightly better than random guessing at contradiction detection, exhibiting high precision but low recall
[

Opinion mining and stance detection research has matured significantly

, but these capabilities remain largely isolated from RAG frameworks

,

. OpinioRAG
[

bridges this gap with RAG-based opinion summarization from product reviews. While OpinioRAG focuses on

—condensing retrieved opinions into coherent narratives—our work addresses the complementary challenge of

Sophisticated approaches have been developed for opinion-rich content on social networking websites, including stance detection

Simply combining these fragmented approaches is insufficient: factual RAG optimizes for convergence toward ground truth, while opinion-aware systems must preserve divergence, and no unified framework exists for jointly optimizing retrieval diversity, synthesis fidelity, and demographic fairness. This gap motivates our formal problem formulation.

4.
Theoretical Framework

We formalize the distinction between factual and opinion-aware retrieval through uncertainty quantification and information theory. Throughout, we use:
q
(query),
y
(response),
\mathcal{D}
(corpus),
\theta
(latent state),
P_{\text{pop}}(\theta)
(population opinion distribution),
\mathcal{R}(q)
(retrieved documents),
\phi:\mathcal{D}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
(opinion embedding),
H(\cdot)
(entropy),
D_{\text{KL}}
(KL divergence), and
\mathcal{W}_{p}
(Wasserstein distance).

4.1.
Epistemic vs. Aleatoric Uncertainty

Consider a query
q
and corpus
\mathcal{D}
. The posterior predictive distribution is:

(1)

P(y|q,\mathcal{D})=\int P(y|\theta,q)P(\theta|\mathcal{D})d\theta

This computes the probability of response
y
by considering all possible underlying states
\theta
, weighted by their likelihood given evidence
\mathcal{D}
.

For
factual queries
, a unique true state
\theta^{*}
exists. Uncertainty is
epistemic
—reducible through additional evidence. As corpus size grows, the posterior collapses: e.g., for “What is 2+2?”,
P(\theta|\mathcal{D})\to\delta(\theta-4)
, where
\delta(\cdot)
is the Dirac delta:

(2)

\lim_{|\mathcal{D}|\to\infty}P(\theta|\mathcal{D})=\delta(\theta-\theta^{*})

For
opinion queries
, uncertainty is
aleatoric
—reflecting genuine heterogeneity rather than ignorance. The posterior converges to the population distribution, not a point: e.g., for “Is pineapple on pizza good?”, we converge to perhaps 40% favor, 45% oppose, 15% indifferent:

(3)

\lim_{|\mathcal{D}|\to\infty}P(\theta|\mathcal{D})=P_{\text{pop}}(\theta)

This distinction has profound implications for system design:
factual RAG minimizes posterior entropy; opinion-aware RAG must preserve it.
We note that opinion queries still involve epistemic uncertainty about
P_{\text{pop}}
itself—O-RAG aims to reduce this epistemic component through comprehensive retrieval while preserving aleatoric uncertainty by representing discovered diversity rather than collapsing to a single view.

4.2.
Divergent Optimization Objectives

Standard RAG maximizes information gain by minimizing conditional entropy:

(4)

\mathcal{L}_{\text{factual}}=H(Y|q)-H(Y|q,\mathcal{R}(q))

This rewards retrieval that eliminates uncertainty—correct for facts, counterproductive for opinions.

For opinions, we seek
distributional fidelity
. Let
v
denote a position in opinion space and
P_{\text{pop}}(v|q)
the true opinion distribution. The opinion-aware objective is:

(5)

\mathcal{L}_{\text{OA}}=D_{\text{KL}}\left(P_{\text{pop}}(v|q)\|P_{\text{model}}(v|q)\right)-\lambda H(P_{\text{model}})

The first term drives distributional accuracy; the second (maximizing entropy) penalizes artificial consensus.

4.3.
Retrieval as Coverage Optimization

Let
\mathcal{R}(q)=\{d_{1},\ldots,d_{k}\}
. The embedding
\phi:\mathcal{D}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
maps documents to opinion space; in our implementation (Section
5
),
\phi
encodes sentiment, sentiment polarity, and topical framing. The empirical opinion distribution from retrieval is:

(6)

\hat{P}_{\mathcal{R}}=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\delta_{\phi(d_{i})}

where
\delta_{\phi(d_{i})}
is a point mass at document
d_{i}
’s opinion embedding.

We measure coverage using the
p
-Wasserstein distance:

(7)

\mathcal{W}_{p}(\hat{P}_{\mathcal{R}},P_{\text{pop}})=\left(\inf_{\gamma\in\Gamma(\hat{P}_{\mathcal{R}},P_{\text{pop}})}\int\|\phi-\phi^{\prime}\|^{p}\,d\gamma(\phi,\phi^{\prime})\right)^{1/p}

Intuitively,
\mathcal{W}_{p}
(earth mover’s distance) measures the minimum “transportation cost” to reshape one distribution into another—naturally penalizing retrieval that misses regions of opinion space. We prefer it over Jensen-Shannon divergence as it remains well-defined when distributions have non-overlapping support, as we demonstrate empirically in Section
6
.

4.4.
Generation as Calibrated Synthesis

Let
P_{\text{reader}}(\phi|y)
represent the opinion distribution a reader infers from response
y
. Faithful generation requires:

(8)

P_{\text{reader}}(\phi|y)\approx\sum_{i=1}^{k}w_{i}\,\delta_{\phi(d_{i})}

where
w_{i}
are importance weights (
\sum_{i}w_{i}=1
). This induces a calibration requirement:

(9)

\text{Var}_{P_{\text{reader}}}[\phi|y]\approx\text{Var}_{P_{\text{pop}}}[\phi|q]

Overconfident synthesis—low-variance output for high-variance opinion landscapes—misrepresents societal consensus.

4.5.
Unified Objective

Opinion-Aware RAG minimizes:

(10)

\mathcal{L}=\underbrace{\mathcal{W}_{2}(P_{\text{pop}},\hat{P}_{\mathcal{R}})}_{\text{coverage}}+\alpha\underbrace{D_{\text{KL}}(P_{\text{pop}}\|P_{\text{reader}})}_{\text{fidelity}}+\beta\underbrace{\max_{g\in G}\Delta_{g}}_{\text{fairness}}

The first term ensures retrieval captures the opinion spectrum via 2-Wasserstein distance. The second ensures faithful communication to readers (
\alpha>0
). The third enforces demographic equity:
\Delta_{g}=D_{\text{KL}}(P_{\text{pop}}(\phi|g)\|\hat{P}_{\mathcal{R}}(\phi|g))
measures representation disparity for demographic group
g\in G
(e.g., business size, tenure, region). By minimizing maximum group disparity, we adopt a minimax fairness criterion ensuring no subpopulation is systematically excluded (
\beta>0
).

We note key assumptions: (1)
P_{\text{pop}}
is estimated from corpus
\mathcal{D}
, treating it as a population sample; (2) the framework assumes static opinions (see Section
10
); (3) query classification (a pre-processor) handles factual/opinion ambiguity (Section
5
). Computationally, directly optimizing Equation 10 is prohibitive; our architecture approximates this through opinion-enriched indexing with author attributes that support fairness analysis, achieving strong coverage without explicit Wasserstein computation.

This formulation reveals Opinion-Aware RAG as a
distribution estimation and communication problem
, fundamentally distinct from the point-estimation paradigm underlying factual retrieval.

5.1.
General Framework

The key insight is that traditional RAG optimizes for semantic similarity, which tends to retrieve documents expressing similar viewpoints. By enriching documents with structured opinion metadata
before
indexing, we enable retrieval that accounts for opinion diversity—operationalizing the coverage objective from Section
4
.

The framework consists of three domain-agnostic components:

Entity Registry.
A hierarchical taxonomy of opinion targets organized by granularity. Entities represent the “what” of opinions—topics, policies, products, or concepts that people hold views about. A tiered structure (general
\rightarrow
specific
\rightarrow
granular) enables flexible aggregation and analysis.

Opinion Attributes.
Structured metadata capturing the nature of expressed opinions. Core attributes include:

•

Sentiment & Intensity
: Polarity and strength of the opinion

•

Stance
: Position on a spectrum (e.g., strongly against to strongly for)

•

Impact Assessment
: Perceived severity or importance

•

Evidence Type
: Basis for the opinion (personal experience, anecdotal, data-driven)

Author Attributes.
Contextual factors about opinion holders that enable fairness analysis. These capture demographic or experiential characteristics relevant to the domain, supporting the fairness term
\max_{g\in G}\Delta_{g}
in our objective (Section
4
).

Per-Entity Document Splitting.
Each source document is split into multiple enriched documents—one per mentioned entity. This ensures that when a document discusses multiple topics with different sentiments, each opinion is indexed separately with its corresponding metadata. The enriched documents embed opinion and author attributes alongside content, making this information available during retrieval and generation.

This architecture directly operationalizes the theoretical framework: per-entity splitting with opinion metadata enables retrieval that minimizes distributional divergence
\mathcal{W}_{2}(P_{\text{pop}},\hat{P}_{\mathcal{R}})
, while structured attributes support analysis of demographic representation.

5.2.
Case Study: E-commerce Seller Forums

We instantiate this framework for e-commerce seller discussion forums, where merchants discuss policies, fees, and operational challenges. This domain is well-suited for studying opinion-aware RAG because: (1) sellers hold genuinely diverse views on policies, (2) author characteristics (business size, tenure) meaningfully influence perspectives, and (3) the content is naturally opinionated with real business implications.

Entity Registry.
We construct a 98-entity registry organized across three tiers: Tier 1 covers broad categories (e.g., “Shipping”, “Fees”), Tier 2 captures intermediate topics (e.g., “Fulfillment Fees”), and Tier 3 addresses specific aspects (e.g., “Storage Fee Changes”). The registry is constructed through domain expert curation and LLM-assisted extraction, seeding Tier 1 from service taxonomy and using clustering to identify finer-grained entities.

Opinion Attributes.
We extract five attributes per entity mention:
sentiment
(positive, negative, neutral, or mixed),
intensity
(low, medium, high),
stance
(five-level scale from strongly against to strongly for),
business impact
(assessed severity), and
evidence type
(personal experience, anecdotal, or data-driven).

Author Attributes.
We capture three seller characteristics:
business size
(revenue band when disclosed),
tenure
(experience level with the service), and
region
(geographic location when available).

Implementation.
We use Claude Sonnet 4.5 with a structured output schema for extraction (see Appendix
C.3
). Validation against manually annotated samples yields
>
90% precision, recall, and F1, confirming reliable metadata extraction. For our 6-month corpus, covering 10K+ discussions, avg. 300 words per discussion, enrichment took approximately 4 hours using parallelized LLM calls at a cost of approx. $200 using AWS Bedrock.

6.
Experiments

We design experiments to evaluate whether opinion enrichment improves the diversity of perspectives surfaced by RAG systems, guided by hypotheses derived from our theoretical framework (Section
4
).

6.1.
Hypotheses

Our theoretical framework identifies three components of the opinion-aware objective (Equation 10): coverage, fidelity, and fairness. We translate these into testable hypotheses:

H1 (Coverage):

Opinion enrichment improves retrieval coverage of the underlying opinion distribution.

\mathcal{W}_{2}(P_{\text{pop}},\hat{P}_{\mathcal{R}})
, the coverage term measures how well retrieved documents represent the full opinion spectrum. We hypothesize that embedding opinion metadata enables retrieval that better spans diverse viewpoints.

H2 (Fidelity):

Opinion-enriched retrieval leads to responses that better communicate opinion diversity to readers.
The fidelity term
D_{\text{KL}}(P_{\text{pop}}\|P_{\text{reader}})
captures whether generated responses faithfully convey the retrieved opinion distribution.

H3 (Fairness):

Opinion-enriched retrieval improves demographic representation in retrieved content.
The fairness term
\max_{g\in G}\Delta_{g}
penalizes under-representation of demographic groups. We hypothesize that structured author attributes enable more equitable retrieval across subpopulations.

6.2.
Evaluation Framework

6.2.1.
Dataset

We use seller discussion data similar to publicly accessible e-commerce seller forums such as Amazon Seller Forums, eBay Community, and Etsy Community. Our dataset spans six months of discussions, comprising approximately 8,000 unique sellers and averaging 500 discussions per week. These forums contain rich opinionated content where sellers discuss policies, fees, operational challenges, and business strategies—making them ideal for studying opinion diversity in RAG systems.

6.2.2.
Knowledge Base Configurations

We construct two knowledge base configurations using identical source content:

Raw Discussions KB (Baseline):
Documents are indexed with their original content only, without any opinion metadata. This represents a traditional RAG setup where retrieval is based purely on semantic similarity.

Opinion-Enriched KB (Ours):
Documents are enriched with structured opinion and author attributes as described in Section
5
, then indexed with embedded metadata. Each document is split per-entity, with opinion attributes included in the indexed content.

Both configurations use Amazon OpenSearch Serverless with Titan Text Embeddings v2 (1024 dimensions) and fixed-size chunking (4000 tokens, 15% overlap). We compare against Raw KB rather than diversity-aware retrieval (e.g., MMR) because our contribution is at indexing, not retrieval—and MMR optimizes for semantic diversity, not opinion diversity.

6.2.3.
Evaluation Protocol

Our evaluation targets entities with high opinion diversity—topics where the underlying corpus contains genuinely mixed perspectives. We follow a four-stage protocol:

1. Entity Selection:
We select entities with genuine opinion diversity by computing sentiment entropy. For each entity
e
, let
p_{s}
denote the proportion of opinions with sentiment
s
(positive, negative, or neutral). We compute:

(11)

H(e)=-\sum_{s}p_{s}\log_{2}(p_{s})

We additionally require that entities have both positive and negative sentiments represented. Entities with
H(e)\geq 0.6
and at least 5 associated opinions are selected for evaluation.

2. Question Generation:
For each selected entity, we generate natural language questions across six question types using Claude Sonnet 4.5, with manual review for quality. We select 4 entities and generate 2 questions per type (48 total):

•

Detailed Overview
: “What do sellers think about X?”

•

Experience
: “What are sellers’ experiences with X?”

•

Business Impact
: “How does X affect sellers’ businesses?”

•

Specific Examples
: “What examples do sellers give about X?”

•

Process
: “What do sellers say about the X process?”

•

Emotional
: “How do sellers feel about X?”

3. Retrieval and Response Generation:
Each question is submitted to both KB configurations. We retrieve the top-
k
documents using different retrieval algorithms and generate responses using Claude Sonnet 4.5 with the retrieved context. Ablation studies (Section
8
) examine the impact of retrieval depth and different retrieval algorithms.

4. Metrics Computation:
Retrieved documents are matched back to the enriched opinion metadata to compute diversity metrics. We treat the LLM-extracted opinion metadata as ground truth based on manual validation showing
>
90% precision, recall, and F1 on manually annotated samples.

6.3.
Metrics

The theoretical objectives (Equation 10) involve distributional quantities that are difficult to compute directly. We design practical proxy metrics to operationalize each hypothesis:

Coverage Metrics (H1).
We approximate the coverage term
\mathcal{W}_{2}(P_{\text{pop}},\hat{P}_{\mathcal{R}})
using variance-based measures that capture how well retrieval spans the opinion space:

•

Sentiment-Intensity
\sigma
: Combines sentiment polarity and intensity into a single score (e.g., Positive+High = +30, Negative+High = -30, Neutral = 0). Higher
\sigma
indicates broader coverage of the sentiment-intensity space.

•

Business Impact
\sigma
: Maps impact levels to numeric values (High = 30, Medium = 20, Low = 10). Higher
\sigma
indicates retrieval of both high-impact and low-impact perspectives.

•

Entity Match Rate
: Fraction of retrieved documents that mention the queried entity, measuring retrieval precision for opinion-relevant content.

Fidelity Metric (H2).
The fidelity term
D_{\text{KL}}(P_{\text{pop}}\|P_{\text{reader}})
measures whether readers perceive the full opinion spectrum—a quantity we cannot compute directly. We approximate this through human evaluation: five annotators compared response pairs (KB identity hidden) on sentiment diversity, providing binary preferences with ties allowed.

Fairness Metrics (H3).
We approximate the fairness term using categorical coverage (
\max_{g\in G}\Delta_{g}
):

•

GMS Band Coverage
: Fraction of business size categories represented

•

Tenure Coverage
: Fraction of experience levels represented

•

Evidence Type Coverage
: Fraction of evidence types (personal, anecdotal, data-driven) represented

Results

We evaluate our hypotheses using hybrid retrieval with
k=20
documents and Claude Sonnet 4.5 for retrieval & response generation. Results are averaged across 48 evaluation questions spanning 4 entities and 6 question types. Statistical significance is assessed via Wilcoxon signed-rank test (
n=48
paired questions), a non-parametric test appropriate for paired samples without normality assumptions.

7.1.
H1: Coverage Improvement

Finding: Supported.
Opinion enrichment significantly improves retrieval coverage of the opinion distribution. Table
2
shows that at
k=20
, Opinion-Enriched KB achieves +26.8%, +14.5%, and +42.7% improvements in sentiment-intensity
\sigma
, business impact
\sigma
, and entity match rate respectively, all at p
<
0.05.

These improvements indicate that embedding opinion metadata enables retrieval that better spans the sentiment-intensity space, operationalizing the coverage term
\mathcal{W}_{2}(P_{\text{pop}},\hat{P}_{\mathcal{R}})
from our theoretical framework. The entity match rate improvement suggests that per-entity splitting provides better semantic anchoring for opinion-relevant content.

7.2.
H2: Fidelity to Readers

Finding: Supported.
Human annotators confirmed that opinion-enriched retrieval produces responses that better communicate opinion diversity. Of 48 question-response pairs, annotators preferred Opinion-Enriched KB responses in 38 cases (79.2% win rate, 95% CI: [65.0%, 89.5%], p
<
0.001).

This result validates that the automated coverage metrics (H1) translate to perceptible improvements in response quality—readers infer a more diverse opinion distribution from enriched responses, reducing the fidelity gap
D_{\text{KL}}(P_{\text{pop}}\|P_{\text{reader}})
.

7.3.
H3: Fairness

Finding: Not supported.
Contrary to our hypothesis, opinion enrichment reduces demographic coverage. At
k=20
, GMS Band coverage drops by 13.0% and tenure coverage by 6.9%, both at p
<
0.05.

This reveals a trade-off in our current approach: per-entity splitting improves opinion coverage but concentrates retrieval on documents mentioning specific entities, which may come from a narrower author demographic. Evidence type coverage shows slight improvement (+4.7%), suggesting this trade-off is specific to author attributes.

Addressing this limitation requires retrieval strategies that explicitly optimize the fairness term
\max_{g\in G}\Delta_{g}
—an important direction for future work (Section
10
). Section
8
examines how this trade-off varies with retrieval depth, showing that larger
k
partially mitigates the fairness penalty while preserving coverage gains.

7.4.
Summary

H1 (coverage) and H2 (fidelity) are supported, validating that opinion enrichment improves both retrieval diversity and response quality. H3 (fairness) is not supported—demographic coverage decreases, revealing a limitation of our current approach that motivates future work on multi-objective retrieval. Appendix
D
provides qualitative examples showing how enriched responses capture sentiment distributions and constructive suggestions missing from baseline responses.

9.
Challenges and Call to Action

Technical Challenges.
Current RAG benchmarks lack datasets with naturally occurring subjective content and rich annotations for stance, sentiment, and perspective diversity. Opinion-aware architectures must balance faithfulness to diverse sources with coherent generation, avoiding bias toward dominant opinions while maintaining efficiency.

Ethical Considerations.
Without opinion-aware capabilities, systems risk algorithmic opinion manipulation through training biases, echo chamber amplification by retrieving only confirming information, and minority voice erasure by underrepresenting marginalized viewpoints. The goal is not eliminating bias but making it transparent and auditable.

Community Call to Action.
We call on the research community to pursue: (1) benchmark development with subjective annotations and opinion synthesis metrics; (2) foundation model research on opinion-aware tasks; (3) retrieval algorithms for diverse perspective identification; and (4) evaluation protocols for subjective content synthesis.

10.
Future Work

Our experiments validate key aspects of the theoretical framework in Section
4
: opinion enrichment improves retrieval coverage (
\mathcal{W}_{2}
reduction through increased sentiment diversity) and entity-relevant retrieval (better alignment with
P_{\text{pop}}
). However, fully realizing opinion-aware RAG requires addressing several open challenges.

Representation Learning for Subjective Content.
Our opinion extraction relies on LLM-based annotation, but the theoretical framework’s opinion embedding function
\phi:\mathcal{D}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
suggests the need for learned representations that jointly encode semantic content with subjective attributes. Future work should explore multi-task learning objectives and hierarchical embedding spaces where documents can be similar at the topic level while differing at the stance level.

Optimizing the Coverage Term.
While our diversity metrics approximate the coverage objective
\mathcal{W}_{2}(P_{\text{pop}},\hat{P}_{\mathcal{R}})
, we do not directly optimize this during retrieval. Query expansion techniques generating stance-diverse queries, and re-ranking mechanisms using submodular optimization, could more directly minimize distributional divergence.

Fairness Constraints.
The framework’s fairness term
\max_{g\in G}\Delta_{g}
addresses demographic equity, but our ablations reveal trade-offs between opinion diversity and author demographic coverage. Future work must develop retrieval strategies that jointly optimize for both, ensuring no subpopulation is systematically under-represented.

Temporal Dynamics.
The framework assumes a static
P_{\text{pop}}
, but opinions evolve continuously. Extending the theoretical foundation to handle temporal opinion dynamics—distinguishing temporary reactive opinions from lasting attitudinal changes—remains an important direction.

11.
Conclusion

We presented Opinion-Aware RAG, an architecture that treats subjectivity as a first-class citizen rather than noise to be filtered. Our novel theoretical framework distinguishes factual queries (minimize posterior entropy) from opinion queries (preserve entropy reflecting genuine perspective heterogeneity), formalizing opinion-aware retrieval through coverage, fidelity, and fairness objectives.

Experiments on e-commerce seller forum data demonstrate that opinion enrichment yields measurably more representative retrieval: +26.8% sentiment diversity, +42.7% entity match rate, and +4.7% evidence type coverage. Human evaluation confirms these gains translate to perceptible quality improvements, with annotators preferring Opinion-Enriched responses 79.2% of the time (p
<
0.001). Ablations reveal that simply retrieving more documents does not achieve comparable diversity—opinion metadata is essential for surfacing varied perspectives.

Our work represents a first step. The trade-off between opinion and author demographic diversity motivates future research on multi-objective retrieval. Fully realizing the theoretical framework requires learned opinion embeddings, direct optimization of distributional coverage, and evaluation metrics for generation fidelity. As AI systems increasingly mediate access to information, their ability to fairly represent diverse perspectives becomes essential for transparent and accountable AI.

Extended Response Examples

C.1.
Emergent Opinion Synthesis Example

The following is a complete example response from a contemporary LLM (e.g., GPT-4.5, Claude Sonnet 4.5) to the query:
“What do small business owners think about the current tax policy?”

Small business owners express mixed views on current tax policy. Many appreciate efforts toward permanence and certainty, particularly wanting key provisions from the 2017 Tax Cuts and Jobs Act made permanent to aid long-term planning. They support targeted deductions and pass-through relief, especially proposals allowing deduction of the first $25,000 of qualifying business income. However, concerns persist: many feel previous tax cuts primarily benefited larger corporations rather than small businesses. Common complaints include tax code complexity, high compliance costs, and perceived unfairness in how large firms can leverage specialists and loopholes. Small business owners consistently advocate for simplified reporting, scaled incentives that actually help smaller firms, and measures ensuring “fair play” against large corporation advantages.

C.2.
Biased Leadership Response Example

The following illustrates how traditional RAG metrics can assign high scores to biased outputs. In response to the query
“What leadership qualities are most important for startup CEOs?”
, a system might generate:

Successful startup CEOs demonstrate strong analytical thinking, decisive action under pressure, and natural assertiveness in competitive markets. Research shows that effective leaders maintain composure during high-stakes negotiations, exhibit confidence in pitching to investors, and demonstrate the aggressive persistence needed to disrupt established industries. The most successful CEOs are typically those who embrace calculated risks, maintain unwavering conviction in their vision, and possess the competitive drive necessary to outperform rivals.

While scoring excellently across standard metrics, this response amplifies gender bias by emphasizing traditionally masculine-coded traits while omitting collaborative, empathetic, or inclusive leadership qualities.

C.3.
Opinion Extraction Prompt

We use Claude Sonnet 4.5 with a structured output schema for opinion extraction. The prompt template is shown below (with service-specific terms anonymized):

You are an expert at analyzing seller opinions from e-commerce service content. Analyze the following post and extract structured opinion information for EACH entity (business topic/issue) mentioned.

POST CONTENT: {content}

SERVICE: {service_type}

CATEGORY: {service_category}

{entity_registry}

Extract the following information and respond ONLY with valid JSON:

{"entities": [{
"entity_name": "entity name",
"confidence": "Low|Medium|High",
"sentiment": "positive|negative|neutral|mixed",
"sentiment_intensity": "Low|Medium|High",
"emotional_markers": ["marker1", "marker2"],
"evidence": "supporting evidence",
"business_impact": "Low|Medium|High",
"context_snippet": "relevant quote",
"evidence_type": "personal|anecdotal|data"
}]}

Key extraction guidelines include:

•

Entity matching
: If the entity matches one from the hierarchical registry, use that exact name; prefer granular (Tier 3) over general (Tier 1) entities

•

Per-entity extraction
: Each entity receives its own complete opinion structure; a single post may yield multiple entities with different sentiments

•

Evidence typing
: Classify as personal (first-hand experience), anecdotal (heard from others), or data (cites specific statistics)

•

Emotional markers
: Common markers include Frustration, Anger, Resignation, Adaptation, Hope, Satisfaction, Confusion, Anxiety

•

Business impact
: Consider financial mentions, operational disruption, and account risks when assigning impact levels

The model outputs a JSON array with one object per entity-opinion pair, enabling downstream per-entity document splitting.
