Tumor-associated collagen signature in relation to metastasis in lung adenocarcinoma
Authors: Michael H Udin, Sarmila Nepali, Swati Sonkawade, Scott T Doyle, Ciprian N Ionita, Yongdong Ouyang, Umesh C Sharma, Saraswati Pokharel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), a subset of tumors metastasizes to lymph nodes while others do not, despite having a similar tumor stage (T stage). The tumor extracellular matrix (ECM), particularly collagen and elastin, may play a critical role in this variability. This study aimed to determine whether differences in collagen architecture and elastin content within the tumor ECM are associated with susceptibility to nodal metastasis in LUAD.MethodsLUAD tissue samples were obtained from 120 patients (54 metastatic and 66 non-metastatic) diagnosed between 2005 and 2022 at Roswell Park Comprehensive Cancer Center. Sections were stained with Masson’s trichrome and Elastica Van Gieson on separate slide replicates. Collagen and elastin volume fractions were quantified using Aperio ImageScope. Collagen fiber alignment was assessed using second harmonic generation (SHG) imaging and analyzed with CurveAlign software.ResultsMetastatic tumors demonstrated significantly higher unidirectional alignment of collagen fibers compared to the crisscross pattern seen in non-metastatic tumors (0.87 vs. 0.78, P=0.003). Elastin volume fraction was significantly lower in metastatic tumors (0.29 vs. 0.38, P=0.001), while total collagen content did not differ significantly (P=0.87). The elastin-to-collagen ratio was significantly reduced in metastatic tumors (P=0.002), indicating a shift in ECM composition rather than an absolute increase in collagen.ConclusionsDecreased elastin content and increased collagen fiber alignment are associated with nodal metastasis in LUAD and may reflect alterations in stromal organization and stiffness that promote tumor dissemination. These ECM features may serve as potential biomarkers for identifying LUAD patients at higher risk for nodal metastasis. Further large-scale studies are needed for validation.Keywords: Lung cancer, adenocarcinoma (ADC), metastasis, elastin, collagen

Highlight box

Key findings

• Metastatic lung adenocarcinomas (LUADs) showed significantly greater collagen fiber alignment than non-metastatic tumors, indicating a more organized stromal architecture.

• Elastin content was significantly lower in metastatic tumors, whereas total collagen content did not differ between groups.

• The elastin-to-collagen ratio was significantly reduced in metastatic tumors, reflecting altered extracellular matrix (ECM) composition rather than increased collagen quantity.

• These findings suggest that ECM remodeling, marked by increased collagen alignment and reduced elastin, may contribute to a pro-metastatic microenvironment.

What is known and what is new?

• Tumor progression and metastasis are influenced by interactions between cancer cells and the ECM. Increased collagen fiber alignment has been associated with tumor invasion and poor prognosis in multiple cancers, including lung cancer. However, reliable biomarkers for predicting nodal metastasis in LUAD remain limited, and the role of elastin in metastasis is poorly understood.

• Metastatic LUADs exhibited increased collagen alignment, reduced elastin content, and a lower elastin-to-collagen ratio. Total collagen content remained unchanged, highlighting ECM organization rather than collagen abundance as a feature of metastasis. These findings identify elastin as an underrecognized component of the metastatic tumor microenvironment.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Collagen alignment and elastin content may serve as biomarkers of nodal metastasis risk.

• ECM assessment could improve prognostic evaluation beyond conventional staging.

• Larger multicenter studies and automated assessment methods are needed for validation.

• Incorporating stromal biomarkers into clinical decision-making may improve surgical planning, lymph node assessment, and adjuvant therapy selection.

Introduction

There are estimated to be more than 230,000 new cases of lung cancer each year in the US, with the disease responsible for over 125,000 deaths annually (1). Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) is the most prevalent subtype, accounting for 75–80% of all lung cancer cases, with adenocarcinoma (ADC) representing 40% of NSCLC cases (2).

Management of lung cancer relies heavily on the tumor stage, which is largely dictated by nodal and extra-nodal metastasis. Although advances in cancer diagnosis have improved detection, accurately distinguishing between less aggressive or more aggressive lung cancer with likelihood of metastasis remains challenging. Identification of nodal metastasis often relies on a combination of imaging [e.g., positron emission tomography-computed tomography (PET-CT)], fine needle aspiration cytology, or biopsy, which sometimes requires multiple attempts and can be time-consuming and costly. In addition, advanced imaging techniques and cytology may still have limitations in detecting early or subtle metastases due to sampling issues (3). The presence of metastasis in regional lymph nodes significantly influences treatment decisions and survival rates in ADC (4). Patients undergoing surgical resection for ADC necessitate adjuvant chemotherapy upon the detection of lymph node metastases post-surgically. Surgical resection decisions, including the extent of lymph node removal and type of resection, depend heavily on presurgical evaluations and overall patient clinical status (5). The standard imaging modality, PET-CT, lacks complete accuracy in detecting nodal metastasis, which often results in undertreatment (5,6). Moreover, certain regional mediastinal lymph node stations remain inaccessible during standard video-assisted thoracic surgery procedures, which increases the risk of leaving metastatic nodes unresected. Tumor size is an important factor in staging and prognosis. However, patients with tumors of the same tumor stage (T stage) do not always follow the same disease trajectory, with some developing metastases while others do not. This was exemplified in a study by Bille et al., who found that tumor size was not significantly associated with metastatic NSCLC in their multivariate analysis (7). Therefore, identification of specific biomarkers for aggressive tumors with lymph node involvement is imperative to develop goal-directed therapies.

Although many studies have explored potential biomarkers for lung cancer screening, there is still no established biomarker for predicting metastatic potential in lung cancer (8). Interaction between tumor cells and extracellular matrix (ECM) plays a crucial role in cancer progression. Recently, several studies in breast cancer showed differences in collagen fiber organization between more and less aggressive invasive breast cancer in both in-vivo and in-vitro models (9-13). Few studies have also shown that collagen fiber alignment is a potential prognostic factor in lung cancer, linking it to differences in long-term survival (14,15). However, the relationship to these changes in collagen and metastasis remains unclear. Additionally, ECM in normal lung is rich in elastic tissue comprising 20–30% of the lung parenchyma, while elastin has been shown to have a role in the behavior of certain cancer; its relationship to metastatic changes in lung cancer has not yet been established (16-18).

In this study, we examine collagen and elastin in lung ADC (LUAD) tissue, quantifying the structure and content of collagen through second harmonic generation (SHG) imaging and Masson’s trichrome staining, respectively, and assessing elastin levels through the use of Elastica Van Gieson staining. We aim to test the hypothesis that collagen architecture and elastin content are associated with nodal metastasis in LUAD. We present this article in accordance with the REMARK reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-1-0356/rc).

Methods

Patients and tissue samples

LUAD tissue samples were collected from 120 patients (54 with metastatic LUAD and 66 with non-metastatic LUAD) who underwent surgery between 2005 and 2022 at Roswell Park Comprehensive Cancer Center. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the institutional review board of Roswell Park Comprehensive Cancer Center (BDR No. 129420). Informed consent was not required because the research involved the use of remnant tissue samples and was considered non-human subjects research.

Demographic and clinical variables, including age at diagnosis, gender, clinical stage, smoking history, and treatment details, were collected. All clinical and outcome patient data were de-identified. For each eligible patient, we selected archived tissue samples containing an invasive ADC. Patients with squamous cell carcinoma, large cell carcinoma, small cell carcinoma, or mixed histology with small cell components were excluded from this study. This study included LUAD cases from the defined study period for which adequate archived tissue blocks were available to support additional histologic and imaging-based analyses. Case inclusion was therefore limited by tissue availability rather than by clinical selection criteria.

Metastatic disease was defined as pathologically confirmed regional lymph node involvement (pN1 or pN2) at the time of surgical resection. Patients without nodal involvement (pN0) were classified as non-metastatic.

Morphometry and quantification of collagen and elastin

All tissue evaluations were conducted within the Department of Pathology at Roswell Park Comprehensive Cancer Center. LUAD tissue sections (obtained from the archive of formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded tissue blocks) were stained with Masson’s trichrome and Elastica Van Gieson stains, using separate replicates for each stain. Slides were then imaged with an Aperio ScanScope XT scanner at 20× magnification to produce digital images for analysis. Collagen and elastin were quantified using the Positive Pixel Count v9 algorithm in Aperio ImageScope after annotation of the tumor region. From the toolbar, the rectangular box was drawn around the tissue to define the area of interest, and a negative pen tool was used to remove unwanted tissue section. Non-tumor regions, including normal lung parenchyma, alveolar air spaces, benign stroma, and other non-tumor tissue, were manually excluded using negative annotation tools prior to pixel-based quantification to minimize bias. The pre-existing positive pixel count v9 algorithm was used for color deconvolution to separate stains. The algorithm calculates the total stained area (percentage of weak, medium, and strong positive and negative staining) corresponding to the red and blue colors of the trichrome stain and black color for elastic stain. Accordingly, elastin measurements represent relative elastin content within tumor-associated stroma rather than direct evidence of elastin degradation or loss. The collagen and elastin values were normalized by area to yield volume fraction in square millimeters. SHG imaging

Acquisition of SHG images for collagen structure was performed with a Leica SP8 multiphoton microscope. The process consisted of first localizing pathologist-annotated regions of interest with fluorescence imaging, then using SHG imaging to capture the collagen structure in those regions. Regions of interest were selected exclusively from pathologist-verified invasive tumor areas and were analyzed blinded to metastatic status. Multiple candidate regions per case were reviewed, and only regions free of imaging artifacts and representative of characteristic stromal-rich areas were included for quantitative analysis. During the acquisition process, there were instances of visual artifacts being present. Example visual artifacts, including high-intensity dots, vertical smearing, shaded regions, and occlusions, are visualized in Figure 2B.

To mitigate these detractions to image quality, image data acquired from the SHG imaging was preprocessed using computational tools developed in Python. The image data was processed to isolate the green channel of SHG images, where the relevant data is stored. Once isolated, intensity values above 30 were removed, as those values were typically associated with artifacts. Reducing the presence of these artifacts was particularly important as they impacted the accuracy of the quantification.

An example application of this process is shown in Figure 2C, and the visual impact of these artifacts is mitigated, creating the ability to better see and eventually quantify the data. After preprocessing, image data were examined patient-by-patient, finding regions that best represented high alignment.

Following image preprocessing, each patient’s dataset was individually examined to identify the regions that best exhibited high collagen fiber alignment. This manual inspection helped ensure consistent correlation with the distribution of collagen fibers. Once regions were selected, collagen fiber alignment was quantified with CurveAlign. This software applies a curvelet transform to calculate an alignment score ranging from 0 to 1 for each image. A score of 0 indicates no alignment, while a score of 1 indicates perfect alignment. Statistical analysis

Baseline clinical and demographic data were compared using Welch’s t-test for continuous variables and the Chi-squared test of independence for categorical variables. Comparisons were made with collagen fiber alignment, elastin positivity, collagen volume fraction (CVF), and elastin-to-collagen ratio between non-metastatic and metastatic LUAD tissue. Data for tissue characteristics were statistically analyzed with standard t-tests (or the Wilcoxon rank sum test if normality does not hold) to determine significance.

Overall survival (OS) was analyzed using the Kaplan-Meier method. Separate Kaplan-Meier curves were generated stratified by metastatic status, elastin expression, and CVF. For each stratification, median follow-up time, median OS, and 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival probabilities were estimated. Survival distributions between groups were compared using the log-rank test. Elastin and CVF were dichotomized into high vs. low groups based on their respective median values. To explore factors potentially associated with OS, exploratory Cox proportional hazards models were constructed. Specifically, associations between OS and three key independent variables (metastatic status, elastin, and CVF) were examined. Each analysis included a predefined set of clinically relevant covariates (age, sex, and tobacco use). Univariable Cox analyses were first performed to assess additional potential confounders, including race, pathological T and N stage, tumor grade, number of positive lymph nodes, tumor location, and initial treatment modality. Variables with a P value <0.10 in univariable analyses were subsequently incorporated into the final multivariable models.

For recurrence outcomes, cumulative incidence functions were estimated to account for the competing risk of death, and comparisons between groups were performed using Gray’s test. Median time to recurrence and 1-, 3-, and 5-year cumulative incidence estimates were calculated to describe recurrence patterns within the cohort. To further examine potential factors associated with recurrence, an exploratory multivariable Fine-Gray subdistribution hazards model was used to estimate subdistribution hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). This model was adjusted for metastatic status, CVF, elastin, and the predefined clinical covariates (sex, age, tobacco use, and histology).

All statistical analyses were conducted using R software, version 4.5 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided P value <0.05.

Results

Patient characteristics

Among patients included in this study, 80 were female (67%) and 40 were male (33%). The median age was 66 years, ranging from 40 to 82 years. History of tobacco use was also examined for all patients with 52 patients (43%) as current smokers, 54 patients (45%) were former smokers, and 14 patients (12%) were never smokers. Examining demographics between the metastatic and non-metastatic group showed that there were no significant differences in age, race, gender, and tobacco use. Additionally, the subtypes of ADC reported were not significantly different between the groups. Furthermore, a comparison of the laterality and location of surgery for each group showed that there were no significant differences. A total of 120 patients met inclusion criteria, and 119 had evaluable recurrence data. Tumor grade (G1–G3) information was available for a subset of patients and there were no significant differences between the two groups. Due to heterogeneity in American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) staging editions (6th, 7th, and 8th editions) across the study period, formal comparative analyses based on pathological T stage were not performed to avoid interpretation biases. Recurrence-free survival

Of the 119 evaluable patients (one missing recurrence data), 16 (13.4%) experienced disease recurrence. A cumulative incidence analysis demonstrated that patients with metastatic disease had a significantly higher risk of recurrence compared to those without metastasis (Gray’s test P=0.006). The cumulative incidence risk for recurrence at 5-year is less than 10% for the non-metastatic group vs. 24% for the metastatic group. Given the limitations of missing data (only 104 of 119 patients included), an exploratory multivariable Fine-Gray competing risk model was performed. The model, which adjusted for CVF, elastin, age, sex, tobacco use and histology, found that the metastatic group was associated with a higher recurrence risk (subdistribution HR =4.23; 95% CI: 1.03–17.33; P=0.05). Relation between collagen content, elastin content and tumor metastasis

Elastin content was significantly reduced in the metastatic group compared to the non-metastatic group (29±0.13 vs. 0.38±0.14, P=0.001), reflecting a relative decrease in elastin fiber abundance within tumor-associated stroma. In contrast, no significant difference was observed in the overall CVF in the two groups. However, the elastin-to-collagen ratio was significantly lower in the metastatic group compared with the non-metastatic group (0.36±0.16 vs. 0.47±0.18, P=0.002), indicating a relative shift in ECM composition rather than an absolute increase in collagen content. Relation between collagen fiber alignment and tumor metastasis

Comparison of collagen fiber alignment between the metastatic and non-metastatic groups revealed that the metastatic group exhibited significantly greater alignment, as quantified by the CurveAlign (0.87±0.08 vs. 0.78±0.10, P=0.003). This indicates a more organized and parallel orientation of collagen fibers in tumors that metastasized compared to those that did not. The increased alignment may reflect a structural remodeling of the ECM that facilitates directional migration of tumor cells along collagen tracks, supporting a pro-metastatic microenvironment. Discussion

This study evaluated collagen structure and content, along with elastin content, in relation to the aggressive behavior of LUAD. In this cohort of patients undergoing tumor resection, analysis of LUAD tissue revealed significant differences between non-metastatic and metastatic tumors, with higher collagen fiber alignment and lower elastin content observed in the metastatic group. These findings provide new insights into the potential role of collagen architecture and elastin content as biomarkers for predicting metastasis, offering a novel perspective on tumor behavior through tissue-level biological changes.

Previous studies have highlighted the role of collagen structure in cancer progression (9). Specifically, high alignment of collagen fibers in tumor stroma has been linked to progression of various solid tumors (10,20). This relationship between collagen fiber alignment and disease progression has also been observed in breast cancer, pancreatic cancer, and lung cancer (10,15,20,21). Building on this foundation, our study found that collagen fiber alignment in LUAD is a promising biomarker for associated with metastasis. This further supports the diagnostic potential of collagen by highlighting its role in tumor microenvironments.

While studies have shown a relationship between elastin and tumor development, no clear link is established to metastasis (22,23). In our study, primary LUADs that metastasized to the lymph nodes generally showed reduced elastin content, suggesting that elastin may be a useful biomarker for assessing metastatic potential. However, because some metastatic tumors did not exhibit decreased elastin levels, its role in metastasis is likely more complex. Therefore, elastin content should be viewed as one component within a broader panel of indicators rather than a standalone marker of metastasis-prone tumors. Future work should aim to refine elastin quantification and integrate it with additional biomarkers to enhance its clinical utility.

The findings of our study indicate clear differences in collagen fiber alignment between non-metastatic and metastatic LUAD. However, SHG imaging required prior annotation of tissue sections to define regions of interest, and selecting appropriate collagen-rich areas for analysis relied heavily on expert interpretation. Should broader application of this approach reach clinical workflows in its current form, the burden of identification may prove to be untenable for skilled pathologists who already carry heavy workloads. Instead, it may be beneficial to develop an artificial intelligence (AI)-assisted approach for annotating regions of interest using guidance from pathologists. Such an approach could apply domain knowledge provided by pathologists to achieve high enough accuracy for clinical applicability. In doing so, it would facilitate this approach while also mitigating annotation burden.

As the examination of collagen fiber orientation broadens, it may be beneficial to explore its relationship with tumor size. A study by Li et al. demonstrated that lung cancer tumor size is associated with the risk of metastasis to specific sites (24). If a link can be established between collagen structure and tumor size, further examination of collagen fiber orientation may reveal patterns associated with metastasis.

Visual artifacts presented a significant challenge during SHG image acquisition, particularly vertical smearing and sensor occlusions that obscured tissue details. Challenges with SHG imaging have also been reported by other researchers, including low signal levels, intensity variations, and interference-based artifacts from tissue structures, which can compromise accurate quantification of tissue structures if not properly addressed (25,26). To address the vertical smearing encountered in our study, we propose a multiple-acquisition continuity check, where regions consistently present across sequential images are retained while transient artifacts are discarded. This approach may offer a practical alternative to standard averaging methods, which often preserve artifacts. Sensor occlusions, likely due to high-intensity regions triggering detector overload, remain a more complex issue, though adjustments to laser power and careful slide preparation may help reduce their occurrence. Through management of imaging artifacts, SHG imaging can produce more reliable representations of tissue structures, which may improve diagnostic evaluation.

In the preoperative setting, core biopsy samples could potentially be analyzed for stromal collagen alignment. When adequate collagen is present, identifying high alignment associated with aggressive behavior could aid the clinical team in surgical decision-making, especially in the context of lymph node involvement, limited pulmonary reserve, comorbidities, or other perioperative risks. Alternatively, assessing collagen alignment in resected tumors could help stratify patients into prognostic groups and guide adjuvant therapy decisions.

Metastatic transition is the leading cause of mortality in solid tumors, marking the shift from localized tumor growth to systemic spread. While this study identifies collagen structure and elastin positivity as potential biomarkers for predicting metastasis in LUAD, much remains unknown about how these biomarkers manifest before, during, and after metastasis. Therefore, expanding our study to assess these biomarkers across different time-points could be informative. This could be achieved through serial tissue analyses when available or by developing new methods or adapting existing ones for accurate, longitudinal evaluation of the tumor stroma. Knowledge gained from the application of these approaches may present opportunities to develop effective countermeasures against the metastatic transition of cancer.

This analysis offers novel insights into survival and recurrence patterns, as well as potential biological correlations, among patients treated at our institution. The observed associations, including those involving the elastin and collagen, suggest that ECM composition may play a role in disease progression and patient outcomes. However, as this was an exploratory analysis based on a limited number of events, the findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory.

Limitations

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. This retrospective study included cases selected from a defined study period based on the availability of archived tissue blocks suitable for additional histologic and imaging-based analyses, which may introduce selection bias. Additionally, tumors in this cohort were originally staged according to different editions of the AJCC staging system (6th, 7th, and 8th editions), resulting in non-uniform T stage definitions. To avoid potential misclassification bias, formal T stage-based comparative analyses were not performed. Tumor grading data were available only for a subset of patients and were therefore included descriptively rather than as adjusted covariates.

Furthermore, the modest cohort size and limited number of outcome events restricted the feasibility of advanced adjustment strategies such as propensity score matching. As a result, multivariable and survival analyses were conducted for exploratory purposes only and should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than definitive. The primary comparisons supported by this dataset are descriptive and analyses examining associations between stromal architecture and metastatic status.

Conclusions

Our findings highlight the potential value of evaluating both collagen fiber alignment and elastin content in LUAD during tissue examination. Further comprehensive studies are required to validate these data, with a focus on isolating specific time frames when changes in collagen fiber alignment and elastin content occur. If validated, these biomarkers have the potential to improve prediction, diagnosis, and prognosis of metastasis, while also reducing the need for repeated imaging and biopsies, streamlining diagnostic workflows, minimizing patient burden, and supporting improved treatment planning and monitoring.
