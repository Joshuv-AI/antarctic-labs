Mild photothermal therapy ameliorates neurogenic bladder after sacral spinal cord injury via multi-target effects
Authors: Kaiping Bai, Susu Guo, Junxiang Wen, Jiajie Zhang, Xiaoling Huang, Fei Yuan, Yanping Hou, Pengtao Liu, Zeyu Xiao, Kai Cui, Jie Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Neurogenic bladder (NB) is a devastating complication following sacral spinal cord injury (SSCI), often leading to urinary incontinence, recurrent infections, and progressive renal dysfunction, which severely impair patients’ quality of life and survival. Current therapeutic options remain largely palliative and fail to reverse the underlying neurogenic pathology. This study employed mild photothermal therapy (MPTT) to treat SSCI-induced NB in rats and systematically investigated its efficacy and mechanisms. Results showed that MPTT effectively improved bladder morphology and urodynamic parameters, with more significant effects observed in early intervention. Mechanistically, MPTT inhibited bladder fibrosis, alleviated chronic inflammation, restored the balance between cell proliferation and apoptosis, promoted functional angiogenesis, and repaired structural damage and function of bladder nerve fibers. MPTT ameliorates SSCI-NB function through multi-target effects, and its time-dependent therapeutic efficacy suggests the necessity of early and repeated interventions, providing experimental and theoretical evidence for new NB treatment strategies.

Introduction

Neurogenic bladder (NB) is a debilitating urological complication commonly associated with neurological disorders such as spinal cord injury and multiple sclerosis. Pathologically, NB is characterized by progressive bladder dysfunction and fibrosis, leading to decreased bladder compliance, reduced bladder capacity, and high-pressure urine storage. These pathological changes may ultimately cause irreversible upper urinary tract injury, thereby severely impairing patients’ quality of life and long-term survival [[1], [2], [3]]. Pediatric patients are particularly vulnerable due to the immature development of the urinary system, making them more susceptible to secondary renal dysfunction and psychosocial complications, which further increases the difficulty of clinical management [4]. Current therapeutic strategies for NB primarily rely on clean intermittent catheterization (CIC), pharmacological treatment, and surgical intervention. However, these approaches mainly provide symptomatic relief and fail to effectively halt bladder fibrosis progression, preserve bladder function, or improve long-term prognosis [5].

Conventional systemic drug administration also faces important limitations in the treatment of bladder diseases. After systemic circulation, only a limited proportion of drugs can reach the target bladder tissue at therapeutically effective concentrations [6]. In addition, some agents with potential therapeutic efficacy may induce severe systemic toxicity, which further restricts their clinical application. Intravesical instillation has emerged as an attractive alternative delivery strategy because it enables direct drug exposure to the bladder mucosa, enhances local bioavailability, minimizes systemic drug loss, and reduces off-target toxicity. Nevertheless, several translational challenges remain, including limited drug retention time and insufficient targeting efficiency within bladder tissue.

For patients with spinal cord injury–associated urinary retention or incomplete bladder emptying, CIC is currently regarded as the gold-standard clinical management strategy [7].

Therefore, intravesical administration immediately following CIC is clinically feasible and particularly suitable for pediatric patients with spinal cord injury [7]. The development of effective intravesical therapeutic strategies may substantially improve patients’ quality of life while also generating important clinical and social benefits.

In recent years, photothermal therapy (PTT) has gradually attracted widespread attention. To ensure the safety of surrounding tissues, reduce damage to cells and tissues, and maintain microenvironmental stability, mild Photothermal Therapy (MPTT) has been proposed [[8], [9], [10], [11], [12]]. This technology preferentially eliminates bacteria or tumors at low temperatures while promoting wound healing. Moreover, studies have demonstrated that PTT can inhibit scar hyperplasia and alleviate the progression of atherosclerosis with minimal damage to normal tissues [13,14]. Thermal stimulation has also exhibited therapeutic efficacy in models such as interstitial cystitis [15]. More importantly, most PTT protocols only require 3–5 min of light irradiation to induce functional tissue remodeling via thermal stress, which meets the stringent time requirements of the dynamic bladder flushing environment for treatment timeliness. Research evidence has confirmed that mild photothermal therapy can achieve excellent therapeutic outcomes and is applicable to practical biomedical applications.

We selected a self-stacked small-molecule (BBT,4,7-bis(4-(2-ethylhexyl)thiophen-2-yl)benzobis[c] [1,2,5]thiadiazole) nanoparticles (NPs). BBT NPs were utilized as the photothermal agent. Unlike some clinically applied nanoparticles that exhibit both photothermal and photodynamic properties, BBT NPs possess exclusive photothermal activity, thus allowing for the independent investigation of photothermal effects on the bladder [16,17].

Based on the above clinical demands and research background, this study, for the first time, applies MPTT to the treatment of NB following SSCI. Unlike conventional single-target interventions, this work systematically evaluates the therapeutic efficacy of MPTT at multiple levels, including bladder function, tissue remodeling, and molecular mechanisms, and further elucidates its multi-target synergistic mode of action. Specifically, MPTT not only improves bladder storage and voiding function, but also suppresses fibrotic progression, alleviates chronic inflammation, maintains the balance between cell proliferation and apoptosis, and promotes neural repair, thereby collectively delaying disease progression. In addition, this study reveals a pronounced time-dependent therapeutic effect of MPTT, highlighting the critical importance of early intervention in the management of neurogenic bladder. Overall, this work provides a novel physical therapeutic strategy and mechanistic insight for neurogenic bladder, with promising potential for clinical translation.

Results and discussion

Photothermal properties of BBT NPs and biosafety of PTT

As a hollow organ requiring precise coordination between smooth muscle contraction and sphincter relaxation, the bladder must maintain wall elasticity during urine storage and generate stable pressure during urination. This physiological characteristic of dynamic-static balance imposes an urgent need for treatments that can immediately regulate function in a short time—a demand that photothermal therapy is uniquely positioned to address. MPTT, a non-invasive, spatiotemporally controllable physical therapy with minimal side effects, has shown efficacy in anti-tumor and anti-infection treatments and can regulate the fibrotic microenvironment [18,19]. In recent years, thermal stimulation has demonstrated therapeutic effects in models such as interstitial cystitis [15,20]. With the development of precise temperature control equipment, MPTT can targetedly induce functional tissue remodeling through thermal stress within minutes of light irradiation. This therapy delivers precise near-infrared light to neural targets around the bladder, using nanophotothermal agents to convert light energy into local heat. It improves bladder function without damaging bladder wall tissue, with core advantages of precise targeting and immediate effect: thermal imaging locates lesion sites, avoiding systemic side effects of traditional drugs; thermal effects can instantly regulate neural excitability, aligning with the bladder's need for real-time responsiveness and overcoming the drawbacks of traditional methods such as catheterization and surgery. PTT was introduced in this study for the treatment of NB induced by SSCI. The therapeutic agents can be delivered into the bladder via intravesical instillation, which rapidly converts light energy into thermal energy to act on bladder tissues, overcoming the limitation of poor retention in the bladder due to constant urine flushing.

The electron micrograph of BBT NPs is shown in the figure (Fig. 1A). We measured the heating curves at different concentrations, and the results showed that BBT NPs exhibited excellent photothermal properties. Under 808 nm laser irradiation (1.0 W/cm2) [17], the solution temperature rapidly increased and demonstrated outstanding photothermal cycling stability (Fig. 1B and C; Fig. S1). Further validation of the photothermal heating effect was conducted using a rat bladder model. Upon 808 nm laser irradiation (0.5 W/cm2) [17], the temperature at the bladder site rapidly rose, reaching 42°C (Fig. 1E). The excellent photothermal properties of this molecule ensured the smooth integration of both diagnosis and therapy. Moreover, the adjustable spot size (with a minimum diameter of 0.5 mm) enabled precise irradiation of bladder tissue, minimizing damage to surrounding healthy tissues. CCK-8 assay results indicated that the viability of SV-HUC-1 cells was not affected by BBT NPs, with no statistically significant difference in cell viability among groups (Fig. 1D). Importantly, under mild photothermal conditions, the PTT group did not exhibit significant reduction in cell viability compared with the control group, indicating that the treatment is well tolerated at the cellular level (Fig. S2). Rats were divided into four groups: control group (NC, normal group), NIR group (infrared irradiation alone), BBT NPs group (nanoparticle perfusion alone), and PTT group (photothermal therapy group). BBT NPs demonstrate excellent photothermal conversion efficiency and stability, with consistent performance even after multiple rounds of photothermal irradiation, thereby exerting therapeutic effects on the bladder. Previous studies from our group showed active changes in bladder morphology and function within the first 2 weeks post-SSCI, with stabilization after 4 weeks; thus, rats were evaluated at 2 and 4 weeks post-treatment.(Fig. 2 A). At 2 weeks, bladder length was significantly increased in the NB group vs. the Sham group. No significant differences were observed between the NIR, BBT NPs, and NB groups, while PTT treatment significantly reduced bladder length vs. the NB group (though still greater than the Sham group) (Fig. 2B and C). Similar morphological trends were noted at 4 weeks, with overall reduced bladder length compared to 2 weeks (Fig. 2B–D). For bladder weight/body weight ratio, the NB group showed a slight increase at 2 weeks and a further increase at 4 weeks vs. the Sham group (attributed to early edema and subsequent collagen deposition). PTT treatment significantly attenuated this increase at both time points, while the NIR and BBT NPs groups showed no differences vs. the NB group (Fig. 2B–E).

Leak point pressure (LPP) and maximum cystometric capacity (MCC) were recorded as urodynamic measurement parameters, and bladder compliance (BC) was calculated using the formula: bladder compliance = maximum bladder capacity/(LPP-initial intravesical pressure). Urodynamic tests revealed that at 2 weeks, the NB group exhibited a marked increase in MCC (1552.22 ± 236.96 μL vs. 184.22 ± 38.14 μL in the Sham group), reduced LPP (22.80 ± 3.98 cmH2O vs. 33.75 ± 1.89 cmH2O in the Sham group), and increased BC. The NIR and BBT NPs groups showed similar MCC, LPP, and BC to the NB group, while the PTT group had significantly lower MCC (1134.11 ± 205.52 μL), improved LPP (29.17 ± 3.71 cmH2O), and BC closer to the Sham group, with more rhythmic urodynamic curves (Fig. 2F and G,H,I).

At 4 weeks, MCC remained elevated in the NB, NIR, and BBT NPs groups (vs. the Sham group), but PTT significantly reduced MCC (804.67 ± 135.52 μL) vs. the NB group. LPP was significantly lower in the NB, NIR, and BBT NPs groups vs. the Sham group, while PTT improved LPP (27.84 ± 1.65 cmH2O) and maintained BC and regular urodynamic curves (Fig. 2J and K,L,M).

Longitudinal comparison (Fig. 2N and O,P) showed decreased LPP, MCC, and BC in all groups at 4 weeks vs. 2 weeks. However, changes were more pronounced in the NB, NIR, and BBT NPs groups, whereas the PTT group maintained stable bladder function with parameters closer to the Sham group, indicating preserved bladder elasticity and structural stability.

The MCC of rats in the SCI-induced NB model group displayed a characteristic pattern of rapid elevation - gradual decline - stabilization. This is consistent with the pathological progression observed clinically in NB patients, where initial elastic compensation of the bladder is followed by detrusor fibrosis leading to capacity regression. In contrast, the MCC change curve of the PTT treatment group was significantly flattened. At 4 weeks, it was markedly lower than that of the NB model group, suggesting that PTT can delay the abnormal fluctuation of bladder capacity. The core mechanism underlying this effect may be associated with the tissue repair function of photothermal therapy. Existing studies have confirmed that the photothermal effect can penetrate pelvic floor tissues, regulate the activity of fibroblasts, and retard the progression of bladder wall fibrosis. Similarly, a previous study indicated that laser irradiation can stabilize bladder storage function by modulating the sensitivity of bladder sensory nerves, and its regulatory effect on bladder capacity fluctuation is fully consistent with the action trend of PTT observed in this study [12,22]. Regarding LPP, a key indicator of urinary function, rats in the NB group exhibited a significant decrease in LPP accompanied by detrusor overactivity. These manifestations are typical of detrusor-sphincter dyssynergia and impaired detrusor contractile function following SCI. Although PTT failed to restore LPP to the level of the sham-operated group, it attenuated the LPP reduction amplitude compared with the NB group and decreased the frequency of unstable contraction waves, indicating that PTT can partially preserve detrusor contractile function. Studies have shown that thermal stimulation enhances micturition efficiency in rats [23]. It has been demonstrated that the transient receptor potential vanilloid 4 channel plays a sensory role in the urothelium, potentially regulating the release of sensory mediators such as adenosine triphosphate (ATP), which in turn modulates afferent nerve activity in response to bladder filling during the micturition cycle [24]. Therefore, we hypothesize that PTT may partially restore detrusor cell excitability and reduce abnormal electrical activity after nerve injury through a similar photothermal-ion channel regulation pathway, thereby alleviating detrusor instability. The advantage of PTT in improving bladder function is also reflected in its precise regulation of the storage-micturition balance. In this study, PTT not only avoided excessive bladder stretch injury caused by abnormally high MCC in the model group but also prevented the risk of urinary retention that may be induced by simple volume control by partially preserving LPP. Clinical studies have reported that thermomodulation may play a role in the management of women with lower urinary tract dysfunction (LUTD) [25]. This may be attributed to the ability of thermal stimulation on bladder sensory neurons to correct micturition rhythm disorders after nerve injury, which provides direct evidence for the neuromodulatory mechanism of PTT.

PTT alleviates fibrotic pathological damage in NB

Bladder tissues were collected from rats in each group for pathological evaluation. HE staining revealed that at 2 weeks postoperatively, the main bladder changes in the NB, NIR, BBT NPs, and PTT groups included subserosal edema and detrusor vacuolation. Detrusor thickness was increased in these four groups compared to the Sham group, with no significant intergroup differences due to edema. Quantitative analysis of Masson staining showed an increase in the NB group; the NIR, BBT NPs, and PTT groups had no significant differences from the NB group, but the PTT group exhibited a reduction relative to the NB group (Fig. 3A and B). At 4 weeks postoperatively, detrusor thickness was significantly increased. The NIR and BBT NPs groups showed no statistical differences from the NB group, while the PTT group had significantly reduced detrusor thickness compared to the NB group. Masson staining indicated that the collagen/smooth muscle ratio in the NB group was significantly higher than that in the Sham group, with no differences between the NIR, BBT NPs, and NB groups. The PTT group had a significantly decreased collagen/smooth muscle ratio. Statistical analysis of detrusor thickness and collagen/smooth muscle ratio changes from 2 to 4 weeks postoperatively (Fig. 3 E) revealed significant increases in detrusor thickness and collagen content in the NB, NIR, and BBT NPs groups, while the PTT group showed minimal increases in both parameters. These results suggest that the PTT group had milder fibrosis and less bladder morphological change, which may partially explain the smaller alterations in LPP, MCC, and BC in the PTT group. Additionally, these changes were associated with the overall bladder shape and weight (Fig. 2 B).

Collectively, no significant differences in fibrosis indicators or urodynamic parameters were observed between the NB, NIR, and BBT NPs groups, indicating that NIR irradiation alone or BBT NPs perfusion alone had no therapeutic effect on neurogenic bladder after SSCI. In contrast, the PTT group showed significant differences in bladder function and fibrosis indicators compared to the NB group. Therefore, bladder samples from the PTT group (2 and 4 weeks post-treatment, PTT 2W and PTT 4W) and the NB group (2 and 4 weeks post-modeling, NB 2W and NB 4W) were selected for further detection.

Immunofluorescence analysis showed no significant difference in the Col-1/α-SMA ratio between the PTT 2W and NB 2W groups, while the ratio was lower in the PTT 4W group than in the NB 4W group (Fig. 3F and G). However, collagen content increased from 2 to 4 weeks in both the NB and PTT groups (Fig. 3F and G), confirming that PTT could delay the progression of bladder fibrosis and reduce collagen production and accumulation in the bladder after SSCI, though the therapeutic effect weakened at 4 weeks.

Extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling is a key process in NB associated fibrosis [26]. During bladder fibrosis, mechanical and chemical stress induces fibroblasts to overproduce collagen, fibronectin and proteoglycans, leading to ECM stiffening [27]; excessive ECM stiffness is a predisposing factor for fibrosis [28]. Studies have shown that high temperature induces irreversible depolymerization of collagen fibers, facilitating ECM remodeling [29]. Our study applied mild PTT at 41 ± 0.5°C, which did not denature surrounding proteins but effectively promoted ECM depolymerization, reduced TGF-β-induced excessive ECM accumulation, maintained tissue elasticity, and thus improved bladder contractility. Elevated TGF-β1 is another critical driver of bladder fibrosis [30]. Local hyperthermia can alleviate oxidative stress, inhibit TGF-β and its receptor expression, and reduce ECM accumulation [31,32]. At 4 weeks, detrusor thickness in the NB group increased significantly, while that in the PTT group was markedly lower, consistent with the gentle fluctuation of MCC in the PTT group, suggesting PTT stabilizes urine storage by inhibiting abnormal detrusor hyperplasia. The collagen/smooth muscle ratio in the PTT group was lower than that in the NB group at 2 weeks, with more significant difference at 4 weeks; the Col-1/α-SMA ratio and collagen increment in the PTT 4W group were both lower than those in the NB 4W group. These confirm PTT delays fibrosis, maintains bladder wall flexibility by inhibiting abnormal collagen deposition, and thus mitigates LPP decrease [33]. Only PTT (not NIR or BBT NPs intervention) improved both functional and structural indicators, verifying the necessity of photothermal synergy. This specificity may be attributed to the targeted thermal effect of BBT NPs under NIR, which regulates fibrosis-related signaling pathways in the bladder wall.

Fibronectin, an important ECM glycoprotein, is involved in cell adhesion, migration, and tissue repair. Chronic bladder injury induces upregulated Fibronectin expression, which serves as an early signal for ECM deposition. It acts as a “pro-fibrotic hub” in bladder fibrosis by recruiting fibroblasts and promoting their transformation into collagen-secreting myofibroblasts, thereby driving abnormal ECM accumulation through multiple mechanisms [34,35]. Therefore, the expression levels of Fibronectin and Collagen I were detected. The results showed that the expression of Fibronectin, Collagen I, and α-SMA in bladder tissues was significantly lower in the PTT group than in the NB group (model group), where these proteins were upregulated. No differences were observed between the NIR, BBT NPs, and NB groups, indicating that NIR irradiation alone or BBT NPs alone had no effect (Fig. 3H and I).

PTT reduces the chronic inflammatory microenvironment of NB

Bladder tissues from the Sham, NB, NIR, BBT NPs, and PTT groups at 2 and 4 weeks were collected for quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR). Statistical analysis of fibrosis-related genes (Collagen I, Collagen III, TGFβ-1) and inflammation-related genes (IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-6, MCP-1) showed no significant differences among the NB, NIR, and BBT NPs groups, indicating that NIR irradiation alone or BBT NPs perfusion alone had no effect. In contrast, the PTT group exhibited significantly lower expression of these genes compared to the NB group, demonstrating potent anti-fibrotic and anti-inflammatory effects (Fig. 4A and B). As core effector cells of inflammatory responses, macrophage infiltration and phenotype directly reflect the degree of inflammation [30,36]. Bladder samples from the PTT group (2 and 4 weeks post-treatment, PTT 2W and PTT 4W) and the NB group (2 and 4 weeks post-modeling, NB 2W and NB 4W) were subjected to macrophage immunofluorescence staining (Fig. 4 C). CD163+ staining analysis revealed a significant increase in the rate of CD163-positive cells in all four groups (NB 2W, PTT 2W, NB 4W, PTT 4W) (Fig. 4 D), with no significant intergroup differences, though the NB 4W group showed a slight increasing trend. CD68+ staining indicated higher expression in the NB 2W group than in the PTT 2W group, while no significant difference was observed between the NB 4W and PTT 4W groups. Over time, CD68+ expression slightly decreased in the NB 4W group compared to the NB 2W group, but remained stable in the PTT group (no difference between PTT 2W and PTT 4W) (Fig. 4 E). The CD68+/CD163+ ratio was highest in the NB 2W group and slightly decreased at 4 weeks, while the PTT groups (both 2W and 4W) exhibited significantly lower CD68+/CD163+ ratios (Fig. 4 F), suggesting that PTT treatment inhibits inflammation and reduces macrophage infiltration.

The pathological progression of NB is closely associated with persistent inflammation [21]. These findings suggest that PTT can suppress inflammatory responses and alleviate bladder tissue damage by regulating macrophage polarization. Mild thermal modulation at 39–43°C has been proven to effectively regulate immune responses [37]. Deng et al. [38] found that mild PTT could remarkably reduce the count of M1-type macrophages; treatment with mild PTT at 40°C could downregulate the expression of inflammatory cytokines in M1-type macrophages within 24 h, thereby shortening the duration of inflammation [39,40]. Li et al. [41]further confirmed that periodic mild PTT at 41 ± 1°C could accelerate the inhibition of macrophage M1 polarization and promote the secretion of anti-inflammatory and osteogenic factors.

Myeloperoxidase (MPO), mainly synthesized and secreted by neutrophils, is a classic marker reflecting neutrophil infiltration and inflammatory damage [42,43]. MPO staining in the PTT group showed significantly weakened positive signals (brown granules), with positive cells scattered in the submucosa and significantly lower in number than in the control group. In the NB 2W and NB 4W groups, MPO-positive cells were diffusely distributed with strong and dense staining signals, indicating massive neutrophil infiltration and MPO release involved in inflammatory damage. Over time, inflammation in the NB group slightly subsided at 4 weeks (Fig. 4G and H). Neutrophils are core effector cells in the early stage of inflammation. In the PTT group, the MPO positive signals were attenuated and distributed dispersedly, indicating that PTT can inhibit the infiltration and activation of neutrophils, reduce the secretion of their toxic substances, block inflammation-mediated tissue damage, and avoid oxidative stress-induced structural destruction of the bladder wall. This effect is consistent with the role of photothermal therapy in reducing neutrophil recruitment in rheumatoid arthritis [44]. These results confirm that photothermal therapy can alleviate inflammation-mediated tissue damage by inhibiting neutrophil infiltration and MPO release.

E-cadherin (E-cad), a key molecule in epithelial cell junctions, is closely associated with epithelial barrier function [45,46]. Inflammatory responses often downregulate E-cad expression, disrupting barrier integrity [47,48]. E-cad staining in the PTT group showed continuous and strong positive signals (green fluorescence or brown labeling) between epithelial cells, with an intact epithelial layer. In the NB 2W and NB 4W group, E-cad positive signals were discontinuously distributed with significantly reduced intensity, and epithelial cells were disorganized (Fig. 4G–I). Maintaining the integrity of the epithelial barrier is a key mechanism by which PTT blocks inflammation spread. E-cadherin is a core molecule involved in bladder epithelial junctions; the downregulation and dysregulated distribution of E-cad can disrupt the epithelial barrier, resulting in the stimulation of the interstitium by urine components and accelerated diffusion of inflammatory cytokines [49]. In the PTT group, the E-cad-positive signals were continuous and highly intense, confirming that PTT can inhibit the trans-barrier penetration of inflammatory cytokines by maintaining epithelial cell junctions, spatially restrict the invasion of inflammation into the muscular and serosal layers, and create favorable conditions for tissue repair. This suggests that photothermal therapy can protect epithelial barrier function by maintaining the normal expression and distribution of E-cad, indirectly inhibiting the diffusion of inflammatory factors and the inflammatory amplification effect.

TNF-α is a core pro-inflammatory cytokine mediating the inflammatory cascade, whose expression level directly reflects the severity of inflammation [50,51]. At 2 weeks, the PTT group showed weakened TNF-α staining signals (brown labeling) and a significantly lower proportion of positive cells, with only a few scattered positive signals in local areas. In the NB group, TNF-α was strongly positive with diffuse distribution of positive cells in inflammatory regions. After 4 weeks of treatment, TNF-α expression slightly decreased in the PTT 4W group, but the reduction was not significant compared to the NB 4W group (Fig. 4G–J). This directly confirms that photothermal therapy can effectively inhibit the expression and secretion of the pro-inflammatory factor TNF-α, though the effect weakened slightly at 4 weeks. Protein expression levels of IL-6 and TNF-α further validated these findings, showing that the PTT group significantly reduced the secretion of inflammatory factors (Fig. 4K and L). TNF-α is an upstream initiator of the inflammatory cascade reaction, which can activate the NF-κB pathway to induce the secretion of downstream pro-inflammatory cytokines, forming an inflammatory amplification cycle [52]. Two weeks after surgery, the number of TNF-α positive cells in the PTT group decreased significantly, verifying that PTT can target and inhibit the synthesis and secretion of upstream pro-inflammatory cytokines, thus attenuating the intensity of inflammation at the source. Although the inhibitory effect weakened at 4 weeks, the early intervention had already suppressed the inflammatory peak and avoided persistent tissue damage. This result is mutually corroborated by the synchronous downregulation of IL-6 expression, clarifying the targeted inhibitory effect of PTT on the inflammatory cytokine secretion pathway.

In summary, photothermal therapy exerts anti-inflammatory effects through multiple mechanisms, including inhibiting the infiltration and activation of macrophages and neutrophils, maintaining the integrity of the epithelial barrier function, and downregulating the expression of the pro-inflammatory factor TNF-α, providing evidence for its anti-inflammatory application.

PTT regulates the homeostasis of cell proliferation and apoptosis in NB

NB tissue exhibits significant imbalance between cell proliferation and apoptosis, as well as abnormal vascular remodeling, whereas normal bladder tissue maintains a dynamic balance between cell proliferation and apoptosis [[53], [54], [55], [56]]. Due to long-term neuroregulatory disorders (such as spinal cord injury, diabetic neuropathy, etc.), patients with NB experience repeated bladder filling and high-pressure states [57,58].

Microenvironmental changes including mechanical stretch and inflammatory stimulation can induce abnormal cell proliferation [59]. Ki67 is a core nuclear antigen reflecting cell proliferation activity [60]. Regarding cell proliferation, Ki67-positive cells were observed in the mucosal and muscular layers of the bladder in the NB group, with the most prominent expression at 4 weeks, indicating abnormal local cell proliferation in neurogenic bladder tissue. In contrast, the number of Ki67-positive cells in the PTT group was significantly reduced compared to the NB group, demonstrating that photothermal therapy can effectively inhibit excessive cell proliferation in lesion areas of neurogenic bladder (Fig. 5A–C).Fig. 5PTT regulates the homeostasis of cell proliferation and apoptosis in NB.(A)(B)(C)(D) TUNEL and Ki67 staining and quantitative analysis of bladder tissues at 2 and 4 weeks after treatment; (E)(F) CD31 staining and quantitative analysis of bladder tissues at 2 and 4 weeks after treatment.(Compared with the sham-operated group, ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001, ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001).

Apoptotic mechanisms are disrupted in lesion tissues, failing to effectively eliminate damaged or abnormal cells, ultimately leading to imbalanced homeostasis of the cell population [61,62]. In terms of cell apoptosis (Fig. 5 B), only a small number of TUNEL-positive apoptotic cells were observed in the NB 2W group. At 4 weeks, the number slightly increased in the NB group but with extremely uneven distribution—positive cells were clustered in the core area of injury. In the PTT group, TUNEL-positive signals were significantly enhanced at 4 weeks compared to 2 weeks, with obvious positive signals in the injured area (Fig. 5A and B). Combined with the Ki67/TUNEL ratio, these results indicate that photothermal therapy not only inhibits excessive proliferation but also achieves a dynamic balance between proliferation and apoptosis, providing a guarantee for maintaining cell population homeostasis and tissue structural integrity (Fig. 5A–D).

CD31, a specific transmembrane protein of vascular endothelial cells [63], whose expression and distribution can directly reflect the microvessel density and vascular structural integrity in tissues [64]. A functionally stable microvascular network is the microenvironmental basis for maintaining the balance between cell proliferation and apoptosis [65]. CD31 staining in the PTT group showed a large number of continuous and intact tubular positive structures (green labeling), with abundant microvascular branches and regular lumens. In the control group, CD31-positive signals were discontinuously or dot-like distributed, with disorganized vascular structures, narrowed or even occluded lumens (Fig. 5E and F). These results confirm that photothermal therapy can improve local tissue blood perfusion, oxygen and nutrient supply by promoting functional angiogenesis, providing the necessary microenvironmental support for maintaining the balance between cell proliferation and apoptosis.

NB is accompanied by an imbalance between cell proliferation and apoptosis and abnormal vascular remodeling in bladder tissues, both of which jointly contribute to the deterioration of bladder function [66]. In the normal bladder, the proliferation and apoptosis of mucosal epithelial cells and detrusor muscle cells maintain a dynamic equilibrium, which ensures the structural and functional stability of the bladder. In contrast, NB patients develop microenvironmental disorders due to abnormal neural regulation, leading to excessive cell proliferation along with epithelial damage and muscular atrophy, and eventually presenting with symptoms such as urinary retention and urinary incontinence [67]. Ma et al. [68] also found that the photothermal effect induces M1 polarization of macrophages through the NF-κB pathway. The exosomes secreted by these macrophages can synergistically inhibit cell proliferation and upregulate the pro-apoptotic protein Bax, restoring the balance between proliferation and apoptosis and thus providing a core guarantee for the homeostasis of bladder tissues. This dual regulatory effect outperforms the single-target modulation of conventional drugs. The value of PTT is also reflected in its improvement of the vascular microenvironment, which is a prerequisite for maintaining cellular homeostasis. The lesion areas of NB exhibit low microvessel density and high permeability, forming a vicious cycle of ischemia-hypoxia-dysfunction; He et al.69 [][72][]confirmed that angiogenesis disorders represent a major challenge for NB repair, whereas promoting the formation of functional blood vessels can ameliorate bladder function. Studies have demonstrated [[70], [71], [72]] that PTT can facilitate the migration of endothelial cells and the formation of vascular lumens, generate stable functional neovessels, improve the blood oxygen saturation in lesion areas, and optimize the microenvironment to promote the restoration of cellular homeostasis.

Overall, through the synergistic effects of regulating proliferation, inhibiting abnormal apoptosis, and improving the microenvironment, PTT maintains the cell population in a stable state of dynamic balance between proliferation and apoptosis, avoiding tissue dysfunction caused by blind proliferation or excessive apoptosis. This regulatory effect, together with the anti-inflammatory effect shown in Fig. 4, constitutes the core advantage of its application in inflammation-related diseases.

PTT regulates the homeostasis of nerve injury and repair in NB

As shown in Fig. 6, PTT can significantly regulate the innervation and neuroregenerative processes of bladder tissue in NB. As a common complication of diseases such as SCI and diabetic neuropathy, NB is characterized by bladder dysfunction caused by disordered neural regulation, accompanied by tissue degeneration and reduced neurorepair capacity, ultimately leading to severe symptoms such as urinary incontinence and urinary retention [73,74]. These symptoms seriously affect patients' quality of life, and there is a lack of radical therapeutic strategies. In normal bladder tissue, nerve fibers are uniformly distributed, cholinergic and sensory nerve functions are stable, mast cell infiltration is minimal, and neuroregeneration is in a quiescent state, which can maintain normal urinary storage and micturition functions. β-III tubulin, a pan-neuronal specific marker, serves as the core basis for evaluating the overall innervation status of the bladder [77]. In this study, the sham group exhibited regular structures of the submucosal and intramuscular nerve plexuses in the bladder, with β-III tubulin-positive fibers distributed continuously throughout the full thickness, reflecting the integrity of the normal bladder innervation network. In contrast, the NB 2W group showed a sharp reduction in the distribution range of nerve fibers, a significant decrease in the positive area percentage, and fractured and distorted fibers; these pathological changes further deteriorated in the NB 4W group, confirming that bladder innervation undergoes time-dependent progressive damage after spinal cord injury. After PTT intervention, β-III tubulin staining was improved: the positive area percentage was significantly restored in the PTT 2W group, with partial reconstruction of the reticular structure; although the positive area percentage in the PTT 4W group showed an upward trend, there was no significant difference compared with the NB 4W group. These results directly confirm that PTT can ameliorate the structural damage of bladder nerves caused by spinal cord injury at an early stage, laying a morphological foundation for the recovery of neural function (Fig. 6A and B).

Toluidine blue staining indicated that in the Sham group, the transitional epithelium of the bladder tissue showed a clear stratification, with tightly and regularly arranged cells, no hyperplasia, shedding or metaplasia; the number of mast cells was extremely low, scattered individually in the superficial submucosa and intramuscular connective tissue without focal aggregation. In the NB 2W group, the epithelium presented with hyperplasia, hypertrophy and disordered stratification, with local thinning and exfoliation in some regions; mast cells showed focal and clustered aggregation, which further increased at 4 weeks, suggesting that the pathological progression of NB is accompanied by persistent degeneration of neural structures and pathological aggregation of mast cells. The mast cell density in the PTT group was lower than that in the NB group at the same time point, and the epithelium remained relatively intact (Fig. 6A–C). These findings indicate that PTT can improve the microenvironment for neurorepair and promote the reconstruction of neural structures by inhibiting the inflammatory response in the nerve injury area [78].

The integrity of neural structure is ultimately reflected by functional recovery, and the staining results of vesicular acetylcholine transporter (VACHT) and calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP) verified the therapeutic effect of PTT from the aspects of motor and sensory nerve functions, respectively.

VACHT is a specific marker of parasympathetic nerve function, which directly mediates acetylcholine transport and regulates detrusor contraction, serving as the core regulatory pathway for bladder micturition function [79]. In this study, VACHT-positive signals were densely distributed in the intramuscular nerve plexuses of the Sham group, with plump ganglion cells. In the NB 2W group, the intensity of VACHT-positive signals decreased, ganglion cells atrophied, and the structure became loose; in the NB 4W group, positive signals almost disappeared, and synaptic connections were completely lost, corresponding to the pathological manifestations of clinical dysuria and urinary retention. In contrast, the intensity of VACHT-positive signals was restored in the PTT 2W group; the staining characteristics of VACHT in the PTT 4W group were similar to those in the NB 4W group. These results confirm that PTT can alleviate the damage to the bladder parasympathetic nerve pathway at an early stage and preserve the neural regulatory mechanism of detrusor contraction, explaining part of the reasons for the recovery of bladder function after PTT treatment (Fig. 6A–D).

As a marker of sensory nerve function, CGRP mediates bladder fullness perception and pain conduction, acting as the afferent end of the neural reflex arc [80]. In the Sham group, CGRP-positive terminals were widely distributed in the basement membrane of the urothelium and the intercellular space of muscle fibers, forming a sensory network. In the NB group, the number of CGRP-positive terminals decreased sharply, and the reticular distribution was disrupted, reflecting the loss of bladder sensory conduction function after spinal cord injury, leading to dull fullness perception and interruption of the micturition reflex arc. After PTT intervention, the density of CGRP-positive signals increased and the sensory network was reconstructed, suggesting that early PTT intervention improved the bladder sensory nerve pathway (Fig. 6A–E).

Growth-associated protein 43 (GAP43), a specific marker of neuroregeneration, provides key clues for elucidating the repair mechanism of PTT [81]. Under physiological conditions, GAP43 was only weakly and sporadically expressed in the Sham group, reflecting a stable neural state. In the NB 2W group, the intensity of GAP43-positive signals was significantly increased, and positive fibers aggregated around the injury site with disordered bud-like distribution, indicating that the nerve initiated a regenerative response after spinal cord injury, but the regenerative process was disordered and failed to form functional innervation. The intensity of GAP43-positive signals remained high in the NB 4W group. In contrast, GAP43 staining in the PTT group showed dynamic repair characteristics: GAP43 maintained positive expression in the PTT 2W group but was lower than that in the NB 2W group; GAP43-positive signals still existed in the PTT 4W group but were lower than those in the PTT 2W group. These changes reveal that PTT can guide the ordered growth and maturation of injured nerve axons, integrate them into the original neural network, and ultimately achieve functional repair rather than disordered fiber hyperplasia by regulating the regenerative microenvironment(Fig. 6A–F).

This analysis comprehensively evaluated transcriptomic changes in NB with or without PTT, identifying key genes and pathways involved in PTT's therapeutic effects.

At 2 weeks, NB samples showed significant enrichment of “immune system process”, “response to stimulus”, and core inflammatory pathways (cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, TNF, NF-κB, Toll-like receptor pathways), indicating early dual inflammatory disorders. PTT reduced the proportion of inflammation-related differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and attenuated enrichment of these pathways, targeting key inflammatory signaling steps (Fig. 7 B, C, F, G). At 4 weeks, NB developed a complex inflammatory network (adding IL-17 and PD-L1/PD-1 checkpoint pathways) with progressive inflammation. PTT showed limited efficacy, only inhibiting basic inflammatory pathways without regulating newly activated ones (Fig. 7 D, E, H, I).

NB exhibited time-dependent activation of fibrosis-related mechanisms (ECM overdeposition, fibroblast activation, abnormal tissue repair). At 2 weeks, NB showed early fibrosis tendencies (abnormal ECM binding, activated cell adhesion molecules and ECM-receptor interaction pathways). PTT reduced DEGs related to membrane/protein complexes and ECM remodeling, blocking early fibrosis (Fig. 7 B, C, F, G). At 4 weeks, NB formed a robust fibrosis pathway network (enhanced cell adhesion, ECM-receptor interaction, PI3K-Akt pathways). PTT had minimal effects, failing to reverse abnormal cell junctions or inhibit key fibrosis pathways (Fig. 7 D, E, H, I).

NB progressed from “basic homeostasis imbalance” to “comprehensive dysregulation”. At 2 weeks, NB showed metabolic imbalance, abnormal proliferation, and disrupted MAPK/AGE-RAGE pathways. PTT restored metabolic-proliferative balance by reducing related DEGs (Fig. 7 B, C, F, G). At 4 weeks, NB exhibited severe metabolic disorder, dysregulated signaling, and uncontrolled proliferation. PTT only slightly inhibited the MAPK pathway without improving glycolipid metabolism (Fig. 7 D, E, H, I).

PTT exerted time-dependent therapeutic effects on NB, significant inhibition of inflammation/fibrosis and restoration of cell homeostasis at 2 weeks (blocking pathological progression), while efficacy diminished at 4 weeks due to consolidated pathological pathway networks in mature NB.

Transcriptome sequencing results further confirm the therapeutic value of PTT for NB from the perspective of gene expression. They reveal a global disorder in the gene expression network of bladder tissues in pathological NB, which is highly consistent with previously observed pathological phenotypes such as bladder dysfunction, fibroproliferation, and inflammatory infiltration. PTT significantly reduced the proportion of inflammation-related differentially expressed genes and pathway enrichment, inhibiting key links of inflammatory signal transmission. However, at 4 weeks, the NB model formed a complex inflammatory network; PTT only regulated basic inflammatory pathways, leading to a marked reduction in therapeutic efficacy. Fibrosis-related pathways showed a time-dependent activation trend: early fibrotic tendency appeared at 2 weeks in NB, and PTT effectively blocked initiating links such as ECM remodeling and abnormal cell adhesion. By 4 weeks, a mature fibrotic pathway network had formed; PTT only slightly inhibited some basic pathways and failed to reverse pathological progression. For cellular homeostasis: the NB model progressed from basic metabolic and proliferative imbalance at 2 weeks to comprehensive homeostatic disorder at 4 weeks. Early PTT intervention significantly restored metabolic and proliferative balance, while late intervention had little effect on the severely disrupted homeostatic network. Thus, the therapeutic effect of a single early PTT on NB is significantly time-dependent: early intervention (2 weeks) blocks pathological progression by inhibiting inflammatory initiation, preventing fibrotic onset, and repairing basic homeostasis; late intervention (4 weeks) shows markedly reduced efficacy due to the solidification of pathological pathway networks in the model.

Conclusion

In this study, mild PTT was applied for the treatment of SCI-induced NB. The results demonstrated that mild PTT significantly improved bladder function, as evidenced by enhanced urodynamic performance. Histological analyses further showed that PTT effectively reduced bladder wall thickening, collagen deposition, and fibrotic remodeling. At the molecular and cellular levels, PTT alleviated inflammatory responses, decreased macrophage infiltration, and restored the balance between cell proliferation and apoptosis in NB bladder tissue. In addition, mild PTT promoted neural repair and improved neural regulation, indicating its beneficial effects on neurogenic dysfunction. Transcriptomic analysis further revealed that the therapeutic efficacy of PTT exhibits a time-dependent pattern, highlighting the importance of early and sustained intervention.

Collectively, these findings suggest that mild PTT exerts therapeutic effects through coordinated regulation of inflammation, fibrosis, cellular homeostasis, and neural repair. This study provides mechanistic insights and a promising translational strategy for the treatment of neurogenic bladder.

Materials and methods

Animals and SSCI model

All experiments received approval from the Experimental Animal Management and Ethics Committee of the Shanghai Experimental Animal Research Center (Approval No.202400502). The healthy female Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats (8 weeks old, 200∼225 g) were acquired from Shanghai Jihui Experimental Animal Breeding Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) and housed under standard conditions, with 12 h light/dark cycle, 40% humidity, free access to water and food, and a controlled temperature of 22 ± 2◦C. The rats were randomly assigned to 5 groups of 15 rats each, with groups designated for sham operation or SSCI. Anesthesia for animal operations was achieved using a 3% pentobarbital sodium solution. 9 rats from each group underwent urodynamic analysis. Bladder tissues from 6 rats per group were collected for morphological and immunohistochemical studies, while the remaining tissues were harvested and stored at −80°C for subsequent molecular biology experiments. Additionally, bladder tissues from 3 rats in the sham operation group, NB 2W group,PTT 2W, NB 4W and PTT 4W were used for RNA-seq analysis. In accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines (Animals in Research: Reporting In Vivo Experiments), the experimental rats were humanely euthanized by cervical dislocation. This study adopted the rat model previously validated by our research group [82]. This model enables a relatively comprehensive characterization of bladder injury changes following SSCI. Meanwhile, it features simple operational procedures, high reproducibility, and stable reliability, thus holding significant clinical value as well as scientific and technological merit. It plays a crucial role in facilitating the rational diagnosis and treatment of NB induced by SSCI, and can provide a solid scientific foundation for subsequent large-scale experimental development and therapeutic research.

Implementation of PTT

SD rats were randomly divided into five groups: the sham operation group (Sham group), the SSCI model group without any intervention (NB group), the SSCI model group treated with near-infrared irradiation alone (NIR group), the SSCI model group administered with BBT nanoparticles alone (BBT NPs group), and the photothermal therapy group (PTT group), which received nanoparticle perfusion followed by NIR irradiation. BBT nanoparticles (two alkyl chains, 4,7-bis(4-(2-ethylhexyl)thiophen-2-yl)benzobis[c] [1,2,5]thiadiazole, BBT; molecular weight 582 Da) were used as the photothermal agent [16,17].

Rats in the NB, NIR, BBT NPs, and PTT groups underwent sacral spinal cord injury surgery to induce neurogenic bladder, while rats in the Sham group received only vertebral arch exposure without spinal cord damage. On the second day after surgery, treatments were initiated. For nanoparticle administration, BBT NPs were prepared at a concentration of 100 μg/mL and delivered into the bladder via ultrasound-guided intravesical injection, with a defined volume of 500 μL per rat to ensure consistent dosing.

In the NIR group, rats were irradiated with an 808 nm near-infrared laser for 15 min. In the PTT group, following intravesical administration of BBT NPs, the bladder region was exposed to an 808 nm near-infrared laser (0.5 W/cm2) for 15 min to activate the photothermal effect. During irradiation, real-time temperature monitoring was performed using an infrared thermal imager (DT-980; detector resolution: 80×80 pixels; thermal sensitivity <0.08°C), and the temperature was strictly maintained at 41.5 ± 0.5°C to achieve mild photothermal therapy conditions.

The room temperature was kept constant throughout the experiment to minimize environmental variation. This intravesical delivery strategy ensures direct contact of nanoparticles with bladder tissue, enhances local bioavailability, and minimizes systemic exposure.

Preparation of BBT nanoparticles

BBT nanoparticles (BBT NPs) were prepared using a nanoprecipitation method with DSPE-PEG2000 as an amphiphilic stabilizing agent. Briefly, BBT and DSPE-PEG2000 were co-dissolved in tetrahydrofuran (THF) at a mass ratio of 1:1 to obtain a homogeneous organic phase. The resulting solution was then added dropwise into deionized water under continuous magnetic stirring, with the aqueous phase maintained at a 10-fold excess volume relative to THF. During this process, the hydrophobic BBT molecules spontaneously self-assembled into nanoparticles in the presence of DSPE-PEG2000.

Subsequently, the suspension was subjected to ultrasonication for 2 min at an output power of 50 W to facilitate nanoparticle formation and improve dispersion homogeneity. Residual THF was then removed by rotary evaporation under reduced pressure. The obtained nanoparticle suspension was filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane to eliminate large aggregates and further concentrated using ultrafiltration centrifuge tubes.

The resulting BBT nanoparticles were stabilized solely by DSPE-PEG2000 without additional targeting ligands or surface functionalization and were directly used for subsequent in vitro and in vivo experiments [17]. (Fig. S3).

Biosafety detection of photothermal therapy

The SV-HUC-1 cell line was purchased from Wuhan Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd. After digestion, SV-HUC-1 cells were resuspended in Ham's F-12K medium supplemented with fetal bovine serum to a density of 2 × 105 cells/mL. A 100 μL aliquot of the cell suspension was evenly seeded into each well of a 96-well plate. The cells were then treated with BBT NPs at various concentrations (0, 50 μg/mL, 100 μg/mL, 200 μg/mL, 400 μg/mL) for 12 h and 24 h, respectively. Subsequently, the culture medium was discarded, and the CCK-8 assay solution was diluted to the working concentration with Ham's F-12K medium containing 10% FBS, followed by addition to the 96-well plate. After incubation at 37°C for 1 h in the dark, the optical density (OD) values were measured at a wavelength of 450 nm using a microplate reader. The cell viability was calculated with the OD values of the blank control group as the reference standard.

Twenty-four SD rats were selected and randomly divided into 4 groups with 6 rats in each group (n = 6): the normal control group (NC group), the near-infrared irradiation group (NIR group), the BBT NPs perfusion group (BBT NPs group), and the photothermal therapy group (PTT group). The NIR group was subjected to near-infrared laser irradiation alone for 15 min; in the absence of nanoparticles, the temperature of this group did not increase. BBT NPs at a concentration of 100 μg/mL were perfused into the bladders of rats in both the BBT NPs group and the PTT group. Subsequently, the PTT group received near-infrared laser irradiation for 15 min after nanoparticle perfusion. All rats were then raised under conventional feeding conditions. After 4 weeks of feeding, the rats were anesthetized, and blood samples were collected from the abdominal aorta. Immediately after collecting 2 mL of blood, the blood collection tubes were inverted repeatedly to prevent blood coagulation. The blood samples were analyzed using a blood and body fluid analyzer for routine blood tests. The white blood cell count, red blood cell count, hemoglobin level, and platelet count of each group were recorded. Subsequently, liver, kidney, lung, and spleen tissues of rats in each group were collected for hematoxylin-eosin (HE) staining to observe histological changes.

Urodynamics

Urodynamic examinations were conducted on each group of rats. All rats were anesthetized using urethane (1 g/kg), the preferred anesthetic for studying urination physiology [83,84]. In brief, a sterile polyethylene catheter (PE-50) was inserted into the bladder dome. Intravesical pressure was measured using this PE-50 catheter, connected to an electrophysiological recorder (BL-420N; TECHMAN, Chengdu, China) with a pressure transducer through a three-way stopcock. An infusion pump (JZB-1800; Fresenius-kabi jianyuan, Changsha, China) was used to pump preheated saline into the bladder at a rate of 0.2 mL/min until saline exuded from the external urinary orifice. Before each infusion, the bladder was gently pressed to expel any residual urine. LPP and MCC were recorded as urodynamic measurement parameters, and bladder compliance was calculated using the formula: bladder compliance = maximum bladder capacity/(LPP-initial intravesical pressure).

Tissue preparation and analysis

Immediately after completion of bladder pressure measurement, the experimental animals were euthanized, and the bladder was completely excised at the bladder neck for tissue collection. After careful removal of adipose and serosal tissues, the bladder was rinsed with saline, blotted dry on filter paper, and its wet weight was measured using a microbalance. The length and width of the bladder were also recorded. Subsequently, a portion of the bladder tissue was immersed in 4% paraformaldehyde (Beyotime, Shanghai, China; Cat#P0099-500 ml) and fixed at 4°C for 24 h. After that, the tissue was dehydrated in a graded ethanol series, embedded in paraffin, and cut into 4 μm thick sections. The remaining tissue was stored in cryovials at −80°C for further studies. The sections were stained with Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) to evaluate histological architecture and with Masson's Trichrome to assess the extent of fibrosis following SCI. Both H&E and Masson's staining were performed according to standard protocols. Stained sections were observed and imaged under a microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan), and the images were analyzed using Image-Pro Plus software (Version 6.0, Media Cybernetics, Silver Spring, MD, USA).

Immunohistochemistry

Paraffin sections were deparaffinized with an eco-friendly agent, dehydrated in a graded ethanol series (absolute, 95%, 75% ethanol sequentially), and rinsed thrice with distilled water. Heat-induced antigen retrieval was conducted with EDTA or citric acid, followed by natural cooling to room temperature and immersion in 3% H2O2 to block endogenous peroxidase activity. After demarcation with a histochemical pen, sections were blocked with 10% goat serum (BOSTER, Wuhan, China; Cat#AR1009) for 30 min at room temperature. Primary antibodies, diluted in 10% goat serum, were added dropwise (50–100 μL per section) and incubated overnight at 4°C. The next day, sections were rewarmed for 15 min at room temperature, rinsed thrice with TBST, and incubated with TBST-diluted secondary antibodies (50–100 μL per section) for 45 min at 37°C, followed by another three TBST rinses. Freshly prepared DAB (50 μL, adjusted by tissue size; Maxim, Fuzhou, China; Cat#DAB-4033) was applied dropwise, with the reaction monitored microscopically and terminated with tap water. Nuclei were stained with hematoxylin (Baso, Zhuhai, China; Cat#BA4041) for 1 min, differentiated in hydrochloric acid alcohol for 1–2 s after washing, blued, rinsed, and checked for nuclear staining. Slides were dehydrated with absolute ethanol, air-dried or baked at 37°C, mounted with an eco-friendly medium, air-dried, and examined under a bright-field microscope or scanner, then stored at room temperature in a cool, dry place. Antibody details are listed in Table S1.

Immunofluorescence staining

Paraffin sections were deparaffinized using an eco-friendly dewaxing agent, dehydrated in a graded ethanol series (absolute, 95%, and 75% ethanol sequentially), and rinsed thrice with distilled water. Antigen retrieval was implemented with EDTA, followed by demarcation of target areas using a histochemical pen and immersion in TBST. Sections were blocked with 10% donkey serum (Antgene, Wuhan, China; Cat# ANT051) for 30 min at room temperature. Primary antibodies were diluted in TBST, vortex-mixed thoroughly, and applied dropwise (50–100 μL per section) for overnight incubation at 4°C. After rewarming at room temperature for 15 min the next day, sections were rinsed thrice with TBST, and incubated with TBST-diluted secondary antibodies (50–100 μL per section, adjusted by tissue size) for 45 min at 37°C. Following TBST rinsing, DAPI working solution (Solarbio, Beijing, China; Cat# C0060), diluted 1:500, was added dropwise for 5-min staining in the dark, and sections were rinsed again with TBST. Finally, sections were mounted using fluorescent mounting medium (Southern Biotech, USA; Cat# 0100-01) and stored at 4°C in the dark. Fluorescent images were observed, captured, and analyzed under a fluorescence microscope. Detailed information on the antibodies used is provided in Table S2.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from bladder tissues using Trizol reagent (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA; Cat#15596018CN). First-strand cDNA was synthesized using a reverse transcription kit (Accurate Biology, Hunan, China; Cat# AG11728). Subsequently, qPCR reactions were performed on a real-time PCR system (Bio-Rad CFX96) using a SYBR Green I-based qPCR kit (Accurate Biology, Hunan, China; Cat# AG11733). The relative expression levels of target genes were calculated using the 2^(-ΔΔCt) method and normalized to the expression level of the reference gene GAPDH. Primer information is provided in Table S3.

Protein extraction and western blotting

Proteins were extracted from both cultured cells and tissue samples using RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China, Cat# P0013B) supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail (Servicebio, Wuhan, China, Cat# G2007-1 ML). The lysates were incubated on ice for 30 min and then centrifuged at 15,000 rpm for 15 min at 4°C. The resulting supernatant was collected and transferred to new microtubes. Protein concentration was determined using a BCA protein assay kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China, Cat# P0012S). Equal amounts of protein (30–60 μg) were separated by 4–20% SDS-PAGE (ACE, Jiangsu, China, Cat# ET15420LGel) and subsequently transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore, USA, Cat# IPVH00010) using a wet transfer system. The membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China, Cat# A600669-0250) for 2 h at room temperature. After blocking, the membranes were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C. Following washes, the membranes were incubated with corresponding horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies for 2h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) kit (APE×BIO, USA, Cat# K1231). Band intensities were quantified with ImageJ software, and the relative protein expression levels were normalized to GAPDH or β-Tubulin as internal controls. Antibodies used are listed in Table S4.

RNA-seq of rats in 5 groups

Total RNA from 15 rat samples was quality-controlled (purity, concentration, integrity) prior to Oligo(dT)-mediated mRNA enrichment. Enriched mRNA was fragmented, reverse-transcribed into double-stranded cDNA, and processed via end repair, A-tailing, adapter ligation, and ∼350 bp fragment screening. cDNA libraries were constructed by PCR amplification, denaturation, and cyclization (DNBSEQ-T7-specific), then sequenced on a high-throughput platform with PE150.

Raw reads were filtered (adapter-contaminated, low-quality, high-N-content) to obtain clean reads, which were aligned to the Rattus norvegicus genome (Rnor_6.0.89) using HISAT2. Gene expression was quantified as FPKM (HTSeq). DEGs were identified via DESeq2/DEGseq with |Fold Change|≥1.5, p ≤ 0.05, padj≤1, and annotated using NCBI, Uniprot, GO, and KEGG. GO/KEGG enrichment (clusterProfiler, padj<0.05), PPI network (STRING + Cytoscape), differential alternative splicing (rMATS), novel transcript prediction (Cuffcompare), variation detection (Samtools + bcfools), and transcription factor analysis (AnimalTFDB) were performed.

Statistics

The data of this experiment were statistically analyzed using Graphpad Prism and Excel. One-way ANOVA was employed for the analysis among multiple groups. The data results were presented in the form of mean ± standard deviation (mean ± SD), and P < 0.05 indicated that the difference was statistically significant.
