Heat stress induces organelle alterations in Macrocystis pyrifera gametophytes
Authors: Maddelyn Harden, David S Koos, Karina Arzuyan, Maxim Kovalev, Rusty Lansford, Filipe Alberto, Sergey Nuzhdin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractKelp, brown macroalgae in the order Laminariales, provide ecosystem services vital to ocean biodiversity. However, kelp forests worldwide are declining due to abiotic stressors such as ocean warming. In this study, we present results from high‐resolution confocal microscopy and in vivo imaging system imaging using protocols developed to visualize kelp gametophyte cells exposed to heat‐stress treatments. Imaging revealed chloroplast mislocalization, fragmentation, and subsequent loss of chloroplasts in heat‐stressed gametophyte cells. Additionally, nuclei exhibited fragmentation and a progressive loss of fluorescent signal, and the associated microbiome proliferated under various heat‐stress treatments. Notably, because brown algae possess a continuous outer membrane that connects the nuclear envelope and the chloroplast envelope, these observations suggest a cellular vulnerability underlying thermal sensitivity in brown macroalgae. Finally, by comparing heat‐stress tolerant and heat‐stress sensitive genotypes, we found that genotypes with higher heat tolerance exhibited substantially fewer abnormalities compared to sensitive ones.Keywords: algae, image analysis, kelp, light, macroalgae, molecular, morphology, photosynthesis, pigments, temperature

INTRODUCTION

The Earth is experiencing unprecedented shifts in ocean temperatures, with marine heat waves and gradual warming trends increasing in frequency, intensity, and duration (National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration [NOAA], 2023). These climatic changes present significant challenges to marine ecosystems, particularly kelp forests, which are highly susceptible to rising ocean temperatures and changes in water chemistry (Assis et al., 2018; Liesner, Fouqueau, et al., 2020; Liesner, Shama, et al., 2020; Schmid et al., 2020; Straub et al., 2022; Wernberg et al., 2013, 2018; Wood et al., 2020). Kelp forests, known for their rapid growth and roles in supporting biodiverse habitats (Graham et al., 2007; Steneck et al., 2002), provide essential ecosystem services including bioremediation, carbon sequestration, and habitat structure (Smale et al., 2013; Teagle et al., 2017; Wernberg et al., 2019). Additionally, they play crucial roles in supporting coastal fisheries, tourism, and recreational activities that drive local economies (Coleman et al., 2020). However, the increasing occurrence of heat waves poses a significant threat to kelp survival, making it critical to understand how these ecosystems respond to heat stress, to preserve marine biodiversity and support the communities that depend on them (Harden et al., 2024; Krumhansl et al., 2016; United Nations Environment Programme, 2023; Wade et al., 2020).

With a primarily temperate and subpolar distribution, kelp are especially vulnerable to elevated seawater temperatures associated with global warming, and anomalously warm, nutrient‐poor conditions during strong El Niño events have repeatedly driven large‐scale kelp mortality across the Northeast Pacific (Edwards & Estes, 2006). For over a century, it has been understood that warming ocean conditions limit kelp distribution and productivity (Cavanaugh et al., 2019; Coleman et al., 2020; Fernández et al., 2020; Gurgel et al., 2020). Marine heatwaves (MHWs), defined as periods when ocean temperatures exceed the climatological average for extended durations (Hobday et al., 2016), further compound this threat, often coinciding with low‐nutrient conditions that intensify stress on kelp forests.

Although studies of microscopic kelp development remain limited, emerging evidence has highlighted the importance of understanding heat‐stress responses for predicting future impacts and identifying resilient genotypes for restoration (Hu et al., 2024; Martins et al., 2023; Schiel & Foster, 2006). Recent warming trends in the Northeast Pacific and California Current, including the 2014 MHW known as “The Blob,” have caused widespread ecological disruption, including species distribution shifts and altered marine food webs (Joyce et al., 2024; Michaud et al., 2022). McPherson et al. (2021) reported over 95 percent loss of kelp canopy from 2014 to 2015 in Northern California due to extreme sea surface temperature anomalies. The 2022 MHW, which began in January and peaked at approximately 8 million square kilometers, was the fourth largest in area and the third longest since records began in 1982. Unlike the previous year, this event included large intrusions into the coastal zone, particularly along the central California coast. These regional trends mirror global ocean warming, with 2022 recorded as the warmest year to date for the Northeast Pacific (NOAA, 2023).

The kelp species Macrocystis pyrifera, commonly known as “giant kelp,” presents a biphasic, haplodiplontic life cycle with distinct gametophyte (1n, microscopic) and sporophyte (2n, macroscopic) stages (Molano et al., 2022). The gametophyte generation of M. pyrifera produces gametes through gametogenesis, with males and females differentiating into sperm and egg cells, respectively. Fertilization results in a diploid zygote that develops into a diploid sporophyte, familiar as the giant forest‐forming kelp thallus (Reed et al., 1997; Schiel & Foster, 2015). Brown algae, including M. pyrifera, diverged from the lineage that led to green algae and land plants over a billion years ago. Their photosynthetic machinery was acquired through a secondary endosymbiotic event involving red algae, resulting in a unique evolutionary relationship between the chloroplast and nuclear genomes (Watson et al., 2018).

Understanding this interaction helps in the interpretation of subcellular changes observed under heat‐stress conditions and may provide insight into the evolutionary constraints on thermal tolerance in brown algae. How the gametophyte stage of Macrocystis pyrifera reacts to heat stress is essential for understanding the reproduction and persistence of kelp forest ecosystems undergoing ocean warming events (Martins et al., 2023; Veenhof et al., 2022, 2024; Vranken et al., 2021). A growing body of research has demonstrated that heat stress affects multiple life stages of kelp, including both microscopic and macroscopic stages. In particular, Edwards (2019) synthesized the effects of repeated El Niño–Southern Oscillation‐associated warming on M. pyrifera populations in the northeast Pacific, showing that thermal anomalies and storm disturbances caused substantial declines in kelp abundance across several major events. More recently, Edwards (2022) emphasized the critical role of microscopic stages in maintaining kelp populations and demonstrated that early life‐history stages often exhibit strong sensitivity to temperature and nutrient limitation, making them key regulators of kelp demographic persistence under climate change. Together, these studies highlight that thermal vulnerability is not restricted to the sporophyte stage but is deeply rooted in the biology of the gametophyte.

In 2018, 550 Macrocystis pyrifera male and female gametophytes were collected and genotyped from four distinct populations along the southern California coastline: Arroyo Quemado (AQ), Leo Carrillo (LC), Catalina Island (CI), and Camp Pendleton (CP; Johansson et al., 2015; Osborne et al., 2023). From this genetic resource, we previously developed a phenotyping pipeline to assess the heat‐stress responses of 204 kelp genotypes across a range of elevated temperatures (21–27°C; Harden et al., 2024). That study uncovered genetic associations with heat tolerance, as well as predictive relationships between early‐stage gametophyte responses and the performance of genetically similar diploid sporophytes grown in warm‐water ocean farm conditions.

In the present study, we expanded on this phenotyping framework by incorporating confocal microscopy and histological validation to resolve the cellular phenotypes underlying heat‐induced deterioration in kelp gametophytes previously described at the whole‐gametophyte phenotypic level by Harden et al. (2024). At the time this study was initiated, there were limited published protocols for fixing, staining, and imaging kelp gametophytes using an in vivo imaging system (IVIS) or confocal microscopy. To support the development of a reproducible cellular imaging approach, gametophytes were exposed to a range of temperatures informed by conditions they may encounter under ocean‐warming scenarios. The selected temperature treatments of 13°C (control) and 21, 23, 25, and 27°C (elevated temperature) were not intended to replicate specific in situ MHWs or exact environmental conditions experienced by giant kelp gametophytes; rather, this gradient was chosen to span conditions over which both physiological decline and subcellular disruption could be detected using complementary imaging methods.

By integrating high‐throughput chlorophyll fluorescence measurements obtained using IVIS with confocal microscopy, we identified temperatures at which chlorophyll fluorescence visibly declined while subcellular changes became apparent. This integrated approach enabled characterization of kelp gametophyte responses to heat stress and identification of approximate temperature thresholds at which those responses emerge. Using confocal imaging, we observed not only mislocalization and fragmentation of chloroplasts but also, unexpectedly, similar fragmentation of nuclei in heat‐sensitive and heat‐compromised genotypes, cellular phenotypes that have not been previously reported for kelp gametophytes. The microbiome around kelp cell walls greatly proliferated under heat‐stress conditions. To confirm that these observations reflected underlying cellular alterations rather than imaging artifacts, histological approaches were developed as independent validation.

Finally, a 2‐week follow‐up experiment was conducted in which the full imaging protocol was repeated using a subset of genotypes previously identified as heat‐resilient or heat‐sensitive. This follow‐up enabled evaluation of the stability of the observed cellular phenotypes over time and confirmed the robustness of the heat‐stress screening approach. Together, these results provide foundational insight into how the microscopic life stage of California giant kelp responds to elevated temperature stress, revealing consistent differences in cellular responses between heat‐resilient and heat‐susceptible natural genotypes.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Four‐week heat stress and subsequent recovery treatments

Four heat‐stress and recovery assays were constructed using a panel of genotyped giant kelp male and female gametophytes, collected first in 2018 from distinct populations along the southern California Bight (Johansson et al., 2015). Sexually dimorphic Macrocystis pyrifera gametophytes were first isolated in laboratory conditions and then stored in red‐wavelength light and low‐nutrient, low‐temperature conditions in Conviron incubators for approximately 4 years before the start of this research.

To obtain clonal gametophytes, individual genotyped gametophytes were selected from an existing germplasm biobank and fragmented to generate sufficient biomass for experimental replication. Fragments were allowed to grow into full gametophytes under ideal growth conditions before being plated as described below. Selection of genotypes was based on the availability of adequate biomass per individual, as some genotypes consisted of only a single, low‐biomass gametophyte and could not support replicated assays. Within each of the four source populations, genotypes were otherwise selected without regard to sex, with the goal of including approximately equal numbers of individuals per population and three biological replicates per genotype. It was assumed that sex did not influence heat‐stress responses. Because the original germplasm collection contained a disproportionate number of male and female gametophytes across populations, the resulting sex ratios in this study reflect the composition of the biobank rather than intentional experimental bias. The full description of the Macrocystis pyrifera germplasm collection, including gametophyte acquisition, sex determination, sequencing, fragmentation, and quality filtering, is provided in Osborne et al. (2023).

One week prior to the heat‐stress treatments, whole gametophytes were randomly distributed across black Eppendorf polymerase chain reaction (PCR) clean, u‐bottomed microplates in three replicates per genotype (Harden et al., 2024). Black well‐plates were used because the IVIS system uses top‐down illumination to induce fluorescence, and black plates are specifically designed to minimize light scatter and background reflection. This is standard practice in IVIS protocols to improve signal‐to‐noise ratio and ensure reliable detection of fluorescence. We standardized the excitation and emission parameters as detailed in the fluorescent imaging protocols section to maintain imaging acquisition consistency across all samples.

A randomized plating design was generated using the Well‐Plate Maker (WPM) package in R (Borges et al., 2021). Each genotype was plated in full‐strength Provasoli enriched seawater (PES) medium following protocols in the New England Kelp Culturing Handbook (Redmond et al., 2014). The 96‐well plates containing randomly plated gametophytes and their replicates were next conditioned in a Conviron Incubator at 13°C under low, red‐light conditions (~615–620 nm) provided by regular white LED lights covered with red plastic cling film on a 12:12 light:dark photoperiod for 1 week. Prior to being plated, gametophytes were also grown within the same Conviron Incubator under these exact conditions.

Following this acclimation period, whole gametophytes were then placed in one of four heat‐stress treatments by way of Thermo Scientific Heratherm IMC18 Benchtop Incubators set at 21, 23, 25, and 27°C or a control treatment at 13°C in a Colzer compressor wine cooler (Model #CZW30SS1). Heat‐stress treatment periods lasted for 4 weeks. Immediately following heat‐stress treatments, plates were placed into the Colzer compressor wine cooler at 13°C for a subsequent 4 weeks for a 4‐week recovery period.

Temperature treatments were controlled with an uncertainty of ±0.06–0.5°C. Each plate per temperature treatment was lidded with a clear lid, and parafilm was used to seal the interstices between the lid and the plate to reduce evaporation and contamination. Marreal brand Red LED strip lights were installed in each incubator to maintain low red wavelengths. Temperature and light data were collected via five Onset HOBO Waterproof Bluetooth Pendant Temperature Data Loggers at 10‐min intervals (model # MX2201).

In vivo imaging system 3D tomography

On Day 0, prior to being placed into their assigned treatment, lidded and parafilmed 96‐well plates containing gametophytes were transported to the IVIS imaging facility, located in the basement of the same building as the heat‐stress chambers, in covered Styrofoam boxes to reduce light exposure and temperature variations. Control treatments were kept at 13°C using cooled gel packs placed in their lidded Styrofoam box during transport. Post‐imaging, the boxes containing microplates were transported two floors upstairs to their respective treatment incubators. For each heat‐stress assay, plates were imaged via IVIS on Day 0 prior to treatment, and on Days 14, 28, 42, and 56. Days 0–28 mark the pre‐treatment period to the end of heat‐stress treatments, whereas Days 28, 42, and 56 mark the beginning and end of the recovery period.

Chlorophyll fluorescence intensity (FI) values were first extracted via IVIS imaging and then recorded using LivingImage software (PerkinElmer Inc., Version 4.3.1.0.158880). The IVIS imaging parameters were set to excitation 500 nm and emission 680 nm for each plate across all assays. Data extraction utilized the LivingImage software's region of interest (ROI) tool, measuring fluorescence intensity in [p · s−1] / [μ W · cm−2].

Chlorophyll standards (Millipore Sigma) were plated in dilutions of 100×, 500×, and 2500× using corn oil as a diluent to ensure accurate readings. Detailed IVIS methods are published in Harden et al. (2024). To enable accurate quantification of fluorescence intensity per kelp gametophyte during IVIS imaging, we prepared three chlorophyll a standards per well plate using lab‐grade chlorophyll diluted in corn oil. This approach mitigates inner filter effects and self‐absorption at high concentrations, which can otherwise distort fluorescence signals and compromise data comparability. Corn oil was chosen for its optical transparency, ability to solubilize chlorophyll, and chemical stability under IVIS imaging conditions.

Gametophyte preparation and imaging parameters

Single gametophyte branches were excised from one genotype for both control and heat stress treatments for Assays 1–3 for the entire 8‐week heat‐stress and recovery treatment period. Kelp gametophytes appear as filamentous structures composed of elongated, uniseriate cells. These filaments can develop small lateral outgrowths, multiple cells in length, that arise at angles from the main filament and may differentiate into reproductive structures such as oogonia or antheridia (Figure 1).

A Catalina Island female gametophyte (Genotype ID: “CI.08.F.C4”) exhibited a healthy cell phenotype across all observed cells. Cells are delineated by the SR220 cell‐wall stain visualized here in pale blue. The gametophyte was treated at the control temperature of 13°C and imaged on Day 0. Staining with SR2200 resulted in consistent, homogenous visualization of the cell wall, characterized by a pale blue fluorescence surrounding the chloroplasts. SYBR Green I was used to effectively stain nuclei and visualize the microbiome, while chlorophyll autofluorescence highlighted the chloroplasts, providing a comprehensive view of subcellular structures. Scale bar: 10 μm.

To preserve cellular architecture, a 2% formaldehyde (FA) fixation solution in 1× filter‐sterilized artificial seawater (FSASW) was prepared. The FSASW was made by combining Instant Ocean synthetic seawater with MilliQ water and filtering through 0.2‐μm filters. To generate 40 mL of a 4% FA solution, 10 mL of 16% methanol‐free formaldehyde (Polysciences, Inc., cat. no. 18814) was added to 4 mL of 10× FSASW and 26 mL of MilliQ water in a 50‐mL Falcon™ tube. This 4% solution was then diluted 1:1 with FSASW to produce the 2% FA solution.

Gametophytes were carefully transferred from sterile, black, U‐bottom 96‐well plates to pre‐labeled FluoroDishes using transfer pipettes. Under a dissecting microscope, individual branches were excised using #5 micro tweezers and placed in small volumes of FSASW at the center of each dish. Excess seawater and PES were removed to concentrate the sample into a central droplet, and 200 μL of the 2% FA fixation solution was then added. Samples were incubated at room temperature (~25°C) for approximately 30 min.

Following fixation, samples were rinsed directly on the dish with FSASW and prepared for staining. In the first two assays, cell walls were stained with Calcofluor White (Fluorescent Brightener 28, Sigma‐Aldrich, cat. no. F3543), prepared as a 1:1 solution with potassium hydroxide (KOH). This solution was applied to the samples, incubated for 15 min at room temperature, and rinsed with FSASW. However, due to precipitation and inconsistent staining results, Calcofluor White was replaced in Assay 3 with SCRI Renaissance Stain 2200 (SR2200, Renaissance Chemicals), which was prepared as a 0.01% (v/v) solution in FSASW. The SR2200 provided more uniform and reliable cell‐wall staining, leading to its adoption as the preferred stain for all subsequent imaging assays.

To visualize nuclear material and the associated microbiome, samples were incubated with SYBR Green I nucleic acid stain (Invitrogen, cat. no. S7563), diluted 1:10,000 in FSASW. Two hundred microliters of the stain were applied directly to the dish and incubated at room temperature for 15 min. After staining, samples were rinsed again with FSASW to remove excess dye. All staining and rinsing steps were performed directly within the FluoroDishes™ to minimize sample handling and reduce the risk of biomass loss.

For all fixed and stained imaging except that shown in Figure 2, gametophyte branches were mounted in one to two drops of PermaFluor Mountant (Thermo Scientific, cat. no. TA‐030‐FM) at the center of a FluoroDish. Branches were carefully arranged flat within the mountant using an ultrafine deer hair tool (Ted Pella, cat. no. 113), then overlaid with a round cover glass. The samples were allowed to cure overnight at room temperature in the dark prior to imaging.

Kelp gametophyte cell histology

Histological samples were independently collected from heat‐stressed and control‐treated gametophytes, separate from the four primary assays, and all were derived from a single genotyped female line (Genotype ID: Camp Pendleton.46.F). After being treated for 2 weeks at 13 (Control), 25, and 27°C, gametophytes were placed in microcentrifuge tubes, and FSASW media was removed, replaced with 300 uL of 2% FA/FSASW, and then incubated for 30 min at room temperature. The wells were then rinsed three times with 300 uL of FSASW. The gametophytes were immersed in optimal cutting temperature compound (O.C.T. Compound for Cryostat Sectioning, Tissue‐Tek Cat # 27050), and frozen as blocks. The Children's Hospital Los Angeles Histology Core sequentially sectioned the gametophytes at 15 μM.

The sections were stained with 4′,6‐diamidino‐2‐phenylindole (DAPI), a fluorescent stain that binds to DNA. This stain highlights the nuclei of the cells and the microbiome. This staining procedure allows for visualizing cellular structures and assessing any morphological changes induced by heat stress.

Stained sections were examined for fluorescence under a Zeiss Axioplan II microscope (Zeiss, Germany). Images were captured using a digital camera attached to the microscope, and the morphological features of the gametophytes were analyzed to assess the effects of heat stress on their cellular structure. The images obtained from the microscopic analysis were subjected to quantitative and qualitative assessment to determine the extent of cellular damage or alterations induced by the heat‐stress treatment. Comparisons were made among the control and heat‐stressed samples to identify any significant differences in cellular morphology and to elucidate the potential mechanisms of heat‐stress tolerance in kelp gametophytes.

Two‐week heat‐stress screen

In a follow‐up experiment, the original IVIS and confocal protocols were replicated by utilizing only genotypes with pre‐defined heat‐stress responses. Three kelp gametophyte genotypes were selected based on heat‐resistance rankings from a prior study (Harden et al., 2024): CP.75.F.D4 (high heat tolerance, rank 2), CI.22.F.D2 (moderate heat tolerance, rank 69), and LC.04.F.D4 (low heat tolerance, rank 162). Hence, the CP.75.F.D4 genotype was predicted to be heat‐stress tolerant (HST), LC.04.F.D4 to be heat‐stress sensitive (HSS), and CI.22.F.D2 to exhibit an intermediate phenotype. These genotypes were exposed to control (13°C) and heat‐stress (25°C) temperatures, and data were collected on Days 0, 7, and 14 to refine the timeline of the original heat‐stress protocol, which had previously spanned 28 days with data collection on Days 0, 14, and 28. This shorter timeframe enabled a more targeted investigation of responses under heat stress conditions.

Fluorescence Z‐stacks of the prepared gametophytes were acquired using an inverted Zeiss LSM 780 confocal microscope running Zen Black software and using a Zeiss LD C‐Apochromat 63×/1.15 W Korr M27 water immersion objective lens. Three channels were collected with the following imaging parameters: SR2200 Renaissance Stain (cell wall): excitation 405 nm, emission 415–483 nm; SYBR Green (gametophyte nuclei and microbiome): excitation 488 nm, emission 500–570 nm; and chloroplasts (chlorophyll): excitation 561 nm, emission 636–758 nm.

Image analysis pipeline to analyze chloroplast surface area loss over time

The image analysis pipeline employed scikit‐image for processing tasks, including grayscale conversion, segmentation using Otsu's thresholding, and morphological operations to remove noise and isolate chloroplasts from the surrounding background. Numerical computations, such as the calculation of mean and median chloroplast areas, were conducted using NumPy, while pandas was used to organize and export chloroplast measurements into structured datasets for statistical analysis. Processed images were visualized with overlaid chloroplast contours using Matplotlib to verify segmentation accuracy and consistency.

RESULTS

Four‐week treatments and in vivo imaging system 3D tomography

In our IVIS imaging analysis, we quantified chlorophyll fluorescence as a proxy for physiological health across a series of four independent temperature exposure assays. Fluorescence intensity significantly declined at 27°C across all genotypes by Week 2, whereas 25°C induced a highly variable response dependent on genotype, with some individuals retaining high signal and others exhibiting early decline, as reported in Harden et al. (2024). No qualitatively outstanding differences in fluorescence were observed among the 13, 21, and 23°C treatments, confirming these temperatures did not elicit a detectable stress response (Figures S1 and S2). Based on this variation, subsequent visual analyses focused on the 25°C condition to resolve genotype‐specific cellular responses. Although a recovery period was included following the 4‐week heat‐stress exposure, no increases in fluorescence intensity compared with the initial values were observed in any treatment group during the subsequent 4 weeks at 13°C. Confocal microscopy corroborated these findings, showing no restoration of chloroplast organization or nuclear fluorescence signal between Weeks 4 and 8. As a result, recovery data were not included in our visual phenotype analysis, which focused on the stress‐response phase captured by IVIS and confocal imaging.

Confocal imaging analysis: Initial 4‐week heat‐stress screen revealed loss of nuclei fluorescent signal within individual cells and other organelle changes

Using confocal microscopy, we aimed to characterize the subcellular phenotypes underlying the 25°C temperature treatment regime and genotype‐dependent responses. To achieve this, we established a suite of confocal, staining, and histological protocols that reliably traced the fate of chloroplasts and nuclei in individual gametophyte cells.

Although there were a total of four experimental heat‐stress and recovery assays, only three of these included confocal imaging due to scheduling constraints. Across our confocal imaging experiments, we identified three distinct cellular states in kelp gametophyte branches: healthy, intermediate, and stressed.

Healthy branches exhibited chloroplasts (evenly aligned along the inner circumference of each cell), centralized nuclei, and a sparse microbiome lightly fringed along the outer cell wall (Figure 1). This phenotype was consistently observed in control treatments and in heat‐stressed individuals up to 25°C.

Stressed branches displayed multiple markers of cellular disruption. Chloroplasts became mislocalized, aggregating asymmetrically to one side of the cell or forming uneven clusters on opposite walls (Figures 4 and 5). Chloroplast morphology also became increasingly irregular, showing greater heterogeneity in size and shape. These characteristics paralleled stress responses observed in terrestrial plants such as Arabidopsis thaliana, in which cell nuclei were observed to relocate to the periphery under light‐induced stress (Iwabuchi et al., 2016). Similarly, in our heat‐stressed gametophytes, nuclear fluorescent signal became undetectable, suggesting nuclear degradation or signal quenching. In addition, microbiome proliferation was markedly elevated in stressed individuals, with dense microbial communities accumulating along the outer surfaces of gametophyte cells (Figure 4).

At 25°C, some genotypes began to exhibit a phenotype we termed the intermediate heat‐stress state, characterized by a mosaic of healthy and stressed cells within a single branch (Figure 5). This transitional phenotype suggests intra‐individual variability in cellular tolerance to heat stress. These cellular phenotypes were consistent with IVIS‐based fluorescence intensity measurements.

Notably, IVIS imaging revealed near‐complete loss of chlorophyll fluorescence by Week 2 at temperatures exceeding 25°C. At exactly 25°C, we observed the greatest variability in fluorescence intensity, aligning with the emergence of intermediate cellular states. By contrast, temperatures of 13, 21, and 23°C did not produce substantially different fluorescence values, suggesting a physiological threshold for heat stress is crossed near 25°C.

Histological evidence of nuclear loss in heat‐treated gametophytes

To confirm that the synergistic IVIS and confocal imaging observations were a result of the loss of nuclear fluorescent signal, a proliferated microbiome, and lost and mislocalized chloroplasts, we cryo‐sectioned DAPI‐stained histology core samples from a Camp Pendleton Female (Genotype ID: CP.46.F) that had been clonally propagated and treated at both a control condition of 13°C and heat‐stress conditions of 25 and 27°C.

When viewed using a Zeiss fluorescent microscope, samples at 13°C showed a dim yet present microbiome and numerous nuclei. At 25°C, a brighter microbiome and a significant loss of nuclei were observed. Finally, at 27°C, a very bright microbiome and a complete loss of nuclei were evident, indicating severe cellular damage under high‐temperature stress. Chloroplasts, microbiome, and nuclei were examined using chlorophyll and DAPI filters, respectively (Figure 6).

Two‐week heat stress screen

Harden et al. (2024) ranked top performing gametophyte genotypes based on their FI slopes under heat‐stress conditions. Here, the original 4‐week IVIS and confocal screen protocol was replicated specifically to enable confocal microscopy for HSS, intermediate, and HST genotypes (see Harden et al., 2024 and the Materials and Methods section). As an example, the high‐ranked HST genotype, CP.75.F.D4, maintained cellular integrity by retaining nuclei fluorescent signaling and showing no loss or mislocalization of chloroplasts, while displaying a proliferated microbiome throughout Days 7 and 14 (Figure 7, Panels a, b). In contrast, the intermediate‐ranked genotype and the HSS genotype CI.22.F.D2 exhibited a mix of healthy and stressed cell phenotypes. At Day 7, cells showed increased microbiomes along with chloroplast loss and mislocalization, and by Day 14, there was noticeable loss of nuclei signaling (Figure 7, Panels c, d). The HSS genotype, LC.04.F.D4, consistently displayed unhealthy cell morphologies; on Day 7, there was a significant increase in the microbiome, severe chloroplast rearrangement, and loss of nuclei signaling. By Day 14, the microbiome proliferation was even more pronounced with no nuclei signaling detected (Figure 7, Panels e, f).

Image analysis to analyze chloroplast Mislocalization

To evaluate the effects of heat stress on chloroplast morphology across genotypes, an automated image analysis pipeline was developed using Python software. This approach facilitated quantification of chloroplast surface area from images obtained under multiple temperature treatments (25 and 27°C) over multiple time points (Days 0 to 28). The analysis incorporated several open‐source software packages to ensure reproducibility and precision in data processing. It is important to note that for this pipeline to work effectively, the images, originally acquired as z‐stacks (3D), must lie as flat as possible within a single plane. This placement ensures accurate representation and quantification of chloroplast surface area loss. Achieving this placement involves preparing samples in a manner that minimizes image depth. Samples were mounted in PermaFluor and allowed to settle overnight to improve image flatness and measurement accuracy. Additionally, the images analyzed contained different branches of the same gametophyte rather than tracking the same branch over time. Thus, the analysis was not designed as a longitudinal study of individual chloroplasts but rather as a visualization tool to highlight patterns of chloroplast loss, fragmentation, and mislocalization under heat‐stress conditions over the treatment period.

DISCUSSION

The selection of 13°C as the control temperature was based on previous research that has identified this temperature as optimal for the growth and development of Macrocystis pyrifera gametophytes (Graham et al., 2007; Hsiao & Druehl, 1973; Redmond et al., 2014). The range of heat‐stress treatments from 21 to 27°C encompassed temperatures above the average sea surface temperature in the Southern California Bight, where M. pyrifera has a native range (Huang et al., 2021). This temperature range aligned with the anomalies recorded during marine heatwaves, making it pertinent for studying kelp gametophyte heat tolerance in the context of climate change (Edwards & Estes, 2006; Di Lorenzo & Mantua, 2016). The significant genetic variation in phenotypic responses at 25°C suggests that this temperature may represent a tipping point in the heat tolerance of M. pyrifera gametophytes. This threshold may indicate the upper limit of the adaptive capacity of the species, beyond which physiological mechanisms cannot compensate for heat stress. Understanding this temperature limit is essential for enhancing our understanding of the resilience of kelp populations facing ocean temperature changes.

Our research focuses on the gametophyte stage, as understanding the response of kelp gametophytes to elevated temperatures is critical for forecasting the resilience of kelp populations to increasing ocean temperatures and for guiding conservation efforts for kelp ecosystems (Wernberg et al., 2019). We used confocal microscopy to compare wild‐type (WT) giant kelp gametophytes with those undergoing heat stress. This analysis revealed significant morphological differences between heat‐stressed gametophytes and WT ones, most notably in the intracellular location of chloroplasts and the loss of fluorescent signal from the nuclei. In our initial trials (Figure 1), we imaged WT gametophytes and compared them against those undergoing extreme heat stress (>21°C) to test visualization of any subcellular changes (Figures 4 and 5).

Fixation and staining results for confocal imaging

Initially, we attempted to stain and visualize the gametophyte cell wall using Calcofluor White (CW), a well‐known cell‐wall stain (O'Shaughnessy et al., 2003). Although CW staining worked on formaldehyde‐fixed gametophytes, it tended to precipitate, leading to uneven staining. Although this inconsistency did not compromise the assessment of subcellular morphology, it was not ideal for consistent imaging. To better visualize the cell wall and other subcellular structures, we tested several vital fluorescent dyes. SCRI Renaissance 2200 (SR2200) was effective for staining both fixed and living Macrocystis pyrifera gametophytes without deleterious effects. SCRI Renaissance SR2200 stain (Musielak et al., 2016), which excites in the ultraviolet range with a 405‐nm laser line and emits between 415 and 476 nm, produced homogeneous staining of the gametophyte cell wall. Its spectral differences from chlorophyll fluorescence, which emits in the 674–685 nm range, minimized interference. This result made SR2200 an optimal choice for visualizing cell walls alongside chlorophyll fluorescence (Figure 1).

Additionally, we tested SYBR Green I to visualize cellular components and the associated microbiome under heat‐stress versus control conditions. SYBR Green I successfully stained the nuclei of both living and formaldehyde‐fixed Macrocystis pyrifera gametophyte cells. It also stained the microbiome associated with the cell wall. Its spectral characteristics, distinct from SR2200 and chlorophyll fluorescence, made it ideal for labelling nuclei and the microbiome in both living and fixed gametophytes (Figure 1).

Chloroplast mislocalization and photoprotection

Chloroplast avoidance movement is a critical mechanism that plants employ to mitigate photodamage under high‐light conditions. In our study, chloroplast's observed mislocalization in heat‐stressed kelp gametophytes seemingly aligned with other research on chloroplast behavior in response to heat stress (Iwabuchi et al., 2016). As temperatures increased, we noted that chloroplasts were not in their normal position along the inner circumference of the cell wall. Instead, they appeared to cluster together along one or more sides of the cell wall. Like nuclei, chloroplast rearrangement has been observed in terrestrial plants such as Arabidopsis thaliana, where chloroplasts cluster along the cell walls in response to light stress (Dutta et al., 2017). Chloroplast movement may serve as a protective response to minimize light absorption and reduce the potential for damage caused by reactive oxygen species (ROS) generated during stress (Kasahara et al., 2002).

Our analysis revealed that heat‐stressed gametophytes exhibited irregular chloroplast shapes and sizes, contrasting sharply with the uniform, dumbbell‐shaped chloroplasts in non‐stressed cells. This contrast suggests a disruption in chloroplast function, likely due to the physiological stress imposed by elevated temperatures. The correlation between chloroplast relocation and the health of the gametophytes underscores the importance of these cellular responses for maintaining photosynthetic efficiency and overall cellular integrity.

Moreover, the significant loss of fluorescent signal in the nuclei of heat‐stressed gametophytes suggests that nuclear function may also be compromised during heat stress, potentially disrupting the signaling pathways that regulate chloroplast behavior and responses to environmental stressors. This phenomenon aligned with previous research indicating that the communication between chloroplasts and the host genome is critical for the adaptive response to temperature changes (Watson et al., 2018).

Microbiome proliferation at temperatures of 25°C and higher

Imaging revealed a consistent and striking increase in microbiome abundance on the outer surface of heat‐stressed gametophyte cells at 25 and 27°C, in which nuclear fluorescence was diminished or absent and chloroplasts were severely mislocalized or fragmented. In contrast, control samples maintained at 13°C and treatment samples of 21 and 23°C exhibited sparse and evenly distributed microbial communities. This shift in microbiome abundance may reflect a breakdown in host‐microbiome homeostasis under heat stress. In healthy kelp gametophytes, microbial communities are likely regulated through host‐derived immune responses, surface chemistry, and nutrient availability. However, elevated temperatures may impair these regulatory mechanisms, allowing opportunistic or stress‐tolerant microbes to colonize more aggressively. The resulting microbial proliferation could exacerbate cellular stress by increasing oxidative load, competing for limited resources, or introducing pathogenic interactions, thereby compounding the physiological burden imposed by heat stress.

Our findings were consistent with prior studies that have shown kelp‐associated microbiomes to be dynamic and responsive to environmental change. For example, microscopy‐based analyses of Macrocystis pyrifera and Ecklonia radiata, the latter another member of the Laminariales, demonstrated that microbial biofilms can thicken and shift in composition under warming scenarios, sometimes favoring taxa associated with disease or stress tolerance (Marzinelli et al., 2015; Minich et al., 2018). These studies, combined with our observations at the gametophyte stage, suggest that microbiome dysbiosis may serve as both a marker and a potential driver of thermal sensitivity in kelp. However, no cause‐and‐effect relationship between the microbiome and the destruction of the cellular membrane system can be inferred.

Histological analysis and temperature effects

Although confocal Z‐stacks provided high‐resolution, 3D cellular imaging, histology served as an independent validation method to confirm that the nuclear and chloroplast changes that we observed were not artifacts of the imaging technique. This additional layer of analysis was used as a morphological control to ensure biological fidelity in interpreting fluorescent signal loss.

Staining with DAPI was used to label nuclei and microbiome components, allowing for clear visualization of cellular structures and stress‐induced morphological changes. At 13°C, samples showed numerous intact nuclei and a sparse microbiome. At 25°C, nuclear presence declined dramatically, with over 50% of cells showing a loss of nuclear signal. At 27°C, we observed a complete loss of nuclear fluorescence along with a markedly intensified microbiome signal.

That the fluorescent nuclear signal was lost along with the mislocation and fragmentation of the chloroplasts is puzzling and potentially unexpected at first given the gametophyte cell structure (Maier et al., 1997). Deeper literature analysis, however, revealed that in brown algae the chloroplasts are surrounded by two envelopes, the outer of which in some cases is continuous with the nuclear envelope (Bouke, 1965). Similar ultrastructure observations of Bisalputra and Bisalputra (1967) pointed to the possibility that this morphological peculiarity might in fact underlie the concordance of cell fates between chloroplasts and nuclei when they are re‐localized or, possibly, mislocalized and fragmented. Electron microscopy analyses of normal and heat‐compromised gametophytes will be required to test this prediction.

Evolutionary history and heat tolerance

Our analysis indicated that kelp gametophytes rapidly lose chloroplast nuclear integrity at temperatures exceeding 25°C, with pronounced deterioration evident within 1 week of heat‐stress exposure. This acute sensitivity aligned with broader patterns of thermal limitation across Laminariales: Recruitment failure of microscopic stages has been documented at temperatures as low as 18°C in several kelp taxa (Muth et al., 2019), and embryonic sporophyte production in Macrocystis pyrifera similarly ceases at 18°C (Matson & Edwards, 2007). Together, these findings underscore the extreme vulnerability of early life‐history stages to warming, even warming well below the thermal limits tolerated by adult sporophytes.

Although the present study did not resolve the mechanistic basis of this limited thermal tolerance, that basis may be informed by the evolutionary history of brown algae. Large, morphologically complex kelps evolved relatively recently from unicellular ancestors (Starko et al., 2021), and their cellular organization reflects a secondary endosymbiotic origin in which chloroplasts inherited from Rhodophyta function within a host genome of stramenopile ancestry.

Brown algal chloroplasts retain a reduced genome of approximately 120 genes and depend extensively on nuclear‐encoded proteins for maintenance, regulation, and inheritance. These nuclear genes were acquired through ancient gene transfer events and subsequently integrated into stramenopile genomic systems that had evolved independently for nearly a billion years (Lipinska et al., 2019). Although this integration was clearly successful, it may impose constraints on how plastid and nuclear functions are coordinated under environmental stress.

In photosynthetic eukaryotes, chloroplasts actively sense temperature and communicate stress signals to the nucleus through retrograde signaling pathways that regulate gene expression and promote cellular survival (Watson et al., 2018). The coordinated loss of chloroplast organization and nuclear fluorescent signal observed in heat‐stressed kelp gametophytes is consistent with the disruption of tightly coupled plastid–nuclear processes. Furthermore, ROS play a central role in chloroplast‐mediated stress signaling, and kelps rely on iodine‐based antioxidant systems rather than on the pathways common in land plants and animals (Tymon et al., 2017). Together, these features suggest that the evolutionary configuration of plastid–host interactions in kelps may shape their cellular responses to elevated temperature. Although these interpretations remain speculative, they provide an evolutionary and cellular context for our observations and highlight the need for future functional studies targeting plastid–nuclear coordination, temperature‐responsive pathways, and chloroplast‐maintenance mechanisms in kelp.

CONCLUSIONS

In this study, Macrocystis pyrifera gametophytes exhibited physiological and cellular deterioration at temperatures of 25°C and above. Elevated temperature was associated with a decline in chlorophyll fluorescence, mislocalization and fragmentation of chloroplasts, and progressive loss of nuclear fluorescent signal. Histological analyses confirmed that the loss of nuclear fluorescence reflected underlying cellular degradation rather than imaging artifacts. Heat‐stressed gametophytes also exhibited increased abundances of associated microbial communities at 25 and 27°C, coincident with organelle disruption and loss of cellular integrity. Cellular responses varied consistently among genotypes, with heat‐resilient genotypes retaining greater chloroplast organization and nuclear integrity than heat‐sensitive genotypes under identical thermal conditions.
