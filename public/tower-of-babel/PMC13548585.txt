Spathodea campanulata extract as an endodontic irrigant against Enterococcus faecalis: an in vitro and ex vivo study
Authors: Jorge Miguel Santacruz-Monzon, Rubén Abraham Domínguez-Pérez, Bertha Fenton-Navarro
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo evaluate the antibacterial activity of leaf extracts derived from Spathodea campanulata as an endodontic irrigant in both in vitro and ex vivo models against Enterococcus faecalis.Materials and methodsAqueous (AQESC), methanolic (MESC), ethanolic (EESC), acetonic (AESC), chloroformic (CESC), and hexanic (HESC) extracts derived from Spathodea campanulata were prepared. The minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) and minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC) were determined. These teeth were subsequently divided into three experimental groups, each receiving positive pressure irrigation with 1 mL of one of the following solutions: phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), 2.5% sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl), and the extract from Spathodea campanulata exhibiting the most favorable MIC, MBC, and inhibition halo in prior tests. Conversely, EESC and AESC at this concentration exhibited bactericidal activity, with no statistically significant difference between them, when compared to 2.5% NaOCl. EESC at 800 mg/mL displayed the most superior antibacterial properties; consequently, this concentration was utilized in the ex vivo model. The analysis of CFU/mL revealed a significant reduction in the EESC group compared to 2.5% NaOCl (P < 0.0001). The extracts are abundant in flavonoids, phenols, polyphenols, and tannins, which may account for the observed results.ConclusionThe extracts of Spathodea campanulata exhibit bacteriostatic and bactericidal properties at low concentrations, with the exception of AQESC. The inhibition halo against Enterococcus faecalis at 800 mg/mL of EESC and AESC is comparable to that produced by 2.5% NaOCl. Introduction

Sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl) is the most commonly employed irrigant in root canal therapy due to its antimicrobial efficacy; the predominant concentration used is 2.5% [1]. However, it functions as a nonspecific agent, and its effects are not confined to microorganisms; it may induce cytotoxicity if inadvertently extruded into the periradicular tissue. Such extrusions frequently result in inflammation, hemorrhage—with the potential formation of facial hematomas—and discomfort. Less frequently, it may lead to localized paresthesia, necrosis, trismus, dyspnea, dysphagia, and even airway obstruction [2]. Beyond adverse effects, a 2.5% NaOCl solution generally fails to eradicate all microorganisms during endodontic procedures, thereby potentially contributing to periradicular exacerbation or apical reinfection [1]. Enterococcus faecalis, a Gram-positive, anaerobic bacterium, accounts for approximately 74% of primary and refractory endodontic infections, indicating its capacity to survive endodontic treatment. This resilience is associated with its multiple virulence factors. Therefore, the development of novel therapeutic strategies to effectively target this microorganism remains imperative [3].

Medicinal plants are complex organisms whose secondary metabolites can interact with other organisms. They have been used to treat infections and other health issues [4]. Spathodea campanulata P. Beauv., commonly known as the African tulip, has multiple therapeutic properties, including antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, analgesic, and antipyretic effects [5]. The methanolic, ethanolic, and hexanic extracts of the bark, as well as the methanolic extract of the flower, have demonstrated antimicrobial effects on fungi and both gram-negative and gram-positive bacteria, especially on Enterococcus faecalis [6, 7].

There are no reports regarding the use of Spathodea campanulata leaf extract as an endodontic irrigant; consequently, the aim of this study was to prepare various extracts of Spathodea campanulata and assess their minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC), minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC), and inhibition halos against Enterococcus faecalis, in order to identify the extract with the most superior antibacterial properties for application in a four-week ex vivo biofilm model.

Material and method

This study obtained approval from the institutional ethics committee (FCMB-UMSNH 002/P/05/2024, UMSNH-CEIIB/451–2025) and is documented in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Laboratory Studies in Endodontology (PRILE) 2021 standards [8].

Plant collection and extracts preparation

Leaves of Spathodea campanulata were collected in Morelia, Michoacán, Mexico. The voucher specimen (MEXU 1489469) was deposited in the Herbarium of the Institute of Biology at the National Autonomous University of Mexico. Leaves were disinfected using 1% NaOCl and rinsed with distilled water, then dried in a low-temperature oven. The dried leaves were ground using a manual mill. One gram of the powdered material was soaked with 10 mL of solvent (water, methanol, ethanol, acetone, chloroform, or hexane) for 24 h. The extracts were homogenized, centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 25 min, and filtered through a grade 1 paper filter. The solvents were evaporated by simple evaporation to obtain the extracts. Subsequently, the extracts were resuspended in 1 mL of Tween 20 and vortex-mixed until homogeneous. These solutions were designated as aqueous (AQESC), methanolic (MESC), ethanolic (EESC), acetonic (AESC), chloroformic (CESC), and hexanic (HESC) extracts of Spathodea campanulata based solely on their solvent of origin; however, at this stage, none of the original solvents remained. Finally, the solutions were stored at 4 °C in amber bottles until use.

Determination of Minimum Inhibitory Concentration (MIC) and Minimum Bactericidal Concentration (MBC)

Enterococcus faecalis ATCC 29212 was cultivated on Mueller-Hinton agar (MH-agar) and subsequently inoculated into Mueller-Hinton broth (MH-broth). The incubation was carried out overnight at 37 °C. The culture was calibrated via spectrophotometry to attain an optical density of 0.08–0.1 at 620 nm, which corresponds to 10^8 colony-forming units (CFU) of Enterococcus faecalis. The MICs were ascertained through microdilution assays employing a modified method. The following controls were included: negative controls, blank: MH-broth without bacteria; Campanulata blank: Spathodea campanulata extract + MH-broth; and either one of the following AQESC, MESC, EESC, AESC, CESC, or HESC without bacteria. The positive control consisted of: MH-broth combined with 2.5% NaOCl and bacteria. Test controls comprised: AQESC, MESC, EESC, AESC, CESC, or HESC (800 mg/mL) in conjunction with MH-broth and bacteria. Samples were allocated into a sterile, flat-bottomed 96-well microtiter plate (12 × 8) and subjected to serial 2-fold dilutions in MH-broth, progressing up to well 12. Incubation was carried out at 37 °C, with absorbance measurements taken using a microplate reader (Thermo Scientific®) at 620 nm at the following time points: 0, 1, 2, 5, 10, 30, and 60 min, as well as at 2 and 24 h. The early readings (up to 2 h) were collected exclusively to evaluate initial bacterial growth dynamics. Following standard microdilution guidelines, the MIC was strictly defined as the lowest concentration that fully inhibited visible bacterial growth at the 24 h endopoint. Consequently, any transient reductions or fluctuations in absorbance observed during the early time points did not influence the final MIC determination, as persistence of inhibition at 24 h was the sole definitive criterion. The MBC was ascertained from wells exhibiting no turbidity on MH-agar after 24 h of incubation [7]. All assays were conducted in triplicate and independently repeated on three occasions. Gram staining was performed to confirm the presence of Enterococcus faecalis.

Antibacterial activity

The antimicrobial activity was assessed utilizing a modified well diffusion assay [9]. Sterile wells, each 6 mm in diameter, were drilled into petri dishes containing MH-agar and subsequently filled with 50 µL of the following solutions: solvents of Spathodea campanulata extracts (serving as negative controls), 2.5% NaOCl as a positive control, and the extracts (AQESC, MESC, EESC, AESC, CESC, or HESC) at a concentration of 800 mg/mL. Standardized Enterococcus faecalis MH-broth was aseptically swabbed onto MH-agar plates and incubated at 37 °C for 18 h. The resulting inhibition halos were measured using a vernier caliper under reflected light. The procedure was conducted in triplicate and independently repeated twice. Gram staining was performed to verify the presence of Enterococcus faecalis.

Ex vivo model

Following a previous report [10], sixty single-rooted human teeth with fully developed apices were extracted for orthodontic or periodontal reasons. The teeth were cleansed of surface debris, calculus, and tissue, and subsequently stored in 0.01% NaOCl to prevent dehydration prior to use. The teeth were sectioned below the cementoenamel junction using a diamond disc to obtain a standardized length of 10 mm, thereby ensuring a consistent sample. The root canals were then prepared employing the crown-down technique with K-files (Dentsply Maillefer, Ballaigues, Switzerland). During the cleaning and shaping process, one milliliter of 5.25% NaOCl was used to irrigate between each instrument. The canals were enlarged to an apical size of #60. A Gates Glidden bur #6 (Dentsply Maillefer, Ballaigues, Switzerland) was inserted to a depth of 5 mm into the canal, with the entry standardized in a buccolingual/palatal direction, resulting in an oval-shaped preparation measuring 4 mm in length and 2 mm in width. The root canals were rinsed with distilled water for 10 min, followed by irrigation with 1 mL of 17% Ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid, and a final rinse with distilled water for 1 min.

Each tooth was randomly assigned to one of three experimental groups, with 20 teeth per group: PBS, 2.5% NaOCl, and Spathodea campanulata extract (800 mg/mL), which demonstrated the most effective antibacterial properties in prior assays. The exterior surface of each specimen was coated with nail polish, and the specimens were subsequently placed in a container containing 50 mL of MH-broth. The container was then sterilized for 30 min at 121 °C and incubated for 48 h at 37 °C to verify the absence of turbidity. They were inoculated with 1 µL of standardized Enterococcus faecalis MH-broth and incubated at 37 °C for a period of four weeks. The MH-broth was replaced weekly to prevent nutrient depletion and the accumulation of toxic by-products. At the conclusion of the fourth week, all groups underwent external disinfection with gauze soaked in 1% NaOCl. A sterile cotton swab was then applied at the canal entrance and exit to absorb MH-broth without contacting the canal interior. The teeth were positioned vertically on a wax sheet, ensuring contact at the canal exit, to simulate periradicular tissues and prevent the leakage of irrigating solutions. Each root canal was irrigated under positive pressure with 1 mL of the respective experimental solution for one minute with a fine metallic needle (27 G x 13 mm) inserted to a depth of 7 mm. The canal was then rinsed with 20 mL of sterile distilled water and dried at room temperature. The wax was removed from the teeth, and each root canal was rinsed with 0.5 mL of PBS). The PBS was collected through the apex into a sterile microtube. Subsequently, each root canal was instrumented with a #70 K-file (Dentsply Maillefer, Ballaigues, Switzerland), which was then placed into the same microtube with PBS to collect dentin powder. The rinsing and collection procedure was repeated. The microtube was shaken to dislodge the dentin shavings from the file, and a brief interval was allowed for the dentin powder to settle. To determine the number of viable bacteria following each treatment, tenfold serial dilutions were prepared. From these dilutions, 10 µL samples were taken and plated on MH-agar plates. The plates were spread with a sterile swab and incubated for 72 h at 37 °C. CFU/mL were subsequently counted, and Gram staining was performed to confirm the exclusive presence of Enterococcus faecalis.

Total secondary metabolites quantification in the extracts

The Folin-Ciocalteu assay was utilized to determine the total phenolic and polyphenol content in plant extracts. This methodology is based on the reaction between phenolic compounds and the Folin-Ciocalteu reagent. A standard curve derived from gallic acid was employed to interpolate the results obtained from the samples. Outcomes are expressed as gallic acid equivalents (GAE) per gram of tissue [11]. For polyphenol, a portion of the sample was treated with polyvinylpolypyrrolidone (PVPP), which selectively binds to polyphenols in an acidic environment. The difference in absorbance at 760 nm between the treated and untreated samples indicates the polyphenol concentration. A standard curve based on phloroglucinol was utilized, and the resulting concentrations were expressed as milligrams of phloroglucinol equivalents per gram of extract. Similarly, for tannin measurement, the same methodology was applied. Samples were read at 750 nm, and the values were interpolated onto a tannic acid standard curve (Sigma-Aldrich). Results were reported as tannin content (mg/g tissue) [12]. Quantification of flavonoids was conducted using the Dowd method, which employs aluminum trichloride (AlCl3) to form stable complexes with flavonoids. These complexes are measurable spectrophotometrically at a wavelength of 715 nm, allowing for the determination of flavonoid concentration within the extracts. The absorbance values from the test samples were interpolated onto a quercetin standard curve. The total flavonoid content was expressed as milligrams of quercetin equivalents per gram of extract [13, 14].

Statistical analysis

Results were presented as mean ± standard deviation. Statistical significance among multiple groups was assessed as follows: for the well diffusion test and ex vivo model data, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was employed; for the total flavonoids, phenols, polyphenols, and tannins content data, two-way ANOVA was utilized. In both scenarios, a post hoc Tukey’s multiple comparisons test was conducted to identify statistically significant differences among groups, using GraphPad Prism 9.3.0. Differences were regarded as significant if P ≤ 0.05.

Results

The MIC and MBC were defined as the lowest concentrations of solutions required to inhibit growth and eradicate Enterococcus faecalis, respectively, after 24 h of exposure. AQESC exhibited no activity against MIC and MBC, whereas the other extracts demonstrated MIC and MBC values ranging from 12.5 to 50 µg/mL (Table 1). AQESC, MESC, CESC, and HESC did not produce an inhibition halo at a concentration of 800 mg/mL. Conversely, EESC and AESC, at the same concentration, yielded positive results and did not differ significantly from 2.5% NaOCl (P = 0.1950 and > 0.9999, respectively). These extracts showed no statistically significant difference from each other (P = 0.1950) (Table 2). The solvents used for the extracts did not generate an inhibition halo.

Discussion

In this study, various Spathodea campanulata leaf extracts were evaluated for the first time utilizing in vitro and ex vivo models against Enterococcus faecalis ATCC 29,212 to determine their antibacterial efficacy. MESC and HESC exhibited lower MIC and MBC values than previously reported, likely because the extracts were derived from leaves, whereas other studies used flowers and bark [6, 7]. Meanwhile, the MIC and MBC values for NaOCl aligned with historical data [15]. The well diffusion assay was selected to facilitate a rapid and straightforward comparison of bacterial susceptibility across the most effective concentrations of the experimental solutions [16]. This technique is highly recommended for diffusion testing involving medicinal plant extracts [9] and the observable halo for NaOCl concurs with prior reports [17].

The solvents employed in plant extraction dissolve distinct secondary metabolites owing to their inherent physicochemical properties, particularly polarity [18]. Notably, the phenol and flavonoid contents observed in the AQESC and MESC samples surpassed those previously reported [19]. Furthermore, the phenol content in MESC exceeded documented levels [20], while the quantities of flavonoids, phenols, polyphenols, and tannins in the EESC and CESC were greater than those registered by other studies [21]. These variations may stem from differences in plant phenology across geographical regions, solvent concentrations, extraction methodologies, storage conditions, and extract preparation protocols [20, 21]. Ethanol and acetone have been demonstrated to extract higher yields of bioactive antimicrobial compounds than other solvents, typically resulting in enhanced efficacy. Moreover, their low boiling points and high volatility relative to aqueous extracts facilitate solvent removal and help prevent the degradation of thermolabile active constituents, as they are exposed to heat for shorter durations during evaporation [22].

The elevated levels of secondary metabolites (Table 4) have been associated with various health benefits and may elucidate the antibacterial effects observed herein [5]. Although specific phytochemicals –such as spathoside, 3-0-β-D-sitosterol glucopyranoside, sitosterol esters, oleanolic acid, pomolic acid, parahydroxybenzoic acid, and phenylethanol esters- have been isolated from the bark of Spathodea campanulata and shown to possess bacteriostatic properties against Enterococcus faecalis [6], the overall antibacterial activity may also be attributable to other secondary metabolites, primarily polyphenols. These constitute the largest class of secondary metabolites in medicinal plants, typically featuring at least one aromatic ring and one or more hydroxyl (OH) groups. Depending on the number of aromatic rings and attached radicals, polyphenols are categorized into phenolic acids, flavonoids (the predominant subclass of polyphenols, representing nearly 50% of all compounds), stilbenes, tannins, coumarins, and lignans [23].

This extensive group of metabolites displays antibacterial activity primarily determined by the quantity, type, and location of functional groups attached to their aromatic rings in their chemical structures. The bactericidal mechanism involves the interaction of these molecules or their byproducts with the bacterial cell wall and membrane, leading to structural rupture, synthesis inhibition, or lipid peroxidation, ultimately culminating in cell death. Conversely, their bacteriostatic mechanism includes the inhibition of quorum sensing and the prevention of biofilm formation through enzyme antagonism. Moreover, these compounds can inhibit bacterial enzymes and toxins, block accessory regulatory genes essential for transcription, or directly disrupt enzymatic activity. Ultimately, they may impair bacterial nucleic acid synthesis, often by antagonizing the B subunit of DNA gyrase [24].

Ex vivo models of bacterial biofilms are primarily employed to evaluate the efficacy of antimicrobial agents or treatment strategies in endodontic therapy, particularly for endodontic irrigants [16]. Utilizing biofilms in such assays provides several advantages over planktonic bacteria, notably an increased resistance to antimicrobials; furthermore, the age of the biofilm (incubation duration) significantly dictates antimicrobial susceptibility [25]. This endodontic biofilm model accurately replicates clinical scenarios, offering both viability and reproducibility. The Enterococcus faecalis monospecies biofilm model is frequently used to assess specific endodontic irrigants prior to progressing to complex models [25]. Furthermore, this bacterium is straightforward to handle in laboratory settings due to its tolerance to a broad spectrum of growth conditions [16]. Nevertheless, mono-species biofilm presents the limitation of potentially overestimating antibacterial effects relative to the multi-species environments encountered in clinical cases [25].

This investigation marks the initial application of the standardized treatment methodology within an ex vivo model of extracted single-rooted teeth. The findings observed in the PBS- and 2.5% NaOCl-irrigated groups align with those documented by authors employing a similar methodology [26]. Because EESC and AESC exhibited the most favorable antibacterial properties in vitro and considering that ethanol functions as a safe, non-toxic, universal solvent capable of extracting both polar and nonpolar compounds [27], EESC was selected for the ex vivo model. The concentration of EESC was established based on previous literature [6, 7, 28] and has not been associated with adverse effects when orally administered to rats [28].

Interestingly, 2.5% NaOCl was significantly less effective than EESC in eliminating Enterococcus faecalis biofilm (P < 0. 0001). This outcome can be attributed to the interaction of NaOCl with the extracellular matrix produced by bacteria over four weeks, which hinders penetration and alters susceptibility. Furthermore, dentin has been shown to reduce the antimicrobial efficacy of NaOCl [25]. This is highly relevant because as the Enterococcus faecalis biofilm develops within the root canal over four weeks, bacterial interaction with dentin results in carbonate and phosphate sediments due to bacterial interaction with dentin. This process occurs through the dissolution of the tissue surface, thereby encapsulating bacteria beneath these sediments and making them harder to eradicate [29]. While the exact mechanism of action of EESC specifically against a mature four- week biofilm remains unknown, the effects of 500 synthetic and natural flavonoids, a polyphenol subgroup, have been documented against 18- hour Staphylococcus aureus biofilms. These flavonoids, based on their antibiofilm activity, have been classified as inactive, moderately active, or highly active, with the highly active exhibiting a significant reduction in biofilm formation [30]. Given that EESC is an extract rich in flavonoids (Table 4), its antibiofilm effect could be directly linked to these polyphenols, though further research is required to confirm this association in both cases.

At present, natural products are gaining interest due to their recognized efficacy against various diseases and their relatively low incidence of side effects [4]. EESC emerges as a promising novel irrigant, owing to its reported anti-inflammatory, analgesic, and antimicrobial properties [5], especially its potent antibacterial effectiveness against Enterococcus faecalis, as demonstrated in this study. EESC possesses features that are not yet available in current commercial endodontic irrigants; however, due to its chemical characteristics, it lacks the tissue-dissolving properties of NaOCl, which remains a crucial advantage in conventional endodontic therapy. Further research is warranted to enhance the understanding of EESC as a complementary therapy to traditional root canal procedures.

Conclusion

Extracts of Spathodea campanulata demonstrate both bacteriostatic and bactericidal activities at low concentrations, with the exception of AQESC. The inhibition halos caused by EESC and AESC against Enterococcus faecalis at 800 mg/mL are comparable to those produced by 2.5% NaOCl. Endodontic irrigation utilizing EESC significantly reduces the CFU/mL of Enterococcus faecalis in comparison to 2.5% NaOCl.
