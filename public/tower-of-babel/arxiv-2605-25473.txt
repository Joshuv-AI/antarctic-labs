The Diagrammar of Quantum Magnusian Preprint: CALT-TH-2026-020 CERN-TH-2026-115 KIAS-P26031
Li Guo, Joon-Hwi Kim, Jung-Wook Kim, Sungsoo Kim, Sangmin Lee, Jian-Rong Li

Abstract
The logarithm of the time-evolution operator has been termed Magnusian, on account of the fact that its expansion describes the Magnus series. The diagrammatic expansion and computation of the classical Magnusian has been completely established in terms of tree graphs and their Hopf algebra. Recent works initiated extensions into quantum field theory, revealing general structures of loop expansions while finding intriguing relations between different diagrams. In this work, we advance the loop expansion further by providing an efficient diagrammatic algorithm to calculate the weight factor of each graph in the quantum Magnusian, known as the Murua coefficient. This is achieved by incorporating two complementary perspectives on the Magnusian at the same time: the color basis and the black-and-white basis. We extract the Murua coefficients from the Magnus series by utilizing these two bases while implementing an exponentiated Wick contraction. In turn, we identify the loop-level extension of Murua’s recursive formula. Eventually, we establish a set of edge contraction rules which facilitate a direct recursive computation of the Murua coefficients at the purely diagrammatic level, without referencing or directly manipulating the underlying Magnus expansion. This shows that the matrix elements of the quantum Magnusian can be computed from graph manipulations alone.



1
Introduction

The time-evolution operator of a closed quantum system
is represented by a unitary matrix.
In the scattering setup,
the
S
-matrix is the unitary operator
encapsulating the time evolution
from far past to far future.
The unitarity of the
S
-matrix
can be made explicit by writing it as an exponential of a Hermitian matrix
\chi
:
S=e^{{}\chi/i\hbar}
.
This exponential representation
appeared in the early days of quantum field theory (QFT)
31

but was largely forgotten until recently
15
;
14
.
This recent revival was motivated by
modern applications of QFT techniques to gravitational-wave physics,
a field that has enjoyed exponential growth since the first direct detection of gravitational waves
1
.
The exponential representation has been shown to
simplify and clarify the classical limit of the
S
-matrix
and its use.

Most computations in QFT are performed in perturbation theory,
where
the
Dyson series
establishes the Feynman diagram expansion of the
S
-matrix.
The importance of diagrammatic tools for QFT computations cannot be overstated.
Historically, Feynman diagrams served as the very tool that
empowered Feynman to reproduce
Slotnick’s six-months-long calculation
within a single evening
17
.
Besides the computational power,
diagrams serve as languages that can be easily accessible to a large audience.
The current
status of QFT—a universal tool for describing physics from the subnuclear scale of colliders to the gigaparsec scale of cosmic correlations—is partly indebted to the accessibility of calculations provided by the diagrammatic expansions.

While textbook expositions
portray Feynman diagrams
as a derived construct from the Dyson series,
it is also possible to revert the point of view, in fact.
In the celebrated paper titled
“Diagrammar”
37
,
’t Hooft and Veltman raise the following question:
“Can QFT be formulated directly as an algebra of Feynman diagrams, with unitarity, causality, gauge invariance, renormalization, and field redefinitions treated as diagrammatic identities?”
In this view,
perturbative QFT is practically an algebra of two-point functions
as per Wick’s theorem.
It makes diagrams the starting point,
and then derives the needed formal properties from them.
It takes Feynman diagrams seriously as the primary language of perturbative QFT,
rather than merely as a mnemonic derived from the canonical formalism or the path integral formalism.

Considering this diagrammar discourse on the QFT
S
-matrix,
it is natural to question whether an analogous construction
exists for the logarithm of the
S
-matrix,
\chi={}i\hbar\log\hskip-1.00006ptS
.

At the
tree level
,
this question was answered in Ref.
21
;
see also Refs.
13
;
18
;
23
for related preceding works.
Ref.
21
pointed out that
the logarithm of the
S
-matrix
is computed by the
Magnus series

32
,
which describes a characteristic nested bracket (commutator) structure
unlike the Dyson series.
By building upon this observation,
it was shown that
the Magnus series
systematically derives
the diagrammatic rules for the classical limit of
\chi
in terms of directed tree graphs.
A graph function
\omega(\tau)
,
known as the Murua coefficient
34
,
assigns the correct weight when converting each directed tree graph
\tau
to its corresponding integrand.
The Murua coefficient
\omega(\tau)
satisfies nontrivial relations referred to as
edge contraction rules
and
sum rules
.
Crucially,
these nontrivial relations can be used to “bootstrap” the values of
\omega(\tau)
purely at the diagrammatic level,
precisely implementing the diagrammar philosophy.
To clarify,
this means that
\omega(\tau)
can be computed
without
making references to or
directly expanding out
the Magnus series.

Through further developments in Refs.
27
;
2
;
28
;
26
;
24
,
it became clear that
this tree-level diagrammar
applies to both particles and fields,
incorporating conservative as well as dissipative dynamics.
It was also realized that
it universally applies to
not only
\chi={}i\hbar\log\hskip-1.00006ptS

but also the logarithm of
any unitary time-evolution operator.
To emphasize this generality,
we reserve the term
Magnusian
.
This term was first coined in Ref.
26

for referring to the logarithm of unitary time-evolution operators in quantum mechanics.

The diagrammatic exploration of
the
loop-level
Magnusian
was initiated in Refs.
9
;
25
,
offering complementary viewpoints on the same problem.

In Ref.
9
,
it was shown that the Murua coefficients
\omega(G)

for generic graphs
G

satisfy a nontrivial relation:
the
cutting rule
which relates diagrams that can be converted to the other by removing an edge. This relation alone, however, was not enough to uniquely determine the loop-level Murua coefficients at the diagrammatic level.
As a result, Ref.
9
’s
determination of
\omega(G)

still required directly manipulating and expanding out the Magnus series
(in terms of a useful reorganized formula),
unpacking nested commutators into operator products.
An explicit demonstration of this computation
was provided up to one-loop in
\phi^{3}
theory,
together with a discussion on higher loop orders.

In Ref.
25
,
it was shown that the frameworks of
phase space formulation
33
;
19
;
36

and deformation quantization
3
;
29

lead to a principled approach to the quantum Magnusian to all loop orders.
A concrete operational definition of the Murua coefficients
\omega(G)
for all graphs
G

was established
by utilizing the Moyal/Wick star product formalisms.
This formulation emphasizes and preserves
the key feature
of the Magnus series,
i.e., the nested bracket structure.
The computation of
\omega(G)

was demonstrated to all loops
at three vertices
with arbitrarily high degrees.
It was observed that
the tree-level coefficients
are recycled into a large portion of the loop-level coefficients,
leading to an idea dubbed fuzzification.

An approach that directly references and manipulates the Magnus series
could be called
a “first principles” approach.
In contrast,
the diagrammar program pursues
a “bootstrap” approach
that seeks a purely graphical formulation and computation
of the Magnusian.
Although the former mode of computation may be efficient enough in practice,
it is still desirable to
establish the latter way of solving
and understanding the problem:

What is the
diagrammar of Magnusian
?

In this work,
we present a complete diagrammar for the quantum Magnusian.
To reiterate,
the major difference from the previous works
9
;
25

is that we focus on
the relations between Murua coefficients of graphs

implied
by the Magnus series, instead of the Magnus series itself.

We utilize two distinct bases for
diagrammatically representing the Magnusian:
color and black-and-white (BW).
These bases correspond to different choices of propagators (as two-point functions) for expressing the integrand.

Firstly,
the color basis considered in Ref.
25
transparently encodes the algebraic structure of the Magnus series.
Consequently, the derivation of edge contraction rules is relatively straightforward.
Another advantage of this basis is that consistency of operator ordering imposes further constraints on the Murua coefficients,
which provide enough information for computing the coefficients without directly expanding the Magnus series.

Secondly,
the BW basis considered in Ref.
9
manifests Lorentz invariance of the Magnusian,
which is obscured in the color basis.
The Hermiticity of the Magnusian also holds term by term in the BW basis,
while it holds via complex conjugation in the color basis.
Despite these differences,
both bases encode the same information and it is possible to switch between bases.

The main technical novelty of this paper is the loop-level extension of the Murua formula
34
,
which is a recursive algorithm to extract Murua coefficients from the Magnus recursion formula
32

without actually evaluating the nested propagators.
The original Murua formula was restricted to rooted tree graphs.
It was then extended to all tree graphs in Ref.
21
.
This paper extends it to all loop graphs.
As an important corollary of the Murua formula, we derive edge contraction rules
in both the color basis and the BW basis.
In practice, the contraction rules offer the fastest way to produce Murua coefficients.

The main body of this paper is divided into two sections.
In Sec.
2
, we spell out the diagrammar of the quantum Magnusian.
The exposition itself may overlap with the two previous papers
9
;
25

to an extent.
The goal is
to establish our notations and connect the two complementary viewpoints of Refs.
9
;
25
,
which we can utilize in computing the Murua coefficients.
One novelty here is that we extend the notion of “fuzzy propagator” of Ref.
25

to the BW basis of Ref.
9
. The fuzzy propagator handles all “banana loop” graphs
and offers further insights on loop graphs.
In Sec.
3
, we extend the Murua formula to loop graphs in the two bases.
The formula leads to the edge contraction rules
which turn to an efficient algorithm to compute the Murua coefficients for all loop graphs.
A summary of the main results of Sec.
2
and Sec.
3
can be found in Sec.
4
.
Finally, we conclude the paper in Sec.
5

with a brief discussion on future directions.

We provide a set of ancillary files in the companion repository
Quantum Magnusian
, where readers can look up the Murua coefficients and explore relations among graphs.

2
Quantum Magnusian

We begin by quickly reminding ourselves of
the basics of Magnus expansion
32
.

2.1
Review of Magnus Series

The Magnus expansion concerns
a class of first-order differential equations
of the form

\displaystyle\dot{Y}(t)\,=\,h\,A(t)\,Y(t)\,,\quad Y(t_{0})\,=\,Y_{0}{\quad\implies\quad}Y(t)\,=\,e^{\Omega(t)}\hskip 0.50003ptY_{0}\,,

(1)

where
A(t)
is valued in a Lie algebra,

Y(t)
is vector-valued in a representation,
and
h
is a formal parameter.
The Lie algebra can be either finite-dimensional or infinite-dimensional.
Since this describes a fairly generic setup,
the Magnus expansion finds broad applications in
diverse fields of mathematics and physics.

The original work by Magnus
32
expresses

\Omega(t)
in Eq. (
1
)
as the unique solution
to the following differential equation:

\displaystyle\dot{\Omega}(t)\,=\,\frac{\operatorname{ad}_{\Omega(t)}}{e^{\operatorname{ad}_{\Omega(t)}}-1}[\,{A(t)}\,]\,=\,A(t)\hskip 1.00006pt+\,\sum_{k\geq 1}\hskip 1.00006pt\frac{B_{k}}{k!}\hskip 1.00006pt\operatorname{ad}^{k}_{\Omega(t)}[\,{A(t)}\,]\,,\quad\Omega(t_{0})\,=\,0\,.

(2)

Here,
\operatorname{ad}^{k}
is the
k
-th iteration of
the adjoint action

\operatorname{ad}_{X}[\,{Y}\,]:=[\hskip 0.50003pt{X},{Y}\hskip 0.50003pt]
,
while
B_{n}
are the Bernoulli numbers
such that
B_{1}=-1/2
.
Explicitly, the Magnus expansion is given by

\displaystyle\Omega(t)\,=\,\sum_{n\geq 1}\,h^{n}\,\Omega_{n}(t)\,,\quad\Omega_{1}(t)\,=\int_{t_{0}}^{t}A(s)\,ds\,,

(3)

where
\Omega_{(n)}
for
n\geq 2

are obtained recursively by integrating
the following equation with the boundary condition

\Omega_{n}(t_{0})=0
:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\dot{\Omega}_{n}(t)\,=\,\sum_{k\geq 1}\,\frac{B_{k}}{k!}\,\sum_{\{r\}_{k}}\,[\hskip 0.50003pt{\Omega_{r_{k}}\hskip-1.00006pt(t)},{\cdots[\hskip 0.50003pt{\Omega_{r_{2}}\hskip-1.00006pt(t)},{[\hskip 0.50003pt{\Omega_{r_{1}}\hskip-1.00006pt(t)},{A(t)}\hskip 0.50003pt]}\hskip 0.50003pt]\cdots}\hskip 0.50003pt]\,.\end{split}

(4)

Here,
\{r\}_{k}
denotes the set of all
k
-tuples of integers satisfying

\displaystyle r_{1}+r_{2}+\cdots r_{k}\,=\,(n-1)\,,\quad r_{i}>0\,.

(5)

See, e.g.,
Refs.
8
;
16
for modern reviews on the Magnus expansion.
It is also known that
some formulas can express

\Omega_{n}(t)
directly
as a time-ordered integral of nested commutators of
A(t)

35
;
9
.
We will not need any such formulas within this paper,
although keeping its possibility in mind might help comprehending the diagrammatic expansion better.

The Magnus expansion is readily applied to quantum mechanics and QFT,
in which case one concerns the commutator Lie algebra
of quantum operators.
In a common physics notation,
the quantum Magnusian
in the interaction picture
is defined as

\displaystyle{}\frac{1}{i\hbar}\,\chi\,=\,\log\hskip-1.00006pt\bigg[\,{\mathcal{T}\exp\hskip-1.00006pt\bigg(\,{\frac{1}{i\hbar}\int H_{I}(t)dt}\,\bigg)}\,\bigg]\,,

(6)

where
\mathcal{T}
implements the time-ordered product,
and
H_{I}(t)
is a Hamiltonian operator
in the interaction picture.
1
1

1

The sign convention in Eq. (
6
) is opposite to those of Refs.
15
;
14
;
21
;
9

and follows Refs.
24
;
25
.
This change of convention removes many unimportant minus signs in formulas.

The formula in Eq. (
4
) translates to

\displaystyle\begin{split}{}\dot{\chi}_{n}(t)\,=\,\sum_{k\geq 1}\,\frac{B_{k}}{k!}\frac{1}{(i\hbar)^{k}}\,\sum_{\{r\}_{k}}\,[\hskip 0.50003pt{\chi_{r_{k}}\hskip-1.00006pt(t)},{\cdots[\hskip 0.50003pt{\chi_{r_{2}}\hskip-1.00006pt(t)},{[\hskip 0.50003pt{\chi_{r_{1}}\hskip-1.00006pt(t)},{H_{I}(t)}\hskip 0.50003pt]}\hskip 0.50003pt]\cdots}\hskip 0.50003pt]\,.\end{split}

(7)

As emphasized in Ref.
21
, the
k
-fold nested commutator is accompanied with
(i\hbar)^{k}
in the denominator, ensuring a well-defined classical limit.

For practical use
of the quantum Magnusian,
one considers the in- and out-states as

\displaystyle{\hskip 0.50003pt\big|\hskip 0.50003pt{\text{out}}\big\rangle}\,=\,\mathcal{T}\exp\hskip-1.00006pt\bigg(\,{\frac{1}{i\hbar}\int H_{I}(t)dt}\,\bigg){\hskip 0.50003pt\big|\hskip 0.50003pt{\text{in}}\big\rangle}\,=\,e^{{}\chi/i\hbar}\hskip 1.00006pt{\hskip 0.50003pt\big|\hskip 0.50003pt{\text{in}}\big\rangle}\,.

(8)

For a quantum observable
\mathcal{O}
,
it then follows that

\displaystyle\big\langle\hskip 0.50003pt{\mathcal{O}}\hskip 0.50003pt\big\rangle_{\hskip-0.40974pt\text{out}}\,=\,{\big\langle{\text{out}}\hskip 0.50003pt\big|\hskip 0.50003pt}\mathcal{O}{\hskip 0.50003pt\big|\hskip 0.50003pt{\text{out}}\big\rangle}\,=\,{\big\langle{\text{in}}\hskip 0.50003pt\big|\hskip 0.50003pt}e^{{-}\chi/i\hbar}\hskip 1.00006pt\mathcal{O}\hskip 1.00006pte^{{}\chi/i\hbar}{\hskip 0.50003pt\big|\hskip 0.50003pt{\text{in}}\big\rangle}\,=\,\big\langle\hskip 0.50003pt{e^{{{-}\hskip-0.81949pt\ad_{\chi/i\hbar}}}\hskip 1.00006pt\mathcal{O}}\hskip 0.50003pt\big\rangle_{\text{in}}\,.

(9)

Again, the classical limit of Eq. (
9
)
is well-defined
22
;
21
.
It leads to the so-called nested bracket formula
22
;
18

for computing the impulse of observables.

The main goal of this paper is to develop
a diagrammatic expansion of the quantum Magnusian,
generalizing the tree result of Ref.
21

as well as
the earlier works
9
;
25

on the loop case.

2.2
Diagrammatic Expansion

The diagrammatic expansion of the quantum Magnusian takes the schematic form,

\displaystyle{}\chi\,\,\,\sim\,\,\,\sum_{G}\,\frac{\omega(G)}{\sigma(G)}\,\hbar^{L(G)}\,\mathcal{I}(G)\,.

(10)

We shall explain all symbols entering the formula and fix the normalization factors, reviewing and
combining similar definitions in Refs.
9
;
25
.

The Magnusian is well-defined for any quantum system
25
, but in most parts of this paper, we will
presume a scalar relativistic QFT as in Ref.
9
.

Graphs

The sum in Eq. (
10
) runs over a set of graphs, which are similar but not identical to Feynman graphs.
Before describing the details, we define some basic quantities common to all graph expansions.
For a graph
G
, we define

\displaystyle\begin{split}V(G)&\,=\,\mbox{(number of vertices of $G$)}\,,\\
E(G)&\,=\,\mbox{(number of edges of $G$)}\,,\\
L(G)&\,=\,\mbox{(number of loops of $G$)}\,.\end{split}

(11)

They are subject to a relation

\displaystyle E\,=\,L+V-1\,.

(12)

For comparison with Ref.
21
, we will sometimes write
|G|
to mean
V(G)
.
Tree graphs (
L=0
) will often be denoted by
\tau
as in Ref.
21
.
As is customary in the QFT literature, we will use
\hbar
as a formal loop counting parameter,
as stated explicitly in Eq. (
10
).
The symmetry factor
\sigma(G)
is integer-valued, on which we will elaborate shortly.

The graph function
\omega(G)
takes values in rational numbers.
It was first introduced by Murua
34
for rooted trees and
extended to non-rooted trees in Ref.
21
.
Some examples of
\omega(G)
for loop graphs were given in Refs.
9
;
25
.
We will generalize it to arbitrary loop graphs. How to compute
\omega(G)
is the main technical novelty of this paper.

Edges: Color vs. BW

The edges of our graphs denote propagators.
We will use two distinct representations of edges:
the color basis of Ref.
25

and
the BW basis of Ref.
9
.
In the color basis, the edges are all directed and can be colored either red or blue.
In the BW basis, the black edges are directed whereas the white (dotted gray for visualization) edges are undirected.
The directions indicate time ordering.
The black and white corresponds to retarded and cut propagators, respectively.
We will specify the precise definitions of all propagators in Sec.
2.4
.
Fig.
1
shows our graphical notation for the edges.

Figure 1:
Edges in the color basis (left) and the BW basis (right).

The number of edges splits into two types in each basis.

\displaystyle\mbox{(Color):}\;\;E\,=\,E_{\text{red}}+E_{\text{blue}}\,,\qquad\mbox{(BW):}\;\;E\,=\,E_{\text{ret}}+E_{\text{cut}}\,.

(13)

The schematic formula in Eq. (
10
) turns into specific formulas in the two bases:

\displaystyle{}\chi\,

\displaystyle=\,\sum_{G_{c}}\,\frac{\omega_{c}(G_{c})}{\sigma_{c}(G_{c})}\,\hbar^{L(G_{c})}\,i^{V(G_{c})-1}\,\mathcal{I}(G_{c})

(14a)

\displaystyle=\,\sum_{G_{\text{bw}}}\,\frac{\omega_{\text{bw}}(G_{\text{bw}})}{\sigma_{\text{bw}}(G_{\text{bw}})}\,\hbar^{L(G_{\text{bw}})}\,\mathcal{I}(G_{\text{bw}})\,.

(14b)

In the color basis, separating the overall factor of
i^{V-1}
—which originates from Eq. (
7
)—will turn out to be useful when
discussing the Hermiticity of the sum.
In the BW basis, the factor
i^{V-1}
has been absorbed into the definition of the propagators such that Hermiticity holds term by term.

Acyclic graphs

Either in the color basis or in the BW basis,
the sum in Eq. (
10
) runs over all
acyclic
graphs;
the graphs should not contain any closed loop of directed edges.
The directions mean time ordering,
so a closed directed loop contains a factor like

\displaystyle\Theta(t_{1}{\hskip 1.00006pt-\,}t_{2})\,\Theta(t_{2}{\hskip 1.00006pt-\,}t_{3})\,\Theta(t_{3}{\hskip 1.00006pt-\,}t_{1})\,,

(15)

which vanishes upon integration over
t_{i}

(i=1,2,3)
.

A special case of directed closed loop is the “tadpole”,
an edge emanating from a vertex and terminating at the same vertex.
The normal ordering of the operators in the vertices, to be specified below, excludes tadpole graphs.

Symmetry factor

The symmetry factor
\sigma(G)
in Eq. (
10
) is defined in the standard way,
which is completely independent of the specifics of the QFT.
One minor concern is that
the symmetry factor should take account of the directions and/or colors of the edges when applicable.
See Fig.
2
for some examples of the symmetry factor.

Symmetry factors of graphs.

Vertices

The vertices in the graphs denote copies of the interaction picture Hamiltonian
H_{I}
in Eq. (
6
).
Quantum mechanically, they represent normal ordered operators.
When we need a concrete theory, we will use the
\phi^{4}
QFT,
although the discussion is valid for any scalar potential,

\displaystyle H_{I}(t)\,=\,\int d^{d-1}\vec{x}\,\,\mathcal{V}(t,\vec{x})\,,\quad\mathcal{V}(x)\,=\,\frac{\lambda}{4!}{\hskip 1.00006pt{:}\hskip 1.00006pt{\phi(x)^{4}}\hskip 0.50003pt{:}\hskip 1.00006pt}\,.

(16)

Here,
\phi(x)
is the free scalar field operator:

\displaystyle\phi(x)\,=\int d\kern-1.99997pt\makebox[0.0pt][l]{$\bar{}$}\kern 1.99997pt^{d}k\,\delta\kern-1.99997pt\makebox[0.0pt][l]{$\bar{}$}\kern 1.99997pt(k^{2}{+\hskip 1.00006pt}m^{2})\,\Theta(k^{0})\hskip 1.00006pt\Big(\,{a(k)\hskip 1.00006pte^{ik\cdot x}+a^{\dagger}(k)\hskip 1.00006pte^{-ik\cdot x}}\,\Big)\,=\,\phi^{+}(x)+\phi^{-}(x)\,.

(17)

Also,
{\hskip 1.00006pt{:}\hskip 1.00006pt{\mathcal{O}}\hskip 0.50003pt{:}\hskip 1.00006pt}
implements the normal ordering
on the operator
\mathcal{O}
,
which pushes all
a^{\dagger}
to the left and all
a
to the right.
When a vertex in a graph has valence
m
(
m
edges connected to the vertex),
it contributes the
m
-th order derivative of
\mathcal{V}
.

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}\hskip 1.00006pt=\hskip 1.00006pt\frac{\lambda}{4!}\phi^{4}\,\,\,\xrightarrow{\>\>\>}\,\,\,\partial_{\phi}\mathcal{V}\hskip 1.00006pt=\hskip 1.00006pt\frac{\lambda}{3!}\phi^{3}\,\,\,\xrightarrow{\>\>\>}\,\,\,\partial_{\phi}^{2}\mathcal{V}\hskip 1.00006pt=\hskip 1.00006pt\frac{\lambda}{2!}\phi^{2}\,\,\,\xrightarrow{\>\>\>}\,\,\,\cdots\,.

(18)

After all the vertices are connected by propagators, one takes the product of the remaining operators and
takes an overall normal ordering.
For example, the operator associated with a triangle graph is

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{\triangle}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})\,=\,\lambda^{3}\hskip 1.00006pt{\hskip 1.00006pt{:}\hskip 1.00006pt{\bigg(\,{\frac{1}{2}\,\phi(x_{1})^{2}}\,\bigg)\bigg(\,{\frac{1}{2}\,\phi(x_{2})^{2}}\,\bigg)\bigg(\,{\frac{1}{2}\,\phi(x_{3})^{2}}\,\bigg)}\hskip 0.50003pt{:}\hskip 1.00006pt}\,\,\,\xrightarrow{\>\>\>}\,\,\,\frac{\lambda^{3}}{2^{3}}{\hskip 1.00006pt{:}\hskip 1.00006pt{\phi_{1}^{2}\phi_{2}^{2}\phi_{3}^{2}}\hskip 0.50003pt{:}\hskip 1.00006pt}\,.

(19)

When we compute the matrix elements of
\chi
, the operator will act on the external bra/ket states.

Operator-valued integrals

Once we specify the vertices and the edges of a graph, we can write down the operator-valued integral
\mathcal{I}(G)
in Eq. (
14a
) or Eq. (
14b
):

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(G)\,=\,\int\prod_{i=1}^{|G|}\,d^{d}x_{i}\,\,\mathcal{O}_{G}(x_{1},\cdots,x_{|G|})\,\mathcal{P}_{G}(x_{1},\cdots,x_{|G|})\,.

(20)

The operator
\mathcal{O}_{G}
generalizes the triangle example in Eq. (
19
).
The other factor,
\mathcal{P}_{G}
, is the product of propagators
whose precise definition will be given in Sec.
2.4
.

2.3
Wick Contraction and Operator Product

A textbook approach to perturbative QFT uses the normal ordering prescription and the Wick contraction.
It is useful to illustrate the key ideas with a toy model emulating
the scalar field in Eq. (
17
),

\displaystyle\phi_{i}\,=\,\bar{f}_{i}\,a+f_{i}\,a^{\dagger}\,,\quad[\hskip 0.50003pt{a},{a^{\dagger}}\hskip 0.50003pt]\,=\,\hbar\,,\quad a{\hskip 0.50003pt\big|\hskip 0.50003pt{0}\big\rangle}\,=\,0\,.

(21)

A simple exercise shows that

\displaystyle\begin{split}\phi_{1}\phi_{2}\;&=\;{\hskip 1.00006pt{:}\hskip 1.00006pt{\phi_{1}\phi_{2}}\hskip 0.50003pt{:}\hskip 1.00006pt}+\,\hbar\,\mathcal{W}_{12}\,,\\
\phi_{1}{\hskip 1.00006pt{:}\hskip 1.00006pt{\phi_{2}^{2}}\hskip 0.50003pt{:}\hskip 1.00006pt}\;&=\;{\hskip 1.00006pt{:}\hskip 1.00006pt{\phi_{1}\phi_{2}^{2}}\hskip 0.50003pt{:}\hskip 1.00006pt}+2\hbar\,\mathcal{W}_{12}\phi_{2}\,,\\
\phi_{1}\phi_{2}\phi_{3}\;&=\;{\hskip 1.00006pt{:}\hskip 1.00006pt{\phi_{1}\phi_{2}\phi_{3}}\hskip 0.50003pt{:}\hskip 1.00006pt}+\,\hbar\,\big(\hskip 1.00006pt{\mathcal{W}_{12}\phi_{3}+\mathcal{W}_{23}\phi_{1}+\mathcal{W}_{13}\phi_{2}}\hskip 1.00006pt\big)\,,\end{split}

(22)

where
\mathcal{W}_{ij}
is a toy Wightman function defined as

\displaystyle\hbar\hskip 1.00006pt\mathcal{W}_{ij}\,=\,\hbar\,\bar{f}_{i}f_{j}\,=\,{\big\langle{0}\hskip 0.50003pt\big|\hskip 0.50003pt}\phi_{i}\phi_{j}{\hskip 0.50003pt\big|\hskip 0.50003pt{0}\big\rangle}\,.

(23)

Let
\mathcal{P}(\phi)

2.5
Symmetry

Time ordering and Lorentz invariance

All
\mathcal{W}
-type functions are Lorentz invariant, but when they are multiplied by the time-ordering function
\Theta(t_{i}-t_{j})
, Lorentz invariance may get broken.
For example, the red and blue propagators in Eq. (
34
) are
not
individually Lorentz invariant, since
\mathcal{W}
does not necessarily vanish when
(x_{1}-x_{2})
is space-like.
In contrast, both propagators in Eq. (
35
) are Lorentz invariant.
For the retarded propagator,

\displaystyle(G_{\text{ret}})_{12}\,=\,i\,\Big(\,{(\mathcal{W}_{\text{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}red}}})_{12}-(\mathcal{W}_{\text{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}blue}}})_{12}}\,\Big)\,\Theta(t_{1}{\hskip 1.00006pt-\,}t_{2})\,,

(39)

the anti-symmetric combination cancels out the value for space-like
(x_{1}-x_{2})
completely.
The
\Theta
function selects the future light-cone and discards the past light-cone,
which respects Lorentz invariance.
The cut propagator does not carry a
\Theta
function.

When the colored propagators are combined to reach a cut propagator, the
\Theta
functions should all be removed.
There are largely two mechanisms. One is

\displaystyle(\mathcal{W}_{S})_{12}\,\big(\hskip 1.00006pt{\Theta_{12}+\Theta_{21}}\hskip 1.00006pt\big)\,=\,(\mathcal{W}_{S})_{12}\,.

(40)

The other is

\displaystyle(\mathcal{W}_{S})_{12}\Theta_{12}\,(\mathcal{W}_{A})_{13}\Theta_{13}\,(\mathcal{W}_{A})_{32}\Theta_{32}\,=\,(G_{\text{cut}})_{12}\hskip 1.00006pt(G_{\text{ret}})_{13}\hskip 1.00006pt(G_{\text{ret}})_{32}\,.

(41)

Here, the product
\Theta_{13}\Theta_{32}
enforces
t_{1}>t_{2}
, so
\Theta_{12}
can be discarded.

Time reversal and color parity

The diagrammatic expansion is invariant under two symmetry transformations. The first one is time reversal. The expansion is invariant under the overall reversal of time ordering.
Time-reversal images share the same
\omega
values, so the sum over all graphs is invariant.

The second symmetry is the “color parity”; flipping all reds to blues and vice versa.
It originates from the fact that
\chi
is Hermitian (self-adjoint).
For a graph
G
in the color basis, let
\mathcal{C}(G)
be the color parity image.
The color parity acts on the
\omega
value as

\displaystyle\omega(\mathcal{C}(G))\,=\,(-1)^{V(G)-1}\omega(G)\,.

(42)

With the
i^{(V(G)-1)}
factor in Eq. (
14a
), and the complex conjugation in Eq. (
36
), this rule ensures that
\chi
is Hermitian.
The color parity translated to the BW basis implies that

\displaystyle L\,-\hskip 1.00006ptE_{\text{cut}}\,=\,E_{\text{ret}}-(V{\hskip 1.00006pt-\,}1)\;\;\in\;\;2\mathbb{Z}\,.

(43)

This condition (called “cut structure” in Ref.
9
) is necessary for the
i
factors to be absorbed into the retarded propagators in going from the color basis to the BW basis.

From Magnus to Murua

The
n
-th order Magnus recursion in Eq. (
4
) involves level
k
nested commutators with
1\leq k\leq n-1
.
For small
n
, say up to
n=4
, it is easy to evaluate the commutators by hand.
But, the computation becomes cumbersome quickly as
k
increases.
It is desirable to find a shortcut to find the values of
\omega(G)
in the diagrammatic expansion in Eq. (
10
).

For rooted trees, a pioneering work by Murua
34
provided a recursive formula to compute
\omega(G)
. The formula was extended to non-rooted trees in Ref.
21
.
Our goal in this section is to further generalize the formula to cover all loop graphs,
both in the color basis and in the BW basis.
As a preparation, we give a brief review of the tree Murua formula of Ref.
21
.

The extended Murua formula for a tree graph is given by

\displaystyle\omega(\tau)\,=\sum_{p\in P_{s}(\tau)}(-1)^{\ell(p^{\prime})}B_{|p|-1}\,e(p^{\prime})\,|p|\,\omega(\tau\backslash p)\,.

(46)

Here,
B_{k}
are the Bernoulli numbers originating from the Magnus recursion.
This equation can be viewed as a diagrammatic representation of Eq. (
7
), where
(-1)^{\ell(p^{\prime})}e(p^{\prime})|p|
encodes the nested commutator structure and
\omega(\tau\backslash p)
encodes
\chi_{r}
.

For a rooted tree, the vertex
s
refers to the unique root, which is the “global minimum”
according to the ordering of vertices set by the directed edges.
For a non-rooted tree, the vertex
s
can be one of the semi-roots, a “local minimum.”
The formula involves the choice of a semi-root
s
, but the resulting
\omega(\tau)
turns out to be independent of the choice.
P_{s}(\tau)
is the set of partitions of
\tau
that contain all edges connected to the semi-root
s
.

A partition
p
of a tree
\tau
is an arbitrary collection of edges of
\tau
; there are
2^{E(\tau)}

partitions when there is no restriction. It is understood that
p
is made connected by
shrinking each connected components of the remainder
(\tau\backslash p)
.
Given a partition
p
, we flip the orientation of all the “wrong”
edges of
p
to obtain a new tree
p^{\prime}
rooted with respect to
s
. The function
\ell(p^{\prime})
counts how many edges of
p
should be flipped to reach
p^{\prime}
.

For a tree graph, the function
e(\tau)
is defined as

\displaystyle e(\tau)\,=\,\frac{1}{|\tau|!}\,\phi(\tau)\,,

(47)

where the integer-valued
\phi(\tau)
counts the number of linear extensions of a poset.
In other words, it counts the number of all possible orderings of labeled vertices that are
consistent with the directed edges of a tree.
See Figure 5. of Ref.
21
for some examples.

The definition of the
e
-function generalizes straightforwardly to any acyclic loop graph.
For a cyclic graph, where a fully consistent ordering of vertices is impossible,
it is convenient to set
e=0
.
The definition of the
e
function implies the contraction rule:

\displaystyle e(G_{[1\leftarrow 2]})\,+\,e(G_{[1\rightarrow 2]})\,=\,e(G_{[1|2]})\,.

(48)

On the LHS, we consider two graphs which differ only by the direction of the edge a specified pair of vertices (labeled 1 and 2). On the RHS, the edge [1-2] is cut while the rest of the graph remains the same.

The
e
function can be further generalized to incorporate colors or undirected edges,
but the one for fully directed single-color graphs will be sufficient in this paper.

3.1
Quantum Murua Formula in the Color Basis

Our proposal for the quantum Murua formula in the color basis is as follows.

\displaystyle\omega(G)\,=\sum_{p\in P_{s}(G)}\sum_{p^{\prime\prime}\in F_{s}(p)}(-1)^{\ell(p^{\prime\prime})}\,e(p^{\prime\prime})\,B_{|p|-1}|p|\,\omega(G\backslash p)\,.

(49)

As in the tree formula, the
(-1)^{\ell(p^{\prime\prime})}e(p^{\prime\prime})|p|
factor reflects the nested commutator structure
while
\omega(\tau\backslash p)
encodes
\chi_{r}
in Eq. (
7
).
Here,
s
is a choice of semi-root and
P_{s}(G)
is the set of partitions of
G
that contains the semi-root.
Again, the value of
\omega(G)
is independent of the choice;
otherwise Lorentz invariance of the Magnusian will be lost.
The modified partition
p^{\prime\prime}
is obtained from
p
following a few steps, to be explained shortly.
A critical complication compared to the tree case is that, for a given
p
, there can be multiple possibilities for
p^{\prime\prime}
and we have to sum over all of them.

Note that the

values of the second column and the third column are the same, as the two graphs are related by the overall time-reversal, but the Murua formula operates differently.

First glance at the formula

We assume that the reader has become familiar with the classical Murua formula in Eq. (
46
), and give a step-by-step guide on how to use the quantum Murua formula in Eq. (

).

We choose a semi-root and list all partitions containing the semi-root. The decomposition of
G
into

and

proceeds as usual, whether

contains loops or not. The factors,
|p|
,

,
\omega(G\backslash p)
contribute in the same way as in the tree case.
We remind the reader that
|p|
is the number of
vertices
of the partition, after

has been shrunken to points.

The challenge with the quantum Murua formula is how to turn

into the set of
p^{\prime\prime}
’s.
The first step is to forget the time ordering and focus on the operator ordering. In practice, we reverse the direction of all blue edges and then ignore all colors. If a cycle is formed, we discard the partition and proceed to the next one. If an acyclic banana loop is formed, we replace it with a simple edge. The outcome is called
p^{\prime}
.
In the sense discussed in Sec.
2.3
,

pays attention to the operator ordering and ignore the time ordering.
The doubled arrowheads in

of Figs.

The second step is to look up the operator product diagrams
discussed in Sec.
2.3
,
in order to enumerate all possible candidates

which can match
p^{\prime}
upon systematic flipping of directions.
To explain how it works, we should digress to take a closer look at the operator product diagrams.

Back to the quantum Murua formula

We are now ready to explain the most subtle step of the quantum Murua formula, namely, figuring out the set of
p^{\prime\prime}
for a given

. Generically,

is not almost-rooted. The candidates for

are taken from the spider web graphs, so by construction

is almost-rooted. The level of spider web is fixed to be
V(p)-1=E(p)-L(p)
, but there can be multiple
(p^{\prime\prime})
’s that match the same

. We should find each and every almost-rooted
p^{\prime\prime}
which can reach the

by a sequence of flipping directed edges.

On the

side, we have a clear distinction between incoming and outgoing edges. To map it to
p^{\prime}
, for each vertex of
p^{\prime\prime}
, we may choose to flip all outgoing arrows simultaneously or not to flip them at all; partial flipping (
e.g.
flipping 2 out of 3 edges from the same vertex) is strictly forbidden.
The sign factor
(-1)^{\ell(p^{\prime\prime})}
in the Murua formula counts the number of vertices on which we flip the outgoing arrows to map
p^{\prime\prime}
to
p^{\prime}
.

The sign flip is based on the relative signs among various operator products coming from a common nested commutator in Eq. (
28
). A crucial point in the derivation of the Murua formula is that the time ordering among non-root vertices in the Mganus recursion relation in Eq. (
7

For example, in the right-most column of Fig.

(p^{\prime\prime})
come from two different time orderings of the level 2 nested propagator.

Contraction rules

It is possible to implement the quantum Murua formula on a computer,
but it tends to be slow as
n
increases.
As in the tree case
21
,
a more efficient route is to derive the “edge contraction” rules (not to be confused with the Wick contracction of QFT) from the Murua formula and then use the rules to compute
\omega(G)
.

In the color basis, the edge contraction rules can be stated as

\displaystyle\omega(G_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}[1\leftarrow 2]}})-\omega(G_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}[1\rightarrow 2]}})\,

\displaystyle=\,-\omega(G_{[1\cdot 2]})\,,

(51a)

\displaystyle\omega(G_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}[1\leftarrow 2]}})+\omega(G_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}[1\leftarrow 2]}})\,

\displaystyle=\,\omega(G_{[1|2]\text{conn}})\,.

(51b)

Taking linear combinations of the two rules, we obtain contraction rules considering one color at a time,

\displaystyle\omega(G_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}[1\leftarrow 2]}})+\omega(G_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}[1\rightarrow 2]}})

\displaystyle\,=\,-\omega(G_{[1\cdot 2]})+\omega(G_{[1|2]\text{conn}})\,,

(52a)

\displaystyle\omega(G_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}[1\leftarrow 2]}})+\omega(G_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}[1\rightarrow 2]}})

\displaystyle\,=\,+\omega(G_{[1\cdot 2]})+\omega(G_{[1|2]\text{conn}})\,.

(52b)

On the LHS of the contraction rules, we consider both orientations of the edge connecting vertices 1 and 2, as well as both colors. The rest of the graph is assumed to be identical. On the RHS,
G_{[1|2]}
means the edge has been cut. For trees
G_{[1|2]}
would be a formal product of two disjoint trees (called forest). Since the removable edge is a part of a loop,
G_{[1|2]}
in the current context remains connected. The notation
G_{[1|2]\text{conn}}
means that the term should be included only when
G_{[1|2]}
is connected. Otherwise, it will spoil the tree-level contraction rule. Finally,
G_{[1\cdot 2]}
means that the edge has collapsed to merge the two vertices.
See Fig.
22
for an illustration.

Figure 22:
The configurations for contraction rules. The color can be either red or blue.

We wish to prove the two rules, Eq. (
51a
) and Eq. (
51b
).
The proof proceeds recursively. The rules hold for
E(G)=1
. Then we assume that the rule holds for
E(G)\leq r\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
and try to prove the same rule for
E(G)=r+1
.

Eq. (
51a
) is easier to prove.
When the edge [1-2] belongs to the partition
p
, the red case and the blue case give the same
p^{\prime}
, which cancel each other. These are precisely the partitions that appear only on the LHS and not on the RHS.
When the edge [1-2] falls into
G\backslash p
, we can apply the same rule for smaller graphs to prove Eq. (
51a
) for the whole graph.

The proof of Eq. (
51b
) is more involved.
When the edge [1-2] is included in
G\backslash p
, we can again apply the same rule for smaller graphs,
so the recursive proof works trivially.
When the edge [1-2] belongs to
p
, the set of
(p^{\prime\prime})
’s differs non-trivially
between the two sides of Eq. (
51b
).
The
(p^{\prime\prime})
’s of the two graphs on the LHS
can be divided into two groups:
“match” or “cancel.”
The
(p^{\prime\prime})
’s in the first group pair up to match the corresponding
(p^{\prime\prime})
of the RHS.
The value of
e(p^{\prime\prime})
match via the
e
contraction rule in Eq. (
48
).
When any
p^{\prime\prime}
is cyclic we take
e(p^{\prime\prime})=0
.
Those
(p^{\prime\prime})
’s in the second group cancel pairwise.
The canceling pair of
(p^{\prime\prime})
’s on the LHS are identical between the red and blue parent graphs
but carry the opposite
(-1)^{\ell(p^{\prime\prime})}
.
An example illustrating the idea of “match or cancel” is given in Fig.
23
.

Figure 23:
Cancellation and matching of

The contraction rule is strong enough to compute
\omega(G)
for all graphs recursively. Our starting point is the set of

values for tree graphs,

, previously obtained in Ref.

. For the colored trees,

is modulated by

.

of an

-loop graphs.

Given a non-colored directed graph with

Eq. (

) relates

values of two graphs which differ only by the color of an edge, up to

of an

If we know

Fortunately, as pointed out in Ref.

Zero-sum rule

Given a directed, acyclic, non-colored graph with
E
edges,
we observe that the sum of
\omega(G)
over
2^{E}
colored graphs sharing the same non-colored graph vanishes.
Let us call it a “zero-sum rule.”
It is easy to prove recursively using Eq. (
51b
).
The reader is encouraged to verify the rule in simple examples in Figs.
10
and
11
.

Physically, the zero sum rule is a necessary condition for Lorentz covariance.
If the sum is nonzero, we can always find a configuration of mutually space-like separated points where its corresponding integrand
\sum\omega(G)\hskip 1.00006ptI(G)
is nonzero,
3
3

3

I(G)
was used in place of
\mathcal{I}(G)
to indicate that the expression refers to the integrand rather than the integral.

because there always exists an ordering of vertices that can satisfy the time-ordering relations imposed by the product of step functions.
For such a configuration of spacetime points, there always exists a Lorentz transformation that maps it to a different configuration where the time-ordering relations are violated, resulting in

.

3.2
Quantum Murua Formula in the BW Basis

The quantum Murua formula in the BW basis can be stated as follows.

\displaystyle\omega(G)\,=\sum_{p\in P_{s}(G)}\sum_{p^{\prime\prime}\in F_{s}(p)}\,(-1/4)^{m(p^{\prime\prime})}(-1)^{\ell(p^{\prime\prime})}\,e(p^{\prime\prime})\,B_{|p|-1}|p|\,\omega(G\backslash p)\,.

(54)

The undirected edges play no role, and it suffices to consider graphs made of directed edges only.
Partitions are taken in the usual way. The partitions with tadpoles are discarded.
Unlike in the color basis, the partition
p
may contain (non-tadpole) cycles.
The cycles do
not
mean a contradictory time ordering such as
\Theta_{12}\Theta_{23}\Theta_{13}

since the vertices of
p
, being connected components
G\backslash p
,
can have two or more time coordinates.
To go from
p
to
p^{\prime}
, one simply forgets the directions.
To go to
p^{\prime\prime}
, one looks for almost rooted trees compatible with
p^{\prime}
, with the additional restriction that the number of outgoing edges must be odd for every (non-root) vertex.
This restriction arises from the fact that each commutator in the Magnus recursion produces the anti-symmetric fuzzy Wightman function
W_{A}^{\#}
in Eq. (
38b
) which is an odd function of
W_{A}
.

Each
p^{\prime\prime}
comes with further multiplicative factors.
One is the sign factor; compare
p^{\prime\prime}
to
p
and count how many edges need to be flipped.
Unlike in the color case, the edges can be flipped individually without restriction;
the sign flip concerns time ordering and not operator ordering.
The sign factor is called
(-1)^{\ell(p^{\prime\prime})}
. The other counts powers of
(-1/4)
originating from the fuzzy Wightman function in Eq. (
38b
). A vertex
v
in
p^{\prime\prime}
with
2k_{v}+1
outgoing edges contribute
(-1/4)^{k_{v}}
. If
m(p^{\prime\prime})
is the sum of all
k_{v}
, the net factor becomes
(-1/4)^{m(p^{\prime\prime})}
.

An example is given in Fig.
25
.
The light blue shades in
p^{\prime\prime}
count
\ell(p^{\prime\prime})
.
The pink shades indicate vertices contributing to
(-1/4)^{m(p^{\prime\prime})}
.
The net result of the formula can be summarized as

\displaystyle\begin{split}\omega(G)\,&=\,\left(\,{\begin{aligned} &\left(-\frac{1}{4}\right)\cdot 1\cdot\left(\frac{(-1)^{2}}{40}+\frac{(-1)^{1}}{120}\right)\left(-\frac{1}{30}\right)\cdot 5\\
&+\left(-\frac{1}{4}\right)\bigg(\,{\frac{1}{3}\cdot\frac{(-1)^{1}+(-1)^{1}}{6}+\frac{1}{4}\cdot\frac{(-1)^{2}+(-1)^{0}}{6}}\,\bigg)\cdot\frac{1}{6}\cdot{3}\end{aligned}}\,\right)\,=\,\frac{1}{240}\,.\end{split}

(55)

Contraction rules

The edge contraction rules are also available in the BW basis.
The first rule concerns the cut propagator:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\omega(G_{[1{\color[rgb]{0.5,0.5,0.5}\cdots}2]})\,=\,\omega(G_{[1|2]\text{conn}})\,.\end{split}

(56)

The value of
\omega
remains the same when the cut propagator is “erased,” as long as the graph remains connected.
This phenomenon is implied by the fuzzy propagator discussed in Sec.
2.4
.
It generalizes the observation of Ref.
9
(up to a convention-dependent powers of factor 2).
The triangle example in Fig.
11
and the quadrangle example in Fig.
15
illustrate how the cut rule in Eq. (
56
) works.

The second rule concerns the retarded propagator,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\omega(G_{[1\leftarrow 2]})\hskip 1.00006pt+\,\omega(G_{[1\rightarrow 2]})\,=\,-\omega(G_{[1\cdot 2]})\,.\end{split}

(57)

The proof of Eq. (
57
) is similar to that of Eq. (
51a
). When the edge [1-2] belongs to the partition
p
, the two graphs of the LHS give the same
p^{\prime\prime}
with
\Delta\ell=1
, which cancel each other. Thus, the remaining terms on the LHS arise when the edge [1-2] belongs to
G\backslash p
, which perfectly matches the RHS by recursively applying Eq. (
57
) to the
\omega
in
G\backslash p
.

Fig.
26
shows some examples of the contraction rule.
After dispensing with cut propagators using Eq. (
56
), we are left with loop graphs composed entirely of retarded propagators. The number of loops,
L
, is even because of color parity.

L
is the same between the two sides of the contraction rule.
So, unlike in the color basis, we cannot run a recursion relating
L
-loop graphs to
(L-1)
-loop graphs.
But the contraction rule reduces the number of edges by 1.
Applying the rule repeatedly, we can reduce all primary loops (triangle, quadrangle, etc.) to
banana loops.
The edges of the banana loops can be removed in pairs, connecting
L
-loop graphs to
(L-2)
-loop graphs.

For a fixed graph topology (undirected graph as a family of oriented graphs), knowing the
\omega
value of one graph
is sufficient to determine
\omega
of all other graphs in the same topology.
Unlike in the color basis, we do not have graphs with
\omega=0
.
We can run the Murua formula for any one representative of the topology and use the contraction rule
to determine
\omega
of all the other graphs.
When flipping the direction of edges to apply the contraction rule, we have to avoid cyclic graphs.
It is easy to show that all graphs in the same
primary
loop topology can be connected by flipping directions
without ever encountering a cyclic graph along the way.

Effective field theory matching

In QFT, it is a common practice to begin with an ultraviolet (UV) theory
with heavy and light fields, and integrate out heavy fields to obtain an infrared (IR) theory with light fields only.
A full-fledged discussion of renormalization is beyond the scope of this paper, but
let us examine the role of the contraction rule in the process of integrating out heavy fields,
in a similar vein to the discussions of Ref.
21
.

Consider a toy model with the UV Lagrangian,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{UV}}\,=\,-\frac{1}{2}(\partial\phi)^{2}-\frac{1}{2}m^{2}\phi^{2}-\frac{\lambda_{0}}{4!}\phi^{4}-\frac{1}{2}(\partial\sigma)^{2}-\frac{1}{2}M^{2}\sigma^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\mu\sigma\phi^{2}\,.

(58)

Integrating out the heavy field
\sigma
, we obtain the IR Lagrangian,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{IR}}\,=\,-\frac{1}{2}(\partial\phi)^{2}-\frac{1}{2}m^{2}\phi^{2}-\frac{\lambda_{1}}{4!}\phi^{4}\,,\qquad\frac{\lambda_{1}}{4!}\,=\,\frac{\lambda_{0}}{4!}-\frac{\mu^{2}}{8M^{2}}\,.

(59)

Suppose we compute the quantum Magnusian of the UV theory and take the
M\rightarrow\infty
limit.
The result should match the Magnusian of the IR theory, up to terms suppressed at least by
m^{2}/M^{2}
.
The BW contraction rule for retarded propagators in Eq. (
57
)
ensures that the
\omega
values of the two diagrams of the UV theory add up to give the
\omega
value of the IR theory.
Fig.
27
shows one of the three channels through which the
\sigma
field mediates
the interaction of
\phi
fields. The effective quartic coupling constant of the IR theory is

\displaystyle\lambda_{0}-3\times\mu\frac{1}{p^{2}+M^{2}}\mu\;\;\approx\;\;\lambda_{0}-3\frac{\mu^{2}}{M^{2}}\,,

(60)

in agreement with the expectation from the Lagrangian in Eq. (
59
).

Figure 27:
Integrating out a heavy field from a Magnusian.

Those UV diagrams with a heavy particle sitting on the cut propagator do not affect the matching.
The cut propagator would enforce the mass-shell condition of the heavy particle,
but as far as the matrix elements of light fields are concerned,
the heavy mass-shell condition is out of reach.
The Magnusian of the IR theory does not admit any diagram corresponding to heavy cut diagrams of the UV theory.

3.3
Change of Basis

Recall that the map between the color basis and the BW basis is based on taking linear combinations of (fuzzy) propagators.
Not surprisingly, the
\omega
values of the two bases satisfy some linear relations.

From color to BW

Having seen how the two bases are related to each other in examples, we now discuss closed form formulas that connect the
\omega
values in one basis to that of the other basis.
We begin with the map from the color basis to the BW basis.

In the color basis, all edges are directed. In the BW basis, the retarded propagators are directed, but the cut propagators are not. Given a graph in the BW basis,
G_{\text{bw}}
, the first step is to introduce the notion of “directed completion”; we assign directions to the cut propagators in a way that does not create any cycle.
In physics terms, we assign a time ordering of the cut propagators that is compatible with the pre-existing time ordering of the retarded propagators.
In general, the directed completion is not unique, so we can make a choice.
See Fig.
28
for an example of
G_{\text{bw}}
which admits two non-equivalent completions.

Figure 28:
Two possibilities for directed completion.

Let
\widetilde{G}_{\text{bw}}
be any one of the directed completion
of
G_{\text{bw}}
.
Define
X(\widetilde{G}_{\text{bw}})
be the set of all colored graphs
G_{\text{color}}
which coincide with
\widetilde{G}_{\text{bw}}

when “bleached” (rid of the color).
To compute
\omega_{\text{bw}}(G_{\text{bw}})
, we take the following weighted sum,

\displaystyle(-1)^{(L-E_{\text{cut}})/2}\,\omega_{\text{bw}}(G_{\text{bw}})\,=\,\frac{1}{2^{\text{ret}(G_{\text{bw}})}}\sum_{G_{\text{color}}\in X(\widetilde{G}_{\text{bw}})}(-1)^{\text{ret-blue}}\,\omega_{\text{color}}(G_{\text{color}})\,.

(61)

Here,
\text{ret}(G_{\text{bw}})
is the number of retarded propagators of
G_{\text{bw}}
.
The sign factor
(-1)^{\text{ret-blue}}
depends on the number of blue edges of
G_{\text{color}}
that match the retarded propagators of
\widetilde{G}_{\text{bw}}
.
An important fact is that the resulting
\omega_{\text{bw}}
is independent of the choice of
\widetilde{G}_{\text{bw}}
.

Physically,
\omega_{\text{bw}}
being independent of the choice is a manifestation of Lorentz invariance.
If the directed completion of a cut propagator is not unique, there always exists a choice of spacetime points consistent with the time-orderings implied by the causal propagators where the relative time-ordering of vertices connected by the considered cut propagator can be reversed by a Lorentz transform.

Let us apply the formula to the example in Fig.
28
.
Working with
\tilde{G}^{(A)}_{\text{bw}}
, we import the
\omega
values of the colored trapezoids from Fig.
12
. The formula gives

\displaystyle\omega_{\text{bw}}\,=\,\frac{1}{2^{3}}\cdot 2\cdot\bigg(\,{-\frac{1}{4}+\frac{1}{12}+\frac{1}{12}-\frac{1}{12}+0-\frac{1}{6}-\frac{1}{6}+\frac{1}{6}}\,\bigg)\,=\,-\frac{1}{12}\,.

(62)

Here,
1/2^{3}
is from the
1/2^{\text{ret}}
factor. The other factor 2 in front is due to the color parity doubling. The eight numbers in the parenthesis comes from the sum over the eight graphs in Fig.
12
, modulated by
(-1)^{\text{ret-blue}}
.

If we choose to work with
\tilde{G}^{(B)}_{\text{bw}}
instead, we import the
\omega
values of the colored diamonds from Fig.
13
. The formula then gives

\displaystyle\omega_{\text{bw}}\,=\,\frac{1}{2^{3}}\cdot 2\cdot\bigg(\,{-\frac{1}{6}+0+0+0-\frac{1}{12}-\frac{1}{12}-\frac{1}{12}+\frac{1}{12}}\,\bigg)\,=\,-\frac{1}{12}\,.

(63)

We confirm that
\omega_{\text{bw}}
is independent of the choice between
\tilde{G}^{(A)}_{\text{bw}}
and
\tilde{G}^{(B)}_{\text{bw}}
.

From BW to color

The inverse formula of Eq. (
61
) reads

\displaystyle\omega_{\text{color}}(G_{\text{color}})\,=\sum_{G_{\text{bw}}\in Y(G_{\text{color}})}\frac{(-1)^{\text{blue-ret}}}{2^{\text{cut}(G_{\text{bw}})}}\,\Big(\,{(-1)^{(L-E_{\text{cut}})/2}\omega_{\text{bw}}(G_{\text{bw}})}\,\Big)\,.

(64)

Here, the colored graph
G_{\text{color}}
is the input. We define
Y(G_{\text{color}})
to be the set of all BW graphs whose suitably directed
\tilde{G}_{\text{bw}}
agrees with the bleached
G_{\text{color}}
.

An example is given in Fig.
29
.

\omega
in the color basis from those of the BW basis.

It is straightforward to show that Eq. (
61
) and Eq. (
64
) are mutually inverse functions. Inserting Eq. (
61
) into Eq. (
64
), we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}\omega_{\text{c}}(G_{\text{c}})\,&=\sum_{G_{\text{bw}}\in Y(G_{\text{c}})}\frac{(-1)^{\text{blue-ret}}}{2^{\text{cut}(G_{\text{bw}})}}\frac{1}{2^{\text{ret}(G_{\text{bw}})}}\sum_{G^{\prime}_{\text{color}}\in X(\widetilde{G}_{\text{bw}})}(-1)^{\text{ret-blue}^{\prime}}\omega_{\text{c}}(G^{\prime}_{\text{c}})\\
\,&=\,\frac{1}{2^{E(G_{\text{c}})}}\hskip 1.00006pt\sum_{Y,X}\,(-1)^{\text{blue-ret}}\hskip 1.00006pt(-1)^{\text{ret-blue}^{\prime}}\,\omega_{c}(G_{\text{c}}^{\prime})\,.\end{split}

(65)

The sign factor are all
(+1)
for
G^{\prime}_{\text{c}}=G_{\text{c}}
.
For any other
G^{\prime}_{\text{c}}
, there are equal number of
(+1)
and
(-1)
summing up to zero.

The two-way transformation rules, Eq. (
61
) and Eq. (
64
), map the contraction rule on one side to the contraction rule on the other side, providing a further consistency check.

4
Diagrammar at a Glance

The discussions in Secs.
2
and
3
establish a purely diagrammatic formulation of the quantum Magnusian
and the Murua coefficient.
To make this point explicit and clear,
we provide a compact summary of
the quantum Magnusian diagrammar
as the core message of this paper.

Sum over graphs

The quantum Magnusian is given by a sum over graphs.
We have two equivalent expressions, one in the color basis and the other in the BW basis.

\displaystyle\begin{split}{}\chi\,&=\,\sum_{G_{c}}\,\frac{\omega_{c}(G_{c})}{\sigma_{c}(G_{c})}\,\hbar^{L(G_{c})}\,i^{V(G_{c})-1}\,\mathcal{I}(G_{c})\\
\,&=\,\sum_{G_{\text{bw}}}\,\frac{\omega_{\text{bw}}(G_{\text{bw}})}{\sigma_{\text{bw}}(G_{\text{bw}})}\,\hbar^{L(G_{\text{bw}})}\,\mathcal{I}(G_{\text{bw}})\,.\end{split}

(66)

The
\omega
values of descendant graphs are completely determined by those of primary graphs.
The graph integral is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(G)\,=\,\int\prod_{i=1}^{|G|}d^{d}x_{i}\,\,\mathcal{O}_{G}(x_{1},\cdots,x_{|G|})\,\mathcal{P}_{G}(x_{1},\cdots,x_{|G|})\,.

(67)

The
\mathcal{P}(G)
factor denotes the product of
all the propagators appearing in the graph,
and
\mathcal{O}(G)
is the product of all vertex factors.

Primary graphs

The time ordering in the Magnus expansion is more intuitive in position space, but the propagators are more succinctly described in momentum space. Our convention for a momentum space two-point function is

\displaystyle G(k)\,=\int d^{d}x\,\,e^{-ik\cdot x}\,G(x;0)\,,

(68)

which means
G(k)
should be analytic on the upper half plane of
k^{0}
if
G(x;0)
vanishes for
t<0
.
The colored propagators read

\displaystyle\begin{split}G_{\text{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}red}}}(k)\,=\,\frac{i}{2E_{\vec{k}}\big(\hskip 1.00006pt{k^{0}-E_{\vec{k}}+i0^{+}}\hskip 1.00006pt\big)}\,,\quad G_{\text{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}blue}}}(k)\,=\,\frac{i}{2E_{\vec{k}}\big(\hskip 1.00006pt{k^{0}+E_{\vec{k}}+i0^{+}}\hskip 1.00006pt\big)}\,,\end{split}

(69)

with
E_{\vec{k}}=(\vec{k}^{2}+m^{2})^{1/2}
.
The Hermiticity is realized as
[G_{\text{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}red}}}(k)]^{*}=G_{\text{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}blue}}}(-k)
.
The retarded and cut propagators in the BW basis are

\displaystyle\begin{split}G_{\text{ret}}(k)\,&=\,\frac{1}{k^{2}+m^{2}-i0^{+}\text{sgn}(k^{0})}=\frac{1}{-\big(\hskip 1.00006pt{k^{0}+i0^{+}}\hskip 1.00006pt\big)^{\hskip-0.81949pt2}+\vec{k}^{2}+m^{2}}\,,\\
G_{\text{cut}}(k)\,&=\,\pi\,\delta(k^{2}+m^{2})\,.\end{split}

(70)

The vertex factors are the same in the two bases.
A valence
m
vertex reads

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{m}\,=\,\frac{\partial^{m}\mathcal{V}}{\partial\phi^{m}}\,.

(71)

Descendant graphs

In the color basis, any red or blue propagator can turn into a banana loop of the same color with multiplicity
r
, producing a multi-propagator weighted by a symmetry factor:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{r!}\,(G_{\text{color}})^{r}\,.

(72)

In the BW basis, a banana loop with
r
retarded and
s
cut propagators is weighted by

\displaystyle\left(-\frac{1}{4}\right)^{\hskip-0.81949pt\hskip-0.81949pt\lfloor r/2\rfloor}\frac{1}{r!s!}\,(G_{\text{ret}})^{r}\hskip 1.00006pt(G_{\text{cut}})^{s}\,.

(73)

Odd/even values
r
correspond to the fuzzy retarded/cut propagators,
respectively.

Edge-contraction rules

The edge-contraction rule in the color basis is

\displaystyle\omega(G_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}[1\leftarrow 2]}})-\omega(G_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}[1\rightarrow 2]}})\,

\displaystyle=\,-\omega(G_{[1\cdot 2]})\,,

(74a)

\displaystyle\omega(G_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}[1\leftarrow 2]}})+\omega(G_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}[1\leftarrow 2]}})\,

\displaystyle=\,\omega(G_{[1|2]\text{conn}})\,.

(74b)

The edge-contraction rule in the BW basis is

\displaystyle\omega(G_{[1{\color[rgb]{0.5,0.5,0.5}\cdots}2]})\,

\displaystyle=\,\omega(G_{[1|2]\text{conn}})\,,

(75a)

\displaystyle\omega(G_{[1\leftarrow 2]})+\omega(G_{[1\rightarrow 2]})\,

\displaystyle=\,-\omega(G_{[1\cdot 2]})\,.

(75b)

G_{[1\cdot 2]}
is the graph obtained from
G_{[1\rightarrow 2]}
or
G_{[1\leftarrow 2]}
by contracting the edge to a vertex, and
G_{[1|2]\text{conn}}
is the connected graph obtained from
G_{[1\rightarrow 2]}
or
G_{[1\leftarrow 2]}
by removing the edge, otherwise it vanishes. See Fig.
22
for examples.

Basis change rules

To map the
\omega
values from the color basis to the BW basis or vice versa, we can use the two formulas below:

\displaystyle(-1)^{(L-E_{\text{cut}})/2}\,\omega_{\text{bw}}(G_{\text{bw}})\,

\displaystyle=\,\frac{1}{2^{\text{ret}(G_{\text{bw}})}}\sum_{G_{\text{color}}\in X(\widetilde{G}_{\text{bw}})}(-1)^{\text{ret-blue}}\omega_{\text{color}}(G_{\text{color}})\,,

(76a)

\displaystyle\omega_{\text{color}}(G_{\text{color}})\,

\displaystyle=\sum_{G_{\text{bw}}\in Y(G_{\text{color}})}\frac{(-1)^{\text{blue-ret}}}{2^{\text{cut}(G_{\text{bw}})}}\,\Big(\,{(-1)^{(L-E_{\text{cut}})/2}\,\omega_{\text{bw}}(G_{\text{bw}})}\,\Big)\,.

(76b)

Use

To connect with the practical needs of physicists,
recall Eq. (
9
).
Once the quantum Magnusian is computed,
the impulse of quantum observables
arises via the formula:

\displaystyle\big\langle\hskip 0.50003pt{\mathcal{O}}\hskip 0.50003pt\big\rangle_{\hskip-0.40974pt\text{out}}\,=\,\big\langle\hskip 0.50003pt{e^{{{-}\hskip-0.81949pt\ad_{\chi/i\hbar}}}\hskip 1.00006pt\mathcal{O}}\hskip 0.50003pt\big\rangle_{\text{in}}\,.

(77)

5
Conclusion

In this paper,
we completed the diagrammar of quantum Magnusian
by combining and extending
insights from pioneering works
9
;
25
.
This diagrammar is summarized in Sec.
4
.
Although we presumed the specific context of relativistic scalar QFTs
for concreteness,
it should be emphasized that the Magnusian is a much general and broader concept
as remarked in Sec.
2.1
.
Hence, up to mild variations,
we expect the relevance of our diagrammar
to generic QFTs,
in other branches of physics,
and even outside physics.

On top of the first principles frameworks

9
;
25
,
the purely diagrammatic approach pursued in this paper
facilitates an efficient algorithm for constructing the quantum Magnusian,
based on the edge contraction rules.

Although we now have sufficiently efficient methods for
computing the quantum Magnusian,
our understanding of its physics is far from complete.
Below, we list a few questions regarding the physics of quantum Magnusian.

•

The modern understanding of QFTs is that they should be understood as effective descriptions that require regularization and renormalization to make sensible predictions.
How can we prove renormalizability of the quantum Magnusian without resorting to the finiteness of the
S
-matrix?

•

The singularities of the
S
-matrix elements are known to be associated with particle production, which can be explored through the Landau equations
30
.
What is the physics behind the singularities (if any) of the quantum Magnusian, and what are the analogues of the Landau equations for exploring its singular loci?

•

Many computations in gauge theories are organized in the
1/N
expansion, such as the planar sector of
\mathcal{N}=4
SYM.
Is there any qualitative difference (e.g. organization of color factors) between the
1/N
expansion of
S
-matrix elements and the
1/N
expansion of Magnus amplitudes?

•

And lastly, Wilson lines are computed as
path-ordered
exponentiated operators.
How can we repurpose the mathematical machinery for diagrammatic expansion of the quantum Magnusian to computations involving Wilson lines?

Another set of questions we can ask about the quantum Magnusian is whether it is compatible with more modern methods developed for computing scattering amplitudes, which avoid explicit references to vertex rules and Feynman diagrams.
Examples of such methods include generalized unitarity
6
;
7
and the color-kinematics dual form of gauge theory integrands for gravity computations
4
;
5
,
which relies on the fact that a family of Feynman diagrams that share a common subgraph can be grouped together.
For tree diagrams, Ref.
21
identified a sum rule dubbed “Feynman reduction” as a possible mechanism for applying such modern tools to the computation of the (classical) Magnusian.
It would be interesting to explore whether similar sum rules exist for diagrams with loops,
and how such modern methods can be applied to the computation of the quantum Magnusian.

In Ref.
21
, the Hopf algebra behind the Magnus expansion, introduced by Calaque, Ebrahimi-Fard, and Manchon
10
;
12
,
was extended from rooted trees to non-rooted trees, providing extra conceptual and technical layers to
the tree diagrammar of the Magnusian. A natural question is how to extend the Hopf algebra further to loop graphs
endowed with the color/BW basis of this paper.
This very question will be the main topic of a companion paper
20
.
