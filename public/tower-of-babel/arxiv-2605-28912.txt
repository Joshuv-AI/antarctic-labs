Cycle-Space Informed Detection of Autoencoded Blind False Data Injection Attacks on Power Systems
Xin Li 1 , Chenhan Xiao 2 , Jonathan Cohen 1 , Aviad Elyashar 3 4 , Yang Weng 2 , Rami Puzis 1 4

Abstract
The rapid growth of AI-driven data centers and large-scale energy storage systems is increasing the reliance of power system operation on real-time measurement data and automated decision-making. However, many existing detection methods rely on statistical or data-driven analysis of measurements and can fail when attackers exploit the same data structure to craft stealthy perturbations. To illustrate this limitation, we demonstrate a blind fdia ( fdia ) in which an autoencoder learns the measurement manifold and generates perturbations aligned with the Jacobian null space, thereby allowing the attack to evade both residual-based bad-data detectors and time-series anomaly detectors. To mitigate data-driven FDIAs which exploit the null space, we propose a topology-informed csd ( csd ) that leverages the Cycle-Space of the network to impose structural constraints that enhance null space estimation. In addition, we prove that by using the mcb ( mcb ), the proposed csd achieves the optimal generalization error for attack detection. By exploiting topology-derived cycle constraints rather than relying solely on numerical null space estimation, the proposed method does not require precise line parameters and improves the separation between normal and attacked measurements. Simulation results on IEEE 14-, 30-, 57-, and 118-bus systems demonstrate that the proposed method effectively detects data-driven FDIAs under realistic measurement noise.

Introduction

Modern power system operation increasingly relies on real-time measurement data, especially with the rapid growth of AI-driven data centers and large-scale energy storage systems

At the same time, emerging grid assets, such as utility-scale battery energy storage systems, are becoming new potential targets for cyber intrusions

These risks are particularly significant as more grid data is processed through cloud platforms and third-party services

One common attack is the false data injection attack (FDIA), which manipulates measurement data used in state estimation. Carefully constructed FDIAs exploit the null space of the measurement Jacobian to bypass traditional bad data detectors (BDDs).
This allows attackers to alter estimated system states without triggering residual-based alarms

Early research on FDIA primarily focused on model-based attacks, in which adversaries assume knowledge of system parameters, such as network topology and line admittances.
Under this assumption, adversaries can construct attack vectors aligned with the column space of the measurement Jacobian, preserving residual statistics and evading BDD-based detection

Subsequent work investigated techniques for reconstructing system information from measurements to relax the assumption of full model knowledge.
These methods include Independent Component Analysis (ICA)

More recent studies have explored data-driven approaches for constructing blind FDIAs that do not require explicit knowledge of system parameters.
This shift is motivated by the limited accessibility of physical system information: grid operators increasingly restrict access to sensitive infrastructure data

To address these limitations, blind FDIA methods attempt to infer system structure directly from measurements.
Examples include eigenvalue-based matrix reconstruction

[

While these techniques demonstrate that stealthy attacks can be constructed solely from measurement data, they also expose a key limitation of many existing detection strategies.
When both attack generation and detection rely primarily on numerical or data-driven analysis of the same measurements, the resulting symmetry makes it difficult to distinguish malicious perturbations from normal system variability reliably.

To expose the limitations of existing detection methods, we study a blind FDIA generated by a tailored autoencoder (AE) that learns the measurement manifold from historical data and produces perturbations aligned with the Jacobian null space. Such perturbations remain close to the learned measurement manifold and can bypass both residual-based bad data detectors (BDDs) and time-series anomaly detectors. These observations highlight the difficulty of detecting attacks using purely statistical or data-driven analysis of measurements.

To address this challenge, we propose a topology-informed detection framework, the

In DC power systems, the Cycle-Space, defined as the kernel of the incidence matrix, captures the fundamental loops whose structure is closely related to the null space of the measurement Jacobian.
By incorporating these topology-derived constraints, the proposed method introduces structural information that cannot be inferred solely from measurement data.
The method constructs a cycle basis for the network and uses cycle-consistent indicators to guide null-space estimation, enabling reliable detection even when precise line parameters are unavailable.

To the best of our knowledge, this work is the first to bridge graph-theoretic Cycle-Space theory with power system state estimation to provide a topology-informed defense against blind

II
Preliminaries

We adopt the DC state estimation formulation. The DC power grid is modeled as a graph
\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E})
where
|\mathcal{V}|=n
denotes the set of buses and
|\mathcal{E}|=m
the set of branches. Let
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\cdots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
denote the system state vector (e.g., voltage phase angles at buses), and
\mathbf{z}=(z_{1},\cdots,z_{m})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
denote the system measurement vector (e.g., active power flows on branches). The linearized DC power flow model relates them through the system Jacobian matrix
\mathbf{H}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
, which encodes network topology and measurement configuration:

\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{H}\mathbf{x}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon},

where
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
represents measurement noise due to sensor error or communication disturbances. The noise is typically modeled as zero-mean Gaussian, i.e.,
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0},\mathbf{R})
, where
\mathbf{R}=\mathrm{diag}(\sigma_{1}^{2},\cdots,\sigma_{m}^{2})
is the noise covariance matrix.
Based on such a relationship above, power system state estimation estimate the state
\hat{\mathbf{x}}
from measurements
\mathbf{z}
by solving the weighted least-squares problem:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{x}}

\displaystyle=\arg\min_{\mathbf{x}}\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{m}\frac{(z_{i}-\mathbf{H}_{i}\mathbf{x})^{2}}{\sigma_{i}^{2}}

(1)

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{H}^{T}\mathbf{R}^{-1}\mathbf{H})^{-1}\mathbf{H}^{T}\mathbf{R}^{-1}\mathbf{z},

(2)

where
\mathbf{H}_{i}
is the
i
-th row of
\mathbf{H}
. The estimated state
\hat{\mathbf{x}}
help decide critical monitoring and control decisions, making it a high-value target for adversarial attacks.
To verify the measurement data
\mathbf{z}
, a widely used approach is Bad Data Detection (BDD)
[
1
,
31
]
. BDD evaluates the consistency between observed measurements and the estimated state by examining the squared residual error:

\|\mathbf{z}-\hat{\mathbf{z}}\|_{2}^{2}=\|\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{H}\hat{\mathbf{x}}\|_{2}^{2}=\|\mathbf{S}\mathbf{z}\|_{2}^{2},

where
\mathbf{S}=\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{H}^{T}\mathbf{R}^{-1}\mathbf{H})^{-1}\mathbf{H}^{T}\mathbf{R}^{-1}
is the residual sensitivity matrix
[
1
]
.
Under the assumption of Gaussian noise, the normalized residual

\text{LNR}(\mathbf{z}):=\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{m}\frac{(\mathbf{S}_{i}\mathbf{z})^{2}}{\sigma_{i}^{2}}=\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{m}\frac{(\mathbf{S}_{i}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon})^{2}}{\sigma_{i}^{2}}\sim\chi^{2}_{m-n},\vskip-5.0pt

(3)

follows a
\chi^{2}
distribution with
m-n
degrees of freedom. This property enables a statistical detection rule: for a given significance level
\alpha
(e.g., 0.05), the residual statistic
\text{LNR}(\mathbf{z})
is compared with the threshold
\tau=\chi^{2}_{m-n,\,1-\alpha}
. If
\text{LNR}(\mathbf{z})\geq\tau
, the measurement set is flagged as containing bad data; otherwise, it is deemed consistent with the assumed noise model.

III
Blind FDIA via Measurement Manifold Learning

Traditional FDIA schemes assume knowledge of system information, in particular the Jacobian matrix
\mathbf{H}
. Under this assumption, an adversary can modify the measurement vector as
\mathbf{z}^{\prime}=\mathbf{z}+\mathbf{H}\boldsymbol{u}

[
22
]
, where
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is an arbitrarily chosen direction in the state space. Because the perturbation
\mathbf{H}\boldsymbol{u}
lies in the column space of
\mathbf{H}
, the attacked measurement preserves the residual error, i.e.,
\|\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{z}+\mathbf{H}\boldsymbol{u})\|=\|\mathbf{S}\mathbf{z}\|
, allowing the attack to remain undetected by the
bdd
(
bdd
). However, this model-based approach has two important limitations. First, access to the exact Jacobian matrix is often unrealistic in practice because such information is sensitive and typically protected by utility operators. Second, although these attacks evade
bdd
, they may still introduce abnormal measurement patterns that deviate from historical data, making them vulnerable to modern time-series anomaly detectors
[
37
]
.

To address these limitations, we construct a blind FDIA that does not require explicit knowledge of the Jacobian matrix. The key observation is that under the DC model
\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{H}\mathbf{x}
, the measurement manifold corresponds to the column space of
\mathbf{H}
. Therefore, if an attacker can learn this manifold from historical measurements, stealth attack directions can be recovered without explicit knowledge of the system model. Based on this insight, we employ a tailored autoencoder (AE) to learn the measurement manifold directly from historical system data.

To ensure that the
ae
(
ae
) faithfully captures the structure of the measurement manifold, we design its latent space dimensionality to match the number of system states
n
. While the exact Jacobian matrix
\mathbf{H}
is typically unavailable to attackers, the value of
n
can often be inferred from system metadata or estimated using model selection and dimensionality reduction techniques. This architectural choice forces the AE to encode only the minimal set of variables required to reconstruct valid measurements, aligning the learned representation with the physical notion of system states in power systems.

Moreover, the
ae
architecture naturally performs a denoising operation. Since the observed measurements follow the noisy model
\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{H}\mathbf{x}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}
, where
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}
denotes Gaussian sensor noise, the AE is incentivized to recover the signal component
\mathbf{H}\mathbf{x}
, while suppressing the noise
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}
. Similar to linear
ae
, which are mathematically equivalent to
pca
(
pca
)
[
5
]
, the latent representation captures directions of maximum variance in the data and filters out noise components. This property is useful for constructing stealthy perturbations that remain close to the learned measurement manifold.

.

ae
architecture is illustrated in Fig.

. It consists of an encoder that maps input measurements
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
to a lower-dimensional latent representation, and a decoder that reconstructs the measurement from the latent space

. The
ae

\rm{AE}(\cdot;\theta_{\text{AE}})
, where
\theta_{\text{AE}}
denotes the network parameters, is trained to minimize the reconstruction loss

\min_{\theta_{\text{AE}}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z}}\left\|\mathbf{z}-\rm{AE}\left(\mathbf{z};\theta_{\text{AE}}\right)\right\|^{2}.

(4)

After the
ae
model in
eq.

4
converges, the attacker can generate an FDIA as follows (see Fig.
2
). Given a real-time measurement
\mathbf{z}_{t}
, the
ae
reconstructs it as
\hat{\mathbf{z}}_{t}=\rm{AE}(\mathbf{z}_{t})
. The reconstruction residual

r_{t}:=\mathbf{z}_{t}-\hat{\mathbf{z}}_{t}

captures the component of
\mathbf{z}_{t}
that lies outside the learned manifold and therefore approximately lies in
\text{Col}(\mathbf{H})^{\perp}
, where
\text{Col}(\mathbf{H}):=\{\mathbf{H}\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\}
denotes the column space of
\mathbf{H}
. This orthogonal complement of
\text{Col}(\mathbf{H})
serves as a proxy for the system’s null space. The attacker then perturbs the real-time measurement along the residual direction

\mathbf{z}^{\prime}_{t}=\mathbf{z}_{t}+\kappa\cdot r_{t},

(5)

where
\kappa>0
controls the attack magnitude. Since
\hat{\mathbf{z}}_{t}
lies on the learned manifold, the perturbed measurement
\mathbf{z}^{\prime}_{t}
remains close to historical measurement patterns, reducing the risk of detection by time-series anomaly detectors. Moreover, because
r_{t}\perp\text{Col}(\mathbf{H})
, it approximately lies in the null space of the sensitivity matrix
\mathbf{S}
, i.e.,
\mathbf{S}r_{t}\approx 0
, which implies that

\mathbf{S}\mathbf{z}^{\prime}_{t}=\mathbf{S}\mathbf{z}_{t}+\kappa\mathbf{S}r_{t}\approx\mathbf{S}\mathbf{z}_{t}.

Thus, the attack preserves the residual used in BDD and successfully bypasses BDD-based detection.

To further reduce the detectability of the attack and ensure statistical indistinguishability from normal measurement noise, we add a Gaussian perturbation
\boldsymbol{\eta}_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0},\gamma\mathbf{R})
, where
\gamma\in(0,1)
controls the variance. The final injected measurement becomes

\mathbf{z}^{\prime}_{t}=\mathbf{z}_{t}+\kappa\cdot(r_{t}+\boldsymbol{\eta}_{t}).

This additional perturbation captures sensor-level variability and helps the attack remain hidden even under fine-grained time-series anomaly detection methods. Unlike traditional FDIA constructions that rely on explicit system matrices, the proposed scheme infers stealth directions directly from historical data without access to
\mathbf{H}
.

III-A

Pseudo-Null Space FDIA extended to AC Systems

While the above FDIA is formulated under the linear DC model, the underlying concept generalizes to the nonlinear AC setting, where the measurement model becomes
\mathbf{z}=\boldsymbol{h}(\mathbf{x})+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}
. In this case, due to the slack bus constraint and physical conservation laws, the image of
\boldsymbol{h}(\cdot)
forms a lower-dimensional nonlinear manifold within the measurement space. Compared to DC system based FDIA, constructing stealthy attacks in this setting requires identifying perturbations
\mathbf{c}_{p}
that satisfy
\mathbf{a}=\boldsymbol{h}(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{c}_{p})-\boldsymbol{h}(\mathbf{x})\approx 0
, thereby preserving the measurement consistency necessary to evade
ml
(
ml
)-based anomaly detection. One strategy for achieving this condition is to formulate a nonlinear optimization problem that directly minimizes the measurement discrepancy:
\min_{\mathbf{c}_{p}}\|\boldsymbol{h}(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{c}_{p})-\boldsymbol{h}(\mathbf{x})\|^{2}
. However, solving such nonlinear optimization problem can be computationally intensive and impractical for large-scale systems or real-time applications.

To address this challenge, we adopt an alternative strategy based on a first-order Taylor approximation of
\boldsymbol{h}(\cdot)
. Linearizing the measurement function around
\mathbf{x}
yields
\boldsymbol{h}(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{c}_{p})\approx\boldsymbol{h}(\mathbf{x})+\boldsymbol{J}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{c}_{p}
, where
\boldsymbol{J}(\mathbf{x})
denotes the Jacobian matrix of
\boldsymbol{h}
evaluated at
\mathbf{x}
. Stealthiness can then be enforced by selecting
\mathbf{c}_{p}\in\text{null}(\boldsymbol{J}(\mathbf{x}))
, ensuring that the perturbation lies in the local linear kernel of the measurement function and thus approximately preserves measurement consistency. This Jacobian-based method is computationally efficient, scalable, and effective when the perturbations are sufficiently small to remain within the linear approximation region. Notably, this procedure closely parallels the approach used in the DC case. The key difference lies in the fact that, under the AC model, the Jacobian
\boldsymbol{J}
must be re-evaluated at each operating point for FDIA construction, whereas the DC formulation involves a constant Jacobian
\mathbf{H}
. In practice, the update frequency of
\boldsymbol{J}
is inherently determined by the sampling rate of the system measurements.

IV
Cycle-Space-Based Detection of Data-Driven Blind
fdia

Fig. 3
:
Overview of the proposed Cycle-Space informed FDIA detection procedure.

The blind FDIA in Section
III
approximates the null space of the Jacobian matrix
\mathbf{H}^{T}
using historical measurements. While this data-driven construction enables the attack to bypass both BDDs and time-series anomaly detectors, its null space approximation is inherently imperfect. As a result, the injected perturbation does not lie exactly in the true null space, leaving a detectable footprint.
This observation motivates the proposed detection method. Existing null space-based detection schemes typically rely on purely numerical tools such as Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) to estimate the null space of
\mathbf{H}^{T}
. However, such methods use only measurement data and do not exploit the physical structure of the power network. Consequently, they provide limited advantage to the defender when the attacker also relies on data-driven null space approximation. In contrast, system operators typically have access to the network topology. We leverage this structural information to construct a more physically meaningful approximation of the null space and use it for attack detection.

In DC power systems, the non-trivial null space of the Jacobian matrix arises from cycles in the network graph. In particular, radial networks do not contain closed loops and therefore have a trivial Cycle-Space. This motivates the use of Cycle-Space theory for null space reconstruction. The resulting topology-informed approach offers two advantages. First, unlike numerical decomposition methods such as SVD, it does not rely on precise values of internal system parameters such as line admittances. Second, it naturally supports decentralized detection because each cycle can serve as an independent local consistency check. Fig.
3
provides an overview of the proposed detection framework, which consists of two components: (1) Cycle-Space informed null space extraction, and (2) decentralized attack detection.

IV-A

Cycle-Space Informed Null Space Extraction

We first introduce the graph-theoretic objects used to construct the topology-informed null space.

Definition 1

(Cycle)
.

Let
\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E})
be a connected graph with vertices (buses)
v_{i}\in\mathcal{V}
and edges (branches)
e_{i}\in\mathcal{E}
. A
cycle
is a closed walk of the form
[v_{1},e_{1},v_{2},\ldots,e_{l-1},v_{l}]
such that
v_{1}=v_{l}
and all other vertices are distinct. Equivalently, we represent a cycle by the set of edges as
\mathbf{c}=\{e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{l-1}\}
.

The dimension of the null space of a graph’s incidence matrix corresponds to the number of independent cycles in the graph. We now extend this concept to the algebraic representation of all independent cycles.

Definition 2

(Cycle basis)
.

A cycle basis
\mathcal{C}=\{\mathbf{c}_{1},\mathbf{c}_{2},\ldots,\mathbf{c}_{m-n+1}\}
is a minimal set of simple cycles that can be combined to generate every cycle in the graph. Each cycle
\mathbf{c}\in\mathcal{C}
is associated with a signed indicator vector
\zeta_{\mathbf{c}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, which encodes the orientation of an edge in the cycle relative to its orientation in the graph:

\zeta_{\mathbf{c}}(e)=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if $e\in\mathbf{c}$ and its orientations in $\mathbf{c}$}\\
&\text{and $\mathcal{G}$ are the same}\\
-1,&\text{if $e\in\mathbf{c}$ and its orientations in $\mathbf{c}$}\\
&\text{and $\mathcal{G}$ are different}\\
0,&\text{if $e\notin\mathbf{c}$ }\end{cases}

(6)

where
e\in\mathcal{E}
denotes an edge in the graph
\mathcal{G}
.

The cycle basis is generally non-unique. The cycle basis set
\mathbb{C}=\{\mathcal{C}^{(1)},\mathcal{C}^{(2)},\ldots\}
denotes the collection of all such cycle bases.

Definition 3

(Cycle-Space)
.

The Cycle-Space of the graph
\mathcal{G}
under cycle basis
\mathcal{C}
is defined as
\mathbf{N}_{c}=\zeta\odot\mathbf{p}=(\mathbf{n}^{(1)}_{c},\mathbf{n}^{(2)}_{c},\ldots,\mathbf{n}^{(m-n+1)}_{c})\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times|\mathcal{C}|}
,
where
\mathbf{p}
is the parameter of the branch (i.e., the reactance).

Since the Cycle-Space coincides with the null space of
\mathbf{H}^{T}
, i.e.,
\mathbf{N}_{c}=Null(\mathbf{H}^{T})
, each direction of
\mathbf{N}_{c}
admits:

\bar{\mathbf{Z}}^{T}\mathbf{n}^{(i)}_{c}=0

(7)

where
\bar{\mathbf{Z}}
is clean measurement data without noise and attack.

The orthogonal complement is the cut space:

\mathbf{N}_{c}^{\perp}=Range(\mathbf{H}).

(8)

Our goal is to extract the null space of the matrix
\mathbf{H}^{T}
from measurement
\mathbf{Z}
. The null space of the matrix
\mathbf{H}^{T}
is defined as
\mathbf{N}=(\mathbf{n}^{(1)},\mathbf{n}^{(2)},\ldots,\mathbf{n}^{(m-n+1)})
. Each
\mathbf{n}^{(i)}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
is a direction of the null space. The null space reconstruction problem is usually formulated as the unconstrained optimization

\displaystyle\mathbf{O}=\min_{\mathbf{N}(i)}

\displaystyle\sum\nolimits_{i}||\mathbf{Z}_{train}^{T}\cdot\mathbf{N}(i)||^{2}

(9)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle||\mathbf{n}^{(i)}||^{2}=1.

Based on Definition
3
, we reformulate the problem by constraining the null space using the cycle structure:

\displaystyle\mathbf{O}=\min_{\mathbf{N}(i)}

\displaystyle\sum\nolimits_{i}||\mathbf{Z}^{T}\cdot\mathbf{N}(i)||^{2}

(10)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle||\mathbf{N}(i)||^{2}=1,\quad\mathbf{N}=\chi_{\mathbf{c}_{i}}\odot\mathbf{N},

where
\mathbf{Z}=\{\mathbf{z}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{z}_{T_{o}}\}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times T_{o}}
contains the noisy measurements,
T_{o}\geq m
is the number of measurements considered for optimization,
\chi_{\mathbf{c}}=\text{abs}(\zeta_{\mathbf{c}})
is the unsigned indicator, and
\chi_{\mathbf{c}}\odot\mathbf{N}
regulates the dimensions of the null space spanned by each cycle, where
\odot
denotes element-wise multiplication. We define the selection
\mathbf{Z}_{\mathbf{c}_{i}}
, which selects the branch data from
\mathbf{Z}
that belongs to cycle
\mathbf{c}_{i}
, such that the dimension of the extracted results is
\mathbb{R}^{|\mathbf{c}_{i}|\times T_{o}}
.
Thus, solving this optimization problem is equivalent to optimizing each cycle separately:

\displaystyle\mathbf{O}_{i}=\min_{\mathbf{N}_{\mathbf{c}_{i}}(i)}

\displaystyle\sum\nolimits_{i}\left\|\mathbf{Z}_{\mathbf{c}_{i}}^{T}\cdot\mathbf{N}_{\mathbf{c}_{i}}(i)\right\|^{2}

(11)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle\left\|\mathbf{N}_{\mathbf{c}_{i}}(i)\right\|^{2}=1,

where
\mathbf{N}_{\mathbf{c}_{i}}(i)
is the optimization entity with dimension
\mathbb{R}^{|\mathbf{c}_{i}|\times 1}
, and
\mathbf{N}_{\mathbf{c}_{i}}(i)
is the
i
-th basis selecting branches that belong to
\mathbf{c}_{i}
. For brevity, we denote
\mathbf{N}_{\mathbf{c}_{i}}(i)
as
\mathbf{N}_{\mathbf{c}_{i}}
.

The solution of
eq.

11
is composed of the null space of each selection. Because the rank of
\mathbf{Z}_{\mathbf{c}_{i}}
is
|\mathbf{c}_{i}|-1
, there is only one null space vector. The solution is the eigenvector of
\mathbf{Z}_{\mathbf{c}_{i}}^{T}\mathbf{Z}_{\mathbf{c}_{i}}
that corresponds to the smallest eigenvalue:

\hat{\mathbf{N}}_{\mathbf{c}_{i}}(i)=\text{eigvec}_{min}(\mathbf{Z}_{\mathbf{c}_{i}}\mathbf{Z}_{\mathbf{c}_{i}}^{T})

(12)

After obtaining all
\hat{\mathbf{N}}_{\mathbf{c}_{i}}(i)
, we recover the full estimated null space,
\hat{\mathbf{N}}
, which can be directly used for
fdia
detection.
Another advantage of the Cycle-Space null space reconstruction is the minimum number of measurements required, namely the number of links of the largest cycle,
\max|\mathbf{c}|
, in contrast to other subspace learning methods, which require at least
\text{rank}(\mathbf{H})
measurements with
\max|\mathbf{c}|\leq\text{rank}(\mathbf{H})
.

By mapping the null space of the Jacobian matrix
H^{T}
directly to the Cycle-Space of the network graph, we introduce a novel structural constraint. This represents the first application of the Cycle-Space formalism, grounded in the network topology, to isolate malicious data injections in power grid systems.
Based on
eqs.

11
and
12
, we next construct a bi-level decentralized detection framework.

IV-B

Decentralized Attack Detector

The Cycle-Space construction naturally supports decentralized detection. In
eq.

11
,
\mathbf{Z}_{\mathbf{c}_{i}}
contains the measurements of the branches in cycle
\mathbf{c}_{i}
, while
\mathbf{N}_{\mathbf{c}_{i}}
is the null space basis associated with that cycle. Since the null space originates from the Cycle-Space, attack detection can also be carried out independently on each cycle. Therefore, each cycle can be assigned to a local detector that collects the corresponding branch measurements and performs detection in parallel.

Since the suspicious components lie in the null space, we project the measurements onto the estimated null space in order to extract the null space component. Let
\bar{\mathbf{Z}}=\{\bar{\mathbf{z}}_{1},\ldots,\bar{\mathbf{z}}_{T_{u}}\}
be the measurements under consideration, where
T_{u}
is the number of measurements. The null space component extracted by the detector associated with cycle

V
Experiments

This section evaluates the effectiveness of the proposed framework from several complementary perspectives.
First, we validate that the proposed autoencoder (AE) successfully learns the measurement manifold and enables the construction of stealthy blind FDIA attacks.
Second, we evaluate the ability of the proposed Cycle-Space-informed detection framework to identify such attacks and compare its performance with existing machine learning based anomaly detectors.
Third, we analyze several practical aspects of the framework, including decentralized detection coverage, extension to AC systems, and robustness to measurement noise. Finally, additional experimental results conducted under partial observability are provided in
Section

-F
.

Dataset Configuration.
We evaluate the proposed framework on several standard IEEE transmission grid test systems, including the 14-bus, 30-bus, 57-bus, and 118-bus systems. These benchmark networks cover a wide range of topological characteristics, from small and sparse networks (e.g., IEEE 14-bus) to larger and more interconnected systems (e.g., IEEE 118-bus). Such test cases are widely used in power system research to validate state estimation and cybersecurity methodologies.

For each system, time-series measurement data are generated using DC power flow simulations implemented in MATPOWER
[
36
]
. To introduce realistic temporal variability, load and generation profiles are scaled using time-series data derived from the Duquesne Light Company (Pittsburgh). Random scaling factors are applied to simulate natural fluctuations in system demand. In addition, Gaussian white noise with a standard deviation of
0.02
p.u. is added to all measurements to emulate realistic sensor noise levels commonly encountered in operational power systems.

Implementation Details.
For constructing the blind FDIA attack, we employ the autoencoder architecture described in Section
III
. The AE learns the measurement manifold directly from historical measurement data. The input to the AE consists of active power flow measurements across all transmission branches of the grid. For example, in the IEEE 14-bus system containing 20 branches, the input vector dimension is 20. The AE output mirrors the input and the network is trained to minimize the mean squared reconstruction error.

To ensure that the AE captures the intrinsic system behavior while filtering out measurement noise, the latent dimension is set equal to the number of independent system states. Under the DC model, this corresponds to the number of buses minus one (excluding the slack bus). For instance, the latent dimension of the IEEE 14-bus system is set to 13. This design allows the AE to learn the low-dimensional physical subspace defined by the power flow equations while suppressing noise components. Both the encoder and decoder consist of five fully connected layers with approximately ten neurons each using
relu
(
relu
) activations.

The AE is trained for up to 300 epochs using the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of
2\times 10^{-4}
and a batch size of 50. When adversarial modules such as generators and discriminators are involved, they are updated every five iterations to maintain stable convergence. All simulations are performed using MATLAB 2022b for power flow generation and Python 3.12 for AE training and attack construction. The experiments are conducted on a workstation equipped with an Intel Core i7 2.2 GHz processor and 16 GB of RAM.

VI
Conclusion and future work

This paper investigates blind false data injection attacks (FDIAs) and corresponding detection mechanisms in power system state estimation. We first propose a data-driven FDIA construction method based on an autoencoder (AE) that learns the measurement manifold directly from historical data. By exploiting the orthogonal direction of this manifold, the attack can generate stealthy perturbations that bypass traditional bad data detection (BDD) without requiring prior knowledge of system parameters. Numerical results demonstrate that the proposed AE-based attack achieves higher BDD bypass rates than several existing blind FDIA approaches.
To counter such attacks, we develop a topology-aware
csd
based on the Cycle-Space of the network, which is shown to achieve optimal generalization error among Cycle-Space estimators associated with the attack detection.
A primary contribution of this research is the first use of Cycle-Space theory in the context of cyber-physical security.
This structural approach provides a fundamental advantage over purely data-driven detectors by grounding security in the invariant physical topology of the grid.
By leveraging graph structural information, the proposed method reconstructs the system null space without relying on precise line parameters and enables decentralized detection across network cycles.
Experimental results on multiple IEEE benchmark systems show that the proposed detector consistently identifies the proposed blind FDIA, as well as competitive/existing blind FDIAs, and outperforms several machine-learning-based anomaly detectors while maintaining robustness under measurement noise.
As a supporting analytical result, we derive a first-order finite-sample expression for the expected generalization error of the null space estimator, which we use to establish the optimality of the Minimum Cycle Basis among cycle-basis-based estimators.
The expression depends only on
\text{rank}(\mathbf{H})
, sample size, and noise variance, and applies to subspace estimation beyond power systems.

Future work will focus on expanding the applicability of the Cycle-Space formalism, particularly in topology estimation and in the optimization of moving target defense strategies.

When the noise std
\sigma
is small, we may safely disregard the second-order term
\mathcal{E}^{T}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
.

Also:

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{n}+\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{n}+\boldsymbol{\epsilon})^{T}(\mathbf{n}+\boldsymbol{\epsilon})

(A.11)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{n}^{T}\mathbf{n}+2\mathbf{n}^{T}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}+\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{T}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}

(A.12)

\displaystyle=1+2\mathbf{n}^{T}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}+\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{T}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}

(A.13)

\displaystyle=1

(A.14)

Disregarding the second order term
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{T}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
, we have
\mathbf{n}^{T}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}=0
.
So the optimization problem becomes:

\min_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}\frac{1}{2}\|\bar{\mathbf{Z}}^{T}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}+\mathcal{E}^{T}\mathbf{n}\|^{2}\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\mathbf{n}^{T}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}=0

(A.15)

Consider the following Lagrangian:

\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\epsilon},\mu)=\frac{1}{2}(\bar{\mathbf{Z}}^{T}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}+\mathcal{E}^{T}\mathbf{n})^{T}(\bar{\mathbf{Z}}^{T}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}+\mathcal{E}^{T}\mathbf{n})+\mu(\mathbf{n}^{T}\boldsymbol{\epsilon})

(A.16)

Taking the derivative with respect to
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
(First-Order Condition):

\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}=\bar{\mathbf{Z}}(\bar{\mathbf{Z}}^{T}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}+\mathcal{E}^{T}\mathbf{n})+\mu\mathbf{n}=0

(A.17)

\bar{\mathbf{Z}}\bar{\mathbf{Z}}^{T}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}+\bar{\mathbf{Z}}\mathcal{E}^{T}\mathbf{n}+\mu\mathbf{n}=0

(A.18)

Solving for the Lagrange Multiplier
\mu
, we left-multiply by
\mathbf{n}^{T}
:

\mathbf{n}^{T}\bar{\mathbf{Z}}\bar{\mathbf{Z}}^{T}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}+\mathbf{n}^{T}\bar{\mathbf{Z}}\mathcal{E}^{T}\mathbf{n}+\mu(\mathbf{n}^{T}\mathbf{n})=0

(A.19)

Applying the following properties: 1) Orthogonality: Since
\bar{\mathbf{Z}}^{T}\mathbf{n}=0
, taking the transpose gives
\mathbf{n}^{T}\bar{\mathbf{Z}}=0
. 2) Unit Vector:
\mathbf{n}^{T}\mathbf{n}=1
. Substituting these expressions into the above equation:

(0)\bar{\mathbf{Z}}^{T}\mathbf{n}+(0)\mathcal{E}^{T}\mathbf{n}+\mu(1)=0

(A.20)

\implies\mu=0

(A.21)

Substituting
\mu=0
back into
eq.

A.18
:

\bar{\mathbf{Z}}\bar{\mathbf{Z}}^{T}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}+\bar{\mathbf{Z}}\mathcal{E}^{T}\mathbf{n}=0

(A.22)

\bar{\mathbf{Z}}\bar{\mathbf{Z}}^{T}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}=-\bar{\mathbf{Z}}\mathcal{E}^{T}\mathbf{n}

(A.23)

Finally, we now isolate
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
. The matrix
\bar{\mathbf{Z}}\bar{\mathbf{Z}}^{T}
may not be invertible (singular), so we use the Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse, denoted as
(\bar{\mathbf{Z}}\bar{\mathbf{Z}}^{T})^{+}
.

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\epsilon}=-(\bar{\mathbf{Z}}\bar{\mathbf{Z}}^{T})^{+}\bar{\mathbf{Z}}\mathcal{E}^{T}\mathbf{n}+(I_{m}-(\bar{\mathbf{Z}}\bar{\mathbf{Z}}^{T})^{+}\bar{\mathbf{Z}}\bar{\mathbf{Z}}^{T})y,

(A.24)

\displaystyle y\quad\text{arbitrary}\in R^{m}

(A.25)

Since
(I_{m}-(\bar{\mathbf{Z}}\bar{\mathbf{Z}}^{T})^{+}\bar{\mathbf{Z}}\bar{\mathbf{Z}}^{T})y
lies in the null space of
\bar{\mathbf{Z}}^{T}
, we disregard this term and
(\bar{\mathbf{Z}}\bar{\mathbf{Z}}^{T})^{+}\bar{\mathbf{Z}}=(\bar{\mathbf{Z}}^{T})^{+}
.

Thus, the simplified first-order solution for
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
is

\boldsymbol{\epsilon}=-(\bar{\mathbf{Z}}^{T})^{+}\mathcal{E}^{T}\mathbf{n}

(A.26)

∎

Expected Generalization Error

Theorem 2

(First Order Finite Sample Expected Generalization Error)
.

The first-order finite sample expected generalization error is given by:

\boxed{E_{\text{gen}}=\sigma^{2}(1+\frac{\mathrm{rank}(\mathbf{H})}{T_{o}-n})}

(A.27)

Proof.

In
eq.

16
, the Expected Generalization Error is:

E_{gen}=\sigma^{2}+\lim_{T_{\star}\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{Z}}[\|\mathbf{Z}_{\star}^{T}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\|^{2}+2\mathbf{n}^{T}\mathcal{E}_{\star}\mathcal{E}_{\star}^{T}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}]}{T_{\star}}

(A.28)

Plugging
eq.

17
into
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{Z}}[\mathbf{n}^{T}\mathcal{E}_{\star}\mathcal{E}_{\star}^{T}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}]
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{Z}}[\mathbf{n}^{T}\mathcal{E}_{\star}\mathcal{E}_{\star}^{T}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}]

(A.29)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{Z}}[\mathbf{n}^{T}\mathcal{E}_{\star}\mathcal{E}_{\star}^{T}(\bar{\mathbf{Z}}^{+})^{T}\mathcal{E}^{T}\mathbf{n}]

(A.30)

\displaystyle=Tr(\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{Z}}[\mathcal{E}_{\star}\mathcal{E}_{\star}^{T}(\bar{\mathbf{Z}}^{+})^{T}\mathcal{E}^{T}\mathbf{n}\mathbf{n}^{T}])

(A.31)

\displaystyle=Tr(\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{Z}}[\mathcal{E}_{\star}\mathcal{E}_{\star}^{T}]\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{Z}}[(\bar{\mathbf{Z}}^{+})^{T}]\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{Z}}[\mathcal{E}^{T}\mathbf{n}\mathbf{n}^{T}])

(A.32)

Because
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{Z}}[\mathcal{E}^{T}\mathbf{n}\mathbf{n}^{T}]=0_{T_{o}\times m}
,
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{Z}}[\mathbf{n}^{T}\mathcal{E}_{\star}\mathcal{E}_{\star}^{T}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}]=0
.

Expanding
\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{Z}}[\|\mathbf{Z}_{\star}^{T}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\|^{2}]}{T_{\star}}
:

\displaystyle\frac{\|\mathbf{Z}_{\star}^{T}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\|^{2}}{T_{\star}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\|(\bar{\mathbf{Z}}_{\star}+\mathcal{E}_{\star})^{T}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\|^{2}}{T_{\star}}

(A.33)

\displaystyle=\frac{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{T}(\bar{\mathbf{Z}}_{\star}+\mathcal{E}_{\star})(\bar{\mathbf{Z}}_{\star}+\mathcal{E}_{\star})^{T}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}{T_{\star}}

(A.34)

\displaystyle=\frac{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{T}(\bar{\mathbf{Z}}_{\star}\bar{\mathbf{Z}}_{\star}^{T}+\mathcal{E}_{\star}\bar{\mathbf{Z}}_{\star}^{T}+\bar{\mathbf{Z}}_{\star}\mathcal{E}_{\star}^{T}+\mathcal{E}_{\star}\mathcal{E}_{\star}^{T})\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}{T_{\star}}

(A.35)

\displaystyle=\frac{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{T}(\mathbf{H}\mathbf{X}_{\star}\mathbf{X}_{\star}^{T}\mathbf{H}^{T}+T_{\star}I_{m}\sigma^{2})\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}{T_{\star}}

(A.36)

Plugging
eq.

17
into
\sigma^{2}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{T}I_{m}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
:

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{T}I_{m}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}=\sigma^{2}\mathbf{n}^{T}\mathcal{E}\bar{\mathbf{Z}}^{+}(\bar{\mathbf{Z}}^{+})^{T}\mathcal{E}^{T}\mathbf{n}

(A.37)

Since:

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{T}I_{m}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}=\sigma^{4}\frac{Tr((\mathbf{H}\mathbf{H}^{T})^{+})}{T_{o}-n-1}=\mathcal{O}(\sigma^{4}\frac{\|\mathbf{H}^{+}\|^{2}}{T_{o}-n-1})

(A.38)

Since
\lim_{T_{\star}\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\mathbf{X}_{\star}\mathbf{X}_{\star}^{T}}{T_{\star}}=I_{n}
:

\lim_{T_{\star}\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\|\mathbf{Z}_{\star}^{T}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\|^{2}}{T_{\star}}=\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{T}\mathbf{H}\mathbf{H}^{T}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}+\mathcal{O}(\sigma^{4}\frac{\|\mathbf{H}^{+}\|^{2}}{T_{o}-n-1})

(A.39)

Using the cyclic property of the trace
[
32
]
:

\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{T}\mathbf{H}\mathbf{H}^{T}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}=\mathrm{Tr}(\mathbf{H}\mathbf{H}^{T}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{T}).

(A.40)

Taking expectation:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{Z}}[\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{T}\mathbf{H}\mathbf{H}^{T}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}]=\mathrm{Tr}\left(\mathbf{H}\mathbf{H}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{Z}}[\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{T}]\right)

(A.41)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}\left(\mathbf{H}\mathbf{H}^{T}\mathrm{Cov}(\boldsymbol{\epsilon})\right).

(A.42)

Using the covariance result in
corollary

1
:

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{Z}}[\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{T}\mathbf{H}\mathbf{H}^{T}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}]=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{T_{o}-n}\mathrm{Tr}\left(\mathbf{H}\mathbf{H}^{T}(\mathbf{H}\mathbf{H}^{T})^{+}\right).

(A.43)

Since
\mathbf{H}\mathbf{H}^{T}(\mathbf{H}\mathbf{H}^{T})^{+}=P_{\mathrm{Col}(\mathbf{H})}
which is the projection matrix on the column space of
\mathbf{H}

[
30
]
, its trace equals the rank:

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{Z}}[\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{T}\mathbf{H}\mathbf{H}^{T}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}]=\frac{\sigma^{2}\,\mathrm{rank}(\mathbf{H})}{T_{o}-n}.

(A.44)

So the Expected Generalization Error is :

E_{\text{gen}}=\sigma^{2}(1+\frac{\mathrm{rank}(\mathbf{H})}{T_{o}-n})+\mathcal{O}(\sigma^{4}\frac{\|\mathbf{H}^{+}\|^{2}}{(T_{o}-n-1)T_{o}}).

(A.45)

∎

Covariance of the First-Order Estimation Error

Corollary 1

(Covariance of the First-Order Estimation Error)
.

Under the setting of
2
, assume

\bar{\mathbf{Z}}=\mathbf{H}\mathbf{X}
with the columns of
\mathbf{X}
drawn i.i.d. from

\mathcal{N}(0,I_{n})
,
\mathrm{rank}(\mathbf{H})=n-1
, and
T_{o}>n
.
Then
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
has zero mean and covariance

\mathrm{Cov}(\boldsymbol{\epsilon})\;=\;\frac{\sigma^{2}}{T_{o}-n}\,(\mathbf{H}\mathbf{H}^{T})^{+}.

(A.46)

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{A}=-(\bar{\mathbf{Z}}^{T})^{+}
. Then (
A.10
) gives

\boldsymbol{\epsilon}=\mathbf{A}\,\mathcal{E}^{T}\mathbf{n}.

(A.47)

Since the entries of
\mathcal{E}
are i.i.d.
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
and
\mathbf{n}

is a deterministic unit vector,

\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{E}}\left[\mathcal{E}^{T}\mathbf{n}\,\mathbf{n}^{T}\mathcal{E}\right]=\sigma^{2}I_{T_{o}}.

(A.48)

Because
\bar{\mathbf{Z}}
and
\mathcal{E}
are independent and
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{E}]=0
, the mean

\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{\epsilon}]=0
, and

\mathrm{Cov}(\boldsymbol{\epsilon})=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{A}\,\sigma^{2}I_{T_{o}}\,\mathbf{A}^{T}\right]=\sigma^{2}\,\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^{T}\right].

(A.49)

Note:

\mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^{T}=(\bar{\mathbf{Z}}^{T})^{+}\big((\bar{\mathbf{Z}}^{T})^{+}\big)^{T}=(\bar{\mathbf{Z}}\bar{\mathbf{Z}}^{T})^{+}.

(A.50)

By plugging (
A.50
) into (
A.49
), we get:

\mathrm{Cov}(\boldsymbol{\epsilon})=\sigma^{2}\,\mathbb{E}\left[(\bar{\mathbf{Z}}\bar{\mathbf{Z}}^{T})^{+}\right].

(A.51)

Since
\bar{\mathbf{Z}}=\mathbf{H}\mathbf{X}
, we have

\bar{\mathbf{Z}}\bar{\mathbf{Z}}^{T}=\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{T})\mathbf{H}^{T}.

(A.52)

Take the SVD
\mathbf{H}=\mathbf{U}_{r}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{r}\mathbf{V}_{r}^{T}
with

\mathbf{U}_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times(n-1)}
,
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{(n-1)\times(n-1)}

diagonal and invertible, and
\mathbf{V}_{r}^{T}\mathbf{V}_{r}=I_{n-1}
. Let

\mathcal{W}=\mathbf{V}_{r}^{T}\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{(n-1)\times T_{o}}
. Since the columns of
\mathbf{X}

are i.i.d.
\mathcal{N}(0,I_{n})
and
\mathbf{V}_{r}^{T}\mathbf{V}_{r}=I_{n-1}
, the
columns of
\mathcal{W}
are i.i.d.
\mathcal{N}(0,I_{n-1})
, so

\mathcal{W}\mathcal{W}^{T}\sim\mathrm{Wishart}_{n-1}(T_{o},I_{n-1})
and is
invertible almost surely for

\bar{\mathbf{Z}}\bar{\mathbf{Z}}^{T}=\mathbf{U}_{r}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{r}\,\mathcal{W}\mathcal{W}^{T}\,\mathbf{\Sigma}_{r}\mathbf{U}_{r}^{T},

(A.53)

and, since

The inverse-Wishart mean

gives

Plugging (
A.55

(
A.54

\mathbb{E}\left[(\bar{\mathbf{Z}}\bar{\mathbf{Z}}^{T})^{+}\right]=\frac{1}{T_{o}-n}\,\mathbf{U}_{r}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{r}^{-2}\mathbf{U}_{r}^{T}=\frac{1}{T_{o}-n}\,(\mathbf{H}\mathbf{H}^{T})^{+},

(A.56)

where the last equality uses the SVD form
(\mathbf{H}\mathbf{H}^{T})^{+}=\mathbf{U}_{r}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{r}^{-2}\mathbf{U}_{r}^{T}
.
By plugging (
A.56
) into (

(
A.46
).

Partial observability analysis

In network monitoring, communication latencies or sensor failures often result in partial observability, where specific meter information is lost.
This degradation in data quality directly impacts the detector’s performance, as the efficacy of attack detection relies on the precision of null space estimation.
The null space is fundamentally characterized by the network’s cycle-space. When link measurements are missing, the corresponding cycles are broken, leading to an incomplete cycle-space, which in turn diminishes the accuracy of the null space estimation.
According to the generalization error defined in
eq.

18
, the estimation error for a cycle basis is linearly correlated with the cycle length.
Consequently, shorter cycles yield higher estimation accuracy.
Therefore, the loss of these small, high-fidelity cycles may reduce the detector’s ability to accurately characterize the null space.

The impact of partial observability on the
csd
performance can be categorized into the following scenarios:

((a))

Partially observed IEEE 14-bus system: unobserved links (dashed) are shown for a large cycle.

((b))

Partially observed IEEE 14-bus system: unobserved links (dashed) are shown for a small cycle.

((c))

F_{1}
scores (colored heatmap) in the attack-magnitude vs. noise plane for the IEEE 14-bus system.
csd
performance curves corresponding to
F_{1}
=0.8 are shown for comparison: full observability baseline (red dashed), missed small cycle (white dashed), and missed large cycle (black dashed).

Fig. 11
:
Partial observability analysis on IEEE-14 bus system

1.

Localized in Missing Cycles: If the attack is applied exclusively within the broken (missing) cycles, it becomes mathematically undetectable.

2.

Localized in Remaining Cycles: If the attack is confined to the observable cycles, detection remains feasible.

3.

Uniformly Distributed Attack: When an attack is applied uniformly across all links, performance may either improve or degrade depending on the specific cycles lost as follows:

•

Loss of Large Cycles: Performance may counterintuitively improve, as the estimation avoids the high generalization errors associated with longer cycles.

•

Loss of Small Cycles: Performance may degrade, potentially substantially, if some of the most accurate components of the null space estimation are not captured.

While scenarios 1) and 2) are deterministic, scenario 3) demonstrates that the quality of detection under partial observability may be influenced by the topological properties of the remaining sub-graph.

The impact of partial observability on detection performance is illustrated in Figs.
11(a)
and
11(b)
, which depict missed links (dashed) pertaining to large and small cycles, respectively.
As shown in Fig.
11(c)
, the resulting
csd
performance curves for missed small cycle (white dashed) and missed large cycle (black dashed) scenarios are evaluated against a full observability baseline (red dashed).
As expected, the unobservability of a small cycle (white dashed) may lead to a noticeable reduction in detector efficacy, with the performance curve falling below the baseline due to the loss of the high-fidelity information inherent in shorter cycles. In contrast, breaking a large cycle (black dashed) counterintuitively improves performance above the baseline, suggesting that excluding the higher generalization errors associated with longer cycles can refine the overall accuracy of the null space estimation.
