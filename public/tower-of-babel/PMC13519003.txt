Risk-sensitive joint inventory-maintenance strategy for bearing health management under prognostic uncertainty: an uncertainty-aware deep reinforcement learning approach
Authors: Zehua Zhang, Ning Shen, Xingfen Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Traditional separation of inventory management and Prognostics and Health Management (PHM) often leads to resource misallocation. While Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) offers a promising solution for joint decision-making, standard agents typically treat Prognostics and Health Management predictions as deterministic ground truths. However, in real-world scenarios, remaining useful life (RUL) predictions inherently contain stochastic errors. Ignoring this uncertainty leads to risk-blind policies that fail to buffer against sudden failures when prediction confidence is low. To address this, this paper proposes an Uncertainty-Aware collaborative adaptive inventory strategy. First, we introduce a Bayesian uncertainty quantification mechanism using Monte Carlo Dropout to estimate not only the RUL value but also its prediction variance. Second, to overcome the agent’s myopic behavior, a novel Asymmetric Cost-Aware Reward Shaping mechanism is designed. By strategically decoupling the training and evaluation reward functions—specifically by introducing safety stock penalties and attenuating holding costs during training—the agent is guided to establish robust inventory buffers against supply chain uncertainties. Simulation results demonstrate that the proposed Risk-Sensitive PPO strategy significantly outperforms deterministic baselines, reducing total costs by 40.3% under high-noise environments.

Introduction

In the evolution of Industry 4.0 and smart manufacturing, the coordinated scheduling of predictive maintenance for equipment and spare parts supply chains has become critical to reducing total lifecycle costs1. As core components of high-end equipment, rolling bearings often incur downtime losses far exceeding the value of the spare parts themselves when sudden failures occur. Therefore, how to ensure high system availability while precisely controlling the capital tied up in spare parts inventory represents a pressing challenge for both academia and industry2.

From Static Rules to DRL Decision-Making Traditional spare parts inventory management has largely relied on statistical assumptions, such as the classic (s, S) strategy3 or the METRIC model4. This approach assumes failures follow a Poisson distribution, but often fails when confronted with time-varying failure rates caused by equipment aging. With the advancement of condition monitoring technology, Pieterjan Dendauw et al.5 proposed a joint optimization model for condition-based maintenance and inventory, demonstrating that utilizing PHM information can significantly outperform periodic inventory strategies. However, existing joint optimization research still has limitations. Van Houtum et al.6 noted that under complex constraints such as multi-component coupling and supplier lead time fluctuations, static rules struggle to find globally optimal solutions and cannot address demand surges triggered by “simultaneous aging of multiple devices.” To address dynamic decision-making in complex systems, some scholars employ system dynamics analysis for macro-level fluctuations7 or utilize multi-agent simulations for decentralized decision-making8,9. However, these approaches often lack sufficient granularity when handling the randomness of individual equipment lifetimes.

In recent years, deep reinforcement learning (DRL) has been introduced into supply chain optimization due to its powerful feature extraction and sequential decision-making capabilities. Boute et al.10 pointed out that DRL can directly learn optimal policies from high-dimensional state spaces, representing a future direction in inventory control. Empirical research by Gijsbrechts et al.11 demonstrated that DRL significantly outperforms classical operations research heuristic algorithms in handling dual-source procurement and multi-level inventory problems. Zhu et al.12 further applied DRL to enhance computational efficiency in joint optimization of spare parts ordering, validating its potential for reducing total costs. Shengang et al.13 proposed an improved deep reinforcement learning algorithm based on the actor-critic framework with stochastic policies, demonstrating the algorithm’s strong scalability.

However, applying DRL to long-term spare parts management presents unique challenges. As noted by Fang et al.14, without effective guidance, agents tend to prioritize immediate cost savings (avoiding holding costs) over long-term reliability, leading to a “myopic” policy that fails to cope with sudden demand shocks. Furthermore, existing studies often overlook the impact of in-transit inventory on decision timeliness. This paper addresses these gaps by proposing a Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) framework enhanced with an asymmetric reward shaping mechanism. Moreover, most joint decision-making models assume that the RUL information provided by PHM systems is a deterministic value with 100% accuracy. In reality, data-driven PHM models contain epistemic and stochastic uncertainties, meaning the predicted RUL represents a probability distribution rather than a single point value. When RUL predictions fluctuate or exhibit low confidence, standard deterministic agents may adopt aggressive inventory reduction strategies. Should bearing failures occur earlier than anticipated, this could lead to severe stockouts. Therefore, equipping deep reinforcement learning agents with the ability to perceive uncertainty and make risk-sensitive decisions has become a critical challenge.

This paper addresses the aforementioned issues by integrating Yang D’s reward shaping theory15 to propose an adaptive inventory strategy for bearing health status based on deep reinforcement learning and asymmetric cost perception. We construct a full-link joint decision-making model and establish a digital twin simulation environment incorporating physical degradation and logistics turnover. By incorporating the in-transit inventory vector into the state space, the agent gains temporal awareness of future supply. An asymmetric cost-aware reward shaping mechanism is proposed, addressing the DRL training convergence challenge identified by De Moor et al.16 through a separated training and evaluation asymmetric reward function. Introducing safety stock penalties during training while strategically weakening holding costs corrects the agent’s short-sighted behavior.

While Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) combined with reward shaping has been explored in inventory management, and Monte Carlo Dropout is a well-established technique for uncertainty quantification in deep learning, their synergistic integration within a joint inventory-maintenance framework under prognostic uncertainty remains unexplored. This paper demonstrates that this integration yields emergent risk-adaptive behaviors, such as dynamically modulating safety stock levels based on prediction confidence—that neither component can achieve in isolation. Through systematic ablation studies, we characterize these emergent properties and show that they translate into substantial economic gains and operational reliability.

Problem description and system modeling

The core of this study is a production system comprising N critical pieces of equipment, each fitted with a key bearing monitored by a PHM system. The system incorporates a central spare parts warehouse responsible for unified inventory ordering and management, while also overseeing maintenance decisions for critical equipment and spare parts procurement decisions. The system’s decision cycle operates at discrete time steps t.

Key assumptions

To ensure the physical authenticity of the model and focus on decision optimization problems, this paper proposes the following key assumptions based on the statistical characteristics of the IEEE PHM 2012 Prognostic Challenge dataset:

Degradation model assumption: The true bearing life L does not follow a normal distribution but instead follows a three-parameter Weibull distribution. Its probability density function is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:f\left(t\right)=\frac{k}{\lambda\:}(\frac{t}{\lambda\:}{)}^{k-1}{e}^{-(\frac{t}{\lambda\:}{)}^{k}}$$\end{document}, where the shape parameter k and scale parameter λ are calibrated based on real data to simulate the strong randomness of failures.

Observation error assumption: The agent cannot obtain the true remaining useful life \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:RU{L}_{true}$$\end{document} but only observes a noisy prediction \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\widehat{RUL}$$\end{document}. The prediction error ε follows a truncated normal distribution, and the prediction confidence gradually increases as the end of life approaches.

Supply chain constraint assumption: Supplier capacity is infinite but subject to response delays. Lead time LT is a random variable following a log-normal distribution. Unfulfilled orders cannot be canceled and accumulate in the “in-transit pipeline.”

Maintenance property assumption: Replacement of spare parts constitutes perfect repair, restoring equipment to new condition. The new lifetime is resampled from a Weibull distribution.

Uncertainty quantification via Monte Carlo dropout

To obtain the uncertainty metric \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\sigma\:}_{t}$$\end{document}, this paper employs the Monte Carlo Dropout technique during the inference phase of the RUL predictor. Specifically, we perform K stochastic forward passes with dropout enabled. The uncertainty is quantified by the standard deviation of these K predictions: 9\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mu\:}_{t}=\frac{1}{K}\sum\:_{k=1}^{K}\:{\widehat{y}}_{k},{\sigma\:}_{t}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{K}\sum\:_{k=1}^{K}\:({\widehat{y}}_{k}-{\mu\:}_{t}{)}^{2}}$$\end{document}

This \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\sigma\:}_{t}$$\end{document} serves as a reliable indicator of prediction risk, which is then fed into the PPO agent.

To implement the Bayesian uncertainty quantification, we set the dropout rate p = 0.1 and perform K = 20 stochastic forward passes during each inference step. This allows the model to approximate the posterior distribution of the Remaining Useful Life. The standard deviation σ derived from these 20 samples serves as the ‘uncertainty feature’, providing the DRL agent with a confidence measure for the prognostic information.

PPO optimization algorithm based on asymmetric cost perception

In joint decision-making MDP models, the state space encompasses continuous RUL predictions and high-dimensional in-transit inventory vectors, while the action space exhibits multi-discrete combinatorial characteristics. Traditional value-function-based algorithms like DQN face challenges of action space exponential explosion and convergence instability when addressing such problems.

To address this, this paper employs a policy gradient-based Nearest Policy Optimization algorithm as the core solver. It further designs an asymmetric cost-aware reward shaping mechanism to resolve the inherent contradiction in inventory management between sparse failure rewards and short-sighted holding costs.

PPO algorithm and actor-critic network architecture

Criteria for algorithm selection

The training process of the agent follows the standard Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) iterative procedure. PPO is an online deep reinforcement learning algorithm that adopts the Actor-Critic architecture17. PPO is selected over value-based methods (e.g., DQN) for two reasons: (1) It effectively handles the multi-discrete action space without exponential complexity18; (2) Its clipped objective function ensures stable convergence in environments with high stochasticity19 (Fig. 3).

Asymmetric cost perception reward shaping mechanism

In standard reinforcement learning, the agent receives a negative reward equal to the financial cost. However, failure penalties are “low-probability, high-impact” events, while holding costs are certain and immediate penalties. A naive agent will quickly discover that ordering nothing maximizes immediate returns, yet fails to connect current inventory operations with future failure risks due to delivery cycle time lags. Directly using the negative value of financial costs as the reward signal \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{R}_{t}=-{C}_{real,t}$$\end{document} is a common practice in inventory management DRL scenarios20. However, under long lead times and high reliability conditions, this symmetric reward design induces severe myopia. Agents discover that not ordering immediately saves holding costs and ordering fees, while failure penalties are low-probability delayed events. Consequently, agents tend to converge to the local optimum of “zero inventory.” This results in extremely low routine operational costs, but leads to substantial losses when random failures occur, as the system shuts down due to a lack of spare parts.

This paper proposes a training-and-evaluation-separated asymmetric reward mechanism, as shown in Fig. 5.The structure introduces a holding cost attenuation coefficient and a safety stock penalty during Training. This essentially reshapes the optimization landscape, creating a dense reward signal that encourages the agent to explore states with healthy inventory levels without being immediately punished by holding costs. The reward function reverts to the strict financial logic to objectively assess economic performance during Evaluation. It is important to distinguish the roles of the two core modules: reward shaping provides a behavioral bias toward safety by attenuating short-term costs and penalizing low inventory, while uncertainty awareness provides perceptual information about the reliability of RUL predictions. In isolation, RS alone encourages inventory building but cannot distinguish between reliable and unreliable predictions, leading to either excessive or insufficient buffers. UA alone perceives risk but lacks the behavioral mechanism to act on it. Their combination enables the agent to dynamically adjust its risk tolerance—acting conservatively when predictions are uncertain and efficiently when they are reliable. This emergent adaptive behavior is the key scientific contribution of our work.

Training reward function

During the training phase, reward-shaping techniques guide the agent to establish a safety stock, represented by the following function: 10\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{R}_{train,t}=-\left[(1-\lambda\:)\cdot\:{C}_{holding}+{C}_{order}+{C}_{maint}+{P}_{safety}\left({I}_{t}\right)\right]$$\end{document}

This function weakens the holding cost by introducing a coefficient λ ∈ [0.9,1.0]. During the early training phase, it significantly reduces the weight assigned to holding costs. This approach eliminates the agent’s psychological resistance to building inventory, encouraging it to stockpile goods proactively before failures occur. Additionally, by introducing a nonlinear virtual penalty term \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mathcal{P}}_{safety}$$\end{document}, the agent receives significant negative feedback whenever inventory falls below the safety threshold. This mechanism compels the agent to treat “maintaining minimum inventory” as its highest-priority survival rule.

11\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mathcal{P}}_{safety}\left({I}_{t}\right)=\left\{\begin{array}{c}\kappa\:\cdot\:({I}_{safe}-{I}_{t}{)}^{2},\hspace{0.25em}\hspace{0.25em}\hspace{0.25em}\hspace{0.25em}if{\:\:I}_{t}<{I}_{safe}\\\:0,\hspace{0.25em}\hspace{0.25em}\hspace{0.25em}\hspace{0.25em}else\hspace{0.25em}\hspace{0.25em}\hspace{0.25em}\hspace{0.25em}\end{array}\right.$$\end{document}

Among these, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{I}_{safe}$$\end{document} represents the preset safety baseline, and κ denotes the strong penalty coefficient.

Evaluate the reward function

During the model validation and testing phase, all shaping mechanisms introduced during training must be removed to restore the true financial logic of the physical world, thereby enabling an objective evaluation of the strategy’s economic performance. The reward function \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{R}_{eval,t}$$\end{document} in the evaluation phase is defined as the negative value of the system’s actual total cost \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{C}_{real,t}$$\end{document} generated at time t: 12\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{R}_{eval,t}=-{C}_{real,t}=-({C}_{holding}+{C}_{order}+{C}_{maint}+{C}_{failure})$$\end{document}

The specific physical meaning and calculation logic of inventory holding costs are as follows: 13\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{C}_{holding,t}={c}_{h}\times\:{I}_{t}$$\end{document}

\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{c}_{h}$$\end{document} represents the daily storage cost per unit of spare parts, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{I}_{t}$$\end{document} denotes the actual inventory level at the current time. During the training phase, this cost was masked by the coefficient λ to induce the agent to maintain high inventory levels. However, in the evaluation phase, this cost is fully accounted for. This implies that the agent incurs significant daily expenses by sustaining high safety stock levels.

The ordering cost is expressed as follows: 14\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{C}_{order,t}={c}_{o}\times\:\mathbb{I}({a}_{order,t}=1)$$\end{document} where K represents the fixed logistics and administrative cost per order, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathbb{I}(.)$$\end{document} denotes an indicative function. This ordering cost incentivizes agents to prefer bulk ordering to spread fixed costs.

The preventive maintenance costs are as follows: 15\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{C}_{maint,t}={c}_{pm}\times\:\sum\:_{i=1}^{N}\:{a}_{maint,t}^{i}$$\end{document}

\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{c}_{pm}$$\end{document} represents the cost of proactively replacing spare parts, which is typically far lower than the cost of repairing failures. This cost incentivizes agents to proactively consume inventory for maintenance when the RUL approaches the threshold.

Failure and downtime costs represent the greatest risk within a system, determined jointly by equipment condition and inventory levels: 16\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{C}_{failure,t}=\sum\:_{i\in\:\mathrm{F}\mathrm{a}\mathrm{i}\mathrm{l}\mathrm{u}\mathrm{r}\mathrm{e}\mathrm{s}}\:\left\{\begin{array}{c}{c}_{cm},\hspace{0.25em}\hspace{0.25em}\hspace{0.25em}\hspace{0.25em}if\:{I}_{t}>0\\\:{c}_{cm}+{c}_{down},\hspace{0.25em}\hspace{0.25em}\hspace{0.25em}\hspace{0.25em}if\:{I}_{t}=0\hspace{0.25em}\hspace{0.25em}\hspace{0.25em}\hspace{0.25em}\hspace{0.25em}\hspace{0.25em}\hspace{0.25em}\hspace{0.25em}\hspace{0.25em}\hspace{0.25em}\hspace{0.25em}\hspace{0.25em}\end{array}\right.$$\end{document}

Here, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{c}_{cm}$$\end{document} represents corrective maintenance costs. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{c}_{down}$$\end{document} denotes the penalty for downtime due to stockouts. Since \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{c}_{down}\gg\:{c}_{cm}$$\end{document}, the logic of the evaluation function indicates that any single loss incurred from downtime caused by stockouts is sufficient to offset several years’ worth of inventory holding costs.

Training process and strategy updates

PPO is an online algorithm based on policy gradients, whose core idea is to optimize the policy while introducing a clipping mechanism to limit the step size of policy updates. This ensures monotonic and stable convergence in complex inventory-maintenance joint decision-making environments20. The training process primarily consists of three iterative steps: data sampling, advantage estimation, and policy update.

Data sampling and experience replay

At the start of each iteration, the agent interacts with the simulation environment based on its current policy network. At each time step t, the agent outputs a joint action \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{A}_{t}\:$$\end{document}based on the observed state \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{S}_{t}=\{{I}_{t},{\mathrm{O}}_{t},{\mathrm{H}}_{t}\}$$\end{document}. After executing the action, the environment returns the next state \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{S}_{t+1}$$\end{document} and the shaped training reward \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{R}_{train,t}$$\end{document}. The trajectory data \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\tau\:=\{{s}_{t},{a}_{t},{r}_{train,t},{s}_{t+1},\mathrm{l}\mathrm{o}\mathrm{g}P({a}_{t}\left)\right\}$$\end{document} generated from this interaction is stored in the experience replay buffer until the preset number of steps \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{T}_{steps}$$\end{document} is reached.

Generalized advantage estimation

To reduce the variance in policy gradient estimation, this paper employs Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE). Using the state value \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{V}_{\phi\:}\left({s}_{t}\right)$$\end{document} output by the Critic network, the temporal difference residual is computed as follows: 17\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\delta\:}_{t}={R}_{train,t}+\gamma\:{V}_{\phi\:}\left({s}_{t+1}\right)-{V}_{\phi\:}\left({s}_{t}\right)$$\end{document}

The advantage function is then expanded as follows: 18\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\widehat{A}}_{t}=\sum\:_{k=0}^{T-t-1}\:(\gamma\:\lambda\:{)}^{k}{\delta\:}_{t+k}$$\end{document}

Here, γ represents the discount factor, and λ denotes the GAE smoothing coefficient. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\widehat{A}}_{t}$$\end{document} measures the relative advantage of the current action \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{a}_{t}$$\end{document} compared to the average, guiding the policy to update toward high-reward regions.

Policy update and objective function

PPO updates the parameters θ of the Actor network and the parameters of the Critic network by maximizing the following composite objective function: 19\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{L}^{CLIP}\left(\theta\:\right)={\widehat{\mathbb{E}}}_{t}\left[min\left({r}_{t}\right(\theta\:){\widehat{A}}_{t},\mathrm{d}\mathrm{i}\mathrm{m}({r}_{t}\left(\theta\:\right),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon\left){\widehat{A}}_{t}\right)\right]$$\end{document}

Here, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{r}_{t}\left(\theta\:\right)=\frac{{\pi\:}_{\theta\:}\left({a}_{t}\right|{s}_{t})}{{\pi\:}_{{\theta\:}_{old}}\left({a}_{t}\right|{s}_{t})}$$\end{document} represents the ratio of probabilities between the old and new policies. ε denotes the clipping threshold. The Actor enforces the pruning mechanism to restrict the policy update magnitude within the range [1 − ε, 1 + ε], preventing policy collapse due to excessively large update steps. The Critic updates the value network by minimizing the mean squared error of value prediction: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{L}^{VF}=\left({V}_{\phi\:}\right({s}_{t})-{V}_{target}{)}^{2}$$\end{document}. An entropy term \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\stackrel{-}{S}\left[{\pi\:}_{\theta\:}\right]$$\end{document} is incorporated into the objective function to encourage exploration and prevent premature convergence to local optima.

The performance and stability of the PPO agent are highly dependent on the selection of hyperparameters. In this study, these parameters were tuned through a combination of heuristic selection based on established literature and empirical validation within the simulation environment. The final optimized hyperparameter configuration is summarized in Table 1. Specifically, the learning rate was set to 3 × 10-4 to ensure stable gradient updates, while the clipping epsilon \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\varepsilon$$\end{document} was maintained at 0.2 to prevent excessively large policy shifts. The entropy coefficient of 0.01 was utilized to encourage sufficient exploration during the early training stages.

The training process for all DRL agents was executed for a total of 2 × 106 environment steps, which is equivalent to approximately 550 episodes over the 3650-day simulation horizon. This ensures that the agents are exposed to a wide spectrum of stochastic degradation scenarios and inventory lead-time uncertainties. A rigorous convergence criterion was established to guarantee the maturity of the learned policies: training was automatically terminated when the moving average reward over the most recent 100 episodes stabilized within a ± 1% fluctuation range for 20 consecutive iterations. This approach prevents over-fitting and ensures that the reported ‘Proposed S4’ performance represents a fully converged and stable policy. All experimental results are presented as the average of 10 independent runs using different random seeds to ensure statistical significance.

Hyperparameter tuning and rationale

The performance of PPO is sensitive to its hyperparameters. The final configuration was selected through a combination of literature-based initialization, preliminary experimentation, and empirical validation. This section discusses the rationale for each key parameter within the context of the 10-year joint inventory-maintenance simulation.

Learning rate (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:3\times\:{10}^{-4}$$\end{document}). This value follows the default recommendation for the Adam optimizer in deep reinforcement learning21 and has been successfully applied in inventory management tasks. In initial trials, rates above \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:5\times\:{10}^{-4}$$\end{document} led to unstable policy updates after the introduction of reward shaping, while rates below \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:1\times\:{10}^{-4}$$\end{document} slowed convergence without improving final outcomes. The chosen rate strikes a balance between stable learning and reasonable convergence speed.

Clipping epsilon (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\varepsilon=0.2$$\end{document}). As proposed in the original PPO paper, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\varepsilon=0.2$$\end{document} limits the policy update magnitude to prevent destructive large updates. In the studied environment, the Reward Shaping module can cause sudden changes in the reward signal when inventory crosses the safety threshold. The 0.2 clipping ensures that the policy adapts gradually to these changes, maintaining stability while still allowing improvement. Reducing \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\varepsilon$$\end{document} to 0.1 did not improve stability but slowed learning, while increasing \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\varepsilon$$\end{document} to 0.3 occasionally triggered performance collapses.

Entropy coefficient ( 0.01). The entropy term encourages exploration by penalizing deterministic policies. The environment exhibits pronounced non-stationarity due to stochastic degradation and lead times; without sufficient exploration, the policy may prematurely converge to a short-sighted strategy. Entropy coefficients in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\{0.0,,\mathrm{0.005,0.01,0.02,0.05}\}$$\end{document} were tested. A coefficient of 0.0 resulted in early convergence to zero-inventory policies with poor long-term performance, while coefficients above 0.02 introduced excessive randomness that prevented convergence. The value 0.01 provided a good trade-off, enabling sustained exploration throughout the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:2\times\:{10}^{6}$$\end{document} training steps while still allowing the policy to specialize.

GAE lambda (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\lambda\:=0.95$$\end{document}). Generalized advantage estimation22 controls the bias-variance trade-off in advantage computation. A high \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\lambda\:$$\end{document} (close to 1) reduces bias but increases variance, while a low \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\lambda\:$$\end{document} has the opposite effect. Given the long-term consequences of ordering decisions and the sparsity of failure penalties, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\lambda\:=0.95$$\end{document} was selected to retain sufficient long-term information while keeping variance manageable.

Training steps (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:2\times\:{10}^{6}$$\end{document}). The total number of environment steps was chosen to ensure convergence. Learning curves were monitored, and performance typically stabilized after approximately \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:1.5\times\:{10}^{6}$$\end{document} steps across multiple random seeds. The additional \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:0.5\times\:{10}^{6}$$\end{document} steps serve as a safety margin to guarantee that all strategies have reached a mature policy.

All hyperparameters were initially selected based on values reported in prior deep reinforcement learning studies for inventory management and the default settings of the Stable-Baselines3 library. A limited grid search was then performed over the most sensitive parameters using the baseline S1 strategy, and the chosen values were verified to remain effective for the more complex S4 variant. Final configurations were validated by observing stable learning curves and consistent performance across independent random seeds.

Simulation experiments and results analysis

Simulation experiment environment setup

To realistically simulate the degradation process of bearings throughout their entire lifecycle and its dynamic interaction with the supply chain, this paper has established a discrete event simulation platform based on the Python programming language.

Simulation platform architecture

The simulation platform adopts a layered architecture design, primarily comprising the following core modules:

Physical simulation layer: Built upon NumPy. Responsible for generating bearing lifespan data conforming to the Weibull distribution, while simulating suppliers’ random lead times and order routing logic. This layer functions as a digital twin environment, invisible to agents, providing only observations and reward signals.

Reinforcement learning interface layer: Built on the Gymnasium standard interface. It converts raw data from the physical layer into noisy observation vectors \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{S}_{t}$$\end{document} and decodes the agent’s joint action \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{A}_{t}$$\end{document} into specific inventory and maintenance instructions.

Algorithm implementation layer: Based on the Stable-Baselines3 deep reinforcement learning library. It implements the Actor-Critic network architecture of the PPO algorithm and integrates the asymmetric reward shaping mechanism proposed in this paper.

Comparative strategy design

To validate the superiority of the proposed adaptive PPO strategy, the following two groups of strategies are established for comparative experiments and ablation studies:

Strategy 1: A standard PPO agent. This agent was trained without incorporating uncertainty awareness, treating the predicted Remaining Useful Life (RUL) values as deterministic inputs and optimizing decisions solely based on the standard cost structure. Serving as the baseline, Strategy 1 (S1) represents the standard Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) model without any specialized enhancements.

Strategy 2: PPO integrated with Uncertainty-Awareness (UA). This model extends S1 by incorporating uncertainty estimates (quantified via Monte Carlo Dropout) of the RUL predictions into its state representation.

Strategy 3: PPO augmented with Reward Shaping (RS). This strategy builds upon S1 by integrating a heuristic reward shaping mechanism designed to mitigate short-sighted agent behavior.

Strategy 4: The proposed hybrid PPO agent. Our final strategy employs a PPO agent trained with an asymmetric reward shaping mechanism. It takes the joint state\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:({I}_{t},{\mathrm{O}}_{t},{\mathrm{H}}_{t})$$\end{document}as input and dynamically outputs joint ordering and maintenance actions.

Deep Q-Network (DQN): A classic value-based DRL algorithm, DQN was selected as a foundational baseline. It utilizes an experience replay buffer to handle discrete action spaces.

Soft Actor-Critic (SAC): An advanced actor-critic algorithm whose core objective includes maximizing policy entropy. This promotes enhanced exploration and helps avoid convergence to local optima.

CVaR-based PPO: A PPO variant based on Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR)23. Instead of solely optimizing for expected return, this model focuses on optimizing the α-quantile at the left tail of the return distribution, thereby demonstrating high sensitivity to tail risks.

For all PPO-based agents, the entropy coefficient was strategically set to 0.01. Given the long-term non-stationary nature of the joint maintenance-inventory environment, this coefficient ensures sustained exploration to prevent the policy from premature convergence to sub-optimal local minima. Concurrently, a clipping epsilon ε of 0.2 was employed to restrict policy update magnitudes. This is particularly critical when integrating the Reward Shaping module; by suppressing drastic policy shifts, the clipping mechanism prevents catastrophic performance degradation in the presence of high failure penalties.

Collectively, these analyses demonstrate that the superior performance of the proposed S4 strategy is not an artifact of a specific parameter configuration. Instead, it reflects the intrinsic robustness conferred by the uncertainty-aware and reward-shaping mechanisms, which enable the agent to adapt its behavior across a wide range of operating conditions.

Conclusions

The scientific contribution of this work is twofold. First, the work demonstrates that the systematic integration of uncertainty quantification and reward shaping within a PPO framework can trigger emergent risk-adaptive behaviors, notably the capability to dynamically adjust safety stock based on prediction confidence. Second, through systematic ablation and counterfactual analyses, these behavioral characteristics are clearly delineated and are shown to translate into significant and robust economic benefits in a challenging joint inventory-maintenance optimization problem. By revealing how the synergistic combination of established techniques can unlock new capabilities, these findings advance the state of the art, thereby providing a reference for designing risk-sensitive deep reinforcement learning agents in other domains subject to prognostic uncertainty.

Extensive experiments conducted on the benchmark dataset validate the superiority of the proposed strategy, achieving the lowest total lifecycle cost. Sensitivity analysis particularly highlights the model’s robustness: under high prognostic noise levels of up to 40%, the agent effectively maintains system stability and attains zero downtime by trading a marginal increase in holding costs for a substantial gain in reliability. Future work will extend the framework to multi-echelon supply chains to address the bullwhip effect and explore Graph Neural Network (GNN) techniques to capture spatial dependencies among coupled equipment in large-scale manufacturing systems.

The current evaluation is conducted within a single simulated environment calibrated using the IEEE PHM 2012 dataset. While this dataset is a well-established benchmark in the prognostics community, and we have performed extensive sensitivity analyses across key environmental parameters, the findings may not directly generalize to all possible industrial settings without further validation. The consistent performance of the proposed S4 strategy across these varied scenarios provides encouraging evidence of its robustness, but future work should extend the evaluation to additional bearing degradation datasets, other equipment types, more complex supply chain structures, and real-world pilot studies.

The significance of this research extends beyond improving inventory policy precision under predictive errors. More importantly, it establishes a risk-sensitive autonomous decision-making framework that bridges the gap between prognostic perception and operational action. In the era of Industrial 4.0 and Digital Twins, traditional deterministic models often fail to account for the stochastic risks inherent in predictive maintenance. By integrating Bayesian uncertainty quantification with asymmetric reward shaping, this study provides a theoretical foundation for resilient industrial logistics. It demonstrates how AI agents can evolve from ‘optimizing for the mean’ to ‘optimizing for reliability,’ which is crucial for minimizing catastrophic downtime costs and ensuring the stability of smart manufacturing supply chains.
