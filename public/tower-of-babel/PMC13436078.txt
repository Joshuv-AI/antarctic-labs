Non-pharmacological interventions for lactational mastitis: a systematic review and meta-analysis protocol
Authors: Siyuan Zhang, Yuehan Ren, Qiong Zhang, Yingyi Fan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLactational mastitis is a common inflammatory condition that can adversely affect maternal well-being and is associated with early cessation of breastfeeding. Although antibiotics and analgesics are widely used in clinical practice, concerns regarding antimicrobial resistance and potential effects on the infant microbiome have prompted increasing interest in non-pharmacological approaches. A range of physical and biological interventions, including probiotics, therapeutic ultrasound, acupuncture and manual therapies, have been investigated; however, the comparative effectiveness and safety of these interventions remain unclear. This study aims to systematically evaluate the efficacy and safety of non-pharmacological interventions for lactational mastitis.Methods and analysisThis protocol is reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis Protocols (PRISMA-P) guidelines. We will systematically search PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, the Cochrane Library, CNKI, Wanfang, VIP and SinoMed as well as relevant trial registries from database inception to March 2026.We will include randomised controlled trials enrolling adult lactating women diagnosed with mastitis. Eligible studies will compare non-pharmacological interventions with usual care, placebo or pharmacological treatments. Interventions involving pharmacologically active agents will be excluded.Two reviewers will independently perform study selection, data extraction and risk of bias assessment using the Cochrane RoB 2 tool. The primary outcome will be resolution of mastitis, defined as the return of body temperature to normal (<37°C) and resolution of localised breast symptoms (eg, pain, erythema and swelling). Secondary outcomes will include inflammatory markers (eg, C-reactive protein), pain intensity (measured using the Visual Analogue Scale or similar validated scales), incidence of adverse events, complete blood count parameters, time to symptom resolution or normalisation of body temperature, incidence of breast abscess and breastfeeding continuation rates.Meta-analysis will be conducted, where appropriate, using random-effects models. Continuous outcomes will be summarised using mean differences or standardised mean differences, and dichotomous outcomes using risk ratios, all with 95% CIs. Statistical heterogeneity will be assessed using the I² statistic. Subgroup and sensitivity analyses will be performed to explore potential sources of heterogeneity. Publication bias will be assessed using funnel plots and Egger’s test when sufficient studies are available. The certainty of evidence will be evaluated using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation（GRADE）approach.Ethics and disseminationEthical approval is not required for this study as it is based on published data. The findings will be disseminated through peer-reviewed publications and conference presentations.PROSPERO registration numberCRD420261347640.

Introduction

Rationale

Lactational mastitis is a prevalent condition that can range from moderate to severe in its clinical presentation, posing a substantial global health burden among breastfeeding women across diverse populations.1–4

The Academy of Breastfeeding Medicine has recently proposed a ‘mastitis spectrum’ framework, which conceptualises mastitis not merely as an acute infectious event but rather as a progressive condition involving localised milk stasis and subsequent tissue inflammation.5 6 However, definitions of mastitis vary. The WHO has defined mastitis as ‘an inflammatory condition of the breast, which may or may not be accompanied by infection’,7 and the diagnostic criteria remain debated.8 9 To address this ambiguity, this review will adopt a clear operational definition of lactational mastitis, requiring both localised breast symptoms (pain, erythema and swelling) and systemic features (fever ≥38.5°C or flu-like symptoms).

The condition arises from a combination of milk stasis, nipple damage and altered breastmilk immune profiles.10 Multiple risk factors have been reported, including improper breastfeeding technique, breast trauma and maternal fatigue.11

During this inflammatory cascade, the profile of immune factors in breast milk is substantially altered, characterised by elevated levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin-8 and interferon-γ, alongside increased lactoferrin concentrations, contributing to localised pain, erythema, induration and systemic fever.12 13

Historically, the standard clinical management of lactational mastitis has relied heavily on narrow-spectrum antibiotics targeting Staphylococcus aureus and pharmacological analgesics.7 14 15 However, the evidence base for routine antibiotic use in mastitis remains limited.16 The indiscriminate prescription of antibiotics poses clinical concerns, including the risk of maternal antimicrobial resistance (AMR),17 potential disruptions to the infant’s developing microbiome18–20 and an associated risk of abrupt breastfeeding cessation.21 Consequently, there is increasing interest in alternative management strategies involving conservative, non-pharmacological interventions.22 Within Western healthcare settings, modern physical and biological therapies have gained significant traction. Several studies have shown that oral administration of specific probiotic strains (Lactobacillus spp.) may reduce the incidence of mastitis and alleviate related symptoms.23 24 Furthermore, topical applications of cold cabbage leaves, alongside physical cold compresses, are widely used to alleviate localised oedema and pain through pure physical cooling and mechanical fluid displacement.25–27 Therapeutic ultrasound has also been investigated for facilitating milk drainage, but randomised controlled trials have shown mixed results, with some reporting no benefit.28 29

Concurrently, Traditional Chinese Medicine and allied physical modalities offer a rich repository of non-pharmacological strategies. Acupuncture has been empirically validated in Western clinical trials for relieving inflammatory breast symptoms via mechanical neuromodulation.30 Moreover, targeted acupoint massage, traditional manual milk expression (tuina), and mechanical scraping (gua-sha) have demonstrated clinical efficacy in resolving ductal blockages and preventing abscess formation.31–33 Recent comprehensive analyses further confirm that non-invasive techniques such as acupressure, scraping and cupping can effectively improve lactation and relieve breast engorgement.34 Currently, a comprehensive systematic review quantitatively synthesising the safety of pure non-pharmacological interventions by the type of intervention (eg, acupuncture-related therapies, manual therapies, device-based therapies and probiotics) is lacking.

Objectives

The primary objective of this study is to systematically evaluate the efficacy and safety of global, non-pharmacological interventions for lactational mastitis. We aim to address the following PICO question: in lactating women with mastitis (P), do non-pharmacological interventions (I), compared with conventional care, placebo or pharmacological monotherapy (C), affect inflammatory markers, pain intensity and demonstrate a favourable safety profile (O)?

Methods

This protocol is reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis Protocols (PRISMA-P) 2015 statement.35 36 The protocol has been prospectively registered in PROSPERO (registration number: CRD420261347640).

Information sources and search strategy

A comprehensive search will be conducted in PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL) and four Chinese databases (CNKI, Wanfang Data, VIP Database and SinoMed) from inception to March 2026.

The search strategy will combine controlled vocabulary terms and free-text terms related to lactational mastitis and non-pharmacological interventions.

The search strategy will be adapted for each database. The full search strategies are provided in the online supplemental material.

Study selection and data extraction

All records will be imported into reference management software and duplicates will be removed. Two reviewers (YR and SZ) will independently screen titles and abstracts, followed by full-text assessment of potentially eligible studies. Disagreements will be resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer (YF). The study selection process will be presented in a PRISMA flow diagram (figure 1).

Data extraction will be conducted independently by two reviewers (YR and SZ) using a pre-piloted standardised form. Extracted data will include study characteristics, participant details, intervention characteristics and outcomes, including adverse events.

For missing or unclear data, we will attempt to contact the corresponding authors. Where necessary, missing SD will be calculated from available statistics (eg, SEs or CIs) in accordance with the Cochrane Handbook.

Risk of bias assessment

Risk of bias will be assessed independently by two reviewers using the Cochrane RoB 2 tool.39

Data synthesis and statistical analysis

Where appropriate, meta-analysis will be performed using random-effects models, considering the anticipated clinical heterogeneity across interventions. Continuous outcomes will be summarised using mean differences or standardised mean differences, and dichotomous outcomes using risk ratios, all with 95% CIs. Statistical heterogeneity will be assessed using the I² statistic, with values of 25%, 50% and 75% representing low, moderate and high heterogeneity, respectively.40

Where substantial heterogeneity is identified (I² >50%), potential sources will be explored through subgroup analyses and sensitivity analyses.

Predefined subgroup analyses will include:

Type of intervention (eg, acupuncture-related therapies, manual therapies, device-based therapies and probiotics).

Type of intervention (eg, acupuncture-related therapies, manual therapies, device-based therapies and probiotics).

Timing of intervention (eg, early vs delayed treatment).

Timing of intervention (eg, early vs delayed treatment).

Sensitivity analyses will be conducted by excluding studies at high risk of bias and by applying alternative statistical models.

If at least 10 studies are included in a meta-analysis, publication bias will be assessed using funnel plots and Egger’s test.

If quantitative synthesis is not appropriate due to substantial clinical or methodological heterogeneity, a narrative synthesis will be conducted. Findings will be summarised descriptively according to intervention type, outcome measures and study characteristics.

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis will provide a comprehensive and methodologically rigorous synthesis of the available evidence regarding the efficacy and safety of non-pharmacological interventions for lactational mastitis. By focusing specifically on interventions that operate through physical or biological mechanisms, this study aims to generate clinically relevant evidence to inform antibiotic-sparing strategies in the management of this common postpartum condition.

Given the increasing concerns surrounding AMR and the potential disruption of the infant microbiome associated with antibiotic use, there is a growing need to identify effective alternative therapies. By systematically comparing a broad range of non-pharmacological interventions, this study is expected to clarify their relative effectiveness and safety profiles, thereby supporting more individualised and evidence-based clinical decision-making.

This study has several methodological strengths. First, the protocol is developed in accordance with PRISMA-P guidelines and incorporates a comprehensive search strategy across both English and Chinese databases, which is expected to maximise evidence coverage and minimise publication bias. Second, rigorous methodological approaches, including duplicate study selection, standardised data extraction, risk of bias assessment using the Cochrane RoB 2 tool and evaluation of evidence certainty using the GRADE framework, will enhance the transparency, reliability and interpretability of the findings.

Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. Substantial clinical and methodological heterogeneity is anticipated due to variations in intervention types, treatment intensity, practitioner expertise and outcome measurement across studies. Such heterogeneity may complicate data synthesis and interpretation, even with the use of random-effects models. Second, blinding is often challenging in trials of physical or manual interventions, which may introduce performance and detection bias, particularly for subjective outcomes such as pain. The overall quality of included trials may also vary, potentially influencing the certainty of evidence. To address these issues, we plan to conduct subgroup and sensitivity analyses to explore sources of heterogeneity and assess the robustness of the findings.
