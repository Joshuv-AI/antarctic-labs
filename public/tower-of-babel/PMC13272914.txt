Measuring children’s spatial accessibility to urban park green spaces
Authors: Alireza Mohammadi, Roya Moghabeli, Arshad Ahmed, Robert Bergquist, Jorge Rocha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChildren’s physical, mental and social development depends on access to green areas in the urban landscape, which varies considerably, especially in cities under rapid urbanization. The modified Huff-based variable three-step floating catchment area (MHV3SFCA) approach, which combines area attractiveness, distance decay and variability of catchment areas, was used to investigate the spatial accessibility of urban parks for children in Ardabil, a city in Iran. The aim was to encourage the creation of inclusive, health-promoting cities that support United Nations’ sustainable development goal (SDG) no. 3 (Health) and no. 11 (Sustainable cities) by emphasizing child-centred urban planning. To evaluate differences in park distribution and accessibility across 6,417 census blocks, this analysis used spatial statistics, such as kernel density estimation (KDE), global and local Moran’s indices and the children-to-population ratio (CPR). The findings indicate significant spatial disparities, with central and south-western neighbourhoods having high park densities and accessibility while northern and north-eastern regions lack green spaces. In 2,615 census blocks, 42% of children did not have access to any urban parks, indicating serious shortcomings in underprivileged areas. Introduction

Evidence-based approaches are needed to improve equitable park access, including the creation of new parks in low-income, high-density neighbourhoods, enhancing transit systems and putting nature-based solutions like pocket parks and green corridors into practice. Urban parks provide numerous environmental, social and health benefits, making them essential parts of sustainable cities 1. Green spaces improve public health, well-being, and quality of life because they offer chances for opportunities for social interaction, physical activity and mental relaxation 2,3.

Urban parks are especially important for children supporting emotional health, physical exercise, and brain development 4,5. Their role in promoting healthy development is also highlighted by association with lower childhood obesity, better mental health and increased social cohesion 1,6. The irregular geographical pattern of park availability represents one of the main challenges when it comes to urban planning. The issue of spatial disparities, as discussed by various scholarly publications on the topic, such as Soja in 1988, represents the fact that “unequal green space distribution creates areas of extreme green space deprivation” that tend to be found in the high-density central part of cities and confirm that green space represents the core component of urban livability 7–9. In addition, Ryan underlines the importance of Bronfenbrenner’s ecological systems theory (1979) that puts these disparities in clear context by connecting park access to child development through nested environmental systems with policies reflecting societal values 10.

Rapid population growth and urbanization, however, pose a threat to fair access to urban public green spaces. According to United Nations (UN), the competition for land and resources is expected to increase as 68% of the world’s population is expected to live in cities by 2050 11. Given that children are particularly susceptible to the negative effects of urban densification, this demographic shift emphasizes the urgent need to prioritize urban green spaces at the top of planning and policy to guarantee their availability for future generations 12,13.

A key component of equitable urban design is spatial accessibility, which includes aspects such as safety, transit options, physical proximity and the standard of green spaces 14. In some of the poorer countries, accessing the parks might not be the same across the cities, yet there’s an agreement on the fact that the parks are important. Disparities are mostly observed in populated areas, and the amenities within the parks have not expanded proportionately to the population growth 15,16. Due to their reliance on caregivers, restricted independence and increased susceptibility to environmental stressors, children are especially susceptible to these disparities 5,13. For instance, parks are frequently few, badly maintained or situated in dangerous areas in low-income neighbourhoods, which exacerbates health disparities and fuels sedentary lifestyles, anxiety and social isolation 1,17. It is therefore important to enhance the spatial access to parks in order to ensure the health of children in urban areas 7.

Spatial analysis methods and geographic information systems (GIS) are essential for assessing urban park accessibility. The Floating Catchment Area (FCA) analysis is a geographic proxy measure of accessibility. The method calculates the availability of the resource (supply) within a given road travel distance of the demand location 18,19. To address these limitations, methods such as gravity models, network analysis and the two-step floating catchment area (2SFCA) approach have been developed 20,21. However, these frameworks still often neglect child-specific factors such as caregiver dependency, safety perceptions and mobility patterns 14. The limitation reduces their effectiveness for equitable urban planning, particularly in rapidly urbanizing areas with socioeconomic and environmental disparities 22,23.

Several studies illustrate the application of 2SFCA-based methods in assessing children’s access to parks. Xing et al24. applied a multi-mode 2SFCA framework in Wuhan, China, revealing greater accessibility challenges for children relying on walking compared to bicycles or public transport. Similarly, Pei et al25. found spatial disparities in low-income neighbourhoods in Tianjin by integrating park attractiveness coefficients and travel time.

In Changsha, China, Liao and Furuya 26developed an enhanced 2SFCA method that incorporated supply and demand dynamics, offering a more nuanced understanding of children’s access to urban parks. The study emphasized integrating park quality and safety perceptions into accessibility models, as these factors significantly influence children’s park usage. Similarly, a study in Beijing by Qin et al 27. modified the 2SFCA method to account for multiple park entrances and real-time travel data, demonstrating that traditional methods often overestimate accessibility in high-density urban areas. A study in Fuzhou, China by Fu et al 28. proposed an improved 2SFCA method based on the physical activity services perspective. This framework introduced a park attractiveness index that incorporated both objective park features and subjective environmental perceptions, evaluated using deep-learning techniques. The study identified underserved neighbourhoods and highlighted spatial disparities in park accessibility, particularly in areas with high child populations.

The FCA method has undergone advancements in recent years, including the Three-Step Floating Catchment Area (3SFCA). In Chengdu, China, Zhou et al 29. introduced the SCM-G2SFCA model, which incorporates supply competition among parks and multiple travel modes to assess spatial accessibility. This study discovered significant differences in park accessibility, with high accessibility in the southern suburbs and low accessibility in the northern urban areas. The model’s evaluation of accessibility is more precise because it incorporates competitive dynamics between parks and the influence of diverse transportation options.

The Variable-width Floating Catchment Area (VFCA) method is yet another strategy that rethinks accessibility by considering different catchment widths depending on the size and quality of parks. Dony et al 30. applied this method to evaluate park accessibility in Zhengzhou, China, revealing that traditional fixed-width catchment areas often underestimate accessibility in densely populated urban regions. Collectively, these studies highlight the evolution of the FCA approach in analysing children’s spatial accessibility to urban parks. They also offer a more thorough understanding of accessibility disparities and inform equitable urban planning strategies by incorporating elements, such as park quality, safety perceptions, and multimodal transportation.

Even though urban parks’ spatial accessibility has been the subject of numerous studies, there are still a lot of unanswered questions about children’s access, especially in the Global South. According to Teeuwen et al 31. and Wang et al 32., existing research frequently concentrates on adult populations or neglects to incorporate socioeconomic, behavioural, and environmental factors into accessibility models. Additionally, not many studies discuss the particular difficulties faced by quickly urbanizing cities, where population expansion surpasses the development of green infrastructure, thereby intensifying spatial disparities 33,34.

Although the body of research on park accessibility is growing, there is still a significant gap in the application of sophisticated spatial models to quickly urbanizing Global South cities that experience extreme spatial disparity. This research finds significance in increasing literature in the domain of park accessibility research as it fills a specific gap in the application of sophisticated spatial models in the rapidly developing Global South cities. It provides a thorough case study in the specific case of Ardabil in Iran using the MHV3SFCA approach. This research renders three primary results. First, it allows for a strong geographical application by assessing this progressive approach within a setting that has a history of spatial disparities in planning. Secondly, it provides valuable planning information by employing high-resolution data to site specific high-priority geographic areas that require planning. Thirdly, it provides an applicable approach for transforming results for spatial accessibility into a form of evidence-based investment priorities.

In this study, we aim to investigate a strategic model for transforming spatial data into focused, equity-oriented policy in addition to quantifying accessibility disparities.

Materials and methods

Study area

About 530,000 people live in Ardabil, the capital of the Ardabil Province in north-western Iran, covering 76 km² 35. It has a high population density almost 7,000 people per km2. Located just east of Mount Sabalan on an open plain at an elevation of 1,500 m (4,900 feet), the city has a climate with cold spells in the winter that can last until late spring (Fig. 1). The primary spatial units for this analysis are the city’s five districts and 44 neighborhoods. A detailed understanding of Ardabil’s physical layout and demographics is possible thanks to data from the Statistical Center of Iran 35, which is based on 6,417 census blocks. It has 109 public parks, whose distribution and features differ greatly among these neighborhoods, which constitute a crucial component of the city’s urban fabric 36. The major features of the parks within the city are the variety of sizes, which range from 610 m² to 382,884 m², and the uneven spatial distribution of the parks within the neighborhoods of the city. Given Ardabil’s high population density and history of rapid urbanization, which have shaped the city’s older areas, the availability of these green spaces is a topic of interest 37. The context for this study of spatial accessibility is defined by the interaction between the city’s neighborhoods and its unevenly distributed park infrastructure.

Geodatabase and procedure

The data for urban parks was obtained from OpenStreetMap (OSM) and cross-checked with official records from the Ardabil Municipality (see supplementary material). The “QuickOSM” plugin in QGIS39 was used to extract park boundaries, and the same program 38 was used to determine their sizes. For analysis, census block and neighborhood-level population data were combined. In accordance with Iran’s compliance with the UN Convention on the Rights of the Child and UNESCO’s standard categorization35,40, this study defines children as those under the age of 18. This definition emphasizes how crucial it is to address their unique developmental needs, which include having access to leisure areas.

A comprehensive geodatabase was developed to enable spatial analysis following the correction of any missing or insufficient records in the population data, with focus on the study’s target group, i.e. those under 18 years of age. This age group is particularly important for assessing the impacts of green space access, as it significantly influences their physical activity, mental health, and overall development. Access to urban parks plays a crucial role in promoting children’s development and well-being by providing opportunities for recreation and social interaction. These layers, sourced from OSM and the Ardabil Municipality, offered a strong basis for assessing spatial accessibility and its effect on children’s access to urban parks. Spatial layers such as census block layouts and administrative boundaries were added into the geodatabase and provide a robust framework for analyzing demographic characteristics and children’s access to urban parks. The MHV3SFCA method was employed to assess children’s accessibility to urban parks, focusing on park sizes, travel distances, and population data at the census block-level.

Cell size and bandwidth play a crucial role in the KDE method. To accurately represent incident clusters, it is necessary to choose the cell size appropriately, while the bandwidth determines the search area used for density estimation. Greater bandwidth values show global patterns, while smaller ones emphasize local trends43. Systematic testing was used to empirically optimize the bandwidth selection (500–2000 m range), and 1000 m was selected because it best captured park distribution patterns while retaining geographic relevance. The ideal resolution was offered by the 30 × 30 m cell size. Although there are statistical techniques like Ripley’s K-function, our strategy gave spatial accuracy for urban planning applications top priority. To determine the spatial density of parks in our study area, we used QGIS and the KDE method.

MHV3SFCA background and algorithms

The MHV3SFCA is an improved version of the traditional FCA method, which incorporates both population demand and the spatial proximity of services like parks. It was used to evaluate the spatial accessibility of urban amenities, such as parks, healthcare, education and other vital services. Specifically, the study assessed children’s access to urban parks at the census block level45.

The MHV3SFCA approach is especially well-suited for evaluating children’s accessibility because its framework is intended to address the shortcomings of traditional accessibility models. By including distance decay, park attractiveness coefficients (using park size as a proxy) and variable catchment areas, our application of the method goes beyond basic proximity and supply-demand dynamics21. The literature highlights the comprehensive MHV3SFCA framework’s ability to incorporate intricate child-specific elements like caregiver dependency, safety perceptions and specific quality metrics—aspects that are often overlooked in traditional park accessibility models14. However, our analysis concentrated on the fundamental, quantitatively measurable spatial components of the model for this particular application. Despite being acknowledged as crucially important, factors like perceived safety and caregiver dependency were outside the purview of our available datasets.

The accessibility estimates derived from the MHV3SFCA model represent potential spatial accessibility rather than actual park use. While geographic proximity, park size, and population distribution capture the structural availability of parks, children’s real access may also depend on factors such as parental supervision, traffic safety, and local mobility conditions, which could not be incorporated due to data limitations. The model nevertheless improves conventional FCA approaches by integrating Huff-based probabilistic choice, variable catchment areas, and distance-decay dynamics to better represent spatial accessibility patterns.

The MHV3SFCA provides a more accurate and nuanced representation of accessibility for children than conventional approaches by concentrating on improved spatial parameters, which include distance decay, attractiveness, proxy as well as different variable catchment areas. Its application is in line with the ideas of child-centered urban planning, assisting in the identification of underprivileged areas and directing tactics for more equitable urban growth46. Thus, the suggested approach provides a solid and scalable basis for measuring children’s access to urban parks.

The MHV3SFCA method incorporates key components such as distance decay, which reduces the impact of parks located farther from a given area, and an attractiveness exponent that assigns greater weight to larger parks. To address the needs of vulnerable populations particularly children CPR is integrated into the model. Furthermore, the floating catchment area approach flexibly captures accessibility by accounting for all parks within a specified distance from each census block45.

The MHV3SFCA method is unique because it considers two important factors: how the likelihood of visiting a park decreases as the distance to the park increases (distance decay effect), and the size of the park itself. By including an exponent that reflects park attractiveness based on size, this method provides a more accurate way to measure accessibility. In essence, the MHV3SFCA approach combines these three main elements to calculate the Spatial Accessibility Index (SAI)26.

Supply-side factors (park size)

The size of a park is an important part of its service capacity26. It is believed that larger parks can accommodate more people, which means they have a greater impact on accessibility than smaller parks.

Demand-side factors (population distribution)

The distribution of the population across different areas should be taken into account, with a specific focus on key demographic groups, such as children, who are the main users of urban parks in this study. Urban parks can be designed and located to ensure accessibility and usability for those who need them the most, creating equitable and inclusive community spaces by prioritizing their needs47,48.

Distance decay

The method uses a distance decay function to reflect real-world travel behaviour, which decreases park accessibility as distance increases49,50. In accessibility studies, travel distance also reflects the mobility capacity of individuals or households to move between locations and participate in everyday urban activities. In the context of children’s access to parks, travel distance can therefore be interpreted as a practical measure of the effort required for children and their caregivers to reach recreational spaces within the metropolitan environment. In this study, we determined the proximity between census blocks and parks using Euclidean (straight-line) distances. Because of its ease of use, computational effectiveness and data accessibility, this method is frequently employed as a baseline in spatial accessibility research.

The MHV3SFCA methodology involves the following steps:

Step 1: Park attractiveness (Aj).

This study’s use of park size as a proxy for park attractiveness (Aj) is in line with earlier studies50. Even though facilities and amenities are great qualitative indicators of attractiveness, there was a lack of standardized data for the 109 urban parks in our study area. As a result, park size was employed as a widely accepted and useful stand-in for supply capacity. In order to model park attractiveness, the size of the park (areaj) (as described in Eq. 3) is raised to an exponent γ, which was set to 1, 2 and 3 based on our sensitivity analysis result as best γ range. It was believed that larger parks offer more amenities and more capacity, which increases their appeal to potential visitors. In accordance with urban planning principles that acknowledge the significance of large parks in serving a larger population, the exponent γ magnifies the impact of park size. In the accessibility model, the exponent is a tuning parameter that modifies the weight assigned to park size45.

Huff represents the likelihood of children in census block i accessing park j (as described in Eq. 4). This step uses the distance decay function to model the reduction in accessibility with increasing distance. The decay parameter β, set at 0.03 to 0.3 adjusts how quickly accessibility decreases with increasing distance, providing a realistic representation of travel behaviour where people are less likely to visit parks that are further away45,51.

In order to maximize the mean accessibility score while maintaining a realistic representation of spatial impedance and park attractiveness, the ideal values (based on a combination of empirical testing and knowledge from previous studies) (β = 0.03, γ = 1) were chosen. In this study, the final SAI is a normalized metric (0–1) that measures the accessibility of urban parks for children in each census block, accounting for park size, population distribution, and distance decay. Children-to-Population Ratio (CPR)

In spatial accessibility research, the CPR is a helpful demographic indicator since it shows the percentage of children under the age of 18 in each census block’s total population. Since children are heavily reliant on local services, this metric sheds light on whether access to resources like parks and health facilities is correlated with their relative representation. CPR can be used in conjunction with accessibility scores to pinpoint underserved locations for children and to inform planning and resource allocation decisions that support social fairness53,54. The normalized SAI and CPR, two key metrics in this study, were calculated based on the outcomes generated by the MHV3SFCA model. To guarantee comparability across census blocks, the normalized final accessibility index was scaled from 0 to 1, taking into account variables like park attractiveness and distance decay to reflect the relative accessibility of parks. In order to determine how equitable park access is, the CPR was computed by dividing the number of children under 18 years in each census block by the total population. In order to guide resource allocation and policy decisions to improve social equity, both indices were mapped using QGIS tools to visualize spatial patterns and identify areas with equitable or inequitable access to parks.

Local analysis

Using local indicators of spatial association (LISA), which is similar to Local Moran’s I (LMI), we were able to examine local spatial patterns of the SAI and identify areas of the city where children may have relatively lower access to urban parks. GMI was used to assess overall spatial correlations. The Z-score (where > 1.96 indicates significance at α = 0.05) measures the strength of deviation from spatial randomness, the p-value (where p < 0.05 rejects the null hypothesis) supports the statistical significance of the observed pattern and the I-statistic (roughly bounded by −1 and + 1) quantifies the direction and magnitude of spatial autocorrelation given by GMI44. Clustering and a strong correlation between variables are indicated by positive values. The LMI/LISA uses cluster maps to pinpoint particular spatial patterns when global autocorrelation is positive. These patterns are used to classify blocks into high-high (HH), low-low (LL), high-low (HL) or low-high (LH) clusters44. In essence, the CPR evaluates equity in access, whereas the LMI/LISA identifies local relationships between variables and spatial patterns44. Using RStudio software (version 2024.12.0 + 467), we measured the LISA statistic; the open access source QGIS software (version 3.40.1; http://qgis.osgeo.org)38 was then used to map the results. The bivariate choropleth map visualizes best the highest and lowest values in a spatial dataset or to find correlations within a dataset. According to the Environmental Systems Research Institute (ESRI), a bivariate map portrays two variables, which represent two different phenomena simultaneously on a map as described by the ESRI55. We compared the number of children in each census block with SAI using bivariate choropleth maps to determine the critical areas. Our study used the ROW method44 for standardization, the False Discovery Rate (FDR) with a value of 499 for the number of permutations and the K nearest neighbours for conceptualizing spatial relationships.

Validation and sensitivity analysis

The attractiveness exponent (γ) and the distance decay factor (β), were tested in different combinations in order to perform a sensitivity analysis and guarantee the robustness of the park accessibility model. The robustness of the SAI was tested by a sensitivity analysis conducted by varying β (0.03–0.3) and also γ (1–3). While higher γ values favoured larger parks and higher β values increased distance decay, the overall spatial patterns of accessibility remained consistent supporting the stability and reliability of the final SAI results. In previous research, β values between 0.03 and 0.3 and γ values at 1, 2 and 3 were measured45. Based on these combinations, the final SAI for census blocks was determined using the MHV3SFCA method. The results were normalized to a range of 0 to 1 for comparability. The optimal parameter combination was then indicated by the highest mean accessibility score, which was calculated for each configuration. The parameter selection was not based solely on maximizing the mean accessibility score. Instead, several combinations of β and γ were evaluated and the final values were retained because they produced stable spatial patterns while remaining consistent with parameter ranges reported in previous FCA-based accessibility studies. Therefore, the resulting Spatial Accessibility Index should be interpreted as a measure of potential accessibility, which is commonly used in spatial accessibility research when behavioural or visitation data are unavailable. We tested various combinations of γ and β to calculate accessibility for each census block based on park size and proximity. The optimal parameters (β = 0.03 and γ = 1) were identified by maximizing the average accessibility score. Using these optimal values, the final accessibility scores for each census block were calculated, normalized, and saved using the MHV3SFCA method.

By modifying accessibility based on proximity, the dynamic β function exp (−⋅) automatically incorporates catchment areas into the MHV3SFCA method. A more accurate depiction of accessibility is made possible by the dynamic nature of the method, since the impact of parks gradually decreases with distance. The sensitivity analysis can be used to simulate different catchment sizes by varying the . Smaller catchments are represented by smaller values, and larger catchments are represented by larger ones. This adaptability offers a continuous, distance-weighted accessibility metric without the need for arbitrary distance thresholds. Without the need for predetermined, fixed catchment areas, the method captures subtle accessibility differences by dynamically adjusting catchment areas based on distance, ensuring that each park contributes proportionately to its distance from a census block45.

Park accessibility and equity for children were evaluated using the CPR and sensitivity analysis. The latter examined how different β and γ values affected accessibility scores, while the former determined how well park access matched the distribution of children across census blocks. While higher β values caused accessibility to deteriorate more quickly with distance, increasing γ improved accessibility, particularly for larger parks. The comparison showed that both equity and accessibility were impacted by parameter changes, with higher β indicating proximity and higher γ favouring larger parks. The optimal parameter setup (β = 0.03, γ = 1) was identified as providing the most balanced and consistent representation of children’s park accessibility.

Results

The pattern of urban parks distribution

According to the findings of the GMI analysis, there was no discernible clustering or dispersion in the parks’ spatial distribution within the study area. With a variance of 0.00, an expected index of −0.00, and a Moran’s I of −0.001, the spatial arrangement was close to spatial randomness. The absence of a statistically significant pattern is confirmed by the z-score of −0.23 and the p = 0.82. The absence of statistically significant global autocorrelation suggests that no clear clustering or dispersion pattern of parks is detected at the spatial scale used in this study. However, this result should not be interpreted as evidence that park planning decisions are random, as urban land-use patterns are influenced by multiple planning, historical, and socioeconomic factors. To provide a more comprehensive understanding, this spatial pattern should therefore be interpreted alongside other contextual factors, such as population density, demographic characteristics and proximity to parks when evaluating spatial accessibility. The effectiveness of park provision ultimately depends on how well existing parks serve the surrounding population. Consequently, factors such as travel distance, population concentration and local accessibility conditions must be considered when assessing whether the current spatial arrangement adequately meets the needs of different population groups.

The majority of areas with park densities near 8 parks per km2 (shaded in red) are located in the south-central and south-western neighbourhoods. These places have a lot of parks, which indicating that these areas contain comparatively more green spaces. The northern and north-eastern neighborhoods, on the other hand, have park densities of less than two parks per km2 (shown in blue and green) indicating a conspicuous lack of green space. However, the northern neighbourhoods which might also have a large population in newly constructed neighbourhoods were found not to have enough number of parks.

SAI: access to parks

The final SAI distribution map (Fig. 3b) offers a thorough representation of park accessibility for children throughout the study area. The MHV3SFCA method was used to derive the SAI values, which were normalized between 0 and 1. Dynamic catchment areas were incorporated to account for differences in accessibility and population distribution. The resulting map shows distinct patterns of disparity in various geographic zones and significant spatial disparities in park accessibility throughout the city. Greater accessibility to parks is indicated by high SAI values, which are mostly found in the city’s central and western parts. The best park distribution, a well-connected transit system and comparatively little competition for these resources are all advantages of these areas. Children in the city’s green zones, indicated by high SAI scores, appear to have comparatively better access to public parks, which may support opportunities for recreation and wellbeing. On the other hand, low SAI values which are particularly noticeable in the city’s North and Southeast highlight relatively lower levels of park accessibility. Higher population densities, disjointed road networks and fewer park amenities are characteristics of these areas that together lessen the usefulness of accessibility. Peripheral areas, shown in red on the map, exhibit the lowest SAI values pointing to underserved areas where children face substantial barriers to accessing parks.

Results of the CPR analysis

The spatial distribution of the CPR across census blocks is shown in Fig. 3c. By dividing the number of children under 18 by the total population in each census block, the CPR determines areas with different concentrations of children. The distribution of the child population ratio on the map exhibits clear spatial patterns. Lighter colours on the map indicate central regions, which typically have lower CPR. On the other hand, darker colours have a higher CPR in the outer regions and the periphery. These trends suggest that additional parks and green spaces may be required in outlying areas where there is a higher concentration of children in order to meet the needs of younger residents. The visualization of the CPR aids urban planners and policymakers. It offers details on parks and playgrounds that might have fewer kid-friendly features than other places. This analysis can direct focused interventions that guarantee children in densely populated areas have sufficient access to open spaces and recreational facilities, thereby enhancing spatial and social justice. It is anticipated that densely populated areas particularly those with higher child population densities (CPR) will have more parks and green spaces. In order to test this theory, a SAI was calculated in the following section to determine how easy it is for residents, especially children, to access green spaces in different locations.

LISA analysis (SAI vs. CPR)

On applying the Local Indicators of Spatial Association (LISA) technique to the total of 6,417 census blocks, it was found that 47% (2,993 census blocks) of them showed statistically significant spatial patterns. These spatial patterns range from High-High (HH in red), which represents Census Tracts of high accessibility with high neighborhood values, Low-Low (LL in blue), which show low accessibility with low neighborhood values, and High-Low (HL in pink) or Low-High (LH in light blue), which represent accessibility with significantly low or high neighborhood values respectively. The other remaining 53% (3,424 census blocks) fall under undefined and show no significant spatial pattern.

Spatially, the clusters are mostly situated in the central and southern parts for HH, which represent areas with high park accessibility. Conversely, LL are mainly situated in the northern and western parts of the city, which represent areas with low accessibility. The results are indicative of large disparities in accessing parks by children in Ardabil. The clusters in HH represent areas with relative service abundance. The LL clusters demonstrate areas requiring particular attention. The HL and LH represent zones with complexities deserving more research. One critical outcome of the LISA analysis is that there are 2,615 census blocks with limited or no access to an urban park for an estimated 86,000 children or 42% of the urban child population.

The bivariate choropleth map (Fig. 4) helps in understanding the correlation between child population density and park accessibility. The map highlights that the central and northwest areas (brown and light red) represent the priority areas because they have a higher child population and less accessible parks. The fact that the current park resources in the priority areas may be inadequate or underutilized likely reflects an underlying constraint in terms of socioeconomic or demographic parameters. The central urban areas (brown), in fact, show an imbalanced allocation in the provision of parks within the city. The areas in the remaining regions (yellow) have relatively less accessible and fewer children; therefore, these areas need to be observed when the city develops in the coming years (Fig. 4).

Model validation

The ideal parameters for evaluating park accessibility were found to be β = 0.03 and γ = 1 by the sensitivity analysis. While the β value guarantees a proportionate relationship between park size and appeal, the β value indicates a mild decrease in distance. A moderate distance decay, where accessibility steadily declines with increasing distance from parks, is indicated by the β value of 0.03. Without overstating the impact of larger parks, the γ value of 1 indicates a linear relationship between park size and attractiveness, meaning that parks’ attractiveness rises proportionately to their size. This combination balanced the effects of park size and distance to create the most accurate and dependable spatial accessibility index, which accurately depicts park accessibility for children. The analysis’s findings guarantee a more equitable perception of park access, especially for children, by determining the parameter configuration that most accurately captures the connection between park size, proximity, and equitable distribution.

Discussion

Spatial Inequity in Children’s Park Access

Our analysis reveals a distinct and concerning spatial pattern in park accessibility across study city (Ardabil). The critical clustering of low-accessibility, high-child-density neighbourhoods in the northern and central regions as well as the observed spatial randomness of parks, are not coincidental. Spatial justice scholars have criticized the lack of planning frameworks that prioritize equitable resource distribution reflected in these findings9. The observed disparities in park accessibility highlight potential spatial mismatches between the distribution of children and the availability of urban green spaces7. While these patterns may suggest emerging equity concerns, the present analysis focuses primarily on spatial accessibility and does not include detailed socioeconomic indicators required for a comprehensive assessment of environmental justice8. This pattern indicates that neighbourhoods with lower SAI values consistently correspond to relatively poorer park accessibility.

By using the MHV3SFCA method, we were able to quantify the disparity between supply and demand and go beyond simple proximity. This disparity is directly responsible for 42% of children’s severe accessibility deficits, a result consistent with research in other quickly urbanizing contexts5657, that is especially severe in Ardabil, where population growth has probably outpaced investments in green infrastructure. Therefore, we identify a “critical need for evidence-based planning” to address these past planning failures.

This disparity has significant ramifications that are closely related to our findings. Not only are the children in the identified LL clusters (such as those in the northern and northwest regions) farther from parks, but they are also more vulnerable to the detrimental effects of lack of green spaces. Previous studies have shown that access to urban green spaces can support children’s physical activity, mental well-being and social interaction16,. Consequently, limited accessibility in these areas may restrict opportunities for children to experience these potential benefits.

As a result, addressing these spatial disparity is essential for public health as well as planning. Improving equitable access to urban parks may contribute to broader urban sustainability and public health objectives, including those highlighted in global frameworks such as the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs).In the context of Ardabil, the spatial analysis suggests targeted interventions in these particular neighbourhoods such as establishing pocket parks in high-density areas or enhancing safe routes to existing parks are crucial.

Limitations of the Study and Directions for Future Research

Consistent with the standards recommended by the UNESCO Institute for Statistics40, we analyzed children under 18 as a single group due to data constraints, while recognizing that future studies with more granular data could examine age-specific usage patterns. Age strongly influences park accessibility, with 0–6-year-olds experiencing greater disparities than 7–12-year-olds23. The dependence of younger children on adults for access emphasizes the significance of urban planning that addresses the requirements of different age groups5859,. According to the demographic categories found in the census data, “children” in this study were broadly defined as people under the age of 18. This definition is simplistic, even though it allowed us to capture the general accessibility trends of the child population. We acknowledge that toddlers, school-aged kids, and teenagers have varying levels of mobility, safety requirements and leisure interests, all of which may affect how they visit and utilize parks. However, due to methodological limitations and population data limitations, it was not possible to measure accessibility separately for each age group at the same time. Future studies should consider disaggregating children into narrower age categories when more detailed data become available, to provide more age-specific insights for urban planning and park provision.

The MHV3SFCA method offers a trustworthy SAI for evaluating children’s access to parks with a focus on equity by taking into account park size and proximity. However, a significant drawback of this research is that the distance decay function used to model accessibility primarily considers spatial proximity, travel impedance, and demand–supply balance, while omitting qualitative factors such as park quality and amenities5260,. As a result, despite their established impact on children’s use of urban parks, significant qualitative elements like amenities, upkeep and perceived safety were left out of the analysis. A common strategy in earlier accessibility research was to use area as a stand-in for attractiveness because there was a lack of trustworthy amenity data for all 109 parks. By incorporating survey or observational data on park amenities and quality, future research could overcome this limitation and provide a more thorough assessment of park attractiveness and children’s equitable access. Additionally, advanced techniques, such as machine learning or remote sensing, could be employed to evaluate park conditions and their impact on accessibility. These drawbacks notwithstanding, the study provides a strong basis for directing urban planning to guarantee equitable access to green areas and improve children’s wellbeing, especially in underprivileged areas.

Euclidean distances were used to determine the centroids of parks and census blocks in this study. This method works well for assessing relative accessibility despite not recording network paths. Absolute precision would increase with network-adjusted distances. Despite being widely used as a stand-in for network distance, Euclidean distance has some significant drawbacks. Since the detour index, which measures the ratio of network to Euclidean distance, is much higher for trips under 500 m, its relationship with network distance is weakest over short distances6162,. Additionally, significant local differences are found close to features such as shorelines, suggesting that straight-line distance is not always accurate enough63. Our MHV3SFCA methodology incorporates a distance-decay function to mitigate the inherent overestimation of straight-line distances, which weights closer parks more heavily than distant ones. However, we acknowledge that this approach cannot capture the complexities of the actual road network, as true accessibility, especially for children, is profoundly influenced by factors like pedestrian pathways, street connectivity, physical barriers (e.g., highways, railroads), traffic safety, and overall network walkability. While Euclidean-based metrics provide a useful baseline, they are unable to accurately reflect these real-world conditions, particularly in urban areas with dispersed connectivity. Future studies must therefore build on this work by incorporating thorough road network data and utilizing network-based features like service area analysis and travel time metrics in order to produce a more accurate representation of movement patterns and actual accessibility.

The effects of socioeconomic status and urban planning on park access and children’s health could also be investigated through longitudinal studies64. Future studies should also integrate socioeconomic and vulnerability indicators such as income levels, housing conditions, or minority status to provide a more comprehensive evaluation of environmental justice in urban park accessibility. Frameworks for fair park distribution and planning may also be improved by comparative studies carried out in various urban contexts. Finally, additional observations are necessary for grey areas in the choropleth map with low values both for balanced park distribution and access.

Implications for child-friendly urban parks planning

Our findings highlight spatial differences in children’s park accessibility that can inform future urban planning strategies. These results suggest the need for policy interventions that prioritize intentional and more equitable park placement in order to better serve children across different neighbourhoods. This interpretation is supported by the spatial analysis, which indicates that parks do not show a strong global clustering pattern at the spatial scale analysed. Our analysis has found the existence of distinct spatial differences for the accessibility of children within urban parks, and such findings can form the basis for designing intervention measures. Establishing parks in high-density, low-income, and underserved areas should be the primary focus of urban planning, with a focus on underutilized areas and communities with a high child population. Better transportation and infrastructure are also necessary to increase park accessibility, which will make it easier for children from far-flung or underdeveloped areas to visit. Examples of nature-based solutions that can be used to expand green spaces and improve accessibility include pocket parks, urban forests, and green corridors. Additionally, policy initiatives should encourage the revitalization of neglected or underdeveloped park areas, enhancing accessibility and child friendliness. By implementing these strategies, cities can enhance children’s overall health and provide more equitable access to parks, especially in underserved areas.

Conclusions

Equitable access to parks is challenging in densely populated areas with dispersed infrastructure, but effective urban planning improves access in some neighbourhoods. This study highlights significant differences in children’s access to urban parks throughout the city, highlighting the unequal distribution of green areas and variable accessibility based on neighbourhood attributes. Poor urban planning, socioeconomic disparity and inadequate infrastructure are among the factors that make it difficult for people in underprivileged areas to access parks. Limited access to parks may reduce opportunities for children to benefit from the physical, psychological and social advantages associated with urban green spaces, as reported in previous studies. This study suggests targeted urban planning initiatives that prioritize equitable park access as a means of addressing these disparities, something that would guarantee that all children can take advantage of the developmental, social and physical benefits offered by urban parks, especially in underprivileged neighbourhoods.
