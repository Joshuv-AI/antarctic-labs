Electromyogram diagnosed chronic lumbar radiculopathy is highly associated with L4-S1 regional multifidus atrophy on magnetic resonance imaging
Authors: Mario Giuseppe Zotti, Danny Mekhail, Ben Kennedy, Mark Wyatt, Ventzi Bonev
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLumbar multifidus atrophy (LMA) is recognised as having clinically significant implications. Pathology producing lumbar radiculopathy (LR) is known to be associated with LMA, but there is a paucity of large studies formally evaluating the relationship between LR electrodiagnostics and LMA. Our aim was to assess the association of electrodiagnostically proven LR with the presence of LMA on Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI).MethodsA cohort of 100 consecutive eligible patients with chronic lumbar spinal symptoms was analysed from 119 patients who underwent electrodiagnostic evaluation. Electrodiagnostic assessment included nerve conduction studies (NCS) and needle electromyography (EMG). NCS was used to exclude peripheral neuropathy and other abnormalities, while the diagnosis of chronic lumbar radiculopathy (CLR) was based on needle EMG findings and overall electrophysiological assessment. Patients with EMG-confirmed CLR were assigned to the positive LR cohort, while those with EMG-negative findings for LR served as the control cohort. LMA was assessed on MRI by two independent observers using 3D proton density weighted gradient echo sequences. LMA was graded according to the modified Goutallier classification and quantified using the percentage of fatty infiltration derived from the Dixon dual echo method 4. A threshold of > 15% fatty infiltration or modified Goutallier grade ≥ 1 was designated as atrophy. These imaging findings were analysed for an association with EMG-confirmed radiculopathy.ResultsOne hundred eligible positive CLR patients were analysed. Modified Goutallier grading demonstrated 2% grade 0, 68% grade 1, 19% grade 2 and 11% grade 3 LMA. Ninety-eight per cent of patients with EMG-proven LR had LMA, compared with 64% of symptomatic patients with normal EMG findings (Fisher’s exact test, p < 0.001; odds ratio 27.2). In the control cohort, 36% demonstrated grade 0, 43% grade 1, and 21% grade 2 LMA, with no patients demonstrating grade 3 atrophy. The prevalence of LMA was significantly higher in the CLR cohort compared with symptomatic patients with normal EMG findings.ConclusionsThis diagnostic study reveals EMG-proven CLR is highly associated with MRI-based LMA. Based on these findings, LMA, although not pathognomonic and commonly observed in symptomatic patients, may represent a potential biomarker for CLR.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Background

Lumbar multifidus atrophy (LMA) is recognised as having clinically significant implications [1]. Pathology producing lumbar radiculopathy (LR) is known to be associated with LMA via various proposed mechanisms, but there is a paucity of large studies formally evaluating the relationship between LR electrodiagnostics and LMA [2, 3]. Multifidus atrophy has been noted in systematic reviews to be commonly observed in patients with symptomatic radiculopathy [4, 5]. Lower back pain (LBP) is the leading cause of disability worldwide and is associated with substantial healthcare resources, socioeconomic burden and reduced quality of life [6]. Improved understanding of the relationship between chronic lumbar radiculopathy (CLR) and LMA may assist clinicians in diagnostic interpretation, rehabilitation planning and identification of patients at risk of persistent pain and disability [1, 7, 8]. This may also inform future research into imaging biomarkers and targeted neuromuscular rehabilitation strategies.

The multifidus muscle is critical for maintaining spinal stability, and its degeneration has been recognised as a key feature of LR [9–11]. The principal fascicles of the lumbar multifidus muscle attach the lateral side of the spinous process to the mamillary processes, iliac crests, and the dorsal surface of the sacrum and span between 2 and 5 spinal levels with a high cross-sectional area, allowing the complex to produce large forces. When functional, its forces enable it to be a stabiliser of the ‘neutral zone’ as described by Panjabi [12] and regulate shear and compression forces in sagittal and rotary trunk movements, as well as fine tuning equilibrating loads in complex movement through motor control and proprioception [13]. Although a complex relationship between LMA and LBP exists, LR is associated with significantly decreased multifidus functional cross-sectional area (CSA) [14], and LMA is significantly associated with an increase in LBP [1, 9–11, 15].

Dysfunction of the nerve root can, in turn, affect the lumbar medial branch, which innervates the zygapophyseal joints and adjacent structures, including the multifidus, and is likely to impact the multifidus muscle as it provides sensory and motor input essential for spinal stability and movement [16]. The denervation theory suggests that LR impacts the dorsal ramus medial branch and disrupts the corticospinal pathway, leading to denervation of the multifidus and, consequently, LMA. Radiofrequency facet joint denervation of the medial branch leads to LMA, which is evident on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), highlighting that following denervation, atrophy will likely follow [16–18]. Similarly, other animal models assessing painful disc lesions or radiculopathy have also shown atrophy, but the direct effect of the radiculopathy can be difficult to separate theoretically from the effect of arthrogenic inhibition and inflammation [19]. The onset of detectable structural muscle changes, including reduced multifidus density (likely due to collagen deposition), is thought to occur in the timeframe of 3–6 months post-denervation [20].

The spinal nerves supplying the anterior tibialis (L4-L5) and gastrocnemius muscles (S1-S2) share spinal nerve roots with the dorsal rami innervating the multifidus. The multifidus itself demonstrates multisegmental innervation from the ipsilateral medial branches of the dorsal rami, with the levels L2-S1 the most clinically relevant [21, 22]. Direct Electromyography (EMG) of the multifidus muscle is challenging due to multiple factors, including significant patient discomfort and non-compliance with instructions [23]. These barriers include the location of the multifidus and difficulty isolating and targeting the deep multifidus without imaging, such as ultrasound (impacting precision).

An alternative view alluded to above is that LMA is due to arthrogenic pain-reflex inhibition [10]. LR frequently causes pain, leading to muscle disuse, unloading and reduced muscle activation (reflex inhibition from the corticospinal tract). This altered muscle function later increases adiposity and regional inflammatory mediators, including TNF and IL-1b and fibrosis, ultimately leading to LMA. Other alternative theories suggest that myopathy and inflammation are responsible for LMA; however, less is published on this than the main theories of denervation and arthrogenic inhibition, which may commonly co-exist [24].

The aim of this study was to investigate the percentage of patients with confirmed objective CLR and its association with evidence of focal LMA on MRI. Our hypothesis was that there would likely be a strong association between LR and MRI appearance of LMA based upon the pathoanatomical consequence of disrupting neural input to the multifidus in the presence of radiculopathy.

Methods

Participants

This retrospective study into variables affecting multifidus health was approved by the University Human Research Ethics Committee (Bond University - MZ00044). Informed consent was obtained from all individual participants included in the study as part of their data collection from the moment of standard clinic enrolment for use in future research. The study involved assessing the suitability for inclusion of 1443 patients presenting to a private spinal surgery clinic with lumbar spinal symptoms with any history of previous or current radicular features, EMG diagnosed chronic LR (with the neurologist blinded to imaging findings), and MRI within 2 months of EMG. Exclusion criteria were posterior spinal surgery, known myopathy, peripheral neuropathy, primary neurological disorders, or normal EMG. Of the 19 who had lumbar spinal symptoms but were excluded due to normal EMG or neuromuscular symptoms, 14 had normal EMG and had no associated peripheral neuropathy or neuromuscular condition and were able to act as a control cohort. In total, 1343 patients were excluded (Fig. 1) to include 100 consecutive eligible positive CLR patients (aged 22–83) to produce a percentage of observed association with LMA on MRI.

Electrodiagnostics

Electrodiagnostic evaluation was performed using nerve conduction studies (NCS) and quantitative needle EMG with a Cadwell Sierra Summit EMG/NCS/EP system. NCS of the left and right deep peroneal and tibial nerves were performed using standardised techniques and normative reference values to detect and exclude peripheral neuropathy and other abnormalities that may invalidate the EMG. This was followed by a needle EMG to assess the nerve root, and the diagnosis of LR was based on needle EMG findings. Needle EMG studies of the right and left anterior tibialis and gastrocnemius muscles (L4-L5 and S1-S2 myotomes, respectively) were performed using 50 mm Natus concentric needle electrodes, placed in safe and reliable locations according to patient anatomy [25, 26]. All patients who had EMG studies were seen in a clinical neurophysiological laboratory and examined by a consultant neurologist, who also completed the needle EMG studies. A neurophysiologist completed the NCS under the supervision of the examining neurologist. Quantitative EMG assessment incorporated multiple neurophysiological parameters, including motor unit potential duration, amplitude and interference patterns. Polyphasic motor units, when present in a sufficiently high percentage of motor units, suggest previous denervation, followed by reinnervation. A threshold of > 20% polyphasic motor unit potentials within the sampled myotome was utilised as a quantitative indicator of abnormality consistent with well-established quantitative EMG principles [27]. However, the diagnosis of CLR was based on the overall electrophysiological assessment rather than isolated polyphasia alone. Quantitative EMG data were generated using automated device analysis, reducing the potential for subjective human error. EMG of the multifidi itself was not undertaken due to technical challenges, including extensive variability between patients in the multifidus muscle fibres and bundles that can be activated and difficulties accurately targeting the deep multifidus muscle [23]. Therefore, needle EMG studies were completed on the anterior tibialis and gastrocnemius muscles as a more reliable and technically feasible undertaking. Several smaller observational studies have reported the association between EMG-proven LR and LMA [2, 3].

Imaging protocol

The MRI systems used were a 2-Tesla and a 3-Tesla imaging system (Philips). Proton density weighted mDIXON Quant sequences were utilised to produce fat percentage maps. One consultant radiologist, blinded to EMG findings, and one consultant spinal surgeon independently assessed multifidus morphology using the Dixon method in conjunction with axial T2-weighted images to improve accuracy, with fat percentage calculated using proprietary software [28, 29]. LMA was evaluated at the L4-L5 and L5-S1 levels at the level of the facet joint for reproducibility. LMA was graded using a modified Goutallier classification adapted for lumbar multifidus fatty infiltration and quantified using percentage fatty infiltration derived from the Dixon dual echo method. Grade 0 represents normal muscle with fatty infiltration up to 10% of the muscle’s CSA, grade 1 represents mild muscle degeneration with 10–30% fatty infiltration, grade 2 represents moderate condition with 30–50% fatty infiltration, and grade 3 represents severe muscle atrophy with > 50% fatty infiltration. Although the modified Goutallier classification considers 10% to represent the lower limit of grade 1, a threshold of > 15% fatty infiltration or modified Goutallier grade ≥ 1 was designated as atrophy in order to reduce potential misclassification of borderline assessments near the 10% threshold and minimise effects of measurement error. Data analysis

Radiological LMA assessments from MRI scans performed at an independent radiology clinic (blinded to EMG findings) were analysed for an association with LR findings. This was done for both the positive CLR cohort (n = 100) and the cohort of symptomatic patients with normal EMG without associated neuromuscular condition / peripheral neuropathy (n = 14). MRI evidence of LMA was independently assessed by dual observers on axial 3D proton density-weighted gradient echo sequence images at the level of the L5 vertebral body and graded using a modified Goutallier classification adapted for lumbar multifidus fatty infiltration, together with percentage fatty infiltration derived from the Dixon dual echo method using silhouette-based digital subtraction analysis [32]. A threshold of > 15% fatty infiltration or modified Goutallier grade ≥ 1 was designated as LMA [30, 31]. The significance of the association between LR and LMA was assessed using Fisher’s exact test for a 2 × 2 contingency table (SPSS V29, IBM, NY, USA). Cohen’s Kappa (GraphPad Prism Calculator, California, USA) was used to measure inter-observer reliability between the two independent reviewers for the modified Goutallier grading and the presence or absence of LMA, providing an objective measure of assessing LMA.

Results

100 eligible patients with proven CLR were analysed (69 males, mean age 53, range 22–83), with 86% and 68% having symptoms for more than 6 and 12 months, respectively, as shown in Table 1; Fig. 3. Ninety-eight of one hundred patients with EMG-proven CLR had LMA, while 9 of 14 in the symptomatic back pain but with normal EMG cohort had any LMA (Fisher’s exact test, p < 0.001; odds ratio 27.2). Modified Goutallier grading for the positive CLR cohort was 2% grade 0, 68% grade 1, 19% grade 2 and 11% grade 3, respectively, while the control cohort was 36% grade 0; 43% grade 1 and 21% grade 2, 0% Grade 3, as seen in Fig. 4. Two patients within the positive CLR cohort demonstrated borderline multifidus changes despite EMG-confirmed CLR and symptom duration of less than 6 months (approximately 10% fatty infiltration with modified Goutallier grade 0). Cohen’s Kappa coefficient assessing agreement between the two independent reviewers for modified Goutallier grading was 0.59, indicating moderate agreement beyond chance, while the Kappa for the presence or absence of LMA was 0.68, indicating substantial inter-observer agreement.

All sites sampled revealed full interference patterns, with no spontaneous activity to suggest acute denervation. There was neurophysiological evidence of chronic bilateral L5 (significantly more pronounced on the right side) and left S1 nerve root dysfunction. Polyphasic motor units were detected in the tibialis anterior (40% of recorded MUPs [motor unit potentials] on the right and 20% on the left) and left gastrocnemius (25%) muscles. Discussion

The present study demonstrated a strong association between EMG-confirmed CLR and LMA on MRI, with 98% of patients with EMG-confirmed CLR demonstrating evidence of LMA. These findings support previous observations that LR is strongly associated with structural degeneration of the multifidus muscle and further support the proposed contribution of denervation-related mechanisms to multifidus degeneration [2, 3, 11]. The multifidus muscle has been recognised as a crucial component in maintaining spinal stability and movement [33], with studies highlighting its rapid atrophy following spinal injury [14]. The neutral zone is an area of intervertebral motion where there is limited resistance from the spinal column and is clinically important in the context of spinal stability and function [12]. The multifidus muscle plays a major role in stabilising the neutral zone. MA and dysfunction reduce effective control of intervertebral motion, increasing the neutral zone and are associated with LBP [34]. CLR is a highly prevalent and debilitating condition causing LBP, which is the single leading cause of disability worldwide [6]. LMA is common in patients with LBP, highlighting the importance and potential of LMA as a biomarker for early CLR. The diagnosis and management of CLR and LMA have proven challenging due to the complex interaction of various factors contributing to its pathophysiology, including denervation and arthrogenic reflex inhibition, with conflicting findings in various animal studies [10, 16, 17]. The findings from our study suggest a high association of LR with LMA.

Association between EMG-diagnosed LR and MA

Electrodiagnostic testing for LR, including NCS and EMG, is an important tool in the diagnosis of neuromuscular disorders [35]. Although NCS may be normal in LR, performing NCS in the zone relevant to the left and right deep peroneal and tibial nerve (L4-S2) is useful in detecting and excluding abnormalities such as peripheral neuropathy. EMG is considered the reference standard electrodiagnostic test for confirming radiculopathy, especially in patients with symptoms. EMG can detect polyphasic motor units, which, when present in 20% of motor unit potentials (or more), suggest previous denervation, followed by reinnervation. The EMG studies conducted showed neurophysiological evidence of chronic bilateral L5 and left S1 nerve root dysfunction, with 98% of patients with EMG-proven CLR having LMA versus 64% in those with normal EMG. This suggests that EMG-diagnosed CLR is highly associated with L4-S1 LMA on MRI (p < 0.001). The high prevalence of LMA in patients with EMG-proven LR suggests a strong role of denervation in LMA and its potential as a biomarker for CLR. Our findings differ from those of Seliverstova et al. [36] who reported no association between EMG findings in L5 radiculopathy and paraspinal muscle fatty infiltration. Several methodological differences may explain this discrepancy. Seliverstova et al. assessed direct paraspinal MUP remodelling within the multifidus muscles, whereas the present study utilised quantitative needle EMG of limb myotomes to identify established CLR using the overall electrophysiological profile. Additionally, the present cohort predominantly demonstrated chronic symptoms (> 6 months in 86% of patients), whereas Seliverstova et al. included an earlier disease cohort with a median symptom duration of 12 weeks. MRI methodology also differed with the present study utilising quantitative Dixon-based fat analysis, which may improve detection of mild multifidus fatty infiltration compared with T2 qualitative visual assessment methods [37]. In 2 patients, LR was observed but did not meet radiological LMA criteria. However, the onset of detectable structural muscle changes is thought to occur within 3–6 months, and these patients had symptoms for less than 6 months, providing a possible explanation for the lack of LMA [10]. Guven and colleagues [18] found an association between lumbar foraminal stenosis affecting the exiting nerve roots and focal LMA, which corroborates similar observations by Hyun et al. [3] and Min et al. [2] Bonython and colleagues [17] and subsequently Guven and colleagues [38] found that radiofrequency denervation of the medial branches produced segmental multifidus atrophy, further giving credence to the theoretical contribution of denervation or deprivation of neural input, which in these cases were iatrogenic, to LMA in human subjects.

MRI as a tool for MA

MRI is an effective and accurate tool for evaluating and objectively quantifying LMA. T2-weighted MRI imaging is particularly helpful in visualising fatty infiltration of paraspinal muscles, especially when combined with Dixon method proton density-weighted sequences. MRI, in conjunction with the Dixon method using silhouette analysis, enables the objective detection and quantification of wasting of the multifidus muscle at the levels L4-L5 and L5-S1. LMA grading according to the modified Goutallier grade and percentage fat atrophy (Dixon dual echo method using silhouette-based digital subtraction) showed > 15% change in muscle composition or modified Goutallier grade ≥ 1, confirming the presence of LMA [32]. Multiple MRI-based approaches for assessment of lumbar multifidus degeneration have been described in the literature, including cross-sectional area analysis and quantitative fat infiltration assessment using region of interest and automated segmentation techniques. MRI enables objective quantification of multifidus degeneration, with Dixon-based quantitative fat assessment increasingly recognised as a reliable and reproducible method for evaluating LMA. This method of fat percentage calculation is more reliable than the previously used, strictly histogram approach [39]. The extent of LMA and fatty infiltration, which is strongly associated with LBP, could inform treatment strategies, including physiotherapy, particularly when targeting rehabilitation of the multifidus muscle [31].

LMA as a biomarker for CLR and clinical implications

The strong association between EMG-proven CLR of L4-S1 and LMA on MRI suggests that LMA could be utilised as a biomarker for the presence of CLR. However, it is not necessarily specific or pathognomonic for CLR, as LMA may also occur in association with non-radicular LBP, age-related degeneration, disuse and pain-related neuromuscular inhibition [40]. The control cohort also demonstrated a relatively high prevalence of LMA, albeit substantially lower than the positive CLR cohort, supporting the concept that non-denervation mechanisms may also contribute to LMA. This study has several important clinical implications and directions for the future. The presence of LMA on MRI, combined with clinical findings suggesting LR, may reduce the need for more invasive diagnostics such as EMG, making the diagnostic and management journey for patients with LBP easier. This is particularly helpful considering the relatively non-invasive nature of MRI, unlike EMG. We propose that LMA is not pathognomonic of CLR and may not confirm that CLR is causing LMA in a given subject when seen on MRI, but CLR should be strongly considered as contributory in those with back pain with any radicular symptoms if LMA is seen. To better understand the relationship between LMA, CLR, and their role in radicular symptoms, establishing temporal precedence and a causation model is important. It is difficult to imagine pathological models other than animal models to be feasible, which would enable a more definitive study of causation by trying to separate out radiculopathy from arthrogenic inhibition or inflammation, and this is a current limitation of the science in this field. Previous experiments by Hodges and colleagues [14] have observed MA following denervation and could be repeated to employ advanced imaging techniques and software such as the dual echo Dixon method to precisely measure multifidus atrophy and fat deposition over multiple time-points to further establish the relationship between denervation and MA [14]. Recent work by Hodges’ group also established that MA from an intervertebral disc injury can be reversed to some extent by targeted neural muscle activation, which again emphasises the role of neural input and activation to this critical muscle complex [41].

Clinically, it is well documented that multifidus atrophy, when left uncorrected after episodes of spinal-related pain, can perpetuate the cycle of pain, disability and dysfunction and can persist even when the patient’s other spinal symptoms, such as leg pain and back pain, diminish [7, 42]. Based upon the findings of this study, it is also unusual to have electrodiagnostic proven CLR but no LMA, which may have forensic and retrospective diagnostic implications.

These findings of a higher prevalence of LMA in patients with CLR compared to symptomatic patients with normal EMG further emphasise the importance of rehabilitation strategies specifically targeting the lumbar multifidus muscle, including isolated lumbar extension resistance training [8]. Recent studies have also suggested the potential benefit of targeted lumbar extension rehabilitation approaches in patients with lumbar radiculopathy and multifidus dysfunction, with improvement in pain, disability, and lumbar extensor function reported following targeted rehabilitation programmes [43, 44].

Limitations

This study has established a strong association between EMG-proven CLR of L4-S1 and LMA, but not causation. Arthrogenic inhibition and LR may present with similar symptoms and involve shared changes in neural signalling pathways, including the spinal reflex and dorsal ramus, making it difficult to differentiate between the contributions of each process as both likely coexist at symptom onset [45]. Both mechanisms alter neuromuscular control and play a role in structural changes, making their individual contribution difficult to ascertain. L4-S1 was studied; the implications of upper LR and the role of inflammation and myopathy are unclear, as denervation was assumed to be the cause of LMA. However, the majority of symptomatic LR patients involve the L4-S1 nerve roots, as the L3-S1 levels are the most commonly afflicted by degeneration in the population of LBP patients and degenerate fastest per decade [46–48].

Previous experimental work has suggested that mechanisms other than direct denervation, including reflex inhibition, inflammation and altered neuromuscular activation, may also contribute to multifidus degeneration, making it difficult to determine the precise contribution of each process in a human clinical population [14, 49]. However, no equivalent controlled human experimental model currently exists to reliably separate the independent effects of denervation, pain-related inhibition, inflammation and disuse in symptomatic LR.

This study included heterogeneous lumbar pathologies with variable durations from symptom onset. Symptom duration was retrospectively estimated from patient-reported history and analysed categorically rather than as a continuous variable, limiting precise temporal assessment of the relationship between symptom duration and severity of LMA. Although structural changes associated with denervation are generally expected to evolve, a high prevalence of LMA was still observed in patients with shorter symptom duration, suggesting that structural multifidus changes may occur earlier than previously appreciated. Additionally, the retrospective single-surgeon study design may limit the generalisability of the findings. Potential confounding variables, including body mass index, physical inactivity, and the severity or subtype of lumbar degenerative pathology (including disc herniation, spinal stenosis, and facet arthropathy), were not uniformly assessed for all patients within the retrospective dataset and may have influenced the observed relationship between CLR and LMA. Constitutional physiological neurodegeneration and sarcopenic muscle decline associated with ageing may also independently contribute to LMA, particularly in older populations. Similarly, physical inactivity and disuse may contribute to multifidus degeneration independent of radiculopathy, although inactivity is frequently associated with chronic spinal pain itself, complicating separation of cause and effect [50–52]. These variables were not objectively quantified within this retrospective dataset and therefore remain important potential confounding factors.

The symptomatic EMG-negative control cohort was heterogeneous and likely included a mixture of non-radicular lumbar pain syndromes, age-related multifidus change, disuse-related atrophy, and potentially electrodiagnostically negative radiculopathy. Many patients demonstrated lumbar or posterior pelvic symptoms without electrodiagnostic evidence of radiculopathy, potentially arising from alternative pain generators, including facetogenic, sacroiliac, or discogenic sources of referred pain. The cohort was utilised as a pragmatic symptomatic comparator group rather than a definitive non-radiculopathy population. As a retrospective observational study, no pre-study sample size calculation was performed, and the relatively small symptomatic EMG-negative cohort may limit statistical power and generalisability. Despite these limitations, LMA remained substantially more prevalent in patients with EMG-confirmed CLR.

Needle EMG may remain normal in purely demyelinating or early-stage radiculopathy, potentially limiting electrodiagnostic sensitivity. Although the majority of patients in this cohort demonstrated chronic symptoms, symptomatic EMG-negative patients may still represent a heterogeneous population, including early or non-axonal radiculopathy. Additionally, quantitative side- and level-specific electromyographic data were not uniformly available across all participants, limiting direct correlation between the anatomical distribution of EMG abnormalities and focal LMA on MRI. Consequently, mechanistic interpretation regarding level-specific denervation and multifidus atrophy remains limited.

Future directions

Future prospective studies incorporating formal clinical grading of radiculopathy severity, longitudinal follow-up of symptomatic EMG-negative patients, and serial MRI/EMG assessment may help further delineate the temporal relationship between clinical radiculopathy, electrodiagnostic abnormalities, and LMA, particularly in cases of purely demyelinating or early-stage nerve root involvement. Future studies incorporating clinically suspected but electrodiagnostically unconfirmed radiculopathy cohorts may additionally help clarify the discriminatory value of LMA as a biomarker of electrophysiologically confirmed disease. Additionally, standardised side- and level-matched quantitative EMG and MRI analysis may help further clarify the relationship between the anatomical distribution of EMG abnormalities and focal multifidus muscle atrophy.

Conclusion

This diagnostic study reveals that EMG-proven CLR is highly associated with MRI-based LMA. There is a statistically significant difference in the prevalence of LMA between symptomatic patients electrodiagnostically proven CLR versus patients with normal EMG but chronic lumbar symptoms, although the prevalence of LMA remains relatively high even within the latter cohort. Based on these results, the finding of LMA represents a potential biomarker for the presence of CLR, but does not establish CLR as the sole cause of LMA. Further prospective research is required to clarify the role of LMA as a biomarker for early lumbar radiculopathy and to better establish causal relationships between radiculopathy and multifidus degeneration. Clinicians should investigate and appreciate LMA as it has multiple clinically significant implications for the rehabilitation of patients with CLR, particularly as MA often persists after apparent clinical resolution of CLR symptoms from natural history or in the post-interventional period. Lumbar multifidus-focused rehabilitation would be important in this context in an attempt to normalise the patients’ condition and reduce future episodes of LBP from ongoing dysfunction.
