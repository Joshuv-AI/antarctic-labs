MeDxAgent : Multi-Agent Consultation for Interactive Medical Diagnosis
Akshat Sanghvi, Naren Akash, Raza Imam, Amit Sharma, Mohit Jain

Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used for health-related decision support. Yet most evaluations treat diagnosis as a single-shot task with complete information provided upfront, often as a multiple-choice selection. This diverges from clinical practice, where diagnosis is interactive and open-ended, involving sequential hypothesis refinement through targeted questioning. We address this gap. We build MeDxBench , a large-scale benchmark of 4,421 4{,}421 clinical cases across 20 specialties. We further propose MeDxAgent , a multi-agent consultation system for interactive diagnosis, and systematically study its prompt-, flow- and agent-level design choices. MeDxAgent achieves a 10.3% accuracy gain over the baseline on MeDxBench , closing 52.3% of the gap to a full-information oracle. We find that specific design choices: collecting demographics first, passing summarized dialogue for diagnosis, and feeding candidate diagnoses for targeted questioning, improve accuracy, mirroring how physicians reason, though their effect emerges fully only in combination. Code and dataset will be released upon publication.

Introduction

LLMs are increasingly used for real-world decision support, with healthcare emerging as one of the most prominent application domains
8
;
9
.
A substantial fraction of LLM usage involves health-related queries, with users relying on them to interpret symptoms and determine next steps.
However, LLMs largely operate in a single-shot setting, producing diagnoses from incomplete symptom descriptions without iterative information gathering.
In contrast, clinical diagnosis is inherently interactive:
physicians ask targeted questions, iteratively refine hypotheses, and perform differential diagnosis.

Despite this, most benchmarks for medical diagnosis evaluate LLMs in static task formulation.
Models are provided complete case descriptions, often exam-style questions such as USMLE, and evaluated on their ability to predict the correct diagnosis
15
;
28
.
Although LLMs perform strongly on such benchmarks, in some cases matching or exceeding physicians
34
;
29
, the task lacks ecological validity.

Recent work
32
;
7
;
1
has begun exploring multi-turn doctor-patient interactions using LLM agents for disease diagnosis.
However, several limitations remain.

First
, there is no standardized diagnostic benchmark that combines diverse clinical cases with reliable diagnoses.
Existing datasets are fragmented across formats (e.g., clinical vignettes, EHRs) and specialties (e.g., dermatology, cardiology), and mix diagnoses with unrelated tasks.

Second
, existing agentic approaches remain limited.
Open systems often use simplistic paradigm of a single “doctor” agent that iteratively questions the patient until it determines enough information has been gathered for diagnosis
7
;
20
;
21
, whereas the sophisticated multi-agent systems are closed-sourced and cannot be reproduced or compared
32
;
22
.

Third
, several setups leak diagnostic information and thereby weaken the task:
For example, some initialize the doctor with part of the case (e.g., by passing the first sentence of the patient’s case description)
20
,
some reduce diagnosis to multiple-choice selection
20
;
14
;
25
,
some allow the patient agent to generate synthetic information beyond the case description using the ground truth diagnosis
22
;
27
;
25
;
33
, and some convert case descriptions into structured fields that occasionally contain information revealing diagnosis
1
.
These choices can artificially inflate accuracy.

Fourth
, prior work typically reports results from a single system configuration,
leaving it unclear which design choices are responsible for observed gains.

We study this design space systematically.
We first construct
MeDxBench
, a large-scale diagnosis benchmark of 4,421 clinical cases collected from five public sources, spanning 20 specialties and ranging from common to rare diseases.
Our simulated patient agent answers only from the case description, preventing diagnosis information leaks during conversation.
We then propose
MeDxAgent
, a multi-agent consultation system for interactive diagnosis. We evaluate its
prompt-
,
flow-
, and
agent-level
design choices independently and in combination, treating each choice as a hypothesis about the capabilities required for effective diagnosis.

MeDxAgent
improves diagnostic accuracy by 10.3% over the baseline on
MeDxBench
, and recovers 52.3% of the gap between the baseline and a full-information oracle.
Our analysis reveals that three capabilities prove particularly important: collecting patient demographics first, summarizing doctor-patient dialogue into a clinical problem representation, and feeding diagnostic reasoning to guide subsequent questioning.
Further, we reveal two broader findings.
First, the effectiveness of an intervention depends strongly on
when
it is applied.
For example, we introduce differential questioning, which directs the agent to ask questions that distinguish among competing diagnoses. Applying this strategy from the second versus the tenth turn produces an 18.1% variation in diagnostic accuracy.
Second, components that hurt performance in isolation can become
beneficial in combination
. Three of the four agents reduce accuracy individually, but the full system integrating all four outperforms every partial configuration. A standard incremental ablation study would have incorrectly rejected these components.

Contributions.

•

A large-scale medical diagnosis benchmark,
MeDxBench
.

We standardize 4,421 clinical cases from five public datasets, spanning 20 specialties and a range of disease prevalence.

•

A multi-agent consultation system,
MeDxAgent
.
Our system gathers information through targeted questions, summarizes clinical evidence, maintains competing hypotheses, and commits to a diagnosis (similar to a physician). It recovers 52.3% of the gap between a baseline agent and a full-information oracle, and the gains transfer across model families.

•

A systematic study of design choices for interactive diagnosis.
We evaluate prompt-, flow-, and agent-based variations independently and in combination, showing that both intervention timing and component interactions critically shape performance.

Static Diagnostic Benchmarks.

Most medical LLM benchmarks evaluate diagnosis in a static setting, where the full case is provided upfront and the model predicts a diagnosis in a single turn, as in MultiMedQA
28
, HealthBench
2
, DiagnosisArena
36
and MedHELM
3
.
LLMs now match or exceed expert-level performance on several such benchmarks
34
;
29
.
Multi-agent approaches such as MDAgents
17
, MDTeamGPT
6
, Agent Hospital
19
, and MedAgents
31
assign specialized roles to different agents, but still operate on fully observed cases.
All these approaches sidesteps the iterative information-gathering central to clinical diagnosis.

Interactive Diagnostic Systems.

Recent work has begun exploring simulated doctor-patient interactions for diagnosis.
Some approaches formulate the task as disease selection rather than open-ended diagnosis prediction.
MediQ
20
studies iterative questioning to identify the correct diagnosis from multiple choices; DDO
14
and MEDDxAgent
25
frame diagnosis as classification over a limited disease set.
Other works focus on benchmarking LLMs by prompting them to act as clinicians conducting follow-up questioning, rather than designing multi-agent systems. CRAFT-MD
16
introduces a conversational evaluation framework spanning multiple specialties;
VivaBench
7
studies cognitive failure modes in LLM diagnosis, and identifies anchoring bias.
Most recently, agentic approaches have been developed for open-ended conversational diagnosis.
AMIE
32
, optimized for diagnostic reasoning and conversations, demonstrated physician-level performance on 149 simulated primary-care consultations with trained human actors; MAI-DxO
22
orchestrated a panel of physician-persona agents to diagnose 304 complex NEJM cases at physician-level accuracy.
However, both systems are closed-source and their evaluation protocols are not reproducible.
Several open systems have also been proposed, such as AgentClinic
26
, AI Hospital
11
and MedAgentSim
1
.
However, these works evaluate either a single doctor agent configuration or have relatively small benchmarks, limiting systematic analysis of design choices (Table
1
).
In contrast, we systematically study how prompt design, interaction flow, and agent composition affect multi-turn diagnostic performance across a large benchmark.

Construction.

From the five datasets, we keep only diagnostic cases with sufficient clinical detail.
We remove cases focused on medical knowledge, treatment recommendations, or other non-diagnostic tasks. We keep only descriptions longer than 200 characters.
Because our work studies text-only diagnosis, we exclude cases requiring image/video interpretation, while retaining those containing textual imaging reports.
We also remove duplicate cases appearing across datasets.

To prevent
diagnosis leakage

We manually verify that redaction does not remove clinically relevant evidence.

4
Simulation Framework

Our framework simulates multi-turn doctor-patient conversations using three agents:
(i) a Patient agent that answers from a case description,
(ii) a Doctor agent that conducts the consultation and
(iii) a Judge agent that evaluates the final diagnosis.
All agents are implemented through prompt engineering; full prompts are provided in Appendix
C
.
The simplest configuration serves as our
baseline
.
Its Doctor agent comprise of two components: a Question agent that interacts with the patient and a Diagnosis agent that predicts candidate diagnoses after each turn. Our full system,
MeDxAgent
, adds a summarizer, specialist ensemble, knowledge-graph module, and evidence-gap finder (Section
5
).

At initialization, the Patient receives the case description and the Judge receives the ground-truth diagnosis.
The Doctor begins with a standard opening question (“What brings you in today?”), to which the Patient responds with the primary concern.
At each subsequent turn, the Doctor asks a question conditioned on the conversation history, and the Patient responds strictly from the case description, returning “I don’t know” if the requested information is not present.
After each turn, the Doctor outputs three candidate diagnoses, each with a short rationale and a confidence score. The interaction continues until either a diagnosis reaches a 95% confidence threshold, selected on a 300-case validation split, or a 20-turn limit is reached (following
20
).
In the latter case, the highest-confidence diagnosis from the last turn is selected.
The final prediction is then evaluated by the Judge.
All experiments use temperature
=0
.

Patient Agent
.
The Patient answers strictly from the case description, without fabricating or inferring unstated details, responding with “I don’t know” otherwise.
This prevents diagnosis leakage,
since the Patient has access only to the case description and not the ground-truth label.
Cases are typically written retrospectively (e.g., as clinical notes), so we instruct the agent to respond as if currently experiencing the condition, in the first person, and in plain language.

Doctor Agent.

The baseline Doctor has two components, both operating at each turn.

(1) Question Agent
generates the next query and a brief rationale, from the conversation history.
We instruct it to ask simple, atomic questions covering key clinical dimensions (including demographics, symptoms, medical and family history, physical examination findings, and test results), to avoid repeating questions, to prioritize diagnostically informative questions, and to shift its line of inquiry when the Patient answers "I don’t know."

(2) Diagnosis Agent
produces three candidate diagnoses from the conversation history, each with a short justification and self-reported confidence score.
The agent is not conditioned on its own prior predictions; Section
5
studies that choice directly.

Judge Agent
.
The Judge uses LLM-based semantic matching to
determine whether the predicted and ground-truth diagnosis refer to the same condition.
A prediction is considered correct if it is (i) an exact match, (ii) a recognized synonym or paraphrase (e.g., high BP vs. hypertension), or (iii) a more specific subtype of the ground-truth (e.g., malignant melanoma for melanoma).
Predictions more general than the ground-truth are considered incorrect.
For multi-label cases, a match with any ground-truth label counts as correct, since multiple diagnoses can explain the same presentation.

5
MeDxAgent
System

We study how design choices affect multi-turn diagnosis.
We treat each design choice as a hypothesis and group them by where they intervene:
(1)
Prompt-based
choices modify the instructions provided to the Question agent, (2)
Flow-based
choices change the interaction flow, and (3)
Agent-based
choices introduce additional agents for summarization, reasoning, or knowledge grounding.
We evaluate each choice incrementally(Table
6
).
The Patient and Judge agents remain fixed across all variants, ensuring that performance differences arise only from the design choice under study.
We use clinician analogies to motivate these design choices, not to claim physician-like reasoning.

5.1
Prompt-Based Variations

We evaluate two prompt modifications to the baseline, as well as their combination.

Turn Awareness.

We provide the Question agent with the remaining question budget and turn-specific guidance: early turns (1-5) focus on chief complaints and high-yield screening questions, middle turns (6-15) gather objective evidence and differentiate symptoms, and late turns (16-20) prioritize the most discriminative questions. Because early questions shape the utility of later follow-ups, question ordering compounds over the consultation. We test whether turn budget awareness helps the agent plan and allocate its questions better.

Demographics First.

We require the Question agent to collect age and sex in the first turn.
This mirrors clinical practice, where demographic information helps anchor the differential diagnosis

5
.
In the baseline, the agent is encouraged, but not required, to ask about demographics.
We test whether fixing demographics at the start orients the diagnostic process by ruling out age- and sex-dependent implausible candidates.

Combined Prompt.

We combine both modifications; apply turn awareness at every step and require demographic collection in the first turn.

5.2
Flow-Based Variations

Building on the best prompt variant (Table
6
),
Combined Prompt
, we study flow-based modifications.

No Early Stopping.

The baseline ends a consultation once the Diagnosis agent reports
\geq 95\%
confidence.
Here, we remove this and run all consultations for the full 20 turns, and select the highest-confidence diagnosis from the final turn.
We test whether gathering more evidence before committing improves diagnostic accuracy.

Differential Questioning.

In the baseline, the next question is generated independently of the current diagnosis predictions.
Here, the Question agent additionally receives the candidate diagnoses, confidence scores, and supporting reasoning, and is instructed to ask questions that differentiate competing hypotheses. This is motivated by hypothetico-deductive reasoning, in which physicians form hypotheses early and gather evidence to confirm or reject them
10
.
This strategy has a known failure mode: conditioning too early on weak hypotheses can lead to
anchoring

13
;
7
.
We therefore activate differential questioning only after a fixed turn threshold, testing switch points at turns 2, 5, and 10.
Activating at turn 2 enables it almost from the start, since the first turn collects demographics; switching at later turns allow more general evidence to accumulate before conditioning future questions on diagnostic hypotheses.

Combined Flow.

We combine no early stopping with differential questioning activated at turn 10.

5.3
Agent-Based Variations

Building on the best Flow variant,
Differential Questioning (Turn 10)
, we introduce four agents.

Summarizer.

Instead of passing the raw conversation to the Diagnosis agent, a Summarizer converts it into a structured clinical representation.
A physician distills an encounter into a concise problem representation that organizes findings and activates relevant diagnosis
4
.
Additionally, LLMs are trained primarily on case-style medical text (e.g., clinical notes, USMLE-style vignettes) rather than conversational transcripts, making summarized representations better aligned with their training data.
We test two formats:
(a) a free-form paragraph-style clinical narrative, and
(b) a structured SOAP-style schema
35
with fields such as
symptoms_present
,
medical_history
, and
test_results
.

Specialist Ensemble.

We use an ensemble of ten physician agents: nine specialists (Cardiology, Pulmonology, Gastroenterology, Neurology, Endocrinology, Nephrology, Rheumatology, Haematology, and Dermatology) and one general physician.
Each agent reviews the conversation and predicts its top three diagnoses with confidence scores and reasoning, producing a diverse candidate pool.
A separate Selector-SE agent, designed as a senior physician, selects the final top three diagnosis by weighting specialists according to their relevance to the current case rather than by majority voting. This follows multidisciplinary clinical practice, where diverse specialists contribute to a single case. We test whether such diversity surfaces hypotheses missed by a single generalist agent.

Knowledge Graph.

Structured medical knowledge sources can surface relevant candidates and improve the factual grounding of predictions
12
.
We therefore ground candidate diagnoses generation in the Unified Biomedical Knowledge Graph (UBKG)
30
.
At each turn, we query UBKG using symptoms revealed by the Patient and aggregate the diseases it returns. Diseases are scored based on the number of matched symptoms, and the top three are passed to the Selector-KG agent.
As UBKG does not incorporate clinical evidence such as test results, the base Diagnosis agent also generates its own top three predictions using the conversation history.
The Selector-KG receives both sets and selects a final top three based on consistency with the conversation.
We test whether knowledge graph grounding contributes complementary hypotheses that the LLM overlooks.

Evidence Gap.

Clinicians gather information selectively, directing questions toward the evidence that would most change the differential diagnosis
4
.
The Evidence Gap agent follows this principle, identifying information still needed to support or reject a diagnosis.
It identifies:
(i)
diagnosis-specific gaps
that help differentiate current candidate diagnoses, and
(ii)
general gaps
covering broadly informative clinical details (such as vitals, lab results).
The agent outputs up to three gaps per type, expressed as short, actionable statements, ranked by estimated diagnostic utility.
Before turn 10, when candidate diagnoses remain unused by the Question agent, it reports only general gaps using the dialog history. From turn 10, when Differential Questioning is active, it also receives the top three candidates and reports both gap types.
The Question agent is prompted to prioritize these gaps when forming the next query.
We test whether explicitly modeling missing evidence improves information gathering.

Combined Agents:
MeDxAgent
.

It integrates all four agents (Figure
1
).
The Summarizer runs at every turn, converting dialogue into a structured clinical representation.
During early turns (1-9), the Question agent gathers information broadly, guided only by general evidence gaps.
From turn 10, the system enters a differential diagnosis phase. The Knowledge Graph retrieves symptom-matched diseases, the Specialist Ensemble proposes additional specialty-specific hypotheses, and the Selector consolidates these candidates into a final top three.
The Evidence Gap agent now reports both general and diagnosis-specific gaps, enabling the Question agent to ask question based on current hypotheses and missing evidence.

Using GPT-4o as the underlying model,
MeDxAgent
achieves 57.4% average diagnostic accuracy on
MeDxBench
, 10.3 points above the baseline (47.1%) (Table
6
).
Performance improves monotonically across the prompt-, flow-, and agent-level additions.
Gains hold across all five constituent datasets, although absolute accuracy varies: from 27.4% on DiagnosisArena to 66.6% on PubMed.
The full information Oracle, which receives the complete case upfront, reaches only 66.8%, indicating that the task is far from solved even with perfect information.
Against this ceiling,
MeDxAgent
closes 52.3% of the baseline-to-Oracle gap.
The remaining 9.4-point gap is likely due to these reasons: degradation of LLM performance in multi-turn settings
18
, incomplete information due to frequent “I don’t know” responses from the Patient agent, and possible training-data contamination suggested by PubMed’s near-ceiling Oracle accuracy (92.0%).

Different variants contribute differently.

Combined Prompt reaches 51.7%, improving over the baseline by 4.6 points through two changes to the Question agent: collecting demographics in the first turn and conditioning on the remaining turn budget.
Combined Flow reaches 52.8%, driven primarily by differential questioning. Treating differential questioning as a timed intervention rather than an always-on capability reveals strong sensitivity.
Activating it at turn 2 reduces accuracy to 34.7%, 12.4 points below the baseline. Delaying activation to turn 5 recovers most of the loss (50.4%) and turn 10 achieves 52.8%, yielding an 18.1-point swing from changing only the switch point.
Removing early stopping does not improve performance over Combined Prompt (51.6% vs. 51.7%), which suggests that the Diagnosis agent’s confidence-based stopping criterion is well-calibrated: consultations end early (mean 16.3 turns) once sufficient evidence is gathered.

The agent layer shows strong compositional effects.
The agent layer provides the largest single gain, from 52.8% at Differential Questioning (Turn 10) to 57.4% with all four agents (+4.6 points).
Individually, only the Summarizer improves performance, in both formats (+2.1 paragraph, +1.8 structured). The other three agents each reduce accuracy by 1-2 points when added in isolation.
Even though the sum of the four individual gains is negative, the full system outperforms every partial configuration.
Leave-one-out ablations further confirm this dependence: removing any agent from
MeDxAgent
reduces accuracy below the full system (Table
5
), indicating that no agent is redundant. The largest accuracy drop occurs when removing the (Summarizer) agent, which contributed the most.

Cross-model transfer.

The design choices developed on GPT-4o transfer to other model families, Grok-4.1 and DeepSeek-V3.2, without re-tuning prompts or thresholds (Table
3
).
Absolute gains are smaller on Grok-4.1 (51.5 to 53.8) and DeepSeek-V3.2 (51.5 to 54.5) than on GPT-4o (47.1 to 57.4), but each layer consistently improves over the baseline across models, and
MeDxAgent
remains the strongest variant, which shows the design generalizes beyond the model it was developed on.
The larger gains on GPT-4o may stem from greater headroom for improvement, since it begins further from its Oracle ceiling than Grok-4.1 and DeepSeek-V3.2.

Note: Scores on Pubmed differ from Table
6
because Vivabench uses a different evaluation setup.
These improvements indicate that
MeDxAgent
’s design choices transfer across datasets and model families.

7
Discussion

What worked and why.
Three design choices account for most of the gains, and each aligns with established principles of clinical reasoning. First, collecting demographics first orient the differential diagnosis because age and sex strongly shape disease priors.
For instance, in 39 pediatric cases, demographics were the key differentiating factor in diagnosis: the baseline defaulted to adult presentations and missed age-specific conditions, whereas the demographics-first variant correctly identified them.
This matches prior reports of demographic biases in medical LLMs
24
and shows a single-line prompt intervention can help mitigate them in an agentic system. Second, Differential Questioning helps, but only after sufficiently strong hypotheses have formed.
This mirrors hypothetico-deductive pattern of clinical reasoning, in which physicians form hypotheses and gather evidence to confirm or rule them out
10
, and its associated failure mode, anchoring, where weak early hypotheses bias subsequent reasoning
13
. Our experiments show that the same instruction and interaction flow can improve or reduce performance depending solely on when it is activated. Third, the Summarizer improves accuracy
by changing how evidence is represented rather than what evidence is available. Clinicians refer to this as problem representation, and prior work links higher-quality representations to more accurate diagnosis
4
. Also, summarization may help because structured clinical summaries
more closely resemble the case-style medical text on which LLMs are predominantly trained.

Why three agents fail in isolation.

The remaining three agents lower accuracy individually, each for a different reason.
The Specialist Ensemble and Knowledge Graph (KG) variants are prone to premature termination (Table
7
). In the Specialist Ensemble, repeated predictions across specialists inflate aggregate confidence during ensembling. In the KG variant, the Selector-KG overweights KG-derived candidates. These causes early stopping before sufficient evidence is gathered, reducing accuracy.
The Evidence Gap agent appears to fail due to confirmation bias. Although it extracts more information, as reflected by a sharp reduction in “I don’t know” answers relative to the baseline (Figure
5
), it tends to direct questions toward confirming the leading hypothesis. When that hypothesis is incorrect, the system gathers increasingly detailed evidence for the wrong disease, lowering performance. Despite this, each agent contributes positively in
MeDxAgent
. Leave-one-out ablations (Table
5
) show that removing any agent from the full system lowers accuracy.
We do not claim that the combined system fully resolves these issues; however, we provide empirical evidence for why the combined setup leads to improved accuracy.

Components work in combination.

The strong compositional effects arise because the modules are designed to operate jointly.
The Summarizer produces a structured representation, which helps the Knowledge Graph and Specialist Ensemble generate ‘diverse’ diagnostic candidates. These candidates enable Evidence Gap to identify missing evidence more reliably, which in turn helps the Question agent to ask more targeted questions and reduce "I don’t know" responses.
As discussed, individually, the Knowledge Graph and Specialist Ensemble tend to be overconfident, leading to premature termination. When combined, however, their predictions are balanced, reflected in lower early stopping rates (Table
7
).
Similarly, the Evidence Gap agent alone is prone to confirmation bias, but benefits from the more diverse candidate set produced by the combined system, reducing anchoring effects.
Overall, each agent in isolation perturbs a system not built to absorb it, often reducing accuracy. Together, the agents become complementary, and removing any one lowers accuracy. Prior multi-agent medical systems typically report aggregate gains over single-agent baselines without isolating individual agent contributions
22
;
17
;
31
, leaving such compositional effects unexplored. We highlight this phenomenon empirically, though
we do not claim it generalizes to all multi-agent systems.

Real-world deployment.

Despite promising performance,
MeDxAgent
should be viewed strictly as a decision-support tool for clinicians, not a replacement for medical professionals. It is not suitable for autonomous use by non-medical personnel.
Incorrect or overconfident diagnoses could delay treatment, trigger unnecessary interventions, or cause serious patient harm, particularly in high-risk clinical settings.
Moreover, conversational diagnostic systems may inherit biases (e.g., demography-related) and reasoning failures from their underlying LLMs.
In practice, such systems may be most appropriate for assisting physicians with differential diagnosis, surfacing alternative hypotheses, or double-checking clinical decisions.

8
Conclusion

We present
MeDxBench
, a benchmark of
4{,}421
clinical cases across 20 specialities for open-ended interactive diagnosis and
MeDxAgent
, a multi-agent system built from a systematic study of prompt-, flow- and agent-level design choices.
Beyond these, our contribution is methodological: we isolate the impact of individual design decisions and reveal interaction effects.

9
Limitations

First, our evaluation is limited to text-only diagnosis, while real-world clinical diagnosis often depends on multimodal evidence such as imaging.
Second, the Patient agent answers strictly from the case description, which prevents diagnosis leakage, but reduces the natural variability and ecological validity of real patient interactions, as patients rarely respond with repeated "I don’t know" responses. Finally, although we systematically study several design choices, a broader sweep over prompts, interaction flows, and agent configurations would strengthen the empirical conclusions; though such sweeps are computationally expensive in multi-turn agentic settings. Future work can address these limitations and evaluate whether the design choices that help in simulation also transfer to supervised clinical use, with multimodal inputs, and learned questioning policies beyond prompt engineering.

A.4
Accuracy of the Patient Agent

We evaluate the Patient agent using an LLM-as-a-judge over all responses generated by
MeDxAgent
, totaling 84,739 question-answer pairs across 4,421 cases. The Patient agent achieves an overall response accuracy of 97.2%.
Among the 2,387 inaccurate responses (2.8%), we identify four error types:
withholding
(1,513 responses; 63.4% of errors), where the Patient omits information present in the case description, typically failing to mention a detail when asked a broad question;
fabrication
(457; 19.1%), where the Patient introduces clinical details not present in the case;
contradiction
(368; 15.4%), where the response conflicts with information in the case; and
oversharing
(48; 2.0%), where the Patient provides information not solicited by the doctor’s question.
Overall, these results suggest that the Patient agent remains highly faithful to the underlying case descriptions.

Summarizer: Structured

MeDxAgent

Summarizer: Paragraph Prompt

Role.

You are a medical scribe summarizing a doctor-patient consultation.

Instruction.

Create a concise case summary from the dialogue for downstream diagnosis.

Rules.

•

Include only information explicitly stated by the patient.

•

Do not infer or add information.

•

Organize the summary logically by demographics, chief complaint, symptoms, history, examination findings, and test results.

•

Use appropriate medical terminology.

•

Write in third person.

•

Keep the summary concise but complete.

Output.

Return only the case summary, without headers or explanations.

C.5
Evidence Gap Agent

The Evidence Gap agent identifies clinically informative missing evidence from the current case state. In early turns, it highlights general missing information (e.g., vitals, labs, history details), while after differential questioning begins, it additionally identifies diagnosis-specific gaps that would help distinguish among the current top candidate diagnoses. These gaps are then used by the Evidence-Guided question agents (EG-QA and EG-DQA, Section
C.2
) to guide subsequent question selection.

Evidence Gap Agent Prompt

Role.

You are a clinical evidence analyst identifying missing diagnostic information.

Instruction.

Given the current clinical summary and top-3 differential diagnoses, produce an evidence-gap report.

Gap types.

•

General gaps:
missing baseline information useful for any diagnostic workup, such as demographics, vitals, symptom timeline, medical history, medications, family history, exposures, and basic tests.

•

Diagnosis-specific gaps:
missing evidence required to confirm or rule out each candidate diagnosis.

Rules.

•

Focus on actionable information that can be obtained from the patient or standard tests.

•

Prioritize gaps that would most strongly shift confidence between competing diagnoses.

•

Include red-flag gaps when missing information could indicate serious disease.

•

Provide concise, clinically useful missing-evidence statements.

Input.

•

Clinical summary: {summary}

•

Current top-3 differential diagnoses: {predictions}

Output format.

{
"general_gaps": [
"missing general information item"
],
"diagnosis_specific_gaps": [
{
"disease": "Diagnosis name",
"missing_evidence": "Required evidence"
}
]
}

C.6
Judge Agent

The Judge evaluates the final predicted diagnosis against the ground-truth label using semantic matching, accepting exact matches, synonyms, paraphrases and more specific subtypes of the ground-truth diagnosis (not vice versa), while rejecting different or even near-misses across distinct conditions (e.g., Type 1 vs. Type 2 diabetes).

Judge Agent Prompt

Role.

You are a medical terminology expert comparing disease names.

Instruction.

Determine whether the predicted disease and the ground-truth disease refer to the same medical condition.

Rules.

•

Treat medical synonyms, abbreviations, and common-name variants as equivalent.

•

Treat a predicted disease as correct if it is a more specific subtype of the ground truth.

•

Be strict when clinically distinct subtypes are involved; they should not be treated as the same condition, e.g., Type 1 vs. Type 2 diabetes.

•

Return false when the diseases are related but not equivalent.

Input.

•

Predicted disease: {predicted}

•

Ground-truth disease: {ground_truth}

Output format.

{
"same": true/false,
"explanation": "brief explanation"
}
