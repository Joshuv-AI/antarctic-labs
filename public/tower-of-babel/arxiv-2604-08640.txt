Galois theory for finite fields
Askold Khovanskii

Abstract
This note presents Galois theory for finite fields. It was written as a handout for the MAT401 course “Polynomial equations and fields” taught at the University of Toronto in Spring 2026. We use without proofs some basic properties of finite fields and of finite field extensions which we already covered in class. Firstly, we describe an extension K ⊂ F K\subset F of a finite field K K of a given degree n n . We show that the set of all intermediate fields for this extension is in one-to-one correspondence with the set of all divisors k k of the degree n n . Then we describe the Galois group of this extension which is the cyclic group of order n n . The set of subgroups of this group also is in one-to-one correspondence with the set of all divisors k k of the degree n n . It allows us to prove the Galois correspondence for that extension. In the last section, we state basic theorems of Galois theory for arbitrary fields which will be proven later in the course.

Preliminaries on finite fields

In this section, we list simple results on finite fields which we studied in class. We will use these results without proof. Such proofs can be found in many places, see for example a textbook
[
1
]
which we use in the course.

1.

Each finite field
F
has characteristic
p
, where
p
is some prime number, i.e.,
F
contains the subfield field
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
. The field
F
is a finite extension of the field
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
. The degree
n
of this extension together with the characteristic
p
totally determines the finite field
F
which is called
the Galois field

\mathbb{F}_{q}
, where
q=p^{n}
. The field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
can be considered as an
n
-dimensional vector space over the field
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
. It contains
q=p^{n}
elements.

2.

A field
K
contains the field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
if and only if
K
has characteristic
p
and the polynomial
x^{q}-x
has exactly
q=p^{n}
roots in
K
. Moreover, the set of elements in the subfield
\mathbb{F}_{q}\subset K
is equal to the set of all roots in
K
of the polynomial
x^{q}-x
.

3.

For any field
K
of characteristic
p
the map
\Phi:K\to K
, which sends
x
to
x^{p}
, is called the
Frobenius homomorphism
. The homomorphism
\Phi
may not be an one-to-one map: some elements of
K
could have no preimages under the map
\Phi
. But if
K
is a finite field, then
\Phi:K\to K
is an onto map.

4.

The statement 2) means that the
\mathbb{F}_{q}
belongs to
K
if and only if the
q
-th power
\Phi^{q}:K\to K
of the Frobenius homomorphism has exactly
q=p^{n}
fixed points, and the set
\mathbb{F}_{q}\subset K
is equal to the set of all fixed points of
\Phi^{n}
.

3
Around Chinese Remainder Theorem

In this section, we recall the famous Chinese Remainder Theorem and its versions.

Let
m_{1},\dots,m_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}
be a collection of natural numbers such that any two different numbers
m_{i},m_{j}
from the collection are relatively prime. Let
\pi:\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{Z}/m_{1}\mathbb{Z}\oplus\ldots\oplus\mathbb{Z}/m_{n}\mathbb{Z}
be a ring homomorphism from
\mathbb{Z}
to the direct sum of the factor rings
\mathbb{Z}/m_{i}\mathbb{Z}
which sends
n\in\mathbb{Z}
to
(n\mod m_{1},\ldots,n\mod m_{n})
.

Lemma 3.1
.

The kernel of the homomorphism
\pi
is the principal ideal
(d)\subset\mathbb{Z}
, where
d=m_{1}\cdot\ldots\cdot m_{n}
.

Proof.

The principal ideal
(m_{i})\subset\mathbb{Z}
of the number
m_{i}
is the kernel of the factorization map
\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{Z}/m_{i}\mathbb{Z}
. The kernel of
\pi
is the ideal
\cap_{1\leq i\leq n}(m_{i})\subset\mathbb{Z}
. Since the numbers
m_{i}
are relatively prime, we have

\cap_{1\leq i\leq n}(m_{i})=(m_{1}\cdot\ldots\cdot m_{n})=(d)
.
∎

Corollary 3.2
.

If the natural numbers
m_{1},\dots,m_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}
are relatively prime, then the ring
\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z}
, where
d=m_{1}\cdot\ldots\cdot m_{n}
, is isomorphic to the direct sum
\oplus_{1\leq i\leq n}\mathbb{Z}/m_{i}\mathbb{Z}
of the rings
\mathbb{Z}/m_{i}\mathbb{Z}
.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.1
, the homomorphism
\pi
provides an embedding of the finite ring
\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z}
to the direct sum
\oplus_{1\leq i\leq n}\mathbb{Z}/m_{i}\mathbb{Z}
of the finite rings
\mathbb{Z}/m_{i}\mathbb{Z}
. Since the rings
\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z}
and
\oplus_{1\leq i\leq n}\mathbb{Z}/m_{i}\mathbb{Z}
contain the same number
d
of elements, the embedding
\pi
is an isomorphism.
∎

Corollary 3.3
.

If the natural numbers
m_{1},\dots,m_{n}
are pairwise relatively prime, then the multiplicative group
U(d)
of invertible elements in the ring
\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z}
, where
d=m_{1}\cdot\ldots\cdot m_{n}
, is isomorphic to the direct product
\prod_{1\leq i\leq n}U(m_{i})
of the multiplicative groups
U(m_{i})
of invertible elements in the ring
\mathbb{Z}/m_{i}\mathbb{Z}
.

Proof.

Corollary
3.3
follows from Corollary
3.2
.
∎

Below, in section
5
, we will provide a description of the group
U(p^{m})
for any prime number
p
and any natural natural
m
.

Now we will compute the number of elements in the group
U(p^{m})
and, more generally, the number of elements in the group
U(n)
for an arbitrary natural number
n
.

Corollary 3.4
.

Let
n=p_{1}^{k_{1}}\cdot\ldots\cdot p_{m}^{k_{m}}
be the prime factorization of a natural number
n
. Then the group
U(n)
contains
\prod_{1\leq i\leq n}p_{i}^{m_{i}-1}(p_{i}-1)
elements.

Proof.

By Corollary
3.3
, it is enough to prove the above corollary for
n=p^{m}
. The number of elements in the group
U(n)
is equal to the number of elements in
\mathbb{Z}/(n)
which are relatively prime with
n
. For
n=p^{m}
the number of elements in
\mathbb{Z}/(p^{m})
, not relatively prime with
p^{m}
, is equal to
p^{m}:p=p^{m-1}
. Indeed, a number is not relatively prime with
p^{m}
if and only if it is divisible by
p
.
∎

Corollary 3.5

(Chinese Remainder Theorem)
.

If the natural numbers
m_{1},\dots,m_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}
are pairwise relatively prime, then the cyclic group
\mathbb{Z}_{d}
of order
d=m_{1}\cdot\ldots\cdot m_{n}
is isomorphic to the direct sum
\oplus_{1\leq i\leq n}\mathbb{Z}_{m_{i}}
of the cyclic groups
\mathbb{Z}_{m_{i}}
of orders
m_{i}
.

Proof.

Corollary
3.5
also follows from Corollary
3.2
.
∎

4
Multiplicative group of roots of unity

In this section, we recall basic properties of cyclic groups, and applications of these properties to studies of roots of unity in an arbitrary field.

Lemma 4.1
.

Any subgroup of a cyclic group is also a cyclic group. The cyclic group
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
of order
n
contains a subgroup of order
k
if and only if
k
divides
n
. Moreover, a subgroup of order
k
is unique and it consists of all elements
x\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}\sim\mathbb{Z}/\mod n
satisfying the relation
kx=0\mod n
.

Proof.

Any nonzero subgroup
G\subset\mathbb{Z}
is a cyclic group generated by the smallest positive element in
G
. The group
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
is a factor group of
\mathbb{Z}
, thus each nonzero subgroup of
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
is a cyclic group, whose order
k
divides
n
, which contains
n:k
elements. All elements
x\in G
satisfy the equation
kx=0/\mod n
. But the number of solutions of this equation is also equal to
n:k
. Thus,
G
coincides with the set of solutions
kx=0\mod n
and, therefore, it is unique.
∎

Theorem 4.2
.

A finite commutative group
G
is cyclic if and only if for any natural number
k
it has at most one cyclic subgroup of order
k
.

Proof.

According to Lemma
4.1
, a cyclic group for any natural number
k
has at most one cyclic subgroup of order
k
.
On the other hand, any finite commutative group
G
is representable as a direct sum of cyclic groups of some orders
m_{1},\dots,m_{n}
. If some different numbers
m_{i}
and
m_{j}
have a nontrivial common
divisor
d
, then both cyclic groups
\mathbb{Z}_{m_{i}}
and
\mathbb{Z}_{m_{j}}
have cyclic subgroups of order
d
. In that case, according to Lemma
4.1
, the group
G
is not a cyclic group. If the numbers
m_{1},\dots,m_{n}
are pairwise relatively prime, then, by Corollary
3.5
, the group
G
is cyclic.
∎

For any field
K
and for any natural number
n
let
K^{*}_{n}\subset K
be the set of all roots of unity of order
n
in the field
K
, i.e.,
K^{*}_{n}
is the set of all solution in
K
of the equation
x^{n}-1=0
.

Theorem 4.3
.

For any field
K
and for any natural number
n
the set
K^{*}_{n}\subset K
form a subgroup in the multiplicative group
K^{*}
of the field
K
. Moreover,
K^{*}_{n}
is a cyclic group.

Proof.

If
x^{n}=1
and
y^{n}=1
, then
(xy)^{n}=1
. If
x^{n}=1
, then
(x^{-1})^{n}=1
. Thus,
K^{*}_{n}
is a subgroup in the multiplicative group
K^{*}
of the field
K
.
The group
K^{*}_{n}
is a finite commutative group. For any natural number
k
the equation
x^{k}=1
has at most
k
solutions in
K
. Thus, the group
K^{*}_{n}
could contain at most one cyclic subgroup of order
k
: such a subgroup exists if and only if the polynomial
x^{k}-1
has exactly
k
different roots in the field
K
. Thus, by Theorem
4.2
,
K^{*}_{n}
is a cyclic group.
∎

5
Multiplicative group
U(p^{n})

In this section, we will show that for any prime number
p\neq 2
and any natural number
n
the multiplicative group
U(p^{n})
is a cyclic group. We also will describe groups
U(2^{n})
for any natural number
n
. First of all, the group
U(p)
for any prime
p\neq 2
is a cyclic group, since it is the multiplicative group of the field
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
which coincides with the group of roots of unity of the order
p-1
in the field
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
. Below, we describe groups
U(p^{n})
assuming that
n>1
.

Assume that
p\neq 2
. Consider the ring homomorphism
\pi:\mathbb{Z}/(p^{n})\to\mathbb{Z}_{p}
. Its kernel consists of all integers modulo
p^{n}
which are divisible by
p
, thus it consists of all not invertible elements of the ring
\mathbb{Z}/(p^{n})
. The kernel of the corresponding group homomorphism
\pi_{1}:U(p^{n})\to U(p)
of the multiplicative groups of these rings contains the element
(1+p)\mod p^{n}
.

Lemma 5.1
.

For
p\neq 2
the kernel of the homomorphism
\pi_{1}
is a cyclic group of the order
p^{n-1}
.

Lemma
5.1
is based on the following observation.

Lemma 5.2
.

For any prime
p\neq 2
and for any
n>1
the order of the element
1+p\mod p^{n}
in the group
U(p^{n})
is equal to
p^{n-1}
.

Proof.

The element
1+p\mod p^{n}
belongs to the kernel of the group homomorphism
\pi_{1}
which is a subgroup in
U(p^{n})
of the order
p^{n-1}
. Thus, the order
k
of the element
(1+p)\mod p^{n}
divides the number
p^{n-1}
, so
k=p^{m}
for some number
m
satisfying the following inequalities
1\leq m\leq n-1
.
For any natural number
m
the identity
(1+p^{m})^{p}\equiv 1+p^{m+1}\mod p^{m++2}
holds. Indeed,
(1+p^{m})^{p}=1+p\cdot p^{m}+\frac{p(p-1)}{2}p^{2m}+\dots\equiv 1+p^{m+1}\mod p^{m+2}
.
(Note that in the proof of above identities for
m=1
one
use that for
p\neq 2
the number
\frac{p(p-1)}{2}
is divisible by
p
.)
∎

Proof of Lemma
5.1
.

As we showed above, for any
k<m-1
the identity
(1+p)^{p^{k}}\equiv 1+p^{k+1}\mod p^{k+2}
holds. The smallest degree
k
, for which
(1+p)^{p^{k}}\equiv 1\mod p^{n}
, is
k=p^{n-1}
. Thus, the order of the element
1+p\mod p^{n}
is equal to the number of elements in the group
\ker\pi_{1}
containing it. So this element generates the group
\ker\pi_{1}
and it is cyclic.
∎

Theorem 5.3
.

For any prime
p\neq 2
and for any natural number
n
the group
U(p^{n})
is a cyclic group.

Proof.

The homomorphism
\pi_{1}:U(p^{n})\to U(p)
is a surjective map. Let
a\in U(p)
be a generator of the cyclic group
U(p)
and let
b\in U(p^{n})
be such an element that
\pi_{1}(b)=a
. Then
b
generates a cyclic subgroup
G_{b}\subset U(p^{n})
of the order
p-1
. One can see that the group
U(p^{n})
is a direct sum of the cyclic subgroup
\ker\pi_{1}
of the order
p^{n-1}
and the cyclic subgroup
G_{b}
of the order
p-1
. Since
p^{n-1}
and
p-1
are relatively prime numbers, the group
U(p^{n})
is cyclic.
∎

The case
p=2
has to be considered separately. Consider the multiplicative group
U(2^{n})
of the ring
\mathbb{Z}/2^{n}\mathbb{Z}
for
n\geq 3
. Elements of the group
U(2^{n})
may be reduced modulo
8
.
Any element of
U(2^{n})
is equal either to
\pm 1\mod 8
or to
\pm 3\mod 8
.

Lemma 5.4
.

1.

If
a\equiv\pm 1\mod 8
, then for any
k\geq 1
we have
a^{2^{k}}\equiv 1\mod 2^{k+3}
.

2.

If
a\equiv\pm 3\mod 8
, then for any
k\geq 1
we have
a^{2^{k}}\equiv 2^{k+2}\mod 2^{k+3}
.

Proof.

One can prove this lemma by induction in
k
.

1.

For
k=1
we have
a^{2^{1}}=a^{2}=(\pm 1+8n)^{2}=1\pm 16n+16n^{2}=1\mod 16=1\mod 2^{1+3}
. If for some
k
we have
a^{2^{k}}=1+2^{k+3}n
, then
a^{2^{k+1}}=1+2\cdot 2^{k+3}n+2^{2k+6}n^{2}\equiv 1\mod 2^{(k+1)+3}
. The needed statement is proven.

2.

For
k=1
we have
a^{2^{1}}=a^{2}=(\pm 3+8n)^{2}=9\pm 16\cdot 3n+16n^{2}=1+8\mod 16=1+2^{1+2}\mod 2^{1+3}
. If for some
k
we have
a^{2^{k}}=1+2^{k+2}+2^{k+3}n
, then
a^{2^{k+1}}=(1+2^{k+2})^{2}+2\cdot 2^{k+3}n(1+2^{k+2})+2^{2(k+3)}n^{2}\equiv 1+2^{k+3}\mod 2^{k+4}
. The needed statement is proven.

∎

Theorem 5.5
.

For any natural number
n>2
the group
U(2^{n})
is isomorphic to the product
\mathbb{Z}/2^{n-2}\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
of cyclic groups
\mathbb{Z}/2^{n-2}\mathbb{Z}
and
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
.

Proof.

The group
U(2^{n})
is a commutative group of the order
2^{n-1}
. Lemma
5.4
implies that the order of each element of the group is less than
2^{n-1}
, thus the group is not cyclic. By Lemma
5.4
part 2), the order of the element
a\equiv 3\mod 2^{n}
is equal to

2^{n-2}
. Thus, the group
U(2^{n})
contains a cyclic subgroup isomorphic to the group
\mathbb{Z}/2^{n-2}\mathbb{Z}
. This index of this subgroup in
U(2^{n})
is equal to two. The only commutative group with such properties is the group

\mathbb{Z}/2^{n-2}\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
.
∎

One can easily check the following lemma which completes the description of the groups
U(p^{n})
.

Lemma 5.6
.

The group
U(4)
is isomorphic to the group
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
. The group
U(2)
contains only an identity element.

6
Multiplicative group of finite field

In this section, using the result on roots of unity,
we show that the multiplicative group of an arbitrary finite field is a cyclic group. As an application, we prove a version of the Primitive Element Theorem for finite fields.

Theorem 6.1
.

The multiplicative group
\mathbb{F}^{*}_{q}
of the Galois field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
with
q=p^{n}
is a cyclic group of order
q-1=p^{n}-1
.

Proof.

The set of all
q-1
nonzero elements of the field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
coincides with the set of all roots of the polynomial
x^{q-1}-1
.
∎

Corollary 6.2
.

In any finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, there is an element
a
such that any nonzero element
b
of the field is representable in the form
b=a^{m}
, where
m
is a natural number.

Proof.

As
a
one can take a generator of the cyclic group
\mathbb{F}^{*}_{q}
.
∎

Remark 6.3
.

An element
a\in E
is called a
primitive element
for a finite field extension
K\subset E
if
E
can be obtained by adjoining
a
to the field
K
. The above corollary implies that a finite extension of a finite field always has a primitive element. One can show that
for any separable finite field extension
K\subset E
of an arbitrary field
K
there exists a primitive element (see Primitive Element Theorem in section
11
).

If
K\subset E
is a finite field extension, then
E
can be obtained by adjoining to
K
a finite set
\{m_{1},\dots,m_{n}\}
of algebraic elements over
K
. By Primitive Element Theorem, instead of adjoining to
K
finitely many elements one can adjoin just one element
s
. Below we will show that for finite fields such a result follows from properties of the greatest common divisor of a collection of elements on
\mathbb{Z}
.

Recall that for any collection
m_{1},\dots,m_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}
of nonzero natural numbers their greatest common divisor
s
is representable in the form
s=k_{1}m_{1}+\dots+k_{n}m_{n}
where
k_{1},\dots,k_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}
, and for any
1\leq i\leq n
the number
m_{i}
is representable in the form
m_{i}=d_{i}s
, where
d_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
. One can apply this result to a cyclic group
G
, since
G
us a factor group
G
of
\mathbb{Z}
. In the next lemma we apply it to a cyclic group
G
in which the group operation is written in the multiplicative form.

Lemma 6.4
.

For any collection
m_{1},\dots,m_{n}\in G
of element in
G
there exist an
n
tuple of integers
k_{1},\dots,k_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}
such that the element
s=m_{1}^{k_{1}}\cdot\ldots\cdot m_{n}^{k_{n}}
has the following property: for any
1\leq i\leq n
the element
m_{i}\in G
is representable in the form
m_{i}=s^{q_{i}}
for some
q_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Theorem 6.5
.

Let
\mathbb{Z}_{p}(m_{1},\dots,m_{n})
be a subfield in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
for
q=p^{k}
generated over
\mathbb{Z}_{p}\subset\mathbb{F}_{q}
by elements
m_{1},\dots,m_{n}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
. Then the field
\mathbb{Z}_{p}(m_{1},\dots,m_{n})
can be generated over
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
by one element having a form
m_{1}^{k_{1}}\cdot\ldots\cdot m_{n}^{k_{n}}
.

Proof.

The multiplicative group of the field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
is cyclic. Thus, the theorem follows from Lemma
6.4
.
∎

Which subfields are contained in a finite field
\mathbb{F}_{p^{n}}
? The following lemma gives an answer to this question.

Lemma 6.6
.

The field
\mathbb{F}_{p^{n}}
contains the subfield
\mathbb{F}_{p^{k}}
if and only if
k
is a divisor of
n
.

In the next section, we prove Lemma
6.6
using the Degree formula.
Below we prove Lemma
6.6
using only properties of cyclic groups.
We will need the following lemma.

Lemma 6.7
.

Let
a,t,s\in\mathbb{Z}
be integers such that
a>1,s>0,t\geq 0
. Then the number
a^{t}-1
is divisible by the number
a^{s}-1
if and only if
t=ks
for some nonnegative
k
.

Proof.

Let us show first that if
t\geq s
, then the numbers
a^{t}-1
and
-(a^{t-s}-1)
are equal modulo
a^{s}-1
. Indeed, the following identity holds:
(a^{t-s}-1)a^{s}+a^{s}-1=a^{t}-1
. Reducing this identity modulo
a^{s}-1
, we obtain the needed equality
(a^{t-s}-1)(-1)=a^{t}-1\mod(a^{s}-1)
.
So if
a^{t}-1
is divisible by
a^{s}-1
and
t\geq s
, then
a^{t-s}-1
also is divisible by
a^{s}-1
. Let us divide
n
by
s
with a remainder

8
Automorphisms of finite fields

In this section, we study automorphisms of finite fields. In particular, we show that any automorphism of a finite field
F
of characteristic
p
is a power of the Frobinius map
\Phi:F\to F
.

Let
F
be a field of characteristic
p
. Recall that the Frobenius map
\Phi:F\to F
is the map which sends an element
a\in F
to the element
\Phi(a)=a^{p}
. The map
\Phi
is a field homomorphism, i.e., it is a one-to-one map which respects arithmetic operations. In general, the map
\Phi
is not an onto map.

Theorem 8.1
.

Let
F
be the finite field
\mathbb{F}_{p^{n}}
. Then:

1.

the Frobenius map
\Phi:\mathbb{F}_{p^{n}}\to\mathbb{F}_{p^{n}}
is an automorphism of
\mathbb{F}_{p^{n}}
;

2.

an element
a\in\mathbb{F}_{p^{n}}
is fixed under the map
\Phi^{k}
if and only if
a
is a root of the polynomial
x^{p^{k}}-x
. In particular, the map
\Phi^{k}
fixes at most
p^{k}
elements of the field
\mathbb{F}_{p^{n}}
;

3.

the map
\Phi^{k}
fixes exactly
p^{k}
elements of the field
\mathbb{F}_{p^{n}}
if and only if
k
is a divisor of
n
. Moreover, if
k
is a divisor of
n
, then the set of fixed elements of the map
\Phi^{k}
is the set of elements in the subfield
\mathbb{F}_{p^{k}}
.

Proof.

1) The map
\Phi:\mathbb{F}_{p^{n}}\to\mathbb{F}_{p^{n}}
is an onto map, since it is one-to-one map and
\mathbb{F}_{p^{n}}
is a finite set. Thus,
\Phi
is an automorphism of the field
\mathbb{F}_{p^{n}}
.

2) Follows from an identity
\Phi^{k}(a)\equiv a^{p^{k}}
.

3) A splitting field of the polynomial
x^{p^{k}}-x
is contained in the field
\mathbb{F}_{p^{n}}
if and only if
k
is a divisor of
n
. If
k
is a divisor of
n
, then the set of all roots in
\mathbb{F}_{p^{n}}
of the polynomial
x^{p^{k}}-x
coincides with the set of elements of the subfield
\mathbb{F}_{p^{k}}
.
∎

Lemma 8.2
.

Any collection
G
of automorphisms of the finite field
\mathbb{F}_{p^{n}}
contains at most
n
elements.

Proof.

Let
a\in\mathbb{F}^{*}_{p^{n}}
be a generator of the cyclic multiplicative group
\mathbb{F}^{*}_{p^{n}}
of the field
\mathbb{F}_{p^{n}}
. Let
P_{a}\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}[x]
be a minimal polynomial of
a
over the field
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
. Since
a
generates the field
\mathbb{F}_{p^{n}}
, the degree of the polynomial
P_{a}
is equal to the degree of the field extension
\mathbb{Z}_{p}\subset\mathbb{F}_{p^{n}}
which is equal to
n
. Any irreducible polynomial over the field
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
has simple roots only. So the polynomial
P_{a}
has at most
n
different roots in the field
\mathbb{F}_{p^{n}}
. Any automorphism
g
of the field
\mathbb{F}_{p^{n}}
fixes pointwise all elements of the subfield
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
. Since all coefficients of the polynomial
P_{a}
belong to the field
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
, an automorphism
g
maps the root
a
of the polynomial
P_{a}
to a root of the same polynomial
P_{a}
. An automorphism
g
is totally determined by the element
g(a)
, since
a
generates the multiplicative group
\mathbb{F}^{*}_{p^{n}}
of the field
\mathbb{F}_{p^{n}}
. Indeed, if
b\neq 0
and
b=a^{r}
, then

, if

, then
g(b)=0
.
Since
P_{a}
has at most
n
different roots in the field

, the collection of automorphisms

contains at most
n

∎

Theorem 8.3
.

Any automorphism of the field
\mathbb{F}_{p^{n}}
is a power
\Phi^{k}
of the Frobenius map
\Phi
. All automorphisms of the field
\mathbb{F}_{p^{n}}
form a cyclic group of order

generated by
\Phi
.

Proof.

The set
A=\{I=\Phi^{0},\Phi^{1},\dots,\Phi^{n-1}\}
contains
n
different automorphisms of the field
\mathbb{F}_{p^{n}}
. By Lemma
8.2
, all automorphisms of the field
\mathbb{F}_{p^{n}}
belong to the set

. All automorphisms of the field

form a cyclic group of order

, since

.
∎

9
Galois group of an extension of a finite field

In this section, we define the Galois group

of a finite extension
K\subset F
, where
K
and
F
are finite fields. We show that the group

is well defined, and describe it.

Definition 9.1
.

A finite extension

is

if there is a finite group

of automorphisms of the field
F
such that an element
x\in F
is fixed under the action of each element
g\in G(F,K)
, i.e.,
g(a)=a
, if and only if

. For a normal field extension

the group

is called the

of the extension.

One can show that the group
G(F,K)
is well defined: a group of automorphisms of the field
F
which fixes the subfield
K
only, if it exists, is unique. Below, we show that any nested pair
K\subset F
of finite fields is normal, and its Galois group is well defined. Moreover, we will completely describe this group.

First of all, Theorem
8.3
implies the following corollary.

Corollary 9.2
.

For any finite field
\mathbb{F}_{p^{n}}
the field extension
\mathbb{Z}_{p}\subset\mathbb{F}_{{}^{n}}
is normal, and its Galois group is well defined. Moreover, the Galois group is the cyclic group of order
n
, generated by the Frobenius map
\Phi
.

Below, we generalize Corollary
9.2
for any degree
n
extension of any finite field.

Theorem 9.3
.

Let
\mathbb{F}_{q}\subset\mathbb{F}_{q^{n}}
be the degree
n
extension of the field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, where
q=p^{s}
. Then this field extension is normal, and its Galois group is well defined. Moreover, the Galois group is the cyclic group of order
n
generated by the

of the Frobenius map
\Phi
.

Proof.

Consider the field extension
\mathbb{Z}_{p}\subset\mathbb{F}_{q^{n}}
of degree

. By Theorem

, its Galois group is well defined. Moreover, the Galois group is the cyclic group of order

, generated by the Frobenius map
\Phi
. Any group of automorphisms of the field
\mathbb{F}_{q^{n}}
is a subgroup of the group

). In particular, any such group is a cyclic group. Thus, the
subgroup of all automorphisms of the field
\mathbb{F}_{q^{n}}
, which fixes the field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, is a cyclic group. Let
\Phi^{k}
be the smallest positive power of
\Phi
which belongs to this subgroup. The power

can not be smaller than

\Phi^{k}
contains at most

. On the other hand, the subgroup contains the map
\Phi^{s}
. Indeed, the set of fixed points of the map

is equal to

\Phi^{s}
.
∎

11
Galois theory

In this section, we present, without proof, basic theorems of Galois theory which widely generalize the results on extensions of finite fields which we discuss above. Proofs of these theorems will be presented in the course later.

Definition 11.1
.

An element
a\in E
is called a
primitive element
for a finite field extension
K\subset E
if
E
can be obtained by adjoining
a
to the field
K
.

Theorem 11.2

(Primitive Element Theorem)
.

For any separable finite field extension
K\subset E
there exists a primitive element
a\in E
.

Theorem
11.2
widely generalized
Corollary
6.2
which plays a similar role for extensions of finite fields.

Above we defined normal field extensions and their Galois group (see Definition
9.1
).

Theorem 11.3
.

A finite field extension
K\subset F
is normal, if and only if it is separable and the field
F
is a splitting field of some polynomial over
K
. The Galois group
G(K,F)
of the normal extension is well defined, i.e., there is a unique group of automorphisms
G(K,F)
of
F
with fixes an element
a\in F
if and only if
a\in K
.

Let
K\subset F
be a field extension of degree
n
. For an intermediate field
B
we denote by
k
and
m
degrees of the extensions
K\subset B
and
B\subset F
, respectively. Then
k
and
m
are divisors of
n
, and
n=km
.
However, in general, it is not true that for every divisor
k
of
n
there exists a unique intermediate field
B
such that the degree of the extension
K\subset B
is equal to
k
. There may be several such subfields, or none at all.

Similarly, the Galois group of a normal extension of degree
n
has order
n
, and, by Lagrange’s theorem, orders of its subgroups divide
n
. Yet, in general, for a given divisor
k
of
n
, there need not be a unique subgroup of order
k
; there may be several such subgroups, or none.

Nevertheless, the following Galois Correspondence Theorem holds for every normal field extension.

Theorem 11.4

(Galois correspondence)
.

Let
K\subset F
be a normal extension of degree
n
. Then the Galois correspondence provides a bijection between the set of all subgroups of the Galois group
G(F,K)
and the set of all intermediate subfields of the extension.

Moreover, for an intermediate field
B
the extension
K\subset B
is normal if and only if
B
corresponds to a normal divisor
G
of the Galois group
G(K,F)
. In that case, there is a natural action of the factor group
G(F,K)/G

on the intermediate field
B
which fixes all elements of the field
K
. It provides an isomorphism between the factor group
G(F,K)/G
and the
Galois group
G(K,B)
of the extension
K\subset B
.
