New physics in multi-lepton tau decays
Yohei Ema, Patrick J. Fox, Matheus Hostert, Tony Menzo, Maxim Pospelov, Anupam Ray, Jure Zupan

Abstract
Dark particles with lepton-flavor-violating couplings to the tau lepton can induce rare neutrinoless τ \tau decays with large final state multiplicities. We study models where transitions of the type τ ± → ℓ ± ​ ϕ \tau^{\pm}\to\ell^{\pm}\,\phi , with ϕ \phi a light new particle, initiate a chain of decays in the dark sector that terminate with decays into electrons, muons, or pions. These decay cascades appear as rare five or even seven-body τ \tau decays with multiple reconstructable resonances. We survey several representative models: kinetically mixed dark photon, gauged L i − L j L_{i}-L_{j} models, and other more exotic charge assignments such as chiral U ​ ( 1 ) ′ U(1)^{\prime} extensions of the Standard Model. The main new ingredient is the possibility of flavor violation at very high scales. In these models, a number of channels that have not yet been searched for experimentally, such as τ → 5 ​ μ \tau\to 5\mu , τ → 3 ​ μ ​ 2 ​ e \tau\to 3\mu\,2e , τ → μ ​ 4 ​ e \tau\to\mu\,4e , and hadronic channels like τ → μ ​ 4 ​ π \tau\to\mu\,4\pi , typically dominate over the previously-considered signatures such as τ → 3 ​ μ \tau\to 3\mu . While some of the models, such as the gauged L i − L j L_{i}-L_{j} ones, also contain more challenging channels with missing energy due to decays to neutrinos, they can still be searched for via fully visible channels.

Experimentally, there are significant differences between

The much lower sensitivity to exotic tau decays involving invisible particles, compared to fully visible final states, is generic and has to do with the fact that the production of taus in experiments always involves additional neutrinos (e.g., in
e^{+}e^{-}\to\tau^{+}\tau^{-}
the neutrino is from the decay of “the other tau”).
This has important consequences for searches.
For instance, while, the experimental signature for
\tau\to\mu a

The exotic two-body
\tau\to\mu a

\tau\to\mu\nu\bar{\nu}

In this work we explore a much larger set of light new physics models that lead to exotic tau decays of the second type: the multi-lepton signatures.
To some extent the new physics models that lead to multi-lepton signatures in tau decays are closely related to similar new physics models that result in rare multi-electron decays of muons, a number of which have already been discussed in the literature
69
;
64
;
58

However, often there are phenomenologically important differences between the two cases, which go beyond merely swapping taus with muons in the initial state. Most importantly, since tau is much heavier, the dark sector particles are kinematically allowed to decay to many different final states. For instance, a kinetically mixed dark photon with mass well above the muon threshold predominantly decays to hadrons, while decays to muons and electrons are subleading. As we will see on concrete examples, it may thus be worthwhile to perform searches with simultaneous bump hunt searches in several related final states.

II
Multi-lepton signatures

A multi-lepton signature can naturally arise in
\tau
decays via decay chains in the dark sector that terminate with a decay back to SM leptons.
We refer to these as dark cascades.
A token example is a two body
\tau\to\ell\phi
decay, where
\phi
is a light scalar or pseudoscalar that
decays to a pair of light dark vectors,
\phi\to VV
, followed by a subsequent decay of
V

to SM lepton pairs,
V\to\ell^{+}\ell^{-}
.

1

1

We use
\phi
for generic scalars or pseudo-scalars and
V
for generic vector particles.
When talking about specific model realizations, we distinguish dark photons from other realizations of vector particles by using
A^{\prime}
as opposed to
Z^{\prime}
.

The end result is a
\tau\to 5\ell
decay, see

The form of various couplings, and the possible decay channels at each step, depend on the details of the new physics model, for which we give several examples below.
However, the generic low energy phenomenology is already captured quite well by a simple
effective interaction Lagrangian of the form

\mathcal{L}_{\text{eff}}\supset g_{V}V_{\mu}J^{\mu}+\frac{\mu_{V}}{2}\,\phi\,V_{\mu}V^{\mu}+(c_{ij}\,\phi\,\bar{\ell}_{L,i}\ell_{R,j}+\text{h.c.}),

(1)

where we assumed that at tree-level
\phi
interacts exclusively with SM leptons and the dark vector.
Depending on the UV completion,
g_{V}
and
\mu_{V}
may be correlated, though in the effective description in
Eq.

1
they are treated as independent.
The SM charged lepton current is given by,

J^{\mu}=\bar{\psi}_{L}\gamma^{\mu}Q_{L}\psi_{L}+\bar{\psi}_{R}\gamma^{\mu}Q_{R}\psi_{R},

(2)

where
Q_{L,R}
are
3\times 3
hermitian matrices in flavor space with elements
[Q_{L/R}]_{ij}\equiv q_{ij}^{L/R}
, and
\psi_{L/R}=(e_{L/R},\mu_{L/R},\tau_{L/R})
a vector of SM charged leptons.
In general,
Q_{L,R}
have both diagonal and off-diagonal entries. The off-diagonal entries
q^{L/R}_{i3}
induce
\tau\to\ell_{i}V
decays,
i=1,2
, with partial decay widths given by

\begin{split}\Gamma(\tau\to\ell_{i}V)&=\frac{g_{V}^{2}m_{\tau}}{32\pi}\lambda^{1/2}(1,r_{{\ell}_{i}}^{2},r_{V}^{2})\biggr[\left(|q_{i3}^{L}|^{2}+|q_{i3}^{R}|^{2}\right)\\
&\times\Big(1+r_{\ell_{i}}^{2}-2r_{V}^{2}+(1-r_{\ell_{i}}^{2})^{2}/{r_{V}^{2}}\Big)\\
&-12\operatorname{Re}({q^{L}_{i3}}^{*}q^{R}_{i3})r_{\ell_{i}}\biggr],\end{split}

(3)

where
r_{\ell_{i}}=m_{\ell_{i}}/m_{\tau}
,
r_{V}=m_{V}/m_{\tau}
, and
\lambda(a,b,c)=a^{2}+b^{2}+c^{2}-2ab-2ac-2bc
is the Källén function.

Similarly, the Yukawa-like interaction,
c_{ij}
in
Eq.

1
, has in general both diagonal and off-diagonal entries. Note that in the UV complete theories, above electroweak symmetry breaking scale, these interactions are due to higher dimension operators, unless
\phi
is part of an electroweak doublet. For a scalar
\phi
with mass
m_{\phi}<m_{\tau}-m_{\ell_{i}}
,
the
c_{3i,i3}
couplings induce
\tau\to\ell_{i}\phi
decays.
The corresponding partial decay widths are

\displaystyle\Gamma(\tau\to\ell_{i}\phi)=\frac{m_{\tau}}{32\pi}\lambda^{1/2}(1,r_{{\ell}_{i}}^{2},r_{\phi}^{2})

(4)

\displaystyle\times\left[(|c_{i3}|^{2}+|c_{3i}|^{2})(1+r_{{\ell}_{i}}^{2}-r_{\phi}^{2})+4\operatorname{Re}(c_{i3}c_{3i})r_{{\ell}_{i}}\right],

where

r_{\phi}=m_{\phi}/m_{\tau}
.

The experimental sensitivity for
\mathcal{B}(\tau\to\ell_{i}\phi)
depends on how
\phi
decays. More often than not, the dominant decay of
\phi
is to a pair of dark vectors, with partial width given by

\Gamma(\phi\to VV)=\frac{m_{\phi}^{3}\mu_{V}^{2}}{128\pi m_{V}^{4}}(1-4r_{V/\phi}^{2}+12r_{V/\phi}^{4})\sqrt{1-4r_{V/\phi}^{2}}~,

(5)

where
r_{V/\phi}=m_{V}/m_{\phi}
.
The
\phi
may also decay to a pair of SM fermions.
For off-diagonal couplings the partial width is given by

\displaystyle\Gamma(\phi\to f_{i}f_{j})=\frac{m_{\phi}}{8\pi}\lambda^{1/2}(1,r_{i}^{2},r_{j}^{2})

(6)

\displaystyle\times\left[(|c_{ij}|^{2}+|c_{ji}|^{2})(1-r_{i}^{2}-r_{j}^{2})-4\mathrm{Re}(c_{ij}c_{ji})r_{i}r_{j}\right],

where
r_{i}=m_{i}/m_{\phi}
and we have summed over the two possible charge assignments
\Gamma(\phi\to f_{i}f_{j})=\Gamma(\phi\to f_{i}^{-}f_{j}^{+})+\Gamma(\phi\to f_{i}^{+}f_{j}^{-})
.
For flavor diagonal decays, assuming real
c_{ii}
, we have

\Gamma(\phi\to f^{+}_{i}f^{-}_{i})=\frac{c_{ii}^{2}m_{\phi}}{8\pi}\left(1-4r_{i}^{2}\right)^{3/2}.

(7)

The final step in the cascade decay is the decay of the dark vector to SM states.
We will mainly be interested in decays to charged fermions,
f

\displaystyle\Gamma_{V\to f_{i}\bar{f}_{j}}

\displaystyle\qquad+12z_{i}z_{j}\operatorname{Re}({q^{L}_{ij}}^{*}q^{R}_{ij})\Big]~,

with

V\to\nu\bar{\nu}
.
Additionally, in certain models,

In the numerical examples, we will use as a benchmark mass spectrum

m_{\phi}=1\,\mathrm{GeV},\quad m_{V}=300\,\text{MeV}.

(9)

The masses of
\phi
and
V
were chosen such that in tau decays both
\phi
and
V
can be produced on-shell, and then decay promptly for the coupling ranges we are interested in.
In the prompt, on-shell regime, we therefore have

\displaystyle\Gamma(\tau\to\ell_{i}\ell^{+}_{j}\ell^{-}_{j}\ell^{+}_{k}\ell^{-}_{k})\simeq

(10)

\displaystyle(2-\delta_{jk})\Gamma(\tau\to\ell_{i}\phi)\times\mathcal{B}(V\to 2\ell_{j})\times\mathcal{B}(V\to 2\ell_{k}),

where we assumed
\mathcal{B}(\phi\to 2V)\approx 1
.
In this regime, a measurement of the partial decay width
\Gamma(\tau\to\ell_{i}\ell^{+}_{j}\ell^{-}_{j}\ell^{+}_{k}\ell^{-}_{k})
gives a direct determination of
c_{3i}^{2}
, see
Eq.

4
.

Above electroweak symmetry breaking scale, the effective coupling of
\phi
to SM charged leptons,
Eq.

1
, must arise from a higher dimension operator. For the models we consider below the coupling can arise at dimension
5
or
6
. The translation of bounds on
c_{ij}
to bounds on the scale of this higher dimension operator differs between the two cases.

If the coupling of
\phi
to SM charged leptons in
Eq.

1
is due to a dimension-5 effective operator, the flavor violating interaction takes the form

\mathcal{L}^{(5)}\supset\frac{c^{(5)}_{ij}}{\Lambda_{5}}\Phi(\bar{L}_{L,i}\ell_{R,j})H+\text{h.c.},

(11)

where
\Phi
denotes the full scalar field prior to symmetry breaking.
After electroweak and
U(1)^{\prime}
symmetry breaking, writing
\Phi=(v^{\prime}+\phi)/\sqrt{2}
and
H^{0}=(v+h)/\sqrt{2}
, the effective coupling is parametrically given by

c\sim c^{(5)}\frac{v}{2\Lambda_{5}}~,

(12)

where
v
is the vacuum expectation value (VEV) of the SM Higgs and we have suppressed the flavor indices which will depend upon the rotation matrices necessary to diagonalize the charged lepton masses.
On the other hand, if the effective coupling is due to a dimension
6
effective operator of the form

\mathcal{L}^{(6)}\supset\frac{c^{(6)}_{ij}}{\Lambda_{6}^{2}}|\Phi|^{2}(\bar{L}_{L,i}\ell_{R,j})H+\text{h.c.},

(13)

then the effective coupling is

c\sim c^{(6)}\frac{v^{\prime}v}{\sqrt{2}\Lambda_{6}^{2}},

(14)

where
v^{\prime}
is the VEV of a light scalar field.

These scalings mean that a bound on the tau branching fraction scales as

\begin{split}\mathcal{B}(\tau\to\ell_{i}\phi)&\simeq 10^{-8}\cdot\biggr(\frac{c_{i3}}{10^{-9}}\biggr)^{2}\\
&\simeq 10^{-8}\cdot\left(c_{i3}^{(5)}\right)^{2}\cdot\biggr(\frac{10^{11}\,\text{GeV}}{\Lambda_{5}}\biggr)^{2}\\
&\simeq 10^{-8}\cdot\left(c_{i3}^{(6)}\right)^{2}\cdot\biggr(\frac{10^{6}\,\text{GeV}}{\Lambda_{6}}\biggr)^{4}\cdot\biggr(\frac{v^{\prime}}{10\,\text{GeV}}\biggr)^{2}~.\end{split}

(15)

For simplicity we have considered the situation with only one non-zero
c_{ij}
.
From
Eq.

15
we see that whether the interaction of
\phi
to the SM fields is due to a dimension 5 operator or due a dimension 6 one, the new physics scale that can in principle be probed in rare tau decays lies well above the electroweak symmetry breaking scale and also well above the direct reach in the colliders.

One important distinction between dimension-5 and dimension-6 operators is the correlation between
\tau\to\ell_{i}V
and
\tau\to\ell_{i}\phi
.
If the mass of
V
comes from the expectation value of the complex scalar that contains
\phi
, the dimension-5 operators will typically predict

\frac{\mathcal{B}(\tau\to\ell_{i}V)}{\mathcal{B}(\tau\to\ell_{i}\phi)}\simeq 1

(16)

when both
\phi
and
V
are light with respect to
m_{\tau}
as a direct consequence of the Goldstone boson equivalence theorem.
This can be understood as the fact that the phase of the complex scalar is allowed to couple to the flavor-violating current.
For dimension-6 operators, this is not the case and we typically expect the ratio in
Eq.

16
to be small or to vanish altogether.
In practice, when
V\to\ell^{+}\ell^{-}
and
\phi\to VV
dominate, the ratio in
Eq.

16
controls the hierarchy between three-lepton and five-lepton tau decays.

III.1
Model I: kinetic mixing

We start with a particularly minimal extension of the SM: the dark photon
67
;
34
, now denoted by
A^{\prime}
(that is,
V=A^{\prime}
in what follows).
We assume the mass of
A^{\prime}
is generated by a light dark Higgs
\Phi
.
It is this scalar particle that, through non-renormalizable couplings, can initiate LFV decays of SM leptons.
The radial mode of the dark Higgs,
\phi
, initiates the chain of decays via
\tau\to\ell_{i}(\phi\to A^{\prime}A^{\prime})
in much the same way that it did in the
\mu\to e(\phi\to A^{\prime}A^{\prime})
decay studied in Ref.
69
.
Because
A^{\prime}
only couples to the SM via kinetic mixing, the decay chain can only terminate on multi-lepton and multi-pion final states, without any missing energy.
Examples of such dark cascade signatures include

\tau\to 5\mu
,
\tau\to 2\pi 3\mu
,
\tau\to 4\pi\mu
,
\tau\to 2e3\mu
(and equivalent decays with
\mu\leftrightarrow e
), see
Table

1
.

We now turn to a quantitative formulation of the above discussion.
The
U(1)^{\prime}
part of the Lagrangian is, for energies well below the electroweak scale, given by

\mathcal{L}_{U(1)^{\prime}}=-\frac{1}{4}F_{\mu\nu}^{\prime 2}-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}F^{\prime}_{\mu\nu}\hat{F}^{\mu\nu}+|D_{\mu}\Phi|^{2}-V(\Phi),

(17)

where
D_{\mu}\equiv\partial_{\mu}-ig_{d}{A}^{\prime}_{\mu}
,
\varepsilon
is the kinetic mixing parameter. The dark scalar potential is
2
2

2

Above the electroweak scale, the potential in general also includes a quartic coupling to the SM Higgs boson,
V(\Phi)\supset\lambda_{\Phi H}|\Phi|^{2}|H|^{2}
. Below electroweak symmetry breaking this contributes to
\Phi
mass term.

V(\Phi)=-\mu^{2}|\Phi|^{2}+\lambda|\Phi|^{4},

(18)

The
U(1)^{\prime}
is spontaneously broken once the field
\Phi
obtains a nonzero VEV,

\langle\Phi\rangle\equiv v^{\prime}/\sqrt{2}\ll\langle H\rangle\equiv v/\sqrt{2}
. The kinetic mixing term in Eq. (
17
) can be removed via the field redefinition
\hat{A}_{\mu}\to A_{\mu}-\varepsilon A_{\mu}^{\prime}
, giving canonically normalized
A,A^{\prime}
, and inducing millicharged couplings of SM leptons and hadrons to
A^{\prime}
. The mass of
A^{\prime}
is proportional to
U(1)^{\prime}
symmetry breaking, and is given by
m_{A^{\prime}}=g_{d}v^{\prime}

assuming that
\Phi
has the
U(1)^{\prime}
charge
+1
.

The LFV is induced in this model via non-renormalizable interactions between
\Phi
and SM fields.
Generically, the first
SU(2)_{L}
-invariant LFV operators appear at dimension-5, though these would require some of the SM fields to be charged under
U(1)^{\prime}
, which we assume not to be the case for the dark photon.
Therefore, the first
U(1)^{\prime}
-invariant LFV operators appear at dimension-6,

-\mathcal{L}_{\text{LFV},{\rm I}}=\frac{\kappa_{ij}}{\Lambda^{2}}|\Phi|^{2}\bar{L}_{i}H\ell_{Rj}+\text{h.c.},

(19)

where
L=(L_{1},L_{2},L_{3})^{T}
and
\ell_{R}=(e_{R},\mu_{R},\tau_{R})^{T}
are vectors in flavor space with the left and right-handed lepton doublets, respectively, where
L_{1}=(\nu_{e},e_{L})
,
L_{2}=(\nu_{\mu},\mu_{L})
,
L_{3}=(\nu_{\tau},\tau_{L})
, and
\kappa_{ij}
is a matrix of Wilson coefficients in flavor space.

After both
\Phi
and
H
obtain VEVs, the leptonic mass matrix receives two contributions: one from the dimension-6 operator in
Eq.

19
and another from the SM Yukawa couplings,

\mathcal{L}_{\rm SM}\supset-y_{ij}\bar{L}_{i}H\ell_{Rj}+{\rm h.c.},

(20)

where
y_{ij}
is the Yukawa matrix in flavor space.
The charged lepton masses are thus given by,

\text{diag}(m_{e},m_{\mu},m_{\tau})=U_{L}^{\dagger}\left(y+\kappa\frac{v^{\prime 2}}{2\Lambda^{2}}\right)U_{R}\frac{v}{\sqrt{2}},

(21)

where
U_{L,R}
are the unitary matrices that diagonalize the mass matrix.
After transforming to the mass basis, the low energy coefficients in
Eq.

1
are given by

c_{ij}=-(U_{L}^{\dagger}\kappa\,U_{R})_{ij}\frac{v^{\prime}v}{\sqrt{2}\Lambda^{2}},\,\,\mu_{V}=2g_{d}m_{A^{\prime}},\text{ and }g_{V}=e\varepsilon.

(22)

Note that because the dark photon inherits the same flavor-conserving structure as the SM photon,
Q_{L}=Q_{R}=\mathbf{1}
, all interactions between the dark photon and the SM leptons are flavor conserving.

III.2
Model II: gauged
L_{\alpha}-L_{\beta}

Similar multilepton signatures arise in models with a local
U(1)^{\prime}
that gauges differences of family lepton numbers:
L_{e}-L_{\mu},L_{e}-L_{\tau}
, or
L_{\mu}-L_{\tau}
. The
U(1)^{\prime}
is spontaneously broken once the scalar
\Phi
, charged under
U(1)^{\prime}
, obtains a VEV, which also gives mass to the
U(1)^{\prime}
gauge boson
V\equiv Z^{\prime}
.
Each of the three
L_{\alpha}-L_{\beta}
choices is anomaly-free, and can lead to similar signatures to those discussed in
Section

III.1
for Model I, as we show below.

For all three
L_{\alpha}-L_{\beta}
choices, the
U(1)^{\prime}
Lagrangian at low energy, below electroweak scale, is given by

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}_{U(1)_{\alpha-\beta}^{\prime}}=&-\frac{1}{4}Z_{\mu\nu}^{\prime 2}-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}Z_{\mu\nu}^{\prime}\hat{F}^{\mu\nu}+g^{\prime}Z_{\mu}^{\prime}J^{\mu}_{\alpha-\beta}\\
&+|D_{\mu}\Phi|^{2}-V(\Phi),\end{split}

(29)

where
D_{\mu}\equiv\partial_{\mu}-ig^{\prime}Q_{\Phi}Z^{\prime}_{\mu}
, with
Q_{\Phi}
the
L_{\alpha}-L_{\beta}
charge of
\Phi
,
\varepsilon
is the kinetic mixing parameter. The charged lepton current
J^{\mu}_{\alpha-\beta}
is given by

\begin{split}J^{\mu}_{\alpha-\beta}&=\bar{L}\gamma^{\mu}Q_{\alpha-\beta}L+\bar{\ell}_{R}\gamma^{\mu}Q_{\alpha-\beta}\ell_{R}\\
&=\bar{\ell}_{\alpha}\gamma^{\mu}\ell_{\alpha}-\bar{\ell}_{\beta}\gamma^{\mu}\ell_{\beta}+\bar{\nu}_{\alpha}\gamma^{\mu}P_{L}\nu_{\alpha}-\bar{\nu}_{\beta}\gamma^{\mu}P_{L}\nu_{\beta},\end{split}

(30)

where
L=(L_{1},L_{2},L_{3})^{T},\ell_{R}=(e_{R},\mu_{R},\tau_{R})^{T}
, and
Q_{\alpha-\beta}
is the
U(1)^{\prime}
leptonic charge matrix. For example, in
L_{\mu}-L_{\tau}
case,
Q_{\mu-\tau}=\text{diag}(0,+1,-1)
. The scalar potential
V(\Phi)
in
Eq.

29
is taken to be the same as in Model I, see
Eq.

18
.
The VEV
\langle\Phi\rangle\equiv v^{\prime}/\sqrt{2}
spontaneously breaks
U(1)^{\prime}
, giving
Z^{\prime}
a mass
m_{Z^{\prime}}=g^{\prime}v^{\prime}Q_{\Phi}
.

The kinetic mixing term in Eq. (
29
) can be removed via field redefinition
\hat{A}_{\mu}\to A_{\mu}-\varepsilon Z_{\mu}^{\prime}
, giving canonically normalized
A
and
Z^{\prime}
. This also induces millicharged
{\mathcal{O}}(\varepsilon)
couplings of SM quarks to
Z^{\prime}
, and changes the couplings of
Z^{\prime}
to the SM charged leptons by
{\mathcal{O}}(\varepsilon)
away from the
J_{\alpha-\beta}^{\mu}
current in
Eq.

30
.
We will assume that
\varepsilon e\ll g^{\prime}
so that the
Z^{\prime}
couplings to leptons due to
L_{\alpha}-L_{\beta}
current in
Eq.

30
always dominate, and the
{\mathcal{O}}(\varepsilon)
corrections can be neglected. Note also, that if instead of
Eq.

29
we consider the
U(1)^{\prime}_{\alpha-\beta}
Lagrangian above the electroweak scale, the kinetic mixing is between hypercharge and
Z^{\prime}
.
After
SU(2)_{L}\times U(1)_{Y}\times U(1)^{\prime}\to U(1)_{\text{EM}}
breaking, there will now also be a mixing between
{Z}^{\prime}
and the SM
Z
. This gives a correction to
Z^{\prime}
mass, shifting

m_{Z^{\prime}}=g^{\prime}v^{\prime}Q_{\Phi}
to
m_{{Z}^{\prime}}(1+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{2}))
, and also induces tree-level couplings of
Z^{\prime}
to the SM Higgs
H
.

An important difference with the
U(1)^{\prime}
model (Model I in
Section

III.1
), is that the LFV interactions can arise already at dimension-5

-\mathcal{L}_{\text{LFV},\text{II}}=\frac{\tilde{k}_{ij}}{\Lambda}\Phi\bar{L}_{i}H{\ell}_{j}+\frac{\tilde{k}^{\prime}_{ji}}{\Lambda}\Phi^{*}\bar{L}_{j}H{\ell}_{i}+\text{h.c.},

(31)

if the
U(1)^{\prime}_{\alpha-\beta}
charge of
\Phi
is
Q_{\Phi}=[L_{i}]_{\alpha-\beta}-[L_{j}]_{\alpha-\beta}
, where
[L_{i}]_{\alpha-\beta}=[\ell_{i}]_{\alpha-\beta}
is the charge of
i
–th generation SM lepton under the
U(1)^{\prime}_{\alpha-\beta}
.
Focusing on
U(1)_{\mu-\tau}
, as a benchmark example, LFV dimension-5 operators are allowed by gauge invariance for

Q_{\Phi}=\begin{cases}+2:&\tilde{k}_{\mu\tau},\tilde{k}_{\tau\mu}^{\prime}\neq 0,\\
+1:&\tilde{k}_{e\tau},\tilde{k}_{\mu e},\tilde{k}_{\tau e}^{\prime},\tilde{k}_{e\mu}^{\prime}\neq 0,\end{cases}

(32)

where we indicated which matrix elements in
\tilde{k}
,
\tilde{k}^{\prime}
can be nonzero in each of the two cases. That is, in the
Q_{\Phi}=+2
case only
\tau\to\mu

Q_{\Phi}=+1
both
\tau\to e
and
\mu\to e

\tau\to\mu

For the two numerical benchmarks we take

\displaystyle Q_{\Phi}=+2\text{~benchm.}

\displaystyle:\tilde{k}_{\mu\tau}=\tilde{k}_{\tau\mu}^{\prime}\equiv k_{\mu\tau},

(33)

\displaystyle Q_{\Phi}=+1\text{~benchm.}

\displaystyle:\tilde{k}_{e\tau}=\tilde{k}_{\tau e}^{\prime}\equiv k_{e\tau},\,\,\tilde{k}_{\mu e}=\tilde{k}_{e\mu}^{\prime}=0,

with
k_{\mu\tau}
and
k_{e\tau}

\Phi=(v^{\prime}+\phi)/\sqrt{2}

\phi

symmetry breaking. For the two benchmarks this interaction term is given by

-\mathcal{L}_{\text{LFV},\text{II}}\supset\frac{v}{2\Lambda}k_{ij}\phi\bar{\ell}_{iL}{\ell}_{jR}+\text{h.c.},

(35)

where

The dimension-5 interactions in
Eq.

31

20

\text{diag}(m_{e},m_{\mu},m_{\tau})=V_{L}^{\dagger}\left(y+k\frac{v^{\prime}}{\sqrt{2}\Lambda}\right)V_{R}\frac{v}{\sqrt{2}},

21

The

The reason is that in general the leptonic charge matrices

30

After transforming to the mass basis, the flavor structure of the

V_{L}^{\dagger}Q_{\mu-\tau}V_{L},\quad V^{\dagger}_{R}Q_{\mu-\tau}V_{R}.

(37)

In the limit

with
\Theta^{\dagger}_{L,R}=-\Theta_{L,R}

V_{L,R}^{\dagger}Q_{\mu-\tau}V_{L,R}\simeq Q_{\mu-\tau}+[Q_{\mu-\tau},\Theta_{L,R}].

For the two benchmarks this then gives for the nonzero entries in

(\Theta_{L,R})_{\ell 3}=-(\Theta_{L,R})_{3\ell}\simeq\frac{k_{\ell\tau}}{m_{\tau}}\frac{v^{\prime}v}{2\Lambda},\quad\ell=e,\mu,

where we neglected contributions suppressed by powers of

. The lepton current coupling to the
Z^{\prime}

Eq.

29

Eq.

33
given by

\displaystyle J^{\mu}_{\mu-\tau}

\displaystyle=\bar{\mu}\gamma^{\mu}\mu-\bar{\tau}\gamma^{\mu}\tau+\bar{\nu}_{\mu}\gamma^{\mu}P_{L}\nu_{\mu}-\bar{\nu}_{\tau}\gamma^{\mu}P_{L}\nu_{\tau}

\displaystyle+\frac{v^{\prime}v}{2m_{\tau}\Lambda}\begin{cases}2k_{\mu\tau}\ \bar{\mu}\gamma^{\mu}\tau~,&Q_{\Phi}=2\\

\frac{\Gamma(\tau\to\ell+4\mu)}{\Gamma(\tau\to\ell+2\mu+2\nu)}\simeq 0.5,\quad\ell=e,\mu,

(44)

in both benchmarks. For other

U(1)^{\prime}
that gauges
L_{e}-L_{\tau}
one would instead have
\tau\to\ell+2e
and
\tau\to\ell+4e
multibody decays, with
\ell=e,\mu
, for the choices of charge assignments for
\Phi
.

Throughout this discussion we worked in the limit where kinetic mixing is negligible, i.e.,
\varepsilon\to 0
in
Eq.

29
. Consequently there were no couplings of
Z^{\prime}
to electrons and quarks. This is unlikely to be the case, however. That is, even if one forbids tree level couplings of
Z^{\prime}
to electrons and quarks, they will be induced at one-loop due to
loop-induced kinetic mixing between hypercharge and
Z^{\prime}
, and thus couplings of
Z^{\prime}
to the electromagnetic current. The effective mixing parameter depends on momentum

flowing through the electromagnetic current, and is for
U(1)^{\prime}
that gauges
L_{\mu}-L_{\tau}
given by
19

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\mu\tau}(q^{2})

\displaystyle=-\frac{eg^{\prime}}{2\pi^{2}}\int_{0}^{1}dx\,x(x-1)\log\left(\frac{m_{\mu}^{2}+q^{2}x(x-1)}{m_{\tau}^{2}+q^{2}x(x-1)}\right).

(45)

For
Z^{\prime}
decays,
q^{2}=m_{Z^{\prime}}^{2}
, so that the loop induced mixing is a constant. Assuming that the tree level
\varepsilon
kinetic mixing parameter vanishes, the bounds on

gauge coupling is
g^{\prime}\lesssim\text{few}\times 10^{-4}
–
10^{-3}
in the mass range of interest,
m_{A^{\prime}}=0.1
–
1\,
GeV
71
;
59
. It is, however, also possible that the sum of one loop and tree level contributions to the mixing,
\varepsilon_{\mu\tau}+\varepsilon
, cancel against each, in which case these bounds would get relaxed.

The LFV
Z^{\prime}
interactions in
Eq.

40
induce
\tau\to\ell\gamma
transition at one loop, due to a vertex correction with
Z^{\prime}
exchange.
The branching ratio
\mathcal{B}(\tau\to\ell\gamma)
is suppressed by
\alpha^{\prime}\alpha_{\mathrm{EM}}
relative to
\mathcal{B}(\tau\to 3\ell)
and
\mathcal{B}(\tau\to 5\ell)
from dark cascades.
Since the current bounds on
\tau\to 3\ell
and
\tau\to\ell\gamma
are both of order
\mathcal{O}(10^{-8})
,
the bounds on
\tau\to 3\ell
at present dominate the constraints on this model.

III.5
Model V: flavor protected scalar

As the final example let us discuss the possibility that the SM field content is extended by a light flavor protected scalar,
\mathcal{S}
. In this model, the cascade
\tau\to\mu 3\mathcal{S}
,
\mathcal{S}\to 2\ell
results in the
\tau\to\mu+6\ell
decay as the main exotic signature. The model is a modification of the flavor protected scalar model, which was introduced in Ref.
58
as an example of a natural model that can lead to a
\mu\to 7e
exotic decay signature.
At low energies the interactions between
\mathcal{S}
, a SM singlet, and the SM fields include the following higher dimension operators,

\mathcal{L}\supset\frac{\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{S}}^{(3)}}{3!\Lambda^{3}}\,\bar{L}_{\mu}H\tau_{R}\,\mathcal{S}^{3}+\frac{\mathcal{C}_{\mu\mathcal{S}}}{\Lambda}\bar{L}_{\mu}H\mu_{R}\mathcal{S}+\frac{\mathcal{C}_{e\mathcal{S}}}{\Lambda}\bar{L}_{e}He_{R}\mathcal{S}+\text{h.c.}\,.

(68)

For
\mathcal{S}
light enough, with a mass
m_{\mathcal{S}}<(m_{\tau}-m_{\mu})/3
, the
\mathcal{S}^{3}
term leads to
\tau\to\mu 3\mathcal{S}
decays. The second interaction facilitates
\mathcal{S}\to 2\mu
decays, so that the
\tau\to\mu 3\mathcal{S}
,
\mathcal{S}\to 2\mu
cascade gives rise to
\tau\to 7\mu
signature, assuming for simplicity
\mathcal{C}_{\mu\mathcal{S}}\gg\mathcal{C}_{e\mathcal{S}}
. If instead,
\mathcal{C}_{\mu\mathcal{S}}\ll\mathcal{C}_{e\mathcal{S}}
, the main decay cascade is
\tau\to\mu 3\mathcal{S}
,
\mathcal{S}\to 2e
, leading to the
\tau\to\mu 6e
signature. Finally, if
\mathcal{C}_{\mu\mathcal{S}}
and
\mathcal{C}_{e\mathcal{S}}
are comparable, then
\tau\to 7\mu
,
\tau\to 5\mu 2e
,
\tau\to 3\mu 4e
, and
\tau\to\mu 6e
decay channels are all important.

The fact that these high charged lepton multiplicity decays are the main exotic signatures is a result of a global
U(1)_{\ell}^{3}\equiv U(1)_{e}\times U(1)_{\mu}\times U(1)_{\tau}
symmetry, broken by two spurions,
\varepsilon_{\mu}
and
\varepsilon_{\tau}
. The decays
\tau\to\mu\mathcal{S}
and
\tau\to\mu 2\mathcal{S}
are in this case parametrically suppressed (we assume that
\cal S
does not obtain a vacuum expectation value). Under
U(1)_{\ell}^{3}
the SM charged lepton fields carry the usual
L_{\alpha}
charges
while the light scalar has
[\mathcal{S}]_{L_{\tau}}=\frac{1}{3}
. The SM flavor diagonal charged lepton Yukawa interactions are allowed by the
U(1)_{\ell}^{3}
global symmetry, with the off-diagonal terms forbidden, thus defining the mass basis for the SM charged leptons.

The
U(1)_{\ell}^{3}
global symmetry is not exact, but rather explicitly broken by two small spurions, with charges
[\varepsilon_{\tau}]_{L_{\tau}}=-\frac{1}{3}
and
[\varepsilon_{\mu}]_{L_{\mu}}=-1
. The Wilson coefficients in
Eq.

68
are therefore of order

\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{S}}^{(3)}\sim{\mathcal{O}}(\varepsilon_{\mu})\,,\qquad\mathcal{C}_{\mu\mathcal{S}}\sim\mathcal{C}_{e\mathcal{S}}\sim{\mathcal{O}}(\varepsilon_{\tau})\,.

(69)

That is, the
S\to 2\mu
and
S\to 2e
decay rates are suppressed by
\varepsilon_{\tau}^{2}
, while the
\tau\to\mu 3\mathcal{S}
decay rate is suppressed by
\varepsilon_{\mu}^{2}
.
In contrast, the operators that would give rise to
\tau\to\mu\mathcal{S}
and
\tau\to\mu 2\mathcal{S}
decays are further suppressed, and are of order,

\bar{L}_{\mu}H\tau_{R}\mathcal{S}^{\dagger}\sim{\mathcal{O}}(\varepsilon_{\mu}\varepsilon_{\tau}^{2})\,,\qquad\bar{L}_{\mu}H\tau_{R}\mathcal{S}^{\dagger 2}\sim{\mathcal{O}}(\varepsilon_{\mu}\varepsilon_{\tau})\,,

(70)

where the scaling refers to the parametric size of Wilson coefficients multiplying the operators.
If
\varepsilon_{\tau}\ll m_{\tau}/(4\pi\Lambda)
, there is a hierarchy among the exotic decays,
\Gamma(\tau\to\mu\mathcal{S})\ll\Gamma(\tau\to\mu 2\mathcal{S})\ll\Gamma(\tau\to\mu 3\mathcal{S})
, so that the
\tau\to\mu+6\ell
signature is the dominant one. Note that
\varepsilon_{\mu}
enters in the same way into all three decay widths, and thus
\tau\to\mu 3\mathcal{S}
can dominate even for
\varepsilon_{\mu}
that is large,
\varepsilon_{\mu}\sim{\mathcal{O}}(1)
. Furthermore, we have assumed that the
U(1)_{e}
global symmetry is unbroken. If instead it is broken by a spurion
\varepsilon_{e}\gg\varepsilon_{\mu}
, with
[\varepsilon_{e}]_{L_{e}}=-1
, the above discussion changes trivially, by simply swapping
\mu\leftrightarrow e
everywhere. In particular, the leading exotic signatures are now
\tau\to e+6\ell
. Note furthermore that
\mu\to e\mathcal{S}
would be allowed by the
U(1)_{\ell}^{3}
is one has both a spurion that is charged under
U(1)_{e}
,
[\varepsilon_{e}]_{L_{e}}=-1
, as well as a spurion charged under
U(1)_{\mu}
,
[\varepsilon_{\mu}]_{L_{\mu}}=-1
. However, the
\mu\to en\mathcal{S}
decays are then suppressed both by
\epsilon_{\mu}
as well as
\epsilon_{e}
and powers of
\epsilon_{\tau}
, and can thus be very small (if
\mathcal{S}
is heavier than
m_{\mu}-m_{e}
then they are also kinematically forbidden).

The exotic decay width is

\Gamma(\tau\to\mu 3\mathcal{S})=\frac{m_{\tau}^{5}}{9216\pi}\biggr(\frac{v_{\text{EW}}}{\Lambda^{3}}\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{S}}^{(3)}\biggr)^{2}\biggr(\frac{1}{16\pi^{2}}\biggr)^{2}~,

(71)

where we have worked in the massless limit for the final state particles. If the decay of the
\tau
takes place instead near threshold the width scales as
\left(1-\xi\right)^{7/2}
where

\xi=3m_{\mathcal{S}}/(m_{\tau}-m_{\mu})
accounts for the phase-space suppression of the decay rate.
Ignoring this additional phase space factor, the branching ratio is

\mathcal{B}(\tau\to\mu 3\mathcal{S})\sim 7\cdot 10^{-10}\,\big(\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{S}}^{(3)}\big)^{2}\biggr(\frac{1\,\text{TeV}}{\Lambda}\biggr)^{6}.

(72)

Assuming that
\mathcal{S}\to 2e
dominates, i.e., taking
\mathcal{C}_{e\mathcal{S}}\gg\mathcal{C}_{\mu\mathcal{S}}
for simplicity, so that we do not need to take into account the phase space dependence on final state masses, we have

c\tau_{\mathcal{S}}=109\,\mathrm{\mu m}\bigg(\frac{10^{-4}}{\mathcal{C}_{e\mathcal{S}}}\bigg)^{2}\bigg(\frac{\Lambda}{1\,\text{TeV}}\bigg)^{2}\bigg(\frac{300\,\text{MeV}}{m_{\mathcal{S}}}\bigg)\,.

(73)

In the region of parameter space we are interested, the
\mathcal{S}
decays are thus essentially prompt, though if
\mathcal{C}_{e\mathcal{S}}
is significantly smaller than
10^{-4}
the decays of
\mathcal{S}
will be displaced.

The above numerical benchmark estimates show that there is a large available parameter space, where
\tau\to\mu 3\mathcal{S}
would be the dominant exotic decay, for instance,
\mathcal{C}_{e\mathcal{S}}\sim\epsilon_{\tau}\sim 10^{-4}\ll m_{\tau}/(4\pi\Lambda)
for
\Lambda\sim{\mathcal{O}}(\text{TeV})
. These rare tau decays would probe new physics mass scales in the TeV regime, and would thus be complementary to direct searches at the LHC (for a UV completion of the above model, involving a scalar and a vector-like lepton, see Ref.
58
, however, now with a modified flavor pattern).

Other collider constraints.

There are a number of constraints from beam-dumps and from collider searches for light scalar and bosons, constraining their flavor conserving couplings. Some of these we already mentioned in the introduction to this section, such as the bounds on flavor diagonal couplings of light vectors given in

23

The main sensitivity to flavor violating
\phi

\tau
–
\ell

The typical sensitivity to the NP scale suppressing the flavor violating couplings of the benchmark models is well above the direct collider reach, i.e., it is in the

\Phi

is small enough,
\lambda\lesssim 10^{-4}
, such that there is no significant tuning in the
\phi
mass, however, implies that the mixing is very small,
\theta\lesssim 10^{-6}
, so that all these effects are unobservably small for current and planned colliders.

V
Experimental prospects

The experimental reach to new physics via searches for multi-lepton tau decays, such as
\tau\to 5\mu
, depends on how large these branching ratios are compared to other exotic tau decay channels, such as, e.g,
\tau\to 3\mu 2\nu
or
\tau\to 3\mu 2\pi
. These relative branching ratios are model dependent and one can increase the sensitivity to particular model by covering more possible exotic decay channels listed in
Table

1
.

For each of the channels, the experimental reach depends on the corresponding experimental efficiencies for that particular decay channels. Below we provide rough estimates of experimental sensitivities, by first estimating the
expected number of taus
at relevant
pp
and
e^{+}e^{-}
colliders, and give approximate expected acceptance rates. These should be viewed only as order of magnitude estimates, motivating a more detailed study of experimental sensitivities for the most promising channels.

V.1
LHC searches

Multi-lepton tau decays can be efficiently searched for both at the general purpose detectors — ATLAS and CMS — as well as at LHCb.

The rare tau decay searches at LHCb benefit from its forward geometry (
2<\eta<5
), low-
p_{T}
muon trigger threshold (
\sim 0.5
GeV), and excellent vertex resolution.
Taus are produced copiously via heavy-flavor decays, predominantly
D_{s}\to\tau\nu
, with an effective cross section
\sigma_{\tau}^{\rm eff}\sim 30~\mu
b within the LHCb acceptance.
The LHCb Run 1+2 dataset (
\sim 9
fb
-1
) consists of approximately
10^{11}
tau decays within LHCb’s fiducial volume.
A preliminary analysis of
\tau\to 5\mu
experimental reach at LHCb obtained a sensitivity of
\mathcal{B}\lesssim 4\times 10^{-8}

85
.
At the HL-LHC with 300 fb
-1
, the tau yield will increase to
\sim 2\times 10^{12}
, which would mean that potentially branching ratios at the
10^{-10}
level could be probed, assuming backgrounds can be controlled.

There are several important sources of backgrounds for such searches. An important consideration is the size of combinatorial background, which will increase at high luminosity where multiple
pp
interactions per bunch crossing produce large backgrounds from random track combinations. This can be mitigated by requiring all five tracks to originate from a common displaced vertex consistent with the tau lifetime (
c\tau_{\tau}=87~\mu
m). In the prompt regime we consider, the
\phi
and
V
decay lengths are negligible compared to this displacement, so all five tracks indeed emerge from a single point.

The
\tau\to 5\mu
decay channel benefits from the clean muon signature, while channels involving pions (e.g.,
\tau\to 3\mu 2\pi
) face significant backgrounds from prompt hadron production. Here, the resonant structure in the
m_{\pi\pi}
mass distribution from
V\to\pi^{+}\pi^{-}
decays, could provide discrimination, but dedicated studies are needed for sensitivity estimates. The most challenging are decays with missing energy; channels with neutrinos cannot be fully reconstructed at a hadron collider, making searches for
\tau\to 3\mu 2\nu
and related signatures impractical at LHCb.

For ATLAS and CMS a significant limitation for such searches is the high-
p_{T}
trigger threshold. That is, while both experiments have a larger instantaneous luminosity than LHCb, they also require muons with
p_{T}\gtrsim 20
–
26
GeV at the hardware trigger level
3
;
90
. For taus produced in heavy-flavor decays, the typical tau
p_{T}
momentum is
\mathcal{O}(1)
GeV, with only
{\mathcal{O}}(10^{-5})
fraction of taus having
p_{T}
above 25 GeV
47
. The muons from
\tau\to 5\mu
will thus be well below the nominal
p_{T}
threshold. Alternatively, one could use taus with sufficiently high
p_{T}
that are produced via
pp\to Z/\gamma^{*}\to\tau^{+}\tau^{-}
, but the cross section for this process (
\sim
nb) is three orders of magnitude smaller than the heavy-flavor production. Furthermore, the boosted tau topology makes vertex reconstruction challenging.

For dedicated searches, the trigger thresholds can be lowered significantly. For instance, for recent
\tau\to 3\mu
search the CMS required two oppositely charged muons above
p_{T}>5(3)\,
GeV at L1 trigger, and at HLT that the third track is above
p_{T}>1.2\,
GeV
61
(for previous searches at CMS, ATLAS and LHCb see
91
;
2
;
6
). An interesting question is whether additional trigger lines could be implemented for multimuon/multihadron tau decays or whether application of scouting
62
or trigger level analyses
1
would be beneficial, in order to boost acceptance for such exotic signals.

V.2
Electron-positron colliders

At
e^{+}e^{-}
machines large tau sample are planned to be collected,
2\,(1.2)\times 10^{11}

\tau^{+}\tau^{-}
pairs at FCC-ee
40
;
80
;
81
(CEPC
43
;
10
) running at the
Z
pole,
4.5\times 10^{10}

\tau^{+}\tau^{-}
pairs at Belle II
17
;
22
, and
3.5\times 10^{10}

\tau^{+}\tau^{-}
pairs at STCF
10
. Unlike the LHC searches the multi-lepton tau decays at
e^{+}e^{-}
machines do not require a trigger. In the case of
Z
pole measurements the challenge may be vertex reconstruction, due to the large
\tau
boost in the lab frame (
\gamma_{\tau}\simeq 26
). Usually the large boost aids searches for exotic tau decays, since it makes it easier to approximately reconstruct the tau rest frame. However, in the case of fully visible final states, such at
\tau\to 5\mu
this is not an issue, and rather the question is whether some of the muon tracks would merge in high-multiplicity events. It is expected that the large lepton multiplicity of the final state will lead to negligible backgrounds, similarly to the search for
\tau\to 3\mu
at Belle II which resulted in current world best bound
9
.
For the projected sensitivity curves in
Figs.

4
and
6
, we estimate branching ratio sensitivities at future facilities by rescaling the current best upper limits, assuming similar sensitivity per tau decay. Specifically, for channels with existing measurements, such as
\tau\to 3\mu
and
\tau\to\mu e^{+}e^{-}
, the projected 90% CL upper limits are assumed to scale as the inverse of the number of produced tau leptons,
\mathcal{B}_{\rm proj}=\mathcal{B}_{\rm current}\times N_{\tau}^{\rm current}/N_{\tau}^{\rm proj}
, as appropriate for signals with negligible backgrounds. For Belle II with 50 ab
-1
this gives, e.g.,
\mathcal{B}(\tau\to 3\mu)\lesssim 1.6\times 10^{-10}
, rescaled from the current Belle II limit of
1.9\times 10^{-8}
obtained with 424 fb
-1

9
. For FCC-ee, we further rescale by the ratio of tau pair yields,
N_{\tau}^{\rm FCC\text{-}ee}/N_{\tau}^{\rm Belle,II}\approx 1.7\times 10^{11}/4.6\times 10^{10}\approx 3.7
, giving
\mathcal{B}(\tau\to 3\mu)\lesssim 4.4\times 10^{-11}
. This procedure is conservative for FCC-ee, where the large tau boost (
\gamma_{\tau}\simeq 26
) is expected to improve reconstruction efficiencies for multi-body final states relative to
B
-factory energies.
Generically, we can expect that
e^{+}e^{-}
facilities can be sensitive to branching ratios for
\tau\to 5\mu
and other fully visible multi-lepton/multi-pion final states in the range
{\mathcal{O}}(10^{-9})
to
{\mathcal{O}}(10^{-11})
. Note that this will translate to a reach of very high UV scales. For instance,
\mathcal{B}(\tau\to\mu\phi,\phi\to 4\mu)=10^{-11}
probes a scale
\Lambda_{5}/c_{23}^{(5)}\simeq 3\cdot 10^{12}\,
GeV, see
Eq.

15
. One could also search for partially invisible multilepton decays, such as
\tau\to 3\mu 2\nu
. Such an analysis would be somewhat akin to the inelastic dark matter search at Belle II
44
;
79
.

At
e^{+}e^{-}
colliders one can also search for
\phi
and
V
via their flavor diagonal couplings. For instance, Belle performed a search for
e^{+}e^{-}\to A^{\prime}\phi\to A^{\prime}A^{\prime}A^{\prime}

72
, while Belle II performed a search for
e^{+}e^{-}\to\mu\mu(X\to\mu\mu)

8
. While these searches constrain the parameter space in our benchmark models, they are not sensitive to flavor off-diagonal couplings, and thus the high UV dynamics.

VI
Conclusions

Rare tau decays provide a sensitive probe of light new physics.
In this work we explored dark sector models that produce multi-lepton signatures via cascade decays such as
\tau\to\ell\,\phi\to\ell\,VV\to\ell\,(XX)(YY)
, where
X,Y
can be charged leptons, hadrons, or neutrinos. We have also explored the possibility of even higher multiplicity tau decays, such as
\tau\to 7\mu
which can arise from
\tau\to\mu 3\mathcal{S},\mathcal{S}\to 2\mu
decay chains.
Such multi-lepton final states are qualitatively distinct from the well-studied
\tau\to 3\ell
decays and offer new experimental opportunities.

The initial step in the cascade, a higher-dimensional operator connecting two lepton flavors,
\ell_{1},\ell_{2}
and light particles
\phi,\,a,\,V,...
can be called a leptonic FCNC portal to light states. Experimental studies of such portals are not as widespread as for the other lower-dimensional flavor-conserving portals
76
. Yet the wealth of new experimental signatures uncovered in this papers, as well as in the previous works addressing
(\ell_{2}\to\ell_{1})
-initiated production of light states
32
;
69
;
58
, and fairly large energy scales
\Lambda
probed via such a portal, should serve as a strong motivation for further experimental searches of these portals.

In this work, we focused on the prompt cascade regime, where the light new physics particles,
\phi,V
or
\mathcal{S}
decay within the resolution of the tau vertex.
For benchmark couplings in the
\tau\to\ell\,\phi\to\ell\,VV
decay chain examples,
\varepsilon\sim 10^{-4}
and
\alpha_{d}\sim\alpha
, the decay lengths are
c\tau_{\phi}\sim 100
fm and
c\tau_{V}\sim 10
–
30\,\mu
m, far smaller than typical vertex resolutions.
In this case, all five final-state particles thus emerge from a single displaced vertex, a signal that is well-suited for existing
\tau\to 3\ell
analysis techniques with appropriate modifications.
The preliminary LHCb sensitivity for
\tau\to 5\mu
is
\mathcal{B}(\tau\to 5\mu)\lesssim 4\times 10^{-8}

85
, while at Belle II one can expect that the sensitivity is comparable to the current bound on
\tau\to 3\mu
, which is
\mathcal{B}(\tau\to 3\mu)<1.9\times 10^{-8}

9
. If the
\tau\to\mu\phi
decay is due to a dimension 5 operator, this would then probe a NP scale
\Lambda_{5}\gtrsim 10^{11}
GeV, see
Eq.

15
.

Exotic tau multilepton/multihadron decays remain almost completely unexplored experimentally.
The benchmark models discussed in this manuscript predict eighteen distinct five-body channels, and eight seven-body decay channels, see
Table

1
. In contrast, so far only
\tau\to 5\mu
has a (preliminary) sensitivity study.

Which of the multi-body channels dominate depends on the model. For instance, the hadronic channels
\tau\to 3\mu\,2\pi
and
\tau\to\mu\,4\pi
— which can
dominate
over
\tau\to 5\mu
in Model I above the two-pion threshold. That is, for
V
a kinetically mixed vector the
R
-ratio enhancement makes hadronic channels for
m_{V}>2m_{\pi}
the leading signatures.
Near the
\rho
resonance, in particular,
\mathcal{B}(V\to\pi\pi)/\mathcal{B}(V\to\mu\mu)\sim 10
, so that
\mathcal{B}(\tau\to\mu\,4\pi)\gg\mathcal{B}(\tau\to 5\mu)
.

Whether or not the exotic decays include missing energy signatures can be an important guide to the symmetry structure of the new physics model. For instance, the signatures
\tau\to 3\mu+\not{E}
and
\tau\to\mu+\not{E}
arise from
V\to\nu\bar{\nu}
decays in gauged lepton-number models, but are absent if
V
is a kinetically mixed dark photon. Measuring ratios between different exotic decay branching fractions can thus discriminate between the models. That is, while large
\tau\to 3\mu\,2\pi
rate relative to
\tau\to 5\mu
points to kinetic mixing, a comparable
\tau\to 5\mu
and
\tau\to 3\mu\,2\nu
rates indicate
L_{\mu}-L_{\tau}
; while
\tau\to 5e
without
\tau\to 5\mu
suggests a chiral model, at least among the benchmark models introduced here.
Even null results across multiple channels provide valuable discrimination.

While multi-lepton decays are exotic enough that the irreducible backgrounds are small, this may not be the case for accidental backgrounds in
pp
collisions. The intermediate
V\to\ell^{+}\ell^{-}
and
V\to\pi^{+}\pi^{-}
decays produce peaks in the corresponding invariant mass distributions, which can aid in constructing best search strategies, distinguishing signal from backgrounds and directly measuring
m_{V}
if observed.

While we focused on prompt decays, the benchmark models can also lead to displaced vertex signatures. For instance, for
\varepsilon\lesssim 10^{-5}
, the dark photon decay length exceeds
\mathcal{O}(\text{mm})
, producing resolvable displaced vertices. While more challenging, such displaced multi-track signatures are thus equally compelling.

Note that higher dimension FCNC operators may induce other decays in addition to tau ones.
The details on this depend on the UV theory and flavor structure of the couplings. For instance in the dark photon model supplemented by higher dimension operators
both couplings to quarks and leptons are allowed by
U(1)^{\prime}
. That is, instead of the
\phi
coupling to leptonic Yukawa operator as in
Eq.

11
one can equally well have couplings of
\phi
to up or down Higgs Yukawa operators. This means that also signatures such at
B\to K4\ell
,
D\to\pi 4\ell,...
could arise from similar cascade decays that we discussed in this paper (the possibility of such dark cascades from muon decays was already discussed in
58
).
Indeed, while there have been searches for
B_{(s)}\to 4\mu
at LHCb
4
;
5
and
B^{0}\to A^{\prime}A^{\prime}\to 4\ell
at Belle
88
, there are no
B\to KA^{\prime}A^{\prime}\to K4\ell
searches to the best of our knowledge (see, e.g.,
24
).
In other models, however, the dark cascades primarily occur only in tau decays. Examples of such model are the flavor protected scalar and
L_{\mu}-L_{\tau}
with
Q_{\Phi}=+2
, in which multilepton decays arise only from tau decays, while in
L_{\mu}+L_{e}-2L_{\tau}
, chiral
U(1)_{\ell}
and
L_{\mu}-L_{\tau}
with
Q_{\Phi}=+1
, both muon and tau dark cascade decays are allowed, but not dark cascade decays of heavy quarks.

Many of these decays could be searched for at currently running experiments. Here, Belle II is uniquely positioned to search the full spectrum of final states due to low backgrounds.
With 50 ab
-1
, sensitivities of
\mathcal{B}\sim 10^{-10}
are achievable. The multi-lepton decay searches can also be performed at LHC experiments — LHCb, CMS and ATLAS — though dedicated or modified triggers may be required.
Future
Z
-factories (FCC-ee, CEPC) with
\sim 10^{11}
tau pairs can reach
\mathcal{B}\sim 10^{-11}
, which for FCNCs induced by dimension 5 operators would probe new physics scales in the
10^{12}
GeV range.

In summary, exotic multi-lepton tau decays offer a largely unexplored window into light new physics.
The theoretical motivation is clear, the signatures are distinctive, and the searches are experimentally feasible. We encourage the experimental collaborations to pursue a comprehensive program across all predicted channels.
