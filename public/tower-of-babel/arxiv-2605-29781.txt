Restriction problems on the three-dimensional Heisenberg nilmanifold
Hajer Bahouri and Véronique Fischer

Abstract
. In this paper, we prove a spectral restriction theorem on the three-dimensional Heisenberg nilmanifold. Since this manifold is an 𝕊 1 \mathbb{S}^{1} -bundle over the flat torus 𝕋 2 \mathbb{T}^{2} , the result provides a sub-elliptic counterpart of Zygmund’s restriction theorem on 𝕋 2 \mathbb{T}^{2} [ 70 ] . We also establish its sharpness by means of the discrete short-time Fourier transform.

1.
Introduction

1.1.
Restriction problems on the torus

We start
with the classical inequality on the two-dimensional torus
\mathbb{T}^{2}
:

(1.1)

\Big\|\sum_{|\omega|=r}c_{\omega}\chi_{\omega}\Big\|_{L^{4}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}\leq 5^{\frac{1}{4}}\|(c_{\omega})_{\omega\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\|_{\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})},

valid for any
r>0
and
(c_{\omega})_{\omega\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})
, and
where we have denoted for any
\omega\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
,

|\omega|:=\sqrt{\omega_{1}^{2}+\omega_{2}^{2}}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\chi_{\omega}(x,y):=e^{2\pi i(\omega_{1}x+\omega_{2}y)},\ (x,y)\in\mathbb{T}^{2}.

This estimate, or rather its dual, was proved by Zygmund
[
70
]
in the 1970’s.
Zygmund’s result may be summarised as

(1.2)

\|\Pi_{r,\sqrt{\Delta}}\|_{\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2}),L^{4}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}=\|\Pi_{r,\sqrt{\Delta}}\|_{\mathscr{L}(L^{4/3}(\mathbb{T}^{2}),L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}\leq 5^{1/4}.

Above, the operator
\Pi_{r,\sqrt{\Delta}}
is given by

(1.3)

\Pi_{r,\sqrt{\Delta}}f=\sum_{|\omega|=r}(f,\chi_{\omega})_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}\chi_{\omega},\qquad f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2}).

This is the spectral projector onto the
r
-eigenspace for
\sqrt{\Delta}
where
\Delta=-\partial_{1}^{2}-\partial_{2}^{2}

denotes the standard Laplacian
on the two-dimensional torus
\mathbb{T}^{2}
.

This result is specific to the two-dimensional case: as we will see in Section
1.3.2
, its proof relies mainly on an underlying simple arithmetic structure. This is no longer the case in higher dimensions: for
\Pi_{r,\sqrt{\Delta}}
, with
\Delta=-\partial_{1}^{2}-\cdots-\partial_{n}^{2}
on the
n
-dimensional torus
\mathbb{T}^{n}
, it has been established by Bourgain
[
11
]
and Bourgain-Demeter
[
12
]
that, for all
n\geq 4
,
1\leq p\leq\frac{2n}{n+3}
and
\epsilon>0
, there holds

(1.4)

\|\Pi_{r,\sqrt{\Delta}}\|_{\mathscr{L}(L^{p}(\mathbb{T}^{n}),L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{n}))}\lesssim_{\epsilon}r^{n(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{2})-1+\epsilon}\,,

while for
n=3
,
1\leq p\leq\frac{3}{2}
and
\epsilon>0
, Bourgain proved in
[
13
]
the following estimate

(1.5)

\|\Pi_{r,\sqrt{\Delta}}\|_{\mathscr{L}(L^{p}(\mathbb{T}^{3}),L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{3}))}\lesssim_{\epsilon}r^{\epsilon}\,.

Such problems are known as discrete restriction problems.
Given the wide range of their applications, they have been extensively studied in recent decades and we discuss a brief historical overview in Section
1.3
below.
For the latest developments on this topic, we refer the interested reader to the surveys by Guth
[
39
]
and Demeter
[
19
,
20
]
.

1.2.
Setting of the problem and statement of the main results

The aim of this paper is to show a discrete restriction problem for the three-dimensional Heisenberg nilmanifold.
Here, the 3-dimensional Heisenberg group
\mathbb{H}_{1}
is realised as the set of
3\times 3
-matrices with real entries, upper triangular and unipotent (that is, with entries equal to 1 on the diagonal):

(1.6)

\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}1&a&c\\
0&1&b\\
0&0&1\end{array}\right),\qquad a,b,c\in\mathbb{R}.

We consider its subgroup
\Gamma
consisting of the elements above corresponding to integer entries
a,b,c\in\mathbb{Z}
.
The standard three-dimensional Heisenberg nilmanifold is the resulting right quotient:

M:=\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H}_{1}.

It is a compact smooth manifold of dimension
3
that may be described as an
\mathbb{S}^{1}
-bundle over the flat torus
\mathbb{T}^{2}
.
A fundamental fact is that

M
is equipped with a measure, a sub-Riemannian structure, and a canonical sub-Laplacian
\mathcal{L}_{M}
;
this will be described in Section
2
. The spectral decomposition of
\mathcal{L}_{M}
is well-known from a spectral viewpoint
[
21
]
and in harmonic analysis
[
47
,
65
]
; it will be recalled in Section
3.1
.

In this paper, we will prove the following result:

Theorem 1.1
.

Considering the canonical sub-Laplacian
\mathcal{L}_{M}
on
M
,
we denote by
\Pi_{\mu,\sqrt{\mathcal{L}_{M}}}
the spectral projector of
\sqrt{\mathcal{L}_{M}}
. We have for all
\mu>0
and
\epsilon>0

(1.7)

\|\Pi_{\mu,\sqrt{\mathcal{L}_{M}}}\|_{\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(M),L^{4}(M))}=\|\Pi_{\mu,\sqrt{\mathcal{L}_{M}}}\|_{\mathscr{L}(L^{4/3}(M),L^{2}(M))}\leq C_{\epsilon}\mu^{\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon}.

To our knowledge,
Theorem
1.1
is the first restriction result in the challenging context of nilmanifolds, which is both spectrally discrete and sub-elliptic.
The estimate (
1.7
) is sharp up to the factor
\mu^{\epsilon}
. More precisely, we will also show the following proposition:

Proposition 1.2
.

The exponent
1/2
in Theorem
1.1
is optimal in the sense that there exists
C>0
such that we have for all
\mu>0
in the spectrum of
\sqrt{\mathcal{L}_{M}}

\|\Pi_{\mu,\sqrt{\mathcal{L}_{M}}}\|_{\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(M),L^{4}(M))}=\|\Pi_{\mu,\sqrt{\mathcal{L}_{M}}}\|_{\mathscr{L}(L^{4/3}(M),L^{2}(M))}\geq C\mu^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Compared to Zygmund’s result (
1.2
),
the factor
\mu^{\frac{1}{2}}
can be interpreted as a fractional loss of derivatives.
Here, the notion of derivative is associated to the nilmanifold
M
and its canonical sub-Riemannian structure; it can also be defined in terms of
\sqrt{\mathcal{L}_{M}}
and its functional analysis.
This concept goes back to the seventies with the founding papers of Hörmander
[
42
]
and Rothschild-Stein
[
57
]
.
However, it is not clear whether the factor
\mu^{\epsilon}
in (
1.7
) is unavoidable: it occurs often in discrete frameworks (see e.g. (
1.4
) and (
1.5
)), and arises with questions related to number theory. This is also the case in our setting.

Theorem
1.1

improves significantly the trivial estimate given by the so-called Bernstein inequality in this context:

Proposition 1.3

(Sub-elliptic Bernstein Inequality)
.

With the notation of Theorem
1.1
, there exists a constant
C>0
such that the following estimate holds for any
\mu>0
:

(1.8)

\|\Pi_{\mu,\sqrt{\mathcal{L}_{M}}}\|_{\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(M),L^{4}(M))}=\|\Pi_{\mu,\sqrt{\mathcal{L}_{M}}}\|_{\mathscr{L}(L^{4/3}(M),L^{2}(M))}\leq C\mu.

The proof of the sub-elliptic Bernstein inequality will be given in greater generality in Theorem
C.3
, see also Remark
C.4
for the case of the Heisenberg nilmanifold.
It should be emphasised that the sub-elliptic Bernstein inequality is governed not by the topological dimension of
M
, but by the homogeneous dimension of
\mathbb{H}_{1}
, which is equal to four.

1.3.
A brief historical overview on restriction problems

1.3.1.
The Euclidean framework

Fourier restriction’s problem which was introduced by E. Stein dates back to the 60s: given a hypersurface
\hat{S}\subset\hat{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
endowed with a smooth measure
d\sigma
, the restriction problem asks for which
p
an inequality of the form

(1.9)

\|\mathcal{F}(f)|_{\hat{S}}\|_{L^{2}(\hat{S},d\sigma)}\leq C\|f\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\,,

holds for all
f
in the Schwartz space
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, where
\mathcal{F}(f)
denotes the Fourier transform of
f
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. When the Gaussian curvature of
\hat{S}
does not vanish at every point, this problem is fully understood, see
[
26
,
60
,
61
,
66
]
and the optimal range of indices
p
satisfying Inequality (
1.9
) is
[1,p_{TS}]
with
p_{TS}
the Tomas-Stein index given by
p_{TS}=(2n+2)/(n+3)
. It turns out that this problem is closely related to many questions in harmonic analysis, partial differential equations, Fourier analysis, spectral theory, number theory, and many others. As a matter of fact, this type of problem, which has been generalised to other hyper-surfaces in Euclidean spaces and other settings, remains a topical issue to this day. We refer the interested reader to the surveys on the subject with various points of view
[
2
,
20
,
39
,
63
]
and the references therein.

In this work, we focus on the case of the nilmanifold
M
for which a Fourier transform is unavailable. We will then adopt the reformulation of the problem of Fourier restriction into a spectral problem as featured for instance in
[
2
,
59
]
. As we shall see in Section
3.1
, the spectral decomposition of the sub-Laplacian
\mathcal{L}_{M}
includes the spectral decomposition of the Laplacian on the two-dimensional flat torus given by (
1.3
). So, we dedicate the following paragraph to the strategy of proof of Zygmund’s theorem
[
70
]
regarding the Fourier restriction’s problem on the two dimensional torus. This emphasises the link between the restriction problem in the discrete case and number theory.

1.3.2.
Sketch of the proof of Zygmund’s theorem

Here we present the ideas of Zygmund’s proof for (
1.1
),
and choose to give the details of the arithmetic considerations sketched in
[
70
]
.
We start with

\displaystyle\Big\|\sum_{2\pi|\omega|=r}c_{\omega}\chi_{\omega}\Big\|_{L^{4}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}^{4}

\displaystyle=\Big\|\sum_{|\omega_{1}|=|\omega_{2}|=\frac{r}{2\pi}}c_{\omega_{1}}\overline{c_{\omega_{2}}}\chi_{\omega_{1}}\overline{\chi_{\omega_{2}}}\Big\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}^{2}=\Big\|\sum_{|\omega_{1}|=|\omega_{2}|=\frac{r}{2\pi}}c_{\omega_{1}}\overline{c_{\omega_{2}}}\chi_{\omega_{1}-\omega_{2}}\Big\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|(\Gamma_{\rho,r})_{\rho\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\|^{2}_{\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})}\,,\qquad\mbox{where}\qquad\Gamma_{\rho,r}:=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\omega_{1}-\omega_{2}=\rho\\
|\omega_{1}|=\frac{r}{2\pi}=|\omega_{2}|\end{subarray}}c_{\omega_{1}}\overline{c_{\omega_{2}}}\,,

by the Plancherel formula.
We have for
\rho=0

by definition,

\Gamma_{0,r}=\sum_{|\omega|=\frac{r}{2\pi}}|c_{\omega}|^{2}=|\Gamma_{0,r}|\leq\sum_{\mu\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}|c_{\mu}|^{2}=\|(c_{\omega})\|^{2}_{\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})},

while
for any
\rho\neq 0

by the Cauchy-Schwartz inequality,

|\Gamma_{\rho,r}|^{2}\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\omega_{1}-\omega_{2}=\rho\\
|\omega_{1}|=\frac{r}{2\pi}=|\omega_{2}|\end{subarray}}|c_{\omega_{1}}\overline{c_{\omega_{2}}}|^{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\omega_{1}-\omega_{2}=\rho\\
|\omega_{1}|=\frac{r}{2\pi}=|\omega_{2}|\end{subarray}}1.

We observe that when
\rho\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{0\}
and
r^{\prime}\geq\frac{1}{2}
are given,
we can compute explicitly the solutions
\omega_{1},\omega_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
for

(1.10)

\displaystyle|\omega_{1}|=|\omega_{2}|=r^{\prime},\ \omega_{1}-\omega_{2}=\rho

\displaystyle\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\omega_{1}=\frac{\rho}{2}+\varepsilon_{0}\sqrt{{r^{\prime}}^{2}-\frac{1}{4}}\rho^{\perp},\ \omega_{2}=-\frac{\rho}{2}+\varepsilon_{0}\sqrt{{r^{\prime}}^{2}-\frac{1}{4}}\rho^{\perp},\ \varepsilon_{0}=\pm 1,

where
\rho^{\perp}:=(-\rho_{2},\rho_{1})
when we write
\rho=(\rho_{1},\rho_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, while when
\rho\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{0\}
and
0\leq r^{\prime}<\frac{1}{2}
, the set of solutions of (
1.10
) is empty.
Consequently, summing over
\rho\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
,

\sum_{\rho\neq 0}|\Gamma_{\rho,r}|^{2}\leq 2\sum_{\omega,\rho\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}|c_{\omega}|^{2}\left(|c_{\omega-\rho}|^{2}+|c_{\omega+\rho}|^{2}\right)=4\Big(\sum_{\mu\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}|c_{\mu}|^{2}\Big)^{2}.

Gathering the equalities and inequalities above, we obtain

\Big\|\sum_{2\pi|\omega|=r}c_{\omega}\chi_{\omega}\Big\|_{L^{4}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}^{4}=\|(\Gamma_{\rho,r})_{\rho\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\|^{2}_{\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})}\leq(1+4)\|(c_{\omega})\|^{4}_{\ell^{2}}=5\|(c_{\omega})\|^{4}_{\ell^{2}},

showing (
1.1
).

1.3.3.
Restriction problems in the sub-elliptic framework

Restriction problems have been studied very sparsely in the sub-elliptic setting and are limited to the framework of certain stratified Lie groups, such as the Heisenberg group, see for instance
[
5
,
8
,
16
,
52
,
54
]
and the references therein. Note in particular the surprising result of Müller
[
54
]
stressing
that for the Heisenberg group there are no non-trivial solutions for Stein’s problem in the
L^{p}
-spaces, for
p>1
,
whereas a positive result can be obtained in anisotropic Lebesgue spaces!

The reason why only a few cases of non commutative Lie groups have been studied is not solely due to the complexity of the group Fourier transform, but also because several phenomena that occur in the Euclidean case are attenuated or fail in the case of these groups. This is for instance the case of the property of unique continuation, dispersion phenomena, Strichartz estimates and observability; see among others
[
4
,
5
,
7
,
8
,
10
,
14
,
15
,
24
,
50
,
54
]
and the references therein.

As mentioned above, the manifold
M
is not itself a group and therefore
does not carry an intrinsic Fourier transform. We instead approach restriction
from a spectral viewpoint, in the spirit of
[
8
,
59
]
, but in a
discrete sub-elliptic setting. The harmonic analysis of the Heisenberg group
\mathbb{H}_{1}

yields the relevant decomposition of
L^{2}(M)
; within this decomposition, we
choose an orthonormal basis of eigenfunctions for
\mathcal{L}_{M}
. As in Zygmund’s
proof on
\mathbb{T}^{2}
, this basis has arithmetic properties that are
essential to the argument.

1.4.
Layout and notation

The paper is organised as follows. In Section
2
, we set the notation and recall the basic properties of the Heisenberg group and the associated nilmanifold
M=\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H}_{1}
. This naturally leads to an important decomposition of
L^{2}(M)
. The paper is written to be almost entirely self-contained: all relevant background is recalled in detail, with some proofs deferred to the appendix.

Section
3
is devoted to the proof of the main result. We first describe the spectral decomposition of
\mathcal{L}_{M}
, then establish special properties of the orthonormal basis used in the argument, which extend classical properties of the Zak transform (see Section
3.2
). The proof of the main theorem then follows.
We conclude by proving sharpness; the argument here is guided by the natural appearance of the discrete short-time Fourier transform in the relevant expressions, which provided key intuition.

The appendix collects background material on Hermite and Laguerre functions, as well as on nilmanifolds with a particular emphasis on the Heisenberg nilmanifold. In particular, it contains the proof of the sub-elliptic Bernstein inequality on the Heisenberg nilmanifold
M
stated in Proposition
1.3
.

We conclude this introduction with a comment on notation. In this paper, the letter
C
will be used to denote universal constants
which may vary from line to line. If we need the implied constant to depend on parameters, we shall indicate this by subscripts. We also use the notation
A\lesssim B
(respectively
A\gtrsim B
) to
denote bounds of the form
A\leq CB
(respectively
A\geq CB
),
and
A\lesssim_{\epsilon}B

for
A\leq C_{\epsilon}B
, where
C_{\epsilon}
depends only on
\epsilon
.

2.
Notation and basic properties

2.1.
The Heisenberg group and its standard nilmanifold

The Heisenberg group arises in a number of
fundamental domains of mathematics and physics. Owing to
this wide variety of topics, there are different ways of realising it. Whatever the chosen realisation is, the Heisenberg group is a non commutative Lie group, that is a group
endowed with a smooth manifold structure, in which the group operations of multiplication
and inversion are smooth.

Throughout this paper, we shall consider the 3-dimensional Heisenberg group
\mathbb{H}_{1}
realised as the set of
upper triangular and unipotent real
3\times 3
matrices, that is, the matrices of the form (
1.6
).
Then
\mathbb{H}_{1}
is the connected simply connected nilpotent Lie group identified with

\{(a,b,c)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\}

equipped with the non commutative group law:

(2.1)

(a,b,c)(a^{\prime},b^{\prime},c^{\prime})=(a+a^{\prime},b+b^{\prime},c+c^{\prime}+ab^{\prime}).

Its neutral element is the origin

(0,0,0)
, the inverse of a generic element
(a,b,c)
of
\mathbb{H}_{1}
is just
(-a,-b,-c+ab)
.

We consider its discrete subgroup

\Gamma:=\{(a,b,c)\in\mathbb{H}_{1}\ :\ a,b,c\in\mathbb{Z}\}.

The standard three-dimensional Heisenberg nilmanifold is then the right quotient

M:=\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H}_{1}.

Its elements are the
\Gamma
-right equivalence classes over
\mathbb{H}_{1}
:

M\ni\dot{x}=\Gamma x,\quad x\in\mathbb{H}_{1}.

We fix the Haar measure

dx=dadbdc
on
\mathbb{H}_{1}
and the counting measure
\sum_{x\in\Gamma}\delta_{x}
on
\Gamma
.
This induces a unique measure
d\dot{x}
on
M
,
see Appendix
B.1.2
.
Moreover, a fundamental domain in
\mathbb{H}_{1}
is given by

\{(a,b,c)\in\mathbb{H}_{1}:a,b,c\in[0,1)\}\sim[0,1)^{3};

on this fundamental domain, the measure induced on
M
identifies with
dadbdc
. In particular,
M
is of volume
{\rm vol}(M)=1
.

Having fixed the Haar measure on
\mathbb{H}_{1}
as above allows us to identify the functional spaces on
\mathbb{H}_{1}
and on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, see Appendix
B.1.1
; for instance, we will write
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{H}_{1})
for the space of Schwartz functions,
C_{c}(\mathbb{H}_{1})
for the space of continuous functions with compact support,
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{H}_{1})
for the space of smooth functions with compact support,
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{H}_{1})
,
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{H}_{1})
,
L^{p}(\mathbb{H}_{1})
,
L^{p}_{loc}(\mathbb{H}_{1})
etc.

Similarly fixing a measure on
M
enables us to consider the space
L^{p}(M)
,
p\in[1,\infty]
.
Moreover, since
M
is a smooth manifold, the identification above
implies that the space
C^{\infty}(M)
of smooth functions on
M
is dense in
L^{p}(M)
for any
p\in[1,\infty]
.

2.2.
The Lie algebra of
\mathbb{H}_{1}

The Lie algebra
\mathfrak{h}_{1}
of the Heisenberg group
\mathbb{H}_{1}
is
the space of its left-invariant vector fields.
Given our realisation of the product law in (
2.1
),
the canonical basis of
\mathfrak{h}_{1}
is given by:

A:=\partial_{a},\qquad B:=\partial_{b}+a\partial_{c},\qquad S:=\partial_{c}.

We readily check that they are invariant under left-translation and satisfy the canonical commutation relations, known as CCR in quantum mechanics:

[A,S]=0=[B,S]\quad\mbox{and}\quad[A,B]=S.

Note that
\mathbb{H}_{1}
can be globally reconstructed from its Lie algebra
\mathfrak{h}_{1}
by means of the exponential map
\exp:\mathfrak{h}_{1}\to\mathbb{H}_{1}
, which is a smooth bijection with a smooth inverse, and the Baker-Cambell-Hausdorff formula. Under the realisation (
1.6
),
\mathfrak{h}_{1}
may be realised as the three-dimensional vector space of matrices of the form

\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}0&a&c\\
0&0&b\\
0&0&0\end{array}\right),\qquad a,b,c\in\mathbb{R},

and the exponential map is therefore nothing else than the matrix exponential
\exp:\mathfrak{h}_{1}\to\mathbb{H}_{1}
.

As the vector fields
A,B,S
are left-invariant, they descend to vector fields
A_{M}
,
B_{M}
,
S_{M}
etc. on
M
; see Section
B.2
.
The vector fields
A_{M},B_{M},S_{M}
form a frame on
M

and satisfy also the canonical commutation relations.

It is natural to define the sub-Laplacians on
\mathbb{H}_{1}
and on
M

\mathcal{L}:=-(A^{2}+B^{2})\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{L}_{M}:=-(A_{M}^{2}+B_{M}^{2}).

These linear differential operators are positive, hypoelliptic and essentially self-adjoint on
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{H}_{1})\subset L^{2}(\mathbb{H}_{1})
and on
C^{\infty}(M)\subset L^{2}(M)
respectively.
We will keep the same notation for their self-adjoint extensions respectively on
L^{2}(\mathbb{H}_{1})
and on
L^{2}(M)
.

2.3.
The Schrödinger representations
\pi_{\lambda}
,
\lambda\neq 0

In this paper, we realise the Schrödinger representations
\pi_{\lambda}
of
\mathbb{H}_{1}
for
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
acting on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
as

(2.2)

\pi_{\lambda}(a,b,c)h(u)=e^{2\pi i\lambda(c+ub)}h(u+a),\quad(a,b,c)\in\mathbb{H}_{1},\ h\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}),\ u\in\mathbb{R}.

Recall that
\pi_{\lambda}
is a group homomorphism between
\mathbb{H}_{1}
and the unitary group
\mathcal{U}(L^{2}(\mathbb{R}))
of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, and plays the same role as the characters for the Euclidean space, as regards the Fourier transform on
\mathbb{H}_{1}
. For an overview on this subject, we refer for instance the reader to
[
6
,
17
,
25
,
30
,
46
]
and the references therein.

All along this paper, we keep the same notation for the infinitesimal Schrödinger representation on the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{h}_{1}
of
\mathbb{H}_{1}
.
We will identify
\mathfrak{h}_{1}
with the space of left-invariant vector fields via

X\phi(g)=\partial_{t}\phi(g\exp tX),\qquad\phi\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{H}_{1}),\ g\in\mathbb{H}_{1};

above we have kept the same notation for the matrix
X\in\mathfrak{h}_{1}
and the corresponding vector field.
The infinitesimal Schrödinger representation on the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{h}_{1}
of
\mathbb{H}_{1}
is then defined as

\pi_{\lambda}(X)=\partial_{t=0}\pi_{\lambda}(\exp tX).

We then easily compute

(2.3)

\pi_{\lambda}(A)h(u)=h^{\prime}(u),\quad\pi_{\lambda}(B)h(u)=2\pi i\lambda uh(u),\quad\pi_{\lambda}(S)=2\pi i\lambda\text{\rm I}.

All along this paper, we also keep the same notation for the Schrödinger representation on the universal enveloping algebra of
\mathfrak{h}_{1}
.
In particular, the Schrödinger representation for the sub-Laplacian is the rescaled harmonic oscillator:

(2.4)

\pi_{\lambda}(\mathcal{L})=-\frac{d^{2}}{du^{2}}+(2\pi\lambda u)^{2}.

The definition of
the Hermite functions

h_{\ell}
is given in (
A.1
). We rescale them via

(2.5)

h_{\ell,\lambda}(u):=(2\pi|\lambda|)^{\frac{1}{4}}\,h_{\ell}\left(\sqrt{2\pi|\lambda|}u\right),\quad u\in\mathbb{R},

so that they become the eigenfunctions of
\pi_{\lambda}(\mathcal{L})
:

(2.6)

\pi_{\lambda}(\mathcal{L})h_{\ell,\lambda}=2\pi|\lambda|(2\ell+1)h_{\ell,\lambda},\quad\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

Furthermore, the spectrum of
\pi_{\lambda}(\mathcal{L})
is
\{2\pi|\lambda|(2\ell+1),\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\}
.

It is well-known that the matrix coefficients of the Schrödinger representation
with respect to the Hermite functions are expressed in terms of Laguerre
functions, see (
A.4
).
With the convention of our paper, this is expressed as

(2.7)

(h_{\ell,\lambda},\pi_{\lambda}(a,b,c)h_{\ell,\lambda})_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}=e^{-2\pi i\lambda c}e^{\pi i\lambda ba}\mathcal{L}_{\ell}\left(\pi|\lambda|(a^{2}+b^{2})\right).

2.4.
Decomposition of
L^{2}(M)
under the regular representation

The Heisenberg group
\mathbb{H}_{1}
acts unitarily on
L^{2}(M)
via the right regular representation:

(2.8)

R:\mathbb{H}_{1}\to\mathscr{U}(L^{2}(M)),\qquad R(g)f(\dot{x})=f(\dot{x}g),\ g\in\mathbb{H}_{1},\ f\in L^{2}(M),\ \dot{x}\in M.

Moreover, this action leads to a decomposition of
L^{2}(M)
that we briefly present here.

2.4.1.
Statement

Here, we recall features of this decomposition, especially the properties that will be useful in the rest of the paper.

Theorem 2.1
.

The decomposition in Fourier series in the
c
-variable yields
an orthogonal decomposition of
the space
L^{2}(M)
into subspaces

(2.9)

L^{2}(M)=\oplus^{\perp}_{\lambda\in\mathbb{Z}}L^{2}_{\lambda}(M),

that respects the action of the regular representation
R

defined by (
2.8
);
the subspaces
L^{2}_{\lambda}(M)
are explicitly described in Section
2.4.3
.

(1)

The space
L^{2}_{0}(M)
admits a further decomposition

L^{2}_{0}(M)=\oplus_{w\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}^{\perp}\mathbb{C}\chi_{\omega},

that is irreducible and respects the action of
R
.
Above,
\chi_{\omega}\in C^{\infty}(M)
is defined via

(2.10)

\chi_{\omega}(\dot{x}):=\exp(2\pi i\omega\cdot(a,b)),\qquad x=(a,b,c)\in\mathbb{H}_{1},\ \omega\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}.

(2)

Let
\lambda\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
.
In order to obtain the further decomposition of
L^{2}_{\lambda}(M)
,
we define the generalised Berezin-Weil-Zak transform as

(2.11)

{\rm BWZ}_{\lambda,q}(h)(\dot{x}):=\frac{\sqrt{|\lambda|}}{\lambda}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}e^{-2\pi iq\frac{k}{\lambda}}e^{2\pi i(\lambda c+kb)}h\left(\frac{k}{\lambda}+a\right),

for
h\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
,
q\in\mathbb{Z}
and
x=(a,b,c)\in\mathbb{H}_{1}
.
The BWZ transform enjoys the following properties:

(a)

For any
q\in\mathbb{Z}
and
h\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
,
{\rm BWZ}_{\lambda,q}(h)
is a well defined smooth function on
M
which belongs to
L^{2}_{\lambda}(M)
. It satisfies
for any
k_{1}\in\mathbb{Z}
,

{\rm BWZ}_{\lambda,\lambda k_{1}+q}(h)={\rm BWZ}_{\lambda,q}(h),

allowing us to see the parameter
q
in
\mathbb{Z}/\lambda\mathbb{Z}
.

(b)

For each
q\in\mathbb{Z}/\lambda\mathbb{Z}
,
the transformation

{\rm BWZ}_{\lambda,q}:\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})\to C^{\infty}(M)
extends uniquely to a unitary linear transformation

{\rm BWZ}_{\lambda,q}:L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\longrightarrow L^{2}_{\lambda}(M).

(c)

For any
q_{0},q\in\mathbb{Z}
and
\Gamma(a,b,c)=\dot{x}\in M
, we have

(2.12)

\displaystyle{\rm BWZ}_{\lambda,q_{0}}(h)\left(\dot{x}\left(\frac{q}{\lambda},0,0\right)\right)

\displaystyle=e^{-2\pi iqb}e^{2\pi iq_{0}\frac{q}{\lambda}}{\rm BWZ}_{\lambda,q_{0}}(h)(\dot{x}),

\displaystyle{\rm BWZ}_{\lambda,q_{0}}(h)\left(\dot{x}\left(0,\frac{q}{\lambda},0\right)\right)

\displaystyle=e^{2\pi iqa}{\rm BWZ}_{\lambda,q_{0}-q}(h)(\dot{x}).

(3)

The subspaces

L^{2}_{\lambda,q}(M):={\rm BWZ}_{\lambda,q}(L^{2}(\mathbb{R})),\qquad q\in\mathbb{Z}/\lambda\mathbb{Z},

of
L^{2}_{\lambda}(M)
are orthogonal and their sums fills:

L^{2}_{\lambda}(M)=\oplus^{\perp}_{q\in\mathbb{Z}/\lambda\mathbb{Z}}L^{2}_{\lambda,q}(M).

Moreover, the regular representation
R
defined by (
2.8
) acts on
L^{2}_{\lambda,q}(M)
,
where it is unitarily equivalent to the (irreducible) Schrödinger representation
\pi_{\lambda}

with
{\rm BWZ}_{\lambda,q}
as intertwiner.

Theorem
2.1
is well-known with perhaps different conventions, but we include the proofs for the sake of completeness:
the decomposition in (
2.9
) and
Part (1) are shown below (in Sections
2.4.3
and
2.4.4
respectively), while the proofs of Parts (2) and (3) are postponed to Appendix
D
.
References for this material include
[
47
,
65
]
.

2.4.2.
The BWZ transform

The generalised Berezin-Weil-Zak transform (
2.11
) plays a key role for our purpose. Such transform is well-known with different scales and up to unitary operators. According to the field of use, it is also referred to as the Bloch-Floquet, Gabor, Gel’fand or Zak transform. It was first introduced by Gel’fand
[
34
]
in 1950 for the sake of a problem in ordinary differential
equations, then extended by Weil
[
68
]
in 1964 to the setting of locally compact abelian groups with respect to arbitrary
closed sub-groups, and rediscovered in 1967 by Zak
[
69
]
who used it to construct a quantum mechanical representation
for the description of the dynamic of an electron in the presence of a constant
magnetic or electric field.
These transforms have been a matter of great interest in the last decades and developed by many authors on locally compact groups (abelian and non commutative) and groupoids, see for instance
[
18
,
32
,
37
,
44
,
45
,
48
,
69
]
and the references therein.

We observe that the orthogonality of the
{\rm BWZ}_{\lambda,q}(L^{2}(\mathbb{R}))=L^{2}_{\lambda,q}(M)
,
q\in\mathbb{Z}/\lambda\mathbb{Z}
,
together with the unitarity of
{\rm BWZ}_{\lambda,q}
are equivalent to

(2.13)

({\rm BWZ}_{\lambda_{1},q_{1}}(h_{1}),{\rm BWZ}_{\lambda_{2},q_{2}}(h_{2}))_{L^{2}(M)}=\delta_{\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{2},q_{1}=q_{2}}(h_{1},h_{2})_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})},

holding for all
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
,
h_{1},h_{2}\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
and
q_{1},q_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}
;
this explains our normalisation for
{\rm BWZ}_{\lambda,q}
.
The
{\rm BWZ}_{\lambda,q}
transform intertwining the regular and Schrödinger representations means that

(2.14)

\forall g\in\mathbb{H}_{1},\qquad{\rm BWZ}_{\lambda,q}\pi_{\lambda}(g)=R(g){\rm BWZ}_{\lambda,q},

so

(2.15)

\forall X\in\mathfrak{h}_{1},\qquad{\rm BWZ}_{\lambda,q}\pi_{\lambda}(X)=X_{M}{\rm BWZ}_{\lambda,q}.

2.4.3.
A first decomposition

We start by resorting to Fourier series in the central variable:

f=\sum_{\lambda\in\mathbb{Z}}f_{\lambda},

where
f_{\lambda}
is loosely defined as

(2.16)

f_{\lambda}(a,b,c):=e^{2\pi i\lambda c}\int_{0}^{1}f(a,b,c^{\prime})e^{-2\pi i\lambda c^{\prime}}dc^{\prime},\quad(a,b,c)\in[0,1)^{3}\sim M.

Let us pause to give a meaning to this formula.
Since
f\in L^{2}(M)
, we know
\phi:=f_{\mathbb{H}_{1}}\in L^{2}_{loc}(\mathbb{H}_{1})
so the function defined via

\phi_{\lambda}(a,b,c):=e^{2\pi i\lambda c}\int_{0}^{1}f(a,b,c^{\prime})e^{-2\pi i\lambda c^{\prime}}dc^{\prime},\qquad(a,b,c)\in\mathbb{H}_{1},

is also in
L^{2}_{loc}(\mathbb{H}_{1})
; we check that it is
\Gamma
-invariant, and we set
f_{\lambda}=(\phi_{\lambda})_{M}
.

In fact, we check that each
f_{\lambda}
above is in the subspace

(2.17)

L^{2}_{\lambda}(M):=\{f\in L^{2}(M)\ \colon\ f(\Gamma(a,b,c))=f(\Gamma(a,b,0))e^{2\pi i\lambda c}\}

of
L^{2}(M)
, and is isomorphic via
f\mapsto f_{\mathbb{H}_{1}}=\phi
onto the space
L^{2}_{\lambda}(\mathbb{H}_{1})
of function
\phi\in L^{2}_{loc}(\mathbb{H}_{1})
that are
\Gamma
-invariant and satisfy

\phi(a,b,c)=\phi(a,b,0)e^{2\pi i\lambda c}\ \mbox{for all}\ (a,b,c)\in\mathbb{H}_{1}.

We have obtained the first decomposition announced in (
2.9
), that is,

L^{2}(M)=\oplus^{\perp}_{\lambda\in\mathbb{Z}}L^{2}_{\lambda}(M).

The orthogonal projectors of this decomposition
are denoted by

(2.18)

{\rm{Pr}}_{\lambda}:L^{2}(M)\to L^{2}_{\lambda}(M),\quad\lambda\in\mathbb{Z}.

They enjoy the following properties:

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
\lambda\in\mathbb{Z}
.
The orthogonal projector
P_{\lambda}
is formally given via

{\rm{Pr}}_{\lambda}f(\dot{x})=e^{2\pi i\lambda c}\int_{0}^{1}f(a,b,c^{\prime})e^{-2\pi i\lambda c^{\prime}}dc^{\prime},\qquad x=(a,b,c)\in\mathbb{H}_{1},\quad f\in C^{\infty}(M).

Moreover, for any
f\in C^{\infty}(M)
,
we have

\|{\rm{Pr}}_{\lambda}f\|_{L^{p}(M)}\leq\|f\|_{L^{p}(M)},\quad p\in[1,\infty].

Proof.

The formula for the projectors follows readily from (
2.16
).
It implies by Jensen’s inequality that we have for any
f\in C^{\infty}(M)

|{\rm{Pr}}_{\lambda}f(\dot{x})|^{p}=\Big|\int_{0}^{1}f(a,b,c^{\prime})e^{-2\pi i\lambda c^{\prime}}dc^{\prime}\Big|^{p}\leq\int_{0}^{1}|f(a,b,c^{\prime})e^{-2\pi i\lambda c^{\prime}}|^{p}dc^{\prime}=\int_{0}^{1}|f(a,b,c^{\prime})|^{p}dc^{\prime},

hence,

\|{\rm{Pr}}_{\lambda}f\|_{L^{p}(M)}^{p}\leq\int_{[0,1)^{3}}\int_{0}^{1}|f(a,b,c^{\prime})|^{p}dc^{\prime}dadbdc=\int_{[0,1)^{3}}|f(a,b,c^{\prime})|^{p}dadbdc^{\prime}=\|f\|_{L^{p}(M)}^{p}.

The case
p=+\infty
also holds true by the same formula.
∎

We observe that Decomposition (
2.9
) respects the action of the regular representation
R
defined by (
2.8
):

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
\lambda\in\mathbb{Z}
.
For any
g\in\mathbb{H}_{1}
,

R(g)
maps
L^{2}_{\lambda}(M)
into itself.
Moreover, we have for any
f\in L^{2}_{\lambda}(M)

\forall c\in\mathbb{R},\quad R(0,0,c)f=e^{2\pi i\lambda c}f.

Proof.

We compute for any
\phi:\mathbb{H}_{1}\to\mathbb{C}

\forall(a^{\prime},b^{\prime},c^{\prime}),(a,b,c)\in\mathbb{H}_{1}\qquad\phi((a^{\prime},b^{\prime},c^{\prime})(a,b,c))=\phi(a^{\prime}+a,b^{\prime}+b,c^{\prime}+c+a^{\prime}b),

and we check that if
\phi\in L^{2}_{\lambda}(\mathbb{H}_{1})
, then so does

(a^{\prime},b^{\prime},c^{\prime})\mapsto\phi((a^{\prime},b^{\prime},c^{\prime})(a,b,c))
.
This shows that
R(g)
maps
L^{2}_{\lambda}(M)
into itself as well as the action of
R(0,0,c)
.
∎

To understand the action of
\mathbb{H}_{1}
via
R
onto each
L^{2}_{\lambda}(M)
, we need to distinguish the cases
\lambda=0
and
\lambda\neq 0
.

2.4.4.
Case
\lambda=0

The construction above implies the following property for
L^{2}_{0}(M)
:

Lemma 2.4
.

The space
L^{2}_{0}(M)
is isomorphic via
f\mapsto f|_{c=0}
to the space
L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
.

Proof.

By construction,

L^{2}_{0}(\mathbb{H}_{1})
coincides with the space of functions
\phi\in L^{2}_{loc}(\mathbb{H}_{1})
which do not depend on the central variable, and as functions of
(a,b)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
which are
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
-periodic.
Hence
f\mapsto f|_{c=0}
is a linear map
C^{\infty}(M)\cap L^{2}_{0}(M)\to C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
that extends continuously uniquely into an isomorphism between
L^{2}_{0}(M)
and
L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
.
∎

We obtain a further decomposition of
L^{2}_{0}(M)
by considering the Fourier series on
\mathbb{T}^{2}
.
For this,
we define the function
\chi_{\omega}\in C^{\infty}(M)
via (
2.10
), or in other words via the
\Gamma
-invariant function
(a,b,c)\mapsto e^{2i\pi(\omega_{1}a+\omega_{2}b)}
on
\mathbb{H}_{1}

for each
\omega=(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
.

Any
f\in L^{2}_{0}(M)
can then be written as

f=\sum_{\omega\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}(f,\chi_{\omega})_{L^{2}(M)}\chi_{\omega}.

We check readily that

R(a,b,c)\chi_{\omega}=e^{2\pi i(\omega_{1}a+\omega_{2}b)}\chi_{\omega},\quad(a,b,c)\in\mathbb{H}_{1}.

Therefore,
R
acts on
\mathbb{C}\chi_{\omega}
and we have an orthogonal decomposition

L^{2}_{0}(M)=\oplus^{\perp}_{\omega\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\mathbb{C}\chi_{\omega}.

This shows Part (1) of Theorem
2.1
.

2.4.5.
Case
\lambda\neq 0

The decomposition for
\lambda\neq 0
is also classical in harmonic analysis,
but its explicit construction is more technical.
For completeness, we recall it in Appendix
D
, thereby
completing the proof of Theorem
2.1
.

3.
Proof of the main results

3.1.
The spectral decomposition of
\mathcal{L}_{M}

We consider the functions
\chi_{\omega}
,
\omega\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, defined in (
2.10
), as well as

h_{\lambda,q,\ell}:={\rm BWZ}_{\lambda,q}h_{\ell,\lambda},\quad\lambda\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\},\ q\in\mathbb{Z}/\lambda\mathbb{Z},\ \ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0},

with
h_{\ell,\lambda}
the normalised Hermite functions defined by (
2.5
), and where
the
{\rm BWZ}_{\lambda,q}
transform was defined in Theorem
2.1
. Then

(3.1)

h_{\lambda,q,\ell}\left(\Gamma(a,b,c)\right)=\frac{\sqrt{|\lambda|}}{\lambda}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}e^{-2\pi iq\frac{k}{\lambda}}e^{2\pi i(\lambda c+kb)}h_{\ell,\lambda}\left(\frac{k}{\lambda}+a\right).

Theorem 3.1
.

The functions
\chi_{\omega}
and
h_{\lambda,q,\ell}
on
M
are smooth and
L^{2}
-normalised:

(3.2)

\|\chi_{\omega}\|_{L^{2}(M)}=1\quad\mbox{and}\quad\|h_{\lambda,q,\ell}\|_{L^{2}(M)}=1.

They are eigenfunctions of the sub-Laplacian
\mathcal{L}_{M}
on
M

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{M}h_{\lambda,\ell,q}

\displaystyle=2\pi|\lambda|(2\ell+1)h_{\lambda,\ell,q},\quad\lambda\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\},\ q\in\mathbb{Z}/\lambda\mathbb{Z},\ \ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0},

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{M}\chi_{\omega}

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{2}|\omega|^{2}\chi_{\omega},\qquad\qquad\omega\in\mathbb{Z}^{2},

and form an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}(M)=\oplus^{\perp}_{\lambda\in\mathbb{Z}}L^{2}_{\lambda}(M)
.
More precisely,
(\chi_{\omega})_{\omega\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}
is an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}_{0}(M)

while, for each
\lambda\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
,
(h_{\lambda,q,\ell})_{q\in\mathbb{Z}/\lambda\mathbb{Z},\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}

is an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}_{\lambda}(M)
.

Proof.

The properties for
\chi_{\omega}
follow from Fourier analysis on the two-dimensional torus, while the ones for
h_{\lambda,\ell,q}
follows from Theorem
2.1
and the properties of
h_{\ell,\lambda}
recalled in Section
2.3
.
Indeed, by virtue of (
2.15
), one has

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{M}h_{\lambda,q,\ell}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}_{M}{\rm BWZ}_{\lambda,q}h_{\ell,\lambda}={\rm BWZ}_{\lambda,q}\pi_{\lambda}(\mathcal{L})h_{\ell,\lambda},

which gives the result thanks to (
2.4
) and (
2.6
).
∎

Remark 3.2
.

The spectral decomposition of
\mathcal{L}_{M}
is well-known.
For instance, Deninger and Singhof in
[
21
]
described an orthogonal basis of
\mathcal{L}_{M}
-eigenfunctions of
L^{2}(M)
in
[
21
]
. With our notation and after
L^{2}
-normalisation, their eigenfunctions are the smooth and
L^{2}
-normalised functions
\chi_{\omega}
,
\omega\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, as above, and

g_{\lambda,r,\ell}
,
r\in\mathbb{Z}/\lambda\mathbb{Z}
,
\lambda\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
,
\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,

g_{\lambda,r,\ell}\left(\Gamma(a,b,c)\right)=e^{2\pi i(\lambda c+rb)}\sum_{k_{1}\in\mathbb{Z}}e^{2\pi i\lambda k_{1}b}h_{\ell,\lambda}\left(a+\frac{r}{\lambda}+k_{1}\right).

Indeed, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{M}g_{\lambda,r,\ell}

\displaystyle=2\pi|\lambda|(2\ell+1)h_{\lambda,r,\ell},\qquad\lambda\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\},\ r\in\mathbb{Z}/\lambda\mathbb{Z},\ \ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0},

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{M}\chi_{\omega}

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{2}|\omega|^{2}\chi_{\omega},\qquad\omega\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}.

This basis is unitarily equivalent to ours since we have
for any
\lambda\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
and
\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,

\displaystyle h_{\lambda,q,\ell}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{|\lambda|}}{\lambda}\sum_{0\leq r<|\lambda|}e^{-2\pi iq\frac{r}{\lambda}}g_{\lambda,r,\ell},\quad q\in\mathbb{Z}/\lambda\mathbb{Z},

\displaystyle g_{\lambda,r_{0},\ell}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{|\lambda|}}{\lambda}\sum_{0\leq q<|\lambda|}e^{2\pi iq\frac{r_{0}}{\lambda}}h_{\lambda,q,\ell},\quad r_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}/\lambda\mathbb{Z}.

3.2.
A key property of the
\mathcal{L}_{M}
-eigenfunctions
h_{\lambda,q,\ell}

As emphasised in the proof of Zygmund’s estimate in Section
1.3.2
,
the main difficulty in the proof of Theorem
1.1
lies in estimating the

L^{4}
norms related to the functions
h_{\lambda,q,\ell}
.
The starting point for these estimates is the following new identity:

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
\lambda\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}

and
\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. For any family of coefficients
\gamma_{q}
,

q\in\mathbb{Z}/\lambda\mathbb{Z}
, we have

(3.3)

\bigg\|\sum_{q\in\mathbb{Z}/\lambda\mathbb{Z}}\gamma_{q}h_{\lambda,q,\ell}\bigg\|_{L^{4}(M)}^{4}=\sum_{a,b\in\mathbb{Z}}\Bigg|\Big(\sum_{q\in\mathbb{Z}/\lambda\mathbb{Z}}\gamma_{q}\overline{\gamma_{q-b}}e^{2\pi iq\frac{a}{\lambda}}\Big)\mathcal{L}_{\ell}\left(\pi\frac{a^{2}+b^{2}}{|\lambda|}\right)\Bigg|^{2},

where
\mathcal{L}_{\ell}
is the Laguerre function of type 0 and degree
\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.

To prove Theorem
3.3
, we start by pointing out that the
traditional Zak transform
Z
coincide with our Berezin-Weil-Zak transform for
\lambda=1
,
q=0
, restricted to a part of the fundamental domain, namely
[0,1)^{2}\times\{0\}
, that is,

Zh(a,b):={\rm BWZ}_{1,0}h(a,b,0)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}e^{2\pi ikb}h\left(k+a\right),\qquad(a,b)\in[0,1)^{2},\ h\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R});

The set

[0,1)^{2}
is often identified with the two-dimensional torus
\mathbb{T}^{2}=\mathbb{R}^{2}/\mathbb{Z}^{2}
.
Well-known identities for the Zak transform

[
32
]
(see also
[
23
]
)
generalise to
{\rm BWZ}_{\lambda,q}
, for instance:

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
\lambda\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
. For any
h\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
and any family of coefficients
\gamma_{q}
,

q\in\mathbb{Z}/\lambda\mathbb{Z}
, we have

\bigg\|\sum_{q\in\mathbb{Z}/\lambda\mathbb{Z}}\gamma_{q}{\rm BWZ}_{\lambda,q}h\bigg\|_{L^{4}(M)}^{4}=\sum_{a,b\in\mathbb{Z}}\bigg|\big(\sum_{q\in\mathbb{Z}/\lambda\mathbb{Z}}\gamma_{q}\overline{\gamma_{q-b}}e^{2\pi iq\frac{a}{\lambda}}\Big)\left(h,\pi_{\lambda}\left(\frac{a}{\lambda},\frac{b}{\lambda},0\right)h\right)_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\bigg|^{2}.

Proof of Proposition
3.4
.

We observe

\displaystyle\bigg\|\sum_{q\in\mathbb{Z}/\lambda\mathbb{Z}}\gamma_{q}{\rm BWZ}_{\lambda,q}h\bigg\|_{L^{4}(M)}^{4}

\displaystyle=\bigg\|\sum_{q_{1},q_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}/\lambda\mathbb{Z}}\gamma_{q_{1}}\overline{\gamma_{q_{2}}}{\rm BWZ}_{\lambda,q_{1}}h\overline{{\rm BWZ}_{\lambda,q_{2}}h}\bigg\|_{L^{2}(M)}^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{[0,1]^{2}}|F(a,b)|^{2}dadb,

with

F(a,b):=\sum_{q_{1},q_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}/\lambda\mathbb{Z}}\gamma_{q_{1}}\overline{\gamma_{q_{2}}}({\rm BWZ}_{\lambda,q_{1}}h\overline{{\rm BWZ}_{\lambda,q_{2}}h})(a,b,0).

We now expand
F\in L^{2}([0,1]^{2})
in Fourier series and obtain by the Plancherel formula:

\displaystyle\int_{[0,1]^{2}}|F(a,b)|^{2}(a,b,0)dadb

\displaystyle=\sum_{a^{\prime},b^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}}\bigg|\int_{[0,1]^{2}}F(a,b)e^{2\pi i(a^{\prime}b-ab^{\prime})}dadb\bigg|^{2}.

Hence, we have obtained:

\displaystyle\bigg\|\sum_{q\in\mathbb{Z}/\lambda\mathbb{Z}}\gamma_{q}{\rm BWZ}_{\lambda,q}h\bigg\|_{L^{4}(M)}^{4}

(3.4)

\displaystyle\quad=\sum_{a^{\prime},b^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}}\bigg|\sum_{q_{1},q_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}/\lambda\mathbb{Z}}\gamma_{q_{1}}\overline{\gamma_{q_{2}}}\int_{[0,1]^{2}}({\rm BWZ}_{\lambda,q_{1}}h\ \overline{{\rm BWZ}_{\lambda,q_{2}}h})(a,b,0)\ e^{2\pi i(a^{\prime}b-ab^{\prime})}dadb\bigg|^{2}.

In order to analyse each integral over
[0,1]^{2}
in the right-hand side of (
3.4
), we start with noticing that for any
x^{\prime}=(a^{\prime},b^{\prime},c^{\prime})\in\mathbb{H}_{1}
, the integral

(3.5)

\int_{M}{\rm BWZ}_{\lambda,q_{1}}h_{1}(\dot{x})\overline{{\rm BWZ}_{\lambda,q_{2}}h_{2}(\dot{x}x^{\prime})}d\dot{x}

is equal to 0 if
q_{1}\neq q_{2}
in
\mathbb{Z}/\lambda\mathbb{Z}
since

L^{2}_{\lambda,q_{1}}\perp L^{2}_{\lambda,q_{2}}
when
q_{1}\neq q_{2}
(see Theorem
2.1
), while if
q_{1}=q_{2}
in
\mathbb{Z}/\lambda\mathbb{Z}
,
then, by virtue of (
2.13
)-(
2.14
) and (
2.2
), it is equal to

\left(h_{1},\pi_{\lambda}(x^{\prime})h_{2}\right)_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}h_{1}(u)\overline{\pi_{\lambda}(x^{\prime})h_{2}(u)}du\\
=\int_{\mathbb{R}}h_{1}(u)\overline{h_{2}(u+a^{\prime})}e^{-2\pi i\lambda(c^{\prime}+ub^{\prime})}du,

and in this case, it is independent of
q_{1}=q_{2}
in
\mathbb{Z}/\lambda\mathbb{Z}
.

According to (
2.12
), for

(3.6)

x^{\prime}=\left(\frac{a^{\prime}}{\lambda},0,0\right)\left(0,\frac{b^{\prime}}{\lambda},0\right)=\left(\frac{a^{\prime}}{\lambda},\frac{b^{\prime}}{\lambda},\frac{a^{\prime}b^{\prime}}{\lambda^{2}}\right),\qquad a^{\prime},b^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z},

the formula in
(
3.5
) is equal to

\displaystyle\int_{[0,1]^{2}}{\rm BWZ}_{\lambda,q_{1}}h_{1}(a,b,0)\overline{{\rm BWZ}_{\lambda,q_{2}}h_{2}\left((a,b,0)x^{\prime}\right)}dadb

\displaystyle\quad=\int_{[0,1]^{2}}{\rm BWZ}_{\lambda,q_{1}}h_{1}(a,b,0)\overline{e^{2\pi ib^{\prime}(a+\frac{a^{\prime}}{\lambda})}{\rm BWZ}_{\lambda,q_{2}-b^{\prime}}h_{2}\big((a,b,0)(\frac{a^{\prime}}{\lambda},0,0)\big)}dadb

\displaystyle\quad=\int_{[0,1]^{2}}{\rm BWZ}_{\lambda,q_{1}}h_{1}(a,b,0)\overline{e^{2\pi ib^{\prime}(a+\frac{a^{\prime}}{\lambda})}e^{-2\pi ia^{\prime}b}e^{2\pi i(q_{2}-b^{\prime})\frac{a^{\prime}}{\lambda}}{\rm BWZ}_{\lambda,q_{2}-b^{\prime}}h_{2}(a,b,0)}dadb

\displaystyle\quad=e^{-2\pi iq_{2}\frac{a^{\prime}}{\lambda}}\int_{[0,1]^{2}}{\rm BWZ}_{\lambda,q_{1}}h_{1}(a,b,0)e^{2\pi i(a^{\prime}b-ab^{\prime})}\overline{{\rm BWZ}_{\lambda,q_{2}-b^{\prime}}h_{2}(a,b,0)}dadb.

We have therefore obtained that each integral in the right-hand side of (

\displaystyle\int_{[0,1]^{2}}{\rm BWZ}_{\lambda,q_{1}}h_{1}(a,b,0)e^{2\pi i(a^{\prime}b-ab^{\prime})}\overline{{\rm BWZ}_{\lambda,q_{2}}h_{2}(a,b,0)}dadb

\displaystyle\quad=e^{2\pi i(q_{2}+b^{\prime})\frac{a^{\prime}}{\lambda}}\int_{M}{\rm BWZ}_{\lambda,q_{1}}h_{1}(\dot{x})\overline{{\rm BWZ}_{\lambda,q_{2}+b^{\prime}}h_{2}(\dot{x}x^{\prime})}d\dot{x}

\displaystyle\quad=\begin{cases}0&\text{if}\ q_{1}\neq q_{2}+b^{\prime}\ \text{mod}\ \lambda\\
e^{2\pi iq_{1}\frac{a^{\prime}}{\lambda}}\left(h,\pi_{\lambda}(x^{\prime})h\right)_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

with

as in (

Going back to (

),
we obtain

\displaystyle\bigg\|\sum_{q\in\mathbb{Z}/\lambda\mathbb{Z}}\gamma_{q}{\rm BWZ}_{\lambda,q}h\bigg\|_{L^{4}(M)}^{4}

\displaystyle\quad=\sum_{a^{\prime},b^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}}\bigg|\sum_{q_{1}\in\mathbb{Z}/\lambda\mathbb{Z}}\gamma_{q_{1}}\overline{\gamma_{q_{1}-b^{\prime}}}e^{2\pi iq_{1}\frac{a^{\prime}}{\lambda}}\left(h,\pi_{\lambda}\left(\frac{a^{\prime}}{\lambda},\frac{b^{\prime}}{\lambda},\frac{a^{\prime}b^{\prime}}{\lambda^{2}}\right)h\right)_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\bigg|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad=\sum_{a^{\prime},b^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}}\bigg|\sum_{q_{1}\in\mathbb{Z}/\lambda\mathbb{Z}}\gamma_{q_{1}}\overline{\gamma_{q_{1}-b^{\prime}}}e^{2\pi iq_{1}\frac{a^{\prime}}{\lambda}}\left(h,\pi_{\lambda}\left(\frac{a^{\prime}}{\lambda},\frac{b^{\prime}}{\lambda},0\right)h\right)_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\bigg|^{2},

showing the desired formula.
∎

The key property of

We have already observed that Zygmund’s proof of the restriction theorem on

\displaystyle\|\Pi_{\mu}{\rm{Pr}}_{\lambda}f\|_{L^{4}(M)}

Proof of Proposition

.

Using (

, and we have

\Pi_{\mu}{\rm{Pr}}_{\lambda}f=\sum_{q\in\mathbb{Z}/\lambda\mathbb{Z}}\gamma_{\lambda,q,\ell}h_{\lambda,q,\ell}.

\displaystyle\lesssim 1+\sum_{(a,b)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\setminus\{(0,0)\}}\left(\frac{2\ell+1}{\pi\frac{a^{2}+b^{2}}{|\lambda|}}\right)^{1+\epsilon},

Since

End of the proof of Theorem

We want to show that

This section is devoted to the proof of Proposition

3.4.1.
A few words about the discrete short-time Fourier transform

Recall that the discrete short-time Fourier transform of any two functions
\gamma,\eta:\mathbb{Z}/\lambda\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{C}
is the function
V_{\gamma}\eta:(\mathbb{Z}/\lambda\mathbb{Z})^{2}\to\mathbb{C}
defined via

V_{\gamma}\eta(a,b):=\sum_{q\in\mathbb{Z}/\lambda\mathbb{Z}}\eta(q)\,\overline{\gamma(q-a)}\,e^{-2\pi ibq/\lambda}.

The finite setting considered here is a special case of Gabor analysis on
locally compact abelian groups;
references can be found in the literature for time-frequency analysis such as

[
27
,
37
,
38
]
.

3.4.2.
Construction of a special function

Our proof of the sharpness (Proposition
1.2
) relies on the construction of a special function
\gamma=g
in the following statement:

Lemma 3.6
.

Let

\lambda\in\mathbb{N}
, and let
A\in\mathbb{N}
with
8A<\lambda
.
We consider the indicatrix function
g:=1_{[-A,A]}
of the interval
[-A,A]
as a function on
\mathbb{Z}/\lambda\mathbb{Z}
.
Then we have

\|g\|_{\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}/\lambda\mathbb{Z})}^{2}=2A+1,

and with
\mathcal{L}_{0}(v)=e^{-\frac{v}{2}}
,

\sum_{a,b\in\mathbb{Z}}\left|V_{g}g(a,b)\,\mathcal{L}_{0}\!\left(\pi\frac{a^{2}+b^{2}}{\lambda}\right)\right|^{2}\geq e^{-\pi\frac{A^{2}}{\lambda}}e^{-\pi\frac{\lambda}{A^{2}}}\left(\frac{2A}{\pi}\right)^{2}\left(\lambda+2A+1\right).

Remark 3.7
.

The idea behind Lemma
3.6
is to take advantage of
the concentration of the weight

e^{-\frac{\pi}{2}\frac{a^{2}+b^{2}}{\lambda}}

near the origin in
(\mathbb{Z}/\lambda\mathbb{Z})^{2}
.
Indeed, an important obstruction to making
V_{g}g
small is
the Moyal formula, which reads for any
\gamma,\eta

\sum_{a,b\in\mathbb{Z}/\lambda\mathbb{Z}}|V_{\gamma}\eta(a,b)|^{2}=\lambda\|\gamma\|_{\ell^{2}}^{2}\|\eta\|_{\ell^{2}}^{2}.

The indicatrix

g=1_{[-A,A]}
is a simple way of pushing a substantial part of the mass of
V_{g}g

away from the region where the weight is large: it localises
V_{g}g
in
the translation variable
a\in[-A,A]
, while forcing it to spread in the frequency
variable
b
, on a scale of order
\lambda/A
.

Proof of Lemma
3.6
.

We compute readily
\|g\|_{\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}/\lambda\mathbb{Z})}^{2}=2A+1
and

V_{g}g(a,b)=\sum_{q\in[-A,A]\cap(a+[-A,A])}e^{-2\pi ibq/\lambda},\qquad-\frac{\lambda}{2}\leq a,b\leq\frac{\lambda}{2}.

Since
8A<\lambda
, one can check that

•

if
|a|>2A
,
the interval
[-A,A]\cap(a+[-A,A])
is empty,
so
V_{g}g(a,b)=0
,

•

if
|a|\leq 2A
,
we may view
[-A,A]\cap(a+[-A,A])
as a non-empty interval of
\mathbb{Z}/\lambda\mathbb{Z}
which has length
2A+1-|a|
, so

|V_{g}g(a,b)|=\left|\sum_{j=0}^{2A-|a|}e^{-2\pi ibj/\lambda}\right|=\left|\frac{\sin\frac{\pi b(2A+1-|a|)}{\lambda}}{\sin\frac{\pi b}{\lambda}}\right|

if
0<|b|\leq\frac{\lambda}{2}
, while if
b=0
,

|V_{g}g(a,0)|=2A-|a|+1.

We consider the rectangle

R_{A}:=\left\{(a,b)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\colon|a|\leq A,\ |b|\leq\frac{\lambda}{2(2A+1)}\right\}

as a subset of
(\mathbb{Z}/\lambda\mathbb{Z})^{2}.

Using the well-known bounds

\forall t\in[-\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{2}],\quad|\sin t|\geq\frac{2}{\pi}|t|\qquad\text{and}\qquad\forall t\in\mathbb{R},\quad 0\leq|\sin t|\leq|t|,

we obtain

(3.12)

|V_{g}g(a,b)|\geq\left|\frac{\frac{2}{\pi}\frac{\pi b(2A+1-|a|)}{\lambda}}{\frac{\pi b}{\lambda}}\right|=\frac{2}{\pi}(2A+1-|a|)\geq\frac{2A}{\pi},

for any
a,b\in R_{A}

with
b\neq 0
. This estimate also holds for
b=0
since
|V_{g}g(a,0)|\geq A+1
for
|a|\leq A
.

We observe that for any
(a,b)\in R_{A}
,

\left|\mathcal{L}_{0}\left(\pi\frac{a^{2}+b^{2}}{\lambda}\right)\right|^{2}=e^{-\pi\frac{a^{2}+b^{2}}{\lambda}}\geq e^{-\pi\frac{A^{2}}{\lambda}}{e^{-\pi\frac{\lambda}{A^{2}}}},

consequently,

\displaystyle\sum_{a,b\in\mathbb{Z}}\left|V_{g}g(a,b)\,\mathcal{L}_{0}\!\left(\pi\frac{a^{2}+b^{2}}{\lambda}\right)\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{(a,b)\in R_{A}}\left|V_{g}g(a,b)\,\mathcal{L}_{0}\!\left(\pi\frac{a^{2}+b^{2}}{\lambda}\right)\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\geq e^{-\pi\frac{A^{2}}{\lambda}}{e^{-\pi\frac{\lambda}{A^{2}}}}\sum_{(a,b)\in R_{A}}\left|V_{g}g(a,b)\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\geq e^{-\pi\frac{A^{2}}{\lambda}}{e^{-\pi\frac{\lambda}{A^{2}}}}\left(\frac{2A}{\pi}\right)^{2}|R_{A}|,

by (
3.12
), where
|R_{A}|=(2A+1)(1+\frac{\lambda}{2A+1})
denotes the cardinal of
R_{A}
.
This concludes the proof of the lemma.
∎

3.4.3.
Proof of the sharpness for
\mu^{1/2}
.

Consider
\gamma=g
given in Lemma
3.6
with
A
equal to the integer part of
\sqrt{\lambda}
; hence

for

large enough.
We have
by Theorem
3.3

\displaystyle\|\sum_{q\in\mathbb{Z}/\lambda\mathbb{Z}}\gamma_{q}h_{\lambda,q,0}\|_{L^{4}(M)}^{4}

Appendix A
Hermite and Laguerre functions

A.1.
Hermite functions

This appendix collects some useful facts about Hermite functions. These functions arise naturally as the eigenfunctions of the harmonic oscillator and therefore play a central role in quantum mechanics. We begin by recalling that the Hermite polynomials
H_{\ell}(u)
are defined on the real line by

H_{\ell}(u)=(-1)^{\ell}e^{u^{2}}\left(\frac{d}{du}\right)^{\ell}e^{-u^{2}},\qquad\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

They satisfy the orthogonality relations

\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}H_{\ell}(u)H_{m}(u)e^{-u^{2}}du=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0&\mbox{if}\ \ell\neq m,\\
\sqrt{\pi}2^{\ell}\ell!&\mbox{if}\ \ell=m,\end{array}\right.

and the recurrence relations (with the convention that
H_{\ell}=0
for
\ell<0
)

H_{\ell+1}(u)=2uH_{\ell}(u)-H^{\prime}_{\ell}(u)\qquad\mbox{and}\quad H^{\prime}_{\ell}(u)=2\ell H_{\ell-1}(u).

We define the associated Hermite functions

(A.1)

h_{\ell}(u):=(\sqrt{\pi}2^{\ell}\ell!)^{-\frac{1}{2}}H_{\ell}(u)e^{-\frac{u^{2}}{2}},\qquad\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0},\ u\in\mathbb{R},

which are Schwartz functions on
\mathbb{R}
, and for which the above recurrence relations imply (again with the conventions

h_{\ell}=0
for
\ell<0
)

(A.2)

\left(-\frac{d}{du}+u\right)h_{\ell}=\sqrt{2(\ell+1)}h_{\ell+1}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\left(\frac{d}{du}+u\right)h_{\ell}=\sqrt{2\ell}h_{\ell-1}.

Furthermore, as
h_{0}(u):=(\pi)^{-\frac{1}{4}}e^{-\frac{u^{2}}{2}}
satisfy

(-\frac{d^{2}}{du^{2}}+u^{2})h_{0}(u)=h_{0}(u),

we get from (
A.2
) and an obvious induction that, for all
\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,

(A.3)

(-\frac{d^{2}}{du^{2}}+u^{2})h_{\ell}=(2\ell+1)h_{\ell}.

The Hermite functions are then eigenfunctions of the
harmonic oscillator

H:=(-\frac{d^{2}}{du^{2}}+u^{2})

which can also be written in the form

H=\frac{1}{2}\big(CA+AC\big),

where
\displaystyle C=-\frac{d}{du}+u
and
\displaystyle A=\frac{d}{du}+u
are the so-called creation
and annihilation operators in quantum mechanics.
It is also well-known that
(h_{\ell})_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
is a Hilbert basis of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
and that the spectrum of the harmonic oscillator
is
\{2\ell+1\colon\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\}
, each eigenvalue having multiplicity one; see for instance
[
51
,
55
,
58
]
.

A.2.
Laguerre functions

Laguerre and Hermite functions are involved in the study of the Fourier transform on the Heisenberg group, see for instance
[
22
,
64
]
and the references therein.

Recall that
\mathcal{L}_{\ell}
the Laguerre function of type 0 and degree
\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
is defined by

\mathcal{L}_{\ell}(v):=L_{\ell}(v)e^{-\frac{v}{2}},\quad

where
L_{\ell}
stands for the Laguerre polynomials of type 0 and degree
\ell
, that is

L_{\ell}(v):=\sum^{\ell}_{k=0}(-1)^{k}\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}\ell\\
\ell-k\end{array}\right)\frac{v^{k}}{k!}.

In particular

\mathcal{L}_{0}(v)=e^{-\frac{v}{2}}.

The following is a well-known connection between the Hermite and Laguerre functions
[
32
,
64
]

(A.4)

\mathcal{L}_{\ell}\left(\frac{x^{2}+y^{2}}{2}\right)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{ix\xi}h_{\ell}(\xi+\frac{y}{2})h_{\ell}(\xi-\frac{y}{2})d\xi.

This readily ensures that, for all
\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
v\geq 0
,

(A.5)

|\mathcal{L}_{\ell}(v)|\leq 1,

It will be also useful to recall
[
53
,
62
]
that, for all
\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
v\geq 0
,

(A.6)

v\mathcal{L}_{\ell}(v)=-(\ell+1)\mathcal{L}_{\ell+1}(v)+(2\ell+1)\mathcal{L}_{\ell}(v)-\ell\mathcal{L}_{\ell-1}(v).

Consequently, combining (
A.5
) and (
A.6
), we obtain

(A.7)

|\mathcal{L}_{\ell}(v)|\leq C\frac{2\ell+1}{v},

with a constant
C>0
independent of
v>0
and
\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.

Appendix B
Nilpotent Lie groups and their nilmanifolds

Here we recall some basic definitions and properties of Lie groups, especially nilpotent.
We refer to textbooks such as
[
41
]
for the general theory of Lie group and to
[
17
]
for the particular case of nilpotent Lie groups and their nilmanifolds.

In this paper, all the Lie groups and Lie algebras are over the field of real numbers.

B.1.
Basic facts

B.1.1.
Lie groups

A
Lie group

G
is a smooth manifold equipped with a smooth group structure.
Its
Lie algebra

\mathfrak{g}
is the vector space of left-invariant vector fields equipped with the commutator bracket. It also identifies with the tangent space at the origin (neutral element) equipped with the corresponding Lie bracket. Consequently
\dim G=\dim\mathfrak{g}<\infty
and
for each vector field
X\in\mathfrak{g}
, we can define
\exp X

as the time-1 flow from the origin. This defines the
exponential map

\exp:\mathfrak{g}\to G
which is a local diffeomorphism. At least formally,
it is also given by the Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff formula
in terms of the adjoint representation

{\rm ad}:\mathfrak{g}\longrightarrow\mathscr{L}(\mathfrak{g}),\qquad X\longmapsto{\rm ad}(X)(Y)=[X,Y].

It also allows us to describe the identification between an element of the
\mathfrak{g}
and a left-invariant vector field:

Xf(x)=\partial_{t=0}f(x\exp tX),\quad f\in C^{\infty}(G),\ x\in G,\ X\in\mathfrak{g}.

A Lie algebra or group is said to be
nilpotent

when
{\rm ad}^{N}=0
for some
N\in\mathbb{N}
.
In this paper, we will always assume that a nilpotent Lie group is connected and simply connected.
In particular, the exponential map together with a choice of basis for
\mathfrak{g}
allows us to realise a nilpotent Lie group as the manifold
\mathbb{R}^{n}
equipped with a group law
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
that is a polynomial map.

A choice of basis for
\mathfrak{g}
also leads to a corresponding Lebesgue measure on
\mathfrak{g}
and the Haar measure
dx
on the group
G
,
hence
L^{p}(G)\cong L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.
This also allows us
[
17
]

to define the spaces

C_{c}^{\infty}(G)\cong C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\quad\mbox{and}\quad\mathcal{S}(G)\cong\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})

of test functions which are smooth and compactly supported or Schwartz,
and the corresponding spaces of distributions

\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(G)\cong\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\quad\mbox{and}\quad\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(G)\cong\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{n}).

Note that this identification with
\mathbb{R}^{n}
does not usually extend to the convolution: the group convolution, i.e. the operation between two functions on
G
defined formally via

(f_{1}*f_{2})(x):=\int_{G}f_{1}(y)f_{2}(y^{-1}x)dy,

is not commutative in general whereas it is a commutative operation for functions on the abelian group
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

B.1.2.
Nilmanifolds

A nilmanifold is the quotient

M:=\Gamma\backslash G

of
a nilpotent Lie group
G
by a discrete subgroup
\Gamma
of
G
.
In this paper,
we will always assume
\Gamma
co-compact. This means that
M
is compact.
A concrete example is the natural discrete subgroup of the Heisenberg group as described in the core of the paper.
Abstract examples and characterisations of co-compact discrete subgroup of nilpotent Lie groups may be found in
[
17
]
.

An element of
M
is a class

\dot{x}:=\Gamma x

of an element
x
in
G
.
The quotient
M
is naturally equipped with the structure of a compact smooth manifold, as well as the action of
G
on
M
via

(B.1)

\dot{x}\longmapsto g\cdot\dot{x}=\Gamma xg^{-1}=\dot{x}g^{-1},\qquad g\in G.

We assume that a Haar measure on
G
is fixed.
Then

M
inherits a measure
d\dot{x}
which is invariant under the action of
G
on
M

[
41
]
.
Recall that the Haar measure
dx
on
G
is unique up to a constant and, once it is fixed,
d\dot{x}
is the only
G
-invariant measure on
M
satisfying
for any function
f:G\to\mathbb{C}
, for instance continuous with compact support,

(B.2)

\int_{G}f(x)dx=\int_{M}\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma}f(\gamma x)\ d\dot{x}.

We denote by
{\rm vol}(M)=\int_{M}1d\dot{x}
the volume of
M
.

Recall that a nilpotent Lie group is unimodular in the sense that its Haar measures are invariant under left and right translations.
Hence, the measure
d\dot{x}
is invariant under the action of
G
defined by (
B.1
).

B.2.
The regular representation

The group
G
acts unitarily on
L^{2}(M)
via the right regular representation defined by

R(g)f(\dot{x})=f(\dot{x}g),\quad f\in L^{2}(M),\ g\in G,\ \dot{x}\in M.

It
decomposes into a countable direct sum of representations
\pi
in
{\widehat{G}}
the dual of the group
G
with finite multiplicity
m(\pi)
, see
[
67
]
. Moreover,
the multiplicity
m(\pi)
may in fact be described more precisely, see
[
56
]
.

Example B.1
.

In the case of the Schrödinger representation
\pi_{\lambda}
of the Heisenberg group
\mathbb{H}_{1}
,
the method in
[
56
]
yields

m(\pi_{\lambda})=|\lambda|,

for each
\lambda\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
.

Denoting by
\Gamma\backslash{\widehat{G}}
the set of these representations,
this means that
L^{2}(M)
decomposes into closed
R(G)
-invariant vector subspaces:

(B.3)

L^{2}(M)=\oplus^{\perp}_{\pi\in\Gamma\backslash{\widehat{G}}}L^{2}_{\pi}(M),

and on each
L^{2}_{\pi}(M)
, the representation
R
is unitarily equivalent to
m(\pi)
copies of
\pi
:

L^{2}_{\pi}(M)\sim\mathcal{H}_{\pi}\oplus\ldots\oplus\mathcal{H}_{\pi}=m(\pi)\mathcal{H}_{\pi}.

In the case of the standard Heisenberg nilmanifold, the decomposition in (
B.3
) is described in Section
3.1
.

Note that the regular representation induces the corresponding representations of
L^{1}(G)
and of
\mathfrak{g}
via

\displaystyle R(\kappa)f(\dot{x})

\displaystyle:=\int_{G}\kappa(y)R(y)^{*}f(\dot{x})dy=\int_{G}\kappa(y)f(\dot{x}y^{-1})dy,\qquad\kappa\in L^{1}(G),

\displaystyle R(X)

\displaystyle:=\partial_{t=0}R(\exp tX),\qquad X\in\mathfrak{g}.

The regular representation of
\mathfrak{g}
will be further described in (
B.4
) below.

B.3.
Functions and operators on
G
and
M

We say that a function
f:G\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
is
\Gamma
-left-periodic or just
\Gamma
-periodic when we have

\forall x\in G,\;\;\forall\gamma\in\Gamma,\;\;f(\gamma x)=f(x).

This definition extends readily to measurable functions and to distributions.

There is a natural one-to-one correspondence between the functions on
G
which are
\Gamma
-periodic and the functions on
M
.
Indeed, for any map
F
on
M
,
the corresponding periodic function on
G
is
F_{G}
defined via

F_{G}(x):=F(\dot{x}),\quad x\in G,

while if
f
is a
\Gamma
-periodic function on
G
,
it defines a function
f_{M}
on
M
via

f_{M}(\dot{x})=f(x),\qquad x\in G.

Naturally,
(F_{G})_{M}=F
and
(f_{M})_{G}=f
.

We also define, at least formally, the periodisation
\phi^{\Gamma}
of a function
\phi
by:

\phi^{\Gamma}(x)=\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma}\phi(\gamma x),\qquad x\in G.

If
E
is a space of functions or of distributions on
G
, then we denote by
E^{\Gamma}
the space of elements in
E
which are
\Gamma
-periodic.
Although

C_{c}^{\infty}(G)^{\Gamma}=\{0\}=\mathcal{S}(G)^{\Gamma},

many other periodised functions or functional spaces have interesting descriptions on
M
:

Lemma B.2

(
[
29
]
)
.

(1)

The map
\phi\mapsto\phi^{\Gamma}
yields a surjective morphism of topological vector spaces from

\mathcal{S}(G)
onto
C^{\infty}(G)^{\Gamma}

and from

C_{c}^{\infty}(G)
onto
C^{\infty}(G)^{\Gamma}
.

(2)

The map
F\mapsto F_{G}
yields an isomorphism of topological vector spaces
from
C^{\infty}(M)
onto
C^{\infty}(G)^{\Gamma}
and
from
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(M)
onto
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(G)^{\Gamma}=\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(G)^{\Gamma}
with inverse
f\mapsto f_{M}
.

(3)

For every
p\in[1,\infty]
,
the map
F\mapsto F_{G}
is an isomorphism of the topological vector spaces (in fact Banach spaces) from
L^{p}(M)
onto
L^{p}_{loc}(G)^{\Gamma}
with inverse
f\mapsto f_{M}
.

A mapping
T:\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(G)\to\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(G)

or
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(G)\to\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(G)
is (left-)invariant under an element
g\in G
when
T(f(g\,\cdot))=(Tf)(g\,\cdot)
for all
f\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(G)
(resp.
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(G))
).
It is invariant under a subset of
G
if it is invariant under every element of the subset.
For instance, (right) convolution operators
f\mapsto f*\kappa
with
\kappa\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(G)
are invariant under (left-)translation under
G
.
We will be interested mainly in the following example:

Example B.3
.

Consider a linear continuous mapping
T:\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(G)\to\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(G)

or
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(G)\to\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(G)
respectively
which is invariant under
\Gamma
. Then it naturally induces
a linear continuous mapping

T_{M}:\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(M)\to\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(M)
on
M
given via

T_{M}F=(TF_{G})_{M},\qquad F\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(M).

Consequently,
if
T
coincides with a smooth differential operator on
G
that is invariant under
\Gamma
, then
T_{M}
is a smooth differential operator on
M
.

In particular, any left-invariant vector field
X\in\mathfrak{g}
on
M
yields a corresponding vector field
X_{M}
on
G
and we have

X_{M}F(\dot{x})=(XF_{G})(x)=\partial_{t=0}F_{G}(x\exp tX)=\partial_{t=0}R(\exp tX)F\,(\dot{x}).

In other words,

(B.4)

R(X)=X_{M}.

B.4.
Construction of functions in
L^{2}(M)
via representations

A standard method to produce functions in
L^{2}(M)

is the following construction
[
35
]
:

Lemma B.4
.

Let
\pi
be a strongly continuous unitary representation on a separable Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{\pi}
of a finite dimensional real Lie group

G
.
For any smooth vector
h\in\mathcal{H}_{\pi}^{+\infty}
and any distributional vector
\nu\in\mathcal{H}_{\pi}^{-\infty}
, the function defined via

\phi_{\pi,\nu,h}(x):=(\nu,\pi(x)h)_{\mathcal{H}_{\pi}^{-\infty}\times\mathcal{H}_{\pi}^{+\infty}},\qquad x\in G,

is smooth, that is,
\phi_{\pi,\nu,h}\in C^{\infty}(G)
.
Moreover, if
\nu
is invariant under
\pi(g_{0})
for some
g_{0}\in G
, i.e.
\pi(g_{0})\nu=\nu
, then
\phi_{\pi,\nu,h}
is invariant under
g_{0}
,

\mbox{i.e.}\qquad\forall x\in G\qquad\phi_{\pi,\nu,h}(g_{0}x)=\phi_{\pi,\nu,h}(x).

Applying Lemma
B.4
to the case of a nilmanifold, we obtain readily:

Corollary B.5
.

Let
M=\Gamma\backslash G
be a nilmanifold.
Let
\pi
be a strongly continuous unitary representation of
G
on a separable Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{\pi}
.

If
\nu\in\mathcal{H}_{\pi}^{-\infty}
is invariant under
\pi(\gamma)
,
\gamma\in\Gamma
, then

(\phi_{\pi,\nu,h})_{M}\in C^{\infty}(M)
, for any
h\in\mathcal{H}_{\pi}^{+\infty}
.

Appendix C
Carnot groups and nilmanifolds

C.1.
Carnot groups

A
stratified
Lie group
G
is a connected and simply connected
Lie group
whose Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}

admits an
\mathbb{N}
-gradation

\mathfrak{g}=\oplus_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\mathfrak{g}_{\ell}

where the
\mathfrak{g}_{\ell}
,
\ell=1,2,\ldots
,
are vector subspaces of
\mathfrak{g}

and satisfying

[\mathfrak{g}_{1},\mathfrak{g}_{\ell}]=\mathfrak{g}_{1+\ell}

for any
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
.
This implies that the group
G
is nilpotent
and that
\mathfrak{g}_{\ell}=\{0\}
for all
\ell>s
for some
s\in\mathbb{N}
. The smallest
s
is called the step of nilpotency, and
\mathfrak{g}_{\ell}\neq\{0\}
for all
\ell\leq s
.
When a basis
\{X_{1},\ldots X_{n_{1}}\}
of
\mathfrak{g}_{1}
is fixed, the group is said to be
Carnot
.
They are the model of sub-Riemannian manifolds
[
1
]
.
Examples of such groups are the Heisenberg group.

The structure of Carnot groups allows us to define the associated sub-Laplacian

(C.1)

\mathcal{L}=-(X_{1}^{2}+\ldots+X_{n_{1}}^{2}),

as well as the homogeneous structure defined below.
The sub-Laplacian
\mathcal{L}
is a smooth differential operator on
M
that is essentially self-adjoint on
\mathcal{S}(G)\subset L^{2}(G)

[
31
]
.
We keep the same notation for their self-adjoint extension.

Let us now discuss the homogeneous structure of Carnot groups.
For any
r>0
,
we define the linear mapping
D_{r}:\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{g}
by

D_{r}X=r^{\ell}X
for every
X\in\mathfrak{g}_{\ell}
,
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
.
Then the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
is endowed
with the family of dilations
\{D_{r},r>0\}

and becomes a homogeneous Lie algebra in the sense of

[
33
]
.
The associated group dilations
still denoted by
D_{r}
and obtained via the exponential mapping
leads in a canonical way to the notions of homogeneity for functions, distributions and operators.
As examples,
the sub-Laplacian
\mathcal{L}
is homogeneous of degree 2 while
the Haar measure is
Q
-homogeneous, where

Q:=\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}}\ell\dim\mathfrak{g}_{\ell},

is called the homogeneous dimension of
G
.

C.2.
Sub-elliptic Bernstein inequalities

A nilmanifold
M=\Gamma\backslash G
where
G
is Carnot is also called Carnot.
The sub-Laplacian
\mathcal{L}
on
G
defined in (
C.1
) is invariant under left-translation. As seen in Section
B.2
, it induces
the sub-Laplacian on
M

\mathcal{L}_{M}=-(X_{1,M}^{2}+\ldots+X_{n_{1},M}^{2}).

This is a smooth differential operator which is essentially self-adjoint on
C^{\infty}(M)\subset L^{2}(M)

[
29
]
;
we will keep the same notation for
\mathcal{L}_{M}
and its self-adjoint extension.
The spectrum of
\mathcal{L}_{M}
is a discrete and unbounded subset of
[0,+\infty)
,
each eigenspace of
\mathcal{L}_{M}
has finite dimension and
the constant functions on
M
form the 0-eigenspace of
\mathcal{L}_{M}

[
29
]
.
A consequence of the semiclassical analysis in
[
29
]
(see also
[
28
]
) is the following statement:

Proposition C.1

(
[
29
]
)
.

Let
\psi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
.

(1)

The integral kernel of
\psi(\mathcal{L}_{M})
is the smooth function
K_{\psi}\in C^{\infty}(M\times M)

given by

K_{\psi}(\dot{g},\dot{h})=\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma}\kappa_{\psi}(g^{-1}\gamma h),\quad g,h\in G.

(2)

Set
\psi_{\varepsilon}:=\psi(\varepsilon^{2}\cdot)
.
We have:

K_{\psi_{\varepsilon}}(\dot{g},\dot{g})=\varepsilon^{-Q}\kappa_{\psi}(0)+O(\varepsilon)^{\infty},

in the sense that

\forall N\in\mathbb{N},\quad\exists C>0,\quad\forall\dot{g}\in M,\,\forall\varepsilon\in(0,1],\quad|K_{\psi_{\varepsilon}}(\dot{g},\dot{g})-\varepsilon^{-Q}\kappa_{\psi}(0)|\leq C\varepsilon^{N}.

Proposition
C.1
readily implies:

Corollary C.2
.

For any
p\in[2,\infty]
and any
\psi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
, there exists
C=C_{p,\psi}>0
such that the following estimate holds for any
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
,

\|\psi(\varepsilon^{2}\mathcal{L}_{M})\|_{\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(M),L^{p}(M))}\leq C\varepsilon^{Q(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{2})}.

Before giving the proof of Corollary
C.2
, let us explain why its statement can be interpreted as the analogous of Bernstein inequality in the Euclidean framework (for a brief review on this topic, one can consult
[
3
]
and the references therein).

We fix an orthonormal basis
e_{\ell}
,
\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, of
L^{2}(M)
such that:

\mathcal{L}_{M}e_{\ell}=\lambda_{\ell}^{2}e_{\ell}.

Then any function
f\in L^{2}(M)
may be written as a Hilbertian series

(C.2)

f=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}(f,e_{\ell})_{L^{2}(M)}e_{\ell}\quad\mbox{in}\ L^{2}(M).

We say that
f
is spectrally supported in
I\subset\mathbb{R}
when the sum above is over
\ell
such that
\lambda_{\ell}\in I
;
this is independent of the chosen basis
(e_{\ell})
.
In particular, a function
f\in L^{2}(M)
is said to be spectrally supported in
[0,\Lambda]
,
\Lambda>0
, if and only if, for all
\phi\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
identically equal to
1
in
[0,1]
, we have

(C.3)

f=\phi(\Lambda^{-2}\mathcal{L}_{M})f.

In this case, applying Corollary
C.2
with
\varepsilon=\Lambda^{-1}
and
\phi=\psi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
such that
\psi=1
on
[0,1]
and
\psi=0
outside
[-1,2]
,
we have
\psi(\Lambda^{-2}\mathcal{L}_{M})f=f
so

\|f\|_{L^{p}(M)}\leq C\Lambda^{Q(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p})}\|f\|_{L^{2}(M)}.

Proof of Corollary
C.2
.

For any bounded function
\psi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
,
the operator
\psi(\mathcal{L}_{M})
applied to
f\in L^{2}(M)
decomposed in the Hilbertian series in (
C.2
) as

\psi(\mathcal{L}_{M})f=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\psi(\lambda_{\ell}^{2})(f,e_{\ell})_{L^{2}(M)}e_{\ell}.

We deduce that
\psi(\mathcal{L}_{M})
is bounded on
L^{2}(M)
with

(C.4)

\|\psi(\mathcal{L}_{M})\|_{\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(M))}\leq\sup_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}}|\psi(\lambda^{2})|,

and that
its integral kernel
K_{\psi}
, that is, the distribution defined via

(C.5)

\psi(\mathcal{L}_{M})f(\dot{x})=\int_{M}K_{\psi}(\dot{x},\dot{y})f(\dot{y})d\dot{y},\quad f\in C^{\infty}(M),

by Proposition
C.1
(1),
is formally given by

(C.6)

K_{\psi}(\dot{x},\dot{y})=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\psi(\lambda_{\ell}^{2})\overline{e_{\ell}(\dot{x})}e_{\ell}(\dot{y}).

Applying the Cauchy-Schwartz inequality in (
C.5
) implies readily

\|\psi_{\varepsilon}(\mathcal{L}_{M})\|_{\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(M),L^{\infty}(M))}\leq\sup_{\dot{x}\in M}\|K_{\psi_{\varepsilon}}(\dot{x},\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(M)}.

Now by (
C.6
), we see

\displaystyle\|K_{\psi_{\varepsilon}}(\dot{x},\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(M)}^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{M}\sum_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}}\psi_{\varepsilon}(\lambda_{\ell}^{2})\overline{\psi_{\varepsilon}(\lambda_{\ell^{\prime}}^{2})}\ \overline{e_{\ell}(\dot{y})}e_{\ell}(\dot{x})\ e_{\ell^{\prime}}(\dot{y})\overline{e_{\ell^{\prime}}(\dot{x})}d\dot{y}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell}|\psi_{\varepsilon}|^{2}(\lambda_{\ell}^{2})e_{\ell}(\dot{x})\overline{e_{\ell}(\dot{x})},

since

\int_{M}\overline{e_{\ell}(\dot{y})}e_{\ell^{\prime}}(\dot{y})d\dot{y}=\delta_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}}
by the Plancherel formula.
We recognise

\|K_{\psi_{\varepsilon}}(\dot{x},\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(M)}^{2}=|K_{|\psi_{\varepsilon}|^{2}}(\dot{x},\dot{x})|.

Hence,

\|\psi_{\varepsilon}(\mathcal{L}_{M})\|_{\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(M),L^{\infty}(M))}\leq\sqrt{\sup_{\dot{x}\in M}|K_{|\psi_{\varepsilon}|^{2}}(\dot{x},\dot{x})|}.

We deduce that

\displaystyle\|\psi_{\varepsilon}(\mathcal{L}_{M})\|_{\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(M),L^{\infty}(M))}

\displaystyle\leq C\varepsilon^{-\frac{Q}{2}},

by Proposition
C.1
(2)
for some positive constant
C
.
This shows the case
p=+\infty
.
The case
p=2
follows from (
C.4
) applied to
\psi_{\varepsilon}
, with
C=\sup_{\mathbb{R}}|\psi|=\sup_{\mathbb{R}}|\psi_{\varepsilon}|
.
By interpolation, we obtain the case of any
p\in(2,\infty)
.
∎

The Bernstein inequality now follows readily:

Theorem C.3

(Sub-elliptic Bernstein Inequality)
.

Let
M=\Gamma\backslash G
be a compact nilmanifold on a Carnot group
G
. For any
p\in[2,\infty]

there exists a constant
C>0
such that the following estimate holds for any
\mu>1
:

\|\Pi_{\mu,\sqrt{\mathcal{L}_{M}}}\|_{\mathscr{L}(L^{p}(M),L^{2}(M))}\leq C\mu^{Q(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p})}.

Proof.

We have

\Pi_{\mu,\sqrt{\mathcal{L}_{M}}}f=\sum_{\ell:\lambda_{\ell}=\mu}(f,e_{\ell})_{L^{2}(M)}e_{\ell},\qquad\mbox{so}\qquad\|\Pi_{\mu,\sqrt{\mathcal{L}_{M}}}f\|_{L^{2}(M)}=^{2}\sum_{\ell:\lambda_{\ell}=\mu}|(f,e_{\ell})_{L^{2}(M)}|^{2},

by the Plancherel formula,
and therefore

\|\Pi_{\mu,\sqrt{\mathcal{L}_{M}}}f\|_{L^{2}(M)}^{2}\leq\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}|\psi|^{2}(\mu^{-2}\lambda_{\ell}^{2})|(f,e_{\ell})_{L^{2}(M)}|^{2}=\|\psi(\mu^{-2}\mathcal{L})f\|_{L^{2}(M)}^{2},

for a function
\psi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
chosen such that
\psi=1
on
[0,1]
and
\psi=0
outside
[-1,2]
. We conclude with applying Corollary
C.2
to
\varepsilon=\mu^{-1}
.
∎

Remark C.4
.

Applying Theorem
C.3
to the standard nilmanifold of the three dimensional Heisenberg group with

and

shows Proposition
1.3
.

Appendix D
End of the proof of Theorem
2.1

Here, we continue the proof of Theorem
2.1
. This leads naturally to the definition of our BWZ transform.
At this point in the proof,
we look for an explicit decomposition of
L^{2}_{\lambda}(M)
when
\lambda\neq 0
.
By harmonic analysis, we already know that such an abstract decomposition exists: indeed,
by Lemma
2.3
and the Stone-Von Neumann theorem
[
32
]
, when
\lambda\neq 0
,
R
decomposes as copies of the Schrödinger representation
\pi_{\lambda}
of
\mathbb{H}_{1}
, that is, the
unique (up to unitary equivalence) unitary irreducible representation of
\mathbb{H}_{1}
satisfying on the centre
\pi_{\lambda}(0,0,c)=e^{2\pi i\lambda c}\text{\rm I}
.
Here,
we will obtain this decomposition concretely.
Our strategy consists in applying the construction explained in Corollary
B.5
to the Schrödinger representations

\pi=\pi_{\lambda}
introduced in Section
2.3
.
This requires first to determine its distributional vectors invariant under
\pi_{\lambda}(\gamma)
,
\gamma\in\Gamma
, which we now do.

D.1.
Further properties of
\pi_{\lambda}

The Schrödinger representation
\pi_{\lambda}
acts unitarily on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
.
Its space of smooth vectors is the Schwartz space
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
,
while its space of distributional vectors is the space
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})
of tempered distributions.
The action of the Schrödinger representations is continuous on the Fréchet space
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
and extends continuously to
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})
. We say that a distribution
\nu\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})
is invariant under
\pi_{\lambda}(g)
for some
g\in\mathbb{H}_{1}
when
\pi_{\lambda}(g)\nu=\nu
, meaning that, for all
h\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
,

(\pi_{\lambda}(g)\nu,h)_{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})\times\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})}=(\nu,h)_{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})\times\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})}\,.

The description of the distributions invariant under

\pi_{\lambda}(\gamma)
for all
\gamma\in\Gamma
is well-known with different conventions, and we also provide a proof below:

Proposition D.1

(
[
47
,
65
]
)
.

For each
\lambda\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
and
q\in\mathbb{Z}

the tempered distribution on
\mathbb{R}

(D.1)

\nu_{\lambda,q}:=\lambda^{-1}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}e^{-2\pi iq\frac{k}{\lambda}}\delta_{\frac{k}{\lambda}},

or alternatively defined via

(\nu_{\lambda,q},h)_{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})\times\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})}=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\widehat{h}(q+\lambda k),\qquad h\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}),

is invariant under
\pi_{\lambda}(\gamma)
,
\gamma\in\Gamma
.
These distributions are constant over
\mathbb{Z}/\lambda\mathbb{Z}
-classes in
q
in the sense that

\forall\lambda\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\},\ q\in\mathbb{Z},\ k_{1}\in\mathbb{Z},\qquad\nu_{\lambda,k_{1}\lambda+q}=\nu_{\lambda,q}.

They also satisfy for any
\lambda\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
,
q,q_{1}\in\mathbb{Z}
,

\pi_{\lambda}\left(\frac{q_{1}}{\lambda},0,0\right)\nu_{\lambda,q}=e^{2\pi iq\frac{q_{1}}{\lambda}}\nu_{\lambda,q}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\pi_{\lambda}\left(0,\frac{q_{1}}{\lambda},0\right)\nu_{\lambda,q}(u)=e^{2\pi i\lambda u\frac{q_{1}}{\lambda}}\nu_{\lambda,q}(u).

Moreover, they span the space
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})^{\pi_{\lambda}(\Gamma)}
of tempered distributions on
\mathbb{R}
invariant under
\pi_{\lambda}(\gamma)
, for
\gamma\in\Gamma
, and we have

\dim\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})^{\pi_{\lambda}(\Gamma)}=|\lambda|.

Proof of Proposition
D.1
.

We check readily that each
\nu_{\lambda,q}
is a
tempered distribution invariant under
\pi_{\lambda}(\gamma)
, for
\gamma\in\Gamma
,
that it satisfies
\nu_{\lambda,k_{1}\lambda+q}=\nu_{\lambda,q}
,
and

\displaystyle\pi_{\lambda}\left(\frac{q_{1}}{\lambda},0,0\right)\nu_{\lambda,q}(u)

\displaystyle=\lambda^{-1}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}e^{-2\pi iq\frac{k}{\lambda}}\delta_{\frac{k}{\lambda}}\left(u+\frac{q_{1}}{\lambda}\right)=\lambda^{-1}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}e^{-2\pi iq\frac{k}{\lambda}}\delta_{\frac{k+q_{1}}{\lambda}}\left(u\right)

\displaystyle=\lambda^{-1}\sum_{k_{1}\in\mathbb{Z}}e^{-2\pi iq\frac{k_{1}-q_{1}}{\lambda}}\delta_{\frac{k_{1}}{\lambda}}\left(u\right)=e^{2\pi iq\frac{q_{1}}{\lambda}}\nu_{\lambda,q}(u).

We also have

\displaystyle\pi_{\lambda}\left(0,\frac{q_{1}}{\lambda},0\right)\nu_{\lambda,q}(u)

\displaystyle=\lambda^{-1}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}e^{-2\pi iq\frac{k}{\lambda}}\pi_{\lambda}\left(0,\frac{q_{1}}{\lambda},0\right)\delta_{\frac{k}{\lambda}}

\displaystyle=\lambda^{-1}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}e^{-2\pi iq\frac{k}{\lambda}}e^{2\pi i\lambda u\frac{q_{1}}{\lambda}}\delta_{\frac{k}{\lambda}}=e^{2\pi i\lambda u\frac{q_{1}}{\lambda}}\nu_{\lambda,q}(u).

We now prove that these distributions span

\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})^{\pi_{\lambda}(\Gamma)}
.

Let
\nu\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})
be invariant under
\pi_{\lambda}(\gamma)
,
\gamma\in\Gamma
.
As
\nu
is invariant under
\pi_{\lambda}(a,0,0)
,
a\in\mathbb{Z}
,
it is
\mathbb{Z}
-periodic distribution. We write its Fourier series expansion as

(\nu,h)_{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})\times\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})}=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}c_{k}\widehat{h}(k),\qquad h\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}),

for some unique coefficients
c_{k}\in\mathbb{C}
depending only on
\nu
.
Above,
\widehat{h}=\mathcal{F}_{\mathbb{R}}h
is the Euclidean Fourier transform of
h
:

\mathcal{F}_{\mathbb{R}}h(\xi)=\widehat{h}(\xi)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-2\pi i\xi u}h(u)du.

As
\nu
is invariant under
\pi_{\lambda}(0,b,0)
,
b\in\mathbb{Z}
,
it satisfies

\nu(u)=e^{i2\pi\lambda ub}\nu(u)\quad\mbox{in}\ \mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}),\ \mbox{for any}\ b\in\mathbb{Z}.

The left-hand side means

(\nu,h)_{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})\times\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})}=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}c_{k}\widehat{h}(k),\quad\quad\mbox{for any}\ h\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}),\ b\in\mathbb{Z},

while the right-hand side means for any
h\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
and any
b\in\mathbb{Z}

\displaystyle(\nu,e^{i2\pi\lambda b\cdot}h)_{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})\times\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}c_{k}\mathcal{F}_{\mathbb{R}}(e^{i2\pi\lambda b\cdot}h)(k)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}c_{k}\widehat{h}(k-\lambda b)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}c_{k+\lambda b}\widehat{h}(k).

Consequently,

c_{k+\lambda b}=c_{k}
for any
b,k\in\mathbb{Z}
so

\displaystyle(\nu,h)_{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})\times\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{0\leq q<|\lambda|}\sum_{k_{1}\in\mathbb{Z}}c_{q+\lambda k_{1}}\widehat{h}(q+\lambda k_{1})=\sum_{0\leq q<|\lambda|}c_{q}\sum_{k_{1}\in\mathbb{Z}}\widehat{h}(q+\lambda k_{1})

\displaystyle=\sum_{0\leq q<|\lambda|}c_{q}(\nu_{\lambda,q},h)_{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})\times\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})},

where
\nu_{\lambda,q}\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})
is defined via:

\displaystyle(\nu_{\lambda,q},h)_{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})\times\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k_{1}\in\mathbb{Z}}\widehat{h}(q+\lambda k_{1})=\sum_{k_{1}\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathcal{F}_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\frac{e^{-2\pi iq\frac{\cdot}{\lambda}}}{\lambda}h(\frac{\cdot}{\lambda})\right)(k_{1})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{e^{-2\pi iq\frac{k_{2}}{\lambda}}}{\lambda}h\left(\frac{k_{2}}{\lambda}\right)

by the Poisson summation formula.
We have obtained

\nu=\sum_{0\leq q<|\lambda|}c_{q}\nu_{\lambda,q}
.
This concludes the proof of the proposition.
∎

D.2.
The Berezin-Weil-Zak transform

For each
\lambda\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
,

\nu\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})^{\pi_{\lambda}(\Gamma)}

and
h\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
,
we consider the functions constructed in Corollary
B.5

(\phi_{\pi_{\lambda},\nu,h})_{M}=f_{\lambda,\nu,h}\in C^{\infty}(M),\qquad\lambda\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\},\ \nu\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})^{\pi_{\lambda}(\Gamma)},\ h\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}),

namely, for all
\dot{x}\in M
,

f_{\lambda,\nu,h}(\dot{x})=(\nu,\pi_{\lambda}(x)h)_{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})\times\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})}.

Lemma D.2
.

Let
\lambda\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
,
\nu\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})^{\pi_{\lambda}(\Gamma)}
and
h\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
.
Then
f_{\lambda,\nu,h}
is in
C^{\infty}(M)\cap L^{2}_{\lambda}(M)
and satisfies the following properties:

(1)

We have, for any
g_{0}\in\mathbb{H}_{1}
,

R({g_{0}})f_{\lambda,\nu,h}=f_{\lambda,\nu,\pi_{\lambda}(g_{0})h},\quad\mbox{i.e.}\quad\forall\dot{x}\in M,\quad f_{\lambda,\nu,h}(\dot{x}g_{0})=f_{\lambda,\nu,\pi_{\lambda}(g_{0})h}(\dot{x}).

(2)

In the case where
g_{0}=\left(a_{0},0,0\right)
with
a_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
, we have

f_{\lambda,\nu,h}\left(\dot{x}\left(a_{0},0,0\right)\right)=e^{-2\pi i\lambda a_{0}b}f_{\lambda,\pi_{\lambda}(a_{0},0,0)\nu,h}(\dot{x}),\qquad\Gamma(a,b,c)=\dot{x}\in M.

Proof of Lemma
D.2
.

By construction
f_{\lambda,\nu,h}\in C^{\infty}(M)
and
we can easily check that

\phi_{\pi_{\lambda},\nu,h}(a,b,c)=e^{2\pi i\lambda c}(\nu,\pi_{\lambda}(a,b,0)h)_{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})\times\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})}=e^{2\pi i\lambda c}\phi_{\pi_{\lambda},\nu,h}(a,b,0),

which in view of (
2.17
) shows that
f_{\lambda,\nu,h}\in L^{2}_{\lambda}(M)
.
The properties in (1) follow readily by construction.
For (2), we observe with
x=(a,b,c)\in\mathbb{H}_{1}

(a_{0},0,0)^{-1}x(a_{0},0,0)=(a,b,c)(0,0,-a_{0}b),

so

\displaystyle f_{\lambda,\nu,h}\left(\dot{x}\left(a_{0},0,0\right)\right)

\displaystyle=e^{-2\pi i\lambda a_{0}b}(\nu,\pi_{\lambda}(a_{0},0,0)\pi_{\lambda}(x)h)_{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})\times\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})}

\displaystyle=e^{-2\pi i\lambda a_{0}b}(\pi_{\lambda}(a_{0},0,0)\nu,\pi_{\lambda}(x)h)_{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})\times\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})}=e^{-2\pi i\lambda a_{0}b}\displaystyle f_{\lambda,\pi_{\lambda}(a_{0},0,0)\nu,h}(\dot{x}).

∎

We define the generalised Berezin-Weil-Zak transform, in short BWZ, of a function
h\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
with parameter
\lambda\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
and
q\in\mathbb{Z}
as

{\rm BWZ}_{\lambda,q}(h):=c_{\lambda,q}f_{\lambda,\nu_{\lambda,q},h},

that is,

(D.2)

{\rm BWZ}_{\lambda,q}(h)(\dot{x})=c_{\lambda,q}f_{\lambda,\nu_{\lambda,q},h}(\dot{x})=c_{\lambda,q}(\nu_{\lambda,q},\pi_{\lambda}(x)h)_{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})\times\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})},\quad x=(a,b,c)\in\mathbb{H}_{1}.

Above
c_{\lambda,q}
is the normalising constant:

c_{\lambda,q}:=\sqrt{|\lambda|}.

Expanding (
D.2
) using Proposition
D.1
,
we obtain the expression (
2.11
) given in Theorem
2.1
.

D.3.
End of the proof of Theorem
2.1

We have already proved Part (1).
Part (2) (a) and (c) follow from Lemma
D.2
and Proposition
D.1
.
In order to prove Part (2) (b), it suffices to show (
2.13
).
As
{\rm BWZ}_{\lambda_{i},q_{i}}(h_{i})\in L^{2}_{\lambda_{i}}(M)
,
i=1,2
, and
L^{2}_{\lambda_{1}}(M)\perp L^{2}_{\lambda_{2}}(M)
for
\lambda_{1}\neq\lambda_{2}
, we have

({\rm BWZ}_{\lambda_{1},q_{1}}(h_{1}),{\rm BWZ}_{\lambda_{2},q_{2}}(h_{2}))_{L^{2}(M)}=0,

when
\lambda_{1}\neq\lambda_{2}
.
This can also be checked more directly with

I:=({\rm BWZ}_{\lambda_{1},q_{1}}(h_{1}),{\rm BWZ}_{\lambda_{2},q_{2}}(h_{2}))_{L^{2}(M)}=\int_{c=0}^{1}e^{2\pi i(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2})c}\ldots dc,

which will be zero when
\lambda_{1}\neq\lambda_{2}
.
Hence, we may assume
\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{2}=\lambda
.
Making the change of variable
\dot{x}=\dot{x}_{1}\left(\frac{q}{\lambda},0,0\right)
, we obtain by Part (2) (c) for any
q\in\mathbb{Z}
:

\displaystyle I

\displaystyle=\int_{M}{\rm BWZ}_{\lambda,q_{1}}(h_{1})\left(\dot{x}_{1}\left(\frac{q}{\lambda},0,0\right)\right)\overline{{\rm BWZ}_{\lambda,q_{2}}(h_{2})\left(\dot{x}_{1}\left(\frac{q}{\lambda},0,0\right)\right)}d\dot{x}_{1}

\displaystyle=e^{2\pi i(q_{1}-q_{2})\frac{q}{\lambda}}I.

Hence,
I=0
when
q_{1}\neq q_{2}
, and we may assume that
q_{1}=q_{2}=q
.
We are left with computing

I=|c_{\lambda,q}|^{2}\lambda^{-2}\sum_{k_{1},k_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}}I_{k_{1},k_{2}},

where

I_{k_{1},k_{2}}:=e^{-i2\pi\frac{q}{\lambda}(k_{1}-k_{2})}\iint_{a,b=0}^{1}e^{i2\pi b(k_{1}-k_{2})}h_{1}\Big(\frac{k_{1}}{\lambda}+a\Big)\bar{h}_{2}\Big(\frac{k_{2}}{\lambda}+a\Big)dadb.

By Fourier analysis, the integral over
b
is zero for
k_{1}\neq k_{2}
.
For
k=k_{1}=k_{2}
, we are left with

\displaystyle I_{k,k}

\displaystyle=\int_{a=0}^{1}(h_{1}\bar{h}_{2})\left(\frac{k}{\lambda}+a\right)da=\int_{\frac{k}{\lambda}}^{\frac{k}{\lambda}+1}(h_{1}\bar{h}_{2})(a^{\prime})da^{\prime},

after the change of variable
a^{\prime}=\frac{k}{\lambda}+a
.
Hence, we obtain

\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}I_{k,k}=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\int_{\frac{k}{\lambda}}^{\frac{k}{\lambda}+1}(h_{1}\bar{h}_{2})(a^{\prime})da^{\prime}=\sum_{k_{1}\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum_{0\leq r<|\lambda|}\int_{\frac{r+|\lambda|k_{1}}{\lambda}}^{\frac{r+|\lambda|k_{1}}{\lambda}+1}(h_{1}\bar{h}_{2})(a^{\prime})da^{\prime},

thanks to the Euclidean division
k=|\lambda|k_{1}+r
by
\lambda
.
Swapping the sums over
k_{1}
and
r
, we obtain

\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}I_{k,k}=|\lambda|\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}(h_{1}\bar{h}_{2})(a^{\prime})da^{\prime}.

This proves (
2.13
) and explains the choice of the normalising
constant
c_{\lambda,q}
, since

|c_{\lambda,q}|^{2}\lambda^{-2}|\lambda|=1.

This proves Part (2).

We have therefore shown the inclusion

\oplus_{q\in\mathbb{Z}/\lambda\mathbb{Z}}^{\perp}L^{2}_{\lambda,q}\subseteq L^{2}_{\lambda}(M).

To see that the equality holds, it is enough to use the fact that

L^{2}_{\lambda}(M)
contains exactly
|\lambda|
copies of
\pi_{\lambda}
,
see Section
B.2
in particular Example
B.1
.
This concludes the proof of
Theorem
2.1
.
