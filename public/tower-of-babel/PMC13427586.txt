Green ultrasound‑assisted extraction of bioactive polyphenols from Inonotus hispidus: Sustainable process with enhanced yield and antioxidant activity☆
Authors: Huimin Huo, Haiying Bao, Tianrui Liu, Mingjie Song, Hong Kan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Ultrasound-assisted extraction (UAE) has emerged as a green and efficient alternative to conventional extraction methods, primarily due to the acoustic cavitation phenomena that minimize solvent consumption and processing time. This study systematically optimized a UAE process for maximized recovery of total polyphenols from Inonotus hispidus. UAE was conducted using a KS-700XDS power-adjustable ultrasonic bath at 50 kHz. In preliminary comparisons, UAE demonstrated more efficacy, affording a total polyphenol content (TPC) of 18.74 mg/g, which exceeded the yields of heat reflux extraction and stirring-assisted extraction by 8.70 % and 14.62 %, respectively. Response surface methodology was subsequently employed to determine the optimal extraction parameters, which were established as an extraction time of 95 min, an ethanol concentration of 60 %, an ultrasonic power of 150 W, and a temperature of 65 ℃. Under these optimized conditions, the experimental TPC reached 25.46 mg/g, representing a 35.86 % increase over the pre-optimized value. A greenness evaluation using the AGREE metric yielded a score of 0.76 for UAE, confirming its environmental sustainability. Antioxidant assays demonstrated that the optimized extract exhibited significantly enhanced radical-scavenging activities and ferric reducing power compared to the unoptimized extract. These findings highlight the critical role of ultrasonic-controlled parameters in maximizing polyphenol yields and validate the proposed method as a highly efficient and sustainable approach for fungal material valorization.

Introduction

Medicinal fungi represent a prolific and largely untapped reservoir of bioactive secondary metabolites with diverse therapeutic potentials, including anticancer, antioxidant, and immunomodulatory activities [1], [2]. Among them, Inonotus hispidus, has been traditionally utilized for its health-promoting properties, and contemporary investigations have identified polyphenols as its predominant bioactive constituents [3], [4]. These phenolic compounds exhibit a broad spectrum of biological effects, notably displaying potent radical-scavenging, anti-lipid peroxidation, and DNA-damage protective capacities, alongside marked antiproliferative activity against malignant cell lines [5]. Despite these promising attributes, research on the efficient recovery of polyphenols from I. hispidus remains surprisingly limited. Machado‑Carvalho et al. (2023) employed ultrasound‑assisted extraction to maximize total polyphenol content and antioxidant activity. However, their optimized total phenolic yield was only 219.01 µg/g dry weight, while demonstrating feasibility, remains insufficient for practical industrial or pharmaceutical applications [6]. Critically, this study systematically investigated the role of ultrasonic power as an independent variable across a broad range, nor did they provide a comprehensive post‑optimization evaluation integrating extraction efficiency, antioxidant activity, and environmental sustainability through greenness metrics such as the AGREE score. These unresolved gaps collectively constrain the practical valorization of I. hispidus polyphenols.

Conventional solid–liquid extraction techniques remain widely employed for the recovery of bioactive polyphenols from natural matrices due to their operational simplicity; however, these methods suffer from several critical and interrelated limitations that fundamentally compromise their efficiency, sustainability, and product quality, including prolonged extraction times, substantial consumption of organic solvents with associated waste disposal concerns, elevated temperatures that inevitably lead to thermal degradation of thermolabile polyphenolic compounds through ionization, hydrolysis, and oxidation reactions, poor mass transfer efficiency that results in incomplete recovery and low yields despite exhaustive processing [7], [8]. Alternative technologies such as supercritical fluid extraction (SFE) and pressurized liquid extraction (PLE) offer improvements in solvent reduction and selectivity, yet their widespread adoption remains constrained by high capital costs, sophisticated instrumentation, lengthy extraction cycles, and operational complexities. In contrast, microwave‑assisted extraction (MAE) and particularly ultrasound‑assisted extraction (UAE) have emerged as more accessible and cost‑effective green technologies, with UAE garnering significant attention, energy‑efficient alternative [9], [10], [11]. The fundamental principle underpinning UAE efficacy is acoustic cavitation which generates extraordinary localized conditions, including transient temperatures reaching thousands of kelvin, pressures exceeding 1000 atm, along with high‑velocity microjets and intense shear forces that synergistically disrupt resilient fungal cell walls, accelerate solvent penetration, and enhance mass transfer of intracellular polyphenols into the bulk solvent [12], [13]. This collapse generates extraordinary localized conditions along with high-velocity microjets and intense shear forces [11], [12], [13], [14], [15]. Consequently, UAE offers higher extraction yields with substantially reduced extraction time, solvent consumption, and energy input compared to conventional methods, while operating effectively under milder temperature conditions that preserve the structural integrity and bioactivity of thermolabile phenolic compounds. These distinctive advantages firmly establish UAE as a green and efficient technology that aligns with the principles of green chemistry while directly addressing the critical shortcomings of conventional extraction approaches.

While UAE has been previously applied to extract polyphenols from I. hispidus, those studies employed fixed ultrasonic conditions and focused narrowly on yield maximization without systematic power optimization, post-extraction bioactivity assessment, or sustainability evaluation. Building upon these mechanistic insights, the present study was designed to establish an optimized, UAE protocol for the efficient and sustainable extraction of total polyphenols from I. hispidus. An initial comparative evaluation of three extraction methodologies was conducted. Subsequently, single-factor experiments systematically assessed the influence of key process variables on TPC yield. Response surface methodology (RSM) was then applied to model and optimize these parameters. The environmental sustainability of the optimized protocol was quantified using the Analytical GREENness (AGREE) metric. Finally, the antioxidant capacity of the optimally extracted polyphenols was rigorously evaluated through multiple in vitro assays to validate the functional preservation of the extracted compounds.

Materials and methods

Materials and chemicals

Fruiting bodies of Inonotus hispidus were collected from Xiajin Sanghuang Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shandong Province, China. Species authentication was performed via ITS rDNA sequencing. Voucher specimens were deposited at the Key Laboratory of Medicinal Fungal Resources and Development Utilization, Jilin Agricultural University. All chemical reagents used were of analytical grade.

Sample preparation

The collected specimens were air‑dried naturally, pulverized, and sieved to obtain a uniform particle fraction through a 60‑mesh screen. The moisture content of the dried fungal material was 85.73 ± 0.42 % (w/w). The extraction yield was 18.74 ± 0.42 mg/g under the preliminary extraction conditions. To assess batch‑to‑batch variation, three independently prepared batches of fungal material were extracted under identical optimized conditions; the relative standard deviation for TPC was 2.14 %, indicating good reproducibility of the extraction process. The dried powder was stored in sealed polyethylene bags at 4 ℃ in the dark prior to use, and all extractions were completed within two weeks of sample preparation to minimize potential degradation of phenolic compounds. Extractions were performed using a KS-700XDS ultrasonic bath (Kunshan Ultrasonic Instruments Co., Ltd., China) equipped with a rectangular stainless-steel chamber (500 mm × 300 mm × 200 mm; total bath volume, 30 L). The instrument operates at a fixed frequency of 50 kHz with a nominal power output of 700 W, adjustable from 10 % to 100 %. The heating temperature is adjustable from room temperature to 80 ℃ (accuracy ± 1 ℃). Post-extraction, all solutions were clarified by filtration through a 0.22 µm membrane to remove any insoluble particulates.

Comparison of several extraction methods

UAE factors were: sonication time 80 min, solid-to-liquid ratio 1:30 g/mL, ethanol concentration 50 % v/v, ultrasonic power 150 W, extraction temperature 50 ℃. Each experiment was performed with the other parameters held constant. To benchmark the performance of UAE, it was compared with stirring-assisted extraction (SAE) and heating reflux extraction (HRE). For methodological consistency, the preliminary constant parameters were set as an 80-min extraction time, 50 % ethanol as the extraction solvent, a solid-to-liquid ratio of 1:30 (g/mL), and a temperature of 50 ℃. UAE was conducted using the aforementioned ultrasonic bath at a constant power of 150 W. SAE was performed with a DF-101S heating magnetic stirrer (Henan Aibot Technology Development Co., Ltd., China) at a constant agitation speed of 100 rpm. HRE was carried out in an HH-6 thermostatic water bath (Nanjing Ronghua Scientific Equipment Co., Ltd., China). Post-extraction, the mixtures were centrifuged at 11,000 rpm for 10 min, and the supernatants were collected for analysis. All experiments were conducted in triplicate. The total energy consumption for each extraction method was calculated as the sum of ultrasonic energy and heating energy:Etotal=Eultrasonic+Eheatingwhere Eultrasonic is the energy consumed by the ultrasonic transducer during operation, calculated as the product of the instrument power setting and sonication time:Eultrasonic=Pultrasonic×Tultrasonicand Eultrasonic is the energy consumed by the heating system, calculated as:Eultrasonic=Pultrasonic×Tultrasonic

Assessment of environmental impact

The environmental footprint of each extraction method was quantitatively evaluated using the Analytical GREENness (AGREE) software (version 0.5), a freely accessible tool grounded in the twelve principles of the Green Analytical Chemistry Index (GAC) [16]. The AGREE metric assigns a composite score on a scale of 0 to 1, with a method considered green if its score exceeds 0.6. The evaluation critically integrates factors such as sample throughput, energy consumption, reagent toxicity, and waste generation. A color-coded radial plot, transitioning from red (non-compliant) to green (fully compliant), visually represents the performance across each principle, enabling a transparent and comprehensive assessment of the overall greenness.

Determination of total polyphenol content

Total phenolic content (TPC) was quantified using a modified Folin-Ciocalteu spectrophotometric method as described by Huo et al. [17]. Briefly, a 50-µL aliquot of appropriately diluted extract was mixed with 500 µL of ten-fold diluted Folin-Ciocalteu reagent. Subsequently, 1000 µL of 1 M NaOH and 500 µL of 10 % Na2CO3 were sequentially added. The reaction mixture was vortexed thoroughly and incubated in darkness for 60 min to ensure complete color development. The absorbance was measured at 750 nm against a reagent blank. TPC values were calculated from a calibration curve constructed with gallic acid standard solutions (y = 0.1624x + 1.2463, R2 = 0.9945).

Single-factor experiment

The influence of six independent process variables on the TPC yield from I. hispidus was individually assessed. The evaluated factors were: sonication time (50, 80, 110, and 140 min), solid-to-liquid ratio (1:20, 1:30, 1:40, and 1:50, w/v), ethanol concentration (40 %, 50 %, 60 %, and 70 %, v/v), ultrasonic power (100, 150, 200, and 250 W), extraction temperature (40, 50, 60, and 70 ℃) and number of extraction cycles (1, 2, and 3). Each experiment was performed with the other parameters held constant. Given the consistency of all experimental conditions, the relative comparisons across power levels remain internally valid.

Design of RSM

Based on the single-factor results, four independent variables exhibiting the most significant effects were selected for optimization via a Box-Behnken design (BBD). The experimental data were fitted to a second-order polynomial regression model, expressed as:(1)Y=β0+∑j=1kβjXj+∑j=1kβjjXj2+∑i∑<j=2kβijXiXj+eiwhere Y is the predicted response (TPC); β0, βj, βjj, and βij are the regression coefficients for the constant, linear, quadratic, and interaction terms, respectively; Xi and Xj are the coded independent variables; and ei is the random error. The BBD comprised 29 experimental runs, including five replicates at the center point. Experimental design, regression analysis, and ANOVA were performed using Design-Expert 13.0 (Stat-Ease, Minneapolis, MN, USA). Model adequacy was evaluated through the coefficient of determination (R2), adjusted R2, lack-of-fit test, and coefficient of variation (C.V.). Determination of ultrasonic power and energy input

The effective ultrasonic power transmitted to the liquid medium was determined by the standard calorimetric approach. In brief, 300 mL of ultrapure water was introduced into an insulated vessel and placed at the center of the ultrasonic bath, corresponding to the same position used during extraction. Under identical sonication conditions (50 kHz, nominal power setting of 150 W), the temperature increase of the water was monitored over 120 s. The acoustic power, P (W), was then calculated using Equation:P=m×CP×ΔTtwhere m is the water mass (kg), Cp is the specific heat capacity of water (4186 J kg−1 ℃−1), ΔT is the measured temperature rise (℃), and t is the sonication time (s). The experiment was carried out in triplicate. The total energy input (E, J) for the UAE process was subsequently estimated from the calibrated power and the overall extraction time (textraction) using formula:E=P×textraction

Anti-oxidant activity

The antioxidant potential of the optimized I. hispidus polyphenol extract was assessed through four complementary assays, adapted from Yang et al. [18]. The DPPH radical-scavenging activity was determined by mixing 100 µL of extract with 100 µL of a 50 µmol/L DPPH ethanolic solution and incubating in the dark for 30 min; absorbance was read at 517 nm [19]. ABTS+ radical cation scavenging was evaluated using a commercial total antioxidant capacity kit following the manufacturer’s instructions [20]. The FRAP assay was performed to measure ferric reducing ability by mixing 5 µL of extract with 180 µL of FRAP working solution, incubating at 37 ℃ for 5 min, and measuring absorbance at 595 nm against a FeSO4 calibration curve [21]. Hydroxyl radical-scavenging capacity was assessed by reacting 5 µL of extract with 6 mmol/L FeSO4, 6 mmol/L salicylic acid (ethanolic solution), and 6 mmol/L H2O2; after incubation at 37 ℃ for 30 min, the absorbance was recorded at 510 nm [22].

Qualitative analysis of polyphenols extracts

The sample matrix underwent reconstitution in 500 μL of an 80 % (v/v) aqueous methanol solution, ensuring optimal solvation and extraction of the targeted compounds. After the reconstitution process, the mixture was subjected to centrifugation, allowing for the separation of solid residues from the soluble components. From the resulting supernatant, a 200 μL aliquot was meticulously diluted with HPLC −MS-grade water, yielding a final methanol concentration of 53 % (v/v), which is crucial for the subsequent analytical processes. The analytical procedure was carried out using HPLC-MS/MS. Chromatographic separation was effectively achieved utilizing a reverse-phase C18 column with dimensions of 2.1 × 100 mm and a particle size of 1.7 μm, which was maintained at a stable temperature of 40 °C to ensure reproducibility of the results. The mass spectral data were collected in multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) mode with polarity switching. To process the obtained data, SCIEX OS software, version 14.0, was employed, facilitating automated peak detection, integration, and baseline correction, which are essential for accurate quantification.

Statistical analysis

All extractions and assays were conducted in triplicate to ensure reproducibility. Results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Statistical significance between groups was determined by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey‘s multiple comparison test. Data analysis and graphical representations were performed using Origin 2021 (OriginLab Corporation, Northampton, MA, USA).

Results and discussion

Comparative efficiency and greenness of extraction methods

The comparative evaluation (Table 1) demonstrated that UAE yielded a markedly higher TPC (18.74 ± 0.42 mg/g) relative to HRE (17.24 ± 0.36 mg/g) and SAE (16.35 ± 0.18 mg/g), corresponding to significant increments of 8.70 % and 14.62 %, respectively. This higherity is directly attributed to the acoustic cavitation phenomenon unique to ultrasound. During UAE, the formation, growth, and implosive collapse of cavitation bubbles generate intense localized shear forces, high-velocity microjets, and shock waves [23], [24], [25]. Moreover, the energy consumption analysis revealed a substantially lower energy footprint for UAE (1.62 × 106 J), which was 4.4-fold and 3.0-fold lower than that required for HRE (7.20 × 106 J) and SAE (4.80 × 106 J), respectively. In contrast, HRE is primarily governed by thermal convection and passive diffusion, which are intrinsically less effective at breaching rigid cellular architectures and carry an elevated risk of thermolabile polyphenol degradation [26]. While SAE employs mechanical agitation, it lacks the cavitation-induced shock waves essential for complete cell wall fragmentation, leading to incomplete solute recovery [27], [28]. The AGREE analysis corroborated these findings, with UAE achieving the highest score of 0.76, surpassing HRE (0.63) and SAE (0.59). The scoring rationale for each criterion is provided in Table S1, ensuring full transparency and reproducibility of the assessment. The UAE system’s higher degree of automation and more energy efficiency directly contributed to this favorable greenness profile, strongly suggesting it as the most sustainable and effective technique for further optimization (Fig. S1). Single-factor experiment

Effect of ultrasonic time

As depicted in Fig. 1A, TPC yield demonstrated a parabolic trend with increasing sonication time. The initial increase in TPC up to 80 min is consistent with the progressive physical disruption of fungal cell walls under sustained ultrasonic, which is widely reported to enhance solvent penetration and mass transfer through acoustic cavitation and microstreaming [29]. However, beyond the optimal range, a decline in extractable polyphenol content was observed. This decline may be associated with thermal accumulation within the cavitation field, which could promote oxidative degradation, polymerization, or other structural transformations of polyphenolic molecules, thereby reducing their detectability [17]. Effect of solid to liquid ratio

Fig. 1B shows that TPC yield remained statistically constant across a solid-to-liquid ratio range of 1:20 to 1:50 g/mL. Although an increased solvent volume can, in theory, promote mass transfer by maintaining a higher concentration gradient and reducing bulk viscosity, an excessive solvent volume may also attenuate ultrasonic field propagation and increase the cavitation threshold by dampening sound wave intensity [30]. In the absence of a statistically significant difference, a ratio of 1:20 g/mL was adopted to prioritize economic feasibility, energy conservation, and operational convenience during downstream processing.

Effect of ethanol concentration

Polyphenol recovery exhibited a strong dependence on the ethanol concentration (Fig. 1C). The TPC increased markedly as the ethanol volume fraction was elevated from 40 % to 60 %, beyond which a plateau was observed. This behavior is governed by solvent polarity and solubility parameters; a 60 % ethanol–water mixture achieves an optimal polarity balance that facilitates both the solvation of phenolic compounds and the swelling of the fungal matrix, enhancing intracellular solute diffusion [31]. Excessively high ethanol concentrations (>60 %) significantly reduce the dielectric constant of the extraction medium, thereby decreasing the solubility of the more polar polyphenol glycosides and triggering a reduction in extraction efficiency. Consequently, 60 % ethanol was selected as the optimal cosolvent.

Effect of ultrasonic power

The impact of ultrasonic power on TPC followed an ascending-then-plateau pattern (Fig. 1D). Increasing the electrical power up to 150 W proportionally increased the nominal acoustic power [32]. At this optimum, the collapse of cavitation bubbles generates microjets and shock waves that are known to effectively disrupt cellular matrices [33]. However, beyond a critical power level, the amplitude of the pressure waves can exceed the transient cavitation threshold, leading to the generation of a dense bubble cloud that scatters the acoustic field, and the simultaneous explosion of bubbles can generate excessive hydroxyl radicals, which may in turn degrade the target antioxidant molecules. To avoid such detrimental sonochemical effects and minimize energy consumption, 150 W was maintained as the optimum ultrasonic power.

Effect of ultrasonic temperature

Temperature exhibited a pronounced biphasic effect on extraction efficiency (Fig. 1E). TPC yield increased progressively with temperature from 40 to 60 ℃, but a sharp decline was witnessed at 70 ℃. The initial enhancement at 60 ℃ is attributed to a synergistic mechanism: thermally induced softening of the lignocellulosic matrix facilitates cell wall disintegration, while reduced solvent viscosity and surface tension improve liquid penetration and solute diffusion [34]. Nevertheless, at elevated temperatures, the vapor pressure of the solvent increases substantially, resulting in a higher proportion of vapor-filled cavities which collapse less violently and thus produce diminished sonophysical effects. More critically, prolonged exposure to 70 ℃ can thermally degrade the polyphenolic structures, causing a net loss in TPC. Based on this thermo-acoustic interplay, 60 ℃ was determined as the optimal extraction temperature.

Effect of extraction cycles

Investigation into the number of extraction cycles (Fig. 1F) revealed that a single cycle was sufficient to exhaust the bulk of easily extractable phenolic compounds from the matrix. The incremental TPC gains from subsequent cycles were negligible and statistically non-significant (p > 0.05). Substantial increases in solvent expenditure, processing time, and energy demand associated with multiple cycles did not yield a commensurate improvement in total recovery. Therefore, a single extraction cycle was deemed the most resource-efficient operational strategy.

ANOVA analysis for RSM model.

Analysis of the regression coefficients revealed distinct contributions from each variable. Ultrasonic time (X1) and temperature (X1) exhibited the largest positive linear coefficients (1.88 and 1.84, respectively), indicating that these were the most influential factors governing TPC yield within the tested ranges. The strong positive effect of time suggests that prolonged sonication facilitates progressive cell wall disruption and polyphenol release, while elevated temperature enhances solute solubility and diffusion rates. However, the significant negative quadratic coefficients for both X12 (–2.33) and X22 (–2.43) indicate optimal ranges beyond which further increases lead to diminished yields. Among the interaction terms, X1X2 showed the largest positive coefficient (1.36), indicating a synergistic effect. Conversely, X1X4 exhibited a negative coefficient (–0.8375), suggesting that prolonged extraction at excessive power levels accelerates polyphenol degradation, consistent with the susceptibility of phenolic compounds to cavitation‑induced free radical attack. The remaining interaction terms showed relatively small coefficients, indicating weaker synergistic effects among these variable pairs.

Effect of extraction parameters on TPC

Based on the regression model, contour plots and three-dimensional response surface plots were generated to visualize the interactions between variables (Fig. S3, 2). A significant interaction was observed between ultrasonic time (X1) and ultrasonic temperature (X2) (p = 0.0024, Table 4), with TPC increasing initially and then declining as both factors increased (Fig. 2A). This biphasic phenomenon reflects the dual role of UAE. Initially, the collapse of shock waves, which disrupt cell wall integrity and facilitate solvent penetration and phenolic release [35], [36]. However, prolonged or excessively heated ultrasonication promotes two concurrent degradation pathways that oxidatively cleave phenolic rings [37]. This trend aligns with findings from Jiang et al. on anthocyanin extraction from blueberries [38]. The interaction between ultrasonic time (X1) and ultrasonic power (X4) followed a similar pattern (p = 0.0388, Table 4), with TPC increasing initially and then declining beyond optimal levels (Fig. 2C). A moderate power–time product maximizes mechanical damage to the matrix, sharply elevating the extractable phenolic content [39], [40]. In contrast, excessive power combined with prolonged exposure intensifies both the frequency, leading to overgeneration of free radicals and localized hotspots [41], [42]. This accelerates phenolic oxidation and polymerization, outweighing any further gain in mass transfer, consistent with alkaloid extraction studies on safflower seeds [43]. The RSM model predicted an optimal TPC yield of 25.23 mg/g under the following conditions: ultrasonic time, 96.72 min; ethanol concentration, 60.84 %; ultrasonic power, 151.63 W; and temperature, 65.41 ℃. To validate the model, these parameters were adjusted to practical values: 95 min, 60 % ethanol, 150 W, and 65 ℃. Triplicate experiments under these conditions yielded 25.46 ± 0.34 mg/g with an extraction yield of 2.546 %, which closely matched the predicted value and indicated the model’s reliability. Compared to pre-optimization conditions, TPC yield increased from 18.74 mg/g to 25.46 mg/g, representing a 35.86 % enhancement in extraction efficiency. The calibrated ultrasonic power was found to be 62.8 ± 0.32 W (n = 3), indicating efficient transmission of acoustic energy under the selected operating conditions. Using this power value and a total extraction time of 95 min (5700 s), the overall energy consumption of the optimized UAE procedure was estimated at approximately 3.58 × 105 J. Assays of antioxidants

The antioxidant potential of the extract produced under optimized UAE conditions was systematically evaluated. The capacities to scavenge DPPH radicals (Fig. 3A) and ABTS++ radical cations (Fig. 3B) both exhibited a pronounced dose-dependent response over the 0.2–1.4 mg/mL range, confirming the abundant presence of electron-donating compounds capable of reducing and stabilizing free radicals [44]. This concentration-dependent behavior is characteristic of polyphenol-rich extracts, where higher concentrations provide a greater number of hydroxyl groups available for hydrogen atom transfer or electron donation to free radicals. The FRAP assay (Fig. 3C) further affirmed the robust reducing power of the extract, reflecting its ability to donate electrons to ferric ions, a crucial mechanism in antioxidation [45]. Moreover, the extract displayed a potent capacity for scavenging highly reactive and destructive hydroxyl radicals (Fig. 3D), which are among the primary contributors to oxidative stress-induced cellular damage in biological systems [46]. The antioxidant activity observed in this study is comparable to, and in some aspects superior to, previously reported values for I. hispidus extracts. Smolskaitė et al. (2015) comprehensively evaluated the antioxidant properties of eight mushroom species and found that I. hispidus methanol fractions exhibited some of the highest antioxidant capacities among the tested species, with values of 9.5 ± 0.04 µM TE/g in DPPH, 54.27 ± 0.46 µM TE/g in ABTS+, and 88.31 ± 1.96 µM TE/g in FRAP assays [47]. Our optimized ethanolic UAE extract showed enhanced performance relative to these reported values, which can be attributed to the higher extraction efficiency of UAE and the superior polyphenol yield achieved under our optimized conditions. Additionally, previous studies have reported that phenolic compounds from I. hispidus exhibit DPPH scavenging activity with IC50 values ranging from 9.82 to 21.43 µM, further confirming the potent antioxidant potential of this fungal species [48].

The concentration-dependent increase in antioxidant activity can be explained by the progressive saturation of free radical sites with increasing polyphenol concentration. At lower concentrations, the limited number of available phenolic hydroxyl groups restricts the extent of radical neutralization; as concentration increases, more hydroxyl groups become available to donate hydrogen atoms or electrons, thereby enhancing radical scavenging and reducing power [46]. This relationship is well-documented for polyphenolic compounds, where the number and position of hydroxyl groups on the aromatic ring critically determine the radical-scavenging efficiency [44]. The significantly enhanced antioxidant efficacy of the optimized extract, compared to its unoptimized counterpart, clearly validates that the applied sonication conditions not only maximized yield but also effectively preserved the structural integrity and bioactivity of the polyphenols.

Qualitative analysis of polyphenols extracts

To elucidate the primary polyphenols components of TPC extract obtained through ultrasound-assisted extraction, we employed HPLC-MS/MS. The analysis of TPC was conducted in both positive and negative ion modes. By comparing mass-to-charge ratios (m/z) and [M−H]−/+ values against databases, we successfully identified and characterized a total of 60 polyphenolic compounds. The identified compounds are listed in Table 5 and comprise 21 flavonoids, 14 coumarins and their derivatives, 12 cinnamic acids and derivatives, and 13 isoflavonoids. Notably, flavonoids are considered the primary bioactive compounds responsible for the antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects observed in the extract [49], [50]. Furthermore, hispidin, luteolin, caffeic acid, ferulic acid, kaempferol, and isorhamnetin were identified as the predominant polyphenolic components in the optimized extract. Among these, hispidin exhibited a prominent relative abundance, consistent with its established role as a major bioactive constituent of I. hispidus. The presence of caffeic acid and ferulic acid as significant phenolic acids, together with flavonoids such as luteolin, kaempferol, and isorhamnetin, further corroborates the diverse phenolic profile of the extract. Conclusion

This study successfully established a systematic, mechanistic, and sustainable approach for the ultrasound-assisted extraction of high-antioxidant polyphenols from Inonotus hispidus. UAE was identified as the higher extraction technique over heat reflux and stirring-assisted method. The integration of single-factor experiments with a Box-Behnken design-based response surface methodology allowed for the precise optimization. The optimized conditions were determined as an extraction time of 95 min, ethanol concentration of 60 %, ultrasonic power of 150 W, and temperature of 65 ℃, yielding a TPC of 25.46 mg/g, which corresponded to a 35.86 % improvement over the unoptimized process. An AGREE greenness score of 0.76 indicated the protocol’s environmental sustainability. Furthermore, the extract obtained under the optimal conditions demonstrated significantly elevated antioxidant capacities in DPPH, ABTS, FRAP, and hydroxyl radical scavenging assays. This integrated methodology offers a highly efficient and eco-friendly for the valorization of medicinal fungi. Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. The study was conducted exclusively at laboratory scale, and pilot‑scale validation is needed. Biological activity was assessed only via in vitro antioxidant assays. Additionally, the calorimetric determination of acoustic power was performed under a single condition, and mechanistic interpretations regarding cavitation remain inferential without direct sonochemical measurements.
