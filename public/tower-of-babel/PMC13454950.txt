A Case Report of Myocardial Infarction Complicated With Blood Pressure Fluctuations Caused by Pheochromocytoma
Authors: HuiPing Li, Wei Yang, Yu Feng, You Zhang, Ganxin Yan, ChuanYu Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPheochromocytoma typically presents with sustained or paroxysmal hypertension, but initial manifestation as acute non‐ST‐segment elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI) is rare. Peri‐procedural hemodynamic fluctuations are often dramatic, making diagnosis and management challenging. We report a 51‐year‐old male admitted with chest tightness for 17 days. Elevated troponin and coronary angiography showing multivessel disease with total occlusion of the mid‐left anterior descending artery (LAD) led to a preliminary diagnosis of NSTEMI. During percutaneous coronary intervention, blood pressure surged to 240/130 mmHg; intravenous nitroprusside caused an instantaneous drop to 95/55 mmHg, with rapid rebound to >200 mmHg upon withdrawal, showing no clear temporal relationship with drug administration. Postoperatively, large rapid blood pressure oscillations persisted, associated with postural changes but without patient discomfort. Workup revealed markedly elevated plasma catecholamines: dopamine 77.58 pg/mL (normal 0–20), normetanephrine 1881.63 pg/mL (0–145), and norepinephrine 5,724.04 pg/mL (217–1,109). CT and PET‐CT identified a left retroperitoneal mass (59×34×44 mm, SUVmax 14.6), suggestive of pheochromocytoma. Surgical resection was performed, and histopathology with immunohistochemistry confirmed the diagnosis. Postoperatively, the patient required no antihypertensive medications and blood pressure remained stable. This case illustrates a rare pheochromocytoma presentation as secondary hypertension manifesting as acute myocardial infarction, characterized by extreme peri‐procedural hemodynamic instability during PCI and an “all‐or‐none” response to conventional antihypertensive therapy, ultimately cured by surgical resection.

Introduction

A 51‐year‐old male patient was admitted with a chief complaint of “sudden onset of chest tightness for 17 days.” Seventeen days prior to admission, the patient experienced a sudden fall, followed by chest tightness and shortness of breath, without chest pain or palpitations. The symptoms subsided after half an hour. Approximately 2 h later, he received treatment in a local hospital where elevated troponin levels were detected (Table 1), leading to a diagnosis of “acute non‐ST‐segment elevation myocardial infarction.” Coronary angiography revealed: right‐dominant circulation; 60% stenosis in the distal left main coronary artery (LM); total occlusion in the mid‐left anterior descending artery (LAD); collateral circulation supplied to the LAD from the distal right coronary artery (RCA); 80% stenosis at the ostium of the left circumflex artery (LCx) and 70% stenosis in its distal segment; diffuse lesions throughout the RCA, with the most severe stenosis being 60%–70%. An attempt to recanalize the LAD was unsuccessful. For further diagnosis and treatment, the patient was admitted to our hospital with a diagnosis of “acute non‐ST‐segment elevation myocardial infarction.” Since the onset of the illness, the patient has remained alert with a stable mental status, normal bowel and bladder habits, and no significant weight changes.

During the PCI procedure, we observed dramatic blood pressure fluctuations in the patient, with a sudden intraprocedural surge to 240/130 mmHg. Intravenous infusion of sodium nitroprusside was administered for blood pressure control, which resulted in an instantaneous drop to 95/55 mmHg. However, upon discontinuation of the infusion, blood pressure rapidly rose again to over 200 mmHg. This pattern was verified through multiple manipulations, revealing no clear temporal relationship between the blood pressure fluctuations and the medication. Post‐procedural evaluation of cardiac function and complications was completed, after which the patient was transferred back to the ward in stable condition, where continuous monitoring showed persistently volatile and rapidly changing blood pressure. Dramatic fluctuations occurred even without any intervention and appeared to be associated with postural changes. The patient reported no significant discomfort during these blood pressure variations, both intraoperatively and postoperatively. Further Treatment Plan: Surgical resection is the only curative treatment for pheochromocytoma. Preoperative preparation involved administration of an alpha‐blocker (phenoxybenzamine) for at least 10–14 days to control blood pressure and prevent intraoperative “hormonal storm.”

The patient was subsequently transferred to the surgical department for tumor resection. The resected specimen revealed a mass measuring 7 × 4.5 × 2.8 cm. Histopathological examination, along with immunohistochemical staining, confirmed the diagnosis of pheochromocytoma (Figure 7, Supplementary material Pathology Report). Postoperatively, the patient required no antihypertensive medication, and blood pressure remained stable without significant fluctuations (Figure 8).

Medications at Discharge: At discharge, the patient was prescribed standard medications for coronary heart disease, including aspirin, ticagrelor, atorvastatin, metoprolol, dapagliflozin, and sacubitril/valsartan, as well as standard antidiabetic therapy.

Follow‐Up: The patient was seen in the outpatient clinic 2 months post‐surgery. His general condition was good, and he reported no specific complaints. Routine laboratory tests, including complete blood count, liver and kidney function, and BNP levels, were within normal limits. Home blood pressure monitoring showed readings ranging from 110–135/65‐90 mmHg, without the use of any antihypertensive medications. Thereafter, he attended outpatient follow‐up every three months, during which his overall condition remained good and blood pressure was stable. Future follow‐up visits will include serial measurements of plasma catecholamine levels and echocardiography to further assess the extent and durability of myocardial functional recovery.

Discussion

Etiology of Myocardial Infarction

The prominent features of this case are a middle‐aged male presenting with chest tightness and shortness of breath, abnormal Q waves in the precordial leads (V1–V3) on electrocardiography, elevated troponin levels, and diagnostic challenges posed by abnormal blood pressure fluctuations. The diagnostic criteria for AMI are based on clinical presentation, electrocardiographic changes, elevated serum cardiac biomarkers, and imaging evidence. The currently adopted “Fourth Universal Definition of Myocardial Infarction (2018)” establishes that a diagnosis of AMI can be made when there is a rise and/or fall of cardiac biomarkers (preferably cardiac troponin I or T), with at least one value exceeding the 99th percentile upper reference limit (URL), in conjunction with at least one of the following: (1) symptoms of myocardial ischemia (e.g., chest pain, dyspnea, syncope); (2) new ischemic electrocardiographic changes (e.g., ST–T changes, pathological Q waves); (3) imaging evidence, such as on echocardiography or myocardial perfusion imaging, of new loss of viable myocardium or new regional wall motion abnormality; or (4) identification of coronary thrombus by angiography or autopsy [1, 2].

In this patient, elevated troponin levels at the outside hospital, accompanied by clinical symptoms, supported the diagnosis of AMI. Given that the diagnosis was established, the critical question arose: what was the underlying cause of the infarction? In this patient, coronary angiography demonstrated total occlusion of the LAD, with collateral flow supplied from the distal right coronary artery, and no other coronary vessels exhibiting significant or life‐threatening stenosis. Admission transthoracic echocardiography revealed globally reduced left ventricular wall motion, without the segmental wall motion abnormalities typically associated with acute LAD occlusion. Myocardial perfusion scintigraphy further demonstrated extensive ischemic changes with areas of non‐transmural infarction. Collectively, these imaging findings were inconsistent with an acute proximal LAD occlusion‐related myocardial infarction. Crucially, emergency coronary angiography performed at the referring hospital shortly after symptom onset disclosed no evidence of acute plaque disruption or intraluminal thrombus formation within the LAD. Taken together, these observations strongly suggest that the LAD occlusion was chronic in nature and did not represent the culprit lesion responsible for the index AMI.

Therefore, this case was classified as Type 2 myocardial infarction [2], in accordance with the latest universal definition. This category recognizes that myocardial injury may occur in the presence of underlying coronary artery stenosis but is not attributable to acute atherothrombotic events [4, 5]. In the present patient, the Type 2 MI was most likely driven by myocardial injury and recurrent hemodynamic fluctuations secondary to pheochromocytoma.

Diagnostic Pitfalls of Pheochromocytoma

Pheochromocytoma is a tumor arising from chromaffin tissue of the sympathetic nervous system that secretes excessive amounts of catecholamines. Although the most common site of occurrence is the adrenal medulla, it can also develop in other locations within the sympathetic nervous system. Its clinical manifestations are complex and variable. The classic triad of paroxysmal hypertension accompanied by palpitations, headache, and diaphoresis carries significant diagnostic value for pheochromocytoma [6].

Intermittent release of large quantities of catecholamines into the bloodstream leads to vasoconstriction, increased peripheral vascular resistance, tachycardia, and elevated cardiac output, resulting in sudden paroxysmal elevations in blood pressure. During these episodes, patients may also experience chest tightness, shortness of breath, a sensation of thoracic oppression, pallor, blurred vision, and dizziness. In severe cases, hypertensive crises such as cerebral hemorrhage or pulmonary edema may occur, accompanied by abnormal cardiac enzyme levels and exacerbation of heart failure [6, 7, 8].

However, this case highlights a critical diagnostic pitfall: when pheochromocytoma coexists with AMI and multivessel coronary artery disease, the likelihood of misdiagnosis is extremely high [9, 10, 11]. Our patient initially presented with chest tightness and shortness of breath, and coronary angiography performed at the outside hospital had already revealed multivessel disease and chronic LAD occlusion. This led us to preconceive that we were dealing with a routine case of Type 1 myocardial infarction, thereby overlooking the important clue of abnormal blood pressure fluctuations. In fact, the patient's sudden fall at symptom onset may itself have been a manifestation of a pheochromocytoma crisis—dizziness or syncope induced by acute massive release of catecholamines [6]. This case serves as a reminder that when managing patients with significant coronary artery disease, we should not mechanically attribute all myocardial injury to atherothrombotic events, but rather maintain vigilance for secondary etiologies, particularly pheochromocytoma.

Intraoperative Management Challenges of Hemodynamic Crisis

One of the most clinically instructive aspects of this case was the extreme hemodynamic event that occurred during the interventional procedure. Intraoperatively, the patient's blood pressure suddenly surged to 240/130 mmHg. Following intravenous bolus administration of sodium nitroprusside, the blood pressure dropped instantaneously to 95/55 mmHg and promptly rebounded to above 200 mmHg upon drug discontinuation. This “all‐or‐none” hemodynamic response pattern to vasodilators is a classic manifestation of unrecognized or untreated pheochromocytoma when exposed to vasodilating agents and carries important clinical implications.

Pheochromocytoma crisis represents one of the most life‐threatening endocrine emergencies in clinical practice, with markedly elevated mortality, especially in the presence of cardiovascular complications [12]. Case reports and registry studies have consistently demonstrated that patients with pheochromocytoma‐induced hypertensive crisis complicated by acute myocardial injury, cardiogenic shock, or multiorgan dysfunction have significantly higher short‐term mortality than those with hypertensive crises from other causes [13]. A key mechanism underlying this excessive mortality is refractory hemodynamic collapse, which may paradoxically ensue during blood pressure control efforts [14]. Massive paroxysmal catecholamine release—primarily norepinephrine—induces severe vasospasm, increased afterload, and direct cardiotoxicity [15]. However, abrupt pharmacological vasodilation, compounded by relative hypovolemia and rapid reversal of catecholamine‐mediated vasoconstriction, can precipitously trigger profound hypotension. This hemodynamic instability is notoriously difficult to manage and often responds suboptimally to conventional vasopressors and fluid resuscitation [16, 17].

In the present case, the administration of sodium nitroprusside—a potent arterial and venous vasodilator—in the setting of uncontrolled pheochromocytoma resulted in a “cliff‐like” drop in blood pressure. This phenomenon can be explained by the following mechanism: patients with pheochromocytoma are in a state of high catecholamine excess, with sustained intense vasoconstriction and relative hypovolemia. When sodium nitroprusside rapidly reverses this vasoconstriction, the relatively depleted intravascular volume is insufficient to fill the suddenly expanded vascular bed, thereby leading to catastrophic hypotension. This observation underscores the critical importance of early recognition, hemodynamically prudent pharmacotherapy (with α‐blockade prior to any intervention), and multidisciplinary team preparation in cases with suspected or confirmed pheochromocytoma.

Pheochromocytoma‐Induced Myocardial Injury and its Reversibility

Cardiac involvement represents a severe complication of pheochromocytoma, encompassing a broad spectrum of clinical presentations, including angina pectoris, myocardial infarction, cardiomyopathy, and heart failure. Although the present patient initially presented with AMI, the disease course deviated from that of a classic myocardial infarction, ultimately leading to a diagnosis of pheochromocytoma‐induced acute myocardial injury with concomitant worsening of heart failure.

The underlying mechanism likely involves the release of large quantities of catecholamines (such as norepinephrine) from pheochromocytoma tumor cells, which exert the following effects on the heart: (1) direct toxic effects on cardiomyocytes, promoting myocardial hypertrophy and increasing myocardial oxygen demand, representing an important mechanism of cardiac injury that can lead to catecholamine‐induced cardiomyopathy [10, 18]; (2) cardiomyocyte degeneration and necrosis, resulting in catecholamine‐induced myocarditis and coronary artery spasm, leading to myocardial ischemia and infarction [9, 10, 19]; and (3) calcium overload in cardiomyocytes, further contributing to cellular damage [10].

These pathophysiological mechanisms are distinct from those of myocardial infarction attributable to coronary atherosclerosis. A hallmark feature is myocardial infarction in the setting of normal coronary arteries or the absence of acute obstructive coronary pathology—a unique subtype of Type 2 myocardial infarction [6, 18]. The prognostic implications also differ; catecholamine‐induced myocardial injury is potentially reversible following complete resection of the underlying tumor [6, 10, 20, 21]. In the present case, the patient achieved sustained blood pressure control (110–135/65–90 mmHg) without any antihypertensive agents postoperatively. Follow‐up assessments at 2 months and quarterly thereafter revealed favorable general status, stable hemodynamics, and normalization of laboratory markers, including B‐type natriuretic peptide (BNP), providing robust evidence supporting the reversibility of the myocardial injury.

Conclusion

This case offers valuable insights into the re‐evaluation of Type 2 myocardial infarction and complex pheochromocytoma. It underscores the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion for pheochromocytoma in patients presenting with acute coronary syndrome accompanied by unexplained marked blood pressure lability. Early recognition, adequate preoperative α‐adrenergic blockade, judicious intraoperative hemodynamic management, and multidisciplinary collaboration are pivotal in averting fatal outcomes and achieving complete reversibility of myocardial injury. Continued clinical experience and knowledge accumulation will enable more patients to derive benefit from optimal therapeutic strategies.
