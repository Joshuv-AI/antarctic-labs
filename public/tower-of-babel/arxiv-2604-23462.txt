Invariant measures for the open KPZ equation: the Gaussian case
James Bona-Landry

Abstract
. In [ 6 ] , Quastel and Gu use Stein’s equation and integration by parts to give a direct proof that drifted Brownian motions are stationary (modulo height shifts) for the full-line KPZ equation. In this article, we consider the open KPZ equation with boundary conditions ∂ x h ⁡ ( t , 0 ) = ∂ x h ⁡ ( t , 1 ) = α \partial_{x}h(t,0)=\partial_{x}h(t,1)=\alpha for a general real parameter α ∈ ℝ \alpha\in\R , and emulate the approach of Quastel and Gu to provide a similar proof that Brownian motion with constant drift α \alpha is invariant (modulo height shifts) in this case.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Background

The open Kardar-Parisi-Zhang (KPZ) equation is a stochastic partial differential equation modeling the random evolution of an interface
h\in C(\R_{+}\times[0,1])
subject to Neumann-type boundary conditions determined by two real parameters
\alpha,\beta\in\R
. Formally, it is written as

(
\text{KPZ}_{\alpha,\beta}
)

\partial_{t}h=\tfrac{1}{2}(\partial_{x}h)^{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}\partial_{x}^{2}h+\xi,\quad\partial_{x}h(t,0)=\alpha,\ \partial_{x}h(t,1)=-\beta,\quad h(0,x)=h_{0}(x)

where
\xi
denotes space-time white noise, the distribution-valued Gaussian field on
\R_{+}\times[0,1]
that is delta-correlated in space and time. Due to the singular nature of
\xi
, the rigorous interpretation of (

KPZ

α
,
β

) is a non-trivial task. Based on the linear theory, we expect the “solution”
h
to have spatial regularity at most Hölder-
\frac{1}{2}-
; the spatial derivative
\partial_{x}h
then exists only as a distribution, rendering the nonlinearity and pointwise boundary conditions appearing in (

KPZ

α
,
β

) ill-defined. A general framework for circumventing these issues and obtaining a rigorous solution theory is provided by Hairer’s work on regularity structures
[
7
,
4
]
. A simpler approach that works in the case of the open KPZ equation, however, (and which leads to the same result as Hairer’s framework) is to define the solution as
h(t,x):=\log Z(t,x)
for
Z\in C(\R_{+}\times[0,1])
satisfying the open stochastic heat equation

(
\text{SHE}_{\hat{\alpha},\hat{\beta}}
)

\partial_{t}Z=\tfrac{1}{2}\partial_{x}^{2}Z+Z\xi,\quad\partial_{x}Z(t,0)=\hat{\alpha}Z(t,0),\ \partial_{x}Z(t,1)=-\hat{\beta}Z(t,1),\quad Z(0,x)=e^{h_{0}(x)}

in the Itô-mild sense with
\hat{\alpha}=\alpha-\frac{1}{2}
and
\hat{\beta}=\beta-\frac{1}{2}
. This solution is referred to as the
Hopf-Cole
solution to (

KPZ

α
,
β

). Note that there is a shift by
\frac{1}{2}
in the boundary parameters here; although inconsistent with what would be suggested by a formal calculation, these shifts are motivated by
[
5
]
and have become standard convention in the literature. Very roughly, they arise from viewing
h
as the scaling limit of a sequence of approximating WASEPs, in which the formal boundary conditions have a natural physical interpretation.

In recent years, one of the central focuses of research activity surrounding (

KPZ

α
,
β

) has been characterizing and understanding its (almost) stationary distributions. The world “almost” here reflects the fact these distributions are not truly stationary (due to the fact that
h
has a deterministic upward drift), but rather invariant modulo height shifts. Descriptions of varying degrees of complexity were obtained by
[
3
,
2
,
1
]
, and it was ultimately shown in
[
2
]
that if
\alpha+\beta\geq 0
and
\min(\alpha,\beta)>-1
, then there is a unique stationary distribution
\mu_{\alpha,\beta}
for the height-shifted process
(h(t,x)-h(t,0))_{x\in[0,1]}
that decomposes into a sum of independent processes as
\mu_{\alpha,\beta}=_{\mathrm{law}}W+X
, where
W\in C([0,1])
is variance
\frac{1}{2}
Brownian motion, and
X\in C([0,1])
is absolutely continuous in law with respect to variance
\frac{1}{2}
Brownian motion, with Radon-Nikodym derivative proportional to

\displaystyle e^{-\beta X(1)}\left(\int_{0}^{1}e^{-2X(x)}dx\right)^{-(\alpha+\beta)}.

Notice that in the special case
\alpha+\beta=0
, the nonlinearity collapses and
X
reduces to a Gaussian process, specifically variance
\frac{1}{2}
Brownian motion with constant drift
\alpha=-\beta
. By independence, the invariant state
\mu_{\alpha,-\alpha}=W+X
then becomes standard Brownian motion with constant drift
\alpha
. The goal of this paper is to provide a “simple” proof (in the sense of being, modulo a key symmetry, a series of straightforward calculations) of invariance in this case by emulating the approach taken by Quastel and Gu in
[
6
]
, which leverages the Gaussian nature of the distribution.

1.2.
Organization

As an application of their approach to an analogous setting, the organization (and notation) of this paper has been deliberately chosen so as to mimic that of the blueprint paper
[
6
]
of Quastel and Gu. We begin by outlining the approach and general setup in Section
1.3
before presenting the main results in Section
2
. Sections
3
and
4
are then dedicated to the proofs of these main results, with the latter focusing on a key symmetry, Proposition
3.1
, on which these proofs rely.

1.3.
Integration by parts method

Because the invariance of
\mu_{\alpha,\beta}
is only up to height shifts, it will be convenient to work with
u:=\partial_{x}h
instead of
h
, which is defined as the solution to the open stochastic Burgers’ equation

(
\text{SBE}_{\alpha,\beta}
)

\partial_{t}u=\tfrac{1}{2}\partial_{x}u^{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}\partial_{x}^{2}u+\partial_{x}\xi,\quad u(t,0)=\alpha,\ u(t,1)=-\beta,\quad u(0,x)=u_{0}(x).

This is a stochastic process in the space of distributions, and the “almost” invariance of standard Brownian motion with drift
\alpha
under the flow of (
KPZ
α,-α
) is equivalent to the true invariance of mean
\alpha
(spatial) white noise under the flow of (
SBE
α,-α
). Our goal will thus be to show that if (
SBE
α,-α
) is initialized with mean
\alpha
spatial white noise, then the solution remains as this at all later times
t>0
. Equivalently,
\tilde{u}_{t}:=u_{t}-\alpha
is spatial white noise for every
t>0
. By Stein’s equation, this will follow if we can show that

(1.1)

\mathbb{E}[F(\langle\tilde{u}_{t},f\rangle)\langle\tilde{u}_{t},f\rangle]=\lVert f\rVert_{L^{2}([0,1])}^{2}\mathbb{E}F^{\prime}(\langle\tilde{u}_{t},f\rangle),\quad\text{for every}\ f\in C_{c}^{\infty}([0,1]),\ F\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\R),

where
\mathbb{E}=\mathbf{E}\mathbb{E}^{\mathrm{init}}
denotes expectation over the probability space
\Omega=\Omega^{\mathrm{noise}}\times\Omega^{\mathrm{init}}
associated to
u_{t}
. To do so, we approximate
u
by a smooth field for which we have access to a nice polymer representation, and compute the left-hand side of (
1.1
) making use of the following integration by parts identity from the Malliavin calculus:

Proposition 1.1

(
[
10
]
, Chapter 1)
.

Let
W
be either space-time white noise on
\mathbb{R}_{+}\times[0,1]
or spatial white noise on
[0,1]
. Then for any deterministic
f\in L^{2}
on the corresponding domain and any random variable
F
in the Malliavin-Sobolev space
\mathbb{D}^{1,2}
, we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[F\langle W,f\rangle\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\langle\mathscr{D}F,f\rangle_{L^{2}}\right],

where
\mathscr{D}
denotes the Malliavin derivative operator associated to
W
.

Our particular choice of approximation for
u=\partial_{x}(\log Z)
is motivated by the formal Feynman-Kac formula

(1.2)

Z(t,x)=\mathbb{E}^{\mathrm{RBM}}_{x}\left[e^{\int_{0}^{t}\xi(t-s,R_{s})ds-\frac{1}{2}\var\left(\int_{0}^{t}\xi(t-s,R_{s})ds\right)-\frac{1}{2}\left(\hat{\alpha}\mathscr{L}^{0}_{t}+\hat{\beta}\mathscr{L}^{1}_{t}\right)+h_{0}(R_{t})}\right],

which would make sense and characterize the solution to (

SHE

^
α

,

^
β

) if
\xi
and
h_{0}
were sufficiently “nice” (see, e.g.
[
8
,
11
]
). Here,
\mathbb{E}^{\mathrm{RBM}}_{x}
denotes expectation with respect to a reflecting Brownian motion
(R_{t})_{t\geq 0}
on
[0,1]
started from
x
, and
(\mathscr{L}^{y}_{t})_{y\in[0,1],t\geq 0}
denotes the jointly continuous version of the local time process associated to
R
, characterized by the occupation density formula

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}f(R_{s})ds=\int_{0}^{1}f(y)\mathscr{L}^{y}_{t}dy\quad\text{for all bounded measurable $f:[0,1]\to\R$.}

Specifically, for
\beta=-\alpha
and
u_{0}
given by mean
\alpha
spatial white noise (and
h_{0}
determined as
h_{0}(x)=\int_{0}^{x}u_{0}(y)dy
), we introduce regularized approximations of the terms appearing in (
1.2
) so as to allow us to take a logarithmic derivative in space and obtain a Feynman-Kac-type formula approximating
u
. More precisely, we let
\varepsilon,\kappa>0
denote smoothing parameters – one for the forcing noise and local time terms, and the other for the initial data – that will characterize these approximations and, in the end, will be taken to zero separately to recover
u
. To make explicit the dependence of certain objects on these parameters, the pair
(\varepsilon,\kappa)
will be denoted by
\theta
and included as superscript or subscript where appropriate. With this in mind, we define

\displaystyle\xi_{\varepsilon}(t,x):=(e^{\varepsilon\Delta_{\mathrm{neu}}}\xi(t,\cdot))(x),\quad h_{0}^{\kappa}(x):=(e^{\kappa\Delta_{\mathrm{neu}}}h_{0})(y)dy,

where
(e^{t\Delta_{\mathrm{neu}}})_{t\geq 0}
denotes the heat semigroup generated by the Laplacian on
[0,1]
with homogeneous Neumann boundary conditions.

Remark 1.1
.

To briefly motivate this particular choice of smoothing, it shall be computationally convenient to “unfold” the problem and view reflecting Brownian motion on
[0,1]
in terms of an underlying “driving” Brownian motion on
\R
as
R_{t}=\varrho(B_{t}+x)
, where
\varrho:\R\to[0,1]
denotes the reflection function

(1.3)

\varrho(x)=\begin{cases}x-\lfloor x\rfloor&\text{if $\lfloor x\rfloor$ is even},\\
\lfloor x\rfloor+1-x&\text{if $\lfloor x\rfloor$ is odd}.\end{cases}

Crucially, the Neumann heat kernel
p^{\mathrm{neu}}(t,x;y):=(e^{t\Delta_{\mathrm{neu}}}\delta_{y})(x)
behaves very nicely with respect to this reflection function. Indeed, when identified with its natural extension to
\R_{+}\times\R^{2}
via the expansion

(1.4)

p^{\mathrm{neu}}(t,x;y)=\sum_{n\in\Z}\left(p(t,x-y-2n)+p(t,x+y-2n)\right),\quad p(t,x):=(2\pi t)^{-1/2}e^{-x^{2}/2t},

it is even and 2-periodic – and hence
\varrho
-invariant – in both spatial variables. It is easy to see that any function of the form
f=e^{t\Delta_{\mathrm{neu}}}f_{0}
is then likewise
\varrho
-invariant, allowing us to freely move between the reflecting process
R
and the underlying non-reflecting process
B
.

For the local time terms, we do something slightly simpler and observe that since
p^{\mathrm{neu}}(t,x;y)
approximates
\delta_{y}
in the weak sense on
C([0,1])
as
t\to 0
, we have that
\int_{0}^{T}p^{\mathrm{neu}}(t,R_{s};y)ds=\int_{0}^{1}p^{\mathrm{neu}}(t,x;y)\mathscr{L}^{x}_{T}dx\to\mathscr{L}^{y}_{T}
as
t\to 0
for every
y\in[0,1]
, and hence we can smoothly approximate
\mathscr{L}^{0}_{t}
and
\mathscr{L}^{1}_{t}
via
\int_{0}^{t}p^{\mathrm{neu}}(\varepsilon,R_{s};0)ds
and
\int_{0}^{t}p^{\mathrm{neu}}(\varepsilon,R_{s};1)ds
, respectively. With this, we define our approximating field
Z^{\theta}
as

(1.5)

Z^{\theta}(t,x):=\mathbb{E}^{\mathrm{RBM}}_{x}\left[e^{\int_{0}^{t}\xi_{\varepsilon}(t-s,R_{s})-\frac{1}{2}C_{\varepsilon}(R_{s})ds+\int_{0}^{t}V_{\varepsilon}(R_{s})ds+h_{0}^{\kappa}(R_{t})}\right],

where
C_{\varepsilon}(x):=p^{\mathrm{neu}}(2\varepsilon,x;x)
,
V_{\varepsilon}(x):=-\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\hat{\alpha}p^{\mathrm{neu}}(\varepsilon,x;0)+\hat{\beta}p^{\mathrm{neu}}(\varepsilon,x;1)\right)
, and
\alpha+\beta=0
. Here, we have written the normalization term for the stochastic integral pathwise as
C_{\varepsilon}(R_{s})
simply for computational convenience. Since everything appearing in (
1.5
) is by construction
\varrho
-invariant, we can now freely replace every instance of
R_{s}
with
B_{s}+x
and take a logarithmic derivative in
x
to obtain an approximation to
u
:

(1.6)

u^{\theta}(t,x):=\frac{\mathbb{E}^{\mathrm{BM}}_{x}\left[e^{-H^{\theta}(t,B)}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\partial_{x}\xi_{\varepsilon}(t-s,B_{s})-\frac{1}{2}C_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(B_{s})ds+\int_{0}^{t}V_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(B_{s})ds+u_{0}^{\kappa}(B_{t})\right)\right]}{Z^{\theta}(t,x)},

where
u_{0}^{\kappa}:=\partial_{x}h_{0}^{\kappa}
and we have used the abbreviation

\displaystyle-H^{\theta}(t,X)

\displaystyle:=\int_{0}^{t}\xi_{\varepsilon}(t-s,X_{s})-\tfrac{1}{2}C_{\varepsilon}(X_{s})ds+\int_{0}^{t}V_{\varepsilon}(X_{s})ds+h_{0}^{\kappa}(X_{t}).

By viewing
\frac{e^{-H^{\theta}(t,B)}}{Z^{\theta}(t,x)}
as the density of a “polymer measure”
\mathbb{P}^{\mathrm{poly},\theta}_{t,x}
with respect to the Wiener measure on continuous paths
B\in C_{x}(\R_{+})
started from
x
, we write this more compactly as

(1.7)

u^{\theta}(t,x)=\mathbb{E}^{\mathrm{poly},\theta}_{t,x}\left[\int_{0}^{t}\partial_{x}\xi_{\varepsilon}(t-s,X_{s})-\tfrac{1}{2}C_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(X_{s})ds+\int_{0}^{t}V_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(X_{s})ds+u_{0}^{\kappa}(X_{t})\right].

The limiting Gibbs-type measure obtained by taking the smoothing parameters
\varepsilon,\kappa\to 0
shall be denoted by
\mathbb{P}^{\mathrm{poly}}_{t,x}
, with corresponding expectation
\mathbb{E}^{\mathrm{poly}}_{t,x}
.

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\partial_{x}p^{\mathrm{neu}}(2\varepsilon,X^{1}_{s};X^{2}_{s})ds

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\partial_{x}p^{\mathrm{neu}}(2\varepsilon,X_{s};\tilde{X}_{s})ds

Contribution from the boundary data

4.
Proof of Proposition
3.1

In this section we prove the key symmetry Proposition
3.1
. As a simplifying reduction, we first note that we can replace the upper limit of integration
t
with
\tau=\inf\{s\geq 0:X^{1}_{s}-X^{2}_{s}\in 2{\Z}\}\wedge t
. This is because if
\tau<t
, then the integral can be broken up as the integral from
0
to
\tau
plus the integral from
\tau
to
t
. The latter vanishes because the diffusions
X^{1}
and
X^{2}
are exchangeable under the polymer expectation after time
\tau
(by the strong Markov property and 2-periodicity of
R_{\varepsilon}
) while the integral is anti-symmetric. Our goal is therefore to estimate

(4.1)

\mathcal{E}_{t,x_{1},x_{2}}=\frac{\mathbb{E}^{\mathrm{BM}}_{x_{1},x_{2}}e^{\sum_{j=1}^{2}\int_{0}^{t}\xi_{\varepsilon}(t-s,X^{j}_{s})-\frac{1}{2}C^{j}_{\varepsilon}(X^{j}_{s})ds+\int_{0}^{t}V_{\varepsilon}(X^{j}_{s})ds+h_{0}^{\kappa}(X^{j}_{t})}\int_{0}^{\tau}R_{\varepsilon}(X^{1}_{s}-X^{2}_{s})d(X^{1}_{s}-X^{2}_{s})}{Z^{\theta}(t,x_{1})Z^{\theta}(t,x_{2})}

for a fixed realization of the initial data. To keep notation simple, we shall denote by
\mathcal{X}_{t,x_{1},x_{2}}
the numerator of (
4.1
). We shall also occasionally use the notation
\lesssim
to mean “less than or equal to, up to a constant”, with a subscript (if included) indicating what the constant depends on. Our first result is a simple application of Cauchy-Schwarz that leverages uniform estimates on the negative moments of solutions to SHE-type problems to separate the numerator and denominator:

Lemma 4.1
.

There is a finite constant
C=C(t,\kappa)>0
such that
\mathbf{E}|\mathcal{E}_{t,x_{1},x_{2}}|\leq C\left(\mathbf{E}\mathcal{X}_{t,x_{1},x_{2}}^{2}\right)^{1/2}
.

Proof.

By applying Cauchy-Schwarz twice, we have that

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}|\mathcal{E}_{t,x_{1},x_{2}}|\leq\left(\mathbf{E}Z^{\theta}(t,x_{1})^{-4}\right)^{1/4}\left(\mathbf{E}Z^{\theta}(t,x_{2})^{-4}\right)^{1/4}\left(\mathbf{E}\mathcal{X}_{t,x_{1},x_{2}}^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

Accordingly, it suffices to show that for
x\in[0,1]
and
p\geq 2
, it holds that
\mathbf{E}Z^{\theta}(t,x)^{-p}\leq C
for some finite
C=C(p,t,\kappa)>0
. To do so, we compare the negative moments of
Z^{\theta}
to those of the solution
\mathcal{Z}^{\theta}
to (
SHE
0,0
) with
\xi_{\varepsilon}
in place of
\xi
and
\mathcal{Z}^{\theta}_{0}=e^{h_{0}^{\kappa}}
, which are easier to bound. Specifically, if
\mathcal{H}^{\theta}=-\int_{0}^{t}(\xi_{\varepsilon}(t-s,B_{s})-\frac{1}{2}C_{\varepsilon}(B_{s}))ds-h_{0}^{\kappa}(B_{t})
is let to denote the analogue of
H^{\theta}
for this object, then (by Feynman-Kac)
\frac{1}{\mathcal{Z}^{\theta}(t,x)}e^{-\mathcal{H}^{\theta}}
is the density of a Gibbs measure
\mathbb{P}^{\mathrm{Gibbs}}
and we can write

\displaystyle Z^{\theta}(t,x)=\mathcal{Z}^{\theta}(t,x)\mathbb{E}^{\mathrm{Gibbs}}e^{\int_{0}^{t}V_{\varepsilon}(X_{s})ds}.

By Jensen’s inequality, we then have that

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}Z^{\theta}(t,x)^{-p}=\mathbf{E}\left[\mathcal{Z}^{\theta}(t,x)^{-p}\left(\mathbb{E}^{\mathrm{Gibbs}}e^{\int_{0}^{t}V_{\varepsilon}(X_{s})ds}\right)^{-p}\right]\leq\mathbf{E}\left[\mathcal{Z}^{\theta}(t,x)^{-(p+1)}\mathbb{E}^{\mathrm{BM}}_{x}e^{-\mathcal{H}^{\theta}-p\int_{0}^{t}V_{\varepsilon}(B_{s})ds}\right].

Applying Schwarz again, we obtain the bound

(4.2)

\mathbf{E}Z^{\theta}(t,x)^{-p}\leq\left(\mathbf{E}\mathcal{Z}^{\theta}(t,x)^{-2(p+1)}\right)^{1/2}\left(\mathbf{E}(\mathbb{E}^{\mathrm{BM}}_{x}e^{-\mathcal{H}^{\theta}-p\int_{0}^{t}V_{\varepsilon}(B_{s})ds})^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

For the second factor, we notice that

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}(\mathbb{E}^{\mathrm{BM}}_{x}e^{-\mathcal{H}-p\int_{0}^{t}V_{\varepsilon}(B_{s})ds})^{2}=\mathbf{E}\left[\mathbb{E}^{\mathrm{BM}}_{x,x}e^{-\mathcal{H}_{1}^{\theta}-\mathcal{H}_{2}^{\theta}-p\int_{0}^{t}V_{\varepsilon}(B^{1}_{s})ds-p\int_{0}^{t}V_{\varepsilon}(B^{2}_{s})ds}\right],

where
\mathcal{H}_{1}^{\theta}
and
\mathcal{H}_{2}^{\theta}
denote the versions of
\mathcal{H}^{\theta}
corresponding to independent Brownian motions
B^{1}
and
B^{2}
, respectively. By Fubini and the moment generating function for Gaussians, the right-hand side equals

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{\mathrm{BM}}_{x,x}e^{\int_{0}^{t}p^{\mathrm{neu}}(2\varepsilon,B^{1}_{s};B^{2}_{s})ds-p\int_{0}^{t}V_{\varepsilon}(B^{1}_{s})ds-p\int_{0}^{t}V_{\varepsilon}(B^{2}_{s})ds+h_{0}^{\kappa}(B^{1}_{t})+h_{0}^{\kappa}(B^{2}_{t})},

and the proof of Lemma
4.5
(together with further applications of Schwarz) shows that this is bounded by a constant
C=C(t,\kappa)>0
. We have thus reduced the problem to bounding the first factor in (
4.2
), i.e. the negative moments of
\mathcal{Z}^{\theta}
. For this, we appeal to the argument of
[
9
, Theorem 2]
. Namely, it is sufficient to show that we have the following large deviation and comparison lemmas:

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
w(t,x)
be a bounded progressively measurable process. Given
\delta>0
, there exist constants
C_{0},C_{1}>0
(independent of
\varepsilon
) such that for all
\lambda>0
and

,

(4.3)

\mathbf{P}\left(\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\sup_{0\leq x\leq 1}\left|\int_{0}^{t}ds\int_{0}^{1}dy\ p^{\mathrm{neu}}(t-s,x;y)w(s,y)\xi_{\varepsilon}(s,y)\right|>\lambda\right)\leq C_{0}\exp\left(-\frac{C_{1}\lambda^{2}}{T^{1/2-\delta}}\right).

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
Z
and
\tilde{Z}
denote two solutions to
(
SHE
0,0
)
with forcing noise
\xi_{\varepsilon}
and initial data
Z(0,x)=z_{0}(x)
and
\tilde{Z}(0,x)=\tilde{z}_{0}(x)
, respectively. Suppose that,
\mathbb{P}^{\mathrm{init}}

for all

. Then,
\mathbf{P}

for all

.

Since solutions to (
SHE

) with the regularized forcing noise

are strictly positive by Feynman-Kac, the comparison lemma is immediate from linearity. For the large deviations lemma, we note that (
4.3

[
9

with

\displaystyle K_{\varepsilon}^{t,x}(s,y)=\int_{0}^{1}dz\ p^{\mathrm{neu}}(t-s,x;z)p^{\mathrm{neu}}(\varepsilon,z;y)w(s,z).

This kernel satisfies the regularity condition of

\displaystyle\lVert K_{\varepsilon}^{t,x}-K_{\varepsilon}^{s,y}\rVert_{L^{2}(\R_{+}\times[0,1])}

\displaystyle=\lVert e^{\varepsilon\Delta_{\mathrm{neu}}}p^{\mathrm{neu}}(t-\cdot,\cdot;x)w-e^{\varepsilon\Delta_{\mathrm{neu}}}p^{\mathrm{neu}}(s-\cdot,\cdot;y)w\rVert_{L^{2}(\R_{+}\times[0,1])}

by contractivity of the heat semigroup and the boundedness hypothesis on

Lemma 4.4
.

We have that

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\mathcal{X}^{2}_{t,x_{1},x_{2}}=\mathbb{E}^{\mathrm{BM}}_{x_{1},x_{2},x_{1},x_{2}}e^{\int_{0}^{t}\bm{R}_{\varepsilon}(\bm{X}_{s})ds+\int_{0}^{t}\bm{V}_{\varepsilon}(\bm{X}_{s})ds+\bm{h}_{0}^{\kappa}(\bm{X}_{t})}\prod_{j=1}^{2}\int_{0}^{\tau_{j}}R_{\varepsilon}(X^{2j-1}_{s}-X^{2j}_{s})d(X^{2j-1}_{s}-X^{2j}_{s}),

where

.

As a next step in the analysis, we view the factor
e^{\int_{0}^{t}\bm{R}_{\varepsilon}(\bm{X}_{s})ds+\int_{0}^{t}\bm{V}_{\varepsilon}(\bm{X}_{s})ds+\bm{h}_{0}^{\kappa}(\bm{X}_{t})}
from Lemma
4.4
as the weight of a Gibbs measure
\mathbb{P}^{\mathrm{Gibbs}}_{\bm{x}}
on paths
\bm{X}\in C_{\bm{x}}([0,t];\R^{4})
started from
\bm{x}\in\R^{4}
, that is, we define

\displaystyle d\mathbb{P}^{\mathrm{Gibbs}}_{\bm{x}}(\bm{X})=\frac{e^{\int_{0}^{t}\bm{R}_{\varepsilon}(\bm{X}_{s})ds+\int_{0}^{t}\bm{V}_{\varepsilon}(\bm{X}_{s})ds+\bm{h}_{0}^{\kappa}(\bm{X}_{t})}}{\mathbb{E}^{\mathrm{BM}}_{\bm{x}}e^{\int_{0}^{t}\bm{R}_{\varepsilon}(\bm{X}_{s})ds+\int_{0}^{t}\bm{V}_{\varepsilon}(\bm{X}_{s})ds+\bm{h}_{0}^{\kappa}(\bm{X}_{t})}}d\mathbb{P}^{\mathrm{BM}}_{\bm{x}}(\bm{X}).

To see why this is useful, notice that by the classical Feynman-Kac formula the denominator above is
\bm{Z}(t,\bm{x})
for
\bm{Z}
solving the following heat problem on
\R_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{4}
:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\bm{Z}=\frac{1}{2}\Delta\bm{Z}+(\bm{R}_{\varepsilon}+\bm{V}_{\varepsilon})\bm{Z},\quad\bm{Z}(0,\bm{x})=e^{\bm{h}_{0}^{\kappa}(\bm{x})},

(equivalently, since
\bm{R}_{\varepsilon}
,
\bm{V}_{\varepsilon}
, and
\bm{h}_{0}^{\kappa}
are all
\varrho
-invariant in each of their spatial variables, we can replace
\R^{4}
with
[0,1]^{4}
and impose homogeneous Neumann boundary conditions). In particular,
\bm{h}(s,\bm{x}):=\log\bm{Z}(s,\bm{x})
solves

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\bm{h}=\frac{1}{2}\Delta\bm{h}+\frac{1}{2}|\nabla\bm{h}|^{2}+(\bm{R}_{\varepsilon}+\bm{V}_{\varepsilon}),\quad\bm{h}(0,\bm{x})=\bm{h}_{0}^{\kappa}(\bm{x}),

so by applying Itô’s formula to
\bm{h}(t-s,\bm{X}_{s})
with
\bm{X}
given by a Brownian motion on
\mathbb{R}^{4}
started at
\bm{x}
, we deduce that

\displaystyle\bm{h}_{0}^{\kappa}(\bm{X}_{t})=\bm{h}(t,\bm{x})-\int_{0}^{t}(\bm{R}_{\varepsilon}(\bm{X}_{s})+\bm{V}_{\varepsilon}(\bm{X}_{s}))ds+\int_{0}^{t}\bm{U}(t-s,\bm{X}_{s})\cdot d\bm{X}_{s}-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}|\bm{U}(t-s,\bm{X}_{s})|^{2}ds,

where
\bm{U}(s,\bm{x})=\nabla\bm{h}(s,\bm{x})
. It follows that the Radon-Nikodym derivative can be re-written as

\displaystyle\frac{e^{\int_{0}^{t}\bm{R}_{\varepsilon}(\bm{X}_{s})ds+\int_{0}^{t}\bm{V}_{\varepsilon}(\bm{X}_{s})+\bm{h}_{0}^{\kappa}(\bm{X}_{t})}}{\bm{Z}(t,\bm{x})}=e^{\int_{0}^{t}\bm{U}(t-s,\bm{X}_{s})\cdot d\bm{X}_{s}-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}|\bm{U}(t-s,\bm{X}_{s})|^{2}ds},

at which point the Girsanov theorem implies that under
\mathbb{P}^{\mathrm{Gibbs}}_{\bm{x}}
the path
\bm{X}
satisfies the diffusion equation

\displaystyle d\bm{X}_{s}=\bm{U}_{t-s}(\bm{X}_{s})ds+d\bm{B}_{s},\quad\bm{X}_{0}=\bm{x}.

By letting
\bm{U}_{s}(\bm{x})=(U^{1}_{s}(\bm{x}),\ldots,U^{4}_{s}(\bm{x}))
and
\bm{B}_{s}=(B^{1}_{s},\ldots,B^{4}_{s})
, it follows that we can then write

(4.4)

\begin{split}&\frac{\mathbf{E}\mathcal{X}_{t,x_{1},x_{2}}^{2}}{\bm{Z}(t,x_{1},x_{2},x_{1},x_{2})}\\
&\quad\quad\quad=\mathbb{E}^{\mathrm{Gibbs}}_{x_{1},x_{2},x_{1},x_{2}}\prod_{j=1}^{2}\int_{0}^{\tau_{j}}R_{\varepsilon}(X^{2j-1}_{s}-X^{2j}_{s})\left([U_{t-s}^{2j-1}(\bm{X}_{s})-U_{t-s}^{2j}(\bm{X}_{s})]ds+dB^{2j-1}_{s}-dB^{2j}_{s}\right).\end{split}

In particular, if we introduce the notation

\displaystyle A_{j}=\int_{0}^{\tau_{j}}R_{\varepsilon}(X^{2j-1}_{s}-X^{2j}_{s})[U^{2j-1}_{t-s}(\bm{X}_{s})-U^{2j}_{t-s}(\bm{X}_{s})]ds,\quad M_{j}=\int_{0}^{\tau_{j}}R_{\varepsilon}(X^{2j-1}_{s}-X^{2j}_{s})d(B^{2j-1}_{s}-dB^{2j}_{s}),

then (
4.4
) becomes

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbf{E}\mathcal{X}_{t,x_{1},x_{2}}^{2}}{\bm{Z}(t,x_{1},x_{2},x_{1},x_{2})}=\mathbb{E}^{\mathrm{Gibbs}}_{x_{1},x_{2},x_{1},x_{2}}A_{1}A_{2}+\mathbb{E}^{\mathrm{Gibbs}}_{x_{1},x_{2},x_{1},x_{2}}A_{1}M_{2}+\mathbb{E}^{\mathrm{Gibbs}}_{x_{1},x_{2},x_{1},x_{2}}A_{2}M_{1}+\mathbb{E}^{\mathrm{Gibbs}}_{x_{1},x_{2},x_{1},x_{2}}M_{1}M_{2},

which by Cauchy-Schwarz, symmetry, and the fact that
M_{1}M_{2}
is a martingale under the Gibbs measure, is bounded by

(4.5)

\mathbb{E}^{\mathrm{Gibbs}}_{x_{1},x_{2},x_{1},x_{2}}A_{1}^{2}+2\left(\mathbb{E}^{\mathrm{Gibbs}}_{x_{1},x_{2},x_{1},x_{2}}A_{1}^{2}\right)^{1/2}\left(\mathbb{E}^{\mathrm{Gibbs}}_{x_{1},x_{2},x_{1},x_{2}}M_{2}^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

To now estimate this, we invoke the following lemma.

Lemma 4.5
.

Given
T>0
, there is a finite positive constant
C=C(T,\kappa)
such that, for all
t\in(0,T]
and
\bm{x}\in\R^{4}
, we have that

\displaystyle C^{-1}\leq\bm{Z}(t,\bm{x})\leq C\quad\text{and}\quad|\bm{U}(t,\bm{x})|\leq C|\log\varepsilon|

with the latter holding for
\varepsilon
sufficiently small.

Proof.

It is sufficient to find individual positive constants
C_{1}=C_{1}(T,\kappa)
,
C_{2}=C_{2}(T,\kappa)
, and
C_{3}=C_{3}(T,\kappa)
such that
C_{1}^{-1}\leq\bm{Z}(t,\bm{x})\leq C_{2}
and
|\bm{U}_{t}(\bm{x})|\leq C_{3}|\log\varepsilon|
(for
\varepsilon
sufficiently small), as we can then take
C=\max(C_{1},C_{2},C_{3})
. With this in mind, we begin by bounding
\bm{Z}(t,\bm{x})
. By iteratively applying Cauchy-Schwarz to the Feynman-Kac representation of
\bm{Z}(t,\bm{x})
, we see that it suffices to bound the quantities

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{\mathrm{BM}}_{x_{1},x_{2}}e^{\lambda\int_{0}^{t}p^{\mathrm{neu}}(2\varepsilon,X^{1}_{s};X^{2}_{s})ds},\quad\mathbb{E}^{\mathrm{BM}}_{x}e^{\lambda\int_{0}^{t}p^{\mathrm{neu}}(\varepsilon,X_{s};0)ds},\quad\mathbb{E}^{\mathrm{BM}}_{x}e^{\lambda\int_{0}^{t}p^{\mathrm{neu}}(\varepsilon,X_{s};1)ds}

by a constant
C=C(T)>0
for any
\lambda\in\R
. For this, we consider only the first quantity, as an identical argument establishes the same bound for the latter two quantities. Moreover, note that we may as well assume
\lambda>0
, since otherwise we can bound by 1. With this in mind, we expand the exponential and order the time integrations to get

(4.6)

\mathbb{E}^{\mathrm{BM}}_{x_{1},x_{2}}e^{\lambda\int_{0}^{t}p^{\mathrm{neu}}(2\varepsilon,X^{1}_{s};X^{2}_{s})ds}=\sum_{n\geq 0}\lambda^{n}\mathbb{E}^{\mathrm{BM}}_{x_{1},x_{2}}\int_{\Delta_{n}}ds_{1}\cdots ds_{n}\ \prod_{i=1}^{n}p^{\mathrm{neu}}(2\varepsilon,X^{1}_{s_{i}};X^{2}_{s_{i}}),

where
\Delta_{n}=\{0<s_{n}<\cdots<s_{1}<t\}
. Next, we note that since
p^{\mathrm{neu}}
is
\varrho
-invariant in each of its spatial variables, we can replace the underlying Brownian motions
X^{j}
on
\R
with reflecting Brownian motions
\tilde{X}^{j}=\varrho(X^{j})
on
[0,1]
started at
\tilde{x}_{j}=\varrho(x_{j})\in[0,1]
. By then expanding the expectation and iteratively applying Chapman-Kolmogorov together with the bound
p^{\mathrm{neu}}(s,x;y)\leq Cs^{-1/2}
for a constant
C>0
that does not depend on
x
or
y
, one obtains

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{\mathrm{RBM}}_{\tilde{x}_{1},\tilde{x}_{2}}\int_{\Delta_{n}}ds_{1}\cdots ds_{n}\ \prod_{i=1}^{n}p^{\mathrm{neu}}(2\varepsilon,\tilde{X}^{1}_{s_{i}};\tilde{X}^{2}_{s_{i}})\leq C^{n}\int_{\Delta_{n}}ds_{1}\cdots ds_{n}s_{n}^{-1/2}\prod_{j=2}^{n}(s_{j-1}-s_{j})^{-1/2}=\frac{C^{n}(\pi t)^{n/2}}{\Gamma(n/2+1)}.

Substituting this bound into (
4.6
), we obtain a convergent series that is increasing termwise in
t
, so that
\mathbb{E}^{\mathrm{BM}}_{x_{1},x_{2}}e^{\lambda\int_{0}^{t}p^{\mathrm{neu}}(2\varepsilon,X^{1}_{s};X^{2}_{s})ds}\leq C(T)
, as desired. For a lower bound on
\bm{Z}(t,\bm{x})
, we simply observe that since
\bm{R}_{\varepsilon}(\bm{x})\geq 0
for all
\bm{x}\in\R^{4}
and
\bm{h}_{0}^{\kappa}
is bounded (as a smooth function on
\R^{4}
determined by its values on
[0,1]^{4}
), the Feynman-Kac representation implies that
\bm{Z}(t,\bm{x})\geq C(\kappa)\mathbb{E}^{\mathrm{BM}}_{\bm{x}}e^{\int_{0}^{t}\bm{V}_{\varepsilon}(\bm{X}_{s})ds}
, which by Jensen is
\geq C(\kappa)e^{\mathbb{E}^{\mathrm{BM}}_{x}\int_{0}^{t}\bm{V}_{\varepsilon}(\bm{X}_{s})ds}>C(T,\kappa)>0
.

Moving on to
\bm{U}=\nabla\log\bm{Z}
, we begin by noting that since
|\bm{U}_{t}(\bm{x})|^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{4}|U^{i}_{t}(\bm{x})|^{2}
it suffices (by symmetry) to bound just
|U^{1}_{t}(\bm{x})|
by a constant
C=C(T,\kappa)>0
independently of
\bm{x}
. To this end, we note that from the mild from representation of the heat problem satisfied by
\bm{Z}
, we have

\displaystyle\partial_{x_{1}}\bm{Z}(t,\bm{x})=\int_{[0,1]^{4}}\partial_{x_{1}}\bm{p}^{\mathrm{neu}}(t,\bm{x};\bm{y})e^{\bm{h}_{0}^{\kappa}(\bm{y})}d\bm{y}+\int_{0}^{t}ds\int_{[0,1]^{4}}d\bm{y}\ \partial_{x_{1}}\bm{p}^{\mathrm{neu}}(t-s,\bm{x};\bm{y})(\bm{R}_{\varepsilon}(\bm{y})+\bm{V}_{\varepsilon}(\bm{y}))\tilde{\bm{Z}}(s,\bm{y}),

where
\bm{p}^{\mathrm{neu}}(t,\bm{x};\bm{y})=\prod_{j=1}^{4}p^{\mathrm{neu}}(t,x_{j};y_{j})
. By noting that
\int_{0}^{1}\partial_{x}p^{\mathrm{neu}}(t,x_{1};y_{1})dy_{1}=0
, we can re-write the first term with
e^{\bm{h}_{0}^{\kappa}(\bm{y})}-e^{\bm{h}_{0}^{\kappa}(x_{1},y_{2},y_{3},y_{4})}
in place of
e^{\bm{h}_{0}^{\kappa}(\bm{y})}
and apply a Lipschitz bound to get, for constants
C,c>0
,

\displaystyle\left|\int_{[0,1]^{4}}\partial_{x_{1}}\bm{p}^{\mathrm{neu}}(t,\bm{x};\bm{y})e^{\bm{h}_{0}^{\kappa}(\bm{y})}d\bm{y}\right|\lesssim_{\kappa}\int_{0}^{1}|\partial_{x_{1}}p^{\mathrm{neu}}(t,x_{1};y_{1})||x_{1}-y_{1}|dy_{1}\leq Ct^{-1}\int_{\R}|u|e^{-cu^{2}/t}du=\tilde{C}.

As for the second term, our work for
\bm{Z}
implies that it is

\displaystyle\lesssim_{T,\kappa}\int_{0}^{t}ds\int_{[0,1]^{4}}d\bm{y}\ |\partial_{x_{1}}\bm{p}^{\mathrm{neu}}(t-s,\bm{x};\bm{y})|(\bm{R}_{\varepsilon}(\bm{y})+|\bm{V}_{\varepsilon}(\bm{y})|).

We now bound the term obtained here by distributing the integrals onto
\bm{R}_{\varepsilon}(\bm{y})
; an identical argument establishes the same bound for the other term. To this end, we note that by making the change of variables
s\mapsto t-s
, expanding
\bm{p}^{\mathrm{neu}}(s,\bm{x};\bm{y})=\prod_{j=1}^{4}p^{\mathrm{neu}}(s,x_{j};y_{j})
and
\bm{R}_{\varepsilon}(\bm{y})=\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq 4}p^{\mathrm{neu}}(2\varepsilon,y_{i};y_{j})
, and using the fact that
|\partial_{x}p^{\mathrm{neu}}(s,x;y)|\lesssim s^{-1/2}p^{\mathrm{neu}}(2s,x;y)
(this follows from the fact that
|x|\leq e^{x^{2}/4}
for all
x\in\R
, from which it is not hard to see that
|\partial_{x}p(s,x)|\lesssim s^{-1/2}p(2s,x)
; the extension of this identity to
p^{\mathrm{neu}}
is then immediate from the expansion (
1.4
)), this term is bounded (up to a constant) by

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq 4}\int_{0}^{t}ds\ s^{-1/2}\int_{[0,1]^{4}}d\bm{y}\ p^{\mathrm{neu}}(2\varepsilon,y_{i};y_{j})p^{\mathrm{neu}}(2s,x_{1};y_{1})\prod_{k=2}^{4}p^{\mathrm{neu}}(s,x_{k};y_{k}).

At this point, the integrations in
\bm{y}
can be computed exactly via Chapman-Kolmogorov. For instance, in the case
(i,j)=(1,2)
, we get

\displaystyle\int_{[0,1]^{2}}dy_{4}dy_{3}\

\displaystyle p^{\mathrm{neu}}(s,x_{3};y_{3})p^{\mathrm{neu}}(s,x_{4};y_{4})\int_{[0,1]^{2}}dy_{2}dy_{1}\ p^{\mathrm{neu}}(2\varepsilon,y_{1};y_{2})p^{\mathrm{neu}}(2s,x_{1};y_{1})p^{\mathrm{neu}}(s,x_{2};y_{2})

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}p^{\mathrm{neu}}(s+2\varepsilon,x_{2};y_{1})p^{\mathrm{neu}}(2s,x_{1};y_{1})dy_{1}=p^{\mathrm{neu}}(3s+2\varepsilon,x_{1};x_{2}).

In any case, we obtain a bound of
(s+\varepsilon)^{-1/2}
and so
|\partial_{x_{1}}\bm{Z}(t,\bm{x})|\leq C(\kappa)+\tilde{C}(T,\kappa)\int_{0}^{t}s^{-1/2}(s+\varepsilon)^{-1/2}ds
, which for sufficiently small
\varepsilon
is
\lesssim_{T,\kappa}|\log\varepsilon|
. Together with our previous bounds for
\bm{Z}
, we conclude finally that
|U^{1}_{t}(\bm{x})|=\bm{Z}(t,\bm{x})^{-1}|\partial_{x_{1}}\bm{Z}(t,\bm{x})|\lesssim_{T,\kappa}|\log\varepsilon|
, completing the proof.
∎

Using Lemma
4.5
, we now bound the terms appearing in (
4.5
). Beginning with
\mathbb{E}^{\mathrm{Gibbs}}_{x_{1},x_{2},x_{1},x_{2}}A_{1}^{2}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{\mathrm{Gibbs}}_{x_{1},x_{2},x_{1},x_{2}}A_{1}^{2}\lesssim_{t,\kappa}|\log\varepsilon|^{2}\mathbb{E}^{\mathrm{BM}}_{x_{1},x_{2},x_{1},x_{2}}e^{\int_{0}^{t}\bm{R}_{\varepsilon}(\bm{X}_{s})ds+\int_{0}^{t}\bm{V}_{\varepsilon}(\bm{X}_{s})ds+\bm{h}_{0}^{\kappa}(\bm{X}_{t})}\left(\textstyle\int_{0}^{\tau_{1}}R_{\varepsilon}(X^{1}_{s}-X^{2}_{s})ds\right)^{2}.

By applying Cauchy-Schwarz to the second factor and bounding the components separately as we did for
\bm{Z}
in the proof of Lemma
4.5
, we deduce that

(4.7)

\mathbb{E}^{\mathrm{Gibbs}}_{x_{1},x_{2},x_{1},x_{2}}A_{1}^{2}\lesssim_{t,\kappa}|\log\varepsilon|^{2}\left(\mathbb{E}^{\mathrm{BM}}_{x_{1},x_{2}}\left(\textstyle\int_{0}^{\tau_{1}}R_{\varepsilon}(X^{1}_{s}-X^{2}_{s})ds\right)^{4}\right)^{1/2}

provided
\varepsilon
is sufficiently small. As for
\mathbb{E}^{\mathrm{Gibbs}}_{x_{1},x_{2},x_{1},x_{2}}M_{2}^{2}
, by Itô’s isometry we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{\mathrm{Gibbs}}_{x_{1},x_{2},x_{1},x_{2}}M_{2}^{2}=\mathbb{E}^{\mathrm{Gibbs}}_{x_{1},x_{2},x_{1},x_{2}}\int_{0}^{\tau_{2}}R_{\varepsilon}^{2}(X^{3}_{s}-X^{4}_{s})ds,

at which point we can employ the same line of argumentation (i.e. Lemma
4.5
and Cauchy-Schwarz) to obtain

(4.8)

\mathbb{E}^{\mathrm{Gibbs}}_{x_{1},x_{2},x_{1},x_{2}}M_{2}^{2}\lesssim_{t,\kappa}\left(\mathbb{E}^{\mathrm{BM}}_{x_{1},x_{2}}\left(\textstyle\int_{0}^{\tau_{2}}R_{\varepsilon}^{2}(X^{3}_{s}-X^{4}_{s})ds\right)^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

Putting together everything we’ve established so far, we have (by Lemmas
4.1
,
4.5
, and (
4.4
))

(4.9)

\mathbf{E}|\mathcal{E}_{t,x_{1},x_{2}}|\lesssim_{t,\kappa}\left(\tfrac{\mathbf{E}\mathcal{X}_{t,x_{1},x_{2}}^{2}}{\bm{Z}(t,x_{1},x_{2},x_{1},x_{2})}\right)^{1/2}\lesssim_{t,\kappa}\left(\mathbb{E}^{\mathrm{Gibbs}}_{x_{1},x_{2},x_{1},x_{2}}A_{1}^{2}+2\left(\mathbb{E}^{\mathrm{Gibbs}}_{x_{1},x_{2},x_{1},x_{2}}A_{1}^{2}\right)^{1/2}\left(\mathbb{E}^{\mathrm{Gibbs}}_{x_{1},x_{2},x_{1},x_{2}}M_{2}^{2}\right)^{1/2}\right)^{1/2}

for all sufficiently small
\varepsilon
, where the terms on the right-hand side are bounded as in (
4.7
) and (
4.8
). We have thus reduced the problem to estimating

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{\mathrm{BM}}_{x_{1},x_{2}}\left(\textstyle\int_{0}^{\tau_{1}}R_{\varepsilon}(X^{1}_{s}-X^{2}_{s})ds\right)^{4}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{E}^{\mathrm{BM}}_{x_{1},x_{2}}\left(\textstyle\int_{0}^{\tau_{1}}R_{\varepsilon}^{2}(X^{1}_{s}-X^{2}_{s})ds\right)^{2}.

We claim that

(4.10)

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}^{\mathrm{BM}}_{x_{1},x_{2}}\left(\textstyle\int_{0}^{\tau_{1}}R_{\varepsilon}(X^{1}_{s}-X^{2}_{s})ds\right)^{4}\lesssim_{t}\varepsilon^{2}|\log\varepsilon|^{4}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{E}^{\mathrm{BM}}_{x_{1},x_{2}}\left(\textstyle\int_{0}^{\tau_{1}}R_{\varepsilon}^{2}(X^{1}_{s}-X^{2}_{s})ds\right)^{2}\lesssim_{t}|\log\varepsilon|^{2}.\end{split}

Indeed, under
\mathbb{P}^{\mathrm{BM}}_{x_{1},x_{2}}
the process
B=X^{1}-X^{2}
is a variance 2 Brownian motion started at
x=x_{1}-x_{2}
. Accordingly, we can re-write the former as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x}\left(\textstyle\int_{0}^{\tau}R_{\varepsilon}(B_{s})ds\right)^{4},

where now
\tau=\inf\{s\geq 0:B_{s}\in 2{\Z}\}\wedge t
(we have been deliberate to use the notation
\mathbb{E}_{x}
instead of
\mathbb{E}^{\mathrm{BM}}_{x}
since
B
is not of unit variance, but this is rather unimportant). By decomposing
R_{\varepsilon}=R_{\varepsilon}\mathbf{1}_{R_{\varepsilon}\geq\varepsilon}+R_{\varepsilon}\mathbf{1}_{R_{\varepsilon}<\varepsilon}
and noting that for
\gamma=\varepsilon^{1/2}|\log\varepsilon|^{1/2}
there is a constant
C>0
independent of
\varepsilon
so that
R_{\varepsilon}\mathbf{1}_{R_{\varepsilon}\geq\varepsilon}\leq C\varepsilon^{-1/2}\sum_{n\in\Z}\mathbf{1}_{[2n-C\gamma,2n+C\gamma]}
, we deduce that

(4.11)

\mathbb{E}_{x}\left(\textstyle\int_{0}^{\tau}R_{\varepsilon}(B_{s})ds\right)^{4}\lesssim t^{4}\varepsilon^{4}+\varepsilon^{-2}\mathbb{E}_{x}\left(\textstyle\int_{0}^{\tau}\textstyle\sum_{n\in\Z}\mathbf{1}_{[2n-C\gamma,2n+C\gamma]}(B_{s})ds\right)^{4}.

Now, by definition, there are only two intervals of the form
[2n-C\gamma,2n+C\gamma]
that
B
can possibly enter up to time
\tau
. These intervals are determined uniquely by the starting point
x
. To bound the second term, it thus suffices (by Minkowski’s inequality and translation invariance of Brownian motion) to bound
\mathbb{E}_{x}\left(\textstyle\int_{0}^{\tau}\mathbf{1}_{[-C\gamma,C\gamma]}(B_{s})ds\right)^{4}
. To do so, we note that by symmetry and the strong Markov property, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x}\left(\textstyle\int_{0}^{\tau}\mathbf{1}_{[-C\gamma,C\gamma]}(B_{s})ds\right)^{4}\lesssim\mathbb{E}_{0}\left(\textstyle\int_{0}^{\tau_{C\gamma}}\mathbf{1}_{[0,C\gamma]}(B_{s})ds\right)^{4}

for
\tau_{C\gamma}=\inf\{s\geq 0:B_{s}=C\gamma\}\wedge t
. By changing variables
s\mapsto\gamma^{2}s
and applying Brownian scaling, the right-hand side becomes

\displaystyle\gamma^{8}\mathbb{E}^{\mathrm{BM}}_{0}\left(\textstyle\int_{0}^{\tau_{C}}\mathbf{1}_{[0,C]}(\tilde{B}_{s})ds\right)^{4}=\varepsilon^{4}|\log\varepsilon|^{4}\mathbb{E}^{\mathrm{BM}}_{0}\left(\textstyle\int_{0}^{\tau_{C}}\mathbf{1}_{[0,C]}(\tilde{B}_{s})ds\right)^{4},

where
\tilde{B}
is another variance 2 Brownian motion and
\tau_{C}=\inf\{s\geq 0:\tilde{B}_{s}=C\}\wedge(\gamma^{-2}t)
. The same argument used in
[
6
]
implies that
\mathbb{E}^{\mathrm{BM}}_{0}(\int_{0}^{\tau_{C}}\mathbf{1}_{[0,C]}(\tilde{B}_{s}))^{4}\lesssim 1
, so that (
4.11
) becomes

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{\mathrm{BM}}_{x}\left(\textstyle\int_{0}^{\tau}R_{\varepsilon}(B_{s})ds\right)^{4}\lesssim_{t}\varepsilon^{2}|\log\varepsilon|^{4},

which is the first estimate in (
4.10
). The second estimate is proved by an identical argument. Substituting these estimates into (
4.9
), we obtain Proposition
3.1
.
