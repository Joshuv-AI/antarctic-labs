Development and validation of a minimum data set for total joint arthroplasty registries
Authors: Khadijeh Moulaei, Ebrahim Kharazinejad, Akram Hemmatipour, Hadi Kazemi-Arpanahi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTotal joint arthroplasties (TJAs), including total knee (TKA) and total hip (THA), are common treatments for end-stage arthritis, significantly improving patients’ quality of life (QoL). Clinical TJA registries play a critical role in monitoring implant performance, revision surgeries, postoperative complications, and long-term outcomes and patient-reported outcomes (PROs). However, the lack of a standardized data collection framework limits data integration, interoperability, and comparison across healthcare systems and studies. This study aims to establish a minimum data set (MDS) for the development of a TJA registry system. The proposed MDS represents the essential data elements needed to consistently capture key information related to TJA procedures. Establishing this MDS can facilitate greater agreement among stakeholders and support effective patient care, clinical research, and healthcare policy decisions.MethodsThis mixed-method study was performed from December 2023 to March 2024 in two phases. Initially, an extensive literature review, along with the review of existing arthroplasty registries and patients’ medical records, was conducted to identify potential administrative and clinical data elements. The extracted items were then organized into a researcher-developed checklist. In the second phase, a 17-member expert panel refined the data elements through a two-round Delphi survey. Clinical and administrative items were evaluated by orthopedic surgeons and other experts involved in arthroplasty care to ensure their clinical relevance and suitability for comprehensive TJA registry purposes. Data elements with ≥ 80% agreement were included in the final MDS. Finally, Quantitative validity was assessed using the content value index (CVI), Kappa, and content validity ratio (CVR).ResultsA total of 154 data elements related to the TJA procedures (TKA and THA) were identified by literature review. Following expert consensus, 136 data elements that capture preoperative, intraoperative, and postoperative data of the TJA procedures were accepted. Finally, 111 data elements were confirmed through CVI and CVR calculation.ConclusionsThe finalized MDS supports standardized and interoperable data collection across healthcare settings. From a clinical perspective, the TJA-MDS may facilitate outcome monitoring, implant performance evaluation, identification of complications and revision trends, PROs assessment, evidence-based decision-making, multicenter research, quality improvement, and healthcare policy development related to TJA procedures.Keywords: Total Knee Arthroplasty, Total Hip Arthroplasty, Primary and Revision Arthroplasty, Registries, Data management

Introduction

Osteoarthritis (OA) is a degenerative joint condition that causes pain, swelling, and stiffness, affecting a person’s ability to move freely and perform daily activities. It impacts the entire joint, including the surrounding tissues. OA most commonly affects the knees, hips, spine, and hands, and several factors such as joint injury, overuse, older age, and being overweight contribute to its development. Since OA is more prevalent among older people (with about 70% being older than 55), its global prevalence is expected to increase as populations age [1]. Total joint arthroplasty (TJA), including total hip arthroplasty (THA) and total knee arthroplasty (TKA), is a relatively safe and highly effective procedure for severe joint diseases such as OA. It significantly alleviates pain and improves articular function and quality of life (QoL) [2–5]. Both TKA and THA involve substantial economic costs and have become among the most commonly performed orthopedic surgeries in recent decades [6], not only for older adults but also for younger populations. This trend is expected to continue due to factors such as an aging population, rising physical inactivity and obesity rates, increased musculoskeletal problems, more sport-related injuries, and patients’ expectations for active, pain-free lifestyles and better mobility [7–11]. To meet high demand and lower costs, surgeons may perform more TJA surgeries by reducing the duration of each operation [12].

Globally, the number of THA and TKA procedures has increased substantially. More than one million hip and knee arthroplasty procedures are performed annually in the United States, and similar rising trends have been reported in Europe and other high-income countries. For example, national registry data from the United Kingdom (National Joint Registry (NJR) and Sweden demonstrate a continuous annual increase in both primary and revision arthroplasty procedures [13, 14].

International arthroplasty registries such as the NJR, the American Joint Replacement Registry (AJRR), and the Swedish Hip Arthroplasty Register have been established to systematically monitor implant performance, revision rates, and patient outcomes over time. These registries differ in methodological structure, including types of collected data checklists, follow-up intervals, inclusion of patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs), and reporting systems. In addition, variability exists in cost structures and funding models, which may influence data completeness and long-term sustainability across different healthcare systems [15–17].

Given the large and growing numbers of TJA procedures, ongoing analysis of trends and indicators related to TJA surgeries, including PROMs, implant survival rates, and cost-effectiveness, will be of interest to surgeons and health care policymakers [18]. TJA registries are essential tools for addressing this need. Registries systematically collect accurate and reliable data for evidence-based medical decision-making, monitoring real-world outcomes, and improving the quality and safety of surgical care [19, 20]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), a patient registry is a structured collection of standardized data on individuals with a specific condition, procedure, or outcome, gathered over time to serve scientific, clinical, or policy purposes [21, 22]. TJA registries collect comprehensive information on patient demographics, procedure details, implant characteristics, fixation methods, revision rates, early failures, long-term survival, and patient-reported outcomes (PROs) [23–25]. One of the main advantages of TJA registries is that they enable long-term monitoring, identify trends, and provide benchmarks for quality improvement. They also facilitate multi-center research and collaboration between public and private hospitals [26, 27]. However, the lack of standardized data sets, inconsistent data collection, and limited interoperability between systems remain key disadvantages that can hinder the comparability and integration of data across institutions. Differences in recording practices between public and private hospitals can further exacerbate these challenges, leading to data fragmentation and poor data quality [28, 29].

Data quality is a fundamental component of successful registry systems and directly influences the validity, reliability, and usability of clinical findings. High-quality registries require standardized definitions, complete and accurate data entry, regular validation processes, and consistent follow-up mechanisms to minimize missing or inconsistent information. Previous studies have demonstrated that variability in documentation practices and differences in electronic medical record systems can negatively affect registry completeness and comparability across institutions [30, 31]. Therefore, developing a standardized minimum data set (MDS) can contribute substantially to improving data quality by promoting uniform data collection and reducing heterogeneity in registry reporting.

To address these issues, it is crucial to design registries using a well-defined MDS. A core MDS defines the essential data elements that should be collected consistently, helping to overcome heterogeneity and fragmentation in clinical data [32, 33]. The MDS is a set of data elements approved to be collected obligatory at the national level from a scientific viewpoint [34]. One of the important stages in developing the clinical registry was determining a required set of data that would necessitate minimal data entry by physicians [33]. Ahmadi and Mirbagheri [32]., emphasized that establishing national data sets is a major step toward integrating and improving patient data management, facilitating storage, retrieval, and exchange of information within registries.

Although several conventional data collection systems and local hospital databases are currently used for documenting arthroplasty procedures, many of these systems lack standardized structures and are primarily designed for administrative or institutional purposes rather than comprehensive clinical outcome evaluation. In contrast, an MDS-based registry framework provides a harmonized and interoperable structure that facilitates consistent data collection, multicenter comparisons, longitudinal follow-up, and evidence-based assessment of patient outcomes. Accordingly, the use of a standardized MDS is considered more suitable for ensuring data comparability, improving registry integration, and supporting national and international benchmarking initiatives in TJA research and clinical practice [35, 36].

Numerous TJA registries worldwide have developed their own MDSs, but to our knowledge, no standardized and validated core data set has yet been scientifically developed for TJA registries in Iran. Despite the existence of various TJA registries globally, the lack of a standardized and validated MDS tailored to Iran’s healthcare context limits the utility of these databases. Differences in healthcare infrastructure, resource availability, and patient demographics necessitate the development of a locally relevant MDS to ensure accurate and comprehensive data capture. Establishing such a standardized MDS will not only enhance the quality and comparability of data but also facilitate better clinical decision-making, enable more effective monitoring of patient outcomes, and support health policy formulation. This study aims to fill this critical gap by developing and validating a national core data set specifically designed for TJA registries in Iran. Therefore, this study aimed to develop and validate a standardized core MDS for TJA registries in Iran. By reaching consensus on essential administrative and clinical data elements through a rigorous narrative literature review and expert panel process, this MDS can help ensure consistent and comparable data collection across different healthcare sectors.

Methods

Study design

This study is a mixed-method investigation performed from December 2023 to March 2024. To standardize the TJA-MDS, a multi-step process involving narrative literature review, expert validation, and quantitative content assessment was performed to ensure the dataset’s relevance, reliability, and clinical applicability according to the following phases:

Phase 1: identification of TJA-MDS data elements

In this phase, to identify and retrieve possible data elements to be included in the TJA registry, a narrative literature review was conducted by a multidisciplinary team, including surgical nursing faculty members (n = 2), health information management (HIM) (n = 1), and medical informatics (n = 1) specialists. The team brings extensive experience in developing and managing health registries, designing data collection methodologies, ensuring data integrity, and implementing analytics to improve clinical practices. Their combined clinical and technical expertise enriched the review. This team collaboratively identified relevant studies, articles, and reports that met the inclusion criteria.

It should be noted that the literature review was not performed as a formal systematic review; rather, it was designed as a structured and comprehensive search to retrieve relevant resources (peer-reviewed articles, reports, forms, websites, and medical records). The team utilized both peer-reviewed sources and gray literature, such as medical records and TJA-related information systems, to ensure a thorough collection of relevant data elements. It is important to note that the reviewed hospital data sources included both electronic health records (EHRs) and traditional paper-based records. The transition from paper to electronic records introduces differences in data completeness, accessibility, and quality, which were considered when extracting and selecting relevant data elements for the TJA registry.

The search process involved several key steps to maximize rigor and transparency. Initially, we collected data elements from multiple sources, including peer-reviewed articles, clinical guidelines, and relevant databases. Scientific databases were searched using the keywords “minimum data set,” “essential data set,” “core data set,” “clinical registry,” “information management,” “data management,” “total knee arthroplasty,” “total knee replacement,” “total joint replacement,” “total joint arthroplasty,” “total hip arthroplasty,” “total hip replacement,” “THA,” “TKA,” and “TJA,” combined with “OR” and “AND” operators. The inclusion criteria comprised Persian and English publications published between 2000 and 2023. Persian studies were included to ensure that the core data elements reflected local clinical practice and healthcare infrastructure in Iran, while English studies ensured alignment with international best practices. In addition to scientific articles, medical records, forms, and TJA-related information systems were reviewed to capture relevant practical data elements.

All retrieved records were screened and reviewed independently by at least two researchers from our multidisciplinary team, including nurses in surgical departments, orthopedic surgeons, and HIM practitioners. Disagreements were resolved through discussion and consensus meetings. Data items were extracted systematically from the retrieved sources and entered into a checklist for further evaluation. The checklist was refined iteratively by incorporating newly identified data elements throughout the review process. Data collection and analysis continued until no new data elements or themes emerged from the reviewed sources, indicating that data saturation had been reached. Thematic analysis was conducted using an inductive approach. Two researchers independently reviewed the extracted data to become familiar with the content and generated initial codes based on concepts relevant to the study objectives. Through iterative comparison and discussion, related codes were grouped into broader categories and themes. To ensure the trustworthiness of the analysis, we engaged in multiple rounds of structured discussions among the research team. These discussions were held through regular consensus meetings conducted both in-person and via virtual platforms, allowing all members to participate actively regardless of location. During these sessions, participants reviewed the coded data and emerging themes and sub-themes, discussed any discrepancies or ambiguities, and collaboratively refined the categories by merging overlapping themes and confirming that each theme represented a distinct aspect of the data. Differences in coding and theme definitions were resolved through open dialogue and voting when necessary. This iterative process continued until no new themes emerged and the existing themes adequately covered the essential aspects of TJA data elements, at which point thematic saturation was achieved. As this study was not designed as a formal systematic review or meta-analysis, statistical heterogeneity was not assessed. Instead, variations among the included sources were managed through inductive thematic analysis, iterative comparison of extracted data elements, multidisciplinary expert review, and consensus meetings until thematic saturation was achieved.

Finally, an initial list of items for the TJA-MDS was compiled and categorized into a checklist. This checklist, consisting of 154 items, was organized into three main sections based on the preoperative, intraoperative, and postoperative phases of the TJA procedures. Each section included subsets and open-ended questions (“Are there any items you would like to include? “) to suggest new items, ensuring that the checklist remained dynamic and responsive to emerging needs in the field.

Phase 2: Delphi survey

In this step, to validate the initial version of the primary checklist, a two-round Delphi survey was conducted by inviting a panel of experts. An expert is defined as an individual possessing knowledge and skills in a specific field [37]. In Delphi surveys, the selection of experts is a critical step as it directly influences the quality of the results obtained. Although there are no universally accepted criteria for determining expertise, selecting a diverse group of experts from various geographical regions and disciplines tends to yield more comprehensive outcomes than panels restricted to a single domain [38–40]. In our study, diversity was ensured through purposive sampling by recruiting experts from multiple disciplines, including orthopedic surgeons, surgical nursing faculty members, and registered nurses (RNs). Such a representative panel is likely to provide more balanced and insightful feedback than a homogenous group from the same field [41, 42]. Typically, Delphi panels include between 15 and 20 members [43]; in this study, we selected 17 experts to reduce the margin of error and ensure robust consensus. The inclusion criteria for panel members were: (1) sufficient expertise in TJA surgery; (2) at least three years of relevant practical experience; and (3) relevant educational qualifications and, when possible, scientific publications or professional work experience related to the field. The Delphi survey and consensus meetings were carefully designed to balance methodological rigor with feasibility. Each Delphi round required 30–45 min per expert, with a two-week interval between rounds for independent review. Consensus meetings lasted 60–90 min and were conducted in-person and virtually. All 17 experts actively participated, with structured reminders ensuring high engagement. Costs were limited to coordination, data compilation, and communication, managed internally without financial compensation. This planning ensured the process was efficient, feasible, and methodologically robust.

The selection of TJA-MDS items was based on identifying variables that are important and meaningful for clinical research. The initial list of potential items was compiled and summarized into a checklist designed to elicit panelists’ opinions during the qualitative content review. Each data element was scored by the expert panel using a four-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (“irrelevant”) to 4 (“extremely relevant”). To minimize subjectivity and bias in the Delphi process, several measures were implemented. Experts provided their ratings independently, without seeing other panel members’ responses, reducing the influence of dominant opinions. Each item was rated using a standardized 4-point Likert scale to ensure uniformity. In the second Delphi round, experts received anonymized summaries of the first-round results, allowing them to reconsider their judgments in light of the group’s feedback. Furthermore, inclusion of items was based on predefined thresholds (≥ 80% agreement and mean score ≥ 3), ensuring that retained items reflected collective expert consensus rather than individual subjective judgment. Throughout the survey, participants were blinded to each other’s responses to minimize bias. Additionally, they were permitted to suggest new items or provide comments beyond the preliminary checklist, ensuring that the survey remained dynamic and comprehensive. To systematically manage feedback and minimize potential biases in the Delphi process, several measures were implemented. All feedback, including suggested new items or comments, was collected using a standardized checklist and open-ended questions to ensure consistency. The research team reviewed all qualitative feedback independently. Comments or suggestions outside the scope of the study or unrelated to TJA-MDS items were documented but not incorporated. During structured consensus meetings, ambiguous or off-topic feedback was discussed, and only relevant, objective, and frequently suggested items were included in the revised checklist for the next Delphi round. To further reduce halo and dominance effects, experts provided their ratings independently, without access to other panel members’ responses or identities, preventing undue influence from any single participant. Each item was rated using a standardized 4-point Likert scale, and anonymized summaries of the first-round results were shared in the second round, allowing experts to reconsider their responses based on collective feedback without revealing individual opinions. These combined steps ensured that the Delphi process was systematic, objective, and reliable, effectively incorporating constructive feedback while minimizing bias, thereby enhancing the validity and robustness of the final TJA-MDS.

We considered the time interval between each Delphi step to be two weeks. After this stage, the data collection tool was modified according to the experts’ responses. The panel members in the second round were also those participants in the first round of Delphi. Although no specific rule is available for considering the threshold value of agreement in Delphi studies, some studies have suggested an agreement level of ≥ 70% as acceptable for each Delphi round [39, 44, 45]. In our study, consensus was defined a priori as ≥80% agreement among experts combined with a mean score of ≥3.0 on a 4-point Likert scale. Items meeting both criteria were accepted for inclusion in the TJA-MDS. Items with <50% agreement and a mean score of <3.0 were excluded, whereas items with 50–79% agreement or a mean score below the consensus threshold were retained for re-evaluation in the subsequent Delphi round [46, 47]. In the second round of Delphi, the opinions and feedback provided by the experts in the first stage were integrated into the initial items. In the second round, the criteria for accepting data elements were the same as in the first round. Newly proposed data elements were also carried forward and assessed using the same consensus criteria. Finally, the collected data were analyzed using SPSS 22 software (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL). Following the Delphi phase, a preliminary TJA-MDS was developed for quantitative assessment, consisting of three main categories and 136 items.

Phase 3: content validity

To ensure the rigor, reliability, and methodological soundness of the TJA-MDS, a systematic approach was applied prior to the quantitative assessment of content validity. The Delphi process was carefully structured to minimize bias and maximize expert input. Experts provided independent, anonymized ratings using a standardized Likert scale, preventing undue influence from dominant individuals (halo or dominance effects) and ensuring that individual opinions did not sway group judgment. All feedback, including suggested new items and comments, was collected using standardized checklists and open-ended questions, reviewed independently by the research team, and discussed during structured consensus meetings. Only feedback that was relevant, aligned with study objectives, or suggested by multiple experts was incorporated into the revised checklist for subsequent rounds. Each content validity round required 45–60 min per expert, with a one-week interval between rounds, and consensus meetings lasted 60–90 min, conducted in-person and virtually to maximize participation. These procedures ensured high engagement, efficient time management, and low resource burden. Through this multi-step process, the MDS items were standardized and refined systematically, providing a reliable and balanced foundation for subsequent quantitative content validity assessment using CVI, CVR, and modified Kappa coefficients. The content validity of the TJA-MDS was assessed through a structured multi-step process, including Delphi expert review, management of feedback, and quantitative evaluation using CVI, CVR, and Kappa coefficients. In addition, to minimize the possibility of artificial consensus, the Delphi process was conducted anonymously, and controlled feedback was provided between rounds. Consensus was predefined as ≥ 80% agreement, and the stability of responses was assessed before terminating the Delphi rounds.

Calculate Content Value Index (CVI)

CVI was done for the initial MDS formed in the previous steps. CVI was used based on the Waltz and Basel content validity index. Moreover, the CVI technique was used to confirm the content validity of the checklist. CVI is measured for each item individually (I-CVI) or for the entire set of items (S-CVI) in an instrument to ensure the validity of its construct content. This measure quantitatively determines the level of agreement among raters for content relevance. This index is graded using a 4-point Likert scale of 1 = not relevant, 2 = somewhat relevant, 3 = quite relevant, and 4 = extremely relevant. Then, for each item, the I-CVI is calculated as the number of experts giving a rating of 3 or 4 divided by the number of experts, i.e., the proportion in agreement on relevance. A CVI threshold of 0.78% was previously considered acceptable (formula1) [48–51]. In our study, CVI was calculated by sending the TJA-MS developed after the Delphi phase to the panel of experts selected in the Delphi phase. Experts were asked to rate each item on a 4-point Likert scale. A CVI score was calculated for each item, and an acceptable score should be greater than 0.8. Participants were given seven days to provide feedback.

Kappa statistic coefficient

The Kappa statistic is a commonly used measure to assess inter-rater agreement. However, the CVI offers advantages over Kappa and other similar measures by focusing solely on relevance. Calculating the CVI involves determining the probability of chance agreement is NOT calculated as part of CVI; rather, it is used later for Kappa adjustment using a specific formula. The next step is to calculate the I-CVI for each item, and then derive the modified Kappa (k*) from both the probability of chance agreement (Pc) (formula2). The Fleiss, Cicchetti, and Sparrow standards recommend a value of 0.75 or higher as excellent for the Kappa coefficient. This coefficient is an indicator designed to eliminate the possibility of chance agreement among multiple raters [52]. In this study, formula 2 was used to calculate the probability of random agreement, and formula 3 was used to compute the Kappa coefficient for each item. The Kappa coefficient, represented as K*, is interpreted as follows: values exceeding 0.74 indicate excellent item relevance, while those within the 0.6 to 0.74 range imply good relevance and are thus retained. To calculate the CVR, the final TJA-MDS from the previous stage is again voted by the experts to rate the necessity of each item using a 3-point Likert scale. The selected panel of experts for the current stage remains in line with the previous stages, meaning that the experts chosen for the Delphi phase have also been utilized for the present phase.

Through this iterative process of literature review, expert consensus, and quantitative validation, the final TJA-MDS was standardized and ensured to include essential, relevant, and reliable items suitable for both clinical and research use. While the TJA-MDS items were carefully validated through expert consensus and quantitative assessments, actual data entry, completeness, and accuracy in clinical practice were not assessed in this study. Evaluating real-world documentation represents an external validation, which will be addressed in future implementation studies. However, during the Delphi rounds, experts were asked to comment on the clarity, feasibility, and potential challenges of recording each item, indirectly supporting data quality considerations in the design phase.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis in this study, grounded in the Delphi methodological framework and the psychometric theories of content validation proposed by Waltz and Bausell (1981) and Lawshe (1975), employed descriptive and consensus-based analyses to systematically quantify expert agreement and assess item-level validity. Delphi consensus was determined through agreement rates (≥ 80%) and mean relevance scores (≥ 3) rather than hypothesis testing, while content validity was evaluated using the CVI, CVR, and modified Kappa (K*) indices to measure relevance, essentiality, and inter-rater reliability. Exploratory comparisons across expert subgroups (profession, experience, education) were performed to ensure balanced consensus and mitigate bias. To ensure analytic rigor and prevent potential sources of analytical bias, several verification procedures were implemented throughout the validation process. All quantitative indices were calculated strictly according to the standard formulas recommended by Lawshe (1975) and Polit and Beck (2006). The accuracy and consistency of all calculations were verified through double data entry and independent cross-checking by two researchers; in cases of discrepancy, values were rechecked and recalculated to ensure precision. The stability and internal consistency of the results between Delphi rounds were also examined to confirm analytical robustness. All analyses were conducted in SPSS version 22 (IBM Corp., Chicago, IL, USA) with p < 0.05 where applicable. These methodological and verification procedures ensured transparency, minimized the possibility of analytic bias, and strengthened the overall rigor and validity of the TJA-MDS development process.

Results

The primary outcome of this study was the development and validation of a final TJA-MDS through expert consensus and content validity assessment. Secondary outcomes included the level of agreement among experts, item reduction across Delphi and validation phases, and the demographic characteristics of the expert panel.

After a thorough literature review, we identified and compiled 185 data elements to create a data pool. These items were then meticulously scrutinized and modified. As a result, an initial checklist was created consisting of 154 data elements categorized into three main classes: preoperative, interoperative, and postoperative.

Discussion

This study aimed to establish a MDS that provides consensus-based and consistent data elements on a registry system for TJA procedures. In contract with scientific evidence, patient medical records, and arthroplasty registries, as well as surveillance systems, our MDS was classified into preoperative, inter-operative, and postoperative Sects [54, 55]. Our developed MDS allowed structured collection of TJAs data in an inclusive and timely manner at the point of care. In other words, this structured data collection tool has allowed surgeons to capture TJAs data efficiently and effectively and also at a rapid pace in parallel to clinical practice. It is important to note that the consensus reflects the expertise diversity of the panel, which included orthopedic surgeons, nursing faculty, and HIM specialists, enhancing the clinical applicability of the selected data elements. By leveraging a Delphi survey that involved multidisciplinary participants with varying standpoints on TJAs, a consensus was achieved on a required set of items to enable assessing and comparing the TJA outcomes.

The implementation of this MDS facilitates the integration of data collection efforts, thereby enabling the comparison of results and fostering more substantial, consolidated analyses across various studies and research groups. When comparing our MDS to existing international TJA registries such as NJR, AJRR, and the Swedish registry, we observe shared core elements but also unique features that balance comprehensiveness and feasibility. These differences reflect regional clinical priorities and resource availability, which influence the design and focus of each registry. In the realm of TJAs, a significant volume of data is generated daily within clinical settings. In such a big data environment, the emphasis shifts from the mere collection of data to a focus on the depth and analytical robustness of the variables collected. This approach is critical for validating or refuting hypotheses and answering specific research questions [56, 57]. Consequently, in our study, the components of the TJA-MDS were predominantly those applicable to clinical research, emphasizing data elements that support robust analysis and evidence generation. This focus underlines the schema’s relevance and utility not only within the Iranian healthcare context but also its potential adaptability and applicability to other settings with similar healthcare structures or research needs. The balance achieved between standardization and flexibility enhances the generalizability of the MDS, promoting consistent, high-quality data collection that can facilitate meaningful comparisons and collaborations internationally.

With regard to data collection processes, registry-based studies emphasize that both the definition of data elements and the feasibility of routine clinical documentation are critical determinants of data quality and completeness. Evidence from previous arthroplasty registry research suggests that overly complex datasets may reduce long-term data completeness due to variability in EMR systems and clinical workload constraints [58, 59]. Therefore, the present MDS was intentionally designed as a structured and feasible core dataset to ensure consistent point-of-care data capture while minimizing documentation burden. In comparison with established international registries such as the NJR, AJRR, and the Swedish Arthroplasty Register, our MDS aligns with core domains including demographic data, surgical variables, implant characteristics, complications, and PROs, while differences in granularity reflect local healthcare priorities and resource variability [15, 60]. Regarding comorbidities, previous surgeries, and prior treatments, these variables are widely recognized as important predictors of postoperative outcomes; however, in this study they were not included as fully detailed standalone categories in order to maintain feasibility and avoid excessive data collection burden. Instead, essential risk-related clinical indicators were retained, and these variables are recommended for inclusion in future expanded or modular registry designs to support more detailed risk stratification and longitudinal outcome analysis [61, 62].

Importantly, the selected variables in the proposed MDS were not treated as equally weighted items; rather, they were conceptually stratified based on their clinical importance and role in outcome assessment. Core variables included essential demographic, surgical, and implant-related data that are fundamental for registry comparability and outcome benchmarking, whereas additional variables such as extended clinical characteristics were considered supporting or supplementary items. This prioritization is consistent with established arthroplasty registry methodologies, where core datasets are distinguished from optional or extended datasets to balance clinical relevance and feasibility [15, 60]. Furthermore, Delphi-based consensus approaches commonly emphasize frequency of agreement and clinical relevance to define variable importance and hierarchy MDSs [63].

Moreover, the development of a standardized MDS for TJA registries plays a pivotal role not only in clinical data management but also in shaping public health policy and guiding healthcare system development. By providing a clear framework for consistent data collection and interoperability, this MDS supports evidence-based decision-making at both clinical and policy levels. Standardization facilitates reliable aggregation and comparison of data across institutions and regions, which is essential for monitoring healthcare outcomes, optimizing resource allocation, and designing targeted interventions. Ultimately, this framework contributes to the advancement of healthcare quality and patient safety, while informing strategies for sustainable health system development and public health planning.

The basis of cooperation and data sharing between registry systems hinges on the standardization of data elements. Although each registry system may aspire to capture the most intricate details, in reality, this proves challenging due to insufficiently structured and documented information, as well as the inconsistency in extracting uniform data from various electronic medical record (EMR) systems. The average time for experts to complete each Delphi questionnaire was approximately 20–30 min, which was effective in maintaining engagement. While our study did not directly analyze differences between public vs. private hospitals or surgeon volume, these factors are recognized as impactful and warrant further investigation. Therefore, it is advisable to devise an MDS- a comprehensive collection suitable for all registries - incorporating not only valuable but also more precise categories or levels for documenting data. This approach aims to enhance the efficiency of recording and extracting data from EMRs, as highlighted in reference [64].

The efficacy of TJA procedures has historically been gauged in some literature based on metrics such as implant survivorship, range of motion, and radiographic outcomes. However, these criteria have proven inadequate for fully capturing the patient’s QoL improvements and overall functional gains post-surgery. Recent studies have underscored the importance of incorporating patient experiences to more accurately reflect the effectiveness of therapeutic interventions [65, 66]. In response to this insight, TJA registries have broadened their data collection scope to include PROs. This expansion aims to deepen the understanding of surgical motivations and identify trends that indicate progress in patient care. By pinpointing specific patient characteristics that influence PROs, healthcare providers can better assess risk factors associated with patient dissatisfaction and tailor interventions accordingly [67]. The systematic collection and analysis of uniform data on TJA outcomes facilitate ongoing patient monitoring and yield insights that can refine surgical practices, optimize resource allocation, and improve personalized care pathways [68]. Risk stratification based on patient comorbidities and functional status is crucial for predicting outcomes such as readmission and complications. Although this study included key clinical indicators for risk assessment, linking registry data with longitudinal outcomes remains an important area for future research to fully realize the clinical impact of registries.

Importantly, the proposed MDS is not only a data collection framework but also has direct clinical applicability in routine orthopedic practice. By standardizing the capture of key perioperative and outcome-related variables, the MDS supports real-time clinical decision-making, including early identification of high-risk patients, monitoring of postoperative complications, and evaluation of implant performance. This structured information flow can improve communication between clinicians and registry systems and enable timely feedback that may influence perioperative planning and postoperative management strategies. Furthermore, the availability of consistently collected data enhances the ability of clinicians and healthcare administrators to benchmark performance, identify variations in care, and implement targeted quality improvement interventions. Therefore, the clinical relevance of the proposed MDS extends beyond registry development and directly contributes to improving patient management, surgical planning, and healthcare quality improvement in TJA practice.

The harmonization of registry systems offers significant benefits in facilitating more meaningful analysis and comparison of practice trends, medical device performance, and patient outcomes across institutions and regions. This integration enhances the generation of real-world evidence and enables the design of registry-based investigations into TJA. Moving forward, such investigations hold the potential to complement and enhance the evidence derived from randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and prospective cohort studies, providing a broader and more nuanced understanding of patient outcomes in diverse clinical settings. By leveraging the wealth of data amassed through harmonized registry systems, researchers and clinicians can gain deeper insights into the effectiveness, safety, and cost-effectiveness of TJA procedures in real-world clinical practice, ultimately leading to improved patient care, optimized treatment protocols, and more informed healthcare policy decisions.

Strengths and Limitations

Our study’s strengths are highlighted by the successful implementation of a two-round Delphi agreement survey. The response rate of panel experts was 89%, which is higher than the recommended response rate of 70% for each round in Delphi studies [39]. The use of open-ended questions in the first round of the Delphi phase was a strategic approach to ensure comprehensive coverage and consideration of all potential items, reflecting our commitment to thoroughness and inclusivity in capturing a wide array of perspectives and expertise.

The anonymity of responses played a crucial role in maintaining the objectivity of the survey, ensuring that all opinions and suggestions were evaluated equally and without bias. This methodology facilitated a significant refinement of the initial items, narrowing them down to 111 key items in the TJA-MDS. The acceptable agreement levels suggested in Delphi surveys vary, with some suggesting 66% [69], 75% [70], and below 78% [71]. However, in our study, we considered an agreement level of 80% to be acceptable, which aligned with similar studies using Delphi survey methods [46, 47, 72].

This streamlined set of items, although extensive, is justified by the complexity of TJAs and the diverse range of medical and surgical treatment options, underscoring the necessity for a broad and comprehensive data collection framework to support meaningful data analysis. Moreover, a major strength of this study is the establishment of a consistent and approved dataset for TJA, which lays the groundwork for collecting data on progressively larger patient cohorts in the future. The MDS framework is designed to aggregate a vast amount of data from various settings, enabling the application of data-driven analyses through artificial intelligence (AI) techniques. This capability is poised to enhance the understanding and treatment of TJA, offering a robust tool for clinical and research advancements. Moreover, the rigorous adherence to the Delphi methodology, characterized by two rounds of anonymous voting among experts, is a cornerstone of this study’s strength. This methodological rigor allowed for the iterative refinement of content, including the adjustment and addition of critical information and the elimination of redundant or unnecessary elements, ensuring the relevance and quality of the MDS.

Our study faces several limitations that warrant consideration. Firstly, the development of the MDS was primarily informed by the consensus of expert physicians from Khuzestan hospitals, located in the south of Iran, limiting its widespread validation and application. While the MDS is grounded in the best available evidence and collective expertise, its scope may not encompass all potential data elements critical for comprehensive analysis, although efforts were made to include core data elements and their corresponding values. Another significant limitation stems from the potential lack of representativeness and diversity in the input received, as most variables in the MDS reflect the perspectives of expert surgeons. This focus may inadvertently narrow the breadth of data, sidelining inputs from other vital stakeholders like hospital managers, public health officers, or implant manufacturers. Such a limitation could affect the MDS’s applicability across different clinical settings and demographic profiles, despite our efforts to ensure its broad relevance. Additionally, the exclusion of patient participation in the study’s development phase may have led to the oversight of crucial PROs. Acknowledging this gap, we aim to incorporate patient perspectives in future enhancements of the MDS, particularly focusing on capturing key PROs that are vital for a holistic understanding of treatment outcomes.

Despite these challenges, the study has successfully established a consistent and vetted dataset for TJA, promising the facilitation of data collection for increasingly large patient cohorts in the future. The TJA-MDS’s capacity to aggregate extensive data across varied settings lays a solid foundation for leveraging AI techniques for data-driven analyses, marking a significant stride towards advancing TJA research and practice. Moving forward, addressing these limitations will involve extending collaboration to a broader spectrum of stakeholders and integrating patient insights, particularly concerning PROs, to enrich the dataset and enhance its utility and applicability in diverse clinical and research contexts. One of the methodological strengths of this study was the effort to minimize artificial consensus during the Delphi process by maintaining anonymity, providing controlled feedback, and using predefined statistical criteria for consensus determination.

Conclusions

This study developed a standardized MDS for total TJA registry systems through a structured process consisting of a comprehensive literature review and a two-round Delphi consensus. A total of 136 candidate data elements were identified, of which 111 achieved quantitative consensus and were retained in the final MDS. The finalized data set includes demographic, clinical, surgical, implant-related, and follow-up information across preoperative, intraoperative, and postoperative phases of care. By defining a unified structure for data collection, the proposed MDS can support consistency in registry reporting, improve data comparability between institutions, and facilitate interoperability across healthcare systems. The MDS may also assist registry developers, policymakers, and healthcare organizations in designing and implementing more effective TJA registries that support clinical evaluation, outcome monitoring, and quality improvement initiatives.
