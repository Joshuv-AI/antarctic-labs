Multistage nanomedicine engineering to overcome sequential barriers to glioblastoma treatment: a review
Authors: Gayeon Kim, Jaewoo Lee, Joo-Hwan Park, Kwang-Min Kim, Dongwoo Khang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGlioblastoma (GBM) is one of the most lethal primary brain tumors in adults. Intravenously administered therapeutics must cross successive barriers, such as the blood–brain barrier (BBB), heterogeneous tumor microenvironment, and dense extracellular matrix, before achieving pharmacologically relevant concentrations at the cellular level. Nanomedicines with a single function (e.g., BBB penetration) have consistently shown limited efficacy in clinical trials, and thus, elevated the need for an integrated multistage delivery paradigm. This review conceptualized recent advances in engineered nanomedicines for glioblastoma within a three-step delivery framework. Specifically, step 1 involved BBB traversal: endogenous transcytotic pathways, adaptive protein-corona programming, biomimetic nanocarriers, and BBB modulation techniques (e.g., focused ultrasound with microbubbles, photodynamic therapy, and chemotherapeutic permeabilizers). Step 2 involved tumor targeting and accumulation: ligand-decorated and cell-mediated carriers, tumor-penetrating peptides, and magnetically guided drug accumulation. Step 3 involved on-site activation: endogenous-responsive linkers (pH, redox, hypoxia, and protease) and Boolean logic-gated nanodevices that synchronize payload release with spatiotemporal tumor-microenvironment cues, as well as exogenous triggers (e.g., light, ultrasound, and magnetic fields). This review evaluates the preclinical efficacy, safety, and translational potential of the approaches, highlighting how multifunctional nanoplatforms can integrate at least two stages to achieve synergistic therapeutic effects. While clinical trials exhibit promising pharmacokinetics, significant hurdles remain regarding manufacturing, regulatory approval, and immunogenicity, which involves immune recognition and the rapid clearance of nanocarriers. For these reasons, this review outlines strategies to overcome GBM barriers and suggests that multistage integration is essential for developing effective, next-generation precision nanotherapies.Graphical abstract Keywords: Glioblastoma, Blood–brain barrier, Multistage nanomedicine, Biomimetic nanoparticles, Focused ultrasound-mediated BBB opening, Tumor-penetrating peptides, Stimuli-responsive/logic-gated drug release, Precision nanotherapy

Introduction

Background

Glioblastoma, IDH-wildtype, WHO grade 4 (GBM) is the most common and aggressive malignant primary brain tumor in adults. Defined by the 2021 WHO Classification of Tumors of the Central Nervous System, this malignancy is characterized by rapid progression, high infiltrative capacity, and profound therapeutic resistance [1, 2].

The current clinical management of GBM involves maximal safe surgical resection, followed by concurrent radiotherapy and chemotherapy with temozolomide, as established by the Stupp protocol [3]. Despite advances in tumor treatment, this protocol remains standard of care [3, 4]. However, this protocol has shown limited improvements in overall survival (median survival time: 15 months) and quality of life, primarily because of the inherent resistance mechanisms within GBM cells and difficulties in achieving therapeutic drug concentrations at the tumor site [3–5]. For example, cellular heterogeneity and diffuse infiltration contribute to therapeutic resistance and poor treatment outcomes [1].

One of the most formidable challenges in GBM treatment is the blood–brain barrier (BBB), a selectively permeable barrier comprising tightly sealed endothelial cells, pericytes, and astrocytes that restricts systemic drug delivery to the central nervous system (CNS) [5]. Another critical challenge is the blood–tumor barrier (BTB), which, although more permeable than the intact BBB, still hinders the attainment of therapeutic drug concentrations and leads to uneven and limited drug distribution within GBM lesions due to its heterogeneous vascular structure [6]. In addition to the BBB and BTB, the complex tumor microenvironment (TME) formed by GBM tumors, characterized by hypoxia, immune suppression, extensive extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling, and physical barriers, further restricts drug penetration and efficacy [6, 7]. These challenges make it difficult for standard chemotherapies and targeted molecular therapies to achieve effective intratumoral concentrations, leading to treatment resistance and disease recurrence [5, 6].

Recent advancements in nanomedicine offer promising avenues for overcoming these critical barriers. Nanoparticles (NPs) offer numerous benefits for GBM drug delivery. They can improve the solubility and bioavailability of poorly soluble drugs, enabling selective targeting via ligand or antibody conjugation to the nanoparticle surface [8]. Building on these capabilities, NP-based drug delivery systems have been designed to traverse the BBB, target GBM cells, and control the therapeutic payloads [6, 9]. NP surface modification strategies, including receptor-specific ligands, biomimetic coatings, and stimuli-responsive elements, have demonstrated considerable potential in preclinical models [6, 10, 11]. However, the clinical translation of NP-based therapies remains challenging due to inadequate BBB penetration, suboptimal targeting specificity, insufficient intratumoral accumulation, and uncontrolled or premature payload release. These challenges are further compounded by concerns about long-term toxicity, genetic safety, and other potential risks, which limit the clinical applicability of these therapies [9, 12].

Addressing these complex delivery barriers for GBM treatment requires a comprehensive multistage strategy that sequentially overcomes critical biological hurdles. In this review, we propose a systematic three-step approach: [1] enhancing BBB and BTB penetration [2], ensuring precise GBM targeting and effective drug accumulation in tumors, and [3] enabling responsive and controlled intratumoral therapeutic activation. This framework extends beyond structural nanoparticle engineering alone to incorporate adjuvant physical modalities, such as focused ultrasound, photodynamic approaches, and magnetic guidance. These complementary systems are essential to facilitate barrier modulation, improve tumor-selective accumulation, and provide spatiotemporal control over therapeutic activation. This integrative approach addresses the multifaceted obstacles associated with GBM treatment, distinguishing our strategy from conventional single-step targeting methodologies. By reviewing recent advances, limitations, and emerging solutions at each stage, we highlight the need for a cohesive multistage nanomedicine platform to significantly improve clinical outcomes in GBM therapy.

Search strategy and selection criteria

To provide a broad yet logically structured overview, the literature search for this review was conducted using PubMed and Web of Science. Foundational studies published since 2010 were first screened using keyword combinations such as (“nanomedicine” OR “nanoparticle”) AND (“blood-brain barrier” OR “glioblastoma”). Greater emphasis was then placed on experimental studies published from 2019 to 2025 that explicitly described “multi-step” targeting strategies, in order to capture the recent shift toward integrated delivery systems. Recent clinical trial reports were also included to reflect the current translational landscape. As a narrative review, this article does not aim to systematically catalog all published studies; instead, it organizes and discusses the literature most relevant to the paradigm of multistage nanotherapeutics.

Biological and pharmacokinetic barriers in GBM

Vascular barriers: BBB and BTB in GBM

The BBB is a highly specialized vascular interface that envelops the cerebral capillaries and strictly regulates the exchange of substances between the systemic circulation and CNS, thereby preserving CNS homeostasis (Fig. 1) [13]. Structurally, the BBB is primarily composed of non-fenestrated brain capillary endothelial cells interlinked by tight adherens junctions. These endothelial cells, in conjunction with pericytes, astrocytic end-feet, and the surrounding basement membrane, constitute the neurovascular unit. Unlike peripheral endothelial cells, brain endothelial cells lack fenestrations and possess highly developed intercellular junctions, which effectively restricts paracellular and transcellular diffusion [13–15]. Furthermore, the luminal surface of these cells carries a negative charge, impeding the adhesion and transport of negatively charged immune cells and molecules, including nucleic acid-based therapeutics [13].

Multilevel Biological Barriers that Hinder Effective GBM Therapies

Due to its selective permeability, the BBB only permits passive diffusion of small lipophilic molecules with molecular weights below 600 Da, and over 98% of small-molecule therapeutics are ineligible [13]. Although this barrier protects the brain from toxins and pathogens, it significantly impedes the delivery of pharmacological agents to the brain [13, 16]. Additionally, the endothelial cells of the BBB express high levels of multidrug resistance transporters (e.g., P-glycoprotein and Breast Cancer Resistance Protein (BCRP)) that actively efflux therapeutic compounds back into the bloodstream, limiting intracerebral drug accumulation [17].

The pathological progression of GBM induces the formation of a structurally and functionally distinct vascular barrier, the BTB. This barrier arises from the partial disruption of the BBB, primarily driven by tumor-mediated angiogenesis and alterations in the TME [18]. GBM cells secrete pro-angiogenic factors (e.g., vascular endothelial growth factor) that stimulate the development of aberrant vasculature. The resulting neovasculature is characterized by impaired endothelial junctions, widened intercellular clefts, fenestrations, and disorganized basement membrane. These structural abnormalities permit the extravasation of macromolecules, chemotherapeutic agents, and nanodrugs that would otherwise be blocked by an intact BBB [14, 19].

GBM is highly invasive; at the invasive margins, the structural integrity of the BBB is largely preserved, resulting in limited disruption of the BBB. Consequently, the spatial heterogeneity of BTB permeability, characterized by an intact BBB at the invasive margins and leaky vasculature at the tumor core, leads to uneven drug distribution [15, 19]. Combined with high levels of multidrug resistance within GBM cells, this further limits the overall therapeutic efficacy.

The vascular barrier comprises the blood-brain barrier (BBB), characterized by tight junctions and efflux transporters, and the blood-tumor barrier (BTB) with abnormal permeability. Simultaneously, the TME presents physical obstacles, including dense extracellular matrix and elevated interstitial fluid pressure, alongside biochemical challenges such as hypoxia, acidosis, and ROS. These barriers synergistically restrict the transport, accumulation, and stability of therapeutic agents within the tumor.

Tumor microenvironment barriers

Physical barrier

The TME of GBM is a substantial barrier to therapeutic delivery, primarily due to alterations in its physical structure. One of the most critical physical impediments is the formation of a dense and rigid ECM with a markedly different composition and mechanical properties from normal brain tissues. The ECM is highly enriched with fibrous structural proteins, including collagen, laminin, and fibronectin, as well as glycosaminoglycans (GAGs), such as hyaluronic acid (HA) and proteoglycans, resulting in increased matrix density and stiffness [20]. HA content is increased by up to four-fold in GBM, contributing to an expansion of the ECM volume from approximately 20% in normal brain tissue to nearly 48% in tumor regions [21].

This aberrantly accumulated ECM forms a physical barrier that impedes the penetration of therapeutic agents into the tumor core, substantially reducing treatment efficacy [22]. Negatively charged GAGs within the ECM electrostatically interact with charged therapeutic agents, including NPs, thereby reducing their diffusion coefficients and limiting their interstitial transport. This effect is particularly pronounced for positively charged therapeutics, whose mobility is significantly hindered by such interactions owing to the overall negative charge of the ECM. In GBM, where the ECM is densely packed and rich in GAGs, these interactions can restrict penetration into the central regions of the tumor, leading to peripheral accumulation of therapeutic agents [23].

Pathologically elevated interstitial fluid pressure (IFP) is another critical factor that limits effective drug delivery in GBMs. This increase is primarily driven by abnormal tumor vasculature, excessive angiogenesis, defective lymphatic drainage, rapid tumor cell proliferation, and solid stress generated by densely packed tumors and stromal cells [24, 25]. These factors lead to interstitial fluid accumulation and increased tissue pressures. Elevated IFP suppresses the convective transport of therapeutic agents from the vasculature into the tumor parenchyma and induces outward-directed interstitial fluid flow toward the peritumoral space. This flow impedes inward drug transport and promotes the washout of therapeutic agents into adjacent normal tissues. These biophysical barriers severely hinder the homogeneous distribution and therapeutic efficacy of systemically administered drugs [24–26].

Biochemical barrier

The TME of GBM creates biochemically unfavorable conditions that hinder the efficacy of therapeutic agents. Hypoxic regions are common in GBM owing to its rapid proliferation and abnormal vascular architecture, which upregulates the expression of hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α) [27]. HIF-1α promotes angiogenesis and enhances glycolysis, leading to excessive lactate production and accelerated TME acidification. The extracellular pH of GBM ranges from 6.8 to 5.9, which is significantly lower than the interstitial pH of normal brain tissues at approximately 7.1 [28]. This hypoxic and acidic environment within the TME suppresses immune cell activation and infiltration, facilitating immune evasion and enhancing tumor survival [28, 29].

Furthermore, reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels increase in GBM cells in response to the activation of various antioxidant systems. Key molecules such as glutathione (GSH), glutathione S-transferase P1, and NAD(P) quinone dehydrogenase 1 detoxify ROS and protect tumor cells. These antioxidant mechanisms protect GBM cells from therapy-induced oxidative stress, thereby diminishing the cytotoxic effects of anticancer drugs [30, 31].

Moreover, proteolytic enzymes such as matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) and cathepsins are overexpressed in the TME of GBM. These enzymes contribute to increased tumor invasiveness, enhanced immune evasion, and a higher risk of post-treatment recurrence [32]. In addition to these biochemical factors posing a therapeutic barrier individually, their interactions further limit drug absorption, accumulation, activation, and retention, thereby collectively reducing the treatment efficacy. Therefore, a comprehensive understanding of the biochemical characteristics of the TME and the development of strategies to overcome these barriers are essential for effective GBM therapy.

Necessity of multimodal strategies in glioblastoma treatment

Therapeutic resistance in GBM arises from a complex network of biological, physical, and chemical barriers that act in a spatiotemporally coordinated manner. For effective treatment, therapeutic agents must first cross the BBB and be precisely delivered to tumor cells within the brain parenchyma [6]. However, even if a drug successfully penetrates the BBB, the pathological characteristics of GBM, such as elevated interstitial pressure and dense abnormal ECM, limit intratumoral penetration and homogenous distribution [25, 26, 33].

Therefore, an active targeting strategy that ensures tumor-specific accumulation is required, beyond BBB traversal. GBM is a highly invasive tumor; tumor cells in the infiltrative margins are difficult to eradicate using monotherapy alone. In addition, inter-patient genetic and phenotypic heterogeneity further reduces the efficacy of single-target strategies [6, 33].

Although considerable research has focused on overcoming the BBB, BBB penetration alone does not guarantee therapeutic specificity in the CNS. Inadvertent toxicity to normal brain tissue can occur, highlighting the necessity for strategies that precisely target GBM cells while sparing the surrounding healthy tissues [6].

Moreover, the acidic and hypoxic TME exacerbates GBM progression and can impair drug efficacy, even after delivery [27, 28, 30]. To address this challenge, stimulus-responsive therapeutic systems that are activated specifically within the TME have recently been developed. These systems enhance therapeutic efficacy while minimizing the off-target toxicity [34–36].

Therefore, monotherapies targeting a single pathway are insufficient for achieving sustained therapeutic outcomes. Instead, a multistep therapeutic strategy that integrates BBB penetration, tumor-specific targeting, and activation in response to GBM-specific microenvironmental cues should be developed as a new paradigm for GBM therapy. From this perspective, novel nanocarrier strategies can overcome the BBB and selectively accumulate at the lesion site while avoiding normal brain tissues. Finally, therapeutic agents should ideally be released in response to GBM-specific signals, such as low pH, elevated ROS, increased MMP activity, and localized hypoxia. Incorporating such responsive strategies can further enhance the therapeutic efficacy. Drug delivery systems with a stepwise design capable of navigating biological barriers in a spatiotemporal manner need to be developed for the successful treatment of GBM.

Step 1 – engineered strategies to traverse the BBB

The BBB is a major obstacle to effective drug delivery in GBM, and failure to overcome it renders downstream strategies ineffective. Thus, overcoming these barriers is a critical first step in the design of effective therapies. This section reviews engineered approaches categorized into two main strategies: BBB penetration, which harnesses endogenous transport mechanisms and cell-mediated or cell-derived delivery systems, and BBB modulation, which transiently disrupts barrier integrity using focused ultrasound (FUS) with microbubbles (MBs), photodynamic/photothermal therapy, and chemical agents (Fig. 2).

BBB penetration strategies

Ligand- and peptide-mediated strategies for BBB transcytosis

One of the key strategies to enable NP-based therapeutics to reach the BBB is the exploitation of the brain’s endogenous transport mechanisms, namely receptor-mediated transcytosis (RMT), adsorptive-mediated transcytosis (AMT), and carrier-mediated transport (CMT) [37]. These highly selective pathways facilitate the uptake of essential nutrients and biomolecules across BBB [16, 38]. By engineering NP surfaces with ligands or peptides that engage these native transport systems, especially RMT and AMT, researchers have developed highly targeted and efficient platforms for drug delivery to the central nervous system [37, 38].

Receptor-mediated transcytosis (RMT)

RMT is an ATP-dependent active transport mechanism that facilitates the translocation of macromolecules across the BBB via specific interactions between ligands and receptors expressed on the surface of brain microvascular endothelial cells (BMECs) [39, 40]. Upon binding to receptors on the luminal membrane, ligand-functionalized NPs are mainly internalized through clathrin-mediated endocytosis, trafficked through endosomal compartments, and ultimately released into the brain parenchyma via the fusion of transport vesicles with the abluminal membrane [39–41]. This pathway is particularly suited for delivering macromolecular therapeutics, including peptide hormones, growth factors, enzymes, and NP-based systems (e.g., liposomes). Representative target receptors include the transferrin receptor (TfR), insulin receptor, low-density lipoprotein receptor (LDLR), and LDLR-related protein 1 (LRP1), all of which are highly expressed in BMECs [39]. Owing to its high specificity and capacity for macromolecular transport, RMT has been extensively investigated as an NP-based strategy for targeted drug delivery to the CNS [42].

Optimizing ligand selection is critical for enhancing drug delivery across the BBB and minimizing off-target distribution to peripheral organs [43, 44]. In a comparative study of mRNA-loaded lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) functionalized with brain-targeting peptides, namely, rabies virus glycopeptide (RVG) 29, T7, Angiopep-2, and mApoE, RVG29 demonstrated the highest therapeutic efficacy, while mApoE exhibited the most favorable brain-to-liver delivery ratio [44]. These results highlight that to reduce systemic side effects and improve targeting precision, effective ligand selection should consider receptor affinity and abundance at the BBB and tissue-specific expression patterns [43].

This comparative study highlighted the necessity of addressing endosomal escape after NP internalization via RMT. Although the T7-functionalized LNPs targeting the TfR showed the highest accumulation in brain tissue, their therapeutic efficacy was lower than that of the unmodified (or non-ligand-modified) nanoparticle groups, possibly due to entrapment in endosomal compartments [44]. To overcome this limitation, a study developed a pH-responsive polyethylene glycol (PEG)-engager TfR antibody that rapidly dissociates in the acidic endosomal environment of BMECs. This modification promoted efficient abluminal release of NPs, significantly improving brain delivery [45].

The study concluded that RMT is a promising strategy for the precise and noninvasive delivery of therapeutics to the BBB [45]. However, several critical limitations of this study must be acknowledged. These include a restricted number of targetable receptors on BMECs, concurrent expression in peripheral organs leading to off-target effects, and inefficient drug release caused by excessive ligand–receptor binding affinity. Addressing these challenges is a prerequisite for translating RMT-based nanocarriers into clinically viable platforms capable of achieving efficient and reproducible drug delivery to the brain [37, 45].

Adsorptive-mediated transcytosis (AMT)

In addition to RMT, AMT provides a complementary receptor-independent pathway for NP transport across the BBB. AMT is driven by non-specific electrostatic interactions between cationic ligands (e.g., polycationic peptides and polymers) and negatively charged endothelial membrane components (e.g., heparan sulfate proteoglycans) [46, 47]. Representative AMT agents include cell-penetrating peptides (CPPs) and positively charged carriers (e.g., chitosan or polyethyleneimine) that facilitate transcytosis [46–48].

The efficacy of CPP-based AMT has been demonstrated in several preclinical models. In a study, chitosan- and RVG-functionalized Pluronic NPs exhibited superior brain accumulation and protein delivery compared to RVG-only formulations, highlighting the role of electrostatic interactions in enhancing BBB transport [48]. Although AMT lacks receptor specificity, its independence from receptor expression and flexibility in molecular design make it broadly applicable for CNS drug delivery. However, this non-specificity, coupled with the absence of GBM-specific targeting moieties, substantially increases the risk of off-target drug distribution [46].

The combined application of RMT and AMT strategies has demonstrated a synergistic potential for enhancing brain-targeted drug delivery. For example, liposomes co-functionalized with transferrin and TAT peptides exhibited improved permeability and enhanced neuronal gene transfection in an in vitro BBB model, with in vivo brain accumulation reaching 7.7% of the injected dose [49]. These results highlight that the dual functionalization of NPs with both RMT and AMT ligands is a promising strategy for enhancing BBB permeability and therapeutic efficacy in CNS-targeted applications [47, 50].

Nutrient-mimetic strategies: carrier-mediated transport(CMT)

CMT uses solute carrier proteins in brain endothelial cells to actively or passively transport essential polar compounds, typically molecular weight less than 500 Da, including glucose, amino acids, vitamins, and monocarboxylates [14, 51]. Among the most frequently targeted CMT systems are GLUT1, LAT1, MCT1, and SMVT, which recognize glucose, large neutral amino acids, monocarboxylic acids, and vitamins, respectively [52–54]. These transporters are asymmetrically distributed on the luminal or abluminal membranes of endothelial cells, enabling the directional influx or efflux of their substrates [55, 56].

CMT mechanisms are characterized by high substrate specificity and saturable kinetics owing to competition at binding sites [57]. To exploit these properties, NPs can be surface-functionalized with transporter-specific nutrient-mimetic ligands that structurally and chemically resemble endogenous substrates to facilitate selective transporter engagement and enhance BBB translocation via facilitated diffusion [14].

Although transporter expression is essential for CMT-based brain delivery, the subcellular localization of transporters on the luminal and abluminal membranes of endothelial cells significantly influences the transcytosis efficiency. Although key transporters, such as LAT1 and GLUT1, are expressed on both membranes, they typically exhibit an asymmetric distribution, with higher expression on the luminal side. For instance, LAT1 expression is approximately two-fold higher on the luminal membrane than on the abluminal membrane [52, 58]. This polarized distribution suggests that targeting luminal transporters alone may lead to intracellular trapping, without effective release into the brain parenchyma. Therefore, understanding transporter orientation and membrane polarity is critical when designing CMT-based nanocarriers [51, 52].

Recent studies have demonstrated that the use of multivalent ligands in CMT strategies can significantly enhance BBB penetration efficiency. Functionalized nanocarriers co-loaded with glucose, amino acids, and vitamin-mimicking ligands have been engineered to simultaneously target multiple transport systems, leading to better cellular uptake and transcytosis than with single-ligand formulations [54, 55]. In a study, dual-ligand NPs targeting glucose and amino acid transporters using glucopyranose and alanine, respectively, achieved a 293% increase in uptake by brain endothelial cells relative to non-targeted particles. In contrast, NPs functionalized with either alanine or glucopyranose alone showed increases of 131% and 130%, respectively. Both in vitro BBB models and in vivo experiments further confirmed superior drug permeability and brain accumulation in the dual-target groups [54]. These results strongly suggest that simultaneously engaging multiple transporter pathways may exert a synergistic effect, offering a promising strategy for enhancing brain-specific drug delivery.

CMT offers several advantages, including high substrate specificity, metabolic compatibility and low systemic toxicity [16, 57, 59]. However, its inherent limitations must be considered. CMT systems exhibit saturable transport kinetics governed by Michaelis–Menten dynamics, indicating that uptake does not scale linearly with drug concentration [41]. CMT is an endogenous pathway primarily responsible for transporting essential nutrients in the brain. Thus, transporters may compete for binding to both drugs and natural substrates, leading to saturation at high drug doses and a sharp decline in the drug delivery efficiency [57]. Therefore, although CMT is a promising platform for BBB-targeted delivery, its effective application requires careful optimization of ligand selection, density, transporter specificity, and physiological compatibility.

Protein-corona programming

Upon entering into the biological environment, NPs rapidly adsorb serum proteins onto their surfaces, forming a protein corona that fundamentally alters their physicochemical properties and biological identity. This corona plays a pivotal role in determining the in vivo fate of NPs, particularly in BBB-targeted applications, where it often hinders delivery efficiency. Opsonin adsorption can obscure surface ligands, disrupt receptor-mediated targeting, and accelerate clearance by the reticuloendothelial system, collectively reducing BBB permeability [60, 61]. Several studies have shown that transferrin-functionalized NPs lose their receptor-binding ability following corona formation, resulting in a marked decline in brain delivery [62, 63]. This effect appears to be context-dependent: while in vitro, corona formation fully abrogates targeting, in vivo, coronas enriched with apolipoproteins, such as ApoA-I, partially preserve BBB transport [63].

These findings have led to a conceptual shift, viewing the corona not merely as a barrier but as a modifiable interface that can be programmed to enhance targeting. For example, ligand-free PEGylated porous silica NPs have been shown to spontaneously adsorb ApoE and albumin in vivo, enabling BBB penetration via the LRP1 and adsorption-mediated transcytosis pathways, respectively, resulting in effective tumor accumulation in glioblastoma models [64].

If NP surfaces are appropriately engineered to selectively adsorb specific proteins during plasma circulation, the resulting corona formation could exert even more potent effects. For example, micellar nanoprobes functionalized with the WES peptide have been shown to selectively recruit ApoE into circulation, thereby enabling RMT via LDLR to actively traverse the BBB and achieve drug delivery [65]. Similarly, the introduction of a β25–35 peptide sequence (CGSNKGAIIGLM) on liposomal surfaces facilitated the in vivo adsorption of ApoA1, ApoE, and ApoJ, thereby improving brain-targeted delivery. Additionally, hollow covalent organic frameworks modified with the T10 peptide could selectively bind to transferrin (Tf) in vivo, forming a Tf-enriched corona that promoted BBB penetration and glioma targeting via TfR-mediated transport [66].

Collectively, these examples demonstrate that the precise modulation of NP surface chemistry or incorporation of bioresponsive functional elements can direct the formation of beneficial protein coronas, which, in turn, can be harnessed for receptor-specific BBB transport. Ultimately, this approach reframes the protein corona from a biological barrier to a programmable interface for targeted drug delivery to the brain [64–66].

Biomimetic nanocarriers using cell membranes

Biomimetic nanocarriers use naturally derived or engineered cell membranes to replicate the key features of endogenous cells, thereby enhancing BBB transport through multivalent and context-specific mechanisms.

Cell membrane-coated nanoparticles (CMNPs)

Cell membrane-coated nanoparticles (CMNPs) are a bioinspired drug delivery platform that utilizes the native protein–lipid composition of cellular membranes to facilitate BBB penetration. Unlike conventional receptor-mediated strategies, CMNPs simultaneously achieve immune evasion, prolonged circulation, and multivalent interactions with endothelial targets, resulting in enhanced barrier traversal [67].

Macrophage-derived membranes, which contain integrins such as VLA-4 and Mac-1, serve as representative examples of this strategy. These surface molecules facilitate robust adhesion to inflamed endothelial cells by interacting with ICAM-1 and VCAM-1, thereby mimicking the physiological mechanisms of leukocyte extravasation in neuroinflammatory conditions [68]. This mechanism has been observed in natural killer (NK) cells. The engagement of NK cell surface integrins LFA-1 and VLA-4 with ICAM-1 and VCAM-1, respectively, can disrupt the BBB, thereby enhancing NK cell transmigration [69].

BMEC-derived coatings use homotypic endothelial interactions to mediate BBB penetration without relying solely on specific receptor–ligand binding. Compared with the uncoated controls, the BMEC-coated NPs exhibited nearly fourfold higher transcytosis efficiency of up to 38% in in vitro BBB models. This enhancement arises from multiple uptake mechanisms, including clathrin- and caveolin-mediated endocytosis, macropinocytosis, and direct membrane fusion [70].

CMNPs integrate multiple functions, including immune evasion, enhanced translocation across the BBB, and tumor-targeting capabilities [69]. For example, NK cells can traverse the BBB and selectively target GBM cells by recognizing stress-induced, germline-encoded ligands upregulated in tumor cells via the activation of receptors (e.g., NKG2D, DNAM-1, and NKp30) [69]. However, the cell membrane coating strategy faces a major limitation: NPs are frequently only partially covered by membranes. Partial membrane coating results from mechanical damage during extrusion and is further aggravated by incomplete membrane adsorption and limited membrane fluidity, which impair self-repair and reduce targeting efficiency [71]. Although the introduction of exogenous phospholipids to increase membrane fluidity and promote complete fusion has recently been proposed, further validation of coating uniformity, stability, and biocompatibility is necessary before clinical applications [71].

Exosome- and ectosome-based nanocarriers

Exosomes are 30–150 nm extracellular vesicles secreted by the fusion of multivesicular bodies with the plasma membrane. These cells retain the native lipid bilayer and integral membrane proteins of their parent cells, including CD9, CD63, CD82, integrins, and the major histocompatibility complex (MHC) molecules, which confer high biocompatibility, immune evasion, and low immunogenicity [72, 73].

Compared with synthetic membrane-mimetic NPs, exosomes offer better structural fidelity and functional preservation, avoiding issues such as membrane destabilization and protein misorientation. This enables efficient drug loading, particularly through passive methods, in which therapeutic agents are incorporated during biogenesis without disrupting the membrane integrity [74].

Exosomes have been actively investigated for their ability to traverse the BBB through various strategies, including nanoscale size, RMT, and membrane fusion [75]. Their high biocompatibility and low immunogenicity are significant advantages in biomedical applications. Additionally, the phospholipid bilayer confers structural stability, enabling exosomes to circulate even in harsh TMEs [76].

Ectosomes, also referred to as microvesicles, are extracellular vesicles shed directly from the plasma membrane and range in size from 100 nm to 1000 nm. Unlike exosomes, which are generated via the endosomal pathway, ectosomes bud outward from the cell surface and carry membrane proteins and cytosolic components that reflect the characteristics of their parent cells [77]. Although ectosomes are less studied than exosomes, they have recently been reported to possess an intrinsic ability to traverse the BBB. For instance, erythrocyte-derived ectosomes undergo caveolin-dependent and adsorptive-mediated transcytosis across in vitro BBB models, concomitantly reducing tight junction integrity and facilitating barrier passage [77, 78]. Ectosomes can be rapidly produced in large quantities under inflammatory conditions owing to their relatively simple biogenesis [79]. However, the broad size heterogeneity poses challenges for standardization and quality control [80].

Although a study has confirmed the ability of exosomes and ectosomes to traverse the BBB, they still face limitations in efficiently crossing it. Accordingly, surface engineering approaches to enhance their targeting capabilities are being studied [81].

BBB modulation strategies

NP-based systems designed to penetrate the BBB have advanced significantly through surface engineering and biomimetic design. However, their efficacy remains limited by the constraints of endogenous transport mechanisms such as receptor saturation, variable expression, and pathological impairment [37]. In this context, a complementary approach involves the active modulation of BBB permeability through physical, biochemical, or pharmacological means. These BBB modulation strategies seek to transiently and reversibly disrupt the endothelial barrier, thereby enhancing the paracellular or transcellular transport of therapeutics, particularly macromolecular drugs or NPs that are otherwise excluded [82, 83].

Focused ultrasound with microbubbles (FUS with MBs)

The combination of FUS and MBs constitutes a highly targeted, noninvasive strategy for transient and reversible disruption of the BBB to facilitate drug delivery. This technique involves cavitation, wherein intravenously administered gas-filled MBs oscillate or collapse upon exposure to ultrasonic waves, generating shear forces and local pressure fluctuations within the cerebrovascular endothelium [82, 84]. These mechanical effects lead to the temporary separation of inter-endothelial junctions, thereby increasing paracellular permeability [84].

By sensitizing the BBB to ultrasound-induced mechanical effects, MBs enable barrier disruption at significantly lower acoustic intensities, thereby minimizing the tissue damage. In this capacity, MBs act as acoustic amplifiers, enhancing localized drug accumulation while reducing off-target exposure and systemic toxicity [84, 85]. Furthermore, the physical properties of MBs—such as their size, morphology, and shell composition—can affect their vascular dynamics and responsiveness to ultrasound. Nonspherical MBs have demonstrated improved margination toward the endothelium and extended circulation times, collectively enhancing BBB permeability and intratumoral drug deposition [86].

Repeated FUS + MB treatments have side effects, such as inflammatory responses, microhemorrhages, and suppression of neural activity. However, preclinical studies have shown that these effects are transient and tend to recover spontaneously when low-intensity ultrasound is used [84]. In contrast, high-intensity ultrasound can cause serious side effects, such as BBB disruption and tissue necrosis, indicating the need for careful consideration of intensity settings [87]. To ensure safe and reproducible clinical translation, current efforts are directed toward optimizing MB design, developing real-time acoustic feedback systems, and engineering multifunctional MB–drug hybrid platforms [82, 88].

Photodynamically triggered BBB modulation

Photodynamic therapy (PDT) is a minimally invasive light-based treatment that selectively damages target tissues by irradiating photosensitizers (PSs) with light of specific wavelengths to generate and accumulate ROS. Traditionally, PDT has been used as an adjuvant therapy following the surgical resection of malignant gliomas. However, recent studies have demonstrated that PDT can transiently and regionally open the BBB [89], enhancing the penetration of macromolecular drugs and NP-based therapeutics into the brain. These findings suggest the potential of PDT as a diagnostic and therapeutic platform for CNS diseases, including GBM [89, 90].

The PDT-mediated opening of the BBB is a complex biological process that involves multiple pathways. Upon light activation, the PS transitions to a triplet state, generating ROS (e.g., singlet oxygen and hydroxyl radicals). ROS induce cytoskeletal rearrangement, cellular rounding, and microtubule depolymerization in endothelial cells, leading to the expansion of intercellular gaps [89–91]. Additionally, β-arrestin-1 expression is upregulated following PDT treatment, possibly as a response to PDT-induced oxidative stress and localized hypoxia. β-arrestin-1 is involved in destabilizing junctional complexes by promoting the internalization of tight and adherens junction proteins, such as claudin-5 and VE-cadherin [90]. These mechanisms facilitate the translocation of macromolecular tracers, such as FITC-dextran (70 kDa) and GM1-liposomes, across the BBB into the brain parenchyma. This increase in permeability was reversible within several hours [89, 90].

Recent technological advancements have significantly improved the efficiency and clinical applicability of PDT for BBB disruption. Traditionally, craniotomy is essential for light delivery because of the limited optical transparency of the skull [89]. However, a recently developed ultrasound-skull optical clearing agent chemically suppresses light scattering in the skull and enhances its transparency, enabling noninvasive laser irradiation of deep brain regions [90]. In addition, nanotechnology-based strategies have been used to improve the targeted loading efficiency of PS, maximize photoactivation in deep tumor regions, and develop oxygen-independent therapeutic approaches that effectively generate ROS even under hypoxic conditions [92, 93].

Nevertheless, several critical challenges remain in the clinical application of PDT. Key issues include the in vivo safety and long-term stability of PSs, physical limitations of light penetration through the skull, off-target effects due to nonspecific ROS generation, and reduced therapeutic efficacy in the hypoxic TME [92, 94].

Chemically induced BBB permeability enhancement

Chemical modulation offers a pharmacologically versatile strategy for transiently enhancing BBB and BTB permeability, thereby improving drug delivery to the CNS. These approaches typically target endothelial junctions, osmotic gradients, or receptor-mediated signaling to facilitate both paracellular and transcellular transport [95, 96].

Representative strategies include the use of hyperosmotic agents, vasoactive receptor agonists, and molecular modulators that target junctional proteins [95–99]. Recent advances have highlighted the transcriptional modulation of tumor-associated endothelial cells using agents such as indirubin derivatives, which selectively enhance BTB permeability while preserving normal BBB function (Table 1) [100].

Chemical modulation of the BBB is a promising approach for enhancing drug delivery to the CNS; however, it is often accompanied by significant limitations and safety concerns. For example, transient BBB disruption using mannitol can trigger pro-inflammatory cytokine pathways, resulting in sterile inflammatory responses and inflammation of the brain parenchyma [97]. NG291, which functions by inhibiting hERG II potassium channels, is associated with an elevated risk of cardiac arrhythmia. Similarly, peptide f1-C5C2 exhibited reduced reversibility with prolonged exposure, raising concerns regarding its long-term safety and tolerability [96, 99].

Additionally, although 6’-bromoindirubin-3’-acetoxime has demonstrated preclinical potential for increasing BTB permeability, it is highly hydrophobic. Although PPRX-1701-based NPs have been proposed to overcome this limitation and improve delivery efficiency, the underlying mechanism of action remains unclear [100].

Efforts have been made to translate these chemical BBB modulation strategies into clinical applications; however, transformative clinical outcomes have yet to be achieved. For example, although Regadenoson showed high BBB permeability in preclinical models, it fell short of achieving the expected enhancement in drug concentrations within the human brain during clinical trials. This discrepancy is likely due to differences in receptor expression between animal models and humans, suboptimal dosing, and an incomplete understanding of the mechanisms underlying BBB opening [98]. Ultimately, the clinical translation of chemical BBB modulation requires a balanced integration of pharmacodynamic precision and translational relevance across species.

Step 2 – tumor targeting and accumulation

Although overcoming the BBB is a critical first step in CNS drug delivery, successful BBB translocation alone does not ensure therapeutic efficacy in GBM. Once this barrier is overcome, drug carriers must contend with a heterogeneous and suppressive TME. Therefore, step 2 strategies focus on enhancing intratumoral accumulation, diffusion, and cellular uptake to achieve a uniform and effective distribution throughout the glioblastoma tissue [33].

Ligand-mediated tumor targeting

Ligand-functionalized NPs use high-affinity ligands, such as small molecules, peptides, aptamers, or monoclonal antibodies, to bind biomarkers that are overexpressed on GBM cells and stromal components. These receptor-mediated interactions enhance tumor accumulation, penetration, and intracellular drug delivery (Fig. 3A) [6, 101–104].

Stage 2 Strategies for Tumor-Selective Accumulation in GBM

Cancer cell targeting

After successfully crossing the BBB, NPs can be selectively directed towards GBM cells by targeting receptors specifically overexpressed on their surface. This approach enhances drug delivery to tumor sites while minimizing off-target toxicity to healthy brain tissue [6, 105]. Representative surface targets include the epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) and its mutant variant EGFRvIII, interleukin-13 receptor alpha 2 (IL-13Rα2), TfR, and folate receptor [6, 106].

Among these, IL-13Rα2 is notable for its high tumor selectivity, as it is minimally expressed in normal brain tissue but overexpressed in over 70% of GBM cells [6, 105, 107]. IL-13Rα2 functions as a decoy receptor with limited downstream signaling, which minimizes interference with physiological IL-13 pathways and makes it a particularly safe and specific target for glioblastoma therapy [108]. NPs conjugated with IL-13 peptides have shown improved cellular uptake and tumor accumulation, thereby significantly enhancing their therapeutic efficacy [105].

EGFRvIII, a mutant form of EGFR that is absent in the normal brain but expressed in approximately 30% of GBM tumors, also serves as a prominent molecular target [109]. Although EGFRvIII lacks the ligand-binding domain of the wild-type receptor, it remains constitutively active and exposes a unique extracellular epitope that can be specifically recognized by antibody- or peptide-modified nanocarriers for selective GBM targeting [109].

Furthermore, transferrin and folate receptors are frequently used to promote receptor-mediated endocytosis of NPs because of their association with the high metabolic activity of GBM cells. TfR is concomitantly highly expressed in BBB endothelial cells, making it a dual target for BBB penetration and tumor-specific drug delivery [6, 107].

Tumor microenvironment targeting

TME-targeting strategies aim to enhance drug penetration and broaden therapeutic efficacy across the entire tumor mass by targeting non-neoplastic cells and ECM components surrounding GBM [6]. Common molecular targets include integrins and fibronectin. The alternatively spliced isoform of fibronectin, extra domain B (EDB)-fibronectin, is highly overexpressed in tumor-associated neovasculature and ECM, rendering it an ideal ligand-binding site for TME-specific drug delivery. Targeting EDB with aptide–drug conjugates has demonstrated potent tumor-suppressive activity and minimal systemic toxicity in GBM models [110].

Integrins, such as αVβ3, are overexpressed in invasive GBM and angiogenic endothelial cells, emphasizing their potential as therapeutic targets. NP functionalization with the Arginine-Glycine-Aspartic acid (RGD) peptides facilitates selective binding to integrins, promoting their accumulation in tumor tissues [6]. This targeting mechanism is applicable to tumor-penetrating peptides, as discussed in later sections.

Additionally, chlorotoxin, a peptide derived from scorpion venom, selectively accumulates in tumor regions by binding to MMP-2 or chloride ion channels expressed on the surface of tumor cells. Although originally developed for imaging applications, chlorotoxins are currently under active investigation as targeting ligands in NP-based drug delivery systems [111].

Moreover, a bispecific targeting strategy was proposed to simultaneously recognize the fibronectin extra domain B and the C-isoform of tenascin C. The PL1 peptide, with the amino acid sequence PPRRGLIKLKTS, exhibited a binding affinity for both targets. PL1-functionalized NPs showed strong tumor accumulation in mouse glioblastoma xenograft models [112].

Multi- and dual-ligand targeting strategies for enhanced GBM drug delivery

Receptor-mediated targeting enables the selective delivery of therapeutics to specific cellular compartments of GBM. Although some markers are expressed in a niche-specific manner, others are shared across multiple tumor components, providing opportunities for dual-targeting strategies. Effective GBM treatment requires the successful BBB penetration and subsequent accumulation within the tumor mass [6, 33]. Multi-ligand-based targeting strategies have emerged as promising approaches to address these challenges. Formulations incorporating ligands targeting BBB transport mechanisms and tumor-specific markers have demonstrated superior anti-GBM efficacy compared to single ligand systems in preclinical studies. (Table 5) However, incorporating multiple ligands requires careful surface design to prevent inter-steric hindrance (i.e., mutual physical interference between adjacent targeting ligands), as suboptimal ligand presentation can compromise nanoparticle stability and disrupt sequential blood-to-tumor targeting [33].

An alternative strategy involves targeting proteins that are simultaneously overexpressed in both BBB and GBM tumor tissues [6]. TfR and LDLR are well-documented examples of proteins that are highly expressed in both endothelial cells of the BBB and GBM. Similarly, αVβ3 integrin is upregulated in both BTB and tumor cells [6]. Folate receptors have been reported to be overexpressed across the BBB, BTB, and tumor compartments, making them attractive candidates for unified targeting with a single ligand [33]. However, potential off-target effects must be carefully considered. Although TfR is a widely used target in brain drug delivery, it is ubiquitously expressed in peripheral tissues, raising concerns regarding systemic off-target accumulation and limiting its selectivity for GBM [6].

Mechanistically, unified dual-targeting systems operate sequentially across distinct biological interfaces. Step 1 facilitates the vascular-to-interstitial transition, primarily driven by receptor-mediated transcytosis across the BBB. Following successful extravasation, Step 2 initiates the interstitial-to-cellular transition, driving deep tissue penetration and direct endocytosis into the tumor cells.

To rigorously evaluate these multistage mechanisms, researchers must experimentally decouple the two barriers. A “Step 1 effect” is typically isolated using in vitro Transwell BBB models to measure transcytosis efficiency independently of the tumor [113–115]. Conversely, the “Step 2 effect” is validated using BBB-free systems, such as in vitro cell uptake assays, to quantify direct tumor internalization [116, 117]. In orthotopic in vivo models, this Step 2 tissue-level penetration is further confirmed by demonstrating the strict colocalization of the therapeutic agent’s signal with the tumor’s bioluminescence. This experimental decoupling provides a rational framework for differentiating the vascular traversal from subsequent tumor accumulation. Indeed, recent experimental results quantitatively validate this stepwise enhancement [113, 116, 118].

Cell-mediated tumor homing strategies

Cancer cell membrane-coated nanoparticles for homotypic tumor accumulation

Coating NPs with cancer cell-derived membranes offers intrinsic self-recognition and tumor self-targeting capabilities. Solid tumor cells naturally favor homotypic adhesion; therefore, NPs cloaked on the same membrane can home onto their parent tumor, latch firmly onto its surface, and be efficiently internalized (Fig. 3B) [119, 120].

In a representative study, plasma membranes were purified from patient-derived GBM cells. In vitro assays confirmed preferential binding and uptake by GBM cells, with minimal interactions observed in healthy oligodendrocytes, indicating low off-target toxicity. Membranes derived from the GBM cells of different patients retained a high affinity for malignant cells while sparing normal brain tissue, highlighting the platform’s specificity and potential for precision therapy (Table 2). However, as in vivo validation is currently lacking, further studies are required to assess its clinical applicability.

In addition, hybrid membrane-coated nanoparticles formed by fusing membranes from different cell types have been recently developed. These hybrid systems enable multimodal targeting by integrating the biological functions of diverse cell sources, such as macrophages, tumor cells, and red blood cells [67, 121]. For example, a macrophage–tumor hybrid designed for GBM therapy demonstrated significant tumor growth inhibition in vivo, benefiting from the BBB-penetrating ability of the macrophage membrane and the excellent tumor-targeting capacity of the U87MG glioblastoma cell membrane [121].

Cell hitchhiking system

Cell hitchhiking strategies exploit the intrinsic trafficking behavior of circulating blood cells to deliver nanotherapeutics across the BBB to tumor sites without requiring external manipulation for direct NP–cell conjugation [122, 123]. Upon systemic administration, NPs can spontaneously associate with or be taken up by blood cells, facilitating their transport across endothelial barriers via cell-mediated mechanisms and subsequent migration toward disease sites [123, 124]. For instance, bone marrow-derived circulating monocytes naturally cross the BBB and infiltrate brain tumor regions, particularly targeting necrotic or hypoxic tumor areas that lack adequate vasculature [122].

In a study, doxorubicin (Dox)-loaded outer membrane vesicles (OMVs) derived from Salmonella were rapidly phagocytosed by neutrophils via Toll-like receptor 4-mediated recognition. These neutrophil-associated OMV/DOX complexes efficiently crossed the BBB, especially when an inflammatory response was activated at the tumor site [124]. Another study functionalized liposomes with lipoteichoic acid (LTA) on their surfaces to specifically target monocytes. LTA selectively binds to CD14 receptors on monocyte surfaces, enabling targeted recognition and uptake by circulating monocytes, thereby facilitating drug delivery deep into brain tumor regions [122].

However, although cell hitchhiking provides a delivery approach characterized by low immunogenicity and excellent biocompatibility, several key challenges remain, including ensuring stable NP attachment, precise control of biodistribution, and scalable and reproducible manufacturing under physiological shear stresses [123, 124]. Moreover, additional strategies are required to achieve therapeutic efficacy. For instance, genetically engineered Salmonella-derived outer membrane vesicles (OMVs) have been used to recruit circulating neutrophils via pathogen-associated molecular pattern (PAMP) signaling, enabling the OMVs to hitchhike on neutrophils, cross the BBB, and accumulate within glioma tissue for targeted drug delivery [124]. Furthermore, low-dose radiation, applied at non-cytotoxic levels, has been applied to increase the chemotactic movement of monocytes toward the tumor sites [122].

Immune and stem cell-based trojan systems for GBM homing

Live cell-based drug delivery systems use viable cells (e.g., immune or stem cells) as active carriers to transport therapeutic agents (e.g., drugs and NPs) to pathological sites, such as tumors (Fig. 3B) [125]. These systems exploit the intrinsic migratory, targeting, and immunomodulatory capabilities of living cells to cross physiological barriers, such as the BBB, and achieve site-specific delivery [126, 127]. Unlike passive cell hitchhiking approaches, this strategy involves ex vivo cellular engineering techniques, such as phagocytic uptake, electroporation, or membrane fusion, to encapsulate therapeutic agents within the cells [123, 126], enabling enhanced control over biodistribution, release kinetics, and therapeutic efficacy [125].

Living cell-based delivery systems possess dual capabilities: they can spontaneously traverse the BBB and selectively accumulate at tumor sites. For example, monocytes are attracted to tumors by chemokine and cytokine signals released in the TME, whereas neutrophils cross the BBB and migrate to residual glioma lesions in response to inflammatory cues [126]. Among the various cell types explored, mesenchymal and neural stem cells exhibit highly robust tumor tropism and accumulation, making them promising carriers for brain tumor-targeted drug delivery [126, 128, 129].

CMNPs retain cell surface proteins but lack critical biological functions such as chemotactic movement [127]. In contrast, living cell-based systems offer substantial advantages, including intrinsic immune evasion, dynamic adaptability to evolving TMEs, and active tumor-targeting capabilities [125–127].

However, these approaches have several limitations. Drug cytotoxicity toward carrier cells may cause premature cellular damage or death, thereby reducing the therapeutic efficacy [129]. The short circulation times resulting from natural cell death, premature drug release before reaching target tumor sites, and nonspecific accumulation in off-target organs are significant concerns [127, 130].

Tumor-penetrating peptides

Tumor-penetrating peptides (TPPs) are short modular sequences that facilitate the delivery of therapeutics to solid tumors, including GBM. Each TPP comprises two functional domains: an N-terminal tumor-homing motif that binds to receptors overexpressed on tumor endothelial or stromal cells, and a C-terminal C-end rule (CendR) motif that remains cryptic until proteolytic cleavage within the TME [131, 132]. The N-terminal tumor-homing motif confers the first level of selectivity by preferentially binding receptors enriched on tumor-associated endothelial or stromal cells, enabling initial localization to the tumor site. Subsequent proteolytic cleavage within the TME exposes the CendR motif, which engages neuropilin-1 (NRP-1) to trigger active tissue penetration [131]. Although NRP-1 is broadly expressed in normal tissues, TPP-mediated delivery remains tumor selective because NRP-1 engagement occurs only after this tumor-restricted, multistep activation process, thereby minimizing off-target effects and systemic toxicity [133].

Binding of the exposed CendR motif to NRP-1 induces active cellular internalization, enhancing the penetration of conjugated or co-administered drugs into tumor tissue. Moreover, TPPs initiate a bulk transport mechanism that generates transient tissue conduits, enabling drugs to access deep intratumoral regions that are otherwise unreachable via passive diffusion [132, 134]. The CendR pathway remains active for approximately 1 h, with peak activation occurring approximately 30 min after peptide administration (Fig. 3C) [132].

Consequently, TPPs establish a tumor-specific, transient tissue gateway through a multistep activation process. This mechanism enables the circumvention of physiological barriers, such as the BTB and partially intact BBB, while minimizing distribution to non-target tissues. Therefore, TPPs represent a promising strategy for enhancing intratumoral drug delivery, improving therapeutic efficacy, and reducing systemic toxicity [132].

Several peptides, including iRGD, LinTT1, and tLyP-1, exerted potent effects in preclinical GBM models (Table 3). For example, TPP-functionalized NPs showed improved accumulation in tumor spheroids and deep brain tumors, leading to enhanced drug distribution and prolonged survival [131, 132, 134]. The tumor-penetrating efficacy of TPPs is retained irrespective of whether the peptides are co-administered in the free form with therapeutic agents or covalently conjugated to nanocarriers [131].

Magnetic nanoparticle-based GBM targeting strategy

The use of magnetic NPs for targeted drug delivery in GBM involves loading chemotherapeutic agents (e.g., Dox or paclitaxel) onto magnetic carriers (e.g., iron platinum [FePt] or superparamagnetic iron oxide). After intravenous administration, a strong external magnetic field can be applied outside the skull to actively guide NPs to the tumor site. This magnetic field, generated by either permanent magnets or electromagnetic devices, enables the accumulation of drug-loaded NPs in brain tumors (Fig. 3D) [136, 137].

Beyond targeted delivery, magnetic-based systems are increasingly integrated into theranostic platforms that combine diagnostics and therapy. Magnetic NPs can function as magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) contrast agents, enabling high-resolution visualization of the tumor location and real-time monitoring of treatment response. FePt NPs exhibit strong T-weighted MRI contrast capabilities, making them effective for the early diagnosis and imaging-guided treatment assessment of GBM [136]. This integrated approach aims to maximize localized drug accumulation in tumors while reducing systemic side effects and represents a growing trend in nanomedicine-oriented theranostics [136, 137].

However, several technical limitations must be addressed before magnetic drug-targeting strategies can be clinically applied. The magnetic force decreases rapidly with increasing distance between the external magnet and tumor, making it difficult to achieve effective targeting in deep-seated tumors such as GBM. Additionally, limitations in magnetic field strength, duration, and gradient can hinder the precise accumulation of NPs within the tumor [137]. To overcome these challenges, it is essential to optimize both the physicochemical properties of NPs and the design and controllability of the magnetic field-generating system [136, 137].

Step 3 - intratumoral activation and therapeutic action

A major barrier to effective cancer treatment is the lack of precise control over the location and timing of therapeutic agent activation. To overcome this limitation, tumor-specific activation strategies have emerged as a crucial third step within the drug delivery cascade, wherein the therapeutic payload is selectively activated in the TME [138]. These strategies can be broadly classified into two categories. The first category relies on endogenous stimuli, such as acidic pH, overexpressed enzymes, and elevated redox potential, which are hallmarks of the tumor milieu. These intrinsic signals serve as biochemical triggers for site-specific drug release or activation [34]. The second category involves exogenous stimuli, wherein spatially confined external inputs, such as light, ultrasound, magnetic fields, or heat, are applied to tumor regions to remotely control drug activation or release [34, 138]. In this chapter, we summarize the control strategies that specifically induce therapeutic effects within the GBM TME among the various tumor-specific activation approaches (Fig. 4).

Endogenous stimuli-responsive drug release

Mechanisms of endogenous stimuli-responsive drug release

The GBM TME exhibits distinctive characteristics, including an acidic extracellular environment resulting from proton accumulation during lactate production and ATP hydrolysis, elevated GSH levels, and high ROS production. Additionally, the rapid growth and abnormal vasculature in GBM often lead to hypoxic regions, along with elevated activity of enzymes such as MMPs, cathepsin B, and phospholipases. Designing drug activation strategies that respond to GBM-specific conditions can enhance tumor selectivity while minimizing the off-target toxicity [139]. Depending on their design, nanocarriers can be selectively activated at tumor sites by local stimuli, inducing physicochemical changes (e.g., surface charge reversal or size shrinkage) to facilitate drug release [35, 140].

To achieve site-specific activation, nanocarriers are equipped with diverse stimulus-responsive mechanisms tailored to biochemical cues of the TME. For instance, pH-sensitive systems maintain stability in the bloodstream but degrade under mildly acidic extracellular conditions (pH 6.5–6.8) or within acidic intracellular compartments (e.g., lysosomes [pH 5–6]), facilitating drug release or the detachment of shielding components, such as PEG [141]. Similarly, GSH-responsive systems incorporate disulfide bonds that remain intact extracellularly but are cleaved in a highly reductive cytosolic environment, enabling controlled intracellular drug release [142]. In ROS-rich tumor regions, nanocarriers designed with oxidant-sensitive linkers (e.g., thioether or selenium bonds) selectively degrade, releasing their payloads in response to oxidative stress [143]. Additionally, hypoxia-responsive systems use moieties such as azobenzene or nitroimidazole derivatives, which are reduced under low-oxygen conditions, enabling drug release in poorly perfused tumor cores [144]. Enzyme-responsive platforms are widely used, particularly those targeting overexpressed enzymes, such as MMP-2, MMP-9, or tumor-specific esterases, which cleave specific substrates to trigger nanocarrier activation or drug release at the tumor site [141].

The therapeutic potential of endogenous stimuli-responsive nanocarriers has been demonstrated in numerous preclinical studies. By responding to tumor-specific physiological signals, these systems activate therapeutic agents exclusively within tumor tissues, thereby minimizing off-target side effects, maximizing local therapeutic efficacy, and ultimately enhancing overall treatment efficiency [35]. Despite these encouraging outcomes, several practical challenges remain unresolved, including the heterogeneity of the TME, the structural and functional complexity of drug delivery systems, and concerns regarding the long-term systemic toxicity associated with nanomedicine use [35, 143]. To advance clinical applications, future designs should aim to simplify carrier structures, enhance targeting specificity, and integrate patient-specific delivery strategies. In addition, a systematic toxicological evaluation is essential to ensure long-term safety [35, 141].

Advanced designs: logic-gated multiresponsive systems

More advanced designs use multi-stimuli-responsive systems that offer enhanced control and personalization of drug delivery. We classified dual stimuli–responsive systems into two categories: those requiring the simultaneous presence of both stimuli (“AND” logic) and those responding when either one of the stimuli is present (“OR” logic). This classification follows a logic-gated framework inspired by Zhang and Soh, who implemented chemical logic gates using stimuli-responsive materials [145].

In “AND” logic-based drug delivery systems, therapeutic effects are triggered only when two pathological conditions coexist. A representative strategy involves NPs with dual-sensitive linkers that degrade in response to both acidic pH and elevated MMP levels. The VPLSLYSG peptide, cleavable by both MMP-2 and MMP-9 in GBM, was combined with an imine bond hydrolyzed under acidic conditions. Within the GBM TME, this design enabled linker cleavage, nanogel disassembly, and drug release. In 3D GBM spheroid models, the system exhibited growth inhibition and cytotoxicity. In vivo studies have confirmed selective accumulation in tumors with minimal diffusion into healthy brain tissue. This dual-response approach enhances therapeutic precision while minimizing the off-target effects [36].

Meanwhile, “OR”-logic-based drug delivery systems are designed to activate therapeutic functions upon the presence of either one of two pathological stimuli. One study developed a supramolecular vesicle that responded to both acidic pH and ROS. Under acidic conditions, the protonation of carboxylic acid groups induced vesicle destabilization and disassembly. In contrast, in ROS-rich environments, boronic ester groups undergo oxidative cleavage by species such as hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂), leading to vesicle disruption and drug release. Thus, the presence of either stimulus is sufficient to trigger a therapeutic activation [146].

Another example involves amphiphilic block copolymer prodrugs (BCPs) that respond to both GSH and ROS (i.e., GSH-/ROS-responsive BCPs). In this system, an anticancer agent is conjugated to the polymer backbone via a thioether linkage, which is susceptible to cleavage under high GSH or ROS concentration. These BCPs exhibited high stability during circulation and demonstrated superior tumor suppression compared to single-stimulus-responsive systems targeting only GSH or ROS [142].

These two examples illustrate strategies that operate in a tumor-specific manner and effectively address intratumoral heterogeneity. Although GBM is characterized by a TME with low pH and high ROS and GSH concentrations, these physiological parameters can be heterogeneously distributed within tumors [142, 146]. The “OR” logic-based approach accommodates the spatial heterogeneity within the TME, and the interpatient physiological variability, potentially broadening clinical applicability and improving therapeutic outcomes.

Given the complementary functionalities of “AND” and “OR” logic–based nanoplatforms, a therapeutic combination of these two systems may provide synergistic advantages for GBM treatment. In such a strategy, “AND” logic–based nanoparticles could be employed to deliver highly cytotoxic agents with spatiotemporally confined activation in tumor regions where multiple pathological cues coexist, thereby minimizing off-target effects. In parallel, “OR” logic–based nanoparticles encapsulating low-toxicity or immunomodulatory agents could ensure broader therapeutic coverage across heterogeneous tumor microenvironments by responding to any one of several tumor-associated stimuli. The concurrent administration of these logic-based systems may thus integrate precision and adaptability within the intratumoral activation stage, offering a conceptually robust direction for future multistage GBM therapies.

Although the strategic application of logic-gated nanomedicine is broadly relevant to a wide range of solid tumors exhibiting intratumoral heterogeneity, GBM is particularly well suited to this concept because of its exceptionally high degree of spatial, cellular, and microenvironmental complexity, which poses a formidable barrier to conventional monotherapies. While logic-gated systems remain at an early proof-of-concept stage and have thus far been validated primarily in general solid tumor models rather than in GBM-specific settings, they represent a highly promising forward-looking design concept for addressing the profound heterogeneity of GBM.

Exogenous stimuli-triggered therapeutic strategies

Although endogenous cues confer baseline selectivity within the TME, exogenous stimuli add a second tier of spatial and temporal control, which is particularly valuable in GBM. The localized application of light, heat, magnetic fields, or focused ultrasound at the tumor site permits the on-demand activation of therapeutic cargos, thereby restricting drug release and cytotoxicity to malignant tissues while sparing healthy brain parenchyma [138, 139].

Photothermal therapy

Photothermal therapy (PTT) is a minimally invasive technique that uses NPs, such as gold nanorods or carbon-based materials, to absorb near-infrared (NIR) light and convert it to heat. Upon NIR irradiation, the localized temperatures increase to 42–45 °C, inducing protein denaturation and membrane disruption, leading to tumor cell death [147]. NIR-II light (around 1064 nm) has demonstrated superior transcranial photon penetration compared with conventional NIR-I wavelengths in preclinical GBM models, enabling efficient intratumoral energy deposition [148]. Under such wavelength-dependent irradiation, photothermal therapy (PTT) using aggregation-induced-emission nanoparticles or GSC membrane-camouflaged nanozymes effectively eradicated glioma stem-like cells (GSCs) and GBM tumor cells, preventing relapse and markedly enhancing therapeutic efficacy [116, 148]. Moreover, PTT-induced hyperthermia can precisely control the release of drugs loaded in NPs, suggesting the potential for implementing more sophisticated and controllable therapeutic strategies [148].

Photodynamic therapy

PDT involves the activation of PSs by visible or NIR light to produce ROS, causing oxidative damage to tumor components (e.g., proteins, lipids, and DNA). In NP-based PDT, PS molecules are either loaded onto NPs or intrinsically integrated within nanocarriers, promoting tumor-specific, light-induced cytotoxicity [149–151]. ROS generation occurs mainly via two pathways: Type I and Type II. In the Type II pathway, excited PS transfers energy to molecular oxygen to generate ROS, making its therapeutic efficacy highly oxygen-dependent. Consequently, its efficacy diminishes in hypoxic environments such as GBM tumors. In contrast, the Type I pathway involves direct electron transfer from nearby biomolecules or oxygen to excited PS, resulting in substantially lower oxygen dependence [149, 150]. The direct insertion of optical fibers into GBM tissues for precise and efficient PDT irradiation has been explored in preclinical studies [151].

Magnetic hyperthermia

Magnetic hyperthermia uses superparamagnetic iron oxide nanoparticles (SPIONs) that generate heat when exposed to an alternating magnetic field, enabling the noninvasive thermal ablation of deep-seated tumors, such as GBM. Recent advances include the use of Mn-doped iron oxide NPs, which exhibit higher saturation magnetization and specific absorption rates, allowing effective tumor heating at lower doses or weaker fields [152, 153]. Magnetic hyperthermia enhances tumor immunogenicity by promoting immune cell infiltration and activating NK cell-mediated cytotoxicity, suggesting its potential for synergistic immunotherapy combinations [154].

Sonodynamic therapy

Sonodynamic therapy (SDT) is an emerging promising noninvasive approach for GBM treatment. SDT uses ultrasound energy to activate sonosensitizers, inducing selective and site-specific apoptosis in cancer cells [82, 121]. Sonosensitizers absorb ultrasound energy to generate ROS, which promote apoptosis of GBM cells through membrane disruption and DNA damage. Organic–inorganic hybrid sonosensitizers demonstrate excellent ROS-generating capabilities, along with high biocompatibility and physiological stability, offering substantial therapeutic potential for GBM treatment [82]. Furthermore, unlike the light used in PDT, ultrasound has superior tissue penetration, allowing for the effective targeting of deeply located GBM tumors [82, 121]. However, SDT still faces challenges, including a limited understanding of the complex interactions among ultrasound, sonosensitizers, and biological tissues, and the need for advanced delivery systems capable of precise targeting in deep-seated tissues [82].

Despite the urgent clinical demand, the clinical translation of nanomedicine for GBM remains in its early stages. Initial clinical trials have primarily focused on overcoming individual drug delivery barriers, particularly the BBB, using various formulations designed to enhance the intratumoral drug accumulation. However, these platforms often fail to fully address the complex biological milieu of GBM (Table 4).

Passive targeting strategies

A representative example of passive targeting is PEGylated liposomal doxorubicin (PLD; Doxil/Caelyx), in which the liposomal surface is modified with PEG to extend systemic circulation and promote tumor accumulation through the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect. The vesicles typically exhibit an average hydrodynamic diameter of approximately 80–100 nm, allowing for prolonged plasma half-life and reduced clearance by the reticuloendothelial system. However, in a Phase II clinical trial combining temozolomide with Caelyx in patients with recurrent glioblastoma, therapeutic efficacy was limited and the study was terminated early because the objective response rate did not meet the predefined threshold [155]. These results emphasize the inherent limitations of EPR-dependent passive targeting in GBM, where incomplete BBB permeability and heterogeneous vascular architecture markedly restrict uniform nanoparticle accumulation and therapeutic efficacy [156]. Collectively, these findings have motivated the development of ligand-mediated and other active targeting approaches aimed at enhancing nanoparticle transport across the BBB and improving intratumoral delivery.

Ligand-mediated targeting strategies

GRN1005 (ANG1005) is an Angiopep-2–paclitaxel conjugate that targets low-density lipoprotein receptor–related protein-1 (LRP-1), which is highly expressed on both BBB endothelial cells and GBM tissues. In a Phase II trial involving patients with recurrent high-grade glioma, ANG1005 demonstrated favorable BBB permeability and measurable tumor uptake, confirming target engagement; however, the median progression-free survival was only 2.7 months and median overall survival 8 months, leading to discontinuation of further development [157].

GBM-LIPO, a PEGylated liposomal formulation of doxorubicin functionalized with anti-EGFR antibodies, selectively accumulates in regions of disrupted blood–tumor barrier (BTB) within EGFR-overexpressing GBM tissues. In the clinical study by Kasenda et al. (2022), the mean hydrodynamic diameter was approximately 110 nm, and intratumoral doxorubicin concentrations reached detectable therapeutic levels; nonetheless, the drug was virtually absent in cerebrospinal fluid, underscoring that these constructs remain ineffective in tumors with intact BBB [156].

Another example, 2B3-101, is a glutathione-PEGylated liposomal doxorubicin designed to exploit glutathione transporter-mediated transcytosis for BBB penetration. In a Phase I/IIa trial including patients with recurrent high-grade gliomas and breast-cancer brain metastases, 2B3-101 achieved safe systemic exposure and verified BBB passage by MRI, yet clinical benefit remained modest: 52% of patients achieved stable disease as the best response, the 3-month progression-free survival rate was 40%, and only 12% showed ≥ 20% intracranial tumor reduction [158].

Collectively, these findings indicate that both BBB-targeted and tumor-targeted single-ligand nanocarriers have yielded only modest clinical benefits in GBM. Despite achieving measurable BBB penetration or selective tumor accumulation, these systems exhibit restricted tissue distribution, limited intratumoral retention, and insufficient pharmacodynamic efficacy within the heterogeneous GBM microenvironment. Therefore, multitargeting strategies and stepwise delivery platforms capable of ensuring deep tumor penetration and sustained local drug activation are required to overcome the inherent shortcomings of current ligand-mediated designs.

External stimuli-enhanced modalities

Physical modalities that transiently increase BBB permeability through external stimulation are currently being translated into clinical practice. A representative example is the SonoCloud-9 system (NCT03744026), which employs implantable ultrasound emitters coupled with intravenously administered microbubbles (MBs) to reversibly disrupt the BBB. In a recent Phase I/II clinical trial involving patients with recurrent GBM, repeated sonications (1 MHz, 1.1 MPa acoustic pressure) enabled safe and reproducible BBB opening, resulting in approximately 5.9-fold higher intracerebral carboplatin concentrations compared with conventional delivery. The treatment was well tolerated and is now being explored as an adjuvant modality to suppress postoperative recurrence following maximal tumor resection [159].

Other strategies utilize external physical energy not only to modulate BBB permeability but also to directly induce tumor cell death. A notable example is AGuIX, a gadolinium-based ultrasmall (≈ 5 nm) polysiloxane nanoparticle acting as a radiosensitizer. In the Phase I/II NANO-GBM trial (NCT04881032), patients received AGuIX intravenously (50–100 mg/kg) prior to chemoradiotherapy with temozolomide. The trial demonstrated selective tumor accumulation, favorable MRI contrast enhancement, and excellent tolerability, confirming safety and BBB passage [160, 161]. However, this platform relies solely on passive accumulation via the EPR effect, and MRI biodistribution analyses revealed predominant accumulation within the tumor core rather than the infiltrative margins, suggesting that AGuIX monotherapy may be insufficient for complete tumor control in diffusely infiltrative GBM [161].

An alternative physical approach is to bypass the BBB entirely through local intratumoral administration. Nanotherm®, based on the direct injection of superparamagnetic iron-oxide nanoparticles (SPIONs) into the tumor cavity, induces localized hyperthermia (42–45 °C) upon exposure to an alternating magnetic field (100–300 kHz). This treatment has demonstrated localized tumor ablation and improved progression-free survival in pilot human studies, but its therapeutic effect remains confined to the injection site, thereby limiting efficacy to radiographically visible lesions [162]. Consequently, microscopic residual disease beyond the treatment field remains a major challenge, highlighting the need for complementary systemic or molecularly targeted approaches.

Overall, external-stimulus-enhanced modalities provide compelling evidence that physical modulation of the BBB or tumor microenvironment can potentiate nanoparticle delivery and therapeutic response. Nonetheless, the large size and diffuse infiltration characteristic of human GBM complicate complete tumor eradication, emphasizing that such energy-based methods must be integrated within multimodal or multistage nanotherapeutic frameworks to maximize clinical benefit.

Necessity of multistage strategies and clinical implications

The limitations of single-stage approaches in achieving clinically meaningful therapeutic outcomes in GBM have become increasingly evident. Despite promising preclinical results, most nanomedicine-based clinical trials have demonstrated limited efficacy. These failures are commonly attributed to insufficient BBB penetration, poor intratumoral distribution, inadequate pharmacodynamic effects, and intrinsic biological resistance of GBM [163]. However, rather than representing independent barriers, these limitations reflect a fundamental issue: most clinical strategies have targeted only a single stage of drug delivery without addressing the sequential and interdependent nature of the delivery process.

At the level of BBB traversal (step 1), several clinical approaches have focused on enhancing drug delivery across the blood–brain barrier. Agents such as GRN1005 and glutathione-PEGylated liposomal doxorubicin (2B3-101), as well as device-based approaches such as focused ultrasound, have demonstrated the feasibility of increasing drug delivery to the brain. However, these strategies have shown limited clinical efficacy. These findings suggest that BBB penetration alone is insufficient for effective treatment. In particular, preserved BBB at infiltrative tumor margins may limit drug access to invasive tumor regions, resulting in incomplete tumor coverage [164]. Furthermore, even when drugs reach the tumor core, heterogeneous intratumoral distribution and insufficient pharmacodynamic activation may limit therapeutic efficacy.

Strategies primarily designed to enhance tumor targeting or accumulation (step 2) have encountered similar translational hurdles. For example, EGFR-targeted immunoliposomes and transferrin receptor–targeted systems have shown evidence of tumor localization; however, clinical outcomes remain modest. These findings suggest that tumor targeting alone does not ensure effective therapy. Limited BBB traversal may restrict delivery to infiltrative tumor regions, while heterogeneous BTB permeability and elevated interstitial fluid pressure may result in uneven intratumoral drug distribution [165]. Consequently, insufficient intracellular uptake further diminishes the therapeutic window.

Efforts to directly enhance intratumoral therapeutic activity (step 3), including convection-enhanced delivery and intratumoral nanoparticle-based therapies, have demonstrated localized drug delivery and acceptable safety profiles. Nevertheless, their clinical benefits remain marginal. While local delivery circumvents vascular barriers and enhances drug activity at the injection site, such approaches are inherently spatially restricted. They fail to address the diffuse, infiltrative nature of GBM, as insufficient distribution beyond the immediate treatment site leaves distant tumor cells viable and drives disease recurrence [166].

Collectively, these observations indicate that optimizing a single stage of drug delivery is insufficient, as unaddressed limitations in other stages compromise overall therapeutic efficacy. This suggests that the limited clinical success of current nanomedicine approaches in GBM arises primarily from a lack of integration across sequential stages of drug delivery.

Preclinical evaluation of multistage multistrategy nanoplatforms for enhanced GBM therapy

Clinical studies have shown that single-strategy-based drug delivery approaches can partially penetrate the BBB; however, they face limitations, such as insufficient precise drug accumulation at tumor sites or significantly reduced BBB penetration efficiency when exclusively targeting tumor cells. The complex and heterogeneous TME of GBM highlights the difficulty of achieving sufficient therapeutic efficacy using single therapeutic strategies [33]. Consequently, recent preclinical studies have aimed to overcome these limitations by developing multi-strategy-based drug delivery systems. (Table 5).

Multi-strategy drug delivery systems integrate various targeting strategies, such as ligand-mediated targeting, cell-based strategies, and TPPs, or are designed to selectively activate drugs in response to TME factors. Additionally, these systems frequently incorporate external stimuli, such as light, ultrasound, and magnetic fields, into complex therapeutic platforms. Such multifaceted approaches offer advantages by efficiently crossing the BBB, selectively accumulating drugs at tumor sites, and maximizing therapeutic efficacy through tumor-specific activation, while minimizing off-target toxicity [148, 167, 168].

Preclinical studies have demonstrated that multifunctional nanoplatforms significantly enhance BBB penetration and drug accumulation within tumors, prolong survival, and reduce toxicity to normal tissues [148, 167]. For example, compared to single strategies, combined targeting of LRP-1 and LAT1 receptors using Angiopep-2 and histidine was associated with approximately 4-fold greater drug accumulation in the brain and a 2.5-fold higher long-term survival rate in an in vivo GBM orthotopic model [167]. Additionally, approaches that integrate tumor environment-responsive platforms and external stimulus activation have shown notable potential for GBM treatment [118, 148, 169].

These findings collectively show that multi-strategy drug delivery systems can significantly enhance tumor-selective delivery, maximize therapeutic efficacy, and minimize adverse effects.

Despite these advances, most multistrategy nanoplatforms remain insufficient because they do not comprehensively address all stages of the drug delivery cascade. While individual systems can improve specific steps—such as BBB penetration, tumor targeting, or microenvironment-responsive activation—few platforms integrate all three in a unified manner. As a result, partial integration often leads to incomplete therapeutic outcomes, as improvements in one or two stages do not compensate for deficiencies in others. These observations highlight that effective GBM nanotherapy requires coordinated control across all stages of the delivery cascade.

Beyond incorporating multiple functional elements, coordination across delivery stages has emerged as a critical factor. Stage-specific regulation of targeting functions can enhance delivery efficiency across distinct biological environments. For example, Martins et al. introduced a PEG-shielded nanoplatform that enables temporally regulated ligand exposure, allowing sequential activation of targeting functions and thereby improving stage-to-stage transitions across biological barriers, including the BBB and tumor microenvironment [167].

Building upon these insights, recent studies have begun to develop more comprehensive multistage strategies that integrate all three stages of drug delivery within a single platform. For example, Liu et al. developed a photoacoustic nanoparticle capable of tumor-site BBB permeability modulation, enhanced tumor accumulation, and localized activation, thereby functionally integrating BBB traversal, tumor targeting, and therapeutic activation. This coordinated design resulted in markedly improved therapeutic outcomes in an orthotopic glioblastoma model, with over 80% survival beyond 40 days, whereas no animals in control groups survived to this time point [170].

Nevertheless, fully integrated multistage nanomedicine remains in its early stages of development. Even the most advanced platforms currently available rely on external stimuli or exhibit limited spatial coverage, which may restrict their effectiveness in diffusely infiltrative tumors. These observations indicate that, although recent studies have made meaningful progress toward integrating the key stages of drug delivery, a truly comprehensive and clinically translatable multistage system has yet to be fully realized.

Critical appraisal and translational outlook of multistage strategies

Clinical-stage therapeutic strategies currently under investigation are regarded as having high clinical potential when they combine physical devices, such as FUS or CED, with nanoparticle platforms. In contrast, targeted nanomedicines, despite promising results in preclinical studies, face substantial real-world barriers in late-stage human trials, including insufficient brain delivery and complex physiological variability [157]. Reflecting this clinical reality, recent preclinical studies have increasingly adopted approaches that integrate external stimuli—such as ultrasound, laser irradiation, and magnetically guided devices—to actively modulate barrier permeability and enhance therapeutic efficacy [171, 172].

While physical interventions dominate clinical trials, preclinical research continues to aggressively refine biological targeting. At the preclinical stage, RMT and cell membrane coating technologies are actively explored as key strategies in both Step 1 and Step 2. Notably, in many instances, a single ligand or targeting strategy can naturally fulfill both steps, as the targets are often shared between the BBB and GBM cells, leading to a significant functional overlap between these stages. Numerous preclinical studies have focused on leveraging this commonality by integrating a single targeting framework with external physical stimuli or tumor-specific activation triggers to maximize delivery efficiency [6, 33]. Interestingly, however, even with such overlap, multi-ligand or ligand–cell membrane hybrid systems demonstrate a significant advantage in tumor-specific binding and therapeutic outcomes compared to single-target systems.

For instance, the combination of Angiopep-2 with tumor-specific ligands such as EP-1 or L-histidine has demonstrated a significant increase in brain accumulation in vivo [167, 173]. Similarly, the additional functionalization of cell membrane with active targeting ligands like Angiopep-2 or cRGD significantly amplifies these effects in both in vitro and in vivo settings [174]. These multi-strategy platforms overcome inherent biological limitations by circumventing the receptor saturation and subsequent uptake bottlenecks associated with single-target reliance.

Beyond functional layering, the organic coordination between delivery steps is emerging as a central design principle. The future of glioblastoma therapy lies in transitioning from partial strategies toward comprehensive multistage platforms capable of seamlessly managing the entire vascular-interstitial-cellular delivery cascade. Yet, the successful clinical translation of such comprehensive frameworks remains contingent upon resolving several formidable real-world challenges.

Practical barriers to the clinical translation of multistage strategies

Mechanistic origins of the translational gap between preclinical models and human glioblastoma

Despite promising outcomes in preclinical studies, the clinical translation of nanomedicine-based therapies for GBM remains limited, largely due to the inability of current models to recapitulate the complexity of human disease.

A key factor is the heterogeneity of the BBB. Conventional xenograft models exhibit relatively uniform and permeable vasculature, leading to overestimation of nanoparticle delivery. In contrast, human GBM presents a heterogeneous BTB, where regions of disruption coexist with intact BBB. Notably, infiltrative tumor cells often reside in BBB-intact regions, limiting drug penetration into clinically relevant areas [164, 175]. This is further compounded by differences in tumor growth patterns: whereas preclinical models typically form well-demarcated masses, human GBM is highly infiltrative, resulting in insufficient drug coverage of dispersed tumor cells [164].

Additionally, current models inadequately capture the dynamic interactions at the BBB–tumor interface, including regional variability in permeability, transporter activity, and microenvironmental conditions. As a result, drug distribution observed in experimental systems may not accurately predict therapeutic exposure in patients [176].

Immunological discrepancies further contribute to this gap. Most xenograft models rely on immunodeficient mice, limiting the assessment of tumor–immune interactions. Although recent advances, including immunocompetent orthotopic models, have improved the representation of tumor–microenvironment interactions, these systems still fall short of fully capturing the complexity of human immune responses [177].

Integrated safety and toxicity considerations in multistage design

The clinical translation of multistage nanotherapeutics for GBM requires not only enhanced delivery efficiency but also comprehensive evaluation of safety across all stages of the delivery cascade. Unlike conventional nanocarriers, multistage platforms incorporate multiple functional modules, increasing the complexity of biological interactions and potential adverse effects. Therefore, safety must be considered a fundamental design parameter rather than a secondary outcome.

Protein corona formation is a key determinant of in vivo nanoparticle behavior, dynamically altering nanoparticle identity upon exposure to biological fluids. While corona formation may reduce direct cytotoxic interactions, it can impair targeting efficiency and unpredictably modulate immune recognition and biodistribution [178–180]. Furthermore, inter-individual variability in corona composition may result in inconsistent pharmacokinetics and toxicity profiles [181].

Long-term biodistribution and organ accumulation represent additional safety concerns. Nanoparticles that evade rapid clearance tend to accumulate in the liver and spleen due to uptake by the mononuclear phagocyte system [182–184]. Although prolonged circulation may enhance tumor delivery, persistent retention of non-biodegradable or slowly degradable materials has been associated with chronic toxicity, including oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and low-grade inflammation [185, 186]. These effects are often not detected in short-term studies, highlighting limitations of conventional evaluation frameworks.

Stimuli-responsive systems and strategies for transient blood–brain barrier modulation depend on precise control over activation conditions and barrier permeability; incomplete or off-target activation may cause variability in pharmacokinetics and biological responses, while increased BBB permeability may allow circulating molecules, including inflammatory mediators, to enter the central nervous system [10, 182, 187–190].

Taken together, these findings emphasize that safety in multistage nanotherapeutics is intrinsically linked to system design. Rational strategies—including the use of biodegradable materials, precise control of physicochemical properties, and engineering of protein corona composition—are required to balance efficacy with biocompatibility. Ultimately, standardized long-term evaluation frameworks will be essential to ensure safe clinical translation.

Manufacturing and translational challenges in multistage nanotherapeutics

Although multifunctional NPs have emerged as a transformative platform for GBM therapy because they can traverse a range of biological barriers, including the BBB, the complexity of their design and manufacturing, coupled with regulatory uncertainty, remains the principal obstacle to clinical translation.

Slight deviations in physicochemical parameters, such as particle size, surface charge, and drug loading, can markedly alter the biodistribution and therapeutic efficacy, making it difficult to reproduce laboratory-grade precision at a commercial scale [191]. Therefore, minimizing batch-to-batch variability requires the early adoption of high-precision quality management frameworks, such as quality-by-design and process analytical technology; however, the associated time, cost, and data requirements are prohibitive for most start-ups and academic laboratories [192–194].

The most formidable regulatory barrier is the absence of a harmonized and enforceable framework dedicated to nanomedicine. The current Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and European Medicines Agency classifications are inadequate for the accurate assessment and categorization of multifunctional NPs [192, 193]. Differing national definitions and safety standards complicate the design of multinational clinical trials. Hence, early and continuous engagement with regulators, incorporating risk evaluation and mitigation strategies, is essential. Establishing a common international framework that supports highly reproducible manufacturing processes and modern safety assessments is a prerequisite for a seamless path to FDA approval [192–194].

The successful clinical translation of multistage nanotherapeutics will require robust, reproducible, and scalable manufacturing workflows, cutting-edge toxicology and immunology assessment tools, and an internationally harmonized regulatory framework. Overcoming these hurdles requires sustained interdisciplinary collaboration and ongoing dialogue with regulatory authorities.

Conclusions and future perspectives

The vascular–interstitial–cellular interface constitutes a spatiotemporally coupled continuous set of checkpoints. Consequently, intravenously administered therapeutics must overcome several barriers before reaching GBM at pharmacologically effective concentrations. Repeated clinical experience has shown that although single-function nanomedicines designed solely to cross the BBB, such as liposomal or peptide-based carriers (e.g., 2B3-101 and GRN1005), display favorable pharmacokinetics in preclinical studies, they achieve only limited efficacy [157, 158]. BBB penetration alone cannot overcome the densely cross-linked ECM, elevated IFP, and heterogeneous TME inside GBM lesions. Therefore, genuine therapeutic benefits require additional steps: tumor-selective accumulation, deep intratumoral penetration, and on-site activation [22, 24]. This necessity substantiates the multistage design paradigm—step 1: BBB traversal → step 2: tumor targeting and intratumoral accumulation → step 3: on-site activation—as an essential framework for precision GBM Therapy.

Multiple preclinical studies have demonstrated the superiority of this stepwise multi-strategy framework over single-function nanomedicines. NPs equipped with BBB-targeting and GBM cell-targeting ligands consistently demonstrate enhanced therapeutic outcomes by addressing the entire cascade of biological barriers, thus overcoming the inherent limitations of single-ligand systems. By rationally integrating steps 1, 2, and 3, these systems enhance intratumoral drug delivery while minimizing off-target toxicity.

Various strategies have been explored in stage 1. The surface engineering of NPs to exploit endogenous routes—RMT, AMT, and CMT— has been well established [37]. Cell membrane coating and cell-based drug loading further enhance BBB permeability [67, 126]. Temporarily modulating BBB permeability via FUS with MBs, PDT, or chemical agents offers complementary approaches [37, 91, 95]. Even after BBB transit, dense ECM and elevated IFP restrict deep drug penetration; minimizing off-target toxicity requires tumor-selective accumulation. Stage 2 strategies improve tumor homing and penetration [131, 195]. Additional modalities include direct targeting of GBM cells or ECM components and the use of tumor-homing cells [126, 128, 129]. Stage 3 introduces nanocarriers that respond to tumor-specific cues (pH, redox potential, enzymes) or external stimuli (ultrasound, magnetic fields, light), enabling spatially confined drug release and activation [35, 138].

Although multistage concepts are vigorously used at the preclinical level, their clinical translation is dominated by single-function formulations. Key barriers include the translation gap in pathophysiological models, the design and manufacturing complexity of multicomponent nanomedicines, and regulatory uncertainties in the characterization of standardized safety and quality-attributes. While refining each individual step is undoubtedly valuable, the success of GBM treatment primarily depends on multistage nanomedicines that can sequentially traverse the entire spectrum of biological barriers. Seamlessly integrating materials science nanostructure design, systems biology barrier mapping, and computational oncology predictive modelling can markedly enhance the clinical translatability of these multistage platforms, serving as a catalyst for subsequent phase I/II exploratory trials and optimization of patient-tailored combination therapies.
