 Milestone Report for MRT 8818
Title: Complete time-resolved X-ray shape capability for a 10 MJ implosion

Completion Criteria: Completion is achieved upon delivery of a report that either
demonstrates > 5 from a single pinhole image in an experiment producing a 10 MJ yield, or
uses data acquired at yields below 10 MJ together with empirical background-scaling models for the
diagnostic.

Executive Summary
This report documents completion of MRT 8818, which established an upgraded equatorial time-
resolved X-ray imaging capability for high-yield inertial confinement fusion experiments at the
National Ignition Facility (NIF) using the equatorial Dilation X-ray Imager (DIXI). The need for this
work arose from the sustained increase in fusion yield at NIF, which progressively intensified the
neutron and gamma-ray environment experienced by target diagnostics. Although the existing polar
imaging capability, particularly the Polar Dilation X-ray Imager (PDIXI), remained viable at high
yields, the equatorial capability became inadequate because of radiation-induced failure of electronic
readout hardware and increasing background levels that degraded image quality and ultimately
caused saturation.

The work performed under MRT 8818 addressed this limitation through a combination of hardware
replacement, background characterization, and targeted mitigation. The DIXI backend was converted
from CCD-based electronic readout to photographic film, and the principal sources of internally
generated background were investigated using prior analyses, dedicated tests, and comparison with
PDIXI performance on high-yield shots. This effort led to implementation of two principal
improvements: a multilayer optical coating to suppress broadband radiation-induced background
generated in the fiber-optic extension cylinder, and replacement of Kodak TMAX 400 film with Agfa
Copex Rapid to reduce background generated directly in the film.

These upgrades were completed in 2025 and performance assessment based on DT shot data since
then, comparing with Polar DIXI and scaling of these experiments to 10 MJ indicates an expected
signal-to-background ratio of approximately 34 for a single pinhole image, substantially exceeding
the MRT 8818 completion criterion of > 5.

1. Background and Diagnostic Evolution
The work reported under MRT 8818 was motivated by the sustained increase in fusion yield achieved
over the course of inertial confinement fusion experiments at NIF. As capsule performance improved,
particularly as experiments progressed into the burning-plasma and ignition regimes, the radiative
and neutron environment experienced by target diagnostics became substantially more severe. This
evolution created a corresponding requirement to adapt X-ray imaging diagnostics in order to

preserve data quality, maintain temporal resolution, and ensure continued measurement capability
for the highest-yield experiments.

Time-resolved X-ray imaging has long been a central element of NIF implosion diagnosis. Gated X-
ray cameras are used to observe the evolution of the implosion from multiple lines of sight,
providing essential information on hot-spot size, shape, symmetry, stagnation dynamics, and
signatures of hydrodynamic degradation mechanisms, including mix. These measurements are
critical for assessing implosion performance, understanding yield-limiting physics, and guiding
target, hohlraum, and pulse-shape optimization. Over the course of the NIF ICF campaign, X-ray
imaging systems have undergone a series of adaptations in response to the increasing severity of the
experimental environment, as summarized in Figure 1.

Figure 1 - The principal ICF X-ray imaging lines of sight through the years. DIXI now replaces ARIANE at the equator
 while HYXI, planned for FY27, will provide a third orthogonal line of sight.

Early configurations relied on framing cameras, or Gated X-ray Detectors (GXDs), inserted into the
target chamber through Diagnostic Instrument Manipulators (DIMs), typically coupled to electronic-
readout detectors such as CCD-based systems. These platforms provided important imaging
capability during lower-yield phases of the campaign, but became increasingly vulnerable to
radiation-induced background and detector degradation as fusion performance improved.

To extend diagnostic viability, the program transitioned to photographic film-based recording media,
known as Hardened Gated X-ray Detectors (HGXDs), which offered greater robustness in the
prompt-radiation environment than conventional electronic image sensors. As yields continued to
increase and NIF experiments entered the burning-plasma regime, additional changes became
necessary. In particular, detector systems were relocated outside the target chamber wall to increase

stand-off distance, improve shielding options, and reduce susceptibility to radiation backgrounds
while preserving image fidelity. This progression led to deployment of external-viewing X-ray
imaging systems, including ARIANE on an equatorial line of sight and the Polar DIXI (PDIXI) system
on a polar line of sight. PDIXI initially employed a CMOS-based backend detector, but that
configuration was subsequently replaced by photographic film as ignition-level performance became
more routine and neutron backgrounds increased beyond the tolerance of electronic-readout
approaches. That transition to more radiation-tolerant architectures was completed under MRT 8478
in 2023.

PDIXI presently serves as the primary time-resolved X-ray imaging diagnostic for high-yield ICF
experiments. Its importance derives from its ability to return high-quality images with manageable
neutron-induced background while providing approximately 10 ps temporal resolution, which is
required to resolve the rapid stagnation and burn dynamics of NIF implosions, whose most critical
evolution occurs over only a few tens of picoseconds. By comparison, ARIANE provides temporal
resolution of approximately 100 ps and is increasingly compromised by neutron-induced
background, becoming fully saturated on experiments producing roughly 3 MJ or more of fusion
yield. Consequently, the equatorial line of sight no longer provided X-ray imaging capability
commensurate with the diagnostic performance available at the pole.

MRT 8818 addressed this gap by establishing on the equatorial line of sight an X-ray imaging
capability equivalent in function to that provided by PDIXI at the pole, thereby restoring
complementary high-quality, time-resolved imaging from multiple lines of sight for the highest-
performing NIF implosions. This capability is important not only for routine performance assessment,
but also for continued investigation of residual asymmetries, mix mechanisms, and other
degradation processes that become increasingly consequential as ignition-scale implosions are
pushed to higher performance and improved reproducibility.

2. Motivation for the Upgrade
The work reported under MRT 8818 was driven by the increasingly severe neutron and gamma-ray
environment associated with high-yield ICF experiments at NIF, and by the resulting impact on the
reliability and utility of time-resolved X-ray imaging diagnostics. As fusion yield increased, this
environment began to affect diagnostic performance in multiple ways, ranging from complete loss of
functionality, for example through radiation-induced failure or single-event effects in sensitive
electronics, to more gradual degradation through elevated background levels that reduce image
contrast, compress usable dynamic range, and ultimately render the data unusable.

This progression was observed directly in the equatorial DIXI diagnostic. The CCD-based backend
detector previously used with DIXI ceased to return usable data on implosions producing neutron
yields above approximately 3 × 10 . This behavior first appeared in late 2020. At that time,
experimental operations relied increasingly on ARIANE, which had been prepared in anticipation of
the loss of electronic-readout capability at those yield levels. DIXI and ARIANE are installed on
adjacent equatorial lines of sight on the target chamber wall, at azimuthal angles of approximately
100 and 89 degrees, respectively.

Even when a diagnostic does not fail completely, increasing neutron-induced background can
significantly diminish its value. Background accumulation reduces effective dynamic range,
complicates pre-shot setup and exposure selection, lowers confidence in faint or spatially localized
features, and degrades the quality of quantitative measurements derived from the images. With
continued yield increase, this process leads to full saturation, or diagnostic blankout. In practical
terms, an instrument may remain nominally operational while no longer providing data of sufficient
quality to support performance assessment or physics interpretation, as illustrated in Figure 2.

  Figure 2 - Simulated diagnostic data (top row) at increasing neutron yields (left to right), and associated lineouts
 (bottom row), showing progressive data degradation until saturation is reached.

These effects were particularly consequential because time-resolved X-ray imaging is one of the few
diagnostics capable of providing direct information on implosion shape and hot-spot evolution near
peak compression. Preserving this capability at both polar and equatorial viewing angles is important
for identifying asymmetry, interpreting yield-degradation mechanisms, and maintaining confidence
in conclusions drawn from single-line-of-sight observations. As NIF experiments advanced into
burning-plasma and ignition conditions, maintaining an equatorial diagnostic with sufficient
temporal resolution and radiation tolerance became a clear programmatic requirement.

3. Scope of Work
The work performed to satisfy MRT 8818 extended beyond replacement of the vulnerable CCD
backend with photographic film, although that transition was itself well motivated. Film-based
recording had already demonstrated superior robustness in the NIF radiation environment, most
notably through the successful operation of PDIXI on high-yield shots. However, detector
replacement alone was not sufficient. It was also necessary to identify, understand, and mitigate the
dominant sources of internally generated background within the DIXI diagnostic assembly so that
the full benefit of a more radiation-tolerant recording medium could be realized.

This effort benefited from several sources of prior knowledge. First, PDIXI was already operating on a
broad set of high-yield experiments, providing an empirical basis for comparing background
behavior across systems and identifying how specific components contributed to signal degradation
as yield increased. Second, the effort drew upon analyses performed during the NIF design phase
and the early years of operation, including studies by Izumi et al. (Rev. Sci. Instrum. 81, 10E515
(2010)), Hagmann et al. (Rev. Sci. Instrum. 81, 10E514 (2010)), Bell et al. (Rev. Sci. Instrum. 81, 10E540
(2010)), and others that examined neutron interactions, gamma production, and radiation transport
in diagnostic systems and surrounding structures. The work supporting MRT 8818 therefore built on
both operational experience and prior design knowledge to develop an equatorial imaging capability
able to function effectively in the post-ignition, high-yield NIF environment.

The scope of work was both investigative and implementation-oriented. The first objective was to
determine the dominant mechanisms responsible for background generation within the DIXI system
under high-yield operating conditions. The second objective was to develop practical mitigations
that could be implemented without compromising the core imaging function of the diagnostic, its
temporal performance, or its operational deployability on NIF experiments.

4. Background Mitigation Approach
The investigation confirmed that the 125 mm fiber-optic faceplate used to relay the visible image
from the rear of the diagnostic onto the photographic film, here referred to as the extension cylinder,
was one of the most important contributors to internally generated background. This result was
consistent with prior expectations, since the component presents a substantial path length of optical
material exposed to the local radiation field. Separate high-yield tests using alternative or mock-up
configurations were therefore performed to evaluate the benefit of eliminating this component.
Those tests showed that removing the extension cylinder reduced background, but by substantially
less than would be expected from simple linear scaling with fiber length.

The extension cylinder is located behind a second fiber-optic faceplate on which a phosphor layer is
deposited, as illustrated in Figure 3a. That phosphor screen, approximately 15 mm thick, converts the
electron image into visible light and is a fundamental part of the DIXI architecture that cannot readily
be replaced. The investigation indicated that background produced in these fiber-optic elements,
through a combination of Cherenkov emission and scintillation processes, does not scale directly
with total component length. A likely explanation is that light generated farther from the film plane is
not transmitted efficiently enough to contribute proportionally to the recorded background, such
that the region nearest the film dominates the effective background seen by the recording medium.
As a consequence, removing the 125 mm extension cylinder did not produce the nearly 90 percent
reduction that might be inferred from total fiber length alone.

This finding led to a change in mitigation strategy. Rather than pursue elimination of the extension
cylinder, which would also have required substantial mechanical redesign and integration effort, the
work shifted toward spectral discrimination of useful signal from radiation-induced background. This
approach, previously identified by Hagmann et al. (Proc. SPIE 8144, 814408 (2011)), was motivated by
the fact that the background generated in the fiber-optic components is broadband in character,
whereas the desired optical signal emitted by the phosphor is concentrated near 545 nm. A

multilayer optical coating was therefore designed and applied to the extension cylinder to reject a
substantial fraction of the broadband background while transmitting the phosphor signal, as
illustrated in Figure 3b. Performance tests on PDIXI demonstrated that this approach reduced
background by approximately a factor of four, confirming that spectral filtering provided an effective
and implementable mitigation path.

 Figure 3 - a) Schematic of DIXI backend showing extension cylinder and new multilayer coating and b) Principle of
 background rejection using the multilayer coating bandpass filter.

A second major area of work concerned direct radiation-induced exposure of the photographic film
itself. Results obtained on PDIXI showed that, once extension-cylinder background was mitigated,
direct interactions in the film became the next most significant contributor to residual background,
followed by background generation in the phosphor layer. For roughly the past decade, the
diagnostic program had relied primarily on Kodak TMAX 400 film. Although this film had provided
acceptable performance over a broad range of operating conditions, it proved increasingly sensitive
to the NIF environment as neutron yield rose to ignition-era levels.

To address this issue, several lower-sensitivity film types were evaluated for suitability in the NIF
radiation environment, with consideration given not only to average background response but also
to spatial uniformity and practical compatibility with diagnostic operations. These studies identified
Agfa Copex Rapid as a suitable replacement. Relative to Kodak TMAX 400, Agfa Copex Rapid
provided substantially lower radiation-induced background, approximately a factor-of-four reduction
on shots at yields near 4 MJ, as well as reduced spatial nonuniformity due to its finer grain structure.
These improvements increased the effective signal-to-background ratio and improved the
interpretability and quantitative utility of the recorded images.

Additional background and technical details on these improvements are documented in Trosseille et
al. (Rev. Sci. Instrum. 95, 073514 (2024)).

5. Expected Outcome
The expected outcome of MRT 8818 was the demonstration and implementation of a set of
complementary upgrades that would enable DIXI to operate effectively in the contemporary high-
yield NIF environment. These upgrades included transition to film-based recording, suppression of

internally generated optical background through spectral filtering, and reduction of recording-
medium sensitivity to radiation-induced exposure through film selection. Taken together, these
improvements were expected to restore a practical equatorial X-ray imaging capability with
substantially improved resistance to neutron- and gamma-induced degradation, while avoiding
unnecessary redesign of core diagnostic hardware. Success was defined as demonstrating that the
diagnostic could record images with a signal-to-background ratio greater than 5 on a 10 MJ fusion-
yield shot, using measured data at MJ-class yields together with validated background-scaling
models.

6. Performance Assessment Following Upgrade
Completion
Upgrades to DIXI were completed in February 2025, followed by an initial recommissioning shot,
N250313, in March 2025. The upgraded hardware configuration is shown in Figure 4. Since
recommissioning, and while awaiting DT shots employing an updated hohlraum window
configuration that provides a direct line of sight to DIXI, the diagnostic has been fielded on DT
experiments to collect neutron-background scaling data and to benchmark its behavior against PDIXI
under comparable high-yield conditions.

 Figure 4 - The DIXI detector seen from the back after completion of the upgraded, equipped with a film pack (a) and
corresponding CAD model (b). (c) shows backend once Contamination Zone (CZ) enclosure is in place, and (d) CAD
  model with the enclosure and radiation shielding drawers surrounding the film pack. (e) is a close-in picture of the
 backend ready for a NIF shot, with both CZ enclosure and shielding drawers in place.

Results from four such shots, spanning fusion yields from 0.5 MJ to 8.6 MJ, are summarized in Figure
5. These data indicate that DIXI and PDIXI exhibit comparable levels of background associated with
extension-cylinder and film contributions. However, the DIXI phosphor screen appears to be
significantly more sensitive to the NIF radiation environment than the corresponding PDIXI
component, by approximately a factor of 5 to 10. Although the two phosphor screens were

fabricated to the same specifications, they were obtained from different suppliers, and differences in
manufacturing methods or material processing may account for the observed response difference.

Figure 5 - Comparison of neutron-induced background level in DIXI and PDIXI on high-yield shots, showing increased
 sensitivity of the phosphor layer used in DIXI, compared to PDIXI.

Despite this increased phosphor sensitivity, the background-scaling behavior of both diagnostics
exhibits an important and favorable feature, namely sublinear growth with increasing neutron yield.
Between the lowest-yield shot in the comparison set, N260109, and the highest-yield shot, N250406,
the fusion yield increased by a factor of 19, while the measured background increased by only a
factor of 5.4 in both DIXI and PDIXI. The available data are well described by a power-law
dependence of background on neutron yield with an exponent of approximately 0.57. Extrapolation
of this trend indicates that the expected average background level for DIXI at 10 MJ is approximately
11,000 counts, which remains below one-half of the dynamic range of the recording medium.

 Figure 6 - Time-resolved data obtained by the upgraded DIXI detector on shot N251122, and result of individual
pinhole image extraction routine on the third strip (b). The evolution of hotspot size (P0) as a function of time inferred
 from these images is given in (c). Analysis by S. Khan.

Targets with updated hohlraum window locations, compatible with the DIXI line of sight, became
available in October 2025. DIXI has since been used to provide equatorial X-ray imaging on three DT
shots: N251025, N251122, and N260404. Raw data and shape analysis for N251122 are presented in
Figure 6. The data obtained by DIXI on these three shots were of good quality; however, the fusion
yield was at most 0.5 MJ, which makes it difficult to infer performance at 10 MJ, especially given the
nonlinear background increase discussed above.

The expected signal-to-background performance of DIXI at 10 MJ was therefore estimated using
measured PDIXI image data and the observed relative background response of the two systems. Shot
N250222, which produced 5.2 MJ of fusion yield, provides a representative basis for this assessment.
On that shot, a single pinhole image recorded at bang time on PDIXI produced a peak signal of 6,000
counts, while the local background, averaged over one resolution element, was 89 counts rms,
corresponding to a signal-to-background ratio of 67, as shown in Figure 7.

Figure 7 - PDIXI data obtained on shot N250222, which produced 5.2 MJ of fusion yield. The bang time image has a
 S/B of 67 which can be scaled to project DIXI performance on a 10 MJ shot.

To project DIXI performance at 10 MJ, this measured result was scaled using the observed neutron-
background dependence and the assumption that rms background variation in film scales linearly
with the average neutron-induced background level, consistent with prior published work (Izumi et
al., Rev. Sci. Instrum. 81, 10E515 (2010)). Applying this scaling yields an estimated local background
variation for DIXI of approximately 175 counts rms on a 10 MJ shot. For a 6,000-count peak image,
this corresponds to an expected signal-to-background ratio of approximately 34.

Similar scaling predicts signal-to-background ratios of 23, 20, and 5 at 20 MJ, 25 MJ, and 33 MJ,
respectively. In practice, however, once fusion yield reaches 20 MJ and above, the elevated
background generated within the diagnostic will require operation at reduced gain to avoid film
saturation. Consequently, the signal amplitude will be lower than the reference 6,000-count peak
image assumed in this scaling, and achievable signal-to-background performance will become
increasingly limited by reduced-gain operation rather than by background scaling alone.

7. Conclusion
The results presented here demonstrate completion of MRT 8818, "Complete time-resolved X-ray
shape capability for a 10 MJ yield." NIF is now equipped with an upgraded equatorial X-ray imaging
capability whose projected performance at 10 MJ substantially exceeds the required completion
threshold of > 5. The implemented upgrades, specifically transition to film-based recording,
suppression of internally generated optical background through spectral filtering, and selection of a

lower-sensitivity film, restore practical equatorial time-resolved X-ray imaging capability for the
highest-yield NIF experiments.
