The association of a combined healthy lifestyle with the risk of cancer among older adults: a prospective large population-based cohort study
Authors: Shao-kun Sun, Tong Shen, Zi-jian Zhou, Qin Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMaintaining healthy habits may curb cancer incidence among Western populations. However, evidence from Chinese seniors remains limited, despite the country facing a rapidly aging population and the well-established fact that older adults are at a disproportionately high risk for cancer. Leveraging a large-scale prospective cohort of Chinese seniors, we examined how a composite lifestyle score correlates with their cancer risk.MethodsA total of 109 018 seniors whose first cancer diagnosis was recorded in Kunshan constituted the prospective cohort under investigation. A healthy lifestyle score was constructed based on five factors assessed at baseline: smoking, alcohol consumption, dietary habits, body mass index (BMI), and physical activity. Cox proportional hazards regression was employed to compute hazard ratios (HR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI), thereby assessing how the combined lifestyle metric influenced the risk of developing cancer.ResultsAmong 109,018 older adults enrolled between 2017 and 2025, throughout a mean follow-up period of 7.34 years, 2,115 incident cancers were documented. A significant inverse dose-response relationship was observed between the healthy lifestyle score and cancer risk (p for trend < 0.001). With the four additional lifestyle factors controlled for, diet and exercise were among the lifestyle factors most strongly associated with cancer risk. Adjusted HR for cancer risk with lifestyle score of 5, versus 0–2, were 0.37 (95% CI: 0.29–0.46) in the overall population, 0.32 (95%CI, 0.22–0.46) in men,0.39 (95%CI 0.29–0.52) in women, 0.45 (95%CI, 0.31–0.65) in those aged 60–70 years, and 0.32 (95%CI, 0.24–0.42) in those aged > 70 years. Notably, this protective association was substantially stronger among adults aged over 70 years compared to those aged 60–70 years.ConclusionEmbracing favorable lifestyle habits was associated with lower cancer susceptibility. This study emphasizes that heightened awareness is required among elderly Chinese populations regarding the potential benefits of healthy habits in cancer prevention.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-28202-6.Keywords: Healthy lifestyle, Cancer risk, Older adults, KunShan cohort

Introduction

Cancer denotes a collection of disorders in which mutant cells multiply without restraint, breach adjacent structures, and colonize distant organs, ultimately precipitating systemic collapse.Its development involves the interaction of multiple factors, including genetic, environmental, and lifestyle factors. Cancer not only destroys normal physiological structures but also exacerbates health deterioration by causing complications such as infections and malnutrition. As a primary contributor to mortality worldwide, cancer results in millions of deaths each year, mainly due to delayed diagnosis, treatment resistance, and metastatic spread. According to the latest International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) data, cancer cases represented approximately 20 million newly diagnosed instances and 9.7 million fatalities globally in the previous year—about 16% of total mortality [1].

In China, there are approximately 4.8247 million new cases of malignant tumors and about 2.574 million deaths in 2022, which place its absolute burden at the top among all countries globally [2]. It is particularly noteworthy that China is undergoing the largest-scale and fastest-paced social aging process in the world. The seventh national population census in 2020 showed that the population aged ≥ 60 years had reached 260 million, accounting for 18.7% of the total population [3]. The “inverted pyramid” shift in population structure has caused the epidemiological curve of cancer to shift rapidly to the right in the elderly group. The elderly are a high-incidence group for cancer. As age advances, the diminishing ability of cells to repair themselves, coupled with the accumulated exposure to various risk factors, results in a marked escalation in cancer prevalence. In the domestic context in 2022, individuals who were 60 years old or older constituted 55.8% of the newly diagnosed cancer cases and 68.2% of the cancer mortality cases [4].

Lifestyle factors serve a crucial role in cancer prevention, possessing the capacity to substantially decrease cancer development risk via behavioral adjustments. The Global Burden of Disease Study has repeatedly demonstrated that approximately 30% to 50% of cancer cases could be avoided through modifiable lifestyle factors [5]. As early as 2005, the research by Danaei et al. revealed that smoking, alcohol use, insufficient intake of fruits and vegetables, and lack of physical activity are the major modifiable risk factors for cancer mortality. Collectively, these behavioural and environmental risk factors account for 35% of cancer-related deaths [6]. Subsequently, multiple organizations, such as the American Cancer Society (ACS) and the World Cancer Research Fund/American Institute for Cancer Research (WCRF/AICR), have issued extensive behavioral recommendations for cancer prevention [7–10].

To date, the association between lifestyle factors and cancer risk has been extensively investigated in Western populations. Large-scale prospective cohort studies, such as the study based on the Netherlands Cohort, have consistently demonstrated that adherence to a healthy lifestyle is associated with a 20% reduction in overall breast cancer [11]. Similarly, a study based on the UK Biobank data found that an unfavorable lifestyle was associated with a higher risk of TC [12]. However, these findings may not be directly generalizable to non-Western populations and overall cancer due to differences in genetic background, lifestyle patterns, environmental exposures, and disease spectrum. Although a prospective study from China demonstrated an association between a healthy lifestyle and reduced cancer risk, it was limited to 23,734 retired participants [13]. Large-scale prospective cohort studies examining lifestyle factors and cancer remain scarce in China, particularly among the elderly population. This investigation represents a prospective cohort study executed in the community of Kunshan City, China. The Kunshan Cohort, a community-based prospective study initiated in 2017, investigates chronic disease determinants in older adults in Kunshan City, China. Unlike previous cohorts focused on single-occupation populations, this cohort enables comprehensive assessment of multiple lifestyle behaviors and their combined effects on cancer risk. The study population comprises elderly individuals who participated in annual health check-ups from January 2017 to June 2025, with newly diagnosed cancer cases identified to examine the link between lifestyle factors and incident cancer.

Methods

Study population

The Kunshan cohort represents a dynamic prospective cohort established in Suzhou city, Jiangsu province of China beginning in 2017. This prospective cohort study was based on data from the health examination program for older adults in Kunshan, China. Participants were community-dwelling residents aged 60 years or older who underwent annual physical examinations. All participants were recruited from the general community through local community health service centers, rather than from hospital-based settings. Specifically, 140,438 individuals were enrolled at baseline from January 2017 through June 2025. All study participants underwent questionnaire completion and physical assessments, while also providing fasting blood specimens for medical evaluation. The cohort has undergone annual follow-up procedures to refresh participant information. After excluding participants with a prior diagnosis of cancer at baseline (n = 1792), age < 60 years at baseline (n = 26422), without lifestyle data (n = 2019) or without information on follow-up data (n = 1187), 109,018 participants were enrolled in the final analysis (Fig. 1). The study protocol was sanctioned by the Medical Ethics Committee of The First People’s Hospital of Kunshan.

Flowchart of the study population

Determination of cancer onset

Cancer cases identified in the follow-up surveys were verified by examining the electronic medical records of hospitals that cover the entire elderly population. Tumors were categorized using codes C00–C97 of the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10).

Development of healthy lifestyle score (HLS)

A healthy lifestyle score was formulated through summation of healthy lifestyle factors. A five-point healthy-lifestyle score was constructed from five components: current non-smoking status, low-to-moderate alcohol consumption, adequate physical activity, a healthy diet, and Body Mass Index (BMI) within the optimal range. All lifestyle information was collected at the baseline enrollment via standardized questionnaires and physical measurements. The healthy lifestyle score was therefore calculated based on baseline data. Detailed definitions of healthy levels of lifestyle factors are as follows: (1) Current non-smoking status and alcohol consumption of 1–28 g/day for men, 1–14 g/day for women were classified as healthy levels [14]; (2) The healthy levels of physical activity were characterized as ≥ 150 min/week of exercise [14]; (3) Diet quality was assessed based on the consumption of fruits, vegetables, and meat, with daily consumption of fruits and vegetables and not consuming meat daily classified as healthy levels [13]; (4) BMI of 18.5–24.9 kg/m2 was categorized as the healthy range [15]. All covariate information was collected using standardized questionnaires administered during annual health check-ups: age (continuous), sex (male/female), education (high school or above and below high school), employment (ever employed and never employed), family history of cancer (yes/no), hypertension (yes/no), diabetes (yes/no), and cardiovascular disease (CVD) (yes/no).

Group differences were assessed using the Kruskal-Wallis test and the χ2 test. Hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the relationship between the composite healthy-lifestyle score and cancer incidence were calculated with Cox proportional-hazards models, using follow-up time as the time-scale. The proportional hazard assumption was tested by the Schoenfeld residuals method, and no significant violation of the assumption was found. Person-years at risk for participants were accrued from baseline to the first of cancer diagnosis, death, emigration, loss to follow-up, or the end of the study period.

The five lifestyle factors were summed into a single composite score for each participant, who were then classified into score-based groups. Because cells for 0, 1 and 2 points were sparse, these categories were merged into a single reference group (0–2 points) to yield stable risk estimates before relating the consolidated healthy-lifestyle score to cancer incidence. The robustness of the link between healthy lifestyle score and cancer was evaluated using two regression models with progressive confounder adjustment: Model 1 without covariate adjustments; Model 2 controlling for age, sex, education, employment, family history of cancer, hypertension, diabetes, cardiovascular disease (CVD). When assessing links between specific lifestyle factors and cancer risk, the remaining four lifestyle factors were adjusted in multivariate Cox regression models. Subgroup analyses by sex, age, hypertension and diatetes were conducted as exploratory analyses. Given the exploratory nature and the prespecified subgroups based on biological plausibility, no adjustment for multiple testing was applied. Results from these subgroup analyses should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating and require confirmation in independent cohorts.

All analyses were executed employing the R software (http://www.R-project.org, The R Foundation) and EmpowerStates (http://www.empowerstates.com, X & Y Solutions, Inc., Boston, MA). Statistical significance was determined at p < 0.05.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 2115 cancer cases were detected (1216 male and 899 female cases) during a median follow- up of 7.34 years among 109,018 participants in the current study. Among the 2,115 incident cancer cases, the distribution is as follows: lung cancer (450, 21.28%), gastrointestinal cancers (756, 35.74%), breast cancer (255, 12.06%), prostate cancer (323, 15.27%), hematologic malignancies (41, 1.94%), and other cancers (290, 13.71%).The results revealed that age, sex, height, education, employment, cardiovascular disease (CVD), Body Mass Index (BMI), exercise, diet, smoke, drink and the healthy lifestyle score (HLS) were both associated with cancer (Supplementary Table 1). The incidence rate was 264.5 per 100 000 person-years.

Age, sex, education, employment, family history of cancer, hypertension, diabetes, CVD, BMI, exercise, diet, smoke, drink were related to healthy lifestyle score (all p < 0.05) (Table 1).

Associations between single healthy lifestyle factor and cancer incidence in all people

The link between specific lifestyle factors and cancer risk demonstrated significance for BMI, exercise, diet, and smoking when adjustments were made for the remaining four lifestyle factors in the models (Fig. 2). Among the individual lifestyle factors, both healthy diet (HR = 0.60, 95% CI: 0.54–0.65) and exercise (HR = 0.64, 95% CI: 0.58–0.69) were associated with the most favorable cancer protection effects. No significant association was observed between alcohol consumption and cancer incidence(p = 0.1594).

Subgroup analyses by sex, age, diabetes, and hypertension

We performed subgroup analyses stratified by sex, age group, diabetes, and hypertension. Subgroup analysis by sex revealed that the observed associations were directionally consistent in both men and women (Supplementary Fig. 1A, 1B). Among men, those achieving the HLS = 5 were associated with a 64% lower cancer risk compared to those with a HLS = 0–2 group in Model 1 (HR = 0.36; 95% CI 0.25–0.51). The magnitude of this inverse association was virtually unchanged after full covariate adjustment in Model 2 (HR = 0.32; 95% CI 0.22–0.46). A comparable association was evident among women.

Age-stratified analyses indicated that the inverse link between lifestyle score and cancer risk was preserved across both age segments(Supplementary Fig. 2A, 2B). In Model 2, participants aged 60–70 years in the highest adherence category (HLS = 5) were associated with a 55% lower hazard of cancer compared to their counterparts with the lowest score (HLS 0–2) (HR = 0.45; 95% CI 0.31–0.65). Among individuals older than 70 years, the risk was even lower (HR = 0.32; 95% CI 0.24–0.42).

We further examined whether the association between healthy lifestyle score and cancer risk was modified by diabetes or hypertension status. As shown in Supplementary Table 2, the inverse association between a higher healthy lifestyle score (5 vs. 0–2 points) and cancer risk was consistently observed across subgroups. The HR was 0.40 (95% CI: 0.25–0.62) for participants with diabetes and 0.36 (95% CI: 0.28–0.46) for those without diabetes; for participants with hypertension, the HR was 0.30 (95% CI: 0.22–0.41) compared with 0.47 (95% CI: 0.34–0.66) for those without hypertension.

Discussion

In this large prospective investigation from the Kunshan elderly cohort, findings revealed that healthy lifestyle patterns were inversely linked to cancer risk and postponed the development of cancer among the elderly Chinese population.

Consistent with previous studies, lifestyle factors—including BMI, smoking, and alcohol consumption—play pivotal roles in carcinogenesis in our study [16]. Elevated BMI is robustly associated with increased risk of multiple cancers [17], while tobacco smoke delivers genotoxic agents that directly inflict DNA damage [18]. Alcohol consumption remains a major contributor to cancer burden, accounting for a substantial proportion of cancer deaths, particularly breast cancer in women [19].In this study, diet and exercise played key roles in cancer development, consistent with previous research. Physical inactivity is a well-established modifiable risk factor for carcinogenesis [5], and unhealthy lifestyle behaviors (including low physical activity) often cluster with metabolic risk factors such as hypertension and hyperglycemia, indirectly increasing cancer susceptibility [20]. Similarly, dietary habits are critical modifiable determinants of cancer prevention; insufficient intake of fruit, vegetables, fiber, and calcium is an independent risk factor for multiple malignancies, whereas a diet rich in fiber, low in red meat, and abundant in polyphenol-rich foods is associated with reduced cancer risk [5, 21].

Our findings align with previous large-scale cohort studies examining the combined effects of lifestyle factors on cancer risk. The study from the Netherlands Cohort reported a one-point increment of the healthy lifestyle score was accompanied by a HR reduction of 20% for overall breast cancer [11]. Unlike that study, which primarily focused on breast cancer, our study investigated overall cancer risk, including both solid tumors and hematologic malignancies. Similarly, the study conducted by Zhang et al. demonstrated that improve the quality of sleep and developing physical activity habits might yield benefits in mitigating the cancer risk [22]. While that study focused specifically on sleep and physical activity, the current study incorporated a wider range of lifestyle factors, including physical activity among others. Our results are consistent with the study demonstrating a comparable risk reduction in a large Chinese cohort. The Dongfeng-Tongji Cohort study reported a 26% reduction in cancer risk associated with a healthy lifestyle score (HR = 0.74, 95% CI: 0.64–0.86) [13]. However, our study extends this evidence by specifically focusing on adults aged ≥ 60 years (vs. 70.6% in the prior study) and employing a community-based design (vs. a single occupational cohort), thereby enhancing generalizability to the general older adult population. Also, a prospective study from the Northern Netherlands cohort showed that there are potential protective effects of healthy lifestyles on cancer, that study still focused on a Western population [23]. Notably, our study extends this evidence to an older population (≥ 60 years) in China, confirming that the protective effects of lifestyle factors persist into later life.

A review conducted by Marino et al. showed that adopting a single healthy behavior is insufficient to prevent cancer or to improve quality and length of life. Rather, adherence to multiple recommended lifestyles confers greater health benefits and disease protection [24]. This finding aligns with the pattern observed in our study, where a progressively increasing healthy lifestyle score was associated with a stepwise reduction in cancer risk (p for trend < 0.001). Furthermore, a study from Korea demonstrated that rather than any single behavior, it was the combination of unhealthy lifestyles—steady light drinking, steady moderate smoking, persistently low physical activity, and obesity—that identified the highest risk class in males. This class had a 1.47-fold higher (95% CI = 1.29–1.69) risk of all cancers than the healthy class [25]. Another study suggested that advice to adopt combined aspects of a healthy diet and lifestyle(a healthy weight, physical activity, alcohol and smoking avoidance, and a healthy diet rich in fruits, vegetables, whole grains, and cereals while discouraging red and processed meat) can be proposed to females to lower breast cancer risk [26].

In our study, after multivariable adjustment, men in the highest- versus lowest-healthy-lifestyle-score quintile were associated with a 68% lower risk of incident cancer (HR = 0.32; 95% CI,0.22–0.46), whereas the corresponding estimate in women was 61% (HR = 0.39; 95% CI,0.29–0.52). These data suggest that higher adherence to a healthy lifestyle is associated with substantially lower cancer risk, with consistent associations observed irrespective of sex, corroborating large-scale Korean cohort findings in which an unhealthy lifestyle pattern was similarly associated with elevated cancer risk in both men and womem [27]. Although men and women differ in hormone profiles, metabolic enzyme levels, and immune function, but tobacco carcinogens, excess alcohol, obesity, and inactivity promote tumorigenesis through shared mechanisms—DNA damage and chronic inflammation—that operate similarly in both sexes. Healthy lifestyles therefore protect men and women alike, supporting population-wide prevention strategies.

Although younger adults exhibit larger absolute behavioural shifts, our data confirm that behavioural plasticity persists into advanced age. Among participants aged 60–70 years, those in the highest-lifestyle-score quintile were associated with a 55% lower cancer incidence compared to their lowest-score counterparts. Remarkably, such an inverse association was still observable in individuals aged > 70 years, highlighting that lifestyle factors remain relevant even in older age. This pattern aligns closely with findings from Chinese older adults: within the 58–69-year age band, the inverse link between healthy lifestyle score and incident cancer displayed no difference between participants above and below 65 years [13]. Substantial evidence suggests that maintaining a healthy lifestyle is associated with considerably lower cancer risk across both middle-aged (50–64 years) and older (≥ 65 years) populations [28]. A prospective cohort investigation of community-dwelling older adults demonstrated that individuals classified as lifestyle-active exhibited a significantly higher proportion of CD4+CD45RA+ T lymphocyte percentage. These findings indicate that sustained physical activity can partially restore immune competence and attenuate immunosenescence [29].

Although individual lifestyle factors have been linked to cancer, the composite lifestyle score used here better reflects the synergistic interplay of multiple real-world behaviors. A healthy lifestyle may lower cancer risk through integrated mechanisms: First, minimizing cumulative DNA damage by avoiding tobacco and alcohol carcinogens [30]; Second, modulating chronic inflammation through weight management, anti-inflammatory diets, and physical activity [31]; Third, strengthening immune competence via exercise and nutrient-rich diets that enhance tumor immunosurveillance [29, 32]; Fourth, controlling hormone levels through weight management and dietary modifications that regulate sex hormone metabolism [33]; Finally, reducing oxidative stress via dietary antioxidants and avoidance of pro-oxidant exposures [34]. Collectively, these mechanisms operate synergistically—rather than independently—to lower cancer risk, highlighting the value of a composite lifestyle score in capturing cumulative and interacting effects.

Our findings have important public health implications. The observed association between a healthy lifestyle score and lower cancer risk in Chinese older adults (aged ≥ 60 years) suggests that promoting healthy behaviors—including weight management, physical activity, balanced diet, smoking avoidance, and moderate alcohol consumption—could serve as an effective population-based strategy for cancer prevention. Notably, the protective effect was consistent across sex and was stronger among adults aged over 70 years, indicating that lifestyle interventions remain beneficial even in advanced age. The use of a composite lifestyle score, which reflects the synergistic interplay of multiple behaviors, aligns with real-world practice and supports holistic health promotion approaches. These findings provide a robust evidence base for developing lifestyle-based cancer prevention programs tailored to the growing aging population in China.

The advantages of this research encompass its substantial sample size and prospective methodology with a median follow-up period of 7.34 years. All participants were community-dwelling adults aged ≥ 60 years from the Kunshan cohort, ensuring a homogeneous study base. We ascertained incidence of all solid tumours and some haematological malignancies, enabling a comprehensive assessment of lifestyle-related “pan-cancer” protection rather than focusing on single cancer types. Subgroup analyses confirmed that the link between lifestyle score and cancer risk was significant and consistent in both men and women and across different age strata. When interpreting the findings, it is important to note that hazard ratios represent relative rather than absolute risk reductions. In our study population, the baseline cancer incidence was 264.5 per 100,000 person-years. Thus, while a higher healthy lifestyle score was associated with a substantial relative risk reduction, the corresponding absolute risk reduction should be considered in light of this baseline incidence.

Several limitations should be noted. First, some variables were obtained by self-administered questionnaires, which may introduce reporting bias. Second, lifestyle factors were assessed only at baseline and were treated as static variables. As lifestyle behaviors are dynamic and may change over time, this could lead to potential misclassification and might not reflect the impact of long-term exposure or changes in habits on cancer risk. Future prospective studies with repeated measurements of lifestyle factors are warranted to address this limitation. Third, we did not account for death as a competing event in our analysis of cancer incidence. In an aging population, death may preclude the occurrence of cancer, and failure to consider this competing risk could potentially lead to an overestimation of the cumulative incidence of cancer. However, the overall mortality rate in our cohort was relatively modest during follow-up. Nonetheless, future studies using competing risk models are warranted to validate our findings. Fourth, site-specific cancer analyses were not conducted due to limited case numbers for certain cancer types and the extreme class imbalance between cases and the cancer-free population, which would have compromised the statistical stability and reliability of the estimates. Future studies with larger sample sizes are required to examine site-specific associations. Fifth, although we adjusted for education and employment status, marital status and sleep quality were not available and residual confounding by these unmeasured factors cannot be excluded. Finally, all participants were elderly adults from the Kunshan cohort. While this focused population allowed for detailed assessment of lifestyle factors in a specific demographic, the generalizability of our findings to other populations or regions remains uncertain. External validation in diverse cohorts is warranted.

Conclusions

In summary, a higher healthy lifestyle score was associated with a substantially lower risk of overall cancer in Chinese older adults. The protective association was consistent across sex groups. Notably, the inverse association was significantly stronger among adults aged over 70 years, suggesting that this age group may derive particularly substantial benefit from lifestyle interventions. These findings highlight the potential of promoting healthy behaviors as an effective strategy for cancer prevention in older populations. However, given that the study population was drawn from a specific region in China, further research is needed to confirm the generalizability of these findings to other populations.
