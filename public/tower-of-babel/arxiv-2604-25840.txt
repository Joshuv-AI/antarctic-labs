PSI-Bench: Towards Clinically Grounded and Interpretable Evaluation of Depression Patient Simulators
Nguyen Khoi Hoang, Shuhaib Mehri, Tse-An Hsu, Yi-Jyun Sun, Quynh Xuan Nguyen Truong, Khoa D Doan, Dilek Hakkani-Tür, [10pt] University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign, VinUniversity[5pt] {nkhoang2, dilek}@illinois.edu

Abstract
Patient simulators are gaining traction in mental health training by providing scalable exposure to complex and sensitive patient interactions. Simulating depressed patients is particularly challenging, as safety constraints and high patient variability complicate simulations and underscore the need for simulators that capture diverse and realistic patient behaviors. However, existing evaluations heavily rely on LLM-judges with poorly specified prompts and do not assess behavioral diversity. We introduce PSI-Bench, an automatic evaluation framework that provides interpretable, clinically grounded diagnostics of depression patient simulator behavior across turn-, dialogue-, and population-level dimensions. Using PSI-Bench, we benchmark seven LLMs across two simulator frameworks and find that simulators produce overly long, lexically diverse responses, show reduced variability, resolve emotions too quickly, and follow a uniform negative-to-positive trajectory. We also show that the simulation framework has a larger impact on fidelity than the model scale. Results from a human study demonstrate that our benchmark is strongly aligned with expert judgments. Our work reveals key limitations of current depression patient simulators and provides an interpretable, extensible benchmark to guide future simulator design and evaluation.

Introduction

Large Language Models (LLMs) are increasingly used to simulate human behavior in interactive settings where real-world practice is costly, sensitive, or difficult to scale
(
33
;
47
;
49
)
. In mental health education, LLM-based patient simulators can generate clinically plausible responses and act as stand-ins for real patients, offering a practical training environment for novice clinicians
(
21
;
44
;
23
)
. The fidelity of these simulated patients is critical, as divergence from real patient communication, such as in language use or conversational progression, can risk reinforcing misconceptions and undermining training effectiveness
(
26
;
1
;
29
)
. Therefore, it is essential to evaluate simulator fidelity with real patient behavior. In this work, we focus on a particularly challenging domain of mental health: depression. Simulating depression is difficult because LLMs’ safety training limits their ability to mimic depressive speech patterns
(
21
)
and because depression varies widely between individuals
(
11
)
.

Current automatic evaluations of patient simulator fidelity rely on LLM-judges to assess how well generated responses align with the target patient profiles
(
21
;
44
)
. They typically score responses on a 5-point Likert scale
(
20
)
using generic prompts, entrusting the LLM-judge to infer nuanced clinical distinctions without explicit grounding in psychological theory or real patient data. LLM-judges are not always reliable evaluators in this setting, as
44
reports opposite trends between LLM-judge fidelity scores and expert ratings. Moreover, these evaluations lack the interpretability needed to reveal
where
a simulator diverges from real patient behavior, offering little guidance for improving future simulators. Additionally, existing evaluation approaches
(
37
;
44
;
21
)
report an average score without examining whether the simulators reflect the distributional diversity of real patient populations. In real clinical settings, patients can exhibit varying linguistic and interactional patterns
36
, especially among depressed patients
11
. If simulators fail to reflect such distributional diversity, they may produce behaviorally homogeneous patients and introduce bias in clinician training
38
.

Recognizing these limitations, we propose
P
atient-
SI
mulator-Bench (
PSI-Bench
), a framework for interpretable, clinically grounded evaluation of depression simulator across turn-, dialogue-, and population-level dimensions. PSI-Bench comprises five dimensions supported by established psychological and psycholinguistic research
(
35
;
40
;
32
;
41
;
39
;
5
)
, ensuring that the evaluation meaningfully captures the characteristics of depression behavior. Using a shared set of patient profiles, we evaluate simulated and real responses independently under these dimensions, and compare the resulting statistics using distance- and similarity-based measures to quantify simulator alignment with real patient behavior. To evaluate whether PSI-Bench provides meaningful and reliable diagnostics of depression simulator behavior, we use PSI-Bench to investigate three research questions:

•

RQ1:
How do responses generated by current depression patient simulators differ from real patients responses across turn-level, dialogue-level, and population-level?

•

RQ2:
How do different LLMs and simulation strategies affect simulator fidelity?

•

RQ3:
Do the findings for RQ1 and RQ2 using PSI-Bench align with expert judgments?

We evaluate seven LLMs across two simulation frameworks (PATIENT-
\Psi

(
44
)
and Roleplay-doh
(
23
)
), and reveal key simulator limitations not captured by previous automatic evaluation approaches. At the population level, simulators show less variability and diverge from real patients in dialogue progression: they move through therapeutic stages more rapidly and follow a uniform negative-to-positive emotional trajectory. They also produce overly long, lexically diverse responses at the message level, and exhibit a more spread out presence of depressive markers at the dialogue level.

We aggregate scores across all dimensions into a unified alignment score to rank the simulators (Table
1
), while retaining interpretable diagnostics at each level. This allows us to examine how simulation frameworks, model scale, and model family influence fidelity. We find that the simulation framework has a larger impact than LLM capability: PATIENT-
\Psi
consistently outperforms Roleplay-doh, and smaller models sometimes surpass larger ones, suggesting that increased linguistic sophistication can reduce behavioral fidelity to real patient communication. Finally, we validate our benchmark via expert evaluation with 20 mental health experts. The results show near-perfect agreement with the PSI-Bench rankings (
\kappa=0.82
), supporting PSI-Bench as a reliable evaluation framework.

Overall, our work aims to enable future research towards advancing depression patient simulators through these main contributions:

•

We introduce PSI-Bench, an interpretable, clinically grounded benchmark for evaluating patient simulators by alignment with real patients, showing strong agreement with expert judgments.

•

Using PSI-Bench, we highlight previously underexplored simulator divergences from real patient behavior and the impact of simulation framework, scale, and model family on simulator fidelity.

Related Work

2.1
LLM-based Simulators

User Simulators for Dialogue Systems
Since the mid-1990s, user simulators have been widely studied to address the high cost and difficulty of collecting real conversational data, particularly in specialized domains
(
34
)
. While traditional rule-based, statistical, and early neural approaches struggled to capture the complexity and naturalness of human behavior, LLMs show potential in overcoming these challenges with their powerful language capabilities
(
30
)
. Existing work mainly explores two applications of LLM-based simulators. First, they are used to
train and evaluate AI systems
, either by generating large volumes of synthetic dialogue data
(
10
;
4
)
, or by serving as interactive environments for online reinforcement learning
(
7
)
. Second, they are used as
training tools for human learners
in domains where practicing with real individuals is costly or sensitive, such as nurse communication training
(
16
)
and counseling
(
28
)
.

LLM-based Patient Simulators in Mental Health
Within the counseling domain, several works
(
8
;
17
)
focus on the first application – generating counseling datasets to improve counselor LLMs, where a patient simulator’s design and evaluation are less prioritized. In contrast, our evaluation framework targets the second application – simulating patients for training novice counselors, where the central emphasis is on designing simulated patients with realistic behaviors
(
21
;
44
;
23
)
.

2.2
Evaluation of Patient Simulators

Evaluating User Simulators
Before the advent of LLMs,
34
grouped the evaluation of user simulators into two groups:
turn-level
metrics that evaluate individual responses (e.g., precision and recall) and
dialogue-level
metrics that evaluate general interaction (e.g., task completion). Though fast and reproducible, these traditional metrics rely on surface lexical overlap and miss deeper nuances
(
13
)
. Along with the rise of LLM-based simulators, LLM-based evaluation methods emerged with their strong reasoning and language capabilities, enabling more context-aware assessment but also introducing new systematic bias
(
13
;
12
)
. Evaluation can also be framed as
intrinsic
versus
extrinsic
: intrinsic methods assess simulator outputs directly (e.g., realism and diversity), while extrinsic methods measure downstream task impact.
29
show that extrinsic evaluation alone can be misleading as over-cooperative LLM users may inflate system performance, motivating greater emphasis on intrinsic methods.

Evaluating LLM-based Patient Simulators in Counseling
Simulation systems aimed at training or benchmarking counselor LLMs
(
17
;
43
)
are often automatically evaluated using extrinsic metrics of counseling effectiveness. Consistent with the findings of
29
, such simulated patients can be implicitly cooperative and demonstrate rapid improvement
(
1
)
, thus increasing these extrinsic performance scores.

Patient simulators aimed at training novice counselors are typically evaluated for alignment, realism, and training utility
(
21
;
44
;
23
;
37
)
. Expert human evaluation and automatic methods assess profile alignment and behavioral fidelity, with experts also judging pedagogical effectiveness.
It is often impractical to use expert evaluation alone as it is costly and not scalable, while current automatic evaluation pipelines, which primarily use LLM-Judge Likert ratings
(
44
)
, have significant limitations. They are reported to be unreliable or even contradict expert judgments
(
44
)
. Additionally, these automatic evaluations typically report an average score without examining broader behavioral distributions reflecting real-world clinical variability. Our evaluation framework addresses these limitations.

3
PSI-Bench

Figure
1
provides an overview of PSI-Bench. We utilize the Eeyore dataset
21
, derived from public depression-related real dialogue datasets, where each conversation is paired with a patient profile describing the situation and traits (see Appendix
A.1
). We generate simulated conversations by conditioning patient simulators on these patient profiles, with another LLM as the therapist to sustain the dialogue. Simulators generate messages turn by turn using prior context, with each turn comprising one patient and one therapist message. Both real and synthetic datasets are then evaluated independently using PSI-Bench dimensions, and the resulting statistics are compared.

We ground our assessment in behaviors observed in depression-related communication and analyze simulator behavior across five interpretable dimensions.
Section
3.1
introduces and independently applies these dimensions to real and synthetic conversations.
Section
3.2
describes how simulators’ scores are compared relative to real patient scores at multiple levels:
turn-level
(e.g., response length),
conversation-level
(e.g., progression and emotional change), and
population-level
(e.g., diversity across profiles), capturing realistic patterns and variation observed in practice. Each dimension is aggregated into normalized scalar scores for comparison, ranking, and correlation with expert evaluation.

3.1
Evaluation Dimensions

Narrative-Emotion Processes (NEP) Markers
are standardized categories rated by observers that capture how patients manifest narrative content and emotional experience during psychotherapy
(
3
)
. Because the evolving pattern of these markers over a session reflects therapeutic progress, they provide a basis for assessing whether simulated patients replicate the change dynamics observed in real clinical conversations. The markers are grouped into three categories:

Problem
: repetitive, “stuck” storylines with limited reflection;
Transition
: more coherent and reflective storytelling as clients begin to examine their thoughts and emotions;
Change
: clear shifts in emotional response and thought patterns, where clients may show a new understanding of key events in their life. To track these patterns, we prompt
gpt-oss-120b

(
31
)
to classify each patient turn in conversations as Problem, Transition, Change, or Filler (neutral procedural replies without meaningful therapeutic content), using explicit definitions and decision criteria (Appendix
A.3
).

Emotion Expression

We examine how simulated and real patients express emotions over the course of a conversation. Beck’s cognitive theory of depression posits that depressed individuals have a negative view of themselves, others, and the future
(
5
)
, leading to more negative emotional expression in conversation. Supporting this,
41
;
35
show that depression correlates with increased negative and decreased positive emotion words. Building on these insights, we investigate the temporal dynamics of emotional expression to determine whether simulated and real patients differ in how their emotions evolve during a dialogue. We employ
gpt-oss-120b
to classify each patient message into one of Plutchik’s eight primary emotions (
trust, fear, anger, disgust, joy, anticipation, sadness
, and
surprise
) along with a
neutral
category. Finally, we analyze the population-level distributions of these labels as conversations progress.

Lexical Diversity

39
showed that patients with depression often exhibit repetition in words or ideas. We evaluate whether simulators can mimic this behavior despite LLMs’ tendency to generate highly diverse text
(
19
)
. We also investigate how lexical diversity varies across real patients and whether the same LLM conditioned on different profiles can mirror such variation. We compute conversation-level lexical diversity by concatenating all patient messages in a conversation and applying the Measure of Textual Lexical Diversity (MTLD)
(
25
)
. MTLD is defined as the mean length of word sequences required to reach a fixed ratio (
0.72
) of unique words to total words (Appendix
A.4
). Additionally, we concatenate all patient messages across profiles into a single corpus and compute its MTLD. A lower corpus-level MTLD than the average per-conversation MTLD would suggest some degree of shared vocabulary across patients.

Response Length

Patients with depression tend to produce shorter utterances and more limited content in conversation
(
40
;
39
)
, while LLMs tend to be more verbose
(
19
)
. We assess response length by computing average number of words per message and average number of words per sentence.

Linguistic Markers of Depression

Psychological research identifies consistent linguistic patterns in depressed patients that correlate with increased use of: (1)
absolutist terms
: words denoting totality of magnitude or probability, reflecting absolutist thinking underlying many cognitive distortions (e.g. "always", "never")
(
2
)
, (2)
depressive words
associated with low mood and hopelessness
(
18
)
, and (3)
non-fluencies
: hesitations and fillers that may signal cognitive or emotional strain
(
32
)
. The complete lexicon used in our analysis is provided in Table
7
. These marker categories enable a structured comparison of depressive linguistic tendencies between humans and simulators. We analyze the proportion of messages containing at least one marker and the rate of marker occurrences per 1,000 tokens.

3.2
Comparative Evaluation

We compare simulator scores with real patient statistics to quantify alignment with real patient behavior. All metrics are converted to
0
–
100
similarity scale, where higher values indicate higher alignment. We convert distance or divergence measures
d
to similarity scores:
100-d
. We denote simulator statistics by
\psi
and real patient by
r
.

Divergence of progression dynamics
For NEP markers and emotions, we compare the temporal progression of label distributions. To ensure comparability, we restrict the analysis to the first
T=16
turns for both real and synthetic conversations, yielding aligned turn-level distributions. For each turn
i
, we compute a population-level distribution on labels by aggregating all patient messages at that turn across profiles.
This produces a sequence of
T
turn-level distributions
(\psi_{i})_{i=1}^{T}
for a simulator and
(r_{i})_{i=1}^{T}
for the real patient population, where each
\psi_{i}
and
r_{i}
is a distribution on labels at turn
i
. This formulation enables us to track how the proportions of the labels evolve over the course of a conversation. To quantify the divergence in progression dynamics between a simulator and the real population,
we compute the symmetric Jensen-Shannon divergence (JSD)
27
at each turn and average across all turns:

\mathrm{d}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{i=1}^{T}\mathrm{JSD}(\psi_{i}\,\|\,r_{i})

(1)

Normalized Wasserstein distance
For lexical diversity, we compare the distribution of per-conversation MTLD scores between simulated and real patients using the Wasserstein distance
42
, which captures difference in both distribution shape and spread. We then convert this distance into a similarity measure and apply min–max normalization.

Log-ratio similarity score
As simulated patients are more verbose than real patients by large factors, we account for this scale disparity using log scale. We measure response length alignment using a log-ratio similarity over average words per message and per sentence. For each average word count
c
, we compute:

s(c_{\psi})=100\cdot\exp\left(-\left|\log\frac{c_{\psi}}{c_{\text{r}}}\right|\right).

(2)

The final response length similarity score is the average of the two similarity scores.

Difference from real statistics
For linguistic markers of depression, we measure the deviation from real patient statistics using the relative (percentage) difference for
ptk
and the absolute difference for
msg
, as it is already a bounded percentage. The final distance
d
is the average of the two.

4
Experimental Setup

4.1
Simulation Strategies

We employ two main patient simulation frameworks instantiated with one proprietary and six open-source LLMs spanning three model families (Table
6
). To ensure a fair comparison, we use the same therapist LLM
gpt-oss-20b

(
31
)
across all settings. Generation is capped at 16 turns or the number of patient turns in the corresponding real conversation, whichever is smaller. Our experiment spans 14 dataset configurations across framework–LLM pairings.

PATIENT-
\Psi

(
44
)
is a cognitive behavioral therapy–based patient simulation framework that constructs diverse patient
cognitive models
– a structured and interconnected representation of thoughts, beliefs, emotions, and behaviors. (More details in Appendix
A.2
)

Roleplay-doh

(
23
)

is a human-LLM collaborative framework that enables experts to customize patient simulators by converting qualitative feedback into natural language “principles” governing behavior. It utilizes a specialized principle-adherence pipeline to verify and self-refine responses to ensure alignment with these expert-defined rules and the underlying patient persona.

4.2
Human study design

To assess the reliability of our metrics and answer RQ3, we conducted a human evaluation study via Prolific with 20 mental health experts, divided into four groups (5 annotators per group), each evaluating conversations from a different set of three patient profiles. For each profile, annotators performed two tasks: (1)
message-level classification
, labeling each patient message by its primary emotion and narrative-emotion process, to evaluate the reliability of our LLM-based classifications; (2)
pairwise comparison
, selecting which of two simulated conversation snippets better reflects how a real patient would communicate, to assess agreement with our benchmark rankings. More details (annotation interface, annotator demographics, etc.) are provided in Appendix
B
.

From our PSI-Bench benchmark rankings, we randomly sample conversation pairs from simulators with a minimum ranking difference of four. We evaluate alignment by whether experts consistently prefer outputs from higher-ranked simulators. For both tasks, we use majority voting within each group to determine the final human annotation. Ties are frequent in emotion classification, especially between similar emotions (e.g., fear and sadness), likely due to subjectivity and the fact that a single message can convey multiple emotions. We accommodate these by evaluating agreement at a coarser level, grouping emotions into three categories:
positive
(trust, joy, anticipation),
negative
(fear, sadness, anger), and
neutral
. We report in Table
2
the model-human agreement percentage and Cohen’s
\kappa
, which measures agreement between two raters while accounting for chance. We also report agreement among human annotators using pairwise agreement and Fleiss’
\kappa
, widely used to assess inter-rater reliability among multiple annotators.

5.1
RQ1: Divergence from Real patients

Comparing real and synthetic conversations across our evaluation dimensions reveals clear divergences in simulator behavior from real patients. We summarize the key findings below, with detailed results in Appendix
C
.

Premature resolution of emotional narratives
Figure
2(a)
displays the progression of population-level distributions of NEP markers across dialogue turns. This figure shows that simulated patients progress through narrative–emotion stages much faster than humans. Although all populations mostly start the conversation in the
problem
stage, real patients transition gradually, with over
40\%
remaining in this stage throughout the first 16 turns. In contrast, simulators quickly shift away from
problem
by around turn 3, with rapid percentage increases in
transition
and
change
. This suggests premature narrative resolution and greater emotional awareness than real patients, who typically require longer interaction to make therapeutic progress.

Uniform, rapid negative-to-positive progression
Figure
2(b)
displays the progression of population-level emotion distributions across dialogue turns (
Disgust
and
surprise
are omitted due to negligible percentages). This figure shows that
fear
and
sadness
dominate simulator messages in early turns, then rapidly decline as positive emotions (
trust, joy, anticipation
) increase by around the fifth turn. This pattern suggests simulators’ consistent emotional trajectory across profiles: shifting from distress to resolution within a few messages. In contrast, human emotional expression is more diffuse and stable: negative emotions appear early but do not dominate and taper gradually, while positive emotions rise modestly before declining.

Lack of conversational neutrality & spontaneity

Human conversations frequently include filler messages and neutral responses, with roughly
25
–
50\%
of profiles not conveying explicit emotion at any given turn. In contrast, simulator outputs almost always map to a NEP marker and a clear emotion, with barely any filler or neutral content beyond the initial turn. This suggests that simulators lack the conversational naturalness of real interactions which often include more neutral and spontaneous behavior, instead producing responses that are consistently task-focused and emotionally explicit. As this tendency exists for all simulator settings, in Section
5.2
we re-normalize distributions by excluding
filler
and
neutral
messages.

Higher lexical diversity, reduced variability
Figure
3
shows that human patients exhibit lower median and more widely distributed MTLD scores, reflecting substantial individual variability. In contrast, almost all simulated patients produce higher and tightly ranged MTLD values, indicating uniformly diverse output that fails to capture the lower diversity and greater variability of real patient populations. Table
3
in Appendix compares mean per-conversation diversity to corpus-level diversity. Interestingly, only human data shows a considerable drop at the corpus level, while simulated patients show similar scores for both. This may suggest that real patients have some lexical overlap between patients while simulated patients do not.

Higher verbosity
Real patient messages are concise (averaging 18 words/message, 9 words/sentence), while simulated patients are substantially more verbose (64 – 319 words/message, 15 – 28 words/sentence).

Higher prevalence but lower density of depression markers

Combined with divergence in verbosity, Figure
4
highlights differences in how linguistic markers of depression are expressed. Simulated patients consistently lie to the left of human data in marker rates but to the right in marker prevalence, indicating that markers appear in more messages but with lower token-level density. This pattern suggests that simulators repeatedly signal depression cues across many messages, but distribute them sparsely within longer utterances. In contrast, human patients express markers in fewer messages but at higher token-normalized rates, indicating that when markers do appear, they are more densely concentrated within shorter utterances.

5.3
RQ3: Correlation with Expert judgements

Table
2
reports agreement between expert judgments and our benchmark rankings and LLM-based classifications. Following the interpretation of
15
, inter-annotator agreement is moderate across tasks (Fleiss’
\kappa
between 0.43 and 0.57). However, agreements between our methods and expert majority labels are high: pairwise preference shows almost perfect alignment (
\kappa=0.82
), while NEP marker and emotion classification show substantial agreement (
\kappa=0.7
and
0.75
, respectively). These results confirm that PSI-Bench provides reliable and expert-aligned evaluations.

Expert annotators’ qualitative feedback corroborates our findings in Section
5.1
on divergences between simulated and real patient behavior. Less realistic conversations were described as overly structured, polished, and cognitively organized. They resemble a “coherent story” rather than spontaneous distress, often featuring sophisticated vocabulary and lacking hesitation or disfluency. Annotators also noted that simulated patients are excessively self-reflective and articulate, which was considered implausible in early interactions where real patients tend to struggle with articulation. Additionally, simulated responses were often prematurely solution-oriented, proposing coping strategies or rational interpretations, unlike real patients who may initially feel overwhelmed or cognitively blocked.

While annotators more often justified their choices by critiquing the less realistic option, some identified reasons certain conversations appeared more realistic. These were described as shorter, less certain, and more fragmented, using simpler language and exhibiting hesitation, self-correction, and slightly disjointed thought patterns. Notably, these conversations largely came from our top-ranked settings. In one instance, an annotator remarked that a conversation from our top-1 setting “seems most human and the realest of all the conversations in this block” (Table
12
). These feedback further provide qualitative support for PSI-Bench’s alignment with expert judgments.

6
Conclusion

We introduce PSI-Bench, an interpretable and clinically grounded evaluation framework that decomposes simulator behavior into psychologically meaningful dimensions and evaluates alignment with real patient conversations. Empirical analysis reveals underexplored divergences between simulated and real patients, showing that simulators are less varied, transition through therapeutic states more quickly, follow a stereotyped emotional trajectory, and produce overly verbose, lexically rich responses compared to real patients. Across configurations, we find that the simulation framework plays a more critical role than the LLM model. Importantly, PSI-Bench demonstrates strong alignment with expert judgments, both quantitatively and qualitatively, supporting its validity as a reliable automatic evaluation framework. We hope PSI-Bench can facilitate future research towards advancing depression patient simulators.

A.4
MTLD Formulation

We use the Measure of Textual Lexical Diversity (MTLD)
(
25
)
, defined as the mean length of word sequences required to reach a fixed type–token ratio (TTR) threshold of
0.72
, where TTR is number of different words (type) divided by total number of words (token). A major limitation of traditional lexical diversity indices is their sensitivity to text length – after reaching a
point of stabilization
, TTR tends to monotonically decrease as the number of tokens increases
(
25
)
. MTLD mitigates this issue by measuring how many tokens, on average, are required to reach this stabilization point. Each time the TTR threshold is reached, a factor count
F
is incremented and TTR computation is reset for subsequent tokens. Lexical diversity is then estimated as the ratio of the total number of words
N
to
F
, computed separately for forward (
F_{\text{fwd}}
) and backward (
F_{\text{bwd}}
) passes through the text. The final MTLD score is obtained by averaging these two estimates, which has been shown to yield more stable measurements.

\text{MTLD}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{N}{F_{\text{fwd}}}+\frac{N}{F_{\text{bwd}}}\right)

(3)

Higher MTLD values indicate more diverse vocabulary use, while lower values reflect more repetitive lexical patterns. As recommended in prior work
(
9
;
48
;
14
)
, we restrict our analysis to conversations with at least 100 patient tokens, since MTLD is unstable for very short texts but more reliable above this length.

Appendix B
Human Study Details

The Human Study procedure was reviewed by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) and determined not to meet the definition of Human Subjects Research.

Participants.

We recruited 20 annotators from Prolific, assigned into four groups of five. Eligibility was restricted to individuals fluent in English and with current job roles in mental health or related care professions, including clinical psychologist, psychologist, counsellor (mental health), occupational therapist, and social worker. Participants were compensated at a rate of $15/hour. Table
8
shows anonymized demographic statistics of these 20 annotators.

Classification Task.

In the message-level classification task, the annotators labeled each patient message with one label
dominant emotion
and one
narrative-emotion process
. Annotators were instructed to classify
patient turns only
; therapist messages were shown only as context. Figure
7
shows the classification interface.

For dominant emotion, annotators selected one label from the following options:
anger, disgust, fear, joy, sadness, surprise, anticipation, trust
, or
neutral
if no clear emotion was present. For narrative-emotion process, annotators selected one label from
problem, transition, change
or
filler
.

Pairwise comparison task.

In the pairwise comparison task, annotators were shown a patient profile, a reference real conversation for that profile from the Eeyore dataset, and two synthetic conversations generated by different simulators. Annotators were instructed to refer to the real conversation for the general style and progression of authentic patient communication, not as a strict template. They should use both the reference conversation and their clinical judgment to decide which conversation more closely reflects how a real patient would communicate.

Figure
8
shows the task overview, and Figure
9
,
11
and
11
illustrates the task interface for the three annotation steps of each profile. In Step 1, annotators read through the patient profile and the reference real conversation. In Step 2, they reviewed two synthetic conversations (only the first three messages) shown side by side. The therapist responses were included solely for conversational context. In Step 3, annotators submitted their preference and provide a short explanation. Annotators were encouraged to make a preference whenever possible, selecting “About Equal” only when no meaningful difference in realism could be identified.

Aggregation and ties.

For both tasks, annotations were aggregated within each annotator group using majority vote. In the pairwise comparison task, vote ties were rare (3 out of 60 unique pairs) and were excluded from the majority-vote analysis. In the classification task, ties occurred more frequently, especially between emotions with similar valence, such as fear and sadness. This likely reflects the subjective nature of emotion annotation and the fact that a single message may convey multiple overlapping emotions.
