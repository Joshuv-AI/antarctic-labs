Thoracic endoscopic spine surgery: systematic review of the literature and exploring the margin of benefit
Authors: Hammad A. Khan, Adhith Palla, Blake Perdikis, Ilya Laufer, Jian Shen, Sanjay Konakondla, Albert E. Telfeian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThoracic disc herniations are rare and challenging to treat surgically due to their complex regional anatomy, and conventional open approaches are associated with considerable morbidity. While full endoscopic approaches have demonstrated success in the cervical and lumbar spine, their role in the thoracic spine is not as well defined. We performed a systematic review of the literature on full endoscopic surgery for thoracic disc herniations in order to elucidate its safety, efficacy, and potential benefit over conventional open approaches.MethodsThe PubMed, Web of Science, and Embase databases were queried from inception to February 2026. Studies describing endoscopic approaches to thoracic disc herniations in at least 5 adult patients with reported postoperative outcomes were included. Exclusion criteria included exclusive reporting of open/thoracoscopic approaches, cadaveric studies, and non-English studies. Data on patient characteristics, operative variables, and perioperative outcomes were extracted.ResultsOur systematic review included 23 articles with 695 patients, of which 586 (84.3%) underwent endoscopic surgery and 109 (15.7%) underwent traditional open surgery. In the endoscopic cohort, the most common indication for surgery was radiculopathy (44.2%), followed by myelopathy (43.7%); and myelopathy (70.6%) in the open cohort. Several calcified or partially calcified discs were treated in both the endoscopic and open cohorts (29.8% vs. 36.4%). Endoscopic approaches were associated with shorter operative time (82.3 vs. 201.4 minutes), lower blood loss (18.8 vs. 355.1 mL), reduced fusion rates (1.4% vs. 94.1%), shorter length of stay (2.65 vs. 10.5 days), and lower cost ($7,625.7 vs. $21,055) compared to open approaches. Readmission (3.3% vs. 9.2%), reoperation (3.7% vs. 5.5%), and disc recurrence rates (2.2% vs. 0%) were comparable between cohorts, as were pain and functional outcomes.ConclusionsFull endoscopic approaches to thoracic disc herniations are safe and effective alternatives to traditional open approaches in carefully selected patients, offering meaningful advantages with comparable postoperative outcomes. High quality, direct comparative, multicenter studies are needed to better define their margin of benefit.

Key findings

• This review of 23 studies encompassing 695 patients found that full endoscopic surgery was thoracic disc herniations was associated with shorter operative time (82.3 vs. 201.4 minutes), lower blood loss (18.8 vs. 355.1 mL), reduced fusion rates (1.4% vs. 94.1%), shorter length of stay (2.65 vs. 10.5 days), and lower cost ($7,625.7 vs. $21,055) compared to open approaches.

• Full endoscopic surgery was utilized in a meaningful proportion of patients with myelopathy (43.7%) and calcified or partially calcified disc herniations (29.8%), challenging the dogma that endoscopic approaches are limited to soft, foraminal thoracic disc herniations.

What is known and what is new?

• Thoracic disc herniations are rare and challenging to treat surgically; traditional open approaches are associated with significant morbidity, prolonged recovery, and high rates of instrumented fusion.

• While full endoscopic approaches have established efficacy for cervical and lumbar disc herniations, their comparative safety, efficacy, and margin of benefit over open surgery for thoracic disc herniations remains underexplored. This review systematically evaluates the available literature to elucidate perioperative outcomes of full endoscopic approaches to thoracic disc herniations.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Full endoscopic surgery appears to be a safe and effective alternative to open surgery for carefully selected patients with thoracic disc herniations, with meaningful perioperative advantages and non-inferior clinical outcomes. However, the available literature is limited. High-quality, prospective, multicenter comparative studies are needed to better define the margin of benefit, establish indications, and guide routine clinical practice.

Introduction

Thoracic disc herniations are relatively rare, estimated to comprise between 0.25% and 1% of all disc herniations (1). As opposed to cervical and lumbar disc herniations, which are fairly common and have well-established treatment pathways (2,3), the surgical management of thoracic disc herniations is more challenging (4). This is partially due to the unique anatomy of the thoracic spine, which articulates with the ribs, borders the pleural cavity, and has narrow canal dimensions along with tenuous vascular supply. Moreover, thoracic disc herniations may lead to a spectrum of neurological symptoms, ranging from axial pain and radiculopathy to severe myelopathy and paraplegia.

Traditional surgical approaches to thoracic disc herniations are associated with high morbidity (5). These include transpedicular, costotransverse, lateral extracavitary, and anterior transthoracic/thoracoscopic approaches that are associated with prolonged recovery, increased cardiopulmonary complications, and destabilization requiring instrumented fusion (4,6-9). Recent evidence suggests that full endoscopic posterior or posterolateral approaches may serve as a safer alternative to conventional open approaches for select thoracic disc herniations (5), including some with calcification (10,11), but there is currently no consensus in the literature.

In this study, we conducted a review of the literature on endoscopic approaches to thoracic disc herniations in order to better elucidate their safety, efficacy, and potential benefit over conventional approaches. We first present a case of a partially calcified, superiorly migrated thoracic disc herniation that was treated endoscopically. We then perform a critical appraisal of the existing literature on endoscopic approaches, with a focus on indications, complications, and perioperative outcomes. Finally, we discuss the margin of benefit of endoscopic approaches over conventional techniques and explore areas that require further investigation. We present this article in accordance with the PRISMA reporting checklist (available at https://jss.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jss-2026-0065/rc).

Methods

We performed a systematic review of the literature on endoscopic approaches to the ventral thoracic spine from inception until February 2026. In order to capture a broad set of articles for initial screening, the PubMed, Web of Science, and Embase databases were queried for the following search terms in the title or abstract: “endoscopic” OR “endoscopy” AND “thoracic” OR “thoracic spine” AND “disc” OR “disc herniation” OR “discectomy” OR “spine surgery” OR “spinal surgery”. Articles were eligible for inclusion if they described posterior or posterolateral endoscopic approaches to thoracic disc herniations for ventral decompression in at least 5 adult patients (age at least 18 years) and reported at least one postoperative outcome. Articles were excluded if they exclusively described only open or mini-open approaches, thoracoscopic approaches, or anterior thoracic approaches. Cadaveric studies, biomechanical studies, case reports, review articles, editorials, expert opinions, and non-English language publications were also excluded. Two reviewers (A.P. and B.P.) performed initial title and abstract screening, and two reviewers (A.P. and H.A.K.) independently screened full texts for inclusion. There were no disputes regarding inclusion of articles. Level of evidence for all included studies was evaluated in accordance with the North American Spine Society (NASS) primary research question framework (12). Variables describing patient demographics, clinical characteristics, surgical details, and postoperative outcomes were extracted and recorded, and a narrative synthesis of results was performed and summarized.

Results

Case presentation

A 35-year-old woman presented with progressive, chronic mid-thoracic back pain with radiation to her anterior right abdomen. She described the pain as sharp and stabbing in quality with exacerbation when recumbent, requiring her to sleep in an inclined position. She denied any associated extremity weakness, numbness, gait imbalance, or bowel and bladder dysfunction. Her neurological exam was notable for bilateral patellar hyperreflexia. Magnetic resonance imaging and computed tomography of her thoracic spine demonstrated a large, right paracentral, partially calcified and cranially migrated disc herniation at T9–10 with compression of the spinal cord and exiting right T9 nerve root consistent with a Type 1 thoracic disc herniation (Figure 1) (13). She underwent electromyography, which confirmed a right-sided radiculopathy in the T9–10 distribution.

Preoperative T2 magnetic resonance imaging. The sagittal series in (A) demonstrates a partially calcified disc herniation at T9–10, and axial plane imaging in (B) demonstrates an eccentric right-sided herniation with compression of the spinal cord and exiting T9 nerve root.

After discussion of her management options, the decision was made to proceed with an awake, right T9–10 endoscopic transforaminal approach for discectomy. The procedure was performed under monitored anesthesia care (MAC) in the prone position using a C-arm for intraoperative fluoroscopy. The right T9–10 facet joint was localized using fluoroscopy. An 8 mm horizontal incision was planned approximately 8 cm from midline on the right side and local anesthesia was administered. A spinal needle was advanced onto the right lateral superior articular process of T10 under fluoroscopic guidance. A K-wire was inserted into the spinal needle and satisfactory positioning was confirmed (Figure 2A). The spinal needle was removed and sequential dilators were placed over the K-wire (Figure 2B). A tubular retractor was docked on the facet, the dilators were removed, and the endoscope was introduced (Figure 2C). A modest lateral facetectomy was performed by drilling the non-articular surface of the right T10 superior articular process in order to unroof the T9–10 lateral disc space. This was carried out cranially to the axilla of the exiting right T9 nerve root in order to expose the lateral canal and the cranially migrated disc fragments. The disc material was removed in piecemeal fashion using micro-graspers until adequate decompression of the thecal sac was observed (Figure 3). Hemostasis was achieved and the closure was performed with absorbable suture and glue. The procedure was 96 minutes in duration, blood loss was minimal, and the patient was discharged 2 hours after surgery. She had a mild residual right T9 radiculopathy that completely resolved by 3-month follow-up. Her postoperative imaging demonstrated complete decompression of the spinal cord (Figure 4). All procedures performed in this case were in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional and/or national research committee(s) and with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. Written informed consent was obtained from the patient for surgery and no identifying information was included in this article.

Summary of review

A total of 614 unique articles were identified from our initial search, of which 23 articles met criteria for inclusion in our final analysis (Figure 5). These included 21 single-center retrospective studies (14-34), 1 multicenter retrospective study (5), and 1 single-center prospective study (35) (Table 1). Seventeen articles were classified as level IV evidence while 6 were classified as level III evidence. Five articles were published by centers in the United States while 18 were published by international centers. The majority of articles (14/23, 60.9%) were published in 2020 or later, while the first article included in this review was published in 2000.

Surgical details

Our review included a total of 695 patients, of whom 586 (84.3%) underwent endoscopic approaches to thoracic discectomy, while 109 (15.7%) underwent conventional open approaches, all of which were posterior-based approaches (Table 2). Patients had a mean age of 52.7 years, the majority were male (392/695, 56.4%), and average follow-up was 20.7 months (range 4–60.2 months). In studies that reported disc quality (16/23), 29.8% of endoscopically treated discs (122/410) were calcified or partially calcified compared to 36.4% of conventionally treated discs (20/55). The majority of endoscopically treated discs were located in the lower thoracic spine (281/571, 49.2%), followed by the middle (223/571, 39.1%), and upper (67/571, 11.7%) thoracic spine; conventionally treated discs followed the same distribution [49 lower (62.8%), 22 middle (28.2%), 7 upper (9.0%)]. When reported, symptoms for patients in the endoscopic group included: myelopathy (163/373, 43.7%), radiculopathy (165/373, 44.2%), axial pain (157/373, 42.1%), atypical pain (4/373, 1.1%), sexual sphincter dysfunction (1/373, 0.3%), and no symptoms (8/373, 2.1%). When reported, symptoms for patients in the open group were: myelopathy (12/17, 70.6%) and radiculopathy (5/17, 29.4%).

Endoscopic surgery was most frequently performed awake (352/489, 72.0%), while open surgery was always performed under general endotracheal anesthesia (109/109, 100%). Mean operative time was 82.3 minutes per level (range 42–153 minutes) for endoscopic surgery and 201.4 minutes per level (range 175–342.8 minutes) for open surgery. Estimated blood loss was 18.8 mL per level (range 3.8–111 mL) for endoscopic surgery as compared to 355.1 mL per level for open surgery (range 350–357.4 mL). When reported, fusion was required in only a small fraction of patients treated endoscopically (6/432, 1.4%; 4 during index surgery, 2 during revision surgery) but in nearly all patients treated via a conventional open approach (16/17, 94.1%).

Perioperative outcomes, cost, and complications

On average, patients who underwent endoscopic surgery had a nearly 4-fold shorter postoperative length of stay (mean 2.65 days for endoscopic group vs. 10.5 days for open group). The overall rate of reported complications was 6.9% (28/407) for the endoscopic group and 5.3% (2/38) for the open group. Eight (2.0%) neurologic complications were reported in the endoscopic group, 3 of which were transient. These included 1 instance of paralysis, 1 neurogenic bladder, 3 deteriorations of myelopathy, 2 transient deteriorations of myelopathy, and 1 transient right leg weakness. The reported readmission, reoperation, and disc recurrence rates were 3.3%, 3.7%, and 2.2% for patients in the endoscopic group, compared to 9.2%, 5.5%, and 0% in the open group, respectively. Mean cost, when reported (2 studies), was $7,625.7 (range $7,520–8,128) for those treated endoscopically and $21,055 (range $8,860–59,792) for those treated via conventional open surgery.

Patient-reported outcomes

There was significant heterogeneity with respect to reported pain and functional outcomes. Pain outcomes were reported in 20 studies. Visual analog scale (VAS) scores were utilized in 16 studies and all reported significant improvement at the latest follow-up. One article (21) reported VAS scores for patients who underwent endoscopic and open surgery, and there appeared to be no significant difference in pain between groups at the latest follow-up.

Functional outcomes were reported in 21 studies. The most frequently reported outcomes were Oswestry disability index (ODI, 10 studies) and Japanese Orthopedic Association (JOA, 7 studies) scores, and all studies reported improvement in outcome. One article (15) compared ODI and SF-36 scores for patients treated endoscopically versus those treated conventionally, and found that patients treated endoscopically experienced greater improvement in outcome at 1-year follow-up compared to those treated conventionally.

Discussion

The surgical management of thoracic disc herniations traditionally involves open approaches associated with significant morbidity, prolonged recovery, and instability requiring fusion (4,9). Full endoscopic spine surgery has demonstrated success for the treatment of lumbar pathology and is increasingly being adapted to the thoracic spine (5). In this systematic review of 23 articles encompassing 695 patients, we evaluated the safety, efficacy, and margin of benefit of full endoscopic approaches to thoracic disc herniations. Our findings suggest that, in appropriately selected patients, endoscopic surgery is a safe and effective alternative to open surgical approaches and may, in some cases, offer several meaningful advantages over open approaches with comparable clinical outcomes.

Our review revealed considerable diversity in the presenting symptoms and disc characteristics for patients treated endoscopically. While the majority of patients in the endoscopic cohort were treated for thoracic radiculopathy (44.2%), a substantial portion had symptoms of myelopathy (43.7%) and harbored calcified or partially calcified disc herniations (29.8%). Some studies additionally described endoscopic approaches to centrally located and/or giant disc herniations (5,14,16,19,25,26,29). This challenges the dogma that endoscopic approaches to the thoracic spine are reserved for the treatment of radiculopathy from soft, paracentral or foraminal disc herniations, and suggests that select patients with myelopathy and complex disc morphology may be appropriate candidates for endoscopic surgery. However, details such as disc location, size, migration, extent of calcification, or use of a consistent disc classification system were not always available in the included studies; these factors have been shown to have a significant influence on disc accessibility and outcome, and must be considered when selecting an optimal surgical approach (13).

We found that endoscopic approaches may be associated with several advantages over open approaches in the perioperative setting. Compared to patients treated via open approaches, those treated endoscopically had substantially shorter operative time (201.4 vs. 82.3 minutes per level), lower estimated blood loss (355.1 vs. 18.8 mL per level), and lower rates of fusion (94.1% vs. 1.4%), although the comparison group in our study was relatively small (109 patients). These findings also compare favorably to findings from a recent meta-analysis of open approaches to thoracic discectomy (estimated blood loss 570 mL, fusion rate 37%) (5). These findings likely reflect the small incision size, lack of paraspinal muscle dissection, maintenance of the posterior tension band, and limited bony resection inherent to endoscopic approaches. Moreover, while all patients treated via open surgery required general anesthesia, the vast majority of those treated endoscopically were awake under MAC. Awake spine surgery has been shown to confer several advantages over general anesthesia, including real-time intraoperative neurologic monitoring, shorter operative time, lower postoperative pain, fewer positioning-related complications, and lower anesthesia-related morbidity (36-38).

These advantages seem to translate to improved perioperative recovery and reduced cost. Our review found that endoscopic surgery was associated with a nearly 4-fold reduction in length of stay compared to open surgery (2.65 vs. 10.5 days), with multiple contemporary series describing same-day discharge (5,16,28). While the rates of complications reported between endoscopic and open cohorts were comparable (6.9% vs. 5.3%), there was considerable heterogeneity with respect to the nature of reported complications and they were only available for 38 patients who underwent open surgery. A meta-analysis of traditional open approaches to thoracic discectomy found a pooled perioperative complication rate of 25% (5), which is nearly four times the pooled complication rate for endoscopic approaches in this review. While only two studies included in our review examined cost, both found that endoscopic surgery was associated with significant cost savings (15,16). In their single-center comparative study of patients undergoing endoscopic versus open surgery for thoracic disc herniations in the United States, Liles et al. (16) found that the endoscopic cohort had a median initial cost (including surgical care and hospital stay) of $8,128 versus $59,792 for the open cohort. Multivariable analysis revealed that higher costs for open surgery were primarily driven by the cost of surgery itself and increased length of stay. In another comparative study from South Korea, Bae et al. (15), found that patients treated endoscopically had lower total cost ($7,520 vs. $8,860) and cost per quality-adjusted life year ($31,333 vs. $44,300) compared to those who underwent open microdiscectomy. Neither of these studies examined factors like return to work which may further contribute to indirect costs. Taken in conjunction with our finding that endoscopic surgery seems to be associated with non-inferior pain and functional outcomes, these data suggest that it may serve as a cost-effective alternative to open surgery for select patients.

Limitations and future directions

This review must be interpreted within the context of its limitations. First, the majority of included studies had retrospective single-center designs, small sample sizes, and were of low evidence with significant heterogeneity in patient selection and reported outcomes. For example, while some studies focused exclusively on endoscopic approaches to calcified disc herniations, others excluded these patients altogether. Second, most series were published by a small number of high-volume surgeons with expertise in endoscopic spine surgery, introducing an element of publication bias and potential for cohort overlap, limiting the generalizability of these results. Third, only a few studies contained comparative data between endoscopic and conventional open approaches, and the number of patients who underwent open surgery was small. This limited our ability to perform a meta-analysis and directly compare outcomes between cohorts.

Future studies should focus on more robust stratification of outcome by disc characteristics and presenting symptoms in order to better delineate criteria for patient selection. Longer-term follow-up is also essential to assess the durability of decompression, disc recurrence, and potential for delayed instability. In parallel, rigorous cost-effectiveness analysis incorporating both direct and indirect cost as well as assessments of the learning curve and complication profiles across centers are critical to determining the generalizability and value of endoscopic approaches in routine practice.

Conclusions

Overall, this review demonstrates that full endoscopic approaches appear to be a safe and effective alternative to conventional open approaches for thoracic disc herniations in carefully selected patients. Endoscopic approaches may offer several advantages over open approaches, including shorter operative time, lower blood loss, reduced length of stay, lower cost, reduced need for fusion, and faster recovery, while maintaining comparable postoperative outcomes. In avoiding the morbidity intrinsic to extensive open techniques, endoscopic surgery may represent a paradigm shift in the treatment of thoracic disc herniations, but higher-quality, direct comparative, multicenter studies are needed.
