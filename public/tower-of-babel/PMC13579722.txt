Effect of modified glaze application protocols on color stability of 3D-printed permanent dental resins
Authors: Ahmet Narin, Bahadır Ezmek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThis study aimed to evaluate the effect of four different surface‑treatment protocols—including a novel Optiglaze before post‑curing approach on the surface morphology and color stability of two 3D‑printed permanent resin materials following thermocycling and staining procedures.MethodsTwo permanent crown materials were fabricated and then divided into four groups, each subjected to one of the following surface‑treatment protocols: mechanical polishing (MP), Optiglaze applied after post‑curing (OG‑Post), Optiglaze applied before post‑curing (OG‑Pre), or a combined pre‑/post‑curing Optiglaze protocol (OG‑Pre/Post). A total of 248 specimens (n = 10 per material × surface treatment × staining solution) were prepared. Color measurements were performed at baseline, after 10,000 thermocycles, and following 30‑day immersion in artificial saliva, coffee, or red wine using a spectrophotometer (VITA EasyShade V). Color changes were assessed using the CIEDE2000 (ΔE₀₀) formula in relation to perceptibility (PT₀₀ = 0.81) and acceptability (AT₀₀ = 1.81) thresholds. Data were analyzed using mixed‑design ANOVA and adjusted post‑hoc tests with statistical significance set at p < 0.05. Surface morphology and glaze layer characteristics were examined using field‑emission scanning electron microscopy (FE‑SEM) on representative specimens, including both surface and cross‑sectional analyses (n = 1).ResultsThermocycling significantly increased ΔE₀₀ values in all groups (p < 0.05), with the greatest change observed in the VarseoSmile MP specimens, which exceeded AT₀₀. Staining solutions significantly affected color change (p < 0.001), and coffee and red wine caused ΔE₀₀ values above AT₀₀ across all groups. OG‑Pre demonstrated significantly lower ΔE₀₀ values than OG‑Post (p < 0.05), particularly in Crowntec specimens. MP groups exhibited significantly higher ΔE₀₀ values than all Optiglaze‑treated groups (p < 0.05). FE‑SEM analysis revealed smoother, more homogeneous, and better‑integrated glaze layers in OG‑Pre specimens, with fewer surface defects.ConclusionApplying Optiglaze before post‑curing represents an effective surface treatment strategy that enhances glaze integration, improves surface morphology, and provides superior resistance to discoloration compared with conventional post‑cure glazing.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12903-026-08972-6.Keywords: 3D-printed permanent dental resin materials, Color stability, Glazing protocols, Optiglaze, Surface morphology

Introduction

Three-dimensional (3D) printing technologies have emerged as a transformative innovation in dentistry, offering significant advantages over conventional subtractive manufacturing techniques. Unlike milling processes, which require industrially fabricated blocks and result in considerable material waste, additive manufacturing enables the precise fabrication of complex geometries with minimal waste and improved cost-efficiency [1, 2]. Initially adopted in engineering and industrial applications, 3D printing has rapidly expanded into healthcare, where its ability to produce customized components has revolutionized treatment workflows. Dentistry, in particular, has embraced this technology to enhance restorative procedures, streamline clinical processes, and improve patient outcomes [3].

The integration of computer-aided design and manufacturing (CAD/CAM) systems with 3D printing has facilitated the production of permanent restorations using polymer-based resins. These materials are increasingly favored for their ease of processing, chairside applicability, and potential to deliver esthetic and functional restorations in a single visit. However, despite these advantages, several challenges persist regarding the mechanical durability, surface integrity, and long-term esthetic performance of 3D-printed restorations [4, 5]. Among these, color stability is considered one of the most critical factors influencing clinical success, as discoloration can compromise both patient satisfaction and restoration longevity. Improving color stability is essential for long-term esthetic success and patient satisfaction in permanent restorations fabricated by 3D printing.

Color changes in polymer-based materials are multifactorial, arising from water absorption, surface roughness, and chemical degradation of the resin matrix over time [6, 7]. These alterations are exacerbated by exposure to staining agents commonly encountered in the oral environment, such as coffee, tea, and wine [8, 9]. In addition, previous studies have also demonstrated that insufficient polymerization of resin matrices leads to residual monomers, which accelerate hydrolytic and erosive degradation, increase surface roughness, and promote pigment adsorption—ultimately resulting in greater color change [10, 11]. Similarly, long-term water exposure and staining beverages have been shown to exacerbate matrix breakdown and discoloration in 3D-printed resins, confirming a strong correlation between chemical degradation and color change increase [12].

Optical glaze materials are used to improve surface smoothness, reduce surface roughness, and enhance the esthetic properties of resin-based materials. These coatings act as a protective barrier that reduces water sorption and pigment penetration [13]. Optiglaze (GC Corporation, Tokyo, Japan), a laboratory‑applied light‑curing glaze, has been widely used for this purpose. However, previous studies have reported limitations such as non‑uniform distribution, weak adhesion to resin substrates, and progressive detachment under functional stress [7, 14]. These shortcomings highlight the need for alternative approaches to improve the performance and durability of glaze coatings on 3D-printed restorations.

The scientific rationale for developing a modified protocol in this study is grounded in evidence suggesting that aluminum oxide air-abrasion can expose an incompletely polymerized subsurface layer in resin materials, thereby improving chemical bonding with resin cements [15, 16]. This observation indicates that polymerization dynamics and surface chemistry play a pivotal role in adhesion and coating performance. Considering evidence that surface polymerization dynamics can influence the adhesion and uniformity of resin‑based coatings, we hypothesized that altering the timing of optical glaze application relative to the post‑curing process might improve the integration of the glaze with the resin substrate. Applying the glaze before final polymerization may allow co‑curing of the resin and the coating, potentially resulting in a more homogeneous surface layer with fewer defects. Such improvements in surface morphology could enhance resistance to discoloration and thereby contribute to better color stability. Based on this rationale, the study focused on evaluating the effects of different glaze‑application sequences on both surface morphology and color stability of 3D‑printed permanent resin materials. Therefore, the aim of this study was to compare the effects of four surface‑treatment protocols on the color stability of two 3D‑printed permanent resin materials after thermocycling and staining. The null hypothesis was that neither the surface‑treatment protocol nor the aging and staining procedures would have a significant effect on the color stability or surface morphology of the 3D‑printed permanent resin materials.

A total of 248 disc-shaped specimens (10 mm diameter, 1.5 mm thickness) were fabricated using two different 3D printable permanent resins (Crowntec (Saremco Dental AG, Switzerland) and VarseoSmile Crown plus (BEGO Bremen, Germany)) using a digital light processing DLP printer (Asiga MAX UV, Asiga, Austria). After printing, specimens were removed from the build platform, supports were carefully trimmed, and cleaned in 96% ethanol (Vin.s. İndustries Ood, Tzerkovski, Bulgaria) using an ultrasonic bath (Sonorex Super RK 102 P, Bandelin Electronic, Berlin, Germany) for 3 min to eliminate residual resin. Specimens were then air-dried. Specimens were then divided into two equal subsets (n = 124). One subset underwent post-curing according to the manufacturer’s instructions using a laboratory light-curing unit (Labolight DUO: LED, GC Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). The process consisted of two cycles of 180 s each. During each cycle, blue LED light with an intensity of 2330 mW/cm² and a wavelength range of 465–485 nm was applied to each surface of the specimen for 180 s. Because specimens was cured in a closed‑chamber laboratory device rather than a chairside LED unit, glycerine gel—typically used to prevent oxygen‑inhibited layers during open‑air curing—was not required.

Specimens were randomly allocated into four surface treatment groups:

Mechanical Polishing (MP): Applied only to post-cured specimens. Sequential polishing was performed using abrasive discs (Rogin Dental E-Lite, Shenzhen Rogin Medical Co, Shenzhen, China; blue: coarse, 100–125 μm; pink: medium, 40–60 μm; gray: fine, 20–30 μm) with a rotary instrument operating at 5000 rpm, and each disc was applied for 20 s using minimal pressure and a rotational motion.

Mechanical Polishing (MP): Applied only to post-cured specimens. Sequential polishing was performed using abrasive discs (Rogin Dental E-Lite, Shenzhen Rogin Medical Co, Shenzhen, China; blue: coarse, 100–125 μm; pink: medium, 40–60 μm; gray: fine, 20–30 μm) with a rotary instrument operating at 5000 rpm, and each disc was applied for 20 s using minimal pressure and a rotational motion.

Optiglaze Application After Post-Curing (OG-Post): For post-cured specimens, a thin layer of Optiglaze Clear was applied using a microbrush in a single, uniform coat. The coating was light-cured for 90 s using a laboratory curing unit (Labolight DUO: LED, GC Corporation, Tokyo, Japan according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Optiglaze Application After Post-Curing (OG-Post): For post-cured specimens, a thin layer of Optiglaze Clear was applied using a microbrush in a single, uniform coat. The coating was light-cured for 90 s using a laboratory curing unit (Labolight DUO: LED, GC Corporation, Tokyo, Japan according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Optiglaze Application Before Post-Curing (OG-Pre): For non-post-cured specimens, Optiglaze was applied immediately after ethanol cleaning and drying using the same technique as OG-Post. The coated specimens were then subjected to the standard post-curing cycle, ensuring simultaneous polymerization of the resin and glaze layer.

Optiglaze Application Before Post-Curing (OG-Pre): For non-post-cured specimens, Optiglaze was applied immediately after ethanol cleaning and drying using the same technique as OG-Post. The coated specimens were then subjected to the standard post-curing cycle, ensuring simultaneous polymerization of the resin and glaze layer.

Optiglaze Before Post-Curing + Surface abrasion + Reapplication (OG-Pre/Post): Initially, Optiglaze was applied before post-curing as described above. After post-curing, the surface was lightly abraded using a fine-grit diamond bur (46 μm) to simulate clinical adjustments. A second layer of Optiglaze was then reapplied and light-cured for 90 s.

Optiglaze Before Post-Curing + Surface abrasion + Reapplication (OG-Pre/Post): Initially, Optiglaze was applied before post-curing as described above. After post-curing, the surface was lightly abraded using a fine-grit diamond bur (46 μm) to simulate clinical adjustments. A second layer of Optiglaze was then reapplied and light-cured for 90 s.

After surface treatment procedures. All specimens were subjected to 10,000 thermal cycle (Thermocycler SD Mechatronik, Feldkirchen-Westerham, Germany) between 5 °C and 55 °C, with a dwell time of 30 s in each bath.

After the aging process, one specimen from each group was randomly selected for the evaluation of the optical glaze layer thickness and the surface distribution of glaze nanofillers using field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FE‑SEM; Hitachi SU5000, Tokyo, Japan) analysis. Each specimen was sectioned at the center using a steel separator. The cut surfaces were polished under water cooling with 2000-grit abrasive paper, then cleaned in an ultrasonic bath with distilled water for 15 min and air-dried. One half of each specimen was used for surface imaging, and the other half for cross-sectional imaging. Prior to imaging, the surfaces were sputter-coated with gold (Leica ACE 200, Leica Microsystems GmbH, Wetzlar, Germany). FE-SEM was performed at 10.0 kV for surface views and 5.0 kV for cross-sectional views.

Specimens were randomly divided into three groups (n = 10 per group) and immersed in the following staining solutions: artificial saliva (control), coffee (Nescafe Gold, Nestle, İstanbul, Türkiye), and red wine (Akdeniz İncisi, Deva Şarapçılık, Manisa, Türkiye), and were stored in an incubator (NÜVE EN 055/120, Ankara, Türkiye) at 37 °C for 30 days. Artificial saliva was prepared according to the formulation described in a previous study [17] (Table 2). The coffee solution was prepared by dissolving 2 g of coffee powder in 200 mL of hot water at 80 °C. Red wine was used directly without any dilution or additional preparation. Solutions were refreshed weekly.

Color measurements were performed at three stages: [1] baseline (before aging) [2], post‑aging (immediately after thermocycling), and [3] post‑staining (after immersion in staining solutions). Prior to color measurements, all specimens were cleaned in an ultrasonic bath with distilled water for 15 min and then dried. Color measurements were performed using a spectrophotometer (VITA EasyShade V; Vita Zahnfabrik, Bad Säckingen, Germany) inside a color measurement box illuminated with a D65 light source, on a white background. The device was calibrated before each measurement. During measurement, the probe tip was positioned perpendicular to the specimen surface. Three readings were taken from the center of each specimen. The CIE L*, a*, and b* coordinates of each specimen were recorded for all measurement time points. These L* (lightness), a* (green–red axis), and b* (blue–yellow axis) values obtained directly from the device served as the basis for the color difference calculations. The color difference (ΔE) between baseline and subsequent measurements was calculated using the ΔE₀₀ formula as proposed by the CIE. In this calculation, differences in lightness (ΔL′), chroma (ΔC′), and hue (ΔH′) were computed from the measured L*, a*, and b* coordinates. The parametric weighting functions SL, SC, and SH were calculated according to the standard ΔE₀₀ equations, representing the weighting factors for lightness, chroma, and hue differences, respectively. The parametric factors were set to kL = 1, kC = 1, and kH = 1, consistent with dental materials research under controlled laboratory conditions. ΔE00 was evaluated according to the results of a previous study [19]; 50:50% color perceptibility (PT00 =0.81) and acceptability (AT00=1.81) thresholds.

A Shapiro–Wilk test was performed to assess intergroup normality, and Levene’s test was used to evaluate homogeneity of variance (α = 0.05). The main effects of measurement time, staining solutions, 3D permanent restorative material, and surface treatment, as well as their interactions on ΔE₀₀ values, were analyzed using a mixed-design ANOVA. Differences between groups were determined with post-hoc tests adjusted by the Holm–Bonferroni correction. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were conducted using R statistical software (version 4.3.1, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

In MP specimens, gaps between filler particles and the resin matrix were observed after artificial aging (Fig. 1). FE-SEM surface images of OG-Post, OG-Pre, and OG-Pre/Post specimens at ×5000 and ×40,000 magnifications are shown in Figs. 2 and 3, respectively. In OG-Post specimens, FE-SEM surface images revealed that the nanoparticles contained within the optical glaze material were not uniformly distributed across the surface, and some filler nanoparticles appeared to have detached from the surface especially VarseoSmile Crown plus specimen. In contrast, OG-Pre and OG-Pre/Post specimens exhibited a uniform distribution of nanoparticles across the surface.

Discussion

The null hypothesis proposed that surface‑treatment protocol, aging, and staining procedures would not significantly influence the color stability or surface morphology of the 3D‑printed permanent resin materials. FE‑SEM analysis demonstrated clear differences in glaze integrity and surface morphology among the treatment protocols, with OG‑Pre producing the smoothest and most homogeneous glaze layer and MP showing the greatest number of surface defects. Based on the results, both thermocycling and immersion in staining solutions significantly influenced ΔE₀₀ values. Additionally, surface treatment protocols demonstrated a significant impact on color change (p < 0.001), and the interaction between measurement time and staining solution was highly significant (p < 0.001), indicating that discoloration patterns varied according to both time and staining medium.

Color stability is a critical determinant of the clinical success of 3D-printed permanent restorations. Previous studies have shown that additive manufacturing resins often exhibit lower degrees of polymerization compared to conventional resin-based materials, which may compromise their resistance to staining [1, 20]. Lower polymerization levels increase water sorption and matrix degradation, creating pathways for pigment penetration and discoloration [11]. Furthermore, the composition and distribution of inorganic fillers strongly influence optical properties and color retention. In this study, specimens fabricated from Crowntec exhibited lower ΔE₀₀ values compared to VarseoSmile Crown plus, which may be attributed to differences in filler content and matrix integrity [6]. These findings align with previous reports indicating that filler concentration and interlayer adaptation in 3D-printed materials directly affect translucency and color stability [1, 5, 12].

After thermocycling, most surface treatments exhibited ΔE₀₀ values below both the perceptibility (PT₀₀ = 0.81) and acceptability (AT₀₀ = 1.81) thresholds, suggesting that thermal aging alone generally did not result in clinically unacceptable color changes [18]. However, VarseoSmile Crown plus specimens finished with mechanical polishing (MP) exceeded AT₀₀ across all solutions (ΔE₀₀ range: 1.967–2.142), indicating that this material is particularly susceptible to discoloration under aging conditions without surface protection. After staining, all surface treatments demonstrated ΔE₀₀ values surpassing AT₀₀ for coffee and red wine, with MP producing the highest discoloration (up to 12.374 for red wine). VarseoSmile Crown plus was especially vulnerable, as even artificial saliva exceeded AT₀₀ in the MP group (ΔE₀₀ = 2.035), while Optiglaze-treated protocols improved performance but did not prevent clinically unacceptable color changes under staining conditions. These findings emphasize the critical role of surface finishing and glaze application in mitigating color instability, particularly for highly pigmented beverages such as coffee and red wine.

The results also demonstrated that staining solutions exerted varying degrees of influence on color change. Specimens immersed in red wine exhibited the highest ΔE₀₀ values, followed by coffee, whereas artificial saliva produced the lowest values. This observation is consistent with earlier studies reporting that acidic environments and alcohol content in beverages accelerate resin matrix softening and filler loss, thereby increasing susceptibility to staining [8, 21–25]. Red wine’s low pH and ethanol content likely contributed to matrix degradation, facilitating pigment penetration into deeper layers of the resin [23]. Coffee-induced discoloration is primarily associated with low-polarity yellow pigments that adhere to the resin surface and penetrate the polymer matrix, causing both extrinsic and intrinsic staining [26]. These mechanisms have been widely documented in previous investigations [12, 22, 27–29].

Optiglaze application demonstrated a clear benefit in reducing color change compared to mechanical polishing, acting as a surface barrier that limits water sorption and pigment penetration [13]. This aligns with previous studies showing that surface sealants improve color stability, particularly against coffee staining [30–32]. However, limitations such as low wear resistance, weak adhesion to resin substrates, and non-uniform distribution have been reported [7, 14], which can compromise long-term performance. These challenges highlight the need for protocol modifications to enhance glaze retention and durability.

The rationale for applying Optiglaze before post-curing is rooted in polymerization chemistry and interfacial bonding principles. Resin-based materials polymerize through free-radical addition reactions of methacrylate groups, forming a cross-linked network. When the glaze is applied after complete curing, bonding relies primarily on mechanical retention, which is inherently weak and prone to failure under thermal and mechanical stress. Conversely, applying the glaze before final polymerization allows unreacted C = C double bonds in the resin to participate in co-polymerization with the glaze during post-curing. This simultaneous curing promotes the formation of covalent bonds and an interpenetrating polymer network at the interface, significantly improving adhesion and reducing the risk of delamination [15, 16, 33]. Literature on resin bonding confirms that the availability of unreacted methacrylate groups enhances chemical coupling and increases the degree of conversion at the interface [15, 16, 33]. By leveraging this mechanism, the modified protocol aimed to overcome adhesion failures observed in conventional approaches. FE-SEM analysis supported this hypothesis, showing more uniform glaze distribution and fewer separation zones in specimens treated before post-curing compared to those glazed afterward. Because the primary aim of this study was to directly evaluate the effect of the glaze‑application sequence, no sandblasting or primer application was performed on the 3D‑printed permanent resin surfaces.

Cross-sectional FE-SEM analysis revealed that specimens subjected to abrasion followed by reapplication of Optiglaze (OG-Pre/Post) exhibited the greatest coating thickness (approximately 130–135 μm), whereas OG-Pre groups showed thinner layers (24–55 μm) compared to OG-Post groups (74–85 μm). This difference can be attributed to the timing of glaze application relative to polymerization: when Optiglaze is applied before post-curing, simultaneous polymerization of the resin and glaze promotes chemical integration but limits the amount of material retained on the surface due to initial viscosity and penetration into the partially polymerized matrix. In contrast, post-curing prior to glaze application results in a fully polymerized substrate, allowing the glaze to remain as a thicker superficial layer [15, 16]. Increased glaze thickness may enhance barrier properties against water sorption and pigment penetration, potentially improving color stability and surface smoothness; however, excessive thickness could compromise wear resistance and lead to marginal chipping under functional stress. Previous studies on surface coatings have shown that glaze application improves stain resistance and surface quality, but mechanical durability under abrasion remains a concern [30–32]. To the best of our knowledge, no previous study has quantitatively evaluated the thickness of Optiglaze coatings and its potential influence on material properties such as color stability, wear resistance, or adhesion in 3D-printed permanent resins. Future research should address this gap by correlating glaze thickness with these performance parameters.

Thermal aging prior to staining also played a critical role in color change. The 10,000-cycle thermocycling protocol likely induced microstructural changes, such as matrix expansion and filler particle loss, which facilitated pigment penetration during subsequent staining [6]. FE-SEM analysis confirmed the presence of filler particle detachment and surface irregularities in mechanically polished specimens after aging, supporting the hypothesis that surface degradation contributes to increased discoloration.

Overall, the findings of this study indicate that color stability of 3D-printed permanent resins is influenced by multiple factors, including material composition, surface finishing protocol, aging, and staining environment. Among these, the combination of mechanical polishing and exposure to red wine produced the most pronounced color changes, whereas Optiglaze application—particularly when performed before post-curing—significantly improved resistance to staining. These results emphasize the importance of optimizing surface finishing strategies and highlight the potential of modified glaze application protocols to enhance the long-term esthetic performance of 3D-printed restorations.

The findings of this study demonstrate that the timing of glaze application plays a clinically significant role in the long‑term esthetic performance of 3D‑printed permanent restorations. The newly proposed pre‑cure Optiglaze protocol (OG‑Pre) produced smoother and more homogeneous surfaces and resulted in markedly lower ΔE₀₀ values compared with conventional post‑cure glazing. This suggests that applying Optiglaze before final polymerization may enhance glaze integration into the resin matrix, reduce surface defects, and improve resistance against staining challenges commonly encountered in daily diet, such as coffee and red wine. Given the limitations of mechanical polishing and the incomplete protection offered by post‑cure glazing alone, clinicians may benefit from adopting OG‑Pre or OG‑Pre/Post protocols to improve the long‑term color stability and esthetic predictability of 3D‑printed permanent restorations.

This study was conducted under in vitro conditions, which cannot fully replicate the complex oral environment. Factors such as salivary flow, pH fluctuations, mechanical wear from mastication, and oral hygiene practices may influence color stability in clinical settings. Additionally, the flat specimen geometry used in this study does not reflect the anatomical complexity of actual restorations, which may affect glaze retention and staining behavior. In addition, the study employed a single laboratory curing unit, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other curing systems. Nevertheless, the use of a closed‑chamber device (Labolight DUO) was intentional, as it reduces oxygen‑inhibition phenomena and therefore eliminates the need for glycerine gel typically required in open‑air chairside curing. Standardizing the curing environment allowed for consistent glaze polymerization across all surface‑treatment protocols. This study did not assess degree of conversion, water sorption/solubility, or surface roughness, which could provide mechanistic insight into color change.

Further research should explore alternative surface coating materials with improved wear resistance and chemical bonding properties. Investigating the effect of different post-curing protocols, including variations in light intensity and wavelength, on glaze adhesion and color stability could provide valuable insights. Additionally, studies assessing the performance of modified glaze application techniques on complex restoration geometries and under dynamic oral conditions would enhance clinical relevance. Exploring nanofiller-reinforced glazes or multifunctional coatings that combine esthetic and antibacterial properties may represent promising directions for improving the longevity and functionality of 3D-printed permanent restorations.

Conclusion

Within the limitations of this study, applying Optiglaze before post‑curing (OG‑Pre) demonstrated superior color stability compared with conventional post‑cure glazing, producing smoother and more homogeneous surfaces with significantly lower ΔE₀₀ values after staining. Mechanical polishing resulted in the highest discoloration, particularly in VarseoSmile Crown Plus, indicating that polishing alone is insufficient for maintaining esthetic durability. Although all glaze‑treated groups reduced staining relative to MP, OG‑Pre consistently performed best, especially under coffee and red wine exposure. FE‑SEM findings supported this improvement by revealing better glaze integration and fewer surface defects in OG‑Pre specimens. Overall, the results suggest that modifying the timing of glaze application represents a clinically advantageous strategy for enhancing the long‑term esthetic performance of 3D‑printed permanent restorations.
