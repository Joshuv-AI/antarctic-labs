DNA metabarcoding for food authentication: identification of freshwater, marine, and terrestrial gastropods in commercial food products
Authors: Julia Andronache, Margit Cichna-Markl, Stefanie Dobrovolny, Rupert Hochegger
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Food fraud through species substitution has been reported for edible gastropods, particularly in processed products where morphological identification is not feasible. DNA-based methods have been used to identify gastropod species in food products, but are largely limited to targeted analyses. To address this limitation, we applied, for the first time, an untargeted DNA metabarcoding approach to detect and identify gastropod species in processed foods. Mitochondrial 16S rDNA fragments of approximately 150 bp were analyzed. In 17 of 18 reference samples, taxa were resolved to genus or species level, in agreement with Codex Alimentarius Austriacus guidelines, facilitated by a curated reference database that enables assignments across freshwater, marine, and terrestrial gastropods. Thirteen DNA extract mixtures were analyzed for method validation, yielding few false-negative results for minor components. These likely resulted from primer–template mismatches causing amplification bias. Analysis of 20 commercial food products revealed seven mislabeled samples. The approach is compatible with established in-house metabarcoding assays targeting other animal species and consequently complements broad application across various taxa. This qualitative screening tool is suitable for routine food authentication, supporting supply chain transparency and regulatory monitoring.

Introduction

Gastropods populate freshwater, marine, and terrestrial habitats1 and have been consumed by humans since prehistoric times2. Often consumed species include freshwater snails of the genus Semisulcospira, marine gastropods such as abalone (Haliotis spp.), and turban snails (family Turbinidae), as well as terrestrial gastropods like Cornu aspersum (synonym Helix aspersa), Helix pomatia, Helix lucorum, and giant African land snails (Achatina spp., Archachatina spp.)3–9. The variety of consumed snail species is matched by their culinary prevalence: gastropods are eaten in various countries on the African, Asian, and European continent2,5–12.

Snail meat is rich in protein, in some species comparable to beef, chicken, or fish13 and its production also generates low greenhouse gas emissions14. Consequently, gastropods represent a promising alternative protein source for both human consumption and animal feed compared to traditional livestock15,16.

Beyond their role as a food source, gastropods are also used in the cosmetics industry, where secretions of Achatina fulica and Cornu aspersum are valued for skin-regenerative, moisturizing, as well as anti-aging properties1. However, whether for culinary or cosmetic purposes, the economic use of gastropods is not without risk for human health.

Gastropods are known vectors for diverse bacteria, viruses, and parasites such as Salmonella spp., and Schistosoma spp.10,17–19. Cultured snails usually contain lower amounts and fewer pathogen varieties than wild snails, which underlines the importance of controlled farming in preventing zoonoses5. Farming may also prevent the use of protected species for commercial exploitation. Aside from microbial risks, snail allergens can cause severe reactions like anaphylactic or asthmatic responses, partly linked to cross-reactivity with house-dust-mite allergenicity20,21. These microbial, exploitative and allergenic risks emphasize the need for accurate species identification and transparent labelling, as required by EU Regulation 1169/201122.

Transparent species labeling is particularly relevant for processed gastropod products. Processing enhances the difficulty of morphological identification, thereby facilitating mislabeling12. This has been reported for apple snails (Pomacea spp.) sold as native field snails (Cipangopaludina spp.)23, for substitution among Haliotis species, and for the use of “false abalone” (Concholepas concholepas)24, revealing supply chain vulnerabilities. DNA-based methods are widely used for species identification because DNA is present in all cell types and has a greater degradation stability than proteins25. However, only a limited number of DNA-based methods were used to identify gastropods in food products. Restriction fragment length polymorphism was applied for the detection and differentiation of Helix species12 or Helix and Achatina species4, while real-time polymerase chain reaction (PCR) was applied to detect traces from the Helix, Bolinus, and Buccinum genera as food allergens26. Furthermore, the concept of DNA barcoding, established by Hebert et al.27, provides a standardized approach for species identification based on mitochondrial markers. In the context of gastropod identification in food, DNA barcoding was applied to the authentication of processed products containing abalone species8 and field snails on Chinese markets23. A limitation of these approaches is the relatively narrow taxonomic coverage, often focusing on limited numbers of species or genera. Regarding DNA barcoding, barcodes had lengths of 390–660 bp, which might be problematic for detection in harshly processed foods.

Gastropods have been studied for their properties as important bioindicators9,28 and have been targeted in several environmental DNA (eDNA) metabarcoding studies29–31 for this reason. DNA metabarcoding combines DNA barcoding with next-generation sequencing technology (NGS) and enables broad taxonomic screening through the use of universal primers, simultaneous detection of species in mixtures, and sequencing of short barcodes for detection in processed samples32. However, despite the growing economic importance of gastropods, DNA metabarcoding has not yet been applied to identify gastropods in processed foods, representing a significant gap in current food authentication approaches.

This study aims to evaluate whether a broad range of gastropod species can be reliably identified in processed foods using DNA metabarcoding. To address this aim, a method targeting the mitochondrial 16S ribosomal rRNA gene with minibarcodes <300 bp was developed. To our knowledge, this is the first study applying DNA metabarcoding to commercial gastropod-containing food products. Metabarcoding was selected instead of species-specific polymerase chain reaction (PCR) or DNA barcoding to enable efficient screening in light of the large diversity of the Gastropoda class, which consists of around 63,000 named and described species33. Additionally, it allows integration with other in-house metabarcoding assays32,34,35 developed on Illumina sequencing platforms. By applying the same marker, barcode length and validated framework across taxonomic groups, we aim to enable standardization and practical feasibility in official food control laboratories. This approach is expected to contribute to food safety standards and provides a framework that can support future harmonized international monitoring strategies for food authenticity.

Results

Primer system and barcodes

The forward primer was designed based on the primer binding site described by Abbadi et al.36, which -together with the reverse primer binding site reported by Palumbi et al.37 - showed good conservation across freshwater, marine, and terrestrial gastropods. Taberlet et al.38 also mentioned primer sequences targeting this combination of binding sites, and Ventura et al.39 successfully applied these primers in their study. However, after our first sequencing run of reference samples, the resulting barcodes did not allow reliable species- or genus-level identification across all reference samples, a limitation likewise reported by Ventura et al.39. Subsequently, a new primer set was designed, combining the reverse primer from the original gastropod primer system (S1) with three newly developed forward primers (S2). The DNA metabarcoding method enabled the identification of most gastropods at the species or genus level. Differentiation of Haliotis spp. to the species level was not possible, as the identified barcode is identical for both H. iris and H. tuberculata (sample 2). Pomacea maculata and Pomacea canaliculata could not be resolved at the species level due to the same reason (sample 8). Buccinoidea in sample 7 was the only taxa assigned to a higher taxonomic level than genus (superfamily). About half of the identified species in the reference samples - excluding samples 4, 7–10, 12, 15, 17, 18 - are listed in the CAA as commonly consumed gastropods. The sample materials comprised two freshwater snails (samples 8 and 18), five sea snails (samples 1, 2, 5–7) and 11 terrestrial snails.

Assessment of primer selectivity and method validation with DNA extract mixtures

Primer selectivity was evaluated as described previously32. Basic Local Alignment Search Tool42 (BLASTn) analysis showed 85–100% sequence overlap ( ≤ 3 mismatches) between primer sequences and database hits. Amplification was observed for frog, fly, mussel, mouse, beef, pork, horse, spider, cephalopod, two fishes and two poultry species using the gastropod primers. The resulting PCR products had the fragment length that was predicted from in-silico analyses.

Economically important freshwater and marine gastropods (mixture 1, Table 3) and terrestrial gastropods (mixture 2, Table 3) were combined in equal proportions (25% w/w each). In mixture 1, read assignments varied widely, ranging from 0.4% to 55.3%, while terrestrial gastropods showed read assignments between 19.7% and 30.1%. All eight species were combined in mixture 3 (Table 3), which exhibited a similar distribution of read assignments to that observed in mixtures 1 and 2. Some species were overrepresented, while others had lower read proportions.

In mixtures 4 and 5 (Table 3), Helix pomatia and Cornu aspersum were combined with DNA from Limax maximus and Arion vulgaris. Slug DNA was present at 10% w/w each. In both mixtures, read assignments were comparable, although Limax maximus was barely detectable (0.2–0.3%) and Arion vulgaris was underrepresented (4.6–4.7%).

Species with varying Ct values were combined in mixtures 6–8 (Table 3), including Lissachatina fulica, Limax maximus, and Haliotis spp. (Ct: 26.09, 27.59, 30.00). Each species served once as the major component and twice as a minor component. With the exception of Limax maximus and Haliotis spp. in mixture 7, all species were detected.

Mixtures 9–13 (Table 3) contained chicken (Gallus gallus), pork (Sus scrofa), gilthead seabream (Sparus aurata), corn (Zea mays), or mussel (Mytilus spp.) as main ingredients. All of these main ingredients, except for corn, were detected. Additionally, chicken, pig, and gilthead seabream were overrepresented in terms of read assignment.

A cutoff was calculated from the impurities with the highest number of reads in the validation mixtures, which amounted to 0.2%. The cutoff was used to distinguish species with low read assignments from background noise. Haliotis spp. in mixture 3 and 7, Limax maximus in mixture 7, and Zea mays in mixture 12 had read numbers below the cutoff, resulting in false negative detection.

Sequencing on MiSeq® achieved a Q30 of 96.49%, a CPF rate of 96.18%, and a cluster density of 615 K/mm², while iSeq® 100 achieved 93.47%, 52.80%, and 327 K/mm², respectively. PhiX accounted for 3.86% with an error rate of 0.45% on MiSeq®, and 6.68% with an error rate of 0.69% on iSeq® 100. Most samples were sequenced on the MiSeq® platform, with single samples and mixtures also successfully processed on iSeq® 100.

Discussion

The results of this study demonstrate that the developed metabarcoding workflow provides a scalable, transferable and robust framework for authenticating gastropod species in processed foods. Most gastropod taxa listed in the CAA were identified at species or genus level. Repeatability was supported by consistent species-assigned read proportions across sequencing runs. Touchdown PCR was optimized to achieve reliable amplification at either 58 °C or 62 °C, enabling simultaneous processing of different assays in a single thermocycler run. The final annealing temperature of 62 °C permits integration with established assays for meat and poultry34 as well as a variety of bivalves43, which is beneficial for routine analysis pipelines. All other in-house metabarcoding systems operate effectively on the MiSeq® platform and single samples and mixtures were successfully sequenced on the iSeq® 100 platform, indicating that the method is transferable and robust across Illumina sequencing platforms. Except for Pomacea spp., only gastropod species listed in the CAA were detected in food products. The detection of mislabeling in 35% of commercial samples emphasizes the practical relevance of this method and highlights a largely unmonitored area of the global food market.

To evaluate the robustness and applicability of the developed workflow, the performance of the primer systems was assessed. Initial sequencing with the S1 primer system revealed that the 104 bp barcode is insufficient for genus- or species-level resolution. To overcome this limitation, a second primer system was designed. As the newly selected binding site exhibited lower sequence conservation, three new forward primers were required to ensure broad amplification. The reverse primer was retained. The new primer system closely resembles the 16S rRNA150 primers described by Zhou et al.31 (Table 1), which were also reported to be efficient. It produced efficient amplification across diverse species, yielding intense gel bands of the expected fragment length and extending coverage from freshwater to marine and terrestrial gastropods.

Despite the overall robustness of the method, several limitations must be considered, which directly influence its applicability in routine analysis. One key limitation relates to primer selectivity. The universal character of this primer system allows broad taxonomic screening beyond gastropods, enabling detection of economically motivated adulteration. Validation experiments showed amplification of non-target taxa (pork, chicken, fish and mussel), with mussel underrepresented, while pork, chicken, and fish were overrepresented and corn DNA remained undetected.

These findings indicate low primer selectivity, which may pose challenges if amplification bias reduces the detectability of gastropod DNA in mixed samples and in terms of sample contamination. As the primer system was designed to amplify DNA barcodes from a large variety of gastropods, these findings are consistent with previous studies highlighting the trade-off between broad taxonomic coverage and reduced primer specificity in metabarcoding approaches, in contrast to more targeted approaches which offer higher specificity39,43. Moreover, the method’s cutoff is higher than in previous systems32,34, demonstrating that reduced primer selectivity increases susceptibility to false positives. Overrepresentation of non-target taxa can lead to underrepresentation of target taxa, potentially distorting relative proportions and affecting the interpretation of adulteration in complex matrices.

An example of non-target taxa amplification was observed in three processed non-food samples, where Homo sapiens DNA was amplified instead of gastropods. Sequence alignment revealed a single mismatch of forward primer Fwd2_S2-OH to the Homo sapiens binding site, while the reverse primer was fully complementary (Supplementary Fig. 3), enabling unintended amplification. These results reflect either the absence of gastropod DNA or cross-contamination during sample handling. One sample contained gastropod extract, whereas the other two contained gastropod secretion filtrate, which undergoes multiple filtering steps44. This may explain DNA loss. Together with results from validation mixtures (mixture 10, Table 3), these observations demonstrate that mammal DNA can be amplified and potentially overrepresented, complicating inference of deliberate adulteration. Additionally, these findings underline the applicability of this method to processed foods, while highlighting limitations for cosmetic products.

In addition to mammal DNA, chicken DNA was also overrepresented in DNA extract mixtures (mixture 9, Table 3). In sample 20 (Table 4), chicken DNA was detected alongside Helix pomatia with a low number of assigned reads, suggesting that unintentional contamination is the most plausible explanation in this case.

Species-specific amplification bias was also observed. While gastropods were detected concurrently in mixtures, relative quantification is not feasible. Species detected in single-species samples but poorly represented in mixtures (mixtures 1 and 3–8, Table 3) included Limax maximus and Haliotis spp. Both species exhibited in-silico primer template mismatches, with two mismatches to the forward primer for each species. Limax maximus may also have mismatches in the reverse-primer region, which was absent from the reference sequence. These taxa accounted for the only false negatives in mixture 7, likely due to these primer–template mismatches. Pomacea spp. also showed a forward-primer mismatch (Fwd1_S2-OH) and was underrepresented in mixtures, although to a lesser extent (mixtures 1 and 3, Table 3).

Mixtures containing Cornu aspersum and Helix pomatia (mixtures 2 and 3, Table 3) were particularly informative, as these species may be co-farmed in heliciculture3. Both were detected semiquantitatively, demonstrating that such species substitutions are identifiable. The Fwd2_S2-OH primer, specifically designed for Helix spp., displayed no mismatches for these taxa (Fig. 3), resulting in reduced amplification bias relative to Limax maximus and Haliotis spp.Fig. 3The displayed sequence of forward primer Fwd2_S2-OH, designed for Helix spp., shows no mismatches with target binding sites, resulting in a lower amplification bias (see results for mixture 2, Table 3).

Considerable read loss was observed during data processing for certain reference samples and processed products, primarily during read joining. Disabling the low-complexity filter and allowing up to 15% error for maximum percentage length did not improve join rates. Read yields also varied between samples of the same species, indicating a sample-dependent effect rather than a species-specific issue. Additionally, short reads (approximately 40 bp) with high counts were detected, which were too short to join, likely arising from sample processing or pre-extraction handling.

Finally, Haliotis spp. could not be resolved to species level because H. iris and H. tuberculata share an identical barcode. Given that H. tuberculata is listed as Vulnerable by the International Union for Conservation of Nature45, detection of Haliotis DNA demonstrates the method’s potential to flag taxa of conservation relevance in processed products.

Overall, this metabarcoding workflow provides a framework for routine authentication of gastropods in processed foods. Its compatibility with existing in-house assays and its capacity to detect both food fraud and taxa of conservation concern demonstrate its relevance for regulatory agencies. The approach therefore represents a practical component for future harmonized monitoring strategies aiming to improve transparency and integrity in global food supply chains.

As the method is not without limitations, different optimization steps might be conducted in the future to broaden the method’s applicability and strengthen species detectability. Different genetic variants could be explored for the identification of geographic origins to strengthen traceability along food supply chains.

The analysis of different markers, such as COI, could potentially compensate the amplification bias of the 16S rRNA gene barcode and improve species-level resolution. Expansion of sample diversity and in consequence the database could strengthen the robustness of the method. Finally, optimization of DNA extraction, particularly for highly processed or low-DNA matrices such as cosmetic products, might also increase the detectability of gastropod DNA. In addition, replicate extraction followed by pooling could increase overall DNA yield.

Reliable species identification is essential for ensuring the authenticity of gastropod products, yet analytical tools applicable to processed matrices or complex mixtures have been limited. Our study demonstrates that the validated metabarcoding assay overcomes these constraints, representing a methodological advance by enabling broad and taxonomically inclusive detection of gastropods across diverse food matrices.

The barcode region selected here provided genus- to species-level resolution for the majority of taxa included, meeting the specifications of the CAA. Importantly, the method showed consistent performance across reference materials, mixtures, and commercial products, underscoring its suitability for simultaneous detection of multiple gastropod species in realistic, heterogeneous matrices.

Although the approach remains qualitative, it offers a sensitive and rapid means to uncover species substitution and potential adulteration in gastropod-containing foods. This method supports the enforcement of EU Regulation 1379/201346 and has the potential to enhance monitoring across the supply chain. However, detection bias from primer–template mismatches remains a limiting factor for some taxa. Nevertheless, given its robustness and compatibility with established laboratory pipelines, the assay can be readily integrated into existing metabarcoding-based screening frameworks for official food control.

Methods

Sample collection and storage

Reference samples were obtained from in-house experts. Twenty processed gastropod-containing food products, including one nutritional supplement, were obtained from supermarkets and online retailers. In addition to food products, a snail syrup, and two cosmetic products were tested to assess the method’s applicability across various sample matrices.

As no processed freshwater or marine gastropod products were available, processed samples were prepared from three reference materials using two processing methods: cooking in tomato sauce (10 min in a 100 °C water bath) or frying in oil until golden brown. These were analyzed alongside other processed food samples. All samples were stored either at room temperature or at –20 °C prior to DNA extraction.

The identity of gastropod species in the reference samples was verified by Sanger sequencing (Microsynth, Balgach, Switzerland), using the forward and reverse primers listed in Table 1, with resulting sequences compared against public databases from the National Center for Biotechnology Information40 (NCBI, Bethesda, MD, USA).

DNA extraction with CTAB protocol and quantification

DNA was extracted from all samples using a cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) buffer according to DIN EN ISO 21571:2013-08, 201347 and applying the previously described protocol32. Shells were removed, if present, and the remaining soft tissue was homogenized in a lab mill. No further preprocessing was performed. Sample digestion was performed by incubating 1.0 g of homogenized tissue with 7 mL CTAB buffer and 80 µL Proteinase K at 56 °C under constant shaking for 14 h. A commercial kit was used for DNA isolation (Maxwell RSC Pure-Food GMO and Authentication Kit (Promega, Madison, WI, USA)) in combination with the Maxwell® 16 instrument (Promega, Madison, WI, USA), following the manufacturer’s instructions. DNA concentrations were determined fluorometrically (Qubit® 2.0 fluorometer, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and DNA purity was analyzed photometrically from the ratio of the absorbance at 260 nm/280 nm and 260 nm/230 nm (QIAxpert spectrophotometer, software version 2.2.0.21, Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). Reagent blanks were used to rule out cross-contamination during DNA extraction. DNA extracts were stored at 4 °C.

Primer selectivity tests and preparation of DNA extract mixtures

Primer selectivity was assessed via BLASTn42 analysis against the NCBI database to identify taxa with primer binding sites complementary to our primer sequences. “Gastropoda” as well as the options “Models (XM/XP)” and “Uncultured/environmental sample sequences” were excluded from the search. qPCR followed by gel electrophoresis was performed on selected species available in our sample stock, representing classes that matched BLASTn hits.

DNA extract mixtures were prepared to validate the method’s repeatability, selectivity, and cutoff value. These mixtures included species that are commercially highly relevant. Individual DNA extracts were diluted to a concentration of 5 ng/µL. DNA extracts were combined in mixtures, with varying ratios (% w/w). Mixtures containing minor components <5% were analyzed in duplicate across two sequencing runs, whereas mixtures containing minor components ≥ 5% were analyzed as single replicates across two sequencing runs.

Thirteen mixtures were prepared, including mixtures containing DNA from non-target taxa like chicken (Gallus gallus), pig (Sus scrofa), gilthead seabream (Sparus aurata), corn (Zea mays), and mussel (Mytilus spp.). These species have been previously reported as adulterants in combination with seafood32. Helix pomatia and Cornu aspersum, both important culinary gastropod species, were mixed with DNA extracts from two slug species to assess method selectivity and the detectability of potential food adulteration.

Additionally, species with differing threshold cycle (Ct) values were combined in the mixtures, with each species serving once as the major component and twice as a minor component.

DNA barcodes, reference sequences, and primers

The 16S rRNA gene was chosen as the target gene due to its conserved universal primer binding sites and short hypervariable regions, which enable species differentiation48. Reference sequences of commercially relevant gastropod species in Austria listed in the CAA were retrieved from the NCBI database between May 2022 and May 2024 and added to the customized database (Supplementary Table 1). Complete mitochondrial DNA reference sequences were preferred; when unavailable, the longest available 16S rDNA barcodes without unidentified nucleotides were used instead. FASTA files were edited with the QIAGEN CLC Genomics Workbench (version 10.1.1, Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). Default software settings were applied. Primer binding sites reported in the literature36,37,49 were annotated and the corresponding barcode sequences were extracted and aligned (Supplementary Fig. 1). Sequence alignments were examined for interspecific variation (Supplementary Fig. 2). Primer binding sites were retained in our customized database entries but excluded from alignment comparisons, as they could bias in-silico estimates of species-level resolution.

Primer annealing temperatures were assessed using the online product information provided by TIBMolbiol (Berlin, Germany). The secondary structures were investigated using the OligoAnlayzer Tool from Integrated DNA Technologies (IDT, Coralville, IA, USA). The primer combinations with the most promising characteristics were selected for wet-lab tests. The HPLC-purified primers were obtained from TIBMolbiol (Berlin, Germany).

Primers were evaluated by real-time PCR using DNA extracts from single-species reference samples. The HotStarTaq Master Mix Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) was used for all PCR reactions at a final 1× concentration, with 12.5 ng of DNA input per reaction34. A touchdown PCR50 was used to identify the optimal annealing temperature range32, after which the final annealing temperature was established. Primer concentrations (0.2–1.2 µM) and addition of magnesium chloride (1.5 mM, 3 mM) were varied to optimize PCR conditions. For the S2 primer system, each forward primer was used at 0.4 µM and the reverse primer at 1.2 µM, with 1.5 mM magnesium chloride added to the master mix.

Library preparation, NGS and NGS data analysis with Galaxy

DNA extracts were diluted to 5 ng/µL before library preparation, and if the DNA concentration was below 5 ng/µL, extracts were used undiluted. Library preparation was performed as previously described32. Barcodes were sequenced on the MiSeq® or iSeq® 100 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). PCR products of reference samples were sequenced twice (one replicate per sequencing run), whereas commercial food products were sequenced once or twice. DNA extract mixtures were sequenced in two or four replicates (two sequencing runs with one or two replicates per run).

Real-time PCR was conducted on the LightCycler® 480 or LightCycler® 96 System (Roche, Penzberg, Germany) using EvaGreen® fluorescent dye (20× in water) in 96-well plates. The following temperature program was applied: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 15 min, followed by 35 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C, annealing at 62 °C, and extension at 72 °C for 30 s each, followed by a final extension at 72 °C for 10 min. Each reaction contained 22.5 µL of reaction mix and either 2.5 µL DNA extract or nuclease-free water (no-template control).

The Agencourt AMPure purification system (Beckman Coulter, USA) was applied according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Indexed libraries were prepared using the Illumina Nextera XT v2 kit B. The MiSeq® Reagent Nano Kit v2, Reagent Micro Kit v2, Reagent Kit v2 (each 300-cycles) or the iSeq® 100 i1 Reagent Kit v2 (300-cycles) were used for sequencing.

After paired-end sequencing, the resulting FastQ files were used as input for data analysis. The raw binary base call (bcl) files generated by Illumina devices were converted to text files using the conversion software bcl2fastq2-v2.19.0.316 (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). The resulting files were processed to remove binding adapters and indices before applying a Galaxy (version 19.01) workflow as described previously51. Data quality was assessed with FastQC. Primer sequences were removed with Cutadapt. Reads were subjected to quality-trimming and length-filtering using Trimmomatic before merging with fastq-join. Identical sequences were combined into a single representative sequence with Dereplicate. During method development, dereplicated sequences were mapped against NCBI, using BLASTn for taxonomic assignments. After creating customized databases (Supplementary Table 1), reads were matched against these databases with a percent identity cutoff set to 97%. Sequences with lower percentage identity were manually checked, and ambiguous assignments were reported at higher taxonomic levels.
