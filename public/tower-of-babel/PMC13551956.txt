Sodium selenate stimulates growth and photosynthetic pigments in sweet potato plantlets in vitro
Authors: Marcelo de Almeida Guimarães, Jorge Braga Ribeiro Junior, Filipe Almendagna Rodrigues, Lailla Sabrina Queiroz Nazareno, Moacir Pasqual, Gabrielen de Maria Gomes Dias, Helon Hébano de Freitas Sousa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveSelenium deficiency is a widespread nutritional problem worldwide, and biofortification has been proposed as a strategy to increase selenium intake through food crops. This study evaluated the effects of sodium selenate on growth, photosynthetic pigments, and selenium accumulation in sweet potato (Ipomoea batatas) plantlets micropropagated in vitro. ResultsExplants were cultured in hormone-free Murashige and Skoog medium supplemented with 0, 5, 10, 15, 20, and 25 µM sodium selenate. Plantlets grown with 5.0 µM sodium selenate showed the highest values for growth-related traits, including number of leaves, shoot length, root length, and dry biomass. Photosynthetic pigments were also enhanced at low selenium concentrations, with maximum chlorophyll and carotenoid contents observed at 5.0 µM. In contrast, higher selenium concentrations reduced plant growth and pigment accumulation, indicating phytotoxic effects. Selenium accumulation increased progressively with increasing sodium selenate concentration, reaching the highest levels at 25.0 µM. These findings indicate that 5.0 µM sodium selenate is suitable for in vitro propagation of sweet potato plantlets, whereas higher concentrations may be useful for initiating selenium biofortification strategies.Graphical abstract Keywords: Selenium accumulation, Biofortification, Ipomoea batatas (L.) Lam., Micropropagation

Introduction

Selenium (Se) plays an important role in biological functions in animals and plants, acting as a cofactor of enzymes such as thioredoxin reductase and glutathione peroxidase, which protect cells against oxidative stress in humans [1–2]. In plants, selenium is involved in antioxidant defense systems and has been reported to stimulate primary and secondary metabolism, as well as improve tolerance to abiotic stresses [3–5].

A large proportion of the world’s population has insufficient dietary selenium intake, with approximately one billion people consuming less than the recommended minimum of 55 µg day⁻¹ [6–7]. Selenium deficiency is considered one of the most widespread micronutrient deficiencies worldwide, largely due to its low concentration in staple foods and agricultural soils [8–9]. In at least 40 countries, including Brazil, selenium concentrations in cultivated soils range from only 0.01 to 0.20 mg kg⁻¹ [10–12].

Selenium deficiency in humans is associated with a range of health disorders, including Keshan disease, a cardiomyopathy, and Kashin–Beck disease, an osteoarthropathy, as well as impaired immune function and increased susceptibility to oxidative stress and thyroid dysfunction. In animals, deficiency can lead to reduced growth, reproductive disorders, white muscle disease, and weakened immune responses. In plants, although selenium is not considered an essential element for most species, its deficiency may reduce antioxidant capacity, leading to increased oxidative damage, reduced growth, and greater sensitivity to environmental stresses [13].

Biofortification strategies aim to increase the concentration of essential micronutrients in edible plant tissues, thereby improving the nutritional quality of food crops and reducing deficiencies in human populations [14]. Plants primarily absorb selenium as inorganic forms, such as selenate and selenite, although organic forms, including selenomethionine and selenocysteine, may also be present. Regardless of the chemical form supplied, selenium is metabolized into organic compounds that are bioavailable to humans [5, 15].

Biofortification programs have been developed mainly for widely consumed crops such as rice, wheat, and beans, whereas relatively few studies have focused on sweet potato (Ipomoea batatas (L.) Lam.) [16–18]. Sweet potato is a hardy crop with wide climatic adaptation, high productivity, short growth cycle, and low production costs, making it particularly suitable for cultivation in diverse environments [19–20].

Sweet potato biofortification has the potential to alleviate nutritional deficiencies, including selenium deficiency, especially in low-income populations with limited access to diversified diets [21]. In this context, in vitro culture represents a useful approach for early-stage biofortification, allowing precise control of nutrient supply during plant development. Therefore, the objective of this study was to evaluate biometric characteristics, photosynthetic pigments, and selenium accumulation in sweet potato plantlets micropropagated in vitro in Murashige and Skoog (MS) culture medium supplemented with different concentrations of sodium selenate.

Materials and methods

The experiment was conducted at the Plant Tissue Culture Laboratory of the Department of Agriculture, Federal University of Lavras (UFLA), Lavras, Minas Gerais, Brazil.

Sweet potato (Ipomoea batatas (L.) Lam.) plantlets of the cultivar ‘Campina’ were obtained from BioClone® (Fortaleza, Ceará, Brazil), a commercial plant tissue culture laboratory specialized in the production of micropropagated plant material. The in vitro-propagated plantlets were used as the source material for the establishment of the experiments described in this study. Explants measuring 1–2 cm in length, containing two buds and one leaf, were inoculated into glass flasks containing 40 mL of Murashige and Skoog (MS) culture medium supplemented with 30 g L⁻¹ sucrose and solidified with 6.0 g L⁻¹ agar (Agargel Ind. e Comércio Ltda) [22]. The pH of the medium was adjusted to 6.0 ± 0.2 prior to autoclaving at 121 °C and 1.2 atm for 20 min. No plant growth regulators were added to the culture medium.

After an initial establishment period of 30 days, plantlets were transferred to fresh MS medium supplemented with sodium selenate (Na₂SeO₄) at concentrations of 0.0, 5.0, 10.0, 15.0, 20.0, and 25.0 µM. The experiment was arranged in a completely randomized design with 12 replicates per treatment, each replicate consisting of one plantlet per test tube.

Plantlets were maintained in a growth room under a 16 h photoperiod, temperature of 25 ± 2 °C, and light intensity of 52.5 W m⁻² s⁻¹, measured using a digital lux meter (Equitherm 813-A).

Biometric characteristics were evaluated at 45 days after the second inoculation (DASI). The number of leaves (NL) was determined by direct counting. Shoot length (SL) and root length (RL) were measured using a millimeter-graduated ruler. Shoot dry mass (SDM) and root dry mass (RDM) were determined after drying plant material in a forced-air circulation oven at 65 °C for 72 h, followed by weighing on a precision balance.

Photosynthetic pigments were quantified in fresh leaves (approximately 0.010 g) collected from three plantlets per treatment at 45 DASI. Leaf samples were placed in test tubes containing 3 mL of 80% acetone and wrapped in aluminum foil to prevent light exposure and pigment degradation. Samples were stored at 4 °C for 24 h prior to analysis.

Absorbance was measured using a Multiskan GO microplate spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at wavelengths of 470, 645, 652, and 663 nm [23]. Chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b, total chlorophyll, and carotenoid contents were calculated using established Eqs. [24–25] and expressed on a fresh weight basis (mg g⁻¹ FW). All measurements were performed in triplicate, and data were processed using SkanIt Software version 5.0.0.42.

Selenium accumulation was determined on a dry mass basis following sample digestion. Selenium concentrations were quantified by inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) according to established protocols [26–27] and expressed as mg kg⁻¹ dry weight (DW).

The data were analyzed using a completely randomized design. For each response variable, an analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed, and when significant, treatment means were grouped using the Scott–Knott test at the 5% significance level. All statistical analyses were conducted in R version 4.4.0 [28]. Mean grouping was performed using the AgroR package (version 1.3.6), and figures were generated with ggplot2 based on summary tables containing treatment means, standard errors, and Scott–Knott grouping letters [29].

Discussion

Although selenium accumulation values were relatively low, they are consistent with the non-accumulator behavior of sweet potato and the early in vitro developmental stage, indicating that selenium supplementation can be used as an initial step for biofortification. The greater number of leaves and length of shoots and roots with 5.0 µM sodium selenate in the culture medium may be related to greater biochemical and physiological efficiency in photosynthesis and phloem discharge, improving the absorption and assimilation of nutrients essential for plant development as reported with iron and sulfur in plants of lettuce, rice and wheat [3, 30–31]. Selenium, in the culture medium, stimulates the expression of the Sulfate Transporters genes (SULTR), SULTR1 and SULTR4, involved in the synthesis of sulfate transporters, increasing their absorption and stimulating plant growth up to a certain limit [32]. Non-toxic concentrations of Se stimulate antioxidant systems with a positive effect on the activity of the enzymes catalase (CAT), superoxide dismutase (SOD) and ascorbate peroxidase (APX), reducing oxidative stress in in vitro culture and stimulating the growth of micropropagated plantlets [33]. On the other hand, high levels of Se can cause oxidative stress, inadequate incorporation of selenoamino acids into protein synthesis and phytotoxicity symptoms in plants [15]. The inorganic form of Se, sodium selenate, tends to induce more severe oxidative stress than organic selenium forms such as selenomethionine (SeMet) and selenocysteine (SeCys) [15, 34].

The greater dry mass of the shoot of sweet potato plantlets with the concentration of 5.0 µM of sodium selenate is due to the greater number of leaves and their length as observed in hydroponic cultivation of sweet potato plantlets, with 0.5 mg L⁻¹ of sodium selenate in the nutrient solution [35]. Furthermore, the increase in intercellular spaces of the leaf mesophyll by sodium selenate makes the leaves thicker and with greater dry mass, as reported for Melissa officinalis with 0.2 µM [36]. The greater root dry mass with 5.0 µM of sodium selenate may be related to stimuli of cell division in the meristems or to the action of enzymes in carbohydrate metabolism increasing the production of energy substrates necessary for growth [37]. However, high concentrations of Se in the culture medium can induce leaf chlorosis, reducing photosynthetic efficiency and plant growth [34, 38]. This was reported with high concentrations of reactive oxygen species (ROS) in rice plants under greater Se causing phytotoxicity, lower growth, development, curved leaves, lower turgidity, chlorotic and burn spots on them [39].

Symptoms of toxicity such as reduced growth of sweet potato plants in culture medium with higher concentrations of sodium selenate (20.0 and 25.0 µM) indicate that they exceeded the adequate level of selenium. Furthermore, the lower growth of sweet potato plants with increasing Se concentrations may be due to its similarity to S, causing its exchange for Se in the amino acids cysteine and methionine and altering metabolic pathways and competition for specific transporters as observed for lettuce plants with 16 µM sodium selenate in the nutrient solution [40].

Higher concentrations of chlorophyll ‘a’ and carotenoids, with 5 µM, and ‘b’ and total, with up to 15 µM of sodium selenate, in sweet potato in vitro are due to the presence of this mineral in the reaction centers of photosystem II, dissipating excess energy, delaying the synthesis of ROS and mitigating oxidative damage to chloroplasts and enzymes responsible for the biosynthesis of photosynthetic pigments, as observed in cotton, sweet potato and quinoa plants [35, 41–42]. Furthermore, concentrations of up to 15 µM sodium selenate in the medium can contribute to the maintenance of chlorophyll structure by reducing methionine levels, a precursor of ethylene biosynthesis that intensifies the activity of the enzyme chlorophyllase [43–44], protecting the photosynthetic process [45] and removing ROS by SOD and glutathione peroxidase [46] and increasing respiration with intensification of the synthesis of chlorophylls ‘a’ and ‘b’, and carotenoids [47–48].

On the other hand, increased oxidative stress in plants with higher Se concentrations damages lipids and proteins, due to DNA methylation and replacement of cysteines (Cys) by selenocysteine (SeCys), in the latter, altering their tertiary structures, in addition to reducing cellular respiration, general metabolism, photosynthesis and cellular homeostasis [34, 49]. The reduction in the production of chlorophyll ‘a’ and carotenoids with 15.0 µM and chlorophyll ‘b’ and total and pigments with concentrations above 5 µM of sodium selenate, may be due to a decrease in the synthesis of the enzyme δ-aminolevulinate dehydratase and the impairment of the structure of amino acids, such as methionine and cysteine, which are originally functional with sulfur, but inactivated by higher concentrations of Se [50]. The reduction in metabolism, due to the accumulation of Se, is mainly due to the competition between S and Se, reducing plant growth and pigments as reported in those of cucumber, causing chlorosis [42, 51].

The highest accumulation of Se in sweet potato plantlets in vitro, up to the highest concentration of sodium selenate (25.0 µM), agrees with that observed for other plants, such as wheat and ora-pro-nobis with Se contents increasing in the tissues up to 42.34 µM in the growth medium [5, 52]. The absorption and translocation of Se in plants is due to the high affinity of proteins transporting sulfate with this mineral [53]. Se accumulation in sweet potato plant tissues, although low, is typical of non-accumulating and non-tolerant plants [54–57].

Conclusion

The biometric characteristics and photosynthetic pigments of sweet potato plantlets (Ipomoea batatas), micropropagated in MS culture medium with 5.0 µM sodium selenate, were higher, and this concentration is indicated to produce in vitro plantlets of this plant. Selenium accumulation was greater at a concentration of 25.0 µM of this mineral, which is indicated for the production of biofortified plantlets.

Limitations

This study was conducted under controlled in vitro conditions using a single sweet potato cultivar (cv. Campina). Although in vitro culture provides a highly controlled environment for evaluating plant responses to selenium supplementation, the results may not fully represent plant performance under greenhouse or field conditions, where environmental factors can influence growth, nutrient uptake, and selenium accumulation. In addition, selenium accumulation was assessed during the micropropagation stage and therefore does not necessarily reflect selenium concentrations that may be achieved in storage roots at harvest. Despite these limitations, the in vitro system allowed the identification of sodium selenate concentrations capable of enhancing plant growth and photosynthetic pigment contents while promoting selenium uptake. Future studies should evaluate the effects of selenium supplementation during acclimatization and field cultivation, as well as its impact on selenium accumulation in edible plant organs and the bioavailability of selenium for human nutrition.
