Biphasic growth and subsequent spontaneous regression of posterior fossa meningioma with progressive calcification: illustrative case
Authors: Toshihide Takahashi, Kiyoyuki Yanaka, Hitoshi Aiyama, Saaya Maruyama, Nobuyuki Takahashi, Aiki Marushima, Eiichi Ishikawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Meningiomas are the most common primary intracranial tumors and are frequently characterized by slow, continuous growth.1,2 Although most lesions progressively enlarge over time, spontaneous tumor stabilization or regression has increasingly been reported with the extensive use of long-term imaging follow-up.3–5 Nevertheless, the biological mechanisms underlying spontaneous regression remain incompletely elucidated.

Intratumoral calcification is a common imaging feature of meningiomas and has conventionally been considered an indicator of chronicity or tumor aging.6,7 However, recent longitudinal studies have suggested an association between calcification and alterations in tumor growth kinetics, particularly growth deceleration and reduced proliferative activity, rather than representing a passive marker of chronicity.8,9 Although several reports3–5,10 have described spontaneous tumor regression, most previously reported lesions exhibited progressive volume reduction from initial detection, without a preceding phase of tumor enlargement. Consequently, the temporal and biological association between progressive calcification and the complete natural history of tumor evolution, including initial stability, subsequent growth, and eventual regression, remains incompletely understood.

Long-term natural history studies on meningiomas remain scarce, particularly for posterior fossa lesions, which frequently become symptomatic before prolonged observation is feasible. Notably, most previously reported cases of spontaneously regressing meningiomas have demonstrated steady or continuous volume reduction from initial detection, without a clearly documented preceding growth phase. By contrast, the present case demonstrates a distinct transition from an initial slow-growth phase to a growth phase followed by spontaneous regression over an approximately 17-year longitudinal imaging follow-up. This exceptionally long-term observation offers rare insight into the complete biological evolution of meningioma, suggesting that progressive calcification can function as a tumor aging and biological involution indicator rather than merely a slow-growth indicator. These findings pose crucial implications for understanding meningioma natural history and optimizing the management of asymptomatic patients.

Illustrative Cases

A 76-year-old female was admitted after falling from a chair at home, during which she struck her left shoulder and head. She was transported to the emergency department, where imaging revealed a displaced left clavicular fracture requiring surgical intervention. Noncontrast head CT was performed as part of the evaluation for head trauma, revealing an incidental finding of a calcified mass in the right posterior fossa. This examination was later defined as the reference time point for longitudinal analysis.

The patient denied headache, dizziness, cranial nerve dysfunction, or other neurological symptoms. Neurological examination was unremarkable. Routine laboratory testing indicated microcytic anemia, which, through internal medicine consultation, was attributed to chronic iron deficiency associated with gastroesophageal reflux disease confirmed using upper gastrointestinal endoscopy. No calcium metabolism abnormalities were identified. The patient had undergone natural menopause before the earliest available imaging examination and had no history of hormone replacement therapy, oral contraceptive use, or other hormonal treatment during the observation period.

For longitudinal consistency, the CT examination at which the tumor was initially identified and clearly documented was defined as the reference time point, as both imaging data and corresponding clinical documentation were available. Earlier CT examinations were retrospectively identified, but the original clinical indications were unavailable because of the prolonged retrospective observation period.

CT studies obtained 10, 8, and 7 years before the reference time point revealed a small extra-axial lesion. Calcification was not clearly identified during initial interpretation; however, a retrospective review suggested subtle hyperdense areas consistent with early calcification (Fig. 1A–C). During this period, no significant interval change in tumor size was noted. At the reference time point, CT demonstrated tumor enlargement with a clearly identifiable intratumoral calcification (Fig. 1D). One year later, tumor size and calcification further increased (Fig. 1E). At the long-term follow-up 7 years later, spontaneous tumor regression was observed, whereas calcification continued to progress and became the dominant lesion component (Fig. 1F).

The maximum tumor diameter increased from 28.6 mm at −10 years to 31.8 and 31.9 mm at −8 and −7 years, respectively. At the reference time point, the tumor diameter had increased to 37.5 mm, with calcification measuring 19.3 mm. One year later, the tumor diameter had increased to 41.5 mm, with calcification measuring 32.4 mm. At the latest follow-up, the tumor diameter had decreased to 33.8 mm, whereas calcification had increased to 34.8 mm.

To more accurately assess longitudinal tumor dynamics, both tumor volume and calcified component volume were calculated using slice-by-slice planimetric analysis with ImageJ (National Institutes of Health). This analysis demonstrated an initial slow-growth phase followed by accelerated enlargement and subsequent spontaneous regression, while the calcified component progressively increased throughout the observation period (Fig. 2). MRI was performed 3 months following the latest CT examination (Fig. 3). Axial MRI revealed a right posterior fossa extra-axial lesion measuring 18.0 mm in maximum diameter. The lesion showed isointensity relative to the brain parenchyma on T1- and T2-weighted images and exhibited homogeneous and strong enhancement on contrast-enhanced T1-weighted imaging (Fig. 3A–C). A dural tail sign was observed adjacent to the lesion (Fig. 3B, arrow). FLAIR imaging showed no surrounding edema (Fig. 3D). These imaging findings aligned with a dural-based tumor, suggesting meningioma. The discrepancy between the CT- and MRI-based measurements likely reflected the extensive calcified component visible on CT but poorly visualized on MRI because of signal void effects, whereas MRI primarily depicted the residual enhancing soft tissue component. Currently, the patient remains neurologically intact. No surgical intervention has been performed; therefore, histopathological confirmation is unavailable.

Discussion

Spontaneous regression of meningiomas represents an infrequent but increasingly recognized phenomenon. Although most meningiomas exhibit slow and continuous growth, several studies have reported tumor stabilization or regression in the absence of therapeutic intervention.3–5 Intratumoral hemorrhage, ischemia, necrosis, reduced vascular supply, and degenerative changes leading to decreased cellularity constitute the proposed mechanisms.3–5 Nevertheless, long-term imaging studies documenting the complete natural history of meningiomas over extended periods remain lacking.

Most previously reported cases of spontaneously regressing ­meningiomas have described lesions demonstrating continuous or gradual volume reduction from initial detection.3–5 By contrast, the present case demonstrated a distinct biphasic growth pattern consisting of an initial slow-growth phase, a subsequent growth phase, and eventual spontaneous regression. This characteristic represents a crucial point of novelty, as it facilitates the evaluation of biological transitions during tumor regression and the preceding growth phase, which has been inadequately characterized in previous studies.

Another noteworthy feature of this case is its remarkably long observation period of approximately 17 years with six serial CT studies, enabling clear documentation of sequential slow-growth, growth, and regression phases. This well-defined temporal evolution has rarely been reported, particularly in posterior fossa meningiomas, which are rarer than supratentorial lesions and frequently become symptomatic before extended observation becomes feasible.

The patient had already undergone natural menopause before the earliest available imaging examination and had no history of hormone replacement therapy or hormonal medication use during the longitudinal observation period. The biphasic growth pattern raises crucial queries regarding the biological mechanisms governing meningioma evolution over time. Hormonal status, hormone replacement therapy, medical treatment, or systemic conditions known to influence meningioma growth exhibited no identifiable changes during the transition from tumor enlargement to spontaneous regression. Despite the unavailability of comprehensive hormonal assays, no clinical indications of hormonal manipulation or therapeutic intervention were noted during this period. Although hormonal influences, particularly progesterone, have been implicated in meningioma growth,11 the absence of identifiable external factors in this patient suggests that intrinsic tumor-related biological changes influenced the transition from growth to regression.

The temporal shift in the balance between proliferative and degenerative processes within the tumor represents a plausible explanation. Proliferative activity may have predominated during the growth phase, causing volumetric enlargement. Over time, progressive degenerative changes accompanied by increasing intratumoral calcification may have outweighed proliferative activity, resulting in growth deceleration and subsequent spontaneous regression. The continuous progression of calcification noted across all phases supports the concept that calcification reflects a biologically involutional process rather than a passive late-stage change. This interpretation aligns with the biphasic biological transition observed longitudinally in the present case.

Although the precise mechanism remains speculative without histopathological analysis, the continuous calcification progression across all phases in this case is particularly noteworthy. Dystrophic calcification, characterized by calcium deposition in nonviable or dying tumor cells, is a well-recognized process in meningiomas and has been implicated in spontaneous regression through subsequent apoptosis and diminished cellularity.3,4 In the present case, calcification increased during the growth phase and became dominant during regression, which may suggest its potential contribution to the shift from proliferative to involutional dominance through compromised intratumoral vascular supply, potentially leading to ischemic changes.12 This pattern diverges from our previously reported case of posterior fossa meningioma regression,5 wherein hormonal fluctuations were considered a potential trigger. No identifiable hormonal or external factors were present, reinforcing an intrinsic time-dependent degenerative cascade, potentially accelerated by long-term tumor senescence, progressive mass effect–induced microvascular compromise, or age-related vascular changes in this older adult patient. These observations align with longitudinal studies showing that calcification progression coincides with exponential-to-linear growth transitions and eventual volume reduction.8,9

These findings suggest that meningioma growth behavior cannot be unidirectional but rather reflects dynamic time-dependent shifts in intrinsic biological activity. Therefore, progressive calcification may represent both a passive end-stage phenomenon and a biologically relevant process associated with tumor involution and regression.

The continuous progression of intratumoral calcification across all tumor evolution phases constitutes a distinctive feature of this case. Calcification was not clearly recognized during the early slow-growth phase at the time of initial clinical interpretation; however, a retrospective review suggests that subtle hyperdense areas consistent with early calcification were already present. Calcification became progressively more conspicuous during tumor enlargement and continuously increased even after volumetric regression began, ultimately becoming the dominant lesion component. This inverse association between tumor volume and calcified fraction suggests that calcification reflects ongoing degenerative or involutional processes rather than simple tumor aging.

Recent longitudinal studies have revealed an association between calcification and meningioma growth dynamics.8–10 Individual tumors transition from exponential-to-linear growth patterns coinciding with calcification development, suggesting that calcification can serve as a marker and mediator of growth deceleration.8,13 Thomann et al. reported a median volume decrease of 21.4% in regressing tumors.10 However, the temporal and mechanistic association between progressive calcification and tumor regression has not been systematically characterized. The clearly documented transition from tumor enlargement to subsequent spontaneous regression represents a significant and distinctive feature of the present case. Most previously reported cases of regressing meningiomas have demonstrated a progressive reduction in tumor volume from initial detection, making it challenging to determine whether regression reflects intrinsic tumor biology or a late-stage degeneration of an already inactive lesion.10 By contrast, the present case exhibited a definite growth phase before regression, providing longitudinal imaging findings consistent with a biological shift from proliferative to involutional dominance. This finding suggests that spontaneous regression may represent an active and dynamic biological process rather than simply a passive end stage of tumor aging. This case provides rare longitudinal imaging evidence that increasing intratumoral calcification may precede and accompany volumetric regression, supporting the hypothesis of an association between calcification and biological deceleration and involution rather than representing a purely passive degenerative change.

Calcifying pseudoneoplasm of the neuraxis (CAPNON) represents an essential differential diagnosis for heavily calcified intracranial lesions.14,15 CAPNON is considered a rare nonneoplastic reactive process characterized by dense calcification, minimal soft tissue components, and little change in lesion size over time. By contrast, the present lesion exhibited a considerable soft tissue component during the growth phase and a clearly documented biphasic volumetric course with enlargement followed by regression. Furthermore, calcification was initially not clearly identifiable but became progressively more conspicuous over time, a temporal pattern inconsistent with the known behavior of CAPNON. These imaging characteristics, alongside the dural-based posterior fossa location, extended asymptomatic course, and dural tail sign on MRI, strongly favor the diagnosis of meningioma with degenerative change.

Another significant dural-based differential diagnosis is solitary fibrous tumor (SFT). However, SFTs frequently exhibit progressive growth and seldom show prominent intratumoral calcification.16 Spontaneous regression has not been described as a characteristic feature of SFT. The long-term stability, transient growth phase, subsequent spontaneous regression, and progressive dense calcification observed in this case render SFT unlikely.

Observations

From a clinical standpoint, the longitudinal volumetric analysis in this case offers a crucial implication for managing asymptomatic meningiomas. The transient increase in tumor size did not predict sustained progression or clinical deterioration but was followed by spontaneous regression and progressive calcification. These results suggest that, in carefully selected asymptomatic patients, a temporary increase in tumor volume alone should not necessarily prompt immediate surgical intervention. Instead, careful longitudinal imaging assessment, encompassing evaluation of calcification dynamics, may help differentiate transient biological phases from true progressive disease and support more tailored conservative management strategies.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. Histopathological confirmation and molecular characterization were unavailable because the lesion was managed conservatively without surgery. In addition, volumetric assessment was based on serial CT rather than contrast-enhanced MRI, which may limit precise characterization of the soft tissue tumor component. Therefore, the proposed biological interpretation regarding calcification-associated involutional change remains speculative and is based solely on longitudinal imaging findings. Despite these limitations, the extended observation period and internally consistent imaging findings provide valuable insight into the biological behavior of this lesion. Advanced molecular imaging modalities such as Ga-DOTATOC PET/CT may provide complementary information regarding somatostatin receptor expression and biological tumor activity in meningiomas. Such imaging may also help clarify the relationship between calcification progression and biological involution in similar longitudinal cases. Although this modality was not used in the present case because of conservative management, future studies incorporating functional imaging may provide additional mechanistic insight. In addition, longitudinal Hounsfield unit quantification of the soft tissue and calcified components, as validated in prior natural history studies, was not performed owing to inconsistent CT acquisition parameters across the 17-year observation period; future prospective studies with standardized protocols may enable this more granular objective assessment.

Lessons

This case provides several practical observations regarding tumor growth dynamic interpretation and management decisions in asymptomatic meningiomas. This case suggests that meningiomas can undergo dynamic biological transitions over extended periods, including growth deceleration and spontaneous regression phases. The sustained intratumoral calcification progression noted across all tumor evolution stages suggests that calcification can represent more than a passive indicator of tumor aging and is potentially associated with biological processes underlying tumor involution. Although histopathological confirmation is unavailable, this exceptionally extended longitudinal observation offers valuable insights into the association between calcification and meningioma growth dynamics. These findings underscore the significance of vigilant longitudinal imaging follow-up and support a cautious individualized management approach in selected asymptomatic patients.
