1 Introduction
As part of its commitment to advancing safety and reliability assessment methodologies, Argonne National
Laboratory pioneered the use of an evaluation method called the Success Path Method (SPM) to improve
risk management for offshore oil and gas operations [1]. The development of the SPM at Argonne has been
driven by the need to improve existing risk assessment methodologies by focusing on the steps necessary
for success rather than failure modes alone. This is particularly important for industrial environments like
offshore facilities that perform multiple functions under a continuously evolving set of operational
conditions – such as water depth and temperature, currents, and weather conditions. In these dynamic
environments, the traditional Probabilistic Risk Assessment (PRA) approach is far too complex as it focuses
on what can go wrong – which comprises an infinite failure space that must be fully explored and understood.
By shifting the focus to a finite space of success paths, the SPM enables operators and decision makers to
prioritize a manageable number of steps that must go right to ensure success.

Building on its five decades of experience in safety assessments for the nuclear industry, Argonne made
major adaptations to existing risk assessment methods utilizing features similar to fault trees that are
traditionally used in PRA to map all pathways in which the system can malfunction. In contrast, SPM
identifies the components and processes that must function correctly to achieve specific outcomes – such as
preventing the uncontrolled release of hydrocarbons during drilling operations. The SPM framework
integrates equipment, procedures, software, processes, and human actions to ensure that physical barriers
meet critical safety functions in dynamic operational conditions. This approach helps identify failure modes
and improve operational risk management by narrowing the focus to key success elements, which in turn
reduces uncertainty and helps users understand, manage, and respond to failures.

In the offshore oil and gas context, SPM maps out critical pathways necessary for the successful operation
of complex systems such as blowout preventers, and outlines necessary steps for the design, maintenance,
and operation of system components. It helps identify root causes of operational safety risks to facilitate
informed planning and decision-making. The approach offers additional benefits, including a consistent
framework for communication among stakeholders, improved risk management, and enhanced training
tools. The SPM also includes Failure Modes, Effects and Criticality Analysis (FMECA) to complete risk
analyses with in-depth component assessments. [2] Details on the SPM methodology and its implementation
are described elsewhere. [3], [4], [5]

One strength of the SPM is its adaptability to high-stakes processes across multiple industries and
environments. Argonne initially developed the SPM in 2012 as part of a seven-year agreement with the U.S.
Bureau of Safety and Environmental Enforcement (BSEE) to support safety improvements following the
Deepwater Horizon incident. [6] Since then, Argonne has continued to improve safety in the Gulf of Mexico
region in partnership with academia and the U.S. Department of Transportation (DOT) by analyzing
voluntary industry data and assessing operational challenges across the industry.

Later in 2020, Argonne became part of a team selected for the U.S.-Israel Energy Center partnership to
enhance energy security in the Eastern Mediterranean. The partnership spans teams from multiple
universities in the U.S. and Israel, as well as Israeli companies, foundations, and government agencies. Here,
Argonne’s role focused on enabling industry to explore offshore natural gas reservoirs in a safe, efficient,
and sustainable manner, and on aligning the SPM with the requirements of the International Organization
for Standardization. [7]

Beyond the energy sector, the SPM was also applied to develop Success Paths for resilient cities and
investigate opportunities for risk reduction in critical infrastructure systems. In collaboration with the United
Nations Development Programme (UNDP) and King Abdullah Petroleum Studies, Argonne provided

recommendations to the Global Solutions Initiative for visualizing, designing, and investing in the necessary
physical and social infrastructures for cities of the future using the SPM approach. [6]

Previous work [8] applied the Success Path Method using Microsoft Visio© software for graphical
visualization of SPM diagrams and to perform the qualification analysis, and SAPHIRE© software [9] for
the quantification analysis. Building on this, Argonne has been developing a dedicated Success Path Method

2 Background
2.1 SPM Tool

Argonne has developed the SPM Tool, which is designed to facilitate system and process analysis using the
Success Path Method. This tool enables users to define an objective, identify associated operational
pathways, and specify the actions and objects needed to achieve the objective while adhering to the stepwise,
iterative nature of the SPM method. [2]

The tool features a user-friendly interface that guides users through the analysis in a step-by-step fashion.
Input data provided by the user is organized and stored in a table format, ensuring easy access for revisions
or additions to existing records.

Figure 1 below illustrates the SPM tool’s landing page, where a navigation panel guides users through the
Success Path analysis with walk-through examples that culminate in the creation of an input file for
OpenPRA. For a detailed description of the tool, users can refer to the User Manual provided in Appendix
A of this report. Some of the tool features will be highlighted in this report while going through the test case
in Chapter 3.

2.2 Integration with OpenPRA Web Editor

The SPM tool is currently coupled with OpenPRA, an open-source software platform for visualization and
quantification of Success Path Method diagrams. OpenPRA is described as “an open-source initiative
building the next generation of probabilistic risk assessment (PRA) tools and methods,” offering users a
free, customizable platform for integrating multiple PRA methods and tools into a holistic framework. [11]

Since OpenPRA was designed as a PRA tool, it works with classical elements of probabilistic risk analysis
such as event trees, fault trees and their combinations. The common fault tree graphical elements include
basic events, intermediate events, and logic gates (AND, OR, and TRANSFER) that interconnect events
within the tree. [12] The SPM, by contrast, includes additional graphical elements as illustrated in Figure 2,
where the first four elements are with fault tree modeling, and the bottom two are specific to the SPM.

Although the traditional fault tree elements can be used to approximate SPM diagrams with some limitations,
the direct quantification of SPM diagrams in OpenPRA requires some modifications since by definition,
success paths belong to the success space and OpenPRA works with the failure space. Section 2.3 provides
a detailed description of the differences between two spaces, along with the data requirements for performing
quantification within each space.

 Figure 2. SPM graphics building elements [4]

2.3 Success Space vs Failure Space

As mentioned above, the SPM focuses on what must go right rather than on the near-infinite number of ways
that things can go wrong. This approach enables analysis to take place in a finite success space, as opposed
to the more traditionally utilized and much larger failure space. These two spaces are complementary, and
the analysis can switch from one space to another as needed.

To illustrate this concept, consider a simple classical fault tree. In a fault tree, the top event represents a
failure (e.g., a system or a process failure). If the system fails, it means that the system did not succeed. In
classical probability theory, an event either occurs or does not occur, in this case, the system or process
either succeeds fails. Thus, failure and success are complements to each other. The key characteristic of
complements is that their combination yields deterministic results: using the logic OR (+) operation, the

    Introduction | Background| Test Case | Conclusions and Next Steps | References | Appendix                5

results are always 1, while using the logic AND (*) operation, the result is always 0. This reflects the fact
that a system cannot simultaneously fail and succeed.

Figure 3 illustrates the transition between the success space (left, green background) and the failure space
(right, red background). In the success diagram on the left, an OR-gate, labeled “Object A” has two events
beneath it: event “A1” and event “A2”. To see this gate in the failure space, the following steps are required:

  1. Replace each element with its complement. For example, “Object A” becomes “Not Object A”, and
 events “A1” and “A2” become “Not A1” and “Not A2”. In Boolean algebra notation, the complement
 is typically denoted with a bar above its name, e.g., “Object A ” represents “Not Object A”.
  2. Transform the gates using De Morgan’s Law. While gates themselves do not have direct
 complements, their behavior can be transformed using De Morgan’s laws:
 (i) The complement of the union of two sets (AND gate) is equal to the intersection (OR gate) of
 their individual complements.
 (ii) The complement of the intersection of two sets (OR gate) is equal to the union (AND gate) of
 their individual complements.

 Figure 3. Success vs Failure Space

Applying the second rule, the original OR gate labeled “Object A” with events “A1” and “A2” is now
converted to an AND gate labeled “Object A” with events “𝐴1” and “𝐴2”. Similarly, the OR gate labeled
“Object B” with events “B1” and “B2” is now converted to an AND gate labeled “Object B” with events
“𝐵1” and “𝐵2”. Finally, the original AND gate labeled “Success” with events “Object A” and “Object B” is
now converted to an OR gate labeled “𝑆𝑢𝑐𝑐𝑒𝑠𝑠” which is equivalent to “Failure” with events “Object A”
and “Object B”. This conversion process is bidirectional, meaning the same steps can be applied regardless
of whether the starting point is the success space or the failure space.

One of the features of the current version of SPM Tool is the automatic conversion of SPM diagrams into
fault trees, simplifying the transition between the two spaces. Further details are provided in later sections
of this report.

It is important to note that the numerical input for quantification analysis depends on the space being
analyzed. In the failure space, basic events are quantified using failure probabilities, whereas in the success
space, with the inputs consists of success probabilities (reliabilities). These two inputs are complements to
each other, reflecting the complementary nature of the spaces themselves.

3.1 System description

A BOP is a specialized valve or similar mechanical device, used to seal, control and monitor oil and gas
wells to prevent blowouts - the uncontrolled release of crude oil or natural gas from a well – and they serve
as the last line of defense in preventing a catastrophic event on the rig. They are usually installed in stacks
of other valves. A typical subsea deepwater BOP system includes components such as electrical
and hydraulic lines, control pods, hydraulic accumulators, test valve, kill and choke lines and valves, riser
joint, hydraulic connectors, and a support frame (see Figure 5). The BOP is controlled as follows. [13] A
direct electrical signal is sent from either the Driller’s Panel or the Toolpusher’s Panel to the Central Control
Unit, which then transmits the signal through the subsea hose bundle down to the Subsea Electronic Modules
(SEMs) located in the subsea control pods. SEMs analyze the signal and generate a correct signal for the
electro-hydraulic solenoid valve. The solenoid valve moves and transfers the pilot fluid through its
corresponding subplate mounted valve for the desired BOP function. The subplate mounted valve is a spring
valve that is normally in a closed position. Applying hydraulic pilot signal pressure opens the subplate
mounted valve, allowing the primary supply of the pressurized fluid to flow through the valve’s bottom port
and to the functions it controls. At the same time, pilot pressure is removed from a paired subplate mounted
valve on the other side of the function, allowing the back pressure to vent to sea.

      Introduction | Background| Test Case | Conclusions and Next Steps | References | Appendix             7

 Figure 5. Basic diagram of a BOP Multiplex Control System (Adapted from [13])

Hydraulic fluid is stored in both surface and subsea accumulators. The two streams are combined before
entering the Regulator which sets the fluid pressure according to the BOP function. Once the function is
performed the pilot pressure is relieved and the valves are automatically closed by the spring load.

The components of interest for this study, the control pods, receive signals from surface systems through
umbilicals or acoustical links to “shut-in” the well in the event of an impending blowout. These pods are
typically provided in redundant pairs, commonly referred to as blue and yellow pods, with one active and
the other on standby. This redundancy is crucial especially for deepwater operation where the BOP is located
on the sea floor. If a problem occurs with an active control pod while a preventer is being closed, the other
pod can immediately be switched on and take over the closing of the preventer. The disabled pod can then
be brought to the surface for repair while the standby pod continues to operate the stack on the normal basis.
This redundancy principle can reduce the non-drilling time to a minimum.

3.2 Development of the Success Path Diagram

Based on the simplified description of the BOP operation discussed above, the Blue Pod can be viewed as
comprising three super components: the SEMs, the electro-hydraulic solenoid valve, and subplate mounted
valve. Each of these super components includes multiple internal components. The activation of the BOP
closure command goes through the SEMs first, then the solenoid valve followed by the subplate mounted
valve.

Let’s look at those components separately. For the SEMs to operate properly, two conditions must be met:
the signal sent by the Central Control Unit must be received, and the AC power must be available for the
SEM modules. Once both conditions are met, the SEMs analyze the signal and generate the appropriate
signal for the electro-hydraulic solenoid valve. The solenoid valve DC power and hydraulic fluid. The AC
power that comes from the surface is converted to DC, by an AC/DC converter. Hydraulic fluid is stored in
the surface and subsea accumulators and is routed through a regulator to achieve the pressure setpoint
required for the selected BOP function. The solenoid valve’s mechanical components must also function
correctly to deliver pilot fluid to the subplate mounted valve.

    Introduction | Background| Test Case | Conclusions and Next Steps | References | Appendix                8

The subplate mounted valve, which is normally closed, receives the hydraulic fluid from the solenoid valve
and passes it to the required BOP function. To prevent over-pressurization, a vent subplate mounted valve
vents the used hydraulic fluid to the sea. As with the solenoid valve, the operational conditions of the
subplate mounted valve components must be sufficient to ensure successful operation.

3.2.1 Creation

There are two ways to enter the information about the system in the SPM Tool: a top-down approach and a
bottom-up approach. The top-down approach follows the steps of the SPM precisely, beginning with the
definition of the system objectives and the success pathways to achieve them. The bottom-up approach
follows the more traditional modeling methods utilizing fault trees, starting from bottom-level components
and working upward to achieve the system objective via success pathways. Both approaches are explained
in detail in the Walk-Through Example provided in the SPM Tool. When applied correctly, either approach
yields the same result – in this case, an identical Success Path Method diagram for the desired system
objective.

Table 1 and Table 2 show the completed inputs on the Object/Action and Pathway screens of the SPM Tool.
The gearwheel icon shown next to an element (e.g., 3K Pilot Supply Accumulators) in the Object/Action
Table indicates that this element is a support system for another system component. Together, these two
tables capture all the user-provided information about the system.

opportunity to detect errors is at the success path method diagram level, as discussed in the next subsection.

3.2.2 Visualization

As noted earlier, the current version of the SPM Tool is coupled with OpenPRA and uses its graphical
capabilities to build Success Path Method diagrams from the two tables created in the previous step. Data
are transferred by saving the SPM model in a JSON-file format using the tool, which can then be opened in
the OpenPRA software. The User Manual provides instructions for creating the required JSON file. Once
the file is created and imported into OpenPRA, the SPM diagram is rendered on the screen for the user.
Figure 6 shows the SPM diagram created for the Blue Pod system. In this figure, the SEMs part is modeled
slightly differently from Figure 4 to separate the hardware elements of the SEMs.

This would be that second opportunity mentioned above to catch any mistakes in the model. The examples
of mistakes one can easily catch at that level would be:

 - Some disconnected elements (i.e., objects/actions) on the screen. This would mean that they were
 entered into the Object/Action table but never used in any of the pathways identified in the Pathway
 table.

 - Some pathways include wrong elements. The solution would be to go back to the SPM Tool and re-
 check the information entered in the Pathway table, to make sure the right elements are combined
 under each pathway. The Tool does allow removal and addition of new elements from and to
 pathways. The changes could also be made directly at the diagram level in OpenPRA.

 - Missing elements. There could be several explanations for this. The first and most probable one is
 when a user forgets to enter a certain component or create a particular pathway (which would also be
 missing from the Object/Action table). A second reason would be if the component was created but
 not connected to the correct pathway (the component will appear in the Object/Action table but will
 be under a wrong pathway in the Pathway table). Additional reasons could be (1) if the user did enter
 the component (so it is in the Object/Action table) and failed to check the ‘show in OpenPRA’ box,
 and (2) the user did not include it within any of the pathways. This would lead to a special case of the
 disconnected component mentioned above; the difference now is that the component is in the system
 (appears in the Object/Action table) but it is just not shown on the diagram.

The examples of possible mistakes (with solutions) listed above can be tracked down by the user at the
success path diagram level. The next opportunity for spotting any mistakes would be at the quantification
level.

3.3 Qualification Analysis

Once the SPM diagram is created, the first series of analyses can be performed. These are mainly “what-if”
type analyses whereby the user investigates the system’s ability to achieve the objective while one or more
success pathways are compromised by a degraded or failed component. The basic procedure is to evaluate
each component in the SPM diagram and ask what would happen if it were out of service, e.g., due to
maintenance, actual failure. Does the failure affect system success, and if so, how severe is the impact?

Using the Blue Pod as an example, a loss of AC Power disables both, the SEMs and the Solenoid valve
(shown as upper package in Figure 6) which would prevent the Blue Pod from performing its function. By
contrast, failure of the 3K pilot supply from the surface does not affect the pod’s function due to an alternate
3K pilot fluid source in the accumulators subsea. Pod performance is degraded but not lost completely. This
type of analysis can help identify components that are critical to system operation (and safety, and support
prioritization of management and maintenance crew deployment across various system components.

3.4 Quantification Analysis

3.4.1 Switching to Fault Tree Diagram

There are two ways to quantify SPM diagrams in OpenPRA depending on the space chosen: failure space
or success space. Their complementarity was discussed in Chapter 2.3 of this report. To demonstrate that
both methods yield the same results, the Blue Pod SPM diagram was quantified with both methods. Each
method is described below.

 1) Quantification in the failure space.

 This method may be more familiar to users with experience in classical fault trees or event trees
 where the input data are in the form of failure probabilities. Most industry reliability data are
 recorded from the failure perspective, for example, the number of failures a component or system
 experienced over the past five years, or the number of times a pump fails to start on demand since
 the beginning of operating cycle. [14] Consequently, failure data are more commonly used than
 success data in system risk assessments.

As noted above, OpenPRA is based on PRA and the SPM uses its fault tree graphics to visualize success
path diagrams. In OpenPRA the success path diagram is a fault tree, and the quantification proceeds as if it
were a fault tree. The SPM Tool includes a built-in option to switch between the success path method
diagram model and the fault tree model.

 Figure 7 shows the Blue Pod SPM diagram converted into its fault tree representation. Several parts
 of the diagram are represented by transfer gates (triangles) indicating that those portions expand
 down. The objective of this test case is not to recreate a full SPM model of the Blue Pod system (as
 previously done by Argonne), but rather to test and demonstrate the SPM Tool developed by
 Argonne. Accordingly, in this test case, the transfer gates were treated as regular basic events with
 input data taken directly from the previous BOP study. With that in mind, the Blue Pod fault tree
 reduces to the version shown in Figure 8.

Figure 12 presents the quantification results from the success space perspective. No conversion to the fault
tree version of the diagram was needed for this run; however, the inputs had to be modified from failure

components that must succeed (not fail) for the Blue Pod to succeed in its operation.

3.5 Results

As shown above, quantifying the model in both spaces, failure and success, yields identical results. Although
OpenPRA is primarily designed for failure space analysis, the test case demonstrates that with some
modifications it can be used effectively in the success space. The alignment with the results from the
previously developed Blue Pod SPM model in SAPHIRE further strengthens confidence in the SPM
Software Tool.

4 Conclusions and Next Steps
This report discussed the development of Argonne’s Success Path Method Software Tool©. The current
version is coupled with the open-source web editor (OpenPRA) which supports visualization and
quantification of SPM diagrams. To assess its performance, the tool was applied to a BOP Blue Pod system
previously analyzed by Argonne using SAPHIRE, with quantification results from that study available for

Appendix A: Success Path Method Software Tool© User Manual
(Last Modification: August 11, 2025)

Introduction

Argonne National Laboratory (Argonne) has developed a technique, Success Path Method (SPM), for
evaluating and managing risk associated with processes. The SPM provides a systematic approach, for a
specific system or process objective, to identify success paths, evaluate their importance, identify areas
for improvement, and incorporate new information. At its basic level, a success path outlines the objects
and actions necessary to achieve a process objective1,2.

A success path analysis is performed in a stepwise, iterative fashion shown in Figure 13 below.

 Figure 13. Argonne Success Path Method1

For the system under consideration, where a system can range from a small mechanical system to the
overall organization, the processes and associated objectives are identified. For each objective of a
process, the operational pathways to achieve the objective are identified and a success path is developed
that includes all objects and actions that are required for success of the operational pathway. The success
path outlines the associated objects and actions' requirements, capabilities, and likelihood of failure (if
quantitative analysis is required or adds sufficient value). Once instituted, the success paths are utilized
to track process performance and identify areas of improvement1.

This user manual describes a software developed to enable Success Path Method (SPM) users with a
capability of qualitative system analysis by providing means to define an objective, associated sets of
operational pathways, and actions and objects necessary to achieve the objective, and finally to visualize
the SPM diagram in OpenPRA2,3.

FAQ

As more users begin using the tool frequently asked questions will be added to share lessons learned.
Here are some of the initial lessons learned:
  When opening the tool if notice there are broken image links and not all functionality seems to
 be working correctly there is a chance that the zip file hasn’t been extracted and you are trying
 to access files from the compressed folder, a folder/file name might have been changed, or you
 may not have internet.

Introduction | Background| Test Case | Conclusions and Next Steps| References |Appendix A-30

 o Please be sure that you have extracted the contents of the zipped SuccessPathMethod file
 to a chosen area on your personal PC. If you have opened the zip file and double clicked
 the html file, it will appear to work but there will be GUI issues and possibly functionality
 issues.
 o Please be sure at the same folder level as CreateJSONforOpenPRA.html file there is the
 named folder TOOLREQUIREDFILES_DONOTMOVE. Please do not relocate or
 rename this folder.
 o Currently this tool needs internet access to correctly display and run (uses common
 shared libraries: bootscrap, ajax, and jquery)
 After starting a SPM I need to come back later and make additional changes or finish will I need
 to restart from the beginning?
 o If you wish to edit a project later, please use the "Save" button to save your work. If you
 do not do that first, once you close your browser or refresh your screen, the data you
 enter is lost.
 When using the “Save” button is there anyway to control where the file is saved?
 o Edge: In the address bar type edge://settings/downloads, here you can toggle on "Ask me
 what to do with each download" by doing this whenever a website is downloading a file
 it will provide you with the "Save As" Dialog box allowing you to pick the location and
 name.
 o Chrome: In the address bar type chrome://settings/downloads, here you can toggle on
 "Ask me what to do with each download" by doing this whenever a website is
 downloading a file it will provide you with the "Save As" Dialog box allowing you to
 pick the location and name.
