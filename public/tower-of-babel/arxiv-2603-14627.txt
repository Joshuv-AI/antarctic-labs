Monotonicity for the fractional semi-linear problem in a half space
Wenxiong Chen, Yahong Guo, Leyun Wu

Abstract
. In this paper, we study semilinear fractional equations ( − Δ ) s ​ u ​ ( x ) = f ⁡ ( u ⁡ ( x ) ) (-\Delta)^{s}u(x)=f(u(x)) in a half-space and prove that all positive solutions are strictly increasing in the x n \displaystyle x_{n} -direction. Previous results typically require the solution u \displaystyle u to be globally bounded in ℝ n \displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{n} . We substantially weaken this condition by assuming only that u \displaystyle u be bounded in each slab. Moreover, our analysis relies solely on the local Lipschitz continuity of the nonlinearity f \displaystyle f , which is weaker than the conditions imposed in earlier works. As a crucial ingredient, we obtained a boundary Hölder regularity estimate that requires only the boundedness of u \displaystyle u near the boundary. This represents a significant improvement over existing results, which often assumed global boundedness of u \displaystyle u throughout ℝ n \displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{n} . The proof introduces a new idea that may be of independent interest. To derive the monotonicity, we employ the method of moving planes. We first obtain a narrow region principle in unbounded domains, which ensures that the moving plane procedure can be initiated from x n = 0 \displaystyle x_{n}=0 . We then establish two averaging effects for the solutions to fractional equations. These key ingredients guarantee that the planes can be moved continuously all the way to x n = ∞ \displaystyle x_{n}=\infty . Previously, narrow region principle can only be applied to a single narrow region. It is for the first time that we establish a multiple narrow region principle that can be applied simultaneously to finitely many narrow regions. Compared with the traditional approaches, methods based on the averaging effect require substantially weaker regularity assumptions and can even accommodate unbounded solutions. We believe that these new ideas and techniques develop here will serve as powerful tools in studying qualitative properties of solutions to fractional equations.



1.
Introduction and main results

In this paper, we study the monotonicity of solutions to the fractional semi-linear problem

(1.1)

\begin{cases}(-\Delta)^{s}u(x)=f(u),&x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+},\\
u(x)=0&x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\backslash\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+},\end{cases}

in a half space
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}=\{x=(x^{\prime},x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:x_{n}>0\}

with
\displaystyle 0<s<1
and
\displaystyle n\geq 2s.

Here
\displaystyle(-\Delta)^{s}
is the fractional Laplacian, a nonlocal operator defined by the singular integral

(1.2)

(-\Delta)^{s}u(x)=C_{n,s}PV\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{u(x)-u(y)}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}dy,

where
\displaystyle PV

stands for the Cauchy principal value, and
\displaystyle C_{n,s}
is a normalization constant.

In order that the integral on the right hand side of (
1.2
) is well defined, we require that
\displaystyle u\in C_{loc}^{1,1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+})\cap\mathcal{L}_{2s}
, where

\mathcal{L}_{2s}=\left\{u\in L_{loc}^{1}\,\Big|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{|u(x)|}{1+|x|^{n+2s}}dx<\infty\right\}

endowed naturally with the norm

\|u\|_{\mathcal{L}_{2s}}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{|u(x)|}{1+|x|^{n+2s}}dx.

Because of the non-locality of the fractional Laplacian, it is necessary to assume
\displaystyle u(x)=0
in the whole complement of
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
, rather than merely on the boundary
\displaystyle\partial\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}.

Our goal is to prove that any nonnegative, nontrivial solutions
\displaystyle u
of (
1.1
) is srictly increasing in the
\displaystyle x_{n}
-direction, without assuming that
\displaystyle u
is bounded in the whole space
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{n}
and under very weak conditions on the nonlinearity
\displaystyle f(u)
. We emphasize that monotonicity in a half-space plays a crucial role in qualitative analysis, as it often serves as a key step toward classification results, nonexistence theorems, and one-dimensional symmetry.

The fractional Laplacian and, more generally, nonlocal elliptic operators arise both in pure mathematical research and in wide range of applications, such as the thin obstacle problem
[
1
,
2
]
, minimal surfaces
[
3
,
4
]
, phase transitions
[
5
]
, crystal dislocation
[

]

[

]
, and fractional quantum mechanics
[
8
]
. For an elementary introduction to the subject, see
[
9
]
and the references therein.

As pointed out in
[
10
]
, the fractional Laplacian is the infinitesimal generator of symmetric stable processes
\displaystyle(0<s<1)
, commonly known in the physics literature as Lévy flights. These processes paly a central role in stochastic modeling, with applications in operations research, queuing theory, mathematical finance, and risk estimation. In contrast to Brownian motion
\displaystyle(s=1)
, which arises as the scaling limit of random walks where particles jump only to nearest-neighbor sites, stable processes can be viewed as the limiting models of random walks in which particles may jump to arbitrary sites, with probabilities decaying according to a power law (see also
[
11

).
The essential difference is that such models incorporate long-range interactions rather than purely local ones.

In particular, they proved that if

To the best of our knowledge, only partial results are available regarding the monotonicity of solutions to the nonlocal problem (

For special nonlinearities of the form

Subsequently, Barrios, Del Pezzo, García-Melián, and Quaas

\displaystyle x_{n}

Most recently, Barrios, García-Melián, and Quaas
[

\displaystyle f

[
33
]
and
[

globally bounded

This common
global boundedness assumption on the solutions
appears to be mainly technical, and it is partly due to the nonlocal nature of the fractional Laplacian.

Can this assumption be weakened? Addressing this issue is one of the motivation of our present paper. After introducing several innovative techniques, we are able to relax the
global boundedness condition
and only require the solutions be bounded on each slab
\displaystyle\Omega_{\lambda}=\{x\mid 0<x_{n}<\lambda.\}
:

(BDS)
For every
\displaystyle\lambda>0
there exists a constant

\displaystyle C(\lambda)>0
such that

\displaystyle 0\leq u\leq C(\lambda)
on
\displaystyle\Omega_{\lambda}.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\displaystyle n\geq 2s,

\displaystyle f\in C_{loc}^{0,1}([0,\infty))

with
\displaystyle f(0)\geq 0
. Assume that
\displaystyle u\in C_{loc}^{1,1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+})\cap\mathcal{L}_{2s}
is a nonnegative, nontrivial classical solution of (
1.1
) satisfying the
(BDS)
condition.
Then

\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{n}}(x)>0,\;\;\forall\,x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}.

As usual, the theorem is proved by using the method of moving planes. In this procedure, one classical approach is to take limit along a sequence of solutions, which requires boundary regularity of solutions. In
[
13
]
, in order to obtain boundary Hölder regularity for the solutions
\displaystyle u
, the authors constructed a super solution; however due to the nonlocal nature of the fractional Laplacian, this construction
requires
\displaystyle u
to be
globally bounded
.

Employing a completely new idea, we are able to establish the same boundary regularity under much weaker assumption that the solutions are merely
locally bounded
near the boundary.

Theorem 1.2
.

(Boundary regularity)
Assume that
\displaystyle u\in\mathcal{L}_{2s}
is a nonnegative solution to

(1.4)

\begin{cases}(-\Delta)^{s}u(x)=f(x),&x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+},\\
u(x)=0,&x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\backslash\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}.\end{cases}

Suppose
\displaystyle u(x)
and
\displaystyle f(x)
are locally bounded near the boundary
\displaystyle\partial\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
.

Then
\displaystyle u(x)
is
\displaystyle s
-Hölder continuous up to the boundary
\displaystyle\partial\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
. More precisely,

u(x)\leq Cx_{n}^{s},\qquad\text{for }x_{n}>0\text{ sufficiently small},

where
\displaystyle C
is a constant depending only on
\displaystyle n
,
\displaystyle s
,
the
\displaystyle L^{\infty}
norm of
\displaystyle u
and
\displaystyle f
near the boundary, and
\displaystyle\|u\|_{\mathcal{L}_{2s}}
.

Previous results on the boundary regularity of solutions to nonlocal equations in half-spaces can be found in
[
33
,
31
]
, where it was shown that
\displaystyle u
is
\displaystyle s
-Hölder continuous on the boundary of the half-space via the Green representation formula, under the assumptions

u\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\mbox{ and }f\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}).

The boundary behavior of solutions to boundary value problems driven by integro-differential operators has been extensively studied
[
19
,
37
,
38
,
42
,
43
,
44
]
.
For boundary value problems of the type (
1.4
), it is often necessary to assume that
\displaystyle u
is bounded throughout the entire space
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

In contrast, in our Theorem
1.2
, by employing a novel iterative technique together with a refined analysis, we establish the same boundary regularity
under the significantly weaker assumption that
\displaystyle u
and
\displaystyle f
are bounded only in a neighborhood of the boundary
\displaystyle\partial\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
, rather than in the whole space
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We believe that this boundary regularity theorem will find broad applications in the study of qualitative properties of solutions to fractional equations.

To establish the boundary regularity (Theorem
1.2
), we first decompose the solution into two parts: the potential function

v(x)=\int_{B_{1}(e_{n})}G(x,y)\,f(y)\,dy,

and the
\displaystyle s
-harmonic part

w(x)=u(x)-v(x),

which can be represented by the Poisson kernel.

We first show that
\displaystyle v(x)
is
\displaystyle s
-Hölder continuous by constructing a suitable supersolution.

Then under the local bounded-ness assumption on
\displaystyle u
, by a careful analysis of the Poisson integral, we derive that

\displaystyle w(x)
is
\displaystyle\min\{s,\,1-s\}
-Hölder continuous,
Hence
\displaystyle u(x)
is also
\displaystyle\min\{s,\,1-s\}
-Hölder continuous. If
\displaystyle s\leq\frac{1}{2}
, we are done. Otherwise, substituting this Hölder regularity of
\displaystyle u

back into the Poisson integral and through another round of fine estimate, we obtain higher regularity of
\displaystyle w
and hence of
\displaystyle u
. After finitely many steps of such iterations, we ultimately arrive at
\displaystyle s
-Hölder boundary regularity of the solution
\displaystyle u
.

Our second main result concerning the monotonicity of the solution without assuming the global boundedness of solutions is as follows.

Theorem 1.3
.

Assume
\displaystyle n\geq 2s,

\displaystyle f\in C_{loc}^{0,1}([0,\infty))

with
\displaystyle f(0)\geq 0
and let
\displaystyle u\in C_{loc}^{1,1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+})\cap\mathcal{L}_{2s}\cap C^{0}(\overline{\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}})

be a uniformly continuous, nonnegative, nontrivial classical solution of (
1.1
).
Then
\displaystyle u(x)
is strictly increasing along the
\displaystyle x_{n}
-direction. Furthermore

\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{n}}(x)>0,\;\;\forall\,x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}.

To overcome the difficulty caused by the nonlocality of the fractional Laplacian, we introduce a completely new approach that repeatedly exploits the
averaging effects
of nonlocal operators; in particular, this mechanism is applied four times, as will be illustrated shortly.

Remark 1.4
.

There are many possible choices for the nonlinearity
\displaystyle f
. A prototypical example is
\displaystyle f(t)=t-t^{3}
, which leads to the fractional Allen-Cahn equation, a widely studied model of phase transitions in media with long-range particle interactions (see, e.g.,
[
45
]
). Other classical choices include
\displaystyle f(t)=t^{p}
with
\displaystyle p>1
and
\displaystyle f(t)=e^{t}
, which give rise to the fractional Lane–Emden and fractional Liouville equations, respectively.

Our results are obtained under substantially weaker conditions on
\displaystyle f
.
For instance, in
[
33
,
25
,
23
,
26
]
, one
typically imposes additional hypotheses, such as
\displaystyle f(t)>0
for
\displaystyle t>0
,
or
\displaystyle f(0)=0
with
\displaystyle f^{\prime}(0)\leq 0
, or the boundedness of
\displaystyle f^{\prime}
.

For other fractional problems on half space, please see
[
30
,
39
,
40
]
.

To obtain the monotonicity, the main frame of the approach is the method of moving planes. Here we list some related notation that will be used throughout this paper.

For
\displaystyle\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, let

T_{\lambda}:=\left\{x=(x^{\prime},x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\;\middle|\;x_{n}=\lambda\right\}

denote the hyperplane perpendicular to the
\displaystyle x_{n}
-axis. Define

\Sigma_{\lambda}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid x_{n}<\lambda\},\qquad\Omega_{\lambda}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}\mid x_{n}<\lambda\},

which represent the regions under the hyperplane
\displaystyle T_{\lambda}
in
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{n}
and in
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
, respectively.
The reflection of a point
\displaystyle x
with respect to
\displaystyle T_{\lambda}
is given by

x^{\lambda}:=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n-1},\,2\lambda-x_{n})=(x^{\prime},\,2\lambda-x_{n}).

To compare the solution
\displaystyle u(x)
with its reflection
\displaystyle u_{\lambda}(x):=u(x^{\lambda})
, we consider

w_{\lambda}(x):=u_{\lambda}(x)-u(x).

Obviously
\displaystyle w_{\lambda}
is antisymmetric with respect to the hyperplane
\displaystyle T_{\lambda}
.

To initiate the method of moving planes from near
\displaystyle x_{n}=0
. the following narrow region principle for antisymmetric functions in unbounded domains is a crucial ingredient.

Theorem 1.5

(Narrow region principle in a union of finitely many narrow slabs)
.

Let
\displaystyle\Omega\subset\Sigma_{\lambda}
be an open set containing in a union of finitely many disjoint narrow slabs:

\Omega\subset\bigcup_{i=1}^{N}\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:a_{i}<x_{n}<a_{i}+l_{i}\},\quad l_{i}>0.

Suppose that

(1.5)

\begin{cases}(-\Delta)^{s}w(x)=c(x)w(x),&x\in\Omega\cap\{w<0\},\\
w(x)\geq 0,&x\in\Sigma_{\lambda}\setminus\Omega,\\
w(x)=-w(x^{\lambda}),&x\in\Sigma_{\lambda}\end{cases}

with the growth condition

w(x)\geq-C_{0}(1+|x|^{\gamma}),\quad 0<\gamma<2s.

Assume that
\displaystyle c(x)
is bounded from above in
\displaystyle\Omega
.

Then there exists a sufficiently small
\displaystyle l>0
, depending on
\displaystyle n,s,\sup_{\Omega}c(x)
and the minimal distance between the slabs, such that if

\max_{1\leq i\leq N}l_{i}<l,

then

w(x)\geq 0\quad\text{in }\Sigma_{\lambda}.

Moreover, if
\displaystyle w(\tilde{x})=0
at some point
\displaystyle\tilde{x}\in\Sigma_{\lambda}
, then
\displaystyle w\equiv 0
in
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Remark 1.6
.

All previous narrow region principles apply only to a single narrow region. Now it is for the first time, a multiple narrow region principle is established here, which relies on a new idea in the proof. Such a principle not only provide a starting point to move the plane in the first step, but also serve as a powerful tool in the second step. As the readers will see in the later section, it allows us to conclude immediately that the sequence under consideration stays away from both narrow boundary slabs, thereby significantly simplifying the subsequent analysis.

This theorem provides a starting point to move the plane near the boundary
\displaystyle x_{n}=0
. We then continuous increase
\displaystyle\lambda
and
prove that the planes
\displaystyle T_{\lambda}
can be moved all the way up
to
\displaystyle\lambda=+\infty
.

Otherwise, suppose that

\lambda_{0}=\sup\{\lambda\mid w_{\mu}(x)\geq 0,x\in\Sigma_{\mu},\;\mu\leq\lambda\}<\infty,

then one could construct a decreasing sequence
\displaystyle\{\lambda_{k}\}
with
\displaystyle\lambda_{k}\to\lambda_{0}
and

\inf_{\Sigma_{\lambda_{k}}}w_{\lambda_{k}}(x)<0.

To derive a contradiction, the main difficulty lies in the fact that
\displaystyle\Sigma_{\lambda_{k}}
are unbounded, and hence the minimum of
\displaystyle w_{\lambda_{k}}
may not
be attained. A classical approach (cf.
[
34
,
26
]
) is to take limit along a sequence of solutions to arrive at limiting equations and
derive a contradiction. However, for such a sequence of equations to converges, one typically requires higher regularity (at least uniformly
\displaystyle C^{2s+\epsilon}
) on the sequence of solutions.

In this paper, we introduce a new and fundamentally different approach–applying the
averaging effects
along such sequences to obtain a contradiction. In this process, we only require the sequence be uniformly continuous and can even accommodate unbounded solutions.

The following averaging effects on the solution
\displaystyle u
and the associated antisymmetric function
\displaystyle w_{\lambda}
play a crucial role in the second step of the method of moving planes.

Theorem 1.7
.

(Averaging effects)
Let
\displaystyle D\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
be a bounded domain. Define

D_{R}:=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\backslash D\,\,\mid\,\,dist(x,D)\leq R,\,\,x_{n}\geq dist(D,\partial\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+})/2\right\}

for any fixed
\displaystyle R>0.

Assume that

\displaystyle u\in C_{loc}^{1,1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\cap\mathcal{L}_{2s}

is a nonnegative uniformly continuous solution of (
1.1
) and there exists a positive constant
\displaystyle C_{0}>0
such that

u(x)\geq C_{0}>0,\,\,\forall x\in D.

Suppose that
\displaystyle f(x)
is continuous in
\displaystyle[0,\infty)
with
\displaystyle f(0)\geq 0.

Then there exists a positive constant
\displaystyle\delta_{0}>0
depending only on
\displaystyle R
and
\displaystyle C_{0}
such that

(1.6)

u(x)\geq\delta_{0},\,\,\forall x\in D_{R}.

However, this
\displaystyle\delta_{0}
is independent of where
\displaystyle D
is.

Remark 1.8
.

It is crucial that the positive constant
\displaystyle\delta_{0}
in the theorem is independent of the location of
\displaystyle D
. In the applications, this set
\displaystyle D
may move toward infinity, while
\displaystyle\delta_{0}
remains unchanged. This is the essential difference between the averaging effect and the strong maximum principle.

Theorem 1.9
.

(Averaging effects for antisymmetric functions)
Let
\displaystyle\tilde{D}\subset\Sigma_{\lambda}
be a bounded domain. Define

\tilde{D}_{R}:=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\backslash\tilde{D}\,\,\mid\,\,dist(x,\tilde{D})\leq R,\,\,x_{n}\leq\lambda-dist(\tilde{D},T_{\lambda})/2\right\}

for any fixed
\displaystyle R>0.

Assume that
\displaystyle w\in C_{loc}^{1,1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\cap\mathcal{L}_{2s}
is uniformly continuous, and satisfies

(1.7)

\begin{cases}(-\Delta)^{s}w=c(x)w(x),&x\in\Sigma_{\lambda},\\
w(x)=-w(x^{\lambda}),&x\in\Sigma_{\lambda}\\
w(x)\geq 0,&x\in\Sigma_{\lambda},\end{cases}

with
\displaystyle c(x)
bounded from below. If there exists a constant
\displaystyle\tilde{C}_{0}>0
such that

w(x)\geq\tilde{C}_{0}>0,\,\,x\in\tilde{D},

then there exists a constant
\displaystyle\tilde{\delta}_{0}>0
depending only on
\displaystyle R
and
\displaystyle\tilde{C}
such that

(1.8)

w(x)\geq\tilde{\delta}_{0},\,\,x\in\tilde{D}_{R}.

Remark 1.10
.

The averaging effect asserts that if a solution to fractional equations is positive in a region

\displaystyle D
, then this positivity is averaged out, or diffused, to any larger region containing

\displaystyle D
. Moreover, the influence becomes stronger as the boundaries of the two regions get closer. These conclusions remain valid for antisymmetric functions as well.

Now in the second step of the method of moving planes, if the plane
\displaystyle T_{\lambda}
has to stop somewhere, we would obtain a sequence
\displaystyle\{x^{k}\}
such that
\displaystyle w_{\lambda_{k}}(x^{k})
approaches its infimum.
Applying the averaging effects twice, we deduce that
\displaystyle u(x^{k})\to 0
as
\displaystyle k\to\infty
.
Then we consider the normalized functions

v_{k}(x)=\frac{u(x)}{u(x_{k})},

along with their antisymmetric counterparts. Employing the averaging effects once more, together with boundary
regularity estimates, we reach a contradiction.

) in the
\displaystyle x_{n}
-direction, thereby proving Theorem

and
1.3
.

2.
Boundary regularity

In what follows, we shall use
\displaystyle C_{i},r_{i}(i=1,2,...)
to denote generic positive constants which may vary in the context.

In this section, we prove Theorem
1.2
on boundary Hölder regularity for fractional equations by decomposing the solutions into two parts and applying an iterative technique.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

For any
\displaystyle\tilde{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
, we aim to show that

u(\tilde{x})\leq C\,\tilde{x}_{n}^{s}.

Denote
\displaystyle x_{0}=(x^{\prime},0)
.
Without loss of generality, we may assume that
\displaystyle x_{0}=0
and

\displaystyle\tilde{x}=(0^{\prime},\tilde{x}_{n})
.

Define

v(x)=\int_{B_{1}(e_{n})}G(x,y)f(y)dy.

Here

G(x,y)=\frac{C_{n,s}}{|x-y|^{n-2s}}\frac{1}{B(s,\frac{n}{2}-s)}\int_{0}^{\frac{(1-|x-e_{n}|^{2})(1-|y-e_{n}|^{2})}{|x-y|^{2}}}\frac{b^{s-1}}{(b+1)^{\frac{n}{2}}}db

denotes the Green function in
\displaystyle B_{1}(e_{n})
.
It is easy to check that
\displaystyle v(x)
satisfies

\begin{cases}(-\Delta)^{s}v(x)=f(x),&x\in B_{1}(e_{n}),\\
v(x)=0,&x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\backslash B_{1}(e_{n}).\end{cases}

Denote

w(x):=u(x)-v(x).

Then
\displaystyle w(x)
satisfies

\begin{cases}(-\Delta)^{s}w(x)=0,&x\in B_{1}(e_{n}),\\
w(x)=u(x),&x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\backslash B_{1}(e_{n}).\end{cases}

We divide the proof into three steps.

Step 1.
We first show that
\displaystyle v(x)
is
\displaystyle s
-Hölder continuous near the boundary and

(2.1)

|v(\tilde{x})|\leq C{\tilde{x}_{n}}^{s}

by constructing a super-solution.

Let

\phi(x)=\begin{cases}C_{0}(1-|x-e_{n}|^{2})^{s}_{+},&x\in B_{1}(e_{n}),\\
0,&x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\backslash B_{1}(e_{n}).\end{cases}

It is well-known that

3.
The narrow region principle and averaging effects

In this section, we first establish the narrow region principle for antisymmetric functions in unbounded domains (Theorem
1.5
). We then derive the averaging effects for solutions to (
1.1
) (Theorem
1.7
), as well as for antisymmetric functions (Theorem
1.9
).

We note that Chen and Wu
[
26
]
established a narrow region principle in unbounded domains for fractional parabolic equations, see also Chen and Ma
[
25
]
for a proof in the context of dual fractional parabolic equations. More recently, the core methodology pertaining to the fractional elliptic case has been systematically expounded in the comprehensive survey
[
21
]
by Chen, Dai and Guo. All these narrow region principles apply only to a single narrow region. In contrast, it is for the first time, a multiple narrow region principle is established here. A new idea is involved in the proof. Such a principle not only provide a starting point to move the plane, but also serve as a powerful tool in the second step in the method of moving planes. As the readers will see in the next section, it allows us to conclude immediately that the sequence under consideration stays away from both narrow boundary slabs, thereby significantly simplifying the subsequent analysis.
