Cost-Effectiveness of Minimally Invasive Tubular vs. Open Laminectomy for Pure Lumbar Spinal Stenosis: A Markov State-Transition Analysis

Rohan A. Phadke, Samer G. Salman, Imaad Virani, James Burnett, Maryam Y. Jawid, Arnav Kak, Alexander Mihas, Lorenzo Deveza

Abstract

Study DesignDecision-analytic cost-effectiveness analysis using a Markov state-transition model.IntroductionLumbar spinal stenosis (LSS) is the most common indication for spine surgery in older adults. Minimally invasive surgery (MIS) tubular decompression and open laminectomy produce equivalent one-year outcomes, but their long-term cost-effectiveness for pure decompression is unknown. We modeled 10-year cost-effectiveness from the U.S. payer perspective.MethodsWe built a five-state Markov model for a simulated cohort of 10,000 patients aged 65 with pure LSS (no instability, deformity, spondylolisthesis, or prior surgery), using 3-month cycles, a 10-year horizon, and 3% annual discounting. QALYs used SPORT EQ-5D utilities. Perioperative complication rates came from the Nerland 2015 propensity-matched cohort (10.6% vs. 14.6%; risk difference 4.1 percentage points, 95% CI -2.6 to 10.7). Recurrence, postoperative utility, and perioperative mortality were equalized across arms. One-way, threshold, and probabilistic sensitivity analyses (PSA) were performed, including wide-uncertainty sampling of the complication difference across its confidence interval.ResultsMIS yielded 5.95 QALYs at $26,283 versus 5.91 QALYs at $30,450 for open laminectomy, saving $4,167 per patient (MIS dominant). MIS was cost-effective in 81% of PSA iterations at $50,000/QALY and in 82% to 88% under wide-uncertainty sampling, and remained cost-effective at $100,000/QALY unless its complication rate exceeded 17.5%. Without any complication advantage, MIS still saved $3,454 with equal QALYs.ConclusionFor pure LSS, MIS tubular decompression was cost-effective and, in the base case, dominant over open laminectomy. The advantage reflects the complication difference and the lower index cost, and should inform rather than determine coverage policy.

Introduction

Lumbar spinal stenosis (LSS) is the most prevalent cause of spinal surgery in adults over 65 years of age.

 The Spine Patient Outcomes Research Trial (SPORT) demonstrated that decompressive surgery improves pain and physical function more than nonoperative care through 4 years.- At 8-year follow-up, the cumulative reoperation rate was 18%.

 This reoperation burden makes the downstream economics of the index approach important.

Minimally invasive surgery (MIS) tubular decompression has emerged as an alternative to traditional open laminectomy for LSS. MIS tubular decompression here denotes a unilateral tubular or microendoscopic approach with bilateral decompression, and open laminectomy conventional midline decompression, performed with or without microscope magnification. The Nerland et al 2015 pragmatic comparative effectiveness study, using data from the Norwegian Registry for Spine Surgery (n = 885), demonstrated equivalence in one-year Oswestry Disability Index and EQ-5D outcomes between microdecompression and open laminectomy, while reporting a lower perioperative complication rate (9.8% vs. 15.0% in the full cohort, p = 0.018; 10.6% vs. 14.6% in the propensity-matched subcohort of 492 patients, the values used in this model) and shorter hospital stays with MIS.

 An operating microscope was used in every microdecompression and in 39.4% of laminectomies in that cohort, so the arms differ in the extent of muscular exposure rather than in magnification. Subsequent meta-analyses have confirmed that MIS tubular decompression is associated with fewer overall complications (OR 0.42, 95% CI 0.21-0.86), with equivalent patient-reported outcomes and reoperation rates.,

Despite equivalent clinical effectiveness, no Markov state-transition model has compared the long-term cost-effectiveness of MIS tubular decompression versus open laminectomy for pure LSS without spondylolisthesis. Prior studies compared surgery with nonoperative care or decompression with versus without fusion, leaving a gap between surgical techniques.- We therefore constructed a Markov model to compare the 10-year cost-effectiveness of MIS tubular decompression versus open laminectomy from the U.S. healthcare payer perspective.

Methods

Model structure and cohort

We constructed a five-state Markov state-transition model () comparing minimally invasive surgery (MIS) tubular decompression with open laminectomy for pure lumbar spinal stenosis (LSS) without instability or spondylolisthesis. In brief, the model follows a group of identical patients through 40 three-month cycles; in each cycle a patient either remains in the current health state or moves to another with a fixed probability, accruing that state’s cost and quality-of-life value. Pure LSS was defined as in the source cohort

: symptomatic central or lateral-recess stenosis at one or two adjacent levels with neurogenic claudication or radiculopathy refractory to non-operative care, excluding dynamic instability, any degenerative or isthmic spondylolisthesis, coronal or sagittal deformity, prior surgery at the index level, and infection, tumor or fracture, each of which independently raises cost and reoperation risk. A hypothetical cohort of 10,000 patients aged 65 entered an arm-specific Postoperative state for one 3-month cycle, then transitioned among Well, Symptomatic, Post-revision, and Death. The cohort is simulated rather than an actual patient sample: hypothetical patients are redistributed across the health states each cycle according to the transition probabilities in . Cohort size scales event counts only and does not affect per-patient costs, QALYs, or the ICER. We used 3-month cycles, a 10-year horizon, half-cycle correction, and a 3% annual discount rate, taking the U.S. healthcare payer perspective. Reporting followed the Consolidated Health Economic Evaluation Reporting Standards (CHEERS) 2022.

Health states and transitions

After the index procedure, patients reached either the Well state (asymptomatic, durable decompression) or the Symptomatic state (persistent or recurrent stenotic symptoms). These states are analytic constructs, not categories reported by the source studies: Well corresponds to durable relief after decompression (EQ-5D 0.78) and Symptomatic to persistent or recurrent claudication at the pre-treatment level (0.58). Entry into each state was governed by the perioperative complication rate and then calibrated to the observed 8-year reoperation rate. Well patients could later become Symptomatic through recurrence, and Symptomatic patients could improve back to Well with non-operative care or progress to revision surgery. Revision moved a patient into the Post-revision state. Every non-Death state carried a per-cycle background mortality risk, and Death was an absorbing state. The two arms differed at only three inputs, index cost, revision cost, and the 90-day complication rate; every transition probability beyond the index procedure was shared, by design (see Input parameters).

Input parameters

All inputs and their evidence tiers are listed in . Inputs followed a prespecified hierarchy: the one comparative input separating the arms came from the best available head-to-head source rather than a pooled estimate, and all other inputs from the largest or most representative source for that parameter. The arms differ at only three inputs, so between-study heterogeneity in the shared parameters cannot by itself create a difference between them; a meta-analytic alternative to the complication estimate is tested in the scenarios. Perioperative (90-day) complication rates were taken directly from the Nerland 2015

 propensity-matched cohort (10.6% for MIS vs. 14.6% for open laminectomy). Health-state utilities were EuroQol-5 Dimension (EQ-5D) index values from the Spine Patient Outcomes Research Trial (SPORT).

 Index procedure costs (Lubelski 2014,

 inflated to 2023 U.S. dollars) were $13,500 for MIS and $15,500 for open laminectomy. Revision surgery occurred at a rate of 40% per year from the Symptomatic state, charged at a weighted cost reflecting 60% repeat decompression and 40% salvage fusion (Adogwa 2012

). Background mortality came from CDC life tables

 and perioperative mortality from the National Inpatient Sample.

 Three long-term parameters, the recurrence rate (1.7% per year), postoperative utility (0.70), and perioperative mortality, were deliberately held equal across the two arms because no decompression-specific study supports an arm-level long-term difference. Nerland 2015

 found equivalent 1-year Oswestry Disability Index and EQ-5D scores and the pooled analyses found no significant difference in reoperation,, so assigning MIS a long-term advantage would be extrapolation; each equalization was relaxed against MIS in sensitivity analysis. Recurrence and revision rates were calibrated so that the model reproduced the 18% cumulative 8-year reoperation rate observed in SPORT (Lurie 2015

).

Analyses

We computed the incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) and the incremental net monetary benefit (iNMB) at willingness-to-pay (WTP) thresholds of $50,000, $100,000, and $150,000 per quality-adjusted life-year (QALY). QALYs were the time spent in each health state multiplied by that state’s EQ-5D index utility, summed across cycles with half-cycle correction and discounting; no disability or functional score was converted into utility, and Oswestry Disability Index data were used only to establish clinical equivalence between the approaches. Uncertainty was assessed by one-way sensitivity analysis (OWSA) on every parameter and by a 10,000-iteration probabilistic sensitivity analysis (PSA) using Beta distributions for probabilities and utilities and Gamma distributions for costs. Seven prespecified scenarios and subgroup analyses were performed (). The complication difference was not statistically significant in the matched cohort (36 of 246 vs. 26 of 246 patients; risk difference 4.1 percentage points, P = 0.23 by McNemar’s test), so we recovered its standard error from the published P value (3.4 percentage points; 95% CI -2.6 to 10.7) and added a threshold analysis; a wide-uncertainty PSA drawing the risk difference from that distribution; and scenarios that relaxed each equalized parameter against MIS, removed the complication advantage, equalized index and revision costs, substituted the meta-analytic odds ratio (0.42), and modeled learning-curve complication rates 25% and 50% above base case ().

Model validation

Internal validity was confirmed by verifying that transition probabilities summed to 1.0 in every cycle. For external calibration, the model reproduced 8-year cumulative reoperation rates of 15.0% for MIS and 18.0% for open laminectomy, the latter matching the 18% pooled rate reported in SPORT, whose surgical arm was open laminectomy (Lurie 2015

).

MIS was the dominant strategy, both less costly and more effective (). MIS produced 5.950 discounted QALYs at $26,283 per patient, versus 5.909 QALYs at $30,450 for open laminectomy, a saving of $4,167 per patient with a net gain of 0.041 QALYs. Robustness of the complication difference

The complication difference was not statistically significant in the matched cohort and was therefore varied widely (). At the unfavorable 95% limit, where MIS carries a higher complication rate than open laminectomy (17.2% vs. 14.6%), MIS lost 0.026 QALYs but still saved $2,998, with iNMB $1,676 at $50,000/QALY but only $355 at $100,000/QALY; the learning-curve scenario at 15.9% behaved similarly. Removing the complication advantage entirely equalized QALYs while MIS still saved $3,454. MIS remained cost-effective at $100,000/QALY until its complication rate reached 17.5%, and at $50,000/QALY until 19.6%. With index costs equalized across arms MIS remained dominant, saving $1,174, as it did when revision costs were also equalized ($749). Relaxing each equalized parameter against MIS left MIS dominant in every case. In the wide-uncertainty PSA, MIS was cost-effective in 82.3% of iterations at $50,000/QALY and 86.0% at $100,000/QALY. Substituting the meta-analytic odds ratio (0.42) made MIS more favorable ($4,862 saved, +0.082 QALYs), so the base case is conservative relative to pooled evidence.

Discussion

This analysis indicates that MIS tubular decompression was the dominant strategy in the base case, producing both lower costs ($4,167 savings per patient) and higher quality-adjusted life-years (0.041 QALY gain) over a 10-year horizon. MIS was cost-effective in approximately 81% to 86% of PSA iterations, including when the complication difference was sampled across its full confidence interval, and was dominant in all seven prespecified scenario and subgroup analyses.

The QALY advantage of MIS was driven entirely by the 4-percentage-point reduction in perioperative complication rates (10.6% vs. 14.6%), derived from the Nerland et al propensity-matched cohort;

 the cost advantage arose mainly from the lower index and revision costs in the MIS arm. This difference was not statistically significant in the matched analysis, so the conclusion is conditional on it; the threshold and wide-uncertainty analyses indicate that the direction of the result is stable while its magnitude is not. A meta-analysis of 2,860 patients confirmed that tubular decompression is associated with significantly fewer overall complications and surgical site infections compared with open laminectomy, with no difference in reoperation rates or patient-reported outcomes.

 The Phan and Mobbs meta-analysis similarly reported lower reoperation rates with MIS, though not when restricted to randomized evidence.

 A matched cohort of 31,000 patients found lower 90-day reoperation and cerebrospinal fluid-related complications with endoscopic decompression, though higher rates of several medical complications.

The model was deliberately conservative in its design. Three key long-term parameters, recurrence rate (1.7%/year), postoperative utility (0.70), and perioperative mortality (0.081%), were equalized across both arms because no decompression-specific study supports an arm-level long-term difference. A multicenter cohort in which functional gains after minimally invasive decompression attenuated over 12 months supports the recurrence pathway.

 Each equalization was relaxed against MIS in sensitivity analysis and MIS remained cost-saving throughout, so the conclusion does not depend on these assumptions; because the equalizations withhold from MIS any long-term benefit of tissue preservation, they bias the QALY comparison against MIS.

Index procedure costs ($13,500 MIS vs. $15,500 open, inflated to 2023 USD) were derived from the Lubelski et al systematic review,

 which reported a $2,311 cost difference favoring the minimal-access approach for multilevel hemilaminectomy but concluded that minimal-access and open lumbar approaches did not differ significantly in cost. Only the between-arm difference affects the ICER; equalizing index costs left MIS dominant ($1,174 saved), as did equalizing revision costs ($749). Health-state utilities came from the SPORT cost-effectiveness analysis by Tosteson et al.

 The post-revision utility of 0.60 is the 2-year value Adogwa et al report directly.

Return to work was examined only as a secondary outcome in the working-age subgroup, because the base-case cohort was aged 65, when most patients are at or near retirement. The return-to-work advantage of MIS (75% vs. 50% at 6 weeks, 92% vs. 78% at 3 months) was derived from length-of-stay and recovery data rather than from directly observed employment outcomes, and applies to the age 55 subgroup, in whom MIS also remained dominant (). Under the societal perspective, which incorporates lost productivity, the per-patient saving increased to $11,546, a figure that applies to employed patients and not to the modeled cohort as a whole.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the perioperative complication rates were drawn from a single propensity-matched observational cohort rather than a randomized controlled trial.

 While this represents the highest-quality comparative data available for this specific comparison, unmeasured confounding cannot be excluded. Although the unmatched complication difference was statistically significant (9.8% vs. 15.0%, p = 0.018), the propensity-matched difference used in the model (10.6% vs. 14.6%) did not reach significance (p = 0.23); its uncertainty was propagated through the probabilistic and threshold analyses, which show the direction of the result is stable across its full confidence interval even though the magnitude is not. Second, the model assumed a homogeneous cohort of 65-year-old patients with pure LSS; real-world populations are heterogeneous in comorbidity burden, stenosis severity, and surgical anatomy. A substantial proportion of surgically treated stenosis patients have concomitant degenerative spondylolisthesis and therefore fall outside the modeled population entirely. Third, the model did not incorporate the MIS learning curve or center-level volume effects; we approximated these by raising the MIS complication rate 25% and 50% above base case, and MIS remained cost-effective in both, but a surgeon or center whose complication rate approaches the 17.5% threshold would not realize the modeled benefit. Fourth, costs were modeled from the U.S. payer perspective and may not be directly transferable to other healthcare systems. Fifth, the 10-year horizon may not capture the full lifetime economic impact, particularly for younger patients.

 Sixth, the model is specific to decompression for pure stenosis and does not extend to other minimally invasive procedures.

 Seventh, the health states are analytic constructs that no source study measured directly, so state occupancy rests on calibration rather than observed data. Eighth, inputs came from different studies, so absolute estimates inherit that heterogeneity even though the arms differ at only three inputs. Finally, the model applied fixed cohort-level inputs rather than individualized perioperative risk; incorporating spine surgery risk-prediction tools could enable more personalized cost-effectiveness estimates in future work.

Conclusion

For patients with pure lumbar spinal stenosis, MIS tubular decompression offers equivalent long-term outcomes with a lower perioperative complication profile, fewer reoperations, and lower costs over a decade. Because MIS was less costly and no less effective in every analysis performed, it is a reasonable default where the necessary expertise and equipment exist. A decision-analytic model cannot by itself establish coverage policy, however: the QALY difference is small, its credible interval crosses zero, and the key comparative input is a single non-randomized estimate. These findings should therefore inform, rather than determine, payer and shared decision-making discussions. Future studies should validate these findings with data from randomized controlled trials with long-term follow-up and should incorporate patient-level heterogeneity through microsimulation modeling.
