RNF34 restrains endometriosis through SREBP1-dependent metabolic-immune crosstalk
Authors: Chenjing Yue, Zilong Li, Mengze Wang, Jie Zhang, Shiyu Du, Xiaofang Ge, Jiayu Liu, Chao Lu, Wushan Li, Zhenhai Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEndometriosis is a chronic inflammatory disease with cancer-like features, yet the mechanisms linking metabolic dysregulation to immune remodeling during lesion progression remain poorly understood. Here, we identify the ubiquitin E3 ligase RNF34 as a central suppressor of endometriosis that integrates cell-intrinsic metabolic control with macrophage-mediated immune regulation. Mechanistically, RNF34 directly interacts with SREBP1 and promotes its K48 and K63-linked ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation, thereby restraining lipogenic gene expression and fatty acid synthesis in endometrial stromal cells. Functionally, RNF34 suppresses stromal cell proliferation, clonogenic growth, migration, and invasion in an SREBP1-dependent manner. Loss of RNF34 stabilizes SREBP1, leading to excessive synthesis and extracellular release of monounsaturated fatty acids, particularly oleic acid. Oleic acid acts as a paracrine metabolic cue that drives macrophage polarization toward an immunosuppressive M2-like phenotype, which in turn reinforces endometriotic cell proliferation, migration, and resistance to apoptosis, establishing a feed-forward metabolic–immune circuit. In vivo, genetic ablation of RNF34 markedly accelerates endometriosis development, accompanied by increased accumulation of M2 macrophages within ectopic lesions, whereas pharmacological inhibition of SREBP1 or macrophage depletion using clodronate liposomes significantly suppresses lesion growth. Consistently, human endometriotic tissues exhibit reduced RNF34 expression that inversely correlates with SREBP1 abundance and M2 macrophage markers. Collectively, our findings define an RNF34-SREBP1-oleic acid axis that links lipid metabolism to immune remodeling in endometriosis, revealing a metabolically driven therapeutic vulnerability.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00018-026-06290-2.Keywords: Endometriosis, RNF34, SREBP1, Lipid metabolism, Macrophage polarization

Introduction

Endometriosis is a chronic inflammatory disorder characterized by the ectopic growth of endometrial-like tissue outside the uterine cavity, affecting up to 10% of reproductive-age women worldwide [1, 2]. Although traditionally considered a benign gynecological disease, endometriosis exhibits multiple cancer-like features, including invasive growth, resistance to apoptosis, metabolic reprogramming, and the ability to remodel its local microenvironment [3–5]. These properties contribute to lesion persistence, recurrence, and therapeutic resistance, rendering current hormonal and surgical interventions largely palliative [6, 7]. Despite extensive investigation, the molecular mechanisms that drive lesion establishment and progression remain incompletely understood.

Accumulating evidence indicates that metabolic dysregulation is a defining hallmark of endometriosis. Endometriotic lesions display enhanced lipid accumulation, altered fatty acid synthesis, and increased reliance on de novo lipogenesis to support rapid proliferation and survival under inflammatory and hypoxic conditions [8, 9]. Lipid metabolic pathways not only provide bioenergetic and biosynthetic substrates but also generate signaling molecules that influence cell fate decisions and intercellular communication [10, 11]. However, the mechanisms by which aberrant lipid metabolism is mechanistically controlled in endometrial stromal cells and how it contributes to disease progression beyond cell-intrinsic effects remain largely unexplored.

Sterol regulatory element-binding protein 1 (SREBP1) is a master transcriptional regulator of fatty acid synthesis and lipid homeostasis [12]. Aberrant activation of SREBP1 has been implicated in a wide range of pathological conditions, including cancer, metabolic disorders and chronic inflammatory diseases [13–15]. More specifically, SREBP1 dysregulation contributes to abnormal lipid metabolism and inflammatory responses in several hormone-associated gynecological disorders, such as endometritis, polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) and endometrial cancer [16, 17]. Despite these observations, whether and how SREBP1 contributes to the pathogenesis of endometriosis remains largely unexplored. In particular, the upstream mechanisms that regulate SREBP1 stability and activity in endometrial stromal cells, and the potential consequences of SREBP1-driven lipid metabolic reprogramming for lesion progression and microenvironmental remodeling, have not been defined.

In parallel with metabolic alterations, immune dysregulation is increasingly recognized as a central driver of endometriosis [18, 19]. The ectopic lesion microenvironment is enriched with immune cells, particularly macrophages, which represent the most abundant immune population within lesions [4, 20]. Rather than mounting an effective clearance response, lesion-associated macrophages predominantly acquire an alternatively activated, immunosuppressive M2-like phenotype [21, 22]. These M2 macrophages secrete growth factors, cytokines, and matrix-remodeling enzymes that promote angiogenesis, tissue invasion, and immune evasion, thereby facilitating lesion persistence [23–25]. However, the signals that instruct macrophage polarization within the endometriotic niche remain incompletely understood.

Emerging studies in cancer and metabolic disease have revealed that lipid-derived metabolites can function as paracrine signals that shape immune cell behavior [26, 27]. Fatty acids, in particular monounsaturated species such as oleic acid, have been shown to modulate macrophage metabolism and polarization through lipid uptake and nuclear receptor signaling, with PPARγ serving as a key mediator of fatty acid-induced M2 polarization [28–32]. Whether aberrant lipid metabolism in endometrial stromal cells contributes to immune remodeling through the release of specific fatty acid signals, and how such metabolic–immune coupling is regulated at the molecular level, remains an open question.

Ubiquitination-mediated protein turnover represents a fundamental mechanism for controlling signaling pathways and metabolic regulators [33–35]. E3 ubiquitin ligases confer substrate specificity within the ubiquitin–proteasome system [36, 37], yet their roles in coordinating metabolic and immune processes in endometriosis are largely unexplored. RNF34 is a RING-type E3 ubiquitin ligase implicated in protein quality control and stress responses [38, 39], but its function in lipid metabolism, immune regulation, and endometriosis has not been investigated.

In this study, we identify RNF34 as a previously unrecognized suppressor of endometriosis progression. We demonstrate that RNF34 directly targets SREBP1 for K48 and K63-linked ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation, thereby restraining lipogenic gene expression and fatty acid synthesis in endometrial stromal cells. Loss of RNF34 leads to excessive production and release of oleic acid, which acts as a paracrine metabolic cue to drive macrophage polarization toward an M2-like phenotype. These macrophages, in turn, reinforce stromal cell proliferation, survival, and invasiveness, establishing a feed-forward metabolic-immune circuit that promotes lesion growth. Together, our study defines an RNF34-SREBP1-oleic acid axis that couples lipid metabolic reprogramming to macrophage polarization, thereby driving endometriosis progression (Fig. 1F).

Materials and methods

Human tissue sample collection and immunohistochemical staining

All the tissue samples were collected from the Affiliated Hospital of Shandong Second Medical University. Ectopic endometrium (n = 30) and eutopic endometrium (n = 30) collected from individuals diagnosed with endometriosis. Normal endometrium (n = 30) were taken from women with menstrual cycles and ages matched to patients with endometriosis, none of whom had a laparoscopically diagnosed adenomyosis, endometriosis, or endometrial abnormalities. All patients have signed informed consent forms. None of the patients had received hormone therapy in the last three months and had no other serious surgical or medical comorbidities. The Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of Shandong Second Medical University granted ethical approval for this study. Tissues were processed for cell isolation and immunohistochemistry. Immunohistochemistry was performed as previously described [40]. Immunohistochemistry staining was semi-quantitatively scored based on frequency and intensity. Frequency scores were: 0 (0–5%), 1 (6–25%), 2 (26–50%), 3 (51–75%) and 4 (76–100%). Intensity scores were: 0 (negative), 1 (weak), 2 (moderate), 3 (strong). The overall staining score (OSS) was derived by multiplying the frequency score by the intensity score.

Cell culture and transfection

This study utilized two stromal cell lines: the immortalized human endometriotic stromal cells (ihESCs) and the immortalized human endometrial stromal cells (HESCs). Both cell lines were purchased from IMMOCELL (Xiamen, Fujian, China). The ihESCs and HESCs were cultured in DMEM/F12 medium, and HEK293T cells (purchased from ATCC, CRL-3519) were cultured in DMEM. Both cell types were maintained in medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 100 µg/ml penicillin, and 100 µg/ml streptomycin, at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO₂ incubator. Cells were transfected with the indicated constructs using Lipofectamine 2000 reagent. Plasmid constructs were generated as described previously [41, 42]. The shRNA sequences, antibodies, and other reagents used are detailed in Table S1.

Western blot and co-immunoprecipitation

Protein lysates were prepared from the specified cells using lysis buffer [43]. Following centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C, the supernatant was collected. For Western blot analysis, an aliquot of the supernatant was mixed with a 1/4 volume of 5×loading buffer and denatured by boiling for 10 min, followed by immunoblotting using the indicated primary antibodies. For co-immunoprecipitation, the collected cells were resuspended in lysate and incubated on ice for 30 min. The resulting mixture was then centrifuged at 12,000 rpm and 4 °C for 10 min, after which 5% of the supernatant was set aside as the input sample. Next, the corresponding antibodies and Protein A/G beads were added to the remaining supernatant, followed by an overnight incubation at 4 °C; normal mouse IgG served as the negative control throughout. Post-incubation, the beads were rinsed five times with lysis buffer, mixed with loading buffer, and boiled for 10 min prior to Western blot analysis.

Cell proliferation and colony formation assays

For the cell proliferation assay, transfected cells were detached with trypsin and counted at 24 h post-transfection, followed by reseeding into 24-well plates at a density of 2 × 104 cells per well. Subsequently, cell counts were determined quantitatively using a hemocytometer every 24 h over a 4-day period. For the colony formation assay, cells were harvested and seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 500–1200 cells per well at 24 h post-transfection. After 10–14 days of incubation, the formed colonies were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min and then stained with 0.5% crystal violet for 20 min, followed by image acquisition for documentation of the stained colonies.

Wound healing assay

On the day following transfection, the treated cells were inoculated into 6-well plates at a seeding density of 5 × 105 to 6 × 105 cells per well to ensure confluency for the scratch assay. A sterile pipette tip was employed to create a consistent scratch wound across each well, and immediate imaging was conducted to document the baseline wound area at 0 h. Following 24 h of incubation under standard conditions, the same visual fields were re-imaged to monitor the dynamic process of cell migration toward the wound area.

Transwell cell migration and invasion assays

Cells were transfected with the indicated plasmids. For Transwell assays, the transfected cells were trypsinized and resuspended in serum-free DMEM/F-12 medium, then seeded into the upper chamber. The lower chamber was filled with complete medium containing 10% FBS as a chemoattractant. For migration assays, non-coated Transwell inserts were used. For invasion assays, the upper chamber was pre-coated with Matrigel (diluted 1:8 in serum-free medium) before cell seeding. For migration assays, HESCs and ihESCs were incubated for 12 h and 16 h, respectively; for invasion assays, HESCs and ihESCs were incubated for 20 h and 24 h, respectively. After incubation, non-migrated or non-invaded cells on the upper surface of the membrane were gently removed with a cotton swab. The cells that had migrated or invaded to the lower surface were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, stained with 0.1% crystal violet, and photographed under a microscope.

Immunofluorescent analysis

Cell crawling assays were performed by seeding 8 × 104 cells and culturing them for 24 h. Fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min, the cells were treated with 0.05% Triton X-100 for 10 min, then blocked with BSA for 1 h prior to overnight incubation with primary antibody at 4°C. The cells were then incubated with fluorescent secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature, and nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Observations and image acquisition were finally carried out under a fluorescence microscope.

Gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS)

Cultured cells were harvested in equal aliquots for fatty acid extraction. The resulting extracts were analyzed by gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS; Agilent 7890 A/5975 C, Agilent Technologies, USA) equipped with a DB-WAX capillary column. GC-MS operation and subsequent statistical analysis were performed following the standard protocols.

Triglyceride and cholesterol assays

Intracellular triglyceride and cholesterol levels were quantified using kits from Applygen Technologies Inc. (Beijing, China), following the protocols of manufacturer. Values were normalized to cellular protein content, which was determined in the lysates using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Scientific, USA).

Animal experiments

The RNF34-knockout (RNF34−/−) mouse model was constructed via CRISPR/Cas9-mediated genome editing in C57BL/6 N embryonic stem cells, using the following gRNA sequences: gRNA-A1: GATAGAAGCCAATTGCTGCGTGG, gRNA-A2: GCTGTGAGGCAACTATTGTCAGG, gRNA-B1: ATTCTAAGTACCCCTAATAAAGG, gRNA-B2: TTTAGTGACTGAGTCAGTTATGG. Due to the special physiological structure, female mice were selected for all experiments. All recipient mice were C57BL/6J wild-type (WT) mice, randomly divided into four groups (n = 8/group). The donor mice were RNF34-knockout (RNF34−/−) female mice and C57BL/6J wild-type (WT) mice. All donor mice were 5 weeks old. Donor mice received intramuscular injections of estradiol benzoate (3 µg/mouse) into the thighs every two days. One week later, the donor mice were euthanized, and the uteri were removed. Under a dissecting stereomicroscope, the uteri were minced into uniform fragments of approximately 1 mm³. To minimize inter-donor variability, all fragments from multiple donors were pooled and mixed thoroughly. For each recipient mouse, 40 mg tissue from the pooled fragments was accurately weighed using a precision balance and suspended in 0.4 mL sterile PBS containing ampicillin (1 mg/mL). The suspension was then injected into the peritoneal cavity of recipient mice by direct intraperitoneal injection. After transplantation, estradiol benzoate was injected three times a week for one week. For one month, mice in the experimental group received intraperitoneal injections of Fatostatin (30 mg/kg), an SREBP1 inhibitor, every three days, while control mice received normal saline. Following euthanasia, ectopic tissues within the abdominal cavity were assessed. The animal experiments were approved by the Ethics Committee of Shandong Second Medical University.

Primary mouse embryonic fibroblasts (MEFs) cells

MEFs cells (RNF34−/−) were obtained from these RNF34−/− mice on day 13.5 of pregnancy. The embryonic tissue was completely cut to pieces with scissors. Trypsin was used to digest the cells in an incubator at 37℃ for 5–10 min. The digested cells were then suspended in DMEM medium and evenly spread on culture dishes, which were kept at 37℃ with a CO2 concentration of 5%.

Macrophage culture and differentiation

Murine macrophages were derived from primary bone marrow cells, whereas human macrophages were obtained from peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs). For mouse macrophage preparation, bone marrow cells were isolated from the femurs and tibias of adult C57BL/6J mice and differentiated into bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) by culturing in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and macrophage colony-stimulating factor (M-CSF) as previously described. Cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂, and non-adherent cells were removed after 48 h. Fully differentiated macrophages were used for subsequent experiments after 6–7 days of culture.

Human macrophages were generated from PBMCs isolated from peripheral blood of healthy donors. Briefly, PBMCs were obtained by density-gradient centrifugation and monocytes were purified using magnetic-activated cell sorting (MACS) with CD14 microbeads according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Purified CD14⁺ monocytes were cultured in DMEM containing 10% FBS and differentiated into macrophages under standard conditions. Unless otherwise indicated, macrophages were maintained in basal medium (DMEM + 10% FBS), and specific treatments or co-culture conditions were applied as described in the corresponding experiments.

Endometrial stromal cell-macrophage co-culture system

An indirect co-culture system was established using Transwell inserts to examine paracrine interactions between endometrial stromal cells and macrophages. Briefly, Transwell plates with 6-well format and 0.4-µm pore size membranes were used to prevent direct cell–cell contact while allowing exchange of soluble factors. Macrophages were seeded in the upper chambers at a density of 5 × 105 cells per well, whereas ihESCs subjected to the indicated genetic or pharmacological manipulations were plated in the lower chambers at densities ranging from 1 × 105 to 5 × 105 cells per well, depending on experimental requirements. Co-cultures were maintained in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum under standard culture conditions. Where indicated, oleic acid (OA) was conjugated to fatty acid-free bovine serum albumin (BSA) at a molar ratio of 5:1 and added to the culture medium at a final concentration of 100 µM, unless otherwise specified. Control groups received an equivalent amount of BSA vehicle. The co-culture system was maintained for 24–48 h, after which macrophages and stromal cells were harvested separately for downstream analyses, including flow cytometry, RNA sequencing, ELISA, and functional assays, as described in the corresponding sections.

Three-dimensional (3D) spheroid co-culture assay

A three-dimensional (3D) spheroid co-culture system was established to evaluate reciprocal interactions between endometrial stromal cells and macrophages under spatially confined conditions. Briefly, ihESCs and macrophages were mixed at a ratio of 9:1, with a total of 1 × 10⁵ cells per spheroid. Cells were resuspended in complete culture medium supplemented with 0.24% methylcellulose to facilitate spheroid formation. 3D spheroids were generated using a modified hanging drop method. Aliquots of 30 µl of the single-cell suspension were pipetted onto the inner surface of a culture dish lid, which was then inverted over a dish containing PBS to maintain humidity and prevent evaporation. Hanging drop cultures were incubated under standard conditions (37 °C, 5% CO₂) and monitored daily for spheroid formation. After 5 days of culture, intact spheroids were gently harvested and transferred to ultra-low attachment plates for further handling. Spheroids were subsequently imaged using confocal laser scanning microscopy under the indicated experimental conditions. Spheroid size and structural integrity were assessed as described in the corresponding figure legends.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

To quantify cytokine secretion by macrophages, culture supernatants were collected from macrophages following co-culture with ihESCs under the indicated experimental conditions. Cell debris was removed by centrifugation at 1,500 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, and clarified supernatants were either analyzed immediately or stored at -80 °C until use. The concentrations of human cytokines, including interleukin-10 (IL-10), transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β), and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), were measured using commercially available ELISA kits (Elabscience Biotechnology, Wuhan, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Absorbance was measured using a microplate reader, and cytokine concentrations were calculated based on standard curves generated for each analyte.

Live/dead cell staining and confocal microscopy

Cell viability was assessed using a live/dead staining assay followed by confocal microscopy. Briefly, ihESCs cultured in the lower chambers of the Transwell co-culture system with macrophages were subjected to the indicated experimental treatments. After 48 h of co-culture, cells were washed with PBS and stained with Calcein-AM and PI using a commercial live/dead viability staining kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Following staining, live cells (Calcein-AM-positive) and dead cells (PI-positive) were visualized and imaged using a confocal laser scanning microscope under identical acquisition settings across experimental groups. Representative images were recorded to assess cell viability under the indicated conditions.

Flow cytometric analysis of apoptosis

Apoptosis was assessed by flow cytometry using Annexin V and propidium iodide (PI) staining. Briefly, ihESCs were subjected to serum starvation by culturing in medium containing 0.5% fetal bovine serum (FBS) for 48 h under the indicated experimental conditions. Following treatment, cells were harvested, washed with cold PBS, and stained with FITC-conjugated Annexin V and PI using a commercial apoptosis detection kit (BD Biosciences), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Stained cells were analyzed by flow cytometry, and apoptotic populations were quantified as the percentage of Annexin V-positive cells. Early apoptotic (Annexin V⁺/PI⁻) and late apoptotic or necrotic (Annexin V⁺/PI⁺) cells were distinguished based on standard gating strategies.

RNA sequencing and bioinformatic analysis

To characterize transcriptional changes associated with stromal cell-macrophage interactions, RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) was performed on murine BMDMs cultured alone or co-cultured with primary mouse endometrial stromal cells. Macrophages were collected after 24 h of indirect co-culture under the indicated experimental conditions. Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 15596018) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA quantity and integrity were assessed using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer with the RNA 6000 Nano LabChip Kit (Agilent Technologies, 5067 − 1511), and only samples with an RNA integrity number (RIN) greater than 7.0 were used for subsequent library construction. Following mRNA enrichment, cDNA synthesis and amplification, sequencing libraries were generated and subjected to paired-end sequencing (2 × 150 bp) on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (LC-Bio Technology Co., Ltd., Hangzhou, China) in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocols. Raw sequencing data were processed and aligned to the mouse reference genome using standard bioinformatic pipelines. Differential gene expression analysis between experimental groups was performed using DESeq2, and edgeR was applied where appropriate. Genes with a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 and an absolute fold change ≥ 2 were considered differentially expressed. Functional enrichment analyses, including Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analyses, were conducted to identify biological processes and signaling pathways associated with transcriptional alterations.

In vivo bioluminescence imaging

R26-CAG-Luc-2 A-EGFP mice (Cat. No. NM-KI-00087) were purchased from Shanghai Model Organisms Center, Inc. These mice ubiquitously express firefly luciferase and were used either directly or crossed with RNF34-deficient mice to generate luciferase-expressing donor mice for endometrial tissue transplantation experiments. Luciferase-positive uterine tissues were isolated from donor mice and transplanted into recipient mice as described above. At the experimental endpoint, mice were subjected to in vivo bioluminescence imaging to assess ectopic lesion burden. Briefly, mice were intraperitoneally injected with D-luciferin (150 mg/kg body weight) and anesthetized with isoflurane. Bioluminescent signals were acquired using an IVIS Lumina imaging system (IVIS Spectrum, PerkinElmer) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Images were collected under identical acquisition settings across experimental groups. Bioluminescence intensity was quantified as total photon flux within a defined region of interest (ROI) using the Living Image software, and values were used to compare ectopic lesion burden among groups.

Macrophage depletion using clodronate liposomes

Macrophage depletion was achieved using clodronate-loaded liposomes. Briefly, clodronate liposomes (FormuMax Scientific) were administered to mice via tail vein injection at a volume of 200 µl per mouse. Control mice received an equal volume (200 µl) of control liposomes following the same injection schedule. To maintain sustained macrophage depletion throughout the experimental period, liposome injections were performed every 3 days as indicated.

Flow cytometric analysis of macrophages in ectopic lesions

Macrophage populations within ectopic endometriotic lesions were analyzed by flow cytometry. Briefly, ectopic lesions were excised from recipient mice, minced into small fragments, and enzymatically digested in RPMI-1640 containing collagenase type IV and DNase I at 37 °C with gentle agitation. After digestion, cell suspensions were filtered through a 70-µm cell strainer to remove undigested tissue, followed by centrifugation to obtain single-cell suspensions. Red blood cells were lysed using an ammonium–chloride–potassium (ACK) lysis buffer when necessary. Cells were washed with PBS supplemented with 2% fetal bovine serum and incubated with fluorophore-conjugated antibodies against CD11b, F4/80, and CD206 for staining, according to standard protocols. After staining, cells were washed and resuspended for acquisition. Data were collected on a flow cytometer and analyzed using FlowJo software. Macrophages were identified as CD11b⁺F4/80⁺ cells, and M2-like macrophages were defined based on CD206 expression within this population.

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed by GraphPad Prism 9.0. Two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test was used to compare between groups and Student’s paired t-test was used to compare within groups. Statistical analysis was expressed by mean ± SEM. P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. (*P < 0.05, n.s. = not significant).

Results

RNF34 is downregulated in endometriotic lesions and inversely correlates with SREBP1 expression

Metabolic reprogramming is increasingly recognized as a hallmark of endometriosis, yet the upstream regulatory mechanisms that coordinate lipid metabolism with disease progression remain poorly defined. Given the central role of ubiquitin-mediated protein turnover in controlling metabolic regulators, we sought to determine whether dysregulation of E3 ubiquitin ligases contributes to aberrant lipid signaling in endometriosis. To address this question, we first examined the expression of the E3 ligase RNF34 and its metabolic target SREBP1 in human endometrial tissues. Immunohistochemical (IHC) analyses were performed on normal endometrium from disease-free controls, as well as eutopic and ectopic endometrial tissues obtained from patients with endometriosis. RNF34 protein was abundantly expressed in normal and eutopic endometrium, whereas its expression was markedly reduced in ectopic lesions (Fig. 1A). In contrast, SREBP1 exhibited weak immunoreactivity in normal and eutopic tissues but was robustly upregulated in ectopic endometrium (Fig. 1A). Quantitative assessment of IHC staining intensity confirmed a significant decrease in RNF34 expression and a concomitant increase in SREBP1 levels in ectopic lesions compared with both normal and eutopic endometrial tissues (Fig. 1B). Notably, correlation analysis revealed a strong inverse relationship between RNF34 and SREBP1 expression in ectopic endometriotic tissues, indicating that loss of RNF34 is associated with enhanced SREBP1 abundance during lesion formation (Fig. 1C).

To independently validate these findings, we performed western blot analysis using protein lysates from paired eutopic and ectopic endometrial samples. Consistent with the IHC results, RNF34 protein levels were significantly reduced in ectopic tissues, whereas SREBP1 expression was markedly elevated (Fig. 1D, E). These alterations were observed across multiple patient samples, indicating that dysregulation of the RNF34-SREBP1 axis represents a recurrent molecular feature of endometriosis. Given the estrogen-dependent nature of endometriosis, we investigated whether estrogen directly regulates this axis. 17β-estradiol (0, 1, 10 nM) was dose-dependently treated with endometrial stromal cells, reducing RNF34 expression in both normal and endometriosis cells and increasing the expression of SREBP1 (Supplementary Fig. 1). These results suggest that local high estrogen signaling contributes to the downregulation of RNF34 in endometriosis. Together, these data demonstrate that RNF34 expression is selectively downregulated in ectopic endometrial lesions and is inversely correlated with SREBP1 abundance, suggesting a potential regulatory relationship between RNF34 and SREBP1 in the pathogenesis of endometriosis.

RNF34 is a novel binding partner for SREBP1

Given the prominent role of SREBP1 as a master transcriptional regulator of lipid and fatty acid synthesis, and its inverse correlation with RNF34 expression in endometriotic tissues, we next sought to determine whether RNF34 directly interacts with SREBP1. We hypothesized that RNF34 may regulate SREBP1 through physical association, thereby modulating its stability and function. To test this hypothesis, we first performed co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assays in HEK293T cells transiently expressing Flag-tagged RNF34 and HA-tagged SREBP1. Reciprocal immunoprecipitation using anti-Flag or anti-HA antibodies revealed a robust interaction between RNF34 and SREBP1 (Fig. 2A, B). To exclude potential artifacts arising from protein overexpression, we further examined endogenous protein interactions in endometrial stromal cells. Co-IP assays using ihESC cell lysates demonstrated that endogenous RNF34 efficiently co-precipitated with endogenous SREBP1, and vice versa, confirming the physiological relevance of this interaction (Fig. 2C, D). Consistent with these biochemical data, confocal immunofluorescence microscopy revealed substantial colocalization of RNF34 and SREBP1 in both the cytoplasm and nucleus of HESCs and ihESCs (Fig. 2E, F). This subcellular distribution is compatible with a functional interaction that may influence SREBP1 processing, stability, or transcriptional activity.

To further delineate the structural basis of the RNF34-SREBP1 interaction, a series of truncation mutants was generated. Mapping analyses revealed that the central and C-terminal regions of RNF34 (amino acids 121-240aa and 241-372aa) were required for binding to SREBP1 (Fig. 2G, I). Conversely, the C-terminal region of SREBP1 (amino acids 281-487aa) was necessary for interaction with RNF34 (Fig. 2H, J). Collectively, these results identify RNF34 as a previously unrecognized binding partner of SREBP1 and provide a molecular framework for understanding how RNF34 may directly regulate SREBP1-dependent lipid metabolic programs in endometriosis.

RNF34 promotes proteasomal degradation of SREBP1 through K48 and K63-linked ubiquitination

Having established that RNF34 physically associates with SREBP1, we next investigated whether RNF34 regulates SREBP1 protein abundance. In HEK293T cells, co-expression of RNF34 resulted in a marked reduction in SREBP1 protein levels, which occurred in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 3A, B). This effect was not restricted to heterologous systems, as RNF34 overexpression similarly decreased endogenous SREBP1 protein abundance in human endometrial stromal cells (HESCs and ihESCs) (Fig. 3C and Supplementary Fig. 3A). Conversely, depletion of RNF34 led to a pronounced accumulation of SREBP1 in both cell types (Fig. 3D and Supplementary Fig. 3B). The knockout efficiency of RNF34 was verified in HESCs and ihESCs using Western blotting (Supplementary Fig. 2A, B). Consistent with these observations, SREBP1 protein levels were also elevated in mouse embryonic fibroblasts (MEFs) derived from RNF34-deficient mice (Supplementary Fig. 3C), indicating that RNF34-mediated regulation of SREBP1 is conserved across cell types. Immunofluorescence analyses further corroborated these findings, revealing an inverse relationship between RNF34 and SREBP1 protein abundance at the single-cell level. RNF34 overexpression markedly reduced SREBP1 staining intensity, whereas RNF34 knockdown resulted in enhanced nuclear and cytoplasmic accumulation of SREBP1 in endometrial stromal cells (Fig. 3E, F).

To determine whether RNF34 regulates SREBP1 protein stability, we performed cycloheximide (CHX) chase assays. RNF34 overexpression significantly accelerated the decay of SREBP1 protein in HEK293T cells, indicating that RNF34 reduces SREBP1 half-life rather than affecting its synthesis (Fig. 3G). We next assessed whether this effect was mediated through the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway. Treatment with the proteasome inhibitor MG132 effectively rescued RNF34-induced SREBP1 downregulation, demonstrating that RNF34 promotes proteasome-dependent degradation of SREBP1 (Fig. 3H). Given the role of RNF34 as an E3 ubiquitin ligase, we directly examined its effect on SREBP1 ubiquitination. Overexpression of RNF34 markedly enhanced polyubiquitination of SREBP1 in endometrial stromal cells, whereas RNF34 knockdown significantly reduced SREBP1 ubiquitination (Fig. 3I, J, and Supplementary Fig. 3D, E). To define the topology of the ubiquitin chains conjugated to SREBP1, we employed ubiquitin mutants restricted to specific lysine linkages. These analyses revealed that RNF34 preferentially promotes K48 and K63-linked polyubiquitin chain formation on SREBP1, a canonical signal for proteasomal degradation (Fig. 3K).

Collectively, these data demonstrate that RNF34 functions as a bona fide E3 ubiquitin ligase for SREBP1, facilitating its K48 and K63-linked ubiquitination and subsequent proteasomal degradation. This regulatory mechanism provides a molecular explanation for the elevated SREBP1 levels and aberrant lipid metabolic reprogramming observed upon RNF34 loss in endometriosis.

RNF34 constrains the proliferation, migration, and survival of endometriotic cells in an SREBP1-dependent manner

Having established that RNF34 directly targets SREBP1 for proteasomal degradation, we next sought to determine whether this regulatory axis functionally governs the proliferative and invasive properties of endometriotic cells. To this end, RNF34 expression was either increased or silenced in human endometrial stromal cells (HESCs and ihESCs), in combination with gain-or loss-of-function manipulation of SREBP1. Efficient modulation of SREBP1 expression was confirmed at the protein level (Supplementary Fig. 2C, D). Cell proliferation assays revealed that RNF34 overexpression markedly suppressed the growth of both HESCs and ihESCs. Notably, enforced expression of SREBP1 largely abrogated the anti-proliferative effect of RNF34, restoring cell growth to near control levels (Fig. 4A). Conversely, RNF34 knockdown significantly enhanced cell proliferation, an effect that was effectively reversed upon concomitant depletion of SREBP1 (Fig. 4B). These findings indicate that RNF34-dependent control of cell proliferation is largely mediated through SREBP1. Consistent with these observations, colony formation assays demonstrated that RNF34 overexpression significantly reduced clonogenic capacity, whereas RNF34 depletion enhanced colony formation. In both contexts, genetic manipulation of SREBP1 reversed the effects of RNF34 on long-term proliferative potential (Fig. 4C, D, and Supplementary Fig. 4A, B), further supporting an essential role for SREBP1 downstream of RNF34.

We next assessed whether RNF34-SREBP1 signaling influences the migratory and invasive behavior of endometriotic cells. Wound healing assays revealed that RNF34 overexpression markedly impaired cell migration, whereas this inhibitory effect was substantially rescued by SREBP1 overexpression (Fig. 4E and Supplementary Fig. 4C). In contrast, RNF34 knockdown accelerated wound closure, an effect that was attenuated by SREBP1 silencing (Fig. 4F and Supplementary Fig. 4D). Similarly, Transwell migration and invasion assays demonstrated that RNF34 suppresses the motile and invasive capacities of endometriotic cells, and that these effects are largely dependent on SREBP1 expression (Fig. 4G-J and Supplementary Fig. 4E-H). Given that ectopic endometrial cells are exposed to metabolic and inflammatory stress within the peritoneal environment, we further examined the role of RNF34-SREBP1 signaling in cell survival under stress conditions. Flow cytometric analysis of apoptosis demonstrated that under serum starvation-induced stress, RNF34 overexpression markedly increased the proportion of apoptotic cells, whereas RNF34 knockdown significantly decreased the apoptotic level. Notably, SREBP1 overexpression or depletion reversed the anti-apoptotic effect of RNF34 knockdown and the pro-apoptotic effect of RNF34 overexpression, respectively. These results indicated that SREBP1 expression is a key mediator in RNF34-mediated apoptotic regulation (Fig. 4K, L, and Supplementary Fig. 4I, J). Collectively, these results demonstrate that RNF34 restrains the proliferative, migratory, and invasive properties of endometriotic cells and enhances their susceptibility to stress-induced apoptosis, predominantly by suppressing SREBP1. These findings establish RNF34-SREBP1 signaling as a critical determinant of the malignant-like behavior of endometriotic cells.

RNF34 limits oleic acid-enriched lipid output in endometrial stromal cells

Having established that RNF34 suppresses SREBP1 stability and constrains the proliferative and invasive behavior of endometriotic cells, we next sought to define the specific lipid outputs downstream of RNF34-SREBP1 signaling. In particular, we aimed to determine which fatty acid species exhibit the most prominent alterations upon RNF34 dysregulation. RNF34 expression was either increased or stably silenced in human endometrial stromal cells (HESCs and ihESCs), and intracellular lipid profiles were analyzed by gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS). RNF34 overexpression resulted in a coordinated reduction of multiple fatty acid species, including palmitic acid (C16:0), palmitoleic acid (C16:1), stearic acid (C18:0), and oleic acid (C18:1). Among these altered lipids, oleic acid (C18:1) consistently displayed the most pronounced decrease across both cell types (Fig. 5A, B). Conversely, RNF34 depletion led to a broad elevation of free fatty acids, with oleic acid exhibiting the largest magnitude of increase relative to other detected species (Fig. 5C, D). Consistent with these fatty acid changes, RNF34 overexpression downregulated the expression of key lipogenic gene (ACC, FASN, SCD, ACLY), while RNF34 knockdown upregulated these genes (Supplementary Fig. 5), indicating that RNF34 suppresses de novo lipogenesis at the transcriptional level.

In line with these fatty acid changes, RNF34 overexpression significantly reduced intracellular triglyceride content, whereas RNF34 knockdown markedly enhanced triglyceride accumulation in HESCs and ihESCs (Fig. 5E, F). Cellular cholesterol levels followed a similar trend, with RNF34 loss promoting cholesterol accumulation and RNF34 overexpression exerting a suppressive effect (Fig. 5G, H). Given that triglycerides represent a major storage form for monounsaturated fatty acids, these findings are consistent with an expansion of oleic acid-dominant lipid pools upon RNF34 loss. Collectively, these data indicate that RNF34 constrains lipid metabolic output in endometrial stromal cells, with oleic acid emerging as the most robustly altered fatty acid species downstream of RNF34-SREBP1 dysregulation. This quantitative prominence of oleic acid provides a mechanistic basis for its subsequent role in paracrine signaling within the endometriotic microenvironment.

Endometrial stromal cells drive M2 macrophage polarization through the oleic acid axis

To comprehensively define how endometrial stromal cells modulate macrophage transcriptional and functional states, we performed RNA sequencing on murine macrophages cultured alone or co-cultured with primary mouse endometrial stromal cells. Unsupervised clustering revealed a clear segregation between macrophages cultured in isolation and those exposed to stromal cells, indicating a profound transcriptional reprogramming driven by stromal-immune interactions. Differential gene expression analysis further confirmed extensive remodeling of the macrophage transcriptome upon co-culture. Heatmap and volcano plot analyses demonstrated robust upregulation of genes associated with alternative (M2-like) macrophage activation, concomitant with a coordinated downregulation of classical pro-inflammatory (M1-associated) gene signatures (Fig. 6A-C). Functional annotation of differentially expressed genes revealed that macrophages co-cultured with stromal cells preferentially activated biological programs linked to tissue remodeling, extracellular matrix organization, fatty acid metabolism and negative regulation of inflammatory responses, as determined by Gene Ontology and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses (Fig. 6D). These transcriptional signatures are characteristic of macrophages that adopt an immunoregulatory, pro-repair phenotype rather than a cytotoxic or pathogen-clearing state, suggesting that endometrial stromal cells actively instruct macrophage polarization toward an immune-suppressive program.

We next sought to validate these transcriptional findings at the phenotypic level using flow cytometric analysis of human macrophages. Consistent with the RNA-seq data, co-culture with ihESCs markedly increased the proportion of CD163⁺ macrophages, a canonical marker of M2-like polarization. In contrast, macrophages co-cultured with SREBP1-silenced ihESCs exhibited a significant reduction in CD163 expression, indicating that disruption of lipogenic signaling in stromal cells compromises their ability to induce the M2 macrophage program. Importantly, supplementation with exogenous oleic acid largely restored CD163 expression in this setting, rescuing M2 polarization despite reduced SREBP1 activity in stromal cells (Fig. 6E, F). These findings identify oleic acid as a key stromal-derived metabolic signal required for macrophage polarization. A similar pattern was observed when RNF34-overexpressing ihESCs were used as the co-culture partner. Enforced expression of RNF34 in stromal cells, which destabilizes SREBP1 and limits lipid synthesis, significantly attenuated macrophage M2 polarization, as reflected by reduced CD163 expression. Strikingly, this defect was again effectively reversed by oleic acid supplementation, restoring macrophage polarization to levels comparable to control co-cultures (Fig. 6G, H). These results place oleic acid downstream of the RNF34-SREBP1 axis as a critical mediator of stromal cell-driven macrophage reprogramming.

To further assess the functional consequences of macrophage polarization, we analyzed cytokine secretion profiles in the co-culture system. ELISA analyses revealed that co-culture with ihESCs significantly increased the secretion of the M2-associated cytokines IL-10 and TGF-β, while simultaneously suppressing the pro-inflammatory cytokine TNF-α. These immunoregulatory effects were markedly blunted when macrophages were co-cultured with SREBP1-silenced or RNF34-overexpressing ihESCs. Notably, oleic acid supplementation reversed these changes, restoring IL-10 and TGF-β production while suppressing TNF-α secretion (Fig. 6I-N). To explore the mechanism by which stromal cell-derived oleic acid promotes M2 polarization, given that oleic acid is a well-established endogenous ligand for the nuclear receptor PPARγ [32, 44], we treated macrophages with conditioned medium (CM) from RNF34-knockdown stromal cells in the presence or absence of the PPARγ antagonist GW9662. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 6A-D, RNF34 knockdown CM significantly decreased the proportion of CD86⁺ macrophages while increasing CD206⁺ macrophages, IL-10 secretion, and the mRNA expression of the PPARγ target gene Arg1. All these effects were largely abrogated by GW9662. Consistent with these findings, exogenous oleic acid recapitulated the M2 polarization response-decreased CD86⁺, increased CD206⁺, elevated IL-10, and upregulated Arg1-and this response was also largely inhibited by GW9662 (Supplementary Fig. 6E-H). Collectively, these results demonstrate that stromal cell-derived oleic acid, acting downstream of the RNF34-SREBP1 axis, drives M2 macrophage polarization primarily through PPARγ, establishing a mechanistic framework for metabolic-immune crosstalk in endometriosis.

M2-polarized macrophages reciprocally promote stromal cell growth and survival through oleic acid signaling

Having established that endometrial stromal cells induce macrophage M2 polarization through oleic acid-dependent signaling, we next asked whether polarized macrophages reciprocally influence stromal cell growth and survival, thereby forming a bidirectional regulatory circuit. To address this question, we employed a three-dimensional (3D) spheroid co-culture system, which more closely recapitulates the spatial organization and cell-cell interactions characteristic of ectopic endometriotic lesions. When cultured alone, spheroids formed by SREBP1-silenced or RNF34-overexpressing ihESCs were significantly smaller than control spheroids, consistent with impaired proliferative capacity and reduced structural growth. In contrast, co-culture with macrophages markedly increased spheroid size in control ihESCs, reflecting enhanced stromal cell expansion and nodular organization. These findings indicate that macrophages exert a strong pro-growth effect on endometrial stromal cells in a 3D context (Fig. 7A, B). Notably, this macrophage-mediated spheroid expansion was largely abolished when ihESCs lacked SREBP1 activity or overexpressed RNF34. Under these conditions, macrophage co-culture failed to significantly increase spheroid size, suggesting that stromal cell-intrinsic lipid metabolic status dictates their responsiveness to macrophage-derived signals. Importantly, supplementation with exogenous oleic acid restored macrophage-driven spheroid growth in both SREBP1-silenced and RNF34-overexpressing ihESCs, resulting in spheroid sizes comparable to those observed in control co-cultures. These results demonstrate that oleic acid signaling downstream of the RNF34-SREBP1 axis is required for macrophage-mediated promotion of stromal cell growth. To further evaluate whether macrophages affect other pathological features of endometriosis, we assessed the invasive ability of stromal cells through the Transwell invasion assay. When cultured alone, the invasive ability of ihESCs with SREBP1 deficiency or RNF34 overexpression was significantly lower than that of the control cells. In contrast, co-culture with macrophages significantly increased the number of invasive ihESCs (Supplementary Fig. 7A, B). Consistent with these observations is the result of cell apoptosis analysis by flow cytometry, which shows that silencing SREBP1 alone or overexpressing RNF34 significantly increased the apoptosis of stromal cells, while co-culture with macrophages significantly reduced their apoptosis rate. Importantly, exogenous oleic acid supplementation largely reversed the pro-apoptotic phenotype induced in cells where SREBP1 was silenced, or RNF34 was overexpressed and co-cultured with macrophages (Supplementary Fig. 7C, D). Collectively, these results demonstrate that M2 macrophages promote stromal cell invasion and suppress apoptosis through the RNF34-SREBP1-oleic acid axis, highlighting a dual role of macrophages in enhancing lesion aggressiveness and survival in endometriosis.

Given that ectopic endometrial cells encounter nutrient deprivation and inflammatory stress in the peritoneal environment, we next investigated whether macrophages also enhance stromal cell survival under adverse conditions. Flow cytometric analysis of apoptosis revealed that serum starvation induced substantial apoptotic cell death in both HESCs and ihESCs. Co-culture with macrophages significantly reduced apoptosis in serum starvation stromal cells, indicating that macrophages confer a survival advantage under metabolic stress. Strikingly, the addition of oleic acid can further enhance the anti-apoptotic protective effect of macrophage co-culture on HESCs, resulting in a further decrease in the proportion of apoptotic cells (Fig. 7C-F).

These findings were further corroborated by confocal live/dead cell staining. Under serum starvation conditions, macrophage co-culture markedly improved ihESCs viability, as evidenced by increased proportions of live cells and reduced cell death. In contrast, ihESCs with disrupted RNF34-SREBP1 signaling exhibited extensive cell death that was not rescued by macrophage co-culture alone. Again, oleic acid supplementation restored macrophage-mediated cytoprotection, substantially improving stromal cell viability under stress (Fig. 7G, H). Collectively, these results demonstrate that M2-polarized macrophages reciprocally promote stromal cell growth, structural expansion, and survival under metabolic stress. This mutual reinforcement critically depends on oleic acid signaling downstream of the RNF34-SREBP1 axis, establishing a feed-forward metabolic-immune circuit that supports endometriotic lesion persistence and progression.

RNF34 suppresses endometriosis progression in vivo through SREBP1-dependent metabolic-immune remodeling

To investigate whether RNF34 regulates endometriosis progression in vivo, we established a murine endometriosis model by intraperitoneal transplantation of uterine fragments derived from wild-type (WT) or RNF34-deficient (RNF34−/−) donor mice into WT recipient mice (Fig. 8A). This experimental design allowed selective assessment of the impact of RNF34 loss within donor endometrial tissue on ectopic lesion development. To quantitatively evaluate lesion burden, luciferase-reporter donor mice were generated by crossing RNF34−/− mice with R26-CAG-Luc-2 A-EGFP mice. Bioluminescence imaging performed at the experimental endpoint revealed markedly increased luminescence signals in mice receiving RNF34−/− donor tissue compared with WT→WT controls, indicating enhanced ectopic lesion burden upon RNF34 loss. Treatment with the SREBP1 inhibitor Fatostatin significantly reduced luminescence signals in both WT→WT and RNF34−/−→WT groups (Fig. 8B, C), suggesting that SREBP1 activity contributes to lesion expansion in vivo. Consistent with imaging results, gross examination of recipient mice and excised ectopic lesions revealed substantially enlarged endometriotic nodules in RNF34−/−→WT mice compared with WT→WT controls, whereas Fatostatin treatment visibly reduced lesion size in both genotypes (Fig. 8D, E). Quantitative analysis further demonstrated that RNF34 deficiency significantly increased ectopic lesion weight and volume, while pharmacological inhibition of SREBP1 markedly suppressed lesion growth and partially normalized the exaggerated phenotype observed in RNF34−/−→WT mice (Fig. 8F).

To examine molecular changes within ectopic lesions, we performed immunohistochemical analyses of RNF34, SREBP1, and the proliferation marker Ki-67. Lesions derived from RNF34−/− donor tissue exhibited reduced RNF34 expression accompanied by robust upregulation of SREBP1 and increased Ki-67 staining, indicative of enhanced proliferative activity. In contrast, Fatostatin treatment significantly attenuated SREBP1 expression and reduced Ki-67 positivity in both WT→WT and RNF34−/−→WT groups (Fig. 8G, H). Given our in vitro findings implicating macrophage polarization downstream of RNF34-SREBP1 signaling, we next assessed macrophage phenotypes within ectopic lesions. Flow cytometric analysis revealed a pronounced increase in M2-like macrophages in RNF34−/− lesions compared with WT controls. Importantly, Fatostatin treatment significantly reduced the abundance of M2 macrophages in ectopic tissues, indicating that SREBP1 activity promotes an immunosuppressive macrophage microenvironment downstream of RNF34 loss (Fig. 8I, J).

To directly evaluate the functional contribution of macrophages to lesion progression, recipient mice were treated with clodronate liposomes to deplete macrophages. In vivo bioluminescence imaging demonstrated that macrophage depletion alone significantly reduced ectopic lesion burden. Strikingly, combined macrophage depletion and SREBP1 inhibition produced the most profound suppression of lesion growth, as evidenced by the lowest luminescence signals among all treatment groups (Fig. 8K, L). Consistent with these findings, flow cytometric analyses of ectopic tissues confirmed a marked reduction in macrophage abundance following clodronate liposome treatment, either alone or in combination with Fatostatin (Fig. 8M, N). Collectively, these in vivo results demonstrate that RNF34 restrains endometriosis progression by limiting SREBP1-dependent lipid metabolic reprogramming and suppressing macrophage accumulation and polarization, thereby establishing RNF34-SREBP1-macrophage crosstalk as a central driver of metabolic-immune remodeling in endometriosis.

Discussion

In this study, we identify the ubiquitin E3 ligase RNF34 as a critical suppressor of endometriosis progression and delineate a mechanistic axis linking lipid metabolic regulation to immune remodeling within ectopic lesions. We demonstrate that RNF34 directly targets the lipogenic transcription factor SREBP1 for K48 and K63-linked ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation, thereby restraining fatty acid synthesis and lipid accumulation in endometrial stromal cells. Loss of RNF34 stabilizes SREBP1, leading to enhanced production and extracellular release of monounsaturated fatty acids, most prominently oleic acid. This altered metabolic output acts as a paracrine signal that promotes macrophage polarization toward an immunosuppressive M2-like phenotype primarily through the nuclear receptor PPARγ, which in turn reinforces stromal cell proliferation, survival under stress, and nodular growth. Furthermore, we identify estrogen as an upstream hormonal regulator of this axis, as 17β-estradiol treatment dose-dependently suppresses RNF34 and induces SREBP1 expression in endometrial stromal cells. Genetic and pharmacological in vivo models further establish that RNF34-SREBP1-dependent metabolic reprogramming cooperates with macrophage-mediated immune remodeling to drive endometriosis progression.

Aberrant lipid metabolism has increasingly been implicated in the pathogenesis of endometriosis [20]. Previous studies have reported enhanced lipogenesis, lipid droplet accumulation, and altered fatty acid profiles in ectopic lesions, supporting the notion that metabolic reprogramming accompanies lesion establishment and persistence [9]. Although SREBP1 is a central regulator of lipogenic gene expression and has been implicated in promoting proliferative and invasive phenotypes in other pathological contexts, including cancer and metabolic disease [45, 46], its functional contribution to endometriotic stromal cells has not been systematically examined. As a result, how lipogenic programs are regulated upstream in endometriosis, and whether lipid metabolic rewiring actively contributes to immune dysfunction within the lesion microenvironment, have remained unresolved questions. Our findings extend the current literature by identifying RNF34 as an upstream post-translational regulator of SREBP1 stability, thereby uncovering a previously unrecognized layer of control over lipogenic signaling in endometriosis and linking stromal lipid metabolism to immune remodeling. The observation that estrogen downregulates RNF34 provides a mechanistic link between the hormonal environment and this metabolic regulatory circuit, offering insight into why RNF34 expression is selectively reduced in ectopic lesions.

In parallel, macrophage accumulation and M2-like polarization are well-established features of endometriotic lesions [47, 48], and prior work has shown that lesion-associated macrophages promote angiogenesis, fibrosis, and immune tolerance [49–51]. Nonetheless, the stromal-derived cues that instruct macrophage polarization have remained incompletely defined. While cytokines, chemokines, and hypoxia have been proposed as contributing factors [52, 53], direct metabolic regulation of macrophage phenotype in endometriosis has received limited attention. Building on emerging concepts from cancer and metabolic disease, our study provides evidence that lipid metabolites themselves can function as instructive immune signals in endometriosis. Specifically, we show that oleic acid released from RNF34-deficient stromal cells is sufficient to restore M2 polarization and immunosuppressive cytokine secretion in macrophages, thereby linking stromal lipid metabolism to immune remodeling. Mechanistically, we identify PPARγ as the primary receptor mediating this effect, as pharmacological inhibition with GW9662 significantly attenuates M2 marker expression in macrophages. This finding aligns with the well-established role of PPARγ as a master regulator of macrophage alternative activation and fatty acid sensing [32, 54]. Consistent with this, our data further demonstrate that stromal cell-derived oleic acid acts as an endogenous PPARγ ligand in the endometriotic microenvironment. This metabolic-immune coupling represents a conceptual advance over prior models that treated metabolic reprogramming and immune dysregulation as parallel but largely independent processes.

Despite these advances, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although oleic acid emerged as the most robustly altered fatty acid species downstream of RNF34-SREBP1 dysregulation, RNF34 loss also affected other lipid classes. Thus, additional lipid species or lipid-derived mediators may contribute to macrophage programming and lesion biology. Second, while our data implicate PPARγ as a key mediator, it remains possible that additional fatty acid-sensing pathways contribute to oleic acid signaling in macrophages. Future studies exploring these pathways may provide further mechanistic insights. Third, endometriosis is a heterogeneous disease, and our models may not capture the full spectrum of lesion subtypes or hormonal contexts encountered in patients. Addressing these limitations will require complementary approaches. Comprehensive lipidomic profiling combined with functional perturbation of individual lipid species could clarify the relative contributions of distinct lipid signals. Mechanistic studies targeting fatty acid transporters, intracellular lipid chaperones, or lipid-responsive transcriptional regulators in macrophages may further elucidate how oleic acid reshapes immune programs. In addition, extending these analyses to patient-derived cells and stratified clinical samples could strengthen translational relevance.

Looking forward, our findings raise several new questions. The precise mechanism by which estrogen regulates RNF34 expression, how this axis evolves during disease progression, and whether combined metabolic-immune interventions can achieve durable disease control warrant need further investigation. More broadly, this work suggests that targeting metabolic-immune crosstalk may represent a promising strategy for endometriosis therapy.
