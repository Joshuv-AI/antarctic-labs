Age and Nocturnal Polyuria in Nocturia: A Multicenter Voiding Diary Cohort Study
Authors: Kota Kawase, Yoshinori Nishino, Kanako Matsuoka, Shunta Makabe, Ryo Tanji, Hiroki Hoshino, Kei Kawada, Kousei Miwa, Masayuki Tomioka, Tomoki Taniguchi, Koji Iinuma, Yuki Tobisawa, Keita Nakane, Yoshiyuki Kojima, Takahiko Mitsui, Takuya Koie

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionAlthough the prevalence of nocturnal polyuria (NP) increases with age, the mechanisms underlying age‐related changes in voiding patterns and the daytime–nighttime distribution of urine volume remain unclear. Given that the severity of nocturia and bladder function may differ across age groups, age‐specific voiding profiles require clarification to enable the development of appropriate management strategies. Therefore, in this multicenter cohort study, we aimed to analyze the urinary patterns and clinical factors associated with NP using a voiding diary.Materials and MethodsThis multicenter retrospective observational study included patients who presented at 17 Japanese institutions with nocturia as the primary complaint in 2018–2022. The enrolled patients documented at least 2 days of voiding diaries, and their daily voiding patterns were thoroughly analyzed. NP was defined as an NP index (NPi) exceeding 0.33 calculated from 24‐h and nocturnal urine volumes. Patients were stratified into five age groups (<50, 50–59, 60–69, 70–79, and ≥80 years) for comparative analysis. The primary study endpoint was identifying the clinical factors associated with NP.ResultsA total of 875 patients were included in the analysis. NPi levels increased with age and were significantly higher in the ≥80 years group than that in the other groups (p < 0.05). The prevalence of NP increased with age. A review of voiding diaries revealed that patients aged ≥80 years had significantly lower 24‐h and daytime urine volumes and a notably higher incidence of NP than the other age groups (p < 0.05). The patients diagnosed with NP were older and had a higher prevalence of overactive bladder (OAB) than those without NP. A multivariate analysis identified age, the presence of OAB, maximal urinary volume, and 24‐h urine volume as independent predictors of NP.ConclusionsThese findings suggest that nocturia in older adults is not exclusively attributable to impaired bladder function. This hypothesis suggests that the condition is multifactorial and involves urinary symptoms, such as OAB, changes in urine volume, and symptoms related to comorbidities.

Introduction

The International Continence Society defines nocturia as the need to wake up at least once during the night to urinate [1]. Nocturia is among the most prevalent lower urinary tract symptoms and exhibits a notable correlation with patients’ daily lives and overall health status [2, 3]. Sleep, in particular, has been identified as a critical component of an individual's physical and mental well‐being, with substantial evidence indicating its association with anxiety disorders and depression [2, 3]. Therefore, sleep fragmentation resulting from nocturia may contribute to adverse mental health outcomes [2, 3].

According to epidemiological evidence, the prevalence of nocturia increases with age; specifically, more than 35% of people aged ≥60 years encounter repeated nocturnal voids. Consequently, nocturia is widely recognized as a prevalent symptom among the older population [4]. The increased frequency of nocturia with aging is thought to be caused by multiple factors, including changes in renal function and endocrine regulation, alterations in sleep architecture, and declining lower urinary tract function [5]. In older people, frequent nighttime awakenings due to nocturia can impair sleep quality and increase the risk of falls, which can lead to serious health complications. Fractures and injuries resulting from falls are associated with an elevated risk of mortality and the need for prolonged nursing care [6, 7, 8, 9]. Given that nocturia is associated with a diminished quality of life, it warrants recognition as a significant clinical and public health concern [10].

Nocturia is a symptomatic syndrome involving multiple underlying pathophysiological mechanisms, including nocturnal polyuria (NP), impaired bladder storage function, and 24‐h total polyuria [11]. In a previous study, 67.4% of 875 patients with nocturia had NP, and its prevalence increased with age [12]. Although the prevalence of nocturia increases with age, the extent to which voiding patterns and urine production profiles differ across age groups remains poorly understood. Furthermore, the frequency of nocturia, bladder capacity, and daytime–nighttime distribution of urine volume may vary by age group, and age‐related differences in various parameters obtained from bladder diaries remain unclear. Achieving a comprehensive understanding of the age‐related disparities in various urinary factors is imperative for the development of effective and age‐appropriate treatment strategies for nocturia.

Therefore, in this observational multicenter cohort study, we aimed to analyze the urinary patterns and clinical factors associated with NP using a voiding diary.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

This retrospective multicenter study was conducted at three institutions in Japan: Gifu University, the University of Yamanashi, and Fukushima Medical University. This study was approved by the ethics committees of each participating institution (approval numbers: 2021–053, 2481, and 2021–154, respectively). The review boards waived the requirement for written informed consent due to the study's retrospective nature. The present study was publicly announced in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and patients were given the opportunity to opt out.

Patients

This multicenter retrospective cohort study focused on patients who presented with nocturia as the chief complaint at 17 medical institutions in Japan between January 2018 and December 2022. Patients with bladder stones, urinary tract infections, genitourinary malignancies or who were using desmopressin were excluded from the study. The following baseline clinical data were collected: age, sex, body mass index, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status [13], and medical history. A voiding diary kept for at least 2 days was used to assess the micturition status and urine volume. Voiding diary data were used to calculate the following parameters: the average number of recorded days, number of voids per 24 h, number of daytime versus nighttime voids, and daytime and nighttime urine volumes. Daytime and nighttime periods were defined based on the recorded bedtime and wake‐up times, respectively. The first micturition after waking was considered part of the daytime period. Nocturnal urine volume was defined as the total urine volume from bedtime to awakening. NP was assessed using the NP index (NPi), which was calculated as the ratio of nocturnal urine volume to 24‐h urine volume, and patients with an NPi > 0.33 were diagnosed with NP [14]. In the present study, the patient population was stratified into five distinct age categories: <50, 50–59, 60–69, 70–79, and ≥80 years. These categories were then subjected to a comprehensive analysis.

Statistical Analysis

The primary study endpoint was NPi, while the secondary endpoints included 24‐h, daytime, and nighttime urine volumes, and maximum voiding volume. Differences across age groups for the aforementioned endpoints were analyzed. For statistical analysis, the Kruskal–Wallis test was used followed by the Steel–Dwass multiple comparison test as a post‐hoc analysis. The patients were divided into NP and non‐NP groups (NPi > 0.33 vs. ≤0.33).

Continuous variables were evaluated using the Mann–Whitney U test, whereas categorical variables were evaluated using the chi‐square test or Fisher's exact test. A multivariate logistic regression analysis was employed to evaluate the predictive factors associated with NP in each age group, including sex, presence of benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH), presence of overactive bladder (OAB), maximum urinary flow rate, and 24‐h urine volume. The results are reported as odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI). The analysis of the presence or absence of BPH was limited to male patients. All tests were two‐tailed, and statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. All analyses were performed using JMP 19 software (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

Age‐Group Differences in NPi

The median NPi increased with age (Figure 1a). The median NPi values were 0.28 (interquartile range [IQR], 0.23–0.36) in the <50 years group, 0.34 (IQR, 0.22–0.42) in the 50–59 years group, 0.34 ((IQR, 0.26–0.46) in the 60–69 years group, 0.39 ((IQR, 0.29–0.48) in the 70–79 years group, and 0.42 ((IQR, 0.34–0.53) in the ≥ 80 years group. The Kruskal–Wallis test revealed a statistically significant difference in the NPi values across the age groups (p < 0.001). The Steel–Dwass comparison revealed that the group aged ≥ 80 years had a higher NPi than all other groups (all p < 0.05). Moreover, the 70–79 years group exhibited a higher NPi than the <50 years and 60–69 years groups (p = 0.013 and p = 0.030, respectively).

Voiding Diary‐Derived Parameters by Age

Figure 2 presents age‐group comparisons of the maximum voiding volume, 24‐h urine output, daytime urine volume, and nighttime urine volume obtained from the bladder diaries. Significant differences were observed across all age groups in maximum urinary flow rate (p < 0.001), 24‐h urine volume (p < 0.001), daytime urine volume (p < 0.001), and nighttime urine volume (p = 0.044). Regarding maximum urinary volume, the ≥ 80 years group exhibited significantly lower urinary volume than the 50–59 (p = 0.008), 60–69 (p = 0.009), and 70–79 (p = 0.006) years groups (Figure 2a). The ≥ 80 years group had significantly lower 24‐h urine volume than the 50–59 (p = 0.020), 60–69 (p < 0.001), and 70–79 (p < 0.001) years groups (Figure 2b). A statistically significant decrease in daytime urine volume was observed in the ≥ 80 years group compared with the < 50 (p = 0.012), 50–59 (p < 0.001), 60–69 (p < 0.001), and 70–79 (p < 0.001) years groups (Figure 2c). A significant increase in nighttime urine volume was observed in the ≥ 80 and 70–79 years groups compared with the < 50 years group (p = 0.031 and p = 0.039, respectively; Figure 2d).

Discussion

The present multicenter cohort study used voiding diary data to examine NP, NPi, and age‐related voiding patterns and identified three key findings. First, the prevalence of both NP and NPi increased with age, with the highest prevalence being observed in patients aged ≥80 years. Second, urinary patterns differed by age group; maximum voiding volume, 24‐h urine volume, and daytime urine volume were significantly lower in the ≥ 80 years group than those in the other four groups. Third, the multivariate analysis revealed a significant correlation between NP and age, OAB, maximum voiding volume, and 24‐h urine volume. As previously reported, the increasing NPi with advancing age indicates that nocturia and NP become prevalent urinary symptoms with increasing age [4, 15, 16]. A notable increase in the prevalence of NP among patients aged ≥80 years further substantiates the notion that aging significantly contributes to NP onset. A previous study also demonstrated a high prevalence of NP among older adults [12]. The present study hypothesized that a more comprehensive examination of urinary patterns in participants grouped by age would provide substantial evidence to support NP‐related findings in older adults.

Among the enrolled patient population, those aged ≥70 years exhibited a higher prevalence of cardiovascular, cerebrovascular, and neurological diseases. These conditions may influence drinking behaviors and urinary patterns and may be related to decreased daytime urine output in older adults as well as age‐related changes in renal and endocrine regulation [17]. The presence of these age‐related comorbidities may promote an increased nocturnal urine volume through mechanisms such as redistribution of peripheral body fluids and changes in the circadian regulation of sodium and water metabolism in the kidneys [18, 19, 20].

Because of the existence of multiple definitions of NP, we may need to consider potential biases that could lead to differing interpretations of the results [18, 21]. However, NPi is calculated based on nocturnal and 24‐h urine volumes; therefore, changes in total or daytime urine volume may influence NPi, even if nocturnal urine production remains unchanged [14, 21]. Consequently, the age‐related increase in NPi may be indicative of an increase in nocturnal urine production as well as a decrease in daytime urine volume/total urine volume attributable to comorbidities and fluid intake. This study's findings suggest that nocturnal urine volume increases with age, as patients aged ≥ 70 years had significantly higher nocturnal urine volumes than those aged < 50 years. Potential causes include disruptions to the circadian rhythm and age‐related changes in arginine vasopressin, a key hormone that regulates fluid homeostasis [19]. Sleep‐related peripheral lower limb edema may also promote nocturnal diuresis and increase nocturnal urine output as interstitial fluid returns to the vascular space, thereby increasing renal blood flow [22]. In this study, 24‐h urine volume was significantly reduced in patients aged ≥80 years. The findings of a previous study suggest that reduced fluid intake in older adults may affect NPi by decreasing the total urine volume and increasing the nocturnal urine volume [23].

In this study, the NP group was older and exhibited a higher prevalence of OAB than the non‐NP group. The multivariate analysis revealed that these factors were independent predictors of NP. Furthermore, maximal and 24‐h urine volumes were associated with NP. These findings suggest that NP in patients with nocturia is not simply caused by aging but may arise from various factors, such as lower urinary tract dysfunction and changes in diurnal variations in urine production. In contrast, the prevalence of NP and NPi increases with age. This is supported by the findings of the present study and previous reports demonstrating higher nocturnal urine volumes in older adults [4, 12, 15, 16]. Consequently, age is a significant risk factor for NP. In terms of bladder storage function, the median maximum voiding volume gradually decreased with increasing age and was significantly lower in the ≥80 years group versus other groups. Our finding of age‐related decline in bladder capacity is consistent with the results of previous studies [24]. This decline in maximum urine volume may be indicative of a reduction in functional bladder capacity in older adults, which could be a contributing factor to increased urinary frequency. These findings suggest that the management of NP in older adults should focus not only on nocturnal urine volume, but also on various other factors. The optimization of treatment for NP may be achieved through behavioral interventions, such as the adjustment of fluid intake and the improvement of daytime urination habits. Additionally, a careful assessment of urinary symptoms, including OAB and decreased functional bladder capacity, may facilitate the identification of effective treatment strategies.

This study had several limitations. First, it was a retrospective analysis of voiding diary data collected from multiple facilities, which may have introduced selection bias or heterogeneity in patient characteristics. Second, the voiding diaries used patient self‐reporting, which may have introduced potential inaccuracies. Third, we were unable to obtain sufficient information regarding the factors that could influence nocturnal urine volume, such as fluid intake, diuretic use, and sleep disorders. Fourth, although the current study examined the definition of NP as an NPi >33%, an age‐based definition has yet to be established. Fifth, the inability to evaluate renal function, including serum creatinine levels and the presence of chronic kidney disease, may have influenced the results of this study, particularly among patients in the elderly population. Finally, given that this study was cross‐sectional, we could not definitively establish a causal relationship between aging, voiding patterns, and NP.

Conclusion

Our findings suggest that nocturia in older adults cannot be attributed exclusively to impaired bladder function. This hypothesis suggests that the condition is multifactorial in nature and involves urinary symptoms such as OAB, changes in urine volume, and symptoms related to comorbidities. Therefore, nocturia in patients with NP is a mixed condition in which age‐related changes coexist with lower urinary tract dysfunction.
