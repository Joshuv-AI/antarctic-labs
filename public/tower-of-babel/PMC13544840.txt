Introduction

Wandering spleen, or lien mobilis, is an uncommon clinical condition characterized by abnormal splenic mobility due to the absence or laxity of its supporting ligaments[1,2,3]. While it is rare, accounting for <0.2% of all splenectomies[1,3], this entity carries significant morbidity, particularly when complicated by splenic torsion resulting from twisting of the splenic pedicle and subsequent vascular compromise[2,4]. The condition exhibits a bimodal age distribution, most frequently affecting women of childbearing age (attributed to hormonal effects on ligamentous integrity) and children (due to congenital ligamentous insufficiency)[1,3,5]. Its clinical presentation ranges from asymptomatic mobility to acute abdomen, often delaying diagnosis as symptoms mimic more common conditions like appendicitis or intestinal obstruction[6,7]. Diagnostic uncertainty remains a key challenge. Early recognition is critical[2,4]. While contrast-enhanced CT is the gold standard for identifying torsion and infarction[6,7], resource limitations in underserved regions often necessitate reliance on clinical suspicion and ultrasound, as demonstrated in our case. This report highlights the diagnostic and therapeutic dilemmas posed by wandering spleen in pediatric patients, emphasizing the role of clinical wisdom and ultrasound and the need for heightened clinical vigilance when evaluating acute abdominal pain. This case report has been reported in line with the SCARE checklist[8].

Case presentation

An 8-year-old girl presented to our emergency department with a 24-hour history of severe abdominal pain. The pain was sudden in onset, constant, non-colicky, and severe. Her medical history was notable for an undocumented abdominal surgery during infancy (approximately 1 month of age), evidenced only by a midline scar (Fig. 1), though no further details were available. On clinical examination, her abdomen was tense with marked guarding and rebound tenderness, along with a palpable firm mass in the right iliac fossa (Fig. 1). Vital signs were stable, except for mild tachycardia.

Laboratory studies revealed leukocytosis (WBC 18,500/μL with neutrophilia), C-reactive protein at 95 mg/L, and normal serum lactate (1.2 mmol/L).

Ultrasound demonstrated a well-defined solid mass measuring approximately 25 × 15 cm in the right iliac fossa. The mass exhibited echogenicity similar to hepatic parenchyma (liver-like) and lacked the typical reniform shape or central echogenic sinus of a kidney, or the lobulated contour of an ovarian mass. The absence of a normal spleen in the left upper quadrant on ultrasound further supported the diagnosis. Color Doppler showed some preserved perfusion (qualitative assessment). The time interval from performing the ultrasound to laparotomy was approximately 3 hours.

Due to financial constraints and limited resources, advanced imaging (CT) was not obtained. The differential diagnoses considered included acute appendicitis (most common), ovarian torsion, and intussusception. The diagnosis of wandering spleen was first raised by the radiologist performing the ultrasound, who identified an absent spleen in the left upper quadrant.

The patient was taken for an emergency laparotomy, where operative findings confirmed a 720° clockwise splenic torsion (Fig. 2). Detorsion was attempted but resulted in no color change after 10 minutes. Intraoperative viability assessment included inspection of splenic color (dark purple-black), texture (friable), absence of capsular bleeding after incision, and lack of improvement following detorsion. The spleen was dark and friable, and a splenectomy was performed. The postoperative course was uncomplicated. Oral fluids were initiated 24 hours after surgery, and the patient was discharged 24 hours later with arrangements for appropriate post-splenectomy vaccinations (pneumococcal, meningococcal, and Haemophilus influenzae type b vaccines). Discussion

Wandering spleen is a rare clinical entity, with fewer than 0.2% of splenectomies attributed to its complications[1,3]. This condition arises from embryological failure of ligamentous development or acquired laxity, as seen in connective tissue disorders or multiparity[1,3,5]. In children, the absence of firm splenic attachments permits pathological mobility, predisposing them to torsion as a life-threatening complication requiring urgent intervention[2,4].

Acquired Wandering Spleen in adults may occur because of trauma, multiparity, connective tissue disorders, and hormonal changes after pregnancy, while the pediatric-acquired form is associated with multiple conditions such as renal agenesis, immunoglobulin deficiency, infectious mononucleosis, malaria, Gaucher disease, Hodgkin’s lymphoma, and Di George syndrome. We also supposed it may occur with a history of surgery, as in our case[9].

The non-specific presentations of splenic torsion, such as acute abdominal pain, tenderness, and a palpable mass, often mimic common pediatric emergencies like appendicitis, intussusception, or ovarian torsion[6,7]. In our case, the right iliac fossa mass initially raised suspicion for appendiceal pathology. However, the absence of fever or vomiting, coupled with ultrasound findings of an ectopic spleen, redirected the diagnosis.

While contrast-enhanced CT is the gold standard for delineating vascular compromise[6,7], our experience underscores that ultrasound can be pivotal in resource-constrained settings. A critical observation in our case was the discordance between preserved Doppler signals on ultrasound and intraoperative necrosis. This likely reflects: (1) the qualitative, operator-dependent nature of Doppler assessment in an emergency setting; (2) partial torsion with intermittent venous congestion but residual arterial inflow at the time of imaging, with progression to complete infarction during the 3-hour interval to surgery; and/or (3) detection of low-velocity venous flow while arterial inflow was already compromised—a known pitfall of qualitative Doppler in splenic torsion. This discordance is a critical learning point: preserved Doppler signals do not definitively exclude splenic infarction.

In viable cases, splenopexy preserves immunologic function[2,4,10]. However, our patient’s 720° torsion resulted in complete infarction, necessitating splenectomy to avert rupture or peritonitis. In low-resource environments, intraoperative judgment becomes paramount when advanced imaging is unavailable. Post-splenectomy care, including vaccinations against encapsulated organisms (Pneumococcus, Meningococcus, H. influenzae), is critical to mitigate the risk of overwhelming post-splenectomy infection (OPSI)[2,4].

We have synthesized data from three recent large reviews and five additional individual case reports published after those reviews. Aljuba et al (2025, Palestine): This review emphasized that wandering spleen is a rare cause of acute abdomen, often misdiagnosed as appendicitis. Recent Individual Case Reports (after or not included in the latest review articles):

Five additional cases have been published recently, further reinforcing the diagnostic and therapeutic principles: Melkamu et al (2025, Ethiopia) reported a case where ultrasound and CT diagnosed a torsed wandering spleen, leading to splenectomy. They emphasized that preserving the organ relies on early detection before infarction[14].Hosoi et al (2025, Japan) presented an 18-year-old female with torsion causing intestinal obstruction, diagnosed by CT. Synthesis of Literature and Diagnostic Approach:

Collectively, these reviews and cases confirm several key points. First, while contrast-enhanced CT remains the gold standard for definitive diagnosis and surgical planning, ultrasound is a highly valuable initial tool, particularly in resource-limited settings where CT may be unavailable[11,13]. Several older reports (e.g., Alimoglu 2004, Turkey; Soleimani 2007, Germany; Ahmed 2022, Ethiopia; Hui Lian 2018, Malaysia; and a 2024 case from Italy) similarly demonstrate that diagnosis can be reliably made based on clinical suspicion and ultrasound findings alone, with CT reserved for doubtful or complex cases[9,19–22]. Second, the decision between splenopexy and splenectomy is overwhelmingly determined by intraoperative splenic viability, as seen in our case and across the literature[12,13]. Third, when infarction is present, splenectomy with appropriate post-operative vaccination is the standard of care to prevent OPSI[12,13].

Conclusion

Wandering spleen with torsion, though rare, demands urgent consideration in children presenting with acute abdominal pain and atypical masses. Recent literature confirms that, while CT is the gold standard, ultrasound-based diagnosis is both practical and reliable, especially when CT is unavailable. The choice of splenopexy versus splenectomy hinges on intraoperative viability, with splenectomy being common for infarcted spleens, followed by mandatory post-splenectomy vaccinations. This case reinforces the need for clinical suspicion, rapid imaging, and systemic preparedness for post-splenectomy care in underserved regions.
