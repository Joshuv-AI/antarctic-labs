 EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

Water intrusion in building enclosure systems can lead to material deterioration, mold growth, corrosion,
reduced service life, and costly repairs. Current water-penetration test methods, including ASTM E331, are
primarily based on visual observation of water leakage and do not provide a standardized method for
quantifying moisture that enters or accumulates within wall assemblies. This Phase 3 study builds on
previous research conducted by Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL), the Air Barrier Association of
America (ABAA), Tremco, Inc., and other industry partners to further evaluate practical methods for
detecting and quantifying moisture intrusion in wall systems.

The objective of this phase was to evaluate the performance of handheld moisture meters, capacitance-type
relative humidity (RH) sensors, and resistance-type moisture pin probes during controlled water-spray
testing of wall assemblies. Two 8 ft × 8 ft steel-stud wall specimens with exterior gypsum sheathing and
self-adhered water-resistive barriers (WRBs) were constructed with intentional holes to create known water
entry paths. A third wall assembly with no penetration was used as a control specimen to evaluate the effect
of changing laboratory conditions on sensor readings.

Each test specimen was exposed to water spray in accordance with ASTM E331. The test sequence included
60 minutes of water spray without an applied pressure differential, followed by 120 minutes of water spray
under a −300 Pa pressure differential. Sensors were installed beneath the WRB, on the interior surface of
the gypsum sheathing, and at mid-depth within the gypsum sheathing. The test walls were continuously
monitored during spraying and subsequent overnight drying. Handheld moisture-meter readings were also
collected from the interior side of the wall after the initial spray period and after completion of the pressure-
spray exposure.

The results showed that all evaluated methods were capable of detecting moisture increases associated with
water exposure. The handheld Delmhorst BD-2100 and Protimeter MMS2 moisture meters generally
produced consistent trends and identified increased moisture levels after the full water-spray and pressure
exposure. The two meters showed strong overall agreement. Both instruments also indicated that the wall
assemblies returned to generally dry conditions after overnight drying.

The embedded capacitance RH sensors provided continuous measurements of moisture conditions at the
gypsum sheathing surfaces. Sensors located beneath the WRB showed rapid RH increases during water
spraying, demonstrating their ability to detect moisture accumulation that may not be visible from the
interior side of the wall. Sensors located on the back side of the gypsum sheathing showed the strongest
responses near fully penetrating 1/8-inch holes, where water was able to pass through the WRB and gypsum
sheathing under depressurization. Sensors near partially drilled 1/4-inch holes generally showed lower
responses because these openings did not create direct through thickness pathways for water.

The resistance type moisture pin probes produced results mostly consistent with those of the capacitance
RH sensors. Pins installed near the exterior surface of the gypsum sheathing beneath the WRB showed the
largest moisture increases, particularly at locations near fully penetrating holes. Mid-depth pins showed
lower responses, indicating that moisture accumulation was concentrated near the exterior surface of the
gypsum sheathing during the test period. The moisture pin calibration was effective over the tested RH
range.

The control wall remained comparatively stable throughout testing, although some sensor readings changed
in response to laboratory humidity conditions. This finding emphasizes the importance of monitoring
ambient temperature and RH, or using a control specimen, when interpreting embedded-sensor data.

 1

Overall, the Phase 3 results confirm that no single measurement method fully characterizes water intrusion
within a wall assembly. Handheld moisture meters provide a practical approach for post-test spot
measurements, while embedded capacitance RH sensors and moisture pins provide continuous information
on the timing, location, severity, and drying behavior of moisture intrusion. Using capacitance RH sensors
and moisture pin probes together can provide greater confidence in identifying moisture conditions and
interpreting water-intrusion test results.

 1. BACKGROUND

Moisture accumulation within building envelope systems, including walls, roofs, and floors, is a widespread
and costly problem in the United States, with significant consequences for structural integrity, material
durability, indoor air quality, and occupant health [1]. Moisture-related failures can lead to premature
deterioration of building assemblies, increased maintenance and remediation costs, business interruption,
and reduced service life of buildings [1,2].

Previous studies by the University of Florida [3], the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) [2],
and the U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development (HUD) [4] have shown that a substantial
portion of residential and commercial buildings experience moisture exposure severe enough to cause
damage or durability concerns. Water intrusion into building envelope components can result in a range of
adverse conditions, including mold growth, wood decay, corrosion, and aesthetic damage.

The scale of the problem is reflected in national survey data. The 2018 American Housing Survey identified
leaks and mold as among the most frequently reported housing problems in the United States, affecting
46.7% of households [5]. In the commercial sector, a U.S. EPA survey of 100 representative public and
commercial office buildings found that approximately 43% showed evidence of current water damage,
while 85% experienced water intrusion at some point during their service life [6]. These findings underscore
the importance of accurately detecting and quantifying water intrusion in building envelopes (i.e., walls in
this study) to support informed decision-making and to mitigate or prevent moisture-related damage.

Current test methods used to evaluate water intrusion in building envelope components are generally
qualitative rather than quantitative. For example, ASTM E331, Standard Test Method for Water Penetration
of Exterior Windows, Curtain Walls, and Doors by Uniform Static Air Pressure Difference [7], requires the
user to “observe and record the points of water penetration, if any,” highlighting its reliance on qualitative
moisture detection. Although this standard was originally developed to assess the performance of
fenestration products, it is often adapted for use in evaluating other enclosure assemblies.

When applied to wall systems, however, the procedure is typically limited to identifying moisture that
becomes visually observable on the backside of the sheathing. It does not account for moisture absorbed
within the layers of the building envelope, which may still compromise the durability and long-term
performance of the assembly. A quantitative method for determining water penetration would therefore
improve the value of this type of testing and provide a better understanding of the impact of moisture
intrusion on enclosure assemblies.

Between 2018 and 2020, Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL), in collaboration with the Air Barrier
Association of America (ABAA), initiated Phase 1 of a research effort to address the challenge of detecting
moisture intrusion in wall assemblies [8]. The objective of that study was to evaluate nine different methods
for identifying water intrusion through wall systems. The test assemblies consisted of metal-framed walls
with exterior gypsum sheathing and included both self-adhered and fluid-applied air and water barriers
(AWBs). The study was not intended to compare the performance of the AWB products themselves.
Instead, fasteners were intentionally installed in ways that would promote water penetration and thereby
activate the different detection methods. Each method was assessed based on five criteria: simplicity of use,

 2

implementation cost, whether the method was quantitative or subjective, accuracy, and applicability. A
green/yellow/red rating system was used to summarize performance in each category, where green
indicated acceptable performance, yellow indicated borderline performance, and red indicated that the
method should not be pursued further at that time.

Between 2022 and 2023, ORNL conducted Phase 2 of the project in collaboration with ABAA and industry
partners to further evaluate and refine methods for detecting water intrusion in wall assemblies under
controlled laboratory conditions [9]. Building on the Phase 1 findings, this study focused on a smaller group
of sensing methods that showed the most promise: embedded moisture pin sensors, capacitance-type
sensors, electric sensor tape, and handheld moisture meters. Test panels consisted of steel-stud wall sections
with exterior gypsum sheathing and a self-adhered water-resistive barrier. Rather than evaluating the
effectiveness of the water-resistive barrier itself, the panels were intentionally configured with overdriven
fasteners, open holes, and control conditions with no penetrations to create known opportunities for water
entry and to compare how each sensing approach responded. The instrumented panels were tested in
accordance with ASTM E331 using a calibrated water spray and pressure differential, with duplicate
specimens exposed for both 2-hour and 4-hour durations. Prior to testing, representative moisture pin and
capacitance sensors were calibrated to relate sensor output to moisture conditions in the gypsum sheathing.
During testing, sensor data were collected continuously to monitor moisture uptake and post-test drying
behavior, and the panels were later examined visually and with handheld moisture meters. The Phase 2
work was intended to assess how well these methods could detect and quantify water intrusion, how sensor
location influenced the measurements, and how suitable the different approaches were for improving the
quantitative evaluation of moisture intrusion in wall systems.

This Phase 3 report presents additional research undertaken to build on the work completed in the first two
phases of this project, with further refinement of selected detection methods and consideration of how these
approaches may be applied in both field and laboratory testing standards.

 2. SCOPE AND TASKS

This project phase consisted of five tasks, which are described in the following sections.

2.1 TASK 1: REVIEW OF PREVIOUS WORK AND SELECTION OF SENSORS AND
 SYSTEMS TO BE EVALUATED

In collaboration with staff from ABAA and Tremco, Inc., ORNL helped develop the experimental plan,
following an approach like that used in the first two project phases.

The team selected three wall panel configurations for evaluation: two test walls and one control wall. Three
8 ft × 8 ft wall assemblies were constructed: two test specimens and one control specimen using wood test
bucks made from 2 × 10 lumber and metal framing. These assemblies were designated as Specimen A,
Specimen B, and Control. Specimens A and B are shown in Figures 1 and 2, respectively, and the control
specimen is shown in Figure 3.

Each panel consisted of 6-inch infill metal studs installed within the wood bucks at 18-inch on-center
spacing. This spacing was selected to avoid conflicts with sensor and hole locations. All three specimens
were finished with 5/8-inch exterior gypsum sheathing and a self-adhered air- and water-resistive barrier
(WRB) membrane applied over the sheathing. To prevent unintended moisture intrusion from the edges
during testing, the perimeter of each specimen was sealed with a self-adhered flashing membrane.

 3

Specimens A and B were designed to evaluate the effects of overdriven fasteners and removed fastener
holes on sensor response. The Control specimen served as an unpenetrated reference panel with no exterior
sheathing penetrations caused by fasteners. This panel was placed near the two test specimens before,
during, and after testing, but outside the spray zone so that it would not be exposed to water. The purpose
of the control panel was to determine whether prolonged spraying in the testing room and the resulting
changes in ambient room conditions could influence sensor readings independent of direct water exposure.

2.1.1 Sensor Layout and Instrumentation

Based on previous experimental results, the team selected the following sensors for installation in all three
specimens:

 • Capacitance-type RH sensors, installed at:

 o Between the WRB and the exterior surface of the gypsum sheathing (labeled C1 in Figures
 1–3)

 o On the backside of the sheathing surface (labeled C2 in Figures 1–3)

 o In the air space at the backside/interior face of the sheathing (labeled RH in Figures 1–3)

 • Thermistor-type temperature sensors, installed at:

 o The air space at the backside/interior face of the sheathing (labeled T in Figures 1–3)

 • Moisture pin probes, embedded in the gypsum sheathing:

 o Inserted from the interior side to measure moisture content near the exterior side of the
 gypsum sheathing (labeled P1 in Figures 1–3)

 o Inserted from the interior side to measure moisture content at mid-thickness of the gypsum
 sheathing (labeled P2 in Figures 1–3)

Holes were drilled through the WRB in each specimen at two diameters (1/8 and 1/4 inch) and at different
depths, as illustrated in Figures 1 and 4. Figures 4a and 4b also provide side-view schematics of the sensor
locations in the test and control specimens. A depth stopper was used during drilling to ensure accurate and
consistent hole depth.

For Specimen A, 1/8-inch diameter holes were drilled 1 inch above the top-row sensors and extended fully
through the WRB and gypsum board. For the bottom row, 1/4-inch holes were drilled 1 inch above the
sensors to a depth of 1/4-inch from the exterior side, penetrating the WRB and partially into the gypsum
board. The sensors in Specimen A were positioned 3 inches away from the studs and 6 inches apart from
one another.

Specimen B was instrumented similarly to Specimen A, but with fewer sensors, as shown in Figures 1 and
2. In addition, the hole configuration was reversed between the top and bottom rows. In Specimen B, the
top-row holes were 1/4-inch in diameter, located 1 inch above the sensors, and drilled 1/4-inch deep from
the exterior side through the WRB and into the gypsum board. The bottom-row holes were 1/8-inch in
diameter, located 1 inch above the sensors, and drilled fully through the WRB and gypsum board, as shown
in Figure 2. This modification was made after initial pressure-spray testing of Specimen A revealed that
water entering through the fully penetrated top-row holes dripped onto the lower RH and moisture pin

sensors from the interior side, thereby affecting the measurements. To reduce this effect, the hole
configuration was reversed in Specimen B. This design adjustment is discussed further in the Results
section. In Specimen B, sensors were installed 6 inches apart and 6 inches away from each stud.

The Control specimen was also instrumented as shown in Figure 3. Because it was not subjected to water
spray, it needed fewer sensors than the two test specimens. Figure 5 shows Specimen B during WRB
installation over the gypsum sheathing after placement of the exterior surface capacitance sensors. Figure
6 shows the capacitance-type RH sensors mounted on the interior surface of the gypsum sheathing and the
moisture pin probes embedded at mid-thickness and through-thickness of the gypsum board.

2.1.2 Data Acquisition System

The data acquisition system (DAQ) for this experimental setup was built around a Campbell Scientific
CR1000X datalogger. The CR1000X supports a wide range of sensor types and runs CRBasic programs
for onboard signal processing and multi-rate data storage. It was equipped with an external rechargeable
battery backup capable of maintaining continuous measurement and data logging for several days during
power outages, thereby preventing data loss caused by short-term power interruptions or utility failures.

To accommodate the total number of sensors installed across the two test specimens and the control
specimen, two Campbell Scientific AM16/32B relay multiplexers were used with the datalogger. Each
AM16/32B provides 32 differential or 64 single-ended input channels in either a 1-wire or 2-wire
configuration [10], enabling efficient sequential measurement of the thermistors, moisture pin probes, and
RH sensor arrays within a single scan cycle. Figure 7 shows the DAQ system used in the project.

The datalogger was programmed to perform synchronized scans at 30-second intervals, during which all
connected sensors were sequentially polled. Raw measurements were processed onboard and stored in
multiple output tables with averaging intervals of 1, 15, 60, and 1,440 minutes. Table 1 summarizes the
sensor types, quantities, measurement ranges, and accuracy specifications used in the project.

2.2 TASK 2: SENSOR CALIBRATION

A 24 in × 24 in sample of the exterior gypsum sheathing board used in the ASTM E331 [7] experiments
was retained for in-laboratory hygrothermal testing to calibrate the sensors used in this study, particularly
the moisture pin probes. Moisture pin probes do not measure moisture content directly. Rather, they
measure electrical resistance, which varies with the material’s moisture condition and defined by the
surrounding relative humidity. Therefore, laboratory testing was necessary to establish a calibration
relationship between probe output and actual moisture content. This calibration was used to determine the
coefficients and slopes needed to accurately convert moisture pin readings into moisture content values for
the gypsum sheathing board under different relative humidity conditions.

To support this effort, representative moisture pin probes and capacitance sensors were evaluated to
determine the relationship between sensor output and moisture content. The retained 24 in × 24 in gypsum
sheathing specimen from Tremco testing specimens was divided into three sets of samples for separate
laboratory measurements.

First, a 6 in × 6 in specimen was prepared for density measurement in accordance with ASTM C303 [11].
Although density is not directly required for moisture pin calibration, it is commonly reported in building
science studies because it provides useful material characterization data.

 9

Second, a 16 in × 16 in specimen was prepared for hygrothermal monitoring in an environmental chamber.
This specimen was instrumented with multiple moisture pin probes and capacitance sensors, as shown in
Figure 8. These sensors were all connected to a Campbell Scientific data logger for continuous data
collection.

Third, several small square and rectangular specimens, approximately 1 to 2 in. in size, were prepared for
sorption isotherm testing using the environmental chamber method in accordance with ASTM C1498-04a
[12], as shown in Figure 9.

After cutting, all specimens were oven-dried at 100 ⁰F for three days and then transferred to a high-precision
environmental chamber capable of maintaining relative humidity to within 1%. The specimens remained in
this chamber for one week to ensure they were fully dried before testing began.

The density of the gypsum sheathing was first measured following ASTM C303. The results indicated a
dry density of 46.2 lbs/ft3 and an as-received density (i.e., at room condition prior to drying) of 46.3 lbs/ft3.

Next, the instrumented 16 in × 16 in specimen was placed in the environmental chamber for hygrothermal
performance monitoring, as shown in Figure 10.

The smaller specimens used for sorption isotherm testing were transferred, immediately after drying, into
weighing cups (glass jars) with the lids closed so that their dry mass could be recorded. Sorption testing
was then conducted in accordance with ASTM C1498. The weighing cups were placed in the environmental
chamber, and their lids were removed and positioned beside the cups, as illustrated in Figure 10. The
specimens were weighed approximately every 24 hours. For each measurement, the cups were temporarily
closed with their lids, removed for weighing, and then returned to the chamber with the lids again placed
beside the cups, following ASTM C1498-04a procedures. This process continued until the daily mass
change was within 0.1% of the previous day’s mass for three consecutive days, indicating equilibrium had
been reached at the target relative humidity. The final equilibrium mass was then used to calculate the
equilibrium moisture content at each specified relative humidity level, as given in Equation 1. Then, the
water content was calculated for the gypsum board sheathing at 50, 70, 90, and 95 percent RH using
Equation 2. Further details on the sorption isotherm testing procedure are presented in Reference [13].

2.3 TASK 3: LABORATORY TESTING

Each test specimen was subjected to water spray on its exterior surface in accordance with ASTM E331,
Standard Test Method for Water Penetration of Exterior Windows, Curtain Walls, and Doors by Uniform
Static Air Pressure Difference, using the facilities at Tremco in Beachwood, Ohio. The Tremco Sustainable
Building Solutions Test Facility is a state-of-the-art laboratory designed for evaluating air and moisture
infiltration and exfiltration performance of building enclosure specimens.

The two test specimens (Specimen A and Specimen B) were each exposed to water spray for 60 minutes
without an applied pressure differential, followed by an additional 120 minutes of water spray under a
pressure differential of −300 Pa (depressurization), as shown in Figure 12. This pressure differential was
selected to represent a severe inward driving pressure condition (approximately 6.3 psf) and to assess the
potential for water intrusion through the exterior enclosure under wind-driven rain conditions.

The water spray system was configured in accordance with ASTM E331 to deliver water uniformly across
the exterior face of the test specimen at a rate of 5.0 U.S. gal/ft2·h. The spray rack consisted of nozzles
arranged on a uniform grid, positioned 14 in from the specimen, and adjusted to provide the specified water
flow rate while uniformly wetting the entire specimen surface and all areas vulnerable to water penetration.
The spray system was calibrated by the Tremco team prior to testing, in compliance with ASTM E331
requirements, which specify that spray systems must be calibrated at least once every six months regardless
of testing frequency.

Only one test specimen was evaluated at a time, with the control specimen positioned adjacent to the active
test specimen, as shown in Figure 12. Wooden test bucks were constructed to support each individual panel
during testing. The test bucks were coated with a waterproofing material, and the test panels were sealed
into the bucks using flashing tape and waterproofing sealant to prevent unintended leakage around the
specimen perimeter. This sealing procedure was not necessary for the control specimen because it was not
subjected to water spray.

Capacitive RH sensors can detect relatively small moisture incursions because they respond to changes in

2.5.3 Task 5c: Moisture Pin Sensor Data

Moisture conditions within the gypsum sheathing were monitored using resistance-type moisture pins
installed from the interior side of the wall assemblies. The moisture pins measure the electrical resistance
between a pair of electrodes that are inserted into the exterior sheathing. Stainless steel nails are used as
electrodes. To electrically isolate the pins, they are coated with an epoxy paint leaving just the 0.25-inch
ends exposed. They are physically driven into the exterior sheathing from the interior side and measure the
moisture content of the exterior sheathing at mid-thickness. The moisture probes were used to measure the
electric resistance around each penetration. Location P1 represents pins driven through the gypsum
sheathing to the surface beneath the WRB, while Location P2 represents pins driven approximately halfway
through the gypsum sheathing thickness. The moisture pins were installed approximately 1 inch below the
holes in Specimens A and B to monitor moisture ingress around that specific penetration. The Control Wall
contained no holes however; pins were installed at corresponding locations to provide a comparison under
the same ambient conditions. These sensors also required a data collection system to compile data during
the experiments. To accomplish this, a Campbell Scientific data-acquisition system was used to excite the
moisture pins, record the measurements, store the data in internal memory, and transfer the data to
spreadsheets for subsequent analysis.

Figures 23 through 25 show the moisture pin layouts for Specimen A, Specimen B, and the Control Wall,
respectively. Sensor labels identify the specimen designation (A, B, or CW), sensor location (P1 or P2),
row position (Top or Bottom), and sensor number between adjacent studs. The Control Wall contained no
openings, but its sensors were installed at corresponding locations for direct comparison. This data mimic
the results from the capacitance sensors displayed in Figures 26-29. Note that the moisture pins were
calibrated (see Section 2.2 Sensor Calibration) over the relative humidity range of 50 – 95 percent.
Therefore, reported values outside this range are extrapolated from the calibration relationship. Four of the
26 moisture pins produced extrapolated values greater than 100% RH during the spray with pressure period,

 26

with a maximum value of approximately 107%. Because RH values above 100% are not physically
meaningful under equilibrium conditions, these results were reported as 100%, indicating a saturated
condition. If additional work is performed on this research topic, the calibration range should be extended
beyond 95 percent relative humidity to include data in the capillary range as the moisture content changes
dramatically within this range. Moisture Content over 100% relative humidity is reported as 100% in the
results.

 3. CONCLUSION

This Phase 3 study evaluated handheld moisture meters, capacitance-type RH sensors, and resistance-type
moisture pin probes for detecting and quantifying moisture conditions associated with water intrusion in
wall assemblies tested in accordance with ASTM E331. Two test walls with intentional openings (holes) in
the WRB and gypsum sheathing were evaluated alongside an unexposed control wall. A summary of the
observations is listed below.

 1. Both handheld moisture meters identified increased moisture after the full water-spray and pressure
 exposure and returned to dry-condition readings after overnight drying. The Delmhorst BD-2100
 and Protimeter MMS2 showed strong overall agreement, with correlation coefficients of R2 = 0.89
 for Specimen A and R2 = 0.88 for Specimen B. These meters are practical for rapid post-test
 assessment, although readings should be taken at consistent locations and depths for better data
 reproducibility.

 2. The capacitance RH sensors and moisture pins successfully tracked moisture accumulation during
 spraying and drying after testing. Sensors located beneath the WRB showed rapid increases in
 moisture during water exposure, while RH sensors on the interior side and moisture pins at mid-
 depth of the gypsum sheathing provided evidence of moisture moving through the assembly. The
 strongest responses occurred near the fully penetrating 1/8-inch holes, where water could pass
 through the WRB and gypsum sheathing resulting in higher moisture. Sensors near the partially
 drilled 1/4-inch holes generally showed lower responses to moisture.

 30

 3. The control wall remained relatively stable, although some changes in sensors reading occurred
 because of changes in room humidity. This demonstrates the value of including a control specimen
 or monitoring ambient conditions during testing.

The results show that no single method provides a complete assessment of water intrusion. Handheld meters
are useful for post-test measurements, while embedded capacitance sensors and moisture pins provide
continuous information on the timing, location, and drying of moisture intrusion. Using both capacitance
RH sensors and moisture pins together is recommended to improve confidence in the results and support
more reliable data interpretation.
