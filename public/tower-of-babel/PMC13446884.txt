Pathological Classification and Clinical Characteristics of Growth Hormone-Secreting PitNETs
Authors: Zhenwei Li, Yinzi Wu, Yike Gao, Ming Feng, Kan Deng, Bing Xing, Wei Lian, Yong Yao, Renzhi Wang, Jian Sun, Xinjie Bao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAcromegaly/gigantism is a rare disease primarily caused by growth hormone (GH)-secreting pituitary neuroendocrine tumors (PitNETs). With the update in the WHO 5th edition classification of pituitary tumors, particularly the introduction of the novel PIT1/SF1 co-expressing tumor subtype, our understanding of these neoplasms has significantly advanced. However, systematic pathological classification and clinical characterization studies of GH-secreting PitNETs remain relatively scarce.MethodsA retrospective study was conducted on 143 patients with acromegaly/gigantism who underwent surgical treatment at Peking Union Medical College Hospital between June 2022 and December 2024. Tumor specimens were re-evaluated for pathological subtyping. Demographic data, radiological characteristics, hormone profiles, immunohistochemical findings, and clinical outcomes were collected and compared among subtypes.ResultsForty-five cases (32%) were classified as pure GH-secreting tumors, while the remainder showed co-expression of other hormones and transcription factors. Among PIT1-lineage tumors, sparsely granulated somatotroph tumors (SGSTs) demonstrated greater cavernous sinus invasion, larger maximal tumor diameter, lower GH secretion index, and immunostaining intensity, and reduced SSTR2 expression, suggesting a more aggressive biological behavior. Co-expression of prolactin increased the risk of hyperprolactinemia (OR = 2.843), though only 23.7% of mammosomatotroph tumors and 32.6% of mixed somatotroph-lactotroph tumors presented with hyperprolactinemia. Additionally, 19 PIT1/SF1 co-expressing tumors were identified, showing diverse hormonal profiles and significantly higher cavernous sinus invasion compared to PIT1-lineage tumors.ConclusionGH-secreting PitNETs exhibit marked heterogeneity in pathological subtypes. Current classification systems require clearer cutoff criteria to improve diagnostic consistency. SGSTs are associated with a more invasive phenotype, warranting close clinical monitoring and long-term follow-up. PIT1/SF1 co-expressing tumors represent a distinct and heterogeneous entity that should be considered in future classification frameworks.

Introduction

Pituitary neuroendocrine tumors (PitNETs), also referred to as pituitary adenomas, are the second most common type of primary intracranial tumors, accounting for approximately 15%–20% of all intracranial neoplasms [1]. These tumors may lead to excessive hormone secretion, resulting in corresponding endocrine syndromes. Additionally, they may cause visual field defects, neurological dysfunction, and hypopituitarism due to mass effect. Although the term PitNET has been adopted in the 5th WHO classification, the nomenclature remains controversial. Some experts have argued that the term “pituitary adenoma” should be retained because most of these tumors are biologically benign and clinically distinct from neuroendocrine tumors arising in other organs [2, 3]. PitNETs are believed to originate from adenohypophyseal cells and exhibit monoclonal proliferation. The 5th edition of the World Health Organization (WHO) Classification of Endocrine and Neuroendocrine Tumors (2022) recommends a more precise categorization of PitNETs based on transcription factor expression, hormone secretion profiles, and other biomarkers such as cytokeratin expression [3]. This heterogeneous group of tumors is thought to derive from six types of anterior pituitary cells or their progenitors and is broadly classified based on the expression of three lineage-specific transcription factors: PIT1 (pituitary-specific POU-class homeodomain transcription factor), TPIT (T-box family member TBX19), and SF1 (steroidogenic factor 1). Among these, PIT1-lineage tumors are the most heterogeneous and complex in classification. They typically secrete one or more of the following hormones: growth hormone (GH), PRL, or thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH), potentially causing conditions such as acromegaly/gigantism, hyperprolactinemia, and central hyperthyroidism [4]. TPIT-lineage tumors usually secrete adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH), leading to Cushing’s disease. SF1-lineage tumors generally secrete luteinizing hormone (LH) and/or follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), but most do not present with distinct clinical symptoms and are often referred to as “silent PitNETs” [5, 6]. Silent behavior may also be observed in other lineages. Some PitNETs express no transcription factors above and are classified as “null cell tumor,” while others co-express multiple transcription factors and secrete hormones from more than one lineage, referred to as “plurihormonal tumors” [7].

Acromegaly/gigantism is typically caused by GH-secreting PitNETs, which produce excessive amounts of GH. The chronic elevation of GH and its downstream effector insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1) results in the characteristic clinical manifestations of acromegaly and can lead to multi-system complications affecting the respiratory, cardiovascular, musculoskeletal, and nervous systems, thereby severely impairing patients’ physical and mental health [8, 9]. According to the 5th edition WHO classification, GH-secreting PitNETs can be subdivided into eight pathological subtypes based on differences in cell morphology, transcription factors, co-expressed hormones, and other biomarkers such as low molecular weight cytokeratins [10, 11]:1.Densely granulated somatotroph tumor (DGST)2.Sparsely granulated somatotroph tumor (SGST)3.Mammosomatotroph tumor (MST)4.Mature plurihormonal PIT1-lineage tumor5.Immature PIT1-lineage tumor6.Acidophil stem cell tumor7.Mixed somatotroph and lactotroph tumor (MSLT)8.Plurihormonal tumor

According to WHO definition, “plurihormonal PitNET/adenomas are PitNETs composed of a single population of tumor cells expressing adenohypophysial hormones and/or transcription factors belonging to more than one cell lineage.” Among these, the most frequently encountered multilineage tumor type is the PIT1/SF1 co-expressing PitNET [12]. This type of tumor may exhibit either diffuse co-expression of PIT1 and SF1 across all tumor cells or a heterogeneous pattern dominated by one transcription factor with patchy expression of the other. These tumors secrete PIT1- and SF1-lineage hormones, and GH is expressed in nearly all PIT1/SF1 co-expressing tumors. Numerous case reports and a few large studies have been published on this novel tumor subtype [11, 13, 14], which may lead to future changes in the current WHO classification. Thus, in this study, we designate GH-secreting PitNETs that co-express both PIT1 and SF1 as PIT1/SF1 co-expressing tumors. In addition, as the current WHO classification relies on immunohistochemistry (IHC), it lacks standardized and authoritative criteria for determining “positive” staining, which may result in diagnostic and statistical bias. To date, large-scale clinical studies of GH-secreting PitNETs based on the 5th edition WHO criteria remain scarce. Comparative analyses specifically examining PIT1/SF1 co-expressing tumors versus other GH-secreting subtypes are also limited. More importantly, current research has not established consistent IHC staining cutoffs, undermining the comparability and reproducibility of findings across studies. To better characterize the pathological and clinical features of GH-secreting PitNETs within the context of the latest classification system – and to explore more scientific approaches to subtype classification – we conducted a retrospective analysis based on a cohort of patients with acromegaly/gigantism treated at our center.

Methods

Patient Inclusion

This study is based on a retrospective analysis using the pituitary tumor database from the Department of Neurosurgery at Peking Union Medical College Hospital (PUMCH). The study subjects were patients with acromegaly/gigantism who underwent surgical treatment at PUMCH from June 2022 to December 2024 and were postoperatively diagnosed with GH-positive PitNETs through pathology. All enrolled patients were diagnosed with acromegaly/gigantism prior to surgery based on clinical manifestations, hormone levels, and postoperative pathology results. In detail, inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Clinical manifestations: the medical records show typical signs of acromegaly or gigantism, such as acromegalic features, enlarged hands and feet, excessive sweating, rough skin, or abnormal tall growth during adolescence; (2) biochemical diagnosis: preoperative random fasting GH >2.0 ng/mL and IGF-1 exceeding 1.3 times the reference range for the corresponding gender and age. If these two criteria are not met, the oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) result should be considered, and if the GH nadir is >1.0 ng/mL, the diagnosis should still be considered valid; (3) imaging evidence: preoperative pituitary MRI (both plain and enhanced) indicating a mass lesion in the sellar region; (4) pathological diagnosis: postoperative pathology confirming GH-positive PitNETs. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) No preserved postoperative pathological results; (2) no preserved imaging data; (3) postoperative pathology does not confirm PitNETs; (4) patients who had received radiotherapy or GH analog treatment within the previous 6 months prior to surgery due to potential pituitary function changes. Finally, a total of 143 patients were included. Informed consent was obtained from all patients included in the study.

Endocrinological Evaluation

This study collected preoperative data (from the most recent measurement before surgery), postoperative day 1 data, and follow-up data from 3 to 6 months after surgery (for patients with available follow-up data) on fasting random GH, IGF-1, PRL, and the GH nadir from the oral glucose suppression test (OGTT). The measurements were performed using the SIEMENS ATELLICA system (Siemens Healthcare Diagnostics, UK) for GH and IGF-1 and the UniCel DXI 800 system (Beckman Coulter, USA) for prolactin. The criteria for result interpretation were as follows: (1) Abnormal hormone levels: hormone levels were evaluated according to the reference ranges set by the laboratory department of PUMCH. GH >2.0 ng/mL and GH nadir >1.0 ng/mL post-OGTT were considered the biochemical diagnostic criteria for acromegaly/gigantism. The reference range for IGF-1 was adjusted based on the patient’s gender and age. Hyperprolactinemia was defined as PRL >13.1 ng/mL (for males) or PRL >30.0 ng/mL (for females); (2) biochemical remission criteria: considering the pulsatile secretion of GH and the difficulty in interpreting the OGTT results, the biochemical remission standard was defined as the restoration of IGF-1 to normal levels (based on age and gender) within 3–6 months after surgery [15–17]; (3) hormone secretion index: to better study the hormonal secretion function of different tumor subtypes, the secretion index was defined as the preoperative hormone concentration divided by the tumor volume [18]. For example, the GH secretion index was calculated as follows:GHsecretingindex=pro−opGHvalueTumorvolume

Pathological Evaluation

The pathological specimens were re-evaluated and classified by two pituitary pathologists from Peking Union Medical College Hospital based on hematoxylin and eosin staining, pituitary anterior lobe hormones (GH, PRL, TSH, ACTH, LH, FSH), and transcription factor (PIT1, T-PIT, SF1, ER) immunohistochemical (IHC) staining results. The prognostic markers SSTR2 and Ki-67 were also recorded. The interpretation criteria were as follows: (1) To exclude residual pituitary anterior lobe cells embedded in the tumor tissue, the staining was considered positive if transcription factors or pituitary hormones were expressed in more than 5% of the tumor cells; (2) For GH, PRL, and SSTR2 staining, the intensity of staining was recorded as weak positive (1+), moderate positive (2+), or strong positive (3+), and the percentage of tumor cells with positive staining (0%–100%) was noted. The H-score was calculated as the sum of the percentage of positively stained tumor cells multiplied by the corresponding staining intensity (0–3), yielding a final score ranging from 0 to 300. (3) CAM5.2 immunostaining was evaluated to assist granulation pattern classification. If positive, the staining pattern was interpreted as “perinuclear pattern” or “fibrous bodies pattern” (fibrous bodies positive in >70% of cells). If both perinuclear and fibrous body pattern were present, the interpretation was classified as “perinuclear pattern” [19]; (4) when significant regional staining heterogeneity was observed, normal pituitary tissue should be excluded by combining hematoxylin and eosin staining and other pituitary hormone staining.

Radiological Evaluation

All patients underwent preoperative pituitary MRI with both plain and enhanced scans at our hospital’s radiology department. The maximum tumor diameter was reported as the largest value among the anteroposterior diameter, lateral diameter, and vertical diameter. To obtain a more accurate measurement of tumor burden, tumor volume was calculated based on manual segmentation of the tumor on preoperative MRI using ITK-SNAP software (version 4.4.0). Tumor invasiveness assessment: Knosp grade 0–2 was defined as no cavernous sinus invasion on imaging, and Knosp grade 3–4 was defined as cavernous sinus invasion on imaging [20, 21]. All pituitary MRI results were jointly assessed by radiologists and neurosurgeons.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses and plotting for this study were performed using GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad Software, USA), with the statistical significance threshold set at α = 0.05 (two-sided). Based on the type of variables and comparison requirements, the following analytical strategies were employed:

Analysis of Continuous Variables

For continuous variables, normality and homogeneity of variance were tested. The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to assess normality, and Levene’s test was used to assess the homogeneity of variance. If the continuous variable followed a normal distribution, the data were described by mean ± standard deviation. If the continuous variable did not follow a normal distribution, data were described by M (P25, P75).

Comparison between two groups: if the data followed a normal distribution with homogeneous variances, an independent t test was used; otherwise, the Mann-Whitney U test was applied.

Comparison among multiple groups: if the data followed a normal distribution with homogeneous variances, ANOVA was used to analyze differences among multiple groups. If significant differences were found, Bonferroni-corrected multiple comparisons were used for pairwise comparisons. If the data did not follow a normal distribution or homogeneous variances, the Kruskal-Wallis H test was used, and Dunn’s test (Bonferroni correction) was applied for pairwise comparisons. After Bonferroni correction, a p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Analysis of Categorical Variables

For categorical variables, comparisons between groups were performed using the chi-squared test (for all expected frequencies ≥5) or Fisher’s exact test (for any expected frequency <5). For comparisons among multiple groups, if significant differences were found, Bonferroni-corrected multiple comparisons were used for pairwise group comparisons. p value or Bonferroni-corrected p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Pathological Staining Characteristics

The tumor specimen staining slides were re-evaluated. Based on cell morphology, transcription factor, and hormone staining, we excluded normal pituitary tissue that may have been inadvertently cut during surgery and performed subtype differential diagnosis according to the 5th edition of the WHO classification (Fig. 2). Among the 143 tumor specimens included, no acidophil stem cell tumors were found (possibly because these tumors mainly cause elevated PRL levels and generally do not lead to acromegaly), so these are not discussed here. All tumors showed strong PIT1 staining. The pathological staining characteristics of each subtype are described as follows:

GH, growth hormone; PRL, prolactin; TSH, thyroid-stimulating hormone; PIT1, pituitary-specific positive transcription factor 1; SSTR2, somatostatin receptor type 2; CAM5.2, cytokeratin 8/18 marker; ER, estrogen receptor; IHC, immunohistochemistry.

DGST: a total of 20 DGST tumors were collected in this study cohort. These tumors are characterized by the exclusive secretion of GH without co-expression with other anterior pituitary hormones, and they exhibit a perinuclear staining pattern for CAM5.2. In this study, most CAM5.2 staining was purely perinuclear and strongly positive but also included coexisting fibrous body-type CAM5.2 staining or even negative staining, which was still categorized as perinuclear CAM5.2 staining. PIT1 and GH staining were generally strong and widespread, and SSTR2 expression was also usually strongly positive (Fig. 2a).

SGST: SGST is also a GH-secreting PitNET, characterized by the formation of distinctive fibrous bodies due to keratin, which causes CAM5.2 staining to show spherical intense staining (in >70% of tumor cells) [19]. This is the main difference from DGST. The GH staining intensity in SGST shows significant heterogeneity, with a wide distribution of H-score values, indicating a strong individual variation in GH secretion function. Typically, SSTR2 staining is weaker compared to DGST (Fig. 2b).

MST: according to the 5th edition of the WHO classification, these tumors are defined as “a well-differentiated PitNET composed of PIT1-lineage adenohypophysial cells with mammosomatotroph differentiation.” The characteristic feature of MST is the co-expression of PRL and GH. Typically, the expression of GH is stronger than PRL, and both hormones are expressed in the same cells, which can be differentiated from MSLT. These tumors may cause hyperprolactinemia and may show positive ER staining. Other features are similar to DGST, such as cytokeratin expression and GH secretion intensity (Fig. 2c).

MSLT: MSLT is defined as “a well-differentiated PitNET composed of PIT1-lineage adenohypophysial cells with distinct populations of somatotroph and lactotroph cells.” The main difference between this tumor and MST is that MSLT consists of two cell populations producing both GH and PRL. Literature indicates that MSLT accounts for about 6%–14% of tumors causing acromegaly [22, 23], and most of these tumors lead to hyperprolactinemia. Pathologically, it is composed of two different cell populations, which may lead to regional heterogeneity in GH and PRL staining. In cells that secrete PRL, ER expression may also occur. In this series of studies, these tumors may exhibit regional heterogeneity in markers such as CAM5.2 and ER. Notably, in our study, the co-expression of ER was also found to be more likely to occur in the regions with intense GH staining (Fig. 2d).

Mature plurihormonal PIT1-lineage tumor: this type of tumor is defined as “a well-differentiated PitNET composed of PIT1-lineage adenohypophysial cells with acidophilic cytoplasm, extensive immunoreactivity for GH, and variable immunoreactivity for TSH, prolactin, and α-subunit.” The tumor cell nuclei are uniform in size, exhibiting characteristics of a mature tumor, and other features are like MST (Fig. 2e).

Immature PIT1-lineage tumor: this type of tumor is an immature and rare tumor, previously referred to as “pituitary adenoma silent subtype 3.” It exhibits immature features such as nuclear atypia, large nucleoli, and nuclear inclusions. Typically, there is weak positive staining for GH, PRL and TSH, with some tumors expressing only one or two of these hormones and some even failing to express any PIT1-lineage hormones. These tumors have the potential for clinical invasiveness [24] (Fig. 2f).

Comparison between PIT1-Lineage GH-Secreting PitNET

Considering the uniqueness of PIT1/SF1 co-expressing tumors in the WHO classification and the heterogeneity of their expression profiles, we first conducted a group comparison among GH-secreting PIT1-lineage tumors. We performed group analysis on the four tumor types with sample sizes greater than 10 cases, namely, DGST, SGST, MST, and MSLT. The results are shown in Figure 3.

Demographic characteristics: we conducted a statistical analysis of the gender composition and age at surgery of the included patients. The results showed that there were no significant differences in gender distribution among the DGST, SGST, MST, and MSLT groups (p = 0.417 > 0.05) (Fig. 3a).

The average age at surgery for the included patients was 43.8 ± 12.0 years. The age differences between the groups were not statistically significant (p = 0.459 > 0.05) (Fig. 3b).

Imaging characteristics: all patients included in this study underwent preoperative pituitary MRI, including plain and enhanced scans, within 3 months before surgery at our hospital’s radiology department. We analyzed their preoperative imaging reports and original image data. We investigated the tumor’s cavernous sinus invasiveness (Fig. 3c). Results showed significant differences in cavernous sinus invasiveness among the four subtypes, with SGST showing the highest invasiveness (invasion rate of 44.0%), significantly higher than MST (invasion rate of 8.3%) (padj. = 0.0498).

We also analyzed the maximum tumor diameter. The maximum diameters of DGST, SGST, MST, and MSLT were 17.0 (11.0–20.3) mm, 19.0 (14.0–26.8) mm, 13.5 (8.9–17.3) mm, and 14.0 (11.9–20.0) mm, respectively. There were significant differences among the 4 groups (p = 0.0451 < 0.05), with SGST showing a significantly larger diameter than MST at the time of surgery (padj. = 0.0354 < 0.05) (Fig. 3d).

Hormone secretion: the serum levels of GH, IGF-1, and PRL (the most commonly co-expressed hormone) were analyzed at three time points: preoperative, 24 h postoperative, and 3–6 months postoperative follow-up. For pre-op GH levels, no significant differences were found among DGST, SGST, MST, and MSLT (p = 0.895), with respective medians of 13.8 (7.6–25.8) ng/mL, 9.8 (7.4–24.7) ng/mL, 12.9 (3.8–29.7) ng/mL, and 12.6 (7.6–28.4) ng/mL (Fig. 3e). Similarly, there were no significant differences in pre-op IGF-1 levels among the four groups (p = 0.385 > 0.05) (Fig. 3h).

Considering that absolute preoperative GH levels are influenced by tumor volume and may therefore reflect the overall hormonal output of the tumor rather than its intrinsic secretory capacity, we additionally calculated the GH secretion index, defined as the preoperative GH level per unit tumor volume. This index was intended to better characterize the secretory features of different tumor subtypes. Significant differences in the GH secretion index were observed among DGST, SGST, MST, and MSLT (p = 0.0149 < 0.05). Notably, SGST had a significantly lower index than MST (padj. = 0.0189 < 0.05). Although DGST showed a higher secretion index than SGST, the difference was not statistically significant (padj. = 0.580 > 0.05) (Fig. 3f). These results suggest that SGST tumors have relatively lower GH secretory function, while MST tumors may be highly functional and capable of causing acromegaly even when small.

Additionally, we analyzed PRL expression across subtypes as PRL is the hormone most frequently co-expressed with GH. Seven patients were excluded due to missing preoperative PRL data. Since DGST and SGST are usually PRL negative, we combined them into one group and compared them separately with MST and MSLT, analyzing PRL levels by gender. Among male patients, median PRL levels were DGST + SGST: 8.30 (6.0–11.4) ng/mL, MST: 11.30 (8.8–23.8) ng/mL, MSLT: 10.60 (8.1–15.5) ng/mL; no significant differences were found (p = 0.090). Among female patients, the corresponding medians were DGST + SGST: 11.4 (6.7–21.9) ng/mL, MST: 14.0 (11.0–22.4) ng/mL, MSLT: 13.6 (8.9–23.8) ng/mL, with no significant differences either (p = 0.545) (Fig. 3i).

We further analyzed the incidence of hyperprolactinemia. Although no significant difference was found among subtypes, PRL-positive tumors (MST + MSLT) showed a significantly higher incidence of hyperprolactinemia compared to PRL-negative tumors (DGST + SGST) (p = 0.047 < 0.05), indicating a significant association between PRL immunoreactivity and the occurrence of hyperprolactinemia (Fig. 3j).

Considering that serum PRL levels can also be influenced by sex, tumor diameter (via the stalk effect), and age, we included these variables as covariates in a binary logistic regression model to assess their influence on hyperprolactinemia. The analysis showed that PRL positivity significantly increased the risk (OR = 2.843, 95% CI: 1.010–8.007, p = 0.048). Male patients were also at higher risk than female (OR = 2.967, 95% CI: 1.116–7.887, p = 0.029). In contrast, tumor diameter and age did not significantly affect the risk of hyperprolactinemia (Fig. 3l).

In summary, PRL immunopositivity is an important risk factor for hyperprolactinemia. However, the actual incidence of hyperprolactinemia among PRL-positive tumors remained relatively low at only 30.8% (20/65), and the elevation in serum PRL was not pronounced.

Patients were defined as biochemically remitted if their serum IGF-1 returned to the age-adjusted normal range at the 3–6-month postoperative follow-up. Of the total cohort, 25 patients were lost to follow-up and lacked data for assessing biochemical remission. Although SGST had the lowest remission rate, the differences among the groups were not statistically significant (p = 0.86) (Fig. 3o).

Pathological features: at the pathological level, we analyzed the GH H-score to semi-quantitatively assess GH secretion in each tumor subtype. The results indicated that DGST exhibited significantly higher GH immunostaining than SGST (padj. = 0.0365), and MST also showed significantly higher GH H-scores than SGST (padj. = 0.0273) (Fig. 3g), suggesting stronger GH secretory capacity in DGST and MST compared to SGST.

For MST and MSLT subtypes, we analyzed the PRL H-score. The results showed that the PRL H-score of MST was significantly higher than that of MSLT (p = 0.0334 < 0.05) (Fig. 3k), indicating a higher level of PRL co-expression in MST than in MSLT at the pathological level. We also assessed tumor proliferative potential via Ki-67 immunostaining. A Ki-67 index >3% was defined as high proliferation, while ≤3% was considered low proliferation. No statistically significant differences in proliferation were found among the groups (p = 0.8443) (Fig. 3n).

Furthermore, we analyzed SSTR2 H-scores to predict potential responsiveness to somatostatin receptor ligands (SRLs). Among the included tumors, DGST showed significantly higher SSTR2 H-scores compared to SGST (padj. = 0.0044) (Fig. 3m), suggesting that DGST may respond better to SRL therapy, while SGST may be less sensitive to first-generation SRLs.

Comparison between PIT1/SF1 Co-Expressing Tumors and PIT1-Lineage Tumors

To investigate PIT1/SF1 co-expressing tumors, and considering their heterogeneity in hormone expression, we performed a comparative analysis with PIT1-lineage tumors as a whole. In terms of hormones co-expressed with GH, PIT1/SF1 co-expressing tumors exhibited diverse patterns. Some secreted only GH, while others co-expressed PRL (7/19, 36.8%), TSH (5/19, 26.3%), or LH (12/19, 63.2%) – hormones associated with either the PIT1 or SF1 lineage. Notably, none of the 19 cases showed FSH-positive staining (Fig. 4). Notably, among PIT1-lineage tumors, a subset also co-expressed LH (22/124, 17.7%). For these LH-positive PIT1-lineage tumors, we performed additional immunostaining for other anterior pituitary hormones. No widespread expression of ACTH or FSH was observed, suggesting that these LH-positive findings did not result from entrapped normal pituitary tissue.

Discussion

Cutoff Values: Limitations of the Current IHC-Based Classification System

According to our results, the proportions of DGST and SGST tumors in this study were relatively low, accounting for only about 32.2% combined. This is significantly lower than the proportions reported in the literature: DGST (49.25%–85%) and SGST (15%–47%) [14, 25, 26]. Among tumors that produce GH and cause acromegaly, the key feature of these two subtypes is their lack of co-expression with other pituitary hormones such as PRL and TSH – thus being “pure” somatotroph tumors. By definition, these tumors should not produce PRL. However, due to the widespread PRL co-expression observed in this study and the possibility of contamination from normal pituitary tissue, we established a staining positivity cutoff value. In fact, neither the WHO classification nor most clinical studies have defined what constitutes “positive” staining. Based on a few prior studies [5, 12, 27, 28], we adopted a 5% cutoff – i.e., more than 5% of tumor cells showing positive staining was defined as positive. Some studies, however, have used cutoff values of 10% or even 80% [29–31]. At present, there are no authoritative guidelines for cutoff values in pituitary tumor pathology reports. As a result, interpretations and tumor subtyping may vary significantly across institutions and studies depending on staining techniques and individual pathologists’ definitions of positivity. Thus, discrepancies in reported subtype proportions and conclusions drawn from subtype-based analyses in the literature may arise due to inconsistencies in interpretation [32]. Consequently, defining an appropriate cutoff value is critical for accurate diagnosis, classification, and personalized treatment of PitNETs.

Based on the difference between our DGST/SGST proportions and those in the literature, our cutoff value may have been relatively low. In addition, the proportions of MST (24/143, 17%) and MSLT (45/143, 31%) in our study were much higher than previously reported for these “rare” subtypes (MST accounts for about 7% of PIT1-lineage tumors and only 0.75% of GH-secreting tumors; MSLT accounts for 6%–14% of GH-secreting tumors). Moreover, the rate of hyperprolactinemia in PRL-positive tumors in this study was only 30.8%, lower than literature reports. Binary logistic regression analysis suggested that PRL immunostaining positivity significantly influenced the occurrence of hyperprolactinemia. Therefore, it is reasonable to infer that our cutoff value may be lower than those used in other studies. In summary, under the current IHC-based PitNET classification system, defining an appropriate cutoff value for positivity is essential.

Within the current IHC-based WHO tumor classification system, determining an appropriate cutoff value for subtyping (taking PRL as an example) requires answering a key question: does PRL expression vary continuously when used to distinguish pure somatotroph tumors from MST or MSLT subtypes? If PRL expression is continuous, then the cutoff value is artificially set, serving more to indicate the likelihood of hyperprolactinemia than to define subtypes absolutely. The WHO classification describes the characteristics of DGST, MST, and mature plurihormonal PIT1-lineage tumors as “similar.” These subtypes mainly differ in hormone expression profiles, suggesting they may exist on a biological continuum. However, we cannot rule out the possibility of fundamental biological differences between these tumors, meaning they are not merely different expressions of the same entity. In such cases, higher-dimensional data – such as electron microscopy, transcriptomics, or genomics – may be required to establish a gold standard for classification and thus determine an appropriate cutoff value. Notably, current pan-genomic classification studies have not yet clearly distinguished PRL and GH co-expressing tumors from purely GH-expressing tumors [33]. Clarifying this issue may represent the future direction of pituitary tumor subtyping.

From the perspective of tumor origin, it has been suggested that MST arises from mammosomatotrophs – precursor cells in the anterior pituitary that give rise to somatotrophs and lactotrophs. Indeed, pituitary cell plasticity has been demonstrated in many studies; for example, in late pregnancy, some GH-secreting cells in the pituitary can switch to PRL-secreting cells [34]. Although X-chromosome inactivation studies confirm the monoclonal nature of PitNETs [35], and their expression phenotype is generally considered stable, multiple reports have documented changes in tumor phenotype upon recurrence [36, 37]. In vitro studies have also shown that PitNET cells may dedifferentiate under specific stimuli, suggesting their secretory phenotype and function may be plastic and continuous [36]. In this study, 2 of 3 recurrent cases showed changes in the proportion of PRL-positive cells upon recurrence, resulting in subtype changes (e.g., SGST to MSLT, or MSLT to DGST). This suggests that the proportion of PRL expression in the same tumor may change over time, implying that PRL-based tumor classification may not be absolute.

Sparsely Granulated Somatotroph Tumors

In this cohort study, SGST tumors exhibited greater heterogeneity compared to other PIT1-lineage tumors. SGSTs were associated with a higher likelihood of cavernous sinus invasion (invasion rate 44.0%), larger tumor diameter, and lower GH secretion index and GH H-score. The reduced GH secretion index and H-score suggest insufficient secretory function, which may result in the absence of noticeable symptoms of hyperprolactinemia during the microadenoma stage. This contributes to the early-stage occult nature of SGSTs. At the time of surgery, the larger maximal diameter and increased cavernous sinus invasiveness of SGSTs raise surgical risks and treatment difficulty, aligning with previous conclusions that classify them as “highly invasive PitNETs” [3, 38, 39]. Although not statistically significant, SGSTs also demonstrated the lowest biochemical remission rate (54.5%) in this study. Therefore, long-term follow-up is recommended for SGST patients in clinical practice.

In addition, SGSTs showed lower SSTR2 H-scores (this study adopted H-score for semiquantitative analysis due to regional heterogeneity in some tumors), indicating that SGSTs may respond poorly to first-generation SRLs, such as octreotide and lanreotide. However, they may be more sensitive to second-generation SRLs like pasireotide, which should be considered when formulating clinical treatment strategies [40].

Mammosomatotroph tumor and MSLT

The key distinction between these two tumor types lies in whether a single cell simultaneously produces both GH and PRL hormones, or whether the tumor consists of two distinct cell populations [26]. In reality, without the aid of electron microscopy, it can sometimes be challenging to differentiate between the two types since MSLTs do not always display clearly identifiable dual-cell populations, and some MSLT cells may also co-express both hormones [11]. In this study, the differential diagnosis primarily relied on whether regional cellular heterogeneity was observed during pathological examination, along with CAM5.2 staining patterns (MST typically shows perinuclear-type staining). However, during interpretation, we found that markers like ER and CAM5.2 in MSLTs also exhibited regional heterogeneity. Moreover, ER expression was not consistently more prominent in PRL-rich regions; in some cases, it appeared more prominently in GH-rich areas (Fig. 2), a phenomenon that warrants further investigation. The origin of MSLTs remains unclear. A pan-genomic classification study of PitNETs suggested that MSLTs cluster closely with pure GH-producing tumors [33]. In terms of hormone secretion, no significant difference was observed between MST and MSLT regarding preoperative serum PRL levels or the incidence of hyperprolactinemia (both approximately 30%). However, in IHC staining, MSTs had significantly higher PRL H-scores than MSLTs. Although both tumors were PRL positive in staining and moderately increased the incidence of hyperprolactinemia, the biological effect of the co-expressed PRL appeared limited, failing to markedly elevate serum PRL levels. Therefore, no significant difference in preoperative PRL levels was observed between genders for these two tumor types. Consequently, the previously common clinical criterion of “PRL >100 ng/mL” to diagnose prolactinomas seems ineffective for these tumors [18].

PIT1/SF1 Co-Expressing Tumors

By definition, PIT1/SF1 co-expressing tumors simultaneously express both PIT1 and SF1 transcription factors and exhibit hormone expression from both lineages in IHC staining. Typically, they express hormones from the PIT1 lineage (GH, PRL, TSH – with GH being the most common) and from the SF1 lineage (FSH and LH), either singly or in combination. According to the 2022 WHO classification, this tumor type has not been independently categorized but is instead included under the category of “plurihormonal tumors.”

In fact, case reports of this tumor type have been documented in the literature for many years [41]. Since the clinical application of transcription factor detection has become more widespread, this type of tumor has increasingly drawn researchers’ attention. Dottermusch et al. published a study of 100 cases of pure GH-producing tumors, and upon re-staining for SF1, found that 74% of DGSTs co-expressed SF1, while no co-expression was observed in SGST. Moreover, in PIT1/SF1 co-expressing tumors, CAM5.2 staining showed significantly fewer fibrous bodies compared to DGSTs, the GNAS mutation rate was 0%, and the surgical remission rate reached 75% [13]. A meta-analysis reported that PIT1/SF1 co-expressing tumors account for approximately 1.3%–12.9% of all PitNETs. Their most common clinical presentation is acromegaly associated with elevated GH and IGF-1 levels. Compared to PIT1-lineage tumors, this subtype is more likely to invade the cavernous sinus, suggesting potentially more aggressive biological behavior [12].

In this study cohort, considering the hormonal diversity of PIT1/SF1 co-expressing tumors, we conducted a comparative analysis with PIT1-lineage tumors. The results revealed that PIT1/SF1 co-expressing tumors exhibited significantly higher cavernous sinus invasiveness, which aligns with previous literature. However, no statistically significant differences were observed between the two groups in terms of demographic characteristics, hormone secretion levels, biochemical remission rates, OGTT suppression rate, or SSTR2 expression. This may be due to insufficient statistical power caused by a discrepancy in sample sizes.

Limitations and Future Directions

In terms of methodology, the IHC slides used in this study were collected from past cases, spanning a long-time frame and processed in different batches. This raises the possibility of batch effects that could introduce bias. Additionally, preoperative PRL levels, OGTT test results, and postoperative follow-up data were missing to varying degrees, which could impact the accuracy of statistical analysis. The sample size for PIT1/SF1 co-expressing tumors was only 19, significantly smaller than the 114 cases of PIT1-lineage tumors. This sample imbalance may lead to insufficient statistical power, and related subgroup analyses should be interpreted cautiously. Being a single-center retrospective study, the research was limited by the completeness of original data (such as follow-up records). Tumor volume was estimated using a simplified method, which may introduce significant errors – especially in cases of microadenomas – thus affecting the accuracy of the secretion index. Furthermore, the study’s duration was only about 1.5 years, resulting in underrepresentation or complete absence of certain subtypes (e.g., acidophil stem cell tumors), and long-term follow-up data were lacking. The absence of acidophil stem cell tumors in this study may be attributed to their limited role in causing acromegaly. Pathological interpretations in this study were based on reference standards from previous research and lacked validation by a “gold standard.” Subjectivity in pathology assessment remains an issue, particularly in differentiating MST from MSLT. From the interpretive standpoint, the study focused purely on clinical and pathological analysis, without investigating underlying mechanisms. As a result, some conclusions may differ from existing literature and require further verification and clarification.

As discussed above, while the pathological classification of PitNETs has evolved significantly – from early classifications based on eosinophilic/basophilic staining to hormone-based classification, and more recently transcription factor-based stratification – there is still room for improvement. Some researchers have advocated for the inclusion of PIT1/SF1 co-expressing tumors in current classification systems, highlighting the significance of further study into this subtype [41]. Additionally, emerging reports of tumors co-expressing TPIT and SF1 may challenge the current classification paradigm [42, 43]. Moreover, the current IHC-based classification standards still lack a uniform definition of “positive” expression, making differential diagnosis difficult for certain tumor types. These issues need to be urgently addressed as they directly affect literature quality and the formulation of personalized clinical treatment strategies. Notably, recent research leveraging transcriptomic and methylation profiling has begun to identify clinically relevant molecular subtypes of PitNETs, providing promising directions and frameworks for developing a more accurate and integrative classification system in the future [14, 33].

Conclusion

Through retrospective analysis and re-classification of 143 GH-secreting PitNET cases from PUMCH, we observed a widespread co-expression of other PIT1-lineage hormones within these tumors. Specifically, SGST tumors exhibited features associated with high aggressiveness, including larger maximum diameters, higher rates of cavernous sinus invasion, and lower GH secretory function. Among the PRL-positive cases, 30.8% of patients presented with hyperprolactinemia. Additionally, our cohort identified 19 cases of PIT1/SF1 co-expressing tumors, characterized by variable hormonal secretion profiles and a higher tendency for cavernous sinus invasion. Future research on PIT1-lineage tumors should focus on the clinical and molecular characteristics of special subtypes such as PIT1/SF1 co-expressing tumors. Moreover, efforts should be made to further standardize IHC staining interpretation criteria to enable more accurate classification and facilitate individualized treatment strategies.
