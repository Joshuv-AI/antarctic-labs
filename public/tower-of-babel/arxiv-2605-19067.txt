Kinetic Theory of the Thermal Farley-Buneman Instability in the E-Region Ionosphere


Abstract
This paper develops a fully kinetic linear theory of the thermal Farley-Buneman instability (TFBI) in the E-region ionosphere with unmagnetized ions. The TFBI combines spatially uniform E-region plasma instabilities, such as the Farley-Buneman instability (FBI), ion thermal instability (ITI), and electron thermal instability (ETI). Similar collision-dominated plasma processes can also occur in the solar and stellar chromospheres, as well as in other planetary atmospheres. For the first time in the theory of the FBI-related processes, the kinetic description of ions includes the driving electric field, resulting in automatic inclusion of the ITI. This analytic theory has produced a comprehensive linear wave dispersion relation. It is remarkable that, similarly to the oversimplified earlier ion-kinetic studies, this much more general kinetic dispersion relation involves only elementary functions and the standard plasma dispersion function (albeit of several different arguments). This new theory is limited to plasma waves with the frequencies of the order, or larger than, the ion-neutral collision frequency. This inherently kinetic frequency range is of importance for accurate interpretation of radar signals scattered from relatively high E-region altitudes, but at altitudes where ions are unmagnetized (mostly, below 110 km).

keypoints

A fully kinetic linear theory of the thermal Farley-Buneman instability (TFBI) in the E-region ionosphere has been developed.
For the first time in the E-region instability theoretical research, the driving electric field has been included in the kinetic description of ions.
The analytical results can enable observers to more accurately interpret observations of electrojet turbulence at
frequency bands for which fluid models are inapplicable.

In the lower E-region ionosphere, mostly at the magnetic equator and high latitudes, sufficiently strong DC electric fields

\vec{E}_{0}
perpendicular to the geomagnetic field
\vec{B}
create intense

plasma instabilities. Electrojets that excite plasma turbulence are observed by sensitive radars, such as
the Jicamarca Radio Observatory (JRO, Peru) at the magnetic equator

The analysis of this paper mostly applies to these strong electric field conditions.

The electrojet instabilities occur at the lower E region and even down to the top

All these instabilities differ by their driving mechanisms: the FBI is

Starting from the pioneering works by
20

fluid approach is simpler, but the kinetic approach is more general and
accurate. The validity of the fluid equations for both electrons and ions is
restricted by the conditions of strong collisionality. Essentially, this means
that the absolute value of the cyclic wave frequency,
\omega=\omega_{r}+i\gamma

\nu_{in}

\omega_{r}

\omega(\vec{k})

relation (where
\vec{k}
is the wavevector), this wave-frequency criterion of
applicability can be re-formulated in terms of the characteristic wavelengths.
Generally, for the fluid model to be applicable, the wavelength should be much
larger than the ion-neutral mean free path (although a more accurate
wavelength criterion may also depend on the driving field strength).
Bearing in mind this rough correspondence between the temporal and spatial wave
characteristics, we will mostly refer to the highly collisional, low-frequency
(
|\omega|\ll\nu_{in}
), waves as the long-wavelength waves. A proper
theoretical description of the weakly collisional, medium-to-short
(
|\omega|\gtrsim\nu_{in}

The FBI-related collisional kinetic theory had been developed or
employed by a number of researchers

using for both ions and electrons kinetic equations

3

There are a number of reasons why theoretical description of the electrojet
instabilities may require the kinetic theory. First, this theory is the most
general approach to describing any wave plasma processes, so that without such
theory no description is complete. Second, for weakly collisional
short-wavelength waves (
|\omega|\gtrsim\nu_{in}
), such purely kinetic factor
as the collisionless Landau damping plays a crucial role for the wave
dissipation. Unfortunately, due to a significant complexity, the analytic kinetic approach can be successfully developed
mostly for the linear instability theory with limited applications. To
determine full characteristics of developed wave turbulence, one needs the
complete nonlinear theory (still looks unfeasible) or can use advanced fully
kinetic simulations

46
;
44
;
47
;
45
.

Fully kinetic PIC simulations usually include the entire kinetic physics and,
automatically, all thermal-driven processes. At present time, this approach is
the most powerful tool for modeling the electrojet instabilities, especially
in the highly nonlinear stage of instability-driven plasma turbulence for
which no comprehensive analytical description even exists. PIC simulations,
however, also have limitations: the restricted box sizes, a higher noise
level due to a limited number of particles, often the artificially increased
electron-to-ion mass ratio. Besides, typical multi-processor, highly
parallelized, PIC simulations require significant supercomputer resources and
often a long computational time.

In general, it is unfeasible to fully understand nonlinear processes without proper
understanding of the linear theory. Also, PIC simulations cannot provide
parameter dependencies and proper scaling. To obtain those, one needs
analytical theory. The main goal of the linear kinetic theory developed in
this paper is to help interpret radar signals. These signals are usually
detected during the nonlinearly saturated stage of instability. However,
applying a simplified linear theory should help understand whether the
detected radar signals were scattered from linearly stable or linearly
unstable plasma waves. Furthermore, for a given wavevector
\vec{k}

determined by the radar frequency and the line-of-sight (LoS), the accurate
linear theory will also determine the corresponding linear instability
growth/damping rates and the wave phase velocities. The latter should help
explain the observed Doppler frequency shifts. Of course, the linear theory alone
cannot predict such energy-related wave characteristics as the amplitudes of
the turbulent waves and the turbulence spectra, ion line widths, etc.
Without nonlinear theory or simulations, the linear theory remains incomplete.

In order to properly interpret the radar signals, one may or may not need the
kinetic theory. This depends upon a specific radar beam frequency and the LoS.
If the detected backscattered wavelength, which equals the half of the
transmitted radar wavelength along the given LoS, falls into the predominantly
collisional long-wavelength range,
|\omega|\ll\nu_{in}
, then one may apply
the much simpler fluid description. If the detected wavelength is within the weakly
collisional, medium-to-short-wavelength range of
|\omega|\gtrsim\nu_{in}
, then
it necessarily requires the more general kinetic theory. Furthermore, for
typical VHF radars (e.g., 50 MHz, as in the JRO and, approximately, in the
ICEBEAR), which detect
\simeq 3
m ionospheric irregularities, scattering of the radar
signal from different altitudes may require a different interpretation: one
based on a fluid model if the signal arrives from lower altitudes and another
based on the kinetic theory if the signal arrives from higher altitudes. The
reason is that the collision frequencies, proportional to the neutral
atmosphere density, exponentially decrease with increasing altitude. We may
loosely define an interface between the two regimes as
|\omega|=\nu_{in}(h)
,
where
h
is the altitude. Given the
\omega(\vec{k})
-dependence [usually,

\omega(\vec{k})\propto k\equiv|\vec{k}|
, with the proportionality coefficient
varying between the
\vec{E}_{0}\times\vec{B}
drift speed and the
ion-acoustic speed
C_{s}\simeq[(T_{e}+T_{i})/m_{i}]^{1/2}
, where

T_{e,i}
are the electron and ion temperatures and
m_{i}
is the ion mass,

53
], we may roughly relate the wave frequency

\omega
to the radar frequency
\Omega_{\mathrm{Radar}}
. Figure 1
illustrates this by plotting the corresponding loosely defined curve

h(\Omega_{\mathrm{Radar}})
. Given
\Omega_{\mathrm{Radar}}
and the
corresponding vertical line, we find the corresponding intersection

h_{0}=h(\Omega_{\mathrm{Radar}})
. For the altitudes well below
h_{0}
, one
may, to a reasonable accuracy, use a fluid model, while for altitudes

h\gtrsim h_{0}
one has to employ the more general, and hence much more
accurate results of the kinetic theory, even if they are approximate.

As mentioned above, the earlier kinetic studies did not include the driving
electric field
\vec{E}_{0}
into the ion description.
As a result, those earlier works completely ignored
any thermal processes.
12
;
13
have
explicitly included
\vec{E}_{0}
in their kinetic description of electrons
(although for ions they also used an isothermal fluid model). This had allowed those authors to predict the ETI

14
;
15
. However,
12
;
13
kinetic theory has
only been developed for sufficiently long-wavelength waves, for which a fluid
model was also applicable, albeit with less accurate results

15
;
52
;
61
;
9
.

Most probably, the main reason why no previous kinetic studies for ions explicitly included
the driving electric field in their treatment was that including
\vec{E}_{0}

in the kinetic theory creates significant mathematical difficulties. Indeed, without

\vec{E}_{0}
, the linear wave perturbations of the particle distribution
function,
f_{\omega\vec{k}}^{e,i}
, relate to the perturbations of the
electrostatic potential,
\Phi_{\omega\vec{k}}

, by a simple algebraic expression.
The resultant relation between the corresponding density perturbations
(obtained by integration of
f_{\omega\vec{k}}^{i,e}(\vec{V}_{i,e})

over the particle velocities,
\vec{V}_{i,e}
) and
\Phi_{\omega\vec{k}}
can be expressed in terms of the well-known
plasma dispersion function
Z(x)

25
. However, if
\vec{E}_{0}
is explicitly included in the kinetic equations, then instead of a
simple algebraic relation between
f_{\omega\vec{k}}^{e,i}
and

\Phi_{\omega\vec{k}}
, one obtains a much less tractable differential
equation. Under reasonably justified approximations, the ion kinetic equation
leads to a first-order differential equation, while the electron kinetic
equation results in a second-order differential equation (see below). The real
problem, however, may be not so much even with solving these differential
equations, but rather with the further integration of the solution over the
corresponding particle velocities in order to obtain the density
perturbations. For practical applications, it is of paramount importance to
have the final dispersion relation in a form of an explicit functional
expression, rather than in terms of tricky integrals.

In this paper, we have overcome these mathematical difficulties. For the first
time ever, we have included the driving electric field into the kinetic
description of ions in the E-region plasma instabilities. For electrons the driving
field had been included previously
12
;
13
,
but in this paper we have shifted that kinetic description well into the short-wavelength range. Within the
framework of this fully kinetic approach, we have derived the wave dispersion
relation that describes the combined TFBI. Some derivations (mostly for electrons) have required
certain approximations, but those are based on the actual physical
conditions in the E-region ionosphere. It is remarkable that, in spite of a
very different from the earlier studies mathematical approach, the resultant kinetic wave
dispersion relation has a form similar to those in the previous studies:
this relation includes only elementary functions combined with a few

Z
-functions of different arguments (for both ions and electrons).

The main restriction of this paper
(as well as of the earlier ion kinetic studies) is that in this paper we have not included (yet)
the finite ion magnetization. This limits our results to the conditions of
\Omega_{i}\ll\nu_{in}
,
which are largely valid at altitudes below 110 km (here
\Omega_{i}
is
the ion gyrofrequency and
\nu_{in}
is the ion-neutral collision frequency).
Usually, this lower altitude range covers most of the
turbulent E region, but some E-region plasma waves exist at higher altitudes as well.
There are questions that still cannot be answered in the framework of this limited study.
For instance, it is not clear yet how medium-to-short wavelength plasma waves behave near
the ion magnetization boundary,
\Omega_{i}=\nu_{in}
. Above the ion magnetization boundary, where
\nu_{in}<\Omega_{i}
, in the long-wavelength limit the FBI destabilizing
mechanism, i.e., the ion inertia, becomes, on the contrary, stabilizing, while
the ITI thermal mechanism extends the unstable altitude range up to a few
kilometers over the ion magnetization boundary
10
.
For the VHF and UHF radars, at those altitudes such waves are deeply within
the kinetic range, see Fig. 1, so it is important to understand the
characteristics of the shorter-wavelength waves above the magnetization
boundary. Inclusion of the finite ion magnetization in the collisional kinetic
theory creates additional mathematical difficulties, which we hope will be
overcome in the future. This would complete the full linear kinetic theory of the E-region plasma
instabilities for the entire E region.

2
Ion Contribution to the Wave Dispersion
Relation

For the ion component of the wave dispersion relation, similarly to the previous ion kinetic
studies, we will use the kinetic equation with the BGK collision operator:

\frac{\partial f_{i}}{\partial t}+\frac{q_{i}}{m_{i}}(\vec{E}+\vec{V}_{i}\times\vec{B})\cdot\frac{\partial f_{i}}{\partial\vec{V}_{i}}+\vec{V}_{i}\mathbf{\,\cdot\,}\frac{\partial f_{i}}{\partial\vec{r}}=-\;\nu_{in}\left(f_{i}-\frac{n_{i}(\vec{r},t)}{n_{0}}\ \ f_{i0}^{\mathrm{Coll}}\right).

(1)

This equation is written in the rest frame of reference for the neutral particles.
Here
f_{i}(\vec{V}_{i},\vec{r},t)
is the space (
\vec{r}
) and time (
t
)
dependent ion velocity (
\vec{V}_{i}
) distribution;
\vec{E}
and
\vec{B}

are the total electric and magnetic fields, respectively;
m_{i}
and
q_{i}

are the mean ion mass and the electric charge, respectively (in the E-region
ionosphere with the vastly predominant singly charged positive ions,
q_{i}=e
,
where
e
is the elementary charge); the ion-neutral
collision frequency,
\nu_{in}
, is assumed constant (see below). Further,
n_{i}(\vec{r},t)

is the time and space-varying ion density, and
f_{i0}^{\mathrm{Coll}}(\vec{V}_{i})
is the Maxwellian velocity distribution normalized to the
spatially uniform and constant background density of electrons and singly
charged ions,
n_{0}=n_{e0}=n_{i0}
,

f_{i0}^{\mathrm{Coll}}(\vec{V}_{i})=n_{0}\left(\frac{m_{i}}{2\pi T_{n}}\right)^{3/2}\exp\left(-\ \frac{m_{i}V_{i}^{2}}{2T_{n}}\right),

(2)

where
V_{i}=|\vec{V}_{i}|
and
T_{n}
is the equilibrium temperature of the
neutral particles (here and across the entire text, all temperatures are given in the energy
units). The two ion distribution functions are normalized according to

n_{i}(\vec{r},t)=\int\ f_{i}(\vec{V}_{i},\vec{r},t)d^{3}V_{i},\qquad n_{0}=\int\ f_{i0}^{\mathrm{Coll}}(\vec{V}_{i})d^{3}V_{i}.

(3)

The left-hand side (LHS) of equation (
1
)
represents the collisionless part of the ion kinetic equation, while the
right-hand side (RHS) is the collisional term in its simplest BGK form.

The BGK collision term
4
does not follow from the accurate dual-collision
integro-differential Boltzmann operator, but is rather an artificial construct
convenient for analytical treatment. This model automatically provides thermal
relaxation of arbitrarily unbalanced particles to their thermal equilibrium.
The BGK model also conserves the local number of particles and reasonably
well describes the balance of the average momentum and energy. In this paper,
we set the dominant neutral particles (N
2
and O
2
) and the major
ions (O
{}_{2}^{+}
and NO
+
) to have approximately the same mass,

m_{i}\approx m_{n}
. In this approximation, the BGK model even provides
accurate expressions for the mean ion frictional heating and cooling rates

16
. An important restriction of the BGK model is the
assumption of constant, i.e., velocity independent, collision frequency

\nu_{in}
. For the E-region ions, this approximation called Maxwell
molecule collisions
57
is valid if the ion
energies are well below 1 eV. A more serious problem with the BGK collision
model, however, is that it includes no angular scattering
of ions through their collisions with the neutral gas particles. In reality,
if the masses of the colliding particles are close to each other, the rates of
the collisional kinetic redistribution of both the particle energies and
velocity angles are comparable. Nevertheless, for the ion-neutral collisions
[unlike the electron-neutral collisions
12
, see below],
even the far-from-ideal BGK model is reasonably well applicable and still remains
quite popular among researchers.

In this paper, we restrict our treatment to unmagnetized ions, so that we
disregard the magnetic field
\vec{B}
, and hence the corresponding Lorentz
force in the LHS of equation (
1
). In the
Earth’s ionosphere, the conditions for such neglect are
\Omega_{i}\ll\nu_{in}
, where
\Omega_{i}=eB/m_{i}
is the ion gyrofrequency. These conditions
are reasonably well satisfied at altitudes below
110
km,

10
. Unlike the previous kinetic studies

;
39
;
56
;
50
;
54
,
here we include in the ion kinetic equation the background electric field,

\vec{E}_{0}
. This inclusion, which will automatically lead to including the
ion thermal (i.e., heating and cooling) effects for plasma waves, is the central
point of our ion-kinetic analysis.

2.1
The background ion velocity
distribution

A strong driving electric field
\vec{E}_{0}
perpendicular to the magnetic
field
\vec{B}
gives rise to plasma instabilities and, simultaneously,
it modifies the background ion distribution function (BIDF),
f_{i0}(\vec{V}_{i})
. If
the driving field is strong enough then it heats and shifts the entire ion
velocity distribution
f_{i0}(\vec{V}_{i})
in such a way that
f_{i0}(\vec{V}_{i})
can deviate significantly from
f_{0}^{\mathrm{Coll}}\left(V_{i}\right)

27
;
38
. The earlier
kinetic studies of the FBI have always assumed
f_{i0}(\vec{V}_{i})=f_{0}^{\mathrm{Coll}}\left(V_{i}\right)
. However, more appropriate
knowledge of the modified background ion distribution function is important
for the accurate instability theory.

Recently, using both an approximate analytic theory and more accurate PIC
simulations, we studied the modification of the BIDF under the action of a strong external electric field

38
. For the analytic theory, we employed
the above kinetic equation with the BGK collision
term (
1

stationary background with neglected magnetic field reduces to

\frac{e}{m_{i}}\ \vec{E}_{0}\cdot\frac{\partial f_{i0}}{\partial\vec{V}_{i}}=-\;\nu_{in}\left(f_{i0}-\ f_{i0}^{\mathrm{Coll}}\right).

(4)

From this equation, it is clear that the imposed electric field

affects the BIDF only in the

,

unknown dependence on the electric field strength. We leave
such analysis for future studies, but currently we include the temperature anisotropy
as a free parameter and will investigate in future its possible effect on the linear
instability characteristics, such as the growth/damping rates, wave phase
velocity, and the instability threshold field.

2.2
Linear wave perturbations: the ion
kinetics

To find the linear wave dispersion relation, we need to determine the wave
perturbations of the ion and electron densities in terms of a wave perturbation of the corresponding
electrostatic potential and then apply Poisson’s equation.
This will allow us to eliminate the electrostatic-potential perturbation and
obtain the unique relation for the complex wave frequency in terms of the real
wavevector. In this section, we deal only with the ion density perturbation. To
find the ion-density wave perturbation, we need to solve the corresponding
linearized kinetic equation for the wave perturbation of the ion distribution
function and integrate this perturbation over velocities.

Here we derive the kinetic relationship that relates the wave perturbations of
the ion density,
n_{i}
, to the corresponding perturbations of the
electrostatic potential,
\Phi
. In
A
,
Sec.
A.2
, we have reproduced the
corresponding no-field (
\vec{E}_{0}=0
) relationship for unmagnetized ions

39
;
56
by using two slightly different
ways, see Sec.
A.2.1
. The comparison of the two results have
produced analytic formulas which become crucial for our general
analytic treatment of ions, see equations (
114
) [or
(
123
)] and (
130
). The formulas derived provide
exact analytic values for the integral convolutions of Gaussian functions with
the error functions (or the corresponding
Z
-functions), whose complex
arguments are, in general, different, and arbitrary (provided the complex integrals converge). Popular tables of integrals
usually totally miss such formulas
17
;
35
;
26
or express the results of such integrations
in terms of more general special functions
51
with no practical value for our
treatment. Our theory allows for an arbitrarily large
external electric field
\vec{E}_{0}
, but, similarly to the previous kinetic
studies, it is still restricted to unmagnetized ions.

We start our linear theory from the full kinetic equation for totally unmagnetized
ions with the BGK collision term,

\frac{\partial f_{i}}{\partial t}+\frac{e}{m_{i}}\ \vec{E}\cdot\frac{\partial f_{i}}{\partial\vec{V}_{i}}+\vec{V}_{i}\mathbf{\,\cdot\,}\frac{\partial f_{i}}{\partial\vec{r}}=-\;\nu_{in}\left(f_{i}-\frac{n_{i}(\vec{r},t)}{n_{0}}\ f_{i0}^{\mathrm{Coll}}\right).

(7)

Using the standard approach, we will derive the relation between small linear
perturbations of the ion density and the electrostatic potential for an
isolated harmonic wave
\propto\exp[i(\vec{k}\cdot\vec{r}-\omega t)]
, where

\omega=\omega_{r}+i\gamma
and
\vec{k}
are the complex wave frequency and
the real wavenumber, respectively. The real wave frequency,
\omega_{r}
, will
always be assumed positive, while the wave growth/damping rate,
\gamma
, can be
either positive (corresponding to the linear instability) or negative (corresponding to a
stable situation when any finite initial wave perturbations will dampen and eventually
disappear). From this point onward, such small harmonic wave perturbations of
any scalar or vector quantity will be denoted by the subscript
\omega\vec{k}
.

For our further linear-wave analysis, it is useful to introduce
dimensionless expressions for the harmonic wave perturbations of the ion
particle density and the electrostatic potential,
\Phi_{\omega\vec{k}}
,

\phi_{\omega\vec{k}}\equiv\frac{e\Phi_{\omega\vec{k}}}{T_{e}},\qquad\eta_{\omega\vec{k}}^{i}\equiv\frac{\int f_{\omega\vec{k}}^{i}d^{3}V_{i}}{n_{0}},

(8)

where the electrostatic wave perturbations are normalized to the electron
temperature,
T_{e}
, for definiteness (the notation
\phi_{\omega\vec{k}}

will be used for both ions and electrons)

Now we start deriving the general kinetic relationship between
\phi_{\omega\vec{k}}
and
\eta_{\omega\vec{k}}^{i}
for the arbitrarily large
external DC electric field
\vec{E}_{0}
. Linearizing the initial kinetic
equation for ions (
1
), we obtain

(\vec{k}\cdot\vec{V}_{i}\mathbf{-}R_{i})f_{\omega\vec{k}}^{i}-i\vec{a}_{i0}\cdot\frac{\partial f_{\omega\vec{k}}^{i}}{\partial\vec{V}_{i}}=\frac{V_{Tn}^{2}}{\beta_{T}}\ \phi_{\omega\vec{k}}\,\left(\vec{k}\cdot\frac{\partial f_{i0}}{\partial\vec{V}_{i}}\right)-i\nu_{in}f_{i0}^{\mathrm{Coll}}\eta_{\omega\vec{k}}^{i},

(9)

where
\vec{a}_{i0}=e\vec{E}_{0}/m_{i}
,
\beta_{T}=T_{n}/T_{e}
,

V_{Tn}^{2}=T_{n}/m_{n}
(
m_{n}=m_{i}
),
R_{i}\equiv\omega+i\nu_{in}
, and the other
notations were introduced by equations (
2
) and
(
75
). Note that, due to the normalization of
\phi_{\omega\vec{k}}
, in purely ion equation (
9
) there
is no actual dependence on any electron parameters. There is also no actual
dependence on the neutral temperature
T_{n}
. Instead of
T_{n}
, we might
have used in
\beta_{T}
any other constant temperature (with the
corresponding change in the definition for the thermal velocity
V_{Tn}
),
e.g.,
T_{\parallel}
or
T_{\perp}
. We have picked
T_{n}
because this
temperature equals the equilibrium ion temperature in the total absence of an
external electric field.

Unlike “classical” equation (
90
) which represents a simple algebraic
relationship between
f_{\omega\vec{k}}^{i}
,
\phi_{\omega\vec{k}}
, and

\eta_{\omega\vec{k}}^{i}
, equation (
9

linear differential equation. Finding its unique solution for
f_{\omega\vec{k}}^{i}(\vec{V}_{i})
and the corresponding normalized ion density,
\eta_{\omega\vec{k}}^{i}\propto\int f_{\omega\vec{k}}^{i}d^{3}V_{i}
, is the subject
of this section with most of the mathematical details given
in
A
, see Secs.
A.3

and
A.4
.

In our treatment, we will introduce a coordinate system such that the
wavevector
\vec{k}
has only two components:
k_{x}
and
k_{y}
, where the

x
-axis is directed along
\vec{a}_{i0}=e\vec{E}_{0}/m_{i}
. The absence of
the third vector component,
k_{z}=0
, suggests switching from
f_{\omega\vec{k}}(\vec{V}_{i})
to a two-dimensional ion distribution function,

F_{\omega\vec{k}}(V_{ix},V_{iy})\equiv\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f_{\omega\vec{k}}^{i}dV_{iz}.

(10)

For this function, equation (
9

\displaystyle\left(k_{x}V_{x}+k_{y}V_{y}\mathbf{-}R_{i}\right)F_{\omega\vec{k}}-ia_{i0}\ \frac{\partial F_{\omega\vec{k}}}{\partial V_{ix}}

\displaystyle=\frac{V_{Ti}^{2}}{\beta_{T}}\ \phi_{\omega\vec{k}}\left(k_{x}\ \frac{\partial}{\partial V_{ix}}+k_{y}\ \frac{\partial}{\partial V_{iy}}\right)\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f_{i0}dV_{iz}-i\nu_{in}\eta_{\omega\vec{k}}^{i}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f_{0}^{\mathrm{Coll}}dV_{iz},

(11)

where, in accord with equations (

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f_{i0}dV_{iz}

\displaystyle=\frac{n_{0}}{2\pi V_{\parallel}V_{\perp}}\exp\left(-~\frac{(V_{ix}-U_{0})^{2}}{2V_{\parallel}^{2}}-\frac{V_{iy}^{2}}{2V_{\perp}^{2}}\right),

(12a)

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f_{0}^{\mathrm{Coll}}dV_{iz}

\displaystyle=\frac{n_{0}}{2\pi V_{Tn}^{2}}\exp\left(-~\frac{V_{ix}^{2}+V_{iy}^{2}}{2V_{Tn}^{2}}\right).

(12b)

Evidently, in the absence of the

-field, i.e., when

and

Since equation (

and the other proportional to

\displaystyle(k_{x}V_{ix}+k_{y}V_{iy}\mathbf{-}R_{i})F_{\omega\vec{k}}^{(1)}-ia_{i0}\ \frac{\partial F_{\omega\vec{k}}^{(1)}}{\partial V_{ix}}=-i\ \frac{\nu_{in}n_{0}\eta_{\omega\vec{k}}^{i}}{2\pi V_{Tn}^{2}}\ \exp\left(-\ \frac{V_{ix}^{2}+V_{iy}^{2}}{2V_{T}^{2}}\right),

\displaystyle(k_{x}V_{ix}+k_{y}V_{iy}\mathbf{-}R_{i})F_{\omega\vec{k}}^{(2)}-ia_{i0}\ \frac{\partial F_{\omega\vec{k}}^{(2)}}{\partial V_{ix}}

\displaystyle=\frac{V_{Tn}^{2}}{\beta_{T}}\ \phi_{\omega\vec{k}}\left(k_{x}\ \frac{\partial}{\partial V_{ix}}+k_{y}\ \frac{\partial}{\partial V_{iy}}\right)\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f_{i0}dV_{iz},

(13b)

where

and

\eta_{\omega\vec{k}}^{i}=\frac{1}{n_{0}}\iint_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left[F_{\omega\vec{k}}^{(1)}(V_{ix},V_{iy})+F_{\omega\vec{k}}^{(2)}(V_{ix},V_{iy})\right]dV_{ix}dV_{iy}.

(14)

Equations (

and

with respect to the

in accord with equation (

and

and

Combining equations (

and

\displaystyle\left[1-\frac{\sqrt{\pi}\ \nu_{in}}{\sqrt{2(k^{2}V_{Tn}^{2}+i\vec{k}\cdot\vec{a}_{i0})}}\ w\left(\frac{R_{i}}{\sqrt{2(k^{2}V_{\perp}^{2}+i\vec{k}\cdot\vec{a}_{i0})}}\right)\right]\eta_{\omega\vec{k}}^{i}

\displaystyle=-\ \frac{k^{2}V_{Tn}^{2}\phi_{\omega k}}{\beta_{T}(k_{\parallel}^{2}V_{\parallel}^{2}+k_{\perp}^{2}V_{\perp}^{2}+i\vec{k}\cdot\vec{a}_{i0})}

\displaystyle\times\left[1+\frac{i\sqrt{\pi}(R_{i}-\vec{k}\cdot\vec{U}_{0})}{\sqrt{2(k_{\parallel}^{2}V_{\parallel}^{2}+k_{\perp}^{2}V_{\perp}^{2}+i\vec{k}\cdot\vec{a}_{i0})}}\ w\left(\frac{R_{i}-\vec{k}\cdot\vec{U}_{0}}{\sqrt{2(k_{\parallel}^{2}V_{\parallel}^{2}+k_{\perp}^{2}V_{\perp}^{2}+i\vec{k}\cdot\vec{a}_{i0})}}\right)\right]\phi_{\omega k}.

(15)

Equation (
15
) is given in terms of the standard error
function
w(x)

1
. Using the simple

(16)

we can easily rewrite equation (

.

above the magnetization boundary of

3
Electron Contribution to the Wave Dispersion
Relation

Under the E-region conditions, the accurate kinetic description of electrons differs significantly from the
kinetic description of ions. For electrons, the simple BGK collision operator
is totally inapplicable for two major reasons: (1) unlike ions that collide with
neutrals of essentially the same mass, the mean rate of the electron energy
losses during one collision of a light electron with a heavy neutral particle
is much less than the corresponding rate of the momentum losses; and (2) the
electron-neutral (
e-n
) collision frequency is strongly
velocity-dependent,
\nu_{en}(V_{e})
, where
V_{e}\equiv|\vec{V}_{e}|
and

\vec{V}_{e}
is the velocity of an individual electron. None of these two factors can be properly described by the BGK
collisional model.

Indeed, at the E-region ionosphere, the effective rate of collisional losses
is given by
\delta_{en}\nu_{en}
, where the fraction of (predominantly
inelastic) collisional energy losses is
\delta_{en}\sim(2
–
4)\times 10^{-3}

(while for the corresponding ion fraction we have
\delta_{in}\simeq 1
)

27
. Furthermore, the
e-n
kinetic frequency in
the lower ionosphere is approximately
\nu_{en}\propto V_{e}^{2\alpha}
, where

\alpha\simeq 1
, while the
i-n
collision frequency,
\nu_{in}
, is
almost constant (the latter can be quite accurately described by the Maxwell molecule
collisions approximation, as we mentioned in Sec.
2
).
These issues have been discussed in detail by

12
;
38
, so that we will not
dwell on them here.

In this paper, we employ for electrons the kinetic approach originally
employed by
27
and later developed further by

12
and
7
. This approach assumes that due to
the strong magnetic field and intense collisional angular scattering in the
velocity space, the electron distribution function (EDF),
f_{e}(\vec{V}_{e})
, is close to an isotropic one,
f_{e}(\vec{V}_{e})\approx f_{e}(V_{e})
,
along with all its wave perturbations. We will assume that the major isotropic
part of
f_{e}(V_{e})
, at least within the thermal bulk of the electron
energy distribution, is reasonably close to a Maxwellian distribution,

f_{0}(V_{e})\approx n_{e}\left(\frac{m_{e}}{2\pi T_{e}}\right)^{3/2}\exp\left(-\ \frac{m_{e}V_{e}^{2}}{2T_{e}}\right),

(17)

where, under sufficiently strong electric field
\vec{E}_{0}
, the background
electron temperature
T_{e}
may differ dramatically from the undisturbed neutral
temperature
T_{n}
. Indeed, the space-averaged electron temperature in the
uniformly dense weakly ionized plasma is determined by the mean-energy balance
equation given by

\frac{3}{2}\ \frac{dT_{e}}{dt}\approx m_{e}\left\langle\nu_{en}\right\rangle\vec{V}_{e}^{2}-\frac{3}{2}\left\langle\delta_{en}\nu_{en}\right\rangle\left(T_{e}-T_{n}\right),

(18)

where
\vec{V}_{e}
is the background electron drift velocity and the angular
brackets denote averaging over the entire electron velocity distribution.
Under stationary conditions, assuming only the regular electron frictional
heating, we have
\vec{V}_{e}\approx

\vec{V}_{0}
, where
\vec{V}_{0}=\vec{E}_{0}\times\vec{B}/B^{2}
, so that equation (
18
)
yields

T_{e}(E_{0})\approx T_{n}+\frac{2m_{e}\left\langle\nu_{en}\right\rangle E_{0}^{2}}{3\left\langle\delta_{en}\nu_{en}\right\rangle B^{2}}.

(19)

For sufficiently strong
\vec{E}_{0}
, the contribution of the electron
frictional heating, i.e., the second term in the RHS of
Eq. (
19
) can be dominant.

Equation (
19
) represents only the minimal electron
temperature in the presence of the DC electric field
\vec{E}_{0}
. During the
nonlinearly saturated stage of the E-region instability driven by this field
there is additional heating caused by turbulent electric fields, especially
those parallel to
\vec{B}
. This effect of anomalous electron heating (AEH)
has been discussed in many papers, e.g.,

24
;
8
;
43
;
59
;
40
, and references
therein. For a sufficiently strong driving field, this anomalous
(i.e., turbulent) heating exceeds significantly the normal (i.e., laminar) heating
produced exclusively by
\vec{E}_{0}
. This paper deals with the linear
dispersion relation which disregards such nonlinear processes as mode
coupling, wave-frequency broadening, etc. We can assume, however, that the
short-wavelength waves under study are not strongly affected by such nonlinear
processes, except the spatially averaged, i.e., macroscopic, effect of AEH. We can
include the latter parametrically by replacing
T_{e}
given by
equation (
19
) with the much larger actual electron
temperature
T_{e}
determined empirically or by using reliable heating models

8
;
43
;
31
;
59
;
40
.

The main implication of equation (
19
), especially if
we assume the full electron heating that includes AEH, is as follows. Whatever strong is the
driving field
E_{0}
(reaching sometimes hundreds mV/m), the corresponding
drift speed,
V_{0}=E_{0}/B
, always remains small compared to the modified
thermal speed
V_{Te}=(T_{e}/m_{e})^{1/2}
. This takes place because within
the eV range of the electron energies we always have
\left\langle\nu_{en}\right\rangle/\left\langle\delta_{en}\nu_{en}\right\rangle\gg 1
(see
above). This means that, unlike the unmagnetized ions whose Pedersen drift,

|\langle\vec{V}_{i}\rangle|\sim eE_{0}/(m_{i}\nu_{in}B)
, can be comparable to, or even exceed, the electron
bulk thermal speed
V_{Ti}=(T_{i}/m_{i})^{1/2}
, see
Sec.
2.1
, electrons usually allow
one to neglect any velocity-space shifts of their background Maxwellian EDF,
including those connected with the neutral wind (the latter is always orders
of magnitude smaller than the electron thermal speed, even for the originally
cold ionospheric electrons).

For the linear wave analysis, we will start from
12

equation (38),

\left(i\Delta_{\omega\vec{k}}+\hat{D}_{\omega\vec{k}}\right)f_{\omega\vec{k}}(V_{e})=\hat{B}_{\omega\vec{k}}f_{0}(V_{e})\,\phi_{\omega\vec{k}}\,,

(20)

where
f_{0}(V_{e})
is the background electron distribution function given by
equation (
17
) with the electron temperature
T_{e}
elevated
by the AEH and often much larger than that given by
equation (
19
);
f_{\omega\vec{k}}(V_{e})
is the
single-harmonic linear perturbation of the electron distribution function,

\Delta_{\omega\vec{k}}\equiv\vec{k}\cdot\vec{V}_{e0}-\omega\approx\vec{k}\cdot\vec{V}_{0}-\omega
, where
\vec{V}_{0}=(\vec{E}_{0}\times\vec{B})/B^{2}
; and the two differential operators
\hat{D}_{\omega\vec{k}}
and

\hat{B}_{\omega\vec{k}}
are given by

\hat{D}_{\omega\vec{k}}=\frac{1}{3V_{e}^{2}}\left[\left(\vec{k}_{\perp}V_{e}+i\vec{a}_{e0}\ \frac{d}{dV_{e}}\right)\frac{\nu_{en}(V_{e})V_{e}^{2}}{\Omega_{e}^{2}}\cdot\left(\vec{k}_{\perp}V_{e}+i\vec{a}_{e0}\ \frac{d}{dV_{e}}\right)\right]+\frac{k_{\parallel}^{2}V_{e}^{2}}{3\nu_{en}(V_{e})},

(21)

where
\vec{a}_{e0}=e\vec{E}_{0}/m_{e}
and the speed derivatives
d/dV_{e}
are
assumed to apply (from right to left) to the expressions right of them, including all
V_{e}
-dependent
multipliers and
f_{\omega\vec{k}}(V_{e})
,

\hat{B}_{\omega\vec{k}}=-\ \frac{V_{Te}^{2}}{3}\left[V_{e}\left(\frac{k_{\perp}^{2}\nu_{en}(V_{e})}{\Omega_{e}^{2}}+\frac{k_{\parallel}^{2}}{\nu_{en}(V_{e})}\right)\frac{d}{dV_{e}}+\frac{2i\left(\vec{k}_{\perp}\cdot\vec{a}_{e0}\right)}{\Omega_{e}^{2}V_{e}^{2}}\ \frac{d}{dV_{e}}\left(V_{e}^{2}\nu_{en}(V_{e})\frac{d}{dV_{e}}\right)\right]\!\!.

(22)

Compared to equation (38) from
12
, we have neglected here two
kinetic operators,
\widehat{\delta\nu}_{en}
and
\hat{\nu}_{ee}
, which
describe the local electron collisional cooling and electron-electron
(
e-e
) collisions, respectively. The former is crucial for the ETI

13
, while the latter may play a role under conditions of
sufficiently dense and cold plasma, as discussed in
12
. The ETI exists
almost exclusively in the “super-long”-wavelength limit of
|\omega|\lesssim\langle\delta_{en}\nu_{en}\rangle\ll\nu_{in}

14
;
15
;
9
. In this paper,
however, we are mostly interested in the medium-to-short wavelength range of

\left|\omega\right|\gtrsim\nu_{in}\gg\left\langle\delta_{en}\nu_{en}\right\rangle
,
where the slow local
e-n
collisional cooling plays
no significant role. At the same time, the frictional heating
may still be of importance, so that we should retain the terms
\propto\vec{a}_{e0}
.
Also, since we are interested in strong driving fields that usually lead to
significant electron heating, we can safely neglect the
e-e
collisions,
described by the quadratically-nonlinear operator of Coulomb collisions

\hat{\nu}_{ee}
, because the efficiency of Coulomb collisions decreases dramatically with
increasing energies of the colliding particles.

3.1
Non-thermal approximation for
electrons

For electrons, we will develop the linear kinetic theory for electrons starting from the
limit of
\vec{a}_{e0}\rightarrow 0
. Unlike the well-known no-field approximation for
ions (reviewed in
A
, Sec.
A.2
), the
results of this subsection have never been published before. We will use the

\vec{a}_{e0}\rightarrow 0
results for future references, but these
results may also be sufficient for a moderately strong electric field
\vec{E}_{0}
.

Unlike in the
\vec{a}_{i0}\rightarrow 0
limit for ions, we will refer
to the
\vec{a}_{e0}\rightarrow 0
limit as the non-thermal approximation (NTA) for electrons.
For magnetized electrons, the driving field
\vec{E}_{0}

has been implicitly included in
\Delta_{\omega\vec{k}}
through the
mean electron drift velocity,
\vec{V}_{e0}
(without this drift, no E-region plasma instability would even be possible).
With this drift, but no the
\vec{a}_{e0}
-related terms explicitly included,
all possible electron thermal effects are totally ignored.

For the Maxwellian background electron distribution function
f_{0}(V_{e})
, when neglecting all terms

\propto\vec{a}_{e0}
, the above differential operators
\hat{D}_{\omega\vec{k}}
and
\hat{B}_{\omega\vec{k}}
reduce to a common algebraic function
of the electron speed
V_{e}
,

\hat{D}_{\omega\vec{k}}\approx\hat{B}_{\omega\vec{k}}\approx S_{\omega\vec{k}}\,\equiv\frac{V_{e}^{2}}{3}\left(\frac{k_{\perp}^{2}\nu_{en}(V_{e})}{\Omega_{e}^{2}}+\frac{k_{\parallel}^{2}}{\nu_{en}(V_{e})}\right).

(23)

This reduction is valid for sufficiently short-wavelength waves satisfying the
conditions

\displaystyle\frac{\left(kV_{Te}\right)^{2}\left\langle\nu_{en}\right\rangle}{3\Omega_{e}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\left(kV_{Ti}\right)^{2}}{\nu_{in}}\frac{\left\langle\psi_{\perp}\right\rangle}{3\beta_{T}}\gg\left\langle\delta_{en}\nu_{en}\right\rangle,\;\frac{kV_{0}\left\langle\nu_{en}\right\rangle}{3\Omega_{e}},

(24)

\displaystyle\frac{\left(kV_{Ti}\right)^{2}}{\beta_{Ti}}

\displaystyle\gg kV_{0}\Omega_{i}=ka_{i0}

where
\beta_{Ti}=T_{i}/T_{e}
and
\left\langle\psi_{\perp}\right\rangle=\left\langle\nu_{en}\right\rangle\nu_{in}/(\Omega_{e}\Omega_{i})

These conditions mean that the electron diffusion described by the LHS of

3.2
General case

In the general case, we should solve full equation (

\displaystyle\hat{B}_{\omega\vec{k}}f_{0}=\frac{n_{0}}{3\left(2\pi\right)^{3/2}V_{Te}^{3}}\exp\left(-\ \frac{V_{e}^{2}}{2V_{Te}^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}f_{\omega\vec{k}}^{e}}{d\xi^{2}}-\left(\chi-\frac{5}{2\xi}\right)\frac{df_{\omega\vec{k}}^{e}}{d\xi}-\left(C+\frac{5\chi}{4\xi}+\frac{\mathcal{P}}{\xi^{2}}\right)f_{\omega\vec{k}}^{e}

We will refer to the NTA limit when discussing the general solution for electrons.

Generally, for electrons (as well as for the ions), instead of a simple
algebraic relation between

and
\phi_{\omega\vec{k}}

43

11

difficulty becomes the integration of the found solution (with the relevant weight
function) in order to determine the wave perturbations of the electron density.

Finding the solution of equation (
43
) and
integrating it over the electron speeds
V_{e}
is precisely what we start doing now.
To obtain the final results in the exact analytical form,
which at the same time would be convenient for a simple analysis and practical
applications, is hardly possible. Of paramount importance to us is obtaining the results in a form of simple
functional relations, rather than in terms of tricky integrals.
We start by obtaining the exact solution of equation (
43

functions. After exhausting the exact steps, we will employ
reasonably accurate approximations based on relations between the actual
physical parameters.

The LHS of second-order ODEs like equation (

\sqrt{x}\ \mathrm{I}_{\mu}
and
\sqrt{x}\ \mathrm{I}_{-\mu}

\sqrt{x}\ \mathrm{I}_{\mu}
and
\sqrt{x}\ \mathrm{I}_{-\mu}

Furthermore, the exact Wronskian of the two linearly independent solutions

\mathrm{I}_{\mu}
and
\mathrm{K}_{\mu}
is given by

W\equiv\sqrt{x}\ \mathrm{K}_{\mu}\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)\frac{d}{dx}\left[\sqrt{x}\ \mathrm{I}_{\mu}\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)\right]-\sqrt{x}\ \mathrm{I}_{\mu}\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)\frac{d}{dx}\left[\sqrt{x}\ \mathrm{K}_{\mu}\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)\right]=1.

1

2

\displaystyle\sqrt{x}\ \mathrm{K}_{\mu}\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{x}\ \mathrm{I}_{\mu}\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)\int_{x}^{\infty}\frac{dt}{\left[\sqrt{t}\ \mathrm{I}_{\mu}\left(t/2\right)\right]^{2}},

(58a)

\displaystyle\sqrt{x}\ \mathrm{I}_{\mu}\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{x}\ \mathrm{K}_{\mu}\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)\int_{0}^{x}\frac{dt}{\left[\sqrt{t}\ \mathrm{K}_{\mu}\left(t/2\right)\right]^{2}}\!.

(58b)

These relations can be used for additional testing the accuracy of

B

Now we proceed to solving equation (

Before proceeding, we will make simple estimates
regarding the crucial parameter

The main contribution to

(60)

By assuming sufficiently short-wavelength waves which are most relevant to the implied kinetic regime,

|\Delta_{\omega\vec{k}}|=|\omega-\vec{k}\cdot\vec{V}_{e0}|\gtrsim\nu_{in},

(61)

E_{0}=m_{e}a_{e0}/e

corresponding to the minimum FBI threshold,
E_{0}=BC_{s}

(for the high-latitude electrojet, typically

extreme, but still possible value of

V/m, by applying currently

4
The TFBI Wave Dispersion
Relation

To close the derivations of the full TFBI wave dispersion relation, we have to relate
ions and electrons by applying Poisson’s equation,
\nabla^{2}\Phi=(n_{e}-n_{i})/\epsilon_{0}
, where
\epsilon_{0}
is the
permittivity of the free space. In our notations, for a given
harmonic perturbation this equation reduces to

\eta_{\omega\vec{k}}^{i}-\eta_{\omega\vec{k}}^{e}=k^{2}\lambda_{D}^{2}\phi_{\omega\vec{k}}\ ,

(69)

where
\lambda_{D}=[\epsilon_{0}T_{e}/(e^{2}n_{e})]^{1/2}
is the
“electron” Debye length [the electron
temperature stems from the definition of
\phi_{\omega\vec{k}}
given by
equation (
8
)]. Substituting
\eta_{\omega\vec{k}}^{i}

from equation (
15
) and
\eta_{\omega\vec{k}}^{e}
from
equation (
67
), and then canceling
\phi_{\omega\vec{k}}
from both
sides of equation (
69
), we obtain the sought-for fully kinetic linear-wave dispersion
relation:

I_{\mathrm{ion}}+I_{\mathrm{electron}}+k^{2}\lambda_{D}^{2}=0.

(70)

The first two terms in the LHS represent the ion and electron contributions to the TFBI wave dispersion relation, while the last term
takes into account the charge separation.

The ion contribution,
I_{\mathrm{ion}}
, is given by

\displaystyle I_{\mathrm{ion}}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{k^{2}V_{Tn}^{2}}{\beta_{T}(k^{2}V_{Tn}^{2}+i\vec{k}\cdot\vec{a}_{i0})}\left[1+\frac{i\nu_{in}}{\sqrt{2(k^{2}V_{Tn}^{2}+i\vec{k}\cdot\vec{a}_{i0})}}\ \tilde{Z}\left(\frac{R_{i}}{\sqrt{2(k^{2}V_{Tn}^{2}+i\vec{k}\cdot\vec{a}_{i0})}}\right)\right]^{-1}

\displaystyle\times\left[1+\frac{R_{i}-\vec{k}\cdot\vec{U}_{0}}{\sqrt{2(k_{\parallel}^{2}V_{\parallel}^{2}+k_{\perp}^{2}V_{\perp}^{2}+i\vec{k}\cdot\vec{a}_{i0})}}\ \tilde{Z}\left(\frac{R_{i}-\vec{k}\cdot\vec{U}_{0}}{\sqrt{2(k_{\parallel}^{2}V_{\parallel}^{2}+k_{\perp}^{2}V_{\perp}^{2}+i\vec{k}\cdot\vec{a}_{i0})}}\right)\right],

(71)

where
\tilde{Z}
is a piece-wise modification of the regular
Z
-function:

\tilde{Z}(z)=\left\{\begin{array}[c]{ccc}Z(z)&\text{if}&\operatorname{Re}(R_{i})>0\ \ (\text{i.e.,\ }\gamma>-\nu_{in}),\\
-Z(-z)&\text{if}&\operatorname{Re}(R_{i})<0\ \ (\text{i.e.,\ }\gamma<-\nu_{in}).\end{array}\right.

(72)

Here “
-Z(-z)
” can be alternatively represented as

-Z(-z)=i\sqrt{\pi}\,e^{-z^{2}}(-1+\operatorname{erf}(iz))=Z(z)-2i\sqrt{\pi}\,e^{-z^{2}}.

The electron contribution,
I_{\mathrm{electron}}
, is given by

\displaystyle I_{\mathrm{electron}}

\displaystyle=\frac{k_{\perp}^{2}V_{Te}^{2}-2i\vec{k}\cdot\vec{a}_{e0}}{k_{\perp}^{2}V_{Te}^{2}-2i\vec{k}\cdot\vec{a}_{e0}-a_{e0}^{2}/V_{Te}^{2}}

\displaystyle\times\left(1+2\alpha_{1}+\frac{q_{0}^{4}+\alpha_{0}+\alpha_{1}q_{0}^{2}}{q_{0}}\ Z(q_{0})-i\,\frac{q_{0}^{4}+\alpha_{0}-\alpha_{1}q_{0}^{2}}{q_{0}}\ Z(iq_{0})\right),

(73)

where
q_{0}
is the only quartic root (
q
) of equation (
65c
) with
\operatorname{Re}q>0
and
\operatorname{Im}q>0
.

We are presenting all these relations in terms of the
Z
-functions only; to express them
in terms of
w(x)
one can use the simple conversion formula
Z(x)=i\sqrt{\pi}\,w(x)
, see equation (
16
).
Recall that here
\beta_{T}=T_{n}/T_{e}
,
\lambda^{2}_{D}=\epsilon_{0}T_{e}/(e^{2}n_{e})
,
k^{2}=k_{\perp}^{2}+k_{\parallel}^{2}
,

R_{i}=\omega+i\nu_{in}=\omega_{r}+i(\gamma+\nu_{in})
,
\vec{a}_{i0}=e\vec{E}_{0}/m_{i}
,
\vec{a}_{e0}=e\vec{E}_{0}/m_{e}
,

a_{e,i0}=|\vec{a}_{e,i0}|
, and the parameters
V_{\parallel,\perp}
and
\vec{U}_{0}=\vec{a}_{i0}/\nu_{in}
describe the
parallel/perpendicular thermal velocities and the mean drift velocity in the assumed bi-Maxwellian
ion velocity distribution given by equation (
5
), respectively.
The parameters
\alpha_{0}
and
\alpha_{1}
are given by equation (
65
),
where
\Delta_{\omega\vec{k}}=\vec{k}\cdot\vec{V}_{e0}-\omega\approx\vec{k}\cdot\vec{V}_{0}-\omega
and
\vec{V}_{0}=e\vec{E_{0}}\times\vec{B}/B^{2}
.

Without terms proportional to
\vec{a}_{i0}
and
\vec{a}_{e0}
, equations (
71
) and (
73
)
reduce to those determined by the “classical” NFA solution for ions, see equation (
93
), and the NTA solution for electrons, see
equations (
25
) and (
40
). These two approximations describe the pure FBI
driving/damping. The terms
\propto\vec{a}_{i0}
in equation (
71
) describe the ion thermal effects
leading to the ITI, while the terms
\propto\vec{a}_{e0}
in equations (
73
) describe the
corresponding electron thermal effects. Thus, equation (
70
)
makes it easy to trace the effects of different physical mechanisms.

Given the wavevector
\vec{k}
and various plasma parameters, wave dispersion equation (
70
)
should be solved for the complex wave frequency,
\omega=\omega_{r}+i\gamma
. The ion contribution
to the wave dispersion relation includes the unknown
\omega
only through the the combination

R_{i}=\omega+i\nu_{in}
, while the electron contribution includes
\omega
only through the Doppler-shifted wave frequency

\Delta_{\omega\vec{k}}=\vec{k}\cdot\vec{V}_{e0}-\omega
, which in turn is involved only through the quantity
q
,
see equation (
65c
). Equation (
70
) contains no other
\omega
-dependent quantities.

Unlike the long-wavelength limit of the fluid-model TFBI dispersion relation
described in Sec. V of
9
, where the instability
growth/damping rate,
\gamma
, turns out to be automatically small compared to the real wave frequency,
\omega_{r}
, and in the
former the different instability mechanisms can be linearly separated from
each other, equation (
70
) is much more involved.
The growth/damping rate can be comparable to the real frequency, especially if

\omega_{r}\sim\nu_{in}
. While in the long-wavelength range the wave phase
velocity is largely unaffected by the instability mechanisms, in the kinetic
regime all instability mechanisms can affect the wave phase velocity.
Equation (
70
) should serve as a basis for such
analyses.

5
Discussion

In this section, we discuss the limitations of our analytic theory. It is to be noted,
however, that the limitations of our theory are inherently much weaker than those
of the previous kinetic studies. The present fully kinetic theory
of the combined TFBI is a serious improvement and a significant step forward in the analytical study of the
electrojet instabilities.

The major limitation of this specific analysis is
associated with the fact that in this paper we neglect the finite ion magnetization.
This means that the ion-related part of our fully kinetic wave dispersion
relation is mostly applicable to the ionospheric altitudes below 110 km. Including the
ion magnetization in the analytic theory is reserved for the future, but the
electron-related part of this dispersion relation remains valid throughout the
entire E region (albeit with a caveat discussed below). If the future kinetic theory includes the ion
magnetization then there should be no need to upgrade the electron-related part.

The next important issue is that for the background velocity distributions of
ions and electrons we assumed modified Maxwellian velocity distributions (a
shifted bi-Maxwellian distribution for ions and an almost isotropic, but strongly heated
Maxwellian distribution for electrons). Note that all previous kinetic studies assumed even
much simpler, fully undisturbed and isothermal, Maxwellian velocity distributions for both
ions and electrons. In our theory, when choosing the parameters for the modified background
velocity distributions, such as the mean particle densities, temperatures, and
the average velocity drift (for ions), we require these parameters to be
consistent with the imposed electric field and plasma parameters. However, some actual
characteristics of the background velocity distributions cannot be fully
mimicked by the modified Maxwellian velocity distributions. Indeed, according
to recent results by
38
(these results
have been partially reviewed in this paper’s Appendix
A.1
),
under strong DC electric field, the PIC-determined ion velocity distribution becomes strongly shifted, has an
anisotropic effective temperature, and is skewed in the
\vec{E}_{0}
-direction. The simple BGK-based analytic theory accurately predicts the mean
ion drift and the overall mean effective temperature, but it greatly
exaggerates the temperature anisotropy and skewness. The employed
bi-Maxwellian distribution ansatz can accurately mimic the ion drift and
anisotropic temperatures, provided they are taken from PIC simulations or from a
more accurate analytical model, but the bi-Maxwellian ansatz cannot mimic the
velocity-distribution skewness. How consequential might be this inaccuracy is
not clear yet, but the shifted bi-Maxwellian ion function with the
consistently chosen parameters seems to be the closest to reality, while remaining still analytically tractable.

Under the action of a strong electric field, the nearly isotropic electron
velocity distribution can deviate significantly from any heated Maxwellian
distribution, but this usually takes place outside of the thermal bulk, i.e.,
closer to the higher-energy tail of the electron velocity distribution, see,
e.g.,
27
;
43
. The major contributions to
all integrals that resulted in our wave dispersion relation have been made by
the thermal bulk of the electron velocity distribution,
V_{e}\lesssim V_{Te}
, so that this inaccuracy does not seem to be crucial. As for the ion
background velocity distribution, there is hardly a better tractable model of the
background electron velocity distribution than the Maxwellian distribution
with the consistently chosen temperature.

For electrons, however, there is one more, potentially serious, limitation associated
with the applicability of the entire electron kinetic approach based on
equation (
20
). This equation had been originally derived
based on the assumption of strong isotropization of the electron distribution
function
27
;
12
, along with all its
wave perturbations. In the perpendicular to
\vec{B}
direction, this
isotropization is provided by fast Larmor rotation of electrons, compared to
the wave period,
\Omega_{e}\gg|\omega|
. At the E region, this condition is
fulfilled with a substantial reserve. However, on any plane that includes the
direction parallel to
\vec{B}
, this isotropization usually requires
sufficiently frequent collisions,
\nu_{en}\gg|\omega|
(or, at least,

\nu_{en}\gtrsim|\omega|
). This represents a much stronger
restriction than that in the perpendicular to
\vec{B}
plane. The electron
kinetic effects are mostly relevant for
|\omega|\gtrsim\nu_{in}
. Under
undisturbed conditions of the cold E-region ionosphere, the electron-to-ion
collision rate ratio,
\nu_{en}/\nu_{in}
, is only about ten (see above), so that
there may seem to be not enough room for the applicability of our kinetic approach for

6
Summary and Conclusions

In this paper, we have developed a fully kinetic analytical theory of the
thermal Farley-Buneman instability (TFBI). This combined instability includes
all known short-scale E-region instabilities, except the spatially inhomogeneous gradient
drift instability (GDI). The latter usually produces relatively
long-wavelength turbulence, typically tens or hundreds meters, for which the
kinetic theory is not urgently needed, though such theories exist,
e.g.,
54
;
3
.

For the first time in the E-region instability theoretical research,
the driving electric field
\vec{E}_{0}
has been
included in the kinetic description of ions. As a result, the ion thermal
instability (ITE) driving has been automatically included in the kinetic
theory. For electrons, in addition to the standard inclusion of
\vec{E}_{0}
via the
\vec{E}_{0}\times\vec{B}
-drift velocity, the driving electric field was included
previously in the collisional kinetic theory developed by

12
;
13
. That theory had allowed the
authors to predict the new effect of the electron thermal instability (ETI).
Compared to that earlier kinetic theory, this paper considers a
shorter-wavelength range, which is especially relevant to the kinetic
treatment. We do not expect this short-wavelength range to be linearly
unstable due to the ETI, but the reverse effect of increased wave stabilization and possible
phase-velocity modifications caused by electron thermal effects associated with
the explicitly included field
\vec{E}_{0}
can affect dramatically the entire
instability process and may be of importance for the accurate interpretation of radar observations.

The principal outcome of our TFBI-related kinetic theory is the linear wave dispersion relation given by
equation (
70
). It is remarkable that, similarly
to the earlier kinetic theories for ions

20
;
39
;
56
;
50
;
54
;
3
, this much more complicated fully kinetic
dispersion relation includes only elementary functions combined with the
plasma dispersion functions,
Z(x)

25
.
Unlike the earlier FBI-related theories, however, our equation (
70
)
includes
Z
-functions of different arguments in both the ion and
electron parts of the dispersion equation.

The results of our theory can be of importance for accurate interpretation of radar signals scattered from
relatively high E-region altitudes where the ionospheric density irregularities with the wavelength
corresponding to the given radar frequency are within the short-wavelength kinetic range.
We should bear in mind, however, that this theory is still limited to altitudes where
ions are essentially unmagnetized (below 110 km).

Using the general kinetic wave dispersion relation given by equation (
70
), one can determine the
instability threshold for the given short-wavelength waves and establish whether the observed
radar signals were scattered from linearly unstable or stable waves. For a sufficiently strong
driving electric field, this equation will also help find the
fastest growing unstable waves over the entire wavevector
\vec{k}
space, provided the wave frequency
\omega
satisfies
the condition
\delta_{en}\nu_{en}\ll|\omega|\lesssim\nu_{en}
. The right-hand restriction is
not as severe as it might look because the
e-n
collision frequency
\nu_{en}
increases with the
electron temperature, which in turn sharply increases with the strong driving field.
Finally, this linear dispersion equation will provide the wave phase velocities which may approximate the observed radar signal
Doppler shifts, even if the plasma turbulence is in the highly nonlinear state. To accurately predict the
full irregularity spectrum and nonlinearly saturated wave amplitudes, fully
kinetic computer simulations or much more complicated nonlinear models should be employed.

(100a)

To conclude this section, we note that for sufficiently large

and the gamma function of a half-integer argument,

A.2.1
Useful integrals

Now we slightly modify the above derivation by using a more general coordinate
system. Instead of directing the wavevector
\vec{k}

strictly along the
x
-axis, we will rotate the coordinate axes around the

z
-axis, so that the wavevector
\vec{k}
has two non-zero components:

k_{x}
and
k_{y}
(but still
k_{z}=0
). In such coordinate system, one
might use, e.g., the polar coordinates in the velocity space,
V_{i}
,
\theta_{i}
, so
that
\vec{k}\cdot\vec{V}_{i}=kV_{i}\cos\theta_{i}
,
k=(k_{x}^{2}+k_{y}^{2})^{1/2}
, and obtain the above result in a more symmetric way with respect
to the
\vec{k}
components. But such derivation would produce nothing new and useful. If,
however, we keep using the Cartesian coordinate system, then by comparing
the results we will arrive at a non-trivial integral whose exact evaluation,
exclusively in terms of the Gaussian and error functions, will help us obtain
the wave dispersion relation in the general case of arbitrarily large
electric field
\vec{E}_{0}
(see below).

Now we proceed with the corresponding treatment. We are no longer interested
in obtaining the relationship between the electric potential and density wave
perturbations because we already know the result (see above). What we are
pursuing here is the mutual consistency of two slightly different mathematical
approaches, so that they give the same result. To this end, we restrict
ourselves to the calculation of the following double integral:

R=\iint_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\exp\left(-u^{2}\right)d^{2}u}{\vec{k}\cdot\vec{u}\mathbf{-}p}.

(105)

assuming
\operatorname{Im}p>0
.

For the quantity
R
, the simple first approach with
k_{y}=0
and positive

k_{x}=k
, yields

\displaystyle R

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\exp\left(-u_{y}^{2}\right)du_{y}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\exp\left(-u_{x}^{2}\right)du_{x}}{k_{x}\left(u_{x}-p\right)}=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{k_{x}}\ I(p)

\displaystyle=\frac{i\pi^{3/2}}{k}\ w\left(p\right)=\frac{i\pi^{3/2}}{k}\ \exp\left(-p^{2}\right)\left[1+\operatorname{erf}\left(ip\right)\right].

(106)

The second approach with two non-zero vector components
k_{x}
and
k_{y}

yields

R=\iint_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\exp\left(-u^{2}\right)d^{2}u}{\vec{k}\cdot\vec{u}\mathbf{-}p}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\exp\left(-u_{y}^{2}\right)du_{y}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\exp\left(-u_{x}^{2}\right)du_{x}}{k_{x}u_{x}+k_{y}u_{y}-kp},

(107)

where the internal integral has the same structure as that in
equation (
97
) with
p
replaced by
p-k_{y}u_{y}/k
(the restriction

\operatorname{Im}p>0
easily transfers to
\operatorname{Im}(p-k_{y}u_{y}/k)>0
). Applying (
100
), the internal integration yields

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\exp\left(-u_{x}^{2}\right)du_{x}}{k_{x}u_{x}+k_{y}u_{y}-kp}=\frac{i\pi}{k_{x}}\ w\left(\frac{kp-k_{y}u_{y}}{k_{x}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{i\pi^{3/2}}{k}\ \exp\left[-\ \left(\frac{kp-k_{y}u_{y}}{k_{x}}\right)^{2}\right]\left[1+\operatorname{erf}\left(i\ \frac{kp-k_{y}u_{y}}{k_{x}}\right)\right].

(108)

In the remaining integral of equation (
107
), we change the variable

u_{y}
to a new complex variable
\tau
,

\tau=\frac{kp-k_{y}u_{y}}{k_{x}},\qquad u_{y}=\frac{kp-\tau k_{x}}{k_{y}}.

(109)

In terms of the new variable
\tau
, we combine
\exp\left(-u_{y}^{2}\right)
with the exponential function in
equation (
108
), using
\left(k_{x}^{2}+k_{y}^{2}\right)^{1/2}=k
, and then deform the corresponding contour of integration in the
complex
\tau
-plane to make this integration along the new real axis (the
required conditions will be discussed later). This yields

R=\frac{i\pi^{3/2}}{k}\exp\left(-p^{2}\right)\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\exp\left[-\ \frac{\left(k\tau-pk_{x}\right)^{2}}{k_{y}^{2}}\right]\left[1+\operatorname{erf}\left(i\tau\right)\right]d\tau.

(110)

Equating this
R
to the same function given by equation (
106
),
we arrive at an unexpected and fairly simple integral relationship:

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\exp\left[-\ \frac{\left(k\tau-pk_{x}\right)^{2}}{k_{y}^{2}}\right]\left[1+\operatorname{erf}\left(i\tau\right)\right]d\tau=1+\operatorname{erf}\left(ip\right).

(111)

In this form, equation (
111
) contains a clear asymmetry with respect
to
k_{x}
and
k_{y}
, which should not be the case. Besides, by its
derivation, it is clear that this relationship may have serious restrictions
associated with the fact that
k_{x}
and
k_{y}
have been implied to be real. With these
restrictions and asymmetry, the usefulness of this relationship may be
limited. It turns out, however, that after a simple generalization the
applicability of a more general relation covers a much broader
domain of complex parameters.

We demonstrate this by introducing complex parameters
A
,
B
, and
C
, which
for equation (
111
) reduce to
A=k/k_{y}
,
B=pk_{x}/k_{y}
,
C=i
, but
in general are not limited to these specific values. In terms of these
parameters, equation (
111
) acquires a more general form:

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\exp\left[-\left(Az-B\right)^{2}\right]\left[1+\operatorname{erf}\left(Cz\right)\right]dz=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{A}\left[\operatorname{sgn(\operatorname{Re}A)}+\operatorname{erf}\left(\frac{BC}{\sqrt{A^{2}+C^{2}}}\right)\right],

(112)

where
\operatorname{sgn}(x)
is the standard signum function of a real argument
x
:

\operatorname{sgn}(x)=x/|x|
if
x\neq 0
and
\operatorname{sgn}(x)=0
if
x=0
.
Below we prove equation (
112
) directly, without reference to preceding
equation (
111
).

Before performing this proof, we note that equation (
112
)
should hold separately for the two separate parts of
\left[1+\operatorname{erf}\left(Cz\right)\right]
: those proportional to
“1” on both sides of this equation,

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\exp\left[-\left(Az-B\right)^{2}\right]dz=\operatorname{sgn}(\operatorname{Re}A)\,\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{A}.

(113)

and those proportional to the error function,

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\exp\left[-\left(Az-B\right)^{2}\right]\operatorname{erf}\left(Cz\right)dz=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{A}\operatorname{erf}\left(\frac{BC}{\sqrt{A^{2}+C^{2}}}\right)

(114)

(recall that by the square root we always mean the branch with the positive real part).
Equation (
113
) presents a
well-known integral whose convergence is restricted by
\left|\arg(A)\right|<\pi/4
or, equivalently,

\operatorname{Re}(A^{2})>0.

(115)

Equation (
114
), however, is non-standard and less
trivial. Popular tables of integrals, such as
17
;
35
;
26
do not present the exact analytic result of such integration.

51
do this, but they present the result of such integration
in terms of more complicated special functions, which are of no practical use.

To proceed with our integral,
we temporarily denote the LHS of equation (
114
) as

K(C)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\exp\left[-\left(Az-B\right)^{2}\right]\operatorname{erf}\left(Cz\right)dz

(116)

and differentiate the function
K(C)
with respect to the complex parameter
C
. This yields

\frac{dK}{dC}=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\exp\left(-\ \frac{B^{2}C^{2}}{A^{2}+C^{2}}\right)\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\exp\left\{-\ \frac{\left[\left(A^{2}+C^{2}\right)z-AB\right]^{2}}{A^{2}+C^{2}}\right\}zdz.

Transforming the integral in the RHS as follows,

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\exp\left\{-\ \frac{\left[\left(A^{2}+C^{2}\right)z-AB\right]^{2}}{A^{2}+C^{2}}\right\}zdz

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\exp\left[-\left(A^{2}+C^{2}\right)\left(z-\frac{AB}{A^{2}+C^{2}}\right)^{2}\right]\left(z-\frac{AB}{A^{2}+C^{2}}\right)dz

\displaystyle+\frac{AB}{A^{2}+C^{2}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\exp\left[-\left(A^{2}+C^{2}\right)\left(z-\frac{AB}{A^{2}+C^{2}}\right)^{2}\right]dz,

(117)

we see that the contribution of the first integral goes away due to the oddity of the
integrand, while the second integral, under restriction

\operatorname{Re}\left(A^{2}+C^{2}\right)>0,

(118)

can be evaluated in a standard way by deforming the integration path in the
complex plane using, yielding
\sqrt{\pi}\left(A^{2}+C^{2}\right)^{-1/2}
.
As a result, we obtain

\frac{dK}{dC}=\frac{2AB}{\left(A^{2}+C^{2}\right)^{3/2}}\exp\left(-\ \frac{B^{2}C^{2}}{A^{2}+C^{2}}\right).

(119)

The definition of the error function is such that
\operatorname{erf}(0)=0
, so that,
according to equation (
116
), we have
K(0)=0
. Using this as the
boundary condition, we integrate equation (
119
) and obtain

\displaystyle K(C)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{C}\frac{2AB}{\left(A^{2}+C^{2}\right)^{3/2}}\exp\left(-\ \frac{B^{2}C^{2}}{A^{2}+C^{2}}\right)dC

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{A}\operatorname{erf}\left(\frac{BC}{\sqrt{A^{2}+C^{2}}}\right).

(120)

This result can be easily verified by differentiating the RHS of
equation (
120
) with respect to
C
.

Equation (
120
) closes the
proof of equation (
114
) and hence of fuller
equation (
112
) for complex parameters
A
,
B
, and
C
,
under constraints imposed by equations (
115
) and
(
118
). In the general case, reduction of the original integral
to this form may involve a linear transform of the original variable in the
complex plane with the corresponding deformation of the integration path to
the real axis of
z
. This makes the total number of parameters (three)
somewhat redundant. To avoid this redundancy and simplify the result, we assign for the parameter

C
a specific value
C=i
, so that the two remaining parameters (
A
and
B
)
could be determined from the original integral. Then
equation (
112
) reduces to a more compact relationship:

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\exp\left[-\left(Az-B\right)^{2}\right]\left(1+\operatorname{erf}\left(iz\right)\right)dz=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{A}\left[\operatorname{sgn}(\operatorname{Re}A)+\operatorname{erf}\left(\frac{iB}{\sqrt{A^{2}-1}}\right)\right].

(121)

The corresponding integral convergence conditions given by separate
equations (
115
) and (
118
) reduce now to a single
condition:

\operatorname{Re}(A^{2}-1)>0.

(122)

Recalling the definition of the standard function
w(x)
given by
equation (
101
) and using transformations similar to those in
equation (
117
), we rewrite equation (
121
) as

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\exp\left[-\left(A^{2}-1\right)\left(z-\frac{AB}{A^{2}-1}\right)^{2}\right]w\left(z\right)dz

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{A}\ \tilde{w}\left(\frac{B}{\sqrt{A^{2}-1}}\right),

(123)

where the extended
w
-function,
\tilde{w}(z)
, is given by

\tilde{w}\left(\frac{B}{\sqrt{A^{2}-1}}\right)=\left\{\begin{array}[c]{ccc}w\left({B}/{\sqrt{A^{2}-1}}\,\right)&\text{if}&\operatorname{Re}A>0,\\
-w\left(-{B}/{\sqrt{A^{2}-1}}\,\right)&\text{if}&\operatorname{Re}A<0\end{array}\right.

(124)

and we ignore the degenerate case of
\operatorname{Re}A=0
. Here “
-w(-z)
” can be identically represented as

-w(-z)=e^{-z^{2}}(-1+\operatorname{erf}(iz))=w(z)-2e^{-z^{2}},

which means that
-w(-z)
is shifted from
w(z)
by a simple Gaussian function.

Due to the proportionality of the
w
and
Z
-functions,
the two
w
-functions on both sides of equation (
123
) can be
simultaneously replaced by the corresponding
Z
-functions:

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\exp\left(-(A^{2}-1)\left(z-\frac{AB}{A^{2}-1}\right)^{2}\right)Z(z)dz

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{A}\ \tilde{Z}\left(\frac{B}{\sqrt{A^{2}-1}}\right),

(125)

where

\tilde{Z}\left(\frac{B}{\sqrt{A^{2}-1}}\right)=\left\{\begin{array}[c]{ccc}Z\left({B}/{\sqrt{A^{2}-1}}\,\right)&\text{if}&\operatorname{Re}A>0,\\
-Z\left(-{B}/{\sqrt{A^{2}-1}}\,\right)&\text{if}&\operatorname{Re}A<0\end{array}\right.

(126)

and “
-Z(-z)
” can be alternatively represented as

-Z(-z)=i\sqrt{\pi}\,e^{-z^{2}}(-1+\operatorname{erf}(iz))=Z(z)-2i\sqrt{\pi}\,e^{-z^{2}}.

We emphasize that using proper linear transformation of the integration
variable and contour deformation, any integrable convolution of the Gaussian and
w
or
Z
-functions
with two different complex arguments can always be reduced to the form given by
equations (
121
), (
123
), or (
125
) with only two adjustable
parameters
A
and
B
.

Now we evaluate another useful integral,

I_{1}\equiv\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}z\exp\left[-(A^{2}-1)\left(z-\frac{AB}{A^{2}-1}\right)^{2}\right]w(z)dz,

(127)

which differs from that in equation (
123
) by the additional multiplier
z
in the integrand.
To proceed with this integral evaluation, we temporarily introduce an auxiliary
integral,

I_{2}\equiv(A^{2}-1)\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left(z-\frac{AB}{A^{2}-1}\right)\exp\left[-(A^{2}-1)\left(z-\frac{AB}{A^{2}-1}\right)^{2}\right]w(z)dz

On the one hand, this definition combined with
equation (
123
) gives

I_{2}=(A^{2}-1)I_{1}-B\sqrt{\pi}\,w\left(\frac{B}{\sqrt{A^{2}-1}}\right).

(128)

On the other hand, we can evaluate
I_{2}
directly, using integration by
parts,

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle=-\ \frac{1}{2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{d}{dz}\exp\left[-(A^{2}-1)\left(z-\frac{AB}{A^{2}-1}\right)^{2}\right]w\left(z\right)dz

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\exp\left[-(A^{2}-1)\left(z-\frac{AB}{A^{2}-1}\right)^{2}\right]\frac{dw}{dz}\ dz,

where, as usual, we imply a good behavior of the integrand at
\pm\infty
.
Then, using the easily verifiable differential equation for the
w
-function,

\frac{dw}{dz}=2\left(\frac{i}{\sqrt{\pi}}-zw\left(z\right)\right),

we obtain

\displaystyle I_{2}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\exp\left[-(A^{2}-1)\left(z-\frac{AB}{A^{2}-1}\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\times\left(\frac{i}{\sqrt{\pi}}-zw\left(z\right)\right)dz=\frac{i}{\sqrt{A^{2}-1}}-I_{1}.

(129)

Comparison of equations (
128
) and (
129
) yields the explicit
expression for
I_{1}
, i.e., another useful integral relation:

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}z\exp\left[-(A^{2}-1)\left(z-\frac{AB}{A^{2}-1}\right)^{2}\right]w(z)dz

\displaystyle=\frac{B\sqrt{\pi}}{A^{2}}\ \tilde{w}\left(\frac{B}{\sqrt{A^{2}-1}}\right)+\frac{i}{A^{2}\sqrt{A^{2}-1}}.

(130)

In terms of the
Z
-functions, the equivalent integral relation is given by

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}z\exp\left(-(A^{2}-1)\left(z-\frac{AB}{A^{2}-1}\right)^{2}\right)Z(z)dz

\displaystyle=\frac{B\sqrt{\pi}}{A^{2}}\ \tilde{Z}\left(\frac{B}{\sqrt{A^{2}-1}}\right)-\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{A^{2}\sqrt{A^{2}-1}}.

(131)

One can easily verify that these integrals remain invariant with respect to the simultaneous change of

A
,
B
to
-A
,
-B
, respectively. In doing so, we should bear in mind that this change toggles the piece-wise conditions
of the extended function
\tilde{w}
, as defined in equation (

\operatorname{Re}(-A)>0
becomes
\operatorname{Re}A<0
and vice versa.

Thus, any integral convolution of a Gaussian function with an error function (or the related

, can
always be reduced to the two non-conventional integrals given by equations (

),
(

), (

)
and (

) (or equivalent equations (

) and (

)
in terms the

-function). This reduction may require a simple linear transformation of the integration

the integration contour in the new complex plane to make it the real axis.
Since the integrand functions are analytical, the main restriction
is the good behavior of the integrand at

(the integrand there should tend to zero faster
than
1/|z|
, providing the convergence of the integral with no contribution from the infinitely large contour).
It is important that the results of these integrations are also represented in terms

) and (

A.3
Solving for
F_{\omega\vec{k}}^{(1)}
and evaluating its
contribution to
\eta_{\omega\vec{k}}^{i}

Here we find the solution for
F_{\omega\vec{k}}^{(1)}
and then evaluate the
corresponding double integral. Equation (

) can be rewritten as

\frac{\partial F_{\omega\vec{k}}^{(1)}}{\partial V_{ix}}+\frac{i}{a_{i0}}(k_{x}V_{ix}+k_{y}V_{iy}\mathbf{-}R_{i})F_{\omega\vec{k}}^{(1)}=\frac{P}{a_{i0}}\exp\left(-\ \frac{V_{ix}^{2}+V_{iy}^{2}}{2V_{Tn}^{2}}\right),

(132)

where

P\equiv\frac{\nu_{in}n_{0}\eta_{\omega\vec{k}}^{i}}{2\pi V_{Tn}^{2}}.

(133)

Before proceeding with solving general differential equation (
132

consider its no-field limit of

. In this limit, the term

\partial F_{\omega\vec{k}}^{(1)}/\partial V_{ix}

to the other, infinitely large terms, so that

reduces to

F_{\omega\vec{k}}^{(1)}=-\ \frac{iP}{k_{x}V_{ix}+k_{y}V_{iy}\mathbf{-}R_{i}}\exp\left(-\ \frac{V_{ix}^{2}+V_{iy}^{2}}{2V_{Tn}^{2}}\right).

Assuming

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-\xi^{2}}}{\xi-p}\,d\xi=i\pi\tilde{w}(p),

we easily obtain

\displaystyle\iint_{-\infty}^{\infty}F_{\omega\vec{k}}^{(1)}dV_{ix}dV_{iy}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{2}\,\pi^{3/2}PV_{Tn}}{k}\ \tilde{w}\left(\frac{R_{i}}{\sqrt{2}\,kV_{Tn}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}\,\frac{\nu_{in}n_{0}\eta_{\omega\vec{k}}^{i}}{kV_{Tn}}\ \tilde{w}\left(\frac{R_{i}}{\sqrt{2}\,kV_{Tn}}\right)

(134)

We will use this equation for comparison with the corresponding limit
following from the solution of general equation (
132
).

Now we proceed with the general case of arbitrary
a_{i0}
. We start from the most important case of
\operatorname{Re}(R_{i})>0
.
After a long and detailed description of the corresponding solution, in the end of this section, we will outline the opposite case of
\operatorname{Re}(R_{i})<0
.

The case of
\operatorname{Re}(R_{i})>0
corresponds to
\gamma>-\nu_{in}
. This case covers all linearly
unstable waves and probably the most of (if not all) other linear waves.
In this case, a simple analysis of linear differential equation (
132
) suggests choice of the asymptotic
boundary condition of
F_{\omega\vec{k}}^{(1)}\rightarrow 0
as
V_{x}\rightarrow-\infty
, resulting in

\displaystyle F_{\omega\vec{k}}^{(1)}(V_{ix},V_{iy})

\displaystyle=\frac{P}{a_{i0}}\int_{-\infty}^{V_{ix}}\exp\left[-\ \frac{U_{x}^{2}+V_{iy}^{2}}{2V_{Tn}^{2}}+\frac{ik_{x}\left(U_{x}^{2}-V_{ix}^{2}\right)}{2a_{i0}}+\frac{i(k_{y}V_{iy}\mathbf{-}R_{i})\left(U_{x}\mathbf{-}V_{ix}\right)}{a_{i0}}\right]dU_{x}.

(135)

To obtain the corresponding contribution to
\eta_{\omega\vec{k}}^{i}
in
equation (
14
), we should integrate
F_{\omega\vec{k}}^{(1)}
with respect to both
V_{ix}
and
V_{iy}
over the entire 2-D
velocity domain. Integrating first with respect to
V_{iy}
, for the
corresponding
V_{iy}
-dependent multiplier we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\exp\left[-\ \frac{V_{iy}^{2}}{2V_{Tn}^{2}}+\frac{ik_{y}V_{iy}\left(U_{x}-V_{ix}\right)}{a_{i0}}\right]dV_{iy}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2\pi}\,V_{Tn}\exp\left[-\ \frac{k_{y}^{2}V_{Tn}^{2}\left(U_{x}-V_{ix}\right)^{2}}{2a_{i0}^{2}}\right].

To evaluate this integral, we have changed the integration variable to a new
complex variable in order to reduce the integrand to an easily integrable
Gaussian function. Then, in the new complex plane we have deformed the
corresponding integration path using a standard approach by making the final
integration path to lie fully along the new real axis. As usual, this has been
done by using the good analytical properties of the integrand, combined with
the proper behavior of the Gaussian function at an infinite contour within the
allowed domains of the complex plane that provide the integral convergence. We
will do similar manipulations in a few other places without dwelling on
such standard and well-known details.

Further, we have to integrate over
V_{ix}
. When doing this, it is more
beneficial to swap the order of integrations between
V_{ix}
and

U_{x}
. Adjusting the internal integration limits accordingly, we obtain

\displaystyle\iint_{-\infty}^{\infty}F_{\omega\vec{k}}^{(1)}(V_{ix},V_{iy})dV_{iy}dV_{ix}=\frac{PV_{Tn}\sqrt{2\pi}}{a_{i0}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dU_{x}\exp\left(-\ \frac{U_{x}^{2}}{2V_{Tn}^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\times\int_{U_{x}}^{\infty}\exp\left[-\ \frac{k_{y}^{2}V_{Tn}^{2}\left(V_{ix}-U_{x}\right)^{2}}{2a_{i0}^{2}}-\frac{ik_{x}\left(V_{ix}^{2}-U_{x}^{2}\right)}{2a_{i0}}+\frac{i\left(V_{ix}-U_{x}\right)R_{i}}{a_{i0}}\right]dV_{ix}.

(136)

Redistributing terms within the exponents as follows,

\displaystyle-\ \frac{k_{y}^{2}V_{Tn}^{2}\left(V_{ix}-U_{x}\right)^{2}}{2a_{i0}^{2}}-\frac{ik_{x}\left(V_{ix}^{2}-U_{x}^{2}\right)}{2a_{i0}}+\frac{i\left(V_{ix}-U_{x}\right)R_{i}}{a_{i0}}

\displaystyle=-\ \frac{k_{y}^{2}V_{Tn}^{2}+ik_{x}a_{i0}}{2a_{i0}^{2}}\left(V_{ix}-\frac{k_{y}^{2}V_{Tn}^{2}U_{x}+iR_{i}a_{i0}}{k_{y}^{2}V_{Tn}^{2}+ik_{x}a_{i0}}\right)^{2}-\frac{\left(R_{i}-k_{x}U_{x}\right)^{2}}{2\left(k_{y}^{2}V_{Tn}^{2}+ik_{x}a_{i0}\right)}

we reduce equation (
136
) to

\displaystyle\iint_{-\infty}^{\infty}F_{\omega\vec{k}}^{(1)}(V_{ix},V_{iy})dV_{iy}dV_{ix}=\frac{PV_{Tn}\sqrt{2\pi}}{a_{i0}}

\displaystyle\times\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\exp\left[-\ \frac{U_{x}^{2}}{2V_{Tn}^{2}}-\frac{\left(R_{i}-k_{x}U_{x}\right)^{2}}{2\left(k_{y}^{2}V_{Tn}^{2}+ik_{x}a_{i0}\right)}\ \right]dU_{x}

\displaystyle\times\int_{U_{x}}^{\infty}\exp\left[-\ \frac{k_{y}^{2}V_{Tn}^{2}+ik_{x}a_{i0}}{2a_{i0}^{2}}\left(V_{ix}-\frac{k_{y}^{2}V_{Tn}^{2}U_{x}+iR_{i}a_{i0}}{k_{y}^{2}V_{Tn}^{2}+ik_{x}a_{i0}}\right)^{2}\right]dV_{ix},

(137)

Then, in accord with the definition of the
w
-function given by
equation (
81
), the internal integral gives

\displaystyle\int_{U_{x}}^{+\infty}\exp\left[-\ \frac{k_{y}^{2}V_{Tn}^{2}+ik_{x}a_{i0}}{2a_{i0}^{2}}\left(V_{ix}-\frac{k_{y}^{2}V_{T}^{2}U_{x}+iR_{i}a_{i0}}{k_{y}^{2}V_{Tn}^{2}+ik_{x}a_{i0}}\right)^{2}\right]dV_{ix}

\displaystyle=a_{i0}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2(k_{y}^{2}V_{Tn}^{2}+ik_{x}a_{i0})}}\exp\left[\frac{\left(R_{i}-k_{x}U_{x}\right)^{2}}{2(k_{y}^{2}V_{Tn}^{2}+ik_{x}a_{i0})}\right]w\left[\frac{R_{i}-k_{x}U_{x}}{\sqrt{2(k_{y}^{2}V_{Tn}^{2}+ik_{x}a_{i0})}}\right].

Here, and elsewhere across the text, we choose the branch of the square
root with the positive real part. As a result, equation (
137
) yields

\displaystyle\iint_{-\infty}^{\infty}F_{\omega\vec{k}}^{(1)}(V_{ix},V_{iy})dV_{iy}dV_{ix}=\frac{\pi PV_{Tn}}{\sqrt{k_{y}^{2}V_{Tn}^{2}+ik_{x}a_{i0}}}

\displaystyle\times\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\exp\left(-\ \frac{U_{x}^{2}}{2V_{Tn}^{2}}\right)w\left[\frac{R_{i}-k_{x}U_{x}}{\sqrt{2(k_{y}^{2}V_{Tn}^{2}+ik_{x}a_{i0})}}\right]dU_{x}

(138)

To evaluate the remaining integral, we make the argument of the error function
a new integration variable

z=\frac{R_{i}-k_{x}U_{x}}{\sqrt{2(k_{y}^{2}V_{Tn}^{2}+ik_{x}a_{i0})}}

(139)

and then deform the integration path is such a way that it becomes the real
axis in the complex
z
-plane. Then equation (
138
) becomes

\displaystyle\iint_{-\infty}^{\infty}F_{\omega\vec{k}}^{(1)}(V_{ix},V_{iy})dV_{iy}dV_{ix}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{2}\,\pi PV_{Tn}}{k_{x}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\exp\left[-\ \frac{k_{y}^{2}V_{Tn}^{2}+ik_{x}a_{i0}}{k_{x}^{2}V_{Tn}^{2}}\left(z-\frac{R_{i}}{\sqrt{2(k_{y}^{2}V_{Tn}^{2}+ik_{x}a_{i0})}}\right)^{2}\right]w(z)dz.

(140)

Designating

\frac{k_{y}^{2}V_{Tn}^{2}+ik_{x}a_{i0}}{k_{x}^{2}V_{Tn}^{2}}=A^{2}-1,\qquad\frac{R_{i}}{\sqrt{2(k_{y}^{2}V_{Tn}^{2}+ik_{x}a_{i0})}}=\frac{AB}{A^{2}-1},

we obtain

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{k^{2}V_{Tn}^{2}+ik_{x}a_{i0}}}{|k_{x}|V_{Tn}},

(141a)

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle=\frac{R_{i}}{|k_{x}|V_{Tn}}\sqrt{\frac{k_{y}^{2}V_{Tn}^{2}+ik_{x}a_{i0}}{2(k^{2}V_{Tn}^{2}+ik_{x}a_{i0})}},

(141b)

where
k^{2}=k_{x}^{2}+k_{y}^{2}
(recall that in the assumed coordinate
system
k_{z}=0
). Now we can apply to equation (
140
) the integral
relation given by equation (
123
). Then, recalling the definition of
P
given by equation (
133
), we
obtain

\iint_{-\infty}^{\infty}F_{\omega\vec{k}}^{(1)}dV_{ix}dV_{iy}=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}\,\nu_{in}n_{0}\eta_{\omega\vec{k}}^{i}}{\sqrt{2(k^{2}V_{Tn}^{2}+i\vec{k}\cdot\vec{a}_{i0})}}\ w\left(\frac{R_{i}}{\sqrt{2(k^{2}V_{Tn}^{2}+i\vec{k}\cdot\vec{a}_{i0})}}\right),

(142)

where we have returned to the coordinate-invariant format,
k_{x}a_{i0}=\vec{k}\cdot\vec{a}_{i0}
. In the limit of
\vec{a}_{i0}\rightarrow 0
,
equation (
142
) reduces to
equation (
134
).

Now we outline the solution for
F_{\omega\vec{k}}^{(1)}
in the opposite case of
\operatorname{Re}(R_{i})<0
,
corresponding to
\gamma<-\nu_{in}
. Although this case may never be realized for any linear waves,
we need to include it for completeness of the wave dispersion relation.

The major difference from the
\operatorname{Re}(R_{i})>0
case is that if
\operatorname{Re}(R_{i})<0

then equation (
132
) should be solved using a different
asymptotic boundary condition, namely
F_{\omega\vec{k}}^{(1)}\rightarrow 0
as
V_{x}\rightarrow\infty
.
After making the same steps that lead to equation (
136
), we arrive now at a slightly modified solution,

\displaystyle\iint_{-\infty}^{\infty}F_{\omega\vec{k}}^{(1)}(V_{ix},V_{iy})dV_{iy}dV_{ix}=-\ \frac{PV_{Tn}\sqrt{2\pi}}{a_{i0}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dU_{x}\exp\left(-\ \frac{U_{x}^{2}}{2V_{Tn}^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\times\int_{-\infty}^{U_{x}}\exp\left[-\ \frac{k_{y}^{2}V_{Tn}^{2}\left(V_{ix}-U_{x}\right)^{2}}{2a_{i0}^{2}}-\frac{ik_{x}\left(V_{ix}^{2}-U_{x}^{2}\right)}{2a_{i0}}+\frac{i\left(V_{ix}-U_{x}\right)R_{i}}{a_{i0}}\right]dV_{ix}.

(143)

In this equation, the integral
\int_{U_{x}}^{\infty}(\cdots)dV_{ix}
from equation (
136
)
is replaced by the same integral, but with different integration limits,
-\,\int_{-\infty}^{U_{x}}(\cdots)dV_{ix}
. Making the
same steps as described above for the
\operatorname{Re}(R_{i})>0
case, we arrive eventually at an expression

\iint_{-\infty}^{\infty}F_{\omega\vec{k}}^{(1)}dV_{ix}dV_{iy}=-\ \frac{\sqrt{\pi}\,\nu_{in}n_{0}\eta_{\omega\vec{k}}^{i}}{\sqrt{2(k^{2}V_{Tn}^{2}+i\vec{k}\cdot\vec{a}_{i0})}}\ w\left(-\ \frac{R_{i}}{\sqrt{2(k^{2}V_{Tn}^{2}+i\vec{k}\cdot\vec{a}_{i0})}}\right)

(144)

where the function
w(z)
in the RHS of equation (
142
) is replaced by
-w(-z)
;
recall that
-w(-z)=w(z)-2\exp(-z^{2})
. The existence of two different functional expressions,
depending on the sign of
\operatorname{Re}(R_{i})
, is the same as in equations (
100
)
and (
124
), although the latter has a different parameter condition that separates them.

A.4
Solving for
F_{\omega\vec{k}}^{(2)}
and evaluating its
contribution to
\eta_{\omega\vec{k}}^{i}

Here we find the solution for
F_{\omega\vec{k}}^{(2)}
and evaluate the
corresponding contribution to
\eta_{\omega\vec{k}}^{i}
. Similarly to the
above treatment for
F_{\omega\vec{k}}^{(1)}
, we provide detailed calculations
for the major case of
\operatorname{Re}(R_{i})>0
(i.e.,
\gamma>-\nu_{in}
), and then present the
result for the heavily damped waves of
\operatorname{Re}(R_{i})<0
(
\gamma<-\nu_{in}
).

Equation (
13b
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\frac{\partial F_{\omega\vec{k}}^{(2)}}{\partial V_{ix}}+\frac{i}{a_{i0}}(k_{x}V_{ix}+k_{y}V_{iy}\mathbf{-}R_{i})F_{\omega\vec{k}}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=\frac{iV_{Tn}^{2}}{\beta_{T}a_{i0}}\ \phi_{\omega\vec{k}}\left(k_{x}\ \frac{\partial}{\partial V_{ix}}+k_{y}\ \frac{\partial}{\partial V_{iy}}\right)\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f_{i0}dV_{iz},

(145)

where, according to Eq. (
5
),

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f_{i0}dV_{iz}=\frac{n_{0}}{2\pi V_{\parallel}V_{\perp}}\exp\left(-~\frac{(V_{ix}-U_{0})^{2}}{2V_{\parallel}^{2}}-\frac{V_{iy}^{2}}{2V_{\perp}^{2}}\right).

(146)

Temporarily denoting the RHS of Eq. (
145
) by

R(V_{ix})
,

R(V_{ix})\equiv\frac{iV_{Tn}^{2}}{\beta_{T}a_{i0}}\ \phi_{\omega\vec{k}}\left(k_{x}\ \frac{\partial}{\partial V_{ix}}+k_{y}\ \frac{\partial}{\partial V_{iy}}\right)\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f_{i0}dV_{iz},

we obtain the solution for
F_{\omega\vec{k}}^{(2)}
,

\displaystyle F_{\omega\vec{k}}^{(2)}(V_{ix})=\exp\left\{-\ \frac{i}{a_{i0}}\left[\frac{k_{x}V_{ix}^{2}}{2}+(k_{y}V_{iy}\mathbf{-}R_{i})V_{ix}\right]\right\}

\displaystyle\times\int_{-\infty}^{V_{ix}}R(U_{x})\exp\left\{\frac{i}{a_{i0}}\left[\frac{k_{x}U_{x}^{2}}{2}+(k_{y}V_{iy}\mathbf{-}R_{i})U_{x}\right]\right\}dU_{x},

that satisfies the boundary condition of
\lim_{V_{ix}\rightarrow-\infty}F_{\omega\vec{k}}^{(2)}(V_{ix})=0
.

In order to find
F_{\omega\vec{k}}^{(2)}
, we need to solve differential
equation (
13b
), where
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f_{i0}dV_{iz}
is
given by equation (
6
). Under assumption of
\lim_{V_{ix}\rightarrow-\infty}F_{\omega\vec{k}}^{(2)}=0
, the formal solution can
be written as

\displaystyle F_{\omega\vec{k}}^{(2)}=Q\int_{-\infty}^{V_{ix}}dU_{x}\left(\frac{k_{x}(U_{x}-U_{0})}{V_{\parallel}^{2}}+\frac{k_{y}V_{iy}}{V_{\perp}^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\times\exp\left\{-~\frac{(U_{x}-U_{0})^{2}}{2V_{\parallel}^{2}}-\frac{V_{iy}^{2}}{2V_{\perp}^{2}}+\frac{i}{a_{i0}}\left[\frac{k_{x}\left(U_{x}^{2}-V_{ix}^{2}\right)}{2}+(k_{y}V_{iy}-R_{i})\left(U_{x}-V_{ix}\right)\right]\right\},

(147)

where

Q\equiv-\ \frac{iV_{Tn}^{2}n_{0}\phi_{\omega\vec{k}}}{2\pi\beta_{T}a_{i0}V_{\parallel}V_{\perp}}.

(148)

To obtain the ion contribution to the linear dispersion relations, we need to
integrate
F_{\omega\vec{k}}^{(2)}
over the two remaining components in the
ion velocity space (
V_{ix}
and
V_{iy}
). First, we integrate
F_{\omega\vec{k}}^{(2)}
over
V_{iy}
. This straightforward integration yields

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}F_{\omega\vec{k}}^{(2)}dV_{iy}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2\pi}V_{T}Q\int_{-\infty}^{V_{ix}}dU_{x}\left[\frac{k_{x}(U_{x}-U_{0})}{V_{\parallel}^{2}}+\frac{ik_{y}^{2}\left(U_{x}-V_{ix}\right)}{a_{i0}}\right]

(149)

\displaystyle\times\exp\left\{-~\frac{(U_{x}-U_{0})^{2}}{2V_{\parallel}^{2}}+\frac{i}{a_{i0}}\left[\frac{k_{x}\left(U_{x}^{2}-V_{ix}^{2}\right)}{2}-R_{i}\left(U_{x}-V_{ix}\right)\right]-\frac{k_{y}^{2}V_{\perp}^{2}\left(U_{x}-V_{ix}\right)^{2}}{2a_{i0}^{2}}\right\}.

Second, we need to integrate this expression over
V_{ix}
. This will be a
multi-step procedure.

We start by introducing an intermediate quantity

\displaystyle M\equiv\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dV_{ix}\int_{-\infty}^{V_{ix}}dU_{x}\left[\frac{k_{x}(U_{x}-U_{0})}{V_{\parallel}^{2}}+\frac{ik_{y}^{2}\left(U_{x}-V_{ix}\right)}{a_{i0}}\right]

\displaystyle\times\exp\left\{-~\frac{(U_{x}-U_{0})^{2}}{2V_{\parallel}^{2}}+\frac{i}{a_{i0}}\left[\frac{k_{x}\left(U_{x}^{2}-V_{ix}^{2}\right)}{2}-R_{i}\left(U_{x}-V_{ix}\right)\right]-\frac{k_{y}^{2}V_{\perp}^{2}\left(U_{x}-V_{ix}\right)^{2}}{2a_{i0}^{2}}\right\},

so that

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}F_{\omega\vec{k}}^{(2)}dV_{ix}dV_{iy}=\sqrt{2\pi}\,V_{T}QM.

(150)

In order to simplify further calculations, we swap the order of integration
between
V_{ix}
and
U_{x}
,

\displaystyle M=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dU_{x}\exp\left[-~\frac{(U_{x}-U_{0})^{2}}{2V_{\parallel}^{2}}\right]\int_{U_{x}}^{\infty}dV_{ix}\left[\frac{k_{x}(U_{x}-U_{0})}{V_{\parallel}^{2}}+\frac{ik_{y}^{2}}{a_{i0}}\left(U_{x}-V_{ix}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\times\exp\left\{-\ \frac{k_{y}^{2}V_{\perp}^{2}}{2a_{i0}^{2}}\left(U_{x}-V_{ix}\right)^{2}+\frac{i}{a_{i0}}\left[\frac{k_{x}\left(U_{x}^{2}-V_{ix}^{2}\right)}{2}-R_{i}\left(U_{x}-V_{ix}\right)\right]\right\}.

(151)

Then, redistributing the terms inside both the pre-exponent and the exponents,
we rewrite equation (
151
) as

\displaystyle M=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dU_{x}\exp\left[-~\frac{(U_{x}-U_{0})^{2}}{2V_{\parallel}^{2}}-\frac{(R_{i}-k_{x}U_{x})^{2}}{2(k_{y}^{2}V_{\perp}^{2}+ik_{x}a_{i0})}\right]\int_{U_{x}}^{\infty}dV_{ix}

\displaystyle\times\left[\frac{k_{x}(U_{x}-U_{0})}{V_{\parallel}^{2}}+\frac{k_{y}^{2}(R_{i}-k_{x}U_{x})}{k_{y}^{2}V_{\perp}^{2}+ik_{x}a_{i0}}-\frac{ik_{y}^{2}}{a_{i0}}\left(V_{ix}-\frac{k_{y}^{2}V_{\perp}^{2}U_{x}+iR_{i}a_{i0}}{V_{\perp}^{2}k_{y}^{2}+ik_{x}a_{i0}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\times\exp\left[-\ \frac{k_{y}^{2}V_{\perp}^{2}+ik_{x}a_{i0}}{2a_{i0}^{2}}\left(V_{ix}-\frac{k_{y}^{2}V_{\perp}^{2}U_{x}+iR_{i}a_{i0}}{k_{y}^{2}V_{\perp}^{2}+ik_{x}a_{i0}}\right)^{2}\right].

(152)

This simplifies evaluating the internal integral over
V_{ix}
, so that we
obtain

M=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\exp\left[-~\frac{(U_{x}-U_{0})^{2}}{2V_{\parallel}^{2}}\right]K(U_{x})dU_{x},

(153)

where

\displaystyle K(U_{x})

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{\sqrt{\pi}\,a_{i0}}{\sqrt{2(k_{y}^{2}V_{\perp}^{2}+ik_{x}a_{i0})}}\left[\frac{k_{x}(U_{x}-U_{0})}{V_{\parallel}^{2}}+\frac{k_{y}^{2}(R_{i}-k_{x}U_{x})}{V_{\perp}^{2}k_{y}^{2}+ik_{x}a_{i0}}\right]

\displaystyle\times\ w\left[\frac{R_{i}-k_{x}U_{x}}{\sqrt{2(k_{y}^{2}V_{\perp}^{2}+ik_{x}a_{i0})}}\right]-\frac{ik_{y}^{2}a_{i0}}{k_{y}^{2}V_{\perp}^{2}+ik_{x}a_{i0}}.

To calculate the remaining integral over
U_{x}
, we make the complex argument
of the
w
-function a new integration variable,

z=\frac{R_{i}-k_{x}U_{x}}{\sqrt{2(k_{y}^{2}V_{\perp}^{2}+ik_{x}a_{i0})}}.

This transforms equation (
153
) to

\displaystyle M

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{2\pi}a_{i0}}{k_{x}V_{\parallel}^{2}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left[\frac{k_{y}^{2}(V_{\parallel}^{2}-V_{\perp}^{2})-ik_{x}a_{i0}}{\sqrt{k_{y}^{2}V_{\perp}^{2}+ik_{x}a_{i0}}}\ z+\frac{R_{i}-k_{x}U_{0}}{\sqrt{2}}\right]

\displaystyle\times\exp\left\{-~\frac{k_{y}^{2}V_{\perp}^{2}+ik_{x}a_{i0}}{k_{x}^{2}V_{\parallel}^{2}}\left[z-\frac{R_{i}-k_{x}U_{0}}{\sqrt{2(k_{y}^{2}V_{\perp}^{2}+ik_{x}a_{i0})}}\right]^{2}\right\}w(z)dz

(154)

\displaystyle-\frac{i\sqrt{2\pi}\,k_{y}^{2}a_{i0}V_{\parallel}}{k_{y}^{2}V_{\perp}^{2}+ik_{x}a_{i0}},

where, in a usual way, assuming the good analytic behavior of the integrand
and its integrability at infinities, we have reduced the new integration path
in the complex
z
-plane to its real axis.

Introducing temporary parameters

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{k_{x}^{2}V_{\parallel}^{2}+k_{y}^{2}V_{\perp}^{2}+ik_{x}a_{i0}}}{k_{x}V_{\parallel}},

(155a)

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle=\frac{R_{i}-k_{x}U_{0}}{k_{x}V_{\parallel}}\sqrt{\frac{k_{y}^{2}V_{\perp}^{2}+ik_{x}a_{i0}}{2(k_{x}^{2}V_{\parallel}^{2}+k_{y}^{2}V_{\perp}^{2}+ik_{x}a_{i0})}},

(155b)

we have

\frac{k_{y}^{2}V_{\perp}^{2}+ik_{x}a_{i0}}{k_{x}^{2}V_{\parallel}^{2}}=A^{2}-1,\qquad\frac{R_{i}-k_{x}U_{0}}{\sqrt{2(k_{y}^{2}V_{\perp}^{2}+ik_{x}a_{i0})}}=\frac{AB}{A^{2}-1}.

so that we now can apply to (
154
) equations (
123
) and (
130
).
After a straightforward algebra and by using equation (
150
), we
arrive at the sought-for result

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\iint\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}}F_{\omega\vec{k}}^{(2)}dV_{ix}dV_{iy}=-\ \frac{k^{2}V_{Tn}^{2}n_{0}\phi_{\omega k}}{\beta_{T}(k_{\parallel}^{2}V_{\parallel}^{2}+k_{\perp}^{2}V_{\perp}^{2}+i\vec{k}\cdot\vec{a}_{i0})}

\displaystyle\times\left[1+\frac{i\sqrt{\pi}(R_{i}-\vec{k}\cdot\vec{U}_{0})}{\sqrt{2(k_{\parallel}^{2}V_{\parallel}^{2}+k_{\perp}^{2}V_{\perp}^{2}+i\vec{k}\cdot\vec{a}_{i0})}}\ w\left(\frac{R_{i}-\vec{k}\cdot\vec{U}_{0}}{\sqrt{2(k_{\parallel}^{2}V_{\parallel}^{2}+k_{\perp}^{2}V_{\perp}^{2}+i\vec{k}\cdot\vec{a}_{i0})}}\right)\right],

(156)

where, similarly to equation (
142
), we have expressed the
result in to the coordinate-invariant form,
k_{x}=k_{\parallel}
,

k_{y}=k_{\perp}
, and recalled that

\vec{U}_{0}=\frac{\vec{a}_{i0}}{\nu_{in}}

(157)

For
V_{\parallel}^{2}=V_{\perp}^{2}=V_{Tn}^{2}
,
\vec{a}_{i0}=0
,
\vec{U}_{0}=0
,
equation (
156
) reduces to

{\displaystyle\iint\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}}F_{\omega\vec{k}}^{(2)}dV_{ix}dV_{iy}=-\ \frac{n_{0}\phi_{\omega k}}{\beta_{T}}\left[1+\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}\ \frac{iR_{i}}{kV_{Tn}}\ w\left(\frac{R_{i}}{\sqrt{2}kV_{Tn}}\right)\right],

(158)

in full agreement with the corresponding no-field expression.

The non-standard case of heavily damped waves,
\gamma<-\nu_{in}
, i.e., of
\operatorname{Re}(R_{i})<0
,
for
F_{\omega\vec{k}}^{(2)}
is fully analogous to that of
F_{\omega\vec{k}}^{(1)}
.
Using the same arguments that led us to equation (
143
), instead
of equation (
147
) we obtain now the solution

\displaystyle F_{\omega\vec{k}}^{(2)}=-Q\int_{V_{ix}}^{\infty}dU_{x}\left(\frac{k_{x}(U_{x}-U_{0})}{V_{\parallel}^{2}}+\frac{k_{y}V_{iy}}{V_{\perp}^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\times\exp\left\{-~\frac{(U_{x}-U_{0})^{2}}{2V_{\parallel}^{2}}-\frac{V_{iy}^{2}}{2V_{\perp}^{2}}+\frac{i}{a_{i0}}\left[\frac{k_{x}\left(U_{x}^{2}-V_{ix}^{2}\right)}{2}+(k_{y}V_{iy}-R_{i})\left(U_{x}-V_{ix}\right)\right]\right\},

which differs from equation (
147
) by the integration limits and the sign in front of
Q
.
As a result, making the same further steps that led us to equation (
