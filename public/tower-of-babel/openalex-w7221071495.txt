Abstract
The rapid rise of antimicrobial resistance, especially in Staphylococcus aureus, highlights the urgent need for improved
antibacterial agents. In 2019 alone, AMR caused an estimated 1.27 million deaths and contributed to nearly 5 million more
globally. Here, we present the design, synthesis, and testing of modiﬁed ciproﬂoxacin analogs bearing nitrofuran, furan, and
spirocyclic diamine moieties. Among these compounds, two showed a more than eight-fold increase in potency against
ciproﬂoxacin-resistant S.aureus ISR14-002 compared to ciproﬂoxacin, demonstrating improved activity against a
ciproﬂoxacin-resistant strain. In vivo testing using a Galleria mellonella infection model with S. aureus SG511
demonstrated that compound 12 conferred a survival beneﬁt comparable to ciproﬂoxacin. Improved activity was most
notable against Gram-positive bacteria, especially our target pathogen S. aureus. However, the spectrum of organism
selectivity varied by analog, highlighting the effects of focused structural modiﬁcation. These results suggest that the
ﬂuoroquinolone scaffold can be modiﬁed to circumvent ciproﬂoxacin resistance and expand its spectrum of activity, while
retaining acceptable cellular toxicity.

all classes of antibiotics used against it, highlighting the

Ciproﬂoxacin (1, Fig. 1) is a second-generation
ﬂuoroquinolone widely used to treat a broad range of
bacterial infections, including those affecting the
respiratory tract, urinary tract, skin, and soft tissues [8,
9]. Its primary mechanism of action is to inhibit bacterial
DNA gyrase (topoisomerase II) and topoisomerase IV,
enzymes essential for DNA replication, transcription, and
repair, leading to bacterial cell death [10]. Ciproﬂoxacin
exhibits notable activity against numerous Gram-negative
bacteria and demonstrates efﬁcacy against several Grampositive pathogens, including S. aureus and Enterococcus
faecalis. Despite its effectiveness, the emergence of
resistance has prompted ongoing research into ciproﬂoxacin analogs with improved potency and resistance
proﬁles [11–13].

The antibacterial efﬁcacy of ﬂuoroquinolones is strongly
inﬂuenced by targeted modiﬁcations to the core quinolone
scaffold 2 (Fig. 1). Substitution at the C7 position with
bulky heterocycles, such as piperazinyl (1) or pyridinyl
groups, has been shown to enhance Gram-positive bacterial
penetration and increase DNA gyrase binding afﬁnity [14].
Incorporation of the bicyclic 2,8-diazabicyclo[4.3.0]nonane
moiety, as seen in Bay y 3114 (3) and the fourth-generation
ﬂuoroquinolone moxiﬂoxacin (4), confers increased
potency against Gram-positive pathogens, like S. aureus, up
to 4-fold relative to ciproﬂoxacin (1, Fig. 1) [15]. Past
studies have similarly demonstrated that introducing spirocyclic groups at C7 (5) can yield derivatives with
improved antibacterial activity, with some compounds
exhibiting comparable or superior efﬁcacy to ciproﬂoxacin
against both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria,
including notable activity against Acinetobacter baumannii

and Bacillus cereus [15, 16]. Foroumadi et al. have extensively investigated aromatic nitroﬂuoroquinolones containing thiadiazole linkers, such as compound 6, which display
broad-spectrum antibacterial activity and typically enhanced
potency against Gram-positive organisms [17–20]. Furthermore, Saeed Emami et al. reported ciproﬂoxacin

demonstrated activity against a range of bacterial strains
[21–23].
Nitrofuran-containing drugs, such as nitrofurantoin
(Fig. 1), are valued for their broad-spectrum antibacterial
activity, which is primarily attributed to a nitroreductasemediated free radical generation that damages bacterial
DNA and proteins [24]. Notably, these drugs maintain
efﬁcacy against multidrug-resistant Gram-positive pathogens, including methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA) and
vancomycin-resistant Enterococci (VRE) [25–27]. The
multi-targeted mechanism of nitrofurans, which includes
both DNA damage and protein inactivation, is thought to
reduce the likelihood of resistance development compared
to single-target antibiotics [28, 29]. Previously, we reported
on ND-7901 [30], a nitrofuran-benzimidazole, which
exhibited impressive potency against drug-resistant S. aureus (MICs of 1-2 µg/mL against 9 MRSA strains) and a
spectrum of activity biased towards Gram-positive organisms (Fig. 1).
To that end, we set out to create nitrofuran-ciproﬂoxacin
hybrids (2) under the hypothesis that combining those two
functionalities could improve potency and alter the spectrum of biological activity as exempliﬁed by compounds 6
and 7. The design of ciproﬂoxacin hybrid molecules has
precedent in the literature [13], especially with triazines
[31, 32], and other drug-like heterocycles such as pyrazole
[33], chalcone [34], and quinoline, but incorporating a

reducible moiety such as the nitrofuran has been explored
less extensively.
In this disclosure, we present the syntheses and biological evaluation of nitrofuran-ciproﬂoxacin hybrids (compounds 9 and 11, Fig. 2), and their efﬁcacy against Grampositive, Gram-negative, and ciproﬂoxacin-resistant bacterial infections. Additionally, we sought to determine the
importance of the nitro functionality by preparing the
spartan furan analogs (compounds 8 and 10, Fig. 2). Next,
we report a novel spirocyclic diamine substitution at the C7
position and assess its impact on antibacterial activity.
These hybrid molecules aim to overcome resistance in
clinically signiﬁcant bacterial pathogens by combining the
broad-spectrum potency of ﬂuoroquinolones with the multifaceted mechanisms of nitrofurans.

HepG2 cytotoxicity assay
The cytotoxicity of the novel nitrofuran-containing compounds
was evaluated by assessing cell viability in HepG2 cells
(Table3). Experiments were conducted under both +glucose
conditions, to assess drug target-speciﬁc toxicity, and +galactose conditions, which force cells to rely on mitochondrial
oxidative phosphorylation, thereby revealing mitochondrialspeciﬁc toxicity [35]. Cellular damage was expressed as growth
inhibition (%) where higher values indicate greater impairment
(Table 3). Under these conditions, several tested compounds
showed comparable or lower toxicity toward the HepG2 cell
line relative to ciproﬂoxacin.

Discussion
Our study demonstrates the signiﬁcant antibacterial activity
of novel ciproﬂoxacin analogs containing a nitrofuran
moiety against a range of bacterial pathogens. These compounds show promise in addressing the growing issue of
antibiotic resistance, offering a potential new line of defense
against multidrug-resistant strains. In addition, we demonstrated the utility of the wax worm model to evaluate the in
vivo efﬁcacy of these compounds against invasive strains of
S. aureus.
As shown in Tables 1a, b and 2b, the ﬁve compounds
synthesized in this study exhibited promising antibacterial
activity against a panel of clinically relevant Gram-positive
and Gram-negative pathogens, including drug-resistant
isolates. As designed, the study also reveals important

ciproﬂoxacin core (compound 9) enhanced activity eightfold against S. aureus ISR14-002 (Table 1a) and over tenfold against S. aureus SG511 (Table 2b). Compound 9 also
showed signiﬁcant activity against the ciproﬂoxacinresistant strains E. faecalis ATCC 49432, B. cenocepacia

addition of the simple furan moiety (8) gave no increase in

potency as compared to ciproﬂoxacin. While spirocyclic
substitution alone (compound 12) did not improve potency
compared to ciproﬂoxacin against the pathogens in
Table 1a, its combination with a nitrofuran group (11)
enhanced activity with a sixteen-fold increase in potency
against S. aureus ISR14-002 compared to the parent spirocyclic amine and the furan derivative (10). To investigate
whether this activity is due to the attachment of the
nitrofuran-ciproﬂoxacin hybrid alone (11), a 1:1 mixture of
ciproﬂoxacin and 5-nitrofuran-2-carboxylic acid (17) was
tested against all the strains, including S. aureus ISR14-002,
and the mixture gave equivalent MIC results to ciproﬂoxacin itself. Additionally, 5-nitrofuran-2-carboxylic acid
(17) was tested alone and showed an MIC of >25 µM
against all strains. This underscores the importance of
incorporating the nitrofuran moiety into the ciproﬂoxacin
scaffold when optimizing efﬁcacy against drug-resistant S.
aureus. That is, the hybrid (11) was much more potent than
the sum of its parts.
Interestingly, the incorporation of a nitro-furan did not
improve the activity proﬁle across all species. In N.
meningitidis and N. gonorrhoeae, incorporation of either
furan or nitrofuran moieties, as well as spirocyclic substitution, all led to diminished antibacterial potency

compared to ciproﬂoxacin. These results highlight the
importance of pathogen-speciﬁc structure-activity relationships and suggest that, while nitrofuran substitution may
enhance activity in certain Gram-positive organisms, its
beneﬁt may not extend to all targeted bacteria. We hypothesize that, as suggested by Le and Rakonjac [24], these
differences may be related to species-speciﬁc reductive
activation of the nitrofuran moiety, although this mechanism has not yet been experimentally validated in the present
study. Further optimization will require balancing broadspectrum efﬁcacy with targeted structural modiﬁcations.
Compound 12 demonstrated a survival beneﬁt in the
Galleria model against S. aureus SG511, but not against the
ciproﬂoxacin-resistant strain ISR14-002 under the singledose conditions tested. Compound 11 exhibited a comparable MIC value as compound 12 against S. aureus SG511
but did not demonstrate a comparable survival beneﬁt in the
Galleria infection model. This difference may reﬂect differences in the pharmacokinetic properties of the compounds; however, evaluation of these properties was
beyond the scope of the present study. Further dose-ranging
and pharmacokinetic studies are warranted to better understand the relationship between in vitro antibacterial activity
and in vivo efﬁcacy. In addition, studies using optimized
dosing regimens will be needed to determine whether the
observed in vitro activity can be translated into in vivo
efﬁcacy against resistant infections.
In conclusion, we have demonstrated that the introduction of a nitrofuran moiety to ciproﬂoxacin results in an
eight to sixteen-fold increase in potency against the drugresistant S. aureus strain ISR14-002, as measured by MIC
assay (for compounds 9 and 11 respectively). This incorporation of nitrofuran also signiﬁcantly enhanced MIC
activity against E. faecalis ATCC 49432, B. cenocepacia
AU0062, and B. dolosa AU0018, strains that were essentially resistant to ciproﬂoxacin. Importantly, this modiﬁcation did not enhance potency against other bacterial
species tested, including N. meningitidis and N. gonorrhoeae, indicating a selective effect for Gram-positive
pathogens S. aureus and E. faecalis and Gram-negative
Burkholderia spp. Additionally, the incorporation of a
novel spirocyclic amine substituent into the ciproﬂoxacin
scaffold was unprecedented in the literature, and the
resulting compound retained antibacterial activity. These
ﬁndings highlight the potential of targeted structural
modiﬁcations to improve the efﬁcacy and selectivity of
ﬂuoroquinolone antibiotics, supporting further medicinal
chemistry efforts to address the urgent need for new therapeutic strategies against resistant bacterial pathogens. Our
future research will prioritize in vivo evaluation to assess
the pharmacokinetics, toxicity, and therapeutic efﬁcacy of
the most promising novel ciproﬂoxacin analogs against
drug-resistant strains.

Materials and methods
General methods
All anhydrous solvents, reagent grade solvents for chromatography and starting materials were purchased from
either Aldrich Chemical Co. (Milwaukee, WI) or Fisher
Scientiﬁc (Suwanee, GA) unless otherwise noted. General
methods of compound puriﬁcation involved the use of silica
(SiO2) purchased from Sorbtec (Norcross, GA) and/or
recrystallization. The reactions were monitored by TLC on
precoated Merck 60 F254 silica gel plates and visualized
using UV light (254 nm). All compounds were analyzed for
purity by HPLC and characterized by 1H and 13C NMR
spectroscopy using a Bruker Ascend AvanceNeo Spectrometer (actively shielded) (400 MHz) and/or Bruker Avance
III NMR Spectrometer (actively shielded) (600 MHz).
Chemical shifts are reported in ppm (δ) relative to the
residual solvent peak in the corresponding spectra; dimethyl
sulfoxide δ 2.50 and δ 39.53 ± 0.05 and coupling constants
(J) are reported in hertz (Hz) (where, s = singlet, bs =
broad singlet, d = doublet, dd = double doublet, t = triplet,
q = quartet, m = multiplet) and analyzed using ACD NMR
data processing. 19F NMR spectra were run without a
standard and are uncorrected. Mass spectra values are
reported as m/z.

LC-MS method
Liquid Chromatography-Mass Spectrometry was performed
on an Agilent 1290 inﬁnity coupled to Agilent 6538 Ultra
High-Deﬁnition Quadrupole Time of Flight (UHD-QToF)
instrument. Separations were achieved by using a reverse
phase Waters Acquity UPLC HSS T3 1.8 µm (2.1 X
100 mm) column from Waters (Milford, USA). The LCMS
Optima grade solvents were purchased from Fischer Scientiﬁc. Water containing 0.1% formic acid was used as
mobile phase A and acetonitrile containing 0.1% formic
acid was used as mobile phase B. The injection volume was
set at 1 µL. Samples were injected in a gradient of 95%
mobile phase A and 5% mobile phase B as the initial
condition to 5% mobile phase A and 95% mobile phase B in
9 minutes. The ratio was held at that composition for an
additional 3 min and switched back to the initial condition
at 12 min.
The MS conditions were as follows: The MS data
acquisition was performed from 50-1000 m/z at a 1.0 spectra/sec scan rate. The source gas temperature was set at 350
°C with a ﬂow of 8 l/min. The nebulizer gas was set at
55 psig. The capillary voltage was set at 3500 volts with
fragmentor at 100, skimmer at 45 and octopole RF 500
volts. Prior to sample runs, the instrument was calibrated
using Agilent low mass calibrant solution. The data

Synthesis and characterization of analogs
General procedure A (SNAr reaction)
To a stirred solution of halogen (0.6 mmol, 1 equiv.) and
amine (0.6 mmol, 1 equiv.) in DMSO was added Et3N
(1.8 mmol, 3 equiv.). The resulting solution was heated to
100 ˚C (oil bath) and stirred until the reaction was complete
as determined by TLC. The resulting solution was subsequently concentrated in vacuo, and the resulting solid was
diluted with CH2Cl2, washed with a 2% aqueous acetic acid
solution (twice), water, saturated aqueous NaHCO3 solution
(twice), brine, dried over Na2SO4, ﬁltered, and concentrated
in vacuo. The resulting solid was then puriﬁed by crystallization from hot CH3CN to yield the desired product.

General procedure B (EDC coupling)
To a sealed tube containing the amine (0.50 mmol, 1 equiv.)
in CH3CN was added the carboxylic acid (0.5 mmol, 1
equiv.), followed by EDC-HCl (0.6 mmol, 1.2 equiv.) and
DMAP (0.6 mmol, 1.2 equiv.), and the resulting solution
was stirred at 45 ˚C until the reaction was complete as
determined by TLC. The resulting solution was subsequently concentrated in vacuo, and the resulting solid was
dissolved in CH2Cl2, washed with a 2% aqueous acetic acid
solution (twice), water, a saturated aqueous NaHCO3 solution (twice) and brine. The resulting solution was subsequently dried over Na2SO4, ﬁltered, and concentrated in
vacuo. The resulting solid was then puriﬁed by crystallization from hot CH3CN to yield the desired product.

General procedure C (Boc deprotection)
To a stirred solution of Boc-protected amine (0.5 mmol, 1
equiv.) in CH2Cl2 was added TFA (5.0 mmol, 10 equiv.),
and the resulting solution was stirred until the reaction was
complete as determined by TLC, whereupon it was concentrated in vacuo. The resulting oil was dissolved in
CH2Cl2, and 10 M NaOH was added until the aqueous layer
became basic (pH >10). The resulting suspension was
stirred at rt for 30 min. The organic layer was subsequently
separated, washed with brine, dried over Na2SO4, ﬁltered,
and concentrated in vacuo. The resulting oil was puriﬁed by
column chromatography through SiO2 to yield the desired
product.

All compounds tested in this study were dissolved in
dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) (Sigma Aldrich, St. Louis,

Minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) analysis

Wax worms

Antibacterial activity of the compounds was determined by
measuring their minimum inhibitory concentrations (MIC)
using the broth microdilution method as described elsewhere [38, 39].

A U-100 BD Micro-Fine™ IV Insulin Syringe (Becton,
Dickinson and Company, USA) was loaded with the indicated concentration of bacteria without air in the syringe.
The syringe was loaded into a Dymax stepper (Cat. No.
T15469, Dymax Corporation, Torrington, CT, USA) with
the 10 µL setting engaged for injection.
Galleria mellonella (waxworms) larvae obtained from a
single vendor (Vanderhorst Wholesale Inc., Saint Marys,
Ohio) were evaluated and found to perform equivalently.
Larvae from a single lot were used for each experiment.
After receipt, larvae were allowed to acclimate for at least
24 h by storage in the dark at 20–22 °C and used within
10 days. After acclimation, the worms were screened for
uniformity in color and any worms with discoloration or
black spots were culled. Worms were selected in the 200300 mg weight range from the pool of pre-screened worms.
During injection, waxworms were gently restrained
between the thumb and foreﬁnger to minimize movement.
A 10 µL Hamilton syringe ﬁtted with a ﬁne-gauge needle
was used to inject bacterial suspensions into the lower right
proleg. The injection volume was dispensed by pressing the
syringe stepper, and the worm was slid off the needle using
the edge of the petri dish. This procedure was repeated for
all worms at each bacterial concentration. Wood shavings
were included in the petri dish during injection to absorb
hemolymph in cases of leakage. The compounds were
prepared in RPMI + 3.5% MOPS at a concentration of
500 µg/mL and drawn into an insulin syringe before the
syringe was loaded into the Dymax stepper. At three h post
infection, each larva was injected with 10 µL of treatment
(500 µg/mL), corresponding to a dose of 5.0 µg per worm,
following the same injection method used for infection [40].
This dose was selected based on our preliminary
compound-only in vivo testing (data not shown).
Following injection, larvae were incubated at 37 °C and
monitored daily for 10 days. Death was deﬁned as: (i) dark
pigmentation and lack of movement, or (ii) maintenance of
normal color with a rigid, straight posture unresponsive to
stimuli. To conﬁrm the latter, worms were placed on their
dorsal side on a ﬂat surface and observed for 1–2 min.
Worms that failed to right themselves, even after gentle
prodding of the ventral surface, were scored dead and
removed. Pupated worms were excluded from survival
analysis and removed from the assay.

Staphylococcus aureus, Pseudomonas aeruginosa,
and Acinetobacter baumannii growth and
susceptibility testing
MICs were determined using the broth microdilution
method in accordance with Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines. Brieﬂy, 96-well microtiter
plates were prepared with 50 μL of Mueller–Hinton (MH)
or modiﬁed MH (MH160) broth per well. Test compounds
were prepared in broth and added to the ﬁrst column of each
plate, followed by two-fold serial dilutions across the plate
to generate a concentration range (typically 25–0.125 μM,
unless otherwise speciﬁed). Ciproﬂoxacin was included as a
reference compound.
Overnight bacterial cultures were grown in appropriate
media at 37 °C and adjusted to a McFarland standard of 0.5
(~1.5 × 10^8 CFU/mL). Cultures were further diluted in fresh
broth to obtain a standardized inoculum, and 50 μL was added
to each well to yield a ﬁnal inoculum of approximately 1 ×
106 CFU/mL (ﬁnal volume 100 μL per well).
Plates were incubated at 37 °C for 18–24 h (up to 72 h
for selected slow-growing organisms). Bacterial growth was
assessed visually by turbidity, and MIC was deﬁned as the
lowest compound concentration that resulted in absence of
visible growth relative to untreated controls.

Neisseria spp. growth and susceptibility testing
Neisseria meningitidis and Neisseria gonorrhoeae strains
were stored at −80 °C and thawed at room temperature
prior to use. Strains were streaked onto blood agar plates
and incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ for 24 h (N. meningitidis) or 72 h (N. gonorrhoeae).
For MIC determination, overnight cultures were prepared in
2× trypticase soy broth and adjusted spectrophotometrically
(OD600) to standardized inoculum density. The inoculum was
diluted in fresh medium to a ﬁnal concentration of approximately 1 × 106 CFU/well. Compound activity was evaluated
over a concentration range of 0.03–30 μM. Plates were incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ for 18–24 h, and MIC endpoints
were determined visually as absence of visible growth.
Ciproﬂoxacin and uliﬂoxacin were used as reference ﬂuoroquinolones for comparison with the synthesized ciproﬂoxacin
analogs. Ceﬁxime and ceftriaxone were included as positive
control antibiotics for Neisseria spp. because they are clinically
used for the treatment of Neisseria infections.

HepG2 cytotoxicity assay
The HepG2 cell line was obtained from ATCC (Manassas,
VA, USA) and maintained in Dulbecco’s Modiﬁed Eagle
Medium [DMEM (1X): + glucose 4.5 g/L and – L-glutamine] (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) supplemented with

sodium pyruvate (100 mM) (Gibco, Grand Island, NY,
USA), and GlutaMAX (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) at
37 °C with 5.0% CO2. The XTT assay was conducted using
a CyQUANT™ XTT Cell Viability Assay Kit (Thermo
Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA) following the manufacturer’s
instructions. Single cell suspensions of HepG2 cells were
seeded on 96-well plates (Corning, Corning, NY, USA) at

XTT reagents were added after washing the plates with PBS
buffer then restoring the culture media. The plates were
incubated for an additional four h and read at 450 nm and
660 nm. The cellular cytotoxicity was calculated using the
equation [Abs450 nm (Test) – Abs450 nm (Blank)] –
Abs660 nm (Test). Cell viability was expressed as a percentage relative to untreated control wells (set to 100%)
using: % viability = (Test/Control) × 100, and growth
inhibition was calculated as 100 − % viability. All procedures were performed in a BSL2 biosafety cabinet.
