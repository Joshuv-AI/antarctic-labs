Multiphase Simulation of Dust-Temperature Distribution with Physicochemical Characterization of Metal Dust in Dust Collectors
Authors: Ya Chen, Yige Liu, Zhongan Jiang, Wei Ma, Zhihao Zhang, Fabin Zeng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Aluminum-magnesium (Al-Mg) alloy dusts, characterized by high chemical reactivity, present significant risks of occupational pneumoconiosis and dust explosions in wheel manufacturing. This study integrates experimental characterization with multiphase numerical simulations to investigate the process-dependent physicochemical properties and dynamic behaviors of Al-Mg dusts generated from machining (MA), smelting (SM), heat treatment (HT), and shot blasting (SB). Analyses of true density, BET surface area, SEM-EDS, and laser diffraction reveal distinct process-specific characteristics: HT dust exhibits the highest specific surface area (5.31 m2/g) and pronounced adsorption capacity, SB dust contains 9.19 wt.% carcinogenic Cr and the maximum true density (6.88 g/cm3), MA dust is predominantly composed of Al (77.92 wt.%), while SM and HT dusts are primarily Al-Mg-Na oxides. Particle size analysis indicates that 82–91% of particles exceed 10 μm, with those below 200 μm being explosible. Multiphase simulations elucidate strong couplings among particle size distribution, morphology, and dynamic behavior: MA dust (D[4,3] = 126.54 μm) deposits inertially; fine SM dust (D50 = 10.28 μm) is retained in inlet vortices; flaky SB dust exhibits a 25% reduction in settling velocity; and elliptical HT dust contributes to a cooler upper zone (413–415 K). These findings provide critical insights for optimizing worker protection strategies, dust explosion prevention, and dust collector design.

Introduction

The low-pressure casting of aluminum alloy wheel hubs involves a series of processesincluding smelting, casting, heat treatment, machining, and shot blastingthat generate fine metal dust. The particle size and physicochemical properties of this dust are significantly influenced by the design and operation of dust extraction systems. − Excessive dust accumulation not only accelerates equipment wear and shortens service life but also poses severe health risks to operators. Prolonged occupational exposure can lead to chronic bronchitis and pneumoconiosis, while specific heavy metals present in the dust exert distinct toxicological effects: chronic cadmium exposure increases risks of pulmonary malignancy, renal dysfunction, and skeletal damage; copper induces gastrointestinal distress and mucosal irritation in the eyes, mouth, and nose; manganese impairs neurological and respiratory systems; nickel is associated with chronic bronchitis, reduced lung function, and cancers of the lung and nasal sinus; lead exposure may cause headaches, abdominal pain, and neurobehavioral changes; and excessive zinc can hinder growth and reproductive function. −

Moreover, Al–Mg alloy dust exhibits high chemical reactivity and can undergo spontaneous combustion under certain conditions; contact with open flames or high-temperature sources may trigger violent combustion or dust explosions, resulting in casualties and substantial economic losses. − In recent years, there has been a marked increase in metal dust explosion incidents globally. − According to incomplete statistics from the China Industrial Accident Database (CIAD), 103 dust explosions were recorded in China between 2005 and 2025, involving metal, wood, food, and coal dust. As illustrated in Figure , metal dust explosions accounted for 46.4% of these incidentsfar exceeding other dust typesthus representing the primary category of dust-related accidents in China. It should be noted that these figures are based on reported cases and may not capture minor or unreported accidents. A critical distinction between metal and nonmetallic combustible dust lies in the former’s high calorific value and extremely low minimum ignition energy,18 enabling ignition even in oxygen-deficient atmospheres and resulting in more severe consequences such as higher temperatures, stronger shock waves, and toxic gas release. − Notable examples include the 2014 Kunshan aluminum dust explosion and the 2018 Beijing university magnesium dust explosion, both of which underscore the need for stringent preventive measures. ,

To mitigate the explosion risks associated with Al–Mg alloy dust, numerous studies have investigated the ignition, combustion, and explosion propagation mechanisms of such dust particles. , The explosion of Al–Mg alloy powder involves complex gas–liquid–solid multiphase reactions. For instance, Du et al. examined the ablation behavior of Al–Mg alloy powder under atmospheric and vacuum conditions; Chakravarti et al. compared the properties of nanoscale and microscale Al–Mg particles of identical composition; Liu et al. demonstrated that Al–Mg alloy dust enhances the agglomeration and combustion efficiency of solid composite propellants compared to pure aluminum dust; and Yao et al. systematically characterized the microstructure and thermal reaction behavior of Al–Mg alloy fuels, elucidating the structure of the oxide layer, oxidation kinetics, and combustion mechanisms.

In pursuit of enhanced industrial safety, international research institutions and enterprises have conducted extensive theoretical and experimental studies. − Based on typical characteristics and influencing factors of metal dust explosions, various suppression technologies have been developed, including inertization, − isolation, flame inhibition, − and explosion suppression agents. − These approaches function by altering dust–air mixing, blocking flame propagation, increasing ignition resistance, or absorbing energy released during reactions.

Numerical simulation has emerged as a key methodology for analyzing dust dynamics in industrial environments. , For example, Zhang et al. developed a numerical model that revealed mechanisms of dust agglomeration and sedimentation, providing a theoretical basis for improving dust removal efficiency. The research team led by Miller employed computational fluid dynamics (CFD) to simulate airflow and dust concentration distributions in workshops, clarifying the relationship between ventilation parameters and dust dispersion patterns. However, most existing studies focus primarily on particle trajectory analysis, with limited attention to the multiphase coupling of flow velocity, dust concentration, and temperature fieldsparticularly in ways that account for differences in the physicochemical properties of metal dust. This limitation hinders a comprehensive quantitative evaluation of dust collector performance.

Despite increased academic and industrial focus on metal dust management, significant knowledge gaps remain. Most existing studies have either examined dust properties from a single manufacturing process or conducted isolated risk assessments, failing to correlate process parameters with variations in dust properties across the entire wheel hub production chain. Moreover, the mechanistic links between intrinsic dust characteristicssuch as particle size distribution (PSD), chemical composition, specific surface area, and morphologyand dynamic behaviors within industrial dust collectors (e.g., vertical stratification, concentration–PSD coupling, and thermal zoning) are still poorly understood. This gap impedes the development of process-specific risk control strategies, as current protective measures often rely on generalized protocols that cannot adequately address process-dependent hazards.

To address these shortcomings, this study integrates experimental characterization with multiphase numerical simulation to systematically investigate metal dust originating from four key wheel hub manufacturing processes: machining (MA), smelting (SM), heat treatment (HT), and shot blasting (SB). Experimental techniques including true density measurement, BET specific surface area analysis, scanning electron microscopy with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (SEM-EDS), and laser diffraction were used to quantify dust physicochemical properties. In addition, computational fluid dynamics (CFD) coupled with the discrete phase model (DPM) was employed to simulate the coupled dust–temperature behavior in industrial collectors. The overarching goal is to establish a process-specific scientific framework for optimizing personal protective equipment, improving dust explosion prevention strategies, and enhancing environmental pollution controlthereby contributing to the safety and sustainability of wheel hub manufacturing.

Experiment

Materials and Instruments

Metal dust samples were collected directly from the dust collectors of four key manufacturing processes: machining (MA), smelting (SM), heat treatment (HT), and shot blasting (SB). The following instruments were employed for physicochemical characterization: a fully automated true density analyzer (3H-2000TD1, Beijing Best Instrument Technology Co., Ltd.), a specific surface area and pore size analyzer (3H-2000PS2, Beijing Best Instrument Technology Co., Ltd.), a high-resolution scanning electron microscope (SEM, ZEISS Sigma 360) coupled with an X-ray energy-dispersive spectrometer (EDS, ZEISS Sigma 560), and a laser diffraction particle size analyzer (Mastersizer 3000, Malvern Panalytical Ltd.). Experimental Methods

The physicochemical properties of the metal dust samplesincluding true density, specific surface area (SSA), pore size distribution, morphology, elemental composition, and particle size distribution (PSD)were characterized using a comprehensive set of analytical techniques. The overall experimental procedure is summarized in Figure . Detailed methodologies for each technique are provided below. All measurements were performed in triplicate (n = 3) to ensure reproducibility, with results expressed as the mean ± standard deviation.

Flowchart of the experimental methodology for characterizing metal dust physicochemical properties.

True Density Measurement

The true density of each sample was measured with a 3H-2000TD1 fully automated true density analyzer using the gas displacement method based on the ideal gas law. Three independent measurements were conducted per sample, and the reported values represent the arithmetic mean ± standard deviation.

Specific Surface Area (SSA) and Pore Structure Analysis

SSA and pore characteristics were evaluated from nitrogen adsorption–desorption isotherms recorded at 77 K using a 3H-2000PS2 analyzer. The SSA was determined via the multipoint Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) method within a relative pressure (P/P 0) range of 0.05–0.30, which ensures a linear BET region for reliable monolayer capacity estimation. − Pore size distributions were obtained from the adsorption branch using the Barrett–Joyner–Halenda (BJH) model for mesopores (2–50 nm). Prior to analysis, samples were degassed under vacuum at 150 °C for 6 h to remove adsorbed contaminants. Triplicate measurements were performed, and the BET surface area and BJH pore volume are reported as mean ± SD. The relative standard deviation (RSD) for BET measurements was consistently below 3%.

Morphological and Elemental Analysis

Particle morphology was examined using a high-resolution scanning electron microscope. Elemental composition and spatial distribution were analyzed using an energy-dispersive X-ray spectrometer integrated with the SEM, allowing for standardless quantification and elemental mapping. For each dust sample, SEM and EDS analyses were carried out on at least five distinct areas to ensure representative sampling.

Particle Size Distribution (PSD)

PSD was determined by laser diffraction using a Mastersizer 3000 analyzer based on Mie scattering theory. Samples were dispersed in isopropanolselected for its effective wetting of metal particles without inducing dissolution or reactionand subjected to 120 s of ultrasonic agitation to ensure complete deagglomeration. Five replicate measurements were conducted for each sample. Particle size parameters (D10, D50, D90, D­[3,2], D­[4,3]) are reported as mean values. The obscuration level was maintained between 10 and 20% to ensure optimal scattering conditions, and the volume-weighted mean diameter (D­[4,3]) showed high reproducibility, with an RSD typically below 1.5%. ,

Simulation Results and Analysis

Following the establishment of grid independence and model feasibility, this section systematically investigates the multiphase coupling of airflow velocity, concentration, and temperature fields for the four process-specific dusts within the collector. Simulations were conducted under standard conditions of 10 m/s inlet velocity, 100 mg/m3 initial dust concentration, and a 150 °C (423 K) operating temperature. Crucially, the dynamic behavior of dust particles is not merely a passive response to the airflow but is fundamentally governed by their intrinsic physicochemical properties. The subsequent analysis explicitly establishes causal links between these properties and the observed particle motion, concentration distribution, and heat transfer behavior, thereby elucidating the underlying mechanisms dictating dust dynamics.

Coupling of Particle Properties with Airflow Velocity Field

The interaction between the airflow and dust particles is governed by a balance of inertial, drag, and gravitational forces, each intrinsically linked to specific particle characteristics. Figure illustrates the resultant airflow velocity distributions, which are significantly modulated by the type of dust being transported.

Airflow velocity distributions (m/s) in the dust collectors.

(1) MA Dust: The large volume-weighted mean diameter (D[4,3] = 126.54 μm, Table ) imparts significant inertia to MA dust particles. Consequently, the 10 m/s inlet airflow is insufficient for full entrainment. This high inertia results in ballistic trajectories, causing most coarse particles to deviate from streamlines, impact the chamber wall opposite the inlet, and settle rapidly into the hopper. This inertial deposition creates a persistent low-velocity zone (3–5 m/s) adjacent to the hopper, further promoting sedimentation. A notable operational implication is the susceptibility of fine MA fractions to re-entrainment during airflow fluctuations, highlighting a potential need for flow stabilizers in the hopper region.(2) SM Dust: In stark contrast, SM dust, characterized by its fine median size (D50 = 10.28 μm) and low true density (1.95 g/cm3), exhibits a low Stokes number. This property renders it highly susceptible to airflow entrainment. The simulations confirm that fine SM particles are effectively captured by the recirculation vortex (1–8 m/s) in the inlet zone, leading to prolonged residence times and diminished primary separation efficiency. While the uniform velocity distribution near the filter bags (6–8 m/s) aids capture, it concurrently elevates the risk of fine-particle penetration and rapid filter blinding, a concern compounded by the dust’s high specific surface area (SSA = 4.64 m2/g, Table ).(3) HT Dust: The ellipsoidal morphology (Figure c) coupled with the highest SSA (5.31 m2/g) endows HT dust with excellent flow-following characteristics. The dust achieves uniform distribution within the chamber at the core airflow velocity of 10 m/s. The moderate velocity between filter bags (5–7 m/s) facilitates efficient capture. Critically, the high SSA enhances adhesion to filter surfaces, promoting more uniform dust cake formation but also contributing to a gradual increase in system resistance over time, necessitating optimized cleaning cycles.(4) SB Dust: The flaky morphology of SB dust (Figure d) significantly increases its drag coefficient relative to a spherical particle of equivalent volume. This heightened drag counteracts gravitational settling, yielding a calculated 25% reduction in sedimentation velocity. As a result, SB dust exhibits the most homogeneous spatial dispersion in the main chamber (8–10 m/s), as indicated by a low concentration standard deviation. However, flow contraction at the outlet accelerates the air to 10–12 m/s, posing a risk of ejecting residual fine particles from the collector.

Thermal Dynamics Governed by Heat Transfer Properties

The temperature distribution within the dust collector is strongly coupled with the concentration field and regulated by the intrinsic heat transfer properties of the dust, which are dictated by specific surface area (SSA), particle size distribution (PSD), and density. Figure depicts the simulated temperature field distributions for the four metal dusts, revealing distinct thermal behaviors linked directly to their physicochemical characteristics.

Temperature distributions (K) in the dust collectors.

(1) MA Dust: The combination of a large particle size (D[4,3] = 126.54 μm) and low SSA (1.56 m2/g) results in a low effective heat transfer coefficient (h ≈ 18 W/(m2·K)) for MA dust. Conclusion

This study systematically investigated the physicochemical properties and multiphase flow dynamics of metal dust originating from four key processesmachining (MA), smelting (SM), heat treatment (HT), and shot blasting (SB)in aluminum alloy wheel hub manufacturing. The integration of experimental characterization and numerical simulation yielded the following principal conclusions:(1) The physicochemical properties of the dusts are highly process-specific. HT dust exhibits the highest specific surface area (SSA, 5.31 m2/g) due to its porous, irregular morphology, whereas SB dust possesses the highest true density (6.88 g/cm3) attributable to its dense flaky structure and high Fe/Cr content. All dusts are microporous (pore sizes: 1.89–2.50 nm), with SM dust showing the largest total pore volume (0.0291 cm3/g), a characteristic linked to its high oxygen content (42.90 wt.%) and Al-Mg oxide composition that enhances its adsorption capacity for gas-phase substances.(2) Elemental composition and particle size distribution (PSD) dictate distinct occupational and safety hazards. SB dust contains carcinogenic Cr (9.19 wt.%), necessitating stringent respiratory protection and workplace monitoring. SM and HT dusts, rich in Al-Mg oxides, pose long-term pneumoconiosis risks due to the persistence of stable oxide particles in the respiratory tract. Regarding PSD, while 82–91% of particles exceed 10 μm, the fraction below 200 μm requires enhanced explosion prevention measures owing to its high surface energy and suspension potential.(3) Particle properties fundamentally govern the concentration distribution and airflow interaction within the dust collector. MA dust, governed by inertia and gravitational settling, forms high-concentration zones in the inlet pipe, chamber, and hopper. Fine SM dust is easily entrained, forming retention vortices in the inlet recirculation zone and increasing the risk of fine-particle penetration. HT dust exhibits a uniform concentration distribution and moderate filter cake formation, yet its strong adsorption of vapors and gases leads to a 15% increase in filter resistance within one hour, demanding optimized cleaning cycles.(4) The thermal evolution is primarily controlled by SSA, PSD, and density, which regulate convective heat transfer and temperature gradients. High-SSA HT dust enhances heat transfer (h ≈ 65 W/(m2·K)), creating a 5–7 K temperature gradient, prolonging suspension, and inducing thermal stress. Large-particle MA dust stabilizes the hopper temperature (421–423 K) but increases oxidation risks via high-temperature deposition (422 K). Fine SM dust causes significant inlet cooling (8–10 K) and a steep 10 K thermal gradient, threatening filter bag integrity. High-density, flaky SB dust maintains a uniform temperature field (419–422 K, h ≈ 25 W/(m2·K)) but requires timely hopper cleaning to prevent accumulation.
