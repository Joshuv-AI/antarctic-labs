TransBreast-Net: An interpretable vision transformer ensemble for breast cancer histopathology classification
Authors: Khandaker Mohammad Mohi Uddin, Muhammad Abdullah Adnan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Histopathology classification of breast cancer is still a case of difficulty in sorting out the complex morphology of the tissues, low inter-class variability, and manual biopsy analysis which is time consuming, costly, and inter-observer-dependent. Convolutional neural network-based methods have been shown to perform well for breast cancer imaging diagnosis, but they frequently lack the ability to capture the long-range spatial dependencies and lack interpretability for clinical decision-making. To tackle these problems, this study introduces an interpretable vision transformer ensemble framework TransBreast-Net for breast cancer histopathology classification. The proposed framework is based on transfer learning from large data augmentation and strong preprocessing on BreakHis and ICIAR datasets. The architectures of three transformers, namely CaiT S24 224, DeiT Small Patch16_224, and Swin Small Patch4_ Window7_224, are used to extract the complementary representations of local and global features. However, ensemble methods with Swin + DeiT for binary classification, and Swin + CaiT for multi-class classification are designed to enhance classification robustness and generalization. Experimental results show good performance with 99.35% accuracy in binary classification (benign vs. malignant) and 97% accuracy in multi-class classification (benign, in situ, invasive, and normal). In addition, visual explanations are embedded using Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping to enhance interpretability by identifying diagnostically relevant tissue regions that are important in the model decision-making process. The proposed TransBreast-Net framework exhibits high classification accuracy, good robustness, and has potential clinical applications for artificial intelligence diagnosis in breast cancer.

Introduction

Breast cancer is one of the most frequent and fatal diseases affecting women worldwide, accounting for a significant portion of cancer-related diseases and mortality. Over 2.3 million new cases were reported in 2020 alone, and over 685,000 people died as a result of breast cancer.1 Getting an early, correct diagnosis is crucial for making treatment decisions and increasing survival chances. Although manual biopsy assessment and histological examination continue to be the clinical gold-standard, they are costly, labor consuming, and sensitive to inter-observer variability, which can compromise reporting consistency.2, 3 With the surge in computational power and the availability of large annotated medical image repositories, deep learning, particularly convolutional neural networks (CNNs), has demonstrated significant promise in tasks such as breast cancer classification.4, 5 Yet, CNNs often struggle to grasp long distance contextual relationships and typically require extensive feature engineering. Inspired by breakthroughs in natural language processing,6 Vision Transformers (ViTs) leverage self-attention mechanisms to model global dependencies. When adapted for image analysis, they have matched or outperformed CNNs across multiple domains, including histopathology image categorization.7

In response to these advances, we introduce TransBreast Net, an ensemble pipeline built around transformer architectures for both multi-class (benign, in situ, invasive, and normal) and binary (benign vs. malignant) breast cancer classification. Our framework integrates three state-of-the-art ViT variants such as CaiT S24 224, DeiT Small Patch16 224, and Swin Small Patch4 Window7 224 harnessing their complementary strengths. We train and evaluate on two benchmark collections: ICIAR 2018, which comprises 400 histological images,8 and BreakHis, containing 7909 images.2 Through careful pre-processing (scaling, normalization) and a rich suite of augmentation strategies, we bolster our model's robustness and guard against overfitting.

A cornerstone of TransBreast Net is transfer learning: by fine-tuning models pretrained on massive datasets such as ImageNet, we overcome the scarcity of large-scale medical image corpora, accelerating convergence and improving classification.9, 10 Our ensemble achieves 97% accuracy on the ICIAR multi-class task and 99.35% on the binary classification challenge, underscoring the power of combining Swin and Class-Attention in Image Transformers (CaiT) transformers. Equally important is making the model's reasoning transparent for clinical adoption. We employ Grad-CAM to generate heatmaps that pinpoint the regions driving each prediction, thereby demystifying the black box and fostering clinician trust.11, 12 Recent literature further validates transformer models in digital pathology. For example, TransMIL Li et al.13 excels at whole-slide image analysis using multiple instance learning, and ViT-based systems have surpassed CNN counterparts in colorectal cancer classification.14 Moreover, ensemble learning combining diverse model predictions has long been recognized for enhancing reliability and accuracy in high stakes settings.15 In our work, CaiT's deep attention layers and Swin's hierarchical scheme synergize to capture both local texture and global structure.16, 17 By uniting modern transformer designs, transfer learning, extensive data augmentation, and model explainability, TransBreast Net delivers a scalable, high-performance solution for histopathology. Our extensive evaluations on two public datasets demonstrate its generalizability and resilience. Looking ahead, future efforts may explore scaling to whole-slide imagery, incorporating self-supervised feature learning in low data scenarios, and integrating clinical metadata for truly multimodal diagnostics. Related works

Medical image analysis has expanded rapidly with the adoption of machine learning and, more recently, deep learning techniques for breast cancer detection and classification. In this section, we review how modern ViTs, ensemble strategies, and interpretability methods have evolved from earlier approaches to inform our proposed framework.

Traditional and CNN-based breast cancer classification

Early methods relied on classic machine learning classifiers such as support vector machines, random forests, and k-nearest neighbours. These models used handcrafted features texture descriptors, shape measurements, and morphological statistics extracted from histology images. Whereas these techniques demonstrated reasonable accuracy, they often struggled to scale across different datasets and lacked the capacity to generalize well.18 The advent of CNNs marked a major shift. CNNs learn hierarchical image features automatically, leading to substantial gains in diagnostic performance. For example, Gelasca et al.18 introduced a baseline CNN on the BreakHis dataset that distinguishes cancerous from non-cancerous tissue across multiple magnifications. Subsequent studies, such as Araújo et al.,19 built bespoke deep architectures that classify breast histology into normal, benign, in situ, and invasive categories. However, CNNs require extensive hyperparameter tuning for different image scales and can miss long-range contextual cues, especially given the subtle variations and overlaps in tissue patterns.20

Vision transformers in medical imaging

ViTs, adapted from natural language models, process images by splitting them into patch-es and applying self-attention to capture global context. Dosovitskiy et al.7 demonstrated that ViTs can match or exceed CNN performance on large-scale image classification. Since then, several transformer variants such as Swin Transformer,21 CaiT,22 and DeiT have improved efficiency and robustness through techniques like hierarchical attention and knowledge distillation. In medical imaging, ViTs have been applied to tasks from whole-slide classification to segmentation,23 showing particular promise in histopathology, where both local details and global tissue architecture are important. He et al.,24 Tummala et al.,25 Zhang et al.,26 Wang et al.27, Guzel et al.28 and Wang et al.29 used ViT to predict breast cancer.

Mi et al.30 introduced TransRAM, a transformer-based model that captures global spatial relationships in breast cancer histopathology images. Its strengths include high accuracy, effective long-range feature learning, and attention-based interpretability. However, it requires large datasets, high computational power, and may lose fine details due to patch-based processing. Chaudhury and Nag31 proposed a BERT–LSTM ensemble, creatively adapting NLP models for image classification. Whereas it performs reasonably well on moderate datasets, it faces conceptual mismatch issues, limited interpretability, and concerns about methodological quality, as the paper was reportedly retracted. Mehta et al.32 developed HATNet, a hierarchical attention network that analyzes images from patch to slide level, closely mimicking a pathologist's workflow. Its main strengths are strong accuracy, multi-level feature learning, and better explainability. However, the model is complex, resource-intensive, and requires careful tuning. Overall, TransRAM and HATNet provide more reliable and interpretable solutions, whereas the BERT–LSTM ensemble shows weaker methodological grounding.

Ensemble learning in histopathology

Combining multiple models into an ensemble is a proven method for boosting stability and accuracy by reducing individual model variance.15 In biomedical imaging, ensembles of CNNs have delivered strong results on benchmarks such as the BACH dataset.33 Our approach extends this idea to transformer models: by ensembling Swin Transformer and CaiT, we leverage Swin's shifting window attention for multiscale feature capture and CaiT's deeper attention layers for enhanced generalization. This synergy yields improvements on both binary and multi-class breast cancer classification tasks.

AI-assisted medical image analysis

The use of artificial intelligence (AI) and medical image processing has greatly enhanced automated disease diagnosis systems in a range of healthcare fields in recent times.19, 34, 35, 36, 37 Spanhol et al.2 presented several machine learning models to detect breast cancer and showed the significance of intelligent feature extraction in breast cancer diagnosis.19 also delved into AI for retinal disease diagnosis and transformer-based medical image segmentation systems, underscoring the increasing success of attention-based architectures in medicine. These works all inspire the use of explainable transformer ensembles in order to classify breast cancer robustly from histopathological images.

Explainable AI and Grad-CAM in breast cancer diagnosis

High accuracy alone does not guarantee clinical adoption; practitioners require insight into model decisions. Grad-CAM produces class specific heatmaps that highlight the image regions driving each prediction.38 These visual explanations help ensure that models focus on relevant tissue structures rather than spurious artifacts. Reviews of explainability methods in healthcare underscore the importance of transparent models in sensitive applications such as cancer detection.39 Recent work by Mahmood et al.40 applied Grad-CAM to breast histology, confirming alignment between attention maps and pathologist annotations. In our system, every transformer model is paired with Grad-CAM to provide interpretable visual feedback, enabling clinicians to verify and understand each automated diagnosis.

Methodology

Histopathological images were collected from the publicly available BreaKHis Spanhol et al.2 and ICIAR Araujo et al.19 datasets. Subsequently, preprocessing techniques, including image normalization, stratified data splitting, and augmentation, were applied before model training. Generalization is improved by thorough data augmentation, which includes flips, rotations, and color jitter. The AdamW optimizer, cross-entropy loss with class weights, and cosine annealing scheduling are used to fine-tune the ViT models (Swin41 Small Patch4 Window7 224, DeiT42 Small Patch16 224, and CaiT43 S24 224) and their ensembles. Accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score are used to assess performance, and Grad-CAM guarantees interpretability for clinical relevance. Robust categorization of breast cancer is achieved by the replicable pathway. Preprocessing phase for transformer-based ensemble models

In order to ensure reliable and consistent input preparation for the transformer-based ensemble models used for breast cancer classification using the BreakHis44 and ICIAR45 datasets, namely Swin Small Patch4 Window7 224 + DeiT Small_Patch16_224 and Swin Small + CaiT S24_224, the preprocessing step is essential. To meet the input dimensions required by the ViT backbones, all input histopathology images are uniformly shrunk to pixels. Where, I stand for the tensor of the raw input image. Stratified sampling guarantees that the distribution of benign and malignant classifications is maintained throughout the training (70%), validation (15%), and testing (15%) subsets for the 4069 microscope images that make up the BreakHis dataset. The BreaKHis dataset originally has 7909 histopathological images, but in this study, 4069 images were used because of the fact that a subset of those images is available in the used Kaggle repository. All images in the designated dataset folder (4069 images, benign and malignant) were automatically imported into the data-loading pipeline. Architecture of the Swin-DeiT ensemble model

Input pipeline

In order to assess histopathological images of breast cancer while ensuring standardization and augmentation for improved generalization, the ensemble model uses the BreakHis dataset as its input pipeline. Each image is reduced to 224 × 224 × 3 pixels in order to meet the input requirements of the Swin Small Patch4 _22417 and DeiT Small Patch16_224 Transformer models.16 The mean and standard deviation parameters of ImageNet are used to apply normalization. Extensive data augmentation is carried out during training, including random rotations, horizontal and vertical flips, color jittering, and random erasing procedures, to improve variety and resilience. To maintain class distribution across benign and malignant categories, the dataset is separated into training (70%), validation (15%), and test (15%) subsets.

Feature extraction backbones

Two cutting-edge ViT backbones, Swin Small and DeiT Small, are included into the ensemble design. Whereas the DeiT Small model uses patch embedding and distilled tokens to provide fine-grained representations optimized for classification, the Swin Small model uses a hierarchical window-based self-attention mechanism with shifted windows to efficiently capture local and global contextual information. In order to ensure stable convergence and efficient feature learning specific to the breast cancer binary classification task, both models are started with pretrained ImageNet weights and then further optimized on the BreakHis dataset using the AdamW optimizer. They have a learning rate of 1e-4 and a weight decay coefficient of 0.01.

Feature fusion and classification head

A weighted attention method is used to combine the logits generated by the Swin and DeiT models, represented by the letters y_Swin and y_DeiT in R2, respectively, for the final prediction. The total output is calculated as:(3)yout=CombinerAttnw1⋅ySwinw2⋅yDeiT

Where, w1 and w2 are learnable scalar weights constrained to the range [0,1], and Attn denotes a multi-head attention layer designed to recalibrate the fused feature space. The loss function applied during training is a weighted cross-entropy formulation expressed as:(4)L=−∑i=12wi⋅yi⋅logy^i

Where, wi represents the class-specific weighting factor, yi the ground-truth label, and y^i the predicted probability corresponding to class i. The model's sensitivity to both benign and malignant categories is maintained by this approach, which also tackles any class imbalance.

Computational efficiency

The ensemble minimizes memory use and computation time without sacrificing model depth or accuracy by using mixed-precision training and contemporary GPU technology. The model can dynamically select important features from both backbone outputs thanks to the fusion stage's implementation of multi-head attention, and overfitting is avoided during fine-tuning by using the AdamW optimization technique in conjunction with cautious weight decay. Training pipeline

In order to enable task-specific adaptation, the training procedure employs a fine-tuning strategy in which the Swin and DeiT models are first loaded with ImageNet-pretrained weights and then completely unfrozen during optimization. The ensemble undergoes 50 epochs of training, which includes dynamic learning rate modifications by cosine annealing and early halting (patience: 10 epochs). By reducing class distribution disparities, the weighted cross-entropy loss maintains classification fidelity for both classes.

Architecture of the Swin–CaiT ensemble model

Input pipeline

Each histological breast cancer image from the ICIAR 2018 dataset is shrunk to a standardized resolution of 224 × 224 × 3 in order to match the pretrained transformer input dimensions before being processed by the input pipeline. To guarantee consistency with the models' pretraining distribution, normalization is done using ImageNet mean and standard deviation statistics. In order to improve the model's capacity for generalization, data augmentation methods such as color jittering, random rotations, and flipping the data horizontally and vertically are used during the training phase. In order to ensure fair representation across the four classes (Benign, InSitu, Invasive, and Normal), the dataset is divided into training, validation, and test sets in a 32:64:32 ratio. Feature extraction backbones

The group combines two cutting-edge transformer designs, CaiT S24 _ 22416 and Swin Transformer Small (Patch4_Window7_224), which are both optimized to extract deep semantic characteristics from histopathology image data. Whereas the CaiT S24 uses class-attention (CA) layers and deep attention blocks to selectively concentrate on discriminative image areas, the Swin Transformer uses a hierarchical architecture with non-overlapping shifted windows to effectively capture both local and global characteristics. To provide rich and varied feature representations, both models were started with pretrained ImageNet-1K weights. They were then adjusted on the ICIAR dataset to suit the particular breast cancer classification job.

Feature fusion and classification head

The Swin Transformer and CaiT models' output logits are combined using an unweighted average technique for ensemble prediction. The final output logits are specifically calculated as:(5)yout=0.5×yCaiT+0.5×ySwin

Where, yCaiT and ySwin stand for the corresponding 4-class logits that each model generated. This combination makes use of the complementing features of both architectures to achieve strong multi-class discrimination. To resolve class imbalance, a weighted cross-entropy formulation defines the total loss function, which is computed as:(6)L=−∑i=14wi⋅yi⋅logy^i

Where, wi denotes the balancing weight for class i, yi represents the true class label, and y^i is the predicted probability output.

Computational efficiency

Computational effectiveness and model depth are balanced in the combined Swin–CaiT ensemble model. The AdamW optimizer is used to optimize both backbones, set up with a weight decay value of 0.01 and a learning rate of 3e-5. By ensuring a progressive learning rate decrease, a CosineAnnealingLR learning rate scheduler enhances convergence stability and guards against overfitting. Because of model parallelism and hardware acceleration with contemporary GPUs like the NVIDIA Tesla V100, GPU memory consumption is still controllable despite the two transformer models' depth and complexity. By decreasing memory cost and preserving numerical stability, mixed-precision training further improves computational efficiency. Training pipeline

Training is regulated by a methodical two-phase fine-tuning pipeline. Both the Swin and CaiT models' feature extraction layers are initially frozen, enabling the ensemble classification head to precisely adjust to the ICIAR dataset over the course of 10 warm-up epochs. Each backbone's chosen top layers are then unfrozen, allowing for fine-grained feature tweaking for 40 more epochs at a lower learning rate of 1e-5. In order to avoid overfitting and guarantee good generalization, regularization strategies include dynamic learning rate changes by CosineAnnealingLR and early halting with a patience parameter of 10 epochs. Self-attention mechanism for transformer-based ensemble models

For breast cancer detection, the Swin Small Patch4 Window7 224 + DeiT Small Patch16 224 and Swin Small + CaiT S24 224 ensemble architectures' incorporation of self-attention mechanisms greatly improves classification performance on the BreakHis (binary) and ICIAR (multi-class) datasets. These models make use of attention-based architectures' inherent capacity to identify local and global interdependence in histopathological images, which is essential for precise lesion classification. Using a shifted window self-attention mechanism, the Swin Transformer calculates attention inside non-overlapping 7 × 7 local windows for the BreakHis dataset. The mathematical definition of the self-attention operation is:(11)AttentionQKV=softmaxQK⊤dkV

Where, Q,K,V∈Rn×dk denote the query, key, and value matrices, respectively, and dk represents the dimensionality of the key vectors. The model can effectively capture fine-grained spatial characteristics while preserving computational efficiency because to its confined attention architecture [23]. DeiT on the other hand, applies global self-attention over 16 × 16 patch tokens that do not overlap. This allows for the thorough modeling of long-range spatial dependencies that are necessary for distinguishing various histological patterns at different sizes. A multi-head attention layer with 4 heads and a hidden dimension size of 512 is used to combine the outputs of Swin and DeiT:yout=CombinerMultiHeadAttnw1ySwinw2yDeiT

Where, w1 and w2 are trainable weights learned during optimization to balance the contributions of Swin and DeiT feature representations. The higher classification performance on BreakHis is driven by this fusion method, which improves the ensemble's capacity to collect complementing local and global information. Swin and CaiT both make use of their own self-attention techniques for the ICIAR dataset. Interestingly, CaiT improves its multi-class discriminating capability by adding more CA layers that concentrate on improving the class token representations via patch token interactions. For the ICIAR dataset, the ensemble output is obtained through trainable weighted averaging of individual model logits, avoiding the need for an additional attention layer.

Transformer encoder architecture in ensemble models

Swin Transformer encoder

Strong breast cancer classification on the BreakHis (binary) and ICIAR (multi-class) datasets is largely attributed to the transformer encoder architectures seen in the Swin Small Patch4 Window7 224 + DeiT Small Patch16 224 and Swin Small + CaiT S24 224 ensemble configurations.

Swin Transformer encoder

The encoder for Swin Transformer is designed to apply window-based multi-head self-attention (W-MSA) to non-overlapping 7 × 7 local image patches without overlap. In order to effectively capture local contextual information, each window analyzes a subset of the image tokens.35 The following is the definition of the attention mechanism for a certain window:(13)W-MSAX=ConcatheadiWO(14)headi=AttentionXWiQXWiKXWiV

Where, X∈Rn×d denotes the input patch embeddings, and WiQ,WiK,WiV∈Rd×dk represent the projection matrices for the queries, keys, and values, respectively. Superior feature expressiveness is promoted by the encoder's ability to concurrently attend to input from many representation subspaces thanks to the multi-head architecture.

DeiT transformer encoder

All patch tokens are subject to global multi-head self-attention by the DeiT Small Patch16 224 encoder, where each patch represents a 16 × 16 area of the input image. DeiT captures wide contextual associations throughout the whole-image domain, which is essential for global structure detection in breast cancer histopathology, in contrast to Swin's limited W-MSA.36

CaiT transformer encoder

By including CA layers, CaiT's encoder design for the ICIAR dataset expands on conventional ViTs and allows for iterative class token refining via interactions with patch tokens. Because ICIAR images are complicated multi-class, this method improves the model's capacity to concentrate on class-relevant representations.37

Encoder processing pipeline

Every transformer encoder uses residual connections and layer normalization at every step to enhance gradient flow and stabilize training. The following is a mathematical description of the operations per encoder block:(15)X′=LayerNormX+W-MSAX,X′′LayerNormX′+FFNX′

Where, X′ and X″ represent the post-attention and post-FFN outputs, respectively, ensuring that both local and global features are effectively learned and preserved.

Output prediction strategy in ensemble models

Superior classification performance on both binary and multi-class breast cancer diagnosis tasks is achieved by the output prediction mechanism in the suggested ensemble architectures, Swin Small + DeiT Small for the BreakHis dataset and Swin Small + CaiT S24 for the ICIAR dataset. This mechanism guarantees the efficient integration of complementary feature representations.

BreakHis: Multi-head attention-based output fusion

A multi-head attention method is used to integrate the final output logits from the Swin Small and DeiT Small encoders for the binary classification of histopathological images in the BreakHis dataset. Before classification, the ensemble may identify inter-model feature relationships using this fusion method:(16)yout=CombinerMultiHeadAttnw1ySwinw2yDeiT

Where, w1 and w2 denote learnable scalar weights for Swin and DeiT outputs, respectively, and the Combiner denotes a feed-forward projection layer aligning the fused features to the output space. A softmax activation then converts these logits into class probabilities:(17)y^i=eyi∑jeyjensuring probabilistic interpretability aligned with clinical decision-making.

ICIAR: Weighted average-based output fusion

Due to their complementing capabilities in local and class-token global feature modeling, the Swin Small and CaiT S24 ensemble outputs from the two models are blended by weighted averaging for the ICIAR multi-class dataset, indicating similar confidence in both pathways:(18)yout=0.5yCaiT+0.5ySwin

Afterward, the final class probabilities, like in the BreakHis configuration, are obtained via softmax normalization.

Loss function and optimization strategy

The suggested ensemble models Swin Small + DeiT Small for the BreakHis dataset and Swin Small + CaiT S24 for the ICIAR dataset achieve strong diagnostic performance, which is mostly dependent on efficient optimization and treatment of class imbalance.

Weighted cross-entropy loss

To address the class imbalance common in medical imaging datasets, both ensemble configurations make use of weighted cross-entropy loss methodology. The formula for the loss function is:(19)L=−∑i=1kwiyilogy^iwhere: yi∈01 is the ground-truth label for class i, y^i∈01 is the predicted softmax probability for class i, wi is the class-specific weight defined as:(20)wi=Nkni

With, N being the total number of samples, ni the number of samples in class i, and k the number of classes. By lessening the bias towards majority classes, this re-weighting increases sensitivity to minority class predictions, which is essential for medical diagnostic tasks like breast cancer detection.

Optimization algorithm and learning rate scheduling

A variation of Adam that separates weight decay from the gradient update to avoid over-regularization is used to improve training: the AdamW optimizer. Each dataset's optimization is customized by using learning rates and schedules appropriate to the model:

BreakHis Ensemble: Learning Rate: 1 × 10-4.

ReduceLROnPlateau scheduler dynamically reduces the learning rate when validation performance plateaus, enhancing convergence stability.

ICIAR Ensemble: Learning Rate: 3 × 10-5.

CosineAnnealingLR scheduler gradually anneals the learning rate following a cosine curve, promoting better minima exploration and generalization.

By prohibiting too large weights, a weight decay coefficient of 0.01 is applied to all models in order to minimize overfitting.

Early stopping and reproducibility

In order to prevent overfitting and facilitate model generalization, early halting is implemented with a patience of 15 epochs for ICIAR and 25 epochs for BreakHis. If validation loss does not decrease throughout the patience period, training is stopped.

Multiple evaluation measures are used to thoroughly test the whole optimization process, proving its robustness and reproducibility two essentials for clinical implementation in breast cancer diagnosis.

Gradient-weighted class activation mapping (Grad-CAM)

Developed by Selvaraju et al.,46 Grad-CAM uses the gradients of the target class score yc with respect to the feature mappings AK of a convolutional layer to identify significant areas in an input image that affect the model's prediction. Global average pooling across the spatial dimensions is used to calculate the important weight αkc for each feature map AK:(25)αkc=1Z∑i∑j∂yc∂Aijkwhere: Z is the total number of pixels in the feature map, i, j denote the spatial locations, ∂yc∂Aijk represents the gradient of the class score yc with respect to the activation at location (i, j) in feature map AK.

A weighted mixture of the feature maps and a ReLU activation are used to create the final Grad-CAM heatmap LGrad-CAMc, which keeps only the characteristics that positively impact the class of interest:(26)LGrad-CAMc=ReLU∑kαkcAk.

In the input image, this heatmap shows the areas that are most important for the class c prediction. The use of Grad-CAM heatmaps across several deep learning architectures (Swin-DeiT Ensemble Model) to distinguish between “benign” and “malignant” classes and highlight regions of interest (ROIs) in histopathology images is shown in Fig. 7. The heatmaps help with AI-assisted pathological diagnosis by offering a visual interpretability of how each model concentrates on tissue features that are helpful for diagnosis. Grad-CAM's insensitivity to deep learning architectures and medical image processing is shown by the typical patterns of activation across models.Fig. 7Grad-CAM visualizations of the Swin–DeiT ensemble on BreakHis dataset.Fig. 7

Grad-CAM sanity check using confidence drop

In order to measure the confidence in the given explanations by the Grad-CAM, the authors performed a sanity check based on a drop in confidence. In the current experiment, the most distinguished areas where Grad-CAM appeared from the input histopathology images were masked and subsequently, the prediction confidence of the transformer ensemble models was re-assessed. If there is a greater confidence reduction after the masking, then the masked areas were important to the model's decision process. The drop in confidence of the models for the images is presented in Table 16 and the average confidence drop after Grad-CAM Saliency masking is presented in Table 17. The Swin+DeiT ensemble achieved an average of 7.36% accuracy decrease with the removal of salient regions in the BreakHis dataset and an average of 10.82% accuracy decrease in the ICIAR dataset. The results show that the captured tissue regions by Grad-CAM were the diagnostically relevant ROIs that influenced the transformer ensemble prediction. The confidence-drop analysis was performed by masking the regions identified by the proposed transformer ensembles and found that the prediction confidence of the proposed transformer ensembles dropped when masked. The ICIAR dataset had a higher reduction in confidence (0.1250), suggesting that the highlighted tissue regions had a strong impact on the predictions of the models. Discussion

In binary breast cancer histopathological image classification, earlier transformer-based models have shown good results but also several limitations. For multi-class classification, Mi et al.,30 He et al.,24 and Mehta et al.32 achieved accuracies between 0.89 and 0.94 AUC, whereas Wang et al.29 and Chen et al.23 struggled with cross-dataset adaptability. Our proposed TransBreast-Net (Swin + CaiT), however, achieved a macro F1-score of 0.98, setting a new benchmark in multi-class classification. These results confirm that our hybrid transformer framework delivers more robust, accurate, and scalable performance than existing methods.

The improvement in the proposed TransBreast-Net ensemble models could be due to the complementary strengths of the transformer architectures used. Shifting windows in the self-attention of the Swin Transformer effectively capture fine-grained local tissue morphology and, as a result, can be used to accurately identify histopathological tissue structures and cell patterns. DeiT and CaiT, on the other hand, have shown to be good at global contextual modeling through their ability to learn long-range dependencies between image patches, especially in cases where complex spatial relationships are crucial for understanding breast cancer histology images. The ensemble strategy also improves the performance by combining complementary local and global feature representations, thus improving the robustness and generalization in the classification. Experimental results further show that BreakHis binary classification task outperforms ICIAR multi-class classification task. Such a difference in performance can be attributed to the greater complexity of multi-class histopathological discrimination; the subtle inter-class similarity between benign, in situ, invasive, and normal tissues makes classification difficult. Also, the ICIAR dataset is relatively small, which can have an impact on the generalizability of the models. In addition, the proposed transformer ensembles are shown to be focused on the diagnostically relevant tissue regions during prediction, as shown by the Grad-CAM visualizations. The interpretability is clinically relevant as it enhances transparency and trustworthiness within the context of AI diagnostics, enabling pathologists to master the AI model's decision-making process. Grad-CAM identifies discriminative histopathological regions related to the patterns of breast cancer, which improves the reliability and applicability of the proposed framework in the clinical world.

Conclusion

The TransBreast-Net Transformer Ensemble Model provides state-of-the-art performance on the BreakHis (99.35% accuracy) and ICIAR (97% accuracy) datasets by combining Swin Transformer alongside DeiT for binary classification and CaiT for multi-class tasks. The approach guarantees strong generalization across a variety of histopathological patterns by using sophisticated ViTs, thorough preprocessing, and data augmentation. By emphasizing clinically important characteristics and improving interpretability, Grad-CAM promotes confidence in AI-assisted diagnosis. By using weighted cross-entropy loss to solve class imbalance, the ensemble's weighted focused and averaging procedures efficiently integrate complementary characteristics. This repeatable pipeline outperforms current techniques in terms of accuracy, recall, and F1-scores after being tuned using AdamW and dynamic learning rate scheduling. TransBreast-Net is a potential tool for clinical decision assistance in the detection of breast cancer because of its versatility and excellent performance. The further development of model robustness and generalizability of transformer-based histopathology classification models will be focused on the incorporation of patient-wise and specimen-level grouped splitting strategies like GroupKFold cross-validation to further minimize potential data leakage risks. Moreover, larger multi-center datasets with standardized patient-level annotations that allow for more reliable clinical evaluation will be explored in future studies. Furthermore, investigating lightweight transformer topologies and integrating multi-modal data (such as genetic profiles) would increase effectiveness and usability in clinical settings with limited resources.
