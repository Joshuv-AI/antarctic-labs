SECARB-USA Overview and Introduction
The “Southeast Regional CO2 Utilization and Storage Acceleration Partnership”
(SECARB-USA) project supported the U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) Office of Fossil
Energy's (FE) mission to help the United States meet its need for secure, affordable, and
environmentally sound fossil energy supplies by utilizing the advancements made since
2003 by the Regional Carbon Sequestration Partnership (RCSP) Initiative to continue to
identify and address knowledge gaps. The SECARB-USA regional initiative
encompasses the states of Alabama, Arkansas, Florida, Georgia, Louisiana, Mississippi,
North Carolina, South Carolina, Tennessee, Virginia, and portions of Kentucky, Missouri,
Oklahoma, Texas, and West Virginia as noted in Figure 1.

Resources Innovation (CRI), Environmental Defense Fund (EDF), Geological Survey of
Alabama (GSA), Oklahoma State University (OSU), and the Virginia Center for Coal and

SECARB-USA under an authorized Field Work Proposal (FWP). In addition to the project
Subrecipients, an industry network consisting of the Clean Air Task Force (CATF),
Denbury Resources, Inc. (DRI), Marathon Petroleum (Marathon), Mitsubishi Heavy
Industries America, Inc. (MHIA), Repsol, Titan America (Titan), SAS Institute, Inc. (SAS),
and Southern Company (SoCo) serves in an advisory capacity.

The SECARB-USA partnership builds upon the proven accomplishments of the SECARB
Regional Carbon Sequestration Partnership Program, which successfully demonstrated
CO₂ injection and monitoring through its Early Test at Cranfield, Mississippi, and the
Anthropogenic Test in Bucks, Alabama. These demonstrations provided critical lessons
regarding the technical, regulatory, and legal requirements for effective carbon capture,
transportation, and storage projects and have contributed substantially to many of the
commercial CCUS successes seen today. The region is currently undergoing rapid
expansion in carbon capture, utilization, and storage (CCUS), with numerous large-scale
commercial and federally supported projects announced in recent years. The growing
importance of CCUS to the regional economy is further reflected in the marked increase
in state legislative activity addressing issues relevant to CCUS development.

Due to the long-term geologic evolution and maturity of sedimentary basins across the
region, the estimated CO₂ storage capacity is substantial, currently projected at
approximately 500 billion metric tons. Although this estimate is likely conservative, it
highlights the considerable potential for future CO₂ storage development. In addition to
assessing the technical, legal, and regulatory barriers to large-scale CCUS deployment,
the SECARB-USA team has focused on evaluating areas with limited subsurface
characterization but high CO₂ emission sources. This work has resulted in the
identification of new storage prospects, including the Cambro–Ordovician carbonate
formations of northwest Georgia and a new class of storage opportunity in tectonically
thickened fold and thrust belts. Furthermore, the SECARB-USA team has invested
significant effort in stakeholder engagement at multiple levels, including policymakers,
regulators, and industry representatives. These interactions have fostered productive
cross-state regulatory collaboration and have supported the announcement and planning

SECARB-USA.

CCUS Potential in the SECARB Region
The Southeast is one of the most active regions of the U.S. for commercial CCUS project
development. This is due primarily to the high concentration of industrial point-source CO2
emissions, the legacy knowledge and workforce necessary to support these projects, and
the existence of “world-class” CO2 storage geology in thick, porous, and highly-permeable
saline reservoirs, owed in large part to the geologic evolution of the region.

Geologic Storage Resources
The CO2 storage capacity for the region is significant and has been estimated at 500
billion metric tons.2 While it is likely that this is an underestimate, not accounting for
storage opportunity in depleted oil and gas reservoirs, associated with enhanced
hydrocarbon extraction, or unconventional reservoirs, it does reflect the many
opportunities available to those interested in developing CO2 storage projects in the
region’s saline reservoirs (Figure 4). The SECARB-USA Task 4.1 Sub-Basin Framework
for Future CO2 Infrastructure Development report further divides the region into 40
individual sub-basins with reference to studies that detail local geology and CO2 storage
readiness based on available information,3 while the Task 4.1.b Techno-Economic
Analysis of Infrastructure Buildout Scenarios contemplates source-sink matching,
associated buildout scenarios, and costs associated with broad buildout in the region.4
Because of the storage resource, it is expected that CCUS projects will continue to locate
in the region.

Overview of Active and Announced CCUS Projects
Over the last several years, there has been significant advances in the number of
operational and in-development CCUS projects announced across the U.S (Figure 5).
Indeed, there is established clustering of large-scale CCUS activity along the Gulf Coast
and in parts of the Midwest, reflecting favorable geology, existing infrastructure, and
industrial density, with emerging projects in the West, and Great Lakes Region. When
viewed alongside mapped CO₂ storage capacity, there is growing momentum for CCUS
deployment as well as persistent regional disparities between emission sources, storage
resources, and infrastructure. Collectively, these patterns provide important context for
SECARB-USA by situating regional efforts within the broader national CCUS landscape
and reinforcing the need for coordinated storage characterization and infrastructure
development.

Testing Under Explored Areas in the Region
During the SECARB-USA period of performance, it was determined that there are
significant gaps in existing storage characterization in the region, largely due to limited
historical oil and gas exploration and production activity. In sum, 425 industrial facilities,
representing nearly half of the region’s point-source emissions, are located outside areas
with proven saline storage capacity. Figure 7 illustrates the geographic mismatch
between major emission sources and confirmed saline reservoir opportunities, implying
the need for extensive CO₂ transport infrastructure or targeted proximal exploration to

enable carbon storage. The distribution of these facilities across multiple industry sectors,
particularly power generation and manufacturing, underscores the scale of the challenge
and the potential opportunity. These factors collectively justified new data acquisition
efforts and align the interests of motivated project partners with assets in these regions.

Alabama Valley and Ridge Province — Westover Area (Shelby County,
Alabama)

The Valley and Ridge Province of central Alabama is characterized by thin-skinned
compressional tectonics, minimal historical oil and gas development, and sparse
subsurface control. Despite limited legacy data, the province lies within the service
territory of Southern Company and in proximity to major electricity generation assets,
making it a strategic candidate for CO₂ storage evaluation. In total, there are
approximately 10 facilities within 25 miles of the identified drilling location emitting nearly
10 million tons of CO2 on an annual basis. The Westover area in Shelby County was
selected to address this data gap through direct drilling and geophysical characterization.

The Westover Stratigraphic Test Well was drilled with the objective of evaluating potential
CO₂ storage reservoirs in Cambrian–Ordovician carbonate formations, specifically
targeting the Knox Group and related units. Drilling encountered more than 6,000 feet of
Mississippian Floyd-Parkwood Shale, which is part of a large ductile duplex structure
referred to as the Vandiver MUSHWAD (malleable, unctuous shale, weak-layer accretion
in a ductile duplex). This shale interval prevented penetration into the targeted carbonate
reservoirs.

The MUSHWAD is composed of intensely deformed shale and sandstone and exhibits
limited seismic reflectivity, appearing as a moderate-amplitude zone with few continuous
reflectors. The base of this interval is marked by a strong, laterally continuous reflector
interpreted as the Fort Payne Chert, which represents a regional décollement surface
separating ductile Mississippian shales from more competent underlying carbonates.

To overcome the challenges encountered during drilling, a comprehensive seismic
acquisition program was implemented. This program included multiple 2D seismic lines
and a pseudo-3D dataset, acquired along existing roadways using vibroseis sources and
geophone arrays. The acquisition strategy ensured good ground coupling and high-
quality resolution.

          SECARB-USA | Results of Existing CO2 Source Sink Databases Analysis               11

The seismic data were processed using Post-Stack Time Migration and Post-Stack
Depth Migration workflows. The data provided reliable imaging to depths of
approximately 17,000 feet, covering the entire prospective reservoir section.

Figure 8. Satellite imagery of seismic lines and well location. Seismic lines follow existing roadways
 in the project area.

Interpretation of the seismic dataset revealed a well-defined structural and stratigraphic
framework. Below the Vandiver MUSHWAD, a laterally continuous high-amplitude
reflector marks the Fort Payne Chert. Beneath this reflector, Ordovician carbonate units,
including the Knox Group, are imaged as semi-continuous, moderate-amplitude
reflectors.

Below the Ordovician carbonates, the Cambrian shales of the Conasauga and Rome
Formations appear as low-amplitude, discontinuous reflectors filling accommodation
space created by the Birmingham Graben, a basement-involved extensional structure
that is clearly imaged on the seismic profiles. Basement and graben-bounding faults are
visible at depths between approximately 10,000 and 13,000 feet (see Figure 9).

Velocity models constructed from nearby sonic logs (including the Arco Anschutz well)
enabled time-depth conversion and constrained the depth to Cambrian–Ordovician
carbonate reservoirs. At the original Westover well location, the target carbonate interval
is estimated to occur at depths between 7,000 and 11,000 feet.

Structural interpretation indicates that thrust ramps and fault-bend folds repeat sections
of the carbonate interval. Modeling suggests that relocating the well approximately 2,000
feet southeast could allow intersection of the carbonate section at depths closer to 6,000
feet, while drilling at the crest of the thrust ramp could encounter the Ordovician
carbonates at depths as shallow as ~1,000 feet, albeit with increased structural risk
associated with faulting and décollement surfaces.

Georgia Valley and Ridge Province — Cartersville Area (Bartow County,
Georgia)
SECARB-USA partners ARI and SSEB conducted a screening of geologically favorable
CO₂ storage opportunities in northwest Georgia. This effort focused on identifying
formations capable of supporting commercial-scale carbon capture and storage,
particularly in proximity to major industrial CO₂ sources such as Plant Bowen. For this
region, detailed geologic characterization of potential storage sites followed by the drilling
of CCUS “wildcat” well(s) is required to confirm favorable locations for CO₂ storage
complexes, largely a consequence of limited regional data. These activities have potential
to significantly accelerate feasibility studies, resource planning (both internal and
external), and the implementation of commercial-scale CCUS projects.

Key Geological Criteria Identified as Part of an Initial Evaluation:

 • Suitable Storage Reservoir: the Rome Formation was identified as a potential
 CO₂ storage reservoir with adequate pore space for sequestration;
 • Effective Caprock: the Conasauga Shale provides an impermeable and laterally
 continuous caprock, critical for preventing upward migration of injected CO₂ and
 protecting underground drinking water sources; and
 • Favorable Structural Setting: Limited up-dip connection to the ground surface
 reduces the risk of surface leakage and minimizes drilling challenges.

To confirm these initial observations, the Cassville Stratigraphic Test Bore was drilled in
August of 2021. Wire line data, including triple combo and formation micro-imager data
were collected, in addition to sidewall core. Well logs and sidewall core were analyzed to
characterize formations at depth and collect samples for routine core analysis. The data

          SECARB-USA | Results of Existing CO2 Source Sink Databases Analysis              15

from each were then used to inform dynamic simulations to determine injectivity. The well
was permanently plugged and abandoned in September of 2021.

Stratigraphy and Reservoir Properties:

 • The base of the Copper Ridge Member of the Knox Group occurs at approximately
 160 ft depth;
 • Beneath the Knox Group lies the Nolichucky Shale, marking the top of the
 Conasauga Group;
 • The Pumpkin Valley Shale, the basal unit of the Conasauga Group, directly
 overlies the Rome Formation, consistent with regional stratigraphic expectations;
 • The upper Rome Formation consists of shale and dolomite with interbedded
 limestone; and
 • Porosity logs indicate porosities of approximately 5 to 15% within the dolomitized
 upper Rome, supporting its potential suitability as a CO2 storage reservoir.

Log and core analyses confirm the presence of a thick succession of Cambrian–
Ordovician carbonate and siliciclastic strata at the study site, including the Rome

          SECARB-USA | Results of Existing CO2 Source Sink Databases Analysis             16

Formation, Conasauga Group, and the basal Copper Ridge Dolomite of the Knox Group.
Core data demonstrate that porosities exceeding 10% are concentrated within the
dolomitized upper Rome Formation over an approximately 85 ft interval (3,713 to 3,798
ft), with associated Klinkenberg permeabilities ranging from 54.1 to 135 mD. These
permeability values indicate a moderately- to well-connected pore system composed of
vuggy, fracture, and intercrystalline porosity.

The petrophysical results confirm that the dolomitic upper Rome Formation possesses
sufficient porosity and permeability to function as a viable reservoir for CO₂ injection and
long-term storage. The overlying shaly basal units of the Conasauga Group exhibit
physical characteristics consistent with an effective caprock seal, supporting containment
of injected CO₂ and protection of overlying groundwater resources. Structural and
petrophysical analyses further suggest that the geologic framework is favorable but would
benefit from additional subsurface definition.

Collectively, these findings reinforce the technical viability of CO₂ storage in northwest
Georgia and justify continued site characterization. Additional investigation, particularly
the acquisition of 2D seismic data, is warranted to refine reservoir geometry, assess
structural continuity, and reduce uncertainty prior to advancing toward commercial-scale
CCUS development.

Central Appalachian Basin — Virginia–West Virginia–Kentucky Area
(Botetourt County, Virginia)

The Central Appalachian Basin is a thin-skinned fold-and-thrust belt containing multiple
vertically stacked storage targets, including unconventional shale reservoirs, coalbed
methane-bearing strata, depleted conventional oil and gas reservoirs, and saline

          SECARB-USA | Results of Existing CO2 Source Sink Databases Analysis            17

reservoirs. Early characterization efforts focused on unconventional reservoirs due to the
availability of dense datasets derived from coalbed methane development, including
electronic well logs, core samples, and detailed production histories.

More recently, this work expanded to include mature oil and gas fields and saline
reservoirs located within the central Appalachian section spanning Virginia, West Virginia,
and Kentucky. These reservoirs were identified as viable primary storage candidates and
as part of stacked-storage strategies that could improve storage efficiency and flexibility.
During the Summer of 2023, a stratigraphic test bore was drilled up-dip from a partnering
facility to acquire the data necessary to support updated numerical models used to
evaluate storage opportunity. The test bore was drilled to 2,500 ft below surface and
successfully sampled the Ordovician–Devonian target lithologies (see Figure 12). The
field program included expansive student engagement as part of drilling campaign and in
support of subsequent subsurface model development.

Conclusions
The SECARB-USA partnership represents a critical next step in advancing carbon
capture, utilization, and storage (CCUS) across the nation. Building on more than two
decades of technical progress under the Regional Carbon Sequestration Partnership
Initiative, SECARB-USA has moved beyond demonstration toward accelerating
commercial readiness through targeted data acquisition, stakeholder coordination, and
infrastructure-focused planning.

The region’s combination of substantial point-source emissions and vast geologic storage
potential underscores both the urgency and opportunity for CCUS deployment. With
nearly one-third of the nation’s operational and announced CCUS projects located within
the SECARB footprint, the Southeast has emerged as a focal point for national
decarbonization efforts. Favorable geology, established energy infrastructure,
experienced workforce capacity, and strong policy engagement continue to attract
sustained commercial and federal investment.

Through strategic drilling and seismic programs in Alabama, Georgia, and Virginia,
SECARB-USA directly addressed critical subsurface data gaps in underexplored areas
near major emission sources. These efforts yielded valuable geological, petrophysical,
and modeling insights, confirming reservoir potential in some settings, identifying
structural challenges in others, and refining best practices for operating in complex fold-
and-thrust terrains. Equally important, the technical lessons learned have been shared
broadly to de-risk future projects and accelerate deployment across similar geologic
provinces.

Collectively, the outcomes of SECARB-USA demonstrate that the Southeast is not only
geologically capable of supporting large-scale CO₂ storage, but institutionally and

economically prepared to do so. Continued characterization, infrastructure investment,
and coordinated policy support will be essential to unlocking the full potential of the
region’s storage resources and achieving long-term emissions reduction goals.
