Time-Inhomogeneous Preconditioned Langevin Dynamics
Alexander Falk, Laurenz Nagler, Andreas Habring, Thomas Pock

Abstract
Langevin sampling from distributions of the form p ⁡ ( x ) ∝ exp ⁡ ( − Ψ ⁡ ( x ) ) p(x)\propto\exp(-\Psi(x)) faces two major challenges: (global) mode coverage and (local) mode exploration . The first challenge is particularly relevant for multi-modal distributions with disjoint modes, whereas the second arises when the potential Ψ \Psi exhibits diverse and ill-conditioned local mode geometry. To address these challenges, a common approach is to precondition Langevin dynamics with problem-specific information, such as the sample covariance or the local curvature of Ψ \Psi . However, existing preconditioner choices inherently involve a trade-off between global mode coverage and local mode exploration, and no prior method resolves both simultaneously. To overcome this limitation, we propose the time-inhomogeneous preconditioned Langevin algorithm (TIPreL), which introduces a time- and position-dependent preconditioner. This design effectively addresses both challenges mentioned above within a single framework. We establish convergence of the resulting dynamics in the Wasserstein-2 distance both in continuous time and for a tamed Euler discretization. In particular, our analysis extends the existing state of the art by proving convergence under time- and space-dependent diffusion coefficients, and only locally Lipschitz drifts, which has not been covered by prior work. Finally, we experimentally compare TIPreLwith competing preconditioning schemes on a two-dimensional, severely ill-posed example and on a Bayesian logistic regression task in higher dimensions, confirming the efficiency of the proposed method.

Introduction

We are interested in sampling from a probability distribution
\mathrm{d}\pi(x)=p(x)\mathrm{d}x
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
d\geq 1
, characterized via a density of Gibbs-Boltzmann form

p(x)=\frac{\exp(-\Psi(x))}{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\exp(-\Psi(\xi))\,\mathrm{d}\xi}

(1)

with potential function
\Psi\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
.
Throughout, we assume that
\Psi
is twice continuously differentiable with
L
-Lipschitz continuous gradient. More specifically, we are interested in sampling from
\pi
via preconditioned Langevin dynamics with temporally and spatially varying preconditioning.

Sampling from Gibbs distributions of the above form is a prevalent task within machine learning and Bayesian inverse problems
[
20
,
28
,
40
]
.
Due to the complexity of
\Psi
in most applications, direct sampling is not feasible, and researchers resort to Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC)methods. Among those, the most popular ones are based on discretizations of the (overdamped) Langevin stochastic differential equation (SDE)

\mathrm{d}X_{t}=-\nabla\Psi(X_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2}\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}.

(2)

It is well-known that under mild conditions, such as Lipschitz-smoothness and certain growth assumptions on the potential, the law of the solution of (
2
) converges to its unique stationary distribution
\pi
as
t\rightarrow\infty
in different metrics such as the Kullback-Leibler divergence (KLD)or the Wasserstein-2 distance
[
32
]
.
Similarly, discretizing (
2
) via,
e.g.
, an Euler-Maruyama discretization leads to an ergodic Markov chain whose stationary distribution approximates
\pi
as the discretization step size vanishes
[
12
,
14
,
32
]
.

However, Langevin sampling suffers from slow convergence, especially in high-dimensional and/or multi-modal settings.
While there exist several techniques for acceleration of Langevin sampling, in particular based on annealing/tempering
[
7
,
11
,
21
,
19
]
or momentum/inertia
[
8
,
16
]
, we believe that the gap to acceleration techniques in numerical optimization is still significant.

In this article, we consider accelerating Langevin sampling by the incorporation of a time- and position-dependent preconditioning, that is, we consider for a symmetric, positive definite matrix
B(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
the Langevin SDE

\mathrm{d}X_{t}=[-B(t,X_{t})\nabla\Psi(X_{t})+\divergence B(t,X_{t})]\,\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2}B^{1/2}(t,X_{t})\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}.

(3)

where the divergence of the matrix-field
B
is defined as the row-wise divergence

(\divergence B(t,x))_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\partial_{x_{j}}B_{ij}(t,x),\quad i=1,\dots,d.

(4)

Note that, in addition to the preconditioning appearing in front of the force
\nabla\Psi
and the Brownian motion, the term
\divergence B
within (
3
) is necessary to maintain
\pi
as a stationary distribution. Whenever
B(t,x)
is independent of
x
, the divergence vanishes, yielding simpler dynamics.
In the case that
B(t,x)
is, in fact, independent of
t
, (
3
) is the so-called Riemannian Langevin dynamics (RML),
i.e.
, Langevin dynamics on a Riemannian manifold with metric
B^{-1}(x)

[
18
]
.
Let us denote for simplicity the drift as
b(t,x)=-B(t,x)\nabla\Psi(x)+\divergence B(t,x)
. As the space dependence of the preconditioner leads to a loss of global Lipschitz continuity of
b(t,x)
, we employ a tamed Euler discretization. More precisely, as proposed in
[
6
]
we consider as a discrete scheme approximating (
3
)

Y_{t_{k+1}}=Y_{t_{k}}+{h}_{k}b_{{h}_{k}}(t_{k},X_{k})+\sqrt{2{h}_{k}}B^{1/2}(t_{k},Y_{t_{k}})Z_{k},\quad Z_{k}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\id)

(5)

with step size
{h}_{k}>0
, time points
t_{k}=\sum_{\ell=0}^{k-1}{h}_{\ell}
and the tamed drift

b_{h}(t,x)=\frac{b(t,x)}{1+{h}|b(t,x)|}.

(6)

This process corresponds to a sampling at the time-steps
(t_{k})_{k}
of SDEdefined piece-wise for
t\in(t_{k},t_{k+1}]
as

\mathrm{d}Y_{t}=b_{{h}_{k}}(t_{k},Y_{t_{k}})\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2}B^{1/2}(t_{k},Y_{t_{k}})\mathrm{d}W_{t}.

(7)

While the considered algorithm will be proven to converge to the target under rather general assumptions on
B(t,x)
, inspired by quasi-Newton methods in optimization, we will specifically consider approximations of the inverse Hessian
B(t,x)\approx\nabla^{2}\Psi(x)^{-1}
(see
section

4
for details) as well as time-dependent convex combinations of the form
B(t,x)=\lambda_{t}B_{1}(x)+(1-\lambda_{t})B_{0}
where
B_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is a global (
i.e.
, position-independent) preconditioner,
B_{1}(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is the above mentioned approximation of the Hessian, and
\lambda_{t}\in[0,1]
satisfies
\lambda_{t}\rightarrow 1
as
t\rightarrow\infty
.

Contributions

In summary, we provide the following contributions.

•

We propose TIPreL, Langevin sampling with time- and position-dependent preconditioning. Specifically, we propose to use a time-dependent convex combination of the (estimated) covariance and a quasi-Newton matrix to combine global and local curvature information in an ideal manner.

•

We provide a rigorous and complete convergence analysis of the proposed method. This convergence analysis provides a novelty beyond the considered problem of preconditioned Langevin sampling since we prove convergence of Langevin sampling via a tamed Euler-Maruyama discretization for locally Lipschitz drift and time-dependent drift and diffusion. Under such general conditions these convergence results have not been available yet to our knowledge.

•

We provide numerical results in two dimensions as well as for a high-dimensional Bayesian logistic regression example. The numerical experiments confirm the theoretical results and the efficacy of the proposed preconditioning.

Related work

In optimization, preconditioners are used to accelerate the gradient-based minimization of ill-conditioned objective functions (see,
e.g.
,
[
29
, Chapter 5]
).
The observed gain in convergence speed has led to the adoption of various preconditioning schemes in the field of MCMCmethods
[

,
22
,
25
,
27
,
31
,
39
,
42
]
.
The simplest way to improve the behavior of the potential function in the Langevin MCMCcase is to employ a

,
i.e.
, time- and space-invariant, preconditioner

[

]
.
There exist various proposals for choices of

most prominently, (the empirical estimate of) the target covariance
B=\cov(X)

[

or the inverse of the expected Fisher information matrix

[

,
38
]
where it should be noted that the latter is the provably optimal global preconditioner with respect to the expected squared jump distance
[
37
]
.

Position-dependent preconditioning

Position dependent preconditioning is significantly more difficult due to the appearance of the divergence correction in (
3
). Nonetheless, multiple efforts in this direction have been made.
As mentioned above, if the preconditioner depends on the position
x
but not on the time
t
, the dynamics are, in fact, an instance of RML, which have been analyzed extensively in the literature
[
3
,
17
,
18
,
41
]
. However, it is worth mentioning that several of the proposed approaches analyze the geometric Euler-Maruyama discretization which assumes access to the manifold exponential
[
17
,
41
]
.
In several methods it has been proposed to avoid the (possibly expensive) computation of the correction term
\divergence B
to maintain invariance of
\pi
and instead metropolize the resulting biased scheme
[
27
,
36
,
39
]
.

Time-inhomogeneous preconditioning

A crucial contribution of the proposed work is the incorporation of time-dependence of the preconditioner. Sampling using time-inhomogeneous Langevin dynamics has recently received increased attention via,
e.g.
, tempering
[
7
]
, annealed Langevin dynamics
[
1
,
11
,
21
,
35
]
, or Moreau envelope-based approximations of the target
\pi

[

]
. All of these approaches, however, differ to the considered setting in two aspects: first, there is no time- or space-dependence of the diffusion term in the mentioned works, and second, the considered Langevin dynamics in these articles do not admit an invariant (but only a limiting) distribution as they aim at a

whereas in our case the invariant distribution remains the same independently of the value of
t
in
B(t,x)
.

3.1
Notation and preliminaries

The set of all (Borel) probability measures on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is denoted as
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and we denote the set of measures with finite
p
-th moment as
\mathcal{P}_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. We denote the distribution of the solution of the continuous time dynamics (
3
) as
\mu_{t}=\law(X_{t})
and the distribution of the approximation as
\hat{\mu}_{t}=\law(Y_{t})
. We will denote the step sizes of the discretization as
{h}_{k}
, one step size sequence as
{\mathbf{h}}=({h}_{0},{h}_{1},\dots)
and correspondingly
t_{k}=\sum_{\ell=0}^{k-1}{h}_{\ell}
.
Moreover, we denote the
solution operator
of (
2
) as
(P_{s,t})_{s\leq t}
. That is, with
(X_{t})_{t}
denoting a solution of (
2
), we have for any sufficiently smooth
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

P_{s,t}f(x)=\mathbb{E}[f(X_{t})|X_{s}=x].

(8)

Analogously, we define the Markov kernels
R_{h}(t)
corresponding to the discrete chain (
5
) via

R_{h}(t)f(x)=(2\pi)^{-d/2}\int f\big(x+{h}b_{h}(t,x)+\sqrt{2{h}}B^{1/2}(t,x)\big)\exp\left(-\frac{|z|^{2}}{2}\right)\mathrm{d}z

(9)

We denote the concatenation of multiple discrete steps as
Q_{t_{\ell},t_{k}}^{\mathbf{h}}=R_{{h}_{\ell}}(t_{\ell})R_{{h}_{\ell+1}}(t_{\ell+1})\cdots R_{{h}_{k}}(t_{k})
. The action of the adjoints of
P_{s,t}
,
R_{h}(h)
, and
Q_{t_{\ell},t_{k}}^{\mathbf{h}}
on measures
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is denoted as
\mu P_{s,t}
,
\mu R_{h}(t)
, and
\mu Q_{t_{\ell},t_{k}}^{\mathbf{h}}
.
As is customary, for
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
we denote
\mu(f)\coloneqq\int f\mathrm{d}\mu
. For any
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
we denote
\min\{a,b\}=a\wedge b
and
\max\{a,b\}=a\vee b
.

3.2
Main results

Before stating and discussing the main results we list the necessary assumptions.

Assumption 1
.

1.

The potential
\Psi\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
\nabla\Psi

L
-Lipschitz. Without loss of generality
\nabla\Psi(0)=0
.

2.

B(t,x)
is bounded above and below, i.e.,
\beta_{\text{l}}\id\preceq B(t,x)\preceq\beta_{\text{u}}\id
for some
\beta_{\text{l}},\beta_{\text{u}}>0
.

3.

B(t,x)
is twice continuously differentiable with respect to
x
and both
B
and
\divergence B
are locally Hölder in time and locally Lipschitz in space in the following sense: There exist
\beta_{\text{l}},\beta_{\text{u}},L,{n_{B}}>0
,
\delta\geq 1/2
such that for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
s,t>0
it holds

\displaystyle|B(t,x)-B(t,y)|\vee|\divergence B(t,x)-\divergence B(t,y)|

\displaystyle\leq L(1+|x|^{n_{B}}+|y|^{n_{B}})|x-y|

(10)

\displaystyle|B(s,x)-B(t,x)|\vee|\divergence B(s,x)-\divergence B(t,x)|

\displaystyle\leq L(1+|x|^{n_{B}})|s-t|^{\delta}

4.

Denoting
b(t,x)=-B(t,x)\nabla\Psi(x)+\divergence B(t,x)
, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{r\rightarrow\infty}\sup_{|x|\geq r,\,t\geq 0}\left\langle\frac{x}{|x|},\frac{b(t,x)}{|b(t,x)|}\right\rangle

\displaystyle<0,\quad\text{and }\lim_{r\rightarrow\infty}\inf_{|x|\geq r,\,t\geq 0}|b(t,x)|=\infty.

(11)

Remark 1
.

1.

The proofs work identically, when
\nabla\Psi
is also only locally Lipschitz similar to
B
, i.e., for a
n\in\mathbb{N}

|\nabla\Psi(x)-\nabla\Psi(y)|\vee|\divergence B(t,x)-\divergence B(t,y)|\leq L(1+|x|^{n}+|y|^{n})|x-y|.

(12)

2.

Of course, the local Lipschitz constants and the growth constants
L,{n_{B}}
could be different for the different assumptions above, but are assumed the same for notational simplicity.

3.

The last assumption is in particular satisfied, if
\divergence B
is bounded and

\displaystyle\lim_{r\rightarrow\infty}\sup_{|x|\geq r,\,t\geq 0}\left\langle\frac{x}{|x|},\frac{B(t,x)\nabla\Psi(x)}{|B(t,x)\nabla\Psi(x)|}\right\rangle

\displaystyle<0,\quad\lim_{r\rightarrow\infty}\inf_{|x|\geq r,\,t\geq 0}|B(t,x)\nabla\Psi(x)|=\infty.

(13)

This assumption, in turn, can be ensured by requiring the identical assumption on the potential without the preconditioning
B
together with some conformity, that is, the angle-preserving property of the preconditioner
B
.

Inspired by
[
9
]
, we additionally define a preconditioned log-Sobolev inequality (PLSI)as follows.

Definition 1

(Preconditioned log-Sobolev inequality)
.

We say that

satisfies PLSIwith preconditioner

such that

\int f^{2}\log\frac{f^{2}}{\int f^{2}\mathrm{d}\nu}\mathrm{d}\nu\leq C_{\mathrm{PLSI}}\int|\nabla f|^{2}_{B}\mathrm{d}\nu,\quad\text{for all locally Lipschitz $f\in L^{2}(\nu)$.}

(PLSI)

As a second assumption, we require the target
\pi
to satisfy such a PLSI.

Assumption 2
.

For all
t
,
\pi
satisfies PLSIwith preconditioner
B(t,x)
and constant
C_{\mathrm{PLSI}}(t)
.

Remark 2
.

1.

Note that in our setting due to boundedness and uniform positive definiteness, the PLSIis equivalent to the regular log-Sobolev inequality (LSI), i.e., with preconditioner
B=\id
. However, we expect

to be smaller for well-chosen

in comparison to the case

2.

An LSIis, in particular satisfies, when the potential is dissipative, that is,
\langle\nabla\Psi(x),x\rangle\geq a|x|^{2}+b
for some

and all

As a first result we can derive exponential convergence as in the setting without preconditioning. However, in this case the convergence rate will be improved for well-chosen

4
Numerical experiments

In this section, we consider several numerical experiments
1
1

1

All the numerical experiments were conducted on an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4090 GPU.
comparing various preconditioner choices and, in particular, demonstrate that time- and position-dependent preconditioning leads to advantageous convergence.
Concretely, we compare the following choices for
B(t,x)
:

•

Constant scalar
:
B(t,x)=1/L
, where
L
is the Lipschitz constant of
\nabla\Psi
or the maximum spectral norm
\|\nabla^{2}\Psi\|_{2}
observed in a representative domain.
This effectively constitutes a worst-case estimate of the potential curvature and thus serves as a baseline method.
Note that, choosing a discretization step size
h=1
, this method is equivalent to performing unadjusted Langevin algorithm (ULA)with a step size of
1/L
.

•

Global covariance
:
B(t,x)\approx\cov_{\pi}[X]=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[(X-\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[X])(X-\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[X])^{\top}]
, which we empirically estimate from samples from
\pi
.
As the covariance of a random variable characterizes its spatial spread along different directions, we expect this preconditioning scheme to accelerate the mixing rate of the simulated chains, leading to faster global mode coverage.
However, in most practical scenarios, it is hard to obtain a good estimate of the covariance.
A strategy to overcome this limitation by local approximations is presented in
[
25
]
.

•

Inverse expected Fisher information
: Analogously, we also estimate
B(t,x)\approx\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[\nabla^{2}\Psi(X)]^{-1}
from ground truth data.
Opposed to the global covariance strategy, this preconditioning scheme describes the expected curvature of the potential we want to sample from.
It is expected that the local behavior of the simulated chains will improve. An assumption also formalized in
[
37
]
.
However, as in the previous case, this strategy will break down when sampling from potentials with diverse local geometries.

•

Curvature-aware preconditioning
: Further, based on the potential Hessian
\nabla^{2}\Psi
, we consider a position-dependent preconditioner of the form
B(t,x)=Q(x)\Lambda^{-1}(x)Q(x)^{\top}
, with
\Lambda(x)=\diag(|\lambda_{1}|_{\varepsilon},\dots,|\lambda_{d}|_{\varepsilon})
, and
\lambda_{i}
the eigenvalues of
\nabla^{2}\Psi(x)
belonging to the eigenvectors in
Q(x)
.
Moreover,
|\cdot|_{\varepsilon}=\max\{|\cdot|,\varepsilon\}
with
\varepsilon>0
denotes an approximation of the absolute value clamped away from zero.
Similar techniques have been applied for Hamiltonian Monte Carlo in
[
2
]
.
We hypothesize that this preconditioning scheme excels when sampling from potentials with locally diverse geometry.
However, approximating this preconditioner without access to
\nabla^{2}\Psi
remains an open problem
[
34
]
.
Also, the position-dependence necessitates the incorporation of the correction term from
eq.

4
, which in general is computationally demanding.

•

Global-local interpolation
: Finally, to combine the strengths of global and position-dependent preconditioning, we propose
B(t,x)=(1-\lambda_{t})\cov_{\pi}[X]+\lambda_{t}Q(x)\Lambda^{-1}(x)Q(x)^{\top}
with
\lambda_{t}\in[0,1]
monotonically increasing.
This constitutes a time-dependent preconditioning that evolves from a global covariance preconditioner to a geometry-adaptive approximate Hessian preconditioner, combining global and local curvature information in an advantageous manner.
The global-to-local nature of this preconditioner is motivated by the fact that inverse-Hessian preconditioning is likely more effective when samples are already close to a mode, which will be the case at later stages of the dynamics.
Conversely, at the beginning of the sampling, we emphasize
mode coverage
by utilizing the covariance.
In practice, we set the schedule
\lambda_{t}=\min\{\frac{2t}{Kh},1\}
, where
K
is the total number of Langevin steps and
h
denotes the (constant) discretization step size.

acts element-wise. The potential fulfills

5
Conclusion

In this work, we have introduced a time- and position-dependent preconditioning framework
for Langevin-based sampling and established convergence guarantees under general conditions.
In particular, the proven convergence results are of interest beyond the proposed applications as they apply more broadly to Langevin sampling with locally Lipschitz continuous drifts and time- and position-dependent drifts and diffusions.
In numerical experiments, we have shown improved sampling performance with position- or position- and time-dependent preconditioning for Langevin sampling compared to constant preconditioning.

5.1
Limitations and future research

The main limitation of the proposed approach is the computation of the divergence
\divergence B
.
In future research, we would like to investigate the approximate computation of this term in greater detail.
Moreover, inspired by quasi-Newton methods in optimization, like L-BFGS
[
29
]
, we would like to explore different efficient approximate Hessian preconditioners such as diagonal or learned preconditioners.

A.3
Drift condition and moment bounds for the continuous dynamics

Based on the Fokker-Planck equation we define the following time-dependent generator of the diffusion

\mathcal{L}_{t}(V)=-\langle\nabla V,B\nabla\Psi\rangle+\divergence(B\nabla V).

(81)

We can derive the following drift condition for
\mathcal{L}_{t}
.

Lemma 4
.

Let
V_{a}(x)=\exp\left(a(1+|x|^{2})^{1/2}\right)
. For any
a>0
, there exist
\lambda,\rho>0
such that for all
t\geq 0

\mathcal{L}_{t}(V_{a})(x)\leq-\lambda V_{a}(x)+\rho.

(82)

Proof.

We compute

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{t}(V_{a})(x)=

\displaystyle\langle\nabla V_{a}(x),-B(t,x)\nabla\Psi(x)\rangle+\divergence(B\nabla V_{a}(x))

(83)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\nabla V_{a}(x),-B(t,x)\nabla\Psi(x)\rangle+\langle\divergence B(t,x),\nabla V_{a}(x)\rangle+\trace(B(t,x)\nabla^{2}V_{a}(x))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\nabla V_{a}(x),b(t,x)\rangle+\trace(B(t,x)\nabla^{2}V_{a}(x))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{aV_{a}(x)}{(1+|x|^{2})^{1/2}}\langle x,b(t,x)\rangle+\trace(B(t,x)\nabla^{2}V_{a}(x))

It holds
\trace(B\nabla^{2}V_{a})\leq\beta|\nabla^{2}V_{a}(x)|_{S^{1}}
with
|\cdot|_{S^{1}}
the Schatten-
1
norm. We can compute

\nabla^{2}V_{a}(x)=aV_{a}(x)\left(\frac{axx^{\top}}{1+|x|^{2}}+\frac{(1+|x|^{2})^{1/2}\id-x\frac{x^{\top}}{(1+|x|^{2})^{1/2}}}{1+|x|^{2}}\right).

(84)

It holds true that
\nabla^{2}V(x)\succeq 0
and we find for any
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
|z|\leq 1

\displaystyle z^{\top}\nabla^{2}V_{a}(x)z=

\displaystyle aV_{a}(x)\left(\frac{a|z^{\top}x|^{2}}{1+|x|^{2}}+\frac{(1+|x|^{2})^{1/2}|z|^{2}-\frac{|z^{\top}x|^{2}}{(1+|x|^{2})^{1/2}}}{1+|x|^{2}}\right)

(85)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle aV_{a}(x)\left(a|z|^{2}+\frac{|z|^{2}}{(1+|x|^{2})^{1/2}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle a(1+a)V_{a}(x).

so that
|\nabla^{2}V_{a}(x)|_{S^{1}}\leq da(1+a)V_{a}(x)
. Inserting into

83
yields

\mathcal{L}_{t}(V_{a})(x)\leq V_{a}(x)\left(\frac{a}{(1+|x|^{2})^{1/2}}\langle x,b(t,x)\rangle+\beta_{\text{u}}da(1+a)\right).

(86)

By
1
we may choose

It follows that there exists some

(88)

Inserting above yields

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{t}(V_{a})(x)\leq

\displaystyle V_{a}(x)\left(-\frac{a(\kappa|x||b(t,x)|-C)}{(1+|x|^{2})^{1/2}}+\beta_{\text{u}}da(1+a)\right)

implying the drift condition since

\displaystyle|x|^{2}+2{h}\langle x,b_{h}(t,x)\rangle+{h}^{2}|b_{h}(t,,x)|^{2}

(97)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|x|^{2}+|x|{h}\bigg(2\bigg\langle\frac{x}{|x|},b_{h}(t,x)\bigg\rangle+\frac{{h}}{|x|}|b_{h}(t,,x)|^{2}\bigg).

By
lemma

12
, we can pick
r,\kappa
independent of
{h}>0
such that

\bigg(2\bigg\langle\frac{x}{|x|},b_{h}(t,x)\bigg\rangle+\frac{{h}}{|x|}|b_{h}(t,,x)|^{2}\bigg)\leq-\kappa

(98)

for
|x|\geq r
. Potentially increasing
r
so that

we obtain

\displaystyle|x+{h}b_{h}(t,x)|^{2}+2d\beta_{\text{u}}{h}\leq|x|^{2}-|x|{h}(\kappa-2d\beta_{\text{u}}/|x|)\leq|x|^{2}-|x|{h}\kappa/2.

(99)

Note that for any
a>0
, the function

is concave on
[-a,\infty)
so that a linear approximation in
s=0
yields for all

(a+s)^{1/2}\leq a^{1/2}+\frac{s}{2a^{1/2}}.

Applied to
\big(1+|x|^{2}-|x|{h}\kappa/2\big)^{1/2}
with
a=1+|x|^{2}
we get

\displaystyle\big(1+|x+{h}b_{h}(t,x)|^{2}+{2d\beta_{\text{u}}{h}}\big)^{1/2}\leq

\displaystyle\big(1+|x|^{2}-|x|{h}\kappa/2\big)^{1/2}

(100)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\big(1+|x|^{2}\big)^{1/2}-\frac{|x|{h}\kappa/2}{2\big(1+|x|^{2}\big)^{1/2}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\big(1+|x|^{2}\big)^{1/2}-\frac{r{h}\kappa}{4\big(1+r^{2}\big)^{1/2}}

where in the last inequality we made use of the fact that
s\mapsto s/(1+s^{2})^{1/2}
is non-decreasing.
Setting
a<\frac{r\kappa}{4\beta_{\text{u}}\big(1+r^{2}\big)^{1/2}}
we obtain from (
96
)

R_{h}(t)V_{a}(x)\leq\exp\left(-ha\left(\frac{r\kappa}{4\left(1+r^{2}\right)^{1/2}}-a\beta_{\text{u}}\right)\right)V_{a}(x).

(101)

where
\lambda\coloneqq a\frac{r\kappa}{4\big(1+r^{2}\big)^{1/2}}-a\beta_{\text{u}}>0
.
On the other hand-side, we find

\displaystyle\big(1+|x+

\displaystyle{\displaystyle h}b_{h}(t,x)|^{2}+{2d\beta_{\text{u}}{h}}\big)^{1/2}

(102)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\big(1+|x|^{2}+2{h}|x||b_{h}(t,x)|+{h}^{2}|b_{h}(t,x)|^{2}+{2d\beta_{\text{u}}{h}}\big)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\big(1+|x|^{2}\big)^{1/2}+{h}\frac{2|x||b_{h}(t,x)|+{h}|b_{h}(t,x)|^{2}+2d\beta_{\text{u}}}{2(1+|x|^{2})^{1/2}}.

By
lemma

11
, there exists
C>0
such that for all
{h}\leq{h}_{\max}
and
|x|\leq R

\frac{2|x||b_{h}(t,x)|+{h}|b_{h}(t,x)|^{2}+2d\beta_{\text{u}}}{2(1+|x|^{2})^{1/2}}\leq C.

(103)

Therefore, for
|x|\leq C
we have

\displaystyle R_{h}(t)V_{a}(x)\leq

\displaystyle\exp\left(a^{2}\beta_{\text{u}}{h}\right)\exp\left(a\big(1+|x+{h}b_{h}(t,x)|^{2}+{2d\beta_{\text{u}}{h}}\big)^{1/2}\right)

(104)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\exp\left(a^{2}\beta_{\text{u}}{h}\right)\exp\left(a\big(1+|x|^{2})^{1/2}+C{h}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\exp\left(a\big(1+|x|^{2})^{1/2}+(C+a^{2}\beta_{\text{u}}){h}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle V_{a}(x)\exp\left((C+a^{2}\beta_{\text{u}}){h}\right)

so that the elementary inequality
1-\exp\left(s\right)\leq s
yields

\displaystyle R_{h}(t)V_{a}(x)-V_{a}(x)

\displaystyle\exp\left(-\lambda{h}\right)\leq V_{a}(x)\left(\exp\left((C+a^{2}\beta_{\text{u}}){h}\right)-\exp\left(-\lambda{h}\right)\right)

(105)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle V_{a}(x)\exp\left((C+a^{2}\beta_{\text{u}}){h}\right)\left(1-\exp\left(-(\lambda+C+a^{2}\beta_{\text{u}}){h}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle V_{a}(x)\exp\left((C+a^{2}\beta_{\text{u}}){h}\right)(\lambda+C+a^{2}\beta_{\text{u}}){h}

so that we obtain the desired result with

b=\exp\left((1+r^{2})^{1/2}\right)\exp\left((C+a^{2}\beta_{\text{u}}){h}_{\max}\right)(\lambda+C+a^{2}\beta_{\text{u}}).

(106)

∎

Lemma 6
.

Let
a<a_{0}
and
V_{a}
as in
lemma

5
. If
\mu_{0}(V_{a})<\infty
, then

\sup_{{\mathbf{h}},k}\mu_{0}Q^{\mathbf{h}}_{0,t_{k}}(V_{a})<\infty.

(107)

In particular, all moments of the discrete scheme are uniformly bounded, that is, for every

such that

(108)

Proof.

We can write

\mu_{0}Q^{\mathbf{h}}_{0,t_{k}}(V_{a})=\mu_{0}(R_{{h}_{1}}(0)\cdots R_{{h}_{k}}(t_{k-1})V_{a}).

(109)

In turn, by

5
we have

\hat{\mu}_{0}(R_{{h}_{0}}(0)\cdots R_{{h}_{k-1}}(t_{k-1})V_{a}(x))\leq\exp\left(-a^{2}t_{k}\right)\hat{\mu}_{0}(V_{a})+b\sum_{\ell=0}^{k}{h}_{\ell}\exp\left(-(t_{k}-t_{\ell})\right)

(110)

so that the result follows if
\sum_{\ell=0}^{k}{h}_{\ell}\exp\left(-(t_{k}-t_{\ell})\right)<\infty
. Let us denote for a specific sequence
{\mathbf{h}}=({h}_{0},{h}_{1},\dots)

S_{k}^{{\mathbf{h}}}=\sum_{\ell=0}^{k}{h}_{\ell}\exp\left(-(t_{k}-t_{\ell})\right).

We have
S_{k+1}^{{\mathbf{h}}}=\exp\left(-{h}_{k+1}\right)S_{k}^{{\mathbf{h}}}+{h}_{k+1}
. Consider the mapping

implying that

\displaystyle\sup_{{\mathbf{h}},k}S^{{\mathbf{h}}}_{k}\leq\sup_{{\mathbf{h}},k}\max\left\{S^{{\mathbf{h}}}_{k-1},\frac{h_{k}}{1-\exp\left(-h_{k}\right)}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{{\mathbf{h}},k}\max\left\{S^{{\mathbf{h}}}_{k-1},\sup_{h\leq{\mathbf{h}}_{\max}}\frac{h}{1-\exp\left(-h\right)}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\max\left\{{h}_{0},\sup_{h\leq{\mathbf{h}}_{\max}}\frac{h}{1-\exp\left(-h\right)}\right\}

\displaystyle<\infty.

∎

Lemma 7
.

The

Via integration over

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\hat{q}_{t}(x)=

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\divergence(D(x,t|y,t_{k})\hat{q}(x,t|y,t_{k}))\hat{q}_{t_{k}}(y)\mathrm{d}y+\int\divergence(B(t,x)\nabla\hat{q}(x,t|y,t_{k}))\hat{q}_{t_{k}}(y)\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\divergence\bigg(\int D(x,t|y,t_{k})\hat{q}(y,t_{k}|x,t)\mathrm{d}y\hat{q}_{t}(x)\bigg)+\divergence\bigg(B(t,x)\nabla\hat{q}_{t}(x)\bigg).

Note that

\displaystyle|(B(t_{k},x_{t_{k}})-

\displaystyle B(t,x))\nabla\log\hat{q}(x,t|x_{t_{k}},t_{k})|

\displaystyle-\int\nabla\log\frac{\hat{q}_{t}}{p}\cdot\int D(x,t|y,t_{k})\hat{q}(y,t_{k}|x,t)\hat{q}_{t}(x)\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle-\int\nabla\log\frac{\hat{q}_{t}}{p}\cdot\int B(t_{k},x)\nabla\Psi(x)\hat{q}(y,t_{k}|x,t)\hat{q}_{t}(x)\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}x

where we

\langle p,q\rangle=\langle(\varepsilon B)^{1/2}p,(\varepsilon B)^{-1/2}q\rangle\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}|p|_{B}^{2}+\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}|q|_{B^{-1}}^{2}\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}|p|_{B}^{2}+\frac{1}{2\varepsilon\beta_{\text{l}}}|q|^{2}

\displaystyle C+D=

\displaystyle-\int\nabla\log\frac{\hat{q}_{t}}{p}\cdot\int(B(t_{k},y)\nabla\Psi(y)-B(t_{k},x)\nabla\Psi(x))\hat{q}(y,t_{k}|x,t)\hat{q}_{t}(x)\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\int\varepsilon|\nabla\log\big(\frac{\hat{q}_{t}}{p}\big)|^{2}_{B(t_{k},x)}\hat{q}_{t}\mathrm{d}x

{\mathbf{h}}
,

\displaystyle\int

\displaystyle\bigg(\int\bigg\{(B(t_{k},y)-B(t,x))\nabla\log\hat{q}(x,t|y,t_{k})\bigg\}\hat{q}(y,t_{k}|x,t)\mathrm{d}y\bigg)^{2}\hat{q}_{t}(x)

\displaystyle\iint\bigg\{L(1+|x|^{n_{B}}+|y|^{n_{B}})(|t-t_{k}|^{\delta}+|x-y|)

\iint\dots\hat{q}(x,t,y,t_{k})\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y=\int\int\dots q(x,t|y,t_{k})\mathrm{d}x\hat{q}_{t_{k}}(y)\mathrm{d}y
. Moreover, we can write

(124)

for some constant

due to the moment bounds.
In particular, for
\delta\geq 1/2

\displaystyle\int|t-t_{k}|^{2(\delta-1)}|y-x|^{2}\hat{q}(x,t|y,t_{k})\mathrm{d}x\leq C_{1}<

\displaystyle\infty

(125)

\displaystyle\int|t-t_{k}|^{-2}|y-x|^{4}\hat{q}(x,t|y,t_{k})\mathrm{d}x\leq C_{2}<

\displaystyle\infty

where we emphasize both constants

,

{\mathbf{h}}
. In summary, we find

\displaystyle C+D\leq

\displaystyle\int\varepsilon|\nabla\log\big(\frac{\hat{q}_{t}}{p}\big)|^{2}_{B(t_{k},x)}\hat{q}_{t}\mathrm{d}x+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}C_{3}

Combining this with the estimate for

\displaystyle-\frac{2}{C_{\mathrm{PLSI}}^{\max}}\KL(\hat{\mu}_{t}|\pi)+2C_{3}

Lemma 8

\displaystyle|B^{1/2}(t,x)-B^{1/2}(t,y)|\vee

Lemma 13
.

A.6
Details for the two-dimensional Rosenbrock potential

To produce ground truth data
X=(X_{1},X_{2})\sim\pi
from the Rosenbrock target, we use ancestral sampling,
i.e.
, we perform

\begin{cases}X_{1}\sim\mathcal{N}(a,\frac{1}{2}),\\
X_{2}\sim\mathcal{N}(X_{1}^{2},\frac{1}{2b}).\end{cases}

(147)

The Rosenbrock probability density function (PDF)is given as

p(x)=\frac{1}{Z}\exp(-\Psi(x)),

(148)

with partition function
Z=\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{b}}
.
Further, the gradient
\nabla\Psi
and Hessian
\nabla^{2}\Psi
of the Rosenbrock potential are implemented numerically in PyTorch in a batch-based fashion using automatic differentiation.

A.7
Details for Bayesian logistic regression

The posterior distribution is given as

p(\beta\mid\{x_{i},y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N})\propto\prod_{i=1}^{N}\varphi(\beta^{\top}x_{i})^{y_{i}}(1-\varphi(\beta^{\top}x_{i}))^{1-y_{i}}p(\beta).

The potential is derived as

\displaystyle\Psi(\beta\mid\{x_{i},y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N})

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i=1}^{N}y_{i}\log\varphi(\beta^{\top}x_{i})+(1-y_{i})\log(1-\varphi(\beta^{\top}x_{i}))-\log p(\beta)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i=1}^{N}y_{i}(\beta^{\top}x_{i}-\log(1+\exp(\beta^{\top}x_{i})))+\log(1-\varphi(\beta^{\top}x_{i}))

\displaystyle\hskip 42.67912pt-y_{i}\log(1-\varphi(\beta^{\top}x_{i}))-\log p(\beta)

by noting that
\log(1-\varphi(u))=-\log(1+\exp(u))\,,u\in\mathbb{R}
we obtain

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log(1+\exp(\beta^{\top}x_{i}))-y_{i}\beta^{\top}x_{i}-\log p(\beta)

plugging in the prior introduced in
Section

4.2
yields

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log(1+\exp(\beta^{\top}x_{i}))-y_{i}\beta^{\top}x_{i}+\frac{1}{2}\|\beta\|_{\Sigma^{-1}}^{2}.

The gradient of
\Psi
is

\displaystyle\nabla\Psi(\beta\mid\{x_{i},y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{x_{i}\exp(\beta^{\top}x_{i})}{1+\exp(\beta^{\top}x_{i})}-y_{i}x_{i}+\Sigma^{-1}\beta

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}(\varphi(\beta^{\top}x_{i})-y_{i})+\Sigma^{-1}\beta

\displaystyle=X^{\top}(\varphi(X\beta)-y)+\Sigma^{-1}\beta.

The Hessian is found to be

\nabla^{2}\Psi(\beta\mid\{x_{i},y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N})=X^{\top}\diag(s_{1},\dots,s_{N})X+\Sigma^{-1},

where
s_{i}=\varphi(\beta^{\top}x_{i})(1-\varphi(\beta^{\top}x_{i}))
.
Recalling that
\Sigma=\diag(\sigma_{1}^{2},\dots,\sigma_{d}^{2})
and using the bound
0<\varphi(u)(1-\varphi(u))\leq\frac{1}{4}
for all
u\in\mathbb{R}
, we get that
L\leq\frac{1}{4}\lambda_{\max}(\bar{X}^{\top}\bar{X})+\max_{i}(1/\sigma_{i}^{2})
. Moreover, since
X^{\top}\diag(s_{1},\dots,s_{N})X\succeq 0
and
\sigma_{1}^{2},\dots\sigma_{d}^{2}>0
it follows that
\nabla^{2}\Psi\succ 0
(
i.e.
, all Eigenvalues of the Hessian are strictly positive). Therefore, we omit Eigenvalue clamping (see
Section

4
) when calculating the curvature-aware preconditioner in the Bayesian logistic regression experiments.
