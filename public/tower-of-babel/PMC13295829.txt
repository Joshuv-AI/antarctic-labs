Understanding self-management and digital health needs in musicians with playing-related musculoskeletal disorders: an explanatory sequential mixed-methods study
Authors: Noudy Eleryan, Rebecca Hemming, Valerie Sparkes, Liba Sheeran
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesTo explore the characteristics of musicians with current or previous troubling musculoskeletal symptoms associated with musician self-limitation of activity (often referred to as playing-related musculoskeletal disorders (PRMSDs)), barriers and facilitators to PRMSD self-management, health needs and perspectives on digital health interventions (DHIs). The purpose of the study was to understand the unique needs of this population to effectively inform future DHIs to support this community.DesignA mixed-methods study comprising an online survey and four online focus groups (2–3 participants per group). Survey data were analysed statistically, and focus group discussions were analysed thematically.SettingOnline survey and focus groups conducted via Zoom.ParticipantsUK-based musicians with current or previous PRMSDs.ResultsA total of 100 musicians participated in the survey study, and 10 musicians participated in the focus group study. Over a third (38%, n=38) of participants had experienced musculoskeletal symptoms that interfered with their ability to play at their usual level 0–2 times in the last 5 years, 24% (n=24) reported 3–5 episodes, and 38% (n=38) had experienced them more than five times. The majority of participants (67%, n=67) continued playing music while experiencing these symptoms. Several barriers to self-management were identified, including limited music-specific health education during training, financial and time constraints and insufficient accessible resources. Key facilitators and health needs included healthcare professionals who understood musicians’ professional demands and performance pressures, personalised instrument-specific health support, peer support from fellow musicians and accessible digital resources including self-assessment tools, automated reminders, health tracking, motivational features, peer support forums and multimedia resources organised by condition or symptom stage. Addressing these needs could enable musicians to better manage their symptoms, maintain playing activity and make informed decisions about when and how to seek help.ConclusionThis study reveals unmet health needs in musicians and offers recommendations for designing DHIs that promote musicians’ health and well-being. Key design recommendations include personalisation by instrument type, health tracking, peer support and consolidated multimedia resources. These findings can inform both intervention design and policy development to better support this population.Keywords: Musculoskeletal Disorders, Self-management, eHealth

Introduction

Musicians may limit playing when they experience symptoms such as pain, weakness, numbness, tingling or burning. This is sometimes referred to as playing-related musculoskeletal disorders (PRMSDs).1,4 Consistent with the biopsychosocial model, the experience of PRMSDs is shaped by physical, psychological and social factors, including stress and distress.5 Despite growing recognition of these issues, research remains limited, particularly regarding demographic and occupational factors6 7 and effective interventions.8 9

Existing interventions primarily focus on health education or exercise, often addressing only physical factors while neglecting psychological and social aspects.8 Such interventions have largely targeted orchestral and student musicians, overlooking self-employed musicians.8 10 Self-employed musicians constitute the majority of the workforce and face additional challenges, including variable workloads, financial pressures and limited healthcare access.10 Digital health interventions (DHIs) may offer a promising avenue for providing personalised, widely accessible self-management support.11 DHIs encompass electronic health (eHealth), initially delivered via computers, and mobile health (mHealth) applications using mobile and wireless devices.12

Although several studies have incorporated DHIs for PRMSD self-management, the evidence is limited.13,19 Existing studies have been constrained by methodological limitations, such as lack of statistical analysis, high attrition rates and insufficient detail about intervention content.13,20 The development processes lack transparency, with no theory-based DHIs or DHIs co-designed with musicians and other relevant partners.13,20 The Medical Research Council Framework for the development of complex interventions21 emphasises early engagement with target populations to identify relevant problems and priorities, while the Behaviour Change Wheel highlights the importance of understanding behavioural determinants when designing interventions.22 Accordingly, this study explored the characteristics of musicians with current or previous PRMSDs, their barriers and facilitators to self-management, health needs and perspectives on DHIs. The purpose of the study was to understand the unique needs of this population to effectively inform future DHIs to support this community.

Methods

Study design

This study was underpinned by pragmatism and used an explanatory sequential mixed-methods design23 comprising two phases. Phase I involved a cross-sectional survey to explore the characteristics of musicians with current or previous PRMSDs, their self-management strategies, perceived health needs and interest in DHI. Phase II comprised focus groups that explored participants’ experiences in depth, including barriers and facilitators to PRMSD self-management, health needs and views on using DHIs to support the needs identified. Survey and focus group data were analysed separately and subsequently integrated during interpretation.

Ethical approval

Ethical approval was obtained from the School of Healthcare Sciences Research Ethics Committee, Cardiff University (REC 889). Survey participants provided written voluntary informed consent. Focus group participants provided both written and verbal informed consent. Prior to participation, all participants were informed of the study purpose, investigator details, data storage procedures and estimated study duration. Participants who agreed to be contacted for Phase II provided contact details, which were stored securely and deleted following Phase II recruitment. All data were anonymised and stored securely in accordance with institutional guidelines. No incentives were offered for participation.

Patient and public involvement

There was no formally recruited patient or public representative to the research team. However, the survey questions were reviewed by professional musicians prior to pilot testing to ensure they were clear, relevant and contextually appropriate for musicians.

Phase I: online survey

Development and pretesting

An online survey was developed using JISC Online Surveys24 and comprised 30 questions (table 1) across four sections. Survey questions were based on existing literature25,27 and piloted.28 The usability and technical functionality of the electronic questionnaire were tested prior to piloting and actual data collection. The pilot results and final survey are provided in the supplementary material. Pilot results were not included in the final analysis.

Eligibility criteria

Eligible participants were professional instrumental musicians aged ≥18 years, whose primary source of income was derived from working in the music industry (eg, professional orchestral musician, freelance musician, chamber musician or music teacher), or full-time tertiary music students (ie, university-level students majoring in a musical instrument). All participants had current or previous PRMSDs (in the past 5 years), defined as self-reported pain, weakness, numbness, tingling or burning that lasted >48 hours and interfered with their ability to play their instrument at their usual level.1 The 5-year time frame was selected because the study was conducted during the COVID pandemic (2021–2023): a period that had a significant negative impact on the music industry.29

Participants were excluded if they were unable or unwilling to provide informed consent, had no internet access or could not read or understand English.

Sampling method

Convenience sampling was used owing to its efficiency in terms of time and resources.30 In 2022, the total population of working musicians in the UK was approximately 37 800.31 Using the most conservative prevalence assumption (50% for the maximum sample size required), a margin of error of 5% and a 95% confidence level, a sample of 381 musicians was calculated using an online sample size calculator.32 Given the novelty of the project, achieving the full sample size within the project’s timeline was challenging. Therefore, it was decided to proceed with the available sample (n=100) while acknowledging the potential lack of statistical power.

Recruitment

Recruitment involved collaboration with the British Association for Performing Arts Medicine (BAPAM), the Musicians’ Union, The Music Agency (https://themusicagency.com), the Royal College of Music, Royal Welsh College of Music and Drama and Cardiff University School of Music. The survey was disseminated through these organisations via emails, newsletters and social media (Instagram, Facebook and X), using an online poster (online supplemental material).

Data collection

Data were collected via a self-administered web-based survey (JISC) between12 October 2022 and February 2023. The survey was distributed in the open mode, meaning no login or password was required to access it.

Data analysis

Survey data were analysed using IBM SPSS V.29. Survey responses were summarised using descriptive statistics. Categorical data were analysed using χ2 tests. Continuous data were analysed using analysis of variance or Kruskal–Wallis tests as appropriate. Ordinal logistic regression explored age, employment type, weekly practice and working hours and PRMSD frequency. Prior to analysis, the proportional odds assumption was verified using the test of parallel lines. Model fit was assessed using the Pearson and Deviance goodness-of-fit statistics. Open-text responses were analysed using content analysis.

Phase II: Focus groups

Design

The focus group topic guide was informed by the survey findings, and open and neutral questions were used to minimise response and recollection bias.33 34 The topic guide was pilot-tested with two eligible participants (one male self-employed classical musician and one female retired orchestral musician). An initial introductory question about musicians’ experiences with PRMSDs proved to be too time-consuming and made it difficult to steer discussion toward the study’s aim; therefore, it was removed from the final topic guide (box 1). As changes were minimal, pilot results were included in the final analysis.

Sampling and recruitment

Participants were purposively sampled from the 69 survey respondents who expressed interest in phase II via email. Twenty confirmed attendance; however, half failed to attend (no reasons provided), resulting in four focus groups of 2–3 participants (no repeat focus groups).

Data collection

Semi-structured focus group discussions were conducted online via Zoom between March and December 2023. Sessions lasted 45–60 min and were audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim. All sessions were led by one female researcher (NE), a chartered physiotherapist experienced in qualitative research methods.

Data analysis

Reflexive thematic analysis was conducted using Braun and Clarke’s35 six-step approach. The analysis was primarily inductive but also included a deductive element guided by the survey findings and the study’s overarching objectives. Automatic transcripts were generated by Zoom, cross-checked against the audio recordings multiple times and anonymised. One researcher (NE) familiarised herself with the data by reading and listening to all transcripts. After familiarisation, initial codes for each transcript were developed by NE and then reviewed and discussed with all members of the research team (VS, RH, LS). An example of a focus group transcript and initial codes are provided in online supplemental material table 4. Codes represented the semantic content of the data, from which themes were generated. Inductive thematic saturation was achieved by the fourth focus group. Saturation was determined through an iterative process, whereby each transcript was coded and compared against previously identified themes until no new codes or themes were generated. The themes were discussed as a team, and any disagreements were debated until a consensus was reached. An initial thematic map was generated, and all authors reviewed the codes (online supplemental material). Some themes and subthemes were recoded to produce the final themes and subthemes (figure 1).

Transferability and dependability were addressed through detailed descriptions of methods, while credibility and confirmability were strengthened via member checking (no changes were required from participants) and reflexivity.36 Participants were aware that all researchers were physiotherapists with expertise in musculoskeletal management and interest in DHIs; none were musicians or had any relationships with the participants. The lead researcher (NE) maintained a reflexive diary (online supplemental material) throughout data collection and analysis.

Results

Phase I: online survey

One hundred musicians completed the survey. Response rates varied considerably, with social media (Facebook/Instagram) yielding the highest response rate at 35.4%, while institutional channels ranged from 0.3% to 6.8% (online supplemental table 2). This estimate should be interpreted with caution because of potential overlap in recruitment channels.

Demographic characteristics

Participants were 67% women (n=67), 28% men (n=28) and 5% non-binary (n=5), aged 18–76 years (median=24, IQR=10), with most aged 20–29 years (54.0%, n=54). Overall, 38% (n=38) were student musicians and 62% (n=62) professionals, of whom 49% (n=49) were self-employed, 8% (n=8) were employed full-time and 5% (n=5) both. Almost a third (30%, n=30) held additional jobs; some reporting multiple roles (total n=34; table 2).

Summary of participants’ characteristics

Frequency and distribution of PRMSDs

Over a third (38%, n=38) of participants had experienced PRMSDs 0–2 times in the last 5 years, whereas 24% (n=24) reported 3–5 episodes, and 38% (n=38) reported >5 episodes. Participants reported discomfort in the shoulders (36%, n=36), cervical spine (33%, n=33), wrists, hands and fingers (25%, n=25) and the lumbar spine (20%, n=20), which are also prevalent sites of musculoskeletal discomfort in the general population. Excessive playing, poor posture, mental stress and lack of rest were most commonly perceived as contributing factors to participants’ symptoms.

PRMSD frequency did not differ significantly by age (p=0.691), employment type (p=0.789) or weekly practice hours (p=0.123). However, there was a weak association between weekly working hours and PRMSD frequency (β=–0.030, p=0.025), with musicians who worked longer hours reporting fewer PRMSDs. This should be interpreted with caution given the lack of overall model significance (p=0.071) and limited sample size.

Impact on musicians’ work and well-being

Most participants (67%, n=67) continued playing, whereas 17% (n=17) reduced their working hours, 6% (n=6) declined demanding job offers and 5% (n=5) transitioned to less demanding roles or stopped accepting offers altogether. Most participants (77%, n=77) reported stress and anxiety due to PRMSDs, and almost half (43%, n=43) reported that stress worsened their symptoms, though only 22% (n=22) reported a negative impact on social connections.

Management of PRMSDs

Most musicians (73%, n=73) sought professional help for PRMSDs. Yoga, Pilates and strengthening and stretching exercises were rated as beneficial by approximately a third of respondents (36%, n=36 and 37%, n=37 respectively), as was music-specific advice such as modifying playing position for comfort (33%, n=33). Heat/ice and steroid injections were rated as helpful by far fewer (12%, n=12 and 2%, n=2, respectively).

Perceived needs and digital health interests

The most frequently cited needs were pain management and recovery information (n=20) and instrument-related advice (n=17; online supplemental table 3). Almost all participants (98%, n=98) had never used DHIs, with only two reporting use of exercise videos in YouTube. Nonetheless, 75% (n=75) believed they could support self-management, favouring advice websites (44%, n=33) over smartphone applications (20%, n=15).

Phase II: focus groups

Demographic characteristics

Four online focus groups were conducted with 10 musicians (two groups of two and two groups of three), aged 20–68 years (mean=28.7). The participants comprised five female, four male and one non-binary musician, of whom four were self-employed, five were students and one was retired/self-employed.

Overview of themes

Six themes were developed (figure 1).

Theme 1: Barriers and challenges to self-management of PRMSDs

Participants described several barriers to self-managing PRMSDs, including early training practices, professional pressures and limited access to specialised care.

“I had a very bad piano teacher … She didn’t teach me how to relax when I played, just to keep practising, and told me to sit still. No one told me what was right, so even now, if I play a lot of very fast passages, my hand gets tired, and I have permanent back pain now” (P1, focus group 1, retired/self-employed musician).

“The money’s always a problem, so we need to play most days … and if you do that for like 10, 14 or 20 days … you don’t sleep very well and everything hurts” (P2, focus group 1, self-employed orchestral musician/music teacher).

“I went to the GP with back pain; they gave me medicines. That actually makes it worse because if you’re not feeling the pain, then you carry on playing” (P2, focus group 2, self-employed orchestral musician/music teacher).

Although organisations like BAPAM aim to connect musicians with specialist practitioners, cost, uncertainty about provider suitability and time constraints remained barriers:

“We have like a list of practitioners … but then … how do I pick that one person? … Cost is one thing … Time is another. … I’d rather just be able to do it myself” (P10, focus group 4, self-employed classical musician/music teacher).

Theme 2: needs and facilitators for self-management of PRMSDs

Participants identified facilitators and needs that could support self-management, emphasising personalisation and professional understanding:

“One of the things I would know to look for now … is someone who specialises in musicians. If that person actually plays an instrument themselves, that’s even better” (P1, focus group 1, retired/self-employed musician).

“I play mostly the clarinet and sax, and he (another participant) percussion, and I feel we probably have different places where we have pains. If there is very specific exercise … applicable to instrument groups, … it would be much more relatable and useful” (P7, focus group 3, student musician).

Posture was another recurrent issue, though views differed on what constitutes good alignment:

“Posture …just like learning how to sit straight and breathe while you play” (P2, focus group 2, self-employed orchestral musician/music teacher).

“You know there’s always the classic, sit up straight and then you end up sticking your stomach out and curving your spine in … which has been my whole issue with my back” (P9, focus group 4, student musician).

In contrast, all participants agreed on the value of body awareness approaches such as the Alexander technique:

“There are methods out there like the Alexander technique. … There’s the body awareness and noticing what feels wrong” (P9, focus group 4, student musician).

Theme 3: views on DHIs

Participants held mixed views towards DHIs. One distrusted online sources, expressing concern about misinformation:

I never went online. Maybe it’s because I’m old. … There’s so much rubbish. … My hands are so precious to me (P1, focus group 1, retired/self-employed musician).

Most participants (9/10) were open to DHIs as a first-line self-management tool before seeking professional help:

Probably digital health will be the first choice for small things… If you have an issue that’s getting severe, you want to be able to have access to a professional (P7, focus group 3, student musician).

Three preferred websites for their perceived credibility and depth of information, while six favoured mobile apps for their accessibility and potential for personalised support:

Having a mobile app would be much more personal (P4, focus group 2, student musician).

Theme 4: design features of DHIs

Participants emphasised that DHIs should be personalised and incorporate well-being features, health tracking and motivational elements.

Something that can track your mood … little affirmations and little stories (P4, focus group 2, student musician).

An app that I used to use… It actually makes you think on your own, like, ask two questions, … and it tracks it for you. Sometimes all we need is to just reflect on our own what we did (P5, focus group 2, self-employed musician).

If the app could handle things like giving reminders for breaks for me (P3, focus group 2, self-employed musician).

Progress tracker or mini-games… that is sort of fun. It gives you a drive to … keep going (P9, focus group 4, student musician).

Theme 5: facilitators to using DHIs

Peer support and accessible, well-organised content were viewed as key facilitators:

A support network is really important, you know like Reddit … just talking to your peers. … Sometimes we’re like too worried to bring it (PRMSDs) out because we don’t know how it’s gonna be perceived by other people and be like, ‘Oh, you're so weird (P10, focus group 4, self-employed classical musician/music teacher).

I think having something like a catalogue … for example, if I have a flare-up in my back, and it’s … really bad; have kind of like something for that stage (P9, focus group 4, student musician).

A video … but also having like a GIF… is just quick, easy, accessible (P3, focus group 2, self-employed musician).

Theme 6: barriers and solutions to using DHIs

Participants identified three main barriers: lack of trust, cost and absence of physical interaction.

My concern is it (DHIs) lacks that physical touch. … Sometimes, just a hand on your shoulder can make all the difference in how you feel (P9, focus group 4, student musician).

Anyone can make an app… I would want to know whether it is sourced by medical professionals (P5, focus group 2, self-employed musician).

Professional endorsement and reducing cost was seen as potential solutions:

Probably I would just use it if my teacher recommended it (P4, focus group 2, student musician).

How much it would cost would be the only thing, yeah, if it’s free, … I wouldn’t see how you couldn’t use it (P3, focus group 2, self-employed musician).

Discussion

This mixed-methods study explored the characteristics of musicians with current or previous PRMSDs, barriers and facilitators to PRMSD self-management, health needs and perspectives on DHIs. The survey findings informed the focus group discussions, which explored these topics and identified potential digital solutions to address the needs identified. Both phases highlighted the recurrent nature of PRMSDs and their impact on the professional activities and psychological well-being of participants. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. Age-related or non-specific changes may have been misattributed as playing-related symptoms; therefore, participants’ perception of their condition should be interpreted with caution. Additionally, grouping pain, aches and injuries within survey and focus group questions may have conflated symptoms with damage. Disentangling these constructs within a self-report design is methodologically challenging; future research should explore musicians’ interpretations of pain and injury separately.

No statistically significant differences in PRMSD frequency were found across employment types (p=0.789), weekly practice hours (p=0.123) or age groups (p=0.691). These findings may therefore be predisposed to recollection, sampling and/or self-selection bias. Only 100 participants completed the survey; therefore, the study was likely underpowered.37 The sample was also disproportionately young, with 58% (n=58) of participants aged 18–29 and only 18% (n=18) aged 40 and over; hence findings may not be representative of the wider musician population.

Some cross-sectional studies suggest that advancing age might be related to increased PRMSD,27 38 whereas others suggest that both the youngest (22–29 years) and oldest (50–61 years) musicians report higher injury rates,39 with both age groups maintaining intensive practice schedules (28.7 and 32 h per week, respectively).39 This contrasts with the current study’s findings, where no significant relationship between PRMSDs and musical practice was identified, and increased working hours corresponded with lower frequency of PRMSDs (p=0.025, β=−0.030). Although this contrasts with broader occupational health research,40 it aligns with musician-specific studies proposing that PRMSD risk plateaus beyond a certain threshold.27 41 This may reflect improved technique or physical conditioning among experienced musicians. However, these interpretations are speculative and warrant further investigation.

Several barriers to PRMSD self-management were identified, categorised using the Capability, Opportunity, Motivation -Behaviour model as capability barriers (limited health education and knowledge, including unhelpful beliefs about pain, fear that treating pain may lead to further damage and limited awareness of healthy technique during early music training), opportunity barriers (financial constraints, time pressures and limited access to specialist care) and motivation barriers (fear of discussing pain and its potential impact on career). Inadequate early education is consistently discussed within the literature as a factor associated with musicians’ health behaviours.42 43 The current study further suggests that challenging unhelpful beliefs about pain may be an important target for early self-management.44,47 Instrument-specific guidance and posture management were highlighted in both phases as health needs. Symptoms may vary by instrument, supporting tailored approaches, though current evidence is limited by small sample sizes, lack of follow-up data and lack of objective outcome measures.48 49 Further, the current consensus is that maintaining a straight posture does not prevent musculoskeletal pain and may lead to hypervigilance, anxiety and a sense of failure.50 51 The Alexander technique may offer benefits for posture and movement, but evidence remains limited and inconclusive.52,55

A key finding was participants’ openness to DHIs in both phases; however, the predominantly young cohort, small sample and online format of the focus group study may have skewed views towards more favourable attitudes to DHIs. There was broad agreement between participants that DHIs should facilitate multiple components of supported self-management, including multimedia education, which is more engaging than written materials.56,58 Peer support was valued, reflecting evidence that shared experiences among musicians normalise pain discussions and enhance self-efficacy,59 although online peer support platforms should be moderated to prevent misinformation.60 Motivational messaging and mood-tracking features may address psychological burden,61 62 which is important given that 77% of survey participants reported stress or anxiety and 43% reported that stress worsened their symptoms. Future studies should explore mental health more comprehensively in musicians.

Alongside these facilitators, focus group participants preferred endorsements from music teachers over healthcare professionals when considering DHI adoption. This preference may stem from negative experiences with healthcare professionals or from the younger demographic in the focus group study, who tend to favour independent digital options.63 Future DHIs should work with music teachers and professional organisations to ensure relevance to musicians’ professional contexts and facilitate adoption. Participants also preferred free or one-time purchase options over subscription models, consistent with evidence that financial burden deters DHI adoption.64 While some participants noted that DHIs lack the physical interaction of face-to-face care, they can nonetheless promote active self-management, empowerment and self-efficacy in line with evidence-based musculoskeletal disease guidelines.65

Implications for practice

This study offers practical guidance for the design of DHIs that align with musicians’ professional contexts and health needs. Both websites and applications could be offered, with an initial self-assessment enabling tailored support. Automated reminders and health tracking links could provide personalised feedback on practice load. Motivational features, such as gamification and progress tracking, could support engagement. Moderated online forums could normalise pain discussions and reduce the stigma associated with seeking help. Multimedia resources, including videos and GIFs, organised by condition or symptom stage, could enhance accessibility.

Conclusion

Musicians may experience musculoskeletal symptoms that can impact their careers, yet remain an underserved population with limited access to tailored support. This study addressed this gap by providing user-centred recommendations for DHI development specific to their needs and professional context.
