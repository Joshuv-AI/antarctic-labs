Locally finite varieties of nonassociative algebras
Yuri Bahturin, Alexander Olshanskii

Abstract
We study locally finite varieties (=primitive classes) of linear algebras over finite fields. We do not assume that our algebras are associative or Lie. We are interested in the basic properties of finite algebras in these varieties such as: nilpotence, solvability, simplicity, freeness, projectivity, and injectivity. We are also interested in the numerical estimates of the ratio of the number of algebras with various classical properties to the total number of all algebras of a fixed dimension n n . Among these properties are having no proper nontrivial subalgebras or no nontrivial automorphisms, etc.

1.1 Topic of the article.

Given a field
\mathbf{F}

algebra
A

\mathbf{F}

\mathbf{F}

\mathbf{F}

universal

\underbrace{A\times\cdots\times A}_{n}\to A
of various
arities

In this paper, we study finite algebras, that is, finite-dimensional algebras over finite fields. The general interest in the identities of finite universal algebras, in particular, finite rings, groups, semigroups, etc. dates back to the papers devoted to the Finite Basis Problem (FBP), asking: Do all identities of a finite (universal) algebra
A
follow from a finite set of them? The positive answers were obtained initially in the classes of finite groups, associative and Lie rings in the papers Oates - Powel
[
32
]

[

]
, Lvov
[

]
,
[

[

[

On the other hand, even earlier, in 1955, Lyndon

[

A natural way to get into this stream is to consider finite universal algebras, in particular, semigroups, latices, and other non-linear algebraic structures. The most general approach to the identities of finite universal algebras can be found in the book

1.2 Absolutely free algebras.

For any field
\mathbf{F}
and a nonempty set
X
, one defines an (absolutely) free algebra
\mathcal{F}(X)
as follows. We first define the
nonassociative monomials
in
X
by induction on their
degree
. The monomials of degree 1 are the elements of
X
. We write
\deg x=1
if
x\in X
. Once the monomials of all degrees less than
n>1
have been defined, the monomials of degree
n
are all possible expressions of the form
(u)(v)
where
\deg u=k\geq 1
and
\deg v=n-k\geq 1
. The set of all nonassociative monomials in
X
is denoted by
\Gamma(X)
. One has
\Gamma(X)=\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}\Gamma_{n}
where
\Gamma_{n}
is the set of monomials of degree
n
. If we drop parentheses on a nonassociative monomial
v
we obtain an associative word, called the
support
of
v
. Conversely, one can set brackets on an associative word
w
to obtain all possible nonassociative monomials with support
w
.

The set
\Gamma(X)
is endowed with an operation
{\scriptstyle\circ}
, as follows. If
u\in\Gamma_{k}
and
v\in\Gamma_{\ell}
then
u{\scriptstyle\circ}v=(u)(v)\in\Gamma_{k+\ell}
. With respect to this operation,
\Gamma(X)
is generated by
X
. In practice, while using this operation, one omits the symbol
\scriptstyle\circ
and all unnecessary parentheses. So, one writes
(x_{1}x_{2})x_{2}
instead of

((x_{1}){\scriptstyle\circ}(x_{2})){\scriptstyle\circ}(x_{2})
.

Finally, one defines the free algebra
\mathcal{F}(X)
as the linear span of
\Gamma(X)
with coefficients in
\mathbf{F}
. The elements of
\mathcal{F}(X)
are often called the
nonassociative polynomials
. The product in
\mathcal{F}(X)
is the extension by linearity of the product in
\Gamma(X)
, and so
\mathcal{F}(X)
is a linear algebra called the
free non-associative algebra with the set of free generators
X
. The cardinality of
X
is called the rank of
\mathcal{F}(X)
, (In this paper nonassociative polynomials have no terms of zero degree!)

If
L
and
M
are subspaces of an algebra, then
LM
denotes the linear span of all products

lm
, where
l\in L
and
m\in L
. The
power

A^{k}
of any algebra
A
is defined by induction as
A^{1}=A
and
A^{k}=\sum_{s+t=k}A^{s}A^{t}
. We list the well-known properties:

Lemma 1
.

Given an algebra
A
over
\mathbf{F}
, for any
i>1
, we have

A^{i}\subset A^{i-1}
,
A^{i-1}A\subset A^{i}
and
A^{i-1}A\subset A^{i}
.
Thus,
A^{i-1}
is an ideal of
A
.

Proof.

Clearly,
A^{2}\subset A=A^{1}
. If
i>2
and
j<i
then by induction, we have
A^{j}\subset A^{j-1}
. Hence, for
k,\ell<i
,
k+\ell\geq 3
, we obtain
A^{k}A^{\ell}\subset A^{k-1}A^{\ell}
or
A^{k}A^{\ell}\subset A^{k}A^{l-1}
. Recalling the definition, we have
A^{i}\subset A^{i-1}
. By the definion, and this inclusion, we have

AA^{i-1}=A^{1}A^{i-1}\subset A^{i}\subset A^{i-1}
. Similarly,
A^{i-1}A\subset A^{i}
,
and so
A^{i-1}
is an ideal of
A
.
∎

If
\mathcal{F}_{n}
is the linear span of
\Gamma_{n}
then
\mathcal{F}_{k}\mathcal{F}_{\ell}\subset\mathcal{F}_{k+\ell}
and so we have the canonical
{\mathbb{N}}
-grading of the free algebra, where
{\mathbb{N}}
is the additive semigroup of natural numbers, as follows

\mathcal{F}(X)=\bigoplus_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{F}_{n}

(1)

The elements of
\mathcal{F}_{n}
are called
homogeneous
of degree
n
. The powers
\mathcal{F}^{n}=\bigoplus_{k=n}^{\infty}\mathcal{F}_{k}
form a
descending filtration
in
\mathcal{F}(X)
:

\mathcal{F}(X)=\mathcal{F}^{1}\supset\mathcal{F}^{2}\supset\cdots\supset\mathcal{F}^{n}\supset\cdots\mbox{ such that }\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{F}^{n}=\{0\}.

(2)

Given a nonassociative monomial
u(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
and the elements
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}
of any algebra
P
over any field
\mathbf{F}
, one can use the induction on
\deg u
and the operation of
P
to define the
value

u(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\in P
. The value of a monomial
x_{i}
is just
a_{i}
, while
(uv)(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})=u(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})v(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})
. Now the value of a nonassociative polynomial
w(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
which is a linear combination of several monomials with coefficients in
\mathbf{F}
, is just the linear combination of the values of these monomials with the same coefficients.

If
\varphi:X\to P
is any mapping then the evaluation of nonassociative polynomials which replaces each
x\in X
by
\varphi(x)\in P
is a unique homomorphism of algebras
\bar{\varphi}:\mathcal{F}(X)\to P
, extending
\varphi
. This property is called the
universal property
of
\mathcal{F}(X)
. An immediate consequence of this property is the following. Given any algebra
P
, there exists
X
such that
P
is a homomorphic image of
\mathcal{F}(X)
under a homomorphism
\varepsilon
mapping
X
onto a generating set of
P
. Taking
K=\Ker\varepsilon
, we get
P\cong\mathcal{F}(X)/K
.

1.3 Varieties and free algebras.

If
w(x_{1},\dots,w_{n})
is a nonassociative polynomial and
w(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})=0
, for all elements
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}
of an algebra
P
, we say that
w(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=0
is an
identical relation
or shortly an
identity
in
P
.

Now let us consider the free algebra of countable rank
\mathcal{F}_{\infty}=\mathcal{F}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},\ldots)
over
\mathbf{F}
. Given a nonempty subset
V\subset\mathcal{F}_{\infty}
, the class
\mathfrak{V}
of all algebras (over fixed field
\mathbf{F}
) satisfying all identities
v(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=0
, where
v(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in V
, is called the
variety of algebras defined by
V
. It follows from this definition that a variety is closed under subalgebras, factor-algebras and Cartesian products of algebras.

The
trivial
variety consists of zero algebra only; it is defined by the identical relation
x=0
.

Given a variety
\mathfrak{V}
and algebra
P
, one considers the ideal
\mathfrak{V}(P)
(more often denoted by a matching notation
V(P)
) of all values
v(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})
, where
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\in P
and
v(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=0
is an identity in
\mathfrak{V}
. By analogy with the group-theoretic notation, one calls
V(P)

the verbal ideal corresponding to the variety
\mathfrak{V}
.
V(P)
is the least ideal of
P
such that
P/V(P)\in\mathfrak{V}
. So
V(P)=\{0\}
iff
P\in\mathfrak{V}
. The ideal
V(P)
is closed under all endomorphisms of
P
. Such ideals are called
fully invariant
.

If
\mathbf{F}
is a fixed field, then there is one-one Galois-type correspondence between the varieties over
\mathbf{F}
and the fully invariant ideals of
\mathcal{F}_{\infty}
:

Proposition 1
.

Given a variety
\mathfrak{V}
, the corresponding verbal ideal
V(\mathcal{F}_{\infty})
is fully invariant. Conversely, every fully invariant ideal is a verbal ideal for an appropriate variety
\mathfrak{V}
.
\hfill\Box

If
U\subset\mathcal{F}_{\infty}
and
P
is an algebra, one denotes by
U(P)
the ideal of
P
generated by all
u(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})
, where
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\in P
and
u(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in U
.
This is the
verbal ideal of
P
generated by the set of nonassociative polynomials
U
. If
\mathfrak{V}(\mathcal{F}_{\infty})=U(\mathcal{F}_{\infty})
, then we say that
\mathfrak{V}
is defined by the identities
\{u=0\,|\,u\in U\}
. We also say that the set of identities
\{u=0\,|\,u\in U\}
is a
basis of identities
of
\mathfrak{V}
.

Given a nonempty set
X
, the algebra
F(\mathfrak{V},X)=\mathcal{F}(X)/V(\mathcal{F}(X))
is called a
\mathfrak{V}
-free algebra with the free generating set
X
or a
free algebra in
\mathfrak{V}
with the free generating set
X
. If
\mathfrak{V}
is nonzero,
X
is injectively embedded in
F(\mathfrak{V},X)
. Each of these algebras possesses the
universal property within the variety
\mathfrak{V}
, namely, any map
\varphi:X\to P\in\mathfrak{V}
extends to a unique homomorphism
\overline{\varphi}:F(\mathfrak{V},X)\to P
. It follows that every algebra
P\in\mathfrak{V}
is isomorphic to a factor-algebra of an appropriate
F=F(\mathfrak{V},X)
. If we do not specify parameters
\mathfrak{V}
or
X
, then we simply say that
F
is a
relatively free algebra
.

For a
\mathfrak{V}
-free algebra
F(\mathfrak{V},X)
, we also have a Galois-type correspondence between the subvarieties
of
\mathfrak{V}
and the verbal ideals of
F(\mathfrak{V},X)
. The join
\mathfrak{V}_{1}\cup\mathfrak{V}_{2}
in the lattice of subvarieties of
\mathfrak{V}
is the smallest variety containing both subvarieties
\mathfrak{V}_{1}
and
\mathfrak{V}_{2}
. The corresponding
verbal ideal of
F(\mathfrak{V},X)
is the intersection
V_{1}(F(\mathfrak{V},X))\cap V_{2}(F(\mathfrak{V},X))
, and the intersection

\mathfrak{V}_{1}\cap\mathfrak{V}_{2}
corresponds the sum
V_{1}(F(\mathfrak{V},X))+V_{2}(F(\mathfrak{V},X))
.

Examples of algebras, which are free in certain varieties, include free associative algebras, free Lie algebras, free commutative algebras, etc.

A useful property of relatively free algebras, following from the universal property, is given below.

Lemma 2
.

Let
0\neq v(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in\mathcal{F}_{\infty}
be a nonassociative polynomial and
F=F(\mathfrak{V},Y)
a free algebra in a variety
\mathfrak{V}
of algebras, with a free generating set
Y=\{y_{1},y_{2},\ldots\}
. Suppose
v(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})=0
is a
relation
among
y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}
that holds in
F
. Then
v(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=0
is an
identical relation
in
\mathfrak{V}
.

\hfill\Box

Next we mention the following.

Any map of a set
X
to a set
Y
extends to a unique homomorphism
F(\mathfrak{V},X)\to F(\mathfrak{V},Y)
. In particular, the embedding
X\subset Y
extends to the embedding
F(\mathfrak{V},X)\subset F(\mathfrak{V},Y)
and
F(\mathfrak{V},X)
is a retract of
F(\mathfrak{V},Y)
under the retraction extending
x\mapsto x
for
x\in X
and
y\mapsto 0
for
y\in Y\setminus X
.

The cardinality of
X
is called the
rank
of
F(\mathfrak{V},X)
. Two relatively free algebras in a nontrivial variety
\mathfrak{V}
are isomorphic if and only if they have the same rank.
So, with
\mathfrak{V}
fixed, we can simply write
F_{n}=F_{n}(\mathfrak{V})
for the relatively free algebra of rank
n
in the variety
\mathfrak{V}
. In this case, we can choose
X=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}
.

Given a set of algebras
\mathcal{S}
over a field
\mathbf{F}
, the
variety generated by
\mathcal{S}
, denoted by
\mathrm{var\,}\mathcal{S}
, is the set of all algebras over
\mathbf{F}
, satisfying all identities satisfied by all algebras from
\mathcal{S}
. If
\mathcal{S}=\{P\}
, where
P
is an algebra, then we simply write
\mathrm{var\,}\mathcal{S}=\mathrm{var\,}P
. Equivalently,
\mathrm{var\,}\mathcal{S}
is the intersection of all varieties
\mathfrak{V}
such that
\mathcal{S}\subset\mathfrak{V}
.

Our aim is to provide new evidence of a close connection between the structure of a finite
algebra
A
, the identities of
A
, and the properties of algebras in the variety
\mathrm{var\,}A
.
A variety generated by a finite algebra is called
finitely generated
. Studying finitely
generated varieties is the main topic of this paper. It is quite apparent that if
A
is
nilpotent or solvable, or simple then these properties must have a strong influence on the
structure of other algebras in
\mathrm{var\,}A
. In addition to finitely generated varieties, we will look at the varieties where all finite algebras are injective, or projective, or, more generally, being subalgebras of free algebras.

1.4 Two one-dimensional algebras.

Let
A
be a one-dimensional algebra over a field
\bf F
. If
A
has a non-zero multiplication,
then there is a basis vector
e\in A
such that
e^{2}=e
, and then the mapping
e\mapsto 1
extends
to an isomorphism of
A
onto the field
F
, which is an algebra over itself.

As a result, up to isomorphism, for every field
\mathbf{F}
, we have just two one-dimensional
\mathbf{F}
-algebras. In this subsection, we look at the identities excluding a one-dimensional algebra from a variety.

For the first statement, we consider the identities of the form
x=f(x)
, where the nonassociative polynomial
f(x)
depends on one variable
x
and contains only monomias of degree at least
2
. We will call such identities
Fermat’s identities
in honor of Fermat’s Little Theorem.

Proposition 2
.

A variety
\mathfrak{V}
of algebras over a field
\mathbf{F}
satisfies some Fermat’s identity
if and only if, it does not contain a one-dimensional algebra with zero multiplication.

Proof.

No Fermat’s identity holds in one-dimensional algebra
A
with zero multiplication,
since the right-hand side of the identity takes only zero values in
A
. Hence
A
does not
belong to a variety satisfying a Fermat’s identity.

Assume now that no Fermat’s identity holds in a variety
\mathfrak{V}
. Then there
are no identities
f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=0
where the left-hand side involves a monomial
x_{i}
of degree
1
. Indeed, otherwise, one can replace other variables in
f
with zero. By Lemma
2
, this mapping extends to the endomorphism on the free
\mathfrak{V}
-algebra and gives a Fermat’s identity in
\mathfrak{V}
. It remains to mention that every polynomial without monomials of degree 1 vanishes in the
one-dimensional algebra
A
with zero multiplication, whence
A\in\mathfrak{V}
.
∎

The degree
d_{i}
of a monomial
v
with respect to a variable
x_{i}
can easily be defined by induction as the number of occurrences of
x_{i}
in the support of
v
. If
|\mathbf{F}|=q
, we call a polynomial
f

quasihomogeneous
if for every
x_{i}
, all the monomial summands of
f
have the same degree with respect to
x_{i}
, taken modulo
q-1
.

Proposition 3
.

A variety
\mathfrak{V}
of algebras over a finite field
\mathbf{F}
does not contain the one-dimensional algebra
\mathbf{F}
with nonzero multiplication if and only if,
\mathfrak{V}
satisfies some quasihomogeneous identity in one variable with nonzero sum of coefficients of its monomial summands.

Proof.

Note that an identity with nonzero coefficient sum of the monomials is not satisfied in algebra
\mathbf{F}
. Indeed, by substituting the identity element in place of all variables, we obtain a nonzero element of the field.

Let conversely, a variety
\mathfrak{V}
have no quasihomogeneous identities in one variable with nonzero sum of coefficients, that is, all quasihomogeneous identities in one variable
x
holding in
\mathfrak{V}
have zero sum of coefficients. Note that
x^{m}=x^{n}
is the identity of
\bf F
if
m\equiv n\mod(q-1)
. Hence the quasihomogeneous polynomials with zero sum of coefficients are in the kernel of any homomorphism of the one-generated
\mathfrak{V}
-free algebra
F_{1}
onto
\mathbf{F}
since under any substitution of any element of
\mathbf{F}
in place of
x
all monomials will take equal values. It follows that these identities hold in
\mathbf{F}
.

But then also all identities in one variable are in the kernel of any such homomorphism, since
[
4
, Section 8.4]
the left side of any identity is the sum of quasihomogeneous polynomials, which also identically equal zero in
\mathfrak{V}
. Hence the mapping of the variable
x
into a nonzero element of
\mathbf{F}
has a well-defined extension to a homomorphism, that is, the algebra
\mathbf{F}
is isomorphic to the factor-algebra of the algebra
F_{1}
. Now it follows that
\mathbf{F}\in\mathfrak{V}
.

∎

1.5 Subalgebras of direct powers of simple algebras.

A variety
\mathfrak{V}
is called
locally finite
if every finitely generated algebra
in
\mathfrak{V}
is finite (equivalently, the
\mathfrak{V}
-free algebras of finite ranks are finite).
Recall that an algebra
A
with nonzero multiplication is called
simple
if it has no proper nozero ideals. For instance, a composition section of any algebra is a simple algebra. If
\mathfrak{V}
has a finite simple algebra
A
, then it contains the direct powers
\underbrace{A\times\cdots\times A}_{n}
of
A
for all
n=1,2,\ldots
. These play an important role in the description of
all
algebras in
\mathfrak{V}
. For further references, this subsection presents an auxiliary information on the structure of such direct products.

As usual, the factors of a direct
product
P
can be identified with ideals of
P
. Recall that a non-zero algebra
A
is called

simple
if
A
has no proper non-zero ideals. Also, we canonically identify algebras
A_{1},\ldots,A_{m}
with the subalgebras, denoted by the same letters in their direct product so that
A_{1}\times\cdots\times A_{m}=A_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus A_{m}
.

Lemma 3
.

Let an algebra
A
be the direct product of simple algebras:

A=A_{1}\times\dots\times A_{t}
,
where
A_{1},\ldots,A_{s}
have non-zero multiplication and
A_{s+1},\dots,A_{t}
are one-dimensional with
zero multiplication. Then

\mathrm{(i)}

every ideal
I
of
A
is the direct sum of some algebras from the first cluster and a subspace of the sum of the subalgebras
A_{s+1},\dots,A_{t}
.;

\mathrm{(ii)}

every factor-algebra of
A
is isomorphic to a product
A_{i_{1}}\times\dots\times A_{i_{r}}
,
where
1\leq i_{1}<\ldots<i_{r}\leq t
.

Proof.

If the projection of
I
to
A_{1}
is non-zero, then using multiplication by
the elements from
A_{1}
, one can obtain the containment
A_{1}\subset I
since
A_{1}
is simple
and has nonzero multiplication. Similar observation applied to
A_{2},\dots,A_{s}

proves the statement (i). The second statement follows from the first one and from
the homomorphism theorem.

∎

A subalgebra
B
of a direct product
P=A_{1}\times\dots\times A_{t}=A_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus A_{m}
is called a
subdirect product
of the algebras
A_{1},\dots,A_{t}
if
B
projects
onto
each
of the factors
A_{i}
.

Lemma 4
.

A subdirect product
B
of simple algebras
A_{1},\dots,A_{t}
is isomorphic to the direct product
A_{i_{1}}\times\dots\times A_{i_{r}}
, where
1\leq i_{1}<\ldots<i_{r}\leq t
.

Proof.

Note that in the above notation, the intersection
B\cap A_{t}
is an ideal in
both
B
and
A_{t}
since
B
projects onto
A_{t}
. So it is either zero or the entire
A_{t}
.
In the first case,
B
is isomorphic to the projection of it to
A_{1}\times\dots\times A_{t-1}
.
In the second case,
B=B^{\prime}\times A_{t}
, where
B^{\prime}
is a subdirect product of the algebras

A_{1},\dots,A_{t-1}
. So in both cases one can use the obvious induction on
k
to
complete the proof.

∎

If
A
is an algebra,
B
is the direct power of
A
, that is, the set of all functions
f:\{1,\ldots,n\}\to A
, then the
diagonal

\Delta
of
B
is the subalgebra of all constant functions
f
, that is, those for which there is
a\in A
such that
f(i)=a
, for all
i=1,\ldots,n
. Given any
n
-tuple of automorphisms
\tau=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n})
of
A
, we consider the subalgebra
\Delta^{\tau}
, consisting of the functions
f^{\tau}
,
f\in\Delta
, given by
f^{\tau}(i)=\alpha_{i}(f(i))
; clearly,
\Delta^{\tau}\cong\Delta
. If there is no confusion, the name of diagonal is used also when speaking about
\Delta^{\tau}
in place of
\Delta
.

Lemma 5
.

Let
S
be a finite simple algebra,
S^{2}\neq\{0\}
,
P
and
Q
are direct powers of
S
; We write
P=S(1)\oplus\cdots\oplus S(\ell)
and
Q=S_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus S_{k}

2 Nilpotent varieties

2.1 Equivalent definitions.

When one transfers the concept of nilpotency from classical algebras to
arbitrary non-associative algebras, there are different ways of
generalisations. In this subsection we want to explain the correlation
of these definitions of nilpotency.

Let us call the descending series of subspaces

A=A_{1}\supset A_{2}\supset\ldots

(3)

a
central filtration
if
A_{i}A_{j}\subset A_{i+j}
for any indices

. The terms
A_{i}
in (

A_{j}A=A_{j}A_{1}\subset A_{j+1}\subset A_{j}
and
AA_{j}\subset A_{j}
.
The lower central filtration is the power series of
A
. By Lemma
1
, it is
a central filtration.

Lemma 6
.

For any central series (
3
) and any
i
, we have
A^{i}\subset A_{i}
.

Proof.

Since
A^{1}=A_{1}
,
A^{2}=A_{1}^{2}\subset A_{2}
, using induction by
i\geq 2
,
we have
A^{k}\subset A_{k}
,
A^{\ell}\subset A_{\ell}
for
k+\ell=i

A^{i}=\sum_{k+\ell=i}A^{k}A^{\ell}\subset\sum_{k+\ell=i}A_{k}A_{\ell}\subset A_{i}.

∎

For any algebra
A
, having central filtration (
3
) of length

(т.е.

) we can define an upper central filtration
\{Z_{j}\,|\,j\geq 1\}
by induction, starting with

. Suppose the subspaces

have been defined for

. Then, by definition,

consits of the elements

(4)

for any bracketing of the left hand side, if for some

we have
b_{j}\in Z_{i_{j}}
and
\sum_{j=1}^{t}i_{j}\geq c-i+1
.

Lemma 7
.

Z_{i}\subset Z_{i-1}
. The upper central filtration is a central filtration, hence consists of the ideals of
A
. For any central filtration (
3
) and any

, we have
A_{i}\subset Z_{i}
.

Proof.

The first claim follows simply because
c-i+1<c-(i-1)+1
.

For the second one, let us assume that
a\in Z_{i},b\in Z_{j}
and consider any product
b_{1}b_{2}\cdots b_{s}(ab)b_{s+1}\ldots b_{t}
where
b_{k}\in Z_{i_{k}}
and

\sum_{k=1}^{t}i_{k}\geq c-i-j+1.

Since

, it follows that

j+\sum_{jk1}^{t}i_{k}\geq c-i+1.

By definition of
Z_{i}
, it follows from

that
b_{1}b_{2}\cdots b_{s}(ab)b_{s+1}\ldots b_{t}=0
.
So by definition of

, it follows that

. As a result,
Z_{i}Z_{j}\subset Z_{i+j}
.

Now if

, then induction and the definition of the central filtration (

) imply that all the products (
4
) are the elements of
A_{c+1}
provided that

∎

We conclude that if

has a finite central filtration reaching
\{0\}
, it follows from Lemmas

that the lengths of the lower and upper central filtrations in

.

By the inductive definition of the power series,

is a linear span of all products of

elements from

with any bracketing. Therefore all algebras satisfying

form the variety

given by all identities

with all possible bracketing on the left hand side. In particular,
\mathfrak{N}_{1}
consists of all algebras with zero product.

We come across another definition by considering an ascending annihilator series of an algebra

\{0\}=A_{0}\subset A_{1}\subset\ldots

(5)

where, for any

the subspace
A_{i}/A_{i-1}
belongs to the annihilator of

. Using induction, we easily prove that each

is an ideal of

. In particular, this is true for the upper annihilator series
\{I_{i}\}
where
I_{1}=\{a\in A\,|\,ab=ba=0\,\;\;\forall b\in A\}
is the annihilator

of

and

is the full preimage in

of the annihilator
I_{j}/I_{j-1}
of

, with trivial basis

, we always have

, for any

. Indeed, if already if

annihilates

.

To define the lower annihilator series of an arbitrary algebra

and we say that the depth of the monomial

,

.

To compare this series withan arbitrary annihilator series, we enumerate the terms of an arbitrary descending annihilator series in the reverse order:

A=A^{\prime}_{0}\supset A^{\prime}_{1}\supset A^{\prime}_{2}\supset\ldots

where for each

, the ideal

Lemma 8
.

For any

A(i)\subset A(i-1)
, the descending series

is an annihilator series. In particular, all its terms are ideals. For any annihilator series (

Proof.

By induction, it follows from the definition of the depth, that any monomial of depth

is the value of some other monomial of depth

and

are the linear spans of some monomials of depth

is the linear span of the values of monomials

in

where one of

, say

∎

As a result, we have obtained that if an annihilator series (

, that is,

It follows from Lemma

that algebras of depth
\leq d

for all multilinear monomials

where the depth of

and, say, the depth of

in the monomial

Proposition 4
.

\mathrm{(i)}

If an algebra

has nilpotent class

then it has nilpotent depth
d\leq c
.

\mathrm{(ii)}

If an algebra

has nilpotent depth

.

Proof.

(i) Every monomial of depth

has degree at least

, an so it takes only zero

. So the first statement
follows from the above description on the identities defining the varieties

{\cal N}_{c}
and
{\cal N}(d)
.

(ii) Arguing by induction, we may assume that all products of degree at least

are in

. But then in any product

of length

one of

has degree at least

hence by induction belongs to

. Then

, as needed. So, the nilpotent class of

is at most

.
∎

Now we are able to formulate, for references, the following

Proposition 5
.

Given an algebra
A
over a field
\bf F
, the following
properties are equivalent.

\mathrm{(i)}

A
has a central series
A=A_{1}\geq A_{2}\geq\dots\geq A_{c+1}=\{0\}
for some
c\geq 1
;

\mathrm{(ii)}

A^{c+1}=\{0\}
for some

;

\mathrm{(iii)}

belongs to the variety
{\cal N}_{c}
for some
c
;

\mathrm{(iv)}

has an annihilator series
\{0\}=A_{0}\subset\dots\subset A_{d}=A
for some

;

\mathrm{(v)}

belongs to the variety

for some

.

This Proposition allows one to say that an algebra is
nilpotent
, by definition, if it has one of the properties (i) - (v) . A variety
\mathfrak{V}
of algebras is called

if every algebra

is nilpotent. The nipotency class (depth) of
the algebras in a nilpotent variety is bounded from above, because if one gets
an infinite series

of algebras in
\mathfrak{V}
with unbounded classes (depths), then the
Cartesian products of them is not nilpotent, being in the variety
\mathfrak{V}
, a contradiction.
Thus the maximal

(resp.,

) such that
\mathfrak{V}
contains an algebra of nilpotent class

(of nilpotent depth

) is called the

(

)
of the variety
\mathfrak{V}
.

Remark 1
.

If we add the associative law to the identities defining the varieties

{\mathfrak{N}}_{c}
and
{\mathfrak{N}}(c)
, respectively, then we obtain equivalent systems of identities,
that is, they define the same variety. Hence, the class and the depth of an associative
nilpotent algebra are equal. The same is true for nilpotent Lie algebras.

In the following lemma, we give some simple properties which are used throughout our treatment of algebras.

Lemma 9
.

The following are true in any algebra
A
over a field
\mathbf{F}
.

\mathrm{(i)}

If
A
is nilpotent then the annihilator
I(A)
has nontrivial intersection with any nontrivial ideal
J
of
A
.

\mathrm{(ii)}

If an ideal
J
belongs to the annihilator
I(A)
of

and

is nilpotent then also

is nilpotent

Proof.

(i) If

has an element

which does not belongs to

, then for some

either

or

. In the first case,
if

, then we are done. Otherwise we can find an element

such that
either

or

. Since by Proposition

.

(ii) Since the factor-algebra

has a finite annihilator series

,
the algebra

. Therefore

(iv).

∎

2.2 Subideals.

A subalgebra
B
of an algebra
A
is called a
subideal
if there is a series of subalgebras
B=B_{0}\subset B_{1}\subset\cdots\subset B_{s}=A
such that each
B_{i-1}
is an ideal in
B_{i}
, for all
i=1,\ldots,s
. The least length
s
of all such series (called the
subideal series
) is called the subideal depth of
B
. Subideals of depth 1 are obviously the usual ideals.

One of characterizations of nilpotent varieties is the following.

Theorem 1
.

A variety of algebras
\mathfrak{V}
over a field is nilpotent if and only if
every subalgebra
B
of any algebra
A\in\mathfrak{V}
is a subideal of
A
.

Proof.

Let a variety
\mathfrak{V}
be nilpotent,
A\in\mathfrak{V}
, and
B
a subalgebra of
A
.
If an algebra
A
is nilpotent of depth
d
, and
\{0\}\subset I_{1}\subset\dots\subset I_{d}=A
is an
annihilator series in
A
, then the following is a subideal series:

B\subset B+I_{1}\subset B+I_{2}\subset\cdots\subset B+I_{d}=A.

For the converse claim, consider a
\mathfrak{V}
-free algebra
F=F_{\mathfrak{V}}(y,x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},\ldots)
and choose a subalgebra
G=F_{\mathfrak{V}}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},\ldots)
. Suppose, there is a subideal series

G=G_{0}\subset G_{1}\subset\ldots\subset G_{k}=F,

Now let
w
be any monomial of depth
d
, in which every generator occurs at most once. If we manage to show that
w
vanishes in
F
for
d=k
, then by Proposition
5
(v), the nilpotence of
F
and
\mathfrak{V}
will be established.

We proceed by induction on the depth
d
to prove that one of variables
x_{i}
in a monomial
w
of depth
d
depending on
x_{1},x_{2},\dots
can be replaced
with
y
so that the value obtained belongs to

G_{k-d}
. Since
G_{k-1}
is an
ideal in
F
if
d=1
and
w=x_{1}x_{2}
it follows that
yx_{2}\in G_{k-1}
. Then let

w=uv
and, say,
u
has depth
d-1
, and so one can replace one variable in
u

with
y
and obtain an element of
G_{k-d+1}
. Since
v\in G
and
G_{k-d}
is an ideal of
G_{k-d+1}
, after this replacement the value of
w=uv
will belong to
G_{k-d}
. Thus, after a single replacement

, any polynomial
w
of depth
k
in which every generator occurs at most once, becomes an element

of
G
.

On the one hand, for any substitution

, the respective homomorphism annihilates

, but on the other hand, if a homomorphism is identical on

then

should be mapped into itself since

. Hence,
v=0
in
F
. Since
w
is an automorphic image of

, by Lemma
2
we have
w=0
in
\mathfrak{V}
, which is what we wanted.
∎

Remark 2
.

A subalgebra of an algebra

of nilpotent

is a subideal of

of depth at most

as it is explained in the first

The part ’if’ follows from Remark

Let

2.3 Other criteria of nilpotency.

We start with the annihilators in the algebras of nilpotent varieties.

Theorem 3
.

A variety of algebras
\mathfrak{V}
over a field is nilpotent
if and only if the annihilator of every nonzero algebra

is non-zero.

Proof.

The part ‘only if’ follows from Proposition
5
(iv).

To prove part ‘if’, we assume that a variety
\mathfrak{V}
is not nilpotent.
Then we will use the fact
that any nilpotent variety of algebras has finite basis of identities

]

\mathfrak{V}
cannot
be a proper union of an infinite increasing chain of verbal ideals in the

\mathfrak{V}
-free algebra

of infinite rank. Using Zorn’s lemma and the Galois type correspondence between the subvarieties of
\mathfrak{V}
and the verbal ideals of

conclude that there is a non-nilpotent subvariety

in

whose proper

Now let

is fully invariant, hence an ideal of identities of a subvariety

is proper in

is free in

, hence nilpotent. But then by Lemma

also

is nilpotent, a contradiction. Thus

, and so

is the required

F=H+K
. Since
N\subset C^{2}=\varphi(F^{2})
, it follows that
K\subset H+F^{2}
. As a result,
F=H+K\subset H+K+F^{2}=H+F^{2}
, as needed since
H\neq F
.
∎

2.4 A residual property of finite relatively free nilpotent algebras.

In this subsection we show one more feature of finite nilpotent algebras.

Let
\cal S
be a set of algebras. An algebra
B
is
residually an algebra in

\cal S
if for every non-zero element
b\in B
and some algebra
S\in\cal S
, there is an epimorphism
\varphi:B\to S
such that
\varphi(b)\neq 0
. If
\mathcal{S}=\{A\}
and
B
is residually an algebra in
\mathcal{S}
, then we say that
B
is residually
A
.

Suppose an
m
-dimensional algebra
A
is nilpotent of class
c
and has a central series, a flag
A=A_{0}\supset A_{1}\supset\ldots\supset A_{m}=\{0\}
, which is a refinement of the power series
A\supset A^{2}\supset A^{3}\supset\cdots
. Let
E=(e_{1},\ldots,e_{m})
be a basis in
A
, compatible with the flag, that is,
e_{i}\in A_{i-1}
, for all
i\geq 1
.

An analogue of the following lemma is known in Group Theory, but its proof there is more complex.

Lemma 10
.

Let
p=p(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})
be a nonassociative polynomial. Then the
j
th coordinate of the value
p(y_{1},\ldots,y_{k})
for
y_{1},\ldots,y_{k}\in A
is a polynomial function
f_{j}
of degree
\leq c
with zero constant term in the coordinates of the
vectors
y_{1},\ldots,y_{k}
in basis
E
. Here
j=1,\ldots,m
.

Proof.

Let us assign to each
e_{i}
a weight
\mathrm{wt\,}e_{j}=s
if
e_{j}\in A^{s}\backslash A^{s+1}
. Since for any
A^{k}
, a part of the basis
E
is a basis of
A^{k}
, if the
\mathrm{wt\,}e_{j}=s
and
\mathrm{wt\,}e_{k}=t
, then

e_{j}e_{k}=\sum_{\ell}c_{jk}^{\ell}e_{\ell}\mbox{ where }c_{jk}^{\ell}=0\mbox{ if }\mathrm{wt\,}e_{\ell}<s+t.

For the proof, we may assume that
p
is a monomial in
x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}
. We will use induction over the degree of
p
to prove a stronger claim, namely, that
f_{j}
has degree
\leq s
, if the weight of
e_{j}
equals
s
. If
\deg p=1
, then the claim is obvious since in this case, the value of the function
f_{j}
equals to the
j
th coordinate of
y_{1}
.

Now let us assume that
p=uv
, and that we have proved for the monomials
u,v
of lesser degrees that the values of
u
can be written as
S_{1}=\sum_{j}\lambda_{j}e_{j}
, where
\lambda_{j}
is a polynomial in the coordinates of
y_{1},\ldots,y_{k}
, such that the degree of each polynomial
\lambda_{j}
is at most the weight of
e_{j}
. Similarly, the values of
v
are given by the sum
S_{2}=\sum_{\ell}\mu_{\ell}e_{\ell}
.

When we multiply two summands of these sums,
\lambda_{j}e_{j}
and
\mu_{\ell}e_{\ell}

with
\mathrm{wt\,}e_{j}=s
,
\mathrm{wt\,}e_{\ell}=t
, we obtain
\lambda_{j}\mu_{l}\sum_{p}c_{jl}^{p}e_{p}
, where the only vectors
e_{p}
with nonzero coefficients are those, whose weights are at least
\mathrm{wt\,}e_{j}+\mathrm{wt\,}e_{\ell}=s+t
. At the same time, the degrees of their coefficients do not exceed
\deg\lambda_{j}+\deg\mu_{\ell}\leq s+t
. Similar restriction on the degrees of coefficients will hold when we consider the product
S_{1}S_{2}
. The proof is complete.
∎

One more lemma we will need is the following.

Lemma 11
.

Let
A
be an
m
-dimensional algebra of nilpotent class
c
over a field
\mathbf{F}
,
|\mathbf{F}|=q
. Assume that a nonassociative polynomial
p=p(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})
takes value
b\in A
after some evaluation in
A
. If
k\geq m(c+1)
, then there exists a generating set of elements
a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}\in A
, such that
p(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k})=b
.

Proof.

Assume
p(c_{1},\ldots,c_{k})=b
, for some
c_{1},\ldots,c_{k}\in A
. We define the function
f(y_{1},\ldots,y_{k})=p(c_{1}+y_{1},\ldots,c_{k}+y_{k})-p(c_{1},\ldots,c_{k})
from the
k
-th direct power of
A
to
A
, for which it is obvious that
f(0,\ldots,0)=0
. All solutions of the system of equations

f(y_{1},\ldots,y_{k})=0

in the coordinates in
E
can be presented in the following way. Remember that
0
on the right hand side has
m
coordinates. According to Lemma
10
, the solutions in
km
coordinates of the vectors
y_{1},\ldots,y_{k}
can be described as the solutions of a system of
m
algebraic equations
p_{j}=0
, where each
p_{j}
is an ordinary (associative-commutative) polynomial of degree at most
c
. Since the set of
km
zeros is the solution of the system, each
p_{j}
has constant term 0.

The sum
d
of degrees of all these polynomials do not exceed
cm
. By the Warning’s Second Theorem
[
36
]
, the number of solutions of this system is at least
q^{km-d}\geq q^{m(k-c)}
. It follows that
p
takes value
b
for at least
q^{m(k-c)}
tuples of vectors
a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}
of the form
c_{1}+y_{1},\ldots,c_{k}+y_{k}
, where
f(y_{1},\ldots,y_{k})=0
.

Thus, the proof will be complete if we check that
q^{m(k-c)}
is greater than the number of ordered subsets of the elements
y_{1},\ldots,y_{k}
, which
do not
generate
A
. Each such subset is contained in a subspace of dimension
(m-1)
of the vector space
A
. The number of these subsets is equal to
q^{k(m-1)}
, while the number of subspaces is less than
q^{m}
. Hence the number of non-generating subsets is less than
q^{km-k+m}
. Since
k\geq m(c+1)
, we finally have
m(k-c)\geq km-k+m
. The proof is complete.
∎

Theorem 5
.

If
A
is a finite nilpotent algebra then all free algebras
F_{n}
of ranks
n\geq m(c+1)
in
\mathrm{var\,}A
are residually-
A
algebras. Here
m=\dim A
and
c
is the nilpotency class of
A
.

Proof.

Assume that
p(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\neq 0
in the algebra
F_{n}=F(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
. Then

p(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=0
is not an identity of the algebra
A
since
A
generates

\mathrm{var\,}A
. Hence there are elements
a^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,a^{\prime}_{n}\in A
such that

b=p(a^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,a^{\prime}_{n})\neq 0
. By Lemma
11
, there are elements
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}
generating
A
such that
b=p(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})
. Thus, the mapping
x_{1}\mapsto a_{1},\dots,x_{n}\mapsto a_{n}
extends to an epimorphism
\varphi:F_{n}\to A
such that

\varphi(p(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}))=p(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})=b\neq 0
. So
F_{n}
is a residually-
A

algebra.

∎

3 Finite algebras in locally finite varieties

3.1 Birkhoff’s construction.

The following is a finite version of the fundamental G. Birkhoff’s Theorem
[
7
]
.

Let
\mathfrak{V}
be a variety generated by a finite algebra
A
over a finite field
\mathbf{F}
. Given a finite set
X=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}
we define the subset
\Phi
of the direct power
A^{X}
as the set of
nonzero
functions from
X
to
A
. Obviously,
|\Phi|={|A|^{n}-1}
. We consider the Cartesian product
B
of
|\Phi|
copies of
A
. It is convenient to view
B
as the set of functions on
\Phi
with values in
A
. The ‘‘componentwise’’ product
fg
of
f,g\in B
is given by
(fg)(\varphi)=f(\varphi)g(\varphi)

where
\varphi\in\Phi
. We will call
B
the
n
th
Birkhoff’s algebra of
A
, denoted by
{\mathcal{B}}_{n}(A)
. We call the dimension
\beta_{A}(n)
of
B
over the ground field
\mathbf{F}
the
Birkhoff’s function of
A
. We have
\beta_{A}(n)=(\dim A)(|A|^{n}-1)
.

For each
x\in X
, the function
\bar{x}\in B
is defined by
\bar{x}(\alpha)=\alpha(x)
, for all
\alpha\in\Phi
. Then the map
\iota:F(\mathfrak{V},X)\to B
, given by
\iota(x)=\bar{x}
is a homomorphism of
F=F(\mathfrak{V},X)\to B
. Actually, this is a monomorphism. Indeed, if
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in X
and
w(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in\Ker\iota
then for any nonzero function
\varphi:\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}\to A
, we would have

\iota(w(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}))(\varphi)=w(\varphi(x_{1}),\ldots,\varphi(x_{n}))=0,

meaning that
w(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=0
is identity in
A
, hence
w(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
is a zero element of
F
. We can restate the conclusion to the above argument, as follows.

Theorem 6
.

[Birkhoff]

Let
\mathfrak{V}=\mathrm{var\,}A
be a finitely generated variety, where
A
is a finite algebra. Then the free
\mathfrak{V}
-algebra
F_{n}=F(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},\mathfrak{V})
of rank
n
is isomorphic to a subalgebra in the Birkhoff’s algebra
{\mathcal{B}}_{n}(A)
of
A
. So
\dim F_{n}\leq\beta_{A}(n)
, or
|F_{n}|\leq|A|^{|A|^{n}-1}
. The same estimate is true for any
n
-generated algebra
C\in\mathrm{var\,}A
:

\dim C\leq(\dim A)(|A|^{n}-1)\mbox{ or }|C|\leq|A|^{|A|^{n}-1}.

(7)

Proof.

The first inequality in (
7
) follows because
C
is a factor-algebra of
F_{n}
, hence

\dim C\leq\dim F_{n}\leq\beta_{A}(n)=(\dim A)(|A|^{n}-1).

If
\mathbf{F}=q
then

|C|=q^{\dim C}\leq q^{(\dim A)(|A|^{n}-1)}=(q^{\dim A})^{|A|^{n}-1}\leq|A|^{|A|^{n}-1},

as claimed.
∎

Corollary 1
.

Let
\mathfrak{V}
be the variety generated by a finite algebra
A
.
Then the orders of chief factors (of composition factors) in any algebra
B\in\mathfrak{V}

do not exceed the maximum of the orders of the chief factors (resp., of composition factors)
of the algebra
A
.

Proof.

Using Jordan - Hölder Theorem for the algebras, we see first that the statement is true if
B
is a direct power of
A
, second, it is true for subalgebras of direct powers,
and so by Theorem
6
, for
\mathfrak{V}
-free algebras of finite rank, and third, it is
inherited by their factor-algebras, which completes the proof.

∎

Remark 3
.

In many cases, the
double exponential
estimate
|F_{n}|\leq|A|^{|A|^{n}-1}
can be improved. This was noticed as early as in 1937 in a paper by B. H. Neumann
[
30
]
.

To see this,
let us denote the extension of
\lambda\in\Phi
to
F_{n}

by the same letter and consider for each
\lambda
the subalgebra
A_{\lambda}=\lambda(F_{n})\subset A
. Then each function from the image
\iota(F_{n})
belong
to the space of dimension

\sum_{\lambda\in\Phi}\dim A_{\lambda}\leq(|A|^{n}-1)\dim A\leq\beta_{A}(n).

(8)

It follows that if an algebra
A
has a proper nonzero subalgebra then the first inequality in (
8
) is strict and the dimension
d(n)
of
F_{n}(\mathfrak{V})
is bounded from above by a function strictly smaller than Birkhoff’s function.

We treat this question in more detail in Theorem
17
.

3.2 Enveloping algebras and varieties.

With each algebra
A
over a field
\bf F
, one can associate three algebras of linear operators on
A
.

The algebra
U_{L}(A)
is a subalgebra in
\End_{\bf F}A
generated by the operators
L(a)
of left multiplication by the elements
a\in A
. Similarly,
U_{R}(A)
is a subalgebra in
\End_{\bf F}A
generated by the operators
R(a)
of right multiplication by the elements
a\in A
. Finally,
U(A)
is a subalgebra in
\End A
, generated by all the operators of both left and right multiplication
L(a),R(a)
. We will call
U_{L}(A)
,
U_{R}(A)
, and
U(A)
the
left, right, and (two-sided) associative envelopes
of
A
.

Having in mind Remark
1
, we state the following Proposition giving one more definition of nilpotency of
nonassociative
algebras in terms of their
associative
enveloping algebras.

Proposition 6
.

An algebra
A
is nilpotent of depth
d\geq 1
if and
only if the associative algebra
U(A)
is nilpotent of class
d-1
.

Proof.

Let
A
be nilpotent of depth
d
. By Proposition
5
(iv), there is
an annihilator series
\{0\}=A_{0}\subset A_{1}\subset\dots\subset A_{d-1}\subset A_{d}=A
. By the definition of this series,
L(a)(b)
and
R(a)(b)
belong to
A_{i-1}
if
a\in A
and
b\in A_{i}
,
i\geq 1
.
Therefore the same is true if one replaces the operators
L(a)
and
R(a)
with any elements
of
U(A)
. Thus, by induction, the product of any
d
elements of
U(A)
is zero so that
U(A)
is an associative algebra of nilpotent class at most
d-1
.

Assuming now that
U(A)
is a nilpotent algebra of class
d-1
, we can see that the value of every monomial
w
of degree
d
and depth
d-1
equals zero in
A
. Indeed, the computation of
a value of
w
in
A
can be interpreted as a consecutive application of
d-1
operators
of left and right multiplication, whence
A\in{\mathfrak{N}}(d)
.
∎

Given a locally finite variety of algebras
\mathfrak{V}
, one can consider three varieties of associative algebras
\mathfrak{M}_{L}(\mathfrak{V})
,
\mathfrak{M}_{R}(\mathfrak{V})
, and
\mathfrak{M}(\mathfrak{V})
, generated by all left, respectively, right and two-sided associative envelopes of finite algebras
A\in\mathfrak{V}
.

Proposition 7
.

If
A
is a finite algebra then the variety
\mathfrak{M}(\mathrm{var\,}A)
is
generated by the finite associative algebra
U(A)
.

Proof.

Let
I
be an ideal in the algebra
A
. Obviously,
I
is invariant under the action of
U(A)
. Thus,
\varphi\in U
naturally acts on
A/I
by
\varphi(a+I)=\varphi(a)+I
.
Hence we have a natural map
U(A)\to U(A/I)
, which is an epimorphism.

Now if
B
is a subalgebra in
A
, then the elements
R(b)
,
L(b)
,
b\in B
, generate a subalgebra
U^{\prime}
of
U(A)
which leaves
B
invariant. The homomorphism of restriction, maps
U^{\prime}
onto
U(B)
. Thus
U(B)\in\mathrm{var\,}U(A)
.

If
B=C\times D
then there are two epimorphisms of restriction from
U(B)
onto
U(C)
and
U(D)
. The intersection of their kernels is trivial. As a result,
U(B)
is isomorphic to the direct product of
U(C)
and
U(D)
.

By Birkhoff’s Theorem
6
, any finite algebra
B\in\mathrm{var\,}A
can be obtained from
A
using Birkhoff’s operators of taking subgroups, homomorphic images and direct products. So it follows from the above that for any finite
B\in\mathrm{var\,}A
, the algebra
U(B)
can be obtained from
U(A)
by similar operations. Hence
U(B)\in\mathrm{var\,}U(A)
.

∎

Remark 4
.

It is possible to define
\mathfrak{W}
with the help of any, not necessarily finite algebras in
\mathfrak{V}
. This follows from the fact, that if a polynomial in the linear operators of, say, left multiplications, defined by the elements
a_{1},\ldots,a_{s}
, acts trivially on any finitely generated subalgebra
\mathbb{\langle}a_{1},\ldots,a_{s},\ldots,a_{t}\mathbb{\rangle}
of a locally finite algebra
A
in
\mathfrak{V}
, then it acts trivially on the whole of
A
.

3.3 Minimal representation of subdirect products.

By Birkhoff’s Theorem
6
, a free algebra
F
in
\mathrm{var\,}A
is a subalgebra of a Cartesian power of the algebra
A
. Therefore
F
is a subdirect product of algebras isomorphic to subalgebras of
A
. Subdirect products of a family of algebras is not defined up to isomorphism. So in this subsection we define
minimal
representations and discuss their propertiess.

Recall that a
section
of an algebra
A
is a factor-algebra of a subalgebra of
A
. All sections of an algebra
A
, except for
A/\{0\}
are called
proper
sections of
A
.
Here we call a set
\cal S
of finite non-zero algebras over a field
\bf F

sectionally closed
if for every
S\in\cal S
and every non-zero section
T
of
S
, the set
\cal S
contains an isomorphic copy of the algebra
T
. If a finite algebra
A
is isomorphic to a subdirect product, where the factors isomorphic
to some algebras from
\cal S
then we will write
A\in\texttt{sd}(\cal S)
.

If
A
is a subdirect product of
S_{1},\dots,S_{m}
, then we may assume that
|S_{1}|\geq|S_{2}|\geq\cdots\geq|S_{m}|
.
Such a representation of
A
as a subdirect product of algebras
S_{i}
from
\cal S
is called
minimal

if the sequence
(|S_{1}|,|S_{2}|,\ldots,|S_{m}|)
is minimal with respect to the lexicographical ordering
in comparison to other such representations of
A
as an element of the class
\texttt{sd}(\cal S)
.

To formulate the next well-known Proposition, we recall that an algebra
S
is called
monolitic
if the intersection
M(S)
of all non-zero ideals of
S
is non-zero. The unique minimal ideal
M(S)
is called the
monolith
of
S
. Every algebra
S
is a sudirect product of the
monolithic factor-algebras of
S
. Therefore if some
S_{i}
in a representation of
A\in\texttt{sd}(\cal S)

is not monolithic, i.e.
S_{i}
is a subdirect product of its proper factor-algebras, then
one can choose a lexicographically lesser representation for
A
. As the consequence we have

Proposition 8
.

Let
\cal S
be a sectionally closed set of finite algebras and suppose that
a finite algebra
A
belongs to the class
sd
(S). Then all the factors
S_{i}
in the
minimal representation of
A
with respect to
\cal S
, are monolithic algebras.

Combining Proposition
8
with Theorem
6
we obtain

Corollary 2
.

Let
\mathfrak{V}
be a variety of algebras over a finite field
\mathbf{F}
. Assume that
\mathfrak{V}
is generated by a finite sectionally closed set of finite algebras
\cal S
. Then any free algebra of finite rank in
\mathfrak{V}
is a subdirect product of finitely many monolithic algebras in
\cal S
.

We will also refer to the following properies of minimal representations.

Lemma 12
.

(
Minimal representations
) Let
S
be a sectionally
closed set of finite algebras and a finite algebra A has a minimal representation with respect
to
\cal S

A\subset P=\prod_{i=1}^{m}S_{i},\mbox{ such that }|S_{1}|\geq|S_{2}|\geq\cdots\geq|S_{m}|,

(9)

Then for all
i=1,\ldots,m
, the following are true:

\mathrm{(i)}

S_{i}
is a monolithic algebra,

\mathrm{(ii)}

the projection of
A
into
S_{i}
equals
S_{i}
,

\mathrm{(iii)}

the intersection
A\cap S_{i}
is an ideal of
S_{i}
,

\mathrm{(iv)}

the monoliths
M(S_{i})
are minimal ideals in
A
.

Proof.

The first property is given by Lemma
8
. The second one follows from
the minimality of the representation (
9
) since the factor
S_{i}
can be replaced
with the projection of
A
to
S_{i}
.

Since
A
projects onto the whole
S_{i}
, any product of an element
b\in A\cap S_{i}

by an element from
S_{i}
can be replaced with a product by an element from
A
,
and property (iii) follows.

For the same reason, if
A
contains a non-zero element
b\in S_{i}
, it has to contain the whole
monolith
M_{i}
, and we are done. Otherwise
A\cap S_{i}=\{0\}
, and it follows that
A
is isomorphic to the projection of
A
to the product of all factors of
P
except for
S_{i}
, which
contradicts the minimality of the representation (
9
). So the proof of (iv) is
complete.

∎

3.4 Variety generated by a finite simple algebra.

A non-zero finite algebra
A
is called
critical
if it does not belong to
the variety generated by all proper sections of
A
. If
A
is not critical then the variety

\mathrm{var\,}A
is generated by the proper sections of
A
. Therefore any locally finite variety
\mathfrak{V}

can be generated by a set of critical algebras from
\mathfrak{V}
. However a variety generated by a finite algebra over a field can contain infinitely many subvarieties
[
33
]
. So we raise the following

Problem 1
.

Can a variety generated by a finite algebra over a field contain
uncountably
many subvarieties?

A critical algebra is monolithic since otherwise there are two non-zero ideals with zero intersection, and the algebra is a subdirect product of two proper factor-algebras.

The following theorem clarifies the structure of finite algebras in a variety
generated by a finite simple algebra. It follows from Corollary
1
that a
finite simple algebra is critical
. (Moreover, a non-zero finite algebra
A
is critical if the product of any two non-zero ideals of
A
is non-zero
[
2
]
.)

Theorem 7
.

Let
C
be a finite algebra in the variety
\mathfrak{V}=\mathrm{var\,}A
, where
A
is a finite simple algebra. Then the following are true

\mathrm{(i)}

C
is the direct sum of two ideals:
C=P\oplus Q
. The ideal
P
is a direct power of the algebra
A
(possibly zero), while
Q
belongs to the variety
\mathfrak{U}
generated by all proper subalgebras of
A
.

\mathrm{(ii)}

If
C=F
is a free algebra of rank
n
in
\mathfrak{V}
then
F=P\oplus Q
, where
P
is a direct power of
A
and
Q
is a
\mathfrak{U}
-free algebra of rank
n
.

Proof.

One may assume that
A^{2}\neq\{0\}
since otherwise the statement of the theorem
is obviously true. We first prove Claim (ii).

By Birkhoff’s argument,
F\in\texttt{sd}(A)
, and one can choose a minimal representation of
F

according to Proposition
8
. Hence
F
is the subdirect product of
P=A_{1}\times\cdots\times A_{s}
, where
A_{i}\cong A
, for all
i=1,\ldots,s
, and several proper sections of
A
, that is,
F\subset K=P\oplus H
, where
H\in\mathfrak{U}
. Since
the monolith of a simple algebra is the whole algebra, by Lemma
12
(iv), we have
P\subset F
. It follows that
F=P\oplus Q
, where
Q=F\cap H\in\mathfrak{U}
.

The orders of the composition sections of finite algebras in
\mathfrak{U}
are strictly less
than
|A|
by Corollary
1
. Therefore
\mathfrak{U}
is a proper subvariety of
\mathfrak{V}

and the algebra
F/\mathfrak{U}(F)
is a
\mathfrak{U}
-free algebra of rank
n
.
On the one hand, since
Q\in\mathfrak{U}

Now to prove part (i), we should consider the factor-algebras

If for some

In this case we automatically obtain that the projection of

∎

3.5 Computation of some free products.

The
free product
of two algebras
B
and
C
in a variety
\mathfrak{V}
is an algebra
D\in\mathfrak{V}
such
that there exist homomorphisms
\beta:B\to D
and
\gamma:C\to D
satisfying the following conditions:

\mathrm{(i)}

D
is generated by the subalgebras
\beta(B)
and
\gamma(C)
,

\mathrm{(ii)}

For any homomorphisms
\beta^{\prime}:B\to E
and
\gamma^{\prime}:C\to E
, where
E\in\mathfrak{V}
, there exists a homomorphism
\delta:D\to E
such that
\beta^{\prime}=\delta\beta
and
\gamma^{\prime}=\delta\gamma
.

The free product
D=B*C
exists and unique up to isomorphism (
[
26
]
, Section 12). It is finite if

B
and
C
are finite algebras in a locally finite variety
\mathfrak{V}
. Therefore one can view
B*C

as an algebra of maximal order in
\mathfrak{V}
generated by two isomorphic copies of
B
and
C
. In this section, we consider an example of computation of the free product in a locally
finite variety
\mathfrak{V}
. We may identify
B
and
C
with their isomorphic images in
D
.

Lemma 13
.

Let
\mathfrak{V}
be a variety generated by a simple algebra
A
,
B
and
C
two finite direct powers of the algebra
A
. Then the free product
D=B*C
in
\mathfrak{V}
is also a
direct power of
A
.

Proof.

By Theorem
7
(i),
D
is a direct sum of two ideals
P
and
Q
, where
P
is a
direct power of
A
and
Q
belongs to a proper subvariety
\mathfrak{U}
of
\mathrm{var\,}A
. Consider
the projection of
B
to
Q
. If the image of a direct factor
A^{\prime}\cong A
of
B
is not zero,
then this image is isomorphic to
A
, because
A
is a simple algebra, but
A\notin\mathfrak{U}
, a contradiction.
So
B
and also
C
are subalgebras of
P
, and
Q=\{0\}
since
D
is generated by
B
and
C
.
∎

In the example below, we assume that the finite simple algebra
A
has no nontrivial automorphisms.
Obviously, this condition is limiting. However, we will show in Section
6
that almost every
n
-dimensional non-associative algebra over a finite field is simple and has trivial
automorphism group.

Proposition 10
.

Let
A
be a finite simple algebra without nontrivial automorphisms and
A^{2}\neq\{0\}
. Assume
that an algebra
B
(an algebra
C
) is isomorphic to the product of
k
(resp., of
\ell
) copies of
A
.
Then the free product
D=B*C
in
\mathfrak{V}=\mathrm{var\,}A
is isomorphic to the direct product of
(k+1)(\ell+1)-1
copies of
A
.

Proof.

We can write
B
as
A(1)\oplus\dots\oplus A(k)
with
A(i)\cong A
for
i=1,\dots,k
and
by Lemma
13
, we have similar decomposition
D=A_{1}\oplus\dots\oplus A_{t}
. By Lemma
5
, there are disjoint subsets
J_{1},\dots,J_{k}
of the set
J=\{1,\dots,t\}
such that

A(i)
is the diagonal
\Delta_{i}
of the sum
\sum_{j\in J_{i}}A_{j}
. It follows that the set

can be partitioned into

, where
J_{0}
complements the union of all other subsets
J_{i}
in

. Hence any element from

has zero projection onto
A_{m}
, if

.

Similar partition
J=J^{\prime}_{0}\cup\ldots\cup J^{\prime}_{\ell}
is defined by the subalgebra
C
of

. Let
J_{ii^{\prime}}=J_{i}\cap J^{\prime}_{i^{\prime}}
. In this case, the projection of any element from
B
or
C
to the sum
\sum_{s\in J_{ii^{\prime}}}A_{j}
belongs to the diagonal
\Delta_{ii^{\prime}}
of this sum. Here we use a simple fact that the direct power of an algebra without nontrivial automorphisms has a unique diagonal.

Hence the sum of the diagonals
\Delta_{ii^{\prime}}
for all disjoint subsets

contains both
B
and
C
, hence equals to the whole
D
. Moreover, the subset
J_{00}
is empty since
B
and
C
generate

. Some of other subsets

could be empty too, but in any case the number of the subalgebras

is at most the number of the subsets

with

, and so

.

To obtain the converse inequality, it is sufficient to construct an algebra in
\mathrm{var\,}
A generated by the images of the algebras
B
and
C
and having dimension
(k\ell+k+\ell)\dim A
. Our preceding argument suggests the following approach. Let us enumerate the copies
A_{ii^{\prime}}
of the algebra
A
by two indexes,
i=0,\ldots,k
,
i^{\prime}=0,\ldots,\ell
,
(i,i^{\prime})\neq(0,0)
, and consider their direct sum
D
.

We isomorphically map each the summand
A(i)
of
B
,
(i=1,\ldots,k)
onto the diagonal of the sum
A_{i0}\oplus\dots\oplus A_{il}
. It is obvious, that all these maps extend to a monomorphism
B\to D
. By mapping the summands of

into the diagonals of the sum
A_{0j}\oplus\cdots\oplus A_{kj}
, we obtain an embedding

.

The product of such two ‘‘orthogonal’’ diagonals for

,

is
A_{ij}^{2}=A_{ij}
since
A_{ij}
is a simple
algebra with non-zero multiplication. Hence we have

for

. The diagonal of the sum
A_{i0}+\cdots+A_{il}
is also in

for
i=1,\ldots,k
. Similarly we get

A_{0j}+\cdots+A_{kj}
in

. Consequently,

contains at least

direct summands isomorphic
to

, and so

∎

The second part of the proof provides us with the construction of the free product
B*C
.
By induction, we have

Corollary 5
.

If

are direct sums of

, resp., copies of the

from Proposition

is a direct sum of

In other words, we have obtained the precise upper bound for the dimensions of any algebra of the variety, which is generated by subalgebras

.

3.6 Characteristically simple algebra.

The properties of characteristically simple and verbally simple finite groups are well-known. But a subalgebra invariant with respect to all automorphisms of an algebra is not necessarily an ideal, as opposed to normal subgroups in Group Theory. So the case of algebras requires a special treatment.

Recall that a subalgebra
B
of an algebra
A
is called
characteristic
if
B
is invariant under all automorphisms of
A
. We say that an algebra
A
is
strongly characteristically simple
, for short a CS-algebra, respectively,
weakly characteristically simple
, for short CW-algebra, if
A
has no nonzero proper subalgebras, respectively, ideals, which are invariant under all of its automorphisms. We also say that
A
is
verbally simple
if it has no nonzero proper verbal ideals.

A finite direct power
P=\underbrace{A\oplus\cdots\oplus A}_{k}
of a simple algebra
A
is both a CW-algebra and verbally
simple by Lemma
3
(i), since there are automorphisms permuting the direct factors
of
P
. Conversely, let
B
be a finite
CW
-algebra and
M
be a minimal
ideal of
B
. Then for any automorphism
\alpha
of
B
the image
\alpha(M)
is a minimal ideal as well, and so the sum of such images over all
\alpha
must be equal to
B

since the sum is a characteristic ideal of
B
. This sum is a direct some of
some ideals isomorphic to
M
. Then any ideal of
M
is an ideal of
B
,
and the minimality of
M
implies that
M
is simple. So a finite algebra is CW (or verbally simple) if and only if it is a direct power of a finite simple algebra.

Speaking about
CS
-algebras, first of all, any algebra with zero multiplication is CS. Also, a minimal algebra is a simple CS-algebra. As for nonsimple CS-algebras, we prove the following.

Theorem 8
.

A finite nonsimple algebra
P
with nonzero multiplication is a CS-algebra if and only if

P=\underbrace{A\oplus\cdots\oplus A}_{k}
,
k>1
, where
A
is a simple
CS
-algebra which is not a minimal algebra without nontrivial automorphisms.

Proof.

Let
P
be a finite CS-algebra. Then it is also a CW-algebra,
and so
P=A_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus A_{k}
, where
A_{i}
are isomorphic copies of
a simple algebra
A
. Since
P^{2}\neq\{0\}
, we have that
A^{2}\neq\{0\}
. If
A
has a proper characteristic subalgebra
B\neq\{0\}
then the sum of the
k
copies
B_{i}\subset A_{i}
is a proper characteristic subalgebra of
P
which is true because any automorphism of
P
permutes the factors
A_{i}
, as it follows from Lemma
3
(i). Thus,
A
must be a
CS
-algebra.

It is not possible that
A
is minimal and has no non-trivial automorphisms. Indeed, then

P
has a unique diagonal
D
, which is proper characteristic subalgebra of
P
,
a contradiction.

Conversely, assume that
A
is a finite simple
CS
-algebra and

P=\underbrace{A\oplus\cdots\oplus A}_{k}
, where
k\geq 2
and
A_{i}
are isomorphic copies of

A
. If
k>1
we also assume that
A
is not a minimal algebra with trivial automorphism group.

Consider a non-zero characteristic subalgebra
B\subset P
. It has to have isomorphic
projections
B_{i}
to each of the factors
A_{i}
. If
B_{1}
is a proper subalgebra
of
A_{1}
then there is an automorphism
\alpha
of
A_{1}
such that
\alpha(B_{1})\neq B_{1}
,
because
A_{1}
is a CS-algebra. It follows that
B\neq\beta(B)
, where
\beta
acts
on
A_{1}
as
\alpha
and acts identically on
A_{2},\ldots,A_{k}
. So
B
is not
a characteristic subgroup of
P
, a contradiction.

Thus,
B
projects surjectively onto every
A_{i}
.
By Lemma
3
,
B
is itself isomorphic
to a direct power of
A
. Obviously
B
cannot be a characteristic subalgebra of
A
if

B
is just the product of some subalgebras
A_{i}
and
B\neq P
. So by Lemma
5
, one of the
direct factors
A^{\prime}\cong A
of

is a diagonal of a the direct sum
A_{i_{1}}\oplus\dots\oplus A_{i_{s}}
, where
s\geq 2
, and other direct factors of

have zero projections to each of

A_{i_{1}},\dots,A_{i_{s}}
. Since
B
is characteristic in
P
and the automorphisms
of
P
can arbitrarily permute the factors
A_{i}
, it follows that
B
is just a diagonal of the whole

. However a diagonal is invariant under all automorphisms of
P
iff it is unique, that
is iff (since
k\geq 2
) the algebra
A
has no non-trivial automorphisms. Since

is CS, in this case,

also has no non-zero proper subalgebras. For this exception,
we obtain that

∎

Note that a finite field

has no non-identical automorphisms. Therefore the product

is not a CS-algebra; the diagonal is a characteristic subalgebra of it.
In the associative case,

refers only to the algebras with zero multiplication and the field of coefficients (as an algebra over itself), that is, the condition looks too strong.

3.7 Semidirect products and extremal algebras.

An algebra

of an ideal

and a subalgebra

C=A+B

and

Semidirect sums are an important tool, which is used in the study of extensions of algebras. Since solvable algebras appear as extensions of smaller dimension, semidirect sums are essential for the study of solvable algebras, as well. They are also important in the study of projectivity and injectivity of algebras in locally finite varieties, see Sections

Let
A
be an ideal with zero multiplication (abelian ideal) of an algebra

(\overline{c_{1}},a_{1})(\overline{c_{2}},a_{2})=(\overline{c_{1}c_{2}},a_{1}c_{2}+c_{1}a_{2}),\mbox{ where }a_{1},a_{2}\in A,\>c_{1},c_{2}\in C.

(10)

One denotes

by

Proposition 11

If

Proof.

It is enough to show that

is an ideal in

is an algebra with zero multiplication. So we set

We define

This setting is well-defined because if we replace

then on the right-hand side we will have

(c+a^{\prime}+a,c+a^{\prime})+{\widetilde{A}}=(c+a,a)+(a^{\prime},a^{\prime})+{\widetilde{A}}=(c+a,c)+{\widetilde{A}}.

If
\varphi(\bar{c},a)=0
than
(c+a,c)\in{\widetilde{A}}
so

\displaystyle\varphi((\overline{c_{1}},a_{1})(\overline{c_{2}},a_{2}))=\varphi((\overline{c_{1}c_{2}},a_{1}c_{2}+c_{1}a_{2}))=(c_{1}c_{2}+a_{1}c_{2}+c_{1}a_{2},c_{1}c_{2})+{\widetilde{A}}

\displaystyle=((c_{1}+a_{1},c_{1})+{\widetilde{A}})((c_{2}+a_{2},c_{2})+{\widetilde{A}})=\varphi(\overline{c_{1}},a_{1})\varphi(\overline{c_{2}},a_{2}).

Thus,

is an isomorphism of

In the remainder of this section we will study a class of algebras, which are close to critical algebras.

4 Miscellaneous properties of locally finite varieties

4.1 Socle height of algebras in finitely generated varieties.

The socle
S_{1}=\soc(A)
of a finite algebra (or ring)
A
is the sum of all its minimal ideals (hence, the direct sum of some of them). We define the second socle
S_{2}
, by saying that
S_{2}/S_{1}=\soc(A/S_{1})
, and so on. As a result, we obtain the ascending series of
A
, ending in
A
. We call this series a socle series of
A
. The length of the socle series of
A
is called the
socle height
of
A
. An equivalent definition of the socle height is the length of the shortest ascending series of ideals:

\{0\}=J_{0}\subset J_{1}\subset J_{2}\subset\cdots,\mbox{where for all }k=1,2,\ldots,\;\;J_{k}/J_{k-1}\subset\soc(A/J_{k-1}).

Theorem 10
.

The socle heights of finite algebras in a finitely generated variety are uniformly bounded.

Proof.

Let
\mathfrak{V}=\mathrm{var\,}A
,
A
a finite algebra. Since under an epimorphism of algebras
P\to Q
, the socle
\soc(P)
is mapped inside the socle
\soc(Q)
, it is enough to prove that the socle heights of free algebras
F_{n}(\mathfrak{V})
,
n=1,2,\ldots
, are uniformly bounded.

Let us remind that a locally finite variety is generated by its monolithic algebras, in particular,
\mathrm{var\,}A
is generated by the set
\mathcal{S}
of all monolithic sections of
A
. We consider the partially ordered set
\mathcal{X}
of subsets
\mathcal{T}\subset\mathcal{S}
which contains all monolithic sections of its members. For each such
\mathcal{T}
, we consider the variety
\mathrm{var\,}\mathcal{T}
. There are only finitely many varieties
\mathrm{var\,}\mathcal{T}
, and to prove our theorem, we can apply induction by
|\mathcal{T}|
. The basis of induction is obvious, when
\mathcal{T}
is empty. Let
\mathcal{T}
be nonempty. We may assume that
\mathrm{var\,}\mathcal{T}
is not generated by proper subsets
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{T}
.

Using the minimal representation, see Lemma
12
, we represent the free algebra
G_{n}
of
\mathrm{var\,}\mathcal{T}
as the subdirect product of algebras in
\mathcal{T}
. Note that in
\mathcal{T}
, there is an algebra
B
, which is not a proper section of other algebras in
\mathcal{T}
. Moreover, one may assume, that
B
cannot be removed from
\mathcal{T}
, without decreasing the variety generated by
\mathcal{T}
. Therefore the set
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}=\mathcal{T}\backslash\{B\}\in\mathcal{X}
and the socle lengths of finite algebras in
\mathfrak{U}=\mathrm{var\,}\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
are uniformly bounded.

As a result, if
n
is not less than the number of generators in
B
, then
B
must be a factor of the direct product
D
containing the minimal representation of the
\mathfrak{V}
-free algebra
G_{n}
. This follows because
B
is a homomorphic image of
G_{n}
and
B\notin\mathfrak{U}
. By Lemma
12
(iv), the monolith
М
of
B
is a minimal ideal of
G_{n}
.

Thus, we can see that for all direct factors
B_{i}
of
D
, isomorphic to
B
, their monoliths
M_{i}
are contained in the socle of
G_{n}
. Let
N
be the sum of all
M_{i}
. If we form
D/N
, then by the choice of
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
and
B
, it follows that
D/N
belongs to the variety
\mathfrak{U}
. Hence also
G_{n}/N\in\mathfrak{U}
. By induction, all finite algebras in
\mathfrak{U}
have socle height at most
h
, for some integer
h
. This also applies to
G_{n}/N
. Since
N
belongs to the socle of an algebra
G_{n}
, the socle height of
G_{n}
is at most
h+1
for all
n
, completing the proof of the theorem.
∎

Using this theorem, we can define the
socle height of a finitely generated variety

\mathfrak{V}
as maximum of socle heights of finite algebras in
\mathfrak{V}
.

Example 4
.

Let be
A
a nilpotent algebra. On the one hand, since every one-dimensional subspace of the annihilator
I(A)
is a minimal ideal in
A
, we have
I(A)\subset\soc(A)
. On the other hand, by Lemma
9
(i), every minimal ideal of
A
is contained in
\soc(A)
. Hence
\soc(A)=I(A)
. Using induction, we conclude that the socle series of

is the annihilator series of
A
, and so
the socle height of

is equal to the nilpotent depth of

(see Subsection
2
).

Remark 9
.

It follows from Theorem
10
and Example
4
that finite
nilpotent algebras in a finitely generated variety
\mathfrak{V}
have uniformly bounded nilpotent
depths (and by Proposition
4
, bounded nilpotent classes). Since
\mathfrak{V}
is a locally finite variety, the same upper bound holds for all nilpotent algebras from
\mathfrak{V}
. Being a subclass of
\mathfrak{V}
closed under all three Birkhoff operations,
the class of all nilpotent algebras in a finitely generated variety

is a subvariety of

.

4.2 Socle height 1 and inherently semisimple algebras.

We call a finite-dimensional algebra
A

inherently semisimple
if any subalgebra of
A
(including
A
itself) is the direct product of simple algebras. Any minimal algebra is inherently semisimple but there are many more, even simple algebras, with this property. For example, any finite field extension
\bf E
of the base field
\bf F
, is a simple inherently semisimple algebra. Another example is the simple algebra having three one-dimensional subalgebras from Example
3
. Moreover, almost all
n
-dimensional algebras have this property because, as we will show in Section
6
, being simple and having no subalgebras of dimension greater than 1 is a generic property.

Obviously, any subalgebra of an inherently semisimple algebra is inherently semisimple.

Remark 10
.

Inherently semisimple algebras need not be semisimple in the traditional sense of associative or Lie algebras because they include algebras with zero multiplication.

Theorem 11
.

The following properties of a non-zero locally finite variety
\mathfrak{V}
are equivalent.

\mathrm{(i)}

In any finite algebra
A\in\mathfrak{V}
, given an ideal
I
, there is an ideal
J
such that
A=I\oplus J
;

\mathrm{(ii)}

Any finite
A\in\mathfrak{V}
is the direct product of simple algebras;

\mathrm{(iii)}

The socle height of
\mathfrak{V}
equals 1;

\mathrm{(iv)}

\mathfrak{V}
is generated by a family of inherently semisimple algebras.

\mathrm{(v)}

All finite algebras in
\mathfrak{V}
are inherently semisimple.

\mathrm{(vi)}

In any algebra
A\in\mathfrak{V}
, given a finite ideal
I
, there is an ideal
J
such that
A=I\oplus J
;.

Proof.

\mathrm{(vi)}\to\mathrm{(i)}\to\mathrm{(ii)}\to\mathrm{(iii)}
are obvious.

\mathrm{(ii)}\to\mathrm{(i)}
follows from Lemma
3
(i).

\mathrm{(iii)}\to\mathrm{(ii)}
Any finite algebra
A\in\mathfrak{V}
is the direct sum of its minimal ideals. These ideals must be simple themselves because their ideals are the ideals of the whole algebra.

\mathrm{(ii)}\to\mathrm{(v)}\to\mathrm{(iv)}
are obvious.

\mathrm{(iv)}\to\mathrm{(ii)}
By Lemma
3
(ii), it is enough to prove that any
\mathfrak{V}
-free algebra

is the direct product of simple algebras. By Lemma
3
,
F_{n}
is contained as a subalgebra in the finite direct product of inherently semisimple algebras. Because subalgebras of inherently semisimple algebras are inherently semisimple themselves, this embedding of

can be subdirect in the direct product of simple algebras in
\mathfrak{V}
. But by Lemma
3
, a subdirect product of finitely many simple algebras is isomorphic to the direct product of some of them.

\mathrm{(ii)},\mathrm{(i)}\to\mathrm{(vi)}
If
I
is a simple direct factor of a finite algebra
A
, then the annihilator
J
of
I
it is either
A
or the direct complement of

. In both cases it is an ideal of
A
. Hence the same is true if an ideal
I
is the direct product of several simple ideals of
A
. Then the condition (ii) and Lemma

(i) imply that the annihilator of any ideal

of a finite algebra

is an ideal of

. Since the variety
\mathfrak{V}
is locally

is an ideal.

By the same Lemma, if
I
is a simple ideal of a finite algebra

and

, then

is the direct sum of
I
and the annihilator of
I

, then

. Since

is locally finite, the same alternative holds for a finite simple ideal in any algebra

. In both cases, we have a complementary ideal to

in

. Indeed, if

, then the vector apace

has a direct complement

in the factor-algebra

, and the ideal

is the direct complement of

in

.

Let now a finite ideal

of an algebra

be the direct sum of simple ideals

. Then by property (i),
I_{1}
is an ideal in every finite subalgebra of

is a locally finite algebra

is an ideal of the whole algebra

For a simple ideal

, we have already obtained a complementary ideal:

. Now
the intersection

is an ideal of

∎

Proposition 12
.

Let
\mathfrak{V}

.

Proof.

If

that an ideal

4.3 Injective algebras in locally finite varieties.

Lemma 14
.

Let
\mathfrak{V}
be a variety of algebras generated by a finite algebra

Then the order of the monolith

Proof.

The following is similar to the ‘chief centralizer argument’ used for the groups in

(see also

is finite, this follows from Corollary

. So we need to deal with the case where

. Then the differences

. Since

is minimal, it is generated, as an ideal, by each of these

be the set of these elements. Since one
needs finitely many element of

, to show that one of the element of

belongs to an ideal generated
by another element of

such that the ’ideal closure’ of any element from

. So there is

of

. Since

are pairwise different, and so

, a contradiction.

∎

If a finitely generated variety

∎

Recall that a subalgebra

The following properties of a locally finite variety

4.4 Projective algebras in finitely generated varieties.

An algebra

in a variety

if it is contained as a retract in a
\mathfrak{V}
-free algebra. In particular,
\mathfrak{V}
-free algebras are projective. The equivalent definition

\alpha:A\to B
, where

, the is a
homomorphism
\beta:B\to A
such that
\alpha\beta=\mathrm{id}_{B}
.

Given an algebra

, we say that an ideal

of

is
semidirectly complementable
in

if there is a subalgebra

in

such that

. We call an algebra

if any ideal

of any subalgebra

is directly complementable in

. It follows
from this definition, that all sections of a nice algebra are nice. For example, the associative algebra of

are not.

Theorem 13
.

Let a variety

\mathrm{(i)}

\mathrm{(ii)}

All finite algebras of

\mathrm{(iii)}

\mathrm{(i)\to(ii)}
If a finite algebra

without a complement in

\mathrm{(ii)\to(iii)}
This is a trivial implication.

\mathrm{(iii)\to(i)}

Suppose all algebras

To proceed, we need an auxiliary result.

(11)

∎

Now we return to the proof of Theorem

As noted in Section

Clearly there are only finitely many subsets with these properties in

generated

So, let

As usual, we may assume this emebedding minimal (see Section

4.5 Anti-Schreier varieties.

All finite algebras in the varieties considered in Subsection

4.6 Solvable varieties.

It is customary to define solvable algebras in two ways: first, by identical relations and, second, by certain series of ideals.

Our calculations below will be preformed in an absolutely free algebra
\mathcal{F}_{\infty}
of countable rank with the free set of generators
x_{1},x_{2},\ldots
. The identities
s_{k}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{2^{k}})=0
are defined by induction. For
k=1
, we set
s_{1}(x_{1},x_{2})=x_{1}x_{2}
. For greater values of
k
, we set

s_{k}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{2^{k}})=s_{k}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{2^{k-1}})s_{k}(x_{2^{k-1}+1},\ldots,x_{2^{k}}).

Also by induction, one defines the
commutator series
of an algebra
A
, namely,
A^{(1)}=A
, and

A^{(k)}=(A^{(k-1)})^{2}
for
k>1
.

Proposition 14
.

Let
A
be an algebra. Then the following are equivalent.

\mathrm{(i)}

A^{(\ell+1)}=\{0\}
for some
\ell\geq 0
;

\mathrm{(ii)}

there exists a
solvable
series of subspaces

A=A_{1}\supset A_{2}\supset\ldots\supset A_{\ell-1}\supset A_{\ell+1}=\{0\},

(12)

such that

A_{j}^{2}\subset A_{j+1}
, for all
j=1,\ldots,\ell
,

\mathrm{(iii)}

A
satisfies the identity
s_{\ell}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{2^{\ell}})=0
.

Proof.

(i)
\to
(ii) is true since the commutator series is solvable.

(ii)
\to
(iii) Clearly,
s_{1}(x_{1},x_{2})=0
if
\ell=1
since
A^{2}=\{0\}
in this case.
By induction, the ideal
A_{2}
from (
12
) satisfies the identity
s_{\ell-1}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{2^{\ell-1}})=0
if
\ell>1
. Since the products
a_{1}a_{2},\ldots,a_{2^{\ell}-1}a_{2^{\ell}}
are in
A^{2}=A^{(2)}
for any
a_{i}\in A
, one can replace the variables of
s_{\ell-1}
with these
2^{\ell-1}
products and obtain the identity
s_{\ell}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{2^{\ell}})=0
in
A
.

(iii)
\to
(i). This is obvious for
\ell=1
. Let
\ell>1
and
A
satisfies
s_{\ell}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{2^{\ell}})=0
. Note that
A^{(2)}=A^{2}
is simply a linear span of all products

uv
,
u,v\in\mathcal{F}_{\infty}
. Therefore, to check
s_{\ell-1}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{2^{\ell-1}})=0
in

A^{2}
, it is sufficient to replace the arguments only by such products. After this, the validity of
s_{\ell-1}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{2^{\ell-1}})=0

in
A^{2}
immediately follows from the validity of
s_{\ell}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{2^{\ell}})=0
in
A
. So by induction,

A^{(\ell+1)}=(A^{2})^{(\ell)}=\{0\}
.

∎

By definition, an algebra
A
is called
solvable
if it satisfies one of the equivalent
conditions of Proposition
12
. The minimal
\ell
is called the
solvable length

of
A
. In particular,
\ell=1
for algebras with zero multiplication. The containment

A_{j}^{2}\subset A_{j+1}
implies that
A_{j+1}
is and ideal of
A_{j}
for
j=1,\dots,\ell
.

An algebra is called
locally solvable
if any of its finitely generated subalgebras is solvable.

A variety
\mathfrak{V}
is called solvable ( locally solvable), if any algebra in
\mathfrak{V}
is solvable (resp., locally solvable). The solvable length of algebras in solvable variety has to be uniformly
bounded. (Use Cartesian products for a proof!)

In contrast with the case of nilpotent algebras, in the case of a solvable algebra
A
, it is not necessary that
A
has non-zero abelian ideals. As an example, let
A
be a 3-dimensional commutative algebra with a basis
a,b,c
and the multiplication table

a^{2}=b,\;ab=b^{2}=c,\;ac=b,\;bc=c,\;c^{2}=0.

Here
A^{2}=\mathbb{\langle}b,c\mathbb{\rangle}
,
(A^{2})^{2}=\mathbb{\langle}c\mathbb{\rangle}
,
((A^{2})^{2})^{2}=\{0\}
. One can easily see that the last nonzero term of the commutator series
(A^{2})^{2}=\mathbb{\langle}c\mathbb{\rangle}
is not an ideal, but only a subideal. Also, there

has no abelian ideals.

But we still can obtain a property similar to that in Remark
9
.

Proposition 15
.

Let
\mathfrak{V}=\mathrm{var\,}A
, where
|A|\leq\infty
. Then the set of all solvable algebras in
\mathfrak{V}
is a subvariety of
\mathfrak{V}
.

Proof.

From Lemma
12
, we know that the orders of all chief sections of finite algebras in
\mathfrak{V}
are bounded by
|A|
. It follows that the lengths of the solvable series of solvable chief sections

are bounded because the length of

cannot be greater than
\dim M
. Therefore the solvability lengths of the solvable socles, which are the sums of minimal ideals, has bounded solvable length. Since the socle height of all finite algebras in
\mathfrak{V}
is bounded by Theorem
10
and the solvable length of an extension of algebras does not exceed the sum of solvable lengths of the participating algebras, it follows that the solvable length of any finite solvable algebra in
\mathfrak{V}
is uniformly bounded from above. Since
\mathfrak{V}
is locally finite, the same is true for all solvable algebras in
\mathfrak{V}
. As a result, the set of all solvable algebras in
\mathfrak{V}
, which is obviously closed under subalgebras and factor-algebras, is also closed under Cartesian product. So, the class of solvable algebras in a finitely generated variety
\mathfrak{V}
is a subvariety of
\mathfrak{V}
, as claimed.
∎

5 Dimension functions of locally finite varieties

One of the most natural numerical invariants associated with a locally finite variety of arbitrary
universal algebras is the function
f(n)=f_{\mathfrak{V}}(n)
, whose value at an integer
n
is the order of the
\mathfrak{V}
-free algebra
F_{n}
of rank
n
. In
[
15
]
, this function is called the
free spectrum
of
\mathfrak{V}
. If
\mathfrak{V}
is a locally finite variety of
linear
algebras over a field
\mathbf{F}
then it is more convenient to replace

with the
dimension function

d(n)=d_{\mathfrak{V}}(n)=\dim_{\bf F}F_{n}(\mathfrak{V})
; in this case
f(n)=q^{d(n)}
, where
q=|\bf F|
. If
\mathfrak{V}=\mathrm{var\,}A
then we write
d_{\mathfrak{V}}(n)=d_{A}(n)
and call
d_{A}(n)
the
dimension function of the algebra

A
. Thus,
d(n)
is the supremum of the dimensions of all
n
-generated algebras in the variety
\mathfrak{V}
.

5.1 First estimates of dimension functions.

We start with some estimates of the growth of the function
d(n)
. Let us define the
contents

\mathrm{cont}(v)
of a monomial

as the (finite) subset

such that

but

where
Z
is a proper subset of

. For example,
\mathrm{cont}((x_{3}x_{2})x_{3})=\{x_{2},x_{3}\}
.

Given an arbitrary relatively free algebra
F=F(X)
and a finite subset
J\subset X
, we denote by
F[J]
the linear span of the images of all monomials
v
with

It is well known that the dimension function of nilpotent varieties is a polynomial functions up to the big O. It fact, it is equal to a polynomial. Let us define polynomials

Corollary 6

.

5.2 Dimension functions for simple algebras.

To estimate the dimension function of the variety generated by a finite simple algebra

\displaystyle x_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(x_{11},\ldots,x_{1N})

\displaystyle x_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(x_{21},\ldots,x_{2N})

\mathrm{(ii)}

Theorem 16

If

Birkhoff’s estimate of the dimension function

Problem 8

If

.

Let

be a finite algebra over a field

∎

5.3 Dimension functions of arbitrary finite algebras.

For non-simple algebras we have less spectacular exponential estimates.

Theorem 18

Let

By choosing

.

We finish this section by a short discussion of the order of the automorphism groups of free algebras
F_{n}(\mathfrak{V})
of a locally finite variety
\mathfrak{V}
of algebras over a finite field
\mathbf{F}
. These grow fast. To see how fast, we set
\alpha(n)=|\Aut F_{n}(\mathfrak{V})|
. A rough estimate from below is given by the inequality
\alpha(n)\geq q^{d(n-1)}
, where
q=|\mathbf{F}|
, because any map

x_{1}\mapsto x_{1},\dots,x_{n-1}\mapsto x_{n-1},x_{n}\mapsto x_{n}+a,\mbox{ where }a\in F_{n-1}=F(x_{1},\dots,x_{n-1})

extends to an automorphism of
F_{n}
.

Using Corollary
7
, this argument implies the first claim in the following.

Remark 16
.

If
\mathfrak{V}
is a non-nilpotent locally finite variety then
\alpha(n)
grows at least as fast as the double exponent. If
\mathfrak{V}=\mathrm{var\,}A
, for a finite algebra
A
,
\alpha(n)
cannot grow faster than the double exponent.

The second claim follows from Birkhoff’s estimate in Theorem
6
because the number of all the homomorphisms
F_{n}\to F_{n}
is equal to
|F_{n}|^{n}=q^{nd(n)}
.

The claim of Remark
16
contrasts with the situation where
\mathfrak{V}
is nilpotent because in that case, the growth of
\alpha(n)
is bounded by a simple exponent. The precise formula is given in
[
4
, Section 8]
:

\alpha(n)=|\mathrm{GL}_{n}({\bf F})|q^{n(d(n)-n)}

where, in this case, by Corollary
6
the dimension
d(n)
is a polynomial .

6 Generic properties of finite algebras

In the next subsections, we will single out several properties of finite algebras that hold in
almost all
algebras. Such properties are called
generic
. Besides, at the end of this section, we compare the rates of growth of the numbers of
m
-dimensional finite algebras, as
m\to\infty
, in three classes: all non-associative algebras, all nilpotent algebras, and all solvable algebras.

6.1 Preliminary remarks.

Informally speaking, one says that a property of algebras of a class
\cal C
is generic if
“almost every” object of
\cal C
enjoys this property. The words “almost every” are understood differently, depending on the class
\cal C
. At the same time, it is always true that if several properties are generic then their finite intersection is also generic. Moreover, sometimes, it is easier to prove that some property is generic than to provide examples. For instance, we prove below that each of the following properties of finite non-associative algebras is generic:
A
is simple,
A
is a one-generated algebra,
A
has no non-trivial automorphisms. It follows that a generic finite algebra enjoys all these characteristics.

Below we use the following formal definition of being general.
Let
\varphi(m)
be the number of non-isomorphic
m
-dimensional algebras
over a finite field
\bf F
. Given an abstract property
\cal P
,
we denote by
\varphi_{\cal P}(m)
the number of
m
-dimensional algebras over
\bf F
with property
\cal P
. If

\lim_{m\to\infty}\frac{\varphi_{\cal P}(m)}{\varphi(m)}=1,

then we call
\mathcal{P}

generic
.
Loosely speaking, one also can say that a generic algebra satisfies
\cal P
.

Recall that every
m
-dimensional algebra
A
can be treated as
a tensor of type
(2,1)
on an
m
-dimensional vector space
V
.
The corresponding trilinear function
T
of type
(2,1)
is
defined by
T(f,x,y)=f(xy)
, where
x,y\in V
,

f\in V^{*}
, and
V^{*}
is the dual space of
V
. With respect to
a basis
E=(e_{1},\dots,e_{m})
of
V
, the tensor
T

has
m^{3}
coordinates
c_{ij}^{l}=e^{l}(e_{i}e_{j})
, where
e^{l}
belongs
to the dual basis
E^{*}
. The individual coordinates
c_{ij}^{\ell}
are called
structure constants
. The multiplication of the basis vectors
in
A
is given by the
structural
m^{3}
-tuple

C_{E}
:

C_{E}=(c_{ij}^{l})_{i,j,l=1}^{m}\mbox{ so that }e_{i}e_{j}=\sum_{l=1}^{m}c_{ij}^{l}e_{l},\;\;i,j=1,\dots,m

(19)

Vice versa, these formulae uniquely define a bilinear multiplication
on
V
for an arbitrary
m^{3}
-tuple
C_{E}=(c_{ij}^{l})_{i,j,l=1}^{m}
.

Let
\cal C
be the set of all
m^{3}
-tuples
(c_{ij}^{l})_{i,j,l=1}^{m}
.
We call two elements
(c_{ij}^{l})_{i,j,l=1}^{m}
and

((c^{\prime}_{ij})^{l})_{i,j,l=1}^{m}
of
\cal C

equivalent
if they
are the structural
m^{3}
-tuples of two isomorphic
algebras with respect to some bases
E
and
E^{\prime}
.
Since there is an obvious isomorphism between two algebras
with the same structural
m^{3}
-tuples, this relation is
transitive, and so it is an equivalence, indeed. By the same
reason, an equivalence class is equal to the set of all
structural
m^{3}
-tuples
C_{E}
for a single

m
-dimensional algebra
A
with respect to the all bases
E
of
A
.

Thus, the number of
m^{3}
-tuples in an equivalence class does not exceed the number of bases in an
m
-dimensional vector space, that is, bounded from above by
|\mathrm{GL}(V)|
, and there is a one-to-one
correspondence between the set of isomorphism classes of
m
-dimensional algebras and the set of equivalence classes in
\mathcal{C}
.

We call a subset
\cal D
of the set
\cal C

perfect
if it is the union of several equivalence classes.

Lemma 19
.

Let
\cal D
be a subset of the set
\cal C
. Suppose every algebra of an abstract class
\cal K
has a basis
E
such that the structural
m^{3}
-tuple
C_{E}
belong to
\cal D
. Also suppose that every
m^{3}
-tuple from
\cal D
defines an algebra from
\cal K
. Then the following are true for the number
r
of pairwise nonisomorphic algebras in
\cal K

\mathrm{(i)}

\frac{|{\cal D}|}{|\mathrm{GL}_{m}({\bf F})|}\leq r\leq|\cal D|
;

\mathrm{(ii)}

if the subset
\cal D
is perfect and every algebra in
\cal K

has no non-trivial automorphisms, then
r=\frac{|{\cal D}|}{|\mathrm{GL}_{m}({\bf F})|}
.

Proof.

\mathrm{(i)}
The first statement follows from the correspondence
between the isomorphism classes of
m
-dimensional algebras over
\bf F
and the equivalence classes of structural
m^{3}
-tuples, and the fact that
the size of every equivalence class is bounded by
|\mathrm{GL}_{m}(\bf F)|
.

\mathrm{(ii)}
We note that every equivalence class contained in

\cal D
contains exactly
|\mathrm{GL}_{m}(\bf F)|
elements. Indeed,
otherwise an algebra
A\in\cal K
has to have the same
m^{3}
-tuples
of structure constants with respect to different bases
E
and
E^{\prime}

of
A
. Hence the mapping
E\to E^{\prime}
would extends to an automorphism
of
A
, a contradiction.
∎

For a field
\mathbf{F}
of order
q
, the order of the group
\mathrm{GL}_{m}(\mathbf{F})
equals

q^{m^{2}}\prod_{i=1}^{m}(1-q^{-i}).

Since the product
\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}(1-q^{-i})
converges to a positive constant
d
as fast as a geometric sequence, we have

|\mathrm{GL}_{m}(\mathbf{F})|=q^{m^{2}-c+O(q^{-m})},

(20)

where

c=-\log_{q}d>0\mbox{ and }-\log_{q}\prod_{i=m+1}^{\infty}(1-q^{-i})=O(q^{-m})>0.

(21)

To simplify the notation,
we write
\exp_{q}(N)
in place of
q^{N}
,
N
a real number.

6.2 Generic algebras have no nontrivial automorphisms.

Our first theorem about the generic properties of finite algebras is the following.

Theorem 21
.

Let
\varphi(m)
be the number of pairwise nonisomorphic algebras of dimension
m
over a field
\mathbf{F}
of order
q
and
\psi(m)
the number of those of them which admit a nontrivial automorphism. Using
c
from (
21
), we have

\varphi(m)=\exp_{q}(m^{3}-m^{2}+c-O(q^{-m})).

Also, as
m\to\infty
,
\frac{\psi(m)}{\varphi(m)}
converges to 0 exponentially fast.

Proof.

Every automorphism
\beta
of a finite-dimensional algebra
V
over the field
\mathbf{F}
is a linear map
V\to V
, which turns
V
into a module over the polynomial algebra
\mathbf{F}[t]
. The least number of the generators of this module is called the
rank

r
of the automorphism
\beta
.

First, we estimate the number of structural
m^{3}
-tuples that define algebras in which
\beta
is an automorphism.

Step 1.
We fix a nonsingular linear operator
\beta
of rank
r\leq\frac{4m}{5}
. It follows from
[
4
, Lemma 10]
that there are subspaces
U\subset W
in
V
, such that
s=\dim U\geq\frac{m}{15}
and
V=W+\beta(U)
is the direct sum. In this case, one can choose a basis
E=(e_{1},\ldots,e_{m})
in
V
, such that the first
s
vectors
e_{1},\ldots,e_{s}
form a basis in
U
, while the last
s
vectors
e_{m-s+1}=\beta(e_{1}),\ldots,e_{m}=\beta(e_{s})
form a basis in
\beta(U)
.

If one knows the structure constants with respect to
E
that define
all the products
e_{i}e_{j}
except for
e_{i},e_{j}\in\beta(U)
, then
the latter
ms^{2}
constants are also uniquely determined since

e_{i}e_{j}=\beta(e_{k})\beta(e_{l})=\beta(e_{k}e_{l})

for
e_{k},e_{l}\in U\subset W,
and the right-hand side is known. Hence there
are at most
\exp_{q}(m^{3}-ms^{2})\leq\exp_{q}\left(m^{3}-\frac{m^{3}}{225}\right)
different
m^{3}
-tuples of structure constants defining in the basis
E
those algebras where
\beta
is an automorphism. The same upper bound
holds for the number of non-isomorphic algebras admitting this
automorphism
\beta
.
The number of linear operators
\beta
is obviously bounded by

\exp_{q}(m^{2})
, and so Step 1 gives, by Lemma
19
(i), less than
\exp_{q}\left(m^{3}-\frac{m^{3}}{225}+m^{2}\right)
algebras admitting an automorphism
of rank
r\leq\frac{4m}{5}
.

Step 2
. We consider now a linear operator
\beta
on the space
V
with the condition
r>\frac{4m}{5}
. By
[
4
, Proposition 6]
it follows, that
V
contains an invariant subspace
U
of dimension
s=2r-m
, on which
\beta
acts as a scalar operator, and
U
has a
\beta
-invariant direct complement in
V
. Hence the centralizer
C(\beta)
in
\mathrm{GL}(V)
contains a subgroup, isomorphic to
\mathrm{GL}(U)
. As a result, the index of
C(\beta)
satisfies

[\mathrm{GL}(V):C(\beta)]\leq\exp_{q}(m^{2}-(2r-m)^{2})=\exp_{q}(4r(m-r)).

(22)

Let us estimate the number
N(r)
of different linear operators
\beta
with given rank
r>\frac{4m}{5}
. In the canonical basis,
\beta
is a block-diagonal square matrix with one square block of size
m-s
and one more, the scalar block
\lambda I_{s}
. Such matrices belong to the subspace of dimension
(m-s)^{2}+1=4(m-r)^{2}+1
. Considering equation (
22
), we obtain

N(r)\leq\exp_{q}(4(m-r)^{2}+1)\exp_{q}(4r(m-r))=\exp_{q}(4m(m-r)+1).

(23)

Applying
[
4
, Lemma 10]
again to such
\beta
, we find subspaces
Z
and
W
in
V
, such that
Z\subset W
,
\dim W=\left[\frac{2m+r}{3}\right]
,
V=W\oplus\beta(Z)
, and

l=\dim Z=m-\left[\frac{2m+r}{3}\right]\geq\frac{m-r}{3}.

Again, we choose a basis
E=(e_{1},\ldots,e_{m})
in
V
, such that its first
\ell
vectors form a basis in
Z
, while the last are the images of the first ones under
\beta
, hence forming the basis in
\beta(Z)
. By
[
4
, Lemma 11]
, there is an
r
-dimensional subspace
U^{\prime}\subset V
, on which
\beta
acts as a scalar multiplication by
\lambda
. Its intersection with
Z
is trivial. Set
Y=U^{\prime}\cap W
. By Grassmann’s formula, the dimension of
Y
is no less than
\left[\frac{2m+r}{3}\right]+r-m
. Since
r>\frac{4m}{5}
, we have
\dim Y\geq\frac{11m}{15}-\frac{2}{3}
. We may assume that the basis of
Y
is included in the basis
E
.

If
e_{i}\in Y
and
e_{j}=\beta(e_{k})\in\beta(Z)
, then under the assumption that

\beta
is an automorphism of an algebra on the space
V
, we
have

e_{i}e_{j}=\lambda^{-1}\beta(e_{i})\beta(e_{k})=\lambda^{-1}\beta(e_{i}e_{k}),

and so all such products
e_{i}e_{j}
(and also products
e_{j}e_{i}
,
where
e_{i}\in Y
and
e_{j}\in\beta(Z)
),
are determined by
\beta
and by the products of other pairs of the basis vectors. Therefore to define an algebra with the automorphism

\beta
one needs at most

m^{3}-2m\left(\frac{11m}{15}-\frac{2}{3}\right)\frac{m-r}{3}=\frac{23m^{3}+22m^{2}r+20m^{2}-20mr}{45}

scalars. Hence we obtain at most

N(r)\exp_{q}\frac{23m^{3}+22m^{2}r+20m^{2}-20mr}{45}\leq

\exp_{q}\left(\frac{23m^{3}+22m^{2}r+20m^{2}-20mr}{45}+4(m(m-r)+1)\right)

of non-isomorphic
m
-dimensional algebras admitting an automorphism of rank
r>\frac{4m}{5}
.

The function in the exponent at the right side is linear in
r
, with coefficient of
r
being
22m^{2}-200m
. So it increases with
r
, if
m\geq 10
. It follows that, with
m
sufficiently large, the function has maximum value on the interval
[0,m-3]
when
r=m-3
. In this case the number of non-isomorphic algebras is bounded by

\exp_{q}\frac{23m^{3}+22m^{2}(m-3)+60+540m+45}{45}=\exp_{q}\left(m^{3}-\frac{22m^{2}}{15}+12m+\frac{7}{3}\right).

We need better estimates for
r=m-1
and
r=m-2
. In both cases, by choosing
Z
and
W
, as before, we have
\dim Z=1
, and in
W
there is a
\beta
-invariant subspace
Y
of dimension
t\geq m-3
, on which
\beta
acts as a scalar. Also we choose the basis

E
as above.
If a basis vector
e_{i}
belongs to
Y
and
e_{n}=\beta(e_{1})\in\beta(Z)
, then
e_{i}e_{n}=\beta(e_{i})\beta(e_{1})=\beta(e_{i}e_{1})
. Hence
m
sructure constants for

e_{i}e_{n}
are determined by
\beta
and by the constants

c_{ij}^{k}
where
i,j<n
. The same argument works for
e_{j}e_{i}
.
Therefore for each of the values
r=m-1
and
r=m-2

we obtain at most
\exp_{q}(m^{3}-2m(m-3))
non-isomorphic
algebras admitting the automorphism
\beta
.

The sum
N(m-1)+N(m-2)
is less than
\exp_{q}(8m+2)
by (
23
).
Hence the number of non-isomorphic
algebras admitting automorphism of ranks
m-1
or
m-2
is less
than
\exp_{q}(m^{3}-2m(m-3)+8m+2)
.

Note that on the algebras with nonzero product the map
\lambda\,\mathrm{id}
is an automorphism only if
\lambda=1
. Thus to obtains our cumulative estimate in the case
r\geq\frac{4m}{5}
, we need to sum up our estimates over
r
from 0 to
m-1
only. We will obtain, that the number of algebras, which admit an automorphism of rank
r>\frac{4m}{5}
, is not greater than

\exp_{q}(m^{3}-\frac{7m^{2}}{5}-1)
,
for sufficiently large
m
.

Combining this with the estimate of Case 1, we obtain that there are less than

\exp_{q}\frac{m^{3}-7m^{2}}{5}

(24)

algebras, admitting a nontrivial automorphism.

Step 3
. Now we can complete the proof.
Let
\mathcal{X}
be the set of all structural
m^{3}
-tuples corresponding to the algebras
admitting a non-trivial automorphism. It follows from the last
estimate and Lemma
19
(i) that the perfect set
\cal X

has cardinality less than

\exp_{q}(m^{2})\exp_{q}\left(m^{3}-\frac{7m^{2}}{5}\right)=\exp_{q}\left(m^{3}-\frac{2m^{2}}{5}\right)
.
So the perfect complement
\cal Y
of
\mathcal{X}
in
\cal C
is
of cardinality at least
\exp_{q}(m^{3})-\exp_{q}\left(m^{3}-\frac{2m^{2}}{5}\right)
which is
\exp_{q}(m^{3}-O(\exp_{q}(-m^{2})))
.

By this estimate for
\cal Y
, Lemma
19
(ii), and formula (
20
), the number of non-isomorphic algebras
of dimension
m
having no non-trivial automorphism is

\frac{\Card({\cal Y})}{|\mathrm{GL}_{m}({\bf F})|}=\exp_{q}(m^{3}-m^{2}+c+O(q^{-m})).

Since the number of algebras with non-trivial automorphisms was bounded by
\exp_{q}\left(m^{3}\frac{-7m^{2}}{5}\right)
, the formula for
\varphi(m)
has been proven.

Comparing the estimates from the last two sentences, we obtain the proof of the second
claim of the theorem.

∎

6.3 Generic algebras are simple.

A rather surprising generic property of finite linear algebras is the contents of the following.

Theorem 22
.

A finite generic algebra is simple.

Proof.

Let
V
be vector space with basis
E=(e_{1},\ldots,e_{d},\ldots,e_{m})
and
U=\mathbb{\langle}e_{1},\ldots,e_{d}\mathbb{\rangle}
a
d
-dimensional subspace of
V
. We denote by
\mathcal{T}(U)
the subset of
m^{3}
-tuples
(c_{ij}^{l})_{i,j,l=1}^{m}
from
\cal C
such that
U
is an ideal in the algebra defined by
(c_{ij}^{l})_{i,j,l=1}^{m}
with respect to the basis
E
.

For
1\leq i\leq d
,
1\leq j\leq m
we have
e_{i}e_{j}\in U
. Invoking (
19
), we have
c_{ij}^{k}=0
, as soon as
k>d
. The number of such equations is
dm(m-d)
. Similar condition for the structure constants is given by the products
e_{j}e_{i}
, where
i\leq d
. We must subtract the number the same equations in these two systems,
which are given by
1\leq i\leq j\leq d
. So the total number of such restrictions equals

2dm(m-d)-d^{2}(m-d)=d(m-d)(2m-d)
. So
there are at most

\exp_{q}(m^{3}-d(m-d)(2m-d))=\exp_{q}(m^{3}-d^{3}+3md^{2}-2m^{2}d)

m
-dimensional algebras having a
d
-dimension ideal.

The cubic polynomial
p(x)=-x^{3}+3mx^{2}-2m^{2}x+m^{3}
has the
derivative
p^{\prime}(x)=-3x^{2}+6mx-2m^{2}
with one zero
x_{0}=\left(1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\right)m
on the segment
[1,m-1]
, and

p(x_{0})\leq\frac{4m^{3}}{5}
. The values of
p(x)
at the ends
of this segment are equal to
m^{3}-1+3m-2m^{2}
and

m^{3}-(m-1)^{3}+3m(m-1)2-2m^{2}(m-1)
. Both these values
are less than
m^{3}-2m^{2}+3m
for sufficiently large
m
,
which, consequently, bounds from above the maximum of
p(x)
on
[1,m-1]
, for all sufficiently large values of
m
..

It follows that the number of
m
-dimensional algebras
containing a
d
-dimensional ideal is less than

\exp_{q}(m^{3}-2m^{2}+3m)
for all sufficiently large
m
.
Summing over
d=1,\ldots,m-1
, we see that the number
of nonisomorphic
m
-dimensional algebras containing a proper
ideal is less than
m\exp_{q}(m^{3}-2m^{2}+3m)
. This
number is exponentially negligible in comparison with
the number of all
m
-dimensional algebras given by
Theorem
21
, as required.
∎

6.4 Generic algebras are cyclic.

It is not clear, whether or not a generic finite-dimensional algebra over a finite field has one-dimensional subalgebras. The estimates given below are sufficient for the following weaker claim. We say that an algebra
A
is
cyclic
, if it can be generated by one element.

Theorem 23
.

Generic finite-dimensional algebras over a finite field are cyclic
and contain no proper subalgebras of dimension greater than 1.

Proof.

The proof is split into several cases.

In Case 1, we show that
\exp_{q}(\frac{m^{3}}{2}+m^{2})
bounds from above the number of algebras in which all 1-dimensional subspaces are subalgebras.

In Case 2, we show that
m\exp_{q}(m^{3}-m^{2}-2m)
bounds from above the number of nonisomorphic algebras, having subalgebras of dimensions from 2 to
\frac{m}{2}
.

In Case 3, we show that
m\exp_{q}(m^{3}-\frac{3}{2}m^{2})
bounds from above the number of nonisomorphic algebras, having subalgebras of dimensions
\ell
, where
\frac{m}{2}<\ell\leq m-1
.

Adding all above three number, we obtain, for large enough
m
, less than
q^{m^{3}-m^{2}-\frac{3m}{2}}
nonisomorphic
m
-dimensional algebras. In the remaining cases, there is a one-dimensional subspace
\mathbb{\langle}a\mathbb{\rangle}
, which is not a subalgebra. In this case,
a
generates a subalgebra of dimension
\ell\geq 2
. But such subalgebras do not exist, unless
\ell=m
.

Since by Theorem
21
, the number of all nonisomorphic algebras of dimension
m
is greater than
q^{m^{3}-m^{2}}
, we derive that the share of algebras, which cannot be generated by one element is less than
q^{-m}
, for all large enough
m
. Similarly,
the estimates of Case 2 and Case 3 prove that a generic algebra
has no proper subalgebras of dimension greater than
1
. So the statement of the theorem follows from
the estimates of these three cases.

Now we treat the three cases, one by one.

Case
1. We consider
m
-dimensional algebras, in which all one-dimensional subspaces are subalgebras. Fix a basis
E=(e_{1},\dots,e_{m})
.

For any
e_{i}\in E
,
e_{i}^{2}=c_{ii}^{i}e_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,m
. Now if
1\leq i,j\leq m
, then there is
\sigma_{ij}\in\mathbf{F}
such that
(e_{i}+e_{j})^{2}=\sigma_{ij}(e_{i}+e_{j})
. So

\sigma_{ij}(e_{i}+e_{j})=(e_{i}+e_{j})^{2}=e_{i}^{2}+e_{j}^{2}+e_{i}e_{j}+e_{j}e_{i}=c_{ii}^{i}e_{i}+c_{jj}^{j}e_{j}+e_{i}e_{j}+e_{j}e_{i}

Therefore the product
e_{j}e_{i}
is determined by the product
e_{i}e_{j}
and the
constants
c_{ii}^{i},c_{jj}^{j},\sigma_{ij}
. Hence the multiplication is uniquely
defined by the constants
c_{ij}^{k}
for
i\leq j
and the constants
\sigma_{ij}

with
i<j
. The total number of such constants is

\frac{m^{2}(m+1)}{2}+\frac{m(m-1)}{2}=\frac{m^{3}}{2}+\frac{m^{2}-m}{2}.

So the number of pairwise non-isomorphic algebras in Case 1
is less than

\exp_{q}\left(\frac{m^{3}}{2}+m^{2}\right)
, as required.

Case
2. Now we estimate, for each
2\leq\ell\leq\frac{m}{2}
, the number of nonisomorphic algebras
A
on a vector space
V
, having an
\ell
-dimensional subalgebra. For this goal, we fix a basis
E=(e_{1},\dots,e_{m})
in
V
and
estimate the cardinality of the set of structural
m^{3}
-tuples
\cal D
, such that
the algebra
A
given in the basis
E
by a structural
m^{3}
-tuple
(c_{ij}^{k})_{i,j,k=1}^{m}\in\cal D
, has an
\ell
-dimensional subalgebra.
Then
\cal D
is a perfect set since it does not depend on the
choice of bases.

Let
U
be an
\ell
-dimensional subspace of
V
. Choose an auxiliary basis
F=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{m})
of
V
such that
(f_{1},\ldots,f_{\ell})
is a basis of
U
. Denote by
{\cal D}_{F}

the set of
m^{3}
-tuples
C^{\prime}=\{(c^{\prime})_{ij}^{k}\}
such that the algebra
defined by
C^{\prime}
with respect to
F
contains the linear space
U
as a subalgebra.

A structural
m^{3}
-tuple
\{(c^{\prime})_{ij}^{k}\}
belongs to
{\cal D}_{F}
if and only if
it satisfies the conditions
(c^{\prime})_{ij}^{k}=0
for
i,j\leq\ell
and
k>\ell
. Altogether, we have
\ell^{2}(m-\ell)
such equalities. It follows that every
m^{3}
-tuple from the set
{\cal D}_{F}
is defined by
m^{3}-\ell^{2}(m-\ell)

constants, and so the cardinality of
{\cal D}_{F}
is at most

\exp_{q}(m^{3}-\ell^{2}(m-\ell)).

Rewriting every
m^{3}
-tuple from
{\cal D}_{F}

in the basis
E
, we obtain a set
{\cal D}_{U}
of
m^{3}
-tuples
C=\{c_{ij}^{k}\}_{i,j,k=1}^{m}
such that the space
U
becomes a subalgebra
in the algebra
A
given in the basis
E
by
C
. Obviously,
\cal D

is the union of the sets
{\cal D}_{U}
over all
\ell
-dimensional
subspaces
U
of
V
.

The summation over all
\ell
-dimensional subspaces, whose number is less than
q^{m\ell}
, provides us with less than
\exp_{q}(m^{3}-\ell^{2}(m-\ell)+m\ell)
elements in

{\cal D}_{U}
. Let
\cal D^{\prime}
(resp.
\cal D^{\prime\prime}
) be the subsets of
\cal D

defining algebras with (resp, without) non-trivial automorphisms.

The number of algebras defined by the
m^{3}
-tuples from
\cal D^{\prime}

is less than
\exp_{q}\left(m^{3}-\frac{7m^{2}}{5}\right)
for sufficiently large
m
by formula
(
24
). By Lemma
19
(ii) and formula (
20
), the number of algebras defined by
\cal D^{\prime\prime}
is equal to

\frac{|{\cal D}^{\prime\prime}|}{|\mathrm{GL}(V)|}\leq\frac{|{\cal D}|}{|\mathrm{GL}(V)|}\leq\exp_{q}(m^{3}-\ell^{2}(m-\ell)+m\ell-m^{2}+m)

for large enough
m
. Thus, for large enough
m
, there are less than

\exp_{q}\left(m^{3}-\frac{7m^{2}}{5}\right)+\exp_{q}(m^{3}-\ell^{2}(m-\ell)+m\ell-m^{2}+m)<\exp_{q}(m^{3}-m^{2}-2m)

non-isomorphic algebras having an
\ell
-dimension subalgebra, because
\ell\in\left[2,\frac{m}{2}\right]
. The summation over
\ell
from this segment gives us the upper bound

m\exp_{q}(m^{3}-m^{2}-2m)
, as desired.

Case
3. We estimate the number of
m
-dimensional algebras with subalgebras of dimension
\ell
, where
m>\ell>\frac{m}{2}
. Such an
m
-dimensional algebra
A
can be obtained from an
\ell
-dimensional subalgebra

by adding
m-\ell

c_{ij}^{k}
, except those where

. Altogether, it is sufficient to have
m(m^{2}-\ell^{2})

By Theorem
21
, there exists less than
\exp_{q}(\ell^{3}-\ell^{2}+const)

\ell
-dimensional algebras. It follows, that there exists at most
\exp_{q}(m(m^{2}-\ell^{2})+\ell^{3}-\ell^{2}+const)
nonisomorphic algebras, having
\ell
-dimensional subalgebra. This value is less than
\exp_{q}(m^{3}-\frac{3}{2}m^{2})
for all sufficiently large

Now the proof is complete.

6.5 Quasi-identities of generic algebras.

Given an absolute free algebra
{\cal F}={\cal F}(x_{1},x_{2},\dots)
over a field
\bf F
and nonassociative polynomials

u_{1}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}),\ldots,u_{m}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}),v(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in{\cal F},

the formula

\displaystyle(\forall x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})u_{1}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=0\&\ldots\&u_{m}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=0

\displaystyle\longrightarrow v(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=0

(25)

is called a
quasi-identity
. We say that an algebra
A
over
\mathbf{F}
satisfies (
6.5
) if for any
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\in A
the equalities
u_{1}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})=0,\ldots,u_{m}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})=0
in
A
imply
v(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})=0
.

Note that in (
6.5
), it is allowed that
m=0
, that is the preamble of the statement can be empty. In that case, (
6.5
) is simply an identical relation
v(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=0
. So if an algebra
B
satisfies all the quasi-identities of an algebra
A
then it also satisfies all the identities of
A
. One denotes by
\mathrm{qvar}A
the
quasi-variety generated by
A
, that is, the class of all algebras over
\mathbf{F}
satisfying all quasi-identities satisfied by
A
. One has
\mathrm{qvar}A\subset\mathrm{var\,}A
.

A very simple example of a quasi-identity:
x^{2}=x\longrightarrow x=0
is satisfied in any algebra without nonzero idempotents.

It follows from the definition, that the Cartesian products and subalgebras of algebras satisfying some set of quasi-identities also satisfy them. So by Birkhoff’s Theorem
7
, free algebras of
\mathrm{var\,}A
are in
\mathrm{qvar}A

for any algebra
A
. For a finite algebra
A
, the structure of the quasi-variety
\mathrm{qvar}A
is very simple: any
C\in\mathrm{qvar}A
, not necessarily finite, is a subalgebra of a Cartesian power of
A
(see
[
26
, Corollary 9 in §11]
). So these two operations are sufficient to obtain any algebra of
\mathrm{var\,}A
, if

\mathrm{var\,}A=\mathrm{qvar}A
for a finite algebra
A
; no forming of the homomorphic images
is needed. Surprisingly, we have

Theorem 24
.

The equality
\mathrm{var\,}A=\mathrm{qvar}A
is a generic property of
algebras
A
of dimension
m
over a finite field
\mathbf{F}
.

Proof.

By Theorem
23
, a finite generic algebra
A
has no proper
subalgebras except for one-dimensional ones, and
A
is simple by Theorem
22
.
Therefore
A
is an inherently semisimple algebra. By Theorem
11
,
any finite algebra
B
from the variety
\mathrm{var\,}A
is a direct product of
algebras isomorphic to subalgebras of
A
, whence
B\in\mathrm{qvar}A
. Since
the variety
\mathrm{var\,}A
is locally finite, it follows that every algebra
from
\mathrm{var\,}A
also satisfies all the quasi-identities of
A
.
Thus, we obtain the inclusion
\mathrm{var\,}A\subset\mathrm{qvar}A
. Since the opposite
inclusion is trivial, the theorem is proved. ∎

6.6 The numbers of nilpotent and solvable algebras.

Recall that
\varphi(m)
denotes the number of non-isomorphic
m
-dimensional algebras
over a field
\bf F
of order
q
. Let
\nu(m)
(let
\sigma(m)
) be the mumber
of nilpotent (resp., of solvable) algebras among them. It follows from the results
below that

\lim_{m\to\infty}\frac{\log\nu(m)}{\log\varphi(m)}=\frac{1}{3}\mbox{ \ and\ }\lim_{m\to\infty}\frac{\log\sigma(m)}{\log\varphi(m)}=\frac{2}{3}.

Proposition 20
.

The number
\nu(m)
of nilpotent algebras of dimension
m
equals
\exp_{q}\left(\frac{m^{3}}{3}+O(m^{2})\right)
.

Proof.

In a nilpotent algebra
A
of dimesion
m
, there is a series of ideals
\{0\}=I_{0}\subset I_{1}\subset\ldots\subset I_{m}=A
with factors of dimension 1 and conditions
AI_{j}\subset I_{j-1}
and
I_{j}A\subset I_{j-1}
for
j=1,\ldots,m
. And, conversely, an algebra
A
with such series is nilpotent (see Section
2.1
).

As a result,
A
is an algebra with a basis
E=\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{m}\}
such that for any
j=1,\ldots,m
the products
e_{j}e_{\ell}
and
e_{l}e_{j}
are linear combinations of basis vectors with indexes
<j
. We denote by
\mu(n)
the number of collections of structure constants, under which these conditions hold with respect to the basis
E
.

For the algebra
A/I_{1}
we have a basis
\{e_{2}+I_{1},\ldots,e_{m}+I_{1}\}
with analogous conditions, which yields
\mu(m-1)
structural
m^{3}
-tuples with respect to this basis, that is, where the structure constants
c_{ij}^{k}
without indexes equal 1. To obtain all the constants for
A
, we can complement them by any constants of the form
c_{ij}^{1}
, where
i,j>1
(
c_{1j}^{\ell}
and
c_{i1}^{\ell}
are zero). These additional constants can be chosen in
\exp_{q}((m-1)^{2})
ways. It follows that
\mu(m)=\exp_{q}((m-1)^{2})\mu(m-1)
, hence by induction
\mu(m)=\exp_{q}(\lambda(m))
, where
\lambda(m)=\frac{(m-1)m(2m-1)}{6}
. This value is
\frac{m^{3}}{3}+O(m^{2})
. By Lemma
19
(i) and the equality (
20
),
\nu(m)
can also be written as
\exp_{q}\left(\frac{m^{3}}{3}+O(m^{2})\right)
.

∎

Remark 17
.

In the classical varieties, the number of non-isomorphic nilpotent algebras of dimension
m
also grows fast, whereas, the simple algebras of dimension
m
are scarce. But in the case of arbitrary linear algebras, the inspection of Theorem
22
and Proposition
20
reveals that, on the contrary, the number of simple algebras, grows approximately as the cube of the number of nilpotent algebras!

Proposition 21
.

The number
\sigma(m)
of solvable algebras of dimension
m
is equals to
\exp_{q}\left(\frac{2m^{3}}{3}+O(m^{2})\right)
.

Proof.

In a solvable algebra
A
of dimension
m
, there is a composition series of subideals
\{0\}=I_{0}\subset I_{1}\subset\ldots\subset I_{m}=A
with all factors of dimension 1 and conditions
I_{j}^{2}\subset I_{j-1}
for
j=1,\ldots,m
. And, conversely, an algebra
A
with such series is solvable.

As a result,
A
is an algebra, having a basis
E=\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{m}\}
such that, for any
j=1,\ldots,m
,
\ell\leq j
, the products
e_{j}e_{\ell}
and
e_{l}e_{j}
are linear combinations of basis vectors with indexes
<j
. We denote by
\rho(n)
the number of structural
m^{3}
-tuples, satisfying these conditions, with respect to the basis
E
.

For the basis
E^{\prime}=(e_{1},\ldots,e_{m-1})
of
I_{m-1}
, there are
\rho(m-1)
structural
m^{3}
-tuples, with the given property. Additional constants
c_{mj}^{\ell}
and
c_{im}^{\ell}
must satisfy just the conditions
c_{mj}^{m}=0
and
c_{im}^{m}=0
. Hence we obtain
\exp_{q}(2m^{2}-3m+1)
possibilities for the choices of additional constants, that is,
\rho(m)=\exp_{q}(2m^{2}-3m+1)\rho(m-1)
In this case, by induction,
\rho(m)=\exp_{q}\left(\frac{2m^{3}}{3}+O(m^{2})\right)
.

By Lemma
19
(i) and the equality (
20
),
\sigma(m)
can also be written as

\exp_{q}\left(\frac{2m^{3}}{3}+O(m^{2})\right)
.

∎

As a result, the number of nonisomorphic solvable algebras of dimension
n
is approximately equal to the square of the number of nonisomorphic nilpotent algebras of this dimension.
