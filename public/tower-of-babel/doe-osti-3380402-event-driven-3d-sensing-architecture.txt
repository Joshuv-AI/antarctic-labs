Event-Driven 3D Sensing Architecture
Adaptive imaging for persistent tracking and three-dimensional reconstruction of dynamic scenes

Value Proposition
Some events happen too quickly to capture twice.

Whether tracking satellites, monitoring aircraft, observing autonomous systems, or recording
high speed industrial processes, imaging systems often have only one opportunity to collect the
right information. If a camera is focused on the wrong distance, uses the wrong exposure, or
cannot keep pace with rapid motion, important details may be lost forever.

Los Alamos National Laboratory has developed an event-driven imaging architecture that
preserves more information during data collection than a conventional camera. Instead of
committing to a single image, the system captures data that can later be used to digitally adjust
focus and exposure while reconstructing the scene in three dimensions. The result is a more
flexible approach for imaging dynamic environments where conventional cameras can struggle.

Overview
Most cameras work by taking a rapid series of photographs. Every time an image is captured, the
camera decides how long to collect light, what part of the scene should be in focus, and how
much of the scene appears sharp. Those decisions become part of the finished image and cannot
be changed later.

This approach works well when the scene changes slowly. It becomes much more difficult when
objects move quickly or lighting changes unexpectedly. A long exposure can blur motion. A
short exposure may not collect enough light. Focusing on one object can leave another out of
focus. Engineers must make these decisions before collecting data because a conventional
camera cannot recover information that was never recorded.

LANL approached the problem differently. Rather than recording complete images at fixed
intervals, the architecture captures only meaningful changes within a scene while also preserving
information about the direction from which light arrives. Recording this additional information
allows software to reconstruct different versions of the same scene after it has been captured,
including images with different focus and exposure settings. The same data can also be used to
recreate three-dimensional views of dynamic objects.

Technology Description
The architecture combines two complementary imaging methods.

The first records only changes in brightness instead of repeatedly capturing complete images.
Imagine watching a busy intersection and writing down only what changes instead of describing
the entire scene every second. A car turns. A traffic light changes. A pedestrian crosses the street.
Everything else remains the same. Recording only those changes produces far less data while
preserving precisely when each change occurred. This approach is called event-based imaging.

The second method records not only where light lands on the sensor but also the direction from
which it arrives. That extra information allows software to digitally change what appears in focus
after the data has already been collected. It can also adjust the apparent depth of field without
taking another image. This technique is known as light-field imaging.

LANL's innovation combines these two approaches into a single imaging architecture. The
event-based sensor provides precise timing information while the light-field measurements
preserve additional spatial information about the scene. Together, they allow users to generate
multiple image reconstructions from the same dataset, each optimized for a different focus plane
or exposure time. The architecture also supports three-dimensional reconstruction of dynamic
scenes from the same captured data.

Because the sensor records only meaningful changes, the resulting data stream is naturally
smaller than that produced by conventional frame-based cameras operating at similar speeds.
Event-based sensors also provide high dynamic range and low power operation, making the
architecture well suited for applications where rapid scene changes, efficient processing, and low
latency are essential.

Advantages
  • Preserves more scene information by enabling digital adjustment of focus and exposure
 after data collection.
  • Supports three-dimensional reconstruction and characterization from a single imaging
 architecture.
  • Captures rapidly changing scenes with microsecond-scale temporal resolution.
  • Reduces motion blur and aliasing during high speed imaging.
  • Produces sparse data streams that reduce memory, bandwidth, and computational
 requirements.
  • Operates across a high dynamic range for imaging under challenging lighting conditions.
  • Supports low power operation for edge computing, autonomous systems, and distributed
 sensing applications.

Overview
Modern defense sensing systems struggle to maintain persistent custody of fast moving and maneuvering
targets. As target dynamics increase, sensing modalities collect fewer informative measurements, forcing
complex sensor fusion and AI pipelines to reconstruct continuous trajectories. This creates latency,
uncertainty, and limits real time target characterization.

Los Alamos has developed a fundamentally different sensing architecture that addresses this challenge
at the sensing layer. By combining event-driven neuromorphic sensing with light field imaging, the system
continuously captures informative scene dynamics while directly encoding spatial information for native
real-time 3D reconstruction and target characterization in post-processing.

Technology Description
The architecture combines neuromorphic event-based sensing with light field optics to produce a sparse
stream of temporally precise measurements that directly encode both spatial and temporal information.
Unlike conventional frame-based systems, the architecture continuously samples scene dynamics at
microsecond timescales, enabling persistent target tracking and native real time 3D reconstruction while
dramatically reducing bandwidth and computational requirements.

Unlike conventional cameras, this imager records brightness changes asynchronously across its pixel
array, producing sparse, low-bandwidth data that naturally scales to dynamic environments. During post-
processing, users can select or synthesize virtual frames with different blur profiles, focus planes, or
exposure lengths from the same underlying dataset.

The architecture supports high dynamic range (90–120 dB), low power usage, and minimal aliasing at
high frame rates—making it well suited for robotic vision, real-time process monitoring, and motion-
intensive environments.

Advantages
 • Captures targets dynamics with microsecond-level temporal resolution and high dynamic range
 during high speed motion.

 • Enables native real-time 3D data capture for target characterization in post-processing.
 • Decreases reliance on complex downstream sensor fusion and AI pipelines for target localization
 and trajectory estimation.
 • Enables post-processed adjustment of focus and exposure—previously fixed at capture time.
 • Reduces motion blur and aliasing in high-speed applications
 • Provides low-bandwidth, low-latency visual data ideal for edge computing, robotics, defense,
 aerospace, and autonomous sensing applications.
 • Compatible with neuromorphic and event-driven computing architectures.

Market Application
This sensing architecture addresses mission critical challenges in persistent tracking and target
characterization where conventional imaging systems struggle to maintain continuous visibility of fast
moving or maneuvering objects, specially considering dynamic, low-latency environments, where
traditional imaging is inadequate.

Potential uses include:
 • Space-domain awareness for persistent tracking and characterization of satellites, debris, and
 non-cooperative objects.
 • Missile warning and defense through continuous tracking of high-speed and maneuvering threats.
 • Airborne intelligence, surveillance, and reconnaissance requiring low-latency situational
 awareness.
 • Robotic perception systems that require fast, adaptable vision in changing conditions
 • Augmented and virtual reality devices seeking low-latency visual capture with depth perception
 • High-speed industrial monitoring or structural dynamics testing

Next Steps
Los Alamos National Laboratory is advancing this sensing architecture toward mission relevant
demonstrations and integration with defense and aerospace partners, while seeking collaborators to
evaluate the technology, optimize performance, and accelerate hardware miniaturization, embedded
processing, and technology transition.

Current development priorities include:
 • Demonstrate persistent target tracking and native 3D characterization in representative
 operational environments.
 • Miniaturize the sensing hardware into a compact, low power architecture suitable for airborne,
 space, and autonomous platforms.
 • Integrate the sensing architecture with electro optical and multi sensor tracking systems.
 • Develop real time embedded processing pipelines for deployment on edge computing platforms.
 • Conduct pilot evaluations with government agencies and defense prime contractors to support
 technology transition and commercialization.
