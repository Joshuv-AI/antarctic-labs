PD-YOLO: a high-precision detection network for fine-grained lung cancer and multi-scale pulmonary nodules in CT images
Authors: Conghan Yang, Chen Zhang, Kuisong Wang, Baiyang Wu, Ziwei Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Timely and accurate Computed Tomography (CT) screening is crucial for the early clinical treatment of lung cancer and preventing the progression of malignant pulmonary nodules. However, owing to the large scale variations and blurred boundaries of lesions, existing deep learning models struggle to strike an optimal balance between computational costs, receptive field size, and the precise reconstruction of fine details. To address these challenges, this paper integrates three complementary components—A2C2f_DFFN, SPPF_LSKA, and DySample—into the YOLOv12n framework to construct PD-YOLO, a high-precision fine-grained lung cancer detection model. The main contribution lies in the synergistic optimization of these modules for CT‑based pulmonary lesion detection, rather than the theoretical novelty of any single component. Specifically, we introduce the A2C2f_DFFN module, which utilizes a Dynamic Feed-Forward Network to significantly enhance the non-linear feature representation of low-contrast early lesions. Furthermore, the SPPF_LSKA module is designed by combining Spatial Pyramid Pooling with Large Separable Kernel Attention, which expands the effective receptive field with extremely low computational overhead to handle large-scale massive tumor consolidations typical of advanced lung cancer. Additionally, an ultra-lightweight dynamic upsampling module (DySample) is employed to adaptively reconstruct high-resolution features, effectively mitigating the loss of edge textures and minor infiltrates during feature propagation. Extensive experiments on a chest CT dataset containing 4200 images demonstrate that PD-YOLO achieves a mean Average Precision (mAP @0.5) of 97.3%, outperforming state-of-the-art detectors including YOLOv12s and YOLOv12n. The proposed model successfully optimizes the trade-off between missed detections and false alarms, exhibiting highly robust overall object detection capabilities for complex clinical applications, particularly in the automated screening and localization of malignant pulmonary lesions.

Introduction

Lung cancer is one of the leading causes of morbidity and mortality worldwide. Timely and accurate screening using high-resolution thoracic Computed Tomography (CT) is crucial for early intervention. In recent years, deep learning-powered Computer-Aided Diagnosis (CAD) technologies1–3 have emerged as vital tools to automate the detection of malignant pulmonary lesions, improving diagnostic efficiency and reducing missed diagnoses.

To achieve efficient automated screening, extensive explorations have been conducted. Early works like CheXNet4 achieved radiologist-level classification but lacked precise spatial localization. Subsequent two-stage detectors5 improved localization accuracy but suffered from complex structures and high latency, limiting their use in rapid, large-scale screening. Consequently, lightweight single-stage detectors based on the YOLO architecture have become the mainstream choice for clinical deployment. For example, Zeng et al.6 proposed a YOLOv8-based model using efficient modulation and dynamic upsampling for lung cancer detection in CT images. Li et al.7 proposed an enhanced YOLOv10 architecture incorporating a multi-dimensional receptive field extractor and background attention, achieving high sensitivity and specificity on LIDC-IDRI and LUNA16. Xu et al.8 introduced a cognitive reasoning framework that emulates a radiologist’s hierarchical workflow, combining multi-scale convolution with hypergraph inference for improved interpretability. Concurrently, DEtection TRansformer (DETR) architectures and their real-time variants (e.g., RT-DETR) have gained significant attention9. By leveraging global self-attention and eliminating Non-Maximum Suppression (NMS), DETRs achieve strong performance in general object detection. For lung nodule detection, Chen et al.10 proposed an enhanced RT-DETR with multi-scale feature fusion and lightweight design, while Zhou et al.11 introduced cross-scale feature fusion and re-weighting to improve accuracy. Furthermore, to handle complex pulmonary environments where lesions overlap with ribs and cardiac shadows, researchers have introduced various feature enhancement mechanisms—such as multi-scale feature pyramids12 and spatial/channel attention modules13,14—to suppress background noise and capture contextual dependencies. Song et al.15 proposed an improved YOLOv11 framework integrating a spatial squeeze-and-excitation module and an aspect-ratio-penalty IoU loss to enhance small pulmonary nodule representation. Wang et al.16 introduced a multi-scale feature fusion module and a non-linear feature learning network into YOLOv8 to improve detection across different nodule scales. Building on these directions, recent studies have explored hierarchical deep feature fusion strategies for fine-grained discriminative capability in medical imaging17. Concurrently, systematic investigations show that integrating both channel and spatial attention (e.g., SE and CBAM) into CNN backbones consistently improves feature localization and diagnostic robustness across modalities18.

Despite these advancements, existing models face significant limitations when applied to complex clinical CT images with large scale variations and faint, blurred lesion boundaries. These challenges manifest in two aspects. First, a dilemma exists between architectural efficiency and global feature modeling: traditional CNNs lack sufficient effective receptive fields (ERF), while Transformer-based models incur heavy computational burdens and memory footprints9, restricting their deployment on resource-constrained edge devices in hospital CAD systems. Moreover, global attention can be insensitive to the highly irregular, blurred boundaries of early-stage, low-contrast pulmonary infiltrates. Second, during feature decoding and upsampling, existing networks (including YOLOv1219) often rely on simple nearest-neighbor interpolation, which irreversibly loses spatial edges and textural details of early-stage malignant nodules (e.g., faint ground-glass opacities), leading to missed detections or severe boundary localization errors.

To address these shortcomings—balancing computational cost, receptive field, and detail reconstruction—this paper proposes PD-YOLO, a high-precision, fine-grained lung cancer detection model based on an improved YOLOv12n architecture. Related work

Deep learning in lung cancer detection

Historically, the diagnosis of Lung Cancer has heavily relied on the subjective assessment of chest X-rays and CT scans by experienced radiologists. The advent of deep learning has fundamentally transformed this paradigm. Early applications primarily focused on image-level classification20–22. For instance, Rajpurkar et al.4 developed CheXNet, a 121-layer convolutional neural network that demonstrated radiologist-level performance in detecting Lung Cancer from a massive dataset. However, classification models lack the ability to delineate the exact boundaries of pulmonary lesions. To address this, object detection frameworks were introduced into medical imaging. Sirazitdinov et al.5 utilized a two-stage detection ensemble comprising RetinaNet and Mask R-CNN, achieving precise localization of Lung Cancer consolidations. While two-stage detectors offer high accuracy, their reliance on region proposal networks introduces significant computational overhead, limiting their deployment in real-time or resource-constrained clinical environments. Consequently, single-stage detectors, particularly the YOLO series, have gained traction due to their optimal balance of speed and accuracy. Sharma et al.23 successfully validated the efficacy of lightweight YOLO architectures for rapid Lung Cancer screening, marking a shift towards real-time automated diagnosis.

Attention mechanisms and receptive field expansion

Accurately detecting such diverse targets requires the network to possess a sufficiently large Effective Receptive Field (ERF) to capture global contextual information. However, traditional convolutional neural networks (CNNs) are inherently limited by their local kernel sizes, making it difficult to establish comprehensive global anatomical context. To overcome these limitations and explicitly model long-range dependencies, building upon the foundational self-attention mechanism24 and earlier localized attention modules like CBAM25, Chen et al.9 introduced Transformer architectures into medical image analysis.

Despite their powerful global modeling capability, Transformer-based models suffer from a massive number of parameters and prohibitively high computational costs (e.g., FLOPs and inference latency), which severely hinders their practical deployment in real-time or resource-constrained clinical screening scenarios. To mitigate these limitations, recent works have explored specialized architectures that integrate Transformer modules with memory mechanisms to enforce anatomical consistency without dramatically increasing computational burdens26. Moreover, controlled comparative benchmarks on cardiac ultrasound have revealed that while plain U-Net achieves competitive performance under optimal preprocessing conditions, Attention U-Net offers modest improvements on small or low-contrast regions, and TransUNet demonstrates the strongest generalization on challenging frames due to its global spatial context modeling capacity27. These findings reinforce the importance of global receptive field expansion for handling low-contrast targets—a principle that underpins our design of the SPPF_LSKA module.

To expand the receptive field without the prohibitive costs of Transformers, large kernel convolutions have been re-evaluated. Lau et al.28 proposed Large Separable Kernel Attention, which decomposes massive 2D convolutional kernels into cascaded 1D depth-wise separable convolutions. This design achieves an ERF comparable to Transformers while drastically reducing parameters and floating-point operations (FLOPs). Existing medical detectors often rely on Feature Pyramid Networks29 or standard Spatial Pyramid Pooling (SPP) for multi-scale feature extraction, but fixed-size pooling kernels struggle with highly irregular tumor infiltrations. Integrating LSKA into the spatial pooling process presents a highly efficient solution to capture the broad structural context of lung lesions under constrained computational budgets.

Feature upsampling and high-resolution reconstruction

In the neck architectures of object detectors, high-quality feature upsampling is critical for fusing deep semantic information with shallow spatial details. Traditional networks, including the recent YOLOv1219, predominantly rely on Nearest-Neighbor (NN) or bilinear interpolation for upsampling. These static, rule-based methods are agnostic to the semantic content of the image, frequently leading to checkerboard artifacts and the irreversible loss of fine-grained boundary textures. In the context of Lung Cancer detection, this flaw causes the network to miss early-stage, low-contrast malignant shadows.

While dynamic convolutions30 and content-aware reassembly features (CARAFE)31 have been proposed to improve upsampling quality, they introduce substantial computational latency. Recently, Liu et al.32 proposed DySample, an ultra-lightweight dynamic upsampler. Bypassing heavy dynamic convolutions, DySample learns point-sampling offsets directly from the input features, dynamically reconstructing high-resolution maps. This point-based learning mechanism is highly adept at preserving sharp boundaries and local textures, making it exceptionally well-suited for restoring the faint features of minor pulmonary infiltrates during the feature fusion stage.

The evolution of YOLO and feature representation

The YOLO series has continuously evolved to push the boundaries of real-time object detection33, progressing from the core structural improvements in earlier versions like YOLOv3 and YOLOv434,35 to increasingly handling complex uncertainty in medical image analyses36,37. The latest iteration, YOLOv12 by Tian et al.19, shifts from pure convolutional scaling to an attention-centric design, utilizing the A2C2f module to unlock superior feature modeling capabilities. However, CT images are characterized by high noise levels, complex background interference, and subtle intra-class variations. When applied directly to Lung Cancer detection, the standard feed-forward networks within the YOLOv12 attention blocks may lack the necessary non-linear mapping capacity to distinguish faint malignant textures from healthy lung parenchyma. Augmenting these attention blocks with dynamic feed-forward networks (DFFN) provides a promising pathway to enhance the localized feature representation of low-contrast lesions without compromising the model’s inherent efficiency.

Proposed methodology

Overall architecture

The proposed network inherits the efficient real-time detection paradigm of the YOLO series while fundamentally restructuring its feature extraction and fusion pathways to suit medical imaging characteristics. Overall architecture of the proposed PD-YOLO model. To clearly illustrate our core contributions, the proposed novel modules (i.e., A2C2f_DFFN, SPPF_LSKA, and DySample) are highlighted with distinct colored shading, whereas the standard baseline components are depicted in neutral colors.

Given an input axial thoracic CT slice, the modified Backbone extracts multi-scale semantic features. Specifically, we completely replace the standard A2C2f attention blocks of the baseline YOLOv12n with the proposed A2C2f_DFFN modules to enhance the non-linear representation of localized, low-contrast malignant regions. Alongside these improvements, the architecture retains the highly efficient C3k and C3k2 modules (Cross Stage Partial bottlenecks with customized kernels) inherited from the YOLO baseline. These modules are strategically distributed across the backbone and neck to perform fundamental spatial feature extraction while minimizing gradient redundancy and computational overhead. At the deepest layer of the Backbone, the SPPF_LSKA module is introduced to capture rich global contextual information and expand the Effective Receptive Field for large lung consolidations. In the Neck network, which fuses deep semantic features with shallow spatial details, we discard the traditional nearest-neighbor interpolation. Instead, we employ the DySample module to dynamically reconstruct high-resolution feature maps, effectively preserving the boundary details of early-stage Lung Cancer infiltrates. Finally, the fused features are fed into the Detection Head to output the predicted bounding boxes and class probabilities.

A2C2f_DFFN

In thoracic CT scans, early-stage Lung Cancer often manifests as faint, low-contrast ground-glass opacities that heavily blend with surrounding healthy lung parenchyma. The static feed-forward networks in standard YOLOv12n attention blocks lack the dynamic adaptability required to isolate these subtle textural differences. To address this limitation, we propose the A2C2f_DFFN module, which integrates a Dynamic Feed-Forward Network (DFFN) to enhance the non-linear representation of localized malignant regions.

The detailed architecture of the proposed A2C2f_DFFN module is illustrated in Fig. 2. The module is designed with a residual learning framework to ensure smooth gradient flow while extracting highly non-linear semantic features. As shown in the figure, the input feature map first bifurcates into two pathways. The main pathway undergoes an initial Convolutional layer to project the channel dimensions and capture preliminary spatial features. This is followed by a core sequential block consisting of multiple Ablock-DFFN units. In our specific backbone configuration, this sequence is repeated 4 times (× 4) to deeply mine the hierarchical representations of the malignant infiltrations. Unlike conventional feed-forward layers with fixed weights, the DFFN introduces a data-dependent dynamic gating mechanism. While the foundational concept of data-dependent computational routing draws inspiration from recent broader advancements in Dynamic Neural Networks38, our specific DFFN architecture is uniquely tailored for this clinical task. It adaptively adjusts its non-linear transformations to emphasize disease-specific textural patterns while effectively suppressing background noise in CT images.Fig. 2Detailed internal architecture of the Dynamic Feed-Forward Network (DFFN) within the Ablock-DFFN module.

SPPF_LSKA module

Lung Cancer lesions exhibit extreme scale diversity in clinical imaging. While early-stage infiltrates may appear as localized, minor ground-glass opacities, advanced Lung Cancer often presents as massive lung consolidations that can span an entire pulmonary lobe. Accurately delineating such extensive targets requires the detection network to possess a sufficiently large Effective Receptive Field. The standard Spatial Pyramid Pooling Fast (SPPF) module extracts multi-scale features using cascaded max-pooling layers, but its localized pooling operations lack adaptive spatial attention, making it challenging to differentiate large irregular consolidations from surrounding anatomical structures like the heart or diaphragm.

To overcome this limitation without incurring the prohibitive computational cost of Vision Transformers, we propose the SPPF_LSKA module. As illustrated in Fig. 4, this module elegantly combines multi-scale spatial pooling with Large Separable Kernel Attention (LSKA)20 to capture broad structural contexts.Fig. 4SPPF_LSKA module.

The LSKA mechanism achieves the effective receptive field of a massive k × k 2D convolutional kernel by decomposing it into cascaded 1D depth-wise and depth-wise dilated convolutions based on a configured maximum dilation rate d. The mathematical processing sequence of the LSKA module is precisely defined as follows:

First, to capture local spatial context, the module applies cascaded horizontal and vertical 1D depth-wise convolutions (DW-Conv). The kernel sizes for these operations are mathematically bounded by the dilation rate to avoid grid artifacts:18\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Z_{1} = {\text{DW - Conv}}_{1 \times (2d - 1)} (F_{fused} )$$\end{document}19\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Z_{2} = {\text{DW - Conv}}_{(2d - 1) \times 1} (Z_{1} )$$\end{document}

Second, to explicitly capture long-range structural dependencies without exploding the parameter count, it applies cascaded horizontal and vertical 1D depth-wise dilated convolutions (DW-D-Conv). The spatial extent of these kernels is determined by the ratio of the target large kernel size k to the dilation rate d:20\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Z_{3} = {\text{DW - D - Conv}}_{{1 \times \left\lfloor {k/d} \right\rfloor }} (Z_{2} )$$\end{document}21\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Z_{4} = {\text{DW - D - Conv}}_{{\left\lfloor {k/d} \right\rfloor \times 1}} (Z_{2} )$$\end{document}

Next, a standard 1 × 1 convolution is applied to map the aggregated spatial features into an attention weight matrix A across the channel dimension:22\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$A = {\mathrm{Conv}}_{1 \times 1} (Z_{4} )$$\end{document}

Finally, the output of the LSKA module (which serves as the final output of the entire SPPF_LSKA block) is obtained by performing an element-wise multiplication (⊗) between the generated attention map A and the original input feature \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_{fused}$$\end{document} via a residual connection:23\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Output = F_{fused} \otimes A$$\end{document}

This precise mathematical decomposition fundamentally allows the network to adaptively model extreme long-range spatial dependencies across the entire lung field with a negligible increase in computational and parameter overhead.

Point-based dynamic feature upsampling

In the feature fusion stage, recovering high-resolution spatial details is crucial for accurately localizing the boundaries of Lung Cancer infiltrates. Traditional upsampling methods, such as nearest-neighbor interpolation, rely on static, rule-based pixel duplication. This static approach is agnostic to the semantic content of the image, frequently leading to checkerboard artifacts and the irreversible loss of fine-grained boundary textures of faint ground-glass opacities. Recent research has highlighted the importance of contour-level structural cues for boundary preservation in low-contrast medical imaging scenarios, demonstrating that integrating such priors can effectively reduce boundary leakage and improve delineation accuracy39. To overcome this, we employ the DySample module, an ultra-lightweight and content-aware dynamic upsampler.

As illustrated in Fig. 5, DySample bypasses computationally expensive dynamic convolutions by framing upsampling fundamentally as a point-sampling task. Experiments and results

To comprehensively evaluate the effectiveness and superiority of the proposed improved YOLOv12n model for lung lesion detection, rigorous experiments were conducted. This section details the experimental setup, evaluation metrics, ablation studies to validate each proposed module, and a comparative analysis with current State-of-the-Art (SOTA) object detection models.

Experimental setup and implementation details

The experiments were conducted on a high-performance computing platform running the Ubuntu 20.04 operating system. The hardware environment was equipped with a 25-core Intel(R) Xeon(R) Platinum 8481C CPU and a single NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4090 GPU (24 GB VRAM). To leverage GPU acceleration for the deep learning models, CUDA 11.8 was utilized. The software framework was constructed using Python 3.8 and PyTorch 2.0.0.

To ensure the fairness and reproducibility of the experiments, all baseline detectors and the proposed PD-YOLO were trained under identical hyperparameter configurations. Specifically, the models were trained for 100 epochs with a batch size of 64. All input CT slices were resized to a unified resolution of 640 × 640 pixels.

We employed the SGD optimizer with an initial learning rate of 0.001 and a momentum of 0.937. To strictly prevent model overfitting during the training process, several regularization techniques were applied. Specifically, an L2 weight decay of 0.0005 was utilized, and spatial data augmentation techniques including random scaling and horizontal flipping were implemented to enhance the model’s generalization capability across varying lesion scales. To verify the stability of the results, each model was trained and evaluated five times independently using the same fixed dataset splits and identical hyperparameters. The experimental outcomes (including all evaluation metrics) were identical across all five runs, confirming the deterministic nature of our setup and the reproducibility of the reported performance.

Datasets

The dataset utilized in this study was derived from the publicly available "Lung Nodule Segmentation study" dataset hosted on Roboflow Universe (https://universe.roboflow.com/varun-18tlk/lung-nodule-segmentation-study), which aggregates de-identified axial chest CT slices from the open-access Lung Image Database Consortium and Image Database Resource Initiative (LIDC-IDRI) maintained by The Cancer Imaging Archive (TCIA)40.

Source data and inclusion criteria. The original CT volumes were acquired from the LIDC-IDRI collection on TCIA under its public research-use policies. Axial slices were selected according to the following inclusion criteria: (i) presence of at least one pulmonary nodule (≥ 3 mm in diameter), malignant lung cancer consolidation, or histologically confirmed adenocarcinoma lesion; and (ii) adequate image quality without severe motion artifacts, beam-hardening artifacts, or field-of-view truncation. Slices lacking pathological findings or with post-operative changes were excluded.

Preprocessing pipeline. To ensure optimal model input and maximize parenchymal contrast, raw DICOM images were first converted to 16-bit grayscale PNG format and specifically windowed to standard pulmonary window settings. All images were subsequently resized to a uniform spatial resolution of 640 × 640 pixels. Furthermore, to standardize intensity distributions across different CT scanners and acquisition protocols, histogram equalization and per-image min-max normalization were systematically applied.

Data augmentation. To enhance the model’s generalization capability and mitigate potential overfitting on specific lesion orientations, comprehensive data augmentation was performed via the Roboflow platform. The augmentation pipeline included random rotation (± 10°), hue shift (± 15°), saturation scaling (± 25%), brightness adjustment (± 10%), and exposure variation (± 15°). Following augmentation and an additional strict author-led quality control process, the final curated corpus comprises 4,200 high-quality images ready for model training.

Annotation protocol. Ground-truth bounding boxes and class labels were generated by three board-certified thoracic radiologists through a two-stage consensus process. In the first stage, all radiologists independently delineated lesion boundaries and classified each region into one of three categories—nodule, cancer, or adenocarcinoma—based on morphological criteria and, where available, corresponding pathological reports. Inter-rater reliability was quantified prior to adjudication: Fleiss’ Kappa for class assignment was 0.83 (95% CI: 0.78–0.88) and mean pairwise bounding-box IoU was 0.86, indicating substantial to almost perfect agreement. In the second stage, cases with inter-observer disagreement (IoU < 0.7 or class label discrepancy) were reviewed in a joint adjudication session to establish final consensus annotations. The authors further validated these consensus annotations against the source TCIA metadata to ensure clinical consistency. The finalized dataset contains 4,303 annotated instances across 4200 images. To facilitate robust model training and unbiased evaluation, the dataset was randomly partitioned using an 8:1:1 split strategy, resulting in a training set of 3360 images, a validation set of 420 images, and a testing set of 420 images. Limitations and failure case analysis

While PD-YOLO exhibits state-of-the-art performance across multiple evaluation metrics, it still encounters specific limitations when processing highly complex and ambiguous CT images. Subtle peripheral lesions with low confidence: As shown in Fig. 9(a), the model detects a small nodule but only assigns a borderline confidence score of 0.58. Such small nodules located near the peripheral pleural wall often blend heavily with the lung boundary and local imaging noise. Although the proposed A2C2f_DFFN module significantly enhances the non-linear feature representation of low-contrast lesions, the pixel gradient difference in this extreme peripheral case remains too subtle for the model to assert high certainty.

Obscured early-stage shadows amidst severe noise: Fig. 9(b) demonstrates an early-stage cancer lesion detected with an exceptionally low confidence score of 0.41. The lesion is extremely faint, lacking distinct morphological boundaries, and is heavily embedded within severe image noise (e.g., speckle artifacts) and dense lung parenchyma. During the aggressive downsampling in the deeper stages of the backbone, the fine-grained texture of such minute infiltrates can be heavily attenuated. Consequently, the model struggles to distinctly separate the pathological features from the background noise, leading to depressed classification confidence and an elevated risk of missed detection in strict clinical thresholds.

Future work: Addressing these limitations constitutes the primary direction for our future research. While our 2D spatial modules (like SPPF_LSKA and DySample) have maximized single-slice feature extraction, relying solely on 2D cross-sections inherently lacks the depth information necessary to confirm ambiguous shadows. We plan to explore temporal feature fusion across multi-slice CT sequences to provide critical 3D spatial context. This would allow the network to cross-verify faint signals across adjacent slices, thereby significantly improving the detection confidence and robustness for early-stage, extremely low-contrast lesions.

Generalization and dataset bias. A major limitation of this study is the absence of external validation. All experiments were conducted on a single dataset derived from the LIDC-IDRI collection, which, despite its widespread use, represents a specific imaging protocol and patient population. The near-saturated precision achieved on the nodule and adenocarcinoma classes, while demonstrating the model’s capacity to fit the training distribution, raises legitimate concerns about potential dataset-specific overfitting. Factors such as consistent slice thickness, uniform reconstruction kernels, and limited scanner diversity in LIDC-IDRI may not capture the full variability encountered in routine clinical practice (e.g., different CT manufacturers, dose levels, or artifact patterns). Therefore, the reported performance metrics, although highly promising, should be interpreted as proof-of-concept efficacy within a controlled research setting rather than directly generalizable to uncurated real-world data.

To rigorously establish generalization, future work will include: (i) cross-dataset testing on publicly available independent lung cancer CT benchmarks such as LUNA16 and NLST; (ii) multi-center retrospective validation using data from at least three geographically distinct institutions with heterogeneous acquisition protocols; and (iii) domain adaptation techniques (e.g., unsupervised domain adaptation or test-time augmentation) to mitigate potential dataset shift. Only after such extensive validation can the model be considered ready for clinical deployment.

Conclusion

This paper proposed a high-precision Lung Cancer detection model based on an improved YOLOv12 architecture to address the challenges of large-scale variations and blurred boundaries of lesions in chest CT imaging. Specifically, the A2C2f_DFFN module was introduced to significantly enhance the non-linear feature representation of low-contrast early lesions. Additionally, the SPPF_LSKA module combined Spatial Pyramid Pooling with Large Separable Kernel Attention to expand the effective receptive field with extremely low computational overhead, effectively handling large-scale lung consolidations. Furthermore, the ultra-lightweight DySample module was employed to adaptively reconstruct high-resolution features, mitigating the loss of edge textures and minor infiltrates during feature propagation. Extensive experimental results on a chest CT dataset demonstrated that the proposed model achieves a mean Average Precision (mAP@0.5) of 97.3%, successfully outperforming state-of-the-art detectors including YOLOv12s and YOLOv12n. Ablation studies further confirmed that all three integrated modules effectively improve network performance, generating a favorable synergistic effect. While each component builds upon known design principles, their combination is specifically tailored to the challenges of CT‑based lung lesion detection.
