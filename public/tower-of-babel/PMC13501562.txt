Treatment patterns and outcomes in elderly patients with brain arteriovenous malformations: a multicenter prospective cohort study
Authors: Kexin Yuan, Ningshuang Hu, Zhipeng Li, Haibin Zhang, Heze Han, Ke Wang, Hengwei Jin, Dezhi Gao, Shibin Sun, Youxiang Li, Shuo Wang, Yu Chen, Xiaolin Chen, Yuanli Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aimed to evaluate long-term outcomes and hemorrhage risk in patients aged 60 years and older with brain arteriovenous malformations (AVMs) across different real-world management modalities.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 94 patients aged ≥ 60 years with AVMs from the MATCH study between August 2011 and December 2021. Patients were categorized into six treatment groups: conservative management, surgical resection, embolization, stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS), embolization plus resection, and combination therapy. Outcomes included annualized hemorrhage rate and functional status assessed by the modified Rankin Scale (mRS). Subgroup analyses were conducted based on sex, pretreatment hemorrhage, Spetzler–Martin grade, lesion location, flow-related aneurysm, and age.ResultsAmong the 94 patients (2.19% of 4286 total AVM cases), the median age was 64.95 years, and most had mild baseline disability (median mRS = 1). No statistically significant between-group differences were detected in perioperative complications or discharge mRS scores, although interpretation is limited by sparse-event distributions and limited statistical power. During follow-up, 9.57% of patients experienced hemorrhage, with higher rates observed in the embolization and SRS groups. Discharge mRS score and rupture history were associated with unfavorable functional outcomes, while embolization was associated with higher hemorrhage risk during follow-up.ConclusionsIn elderly AVM patients, functional outcomes should be interpreted in the context of baseline status and treatment selection. Resection-based approaches achieved the highest obliteration rates, whereas embolization and SRS were associated with higher observed follow-up hemorrhage risk. These findings support individualized management and close follow-up.Trial registrationClinicalTrials.gov, NCT04572568. Introduction

Cerebral arteriovenous malformations (AVMs) are complex vascular lesions that present unique clinical challenges [1], particularly in elderly patients. Approximately 5%–18% of AVM patients have been reported to present over the age of 60 years [2]. Patients over the age of 60 represent a distinct subset due to the differences in both physiological resilience and the presentation of brain AVMs (bAVMs) compared to younger populations [3–9].

Prior observational studies suggest that increasing age is associated with hemorrhagic presentation and distinct angioarchitectural patterns [5, 6, 9–12]. Specifically, older patients have been reported to more frequently harbor high-risk features that are linked to rupture risk, such as associated aneurysms and deep venous drainage, and may show a distribution shift toward more deep or infratentorial lesions [6, 9, 13, 14]. Consistent with this concept, patient-level and cohort-level evidence indicates that age is an independent predictor of hemorrhage risk in untreated bAVMs, with risk increasing across decades of life [5, 9]. Furthermore, a large systematic review and meta-analysis of 13,698 treated bAVM patients demonstrated that advanced age was significantly associated with poorer functional outcomes and higher treatment-related morbidity and mortality after intervention for bAVMs [15].

Despite these signals, evidence guiding management in patients ≥ 60 years remains limited and often inconsistent across treatment modalities [12, 16, 17]. While some studies suggest that stereotactic radiosurgery may be attractive in older patients due to its noninvasive nature, others favor conservative management because procedural risks may offset long-term benefit, particularly in patients with competing comorbidities and shorter life expectancy [16, 18–24]. Importantly, most existing studies are small, single-center, and prone to referral and selection biases, leaving uncertainty regarding real-world outcomes in this growing population. In addition, comprehensive data specifically targeting this age group are lacking.

Accordingly, we used data from a multicenter registry to characterize presentation, treatment patterns, and long-term outcomes in patients aged ≥ 60 years with bAVMs, and to explore factors associated with hemorrhage and unfavorable functional outcomes during follow-up. By focusing on an older cohort in routine clinical practice, this study aims to inform individualized, evidence-based decision-making for elderly patients with bAVMs.

Methods

Study design and participants

The authors retrospectively reviewed 94 AVMs in patients aged 60 years and older admitted at participating hospitals in the MATCH study (Multimodality Treatment for Brain AVMs in Mainland China, ClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: NCT04572568) from August 2011 through December 2021. The MATCH study is a multicenter, prospective, real-world registry led by Beijing Tiantan Hospital in collaboration with multiple comprehensive hospitals across mainland China. This is an observational cohort with planned follow-up for up to 20 years. This registry was established to explore the natural history of brain AVMs and to develop optimal management strategies specific to the Asian population [25]. Patients are enrolled prospectively, and treatment strategies are not assigned by protocol. The treatment decisions are made by the treating teams according to clinical judgment and patient preference. Prior studies have validated the reliability of this database [26, 27].

Patients were included if they met the following eligibility criteria: 1) AVM diagnosis confirmed by digital subtraction angiography (DSA) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI); 2) availability of clinical and radiological follow-up data; and 3) aged 60 years or older. Patients were excluded if they 1) lacked essential baseline data or 2) were lost to follow-up.

The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Beijing Tiantan Hospital (IRB approval number: KY 2020–003-01), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants, in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The study adhered to the STROBE guidelines for observational cohort studies. Data consistency among investigators was ensured through a rigorous quality assurance protocol, as detailed in Supplementary Material 1. Patients were categorized into six groups based on treatment modality: conservative management, surgical resection, embolization, SRS, embolization + surgical resection, and combination therapy. Neurofunctional outcomes were evaluated using the score of modified Rankin Scale (mRS) (range 0–6), with higher scores indicating more severe disability and with 6 indicating death [28]. Subgroup analysis was conducted to compare long-term outcomes among different characteristics.

Baseline characteristics

Baseline characteristics including demographic data (age, sex), clinical presentation (hemorrhage, mRS score at admission), morphological features, and angio-architectural parameters were recorded at enrollment using standardized definitions. Key variables (including presentation status, admission neurological condition, and imaging features) were abstracted from medical records and imaging reports at the time of care and entered into the registry database. Hemorrhagic stroke was defined as a clinically symptomatic event (any new focal neurological deficit, seizure, or new-onset dramatic headache) confirmed by imaging findings, such as intracranial hematoma or subarachnoid hemorrhage attributed to AVM on computed tomography (CT) or MRI. Symptomatic surgery refers to symptom-directed procedures for acute symptom relief (e.g., hematoma evacuation/decompressive craniectomy) without resection of the AVM nidus. The number of rupture events was defined as the number of distinct, imaging-confirmed AVM-related intracranial hemorrhage events (intracerebral, intraventricular, and/or subarachnoid hemorrhage) that occurred prior to registry enrollment. Transfers or repeat admissions for the same hemorrhagic event were counted once, and treatment-related hemorrhages were not included.

Morphological attributes, in accordance with the nomenclature established by the joint committee of the American Society of Interventional and Therapeutic Neuroradiology, included supratentorial location, involvement of eloquent brain regions, deep venous drainage, diffuse nidus morphology, deep AVM location, and presence of associated aneurysms. The radiological information was determined via DSA and MRI, and the definition of these features was consistent with the reporting terminology guidelines [29]. Spetzler-Martin grade was used to assess nidus complexity and predict long-term outcomes [30]. The definition of eloquent regions complied with the Spetzler-Martin grade. Additionally, Lawton-Young grade was used as a supplementary grading system to improve prediction of surgical risk [31].

The following variables are specially defined in this study: The nidus volume prior to embolization was calculated using the ABC/2 formula applied to DSA images [32]. Venous drainage was dichotomized into any deep drainage (deep drainage with or without superficial venous drainage), exclusively deep venous drainage, or superficial-only drainage. AVM location was dichotomized into deep (brainstem, basal ganglia, thalamus, cerebellum, insular lobe, and corpus callosum) and superficial (all other locations) [30]. Ventricular system involvement was classified as the nidus (with a contrast-enhancement or flow void) contacting the ventricle's ependymal lining on contrast-enhanced T1- and T2-weighted images. Multiple source supply (Arterial borderzone) was defined as AVM nidus supplied by branches of at least two of the individual major circle of Willis arteries: the anterior cerebral artery, the middle cerebral artery, and the posterior circulation arteries [33]. Hemorrhagic presentation was defined as an AVM whose clinical manifestation in the whole disease history, involving intracranial hemorrhage like intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH), intraventricular hemorrhage (IVH) and subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH) attributable to the AVM and confirmed by CT or MRI. All radiological characteristics were independently evaluated by two credentialed senior neurointerventional radiologists (YKZ and YZ). In cases of inconsistency, the final determination was made by a professor of senior neurointerventional radiology (YXL) with more than 30 years of clinical experience.

In addition, we collected baseline medical comorbidities by additional chart review and summarized comorbidity burden using the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) [34]. Comorbidity data were available for 91 of 94 patients and have been incorporated into the baseline characteristics table (Table 1). CCI was calculated according to standard weighting and, to avoid overlap with AVM-related neurological events, the “cerebrovascular disease” component was excluded from the CCI score in this study.

Outcomes and follow-up

Neurological status was assessed using the mRS score, where a score of 0 represented no symptoms and a score of 6 indicated death. The primary outcome was neurological dysfunction, defined as a follow-up mRS score of > 2, with a score of > 3 indicating severe neurological dysfunction. The rupture risk was represented by the annualized hemorrhagic rate. The secondary outcome was hemorrhagic stroke. AVM obliteration was characterized by the absence of flow voids on MRI or the lack of abnormal arteriovenous shunting on DSA.

Clinical follow-up was conducted via phone interviews or record review by well-trained clinical research coordinators at 3 months, 6 months, annually (1, 2, and 3 years), and every 5 years after admission, using telephone interviews or medical record reviews. Suspected hemorrhagic events were identified during these follow-ups. Radiological evaluations included noninvasive MRI at 6-month intervals for the first two years, followed by annual imaging. For patients with MRI-confirmed AVM obliteration, DSA was recommended unless contraindicated. The primary outcome was tracked from the intervention until the last follow-up. Secondary outcomes were tracked from intervention until either the occurrence of a hemorrhagic stroke or death or AVM obliteration, or until the last follow-up if no such events occurred. Clinical and radiological data were independently reviewed by two blinded neuroradiologists (YKZ and YaZ).

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were presented as mean values with standard deviations (SD) or medians with interquartile ranges (IQR), as appropriate. Categorical variables were described using counts and percentages. Baseline characteristics were compared between the treatment groups. For continuous data, Student's t-test or Mann–Whitney U-test was employed, while categorical data were analyzed using Pearson’s chi-square test. Cumulative rupture risk after treatment was estimated as 1 minus the Kaplan–Meier event-free survival estimate and compared across treatment categories using the log-rank test. Hazard ratios (HRs) were estimated using the Cox proportional hazards regression model for the hemorrhagic stroke after treatment event. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed by examining Schoenfeld’s global test and was visually inspected for potential time-variant biases. Collinearity among variables included in the Cox proportional hazards regression model was assessed using the variance inflation factor (variance inflation factor < 3). Prespecified subgroup analyses categorized total cohorts by sex (male vs. female), hemorrhage before treatment (yes vs. no), Spetzler-Martin (SM) grade (I, II, III, IV, V), AVM location (superficial vs. deep), presence of flow-related aneurysm (absent vs. aneurysm), and age ([60–65) vs. ≥ 65 years). Interaction tests were conducted for these subgroups. Univariable logistic regression models were utilized to calculate odds ratios (ORs) for AVM hemorrhage after treatment (yes vs. no) and mRS > 2.

All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.1.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing). Univariable logistic regression was first performed to screen candidate predictors. Variables with p < 0.05 in univariable analyses were entered into the multivariable logistic regression model; clinically relevant covariates were also considered when appropriate. To reduce model overfitting given the limited number of events, the number of covariates was restricted based on the events-per-variable principle, and collinearity among predictors was assessed before final model fitting. P values were 2-sided, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

This study analyzed a cohort of 94 AVMs (2.19%), derived from the MATCH database which contains records for 4,286 AVM patients. The participant selection process is illustrated in Fig. 1, and baseline characteristics are detailed in Table 1. Among these patients, 55 (58.5%) presented with hemorrhage. Twenty patients (21.3%) received conservative management, while 74 (78.7%) underwent intervention. The interventions included surgical resection in 24 cases (25.53%), endovascular embolization in 23 cases (24.47%), SRS in 13 cases (13.83%), combined embolization and surgical resection in 4 cases (4.26%), and multiple treatments in 10 cases (10.64%).

Participant selection process

The median admission age was 64.95 years [IQR 62.10–67.07], with a median AVM volume of 4.18 cm3 [IQR 1.03–13.28] and a median admission mRS score of 1 [IQR 1–2]. Most patients experienced only one rupture event (46 cases, 48.94%). From first diagnosis to admission, or from age 60 years to admission, 69 rupture events occurred in 56 patients over a cumulative observational period of 645.37 patient-years, translating to the natural annualized rupture risk of 10.69%. In the conservative management cohort (n = 20), follow-up from 60 years of age documented 9 hemorrhagic events in 7 patients over 249.10 patient-years, corresponding to an annualized rupture risk of 3.61%. Among the 94 AVMs, 50 (53.19%) were located in eloquent regions, and 32 (34.04%) were in deep regions. Deep venous drainage was observed in 24 AVMs (25.53%), while 13 AVMs (13.83%) exhibited exclusively deep venous drainage. Flow-related aneurysms were associated with 32 AVMs (34.04%), and 24 AVMs (25.53%) were classified as having a diffuse nidus based on time-of-flight (TOF) images.

Comparison of treatment modalities for arteriovenous malformations

In the total AVMs cohort, the median follow-up duration for the primary outcome was 4.3 years (IQR, 1.9 to 6.8 years). We further compared perioperative complications and follow-up outcomes across treatment modalities in patients with AVMs (Table 2). Admission mRS, follow-up mRS, and AVM-related hemorrhage during follow-up by treatment modality after stratification according to hemorrhage at admission are summarized in Supplementary Table 1. Among the 24 patients who underwent surgical resection, 4 (16.67%) experienced perioperative complications, including 2 cases of hemorrhage and 2 cases of cerebral infarction. Similarly, 4 patients (17.39%) in the embolization group experienced complications (1 case of hemorrhage and 3 cases of cerebral infarction), while 1 patient (25%) in the combined embolization-resection group experienced perioperative cerebral infarction. No statistically significant between-group differences in complication rates were detected. The median mRS scores at discharge did not show statistically significant differences across the treatment groups. However, during long-term follow-up, hemorrhagic events occurred in 9 patients (9.57%) overall. Specifically, 2 patients (10%) in the conservative treatment group, 4 patients (17.39%) in the embolization group, 2 patients (15.38%) in the stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS) group, and 1 patient (10%) in the combined treatment group experienced hemorrhage. In terms of lesion obliteration, 100% obliteration was achieved only in the surgical resection and combined embolization-resection groups. In the embolization group, 5 patients (21.74%) achieved complete obliteration, compared to 6 patients (46.15%) in the SRS group and 8 patients (80%) in the combined treatment group. No obliteration was observed in the conservative group, and the differences between groups were statistically significant. Follow-up mRS scores did not show statistically significant differences across treatment categories in this real-world cohort.

Univariate and multivariate analysis of factors influencing poor functional outcomes

The results of the univariate and multivariate analyses of factors associated with poor functional outcomes are shown in Table 3. For neurological dysfunction defined as mRS > 2, univariate analysis identified several significant factors, including higher admission mRS scores (OR = 1.3, 95% CI: 1.01–1.68, p = 0.043), hemorrhagic presentation (OR = 3.43, 95% CI: 1.15–10.25, p = 0.027), increased rupture times (OR = 1.6, 95% CI: 1.07–2.41, p = 0.024), higher discharge mRS scores (OR = 1.76, 95% CI: 1.36–2.28, p < 0.001), and basal ganglia involvement (OR = 6.29, 95% CI: 1.78–22.24, p = 0.004). Multivariate analysis showed that discharge mRS score was the only variable that remained statistically significant in the multivariable model (OR = 1.73, 95% CI: 1.27–2.36, p < 0.001).

For severe neurological dysfunction defined as mRS > 3, univariate analysis revealed that increased rupture times (OR = 1.63, 95% CI: 1.04–2.57, p = 0.035), higher discharge mRS scores (OR = 1.89, 95% CI: 1.42–2.53, p < 0.001), parietal lobe lesions (OR = 2.88, 95% CI: 1.02–8.08, p = 0.045), and basal ganglia involvement (OR = 5.5, 95% CI: 1.2–25.26, p = 0.028) were significant predictors. Multivariate analysis indicated that both discharge mRS score (OR = 1.82, 95% CI: 1.32–2.52, p < 0.001) and rupture times (OR = 1.8, 95% CI: 1.04–3.14, p = 0.037) were independently associated with poor outcomes.

Univariate and multivariate analysis of risk factors for hemorrhage during follow-up in elderly AVM patients

Univariate analysis identified several factors significantly associated with an increased risk of hemorrhage during follow-up in elderly patients with AVMs (Table 4). Variables associated with hemorrhage in univariable analysis included a greater number of prior rupture events (HR = 3.03, 95% CI: 1.74–5.3, p < 0.001), embolization treatment (HR = 4.14, 95% CI: 1.17–14.71, p = 0.028), lesions located in the insular lobe (HR = 9.16, 95% CI: 1.01–82.71, p = 0.049), larger AVM size (HR = 5.66, 95% CI: 1.2–26.82, p = 0.029), and the presence of draining vein stenosis (HR = 4.89, 95% CI: 1.37–17.42, p = 0.014). In the multivariate analysis, embolization treatment (HR = 1.69, 95% CI: 1.04–2.75, p = 0.036) remained independently associated with an increased risk of hemorrhage during follow-up.

Trends in mRS scores across admission, discharge, and follow-up

Figure 3 illustrates the distribution of mRS scores at admission, discharge, and follow-up in patients with brain arteriovenous malformations (AVMs). Overall, the distribution of mRS scores suggests generally favorable functional status at baseline with heterogeneity in short-term and long-term outcomes. At admission (N = 94), 14 patients (14.9%) had no symptoms (mRS 0) and 70 (74.5%) had mild disability with mRS 1–2, whereas 10 (10.6%) had neurological dysfunction (mRS > 2) and 7 (7.45%) had severe neurological dysfunction. At discharge (N = 94), functional status was overall favorable, with 37 (39.36%) having mRS 0; however, a subset of patients experienced worse short-term outcomes, with 12 (12.77%) having mRS > 2 and 8 (8.51%) having mRS > 3, which may reflect peri-treatment recovery or hospitalization-related morbidity. At last follow-up (N = 94), functional outcomes were generally stable or improved compared with discharge, and the number of patients with mRS 0 increased to 43 (45.74%). Importantly, unfavorable outcomes were also observed: at last follow-up, 21 (22.34%) had mRS > 2 and 17 (18.09%) had mRS > 3. In addition, 10 patients had died by last follow-up (mRS 6). Of these, three deaths were directly related to AVMs, while the remaining 7 were due to causes unrelated to the AVM.

Discussion

Cerebral AVMs pose particular clinical challenges in elderly patients, yet evidence guiding management in those aged 60 years and older remains limited. This study presents a comprehensive evaluation of cerebral AVMs in elderly patients aged 60 years and older, providing insights into the natural history, treatment patterns, and long-term outcomes in various treatment groups. Our findings highlight the complexities of managing AVMs in elderly patients, including the influence of treatment modality, baseline clinical characteristics, and lesion-specific factors on functional outcomes.

Functional outcomes across treatment modalities

We did not observe statistically significant differences in follow-up functional outcomes (as measured by the modified Rankin Scale, mRS) across the different treatment modalities for elderly AVM patients. This finding is broadly consistent with a previous study that reported similar long-term mRS scores and mortality among different management modalities in the unruptured subgroup of elderly AVM patients [17]. Supplementary Table 1 further summarizes admission mRS, follow-up mRS, and AVM-related hemorrhage during follow-up by treatment modality within hemorrhagic and non-hemorrhagic presentations. Nevertheless, our findings differ from the prior Randomized Trial of Unruptured Brain Arteriovenous Malformations (ARUBA), which was the first clinical trial evaluating treatment strategies for brain AVMs [16]. The ARUBA and its final follow-up study showed significant differences favoring medical management over intervention treatment [18, 35]. This inconsistency may be attributed to the differences in study design, patient selection, rupture status, and treatment grouping.

Additionally, our results are inconsistent with some studies involving younger populations, where aggressive interventions such as surgical resection or SRS have been shown to improve long-term outcomes [19–21, 36, 37]. While it was suggested that stereotactic radiosurgery may be a favorable option for older patients due to its non-invasive nature and relatively lower risk of complications [2, 22], we did not observe a clear functional advantage of SRS in this observational cohort. This may partly reflect that the benefit of SRS is closely linked to eventual nidus obliteration, which is delayed rather than immediate [38, 39]. During the latency period before complete obliteration, the nidus remains patent, and hemorrhage risk may persist until radiation-induced endothelial injury, intimal proliferation, thrombosis, and progressive vascular occlusion ultimately lead to angiographic cure [40]. The risk of unfavorable functional outcome (mRS > 2) showed no statistically significant difference across treatment categories (p = 0.863) (Table 2), although this observation should be interpreted in light of the limited subgroup sizes and real-world treatment selection. Overall, this finding underscores the importance of integrating baseline neurological status, hemorrhagic presentation, comorbidities, lesion location, AVM size, and treatment feasibility when making individualized management decisions, rather than relying solely on the modality itself.

Treatment selection in this multicenter observational cohort was closely related to AVM surgical grade and angioarchitectural characteristics. The Spetzler–Martin grading system and the Lawton Young supplementary grading scale were developed to stratify surgical risk and guide selection of patients for microsurgical management [30, 41]. Consistent with guideline frameworks, microsurgical resection alone or combined with embolization is generally favored for lesions with more favorable surgical profiles, whereas radiosurgery and endovascular approaches are more commonly considered for deep or eloquent lesions or when expected open-surgical morbidity is high [2, 42]. Consequently, the observed follow-up disability patterns should be interpreted in the context of confounding by indication and selection bias, as treatment groups are not exchangeable at baseline [39, 42].

Perioperative complications were observed only in intervention-based groups; however, no statistically significant between-group differences were detected, likely reflecting the small number of events across treatment categories. We noted that lesion obliteration was most successful in patients undergoing surgical resection or combined embolization-resection treatments, with 100% obliteration rates in these cohorts. The embolization group had a substantially lower obliteration rate, and no obliteration was achieved in the conservative management group. This is consistent with prior studies that have shown the limitations of non-surgical approaches in achieving complete AVM obliteration, particularly in cases involving larger or more complex lesions, with reported obliteration rates varying across treatment settings [43–45]. The lower obliteration rate after embolization may be partly explained by the angioarchitectural complexity of AVMs, as complete endovascular occlusion requires safe penetration of embolic material into the entire nidus, which may be limited by multiple feeders, inaccessible deep or perforating arteries, and the need to avoid non-target embolization [42, 46, 47].

Our univariate and multivariate analyses identified several key factors associated with poor functional outcomes in elderly AVM patients, including higher admission and discharge mRS scores, hemorrhagic presentation, increased rupture events, and basal ganglia involvement. Notably, higher discharge mRS scores were associated with worse long-term functional outcomes, consistent with the expected relationship between early and later neurological status and with prior AVM literature on functional recovery trajectories [5, 15, 17, 48, 49]. Additionally, our findings that hemorrhagic presentation and rupture history are associated with worse outcomes are consistent with studies showing that hemorrhagic AVMs tend to have a more aggressive clinical course and a higher likelihood of poor recovery [5, 48, 50, 9].

Hemorrhage risk and long-term prognosis

The overall annualized rupture risk observed in our elderly cohort provides a person-time–based estimate that can be contextualized against previously reported natural-history hemorrhage risks in broader AVM populations. Prior long-term natural history series and meta-analyses have consistently shown that hemorrhage risk is heterogeneous and is strongly influenced by presentation status and angioarchitectural factors (e.g., prior hemorrhage, deep location, and associated aneurysms) [3–5]. Therefore, any differences between our overall annualized rupture risk and prior estimates likely reflect the older age structure of our cohort and the enrichment of hemorrhagic presentation and high-risk morphological features, rather than indicating a directly comparable baseline risk across studies [3–5, 51]. Cross-study comparisons should also be interpreted cautiously given differences in cohort composition and in the definition of time-at-risk.

Our study provides additional evidence regarding factors associated with hemorrhage during follow-up in elderly patients with brain AVMs. In univariable analyses, rupture times, AVM size, insular lobe location, and draining vein stenosis were associated with subsequent hemorrhage; however, these associations did not remain statistically significant after adjustment. In the multivariable Cox model, embolization was the only variable that remained statistically significant in the multivariable Cox model with a higher risk of hemorrhage during follow-up (adjusted HR 1.69, 95% CI 1.04–2.75, p = 0.0357). Previous studies have reported heterogeneous outcomes after embolization for bAVMs, depending on rupture status, lesion complexity, treatment intent, and whether embolization was used as stand-alone or adjunctive therapy [15, 26, 52]. In our elderly cohort, embolization was associated with a higher observed risk of hemorrhage during follow-up. This association should be interpreted with caution, as embolization is often selected for more complex lesions or used in staged treatment strategies [15, 16, 52], and residual confounding by indication may contribute to the observed findings. Table 2 and Fig. 2 provide further context for this observation. Surgical resection and embolization plus resection achieved the highest obliteration rates and had no follow-up hemorrhage events in this cohort, whereas follow-up hemorrhage was numerically more frequent after embolization and SRS. However, these two modalities may reflect different mechanisms of residual risk. For embolization, the main concern may be incomplete nidus obliteration: complete endovascular occlusion requires safe penetration of embolic material into the entire nidus, which may be limited by complex angioarchitecture, multiple feeders, inaccessible deep or perforating arteries, and the need to avoid non-target embolization [46, 47]. Residual arteriovenous shunting after incomplete embolization may therefore continue to carry hemorrhage risk during follow-up [42]. In contrast, SRS showed a higher observed obliteration rate than embolization in this cohort, but its protective effect depends on delayed nidus obliteration. During the latency period before angiographic cure, the nidus remains patent and hemorrhage risk may persist until radiation-induced endothelial injury, intimal proliferation, thrombosis, and progressive vascular occlusion ultimately lead to obliteration [7, 22, 38–40]. The cumulative rupture-risk curves showed a trend toward separation across treatment categories in the total cohort and in the unruptured subgroup, although the differences did not reach conventional statistical significance. Taken together, these observations suggest that follow-up hemorrhage after embolization or SRS may be better interpreted in relation to the completeness and timing of nidus obliteration, residual angioarchitectural risk, rupture status, and treatment selection.

Despite hemorrhage events in a subset of patients, functional outcomes in this cohort were generally stable or improved over time. Many patients achieved favorable mRS scores at last follow-up, which may reflect the generally favorable baseline neurological status of this cohort as well as individualized treatment strategies. Importantly, we additionally characterized baseline medical comorbidity burden (e.g., hypertension and diabetes) and summarized it using the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), which may be relevant when counseling elderly patients and tailoring management decisions [34]. Finally, the short-term worsening in functional status observed in some patients at discharge followed by improvement at follow-up is consistent with peri-treatment recovery dynamics and highlights the need to set realistic expectations regarding transient post-treatment morbidity in elderly individuals.

Limitations and implications for future research

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although the MATCH registry prospectively collected clinical and radiological data, the present study represents an observational subgroup analysis of elderly patients, and treatment selection was based on real-world clinical judgment rather than random assignment. Therefore, residual confounding related to baseline neurological status, hemorrhagic presentation, lesion characteristics, comorbidities, and treatment indication could not be fully eliminated. Second, the number of elderly patients was modest, and distribution across multiple treatment categories resulted in small subgroup sizes, particularly for less frequent outcomes such as follow-up hemorrhage. Accordingly, treatment-category findings should be interpreted primarily as descriptive and exploratory observations rather than definitive comparative evidence. Third, most patients were in the early elderly age range, limiting further assessment of outcomes in very old patients. Future studies with larger elderly cohorts, longer follow-up, and more granular assessment of frailty and comorbidities are warranted to better inform individualized management of elderly bAVM patients.

Conclusions

In conclusion, managing AVMs in the elderly requires individualized strategies. While no statistically significant between-category differences in follow-up functional outcomes were detected in this real-world cohort, resection-based approaches were associated with the highest observed obliteration rates. Follow-up hemorrhage was more frequently observed after embolization and SRS, highlighting the need for careful post-treatment surveillance. Key predictors of poor outcomes were higher admission and discharge mRS scores, hemorrhagic presentation, and rupture history. Overall, these findings emphasize the importance of integrating baseline status, lesion characteristics, obliteration potential, and treatment risk in elderly AVM management.
