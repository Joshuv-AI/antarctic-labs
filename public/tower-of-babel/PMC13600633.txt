Recurrent Gastrointestinal Bleeding in Severe Aortic Stenosis: A Case of Heyde’s Syndrome
Authors: Prajakta Kakde, Vangala Uday Kumar, Shourya Acharya, Sunil Kumar, Saket Satyasham Toshniwal, Anusha Jha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Heyde’s syndrome is a recognized but often underdiagnosed cause of recurrent gastrointestinal bleeding in elderly patients, characterized by the triad of severe aortic stenosis, gastrointestinal angiodysplasia, and acquired von Willebrand factor (vWF) deficiency. We report the case of a 72-year-old male who presented with recurrent melena over 6 months, associated with progressive anemia and fatigue. Despite multiple hospitalizations and repeated upper gastrointestinal endoscopy and colonoscopy, no bleeding source was identified. Further evaluation revealed severe calcific aortic stenosis on echocardiography and evidence of acquired vWF deficiency. Capsule endoscopy identified angiodysplasia in the proximal jejunum. The patient was initially managed with supportive and endoscopic measures; however, due to persistent bleeding, he underwent surgical aortic valve replacement. Following valve replacement, gastrointestinal bleeding resolved completely, hemoglobin levels normalized, and vWF multimers were restored. This case highlights the importance of recognizing the diagnostic triad of Heyde’s syndrome and supports aortic valve replacement as definitive therapy.

INTRODUCTION

Recurrent gastrointestinal bleeding in elderly patients is a frequent diagnostic challenge, particularly when routine upper and lower endoscopic evaluations are unrevealing.[1] Among the less commonly recognized causes is the association between aortic stenosis and gastrointestinal angiodysplasia, a clinical entity classically described as Heyde’s syndrome. This syndrome is characterized by the triad of calcific aortic stenosis, bleeding from gastrointestinal angiodysplasia, and an acquired form of von Willebrand disease resulting from high shear stress across the stenotic valve.[2]

The pathophysiology of Heyde’s syndrome involves increased mechanical shear forces across a severely stenotic aortic valve, leading to proteolytic degradation of high-molecular-weight von Willebrand factor (vWF) multimers.[2] These multimers are essential for normal platelet adhesion under high-flow conditions, and their loss results in a bleeding tendency, particularly from fragile vascular lesions such as angiodysplasia.[3] The small intestine, especially the proximal jejunum, is a common site for these lesions and may remain undetected on conventional endoscopic evaluation.[4]

Capsule endoscopy has emerged as a valuable diagnostic tool in patients with obscure gastrointestinal bleeding, enabling the visualization of small-bowel pathology that is inaccessible by standard endoscopy. Identification of angiodysplasia in the setting of severe aortic stenosis should prompt consideration of an underlying acquired vWF abnormality.[5] Importantly, correction of the valvular lesion has been shown to reverse the hematologic defect and significantly reduce or eliminate recurrent bleeding episodes.[6]

We report a case of an elderly male with recurrent melena and transfusion-dependent anemia, in whom the diagnosis of Heyde’s syndrome was established through a combination of echocardiography, capsule endoscopy, and vWF multimer analysis, with complete resolution of bleeding following surgical aortic valve replacement.

CASE REPORT

A 72-year-old male presented with a 6-month history of recurrent episodes of melena accompanied by progressive fatigue and exertional dyspnea. During this period, he required multiple hospital admissions for symptomatic anemia and received several blood transfusions. His medical history was significant for hypertension and well-controlled type 2 diabetes mellitus. There was no history of ischemic heart disease, chronic liver disease, bleeding diathesis, or use of antiplatelet or anticoagulant medications.

On examination, the patient appeared pale but was hemodynamically stable, with a blood pressure of 138/72 mmHg and a regular pulse rate of 78 beats per minute. Cardiovascular examination revealed a harsh systolic ejection murmur best heard at the right second intercostal space with radiation to the carotid arteries. There were no signs of heart failure, and abdominal and systemic examinations were unremarkable. No clinical features suggestive of chronic liver disease or connective tissue disorders were observed.

Laboratory investigations revealed severe anemia, with a hemoglobin level of 7.8 g/dL and a reduced serum ferritin level of 18 ng/mL, consistent with iron-deficiency anemia. Repeated upper gastrointestinal endoscopy and colonoscopy failed to identify a bleeding source [Video 1]. Transthoracic echocardiography with continuous-wave Doppler interrogation demonstrated severe calcific aortic stenosis with restricted aortic valve opening, an aortic valve area of 0.7 cm2, and a mean transvalvular gradient of 45 mmHg, with preserved left ventricular systolic function [Figures 1 and 2].

Further evaluation revealed a reduction in high-molecular-weight vWF multimers, indicating an acquired vWF abnormality. Capsule endoscopy subsequently demonstrated multiple flat, bright-red vascular ectasias with arborizing vessels in the proximal jejunum, consistent with angiodysplasia, along with evidence of recent bleeding. Despite initial management with iron supplementation, blood transfusions, and endoscopic therapy, bleeding persisted. Despite optimal conservative and endoscopic management, the patient continued to experience recurrent bleeding over a period of approximately 6 months, necessitating repeated transfusions and prompting consideration of definitive surgical intervention. The patient therefore underwent surgical aortic valve replacement with a bioprosthetic valve. At the 12-month follow-up, he remained asymptomatic, with complete resolution of gastrointestinal bleeding, normalization of hemoglobin levels, and restoration of the vWF profile.

DISCUSSION

The coexistence of severe aortic stenosis, gastrointestinal bleeding, and angiodysplasia is classically referred to as Heyde’s syndrome. This condition is increasingly recognized in elderly patients presenting with recurrent, unexplained gastrointestinal bleeding, particularly when routine upper gastrointestinal endoscopy and colonoscopy fail to identify a bleeding source.[7] Small-bowel angiodysplasia, most commonly involving the jejunum, is frequently responsible and often requires capsule endoscopy for diagnosis, as demonstrated in the present case.[8]

The underlying pathophysiology is primarily attributed to an acquired von Willebrand syndrome caused by high shear stress across a stenotic aortic valve. This shear stress leads to enhanced proteolysis of high-molecular-weight vWF multimers, which are essential for platelet adhesion under high-flow conditions.[9] The resulting hemostatic defect predisposes patients to bleeding from fragile vascular lesions such as angiodysplasia. Demonstration of reduced high-molecular-weight multimers on electrophoresis provides strong laboratory evidence in support of this mechanism.[10]

Establishing the diagnosis of Heyde’s syndrome requires a combination of clinical suspicion, echocardiographic confirmation of significant aortic stenosis, identification of gastrointestinal angiodysplasia, and evidence of acquired vWF dysfunction.[11] Conventional endoscopic therapies and supportive measures such as transfusions and iron supplementation may offer temporary control but are frequently associated with recurrent bleeding if the valvular pathology remains untreated.[12]

Definitive treatment involves correction of the aortic valve lesion. Both surgical aortic valve replacement and transcatheter aortic valve implantation have been shown to restore vWF multimer distribution, resulting in sustained resolution of gastrointestinal bleeding.[13] In the present case, surgical aortic valve replacement resulted in normalization of hemoglobin levels and the vWF profile, with complete cessation of bleeding during follow-up. This reinforces the importance of early multidisciplinary evaluation and timely valve intervention in patients with suspected Heyde’s syndrome.

CONCLUSION

This case highlights the importance of considering aortic stenosis-related gastrointestinal bleeding in elderly patients with recurrent, unexplained melena and negative conventional endoscopic evaluations. Recognition of the association between severe aortic stenosis, small-bowel angiodysplasia, and acquired vWF deficiency is crucial, as definitive correction of the valvular pathology can lead to resolution of bleeding and hematologic abnormalities. Early multidisciplinary evaluation can help prevent prolonged morbidity and repeated hospitalizations.
