Covalently Immobilized Lignin–Xanthene Conjugates for Persistent Surface Photochemistry and Self‐Sterilizing Interfaces Under Ambient Visible Light
Authors: Euna Oh, Surjith Kumaran, Sumin Han, Kiseok Lee, Hyerin Shin, Seokmin Oh, Dae‐Hong Ko, Hyo‐Jick Choi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSurface‐localized photochemical systems that operate under ambient visible light provide an attractive strategy for persistent self‐sterilizing interfaces. Here, we report a bio‐derived photoactive material platform based on covalently immobilized lignin–Eosin Y conjugates that enable stable, non‐leaching generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) under low‐intensity visible light. Lignin was conjugated with the xanthene dye Eosin Y via benzophenone‐3,3′,4,4′‐tetracarboxylic dianhydride (BTDA) and further functionalized with methacrylate groups to produce a photoactive macromer (LBDRB). Incorporation of LBDRB into a UV‐curable acrylate formulation enabled rapid spray coating and photopolymerization, yielding mechanically robust films on diverse substrates. The resulting surfaces exhibited persistent photoactivity under both indoor lighting and sunlight, achieving efficient inactivation of Gram‐negative and Gram‐positive bacteria as well as enveloped RNA viruses. Notably, antibacterial activity was maintained for over one month and antiviral activity for up to two months, demonstrating long‐term photochemical durability. These results establish lignin–dye macromolecular networks as a scalable, bio‐derived platform for ambient‐light‐driven self‐sterilizing surfaces.

Introduction

Developing safe and effective antimicrobials against emerging and pandemic pathogens requires a universal design platform that can be customized for diverse microorganisms [1]. In recent years, numerous antimicrobial technologies have been developed, including polymers, natural essential oils, peptide‐based agents, quaternary ammonium halides, and metal ion coatings (e.g., copper, silver, titanium/zinc oxides, and quantum dots), which have been applied in various settings to reduce infection risk [2, 3, 4]. Most existing antimicrobial materials rely on direct contact killing mechanisms, requiring pathogens to physically interact with the antimicrobial surface. However, such coatings lack controllable on/off switching of antimicrobial activity, and metal‐ion‐based systems have been reported to suffer from durability limitations and cytotoxicity concerns, restricting their long‐term use. Moreover, the escalating threat of antibiotic‐resistant superbugs emphasizes the urgent need for stable, surface‐bound antimicrobial systems capable of continuously eliminating a broad range of microorganisms [5].

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) play a central role in antimicrobial mechanisms and biological redox processes [6]. They are naturally generated in living organisms and are key intermediates in many physiological and catalytic reactions [7]. For example, silver and copper ions have been reported to inactivate pathogens through mechanisms that include ROS generation and interactions with thiol‐containing biomolecules, leading to oxidative damage of microbial DNA, proteins, and lipids [8, 9, 10]. Various ROS, including hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), hydroxyl radicals (·OH), superoxide anions (O2 −), and singlet oxygen (1O2) are produced during cellular metabolism and can disrupt essential biomolecules when generated in excess [11]. Although living cells possess detoxification mechanisms to control ROS levels, rapid and localized ROS production by antimicrobial agents can overwhelm these systems, leading to microbial inactivation [6]. Thus, oxidative stress induced by ROS generation is widely recognized as a major mechanism of antimicrobial action, making ROS‐generating materials attractive for infection control applications [6].

Synthetic photocatalytic materials such as quantum dots, zinc oxide, titanium dioxide, metal–organic complexes, and photoactive dyes have been explored for ROS‐based antimicrobial coatings for several decades [12]. However, many engineered nanomaterials require high‐intensity UV irradiation to generate ROS and often raise toxicity and environmental safety concerns due to heavy metal accumulation [13]. Compared with many inorganic photocatalytic nanomaterials that may raise concerns related to long‐term metal accumulation and cytotoxicity, lignin‐based materials have been reported to exhibit relatively favorable biocompatibility and low cytotoxicity in various biomedical and environmentally relevant applications [14, 15, 16]. In addition, Eosin Y has been extensively utilized in biological staining, fluorescence imaging, photodynamic therapy, and related biomedical photochemical systems because of its established photochemical functionality and broad applicability in biological applications [17, 18]. These characteristics suggest that lignin–Eosin Y‐based photoactive systems may provide a potentially safer alternative to conventional heavy‐metal‐based photocatalytic antimicrobial coatings. In contrast, visible‐light‐responsive systems offer safer and more energy‐efficient operation, avoiding the hazards of UV exposure and enabling continuous activity in human‐occupied environments [19, 20]. For example, Ag–Pd alloy nanocage coatings were reported to produce surface‐bound ROS that selectively attacked bacterial membranes without harming mammalian cells, showing strong efficacy even against antibiotic‐resistant strains [21]. Similarly, mesoporous silica thin films functionalized with propyl–copper–phosphonate groups exhibited concentration‐dependent ROS generation and antimicrobial activity, demonstrating a correlation between copper functional group density and oxidative bactericidal performance [22]. In another study, organic photosensitizer‐anchored coatings generated singlet oxygen under low‐intensity visible or near‐infrared light, effectively inactivating Gram‐positive and Gram‐negative bacteria [23]. These examples collectively demonstrate the promise of light‐activated ROS coatings, but most rely on inorganic nanoparticles, require high‐intensity irradiation, or lack scalability for practical substrates.

An ideal antimicrobial surface should exhibit broad‐spectrum activity, long‐term stability, minimal cytotoxicity, and controllable activation. It should also maintain transparency and mechanical flexibility for integration into medical devices, personal protective equipment (PPE), and consumer products. Despite extensive research, few systems combine visible‐light photoactivation, biocompatibility, and durable performance under both indoor and outdoor lighting. Previously reported visible‐light‐responsive antimicrobial coatings can be broadly categorized into inorganic photocatalysts and organic photosensitizer‐based systems, both of which exhibit unresolved limitations. Inorganic photocatalysts such as TiO2, ZnO, and doped metal oxides have been extensively investigated for antimicrobial applications; however, their antimicrobial efficacy is typically achieved under ultraviolet irradiation or relatively high‐intensity visible light [19, 24, 25]. As a result, ROS generation under low‐intensity indoor lighting remains limited, and practical development raises concerns related to long‐term photostability, material aging, and the need to avoid prolonged UV exposure in human occupied environments [19, 24, 25] Even when bandgap engineering or dopant strategies are employed to extend photoresponse into the visible range, multiple studies have shown that ROS generation efficiency under realistic indoor illumination conditions remains limited [24, 26]. Organic photosensitizer‐based coatings, including porphyrins, phthalocyanines, and xanthene dyes, enable direct activation under visible light without relying on metal ion‐mediated antimicrobial mechanisms [27, 28, 29]. However, in most reported dye‐based photodynamic antimicrobial coatings, the photosensitizers are either freely dispersed or physically entrapped within polymer matrices. While such systems can generate ROS under controlled illumination, weak dye‐matrix interactions often result in dye leaching, rapid photobleaching, and a strong dependence on high‐intensity light sources [27, 29, 30]. These limitations collectively restrict long‐term antimicrobial performance and scalability on practical substrates, particularly beyond model surfaces such as glass or silicon wafers [27, 29, 30]. Consequently, achieving low‐light activation, long‐term stability, minimal cytotoxicity, and compatibility with scalable coating processes and practical substrates within a single system remains challenging [27, 29, 31].

Although microbial infections have been largely controlled over the last few decades, maintaining proper hygiene and preventing pathogen transmission remain global priorities [32]. The recent COVID‐19 pandemic and the emergence of respiratory pathogens such as respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) and influenza (H1N1) have revealed the vulnerability of human populations to novel infectious agents [33, 34, 35]. Furthermore, the rapid evolution of antimicrobial resistance through horizontal gene transfer exacerbates these risks [36]. Pathogens can survive for extended periods on high‐touch surfaces, including those found in hospitals, transportation, homes, and public facilities, facilitating transmission via direct contact or aerosols [37]. Contaminated surfaces and bioaerosols can persist even after standard cleaning, posing serious risks of infection and mortality [38]. Regular cleaning and disinfection remain the primary mitigation strategies, but they depend heavily on human compliance and cannot fully eliminate persistent pathogens [39]. In contrast, antimicrobial coatings provide passive, self‐disinfecting surfaces that continuously inactivate microorganisms without user intervention [40]. The COVID‐19 pandemic revealed the importance of such non‐pharmaceutical interventions, particularly in healthcare and community settings [41]. Moreover, the integration of antimicrobial coatings into reusable PPE such as masks, aprons, gloves, and face shields can enhance infection prevention while simultaneously addressing environmental challenges associated with single‐use waste [42, 43]. Beyond infection control, these technologies align with global sustainability goals by reducing plastic and biomedical waste generated during pandemics.

Among bio‐based materials, lignin has emerged as a sustainable platform for photoactive coatings [44]. Lignin, an aromatic biopolymer abundantly found in plant biomass, exhibits intrinsic UV absorption and contains phenolic and carboxylic functional groups that enable facile chemical modification [45, 46]. Recent reports have demonstrated the potential of lignin‐derived materials in antimicrobial coatings. For instance, Boarino et al. reported a lignin‐modified phenolic and benzaldehyde‐based antiviral coating that generated ROS under visible light and remained stable for over six months [44]. Similarly, Morena et al. developed lignin nanoparticles loaded with porphyrin photosensitizers, which produced singlet oxygen (1O2) under LED illumination and effectively killed Staphylococcus aureus, as confirmed by TEM imaging of cell‐wall damage [47]. Moreover, Etxeberria et al. demonstrated lignin–alkyd resin coatings incorporating thymol and hop resins with strong antibacterial and antiviral activity against Escherichia coli, S. aureus, and influenza A (H3N2) [48]. These studies demonstrate the versatility of modified lignin as a bio‐derived scaffold for sustainable antimicrobial coatings [44, 47, 48]. However, in many reported lignin‐based antimicrobial systems, lignin primarily functions as a structural matrix or carrier for active agents, while antimicrobial activity arises from passive chemical interactions or embedded additives rather than from an actively switchable lignin‐centered photoresponse [44, 47, 48]. Beyond its structural role in such a coating system, non‐functional lignin is known to exhibit intrinsic antioxidant and radical‐scavenging functionalities arising from its phenolic chemical structure [49]. These properties have been extensively characterized and are distinct from those of conventional synthetic polymer matrices commonly used in photoactive antimicrobial coatings [27, 49]. As a result, lignin‐based antimicrobial systems reported to date encompass materials in which structural support and radical‐scavenging behavior coexist within the same polymeric framework [48, 49].

To address these challenges, we developed a universal antimicrobial coating system based on a covalently integrated lignin‐dye photosensitizer design that generates ROS under low‐intensity visible light in both indoor and outdoor conditions. Light is a universal, inexpensive, and renewable energy source, making light‐activated antimicrobial coating an attractive strategy for continuous surface disinfection. The coating is composed of lignin covalently conjugated with Eosin Y dye through a benzophenone linker, forming a stable and photoactivatable polymer matrix. In this design, lignin is not merely a passive structural component but is covalently integrated with Eosin Y to form a unified photoresponsive architecture. Upon light exposure, the coating produces surface‐bound ROS that inactivate representative Gram‐positive and Gram‐negative bacteria as well as clinically important enveloped viruses. The polymer can be rapidly spray‐coated and UV‐cured on nonwoven fabrics and glass substrates, maintaining activity under both indoor and sunlight conditions. Such materials are widely employed in healthcare, consumer, and industrial applications where durable and light‐activated antimicrobial performance is desirable [13, 19, 43]. Incorporating the lignin–Eosin Y coating onto diverse surface types therefore expands its applicability to next‐generation self‐sterilizing soft materials that combine mechanical flexibility with long‐term visible‐light antimicrobial functionality. This visible‐light–activated, lignin‐conjugated Eosin Y‐based coating provides a sustainable, potentially biocompatible, and user‐independent approach for continuous surface disinfection. By combining bio‐derived materials with photoresponsive chemistry, this system offers a scalable solution for infection prevention in healthcare, food, and community environments, supporting the next generation of safe and eco‐friendly antimicrobial technologies.

Materials and Methods

Chemicals and Materials

All chemicals were purchased from Aldrich Canada (Oakville, ON, Canada) or purchased from Fisher Scientific Canada and were used as received unless otherwise stated. All reactions were conducted under a nitrogen environment. Reagents including pentaerythritol tetraacrylate, 2‐hydroxy‐2‐methylpropiophenone, glycidyl methacrylate, alumina beads (inhibitor removal resin), dimethyl formamide, anhydrous tetrahydrofuran (THF), softwood Kraft lignin powder, and 3,3’,4,4’‐Benzophenone tetracarboxylic dianhydride (BTDA) were purchased from Sigma–Aldrich Canada. Surgical masks (EPIC, 40578‐RS‐5), acetone, diethyl ether, ethanol, magnesium sulfate, Eosin Y, and Whatman qualitative filter paper (No. 4) were purchased from Fisher Scientific (Pittsburgh, PA, USA). Prior to use, glycidyl methacrylate and penaerythritol tetraacrylate were passed through inhibitor removal resin. Ebecryl 8409, an aliphatic acrylate monomer, was purchased from Allnex Canada Inc. (Etobicoke, ON). UV crosslinking was performed using a RealUV‐LED system (Waveform lighting; Vancouver, WA, USA). Inner spunbond polypropylene fabrics (∼ 22 g/m2) were collected from three‐ply surgical masks to evaluate the universal applicability of the antimicrobial coatings.

NMR Analysis

1H NMR spectra of the synthesized light‐responsive monomer were characterized using a Varian VNMRS 600 MHz spectrometer equipped with a Z‐axis pulsed‐field‐gradient triple‐resonance probe. Samples were prepared by dissolving the synthesized polymer in deuterated dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO). 50 mg of polymer was transferred to an NMR tube, followed by the addition of 0.7 mL of deuterated DMSO (d6 ), and the mixture was gently heated to obtain a clear solution. Chemical shifts (δ) are reported in ppm relative to the residual solvent signal (DMSO‐d 6: 2.50 ppm). Spectra were acquired for 64 scans, a relaxation delay of 5 sec, and an acquisition time of 5.4 sec. The resulting FID files were baseline‐corrected and converted to ASCII files using MestReNova software.

FTIR Analysis

Attenuated total reflection Fourier transform infrared (ATR‐FTIR) analysis was collected using a Bio‐Rad FTS Varian 7000 spectrometer equipped with a ZnSe IRE detector. Measurements were performed with p‐polarized radiation (ZnSe wire grid polarisers) incident at 75°. Spectra were recorded over the range of 4000 to 500 cm−1 at a resolution of 16 cm−1, using 100 scans to improve the signal‐to‐noise ratio. All spectra are presented in the % transmittance form. The background spectra were recorded prior to analysis. Solid polymer samples (3‐4 mg) or polymer‐grafted polyethylene were dried under high vacuum for 2 h and placed on the FTIR‐ATR diamond crystal for analysis. To monitor the time‐dependent UV crosslinking process, a mixture of monomers and cross‐linker containing a photo‐initiator (without mask and UV absorbance additives) was placed directly onto the ATR crystal. The sample was irradiated with UV light for predetermined time intervals, and FTIR spectra were recorded after each exposure to evaluate the progression of the crosslinking reaction. For mask samples, 1 × 1 cm2 sections of the fabric, collected before and after application of the light‐responsive antimicrobial LBDRB coating, were positioned on the FTIR‐ATR diamond crystal for analyzed under the same conditions.

UV–Vis Spectroscopy Analysis

In situ diffuse reflectance spectra were collected using a PerkinElmer Lambda 1050 spectrophotometer equipped with a Universal reflectance accessory. Measurements were performed using an InGaAs detector with a slit width of 2 nm, an integration time of 0.2 s, and a scan range of 250 to 400 nm. A BaSO4 white reflectance standard (Kodak, USA) was used to record the baseline at 22°C in the Praying Mantis cell prior to sample analysis.

Fluorescence Spectroscopy Analysis

Fluorescence measurements were carried out using a PTI QM40 Fluorescence Spectrophotometer. Emission spectra were recorded from 400 to 700 nm with an excitation wavelength of 510 nm and a slit width of 2.5 nm. All measurements were performed using a standard 10 mm path length cuvette and a scan rate of 600 nm s−1. Fluorescence quantum yields were calibrated using established standards, including quinine hemisulfate salt monohydrate (λex = 350 nm, λem = 446 nm) and harmane in H2SO4 (λex = 355 nm, λem = 450 nm).

SEM Analysis

The surface morphology of the face mask fabrics was analyzed in secondary electron mode using Hitachi S‐3000N scanning electron microscope (Hitachi, Toronto, Canada) operated at an acceleration voltage of 15 kV and 81 µA, with a working distance of 15 cm. Samples were cut into 1 × 1 cm2 pieces, mounted on a carbon tape, and coated with a 10 nm gold a multi‐cathode sputter deposition system manufactured by Desk II sputter coating system (Denton Vacuum Inc. Moorestown, NJ) operated at 1 mTorr prior to analysis.

Contact Angle Measurement

The wetting properties of the mask fabrics were characterized by measuring static contact angle using the sessile drop technique. Before and after coating, the light‐responsive antimicrobial mask fabrics were mounted onto a microscope glass slide (3ʺ × 1ʺ × 0.04ʺ) using plastic tape. A 5 µL droplet of deionized (DI) water was applied to the fabric surface with a syringe needle. Contact angle images were acquired and analyzing using an FTA‐200 (First Ten Angstroms; Newark, CA, USA). A minimum of five replicates was measured for each sample.

Adhesion Test

The adhesion performance of the LBDRB coating was evaluated using the cross‐cut tape test in accordance with ASTM D3359. Briefly, the coated surface was allowed to cure under ambient conditions for 12 h prior to testing. A lattice pattern consisting of six parallel cuts spaced 1 mm apart in two perpendicular directions was made through the coating using a sharp multi‐blade cutter (Part No. CNA420, CGOLDENWALL, Canada), producing 25 uniformly sized squares. The spacing between adjacent cuts was selected based on the coating thickness in accordance with ASTM D3359. After any detached particles were removed with a soft brush, a pressure‐sensitive adhesive tape (3 m clear tape) was firmly applied over the grid and smoothed to ensure intimate contact. The tape was then removed rapidly at an angle of approximately 180°. The extent of coating removal was visually assessed and classified according to the ASTM D3359 rating scale.

HPLC Analysis

To evaluate the potential leaching of unreacted monomers and other residual species, high‐performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) analysis was performed on UV‐cured LBDRB‐coated masks. Residual components were assessed by extracting the cured coatings and analyzing the extracts for unreacted monomers, photoinitiator residues, and solvent traces. LBDRB‐coated mask samples (2.4 cm2) were immersed in 1 mL of deionized (DI) water or ethanol for 30 min. The resulting extracts were analyzed using an Agilent 1100 Infinity HPLC System (Santa Clara, CA, USA) equipped with a UV detector, and absorbance was monitored over the wavelength range of 200–350 nm. Separation was carried out using an Agilent ZORBAX SB‐C18 column (4.6 mm × 150 mm) maintained at 22°C. The mobile phase consisted of acetonitrile and methanol delivered at a flow rate of 1.0 mL min− 1. Each extract was analyzed in triplicate to assess repeatability, and three independent samples were prepared for each extraction condition. The reported values represent the mean of the replicate measurements.

Molecular Orbitals and Computational Calculation

The molecular orbitals of the dye‐functionalized chemicals were generated using the molecular modeling software Molden (Version 6). Density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed using the B3LYP hybrid functional with the split‐valence polarized 6–311G(d,p) basis set for all heavy atoms and hydrogens. The structures were optimized without symmetry constraints and checked for imaginary frequencies through vibrational frequency analysis. Calculations were performed in both gas‐phase (vacuum) and water, modeled with a dielectric constant ε = 78.36. Molecular structures and orbitals were visualized using the OpenGL version of Molden 6 visualization software. To determine the local excitation (LE) and charge transfer excitation (CT), the HOMO‐HOMO−4 and LUMO‐LUMO+4 occupied molecular orbitals were plotted to illustrate the donor and acceptor orbitals in the main DFT excitations.

Mask Filtration Performance

The filtration efficiency and breathing resistance of the LBDRB‐coated surgical mask were evaluated using a Filter Media Test Rig PMFT 1000 m (Palas, Germany), following the procedures outlined in ASTM F2299 for filtration efficiency and EN 149:2001+A1:2009 for breathing resistance measurements. Penetration testing was performed using sodium chloride (2% in DI water) and paraffin oil aerosol with particle sizes ranging from ca. 0.14 to 4 µm after 3 min of aerosol exposure. Pressure drop or breath resistance was measured by passing compressed air through the filter at flow rates of 30, 90, and 160 L/min, simulating inhalation to extreme exhalation conditions defined in EN149 standard. Results were expressed in pascals (Pa). The dimension of the samples measured was 100 cm2, and all measurements were conducted at 22°C and 5% relative humidity (RH).

Light Irradiation Conditions

The LBDRB‐coated mask samples were exposed to indoor light conditions using a Philips 479626 F32T8/941/ALTO 4' Fluorescent T8 Lamp (32 W, 2600 Lumens, 4100K). Illumination power density was measured with a Thorlabs PM100D power meter (Thorlabs, NJ, USA), capable of monitoring light intensity across the visible spectral range (380–760 nm). Two light sources were considered to reflect practical application environments: average sunlight (200 mW/cm2) to represent outdoor conditions, and indoor lighting (1.7 mW/cm2, 22°C, 30–40% relative humidity (RH)) to represent enclosed or indoor settings. Indoor light irradiation consisted of an 8‐h light exposure phase, followed by dark storage at 22°C by placing the samples in aluminum‐foil‐covered Petri dishes. The average illumination power density was continuously monitored throughout the irradiation period. A second set of samples was exposed to natural outdoor sunlight, encompassing the full terrestrial solar spectrum (UV–visible–near‐infrared, approximately 300–2500 nm), with typical irradiance conditions from 9:00 AM to 5:00 PM during the summer season. Outdoor ambient temperature, cloud conditions, and relative humidity were not actively controlled and followed natural diurnal variations. Exposure cycles of the coated masks were repeated for the specified test duration, with samples stored in aluminum‐foil‐covered Petri dishes during non‐irradiation periods.

Synthesis of Lignin Methacrylate Functionalized 2‐(2,4,5,7‐Tetrabromo‐6‐Oxido‐3‐oxo‐3H‐Xanthen‐9‐yl)Benzoate Benzophenone ‐3,3′,4,4′‐Tetracarboxylate (LBDRB)

5 g of dried softwood Kraft lignin powder (containing 3.6 mmole of hydroxyl group per g of lignin) dissolved in 70 mL of DMF solution under a N2 atmosphere was added to the first reaction mixture, stirred at 50°C for 15 min to completely dissolve the lignin. 6.4 g (20 mmole) of Benzophenone‐3,3′,4,4′‐tetracarboxylic dianhydride, 13 g (20 mmole) of 2,4,5,7‐tetrabromo‐6‐oxido‐3‐oxo‐3H‐xanthen‐9‐yl, and 1.4 g (10 mmole) of glycidyl methacrylate were then added to the first reaction mixture, stirred at 50°C for 12 h. The reaction flask was also covered with aluminum foil to prevent light exposure. After completion, the crude reaction mixture was poured slowly into 300 mL of acetone with continuous stirring, resulting in the formation of a pink‐brown precipitate. The precipitated product was collected by filtration using grade 4 Whatman filter paper and washed with an additional 200 mL of acetone. Further purification was achieved by repeated precipitation (i.e., dissolving in DMF and precipitation in acetone for 3 times). The solvent was removed under high vacuum to obtain brown colored lignin‐conjugated methyl (glycidyl methacrylate) (LBDRB). The purified product was dried under vacuum for 3 h and stored at 4°C in an amber bottle.

Fabrication of the UV‐Cured Lignin‐conjugated Eosin Y Antimicrobials Coating on Mask and Glass

In a 5 mL Eppendorf tube, 2 mL of polymerization solution containing 95 w/w% Ebecryl 4883 (monomer), 4.9 w/w% of pentaerythritol tetraacrylate (cross‐linker), and 0.1 w/w% hydroxy‐2‐methylpropiophenone (UV initiator) were mixed altogether in 5 µL of DMF. Subsequently, 750 mg of LBDRB dissolved in 95 µL DMF was added, and the mixture was vortexed for 2 min. The obtained solution was then purged with nitrogen (10 mL/min) for 2 min to remove any dissolved oxygen. Light‐responsive antimicrobial coating was conducted as described in a previous report [51]. Briefly, PP mask fabrics were cut into circular pieces (3 cm radius), and glass 2.7 × 7 cm2 were spray coated using a coating solution containing 75 mg of LBDRB antimicrobial in 1 mL DMF. Spray coating using a custom‐made spray‐coating system (coated at a height of 15 cm) equipped with an atomizing nozzle (Spraying system co, Illinois, USA), operated with compressed nitrogen gas (NI‐5.0 UH‐T, Praxair Inc, Canada) at 40 psi and a spray solution flow rate of ∼15 mL/s. Excess coating solution on mask samples was removed by applying a vacuum (10 In Hg) for 10 sec, while no vacuum was applied for glass or other flat substrates. The LBDRB antimicrobial infused spunbond mask fabrics were then placed on the custom‐made chamber RealUV‐LED (Waveform lighting; Vancouver, WA, USA) and irradiated with 360 nm LED UV light (power density 6 mW/cm2) for 30 sec on each side. After curing, the coated fabrics were washed 3 times with 1 mL of ethanol followed by 1 mL of DI water, then dried at 37°C for 12 h to obtain the final LBDRB‐coated materials.

Pathogen Strains and Preparation

Frozen stocks of Klebsiella pneumoniae (ATCC BAA‐1705) and methicillin‐resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA; ATCC 33591) were streaked onto Mueller–Hinton agar (MH agar; BD, Sparks, MD, USA) and incubated overnight at 37°C. A single colony was inoculated into 3 mL of Mueller–Hinton broth (MH broth; BD, Sparks, MD, USA) and incubated at 37°C for 16 h with shaking at 200 rpm. Subsequently, 2 mL of each overnight culture was transferred into 198 mL of fresh MH broth and incubated at 37°C with shaking (200 rpm) until the early exponential growth phase (approximately 4–5 h). The cultures were then centrifuged at 10 000 rpm for 15 min at 4°C. The supernatant was discarded, and the pellets were washed three times with phosphate‐buffered saline (1×PBS, pH 7.4) and resuspended in 3 mL of autoclaved DI water. Viral strains used in this study included influenza A virus H1N1 (Puerto Rico/8/34, PR8) and human coronavirus OC43 (HCoV‐OC43). Viral propagation and preparation followed procedures described in our previous report [50]. Briefly, PR8 was inoculated into 10‐day‐old embryonated chicken eggs, and viral particles were harvested from the allantoic fluid. The collected fluid was filtered through a 0.8 µm filter and centrifuged at 30 000 rpm for 1 h at 10°C. The resulting pellet was resuspended in autoclaved DI water and stored at 4°C until use. For HCoV‐OC43 (ATCC VR‐1558), infection was carried out in MRC‐5 human fetal lung fibroblast cells (ATCC CCL‐171) at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 0.01. MRC‐5 cells were maintained in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and antibiotics (100 U/mL penicillin and 100 µg/mL streptomycin; Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 33°C under 5% CO2. Prior to viral infection, the cells were washed twice with Dulbecco's phosphate‐buffered saline (1×DPBS), and the virus was inoculated in DMEM containing antibiotics (100 U/mL penicillin and 100 µg/mL streptomycin), and 1 µg/mL L‐(tosylamido‐2‐phenyl) ethyl chloromethyl ketone (TPCK)‐treated trypsin (Sigma–Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). Infected cells were incubated at 33°C for 5 days under 5% CO2. The virus‐containing supernatant was collected by centrifugation at 30 000 rpm for 1 h at 10°C, and the pellet was resuspended in 1 mL of autoclaved DI water and stored at 4°C until use.

Bacterial/Viral Inactivation Test

For bacterial inactivation experiments, K. pneumoniae suspensions were prepared at a final concentration of 5 × 106 CFU/mL in autoclaved DI water and tested on LBDRB‐coated materials, including mask fabric and glass substrates. MRSA suspension was prepared at the same concentration and tested on LBDRB‐coated glass substrate. For each assay, a total of 20 µL (applied as five 4‐µL droplets) of the bacterial suspension was deposited onto the surface of LBDRB‐coated materials. The samples were incubated at room temperature (RT) for 5, 10, or 30 min under either illuminated or dark conditions. For illuminated conditions, samples were exposed to indoor light at an intensity of 1.7 mW/cm2, while dark controls were covered with aluminum foil. Following incubation, the treated samples were rinsed with 1 × PBS (pH 7.4), and the collected bacterial suspensions were centrifuged at 14 000 rpm for 15 min at 4°C. The resulting pellet was resuspended in DI water, and total protein concentration was quantified using the Micro BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). For viability assessment, 5 µL of appropriately diluted bacterial suspension was spotted onto Mueller–Hinton agar and incubated at 37°C for 16 h. Colony‐forming units (CFU) were subsequently enumerated to determine bacterial viability. For viral inactivation tests, influenza A virus (A/PR/8/34 H1N1, PR8) and human coronavirus OC43 (HCoV‐OC43) suspensions were adjusted to a protein concentration of 1 mg/mL in DI water. A total of 20 µL (five 4‐µL droplets) of each viral suspension was applied onto the LBDRB‐coated mask fabrics and incubated for 5, 10, or 30 min under either light (1.7 mW/cm2) or dark (aluminum‐foil‐covered) conditions. After incubation, the samples were washed with 1×PBS (pH 7.4) and centrifuged at 14 000 rpm for 15 min at 4°C. The viral pellets were resuspended in 200 µL of DI water, and viral RNA was extracted using the Viral RNA Purification Kit (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. RNA concentration was measured with a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (ND‐1000; Thermo Fisher Scientific), and all samples were normalized to equal RNA concentrations. Complementary DNA (cDNA) synthesis was performed using the High‐Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA) following the manufacturer's protocol. Quantitative PCR (qPCR) analysis was conducted using SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Applied Biosystems) on a 7500 Fast Real‐Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems). The primer sequences used for amplification were obtained from our previous publication [50].

TCID50 Infectivity Assay

The infectivity of influenza A virus H1N1 PR8 following exposure to LBDRB‐coated samples was evaluated using a median tissue culture infectious dose (TCID50) assay based on the Reed–Muench method. MDCK cells were maintained in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and antibiotic solution and seeded into 96‐well plates at a density of 105 cells/well. Cells were incubated for 24 h at 37°C under 5% CO2 until approximately 80–90% confluency was reached. PR8 suspensions were exposed to bare and LBDRB‐coated samples under visible‐light irradiation or dark conditions for 5, 10, and 30 min. Following exposure, viral suspensions were recovered and serially diluted in serum‐free DMEM supplemented with TPCK‐treated trypsin using 10‐fold dilution steps. Diluted viral samples were subsequently added to MDCK cell monolayers and incubated for 4 days at 37°C under 5% CO2. Following incubation, culture media were removed, and cells were stained using 0.2% crystal violet solution to evaluate virus‐induced cytopathic effects (CPE). TCID50/mL values were calculated using the Reed–Muench method.

Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS) Analysis

ROS generation from LBDRB coatings was evaluated under the same visible‐light irradiation conditions used for the antibacterial and antiviral experiments. Singlet oxygen generation was analyzed using Singlet Oxygen Sensor Green (SOSG, Thermo Fisher Scientific), while hydroxyl radical generation was evaluated using hydroxyphenyl fluorescein (HPF, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Hydrogen peroxide generation was quantified using an Amplex Red Hydrogen Peroxide/Peroxidase Assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The LBDRB exhibited intrinsic coloration and background fluorescence. Fluorescence signals obtained from LBDRB without ROS probes were separately measured and subtracted from experimental measurements to minimize LBDRB‐associated background interference. Fluorescence intensity was measured following visible‐light exposure for 5, 10, and 30 min, and dark condition samples were analyzed under identical conditions without irradiation. Intracellular oxidative stress induced by visible‐light irradiated LBDRB coatings was further evaluated using CM‐H2DCFDA‐loaded K. pneumoniae cells. Briefly, K. pneumoniae suspensions were adjusted to OD600 = 0.5 and incubated with 5 µM CM‐H2DCFDA (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 37°C for 30 min. Following dye loading, bacterial cells were washed with PBS to remove excess extracellular dye and subsequently exposed to coated samples under visible‐light irradiation and dark conditions. Fluorescence intensity corresponding to intracellular oxidative stress was measured after 5, 10, and 30 min exposure.

Statistical Analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate unless otherwise stated. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics, version 29 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). For selected experiments involving multiple factors or repeated measures, generalized linear models (GLMs) were applied. A p‐value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results and Discussion

Synthesis and Chemical Characterization of LBDRB

As illustrated in the synthetic scheme in Figure 1a, LBDRB was obtained via esterification of alkali lignin with Eosin Y and glycidyl methacrylate in the presence of the reactive coupling agent benzophenone‐3,3′,4,4′‐tetracarboxylic dianhydride (BTDA). The dianhydride‐mediated coupling facilitates covalent linkage between the dye and lignin, introducing flexible ester linkages, structural irregularity, and localized π‐conjugated domains. These features are expected to enhance photoinduced electron transfer and promote efficient ROS generation [51]. Eosin Y, serving as the photoactive component within the lignin‐based coating, was conjugated to the lignin backbone following a previously reported procedure developed by our research group [35]. Chemical modification of lignin generally proceeds through two principal pathways that exploit the reactivity of its phenolic moieties [52]. Electrophilic aromatic substitution enables functionalization at free ortho positions activated by electron‐donating phenolic ─OH groups, while base‐catalyzed deprotonation generates phenolate species that can react with nucleophiles to form phenolic ethers or esters [53, 54].

Although lignin inherently possesses a conjugated aromatic structure, conjugation with relatively photostable chromophores can significantly expand its potential scope, particularly for including applications in optical materials [55]. In this context, Eosin Y, a tetrabrominated xanthene dye exhibiting strong absorption in the green–yellow region and intense fluorescence, was selected as an effective photosensitizer for photocatalysis, chemical sensing, and biological imaging [56]. The successful synthesis of lignin‐conjugated BTDA (LBTDA) precursor was confirmed by 1H NMR (Figure 1b). The 1H NMR signals at δ 8.56, 8.46, 8.41, represent aromatic peaks related to benzophenone, peaks at 7.68, 7.61, 7.46, and 7.06 correspond to aromatic protons of lignin. Additional peaks at δ 4.02, 3.62, 2.09, and 2.04 are attributed to methoxy groups in ester linkages, while the resonance at δ 1.78 arises from lignin carbonyl‐adjacent protons. The peaks at δ 1.29 and 0.87 correspond to lignin aliphatic C─H groups. Together, these signals confirm successful LBTDA functionalization of lignin. The FTIR spectrum (Figure 1c) of LBTDA displays the characteristic vibrational signatures of its aromatic biopolymer structure. A broad O─H stretching band near ∼3400 cm− 1 reflects phenolic and aliphatic hydroxyl groups engaged in extensive hydrogen bonding, while C─H stretching from methyl and methylene units appears around ∼2930 cm− 1. The aromatic backbone is marked by strong absorptions at ∼1600 and ∼1510 cm− 1, corresponding to guaiacyl and syringyl ring vibrations, with additional peaks at ∼1260 and ∼1125 cm− 1 arising from C─O stretching of aryl–alkyl ethers and secondary alcohols. Following esterification with benzophenone‐3,3′,4,4′‐tetracarboxylic dianhydride (BTDA), the FTIR spectrum (Figure 1c) shows the O─H band decreases in intensity, indicating consumption of hydroxyl groups, while new carbonyl absorptions at ∼1720– 735 cm− 1 confirm formation of ester linkages via anhydride ring opening. Weak features near ∼1780 cm− 1 suggest traces of unreacted anhydride.

Covalent incorporation of Eosin Y into lignin via esterification of phenolic (Figure 1d) and aliphatic hydroxyl groups introduces the dye chromophore directly within the lignin backbone. This modification significantly alters the electronic structure of lignin by introducing extended π‐conjugation and dye‐derived electronic states, while simultaneously reducing its intrinsic polarity and structural heterogeneity. The combined functionalization with Eosin Y and methyl methacrylate enhances lignin solubility by reducing intermolecular hydrogen bonding. Methacrylate chemistry has previously been employed to functionalize the hydroxyl groups of lignin, as lignin exhibits a substantial reduction in ─OH content accompanied by the appearance of methacrylate groups at the modified hydroxyl sites [50]. Here, esterifying lignin's phenolic (Figure 2a) and aliphatic hydroxyl groups with glycidyl methacrylate introduces monomer functionalities while allowing its covalent incorporation into the poly methyl methacrylate network during UV‐cured free‐radical polymerization. In the present system, covalent conjugation of Eosin Y into lignin as an integral component of the photoresponsive antimicrobial architecture rather than solely as a passive structural matrix. Fabrication, Structural Characterization, and Photochemical Mechanism of LBDRB Antimicrobial Coatings

To confirm UV‐induced photo‐polymerization, FTIR spectra were recorded (Figure S1a) as a function of irradiation time for a DMF solution containing LBDRB, PETTA (cross‐linker), Ebecryl 8409 (monomer), and 2‐hydroxy‐2‐methylpropiophenone as the photoinitiator. The decrease in the methacrylate C═C stretching band (Figure S1b) at 1640–1610 cm− 1 was monitored to evaluate the progress of crosslinking. Upon UV irradiation, the intensity of this band gradually decreased and nearly disappeared, indicating extensive consumption of the reactive double bonds and formation of a cross‐linked polymer network. The degree of conversion was calculated from the integrated peak areas using the standard area‐based method and was determined to be 91.34%. The small residual signal was attributed to overlap between aromatic C═C vibrations and the methacrylate C═C absorption band.

To investigate light‐activated antimicrobial coatings as an alternative to release‐based systems, lignin‐based dye–radical‐bearing (LBDRB) antimicrobial coatings were fabricated and characterized. The covalent conjugation of Eosin Y to the lignin backbone enables the formation of an integrated photoactive system, in which ROS generation arises from the lignin‐photosensitizer architecture rather than from a freely dispersed dye molecule. The LBDRB formulations were designed to form covalently crosslinked polymer networks under UV irradiation, enabling stable immobilization of photoactive lignin‐dye conjugates on solid substrates. The coatings were applied to both glass and polypropylene spun‐bond (SB) mask fabrics to evaluate their compatibility with flat and fibrous surfaces commonly used in protective materials. Figure 2a illustrates the schematic of fabrication process of lignin‐based dye ‐radical‐bearing (LBDRB) antimicrobial coatings using a spray‐assisted UV‐curable system. A uniform precursor solution containing functional monomers (including LBDRB), a multifunctional crosslinker, and a photoinitiator was spray‐applied at a controlled pressure of 80 psi, yielding consistent deposition across both fibrous and flat substrates. Subsequent UV irradiation (365 nm, 60 s) generated reactive radicals that initiated polymerization and crosslinking, forming a robust, covalently bonded coating layer.

Spray‐based LBDRB antimicrobial coatings were successfully fabricated on SB mask fabrics and glass substrates using a UV‐curable acrylate system. The LBDRB‐coated both glass and spunbond mask samples exhibited pronounced absorption bands corresponding to carbonyl (C═O) stretching, ether (C─O─C) stretching, and methylene (─CH2–) groups, indicating successful acrylate polymerization and effective crosslinking of the lignin‐based antimicrobial components. Chemical incorporation of LBDRB into the crosslinked polymer network was confirmed by FTIR spectroscopy. As Figure 2b of bare SB layer (I ×1) FTIR stretching peaks indicated 2959 cm− 1 (asymmetric CH3), 2906 cm− 1 (asymmetric CH2), 2846 cm− 1 (asymmetric CH), 1453 cm− 1 (symmetric bending CH3), and 1173 cm− 1 (rocking CH3), confirmed a typical polypropylene‐based SB mask. LBDRB‐coated SB (ILBDRB x1), the FTIR stretching peaks included 3477 cm− 1 (O─H stretching), 2984–2881 cm− 1 (aromatic, CH2, and C─H stretching), 1723 cm− 1 (C═O), 1629 cm− 1 (aromatic C═C), and 1472 cm− 1 (C─O). Additional bands at 1283, 1196, and 1109 cm− 1 correspond to C─O─C linkages, while peaks at 997 and 807 cm− 1 are attributed to C─H out‐of‐plane vibrations. These features collectively verify the formation of the modified lignin‐based coating.

Surface morphology and wetting properties of the LBDRB‐coated SB mask PP fabrics were evaluated using SEM imaging and water contact angle measurements (Figure 3). SEM images of the uncoated SB fabrics (Figure 3a; (i) low magnification and (ii) high magnification) revealed the characteristic smooth and continuous fiber morphology typical of spun‐bond polypropylene (SB‐PP, I×1) materials. In contrast, the SB fabrics coated with the light‐responsive LBDRB layer (ILBDRBx1) exhibited (Figure 3b; (i) low magnification and (ii) high magnification) a conformal coating distributed along the fiber surfaces, with visible deposited spray droplets, indicating successful formation of the antimicrobial coating without apparent fiber fusion or pore blockage. The incorporation of LBDRB resulted in increased surface roughness of the SB fibers, consistent with the presence of a deposited polymeric layer. Despite the added coating, the fibrous structure of the SB substrate remained intact, demonstrating good interfacial compatibility and stable integration between the LBDRB coating and the PP fibers. Surface wettability was further examined by static water contact angle (WCA) measurements (Figure 3c). The unmodified SB‐PP fabric (I×1) exhibited a high contact angle of 116° ± 3° (Figure 3c‐i), reflecting the inherently hydrophobic nature of polypropylene. After LBDRB coating (ILBDRB×1), the water contact angle decreased to 69° ± 6° (Figure 3c‐ii), indicating a partial reduction in hydrophobicity upon incorporation of the lignin‐dye conjugates. This change in wettability is attributed to the presence of hydroxyl and carboxylate functional groups in the lignin–conjugated Eosin Y components of the LBDRB coating, which contrast with the aliphatic hydrocarbon backbone of polypropylene.

The durability of the LBDRB coating deposited on glass substrates was evaluated by assessing both coating adhesion and the potential release of residual components. Adhesion was determined using the cross‐cut tape test (Figure S2a) in accordance with ASTM D3359. A 1 mm lattice pattern was cut through the coating, followed by application and rapid removal of pressure‐sensitive adhesive tape. No visible coating detachment was observed after tape removal, corresponding to the highest ASTM classification (5B), which indicates excellent adhesion between the coating and the glass substrate. The potential for leaching of unreacted monomers, photoinitiator residues, or solvent traces was investigated by high‐performance liquid chromatography (HPLC). UV‐cured LBDRB coatings (sample size 2.4 cm2; n = 3, mean ± SD) deposited on the mask were immersed separately in water and ethanol for 30 min, and the resulting extracts were analyzed under the same conditions used for previously reported LBDRB polymer coatings. No detectable peaks associated with residual species were observed in the chromatograms (Figure S2b), indicating negligible release of extractable components. Considering the analytical limit of detection of 0.2 ng cm− 2, these results demonstrate that the UV‐cured coating was effectively cross‐linked and did not exhibit measurable leaching under the conditions tested.

Photographic images (Figure 3d‐i,ii) of the inner SB mask layer before (I×1) and after LBDRB coating (ILBDRB×1) further confirm uniform coating coverage without visible macroscopic defects. Collectively, these results demonstrate that the spray‐assisted UV‐curing process enables the formation of mechanically stable, chemically robust, and uniformly distributed LBDRB antimicrobial coatings suitable for fibrous mask substrates and other high‐touch surfaces.

The antimicrobial activity of LBDRB‐coated films on glass and SB mask surfaces is governed by their ability to generate ROS upon light exposure. Upon illumination, LBDRB is hypothesized to absorb incident photons and transition to an excited singlet state (Sn), followed by efficient intersystem crossing (ISC) to a longer‐lived triplet excited state (T1) due to its dye‐modified electronic structure [58]. The triplet state is expected to participate in Type I photoreaction pathways, involving electron or hydrogen abstraction, and Type II photoreaction pathways, involving energy transfer to ground‐state molecular oxygen. Both pathways are anticipated to result in the generation of ROS [59]. Consequently, LBDRB‐coated films can produce elevated levels of oxidative species under ambient daylight, thereby enabling potent biocidal activity against pathogenic microorganisms.

Figure 4a presents the UV–vis absorption spectra of lignin, lignin‐conjugated benzophenone‐3,3′,4,4′‐tetracarboxylate, and the resulting LBDRB material. Xanthene dyes and lignin are well‐recognized photosensitive materials that exhibit strong ROS generation efficiency under daylight [60, 61]. The UV–vis spectra of LBDRB show characteristic absorption bands at approximately 281 nm and 457 nm (ε ≈ 1.18 × 108 M− 1·cm− 1 ), consistent with the presence of lignin‐derived chromophores, including phenolic and aliphatic hydroxyl groups, methoxy substituents, carbonyls, and carboxyl groups. These functional moieties enable broad absorption across the UV and visible regions, with UV absorption primarily arising from carbonyl (C═O) functionalities and aromatic ring systems within the lignin backbone. The higher molar extinction coefficient of LBDRB compared to Eosin Y may be attributed to the covalent incorporation of the dye into the lignin‐based polymer matrix, which can enhance effective chromophore conjugation and improve light absorption within the macromolecular structure.

Fluorescence emission behavior of the LBDRB system is shown in Figure 4b. Under light irradiation, LBDRB exhibits a distinct emission peak centered at 545 nm, characteristic of the Eosin Y chromophore covalently incorporated within the lignin backbone. The intensity of this emission increases progressively with prolonged illumination for several days, suggesting continuous photoexcitation and stabilization of the excited singlet states within the conjugated lignin–dye system. Photoactivity and the accumulation of long‐lived excited states or emissive species arising from the lignin–Eosin Y conjugate, potentially associated with triplet‐state population or charge‐transfer interactions within the modified lignin matrix. Collectively, these observations confirm the persistent photophysical activity of LBDRB under illumination, supporting its suitability for light‐driven reactive oxygen species generation and photodynamic functionality.

To further investigate visible‐light‐driven ROS generation by the LBDRB coating, ROS production was evaluated under the same irradiation conditions used in this study (Figure 4c,d, and Figure S3). The LBDRB coating exhibited substantial ROS generation upon visible‐light exposure, whereas only minimal signals were observed under dark‐control conditions. Among the detected ROS species, singlet oxygen showed the strongest response (Figure 4c; GLM, p < 0.0001 at 5, 10, and 30 min), indicating that Type II photodynamic processes play a dominant role in the photoactivated behavior of the coating. The predominance of singlet oxygen generation is consistent with the well‐established photophysical behavior of Eosin Y‐based photosensitizers, which primarily undergo Type II photodynamic processes under visible‐light irradiation [17, 18]. Furthermore, significantly increased intracellular oxidative stress was observed in CM‐H2DCFDA‐loaded K. pneumoniae following exposure to the irradiated LBDRB coating (Figure 4d; GLM, p < 0.0001 at 5, 10, and 30 min), confirming that the generated ROS effectively induced oxidative damage within the bacterial cells. In addition, hydroxyl radicals and peroxide‐related oxidative species were also detected (Figure S3a,b). Hydroxyl radical generation measured by HPF fluorescence increased significantly at all tested time points (Figure S3a; GLM, p < 0.0001 at 5 min, p = 0.0012 at 10 min, and p = 0.0002 at 30 min), while hydrogen peroxide generation remained highly significant throughout visible‐light irradiation (Figure S3b; GLM, p < 0.0001 at 5, 10, and 30 min), indicating the involvement of multiple ROS pathways in the photodynamic process. Collectively, these findings provide strong mechanistic evidence that the photoactive antimicrobial and antiviral activities of the LBDRB coating are primarily governed by visible‐light‐induced ROS generation and subsequent oxidative stress. Notably, the antimicrobial experiments were performed under low‐intensity visible‐light irradiation conditions intended to simulate ambient indoor environments, under which substantial photothermal heating was not expected. The observed correlation between ROS generation and antimicrobial activity further supports that ROS‐mediated photochemical processes are the dominant contributors to microbial inactivation under the present experimental conditions.

The electronic structure and frontier molecular orbitals of the lignin‐conjugated Eosin Y are illustrated in Figure S4. Optimized geometries of Eosin Y bearing a carboxyl anchoring group (Figure S5a) were used to compute the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) and lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO). The HOMOs of Eosin Y structures are delocalized over the three benzene rings and consist predominantly of 2p contributions from C, Br, and O atoms. The similar HOMO distributions observed for the modified dye (Figure S5b) indicate that conjugation via the anchoring group preserves the intrinsic electronic character of Eosin Y.

Upon photoexcitation of lignin‐conjugated Eosin Y (LBDRB), electrons are promoted from the HOMO to the LUMO. Eosin Y typically exhibits HOMO and LUMO energy levels of approximately −4.8 and −3.0 eV, respectively, corresponding to a HOMO–LUMO gap of ∼1.8 eV (DFT/PBE0). In the lignin–Eosin Y conjugate, the HOMO–LUMO gap is reduced to 0.35 eV, while the energy separation between the HOMO and LUMO+1 is approximately 1.2 eV, providing an additional accessible excited state. This close energetic spacing facilitates vibronic coupling and enhances ISC efficiency.

As reported previously, the presence of bromine atoms in the Eosin Y moiety introduces a pronounced heavy‐atom effect, increasing spin–orbit coupling and promoting the formation of long‐lived triplet excited states capable of efficiently generating singlet oxygen [62]. Molecular orbital analyses further reveal that the dominant electronic transitions possess strong long‐range charge‐transfer character. Specifically, the primary S1 excitation involves charge transfer between the lignin and Eosin Y segments while retaining significant π→π* contributions similar to the monomeric dye. This combination of charge‐transfer and π→π* transitions supports efficient ISC by coupling closely spaced singlet and triplet states. Continuous visible‐light irradiation sustains the population of excited Eosin Y states, leading to the formation of long‐lived triplet species. Upon absorption of visible light, Eosin Y is promoted from the ground state (S0) to the singlet excited state (S1) and rapidly undergoes intersystem crossing (ISC) to the triplet state (T1), a behavior well documented for halogenated xanthene dyes with reported triplet lifetimes of approximately 24 µs [63, 64]. Repeated photoexcitation establishes a photostationary population of triplet states that serves as an efficient source of ROS in the LBDRB coating. In the lignin–conjugated Eosin Y, the presence of bromine atoms imparts a pronounced heavy‐atom effect that enhances spin–orbit coupling and accelerates the S1→T1 transition [65]. In addition, conjugation with lignin introduces long‐range charge‐transfer interactions that further promote ISC. Under continuous irradiation, relaxation from the T1 state to S0 is slower than repeated excitation, maintaining a sustained population of long‐lived triplet states [66]. These triplet species efficiently transfer energy to ground‐state molecular oxygen via a Type II sensitization pathway, resulting in the generation of singlet oxygen (1O2).

Previous studies have shown that pristine lignin possesses intrinsic ROS scavenging capability, primarily arising from its native aryl‐propanoid phenolic architecture [67]. Intact phenolic moieties are known to quench radical species through complementary hydrogen atom transfer (HAT) and single‐electron transfer (SET) pathways, while oxygen‐centered radicals may additionally associate with aromatic rings via radical adduct formation (RAF) [68]. The resulting phenoxyl radicals are stabilized by resonance delocalization within the lignin aromatic framework, thereby suppressing radical propagation. Xanthene dyes such as fluorescein derivatives, despite their high fluorescence quantum yield, are known to exhibit limited photostability under oxygenated conditions due to photosensitized oxidation [69]. Upon photoexcitation, organic dyes populate excited singlet states that undergo efficient intersystem crossing (ISC) to long‐lived triplet states, which can transfer energy to ground‐state molecular oxygen to generate singlet oxygen (1O2). Singlet oxygen subsequently reacts with electron‐rich chromophoric regions through ene‐type and cycloaddition reactions, leading to oxidation of aromatic frameworks and disruption of π‐conjugation [70]. In the LBDRB system, lignin hydroxyl groups are covalently conjugated with the xanthene dye Eosin Y, providing chemical immobilization and spatial isolation of photoactive sites. Under light irradiation, this conjugated architecture is proposed to facilitate intramolecular charge separation between electron‐donating aromatic units and electron‐accepting carbonyl functionalities within the lignin backbone. Photoexcited electrons are transferred to molecular oxygen, generating superoxide radicals (O2•−) that subsequently form hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), while photogenerated holes oxidize water or surface hydroxyl groups, collectively contributing to ROS formation [71]. The high density of aromatic and hydroxyl functionalities in lignin is therefore expected to promote π–π stacking and hydrogen‐bonding interactions with Eosin Y, influencing molecular packing and photophysical behavior. Fluorescence emission and ROS generation in the LBDRB system are governed by aggregation state, electronic coupling, and the local chemical environment of the covalently bound dye [72].

Time‐Dependent Photoactivated Antimicrobial Activity of LBDRB‐Coated Materials Under Indoor and Sunlight Conditions

UV‐cured LBDRB‐coated mask fabrics exhibited distinct time‐dependent antibacterial activity following aging under indoor lighting and full‐spectrum sunlight (Figure 5). Under indoor illumination (1.7 mW·m–2), the indoor light‐aged LBDRB‐coated mask PP fabric (single layer of LBDRB‐coated inner mask fabric; ILBDRB ×1) reached maximal antibacterial activity by day 7, at which point the CFU dropped below the detection limit within 5 min of light exposure compared with dark‐stored and uncoated controls (single layer of inner mask fabric; I×1) controls (GLM, p = 0.0006; Figure 5a). This high level of activity, corresponding to more than a 5‐log reduction within 30 min, was maintained through day 44. Thereafter, antibacterial efficacy gradually declined and became statistically insignificant by day 65 (GLM, p = 0.3205; Figure 5a).

Long‐term light‐activated antibacterial activity of LBDRB‐coated mask fabrics (ILBDRB×1) against K. pneumoniae. Antibacterial performance of the light‐responsive mask fabrics under (a) indoor light and (b) full‐spectrum sunlight illumination during multi‐day storage. Asterisks (*) indicate time points at which no bacterial colonies were detected on the plated samples. Data are presented as mean ± SD (n = 3).

These results indicate that visible light effectively excites the embedded chromophores within the LBDRB coating, enabling robust ROS production during the early and mid‐stage aging periods. The coating retained its ROS‐generating capability for approximately six weeks, after which prolonged illumination during aging led to progressive photo‐oxidation and partial photobleaching of the dye‐lignin conjugate, consistent with gradual loss of photoactive chromophores and reduced photoactivity reported for similar photosensitized systems [63, 65]. The observed light‐driven ROS generation in the LBDRB coating is consistent with the Type I and Type II photochemical pathways typical of brominated xanthene dyes such as Eosin Y, in which photoexcited triplet states transfer electrons or energy to molecular oxygen to produce singlet oxygen (1O2) and, under certain conditions, additional radical oxidants [73].

Under full‐spectrum sunlight aging, the LBDRB‐coated mask fabrics exhibited a delayed onset of maximal antibacterial activity relative to the indoor‐aged samples but ultimately achieved comparable overall efficiency (Figure 5b). At early aging stages (day 1–7), antimicrobial activity resulted in log reductions of approximately 1.6–2.5 after 30 min of illumination. The strongest antibacterial performance was observed on day 24, at which point K. pneumoniae cells were completely inactivated (below the detection limit) within 5 min of illumination (GLM, p = 0.0007; Figure 5b). This high‐efficacy state persisted until day 44, with log reductions exceeding 4–5 depending on exposure time, followed by a gradual decline beginning at day 58, where log reduction fell below 1 even after 30 min of illumination. The time‐dependent behavior observed in Figure 5 reflects a balance between initial photoactivation and longer‐term photofatigue of the lignin‐Eosin Y conjugate. During the early aging period, sustained photoexcitation is hypothesized to promote gradual conformational rearrangement and increased molecular mobility within the lignin‐dye matrix, potentially enhancing accessibility of photoactive sites and ROS generation. With continued irradiation, extended exposure is hypothesized to induce cumulative photo‐oxidative modification and degradation of the photoactive chromophores, resulting in depletion of effective sensitizers and attenuation of antibacterial efficacy at later aging stages. This interpretation is consistent with prior reports showing that visible‐light photosensitizers undergo progressive photobleaching, triplet‐state quenching, and structural degradation under prolonged irradiation, leading to reduced ROS‐generating efficacy [74, 75, 76].

The difference in peak activity, day 7 for indoor and day 24 for sunlight exposure, reflects the interplay between photon flux and coating stabilization (Figure 5). Low‐intensity indoor light induces rapid early activation dominated by visible‐light excitation, whereas the higher‐energy, broad‐spectrum solar illumination (containing UV‐A and blue‐green wavelengths) drives a more gradual activation process [77]. Despite these differences in early‐phase kinetics, both conditions sustained similarly high antibacterial activity through day 44, after which chromophore fatigue and photo‐oxidative degradation led to the sharp decline observed beginning around day 58. The long‐term stability of the LBDRB coating suggests that the UV‐curing process effectively immobilized the photoactive molecules within a rigid polymeric matrix, minimizing leaching and degradation during storage. The decline in activity after 58 days is likely attributable to cumulative oxidation or depletion of chromophoric sensitizers, resulting in a reduced ROS quantum yield.

Supporting experiments conducted under UV‐blocked conditions (Figure S6) further confirmed that, in the absence of UV radiation, indoor (Figure S6a) light‐aged LBDRB‐coated samples (ILBDRB×1) exhibited a markedly delayed maximum antibacterial response, reaching approximately a 5‐log reduction at day 37. In contrast, sunlight‐aged samples (Figure S6b) still achieved complete inactivation by day 24. In both cases, measurable antibacterial activity persisted through day 44, indicating that visible‐light excitation alone is sufficient to activate the embedded chromophores (Figure S6). The delayed peak under visible‐only illumination is consistent with previous reports showing that removal of high‐energy UV photons suppresses early‐stage photobleaching and slows chromophore degradation [74, 77, 78]. These findings indicate that the coating operates via two complementary pathways: (i) a UV‐assisted pathway that enhances ROS generation efficiency and accelerates antibacterial activity, and (ii) a visible‐light‐driven pathway in which activation kinetics are slower but photobleaching is reduced, enabling sustained antimicrobial performance.

The future perspective of the LBDRB system is its potential use as an antimicrobial coating for surgical masks; therefore, filtration efficiency and breathability tests were conducted using both bare and antimicrobial LBDRB‐coated SB layers with 5% NaCl aerosols (Figure S7). Figure S7a,b present the particle‐size‐dependent filtration efficiency (FE) of uncoated and LBDRB‐coated SB inner‐layer configurations, respectively. As shown in Figure S7a,b, particles generally sized between 0.1 and 3 µm exhibited characteristic size‐dependent filtration behavior. The filtration efficiency of both bare and LBDRB‐coated SB layers increased sharply as particle size decreased below the most penetrating particle size (MPPS) and similarly increased for particles larger than the MPPS, reflecting typical aerosol capture mechanisms. The uncoated control exhibited the expected particle‐size‐dependent filtration behavior (Figure S7a), while the LBDRB‐coated samples showed similar particle‐size‐dependent filtration behavior, with a modest increase in filtration efficiency as the layer number increased (Figure S7b). The overall FE of bare SB layers increased from I×1 (15.9 ± 9.4%) to I×2 (19.6 ± 8.3%) and I×3 (24.0 ± 8.5%). With LBDRB coating (Figure S7c), the FE further increased for stacked SB inner layers, reaching 21.4 ± 8.3% for ILBDRB×1, 26.4 ± 8.4% for ILBDRB×2, and 29.6 ± 8.1% for ILBDRB×3, indicating that FE increased with both coating application and layer number. Accordingly, the overall filtration efficiency of the coated samples was higher than that of the uncoated controls and gradually increased with increasing layer number. The pressure drop (Figure S7d), an indicator of comfort and breathability, increased with both LBDRB coating and increasing layer number, reaching 6.0 ± 0.3 Pa for I×3 and 16.4 ± 1.5 Pa for ILBDRB×3. In particular, the LBDRB‐coated samples exhibited higher pressure drop values than the uncoated samples at equivalent layer numbers, likely due to the additional coating layer and reduced pore volume introduced by the spray‐assisted UV‐curing process. Notably, despite this increase, the absolute pressure drop values remained relatively low even for the three‐layer LBDRB‐coated configuration, suggesting that the coated mask fabrics retain acceptable breathability for practical use.

From an application perspective, maintaining greater than 99% bacterial inactivation for over one month under realistic illumination demonstrates the potential of the LBDRB coating as a self‐sterilizing functional material for masks, textiles, and frequently touched surfaces. The persistence of antibacterial activity under low‐intensity visible light (1.7 mW·m–2) suggests the feasibility of continuous passive disinfection without the need for dedicated UV sources. Moreover, the ROS‐mediated disruption of K. pneumoniae cell envelopes is consistent with prior studies demonstrating that singlet oxygen and hydroxyl radicals oxidize lipopolysaccharide and phospholipid layers in Gram‐negative bacteria, leading to irreversible membrane permeabilization [79, 80]. Given the strong biofilm‐forming capability of K. pneumoniae, these results further suggest that the LBDRB coating may be broadly effective against other Gram‐negative pathogens and enveloped viruses.

The photoactivated antiviral activity of UV‐cured LBDRB coatings was evaluated against two enveloped RNA viruses, influenza A/PR8 (H1N1) and human coronavirus OC43 (HCoV‐OC43) (Figure 6). For PR8, antiviral performance was assessed using indoor‐aged samples under indoor illumination (Figure 6a) and sunlight‐aged samples under full‐spectrum sunlight (Figure 6b), both without UV blocking. For PR8 (Figure 6a,b), both hemagglutinin (HA) and nucleoprotein (NP) genes exhibited pronounced time‐dependent reductions. In indoor‐aged samples under indoor illumination (Figure 6a‐i–ii), HA gene copies decreased by approximately 2–3 log from day 1 to 7, followed by a sharp decline to ∼5 log at day 24 and ∼6 log at day 37 (GLM, p < 0.0001 for day 1, 7, 24, and 37). NP followed a similar trend, decreasing by approximately ∼1.5–3 log between days 1 and 7, ∼5 log at day 24, and ∼6 log at day 37 (GLM, p < 0.0001 for day 1, 7, 24, and 37). Significant reductions in both HA and NP persisted through day 44 (GLM, p < 0.0001 for both HA and NP genes), after which antiviral efficacy declined.

In sunlight‐aged LBDRB‐coated mask fabrics (Figure 6b‐i–ii), inactivation followed a comparable trajectory. HA gene copies decreased by ∼4 log at day 24 and ∼5 log at day 37, while NP decreased by about 2 log during days 1–7, followed by ∼5 log at day 24 and ∼6 log at day 37. After day 44, antiviral efficacy diminished to ∼2.6 log reduction. To further evaluate whether the antiviral effects observed by RT‐PCR analysis correlated with loss of viral infectivity, TCID50 infectivity assays were additionally performed for H1N1 PR8 using the Reed–Muench method. Bare‐material control groups retained high infectivity throughout the experimental period under both visible‐light and dark conditions, with TCID50 values remaining on the order of approximately 1010–1011 TCID50/mL. Specifically, bare‐material visible‐light samples exhibited infectivity values of 2.20 × 1011, 1.00 × 1011, and 3.00 × 1010 TCID50/mL after 5, 10, and 30 min exposure, respectively, while dark‐control samples showed infectivity values of 5.32 × 1010, 1.78 × 1011, and 1.62 × 1010 TCID50/mL at the corresponding time points. In contrast, visible‐light‐irradiated LBDRB coatings dramatically reduced viral infectivity to 1.22 × 102, 2.15 × 102, and 5.72 × 102 TCID50/mL after 5, 10, and 30 min exposure, respectively, corresponding to approximately 8–9 log reductions in infectivity relative to the bare‐material controls. Although LBDRB coatings under dark conditions exhibited partial antiviral activity, with infectivity values of 4.06 × 107, 2.15 × 107, and 5.72 × 108 TCID50/mL after 5, 10, and 30 min exposure, respectively, the substantially greater reductions observed under visible‐light irradiation indicate that photodynamic ROS‐mediated processes are the dominant contributors to viral inactivation. Together, these findings support that the antiviral effects observed by RT‐PCR analysis correlate with substantial reductions in infectious viral particles rather than solely viral RNA degradation.

Collectively, indoor‐ and sunlight‐aged LBDRB coatings exhibited essentially similar antiviral activity against PR8, with maximal reductions in HA and NP observed between days 24 and 37. This trend remained largely unchanged even when UV transmission was blocked (Figure S8), indicating that visible‐light excitation alone is sufficient to drive antiviral activity. Consistent with the antibacterial mechanism, the concurrent decline of HA and NP gene copies reflects progressive oxidative damage to the viral envelope and genomic RNA. Previous studies have shown that singlet oxygen (1O2) and hydroxyl radical (·OH) oxidize amino acid residues in hemagglutinin and induce RNA base modification and strand cleavage, resulting in irreversible loss of infectivity [81, 82, 83]. Such late‐stage attenuation is consistent with reports that visible‐light photosensitizers undergo progressive photobleaching and photo‐oxidative degradation during prolonged irradiation, leading to diminished absorption and reduced ROS‐generating efficiency.

The sunlight‐aged LBDRB‐coated samples likewise exhibited strong antiviral activity against HCoV‐OC43 (Figure 6c,d). Without a UV blocker (Figure 6c), qPCR analysis showed that the M gene decreased by ∼1.5 log (E gene) and ∼2.0 log (E gene) at day 1, reaching ∼4.0 log (M) and ∼3.8 log (E) by day 37 (GLM, p < 0.0001 for both M and E genes at day 37). Notably, even at day 65, both genes remained 1.9–2.0 log below initial levels, indicating that residual photoactivity persisted for over two months. With a UV blocker (Figure 6d), initial reductions (∼2 log for both genes) and mid‐term efficacy (day 37: 3.8 log for M and 4.8 log for E) were comparable, although activity at day 65 decreased to ∼1.0 log (M) and ∼1.8 log (E). These results demonstrate that visible‐light excitation alone sustains substantial antiviral activity, with UV exposure during aging contributing primarily to modest differences in the extent of gene disruption.

Unlike PR8, which exhibited substantially reduced antiviral activity after day 44, HCoV‐OC43 remained susceptible to LBDRB‐mediated inactivation through day 65. This divergence is consistent with documented structural and compositional differences between the viruses. HCoV‐OC43 possesses a lipid‐rich, heavily glycosylated envelope and a flexible, long RNA genome that increases susceptibility to oxidative modification, whereas PR8 features a matrix‐supported, protein‐dense envelope and tightly condensed ribonucleoprotein complexes that confer greater structural stability [84, 85, 86, 87, 88]. Overall, LBDRB‐coated materials demonstrated prolonged photodynamic antiviral efficacy under realistic illumination conditions, with PR8 retaining activity for approximately one month and HCoV‐OC43 maintaining measurable susceptibility for nearly two months. The sustained performance under both UV‐assisted and visible‐light–only operation highlights the robustness and practical applicability of the LBDRB coating for self‐sterilizing surfaces.

Photoactivated Antibacterial Activity and ROS‐Induced Bacterial Damage on Glass Surfaces

The photoactive LBDRB coating maintained strong antibacterial activity even on smooth, optically transparent glass substrates, where photon scattering and interfacial contact area are minimal (Figure 7). Under indoor light, MRSA exhibited more than a 4‐log reduction in viability across all measured time points (days 1–44). Complete inactivation was achieved at days 7, 14, 21, and 28 within 30 min of light exposure (GLM, p < 0.0001 for days 7, 21, and 28; p = 0.0004 for day 14), and no viable colonies were detected after 5 min incubation on days 21 and 28 (Figure 7a). K. pneumoniae showed a similarly rapid response, with no detectable CFU after 10 and 30 min of exposure on day 1, and complete inactivation was again observed after 30 min on day 7 (Figure 7b). In contrast, neither dark‐stored nor uncoated glass samples exhibited measurable reductions (One‐way ANOVA, p = 0.4221 for day 1; p = 0.2464 for day 7), confirming that bacterial loss was exclusively photoinduced. Despite the smooth and nonporous nature of glass, the LBDRB coating retained pronounced photoactivated antibacterial activity. This strong response indicates that immobilized Eosin Y chromophores within the lignin matrix efficiently absorb visible light and generate reactive oxygen species (ROS) even on optically transparent surfaces. The localized excitation of Eosin Y and its subsequent transition to the triplet state follow established photochemical pathways that produce singlet oxygen (1O2) and related oxidizing species known to disrupt bacterial cells [19, 23].

TEM directly visualized structural damage in K. pneumoniae following exposure to the photoactivated LBDRB coating (Figure 7c). Control cells (i) displayed intact outer membranes and uniform cytoplasmic density, whereas cells exposed to the light‐activated coating for 30 min (ii) exhibited clear membrane disruption, cytoplasmic leakage, and a loss of structural integrity. These morphological differences are consistent with previous reports describing ROS‐induced membrane damage in photocatalytic and dye‐based photodynamic systems [12, 19, 20, 23]. The TEM observations support the interpretation that antibacterial activity on glass surfaces involves surface‐localized photochemical damage at the coating‐cell interface.

The strong bactericidal efficiency observed on smooth, nonporous glass surfaces further indicates that the lignin‐conjugated Eosin Y coating retains its functional integrity under conditions that typically challenge dye‐based antimicrobial films (Figure 7). Previous studies have reported reduced activity of dye‐sensitized coatings on glass due to photobleaching or loss of surface attachment [12, 20, 22]. In contrast, the covalent incorporation of Eosin Y into the lignin matrix provides effective chromophore stabilization, enabling sustained photoactivity over extended periods. The coating maintained antibacterial efficacy for more than 44 days, demonstrating notable durability that exceeds that commonly reported for metal ion–based or nanoparticle antimicrobial systems, which often lose effectiveness through oxidative degradation or ion release [9, 10, 21]. Moreover, the retention of photoactivity on an optically smooth surface demonstrates that the LBDRB coating can generate bactericidal ROS independently of surface roughness or porosity. Similar behavior has been reported for other visible‐light‐responsive antimicrobial coatings, where ROS generation occurs in close proximity to the material surface [13, 21, 22]. The ability to maintain robust antibacterial activity on transparent substrates highlights the suitability of this system as a self‐sanitizing coating for glass and other impermeable materials.

Conclusion

This study establishes a visible‐light‐responsive, bio‐derived photoactive material platform in which lignin–Eosin Y conjugates are covalently immobilized within a UV‐curable polymer network to enable stable, non‐leaching generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) under ambient illumination. The LBDRB system can be readily integrated onto diverse substrates through spray‐assisted UV curing, forming mechanically robust, surface‐bound films with confirmed chemical incorporation and efficient photoactivation. Under low‐intensity visible light, the immobilized chromophores generate sufficient ROS to inactivate both bacteria and viruses while retaining antimicrobial functionality over extended aging periods. Notably, sustained antibacterial and antiviral activity is achieved without the need for external disinfectants or high‐energy light sources, demonstrating persistent photochemical performance under practical illumination conditions. The consistent activity observed on both fibrous and optically transparent surfaces highlights the robustness and substrate versatility of this surface‐integrated system. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that the LBDRB platform enables passive, ambient‐light‐driven disinfection and provides a scalable route toward self‐sterilizing interfaces. The compatibility with flexible and transparent materials supports potential integration into masks, personal protective equipment, medical and diagnostic devices, and other frequently contacted surfaces. More broadly, the lignin–chromophore conjugation strategy presented here offers a modular framework for the development of tunable photoactive materials with controllable photochemical and antimicrobial functionality.
