Conservative neural posterior estimation via distributionally robust training
William Laplante, Yuga Hikida, Charita Dellaporta, [0.8ex] François-Xavier Briol, Ayush Bharti, [1ex] Department of Statistical Science, University College London, UK

Abstract
Simulation-based inference with neural posterior estimation (NPE) often yields overconfident and unreliable posteriors under limited simulation budgets. To address this, we propose DRO-NPE, a distributionally robust approach that replaces the standard NPE objective with a worst-case loss over a Wasserstein ambiguity set. We introduce KL-based metrics for miscoverage and miscalibration, and use these to show that the DRO-NPE objective controls overfitting and reduces posterior overconfidence. Our method is tractable, parallelisable, and readily integrates with standard normalising flows. Across benchmark SBI tasks, DRO-NPE consistently improves coverage and calibration, while narrowing the gap between empirical and population NPE loss, leading to more reliable inference in low-simulation regimes.

Introduction

Simulation-based inference
(
12
, SBI;)
is a powerful framework for inferring parameters of scientific models whose likelihood functions are unavailable or computationally prohibitive to evaluate, but for which simulating data is straightforward. The use of flexible neural conditional density estimators has substantially expanded the applicability of SBI to challenging problems, especially in fields such as particle physics
(
9
)
, cognitive neuroscience
(
21
)
, economics
(
19
)
and cosmology
(
2
;
35
)
.
Neural SBI methods rely on simulations from the scientific model to approximate intractable quantities such as the posterior, the likelihood, the likelihood-to-evidence ratio, or the score function; see
78
for a recent review. In this work, we focus on the widely used neural posterior estimation (NPE) method
(
53
;
59
)
.

A central practical limitation of NPE is the simulation budget required to train the conditional density estimator. As many scientific simulators are expensive to run, generating a sufficiently large training set is often the main computational bottleneck. Prior work has sought to reduce simulation cost using sequential training schemes
(
53
;
48
;
28
)
, multi-fidelity simulations
(
32
;
38
;
68
;
63
)
, or by exploiting heterogeneous simulation costs
(
6
)
.
However, these strategies do not directly address the challenge of ensuring reliable uncertainty estimates in low-simulation regimes.
In practice, the adequacy of a given simulation budget is rarely clear
a priori
, and neural SBI methods trained on limited simulations can exhibit poor uncertainty quantification, leading
30
to warn of the potential for a “trust crisis”.

Under finite simulations, NPE can yield
overconfident
posterior approximations whose credible regions contain the true parameter less often than their nominal coverage suggests.
Such undercoverage is especially problematic in scientific applications, where overstated certainty can lead to misleading conclusions. By contrast, conservative posteriors are often safer as they widen uncertainty statements rather than overstating confidence. This has motivated several works on conservative uncertainty quantification in neural SBI:

15
proposed regularised balanced neural ratio estimation
(
31
, NRE;)
to inflate estimator uncertainty, and
16
extended this idea to contrastive NRE
(
50
)
and NPE.

20
instead regularise NPE directly with a coverage error term.
However, these methods remain largely heuristic: they approximate constrained objectives to encourage conservativeness without statistical guarantees for the resulting uncertainty quantification.
Instead of regularising standard NPE,
14
use Bayesian neural networks for conservative inference, at the cost of Bayesian inference over network weights.
Complementarily, conformal and Neyman-inversion methods
(
49
;
57
;
10
)
post-hoc calibrate credible sets with coverage guarantees, but do not directly address poor conditional density estimation.

In this paper, we view NPE through the lens of
empirical risk minimisation
. Standard NPE minimises an empirical risk over a finite set of simulated parameter–data pairs. When the simulation budget is limited, the empirical risk can be a poor proxy for the population risk, leading to overfitting and poorly calibrated posteriors
(
30
)
. This is illustrated in

We make NPE conservative using

Our contributions are as follows. First, we introduce Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence-based uncertainty-quantification metrics (

Second, we propose a Wasserstein DRO objective that upper-bounds these metrics and the population NPE loss, so that, under suitable conditions, minimising it controls both posterior-fitting and uncertainty-quantification error (

We also derive a tractable regularised objective that upper-bounds the DRO objective (

Third, we establish excess-risk, miscoverage, and miscalibration guarantees for DRO-NPE, and compare them to those of standard NPE (Appendix

Finally, we show that DRO-NPE improves uncertainty quantification and generalisation across challenging benchmarks (

2
Background

We introduce NPE and uncertainty-quantification metrics revealing its finite-simulation failure modes.

Simulation-based inference with neural posterior estimation.

Let
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathcal{X}}}
and
\Theta\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d_{\Theta}}
denote the data and parameter spaces, and let
\mathcal{Z}:=\mathcal{X}\times\Theta\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathcal{Z}}}
, where
d_{\mathcal{Z}}=d_{\mathcal{X}}+d_{\Theta}
.
Consider a prior
\Pi
on
\Theta
with density
\pi
and a conditional distribution
P_{\bm{x}\mid\bm{\theta}}
from which one can simulate, but whose density
p(\bm{x}\mid\bm{\theta})
, and therefore the likelihood, cannot be evaluated pointwise.
Together,
\Pi
and
P_{\bm{x}\mid\bm{\theta}}
induce a joint distribution
P_{\bm{x},\bm{\theta}}
, denoted by
P
for simplicity, with marginal
P_{\bm{x}}
and posterior
P_{\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x}}
.
The goal of SBI is to approximate this posterior distribution
P_{\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x}_{0}}
given observed data
\bm{x}_{o}
.

NPE is an amortised SBI method that approximates the posterior using flexible conditional density estimators such as mixture-density networks
(
7
)
and normalising flows
(
54
)
. Let
q_{\phi}(\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x})
denote such an estimator with parameter
\phi\in\Phi\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d_{\Phi}}
, and let
Q_{\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x}}
be the corresponding distribution (we remove the explicit dependence on
\phi
for notational simplicity). Assuming
P_{\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x}}
is absolutely continuous with respect to
Q_{\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x}}
, the NPE objective is obtained by minimising the expected KL divergence between
P_{\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x}}
and
Q_{\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x}}
over
\phi
:

\textstyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}\sim P_{\bm{x}}}\left[\mathrm{KL}\Big(P_{\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x}}\;\|\;Q_{\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x}}\Big)\right]\overset{+K(P)}{=}\mathbb{E}_{(\bm{\theta},\bm{x})\sim P}[-\log q_{\phi}(\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x})]:=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{NPE}}(q_{\phi};P),

(1)

where
\bm{z}=(\bm{x},\bm{\theta})
, and the additive constant
K(P)
does not depend on
\phi
. Note that
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{NPE}}(q_{\phi};P)
can be thought of as a
population risk
, as it is defined as an expectation under
P
, induced by the
pointwise loss

\ell_{\mathrm{NPE}}(q_{\phi};\bm{z}):=-\log q_{\phi}(\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x})
.
In practice,
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{NPE}}(q_{\phi};P)
is approximated using independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) samples
\bm{z}_{1:n}\sim P
, where each
\bm{z}_{i}
is obtained by first sampling
\bm{\theta}_{i}\sim\Pi
, and then simulating
\bm{x}_{i}\sim P_{\bm{x}\mid\bm{\theta}_{i}}
.
These samples induce the empirical measure
P_{n}:=\nicefrac{{1}}{{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{\bm{z}_{i}}
, such that
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{NPE}}(q_{\phi};P_{n}):=\nicefrac{{1}}{{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell_{\mathrm{NPE}}(q_{\phi};\bm{z}_{i})
is the associated
empirical risk
.
The conditional density model is then trained by minimising the empirical risk,
\hat{\phi}_{n}:=\arg\min_{\phi\in\Phi}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{NPE}}(q_{\phi};P_{n})
.
As a result, the estimator
q_{\hat{\phi}_{n}}
is amortised: for new observed data
\bm{x}_{o}
, the posterior is approximated by a simple forward pass, namely
q_{\hat{\phi}_{n}}(\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x}_{o})\approx p(\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x}_{o})
, without requiring retraining.

Uncertainty quantification metrics in SBI.

Despite its widespread success, NPE remains prone to failures in uncertainty quantification. We focus on the two most widely used diagnostics for assessing this: expected coverage of high-probability density region (HPDR)
(
75
;
30
)
and simulation-based calibration
(
67
;
62
;
51
)
. Other diagnostics have also been studied
(
80
;
44
;
43
;
11
;
17
)
, but are less common in SBI.

Definition 2.1

(Expected coverage of HPDR)
.

The
(1-\alpha)
HPDR induced by
q
at
\bm{x}
is the region where
q(\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x})
is largest while containing
1-\alpha
of its mass.
Formally,
R_{\alpha}^{q}(\bm{x}):=\left\{\bm{\theta}\in\Theta:q(\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x})\geq\tau_{\alpha}^{q}(\bm{x})\right\}
, where
\tau_{\alpha}^{q}(\bm{x})
is a threshold such that
Q_{\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x}}\!\left(R_{\alpha}^{q}(\bm{x})\right)=1-\alpha
.
The expected coverage of the HPDR is then:

\textstyle C_{\alpha}^{P}(q):=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}\sim P}\left[\mathbf{1}\Big\{\bm{\theta}\in R_{\alpha}^{q}(\bm{x})\Big\}\right].

(2)

Expected coverage of HPDR is estimated by repeatedly drawing
\bm{z}_{i}=(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{\theta}_{i})\sim P
. For each
\bm{x}_{i}
,
M
posterior samples
\tilde{\bm{\theta}}_{i,1},\dots,\tilde{\bm{\theta}}_{i,M}\sim Q_{\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x}_{i}}
are used to evaluate the densities
q(\tilde{\bm{\theta}}_{i,m}\mid\bm{x}_{i})
for
m=1,\dots,M
, and the empirical
\alpha
-quantile of these values approximates the threshold
\tau_{\alpha}^{q}(\bm{x}_{i})
defining the
(1-\alpha)
HPDR. Expected coverage of HPDR is then assessed by whether the ground-truth parameter
\bm{\theta}_{i}
satisfies
q(\bm{\theta}_{i}\mid\bm{x}_{i})\geq\tau_{\alpha}^{q}(\bm{x}_{i})
.
We say
Q_{\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x}}
is conservative when
C_{\alpha}^{P}(q)>1-\alpha
and overconfident when
C_{\alpha}^{P}(q)<1-\alpha
.

Definition 2.2

(Calibration w.r.t.
S
)
.

Let
S:\Theta\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
be a summary, and
Q_{S\mid\bm{x}}
,
P_{S\mid\bm{x}}
be the distributions of
S(\bm{\theta};\bm{x})
under
\bm{\theta}\sim Q_{\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x}}
and
\bm{\theta}\sim P_{\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x}}
, respectively.
Let the CDF of
Q_{S\mid\bm{x}}
be denoted as
F^{Q}_{S\mid\bm{x}}
.
We say that
Q_{\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x}}
(or equivalently
q
) is calibrated with respect to
S
if, for
S^{P}_{\bm{x}}\sim P_{S\mid\bm{x}}
, the random variable
F^{Q}_{S\mid\bm{x}}(S_{\bm{x}}^{P})
is distributed as
\mathcal{U}[0,1]
under
\bm{x}\sim P_{\bm{x}}
.

Calibration with respect to
S
means that, under mild conditions on the CDF of
Q_{S\mid\bm{x}}
, the distribution of
S
under
P_{\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x}}
is the same as that of
S
under
Q_{\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x}}
, for
P_{\bm{x}}
-almost every
\bm{x}
.
In practice, simulation-based calibration (SBC) is used to approximate calibration w.r.t.
S
by repeatedly drawing
(\bm{\theta}_{i},\bm{x}_{i})\sim P
and computing
u_{i}:=F^{Q}_{S\mid\bm{x}_{i}}\big(S(\bm{\theta}_{i};\bm{x}_{i})\big),i=1,\dots,n
.
The empirical distribution of
u_{1:n}
is then compared to
\mathcal{U}[0,1]
, for example via histograms, empirical CDFs, or summary statistics.
The function
S
allows SBC to be applied in higher-dimensional settings.
For example,
S(\bm{\theta};\bm{x})=\|\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}_{0}\|_{2}
for a reference point
\bm{\theta}_{0}
is used in the
BayesFlow
library
(
40
)
. Note that expected coverage of HPDR is a special case of SBC when
S(\bm{\theta};\bm{x})=q(\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x})

(
15
)
; see also Appendix
A
,
Lemma

A.1
.

3
A new KL-based perspective on uncertainty quantification metrics

We consider errors in the expected coverage of HPDR and in calibration w.r.t.
S
, which we refer to as
miscoverage
of HPDR and
miscalibration
w.r.t.
S
, respectively.
Miscoverage is often measured by the absolute deviation
(
20
;
77
)
:

\textstyle\Delta_{\mathrm{cov}}^{\alpha}(q;P):=\left|C_{\alpha}^{P}(q)-(1-\alpha)\right|.

However, this definition is blind to the prior, as the latter attains perfect expected coverage of HPDR, i.e, for any
\alpha\in(0,1)
,

C_{\alpha}^{P}(\pi)=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}\sim P}[\mathbf{1}\{\bm{\theta}\in R_{\alpha}^{\pi}\}]=\Pi(R_{\alpha}^{\pi})=1-\alpha;

therefore,
\Delta_{\mathrm{cov}}^{\alpha}(\pi;P)=0
for all valid
\alpha
. This motivates an alternative notion of miscoverage. We adopt a KL-based approach because it removes prior blindness, links miscoverage and miscalibration to the NPE objective (
Theorem

4.1
), and provides a natural target for hyperparameter selection.

Definition 3.1

(KL-based miscoverage of HPDR)
.

Fix
\alpha\in(0,1)
, and let
T_{A}(\bm{\theta}):=\mathbf{1}\{\bm{\theta}\in A\}\in\{0,1\}
denote the indicator of a set
A\subseteq\Theta
.
Let
(T_{A})_{\#}P_{\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x}}
denote the pushforward of
P_{\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x}}
through
T_{A}
, i.e., the Bernoulli distribution of
T_{A}(\bm{\theta})
when
\bm{\theta}\sim P_{\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x}}
.
Define
(T_{A})_{\#}Q_{\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x}}
analogously.
Then, KL-based miscoverage of HPDR is:

\textstyle\mathrm{kl}_{\mathrm{cov}}^{\alpha}(q;P):=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}\sim P_{\bm{x}}}\left[\mathrm{KL}\left((T_{R_{\alpha}^{q}(\bm{x})})_{\#}P_{\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x}}\;\Big\|\;(T_{R_{\alpha}^{q}(\bm{x})})_{\#}Q_{\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x}}\right)\right].

KL-based miscoverage of HPDR is weaker than the expected KL divergence between the full conditionals
P_{\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x}}
and
Q_{\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x}}
in
Equation

1
, since it compares only the Bernoulli distributions induced by whether
\bm{\theta}
lies in the HPDR
R_{\alpha}^{q}(\bm{x})
. This follows from the data-processing inequality
(
58
)
, which states that KL divergence cannot increase under pushforwards. Moreover,
\mathrm{kl}_{\mathrm{cov}}^{\alpha}
upper-bounds
\Delta_{\mathrm{cov}}^{\alpha}
via Pinsker’s inequality:
\Delta_{\mathrm{cov}}^{\alpha}(q;P)\leq\sqrt{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}\mathrm{kl}_{\mathrm{cov}}^{\alpha}(q;P)}
; see
Lemma

A.3
.
Small KL-based miscoverage of HPDR then implies small
\Delta_{\mathrm{cov}}^{\alpha}
, but not conversely:
\mathrm{kl}_{\mathrm{cov}}^{\alpha}(q;P)
may remain positive even when
\Delta_{\mathrm{cov}}^{\alpha}(q;P)=0
, and, unlike
\Delta_{\mathrm{cov}}^{\alpha}
,
\mathrm{kl}_{\mathrm{cov}}^{\alpha}(q;P)
does not generally vanish for the prior. The latter would require the posterior to place the same mass as the prior on the HPDR for almost every
\bm{x}
.

The standard notion of CDF-based miscalibration quantifies deviation of
the distribution of
F^{Q}_{S\mid\bm{x}}(S_{\bm{x}}^{P})
from standard uniform

(
67
;
27
)
.
For the pushforward distributions
(F^{Q}_{S\mid\bm{x}})_{\#}P_{S\mid\bm{x}}
and
(F^{Q}_{S\mid\bm{x}})_{\#}Q_{S\mid\bm{x}}
, where the latter is uniformly distributed under mild assumptions, this deviation is often measured via Kolmogorov–Smirnov distances or via Cramér–von Mises criteria
(
61
;
80
;
20
)
.
We introduce a KL-based alternative that captures deviation from uniformity and can be related to the population NPE risk.

Definition 3.2

(KL-based miscalibration with respect to
S
)
.

For
S:\Theta\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
, KL-based miscalibration is:

\displaystyle\mathrm{kl}^{S}_{\mathrm{cal}}(q;P):=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}\sim P_{\bm{x}}}\left[\mathrm{KL}\left((F^{Q}_{S\mid\bm{x}})_{\#}P_{S\mid\bm{x}}\,\middle\|\,(F^{Q}_{S\mid\bm{x}})_{\#}Q_{S\mid\bm{x}}\right)\right].

This notion is motivated by KL-based miscalibration discrepancies previously used in probabilistic regression
(
70
;
18
)
.
As with
\mathrm{kl}_{\mathrm{cov}}^{\alpha}
,
\mathrm{kl}^{S}_{\mathrm{cal}}
is weaker than the full KL divergence between
P_{\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x}}
and
Q_{\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x}}
by the data-processing inequality. The two nevertheless capture different notions of mismatch: for fixed
\alpha
,
\mathrm{kl}^{\alpha}_{\mathrm{cov}}
compares only the mass assigned to the HPDR, so
\mathrm{kl}^{\alpha}_{\mathrm{cov}}(q;P)=0
does not imply
P_{\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x}}=Q_{\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x}}
. By contrast,
\mathrm{kl}_{\mathrm{cal}}^{S}=0
implies equality of the induced distributions, and under mild conditions on
F^{Q}_{S\mid\bm{x}}
, equality of
P_{S\mid\bm{x}}
and
Q_{S\mid\bm{x}}
. If
S
is sufficiently informative, this further implies
P_{\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x}}=Q_{\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x}}
. For
S(\bm{\theta},\bm{x})=q(\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x})
, under which coverage of HPDR is a special case of SBC,
\mathrm{kl}^{S}_{\mathrm{cal}}
upper-bounds
\mathrm{kl}^{\alpha}_{\mathrm{cov}}
; see
Theorem

4.1
.

4
Distributionally robust optimisation for NPE

NPE would ideally target the population risk
\mathcal{L}(q_{\phi};P)
, but
P
is accessible only through the empirical measure
P_{n}
, which can be a poor approximation when
n
is not sufficiently large.
In such settings, this leads to substantial performance degradation
(
74
)
, and gives rise to the classical
generalisation gap

(
71
;
65
)
.
In particular, minimisation over
P_{n}
yields an overly optimistic estimate of the achievable population risk
(
41
)
:

\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}_{1:n}\overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim}P}[\inf_{\phi}\mathcal{L}(q_{\phi};P_{n})]\leq\inf_{\phi}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}_{1:n}\overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim}P}[\mathcal{L}(q_{\phi};P_{n})]=\inf_{\phi}\mathcal{L}(q_{\phi};P).

In NPE, this gap can translate into poor conditional density estimation.
We therefore use distributionally robust optimisation (DRO) to minimise an upper bound on the population risk, thus accounting for distributional uncertainty
(
41
;
60
;
74
)
.

Motivating DRO-NPE.

We formalise DRO-NPE as a Wasserstein distributionally robust objective.
Rather than minimising the empirical NPE risk directly, we replace it by its worst-case value over an
ambiguity set

\mathcal{A}(P_{n})
of distributions centred at
P_{n}

(
8
;
42
)
:

\textstyle\inf_{\phi}\,\overbrace{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{NPE}}(q_{\phi};P_{n})}^{\text{empirical risk}}\;\xrightarrow{\text{risk aversion}}\;\inf_{\phi}\,\overbrace{\sup_{\tilde{P}\in\mathcal{A}(P_{n})}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{NPE}}(q_{\phi};\tilde{P})}^{\text{DRO-NPE risk}}.

We define the ambiguity set to be a Wasserstein ball around
P_{n}
, i.e.,
\mathcal{A}_{p}(P_{n};\varepsilon):=\{\tilde{P}\in\mathcal{P}_{p}:W_{p}(\tilde{P},P_{n})\leq\varepsilon\}
, where
W_{p}
is the
p-
Wasserstein between
\tilde{P}
and
P_{n}
,
\mathcal{P}_{p}:=\{\tilde{P}:\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}\sim\tilde{P}}[\|\bm{z}-\bm{z}^{\prime}\|^{p}]<\infty\quad\forall\bm{z}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Z}\}
is the set of probability distributions with finite
p
-th moment, and
\|\cdot\|
is the Euclidean norm.
The radius
\varepsilon\geq 0
controls the size of the ambiguity set
(
60
;
42
, see)
:
\varepsilon=0
recovers the empirical risk, while larger values yield a more conservative objective by guarding against perturbations of
P_{n}
within the Wasserstein ball
(
74
)
.
Although many distances and divergences have been considered for defining ambiguity sets in DRO, Wasserstein balls are particularly natural here.
For example, the commonly used
f
-divergence-based ambiguity sets
(
33
;
4
;
34
)
primarily re-weight the support of the empirical distribution.
By contrast, Wasserstein balls are based on mass transport
(
41
)
, and can contain distributions supported away from the observed simulations, including continuous distributions.

We first show that the population NPE risk upper bounds both
\mathrm{kl}_{\mathrm{cov}}^{\alpha}
and
\mathrm{kl}^{S}_{\mathrm{cal}}
by the data-processing inequality for the KL divergence (see
Lemma

A.2
). Thus, if
P
were available, the NPE objective would provide a principled criterion for controlling these forms of posterior uncertainty.
In finite-simulation settings, however,
P
is unavailable, so we instead construct a high-probability upper bound on the population risk.
To do so, we rely on Wasserstein concentration results
(
23
;
41
)
, stating that for a suitable radius
\varepsilon
decreasing with
n
, the true distribution
P
belongs to the ambiguity set
\mathcal{A}(P_{n})
with high probability. This directly implies that the DRO-NPE objective upper-bounds the population NPE risk, and therefore also
\mathrm{kl}_{\mathrm{cov}}^{\alpha}
and
\mathrm{kl}^{S}_{\mathrm{cal}}
; see Appendix
B.1
for the proof.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
S(\bm{\theta};\bm{x})=f(q(\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x}))
, for some strictly increasing function
f
, then for any
q_{\phi}
:

\textstyle\sup_{\alpha\in(0,1)}\mathrm{kl}_{\mathrm{cov}}^{\alpha}(q_{\phi};P)\leq\mathrm{kl}_{\mathrm{cal}}^{S}(q_{\phi};P)\overset{+K(P)}{\leq}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{NPE}}(q_{\phi};P).

Moreover, suppose
p\in[1,d_{\mathcal{Z}}/2)
, and that there exists
\alpha>2p
such that
M_{\alpha}:=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}\sim P}[\|\bm{z}\|^{\alpha}]<\infty
. Fix
\delta\in(0,1)
. Then, for every
\varsigma\in(0,\alpha)
there exist positive constants
C_{1},C_{2}
depending only on
p,d_{\mathcal{Z}},\alpha,M_{\alpha},\varsigma
such that for any
q_{\phi}
, whenever
\varepsilon\geq\varepsilon(n,\delta)
, where

\textstyle\varepsilon(n,\delta):=\max\left\{\left(\frac{1}{C_{1}n}\log\frac{2C_{2}}{\delta}\right)^{\frac{1}{d_{\mathcal{Z}}}},\;\left(\frac{2C_{2}}{\delta}\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha-\varsigma}}n^{\frac{1}{\alpha-\varsigma}-\frac{1}{p}}\right\},

with probability at least
1-\delta
:
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{NPE}}(q_{\phi};P)\leq\sup_{\tilde{P}\in\mathcal{A}_{p}(P_{n};\varepsilon)}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{NPE}}(q_{\phi};\tilde{P}).

The moment condition on
P
is mild: for
p=2
, which is what we use (see
Section

4
), it reduces to
P
having at least a finite fourth moment, which is satisfied by many light-tailed distributions.
The dependence of
\varepsilon(n,\delta)
on
d_{\mathcal{Z}}

reflects the curse of dimensionality inherent to Wasserstein concentration, with rates that are essentially optimal
(
41
;
76
;
23
)
. In practice, however, SBI is often performed on handcrafted or learned summary statistics rather than raw observations
(
59
;
13
)
, which can substantially reduce the effective dimension.
We state the results for
p\in[1,d_{\mathcal{Z}}/2)
, the regime most relevant here; other cases are covered by
23
and mainly affect the form of
\varepsilon(n,\delta)
.
Alternative DRO-based upper-bounding strategies may yield faster rates in some settings
(
3
;
25
, see, e.g.,)
but require stronger assumptions, particularly on the loss, whereas our approach accommodates the general losses used in SBI. Next, we discuss the implementation of DRO-NPE.

A tractable objective for DRO-NPE.

DRO-NPE admits two closely related formulations: a strong dual form (
Equation

3
) and a regularised form (
Proposition

4.2
).
Both provide upper bounds on the NPE population risk as in
Theorem

4.1
; the strong dual formulation is exact but challenging to obtain numerically, while the regularised form is computationally cheaper but approximate.

We begin with the strong dual view, which replaces the generally intractable primal Wasserstein-DRO objective with an optimisation problem over auxiliary variables.
Let the class of functions satisfying
\rho-
growth
for
\rho\geq 1
be

\mathcal{G}_{\rho}(\mathcal{Z}):=\left\{f:\mathcal{Z}\to\mathbb{R}\;:\;\exists\,C>0\;\mathrm{s.t.}\;|f(\bm{z})|\leq C(1+\|\bm{z}\|^{\rho})\;\forall\bm{z}\in\mathcal{Z}\right\}.

Under the assumptions that for fixed
q_{\phi}
,
\bm{z}\mapsto\ell_{\mathrm{NPE}}(q_{\phi};\bm{z})
is upper-semicontinuous and belongs to
\mathcal{G}_{p}(\mathcal{Z})
, the
strong dual formulation
is exact and finite
(
24
, Theorem 1)
:

\displaystyle\sup_{\tilde{P}\in\mathcal{A}_{p}(P_{n};\varepsilon)}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{NPE}}(q_{\phi};\tilde{P})=\inf_{\lambda\geq 0}\!\left\{\lambda\varepsilon^{p}+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sup_{\bm{z}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Z}}\!\Big(\ell_{\mathrm{NPE}}(q_{\phi};\bm{z}^{\prime})-\lambda\|\bm{z}^{\prime}-\bm{z}_{i}\|^{p}\Big)\right\}.

(3)

Although the dual formulation retains the upper bound guarantee we seek and is more tractable than the primal Wasserstein-DRO objective, the inner supremum remains computationally demanding.
Focusing on the case
p=2
, we therefore derive in
Proposition

4.2
a tractable finite-sample upper bound on the dual objective based on a second-order Taylor argument and growth control of the loss.
This yields a regularised objective well-suited for gradient-based optimisation.
In practice, we omit the
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{2})
remainder, which we expect to be negligible in most cases.
The proof is in Appendix
B.2
.

Proposition 4.2
.

Fix
q_{\phi}\in\mathcal{Q}
.
Suppose that
\bm{z}\mapsto\ell_{\mathrm{NPE}}(q_{\phi};\bm{z})
admits a twice continuously differentiable extension to
\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathcal{Z}}}
, and belongs to

\mathcal{G}_{2}(\mathcal{Z})
.
Then, for every
\varepsilon\geq 0
,

\textstyle\sup_{\tilde{P}\in\mathcal{A}_{2}(P_{n};\epsilon)}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{NPE}}(q_{\phi};\tilde{P})\leq\underbrace{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{NPE}}(q_{\phi};P_{n})+\varepsilon\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\nabla_{\bm{z}}\ell_{\mathrm{NPE}}(q_{\phi};\bm{z}_{i})\|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}}_{\mathrm{the\;tractable\;DRO-NPE\;objective}}+\,C_{\phi}^{n}\varepsilon^{2}.

Further, if
\|\nabla_{\bm{z}}\ell_{\mathrm{NPE}}(q_{\phi};\cdot)\|\in\mathcal{G}_{\beta}(\mathcal{Z})
,
\|\nabla_{\bm{z}}^{2}\ell_{\mathrm{NPE}}(q_{\phi};\cdot)\|\in\mathcal{G}_{\alpha}(\mathcal{Z})
,
and
P
is light-tailed, i.e. there exist
\eta>0
,
a>0
, and
M<\infty

such that
\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}\sim P}\big[\exp(\eta\|\bm{z}\|^{a})\big]\leq M
,
then, for
m:=\max\{2,2\beta,\alpha\}
and any sequence

\varepsilon_{n}=o((\log n)^{-m/2a})
, the second-order term
C_{\phi}^{n}\varepsilon_{n}^{2}
is negligible in probability.

Proposition

4.2
assumes twice continuous differentiability and growth control to upper-bound the 2-Wasserstein DRO objective by the tractable DRO-NPE objective with remainder of order
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{2})
.
Under further light-tail and derivative-growth conditions, this remainder vanishes in probability for any
\varepsilon_{n}
decaying slightly faster than logarithmically, a mild requirement satisfied in particular by polynomial rates.
Importantly, as shown in
Lemma

4.3
below, these assumptions hold for Masked Autoregressive Flows (MAFs)
(
55
)
and coupling flows, the default density estimators in the
sbi

(
69
)
and
BayesFlow

(
40
)
libraries; see Appendix
B.4
for a proof.
By contrast, existing upper bounds for
p=2
Wasserstein DRO typically require global smoothness assumptions on the loss, such as Lipschitz gradients
(
25
, e.g.,)
, and are therefore not directly suited to NPE with normalising flows.

Lemma 4.3
.

Suppose
q_{\phi}
is a MAF or coupling flow (formally defined in Appendix
B.4
), then
\ell_{\mathrm{NPE}}
satisfies the assumptions of
Proposition

4.2
.

Following
Proposition

4.2
, in practice, we minimise the tractable DRO-NPE objective:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{NPE}}(q_{\phi};P_{n})+\varepsilon\Omega(\phi;P_{n}),\quad\text{where}\quad\Omega(\phi;P_{n}):=\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\nabla_{\bm{z}}\ell_{\mathrm{NPE}}(q_{\phi};\bm{z}_{i})\|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

As
\varepsilon\to 0
, this reduces to standard NPE.
As
\varepsilon
increases, the regularisation term dominates, and the optimisation problem approaches

\inf_{\phi}\Omega(\phi;P_{n})\geq 0
.
If this infimum is attained at zero, then any minimiser
\phi_{\text{min}}
satisfies

\nabla_{\bm{z}}\log q_{\phi_{\text{min}}}(\bm{\theta}_{i}\mid\bm{x}_{i})=0\;\forall i=1,\dots,n.

Provided
q_{\phi_{\text{min}}}(\bm{\theta}_{i}\mid\bm{x}_{i})>0
, this implies
\nabla_{\bm{z}}q_{\phi_{\text{min}}}(\bm{\theta}_{i}\mid\bm{x}_{i})=0\quad\forall i=1,\dots,n
.
Thus, for large
\varepsilon
, the objective favours conditional densities that vary less sharply in
\bm{z}
at the simulated pairs.
This is consistent with improving expected coverage of HPDR, since overconfident densities tend to allocate excess mass around training pairs.

The DRO-NPE objective also requires the selection of
\varepsilon
.
The theoretical choice of
\varepsilon
in
Theorem

4.1
is a high-probability concentration radius ensuring
P\in\mathcal{A}_{p}(P_{n};\varepsilon)
, but it depends on unknown constants and is not directly usable.
We therefore follow common DRO practice and treat
\varepsilon
as a hyperparameter selected using a validation set
(
52
)
.
In our setting, a natural criterion is KL-based miscalibration.
Indeed,
Theorem

4.1
implies that miscalibration upper-bounds miscoverage, so reducing the former can also improve the latter when the bound is sufficiently tight.
As noted in
Section

3
, miscalibration is also linked to entropy maximisation, making it a principled and interpretable criterion for choosing
\varepsilon
.
We estimate
\mathrm{kl}_{\mathrm{cal}}^{S}
by density-ratio estimation, using its representation as the KL divergence between the dependent joint
P_{\tilde{U},\bm{x}}
, where
\tilde{U}\mid\bm{x}\sim(F^{Q}_{S\mid\bm{x}})_{\#}P_{S\mid\bm{x}}
, and the independent reference
P_{U,\bm{x}}=P_{U}\otimes P_{\bm{x}}
, where
\otimes
denotes the product distribution, i.e.
P_{U,\bm{x}}
is the joint distribution obtained when
U\sim\mathcal{U}[0,1]
is independent of
\bm{x}
. This yields an
\varepsilon
-dependent objective, optimised using standard training-validation splits and a one-dimensional search via Bayesian optimisation
(
26
)
; see Appendix
C.1
for details.

Further statistical guarantees for DRO-NPE.

We derive statistical guarantees on the excess risk, miscalibration and miscoverage of both the exact and tractable formulations of DRO-NPE and NPE relative to the oracle estimator
\phi^{\star}:=\arg\min_{\phi}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{NPE}}(q_{\phi};P)
(see Appendix
B.3
). To the best of our knowledge, comparable guarantees are not available for existing methods. In particular, under a stronger Lipschitz gradient assumption that could be satisfied in the NPE setting for bounded
\Theta
, we derive a DRO-NPE excess risk bound that depends on
\Omega(\phi^{\star};P_{n})
along with other
n-
dependent constants. In contrast, the equivalent excess risk bound for NPE additionally depends on the regulariser
\Omega(\hat{\phi}_{n};P_{n})
, which relies on the empirical risk minimiser. As a result, DRO-NPE can yield sharper bounds compared to NPE, when
q_{\hat{\phi}_{n}}
exhibits high functional variation relative to
q_{\phi^{\star}}
.

Analysing DRO-NPE

We now study the behaviour of DRO-NPE with respect to the key design choices involved in its implementation. All experiments are conducted using the Lotka–Volterra simulator with

.

Generalisation gap versus
\varepsilon
.

We first examine how
\varepsilon
affects the generalisation gap by comparing empirical risk on training and test data. As shown in

(a), small values of
\varepsilon
lead to a large generalisation gap and poor test performance, reflecting the optimistic bias of empirical risk minimisation in this setting.
As
\varepsilon
increases, the gap narrows and test performance improves, attaining its minimum at
\varepsilon=0.14
. Further increasing
\varepsilon
continues to reduce the gap but worsens test performance, as the posterior estimator becomes excessively conservative.

Choice of hyperparameter-selection metric.

In
Section

5.1
, we select
\varepsilon
using the KL-based miscalibration metric
\mathrm{kl}_{\mathrm{cal}}^{q}
, corresponding to
S(\bm{\theta},\bm{x})=q(\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x})
.
We compare this choice with three alternatives: NLPD, absolute miscoverage
\Delta_{\mathrm{cov}}^{0.05}
, and
\mathrm{kl}_{\mathrm{cal}}^{\|\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}_{0}\|_{2}}
, computed with
S(\bm{\theta},\bm{x})=\|\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}_{0}\|_{2}
for a fixed reference point
\bm{\theta}_{0}
.
NLPD is a natural validation metric because it equals the NPE loss and upper-bounds
\mathrm{kl}_{\mathrm{cal}}^{q}
, while absolute miscoverage directly measures deviation from nominal coverage.
As shown in
Figure

3
(b), selecting
\varepsilon
via
\mathrm{kl}_{\mathrm{cal}}^{q}
yields a coverage curve closest to the diagonal.
Selection via NLPD and
\Delta_{\mathrm{cov}}^{0.05}
produces more conservative posteriors, whereas
\mathrm{kl}_{\mathrm{cal}}^{\|\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}_{0}\|_{2}}
leads to undercoverage and higher variability across runs. Results for SLCP are in Appendix

.

To understand the behaviour of absolute miscoverage,

as a function of
\varepsilon
, together with
\mathrm{kl}_{\mathrm{cal}}^{q}
.
We observe that

decreases nearly monotonically with

, as larger values produce wider, more conservative posteriors and hence better coverage. This explains why selecting

By contrast,

is minimised at an intermediate value of

, making it more informative for selecting

in DRO-NPE.

Computational cost.

DRO-NPE is more expensive than standard NPE, but only by a moderate constant factor. The main overhead comes from higher-order automatic differentiation through
\nabla_{\bm{z}}\log q_{\phi}(\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x})
, so the computational cost of this regularisation term grows with the joint parameter–data dimension

.
As shown in
Figure

(d), DRO-NPE takes roughly

the wall-clock time of standard NPE on CPU, but only about

on GPU, as the additional operations are highly parallelisable. This relative overhead varies little with

over the range considered, though it may increase once GPU resources are saturated.

6
Conclusion

We proposed DRO-NPE, a conservative formulation of NPE, to address finite-simulation failure modes including overconfidence, poor calibration, and large generalisation gaps. Our approach yields an objective that upper bounds the population NPE loss and KL-based metrics for uncertainty-quantification error, admits a tractable regularised objective, and enjoys statistical guarantees on excess risk, miscoverage, and miscalibration.
Importantly, it remains compatible with standard normalising-flow architectures commonly used in SBI.
However, DRO-NPE inherits limitations from DRO: it is more expensive than standard NPE, and there remains a theory–practice gap in selecting
\varepsilon
, since computing a theoretical radius guaranteeing the derived upper bounds is typically infeasible.

Although we focused on NPE, DRO-NPE could be extended to other conditional density estimation problems, variational inference, amortised inference tasks, and losses beyond NPE via analogous DRO formulations.
It could also be combined with complementary post-hoc calibration methods
(
49
;
57
;
10
)
and ensembles
(
30
)
.
More broadly, DRO has provided one route to upper-bounding the population NPE objective; future work could investigate whether alternative frameworks, such as PAC-Bayes
(
1
;
29
)
, offer analogous guarantees that can yield practical training objectives.

Appendix A
Theory: calibration and expected coverage of HPDR

In this section, we first show that the expected coverage of HPDR is a special case of calibration. Second, we present the data-processing inequality (DPI) and the chain rule for the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence, which lets us compare
P
and
Q
after applying pushforwards. Third, we prove that KL-based miscoverage upper-bounds its absolute value notion. Finally, we establish that KL-based miscalibration upper-bounds miscoverage.

We first prove that the expected coverage of HPDR is a special case of calibration, a result that was briefly shown in
15
.
This will require a statement more mathematical than
Definition

2.2
, presented below.
We say that
Q_{\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x}}
(or equivalently
q
) is calibrated w.r.t. to
S
if:

\textstyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}\sim P_{\bm{x}}}\left[P_{S\mid\bm{x}}\left(F^{Q}_{S\mid\bm{x}}(S_{\bm{x}}^{P})\leq u\right)\right]=u\qquad\forall u\in[0,1].

(4)

This is equivalent to stating that
F^{Q}_{S\mid\bm{x}}(S_{\bm{x}}^{P})
is distributed as
\mathcal{U}[0,1]
under
\bm{x}\sim P_{\bm{x}}
.

Lemma A.1

(Expected coverage of HPDR as a special case of calibration)
.

Fix
q
. Let
S(\bm{\theta};\bm{x}):=q(\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x})
, and assume that the CDF of
Q_{S\mid\bm{x}}
is continuous and that
F^{Q}_{S\mid\bm{x}}
is strictly increasing. Then, for every
\alpha\in(0,1)
,

C_{\alpha}^{P}(q)=1-\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}\sim P_{\bm{x}}}\left[P_{S\mid\bm{x}}\left(F^{Q}_{S\mid\bm{x}}(S_{\bm{x}}^{P})<\alpha\right)\right].

Proof.

Suppose
S(\bm{\theta};\bm{x})=q(\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x})

\displaystyle F^{Q}_{S\mid\bm{x}}(\tau^{q}_{\alpha}(\bm{x}))

\displaystyle=Q_{S\mid\bm{x}}((-\infty,\tau_{\alpha}^{q}(\bm{x})])

\displaystyle=Q_{\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x}}(\{S(\bm{\theta};\bm{x})\leq\tau_{\alpha}^{q}(\bm{x})\})

\displaystyle=Q_{\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x}}(\{q(\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x})\leq\tau_{\alpha}^{q}(\bm{x})\})=\alpha,

where the first equality is by definition of the cumulative distribution function, the second by that of the pushforward, the third by that of
S
, and the last by that of
\tau_{\alpha}^{q}(\bm{x})
together with continuity of the CDF of
Q_{S\mid\bm{x}}

Therefore, denoting
S_{\bm{x}}^{P}=S(\bm{\theta};\bm{x})

\displaystyle P_{\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x}}\left(R_{\alpha}^{q}(\bm{x})\right)

\displaystyle=P_{\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x}}\left(q(\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x})\geq\tau^{q}_{\alpha}(\bm{x})\right)

\displaystyle=P_{S\mid\bm{x}}\left(S_{\bm{x}}^{P}\geq\tau^{q}_{\alpha}(\bm{x})\right)

\displaystyle=P_{S\mid\bm{x}}\left(F^{Q}_{S\mid\bm{x}}(S_{\bm{x}}^{P})\geq F^{Q}_{S\mid\bm{x}}(\tau^{q}_{\alpha}(\bm{x}))\right)

\displaystyle=P_{S\mid\bm{x}}\left(F^{Q}_{S\mid\bm{x}}(S_{\bm{x}}^{P})\geq\alpha\right).

The first equality is the definition of the HPDR at level

The second follows from the choice

.
The third uses that

The last equality uses that

\displaystyle C_{\alpha}^{P}(q)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}\sim P_{\bm{x}}}\left[P_{\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x}}(R_{\alpha}^{q}(\bm{x}))\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}\sim P_{\bm{x}}}\left[P_{S\mid\bm{x}}\left(F^{Q}_{S\mid\bm{x}}(S_{\bm{x}}^{P})\geq\alpha\right)\right]

\displaystyle=1-\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}\sim P_{\bm{x}}}\left[P_{S\mid\bm{x}}\left(F^{Q}_{S\mid\bm{x}}(S_{\bm{x}}^{P})<\alpha\right)\right].

and

We now define the expected Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence, which will be used frequently in this paper. Suppose
P_{\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x}}
is absolutely continuous w.r.t.
Q_{\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x}}
, and for simplicity, that
P_{\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x}}
and

admit densities
p(\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x}),q(\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x})
. Then, the KL divergence is:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}\sim P_{\bm{x}}}\left[\mathrm{KL}\Big(P_{\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x}}\;\|\;Q_{\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x}}\Big)\right]:=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}\sim P_{\bm{x}}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\theta}\sim P_{\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x}}}\left[\log\frac{p(\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x})}{q(\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x})}\right]\right].

We can then state the data-processing inequality and the chain rule for the KL divergence.

Lemma A.2

(Theorem 2.15 & Corollary 2.18 from
58
. Chain rule and data-processing inequality for KL divergence)
.

Suppose that for some measurable space
\mathcal{Y}

f:\Theta\to\mathcal{Y}
is a measurable function. Then, for any fixed
\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}
and any two distributions
P_{\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x}},Q_{\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x}}
, such that
P_{\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x}}\ll Q_{\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x}}
, i.e.
P_{\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x}}
is absolutely continuous w.r.t.
Q_{\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x}}
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{KL}(P_{\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x}}\|Q_{\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x}})=\mathrm{KL}(f_{\#}P_{\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x}}\|f_{\#}Q_{\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x}})+\mathbb{E}_{t\sim f_{\#}P_{\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x}}}\left[\mathrm{KL}(P_{\bm{\theta}\mid f(\bm{\theta})=t,\bm{x}}\|Q_{\bm{\theta}\mid f(\bm{\theta})=t,\bm{x}})\right].

(5)

In particular,

\mathrm{KL}(f_{\#}P_{\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x}}\|f_{\#}Q_{\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x}})\leq\mathrm{KL}(P_{\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x}}\|Q_{\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x}}),

and equality holds if
f
is one-to-one, or when
f
is a sufficient statistic for testing
P_{\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x}}
against
Q_{\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x}}
.

We now show that KL-based miscoverage controls the usual absolute coverage error.
In particular, by Pinsker’s inequality, small KL-based miscoverage implies small absolute miscoverage.
When this upper bound is reasonably tight, reducing the former also reduces the latter.
The proof uses the following elementary identity for Bernoulli distributions.
Let
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
be Bernoulli distributions with probability parameters
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
, respectively. Then

\mathrm{TV}(P_{1},P_{2}):=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{s\in\{0,1\}}|P_{1}(s)-P_{2}(s)|=|p_{1}-p_{2}|.

Lemma A.3

(KL-based miscoverage upper-bounds its absolute value notion)
.

Define
\mathrm{kl}_{\mathrm{cov}}^{\alpha}
as in
Definition

3.1
, and
\Delta_{\mathrm{cov}}^{\alpha}(q;P):=\left|C_{\alpha}^{P}(q)-(1-\alpha)\right|
. Then, for any
\alpha\in(0,1)
and
q(\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x})
,

\Delta_{\mathrm{cov}}^{\alpha}(q;P)\leq\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{kl}_{\mathrm{cov}}^{\alpha}(q;P)}.

Proof.

By definition of
C_{\alpha}^{P}
, we have that

\displaystyle\Delta_{\mathrm{cov}}^{\alpha}(q;P)=|\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}\sim P_{\bm{x}}}[P_{\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x}}(R_{\alpha}^{q}(\bm{x}))-(1-\alpha)]|\leq\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}\sim P_{\bm{x}}}[|P_{\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x}}(R_{\alpha}^{q}(\bm{x}))-(1-\alpha)|].

Then, for each fixed
\bm{x}
,

|P_{\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x}}(R_{\alpha}^{q}(\bm{x}))-(1-\alpha)|=\mathrm{TV}\left((T_{R_{\alpha}^{q}(\bm{x})})_{\#}P_{\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x}},\,(T_{R_{\alpha}^{q}(\bm{x})})_{\#}Q_{\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x}}\right),

since
(T_{R_{\alpha}^{q}(\bm{x})})_{\#}P_{\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x}}
and
(T_{R_{\alpha}^{q}(\bm{x})})_{\#}Q_{\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x}}
are Bernoulli laws with parameters
P_{\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x}}(R_{\alpha}^{q}(\bm{x}))
and
Q_{\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x}}(R_{\alpha}^{q}(\bm{x}))=1-\alpha
. Therefore, applying Pinsker’s inequality pointwise in
\bm{x}
yields:

\displaystyle\Delta_{\mathrm{cov}}^{\alpha}(q;P)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}\sim P_{\bm{x}}}\left[\mathrm{TV}\left((T_{R_{\alpha}^{q}(\bm{x})})_{\#}P_{\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x}},\,(T_{R_{\alpha}^{q}(\bm{x})})_{\#}Q_{\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}\sim P_{\bm{x}}}\left[\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{KL}\left((T_{R_{\alpha}^{q}(\bm{x})})_{\#}P_{\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x}}\,\|\,(T_{R_{\alpha}^{q}(\bm{x})})_{\#}Q_{\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x}}\right)}\right].

Finally, Jensen’s inequality gives:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}\sim P_{\bm{x}}}\left[\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{KL}\left((T_{R_{\alpha}^{q}(\bm{x})})_{\#}P_{\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x}}\,\|\,(T_{R_{\alpha}^{q}(\bm{x})})_{\#}Q_{\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x}}\right)}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}\sim P_{\bm{x}}}\left[\mathrm{KL}\left((T_{R_{\alpha}^{q}(\bm{x})})_{\#}P_{\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x}}\,\|\,(T_{R_{\alpha}^{q}(\bm{x})})_{\#}Q_{\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x}}\right)\right]}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{kl}_{\mathrm{cov}}^{\alpha}(q;P)}.

∎

We end this section by showing that miscalibration is an upper-bound to miscoverage. This result will be used to prove
Theorem

4.1
in the next section.

Lemma A.4

(Miscalibration upper-bounds miscoverage)
.

Define

\mathrm{kl}_{\mathrm{NPE}}(q;P):=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}\sim P_{\bm{x}}}\left[\mathrm{KL}(P_{\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x}}\;\|\;Q_{\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x}})\right]=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{NPE}}(q;P)+K(P).

Then, for any distribution
Q
with density
q
,

\sup_{\alpha\in(0,1)}\mathrm{kl}_{\mathrm{cov}}^{\alpha}(q;P)\leq\mathrm{kl}_{\mathrm{NPE}}(q;P)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathrm{kl}_{\mathrm{cal}}^{S}(q;P)\leq\mathrm{kl}_{\mathrm{NPE}}(q;P).

Further, suppose that
S(\bm{\theta};\bm{x})=f(q(\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x}))
for some strictly increasing
f:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
, and
F^{Q}_{S\mid\bm{x}}
is also strictly increasing. Then

\sup_{\alpha\in(0,1)}\mathrm{kl}_{\mathrm{cov}}^{\alpha}(q;P)\leq\mathrm{kl}_{\mathrm{cal}}^{S}(q;P)\leq\mathrm{kl}_{\mathrm{NPE}}(q;P).

Proof.

Showing that
\mathrm{kl}_{\mathrm{cov}}^{\alpha}(q;P)\leq\mathrm{kl}_{\mathrm{NPE}}(q;P)
and
\mathrm{kl}_{\mathrm{cal}}^{S}(q;P)\leq\mathrm{kl}_{\mathrm{NPE}}(q;P)
is a direct application of the data-processing inequality (DPI) from
Lemma

A.2
.
In particular, since
\mathrm{kl}_{\mathrm{cov}}^{\alpha}(q;P)\leq\mathrm{kl}_{\mathrm{NPE}}(q;P)
holds for any
\alpha\in(0,1)
, we can further conclude that
\sup_{\alpha\in(0,1)}\mathrm{kl}_{\mathrm{cov}}^{\alpha}(q;P)\leq\mathrm{kl}_{\mathrm{NPE}}(q;P)
.
We then focus on showing
\mathrm{kl}_{\mathrm{cov}}^{\alpha}(q;P)\leq\mathrm{kl}_{\mathrm{cal}}^{S}(q;P)
.

First, whenever
S(\bm{\theta};\bm{x})=f(q(\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x}))
and
f
is strictly increasing, then

\displaystyle(T_{R_{\alpha}^{q}(\bm{x})})_{\#}P_{\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x}}(1)

\displaystyle=P_{\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x}}(R_{\alpha}^{q}(\bm{x}))

\displaystyle=P_{\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x}}(\{q(\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x})\geq\tau_{\alpha}^{q}(\bm{x})\})

\displaystyle=P_{\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x}}(\{f(q(\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x}))\geq f(\tau_{\alpha}^{q}(\bm{x}))\})\quad\text{since }f\text{ is strictly increasing,}

\displaystyle=P_{S\mid\bm{x}}(\{s\geq f(\tau_{\alpha}^{q}(\bm{x}))\})\quad\text{using that }S(\bm{\theta};\bm{x})=f(q(\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x})),

\displaystyle=(T_{A_{\alpha}^{q}(\bm{x})})_{\#}P_{S\mid\bm{x}}(1),

where
A_{\alpha}^{q}(\bm{x}):=\{s\geq f(\tau_{\alpha}^{q}(\bm{x}))\}
, and similarly,

(T_{R_{\alpha}^{q}(\bm{x})})_{\#}P_{\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x}}(0)=P_{S\mid\bm{x}}(\{s<f(\tau_{\alpha}^{q}(\bm{x}))\})=(T_{A_{\alpha}^{q}(\bm{x})})_{\#}P_{S\mid\bm{x}}(0)

Therefore, the laws
(T_{R_{\alpha}^{q}(\bm{x})})_{\#}P_{\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x}}
and
(T_{A_{\alpha}^{q}(\bm{x})})_{\#}P_{S\mid\bm{x}}
coincide. This also holds for
(T_{R_{\alpha}^{q}(\bm{x})})_{\#}Q_{\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x}}
and
(T_{A_{\alpha}^{q}(\bm{x})})_{\#}Q_{S\mid\bm{x}}
.
Hence, we can rewrite miscoverage as follows:

\displaystyle\mathrm{kl}_{\mathrm{cov}}^{\alpha}(q;P)=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}\sim P_{\bm{x}}}\left[\mathrm{KL}\left((T_{A_{\alpha}^{q}(\bm{x})})_{\#}P_{S\mid\bm{x}}\;\Big\|\;(T_{A_{\alpha}^{q}(\bm{x})})_{\#}Q_{S\mid\bm{x}}\right)\right].

But,

\displaystyle T_{A_{\alpha}^{q}(\bm{x})}(s)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{1}\{s\geq f(\tau^{q}_{\alpha}(\bm{x}))\}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{1}\{F^{Q}_{S\mid\bm{x}}(s)\geq F^{Q}_{S\mid\bm{x}}(f(\tau^{q}_{\alpha}(\bm{x})))\}\quad\text{since }F_{S\mid\bm{x}}^{Q}\text{ is strictly increasing},

\displaystyle=T_{B^{q}_{\alpha}(\bm{x})}(F^{Q}_{S\mid\bm{x}}(s))

for
B_{\alpha}^{q}(\bm{x}):=[F^{Q}_{S\mid\bm{x}}(f(\tau^{q}_{\alpha}(\bm{x}))),1]
. Therefore,

(T_{A_{\alpha}^{q}(\bm{x})})_{\#}P_{S\mid\bm{x}}=(T_{B_{\alpha}^{q}(\bm{x})})_{\#}\Big((F^{Q}_{S\mid\bm{x}})_{\#}P_{S\mid\bm{x}}\Big),

and similarly for
Q_{S\mid\bm{x}}
.
Thus,

\displaystyle\mathrm{kl}_{\mathrm{cov}}^{\alpha}(q;P)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}\sim P_{\bm{x}}}\left[\mathrm{KL}\left((T_{B_{\alpha}^{q}(\bm{x})})_{\#}\Big((F^{Q}_{S\mid\bm{x}})_{\#}P_{S\mid\bm{x}}\Big)\Big\|(T_{B_{\alpha}^{q}(\bm{x})})_{\#}\Big((F^{Q}_{S\mid\bm{x}})_{\#}Q_{S\mid\bm{x}}\Big)\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}\sim P_{\bm{x}}}\left[\mathrm{KL}\left((F^{Q}_{S\mid\bm{x}})_{\#}P_{S\mid\bm{x}}\Big\|(F^{Q}_{S\mid\bm{x}})_{\#}Q_{S\mid\bm{x}}\right)\right]\quad\text{by the DPI,}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{kl}_{\mathrm{cal}}^{S}(q;P).

Since this line of reasoning holds for any
\alpha\in(0,1)
, we further conclude that

\sup_{\alpha\in(0,1)}\mathrm{kl}_{\mathrm{cov}}^{\alpha}(q;P)\leq\mathrm{kl}_{\mathrm{cal}}^{S}(q;P).

∎

Lemma B.1

(Adapting Theorem 2 from
23
)
.

Suppose that
p\in[1,d_{\mathcal{Z}}/2)
, and that there exists
\alpha>2p
such that
M_{\alpha}:=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}\sim P}[\|\bm{z}\|^{\alpha}]<\infty
. Then,
\forall\varsigma\in(0,\alpha)
there exists positive constants
C_{1},C_{2}
depending only on
p,d_{\mathcal{Z}},\alpha,M_{\alpha},\varsigma
such that for any
\delta>0
,

\mathbb{P}(W_{p}(P_{n},P)\leq\varepsilon)\geq 1-\delta,

whenever
\varepsilon\geq\varepsilon(n,\delta)
, where:

\varepsilon(n,\delta):=\max\left\{\left(\frac{1}{C_{1}n}\log\frac{2C_{2}}{\delta}\right)^{\frac{1}{d_{\mathcal{Z}}}},\;\left(\frac{2C_{2}}{\delta}\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha-\varsigma}}n^{\frac{1}{(\alpha-\varsigma)}-\frac{1}{p}}\right\}

Proof.

From Theorem 2 of
23
, whenever
P
is probability distribution on
\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathcal{Z}}}
, if there exists
\alpha>2p
such that
M_{\alpha}:=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}\sim P}[\|\bm{z}\|^{\alpha}]<\infty
, then for all
n\geq 1
,
\varepsilon>0
,

\mathbb{P}(W_{p}^{p}(P_{n},P)\geq\varepsilon)\leq a(n,\varepsilon)\mathbf{1}_{\varepsilon\leq 1}+b(n,\varepsilon),

where

a(n,\varepsilon)=C_{2}\begin{cases}\exp(-C_{1}n\varepsilon^{2}),&\text{ if }\;p>\frac{d_{\mathcal{Z}}}{2}\\
\exp\left(-C_{1}n\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{\log\left(2+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)}\right)^{2}\right)&\text{ if }\;p=\frac{d_{\mathcal{Z}}}{2}\\
\exp\left(-C_{1}n\varepsilon^{\frac{d_{\mathcal{Z}}}{p}}\right)&\text{ if }\;p\in[1,\frac{d_{\mathcal{Z}}}{2})\end{cases}

and

b(n,\varepsilon)=C_{2}n(n\varepsilon)^{-\frac{\alpha-\varsigma}{p}}\quad\forall\varsigma\in(0,\alpha).

The positive constants
C_{1},C_{2}
depend only on
p,d_{\mathcal{Z}}
and on
\alpha,M_{\alpha},\varsigma
.
Therefore,

\mathbb{P}(W_{p}(P_{n},P)\leq\varepsilon)=1-\mathbb{P}(W_{p}(P_{n},P)\geq\varepsilon)\geq 1-a(n,\varepsilon^{p})\mathbf{1}_{\varepsilon^{p}\leq 1}-b(n,\varepsilon^{p})

For simplicity, constrain
p\in[1,d_{\mathcal{Z}}/2)
, which is most relevant in our setting. The other cases follow the same logic.

Fix
\delta>0
. We wish to find conditions for
\varepsilon
in terms of
\delta,n
and other constants such that:

\mathbb{P}(W_{p}(P_{n},P)\leq\varepsilon)\geq 1-\delta.

We do so by selecting
\varepsilon
such that it satisfies both
a(n,\varepsilon^{p})\leq\delta/2,\;b(n,\varepsilon^{p})\leq\delta/2
, so that:

1-a(n,\varepsilon^{p})\mathbf{1}_{\varepsilon^{p}\leq 1}-b(n,\varepsilon^{p})\geq 1-\delta\implies\mathbb{P}(W_{p}(P_{n},P)\leq\varepsilon)\geq 1-\delta.

Therefore,

a(n,\varepsilon^{p})=C_{2}\exp(-C_{1}n\varepsilon^{d_{\mathcal{Z}}})\leq\frac{\delta}{2}\implies\varepsilon_{a}=\left(\frac{1}{C_{1}n}\log\frac{2C_{2}}{\delta}\right)^{\frac{1}{d_{\mathcal{Z}}}},

and similarly

b(n,\varepsilon^{p})=C_{2}n(n\varepsilon)^{-\frac{\alpha-\varsigma}{p}}\leq\frac{\delta}{2}\implies\varepsilon_{b}=\left(\frac{2C_{2}}{\delta}\right)^{\frac{1}{q-\varsigma}}n^{\frac{1}{\alpha-\varsigma}-\frac{1}{p}}.

We then select:

\varepsilon(n,\delta):=\max\left\{\left(\frac{1}{C_{1}n}\log\frac{2C_{2}}{\delta}\right)^{\frac{1}{d_{\mathcal{Z}}}},\;\left(\frac{2C_{2}}{\delta}\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha-\varsigma}}n^{\frac{1}{\alpha-\varsigma}-\frac{1}{p}}\right\}.

B.2
Proof of
Proposition

4.2

We first show that under the assumption of
2-
growth, we can prove a local quadratic upper bound, so long as the function class is also twice continuously differentiable on
\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathcal{Z}}}
.

Lemma B.2
.

Fix
q_{\phi}\in Q
. Suppose that
\bm{z}\mapsto\ell(q_{\phi};\bm{z})\in\mathcal{G}_{2}(\mathcal{Z})
, and that it is twice continuously differentiable on
\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathcal{Z}}}
. Then, for any fixed
\bm{z}_{0}\in\mathcal{Z}
, there exists a
C_{\phi}(\bm{z}_{0})<\infty
which is constant in
\bm{z}
such that

\ell(q_{\phi};\bm{z})\leq\ell(q_{\phi};\bm{z}_{0})+\nabla_{\bm{z}}\ell(q_{\phi};\bm{z}_{0})^{\top}(\bm{z}-\bm{z}_{0})+C_{\phi}(\bm{z}_{0})\|\bm{z}-\bm{z}_{0}\|^{2},\quad\forall\bm{z}\in\mathcal{Z}.

Proof.

We proceed by considering two cases: when
\|\bm{z}-\bm{z}_{0}\|<1
and
\|\bm{z}-\bm{z}_{0}\|\geq 1
. We denote
B(\bm{z}_{0},1):=\{\bm{z}:\|\bm{z}-\bm{z}_{0}\|<1\}
, and
\bar{B}(\bm{z}_{0},1)
its closure.

Case 1:
\|\bm{z}-\bm{z}_{0}\|<1
: Since
B(\bm{z}_{0},1)\subset\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathcal{Z}}}
, and the mapping
\bm{z}\mapsto\ell(q_{\phi};\bm{z})
is continuously differentiable on
\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathcal{Z}}}
, which is open and convex, Taylor’s theorem (Theorem 2.68 of
22
) applies at
\bm{z}_{0}
for every
\bm{z}\in B(\bm{z}_{0},1)
:

\ell(q_{\phi};\bm{z})=\ell(q_{\phi};\bm{z}_{0})+\nabla_{\bm{z}}\ell(q_{\phi};\bm{z}_{0})^{\top}(\bm{z}-\bm{z}_{0})+R_{1}(\bm{z}_{0},\bm{z}-\bm{z}_{0}),\quad\forall\bm{z}\in B(\bm{z}_{0},1)

where
R_{1}(\bm{z}_{0},\bm{z}-\bm{z}_{0})
is the Taylor expansion remainder term.
Further, since
\bar{B}(\bm{z}_{0},1)
is compact and the Hessian
\nabla_{\bm{z}}^{2}\ell(q_{\phi};\bm{z})
is continuous by assumption, the Hessian has finite norm:
\|\nabla_{\bm{z}}^{2}\ell(q_{\phi};\bm{z})\|\leq M(\bm{z}_{0})\;\forall\bm{z}\in\bar{B}(\bm{z}_{0},1)
for some constant
M(\bm{z}_{0})
independent of
\bm{z}
.
Therefore, with Corollary 2.75 of
22
, we obtain:

|R_{1}(\bm{z}_{0},\bm{z}-\bm{z}_{0})|\leq\frac{M(\bm{z}_{0})}{2}\|\bm{z}-\bm{z}_{0}\|^{2},\quad\forall\bm{z}\in B(\bm{z}_{0},1).

We then conclude:

\ell(q_{\phi};\bm{z})\leq\ell(q_{\phi};\bm{z}_{0})+\nabla_{\bm{z}}\ell(q_{\phi};\bm{z}_{0})^{\top}(\bm{z}-\bm{z}_{0})+\frac{M(\bm{z}_{0})}{2}\|\bm{z}-\bm{z}_{0}\|^{2},\quad\forall\bm{z}\in B(\bm{z}_{0},1).

Case 2:
\|\bm{z}-\bm{z}_{0}\|\geq 1
: Notice that

-\nabla_{\bm{z}}\ell(q_{\phi};\bm{z}_{0})^{\top}(\bm{z}-\bm{z}_{0})\leq\|\nabla_{\bm{z}}\ell(q_{\phi};\bm{z}_{0})\|\|\bm{z}-\bm{z}_{0}\|\leq\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla_{\bm{z}}\ell(q_{\phi};\bm{z}_{0})\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|\bm{z}-\bm{z}_{0}\|^{2},

where the first inequality is by Cauchy-Schwarz, and the second holds because
whenever
a,b>0
,
ab\leq\frac{1}{2}a^{2}+\frac{1}{2}b^{2}
.
Further, using the growth condition of the map
\bm{z}\mapsto\ell(q_{\phi};\bm{z})
for
\rho=2
, for some constant
\tilde{C}_{\phi}
,

\ell(q_{\phi};\bm{z})\leq\tilde{C}_{\phi}(1+\|\bm{z}_{0}+\bm{z}-\bm{z}_{0}\|^{2})\leq\tilde{C}_{\phi}(1+2\|\bm{z}_{0}\|^{2}+2\|\bm{z}-\bm{z}_{0}\|^{2}),

where the second inequality is using that
\|a+b\|^{2}\leq 2\|a\|^{2}+2\|b\|^{2}
. Together, these results imply:

\displaystyle\ell(q_{\phi};\bm{z})-\nabla_{\bm{z}}\ell(q_{\phi};\bm{z}_{0})^{\top}(\bm{z}-\bm{z}_{0})

\displaystyle\leq\tilde{C}_{\phi}(1+2\|\bm{z}_{0}\|^{2}+2\|\bm{z}-\bm{z}_{0}\|^{2})

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla_{\bm{z}}\ell(q_{\phi};\bm{z}_{0})\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|\bm{z}-\bm{z}_{0}\|^{2}+\ell(q_{\phi};\bm{z}_{0})-\ell(q_{\phi};\bm{z}_{0})

\displaystyle:=K_{\phi}(\bm{z}_{0})+\ell(q_{\phi};\bm{z}_{0})+\left(2\tilde{C}_{\phi}+\frac{1}{2}\right)\|\bm{z}-\bm{z}_{0}\|^{2},

for some constant
K_{\phi}(\bm{z}_{0})
independent of
\bm{z}
, defined as:

K_{\phi}(\bm{z}_{0}):=\tilde{C}_{\phi}+2\tilde{C}_{\phi}\|\bm{z}_{0}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla_{\bm{z}}\ell(q_{\phi};\bm{z}_{0})\|^{2}-\ell(q_{\phi};\bm{z}_{0}).

Finally, since
\|z-\bm{z}_{0}\|\geq 1
, we have that

K_{\phi}(\bm{z}_{0})+\left(2\tilde{C}_{\phi}+\frac{1}{2}\right)\|\bm{z}-\bm{z}_{0}\|^{2}\leq\left(|K_{\phi}(\bm{z}_{0})|+2\tilde{C}_{\phi}+\frac{1}{2}\right)\|\bm{z}-\bm{z}_{0}\|^{2}:=A_{\phi}(\bm{z}_{0})\|\bm{z}-\bm{z}_{0}\|^{2},

implying

\ell(q_{\phi};\bm{z})\leq\ell(q_{\phi};\bm{z}_{0})+\nabla_{\bm{z}}\ell(q_{\phi};\bm{z}_{0})^{\top}(\bm{z}-\bm{z}_{0})+A_{\phi}(\bm{z}_{0})\|\bm{z}-\bm{z}_{0}\|^{2},\quad\forall\bm{z}\in\{\bm{z}\in\mathcal{Z}:\|\bm{z}-\bm{z}_{0}\|\geq 1\}.

Finally, taking
C_{\phi}(\bm{z}_{0}):=\max\left\{\frac{M(\bm{z}_{0})}{2},A_{\phi}(\bm{z}_{0})\right\}
, we get that

\ell(q_{\phi};\bm{z})\leq\ell(q_{\phi};\bm{z}_{0})+\nabla_{\bm{z}}\ell(q_{\phi};\bm{z}_{0})^{\top}(\bm{z}-\bm{z}_{0})+C_{\phi}(\bm{z}_{0})\|\bm{z}-\bm{z}_{0}\|^{2}\quad\forall\bm{z}\in\mathcal{Z}.

∎

This quadratic upper bound can then be used to solve the inner supremum in the strong dual of the Wasserstein DRO objective and prove

.

Proof of

4.2
.

We divide this proof into two parts. First, we derive the upper bound in terms of the gradient regulariser and the

remainder term. Second, we show that the remainder term remains negligible under the assumptions considered in the Proposition.
We keep the proof at a general level of loss function

First, recall that, as in

3

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{NPE}}(q_{\phi_{n}^{\mathrm{DRO-NPE}}};P)-\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{NPE}}(q_{\phi^{\star}};P)\leq 2\varepsilon_{n}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\nabla_{\bm{z}}\ell_{\mathrm{NPE}}(q_{\phi^{\star}};\bm{z}_{i})\|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}+2\rho_{n}+2h\varrho_{n}

Proof.

It follows from Corollary 3 of
25
that with probability at least
1-\frac{2}{n}-2e^{-t}
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}\sim P}[\ell_{\mathrm{NPE}}(q_{\phi};\bm{z})]\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell_{\mathrm{NPE}}(q_{\phi};\bm{z}_{i})+\varepsilon_{n}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\nabla_{\bm{z}}\ell_{\mathrm{NPE}}(q_{\phi};\bm{z}_{i})\|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}+\rho_{n}+h\varrho_{n},\,\forall\phi,

(9)

Note that the same result may be applied to the function
-\ell_{\mathrm{NPE}}(q_{\phi};\bm{z})
; this will be used below.
Importantly, since the bound holds uniformly over
\phi
, we may use it to handle
\hat{\phi}_{n}
.

Hence, applying the bound for
\ell_{\mathrm{NPE}}
and
\hat{\phi}_{n}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{NPE}}(q_{\hat{\phi}_{n}};P)-\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{NPE}}(q_{\phi^{\star}};P)

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{NPE}}(q_{\hat{\phi}_{n}};P_{n})+\varepsilon_{n}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\nabla_{\bm{z}}\ell_{\mathrm{NPE}}(q_{\hat{\phi}_{n}};\bm{z}_{i})\|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}+\rho_{n}+h\varrho_{n}-\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{NPE}}(q_{\phi^{\star}};P)

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{NPE}}(q_{\phi^{\star}};P_{n})+\varepsilon_{n}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\nabla_{\bm{z}}\ell_{\mathrm{NPE}}(q_{\hat{\phi}_{n}};\bm{z}_{i})\|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}+\rho_{n}+h\varrho_{n}-\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{NPE}}(q_{\phi^{\star}};P)

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon_{n}\left(\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\nabla_{\bm{z}}\ell_{\mathrm{NPE}}(q_{\phi^{\star}};\bm{z}_{i})\|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}+\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\nabla_{\bm{z}}\ell_{\mathrm{NPE}}(q_{\hat{\phi}_{n}};\bm{z}_{i})\|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)+2\rho_{n}+2h\varrho_{n},

where in the first inequality we use the corollary’s bound for
\phi=\hat{\phi}_{n}
; in the second inequality we use the fact that
\hat{\phi}_{n}
minimises
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{NPE}}(q_{\phi};P_{n})
; and in the third inequality we use
Equation

9
, applied to the pointwise
-\ell_{\mathrm{NPE}}(q_{\phi^{\star}};\bm{z})
.
Similarly,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{NPE}}(q_{\phi_{n}^{\mathrm{DRO-NPE}}};P)-\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{NPE}}(q_{\phi^{\star}};P)

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{NPE}}(q_{\phi_{n}^{\mathrm{DRO-NPE}}};P_{n})+\varepsilon_{n}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\nabla_{\bm{z}}\ell_{\mathrm{NPE}}(q_{\phi^{\mathrm{DRO-NPE}}};\bm{z}_{i})\|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}+\rho_{n}+h\varrho_{n}-\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{NPE}}(q_{\phi^{\star}};P)

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{NPE}}(q_{\phi^{\star}};P_{n})+\varepsilon_{n}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\nabla_{\bm{z}}\ell_{\mathrm{NPE}}(q_{\phi^{\star}};\bm{z}_{i})\|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}+\rho_{n}+h\varrho_{n}-\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{NPE}}(q_{\phi^{\star}};P)

\displaystyle\leq 2\varepsilon_{n}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}\sim P_{n}}[\|\nabla_{\bm{z}}\ell_{\mathrm{NPE}}(q_{\phi^{\star}};\bm{z})\|^{2}]^{\frac{1}{2}}+2\rho_{n}+2h\varrho_{n}

where we follow the same steps as before, except for the second inequality, where we use that
\phi^{\mathrm{DRO-NPE}}_{n}
minimises
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{NPE}}(q_{\phi};P_{n})+\varepsilon_{n}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\nabla_{\bm{z}}\ell_{\mathrm{NPE}}(q_{\phi};\bm{z}_{i})\|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}
.

∎

Lemma

B.4
derives an excess risk upper bound for NPE and for the regularised objective of DRO-NPE. The two bounds are derived in similar ways and differ only in the regulariser term
\Omega(\phi;P_{n})
, which can be thought of as an empirical measure of functional variation via the loss gradient.
Indeed, the key distinction between the DRO and NPE bounds derived is that the DRO-NPE bound only depends on the oracle variation
\Omega(\phi^{\star};P_{n})
.
In contrast, the NPE bound depends on both the oracle variation and the variation of the learned empirical minimiser,
\Omega(\hat{\phi}_{n};P_{n})
.
This means that the NPE bound can be affected by the roughness of the empirical minimiser.
Thus, the DRO guarantee is sharper whenever the empirical minimiser has larger variation than the oracle.

B.4
Verifying conditions for normalising flows (proof of
Lemma

4.3
)

In this section, we formally introduce MAFs and coupling flows and prove
Lemma

4.3
, showing that all assumptions of
Proposition

4.2
are satisfied when
q_{\phi}
is chosen to be one of these commonly used normalising flows.

Masked autoregressive flows.

We first write the masked autoregressive flows (MAFs;
55
) for
q_{\phi}(\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x})
, implemented in the
sbi
library
(
69
)
, defined through a single autoregressive transform
u=u(\bm{\theta},\bm{x})\in\mathcal{R}^{d_{\Theta}}
with coordinates

u_{i}(\bm{\theta},\bm{x}):=\frac{\theta_{i}-\mu_{i}(\bm{\theta}_{<i},\bm{x})}{\sigma_{i}(\bm{\theta}_{<i},\bm{x})},\qquad\bm{\theta}_{<i}:=(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{i-1}).

We set

\displaystyle\mu_{i}(\bm{\theta}_{<i},\bm{x})

\displaystyle=v_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{g}_{i}^{(L)}(\bm{\theta}_{<i},\bm{x})+c_{i},

\displaystyle\mathbf{g}_{i}^{(\ell)}(\bm{\theta}_{<i},\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\tanh\!\big(B_{i}^{(\ell)}\mathbf{g}_{i}^{(\ell-1)}(\bm{\theta}_{<i},\bm{x})+d_{i}^{(\ell)}\big),\quad\ell=1,\dots,L,

\displaystyle\mathbf{g}_{i}^{(0)}(\bm{\theta}_{<i},\bm{x})

\displaystyle=[\bm{\theta}_{<i};\bm{x}],

\displaystyle\sigma_{i}(\bm{\theta}_{<i},\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\text{softplus}\!\big(s_{i}(\bm{\theta}_{<i},\bm{x})\big),\qquad\text{softplus}(t):=\log(1+e^{t}),

\displaystyle s_{i}(\bm{\theta}_{<i},\bm{x})

\displaystyle=w_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{h}_{i}^{(L)}(\bm{\theta}_{<i},\bm{x})+b_{i},

\displaystyle\mathbf{h}_{i}^{(\ell)}(\bm{\theta}_{<i},\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\tanh\!\big(A_{i}^{(\ell)}\mathbf{h}_{i}^{(\ell-1)}(\bm{\theta}_{<i},\bm{x})+a_{i}^{(\ell)}\big),\qquad\ell=1,\dots,L.

\displaystyle\mathbf{h}_{i}^{(0)}(\bm{\theta}_{<i},\bm{x})

\displaystyle=[\bm{\theta}_{<i};\bm{x}],

for some depth
L
, weight matrices
\{B_{i}^{(\ell)}\}_{\ell=1}^{L}
and
\{A_{i}^{(\ell)}\}_{\ell=1}^{L}
, biases
\{d_{i}^{(\ell)}\}_{\ell=1}^{L}
and
\{a_{i}^{(\ell)}\}_{\ell=1}^{L}
, and final weights
(v_{i},c_{i})
and
(w_{i},b_{i})
; all of these parameters are part of
\phi
. Here
[\bm{\theta}_{<i};\bm{x}]
denotes vector concatenation.
Finally, we let the base density be standard Gaussian, i.e.

p_{0}(u)=(2\pi)^{-d_{\Theta}/2}\exp\!\left(-\frac{1}{2}\|u\|^{2}\right).

Hence,

\displaystyle\log q_{\phi}(\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x})

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\|u(\bm{\theta},\bm{x})\|^{2}-\sum_{i=1}^{d_{\Theta}}\log\sigma_{i}(\bm{\theta}_{<i},\bm{x})-\frac{d_{\Theta}}{2}\log 2\pi

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{d_{\Theta}}\left(\frac{\theta_{i}-\mu_{i}}{\sigma_{i}}\right)^{2}-\sum_{i=1}^{d_{\Theta}}\log\sigma_{i}-\frac{d_{\Theta}}{2}\log 2\pi.

For simplicity, in what follows, we will use the notation
\mu_{i}:=\mu_{i}(\bm{\theta}_{<i},\bm{x})
,
\sigma_{i}:=\sigma_{i}(\bm{\theta}_{<i},\bm{x})
,
\;\mathbf{g}_{i}^{(\ell)}:=\mathbf{g}_{i}^{(\ell)}(\bm{\theta}_{<i},\bm{x})
,
\mathbf{h}_{i}^{(\ell)}:=\mathbf{h}_{i}^{(\ell)}(\bm{\theta}_{<i},\bm{x})
, and
s_{i}:=s_{i}(\bm{\theta}_{<i},\bm{x})
.

Lemma B.5
.

Let
q_{\phi}(\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x})
be a masked autoregressive flow of the form described above, with tanh hidden layers, softplus scale function, and standard Gaussian base density.
Let
\ell_{\mathrm{NPE}}(q_{\phi};\bm{z}):=-\log q_{\phi}(\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x})
,
\bm{z}=(\bm{\theta},\bm{x})\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathcal{Z}}}
.
Then the map
\bm{z}\mapsto\ell_{\mathrm{NPE}}(q_{\phi};\bm{z})
is twice continuously differentiable on
\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathcal{Z}}}
, and there exists a constant
C>0
, depending on
\phi
, such that for all
\bm{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathcal{Z}}}
,

|\ell_{\mathrm{NPE}}(q_{\phi};\bm{z})|\leq C(1+\|\bm{z}\|^{2}).

Moreover, there exist constants
C_{1},C_{2}>0
, depending on
\phi
, such that for all
\bm{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathcal{Z}}}

\|\nabla_{\bm{z}}\ell_{\mathrm{NPE}}(q_{\phi};\bm{z})\|\leq C_{1}(1+\|\bm{z}\|^{2}),\qquad\|\nabla_{\bm{z}}^{2}\ell_{\mathrm{NPE}}(q_{\phi};\bm{z})\|\leq C_{2}(1+\|\bm{z}\|^{2}).

Proof.

We first show that the loss
\ell_{\mathrm{NPE}}
has at most a quadratic growth rate.
Recall that

\displaystyle\log q_{\phi}(\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x})=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{d_{\Theta}}\left(\frac{\theta_{i}-\mu_{i}}{\sigma_{i}}\right)^{2}-\sum_{i=1}^{d_{\Theta}}\log\sigma_{i}-\frac{d_{\Theta}}{2}\log 2\pi.

(10)

Since the hidden activations are tanh, all hidden-layer outputs are uniformly bounded. Hence, for each
i
,

1.

there exists
C_{\mu,i}<\infty
such that
|\mu_{i}(\bm{\theta}_{<i},\bm{x})|\leq C_{\mu,i}
for all
\bm{z}
;

2.

s_{i}(\bm{\theta}_{<i},\bm{x})
is uniformly bounded, and therefore
0<\sigma_{i,\text{min}}\leq\sigma_{i}(\bm{\theta}_{<i},\bm{x})\leq\sigma_{i,\text{max}}<\infty
for all
\bm{z}
.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\theta_{i}-\mu_{i}}{\sigma_{i}}\right)^{2}\leq\left(\frac{\theta_{i}-\mu_{i}}{\sigma_{i,\text{min}}}\right)^{2}\leq\frac{2\theta_{i}^{2}+2\mu_{i}^{2}}{\sigma_{i,\text{min}}^{2}}\leq\frac{2\theta_{i}^{2}+2C_{\mu,i}^{2}}{\sigma_{i,\text{min}}^{2}}.

Substituting this bound and using
|\log\sigma_{i}|\leq\max\{|\log\sigma_{i,\text{min}}|,|\log\sigma_{i,\text{max}}|\}:=C_{\log\sigma}
, we get that

\displaystyle\left|\log q_{\phi}(\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x})\right|\leq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{d_{\Theta}}\left(\frac{2\theta_{i}^{2}+2C_{\mu,i}^{2}}{\sigma_{i,\text{min}}^{2}}+C_{\log\sigma}\right)+\frac{d_{\Theta}}{2}\log 2\pi.

Hence, there exists constants
A_{\phi},B_{\phi}>0
such that

\displaystyle\left|\log q_{\phi}(\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x})\right|\leq A_{\phi}\|\bm{\theta}\|^{2}+B_{\phi}.

Since
\|\bm{\theta}\|\leq\|(\bm{\theta},\bm{x})\|=\|\bm{z}\|
, we obtain

\displaystyle\left|\ell_{\mathrm{NPE}}(q_{\phi};\bm{z})\right|\leq A_{\phi}\|\bm{z}\|^{2}+B_{\phi},

showing that the MAF with log-loss has a finite
2-
growth rate.

Next, we show that the map
\bm{z}\to\ell_{\mathrm{NPE}}
is twice continuously differentiable on
\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathcal{Z}}}
. Indeed, since each layer of the networks defining
\mu_{i}
and
s_{i}
consists of affine transformations followed by
tanh
, it follows that
\mu_{i},s_{i}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathcal{Z}}})
. Since
\sigma_{i}=\text{softplus}(s_{i})
and
softplus
is
C^{\infty}
with strictly positive output, we have
\sigma_{i}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathcal{Z}}})
and
\sigma_{i}(\bm{z})>0
for all
\bm{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathcal{Z}}}
. Therefore, the map
\bm{z}\to\ell_{\mathrm{NPE}}
is a composition of
C^{\infty}
functions and is thus
C^{\infty}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathcal{Z}}}
.

We now verify the condition on the growth of gradients. Note that from the proof of Proposition 3 (see Equation 38) in
5
, it follows by the same arguments after swapping
\bm{\theta}
and
\bm{x}
(since this proposition refers to the NLE case in which the MAF has an identical form after swapping
\bm{\theta}
and
\bm{x}
everywhere) we have that there exists
C_{0}<\infty
such that:

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\log q_{\phi}(\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x})\right\|\leq C_{0}(1+\|\bm{\theta}\|^{2}).

We now verify that a similar argument holds for
\|\nabla_{\bm{x}}\log q_{\phi}(\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x})\|
. In particular, for each
j=1,\dots,d_{\mathcal{X}}
:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\bm{x}_{j}}\log q_{\phi}(\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x})

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{d_{\Theta}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\bm{x}_{j}}\left(\frac{\theta_{i}-\mu_{i}}{\sigma_{i}}\right)^{2}-\sum_{i=1}^{d_{\Theta}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\bm{x}_{j}}\log\sigma_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d_{\Theta}}\frac{\theta_{i}-\mu_{i}}{\sigma_{i}^{2}}\frac{\partial\mu_{i}}{\partial\bm{x}_{j}}+\sum_{i=1}^{d_{\Theta}}\frac{(\theta_{i}-\mu_{i})^{2}}{\sigma_{i}^{3}}\frac{\partial\sigma_{i}}{\partial\bm{x}_{j}}-\sum_{i=1}^{d_{\Theta}}\frac{1}{\sigma_{i}}\frac{\partial\sigma_{i}}{\partial\bm{x}_{j}}.

Following Equation (32) of
5
we have that since outputs of
\mu_{i}
and
s_{i}
are affine functions of
\mathbf{h}_{i}^{L}
, there exists
C_{1},C_{2}<\infty
such that:

\displaystyle\left|\mu_{i}(\bm{\theta}_{<i},\bm{x})\right|\leq C_{1},\quad\left\|\frac{\partial\mu_{i}}{\partial\bm{x}_{j}}\right\|\leq C_{2}.

We now turn to the derivative of
\sigma_{i}
, recalling that

\displaystyle\sigma_{i}(\bm{\theta}_{<i},\bm{x})=\mathrm{softplus}\big(s_{i}(\bm{\theta}_{<i},\bm{x})\big),\qquad s_{i}(\bm{\theta}_{<i},\bm{x})=w_{i}^{\top}h_{i}^{(L)}(\bm{\theta}_{<i},\bm{x})+b_{i},

\displaystyle\mathrm{softplus}^{\prime}(t)=\frac{1}{1+\exp(-t)},\qquad\qquad\;\,\mathrm{softplus}^{\prime\prime}(t)=\frac{\exp(-t)}{(1+\exp(-t))^{2}},

where, for each
\ell\in\{1,\dots,L\}
,
h_{i}^{(\ell)}=\tanh\big(A_{i}^{(\ell)}h_{i}^{(\ell-1)}+a_{i}^{(\ell)}\big)
.
Hence,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\sigma_{i}}{\partial\bm{x}_{j}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1+\exp(-s_{i})}\frac{\partial s_{i}}{\partial\bm{x}_{j}}=\frac{1}{1+\exp(-s_{i})}\,w_{i}^{\top}\frac{\partial h_{i}^{(L)}}{\partial\bm{x}_{j}}.

Moreover, for each hidden layer,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial h_{i}^{(\ell)}}{\partial\bm{x}_{j}}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{sech}^{2}\!\big(A_{i}^{(\ell)}h_{i}^{(\ell-1)}+a_{i}^{(\ell)}\big)\odot\left(A_{i}^{(\ell)}\frac{\partial h_{i}^{(\ell-1)}}{\partial\bm{x}_{j}}\right).

where
\odot
denotes element-wise multiplication of vectors.
Since i)
\mathrm{sech}^{2}
is uniformly bounded, ii) the network has finitely many layers with fixed finite weight matrices, and iii)
\partial h_{i}^{(0)}/\partial\bm{x}_{j}=e_{r}
, it follows that
\partial h_{i}^{(\ell)}/\partial\bm{x}_{j}
is uniformly bounded on
\mathcal{X}
for every
\ell,i,j
.
Therefore, both
\partial s_{i}/\partial\bm{x}_{j}
and
\partial\sigma_{i}/\partial\bm{x}_{j}
are uniformly bounded on
\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathcal{X}}}
and there exists
C_{3}<\infty
such that:

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{\partial\sigma_{i}}{\partial\bm{x}_{j}}\right\|\leq C_{3}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\partial}{\partial\bm{x}_{j}}\log q_{\phi}(\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\sigma_{i,\text{min}}^{3}}\sum_{i=1}^{d_{\Theta}}(C_{2}|\theta_{i}-\mu_{i}|+|\theta_{i}-\mu_{i}|^{2}C_{3}+C_{3})

and since
|\mu_{i}|\leq C_{1}
it follows that there exist
C_{4},C_{5}<\infty
such that:
|\theta_{i}-\mu_{i}|\leq C_{4}(1+|\theta_{i}|)
and
|\theta_{i}-\mu_{i}|^{2}\leq C_{5}(1+|\theta_{i}|^{2})
. Thus, there exists
C_{6}<\infty
such that

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\partial}{\partial\bm{x}_{j}}\log q_{\phi}(\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x})\right|\leq\frac{1}{\sigma_{i,\text{min}}^{3}}\sum_{i=1}^{d_{\Theta}}(C_{2}C_{4}(1+|\theta_{i}|+C_{3}C_{5}(1+|\theta_{i}|^{2})+C_{3})\leq C_{6}(1+\|\bm{\theta}\|^{2}).

Therefore, we have:

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\log q_{\phi}(\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x})\right\|\leq C_{0}(1+\|\bm{\theta}\|^{2}),\quad\left\|\nabla_{\bm{x}}\log q_{\phi}(\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x})\right\|\leq C_{6}\sqrt{d_{\mathcal{X}}}(1+\|\bm{\theta}\|^{2})

and hence:

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{\bm{z}}\ell_{\mathrm{NPE}}(q_{\phi};\bm{z})\|=\|\nabla_{\bm{z}}(-\log q_{\phi}(\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x}))\|

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\log q_{\phi}(\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x})\right\|+\left\|\nabla_{\bm{x}}\log q_{\phi}(\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x})\right\|

\displaystyle\leq C_{0}(1+\|\bm{\theta}\|^{2})+C_{6}\sqrt{d_{\mathcal{X}}}(1+\|\bm{\theta}\|^{2})

\displaystyle:=C_{7}(1+\|\bm{\theta}\|^{2})

\displaystyle\leq C_{7}(1+\|\bm{z}\|^{2}).

Lastly, we verify the growth condition for the norm of the Hessian.

Recall that

\displaystyle-\log q_{\phi}(\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{d_{\Theta}}u_{i}^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{d_{\Theta}}\log\sigma_{i}+\frac{d_{\Theta}}{2}\log 2\pi,

where
u_{i}=\frac{\theta_{i}-\mu_{i}}{\sigma_{i}}
. Therefore, for any
j,k\in\{1,\dots,d_{\mathcal{Z}}\}
,

\displaystyle\partial_{z_{j}}(-\log q_{\phi}(\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x}))

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d_{\Theta}}\left(u_{i}\,\partial_{z_{j}}u_{i}+\frac{1}{\sigma_{i}}\partial_{z_{j}}\sigma_{i}\right),

(11)

\displaystyle\partial_{z_{k}}\partial_{z_{j}}(-\log q_{\phi}(\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x}))

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d_{\Theta}}\left(u_{i}\,\partial_{z_{k}}\partial_{z_{j}}u_{i}+(\partial_{z_{k}}u_{i})(\partial_{z_{j}}u_{i})-\frac{1}{\sigma_{i}^{2}}(\partial_{z_{k}}\sigma_{i})(\partial_{z_{j}}\sigma_{i})+\frac{1}{\sigma_{i}}\partial_{z_{k}}\partial_{z_{j}}\sigma_{i}\right).

(12)

By points 1. and 2. above we have,
|u_{i}|=\left|\frac{\theta_{i}-\mu_{i}}{\sigma_{i}}\right|\leq\frac{|\theta_{i}|+C_{\mu,i}}{\sigma_{i,\text{min}}}\leq C_{u}(1+|\theta_{i}|)
for
C_{u}:=\max_{i}(\max\{1/\sigma_{i,\text{min}},C_{\mu,i}/\sigma_{i,\text{min}}\})<\infty
. Moreover, since we have shown that both
|\partial_{z_{j}}\mu_{i}|\leq C_{\mu^{\prime}}
and
|\partial_{z_{j}}\sigma_{i}|\leq C_{\sigma^{\prime}}
where
C_{\mu^{\prime}}
and
C_{\sigma^{\prime}}
are constants independent of
\bm{z}
and

\displaystyle\partial_{z_{j}}u_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial_{z_{j}}(\theta_{i}-\mu_{i})}{\sigma_{i}}-\frac{\theta_{i}-\mu_{i}}{\sigma_{i}^{2}}\partial_{z_{j}}\sigma_{i},

it follows that
|\partial_{z_{j}}u_{i}|\leq C_{u^{\prime}}(1+|\theta_{i}|)\leq C_{u^{\prime}}(1+|\theta_{i}|)
for some
C_{u^{\prime}}<\infty
depending on
\sigma_{i,\text{min}},C_{\mu^{\prime}}
and
C_{\sigma^{\prime}}
.

We now consider the second derivative of
\sigma_{i}
:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}\sigma_{i}}{\partial z_{k}\partial z_{j}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\exp(-s_{i})}{(1+\exp(-s_{i}))^{2}}\frac{\partial s_{i}}{\partial z_{k}}\frac{\partial s_{i}}{\partial z_{j}}+\frac{1}{1+\exp(-s_{i})}\frac{\partial^{2}s_{i}}{\partial z_{k}\partial z_{j}}.

(13)

Thus it remains to bound
\partial^{2}s_{i}/(\partial z_{k}\partial z_{j})
.
Since

\displaystyle\frac{\partial s_{i}}{\partial z_{j}}=w_{i}^{\top}\frac{\partial h_{i}^{(L)}}{\partial z_{j}},\qquad\frac{\partial^{2}s_{i}}{\partial z_{k}\partial z_{j}}=w_{i}^{\top}\frac{\partial^{2}h_{i}^{(L)}}{\partial z_{k}\partial z_{j}},

it suffices to bound the second derivatives of the hidden states. Differentiating once more,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}h_{i}^{(\ell)}}{\partial z_{k}\partial z_{j}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial z_{k}}\left[\mathrm{sech}^{2}\!\big(A_{i}^{(\ell)}h_{i}^{(\ell-1)}+a_{i}^{(\ell)}\big)\odot\left(A_{i}^{(\ell)}\frac{\partial h_{i}^{(\ell-1)}}{\partial z_{j}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\left[-2\mathrm{sech}^{2}\!\big(A_{i}^{(\ell)}h_{i}^{(\ell-1)}+a_{i}^{(\ell)}\big)\tanh\!\big(A_{i}^{(\ell)}h_{i}^{(\ell-1)}+a_{i}^{(\ell)}\big)\right]\odot\left(A_{i}^{(\ell)}\frac{\partial h_{i}^{(\ell-1)}}{\partial z_{k}}\right)\odot\left(A_{i}^{(\ell)}\frac{\partial h_{i}^{(\ell-1)}}{\partial z_{j}}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+\mathrm{sech}^{2}\!\big(A_{i}^{(\ell)}h_{i}^{(\ell-1)}+a_{i}^{(\ell)}\big)\odot\left(A_{i}^{(\ell)}\frac{\partial^{2}h_{i}^{(\ell-1)}}{\partial z_{k}\partial z_{j}}\right).

Now, we have that: i)
\tanh
and
\mathrm{sech}^{2}
are uniformly bounded, ii) the first derivatives
\partial h_{i}^{(\ell-1)}/\partial z_{j}
have already been shown to be bounded, iii) the flow has finitely many layers with fixed finite weight matrices, and iv)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}h_{i}^{(0)}}{\partial z_{k}\partial z_{j}}=0.

Hence, or each layer,

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\partial^{2}h_{i}^{(\ell)}}{\partial z_{k}\partial z_{j}}\right|<\infty\qquad\forall\,\ell,i,j,k,\;\bm{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathcal{Z}}}.

Consequently,
\partial^{2}s_{i}/(\partial z_{k}\partial z_{j})
is uniformly bounded on
\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathcal{Z}}}
.
Returning to (
13
), and using that

\displaystyle 0<\frac{\exp(-s_{i})}{(1+\exp(-s_{i}))^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{4},\qquad 0<\frac{1}{1+\exp(-s_{i})}<1,

we conclude that

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\partial^{2}\sigma_{i}}{\partial z_{k}\partial z_{j}}\right|\leq C_{\sigma^{\prime\prime}}

for some finite constant
C_{\sigma^{\prime\prime}}
, uniformly over
\bm{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathcal{Z}}}
.
We now treat
\mu_{i}
. Recall that

\displaystyle\mu_{i}(\bm{\theta}_{<i},\bm{x})=v_{i}^{\top}g_{i}^{(L)}(\bm{\theta}_{<i},\bm{x})+c_{i},

where the hidden layers
g_{i}^{(\ell)}
are again defined through finitely many linear maps composed with
\tanh
. Therefore, using the same argument as above, but without the final softplus layer, we get that

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\partial^{2}\mu_{i}}{\partial z_{k}\partial z_{j}}\right|\leq C_{\mu^{\prime\prime}}

for some finite constant
C_{\mu^{\prime\prime}}
, uniformly over
\bm{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathcal{Z}}}
.
Using the bounds on the first and second derivatives of
\mu_{i}
and
\sigma_{i}
we first have that

\displaystyle\partial_{z_{j}}u_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial_{z_{j}}(\theta_{i}-\mu_{i})}{\sigma_{i}}-\frac{\theta_{i}-\mu_{i}}{\sigma_{i}^{2}}\partial_{z_{j}}\sigma_{i},

and hence
|\partial_{z_{j}}u_{i}|\leq C_{u^{\prime}}(1+\|\bm{\theta}\|)
for some positive constant
C_{u^{\prime}}<\infty
. Differentiating once more,

\displaystyle\partial_{z_{k}}\partial_{z_{j}}u_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial_{z_{k}}\partial_{z_{j}}(\theta_{i}-\mu_{i})}{\sigma_{i}}-\frac{\partial_{z_{j}}(\theta_{i}-\mu_{i})}{\sigma_{i}^{2}}\partial_{z_{k}}\sigma_{i}-\frac{\partial_{z_{k}}(\theta_{i}-\mu_{i})}{\sigma_{i}^{2}}\partial_{z_{j}}\sigma_{i}

\displaystyle\qquad-\frac{\theta_{i}-\mu_{i}}{\sigma_{i}^{2}}\partial_{z_{k}}\partial_{z_{j}}\sigma_{i}+2\frac{\theta_{i}-\mu_{i}}{\sigma_{i}^{3}}(\partial_{z_{k}}\sigma_{i})(\partial_{z_{j}}\sigma_{i}),

so that
|\partial_{z_{k}}\partial_{z_{j}}u_{i}|\leq C_{u^{\prime\prime}}(1+\|\bm{\theta}\|)
for some positive constant
C_{u^{\prime\prime}}<\infty
.
Returning to the expression in
Equation

12
,
we have that for each of the four terms:

\displaystyle\left|u_{i}\partial_{z_{k}}\partial_{z_{j}}u_{i}\right|

\displaystyle\leq C_{u}(1+\|\bm{\theta}\|)C_{u^{\prime\prime}}(1+\|\bm{\theta}\|)\leq C_{8}(1+\|\bm{\theta}\|^{2})

\displaystyle\left|(\partial_{z_{k}}u_{i})(\partial_{z_{j}}u_{i})\right|

\displaystyle\leq C_{u^{\prime}}^{2}(1+\|\bm{\theta}\|)^{2}\leq C_{9}(1+\|\bm{\theta}\|^{2})

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{\sigma_{i}^{2}}(\partial_{z_{k}}\sigma_{i})(\partial_{z_{j}}\sigma_{i})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sigma_{i,\text{min}}^{-2}C_{\sigma^{\prime}}^{2}:=C_{10}

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{\sigma_{i}}\partial_{z_{k}}\partial_{z_{j}}\sigma_{i}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sigma_{i,\text{min}}^{-1}C_{\sigma^{\prime\prime}}:=C_{11}

for some finite constant
C_{8},C_{9},C_{10},C_{11}<\infty
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle\left|\partial_{z_{k}}\partial_{z_{j}}(-\log q_{\phi}(\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x}))\right|

\displaystyle\leq d_{\Theta}\left(C_{8}(1+\|\bm{\theta}\|^{2})+C_{9}(1+\|\bm{\theta}\|^{2})+C_{10}+C_{11}\right)

\displaystyle\leq C_{12}(1+\|\bm{\theta}\|^{2})

\displaystyle\leq C_{12}(1+\|\bm{z}\|^{2})

for some positive constant
C_{12}<\infty
. To conclude note that, for
\|\cdot\|_{F}
denoting the Frobenius norm, it follows that:

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla^{2}_{\bm{z}}\ell_{\mathrm{NPE}}(q_{\phi};\bm{z})\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\nabla^{2}_{\bm{z}}\ell_{\mathrm{NPE}}(q_{\phi};\bm{z})\right\|_{F}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{d_{\mathcal{Z}}}\sum_{k=1}^{d_{\mathcal{Z}}}\left|\partial_{z_{k}}\partial_{z_{j}}(-\log q_{\phi}(\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x}))\right|^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{d_{\mathcal{Z}}^{2}C_{12}^{2}(1+\|\bm{z}\|^{2})^{2}}

\displaystyle=d_{\mathcal{Z}}C_{12}(1+\|\bm{z}\|^{2}).

∎

Coupling flows.

We next describe coupling flows
(
36
;
59
)
implemented in BayesFlow package
(
40
)
for
q_{\phi}(\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x})
.
First, the Actnorm layer, which is an invertible adaptation of batch normalisation for stable training, is applied to
\bm{\theta}
. It is followed by a permutation, multiplying by the permutation matrix
P\in\{0,1\}^{d_{\Theta}\times d_{\Theta}}
:

\tilde{\bm{\theta}}=P(\alpha\odot\bm{\theta}+\beta)
where
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\Theta}}
are trainable parameters, and
\odot
denotes element-wise multiplication.

We then split the dimension into two parts, followed by affine transformation:

\tilde{\bm{\theta}}=[\tilde{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathcal{A}};\tilde{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathcal{B}}]
where
\tilde{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathcal{A}}=\tilde{\bm{\theta}}_{1:\lfloor d_{\Theta}/2\rfloor}
is the first
\lfloor d_{\Theta}/2\rfloor
elements and

\tilde{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathcal{B}}=\tilde{\bm{\theta}}_{\lfloor d_{\Theta}/2\rfloor+1:d_{\Theta}}
is the last
d_{\Theta}-\lfloor d_{\Theta}/2\rfloor
elements of
\tilde{\bm{\theta}}
.
An affine coupling transformation
u=u(\tilde{\bm{\theta}},\bm{x})\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\Theta}}
is defined by

u(\tilde{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathcal{A}},\bm{x}):=\sigma(\tilde{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathcal{B}},\bm{x})\odot\tilde{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathcal{A}}+\mu(\tilde{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathcal{B}},\bm{x}).\qquad u(\tilde{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathcal{B}},\bm{x}):=\tilde{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathcal{B}}.

Note that the forward coupling transformation is defined differently than for MAFs.

The parameters
\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{\lfloor d_{\Theta}/2\rfloor}
and
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{\lfloor d_{\Theta}/2\rfloor}
are defined in a similar way as in
MAF
in sbi package. However, we use the tanh activation function:

\displaystyle\sigma(\tilde{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathcal{B}},\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\text{softplus}\!\big(\sinh^{-1}(\tilde{\sigma}(\tilde{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathcal{B}},\bm{x}))+\mathbf{1}\log(e-1)\big),\qquad

\displaystyle[\mu(\tilde{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathcal{B}},\bm{x});\tilde{\sigma}(\tilde{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathcal{B}},\bm{x})]

\displaystyle=W^{\top}\mathbf{h}^{(L)}(\tilde{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathcal{B}},\bm{x})+b,

\displaystyle\mathbf{h}^{(\ell)}(\tilde{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathcal{B}},\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\tanh\!\big(A^{(\ell)}\mathbf{h}^{(\ell-1)}(\tilde{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathcal{B}},\bm{x})+a^{(\ell)}\big),\qquad\ell=1,\dots,L.

\displaystyle\mathbf{h}^{(0)}(\tilde{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathcal{B}},\bm{x})

\displaystyle=[\tilde{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathcal{B}};\bm{x}],

where
\text{softplus},\text{sinh}^{-1},\text{tanh}
are applied element-wise.
The log-determinant of the Actnorm and the affine transformation is defined by

\log\left|\det\frac{\partial\tilde{\bm{\theta}}}{\partial\bm{\theta}}\right|=\sum_{i=1}^{d_{\Theta}}\log|\alpha_{i}|,\qquad\log\left|\det\frac{\partial u}{\partial\tilde{\bm{\theta}}}\right|=\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor d_{\Theta}/2\rfloor}\log\sigma_{i}(\tilde{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathcal{B}},\bm{x})

Finally, taking the base density to be standard Gaussian, we have

\displaystyle\log q_{\phi}(\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x})

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor d_{\Theta}/2\rfloor}\left(\sigma_{i}(\tilde{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathcal{B}},\bm{x})\tilde{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathcal{A},i}+\mu_{i}(\tilde{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathcal{B}},\bm{x})\right)^{2}+\|\tilde{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathcal{B}}\|_{2}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=1}^{d_{\Theta}}\log|\alpha_{i}|+\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor d_{\Theta}/2\rfloor}\log\sigma_{i}(\tilde{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathcal{B}},\bm{x})-\frac{d_{\Theta}}{2}\log 2\pi.

In practice, the sequence of the Actnorm layer, the permutation, and the mapping from
\tilde{\bm{\theta}}
to
u
forms a block, which is repeated multiple times to increase the flow’s expressiveness.

Lemma B.6

(Single BayesFlow coupling block)
.

Let
\bm{z}=(\bm{\theta},\bm{x})\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\Theta}+d_{\mathcal{X}}}
, and consider the BayesFlow coupling block defined above by

\tilde{\bm{\theta}}=P(\alpha\odot\bm{\theta}+\beta),\qquad u_{\mathcal{A}}=\sigma(\tilde{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathcal{B}},\bm{x})\odot\tilde{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathcal{A}}+\mu(\tilde{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathcal{B}},\bm{x}),\qquad u_{\mathcal{B}}=\tilde{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathcal{B}},

where
\mu
and
\sigma
are defined through the network architecture above. Then
\bm{z}\mapsto\ell_{\mathrm{NPE}}(q_{\phi};\bm{z})
belongs to
\mathcal{G}_{2}(\mathcal{Z})
and is of class
C^{2}
. Moreover,

\|\nabla_{\bm{z}}\ell_{\mathrm{NPE}}(q_{\phi};\cdot)\|\in\mathcal{G}_{2}(\mathcal{Z}),\qquad\|\nabla_{\bm{z}}^{2}\ell_{\mathrm{NPE}}(q_{\phi};\cdot)\|\in\mathcal{G}_{2}(\mathcal{Z}).

Proof.

By construction, the ActNorm parameters satisfy
\alpha_{i}\neq 0
for all
i
. Moreover, since the hidden activations are
\tanh
and the output layers are affine, the maps
\mu
and
\tilde{\sigma}
are bounded and belong to
C^{2}
, with uniformly bounded first and second derivatives. Since

\sigma=\mathrm{softplus}\!\big(\sinh^{-1}(\tilde{\sigma})+\mathbf{1}\log(e-1)\big),

the same holds for
\sigma
. As
\tilde{\sigma}
is bounded, there exist constants

0<\sigma_{\min}\leq\sigma_{i}(\tilde{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathcal{B}},\bm{x})\leq\sigma_{\max}<\infty

for all
i
and
(\tilde{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathcal{B}},\bm{x})
. Hence there exist constants

C_{\mu},C_{\mu^{\prime}},C_{\mu^{\prime\prime}},C_{\sigma^{\prime}},C_{\sigma^{\prime\prime}}<\infty

such that for all
i,j,k
,

|\mu_{i}|\leq C_{\mu},\qquad|\partial_{z_{j}}\mu_{i}|\leq C_{\mu^{\prime}},\qquad|\partial_{z_{k}}\partial_{z_{j}}\mu_{i}|\leq C_{\mu^{\prime\prime}},

|\partial_{z_{j}}\sigma_{i}|\leq C_{\sigma^{\prime}},\qquad|\partial_{z_{k}}\partial_{z_{j}}\sigma_{i}|\leq C_{\sigma^{\prime\prime}}.

Since
P
is a permutation matrix, it preserves the Euclidean norm. Hence

\|\tilde{\bm{\theta}}\|_{2}=\|P(\alpha\odot\bm{\theta}+\beta)\|_{2}=\|\alpha\odot\bm{\theta}+\beta\|_{2}\leq\|\text{diag}(\alpha)\|\,\|\bm{\theta}\|_{2}+\|\beta\|_{2}\leq C_{0}(1+\|\bm{\theta}\|_{2})

for some
C_{0}<\infty
. Also,
\tilde{\bm{\theta}}
is affine in
\bm{\theta}
, so all first derivatives
\partial_{z_{j}}\tilde{\bm{\theta}}_{i}
are bounded and all second derivatives
\partial_{z_{k}}\partial_{z_{j}}\tilde{\bm{\theta}}_{i}
vanish.

We first control the loss. Since
u_{\mathcal{B}}=\tilde{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathcal{B}}
, we have

\|u_{\mathcal{B}}\|_{2}\leq\|\tilde{\bm{\theta}}\|_{2}\leq C_{0}(1+\|\bm{\theta}\|_{2}).

For each
i\leq\lfloor d_{\Theta}/2\rfloor
,

|u_{\mathcal{A},i}|=|\sigma_{i}(\tilde{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathcal{B}},\bm{x})\tilde{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathcal{A},i}+\mu_{i}(\tilde{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathcal{B}},\bm{x})|\leq\sigma_{\max}|\tilde{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathcal{A},i}|+C_{\mu}\leq C_{1}(1+\|\bm{\theta}\|_{2})

for some
C_{1}<\infty
. Therefore,

\|u\|_{2}^{2}\leq C_{2}(1+\|\bm{\theta}\|_{2}^{2})

for some
C_{2}<\infty
. Since
\sum_{i=1}^{d_{\Theta}}\log|\alpha_{i}|
is constant and each
\log\sigma_{i}(\tilde{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathcal{B}},\bm{x})
is uniformly bounded, it follows from the expression for
\log q_{\phi}(\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x})
that

|\ell_{\mathrm{NPE}}(q_{\phi};\bm{z})|=|-\log q_{\phi}(\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x})|\leq C_{3}(1+\|\bm{\theta}\|_{2}^{2})\leq C_{3}(1+\|\bm{z}\|_{2}^{2})

for some
C_{3}<\infty
. Hence
\ell_{\mathrm{NPE}}(q_{\phi};\cdot)\in\mathcal{G}_{2}(\mathcal{Z})
.

We next control the gradient. For
u_{\mathcal{B}}=\tilde{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathcal{B}}
, the first derivatives are bounded and the second derivatives vanish. For
u_{\mathcal{A}}
, for each
i,j
,

\partial_{z_{j}}u_{\mathcal{A},i}=(\partial_{z_{j}}\sigma_{i})\tilde{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathcal{A},i}+\sigma_{i}\,\partial_{z_{j}}\tilde{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathcal{A},i}+\partial_{z_{j}}\mu_{i}.

Hence

|\partial_{z_{j}}u_{\mathcal{A},i}|\leq C_{\sigma^{\prime}}C_{0}(1+\|\bm{\theta}\|_{2})+\sigma_{\max}\sup_{i,j}|\partial_{z_{j}}\tilde{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathcal{A},i}|+C_{\mu^{\prime}}\leq C_{4}(1+\|\bm{\theta}\|_{2})

for some
C_{4}<\infty
. Therefore,

\left|\partial_{z_{j}}\frac{1}{2}\|u\|_{2}^{2}\right|=\left|\sum_{i}u_{i}\partial_{z_{j}}u_{i}\right|\leq C_{5}(1+\|\bm{\theta}\|_{2}^{2})

for some
C_{5}<\infty
. Moreover, since
\sigma_{i}\geq\sigma_{\min}
,

\left|\partial_{z_{j}}\log\sigma_{i}\right|=\left|\frac{\partial_{z_{j}}\sigma_{i}}{\sigma_{i}}\right|\leq\sigma_{\min}^{-1}C_{\sigma^{\prime}}.

Thus

\|\nabla_{\bm{z}}\ell_{\mathrm{NPE}}(q_{\phi};\bm{z})\|\leq C_{6}(1+\|\bm{\theta}\|_{2}^{2})\leq C_{6}(1+\|\bm{z}\|_{2}^{2})

for some
C_{6}<\infty
, and hence
\|\nabla_{\bm{z}}\ell_{\mathrm{NPE}}(q_{\phi};\cdot)\|\in\mathcal{G}_{2}(\mathcal{Z})
.

Finally, for the Hessian, for each
i,j,k
,

\displaystyle\partial_{z_{k}}\partial_{z_{j}}u_{\mathcal{A},i}

\displaystyle=(\partial_{z_{k}}\partial_{z_{j}}\sigma_{i})\tilde{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathcal{A},i}+(\partial_{z_{j}}\sigma_{i})(\partial_{z_{k}}\tilde{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathcal{A},i})+(\partial_{z_{k}}\sigma_{i})(\partial_{z_{j}}\tilde{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathcal{A},i})

\displaystyle\qquad+\sigma_{i}\,\partial_{z_{k}}\partial_{z_{j}}\tilde{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathcal{A},i}+\partial_{z_{k}}\partial_{z_{j}}\mu_{i}.

Since
\partial_{z_{k}}\partial_{z_{j}}\tilde{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathcal{A},i}=0
, this yields

|\partial_{z_{k}}\partial_{z_{j}}u_{\mathcal{A},i}|\leq C_{7}(1+\|\bm{\theta}\|_{2})

for some
C_{7}<\infty
. Consequently,

\left|\partial_{z_{k}}\partial_{z_{j}}\frac{1}{2}\|u\|_{2}^{2}\right|=\left|\sum_{i}\Big[(\partial_{z_{k}}u_{i})(\partial_{z_{j}}u_{i})+u_{i}\,\partial_{z_{k}}\partial_{z_{j}}u_{i}\Big]\right|\leq C_{8}(1+\|\bm{\theta}\|_{2}^{2})

for some
C_{8}<\infty
. Also,

\partial_{z_{k}}\partial_{z_{j}}\log\sigma_{i}=\frac{\partial_{z_{k}}\partial_{z_{j}}\sigma_{i}}{\sigma_{i}}-\frac{(\partial_{z_{k}}\sigma_{i})(\partial_{z_{j}}\sigma_{i})}{\sigma_{i}^{2}},

so

|\partial_{z_{k}}\partial_{z_{j}}\log\sigma_{i}|\leq\sigma_{\min}^{-1}C_{\sigma^{\prime\prime}}+\sigma_{\min}^{-2}C_{\sigma^{\prime}}^{2}.

Thus

\|\nabla_{\bm{z}}^{2}\ell_{\mathrm{NPE}}(q_{\phi};\bm{z})\|\leq C_{9}(1+\|\bm{\theta}\|_{2}^{2})\leq C_{9}(1+\|\bm{z}\|_{2}^{2})

for some
C_{9}<\infty
, and hence
\|\nabla_{\bm{z}}^{2}\ell_{\mathrm{NPE}}(q_{\phi};\cdot)\|\in\mathcal{G}_{2}(\mathcal{Z})
. Since all components in the construction are
C^{2}
, we also have
\ell_{\mathrm{NPE}}(q_{\phi};\cdot)\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{\Theta}+d_{\mathcal{X}}})
.
∎

Lemma B.7

(Multiple BayesFlow coupling blocks)
.

Fix
M\geq 1
, the number of blocks, and let

\bm{z}^{(0)}=\bm{z}=(\bm{\theta},\bm{x}).

For
\ell=1,\dots,M
, write
\bm{z}^{(\ell)}=(\bm{\theta}^{(\ell)},\bm{x})
, where
\bm{\theta}^{(\ell)}
is obtained from
\bm{\theta}^{(\ell-1)}
as follows:

\tilde{\bm{\theta}}^{(\ell)}=P^{(\ell)}\bigl(\alpha^{(\ell)}\odot\bm{\theta}^{(\ell-1)}+\beta^{(\ell)}\bigr),

split

\tilde{\bm{\theta}}^{(\ell)}=\bigl[\tilde{\bm{\theta}}^{(\ell)}_{\mathcal{A}};\tilde{\bm{\theta}}^{(\ell)}_{\mathcal{B}}\bigr],

and define

\bm{\theta}^{(\ell)}_{\mathcal{A}}=\sigma^{(\ell)}\bigl(\tilde{\bm{\theta}}^{(\ell)}_{\mathcal{B}},\bm{x}\bigr)\,\odot\tilde{\bm{\theta}}^{(\ell)}_{\mathcal{A}}+\mu^{(\ell)}\bigl(\tilde{\bm{\theta}}^{(\ell)}_{\mathcal{B}},\bm{x}\bigr),\qquad\bm{\theta}^{(\ell)}_{\mathcal{B}}=\tilde{\bm{\theta}}^{(\ell)}_{\mathcal{B}},

where each
\mu^{(\ell)}
and
\sigma^{(\ell)}
is defined through the BayesFlow architecture above.

Then
\bm{z}\mapsto\ell_{\mathrm{NPE}}(q_{\phi};\bm{z})
belongs to
\mathcal{G}_{2}(\mathcal{Z})
and is of class
C^{2}
. Moreover, with

\beta_{M}:=M+1,\qquad\alpha_{M}:=2M,

we have

\|\nabla_{\bm{z}}\ell_{\mathrm{NPE}}(q_{\phi};\cdot)\|\in\mathcal{G}_{\beta_{M}}(\mathcal{Z}),\qquad\|\nabla_{\bm{z}}^{2}\ell_{\mathrm{NPE}}(q_{\phi};\cdot)\|\in\mathcal{G}_{\alpha_{M}}(\mathcal{Z}).

Proof.

For each
\ell=1,\dots,M
, let
F^{(\ell)}:\mathcal{Z}\to\mathcal{Z}
denote the
\ell
-th block map, defined by

\bm{z}^{(\ell)}=F^{(\ell)}(\bm{z}^{(\ell-1)}),\qquad\bm{z}^{(0)}=\bm{z}=(\bm{\theta},\bm{x}).

By Lemma
B.6
, each
F^{(\ell)}
belongs to
C^{2}
, and there exist constants
a_{\ell},b_{\ell},c_{\ell}<\infty
such that for all
\bm{z}\in\mathcal{Z}
,

\displaystyle\|F^{(\ell)}(\bm{z})\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq a_{\ell}(1+\|\bm{z}\|_{2}),

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{\bm{z}}F^{(\ell)}(\bm{z})\|

\displaystyle\leq b_{\ell}(1+\|\bm{z}\|_{2}),

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{\bm{z}}^{2}F^{(\ell)}(\bm{z})\|

\displaystyle\leq c_{\ell}(1+\|\bm{z}\|_{2}).

Since each
P^{(\ell)}
is a permutation matrix, it preserves Euclidean norms, so it affects only constants and not growth orders.

We first bound the iterates. Since

\|\bm{z}^{(\ell)}\|_{2}=\|F^{(\ell)}(\bm{z}^{(\ell-1)})\|_{2}\leq a_{\ell}(1+\|\bm{z}^{(\ell-1)}\|_{2}),

an induction on
\ell
yields

\|\bm{z}^{(M)}\|_{2}\leq C_{M}(1+\|\bm{z}\|_{2})

for some
C_{M}<\infty
. In particular,

\|\bm{\theta}^{(M)}\|_{2}\leq C_{M}(1+\|\bm{z}\|_{2}).

Now the full log-density is given by

\log q_{\phi}(\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x})=-\frac{1}{2}\|\bm{\theta}^{(M)}\|_{2}^{2}+\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}\log\left|\det\frac{\partial\bm{\theta}^{(\ell)}}{\partial\tilde{\bm{\theta}}^{(\ell)}}\right|+\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}\log\left|\det\frac{\partial\tilde{\bm{\theta}}^{(\ell)}}{\partial\bm{\theta}^{(\ell-1)}}\right|-\frac{d_{\Theta}}{2}\log(2\pi).

The second sum is constant in
\bm{z}
, since each term equals
\sum_{i}\log|\alpha_{i}^{(\ell)}|
. The first sum equals

\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor d_{\Theta}/2\rfloor}\log\sigma_{i}^{(\ell)}\bigl(\tilde{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathcal{B}}^{(\ell)},\bm{x}\bigr),

which is uniformly bounded because each
\sigma_{i}^{(\ell)}
is bounded above and below away from zero by the single-block analysis. Hence

|\ell_{\mathrm{NPE}}(q_{\phi};\bm{z})|\leq C^{\prime}_{M}(1+\|\bm{\theta}^{(M)}\|_{2}^{2})\leq C^{\prime}_{M}(1+\|\bm{z}\|_{2}^{2})

for some
C^{\prime}_{M}<\infty
. Therefore
\ell_{\mathrm{NPE}}(q_{\phi};\cdot)\in\mathcal{G}_{2}(\mathcal{Z})
.

We next control the gradient. By the chain rule,

\nabla_{\bm{z}}\bm{z}^{(M)}(\bm{z})=\nabla_{\bm{z}}F^{(M)}\!\bigl(\bm{z}^{(M-1)}(\bm{z})\bigr)\cdots\nabla_{\bm{z}}F^{(1)}\!\bigl(\bm{z}^{(0)}(\bm{z})\bigr).

Hence

\|\nabla_{\bm{z}}\bm{z}^{(M)}(\bm{z})\|\leq\prod_{\ell=1}^{M}b_{\ell}\bigl(1+\|\bm{z}^{(\ell-1)}(\bm{z})\|_{2}\bigr)\leq C^{\prime\prime}_{M}(1+\|\bm{z}\|_{2})^{M}

for some
C^{\prime\prime}_{M}<\infty
. Since
\bm{\theta}^{(M)}
is the first component of
\bm{z}^{(M)}
, this implies

\|\nabla_{\bm{z}}\bm{\theta}^{(M)}(\bm{z})\|\leq C^{\prime\prime}_{M}(1+\|\bm{z}\|_{2})^{M}.

Therefore

\nabla_{\bm{z}}\Big(\tfrac{1}{2}\|\bm{\theta}^{(M)}(\bm{z})\|_{2}^{2}\Big)=\nabla_{\bm{z}}\bm{\theta}^{(M)}(\bm{z})^{\top}\bm{\theta}^{(M)}(\bm{z}),

and so

\left\|\nabla_{\bm{z}}\Big(\tfrac{1}{2}\|\bm{\theta}^{(M)}(\bm{z})\|_{2}^{2}\Big)\right\|\leq\|\nabla_{\bm{z}}\bm{\theta}^{(M)}(\bm{z})\|\,\|\bm{\theta}^{(M)}(\bm{z})\|_{2}\leq C^{\prime\prime\prime}_{M}(1+\|\bm{z}\|_{2})^{M+1}

for some
C^{\prime\prime\prime}_{M}<\infty
. The derivatives of the accumulated log-determinant terms are controlled similarly, since each block contributes derivatives of
\log\sigma_{i}^{(\ell)}
composed with the pre-coupling variables
(\tilde{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathcal{B}}^{(\ell)},\bm{x})
, and the derivatives of
\log\sigma_{i}^{(\ell)}
are bounded by the single-block analysis. Hence

\|\nabla_{\bm{z}}\ell_{\mathrm{NPE}}(q_{\phi};\bm{z})\|\leq C^{\prime\prime\prime}_{M}(1+\|\bm{z}\|_{2})^{M+1},

so
\|\nabla_{\bm{z}}\ell_{\mathrm{NPE}}(q_{\phi};\cdot)\|\in\mathcal{G}_{M+1}(\mathcal{Z})
.

Finally, we control the Hessian. Differentiating

\nabla_{\bm{z}}\Big(\tfrac{1}{2}\|\bm{\theta}^{(M)}(\bm{z})\|_{2}^{2}\Big)=\nabla_{\bm{z}}\bm{\theta}^{(M)}(\bm{z})^{\top}\bm{\theta}^{(M)}(\bm{z})

gives

\nabla_{\bm{z}}^{2}\Big(\tfrac{1}{2}\|\bm{\theta}^{(M)}(\bm{z})\|_{2}^{2}\Big)=\bigl(\nabla_{\bm{z}}\bm{\theta}^{(M)}(\bm{z})\bigr)^{\top}\nabla_{\bm{z}}\bm{\theta}^{(M)}(\bm{z})+\sum_{i}\theta_{i}^{(M)}(\bm{z})\,\nabla_{\bm{z}}^{2}\theta_{i}^{(M)}(\bm{z}).

It therefore remains to bound
\nabla_{\bm{z}}^{2}\bm{z}^{(M)}(\bm{z})
. Since
\bm{z}^{(0)}(\bm{z})=\bm{z}
, we have

\nabla_{\bm{z}}\bm{z}^{(0)}(\bm{z})=I,\qquad\nabla_{\bm{z}}^{2}\bm{z}^{(0)}(\bm{z})=0.

The second-order chain rule gives the recursion

\nabla_{\bm{z}}^{2}\bm{z}^{(\ell)}(\bm{z})=\nabla_{\bm{z}}^{2}F^{(\ell)}\!\bigl(\bm{z}^{(\ell-1)}(\bm{z})\bigr)\bigl[\nabla_{\bm{z}}\bm{z}^{(\ell-1)}(\bm{z}),\nabla_{\bm{z}}\bm{z}^{(\ell-1)}(\bm{z})\bigr]+\nabla_{\bm{z}}F^{(\ell)}\!\bigl(\bm{z}^{(\ell-1)}(\bm{z})\bigr)\,\nabla_{\bm{z}}^{2}\bm{z}^{(\ell-1)}(\bm{z}),

Hence

\|\nabla_{\bm{z}}^{2}\bm{z}^{(\ell)}(\bm{z})\|\leq c_{\ell}(1+\|\bm{z}^{(\ell-1)}(\bm{z})\|_{2})\|\nabla_{\bm{z}}\bm{z}^{(\ell-1)}(\bm{z})\|^{2}+b_{\ell}(1+\|\bm{z}^{(\ell-1)}(\bm{z})\|_{2})\|\nabla_{\bm{z}}^{2}\bm{z}^{(\ell-1)}(\bm{z})\|.

Using the bounds

\|\bm{z}^{(\ell-1)}(\bm{z})\|_{2}\leq C_{M}(1+\|\bm{z}\|_{2}),\qquad\|\nabla_{\bm{z}}\bm{z}^{(\ell-1)}(\bm{z})\|\leq C^{\prime\prime}_{M}(1+\|\bm{z}\|_{2})^{\ell-1},

an induction on
\ell
yields

\|\nabla_{\bm{z}}^{2}\bm{z}^{(M)}(\bm{z})\|\leq C^{\prime\prime\prime\prime}_{M}(1+\|\bm{z}\|_{2})^{2M-1}

for some
C^{\prime\prime\prime\prime}_{M}<\infty
. In particular,

\|\nabla_{\bm{z}}^{2}\bm{\theta}^{(M)}(\bm{z})\|\leq C^{\prime\prime\prime\prime}_{M}(1+\|\bm{z}\|_{2})^{2M-1}.

Therefore

\left\|\nabla_{\bm{z}}^{2}\Big(\tfrac{1}{2}\|\bm{\theta}^{(M)}(\bm{z})\|_{2}^{2}\Big)\right\|\leq\|\nabla_{\bm{z}}\bm{\theta}^{(M)}(\bm{z})\|^{2}+\|\bm{\theta}^{(M)}(\bm{z})\|_{2}\,\|\nabla_{\bm{z}}^{2}\bm{\theta}^{(M)}(\bm{z})\|\leq C^{\prime\prime\prime\prime\prime}_{M}(1+\|\bm{z}\|_{2})^{2M}

for some
C^{\prime\prime\prime\prime\prime}_{M}<\infty
. The second derivatives of the accumulated log-determinant terms are controlled in the same way and are of no larger polynomial order. Hence

\|\nabla_{\bm{z}}^{2}\ell_{\mathrm{NPE}}(q_{\phi};\bm{z})\|\leq C^{\prime\prime\prime\prime\prime}_{M}(1+\|\bm{z}\|_{2})^{2M},

so
\|\nabla_{\bm{z}}^{2}\ell_{\mathrm{NPE}}(q_{\phi};\cdot)\|\in\mathcal{G}_{2M}(\mathcal{Z})
. Since each
F^{(\ell)}
is
C^{2}
, the composition is also
C^{2}
, and therefore
\ell_{\mathrm{NPE}}(q_{\phi};\cdot)\in C^{2}(\mathcal{Z})
.
∎

Lemma

4.3
then follows directly from Lemmas
B.5
and

C.1
Hyperparameter selection – selection method for DRO-NPE

Estimating KL-based miscalibration.

Recall that
P_{\bm{x}}
is the marginal distribution of the joint simulator
P\equiv P_{\bm{\theta},\bm{x}}
, and
F^{Q}_{S\mid\bm{x}}
is the CDF of
Q_{S\mid\bm{x}}
, which is the distribution of
S(\bm{\theta};\bm{x})
whenever
\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x}\sim Q_{\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x}}
. For each
\bm{x}
, we define
\tilde{U}\mid\bm{x}\sim(F^{Q}_{S\mid\bm{x}})_{\#}P_{S\mid\bm{x}}
. Let
P_{\tilde{U},\bm{x}}
denote the resulting joint distribution of
(\tilde{U},\bm{x})
. We also define an independent reference pair
(U,\bm{x})
, where
U\sim\mathcal{U}[0,1]
is independent of
\bm{x}\sim P_{\bm{x}}
, so that
P_{U,\bm{x}}=P_{U}\otimes P_{\bm{x}}
. We write the respective densities as
p_{U,\bm{x}}(u,\bm{x})=p_{U}(u)p_{\bm{x}}(\bm{x})=p_{\bm{x}}(\bm{x})
since
p_{U}(u)=1
and
p_{\tilde{U},\bm{x}}(\tilde{u},\bm{x})=p_{\tilde{U}\mid\bm{x}}(\tilde{u}\mid\bm{x})p_{\bm{x}}(\bm{x})
.
For any
Q_{\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x}}
with density
q_{\phi}
, the KL-based miscalibration is

\mathrm{kl}_{\mathrm{cal}}^{S}(q_{\phi};P)=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}\sim P_{\bm{x}}}\left[-h\left((F^{Q}_{S\mid\bm{x}})_{\#}P_{S\mid\bm{x}}\right)\right]=\int p_{\bm{x}}(\bm{x})\int p_{\tilde{U}}(\tilde{u}\mid\bm{x})\log p_{\tilde{U}}(\tilde{u}\mid\bm{x})d\tilde{u}\;d\bm{x}

where
h(\mu)=-\int p(u)\log p(u)\,du
denotes the differential entropy. Since
p_{U}(\tilde{u})=1
on
[0,1]
, we can equivalently write:

\displaystyle\int p_{\bm{x}}(\bm{x})\int p_{\tilde{U}}(\tilde{u}\mid\bm{x})\log p_{\tilde{U}}(\tilde{u}\mid\bm{x})d\tilde{u}\;d\bm{x}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\int\int p_{\tilde{U}}(\tilde{u}\mid\bm{x})p_{\bm{x}}(\bm{x})\log\frac{p_{\tilde{U}}(\tilde{u}\mid\bm{x})p_{\bm{x}}(\bm{x})}{p_{U}(\tilde{u})p_{\bm{x}}(\bm{x})}d\tilde{u}\;d\bm{x}}_{=\mathrm{KL}(P_{\tilde{U},\bm{x}}\|P_{U,\bm{x}})},

Therefore,
\mathrm{kl}_{\mathrm{cal}}^{S}(q_{\phi};P)
can be estimated by density-ratio estimation between samples from the dependent joint
P_{\tilde{U},\bm{x}}
and the independent reference
P_{U}\otimes P_{\bm{x}}
.
To this end, we formulate a binary classification problem: samples from class
y=1
are drawn from
(\tilde{u},\bm{x})\sim P_{\tilde{U},\bm{x}}
, while samples from class
y=0
are drawn from
(u,\bm{x})\sim P_{U,\bm{x}}=P_{U}\otimes P_{\bm{x}}
.
With equal class priors, the Bayes-optimal classifier
D^{\star}:[0,1]\times\mathcal{X}\to[0,1]
is given by

\displaystyle D^{\star}(u,\bm{x})=\frac{p_{\tilde{U},\bm{x}}(u,\bm{x})}{p_{\tilde{U},\bm{x}}(u,\bm{x})+p_{U,\bm{x}}(u,\bm{x})}.

Rearranging gives

\frac{p_{\tilde{U}|\bm{x}}(u\mid\bm{x})p_{\bm{x}}(\bm{x})}{p_{U}(u)\,p_{\bm{x}}(\bm{x})}=\frac{p_{\tilde{U},\bm{x}}(u,\bm{x})}{p_{U,\bm{x}}(u,\bm{x})}=\frac{D^{\star}(u,\bm{x})}{1-D^{\star}(u,\bm{x})}.

Thus, the desired ratio can be recovered directly from the classifier via its odds. In practice, we train a discriminator
D_{\psi}
by minimising the binary cross-entropy loss, yielding the approximation

\frac{p_{\tilde{U},\bm{x}}(u,\bm{x})}{p_{U,\bm{x}}(u,\bm{x})}\approx\frac{D_{\hat{\psi}}(u,\bm{x})}{1-D_{\hat{\psi}}(u,\bm{x})}.

Further, we do not use
\bm{x}
directly in the discriminator. Instead, we map it to the scalar summary

\gamma_{\phi}(\bm{x}):=\sum_{i=1}^{d_{\Theta}}\operatorname{Var}_{\bm{\theta}\sim Q_{\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x}}}(\theta_{i}\mid\bm{x}),

where
Q_{\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x}}
denotes the distribution with density
q_{\phi}(\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x})
.
We then model the discriminator as a function of
(u,\gamma_{\phi}(\bm{x}))
via logistic regression:

D_{\psi}(u,\bm{x})=\frac{1}{1+\exp\!\bigl(-(\psi_{0}+\psi_{1}u+\psi_{2}\gamma_{\phi}(\bm{x}))\bigr)}.

Selecting
\varepsilon
.

Given an estimate of
\mathrm{kl}_{\mathrm{cal}}^{S}
, we choose
\varepsilon
using a validation set.
We split
n
samples from
P
into training and validation sets, and minimise the validation estimate of
\mathrm{kl}_{\mathrm{cal}}^{S}
over
\varepsilon\in[\varepsilon_{\min},\varepsilon_{\max}]
. For each queried value of
\varepsilon
, we train
q_{\phi}
on the training set and evaluate
\mathrm{kl}_{\mathrm{cal}}^{S}
on the validation set.
The selected value
\varepsilon^{\star}
is then used to retrain
q_{\phi}
on the full dataset.
In our experiments, we perform Bayesian optimisation
(
26
)
over
\log\varepsilon
on the interval
[\log 0.001,\log 10]
, using a budget of 10 iterations.
Optimising over
\log\varepsilon
explores each order of magnitude equally, which is appropriate because
\varepsilon
controls the strength of regularisation.

C.2
Description of baseline methods

Balanced NPE
(
16
)
.

The method extends the Balanced NRE method proposed by
15
to NPE. NRE relies on the ratio
r(\bm{x}\mid\bm{\theta}):=\frac{p(\bm{\theta},\bm{x})}{p(\bm{\theta})p(\bm{x})}
and the Bayes-optimal classifier
d^{\star}:\Theta\times\mathcal{X}\to[0,1]
, which distinguishes samples from the joint distribution
P_{\bm{\theta},\bm{x}}
and the product of marginals
\Pi\otimes P_{\bm{x}}
. The optimal classifier is defined as
d^{\star}(\bm{\theta},\bm{x}):=\frac{p(\bm{\theta},\bm{x})}{p(\bm{\theta},\bm{x})+p(\bm{\theta})p(\bm{x})}
, and relates to
r(\bm{x}\mid\bm{\theta})
via
r(\bm{x}\mid\bm{\theta})=\frac{d^{\star}(\bm{\theta},\bm{x})}{1-d^{\star}(\bm{\theta},\bm{x})}
.

In practice, the optimal classifier is not available and is estimated from finite samples, denoted
\hat{d}
.
16
suggest that overconfident posteriors obtained from NRE originate from overconfidence in
\hat{d}
. To mitigate this, they introduce a regularisation term that encourages
\hat{d}
to be more conservative than
d^{\star}
. Specifically, they define a balanced classifier
d_{b}
satisfying
\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\theta},\bm{x}\sim P_{\bm{\theta},\bm{x}}}[d_{b}(\bm{\theta},\bm{x})]+\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\theta},\bm{x}\sim\Pi\otimes P_{\bm{x}}}[d_{b}(\bm{\theta},\bm{x})]=1
. They show that any balanced classifier satisfies
\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\theta},\bm{x}\sim P_{\bm{\theta},\bm{x}}}\left[\frac{d^{\star}(\bm{\theta},\bm{x})}{d_{b}(\bm{\theta},\bm{x})}\right]\geq 1
and
\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\theta},\bm{x}\sim\Pi\otimes P_{\bm{x}}}\left[\frac{1-d^{\star}(\bm{\theta},\bm{x})}{1-d_{b}(\bm{\theta},\bm{x})}\right]\geq 1
, implying that
d_{b}
is less confident than
d^{\star}
in expectation.

To impose this property in the NPE setting,
16
add the balancing regularisation term
\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\theta},\bm{x}\sim P_{\bm{\theta},\bm{x}}}[\hat{d}(\bm{\theta},\bm{x})]+\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\theta},\bm{x}\sim\Pi\otimes P_{\bm{x}}}[\hat{d}(\bm{\theta},\bm{x})]-1
on top of the standard NPE loss. This term is estimated using the approximate posterior density by noting that
r(\bm{x}\mid\bm{\theta})=\frac{p(\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x})}{p(\bm{\theta})}\approx\frac{q(\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x})}{p(\bm{\theta})}
and
\hat{d}(\bm{\theta},x)\approx r(\bm{x}\mid\bm{\theta})/(1+r(\bm{x}\mid\bm{\theta}))
. The resulting regularised objective is

\textstyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Bal}}(q;P_{\bm{\theta},\bm{x}},\Pi\otimes P_{\bm{x}}):=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{NPE}}(q;P_{\bm{\theta},\bm{x}})+\lambda\left[\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\theta},\bm{x}\sim P_{\bm{\theta},\bm{x}}}\left[\frac{\frac{q(\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x})}{p(\bm{\theta})}}{1+\frac{q(\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x})}{p(\bm{\theta})}}\right]+\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\theta},\bm{x}\sim\Pi\otimes P_{\bm{x}}}\left[\frac{\frac{q(\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x})}{p(\bm{\theta})}}{1+\frac{q(\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x})}{p(\bm{\theta})}}\right]-1\right],

where
\lambda>0
is a regularisation parameter. In their implementation, samples from
\Pi\otimes P_{\bm{x}}
are approximated using joint samples
\{(\bm{\theta}_{i},\bm{x}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}\sim P_{\bm{\theta},\bm{x}}
. While these are used for estimating terms involving the joint, samples from the marginals are constructed by cyclically shifting
\bm{\theta}
, yielding mismatched pairs
\{(\bm{\theta}_{n},\bm{x}_{1}),(\bm{\theta}_{1},\bm{x}_{2}),\dots,(\bm{\theta}_{n-1},\bm{x}_{n})\}
.

Coverage-regularised NPE
(
20
)
.

This method penalises deviations of the SBC ranks—computed as in
Definition

2.2
—from the standard uniform distribution, using
S(\bm{\theta};\bm{x})=q(\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x})
. Given
(\bm{\theta},\bm{x})
from the joint distribution, define

u=u(q;\bm{\theta},\bm{x})=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\mathbf{1}\bigl\{q(\tilde{\bm{\theta}}^{(m)}\mid\bm{x})<q(\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x})\bigr\}
,
where
\tilde{\bm{\theta}}^{(1)},\dots,\tilde{\bm{\theta}}^{(M)}\sim Q_{\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x}}
. Let
u_{(1)},\dots,u_{(n)}
denote the sorted sequence of
\{u_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
in ascending order with
u_{i}=u(q;\bm{\theta}_{i},\bm{x}_{i})
. The empirical CDF evaluated at these points is given by
\hat{F}(u_{(i)})=\frac{i}{n},\quad i=1,\dots,n.
This empirical CDF is compared to the CDF of the standard uniform distribution, which satisfies
F(u_{(i)})=u_{(i)}
. The resulting calibration error over
n
samples is defined as
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(u_{(i)}-\frac{i}{n}\right)^{2}
.
To encourage conservative approximate posteriors, they propose penalising only overconfident deviations. This is achieved by introducing a one-sided penalty that considers only cases where the uniform CDF dominates the empirical CDF, namely

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\max\left\{u_{(i)}-\frac{i}{n},0\right\}\right)^{2}
,
which is the formulation adopted in their main experiments.

To avoid backpropagation through posterior samples for broad applicability,
u
is estimated via self-normalised importance sampling with the prior as a proposal distribution such that

u=\frac{\sum_{m=1}^{M}\frac{q(\tilde{\bm{\theta}}^{(m)}\mid\bm{x})}{p(\tilde{\bm{\theta}}^{(m)})}\mathbf{1}\bigl\{q(\tilde{\bm{\theta}}^{(m)}\mid\bm{x})<q(\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{x})\bigr\}}{\sum_{m=1}^{M}\frac{q(\tilde{\bm{\theta}}^{(m)}\mid\bm{x})}{p(\tilde{\bm{\theta}}^{(m)})}},

where
\tilde{\bm{\theta}}^{(1)},\dots,\tilde{\bm{\theta}}^{(M)}\sim\Pi
. Moreover, differentiable relaxations of both the indicator function and the sorting operation are employed.
The resulting regularised objective is

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Cal}}(q;P_{n}):=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{NPE}}(q;P_{n})+\lambda\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\max\left\{u_{(i)}\left(q;\bm{\theta}_{(i)},\bm{x}_{(i)}\right)-\frac{i}{n},0\right\}\right)^{2}.

C.3
Implementation details

Conditional density estimator.

Across all experiments, we employ a conditional normalising flow model based on coupling layers, as implemented in the
Bayesflow
package
(
40
)
, for both DRO-NPE and the baseline methods. We use six coupling layers and adopt affine transformations as the coupling functions, following the default configuration. For the multi-layer perceptron used to predict the parameters of the affine transformations, we retain the default architecture except for the choice of activation function: we replace the standard activation with the tanh, which ensures that the theoretical assumptions underlying our method are satisfied.

Training procedure.

The model is trained for 1000 epochs with a batch size of 64. We train the network using the AdamW optimiser
(
45
)
with a learning rate of
5\times 10^{-4}
. Unless otherwise stated, we do not use a validation set for early stopping or model selection, in order to isolate the effect of the training objective. For CR-NPE, we observed numerical instabilities during prolonged training. Therefore, for this method, we train the network until instability occurs (i.e., loss value becoming
nan
) or for 1000 epochs (whichever occurs first). In case of instability, we set the final model to be the one in the last stable training epoch.
Note that while this instability is also present in the original implementation, it is more pronounced in our setting, potentially due to the use of a different conditional density estimator from the original paper. Nevertheless, we retain this choice to ensure a fair and meaningful comparison across methods. For
\varepsilon
-selection in DRO-NPE, we use 10% of the data as validation set.

Hyperparameters for other methods.
Following the recommendations in the respective works, we set
\lambda=5
and
M=16
in CR-NPE and
\lambda=100
in Bal-NPE.

The Lotka–Volterra model describes the nonlinear dynamics of interacting prey and predator populations. The objective is to infer the parameter vector

C.5
Computational cost comparison

Here, we briefly compare the computational overhead of DRO-NPE with that of the baselines, focusing primarily on the additional memory requirements during training. Following
20
, we focus on memory because it can be characterised more directly than wall-clock time, which depends strongly on the automatic-differentiation backend and hardware. We do not report wall-clock times for all baselines because CR-NPE uses PyTorch, whereas the other methods use JAX through BayesFlow/Keras due to package-specific support for differentiable sorting; this would make timing comparisons unfair.

DRO-NPE incurs additional overhead from computing input gradients with respect to
z
and differentiating through them, requiring higher-order automatic differentiation and memory that scales with the mini-batch size
B
and
d_{\mathcal{Z}}
. CR-NPE incurs overhead from importance sampling, which scales with
B
,
d_{\Theta}
, and the number of samples
M
. Bal-NPE only requires additional density evaluations on cyclically shifted mini-batch pairs, so its extra memory usage scales mainly with
B
. Thus, although DRO-NPE introduces higher-order automatic differentiation, its memory overhead should remain moderate in typical SBI settings, especially when low-dimensional summary embeddings are used.

Comparison with NPE using early stopping.

Another common way to reduce generalisation error and mitigate overfitting is early stopping. Although its effect on miscoverage is less well understood theoretically, except in relatively simple settings such as linear models
(
66
)
, it may still help in practice by reducing the generalisation gap. To assess this empirically, we compare DRO-NPE with standard NPE and NPE trained with early stopping. For early stopping, we use the default configuration of the
sbi
package
(
69
)
:
10\%
of the training data is held out for validation, training stops if the validation risk does not improve for
20
consecutive epochs, and the model with the lowest validation risk is returned.

Figure

12
reports the resulting coverage curves and NLPD. Early stopping substantially improves both coverage and NLPD relative to standard NPE, confirming that reducing overfitting can already be beneficial. However, in the low-data regime it still yields overconfident posteriors, whereas DRO-NPE remains conservative. As the simulation budget increases, the gap narrows, but DRO-NPE continues to offer comparable performance.

A more conservative variant of DRO-NPE.

In our main experiments, we select
\varepsilon
by minimising
\mathrm{kl}_{\mathrm{cal}}^{q}
.
Although this criterion yields good overall coverage, it does not explicitly encourage coverage curves to lie above the diagonal; rather, it penalises deviations from the diagonal in either direction. We therefore consider a more conservative
variant of DRO-NPE, obtained by slightly modifying the selection criterion. Specifically, instead of minimising
\mathrm{kl}_{\mathrm{cal}}^{q}
, we minimise

\mathrm{kl}_{\mathrm{cal}}^{q}-\gamma\tilde{\varepsilon}
, where

\tilde{\varepsilon}=\frac{\log\varepsilon-\log\varepsilon_{\mathrm{min}}}{\log\varepsilon_{\mathrm{max}}-\log\varepsilon_{\mathrm{min}}}

is the log-normalised value of
\varepsilon
. The additional term favours larger values of
\varepsilon
, and hence more conservative posteriors. Here,
\gamma\geq 0
is a user-specified hyperparameter controlling the trade-off between calibration and additional conservativeness.

We illustrate this variant in
Figure

13
, reporting the resulting coverage and NLPD for
\gamma=0.8
. The modified criterion yields a more conservative posterior approximation. Interestingly, it also achieves a slightly lower NLPD, suggesting that in this case the value of
\varepsilon
selected using the validation set with
\mathrm{kl}_{\mathrm{cal}}^{q}
alone may have been slightly smaller than optimal.
