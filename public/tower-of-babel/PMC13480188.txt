MelC tyrosinase-dependent enzymatic synthesis of functional melanins from phenolic monomers
Authors: Nimya Krishnan, Koijam Monica Devi, Chan-Seo Yeo, Kwon-Young Choi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Sustainable microbial bioproduction offers a promising route to synthesize high-value melanin biopolymers, yet its scalability is fundamentally limited by the high cost of the L-tyrosine precursor. Here, we aimed to overcome this economic barrier by a highly efficient, engineered microbial platform that utilizes recombinant Escherichia coli expressing MelC tyrosinase to convert diverse, cost-effective noncanonical phenolic monomers into customizable melanin-like biopolymers. Among 13 screened substrates, p-Cresol (PC) and 4-hydroxyphenylacetic acid were the most efficient precursors, yielding 2.40 g/L and 3.92 g/L, respectively. Structural analyses (Ultraviolet-visible spectroscopy, Fourier-transform infrared, Solid-state NMR, MALDI-TOF MS, scanning electron microscopy, Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy, X-ray diffraction, EPR) revealed that these biopolymers possess a native eumelanin-like amorphous architecture while exhibiting partial Dimethyl sulfoxide solubility for improved processability. The synthesized eumelanin-like pigments exhibited high thermal stability (retaining > 37% mass at 800 °C) and strong antioxidant activity, with PC-melanin outperforming conventional eumelanin by twenty-fold. Most importantly, these novel substrates are effluents of industrial and agriculture waste. Thus, this strategy frames a new method for bioremediation. These customizable eumelanin-like pigments hold strong potential for demanding industrial applications, including high-temperature coatings and advanced electronics.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13036-026-00710-4.

Introduction

Melanins are multifunctional dark brown-black pigments widely distributed in nature and produced by diverse organisms [1]. Based on their chemical precursors and metabolic pathways, melanins are broadly classified into eumelanin, pyomelanin, allomelanin, pheomelanin, and neuromelanin [2]. These biopolymers result from oxidative polymerization of indolic or phenolic compounds [3]. Melanin structure and biological functions vary across species. Melanin functions as a primary biological safeguard, providing essential functions such as photoprotection against ultraviolet (UV) radiation, antioxidant activity, and heavy metal sequestration in nature [3–5].

Despite the high demand in the cosmetic, pharmaceutical, and electronics industries, melanin commercialization is limited by significant bottlenecks [6–8]. Traditional extraction from natural sources, including cuttlefish ink (Sepia officinalis), is costly, unsustainable, and raises ethical concerns. Chemical synthesis often requires harsh conditions, expensive catalysts, and toxic byproduct generation [3, 9]. While chemical synthesis utilizing inexpensive racemic mixtures like dl-dopa is viable, it often requires harsh conditions. Conversely, sustainable microbial bioproduction typically depends on the supplementation of L-tyrosine, a costly substrate representing 30–40% of industrial production costs. Therefore, exploring noncanonical precursors is vital not only to bypass this economic bottleneck but to unlock novel, customizable material properties [10].

Microbial bioproduction offers a promising, eco-friendly, and versatile alternative to natural extraction and chemical synthesis [11]. Noncanonical phenolic monomers are a key frontier in melanin research for producing custom biopolymers. Utilizing structurally varied precursors—catechol, caffeic acid, gallic acid, and lignin- derived aromatics—the final macromolecular structure of melanin can be tailored [12]. Genetically engineered platforms, including Escherichia coli, Streptomyces species, and Cupriavidus necator, efficiently convert simple carbon sources or renewable feedstocks into high-value melanin [10, 13].

Modern metabolic engineering strategies have enabled the development of self-sufficient microbial systems that synthesize precursors such as L-tyrosine from glucose or glycerol, bypassing substrate dependency and reducing raw material costs [8, 14]. Catechol-based allomelanins are synthesized from glycerol, offering unique electrochemical properties for energy storage devices. Gallic acid-based melanin exhibits improved water solubility and antioxidant activity, making it suitable for flexible motion sensors and bioelectronics. Lignin hydrolysates are converted into isomelanin for sun protection, supporting a circular bioeconomy by repurposing agricultural waste.

To overcome existing economic and functional bottlenecks, this study aims to screen 13 phenolic monomers to identify efficient and cost-effective novel substrates for microbial melanin production using a recombinant E. coli BL21 strain expressing the MelC tyrosinase gene from Priestia megaterium. This screening yielded novel melanin-like pigments derived from p-cresol (PC-melanin) and 4-hydroxyphenylacetic acid (4-HPA melanin), and their production yields were optimized. The resulting chemical structures were rigorously characterized via scanning electron microscopy (SEM), Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS), Ultraviolet (UV)-vis absorption spectroscopy, Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR), and their physicochemical properties and antioxidant capacities were evaluated and compared to L-tyrosine derived eumelanin. Furthermore, thermogravimetric analysis revealed high thermal stability of these novel pigments and the oligomeric fragmentation patterns were elucidated via high-resolution solid-state NMR, MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry, and solid-state EPR spectroscopy. These findings could establish a fundamental framework for studying microbial melanins and open new avenues for the industrial development and use of PC-melanin and 4-HPA melanin in advanced material applications.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and reagents

L-tyrosine, p-Cresol, 4-Hydroxyphenylacetic acid, butylated hydroxytoluene (BHT), 4-hydroxybenzoic acid, vanillic acid, pyrogallol, phloroglucinol, tannic acid, 4-methylcatechol, 2,4-dihydroxybenzoic acid, coumarin, isatin, anthranilic acid, copper sulfate, 2,2′-azino-bis(3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) (ABTS), and mushroom tyrosinase were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich and Duksan (Suwon, Gyeonggi-do, South Korea). Analytical-grade solvents, including acetone, chloroform, methanol, ethanol, sodium hydroxide (NaOH), ethyl acetate, and dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), were obtained from Daejung (Seoul, South Korea). Luria-Bertani (LB) medium was obtained from Difco (Seoul, South Korea).

Bacterial strains and culture conditions

Escherichia coli BL21(DE3) was used as the host strain for recombinant protein expression. The cells were transformed with a pACYC184 vector carrying the MelC tyrosinase gene derived from Priestia megaterium (formerly Bacillus megaterium) ATCC 10,778 [13]. Transformed cells were cultured in LB medium at 37 °C at 200 rpm. Protein expression was induced at the mid-logarithmic growth phase (optical density [OD600] = 0.65–0.7) by adding 0.25 mM isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside [15]. Following induction, selected phenolic monomers for screening were added to the culture at final concentrations of 1,10 and 20 mM, CuSO4 was used as a cofactor, and their final concentration was maintained to 2mM irrespective of the changing substrate concentration. The biotransformation process was maintained at 30 °C with constant shaking at 200 rpm for 48 h. The control eumelanin from l-tyrosine was also produced following the above procedure.

Physicochemical solubility profiling

Solubility assays were conducted in 10 polar and nonpolar solvents to characterize the physicochemical properties of the synthesized pigments. Dried melanin samples were dispersed at ~ 1 mg/mL in acetone, chloroform, methanol, ethanol, ethyl acetate, DMSO, distilled water, 1 M sodium bicarbonate, 1 M sodium carbonate, and 10 mM NaOH. The mixtures were vigorously shaken and vortexed at room temperature to facilitate dissolution. The solubility of each variant was assessed qualitatively via visual inspection and OD measurements, categorized as well dissolved (+++), partially dissolved (++), or insoluble (-) [16].

Separation and purification of biosynthesized melanin-like pigments

The synthesized melanins were separated from the culture medium via centrifugation at 8,000 rpm and 4 °C for 10 min. We implemented a purification method for biosynthesized melanin reported by Park S et al. [12]. Briefly, the supernatant containing the soluble pigment was transferred to a fresh conical tube, and the pH was adjusted to 2.0 with 6 M HCl to induce precipitation. The acidified mixture was incubated at 25 °C for 4 h to maximize pigment aggregation. The precipitated melanin was collected via centrifugation at 8,000 rpm for 15 min. The resulting pellets were washed two to three times with distilled water to remove residual salts and impurities. Finally, the purified melanin was dried in a hot-air oven at 60 °C for 24 h before characterization [17].

Ultraviolet-Visible spectroscopic characterization

UV-visible spectroscopy was performed to evaluate the optical properties and electronic structure of the synthesized pigments. The purified melanin samples were dissolved in 1 M sodium carbonate to ensure complete solubility before analysis. Spectral data were collected using an Epoch Microplate Spectrophotometer (BioTek, Seoul, South Korea). A wavelength scan was conducted from 245 to 750 nm to capture the UV and visible regions. The ODs were recorded to generate characteristic absorbance profiles for each melanin variant [3].

Substrate docking

The binding affinity of noncanonical substrates for the enzyme active site was evaluated through in silico molecular docking simulations. The tyrosinase crystal structure (PDB ID: 4P6R) served as the receptor model. The original crystallographic zinc ions (Zn) in the active site of 4P6R were computationally replaced with copper ions prior to receptor preparation to accurately model the active site of tyrosinase. Structural preparation was performed using AutoDockTools (MGLTools 1.5.6). Crystallographic water molecules were initially removed to simulate a solvent-free environment, then polar hydrogens were added, and Kollman united atom charges were assigned to the macromolecule.

Ligands—novel substrates (PC and 4-HPA) and the reference substrate (L-tyrosine)—were prepared by defining rotatable bonds and applying torsion corrections to ensure energy-minimized conformations. Receptor and ligand structures were converted to PDBQT format before docking. Subsequently, a search grid was centered on the tyrosine-bound active site to encompass the catalytic pocket. During docking simulations using Auto Dock Vina 1.2.7, the copper cofactors and the receptor backbone were treated as rigid to preserve the native catalytic geometry of the binuclear center. To ensure robust conformational sampling and algorithmic reproducibility, the search exhaustiveness was set to 14 and 20 binding modes were generated per ligand. Reproducibility was validated by observing tight geometric convergence among the top-ranked (lowest-energy) binding poses.

Structural analysis of melanin samples using scanning electron microscopy and energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy

The morphology of the synthesized melanins was examined via field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM; JSM-7900 F). Samples were mounted on conductive stubs and imaged to visualize surface topography and aggregation patterns. Simultaneously, EDS was conducted at an accelerating voltage of 5 kV to quantify elemental composition. The analysis revealed the atomic percentages of carbon (C), oxygen (O), nitrogen (N), and sulfur (S) to confirm the chemical composition of the synthesized polymers.

Structural analysis of melanin samples: X-ray diffraction study

The crystallographic structure and phase composition of the synthesized melanins were assessed via X-ray diffraction (XRD). Diffraction patterns were recorded at room temperature over a scanning range (2θ) of 10°–90° to assess crystallinity of the material. The diffractograms were analyzed to confirm the amorphous characteristics of the biopolymers and detect potential crystalline impurities.

Structural analysis of melanin samples: Fourier-Transformed infrared analysis

Melanin functional groups and structure were analyzed using attenuated total reflectance Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (Nicolet™ iS50 FTIR spectrometer, Thermo Fisher Scientific) equipped with a deuterated triglycine sulfate detector. Spectra were recorded 4000 to 400 cm− 1 at 4 cm− 1 resolution. Each spectrum was background-corrected using a clean crystal measurement before sample loading.

Structural analysis of melanin samples: solid-state NMR

Solid-state 13 C nuclear magnetic resonance (ssNMR) experiments were conducted to elucidate the structural features and polymerization modes of the control eumelanin and the synthesized melanin-like pigments (PC-melanin and 4HPA-melanin). Spectra were acquired on a Bruker Avance III HD spectrometer (NCIRF, SNU, South Korea) operating at a ¹H Larmor frequency of 500.13 MHz and a ¹³C Larmor frequency of 125.77 MHz. Samples in the form of dry powders were packed into standard zirconium dioxide rotors and spun at a magic-angle spinning (MAS) rate of 5 kHz in a double-resonance H/X probe.

To mitigate the interference of spinning sidebands characteristic of the highly anisotropic chemical shift tensors in aromatic systems, ¹³C spectra were collected using a Cross-Polarization Total Suppression of Spinning Sidebands (CP-TOSS) pulse sequence [18, 19].

The acquisition parameters were uniformly applied across all samples to ensure quantitative comparability: a recycle delay (D1) of 3.0 s, a contact time of 3.0 ms to optimize magnetization transfer from ¹H to ¹³C and an accumulation of 2048 scans (NS) per sample at an ambient probe temperature of ~ 298 K. High-power SPINAL-64 ¹H heteronuclear decoupling was applied during the acquisition period to enhance resolution. Free induction decays (FIDs) were processed using an exponential multiplication function with a line broadening (LB) factor of 200 Hz prior to Fourier transformation to maximize the signal-to-noise ratio in these intrinsically heterogeneous bio macromolecular samples [20].

Structural analysis of melanin samples: MALDI-TOF MS

The chemical profiles and structural fragmentation patterns of the synthesized melanin-like pigments (PC-melanin and 4HPA-melanin) were evaluated against a standard eumelanin positive control. Mass spectrometry analysis was performed using an ASTA IDSys MALDI-TOF mass spectrometer (ASTA, Suwon-si, South Korea) equipped with a high-resolution optical camera for precision sample alignment. Analytes were analyzed without a conventional matrix; instead, powdered melanin samples and a liquid mass calibration standard (C9H6NOLi) were directly immobilized onto conductive carbon tape (Teroka Tape, 0.02 mm thickness) to minimize electrostatic charging and low-mass background noise during ionization. Desorption and ionization were achieved using a Yb: YAG UV laser (lambda = 343 nm) operating at a 1 kHz repetition rate with a 1 ns pulse width. Spectra were acquired in both positive and negative ion modes. To ensure high signal-to-noise ratios and representative sampling, data were integrated over 250 laser shots per sample at a 55% laser energy output. Primary data visualization, baseline correction, and peak picking were executed using IDSys analysis software (ver. 2.0.7).

Structural analysis of melanin samples using thermogravimetric analysis

The thermal decomposition profiles and stability of the synthesized melanins were evaluated via thermogravimetric analysis (TGA, Discovery TGA 5500, TA Instruments). Melanin samples were heated from room temperature to 1000 °C at 10 °C/min to quantify thermal resistance. Weight loss was continuously monitored as a function of temperature. TGA curves were used to determine the residual mass fraction, and the first derivative of the TGA curve facilitated the identification of maximum decomposition temperatures.

2,2’- Azino-bis(3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) Assay

Eumelanin-like pigments antioxidant activity was evaluated by measuring its radical scavenging ability. The reaction mixture was prepared by combining 14.8 mM ABTS solution in PBS and 4.9 mM potassium persulfate solution, followed by pH adjustment to 7.4. The reaction solution was incubated in the dark for 24 h and diluted to an OD of 0.7 at 734 nm. ABTS scavenging capacity was expressed as percentage antioxidant activity (AA%), calculated as: [(Acontrol − Asample)/Acontrol] × 100 [21].

Electro paramagnetic resonance

Solid- state Electron Paramagnetic Resonance (EPR) spectroscopy was performed to analyze the free radical properties of the synthesized melanin variants. The samples analyzed included Eumelanin (5.2 mg), PC-melanin (5.3 mg), and 4-HPA-melanin (6.6 mg). All measurements were conducted at room temperature (R.T.). Data were acquired using an EPR spectrometer at the Korea Basic Science Institute (KBSI). The continuous-wave EPR spectra was recorded systematically under a microwave frequency of 9.86 GHz and a microwave power of 1 mW. The magnetic field modulation was set to a frequency of 100 kHz with a modulation amplitude of 10 G. The acquisition parameters included a time constant of 20.48 ms, a conversion time of 20.00 ms, and a single sweep scan.

Results and discussion

Screening and biosynthesis of melanins

Microbial eumelanin biosynthesis depends on L-tyrosine, a costly substrate that makes industrial-scale production expensive. Current research uses canonical eumelanin precursors, limiting the exploration of noncanonical precursors for bioremediation. The biosynthesis of eumelanin begins with the tyrosinase catalyzed oxidation of L-tyrosine to L-DOPA followed by its subsequent conversion to dopaquinone. Dopaquinone then undergoes intramolecular cyclization, oxidation, and rearrangements to form eumelanin. Polyphenols and phenolic monomers serve as tyrosinase substrates. Compounds with an accessible ortho position for hydroxyl group attachment can form o-diphenols, which oxidize to o-quinone. Precursors directed polymerization and nucleophilic attack by media amine species lead to aggregation and formation of brownish-black eumelanin-like pigments (Fig. 1A).

Thirteen substrates, selected for structural similarity and cost-effectiveness, were screened for melanin production (Table 1). For biotransformation, MelC tyrosinase from Priestia megaterium (Bacillus megaterium), exhibiting high homology to human tyrosinase (33–35%), was cloned into the pACYC vector (previously expressed by our lab). Among the screened substrates, BHT and coumarin produced a slight deep brown color, but yields were insufficient after extraction. Pyrogallol, phloroglucinol, 4-methylcatechol, 4-hydroxybenzoic acid, 2,4-dihydroxybenzoic acid, tannic acid, isatin, vanillic acid, and anthranilic acid did not produce melanin. Some samples showed deep coloration in the control and test due to oxidation (Supplementary Fig. 1). Precursors, including p-cresol and 4-HPA, produced significant melanin-like pigment production (Fig. 1B & C) through MelC tyrosinase-mediated oxidation. This approach successfully produced novel melanin-like pigments, including PC-melanin and 4-HPA melanin. Eumelanin derived from L-tyrosine served as a positive control in all the experiments.

Consistent with the intrinsic hydrophobicity of aggregated melanin-like pigments, all synthesized variants were insoluble (-) in distilled water and organic solvents, including acetone, chloroform, ethyl acetate, methanol, and ethanol. Conversely, all variants were highly soluble (+++) in alkaline solutions, including 1 M sodium bicarbonate, 1 M sodium carbonate, and 10 mM NaOH. Alkali solubility and insolubility in neutral/acidic aqueous and organic media confirm that the biosynthesized pigments retain the chemical identity of eumelanin-like pigments [20, 22].

A critical divergence was observed in DMSO. In the solubility table, the eumelanin control was insoluble (-) in DMSO (Fig. 2A). Conversely, both novel eumelanin-like pigments were partially soluble (++). This finding suggests that noncanonical precursors (PC and 4-HPA) introduce specific side-chain modifications that disrupt the rigid, highly cross-linked supramolecular structure of standard eumelanin (Fig. 2B and C). The partial solubility in DMSO could theoretically arise from incomplete polymerization or residual impurities. However, our extensive acid-precipitation and washing protocols, combined with the absence of sharp monomeric peaks in XRD and FTIR, suggest that this solubility reflects an inherently altered polymer architecture. As supported by FTIR analysis, the incorporation of PC and 4-HPA likely reduces the degree of cross-linking and conjugation, thereby relaxing the rigid supramolecular network and permitting enhanced interaction with polar aprotic solvents. This improved solubility profile facilitates downstream processing and formulation of these eumelanin-like pigments for bio applications without requiring harsh alkaline conditions.

Docking simulation of MelC tyrosinase enzyme with phenolic substrates

Molecular docking revealed the structural feasibility of p-cresol and 4-HPA as tyrosinase substrates. Twenty docked conformations (Num modes = 20) per substrate were generated using an extensive conformational search (exhaustiveness = 14). Optimal binding poses were selected based on the lowest binding energy and compatibility with the catalytic binuclear copper center. Docking scores revealed that the novel substrates exhibited binding affinities similar to the natural substrate. The reference L-tyrosine showed a binding energy of -6.488 kcal/mol (Fig. 3A). 4-HPA showed a similar affinity of -6.280 kcal/mol (Fig. 3C), while p-cresol exhibited a slightly higher but still favorable energy of -5.362 kcal/mol (Fig. 3B).

Residue-level interaction analysis performed using the docking analysis module revealed the conservation of key binding motifs. Residue HIS208 and copper ions were key interaction points, anchoring all tested substrates in the active site. 4-HPA showed the most extensive interaction network, engaging five distinct residues, consistent with its higher binding score compared to p-cresol. These in silico findings suggest that p-cresol and 4-HPA can effectively occupy the catalytic pocket and engage key residues similarly to native tyrosine, supporting the experimental bioproduction data.

Optical properties and ultraviolet-screening capacity

UV-Visible spectroscopy revealed the eumelanin identity, indicating that the novel pigments share the characteristic optical profile of natural eumelanin. The absorption spectra of PC-melanin and 4-HPA melanin exhibited a broad monotonic decay from the UV to the visible region (Fig. 4A). Quantitative analysis revealed that both novel eumelanin-like pigments had a maximum absorbance (λmax) at 254 nm, closely matching the standard eumelanin control at 253 nm. In the UV region (250–260 nm), all spectra showed high OD (≈ OD 3.0), indicating strong UV-screening potential. Absorbance gradually decreased to ~ 1.0 OD in the visible range (400–450 nm). This residual absorbance in the visible spectrum accounts for the dark pigmentation of the purified samples [23].

This spectral similarity suggests that chromophore energy distribution and the extent of π-electron delocalization within the supramolecular stacked oligomeric architecture are preserved, despite the use of different noncanonical precursors. These findings suggest that novel eumelanin-like pigments have UV-protective properties like native eumelanin.

Structural analysis

To investigate the chemical structure of the synthesized eumelanin-like pigments, ssNMR and FTIR analyses were conducted. FTIR spectroscopy was used to identify the functional groups and chemical bonding patterns within the eumelanin-like pigments. The resulting spectra are presented in Fig. 4B, along with the L-tyrosine–derived eumelanin standard synthesized using the same experimental procedure as the novel eumelanin-like pigments. PC-melanin and 4-HPA melanin exhibited characteristic absorption bands typical of eumelanin polymers, confirming the presence of overlapping functional groups derived from phenolic and indolic structures. Eumelanin displayed a broad absorption band at 3200–3300 cm− 1, corresponding to the stretching vibrations of hydroxyl and amine groups, which reflect the extensive hydrogen bonding within the polymer network [24].

A moderate absorption band at 2925 cm− 1 is attributed to aliphatic C-H stretching, indicating the presence of side chain or aromatic substituents. Prominent absorption maxima between 1620 and 1515 cm− 1 correspond to aromatic C = C and C = N stretching vibrations of indole and pyrrole rings, which are characteristic of eumelanin-type conjugated backbones. Compared to the standard, the other two eumelanin-like pigments peaks exhibit slightly intensified and better-resolved signals in the OH/N-H and C-O stretching regions 1200–1050 cm− 1, suggesting increased hydroxylation and reduced cross-linking, consistent with lower polymerization or oxidation states. Collectively, these spectral features confirm that 4-HPA and PC-melanins maintain the core eumelanin-like molecular architecture while exhibiting increased hydrophilicity and reduced conjugation, reflecting differences in their biosynthetic pathways and polymerization dynamics.

To determine the structural composition and covalent linkages governing the architectural framework of the newly synthesized eumelanin-like pigments, high-resolution solid-state ¹³C CP-TOSS NMR spectroscopy was performed. The comparative spectra of the standard eumelanin, PC-melanin, and 4HPA-melanin reveal a highly heterogeneous complex, resembling eumelanin-like pigments.

Recent literature comparing biological black soldier fly (BSF) eumelanin to synthetic models demonstrates that intense signals in the aliphatic region (0–90 ppm) of natural melanins are predominantly of residual protein matrix and environmental impurities, while synthetic DL-DOPA eumelanin presents a largely vacant aliphatic profile [25]. In contrast, the prominent aliphatic resonances observed in our microbially synthesized eumelanin-like pigments are intrinsic, with covalently integrated structural features. For PC-melanin, this is evidenced by a sharp, dominant resonance at 25.1 ppm (with a shoulder at 30.8 ppm), corresponding to the preservation of the methyl group directly attached to the aromatic scaffold (Fig. 5B). Similarly, 4HPA-melanin exhibits a robust signal at 39.8 ppm, assigned to the structural retention of the methylene bridge (-CH₂-) connecting the aromatic ring to the carboxylate group (Fig. 5A). Analysis of the aromatic region (95–155 ppm) further highlights the divergent polymerization pathways. Previous studies have noted that extensive cross-linking in complex biological eumelanins (such as DHI/DHICA-protein complex) significantly suppresses aromatic signal intensity in CP/MAS experiments due to altered proton concentration and relaxation dynamics [26, 27]. The spectrum of our control eumelanin aligns with standard highly condensed indole-based architectures, displaying a broad aromatic envelope (110–160 ppm) with specific intra-ring C-C cross-link signals at 137.9 and 129.5 ppm.(Fig. 5C) Conversely, the PC-melanin spectrum displays shifted and narrowed unsubstituted aromatic C-H signals (128.9 and 118.2 ppm) alongside strong phenolic C-OH resonances (144.2 ppm). This narrowing indicates a departure from random, highly suppressed heterogeneous cross-linking, suggesting instead that PC-melanin polymerization proceeds primarily via specific ortho-ortho and ortho-meta coupling pathways, yielding a uniquely hydrophobic, methyl-decorated conjugated backbone.

The carboxyl and quinone region (160–200 ppm) provide critical insight into the oxidation state and functionalization of the polymers. While an elevated carboxyl-to-aromatic ratio in biological samples is typically attributed to a high baseline DHICA content and generalized phenolic oxidation, the exceptional carboxyl signature in our 4HPA-melanin represents targeted precursor retention. The spectrum features an exceptionally prominent, narrow carbonyl resonance at 172.39 ppm. Rather than representing the oxidative cleavage like in melanogenesis (observed as a broader signal at 172.3 ppm in the control), this peak confirms the high-density preservation of the acetic acid side chains extending from the aromatic core [28].

Collectively, these NMR data demonstrate that while the novel PC- and 4HPA-melanins adopt the broad structural heterogeneity defining eumelanin-like pigments, they successfully retain their precursor-specific functional groups.

MALDI-TOF MS analysis

To confirm the structural identity and polymerization profile of the samples, MALDI-TOF MS was employed to compare the experimental PC-melanin and 4HPA-melanin against eumelanin control [29]. Due to the characteristic insolubility and heteropolymer nature of melanin, analysis of the low-molecular-weight fragments provided critical structural fingerprints [30].

The detection of diverse melanin monomeric and oligomeric species via MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry was expected, as the high-energy laser application inherently fragments the structural backbone of the melanin polymer [13]. The standard eumelanin control exhibited distinct, reproducible fragmentation patterns in both ionization modes. In negative ion mode, the eumelanin spectrum was dominated by high-intensity peaks at m/z 26.0, 42.0, 50.0, 78.9, and 114.8(Supplementary Fig. 2A). In positive ion mode, eumelanin yielded a characteristic mass signature with prominent fragments at m/z 38.9, 71.9, 87.9, 108.9, 124.9, and 160.8 [31].

The synthesized test samples demonstrated mass profiles that were highly homologous to the eumelanin standard, confirming their successful melanogenesis. PC-melanin’s negative mode spectrum conserved the primary eumelanin peaks at m/z 26.0, 42.0, 78.9, and 114.8 (Supplementary Fig. 2B). Additionally, PC-melanin produced unique higher-mass oligomeric fragments in negative mode at m/z 130.8, 203.6, and 219.6. The positive ion profile for P C-melanin closely mirrored the control, exhibiting major signals at m/z 71.9, 87.8, 108.8, and 160.8. The negative mode analysis of 4HPA-melanin displayed strong peak alignment with the control at m/z 26.0, 50.0, 78.8, and 114.9, alongside a prominent signal at m/z 203.8. In positive mode, the 4HPA-melanin variant exhibited a more streamlined fragmentation profile with defining peaks at m/z 38.9, 70.0, and 124.9 (Supplementary Fig. 2C).

Collectively, the robust conservation of key diagnostic peaks (such as m/z 114.8/114.9 in negative mode and ~ 71.9 in positive mode) across the test samples strongly indicates that both PC-melanin and 4HPA-melanin possess the core indole-based structural subunits characteristic of natural eumelanin. The presence of unique derivative peaks further suggests successful functional incorporation of their respective precursor molecules.

Scanning electron microscopy and Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy

FE-SEM was conducted to examine the structural topography of the biosynthesized pigments. The micrographs revealed a characteristic amorphous morphology composed of irregular, aggregated granules for PC and 4-HPA melanins (Fig. 6B and C respectively). This nonuniform, nano-aggregated architecture resulted in a material with a high surface area, a feature characteristic of natural eumelanin (Fig. 6A). The observed porosity and granular aggregation are key structural prerequisites for the functional versatility of eumelanin, particularly regarding antioxidant activity and capacity for physicochemical interactions with other molecules. The structural consistency across samples suggests that the biotransformation process produces a stable polymeric scaffold comparable to native eumelanin pigments.

The standard eumelanin exhibited a composition of C 66.24%, N 14.91%, and O 17.96%, with a trace sulfur content of 0.25%. The novel eumelanin-like pigment variants exhibited comparable atomic ratios. PC-melanin showed slightly higher nitrogen (17.35%) and oxygen (18.91%), while 4-HPA melanin maintained a carbon content of 66.58%. These data provide definitive chemical evidence distinguishing the synthesized pigments from pheomelanin, which contains high sulfur due to cysteine incorporation [32]. The high nitrogen and trace sulfur profile confirms that, despite using non-canonical precursors, the synthesized pigments remain eumelanin-type polymers.

X-ray diffraction analysis

XRD analysis revealed the amorphous structural organization of the synthesized pigments. The diffractograms of the novel variants (PC-melanin and 4-HPA melanin) closely matched that of the standard eumelanin control (Fig. 7B). All samples exhibited a characteristic broad diffraction halo centered at approximately 2θ ≈ 20°, with weak shoulders and no sharp Bragg peaks, indicative of the absence of long-range crystallographic order. The observed pattern confirms that the synthesized pigments are non-crystalline, amorphous solids. This structural disorder, represents a typical van der Waals spacing of ~ 3.4 A° between stacked planar oligomers, is fully consistent with the established X-ray scattering profiles of natural and synthetic eumelanins and aligns with the irregular, aggregated granule morphology seen in the SEM analysis [12, 28, 33].

Thermal stability

The thermal stability of the novel eumelanin-like pigment variants was rigorously assessed via TGA to determine their suitability for high-performance industrial applications. PC-melanin and 4-HPA melanin demonstrated exceptional resistance to thermal degradation across a broad temperature range (0–1000 °C). The TGA profiles revealed a multi-stage decomposition pattern typical of eumelanin polymers [34]. Following initial bound water loss at < 150 °C, the polymers exhibited gradual mass loss in the 150–300 °C range. According to Gonçalves et al., this region corresponds to the decarboxylation of the polymeric structure and the evolution of CO2. Notably, PC-melanin and 4-HPA melanin exhibited enhanced stability through this intermediate degradation phase compared to standard synthetic eumelanin, ultimately retaining 37% of their mass at 800 °C.

PC-melanin (Fig. 7C) exhibited robust thermal stability, retaining 38% of its initial mass at 800 °C with a final residual mass of 27% at 1000 °C. 4-HPA melanin (Fig. 7D) showed a similar profile, retaining 37% of its mass at 800 °C and 24% at 1000 °C. At 500 °C PC-melanin and 4-HPA retained 49.89% and 51.47% mass respectively. These retention values represent a significant enhancement over previously reported natural and synthetic melanins, which typically exhibit significantly lower stability (often retaining only 12–30% of their mass at 500 °C) [33, 35]. This enhanced thermal durability indicates a highly stable, cross-linked polymeric structure. The ability to withstand temperatures > 800 °C suggests that these novel biosynthetic eumelanin-like pigments possess strong potential for high-demand industrial applications, including heat-resistant additives in electronic components, protective high-temperature coatings, and advanced composite materials.

Antioxidant activity

The antioxidant activity of the eumelanin-like pigments was assessed using the ABTS radical scavenging assay. The newly synthesized eumelanin-like pigments were tested at various concentrations for their free radical-scavenging activity. The results showed a concentration-dependent increase in scavenging activity for both synthesized eumelanin-like pigments. The scavenging percentage of PC-melanin (red) and 4-HPA melanin (green) increased steadily as concentrations increased from 0.008 to 0.064 mg/mL (Fig. 7E & F). PC-melanin was the most potent antioxidant, with a low IC50 of 0.0139 ± 0.008 mg/mL, ~ 20-fold lower than conventional eumelanin (0.28 ± 0.15 mg/mL; Table 3).

This superior performance suggests that PC-melanin and 4-HPA melanin have denser reactive phenolic hydroxyl groups or a more favorable redox potential. These structural advantages enable the synthesized pigments to more effectively neutralize the ABTS chromophore at significantly lower inhibitory concentrations than other bio-melanin benchmarks. However, the industrial standard for radical scavenging (DPPH) needs to be verified and addressed in future studies. To overcome the intrinsic free-radical dispersion effects of eumelanin like pigments we further evaluated the solid state EPR spectra of the obtained pigments.

EPR analysis

To ascertain the stable free radical signatures inherent to the synthesized eumelanin-like pigments, solid-statewide-scan EPR spectra (0–600 mT) were acquired. All three variants—Eumelanin, PC-melanin, and 4-HPA-melanin—exhibited a prominent, anisotropic resonance signal centered in the 340–350 mT region, which is characteristic of stable semiquinone free radicals trapped within the conjugated polymer matrix [36].

While the general spectral morphology remains consistent across the variants, distinct variations in the absolute signal intensities were observed. The standard Eumelanin sample demonstrated a peak-to-peak intensity spanning approximately from + 1.5 to -3 a.u (Supplementary Fig. 3A). In comparison, PC-melanin signal spanned approximately + 2 to -4.5 a.u 9 Supplementary Fig. 3B). while 4-HPA-melanin signal ranges + 2.5 to -5.0 a.u (Supplementary Fig. 3C).

Notably, in addition to the primary central organic free radical signal, all three spectra revealed distinct, smaller resolved features in the lower magnetic field region between approximately 280 and 320 mT. This secondary structural feature suggests the presence of multiple paramagnetic species, such as chelated trace transition metals, or complex hyperfine interactions localized within the melanin macromolecular network. Across all variants, the peak-to-peak linewidths remained consistent within the 4–6 mT range, confirming that the metabolic engineering of p-cresol and 4-hydroxyphenylacetic acid pathways preserved the fundamental redox-active semiquinone scaffold while successfully modulating the free radical density [37].

Conclusion

This study shows an efficient microbial platform using recombinant E. coli expressing MelC tyrosinase to synthesize novel functional melanins. Among the 13 screened phenolic monomers, PC and 4-HPA were identified as highly effective substrates, yielding 2.40 g/L of PC-melanin and 3.92 g/L of 4-HPA melanin at the optimized 10 mM concentrations. In silico molecular docking supported these findings revealing binding affinities and catalytic geometries at the binuclear copper center similar to those of the native L-tyrosine substrate. Comprehensive structural and magnetic characterizations (FE-SEM, XRD, EDS, FTIR, Solid-state NMR, MALDI-TOF MS, and EPR) confirmed that these biosynthesized polymers retain the core amorphous, nano-aggregated architecture of natural eumelanin and exhibit high nitrogen with trace sulfur content (< 0.30%). However, unlike standard eumelanin, unique side-chain modifications conferred partial solubility in DMSO, offering distinct advantages for downstream processing and formulation. Functionally, these eumelanin-like pigments exhibited exceptional thermal stability, retaining > 37% of their mass at 800 °C, and strong antioxidant activity. PC-melanin exhibited an ABTS radical-scavenging IC50 ~ 20-fold lower than that of standard eumelanin. Ultimately, the high thermal stability, strong UV-protection, and potent antioxidant activity of PC-melanin and 4HPA- melanin highlight their potential as customizable biomaterials for demanding industrial applications, including high-temperature protective coatings, electronic components, and advanced composite materials.
