Bounded Ramsey’s theorem for triples in computability theory
Ludovic Patey, Paul Shafer

Abstract
We study a restriction of Ramsey’s theorem for 2-coloring of triples, in which homogeneous sets for color 1 are of bounded size ( 𝖡𝖱𝖳 2 3 \mathsf{BRT}^{3}_{2} ). We prove that the computational content of this statement is very close to Ramsey’s theorem for pairs ( OPEN 𝖱𝖳 2 2 ) \mathsf{RT}^{2}_{2}) , in that it satisfies the same known computability-theoretic upper bounds, but that 𝖡𝖱𝖳 2 3 \mathsf{BRT}^{3}_{2} is not computably-reducible to 𝖱𝖳 2 2 \mathsf{RT}^{2}_{2} , even when allowing multiple applications of 𝖱𝖳 2 2 \mathsf{RT}^{2}_{2} .



1
Introduction

In this article, we study a bounded version of Ramsey’s theorem for triples from the viewpoint of reverse mathematics and computable reductions. Ramsey’s theorem is a fundamental theorem in combinatorics at the origins of Ramsey theory. Given a set
X\subseteq\mathbb{N}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we write
[X]^{n}
for the collection of all unordered
n
-tuples over
X
. For every
n,k\geq 1
, Ramsey’s theorem for
n
-tuples and
k
-colors (
\mathsf{RT}^{n}_{k}
) states that every coloring
f:[\mathbb{N}]^{n}\to\{0,\dots,k-1\}
admits an infinite set
H\subseteq\mathbb{N}
such that
f
uses exactly one color
i
on
[X]^{n}
. We then say that
H
is
f
-homogeneous (for color
i
)
.

In the same way that most applications of Borel determinacy require only low levels in the Borel hierarchy, many applications of Ramsey’s theorem in other areas of mathematics involve degenerate colorings. For instance, Ramsey’s theorem for transitive colorings is used in a factorization theorem in automata theory
[
18
]
. In this article, we study another restriction of Ramsey’s theorem for 2-colorings, in which the homogeneous sets of color 1 are of bounded size.

Statement

(Bounded Ramsey’s theorem)
.

\mathsf{BRT}^{n}_{2,\ell}
is the statement “Every coloring
f:[\mathbb{N}]^{n}\to 2
which has no
f
-homogeneous set for color 1 of size
\ell
has an infinite
f
-homogeneous set for color 0.”
\mathsf{BRT}^{n}_{2}
is the statement
\forall\ell\mathsf{BRT}^{n}_{2,\ell}
.

Such bounded colorings appear naturally in the proof that
\mathsf{RT}^{n+1}_{2}
implies
\forall k\mathsf{RT}^{n}_{k}
over
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}
, for example. The systematic study of this restriction was started by Frittaion, and it was pursued by Soldà
[
25
, Section 4.1]
and, more recently, by Le Houérou and Patey
[
11
]
, who studied bounded colorings of pairs.

Ramsey’s theorem is extensively studied in reverse mathematics
[
13
,
24
,
23
]
. For
n\geq 3
,
\mathsf{RT}^{n}_{2}
is equivalent to the Arithmetic Comprehension Axiom (
\mathsf{ACA}_{0}
) over
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}
, but this collapsing does not reflect the computability-theoretic content of Ramsey’s theorem, which is better explained in terms of pure computability theory.

The computational strength of Ramsey’s theorem for
(n+1)
-tuples (
\mathsf{RT}^{n+1}_{2}
) can be roughly understood as the “jump” of the strength of its version for
n
-tuples (
\mathsf{RT}^{n}_{2}
). Indeed, by Shoenfield’s limit lemma, every
\emptyset^{\prime}
-computable coloring of
n
-tuples can be represented as a computable coloring of
(n+1)
-tuples, and conversely, every stable computable coloring of
(n+1)
-tuples induces a
\emptyset^{\prime}
-computable coloring of
n
-tuples by considering the limit coloring. Based on this correspondence, the computability-theoretic bounds of Ramsey’s theorem for pairs translate to similar bounds for
n
-tuples by increasing the level in the arithmetic hierarchy accordingly. For instance, Jockusch
[
13
]
proved that for every
n\geq 2
, there exists a computable instance of
\mathsf{RT}^{n}_{2}
with no
\Sigma^{0}_{n}
solution, while every computable instance of
\mathsf{RT}^{n}_{2}
admits a
\Pi^{0}_{n}
solution. Moreover, again by Jockusch
[
13
]
, for every
n\geq 2
, there exists a computable instance of
\mathsf{RT}^{n}_{2}
such that every solution computes
\emptyset^{(n-2)}
; while Seetapun
[
23
]
and Cholak, Jockusch and Slaman
[
3
]
proved that for every
n\geq 2
, every computable instance of
\mathsf{RT}^{n}_{2}
has a solution which does not compute
\emptyset^{(n-1)}
.

There exists a similar hierarchy for the bounded version of Ramsey’s theorem, with a subtlety on the bound: We shall see that
\mathsf{BRT}^{n+1}_{2,\ell+1}
can be thought of as the jump version of
\mathsf{BRT}^{n}_{2,\ell}
. However, the
\mathsf{BRT}_{2}
-hierarchy seems to be shifted compared to the
\mathsf{RT}_{2}
-hierarchy, in that the computational strength of
\mathsf{BRT}^{n+1}_{2}
is very close to that of
\mathsf{RT}^{n}_{2}
. For instance,
\mathsf{BRT}^{2}_{2}
, like
\mathsf{RT}^{1}_{2}
, is a computably true statement, which means that every computable instance admits a computable solution. It follows that the strength of
\mathsf{BRT}^{2}_{2}
is better understood in terms of the amount of induction needed to prove the existence of a solution
[
11
]
. Bounded Ramsey’s theorem for triples is the first level of this hierarchy which is not computably true, and therefore is relevant for a computability-theoretic analysis. This is our main object of study in this article.

1.1
Reverse mathematics and computable reductions

Reverse mathematics is a foundational program started by Harvey Friedman
[
8
]
, whose goal is to find optimal axioms to prove ordinary theorems. It uses the framework of subsystems of second-order arithmetic, with a base theory
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}
capturing “computable mathematics.” More precisely,
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}
is composed of Robinson’s arithmetic together with the
\Sigma^{0}_{1}
-induction scheme and the
\Delta^{0}_{1}
-comprehension scheme. See Simpson
[
24
]
or Dzhafarov and Mummert
[
5
]
for an introduction to reverse mathematics.

Structures in the language of second-order arithmetic are of the form
\mathcal{M}=(M,S,0,1,<,+,\cdot)
, where
M
denotes the set of integers of the structure, and
S\subseteq\mathcal{P}(M)
is the collection of its reals, also known as its second-order part. An
\omega
-structure
is a structure for which
M
consists of the standard integers, together with the usual interpretations of the constants, relations, and operations. Such structures are of particular interest, especially when proving a separation, as they are closer to the intended model. An
\omega
-structure is fully characterized by its second-order part
S
, and
\omega
-models of
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}
admit a purely computability-theoretic characterization. Indeed, by Friedman, an
\omega
-structure is a model of
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}
if and only if its second-order part
S
is a
Turing ideal
, that is, a non-empty collection of sets which is downward-closed under Turing reduction
(\forall X\in S,\forall Y\leq_{T}X,Y\in S)
and closed under Turing join (
\forall X,Y\in S,X\oplus Y=\{2n:n\in X\}\cup\{2n+1:n\in Y\}\in S
).

Many statements
\mathsf{P}
studied in reverse mathematics are of the form

\forall X(\Phi(X)\to\exists Y\Psi(X,Y))

where
\Phi
and
\Psi
are arithmetic formulas. Such statements, called
\Pi^{1}_{2}
-problems
, can be seen as mathematical problems, whose
instances
are sets
X
such that
\Phi(X)
holds, and a set
Y
is a
solution
to an instance
X
if
\Psi(X,Y)
holds. For example, an instance of
\mathsf{BRT}^{n}_{2,\ell}
is a coloring
f:[\mathbb{N}]^{n}\to 2
with no
f
-homogeneous set for color 1 of size
\ell
, and a solution to
f
is an infinite
f
-homogeneous set for color 0. An
\omega
-model
\mathcal{M}
of
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}
satisfies a
\Pi^{1}_{2}
-problem
\mathsf{P}
if every
\mathsf{P}
-instance in
\mathcal{M}
admits a
\mathsf{P}
-solution in
\mathcal{M}
. We say that a
\Pi^{1}_{2}
-problem
\mathsf{P}
is
\omega
-reducible
to another
\Pi^{1}_{2}
-problem
\mathsf{Q}
(written
\mathsf{P}\leq_{\omega}\mathsf{Q}
if every
\omega
-model of
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}+\mathsf{Q}
is an
\omega
-model of
\mathsf{P}
. In particular, if
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}\vdash\mathsf{Q}\to\mathsf{P}
, then
\mathsf{P}\leq_{\omega}\mathsf{Q}
.

The
\omega
-reduction is a bridge between the proof-theoretic notion of implication over
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}
and the computability-theoretic realm. Hirschfeldt and Jockusch
[
9
]
gave a characterization of
\omega
-reducibility in terms of games, enabling finer control on the number of applications of
\mathsf{P}
in the reduction
\mathsf{Q}\leq_{\omega}\mathsf{P}
.

Definition 1.1

(Hirschfeldt and Jockusch
[
9
]
)
.

For problems
\mathsf{P}
and
\mathsf{Q}
, the
reduction game

G(\mathsf{Q}\to\mathsf{P})
is a two-player game that proceed as follows.
If at any point, one of the players does not have a legal move, then the game ends with a victory for the other player.
On the first move, Player 1 plays a
\mathsf{P}
-instance
X_{0}
, and Player 2 either plays an
X_{0}
-computable
\mathsf{P}
-solution to
X_{0}
and declares victory, in which case the game ends, or responds with an
X_{0}
-computable
\mathsf{Q}
-instance
Y_{1}
.
For
n>1
, on the
n
th
move, Player 1 plays a
\mathsf{Q}
-solution
X_{n-1}
to the
\mathsf{Q}
-instance
Y_{n-1}
. Then Player 2 either plays a
(\bigoplus_{i<n}X_{i})
-computable
\mathsf{P}
-solution to
X_{0}
and declares victory, in which case again the game ends, or plays a
(\bigoplus_{i<n}X_{i})
-computable
\mathsf{Q}
-instance
Y_{n}
.
Player 2 wins this play of the game if it ever declares victory or if Player 1 has no legal move at some point of the game. Otherwise, Player 1 wins.

Hirschfeldt and Jockusch
[
9
]
proved that
\mathsf{P}\leq_{\omega}\mathsf{Q}
if and only if Player 2 has a winning strategy for the game
G(\mathsf{Q}\to\mathsf{P})
. Given
k\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\mathsf{P}\leq_{\omega}^{k}\mathsf{Q}
hold if Player 2 has a winning strategy for the game
G(\mathsf{Q}\to\mathsf{P})
that guarantees victory in
k+1
or fewer moves. By the correspondence between
\omega
-reducibility and provability over
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}
, showing that
\mathsf{P}\not\leq_{\omega}^{k}\mathsf{Q}
could be understood as stating that there is no proof of
\mathsf{P}
over
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}+\mathsf{Q}
with at most
k
applications of
\mathsf{Q}
. However, this intuition should be tempered by a result from Hirst and Mummert
[
10
]
, who showed that
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}\vdash\mathsf{RT}^{n}_{2}\to\mathsf{RT}^{n}_{k}
for any standard
n,k\in\mathbb{N}
with only one application of
\mathsf{RT}^{n}_{2}
, while it is known by Patey
[
20
]
that
\mathsf{RT}^{n}_{2}\not\leq_{c}\mathsf{RT}^{n}_{3}
.

1.2
Stability and cohesiveness

In order to better understand the computational strength of Ramsey’s theorem for pairs, Cholak, Jockusch and Slaman
[
3
]
decomposed
\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{2}
into its stable and cohesive versions.
A coloring
f:[\mathbb{N}]^{n+1}\to k
is
stable
if for every
\vec{x}\in[\mathbb{N}]^{n}
,
\lim_{y}f(\vec{x},y)
exists. The
limit coloring
of
f
is the coloring
g:[\mathbb{N}]^{n}\to k
defined by
g(\vec{x})=\lim_{y}f(\vec{x},y)
.

As mentioned, Shoenfield’s limit lemma forms a bridge between stable Ramsey’s theorem for computable colorings of
(n+1)
-tuples and Ramsey’s theorem for
\Delta^{0}_{2}
colorings of
n
-tuples. We now give a formal correspondence between the bounded counterparts of these statements.

Lemma 1.2
.

Let
f:[\mathbb{N}]^{n+1}\to 2
be a computable stable instance of
\mathsf{BRT}^{n+1}_{2,\ell+1}
.
Its limit coloring is a
\Delta^{0}_{2}
instance of
\mathsf{BRT}^{n}_{2,\ell}
.

Proof.

Let
g:[\mathbb{N}]^{n}\to 2
be the limit coloring of
f
. By Shoenfield’s limit lemma,
g
is
\Delta^{0}_{2}
. Suppose for a contradiction that there is a
g
-homogeneous set
H
for color 1 of size
\ell
. Then for every
\vec{x}\in H
,
\lim_{y}f(\vec{x},y)=1
, so for some sufficiently large
y
,
H\cup\{y\}
is
f
-homogeneous for color 1 and has size
\ell+1
, which is a contradiction.
∎

Lemma 1.3
.

For every
\Delta^{0}_{2}
instance
g:[\mathbb{N}]^{n}\to 2
of
\mathsf{BRT}^{n}_{2,\ell}
,
there is a stable computable instance of
\mathsf{BRT}^{n+1}_{2,\ell+1}
whose limit coloring is
g
.

Proof.

By Shoenfield’s limit lemma, let
h:[\mathbb{N}]^{n+1}\to 2
be a computable stable coloring whose limit coloring is
g
. Fix a computable ordering of
[\mathbb{N}]^{n}
of type
\omega
. Compute a coloring
f:[\mathbb{N}]^{n+1}\to 2
as follows. At step
s
, consider the elements
\vec{x}
of
[s]^{n}
in order. For each
\vec{x}\in[s]^{n}
, set
f(\vec{x},s)=1
if
h(\vec{x},s)=1
and there is not an
F\subseteq s
of size
\ell
where the maximum element of
[F]^{n}
is
\vec{x}
and we have already set
f(\vec{z},s)=1
for all
\vec{z}\in[F]^{n}
with
\vec{z}<\vec{x}
. Otherwise set
f(\vec{x},s)=0
. Consider a set
H
of size
\ell+1
, and let
s
be its maximum element. Let
\vec{x}
be the maximum element of
[H\setminus\{s\}]^{n}
. If
h(\vec{x},s)=0
, then
f(\vec{x},s)=0
. If
h(\vec{x},s)=1
, then either there is a
\vec{z}<\vec{x}
in
[H\setminus\{s\}]^{n}
with
f(\vec{z},s)=0
, or we set
f(\vec{x},s)=0
. In all cases, we see that
H
is not
f
-homogeneous for color
1
.

For every
\vec{x}\in[\mathbb{N}]^{n}
,
\lim_{s}h(\vec{x},s)=0
implies that
\lim_{s}f(\vec{x},s)=0
. It remains to show that for every
\vec{x}\in[\mathbb{N}]^{n}
,
\lim_{s}h(\vec{x},s)=1
implies that
\lim_{s}f(\vec{x},s)=1
. Suppose for a contradiction that
\vec{x}\in[\mathbb{N}]^{n}
is least such that
\lim_{s}h(\vec{x},s)=1
but that there are infinitely many
s
with
f(\vec{x},s)=0
. Thus for infinitely many
s
, there is a set
F
of size
\ell
where the maximum element of
[F]^{n}
is
\vec{x}
, and
f(\vec{z},s)=1
for all
\vec{z}\in[F]^{n}
with
\vec{z}<\vec{x}
. There are only finitely many sets
F
of size
\ell
where the maximum element of
[F]^{n}
is
\vec{x}
, so there is a fixed such
F
where, for infinitely many
s
,
f(\vec{z},s)=1
for all
\vec{z}\in[F]^{n}
with
\vec{z}<\vec{x}
. By the minimality of
\vec{x}
, it must be that
1=\lim_{s}f(\vec{z},s)=\lim_{s}h(\vec{z},s)=g(\vec{z})
for all
\vec{z}\in[F]^{n}
with
\vec{z}<\vec{x}
. By assumption,
1=\lim_{s}h(\vec{x},s)=g(\vec{x})
as well. Thus
F
is a contradictory
g
-homogeneous set for color
1
of size
\ell
. Thus
f
is a stable computable instance of
\mathsf{BRT}^{n+1}_{2,\ell+1}
whose limit coloring agrees with that of
h
and therefore is
g
.
∎

Given an infinite sequence of sets
\vec{R}=R_{0},R_{1},\dots
, an infinite set
C\subseteq\mathbb{N}
is
\vec{R}
-cohesive
if for every
n
,
C\subseteq^{*}R_{n}
or
C\subseteq^{*}\overline{R}_{n}
, where
\subseteq^{*}
means “included up to finitely many elements.” Cohesiveness is an essential tool in restricting a computable coloring to a stable sub-domain, as follows:

Lemma 1.4

(Cholak, Jockusch and Slaman
[
3
]
)
.

Let
f:[\mathbb{N}]^{n+1}\to k
be a computable coloring.
There exists a uniformly computable sequence of sets
\vec{R}

such that for every
\vec{R}
-cohesive set
C
,
f
is stable on
[C]^{n+1}
.
That is, for every
\vec{x}\in[C]^{n}
,
\lim_{y\in C}f(\vec{x},y)
exists.

Proof.

For every
\vec{x}\in[\mathbb{N}]^{n}
and
i<k
, let
R_{\vec{x},i}=\{y:f(\vec{x},y)=i\}
. Let
C
be an infinite
\vec{R}
-cohesive set, where
\vec{R}=\{R_{\vec{x},i}\}_{\vec{x}\in[\mathbb{N}]^{n},i<k}
. Let
\vec{x}\in[C]^{n}
. Suppose that
\lim_{y\in C}f(\vec{x},y)
does not exist. Then there are some
i\neq j
such that
C\cap R_{\vec{x},i}
and
C\cap R_{\vec{x},j}
are both infinite. By the
\vec{R}
-cohesiveness of
C
,
C\subseteq^{*}R_{\vec{x},i}\cap R_{\vec{x},j}=\emptyset
, which is a contradiction.
∎

The strength of computing an infinite
\vec{R}
-cohesive set for a computable sequence of sets
\vec{R}
is well-understood in terms of jump computation
[
14
,
1
]
.
Based on the notions of stability and cohesiveness, our study of computable instances of
\mathsf{BRT}^{3}_{2}
will essentially consist in proving theorems about
\Delta^{0}_{2}
instances of
\mathsf{BRT}^{2}_{2}
, in the same way that the study of Ramsey’s theorem for pairs mainly relies on the study of
\Delta^{0}_{2}
instances of the pigeonhole principle.

1.3
Main contributions

The theorems in this paper divide into two categories: those showing that
\mathsf{BRT}^{3}_{2}
satisfies the same known computability-theoretic bounds as Ramsey’s theorem for pairs, and those separating
\mathsf{BRT}^{3}_{2}
from
\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{2}
over variations of the
\omega
-reduction. First and foremost, a classical argument shows that
\mathsf{RT}^{2}\leq_{c}\mathsf{BRT}^{3}_{2}
and that
\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{2}\leq_{c}\mathsf{BRT}^{3}_{2,4}
. Therefore, all the upper bounds of
\mathsf{BRT}^{3}_{2}
have to be at least as complicated as those of
\mathsf{RT}^{2}
.

Similar upper bounds
. Seetapun
[
23
]
proved that Ramsey’s theorem for pairs admits cone avoidance, and Liu
[
17
]
proved that it admits constant-bound trace avoidance (defined in
Section

2
). In his thesis, Soldà
[
25
, Section 4.1]
proved that
\mathsf{BRT}^{3}_{2}
also satisfies cone avoidance, using a general framework defined by Patey
[
21
]
. Using the Erdős-Moser theorem, we give an alternative proof of this fact, and we prove the following theorem:

Theorem 1.5
.

\mathsf{BRT}^{3}_{2}
admits constant-bound trace avoidance.

In terms of effectiveness, Cholak, Jockusch and Slaman
[
3
]
proved that
\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{2}
admits a weakly low basis, meaning that for every computable instance, any PA degree over
\emptyset^{\prime}
computes the jump of a solution. We prove the same theorem for
\mathsf{BRT}^{3}_{2}
:

Theorem 1.6
.

\mathsf{BRT}^{3}_{2}
admits a weakly low basis.

Separations from Ramsey’s theorem for pairs
. The similarity of the bounds satisfied by
\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{2}
and
\mathsf{BRT}^{3}_{2}
makes it complicated to separate the statements. In particular, we leave open the question whether
\mathsf{BRT}^{3}_{2}\leq_{\omega}\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{2}
. Using the framework of preservations of hyperimmunities, we however prove that
\mathsf{BRT}^{3}_{2,4}
cannot be solved by any fixed number of applications of
\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{2}
:

Theorem 1.7
.

For every
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
\mathsf{BRT}^{3}_{2,4}\not\leq_{\omega}^{k}\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{2}
.

Last, using an ad-hoc construction, we prove the existence of a computable instance of
\mathsf{BRT}^{3}_{2,4}
which defeats every computable instance of
\mathsf{RT}^{2}
, no matter the number of colors.

Theorem 1.8
.

\mathsf{BRT}^{3}_{2,4}\not\leq_{c}\mathsf{RT}^{2}
.

2
\mathsf{BRT}^{3}_{2}
and the Erdős-Moser theorem

As explained in
Section

1.2
, the computability-theoretic analysis of
\mathsf{BRT}^{3}_{2}
is closely related to the one of
\mathsf{BRT}^{2}_{2}
for
\Delta^{0}_{2}
instances. Le Houérou and Patey
[
11
]
and Soldà
[
25
, Section 4.1]
independently proved that
\mathsf{BRT}^{2}_{2}
follows from the Erdős-Moser theorem over
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}
. We use this relation to derive a framework for translating upper bounds from the Erdős-Moser theorem to
\mathsf{BRT}^{3}_{2}
. A
weakness property
is a collection of sets
\mathcal{W}\subseteq\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{N})
which is downward-closed under Turing reducibility.

Definition 2.1
.

A problem
\mathsf{P}

preserves
a weakness property
\mathcal{W}
if for every set
Z\in\mathcal{W}
and every
Z
-computable
\mathsf{P}
-instance
X
, there is a
\mathsf{P}
-solution
Y
to
X
such that
Y\oplus Z\in\mathcal{W}
.
Moreover,
\mathsf{P}

strongly preserves

\mathcal{W}
if the restriction to
Z
-computable
\mathsf{P}
-instances is replaced by arbitrary
\mathsf{P}
-instances.

Many basis theorems studied in reverse mathematics can be formulated as preservations for families of weakness properties.

Example 2.2
.

A problem
\mathsf{P}
admits
cone avoidance
if for every set
Z
, every set
C\not\leq_{T}Z
and every
Z
-computable
\mathsf{P}
-instance
X
, there is a
\mathsf{P}
-solution
Y
to
X
such that
C\not\leq_{T}Y\oplus Z
.
Equivalently,
\mathsf{P}
admits cone avoidance if it preserves
\mathcal{W}_{C}=\{Z:C\not\leq_{T}Z\}
for every set
C
.

The Erdős-Moser theorem
[
6
]
is a statement from graph theory about finding transitive sub-tournaments. A tournament is an oriented complete graph, and it is transitive if it contains no cycle. In our situation, it is more convenient to formulate it in terms of transitive colorings of pairs. Given a coloring
f:[\mathbb{N}]^{2}\to 2
, a set
H\subseteq\mathbb{N}
is
f
-transitive
if for every
x,y,z\in H
with
x<y<z
, if
f(x,y)=f(y,z)
, then
f(x,y)=f(x,z)
.

Statement

(Erdős-Moser theorem)
.

\mathsf{EM}
is the statement “Every coloring
f:[\mathbb{N}]^{2}\to 2
has an infinite
f
-transitive subset.”

The statement
\mathsf{EM}
was introduced in reverse mathematics by Bovykin and Weiermann
[
2
]
to provide an alternative decomposition of Ramsey’s theorem for pairs. Lerman, Solomon and Towsner
[
15
]
proved that
\mathsf{EM}
is strictly weaker than
\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{2}
over
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}
.

The following lemma is an adaptation of the fact that

is strongly computably reducible to

and that strong preservation of a weakness property is downward-closed under strong computable reducibility.

Lemma 2.3
.

If
\mathsf{EM}
strongly preserves a weakness property

, then so does
\mathsf{RT}^{1}
.

Proof.

It suffices to prove that
\mathsf{RT}^{1}_{2}
strongly preserves
\mathcal{W}
.
Let
Z\in\mathcal{W}
, and let

be an instance of
\mathsf{RT}^{1}_{2}
.
Define

by
f(x,y)=1
if and only if

.
Since
\mathsf{EM}
strongly preserves
\mathcal{W}
, there is an infinite
f
-transitive set
H
such that
H\oplus Z\in\mathcal{W}
.
We claim that

is, up to finite changes,

-homogeneous. Indeed, if there are some

∎

The following proposition essentially follows the proof by Le Houérou and Patey

followed by an application of

.

If
\mathsf{EM}
strongly preserves a weakness property

, then so does
\mathsf{BRT}^{2}_{2}

Let

Let

2.2

Constant-bound trace avoidance was introduced by Liu
[

to separate Ramsey’s theorem for pairs from weak weak König’s lemma (

\mathsf{WKL}
to trees of positive measure.

Let
\mathcal{C}\subseteq 2^{\mathbb{N}}
be an
F_{\sigma}
class. A

-trace
of

is a sequence
(F_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
such that for every
n\in\mathbb{N}

F_{n}
is a set of size
k
of strings of length

(that is, there is some

and some

such that

.

A problem
\mathsf{P}
admits

if for every set

F_{\sigma}

with no
Z
-computable c.b-trace and every
Z
-computable
\mathsf{P}
-instance
X
, there is a
\mathsf{P}
-solution
Y
to
X
such that
\mathcal{C}
has no
Y\oplus X
-computable c.b-trace.

If we consider arbitrary
\mathsf{P}
-instances instead of
Z
-computable
\mathsf{P}
-instances, then this yields
strong c.b-trace avoidance
.
Liu
[
17
]
proved that
\mathsf{RT}^{1}_{2}
admits strong c.b-trace avoidance and that
\mathsf{COH}
admits c.b-trace avoidance, and he deduced that
\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{2}
admits c.b-trace avoidance. Patey
[
19
]
proved that
\mathsf{EM}
admits strong c.b-trace avoidance.

Theorem
1.5
.

\mathsf{BRT}^{2}_{2}
admits strong c.b-trace avoidance, and
\mathsf{BRT}^{3}_{2}
admits c.b-trace avoidance.

Proof.

Immediate by

2.4
and
Corollary

2.6
and the facts that

admits c.b-trace avoidance (Liu

]
) and

admits strong c.b-trace avoidance (Patey

]
).
∎

It follows that
\mathsf{WWKL}\not\leq_{\omega}\mathsf{BRT}^{3}_{2}
, hence that

does not imply

over
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}
.

3
Effective bounds of
\mathsf{BRT}^{3}_{2}

Jockusch
[
13
]
studied the complexity of solutions to Ramsey’s theorem in the arithmetic hierarchy. More precisely, he proved that for every
n,k\geq 1
, every computable instance of
\mathsf{RT}^{n}_{k}
admits a
\Pi^{0}_{n}
solution and that for every
n\geq 2
, there is a computable instance of
\mathsf{RT}^{n}_{2}
with no
\Sigma^{0}_{n}
solution. The bounded version of Ramsey’s theorem shares the same arithmetic bounds shifted by one. That is, for every
n\geq 2
, every computable instance of
\mathsf{BRT}^{n}_{2}
admits a
\Pi^{0}_{n-1}
solution, and for every
n\geq 3
, there is a computable instance of
\mathsf{BRT}^{n}_{2}
with no
\Sigma^{0}_{n-1}
solution.

However, arithmetic bounds do not behave well with respect to iterations and constructions of
\omega
-models, in that a
\Delta^{0}_{n}
set relative to a
\Delta^{0}_{n}
set is not in general
\Delta^{0}_{n}
. This motivates the study of preservations of lowness notions.

Definition 3.1
.

A problem
\mathsf{P}
admits a
low
n
basis
if for every set
Z
and every
Z
-computable
\mathsf{P}
-instance
X
, there is a
\mathsf{P}
-solution
Y
to
X
such that
(Y\oplus Z)^{(n)}\leq_{T}Z^{(n)}
.

In particular, if a problem
\mathsf{P}
admits a low
n
basis, it admits an
\omega
-model with only low
n
sets. This provides an alternative way of separating a statement from
\mathsf{ACA}
over
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}
, since the halting set is not of low
n
degree for any
n
. In some situations, a problem is “close” to admitting a low
n
basis, in that one can decide the
\Sigma^{0}_{n}
properties of a solution, except that the whole construction must be carried out with a PA degree over
\emptyset^{(n)}
instead of only a
\emptyset^{(n)}
oracle. This yields the notion of weakly low
n
basis.

Definition 3.2
.

A problem
\mathsf{P}
admits a
weakly low
n
basis
if for every set
Z
, every set
P
of PA degree over
Z^{(n)}
and every
Z
-computable
\mathsf{P}
-instance
X
, there is a
\mathsf{P}
-solution
Y
to
X
such that
(Y\oplus Z)^{(n)}\leq_{T}P
.

The most notable example of problem admitting a weakly low basis, but no low basis, is Ramsey’s theorem for pairs. Indeed, by Jockusch
[
13
]
, there is a computable instance of
\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{2}
with no
\Sigma^{0}_{2}
(hence no low) solution, but Cholak, Jockusch and Slaman
[
3
]
proved that
\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{2}
admits a weakly low basis. By the low basis theorem, if a problem admits a weakly low
n
basis, then it admits a low
n+1
basis. Since any PA degree bounds a Scott ideal, if
\mathsf{P}
admits a weakly low
n
basis, then for every set
P
of PA degree over
\emptyset^{(n)}
, there is an
\omega
-model
\mathcal{M}
of
\mathsf{P}
such that for every
Z\in\mathcal{M}
,
Z^{(n)}\leq_{T}P
.

Wang
[
26
]
proved that
\mathsf{COH}
for
\Delta^{0}_{2}
instances admits a weakly low
2
basis, hence a low
3
basis, and Levy Patey and Mimouni
[
16
, Theorem 7.1]
proved that
\mathsf{EM}
for
\Delta^{0}_{n}
instances admits a weakly low
n
basis for
n\geq 1
. Following the relation between the Erdős-Moser theorem and
\mathsf{BRT}^{3}_{2}
developed in
Section

2
, we deduce a first effective upper bound for
\mathsf{BRT}^{3}_{2}
.

.

\mathsf{BRT}^{3}_{2}

Proof.

Let

be of PA degree over

3.1
Weakly low basis for
\Delta^{0}_{2}
cohesive
\mathsf{BRT}^{2}_{2}

The goal of this section is to prove the following theorem:

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
f:[\mathbb{N}]^{2}\to 2
be a
\Delta^{0}_{2}
instance of
\mathsf{BRT}^{2}_{2}
and
P
be of PA degree over
\emptyset^{\prime}
.
There is an infinite set
G
with
G^{\prime}\leq_{T}P
on which
f
is stable and the limit is aways 0:
\lim_{y\in G}f(x,y)=0
for all
x\in G
.

Proof.

Fix
f
, and let
k\in\mathbb{N}
be such that there is no
f
-homogeneous set for color 1 of size
k
.

f
-tree.

An
f
-tree
is a non-empty tree
T\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{<\mathbb{N}}
such that for every
\sigma\in T
,

\sigma
is an increasing sequence that is
f
-homogeneous for color 1. That is, for every
i<|\sigma|-1
,
\sigma(i)<\sigma(i+1)
, and for every
i<j<|\sigma|
,
f(\sigma(i),\sigma(j))=1
.
Given
\sigma\in T
, we write
F(T,\sigma)
for the set
\{x\in\mathbb{N}:\sigma\cdot x\in T\}
. Note that
F(T,\sigma)
is not necessarily
f
-homogeneous for color 0. Requiring
T
to be non-empty is equivalent to saying that
T
contains the node
\epsilon
of length 0. Also note that since
f
is an instance of
\mathsf{BRT}^{2}_{2,k}
, every
f
-tree has depth at most
k
. We write
T^{*}
for the sub-tree of
T
of depth at most
k-1
, that is,
T^{*}=\{\sigma\in T:|\sigma|<k-1\}
. If
T
is an infinite
f
-tree, then there is a node
\sigma\in T^{*}
such that
F(T,\sigma)
is infinite. We also write
D(T)
for the union of the range of every node, that is,
D(T)=\bigcup_{\sigma\in T}F(T,\sigma)
.

Notion of forcing.

Consider the notion of forcing whose conditions are pairs
(T,X)
, where

(1)

T\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{<\mathbb{N}}
is a finite
f
-tree;

(2)

X\subseteq\mathbb{N}
is an infinite set such that
D(T)<X
;

(3)

X
is low.

A condition
q=(S,Y)

extends

p=(T,X)
if
T\subseteq S
,
Y\subseteq X
,
D(S)\setminus D(T)\subseteq X
and finally
f(x,y)=0
whenever
\sigma\in T^{*}
,
x\in F(T,\sigma)
and
y\in F(S,\sigma)\setminus F(T,\sigma)
.
Think of a condition
p=(T,X)
as simultaneous Mathias conditions
p^{[\sigma]}=(F(T,\sigma),X)
for every
\sigma\in T^{*}
. In particular, if
q=(S,Y)
extends
p=(T,X)
, then for every
\sigma\in T^{*}
,
q^{[\sigma]}
Mathias extends
p^{[\sigma]}
. A
code
of a condition
(T,X)
is a pair
(T,e)
such that
e
is a lowness index for
X
, that is,
\Phi_{e}^{\emptyset^{\prime}}=X^{\prime}
.

Any decreasing sequence of conditions
p_{0}\geq p_{1}\geq\cdots
with
p_{s}=(T_{s},X_{s})
induces an
f
-tree
T=\bigcup_{s}T_{s}
, and every
\sigma\in T^{*}
induces a set
G_{\sigma}=F(T,\sigma)=\bigcup_{s}F(T_{s},\sigma)
. As mentioned, if
T
is infinite, then there is some
\sigma\in T^{*}
such that
G_{\sigma}
is infinite.
Given
s\in\mathbb{N}
and
\sigma\in T_{s}^{*}
,
F(T_{s},\sigma)
is not necessarily
f
-homogeneous for color 0, and so neither is
G_{\sigma}
, but by the definition of condition extension, for every
x\in F(T_{s},\sigma)
and
y\in G_{\sigma}\setminus F(T_{s},\sigma)
,

. It follows that every element in
G_{\sigma}
will have limit color 0 in
G_{\sigma}
.

Forcing
\Sigma^{0}_{1}
and
\Pi^{0}_{1}

Let
p=(T,X)
be a condition,
\varphi(G)
be a
\Sigma^{0}_{1}
-formula and
\sigma\in T^{*}
. We shall always assume that
\varphi(G)
is in normal form and induces a monotone formula
\varphi(\rho)
on strings such that
\varphi(G)
holds if and only if there is some
\rho\prec G
such that
\varphi(\rho)
holds.

(1)

p\Vdash\varphi(G_{\sigma})
if
\varphi(F(T,\sigma))
holds.

(2)

p\Vdash\neg\varphi(G_{\sigma})
if for every finite set
H\subseteq X
,
\varphi(F(T,\sigma)\cup H)
does not hold.

We say that
p

decides

\varphi(G_{\sigma})
if
p\Vdash\varphi(G_{\sigma})
or
p\Vdash\neg\varphi(G_{\sigma})
. Note that the forcing relation for
\Pi^{0}_{1}
-formulas is stronger than the semantic one and yields the following lemma.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
p_{0}\geq p_{1}\geq\cdots
be a decreasing sequence of conditions with
p_{s}=(T_{s},X_{s})
.
Let
T=\bigcup_{s}T_{s}
and
\sigma\in T^{*}
. If for every
\Sigma^{0}_{1}
-formula
\varphi(G)
there is some
s
such that
p_{s}
decides
\varphi(G_{\sigma})
, then
G_{\sigma}

Proof.

The goal of this section is to prove the following theorem:

4
Preserving multiple hyperimmunities

We now turn to proofs of separations for
\mathsf{BRT}^{3}_{2}
from
\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{2}
. Although the separation over
\omega
-models remains open, we prove that any fixed number of applications of
\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{2}
is not sufficient to solve
\mathsf{BRT}^{3}_{2}
. More precisely, this section is devoted to the proof of the following theorem:

Theorem
1.7
.

For every
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
\mathsf{BRT}^{3}_{2,4}\not\leq_{\omega}^{k}\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{2}
.

Note that if
\mathsf{BRT}^{3}_{2}\leq_{\omega}\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{2}
, then
Theorem

1.7
cannot be obtained by proving that
\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{2}
preserves some weakness property in the sense of
Definition

2.1
. Indeed, such a notion of preservation can be iterated and would yield a separation with respect to
\omega
-reducibility. Also note that
Theorem

1.7
implies that
\mathsf{BRT}^{3}_{2,4}\not\leq_{\omega}^{k}\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{\ell}
for every

, as a simple color-merging argument shows that
\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{\ell}\leq_{\omega}^{\ell-1}\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{2}
.

We shall use the framework of preservation of hyperimmunities, which was already shown to be fruitful for separating
\mathsf{RT}^{n}_{k}
from
\mathsf{RT}^{n}_{k+1}
with respect to computable reducibility, while those two statements are equivalent over
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}
.

A function

is
Z
-hyperimmune
if it is not dominated by any
Z
-computable function. When
Z=\emptyset
, we shall simply say
hyperimmune
. As many computability-theoretic notions, this induces multiple notions of preservation, as follows:

Definition 4.1
.

Fix

.
A problem
\mathsf{P}
admits
preservation of
k
hyperimmunities
if for every set

,
every sequence
\langle g_{s}:s<k\rangle
of
Z
-hyperimmune functions and every
Z
-computable
\mathsf{P}
-instance

, there is a
\mathsf{P}
-solution
Y
to
X
such that all the
g_{s}
are
(Y\oplus Z)
-hyperimmune.

Downey, Greenberg, Harrison-Trainor, Patey and Turetsky
[
4
]
proved that preservation of 1 hyperimmunity is equivalent to cone avoidance, which is itself equivalent to simultaneous avoidance of countably many cones. On the other hand, preservation of
k
hyperimmunities forms a strictly increasing hierarchy when
k
increases. We shall actually work with a twisted notion of preservation of
k
hyperimmunities in which only
\ell
of the
k
hyperimmune functions are required to be preserved.

Definition 4.2
.

Fix
\ell,k\geq 1
.
A problem
\mathsf{P}
admits
preservation of
\ell
among
k

if for every set

,
every sequence
\langle g_{s}:s<k\rangle
of
Z
-hyperimmune functions and every
Z
-computable
\mathsf{P}
-instance

,

\mathsf{P}
-solution

to

and some

of size

,

is

-hyperimmune.

This is not a notion of preservation in the sense of

2.1
, but it can be partially iterated as follows: if
\mathsf{P}
admits preservation of

among

hyperimmunities and of

among
k_{1}
hyperimmunities, then intuitively, two consecutive applications of

preserve

among

hyperimmunities.
Patey
[

]
proved that for every

,

admits preservation of

that are already edges.
\Gamma

Again suppose we have defined a triangle-free

for some

•

If

.

•

Let

•

•

.

. Let
