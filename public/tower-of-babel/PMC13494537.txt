IAN, an intelligent system for omics data analysis and discovery
Authors: Vijayaraj Nagarajan, Reiko Horai, Guangpu Shi, Cheng-Rong Yu, Jaanam Gopalakrishnan, Manoj Kumar Yadav, Michael H. Liew, Calla Gentilucci, Rachel R. Caspi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryIAN is an R package addressing the challenge of integrating, analyzing, and interpreting gene expression data using an artificial intelligence (AI) system. It leverages popular pathway (KEGG, WikiPathways, Reactome, and GO) and regulatory datasets (ChEA), along with STRING for protein-protein interactions, to perform standard enrichment analysis. A multi-agent architecture uses individual enrichment results to generate summaries using large language models (LLMs). These summaries are contextually integrated and interpreted by the LLM, guided by engineered prompts and grounding instructions, to provide useful explanations, system overviews, key regulators, and data-driven insights. We demonstrate IAN’s function on published human transcriptomic data, evaluate IAN’s robustness to underlying LLM choice and report human expert evaluations of the clarity and relevance of IAN’s outputs. The IAN package is available at https://github.com/NIH-NEI/IAN.

Introduction

“Omics”-scale technologies like genomics, transcriptomics, proteomics, and metabolomics are helping researchers capture the whole of the biological system state at any given point of interest. The common workflow in most of these studies involves generating system-level molecular data between different conditions and then identifying molecules that are significantly differentially behaving between the conditions under study. This often results in a list of differentially behaving molecules (DEGs—differentially expressed genes, for example in transcriptome studies). In a human system, this list usually contains at least a couple of thousands of genes, making it challenging to integrate it with other relevant data and study it as a system.1,2 Since it is impossible to study and understand the relationship of each of those DEGs with respect to the phenotype that is being studied, researchers often perform enrichment analysis to get a bird’s-eye-view idea on the system.

With numerous enrichment analysis tools and methods available, along with hundreds of gene sets to compare against, researchers have shown that current methods each possess limitations and can introduce biases.3 Without tools that enable a systems-level understanding, researchers frequently focus on a limited number of top DEGs or transcripts to propose disease mechanisms and identify targets. Performing enrichment analysis across a multitude of resources—representing various biological system components like biochemical pathways, transcriptional factors, non-coding RNA targets, known disease-target associations, and drug-target interactions—and integrating them to obtain biological insights remains a challenging task.

Several tools have been proposed to address this challenge, such as the DAVID enrichment analysis platform,4 which integrates multiple gene annotation and knowledge sources using a clustering approach. Other popular tools include GSEA5 (using hundreds of signatures from MSigDB)6 and EnrichR.7 Expanding on these, platforms like EnrichmentMap,6 Enrichr-KG,8 Metascape,9 and multiGSEA10 allow for the integration of multiple data sources and network visualization of enrichment results; however, they are generally limited to simple term similarity and known associations rather than a genuine, synthesized understanding. Similarly, emerging LLM-integrated tools such as GeneAgent,11 CellAgent,12 and others like RefAI, Valsci, LINS and SCassist13,14,15,16 offer summarization and advanced biomedical literature synthesis capabilities, but the potential remains for tools that can better synthesize insights directly from “omics”-scale data.

In the past couple of years, LLMs have dramatically advanced the field of generative applications. In general, LLMs have significantly advanced the field of text generation, text summarization, translation, question answering, image generation, audio/video generation, code generation, etc.17 Numerous LLM-based applications are also being explored in the field of biomedical research and clinical settings, for their potential contributions toward drug design, drug discovery, sequence analysis, target discovery, clinical diagnosis, treatment recommendations, outcome predictions, etc.18,19,20 The problem of hallucination is a major concern with LLMs use in the field of text generation.21 Researchers have proven that hallucination is an inevitable limitation of LLMs, but are working toward identifying and using approaches, methods and tools that could be used to mitigate hallucination and improve LLMs capabilities.22 A recent study evaluated the use of LLMs for gene set enrichment and found that they were unsuitable as a replacement for standard enrichment analysis.23 Other studies have also shown that LLMs are inferior in their ability to generate standards-based scientific abstracts and scientific reports.24,25 Although standalone large language models (LLMs) often struggle with the precision required for scientific literature summarization, the integration of retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) has enabled state-of-the-art performance by grounding model outputs in verified, external evidence.26 Previous studies have also found that hallucinations are rare to non-existent when summarizing gene sets through LLMs,23 suggesting that with appropriate grounding, LLMs can be effective for specific biological tasks.

In this work, we have developed IAN, a multi-agent AI system for transcriptomics data analysis and discovery, implemented as an open-source R package. We demonstrate integration and interpretation of transcriptomic data as well as reproducibility of results across different LLMs.

Results

Overview of IAN architecture and system design

IAN was engineered to bridge the gap between data generation and biological interpretation. The core architecture integrates standard bioinformatics processing pipelines with a LLM framework. To ensure outputs are biologically grounded, IAN systematically cross-references user-input data with established biological databases—including Gene Ontology (GO), KEGG, Reactome, and the STRING database—to gather comprehensive functional annotation and protein-protein interaction data.

These curated database outputs, alongside the primary input data, are ingested by the LLM component. Guided by domain-specific engineered prompts and grounding instructions, IAN synthesizes this multi-layered information. The system’s architecture (Figure 1) is specifically designed to transform disparate lists of genes and pathways into cohesive biological narratives, highlighting key regulatory nodes and data-driven gene-level associations. This integrated design provides researchers with an intuitive, system-level overview of their datasets, streamlining the transition from data analysis to hypothesis generation.Figure 1End-to-end architecture and multi-agent workflow of the IAN systemThe schematic illustrates the data processing and AI integration pipeline. IAN’s HTML report starts with the LLM generated title, a high-level summary of the entire report, affected processes, potential regulatory networks, hub genes, similar systems, novelty exploration, system model overview, mechanistic model, and hypothesis. These high-level results sections are followed by individual reports and summaries for each of the agents.

The LLM-driven analysis of uveitis pathogenesis by IAN proposes the involvement of immune dysregulation, ECM remodeling, and potential neuronal/infectious involvement, with FN1 as a key hub. The analysis proposes RELB, SMAD4, and ATF3 as key transcription factors orchestrating these processes. While IAN’s analysis identified several potential important mediators of uveitis pathogenesis, this discussion focuses specifically on the role of RELB, a key transcription factor implicated in immune regulation. Further investigation into the other hub genes and regulatory networks identified by IAN, such as FN1, CD274, SMAD4, ATF3, and the complement cascade, represents a promising avenue for future research to fully elucidate the complex mechanisms underlying uveitis.

IAN implicates RELB as a key regulator of genes involved in interferon signaling and complement activation, orchestrating the uveitis phenotype. Although there is no known literature that has reported any direct role for RELB in uveitis, there are studies that indirectly support this inference, due to its general involvement in immune processes. For example, Tu et al.27 explored a recently described TTP-RELB regulatory network for innate immunity gene expression, and Gupta et al.28 showed that RELB controls adaptive responses of astrocytes during sterile inflammation. As summarized by Barnabei et al.29 accumulated evidence highlights RELB’s role in various immune cell types and its implications for autoimmunity and inflammation. Their synthesis notes that documented mutations in NF-κB pathway components, including RELB, have deepened our understanding of autoimmune disease. Furthermore, the presence of NFKBIB, IKBKG, and IKBKE genes in the uveitis gene expression profile established by the Nussenblatt’s laboratory (Li et al.30), provides indirect support for the involvement of NF-κB signaling pathways. Given its role in regulating inflammatory responses in other tissues, as highlighted by Elssner et al.31 showing increased RELB translocation in diseased human liver, it is plausible that RELB contributes to the chronic inflammation and tissue damage observed in uveitis.

Evaluation

Groundedness score

To measure IAN’s susceptibility to hallucination, we calculated a groundedness score, defined as the percentage of LLM-reported genes, pathways, and metrics that are verifiably present in the provided input data. IAN scored a perfect 100% on the groundedness score, for a total of 7,942 input tokens that we used to evaluate both the UV and BD datasets. The evaluated token categories included network property scores, all responses genes, final response genes, integrated network genes, system model genes, WikiPathway IDs, KEGG pathway IDs, and Reactome pathway IDs. This groundedness metric suggests the reliability of IAN in generating insights that are directly supported by the provided input data. Semantic similarity

To assess the contextual relevance of IAN’s narratives, we calculated a semantic similarity score using a bidirectional encoder representations from transformers (BERT)-based language model. This score calculates the cosine similarity (on a scale of 0–1) between the LLM-generated summaries and the original input data, such as enriched pathways and GO terms. Semantic similarity measured between the terms contained in all of the input contents and different responses from IAN showed a high degree of similarity across different comparisons, with an average score of 0.75. The strong similarity score demonstrates that IAN effectively captures and reflects the content of the input terms in its generated responses. Expert human evaluation

To assess the quality and utility of IAN-generated reports, eight domain experts (four senior staff scientists and four junior research fellows) evaluated the AI outputs on a 5-point Likert scale across five key metrics. The evaluation revealed strong performance (Table S2), with a mean accuracy of 4.75 (out of 5), high relevance (mean 4.50), excellent clarity (mean 4.63), and strong overall satisfaction (mean 4.75). While trustworthiness scored slightly lower (mean 4.25), these results collectively suggest that IAN’s AI-driven approach generates results that are not only accurate and relevant but also understandable and generally trusted by human experts.

To determine whether evaluation scores varied based on the evaluator’s experience level or the specific dataset analyzed, we performed Wilcoxon rank-sum tests comparing scores between these groups (senior vs. junior evaluators and UV vs. BD datasets) (Table S3). These tests yielded non-significant (high) p values, indicating no statistical difference between the groups. Furthermore, the calculated effect sizes—which measure the magnitude of the difference between groups—were relatively small, confirming that any variations were negligible. This demonstrates that IAN’s results are reliably similar irrespective of who evaluated the reports and what dataset was used. We did note that while the difference in trustworthiness scores between the senior and junior groups was not statistically significant, an effect size of 0.5 suggests a potentially moderate difference in how much these two demographics trust AI-generated reports. Given the importance of trustworthiness in the field of AI and biomedical research, we suggest that a large-scale study to further assess any disparities between senior and junior populations could be helpful to increase the adoption of the platform across researcher age groups.

Finally, a Wilcoxon signed-rank test comparing all assigned scores against a neutral baseline (a score of 3 on the 5-point scale) revealed a statistically significant skew toward positive ratings (p value = 0.0001264), indicating that IAN’s reports consistently scored highly across both datasets, in both expert levels, and across metrics.

LLM consistency evaluation

We sought to test whether IAN’s performance would remain consistent when different LLMs are used for inference. To do this, we implemented IAN with different LLMs on our two transcriptomic datasets and then computed the groundedness metric by comparing the genes and combined network property scores reported in IAN’s “All Agents Responses” against the ground truth derived from the input data (see detailed calculation procedures in STAR Methods). This analysis revealed high groundedness scores across most LLMs, averaging 99% for genes (Table S4) and 72% for network properties (Table S5). Specifically, Gemini 1.5 Flash, Gemini 2 Flash Lite, GPT-4o Mini, Llama 2, Llama 3, and Llama 4 achieved close to 100% groundedness scores for genes. Minor deviations observed in specific GPT and Gemini versions do however suggest the potential impact of model selection and versioning. Our evaluation of contextual relevance—measured via the BERT-based semantic similarity score between the generated summaries and the original input data—showed a high average score of 0.82 for IAN’s outputs across all LLMs when processing the UV dataset (Table S6).The consistent semantic similarity across different LLMs further supports the effectiveness of IAN’s grounding strategy in producing coherent and contextually relevant interpretations.

Overall, these comparative evaluations suggest consistency of IAN’s results across underlying LLMs. We hypothesize that this robustness is aided by our grounding methodology. Detailed metrics and analysis are provided in Tables 1 and 2; Tables S2 and S3 and further elaborated in the “methods” section.

Discovery benchmark

Our evaluation using the grounded discovery bench (GDB) confirms IAN’s performance in higher-order reasoning and biological interpretation of transcriptomics data. When compared against baseline, unaugmented general-purpose LLMs (Claude, ChatGPT, and Gemini), IAN achieved the highest “Grounded Discovery Score” (GDS) (Table S10). While baseline tools—whether prompted with DEGs alone or DEGs combined with experimental context—show varying factual recall (“Fidelity Score”), IAN’s integrated approach leveraging comprehensive data integration via our engineered augmentation strategy performed well in the critical “Discovery & Synthesis Score.” This balanced strength in both strict data adherence and advanced reasoning positions IAN in the “High Fidelity, High Discovery” performance quadrant (Figure 3), suggesting its potential for verifiable biological discovery.Figure 3Performance profile of IAN and baseline LLMs on the GDBQuadrant plot illustrating the relationship between the Fidelity Score (x axis, measuring factual recall and data adherence) and the Discovery & Synthesis Score (y axis, measuring higher-order reasoning and hypothesis generation). Cost

During our development process, we made 1,306 API calls to Google Gemini, spread across 30 days, costing us a pre-tax total of $3.48. We choose Google Gemini for the large context window as well as the low-cost pay-as-you-go model.

Discussion

In this work, we developed IAN, a multi-agent AI system to analyze transcriptomics data, combining user data with tools output and appropriate data metrics. By analyzing and evaluating IAN’s performance on two published datasets, we demonstrate that IAN is capable of generating results that are grounded in the user provided data and are conceptually similar to the content provided as input. Our evaluation across multiple LLMs further support the robustness of IAN’s outputs irrespective of the underlying LLM. This consistency is crucial for the adoption of AI-driven tools in complex biological research, providing researchers with a dependable system for generating hypotheses. The rapid emergence of autonomous AI agent frameworks highlights a growing shift toward agent-based scientific workflows. In this context, IAN is designed with a strong emphasis on biologically grounded analysis, integrating curated omics resources within a structured framework for enrichment, regulatory interpretation, and network-based system modeling. While distinct from open-ended agent systems, future iterations of IAN may extend to interoperate with broader agent ecosystems while preserving its emphasis on domain-specific rigor and reproducibility.

Limitations of the study

IAN currently works only for human and mouse datasets, with ENSEMBL or ENTREZID or SYMBOLS, focusing on transcriptomics. We plan to include other model organisms and other “omics” technologies like proteomics and metabolomics in our future version. Current version supports KEGG, WikiPathways, Reactome pathways, GO, ChEA, and STRING data. Future version would include other data sources and relevant computed metrics. While users can run IAN separately for up- or down-regulated genes, using the current version, future version would include options to specify and use the up/down information, along with the DEG. Current version of IAN is optimized for Google Gemini, Gemini 1.5 Flash latest model. Future versions would include other proprietary models, as well as opensource models like Llama. We plan to implement several other features in the future versions of IAN, including multi-dataset comparison, multi-omics analysis, autopiloting (LLM-driven choice of tools, parameters, and automated workflow design), custom user “notes” for the agents to focus on certain aspects and new analysis concepts in the summary report.

Method details

Architecture

IAN is implemented as an R package, integrating diverse data sources and analytical tools to facilitate systems-level biological discovery. IAN takes gene expression data (custom DEG lists, DESeq2 results and Seurat FindMarkers output) and performs enrichment analysis using clusterProfiler, ReactomePA, enrichR, and STRINGdb, generating a suite of pathway and network-based data/metrics. A multi-agent system then leverages these data/metrics, with each agent employing carefully crafted analysis instructions, as LLM prompts to summarize, categorize, and interpret specific aspects of the analysis. The resulting agent responses, combined with experimental design information, are used to construct a comprehensive augmented prompt (a comprehensive list of detailed prompt templates is provided at the project GitHub page - https://github.com/NIH-NEI/IAN/blob/main/inst/docs/ian_prompt_templates.txt. An example prompt template is provided in the Methods S1), which is then employed for an integrated final LLM response. The integrated response is also used to generate an LLM derived system model. All the results and responses are parsed and presented, along with system insights, in a comprehensive HTML report. The IAN package was developed using R 4.4.1 on macOS Ventura platform. An overview of IAN’s system architecture is presented in Figure 1. A detailed procedural workflow is provided in the Figure S1.

Pathway enrichment analysis

To identify pathways involving the list of DEGs, IAN performs analysis using established R packages and data sources like clusterProfiler’s35 KEGG pathway enrichment, WikiPathways enrichment and Gene ontology enrichment, Reactome pathway enrichment36 and enrichR’s ChEA transcription factor enrichment.37 These resources were chosen as a robust proof of concept due to their broad coverage and wide adoption in biological interpretation. Standard parameters, including a pvalueCutoff of 0.05, Benjamini-Hochberg multiple testing correction (pAdjustMethod = “BH”), and a qvalueCutoff of 0.2, are applied by default, with background gene sets defined using all annotated genes for each respective database (or all detected genes for GO). All specific versions, parameters, and database details are comprehensively documented in Table S8 and the project’s GitHub repository for full reproducibility.

User provided DEGs are first mapped to ENTREZ IDs and Gene symbols, using clusterProfiler’s bitr function. The ENTREZ IDs are used as input for GO (Biological Process), KEGG, Reactome, and WikiPathways enrichment analysis. Gene symbols are used as input for ChEA. A p-value threshold of 0.05 is applied to filter the enrichment results for the top enriched terms. All enrichment results are further processed to map gene IDs to gene symbols and include only the enriched terms, enrichment scores and gene symbols for further processing. The processed enrichment results are also compared against each other to identify overlapping and unique genes, to provide additional input for the LLMs.

Network properties

For the given list of DEGs, IAN extracts all known interactions from the STRING database.34 The STRING interaction analysis is configured by default to include the top 1000 interactions, sorted by combined score (with a score_threshold = 0), a pragmatic choice balancing the potential discovery of putative associations with the practical constraints of LLM integration, a parameter user-configurable within the IAN package. This extracted data is processed to remove duplicate interactions, convert STRING IDs to gene symbols, and retain only Protein1, Protein2, and Combined Score columns. The interactions are then sorted based on combined_score, and the top 1000 are kept for further analysis.

To identify key nodes within the protein-protein interaction network, IAN calculates several network properties for each gene, including degree (number of connections), betweenness (influence on network paths), closeness (proximity to other nodes), and centrality (connection to other influential nodes), using the igraph package.38 To integrate these diverse measures, each property is scaled to a mean of zero and standard deviation of one, ensuring equal contribution across different properties. A combined_properties_score is then calculated for each node by averaging its scaled property values, providing a holistic measure of network importance. Nodes are ranked based on the combined_properties_score, with higher scores indicating greater overall importance and influence within the system being studied.

Multi-agent system

The R6 class system is used to implement IAN’s multi-agent core, creating the modular and reusable agent objects. Each agent (one for each of KEGG, WikiPathways, Reactome, GO, ChEA, STRING) is initialized with a specific LLM prompt and a corresponding prompt type, representing a distinct analytical task. The Environment class manages these six agents, orchestrating their execution using the future packages for parallel processing. The run_agents method distributes the prompts to the agents, retrieves responses from the LLM via a user-defined function, and handles retries and error conditions. The resulting agent responses are then aggregated and used to construct a final, combined prompt for a comprehensive system-level analysis.

Large language model

IAN is optimized for Google’s “gemini-1.5-flash-latest” LLM, with a default temperature of 0 and the maximum token size of 8192. A five second delay is introduced with a maximum of three retries, to stay within the server overload limits. The user is expected to provide an API key to run IAN. We choose Google’s LLM server for their large context window availability. Future versions of IAN will also incorporate support for other proprietary and open-source LLMs, including GPT and Llama models, to provide users with greater flexibility.

Report

Based on results and important concepts identified throughout the analysis, IAN generates a dynamic and comprehensive HTML report which is easy to share and user friendly to navigate. Tables of processed data, LLM summaries, plots and network graphs are included in the report, along with links to download all original results, processed results and full LLM reports.

Dependencies

IAN depends on Internet connectivity to run enrichment analysis and call the LLM. Critical R package dependencies include dplyr for data manipulation, clusterProfiler, ReactomePA, and enrichR for enrichment analyses, STRINGdb for protein-protein interaction data, and igraph and visNetwork for network analysis and visualization. Communication with the LLM is facilitated by httr, while parallel processing is enabled through future. Report generation leverages rmarkdown, and the overall architecture is built upon the R6 class system. IAN can be installed and run on any operating system using R 4.4.1 and above.

Evaluations

Dataset

We used two published RNA-Seq based transcriptomics datasets for evaluating IAN’s performance. The first study,32 provided a custom DEG list, generated by comparing uveitis patient transcriptomes against healthy controls. The second study33 provided raw RNA-Seq transcriptomics data generated by comparing Behcet’s disease (BD) patients and healthy controls. The BD RNA-Seq data was downloaded from NCBI GEO (GSE198533) and processed using the standard DESeq239 method.

Groundedness score

To assess the IAN’s ability to use the provided content in generating its responses, we calculated a groundedness score. We used this score as an indirect measure of hallucination by quantifying the extent to which the LLM’s output represents the provided input data. As the ground truth, we used the user provided list of differentially expressed genes and the significant terms identified from enrichment analysis. We then calculated the groundedness score (G) as the cardinality of the intersection between the ground truth (GT) and the genes/terms reported in the LLM’s response (LLM), divided by the cardinality of the genes/terms reported in the LLM’s response: G = |GT ∩ LLM|/|LLM|. This metric provides a quantitative assessment of the LLM’s ability to synthesize and integrate information from diverse sources, while still grounded in the provided content.

Contextual relevance

To evaluate the contextual relevance of the LLM’s responses in IAN, we measured the semantic similarity between the LLM-generated summaries and the input data containing enriched pathways and GO terms. We employed the transformer-based language model BERT (Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers) to compare the LLM’s responses to the input data. Briefly, the text was tokenized, augmented with special tokens to delineate content boundaries, and chunked to accommodate BERT’s input length limitations. These chunks were then converted into input IDs, and relevant embeddings were extracted. The resulting chunked embeddings were combined and used to compute the cosine similarity score. This semantic similarity analysis was performed in a Python virtual environment using the transformers, torch, and sklearn.metrics.pairwise packages. The fully documented Python script used for this analysis is available on our project’s GitHub repository.

Human evaluation

To assess the quality and utility of the IAN-generated reports, we conducted a human evaluation comparing them against the standard manually curated analyses. We employed a Likert scale (1–5) to evaluate key aspects of the reports, including Accuracy, Relevance, Clarity, Trustworthiness, and Overall Satisfaction. To minimize ambiguity and ensure consistent scoring, we designed a scoring sheet with clear anchors and detailed descriptions for each score level. A panel of expert evaluators, comprising both senior scientists (four staff scientists) and junior researchers (four research fellows) from different laboratory sections assessed the reports based on the defined criteria. The template scoring sheet is provided as supplementary material (Table S1).

LLM consistency evaluation

To rigorously assess the consistency and reproducibility of IAN’s LLM-driven insights, we conducted a comparative analysis across multiple large language models and their versions (Table S7). This evaluation was designed to address concerns regarding LLM variability and to demonstrate the robustness of our grounding methodology. We compared the outputs generated by IAN using three prominent LLMs: Google’s Gemini (specifically, Gemini 1.5 Flash, Gemini 2 Flash Lite, and Gemini 2.5 Flash Preview), OpenAI’s GPT (GPT-4o Mini and GPT-4.1 Nano), and Meta’s Llama (Llama 2, Llama 3, and Llama 4). For each LLM, we evaluated IAN’s performance on the uveitis (UV) dataset, focusing on two key aspects: the groundedness of the reported genes and the semantic similarity of the generated text. Crucially, identical prompts, parameters, and input data were used for all LLM runs to ensure a fair comparison.

Groundedness was quantified by comparing the genes and combined network property scores, reported in IAN’s ‘All Agents Responses' outputs against the ground truth gene lists and network property scores derived from the input data. We calculated a groundedness score as the ratio of correctly reported genes/scores to the total reported genes/scores by each LLM.

Semantic similarity was measured using BERT-based cosine similarity to compare the LLM-generated summaries against the original input data (enriched pathways and GO terms).

Benchmarking

To rigorously evaluate IAN’s ability to perform higher-order reasoning and synthesize ‘Omics' data into biologically meaningful insights, we benchmarked its performance against standard, unaugmented LLM baselines using the Grounded Discovery Bench (GDB) framework. GDB is an open-source benchmark designed to evaluate AI tools on their capacity for structured, verifiable, and data-driven biological interpretation. A comprehensive methodological description of the full GDB framework is currently in preparation for a separate, dedicated publication. Preliminary details are available at the project GitHub page at https://github.com/NIH-NEI/gdb.

Briefly, GDB assesses tools across 12 distinct tasks—ranging from hub gene identification and pathway enrichment fidelity to hypothesis generation and system model reconstruction—utilizing 8 diverse, publicly available human ‘Omics' datasets. Ground truth for these datasets was strictly established and curated from the original peer-reviewed publications.

For the purpose of evaluating IAN in this study, we compared its performance against standard versions of general-purpose LLMs (Claude, ChatGPT, and Gemini) (Table S10; Figure 3). To establish rigorous baselines, these models were evaluated using zero-shot prompts under two configurations: providing only the Differentially Expressed Genes (“DEG Only”) and providing DEGs along with the experimental design context (“DEG + Exp”). Default API parameters (e.g., temperature = 0) were used to ensure deterministic outputs.

The GDB methodology involves standardizing tool outputs, validating inputs, and performing task-by-task scoring against the curated ground truth. The scoring comprises two main axes: a ‘Fidelity Score' (assessing factual recall and absence of hallucination via exact match, F1-score, and Jaccard overlap with ground truth) and a ‘Discovery & Synthesis Score' (evaluating higher-order reasoning via BERT-based cosine similarity to expert-curated hypotheses and normalized discounted cumulative gain (NDCG)). The final ‘Grounded Discovery Score' represents a weighted average of these task-specific metrics, prioritizing structured and verifiable reasoning. All benchmark data, scripts for data preparation and scoring, baseline tool outputs, and detailed ground truth information are publicly available on the GDB GitHub repository (https://github.com/NIH-NEI/gdb).

Metrics for evaluating IAN output quality

Detailed definitions, metrics, and baselines used to evaluate the quality, consistency, and hallucination avoidance of IAN’s generated biological insights are provided here.1.Consistency metrics

1.Consistency metrics

Consistency metrics

Definition: Consistency refers to the coherence and logical alignment of IAN’s outputs across its different analytical components (agents, databases, LLM-generated summaries) and with the provided input ‘Omics' data and experimental context. High consistency ensures a unified and reliable biological narrative.

Metrics.

Inter-agent concordance (LLM-based)

Description: IAN uses an LLM to compare results from different agents for concordance before full integration. This process aims to identify overarching themes and potential regulatory relationships as interpreted by the LLM.

Method: LLM-based comparison of identified pathways, TFs, and key genes across agent outputs to assess thematic alignment.

Cross-Database consistency (against Ground Truth):

Description: Assesses the extent to which similar biological themes or pathways are identified across different enrichment databases and how well these align with the ground truth enrichment results from raw analysis. High concordance indicates robust findings.

Method: Comparison of enriched terms and pathways identified by different databases and LLM interpretations against the ground truth using BERT-based Cosine similarity for semantic meaning.

Grounded Discovery Benchmark (GDB) tasks contributing to consistency:

Task 3 (Enrichment categorization): Measures the ability to group related terms into coherent themes, reflecting consistent interpretation. (Metric: Composite Jaccard Similarity).

Task 5 (Biological process synthesis): Evaluates the coherence and accuracy of the overall narrative summary against ground truth. (Metric: Cosine Similarity).

Task 10 (System model reconstruction): Tests the ability to group genes into functional modules, reflecting consistent structural understanding against ground truth. (Metric: Composite Jaccard Similarity).2.Hallucination avoidance metrics

2.Hallucination avoidance metrics

Hallucination avoidance metrics

Definition: Hallucination avoidance refers to the ability of IAN to generate outputs that are factually accurate, directly supported by the input ‘Omics’ data and experimental context, and traceable to verifiable biological knowledge, thereby minimizing the generation of fabricated or unsubstantiated claims. This is fundamentally achieved through IAN’s traceability and grounding mechanism.

Metrics:

GDB “Fidelity Score”:

Description: Directly assesses how well the tool’s outputs align with the factual content of the source publication, indicating the degree to which claims are supported by evidence and thus minimizing fabricated information.

Metric: Measured via various GDB tasks including:

Task 1 (Hub gene identification): Normalized Discounted Cumulative Gain (NDCG).

Task 2 (Enrichment analysis fidelity): Jaccard Similarity and Semantic Similarity (Cosine Similarity).

Task 4 (Regulatory network edge discovery): Mean Reciprocal Rank (MRR).

Task 11 (Hub gene annotation): Average F1 Score.

GDB “Grounded Reasoning Score”:

Description: A composite score that penalizes unsubstantiated claims and rewards outputs grounded in data and verifiable insights.

Metric: Weighted average of task scores, prioritizing verifiable reasoning.

IAN’s Internal “Groundedness Score”:

Description: Quantifies how much of the LLM’s output (genes, terms, network properties) is directly represented in the provided input data (DEGs, significant enrichment terms, etc.). A higher score indicates better adherence to the input data.

Metric: Calculated as G = |GT ∩ LLM|/|LLM|, for genes/terms and specific scores like Network Properties Score. (See Table 1 for examples from UV and BD datasets, showing 100% groundedness for IAN across various output components).

Contextual relevance (BERT-based Cosine Similarity):

Description: Measures the semantic similarity between LLM-generated summaries and the input data (enriched pathways, GO terms).

Metric: BERT Similarity Score (Cosine Similarity on BERT embeddings).

Values: See Table 2 (UV: 0.71 avg; BD: 0.79 avg).

Expert review (Existing & Planned):

Description: Direct assessment by domain experts of IAN-generated hypotheses and mechanistic models.

Metrics: We have already conducted an initial human evaluation using a Likert scale (1–5) for Accuracy, Relevance, Clarity, Trustworthiness, and Overall satisfaction, comparing IAN’s reports based on human subject domain experts knowledge. Statistical analysis (Wilcoxon Signed-Rank, Wilcoxon Rank-Sum tests) confirms robustness. (See Table S1 for scoring sheet; Table S2 for summary stats; Figure S1 for frequency distributions; Table S3 for statistical tests).

Planned: Explicit quantification of hallucination rates and further expert validation, to be integrated into the GDB framework.

Baselines:

Recall-focused LLM Baselines (from GDB): LLMs prompted only with DEGs show poor recall and likely higher hallucination risk. LLMs with DEGs + experimental design shows improved recall but lag IAN in discovery. This highlights that while recall is important, IAN’s balanced approach avoids the pitfalls of pure recall-based generation, preventing speculative claims by grounding them in data while still facilitating data-driven insights.3.Rigor of evaluation framework (GDB)

Planned Expansion of GDB for Expert Evaluation

Recognizing the irreplaceable value of human expertise in scientific interpretation, we are currently working to formally expand the GDB framework to incorporate domain expert evaluation. This initiative will involve developing standardized protocols for expert review of AI-generated insights, allowing for quantitative assessment of aspects such as biological plausibility, discovery value, and clinical relevance, thereby further enhancing the benchmark’s evaluative capabilities.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed in R (v4.4.1). Eight evaluators (four senior staff scientists and four junior research fellows) scored IAN-generated reports using a Likert scale (1–5) across five metrics. A one-sample Wilcoxon Signed-Rank test was used to compare the assigned expert scores against a neutral baseline (a score of 3 on the 5-point Likert scale) to assess whether ratings were significantly skewed toward positive evaluations. Additionally, Wilcoxon Rank-Sum tests evaluated differences by evaluator expertise (Senior vs. Junior) and dataset (UV vs. BD). Non-parametric tests were employed; tied values in some comparisons yielded approximate p-values, which did not materially affect conclusions. Summary statistics, frequency distributions, and full test results are reported in Tables S2 and S3 and Figure S1. Groundedness and BERT-based semantic similarity scores are reported as ratios and averages, with full details in Tables 1 and 2; Tables S2 and S3. Significance was defined as p < 0.05. The evaluation panel comprised all available expert evaluators; no randomization, sample size estimation, or data exclusion was performed.
