DNA-damage-targeting copper nanoparticles induce cuproptosis for enhanced cancer radiotherapy
Authors: Yuetong Chen, Zhiping Liu, Ruohong Bi, Xiaofei Liu, Jufang Wang, Jinpeng He, Yi Lyu, Suxia Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRadiotherapy is a cornerstone of cancer therapy but is often undermined by tumor radioresistance arising from insufficient generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and limited DNA damage. Cuproptosis-a newly identified form of copper-dependent cell death-offers an innovative, yet untapped, approach to enhance radiosensitivity. Here, we report a first-of-its-kind copper-doped metal-organic framework nanoplatform, based on a ZIF-8 framework and loaded with the copper ionophore elesclomol, to concurrently induce cuproptosis and amplify radiotherapy. This nanoplatform (Cu-MOF@ELS) efficiently shuttles copper into cancer cell mitochondria, triggering excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) production and mitochondrial dysfunction that synergize with radiation to promote extensive DNA damage. We demonstrate that Cu-MOF@ELS strongly induces cuproptosis in tumor cells and significantly boosts radiotherapeutic efficacy in vitro and in vivo, with treated mice showing pronounced tumor regression and minimal systemic toxicity. Our work pioneers a novel therapeutic paradigm by integrating cuproptosis induction with radiotherapy, establishing Cu-MOF@ELS as a groundbreaking radiosensitizer that exploits metabolic cell-death mechanisms to overcome tumor radioresistance.Keywords: Nanocarriers, Cuproptosis, Radiotherapy, Skin melanoma

Introduction

Radiotherapy (RT), employing ionizing radiation to eradicate malignant cells and impede their proliferation, has been a cornerstone of cancer treatment since its introduction in the previous century [1]. Presently, radiation therapy is a necessary component of treatment for over half of all patients diagnosed with malignant tumors. However, the development of radioresistance remains a significant clinical challenge, limiting long-term survival prospects to a subset of patients [2]. RT resistance in tumor cells remains a significant obstacle in clinical oncology. The selective pressure exerted by the radiation exposure environment during radiotherapy favors the survival of cells better adapted to withstand radiation, leading to a reduction in radiotherapy sensitivity and, consequently, the diminished efficacy of anti-tumor therapies. This phenomenon, termed radiotherapy resistance, is frequently associated with unfavorable prognoses, tumor recurrence, and compromised quality of life. Consequently, monotherapy with radiotherapy often fails to achieve optimal therapeutic outcomes in the majority of cancer treatments. Therefore, improving the efficiency of RT in clinical practice remains a significant challenge [3]. To overcome radiation resistance, various radiosensitizers have been developed [4]. However, commonly used radiosensitizers like cisplatin often exhibit limited efficacy and restricted clinical applicability due to their significant side effects [5]. In recent years, metal-based nanocarriers have emerged as promising radiosensitizers, owing to their inherent radiation-sensitive activity, high drug-loading capacity, enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect, and good biocompatibility. The design of multifunctional nanosystems has further expanded the dimensions of radiosensitization [6]. These strategies significantly improve local tumor control and anti-metastatic efficacy while reducing the radiation dose and minimizing normal tissue toxicity by remodeling the tumor microenvironment, activating immune responses, or precisely targeting cell death pathways. This provides diverse innovative insights for clinical radiosensitization [7,8]. Furthermore, the chemical inertness often displayed by these materials can potentially reduce cellular toxicity, facilitating their translation into clinical applications [9,10].

RT aims to impede tumor cell proliferation and instigate cell death. Skin cancer, the fifth most common malignancy worldwide, often exhibits a high degree of malignancy and inherent radioresistance, thereby limiting the effectiveness of RT in treating cutaneous melanoma. However, the identification of novel cell death mechanisms presents promising opportunities for developing radiation sensitization strategies. One such mechanism is cuproptosis, a recently characterized form of programmed cell death driven by the disruption of cellular copper homeostasis. The initiation of this process involves the intracellular accumulation of copper ions (Cu2+), which then bind to lipoylated components of the mitochondrial tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle [11,12]. This copper-lipoyl protein aggregation subsequently downregulates iron-sulfur (Fe-S) cluster proteins, inducing proteotoxic stress and ultimately culminating in programmed cell death [13].

The tumor suppressor gene TP53 encodes a transcription factor that orchestrates cell fate decisions in response to cellular stress through the regulation of its target genes [14]. Activation of the p53 tumor suppressor protein and its downstream apoptotic pathway have been shown to effectively enhance cellular radiosensitivity [[15], [16], [17]]. Prior research indicates that aberrant copper homeostasis and physiological concentrations of copper can influence p53-mediated apoptosis through direct interaction with p53, inhibiting its DNA binding capacity. Conversely, elevated copper levels can induce p53 activation, potentially leading to apoptosis in cancer cells [18,19]. While p53 is known to regulate the biogenesis of Fe-S clusters and the copper chelator glutathione, both critical components of the copper protein pathway, this suggests a potential, but as yet undefined, role for this tumor suppressor in the context of copper protein disorders [20]. Mechanistically, p53 inhibits glucose uptake and glycolysis, thereby promoting a metabolic shift towards the TCA cycle and oxidative phosphorylation, ultimately increasing cellular sensitivity to cuproptosis [21].

Exploring a novel radiation sensitization strategy predicated on the mechanism of cuproptosis holds promise for overcoming the challenges associated with radiation sensitization in radioresistant tumors. Recent investigations have initiated the exploration of cuproptosis therapeutic potential in cancer treatment [[22], [23], [24]]. Our study has demonstrated the efficacy of nutrient deprivation therapy, in conjunction with cuproptosis, in significantly inhibiting energy metabolism in HCC cells [25]. Emerging strategies have been developed to apply cuproptosis in the treatment of melanoma, while radiotherapy can also trigger cuproptosis to eliminate tumors via a novel mechanism [26,27]. In clinical samples and mouse models, the key cuproptosis regulatory factors FDX1 and LIAS are significantly upregulated in residual tumors after radiotherapy, rendering tumor cells more sensitive to copper ion-induced cuproptosis. Meanwhile, mitochondrial metabolic reprogramming (such as activation of the TCA cycle) in tumor cells following radiotherapy enhances their susceptibility to cuproptosis, providing a target window for combinatorial therapy [[28], [29], [30], [31]]. Building upon these insights, we have developed an innovative cuproptosis-inducing radiosensitizer utilizing a zeolitic imidazolate framework (ZIF-8). This novel agent presents a promising strategy for circumventing radiation resistance in residual tumors that persist after initial radiotherapy, potentially leading to improved therapeutic outcomes.

ZIF-8, a subclass of metal-organic frameworks (MOFs), is distinguished by its tunable pore size, exceptional porosity, biocompatibility, and robust stability [32]. Elesclomol (ELS), an effective copper ionophore, facilitates the transport of Cu2+ into cells via the formation of an ELS-Cu2+ complex [33]. To induce cuproptosis, we engineered an intelligent nanosystem by encapsulating ELS within Cu-MOF nanocarriers (Fig. 1A). Cu-MOF@ELS nanocarries enter cells through endocytosis and, upon reaching the acidic environment of the lysosome, release Cu2+ and ELS. This release leads to the depletion of Fe-S cluster proteins in a ferredoxin 1 (FDX1)-dependent manner, with ELS exhibiting specific binding affinity to the α2/α3 helix and β5 chain of FDX1. Guided by FDX1, the released Cu2+ is reduced to Cu+, and the consequent elevation of Cu+ destabilizes Fe-S cluster proteins, disrupting mitochondrial protein liposomes and electron transfer processes [34]. Furthermore, as a copper ionophore, ELS enhances the transport of Cu2+ from the extracellular environment into cells, thereby amplifying the accumulation of Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS) and intracellular accumulation of Cu2+. Beyond its role in mediating Cu + production, FDX1 also promotes the interaction between lipoic acid synthase (LIAS) and glycine cleavage system H protein (GCSH), a lipid carrier protein, via direct binding to LIAS, thereby facilitating protein acylation [35]. Dihydrolipoamide dehydrogenase (DLAT), a crucial enzyme in the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, catalyzes the conversion of acetyl-CoA to CoA, concurrently transferring acetyl groups. Under FDX1 regulation, LIAS attaches the acyl moiety to DLAT, resulting in the formation of fatty-acylated DLAT. Subsequently, Cu+ directly binds to fatty-acylated DLAT, inducing aberrant oligomerization of the latter. This increase in insoluble DLAT contributes to cytotoxicity, while the concomitant decrease in functional DLAT disrupts mitochondrial metabolism and ultimately triggers cell death [36]. Combined with low-dose radiotherapy, Cu-MOF@ELS demonstrates a comparatively significant radiosensitization effect on subcutaneous melanoma in C57 mice. The underlying mechanism is multifaceted, operating through two primary pathways. First, ROS generated via reaction associated with cuproptosis and radiotherapy radiation, thereby exacerbating damage to tumor cells. Second, the cuproptosis-mediated reduction of Fe-S cluster proteins impairs DNA repair mechanisms, further amplifying radiotherapy-induced DNA damage. The synergistic action of these two processes ultimately contributes to the observed enhanced therapeutic efficacy (Fig. 1B–C).

Results and discussion

Preparation and characterization of Cu-MOF@ELS particles

Cu-MOF@ELS particles were synthesized via a methanol-based system utilizing Zn (NO3)2·6H2O, Cu (NO3)2·3H2O, and 2-Methylimidazole (2-MIM), aiming to develop a nano-regulator for the systemic and intracellular co-delivery of Cu2+ and ELS. ELS was encapsulated within the Cu-MOF matrix during synthesis. High-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) was employed to characterize the size and morphology of both the Cu-MOF and Cu-MOF@ELS nanocarriers. The crystalline structure of the Cu-MOF and Cu-MOF@ELS samples was subsequently confirmed by X-ray diffraction (XRD), as illustrated in Fig. 2A.

(C) Size distribution of Cu-MOF@ELS (D) Zeta potentials of Cu-MOF and Cu-MOF@ELS, respectively. (E) XPS analysis of Cu-MOF and Cu-MOF@ELS. TEM images revealed that the Cu-MOF@ELS composite exhibited a spherical morphology with an average particle size of approximately 40 nm (Fig. 2B–C, S1A-C). Elemental mapping analysis did not detect sulfur (S) due to its low concentration in the ELS molecular formula. However, inductively coupled plasma (ICP) analysis of Cu-MOF@ELS indicated an S content of 0.61% following ELS loading, confirming the successful incorporation of ELS. Furthermore, zeta potential measurements, employed as an indicator of successful surface modification, demonstrated a decrease from 27.32 mV for Cu-MOF to 24.90 mV for Cu-MOF@ELS. This reduction in zeta potential can be attributed to the presence of ELS on the surface of the Cu-MOF (Fig. 2D). XPS analysis, detailed in Fig. 2E, corroborated the presence of carbon, nitrogen, oxygen, zinc, and copper within the Cu-MOF structure. Inductively coupled plasma (ICP) analysis revealed a Cu atomic content of only 1.05% within the Cu-MOF material across multiple trials. Furthermore, the successful loading of ELS onto Cu-MOF was confirmed by the detection of a sulfur peak in the XPS spectra of Cu-MOF@ELS. Nitrogen adsorption-desorption isotherms were employed to assess the textural properties of both Cu-MOF and Cu-MOF@ELS. The Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) surface area was determined to be 1168.10 m2/g for Cu-MOF and 707.56 m2/g for Cu-MOF@ELS (Fig. 2F). The average pore diameters were 27.77 nm and 16.63 nm for Cu-MOF and Cu-MOF@ELS. Using a validated High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) method (standard curve shown in Fig. S2), we determined the ELS loading capacity and encapsulation efficiency (EE) for the Cu-MOF@ELS nanoparticles. The corresponding drug loading capacity was 4.54% and EE was calculated to be 99.8% by HPLC. In Fig. 2G, the pH-sensitive dissociation behavior of Cu-MOF@ELS to release copper ions was explored by Inductively Coupled Plasma Mass Spectrometry (ICP-MS), revealing that the release of copper ions was 583.1 ± 17.0 μg/L at pH 4.5, while the release was only 89.3 ± 2.16 μg/L at pH 7.5. Parallel to this, the cumulative release of ELS from the nanoparticles was markedly accelerated under acidic conditions (pH 5.5 and pH 4.5) compared to the near-neutral condition (pH 7.5), confirming the desired pH-activated release mechanism (Fig. S3).

This indicates that Cu-MOF@ELS can further release copper ions within the tumor's acidic microenvironment, thereby helping to reduce cytotoxicity in healthy tissue. In addition, we evaluated the colloidal stability and premature leakage of Cu-MOF@ELS in 10% FBS-containing medium, simulating the protein-rich bloodstream. Together with the previously reported pH-responsive release, these new data substantiate the systemic stability and tumor-specific release capability of the nanosystem (Fig. S4).

Bioinformatics analysis

RNA sequencing analysis was performed on B16 cancer cells treated with various therapies to elucidate the relationship between cuproptosis and radiotherapy, an area previously unexplored, and to investigate the subsequent biological effects. Specifically, transcriptomic analysis was conducted on B16 cells treated with PBS (control), RT, Cu-MOF@ELS, and RT combined with Cu-MOF@ELS. A heatmap visualization of differentially expressed genes revealed distinct genetic alterations across the treatment groups compared to the control group (Fig. 3A). Notably, RT resulted in the upregulation of 606 genes and downregulation of 1189 genes in B16 cells, while Cu-MOF@ELS treatment led to the upregulation of 872 genes and downregulation of 995 genes. Treatment with Cu-MOF@ELS + RT resulted in the upregulation of 1781 genes and downregulation of 1889 genes (Fig. 3B). Venn diagram analysis identified unique differentially expressed genes for each treatment group (Fig. 3C). The clustering heatmaps reveal that genes associated with DNA damage repair, such as XRRC6, are activated following radiotherapy to participate in DNA damage repair. However, their expression levels are downregulated upon drug administration (Fig. 3D). Further Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis of upregulated genes revealed involvement in various metabolic pathways, including, but not limited to, reactive oxygen species production, glucose metabolism, and protein metabolism (Fig. 3E). In the combination therapy group, radiotherapy-induced DNA repair regulation is suppressed by the drug, suggesting it may enhance tumor cell sensitivity to radiotherapy by inhibiting DNA damage repair pathways (Fig. 3F). These results provide insights into the potential mechanisms of cuproptosis and the enhanced effects of radiotherapy, thereby facilitating a deeper understanding of their impact on gene expression in B16 cells. Furthermore, both upregulated and downregulated genes were associated with diverse tumor signaling pathways, including the PI3K-Akt, VEGF, Apelin, and Hippo signaling pathways. Many downregulated genes were also associated with autophagy and ATP energy metabolism (Fig. S5A–D). Concurrently, we investigated cuproptosis-related protein expression in the ICGC and TCGA databases. As shown in Fig. 3G, cuproptosis-related gene expression in cancer patients differed significantly from that in normal individuals. In particular, DLAT, a critical target of cuproptosis, was overexpressed in SKCM and highly correlated with survival (Fig. 3H), suggesting the potential efficacy of cuproptosis-based treatments for SKCM. The transcriptome sequencing results indicated that the combined application of RT and Cu-MOF@ELS significantly impacts a multitude of molecular pathways, with notable regulation of genes and pathways related to tumor cell metabolism and DNA damage repair responses.

Cellular uptake

Given the increasing interest in ELS as an adjuvant chemotherapy drug, particularly in melanoma clinical trials over the past decade, we employed the murine melanoma cell line B16 as our in vitro model [[37], [38], [39]]. To investigate the intracellular uptake mechanism of Cu-MOF@ELS, we synthesized Cu-MOF@ELS-FITC by labeling Cu-MOF@ELS with fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC). Subsequent subcellular localization analysis revealed that, after 6 h of incubation, the fluorescent signal was predominantly localized within the lysosomes of treated B16 cells (Fig. 4A–B, S6). Furthermore, the copper content in B16 cells was determined using ICP-MS. The Cu-MOF@ELS-treated B16 cells exhibited a significantly elevated Cu2+ content (∼181.27 ng 10−6 cells). In contrast, the Cu2+ concentration in the control group (∼1.03 ng 10−6 cells) was undetectable (Fig. 4C). These findings suggest that Cu-MOF holds promise as a copper-based nanocarrier, potentially enhancing the bioavailability and activity of Cu2+ within B16 cells.

Cytotoxicity of Cu-MOF@ELS in vitro

To assess the in vitro cytotoxicity of Cu-MOF@ELS against B16 tumor cells, we employed the cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) assay. The results demonstrated a significantly enhanced anti-tumor effect in the Cu-MOF@ELS treatment group (Fig. 4E) while Cu-MOF exhibited no observable cytotoxicity at therapeutic doses across three different cell lines (Fig. S7). As a copper chelator, DL-Penicillamine (DL) can inhibit the cuproptosis pathway, and further, that our experiments confirmed low-dose DL exhibited no discernible cytotoxicity in B16 cells (Fig. S8C) [40]. We proceeded to determine the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) of Cu-MOF@ELS in B16 cells (Fig. S8A–B) and then used Calcein-AM and propidium iodide (PI) staining to assess B16 cell mortality following various treatments (Fig. S9). Subsequent flow cytometry analysis revealed a 3.24-fold increase in the necrosis rate of B16 cells treated with Cu-MOF@ELS compared to those treated with Cu-MOF@ELS + DL (Fig. 4D), indicating the efficacy of Cu-MOF@ELS in inducing tumor cell death in vitro. Furthermore, the anti-tumor effect of Cu-MOF@ELS was validated through plate colony formation assays, which demonstrated an approximate 95% reduction in colony number in B16 cells (Fig. 4F–G).

Cu-MOF@ELS induced cuproptosis in vitro

To elucidate the mechanism by which Cu-MOF@ELS induces cuproptosis, we first investigated DLAT oligomerization, a hallmark of this process wherein Cu2+ specifically coordinates with DLAT, triggering its aggregation and subsequent copper-mediated cell death. Immunofluorescence imaging revealed pronounced DLAT lesion aggregation in tumor cells treated with Cu-MOF@ELS (Fig. 5A) and this has also been demonstrated at the protein level (Fig. 5B). Furthermore, FDX1, a crucial regulator in cuproptosis responsible for reducing Cu2+ to Cu+ and facilitating the copper-enhanced ROS accumulation, exhibited significantly downregulated expression in the Cu-MOF@ELS treatment group compared to control groups (Fig. 5C). Consistent with this, mRNA expression levels of key cuproptosis genes, including FDX1, DLAT, and LIAS, were also reduced (Fig. S10A–C, Table S1). Additionally, further testing revealed that copper ion levels, DNA damage levels, and ROS levels increased after drug administration, while ATP levels decreased significantly (Fig. 5D–F). Furthermore, experimental results showed that cell death induced by Cu-MOF@ELS was not reversed by inhibitors of ferroptosis, apoptosis, autophagy, pyroptosis, or necrosis, but was significantly reversed upon addition of a cuproptosis inhibitor (Fig. S11, Table S2). These findings collectively suggest that Cu-MOF@ELS nanocarriers possess the capacity to induce cuproptosis in malignant cells.

In vivo biodistribution of Cu-MOF@ELS

Having demonstrated significant in vitro antitumor activity with Cu-MOF@ELS, we proceeded to evaluate its therapeutic efficacy in vivo. Initially, we assessed the pharmacokinetic profile of Cu-MOF@ELS. B16 tumor-bearing mice were intravenously administered CY5-labeled Cu-MOF@ELS (Cu-MOF@ELS-CY5), and their biodistribution was monitored via in vivo optical imaging at predetermined time points. In vitro experiments, the accumulation of CY5-labeled Cu-MOF@ELS represents a time-dependent manner in B16 cells and exhibited significant red fluorescence after 4 h (Fig. 6A). Based on vitro cellular uptake, we detected the distribution of organs at 1, 5, 24, 48 and 72 h in vivo experiments. The results indicated a substantial accumulation of fluorescence signals within the tumor region as early as 1-h post-injection. Conversely, free CY5 exhibited rapid clearance from the tumor site within 5 h of injection. Notably, at the 24-h mark, the tumor fluorescence intensity in mice treated with Cu-MOF@ELS-CY5 remained robust (Fig. 6B). Subsequent biodistribution analysis at 5 h post-injection revealed a higher fluorescence intensity in the tumors of mice injected with Cu-MOF@ELS-CY5 compared to those injected with free CY5, despite some unavoidable accumulation in major organs, including the heart, lungs, liver, spleen, and kidneys (Fig. 6C–S12). Moreover, the fluorescence quantitative was detected at 5, 24, 48 and 72 h and we found that Cu-MOF@ELS has approached complete metabolism after 48 h in major organs (Fig. S13). We also detected the copper ion concentration in serum and the results show that the serum copper ion level has a transient increase at a low dose after Cu-MOF@ELS injection, and recovers to the normal level at 24 h post-injection, which is consistent with the drug metabolism level observed by in vivo small animal fluorescence imaging (Fig. S14). Collectively, these findings suggest that Cu-MOF@ELS exhibits prolonged retention and effective accumulation within the tumor microenvironment.

Antitumor ability in vivo of Cu-MOF@ELS

To further evaluate the antitumor efficacy in vivo, we employed a B16 melanoma-bearing mouse model. FemaleC57BL/6N mice were subcutaneously inoculated with B16 tumor cells, and once tumors reached an approximate volume of 80 mm3, animals were randomized into four treatment groups: PBS (control), Cu-MOF, ELS, and Cu-MOF@ELS. Mice received intravenous administrations of the respective treatments on days 1, 3 and 5 (Fig. 7A). As depicted in Fig. 7B, treatment with Cu-MOF or ELS alone resulted in limited inhibition of tumor growth compared to the PBS control group. In contrast, the Cu-MOF@ELS group exhibited a significantly enhanced antitumor effect compared to all other treatment groups (Fig. 7C–E), suggesting a synergistic therapeutic benefit in solid tumor treatment. Immunohistochemical staining for FDX1 in tumor tissues further corroborated the in vivo efficacy of Cu-MOF@ELS (Fig. 7F).

Cu-MOF@ELS combined with RT synergistically enhances antitumor efficacy

Radiotherapy employs high-energy ionizing radiation to induce DNA double-strand breaks, triggering cell cycle arrest, senescence, or various forms of cell death, including apoptosis, necrosis, autophagy, and mitotic catastrophe. Beyond directly damaging DNA, ionizing radiation can also exert indirect effects on organisms. For instance, it can generate ROS, such as oxygen free radicals and hydrogen peroxide, via the radiolysis of cellular water and stimulation of oxidases. This, in turn, can lead to damage to nucleic acids, proteins, and lipids, ultimately resulting in cellular injury or death. Fe-S clusters, serving as the active centers of critical enzymes, are essential for mitochondrial oxidative metabolism and epigenetic regulation [41]. Impairment of Fe-S cluster proteins can disrupt multiple biological processes, including DNA demethylation, epigenetic regulation, DNA repair, and apoptosis. In vitro experiments, we used the Chou-Talalay method to investigate radiosensitizing effect of Cu-MOF@ELS and calculated the Combination Index (CI) by using CompuSyn software. According to the experimental results, the CI value is located in the range of 0.29-0.63, showing a significant synergy effect (Fig. S15). In our experiment, we observed that radiotherapy increased the mRNA levels of cuproptosis-related proteins (Fig. S10A–C). Furthermore, tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), a proinflammatory mediator known to induce tumor cell death, was significantly increased upon combined treatment with Cu-MOF@ELS and radiotherapy (Fig. S10D) [42]. These mechanistic insights suggest that the combination of Cu-MOF@ELS with RT may represent a promising anti-tumor therapeutic strategy.

Immunotherapy plays an important role in cuproptosis, and many new nanomaterials have been developed to treat tumors by activating immune pathways [[43], [44], [45], [46], [47]]. We next detected the concentrations of inflammatory factors in serum, but there were no significant changes in serum levels of inflammatory factors (Fig. S16). This suggests that the therapeutic effect of the dominant factor may be more caused by the damage of DNA double stranded by radiation therapy and the combination of Cu-MOF@ELS strengthens this process.

To further elucidate the anti-tumor efficacy of Cu-MOF@ELS in combination with RT, we investigated its impact on DNA damage repair. γ-H2AX, a well-established marker of DNA double-strand breaks (DSBs), revealed that the combined treatment significantly reduced DNA damage repair capacity compared to RT alone (Fig. 8A) [48].

Fe-S clusters are essential components for DNA synthesis, and it has been reported that cuproptosis reduces the synthesis of Fe-S clusters. We measured the mRNA levels of genes related to Fe-S cluster synthesis and Fe-S protease (Fig. S17A). The results showed that the mRNA levels of NSF1, ISCU, FXN, NFU1 and ACO2 all had a decreasing trend after adding Cu-MOF@ELS, indicating that Fe-S cluster synthesis was inhibited, which is consistent with the results of enzyme activity detection experiments. In addition, Aconitase (Aco) is an important intracellular Fe-S protease, mainly located in the cytoplasm and mitochondria, and is positively correlated with the content of Fe-S clusters in the cell. Therefore, we tested the activity of Aco to indirectly quantify the content of Fe-S clusters. Based on the experimental results, we found that adding Cu-MOF@ELS, the average enzyme activity decreased to 50.7% compared to the control group, indicating that Cu-MOF@ELS can significantly reduce the level of intracellular Fe-S clusters (Fig. S17B).

Currently, many new types of nanoparticles are used to treat tumors by mediating ROS production [[49], [50], [51]]. To assess the generation of intracellular ROS following various treatments, we employed 2,7-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA). As depicted in Fig. 8B, the Cu-MOF@ELS + RT group exhibited the most pronounced fluorescence, further substantiating the excellent catalytic performance of Cu-MOF@ELS in radiosensitization. Concordantly, examination of protein expression levels associated with cuproptosis and DNA damage revealed that the combination therapy induced the most significant cuproptosis and the highest levels of DNA damage (Fig. 8C–D).

To corroborate these findings in vivo, we established a subcutaneous B16 tumor-bearing mouse model. Mice were intravenously administered Cu-MOF@ELS on days 1, 3 and 5, followed by external irradiation (2 Gy) to the tumor region (Fig. 9A). As shown in Fig. 8B, tumor growth in the low-dose RT group was comparable to the PBS control group, highlighting the limited efficacy of low-dose RT monotherapy. Conversely, the Cu-MOF@ELS + RT group exhibited significantly reduced tumor volume (Fig. 9B–S18) and the highest survival rate (Fig. S19) compared to all other treatment groups. This trend was consistently observed in tumor weights (Fig. 9C) and tumor growth curves (Fig. 9D). Histological analysis via Histopathological examination (H&E) staining further corroborated the therapeutic efficacy of Cu-MOF@ELS + RT, with the area of necrotic cells within tumor tissue being significantly greater than in other groups (Fig. 9E). Furthermore, quantitative analysis of Ki67 immunohistochemical (IHC) staining confirmed that the combination of Cu-MOF@ELS with RT showed significant tumor-suppressing effects (Fig. 9E–S20). Notably, this combination therapy markedly improved survival rates in B16 tumor-bearing mice relative to all other treatment groups. Finally, we have quantitatively calculated the radio-sensitization effect of radiotherapy based on the results of animal experiments by using The Tumor Growth Inhibition value (TGI). According to the experimental results in vivo, the TGI reached 36.1% under single radiation therapy (2Gy). By contrast, the combined treatment group achieved a TGI of 89.3%. The calculated radiosensitization efficiency of Cu-MOF@ELS was 83.3%, revealing promising potential for radio-sensitization application (Fig. S21).

Safety evaluation of combined Cu-MOF@ELS and RT

To evaluate the in vivo biosafety of Cu-MOF@ELS in combination with RT, we performed comprehensive hematological and histopathological analyses on treated mice. Healthy C57 mice received multiple doses of Cu-MOF@ELS, and specimens were collected on day 7 to assess short-term safety. As demonstrated in Fig. 9F and Fig. S22, Cu-MOF@ELS treatment exhibited minimal impact on hematological parameters (RBC, Hb, WBC, PLT, MCV, MCH), suggesting the absence of hemolysis or bone marrow hematopoietic dysfunction. The H&E staining revealed that the Cu-MOF@ELS + RT combination treatment did not induce significant morphologic abnormalities or inflammatory infiltration in major organs, compared with the control group (Fig. S23). Furthermore, serum biochemical analyses indicated that the combined treatment caused no discernible hepatorenal toxicity, as evidenced by the maintenance of liver function markers (ALT, AST) and renal function indicators (BUN, CREA) within normal reference ranges (Fig. 9G). The hemolysis rate of Cu-MOF@ELS remained below 5% even at a high concentration of 400 μg/mL, representing a 100-fold increase over the in vivo administered concentration (Fig. S24), which provides strong evidence for the favorable blood compatibility of Cu-MOF@ELS for in vivo applications. Regarding the concern about long-term biosafety raised by the accumulation of Cu-MOF@ELS in the liver and kidneys, we have detected the relevant liver and kidney function indicators of mice at 14 days and 28 days after treatment (Fig. S25). The results clearly show that Cu-MOF@ELS does not cause obvious liver or kidney toxicity in mice even after a relatively long period of injection, indicating that the nanoplatform has favorable long-term biosafety profiles despite its transient enrichment in the liver and kidneys (the major metabolic organs). In summary, these results suggest that the combination of Cu-MOF@ELS with RT exhibits a favorable biosafety profile for antitumor therapy.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study introduces a novel ZIF-8-based copper-organic framework loaded with the copper ionophore elesclomol (Cu-MOF@ELS) that integrates radiotherapy enhancement with cuproptosis induction in a single nanoplatform. Cu-MOF@ELS amplifies radiation-induced tumor destruction via two synergistic mechanisms: elevated reactive oxygen species generation and disruption of Fe-S cluster dependent DNA repair, both of which intensify DNA damage in cancer cells. In vivo, the combined Cu-MOF@ELS and low-dose RT treatment significantly suppressed melanoma tumor growth and improved survival, while exhibiting minimal systemic toxicity and a favorable safety profile. Altogether, this work highlights a new radiosensitization paradigm, demonstrating the clinical potential of cuproptosis driven nanomedicines like Cu-MOF@ELS as next-generation radiosensitizers to overcome tumor radioresistance.

Experimental section

Chemicals

Zinc nitrate hexahydrate (Zn(NO3)2·6H2O) was purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. Copper(II) nitrate trihydrate (Cu(NO3)2·3H2O) and 2-Methyl imidazole (2-MIM) were obtained from Shanghai Adamas Reagent Co., Ltd. 1, 2-Distearoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine-N-(methoxy (polyethylene glycol)-2000) (DSPE-PEG2000), elesclomol were purchased from Xi'an Ruixi Biological Technology Co., Ltd. All chemicals were used without further purification.

Synthesis

Synthesis of Cu-MOF particles

The synthesis method of Cu-MOF nanocarriers is similar to the previously reported method [32]. The Zn (NO3)2·6H2O with 0.20 M, Cu (NO3)2·3H2O with 0.033 M were first dissolved in methanol. And 2-MIM with 1.60 M was dissolved in methanol to form the transparent reactant solutions. Prior to the reaction, both reactants were cooled in the ice bath until their temperature reached 5 °C. Upon reaching this temperature, Cu-MOF synthesis was initiated by simultaneously adding 100 mL of Zn (NO3)2·6H2O and Cu (NO3)2·3H2O solution into 100 mL of 2-MIM solution. This addition process was consistently maintained over a period of 10 min while the reaction mixture was stirred at 250 rpm. Then the reaction was terminated after 10 min by adding methanol to bring the total volume to 100 mL. After that, the Cu-MOF nanocarriers were collected by centrifugation.

Synthesis of Cu-MOF@ELS particles

To synthesize Cu-MOF@ELS, 19 mg of Cu-MOF, 1 mg ELS, 2 mg DSPE-PEG2000 were dissolved in 5 mL of iced water in ultrasound for 3 h. Afterwards, these samples were freeze-dried.

Synthesis of Cu-MOF@ELS-CY5 particles

To synthesize Cu-MOF@ELS-CY5, 38 mg of Cu-MOF, 2 mg ELS, 4 mg DSPE-PEG2000, 2 mg CY5 were dissolved in 25 mL of DI water in ultrasound for 10 h in 4 °C. Afterwards, these samples were freeze-dried.

Characterization

X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were collected using an X-ray diffractometer (Bruker D8 ADVANCE diffractometer). High-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) was conducted using the JEM-F200 instrument. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurements were conducted using the Thermo Fisher ESCALAB Xi + spectrometer. The Cu content was analyzed using ICP-OES with the NexION 350D instrument. The N content was confirmed by element analysis (EuroEA3000). The Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR) spectrum was acquired using the VERTEX70 FT-IR spectrometer.

The particle porosity was analyzed by the Micromeritics ASAP 2020 instrument. Initially, Cu-MOF particles powder was degassed under vacuum at 300 °C for 5 h before to nitrogen (N2) sorption analysis at 77 K. Prior to N2 sorption measurement, Cu-MOF@ELS particles powder was evacuated under vacuum at ambient temperature for 24 h.

Cell culture

B16, T47D, and Hep3B cell lines were purchased from the cell Bank/Stem Cell Bank, Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). B16 cells (CSTR: 19375.09.3101MOUSCSP5096) were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium, T47D cells (CSTR: 19375.09.3101HUMTCHu87) were cultured in DMEM medium, and Hep3B (CSTR: 19375.09.3101HUMSCSP5045) cells were cultured in MEM medium.

Cellular uptake

B16 cells were planted in 6-well plates and subsequently treated with CY5-labeled Cu-MOF@ELS (Cu-MOF@ELS-CY5) for 0.5, 2, 4, and 8 h. To investigate the uptake pathway of Cu-MOF@ELS in greater detail, cells were treated with FITC-labeled Cu-MOF@ELS (Cu-MOF@ELS-FITC) for 6 h and stained with LysoTracker Red for 20 min, followed by observation using an Echo Revolve microscope.

Radiotherapy

X-rays were generated by a PXI Precision X-Rad 225 generator (Minneapolis, Minnesota, USA) at 225 kV/13.3 mA, with a dose rate of 1.98 Gy/min. When tumor volume reached approximately 80 mm3, mice bearing melanoma tumors were anaesthetized with 1.25% tribromoethanol (0.2 mL/10 g) and positioned on a platform. Fractionated radiotherapy was administered to the tumors, with each irradiation delivering a dose of 2 Gy of X-rays.

Cell viability, colony formation assay, and apoptosis analysis in vitro

Cell viability was assessed in B16, T47D, and Hep3B cells using the CCK8 assay. Cells were planted into 96-well plates with five replicate wells per condition. After one day of culture, cells were treated with varying concentrations of Cu-MOF@ELS for 24 h and/or exposed to 2 Gy of X-ray irradiation. Approximately 24 h post-treatment, cell viability was determined using the CCK8 assay kit according to the manufacturer's protocol.

In regard to the clone formation, seven groups were established to evaluate cytotoxicity: 1) Control group, 2) Cu-MOF (3 μg mL−1) group, 3) ELS (50 nM) group, 4) Cu2+ + ELS (5 μM + 50 nM) group, 5) Cu-MOF@ELS (3 μg mL−1) group, 6) Cu-MOF@ELS + DL (3 μg mL−1 + 2 μM) group, 7) DL (2 μM) group. The procedure was as follows: cells were planted in six-well plates and cultured overnight. Subsequently, different treatments were applied for 24 h. Cells were incubated for 14 days with medium changes every 3 days until most single clones exceeded 50 cells. For cell apoptosis analysis, similarly to clone formation, seven groups were built and cells were pretreated with treatments for 24 h, then the cells were stained using the Annexin V/RedNucleus II apoptosis detection kit (Uelandy, Y6102L), and subsequently analyzed by flow cytometry (Beckman Coulter, USA).

Western blot

Western blotting was employed to detect the expression levels of DLAT, FDX1, γ-H2AX, and p53. In short, B16 cells were plated in 60 mm petri dish and added with Cu-MOF@ELS for 24 h. The total protein was extracted with lysis buffer, standard SDS-PAGE and LDS-PAGE were performed, and then immunoblotting was performed. Antibodies used for the study were as follows: p53 (10442-1-AP, 1:1000), FDX1 (12592-1-AP, 1:1000), DLAT (13426-1-AP, 1:1000), GAPDH (60004-1-Ig), β-actin (66009-1-Ig) from Proteintech (Rosemont, USA), γ-H2AX (#9138, 1:1000 for WB,1:200 for IF, CST).

In vivo experiment

C57BL/6 female mice aged 6-8 weeks were purchased from and fed in Experimental Animal Center of Xi'an Jiaotong University. All animal procedures were approved by Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Xi'an Jiaotong University (Number. XJTUAE2025-1764).

In the experiment, mice with similar body weight were selected and randomly divided into four groups: i) PBS, ii) Cu-MOF, iii) ELS, iv) Cu-MOF@ELS and received treatments until the tumor volume reached 80 mm3. 100 μg mL−1 of PBS or Cu-MOF@ELS were administrated intravenously for each mouse and treated every 2 days. In experiments combining Cu-MOF@ELS with RT, mice received radiotherapy 5 h after drug injection.

A tumor-bearing model was established by subcutaneously implanting 1 × 105 B16 cells into each mouse. Tumor volume was recorded every two days and survival curves plotted. Tumor volume was calculated using the following formula: (a × b2)/2, where a represents the longest side and b is the shortest side.

Statistical analysis

All data were from three independent experiments unless otherwise stated. Data were analyzed and visualized using GraphPad Prism 8 software. Significant differences between groups were assessed using two-tailed Student's t-test and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) test, with statistical significance levels defined as: ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, and ∗∗∗p < 0.001.
