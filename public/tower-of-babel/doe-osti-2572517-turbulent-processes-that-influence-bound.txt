Final Technical Report

 for

                  DOE Award DE-SC0020398

Title: Turbulent Processes that Influence Boundary-Layer Cloud
 Structure

 PI: Vincent Larson

Institution: University of Wisconsin — Milwaukee, Milwaukee, WI

             Project Period: 9/15/2019 - 9/14/2024

Many models of the atmosphere simulate vertical profiles of observable fields, such as
temperature, moisture, and vertical velocity. To assess the realism of such model output, it is
important to evaluate the modeled profiles against observations. However, many extant
evaluations consist of comparing time averaged profiles from the model and the observations.
Such time averages can smooth out unrealistic features that appear in instantaneous profiles.
In this way, the time averages may obscure certain model errors from detection.

For instance, many models misrepresent turbulent processes, leading to profiles that are too
smoothed out or too noisy. E.g., if a model has coarse vertical resolution and overly strong
diffusion, then the model may be unable to produce observed sharp peaks. On the other hand,
a model may have artificial approximations that produce spurious jumps in the vertical.

Much can be learned by browsing through instantaneous snapshots of model output. But the
volume of output produced by a global model is far too great to rely purely on visual inspection.
In addition, we need a way to statistically characterize the profiles, so that anomalies can be
detected more conveniently.

For instance, we may wish to have a statistical “jump detector.” How do we develop one?
Looking at the variance of a profile, w(z), merely tells us whether the profile varies from top to
bottom, but it doesn’t tell us whether that variation is confined to a sharp jump. Instead, in this
project, we look at the distribution of the jumps ∆w, where

∆𝑤 = 𝑤(𝑧 + ∆𝑧) − 𝑤(𝑧).

This requires the choice of a jump size, ∆𝑧. Once the view is switched from the statistics of w(z)
to the statistics of ∆w, then we can bring classical statistical measures to bear on the problem.
The kurtosis (a normalized fourth-order moment) tells us whether we have sharp jumps. Like
radar reflectivity, a sixth-order moment, the kurtosis is sensitive to the largest fluctuations at the
tail of the distribution. The skewness (a normalized third-order moment) tells us whether those
jumps are predominantly jumps to the right (jumps from low to high values of w as ∆𝑧 increases)
or jumps to the left.

With these statistical measures in hand, we can do hypothesis testing to see if the modeled
profiles are consistent with the observed profiles. E.g., in this project, we use bootstrap
sampling from the model output in order to ask whether the observations lie within the spread of
sampled model output.

In this project, we use new evaluation diagnostics to show that profiles from two models differ
from observations. The observations come from an upward-staring Doppler lidar instrument at
the ARM site in Oklahoma. The lidar measures vertical velocity, w. One model is a large-eddy
simulation (LES) model that is run routinely as part of the LES ARM Symbiotic Simulation and
Observation (LASSO) project. Due to computational limits, it uses relatively coarse vertical grid
spacing. To ensure numerical stability, it uses numerical diffusion. For these reasons, the LES
model produces, perhaps unsurprisingly, instantaneous profiles that are too smooth. Our
statistics readily detect that. The second model is a single-column model, namely the Subgrid
Importance Latin Hypercube Sampler (SILHS). SILHS uses a simplified strategy to draw
sample points from a distribution that is too bimodal. The sampled points occasionally jump
from one mode to the other, producing an abrupt and spurious jump in the simulated profiles.
Our statistics detect the spurious jumps too.

We are able to mitigate the spurious jumps in SILHS by use of a smarter sampling strategy
based on direct sampling from the cumulative distribution function. The revised code produces
statistics that are more in line with observations.

The aforementioned results have been published in Stephens et al. (2024), a journal article
cited in the references below.
