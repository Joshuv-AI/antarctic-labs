Non-crystallographic systems of integers over composition algebras
Daniele Corradetti

Abstract
. In this work we revisit classical systems of integers inside the real normed division algebras from the point of view of finite norm shells and root systems. Building on the icosian framework of Moody–Patera and on the integral root-system viewpoint of Chen–Moody–Patera and of Johnson, we isolate the precise axiomatic ingredients of the non-crystallographic analogue: an order over the golden ring ℤ ⁡ [ φ ] \mathbb{Z}[\varphi] together with a distinguished finite root shell whose Cartan coefficients lie in ℤ ⁡ [ φ ] \mathbb{Z}[\varphi] . We show that the usual Gaussian, Eisenstein, Hamilton, Hurwitz and Coxeter–Dickson examples are recovered by separating the order, its units, and its distinguished finite shells; once the lattice requirement is replaced by a finite root-shell requirement, the golden integer ring becomes the natural coefficient ring for the non-crystallographic cases H 2 H_{2} and H 4 H_{4} . We then construct a weak golden octonion order by Cayley–Dickson doubling of the icosian ring; the resulting free rank- 8 8 ℤ ⁡ [ φ ] \mathbb{Z}[\varphi] -order has a 240 240 -element finite shell of type H 4 ⊕ H 4 H_{4}\oplus H_{4} and its multiplication is genuinely octonionic. Finally, we prove (i) that this weak double is self-dual with respect to the polar norm pairing, hence has no strict norm-integral overorder, and (ii) that the first trace-integral discriminant tower over it contains no octonion-stable nonzero isotropic gluing.

In this work we isolate and formalize an integral root-shell axiomatization for the non-crystallographic case. The construction is deliberately modest: it is not a classification theorem. Rather, it is a framework that recovers the crystallographic examples, explains why

2.
Orders, traces, norms and root shells

Before defining non-crystallographic integer systems, one has to separate three objects that are often conflated: the full order, its multiplicative unit shell, and the root system carried by a distinguished finite shell. This separation is harmless in the classical examples and indispensable in the golden examples, where the full unit group of
\mathbb{Z}[\varphi]
is infinite.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
R
be a commutative ring, typically
R=\mathbb{Z}
or
R=\mathbb{Z}[\varphi]
. Let
A
be a unital composition algebra over the fraction field of
R
, equipped with an involution
x\mapsto\overline{x}
, trace

(2.1)

\operatorname{Tr}_{A}(x)=x+\overline{x},

and quadratic norm

(2.2)

\operatorname{N}_{A}(x)=x\overline{x}.

An
R
-order
in
A
is a subset
\mathcal{O}\subset A
such that:

(1)

\mathcal{O}
is a finitely generated
R
-module;

(2)

1\in\mathcal{O}
;

(3)

\mathcal{O}
is closed under addition and under every (bracketed) product of finitely many of its elements; in the alternative octonionic case this is equivalent, by Artin’s theorem, to closure under all products of any two elements;

(4)

\overline{x}\in\mathcal{O}
for every
x\in\mathcal{O}
;

(5)

\operatorname{Tr}_{A}(x),\operatorname{N}_{A}(x)\in R
for every
x\in\mathcal{O}
.

For an alternative algebra the closure clause in (3) is equivalently expressed by requiring that
\mathcal{O}\cdot\mathcal{O}\subset\mathcal{O}
in the sense of pointwise products. We refer to
[
Co46
,
CS03
,
CS99
]
for the classical examples and to
[
Di23
]
for the original formulation.

In analogy to the octonionic case, the order is generally infinite, whereas the most geometric information is concentrated in finite shells.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
\mathcal{O}
be an
R
-order in
A
. For
m\in R
, the
norm shell
of value
m
is

(2.3)

S_{m}(\mathcal{O})=\{x\in\mathcal{O}:\operatorname{N}_{A}(x)=m\}.

A
root shell
is a finite subset
S\subset S_{m}(\mathcal{O})
that carries a root-system structure with respect to the bilinear form

(2.4)

\langle x,y\rangle=\operatorname{Re}(x\overline{y}).

Remark 2.3
.

The unit group or unit loop of an order is not the same thing as a root shell. In the classical Hurwitz examples the norm-one shell is finite and therefore both notions can coincide. In the golden case, however,
\mathbb{Z}[\varphi]^{\times}=\{\pm\varphi^{k}:k\in\mathbb{Z}\}
is infinite, while
H_{2}
and
H_{4}
are finite root systems. This is the central distinction of the paper.

The following elementary criterion is the computational form of the definition.

Proposition 2.4

(Order criterion)
.

Let
b_{1},\ldots,b_{n}
be an
R
-basis of a finitely generated
R
-module
\mathcal{O}\subset A
, with
b_{1}=1
. Assume that

(2.5)

b_{i}b_{j}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}c_{ij}^{k}b_{k},\qquad c_{ij}^{k}\in R,

and

(2.6)

\overline{b_{i}}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}d_{i}^{k}b_{k},\qquad d_{i}^{k}\in R.

Assume moreover that the coordinate polynomials for
\operatorname{Tr}_{A}(x)
and
\operatorname{N}_{A}(x)
, written in the basis
b_{i}
, have coefficients in
R
. Then
\mathcal{O}
is an
R
-order.

Proof.

The
R
-module structure gives closure under addition. Equation (
2.5
) gives closure under multiplication, and (
2.6
) gives stability under the involution. The last assumption gives
\operatorname{Tr}_{A}(x),\operatorname{N}_{A}(x)\in R
for a general element
x=\sum_{i}r_{i}b_{i}
, with
r_{i}\in R
. Hence all conditions in the definition of an
R
-order are satisfied.
\square

∎

3.
Crystallographic integer systems over composition algebras

We now review the classical cases, but in the language of orders and shells. This is not meant as a new classification of integral elements; rather, it is the crystallographic part of the dictionary that will be used later.

3.1.
Gaussian and Eisenstein integers

Let
i^{2}=-1
. The Gaussian order is

(3.1)

\mathbb{Z}[i]=\{a+bi:a,b\in\mathbb{Z}\}\subset\mathbb{C}.

Its trace and norm are

(3.2)

\operatorname{Tr}(a+bi)=2a,\qquad\operatorname{N}(a+bi)=a^{2}+b^{2}.

The unit shell is

(3.3)

S_{1}(\mathbb{Z}[i])=\{\pm 1,\pm i\}.

Geometrically this is the square root shell, which we will consistently denote by
A_{1}\oplus A_{1}
(the rotation-group label
C_{4}
is used only for the multiplicative unit object).

Let

(3.4)

\omega=e^{2\pi i/3},\qquad\omega^{2}+\omega+1=0.

The Eisenstein order is

(3.5)

\mathbb{Z}[\omega]=\{a+b\omega:a,b\in\mathbb{Z}\}.

The norm-one shell is

(3.6)

S_{1}(\mathbb{Z}[\omega])=\{\pm 1,\pm\omega,\pm\omega^{2}\},

which has cardinality
6
, not
3
. With complex multiplication it is the cyclic group
C_{6}
, and as a root shell it realizes

3.2.
Hamilton, hybrid and Hurwitz quaternions

Let

For

(3.8)

\operatorname{Tr}(x)=2a,\qquad\operatorname{N}(x)=a^{2}+b^{2}+c^{2}+d^{2}.

Its norm-one shell is

(3.9)

\{\pm 1,\pm i,\pm j,\pm k\},

which is the quaternion group
Q_{8}

The Hurwitz order can be written as

(3.10)

\mathcal{H}=\mathbb{Z}[1,u,v,w],

(3.11)

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(1-i-j+k),

\displaystyle v

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(1+i-j-k),

Its

(3.12)

\{\pm 1,\pm i,\pm j,\pm k\}\cup\left\{\frac{1}{2}(\pm 1\pm i\pm j\pm k)\right\},

Finally, the hybrid quaternionic order is naturally written as

(3.13)

\mathbb{Z}[\omega]\oplus\mathbb{Z}[\omega]j.

Its

(3.14)

\{\pm 1,\pm\omega,\pm\omega^{2},\pm j,\pm\omega j,\pm\omega^{2}j\}.

This shell realizes

3.3.
Graves–Cayley and Coxeter–Dickson octonions

The octonionic examples require more care because
\mathbb{O}
is not associative. Thus the finite multiplicative shells are not groups but Moufang loops. This is perfectly analogous to the way in which the octonionic unit sphere is multiplicative but not associative.

Let

(3.15)

\{1,i,j,k,l,il,jl,kl\}

be a Dickson basis of
\mathbb{O}
, with multiplication fixed by the Fano-plane convention. The Graves–Cayley order is the
\mathbb{Z}
-span of this basis. Its norm-one shell is

(3.16)

\{\pm 1,\pm i,\pm j,\pm k,\pm l,\pm il,\pm jl,\pm kl\}.

The presence of
-1
is essential; without it the shell is not closed under taking opposites and cannot be a root system.

Coxeter’s integral Cayley numbers
[

]
give octonionic orders whose minimal shells have

which has
\operatorname{N}(h)=1
and therefore belongs to the unit shell of the order.
The exact list of the

Remark 3.2
.

The octonionic review also explains why the word “order” must be used with some care in non-associative algebras. Closure under multiplication is still meaningful, and alternative laws are enough for the standard inverse and Moufang-loop statements, but one must not silently import associative ring language.

4.
Root systems, lattices and crystallographicity

In order to define a non-crystallographic analogue of integral elements, we need the correct crystallographic condition. This is the point where the ordinary lattice condition over
\mathbb{Z}
must be replaced by a golden integrality condition.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
E
be a Euclidean space with inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
. A finite subset
\Phi\subset E\setminus\{0\}
is a
reduced root system
if

(4.1)

\Phi\cap\mathbb{R}\alpha=\{\pm\alpha\}

for every
\alpha\in\Phi
, and if for every
\alpha\in\Phi
the reflection

(4.2)

r_{\alpha}(v)=v-\frac{2\langle v,\alpha\rangle}{\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle}\alpha

preserves
\Phi
. All root systems considered below are reduced.

The group generated by the reflections
r_{\alpha}
is a Weyl group in the crystallographic cases and, more generally, a Coxeter reflection group
[
Hum90
]
. For
H_{2},H_{3},H_{4}
we will use the term Coxeter group.

Definition 4.2
.

A reduced root system
\Phi
is
crystallographic
if

(4.3)

\frac{2\langle\beta,\alpha\rangle}{\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle}\in\mathbb{Z}

for every
\alpha,\beta\in\Phi
. Its root lattice is

(4.4)

\Lambda(\Phi)=\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{Z}}(\Phi)=\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{r}n_{i}\alpha_{i}:n_{i}\in\mathbb{Z},\ \alpha_{i}\in\Phi\right\}.

.

2

(4.5)

\frac{\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle}{\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle}=-\frac{1}{2},\qquad\frac{2\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle}{\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle}=-1.

6.3.

A natural question is whether the octonionic analogue of the icosian construction is only a slogan or a genuine order. The cleanest positive answer is obtained by Cayley–Dickson doubling of the icosian ring.

6.4.
The strong G3 problem

The weak construction above does not exhaust the golden octonion problem. A stronger analogue of the Coxeter–Dickson order would have to contain a finite shell with genuinely mixed elements

To state this precisely we use the following indecomposability convention.

Definition 6.7
.

A pair

consisting of a

. Otherwise

is

We separate the strong G3 problem into increasingly rigid tests. A purely Coxeter-theoretic remark first explains why an irreducible
H

Remark 6.8

(Coxeter rank obstruction)

The finite Coxeter classification

A useful counting criterion is the following.

Lemma 6.9

(Mixed-projection criterion)
.

Let

The forward direction is the contrapositive of Definition

Lemma 6.17

(Trace-norm lattice)

7.
Applications to quasicrystals, aperiodic algebras and physics

The appearance of
\mathbb{Z}[\varphi]
is not accidental. Inflation by the golden ratio is one of the basic symmetries of Penrose tilings and icosahedral quasicrystals. The algebraic role of
\mathbb{Z}[\varphi]
is therefore the arithmetic counterpart of a geometric scaling symmetry.

It is of paramount importance to notice that the physical claim of the present work is modest. We do not claim that non-crystallographic integers directly produce a fundamental theory of matter. Rather, they provide exact arithmetic data which can be transported into quasicrystalline and aperiodic models. The useful chain is

7.2.
Spectral and elastic models

Once a finite quasicrystal patch has been generated from a non-crystallographic integer system, one can pass from geometry to dynamics. The most immediate model is a tight-binding Hamiltonian on the adjacency graph of the patch:

(7.3)

H=-t\sum_{\langle x,y\rangle}|x\rangle\langle y|\;+\;\sum_{x}V_{x}|x\rangle\langle x|.

Here the edge relation
\langle x,y\rangle
comes from the tiling or from a chosen distance shell, and
V_{x}
may depend on the local aperiodic environment. The quantities to compute are then the spectrum, the density of states and the inverse participation ratio

(7.4)

\operatorname{IPR}(\psi)=\sum_{x}|\psi(x)|^{4}.

The role of
\mathbb{Z}[\varphi]
is to give exact labels for inflation, local environments and candidate gap labels.

Similarly, elastic or phononic models can be built from the same point sets. A discrete harmonic-spring energy has the form

(7.5)

E(u)=\sum_{\langle x,y\rangle}k_{xy}\left(|u_{x}-u_{y}|-\ell_{xy}\right)^{2},

or, in its linearized version about the rest configuration,

(7.6)

E_{\mathrm{lin}}(u)=\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{\langle x,y\rangle}k_{xy}\bigl(\hat{n}_{xy}\cdot(u_{x}-u_{y})\bigr)^{2},

where
\hat{n}_{xy}
is the unit vector along the equilibrium bond.
In a periodic crystal, the normal modes are organized by a reciprocal lattice. In a quasicrystal, the reciprocal object is a module, and the internal cut-and-project space gives phason degrees of freedom. The arithmetic developed above supplies exact coordinates for both the physical and internal components.

7.3.
Aperiodic algebras

There is a second, more algebraic, direction. Aperiodic Lie and Jordan algebras built over quasicrystalline point sets can be viewed as later layers over the same arithmetic

In analogy to the octonionic case, the icosian double gives a natural next test object. The order

is genuinely octonionic, since its associator is nonzero, but its visible shell is still

measured on triples of shell elements. Comparing the distribution of

with the quaternionic

Remark 7.2
.

This distinction is important. The strict and relaxed G3 searches above are negative inside the present icosian-double tower. Therefore the weak golden octonion order should be used in physics as a controlled non-associative model, not as evidence for an indecomposable golden Coxeter–Dickson shell.

8.
Conclusions and Future Developments

In this work we defined a root-shell formulation of non-crystallographic systems of integers over composition algebras. The construction recovers the classical crystallographic examples, explains the role of finite norm shells, and shows why
\mathbb{Z}[\varphi]
is the natural coefficient ring for the
H_{2}
, icosian
H_{4}
, and icosian-double
H_{4}\oplus H_{4}
examples. In particular, the icosian double gives a genuine octonionic
\mathbb{Z}[\varphi]
-order, albeit with a decomposable
H_{4}\oplus H_{4}
shell.

The analogy with the octonionic case is preserved once the lattice requirement is replaced by the root-shell requirement. Coxeter–Dickson octonions recover the crystallographic shell
E_{8}
; icosians recover the non-crystallographic shell
H_{4}
. The cost of the analogy is the requirement to distinguish four objects which the crystallographic case allows one to conflate: the full order, the finite shell, the multiplicative unit object and the arithmetic coefficient ring.

From the physical point of view, the most robust application is the quasicrystalline one. The
H_{2}
shell gives exact Penrose arithmetic,
H_{3}
gives icosahedral star vectors, and
H_{4}
gives the icosian arithmetic parent. This produces concrete observables such as diffraction intensities, reciprocal-module labels, tight-binding spectra and phason modes. The weak golden octonion order enters only later, as a controlled non-associative model of coupled icosian sectors through the associator.

A natural next direction is to ask whether a stronger golden Coxeter–Dickson analogue can be made precise outside the present icosian-double tower. The strict overorder problem over
G_{0}
is closed by
G_{0}^{\#}=G_{0}
, and the first trace-integral discriminant tower has no octonion-stable isotropic gluing. The remaining open directions are therefore: different ambient golden octonion orders, twisted Cayley–Dickson doubles, neighbor constructions in the sense of Kneser, and a first physical implementation of the
H_{2}
Penrose pipeline. The present article fixes the arithmetic language before further work can address the physical and octonionic ramifications.
