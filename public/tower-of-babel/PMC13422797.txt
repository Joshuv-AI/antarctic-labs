Characteristics and outcomes of paediatric intensive care unit admissions across Kuwait: multicentre national-level cohort study
Authors: Abdulla Alfraij, Mohammad Alghounaim, Mostafa Galal, Farahat Elsayed, Ahmed Abdelmoniem, Ibrahim Hikal, Shishir Shetty, Deyaa Madian, Waleed Ramadan, Hayam Yahia, Abdullah Almajran, Abdulrahman AlDaithan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNational paediatric intensive care registries are essential for benchmarking care, understanding population-level disease burden and identifying inter-site variation. However, such national-level data from the Middle East remains limited. This study aims to describe national patterns of paediatric intensive care unit (PICU) admissions, case mix, interventions, seasonality and outcomes across Kuwait.MethodsThis retrospective multicentre cohort study included all PICU admissions across the governmental hospitals in Kuwait between 1 January 2023, and 30 June 2025. Demographic characteristics, clinical data and outcomes were extracted from a national PICU registry. Data were analysed at national and centre levels using descriptive statistics.ResultsA total of 5,534 PICU admissions were analysed. Median age was 10.5 (IQR 2.4–57) months, with infants accounting for 44.4% of admissions. Respiratory disorders were the predominant diagnosis (63.9%). The median PICU length of stay was 3 (IQR 1–6) days. The intubation rate among PICU admissions was 20.9%. Central venous access was used in 18.1% of cases. Admission peaks occurred during late autumn and winter, with a 1-month shift in peak timing in 2024 compared with that in 2023. The overall PICU mortality rate was 2.6%, with centre-level mortality ranging from 0.9% to 5.6%. Substantial inter-centre variability was observed in respiratory support practices and procedural utilisation.ConclusionThis study represents the first national-level analysis of PICU admissions across Kuwait. Admissions were characterised by infant predominance, a high burden of respiratory illness, short lengths of stay and low overall mortality. Marked inter-centre variability in respiratory support practices highlights opportunities for national standardisation, capacity planning and registry-driven quality improvement.

Introduction

Multicentre paediatric intensive care unit (PICU) data are essential for benchmarking performance, quantifying national critical care demand and identifying unwarranted variation in practice and outcomes. Single-centre reports cannot reliably capture system-level case mix, seasonal surges or inter-hospital differences in resource utilisation.1 National registries address these gaps by standardising definitions, enabling comparative reporting and supporting quality improvement and health service planning across institutions.2 3 They also provide an infrastructure for monitoring epidemiological shifts over time, evaluating resource utilisation and establishing reference values for key outcomes such as length of stay and mortality.4 These functions are particularly important in settings where paediatric critical care delivery is distributed across multiple hospitals and where admission pathways (eg, medical vs surgical/cardiac critical care) influence observed case mix. Published national PICU data remain limited in several regions, including the Middle East.5 The lack of integrated datasets restricts accurate assessment of disease burden, resource utilisation and inter-centre variation. Moreover, country-level benchmarks are critical for interpreting institutional performance and informing health system planning.6 These evaluations are particularly pertinent in systems with centralised referral pathways and diverse PICU functions.

Paediatric critical care services in Kuwait are provided through seven governmental general PICUs, one dedicated paediatric cardiac ICU, one paediatric surgical ICU and one Kuwait Oil Company–operated PICU at Ahmadi Hospital. Although the Ahmadi PICU was operational throughout the study period and accounted for approximately 3.5% of national PICU admissions, it joined the national PICU registry in 2026 and was therefore not included in the present analysis. No private PICUs were operating in Kuwait during the study period. As of June 2025, these PICUs collectively provide critical care services for population of 870 520 children (≤14 years).7 Using the first national dataset from the Kuwait PICU network, the study characterises patient profiles, resource utilisation and outcomes across participating sites. This study aimed to provide national benchmarks and quantifies inter-centre variation to inform future standardisation efforts, capacity planning and registry-driven research.

Methods

Study design and setting

This retrospective multicentre cohort study was conducted across all governmental general (medical) PICUs in Kuwait. The dedicated paediatric cardiac intensive care unit and paediatric surgical intensive care unit were not included in this analysis because they function as separate subspecialty services with distinct patient populations and referral pathways. Participating centres were anonymised and are reported as sites 1–7 throughout the results, tables and figures. Participating units accounted for 123 PICU beds and approximately 2200–2300 admissions annually. Unit capacity ranged from 4 to 24 beds, with variation in referral pathways and availability of subspecialty services. The registry development, data collection procedures and detailed unit characteristics have been described previously.5 8

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research.

Study population

All PICU admissions between 1 January 2023 and 30 June 2025 were included. Eligible patients ranged from neonates to 14 years of age, with inclusion extended to 18 years for medically complex patients, in accordance with national Ministry of Health regulation. Data were recorded retrospectively at the time of PICU discharge.

Data collection and registry management

Data were collected using a secure, web-based national PICU registry. Each site assigned a trained data officer responsible for data entry.5 All entries were reviewed for completeness and accuracy at least three times annually (April, August and December). Duplicate entries were removed. Inter-hospital transfers were merged and analysed as a single admission, with the first admitting hospital designated as the index site and cumulative length of stay calculated across facilities. Readmissions were defined as subsequent returns to the PICU occurring within 48 hours of discharge.9 Admissions occurring after 48 hours were considered new episodes. No substantial missing data were identified for the variables included in the analysis.

Variables and definitions

Collected variables comprised patient demographics, admission characteristics, interventions (respiratory support, central venous access) and discharge outcomes. Age categories were defined as neonate, infant, preschooler, school-aged child and adolescent. Primary admission diagnoses were categorised as respiratory disorders (upper or lower airway diseases, lung parenchymal diseases or respiratory infections); neurological disorders or infections (including status epilepticus, seizure disorders, meningitis, encephalitis, spinal muscular atrophy or Guillain–Barré syndrome); clinically diagnosed sepsis or septic shock; endocrine disorders (including diabetic ketoacidosis or thyroid diseases); cardiovascular disorders (heart failure, cardiomyopathies, structural heart diseases, arrhythmias, hypertensive emergencies or post-cardiac arrest); postoperative or postprocedural admissions; trauma, toxicological or environmental conditions (including drowning or smoke inhalation); procedural admissions like central venous catheter (CVC) insertion; gastrointestinal or hepatic disorders; haematologic or oncologic disorders; and other diagnoses, including renal or life-threatening electrolyte derangements, metabolic disorders and allergic or immunologic conditions.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were summarised as frequencies and percentages and continuous variables as medians with IQRs. Descriptive analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics V.29.

Results

A total of 5,534 PICU admissions and 143 deaths (2.6%) were recorded across all participating sites during the study period. Table 1 summarises the national characteristics of the PICU cohort. The median age at admission was 10.5 (IQR 2.4–57) months, with neonates and infants accounting for 52.2% of PICU admissions. Overall, 57.2% of admissions were male. The median PICU length of stay (LOS) was 3 (IQR 1–6) days, which was comparable between hospitals. Respiratory disorders were the most frequent primary admission diagnosis, accounting for around two-thirds (63.9%) of cases. Intubation rate was 20.9% of all cases, with a median invasive mechanical ventilation duration of 5 days (IQR 2–8). At the national level, the median PICU admissions per month were 159 cases (IQR 128–209). Seasonal variation in admissions was observed (figure 1). In 2023, peak admissions occurred during October–December, whereas in 2024, the peak shifted to November–January. A smaller secondary peak was observed in May.

Non-invasive respiratory modalities, such as non-invasive positive pressure ventilation (NIPPV), high-flow nasal cannula (HFNC) or both, were applied in 52.9% of admissions. Substantial variability in respiratory support practices was observed between hospitals (table 3). Some PICUs demonstrated a predominantly HFNC-based approach, whereas others relied heavily on NIPPV as first-line therapy. Invasive mechanical ventilation rates varied nearly threefold across sites, and the proportion of patients intubated without prior NIPPV or HFNC support (primary intubation) differed substantially. At site 1, HFNC monotherapy was rarely used (0.6%), whereas NIPPV monotherapy was used in 64.5% of admissions. In contrast, sites 2 and 4 reported low solo use of NIPPV (1.6% and 1.9%, respectively) and greater reliance on HFNC (35.3% and 69.5%, respectively). Invasive mechanical ventilation rates ranged from 10.6% to 30.5%. Primary intubation accounted for 4.8%–13.0% of admissions with a majority of cases being in the school-age category. Sites 1, 4 and 7 had more primary intubations in their cases. The median duration of invasive ventilation ranged from 2 to 6 days.

Discussion

This study provides the first national overview of PICU admissions across Kuwait, offering insights into the demographic and clinical characteristics of this population. The age distribution demonstrates a marked predominance of infants, consistent with global PICU epidemiology, where children aged <1 year account for the largest proportion of critical care admissions.10 This pattern reflects age-specific vulnerability to acute respiratory illnesses, immature immune responses and limited physiologic reserve.11 This finding may support the need for age-focused resource allocation and requiring of more staffing expertise in infant critical care. The short median LOS observed across centres further supports acute, rapidly evolving conditions rather than prolonged multisystem disease.12 13 Previous studies have similarly reported institutional disparities in LOS, often linked to differences in clinical decision-making, availability of step-down units, and access to post-ICU rehabilitation or subacute care beds.14

Respiratory disorders constituted nearly two-thirds of all admissions, substantially exceeding other diagnostic categories. This distribution reflects both disease epidemiology and historical system organisation, as PICUs in Kuwait have functioned primarily under the umbrella of paediatric departments, that is, as purely medical units, with trauma and many surgical cases traditionally managed in surgical or adult ICUs. The recent transition toward admitting any children with critical illness, regardless of being medical or surgical, to PICUs likely explains the limited representation of trauma and postoperative cases during the study period. The respiratory dominance aligns with international PICU reports but is amplified in magnitude, indicating a disproportionately high respiratory burden within the national critical care system.15 Seasonal analysis demonstrated a shift in peak respiratory admissions in 2024 compared with 2023, with the peak occurring 1 month later. Similar temporal shifts in respiratory virus circulation have been reported internationally in the post–COVID-19 pandemic era and are attributed to changes in population immunity, viral interference and altered transmission dynamics.16 17 The presence of a secondary spring peak further supports evolving seasonality patterns, emphasising the importance of flexible workforce planning and dynamic surge strategies.

Marked inter-unit variability in respiratory support practices was observed despite comparable age distributions, LOS and diagnostic profiles. Differences in utilisation of invasive ventilation, HFNC and NIPPV likely reflect institutional policies, thresholds for escalation, monitoring capacity, staffing models and availability of some respiratory support modalities in general wards. These findings are consistent with international literature indicating that intubation rates vary widely between PICUs, driven by factors like local protocols, staffing models, training and even cultural attitudes toward invasive interventions.18 Sites permitting HFNC use on general wards, like site 1, demonstrated higher reliance on NIPPV within the PICU, whereas units requiring PICU admission for any respiratory support, such as sites 2 and 4, reported greater HFNC utilisation inside their units. These findings highlight practice heterogeneity rather than patient-driven differences, a pattern similarly reported in multinational regional surveys of PICU management approaches for critical pertussis.19 This highlights opportunities for national harmonisation of respiratory care pathways.

Central venous access requirement among patients on invasive ventilation demonstrated marked inter-unit variability, spanning from less than one-third to more than four-fifths of cases. Comparable dispersion in procedural practices has been described by Mahendra et al, reporting that differences in invasive access utilisation persisted after accounting for measurable patient characteristics.20 The persistence of this variability despite similar ventilation rates across sites suggests that institutional determinants play a substantial role. Potential contributors include variation in vascular access policies, differences in perceived indications for central access, operator availability and expertise, unit workflow models and the accessibility of alternative strategies such as midlines or ultrasound-guided peripheral access. Prior multicentre investigations have similarly reported that invasive procedure rates, including central venous access placement, may vary considerably across PICUs, independent of illness severity distributions, reflecting local practice environments and decision-making frameworks.20 These findings highlight procedural heterogeneity as an important system-level phenomenon with implications for benchmarking, complication risk and standardisation efforts.

The overall PICU mortality in Kuwait (2.6%) aligns with contemporary benchmarks reported in high-income paediatric critical care settings, where crude mortality rates have been observed at approximately 2.8% in registry-style analyses.21 The observed centre-level variability in mortality likely reflects differences in case complexity, referral patterns and subspecialty coverage rather than the quality of care alone. Higher mortality observed in site 7 is plausibly related to their role as national referral units for complex haematology/oncology cases, including advanced or terminal malignancy. In the absence of legally recognised limitations of life-sustaining therapy, such patients may remain in PICUs until death, inflating unit-level mortality compared with centres managing predominantly reversible conditions. These findings highlight the importance of contextualising mortality data within institutional case mix and legal frameworks.

Despite the strength of the nationally inclusive, multicentre design, this study has limitations inherent to its retrospective and descriptive nature. First, data were dependent on the accuracy and completeness of documentation at the time of discharge, which may have introduced inconsistencies or reporting bias. Second, although standardised variable definitions and direct communication among data providers were employed to reduce heterogeneity, subtle differences in interpretation or clinical coding may still have occurred. Additionally, the absence of PICU severity scoring systems (eg, Paediatric Risk of Mortality, Paediatric Index of Mortality and Paediatric Logistic Organ Dysfunction) limited our ability to adjust outcomes by case acuity. The lack of time-stamped respiratory support data precluded determination of escalation or de-escalation sequences. Differences in admission policies, ward-level respiratory support availability and referral pathways may introduce unmeasured confounding. Long-term sustainability of data quality depends on continued institutional engagement and automated data integration. In addition, this cohort reflects admissions to medical PICUs and does not include patients managed in dedicated cardiac or surgical PICUs. As a result, children with primary postoperative, congenital cardiac or cardiothoracic conditions are underrepresented, introducing selection bias. This limitation likely contributed to the predominance of respiratory and medical diagnoses, lower overall procedural complexity and a mortality profile reflective of medical PICU populations rather than the full spectrum of paediatric critical illness at a national level. Finally, the absence of post-discharge outcomes limits assessment of longer-term morbidity and survival.

Conclusions

This study is the first country-level analysis of PICU admissions in Kuwait. It reports data from a centralised small high-income health system with distributed PICUs showing practice heterogeneity without formal benchmarking. The national PICU population is characterised by a predominance of infant admissions, a high burden of respiratory disease, short LOS and low overall mortality. Marked inter-unit variability in respiratory support practices and outcomes was observed despite broadly similar demographic profiles, highlighting opportunities for national standardisation of care pathways and data integration. These findings establish a foundational benchmark for paediatric critical care in Kuwait and support future initiatives in system planning, quality improvement and multicentre research.
