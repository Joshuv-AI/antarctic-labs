MultiGSA: wind power prediction based on a multi-scale cross-graph network combined with an enhanced simple attention mechanism
Authors: Xufei Zhang, Zhe Qiu, Yaling Han, Xuchu Jiang, Hui Qi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Wind power generation datasets are widely encountered in renewable energy systems because of their inherent periodicity, non-stationarity, and substantial noise. Recent advancements in graph neural networks have improved wind power time series forecasting via multi-scale feature extraction. However, a key challenge is effectively capturing multi-scale temporal trends, suppressing intrinsic noise, and leveraging correlations among wind power variables. We propose a novel architecture: Multi-scale coupling graph neural network with enhanced simple attention (MultiGSA). In contrast to traditional approaches that fail to address the instability, high sampling frequency, and intrinsic noise of wind power data, MultiGSA extends feature learning across multi-scale temporal dynamics and cross-variable correlations. It uses a cross-scale Co-GNN module to capture multi-scale trends and suppress time-domain noise, leveraging inter-scale complementary information. The cross-variable Co-GNN capitalizes on variable homogeneity and heterogeneity to strengthen correlation detection, whereas the enhanced simple attention mechanism alleviates frequency-domain noise. We evaluate MultiGSA on four real-world wind farm datasets, and the results show that it consistently outperforms state-of-the-art methods. Specifically, MultiGSA achieves up to 4.8% MAE and 3.2% RMSE improvements over the baselines, demonstrating its effectiveness and robustness in managing wind power data complexity.

Introduction

Time series forecasting plays a pivotal role in a wide range of practical applications, among which wind power forecasting is particularly critical for the effective integration of renewable energy into power systems1. The inherent intermittency and volatility of wind energy lead to intrinsic randomness in wind power generation, presenting significant challenges for accurate forecasting2. This complexity is multifaceted, arising from the interaction of multiple factors across diverse temporal and spatial scales. Atmospheric processes influencing wind patterns exhibit multi-scale characteristics, ranging from large-scale weather systems to local turbulence, thus requiring forecasting models to capture both long-term trends and short-term fluctuations simultaneously3. In addition, the quality and accessibility of input data present considerable challenges, such as measurement inaccuracies, missing data, the sparse spatial distribution of meteorological stations, and the fusion of heterogeneous data sources4. Climate change has induced constant shifts in environmental conditions, and power system dynamics are evolving continuously. Models must adapt to these changes, further exacerbating the complexity of forecasting tasks5.

Traditional statistical methods such as autoregressive integrated moving average (ARIMA) models and exponential smoothing have been widely employed in wind power forecasting6. Nevertheless, such classical methods are generally inadequate for characterizing the intricate nonlinear and nonstationary behaviors embedded within wind power sequences7. This inherent shortcoming has propelled a paradigm shift in predictive modeling toward deep learning architectures, which have exhibited markedly enhanced predictive power compared with traditional approaches8,9.

The development of deep learning methods in the field of wind power forecasting is characterized by the application of increasingly sophisticated network architectures. Recurrent neural networks (RNNs), such as long short-term memory (LSTM) networks and gated recurrent units (GRUs), have been extensively utilized because of their ability to model long-term dependencies10. Convolutional neural networks (CNNs), which were originally designed for image processing, have been adapted for time series forecasting because of their ability to capture local patterns11. In recent years, attention mechanism-based models (e.g., transformers) have achieved state-of-the-art performance in various sequence modeling tasks, including wind power forecasting12,13.

Despite these impressive advances, current deep learning frameworks deployed for wind power prediction continue to exhibit several critical limitations. First, the inherent tension between predictive precision and computational overhead persists as a pressing concern, particularly for grid management entities that rely on real-time or near-real-time forecasts to inform operational scheduling and decision-making14. Second, the quantification of predictive uncertainty remains indispensable yet underdeveloped; standalone point predictions cannot adequately characterize the full spectrum of possible generation outcomes, a key input for risk evaluation and robust decision support within power system operations15. Third, wind power forecasting is inherently interdisciplinary, demanding the integration of knowledge from multiple fields, which necessitates overcoming barriers in interdisciplinary communication and collaboration.

Recent advances in machine learning and artificial intelligence have opened new avenues for addressing the complex challenges in wind power forecasting. Zhang et al. demonstrated the ability of robust algorithms to handle noisy and complex data in 3D point cloud registration, a technology that can be adapted for spatial dimension processing in wind farm modeling16. In the field of deep learning, Liu and Zhang emphasized the effectiveness of reinforcement learning in optimizing complex systems, a method that holds significant value for dynamic wind power forecasting and grid integration17. Furthermore, the continuous improvement of artificial intelligence methods has provided novel paradigms for addressing dynamic data distribution shifts and complex feature extraction. For example, the recent development of adaptive fusion domain cyclic variational generative adversarial networks and dynamic collaborative adversarial domain adaptation networks18,19 has demonstrated remarkable capabilities in aligning cross-domain features and mitigating dynamic environmental noise. Moreover, the application of multimodal technologies in complex industrial scenarios, such as arc detection in railway systems20, highlights the immense potential of integrating heterogeneous data sources to increase model robustness and detection accuracy. Collectively, these multifaceted investigations underscore the pivotal role of interdisciplinary methodologies in propelling wind power forecasting research forward. They suggest that fusing technologies from cross-cutting fields—including computer vision, network optimization, and sophisticated neural network architecture design—will likely yield remarkable enhancements in both the precision and dependability of forecasting outcomes.

To address these challenges, this paper proposes the MultiGSA model, which adopts a multi-scale collaborative graph neural network (Co-GNN)21 in the temporal dimension to simulate dependencies between different time scales. This approach facilitates the extraction of critical scale-specific information from the temporal dimension, enabling the variable dimension to assign higher edge weights to scales with clearer trends and lower noise levels. The Co-GNN in the variable dimension leverages the homogeneity and heterogeneity of positive and negative edge weights among different variables to effectively distinguish the importance of wind power features and achieve efficient feature extraction. Finally, the improved simple, parameter-free attention module (SimAM)22 enhances global dependency modeling through multi-head self-attention, stabilizes training via residual connections and LayerNorm, and replaces traditional DCT blocks with an efficient attention mechanism to capture frequency-domain feature information. This design ensures the maximum extraction of wind power features from both the time and frequency domains while enhancing the model’s robustness to noise in the wind power data.

This study makes the following important contributions to the field of wind power forecasting. First, a comprehensive analysis of real-world wind power datasets is conducted, revealing key challenges in handling time-domain and frequency-domain noise as well as variable heterogeneity. To address these challenges, a novel MultiGSA model is proposed, which innovatively integrates a multi-scale cross-interactive collaborative graph neural network (Co-GNN) with an improved simple parameter-free attention module (SimAM). This unique architecture supports advanced multidimensional feature extraction and can capture multi-scale temporal trends, model complex variable interactions, and adaptively focus on relevant frequency components. The multi-scale and variable-dimensional Co-GNN components effectively reduce time-domain noise and enhance feature extraction capabilities, whereas SimAM suppresses frequency-domain noise—a combination that has not been explored in the field of wind power forecasting. Extensive experimental validation on four distinct wind power datasets demonstrates that the proposed model outperforms nine state-of-the-art time series forecasting methods. Additionally, detailed noise robustness analysis and thorough ablation experiments provide valuable insights into the model’s practical application value and the contributions of each component. This comprehensive approach provides a novel perspective for wind power forecasting and demonstrates competitive performance under the evaluated conditions, offering an effective solution for handling complex, noisy wind power data.

Related work

Multivariate time series forecasting (MTS)

In recent years, scholars have conducted extensive research on multivariate time series (MTSs). Some researchers have achieved remarkable results by using long short-term memory (LSTM) networks23 to model long- and short-term dependencies in time series. Other scholars, aiming to address temporal issues in unstructured data, have designed graph neural networks (GNNs)24, which convert unstructured data into graph data for training. Additionally, some researchers have focused on long-sequence challenges and have attempted to use the transformer framework to handle dependencies in long sequences. On the basis of graph neural networks, this paper integrates a frequency-domain attention mechanism to provide a new perspective for time series modeling. This method overcomes the limitations of the time domain and considers both the time and frequency domains from a graph perspective, offering an innovative approach to time series modeling.

Graph neural networks (GNNs)

Graph neural networks (GNNs)25,26 have been proven to be effective methods for analyzing unstructured data. In recent years, the SpecFormer model27 based on graph neural networks has demonstrated significant effectiveness in handling graph-structured data by seamlessly integrating the network architectures of GNNs and transformers, along with introducing frequency-domain filtering methods. Graph spectral enhancement techniques28 have been shown to substantially improve the performance of self-supervised contrastive learning. OTG-Net29 is a novel class-incremental learning method that addresses the catastrophic forgetting problem and the challenge of class set expansion in dynamic graph neural networks, paving a new path for general time series modeling on graphs.

Finally, the decoder uses the direct multiplexing (DMS) prediction approach to project future sequence trends.

Simple parameter-free attention mechanism (SimAM)

The SimAM parameter-free attention mechanism18 is characterized as a ‘learnable-parameter-free’ module. While SimAM introduces no additional weights requiring gradient-based optimization (backpropagation), its core computation relies strictly on the variance \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\sigma\:}_{c,t}^{2}$$\end{document} and mean \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mu\:}_{c,t}$$\end{document} of the local temporal window. By utilizing these local statistical moments, the mechanism inherently incorporates an inductive bias aligned with the local data distribution. This statistical prior functions as a structural constraint, allowing the network to adaptively regulate attention weights and suppress frequency-domain noise without expanding the model’s parametric complexity.

First, the difference between the response value \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{x}_{c,t}$$\end{document} at time step t on channel c and its temporal neighborhood mean \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mu\:}_{c,t}$$\end{document} is evaluated. Multi-scale feature generation and noise suppression

To effectively capture the diverse temporal patterns inherent in wind power data—spanning from high-frequency short-term fluctuations to low-frequency long-term trends—and to mitigate intrinsic noise, the cross-scale Co-GNN module employs a pooling-based multi-scale feature generation strategy.

Instead of relying on computationally expensive decomposition methods, we utilize a hierarchical average pooling mechanism. In the context of time series signal processing, an average pooling operation inherently functions as a local low-pass filter. It smooths out high-frequency noise spikes and stochastic volatility, thereby compelling the network to focus on structurally significant evolutionary trends.

Given the input multivariate time series \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:X\in\:{\mathbb{R}}^{B\times\:T\times\:C}$$\end{document}, where B is the batch size, T is the sequence length, and C is the number of variables, the multi-scale generation is processed across three distinct resolution scales.

MultiGSA model framework construction

As illustrated in Fig. 1, the CoGNN model adopts a modular, collaborative architecture that integrates cross-scale Co-GNN, cross-variable Co-GNN, and the SimAM parameter-free attention mechanism, forming an end-to-end wind power forecasting system.

The cross-scale Co-GNN serves as the core temporal feature extractor, specializing in modeling interactive dependencies across multiple time scales. It excavates and aggregates scale-specific information from input sequences, prioritizing temporal trends with strong correlations while suppressing noise interference. In parallel, the cross-variable Co-GNN functions as an intervariable feature fuser, leveraging external multivariate data to decompose and reconstruct input sequences. It captures both homogeneous and heterogeneous correlations among different variables, effectively distinguishing the relative importance of wind power-related features and enhancing the model’s ability to exploit multisource information.

The SimAM parameter-free attention mechanism acts as a feature enhancer and noise suppressor, complementing the two Co-GNN modules. It receives multidimensional feature representations from the Co-GNN modules and adaptively adjusts feature weights on the basis of local statistical characteristics. Without relying on additional learnable parameters, SimAM strengthens the emphasis on key discriminative features (such as critical temporal patterns and variable correlations) while suppressing redundant information and residual noise. This not only optimizes the purity of feature representations but also reduces computational complexity, ensuring efficient model inference.

To effectively balance the contributions of temporal and variable features, the representations \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{H}^{time}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{H}^{var}$$\end{document} are not simply added; instead, they are integrated via a learnable gated fusion mechanism. Specifically, the two tensors are concatenated and passed through a linear layer with a sigmoid activation function to generate an adaptive gating matrix g. The fused representation is then computed as a weighted sum in Eq. (20):20\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$H^{{fused}} = g \odot H^{{time}} + \left( {1 - g} \right) \odot H^{{\mathrm{var} }}$$\end{document}

where ⊙ denotes elementwise multiplication. This fused representation \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{H}^{fused}$$\end{document} is subsequently passed into the SimAM module. SimAM calculates a 3D attention map to weigh the importance of each feature position without introducing additional parameters, effectively enhancing the signal-to-noise ratio.

After passing through N stacked layers of this Co-GNN architecture, the refined feature map is projected by the decoder. The decoder employs a temporal linear projection to map the sequence to the target prediction horizon T. Experiment

Dataset and experimental setup

This study utilizes hourly data collected from four wind monitoring stations between 00:00 on January 2, 2017, and 23:00 on December 31, 2021, with each station comprising a total of 43,800 records. The dataset includes the following features: timestamp, air temperature at 2 m (°F), relative humidity at 2 m (%), dew point temperature at 2 m (°F), wind speed at 10 m (m/s), wind speed at 100 m (m/s), wind direction at 10 m, wind direction at 100 m, gust speed at 10 m (m/s), and normalized wind power. Specifically, the normalization process was executed strictly at the individual wind farm level (scaled to the [0, 1] interval). The power output sequence of each monitoring station was scaled relative to its own specific maximum theoretical capacity, ensuring that the distinct operational scales and idiosyncratic generation baselines of each localized site were accurately preserved and isolated from cross-site scaling artifacts. The power is defined as the prediction target of the model. To prevent future information leakage, the entire dataset was partitioned chronologically. Specifically, the data were divided into training, validation, and test sets at a 7:1:2 ratio on the basis of temporal order, ensuring that the training set strictly predates the validation and test sets. During model training, the hyperparameters were configured as follows: the initial learning rate was set to 0.0005, the input historical sequence length (look-back window) was set to 96, the forecasting horizon was set to 48, the number of training epochs was set to 20, the number of early stopping patience steps was set to 10, and the batch size was set to 32.

Specifically, at Location 1, our model attains an error standard deviation of 0.921 and a skewness indicator of -0.392, indicating a relatively concentrated error distribution with a negative skew. A similar pattern is observed at Location 2 (StdDev: 0.785, skewness: -0.874) and Location 3 (StdDev: 0.849, skewness: -0.902). Notably, at Location 4, although MTGNN achieves a relatively low error standard deviation (0.873), our model maintains a competitive skewness indicator (-1.050). Overall, the skewness indicators across all locations and methods generally fall within the range of -1.169–0.004, suggesting that the error distributions of most models are left skewed, with occasional larger negative errors. The experimental results show that our model performs outstandingly across all four real‑world datasets. Notably, the transformer and STGNN also exhibit competitive skewness indicators at certain locations (e.g., Location 2 and Location 3). Moreover, graph-neural-network models (such as MTGNN and ASTGNN) achieve relatively good overall performance. However, the degree of improvement over the baselines is not uniform and heavily depends on the local data distribution. For example, at Location 1, which suffers from highly volatile wind resources and significant noise interference, our model achieves a substantial lead. This is because the frequency-enhanced SimAM and cross-variable Co-GNN effectively isolate dynamic meteorological shifts (e.g., sudden temperature drops or gust variations) from background noise. Conversely, at Location 3, characterized by complex and long-term temporal periodicities, the performance gain is driven primarily by the cross-scale Co-GNN. Standard spatial-temporal graph models (such as MTGNN and ASTGNN) often struggle with feature blurring over long horizons, whereas our cross-scale interaction explicitly prevents the blending of high-frequency short-term fluctuations with low-frequency main trends, thereby yielding a more pronounced performance gap.

Ablation study

Comprehensive ablation experiments were conducted on four different wind power datasets to elucidate the specific contributions of each key component in the proposed CrossGNN model. The results are illustrated in Figs. 7 and 8. The experimental results indicate the following:

SimAM attention mechanism

Removing the SimAM attention mechanism led to a noticeable decline in model performance. Across the four datasets, the average MAE increased by 4.4% (from 0.6689 to 0.6986), and the average RMSE increased by 1.1% (from 0.8698 to 0.8793). This impact was most pronounced at Location 2, where the MAE increased by 8.6% (0.5970 → 0.6502) and the RMSE increased by 3.1% (0.7896 → 0.8141). These findings highlight the critical role of SimAM: (a) effectively suppressing high-frequency noise, which is especially relevant for the rapid fluctuations common in wind power data; (b) extracting salient feature information in the frequency domain, enabling the model to capture wind power variation patterns more accurately; and (c) enhancing the model’s adaptability to different wind conditions, thereby improving prediction stability.

Cross-scale module (cross scale)

Removing the cross-scale module results in significant performance degradation. The average MAE across the four datasets increased by 46.6% (from 0.6689 to 0.9806), and the average RMSE increased by 44.7% (from 0.8698 to 1.2587). Location 3 exhibited the largest decline, with the MAE increasing by 65.3% (0.6731 → 1.1124) and the RMSE increasing by 51.1% (0.8779 → 1.3268). This underscores the essential function of the cross-scale module: (a) accurately modeling complex relationships among different temporal scales, ranging from minute-level fluctuations to daily cycles; (b) adapting to the temporal dynamic characteristics of specific datasets, effectively capturing both short-term variations and long-term trends in wind power; and (c) strengthening the model’s ability to discern multi-scale temporal patterns.

Cross-variable module (cross-variable)

After removing the cross-variable module, the average MAE across the four datasets increased by 7.5% (from 0.6689 to 0.7191), and the average RMSE increased by 1.7% (from 0.8698 to 0.8846). The effect was most notable at Location 1, where the MAE increased by 10.3% (0.7496 → 0.8254) and the RMSE increased by 4.0% (0.9306 → 0.9687). These results emphasize the importance of the cross-variable module in (a) effectively modeling the complex interdependencies among various meteorological variables that influence wind power generation; (b) capturing nonlinear interactive effects among multiple factors such as wind speed, wind direction, temperature, and humidity; and (c) improving the model’s ability to leverage complementary information from multiple input features, thus enhancing overall prediction accuracy.

Synergistic effect analysis

Simultaneous removal of both the cross-scale and cross-variable modules caused the most severe performance degradation. The average MAE across the four datasets increased by 40.4% (from 0.6689 to 0.9389), and the average RMSE increased by 32.4% (from 0.8698 to 1.1517). Notably, this decline exceeded the sum of the impacts observed when each module was removed individually, demonstrating a clear synergistic effect. Taking Location 3 as an example, the MAE increased by 47.2% (0.6731 → 0.9909), and the RMSE increased by 37.0% (0.8779 → 1.2026), indicating that (a) integrating multi-scale temporal information with cross-variable relationships is crucial for comprehensively capturing the complexity of wind power dynamics; (b) these modules work synergistically to characterize the underlying physical processes governing wind power generation more fully; and (c) the complementarity between the modules enhances the model’s capacity to represent complex wind power scenarios.

Summary and insights

This detailed ablation experiment provides strong empirical evidence for the core role of each component in the CrossGNN model. The SimAM attention mechanism demonstrated the most significant impact across all datasets, establishing itself as the cornerstone of model performance. The cross-scale and cross-variable modules demonstrated their critical importance in capturing complex temporal dynamics and intervariable relationships, respectively. The varying impacts of these modules across different datasets emphasize their adaptability to various wind power scenarios. The cross-variable module plays the most prominent role in Location 1. The underlying mechanism is that highly volatile wind power generation at this site is heavily driven by sudden, heterogeneous shifts in related meteorological variables (such as the gust speed and dew point). The cross-variable Co-GNN seamlessly disentangled these heterogeneous correlations that independent scalar predictions would miss. In contrast, the cross-scale module contributed most significantly to Location 3, where complex temporal periodicities dictate the data distribution. In this scenario, the adaptive multiscale identifiers ensure that long-term diurnal/seasonal trends and short-term chaotic fluctuations are processed in isolated network spaces, preventing feature degradation that typically plagues standard ablation baselines.

These findings not only confirm the importance of each component for improving wind power prediction accuracy but also provide valuable insights into their dataset-specific contributions, offering important references for customizing model architectures on the basis of target wind power system characteristics. Through the organic combination of the SimAM attention mechanism, cross-scale module, and cross-variable module, the model achieves comprehensive capture of spatiotemporal features across different wind power scenarios, significantly enhancing prediction accuracy and robustness.

Noise robustness analysis

To evaluate the noise robustness of the proposed model, four typical types of noise—Gaussian white noise, spike noise, periodic noise, and impulse noise—are injected into the original multivariate time series data, and the performance of each model under different noise intensities is systematically compared. As shown in Fig. 9, the RMSE‒SNR curves of the MultiGSA model on the Location 1 to Location 4 datasets exhibit the most favorable robustness characteristics. When the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) gradually decreases from 100 dB to 0 dB, the growth rate of MultiGSA’s RMSE is significantly lower than that of the three ablation variants (without the SimAM module, without the cross‑scale module, and without the cross‑variable module). The experimental results indicate that the improvement in noise resistance is attributable not only to the explicit modeling of interactions across scales and variables but also, more critically, to the introduction of the frequency‑enhanced channel attention mechanism. This mechanism can effectively filter out various types of noise interference, enabling the model to maintain accurate modeling capabilities even in complex noisy environments.

Conclusion

In summary, MultiGSA has achieved promising performance in wind power prediction and outperforms selected baseline models in terms of the mean absolute error (MAE) and root mean square error (RMSE) across multiple datasets. Crucially, the extensive evaluations reveal that the model architecture exhibits targeted adaptability: the cross-variable and SimAM modules act as critical stabilizers in high-volatility, noisy environments, whereas the cross-scale module is vital for deciphering complex temporal periodicities. Despite these structural advantages, several limitations are worthy of further research. These limitations include the model’s computational complexity (which may hinder real-time applications), reduced interpretability arising from its intricate structure, potential constraints on generalization ability under completely different geographical or climatic conditions, a focus on short-to-medium-term prediction, and the lack of inherent uncertainty quantification.

Future work will address these challenges through the following approaches: optimizing implementation to reduce computational overhead, integrating explainable AI (XAI) technologies, extending evaluations to more diverse locations, adjusting the architecture to accommodate long-term prediction, incorporating probabilistic prediction capabilities, and exploring the introduction of external variables.

Furthermore, we aim to investigate the potential of MultiGSA in relevant applications, such as solar power generation prediction and power grid stability prediction. By addressing these areas, we strive to further advance wind power prediction technology and contribute to the more efficient and reliable integration of wind energy into global power grids.
