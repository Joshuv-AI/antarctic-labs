Molecular characterisation of extended-Spectrum-Beta-lactamase-producing Klebsiella pneumoniae among children and chicken in the rural Korogwe District, Tanzania
Authors: Joyce R. Mbwana, Thorsten Thye, Ralf Krumkamp, Hagen Frickmann, John P.A. Lusingu, Daniel T.R. Minja, Neyaz A. Khan, Charity Wiafe Akenten, Daniel H. Chercos, Joseph Kaseka, Maike Lamshöft, Samwel Gesase, Anna Jaeger, Jürgen May, Wolfgang R. Streit, Denise Dekker
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Klebsiella pneumoniae expressing Extended-Spectrum-Beta-Lactamases (ESBLs) are a significant global public health challenge, affecting humans and livestock. However, the molecular epidemiology and relatedness between isolates from human and livestock reservoirs in Tanzania have not been well studied. This study aimed to address this knowledge gap by conducting a cross-sectional analysis in the Korogwe District, Tanzania. Klebsiella pneumoniae complex isolates were collected from children with and without diarrhoea and from chickens in commercial and smallholder farms. ESBL-positive isolates were subsequently tested for phenotypic resistance and analysed by whole-genome sequencing. ESBL-positive K. pneumoniae complex isolates were encountered from children with diarrhoea, 14% (n/N = 37/259), and 7% (n/N = 17/258) without diarrhoea. For chicken isolates, 10% (n/N = 38/371) of the commercial and 5% (n/N = 21/406) of the smallholder farms were ESBL-positive. The sequenced ESBL-positive isolates from humans and chickens comprised 73% (n/N = 69/95) K. pneumoniae, 14% (n/N = 13/95) K. quasipneumoniae subsp. quasipneumoniae, 13% (n/N = 12/95) K. quasipneumoniae subsp. similipneumoniae, and 1% (n/N = 1/95) K. quasivariicola. A broad variety of sequence types was observed, with 15 clusters of clonal identity identified, mostly in chickens from the same farm. Our findings also showed 68 isolates with 44 distinct capsular types. blaCTX-M-15 was the most commonly identified ESBL-expressing gene. In conclusion, the study highlights the possibility of interspecies transmission of ESBL-positive K. pneumoniae complex isolates between humans and chickens in the study area, emphasising the need for integrated One Health strategies to mitigate the spread of antimicrobial resistance in Tanzania.

Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is a rapidly growing concern, posing a significant threat to public health. If no action is taken, it is estimated that by 2050, AMR-related infections could result in 10 million deaths annually [1]. Almost half of these deaths are expected to occur in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) [2]. AMR in SSA is believed to stem from limited diagnostic options, widespread empirical treatment, the general overuse of antibiotics in human medicine, livestock farming, and the use of antibiotics without prescriptions.

AMR Enterobacterales, such as those of the Klebsiella pneumoniae complex, have been detected globally, also affecting SSA countries, like Tanzania [3], [4]. Of particular concern is the widespread emergence of Extended-Spectrum-Beta-Lactamase (ESBL)-producing Enterobacterales in the country [5], conferring resistance to penicillins, third-generation cephalosporins (e.g. ceftazidime, cefotaxime, and ceftriaxone) and aztreonam. The ESBL gene blaCTX-M-15, the most widely disseminated gene, was first detected in Tanzanian Enterobacterales as early as the 2000s [6], [7].

Next to human sources, ESBL-and carbapenemase-gene-positive Enterobacterales have also been isolated from livestock and environmental sources in Tanzania [8], [9]. A study conducted in the Neonatal Intensive Care Unit in Mwanza, Tanzania, found that 54.6% of neonates were colonised with ESBL-producing Enterobacterales, of which 67.5% were K. pneumoniae [10]. This is noteworthy because gastrointestinal carriage of ESBL-producing Enterobacterales serves as a reservoir for antimicrobial resistance spread, either due to transmission of resistant bacteria themselves or of mobile genetic elements mediating resistance, and it is strongly associated with endogenous infections, e.g., due to translocation events from the enteric tract to the bloodstream in severely ill individual [2], [11]. In particular, ESBL-positive Klebsiella spp. are among the common causes of neonatal sepsis in SSA [5], [12].

According to a systematic review conducted in 2017 by Flokas et al., the mortality rate in neonates with sepsis caused by ESBL-producing Enterobacterales was twice that of those infected by all other bacterial pathogens combined [13]. Focusing on mortality caused by bacteraemia due to ESBL-positive K. pneumoniae compared to ESBL-negative K. pneumoniae isolates, odds ratios for increased mortality of 1.82, 1.57 and 1.57 for 14-day mortality, 28-or-30-day mortality, and in-hospital mortality, respectively, were recorded in a recently published meta-analysis paper [14]. Nevertheless, in Africa, anthroponotic and zoonotic transmission pathways have hardly been investigated at a molecular level, and therefore, transmission reservoirs or disease outbreaks often remain undetected [15], [16]. Consequently, SSA's picture of ESBL-producing Enterobacterales is incomplete [4], [17]. Poultry, especially chicken and related products, are widely used as a source of proteins in the study setting and are known reservoirs of K. pneumoniae that could foster onward transmission from chicken to humans. However, the degree of transmission and the epidemiological connection between humans and chickens is still not well understood [18], [19]. To bridge the epidemiological knowledge gap regarding AMR in Klebsiella spp. among humans and livestock in Tanzania, we analysed isolates using next-generation sequencing to identify the most common clonal lineages and resistance mechanisms, generating data on circulating strains and potentially identifying possible reservoirs for transmission.

Materials and methods

Study design and data collection

Enrolment of study participants was performed at the Outpatient Department (OPD) and Vaccination Clinic of Korogwe Town Council Hospital (Magunga). The hospital is situated in a rural area of Korogwe District, Tanga Region, northeastern Tanzania. Korogwe District has an estimated human population of 359,421 inhabitants (Tanzania National Bureau of Statistics 2022).

The inclusion criteria for the study were as follows: (i) children aged five years and below with diarrhoea presenting to the hospital OPD, and (ii) children of the same age without diarrhoea receiving care at the vaccination clinic. Only children whose parents or guardians provided written informed consent and were willing to provide faecal samples for the study were enrolled. Diarrhoea was defined as the passing of three loose stools or more per day within the past three days.

Individual faecal samples from chickens were collected at commercial- or small-scale farms within the hospital catchment area of Korogwe District. Commercial farms were defined as those keeping more than 100 livestock within a cage, while small-scale farms were those owned by smallholders and kept less than 50 livestock in their backyard, allowing birds to roam freely. Global Positioning System (GPS) coordinates were recorded for all farms where faecal samples were collected (Fig. 1). Furthermore, demographic, clinical, and laboratory data from children were captured and documented in Research Electronic Data Capture Software (REDCap Version 12.0.19, Vanderbilt University, Nashville, Tennessee) for all participants enrolled in this study.Fig. 1This map shows spatial distribution of faecal samples collected from chickens. Sample collection

Human stool sampling: Approximately 1 g of fresh-formed stool or 1 mL of loose stool was collected in a sterile container from children aged 5 years and below, both with and without diarrhoea.

Chicken faecal sampling: Sample collection was performed by trained field workers. Hygiene practices and the appropriate personal protective equipment were in place at all stages, from sample collection to processing, to prevent contamination. Prior to sample collection, chickens were observed for the following reasons: 1) to ensure fresh stool was collected from the identified chicken; 2) to avoid mixing samples or collecting stool samples twice from the same chicken. During sample collection, a sterile scoop was used to collect individual fresh faecal droppings (1 g) from chickens and to place them into a sterile container. Faecal samples were kept in a cool box with ice packs maintained at 4–8 °C and transported to the microbiology laboratory within 2–4 h of collection for further processing. GPS coordinates were also collected from individual farms.

Bacterial identification and antimicrobial susceptibility testing

For preliminary identification, faecal specimens (human and chicken) were cultured on ESBL chromogenic medium (ESBL Chrome agar orientation, CHROMagar™, France) and incubated at.

35–37 °C for 18–24 h in normal atmospheric conditions. Morphologically mucoid colonies with characteristics for K. pneumoniae based on colour description following the manufacturer's guidelines (not exceeding 3 colonies), were sub-cultured on Columbia agar (Oxoid, UK) with 5% sheep blood. The VITEK2®Compact system (BioMérieux, France), was used for identification and antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) (GN-ID and N214-AST cards) and interpreted following the EUCAST (European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing) clinical breakpoint V_9.0_2019 guidelines. In addition, a double disc diffusion test (DDDT) was performed to confirm ESBL production (MASTDISCS®Combi, Mast Group, UK). Confirmed ESBL-producing strains were subsequently stored at −80 °C using the Microbank System (ProLab Diagnostics, Germany) until further analyses.

Extraction of genomic DNA and whole genome sequencing

Genomic DNA was extracted from pure colonies using the Master Pure Complete DNA and RNA Purification Kit (Lucigen, USA) as per the manufacturer's instructions. Before library preparation, DNA concentration was determined using the Quant-iT dsDNA Assay Kit Q33130 (ThermoFisher, UK), and libraries were sent to BGITech Solutions (Hong Kong, China) for whole-genome sequencing.

Data processing and quality control

Quality of raw sequencing data was checked using the AQUAMIS software v1.2.0, which implements FASTP v0.19.5 for trimming (41), SHOVILL v1.1.0 (https://github.com/tseemann/shovill) for assembly, reference search using MASH v2.2.2, and assembly quality analysis using QUAST v5.0.2 (https://github.com/ablab/quast). In addition, contamination checks of the assembled bacterial genomes were done using CONFINDR v0.7.1 (https://github.com/OLC-Bioinformatics/ConFindr) and CHECKM2 (https://github.com/chklovski/CheckM2).

Phylogeny construction

All 95 assembled Klebsiella genomes were compared on a whole genome scale using core genome MLST (cgMLST) analysis with CHEWIESNAKE (https://gitlab.com/bfr_bioinformatics/chewieSnake) using the allele calling algorithm CHEWBBACA (https://github.com/B-UMMI/chewBBACA). To infer alleles, the KlebsiellacgMLST scheme of the Enterobase database (https://enterobase.warwick.ac.uk) was applied. A maximum likelihood phylogenetic tree was computed using the IQTREE (http://www.iqtree.org) software. Clonal identity between two different isolates was defined as fewer than six allelic differences in the cgMLST assessment.

In silico antimicrobial resistance, plasmid replicon, and virulence factor detection

AMR determinants were identified within the 95 Klebsiella genome assemblies, using the AMRFinderPlus v3.10 (https://github.com/ncbi/amr) software. The presence of virulence factors in the genome assemblies were analysed using ABRICATE v1.0.1 (https://github.com/tseemann/abricate) implementing the VFDB database (https://github.com/tseemann/abricate) and the KLEBORATE software (https://github.com/klebgenomics/Kleborate). Only selected virulence factors were presented, with a particular focus on genes associated with hypervirulent Klebsiella spp. Screening for the presence of plasmids was performed applying the MOB-SUITE software package (https://github.com/phac-nml/mob-suite) using the mob-cluster algorithm, and plasmid detection was performed using the Plasmidfinder database analysed with the ABRICATE software.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were described by frequency and percentage. Continuous variables were described by median and the interquartile range (IQR). Associations between two binary variables were calculated with the prevalence ratio (PR) and the respective 95% confidence interval (CI).

To assess the clustering of infections at the farm level, a bootstrap approach was applied. Repetitively, two samples per household were randomly selected from a bootstrap sample of 100 farms, with the ESBL-producing K. pneumoniae test result in the first sample considered the outcome and the result in the second sample as the exposure. This sampling process was repeated 1000 times, and the PR was estimated on each occasion. The median PR was subsequently reported as a point estimate, while the 2.5% and 97.5% percentiles of the PR distribution delineated the 95% CI. These estimates show the risk of infection if a second sample at the same farm contained ESBL-producing K. pneumoniae. This calculation was performed for both commercial and smallholder farms.

Genotypic characterisation of K. pneumoniae complex

In total, 95 isolates were subjected to whole genome sequencing. Three isolates were lost during sub-culture. The genomic characterisation of K. pneumoniae complex isolates from children and chickens revealed a diverse distribution of sequence types (STs), capsule types (K loci), and antibiotic-resistant genotypes (Fig. 3, Fig. 4). In total, 54 unique STs were identified, of these 27 were from chickens, the most prevalent being ST1040-1LV, which occurred in 10 (n/N = 10/95; 10%) isolates, all from the same farm. Other frequently observed STs included ST17 (n/N = 6/95; 6%), and the three STs, ST469, ST711, and ST3108-1LV with four occurrences each (n/N = 4/95; 4%). The remaining STs showed lower frequencies, with a diverse spectrum of different STs. The predominant ST in K. pneumoniae was ST17, occurring in six isolates (n/N = 6/69; 9%), while all K. quasipneumoniae subsp. quasipneumoniae belonged to ST71040-1LV (n/N = 10/13; 77%).

The phenotypic distribution of sequenced isolates included those from chicken (n/N = 52/95; 55%), diarrhoeal cases (n/N = 28/95; 29%), and healthy controls (n/N = 15/95; 16%). Among the frequently observed STs discussed above, ST3108-1LV, ST1040-1LV, and ST469 were exclusively encountered in chicken, while the rest were observed in both humans and chicken.

Among ESBLs, blaCTX-M-15 was the most frequent gene, detected in 90 isolates (n/N = 90/95; 95%), followed by the ESBL genes blaSHV-2A and blaSHV-2/2A, detected in two and three isolates, respectively, accounting for a total of five isolates (n/N = 5/95; 5%). The most frequent non-ESBL beta-lactamase gene present in 71 isolates (n/N = 71/95; 75%) was blaTEM-1. Another notable beta-lactamase gene included blaSHV-11, found in 33 isolates (n/N = 33/95; 35%).

Novel mutations leading to amino acid substitutions were identified in four beta-lactamase blaSHV alleles: blaSHV-1_S196C (in ST101), blaSHV-11_A243T (in ST2126), blaSHV-110_D59N, (in ST2136) and blaSHV-76_R194H (in ST405). Aminoglycoside resistance genes were also prominent, with aph(3″)-Ib and aph(6)-Id each identified in 70 isolates (n/N = 70/95; 74%). Quinolone resistance was mediated by oqxA, present in 82 isolates (n/N = 82/95; 86%), and oqxB, detected in 50 isolates (n/N = 50/95; 53%). Additionally, the plasmid mediated quinolone resistance gene, qnrS1 was found in 59 isolates (n/N = 59/95; 62%). Fosfomycin (not typically recommended for Enterobacterales) resistance was conferred by fosA, found in 85 isolates (n/N = 85/95; 90%), while sulfonamide resistance was mediated by sul2, identified in 77 isolates (n/N = 77/95; 81%). The most prevalent tetracycline resistance gene was tet(A), found in 65 isolates (n/N = 65/95; 68%). Among trimethoprim resistance genes, dfrA14 was the most common, identified in 63 isolates (n/N = 63/95; 66%).

The distribution of resistance genes between plasmids and the genome revealed notable differences. The blaCTX-M-15 ESBL gene was found with high percentages on plasmids (n/N = 84/90; 93%), with 7% located in the genome (n/N = 6/90), whereas emrD and kdeA, both efflux pump genes, were exclusively located in the genome (n/N = 95/95; 100% each). The sul2 gene was predominantly plasmid-borne (n/N = 69/76; 91%), with the remaining 9% located in the genome (n/N = 7/76, 9%). Similarly, tet(A) was primarily associated with plasmids (n/N = 45/65;69%), while 31% (n/N = 20/65) were genome encoded. Among beta-lactamase genes, blaTEM-1 was predominantly plasmid-borne (n/N = 66/71;93%, plasmid; n/N = 5/71,7%, genome), while blaSHV-11 was mostly located in the genome (n/N = 32/33, 97%), with only one instance detected on a plasmid (n/N = 1/33;3%).

The distribution of resistance genes also varied significantly among bacterial strains. For example, oqxA(RND efflux pump) was detected in 58 instances (n/N = 58/69; 84%), in K. pneumoniae and twelve times in other strains. Similarly, sul2 (sulphonomide-resistant gene) was associated with K. pneumoniae in 53 cases (n/N = 53/69; 76.8%). These findings highlight substantial variation in the localisation and distribution of resistance genes, both within genomic compartments and among bacterial strains.

Phylogenetic analysis and clonal relationship

A phylogenetic tree based on cgMLST alleles of 95 K. pneumoniae complex isolates from chicken and children was constructed (Fig. 4) to assess the clonal relationship within the Klebsiella population isolated in this study. Three major clades with isolates and several singletons were observed. Applying the definition of clonal identity as stated above, there was a total of 15 clusters of clonality ranging between 2 and 10 isolates. The vast majority of nine of these clusters comprised isolates from chicken only (comprising ST34, ST469, ST586-1LV, ST711, ST1040-1LV, ST1128, ST1408, ST2131-1LV, ST3108-1LV), while there were three inter-species clusters comprising isolates from children and chicken (comprising ST17, ST1471-LV and ST2938) as well as three additional clusters comprising specimens collected from humans only (comprising ST405, ST482, ST2136). ST482 and ST2136 were collected from children with and without diarrhoea, whereas ST405 was isolated exclusively from children with diarrhoea. All clusters comprising more than two isolates were from chicken samples. In detail, clusters of three samples were observed for the sequence types ST34, ST469, ST586-1LV, ST711 and ST3108-1LV, respectively, in addition to a big cluster comprising a total of ten ST1040 isolates. As confirmed using the GPS data of the chicken farms, clonal clusters within the same farm were associated with ST1040-1LV (n/N = 10/52), ST586-1LV (n/N = 3/52), ST711 (n/N = 3/52), ST1228 (n/N = 2/52), ST2131-1LV (n/N = 2/52), ST3108-1LV (n/N = 2/52) and ST34 (n/N = 2/52).

Virulence factor analyses

Virulence factor assessment did not indicate the abundance of hypervirulent isolates. In particular, genes commonly associated with hypervirulence in K. pneumoniae [28] like the capsule polysaccharide-associated genes rmpA and rmpA2 as well as siderophores like salmochelin and aerobactin were not recorded (Fig. 3). In a minority of 15 isolates, however, yersiniabactin genes were recorded by Kleborate analysis, comprising the sequence types ST25 (ybt9; ICEKp3), ST348 (ybt14; ICEKp5), two isolates of ST405 (ybt13; ICEKp2), ST513 (ybt18; ICEKp15), 4 isolates of ST711 (ybt15; ICEKp11), ST14 (ybt14; ICEKp5), two isolates of ST45 (ybt10; ICEKp4), 2 isolates of ST1228 (ybt15; ICEKp11) and ST307 (ybt9; ICEKp3), respectively. As shown in Fig. 3, the co-occurrence of the genes fyuA, irp1, and ybtS indicates that yersiniabactin-related genes are associated with both the siderophore biosynthesis pathway and its receptor. In addition, a total of 44 distinct capsular types (K-locus) were identified from 68 K. pneumoniae isolates, with KL42 (15%; n/N = 10/68) being the most frequent. These isolates were exclusively found in chickens, which belonged to the same farm. Interestingly, KL2 capsular type, associated with hypervirulence, was found in three human isolates—two linked to ST25, one from children without diarrhoea and another from a child with diarrhoea. The third KL2 isolate was associated with ST14, also found in a child with diarrhoea.

Discussion

The study was performed to comparatively ascertain the molecular epidemiology of ESBL-positive K. pneumoniae complex isolates from Tanzanian children with and without diarrhoea and from locally bred chickens. The prevalence observed in both human and poultry populations was broadly comparable to previous studies from sub-Saharan Africa [19], [20], [21], although slightly higher detection rates were observed compared to a previous assessment from Ghana [22] and Mwanza, Tanzania. Notably, colonization rates in chicken from commercial farms were slightly higher than those in chicken raised in smallholder settings, suggesting that differences in husbandry practices, stocking densities, or antimicrobial exposure may influence the dissemination of ESBL-producing strains within poultry production systems. Consistent with previous observations [23], ESBL-positive K. pneumoniae carriage was also increased in children with diarrhoea compared to asymptomatic children. Although the cross-sectional design precludes conclusions regarding causality, this finding supports previous evidence indicating that gastrointestinal disturbances may be associated with increased carriage of resistant Enterobacterales [24].

In line with previous reports from Tanzania [3], [6], [25], blaCTX-M-15 was the predominant molecular determinant of ESBL-type resistance from both children and chickens, further highlighting its central role in the epidemiology of ESBL-producing Enterobacterales in East Africa. The widespread occurrence of blaCTX-M-15 across isolates from different hosts may reflect the circulation of common resistance reservoirs and genetic elements within interconnected human and animal populations. In contrast, globally distributed ESBL genes such as blaSHV-2 [26] were only sporadically detected. Instead, blaSHV-110, blaSHV-106, and blaSHV-145 were found in both children and chicken, suggesting that a diverse pool of ESBL-associated genes contributes to resistance dissemination in the study setting. Similarly, high frequencies of the quinolone resistance genes, particularly qnr variant, reported among ESBL-producing Enterobacterales in other Tanzanian studies [27], [28]. The frequent co-occurrence of resistance determinants targeting multiple antimicrobial classes may facilitate the persistence and dissemination of multidrug-resistant strains under diverse antimicrobial selection pressure in both human and animal populations.

The majority of ESBL-associated genes were located on mobile genetic elements, highlighting their potential for horizontal transfer between bacterial lineages and potentially across host populations. Such mobile elements are recognised as major drivers of antimicrobial resistance dissemination within One Health systems, enabling the movement of resistance determinants across ecological boundaries linking humans, animals, and their shared environments. Although the present study did not investigate environmental reservoirs or directly assess gene transfer events, the widespread distribution of mobile resistance determinants among isolates from different hosts underscores the importance of integrated surveillance approaches that consider the interconnected nature of antimicrobial resistance emergence and dissemination.

Analysis of sequence type distribution revealed a genetically diverse K. pneumoniae population. Among the dominant lineages, ST36 are so far lacking from Tanzania, while ST17 and ST711 have been previously linked to sepsis in Tanzanian neonates [10]. The latter finding is in contrast to our study, detecting ST711 isolates exclusively in specimens from chicken. This discrepancy may reflect regional differences in strain circulation, host adaptation, or differences in sampling strategies between studies. In contrast, ST14, ST45, and ST25 have been reported as abundant in Tanzania [27], were only occasionally found in our work. Although ST405 was exclusively detected in two children with diarrhoea, epidemiological information was insufficient to determine whether these isolates represented a localised transmission event. Overall, the presence of both shared and host-associated lineages suggests a complex population structure that cannot be explained solely by host-specific adaptation.

Although genes encoding the siderophore yersiniabactin, commonly found in Enterobacterales [29], [30], [31], were occasionally detected, other virulence genes associated with hypervirulent strains [32] were largely absent, suggesting that hypervirulent lineages were uncommon among ESBL-positive isolates analysed. Nevertheless, substantial diversity in capsule types was observed. Notably, the predominance of capsule type KL42 among chicken isolates from a single farm and their uniform association with ST1040-1LV are suggestive of clonal transmission within that production setting. Such clonal expansion may facilitate the maintenance and persistence of resistant lineages within poultry populations and has previously been linked to the successful spread of antimicrobial-resistant K. pneumoniae [33], [34]. However, the present study was not designed to determine whether such strains are transmitted between poultry and humans. Capsule type KL2, which has been linked to particularly virulent phenotypes, severe human infections, and multidrug resistance (MDR) [35], was only occasionally observed in humans. One KL2 isolate was associated with ST14, a presumed high-risk clone observed in a child with diarrhoea, which has previously been linked to global outbreaks of carbapenem-resistant K. pneumoniae [36].

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the availability of short-read sequencing data restricted the resolution of mobile genetic elements and prevented complete reconstruction and comparison of plasmids and other resistance-associated genetic structures. Future studies incorporating long-read sequencing technologies could provide a more comprehensive understanding of the genetic context and transmission dynamics of resistance determinants. Second, the study was conducted within a restricted geographic area, limiting the generalisability of the findings to other regions of Tanzania. Third, although shared lineages and resistance determinants were identified in children and poultry, the cross-sectional study design did not permit definitive conclusions regarding transmission pathways or directionality between hosts. Finally, environmental reservoirs, including water sources, farm environments, feed, and food-processing interfaces, were not investigated and may represent important components of the local transmission network. Future longitudinal One Health investigations integrating human, animal, and environmental sampling will be essential to better understand the ecological pathways underpinning the dissemination of ESBL-producing K. pneumoniae in Tanzania.

Conclusion

Our findings provide insights into the molecular epidemiology of ESBL-expressing K. pneumoniae isolates from children and chickens in Tanzania. The presence of ESBL-type beta-lactamase lineages and other resistance genes suggests potential interspecies transmission between humans and chickens. This underscores the need for One Health strategies to curb antimicrobial resistance in the region.
