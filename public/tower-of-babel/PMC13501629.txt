The relationship between stroke length, stroke frequency, glide time and performance in swimmers: a biomechanical study
Authors: Barış Baydemi̇r, Tunay Di̇li̇can, Gökhan Çeti̇noğlu, Murat Deni̇z
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe aim of this study was to determine the effects of fundamental swimming technique parameters such as stroke length (SL), stroke frequency (SF), and glide time (GT) on swimming performance in freestyle swimmers and to analyze the relationships among these variables. The study included 12 licensed male swimmers competing in freestyle events. The participants were athletes aged between 18 and 25 years, who trained regularly and had at least three years of competitive swimming experience. The swimmers’ anthropometric characteristics (height, body weight, and body mass index [BMI]) were recorded. In addition, their best 25-meter freestyle times were documented, and the number of strokes (SC), stroke length (SL), stroke frequency (SF), and glide time (GT) were measured using Kinovea, a two-dimensional (2D) motion analysis program. Based on the data obtained in the study, swimming time was found to be significantly and negatively correlated with stroke frequency and glide time and positively correlated with stroke length (p < .05, p < .01). These results indicate that high stroke frequency and effective use of the glide phase are important for achieving faster swim times. However, an increase in stroke length may lead to longer swim times and should therefore be addressed through individualized technical assessment. It is recommended that these technical parameters be developed in a balanced manner within training programs.Keywords: Swimming, Stroke length, Stroke frequency, Glide time

Introduction

In recent years, swimmers and coaches have increasingly adopted a scientific approach to both planning the competitive season and monitoring training. Within this context, numerous studies have investigated various workload monitoring methods to assess physical effort and track athletes’ responses to specific training programs [1]. The primary goal in swimming competitions is to complete a set distance in the shortest possible time [2]. Achieving this depends on average swimming speed, which is defined as the product of stroke length (SL) and stroke frequency (SF). Many studies have demonstrated an inverse relationship between these two parameters, indicating that an increase in SF often corresponds to a decrease in SL [3]. Additionally, both SF and SL have been shown to play important roles in monitoring performance in freestyle events [4]. Gliding in swimming refers to the phase during which the swimmer maintains speed without any propulsive movement, typically occurring after starts, turns, and within stroke cycles. During this phase, swimmers adopt an aerodynamic position characterized by arms extended forward, hands overlapped, and feet together in plantar flexion. Glide performance depends on initial velocity, deceleration rate, and glide duration. According to Newton’s second law, deceleration is determined by resistive forces and the body’s inertia. The sum of body mass and the “added mass” of water displaced forms the “virtual mass.” The lower the resistance and the higher the virtual mass, the greater the glide efficiency. While increasing initial velocity can enhance performance, the primary advantage lies in improving glide efficiency, as this allows speed to be maintained longer without additional metabolic cost [5]. In the existing literature, studies that simultaneously examine glide time (GT), stroke frequency (SF), and stroke length (SL) are relatively limited.

It has also been demonstrated that swimmers with superior performance tend to exhibit enhanced kinematic characteristics, particularly higher SF and longer SL [6]. Specifically, in a recent study of the men’s 50-meter freestyle event (official competition), top-performing swimmers were significantly faster throughout the entire race, largely due to their longer SL [7]. These findings suggest that swimmers who can maintain, or at least minimize the reduction in, SL despite a decrease in SF are more likely to achieve higher swimming speeds.

From a kinematic perspective, swimming speed is determined by SF and SL. Accordingly, improvements in either or both of these parameters result in increased speed. However, developing SL typically requires a long-term and systematic training process. In contrast, a positive correlation between SF and swimming speed has been observed when swimmers perform at maximal effort across distances of 3000 m, 1500 m, 800 m, 400 m, 200 m, 100 m, and 50 m [8]. Therefore, in the short term, increasing SF contributes more significantly to improving swimming speed than enhancing SL [9].

Among the various phases of swimming, the underwater glide phase at the start of a race stands out as a critical determinant of performance. Glide performance is defined as the ability to maintain a certain speed over time while minimizing deceleration. This concept assumes that reducing deceleration during the glide phase increases efficiency and enables the swimmer to cover a longer distance underwater [10]. It is known that thoracic flexion increases water resistance in adult swimmers, whereas positioning the head and shoulders forward enhances glide capacity [5].

The underwater phase of swimming is typically divided into three distinct components: the glide phase, which begins when the toes enter the water and ends with the initiation of the first propulsive leg movement; the leg kick phase, which lasts from the start of leg propulsion to the beginning of the first underwater arm stroke; and the swimming phase, which continues from the first underwater stroke until the swimmer’s head reaches the 15-meter mark [11]. Each of these phases contains technical elements that directly influence the swimmer’s hydrodynamic efficiency and overall underwater performance. Body positioning during the glide phase, the ability to overcome water resistance, and fluid transitions between movements can make a significant contribution to total race time. Therefore, accurate analysis of these phases considering individual differences and targeted training focused on improving them are essential for enhancing swimming performance.

The main objective of this study is to examine the relationship between the kinematic characteristics exhibited by elite swimmers during the underwater phases specifically glide time, leg kick time, and swimming time and overall performance. Additionally, the study aims to investigate the combined influence of surface kinematic variables, such as stroke frequency and stroke length, on performance when evaluated in conjunction with these underwater phases. The findings are expected to contribute to a more comprehensive understanding of the multidimensional kinematic components that determine swimming performance, while also providing practical insights to help coaches and sports scientists optimize training strategies.

Method

Study design

This study was conducted using a correlational survey design within the framework of quantitative research methods. The primary aim was to examine the relationships between technical parameters that may influence freestyle swimming performance namely stroke frequency (SF), stroke length (SL), glide time (GT), and stroke count (SC) and swimming time. Data were obtained through technical performance assessments of the swimmers. Ethical approval for the study was granted by the Çanakkale Onsekiz Mart University Health Sciences Ethics Committee (Decision No. 19/21, dated 28 May 2025).

Participants

This study involved 12 elite male freestyle swimmers aged between 18 and 25 years, each with a minimum of five years of competitive experience. All athletes were actively training and competing in local and national swimming competitions in Türkiye at the time of data collection. In order to evaluate the adequacy of the sample size, an a priori power analysis was conducted using G*Power software (version 3.1). Assuming a medium effect size (r = .50), α = 0.05, and a desired statistical power of 0.80, the minimum required sample size was determined as 11 participants. With 12 swimmers included, the sample size was considered sufficient for the primary analyses. Prior to participation, all individuals were fully informed about the purpose, procedures, and potential risks of the study and voluntarily signed an informed consent form. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Participant anonymity was strictly maintained, and all data collected were used exclusively for scientific research purposes.

Data collection tools

Anthropometric measurements

Anthropometric measurements of the swimmers were taken using a Dikomsan BW 200 scale to assess height and body weight. All measurements were conducted in the morning, with participants barefoot and in a fasted state. In addition to basic anthropometric data (height, body weight, and BMI), stroke length (SL), stroke frequency (SF), and glide time (GT) were obtained through frame-by-frame video analysis using Kinovea motion analysis software. Although specific anthropometric measures such as arm span, hand, and foot length were not directly recorded, these kinematic variables provided performance-related indicators of swimming efficiency.

Determination of swimming time

Swimming times were measured in a 25-meter semi-Olympic pool. Each participant was instructed to swim 25 m using the freestyle technique, starting from the starting block. The entire swim was recorded from both front and side views using high-resolution digital video cameras (minimum 60 frames per second).

Biomechanical image analysis

A two-dimensional (2D) motion analysis technique was employed to evaluate swimming performance. Video recordings were analyzed using Kinovea 0.9.5, an open-source software widely used in biomechanical research [12]. The software enabled the extraction of performance parameters such as movement time, stroke time, glide time, distance, joint angles, and stroke frequency.

Calculation of stroke frequency

Stroke count was defined as the total number of arm pulls executed by each swimmer from the start to the finish of the 25-meter freestyle swim. These measurements were obtained from the video footage using Kinovea’s 2D motion analysis tools. A frame-by-frame analysis approach was applied to ensure accuracy, and the total stroke count for each participant was recorded [11]. Calculation of glide time

Glide time is defined as the time interval during which the swimmer glides on the water without pulling their arms after each stroke. This time was determined from video recordings, and analyses were performed using the 2D motion analysis software Kinovea with frame based timing. In the measurement, the time between the end of each stroke and the start of the next stroke was recorded, and the average glide time was calculated. Data analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using the SPSS version 25.0 software package (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The distribution of the data was tested for normality, and descriptive statistics (mean and standard deviation) were calculated. Since the variables did not meet the assumption of normality, the relationships between performance and stroke parameters were examined using Spearman’s rank correlation analysis. In order to evaluate the adequacy of the sample size, an a priori power analysis was conducted using G*Power software (version 3.1). Stroke length (SL), stroke frequency (SF), and glide time (GT) were obtained through video recordings and analyzed using the Kinovea (version 0.8.15) motion analysis software.

Discussion

This study was conducted to investigate the effects of fundamental swimming technique parameters namely stroke length (SL), stroke frequency (SF), and glide time (GT) on freestyle swimming performance and to examine the relationships among these variables. The findings revealed a strong negative correlation between stroke frequency and swimming time, a strong negative correlation between glide time and swimming time, and a positive correlation between stroke length and swimming time. These relationships indicate that swimming performance is closely associated with technical parameters. The strong negative correlation between stroke frequency and swimming time suggests that swimmers with higher stroke frequency tend to complete the distance in a shorter time. In other words, swimmers who perform more strokes per unit of time can achieve faster performance. This implies that a higher stroke frequency may contribute positively to sprint performance, where time efficiency is critical. The negative relationship between glide time and swimming time is also noteworthy. Swimmers who sustain longer glide phases tend to have shorter overall swimming times, indicating that efficient utilization of the glide phase offers a performance advantage. Since glide time reflects the swimmer’s ability to move through the water with minimal resistance and maximum propulsion, its effective use serves as a key indicator of technical proficiency. In contrast, a positive and significant correlation was found between stroke length and swimming time. This result suggests that as stroke length increases, swimming time also increases implying that longer strokes, when not executed efficiently, may negatively impact performance. The fact that stroke length is more closely associated with increased time than with efficiency highlights the need for a more nuanced technical evaluation of this variable. Taken together, these findings underscore that swimming performance is influenced not only by physical conditioning but also by the optimal balance of technical elements, particularly stroke frequency, glide time, and stroke length.

Swimmers may experience misalignments along their longitudinal axis while attempting to generate high propulsive forces. This misalignment can increase the body’s surface area, resulting in greater drag forces. Consequently, even if swimmers produce higher propulsive forces, their swimming speed may decrease due to the corresponding increase in resistance. During swimming, the swimmer’s body is subject to alternating phases of acceleration and deceleration, which leads to fluctuations in swimming velocity. Swimming speed is characterized by a cyclic pattern of acceleration, driven by the net balance between thrust (i.e., propulsive force) and drag forces acting on the swimmer’s body [13]. Another study found that swimmers can achieve higher speeds when they are able to minimize resistance forces those that hinder forward motion while simultaneously generating sufficient propulsion [14]. Research has also shown that the amount of fluid accelerated during propulsive movements is influenced by body shape and water flow patterns around the swimmer. Therefore, heavier swimmers require greater propulsive forces to overcome inertia and move their additional mass. A significant relationship has been observed between propulsive force and swimmers’ anthropometric characteristics [15]. Moreover, several studies have reported that swimming performance is directly related to the pitch (forward/backward tilt) and sweepback (rearward angle) of the hands. These angles significantly affect the magnitude and direction of the propulsive force generated by the swimmer’s upper limbs [16, 17]. In one study, propulsive force was not evaluated merely as the force exerted by the hands, but rather as a deliberate action aimed at propelling the body forward. Accordingly, the duration of propulsive force was defined as the combined time of the pull and push phases, whereas the non-propulsive phase included the entry, catch, hand lag, recovery, and release stages [18].

To assess flutter kick power, swimmers were pulled by an electromechanical motor at six different speeds. Results showed that leg power output decreased as towing speed increased [19]. This finding suggests that the power-generating capacity of the legs diminishes at speeds exceeding the swimmer’s maximal flutter kick velocity. The paradoxical nature of these results has been linked to physiological and biomechanical adaptations that occur during freestyle swimming, including inter-limb coordination, energy efficiency, and chain-of-movement optimization [20]. Furthermore, it was noted that the propulsive effect of the flutter kick, which is believed to be especially important at higher speeds, becomes more pronounced as swimming velocity increases potentially activating hydrodynamic mechanisms that are not observable at maximal kick speed.

This study demonstrates that freestyle swimming performance depends not only on physical capacity but also on the balanced application of technical parameters such as stroke frequency (SF), stroke length (SL), and glide time (GT). It was found that SF and GT are effective in reducing swim time, whereas SL may negatively impact performance if not executed efficiently. Moreover, the relationships between propulsive force generation, resistance management, and anthropometric characteristics highlight the importance of multidimensional biomechanical and physiological factors in determining swimming performance. In conclusion, training programs should prioritize not only the development of physical strength but also the optimization of technical skills tailored to individual characteristics. In particular, the effective execution of flutter kicks at high speeds and detailed analysis of underwater phases are considered critical for enhancing competitive performance.
