Graft survival and rejection with repeated human leukocyte antigen (HLA) mismatch in kidney transplantation: a retrospective multicentre cohort study protocol
Authors: Henry Guo, William Mulley, Narelle Watson, Rhonda Holdsworth, Anne Taverniti, Jon Downing, Alycia Thornton, Lucy Sullivan, Fiona Hudson, Christopher E Davies, Ryan Gately, Armando Teixeira-Pinto, Germaine Wong, John Kanellis, Wai Lim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCandidates awaiting repeat kidney transplantation face substantial immunological barriers due to prior human leukocyte antigen (HLA) allo-sensitisation. Re-exposure to previously mismatched HLA antigens may increase the risk of acute rejection and premature graft loss. Although avoidance of repeat HLA mismatches (RMM) has been standard practice, emerging evidence suggests this risk varies according to the specific HLA allelic RMM and anti-HLA antibody profile to the RMM. Here we describe the protocol for a retrospective registry-based cohort study that aims to determine the differences in graft outcome between recipients who received kidney transplants with and without RMM, and to examine the immunogenicity of RMM that are associated with allo-sensitisation.MethodsUsing linked data between the Australia and New Zealand Dialysis and Transplant Registry (ANZDATA) and Australian organ waitlisting and matching system (2012–2023), we will evaluate the association between Class I and II HLA RMM and the risk of acute rejection and graft loss using Cox regression and competing risk models in recipients who have received repeat kidney transplants in Australia. Epitope analysis of each RMM will be examined.DiscussionThis study will improve understanding of the impact of RMM on kidney transplant outcomes and identify higher immunological risk RMM associated with poorer graft outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12882-026-05122-6.

Introduction

Kidney transplantation is the preferred treatment for patients with end-stage kidney disease (ESKD), offering superior survival and quality of life compared to maintenance dialysis [1–3]. Although short- and medium-term graft survival have improved substantially over recent decades, corresponding improvements in longer-term graft survival have been more modest [4]. In Australia, kidney graft half-life ranges between 12 and 15 years, with estimated 10-year graft survival of 60–65% [5]. Consequently, a proportion of patients will require repeat transplantation in their lifetime. In 2024, 13% of all kidney transplants in Australia were repeat transplants, with comparable proportions reported in the United States (10%) and the United Kingdom (16%) [5–7].

Patients waiting for a repeat transplant present significant immunological challenges due to prior human leukocyte antigen (HLA) allo-sensitisation, which limits donor compatibility and therefore reduces retransplant potential. These patients typically have an extended wait time on dialysis compared to unsensitised patients, because of the difficulty in finding an immunologically compatible kidney for transplantation. In Australia, 1 in 13 patients on the deceased donor transplant waiting list in 2024 was highly sensitised, with a measured panel reactive antibody (mPRA) of at least 99%, indicating that no more than 1% of the donor kidney pool may be suitable for transplantation with average waiting times approximately twofold longer than unsensitised patients [8]. Data from the United Kingdom reported a median waiting time of approximately six years for highly sensitised kidney transplant candidates, with similarly prolonged wait time observed in the United States despite allocation prioritisation of these candidates [9, 10].

There is a general reluctance to allocate and accept a donor kidney with a repeated HLA mismatch (RMM) because of concerns that re-exposure to the mismatched HLA could potentially evoke an anamnestic alloimmune T- and B- cell response, leading to severe early antibody mediated rejection (AMR) and premature graft loss [11, 12]. The outcome of kidney transplantation across RMM remains unclear, with some studies, particularly older registry analyses, demonstrating inferior graft survival in recipients with Class I RMM, while others, including more contemporary and better HLA-characterised cohorts, report no adverse impacts of RMM on allograft outcomes [12–18]. However, these studies were limited by an inconsistent definition and assignment of RMM as unacceptable antigen(s), lack of high-resolution HLA typing and reporting of donor-specific anti-HLA antibody (DSA) to the RMM, thereby precluding an accurate assessment of the association between RMM and graft outcome [18, 19]. With the advances in HLA typing methods to enable detailed epitope or molecular mismatch analysis in kidney transplantation, it may now be possible to establish more accurately the immunogenicity of each RMM and the association with graft outcome. In Australia, there is no consensus among HLA laboratories in the assignment of RMM as unacceptable or acceptable HLA antigens in those with prior failed grafts, with almost all RMM assigned as unacceptable antigen in those relisted for repeat transplantation. A greater understanding of the immunogenicity and effect on graft outcome of RMM would assist clinicians and HLA laboratories in the assignment of RMM, potentially informing the approach in allocating kidneys with RMM in those with prior failed grafts.

The overarching objective of this study is to examine differences in allo-sensitisation and graft outcomes between patients who have received a repeat kidney transplant with or without RMM. Methods/design

Study design

The study cohort will comprise adult patients aged 18 years or older with a prior failed kidney-only transplant who received a subsequent kidney-only live or deceased donor transplant between 1st January 2012 and 31st December 2023 (Fig. 1). This study will be conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethics was approved by Sir Charles Gairdner Hospital Human Research Ethics Committee (PRN: RGS0000007519), with a waiver-of-consent granted for the conduct of this study. Reciprocal approval for participating Australian transplant centres is included in this ethics application (Supplementary S1). Recipients of failed multi-organ transplants and those who have received repeat multi-organ transplants (that may include kidney) will be excluded. The conduct and reporting of this study will adhere to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines [20].Fig. 1Overview of study design

Overview of study design

Data source

Deidentified data of this cohort will be extracted from the Australia and New Zealand Dialysis and Transplant Registry (ANZDATA), Australia and New Zealand Organ Donation Registry (ANZOD) and National Organ Matching System (NOMS)/ OrganMatch System (OMS) (Table 1). ANZDATA is a binational registry established in 1977 that prospectively collects pre-specified demographic, clinical, and outcome data for all patients receiving kidney replacement therapy, including dialysis and kidney transplantation, across Australia and New Zealand. Written consent for inclusion in ANZDATA was required between 1977 and 2002, after which an opt-out consent model was adopted. The consent allows for reporting of patient data, including health outcomes to ANZDATA, but also for the use of de-identified aggregate data for research. ANZOD is a binational registry, established in 1989, that collects demographic, clinical and outcome data for all patients who underwent organ donation across Australia and New Zealand. Histocompatibility, immunological and allocation data will be obtained from OMS, a web-based national platform used to facilitate deceased donor kidney allocation in Australia. OMS also contains data relating to donor and recipient HLA typing and pre- and post-transplant de novo donor-specific anti-HLA antibody (dnDSA) for both live and deceased donor kidney transplants, including historical data migrated from its predecessor, NOMS. Together ANZOD and OMS provide complementary donor and transplant-related data to ANZDATA.

Data flow, storage, and confidentiality

Linkage of ANZDATA, ANZOD and NOMS/OMS will be undertaken by a nominated ANZDATA biostatistician, with a deidentified dataset provided to the investigators. Each participant can only be identified by a project-specific unique identifier, which is not re-identifiable. Updated data on rejection episodes and dnDSA up to 31st December 2025 will be obtained from each participating Australian transplant centre using pre-specified data fields, with these extra data provided back to ANZDATA for merging with the deidentified master dataset. The master dataset will be stored on secure, password-protected institutional servers with access restricted to authorised investigators. Data will be retained and destroyed in accordance with local data governance requirements.

Data variables

Donor, recipient, transplant and immunological data will be obtained from ANZDATA, ANZOD and OMS, with missing or additional data requested from participating transplant centres. Table 1 shows the data type and sources, including repeated data at pre-specified timepoints.

HLA typing

Molecular HLA typing for both donors and recipients will be available for all transplants, although high resolution 2-field HLA typing may not be available for all donor/recipient pairs. Even though deceased donor HLA typing is not typically undertaken using high-resolution methods at time of deceased donor kidney allocation, a confirmatory follow-up HLA typing using next-generation sequencing (NGS) is often completed after transplantation and these results are recorded in NOMS/OMS. NGS-based typing was progressively implemented across Australian tissue typing laboratories from approximately 2015–2016, with broad adoption by 2018. A description of the HLA typing methods used by each participating HLA laboratory and the proportion of donor/recipient pairs with complete high-resolution typing by era are described in Supplementary Table 1.

Donor and recipient are typed at all HLA loci of HLA-A, -B, -C, -DRB1, -DRB3/4/5, -DQA1, -DQB1, -DPA1, and -DPB1. For kidney transplants with RMM, high resolution HLA typing data of donor/recipient pairs without recorded high resolution typing in NOMS/OMS will be requested from each state’s HLA laboratory where possible. For kidney transplants without RMM, imputation using population HLA allele frequencies in those without high resolution 2-field HLA typing will be undertaken by experienced HLA scientists [21]. For the epitope-level and PIRCHE-II analysis, only recipients with complete high-resolution donor and recipient typing will be included, given the potential inaccuracy of HLA imputation for rare alleles or less common loci [22]. The proportion of pairs with complete 2-field typing and a comparison of characteristics between included and excluded pairs will be reported to assess generalisability.

Donor-specific anti-HLA antibody testing

The presence of anti-HLA antibodies is identified using a solid-phase assay, with antibody specificity identified using a single HLA molecule target across all HLA loci of HLA-A, -B, -C, -DRB1, -DRB3/4/5, -DPA1, -DPB1, -DQA1 and -DQB1 (single antigen bead [SAB] assay). In Australia, the SAB assays used are either One Lambda Inc. (OLI) or Immucor assays, with a positive test defined as antibody with mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of at least 500 or 750, respectively. This MFI threshold was chosen because prior cohort studies have shown that pre-transplant DSA with MFI of at least 500 MFI was associated with an increased rejection risk [23]. This threshold aligns with standard interpretation criteria for OLI assays [24]. Screening for dnDSA is typically undertaken either routinely and/or for indication purposes, according to standard local laboratory practices.

Exposure

The exposure is RMM, defined as mismatched HLA to a prior failed graft, irrespective of the DSA status (to the RMM). The mismatch to prior donor HLA will be determined at the antigen level. Serological broad antigens and their recognised splits will be treated as equivalent, such that broad-to-split or split-to-broad comparisons will not generate spurious mismatches. This approach is consistent with standard Australian registry conventions for antigen-level HLA mismatch reporting. The comparator group comprises repeat kidney transplants without RMM. Two sensitivity analyses will be undertaken. Firstly, RMM will be defined as mismatched HLA at the allele level. Secondly, RMM will be restricted to recipients with documented pre-transplant donor-specific antibodies directed against the repeated mismatch antigens (MFI ≥ 500 (OLI) or ≥ 750 (Immucor)), to evaluate effect modification by preexisting antibody sensitisation. This second sensitivity analysis will be clearly distinguished from the primary analysis in all reporting.

Outcome measures

The primary outcomes are all-cause and death-censored graft loss. All-cause graft loss is defined as the return to maintenance dialysis or repeat transplantation after graft loss or death with a functioning graft; whereas death-censored graft loss is defined as irreversible graft loss requiring return to dialysis or repeat transplantation but excluding death with a functioning graft.

Secondary outcomes include cause-specific graft loss, allo-sensitisation, biopsy-proven acute rejection (BPAR), and specific rejection subtypes (AMR and transplant glomerulopathy). Cause-specific graft loss is defined according to the pre-specified causes of graft loss in ANZDATA, and for this study, we will focus on rejection- or chronic allograft nephropathy-related graft loss. Allo-sensitisation is defined as the development of dnDSA with MFI of at least 500 (OLI) or 750 (Immucor) to the repeated graft (donor antigen). Each episode of BPAR is reported to ANZDATA by each site, with details of Banff classification recorded for each episode (from 2019). Each episode will be categorised as acute, chronic, or acute-on-chronic, and further classified as cellular, vascular, AMR, or mixed rejection. Severe rejection is refined as the presence of either AMR or vascular rejection. For mixed rejection episodes, the presence of AMR or vascular rejection will be considered the dominant or co-dominant lesion. Transplant glomerulopathy (Banff “cg” score of 1–3) will be coded according to Banff criteria and considered a morphological manifestation of chronic AMR (cAMR). For rejection episodes without or incomplete Banff classification, the responsible transplant centre will be requested to update the data where possible (for transplants with RMM).

T and B cell epitope analysis

Detailed epitope analysis will be undertaken for transplantation across RMM, focusing on both B- and T-cell epitope incompatibility. Eplets, defined as small clusters of polymorphic amino acids critical for antibody recognition, will be quantified using HLA Eplet Mismatch Calculator embedded within the International HLA Eplet Registry (http://www.epregistry.com.br). The eplet table version and date of access will be recorded and reported to ensure reproducibility.

Higher eplet mismatch loads have been consistently associated with increased risks of allo-sensitisation, transplant glomerulopathy, AMR, and graft loss [25–29], although clinically relevant thresholds remain uncertain [29, 30]. Importantly, individual eplet mismatches may not be equally immunogenic [31, 32], and thus results of eplet mismatch analysis will be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than as definitive measures of immunogenicity.

T-cell epitope incompatibility will be assessed using the PIRCHE-II algorithm, which estimates indirect CD4+ T-cell alloreactivity. Higher PIRCHE-II scores have been associated with increased risks of dnDSA formation, AMR, and graft loss, and may provide complementary prognostic information to eplet mismatch load for graft outcome [33–35].

Statistical methods

Sample size calculation

In the Collaborative Transplant Study, among 6,711 recipients of a second kidney transplant, 1438 patients (21%) had at least one repeated HLA mismatch, including 485 (7%) with repeated HLA-DR mismatches [36]. The association between the presence of RMM and graft loss was confined only to recipients with HLA-DR RMM, with the adjusted hazard ratio (HR) for graft loss within the first-year post-transplant of 1.61 (95% confidence intervals [CI] 1.16–2.23) in those with repeat HLA-DR RMM compared to those without. According to ANZDATA, there were 2175 patients who received a repeat kidney transplant in the period between 2012 and 2023 [5], with approximately 10% reported to have RMM. Using these parameters, with a 30% graft-loss rate in non-RMM recipients, and two-sided alpha of 0.05 with 80% power, the study has adequate power to detect a minimum HR of 1.45 for all-cause graft loss between those with and without RMM.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics will be summarised between kidney transplant recipients with and without RMM. Continuous variables will be reported as means with standard deviations or medians with interquartile ranges, and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. Group comparisons will be performed using chi-square, ANOVA, or Mann–Whitney U tests, as appropriate.

The associations between second graft outcomes with and without RMM will be assessed using Cox regression and competing risk modelling. The directed acyclic graph of the relationships between exposure, confounders and potential mediators are shown in Fig. 2. Confounders are selected a priori based on established biological relationships with graft outcomes, which will include donor and recipient factors, but also the presence of pre-transplant DSA, HLA mismatches and changes in immunosuppression (as time-varying covariates of the use of prednisolone, calcineurin inhibitor of cyclosporine or tacrolimus, anti-metabolite of mycophenolic acid or azathioprine, and mammalian target of rapamycin inhibitor at pre-specified timepoints [i.e. time 0, 3, 6, 12, 24 and 36 months post-transplant]). Cox regression modelling will be constructed to examine the associations between exposure and all-cause and death-censored graft loss. A Fine-Gray sub-distribution hazard model, with death as the competing event, will be reported as a sensitivity analysis for death-censored graft loss.Fig. 2Directed acyclic graph (DAG)

The proportional hazard assumptions of these models will be checked graphically by plotting the Schoenfeld residuals. The estimates of the Cox regression models will be reported as HR and 95% confidence intervals (95%CI) and the estimates of the competing risk models reported as sub-distribution HR with 95%CI. A general multiple mediation analysis based on the counterfactual framework will be used to examine the effects of the potential mediators (induction treatment and rejection) on the association between the exposure groups and graft loss, with the estimated total effect (TE), direct effect (DE), and indirect effect (IE) of the mediators reported as log hazards with 95% confidence interval (95% CI). A p-value of ≤0.05 will be considered statistically significant [37, 38]. Two sensitivity analyses will be undertaken. First, we will apply inverse probability weighting (IPW) to balance baseline covariates across the exposure groups, with propensity scores constructed using baseline covariates selected as the main models. The individual recipient with and without RMM will be weighted by the inverse probability of the observed exposure groups. Mean standardised differences before and after weighting will be evaluated between groups. Second, the models for graft outcomes will be reconstructed following the reclassification of RMM at the allele level.

The associations between RMM and secondary outcomes of cause-specific graft loss, first BPAR, first AMR, development of dnDSA, and development of transplant glomerulopathy will be examined using cause-specific Cox proportional hazards regression, with recipients censored at graft loss or death. For the epitope analysis, each RMM will be evaluated to determine the total number of eplet mismatch load, number of antibody-verified eplet mismatches and PIRCHE-II score. In recipients with RMM who developed allo-sensitisation to the RMM antigen post-transplant, differences in the eplet and PIRCHE-II scores will be compared to the RMM without allo-sensitisation.

All analyses will be conducted using Stata (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA) and R (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Discussion

This study will add to the current understanding of the clinical impact of RMM in determining kidney transplant outcomes and establish whether there are specific immunological risk profiles that can identify the RMM at risk of allo-sensitisation.

Several limitations warrant acknowledgement. Centre variation in the surveillance of dnDSA presents a potential ascertainment bias, which must be carefully considered when interpreting the outcome data of dnDSA. Where HLA-DQA1 and HLA-DP typing is unavailable for first donors, undetected repeat mismatches at those loci cannot be excluded. Finally, eplet mismatch and PIRCHE-II analyses should be considered exploratory, owing to the equivalence of eplets to immunogenic epitopes lacking empirical evidence at this point in time, and incomplete 2-field allele-level typing across the entire cohort.

Nonetheless, future work should focus on prospective validation of epitope-informed unacceptable antigen assignment of RMM, which could improve equity and efficiency within the national allocation systems for this disadvantaged population. Our study has the potential to inform and help guide HLA laboratories and clinicians in establishing policies in the assignment of RMM for repeat transplantation.
