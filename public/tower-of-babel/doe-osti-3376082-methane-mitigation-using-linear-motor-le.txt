EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
GTI Energy (GTI), in collaboration with The University of Texas at Austin - Center for
Electromechanics (UT‑CEM), designed, fabricated, and tested a linear motor–driven
compressor intended to mitigate methane emissions at natural gas facilities across
transmission, storage, gathering, and processing sectors of the gas value chain. The
technology was developed to capture low-pressure vented methane from multiple
emission sources—including compressor seals/rod packing vents, pneumatic device
vents, and planned operational events such as blowdowns—and compress the
recovered gas back to pipeline pressure, thereby reducing emissions while retaining
product value.
Methane mitigation at compressor stations is challenging because a meaningful fraction
of emissions can originate from components and operating events that are intermittent,
distributed, or difficult to route to conventional recovery systems (e.g., blowdowns and
certain vented sources). Compressor rod packing vents, pneumatic devices, and
blowdowns among key contributors to lost gas that would be addressed by this solution.
The project included dynamic simulation and modeling, preliminary and detailed design,
prototype fabrication and integration into a test skid, and commissioning and testing
activities. Quarterly reporting documented that the compressor was fully assembled,
installed, commissioned, and tested using nitrogen, before subsequently being tested
with natural gas. Testing focused on the flow control required to maintain attached vent
systems near atmospheric pressure, while demonstrating that the compressor could
also reach midstream discharge pressures between 1,000 to 1,500 psig.
Economic feasibility was also treated as a core requirement throughout the project. The
system economics are highly dependent on the site operating conditions, specifically
emphasizing compressor utilization and discharge pressure as key drivers of payback
and capital cost.
Overall, the development was successful, and a promising prototype was designed and
tested. However, the compressor still requires additional testing and improvement to
fully validate that the performance can translate to the field.

INTRODUCTION

Background
Methane is the primary component of natural gas and a valuable energy source, making
mitigation of methane emissions a national priority with economic and public benefit
implications. Annually, an estimated ~7,600 kilotons of methane are lost during oil and
gas production. [Reference 1] Assuming a Henry hub price of $3, that is $1.2 billion of
natural gas lost annually. Studies of these emissions indicate that they are
concentrated in a few locations that include oil production (18%), gas production (42%),
and gas transmission and storage facilities (19%). [Reference 1]
Common methane sources relevant to this project include:
 • Compressor seals, rod packing and wet/dry gas seals, are found on every
 compressor from production to transmission and storage. These seals leak
 continuously under normal operation and increase leakage as components wear.
 Estimates of the total impact vary, but most sources agree that they make up a
 large fraction of the total emissions found across the value chain.
 • Pneumatic controllers and other gas-actuated devices are used widely in
 production, gathering/boosting, and transmission/storage settings, which can
 vent methane during continuous bleed operation or actuation. These devices are
 considered by many to be one of the largest sources of emission due to their
 widespread use.
 • Blowdowns and maintenance-related venting, where pressurized gas is
 released for safety and operational reasons. Safety related blowdowns are
 difficult if not impossible to recover; however, maintenance related blowdowns
 can be recovered and compressed downstream if planned for ahead of time.
There are a number of existing solutions to these emissions; however, they are often
expensive, are designed to address one emission source, and may only offer an
emission reduction rather than complete mitigation. For example, the best
recommendation for rod packing is simply maintenance. Dry gas seals are a
replacement for wet seals but still emit gas. Even other vapor recovery compressors
have a limited discharge pressure, so they are only suitable at low pressure sites or
must deliver the gas to the fuel system of a nearby engine or generator, assuming it is
running.
In place of these partial solutions, the team proposed a central methane recovery utility
where all emitted methane can be captured and compressed back to pipeline pressure
for complete recovery. This simple, direct solution allows sites to keep all their existing
equipment, recovers all emissions directed to the recovery compressor, and has zero
impact on the site equipment as the emissions are compressed back into the pipeline,
rather than into an engine or other key piece of equipment.
How This Project Advances the State of Practice

 Page 4

This project accomplished the proposed approach using a linear motor compressor
architecture intended to provide (1) broad operating flexibility, (2) rapid controllability to
match dynamic leak rates, (3) high discharge pressures suitable for reinjection into
pipeline systems, and (4) a sealed, oil-free design intended to reduce maintenance
burdens and avoid introducing new leak points. This specialized compressor was
integrated into a leak recovery system that would only require power, connection of all
vent systems to the inlet buffer vessel, and a discharge connection to the pipeline or
storage system. With these simple site modifications, the recovery compressor would
automatically maintain the connected vent systems near atmospheric pressure,
regardless of the vent gas flow rates, ensuring that all connected equipment still worked
as intended and that all vented emissions could be safely and efficiently recovered.

Project objectives
The objective of this project was to design, build, and test a high-pressure methane leak
recovery compressor capable of cost-effectively capturing and recovering methane
emissions from natural gas facilities across the transmission, storage, gathering, and
processing segments of the natural gas value chain. The technology was intended to
address methane emissions that are typically vented to atmosphere during normal
operations or maintenance activities, including emissions from compressor seal
systems, pneumatic devices, and planned operational events such as blowdowns.
A core motivation for this effort was the lack of a commercially viable, purpose-built
solution capable of recovering low-pressure, variable-flow methane emissions and
returning that gas directly to high-pressure pipeline systems without disrupting normal
facility operations.
Primary Technical Objectives
The primary technical objective of this project was to develop and demonstrate that the
linear motor–driven methane leak recovery compressor could operate over the wide
range of pressures and flow conditions characteristic of vented methane emissions at
natural gas facilities. The compressor was required to accept methane at
near-atmospheric inlet pressures and compress that gas to high discharge pressures
representative of transmission, storage, gathering, and processing systems. Achieving
this pressure ratio within a single, integrated recovery system was essential to enable
direct reinjection of recovered methane without intermediate handling or modification of
existing facility equipment.
A second objective was to demonstrate precise and reliable control of compressor
operation to support safe integration with vented emission sources. Many methane
leaks and vents originate from systems that are not designed to tolerate backpressure,
and therefore the recovery compressor needed to dynamically match incoming flow
while maintaining inlet pressures within a narrow, safe operating range. The project
sought to show that a linear motor–driven compressor, coupled with appropriate control
logic, could respond to changing inlet conditions in real time without pressurizing
upstream equipment or disrupting normal facility operations.

 Page 5

In addition to pressure control, the project aimed to demonstrate operational flexibility
across highly variable emission profiles. Methane emissions at natural gas facilities can
range from small, continuous leaks to large, short-duration releases associated with
planned operational events such as blowdowns. The compressor system was therefore
designed to operate efficiently over a broad turndown range, allowing it to ramp output
up or down as needed without hardware changes or manual intervention. This flexibility
was a critical requirement for enabling recovery of emissions from multiple sources
using a single, centralized system.
Another key technical objective was to minimize maintenance requirements and
secondary emissions through careful system design. The project pursued a sealed,
oil-free compressor architecture intended to reduce wear, eliminate the need for
lubricating oil, and avoid introducing additional fugitive emission points. By limiting the
number of moving parts and simplifying mechanical interfaces, the design sought to
improve reliability and reduce lifecycle operating costs relative to conventional
compression technologies. This simplified design was then installed in a sealed
housing designed to eliminate any additional emissions from the recovery compressor
itself.
Collectively, these technical objectives were intended to demonstrate not only that
methane recovery from low-pressure vent sources is technically feasible, but that it can
be accomplished in a manner that is safe, responsive, and compatible with real-world
natural gas facility operations. Success in meeting these objectives would establish a
foundation for broader deployment of leak recovery compressors as a practical methane
mitigation strategy across the natural gas value chain.

METHODS
Overview of Technical Approach
The project employed a staged development and validation methodology to design,
construct, and evaluate a linear motor–driven methane leak recovery compressor
suitable for application at natural gas facilities. The methodological approach combined
engineering analysis, dynamic simulation, prototype fabrication, control system
development, and pilot-scale testing to ensure that the system could meet the
functional, safety, and operational requirements associated with recovering
low-pressure methane emissions and reinjecting that gas into high-pressure pipeline
systems.
The work was structured to progressively reduce technical risk by first validating
performance assumptions through modeling and component-level testing, followed by
full system integration and controlled experimental evaluation. At each stage, design
decisions were informed by the need to balance performance, reliability, operational
flexibility, and cost, with the overarching goal of demonstrating a technically viable and
deployment-ready methane mitigation solution.
System Design and Architecture
Compressor Concept and Operating Principle
The leak recovery system is based on a linear motor–driven reciprocating compressor
architecture. Unlike conventional crankshaft-driven reciprocating compressors, the
linear motor directly actuates the piston, eliminating the need for rotary-to-linear
mechanical conversion. This architecture enables precise control of piston motion,
including stroke length, speed, and frequency, which is essential for managing variable
inlet conditions and maintaining stable operation over a wide range of flow rates.
The compressor was designed to accept methane at near-atmospheric inlet pressures
representative of vented emission sources and compress the gas to discharge
pressures suitable for reinjection into natural gas pipelines operating at pressures up to
1,500 psig. Three stages of compression were used to achieve the required pressure
ratio, each driven by an independent linear motor. This modular design was selected so
that the compressor could be adapted to any site operating conditions simply by adding
or removing stages to reach the site’s operating pressure or adding/removing motors to
adapt to the site’s expected flow rates.
Leak Recovery System Integration
The compressor was integrated into a complete leak recovery system that included
safety and monitoring equipment, inlet piping and filtration, interstage cooling and
filtration, discharge piping, power electronics, instrumentation, and a centralized control
system. The system was configured to accept gas from one or more vented sources
and route recovered methane directly to a high-pressure outlet. Particular attention was
paid to inlet design to ensure that upstream vent systems would not experience
unacceptable backpressure during operation.

 Page 7

The system architecture was intentionally modular to support future adaptation to
different facility configurations and to facilitate testing, maintenance, and potential field
deployment.
Modeling and Design Analysis
Dynamic Simulation and Performance Modeling
Dynamic modeling and simulation in Matlab Simulink were used to guide key design
decisions related to compressor sizing, motor selection, staging configuration, and
control strategy. Simulations evaluated system performance across a range of inlet
pressures, flow rates, and discharge pressures to ensure that the compressor could
meet the expected operating envelope associated with vented methane emissions.
The modeling effort focused on predicting piston dynamics, compression
thermodynamics, power consumption, and control responsiveness under variable
conditions. These analyses informed design tradeoffs related to stroke length, operating
frequency, and motor force requirements, as well as expected efficiency and thermal
behavior.
Design Iteration and Optimization
Simulation results were used iteratively to refine mechanical design features, including
piston geometry, sealing arrangements, and staging layout. Design optimization
emphasized achieving sufficient compression capability while minimizing mechanical
complexity and reducing wear. The final design balanced performance requirements
with manufacturability and reliability considerations.
Component Development and Subsystem Testing
Linear Motor and Drive System
The linear motor and associated drive electronics were developed and evaluated to
confirm their ability to deliver the required force, speed, and positional accuracy across
the intended operating range. Testing focused on validating motor responsiveness,
controllability, and thermal performance under representative operating conditions.
Motor characterization informed the final control algorithms and ensured that the motor
could support rapid changes in operating state without loss of stability or excessive
power consumption.
Compression Components and Sealing
Compression components, including pistons, cylinders, valves, and seals, were
fabricated and assembled to meet the mechanical and thermal demands of multi-stage
compression. Sealing strategies were selected to minimize leakage and wear while
avoiding the use of lubricating oil. The oil-free design was intended to reduce
maintenance requirements and prevent contamination of recovered gas.
Control System Development

Control Objectives
A central element of the project methodology was the development of a control system
capable of managing compressor operation in response to changing inlet conditions.
The primary control objectives were to:
 • Maintain inlet pressure within a narrow, safe range to prevent pressurization of
 upstream vent systems,
 • Adjust compressor throughput dynamically to match variable methane flow rates,
 • Ensure stable operation across the full operating envelope, and
 • Protect equipment through appropriate interlocks and safety logic.
Control Architecture and Implementation
The control system was implemented using an industrial programmable logic controller
(PLC) interfaced with sensors measuring pressure, temperature, flow, and motor
position. Control algorithms modulated piston motion parameters in real time to regulate
compressor performance. Feedback control loops were designed to respond to changes
in inlet pressure and flow, allowing the system to ramp output up or down as conditions
changed.
Safety features included pressure limits, emergency shutdown logic, and fault detection
routines to ensure safe operation during testing and anticipated field use.
Pilot-Scale Testing Methodology
System Assembly and Integration
Following component-level validation, the compressor and all supporting subsystems
were assembled into a fully integrated leak recovery system. Electrical, mechanical, and
control interfaces were verified during assembly to ensure compatibility and correct
operation. The system was prepared for testing in a controlled environment
representative of industrial conditions, including hazardous-area considerations and
appropriate isolation of power electronics.
Commissioning and Test Configuration
The assembled system was commissioned in a controlled test environment. Initial
testing was conducted using inert gas to verify mechanical integrity, control functionality,
and overall system stability without introducing flammable gas hazards. Commissioning
activities included leak checks, sensor calibration, control tuning, and verification of
safety systems.
Following successful inert gas commissioning, the system was prepared for operation
with natural gas to evaluate performance under representative process conditions. The
system was purged with natural gas, then tested across a range of operating conditions.
Test Objectives and Measurements

 Page 9

Pilot-scale testing was designed to evaluate the following aspects of system
performance:
 • Ability to accept gas at near-atmospheric inlet pressure without imposing
 excessive backpressure,
 • Capability to achieve target discharge pressures,
 • Responsiveness to changes in inlet flow rate,
 • Power consumption under varying operating conditions,
 • Stability of control during steady-state and transient operation.
Instrumentation recorded pressures, temperatures, flow rates, motor position, and
electrical power to support performance evaluation and analysis.
Data Collection and Analysis
Test data was collected continuously during operation. Performance metrics were
evaluated against the project objectives to assess whether the system met design
expectations. Observed deviations were used to identify opportunities for control
refinement and design improvement.
Economic Assessment Methodology
In parallel with technical development, an economic assessment framework was applied
to evaluate potential deployment scenarios. The methodology examined capital cost
drivers, energy consumption, and recovered gas value under representative operating
conditions. Sensitivity analyses explored how utilization rate and discharge pressure
requirements influence economic performance.
The economic assessment was intended to inform design prioritization and identify
pathways for improving cost-effectiveness through future optimization and scale-up.
Methodological Summary
Through a combination of modeling, controlled experimentation, and system-level
integration, the project methodology was designed to rigorously evaluate the feasibility
of a linear motor–driven leak recovery compressor for methane mitigation. The
approach emphasized technical rigor, operational realism, and alignment with the
practical constraints of natural gas facility operations.

PROJECT RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
The overarching goal of the project was to simulate, design, build, test, and validate a
linear motor methane leak recovery compressor capable of capturing low-pressure
methane emissions and compressing them back to pipeline pressures, while
maintaining safe inlet pressures at connected vent systems. A summary of the
anticipated and realized outcomes is listed below.
Anticipated versus realized performance:
 • Target inlet / suction pressure: The compressor inlet was designed for 0-0.25
 psig (target range for connected vent systems set by industry partner); Testing
 used a suction pressure of ~0.25-0.5 psig. This slightly higher pressure was
 used during testing to prevent a suction pressure switch from inadvertently
 shutting down the compressor when gathering performance data and has
 minimal impact on the actual performance of the unit. The compressor will
 operate down to atmospheric pressure in the field.
 • Target discharge pressure: The compressor discharge pressure was designed
 for 1,000-1,500 psi. The compressor reached 1,500 psig, but most testing was
 done at 1,100-1,250 psi to reduce the load on the system and prevent the
 discharge safety shutdowns from triggering.
 • Target flow: The simulation demonstrated ~50-60 SCFM from atmospheric inlet
 to 1,500 psi discharge. The measured flow was about 36-38 SCFM. The
 shortfall is the result of several factors that include higher than expected internal
 leakage back to the compressor suction at 8-10 SCFM, a supply voltage of
 400Vac instead of 480Vac that reduced the system speed, and motor control
 tuning that can continue to be improved over time. Minor changes to the motor
 construction, supply voltage, seals, and controls should result in a significant
 increase in the flow. The most significant change is the use of a drop-in low-
 inductance motor that would increase the system speed by up to 2.5x, increasing
 the flow to 90-100 SCFM.
 • Controls / flow matching demonstration: The compressor was designed to
 rapidly adjust for flow changes so that the suction pressure was maintained
 between 0-0.25 psig. The target pressure used during testing was 0.5 psi to
 demonstrate flow control and avoid shutdowns caused by the suction pressure
 switch. The compressor held the suction pressure well within 0-1 psig despite
 the flow alternating between about 5 and 35 SCFM. This demonstrated that the
 design can rapidly adapt to changes in the methane leak flow rate and the team
 is confident that this control can be improved with further testing.
 • Safety readiness for natural gas testing: The safety and monitoring equipment
 were installed and tested (including shutdowns, oxygen monitoring, suction
 protection), prior to switching to natural gas.

 Page 11

Overall, the compressor is achieving or close to achieving each of the target metrics.
The team is confident that the flow and suction pressure control will continue to be
improved beyond the scope of this project as these compressors are deployed for field
use at operational gas production, processing, storage, and transport facilities.

Key Challenges and Design Iterations
The linear motor and compressor designs went through multiple iterations as the team
worked to balance the performance, design flexibility, manufacturability, and other key
design characteristics. Some of the most significant changes are described below.
 1) The original compressor design had 4 stages of compression driven by a single
 linear motor. The design used 4 stages to ensure the discharge gas for each
 stage was kept relatively cool to help extend the life of the seals and valves.
 However, the team was ultimately forced to move to 3 stages of compression to

 Page 14

 enable the use of a double acting stage 1 within the single linear motor
 configuration. This change was deemed necessary after extensive simulation of
 the compressor and leak recovery system showed that the suction pressure
 would be impossible to control within the specified limits without a double acting
 stage 1 to constantly pull in gas from the supply. While this change was made
 early in the project, it still delayed the design which could not proceed until the
 simulation had fully validated the performance of the system.
 2) After settling on the 3-stage design, the team began working on the model of the
 compressor. One of the key components that needed to be selected were the
 piston seals. The team had previously used lip seals in prototype compressors,
 so that was the default choice for the design. Unfortunately, after designing the
 fluid ends for lips seals, the lip seal vendor no-bid the design. This forced the
 team to search for a new seal supplier. After identifying a new vendor, seals
 suitable for the operating conditions were designed, but these seals required
 more real estate than the original lip seal design. This forced the team to make
 extensive changes to the compressor design to fit the new seals into the model.
 3) After the seal redesign, the team provided the piston geometry to Cook
 Compression to evaluate and finalize the valve design. Their extensive industry
 experience and models indicated that there would be a flow imbalance between
 the compression stages. This took some time and effort to validate. Ultimately,
 the team determined the problem was real and that the compressor fluid ends
 would need to be modified again to address the compression imbalance. The
 new design simplified the compressor stages by moving from double acting
 pistons to two single acting pistons for each stage of compression. These
 modified designs were evaluated again and ultimately approved by Cook
 Compression who could then finalize the valve design.
 4) At this point, all the stages of compression were still integrated into a single
 motor. However, GTI had started working with additional industry partners that
 made it clear that the original motor design was oversized for most sites and that
 it needed to be easily scalable for different site operating conditions as no two
 gas facilities are the same. The change didn’t require a compressor redesign but
 did require that each stage be separated and driven by smaller, independent
 motors. This change had the added benefit of reducing the operational vibration
 as the multiple motors could be operated out of phase.
The final design consisted of four linear motor assemblies driving three stages of
compression. Each stage of compression was powered by one motor assembly. The
fourth linear motor was operated without a compressor fluid end attached but was used
to balance the system dynamics and test additional bearing configurations in the motor
assembly. The team considered circling back to the four-stage design that was
originally conceived, but that would have caused additional delays, so that idea was
abandoned.
Figure 1 shows the final solid model of a single linear motor and compressor assembly.
The hermetically sealed motor is located at the center of the assembly with the stage 1

 Page 15

compression pistons attached to either end of the motor. This design was developed
because it was highly modular, making it easy to change the stroke length and power of
the linear motor and compressor. The assembly was also designed to be hermetically
sealed up to pressures exceeding 1,500 psig, making the design useful for applications
beyond methane recovery. The hermetically sealed design also prevents any seal
leakage from venting to atmosphere. Instead, all seal leakage was routed from the
linear motor housing, back to suction where it was simply captured and compressed.
Figure 2 shows the complete compressor assembly installed in GTI Energy’s
environmental chamber inside a Class 1 Div 2 test area. The assembly closest to the
camera is stage 1. The 2” piping on the left side of the image is the suction that is
supplying the stage 1 piston on either end of the assembly. The suction piping was later
connected to the discharge of a filter that was used to catch any liquids and solids
before the gas entered the compressor. The other motors and compression stages
were all mounted on a single skid along with stage 1. The skid was designed so that the
entire four motor assembly could easily be moved around using a standard forklift.
Figure 3 shows most of the balance of plant components including the intercoolers,
filters, and control valves that were used to control the compressor discharge pressure
and simulated leak rates. Not shown are the suction buffer vessel that was located just
outside the environmental chamber, the suction filter that was located next to the
compressor, and the oxygen sensor that was located on the wall out of view.
The compressor was designed to operate like it would out in the field. It drew gas in
from the suction buffer vessel and compressed it up to the target discharge pressure.
The compression was evenly split between 3 stages, each included intercoolers, filters,
pressure sensors, and temperature sensors. The discharge pressure was controlled
between 0 to 1,500 psig using a flow control valve that would stay shut until the target
discharge pressure was reached. Then the control valve would open enough to
maintain the target discharge pressure at whatever flow the compressor was currently
producing.
Downstream of the discharge control valve, there was an intermediate buffer vessel that
operated between 50-150 psig. The intermediate buffer vessel supplied a second flow
control valve that was used to simulate methane leaks. This was accomplished by
opening or closing the valve to predetermined setpoints. These setpoints changed the
effective orifice of the valve and therefore the amount of gas that could “leak” from the
intermediate buffer to the suction buffer. The setpoints used during testing resulted in
leaks that alternated between about 5 and 35 SCFM every 10 seconds. These leaks
were operated completely independently from the compressor control loop, so the team
could demonstrate that the compressor was in fact able to independently react to
changes in the leak flow rate.
The goal of the compressor controls was to change the speed of the compressor in real
time so that the pressure of the suction vessel was held between 0-0.25 psig despite
the rapid changes in flow feeding the suction vessel. If the compressor ran too fast,
then the buffer vessel would drop in pressure and the compressor risked drawing in air
that could create a flammable mixture inside the compressor or pipeline. If the

 Page 16

compressor ran too slowly, then the buffer vessel pressure would rise, risking over
pressurization of any connected equipment that was venting natural gas. The team was

The isentropic efficiency of the entire test was about 35%. The compressor itself is still
reasonably efficient as the flow decreases. However, as the flow decreases towards the
internal seal leak rate, then compressor is eventually just internally circulating the gas
leaking by the seals, consuming power without recovering any gas. This results in a
minimum practical leak rate that can be recovered by the unit and will influence how the
compressors need to be sized for a particular site. The internal leak rate is driven by
the pressure in the compression stages and not the flow of the discharge. So as the
team works to speed up the compressor, the leak rate is expected to remain relatively
constant and will become a smaller percentage of the maximum flow and less of an
issue. Further, there are potential design changes to the fluid ends and seals that can
be made to help reduce the internal leakage. Ultimately, this is not a fundamental
barrier to the technology, but an engineering issue that can be improved with
incremental changes to the design.

TEA Results
The team assessed the economics of the following design cases.
 1) Low flow case: This case used early flow and performance results from the unit
 that was tested at GTI (36 kg/hr / 17.5 kW). The payback period was estimated
 to be about 12 years.
 2) High flow case: This case used the target flow and scaled the power
 consumption linearly (65 kg/hr / 31.6 kW). The payback period was 6 years.

 Page 27

 3) Low flow and low-pressure case: This case assumed the unit achieved the flow
 as tested (36kg/hr), but that the site only needed a single stage of compression
 to enable recovery of the gas. The payback period was estimated at 4 years.
The results are not unfavorable considering the compressor was designed to be durable
and repairable with an equipment lifespan easily exceeding 20 years. However, it’s
clear that improving the flow can drastically lower the expected payback period as more
performance can be achieved for little to no impact on the capital cost of the equipment.
The following changes are being considered to improve the flow and have little to no
impact on the capital cost of the design. For starters, the supply voltage is 400 Vac
instead of 480Vac. This is because the team used an existing isolation transformer that
dropped the 480 supply to 400 volts. By using the more common 480 Vac supply, the
existing motors will be able to operate about 15-20% faster, resulting in 15-20% more
flow. This is a simple change that would have essentially zero impact on the capital
cost and may even lower the cost if the isolation transformer can be eliminated entirely,
which is being considered
The second change would be to improve the controls and system software. There are
several changes being considered that could theoretically be made to the controls and
power electronics that would add an additional 15-20% to the speed and flow of the
existing design. These changes are still conceptual, but it’s possible that the flow could
be further improved just by modifying the compressor’s software that would have zero
cost once implemented.
The last and most impactful change would be to modify the motor design. By winding
the motor with a larger gauge of wire and fewer turns, the motor inductance could be
reduced, significantly increasing the top speed of the motor at a given supply voltage.
The tradeoff is that the motor would also require more current to achieve the same
force, so while the low inductance motor would likely cost about the same amount as
the current motors, the drive would need to get slightly larger. However, the drive only
makes up a small fraction of the capital cost, and the flow has a direct impact on the
payback period, so this trade would likely have a net benefit.
Some improvements to the controls have already been made since the TEA was
evaluated. For example, the flow used in the TEA was 36 kg/hr, but the compressor has
achieved peak flows of 40-43 kg/hr at an average speed of 1.25 m/s. By further
improving the power electronics and bus voltage, the speed can theoretically be
increased from ~1.25 m/s to ~1.8 m/s, resulting in a flow of about 55-60 kg/hr, close to
the original target. By making the additional changes to the motor windings, it should be
possible to reach speeds in excess of 3 m/s and a flow rate of 90-100 kg/hr,
demonstrating that there is significant room for improved performance and therefore
significant potential to improve the system economics and payback period, making the
methane recovery compressor an attractive solutions for operational gas facilities
across the country.

SIGNIFICANT ACCOMPLISHMENTS AND CONCLUSIONS
Overview
This project successfully demonstrated the technical feasibility of methane leak
recovery using a linear motor–driven compressor, validating a novel compression
architecture for capturing low-pressure methane emissions and returning that gas to
pipeline-relevant pressures. The work progressed from simulation through detailed
design, fabrication, commissioning, and pilot-scale testing, establishing a clear
performance baseline while identifying targeted pathways for improvement and
commercialization.
While not all original performance targets were fully achieved, the results confirm that
the core recovery concept functions as intended and that remaining gaps are
engineering-limited rather than fundamental. This distinction is significant for assessing
technical readiness and future investment decisions.
Significant Technical Accomplishments
Demonstration of High-Pressure Methane Recovery Capability
A major accomplishment of the project was demonstrating that the linear motor
compressor architecture can compress gas from near-atmospheric inlet conditions to
discharge pressures up to 1,500 psi, enabling reinjection into pipelines at pressures
relevant to most midstream facilities. This directly addresses a key barrier to methane
mitigation—namely, the lack of practical solutions for recompressing low-pressure
vented gas back into operating pipelines.
The ability to reach these pressures confirms that the compressor can be customized or
staged to meet site-specific pressure requirements across a wide range of transmission,
storage, gathering, and processing applications. This flexibility is a core advantage of
the linear motor architecture and significantly expands the potential deployment
envelope compared to fixed-speed, mechanically constrained compressors.
Validation of Variable Leak-Rate Matching and Suction Pressure Control
Another critical accomplishment was the demonstration of variable-speed operation
capable of matching changing leak rates while maintaining safe suction pressures.
Testing showed that the compressor could respond to aggressive step changes in leak
rates while keeping the inlet pressure close to atmospheric conditions.
This capability is essential for real-world deployment because many upstream vent
systems can tolerate only minimal backpressure. By maintaining accurate suction
pressure control despite rapid changes in inlet flow, the compressor prevents
over-pressurization of connected equipment and avoids creating new safety hazards.
The results confirm that the linear motor’s fine motion control provides a meaningful
operational advantage over conventional compression approaches in leak recovery
applications.
Hermetically Sealed, Oil-Free Operation with Natural Gas

 Page 29

The project also successfully demonstrated a hermetically sealed compressor design,
an important accomplishment for emissions control and safety. Leak checks using
handheld flammable gas detectors verified that the compressor enclosure remained
sealed during operation. The compressor was operated using natural gas, further
validating its suitability for the intended working fluid.
During testing, the motor housing experienced internal pressures on the order of 10–20
psig, and the system continued to operate as intended. This confirms the robustness of
the sealed design and supports the conclusion that the compressor does not introduce
additional fugitive emission pathways—an essential requirement for any methane
mitigation technology.
Confirmation of Overall Leak Recovery Functionality
Taken together, the pressure capability, suction control, and sealed operation
demonstrates that the unit can accomplish methane leak recovery as designed. The
compressor was shown to accept low-pressure gas, dynamically adjust to changing inlet
conditions, and deliver compressed gas suitable for reinjection, validating the
fundamental recovery concept that motivated the project.
This outcome is significant because it establishes a working prototype that can be
refined rather than requiring a redesign of the underlying approach.
Performance Gaps, Challenges, and Lessons Learned
Flow Rate Shortfall and Identified Improvement Pathways
The most significant performance gap identified during the project was lower-than-target
flow capacity. While simulations predicted 50–60 SCFM, measured flow during testing
was approximately 36 SCFM. Contributing factors included operation at reduced motor
speed and higher-than-expected internal leakage (on the order of 8–10 SCFM).
Importantly, these limitations were not attributed to fundamental constraints of the linear
motor architecture. Instead, the project identified clear pathways for increasing flow,
including:
 • Increasing motor speed through improved control algorithms,
 • Raising DC bus voltage,
 • Implementing alternative motor windings,
 • Applying more advanced PWM control strategies, and
 • Incorporating additional compression stages where appropriate.
This distinction is critical: the shortfall reflects an engineering optimization opportunity,
not a failure of the core concept.
Power Consumption and Efficiency Considerations
Power consumption was monitored throughout testing using integrated instrumentation
and industrial control systems. While the project did not fully optimize energy efficiency,
the data collected provided a foundation for future improvements. As with flow rate,

 Page 30

efficiency is expected to improve as internal seal leakage is reduced, motor utilization
increases, and controls are refined to better match real-world leak dynamics.
Controls Complexity and Development Sequencing
Developing controls capable of both protecting upstream equipment and maximizing
compressor throughput proved to be a non-trivial challenge. Early testing relied on
conservative configurations to ensure safety, which limited achievable speed and flow.
The project demonstrated that more advanced control strategies—particularly those that
decouple leak simulation valves from compressor control—are necessary to fully
validate autonomous leak-matching performance.
This experience highlighted the importance of controls development sequencing and
reinforced that control sophistication is as critical as mechanical design in methane
recovery applications.
Economic Considerations and Commercialization Outlook
Economic analysis conducted during the project indicates that the leak recovery
concept is promising but highly sensitive to flow rate and manufacturing scale.
Increasing flow directly improves economic performance by increasing recovered gas
value and reducing payback time. Similarly, scaling manufacturing is expected to
significantly reduce unit costs through learning curves and supply-chain optimization.
Planned cost-reduction strategies therefore focus on:
 • Increasing achievable flow through design and controls improvements, and
 • Transitioning from prototype fabrication to scalable manufacturing approaches.
Overall Conclusions
This project successfully demonstrated that a linear motor–driven compressor can
recover methane emissions safely, responsively, and at pressures suitable for
reinjection into midstream infrastructure. The compressor met its most critical functional
requirements—pressure capability, suction control, sealed operation, and dynamic
response—while revealing specific areas for performance optimization.
The results support the conclusion that the technology is technically viable and
well-positioned for further development, with remaining challenges concentrated in flow
optimization, controls refinement, and cost reduction rather than fundamental feasibility.
As such, the project provides a strong foundation for subsequent engineering
refinement and potential field deployment of methane leak recovery systems based on
linear motor compression.

PATH FORWARD
Overview
The results of this project establish a strong technical foundation for methane leak
recovery using a linear motor–driven compressor, while also clearly identifying the
remaining engineering, economic, and deployment steps required to advance the
technology toward commercialization. Future work should focus on performance
optimization, cost reduction, and field-representative demonstrations to transition the
system from a successful pilot-scale prototype to a commercially deployable solution.
The technology also shows promise beyond methane mitigation, with potential
applicability to hydrogen, air, carbon dioxide, and other compressible gases, further
expanding its value proposition across emerging energy and industrial markets.
Near-Term Research and Development Priorities
Controls and Speed Optimization
The most critical near-term R&D priority is further optimization of controls and motor
speed capability, as these directly impact flow rate, efficiency, and cost per unit of gas
recovered. While the project demonstrated stable variable-speed operation and
accurate suction pressure control, additional development is expected to yield
substantial performance gains.
Key areas for future work include:
 • Increasing achievable motor speed through refined control algorithms,
 • Expanding the operating envelope using higher DC bus voltage,
 • Evaluating alternative motor windings optimized for higher flow operation,
 • Implementing advanced pulse-width modulation (PWM) strategies to improve
 motor efficiency and responsiveness, and
 • Refining autonomous leak-rate matching logic to better reflect real-world vent
 dynamics.
Improvements in these areas are expected to simultaneously increase flow capacity,
reduce specific energy consumption, and lower the effective cost of methane recovery
per unit volume.
Mechanical Refinement and Leakage Reduction
Internal seal leakage identified during pilot testing represents another important
opportunity for improvement. Future design iterations should focus on refining sealing
strategies to reduce internal losses, thereby increasing net recovered flow without
increasing power input.
Expanded Application Opportunities
Natural Gas Recovery and Methane Mitigation

 Page 32

The primary commercialization pathway remains natural gas recovery across upstream,
midstream, and storage assets. The demonstrated ability to maintain near-atmospheric
suction pressure while compressing gas to pipeline-relevant pressures positions the
technology well for recovering emissions from vent systems, compressor seals,
pneumatic devices, and planned operational releases.
Future deployment efforts should prioritize sites with:
 • Persistent or recurring vented emissions,
 • Sufficient discharge pressure requirements to justify recompression,
 • Existing infrastructure that benefits from centralized recovery systems.
Hydrogen Compression
The oil-free design of the linear motor compressor makes it particularly attractive for
hydrogen service, where contamination is a key concern. With appropriate material
selection and sealing optimization, the technology could support hydrogen compression
for a variety of applications.
The team has demonstrated this capability during the development and testing of a
12,000 psi hydrogen expander. The team is also currently working on a small-scale
6,000 psi hydrogen compressor.
Air and Carbon Dioxide Applications
Beyond energy applications, the compressor architecture may also be well suited for air
and CO₂ compression, including applications related to industrial processes, carbon
capture systems, and emissions control. The ability to precisely control flow and
pressure over a wide operating range provides flexibility that is attractive in these
contexts, particularly where variable demand or intermittent operation is required.
Field Demonstration and Deployment Pathways
Deployment Projects
A logical next step for advancing the technology is a field-based deployment project
focused on real-world operating environments. Such a project could take multiple forms,
including:
 • Upstream sites, where low-pressure vented emissions are common and
 infrastructure is often dispersed,
 • Midstream compressor stations, where recovered gas can be readily reinjected
 into high-pressure systems, and
 • Midstream storage facilities, where intermittent venting events present attractive
 recovery opportunities.
These deployments would serve to validate long-duration reliability, maintenance
requirements, and operational integration while providing high-confidence data to
support commercialization decisions.
Economic Scale-Up and Manufacturing Strategy

 Page 33

Cost Reduction Through Scale
While the prototype system demonstrated technical viability, cost reduction remains a
key requirement for widespread adoption. Future efforts should focus on:
 • Increasing flow capacity to improve economic return per installed unit,
 • Transitioning from prototype fabrication to repeatable manufacturing processes,
 and
 • Leveraging economies of scale in motors, power electronics, and structural
 components.
As production volumes increase, unit costs are expected to decline substantially,
improving payback periods and enabling broader deployment across the natural gas
value chain.
Remaining Technical and Commercialization Risks
Despite the strong progress achieved, several risks and barriers remain that must be
addressed before immediate commercialization:
 • Flow and efficiency optimization risk: While improvement pathways are well
 understood, additional engineering effort is required to fully realize predicted
 performance gains.
 • Controls complexity: Advanced autonomous control strategies must be robustly
 validated under a wide range of real-world operating conditions.
 • Economic sensitivity: Project economics remain sensitive to recovered flow rates
 and utilization levels, making performance optimization essential.
 • Field integration challenges: Site-specific constraints, safety requirements, and
 operational practices may require customized system configurations.
None of these risks are considered fundamental barriers; rather, they represent typical
development challenges for transitioning the linear compression technology from pilot
scale to commercial readiness.
Concluding Outlook
The work completed under this award demonstrates that linear motor–driven
compression is a viable and flexible platform for methane leak recovery and related gas
handling applications. With targeted follow-on R&D focused on controls optimization,
leakage reduction, and manufacturing scale-up—combined with carefully selected field
demonstrations—the technology is well positioned to advance toward commercialization
and to support emissions reduction goals across multiple sectors.
