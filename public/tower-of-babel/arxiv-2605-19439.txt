Enhancing ultracold atomic batteries using tunable interactions Preprint: APS/123-QED
Duc Tuan Hoang, Thomas Busch, Thomás Fogarty

Abstract
We study the charging performance of a one-dimensional, many-body bosonic quantum battery driven by a harmonic-oscillator charger, focusing on how many-body effects and intra-species interactions influence the energy-transfer dynamics. We show that by tuning the charger frequency, the system can reach a resonance condition where perfect energy transfer and maximal extractable work are achieved. In the weak-coupling limit this can be understood by approximating the battery–charger dynamics using an effective two-level model, which accurately predicts the maximum stored work, ergotropy, and optimal charging time. In this regime, many-body batteries exhibit enhanced charging power, reduced quantum speed limit (QSL) times, and comparable or lower irreversible work relative to single-particle batteries. We further examine the role of intra-species interactions: repulsive interactions inside the battery medium suppress performance, whereas attractive interactions can significantly enhance it, with both types of interactions generating additional charging resonances. Our results show that particle number and interaction control provide powerful tools for designing fast, efficient, and scalable quantum batteries, and point toward a feasible experimental implementation in ultracold-atom platforms.

In particular, we propose a one-dimensional, bosonic quantum battery model coupled to a harmonic-oscillator charger. This coupling is realised by a sudden quench of the two-body inter-species contact interaction between the two systems, which can be tuned experimentally using Feshbach resonances

Our focus is on the interplay between the interaction strength and the charger’s trap frequency, which together determine the resonance condition under which maximum energy transfer and the highest ergotropy can be achieved. In addition to numerically simulating the complex quantum many-body process, we also derive an effective two-level model that provides an approximate description of the battery dynamics in the weak-interaction regime. ;
Mandelstam and Tamm 1991

A central outcome of our analysis is a many-body speedup: increasing the particle number shortens the time required to reach the first energy-transfer maximum and enhances the charging power. Importantly, this enhancement does not incur excessive thermodynamic overhead. We also investigate the role of intra-species interactions, which enrich and shift the resonance spectrum relative to the non-interacting battery. While repulsive interactions suppress the charging power due to elongating the optimal charging time, attractive interactions can significantly shorten the QSL time for moderate initial charger energies and can enhance the charging power beyond the non-interacting case.

II
Model

II.1
Charger-mediated protocol

We consider a quantum battery, described by the Hamiltonian
H^{B}
, which is charged through unitary dynamics via the coupling with another quantum system, denoted as a quantum charger
H^{C}
. Initially, the Hamiltonian of the uncoupled battery-charger system is given by

H_{0}^{BC}=H^{B}+H^{C},

(1)

and the battery is prepared in its ground state
\rho^{B}_{0}
, while the charger is in its excited state
Andolina et al. 2018
;
Campaioli et al. 2024
. The corresponding separable state is given by

\rho^{BC}(t=0)=\rho^{B}(0)\otimes\rho^{C}(0)=\rho^{B}_{0}\otimes\rho^{C}(0).

(2)

At
t=0
, the interaction
H^{\text{int}}
between the battery and the charger is activated, and the charging process starts. The time-dependent Hamiltonian of the system then becomes

H_{1}^{BC}(t)=H^{BC}_{0}+\lambda(t)H^{\text{int}},

(3)

where
\lambda(t)
is an external classical control that modulates the coupling between the battery and the charger, allowing the composite BC system to exchange energy.

In our work, charging dynamics are described by a sudden quench of the coupling, which are modeled by defining
\lambda(t)
as a step function

\lambda(t)=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if}\ t\in[0,\tau],\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(4)

During the charging the state of the charged battery becomes time-dependent and is governed by the Liouville-von Neumann equation

\dfrac{d}{dt}\rho^{BC}(t)=-\dfrac{i}{\hbar}\left[H_{1}^{BC},\rho^{BC}(t)\right].

(5)

At the end of the charging protocol, the interaction is switched off (modeled as another step function) and the quantum state of the battery can be obtained by tracing out the contribution of the charger from the composite density matrix

\rho^{B}(t)=\text{Tr}_{C}[\rho^{BC}(t)].

(6)

Notably, if the interaction Hamiltonian
H^{\text{int}}
does not commute with the bare Hamiltonian
H_{0}^{BC}
, such that
[H_{0}^{BC},H^{\text{int}}]\neq 0
, the modulation of
\lambda(t)
contributes partially to the energy stored in the quantum battery during switching times
Andolina et al. 2018
. The energy transfer is therefore not solely from the charger to the battery, but may also include the irreversible work created during the charging process

W_{\text{irr}}=\text{Tr}\{H_{0}^{BC}\left[\rho^{BC}(t)-\rho^{BC}(0)\right]\}.

(7)

II.2
Work extraction process

After the charging time
t
, the total work stored in the quantum battery is given by

\displaystyle W_{B}(t)

\displaystyle=\text{Tr}\{H^{B}\left[\rho^{B}(t)-\rho^{B}_{0}\right]\}.

(8)

To quantify the maximal extractable work from a battery through a reversible cyclic unitary process, one can compute the ergotropy by optimizing over all possible unitaries
\mathcal{U}
that lower the energy of the charged state
\rho^{B}(t)=\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}|\varphi_{i}\rangle\langle\varphi_{i}|
(with
\lambda_{i}>\lambda_{i+1}
) with respect to
H^{B}=\sum_{i}\varepsilon_{i}|\psi_{i}\rangle\langle\psi_{i}|
(with
\varepsilon_{i}<\varepsilon_{i+1}
)
Allahverdyan et al. 2004

\mathcal{E}_{B}=\text{Tr}\left[H^{B}\rho^{B}(t)\right]-\text{min}_{\mathcal{U}}\left\{\text{Tr}\left[H^{B}\mathcal{U}\rho^{B}(t)\mathcal{U}^{\dagger}\right]\right\},

(9)

which can also be expressed as

\mathcal{E}_{B}=\text{Tr}\left[H^{B}\rho^{B}(t)\right]-\text{Tr}\left[H^{B}P_{\rho}\right].

(10)

In other words, the ergotropy represents the difference between the mean energy
of the state
\rho^{B}(t)
and the passive energy, defined as the energy of a passive state
P_{\rho}
, from which no additional work can be extracted via unitary operations. A quantum state is classified as passive when any unitary transformation applied to it increases its energy

\text{Tr}\left[H^{B}P_{\rho}\right]\leq\text{Tr}\left[H^{B}\mathcal{U}P_{\rho}\mathcal{U}^{\dagger}\right].

(11)

After the charger is decoupled, the entropy
S(\rho^{B})=-\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}\log{\lambda_{i}}
is conserved, and therefore the eigenvalues
\lambda_{i}
remain constant. A passive state, which is stationary due to its commutation with
H_{0}^{B}
, is defined in the diagonal basis of the local Hamiltonian with reordered occupations such that higher occupation numbers correspond to lower energy levels

P_{\rho}=\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}|\psi_{i}\rangle\langle\psi_{i}|.

(12)

The ergotropy can then be expressed as

\mathcal{E}_{B}=\sum_{i}(p_{i}-\lambda_{i})\varepsilon_{i},

(13)

where
p_{i}=\sum_{j}\lambda_{j}|\langle\varphi_{j}|\psi_{i}\rangle|^{2},
is the projection of
\rho^{B}(t)
onto
H_{0}^{B}
, representing the energy probability distribution of the charged battery state. For non-zero ergotropy, it is required that
p_{i}\neq\lambda_{i}
.

In general, the ergotropy
\mathcal{E}_{B}
is less than or equal to the total work stored in the battery
W_{B}
, expressed as
\mathcal{E}_{B}\leq W_{B}
. The equality holds when the state
\rho^{B}
is pure. For a mixed state
\rho^{B}
, a nontrivial gap between
\mathcal{E}_{B}
and
W_{B}
arises because some energy is locked within correlations between the charger and the battery
Andolina et al. 2019a
;
Rossini et al. 2019
;
Campaioli et al. 2024
. It is also useful to define the von Neumann entropy of the battery state
\rho^{B}
to quantify the amount of correlations created between the battery and the charger that can be created during the coupling process

S_{B}=-\text{Tr}(\rho^{B}\ln{\rho^{B}}).

(14)

To compare the performance between the many-body battery and its single-particle counterpart, we define the charging power

\mathcal{P}_{W}=\dfrac{W_{B}(t_{\text{max}})}{t_{\text{max}}},

(15)

where
t_{\text{max}}
denotes the shortest time for which we get the maximum stored work
W_{B}
.

II.3
Many-body ultracold atoms battery

We will study a charger-mediated protocol by modeling a quantum battery using a two-component bosonic system in one-dimensional harmonic traps, where one component acts as the battery, with trap frequency
\omega_{B}
, and the other as the charger, with trap frequency
\omega_{C}
. The initial Hamiltonian is expressed as (
1
), where

\displaystyle H^{B}=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{B}}\Big(

\displaystyle-\dfrac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\dfrac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{B,i}^{2}}+\dfrac{1}{2}m\omega_{B}^{2}x_{B,i}^{2}\Big)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle g_{B}\sum_{i<j}^{N_{B}}\delta(x_{B,i}-x_{B,j}),

(16)

\displaystyle H^{C}=-\dfrac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}

\displaystyle\dfrac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{C}^{2}}+\dfrac{1}{2}m\omega_{C}^{2}x_{C}^{2}.

(17)

Here, the many-body battery consists of
N_{B}
bosonic particles and is charged by a single-particle charger, with equal masses
m_{B}=m_{C}=m
. The parameter
g_{B}
characterizes the short-range intra-species contact interaction strength between particles in the battery. This interaction originates from low-energy
s
-wave scattering between bosons confined in one dimension and is well described by a delta-function potential. In ultracold-atom realizations, the effective interaction strength
g_{B}
can be tuned experimentally by adjusting the transverse confinement
Olshanii 1998
or via Feshbach resonances
Chin et al. 2010
;
Courteille et al. 1998
, providing direct control over the many-body correlations within the battery.

Without loss of generality we assume that the battery is initially prepared in the ground state of the Hamiltonian
H^{B}
with energy
E_{B}(0)=\frac{N}{2}\hbar\omega_{B}
while the charger is prepared in the 1st excited state of the Hamiltonian
H_{C}
with energy
E_{C}(0)=\frac{3}{2}\hbar\omega_{C}
. To charge the battery, an inter-species interaction term
H^{\text{int}}
is introduced, which couples the battery and charger

H^{\text{int}}=g_{BC}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{B}}\delta(x_{B,i}-x_{C}),

(18)

and where
g_{BC}
denotes the short-range inter-species coupling strength. By switching on this coupling, energy is transferred from the charger to the battery, enabling the charging process. For the charger, the initial stored work can be varied through the trap frequency
\omega_{C}

W_{C}(0)=\varepsilon_{1}^{C}-\varepsilon_{0}^{C}=\hbar\omega_{C},

(19)

where
\varepsilon_{i}^{C}
are the eigenenergies of
H_{C}
. The work that is stored in the battery is given by

W_{B}(t)=E^{B}(t)-\varepsilon^{B}_{0},

(20)

with
E^{B}(t)=\text{Tr}\left[H^{B}\rho^{B}(t)\right]
the energy of the battery state during the charging dynamics. In a perfect charging process we should find an optimal time
max
such that
W_{B}(t_{\text{max}})=W_{C}(0)
, which signifies that all of the energy from the charger has been successfully transferred to the battery.

III
Results

III.1
Approximated two-level model

Let us first consider a non-interacting,
g_{B}=0
, quantum battery system with
N_{B}
bosons, which is charged through a weak battery-charger coupling
g_{BC}\ll 1
.
Since the battery consists of identical bosons, the particles are indistinguishable and the excited many-body state must be properly symmetrized. We therefore define the fully charged battery as the bosonic state in which a single excitation to the
n
th excited level is coherently shared among all
N_{B}
particles, while the remaining population resides in the ground state. At the same time, the charger is fully discharged, with the corresponding excitation relaxed to its ground state.
In this case we can approximate the system by a two-level model

\displaystyle|0\rangle

\displaystyle=\left[\prod_{i=1}^{N_{B}}\phi_{0}(x_{B,i})\right]\varphi_{1}(x_{C}),

(21)

\displaystyle|1\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{B}}}\,\mathcal{S}\!\left[\phi_{n}(x_{B,1})\phi_{0}(x_{B,2})\cdots\phi_{0}(x_{B,N})\right]\varphi_{0}(x_{C}),

(22)

where
\phi_{n}(x_{B})
and
\varphi_{n}(x_{C})
are single-particle wave functions with respect to
\hat{H}^{B}_{\text{sp}}
and
\hat{H}^{C}_{\text{sp}}
, respectively, and
\mathcal{S}
is the symmetrization operator.
Using the analytical expressions of harmonic oscillator eigenfunctions, we can write the matrix form of the charging Hamiltonian, with the four matrix elements are defined as

\displaystyle\langle 0|H_{1}^{BC}|0\rangle

\displaystyle=\dfrac{N_{B}}{2}\hbar\omega_{B}+\dfrac{3}{2}\hbar\omega_{C}+N_{B}g_{BC}I_{01},

(23)

\displaystyle\langle 0|H_{1}^{BC}|1\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle 1|H_{1}^{BC}|0\rangle=g_{BC}\sqrt{N_{B}}\mathcal{I}_{n},

(24)

\displaystyle\langle 1|H_{1}^{BC}|1\rangle

\displaystyle=\left(\dfrac{N_{B}}{2}+n\right)\hbar\omega_{B}+\dfrac{1}{2}\hbar\omega_{C}

\displaystyle\hskip 40.0pt+g_{BC}\left[(N_{B}-1)I_{00}+I_{n0}\right],

(25)

with the corresponding integrals

\displaystyle I_{00}

\displaystyle=\int\phi^{2}_{0}(x)\varphi_{0}^{2}(x)dx=\sqrt{\dfrac{m}{\pi\hbar}}\sqrt{\dfrac{\omega_{B}\omega_{C}}{\omega_{B}+\omega_{C}}},

(26)

\displaystyle I_{01}

\displaystyle=\int\phi^{2}_{0}(x)\varphi_{1}^{2}(x)dx=\sqrt{\dfrac{m}{\pi\hbar}}\dfrac{\omega_{C}\sqrt{\omega_{B}\omega_{C}}}{(\omega_{B}+\omega_{C})^{3/2}},

(27)

\displaystyle I_{n0}

\displaystyle=\int\phi^{2}_{n}(x)\varphi_{0}^{2}(x)dx,

(28)

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{n}

\displaystyle=\int\phi^{*}_{0}(x)\varphi_{1}^{*}(x)\phi_{n}(x)\varphi_{0}(x)dx.

(29)

The effective two-level charging Hamiltonian can be written in terms of Pauli matrices as

H_{1,\text{eff}}^{BC}=\dfrac{\delta}{2}\sigma_{z}+J\sigma_{x}+\text{const},

(30)

where the detuning
\delta=\langle 0|H_{1}^{BC}|0\rangle-\langle 1|H_{1}^{BC}|1\rangle
the difference between two diagonal elements and the off-diagonal elements
J=g_{BC}\sqrt{N_{B}}|\mathcal{I}_{n}|
are effective coupling terms.

The time-evolved state can be derived as

\displaystyle|\Psi(t)\rangle

\displaystyle=\left[\cos{\left(\dfrac{\Omega t}{2\hbar}\right)}-i\dfrac{\delta}{\Omega}\sin{\left(\dfrac{\Omega t}{2\hbar}\right)}\right]|0\rangle

\displaystyle\hskip 80.0pt-i\dfrac{2J}{\Omega}\sin{\left(\dfrac{\Omega t}{2\hbar}\right)}|1\rangle,

(31)

where
\Omega=\sqrt{(2J)^{2}+\delta^{2}}
is the frequency of the charging dynamics. The total amount of work stored in the battery is then expressed as

W_{B}(t)=n\hbar\omega_{B}\left(\dfrac{2J}{\Omega}\right)^{2}\sin^{2}\left(\dfrac{\Omega t}{2\hbar}\right),

(32)

while the ergotropy is

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{B}(t)=\max\Big[(n-1)\hbar\omega_{B}\left(\dfrac{2J}{\Omega}\right)^{2}\sin^{2}\left(\dfrac{\Omega t}{2\hbar}\right),

\displaystyle(n+1)\hbar\omega_{B}\left(\dfrac{2J}{\Omega}\right)^{2}\sin^{2}\left(\dfrac{\Omega t}{2\hbar}\right)-\hbar\omega_{B}\Big].

(33)

One can immediately see that for each value of
n
(assuming a fixed battery trap frequency (e.g.
\omega_{B}=1
)) the value of
\omega_{C}
can be tuned to meet the resonance condition, such that
\delta=0
and
\Omega=2J
. This results in the maximum energy transfer from the charger to the battery and also the largest possible ergotropy. In Appendix
B

N_{B}=2

To understand how the battery size affects the charging speed, we compute the Mandelstam–Tamm quantum speed limit time
\tau_{\text{QSL}}

Mandelstam and Tamm 1991
, which represents the shortest time required for a system to evolve from its initial state to an orthogonal state,

\tau_{\text{QSL}}=\frac{\pi}{2\sqrt{\langle\Psi(0)|\mathcal{H}^{2}|\Psi(0)\rangle-\langle\Psi(0)|\mathcal{H}|\Psi(0)\rangle^{2}}}.

(34)

Using the two-level model derived above, with
|\Psi(0)\rangle=|0\rangle
and
\mathcal{H}=H_{1}^{BC}
, one finds

\tau_{\text{QSL}}=\frac{\pi}{2g_{BC}\sqrt{N_{B}}|\mathcal{I}_{n}|}.

(35)

As long as the battery is charged using a weak coupling interaction where the system can still be described with the two-level model, the quantum speed limit time (
35
) is a tight bound for the shortest charging time to reach the maximum work transfer. Therefore, the charging power at the time when the maximum energy transfer occurs is given by

\mathcal{P}_{W}(t_{\text{max}})=\frac{W_{C}(0)}{\tau_{\text{QSL}}}=\frac{2W_{C}(0)g_{BC}\sqrt{N_{B}}|\mathcal{I}_{n}|}{\pi}.

(36)

One can increase the power by employing a stronger coupling strength
g_{BC}
between the battery and the charger. However, a strong interaction quench leads to significantly more complicated dynamics due to the creation of excitations and the production of irreversible work. In this regime, the system can no longer be approximated by a two-level model, as particles in the battery may be excited to higher energy states. Moreover, quantifying the amount of energy transferred from the charger to the battery can become challenging because substantial irreversible work production may increase the energy of both the battery and the charger. Alternatively, the power can be enhanced by adding more particles to the battery while keeping the coupling strength
g_{BC}
fixed, since the power scales with the particle numbers as
\sqrt{N_{B}}
. Alternatively, one can modify the integral
\mathcal{I}_{n}
by having finite inter-particle interactions in the battery, which will be studied in Sec.
III.3
.

For odd values
n=2k+1
, the coefficient
\mathcal{I}_{n}
can be computed as

\mathcal{I}_{n}=(-1)^{(n-1)/2}\frac{1}{\left(\frac{n-1}{2}\right)!}\sqrt{\frac{n!}{2^{\,n-1}\pi}}\frac{\omega_{B}\,\omega_{C}^{(n+1)/2}}{(\omega_{B}+\omega_{C})^{(n+2)/2}}.

(37)

.

Assuming
g_{BC}
is small, the resonance condition gives
W_{C}(0)\sim n
, and with
\omega_{B}=1
we indeed find that
|\mathcal{I}_{n}|
decreases with
n
, as shown in Fig.

(a). This implies that, for higher initial energies in the charger, the time required to reach the maximum energy transfer becomes longer. However, since
|\mathcal{I}_{n}|
decreases slowly with
n
, while the power scales with
n
as
n|\mathcal{I}_{n}|
, the overall charging power still increases as the function of
n
, but not linearly. This is not surprising, as higher excited states are less affected by the weak interaction term, leading to a decrease in interaction energy and therefore charging power. To counteract this, stronger coupling
g_{BC}
can be used to ensure the interaction energy is equal for each excitation
n
, as long as the irreversible work created is not increased. This will be discussed in more detail in the next section.

Similarly, we can compute the quantum speed-limit time when the battery consists of
N_{B}
non-interacting fermions as

\tau^{F}_{\text{QSL}}=\frac{\pi}{2g_{BC}|\mathcal{I}^{F}_{n}|},

(38)

where the overlap factor is given by

\mathcal{I}^{F}_{n}=\int\phi^{*}_{N_{B}-1}(x)\,\varphi_{1}^{*}(x)\,\phi_{N_{B}+n-1}(x)\,\varphi_{0}(x)\,dx.

(39)

This behavior can be understood in terms of Fermi blocking. Due to the Pauli exclusion principle, fermions are forbidden from occupying the same single-particle orbital. As a result, only the particle at the surface of the Fermi sea, occupying the
(N_{B}-1)
th orbital, can participate in the charging process and be excited to the
(N_{B}+n-1)
th orbital. Consequently, the collective bosonic enhancement present in the bosonic battery, where multiple particles coherently contribute to the charging dynamics through symmetric occupation of low-energy states, is entirely absent. The charging process is therefore limited to a single effective fermionic transition, and the charging power no longer exhibits any scaling with the particle number
N_{B}
.

Moreover, as
N_{B}
increases, the overlap
\mathcal{I}^{F}_{n}
further decreases. This suppression arises because the charger wave functions
\varphi_{0}(x)
and
\varphi_{1}(x)
are strongly localized near the center of the harmonic trap, while the higher-energy fermionic orbitals
\phi_{N_{B}-1}(x)
and
\phi_{N_{B}+n-1}(x)
become increasingly delocalized and oscillate rapidly over a much larger spatial region. The resulting spatial mismatch strongly suppresses the overlap between the initial and charged states, leading to a rapid decay of
|\mathcal{I}^{F}_{n}|
with increasing
N_{B}
.

As a consequence, the quantum speed-limit time
\tau^{F}_{\text{QSL}}
increases with particle number, further degrading the charging performance. The fermionic battery thus behaves effectively as a collection of independent single-particle modes, for which collective speedup and power enhancement are intrinsically forbidden, resulting in substantially reduced charging efficiency compared to the bosonic case.

III.3
Interacting battery

In this section, we investigate the many-body quantum battery with finite intra-species interaction
g_{B}\neq 0
, and examine how this interaction modifies the resonance spectrum and affect the power relative to the non-interacting scenario.
When we introduce a finite intra-species interaction among the battery particles, one can no longer use the analytical expression derived for the non-interacting case to predict the resonance conditions or the overall performance of the battery. Therefore, the new resonance conditions must be calculated numerically, first by finding degeneracies between the decoupled energy spectra of the battery and charger systems, and then fine-tuning
\omega_{C}
to ensure complete energy transfer.

\mathcal{I}_{n}

\tau_{\text{QSL}}\propto 1/\mathcal{I}_{n}

However, for larger initial energies in the charger, the resonance peak shifts to higher values of
\omega_{C}
, which increases the characteristic charging timescale more rapidly than in the non-interacting case. This shift can ultimately lead to an overall reduction in charging power relative to the non-interacting battery. However, this can be mitigated in two ways. The first is by taking larger attractive interactions
g_{B}
, thereby increasing the overlap between the battery and charger states, which reduces the QSL time. The second is by increasing the coupling strength
g_{BC}
, however, care must be taken that the irreversible work created is still small enough to remain negligible throughout the charging process.

IV
Conclusions

We have studied the charging performance and the ergotropy of a bosonic many-body quantum battery, which is charged by a single particle in a 1D harmonic oscillator. We have shown that at weak coupling
g_{BC}
to the charger, the non-interacting battery (
g_{B}=0
) can be well described by the approximated two-level model. In other words, in the weak coupling limit the essential features of the energy-transfer dynamics, such as the scaling of the charging power, the quantum speed limit time, the maximal stored work and the resonance frequency can be captured analytically. We have compared the analytical expression with the numerical results using Exact Diagonalization, confirming that the two-level approximation provides reliable predictions as long as excitations to higher-energy states remain suppressed. As the inter-species interaction becomes stronger, the system can be no longer described by the two-level model due to the larger amount of irreversible work production, as shown in Appendix.
C
.

We have shown that many-body batteries (
N_{B}>1
) exhibit a clear advantage over their single-particle counterparts. The charging time decreases with increasing particle number, leading to an enhancement of the charging power that scales as
\sqrt{N_{B}}
. This improvement is reflected in the quantum speed limit, whose reduction for larger
N_{B}
directly explains the observed speedup in energy transfer. Furthermore, in the weak coupling regime, many-body batteries maintain a comparable or even smaller amount of irreversible work generated during the sudden interaction quench, indicating that faster charging does not come at the cost of significantly increased energetic overhead. The reduced von Neumann entropy at the time of maximal charging for larger
N_{B}
further reveals that correlations between the battery and charger build up more efficiently in the many-body setting.

When finite intra-species interactions are introduced, the resonance structure becomes richer and more tunable. Repulsive interactions shift the resonance to lower charger energies and generally suppress the charging power, with performance decreasing as the system approaches the Tonks–Girardeau limit. In contrast, sufficiently strong attractive interactions shift the resonance to higher energies and can reduce the characteristic charging time, thereby enhancing the charging power beyond the non-interacting case. However, for large initial charger energies, this enhancement may be offset by the faster increased quantum speed limit time. Additionally, strong interactions lead to the appearance of multiple resonance peaks, offering further opportunities for engineering optimal operation points.

Overall, our results highlight several mechanisms through which many-body effects and interparticle interactions can be harnessed to enhance quantum battery performance. The optimal charging strategies can be achieved from a careful balance between interaction strength, particle number, and charger energy to reach the resonance conditions and decrease the QSL time. These findings open paths for the experimental realization in ultracold atom platforms, where precise control of inter-species and intra-species interactions is readily available, and suggest new directions for designing scalable and high-power quantum energy-storage devices.

In the following, we study the performance of the battery charged under strong inter-species interaction
g_{BC}
