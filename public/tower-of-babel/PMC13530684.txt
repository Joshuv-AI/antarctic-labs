Poster 105. High Patient Satisfaction Rates After Leg Fasctiotomies for Exertional Compartment Syndrome Despite Moderate Levels of Symptom Recurrence
Authors: Justin J. Ernat, Collin DR. Hunter, Timothy Torrez, Elise Vandamn, Cody McGrale
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objective:Chronic exertional compartment syndrome (CECS) is a unique cause of lower leg pain, especially in young endurance athletes such as runners and military personnel. When conservative treatment fails, patients may be indicated for leg fasciotomy to alleviate intra-compartmental pressures. Fasciotomy to treat CECS has demonstrated favorable patient reported outcomes following in the legs, however, how functional outcomes vary based on alleviation, persistence, or recurrence of symptoms is not well understood. Therefore, the purpose of this study is to determine whether the presence and/or severity of recurrent symptoms post-fasciotomy result in worse satisfaction, pain, function, and strength outcomes.Methods:This is a retrospective review of patients who underwent leg fasciotomy for CECS. Demographic data (age, sex, and body mass index (BMI)) and surgical details were collected. Individuals with available minimum 2-year follow-up were included. PROs included pain, function, and strength after surgery, each assessed using a four-level Likert scale: (1) 'Worse than prior to surgery,' (2) 'Same as prior to surgery,' (3) 'Slightly improved,' and (4) 'Much improved'. Patient satisfaction with surgery was measured on a five-level Likert scale, ranging from (1) 'Very dissatisfied' to (5) 'Very satisfied'. Additionally, patients were asked directly, 'Are you satisfied with your surgery?' with 'yes' or 'no' as answer options. Note was made of any revision fasciotomy surgery on the same leg. Patients were asking specifically if preoperative symptoms had returned postoperatively (Yes/No). Thirty-eight patients were included with most having bilateral surgery (33/38). 2-compartment fasciotomies (anterior/lateral) were most frequent (26/38). All 1-compartment fasciotomies were for the anterior compartment. A majority reported improvements: 74% (28/38) experienced 'Much improved' pain and 55% (21/38) 'Much improved' function. Regarding strength, 39% (15/38) noted 'Much improved,' while an equal 39% (15/38) reported it as the 'Same as prior to surgery.' No patients reported their pain, function, or strength as 'Worse than prior to surgery.' 92% of patients were either 'Very satisfied' (63%, 24/38) or 'Slightly satisfied' (29%, 11/38) with their surgery. Symptom recurrence occurred in 39% (15/38) of patients, with females accounting for 80% (12/15) of these cases. Despite recurrence, 53% (8/15) of this subgroup still described their pain as 'much improved,' and 80% (12/15) were at least 'slightly' or 'very' satisfied. Only one patient (2.6%) required revision fasciotomy on the same leg.Conclusions:Fasciotomy for chronic exertional compartment syndrome (CECS) demonstrated significant improvements in pain and function for the majority of patients, coupled with high overall satisfaction rates (92% very or slightly satisfied). Although strength improvements were less consistently reported as 'much improved' (39%), importantly, no patients experienced a worsening of their symptoms. These outcomes are consistent with the typical clinical presentation of CECS, predominantly affecting bilateral limbs and involving two compartments. A notable portion of patients (39%) experienced a recurrence of their symptoms postoperatively. A striking finding within this subgroup was that females constituted a significantly higher proportion (80%), suggesting a potential sex-specific predisposition to recurrence that warrants further investigation. Despite this recurrence, it is important to emphasize that most individuals continued to report meaningful improvements in their pain and function, and their overall satisfaction with the procedure remained encouragingly high. Furthermore, the requirement for revision surgery was rare, occurring in only 2.6% of the total cohort. These findings underscore that while fasciotomy is a generally effective and well-tolerated intervention for CECS, comprehensive preoperative patient counseling is paramount. This counseling should particularly address the potential for symptom recurrence, especially for female patients, while also contextualizing that recurrence does not invariably negate the procedure's overall benefits or lead to dissatisfaction for most. Future research should prioritize elucidating the specific factors contributing to symptom recurrence, including anatomical, physiological, or surgical technique-related variables, and aim to develop targeted strategies for its mitigation to further optimize long-term outcomes.
