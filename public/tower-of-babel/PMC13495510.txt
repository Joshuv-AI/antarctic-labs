Acute chest pain in a 14 year old following chronic foreign body inhalation: a case report
Authors: Ibrahim Aliyu, Mubarak Zubair Abdullahi, Halimat Mono Mohammed, Nafisa Bello, Muhammad Haruna Rasheed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundForeign body inhalation is a common, life-threatening emergency that may present acutely or remain undiagnosed for years. While it typically occurs in young children, delayed presentation in older children is also possible, particularly when the initial event goes unwitnessed.Case presentationWe present the case of a 14-year-old male of Hausa ethnicity, who developed acute right-sided chest pain, superimposed on a 4-year history of mild chronic cough. Notably, he had remained asymptomatic for 1 year following the initial foreign body inhalation event. He had a history of inhaling and aspirating a button battery at age nine, for which no diagnostic workup was conducted at that time. On current presentation, an urgent chest radiograph revealed a disc-shaped metallic foreign body in the right bronchial tree with associated middle and lower lobe collapse. A “double ring” halo sign on the X-ray indicated the object was a button battery. Chest Computed tomography (CT) confirmed the battery’s location in the right bronchus intermedius with surrounding fibrosis and multiple segmental lobar collapses. Bronchoscopic removal was attempted but failed due to occlusion of the bronchus by cicatrization and fibrosis. The patient subsequently underwent a right thoracotomy and bronchotomy to retrieve the battery. Postoperatively, the collapsed lung segments re-expanded, and his symptoms resolved.ConclusionsThis case illustrates that an inhaled foreign body can remain undetected for years, leading to chronic pulmonary changes. Inhaled button battery requires a high index of suspicion and urgent imaging—even in delayed presentations. Early diagnosis, prompt imaging and timely intervention are essential to prevent chronic complications and the need for invasive surgical procedures.

Background

Inhalation of a foreign body remains a common pediatric emergency and a significant cause of morbidity, particularly in children under 5 years [1]. However, it is not uncommon in late childhood and has been described as one of the causes of accidental death [2]. Young children are most at risk due to their tendency to place objects in their nose or mouth and their incomplete airway protective reflexes. Delayed or missed diagnoses of foreign body aspiration have been reported in older children and even adults, where chronic respiratory symptoms may mimic asthma or pulmonary eosinophilia [3]. A long-standing aspirated object can remain clinically silent or cause only mild, intermittent symptoms, leading to a late presentation years after the initial aspiration. Inhaled button batteries pose unique hazards due to their potential to induce local tissue necrosis and chronic airway damage [4]. Delayed diagnosis can lead to severe pulmonary complications, including bronchial stenosis, lung collapse, and the need for open surgery.

Among aspirated objects, button batteries are particularly dangerous. Unlike inert items, a button battery lodged in the airway can cause an electrochemical reaction that induces rapid liquefactive necrosis of the surrounding mucosa [3]. Within hours of contact, a strongly alkaline environment develops at the battery’s negative pole, leading to deep tissue injury—experimental models show bronchial cartilage damage can occur in less than 4 h of exposure [4]. Delayed diagnosis of an inhaled battery can therefore result in severe airway and pulmonary complications, including local necrosis, bronchial strictures, lung collapse, or fistula formation. We report a case of a chronically retained button battery in a teenager, highlighting the radiological imaging findings and the management challenges of this unusual presentation.

Case presentation

A 14-year-old male of Hausa ethnicity, presented with a 1-week history of acute right-sided chest pain, superimposed on a four-year history of recurrent chronic cough that began 4-year after he accidentally inhaled a button battery at age nine. On examination, breath sounds were reduced in the right lower lung zone. A chest radiograph revealed a rounded metallic opacity with a double ring sign in the right lung field, projecting between the fourth anterior and eighth posterior intercostal spaces (Fig. 1). There was evidence of right lower lobe volume loss and opacification, with obscuration of the right heart border and hemidiaphragm. Chest CT, showed a 1.4 × 1.3 cm hyperdense metallic object with streak artifacts impacted at the terminal part of the right bronchus intermedius (Fig. 2A–F). Multiple segmental lobe collapses were seen involving the medial segment of the right middle lobe and the apical, posterior basal and medial segments of the right lower lobe on the post contrast CT (Fig. 3A–D). Volume-rendered 3D reconstruction confirmed reduced right lung volume (Fig. 3E–F). Bronchoscopic retrieval was unsuccessful due to cicatrization, fibrosis and complete occlusion of the bronchus intermedius. The patient subsequently underwent thoracotomy with bronchotomy. Intraoperative findings included a collapsed right lower lobe and a button battery located in the bronchus intermedius. Postoperative period was uneventful. A 48-h postoperative chest X-ray showed opacification of the right lung field with a chest tube in situ (Fig. 4A). This was accompanied by a 6-week follow-up X-ray which showed improved aeration of the previously collapsed lung segments (Fig. 4B). Discussion

Foreign body aspiration most commonly affects young children, with the majority of cases occurring in those under 3–5 years of age [1]. The clinical presentation is classically an acute choking episode followed by coughing, wheezing, or respiratory distress. However, as this case illustrates, the diagnosis can occasionally be missed—especially if the aspiration event is unwitnessed or if early symptoms are subtle and transient [5]. A “forgotten” airway foreign body may remain lodged for months or years, with the patient experiencing only nonspecific symptoms such as chronic cough, mild dyspnea, or recurrent pneumonia. Over time, the retained object can elicit chronic inflammation, which may lead to serious complications. Late sequelae of an untreated aspirated foreign body include persistent atelectasis, bronchiectasis, lung abscesses, and bronchial stricture formation [5].

In the index patient, the long interval between aspiration and definitive diagnosis—approximately 5 years—allowed significant pulmonary collapse and fibrosis to develop. Somewhat similar delayed presentations have been documented. Razafimanjato et al. reported an 11-year-old girl who aspirated a plastic pen cap that went unrecognized for 4 years, ultimately destroying the left lung and necessitating a pneumonectomy [6].

Management of tracheobronchial foreign bodies depends on the nature, location, and duration of impaction. Bronchoscopic removal—rigid bronchoscopy in most pediatric cases—is the first-line treatment [7], and is successful in the vast majority of acute aspiration cases [6]. In chronic cases such as the one presented, however, the airway may be altered by inflammation: friability, fibrosis, and ingrowth of granulation tissue can make endoscopic retrieval extremely challenging. These factors contributed to the failure of bronchoscopic extraction in this patient. Surgical intervention is required in a small percentage of cases, where endoscopy alone cannot safely remove the foreign body. This may involve thoracotomy with bronchotomy [8]. In the index case, performing a bronchotomy allowed for removal of the battery while preserving the lung. Similar surgical strategies have been reported, including in infants, where a bronchotomy via thoracotomy was successfully used to retrieve an impacted bronchial foreign body after bronchoscopic methods failed [7, 8]. Such reports, along with our experience, suggest that bronchotomy without pneumonectomy can be a safe and effective option in complex foreign body cases, avoiding the morbidity of lung resection when the distal lung parenchyma remains viable.

Imaging played a pivotal role in the diagnosis and management of this case. A plain chest radiograph is an invaluable first step when foreign body aspiration is suspected. In this case, the chest X-ray immediately raised suspicion for a button battery by revealing the telltale “double rim” halo sign, which distinguishes it from a coin [9]. Radiographs may also show indirect signs of foreign body aspiration, such as unilateral hyperlucency (air trapping) or lobar collapse, which may be the only clues when the object is radiolucent. Computed tomography further defined the nature and exact location of the foreign body and depicted the extent of associated lung collapse. CT has high sensitivity for detecting airway foreign bodies and provides detailed anatomical information—especially in chronic cases, where complications such as bronchiectasis or abscess formation may be present. Modern multidetector CT can be combined with virtual bronchoscopy to create 3D airway visualizations, which aid in both diagnosis and surgical planning [10]. In the index patient, imaging not only confirmed the long-retained battery but also guided the surgical team by delineating the affected bronchus and collapsed lobes. This case underscores that when the history is suggestive, a normal initial exam or delayed presentation should not preclude radiologic evaluation. Early imaging is essential to expedite the diagnosis of occult or missed foreign body aspiration and to guide timely intervention.

Conclusion

This case highlights the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion for foreign body aspiration, even in older children and in patients presenting with chronic, nonspecific respiratory symptoms. A history of a choking episode—no matter how remote—should prompt thorough evaluation. Inhaled button batteries, in particular, are dangerous foreign bodies capable of causing significant airway damage if not identified and removed promptly. Early recognition and prompt radiological imaging are crucial to confirming the diagnosis and preventing complications that may necessitate open surgical intervention, such as thoracotomy and bronchotomy. While bronchoscopic removal remains the gold standard of management, timely intervention can restore lung function and prevent the severe, potentially irreversible complications associated with chronic foreign body retention.
