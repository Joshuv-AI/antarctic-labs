Background

Lymphatic malformations are benign congenital lesions arising from abnormal development of the lymphatic system. They most commonly occur in the head and neck region, whereas intra-abdominal involvement is rare, accounting for less than 10% of cases [1, 2]. Among abdominal lesions, intestinal and mesenteric lymphatic malformations are particularly uncommon [3].

Although histologically benign, intra-abdominal lymphatic malformations may lead to significant morbidity due to complications, such as bowel obstruction, volvulus, intussusception, gastrointestinal bleeding, or acute abdomen [3–7]. Clinical manifestations vary widely, ranging from incidental findings to life-threatening emergencies. Consequently, these lesions often mimic other conditions, including hemangiomas, inflammatory bowel disease, intestinal tuberculosis, or enteric duplication cysts [6, 8].

Definitive diagnosis is usually established by histopathological examination. Surgical excision remains the mainstay of treatment for symptomatic lesions, with complete resection associated with the lowest risk of recurrence [1, 5, 9, 10]. We report a rare case of microcystic lymphatic malformation of the small intestine presenting as acute abdominal pain in a child and review the relevant literature.

Case presentation

A previously healthy 5-year-old Vietnamese boy with no significant past medical, family, or psychosocial history was admitted with acute onset abdominal pain, vomiting, and fever lasting 1 day. There was no history of trauma, gastrointestinal bleeding, or chronic abdominal symptoms. There were no prior medical or surgical interventions.

On physical examination, the patient was febrile. The abdomen was soft, with mild tenderness and guarding localized to the hypogastric region. No abdominal mass was palpable.

Abdominal ultrasonography revealed a mixed echogenic mass measuring 56 × 33 mm in the hypogastric region, composed of multiple cystic components (Fig. 1). Contrast-enhanced computed tomography demonstrated an abnormal segment of small bowel in the left abdomen, approximately 101 mm in length, with irregular wall thickening up to 11 mm, loss of normal mural stratification, scattered calcifications, and tortuous dilated intramural vessels (Fig. 2). Differential diagnoses included vascular malformation, intestinal tuberculosis, and inflammatory bowel disease.

Contrast-enhanced CT scan. An abnormal 101-mm small bowel segment with irregular wall thickening (11 mm), scattered calcifications, and tortuous intramural vessels

Given the acute presentation and inconclusive imaging findings, exploratory laparotomy was performed. Intraoperatively, a segment of small bowel was markedly thickened and hypervascularized, with dark purple discoloration of the serosal surface (Fig. 3). Segmental bowel resection with primary end-to-end anastomosis was carried out.

Intraoperative and gross findings. a Intraoperative view of the thickened, hypervascularized, and dark purple small bowel segment. b Resected 7 × 4 cm bowel segment with a brownish-black surface and multiple small cysts (0.1–0.3 cm)

Gross examination of the specimen revealed a 7 × 4 cm bowel segment with a brownish-black surface and multiple small cysts measuring 0.1–0.3 cm. Histopathological analysis demonstrated numerous dilated lymphatic channels lined by flattened endothelial cells, diffusely infiltrating the mucosa, submucosa, muscularis propria, and serosa (Fig. 4). Focal intralesional hemorrhage was present. These findings confirmed the diagnosis of microcystic lymphatic malformation of the small intestine with secondary hemorrhage.

Histopathological examination (H&E stain). a Low-power magnification showing disorganized lymphatic vessel proliferation infiltrating all layers of the intestinal wall. b High-power magnification revealing lymphatic channels lined by flattened endothelial cells with focal intralesional hemorrhage

The postoperative course was uneventful. The patient received short-term parenteral nutrition, gradually resumed oral intake, and was discharged on postoperative day 7. He remained asymptomatic at follow-up.

Imaging findings

Abdominal ultrasound: Revealed a mixed echogenic mass in the hypogastric region, measuring 56 × 33 mm, containing multiple fluid-filled cystic components.

Contrast-enhanced CT scan: Confirmed an abnormal small bowel segment in the left abdomen, approximately 101 mm long. The bowel wall was irregularly thickened (up to 11 mm) with a loss of normal layered architecture. Notably, scattered calcifications and tortuous, dilated intramural vessels were observed. Initial differential diagnoses included venous malformation, intestinal tuberculosis, or Crohn’s disease. However, due to nonspecific imaging features and the acute clinical presentation, a definitive preoperative diagnosis could not be established.

Surgical management

Based on the clinical and imaging findings, the surgical board indicated an exploratory laparotomy. Intraoperatively, a segment of the small bowel was found to be thickened and hypervascularized, showing a dark purple appearance on the serosal surface. The affected segment was resected, and a primary end-to-end anastomosis was performed.

Pathological findings

Gross examination: A resected bowel segment measuring 7 × 4 cm, brownish-black with signs of hemorrhage and multiple small cysts (0.1–0.3 cm in diameter).

Microscopic examination: Histopathology showed proliferation of numerous small lymphatic vessels lined by flattened endothelial cells. These vascular structures were disorganized and infiltrated across all layers of the intestinal wall, including the mucosa, submucosa, muscularis, and serosa. Focal areas of hemorrhage into the vascular lumens were noted.

Final diagnosis: Intestinal Lymphatic Malformation (Lymphangioma) with hemorrhage.

Conclusions and discussion

Discussion

Intra-abdominal and intestinal lymphatic malformations are rare and continue to pose diagnostic challenges in pediatric surgery. Early series by Roisman et al. described intra-abdominal lymphangiomas as a heterogeneous group of lesions with variable clinical presentations, often leading to delayed or incorrect diagnosis [3].

Diagnostic considerations

Radiological differentiation between lymphatic malformations and other vascular lesions, such as hemangiomas or venous malformations, remains difficult. Both may present as localized bowel wall thickening with associated hypervascularity. Calcifications within a lesion often suggest venous malformations due to phlebolith formation; however, dystrophic calcifications have also been described in chronic lymphatic malformations [6, 11]. In the present case, the presence of tortuous intramural vessels and scattered calcifications on computed tomography initially raised suspicion of a vascular tumor.

Advanced endoscopic techniques, including wireless capsule endoscopy, have been increasingly used to identify small bowel lesions responsible for obscure gastrointestinal bleeding [12–14]. Nevertheless, in patients presenting with acute abdominal symptoms and suspected complications, surgical exploration remains the most appropriate diagnostic and therapeutic approach.

Pathological classification and evolution of terminology

Histopathological examination remains the gold standard for diagnosis. Historically, lymphatic malformations of the gastrointestinal tract were commonly reported as cavernous lymphangiomas, characterized by dilated lymphatic channels lined by flattened endothelial cells infiltrating multiple layers of the bowel wall [5–7]. Numerous published case reports of small intestinal lesions have employed this terminology [5–9].

However, contemporary peer-reviewed classifications emphasize that such lesions represent developmental vascular anomalies rather than true neoplasms. According to the widely accepted classification summarized by Wassef et al., these lesions are now grouped under lymphatic malformations and further subdivided into macrocystic, microcystic, and mixed types based on cyst size and architectural features [2]. Microcystic lymphatic malformations are characterized by numerous small lymphatic channels that infiltrate surrounding tissues and do not form large, well-defined cysts [2, 10].

In the present case, the lesion consisted of multiple small cystic spaces measuring 0.1–0.3 cm with diffuse involvement of all layers of the intestinal wall and absence of macroscopic cysts. Based on these features, the lesion is best classified as a microcystic lymphatic malformation of the small intestine. Secondary intralesional hemorrhage likely contributed to the acute clinical presentation by causing rapid expansion of the bowel wall, as described in previous reports [8, 9, 15].

Management and outcomes

Complete surgical excision remains the treatment of choice for symptomatic intestinal lymphatic malformations. Alqahtani et al. demonstrated significantly lower recurrence rates following complete resection compared with partial excision or nonoperative approaches [1]. Although medical therapies such as sclerotherapy or sirolimus have shown promise in complex cervicofacial lymphatic malformations [10], their role in intestinal involvement remains limited. Therefore, surgery remains the definitive treatment in most cases.

This case supports existing evidence that timely surgical intervention provides both definitive diagnosis and excellent clinical outcomes in pediatric patients with symptomatic small intestinal lymphatic malformations.

This case highlights the diagnostic challenges of intestinal lymphatic malformations due to their rarity and nonspecific clinical and radiological features.

A major strength of this report is the detailed correlation between imaging findings, intraoperative appearance, and histopathological confirmation.

However, this report is limited by its nature as a single case, and the findings may not be generalizable to all patients with similar presentations.

Conclusions

Microcystic lymphatic malformation of the small intestine is an exceptionally rare cause of acute abdominal pain in children. Owing to nonspecific clinical and radiological features, preoperative diagnosis remains challenging. Complete surgical resection allows definitive diagnosis and offers the best chance for cure with a minimal risk of recurrence.
