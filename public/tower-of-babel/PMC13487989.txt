ABSTRACT
BackgroundParaneoplastic neurologic syndromes (PNS) are rare immune-mediated disorders that may precede the diagnosis of an underlying malignancy, including non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC). Tumor heterogeneity in NSCLC, particularly in rare subtypes such as adenosquamous carcinoma, may result in variable histologic findings across different biopsy sites, posing significant diagnostic challenges.Case presentationWe report a case of a 45-year-old Tanzanian male, a never-smoker, who presented with progressive neurologic symptoms, including ageusia, dystonia, paresthesia, and ataxia, without respiratory complaints. Brain Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) demonstrated diffuse cerebral microhemorrhages, and extensive autoimmune and neuronal antibody testing was negative. He received intravenous methylprednisolone followed by an oral prednisolone taper with transient stabilization. Positron emission tomography–computed tomography (PET-CT) revealed an ill-defined FDG-avid right lower lobe lesion and vertebral metastases. Biopsy of a T10 vertebral lesion confirmed metastatic lung adenocarcinoma (TTF-1 positive), and the disease was staged as stage IV (cTxNxM1b). The patient was treated with carboplatin and pemetrexed, with partial neurologic improvement. During disease progression, repeat bronchoscopy biopsy revealed tumor cells with squamous features (p63 positive, patchy TTF-1 expression), raising the possibility of mixed histology (adenosquamous). However, histologic transformation cannot be completely excluded due to a lack of molecular analysis. His condition deteriorated rapidly, and he died from complications of advanced metastatic disease with progressive neurologic involvement.ConclusionThis case highlights the diagnostic challenges of antibody-negative paraneoplastic neurologic syndromes. The study underscores the important role of PET-CT in identifying occult malignancy and the role of repeat biopsy in progressive disease. Discordant histologic findings should be interpreted cautiously, as they may reflect tumor heterogeneity or possible adenosquamous carcinoma rather than true histologic evolution. Limited molecular testing, and sampling constraints remain important challenges, particularly in resource-limited settings.

Introduction

Paraneoplastic neurologic syndromes (PNS) are immune-mediated disorders of the central or peripheral nervous system that occur in association with malignancies but are not due to direct tumor invasion or metastasis [1]. Lung cancer, particularly small-cell lung cancer, is one of the most frequently associated malignancies [2]. Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), although less common, can also give rise to PNS [3].

PNS is not caused by direct tumor invasion, metastasis, or treatment-related complications, but rather by an autoimmune response against shared neuronal and tumor antigens. A hallmark of PNS is the presence of well-characterized onconeural antibodies in the serum or cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), which serve as highly specific markers of an underlying neoplasm. These antibodies include anti-Hu (ANNA-1), anti-Yo (PCA-1), anti-Ri (ANNA-2), anti-CV2 (CRMP5), anti-Ma2 (PNMA2), and anti-Amphiphysin. Additionally, antibodies targeting cell surface antigens such as anti-NMDA receptor, anti-AMPA receptor (GluR1, GluR2), anti-GABA < sub > B < /sub > receptor, anti-LGI1, and anti-CASPR2 are often found in autoimmune encephalitis with or without an associated tumor [1, 4, 5]. Some of these antibodies are directly pathogenic and respond to immunotherapy (e.g., NMDA receptor encephalitis, anti-GABA < sub > B < /sub > receptor encephalitis), whereas others reflect T-cell-mediated cytotoxicity against intracellular antigens and are less responsive to immunosuppression [4, 5]. Clinically, PNS may present with rapidly progressive cerebellar degeneration, limbic encephalitis, sensory neuronopathy, opsoclonus-myoclonus, or Lambert-Eaton myasthenic syndrome, and often precedes the diagnosis of malignancy [4] Thus, their detection not only aids in diagnosing an occult tumor but also informs prognosis and therapeutic strategy.

Lung cancer remains a biologically heterogeneous disease, with multiple histological subtypes that may coexist within the same tumor. Among these, adenosquamous carcinoma is a rare subtype of NSCLC, accounting for approximately 0.4%-4% of cases, and is defined by the presence of both adenocarcinoma and squamous cell carcinoma components, each comprising at least 10% of the tumor [6]. It comprise of 51.5% of all adenosquamous tumors [7]. This subtype is clinically important due to its aggressive behavior, frequent presentation at the advanced stage, and poorer prognosis compared to pure adenocarcinoma or squamous cell carcinoma [8]. Importantly, the coexistence of distinct histologic components within a single tumor introduces diagnostic challenges, particularly when different biopsy sites yield discordant morphologic findings [9].

Recent advances in cancer biology have emphasized that tumor histology should not be viewed as a fixed entity, but rather as part of a dynamic spectrum influenced by tumor heterogeneity and lineage plasticity. Lineage plasticity refers to the ability of tumor cells to transition between phenotypic states, contributing to intratumoral diversity and therapeutic resistance [10]. In lung cancer, this phenomenon may manifest as the coexistence of multiple histologic subtypes within the same tumor or as apparent changes in histology over time. Notably, studies have demonstrated that up to approximately 5% of lung tumors may exhibit combined histology, including adenosquamous carcinoma, supporting the concept that tumor histology represents a continuum rather than discrete categories [6, 10, 11].

In clinical practice, distinguishing between true histologic transformation and sampling of different components of a heterogeneous tumor, such as adenosquamous carcinoma, remains challenging. This distinction is particularly important because it has implications for prognosis, therapeutic decision-making, and interpretation of disease progression [12]. While histologic transformation has been reported, it is relatively uncommon and typically requires molecular confirmation demonstrating clonal evolution [10]. In contrast, adenosquamous carcinoma may present with discordant histologic findings across biopsy sites, reflecting the tumor's inherent heterogeneity [12].

In this report, we present a case of advanced lung cancer initially diagnosed as metastatic adenocarcinoma in the setting of probable PNS, with subsequent biopsy demonstrating squamous cell carcinoma. This case highlights the diagnostic challenges in distinguishing tumor heterogeneity from true histologic transformation and underscores the importance of considering adenosquamous carcinoma as a plausible explanation, particularly in resource-limited settings where comprehensive molecular profiling may not be readily available.

Case presentation

A 45-year-old, previously healthy Tanzanian male presented in May 2023 with ageusia, which was followed by progressive paresthesia involving the left face, limbs, and trunk (Table 1). The patient had no known chronic medical illnesses, including hypertension, diabetes mellitus, or autoimmune disease, and had not undergone any prior major surgeries. He was not taking any long-term medications before symptom onset. There was no history of previous malignancy or tuberculosis. He had no significant family history of cancer or neurological disorders. Psychosocially, he was employed in a non-industrial occupation with no known exposure to asbestos, heavy metals, or other occupational carcinogens. He reported no history of cigarette smoking or alcohol misuse. He reported no chest symptoms, night sweats, or weight loss. Over the following weeks, he developed slurred speech, insomnia, left-sided dystonia, and unsteady gait. Neurological examination revealed left hemifacial sensory loss, reduced pinprick sensation on the left side of the body, brisk reflexes, dysdiadochokinesia, and ataxia, with preserved higher mental functions. An electroencephalogram (EEG) demonstrated generalized background slowing, consistent with diffuse cerebral dysfunction. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain showed multiple hyperintense foci suggestive of hemorrhagic lesions in the cerebral hemispheres, basal ganglia, thalami, brainstem, and cerebellum, with additional hemorrhagic foci in the subarachnoid space and Sylvian fissure. He had elevated CA 19‑9; however, the CT scan of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis was normal (Fig. 1). Given the presence of multifocal hemorrhagic brain lesions, several alternative diagnoses were considered. These included cerebral metastases, primary central nervous system vasculitis, infectious etiologies, and other autoimmune encephalitis. Routine laboratory investigations, including complete blood count, metabolic panel, inflammatory markers, and infectious screening, were unremarkable. Neuroimaging showed diffuse microhemorrhages without a discrete enhancing mass lesion to suggest metastatic disease. Given the strong clinical suspicion of immune-mediated PNS, the patient was treated with high-dose intravenous methylprednisolone (1 g daily for 5 consecutive days), followed by oral prednisolone at 60 mg once daily with a gradual taper over four weeks. This corticosteroid therapy was initiated before referral abroad for Positron emission tomography–computed tomography (PET-CT) and advanced cerebrospinal fluid molecular testing. Neurological outcome

Serological investigations for autoimmune and paraneoplastic encephalitis were negative, including NMDA, AMPA-R1, AMPA-R2, GABA-B receptor, LGI1, and CASPR2 antibodies. A comprehensive CSF neuronal antibody panel, including Amphiphysin, CV2, PNMA2, Ri, Yo, Hu, Recoverin, SOX1, Titin, Zic4, GAD65, and Tr antibodies, was also negative. ANA was negative, but ANCA was positive with an atypical pattern. According to the updated PNS-Care diagnostic criteria [4], this patient presented with a high-risk neurologic phenotype (encephalitis/encephalopathy) in temporal association with lung cancer, but with negative high- and intermediate-risk neuronal antibodies. While antibody negativity limits classification as “definite” PNS, the clinical phenotype, together with confirmed malignancy, supports a diagnosis of probable paraneoplastic neurologic syndrome. Using the PNS-Care scoring system (high-risk phenotype = 3; cancer = 4; antibodies = 0), the estimated score was 7, corresponding to probable PNS (Table 3), although antibody-negative cases should be interpreted cautiously. These criteria emphasize that antibody negativity does not exclude PNS, particularly in resource-limited settings where advanced confirmatory testing may not be readily available. These findings suggested a paraneoplastic process in the absence of confirmed autoimmune or infectious causes. A PET-CT scan revealed an FDG-avid, ill-defined soft tissue lesion in the superior segment of the right lower lobe of the lung, suspicious for malignancy. There were also mildly metabolically active lytic–sclerotic skeletal lesions, small nodules along the right lung fissures, and multiple cerebral hemorrhagic foci corresponding to previously identified lesions on MRI. Interestingly, the PET-CT also demonstrated suppressed FDG uptake in the cerebral cortex with relatively increased activity in the frontotemporal and peri-Sylvian regions, reinforcing the suspicion of a paraneoplastic encephalitic process.

Further imaging with spinal MRI showed lesions at the T10 and L5 vertebrae. A CT-guided biopsy from the T10 vertebral lesion revealed metastatic carcinoma. Immunohistochemistry (IHC) analysis of the biopsy tissue demonstrated positivity for CK7 and TTF-1, and negativity for CK20, p40, CDX2, EGFR, and ALK-1, supporting a diagnosis of primary lung adenocarcinoma (Fig. 2). Comprehensive molecular testing, including EGFR mutation analysis and confirmatory testing for ALK rearrangement, was not performed due to financial constraints. EGFR assessment by IHC is not a validated method for mutation detection; therefore, the presence of actionable driver mutations cannot be definitively excluded. The PD-L1 expression was 0%, indicating limited suitability for immunotherapy at that stage. At the time of diagnosis, the primary lung lesion was not clearly detectable on conventional CT imaging. It was only suspected as an ill-defined FDG-avid focus on PET-CT. The patient was initiated on a chemotherapy regimen consisting of carboplatin and pemetrexed. The patient’s functional status at initiation of systemic therapy was ECOG performance status 2. He received one cycle abroad and completed three additional cycles in Tanzania. During treatment, his neurological symptoms showed transient improvement (Table 2). However, one-month post-chemotherapy, he developed a recurrent productive cough and constitutional symptoms. A repeat CT scan of the chest revealed a 2 × 2 cm mass in the superior segment of the right lower lobe, and the disease was restaged as stage IV (cT1bN0M1b) (Fig. 1B). A measurable lung mass became evident later in the course, consistent with the suspected pulmonary primary. The MRI brain showed increased microhemorrhages (Fig. 1D). A subsequent bronchoscopy revealed endobronchial obstruction. A biopsy was taken, and histopathological analysis revealed a malignancy with squamous cell carcinoma (Fig. 3). The IHC marker for p63 was strongly positive in tumor cells, indicating the presence of a squamous component. IHC for TTF-1 showed patchy nuclear positivity in a subset of tumor cells, suggesting a glandular component consistent with a tumor of lung origin. These findings indicate the coexistence of glandular and squamous features and raise the possibility of adenosquamous carcinoma. Discussion

This case highlights several clinically important issues. First, the initial presentation with multifocal neurological deficits and negative autoimmune panels underscores the diagnostic complexity of paraneoplastic neurological syndromes. PNS can precede cancer diagnosis by weeks to months and may be the only early manifestation of an occult malignancy. In this case, despite negative CSF and autoimmune serology, persistent neurological symptoms and hemorrhagic brain lesions prompted a PET-CT, which identified a metabolically active pulmonary lesion and spinal metastasis, leading to tissue diagnosis.

Although plasmapheresis is sometimes considered in severe autoimmune encephalitis, it was not performed in this case due to limited local availability, high cost, and logistical challenges associated with accessing specialized apheresis services. In addition, the patient’s extensive neuronal antibody testing was negative, and management focused primarily on treatment of the underlying malignancy, which remains the cornerstone of therapy in paraneoplastic neurologic syndromes. These constraints reflect the practical challenges of delivering advanced neuroimmunologic care in resource-limited settings.

Paraneoplastic cerebellar degeneration (PCD) is one of the classical presentations of PNS, typically associated with small-cell lung cancer but increasingly recognized in adenocarcinoma as well. A case report from Iraq described a patient with advanced lung adenocarcinoma who presented with progressive cerebellar symptoms, ataxia, dysmetria, and dysarthria [14]. Despite an extensive work-up, including negative paraneoplastic antibody panels (e.g., anti-Hu, anti-Yo, anti-Ri), the clinical diagnosis of PCD was made based on the classical neurologic presentation, exclusion of alternative causes, and subsequent identification of an underlying malignancy. This case reinforces an important principle in the diagnosis of PNS: a negative paraneoplastic antibody panel does not exclude the diagnosis. According to updated diagnostic criteria established by the PNS-Care Panel and literature reviews, a definite diagnosis of PNS can still be made in the setting of a well-characterized neurologic syndrome when accompanied by a temporally associated malignancy [4]. Several pathophysiologic mechanisms account for seronegative PNS. These include (1) immune responses mediated primarily by cytotoxic T-cells rather than B-cell–derived antibodies; (2) paraneoplastic antigens not yet identified or not included in commercial testing panels; and (3) immune privilege of the central nervous system, leading to antibody production restricted to the CNS without spillover into serum or CSF[15].

Furthermore, the patient in the report showed neurologic improvement following corticosteroid therapy and initiation of systemic cancer treatment, supporting the hypothesis of immune-mediated pathogenesis. This therapeutic response further substantiates the diagnosis of PNS, even in the absence of antibody confirmation. Taken together with our case, this evidence highlights the need for a high index of clinical suspicion for PNS in patients presenting with subacute neurologic symptoms and known or suspected malignancy. It also emphasizes the importance of integrating clinical presentation with oncologic and neurologic findings to guide timely diagnosis and intervention. In the setting of NSCLC, particularly adenocarcinoma, clinicians should remain alert to rare but impactful presentations such as PCD, which may offer the first clinical clue to the underlying cancer or significantly alter the patient’s functional prognosis.

A key diagnostic challenge in this case relates to the discordant histologic findings observed during the disease course. The vertebral biopsy demonstrated TTF-1-positive metastatic adenocarcinoma, while a subsequent bronchoscopic biopsy revealed tumor cells with squamous carcinoma, characterized by strong p63 positivity and only patchy TTF-1 expression. Although such findings may raise the possibility of histologic transformation, this interpretation should be approached with caution, particularly in the absence of molecular confirmation [16].

An alternative explanation is that this tumor may represent adenosquamous carcinoma, a rare subtype of non-small cell lung cancer composed of both adenocarcinoma and squamous cell carcinoma components. However, each of these should constitute at least 10% of the tumor to establish this diagnosis [6]. In the present case, however, this distinction could not be definitively confirmed, as the biopsies were obtained from different anatomical sites and did not allow a comprehensive assessment of the proportional representation of each component within a single tumor specimen. Therefore, while the findings are suggestive of mixed histology, a formal diagnosis of adenosquamous carcinoma remains limited by sampling constraints.

Similar observations have been reported in the literature, where discordant histologic findings between primary and metastatic sites were ultimately attributed to adenosquamous carcinoma rather than true histologic transformation. For instance, a case of ALK-rearranged lung cancer demonstrated adenocarcinoma in the primary tumor and squamous cell carcinoma in metastatic lymph nodes, with molecular analysis confirming a common clonal origin [17]. This finding supports the concept that tumors with mixed histology may metastasize as a single component and that sampling variability can mimic apparent histologic transformation. In our case, although molecular confirmation was not available, a similar mechanism of tumor heterogeneity provides a plausible explanation for the observed discordant histologic findings.

The observed discordance in histology may instead reflect intratumoral heterogeneity, a well-recognized feature of lung cancer biology. Emerging evidence suggests that tumor histology exists along a spectrum rather than as a fixed entity, with the capacity for phenotypic diversity within the same tumor [10]. A subset of lung cancers may exhibit combined histology, including adenosquamous carcinoma, supporting the concept of a shared clonal origin with divergent differentiation [11, 12]. In this context, the vertebral metastasis may represent the adenocarcinoma component, whereas the bronchial biopsy may have sampled an area with a squamous component.

Histologic transformation from adenocarcinoma to squamous cell carcinoma has been described, particularly in the context of targeted therapy resistance; it remains relatively uncommon and typically requires molecular confirmation demonstrating clonal evolution [16]. In the present case, such confirmation was not feasible due to limited molecular testing. Importantly, EGFR status was assessed using IHC, which is not a validated method for determining EGFR mutation status in lung adenocarcinoma. Molecular techniques such as polymerase chain reaction (PCR) or next-generation sequencing are required for accurate assessment; therefore, EGFR mutation cannot be definitively excluded in this patient [8, 18, 19]. This represents a significant limitation in interpreting tumor biology and potential mechanisms underlying the observed histologic variation.

This distinction has important clinical implications. Adenosquamous carcinoma and tumors exhibiting significant histologic heterogeneity are generally associated with more aggressive clinical behavior, higher metastatic potential, and poorer prognosis compared to other NSCLC subtypes [7, 12]. The rapid clinical deterioration observed in this patient is consistent with this aggressive phenotype. Furthermore, tumor heterogeneity may influence treatment response, as different tumor components may exhibit variable sensitivity to chemotherapy or targeted therapies.

Finally, this case highlights the diagnostic complexity of antibody-negative paraneoplastic neurologic syndromes and the critical role of PET-CT in identifying occult malignancy when conventional imaging is non-diagnostic. The discordant histologic findings are more consistent with tumor heterogeneity, with a possible explanation being adenosquamous carcinoma, although this could not be definitively established due to sampling limitations and lack of molecular testing.

Conclusion

This case highlights the complexity of diagnosing and managing paraneoplastic neurologic syndromes in the setting of occult malignancy, particularly when antibody testing is negative. The patient’s presentation highlights the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion for paraneoplastic processes even in the absence of confirmatory serological markers. Notably, PET-CT played a pivotal role in this case by identifying the underlying malignancy when conventional imaging was non-diagnostic, thereby enabling tissue diagnosis and guiding oncologic management. Furthermore, the discordant histologic findings observed during disease progression illustrate the challenges in distinguishing true histologic transformation from tumor heterogeneity. While the coexistence of adenocarcinoma and squamous features raises the possibility of adenosquamous carcinoma, definitive diagnosis was limited by sampling constraints and the requirement that each component constitutes at least 10% of the tumor. The absence of comprehensive molecular testing further restricts confirmation of clonal relationships and tumor biology. Collectively, this case emphasizes the need for an integrated diagnostic approach combining clinical, radiologic, and pathologic data, the value of repeat biopsy, and the importance of expanding access to advanced imaging and molecular diagnostics, particularly in resource-limited settings.
