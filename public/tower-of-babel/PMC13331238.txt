Type I interferons enhance B cell activation and promote differentiation of double negative 2 cells in SLE
Authors: Zoha Faheem, Giselle M Boukhaled, Carol Nassar, Kieran Manion, Michael Kim, Dennisse Bonilla, Dafna D Gladman, Laura Whittall Garcia, Zahi Touma, David G Brooks, Joan Wither
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesSLE is characterised by unpredictable flares and periods of quiescence. Currently, the mechanisms driving flares remain incompletely understood. Type I interferon (IFN) signalling is strongly implicated in SLE pathogenesis and promotes expansion of age-associated B cells in murine lupus, which are phenotypically similar to double negative 2 (DN2) B cells in humans. We investigated how IFN exposure influences B cell activation and differentiation in SLE, and whether type I IFN promotes DN2 differentiation in human naïve B cells in vitro.MethodsA cytometry by time of flight panel was developed to identify B cell subsets, markers associated with B cell activation and IFN-stimulated proteins (ISPs), with a composite ISP score used as a surrogate for IFN exposure. Peripheral blood mononuclear cells from 18 healthy controls (HCs), 25 quiescent patients and 41 recently flaring patients with SLE were analysed. HC naïve B cells were stimulated under conditions that promote or inhibit DN2 differentiation with and without IFNs.ResultsAll B cell subsets exhibited higher ISP levels in flaring than quiescent disease. DN2 cells were more abundant in flaring patients and correlated with the ISP score. ISP scores strongly correlated with markers associated with B cell activation even after controlling for disease status. In vitro, IFNα and IFNβ promoted DN2 differentiation in HC B cells. In patients treated with the type I IFN receptor-blocking therapy anifrolumab, DN2 frequency and ISP scores significantly decreased.ConclusionsType I IFN promotes human B cell activation and DN2 differentiation, supporting IFN blockade as a strategy to reduce pathogenic B cells and prevent SLE flares.

Introduction

SLE is characterised by episodic disease flares interspersed with variable periods of disease quiescence in the majority of patients.1 A hallmark of SLE is the production of ANAs that deposit in tissues and elicit inflammation.24 While the levels of ANAs, such as anti-double-stranded DNA antibodies (anti-dsDNA Abs), and the cells that secrete them parallel disease flares in a subset of patients,58 the precise immunological mechanisms that lead to flares of disease have not been fully elucidated.

One family of cytokines that may act to promote flares in SLE are interferons (IFNs). Type I IFNs, such as IFNα and IFNβ, have pleiotropic effects on the immune system, many of which are predicted to enhance the immune dysregulation in SLE.9 10 The majority of patients with SLE have elevated levels of IFN-stimulated gene (ISG) expression in their peripheral blood,11 12 and elevated blood levels of ISGs or IFNα are associated with an increased likelihood of subsequent flares.13 14 They are also associated with more severe disease, as indicated by an increased requirement for corticosteroid and immunosuppressive treatment over the subsequent 5 years.13 Conversely, treatment of SLE with anifrolumab, a monoclonal antibody that blocks the type I IFN receptor (IFNAR), is associated with an increased likelihood of achieving a low disease activity state and remission.15

In previous work, we showed that injection of an adenovirus that produces sustained elevation of blood IFNα levels into mice that were genetically engineered to have increased numbers of anti-DNA reactive B cells resulted in increased B cell activation and production of anti-dsDNA Abs.16 These changes were accompanied by expansion of a subset of B cells known as age-associated B cells (ABCs) that are increased in mouse strains with lupus.1719 Using mice with a conditional knockout of IFNAR in their B cells, IFNα was shown to act directly on B cells to promote ABC development.16

There are several B cell populations in humans reported to have the CD11c+/hi T-bet+ phenotype of murine ABCs, including activated naïve (CD27−IgDloCD21lo), double negative 2 (DN2; CD27−IgD−CD21lo), reactivated memory (CD27loIgD−CD21lo) and activated memory (CD27+IgD−CD21lo) B cells, with the three CD27−/lo B cell subsets reported to be increased in SLE.62022 In SLE, DN2 or reactivated memory CD11c+T-bet+ cells have transcriptional changes suggesting that they are the immediate precursors of autoantibody-secreting cells6 20 21 and in cross-sectional studies, patients with SLE with elevated levels of DN2 cells were shown to have higher ISG expression in their peripheral blood.20 23 In a recent longitudinal study examining the peripheral blood of patients with SLE, we showed that the immunological changes cosegregated into five distinct clusters, one of which was characterised by increased B cell activation and expansion of ABCs, as well as T peripheral helper cells, which have been proposed to support ABC differentiation.23 Notably, this cluster contained the majority of patients that were acutely flaring at baseline and remained active or that reflared on follow-up. It also had the highest levels of ISG expression as compared with the other clusters.

Taken together, the above findings suggest that one mechanism by which type I IFNs promote flares is by direct effects on B cells, leading to increased activation and differentiation to ABCs and autoantibody-secreting cells. In this study, we use several different approaches to demonstrate the importance of type I IFNs in the development of these populations, confirming the importance of these cytokines as therapeutic targets in SLE.

Materials and methods

Study participants

All patients with SLE were recruited through the University of Toronto Lupus Clinic, ≥18 years old and satisfied the 2019 European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology (EULAR)/American College of Rheumatology (ACR) classification criteria for diagnosis of SLE.24 The cohort used for analysis of IFN-stimulated protein (ISP) expression was derived from the previously published SLE flare cohort (online supplemental table 1),23 whereas that used to investigate the impact of anifrolumab on B cell phenotypes was newly recruited (online supplemental table 2). Additional details in the online supplemental information.

Cell staining

Frozen archived peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from baseline visit (Flare study) or 6–12 months post-treatment (anifrolumab study) were thawed and stained for cytometry by time of flight (CyTOF), as previously published with minor changes (see online supplemental information 1).25

Measurement and calculation of ISG scores

Total RNA was isolated from blood archived in Tempus tubes (Applied Biosystems) and gene expression quantified using a custom NanoString (NanoString Technologies), as previously reported.23 Three sets of ISGs were examined that were representative of the M1.2 (ISG.1), M3.4 (ISG.2) and M5.12 (ISG.3) gene clusters previously identified on transcriptomic profiling of patients with SLE.26 Log2 normalised expression levels of the ISGs were summed to generate composite scores for each cluster. Further details are provided in the online supplemental information.

B cell differentiation assay

Freshly isolated PBMCs from ANA-negative healthy controls were purified for naïve B cells by negative selection (online supplemental figure 1) and differentiated in vitro using an established protocol.21 Further details are provided in the online supplemental information.

Cellular phenotyping and statistical analysis

For the CyTOF analysis, batch correction was done using CyCombine27 on all phenotyping markers and then adjusted for differences in marker expression based on a custom arcsinh transformation. The phenotypes of the cell subsets were then determined and analysed, as previously described.25 Full details are contained within the online supplemental information.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or plans for dissemination of our research.

Results

IFN-stimulated protein expression is increased in the B cell subsets of patients with SLE and correlates with B cell activation

To examine the association between IFN levels and B cell activation/differentiation, the B cell populations of 41 patients with SLE with a recent flare, 25 quiescent patients with SLE and 18 healthy controls were examined by CyTOF. The panel included antibodies identifying key B cell populations, their activation state and six ISPs as surrogate markers of ISG expression, ISG15, IFIT3, MX1, PKR, IRF7 and BST2.26

Seven distinct B cell subsets were identified (figure 1A). This included naïve (CD27−IgM+IgD+CD10−), transitional (CD27−IgMhiIgDloCD10+), activated naïve (CD27−IgM+IgD+CD11c+T-bet+), DN2 (CD27−IgD−CD11chiT-bet+CD21−), unswitched memory (CD27+IgM+IgD+), switched memory (CD27+IgD−IgM−) and plasmablast (CD27hiCD38hiCD19lo) populations (figure 1B). While a number of differences were seen in the abundance of the individual B cell populations between healthy controls and quiescent or flaring patients with lupus, consistent with our previous results using flow cytometry,23 the most significant difference seen between flaring and quiescent patients was in the proportion of DN2 cells, which was markedly elevated in flaring patients (figure 1C).

To compare the levels of ISPs in the different B cell subsets between study participant groups, a global ISP score that incorporated the levels of the six ISPs was calculated.25 As shown in figure 1D, global ISP scores were significantly higher in both quiescent and flaring patients with SLE as compared with healthy controls for all B cell subsets, with the highest levels of ISPs observed in plasmablasts. We and others have shown that the global IFN signature is significantly higher in flaring than quiescent patients with SLE,2830 and these differences were reflected by the ISP scores in each B cell population. Although some differences were seen between the flaring and quiescent patient groups in the distribution of ethnicities, multivariable analysis including disease activity and ethnicity as covariates demonstrated that both the proportion of DN2 B cells (p=0.004) and the ISP score within this population (p=0.007) remained significantly associated with disease activity after adjusting for ethnicity.

Type I IFNs have been shown to induce expression of CD86, toll-like receptor (TLR)7 and TLR9 in B cells in vitro, enhancing their ability to activate T cells or respond to nucleic acids.3135 We were therefore interested in the relationship between IFN levels and expression of these molecules in vivo. Overall, there was a trend to increased levels of these molecules in patients with SLE as compared with healthy controls that was variably significant depending on the B cell subset examined (figure 1E). Despite this, comparison of the levels of these markers between patients demonstrated a strong positive correlation with the ISP score for most of the cell subsets (figure 1F). Since flaring patients had higher ISP expression than quiescent patients, we questioned whether the association between the ISP score and these molecules was due to their association with disease activity rather than a direct effect of IFN. To investigate this possibility, patients were stratified by the presence or absence of disease activity, or by a high or low global ISG score based on being in the top or bottom 15% of ISG expression among all patients. As shown in figure 1G, no association was seen between marker expression and disease status in most populations, whereas associations between marker expression and IFN status were seen for many populations and were similar to those seen with the ISP score for all patients. To further explore this association, a within-patient comparison between the top and bottom 10% of ISP-expressing cells for each cell population was performed (figure 1H). Consistent with the comparisons between patients, all three markers were significantly upregulated in ISP high relative to ISP low cells.

Type I IFNs enhance differentiation of B cells to DN2 and antibody-secreting cells

Transitional and resting naïve B cells from patients with SLE have elevated levels of ISGs and transcriptional changes suggesting that they are poised for DN2 development.20 23 36 Previous studies have shown an association between high levels of ISG expression in the peripheral blood and expansion of the DN2 cell subset,20 23 suggesting that IFNs facilitate the differentiation of B cells down this pathway. Consistent with this possibility, we found a correlation between the proportion of these cells and their ISP expression, as well as three different ISG scores representative of three independent clusters of ISGs reported in patients with SLE26 (see Methods, figure 2A). Although previous studies have largely focused on the ability of IFNγ and TLR7 activation to promote the differentiation of naïve B cells to DN2 cells,203739 findings in mice suggest that type I IFNs may play a similar role, and that this occurs not only in tandem with TLR7 but also TLR9 activation.16 To address this question, naïve B cells were isolated from healthy controls and cultured under conditions previously shown to induce ABC-like CD11chiT-bet+ B cell development (anti-IgM, anti-CD40 and CpG),21 with and without the addition of 1000 units/mL IFNα, IFNβ or IFNγ. We also evaluated the ability of IFNs to drive the differentiation of these cells in the absence of anti-CD40 or CpG to assess their ability to promote differentiation under suboptimal conditions, as well as in the presence of interleukin (IL)-21 or IL-4, which promote or inhibit DN2 differentiation20 21 37 39 40 respectively. Since DN2 cells have been shown to be the immediate precursors of plasmablasts and plasma cells,6 20 21 the impact of IFNs on differentiation to these cells was also examined.

As shown in figure 2B–E, the addition of IFNα, IFNβ or IFNγ consistently increased the proportions of DN2 B cells, plasma cells and plasmablasts (raw data shown in online supplemental figure 2). In conditions without exogenous ILs, IFNs significantly induced DN2 differentiation, especially in the presence of both anti-CD40 and CpG. There was also a trend for the DN2-promoting effect of IL-21 to be amplified by IFNs. These increases occurred regardless of the presence of T cell signals (anti-CD40) or the TLR9 agonist, CpG, suggesting potential compensatory mechanisms allowing IFNs to overcome the absence of other DN2-promoting molecules.

Notably, although IL-4 typically suppresses DN2 differentiation,37 40 addition of IFNs rescued DN2, plasma cell and plasmablast development in both the IL-4-only and IL-4+IL-21 conditions, and this effect persisted without added anti-CD40 or CpG (figure 2B–E, online supplemental figure 2). Interestingly, in many conditions, IFNα and IFNβ were able to induce these populations equivalently, or in the case of IFNβ, better than the previously established IFNγ. This suggests that as observed in mice,16 type I IFNs may also be key drivers of DN2 and plasmablast/plasma cell differentiation in the human disease, particularly in IL-4-rich environments.

These findings prompted us to further explore how IFN overcomes the detrimental effects of IL-4 on DN2 and antibody-secreting cell development. We hypothesised that IFNs might alter naïve B cell responsiveness to IL-4 by modulating IL-4 receptor (IL-4Rα) expression. To investigate this, naïve B cells were cultured under the same conditions as above and analysed on days 1, 2 and 3 to detect early changes that might favour DN2 differentiation. Assessment of IL-4Rα on non-DN2 B cells (figure 3A) with and without IFNs revealed that all 3 IFNs reduced IL-4Rα expression on day 1, regardless of whether anti-CD40 and CpG were present (figure 3B), with IFNα and IFNγ having the most pronounced effect. This downregulation persisted across all time points under both baseline and IL-21 conditions (data not shown).

While the importance of TLR7 in the differentiation of DN2 cells is well established,17 18 20 37 it remains unclear how IFN signalling impacts TLR7 expression during this process. TLR7 expression was modestly upregulated in the CD11c+CD21− B cell population after 1 day of incubation with type I, but not type II, IFN (figure 3C). By day 3, although the proportion of TLR7+ cells was similar in the T-bethiCD11c+CD21− B cell population in the presence or absence of IFN exposure, IFN-experienced cells clearly expressed higher levels of TLR7 expression. Importantly, the cells with the highest TLR7 levels were also the largest based on forward scatter, potentially reflecting increased activation in response to IFN. In contrast, IFNs did not appreciably enhance TLR9 expression (online supplemental figure 3), likely due to the known ability of B cell receptor stimulation alone to rapidly upregulate TLR9 in naïve B cells.4143

IFN blockade with anifrolumab normalises DN2 cell levels in SLE

Having established that IFN promotes B cell differentiation down the DN2 cell pathway, we investigated whether blockade of type I IFN signalling via the IFNAR-blocking monoclonal antibody, anifrolumab, could reverse this phenotype in vivo. PBMCs from 13 patients treated with anifrolumab were profiled 6 months to 1 year after initial treatment, and compared with 10 clinically matched patients (rash, alopecia, arthritis) receiving standard of care (figure 4A). Although several of the seven major B cell subsets identified appeared to be reduced in anifrolumab-treated patients, including the proportion of activated naïve B cells, DN2 cells and switched memory B cells, only the proportion of DN2 cells achieved statistical significance (figure 4B). IFN activity was broadly diminished across all B cell subsets, as seen by the lower ISP scores and reduced expression of individual ISPs in the anifrolumab group compared with standard of care (figure 4C–D), with the residual proportion of DN2 cells remaining positively associated with ISP score in anifrolumab-treated patients (figure 4E). Thus, anifrolumab-mediated inhibition of IFN signalling attenuates the DN2 programme in vivo, mirroring the IFN-driven differentiation observed in vitro.

Discussion

The importance of type I IFNs in promoting immune dysregulation in SLE is well established,9 10 however the mechanisms through which they promote disease activity remain to be fully characterised. Over the past few years, it has become increasingly apparent through the dramatic efficacy of CD19-targeted chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-T cells44 45 that SLE is fundamentally an autoantibody-mediated disease, and that the DN2 cell pathway plays an important role in the generation of autoantibody-producing cells.6 20 21 Previous work has focused on the importance of BCR and TLR signalling, together with signals provided by T cells including CD40L, IL-21 and IFNγ in driving B cells down the DN2 cell pathway,2021 3739 but the role of type I IFNs has not been resolved. Here, we provide several lines of evidence suggesting that type I IFNs promote the differentiation of DN2 cells both in vitro and in vivo. We show that ISPs are markedly elevated in all B cell subsets in patients with SLE relative to healthy controls, and that the levels of ISPs correlate with the size of the DN2 and plasmablast populations. In vitro, type I IFNs facilitated the differentiation of naïve B cells into DN2 cells roughly equivalently to IFNγ, both with the full complement of signals used for DN2 differentiation and in the absence of anti-CD40 or CpG, indicating that type I IFNs can synergistically cooperate with canonical DN2-promoting signals to achieve a DN2 cell fate. Consistent with a role for type I IFNs in promoting expansion of the DN2 cells in vivo, treatment of patients with SLE with anifrolumab significantly reduced the proportion of DN2 cells as compared with similar patients treated with standard of care.

Expression of B cell activation markers was also found to be associated with ISP expression at a single cell level. These findings corroborate our earlier work in mice showing that IFNα induces CD86 expression and bypasses the impaired ability of anergic self-reactive B cells to upregulate CD86, allowing them to enter the mature B cell repertoire and differentiate to antibody-producing cells in a T cell-dependent fashion.16 Our findings suggest that similar mechanisms may operate in human SLE. The speed at which these molecules were upregulated suggests that IFN signalling directly activates B cells into a ‘primed’ state, which may lower the threshold for subsequent activation by TLRs and enhance their ability to solicit T cell help. This rapid priming is relevant in SLE, where elevated levels of endogenous nuclear antigens are found in the circulation and tissues46 47 and the impaired ability of anergic self-reactive B cells to upregulate CD86 constitutes an important tolerance checkpoint.48

A particularly notable finding was that type I IFNs were able to overcome the inhibitory effects of IL-4 on DN2 cell differentiation, an effect that has only been established with IFNγ in previous studies.37 A potential interplay between IL-4 and type I IFN signalling in determining B cell fate was previously suggested by comparison of the transcriptional profiles of transitional and resting naïve B cells in healthy controls and patients with SLE.36 In healthy controls, these B cell populations had increased expression of genes predicted to enhance IL-4 signalling (including IL4R), whereas in patients with SLE these populations had increased levels of ISGs with many of these cells lacking the IL-4R. Subsequently, IL-4 exposure was shown to suppress T-bet+ ABC differentiation, facilitating a follicular B cell fate and entry into germinal centres in lupus prone mice.40 It also inhibited DN2 cell differentiation in SLE B cells in vitro.40 Here we show the converse, with addition of type I IFNs to IL-4-containing DN2 cell differentiation conditions significantly enhancing differentiation of DN2 cells, restoring them to levels comparable to those seen in the absence of IL-4. This appears to result, at least in part, from the ability of IFNs to reduce IL-4Rα expression on B cells that have not yet committed to the DN2 lineage. Type I IFNs thus reduce the ability of B cells to respond to IL-4, thereby allowing them to enter the DN2 trajectory when they would normally be funnelled into a canonical B cell pathway. This mechanism provides an explanation for how IFN-driven inflammation could overcome the DN2-attenuating effects of the IL-4 secreted from T peripheral helper 2 cells, a population that we previously found to be increased in SLE and to cosegregate with the expanded DN2 population.23 49

In addition to altering IL-4 signalling, type I IFNs have multiple other effects that could enhance the differentiation of B cells down the DN2 pathway. We previously showed that the B cells of patients with SLE are hyper-responsive to BCR signalling, and that this is due in part to their elevated levels of serum IFNα.50 Here, we show that IFNs increase expression of TLR7, a key determinant of DN2 fate and expansion, particularly in T-bethiCD11c+CD21− B cells, and that this increase was enhanced in proportion to the length of IFN exposure. We also show that type I IFNs can bypass the need for effective BCR signalling to induce CD86, enhancing their ability to costimulate T cells under limiting conditions. Type I IFNs have also been shown to enhance differentiation of the IL-21-producing T helper cells that support differentiation of DN2 cells.51 52

Multiple lines of evidence indicate that DN2 cells are the precursors to pathogenic autoantibody-producing cells.6 20 21 Accordingly, we found that IFNs also enhanced the differentiation of plasmablasts and plasma cells. As observed for DN2 cells, this effect was most pronounced in the presence of IL-4, where IFNs restored the numbers of these cells to those seen with equivalent signals in the absence of IL-4. While IFNα has been shown to promote plasma cell expansion,53 the similar effects seen with IFNβ and IFNγ indicate that there is notable redundancy of the downstream effects with the different IFN subtypes.

Some limitations of this study include that the in vitro assays were conducted on the PBMCs of healthy controls, which allowed us to gain insight into the direct effects of IFN on the activation and differentiation of B cells in a relatively homogeneous population, but may not recapitulate the exact process by which IFN interacts with the B cells of patients with SLE due to significant heterogeneity in the lupus environment. Future studies using the PBMCs of SLE patients may be beneficial in validating the effects of IFN in promoting differentiation of preactivated or autoreactive B cells into DN2 cells. Moreover, the size of our anifrolumab cohort was modest and only included one time-point following treatment. Acquiring longitudinal samples over an extended time period may be beneficial in assessing whether DN2 cells remain meaningfully depleted long term, as well as determining any changes in the activation state of B cells over time. Since DN2 cells persist despite treatment, although at lower numbers, it would be interesting to see whether alternative factors such as IFNγ could sustain or restore DN2 differentiation independently of type I IFN.

Our findings provide new insight into the clinical association of the IFN signature with disease flares in SLE by demonstrating that type I IFNs promote a DN2 fate. Since DN2 cells are the precursors of autoantibody-secreting cells,6 20 21 and disease flares of SLE occur concurrently with increased secreted autoantibodies,7 8 our data support a mechanism of disease flare that is driven, at least in part, as a direct result of IFN activation. More broadly, our data point to the importance of clinical interventions that disrupt IFN-driven DN2 differentiation and activation, such as anifrolumab and CD19-CAR T cell therapies,54 55 in promoting more sustainable disease remissions and better patient outcomes.
