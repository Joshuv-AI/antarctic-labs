Qualitative attributes, nutritional traits and yield components of Calendula officinalis L.: response to liquid NPK fertilizer, humic acid, and moringa leaf extract
Authors: Fanar Hashum Al-Hashemi, Zohoor Fuaad Abdalijabar, Ahmed Ebtisam Esmaael, Sheren Ghazi Mahmood, Fatimah Ali Hasan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCalendula officinalis L. is an economic annual or biennial herbaceous medicinal plant which is widely cultivated in the world due to its medicinal and ornamental assets of flowers. In order to improve flower yield and quality, a two-sectional factorial experiment (CRD, r = 3) was conducted in Mosul, Iraq, in 2023. The plants were exposed to different fertilization: Factor A; NPK Liquid Fertilizer (0:33:42) (1.5- and 3-mL L−1) and Factor B; Moringa leaf extract at 0-, 2-, and 4-mL L−1 (section a) and in section b were exposed to 0, 1.5, and 3 g L−1 Humic acid and Moringa leaf extract at 0-, 2-, and 4-mL L−1. The liquied NPK fertilizer (CF), Moringa extract (ME) and Humic acid (HA), as well as their interactions, significantly improved vegetative growth, flowering and phytochemical properties of flowers. In section a, the highest plant height (16.66 cm) was obtained with application of 3 mL L−1 liquied NPK fertilizer without extract, branch number (13.33) with 3 mL L−1 CF + 4 mL L−1 ME, SPAD (43.54 value) 3 mL L−1 CF + 2 ml L−1 ME, flower diameter (73.75 mm) 3 mL L−1 CF + 0 mL L−1 ME, fresh flower weight (270 g) 3 mL L−1 CF + 2 mL L−1 ME, flower dry weight (100 g) 1.5 mL L−1 CF + 4 mL L−1 ME, carotene content (0.86%) 1.5 mL L−1 CF + 4 mL L−1 ME and 3 mL L−1 CF + 2 mL L−1 ME, phosphorus (0.359%) and potassium (1.39%), and nitrogen content (2.47%) in 3 mL L−1 CF + 4 mL L−1 ME. In second experiment (b), the responses of different traits to treatments were less diverse, so that the combined application of 3 g L−1 humic acid and 2- or 4-mL L−1 moringa extract consistently produced the greatest improvements across vegetative, reproductive, and biochemical traits, demonstrating a strong synergistic effect on the growth and quality of C. officinalis. Furthermore, the PCA results demonstrated the superior performance of Humic acid 3 g L−1 × Moringa extract 2 mL L−1, highlighting the importance of combining higher humic acid levels with moderate Moringa extract concentrations to maximize nutrient assimilation, vegetative growth, and reproductive output. Furthermore, the HPLC analysis indicated that the synergistic application of liquied NPK fertilizer or humic acid with 4 mL L−1 of Moringa extract promotes the biosynthesis of key phenolic metabolites in C. officinalis.Keywords: Biostimulants, Medicinal plants, Pot marigold, Phenolic compounds, Sustainable agricultureSubject terms: Biochemistry, Plant sciences

Introduction

Marigold (Calendula officinalis L.), a medicinal plant belonging to the Asteraceae family, is an annual or biennial herbaceous plant native to Mediterranean areas, Europe and China1,2. This plant is extensively cultivated in the world, including Middle East, Africa and United States3. Pot marigold is an economic plant due to ornamental and medicinal properties of flowers, which is rich in bioactive compounds and essential oils4–6. It is a main source of various bioactive constituents including coumarins, quinones, carotenoids, terpenoids, and flavonoids6. Calendula officinalis is extensively used as analgesic, antiseptic, and anti-inflammatory agents, especially to treat inflammations of oral and pharyngeal mucosa, skin injuries, and burns6.

Although chemical fertilization significantly improves plant performance, these fertilizers pollute the environment and endanger human health. On the other hand, extreme usage of chemical fertilizers causes detrimental effects on the soil structure, microorganisms, water holding capacity, imbalances in soil nutrients, and soil fertility7–9. Therefore, it is necessary to take help from sustainable and environmentally friendly agricultural systems to provide optimal nutrition for plant production without imperiling the environment10–12. We hypothesized that supplementing macronutrients through the use of chemical fertilizers along with biofertilizers such as humic acid and moringa extract as sources of micronutrients, organic matter and natural PGRs would help increase the yield and quality of pot marigolds and lead to sustainable and environmentally friendly agriculture. Thus, in two different experiments, we exposed the pot marigold plants to the different levels of these fertilizers and Moringa extract.

Humic acid (HA) is widely used in sustainable agriculture as an organic product derived from the decomposition of organic matter. Humic acid provides abundant organic carbon and helps to maintain a hydrated, pH and redox buffered environment hosting the soil microbiome13. It effects plant growth through the processes of respiration and carbon construction and by increasing nutrient availability, enhancing cation exchange capacity, and improving soil chemical and physical properties14–16. It’s environmentally friendly, low-cost, and contributes to high-quality flower yields. HA activates the enzymes Cytochromes, Phosphates, Oxidase, Phosphorylase and also helps to inhibit other enzymes such as peroxidase, Fitase and IAA oxidase17. HA supports plant growth by chelating unavailable nutrients and adjusting the pH of the growing medium18–20. Humic substances consist of complex hydrophilic organic chemicals, appearing as black liquids or powders. They’re relatively stable products of organic matter decomposition, accumulating in ecosystems and promoting growth by increasing the absorption of macro- and micronutrients, especially during stress conditions16,18,21–24.

The Moringa tree (Moringa oleifera L.), belonging to the Moringaceae family, is highly valued for its nutritional and medicinal properties25,26. Moringa oleifera is classified as an important medicine plant and traditionally is used to cure wounds, pain, ulcers, liver disease, heart disease, cancer, and inflammation27,28. Moringa leaves, pods, and seeds are rich in nutritional compounds, protein containing all amino acids, minerals, nutrients, vitamins, and antioxidants, making it a multipurpose plant used for food, medicine, and oil production in tropical regions26,29–32. Moringa extract contains growth factors, proteins, fats, carbohydrates, and dietary fiber, vitamin C and A, iron and calcium33–36. Recent studies indicate that Moringa leaves are considered a natural source of PGRs, amino acids, zeatin, and antioxidants such as ascorbic acid, flavonoids, phenols, carotenoids, and macro- and micronutrients37–39. Moringa leaf extract is an inexpensive, sustainable, eco-friendly and natural biostimulant that can be used to improve the plant growth and productivity of various crops under different conditions39,40. This study aims to evaluate the effects of biostimulants humic acid and Moringa leaf extract as natural alternatives to chemical fertilizer in Calendula production and reducing uses of chemical fertilizers and environmental pollution.

Materials and methods

Experimental site

A factorial experiment based on Completely Randomized Design (CRD, r = 3) was conducted in an open plastic greenhouse during the autumn season of 2023, College of Agriculture and Forestry, Department of Horticulture and Landscape Engineering, (43° 7′ 52″ E, 36° 20′ 43″ N). The seeds were prepared from Department of Horticulture, University of Mosul and authenticated by Dr. Fanar Hashum Al-Hashemi and sown on 17 September, 2023. When the plants reached a height of 10 cm at the 4–5 true leaf stage, they were transplanted on 27 December into ridges that measured 1.60 m in length and each ridge contained five seedlings, with 30 cm spacing between plants and 25 cm between ridges. Experiential design and treatments

The experiment was conducted in two separate sections. Section a) included two factors: Factor A; NPK Liquid Fertilizer (chemical fertilizer, CF) (0:33:42) manufactured Al-Jawhra company, Jordan, contains 33% phosphorus (P₂O₅) and 42% potassium (K₂O), and was applied as a foliar spray at concentrations of 1.5- and 3-mL L−1, repeated every 15 days (four times). Factor B; Humic acid (HA) was applied in aqueous form at concentrations of (0, 1.5, 3) g L−1 using fertigation (Fertilizer application through irrigation), referring the manufacturer’s recommendations. Approximately 20–30 days after transplanting, following the irrigation at saturated point, each plant received 200 mL of humic acid solution, repeated every 15 days for a total of four times. Measurements of characteristics

Vegetative growth attributes

The traits of plant height (cm), fresh weight of plant (g), dry weight of plant (g), flower diameter (mm), flower stalk diameter (mm), and flower stalk length (cm) were measured. Furthermore, number of branches (branch/plant), date of flower bud appearance, and number of flowers were counted. Total chlorophyll (SPAD value) was estimated using the SPAD 502 chlorophyll measuring device.

Flower petals carotene content (mg 100 mL−1)

The carotene extracted by acetone (80%) from the flower petals by taking 2 g of flower petals, mixed by an electrical blender (Heru, M22619), and taking 20 ml of 80% acetone. Furthermore, 0.1 g of sodium bicarbonate was added to prevent the oxidation of the carotene stain. They mixed in the blender. The solution after filtration was transferred to a 100 ml volumetric flask, and the volume was completed by 80% acetone and well mixed. Finally, the photon density of the sample was measured by a spectrophotometer at a wavelength of 480 nm41.

Measurements of nutrient content

Approximately 0.5 g of fresh leaf tissue were analyzed to estimate the concentration of nutrients by conducting a digestion process, as part of the assessment of the plant’s nutritional status. Nitrogen content (N%) was determined using the Kjeldahl method. Phosphorus concentration (P%) was measured using a colorimeter through a colorimetric method. Potassium concentration (K%) was estimated using a flame photometer.

All vegetative growth measurements and chemical traits were taken after the flowering period had ended, whereas the floral traits, including the number of flowers, flower diameter, flower stalk length, and stalk diameter, were recorded on 20 April, 2024.

Chromatography assays

The flowers extracts were extracted using a continuous Soxhlet extractor, where the samples were soaked in absolute ethanol (99.8%) for 48 h after oil extraction using petroleum ether (60–80 °C) to remove fats (defatting). The obtained solution was then concentrated using a rotary evaporator (RVE) to approximately 20 mL for all samples. An acid hydrolysis process was then carried out by taking 5 mL of the crude ethanolic extract and adding 20 mL of HCl (1N), followed by heating in a water bath for 30 min. After cooling, 30 mL of ethyl acetate was added in two stages and separated using a separating funnel. The upper ethyl acetate layer was collected twice and stored in a refrigerator until chromatographic analyses were performed42.

The acid-hydrolyzed samples were analyzed using an HPLC device of the type Shimadzu LC-2010HT, after purification through membrane filters with a pore size of 0.1 µm. The analysis was performed at a wavelength of 320 nm, with a flow rate of 1 mL/min, using a mobile phase consisting of 85% acetonitrile and 15% water, and a C18 column with dimensions of 240 × 4.60 mm. The retention time (Rt) for each standard compound is shown in Table 5. The standards were supplied by Fluka (Switzerland) and BDH companies. The measurements were carried out at the University of Mosul, College of Agriculture and Forestry.

Statistical analysis

Both sections of the study were conducted as factorial experiments arranged in a Completely Randomized Design (CRD) with three replications.Statistical analysis, including Two-way ANOVA, Tukey’s HSD test (α = 0.05), PCA, Variables Cluster Analysis, and Pearson’s Correlation Estimation, was performed using the R software Ver. 4.5.

Results

Results of section a

In general, increasing the concentration of liquid NPK fertilizer improved vegetative growth and flowering performance of Calendula officinalis. The addition of Moringa extract further enhanced several morphological and nutritional traits, particularly branch formation, flower quality, and nutrient accumulation. The combined application of high NPK fertilizer and Moringa extract generally produced the best plant performance.The two-way ANOVA revealed that for plant height, both the liquied NPK fertilizer (CF) (p < 0.01) and Moringa extract (ME) (p < 0.05) exerted significant main effects, while their interaction was extremely significant (p < 0.001), indicating that the response to Moringa extract was strongly dependent on fertilizer level. A similar pattern emerged for branch number, where liquid NPK fertilizer (p < 0.001) and Moringa (p < 0.001) had highly significant independent effects; however, the interaction was not significant, showing that each factor improved branching additively rather than synergistically. In contrast, chlorophyll content showed no significant effect of liquied NPK fertilizer, Moringa extract, or their interaction. For appearance of flower buds, the main effect of Moringa extract was highly significant (p < 0.001), while the main effect of liquied NPK fertilizer and the interaction of liquied NPK fertilizer and Moringa extract were not. Application of liquied NPK fertilizer and Moringa extract consistently outperformed the control (1.5 mL L−1 CF without ME), although the magnitude and direction of response differed among traits. The highest values for plant height, flower number, flower stalk height, and fresh and dry flower weight were obtained under the 3 mL L−1 CF without ME, indicating that vegetative and floral structural growth is primarily driven by fertilizer intensity rather than biostimulant addition. In contrast, branch number, flower diameter, flower stalk diameter, and nutrient concentrations (P, K, N) increased most markedly under 3 mL L−1 CF combined with 4 mL L−1 ME, demonstrating a strong synergistic effect of Moringa-derived phytohormones on canopy development and floral robustness. SPAD values and carotene content responded optimally to moderate ME supplementation (3 mL L−1 CF + 2 mL L−1 ME), suggesting a threshold-dependent enhancement of chlorophyll stability and pigment biosynthesis. Across traits, the Tukey’s groupings clearly segregated the high-liquied NPK fertilizer treatments (3 mL L−1) into superior statistical clusters relative to the low- CF groups, while Moringa extract exerted trait-specific improvements most evident in parameters associated with branching, floral size, and nutrient accumulation. Collectively, the results demonstrate that increasing fertilizer concentration is the primary driver of structural growth, whereas the addition of Moringa extract selectively enhances physiological and biochemical attributes, with the most effective treatment varying according to the targeted trait.

Vegetative attributes, chlorophyll content and flower appearance

Application of Moringa extract meaningfully improved growth, flowering and chlorophyl content of Calendula officinalis compared to liquied NPK fertilizer. While the lowest plant height (8.77 cm), branch number (5.44), SPAD value (42.22), and flower number (14.11) were observed in the control group (treated with 1.5 mL L−1 liquied NPK fertilizer without extract (Fig. 2a–d). However, the maximum plant height occurred under application of 3 mL L−1 liquied NPK fertilizer without extract (16.66 cm), representing a 90.0% increase over the control (Fig. 2a), whereas the highest branch number (13.33) was recorded in plants which were treated with 3 mL L−1 liquied NPK fertilizer + 4 mL L−1 Moringa extract, corresponding to a 144.9% increase compared to control (Fig. 2b). The greatest SPAD value (43.54) was observed under effect of 3 mL L−1 liquied NPK fertilizer + 2 mL L−1 Moringa extract, which was 3.1% higher than the control (Fig. 2c). Flower bud appearance peaked (201.33) under 1.5 mL L−1 liquied NPK fertilizer + 2 mL L−1 Moringa extract, showing a marginal 1.7% increase (Fig. 2d).

Flower characteristics

Application of Moringa extract significantly enhanced flower production of Calendula officinalis. Flower number, flower diameter, and flower stalk height showed highly significant responses to liquied NPK fertilizer (p ≤ 0.001), Moringa extract (p ≤ 0.001), and their interaction (p ≤ 0.001), indicating a clear synergistic enhancement of reproductive potential. Flower stalk diameter was significantly affected by liquied NPK fertilizer (p ≤ 0.01), Moringa extract (p ≤ 0.001), and their interaction (p ≤ 0.05). For flower fresh weight, liquied NPK fertilizer (p ≤ 0.001) and Moringa extract (p ≤ 0.001) showed very strong effects, while their interaction was not significant, suggesting Moringa extract was the primary driver of floral biomass accumulation. However, flower dry weight displayed strong significance responses against liquied NPK fertilizer (p ≤ 0.001), Moringa extract (p ≤ 0.001), and their interaction (p ≤ 0.001), revealing a powerful synergistic effect on dry matter deposition.

The highest flower numbers per plant (13.66) was observed in response to 3 mL L−1 CF + 4 mL L−1 ME, which showed an improvement of 55.8% comparable to its value in the control plants (8.77 numbers, lowest) (Fig. 3a) Flower diameter varied between 57.75 mm (lowest) in plants treated with 1.5 mL L−1 CF + 0 mL L−1 and 73.75 mm (maximum) in response to 3 mL L−1 CF + 0 mL L−1 ME, yielding a 27.7% improvement (Fig. 3b). Flower stalk diameter ranged from 2.33 cm (in control plants) to 7.0 cm in plants treated with 3 mL L−1 CF + 4 mL L−1 ME, representing a 200.4% increase (Fig. 3c). Flower stalk height showed a minimum of 13.5 cm in the control plants and a maximum of 24.5 cm in response to 1.5 mL L−1 CF + 4 mL L−1 ME, resulting 81.5% improvement (Fig. 3d). Fresh flower weight exhibited a strong fertilizer-dependent response, rising from 190 g in the control to 270 g under 3 mL L−1 CF + 2 mL L−1 ME, representing + 42.1% enhancement (Fig. 3e). Dry weight ranged from 56.66 g in plants treated with 1.5 mL L−1 CF + 2 mL L−1 ME to 100 g in 1.5 mL L−1 CF + 4 mL L−1 ME, corresponding to a 59.5% increase over the control average (62.44 g) (Fig. 3f).

Carotene and nutrients content of flowers

Liquied NPK fertilizer and its interaction with Moringa extracts (p ≤ 0.001) had significant effects on carotene content, while Moringa alone did not significantly alter carotene levels. Liquied NPK fertilizer and Moringa extract extremely affected the leaf phosphorus (p ≤ 0.001), while their interaction exhibited lower significant effect on leaf P content (p ≤ 0.01). The potassium (K) content of leaves was significantly affected only by liquied NPK fertilizer (p ≤ 0.05), with no significant effects of Moringa extract or its interaction with liquied NPK fertilizer. Finally, nitrogen (N) content showed strong significance responses to liquied NPK fertilizer and Moringa extract at the level of 0.001, and to their interaction at the level of 0.01. Carotene content was lowest (0.76%) in the control and peaked at 0.86% in plants treated with 1.5 mL L−1 CF + 4 mL L−1 ME and 3 mL L−1 CF + 2 mL L−1 ME, reflecting a 13.1% increase (Fig. 4a). Phosphorus ranged from 0.15% under effect of 1.5 mL L−1 CF (control) to 0.359% in plants treated with 3 mL L−1 CF + 4 mL L−1 ME, a 139% increase compared to control (Fig. 4b). Potassium increased from 0.92% in the control to a maximum of 1.39% under application of 3 mL L−1 CF + 4 mL L−1 ME (51.1% improvement) (Fig. 4c). Nitrogen content reached its minimum in the control plants (1.24%) and maximum (2.47%) under effect of 3 mL L−1 CF + 4 mL L−1 ME, an increase of 99.1% (Fig. 4d).

PCA

Principal component analysis (PCA) (Table 6) revealed that PC1 contributed for 39.08% of the variance and was primarily associated with morphological and nutritional traits. High positive loadings were observed for branch number, N content, flower stalk height, P content, flower stalk diameter, flower diameter, plant height, and K content, indicating that these variables contributed strongly to variation along this axis. PC2 explained an additional 18.89% of the variance and was mainly influenced by floral quality–related traits. Flower fresh weight, flower number, appearance of flower buds, SPAD value, and flower dry weight showed the highest positive loadings on this component, suggesting that PC2 represents variation in flower biomass accumulation, developmental stage, and chlorophyll status.

The PCA biplot (Fig. 5), explained 39.1% of the variance on PC1 and 18.9% on PC2, clearly differentiating the treatment interactions based on their multivariate response patterns. Treatment of 3 mL L−1 CF + 2 mL L−1 ME (2.2 group) closely associated with traits such as flower number, flower diameter, content of N, P, and carotene; this suggests that 3 mL L−1 CF + 2 mL L−1 ME consistently enhances major reproductive and biochemical traits. Similarly, treatment of 3 mL L−1 CF + 0 mL L−1 ME (2.1 group), aligning with carotene and macronutrient accumulation, reflects their contribution to biochemical improvement. In contrast, 1.5 mL L−1 CF + 4 mL L−1 ME (1.3 group) show distinct loading along the negative PC2 axis and moderate positive PC1 values, indicating that these treatments are primarily associated with flower stalk height and flower stalk diameter, rather than biochemical or floral traits. The control treatment (1.5 mL L−1 CF; 1.1 group) clusters strongly in the negative region of PC1, aligning with no major trait vectors, suggesting generally weak performance across morphological and biochemical indices. Treatment of 1.5 mL L−1 CF + 2 mL L−1 ME (1.2) is closely associated with flower fresh weight, SPAD, and bud appearance, indicating a selective but narrower influence focused on vegetative or early floral development. Finally, the 3 mL L−1 CF + 4 mL L−1 ME (2.3) showed exclusive association with flower dry weight, reflecting a singular but strong effect on flower biomass accumulation. Collectively, the PCA indicates that the combined treatment of 3 mL L−1 CF + mL L−1 ME (2.2) is the most comprehensive and high-performing treatment, improving both floral and biochemical traits.

Cluster analysis of variables and treatments

The hierarchical cluster analysis further supported the multivariate patterns observed in the PCA by grouping both traits and treatments according to their similarity profiles, reflecting coherent physiological and morphological responses to the liquied NPK fertilizer × Moringa extract combinations (Fig. 6). Traits associated with vegetative vigor and floral production including flower fresh weight, flower dry weight, flower diameter, flower stalk diameter, flower stalk height, plant height, branch number, and nutrient contents (N, P, K) clustered closely together, indicating strong positive associations and confirms that enhancements in nutrient uptake were directly linked to improvements in vegetative growth and reproductive performance. In contrast, SPAD value and flower number formed a related but slightly distinct subcluster, suggesting that chlorophyll concentration and total flower production were responsive to nutrient-driven treatments but varied independently from some morphological traits. Appearance of flower bud and carotene content grouped into a separate cluster. Treatment clustering revealed distinct groupings primarily driven by liquied NPK fertilizer concentration and, secondarily, by Moringa extract levels. Treatments containing high liquied NPK fertilizer 3 mL L−1 (H2), particularly 3 mL L−1 CF and 2 mL L−1 ME (H2_M2), 3 mL L−1 CF and 4 mL L−1 ME (H2_M3), formed a tight cluster associated with strong performance across nutrient content (N, P, K), high SPAD values, greater floral biomass, and enhanced plant structural attributes. This grouping mirrors their strong alignment with the high-performing trait cluster in the PCA. Treatments with low liquied NPK fertilizer (1.5 mL L−1 CF) and low Moringa levels (e.g., 1.5 mL L−1 CF and 0- or 2-mL L−1 ME) clustered together and were characterized by weaker performance across most traits, which indicates reduced nutrient assimilation and lower vegetative and floral growth. The heatmap of cluster analysis strongly corroborated the PCA finding that 3 mL L−1 CF + 2 mL L−1 ME (H2_M2) is the superior treatment. This combination showed consistently high values across nutrient contents (N, P, K), SPAD, flower number, flower fresh and dry weight, flower diameter, stalk diameter, and plant height. Taken together, the cluster analysis reinforced the PCA conclusions, demonstrating that liquied NPK fertilizer and Moringa extract act synergistically to improve plant nutrient status, vegetative vigor, and reproductive performance.

Pearson’s correlation estimation

The correlation matrix (Fig. 7) showed clear associations among the vegetative, reproductive, and biochemical traits of Calendula officinalis. Plant height exhibited strong positive correlations with flower diameter (r = 0.718**) and flower stalk diameter (r = 0.719**), indicating that taller plants generally produced larger floral structures. Branch number was also strongly associated with nutrient accumulation, showing high correlations with phosphorus (r = 0.824**) and nitrogen (r = 0.885**), as well as with flower stalk height (r = 0.698**). SPAD values showed weak correlations with most traits but were moderately associated with flower fresh weight (r = 0.318) and negatively correlated with flower dry weight (r = –0.450). Reproductive traits were broadly interconnected. Flower number showed strong positive correlations with flower fresh weight (r = 0.740**) and carotene content (r = 0.725**). Flower diameter was significantly correlated with phosphorus and nitrogen (r = 0.649* and r = 0.655*, respectively), while flower stalk diameter also correlated strongly with nitrogen (r = 0.707**). Section b) interaction of humic acid and moringa extract

Vegetative traits, Chlorophyll content and flowers appearance.

The combination of humic acid (HA) and Moringa extract (ME) had significantly meaningful effects on plant height, SPAD value, branch number, and appearance of flower buds (p ≤ 0.01). The untreated control (0 g L−1 humic acid + 0 mL L−1 Moringa extract) produced the lowest overall performance, with minimum plant height (7.33 cm), SPAD value (31.1), and branch number, while control plants exhibited the longest time to appearance of flowers (Fig. 8a–d). Plant height increased markedly in response to HA and ME, with the highest values observed under 0 and 3 g L−1 HA combined with 4 mL L−1 ME (14.0 cm), significantly exceeding the untreated control (Fig. 8a). Branch number responded strongly to intermediate humic acid levels, reaching a maximum at 1.5 g L−1 HA + 2 mL L−1 ME (13.22 branches) (Fig. 8b). SPAD readings followed a similar positive trend, peaking at 45.18 under 3 g L−1 HA + 4 mL L−1 ME (Fig. 8c). In contrast, the appearance of flower buds tended to decline at higher treatment levels, with the earliest bud appearance (191 days) occurring under the highest combination of humic acid and moringa extract (3 g L−1 HA + 4 mL L−1 ME) (Fig. 8d).

Flower number increased with both factors, reaching its maximum (33.77 flowers plant−1) under effect of 3 g L−1 humic acid plus 4 mL L−1 Moringa extract (Fig. 9a). Flower diameter was similarly enhanced, with the largest flowers (74.61 mm) recorded with application of this same treatment combination (Fig. 9b). Flower stalk diameter and height increased notably with rising humic acid and moringa doses, culminating in the thickest (8.77 cm) and tallest (24.1 cm) stalks in response to 3 g L−1 humic acid × 4 mL L−1 Moringa extract (Fig. 9c,d). Consistent with these improvements, flower fresh weight increased substantially, peaking at 321 g in plants treated with 3 g L−1 humic acid + 2 mL L−1 moringa extract (Fig. 9e), while flower dry weight was highest in response to 1.5 g L−1 HA with 4 mL L−1 ME (107.0 g) (Fig. 9f).

Similarly, phosphorus concentrations of flowers rose sharply with increasing humic acid, with the highest values (0.284%) in plants treated with 3 g L−1 HA and 2/4 mL L−1 ME treatments (Fig. 10b). Identification of functional groups of phenolic compounds using infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy

Some samples of ethanolic extracts, which had previously been obtained using a continuous extraction device (Soxhlet), were taken for the purpose of fat removal (defatting). Selected samples were analyzed before and after undergoing acid hydrolysis, using ethyl acetate as a solvent to separate the non-sugar portion (aglycone). These samples were then passed through an FTIR spectrometer to identify the active groups of phenolic compounds in these treatments. The FTIR results revealed the presence of several functional groups in the ethanolic extract sample of plants treated with 3 ml L−1 CF, combination + 2 ml L−1 ME. The active groups were identified prior to acid hydrolysis. A very broad band was observed in the region 2839–3801 cm−1, corresponding to the hydroxyl (OH) group, which is most likely of a glycosidic nature (Fig. 17a). In addition, a band appeared at 1645 cm−1 corresponding to the carbonyl group, and two bands at 1449 cm−1 and 1518 cm−1 corresponding to aromatic double bonds. Furthermore, two bands were recorded at 1019 cm−1 and 1163 cm−1, indicating the presence of the C–O–C bond. These functional groups most probably belong to glycosidic phenolic compounds (glycosides) (Fig. 17a). After performing acid hydrolysis, a band was observed at 3429 cm−1 corresponding to a non-glycosidic hydroxyl (OH) group, along with a band at 1715 cm−1 indicating the presence of a carbonyl (C=O) group. Another band at 1645 cm−1 was attributed to the aliphatic double bond (C=C), while two bands at 1046 cm−1 and 1274 cm−1 were associated with symmetric and asymmetric C–O–C stretching vibrations (Fig. 17b).

Infrared (FTIR) spectrum of some functional groups in the ethanolic extract of pot marigold plants treated with liquied NPK fertilizer at a concentration of 3 mL L−1 combined with 10% Moringa extract, prior to the acid hydrolysis process (a) and after the acid hydrolysis process (b).

Discussion

The chemical fertilizer (liquied NPK fertilizer) was the dominant driver of vegetative enlargement, while humic acid and especially Moringa extract acted as efficient biostimulants that enhanced nutrient assimilation, branching, pigment content, and floral robustness. Traits associated with structural growth (plant height, flower diameter, stalk height, fresh weight) responded most strongly to liquied NPK fertilizer intensity. In a recent study, flower yield and yield attributes of marigold significantly increased with NPK fertilizer and highest flower yield (22.25, 27.38 and 25.15 ton ha−1) was achieved with application of the nutrients N90P40K90 kg ha−1 along with a blanket dose of 2 kg B and 4 kg Zn ha−144.

In contrast, traits reflecting physiological enhancement, nutrient concentrations (N, P, K), carotene acclimation, branch proliferation, and stalk diameter were most responsive to the phytohormonal and antioxidant components of Moringa extract, with 3 mL L−1 CF + 4 mL L−1 ME consistently dominating the highest statistical grouping. The strong enhancement of nutrient contents under high ME levels highlights Moringa’s role in upregulating root absorption and metabolic activity. Overall, the data demonstrated a clear synergistic interaction wherein fertilizer governed plant size while Moringa extract primarily enhanced physiological efficiency, and the optimal combination depends on the target trait. For integrated production objectives, the treatment 3 mL L−1 CF + 2–4 mL L−1 ME emerged as the most agronomically advantageous, consistently outperforming the control across nearly all traits with increases ranging from 13 to 151%. In second experiment, the factorial application of humic acid and Moringa extract produced highly significant variation across all morphological, floral, and biochemical traits. The control (0 g L−1 HA + 0 mL L−1 ME) exhibited the lowest performance for most traits. However, maximum values for vegetative growth, floral characteristics, and nutrient contents were obtained in response to 1.5 or 3 g L−1 HA in combination with 2- or 4-mL L−1 ME.

The strong response to Moringa extract aligns with the well-documented presence of cytokinins, auxins, ascorbates, and mineral cofactors that enhance photosynthesis, nutrient assimilation, and sink–source activation39,45. Numerous studies confirm that Moringa extracts stimulate leaf expansion, branching, chlorophyll stability, flowering, and nutrient uptake through up-regulation of N-assimilation enzymes and enhanced antioxidant capacity46–51. Simultaneously, the strong fertilizer-driven effect on structural biomass reflects improved nitrogen metabolism, osmotic regulation, and carbon partitioning under higher nutrient availability52–57. The synergistic nature of the combined treatments supports earlier evidence that biostimulants enhance nutrient-use efficiency (NUE) in fertilized systems by increasing root absorptive area and enzymatic activity58–64. Overall, the superior performance of 3 mL L−1 CF × 2–4 mL L−1 ME, in present study, confirms a clear synergistic interaction, yielding the highest morphological, floral, and biochemical quality in agreement with modern biostimulant literature60,65,66. The interactive effects of liquied NPK fertilizer and Moringa extract observed in this study are consistent with the well-documented bioactive properties of Moringa, which include cytokinins, antioxidants, mineral cofactors, and amino acids that enhance cellular metabolism and nutrient acquisition67,68. The substantial increases in plant height, flower diameter, stalk height, and biomass under high fertilizer regimes align with earlier findings showing that nitrogen–phosphorus–potassium availability predominantly drives vegetative expansion and structural development. By contrast, Moringa extract exerted its strongest effects on branching, chlorophyll-linked metabolism, nutrient concentration, and carotenoid synthesis, confirming reports that Moringa-derived zeatin, ascorbate, and phenolics act as potent biostimulants promoting photosynthetic efficiency and nutrient uptake50,51. The elevated concentrations of N, P, and K in treatments receiving the highest extract levels may be attributed to improved root membrane permeability and enhanced enzymatic activity, mechanisms previously described in biostimulant-treated horticultural crops69. The combined results highlight a synergistic interaction: fertilizer establishes the structural capacity for growth, while Moringa extract enhances the physiological efficiency required to maximize that capacity. The consistently superior performance of 3 mL L−1 fertilizer combined with 2–4 mL L−1 Moringa extract across most traits, yielding significant improvements of over the control, supports the use of integrated nutrient–biostimulant management strategies for optimizing floral and biochemical quality. These findings reinforce the growing body of evidence that Moringa leaf extract represents an effective, eco-friendly enhancer of both productivity and nutrient-use efficiency in agricultural systems39,67.

In Jatropha curcas plants, ME treatment improved plant height by 22–28%, branch number by 185–20%, and flower production by 25–30% compared with untreated salt-stressed plants, associated with enhanced antioxidant enzyme activity and reduced membrane damage, ion balance and ROS detoxification48. 10% MLE modulates the antioxidant scavenging activity by enhancing carotenoids (75%), proline (70%), and total phenolic content (24%) of sunflower70. Carthamus tinctorius L exposed to 3 and 20% ME exhibited enhanced shoot and root length, fresh and dry biomass, chlorophyll content, and proline, glycine betaine, and mineral content71. In organic fennel cultivation, the total phenols, plant height, branch number per plant, herb fresh weight, fruit weight, umbel number per plant, and fruit yield were significantly enhanced with the application of ME72. Spraying ME on grape markedly improved shoot length, chlorophyll content, and mineral content of N, P and K73.

Anwar et al.30,31 believed that ME can enhance the photosynthesis efficiency and carbon–nitrogen metabolism30,31. Also, phytohormones that exist in ME has an important role in increasing cell division and elongation, resulting in plant growth and yield45. Furthermore, ME contains mineral elements, proteins, vitamins, amino acids, phenolics, and plant hormones, which can improve the crops’ growth and development73,74. The strong positive effect of Moringa extract aligns with its cytokinin-rich profile and its capacity to improve photosynthetic pigments, flower initiation, and antioxidant balance.

Humic acid promotes plant growth through a combination of physiological and biochemical mechanisms. It alters plasma membrane transporters, enhancing ion exchange and facilitating improved nutrient absorption, assimilation, and overall metabolism75. Humic acids improve root morphology and rhizosphere activity, thereby enhancing uptake of N, P, K and stimulating hormone-like signaling pathways that regulate shoot–root balance76–78. HA improve water and nutrient absorption efficiency, as well as cell division and elongation, while ME induces cell division, shoot growth, chlorophyll synthesis, floral differentiation, and nutrient remobilization via phytohormone activity18,24,79.

Some findings from other experiments are in line with our findings and confirm the results of present study. For example, Growth characteristics of Quinoa plants, such as plant height, grain yield, and plant fresh weight, as well as mineral elements, significantly improved with ME application80. In a research, mixed application of HA and ME enhanced the tolerance index of shoots, roots, and thiol compounds and free and bound phenolic compounds81. In pot marigold (Calendula officinalis L.), 10 kg ha−1 HA, as well as 1 or 1.5 g L−1 HA, positively impacted on leaf and flower pigments, total flavonoid content, and total phenolic contents82,83. In snapdragon cultivars highest number of leaves plant−1 (114), stem diameter (11 mm), plant height (99 cm), number of florets spike−1 (35), flowering duration (44 days), and vase life (8 days) were recorded in plants treated with 30% ME84. In Eruca vesicaria subsp. sativa the application of 10 g L−1 ME resulted in the greatest plant height (57.95 cm), number of leaves (19.09 leaves.plant−1), and percentage of dry matter (6.896 kg) and 4 kg ha−1 humic acid excelled by giving the highest plant height (58.87 cm), dry matter percent (7.083 kg), and the highest yield (2.093 tons)85. The findings of a research showed that the 2.5 ml L−1HA significantly improved number of branches, number of leaves, dry matter, the chlorophyll content, and total phenols of vinca plant86.

The comparing of functional groups identified by FTIR with the phenolic compounds present in the ethanolic extract of pot marigold petals, it was found that HPLC identified the same compounds, those that characterized by infrared spectroscopy. These findings are in line with those resulting from olive oil87. Fakour and Lin88 reported that the FTIR spectra of the analyzed humic compounds showed the presence of carboxyl, ketone, phenol, and hydroxyl functional groups88.

Biofertilizers such as HA and ME contain nutrient minerals, plant hormones, organic matter, PGRs, etc. The Cytokinins and essential minerals exists in these biostimulants promote cell division, as well as nutrient uptake and enzymatic activity. These interactions collectively enhance photosynthesis and biomass accumulation, leading to improved crop performance40.

Conclusion

The Liquied NPK fertilizer (CF), Moringa extract (ME) and Humic acid (HA), as well as their interactions, significantly improved vegetative growth, flowering and phytochemical properties of flowers. Furthermore, the HPLC analysis indicated that the synergistic application of liquied NPK fertilizer or humic acid with 2 mL L−1 of Moringa extract promotes the biosynthesis of key phenolic metabolites in C. officinalis. The application of 1.5 mL L−1 CF + 4 mL L−1 ME or 3 mL L−1 CF + 2 mL L−1 ME, as well as 3 g L−1 humic acid and 2- or 4-mL L−1 moringa extract, consistently produced the greatest improvements across vegetative, reproductive, and biochemical traits with strong synergistic effects on the growth and quality of Calendula officinalis. Overall, Tukey’s test and the PCA results highlighted the superior performance of Humic acid 3 g L−1 × Moringa extract 2 mL L−1, the importance of combining higher humic acid levels with moderate Moringa extract concentrations to maximize nutrient assimilation, vegetative growth, and reproductive output. This treatment represented the optimal combination for achieving the highest overall plant performance across the evaluated traits.
