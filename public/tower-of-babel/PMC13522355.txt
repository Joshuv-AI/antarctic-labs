Regional transcriptomic divergence reveals thermal adaptation mechanisms in the giant kelp Macrocystis pyrifera
Authors: Alexis Bunster, Sylvain Faugeron, Suany Quesada-Calderon, Cristian Molina, Alvaro Figueroa, Maribel Solas, Carolina Camus, Marie-Laure Guillemin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Understanding how gene expression shapes locally adapted phenotypes is pivotal in evolutionary ecology, yet it remains unexplored in keystone marine species like the giant kelp Macrocystis pyrifera. We hypothesized that juvenile sporophytes display transcriptomic signatures of thermal adaptation to their native Chilean habitats (from the regions of Atacama, Southern Chile and Magallanes). Individuals from each region were exposed to 8, 12, and 16 °C and their gene expression profiles were analysed via CLARA clustering and LEfSe discriminant analysis using a statistical framework designed to test for local adaptation, i.e. by comparing “Local versus Foreign” conditions and “Sympatric versus Allopatric” combinations of populations and thermal conditions. The number and biological roles of candidate adaptation genes (i.e. consistently up- or down regulated in the local or sympatric condition) varied across regions, reflecting divergent selective regimes. Individuals from Atacama specifically upregulated genes related to the synthesis of antioxidant compounds and intracellular protein turnover, when exposed to 16 °C. The population of Magallanes coped with low temperatures (i.e. 8 °C) using other mechanisms, such as the activation of the glyoxalase pathway and production of photoprotective pigments. These findings identify transcriptional patterns associated with regional thermal adaptation, providing candidate genes for future functional investigation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-56462-0.

Introduction

Kelp forests, the coastal marine ecosystems dominated by large brown seaweeds, are globally regressing, partly due to global warming1. The limited physiological tolerance of kelps to warming2 particularly affects low-latitude populations3. Kelp species are present in most temperate to cold coastal environments around the world4, covering nearly a quarter of rocky coastlines from the intertidal to around 40 m depth5. Their distribution range most often encompasses large latitudinal sections and is therefore exposed to a variety of environmental conditions that can promote genetic divergence, as a result of directional selection on fitness-related traits in each local environment6,7. This process of local adaptation is expected to promote divergence in thermal tolerance among populations and could be key to sustainable management and restoration strategies for declining kelp populations facing increasing thermal stress8.

So far, limited examination of local adaptation has been addressed in kelps8–10. Several common garden experiments have evidenced population divergence in response to thermal variability across the latitudinal gradient11, in agreement with the local adaptation hypothesis. In particular, a higher heat tolerance has been observed in low-latitude populations12, suggesting selection effectively promotes adaptation even at the species range limits. Moreover, breeding strategies promoting heterosis in heat tolerance provided valuable phenotypes for kelp farming in low-latitude regions of Korea and China13; and heat stress tolerance has been identified as a heritable trait across the complex, haploid-diploid life cycle of the giant kelp Macrocystis pyrifera14. Additionally, the gametophyte and sporophyte stages of kelps display distinct thermal sensitivities that vary among populations across latitudinal gradients9,15,16. These examples support the idea that thermal-tolerance traits have substantial heritability, even if thermal adaptation operates slightly differentially across life cycle stages, and can evolve under natural or artificial selection, and can evolve under natural or artificial selection. Yet the underlying genetic variance has been scarcely explored so far.

Selection can shape heritable traits via divergence in coding sequences or regulatory elements17. Indeed, gene expression patterns shift through mutations in promoters or trans-regulatory elements that underlie phenotypic variation. For example, in the kelp Saccharina latissima, transcriptional patterns differed between temperate and sub-Arctic populations, with population divergence in cell wall synthesis genes implicated in local adaptation18. Specifically, genes encoding enzymes involved in modifying polysaccharide composition and cell wall properties were differentially expressed between geographic origins, even under identical control conditions, suggesting that cell wall structural properties are a fundamental local adaptation trait in this species. Moreover, epigenetic regulation can modulate traits without altering DNA sequence19; and in stable environments, repeated parental epigenetic patterns may produce de facto transgenerational regulation, significantly contributing to adaptation20. In particular, DNA methylation in S. latissima has been proposed as an epigenetic mechanism contributing to thermal acclimation and the existence of ecotypes21. Importantly, origin-specific methylation marks persist even under identical laboratory cultivation conditions, suggesting transgenerational epigenetic priming in kelp. The intertwined contribution of these two mechanisms (i.e. selection on specific alleles and stable, transgenerational epigenetic regulations) shapes phenotypes and fitness by modulating gene expression profiles in model organisms such as Escherichia coli22 and Arabidopsis thaliana7. Several studies suggest that changes in gene expression linked to environmental variability may play a crucial role in adaptive evolution23, based on observed genotype-by-environment (GxE) interactions in the expression level of specific genes associated with fitness in a given environment.

Identifying genes underlying local or regional adaptation requires testing gene–environment interactions across genotypes23, although GxE alone does not confirm local adaptation6,24,25. Three experimental frameworks assess local adaptation: 1) Home versus Away compares fitness in native versus foreign habitats7; 2) Local versus Foreign contrasts resident and non-resident performance under the same conditions24; 3) Sympatric versus Allopatric integrates both by evaluating locals in their habitat against foreigners there and locals in non-native environments25. Reciprocal transplantation or common-garden experiments apply these designs across diverse taxa and conditions24. By examining genes specifically up- or down-regulated in “Home,” “Local,” or “Sympatric” contexts versus “Away,” “Foreign,” or “Allopatric” conditions, it becomes possible to pinpoint candidate loci driving phenotypes that maximize fitness under local adaptation.

Macrocystis pyrifera, the largest and most widespread kelp species, forms productive underwater forests in cool-temperate coastal waters5. In South America, it spans from 12°S to 55°S along the Pacific coast. It endures a sea-surface temperature gradient (~ 18 °C in Peru to 6–8 °C near Cape Horn) and its distribution range encompasses biogeographic discontinuities at 30–33°S and 42°S driven by changes in upwelling regimes, exposure to wave action and freshwater inputs26. Genetic structuring reflects these discontinuities and thermal clines27–29, suggesting adaptive divergence may have shaped the distribution of the giant kelp. Solas et al.8 tested local thermal adaptation25 in M. pyrifera using a reciprocal common garden experiment with juvenile sporophytes from these three biogeographically distinct regions, assessing performance through blade health and mortality as fitness proxies. Sporophytes consistently performed better under their native thermal conditions, with blade damage and mortality increasing significantly under non-native temperatures. Building on these phenotypic findings, the present study analyzes transcriptomes of the same experimentally reared juvenile sporophytes to identify gene expression patterns under "Local versus Foreign" and "Sympatric versus Allopatric" contrasts, and to characterize the molecular mechanisms underlying the divergent thermal phenotypes documented by Solas et al.8.

Materials and methods

Laboratory-reared young sporophytes and common garden experiments

The common garden experiment of Solas et al.8 assessed local thermal adaptation along the Peru–Chile Pacific coast using juvenile sporophytes of M. pyrifera obtained from controlled crosses of parents sampled in three biogeographically distinct coastal regions: Atacama (ATA, 19°S–23°S), Southern Chile (SCH, 36°S–41°S) and Magallanes (MAG, 51°S–53°S), characterized by contrasting mean annual sea surface temperatures of approximately 16 °C, 12 °C and 8 °C, respectively (Fig. 1B; SST data from the NASA Giovanni platform, 10-year January monthly averages). Five male and five female gametophytes were isolated from separate parental sporophytes collected from each site (Fig. 1A). Sibling female gametophytes were pooled, and crosses were performed using a pool of male gametophytes from different sites within the same region (i.e. intra-region crosses;8). Gametophytes were cultured in filtered natural seawater enriched with Provasoli nutrient solution, under white light at 25–30 µmol photons m⁻2 s⁻1 and a 12 h:12 h light:dark photoperiod, with weekly seawater changes. All crosses were performed at the mean regional SST representative of the parental origin (ATA: 16 °C; SCH: 12 °C; MAG: 8 °C). Young sporophytes were reared for three months under these respective temperatures until they reached 20–40 mm in length, ensuring sufficient biomass for downstream RNA analyses. At this point, juvenile sporophytes from each regional pool were divided into three aliquots, each exposed to one of the three regional average SSTs (i.e. 8 °C, 12 °C, or 16 °C) for one month and then flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen. A one-month temperature exposure allows assessment of acclimatory gene expression responses rather than acute stress reactions to temperature shifts (see8 for full experimental details).

RNA Extraction, library construction and sequencing

RNA was extracted using the RNeasy PowerPlant ® kit (Qiagen) following the manufacturer’s protocol, with slight modifications in incubation time (1 h vs. 20 min.), in volumes of cleaning buffer (450 ul vs. 300 ul), and in the number of cleaning steps (2 steps vs. 1 step). Residual genomic DNA was removed using a RNase-Free DNase I for on-column digestion (Qiagen), following the manufacturer’s protocol. The quality and quantity of RNA was assessed using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer in the laboratory facilities of BGI Genomics (Hong Kong, China). Only samples with the highest RNA quality were selected for each of the nine experimental groups (Fig. 1A), resulting in a total of N = 32 samples used for RNA sequencing. Depending on the samples’ region of origin (i.e. ATA, SCH, or MAG) and temperature treatment (i.e. 8 °C, 12 °C, or 16 °C), three to four juvenile sporophytes were sequenced per experimental group (see Supplementary Table S1 for more details).

The generation of libraries and sequencing took place at BGI Genomics laboratories (Hong Kong, China). Libraries were built using an mRNA enrichment method, which captures 3’-poly-A tails. The transcriptomes were sequenced in paired end mode (2 × 150 bp) on a DNBSEQ-500 platform (MGI, China).

Sequence data pre-processing, mapping and annotation

The quality of unprocessed reads was evaluated using the FASTQC30. Primers were removed using Trimmomatic31. Quality control was performed using Prinseq32: bases with a Phred Quality Score (QS) < 30 were trimmed from the 3’ end, and reads with a mean QS < 30 or a length < 50 bp were discarded. Contaminant reads were removed using Kraken233 with the Standard-FULL Prebuilt RefSeq index (2022–06-07), retaining only unclassified and eukaryotic reads.

Cleaned reads were mapped against a reference transcriptome of Macrocystis pyrifera comprising 63,219 transcripts (34, initial transcriptome contig assembly before any filtering steps was used, NCBI BioProject PRJNA661280). This reference transcriptome was assembled from both gametophyte and sporophyte life stages. TransDecoder v. 5.7.0 (https://github.com/TransDecoder/TransDecoder;35) was used to filter gene-coding transcripts. RNA-seq reads were mapped to these M. pyrifera gene-coding transcripts using STAR36.

After mapping and filtering for expression in our juvenile sporophyte samples, 39,928 transcripts showed detectable expression and passed quality thresholds for downstream analyses. The assembled transcriptome from the present study (39,928 transcripts) has been deposited to support full reproducibility of this work [https://doi.org/10.5061/dryad.hqbzkh1xh].

Differential gene expression analysis

The aligned reads were counted using BEDTOOLS multicov37. Differential gene expression analysis was performed using a negative binomial distribution in the DESeq2 package38. Since we were interested in identifying transcripts with a unique expression pattern corresponding to Local versus Foreign contrast, pairwise comparisons between regions (i.e. ATA, SCH, or MAG) were performed independently at each temperature treatment (i.e. 8 °C, 12 °C, or 16 °C).

Transcripts with Benjamini–Hochberg adjusted p-value < 0.01 and absolute log₂ fold change > 2 were considered differentially expressed. Transcripts clustering of the subset of differentially expressed genes was performed by k-medoids clustering using CLARA39. The gene expression data for individual replicates were averaged and normalised to zero-mean in DESeq238. Analyses were performed independently for each temperature treatment (i.e. 8 °C, 12 °C, or 16 °C) using K = 6 clusters.

Linear discriminant analysis of effect size40 were used to further identify significantly and consistently up- or downregulated genes associated with local adaptation expectations (e.g.,41). LEfSe identifies gene expression features that may explain differences among the defined classes, tests their significance with Kruskal–Wallis and Wilcoxon rank-sum tests, and then uses redundancy analysis to estimate the effect size associated with each feature40. Six LEfSe analyses were performed, using the complete normalized transcript abundance matrix. LEfSe comparisons are detailed in Supplementary Table S2. Briefly, the first three analyses aimed to test gene expression patterns expected in typical “Local versus Foreign” comparisons. Analyses were as follows: Local = ATA-16 °C versus Foreign = SCH-16 °C and MAG-16 °C; Local = SCH-12 °C versus Foreign = ATA-12 °C and MAG-12 °C; Local = MAG-8 °C versus Foreign = ATA-8 °C and SCH-8 °C. The second group of analyses tested gene expression patterns expected in “Sympatric versus Allopatric” comparisons. Analyses were as follows: Sympatric = ATA-16 °C versus Allopatric = ATA-12 °C, ATA-8 °C, SCH-16 °C and MAG-16 °C; Sympatric = SCH-12 °C versus Allopatric = SCH-16 °C, SCH-8 °C, ATA-12 °C and MAG-12 °C; Sympatric = MAG-8 °C versus Allopatric = MAG-16 °C, MAG-12 °C, ATA-8 °C and SCH-8 °C. LEfSe analyses were performed on GALAXY (http://huttenhower.sph.harvard.edu/galaxy/), using α = 0.05 and Log LDA Score = 2.0.

Functional annotation

The functional annotation was done by blasting transcript sequences against the NCBI UniRef 90 databank (42; UniProt release 2025_01, published February 2025) and the NCBI non-redundant (nr) protein database using BlastX with an e-value cut-off set to 10⁻543. Functional descriptions and GO terms44 were assigned from the best UniRef90 hits via UniProtKB mapping.

Results

From the 32 samples of M. pyrifera, 747,482,172 raw paired-end reads were obtained and deposited as a Short Read Archive (SRA) in NCBI (BioProject PRJNA1055545). A total of 655,848,296 paired-end reads remained after filtering, leading to 39,928 assembled transcripts, with an average length of 780 bp per transcript and an average coverage of 7,700,000 paired-end reads per individual. Of these assembled transcripts, 29,668 (i.e. 74.3%) were successfully annotated, while only 15,191 (i.e. 51.2%) had a GO associated with biological processes.

Effect of the region of origin on gene expression profiles

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs; i.e. either up- or down-regulated) were identified among pairs of experimental groups of distinct origins (Fig. 2, see Supplementary Table S3 for more details). Clear differences were found in the quantity of DEG transcripts among sporophytes from distinct regions of origin, with less DEG counted at 16 °C (total = 349 DEGs, Fig. 2A) than at 12 °C (total = 969 DEGs, Fig. 2B), or 8 °C (total = 1468 DEGs, Fig. 2C). Most DEGs identified at 16 °C were between ATA and MAG (255 DEGs, representing 73% of the total identified DE transcripts at this temperature, Fig. 2A). At both 12 °C and 8 °C, most of the identified DEGs were between SCH and MAG (524 and 1,085 DEGs, respectively, Fig. 2B,C). Some candidate genes detected by the LEfSe analyses were also detected in the k-medoids clustering (Table 1, Supplementary Table S4). In total, regardless of the experimental group defined as “Local” or “Sympatric”, 23% (i.e. 203 out of the 885 candidate genes) of the candidate genes detected by the LEfSe using the LOC versus FOR comparison were shared with the k-medoids clustering analyses, while 19% (i.e. 92 out of the 497 candidate genes) of the candidate genes detected by the LEfSe using the SYM versus ALO comparison were also detected using k-medoids clustering (Table 1, Fig. 3, Supplementary Table S4). Most of these candidate genes shared between the LEfSe and the k-medoids clustering analyses were detected in groups of transcripts uniquely upregulated (Table 1, Supplementary Table S4). For example, of the 442 candidate genes potentially sustaining local adaptation in MAG–8 °C detected using the LOC versus FOR comparison (Table 1), 61 of the 123 over-expressed transcripts detected in the LEfSe were also detected using k-medoids clustering (i.e. 50%) while only 20 of the 319 under-expressed transcripts detected in the LEfSe were detected using the k-medoids clustering (i.e. 6%).

Between 20 and 100% of the differentially expressed transcripts identified by both the k-medoids clustering and the LEfSe analyses, listed in Table 1, were successfully annotated. However, some specific combinations recovered mostly unknown genes. For example, only 43% of the downregulated transcripts in LOC versus FOR comparisons and 20% of the SYM versus ALO comparisons for SCH at 12 °C were annotated. Similarly, only 50% of the downregulated transcripts in SYM versus ALO comparison for MAG at 8 °C, were annotated.

Functions associated with candidate genes potentially sustaining local adaptation in each region

Candidate genes uniquely and consistently upregulated in ATA at 16 °C (5 candidate genes, Table 2) were associated with resistance/resilience to stressors, such as the synthesis of antioxidants (i.e. Glutathione S-transferase), the regulation of intracellular protein turnover (i.e. Ubiquitin thioesterase OTU), detoxification enzymes (i.e. Aldo–keto reductase), as well as DNA repair (i.e. 4-aminobutyrate aminotransferase). Two of the five genes uniquely and consistently downregulated in ATA at 16 °C are involved in cell transport of ions and proteins (i.e. MOB kinase activator-like and Copper-transporting ATPase; Table 2). Among the 24 transcripts identified in SCH (Table 3), 22 were over-expressed and involved the metabolism of diverse molecules such as lipids, protein, carbohydrates, and polysaccharides (e.g. Ubiquitin carboxyl-terminal hydrolase, Alpha/beta hydrolase protein, Phytanoyl-CoA dioxygenase, Beta-glucosidase, Phosphoglycerate mutase-like protein 1, and Enoyl-CoA hydratase) and the mechanism controlling cell structure and dynamic, such as cell division and apoptosis (e.g. SAM-dependent methyltransferase, SAP domain-containing protein, and EF-hand domain-containing protein). One over-expressed gene (i.e. Succinate dehydrogenase [ubiquinone] cytochrome b small subunit) and one of the two under-expressed transcripts are involved in the regulation of mitochondrial metabolic processes, signalling and homeostasis (Table 3). Among the 27 transcripts under selection in MAG (Table 4), 22 were over-expressed and linked to photoprotective function (three genes related to melanin production), nitrate and sulphate assimilation, carbohydrates metabolism (i.e. acetyl-CoA carboxytransferase and Pyrophosphate-fructose 6-phosphate 1-phosphotransferase) and immune system. Of the five genes uniquely and consistently downregulated in MAG at 8 °C (Table 4), two have known functions and corresponded to 60S ribosomal export protein NMD3 and a WW domain-containing protein.

Discussion

In this study, transcriptomic data from sporophytes of the giant kelp M. pyrifera subjected to a common garden experiment was used to compare different regions and experimental temperatures. By comparing gene expression levels according to two established methods for detecting local adaptation, i.e. “Local versus Foreign”24 and “Sympatric versus Allopatric’’25, we were interested in exploring the genetic bases of among-habitats divergence in thermal tolerance. These gene expression patterns, by themselves, do not indicate local adaptation. However, they match previously detected fitness differences among regions according to the same “Local versus Foreign” and “Sympatric versus Allopatric’’ contrasts and using the same experimentally reared juvenile sporophytes. In8, local adaptation was detected in M. pyrifera by evidencing up to 20-fold increased risk of fitness loss in terms of frond bleaching and tissue loss, when exposing sporophytes to non-native temperature, following the “Sympatric versus Allopatric’’ statistical framework. The individuals exposed to the most contrasting conditions, such as individuals from Atacama exposed to 8 °C or individuals from Magallanes exposed to 16 °C, were the most affected by fitness reductions8. By analysing the fold-change in gene expression levels following the same statistical framework, the present work provides a useful approach to identify genes potentially sustaining local adaptation to the thermal seascape.

Although transcription is the first of many steps on the pathway connecting the genotype to the phenotype and to fitness, mRNA’s relative abundance can be treated as a quantitative molecular phenotype subject to both genetic and environmental control, and capable of responding to selection45. Other powerful mRNA-based methods identify genes related with local adaptation (e.g.26,28,35). These methods are, however, mostly restricted to model organisms for which extensive genomic resources are available and experimental manipulation and reproduction is highly controlled and standardized, enabling the integration of phenotypic measurements, genomics and classical genetics to estimate the heritability of transcriptional variance and to link gene expression patterns with adaptive changes (e.g. maize46, thale cress,47). Because regulation of expression often occurs through the cumulative effect of numerous small-effect loci47, connecting genotype, transcripts abundance and phenotypic traits directly affecting fitness is still highly complex. Nonetheless, specific genetic variants have been directly linked to changes in transcript expression conferring adaptation, including thermal adaptation46,48. For example, mutations in the promoter region of the transcription factor Ghd8 and the gene HAN1 alter their expression levels, leading to enhanced cold tolerance in rice49,50.

In macroalgae, as in most marine non-model organisms, few studies have focused on in gene expression changes potentially sustaining local adaptation. Most of these were based on the correlation between thermal tolerance and gene expression using genotypes isolated from habitats of contrasting thermal regimes42,51,52. Usually, these studies have focused primarily on genes involved in stress response mechanisms and used either “Home versus Away” or “Local versus Foreign” approaches for physiological measurements. For example,51 examined thermal stress resilience across four North Atlantic populations of the brown algae Fucus serratus, assessing both physiological (i.e. photosynthetic performance) and transcriptomic responses (i.e. gene expression of specific heat shock proteins) under common garden conditions (i.e. “Home versus Away” approach). A higher tolerance to extreme heat linked to higher constitutive expression of HSPs was observed in the low latitude population. In the kelp S. latissima, transcriptional patterns were studied in blades from a temperate eastern Atlantic population (Roscoff, France) and a sub-Arctic population (Spitsbergen, Norway) subjected to experimental temperatures following “Local versus Foreign” approach, and found evidence of the influence of the geographic origin on gene expression18, underlining the potential importance of various cellular processes, such as cell wall synthesis and reorganization, in sustaining local adaptation. In this species, evidence of population divergence in epigenetic regulation through DNA methylation was proposed as a mechanism contributing to both thermal acclimation and the existence of ecotypes21. Since some epigenetic marks developed in parental sporophytes under natural environmental conditions can be passed on to several generations, gene expression may well be under selection and participate in the local adaptation process19.

Although gene regulation likely contributes to local adaptation, the complexity of these processes can vary considerably depending on regional environmental conditions. While our sampling strategy spanned multiple sites per region, we acknowledge that without genotyping the sporophytes used in the experiment [e.g., 28], we cannot be completely certain that our sample captured the full extent of the genetic diversity known to exist within regional populations. Nonetheless, the substantial regional differences in both the number and identity of candidate thermal-adaptation genes we observed align coherently with the distinct thermal regimes and selection pressures characterizing each region.

Surprisingly, Atacama yielded far fewer candidates than the other two regions, while Magallanes exhibited the most. This suggests that responses to cold conditions engage a broader array of metabolic pathways and cellular functions than responses to moderate heat, consistent with findings in S. latissima, where low temperature also elicited a larger and more diverse transcriptomic response than higher temperatures18. The coastal ocean of the Atacama region is characterized by considerable inter-annual temperature fluctuations53, strongly driven by upwelling which cools coastal temperatures in specific areas (i.e. upwelling centres,54) and leads to a spatially and temporally heterogeneous thermal environment. High temperatures (i.e. > 20 °C) are occasionally reached during summer, leading to decreased growth and high mortality in M. pyrifera55. It is also in this region that kelps experience the strongest influence of warm episodes of El Niño-Southern Oscillations56, leading to massive mortalities54,57. Such a stochastic environment may constrain adaptation by at least two different mechanisms. Strong demographic fluctuations and recurrent thermal stress can reduce population effective size and therefore restrict selection efficiency58, potentially leading to few genes showing signals of positive selection. Alternatively, it can promote phenotypic plasticity to cope with such a spatio-temporal environmental variability59,60. Contrastingly, Magallanes endures persistently cold seawater (i.e. between 5 and 10 °C;61, where stable directional selection could favour cold-water-adapted phenotypes62. Southern Chile has a specific thermal regime, with overall temperate coastal conditions and strong but seasonally variable upwelling influence63. Such a predictable thermal regime may be also a persistent source of directional selection, potentially requiring specific metabolic pathways and cellular processes.

Candidate genes potentially supporting local thermal adaptation in each region were characterized by specific and distinct biological functions, including physiological and metabolic processes (Tables 2, 3 and 4; Fig. 4). In Atacama, candidate genes included glutathione S-transferase and aldo–keto reductase for ROS and carbonyl detoxification64 and ubiquitin proteins related with the turnover of structurally damaged (e.g. misfolded) proteins65 (Fig. 4), both linked to resistance/resilience to high temperatures in other algae and terrestrial plants66–68. In photosynthetic organisms, the loss of protein or enzyme function can decouple stages of important metabolic cycles, such as the Calvin-Benson cycle and cause an energy imbalance that further favours the production of ROS67–69. In Magallanes, upregulated genes included those involved in carbohydrate metabolism, crucial for cold adaptation in photosynthetic organisms69,72,73, including brown algae74 (Fig. 4). Cold reduces membrane fluidity and limits photosynthetic rate66,74. Some sugar molecules are known to stabilize lipid membranes in plants by binding to the phosphate and choline groups of membrane lipids73, while upregulation of glycolysis and the TCA cycle provides carbon flux for respiration72,75. Like Atacama, Magallanes’ adaptation also mitigates oxidative stress, although through different mechanisms: glyoxalase pathway activation (protecting against oxoaldehyde toxicity) and phlorotannin oxidation and mobilization (Fig. 4). A comparatively low expression of glutathione S-transferase at 8 °C suggests an alternative oxidative stress response, where hydroperoxides may convert to toxic aldehydes, countered by glyoxalase activation, a mechanism already reported as leading to cold tolerance in plant crops76. The southern Patagonian environment also presents high UV-B radiation, as a consequence of lowered stratospheric ozone levels77. The high expression of TYR protein synthesis genes (type-3 copper oxidases) in Macrocystis at 8 °C suggests activation of phlorotannin oxidation in response to combined cold and UV stress. In brown algae, phlorotannins accumulate as soluble compounds in physodes (specialized vesicles) and undergo oxidative transformation and subsequent release into the cell wall and extracellular space, generating reactive phenolic compounds with antioxidant capacity78. Oxidized phenolic compounds released from peripheral cells form protective layers for underlying photosynthetic tissue, providing both ROS scavenging and UV-B absorption78. The accumulation of sunscreen molecules has been proposed as a central adaptive strategy to low-temperature stress for photosynthetic organisms79 and could also represent an efficient adaptation to the extreme environmental conditions of southern Patagonia.

In Southern Chile (SCH), most overexpressed gene functions related to gene regulation and protein metabolism (e.g. assembly, modification, translocation), likely acting as an “assembly line” for protein maturation under specific conditions (Fig. 4). The down-regulation of Letm1 RBD domain-containing protein at 12 °C could impact carbon storage by reducing mitochondrial ATP synthase accumulation (as shown in Arabidopsis thaliana80;), affecting carbon usage from photosynthesis. Letm genes, known to be stress-inducible, may also contribute to abiotic stress responses. Limited annotation of other transcripts currently hinders a comprehensive view of adaptation in this region.

The regional transcriptomic patterns align coherently with the fitness differences documented by Solas et al.8 using the same experimental material. In Atacama, the upregulation of ROS and carbonyl detoxification genes (glutathione S-transferase, aldo–keto reductase) alongside DNA repair genes provides a plausible molecular basis for the observed warm-adapted phenotype, as the co-activation of these pathways reflects a coordinated cellular response to oxidative distress70,71. In Magallanes, sporophytes performed best at 8 °C and suffered the largest fitness costs under warming8; the molecular response we identify (i.e. carbohydrate metabolism reprogramming, glyoxalase activation, and phlorotannin mobilization) is consistent with a strategy to sustain energy flux and manage cold-induced oxidative stress. For Southern Chile, the transcriptomic signal, focused on gene regulation, protein modification, and cell cycle control, may reflect fine-tuning to maintain performance under the seasonally variable thermal conditions of this region.

Among the genes putatively under differential selection in each region, most with known functions showed over-expression. This may stem from annotation bias, as key processes in model species are often identified by over-expression, supporting resilience under thermal stress67. Under-expressed genes might also contribute to local adaptation, but few were annotated in M. pyrifera. Despite advances using Ectocarpus sp. as a model species for brown algae81, functional genomics remains limited in this group, with scarce genetic resources (but see82,83). The limited number of annotated candidate genes reflects these incomplete resources; however those that were annotated represent coherent functional categories strongly supported by the thermal stress literature. This study identified a set of transcripts with potential adaptive value in kelps across their thermal seascape, by using a statistical framework fundamentally dedicated to testing local adaptation. Future progress in identifying the functions of kelp genes will allow the identification of more genes from our catalogue. Further functional analysis should aim at clarifying how these expression changes improve fitness and contribute to kelp thermal tolerance84. Such insights are also relevant for reforestation, conservation, and cultivation efforts, where understanding the genetic basis of adaptation could guide selective breeding or the management of genetic resources.
