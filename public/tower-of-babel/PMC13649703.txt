Higher-dimensional embedding of time-series data for machine learning

Karan Singh, Pingal Pratyush Nath, Urbasi Sinha, Aninda Sinha

Abstract

IntroductionDeep learning has revolutionized image analysis, yet most clinical biosignals, especially multi-lead electrocardiograms (ECGs), remain one-dimensional and awkward for modern vision models.MethodsWe introduce an orthogonal-polynomial imaging (OPI) framework that encodes 12-lead ECGs into a single two-dimensional image through an invertible transformation prior to coarse-graining, producing compact image representations that retain sufficient information for accurate classification. We benchmark the proposed representation against established architectures including ResNet, Transformers, and 1D temporal convolutional models on large-scale PhysioNet ECG datasets.ResultsOur results demonstrate that OPI-based image representations achieve highly competitive classification performance while preserving diagnostically relevant temporal and cross-lead information.DiscussionThe framework bridges raw multi-channel biosignals and standard image-based learning architectures without representation loss prior to coarse-graining, offering an effective, scalable, and physics-inspired representation for automated cardiac diagnostics.

Introduction

Machine learning (; ; ; ; ; ; ) has become a cornerstone of modern medical diagnostics, delivering significant improvements in accuracy and efficiency and thereby enabling early detection across diverse healthcare applications (; ; ; ,; ; ; ; ; ; ; ; ). In the field of medical imaging, machine learning algorithms, particularly deep learning, have demonstrated exceptional effectiveness, often matching or surpassing human expertise in tasks such as tumor detection and disease classification (; ; ; ; ; ; ; ; ; ; ; , ). This capability to rapidly and accurately analyze complex visual data has made machine learning an indispensable tool for improving patient care and outcomes.

Despite these successes, many physiological signals—including electrocardiograms (ECGs)—are recorded as one-dimensional time series, where conventional analysis relies on handcrafted features or independent lead-by-lead processing. Such approaches often fail to fully capture the intricate spatiotemporal dependencies among signals. While deep neural networks can operate directly on raw time series, image-based representations provide an alternative means of exploiting spatial inductive biases and can achieve competitive performance on certain tasks. This discrepancy motivates a shift toward leveraging image processing techniques for problems traditionally rooted in one-dimensional signal analysis. The central challenge, however, is to transform one-dimensional signals into structured representations suitable for image processing, while preserving the inherent information and physiological structure. We will refer to this representation as Orthogonal Polynomial Imaging–OPI for short.

To address this, we introduce a novel encoding framework inspired by the partial wave decomposition of scattering amplitudes routinely used in high energy physics. Our method maps multiple one-dimensional signals into a unified two-dimensional representation. By superposing different signals using orthogonal polynomials, we generate compact images that embed both temporal dynamics and inter-lead relationships. This representation enables the direct use of advanced image classification models and facilitates the integration of diverse data sources into a single coherent structure ().

The relevance of this approach is particularly evident for the 12-lead ECG (; ; ; ; ; ; ; ; ,; ; ; ; ), the clinical gold standard for non-invasive cardiac assessment. Each lead provides a distinct projection of the heart's electrical activity, and accurate diagnosis requires synthesizing information across all 12. Analyzing these signals independently may overlook subtle inter dependencies, whereas our method integrates them naturally into a single image. This not only improves diagnostic accuracy but also provides a holistic view of cardiac electrophysiology. In this paper, we present a comprehensive evaluation of our encoding framework by classifying 12-lead ECG data from PhysioNet database and benchmarking our strategy against existing methods. Although our focus is on ECGs, the approach is general and can be extended to other multi-channel time-series data with similar characteristics.

12-Lead ECG

For over a century, the 12-lead ECG (; ; ) has been the primary tool for assessing cardiac electrical function, providing a rapid, inexpensive, and widely accessible means to diagnose various cardiovascular conditions. Using ten electrodes on the limbs and chest, it records 12 voltage signals which are distinct projections of the heart's electrical field offering a detailed spatiotemporal view of cardiac activity.

These 12 leads are anatomically organized to capture electrical vectors in two principal planes: the frontal plane, represented by the limb leads (I, II, III, aVR, aVL, aVF), and the horizontal plane, captured by the six precordial chest leads (V1–V6). This dual-plane perspective allows clinicians to localize myocardial ischemia, detect conduction blocks, and interpret complex arrhythmias with spatial specificity. Each lead contributes a unique window into the electrophysiological state of specific cardiac regions, making the 12-lead ECG an indispensable tool for diagnosing conditions ranging from atrial fibrillation and ventricular tachycardia to myocardial infarction and inherited channelopathies.

At the heart of ECG interpretation is the P-QRS-T waveform, which encodes the sequential electrical events of the cardiac cycle. The P wave reflects atrial depolarization, the QRS complex corresponds to the rapid depolarization of the ventricles, and the T wave represents ventricular repolarization. Clinical interpretation hinges not only on the morphology and amplitude of these components but also on the timing between them. Intervals such as the PR segment (atrial-to-ventricular conduction), ST segment (isoelectric period following depolarization), and QT interval (duration of ventricular electrical activity) carry critical diagnostic significance. For example, a left bundle branch block is identified by a QRS duration exceeding 120 milliseconds, along with specific waveform changes in lateral leads such as I, V5, and V6.

Despite its diagnostic richness, interpreting a 12-lead ECG is inherently complex (; ; ; ; ; ; ; ; ; ; ; ; ). Clinicians must synthesize information across multiple leads, correlate electrical signatures with anatomical context, and distinguish between normal variants and pathological findings, all while accounting for confounders like baseline noise, artifacts, or comorbidities. This level of interpretation requires years of training and is subject to variability between readers, often leading to missed or delayed diagnoses in clinical practice.

In this setting, automation through artificial intelligence becomes not only advantageous but essential. The 12-lead ECG is uniquely suited for machine learning applications due to its structured yet information-dense nature, combining temporal dynamics with spatially distributed features. AI models can be trained to recognize subtle patterns across leads, detect rare syndromes, and triage critical abnormalities with speed and consistency. By embedding expert-level interpretation into scalable software systems, AI has the potential to democratize access to cardiac diagnostics, reduce human error, and support timely clinical decision-making—especially in high-volume or resource-limited settings.

Deep learning architectures for automated ECG interpretation

The 12-lead ECG's rich spatiotemporal structure and multidimensional signal make it particularly well-suited for deep learning. Traditionally, ECG interpretation relied on handcrafted features derived from domain knowledge, such as RR intervals, QRS durations, and ST-segment deviations, fed into classical machine learning pipelines. However, in recent years, this paradigm has shifted dramatically with the advent of deep learning models that operate directly on raw or minimally preprocessed waveforms. These end-to-end approaches learn task-specific representations automatically, often capturing subtle or subclinical patterns that may be imperceptible to human experts (; ; ). This shift has been propelled by several key innovations. Contrastive learning techniques (; ; ; ; ; ; ; ; ; ) have enabled models to learn discriminative features from unlabeled ECG data by contrasting similar and dissimilar samples. Self-supervised learning and pretraining strategies (; ; ; ; ; ; ; ; ) have further improved generalization by leveraging large-scale unannotated datasets. In parallel, attention-based architectures (; ; ; ; ; ; ; ; ; ) have enhanced model interpretability and performance by dynamically focusing on the most relevant temporal or spatial regions of the signal.

Convolutional neural networks : capturing morphology

Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) form the backbone of ECG classification models due to their ability to hierarchically learn key morphological features such as wave amplitude, duration, and slope (; ; ; ). Approaches range from simple 1D-CNN architectures for ECG classification (; ) to more advanced models that integrate Recurrent Neural Networks for improved temporal modeling and classification accuracy (; ). Generic CNNs have also been successfully adapted for ECG analysis on wearable devices (), and some have been further enhanced using wavelet transforms to better capture time-frequency information (). Given the black-box nature of deep learning models, recent efforts such as the CNN Explanations Framework for ECG Signals (CEFEs) have focused on providing interpretable insights into how CNN-based models make decisions ().

Recurrent neural networks

Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs), particularly Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) and Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU) variants, are well-suited for modeling temporal dependencies in ECG signals, both across and within cardiac cycles (). Their strength lies in capturing sequential patterns, making them especially effective for tasks like arrhythmia classification and atrial fibrillation detection, where inter-beat relationships play a crucial role (; ; ).

To leverage both spatial and temporal information, hybrid architectures that combine Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) with RNNs have gained popularity. These models first extract morphological features using CNN layers and then model temporal dynamics through RNNs, enabling more comprehensive ECG analysis (; ; ; ; ; ; ; ).

Beyond classification, RNNs have also been explored in a range of ECG-related applications, including biometric identification (; ), and sleep apnea detection (), highlighting their versatility in capturing the temporal structure inherent to physiological signals.

The attention revolution: transformers in ECG analysis

Transformers have recently surpassed recurrent neural networks (RNNs) in sequence modeling, owing to their self-attention mechanism, which efficiently captures long-range dependencies in parallel (). In the context of ECG analysis, several domain-specific adaptations, such as lead-wise attention, temporal-channel attention, and multi-head spatiotemporal transformers, have been developed to allow models to focus simultaneously on diagnostically relevant waveform segments and leads ().

Beyond general classification, attention mechanisms have been integrated into diverse ECG-related applications. These include biometric identification (; ), CNN-based classification frameworks (; ; ), fetal ECG extraction from maternal recordings (), and expert-guided attention systems leveraging deep visual interpretability for multi-class disease detection (; ; ; ; ; ; ; ). Attention-based models have also been employed for stress detection from ECG signals () and refined self-attention frameworks for disease classification (). Collectively, these advances illustrate the versatility of attention architectures in extracting clinically meaningful information from complex ECG data.

Self-supervised and contrastive pre-training

Self-supervised learning, particularly contrastive learning, has emerged as a powerful approach for ECG representation learning and downstream tasks. Applications include ECG representation and phenotyping (), classification using transformer-based contrastive models (; ), general ECG modeling (), stress detection from ECG signals (), biometric identification through contrastive learning of ECG (), and anomaly detection frameworks (). Several works also explore physiologically inspired augmentations to enhance the robustness and clinical relevance of learned representations.

Hybrid models: the best of all worlds

Many of the best-performing models today are hybrids. For example, CNNs may be used to extract short-term features, followed by transformers for capturing long-range dependencies. Some even combine self-supervised pretraining with supervised fine-tuning on patient-specific data. This modular approach enhances flexibility and makes models more transferable across tasks and institutions.

Together, these developments have led to significant advances in ECG-based diagnostics across a wide range of clinical tasks (; ; ; ; ; ; ; ; ), moving the field toward more accurate, data-driven, and scalable cardiovascular assessment.

Materials and methods

This section is organized into three parts: first, we describe the datasets along with the processing and pre-processing techniques applied; second, we outline the models employed in our study; and third, we detail the experiments conducted to evaluate their performance.

Data processing

Dataset

We evaluated our 12-lead ECG classification framework using the PhysioNet dataset. The first is the PhysioNet ECG-Arrhythmia Database, which comprises 45,152 10-s, resting 12-lead ECG recordings sampled at 500 Hz. ECGs were collected from multiple clinical sites including Chapman University, Shaoxing People's Hospital, and Ningbo First Hospital. Expert clinicians annotated each recording with both rhythm and conduction labels, and the data are stored in standard WFDB format along with demographic metadata. The dataset includes expert-validated annotations for over 60 rhythm and conduction conditions, such as atrial fibrillation, sinus bradycardia, ventricular premature beats, bundle branch blocks, myocardial infarctions in various walls, axis deviations, and repolarization abnormalities, each mapped to corresponding SNOMED CT codes.

The database contains one recording per patient (45,152 patients corresponding to 45,152 recordings), facilitating patient-wise data partitioning without risk of data leakage.

Data preparation

The raw 12-lead ECG recordings were obtained from the PhysioNet database. We curated the data into two distinct datasets based on the type of labels:

- Type 1: ECG records containing a single disease code per patient.
- Type 2: ECG records with one primary disease code and possibly additional comorbidities.

We analyzed three disease categories—Sinus Tachycardia (ST), Sinus Bradycardia (SB), and Sinus Rhythm (SR)—with their SNOMED CT codes and sample sizes listed in .

To construct a unified high-dimensional representation, we transform this multi-channel time series into a two-dimensional spatial–temporal image I by superposing the individual leads along an orthogonal spatial basis ().

Each ECG lead is associated with a distinct orthogonal polynomial Pl(x), drawn primarily from the Legendre family, though Chebyshev polynomials were also explored. These bases possess desirable mathematical properties, including mutual orthogonality and completeness over finite domains, enabling a compact and non-redundant encoding of lead-specific information. Although OPI is named after the underlying family of orthogonal polynomials, the basis functions are normalized in practice, yielding an orthonormal basis that permits exact inversion before coarse-graining. For clarity, we refer to the resulting normalized polynomial basis simply as the orthonormal basis throughout the remainder of this work.

Since the normalized polynomial basis forms an orthonormal basis, OPI provides a bijective mapping between the multi-lead ECG time series and its two-dimensional representation prior to coarse-graining, enabling downstream learning models to operate on a unified spatial structure while retaining full access to lead-wise temporal information.

The resulting function I(x, t) forms a 5,000 × 5,000 superposition image that preserves information from all leads while compactly fusing the multi-lead signals into a single higher-dimensional structure.

To reduce computational cost and facilitate efficient training, these superposition images were coarse-grained using an averaging operation over non-overlapping blocks. Each pixel in a coarse-grained image corresponds to the mean value of the corresponding block in the original superposition matrix, given by :

In practice, we used block sizes of 50 × 50 and 100 × 100, producing final images of size 100 × 100 and 50 × 50, respectively.

Before applying the superposition procedure, each individual 12-lead ECG sample, represented as a matrix of size 5,000 × 12, is independently normalized and standardized using fit_transform(each sample). All normalization parameters are computed on a per-sample and per-lead basis, without using statistics from other samples or from the test set, thereby preventing information leakage.

For each ECG sample, we applied fit_transform from StandardScaler in scikit-learn, which independently standardizes each lead by subtracting its temporal mean and scaling it to unit variance.

This preprocessing step ensures uniform scaling across leads within each ECG recording and allows each lead to contribute comparably to the final image representation.

This approach effectively captures inter-lead relationships while maintaining salient temporal features of the ECG, resulting in compact 2D images that are suitable as inputs for both fully connected and convolutional neural networks.

The average preprocessing time was approximately 222.55s (about 3.71 min) per batch of 500 ECG records.

Splits

For all experiments, data are partitioned using a patient-wise 70–15–15 train-validation-test split. Since each patient in the PhysioNet ECG Arrhythmia database contributes exactly one recording, this ensures no subject appears in multiple sets. The splits are implemented using five different random seeds (random_state ∈ {40, 41, 42, 43, 44}) to ensure reproducibility.

Early stopping often terminated training before reaching the maximum number of 25 epochs. The best validation accuracy achieved during training was recorded and used for final evaluation.

All experiments were implemented in PyTorch, and computations were carried out on an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3050 GPU with CUDA support, otherwise defaulting to a CPU environment.

Convolutional neural networks

To further enhance the model's ability to capture spatial hierarchies and complex local patterns inherent in medical image data, we employed a unified Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) architecture, referred to as SmallCNN. Unlike fully connected networks, CNNs exploit spatial locality through convolutional kernels, enabling the model to learn translationally invariant features from the encoded ECG images.

To facilitate experiments across multiple spatial resolutions, the architecture includes an Adaptive Average Pooling layer. This allows the network to process inputs of varying sizes (e.g., 100 × 100 or 50 × 50) and produce a fixed-size feature representation for the classification head without changing the model parameters.

Reproducibility and Data Integrity: To ensure the scientific validity of our results, the following experimental protocols were strictly followed:

- Patient-Independent Splits: All datasets were split into training, validation, and test sets using a strictly patient-wise strategy with a 70–15–15 split. No data from any single subject appears in more than one set, preventing identity-based leakage.
- Stratification and Seed: Splits were stratified to maintain the original class distribution of ST, SB, and SR. All experiments were conducted with five different random seeds (40, 41, 42, 43, 44) for deterministic weight initialization and reproducibility.

Vanilla transformer

To evaluate the OPI framework against a high-capacity sequence-modeling baseline, we implemented a Vanilla Transformer architecture designed for multi-lead ECG classification. This baseline operates directly on the raw 1D temporal signals, allowing for a comparison between attention-based sequence modeling and our proposed 2D vision-based embedding.

Architecture and Tokenization: The input ECG signal X ∈ R12×5,000 is processed using a patch-based tokenization approach. The sequence length (L = 5,000) is divided into N = 100 non-overlapping patches, each of size P = 50 samples. To capture inter-lead correlations within each time-step, patches from all 12 leads are concatenated, resulting in a flattened vector of dimension 12 × 50 = 600 per patch.

These vectors are projected into a dmodel = 128 latent space using a linear embedding layer. A learnable class (CLS) token is prepended to the sequence, and fixed sinusoidal positional encodings are added to the embeddings to retain temporal order. The core of the model consists of 4 Transformer encoder layers, each utilizing 8 attention heads and a feedforward network dimension of 512 with GELU activation.

Training Protocol: The Transformer was trained following the same data integrity standards as the OPI-CNN models:

- Data Splits: A strictly patient-independent 70–15–15 split was maintained to prevent data leakage.
- Optimizer: Adam optimizer was used with an initial learning rate of 1 × 10−4 and a weight decay of 1 × 10−4.
- Regularization: A dropout rate of 0.1 was applied within the encoder layers, and 0.5 within the final classification head to mitigate overfitting.

ResNet model

To evaluate whether the OPI framework provides superior feature representation compared to standard lead-by-time matrices, we implemented a modified ResNet-18 baseline, hereafter referred to as ECGResNet. This model processes the raw 12-lead ECG signal as a single-channel 2D image of dimension 12 × 5,000 without any orthogonal embedding.

Architecture and Adaptation: We adapted the standard ResNet-18 architecture to handle grayscale medical data:

- Input Layer: The initial convolutional layer was modified to accept 1 input channel instead of the standard 3 (RGB). To leverage transfer learning, the weights were initialized by averaging the pretrained ImageNet weights across the input channels.
- Spatial Hierarchy: The model utilizes the standard four-stage residual blocks, followed by global average pooling and a final dense layer for 3-class classification.

This baseline tests the 2D approach, allowing us to quantify the performance gain achieved by projecting signals onto an orthogonal spatial manifold (OPI) vs. using a raw data grid.

SelfEEG ResNet1D baseline

To provide a strong temporal baseline operating directly on the raw signal, we trained the ResNet1D architecture from the SelfEEG library (), hereafter referred to as SelfEEGResNet1D. This model is a residual 1D convolutional neural network designed for time-series data and processes the raw ECG waveform without any spatial encoding. The network takes as input the multi-lead or single-lead ECG signal (either 12 × 5,000 for all leads or 1 × 5,000 for Lead 1 alone), where convolutions operate exclusively along the temporal dimension to learn hierarchical temporal representations.

This baseline evaluates a purely temporal convolutional architecture trained directly on the raw ECG signals, providing a strong 1D CNN reference for comparison with both the 2D ResNet baseline and the proposed OPI-based representation. A single-lead variant using only Lead 1 was also trained to assess the contribution of multi-lead information within the same architectural framework.

Architecture:

- Input Stem: An initial convolutional layer with kernel size 7 followed by batch normalization and ReLU activation expands the feature representation while preserving the temporal resolution.
- Residual Stages: Four sequential residual stages with channel progression 32 → 64 → 128 → 256. Each stage contains two basic residual blocks () with kernel size 7. Temporal downsampling is introduced via strided convolutions at the transitions between stages.
- Classification Head: A global average pooling layer aggregates temporal features, followed by a fully connected layer producing predictions for the three ECG classes.

Experiments

We conducted experiments on the two dataset configurations Type 1 and Type 2 mentioned in Section 2.1.2. Across both dataset types, we evaluated three categories of input representations: (1) orthogonal polynomial image encodings of multi-lead ECGs, (2) raw multi-lead ECG signals, and (3) raw single-lead ECG signals. Neural network architectures were selected based on the input modality, as summarized below.

Image-based encodings

Using the superposition and coarse-graining framework, the 12-lead ECG signals were encoded into two-dimensional images via orthogonal polynomials (Legendre and Chebyshev).

- CNN on 100 × 100 images: Trained separately on Legendre, and Chebyshev.
- CNN on 50 × 50 images: Trained on Legendre encoding.

Single-lead signals

To provide a baseline independent of polynomial encoding, we also trained a fully connected neural network (FFNN) directly on the raw ECG waveform from the first lead. Each input sample consisted of a one-dimensional vector of length 5,000, corresponding to the full temporal sequence.

ResNet

To benchmark against standard 2D image-based approaches, we trained a ECGResNet model on the raw lead-by-time ECG matrix of size 12 × 5,000 on both Type 1 and Type 2 datasets. In addition, we trained a non-pretrained variant of the same architecture (randomly initialized weights) to isolate the effect of transfer learning from ImageNet pretraining.

Vanilla transformer

To benchmark against high-capacity sequence models, we trained the Vanilla Transformer model on the raw 12-lead ECG signals on both Type 1 and Type 2 datasets.

ResNet1D

To benchmark against strong temporal convolutional architectures, we trained a ResNet1D model from the SelfEEG library on the raw 12-lead ECG signals. The model operates directly on the temporal dimension and processes the input as a multichannel time-series signal, providing a strong 1D CNN baseline for both Type 1 and Type 2 datasets. A single-lead variant using only Lead 1 (1 × 5,000) was also trained to evaluate the contribution of multi-lead information within the same 1D residual framework.

Baseline 1 : ResNet

We use a modified ResNet-18 architecture (described in Section 2.2.4), denoted as ECGResNet, as the baseline model for several reasons:

- ResNet architectures are well-established benchmarks and have demonstrated strong performance on ECG classification tasks.
- A pretrained ResNet-18 provides a robust signal-domain reference and serves as a widely adopted baseline for learning directly from raw ECG signals.
- Its proven effectiveness enables a fair evaluation: if orthogonal-polynomial imaging (OPI) representations achieve competitive or superior performance, this validates their utility against a strong baseline.
- The 12-lead ECG is treated as a 2D signal with leads and time forming the two spatial dimensions, and the architecture accepts input of shape (12, 5,000).

The motivation is to compare learning directly from raw ECG signals with learning from orthogonal-polynomial imaging (OPI) representations of the same data, thereby isolating the impact of the input representation on classification performance.

Discussion

Comparison of encoding methods

To evaluate the effect of different basis functions on model performance, we compared CNN classifiers trained on PhysioNet ECG data encoded with Legendre, and Chebyshev polynomials. Alongside accuracy, we report the Macro F1-Score (balance of precision and recall), Macro AUC-ROC (threshold-independent discrimination), and Macro Recall (clinical sensitivity). Results are presented separately for Data Type 1 (clean, single-diagnosis) and Data Type 2 (comorbidity cases). The full benchmarking results across all architectures on Data Type 1 are summarized in .

Both encoding methods achieved excellent and highly comparable performance on the clean dataset. The Legendre polynomial encoding yielded the highest peak accuracy at 98.58% ± 0.13%, with a slightly lower performance by Chebyshev at 98.34% ± 0.13%. The other metrics remained uniformly high, confirming that both polynomial families produce highly effective image representations from the clean signals. To evaluate whether the differences among top-performing models on Data Type 1 are statistically distinguishable, we conducted seed-level paired t-tests across all five evaluation seeds. The Legendre CNN (98.58%±0.13%) is statistically indistinguishable from the 12-lead 1D-ResNet (98.40%±0.28%, paired t-test p = 0.191), confirming that the OPI 2D image representation achieves performance parity with a raw temporal ResNet. However, the Legendre CNN achieves a statistically significant improvement over Chebyshev CNN (98.34%±0.13%, p = 0.0008) and non-pretrained 2D ResNet (98.23%±0.20%, p = 0.0026). Furthermore, the 12-lead 1D-ResNet and Chebyshev CNN remain statistically indistinguishable (p = 0.669). The corresponding performance comparison across all models on Data Type 2 is reported in .

For the more complex dataset containing comorbidities, both encodings again performed comparably well, though the ranking between them reversed. The Legendre and Chebyshev encodings delivered similar peak accuracies of 97.19% ± 0.29% and 97.53% ± 0.12%, respectively, with the Chebyshev polynomial encoding performing slightly better.

Sensitivity to lead-to-polynomial assignment

To evaluate whether the performance of the Orthogonal Polynomial Imaging (OPI) framework depends on the specific mapping between ECG leads and polynomial basis functions, we conducted an empirical permutation experiment. We evaluated five distinct lead-to-polynomial assignments (Permutation 0 representing the default order P0 ... P11, alongside four randomly generated lead permutations, Permutations 1–4) using the Legendre CNN (100 × 100) architecture across five random evaluation seeds for both Data Type 1 and Data Type 2. As shown in , performance remains highly consistent across all five lead assignments on both clean (Type 1) and comorbidity (Type 2) datasets. On Data Type 1, mean classification accuracy ranges within a narrow band of 98.19%−98.58%, while on Data Type 2, accuracy spans 96.92%−97.35%. Paired t-tests between each permuted assignment and the default order reveal no statistically significant differences (p > 0.05). These results empirically confirm that the spatial inductive bias of the 2D CNN effectively extracts cross-lead features regardless of the specific ordering of spatial polynomial basis functions, establishing that the OPI encoding is robust and shows no statistically significant dependence on lead ordering.

Comparison with past work

Recent work has increasingly focused on leveraging 12-lead ECGs with machine learning to improve automated diagnosis. explored a self-supervised pretraining strategy in which a U-ResNet model reconstructs masked subsequences across all 12 leads, thereby learning generalizable representations from the large unlabeled CODE dataset. When fine-tuned on labeled datasets such as PTB-XL and CPSC, the pretrained model showed modest but consistent improvements (e.g., AUC 0.953 → 0.954 on CPSC) and enhanced training stability, highlighting the potential of self-supervision for ECG analysis in low-label scenarios.

Building on this direction, proposed ECG-MAE, a generative self-supervised framework that masks up to 75% of spatiotemporal patches—across both time and leads—and reconstructs them to learn robust representations. They introduced a hybrid CNN–Transformer encoder (MLE-ViT) specifically designed for multi-lead ECGs, and pretrained it on large corpora (CinC2020, Chapman) before fine-tuning on PTB-XL. This approach achieved state-of-the-art performance, with macro-AUC improving from 0.9325 (supervised) to 0.9474 after pretraining, demonstrating both stronger generalization and better label efficiency compared to purely supervised and contrastive self-supervised methods.

presented one of the earliest large-scale applications of deep learning to 12-lead ECGs, proposing an end-to-end ResNet-based model for automatic diagnosis of six common abnormalities. Trained on more than 2.3 million ECG exams from Brazil's Telehealth Network (CODE study), the network processed raw ECG signals sampled at 400 Hz and standardized to 4096 samples per lead. The model achieved high diagnostic accuracy, with F1 scores above 87% and specificity exceeding 99% across all classes. In head-to-head comparisons, the DNN matched or outperformed cardiology residents and medical students, with error analysis showing that network errors often arose from borderline threshold cases, whereas humans made more conceptual or attentional mistakes. This work provided compelling evidence for the clinical viability of deep learning in ECG interpretation, particularly in large-scale or resource-limited settings.

Taking a different perspective, proposed a framework that transforms 1D ECG signals into 2D images (scalograms) using continuous wavelet transform (CWT) and short-time Fourier transform (STFT). These image-based representations were classified using a custom 34-layer CNN, and further enhanced by combining the CNN as a deep feature extractor with an SVM classifier. This hybrid CNN–SVM achieved very high performance, reaching 99.21% accuracy with CWT-based scalograms, thereby demonstrating the effectiveness of converting ECG signals into images for deep learning–based classification.

More recently, proposed a geometric image representation of ECG signals by combining two leads through an outer-product construction, enabling beat-by-beat arrhythmia classification with a conventional 2D CNN. Their results further demonstrated the promise of image-based ECG representations while preserving clinically interpretable waveform characteristics. In contrast, our Orthogonal Polynomial Imaging (OPI) framework extends this line of work by encoding all 12 ECG leads simultaneously into a single structured image through orthogonal polynomial superposition, providing a mathematically principled multi-lead representation that preserves lead-specific information prior to coarse-graining.

Conclusion

This work introduces a framework for transforming multi-lead ECG signals into two-dimensional image representations through orthogonal polynomial superposition and coarse-graining. Inspired by techniques from high energy physics, the proposed encoding preserves the essential structure of the original signals while enabling image-based deep learning. Our extensive experiments across multiple datasets and model families establish three key findings. First, convolutional neural networks (CNNs) operating on polynomial-encoded images demonstrated that the proposed OPI representation can achieve performance comparable to strong temporal baselines, exceeding 98% accuracy on clean single-diagnosis data (Type 1) and maintaining robust performance (96–97%) in the presence of comorbidities (Type 2). In contrast, feed-forward neural networks trained on single-lead signals lagged behind, highlighting the diagnostic advantage of multi-lead integration. Comparisons with pretrained ResNet models yielded comparable classification accuracies but required substantially larger models and longer training times (). The proposed OPI representation also maintained strong performance under aggressive coarse-graining, demonstrating that compact image representations can remain highly effective for ECG classification. Similarly, models employing 1D attention mechanisms were unable to match the accuracy of the OPI-based approaches despite using larger and more complex network architectures.

Second, our comparative analysis of Legendre and Chebyshev encodings revealed uniformly strong results. This suggests that while the specific basis function can fine-tune performance, the broader principle of orthogonal polynomial superposition is highly robust. Third, we demonstrated that coarse-graining to lower resolutions (e.g., 50 × 50) retained excellent discriminative power, offering an efficient trade-off between accuracy and computational cost, which is crucial for real-time clinical deployment.

Relative to prior approaches, including direct raw waveform modeling with ResNets and image-based representations such as wavelet scalograms, our method offers a conceptually simple and generalizable encoding framework. However, the present study does not include a direct experimental comparison with alternative image-encoding approaches, and therefore no conclusions regarding superiority over such methods should be drawn. Unlike handcrafted feature pipelines or purely self-supervised pretraining schemes, our framework provides a physics-inspired, interpretable bridge between raw signals and deep learning architectures. Importantly, the confusion matrices and ROC analyses across experiments consistently show that sinus bradycardia is the most separable class, while sinus tachycardia and sinus rhythm exhibit higher overlap.

Looking ahead, several directions remain open. Integrating self-supervised or contrastive pretraining may improve generalization in low-label settings, while attention-based models could enhance interpretability by identifying the leads and temporal regions driving diagnostic decisions. Extending the framework to multi-label classification, rare arrhythmias, and longitudinal monitoring would broaden its clinical applicability. Beyond cardiology, the proposed encoding may also prove useful for multichannel time-series data in fields such as seismology, speech recognition, and financial forecasting. Finally, OPI is compatible with standard 12-lead ECG formats and existing clinical workflows, while its compact, partially invertible representation supports efficient inference and post-hoc interpretability.

While OPI preserves diagnostically relevant information and achieves performance comparable to strong temporal baselines, the present study does not isolate a representation-specific advantage arising from multi-lead encoding. While the lead-to-polynomial assignment was initially fixed, our empirical evaluations across permuted lead assignments () confirm that classification performance exhibits no statistically significant dependence on lead ordering (p > 0.05). Exploring adaptive polynomial orders and dynamic basis constructions remains an important direction for future work.

In summary, we have introduced a physics-inspired framework for encoding multi-lead ECGs into compact image representations suitable for modern vision models. Our results show that orthogonal polynomial encoding, combined with convolutional architectures, provides an effective and scalable approach to ECG classification. We hope this work motivates further exploration of principled representations for multichannel biomedical signals.
