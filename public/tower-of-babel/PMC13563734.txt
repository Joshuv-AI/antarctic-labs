Multimodal ultrasound in the diagnosis and treatment of caudate lobe Klebsiella pneumoniae liver abscess complicated by rare gas-containing inferior vena cava thrombosis: a case report
Authors: Wenting Yang, Yimei Deng, Yu Wang, Yang Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundKlebsiella pneumoniae liver abscess (KLA) is an emerging public health concern in Asia. Involvement of the caudate lobe with concurrent inferior vena cava thrombosis is exceedingly rare but life-threatening. Timely diagnosis and management are critical but challenging. Multimodal ultrasound can play a pivotal role in both diagnosis and treatment guidance.Case presentationA 66-year-old woman with diabetes presented with 10 days of fever and chills. Imaging revealed a caudate lobe liver abscess containing gas with extension to the inferior vena cava (IVC) and associated septic thrombosis. Contrast-enhanced ultrasound and CT confirmed a Klebsiella pneumoniae liver abscess. Ultrasound-guided percutaneous drainage was performed, and cultures identified an ESBL-producing Klebsiella pneumoniae. The patient was treated with broad-spectrum antibiotics, therapeutic anticoagulation, and catheter drainage. Despite an episode of septic shock during hospitalization, she gradually improved, and follow-up imaging showed near-complete resolution of the abscess and complete disappearance of the IVC thrombus.ConclusionThe caudate lobe KLA complicated by IVC thrombosis, particularly gas-containing septic thrombosis, is rare but life-threatening. Multimodal ultrasound plays an important role in the early diagnosis and interventional management of KLA complicated by IVC thrombosis. Timely ultrasound-guided drainage with targeted antimicrobial therapy and individualized anticoagulation can achieve effective source control and favorable outcomes without open surgery.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12879-026-13712-w.

Introduction

Klebsiella pneumoniae liver abscess (KLA), initially identified predominantly in Southeast Asia (particularly in Taiwan), has increasingly been reported in other regions, including Europe [1]. Even though liver abscess is a rare disease with an incidence of about 8–20 cases per 100,000 hospitalized patients, it is associated with a high mortality rate [2]. KLA typically affects the right or left hepatic lobes; involvement of the caudate lobe is extremely uncommon and presents unique diagnostic and interventional challenges due to its deep location adjacent to the inferior vena cava (IVC). Patients with PLA may present with typical infection symptoms such as fever and chills, or with invasive manifestations affecting the eyes or the central nervous system [3, 4]. Compared with other bacterial liver abscesses, KLA is more likely to cause thrombophlebitis involving the portal vein, hepatic vein, and IVC [5]. IVC thrombosis is extremely rare but potentially life-threatening, and early detection is critical to prevent catastrophic events such as pulmonary embolism [6–8]. Ultrasonography, due to its accessibility, non-invasiveness, and reproducibility, has become the preferred tool for initial diagnosis, guiding percutaneous drainage, and follow-up of liver abscesses [9]. We report a rare case of caudate lobe KLA complicated by IVC gas-containing thrombosis and highlight the pivotal role of multimodal ultrasound in its diagnosis and treatment.

Case report

The patient was a 66-year-old woman with a past medical history significant for type 2 diabetes mellitus and underlying biliary tract disease. She had no history of alcohol abuse, intravenous drug use, or smoking.

She presented with a 10-day history of fever and chills (maximum temperature 39.9℃). No obvious abdominal symptoms were reported at presentation. She was initially treated at a local hospital with empirical broad-spectrum antibiotic therapy (meropenem) for several days without improvement. However, detailed laboratory data, cultures prior to antibiotic initiation, and exact treatment duration were not available.

Abdominal CT performed at the referring hospital revealed a low-density lesion in the caudate lobe of the liver with internal gas. Additionally, gas was noted within the inferior vena cava, along with pneumobilia and gallbladder wall thickening. The patient was transferred to our hospital for further evaluation.

Upon admission to our hospital, physical examination revealed a lethargic mental status. Pulmonary auscultation demonstrated coarse breath sounds with pleural friction rub and bilateral moist rales. Cardiac and abdominal examinations were unremarkable. No clinical signs of jaundice were observed. Laboratory investigations demonstrated severe bacterial infection, including elevated levels of C-reactive protein, neutrophil alkaline phosphatase, and leukocytosis with neutrophilia. Arterial Blood gas analysis revealed type I respiratory failure, with concomitant hypokalemia and metabolic alkalosis. Liver function tests revealed a cholestatic pattern, characterized by elevated cholestatic enzymes and mildly increased bilirubin levels, while aminotransferases remained within normal ranges, with a slightly elevated AST/ALT ratio. Evidence of impaired hepatic synthetic function was also observed, as reflected by reduced serum albumin, total protein, and prealbumin levels. Her platelets, lactate, and tumor markers (including CA19-9, alpha-fetoprotein, and carcinoembryonic antigen) were within normal limits (Supplementary Table 1). Blood cultures obtained prior to antibiotic administration were negative.

Initially, the patient underwent abdominal ultrasonography (Fig. 1-a, b), revealing a 7.3 × 4.5 cm mixed echo in the caudate lobe of the liver, with the cystic space filled with moderate echo spots and gas-like hyperechoic areas. Subsequent plain chest CT showed multiple nodules and patchy shadows in both lungs, suggestive of infectious involvement. Plain and contrast-enhanced abdominal CT (Fig. 2) revealed a low-density lesion measuring approximately 4.9 × 6.2 cm in the caudate lobe of the liver, containing internal gas, consistent with a liver abscess. The lesion was closely adjacent to the IVC, with a slightly indistinct interface. In addition, filling defects with intraluminal gas density were observed within the IVC, suggesting septic thrombosis. Following contrast-enhanced CT, contrast-enhanced ultrasound (CEUS) was subsequently performed to further characterize the lesion and better assess its relationship with the IVC. CEUS demonstrated peripheral rim enhancement of the lesion in the arterial phase, with progressive washout in the portal venous phase and no enhancement in the central area. The lesion was noted to partially protrude into the IVC. These findings favored a diagnosis of liver abscess; however, a necrotic malignant hepatic tumor with superimposed infection could not be completely excluded (Fig. 1c, d).

Given the presence of septic thrombosis of the inferior vena cava and the associated risk of thrombus propagation and pulmonary embolism, the vascular surgery team recommended initiation of therapeutic anticoagulation with low-molecular-weight heparin (enoxaparin 6000 IU subcutaneously every 12 h). In addition, chest CT revealed multiple bilateral pulmonary nodules and patchy opacities consistent with septic pulmonary emboli originating from the liver abscess. Empirical antimicrobial therapy with meropenem plus cefoperazone-sulbactam was simultaneously initiated because of severe systemic infection. To confirm the diagnosis and treatment, ultrasound-guided percutaneous liver lesion biopsy and percutaneous catheter drainage were performed (Fig. 3). Approximately 40 ml of light yellow fluid was obtained during the initial drainage, after which a drainage catheter was placed for continuous drainage of the abscess cavity. Pathological examination of the biopsy specimen revealed extensive inflammatory cell infiltration with focal abscess formation and no evidence of malignancy. Culture of the puncture fluid showed Klebsiella pneumoniae subspecies and was positive for extended-spectrum enzyme β-lactamase (ESBL). Based on antimicrobial susceptibility testing, the initial antibiotic regimen was continued. With ongoing antimicrobial therapy and comprehensive supportive care(including supplemental oxygen via nasal cannula and correction of electrolyte disturbances), the patient showed significant clinical improvement.

However, on hospital day 11, the patient developed a high fever (38.8 °C) and hypotension (77/55 mmHg). Septic shock was suspected, likely secondary to repeated bacterial seeding from the IVC thrombus. Procalcitonin levels, which had initially decreased following drainage, showed a secondary increase, suggesting persistent infection. Systemic inflammatory response was evident, including elevated C-reactive protein and leukocytosis with neutrophilia, along with severe anemia, thrombocytopenia, and hypoalbuminemia (Supplementary Table 1). Type I respiratory failure was also present. The most recent abdominal CT prior to septic shock (on hospital day 10) showed appropriate positioning of the drainage catheter and a reduction in abscess size compared with earlier imaging. During the shock episode, the patient received prompt fluid resuscitation, oxygen therapy, blood transfusion for severe anemia, and correction of electrolyte disturbances. Based on clinical pharmacy consultation and antimicrobial susceptibility testing, the antibiotic regimen was escalated to meropenem plus tigecycline. With comprehensive supportive treatment, the patient gradually stabilized.

The patient was hospitalized for 23 days, during which intravenous antibiotic therapy was administered continuously, and anticoagulation therapy was given for 20 days. No clinically evident bleeding complications, including petechiae, ecchymosis, hematuria, or melena, were observed during hospitalization. During the course of anticoagulation therapy, platelet counts decreased from a pre-anticoagulation level of 181 × 10⁹/L to a nadir of 91 × 10⁹/L, but subsequently recovered. Given the presence of severe sepsis and septic shock, the etiology of thrombocytopenia was considered multifactorial, and no definitive diagnosis of heparin-induced thrombocytopenia was established. Follow-up contrast-enhanced CT prior to discharge demonstrated a reduction of the liver abscess to approximately 32 × 30 mm, with concomitant improvement of pulmonary lesions. At discharge, the patient was afebrile, hemodynamically stable, and showed significant improvement in laboratory parameters (Supplementary Table 1). After discharge, antibiotic therapy was continued, with a planned total treatment duration of 2–3 months.

Approximately 2.5 months after discharge, the follow-up showed that the patient was in good condition. Imaging showed near-complete resolution of the hepatic lesion and complete disappearance of the IVC thrombus(Fig. 4). No recurrence was reported during subsequent follow-up, although complete post-discharge treatment records were unavailable because subsequent care was provided at another institution. Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, the present case is the only reported case of a gas-containing thrombus within the IVC secondary to KLA. The causative pathogen was identified as an ESBL-producing strain of Klebsiella pneumoniae. The patient was an elderly female with diabetes mellitus, which is consistent with established high-risk factors for both KLA development and thrombotic complications [10–12]. In addition, underlying biliary tract disease was present at admission. A previous study has demonstrated that a history of biliary disease is an independent risk factor for ESBL-producing KLA, which may partly explain the clinical and microbiological characteristics observed in this case [13].

In recent decades, Klebsiella pneumoniae (K. pneumoniae) has emerged as the primary pathogen for community-acquired liver abscesses in many Asian countries, surpassing Gram-positive cocci [4, 10, 14]. According to previous reports, KLA predominantly involves the right hepatic lobe, with the left lobe being the second most common site. In contrast, caudate lobe involvement is exceedingly uncommon and has been described only in isolated case reports [15]. KLA’s high virulence, often associated with K1/K2 serotypes, hypermucoviscosity, and magA expression, contributes to severe local and systemic complications [4, 5, 10, 16]. Inferior vena cava involvement is extremely uncommon, but its occurrence generally reflects a life-threatening and advanced disease state [2, 5]. Previous reports by Yelamanchi et al. [6] and Nayak et al. [17] have documented cases of Klebsiella pneumoniae liver abscess complicated by IVC thrombosis; notably, in the case reported by Nayak et al., the thrombus extended into the right atrium. In the largest retrospective study available, Molton et al. reported regional thrombophlebitis in 53 of 169 patients with KLA; however, only one patient demonstrated direct IVC thrombosis, while three additional patients showed thrombus extension into the IVC. All four patients underwent abscess drainage and antibiotic therapy. Among the three patients with thrombus extension into the IVC, anticoagulation therapy was not administered, and follow-up imaging demonstrated spontaneous recanalization or improvement. The patient with direct IVC thrombosis received anticoagulation therapy, with subsequent thrombus resolution [10].

Many studies have explored the causes of venous thrombosis. However, the pathophysiology of vascular thrombosis is still uncertain [7]. On the one hand, Klebsiella infection can lead to endocarditis and vascular thrombosis. On the other hand, compression of the IVC by the abscess may also be one of the causes of thrombosis [7, 18–20].

Ultrasound and CT angiography are now the most useful diagnostic tools for the detection and characterization of venous extension [17, 21]. CT has a sensitivity of 95% and can detect abscesses as small as 0.5 cm [2]. CEUS has demonstrated substantial diagnostic value in the characterization of focal liver lesions, with reported sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy comparable to CT and MRI [22]. According to Gadour et al. CEUS should be the first choice method in the diagnosis of liver abscess, especially in subjects with contraindications to iodinated contrast agents, such as patients affected by chronic kidney disease (CKD), heart failure, and acute hemodynamically unstable patients [23].

Liver abscesses may exhibit highly variable imaging manifestations on conventional ultrasound and CEUS, particularly during the early or solid-appearing stage, when they can closely mimic necrotic malignant hepatic lesions such as hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma, and hepatic metastases. Therefore, differential diagnosis remains a major diagnostic challenge in clinical practice. On CEUS, most liver abscesses characteristically demonstrate irregular peripheral rim enhancement during the arterial phase, with non-enhancing central necrotic areas and occasionally enhancing internal septations. In addition, transient hyperenhancement of the surrounding liver parenchyma caused by inflammatory hyperemia may be observed. During the portal venous and late phases, liver abscesses usually appear hypoenhanced. However, necrotic malignant hepatic lesions may also exhibit arterial phase hyperenhancement followed by washout, resulting in imaging overlap with infectious lesions. Nevertheless, malignant lesions generally demonstrate more irregular tumoral vascular architecture, earlier and more pronounced washout, and usually lack the surrounding inflammatory hyperemia commonly seen in liver abscesses [24].

In addition to malignant lesions, several benign inflammatory or cystic hepatic lesions should also be considered in the differential diagnosis. Hepatic inflammatory pseudotumor lacks specific clinical and imaging features and is frequently misdiagnosed as malignant tumors, such as hepatocellular carcinoma or cholangiocarcinoma, or as inflammatory lesions, including liver abscesses [25]. Hepatic adenomas with hemorrhagic or degenerative changes may also present with overlapping imaging findings [26]. Simple hepatic cysts, in contrast, typically show no enhancement throughout the arterial, portal venous, and delayed phases on CEUS, which represents an important distinguishing feature from liver abscesses [27]. In late-stage or multiloculated abscesses with cystic appearances, careful evaluation of abscess wall enhancement, internal septations, and surrounding hepatic inflammatory changes may further assist in differentiation from simple cystic lesions.

The cornerstone of pyogenic liver abscess treatment remains adequate drainage combined with effective antimicrobial therapy. Percutaneous drainage under ultrasound- or CT-guidance, together with targeted antibiotic treatment, has become the first-line therapeutic strategy for most patients [28]. However, the optimal duration of anticoagulation for KLA-associated IVC thrombosis remains unclear, and treatment decisions should be individualized according to thrombus burden, infection status, and bleeding risk. In the present case, therapeutic-dose enoxaparin (6000 IU every 12 h) was initiated immediately after identification of the IVC thrombus on hospital day 3 and was continued throughout the 20-day hospitalization. Catheter-directed thrombolysis or thrombectomy was not pursued because of active, uncontrolled infection (inadequately drained abscess and ongoing bacteremia), high bleeding risk related to thrombocytopenia and coagulopathy, and the absence of massive pulmonary embolism or refractory shock.

At discharge, prolonged antimicrobial therapy (2–3 months) was planned due to the patient’s complicated clinical course, including septic shock, infection caused by an ESBL-producing organism, septic IVC thrombosis, and diabetes mellitus as an important host risk factor. In addition, follow-up CT before discharge demonstrated incomplete resolution of both the liver abscess and the IVC thrombus, suggesting persistent infection and a potential ongoing septic source. Currently, no established guidelines define the optimal duration of antibiotic therapy for septic IVC thrombosis secondary to Klebsiella pneumoniae liver abscess. Cui et al. [29] reported that intravenous antibiotic therapy for pyogenic liver abscess generally ranges from 2 to 6 weeks, depending on clinical response; however, prolonged treatment may be necessary in complicated cases such as ours. Therefore, extended antimicrobial therapy with continued radiologic and clinical follow-up was considered necessary to reduce the risk of relapse and ensure adequate infection control.

Following prompt drainage, targeted antimicrobial therapy, supportive care, and therapeutic anticoagulation, the patient gradually improved. Notably, follow-up imaging performed approximately two and a half months after discharge demonstrated complete resolution of the IVC thrombus, highlighting the potential effectiveness of early multidisciplinary conservative management in selected patients.

In this case, multimodal ultrasound played a key role throughout the diagnostic and therapeutic process. Conventional two-dimensional ultrasound provided an initial assessment of the lesion, facilitating prompt clinical decision-making. CEUS served as a problem-solving adjunct to contrast-enhanced CT, further characterizing the lesion, clarifying its relationship with the IVC, and assisting in pre-procedural planning of a safe puncture pathway. Although blood cultures obtained after admission prior to antibiotic administration were negative—likely due to prior antibiotic exposure—ultrasound-guided percutaneous drainage enabled microbiological confirmation of the pathogen and effective source control, significantly accelerated the healing process of the primary lesion. Ultimately, the combination of percutaneous drainage and targeted antimicrobial therapy led to a favorable clinical outcome.

Conclusions

This case highlights the critical role of multimodal ultrasound in the management of caudate lobe KLA complicated by IVC thrombosis. Early vascular evaluation may facilitate timely recognition of septic thrombotic complications and assist therapeutic decision-making. In the present case, a multidisciplinary conservative approach consisting of targeted antimicrobial therapy, percutaneous drainage, supportive care, and anticoagulation resulted in favorable clinical and radiologic outcomes without the need for open surgical intervention.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, as a single case report, the findings may not be generalizable. Second, complete microbiological characterization of the isolate, including hypervirulence-associated serotyping or genotyping, was unavailable; therefore, the potential relationship between hypervirulent Klebsiella pneumoniae strains and septic IVC thrombosis could not be fully assessed. In addition, some clinical data from the referring hospital and post-discharge follow-up records from the outside institution were incomplete. Nevertheless, the diagnosis was supported by consistent imaging, pathological, and microbiological findings, and favorable follow-up imaging further supported the effectiveness of the multidisciplinary treatment strategy.
