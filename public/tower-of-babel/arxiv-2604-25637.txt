Abstract
. We consider a Ziegler pair of plane arrangements, that is two plane arrangements 𝒜 : f = 0 \mathcal{A}:f=0 and 𝒜 ′ : f ′ = 0 \mathcal{A}^{\prime}:f^{\prime}=0 in the projective space ℙ 3 \mathbb{P}^{3} , such that the intersection lattices L ⁡ ( 𝒜 ) L(\mathcal{A}) and L ⁡ ( 𝒜 ′ ) L(\mathcal{A}^{\prime}) are isomorphic, but the Betti numbers of the minimal resolutions of their Jacobian algebras are not the same. We introduce several properties for such pairs and relate them to cones over Ziegler pairs of line arrangements in ℙ 2 \mathbb{P}^{2} .



1.
Introduction

Let
S=\mathbb{C}[x,y,z,w]
be the polynomial ring in four variables
x,y,z,w
with complex coefficients, and let
X:f=0
be a reduced surface of degree
d\geq 3
in the complex projective space
\mathbb{P}^{3}
.
We denote by
J_{f}
the Jacobian ideal of
f
, i.e. the homogeneous ideal in
S
spanned by the partial derivatives
f_{x},f_{y},f_{z},f_{w}
of
f
, and by
M(f)=S/J_{f}
the corresponding graded quotient ring, called the Jacobian (or Milnor) algebra of
f
.
Consider the general form of the minimal resolution of the Milnor algebra
M(f)
of a reduced surface
X:f=0
, namely

(1.1)

0\longrightarrow\bigoplus_{k=1}^{r}S(1-d-b_{k})\longrightarrow\bigoplus_{j=1}^{q}S(1-d-c_{j})\longrightarrow\bigoplus_{i=1}^{p}S(1-d-d_{i})\longrightarrow S^{4}(1-d)\longrightarrow S,

where
p\geq 3
,
q\geq 0
and
r\geq 0
. Equivalently, the minimal resolution of the graded
S
-module
D_{0}(f)
of derivations killing the polynomial
f
, that is

D_{0}(f)=\{\delta=\alpha^{x}\partial_{x}+\alpha^{y}\partial_{y}+\alpha^{z}\partial_{z}+\alpha^{w}\partial_{w}\ |\ \delta(f)=0\}

with
\alpha^{x},\alpha^{y},\alpha^{z},\alpha^{w}\in S
is given by

(1.2)

0\longrightarrow\bigoplus_{k=1}^{r}S(-b_{k})\longrightarrow\bigoplus_{j=1}^{q}S(-c_{j})\longrightarrow\bigoplus_{i=1}^{p}S(-d_{i})\longrightarrow D_{0}(f).

We call the increasing sequences of degrees

{\bf d}=(d_{1},\ldots,d_{p}),\ {\bf c}=(c_{1},\ldots,c_{q})\text{ and }{\bf b}=(b_{1},\ldots,b_{r})

the
graded Betti numbers of the Jacobian algebra

M(f)
, since they determine and are determined by the usual
graded Betti numbers of the Jacobian algebra
M(f)
as defined for instance in
[
15
]
.

It is known that there is a unique polynomial
P(M(f))(u)\in\mathbb{Q}[u]
, called the
Hilbert polynomial
of
M(f)
, and an integer
k_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that

(1.3)

H(M(f))(k)=P(M(f))(k)

for all
k\geq k_{0}
. We denote by
\Sigma
the singular subscheme of
X
, which is defined by the Jacobian ideal
J(f)
. The general theory of Hilbert polynomials says that the degree of
P(M(f))
is given by the dimension of the support of
\mathcal{O}_{\Sigma}
, the coherent sheaf associated to the graded
S
-module
M(f)
. Hence the assumption
\dim\Sigma=0
implies that the polynomial

P(M(f))
is a constant, namely the total Tjurina number of
X
, given by

(1.4)

P(M(f))=\tau(X)=\sum_{s\in\Sigma}\tau(X,s),

where
\tau(X,s)
denotes the Tjurina number of the isolated singularity
(X,s)
, and

\dim\Sigma=1
implies that

(1.5)

P(M(f))(u)=au+b,

where
a=\deg(\Sigma)
, the degree of the subscheme
\Sigma
.
There is a lot of interest in understanding the relations between these numerical invariants coming from the resolution of
M(f)
and the combinatorics of
X
, when
X
is a plane arrangement in
\mathbb{P}^{3}
.
In fact, we introduce in this paper the following properties, which may be stated for any pair of hyperplane arrangements.

Definition 1.1
.

Two hyperplane arrangements
\mathcal{A}:f=0
and
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}:f^{\prime}=0
in
\mathbb{P}^{n}
are said to form a Ziegler pair if they have isomorphic intersection lattices
L(\mathcal{A})
and
L(\mathcal{A}^{\prime})
, obtained from the corresponding central arrangements in
\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
, and distinct sets of graded Betti numbers coming from the minimal resolutions of their Jacobian algebras
M(f)
and
M(f^{\prime})
.

A Ziegler pair
\mathcal{A}:f=0
and
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}:f^{\prime}=0
may have one or several of the following additional properties.

(1)

We say that the Ziegler pair
\mathcal{A}:f=0
and
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}:f^{\prime}=0
satisfies the condition
{\rm(HP)}
if the Jacobian algebras
M(f)
and
M(f^{\prime})
have the same Hilbert polynomial, that is

\dim M(f)_{k}=\dim M(f^{\prime})_{k}\text{ for any integer }k\geq k_{0}

for some
k_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}
.

(2)

We say that the Ziegler pair
\mathcal{A}:f=0
and
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}:f^{\prime}=0
satisfies the condition
{\rm(HF)}
if the Jacobian algebras
M(f)
and
M(f^{\prime})
have the same Hilbert function, that is

\dim M(f)_{k}=\dim M(f^{\prime})_{k}\text{ for any integer }k.

(3)

We say that the Ziegler pair
\mathcal{A}:f=0
and
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}:f^{\prime}=0
satisfies condition
{\rm(MDR)}
if the Jacobian algebras
M(f)
and
M(f^{\prime})
have the same minimal degree of a first Jacobian syzygy. More precisely, using the notation from (
1.2
), we set

{\rm mdr}(f)=d_{1}=\min_{i}\{d_{i}\}.

Then the condition
{\rm(MDR)}
is equivalent to

{\rm mdr}(f)={\rm mdr}(f^{\prime}).

(4)

We say that the Ziegler pair
\mathcal{A}:f=0
and
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}:f^{\prime}=0
satisfies condition
({\rm SPEC}_{0})
if the arrangement
\mathcal{A}
is a specialization of the arrangement
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
. More precisely, this means that there exists an interval
I=[0,t_{0}]\subset\mathbb{R}
and a smooth family of homogeneous polynomials
f_{t}
, for
t\in I
, such that

f_{0}=f,\qquad f_{t_{0}}=f^{\prime},

and the corresponding family of hyperplane arrangements
\mathcal{A}_{t}:f_{t}=0
has constant intersection lattice, i.e.

L(\mathcal{A}_{t})\cong L(\mathcal{A}_{t^{\prime}})\quad\text{for all }t,t^{\prime}\in I.

If, in addition, the graded Betti numbers of the minimal resolutions of the Jacobian algebras
M(f_{t})
are constant for all
0<t\leq t_{0}
, then we say that the Ziegler pair
\mathcal{A}:f=0
and
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}:f^{\prime}=0
satisfies condition
({\rm SPEC})
.

Examples of Ziegler pairs satisfying or not satisfying some of these conditions will be given below. Note that for line arrangements in

\mathbb{P}^{2}
, a Ziegler pair always satisfies the condition
{\rm(HP)}
, since in this case we have
P(M(f))=\tau(\mathcal{A})
and
\tau(\mathcal{A})
is determined by the intersection lattice
L(\mathcal{A})
.

It came as a surprise that for a plane arrangement
\mathcal{A}:f=0
in
\mathbb{P}^{3}

the Hilbert polynomial is not determined by the intersection lattice
L(\mathcal{A})
of
\mathcal{A}
. Indeed, the Ziegler pair of plane arrangements

\mathcal{A}:f=xyzw(x+y+z)(2x+y+z)(2x+3y+z)(2x+3y+4z)(3x+5z)(3x+4y+5z)=0

and

\mathcal{A}^{\prime}:f^{\prime}=xyzw(x+y+z)(2x+y+z)(2x+3y+z)(2x+3y+4z)(x+3z)(x+2y+3z)=0

have

P(M(f))(u)=51u-223\text{ and }P(M(f^{\prime}))(u)=51u-222,

see
[
16
, Example 4.6]
.
We introduce the following definition, which is widely used in hyperplane arrangement theory, see
[
8
,
18
]
.

Definition 1.2
.

Let
R=\mathbb{C}[x,y,z]
and consider a reduced curve
C:g=0
in
\mathbb{P}^{2}
, where
g\in R
is a homogeneous polynomial of degree
d-1
.
The surface
S=cC:f=0
, where
f=wg
, is called the cone over the curve
C
.

This terminology, which seems strange at first, is perhaps motivated by the fact that the complement

U(f)=\mathbb{P}^{3}\setminus cC
is exactly the complement in
\mathbb{C}^{3}
of the usual cone over
C
given in
\mathbb{C}^{3}
by the equation
g=0
.

Note that the above plane arrangements are in fact cones over a Ziegler pair of line arrangements, namely
\mathcal{A}=c\mathcal{B}
and
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}=c\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
, where

(1.6)

\mathcal{B}:g=xyz(x+y+z)(2x+y+z)(2x+3y+z)(2x+3y+4z)(3x+5z)(3x+4y+5z)=0

and

(1.7)

\mathcal{B}^{\prime}:g^{\prime}=xyz(x+y+z)(2x+y+z)(2x+3y+z)(2x+3y+4z)(x+3z)(x+2y+3z)=0.

More information on this Ziegler pair is given in Example
3.1
, where we show that it satisfies the condition
{\rm(SPEC)}
and does not satisfy the condition
({\rm HF})
.
The first result of this note says that we can use any such Ziegler pair of line arrangements to produce similar examples.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
\mathcal{B}
and
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
be a pair of line arrangements in
\mathbb{P}^{2}
.

(1)

If
\mathcal{B}:g=0
and
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}:g^{\prime}=0
have isomorphic intersection lattices, then the cones
\mathcal{A}=c\mathcal{B}:f=wg=0
and
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}=c\mathcal{B}^{\prime}:f^{\prime}=wg^{\prime}=0
have also isomorphic intersection lattices.

(2)

If
\mathcal{B}:g=0
and
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}:g^{\prime}=0
is a Ziegler pair satisfying the condition
{\rm(SPEC)}
and not satisfying the condition
({\rm HF})
, then

P(M(f))\neq P(M(f^{\prime})).

Remark 1.4
.

It is clear that the claim in Theorem
1.3
(1) is valid, with essentially the same proof, for a pair
(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{B}^{\prime})
of hyperplane arrangements in
\mathbb{P}^{n}
. In particular, if
(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{B}^{\prime})
is a pair of line arrangements in
\mathbb{P}^{2}
with isomorphic intersection lattices, and we take the
k
-fold cones, that is if we set

\mathcal{A}_{k}:w_{1}\cdots w_{k}g=0
and
\mathcal{A}_{k}^{\prime}:w_{1}\cdots w_{k}g^{\prime}=0
, where
w_{1},\ldots,w_{k}
are new variables, then the hyperplane arrangements
\mathcal{A}_{k}
and
\mathcal{A}_{k}^{\prime}
in
\mathbb{P}^{k+2}
have isomorphic intersection lattices.

The following result shows the big difference with the curve case, where
the Hilbert polynomial
P(M(g))
is determined by the total Tjurina number of
C
, which for a line arrangement
\mathcal{B}:g=0
is determined by the topology of the complement

U(g)=\mathbb{P}^{2}\setminus\mathcal{B}
.

Corollary 1.5
.

For plane arrangements
\mathcal{A}:f=0
in
\mathbb{P}^{3}
, the Hilbert polynomial
P(M(f))
is determined neither by the topology of the complement
U(f)=\mathbb{P}^{3}\setminus\mathcal{A}
, nor by the topology of the corresponding Milnor fibration

\mathbb{C}^{4}\setminus\{(x,y,z,w)\in\mathbb{C}^{4}\ :\ f(x,y,z,w)=0\}\to\mathbb{C}^{*},\ (x,y,z,w)\mapsto f(x,y,z,w).

The second main result shows that the surfaces which are cones over curves enjoy nice properties in relation with tameness, a subtle property introduced and studied in
[
7
,
12
]
.

Theorem 1.6
.

Let
C:g=0
be a reduced curve in
\mathbb{P}^{2}
of degree
d-1
with
{\rm mdr}(g)>0
. Consider the cone
S=cC:f=wg=0
in
\mathbb{P}^{3}
.

(1)

If
r_{j}=(a_{j},b_{j},c_{j})
for
j=1,\ldots,m
is a minimal set of generators for the first syzygy
R
-module
D_{0}(g)
, then

\rho_{0}=(x,y,z,-(d-1)w)

and

\rho_{j}=(a_{j},b_{j},c_{j},0)

for
j=1,\ldots,m
is a minimal set of generators for the first syzygy
S
-module
D_{0}(f)
. In particular, one has
{\rm mdr}(f)=1
. Moreover, if the minimal resolution for
D_{0}(g)
is given by (
3.1
),
then the minimal resolution for
D_{0}(f)
has the form

(1.8)

0\longrightarrow\bigoplus_{j=1}^{q}S(-c_{j})\longrightarrow S(-1)\oplus\left(\bigoplus_{i=1}^{p}S(-d_{i})\right)\longrightarrow D_{0}(f).

(2)

The surface
S=cC:f=wg=0
is tame with respect to the pair
(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})
.

(3)

The surface
S
is free (resp. nearly free, resp. strictly plus-one generated with respect to
\overline{\rho}_{S}=(\rho_{0},\rho_{1},\rho_{2},\rho_{3})
) if and only if the curve
C
is free (resp. nearly free, resp. plus-one generated with respect to
\overline{\rho}_{C}=(\rho_{1},\rho_{2},\rho_{3})
).

Remark 1.7
.

It is clear that the claim in Theorem
1.6
(1) is valid, with essentially the same proof, for a hypersurface in
\mathbb{P}^{n}
. In particular, if
C:g=0
is a curve in
\mathbb{P}^{2}
, and we take the
k
-fold cones, that is if we set

D:f_{k}=w_{1}\cdots w_{k}g=0
, where
w_{1},\ldots,w_{k}
are new variables, then
D_{0}(f_{k})
is generated by
\rho_{j}
for
j=1,\ldots,m
and
k
additional syzygies, namely

\theta_{i}=x\partial_{x}+y\partial_{y}+z\partial_{z}-dw_{i}\partial_{w_{i}}

for
i=1,\ldots,k
, where
\deg g=d
.

One may ask if plane arrangements which are not cones over line arrangements behave nicer with respect to Hilbert polynomials. Example
4.1
in Section 4 shows that this is not the case. To better understand this example, we study a related moduli space in Proposition
4.2
.

Finally in Section 5 we construct several examples of Ziegler pairs of plane arrangements in
\mathbb{P}^{3}
which are not cones, but satisfy the condition
({\rm HF})
in Definition
1.1
.

2.
Proof of the main results

2.1.
Proof of Theorem
1.3

To prove (1), note that a family of lines
L_{1},\ldots,L_{s}
in
\mathcal{B}
intersect in a point in
\mathbb{P}^{2}
if and only if the corresponding planes in
\mathcal{A}
,
that is those defined by the same equations, intersect in a line
L
in
\mathbb{P}^{3}
. Since any line
L
in
\mathbb{P}^{3}
intersects the plane
P:w=0
, it follows that the lattice
L(\mathcal{B})
determines the lattice
L(\mathcal{A})
.

To prove the claim (2), we use the following equality, see
[
9
, Proposition 3.13]
,

P(M(f))(u)=au+b,

where

a=\tau(\mathcal{B})+d-1

where
d-1
is the number of lines in
\mathcal{B}
, and

b=\sum_{j=0}^{st(g)-1}H(M(g))(j)-st(g)\tau(\mathcal{B})-\frac{(d-4)(d-1)}{2}.

Here
H(M(g))(j)=\dim M(g)_{j}
is the Hilbert function of the Jacobian algebra
M(g)
and

st(g)=\min\{j_{0}\ :\ H(M(g))(j)=\tau(\mathcal{B})\text{ for any }j\geq j_{0}\}

is the stabilization threshold of
g
. It is clear from the above formulas that in the equality for
b
we may replace
st(g)
by any larger integer, namely by
T=3(d-2)+1
.
Since
(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{B}^{\prime})
satisfies the condition
{\rm(SPEC)}
, we get the inequality by the semicontinuity of the Hilbert functions

\sum_{j=0}^{T-1}H(M(g))(j)\geq\sum_{j=0}^{T-1}H(M(g^{\prime}))(j).

Since
(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{B}^{\prime})
does not satisfy the condition
({\rm HF})
, it follows that this inequality is strict and hence

P(M(f))(0)=b>b^{\prime}=P(M(f^{\prime}))(0).

2.2.
Proof of Theorem
1.6

Let
\rho^{\prime}=a^{\prime}\partial_{x}+b^{\prime}\partial_{y}+c^{\prime}\partial_{z}+d^{\prime}\partial_{w}
be a derivation in
D_{0}(f)
. Then we get

w(a^{\prime}g_{x}+b^{\prime}g_{y}+c^{\prime}g_{z})+d^{\prime}g=0.

If
d^{\prime}=0
, then
\rho^{\prime}
is in the
S
-submodule
D^{\prime}
generated in
D_{0}(f)
by the derivations
\rho_{j}
’s for
j=1,\ldots,m
. If
d^{\prime}\neq 0
, then
d^{\prime}=wd_{1}
for some
d_{1}\in S
, and hence

\rho^{\prime}+\frac{d_{1}}{d-1}\rho_{0}\in D^{\prime}.

Hence
\rho_{j}
for
j=0,\ldots,m
minimally generate
D_{0}(f)
, and clearly all secondary syzygies involving the
\rho_{j}
come from the secondary syzygies of
g
. This proves the first claim (1).

To prove (2), consider the
2\times 4
matrix
M(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})
having as first row the components of
\rho_{0}
and as second row the components of
\rho_{1}
. Let
I_{2}
be the ideal in
S
generated by the 2-minors of
M(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})
. Note that

wa_{1},wb_{1}
and
wc_{1}
are in
I_{2}
. If
w\neq 0
, then the equations

a_{1}=b_{1}=c_{1}=0

which defines a one-dimensional subspace in
\mathbb{P}^{3}
.
Indeed,
a_{1}=b_{1}=c_{1}=0
define a 0-dimensional subspace in
\mathbb{P}^{2}
,
since
a_{1},b_{1},c_{1}
have no common factor in
R
, the syzygy
\rho_{1}
being primitive.
If
w=0
, then note that the matrix
M^{\prime}=M(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})(x,y,z,0)

has rank 2 over the fraction field of
R
, since a relation

aE+b\rho_{1}=0

with
a,b\in R
not both 0 and
E=(x,y,z)
is impossible.
It follows that the 2-minors in
M^{\prime}
define subset in
\mathbb{P}^{2}
of dimension at most 1. These two cases show that the ideal
I_{2}
defines a subset of codimension at least 2 in
\mathbb{C}^{4}
, and the claim (2) follows using
[
7
, Lemma 2.3 (ii)]
or
[
12
, Theorem 3.3 and Remark 3.4]
.

To prove the claim in (3) about the free surface
S
, we apply Saito’s Freeness Criterion, see
[
8
, Theorem 8.1]
. This claim is then clear, since the corresponding two determinants
\phi(g)
and
\phi(f)

are related by the formula

\phi(f)=-w\phi(g)

when we use generators for
D_{0}(f)
and
D_{0}(g)
as in the claim (1).
An alternative proof can be obtained using
[
12
, Theorem 4.1 (4)]
.
The claims about nearly free or strictly plus-one generated surfaces with respect to

\overline{\rho}=(\rho_{0},\rho_{1},\rho_{2},\rho_{3})

follow from

[
12
, Definition 1.1]
and the fact that the second order syzygies of
g
and
f
coincide. For the definition of (strictly) plus-one generated hyperplane arrangements we refer to
[
1
]
, where this notion was introduced. We recall that a plane curve is strictly plus-one generated if and only if it is plus-one generated, see
[
1
,
10
]
.

3.
Some examples of cones and the proof of Corollary

Example 3.1
.

It is straightforward to verify that the Ziegler pair
(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{B}^{\prime})
from (
1.6
) and (
1.7
) satisfies neither condition
({\rm HF})
nor condition
{\rm(MDR)}
. Indeed, if the minimal graded resolution of the
R
-module
D_{0}(g)
is given by

(3.1)

0\longrightarrow\longrightarrow\bigoplus_{j=1}^{q}R(-c_{j})\longrightarrow\bigoplus_{i=1}^{p}R(-d_{i})\longrightarrow D_{0}(g),

then we call the increasing sequences of degrees

{\bf d}=(d_{1},\ldots,d_{p})\text{ and }{\bf c}=(c_{1},\ldots,c_{q})

the
graded Betti numbers of the Jacobian algebra

M(g)
. Note that there is a small shift in notation when compared to
[
13
]
.
Then, a direct computation shows that the graded Betti numbers of
g
are

{\bf d}=(6_{6})\text{ and }{\bf c}=(7_{4})

with the obvious notations as explained in
[
14
]
,
while the graded Betti numbers of
M(g^{\prime})
are

{\bf d}=(5,6_{3})\text{ and }{\bf c}=(7,8).

Geometrically,
\mathcal{B}
and
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
are constructed from a hexagon
\mathcal{H}
, by adding the 3 main diagonals. The 6 vertices of the hexagon are situated on a conic for
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
, but not for
\mathcal{B}
, see
[
11
]
for more details on this construction. This
Ziegler pair
(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{B}^{\prime})
satisfies the condition
{\rm(SPEC)}
. Indeed, it follows from
[
11
, Theorem 1.6 and Theorem 1.8]
that the family

f_{t}=xyz(x+y+z)(2x+y+z)(2x+3y+z)(2x+3y+4z)

((3-t)x+(5-t)z)((3-t)x+(4-t)y+(5-t)z)

for
t\in[0,2]
satisfies all the properties from Definition
1.1
(4).

By writing the Hilbert function as a Hilbert series, one has the following equalities

HF(g)=1+3t+6t^{2}+10t^{3}+15t^{4}+21t^{5}+28t^{6}+36t^{7}+42t^{8}+46t^{9}+48t^{10}+

+48t^{11}+46t^{12}+42t^{13}+\ldots

and

HF(g^{\prime})=1+3t+6t^{2}+10t^{3}+15t^{4}+21t^{5}+28t^{6}+36t^{7}+42t^{8}+46t^{9}+48t^{10}+

+48t^{11}+46t^{12}+43t^{13}+42t^{14}+\ldots

If we write
P(M(f))(u)=au+b
and
P(M(f^{\prime}))(u)=a^{\prime}u+b^{\prime}
, we have

d=10
,
st(g)=13
and
st(g^{\prime})=14
. The above formulas yield

a=a^{\prime}=42+(10-1)=51

and

b=223\text{ and }b^{\prime}=222.

3.2.
Proof of Corollary
1.5

To prove this claim, it is enough to recall that two central hyperplane arrangements that are lattice-isotopic have equivalent Milnor fibrations and complements, see
[
8
, Theorem 5.1]
for the precise statement and
[
19
]
for the proof of this deep result. Our discussion above in Example
3.1
shows that
f
and
f^{\prime}
define lattice-isotopic central arrangements in
\mathbb{C}^{4}
, and hence all the topological invariants of the corresponding Milnor fibrations or of the complements
U(f)
and
U(f^{\prime})
are the same.

Example 3.3
.

Using the graded Betti numbers of the Ziegler pair of line arrangements

\mathcal{B}:g=0
and
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}:g^{\prime}=0
from Example
3.1
and Theorem
1.6
(1), we get the graded Betti numbers of
M(f)
where
f=wg
as follows

{\bf d}=(1,6_{6})\text{ and }{\bf c}=(7_{4}),

while the graded Betti numbers of
M(f^{\prime})
, where
f^{\prime}=wg^{\prime}
, are

{\bf d}=(1,5,6_{3})\text{ and }{\bf c}=(7,8).

Let us consider the following hyperplane arrangements in
\mathbb{P}^{4}
:

\mathcal{D}:g=xyzw_{1}w_{2}(x+y+z)(2x+y+z)(2x+3y+z)(2x+3y+4z)(3x+5z)(3x+4y+5z)=0,

and

\mathcal{D}^{\prime}:g^{\prime}=xyzw_{1}w_{2}(x+y+z)(2x+y+z)(2x+3y+z)(2x+3y+4z)(x+3z)(x+2y+3z)=0,

which are double cones over the line arrangements in Example
3.1
.
In light of Remark
1.7
, we can anticipate the structure of the modules
D_{0}(g)
and
D_{0}(g^{\prime})
. In both cases, besides the derivations inherited from the corresponding arrangements in the
(x,y,z)
-variables, there are two additional derivations given by

\theta_{i}=x\partial_{x}+y\partial_{y}+z\partial_{z}-9\,w_{i}\partial_{w_{i}},\qquad i\in\{1,2\}.

Thus, the original derivations persist, and the extension to
\mathbb{P}^{4}
contributes these two extra logarithmic derivations corresponding to the variables
w_{1}
and
w_{2}
.

4.
Some generic sections of double cones

We start with an example of a Ziegler pair not involving cones and still failing property
({\rm HP})
.

Example 4.1
.

For

\mathcal{A}:f=(w+x-3y+5z)wxy(x-y-z)(x-y+z)(2x+y-2z)(x+3y-3z)

(3x+2y+3z)(x+5y+5z)(7x-4y-z)=0

we get that the Hilbert polynomial
61u-307
,
while for

\mathcal{A}^{\prime}:f^{\prime}=(w+x-3y+5z)wxy(4x-5y-5z)(x-y+z)(16x+13y-20z)

(x+3y-3z)(3x+2y+3z)(x+5y+5z)(7x-4y-z)=0

we get the Hilbert polynomial
61u-308
.
It is not difficult to show that
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
are irreducible, hence not cones, and have isomorphic intersection lattices. In fact, the graded Betti numbers of
M(f)
as defined above are

{\bf d}=(2,6_{2},7_{7}),\ {\bf c}=(7,8_{7},9_{2})\text{ and }{\bf b}=(9_{2},10),

while the
the graded Betti numbers of
M(f^{\prime})
are

{\bf d}=(2,7_{13}),\ {\bf c}=(8_{16})\text{ and }{\bf b}=(9_{5}).

Hence
{\rm mdr}(f)={\rm mdr}(f^{\prime})>1
, which shows that
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
are not cones using Theorem
1.6
(1).

Geometrically, this example is obtained as follows. We start with a pair of Ziegler line arrangements and apply twice the coning construction. In this way we obtain a pair of hyperplane arrangements
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
in
\mathbb{P}^{4}
, having isomorphic intersection lattices, as explained in Remark
1.4
. Then
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
are obtained by taking a generic hyperplane section of
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
,
and this construction preserves the isomorphism of intersection lattices.

Since the Ziegler pair
(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{A}^{\prime})
in Example
4.1
has an interesting geometry, we now determine the moduli space of arrangements that share the same intersection poset
L(\mathcal{A})
.

In order to do so we need a short preparation.
Let
M=(E,\mathcal{B}(M))
be a rank
n
matroid on a ground set
E
with
|E|=d
, realizable over a field
\mathbb{F}
. Recall that a realization of
M
is an arrangement of hyperplanes

\mathcal{A}=\{H_{1},\dots,H_{d}\}\subset\mathbb{P}^{n-1}_{\mathbb{F}}

such that

\mathcal{B}(M)=\left\{\lambda\subseteq E:|\lambda|=n,\ \bigcap_{i\in\lambda}H_{i}=\emptyset\right\}.

Choosing homogeneous coordinates, we represent
\mathcal{A}
by a family
\mathcal{V}
of vectors
v_{i}\in\mathbb{P}^{n-1}_{\mathbb{F}}
for
i\in E
, where
v_{i}
defines the hyperplane
H_{i}
. Then
\mathcal{V}
realizes
M
if and only if for every
\lambda\subseteq E
with
|\lambda|=n
,

\det(A_{\lambda})\neq 0\iff\lambda\in\mathcal{B}(M),

where
A_{\lambda}
denotes the corresponding
n\times n
matrix obtained by using as columns some representatives
v_{i}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{F}^{n}
of the vectors
v_{i}
for
i\in\lambda
.

Thus, the set of realizations is given by

\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}(M;\mathbb{F})\subset(\mathbb{P}^{n-1}_{\mathbb{F}})^{d},

a Zariski locally closed subset defined by determinantal equalities and non-vanishing conditions.
Two realizations differing by a projective change of coordinates define the same arrangement. This corresponds to the natural diagonal action of
\mathrm{PGL}(n,\mathbb{F})
on
\mathbb{P}=(\mathbb{P}^{n-1}_{\mathbb{F}})^{d},
which induces a natural action on

\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}(M;\mathbb{F})
. The
realization space
of
M
is then defined as the quotient

\mathcal{R}(M;\mathbb{F}):=\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}(M;\mathbb{F})/\mathrm{PGL}(n,\mathbb{F}).

The space
\mathcal{R}(M;\mathbb{F})
is a moduli space parametrizing all realizations of
M
up to projective equivalence. In particular, it can be viewed using GIT as a quasi-affine variety, that is an open subset of an affine variety, when the field
\mathbb{F}
is algebraically closed of characteristic
0
.
Indeed, all the equations
\det(A_{\lambda})=0
for
\lambda\notin\mathcal{B}(M)
, define a closed subvariety
Z
in
\mathbb{P}
. One can consider only essential arrangements, and then the set
\mathcal{B}(M)
is non-empty. If we choose one element

\lambda_{0}\in\mathcal{B}(M)
, then the condition
\det(A_{\lambda_{0}})\neq 0
defines an open subset in
\mathbb{P}
which is an affine variety
U
. It follows that
Y=Z\cap U
is an affine variety and

\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}(M;\mathbb{F})
is clearly an open subset of
Y
.

5.
The Elliptic Matroids
T_{n}

Let
n\geq 3
. We define a rank-
3
matroid
T_{n}
on the ground set

E:=\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}

by declaring a triple
\{i,j,k\}\subset E
to be dependent if and only if

i+j+k\equiv 0\pmod{n}.

All other triples are bases.

This defines a matroid. Non-emptiness and uniform rank are immediate. For the exchange axiom, let
A=\{a,x,y\}
and
B
be bases with
a\in A\setminus B
. A set
\{x,y,b\}
fails to be a basis if and only if
x+y+b\equiv 0
, which determines at most one
b\in E
. Since
B\setminus A\neq\varnothing
, one can choose
b\in B\setminus A
avoiding this value, and
(A\setminus\{a\})\cup\{b\}
is a basis.

The construction is invariant under affine transformations of the form

i\longmapsto ui+a\,\text{ such that }\,u\in(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})^{*},\ a\in\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z},

so
\mathrm{Aut}(T_{n})
contains
\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}\rtimes(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})^{*}
.

A realization of
T_{n}
over
\mathbb{C}
is a family of points

(p_{i})_{i\in E}\subset\mathbb{P}^{2}

such that

p_{i},p_{j},p_{k}\text{ are collinear }\iff i+j+k\equiv 0\pmod{n}.

Equivalently, writing
p_{i}
as homogeneous column vectors, the realization space is cut out by the conditions

\det(p_{i},p_{j},p_{k})=0\quad\text{for all }i+j+k\equiv 0\pmod{n},

together with the non-vanishing of the remaining
3\times 3
minors.

Assume
n\geq 7
. Then there exist four elements of
E
with no dependent triple; fixing such a set (e.g.
\{0,1,2,3\}
), any realization is projectively equivalent to one satisfying

p_{0}=[1\!:\!0\!:\!0],\quad p_{1}=[0\!:\!1\!:\!0],\quad p_{2}=[0\!:\!0\!:\!1],\quad p_{3}=[1\!:\!1\!:\!1].

The remaining points are then determined by the relations
\det(p_{i},p_{j},p_{k})=0
for
i+j+k\equiv 0\pmod{n}
, which allow one to compute each
p_{k}
as the intersection of two lines defined by previously constructed points.

These incidence relations impose a global algebraic constraint. Concretely, nine consecutive points determine a unique cubic curve
E\subset\mathbb{P}^{2}
, and the recursive construction forces all points
p_{i}
to lie on this same cubic. In particular, any realization of
T_{n}
(for
n\geq 7
) is contained in a plane cubic, possibly singular. It turns out that the realization space for
\mathcal{R}(T_{n},\mathbb{C})
with
n\geq 10
is isomorphic to the modular curve
X_{1}(n)
– for details please consult
[
3
]
.

We now briefly discuss the geometry of realizations, i.e. line arrangements realizing the matroids
T_{n}
. These configurations are closely related to classical constructions arising from the orchard problem. In particular, they can be traced back to the work of Burr, Grünbaum, and Sloane
[
4
]
, where geometric properties of plane cubic curves were used to construct extremal configurations of points and lines with many triple intersections.

In the case of
T_{n}
, the defining condition

i+j+k\equiv 0\pmod{n}

has a direct geometric interpretation: the corresponding lines
\ell_{i},\ell_{j},\ell_{k}
intersect in a common point. Thus, this condition encodes precisely the triple intersection structure of the arrangement.

From a numerical perspective, this yields an arrangement with exactly

n_{3}=1+\left\lfloor\frac{n(n-3)}{6}\right\rfloor

triple points
[
17
, A058212]
, while all remaining intersection points are double points.

Having established this description, we now turn to the specific case of
n=10
, which was previously studied in
[
6
]
.

Matroid
T_{10}
.

Consider the family of line arrangements defined by

\displaystyle Q_{t}(x,y,z)=

\displaystyle xyz(x+y+z)(y+z)((t-2)x+(t^{2}-t-1)y+t(t-2)z)

\displaystyle\times((t-2)(x+y)+t(t-2)z)(t(t-2)x+(t^{2}-t-1)y+t(t-2)z)

\displaystyle\times((t-2)x+(t-1)(t-2)z)((t-2)(1-t)x-(t-1)^{2}y),

subject to the condition
t\not\in\bigg\{0,1,2,(1\pm\sqrt{5})/2\bigg\}
.

This implies that the realization space of the matroid
T_{10}
is isomorphic to the complex line with finitely many points removed, corresponding to degenerate configurations (i.e., arrangements with distinct intersection lattices). Moreover, this family admits at least two homologically distinguished realizations, namely

L_{10}^{1}:Q_{3}=0,\qquad L_{10}^{2}:Q_{\sqrt{5}+3}=0.

The homological behavior of these arrangements is captured by the minimal graded free resolution of the module of Jacobian relations
D_{0}(Q_{t})
. One computes:

0\to S(-8)\to S(-6)^{\oplus 2}\oplus S(-5)\to D_{0}(Q_{3})

for
L_{10}^{1}
, and

0\to S(-8)\oplus S(-7)\to S(-7)\oplus S(-6)^{\oplus 2}\oplus S(-5)\to D_{0}(Q_{\sqrt{5}+3})

for
L_{10}^{2}
. In particular,
L_{10}^{1}
is a curve of type
2A
, whereas
L_{10}^{2}
is of type
2B
; see
[
2
]
for a detailed discussion of curves of type
2
. Since they have identical values of
{\rm mdr}
, both arrangements satisfy the
{\rm(MDR)}
condition. The pair
(L_{10}^{1},L_{10}^{2})
satisfies
{\rm(HF)}
condition, namely we have

HF(Q_{3})=HF\bigg(Q_{\sqrt{5}+3}\bigg)=1+3t+6t^{2}+10t^{3}+15t^{4}+21t^{5}+28t^{6}+36t^{7}\\
+45t^{8}+52t^{9}+57t^{10}+60t^{11}+61t^{12}+60t^{13}+58t^{14}+57t^{15}+\ldots

We conclude our analysis of the case
n=10
by discussing the special value
t=3
.
First, we observe that for this parameter the arrangement may exhibit the generic type of the resolution, namely that the generic element in the moduli space is of type
2A
, as we obtain the same results for many values of
t
.

However, at
t=3
the arrangement acquires an additional geometric feature, reminiscent
of the phenomenon observed in the first Ziegler pair of line arrangements.
More precisely, one can compute the coordinates of the twelve triple points, which are

\displaystyle[0:0:1],\quad[0:1:0],\quad[0:1:-1],\quad[0:3:-5],

\displaystyle[1:0:0],\quad[1:0:-1],\quad[3:0:-1],\quad[1:-1:0],

\displaystyle[2:-1:-1],\quad[2:-1:1],\quad[2:1:-1],\quad[6:-3:-1].

It turns out that there exists a unique irreducible conic

C:\quad x^{2}+xy+xz+4yz=0

passing through the following six triple points:

[0:0:1],\quad[0:1:0],\quad[1:0:-1],\quad[1:-1:0],\quad[2:-1:1],\quad[2:1:-1].

It is clear that the fact an irreducible conic passes through six points is a rather surprising geometric property.

Concluding this part, let us point out that similar properties appear in the case
n=12
. A feature shared by these two examples appears to be that their moduli space is rational, that is, it can be identified with the complex line with a finite number of points removed. It should be noted, however, that this does not hold in general for elliptic matroids: for example, when
n=11
or
n=13
, the corresponding moduli spaces no longer appear to be rational curves.
