Comparison of human metapneumovirus and respiratory syncytial virus in children with acute lower respiratory infections in Wenzhou, China from 2021 to 2022: a retrospective study
Authors: Lili Zhu, Luyao Zeng, Manjun Zhang, Xinyu Chen, Yilin Li, Jian Yu, Shunhang Wen, Hailin Zhang, Gang Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo compare the clinical characteristics of human metapneumovirus (HMPV) and respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) infections in children hospitalized with acute lower respiratory infections (ALRI).MethodsChildren hospitalized with ALRI in Wenzhou, China from 2021 to 2022 were detected for HMPV and RSV in sputum or nasopharyngeal secretions by multiplex reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR). A comparative analysis of demographic, clinical, laboratory, and radiological characteristics was performed retrospectively for patients in the HMPV and RSV groups.ResultsA total of 15,772 hospitalized children were screened and tested by multiplex RT-PCR. 889 (5.6%) were HMPV-positive and 2,196 (13.9%) were RSV-positive. Compared with the RSV-associated ALRI group, the HMPV-associated ALRI group exhibited a lower proportion of males (55.2% vs. 62.2%, P = 0.002), an older median age (2.5 years vs. 1.6 years, P < 0.001), and a higher proportion of underlying medical conditions (9.9% vs. 6.0%, P = 0.001). The HMPV group exhibited significantly longer duration of fever (P < 0.001) and higher peak temperature (P < 0.001) compared with the RSV group. In contrast, the RSV group were more likely to present with respiratory distress, including retractions (P = 0.006) and nodding breathing (P = 0.032). Levels of C-reactive protein (14.18 mg/L vs. 7.80 mg/L, P < 0.001), procalcitonin (P = 0.001), lactate dehydrogenase (422.0 U/L vs. 381.0 U/L, P < 0.001), and neutrophil percentage (60.6% vs. 56.5%, P = 0.007) in the HMPV group were significantly higher than the RSV group. Children with HMPV-associated ALRI were more likely to exhibit patchy or linear opacities (71.5% vs. 65.1%, P = 0.016), pulmonary consolidation (4.1% vs. 2.1%, P = 0.047), and atelectasis (1.8% vs. 0%, P = 0.001) compared with those in the RSV group.ConclusionIn hospitalized children, those with HMPV infection may be more common in older children and more likely to have underlying medical conditions than those with RSV infection. Children with HMPV-associated ALRI may present with prolonged, higher-grade fever, more pronounced systemic inflammatory responses, and an increased risk of developing pulmonary consolidation and atelectasis.

Introduction

According to the Global Burden of Disease database, approximately 4.6 million children under five years of age died globally in 2023, with lower respiratory infections accounting for about 600,000 deaths, which is the second leading cause of mortality in this age group [1]. Human metapneumovirus (HMPV) and respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) are recognized as major respiratory viral pathogens responsible for acute lower respiratory infections (ALRI) in children [2, 3]. Globally, HMPV is attributable to approximately 11.1 million (accounting for 8% of all ALRI cases) in children under five, leading to 500,000 hospitalizations and over 10,000 deaths annually [4]. In comparison, RSV is estimated to cause 33.1 million ALRI episodes, 3.2 million hospitalizations, and over 50,000 in-hospital deaths [5], demonstrating a substantial global healthcare burden [6].

Both HMPV and RSV are enveloped, single-stranded, negative-sense RNA viruses, and they are the only two human pathogens in the Pneumoviridae family. Children with HMPV infection typically present with upper respiratory tract symptoms; in severe cases, it can progress to bronchiolitis or pneumonia, manifesting as wheezing and respiratory distress. These findings are similar to those observed in RSV infection [7]. Therefore, the early identification, diagnosis, and prognosis evaluation of these two viruses present notable challenges for clinicians. Some studies suggest that HMPV infection is associated with a prolonged fever and a higher risk of pneumonia compared with RSV infection [8]. However, the differences in their epidemiological features, radiological characteristics, and inflammatory responses remain unknown. Specifically, a comprehensive characterization of HMPV and RSV infections in the pediatric population of Wenzhou, China, is currently unavailable.

To address this gap, this retrospective study comparatively analyzed the epidemiological patterns and clinical features of hospitalized children with either HMPV or RSV infection in Wenzhou, China. The data from this study are expected to support future recognition, diagnosis, and prevention strategies of HMPV and RSV infections in children.

Methods

Study design

This study was a retrospective cohort analysis. Children hospitalized with ALRI were categorized into either HMPV group or RSV group based on the results of respiratory pathogen nucleic acid detection by multiplex reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR). Propensity score matching (PSM) was used to balance potential confounding variables between the two groups, including age, sex, preterm birth, and the presence of underlying medical conditions (e.g., asthma, congenital heart disease, airway malformations, neuromuscular disorders, and malnutrition). Clinical characteristics were subsequently compared between the matched cohorts. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of The Second Affiliated Hospital & Yuying Children’s Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University (Approval No. 2021-K-224-02), with a waiver of informed consent from legal guardians.

Study population

The study population comprised children hospitalized for ALRI at The Second Affiliated Hospital & Yuying Children’s Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University between January 1, 2021, and December 31, 2022. The study center is the only tertiary pediatric hospital in southeast Zhejiang.

The diagnostic criteria for ALRI were based on standard clinical practice [9]. It included the presence of at least one clinical manifestation combined with radiographic confirmation. Clinical criteria included the acute onset of fever, cough, or wheezing; age-specific tachypnea (≥ 60 breaths/min for infants < 2 months, ≥ 50 breaths/min for 2–12 months, and ≥ 40 breaths/min for children 1–5 years); signs of respiratory distress (e.g., chest wall indrawing, nasal flaring, and grunting); and abnormal auscultatory findings (e.g., crackles, wheezing, or diminished breath sounds). Radiographic confirmation was defined by chest X-ray evidence of bronchitis, bronchiolitis, pulmonary infiltration, consolidation, or atelectasis. Hospitalized ALRI was defined as pediatric patients meeting the above ALRI criteria who required inpatient admission due to disease severity [10].

Inpatients were included in the study if they met all the following criteria: Age under 18 years; A clinical diagnosis of ALRI; Symptom onset within 7 days prior to enrollment; Positive detection of HMPV or RSV nucleic acid via Multiplex RT-PCR in sputum or nasopharyngeal secretion; Negative detection of COVID-19 nucleic acid. Exclusion criteria included: Co-infection with both RSV and HMPV; Nosocomial respiratory infection; Immunodeficiency or immunosuppression; Cases with severe missing data (refers to patients whose clinical records contain significant data gaps. Specifically, the birth date of the child from the welfare home remains uncertain, and the family members’ refusal to participate in routine clinical examinations has resulted in the absence of crucial clinical indicators and essential laboratory test results).

In accordance with national COVID-19 protocols, all children admitted to hospital were screened for SARS-CoV-2 using real-time RT-PCR of nasopharyngeal specimens. Patients with suspected or confirmed SARS-CoV-2 infection were transferred to designated specialized hospitals for isolation and standardized management, and were subsequently excluded from our cohort.

Respiratory pathogen detection

Multiplex RT-PCR detection was performed using the SureX 13 respiratory pathogen multiplex kit (Ningbo Health Gene Technologies Ltd, Ningbo, PR China) in sputum or nasopharyngeal secretions. This assay simultaneously identified 13 respiratory tract pathogens: influenza A (including subtypes H1N1 and H3N2), influenza B, parainfluenza virus, adenovirus, bocavirus, rhinovirus, HMPV, coronavirus (including OC43, 229E, NL63, and HKU1), RSV, Mycoplasma pneumoniae, and Chlamydia. To verify reaction validity, the assay incorporated three built-in internal controls (human RNA control, human DNA control, and RT-PCR control). The 25 µL reaction was performed in a single tube containing 14 µL of ResP mixture, 1 µL of RT-PCR enzyme mix, and 5 µL of extracted nucleic acid. A two-stage gradient annealing amplification procedure was employed to ensure high specificity. The thermal cycling profile was as follows: initial steps at 25 °C for 5 min, 50 °C for 15 min and 95 °C for 2 min; followed by five cycles of 94 °C for 30 s, 65 °C for 30 s and 72 °C for 1 min (the annealing temperature was decreased by 1 °C in each cycle), and then 25 cycles of 94 °C for 30 s, 60 °C for 30 s and 72 °C for 1 min; and a final incubation at 72 °C for 10 min. Amplification products were analyzed via advanced fragment analysis on an Applied Biosystems 3500Dx Genetic Analyzer (using LIZ500 as the internal standard), and raw data were processed using GeneMapper 4.1 software (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA).

Bacterial co-infection was defined as a positive culture from sputum, or bronchoalveolar lavage fluid, or nasopharyngeal secretions [11], combined with at least two of the following three conditions: C-reactive protein (CRP) ≥ 40 mg/L; procalcitonin (PCT) ≥ 0.5 µg/L; or both white blood cell (WBC) count and neutrophil percentage exceeding age-appropriate normal values.

Clinical data collection

A standardized case report form was used to collect demographic and clinical information, including sex, age, clinical symptoms and signs, underlying medical conditions, laboratory findings, radiological features, microbiological results and treatments.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 25.0. Continuous variables were assessed for normality. Normally distributed data were analyzed using analysis of variance (ANOVA). Non-normally distributed data were compared using the Mann-Whitney U test (or Wilcoxon rank-sum test). Categorical variables were analyzed using the Pearson χ² test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 15,772 children hospitalized between January 1, 2021, and December 31, 2022 were examined for 13 respiratory tract pathogens using multiplex RT-PCR detection. Overall, viral pathogens were identified in 10,539 cases (66.8%), while Mycoplasma pneumoniae and Chlamydia accounted for 1,861 cases (11.8%). The most frequently detected pathogen was Rhinovirus (25.1%, n = 3,962), followed by RSV (13.9%, n = 2,196) and Mycoplasma pneumoniae (11.5%, n = 1,812). Detection rates for remaining pathogens included Adenovirus (7.5%, n = 1,178), Parainfluenza virus (7.0%, n = 1,103), HMPV (5.6%, n = 889), Bocavirus (2.5%, n = 398), Coronavirus (1.8%, n = 282), Influenza B (1.7%, n = 271), H3N2 (1.6%, n = 260), and Chlamydia (0.3%, n = 49), with no H1N1 cases detected. The co-infection with two or more pathogens was observed in 1,710 cases (10.8%).

HMPV exhibited distinct seasonal peaks during the winter of 2021 and the spring-winter period of 2022, whereas RSV showed prominent circulation during the summer-autumn of 2021 and the autumn-winter of 2022. Notably, both viruses demonstrated a concurrent epidemic peak during the winter of 2022 (Fig. 1).

Two thousand four hundred forty-seven children hospitalized for ALRI either infected with HMPV or RSV were included in the analysis. There were 675 children with HMPV-associated ALRI in the HMPV group, and 1,772 children with RSV-associated ALRI in the RSV group. In the HMPV group, 373(55.2%) were male, the median age was 2.5 years (IQR, 1.1–3.7), and 70 (10.3%) were born preterm. In contrast, the RSV group had a higher proportion of males (62.2%) and a younger median age of 1.6 years (IQR, 0.6–2.9; P < 0.001). The HMPV group had a higher proportion of underlying medical conditions compared with the RSV group (9.9% vs. 6.0%; P = 0.001).

PSM was performed to balance baseline characteristics between the two groups. A total of 597 matched pairs were successfully generated (Fig. 2). No statistically significant differences were observed after matching between the HMPV and RSV groups in terms of sex, age, preterm birth, or presence of underlying medical conditions. Comparative analysis of clinical manifestations between HMPV and RSV infection

The predominant clinical symptoms among the enrolled children were cough (99.9%), fever (87.2%), and wheezing (56.9%). Compared with the RSV group, the HMPV group exhibited a significantly longer duration of fever (P < 0.001) and higher peak body temperature (P < 0.001) (Table 2).

Children in the RSV group were more likely to present with signs of respiratory distress compared with the HMPV group, including retractions (30.4% vs.23.4%; P = 0.006) and nodding breathing (4.8% vs. 2.5%; P = 0.032) (Table 2).

Pulmonary auscultation showed statistical differences between the two groups. The HMPV group was characterized by a longer duration of fine-to-medium crackles (P = 0.007), while in contrast, the duration of wheezing was shorter compared with the RSV group (P = 0.019) (Table 2).

No statistically significant difference was observed between the two groups in the proportion of severe pneumonia cases (P = 0.826).

Comparison of systemic inflammatory responses between HMPV and RSV infection

There was no statistically significant difference in WBC count between the two groups. However, children in the HMPV group exhibited significantly higher levels of CRP (14.18 mg/L vs. 7.80 mg/L; P < 0.001), neutrophil percentage (60.6% vs. 56.5%; P = 0.007), PCT (P = 0.001) and LDH (422.0 U/L vs. 381.0 U/L; P < 0.001) compared with those in the RSV group (Table 3).

Co-infection detection in two groups

Co-infections were identified in 380 children (31.8%), with no significant difference between the HMPV and RSV groups (P = 0.384). Bacterial co-infections were detected in 98 (8.2%) cases, including 42 (3.5%) with Streptococcus pneumoniae, 36 (3.0%) with Haemophilus influenzae, and others such as Moraxella catarrhalis. No significant difference was observed in the rate of bacterial co-infection between the HMPV and RSV groups (8.7% vs. 7.7%; P = 0.527).

Co-infection with Mycoplasma pneumoniae or Chlamydia was detected in 110 (9.2%) children. Viral co-infections were detected in 215 (18.0%) patients, including 124 (10.3%) with rhinovirus, 43 (3.6%) with adenovirus, 26 (2.1%) with parainfluenza virus, 19 (1.5%) with coronavirus, 12 (1.0%) with bocavirus, 8 (0.6%) with influenza B, and 2 (0.1%) with influenza A. Notably, the HMPV group had a higher proportion of co-infections with parainfluenza virus (3.0% vs. 1.3%; P = 0.047) and coronavirus (2.3% vs. 0.8%; P = 0.037) compared with the RSV group.

Comparison of radiological findings in HMPV and RSV infection

Compared with the RSV group, children in the HMPV group showed significantly higher rates of patchy or linear opacities (71.5% vs. 65.1%; P = 0.016), pulmonary consolidation (4.1% vs. 2.1%; P = 0.047), and atelectasis (1.8% vs. 0%; P = 0.001) on chest imaging. In contrast, children in the RSV group more frequently presented with bilaterally increased and thickened bronchovascular markings (32.6% vs. 24.1%; P = 0.001) (Table 3).

Treatment and disease burden in HMPV and RSV infection

Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was administered in 111 (9.2%) children, and antibiotics were used in 882 (73.8%) children. No significant differences were observed in the utilization rates of either IVIG or antibiotics between the two groups. However, the duration of systemic corticosteroid therapy was significantly longer in the RSV group compared with the HMPV group (P = 0.002) (Table 4).

The median length of hospital stay was 5.0 days (IQR, 4.0–6.0), and there were no significant differences between the two groups in either length of hospital stay (P = 0.814) or hospitalization costs (P = 0.956) (Table 4).

Discussion

This study systematically compared the clinical, inflammatory, and radiological features of HMPV and RSV infections in children hospitalized with ALRI between 2021 and 2022 in Wenzhou, a coastal city in southeastern China with distinct climatic and demographic characteristics. We found that hospitalized children with HMPV infection were older and had a higher prevalence of underlying medical conditions compared with RSV-infected children. Children with HMPV-associated ALRI presented with a longer fever duration, higher peak temperatures, more persistent fine-to-medium crackles, and more pronounced systemic inflammatory responses (elevated CRP, neutrophil percentage, PCT, and LDH), as well as a higher likelihood of developing pulmonary consolidation and atelectasis. Conversely, Children with RSV-associated ALRI more frequently presented with respiratory distress and longer wheezing duration.

In our cohort of hospitalized children, the HMPV detection rate was 5.6%, the median age was 2.5 years, and epidemic peaks occurred in winter 2021 and winter–spring 2022. These results align with a multicenter prospective study (2017–2019) across six Chinese cities, including Beijing and Wenzhou, which reported a 5.3% HMPV detection rate among children with ALRI, with 67.6% of cases occurring in children under two years of age and a peak incidence in the spring [12]. However, the HMPV detection rate was slightly lower in two other studies involving hospitalized children with acute respiratory infections (ARIs). Data from the Capital Institute of Pediatrics (2023–2024) showed a 3.1% positivity rate among 8,374 hospitalized children with ARIs, occurring predominantly in children under six years of age [13]. Similarly, a 2010–2019 multicenter retrospective study of 22 hospitals in southern China reported a 3.5% HMPV rate in children with ARIs, primarily affecting those under five and characterized by stable springtime seasonal peaks [14]. This seasonal pattern aligns with a recent multicenter prospective study across seven US pediatric hospitals and emergency departments (December 2016–March 2020), which identified HMPV activity peaking between March and April [15]. Collectively, these findings indicate that HMPV exhibits a consistent winter-spring seasonal pattern in both northern and southern China, predominantly affecting young children, without significant geographic variation. The discrepancies in reported detection rates may be attributed to regional epidemiological and climatic characteristics, as well as methodological factors, including variations in study population inclusion criteria and demographic differences across the cohorts.

In our study, the RSV detection rate in children with ARIs during 2021–2022 was 13.9%. The median age of RSV-infected patients was 1.6 years, which was significantly younger than that of HMPV cases (median 2.5 years). RSV typically was prevalent in winter and early spring [14], but we observed an atypical seasonal pattern that a prominent surge during summer and autumn 2021, followed by a decline in winter 2021 and spring 2022, and a resurgence in autumn-winter 2022. Similar off-season RSV peaks were reported in Morocco during summer and autumn 2021 [16]. These findings suggest that this seasonal shift was likely attributable to the concurrent COVID-19 pandemic, despite the overall positive detection rate remained relatively stable throughout the year. Non-pharmaceutical interventions implemented during this period markedly reduced population-level exposure to RSV, leading to declined natural immunity. With relaxation of these measures, delayed or atypical seasonal epidemics emerged [17]. Similarly, a 7-year Canadian cohort study (2017–2024) by Tiffany F. et al. [18] demonstrated a sharp decline in pediatric respiratory infections during 2020–2021, gradual recovery in 2021–2022, and a significant rebound in RSV and HMPV burden in 2022–2023, with seasonal patterns largely returning to pre-pandemic norms by 2023–2024. Notably, this study revealed that post-pandemic hospitalized children with ARIs were older and exhibited a lower male predominance, while direct impacts of COVID-19-related hospitalizations on overall ALRI admissions appeared minimal. Global surveillance data further confirmed that HMPV and RSV have re-established typical seasonal circulation in most regions following the COVID-19 pandemic since 2022 [19]. Thus, the atypical seasonality of HMPV and RSV observed in these studies is likely driven not only by climate and environmental factors but also by complex patterns of viral interference [20]. SARS-CoV-2 infection might suppress the acquisition and subsequent replication of HMPV and RSV, resulting in a distinct antagonistic interaction. This interference is probably mediated by the robust induction of a Type I and III interferon-driven antiviral state following SARS-CoV-2 exposure, which provides transient heterologous protection and leads to the observed temporal shifts and decoupling of traditional seasonal peaks. In addition, this study showed HMPV-infected children were more likely to co-infection with parainfluenza virus (3.0% vs. 1.3%) and coronavirus (2.3% vs. 0.8%) than RSV-infected children. The mechanisms underlying this viral interference warrant further investigation in future studies.

Clinically, HMPV infection typically presents with fever, cough, rhinorrhea, tachypnea. And severe cases may progress to bronchiolitis, severe pneumonia, or acute respiratory distress syndrome. These symptoms are overlapping considerably with RSV, leading to clinical differentiation challenging [12, 13]. In this study, HMPV-associated ALRI was characterized by longer fever duration, higher peak temperature, prolonged fine-to-medium crackles compared with RSV-associated ALRI. However, the slight variation in the duration of respiratory signs between the two viruses has limited clinical significance for distinguishing between HMPV and RSV infections. The primary diagnosis for both HMPV and RSV was pneumonia, followed by bronchiolitis and bronchitis, consistent with prior reports [21]. No significant differences were observed in the incidence of severe pneumonia or the severity of acute bronchiolitis between the two viruses. However, HMPV-infected children had a higher prevalence of underlying medical conditions (9.9% vs. 6.0%), aligning with recent large-scale multicenter prospective data [15], suggesting HMPV may preferentially affect children with preexisting conditions. These observed differences are clinically meaningful and may provide clinicians with valuable insights for the early identification and assessment of the risk factors in HMPV infections.

Compared with RSV, HMPV-associated ALRI was characterized by a more robust systemic inflammatory response, evidenced by significantly elevated levels of CRP, PCT, and LDH, as well as a higher neutrophil percentage. This finding is consistent with a multicenter prospective study that also indicated a strong systemic inflammatory response in HMPV infection [15]. Samoriski et al. [22] identified 197 differentially expressed genes between HMPV- and RSV-infected individuals by using whole-blood RNA sequencing analysis. They found that HMPV infection was associated with up-regulation of genes involved in antigen binding, immunoglobulin production, and adaptive immunity, whereas RSV infection showed stronger expression of genes linked to natural killer T-cell pathways. A recent comparative transcriptomic analysis of HMPV and RSV infection indicates that HMPV infection specifically enriches pathways related to extracellular activity, ion channels, and neuroactive signaling [23]. Furthermore, in contrast to the role in RSV infection, macrophages seem to involve in HMPV-mediated pathogenesis rather than protecting against infection. These studies suggest that HMPV and RSV trigger distinct immunopathogenic mechanisms, although they share structural similarities.

Radiological analysis demonstrated that patients with HMPV had a higher incidence of pulmonary consolidation and atelectasis, indicating greater parenchymal involvement. This finding is corroborated by the results of a large multicenter prospective study [15]. The study revealed that the RSV infection-induced Gas6/Axl axis facilitates pneumococcal overgrowth [24], suggesting that bacterial co-infection may play a role in the pathogenesis of pulmonary parenchymal lesions. However, this study showed bacterial co-infection rates between the HMPV and RSV groups were comparable, indicating that the high incidence of pulmonary parenchymal lesions in HMPV-infected cases is less likely to be attributed to bacterial co-infection. Kuiken T. et al. [25] found that ciliated respiratory epithelium serves as the primary target for HMPV following initial infection, the virus disseminates within airway cells and demonstrates enhanced replication capacity in the lower respiratory tract compared with upper respiratory tract. These findings may reflect HMPV’s propensity for causing lung injury. The mechanisms underlying the differential changes between the HMPV- and RSV-infected children observed in our study remain to be elucidated.

Co-infections were detected in 31.8% of our patients. Bacterial co-infection occurred in 8.2% of cases, predominantly Streptococcus pneumoniae and Haemophilus influenzae. Nevertheless, antibiotic use was remarkably high at 73.8%. This discrepancy mirrors findings by Papenburg J. et al. [26], who reported ~ 70% antibiotic administration in virally infected children despite low bacterial co-detection rates, likely due to pre-hospital antibiotic use reducing culture yield or clinical misattribution of viral symptoms to bacterial etiology. Therefore, we should focus on identifying more sensitive biomarkers to provide rational antibiotic stewardship in future.

This study has several limitations. Firstly, the single-center, retrospective design limits the external validity of our findings, restricting the generalizability of observed seasonality patterns and precluding confirmation of an underlying biological basis for the co-infection. Secondly, this study is limited by its methodology, including the lack of viral load detection, subtype analysis, or imaging severity scoring to objectively quantify disease severity, as well as the assessment of bacterial co-infection using only clinically-based methods. Additionally, COVID-19-postive pediatric cases were excluded from this study, so the influence of COVID-19 on HMPV and RSV infections could not be evaluated directly. As this study only collected clinical data during the pandemic control period, without comparative data from pre-pandemic and post-pandemic relaxation stages, we could not quantitatively assess the direct impact of lockdown policies on the volume of ALRI cases and pathogen detection rates.Thirdly, a long-term follow-up to evaluate patient outcomes and prognosis was not available in this retrospective cohort. At last, the underlying immunological mechanisms of potential viral interference among viruses were also not explored. In future, the multi-center and prospective studies using advanced molecular methods will be directed toward addressing these gaps to provide stronger evidence supporting the pathogenesis, prevention, and management strategies for HMPV and RSV infections in children.

Conclusion

HMPV infection in hospitalized children were more likely to be older and have medical underlying conditions compared with RSV-infected children. HMPV-related ALRI was characterized by prolonged and higher-grade fever, a pronounced systemic inflammatory response, and an increased propensity for pulmonary consolidation and atelectasis. These findings may assist physicians in differentiating between HMPV and RSV infection, identifying virus-specific phenotypes, and personalizing patient risk assessment in clinical practice.
