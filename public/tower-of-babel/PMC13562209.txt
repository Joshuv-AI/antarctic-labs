Global trends in the burden of inflammatory bowel disease from 1990 to 2021: socioeconomic and sex-specific disparities with exploratory mendelian randomization analyses
Authors: Yutong Cheng, Ziwei Wang, Yuxin Peng, Yongze Dang, Yingfan Deng, Sumei Sha, Xin Liu, Jinhai Wang, Haitao Shi, Ning Xie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractInflammatory bowel disease (IBD) shows substantial variation in burden across regions and populations worldwide. However, patterns of IBD b3urden across levels of socio-demographic development remain incompletely characterized, and complementary evidence on selected individual-level exposures is limited. Utilizing GBD 2021 data, we assessed age-standardized incidence rate (ASIR), prevalence rate (ASPR), mortality rate (ASMR), and disability-adjusted life years rate (ASDR) from 1990 to 2021 across socio-demographic index (SDI) strata. Temporal trends were estimated using estimated annual percentage change and Joinpoint regression. Pearson analysis and frontier analyses assessed SDI correlations, while inequality was measured using the concentration index and slope index. Decomposition analysis was performed to identify the contributors of population growth, population aging, and epidemiological change. Two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis was conducted to examine potential associations between selected exposures and IBD. From 1990 to 2021, the global ASIR rose by 0.29 annually, while ASPR, ASMR and ASDR decreased by 0.13, 0.31 and 0.52. Burden patterns differed across SDI strata. High SDI regions had the highest age-standardized burden in 2021. The largest increases in ASIR and ASPR were observed in middle SDI regions, and declines in ASMR and ASPR began later in low SDI regions than in high SDI regions. ASDR was positively correlated with SDI in females but not in males, and cross-country inequality in ASDR was greater in females than in males. Decomposition analysis showed that population aging contributed more to changes in ASMR and ASDR in high SDI regions, while population growth was the dominant contributor in low SDI regions. Fifteen countries showed large effective differences between observed ASDR and frontier values at comparable SDI levels. In MR analyses, several nominal associations were observed, but none remained significant after false discovery rate (FDR) correction. The burden of IBD showed substantial heterogeneity across SDI levels and between sexes in GBD 2021, with a more pronounced inequality pattern in disability burden among women. The MR findings did not remain significant after FDR correction and should therefore be interpreted cautiously as exploratory signals. These findings underscore the importance of context-specific strategies to reduce inequalities in IBD burden across populations and between women and men.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s10238-026-02224-1.Keywords: Inflammatory bowel disease, Health inequality, Socio-Demographic index, Mortality, Incidence, Prevalence, Disability-adjusted life-years

Introduction

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), including Crohn’s disease (CD) and ulcerative colitis (UC), is a chronic and recurrent autoimmune-related condition characterized by alternating periods of inflammation and remission, and a definitive cure remains elusive [1]. IBD has become a major global health issue, affecting millions of individuals worldwide and imposing substantial burdens on both public health systems and economies. It is associated with considerable disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) and mortality [2], as well as substantial direct healthcare costs and broader indirect economic burdens, particularly in high-income regions where annual per-person direct care costs are high [3]. A clear description of the global distribution and temporal trends of IBD burden is important for public health planning and resource allocation.

Recent epidemiological data indicated that IBD exhibits distinct regional and population-specific patterns [4]. While high-income regions have seen the incidence stabilize at high levels, several countries undergoing rapid social and economic transition have witnessed significant increases in incidence. In this context, the socio-demographic index (SDI) provides a useful macro level framework for comparing IBD burden across regions with differing levels of development. SDI is a composite index based on total fertility rate, per capita income, and average years of education for individuals aged 15 and above [5]. Importantly, SDI should be interpreted as a contextual indicator reflecting broader structural and societal conditions, rather than as a direct biological determinant of IBD.

Differences in IBD burden across regions may coincide with variation in environmental and lifestyle-related exposures, including dietary patterns, urbanization-related factors, and environmental pollution, which have been proposed as potential contributors to IBD risk [6–9]. These exposures may be unevenly distributed across socio-economic contexts and may be relevant to observed variation in IBD burden. In addition, IBD is widely considered to result from complex interactions between genetic susceptibility and environmental factors, as suggested by previous epidemiological and mechanistic studies. However, evidence on the potential associations of selected environmental and socioeconomic-related exposures with IBD at the individual level remains limited.

In this study, we aimed to systematically evaluate the global, regional, and national burden of IBD using data from the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2021 database, with a particular focus on patterns across different SDI levels. Additionally, Mendelian randomization (MR) analyses were used to explore the potential associations between selected exposures spanning socioeconomic-related, air pollution-related, and dietary domains and IBD at the individual level. These two approaches were designed to address distinct but complementary questions: GBD analyses were used to characterize population-level patterns and disparities, while MR analyses were used to examine potential associations for specific exposures at the individual level. As these approaches operate at different levels of inference, they were intended to provide complementary evidence without implying direct cross-level causal interpretation.

Materials and methods

This study combined GBD 2021 database and Genome-wide association study (GWAS) summary data to investigate IBD from complementary but distinct analytical perspectives (Fig. 1). GBD 2021, which provides standardized health estimates for 204 countries and territories, was used to characterize population-level patterns, temporal trends, and inequalities in IBD burden across SDI strata. A range of methods, including estimated annual percentage change (EAPC), Joinpoint regression, Pearson correlation coefficients, frontier analysis, cross-country inequality analysis, and decomposition analysis, were employed for the GBD component. GWAS summary statistics were used to perform MR analyses to evaluate the potential association between selected exposures and IBD at the individual level.

Global data source

This study used GBD 2021 data to characterize the burden of IBD at the global, regional, and national levels, with additional comparisons across SDI strata. We extracted data on the incidence, age-standardized incidence rate (ASIR), prevalence, age-standardized prevalence rate (ASPR), DALYs, age-standardized disability-adjusted life year rate (ASDR), mortality and age-standardized mortality rate (ASMR) of IBD from 1990 to 2021 through the GBD Results Tool (https://vizhub.healthdata.org/gbdresults/). DALYs include years of life lost due to premature mortality and years lived with disability, providing a comprehensive measure of disease burden. SDI was used as a macro-level contextual indicator to describe variation in IBD burden across regions with differing levels of socio-demographic development. GBD 2021 categorizes countries and regions into five SDI levels (low, low-middle, middle, high-middle, and high) [10]. Detailed quantitative information on SDI values is shown in Supplementary Table 1. A higher SDI value indicates a greater level of socio-demographic development.

Definition of IBD

The diagnosis and classification of IBD in this study adhere to the International Classification of Diseases (ICD) system developed by the World Health Organization. According to ICD-10 (https://icd.who.int/browse10/2019/en), IBD is classified under the following codes: K50-K52, K52.8 and K52.9 [11, 12].

Statistical analysis of GBD

To comprehensively assess the burden of IBD, a descriptive analysis was performed at global, regional, and national levels. The incidence, prevalence, mortality, and DALYs for IBD were visually illustrated through case numbers, crude rates, and ASRs for both sexes from 1990 to 2021. All metrics were reported with 95% uncertainty intervals (95% UI). Additionally, these metrics were compared across five SDI quintiles.

The average annual percentage change (AAPC) for the entire study period was calculated as the weighted average of the APCs across all segments, with weights proportional to the length of each segment. When the 95% CI of the APC or AAPC was entirely above 0 or entirely below 0, the corresponding trend was considered significantly increasing or decreasing, respectively; otherwise, the trend was considered stable.

Pearson correlation coefficients were used to examine the relationship between SDI and age-standardized IBD burden indicators, including ASIR, ASPR, ASMR, and ASDR. Additionally, frontier analysis was conducted based on ASDR to identify the optimal burden levels across the SDI spectrum using a frontier approach [17]. The frontier curve represented the lowest observed burden at comparable levels of socio-demographic development. The frontier value at a given SDI level was interpreted as the optimal burden within the observed data rather than a theoretical optimum. To account for uncertainty, bootstrap resampling was performed using 100 bootstrap samples, and the frontier curve was smoothed using LOESS regression with a span of 0.3. The gap between a country’s observed ASDR and the corresponding frontier value, calculated as the difference between the observed ASDR and the frontier ASDR at the same SDI level, was defined as the effective difference [18]. Larger effective differences were interpreted as indicating greater unrealized opportunities for burden reduction.

To quantify inequalities in the distribution of IBD burden across countries, we employed the Slope Index of Inequality (SII) and the Concentration Index. The SII measures absolute inequality and is calculated through regression analysis of rates for the entire population on a relative position scale associated with sociodemographic development. In contrast, the Concentration Index accesses relative inequality by numerically integrating the area under the Lorenz concentration curve, which represents the relationship between the cumulative distribution of rates and the cumulative distribution of the population ranked by SDI.

Finally, to quantify the components contributing to changes in the burden of IBD from 1990 to 2021, we conducted decomposition analyses by sex at the global level and across the five SDI levels. This approach decomposed the overall changes in incidence, prevalence, mortality, and DALYs into the separate contributions of population growth, population aging, and epidemiological change, with each component estimated while the other two were held constant [19].

All statistical analyses and graphical representations were conducted using R software (version 4.3.3). All GBD analyses were conducted using the ‘dplyr’, ‘maps’, ‘ggmap’, ‘ggplot2’ and ‘devtools’ packages.

MR study design

To provide complementary individual-level evidence, we performed MR analyses using genetic variants as instrumental variables (IVs) to assess the potential associations between selected exposures and IBD. The MR design satisfied the following three core assumptions: (1) IVs were strongly associated with the exposure; (2) IVs were independent of any confounders; (3) IVs influenced the outcome only through the exposure factor.

MR data sources

The GWAS summary statistics for the exposures were obtained from the publicly accessible Integrative Epidemiology Unit OpenGWAS database. We selected representative individual-level exposures spanning socioeconomic-related, air pollution-related, and dietary domains. These exposures were chosen on the basis of previous epidemiological literature on urbanization, environmental risk factors, and IBD epidemiology [9, 20–23]. They were analyzed as individual-level exposures rather than as direct proxies for the macro-level SDI construct. Socioeconomic-related traits included household income, educational attainment, and the Townsend Deprivation Index. Air pollution-related exposures included PM2.5, PM2.5−10, PM10, and NO2. Dietary exposures included pork, lamb, beef and processed meat intake. The outcomes included IBD and its subtypes, UC and CD. GWAS summary statistics for IBD, UC, and CD were obtained from the FinnGen consortium. Details of all datasets are presented in Table 1. All exposure and outcome datasets were restricted to individuals of European ancestry.

Instrumental variable selection

To ensure the quality of IVs, we employed a series of methods to select single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs). First, SNPs associated with each exposure were selected at the genome-wide significance threshold of p5 × 10⁻⁸. Linkage disequilibrium clumping was performed to remove undesirable SNPs, using an r² threshold of0.001 within a 10,000 kb window. When fewer than three SNPs were available for a given exposure at the genome-wide significance threshold, a relaxed threshold of p5 × 10⁻6 was applied. Then, to reduce potential violation of the IV assumptions, we used the GWAS Catalog (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/gwas/) to exclude SNPs associated with potential confounders, including body mass index, smoking, alcohol consumption, hyperlipidemia, and neuropsychiatric disorders. Finally, we harmonized the exposure and outcome datasets and removed palindromic SNPs based on effect allele frequency.

Statistical analysis

MR analyses were used to evaluate the associations between the selected exposures and IBD. The inverse variance weighted (IVW) method was used as the primary method, supplemented by the weighted median (WM) and MR-Egger. Cochran’s Q-test was utilized to estimate the heterogeneity of SNPs, while MR-Egger, leave-one-out analysis, and MR-PRESSO (NbDistribution3000; SignifThreshold0.05) were used to evaluate pleiotropy [26]. Because multiple exposures and outcomes were examined, p values were additionally adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure to control the false discovery rate (FDR). All statistical analyses and graphical representations were conducted using R software (version 4.3.3). All MR analyses were conducted using the “TwoSampleMR”, “ggplot2” and “MRPRESSO” packages. An adjusted p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, whereas unadjusted p < 0.05 was interpreted as nominal evidence only. For environmental exposures, particularly air pollution-related traits, genetic instruments should not be interpreted as direct measures of externally measured exposure. They may reflect liability to exposure-related contexts, correlated behavioral or residential patterns, or other phenotypes associated with the measured exposure. Accordingly, even when conventional instrument strength criteria were met, the biological specificity and interpretability of these instruments may be more limited than for traits with clearer individual-level phenotypic definitions. Therefore, the MR analyses for environmental traits were interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than as definitive evidence of direct causal effects.

Ethics statement

The University of Washington’s Institutional Review Board granted a waiver of informed consent for GBD studies. Ethical approval and consent to participate were obtained in all original studies related to MR analysis.

Results

Global, regional, and national burden of IBD

Globally, crude rates remained relatively stable between 1990 and 2021, whereas the absolute IBD burden increased (Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2). Incident cases increased from 199,235.85 (95% UI:174,583.84 to 232,676.16) in 1990 to 375,139.75 (95% UI:327,686.17 to 436,925.14) in 2021 (Table 2). Prevalent cases increased from 2,170,243.30 (95% UI:1,892,401.81 to 2,522,561.30) to 3,830,119.27 (95% UI:3,312,834.00 to 4,511,554.52) (Table 3). Mortality increased from 21,417.72 (95% UI:18,422.82 to 23,612.63) to 42,422.66 (95% UI:37,536.90 to 46,502.10), and DALYs increased from 948,860.80 (95% UI:808,100.64 to 1,096,717.03) to 1,510,783.81 (95% UI:1,308,507.79 to 1,750,363.18) (Tables 4 and 5). Over the same period, ASIR increased from 4.22 (95% UI:3.72 to 4.88) to 4.45 (95% UI:3.87 to 5.19) per 100,000 population, corresponding to an EAPC of 0.29 (95% CI:0.20 to 0.38), whereas ASPR, ASMR and ASDR declined (Tables 2, 3, 4 and 5; Fig. 2). ASPR declined from 48.02 (95% UI:41.94 to 55.78) to 44.88 (95% UI:38.80 to 52.86) (EAPC − 0.13, 95% CI:-0.25 to 0.00) (Table 3). ASMR decreased from 0.60 (95% UI:0.52 to 0.66) to 0.52 (95% UI:0.46 to 0.57) (EAPC − 0.31, 95% CI:-0.48 to -0.14), and ASDR declined from 21.54 (95% UI:18.47 to 24.82) to 18.07 (95% UI:15.67 to 20.91) (EAPC − 0.52, 95% CI:-0.60 to -0.43) (Tables 4 and 5).

Case number, ASR and EAPC of IBD DALYs in 1990 and 2021

Marked regional heterogeneity was observed across the 21 GBD super-regions in 2021 (Tables 2, 3, 4 and 5). Australasia had the highest ASIR (19.66, 95% UI:17.07 to 23.20) and ASPR (203.27, 95% UI:174.69 to 240.93). Western Europe had the highest ASMR (1.11, 95% UI:0.95 to 1.20) and high-income North America had the highest ASDR (49.59, 95% UI:40.23 to 60.57). Central Latin America had the lowest ASIR (0.57, 95% UI:0.48 to 0.69) and ASPR (5.56, 95% UI:4.62 to 6.66). High-income Asia Pacific had the lowest ASMR (0.12, 95% UI:0.10 to 0.16), and Southeast Asia had the lowest ASDR (6.14, 95% UI:4.63 to 7.23). EAPCs also varied substantially across super-regions. The largest increases in ASIR (2.88, 95% CI:2.37 to 3.40) and ASPR (2.49, 95% CI:1.87 to 3.11) were observed in East Asia, while the largest increases in ASMR (3.54, 95% CI:2.62 to 4.46) and ASDR (1.08, 95% CI:0.75 to 1.41) were observed in Australasia. The steepest declines in ASPR, ASMR, and ASDR were observed in Central Sub-Saharan Africa (-0.33, 95% CI:-0.50 to -0.16), high-income Asia Pacific (-3.42, 95% CI:-3.77 to -3.07), and East Asia (-2.89, 95% CI:-3.16 to -2.62).

Nationally, the age-standardized IBD burden showed substantial variation worldwide in 2021 (Fig. 3A and Supplementary Tables 2–5). Canada had the highest ASIR (26.83, 95% UI:23.30 to 30.76) and ASPR (326.64, 95% UI:278.94 to 385.56), whereas the Netherlands had the highest ASMR (2.21, 95% UI:1.85 to 2.43) and ASDR (74.96, 95% UI:60.11 to 91.48). By contrast, Mexico had the lowest ASIR (0.20, 95% UI:0.17 to 0.25) and ASPR (1.86, 95% UI:1.54 to 2.29), while Singapore had the lowest ASMR (0.03, 95% UI:0.02 to 0.03) and ASDR (1.97, 95% UI:1.45 to 2.60). Substantial variation in EAPCs was also observed (Fig. 3B and Supplementary Tables 2–5). The highest EAPCs were observed in China for ASIR (2.93, 95% CI:2.41 to 3.46) and ASPR (2.54, 95% CI:1.91 to 3.17), Germany for ASMR (4.12, 95% CI:3.51 to 4.73), and Libya for ASDR (1.79, 95% CI:1.55 to 2.03). The lowest EAPCs were observed in Finland for ASIR (-1.04, 95% CI:-1.44 to -0.64), Italy for ASPR (-0.70, 95% CI:-0.90 to -0.48), Estonia for ASMR (-6.75, 95% CI:-8.36 to -5.11), and American Samoa for ASDR (-5.27, 95% CI:-5.70 to -4.84).

Distinct patterns across SDI levels were observed in both burden levels and temporal trends (Tables 2, 3, 4 and 5). High SDI regions had the highest ASIR (11.59, 95% UI:10.14 to 13.38), ASPR (132.76, 95% UI:115.02 to 154.34), ASMR (0.80, 95% UI:0.70 to 0.86) and ASDR (35.00, 95% UI:28.33 to 42.95) in 2021. From 1990 to 2021, the largest increase in ASIR was observed in middle SDI regions (1.54, 95% CI:1.38 to 1.70), while the smallest increase was observed in high SDI regions (0.33, 95% CI:0.21 to 0.46). The largest increase in ASPR was also observed in middle SDI regions (1.34, 95% CI:1.19 to 1.50), while the largest decline was observed in high-middle SDI regions (-0.15, 95% CI:-0.31 to 0.02). ASDR and ASMR increased in high SDI regions (EAPC in ASDR 0.31, 95% CI:0.1 to 0.51; EAPC in ASMR 0.84, 95% CI:0.52 to 1.17) but declined in other SDI regions. Sex-specific differences were also observed. In high SDI regions, females had higher ASIR, ASPR, ASMR and ASDR than males, whereas in high-middle, middle, low-middle, and low SDI regions, males had higher ASMR and ASDR than females (Fig. 2).

Joinpoint regression analysis of temporal trends in IBD burden

Joinpoint regression analysis identified multiple changes in temporal trends in age-standardized IBD burden between 1990 and 2021 (Supplementary Table 6). ASIR (Fig. 4A) and ASPR (Supplementary Fig. 3A) showed similar patterns, with an initial increase, a subsequent decline, and a further increase reaching a peak around 2010, followed by declines. ASMR (Supplementary Fig. 3B) showed three turning points and an overall downward trend, although an increase was observed during 1998–2003. ASDR (Fig. 4B) showed five turning points, with slight increases during 1990–1994 and 1997–2002, followed by declines at different rates, resulting in an overall downward trend.

Joinpoint analysis across SDI levels revealed distinct temporal trends in ASIR (Fig. 4C), ASPR (Supplementary Fig. 3C), ASMR (Supplementary Fig. 3D), and ASDR (Fig. 4D). In low SDI regions, declines in ASDR and ASMR began later than in high SDI regions, around 2014.

Socioeconomic development and IBD burden: an SDI-based analysis

Correlation analysis was performed to assess the associations between SDI and age-standardized IBD burden in 2021. Positive correlations were observed between SDI and both ASIR and ASPR in both sexes combined (ASIR: ρ0.30, p0.001; ASPR: ρ0.31, p0.001), with similar patterns in males (ASIR: ρ0.30, p0.001; ASPR: ρ0.31, p0.001) and females (ASIR: ρ0.30, p0.001; ASPR: ρ0.32, p0.001) (Fig. 5A and Supplementary Fig. 3E). Sex-specific differences were observed in the correlations of ASMR and ASDR with SDI. For ASDR, significant positive correlations with SDI were identified in both sexes combined (ρ0.16, p0.023) and in females (ρ0.24, p0.001), while no significant correlation was observed in males (ρ0.07, p0.315) (Fig. 5C).

Cross-country health inequality in IBD burden

Cross-country health inequalities in IBD burden varied by burden measures, time points, and sex groups (Supplementary Table 8). For ASIR and ASPR, both the SII and Concentration Index remained positive in 1990 and 2021. From 1990 to 2021, the SII increased for both ASIR and ASPR, whereas the Concentration Index decreased. For ASDR, both absolute and relative inequality decreased between 1990 and 2021. The SII decreased from 11.37 (95% CI:5.98 to 16.75) to 6.30 (95% CI:1.21 to 11.40) (Fig. 6A). The Concentration Index also decreased from 0.11 (95% CI: 0.07 to 0.15) to 0.09 (95% CI: 0.03 to 0.15) (Fig. 6B). In 2021, both absolute and relative ASDR inequalities were greater in females than in males. The SII was 3.65 (95% CI:-1.68 to 8.97) in males and 8.89 (95% CI:3.44 to 14.33) in females, while the corresponding Concentration Index values were 0.07 (95% CI:0.02 to 0.13) and 0.11 (95% CI: 0.05 to 0.17), respectively (Fig. 6C and D). ASMR showed a weaker and more sex-specific pattern. In both sexes combined and in females, the Concentration Index remained positive in 1990 and 2021. In males neither the SII nor the Concentration Index was statistically significant.

Decomposing the IBD burden: contributions of aging, population growth, and epidemiological changes

Decomposition analysis showed distinct contributions of population growth, population aging, and epidemiological change to changes in IBD burden (Fig. 6E-H and Supplementary Table 9). At the global level, population growth contributed most to changes in incidence (61.54%), prevalence (68.85%), and DALYs (84.29%), whereas population aging contributed most to mortality (66.90%). In low SDI regions, population growth was the largest contributor to changes in incidence (78.20%), prevalence (83.08%), mortality (107.97%), and DALYs (99.43%). In high SDI regions, population growth remained the largest contributor to incidence (52.78%) and prevalence (50.28%), whereas population aging contributed more to mortality (63.13%) and DALYs (48.93%).

Association between selected exposures and IBD: a two-sample MR analysis

MR analyses were used to evaluate associations between selected exposures and IBD. A total of 11 exposures spanning socioeconomic-related, air pollution-related, and dietary domains were examined. SNPs for the air pollution-related exposures were selected using a relaxed significance threshold of p5 × 10− 6, while others were selected at a threshold of p5 × 10− 8. Each analysis included at least 9 SNPs (Supplementary Table 10). Instrument strength was adequate across all analyses, with a minimum F-statistic of 20.88 (Supplementary Table 10). Detailed information on the used SNPs was provided in Supplementary Table 11.

Because multiple exposures were examined, FDR correction was applied to account for multiple comparisons. In the IVW analyses, several associations reached significance (Fig. 7 and Supplementary Table 10). However, none remained significant after FDR correction (Supplementary Table 10). Specifically, genetically predicted PM2.5-related exposure suggested a positive association with IBD (OR 1.982, 95% CI:1.184 to 3.317, p0.009, adjusted p0.101), and genetically predicted NO2-related exposure also suggested a positive association with IBD (OR1.831, 95% CI:1.074 to 3.121, p0.026, adjusted p0.144). For subtype analyses, genetically predicted PM2.5-related exposure (OR2.096, 95% CI:1.080 to 4.068, p0.029, adjusted p0.161) and NO2-related exposure (OR2.049, 95% CI:1.075 to 3.900, p0.029, adjusted p0.161) suggested positive associations with UC. In addition, genetically predicted educational attainment showed a nominal inverse association with CD (OR0.903, 95% CI:0.820 to 0.996, p0.04, adjusted p0.404).

The forest plot shown the MR results for the associations between 11 selected exposures and IBD (including ulcerative colitis and Crohn’s disease). MR: Mendelian randomization; IBD: inflammatory bowel disease.

Sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of these nominal associations (Supplementary Table 12). The PM2.5-related analyses did not indicate substantial heterogeneity or directional pleiotropy, supporting the robustness of the nominal associations with IBD and UC. In contrast, the NO2-related analyses showed evidence of heterogeneity and MR-PRESSO outlier signals. The analysis between educational attainment and CD showed potential directional pleiotropy based on the MR-Egger intercept test. Leave-one-out analyses showed that the nominally significant estimates were not materially altered by sequential removal of individual SNPs, suggesting that they were not driven by any single variant (Supplementary Figs. 4–14). Nevertheless, none of these associations remained significant after FDR correction.

Discussion

This study analyzed the global, regional, and national burden of IBD from 1990 to 2021 and identified heterogeneity in the epidemiological pattern of IBD across SDI levels. High SDI regions had the highest ASIR, ASPR, ASMR and ASDR, suggesting that measured burden of IBD remained greatest in more socio-demographically developed settings. This pattern may reflect a combination of lifestyle and environmental transitions, as well as differences in case ascertainment, earlier diagnosis, and disease registration across settings. In contrast, the lower ASIR and ASPR in low SDI regions may partly be related to underdiagnosis and underreporting rather than a truly lower underlying burden. Although the absolute numbers of incident cases, prevalent cases, deaths, and DALYs all increased between 1990 and 2021, most age-standardized burden measures declined globally. This divergence is consistent with the decomposition findings, which indicate important contributions from population growth and population aging to changes in IBD burden.

Joinpoint regression identified important temporal turning points in the burden of IBD from 1990 to 2021. Globally, both ASIR and ASPR increased significantly between 2005 and 2010, peaked around 2010, and then entered a phase of decline and stabilization. High SDI regions followed a similar pattern, whereas low SDI regions did not show a clear decline. These findings are broadly consistent with the four-stage theory of IBD epidemiology and may indicate that regions at different levels of development are undergoing different stages of epidemiological transition [27, 28]. In high SDI regions, such as North America and Europe, IBD appears to have entered the “prevalence compound phase”, in which incidence stabilizes while the number of individuals living with the disease continues to accumulate [27]. This pattern may be consistent with relatively stable exposure profiles and longer survival with disease in high-burden settings. By contrast, some lower SDI regions may still be in the “emergence” phase [29]. Notably, the largest increases in ASIR and ASPR were observed in middle SDI regions, consistent with previous evidence that the acceleration in IBD incidence has increasingly shifted toward newly industrialized settings undergoing rapid social and lifestyle transition rather than the highest SDI regions [30, 31]. The increases in ASIR and ASPR in middle SDI settings may be consistent with the environmental and lifestyle transitions described in prior epidemiological literature, including urbanization-related changes, dietary westernization, and improved disease recognition. At the same time, high SDI regions were the only group in which the overall EAPCs for ASMR and ASDR remained positive over 1990–2021. However, joinpoint analysis showed that both indicators increased in earlier years and began to decline after turning points around 2011–2012, suggesting a more complex temporal pattern than a sustained increase. In addition, the onset of decline in ASMR and ASDR appeared later in low SDI regions than in high SDI regions, which may be consistent with persistent limitations in healthcare access, diagnostic capacity, and long-term disease management.

Our study showed that sex disparities in IBD burden were not consistent across regions at different levels of development. In high SDI regions, females had higher ASIR, ASPR, ASMR, and ASDR than males, whereas in other SDI regions, males showed a more evident disadvantage in ASMR and ASDR. In addition, ASDR was positively correlated with SDI in females, whereas no significant correlation was observed in males. The cross-country inequality analysis further supported this pattern by showing that the degree of inequality in ASDR was greater in females than in males. This pattern is difficult to explain by biological sex alone [32]. At the same time, some studies have recognized important links between healthcare accessibility, disease outcomes, and social determinants in IBD care [33, 34]. On this basis, the interaction between sex and social determinants may further shape the burden of IBD across different developmental settings. Relevant studies have suggested that sex, together with other social determinants, can influence exposure risk, the use of and access to healthcare services, bias within healthcare systems, and the ways in which disease is recognized, treated, and managed [35, 36]. In addition, socialization and societal responses to sex-specific behaviors may also amplify or suppress underlying biological differences [37]. Previous studies have shown that life expectancy is generally longer in females than in males in high SDI regions, and that chronic burden, functional impairment, and treatment burden over a long disease course are also more likely to be recognized, recorded, and accumulated [38]. This may be related to the finding that the measured burden in females was higher than that in males in high SDI regions. In contrast, in resource-limited settings, although females may still have a longer life expectancy than males, they may be treated less favorably within healthcare services than males, which may affect disease recognition and diagnosis and contribute to under-recording, thereby failing to reflect the true burden level [37]. However, research on the interaction between sex and social factors in IBD remains limited. Therefore, these explanations should be regarded as a reasonable framework consistent with our findings rather than as directly proven mechanisms. From an epidemiological perspective, these findings indicate that sex disparities in IBD should be interpreted not only in biological terms, but also within the broader context of healthcare systems and social development. The disability burden of IBD in females may be particularly sensitive to the interaction among biological sex, gender-related context, and development-related conditions. From a policy perspective, this pattern suggests that reducing the disability burden of IBD in females may require not only effective treatment, but also more equitable access to timely diagnosis, specialist care, and long-term disease management.

Frontier analysis further showed that countries with similar SDI levels could still differ markedly in ASDR, suggesting that socio-demographic development alone does not fully account for cross-country variation in disability burden. The large effective differences observed in several countries suggest substantial unrealized opportunities for burden reduction and may help inform priorities for diagnosis, continuity of care, and long-term disease management. More broadly, the decomposition analysis highlighted distinct contributors to variation in IBD burden across development settings. In low SDI countries, population growth was the dominant contributor. In high SDI countries, population aging contributed more substantially to the burden pattern, which is consistent with the growing clinical importance of older adults with IBD, who often have multiple comorbidities and more complex long-term management needs [39, 40]. These findings are consistent with the need for context-specific policy priorities, including strengthening core diagnostic and treatment capacity in low SDI regions and improving long-term, age-sensitive chronic disease management in high SDI settings.

Several associations in the MR analyses reached nominal significance in the primary IVW models, including PM2.5- and NO2-related traits in relation to IBD and UC, as well as educational attainment in relation to CD. However, none remained significant after FDR correction, indicating that these findings do not provide statistically robust evidence for these associations. This should be interpreted cautiously. Although FDR control is less conservative and generally more powerful than family-wise error rate procedures such as Bonferroni correction, it may still reduce power and attenuate weaker signals in exploratory analyses involving multiple tests [41, 42]. Accordingly, these nominal associations may still be informative for hypothesis generation and for prioritizing exposures for further investigation, but they should not be interpreted as robust evidence or as mechanistic explanations for the SDI-related patterns observed in the GBD analyses [43]. The sensitivity analyses further indicate that these nominal associations were not equally robust. The PM2.5-related results appeared relatively more stable, whereas the NO2-related analyses showed heterogeneity and MR-PRESSO outlier signals. In addition, the association between educational attainment and CD should be interpreted cautiously because the MR-Egger intercept suggested possible pleiotropy.

Among the nominal findings, the air pollution-related signals warrant cautious attention because they are directionally consistent with parts of the existing literature. A UK Biobank study reported that long-term exposure to several air pollutants was associated with UC but not CD [44], and recent work has linked air pollution exposure to UC susceptibility through epigenetic alterations involving the CXCR2 and MHC class III regions [45]. Nevertheless, the overall evidence relating air pollution to IBD remains limited and heterogeneous [46]. Interpretation of these findings also requires caution. For environmental traits such as air pollution, genetic instruments may capture liability related to exposure-associated contexts or correlated phenotypes rather than directly measured external exposure itself [43, 47]. Moreover, although multiple sensitivity analyses were performed, horizontal pleiotropy and residual instrument invalidity cannot be completely excluded [48]. The MR analyses do not support statistically robust evidence of effects for the examined exposures, but they provide complementary, hypothesis-generating evidence that may help prioritize candidate exposures for future investigation [49].

This study has several limitations. First, although the GBD 2021 framework provides comprehensive global estimates, data coverage remains incomplete in some regions, and residual confounding cannot be entirely excluded despite standardized modelling procedures. Second, the burden analyses were based on aggregated IBD estimates because subtype-specific epidemiological data for CD and UC are not available within the GBD 2021 dataset. This may obscure subtype-specific epidemiological patterns because CD and UC are clinically and epidemiologically heterogeneous conditions. Third, the GBD and MR components of this study operate at different levels of inference, which limits direct comparison between the population-level patterns identified in the GBD analysis and the individual-level associations examined in the MR analysis. Finally, the MR analyses were limited to populations of European ancestry and lacked sex-stratified subtype instruments, which may make the findings less generalizable to other populations. In addition, MR analyses of environmental traits, particularly air pollution-related exposures, have inherent interpretational limitations because genetic instruments may not proxy directly measured external exposure and may instead reflect correlated contextual or behavioral factors. Although we performed multiple sensitivity analyses, pleiotropy and residual instrument invalidity cannot be fully excluded. Moreover, air pollution-related analyses relied on a relaxed SNP selection threshold, which may further reduce instrument specificity. These considerations support interpreting the MR findings as exploratory rather than definitive evidence of effects.

Conclusion

Using GBD 2021 data across SDI strata, this study showed substantial heterogeneity in the burden of IBD across socio-demographic settings. The distribution and temporal patterns of IBD burden varied across development levels and between sexes, with a more evident inequality pattern in disability burden among women. Although several nominal associations were observed in the MR analyses, none remained significant after FDR correction, and these findings should therefore be interpreted as exploratory signals. Overall, the results support the need for context-specific strategies to reduce inequalities in IBD burden across populations and between women and men.
