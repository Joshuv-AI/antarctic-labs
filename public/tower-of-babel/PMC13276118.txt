ABSTRACT

Introduction

Non-invasive spirometry-based lung function measures (forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1), forced vital capacity (FVC) and FEV1/FVC) are used to evaluate the physiological state of the lungs, diagnose respiratory diseases, monitor disease progression and assess treatment efficacy. Additionally, lower lung function is a well-established independent predictor of comorbidities and mortality [1, 2]. Although large multi-ancestry genetic and epigenetic studies have identified numerous loci associated with lung function, including many linked to lung diseases [3–5], additional genomic approaches can enhance the interpretation of these findings. Studying proteins, the functional molecules in many biological processes, can provide new insights into an understanding of lung function and respiratory diseases.

Circulating proteins can provide a snapshot of an individual's current health status. Recent advances in proteomic technologies hold promise for the discovery of reliable new biomarkers and therapeutic strategies for various diseases [6]. Studies with moderate sample sizes have demonstrated the potential of proteomics in identifying subtypes of respiratory diseases, including asthma and COPD [7, 8]. Several proteomic studies have identified proteins related to lung function, mostly in populations of European ancestry [9–12]. Moreover, most studies utilised older proteomic assays that captured fewer proteins. The current literature is limited in terms of the proteomics assays employed and the diversity of the study populations.

Here, we present findings from a large meta-analysis of differential abundance of 4693 circulating proteins in relation to lung function measures in a multi-ancestry population (20 823 participants of European, African and Asian ancestries). We explored pathway enrichment and protein–protein interaction networks involving significant proteins. Mendelian randomisation (MR) analysis was employed to investigate potential causality. Furthermore, we looked up our significant proteins in data from independent studies using different proteomic assays. We also cross-referenced the genes related to lung function in our data with those implicated in previous large genetic and epigenetic studies.

Methods

Additional details are provided in the supplementary material.

Study population

This study included 20 823 adults (≥30 years old) from five cohorts: Age, Gene/Environment Susceptibility–Reykjavik (AGES-Reykjavik), Agricultural Lung Health Study (ALHS), Atherosclerosis Risk in Communities (ARIC), Cardiovascular Health Study (CHS) and China Kadoorie Biobank (CKB). Participants were from three ancestry groups: European ancestry (n=14 737) from AGES, ALHS, ARIC and CHS; African ancestry (n=3041) from ARIC and CHS; and Asian ancestry (n=3045) from CKB.

Lung function and proteomic measurement

Pre-bronchodilator lung function (FEV1 and FVC in mL) was measured by trained staff using spirometry in each study [13]. Protein abundance in blood samples was measured using the SomaScan platform (https://somalogic.com/somascan-platform).

Association analyses

After study-level normalisation and quality control filtering, 4979 aptamers (targeting 4693 human proteins) were retained for association analyses. Each study evaluated the association between log2-transformed protein abundance and lung function measures (FEV1, FVC or FEV1/FVC) using robust linear regression, adjusting for age, age squared, sex, height, height squared, weight (for FVC only), cigarette smoking (current and former, each compared to never) and pack-years. Additionally, where appropriate, studies adjusted for selection factor, study centre, time in transit and ancestry principal components (based on genome-wide genotypes). To mitigate the potential impact of extreme outliers, studies applied winsorisation (0.25% on both sides) [14]. In cohorts with more than one ancestry group or proteomic assay (5K or 7K) analyses were conducted separately by ancestry and assay. We meta-analysed study-level results using fixed effects inverse variance weighting [15], implemented in the metafor R package [16]. A Bonferroni significance threshold of p<1×10−5 (0.05/4979 aptamers) was set to account for multiple testing. Significant proteins were then subjected to downstream analyses.

Functional downstream analyses

UniProt IDs of significant proteins were used to test for pathway enrichment and visualise protein–protein interaction networks using Ingenuity Pathway Analysis [17]. Additionally, we explored the expression of our significant proteins in the lung using the Human Protein Atlas [18].

Protein quantitative trait loci and MR

To assess evidence for potential causality, we examined significant proteins under the two-sample MR [19] framework, which leverages summary statistics from two independent datasets. We integrated the protein (exposure) genetic association data (n=7213) [20] with lung function (outcome) genetic association data (n=454 479) [3]. We utilised the sentinel cis-protein quantitative trait loci (pQTL) variants (within ±100 kb of the target protein-coding gene’s transcription start site with a significance threshold of p<5×10−9) for each protein as the instrumental variable to minimise bias from horizontal pleiotropy [21]. We implemented the Wald ratio to estimate the causal effect of protein on lung function [22]. Given that large sample sizes are required for MR-derived effect size estimates, which incorporate uncertainty of both the variant-protein and variant-lung function estimates, we limited the MR estimates to European ancestries.

Associations with COPD

We examined whether the proteins we found to be significantly associated with at least one lung function measure were also associated with COPD, defined using pre-bronchodilator spirometry criteria: FEV1 <80% predicted [23, 24] and FEV1/FVC <0.7. Non-cases had FEV1 ≥80% predicted and FEV1/FVC ≥0.7.

Look-up validation

To validate the significantly associated proteins we identified using SomaScan, we examined UK Biobank (UKB) proteome data from the Olink Explore 3072 (3K) platform (the 560 overlapping proteins based on UniProt IDs) [25] in relation to lung function (34 019 European ancestry participants) [26]. Additionally, we interrogated Qatar Biobank (QBB) proteome data using an earlier SomaScan 1.3K platform in relation to lung function (1955 Middle-East Arabians). Furthermore, we looked up our significant proteins in previous proteome studies of lung function [10, 12].

To explore the potential clinical utility of our findings, we examined whether previously identified circulating protein biomarkers of COPD outcomes [27–30] were related to lung function in our data.

Results

Lung function-related proteins

We identified 1091 aptamers (denoted by SeqIDs) representing 1055 proteins, as denoted by unique UniProt IDs, significantly differentially abundant (p<1×10−5) in relation to lung function (top 30 aptamers for each trait in tables 2–4; all significant proteins in supplementary table E2). Of these 1091 aptamers, 742 (68%) were related to multiple lung function measures; the remainder were associated with a single trait (figure 2a). Proteins related to multiple measures include the top three proteins for FEV1 and FVC: HTRA1, CRP and TSP2. Proteins unique to a specific trait include HSPB6 unique to FEV1, Nr-CAM unique to FVC and afamin unique to FEV1/FVC. We found twice as many proteins showing significant associations with FEV1 or FVC than FEV1/FVC: 806 for FEV1, 811 for FVC and 389 for FEV1/FVC. Of significant signals, 71% for FEV1, 73% for FVC and 58% for FEV1/FVC were inverse associations (figure 2b–d), linking lower lung function to higher protein levels. Notably, 83% of our significant proteins had abundance profiles available in the Human Protein Atlas and 59% of these proteins were expressed in the lung (supplementary table E2). At FDR <0.05, there were 1802 significant aptamers for FEV1, 1795 for FVC and 960 for FEV1/FVC. Complete meta-analysis results are provided in the supplementary material.

Enriched pathways

Protein–protein interaction

Significant proteins exhibited more interactions among them than expected for a random set of assayed proteins (p<1×10−16 for all measures) (supplementary table E3). Not surprisingly, the protein–protein interaction networks for FEV1 and FVC, where more protein associations were identified, were more complex than the network for FEV1/FVC (supplementary figure E1). Proteins identified as hubs (proteins connected to more than five other proteins) in the networks include STAT3 and EGFR (figure 3b), suggesting key roles in regulating lung function.

Evidence for potential causality

Of the 831 aptamers (representing 803 proteins) significant in our European ancestry meta-analysis (supplementary table E4), 499 with at least one cis-pQTL [20] were available for MR investigation. Among these, we found 34 unique proteins with potential causal evidence after multiple testing correction, representing 44 significant associations: 15 (4% of 338; p<1.5×10−4) for FEV1, 12 (4% of 331; p<1.5×10−4) for FVC and 17 (6% of 269; p<1.9×10−4) for FEV1/FVC (supplementary table E5 and supplementary figure E2). Of the 34 proteins, 25 were relevant to a single lung function measure, while nine were relevant to multiple measures.

Associations with COPD

When we examined whether the 1091 lung function-associated aptamers were related to COPD (2723 cases and 13 052 non-cases) in our data (top 30 in table 5; all results in supplementary table E6), 187 aptamers, representing 179 proteins, were significantly related to COPD after multiple testing correction (p<4.6×10−5). These proteins overlap more with our findings for FEV1/FVC (30%) than those for FEV1 (16%) and FVC (14%). As expected, proteins associated with higher lung function were linked to a lower risk of COPD (supplementary figure E3).

Validation of our findings in independent data

Notably, our significant associations were confirmed in independent blood proteome data using different proteomic assays. In the large UKB Olink proteomics dataset (34 019 European ancestry participants) [25], 53% of our significant proteins were available. After multiple testing correction, significant protein–lung function associations among these overlapping proteins were replicated for 77% of FEV1, 72% of FVC and 57% of FEV1/FVC associations (supplementary table E7 and supplementary figure E4). In the SomaScan 1.3K platform data (QBB; 1955 Middle-East Arabians), 349 of our significant aptamers were available. In this much smaller population, after multiple test correction replication was seen for 27% of associations with FEV1, 13% for FVC and 1.5% for FEV1/FVC (supplementary table E8 and supplementary figure E5). In a study using the SomaScan 1.3K platform (4566 predominantly White participants) [10], among our findings available for look-up, 16% for FEV1, 25% for FVC and 3% for FEV1/FVC were significant after multiple testing correction (supplementary table E9). Notably, five of our FEV1-related proteins showed associations with FEV1 decline in that data (p<2.02×10−4): FSH, leptin, CSRP3, VEGF and IL-1R4 (supplementary table E10).

Additionally, we validated our findings in a COPD study of the lung proteome (150 lung samples, including 100 cases) using mass spectrometry [27]. Of our significant proteins, 11 were available for look-up, and four were related to COPD after multiple testing correction: ferritin, ferritin light chain, histone H3.1 and serine/arginine-rich splicing factor 6 (supplementary table E11).

Validation of previously identified biomarkers in our data

When we cross-referenced previously identified protein biomarkers of COPD outcomes [27–29] with our results, we had 200 aptamers (representing 196 proteins) available. Of these, 119 (60%) aptamers were significantly associated with at least one lung function measure after multiple testing correction (p<0.00025=0.05/200) (supplementary table E12). Additionally, we validated 18 of the 35 proteins previously related to computed tomography-based percent emphysema [30] after multiple testing correction (p<0.0014=0.05/35) in our data (supplementary table E13).

Robustness of findings

Additional adjustment for weight or body mass index (BMI) had minimal impact on our findings (supplementary table E7 and supplementary figure E6). Specifically, the associations between metabolism-related proteins such as leptin, IGFBP-2 and adiponectin and lung function measures remained significant after the adjustment, with the exception of leptin in relation to FEV1/FVC. Similarly, additional adjustment for a polygenic risk score [3] did not influence our findings (supplementary figure E7). We also confirmed that associations between proteins and lung function were minimally impacted by additional adjustment by estimated glomerular filtration rate, used as a measure of kidney function (supplementary figure E8). Of our significant associations, only 3% had p-value for heterogeneity <0.05 after multiple testing correction (supplementary table E2), suggesting minimal heterogeneity despite the diverse characteristics of the participating studies.

Associations by ancestry

We identified 831 aptamers (803 proteins) related to at least one lung function measure in 14 737 individuals of European ancestry (p<1×10−5) (supplementary table E4). Of these, 31% were significant in African ancestry and 10% were significant in Asian ancestry after multiple testing correction. As expected, fewer associations were significant in the much smaller datasets of African ancestry (3041 individuals, 235 aptamers, representing 222 proteins) (supplementary table E14) and Asian ancestry (3045 individuals, 39 aptamers, representing 37 proteins) (supplementary table E15). However, 93% of the African ancestry findings and 87% of the Asian ancestry findings were significant in the larger European ancestry results after multiple testing correction. Effect estimates were highly correlated across ancestries (Pearson correlation >0.89) (supplementary figure E9). Notably, 18 associations were ancestry specific (p-value for difference by ancestry <0.05/4979): 12 for European ancestry, five for African ancestry and one for Asian ancestry.

Discussion

In this first large-scale multi-ancestry meta-analysis interrogating circulating proteins in relation to spirometric measures of lung function, 1055 proteins were significantly differentially abundant in relation to these parameters. Pathways related to inflammation, organismal injury and lung development were enriched among the significant proteins. Notably, our findings include protein biomarkers identified in studies of lung function or COPD. More importantly, we provide circulating proteins not implicated in previous studies as biomarkers potentially useful in diagnosing and monitoring respiratory conditions. This comprehensive investigation of circulating proteins deepens our understanding of the pathophysiological processes underlying impaired lung function.

Many of the proteins we identified have not been previously implicated in genetic, epigenome (specifically methylation) or proteome studies of lung function [3, 4, 10, 27]. Notably, only 11% of our significant proteins, including SREC-II:ECD, LTBP4 and SOCS-3, have been previously implicated in the large genetic and/or epigenetic studies of lung function [3, 4]. The many proteins not implicated in those studies [3, 4] include SVEP1:Sushi 15–18 and ATL2. These two proteins are known to play a role in tissue development and remodelling that are crucial in the pathogenesis and progression of lung function impairment [31, 32]. The lack of overlap with larger studies using other genomic modalities highlights the strength of examining the circulating proteome to identify novel associations.

The circulating proteome reflects current health status, highlighting the importance of understanding the potential utility of plasma proteome analytes as clinical biomarkers in pulmonary health, especially given that access to lung tissue is limited. The validation of previously reported biomarkers of COPD outcomes in our lung function data underscores the potential of the proteins we identified as promising candidates for biomarkers of various lung conditions. By leveraging data from the Human Protein Atlas, we gained valuable insights into protein expression in the lung. Proteins not expressed in the lung, including CRP, play broad systemic roles. These proteins include known biomarkers reflecting the body's response to inflammation and infection. Conversely, circulating proteins also expressed in the lung may have more direct implications for lung pathology, making them better candidates for biomarkers to monitor lung diseases. Proteins expressed in the lung are more likely to be involved in critical processes such as airway remodelling, lung inflammation or vascular regulation, which are central to lung function and disease. These include SVEP1:Sushi 15–18 and PTPRU. The implications for biological processes could open new avenues for clinical applications. Our findings can contribute to the acceleration of novel circulating biomarker development, thereby enhancing the management of respiratory health.

By exploring protein–protein interactions, we identified potential key orchestrators of lung function. For example, STAT3 emerged as one of the hubs in the network for FEV1. Interestingly, the effect size (−37 mL) and p-value (1.2×10−9) of STAT3 were modest compared to those of other significant proteins in our data in relation to FEV1. However, the network clearly illustrates the connections of this protein with other significant proteins, highlighting its crucial role in processes such as cell growth and differentiation, tissue repair and remodelling, and inflammation and the immune response [33]. Notably, the hub proteins highlight potential biomarkers of COPD outcomes or drug targets for lung diseases, including EGFR [28, 34].

By implementing MR, we identified candidate proteins with a potential causal role in lung function. Notably, some of these proteins, including MET, are linked to approved drugs. Crizotinib, approved for the treatment of lung cancer, targets MET, a key receptor in cell growth, survival and metastasis. These findings can have potential for identification of drug repurposing to improve lung function. However, we acknowledge the limitation of identifying therapeutic targets in peripheral circulation.

The relationship between metabolic state/obesity and COPD is complex, with some evidence suggesting a paradoxically beneficial effect of obesity in severe COPD, perhaps because reduced wasting reflects better clinical outcomes, and others reporting obesity associated with increased morbidity and mortality in moderate to severe COPD [35, 36]. Notably, our data highlight metabolism-related proteins such as leptin, IGFBP-2 and adiponectin as leading biomarkers, with associations significant even after adjusting for weight or BMI. This suggests that these protein–lung function associations in our data are largely independent of these factors. Our findings show adiponectin positively associated with COPD and negatively correlated with FEV1/FVC, consistent with earlier studies indicating higher plasma adiponectin levels in individuals with radiographically determined COPD [37]. Collectively, these results underscore the complex interplay between obstructive lung disease and circulating markers of metabolic state, warranting further investigation into the implications of these proteins and metabolic pathways in COPD pathogenesis. With growing interest in GLP-1 agonists as a therapeutic option for COPD management [38], future research examining metabolic pathways and related drugs in the management of respiratory disorders could be of interest and potentially offer a more holistic approach to COPD management; however, these aspects are beyond the scope of our study.

It is reassuring that many of our findings based on SomaScan technology were validated using a distinct proteomic measurement technology. In the large independent UKB population with the circulating proteome measured using the Olink platform, we validated 71% of available associations from our SomaScan findings. While both platforms are affinity-based and excel at detecting low-abundance proteins, studies have found the aptamer-based SomaScan assay to be more precise than the antibody-based Olink assay [39, 40].

This extensive investigation leveraging a large sample size of individuals in the general population, including those with lung function in the normal range, identified associations with numerous circulating proteins. The estimated effects on FEV1 associated with interquartile range differences in the significant proteins ranged from −10 mL to +9 mL, with a median effect of −3 mL (supplementary table 1). Given a median annual FEV1 decline of 22 mL from ageing [41] and declines of 7.4 mL per pack-year smoked in males and 4.4 mL in females [42], the per-protein effect sizes observed in our study, while modest, are non-trivial. We acknowledge that the protein abundance using SomaScan is relative quantification based on an arbitrary unit (relative fluorescence units). Therefore, the interpretation needs caution as these units do not directly translate to absolute protein concentrations, and the biological significance of these relative changes requires further validation. Our proteomic findings could enhance interpretation of the extensive data from genetic association studies of these traits.

As with other genomic studies we expect the percent variance explained by the significant associations to be minimal. In UKB, the percent variance explained by significant proteins ranged from 0.003% to 1.77% (median 0.14%; mean±sd 0.21±0.22%). Only nine proteins explained >1% of the variance of a lung function measure. The highest percentages explained were for FEV1 with leptin (1.77%) and FABP4 (1.39%).

This study has limitations. Measuring proteins in blood limits inference about protein levels in other tissues; however, we examined lung expression profiles using the Human Protein Atlas. The cross-sectional design restricts causal inference. We acknowledge the possibility of reverse causation [43]. MR analyses suggest potential causal roles for some proteins, but these findings are preliminary, and most proteins identified are likely biomarkers rather than causal agents. Although the sentinel cis-pQTL approach is less powerful than multi-instrument methods, it provides strong and direct evidence when the instrument is valid and it minimises the risk of horizontal pleiotropy [21]. We confirmed some associations using available longitudinal lung function data. The predominance of individuals of European ancestry (∼70%) limits generalisability and power to detect ancestry-specific effects. Lung function was assessed using pre-bronchodilator spirometry, consistent with previous large meta-analyses [3–5]. We analysed actual spirometric values adjusting for factors used in prediction equations rather than z-scores or percent predicted; these were highly correlated with percent predicted values using Global Lung Function Initiative equations (Pearson correlation >0.95 for FEV1 and FVC in ALHS). We acknowledge potential sources of heterogeneity in our data. Studies report moderate to high correlation between the serum and plasma proteome [44], and associations identified in AGES using serum were replicated in an independent plasma-based SomaScan 4K study [45]. To ensure comparability across SomaScan versions, scaling factors were applied to aptamers in SomaScan version 4.1 before analyses in CHS. SomaScan protein measures represent relative abundance following standardised calibration and normalisation procedures within each study [46]. Association results were combined using fixed effects inverse variance weighted meta-analysis [15], appropriate for discovery analyses assuming the true effect size is consistent across studies. Normalisation and scaling of proteome data within each study reduced within-study variability, and significant associations were robust and not driven by a single study. Given our focus on association discovery rather than clinical threshold development, any residual heterogeneity would likely bias results toward the null. Sex-stratified meta-analyses were not performed and warrant investigation in larger studies, and residual confounding by medication use cannot be excluded.

In summary, this comprehensive investigation identified numerous circulating proteins in relation to lung function. This study extends the current literature by increasing sample size, expanding the number of proteins, including diverse ancestry populations and implementing various approaches to highlight functional implications. We identified novel protein associations with lung function and COPD, including those relevant to previously reported biomarkers or existing drug targets. The results can be leveraged for the development of preventive and therapeutic strategies. This compendium of proteomic signatures can play a critical role in expanding our understanding of lung function in respiratory health.
