Electrocardiogram competency of Canadian emergency medicine residents: curriculum gaps and opportunities for improvement
Authors: Emanuel Mastrangelo, Mazen El-Baba, Catherine Patocka, Jesse T. T. McLaren
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveElectrocardiogram (ECG) interpretation is crucial for emergencies, alongside point-of-care ultrasound (PoCUS). A prior survey of Emergency Medicine program directors identified “must know” ECG diagnoses. But whether ECG interpretation is adequately promoted by the Royal College, included in structured curriculum, and learned by residents is unknown. We sought to identify curriculum gaps and opportunities for improvement.MethodsUsing constructive alignment framework, we conducted a multi-modal evaluation of Canadian Emergency Medicine residency program ECG education. For intended learning outcomes, we reviewed the college’s Entrustable Professional Activities, comparing ECG with PoCUS. For teaching and learning activities, we administered a national cross-sectional survey of program directors. For assessment, we surveyed residents on self-reported efficacy in interpreting “must know” ECG diagnoses.ResultsThere is no Royal College Entrustable Professional Activities for ECGs, and only 1 milestone compared with 9 for PoCUS. A majority (60%) of program directors reported no structured ECG curriculum, and most (57.4%) residents reported < 1 h of ECG self-study per month. There were 60% of program directors and 92% of residents who agreed they would benefit from structured teaching. Self-reported efficacy increased through residency, from 60% for PGY-2 to 90% for PGY-5. But there were gaps with low efficacy (e.g., for bifascicular block, failure to capture, Brugada pattern, and myocardial infarction in the presence of bundle branch block). PGY-5 residents reported autonomy for ventricular tachycardia, hyperkalemia, and acute myocardial infarction, despite known limitations from the literature of Emergency physicians.ConclusionECG curriculum gaps include lower emphasis from Royal College Entrustable Professional Activities compared with PoCUS, limited structured ECG curriculum, and little self-study. While self-reported efficacy increases through residency, there are perceived gaps for many “must know” diagnoses, and unperceived gaps with confidence that exceeds known practice variation. The majority of survey responses agree they would benefit from structured training.

Introduction

The electrocardiogram (ECG) is crucial for guiding the time-sensitive management of a variety of emergencies including arrhythmias [1], acute coronary occlusion [2], and metabolic emergencies [3]. Automated computer interpretations have high rates of error [4, 5], which can mislead emergency physicians [6], and artificial intelligence has its own limitations [7] so independent ECG interpretation is a core skill.

Emergency physicians often use ECG and point-of-care ultrasound (PoCUS) in conjunction for cardiac emergencies—including tamponade, pulmonary embolism [8], acute coronary occlusion [9], and cardiac arrest [10]. But, paradoxically, while PoCUS is a newer technology, it has been further developed for Emergency Medicine residents than ECGs. A decade ago, two national surveys of Canadian Emergency Medicine program directors identified essential resident competencies for ECG [11] and PoCUS [12]. Since then, programs have expanded PoCUS teaching including structured instructional material, minimum scan requirements, and designated PoCUS leads [13]; in addition, national certification has developed for PoCUS competency. But there has been no equivalent advances for ECG teaching.

The program director survey by Patocka et al. identified a list of ECG diagnoses that Emergency Medicine residents “must know” prior to completion of training [11]. Since then residency programs have transitioned to Competency by Design, based on Entrustable Professional Activities [14]. But it is unknown whether these adequately promote ECG competency, whether residency programs have a structured curriculum to teach ECGs, and whether residents feel confident in ECG interpretation.

We conducted a multi-modal evaluation of ECG training in Canadian Emergency Medicine residency programs, to identify curriculum gaps and opportunities for improvement.

Methods

Study design and participants

We conducted a national, multi-modal evaluation of ECG education in Canadian Royal College of Physicians and Surgeons’ Emergency Medicine residency programs using Biggs’ constructive alignment framework [15–17].

To evaluate intended learning outcomes, we reviewed the Entrustable Professional Activities: Emergency Medicine guide to extract ECG-related competencies and compared them with PoCUS [14]. Although different skills, ECG and PoCUS have comparable features: (1) rapid, non-invasive and repeatable images used to diagnose and risk stratify emergencies, (2) used in conjunction for assessing cardiac emergencies or undifferentiated unstable patients, (3) wide range in competencies from novice to expert, and (4) require emergency physicians to act on their own interpretations without real-time over-reading like radiology. The Emergency Medicine Specialty Committee has a list of Emergency Medicine Required Training Experiences including ECG and PoCUS [18], but the Entrustable Professional Activities document details key features, assessment plans, and milestones to achieve competency.

To evaluate teaching and learning activities, we distributed cross-sectional surveys to Emergency Medicine program directors and across Canada to ask about the structure, frequency, and format of ECG teaching within their programs. They were invited to provide qualitative descriptions of their educational approach, and whether residents would benefit from additional formal ECG training.

To evaluate assessment and feedback, we conducted a national, cross-sectional survey to FRCPC Emergency Medicine residents (PGY-2 to PGY-5) across Canada including methods of learning ECGs, whether they would benefit from more formal training, and self-reported efficacy of “must-know” ECG diagnoses. Surveys were distributed through CAEP program leads, program directors, and direct researcher contact. To encourage resident participation, all respondents were entered into a random draw for a $50 gift card. According to institutional policy, quality assurance and program evaluation activities and performance reviews, when used exclusively for assessment and improvement purposes, do not fall within the scope of Research Ethics Board Review (Tri-Council Policy Statement, Article 2.5).

Study survey

The first part of the resident survey included questions on how residents learn ECGs, and whether they would benefit from additional teaching. The second part was on specific ECG diagnoses. The program director survey by Patocka et al. identified 54 “must know” ECG diagnoses, grouped into 8 categories [11]. For ease of survey completion, this list was condensed to 20, with the goal being representation from all categories without being comprehensive (Table 1). The list was chosen by an emergency physician with expertise in emergency electrocardiography (JTTM) based on clinical significance, prevalence, and interpretive difficulty. For example, diagnoses not selected because of ease of interpretation included sinus bradycardia, sinus tachycardia, atrial fibrillation, atrial flutter, atrial flutter with 3:1 conduction, atrial flutter with variable conduction, RBBB, and LBBB. Diagnosis not chosen for based on low clinical significance included normal axis, first degree atrioventricular (AV) block, premature atrial contraction, premature junctional contraction, and premature ventricular contraction. Clinically significant diagnoses not chosen included beta-blocker toxicity (no specific ECG sign) and reciprocal change (included in ischemia diagnoses). 20 ‘Must know’ ECG diagnoses

We evaluated self-perceived efficacy with analytic interpretation of the ECG rather than clinical accuracy because (1) prior studies have already assessed resident ECG accuracy and found it to be suboptimal [19–22]; (2) each of the “must know” ECG diagnoses has a wide variation of possible examples and clinical presentations, both ranging from subtle to obvious, and accuracy for the specific ECG example and clinical vignette chosen would not be generalizable to that diagnosis as a whole; and (3) asking residents to self-report efficacy helps identify and differentiate perceived gap (low reported efficacy) from unperceived gap (high reported efficacy out of proportion to the literature on Emergency physician practice patterns).

Self-assessed performance metrics based on a 4-point Likert scale commonly employed in Entrustable Professional Activities, which the Royal College uses and which residents are familiar with [23]. This includes the following: Intervention—requires staff/specialist to make diagnosis; Direction—requires substantial supervision or prompting to make diagnosis; Support—requires minimal support and/or staff approval to make diagnosis; Autonomy—can independently make the diagnosis.

Data analysis

Results were reported using simple descriptive statistics using Microsoft Excel, 2014.

Intended learning outcomes

There is no specific Entrustable Professional Activity for achieving competency in interpreting ECGs. There is one learning objective relating to ECGs under the umbrella of the first Entrustable Professional Activity: “recognizing the unstable/critically ill patient, mobilizing the healthcare team and supervisor, and initiating basic life support.” This Activity includes 14 milestones, one of which is “interpret electrocardiogram (ECG) recognizing conditions requiring immediate intervention, including but not limited to ischemia, or dysrhythmia.” The overall Activity requires at least one clinical presentation each of cardiorespiratory arrest and unstable dysrhythmia [14].

In contrast, not including a reference to ultrasound guidance for central venous access, there is a specific Entrustable Professional Activity as part of Core of Discipline (#14) on “performing and interpreting point-of-care ultrasound to guide patient management.” This includes nine separate milestones including image acquisition/interpretation, use of PoCUS vs. alternate diagnostic strategies, integrating information to develop a diagnosis, accurate record keeping, archiving images, communicating effectively, and consulting with others recognizing limitations. This requires 50 observations of achievement, including 5 each for many critical diagnoses [14].

Teaching and learning activities

There were responses from 10 programs (62.5%), and a majority (60%) reported no structured and longitudinal ECG curriculum. Program directors ranked how residents learn ECGs, in decreasing order: (1) clinical exposure, (2) self-study, (3) unstructured academic day teaching, and (4) structured longitudinal ECG education. There were a range of written responses describing their curriculum, from “monthly ECG teaching sessions at academic day with a structured curriculum,” to “learn on shift, unstructured time at academic half day.” There were 60% of program directors who agreed residents would benefit from additional formal, longitudinal ECG training. This included 4/6 of those who responded their programs do not have structured, longitudinal ECG teaching, and 2/4 of those who responded their programs already have such teaching.

Assessment and feedback

There were 66 resident responses from 8 sites, representing 36% of total eligible contacted participants. Respondents were 38% PGY-2, 29% PGY-3, 16% PGY-4, and 17% PGY-5. In response to how they learn critical ECG diagnoses, residents ranked how they learn, in decreasing order, the same as program directors: (1) clinical exposure, (2) self-study, (3) unstructured academic day teaching, and (4) structured longitudinal ECG education. A majority (57.6%) reported < 1 h of ECG self-study in any given month, and 92% agreed they would benefit from structured, analytic ECG training. There were a range of written responses, from “I think this is a major gap in our training,” to “I no longer feel I need to study this topic formally post Royal College exam.”

Discussion

Interpretation of findings

Using Biggs’ constructive alignment framework [15], our multi-modal evaluation of national ECG competency found multiple gaps and opportunities for improvement. Starting with the intended learning outcomes, while both ECG and PoCUS are listed as Emergency Medicine Training Experiences, there is no Entrustable Professional Activity specific for ECG interpretation and only one milestone, positioned early in training, while PoCUS has a clearly defined Entrustable Professional Activity embedded within the core stage with multiple milestones. In evaluating teaching and learning activities, a majority of surveyed program directors reported no formal ECG curriculum and reliance on clinical exposure and self-study. In evaluating resident assessment and feedback, the resident survey identified little time for self-study and highlighted both perceived and unperceived gaps.

Comparison to previous studies

Although ECG interpretation may be seen as a daily workplace-based learning activity, Patocka et al. found a decade ago, “although trainees encounter a significant number of the important diagnoses during their clinical rotations in the ED, it is unlikely that each trainee would encounter all of the diagnoses identified in this study…Educators can use the list to identify components that are routinely not well-addressed by practice-based education” [7]. Using this list, we found that self-reported autonomy predictably increased through residency [20, 22], but that residents across all levels of training reported low efficacy for multiple critical ECG diagnoses—including bifascicular block, failure to capture, Brugada pattern, and acute MI with bundle branch block. While a prior US survey of program directors found 96% were comfortable with Emergency Medicine residents’ abilities to interpret ECGs by the third year [24], we found only 47.1% of PGY-3 had self-perceived autonomy for these critical diagnoses and 12% felt the need for substantial supervision for these ECGs. These ongoing gaps relate to the reliance on daily clinical exposure and self-study. Viljoet et al. found that learning ECGs through clinical exposure is “largely opportunistic and haphazard” and “should not rely on experiential learning alone, but instead be supplemented by other formal methods of instruction” [25]. Most residents reported spending less than an hour per month on ECGs, and Mahler et al. found self-directed learning is less effective than ECG workshops or lectures [26].

With low Entrustable Professional Activity expectations, limited curriculum and little time for self-study, it is surprising that PGY-5 reported autonomy for so many challenging ECG diagnoses, along with a written response that no further ECG education is required after residency exam. This may reflect selection bias from a low response rate, or this may represent overconfidence that mirrors their supervising attending physicians [27]. For example, 100% of PGY-5 residents reported autonomy for monomorphic VT, hyperkalemia, and MI without bundle branch block of any territory. However, studies of practicing emergency physicians have found only 82% accuracy for wide complex tachycardias [1]; delayed cath lab activation from not recognizing subtle signs of occlusion [2]; and delayed treatment for severe hyperkalemia from not recognizing ECG changes [3]. While self-reported autonomy may apply to traditional STEMI criteria, the recent American College of Cardiology expert consensus on chest pain in the ED warns that ST-Elevation MI (STEMI) criteria “will miss a significant minority of patients who have acute coronary occlusion” and that physicians need to look for subtle ECG signs of vessel occlusion [28]. There is proposed paradigm shift from STEMI to Occlusion MI [29], which creates the need for advanced ECG training for Emergency residents and physicians [30].

Strengths and limitations

Strengths include a multi-modal evaluation of intended learning outcomes, residency program curriculum, and both perceived and unperceived resident self-reported efficacy.

Limitations include a low survey response rate, and two-thirds of resident responses were from second to third-year residents, which limits generalizability. This is common for cross-sectional studies of Canadian residents [31] and could also reflect the low perception of ECGs as a core skill. Despite this, survey responses included 10 programs from across the country, and residents from junior to senior, which provides a general overview for the purposes of identifying gaps and opportunities for improvement. For ease of survey completion, we did not include a comprehensive list of ECG diagnosis. There are important ECGs from the Patocka study [11] that were not included (e.g., long QT). In addition, while this 2015 study included some ‘STEMI equivalents’ (e.g., acute MI with left bundle branch block, posterior MI, and Wellens syndrome) this list has expanded this then [29], which emphasizes the need for residency programs to update curriculum to include evidence-based ECG advances.

Education implications

Starting with the intended learning outcomes, the absence of explicit ECG competencies creates an opportunity for improvement. Updating ECG competencies would “(i) establish objective benchmarks for trainees and practicing clinicians, (ii) guide educators in curriculum development, and (iii) support learners in defining meaningful, transferable educational goals” [32]. Many of the existing PoCUS Entrustable Professional Activities could be adapted to ECG: recognizing acquisition errors [33], integrating ECG into diagnostic decision making [34], and multiple observations of achievement for each critical diagnosis. ECG competency should also include knowledge of the strengths and limitations of artificial intelligence interpretation and how to apply it in clinical context. As with PoCUS [35], artificial intelligence applied to ECGs does not replace education but requires integration to identify its advantages, limitations, and clinical use. As the Canadian Journal of Cardiology noted, “it will be most beneficial if AI-related topics are formalized in curricula…not only to train but also need to get feedback from seasoned clinicians and follow-up from outcomes and erroneous interpretations” [7]. A large majority of resident responses agreed they would benefit from additional ECG teaching, and there are multiple evidence-based approaches available [36].

Research implications

As with other Canadian national resident surveys [37], we evaluated self-perceived efficacy rather than clinical accuracy. The perceived and unperceived gaps we found could be used to assess accuracy, stratified by multiple examples of each diagnosis.

Conclusions

We found that Royal College Entrustable Professional Activities de-emphasize ECG compared with PoCUS, Emergency Medicine program directors report limited ECG curriculum, and residents reported many perceived and unperceived gaps in competency. This provides opportunities for improvement—including updating ECG competencies and expanding formal ECG curriculum.
