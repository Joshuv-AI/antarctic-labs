Comparing daily step counts between a hip and wrist-worn device in adults with Parkinson's disease
Authors: Madison S Smith, Kimberly J Waddell, Stephanie Wood, James F Morley
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionWearable devices are increasingly used for measuring physical and physiological activity in daily life. Common appendicular symptoms of Parkinson's disease (PD), such as tremor and rigidity, could impact the accuracy of step counts when the device is worn in different body locations. Few studies have compared wrist- and hip-worn activity monitoring devices worn simultaneously in the home environment among adults with PD. The purpose of this study was to examine the agreement in daily step counts between two identical devices worn simultaneously at the wrist and hip in patients with PD.MethodsTwenty-one individuals with PD wore Fitbit Inspire 2 devices simultaneously on the wrist and hip during waking hours for 7 days. Total step counts from both devices were obtained for each day. The agreement between devices was examined using an intraclass correlation coefficient. A Bland-Altman plot was used to visually examine agreement.ResultsThe mean (SD) step count collected by the wrist-worn devices was 5221 (4345) and 5371 (3936) for the hip-worn devices, with an average difference of 150 steps. There was good overall agreement between the two devices with an intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) of 0.84 (95% CI 0.78, 0.88), which was visualized with the Bland Altman plot.ConclusionsIn an independently ambulatory PD population, wrist- and hip-worn Fitbit devices showed good agreement when measuring daily steps, with the strength of agreement varying by overall step activity. These findings support the use of wrist worn activity devices in PD and their utility for capturing physiological metrics beyond step count, such as heart rate, activity intensity, and sleep quality, which cannot be captured with a hip-worn device.Keywords: Parkinson's disease, Physical activity, Wearable devices

Introduction

Wearable, consumer-grade activity devices have become widely available for monitoring health behaviors at home. An estimated 29% of all US adults reported using a wearable device for monitoring their health and activity in 2019 [1]. These devices can measure multiple health metrics including daily steps, physical activity such as running or cycling, heart rate, sleep quality and duration, blood oxygen saturation (SpO2), and breathing rate [2]. The use, validity, and acceptance of wearable devices among older adult populations is well-established [[3], [4]]. Wearable devices facilitate continuous monitoring of clinically relevant metrics within the home environment, which may reduce the frequency of in-office visits and improve the availability of health data for clinicians [[3], [5]].

Wearable devices have been used in Parkinson's disease (PD) to quantify health behaviors that may be impacted by motor and non-motor symptoms [[5], [6]]. For example, exercise and physical activity are well-known to improve symptoms in PD, yet participation in these activities declines with disease progression [4]. Several studies have established the reliability and validity of commercially available devices for quantifying daily steps and used these devices to track physical activity in PD populations [[5], [7], [8], [9], [10]]. Additional metrics including moderate to vigorous physical activity (MVPA), which is thought to be the most effective form of exercise, use factors such as heart rate which require the device to maintain continuous skin contact. Wearing an activity device on the wrist is common in the general population. However, motor symptoms of PD, including tremor, rigidity, and dyskinesia could potentially interfere with readings from the wrist. For example, a device may inflate step counts if the device is worn on a wrist where there is tremor or dyskinesia, or underestimate based on poor arm swing, though these possibilities have not been directly examined.

Prior studies have compared the reliability, validity, and accuracy of different wearable devices to each other but there has been little research to determine the agreement between the same device worn at different locations on the body in the home, not laboratory, environment [7], [8], [9], [10]. Before wearable devices can be most effectively applied in clinical and research settings for PD, there must be a better understanding of how recording may vary by location. The purpose of this study was to compare daily step counts from two Fitbit Inspire 2 devices worn simultaneously on the hip and wrist over a 7-day period in an independently ambulatory PD population.

Methods

This was a secondary cohort analysis using data from a clinical trial examining the effect of gamification on daily steps for Veterans with PD. The primary trial assigned participants a step goal 20% above their baseline average, which they were encouraged to meet through a point-based game [11]. The primary trial included a 6-week intervention period and a 4-week follow-up period. For this study, enrolled participants were offered the opportunity to extend their trial enrollment by one week after the follow-up period to participate in the dual device study. Step counts from the extended week that was not part of the primary trial were used in this analysis. Potential participants were identified using data from the VA Corporate Data Warehouse (CDW). The VA CDW is a data repository that includes electronic health record data for the Veterans Health Administration. We identified all Veterans who had a confirmed diagnosis of idiopathic PD (ICD-10 codes) and mailed an invitation to participate in the trial. Interested individuals contacted the study team via telephone, completed an eligibility screen, and provided oral informed consent. The primary trial and this study were approved by the Corporal Michael J. Crescenz VA Medical Center Institutional Review Board and all participants provided informed consent.

Individuals were eligible to participate in the trial, and therefore this study, if the following eligibility criteria were met: 1) a clinical diagnosis of PD, confirmed using the ICD-10 code for PD, 2) on a stable PD medication regimen, 3) no prior history of a fall resulting in hospitalization or medical visits within 6 months, 4) resided within Veterans Integrated Service Network 4, which comprises 5 states (all counties in Pennsylvania and Delaware, partial counties in Ohio, New York, West Virginia, and New Jersey). Individuals were excluded from the trial if they: 1) had a diagnosis of an atypical parkinsonian syndrome, 2) used an assistive device when walking or unable to walk outside of the home, 3) received medication that could cause parkinsonian symptoms, 4) had medical comorbidities that precluded participation in a physical activity intervention, 5) did not have daily access to a smartphone or tablet that was compatible with the Fitbit application, 6) had a diagnosis of dementia, or 7) had a diagnosis of an active impulse control disorder.

Step tracking and collection

Eligible participants were issued two Fitbit Inspire 2 devices, one with a wrist strap to be worn on the least affected wrist and a belt clip for the other to be worn on the ipsilateral hip. Participants were instructed to wear both devices during waking hours. For the hip-worn device, which was the location used for the primary trial, participants downloaded the Fitbit smartphone application to their personal device and connected their Fitbit account to the Way to Health platform. Way to Health is a web-based digital health platform that automates daily step data collection and was used in the primary trial. Every time the participant opened the smartphone application on their personal device, Way to Health automatically obtained the most recent step count for each day. For the wrist-worn device, the study team issued each participant a tablet that was pre-loaded with the Fitbit application and a study-issued Fitbit account. Participants were instructed to sync their step data at least once a day by opening the Fitbit application on the provided tablet. A research team member retrieved the daily step counts from the wrist-worn device remotely by accessing the study-issued account through the Fitbit website. The Way to Health platform can only connect to one Fitbit device per participant, so the use of a tablet allowed for remote data capture and did not require participants to self-report their daily steps. Overall, participants wore both the hip- and wrist-worn devices simultaneously for 7 days, and there was no self-reporting of step counts for either device.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were summarized using descriptive statistics. Agreement between devices was examined using three approaches. First, the mean daily step count was calculated for the hip and wrist-worn devices, and the difference between these mean values was calculated. Second, we calculated an intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) using a two-way agreement model to evaluate the consistency between devices. Lastly, a Bland-Altman plot was created to visualize the difference in daily steps between the two devices as a function of the step average. We tested for proportional bias by fitting a linear regression model where the difference in daily step counts between the two devices was predicted by the mean of the devices. Lastly, to investigate whether daily physical activity levels influence device agreement, we dichotomized the sample using the overall mean of daily steps. We evaluated the ICC for both the below-average and above-average groups to explore how device agreement potentially varied across different levels of daily activity.

Results

A total of 21 participants were included in the analysis. The mean age (SD) of participants was 73.9 (6.7) years, with 95.2% of participants being male, and a mean disease duration in years (SD) of 4.9 (3.2) years. Less than half of the sample (n = 10, 47.6%) self-reported prior experience using a wearable device to monitor their health (Table 1).

The mean (SD) step count for wrist-worn device was 5221 (4345), and the mean (SD) for hip-worn device was 5371 (3936), with a mean difference of 150 steps. The overall ICC was 0.84 (95% CI 0.78, 0.88). Results from the linear regression model demonstrated a significant, negative proportional bias (−0.11, t = −2.2, P = 0.03). Because the assumption of constant bias was violated, we used regression-based, sloped limits of agreement using the model parameters and the residual standard error for the Bland-Altman plot. The upper limit of agreement was 4715.8 steps and the lower limit of agreement of −4415.8 steps. The Bland-Altmann plot (See Fig. 1) visually demonstrated no reliable pattern of over or underestimation of step counts by either Fitbit and their respective locations. The overall mean daily step value was 5130 steps. The ICC between the two devices for participants whose mean daily steps was below the overall mean (n = 13) was 0.41 (95% CI 0.22, 0.56, P < 0.001). For those whose mean daily steps were above the overall mean (n = 8), the ICC was 0.88 (95% CI 0.78, 0.94, P < 0.001).

Discussion

The modest difference between the mean step counts, high overall ICC, and Bland-Altmann plot visualization are indicative of good agreement between a wrist and hip-worn Fitbit device for recording daily steps. These findings indicate that wrist and hip-worn wearable devices can provide comparable measurement of daily steps in an independently ambulatory PD population who are able to safely ambulate outside of their home without the use of an assistive device. Importantly, this level of agreement suggests that tremor, dyskinesia, and rigidity may not appreciably interfere with collecting step count data from a wrist-worn device but should be further evaluated in future work with larger sample sizes. These findings add to a growing literature on the utility of wearable devices for tracking daily activity in people with Parkinson's disease, while offering new evidence regarding the influence of body placement on step counts [7], [8], [9], [10].

While the small sample size and predominantly older male veteran population of this study may limit generalizability, these findings indicate that wrist-worn devices can record daily steps in independently ambulatory people with PD, which is relevant for both clinical and research settings. Moving forward, this creates opportunities for quantifying other metrics, such as heart rate, sleep, or heart rate variability that can only be reliably measured when the device contacts skin, such as on the wrist. Heart rate is a particularly important metric as it is the most reliable way to quantify MVPA, which has an important role in overall health and symptom mitigation for adults with PD. [12]

Although the overall sample demonstrated good inter-device agreement, our exploratory findings revealed that this agreement may be modified by daily activity levels. The significant negative proportional bias and wide sloped limits of agreement, coupled with the variable strength of agreement between devices among those whose daily activity was below the overall mean compared to above the overall mean underscores the nuances of daily activity monitoring in PD populations. These results should be interpreted with caution, however, given the small sample size. Nevertheless, this discrepancy in agreement may be due to motor symptoms such as bradykinesia or slowed gait. Given the remote nature of this study, we had limited ability to examine the relationship between traditional measures of disease severity (UPDRS, tremor versus gait predominance) and step counts. Future research should stratify results by symptom severity, particularly tremor scores, as tremor-related movements at the wrist could influence the accuracy of recorded step counts. Examining these disease-related factors, such as disease duration, motor symptoms, and medication profile and timing, would further clarify and generalize the reliability of wrist-worn devices in the PD population.

Together with existing literature, these findings support the use of wearable devices in clinical practice to monitor free-living activity levels or to track change in response to provider recommendations or behavioral interventions. While the present study provides evidence regarding internal consistency, further research is needed to validate device accuracy using standardized measures and generalize to the broader PD population.

Conclusion

Wearable devices used for recording step counts provide comparable readings in an independently ambulatory PD population when the device is worn on either the hip or the wrist. The degree of agreement, however, was varied by daily activity levels that suggests a hip and wrist worn device may not be purely interchangeable in PD populations with low daily activity levels. These findings expand the use of these devices in this population without limitations as to where the device can be worn.
