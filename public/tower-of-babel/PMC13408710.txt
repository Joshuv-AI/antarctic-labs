Expertise-driven differences in drummers’ wrist-muscle motor unit behaviour
Authors: Steffen A Herff, Gerard Breaden Madden, Scott Beveridge, Eckart Altenmüller, Hans-Christian Jabusch
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn order to improve their performance, musicians devote thousands of hours to practising their instrument. However, how practise shapes the behaviour of muscles involved in music-making is not yet fully understood. Using surface electromyography, we report the muscle spike shapes of twenty-two drummers with different levels of playing experience, who performed a uni-manual isochronous drumming task at five different tempi. We used Bayesian mixed effects models to predict the mean (i) Spike Frequency, (ii) Spike Duration, and (iii) Number of Peaks per Spike in the Flexor Carpi Ulnaris and Extensor Carpi Radialis wrist/forearm muscles, and how these parameters vary with expertise (Cumulative Life Practice time; CLP, in hours). As to be expected, participants with more drumming experience showed better overall technical drumming performance (i.e., less variable timing) and less strike velocity asymmetries between hands. Furthermore, higher CLP – mediated by muscle, arm, and tempo – predicted greater Mean Spike Frequency, shorter Mean Spike Duration, and fewer Peaks per Spike. These findings suggest that the long-term training undertaken by individuals involved in drumming may contribute to changes in muscle activation patterns which are characterised by overall more efficient muscle usage. The results provide new insights with respect to practice-related muscle activation adaptation in drumming.Keywords: Drummers, Expertise, Physiology, Muscle Activation Adaptation, Surface Electromyography, sEMG, Spike ShapeSubject terms: Physiology, Psychology

Introduction

It is well established amongst musicians that both the quantity and quality of practice can directly improve performance1–3. Such improvements can be seen with respect to artistic4,5, cognitive6–8, perceptual9–11 and physiological12–16 measures, and also in terms of motor control17,18. However, despite the many benefits of practice, playing-related pain and injury is quite common in professional musicians19–25 and is often traceable to the repetitive motor actions undertaken during musical practice. In this sense, understanding practice-related adaptation of muscle physiology is valuable in order to support musicians in developing high level motor control whilst reducing exposure to risk factors of pain or injury.

In a seminal study, Fujii et al26 used Surface Electromyography (sEMG) to investigate the muscle activation patterns in the wrist/forearm muscles of the “World’s Fastest Drummer”, an individual capable of wrist tapping movements above 10Hz, considerably faster than the maximum tapping frequency in the general population (5-7Hz)27. In that study, the authors described the characteristics of these muscle activation patterns in terms of the shape of the muscle spikes (i.e., upward- and downward deflections of the muscle waveform, both of which cross the zero baseline) during contractions of the Flexor Carpi Ulnaris (FCU) and Extensor Carpi Radialis (ECR) muscles. Spike-shape analysis of sEMG signals can provide valuable insight into the properties of different motor unit activity patterns in terms of firing rate, recruitment, and synchronisation28–32. In their study, Fujii et al26 focused on three discrete yet related spike shape descriptors; (i) Mean Spike Frequency; MSF, (ii) Mean Spike Duration; MSD, and (iii) Mean Number of Peaks per Spike; MNPPS.

(i) MSF is defined as the number of spikes divided by the duration of the muscle contraction29. A higher MSF generally indicates a higher firing rate of motor units, which leads to stronger muscle contractions33,34. Essentially then, a higher MSF is indicative of greater muscle force production, which tends to reduce when muscles are fatigued35,36. (ii) MSD describes the average duration of spikes during a muscle contraction. A shorter MSD is generally associated with both a higher motor unit firing rate and an increase in motor unit recruitment. When muscles become fatigued, the MSD tends to become longer, although is generally less affected by effort35. (iii) MNPPS describes how many peaks on average flank each spike during a muscle contraction. Fewer peaks per spike generally indicate more synchronised firing amongst motor units, and high motor unit synchronisation may afford rapid force development37. As effort or strain increases, so too does MNPPS, although it is less affected by muscle fatigue35. The World’s Fastest Drummer showed significantly higher MSF, shorter MSD, and fewer peaks per spike compared to groups of non-drummers and ordinary drummers26. These qualities suggest that this individual possesses exceptionally efficient motor unit firing rate, recruitment and/or synchronisation. Such patterns indicate that motor units may be discharged in such a way as to generate rapid striking force whilst keeping striking actions precise and economical. This kind of activation supports sustained, high-speed performances, and may help delay fatigue by avoiding unnecessary muscular effort.

Although the observed differences in muscle behaviour of the World’s Fastest Drummer may simply reflect a unique individual physiology, an alternative possibility is that increasing quantity of practise contributes to muscle activation adaptation, in turn leading to observable differences in muscle spike-shapes. This premise gains credibility when considering other evidence of practise-related muscle activation adaptation both in drummers and in musicians of other instrument families. For example, inefficient co-contraction of the FCU and ECR muscles in drummers tends to decrease as expertise increases17. Pianists – compared to non-pianists – showed higher firing rates, greater amplitudes, and shorter activation in motor units of the first dorsal interosseous muscle during minimal muscle contractions in a study using indwelling EMG38. Further evidence of practise-related muscle activation adaptation comes from sports. For example, weight lifters who require rapid, strong muscle force output show greater motor unit synchronisation in their hands compared to untrained individuals39,40. Increased motor unit firing rate and recruitment with increasing expertise are commonly observed in additional activities such as sprinting, volleyball41, soccer42, and various strength exercises43,44. Nevertheless, so far it is unclear to which extent these findings generalise to a drumming context and whether increasing quantity of practise is predictive of spike-shape differences in drummers.

In this study, we used sEMG to investigate the relationship between activation patterns in muscle bursts in the FCU and ECR forearm muscles, and participants’ reported Cumulative Life Practice time (CLP; in hours, calculated from retrospective self-reported daily/weekly practice time at different age blocks). We characterised spike shape activation patterns through MSF, MSD, and MNPPS (analogous to Fujii et al., 201226) in participants with different levels of drumming experience, who performed single-handed isochronous eighth-note repetitive drum strikes at five different tempi (40, 80, 120, 160, 200 BPM, for a quarter note; 1.33, 2.67, 4.00, 5.33, and 6.67 strikes per second, respectively; drawn from a broader drumming task, see Methods).

Methods

This research used data collected but only partly analysed from prior work on electrophysiology and kinematics in drummers. Only the modules of that experimental procedure relevant to the current analysis are included here. Please refer to Beveridge et al.17 and Buck et al.18 for a complete description of all methods and materials used.

Ethical approval for this study was provided by the responsible institutional review board (see the section “Ethics Statement”) and the study adhered at all times to the declaration of Helsinki. After providing informed consent to take part, participants performed five different single-handed repetitive drumming tasks at five different tempi with both hands, respectively, and completed a questionnaire regarding their musical biography and self-reported handedness. During the drumming tasks, surface electromyography (sEMG) was used to record the activation of the Flexor Carpi Ulnaris (FCU) and Extensor Carpi Radialis (ECR) wrist/forearm muscles in both arms, respectively.

Participants

Twenty-two participants took part in this study (Male n = 19, Female n = 3; median age at the time of study = 25). All participants were required to have a minimum of one year playing experience, and all reported being musically active (e.g., playing in a band/ensemble, receiving and/or giving drum lessons, etc.). They reported a median of 5544 hours of Cumulative Life Practice Time (calculated from year-by-year daily/weekly practice times according to retrospective self-report, range = 506h to 15340h). According to the Edinburgh Handedness Inventory74, twenty-one out of the twenty-two participants were right-handed (one had a tendency for being ambidextrous). Eighteen participants reported playing at least one instrument secondary to drums. Piano was the most frequently reported (n = 14), followed by guitar (n = 8), bass guitar (n = 4), and others (n = 5). Additional self-reported experience-related variables indicated that participants commenced playing drums at a median age of 11.5 years (minimum/maximum = 4/22), typically practised a median of four days per week (min/max = 1/7), and engaged in approximately three hours of drumming practice on active days (min/max = 0.5/6).

Note, in the former studies which used these data17,18, participants were classified as either expert or amateur drummers based on minimum years of reported playing experience (specifically - minimum ten years for experts, minimum one year for amateurs). In the current study, we opted instead to use CLP as a continuous measure of expertise. As a measure of accumulated experience, CLP is well established as an important – albeit not exclusive – predictor of expertise2,75–77. This continuous approach allows for a clearer, more nuanced interpretation of results and a better understanding of how changes in CLP may affect spike shape descriptors whilst avoiding somewhat arbitrary thresholds to divide experts and amateurs. Other expertise-related variables were considered descriptively, but CLP was included as the sole analytical predictor because it offers the most interpretable measure of expertise.

Experimental setup and task

Participants sat on an adjustable drum stool in front of a drum practice pad (ProMar X-Pad). They were provided with an identical pair of drumsticks (Wincent 5B). All tasks were to be performed using the “matched grip” technique (a widely used technique in which the forearms are pronated and the sticks are held in the same way by each hand, between the index finger and thumb with the additional fingers wrapped around the stick for support, creating a matched appearance78). Participants were instructed to play at a consistent loudness, between 55 and 65 dB(A). This served as an indirect way to control for striking force. To help participants familiarise themselves with the required loudness, they first completed their own choice of warm-up, during which time visual feedback of the loudness of their playing was provided using a digital sound pressure level meter (PCE Instruments PCE-318). This visual feedback was removed during the actual task recording to provide room for a music stand, upon which an eighteen-bar drum exercise written in drum notation was presented to each participant. Guided by a metronome, each participant performed an eighth-notes exercise at 40, 80, 120, 160, and 200 beats per minute (BPM) for a quarter note, respectively. The respective desired eighth note repetition rates in each hand were 80, 160, 240, 320, 400 hits per minute (HPM). At these tempi, this corresponds to approximately 1.33, 2.67, 4.00, 5.33, and 6.67 strikes per second, respectively. Performing the entire drumming exercise at (for example) 160 HPM lasted approximately thirty-five seconds, which corresponds to a total playing duration of approximately four minutes and thirty seconds. Short breaks of approximately one or two minutes each were provided in between each performance of the exercise to avoid potential muscle fatigue. For every performance, each tempo was performed sequentially and in the same order for each participant. This was done to simplify the overall requirements of the task and limit the potential impact of transition effects when changing between tempo conditions. Indeed, to further protect against learning or transition effects, we included an intermediate filler condition which consisted of striking the drum pad with both sticks simultaneously, before proceeding to the next tempo single-handed striking phase. Data acquisition and processing

Muscle activity and sEMG

Muscle activity was recorded using a wireless sEMG system (Desktop DTS, Noraxon Inc). Electrodes (10mm diameter, inter-electrode distance = 20mm) were attached over the wrist flexor [Flexor Carpi Ulnaris (FCU)], and wrist extensor [Extensor Carpi Radialis (ECR)] muscles of both forearms, respectively. The sensors were aligned to the presumed muscle fibre direction following established anatomical placement guidelines79. The 20mm inter-electrode distance was selected according to standard kinesiological EMG recommendations80,81 as a commonly used compromise between signal amplitude, spatial selectivity, and potential crosstalk when recording dynamic contractions of adjacent forearm muscles. sEMG signals were sampled at 1,500 Hz and synchronized with an external 30 Hz SMPTE clock.

Raw sEMG signals (see Figure 2 for an example from a single-participant trace) underwent several processing steps prior to analysis, including zero-offset removal and band-pass filtering (low/high pass frequency, 5/500 Hz). Spike detection and feature extraction followed the definitions described in29 (see Section 5.4 for details). Next, the signals were normalised to a muscle-specific reference value represented by the Maximum Voluntary Contraction (MVC) of each muscle. The MVC constitutes the maximum sEMG output, and was calculated for the FCU and ECR in a “best of three” procedure involving isometric maximal contractions against a static object, with short rest intervals between contractions to minimise fatigue. For normalising, MVC amplitude was defined using a short moving window capturing the highest mean activation rather than a single peak, thereby reducing sensitivity to transient spikes. The expression of an sEMG signal amplitude relative to the MVC constitutes a scaling factor which reduces inter-participant differences in force production, as it represents activity as a fraction of each participant’s maximum. That being said, as muscle contraction level may influence spike amplitude independently of motor-unit behaviour, normalised MCV values do not exclude the possibility that differences in overall activation (e.g., lower activation in participants with greater CLP) may contribute to expertise-related differences in spike-shape values. For transparency, we report individual MVC values for all participants in the online supplement (https://osf.io/mt5za/).

Technical drumming performance

Participants’ technical performance on the drumming task is based on a Coefficient of Variance of the Inter-Tap Interval (CVITI). CVITI was calculated for each participant by dividing the SD of the ITI by the mean ITI for the strikes performed at each tempo, and then expressing this value as a percentage (CV = SD / mean 100). Smaller CVITI values represent greater temporal evenness, and vice versa. CVITI has been used in various other studies as a measure of technical drumming performance17,18,27,63,82–84.

Stick height and velocity

Kinematic information about drumstick movement was obtained using a six-camera 3D motion capture system (Qualisys, QTM, Sweden). As a full biomechanical analysis was not the primary focus of this research, we provide only a brief summary of the key processing steps relevant to the extraction of performance variables. For a complete description, please see Buck et al. (2021)18.

Each drumstick was fitted with three noncollinear retroreflective markers, enabling full six degrees of freedom (6DOF) tracking. Following initial calibration with temporary markers on the stick tips, virtual tip markers were created, allowing analysis of their vertical position and velocity. Virtual markers were required to avoid damage to the physical markers during the drum exercise. The vertical trajectory of each virtual stick tip was then used to derive the two kinematic variables reported in this study; stick height and stick velocity. Stick height was calculated by identifying the highest maximum vertical position reached by the virtual tip prior to each strike. Peaks were detected using an automated algorithm applied to the unfiltered displacement signal, and the resulting peak values were averaged across all strikes within a tempo condition. Stick velocity was calculated from the maximum downward vertical velocity of the tip in the 20 ms preceding each drum strike. To reduce high-frequency artefacts associated with impact, the velocity signal was smoothed using a short Savitzky–Golay window (10 ms). We included stick height and velocity as they reflect drummers’ control strategies and influence both impact force and the dynamics of a resulting drum sound.

Spike shape descriptors

In the context of sEMG signal analysis, a spike refers to a waveform which comprises an upward and downward deflection, both of which cross the zero baseline. This is differentiated from a peak, which is a pair of deflections which may or may not be part of a full spike. If a spike has only one peak, they are essentially the same thing. Spikes may, however, have multiple peaks. Please see Fujii and Moritani26 for a complete explanation.

We investigate the effect of expertise on three separate spike shape descriptors; Mean Spike Frequency (MSF), Mean Spike Duration (MSD), and Mean Number of Peaks per Spike (MNPPS). The calculation of each descriptor is described in detail in Gabriel et al29 and Fujii and Moritani63 and are briefly summarised below.

Mean spike frequency (MSF)

MSF indicates the frequency of spikes in an sEMG signal within a given time frame. It reflects the overall firing rate of motor units during contractions. It is calculated by dividing the total number of spikes (NS) by the duration of the relevant time frame (DR). See (1) below.

Mean spike duration (MSD)

MSD refers to the average time a spike in an sEMG signal takes to complete one full upward- and downward deflection from a baseline. It is calculated by measuring the time interval between the start () and end () of each spike, where i denotes the index of each spike within the analysis window. See (2) below.

Mean number of peaks per spike (MNPPS)

MNPPS refers to the average number of peaks within an sEMG signal. It is calculated by tallying the number of peaks (NP) within each spike and then averaging this count across the number of spikes (NS) within the relevant analysis window. See (3) below.

Statistical approach

We employed Bayesian mixed effects models using the brms package85 in R86 to model the collected data, and provide inference through directed Bayesian-hypothesis tests on the predictions of the fitted models using the marginal effects package87. This approach provides several advantages over classical frequentist statistics. It is particularly well-suited to modelling hierarchical data structures such as within-subjects designs, where trials are nested within participants (such as multiple strikes per participant, per tempo) and it allows us to incorporate prior information in a model in a principled manner88. Additionally, Bayesian models provide posterior distributions for every model parameter. From this, we can derive continuous evidence ratios for or against an effect in terms of the strength of evidence, rather than a binary threshold (as is the case with null-hypothesis testing). An additional advantage of this framework is that posterior predictions (e.g., mean ± SD) are normally distributed due to the inclusion of Gaussian identity links, meaning that assumptions of normality apply to model residuals rather than raw EMG-based observations. These advantages are increasingly recognised in the motor control and music psychology literature89,90, where Bayesian approaches have been successfully applied to capture within-subject variability and complex interaction effects.

All models ran on 4 chains across 4 cores with 5000 iterations and 2000 warmups each, initialised through zeros. Each model was provided with random intercepts for Participant and Strike number, as well as weakly-informative priors for both population-level effects (t(3,0,1)) as well as group-level effects (cauchy(0,1)). All continuous variables were scaled to have a mean of 0 and a standard deviation of 1. An equivalent model approach has been used in other studies on music psychology and music perception89–95

To determine whether Cumulative Life Practice Time (CLP, in hours) captured the construct of technical drumming performance in this study, we first used a mixed effects model predicting temporal evenness in the form of the Coefficient of Variance of the Inter-tap Intervals (CVITI). This prediction was based on the interaction between expertise, tempo, hand, muscle, and three spike shape descriptors: Mean Spike Frequency (MSF), Mean Spike Duration (MSD), and Mean Number of Peaks per Spike (MNPPS). We then evaluated whether expertise had a general effect on technical drumming performance. If CLP was predictive of CVITI, then we considered CLP to be a valid measure of expertise for our purposes. Note, here we were not concerned with whether spike shapes affect performance, but whether expertise affected performance as well as spike shapes, due to the physiological implications of the three spike shape descriptors considered here.

To investigate possible effects of expertise on the three spike shape descriptors, we deployed three Bayesian mixed effects models, each predicting the respective spike shape descriptors based on CLP and its interaction with tempo, hand, and muscle. Directional expectations were specified a priori, such that greater CLP was expected to be associated with higher MSF, shorter MSD, and fewer MNPPS.

To assess the outcome of these models, we report Bayesian-hypothesis tests which evaluate the Evidence Ratio (ER) of a given effect to be smaller or larger than zero (expressed as Odds( ) as well as the posterior probability, Post.Prob), the coefficient estimate ( ), and the error within each estimate (EE ). All Bayesian-hypothesis tests were specified as one-sided, reflecting our a priori expectations regarding expertise-related changes in spike shape descriptors. We considered ERs 19 (i.e., Post.Prob 0.95) as strong evidence for an effect45.

In a final step, we also report how large a difference in CLP (i.e., CLP) has to be for the fitted models to predict posterior probabilities to observe an expertise-related difference in the spike shape descriptors with either weak (Post.Prob 0.8), moderate ( 0.9), or strong ( 0.95) evidence. This approach allows us to relate the results to practical considerations, by providing a general idea of how large a difference in CLP may have to be to generate statistically observable differences in the spike shape of muscle activity.

Practice quantity did not predict strike velocity

As spike-shape descriptors may be impacted by the force an individual applies during isometric muscle contractions32, we first tested for systematic stick tip velocity difference based on CLP. We examined striking velocity because it constitutes a performance parameter that reflects motor control strategies, directly influencing the force of a stick impact and resulting sound dynamics. However, we observed no evidence for an overall difference in the final velocity as the stick tip hits the drum pad based on CLP ( 0.05, EE 0.01, Odds ( > 0) 6.97, Post.Prob 0.87).

Strike velocity asymmetry between hands decreased with greater quantity of practice

Next, we tested for striking velocity differences between the left and right hands. We identified strong evidence for an asymmetry in the striking velocity whereby the left hand generally produced faster final stick tip velocities compared to the right hand. ( 0.16, EE 0.01, Odds ( > 0) > 9999, Post.Prob 1). This asymmetry became smaller as CLP increased ( -0.08, EE 0.01, Odds ( < 0 ) > 9999, Post.Prob 1).

Higher stick tip heights predicted faster stick tip velocity

Furthermore, and as one would expect, greater stick tip height predicted greater final stick tip velocity ( 1.06, EE 0.01, Odds ( > 0) > 9999, Post.Prob 1). Additionally, we observed strong evidence that this relationship became stronger with increasing CLP ( 0.02, EE 0.01, Odds ( > 0) 28.93, Post.Prob 0.97).

Discussion

In this study, we showed that greater quantity of Cumulative Life Practice time (CLP) predicted better technical drumming performance (greater temporal evenness), reduced hand asymmetries in strike velocity, increased velocity gain by stick height, and was predictive of characteristic FCU and ECR wrist/forearm muscle activation patterns in participants actively involved in drumming. These relationships are complex, and we identified strong evidence in the majority of, but not all, hand-muscle-tempo combinations. Overall though, as CLP increased, there was a clear tendency towards more efficient muscle activation patterns, which may include changes in firing rate, recruitment, and synchronisation of motor units as measured by Mean Spike Frequency (MSF), Mean Spike Duration (MSD), and Mean Number of Peaks per Spike (MNPPS).

Firing rate and recruitment

With increasing CLP, we observed evidence for higher motor unit firing rates and increased motor unit recruitment at various tempi in the ECR-Left, FCU-Left, and FCU-Right muscles, as indicated in all cases by larger MSFs and shorter MSDs29,35. Higher motor unit firing rates and recruitment are generally associated with the possibility of generating more striking force46. The capacity for such additional force affords greater dynamic range by enabling experts to strike a drum with greater stick tip velocity. In addition, higher motor unit firing rates in more experienced participants may also facilitate more sustainable practice. This is demonstrated by the fact that whilst participants across the expertise spectrum can generate faster terminal stick velocity gains with increasing stick height, we observed this relationship to become much stronger with increasing expertise. This should not be taken to mean that more experienced participants simply hold the stick higher than those with less experience. In fact, we did not observe any evidence suggesting that CLP would predict higher stick height (all ER < 19, consistent with Buck et al.18). Instead, our findings suggest that when experts raise the stick by a given amount (for example, 10 cm), they may achieve a higher terminal stick velocity than beginners. In other words, following a “more bang for your buck” principle, experts may generate greater striking force, using the same increase in amplitude to produce a larger increase in striking velocity. This interpretation may shed light on how more experienced drummers may utilise movement amplitude more effectively and complements the prior observation that experts –when compared with beginners– perform a more ’whiplash’-like preparatory motion when hitting the pad47.

Expertise-related differences in firing rate and recruitment were more pronounced in the left hand than the right. One possible explanation is that virtually all of the participants were right-handed (one had an ambidextrous tendency). As expertise in a motor task develops, movement variability typically decreases47. Accordingly, the less-skilled left hand likely showed greater variability than the more-skilled right hand, particularly in less experienced participants. In the earlier stages of training, the left hand may therefore show increased movement variability associated with lower motor proficiency, which may contribute to greater inter-hand asymmetry in terms of stick tip velocity and strike velocity variability. Consistent with this interpretation, we observed smaller inter-hand differences in stick-tip velocity in more experienced participants, suggesting that skill-related asymmetries between the dominant and non-dominant hands may diminish with additional practice. As firing rate and recruitment optimise over time, the non-dominant hand appears to achieve striking velocities more similar to those of the dominant hand. To further explore this possible explanation, we conducted a post-hoc model predicting stick height based on expertise and hand. This model showed not only that less experienced participants tended to raise the stick higher with the non-dominant left hand than the dominant right (ER; Odds ( ) = 2999), but also that this asymmetry reduced as CLP increased (ER; Odds ( ) = 570.43).

Furthermore, the effects of expertise-related differences in firing rate and recruitment were particularly strong in the left hand. This explanation is consistent with evidence suggesting no sensorimotor disadvantages in the left hand amongst samples of right handed expert pianists and string players in standardised music-related motor tasks48,49. In addition, expertise-related effects were stronger in the FCU flexor compared to the ECR extensor muscle. This complements prior research which has shown that expert drummers engage the flexor muscle more than the extensor, and that flexor dominance predicts better drumming performance17,26,50. In addition, individuals with more drumming experience display a more effective usage of rebound compared to those with less experience17. This is an important skill in drumming as unnecessary use of force (i.e., in the ECR muscle), which can be a source of playing-related pain or injury51–55, may be avoided.

Taken together, these results suggest that muscle spike shapes change as CLP increases, adapting towards being more task-beneficial. Based on our conservative model (see Figures 8 and 10), such adaptation effects can be expected after an increase of approximately 3000 hours of practise (corresponding roughly to 20 hours of practice per week over a three-year period).

Synchronisation rate

With increasing CLP, we observed evidence for greater motor unit synchronisation at various tempi in the ECR-Left, ECR-Right, and FCU-Right muscles, as indicated by fewer peaks per spike (lower MNPPS)29,35. Greater synchronisation allows for increased muscle force generation during rapid contractions37,56 and as such, can be seen as a great asset for drummers. CLP-related differences in MNPPS were particularly pronounced at 160 and 200 BPM. These faster tempi require rapid movements and, thus, higher motor unit synchronisation is particularly useful in order to meet performance requirements. Fewer peaks per spike at higher tempi (see Figure 10), suggests that whilst all participants may already display increased synchronisation in these faster categories, those with more experience (0.5 SD above the mean CLP) may simply utilise motor unit synchronisation more efficiently compared to those with less experience (0.5 SD below the mean CLP).

Interestingly, we observed CLP-related differences in the MNPPS more so in the right rather than left hand. This contrasts with our findings in terms of MSF and MSD. One possible explanation is that fewer peaks per spike is a type of adaptation that, without any targeted training, only develops towards the higher end of the expertise spectrum. What we observed here may reflect the beginning of increased motor synchronisation with experts, evident earlier in the dominant hand.

Preliminary implications for the practising drummer

The present findings emphasise the importance of musical practice in economising muscular force when playing the drums. The insights gained here concerning muscle activation adaptation may reinforce the importance of practice, from a quantity-of-practice perspective. With this in mind, we modelled how large a difference in CLP must be in order to produce detectable changes in spike-shape activity patterns, in a specific muscle, arm, and at each given tempo. Whilst specific applications of our findings into drum pedagogy may be limited at present, we hope that, alongside expanding repertoire or learning new techniques, drummers of all experience levels understand that their muscles will undergo fundamental, measurable changes over their careers, changes that are associated with reduced variability and hand asymmetry, greater dynamic range, and greater resilience to fatigue. Drumming students may find it encouraging that, whilst changes may be gradual, they will ultimately happen.

At an electrophysiological level, our results show CLP to be a meaningful predictor of expertise. However, we did not examine practice-quality factors (e.g., time spent practising rudiments, attitudes toward practice, etc.). As more frequent practise of technical exercises and factors such as enjoyment of practice and enjoyment of music play a key role in improving musically-relevant motor skills in addition to practice quantity57, drummers should avoid blindly increasing practice time as this may pose a risk of muscle injury. Instead, our findings should be taken as demonstrating expertise-related muscle activation patterns that develop as part of regular musical practice. How to optimise practice to bring about such patterns is a question for future studies. With caution, conservative projections can be made regarding potential progress over time with respect to muscle activation adaptation on a group level. For the individual drummer, the time required in reality will of course vary based on a plethora of factors that we did not account for in this study.

Limitations and future directions

With respect to all MSF, MSD, and MNPPS findings discussed throughout the Firing Rate and Recruitment and Synchronisation Rate sections, our interpretations should be treated with caution. Although expertise-related differences in sEMG-based spike shape features may be consistent with motor-unit firing rate, recruitment, and synchronisation patterns, it is important to note that features may also vary with overall level of activation and/or antagonist muscular co-activation. As prior studies using spike-shape analysis have been typically applied to tasks involving isometric contractions32, this method has not been validated as an assessment of motor-unit behaviour in dynamic drumming tasks such as the kind described here. Accordingly, our findings should instead be understood as reflecting changes in neuromuscular activation more generally rather than as specific direct evidence of motor-unit mechanisms such as recruitment or discharge.

The design of the present study was cross-sectional rather than longitudinal, which means that causality, although possible, has not been established. Furthermore, the CLP analysis revealed relatively large practice-hours requirements to observe change in spike shapes features. That being said, the small number of participants tested here (n = 22), possessing a rather broad spread in CLP, contributed substantially to model uncertainty, which means the estimates of the CLP required to observe meaningful change in spike shapes are conservative. In reality, fewer hours may suffice to see meaningful muscle activation adaptation. It is also important to note that not all practise needs to be physical. Mental imagery exercises can complement physical exercises and have demonstrated beneficial effects on both performance and muscle development58–61.

There are reported inconsistencies between indwelling and surface electrodes in terms of spike-shape results62. Although indwelling electrodes may be ideal, this was not viable in the present study as there are ethical concerns in using these invasive methods during rapid movements due to an increased risk of injury. For this reason, we relied exclusively on sEMG to derive spike-shape measures. Although sEMG is subject to variability related to electrode placement and muscle fibre orientation, recent research indicates that surface spike-shape measures can reliably capture contraction levels in terms of recruitment, firing rate, and synchronisation32. The lower resolution and interference associated with sEMG are random rather than systematic, which would ordinarily make it harder to detect effects. That we nevertheless observed compelling evidence for expertise-related differences in MSF, MSD, and MNPPS suggests the presence of systematic physiological adaptations. Future studies may profit from using high density EMG to further confirm these effects.

Our spike analysis closely followed existing approaches26,29,63, including consistent transverse distal placement of EMG sensors on each muscle, for every participant. However, one limitation in this approach is that signal polarity, which depends on sensor placement and orientation, may vary due to individual differences in nerve innervation zone locations64. Rather than introducing systematic bias, such inconsistencies in polarity may add noise, reducing sensitivity to detect effects. Nevertheless, to explore the potential effects of signal polarity, we inverted the original EMG signals and recalculated all spike metrics. As expected (due to the zero-mean stochastic nature of motor unit action potentials65), our inverted spike-shape measures were statistically significantly correlated with the originals. Specifically, MSF (Spearmans’s , ) and MSD (, ) were almost perfectly correlated, and MNPPS showed a significant, albeit low correlation (, ). Whilst beyond the scope of the current research, future work could examine sEMG-MNPPS during de- and repolarisation phases to better understand polarity differences in task-specific motor unit behaviour.

Although the drum pad featured in our experimental setup is a common practice accessory and is designed to resemble a snare drum with respect to dimensions and handling, it is nonetheless not an actual acoustic drum. We therefore cannot exclude the possibility that this may have impacted participants’ movements with respect to stick rebound.

Looking ahead, the spike-shape descriptors investigated here lend themselves to be implemented in biofeedback systems. Such systems may be valuable as learning aids, particularly if combined with factors related to practice quality and content. Whilst such prospects are exciting, there are certain key considerations that should be addressed first. For example, although important work incorporating biofeedback into musical pedagogy has already taken place66–72, accessible and drumming-specific EMG systems remain limited. In addition, whether biofeedback of spike shape features provides the same advantages as that of other physiological measures73 is an empirical question that requires testing. An up-to-date systematic review focused on the efficacy of biofeedback systems and providing design recommendations is also urgently needed.

Conclusion

In this study, we investigated the relationship between cumulative life practice time and spike-shape descriptors of muscle activity in drummers who varied in experience. We found that greater cumulative life practice time predicted changes in several spike-shape features, which may reflect more efficient patterns of muscle activation. Although the pedagogical implications are preliminary, our findings emphasise the importance of practice quantity. We hope that our findings encourage researchers to incorporate practice quality measures alongside spike-shape analyses in future studies to provide drummers a rich source of information with which to optimise their practice.
