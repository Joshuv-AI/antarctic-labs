Evaluating a novel online acute oncology education and competence assessment passport in the UK
Authors: Gina Madera, Donna Munro, Leigh Hamilton, Helen Davies, Lorraine Petrie, Verna Lavender
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAcute oncology e-learning for staff who treat and care for people with cancer in the United Kingdom exists; however standardised, acute oncology competence assessment does not. This study reports the development and evaluation of a standardised acute oncology competence assessment integrated within an existing e-learning resource, which ultimately aims to improve cancer patient safety outcomes across the UK. The e-learning and assessment were developed for multidisciplinary staff working in any clinical setting where patients with cancer may present with urgent or emergency symptoms. For example, acute medical units, accident and emergency, same day emergency care, primary care and pre-hospital settings, such as paramedic services and general practice, as well an introductory resource for staff new to working in oncology.MethodsIn Phase 1, the study team multidisciplinary professionals and cancer-specialist educators from across the UK co-designed a Digital Competence Assessment for ‘practice Level 1’. An existing ‘Introduction to Acute Oncology E-learning Module’ was revised to include the novel Digital Competence Assessment (together the e-learning and competence assessment were termed: The Level 1 Acute Oncology Passport). This was piloted and evaluated across the UK during Phase 2. Quantitative data from pre (n = 376) and post (n = 176) Level 1 Acute Oncology Passport responses were analysed using descriptive and inferential statistics. Free-text responses were analysed using content analysis.ResultsThere was a significant increase in learners’ level of confidence about their acute oncology knowledge and skills pre and post e-learning and digital competence assessment. The Level 1 Acute Oncology Passport was evaluated as relevant, pitched at the correct level and an appropriate measure for assessing acute oncology knowledge and skills competencies. The main challenge reported was lack of study time. There was also a desire for support of acute oncology knowledge and skills development in the workplace, alongside or following e-learning.ConclusionsAcute oncology is an important area of practice for improving outcomes for people with cancer, but for acute oncology to be effective it requires a knowledgeable and skilled workforce. The acute oncology learning needs of staff caring for people with cancer in other countries are likely to be similar to the UK. Although respondents were staff who opted to complete the online learning, assessment and evaluation survey, our e-learning and digital assessment of competence Passport was found to be engaging, relevant and acceptable, demonstrating the value of co-design. It also increased learners’ confidence and knowledge about acute oncology. However, dedicated time and support are needed for healthcare staff to develop knowledge and skills to ensure safe outcomes for people with cancer. We suggest that similar education and competence assessment resources are needed to address acute oncology learning needs among the broader cancer care workforce.

Background

There are approximately 3 million people living with cancer in the United Kingdom (UK) predicted to rise to 4 million by 2030 [1]. People with cancer are at risk of becoming rapidly and acutely ill, requiring urgent or emergency treatment and care [2]. The increase in cancer incidence inevitably means a rise in acute cancer-related symptoms [2].

The National Confidential Enquiry into Patient Outcomes and Death (from systematic anti-cancer therapy, SACT) [3] report, made several recommendations to develop acute oncology (AO) services to coordinate and manage oncological emergencies. Nowadays, each of the four nations of the UK have established AO services, to promote safety and quality in unplanned cancer care [4–7]. The Society for Acute Medicine Benchmarking Audit 2022 [2] highlighted that despite robust historical reporting of requirements to improve suspected oncological emergency outcomes, significant variability in provision of AO services exists UK-wide. This emphasises the necessity for staff caring for patients with cancer to have practice level-appropriate AO knowledge and skills [8].

However, the breadth of staff providing acute oncology care is diverse, as acute oncology services in the UK uses a model of shared-care, spanning primary, emergency, oncology, specialist acute oncology services. Major, not-for-profit, charitable organisations in the UK, the UK Oncology Nursing Society (UKONS) [9] and Macmillan Cancer Support, have influenced AO services across the UK through the development of standardised guidelines, triage assessment tools and clinical management guidelines, which are widely adopted.

Following extensive UK-wide consultation, UKONS published multi-disciplinary, four practice-level Acute Oncology Knowledge and Skills Guidance (Version 1) [10]. Using this guidance, UKONS and Macmillan Cancer Support then mapped acute oncology online learning for each level of practice. The learning outcomes and competences at Level 1 were designed for a broad group of healthcare workers who need to recognise ‘red-flag’ symptoms of common oncological emergencies and have knowledge of local AO referral pathways. This is because these staff might interact with someone with cancer who could report an acute oncology indication and, therefore, these staff need awareness of acute oncology indications and how to refer patients to acute oncology services following national guidelines. Thus, acute oncology is “everyone’s business” [8]. These staff included: patient-facing administrative staff, supportive and assistive staff, pre-registration healthcare students, emergency service/ambulance call handlers, primary care professionals and hospital healthcare professionals who did not work in cancer or specialist acute oncology services, for example in emergency, acute and same-day medicine.

Macmillan Cancer Support’s freely-accessible ‘Introduction to Acute Oncology’ e-learning module [11] addressed most of the learning outcomes and competences for Level 1. This module originally evolved from a face-to-face class, to a virtual classroom, then to further enhance accessibility and scalability, the course was transformed into a e-learning module. The module focused on the use of UKONS Primary Care Risk Assessment tool [12], which enables assessment based on patient-reported symptoms, as well as providing learners with information on AO services and their development in the UK.

Working with UKONS, the UK Acute Oncology Society and the National Acute Oncology Education Group, Macmillan Cancer Support reviewed the Introduction to Acute Oncology e-learning to identify revisions needed to fully align to the UKONS Acute Oncology Knowledge and Skills Guidance Level 1 learning outcomes. Work was also undertaken to co-design and integrate digital competence assessment (Passport Level 1) within this e-learning module, aligned to the competences in the UKONS Acute Oncology Knowledge and Skills Guidance [10]. The acute oncology Passport was developed using similar methodology to the development and successful, national implementation of the UKONS SACT Competence Assessment Passport [13]. The UKONS SACT Competence Assessment Passport has provided the UK with a standardised approach to SACT competence assessment, which aimed to reduce variability in SACT care provision and facilitate the transition of staff between organisations across the UK without the need for repeated competence assessment.

This study reports the co-design and evaluation of the revised Introduction to Acute Oncology course, incorporating the Level 1 acute oncology Passport, specifically pre- and post-course levels of confidence and knowledge, and post-course perspectives and experiences, among UK healthcare staff. The evaluation of the AO Passports at Levels 2–4 will be reported in our companion paper.

1a. Development of the digital competence assessment

Competence at practice Level 1 requires learners to safely interact with patients with a suspected AO indication, e.g., recognising red-flags at telephone calls and appointments, referring patients and understanding the urgency of their actions (See Column A Table 1).

Co-design, where end-users have meaningful involvement in research [15], was used to conduct the project to ensure validity and rigor. A reiterative review approach to Passport development was taken, emulating the agile co-design approach [16]. Online workshops were held between September 2022 and January 2023 to co-design the novel digital competence assessment. Workshops were attended by 37 participants from 27 National Health Service (NHS) organisations, three UK universities, UKONS, UK Acute Oncology Society and Macmillan Cancer Support. Workshop participants included: AO clinical nurse specialists, AO advanced clinical practitioners, AO lead nurses, nurse consultants, medical oncologists, cancer charity staff, clinical educators and practice development staff.

Drafts of the competence assessment working document were shared two-weeks ahead of the workshops. Workshop attendees were provided with information about the project, their participation in the workshops, that the workshops would be recorded and transcribed, and that recordings and transcriptions would be used to develop the digital competence assessment Passport. Workshop attendees were then asked prior to each workshop to consent for the workshop to be recorded and transcribed. Draft questions and model answers were discussed within the workshop. Based on discussions, revised versions of the draft Passport were circulated via email to validate amendments and capture additional comments. Revisions were agreed via email. Consensus was reached to develop a digital competence assessment to increase accessibility across the UK.

The CASCADE Project team (VL, GM) collaborated with the Macmillan Cancer Support Professional Development and Knowledge team (DM, LH, HD, LP) to develop the Level 1 AO Passport, by incorporating digital competence assessment into the revised e-learning (the Level 1 AO Passport). Revisions, based on the UKONS Acute Oncology Knowledge and Skills Guidance for Level 1 [10], followed the pedagogic framework of the ‘Arena, Blended, Connected’ curriculum design [17] (See Table 1). This ensured a robust, comprehensive and rapid redesign of the learning activities [18, 19]. In addition, the ABC process fostered a collaborative approach, allowing for iterative feedback and discussion [20]. A visual, storyboarding approach was also used to account for a variety of learner needs and preferences [21].

The revised e-learning module included additional content about communication, patient assessment records and the important role of communication and patient records in safe practice in acute oncology. Additional literature was also cited for each question to signpost learners and to ensure the model answers were evidence-based and current. An optional reflective learning section, based on Gibbs’ Reflective Cycle [22], was also included at the end of the module.

The aim of digitalising the competence assessment Passport was to reach staff working in diverse care settings, due to the likely absence of an AO specialist practice assessor. The revised module had the addition of six automatically-assessed knowledge checks. Learning media included multiple-choice questions, drag and drop answers and free-text reflective exercises using Padlet, to facilitate asynchronous and shared-learning and scenario-based activities to ensure engaging, person-centred learning. Learners could only progress from one section to the next once the knowledge check had been successfully passed, thus ensuring competence in each section of the course.

1b. Development of the pre and post evaluation survey

The online evaluation questionnaires were co-designed with the stakeholder group described above to ensure face-validity [23]. Questionnaires included multiple-choice, Likert-scale, confidence-rating and free-text response questions. The Likert-scale questions were clustered into the AO knowledge constructs: Services; Initial Assessment and Referral; and Communication and Reporting, which aligned to Level 1 competences [10].

Phase 2: Pilot and evaluation

2a. Study participants

Participants were learners who care for people with cancer in the UK, had registered to take the Level 1 AO Passport and had voluntarily agreed to complete online questionnaires. Based on prior evaluation of another national online learning resource [24], we calculated a sample size to detect a 0.5 difference in median with a standard deviation of 0.9 on a 1 to 5-point Likert Scale. Based on dependent (post-test) sample for non-parametric testing, the required size is n = 58 to achieve a 95% confidence level and 80% power to detect the difference. The conservative response rate was deemed sufficient for statistical analyses and may have limited, or no, reduction on non-response bias [25]. As a national quality improvement project, a clinical trial number was not applicable.

2b. Ethical considerations

Approval was sought from Macmillan Cancer Support Information Governance Team. Collection of anonymous responses was governed by an existing Data Protection Impact Assessment for all registered Macmillan Learning Hub users. All the study team complied with The Data Protection Act 2018 [26] in regard to the collection, storage and processing of data. Response data was deidentified by other Macmillan Cancer Support personnel prior to sharing password protected electronic files with the study team. Data will be destroyed once the study is fully reported within five years of data collection.

2c. Data collection

We sought responses from staff representing a wide-range of service settings in the UK, including cancer boards, networks and alliances, professional societies, groups and registered cancer charities, through e-mails, webinars, conference presentations, social media, e-bulletins and newsletters, and a community webpage on the host Learning Hub. We also consulted with patient and carer representatives through the cancer voices groups and cancer research consumer panels.

Participant information was shared via ‘community page’ in the Macmillan Learning Hub [11] to be read prior to commencing the Passport. This included: the purpose of the evaluation; the anonymous and voluntary nature of responses; consent being indicated by submission of completed questionnaires; and invitation to participate by completing questionnaires before and after the Level 1 AO Passport.

2d. Data analysis

Quantitative data were cleaned (removing duplicate and null responses) and numerically coded in Microsoft® Excel. Statistical analyses were conducted in IBM®SPSS®v29. Demographics were compared pre and post to ensure post-respondents were representative of the pre-respondent population. We did this using the Pearson Chi-squared goodness-of-fit analysis based on the assumption of the mean tending to an approximate normal distribution due to a large sample size. Median and range were used for ranked, ordinal data (e.g. time to complete). Mean and standard deviation (SD) were used to describe continuous data (e.g. level of confidence rating) and pre and post continuous data were compared using the Students’ t-test. Likert scale responses, e.g. Strongly agree; Agree; Neither agree nor disagree; Disagree; Strongly disagree, were assigned arbitrary ratings from 1 to 5, where 1 was Strongly disagree and 5 was Strongly agree. Ordinal pre and post data were compared using Mann Whitney U-test, for non-parametric data skewed to the left due to the upper limit of 5. Association between nominal and ranked ordinal data was analysed using the univariate non-parametric ANOVA Kruskal-Wallis test (H) with adjusted significance values using the Bonferroni correction for multiple tests. Results

Data quality

There were n = 376 pre-Level 1 AO Passport respondents, which was higher than expected. The post response rate was 47% (n = 176) of the pre-response rate. There was minimal missing data (pre-data completeness = 99.52%; post-data completeness = 99.72%). There was a high-level of internal consistency of the knowledge constructs in the evaluation survey: ‘Knowledge of AO’ (Cronbach’s α = 0.882) ‘Assessment and Referral’ (Cronbach’s α = 0.943) and ‘Communication and Reporting’ (Cronbach’s α = 0.879). We conducted Chi-square Goodness of Fit analysis on demographic data; there were no significant differences between the pre and post data (Table 2).

Learner characteristics

The highest level of qualification for the majority of learners was a Bachelors’ degree. Most respondents had more than 10 years’ experience in healthcare and approximately three-quarters had experience working in cancer care. The majority of respondents were from the North and Southeast regions of England, and most respondents worked in acute hospitals. Using Pearson Chi-Square (p value < 0.05) there was no significant difference in demographic characteristics of respondents pre and post the Level 1 AO Passport (Table 2).

Completion rates and time of completion

Learners were asked how long it took to complete the Level 1 AO Passport, e.g. a sum of the time spent studying in minutes, hours, days, and the period of time they took to complete, e.g. the start time and date to the end time and date in terms of days, weeks and months. The median study time was four hours (n = 125, range 1 h to 10 h) over a median period of 2 days (n = 125, range 1 day to 10 days) where learners logged on and off around other commitments. There was a significant difference between time to complete and level of qualification using the Kruskal-Wallace test (H), p = < 0.001, where learners with a higher level of qualification completed the e-learning and assessment in less time.

Levels of knowledge and understanding

Learners were asked to report their level of confidence in their knowledge and skills about AO before and after the Level 1 AO Passport. There was a statistically significant difference (t-value = -13.206, degrees of freedom [df] 549, p = < 0.001), where learners had increased confidence in their AO knowledge and skills. Learners were also asked how much they agreed or disagreed with sixteen Likert scale statements about their perceived level of knowledge, within the three knowledge constructs (Table 3). There was a statistically significant increase in perceived levels of AO knowledge post Level 1 AO Passport in comparison to pre-levels of AO knowledge using the Mann-Whitney U test, with a small to medium effect size (Table 3).

Learner perceptions and experiences

Content analysis of free-text comments highlighted a lack of study time, a desire for additional training support in practice, issues with some digital access for some learners (which have been resolved), and that the Level 1 AO Passport was informative, relevant, pitched at the correct level, engaging and easy to use (Table 4).

Discussion

We co-designed a novel digital competence assessment integrated into an e-learning introductory AO course, the Level 1 AO Passport, which was designed to meet the core learning needs of all staff who interact with people at risk of having an oncology emergency. This was successfully piloted across the UK. We found that the Level 1 AO Passport increased learners’ level of confidence in AO knowledge and skills. Furthermore, the resource was deemed to be relevant for Level 1 AO practice, engaging and user-friendly. Some learners wished to continue their acute oncology education and training on completion of the e-learning, therefore, the Macmillan Cancer Support Learning Hub now includes signposting to Level 2 and Level 3 AO education and training resources.

Key challenges reported by the learners were a lack of study time and a desire for work-based learning support while studying. Barriers to completing additional, non-mandatory training are commonly reported by the NHS cancer care workforce where delivery of clinical care is understandably prioritised over study time [28]. However, in creating this resource, we have addressed a workforce education, training and assessment need aimed to improve the outcomes of people who are at risk of an oncological emergency [8].

This education, training and assessment resource was developed as an online resource to increase accessibility across care settings, in line with the NHS Long Term Workforce Plan [29], which called for digital and technological innovations to support workforce training and education. However, as our evaluation highlights, the importance of work-based learning must remain a core element of education and training for healthcare staff. We, therefore, recommend that managers support staff to complete the Level 1 AO Passport as part of their professional development, for example agreeing study time and relevant clinical education support and/or supervision at annual appraisals.

The Level 1 AO Passport was developed alongside AO Passports for Level 2, 3 and 4 (reported elsewhere) as part of a suite of assessment resources standardising AO competence assessment across the four levels of practice. The education of cancer nurses across Europe is defined by the European Oncology Nursing Society Cancer Nursing Education Framework [29]. The Level 1 Digital Passport can support European Cancer Nurses to evidence AO competence at the ‘Generalist’ Level of Practice. In addition, the devolved nations of the UK each have their own strategic plans for improving cancer patient outcomes [30–33], and multidisciplinary workforce education, training and development frameworks and implementation plans [33–36].

The NHS England Aspiring Cancer Career and Education Development (ACCEND) programme [37], was developed as a multi-agency programme with the intention of being implemented across the UK to educate and train the workforce outside of medicine to meet the needs of people with cancer. To facilitate transferability and implementation of the Level 1 AO Passport in the UK, we have aligned it to the ACCEND Capability Framework. As such, completion of the Level 1 AO Passport demonstrates achievement of all the Domains of the ACCEND Capabilities Framework for the supportive/assistive, pre-registered and newly registered nurses and allied health professionals’ workforce, with exception of the ‘Prehabilitation and Rehabilitation Interventions’ Domain.

Strengths and limitations

The co-design was facilitated by a project manager with clinical AO experience (GM) who drafted the initial Level 1 AO Passport, reducing burden on subject matter expert stakeholders and attaining high-level stakeholder engagement. Consideration was given to AO service variation across the UK, this meant that developing the Level 1 AO Passport was challenging. This project was driven by workforce need and has garnered a high-level of broad stakeholder engagement. The co-design process with stakeholders from all four nations of the UK and from different healthcare staff groups, helped identify commonalities for assessing competence, and improved face, content and construct validity [23].

Learners were staff who opted whether or not to undertake the online learning and assessment and participate in the evaluation, which risks selection bias and is a key limitation of the study design. Interpretation of our findings should consider that responses about levels of knowledge were based on learners self-assessment. However, for learners to complete the post-evaluation survey, they needed to have successfully achieved competence against completion of all the knowledge checks within the Passport.

The intended learners for the pilot and evaluation of the Level 1 AO Passport included non-clinical staff, non-registered staff and registered staff working in non-cancer settings. One limitation of the study is that most respondents worked in acute hospital trusts. In addition, some learners were experienced health professionals (that worked in cancer services in acute hospital settings). We, therefore, conducted sub-group analysis to investigate if findings were different dependent on academic qualification, years’ experience in healthcare and years’ experience in cancer care. While pre and post self-reported knowledge constructs in the evaluation survey showed statistically significant difference, the effect sizes were small to medium. To fully understand how these effect sizes translate to changes in practice requires evaluation using higher levels of the Kirkpatrick model [38].

Due to limited funding and project duration we were not able to evaluate the long-term impact of this resource. Further evaluation is required to determine knowledge gains, changes in confidence and impact in practice over time. We were also not able to use a controlled design for the evaluation, as the e-learning and competence assessment Passport were freely-accessible on a national cancer charity online learning hub.

Conclusion

We are confident that the novel digital Level 1 AO Passport is relevant and acceptable to practice following thorough, UK-wide evaluation which is likely due to the co-design process. We suggest that the co-design process described herein for the development, pilot and evaluation of this Level 1 AO Passport is transferrable to co-designing multidisciplinary, multi-agent competence assessment in other areas of practice.
