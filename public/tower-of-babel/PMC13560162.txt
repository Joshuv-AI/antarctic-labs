Advance care planning with patients of Islamic migration background: a qualitative study identifying the need for relational autonomy and hope-sustaining communication
Authors: Anouk Putker, Amine Haddane, Yvonne Engels, Evelien Kuip, Marieke Perry
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAdvance Care Planning (ACP) is largely based on ideas of individual autonomy and direct communication, which may conflict with the relational, cultural and religious perspectives of patients with an Islamic migration background. We explored the perspectives of patients with an Islamic background on ACP conversations within a Dutch healthcare system and which communication strategies may support more culturally appropriate ACP.MethodsA qualitative study was conducted consisting of two focus groups with cultural experts and healthcare professionals (N = 14) and interviews with patients with an Islamic migration background receiving palliative cancer treatment (N = 6) in the Netherlands. Semi-structured interview guides and an ACP introduction video were used to initiate discussion and reflection in both settings. Inductive thematic analysis was applied to all interview transcripts.ResultsThree main themes emerged: (1) ACP decision-making is predominantly family-driven: patients rely on relational autonomy through their family network, although individual preferences vary across generations. This highlights the importance of exploring patients’ preferred role of the family in decision-making, facilitating family involvement in ACP conversations, and avoiding assumptions of individual decision-making as the default. (2) Cultural and religious meaning of illness and suffering strongly shapes how patients interpret the end of life. Rather than using concepts such as “quality of life”, clinicians should explore what gives patients (spiritual) meaning and hope. Palliative care was sometimes misunderstood as hastening death, highlighting the importance of explaining the difference between symptom management and ending life. (3) Hope is a crucial determinant for maintaining engagement in ACP discussions. Direct communication, especially statements removing all hope, was experienced as distressing and damaging to trust. Patients valued communication that preserved hope, matched their emotional readiness, and showed genuine personal and cultural interest.ConclusionACP with patients with an Islamic migration background requires a relational, hope-sustaining and culturally sensitive approach. Decision-making is often family-driven, and individualistic perspectives on autonomy may not align with patient preferences. Religious meanings of illness and suffering influence perceptions of palliative care, while maintaining hope is essential for engagement. Adapting communication including preserving hope may enhance trust and offers concrete strategies to support appropriate ACP conservations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12904-026-02207-9.Keywords: Advance care planning, Relational autonomy, Palliative care, Cultural sensitivity, Oncology

Background

End-of-life care conversations are complex, particularly when perspectives of patients and healthcare professionals fundamentally differ. In increasingly culturally diverse societies [1], such differences are becoming more common, as patients bring a diverse combination of medical, psychosocial, spiritual, and cultural needs into end-of-life conversations [2]. Advance care planning (ACP) has been introduced to support alignment of healthcare with patients’ personal values and daily lives [3, 4] and is widely adopted in international healthcare guidelines [5]. ACP is a continuous, open dialogue where patients, together with their families and healthcare providers, reflect on their personal values and life goals and record their preferences for future care and treatments [6–9]. Effective ACP has been shown to enhance patient-provider communication, improve quality of life, and ensure that care received near death matches the patient’s wishes [10].

But implementing complex, culturally sensitive ACP communication processes into daily practice remains difficult [11, 12]. Particularly in the Netherlands, but also in other Northern European healthcare contexts, ACP and palliative care principles are more often operationalized in ways that foreground individual autonomy and relatively direct communication regarding end-of-life topics [13, 14]. While these approaches can support clarity and respect for patient preferences, they may not always align with the needs of all patients. For instance, patients with an Islamic migration background may experience significant barriers when engaging in ACP, where initial hesitation or unreadiness is easily misunderstood as unwillingness [15, 16]. In many Islamic communities, openly discussing death may be perceived as taboo due to beliefs that illness and death are determined by God, and critical medical decisions are typically navigated collectively by the family network rather than the individual [17, 18]. Furthermore, generational differences add complexity; while first-generation migrants often face language barriers and hold on to traditional values, subsequent generations attempt to balance ideas of autonomy with traditional family dynamics [16, 19–22]. As a result, standard ACP models frequently fail to meet the needs of these patients, contributing to inequalities such as lower utilization of palliative care services and a higher rate of hospital admissions during the final phase of life [14].

Demographic developments and continued migration are expected to further increase the proportion of people with diverse cultural and religious identities in Western countries [1, 23]. This growing diversity extends to healthcare professionals, creating a clinical environment where different cultural values and communication styles frequently intersect [24]. Although cultural barriers have been widely documented, research is lacking on how to overcome these barriers and on which communication strategies best align with the needs of patients with an Islamic cultural background.

Therefore, this qualitative study aimed to explore the perspectives of patients with an Islamic migration background on ACP conversations within the Dutch healthcare system and to identify communication strategies that may support more culturally appropriate ACP.

Methods

Study design

A qualitative study using semi-structured interviews with patients and focus groups with cultural experts and healthcare professionals was performed. The COREQ criteria (Consolidated criteria for Reporting Qualitative research) were followed in reporting the results [25].

Setting and participant selection

This study was performed within the Radboud university medical center; an academic hospital in the southeastern part of the Netherlands.

Participants for the focus groups were recruited through purposive sampling via the researchers’ professional networks. Professionals were eligible if they had at least one year of professional experience with regular and direct engagement with this patient population in clinical or advisory roles. Individuals who provided consent were also asked to nominate other professionals whom they considered experts in this field; these recommendations were critically assessed before additional invitations were issued. Participants were based in the Netherlands and Belgium. As both focus groups were conducted in Dutch, only individuals able to participate in Dutch were included.

Eligible patients were adults identifying with an Islamic migration background who were currently receiving palliative treatment for advanced or metastatic cancer. Recruitment relied on convenience sampling from the Department of Medical Oncology from both the inpatient ward and outpatient clinic.

Patients were approached face-to-face or by telephone by their treating physician and were then formally invited by researcher AH. To prevent the exclusion of vulnerable groups, patients with a language barrier were explicitly included. To ensure high-quality and consistent translations, exclusively professional interpreters were used. The use of informal interpreters (e.g., family members) was deliberately avoided to minimise translation bias and standardise the data collection process, particularly as previous research has shown that decision-making often occurs within the family rather than at the individual level [17, 18], which may blur roles and compromise objective data collection.

In total, eleven patients were approached, of whom five declined to participate (reasons included: too fatigued, lack of interest), resulting in a final sample of six participants. All but one of the participating patients identified as practising Muslims.

Data collection

Data were collected between December 2025 and March 2026 in focus group interviews and individual face-to-face interviews. Focus groups took place digitally via Microsoft Teams. They were initiated and facilitated by the primary researcher (AP), while the discussions were moderated by an experienced focus group moderator (YE). Each session lasted approximately 90 min. Individual interviews were conducted after the focus groups and took place face-to-face at a location convenient for the participant. The interviews were conducted by researcher AH. Besides the patient, researcher AH and a professional interpreter when needed, no other non-participants were present. Interviews lasted an average of 59 min (range: 51–71 min).

Both the focus groups and the individual interviews used a semi-structured interview guide that was specifically developed for the purpose of this study. Because the focus groups were conducted first, the insights gained from these discussions were used to inform the topic guide for the patient interviews. An ACP introduction video, available in all relevant languages and tailored to persons with an Islamic migration background, was used to open the dialogue in both settings. This short, animated film was developed in collaboration with healthcare professionals, patients, and communication experts, and reviewed by the national Expert Centre for Palliative Care. The 2.5-minute video provides patients with a brief introduction to the concept of ACP, including its purpose and the role of communication about future care preferences. Cultural appropriateness and perceived acceptability of the film were addressed first, and subsequently experiences within the Islamic migration communities, cultural norms, and preferences related to ACP and end-of-life communication. Data saturation was discussed in meetings within the research team. Enrolment of participants ended when data saturation was reached and no new concepts emerged.

All interviews were audio-recorded, using Microsoft Teams. The researchers took field notes during and immediately after the focus groups and interviews to capture contextual details. Repeat focus groups or interviews were not conducted. Transcripts were not returned to participants for review.

Data analysis

Audio recordings were transcribed verbatim. During the analysis, the translated speech from the professional interpreters served as the official source of the interviewee’s answers. Inductive thematic analysis was applied to the transcripts, using ATLAS.ti [version 24.0.0] to support this process.

Two researchers (AH and AP) independently read and coded all eight transcripts (both focus groups and interviews). A four-step coding process was used. First, open coding was applied to identify initial concepts. Second, axial coding was used to explore connections between these open codes. Third, the codes were grouped into broader categories. Finally, these categories were organised into overarching themes (see Fig. 1 for a summarized coding tree and Supplementary file 1 for the full coding tree).

Summarized coding tree

The initial code lists were compared by researchers AH and AP, and any differences in coding were resolved through discussion between them. Subsequently, the complete coding tree including categories and themes were discussed and finalized during three meetings with the full research team. The final codebook was translated into English.

Credibility of the findings

The research team consisted of members with diverse professional backgrounds, to warrant a multi-perspective view during the thematic analysis. The team included a senior researcher who is a medical oncologist and palliative care specialist (EK, female), a senior researcher and general practitioner (MP, female), a professor of meaningful healthcare (YE, female), a PhD candidate and medical oncologist in training (AP, female), and one medical student (AH, male).

All researchers had a Dutch cultural background, with the exception of AH, who is a third generation Dutch Moroccan with an Islamic faith background.

Researcher AH has basic knowledge of qualitative research and was closely guided by the experienced qualitative researchers throughout the study. The analytic contributions were regularly discussed and reviewed in team meetings to ensure methodological consistency and rigor. All senior researchers were formally trained and experienced in qualitative research methods.

The researcher conducting the individual interviews (AH) had no prior relationship with the participating patients, which helped minimize the chance of socially desirable answers during the interviews. The use of professional interpreters strengthened the credibility of the findings by ensuring accurate, consistent translation. Some members of the research team had existing professional relationships with several focus group participants, due to the network-based recruitment strategy.

Data triangulation was achieved by incorporating both perspectives from participants with diverse professional backgrounds and perspectives of patients, which strengthened the credibility and robustness of the findings [26].

Ethical approval

The study was performed according to Dutch law and Good Clinical Practice guidelines. The Medical Review Ethics Committee region Arnhem-Nijmegen concluded this study was not subject to the Medical Research Involving Human Subjects Act (case number CMO 2025–18273) and therefore did not require full ethical review.

Thematic analysis

Through thematic analysis, the data were organised into 162 codes, 37 axial codes, eight categories and three individual themes: ‘Theme 1: Relational autonomy, family-driven and individual decision-making’, ‘Theme 2: Cultural and religious meaning of illness, suffering, and death’ and ‘Theme 3: The reciprocal dynamic of hope and trust in the care relationship’. Within each theme, concrete strategies are identified that enable clinicians to adapt their communication and care to better meet patients’ needs.

The first theme showed strong resemblance to the concept of relational autonomy. Although this concept was not predefined prior to the analysis, we interpreted the findings in light of the literature on relational autonomy [28–30]. This concept describes how individuals’ preferences and decisions are shaped through relationships and social interactions. Within our data, this was reflected in how participants described decision-making as a shared process involving family members, in which preferences were often negotiated, supported, or delegated.

Across the data, cultural and religious perspectives emerged as closely intertwined across the data and were therefore treated as overlapping dimensions rather than as distinct categories, jointly shaping how participants interpreted illness, suffering, and the end of life.

Theme 1: Relational autonomy, family-driven and individual decision-making

Across the interviews and focus groups, ACP decision-making among patients with an Islamic background was acknowledged as fundamentally relational, shaped by family consultation. Patients framed autonomy as enacted through others rather than an individual act: “It is more a we-culture than an I-culture. Such decisions are not taken individually. Almost the whole family is involved.” Consequently, family presence during ACP conversations was considered the norm. Choices about treatment or talking about prognosis were often only meaningful when deliberated collectively (“we think together and decide together”). Focus group participants noted that standard ACP materials, which assume a single patient in a consultation room, fail to reflect this reality.

Although most focus group participants viewed this family-driven approach as the standard, patient interviews revealed individual differences. One male patient strongly preferred strict individual autonomy to protect their loved ones from emotional distress, as he stated firmly: “I do not want my wife and children there for such a conversation. I will arrange it alone.”

When relational norms take precedence, they may create tension within clinical practices that emphasise direct disclosure and individual autonomy. Professionals frequently encountered ‘protective shielding,’ where family members conceal a poor prognosis or bad news from the patient. Patients often viewed this not as deception, but as profound care: “He did not tell his own mother that she had cancer. That is a kind of protection… He didn’t dare.” For clinicians this dynamic may be experienced as conflicting with their professional responsibility to inform creating a significant ethical dilemma: “The patient obviously has the right to know, but you have to weigh the damage to the family relationship.”

This protective dynamic may sometimes limit the patient’s direct involvement in decision-making. While professionals advocated for separate consultations to center the patient’s voice, patients themselves expressed diverse preferences. Some preferred to defer entirely to medical authority (“I don’t want questions. I think the doctor knows best”). This preference can be understood as another expression of relational autonomy, in which decision-making is intentionally entrusted to a healthcare professional. Others warned that bypassing the patient to involve the family first could also cause harm: “The message of being incurably ill was directly discussed with my family; that made the blow even harder.”

Relational autonomy appeared not a fixed cultural trait, but rather something that shifts with acculturation, health literacy and individual preferences and circumstances. While first-generation migrants often rely deeply on their family network to navigate the healthcare system, subsequent generations display more individualistic values. These generational differences illustrate the variability of autonomy preferences across contexts and life experiences. As one older male patient concluded, “I am of the second generation. My son and his children were born in the Netherlands. They think differently than how I think about it.”

In ACP situations with language barriers, professional interpreters were considered indispensable by both professionals and patients. Relying on family members for translation altered family dynamics and diminished patient agency. Interpreters did more than provide literal translations; they were perceived to act as cultural mediators. They helped bridging the gap between the healthcare system’s emphasis on individual autonomy and the patient’s preference for family-driven decision-making, clarifying these differing expectations to both clinicians and patients. In this way, interpreters actively shaped how autonomy was enacted in practice, either reinforcing patient agency or supporting more relational forms of decision-making. As one patient expressed, “In the past the doctor was difficult for me to understand, but now that I have an interpreter, things are much better. I feel much more at ease.”

Theme 2: Cultural and religious meaning of illness, suffering, and death

In this study, cultural and religious perspectives were closely intertwined and often difficult to distinguish in practice. We therefore describe them as overlapping dimensions that jointly shape how patients interpret illness, suffering, and the end of life.

The analysis of both the focus groups and patient interviews showed a strong consensus: ACP is closely tied to how patients assign religious meaning to illness and the end of life. While focus group participants often approach death as a biological inevitability, the interviews showed that patients primarily accept it as a predetermined moment governed entirely by God. For most patients, this belief in destiny brought a sense of peace. As one patient explained: “I do not find it difficult to talk about death at all. We believe that passing away has to do with fate, that it is already determined.” However, clinicians frequently misinterpret this acceptance.

Trust in God will rarely equate to a desire to stop medical treatment. Instead, it creates an expectation of continuous medical action to honor hope: “If it were a Muslim doctor, he would say there is still a God and he does not give up hope yet. God is great, we are going to try.” As an example of this reliance on visible medical action, one patient explained that sick people in his culture are immediately reassured the moment they get intravenous therapy, even if it has no medical effect anymore, purely because it radiates action and hope.

Cultural differences also became apparent around the concept of ‘quality of life’, which is a central en frequently used construct in many Northern European healthcare contexts when discussing treatment decisions. While clinicians often link quality of life to physical independence and the absence of pain, Islamic patients may view physical decline or suffering as a spiritual trial. A focus group participant explained: “This concept is difficult to reconcile with the principle that a Muslim is grateful for the life he lives. It is believed that suffering pain is the remission of sins.” Patients measure well-being differently: “Quality of life is seen as the degree to which divine proximity is experienced.” Using the term ‘quality of life’ to justify stopping treatment signals to the patient that the doctor is giving up, creating an immediate dead end in communication. As one healthcare professional stated: “To prevent this, clinicians should avoid this container concept and instead explore what gives the patient (spiritual) peace and meaning.”

Beyond conceptual differences, discussing end of life treatments potentially triggers deep mistrust. Focus group participants described that patients often incorrectly associate routine palliative interventions, such as pain relief or palliative sedation, with euthanasia: “A lot of people think that pain relief is also something that actively ends life.” This anxiety stems from the core religious principle that the active termination of life is strictly forbidden in Islam.

Because of this misunderstanding, initiation of an ACP conversation can make patients suspect that the physician wants to hasten their death. This mistrust extends to non-medical support; proposing an imam can trigger acute panic, as it is interpreted as a sign that the medical system has given up: “When they asked if they could arrange an imam, I thought: then my days are numbered, then I am going to die.”

As one intercultural healthcare consultant explained: “To protect the care relationship, healthcare providers must explicitly clarify the distinction between symptom management and active life termination. They must reassure patients that ACP is about comfort and care, rather than a covert decision to withdraw treatment or end life.”

Theme 3: The reciprocal dynamic of hope and trust in the care relationship

From all perspectives, maintaining hope was experienced essential for patients, in situations where they receive and interpret difficult information. Because of this central role of hope, the way physicians communicate strongly influences whether conversations feel supportive or overwhelming. Participants identified a relatively direct communication style as a primary threat to this need for hope, appointing a gap between bad-news delivery and patients’ emotional needs: “The Western communication is simply blunt, direct, and coarse. Sometimes you feel no empathy in the doctor’s conversation.” This experience of bluntness, especially definitive language that removes all hope, causes severe distress. Rather than absolute transparency, patients and families often value a softer approach. As one patient explained: “Within my culture, you do not say all at once: you have cancer and we can do nothing more. And if people are around? Then things are covered up a bit.”

Timing and pacing were found to determine whether a patient stays engaged in ACP discussions, though preferences vary. Some patients experienced severe shock from a blunt prognosis and preferred a gradual approach, while others were frustrated by receiving information in phases. One patient advocated for immediate clarity to allow collective grieving: “I found receiving information bit-by-bit terrible. You should have explained it all at once. Then you can cry it out once with your family.”

Showing genuine personal interest is essential for discussing end-of-life topics. Patients indicated that a doctor’s effort to ask about their background or use a few words in their native language immediately builds trust: “If I come here and she says ‘Good morning’ in Turkish, that shows interest. Just check the internet to see where someone is from. That is a piece of trust.”

Finally, maintaining this trust requires time and ongoing moral commitment. Patients desired continuous support from their general practitioner after a severe diagnosis and felt abandoned when curative options ended: “The general practitioner called me once. She said: ‘I don’t know anything about cancer and such. I can’t help you.’ She never called me again. I consider that a real letdown.” Professionals viewed this as an ethical shortcoming, arguing that building trust extends beyond standard consultation times: “You have received income from caring for this patient for 20 or 30 years. Ethically, you should be able to make one hour of time available to discover his or her wishes in detail.” Without this dedicated time and relational proximity, the foundation of trust required for ACP cannot be established.

Discussion

Summary of main findings

In this qualitative study, we found that standard ACP models in Dutch healthcare do not always align with the lived realities of patients with an Islamic migration background and identified strategies to improve end-of-life conversations across three main domains. First, decision-making is fundamentally relational and family-driven. Therefore, clinicians should actively involve the family network and utilize professional interpreters to bridge cultural gaps. Second, the clinical concept of “quality of life” conflicts directly with Islamic frameworks that view suffering as spiritually meaningful. To prevent patients from fearing that symptom management equates to active life termination, healthcare providers should explicitly separate palliative care from active life termination, and avoid using “quality of life” to justify stopping treatment. Finally, a direct or possibly blunt communication style actively threatens the care relationship. Physicians should adopt a hope-sustaining approach and demonstrate genuine cultural interest to build the interpersonal trust necessary for effective ACP.

Comparison with existing literature

Our findings align with earlier research indicating that current palliative care frameworks prioritize individual autonomy and explicit communication [10, 13]. However, our study illustrates exactly how that can cause potential friction in the consultation room.

Regarding autonomy, the phenomenon of “protective shielding”, where families hide poor prognoses from the patient, is a well-documented practice in family-centric cultures [27, 28]. While healthcare providers may view this as an ethical violation, our patient interviews revealed it is considered an act of profound care. Protective shielding should not be considered specific to patients with an Islamic background. Similar forms of protective communication have been reported across different healthcare contexts [29–31].

Furthermore, our findings shows that second- and third-generation migrants increasingly desire individual autonomy despite being raised in cultures that prioritize relational autonomy [17, 18]. This aligns with the literature, in which this phenomenon is described as cultural hybridity: a close interweaving of different, partly conflicting backgrounds [32, 33]. Similar challenges between individual autonomy and relational decision-making have also been described in other areas of patient care, where choices are formally framed as individual but are shaped within family relationships [34, 35]. This suggests that the mismatch we observed is not uniquely cultural but reflects a broader limitation of individualistic ACP models when relational dynamics are overlooked [36].

Our findings, consistent with existing literature, show that belief in divine predestination does not lead to a single orientation toward care [37, 38]. While for some patients trust in God was associated with continued pursuit of medical treatment to honour divine hope [17, 18], for others it was associated with acceptance of God’s will regarding illness, suffering and time of death. This highlights that such theological beliefs are interpreted in multiple ways and may lead to diverse, and sometimes contrasting, expectations regarding care [39]. Rather than representing inconsistency, this reflects a relational and context-dependent understanding of hope, responsibility, and trust in both God and healthcare professionals.

This variability in expectations regarding care also appeared to be shaped by differences in understanding and communication about medical care, particularly in relation to palliative care. In our study, patients sometimes interpreted palliative care as a cessation of hope or a forbidden attempt to hasten death, a finding that is consistent with existing literature [28, 39, 40]. This suggests that interpretations of care are not solely grounded in religious beliefs, but are co-shaped by how care options are communicated and understood in practice [39].

A recent scoping review describes the cultural and religious tensions in end-of-life care for Muslim patients, but it also reveals a notable gap in literature regarding how clinicians navigate these tensions in practice [41]. Our findings help to bridge this gap by demonstrating how family involvement, religious meaning-making, and perceptions of palliative care influence engagement in ACP. Clinicians must look beyond cultural stereotypes and individually assess family dynamics, ensuring the ACP approach fits the patient’s specific generation and personal needs [20–22].

In our study, the use of the clinical concept “quality of life” often shuts down the conversation for patients with an Islamic background, a dynamic that has not been described in prior literature.

Our findings show that, whereas existing literature may associate physical decline with a reduced quality of life and describe it as potentially challenging for patients’ sense of dignity [42, 43], participating patients framed suffering as a remission of sins and a means of achieving divine proximity [44–47].

Our study highlights a mismatch between a perceived blunt and direct communication style and the emotional needs of these patients. Statements such as “there is nothing we can do” strip away all hope and destroy trust. However, our findings also offer a new, hopeful and constructive perspective for clinical practice. They show that these patients do not have a strict religious taboo against discussing the future and death itself. Rather, the primary challenge in discussing ACP is matching both the pace and the framing of the conversation to the patient’s emotional readiness. In fact, they are very open to discussing a wide range of ACP topics, provided they feel the clinician makes a genuine effort to understand and empathize with their cultural and religious circumstances [48, 49]. When physicians shift to a relationally driven dialogue and demonstrate this cultural and personal interest, patients are highly receptive, allowing for successful and comprehensive end-of-life conversations.

Although this study focused on patients with an Islamic migration background, many of these findings may extend beyond this specific group. They underline that a personalized and relational approach, attuned to the individual patient rather than based on assumptions or cultural stereotypes, is fundamental to advance care planning and high-quality palliative care more broadly. This emphases that person-centred ACP relies on understanding patients’ values, preferences, and readiness, and on adapting communication to the individual context to build trust and support meaningful decision-making [50–53]. In addition, it requires healthcare professionals to be aware of their own cultural frames of reference and beliefs, as these may implicitly shape interactions and decision-making processes.

Strengths and limitations

A primary strength of this study is data triangulation: interviewing a heterogeneous sample of healthcare professionals and cultural experts, and individual patients allowed us to compare clinical assumptions with lived realities. To our knowledge, this form of data triangulation has not previously been applied to this topic in literature. The dependability of our study is strong, as many foundational findings align with existing qualitative literature. Our study contributes practical strategies for clinicians to overcome specific ACP complexities with patients with migrant backgrounds. Next, the use of professional interpreters instead of informal family translators captured the authentic voices of first-generation migrants and minimised translation bias. The structured use an ACP video concretized a complex topic and served as an effective opening to the conversation. Finally, the cultural background of researcher AH provided rapid trust, encouraging patients to speak openly about sensitive topics.

Several limitations must be acknowledged. The transferability of the findings may be limited for several reasons. First, the number of patients involved was relatively small (N = 6). Because five out of eleven approached patients declined due to fatigue or lack of interest, we relied on convenience sampling rather than purposive sampling. Participating patients may therefore represent a more open or health literate part of the total population. Second, recruiting exclusively advanced oncology patients from a single academic hospital influences perspectives on ACP, as disease trajectories may differ for non-malignant illnesses. Additionally, several patients perceived the healthcare professional’s communication style as blunt This perception may partly reflect specifically Dutch communication norms, which are known to be more direct than in some other European countries [54]. In addition, communication styles can vary substantially between individual clinicians, meaning that patients’ experiences may not be representative of all healthcare professionals. Finally, the predominantly Turkish sample means nuances from other Islamic ethnic groups may not be fully represented.

Conclusion

Standard Northern European ACP models prioritize individual autonomy and direct communication. This study shows that this approach frequently conflicts with the relational and religious values of patients with an Islamic migration background. Frequently used terminology, such as ‘quality of life’, clash with Islamic beliefs that view suffering as spiritually meaningful. Consequently, patients often mistake palliative care for prohibited active life termination, and direct prognostic communication quickly destroys their hope and trust in the physician.

However, we found that discussing the end of life is not a strict taboo for this group. Patients are generally very willing to engage in ACP discussions, provided they feel a foundation of trust and genuine personal interest from their healthcare provider. To facilitate these conversations, clinicians must look beyond cultural stereotypes and assess family dynamics on an individual basis. Furthermore, healthcare providers must explicitly separate symptom management from hastening death and replace direct communication with a hope-sustaining approach. Ultimately, building trust through genuine cultural and personal interest is the essential key to effective ACP with these patients.
