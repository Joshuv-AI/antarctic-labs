Optimal Spectral Bounds for Antipodal Graphs
Samuel Korsky

Abstract




1
Introduction

Suppose
\displaystyle\left\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\right\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a set of
\displaystyle n
points in the plane with diameter
\displaystyle 1
, so that
\displaystyle\|x_{i}-x_{j}\|\leq 1
for all
\displaystyle i,j
. For a small fixed
\displaystyle\varepsilon>0
, define a pair of points
\displaystyle x_{i},x_{j}
to be
\displaystyle\varepsilon
-
antipodes
if
\displaystyle\|x_{i}-x_{j}\|\geq 1-\varepsilon
and
\displaystyle\varepsilon
-
neighbors
if
\displaystyle\|x_{i}-x_{j}\|\leq\varepsilon
. Steinerberger makes the observation in [1] that for sufficiently large
\displaystyle n
, the existence of many antipodes implies the existence of many neighbors.

He provides two spiritually different extremal examples of sets with many antipodes:

(0)

\displaystyle n
points evenly distributed on the circle of diameter
\displaystyle 1

(0)

\displaystyle n-\left\lfloor\sqrt{\varepsilon}n\right\rfloor
points evenly distributed on a radius-
\displaystyle 1
circular arc of length
\displaystyle\frac{\pi}{3}
and
\displaystyle\left\lfloor\sqrt{\varepsilon}n\right\rfloor
points at the center of the circle corresponding to the arc

In the first example, each point has
\displaystyle\sim\sqrt{\varepsilon}\cdot n
antipodes and
\displaystyle\sim\varepsilon\cdot n
neighbors, for a ratio of
\displaystyle\#\text{neighbors}\sim\sqrt{\varepsilon}\cdot\#\text{antipodes}
. In the second example, each point on the arc is antipodal with each point at the center of the circle so there are
\displaystyle~\sim\sqrt{\varepsilon}\cdot n^{2}
antipodes, and there are
\displaystyle\sim\varepsilon\cdot n^{2}
neighbors on the arc, again for a ratio of
\displaystyle\#\text{neighbors}\sim\sqrt{\varepsilon}\cdot\#\text{antipodes}
.

These examples indicate that
\displaystyle\sim\sqrt{\varepsilon}
is a natural candidate for the optimal scaling. In [1], Steinerberger proves the following weaker result:

Theorem 1.1 (Steinerberger):
There exists a universal constant
\displaystyle c>0
such that for all
\displaystyle\varepsilon>0
and any set
\displaystyle\left\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\right\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
with diameter
\displaystyle\leq 1
,

\#\left\{1\leq i<j\leq n:\|x_{i}-x_{j}\|\leq\varepsilon\right\}\geq\frac{c\cdot\varepsilon^{3/4}}{\left(\log\varepsilon^{-1}\right)^{1/4}}\cdot\#\left\{1\leq i<j\leq n:\|x_{i}-x_{j}\|\geq 1-\varepsilon\right\}.

We shall improve his bound to the optimal asymptotic, up to a polylog factor:

Theorem 1.2:
There exists a universal constant
\displaystyle c>0
such that for all
\displaystyle\varepsilon>0
and any set
\displaystyle\left\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\right\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
with diameter
\displaystyle\leq 1
,

\#\left\{1\leq i<j\leq n:\|x_{i}-x_{j}\|\leq\varepsilon\right\}\geq\frac{c\cdot\varepsilon^{1/2}}{\left(\log\varepsilon^{-1}\right)^{1/2}}\cdot\#\left\{1\leq i<j\leq n:\|x_{i}-x_{j}\|\geq 1-\varepsilon\right\}.

2
Refinements

Steinerberger’s proof sketch is as follows:

(0)

It is enough to prove the theorem for
\displaystyle\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small.

(0)

In any extremal example all points are within distance
\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon
of the boundary of the convex hull of the points, since any points inside this boundary can contribute to the neighbor count but not the antipode count.

(0)

One may partition the resulting boundary neighborhood into
\displaystyle\lesssim\varepsilon^{-1}
sets of diameter
\displaystyle<\varepsilon/2
, which naturally leads to a bound in the form of a quadratic form.

(0)

Rephrase the problem as bounding the largest eigenvalue
\displaystyle\lambda_{1}(M)
of the adjacency matrix
\displaystyle M
of the “antipode” graph
\displaystyle G=(V,E)
. Since
\displaystyle M
is symmetric,
\displaystyle\lambda_{1}(M)=\sqrt{\lambda_{1}(M^{T}M)}
, and

\lambda_{1}(M^{T}M)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}(M^{T}M)=\mbox{tr}(M^{T}M)=2|E|.

Thus one may produce a bound by estimating
\displaystyle|E|
.

(0)

Bound
\displaystyle|E|
indirectly by bounding the size of common neighborhoods of pairs of vertices in
\displaystyle V
.

Steinerberger notes that his bound loosens in step (4), since the matrix
\displaystyle M^{T}M
is positive semi-definite and the trace can be much larger than the largest eigenvalue. This is where we refine his argument; by moving from a global trace argument to a local Collatz-Wielandt formula [2], we can bypass the “wasteful” degree sums and arrive at the optimal spectral rate. In particular, we:

(0*)

Bound
\displaystyle\lambda_{1}(M)
using Collatz-Wielandt and Cauchy-Schwarz:

\lambda_{1}(M)\leq\max_{i}\sqrt{\sum_{j\in N(i)}d_{j}}

where
\displaystyle d_{i}
denotes the degree of vertex
\displaystyle i
(corresponding to the adjacency matrix
\displaystyle M
) and
\displaystyle N(i)
denotes the neighbor set of vertex
\displaystyle i
.

(0*)

Sharpen Steinerberger’s common neighbor bound using a careful analysis of annuli intersection dimensions, and use it directly.

3
Definitions and Problem Reduction

Following [1], let
\displaystyle K\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be the convex body of diameter
\displaystyle 1
that is the convex hull of our point set. We discretize the boundary
\displaystyle\partial K
into
\displaystyle k\sim\varepsilon^{-1}
boxes of length
\displaystyle\varepsilon/2
. Let
\displaystyle G=(V,E)
be the antipodal graph where vertices
\displaystyle i,j
are connected if boxes
\displaystyle B_{i}
and
\displaystyle B_{j}
satisfy
\displaystyle\max_{x\in B_{i},y\in B_{j}}\|x-y\|\geq 1-\varepsilon
. Let
\displaystyle M
be the
\displaystyle k\times k
adjacency matrix of this graph, defined by:

M_{ij}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }\max_{x\in B_{i},y\in B_{j}}\|x-y\|\geq 1-\varepsilon\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

The degree of a vertex
\displaystyle i
is
\displaystyle d_{i}=\sum_{j}M_{ij}\approx|L_{i}|/\varepsilon
, where
\displaystyle L_{i}
is the arc on
\displaystyle\partial K
corresponding to the
\displaystyle\varepsilon
-antipodal set of
\displaystyle i
. To prove the measure theorem regarding the density of antipodes, Steinerberger shows the problem reduces to bounding the spectral radius
\displaystyle\lambda_{1}(M)
. Specifically, if
\displaystyle\lambda_{1}=O(\varepsilon^{-\alpha})
, the ratio of neighbors to antipodes is
\displaystyle\gtrsim\varepsilon^{\alpha}.

We transform the problem into a statement about the
\displaystyle d_{i}
by employing the Collatz-Wielandt formula, which implies that for any positive vector
\displaystyle x\in\mathbb{R}^{k}

\lambda_{1}(M)\leq\max_{i}\frac{(Mx)_{i}}{x_{i}}.

Taking
\displaystyle x_{i}=\sqrt{d_{i}}
(we may assume without loss of generality that
\displaystyle G
has no lone vertices) yields

\displaystyle\displaystyle\lambda_{1}(M)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq\max_{i}\frac{1}{\sqrt{d_{i}}}\sum_{j\in N(i)}\sqrt{d_{j}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq\max_{i}\frac{1}{\sqrt{d_{i}}}\sqrt{(\underbrace{1+1+\cdots+1}_{d_{i}\text{ ones}})\sum_{j\in N(i)}d_{j}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\max_{i}\sqrt{\sum_{j\in N(i)}d_{j}}

where the second inequality follows directly from Cauchy-Schwarz.

Thus it suffices to show that high-degree vertices must have (on average) low-degree neighbors. We do this by examining the common neighborhoods of pairs of vertices corresponding to sufficiently distant boxes.

4
Annuli Intersections

Before we return to the graph-theoretic formulation, we proceed with a lemma concerning the size of thin annuli intersections; here we go into more detail than [1], as we require a more precise bound.

Lemma 4.1
. Let
\displaystyle A_{0}
and
\displaystyle A_{1}
be two annuli in
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{2}
with centers at distance
\displaystyle 4\varepsilon\leq d\leq 1
from one another, inner radii
\displaystyle 1-\varepsilon
, and outer radii
\displaystyle 1
. Then the intersection of the two annuli consists of two regions, each a curvilinear quadrilateral with horizontal span
\displaystyle\lesssim\varepsilon/d
and maximum vertical height
\displaystyle\lesssim\varepsilon
. In particular, these regions may be covered by
\displaystyle\lesssim 1/d
boxes of size
\displaystyle\varepsilon/2\times\varepsilon/2
.

Proof:
Following [1], let the centers of the annuli be at points
\displaystyle(-d/2,0)
and
\displaystyle(d/2,0)
respectively. Then, focusing only on the intersection points above the
\displaystyle x
-axis (since the region below the
\displaystyle x
-axis is symmetric), we find the following four intersection points:

•

Two points on the
\displaystyle y
-axis at coordinates
\displaystyle\left(0,\frac{\sqrt{4-d^{2}}}{2}\right)
and
\displaystyle\left(0,\frac{\sqrt{4-d^{2}+4\varepsilon^{2}-8\varepsilon}}{2}\right)

•

Two points symmetric with coordinates
\displaystyle\left(\pm\frac{2\varepsilon-\varepsilon^{2}}{2d},\frac{\sqrt{-d^{4}+2d^{2}\varepsilon^{2}-4d^{2}\varepsilon+4d^{2}-\varepsilon^{4}+4\varepsilon^{3}-4\varepsilon^{2}}}{2d}\right)

The horizontal distance between the second pair of points is
\displaystyle\approx 2\varepsilon/d
which implies that the horizontal span of the curvilinear quadrilateral is
\displaystyle\lesssim\varepsilon/d
. Furthermore, because the maximum
\displaystyle x
-coordinate among points in the intersection region is
\displaystyle\varepsilon/d
, the maximum vertical height is upper bounded by the maximum vertical height of the annulus
\displaystyle A_{0}
at a
\displaystyle d/2+\varepsilon/d
horizontal offset from center. Because
\displaystyle 4\varepsilon\leq d\leq 1
we have that
\displaystyle d/2+\varepsilon/d\leq 0.51
, and at this maximum offset the vertical height of the annulas is at most
\displaystyle\sqrt{1-0.51^{2}}-\sqrt{(1-\varepsilon)^{2}-0.51^{2}}<1.2\varepsilon
, concluding the proof of the lemma. ∎

This lemma will allow us to bound the common neighborhoods of pairs of points in
\displaystyle V
in the same way as [1]. Note that [1] only uses this result for
\displaystyle d\gtrsim\sqrt{\varepsilon}
, while we require the full domain
\displaystyle d\gtrsim\varepsilon
. The vertical span of the curvilinear quadrilateral is as large as
\displaystyle O\left(\varepsilon+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{d^{2}}\right)
, so our more precise analysis of its vertical thickness was required to extend the domain.

For our explicit use case in Section
\displaystyle 5
, we require an easy corollary of Lemma
\displaystyle 4.1
:

Corollary 4.2
. Let
\displaystyle B_{0}
and
\displaystyle B_{1}
be two
\displaystyle\varepsilon/2\times\varepsilon/2
boxes with centers at distance
\displaystyle 12\varepsilon\leq d\leq 1
. Additionally, let
\displaystyle R_{0}
be the union of all annuli with centers in
\displaystyle B_{0}
and inner and outer radii
\displaystyle 1-\varepsilon
and
\displaystyle 1
, and define
\displaystyle R_{1}
similarly. Then
\displaystyle R=R_{0}\cap R_{1}
may be covered by
\displaystyle\lesssim 1/d
boxes of size
\displaystyle\varepsilon/2\times\varepsilon/2
.

Proof.
It is clear that
\displaystyle R_{0}
is contained in the annulus
\displaystyle A_{0}
with the same center as
\displaystyle B_{0}
and inner and outer radii
\displaystyle 1-2\varepsilon
and
\displaystyle 1+\varepsilon
. Similarly,
\displaystyle R_{1}
is contained in the corresponding annulus
\displaystyle A_{1}
. Because
\displaystyle R
must then be contained in the intersection of the two annuli, we immediately recover the result by applying Lemma
\displaystyle 4.1
with a small rescaling of
\displaystyle\varepsilon
. ∎

5
Neighbor Bounds

Now, consider any vertex
\displaystyle i\in V
, and let
\displaystyle W\subseteq V
be the set of vertices
\displaystyle w
for which
\displaystyle\min_{x\in B_{i},y\in B_{w}}\|x-y\|\leq 100\varepsilon
. By double counting, it is easy to see that

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{j\in N(i)}d_{j}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in V}|N(j)\cap N(i)|

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in W}|N(j)\cap N(i)|+\sum_{j\in V\setminus W}|N(j)\cap N(i)|

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq|W|d_{i}+\sum_{j\in V\setminus W}|N(j)\cap N(i)|.

Clearly
\displaystyle d_{i}\leq k-1
and because
\displaystyle K
is convex, there are
\displaystyle\lesssim\varepsilon^{-1}d
boxes at distance
\displaystyle d
from
\displaystyle B_{i}
. Thus by definition
\displaystyle|W|=O(1)
, so the first term in the sum is
\displaystyle O(k)
.

We now may repeat the proof of Lemma
\displaystyle 2.6
from [1] with our more careful annuli intersection result in Corollary
\displaystyle 4.2
(allowing for
\displaystyle d\gtrsim\varepsilon
rather than just
\displaystyle d\geq\sqrt{\varepsilon}
) to show that for each
\displaystyle 1\leq s\leq k
, the number of vertices in
\displaystyle V\setminus W
that have at least
\displaystyle s
common neighbors with
\displaystyle i
is bounded by
\displaystyle ck/s
for some universal constant
\displaystyle c
. Thus

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{j\in V\setminus W}|N(j)\cap N(i)|

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\sum_{s=1}^{k}|\{j\in V\setminus W:|N(i)\cap N(j)|\geq s\}|

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq c\sum_{s=1}^{k}\frac{k}{s}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\lesssim k\log{k}.

Substituting into our degree bound in Section
\displaystyle 3
yields that
\displaystyle\lambda_{1}(M)=O\left(\varepsilon^{-1/2}\log{(1/\varepsilon)}^{1/2}\right)
, implying Theorem
\displaystyle 1.2
. ∎
