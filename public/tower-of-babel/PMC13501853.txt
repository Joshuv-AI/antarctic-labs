Effect of blood decontamination methods on dentin bond strength across adhesive systems: a systematic review
Authors: Katayoun Katebi, Soodabeh Kimyai, Fatemeh Salehnia, Hosein Azizi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis systematic review aimed to investigate the effect of different blood decontamination methods on dentin bond strength across various adhesive systems.MethodsThis systematic review was conducted in accordance with PRISMA 2020 guidelines and registered in PROSPERO (CRD42022381287). A comprehensive electronic search of Medline/PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, and Embase was performed up to February 26, 2026. Introduction

Effective adhesion between composite resins and dental tissues is essential for the long-term success and durability of restorations [1, 2]. Contamination of the bonding substrate with blood, saliva, gingival fluid, hemostatic agents, or handpiece oil can significantly compromise adhesion and reduce bond strength [3]. Despite advances in adhesive technology, contamination of the bonding surface remains a persistent clinical challenge [4].

Although rubber dam isolation is the gold standard for preventing contamination, it is not routinely used in clinical practice due to time-consuming application, technique sensitivity, operator training requirements and patient discomfort [5, 6]. Blood is a particularly problematic contaminant during restorative procedures. Several studies have demonstrated that blood contamination reduces dentin bond strength [7–9]. Plasma proteins adsorbed on the dentin surface can interfere with adhesive monomer infiltration into dentinal tubules, disrupting hybrid layer formation and impairing adhesion [10]. Residual blood components may remain even after water rinsing, adversely affecting adhesive bonding by interfering with proper resin penetration and polymerization [11]. Electron microscopy studies show that resin tags formed under contaminated conditions are fewer and shorter than those in uncontaminated controls [12].

Contamination during bonding can lead to clinical complications such as post-restoration sensitivity, marginal discoloration, microleakage, and recurrent caries [13]. The effects of contamination are influenced by multiple factors, including adhesive type, substrate characteristics, stage of contamination, and the method of decontamination [3, 14]. Contamination may occur at different stages of the bonding process, including before or after etching, before or after adhesive application, or after adhesive curing, and the timing can affect both bond strength and marginal adaptation of restorations [14, 15].

To address contamination-related challenges, adhesives with increased moisture tolerance and simplified application steps (e.g., self-etch and single-bottle adhesives) have been developed [16, 17]. Various decontamination strategies, including mechanical cleaning and chemical agents such as sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl) alone or in combination with antioxidants like sodium ascorbate, have been proposed to restore bond strength after blood contamination [11].

Despite numerous in vitro studies evaluating the effects of blood contamination and decontamination, no systematic review has comprehensively synthesized the evidence on how different blood decontamination methods influence the bond strength of adhesive systems to dentin. Therefore, the objective of this systematic review was to evaluate the impact of different blood decontamination strategies on dentin bond strength across adhesive systems.

Materials and methods

Protocol registration and reporting standards

This systematic review was conducted in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 statement for reporting systematic reviews [18]. The review protocol was prospectively registered in the PROSPERO database (CRD42022381287). The study received approval from the Ethics Committee of Tabriz University of Medical Sciences (IR.TBZMED.VCR.REC.1401.358).

Focused research question and eligibility criteria

The research question was formulated using a modified PICO framework appropriate for laboratory-based investigations:

Population (P): In vitro dentin specimens derived from human or bovine teeth.

Intervention (I): Blood decontamination protocols applied to blood-contaminated dentin surfaces.

Outcome (O): Dentin bond strength.

The focused question was: What is the effect of different blood decontamination methods on the bond strength of dentin when using different adhesive systems under laboratory conditions?

Information sources and search strategy

A comprehensive and systematic literature search was conducted in Medline/PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, and Embase. All databases were searched from inception to February 26, 2026. Search strategies combined keywords related to dentin, blood contamination, decontamination procedures, adhesive systems, and bond strength testing. Database-specific search strategies were adapted to account for indexing differences. The complete electronic search strategies for all databases are provided in Additional file 1.

Additionally, the reference lists and citation trails of all included studies and relevant reviews were manually examined by (K.K and S.K) to identify further eligible articles. When essential data were missing or unclear, corresponding authors were contacted for clarification. All retrieved records were imported into EndNote Basic software, and duplicates were removed prior to screening.

Eligibility criteria

Studies were eligible for inclusion if they met all of the following criteria: In vitro studies, use of blood as a contaminant on dentin substrates, evaluation of the effect of a decontamination protocol on dentin bond strength, use of resin-based adhesive systems, reporting of quantitative bond strength outcomes, published in English.

Studies were excluded if they used heparinized blood or mixture of blood and saliva as the contaminant, used deciduous teeth, were clinical studies, narrative reviews, systematic reviews, case reports, or conference abstracts.

Study selection

Study selection followed the PRISMA 2020 flow process (Fig. 1). Two reviewers (K.K. and S.K.) independently screened titles and abstracts to identify potentially relevant studies. Full texts of eligible articles were retrieved and assessed independently against the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion with a third reviewer (H.A.). Lastly, the reviewers thoroughly analyzed the full-text of the included articles.

PRISMA flow diagram of the study selection process

Risk of bias assessment

The methodological quality and risk of bias of the included studies were assessed independently by two reviewers (K.K and S.K) using the Quality Assessment Tool for In Vitro Studies (QUIN) [19]. This tool evaluates 12 methodological domains related to sample preparation, standardization, blinding, and outcome reporting. Each study was categorized as having low, moderate, or high risk of bias based on overall QUIN scores. Each domain was scored as 2 (adequately specified), 1 (inadequately specified), or 0 (not specified), while non-applicable criteria were excluded from scoring. The final score for each study was calculated as: total score × 100 / (2 × number of applicable criteria). Based on the overall percentage score, studies were classified as having low risk of bias (> 70%), moderate risk of bias (50–70%), or high risk of bias (< 50%).

Data extraction

Data extraction was performed independently by two reviewers (K.K and S.K) using a predefined and standardized data extraction form developed in Microsoft Excel. Extracted variables included: authors and year of publication, type and source of dentin substrate, sample size per group, blood contamination protocol (source, timing, duration), decontamination method(s) and protocol details, type of adhesive system, bond strength test method, quantitative bond strength outcomes (mean and standard deviation). Disagreements in data extraction were resolved by consensus.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome was dentin bond strength, expressed in megapascals (MPa). Bond strength was measured using various laboratory testing methods, including micro-tensile bond strength, shear bond strength, micro-shear bond strength, and push-out tests.

Data synthesis and analysis

A qualitative synthesis was conducted. Results were stratified according to decontamination method and adhesive system category. Studies were then compared with respect to reported outcome (bond strength).

Results

Study selection

The electronic search yielded 169 records. After removal of duplicates, 120 records remained for title and abstract screening. Following this process, 22 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility. Seven studies [4, 10, 16, 20–22] were excluded at the full-text stage for reasons including deciduous teeth substrate [16, 20], use of contaminants other than blood alone [4, 10, 21, 22], no specific decontamination procedure [17]. Ultimately, 15 in vitro studies fulfilled the inclusion criteria and were included in the qualitative synthesis. The study selection process is illustrated in the PRISMA 2020 flow diagram (Fig. 1).

Risk of bias assessment

Risk of bias assessment using the QUIN tool showed that 11 studies were judged to have a low risk of bias [11, 15, 23–31], while 4 studies demonstrated a moderate risk of bias [32–35]. No study was classified as having a high risk of bias. The most frequently unmet criteria were related to sample size calculation and operator details. Summary of included studies

Bond strength testing methods

Bond strength was evaluated using different mechanical tests. Nine studies used micro-tensile bond strength [11, 23–27, 31–33], five studies used shear or micro-shear bond strength [28–30, 34, 35], and one used Push-out bond strength test [15].

Substrate and aging protocols

Twelve studies used human dentin, while three studies [15, 23, 32] employed bovine dentin. All samples were coronal dentine. Aging protocols varied among studies; most used water storage at 37 °C for 24 or 48 h, whereas two studies [15, 24] applied thermocycling between 5 °C and 55 °C with a dwell time of 15 s.

Adhesive systems

Self-etch adhesives were evaluated in five studies [25, 26, 33–35], etch-and-rinse adhesives in four studies [11, 15, 30, 32], and universal adhesives in self-etch mode in five studies [23, 24, 27, 28, 31]. One study evaluated an etch-and-rinse adhesive and a universal adhesive in self-etch mode [29].

Qualitative synthesis of findings

Due to substantial heterogeneity in contamination protocols, decontamination procedures, adhesive systems, aging protocols, and bond strength testing methods, qualitative synthesis was performed. Across all included studies, blood contamination without subsequent decontamination consistently resulted in reduced dentin bond strength, regardless of adhesive type or testing method.

Influence of timing of blood contamination

Blood contamination was introduced before adhesive polymerization in 12 experimental protocols [11, 15, 24–28, 30, 32–35] and after adhesive curing in 10 protocols [15, 23–25, 27, 29–31, 33, 34]. Timing of contamination influences the outcome; however, degree and pattern of this effect vary across studies.

Effectiveness of decontamination strategies

Mechanical approaches such as water rinsing, air-drying, blot-drying, and re-application of adhesive were frequently evaluated [24–27, 33, 34]. Some studies reported that re-application of the adhesive following rinsing and drying improved bond strength compared with no decontamination [15, 24, 30], although complete recovery to uncontaminated control levels was not consistently observed.

Chemical agents investigated included NaOCl [11, 15, 28, 29, 31], hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) [11, 23, 35], ethanol [11, 30], chlorhexidine [23], and antioxidant combinations such as NaOCl with sodium ascorbate or proanthocyanidin [11, 23, 28]. Chemical decontamination generally resulted in greater bond strength recovery than mechanical methods alone; however, outcomes varied depending on adhesive type and contamination timing.

Protocols combining mechanical and chemical approaches demonstrated variable effectiveness. In some studies, combined strategies achieved bond strength values comparable to uncontaminated controls, whereas in others, only partial recovery was observed.

Discussion

Blood contamination remains a clinically relevant challenge in adhesive dentistry, particularly in subgingival areas, or deep cervical margins, where optimal isolation is difficult [5, 6]. This systematic review qualitatively synthesized the available in vitro evidence on the effect of different blood decontamination strategies on dentin bond strength across adhesive systems. Consistent across included studies, blood contamination resulted in a reduction in dentin bond strength compared with uncontaminated controls. This finding was independent of adhesive strategy or bond strength testing method and confirms that blood is a potent contaminant at the adhesive–dentin interface. The negative effect of blood contamination has been attributed to the presence of macromolecular proteins that interfere with dentin surface energy, resin infiltration, and polymerization processes [36].

The effect of decontamination methods appeared to be influenced by the timing of blood exposure relative to adhesive application and curing. When contamination occurred prior to adhesive polymerization, studies suggested that blood components may interfere with dentinal tubule access and hybrid layer formation [15]. In contrast, contamination occurring after adhesive curing was reported to affect the adhesive surface and its interaction with subsequent resin layers [33]. However, the degree and nature of these effects varied across studies. Contamination before adhesive polymerization was more amenable to reversal. In Kucukyilmaz et al., contamination of uncured universal adhesives followed by adhesive reapplication restored bond strength close to control values [27]. Similarly, Shaikh et al. reported that adhesive reapplication after water spraying and air-drying increased bond strength to 20.0 ± 5.6 MPa, approaching the uncontaminated control (23.2 ± 6.2 MPa) [24]. In contrast, contamination after adhesive polymerization was generally more difficult to reverse. Elkassas and Arafa reported that post-cure contamination reduced bond strength to 4.2 ± 1.2 MPa, re-etching followed by adhesive reapplication increased values further to 16.7 ± 3.2 MPa; However, the same protocol in pre curing contamination had increased the bond strength to 28.9 ± 2.2 more close to the non-contaminated control (32.3 ± 1.6 MPa) [30].

A similar stage-dependent effect was reported by Mendonça et al. When contamination occurred before primer and bond application, bond strength remained comparable to the control regardless of whether water rinsing/drying or blot-drying was used. However, contamination after primer, bond application, and curing substantially reduced bond strength, particularly after blot-drying alone, which resulted in complete bond failure [33].

Mechanical decontamination strategies, including water rinsing, air-drying, blot-drying, and adhesive reapplication, frequently improved bond strength relative to untreated contamination, although outcomes were inconsistent and occasionally detrimental depending on adhesive system and contamination timing [11, 30]. Adhesive reapplication after cleaning was consistently more effective than rinsing alone in several studies. In Shaikh et al., water spraying and air-drying alone after contamination of uncured adhesive resulted in complete bond failure (0 MPa), whereas subsequent adhesive reapplication increased bond strength to 20.0 ± 5.6 MPa [24].

Yoo and Pereira reported that water rinsing, air-drying, and adhesive reapplication improved bond strength in One-Up Bond F. In contrast, the same protocol reduced performance in Xeno III from and Adper Prompt L-Pop [25]. These findings indicate that mechanical cleaning efficacy is strongly material-dependent and may be influenced by solvent system, adhesive acidity, viscosity, and hydrophilicity [10].

Chemical decontamination protocols generally produced superior outcomes to mechanical cleaning alone when appropriately selected. Sodium hypochlorite demonstrated beneficial effects in several studies, likely through dissolution of organic blood residues. Elkassas and Arafa reported improvement to 23.9 ± 1.1 MPa after application of 5.25% NaOCl following contamination after acid etching [30]. Juneja et al. similarly reported improved bond strength after 10% NaOCl treatment [11]. However, NaOCl alone did not consistently restore control values, likely because residual oxygen radicals may inhibit adhesive polymerization [28, 29, 35].

This limitation appeared to be partially overcome by antioxidant application. In Juneja et al., NaOCl followed by sodium ascorbate restored Prime & Bond NT to 24.3 ± 3.9 MPa, essentially equivalent to the 24.4 ± 2.8 MPa control, while Adper Single Bond 2 improved to 20.2 ± 8.6 MPa but remained below control (24.7 ± 3.2) [11]. Similarly, Kewlani et al. demonstrated improvement in bond strength following addition of sodium ascorbate and proanthocyanidin to NaOCl protocol [28]. These findings support the role of antioxidants in neutralizing residual oxygen radicals and improving polymerization [37].

Gaviolli et al. evaluated chlorhexidine and reported some recovery after contamination of polymerized Scotchbond Universal [23]. Hydrogen peroxide was not much effective in restoring the bond strength [11, 23, 35].

Recent evidence from Liu et al. demonstrated that spray rinsing alone was ineffective after blood contamination of polymerized Single Bond Universal, whereas phosphoric acid, sodium hypochlorite, antioxidant combinations, and diamond bur repreparation restored bond strength to approximately control values [31]. Although diamond bur repreparation restored bond strength completely, it is invasive and sacrifices sound tooth structure, limiting clinical conservativeness.

The variability observed across studies likely reflects differences in adhesive composition, contamination timing, blood volume, contamination duration, decontamination sequence, aging conditions, and bond strength testing methodology. These material-dependent factors help explain why similar decontamination protocols yielded divergent results across studies.

In many studies, these parameters were inadequately reported, limiting comparability and reproducibility.

Limitations

This systematic review has several limitations. First, all included studies were in vitro investigations, which inherently limit direct clinical extrapolation. Second, substantial methodological heterogeneity precluded quantitative meta-analysis and necessitated a qualitative synthesis. Third, many studies lacked standardized contamination protocols, detailed sample size reporting, and long-term aging procedures. Fourth, although bovine dentin was used in some studies, prior evidence suggests that its bonding behavior is comparable to human dentin; nonetheless, substrate variability remains a potential confounder. Finally, the absence of standardized testing protocols across studies, inconsistencies in experimental design—such as the absence of non-decontaminated contaminated controls or incomplete reporting of specimen allocation in micro-tensile testing—further complicate interpretation of results.

Clinical implications and future directions

While in vitro findings cannot fully predict clinical performance, the consistent negative impact of blood contamination on dentin bonding underscores the importance of preventing contamination whenever possible. When contamination occurs before adhesive polymerization, rinsing followed by adhesive reapplication appears to be the most consistently effective mechanical strategy. In etch-and-rinse systems, phosphoric acid re-etching may be beneficial in selected contamination scenarios, particularly after contamination following acid etching. Sodium hypochlorite may be useful for removal of organic contaminants, especially when followed by antioxidants such as sodium ascorbate or proanthocyanidin. In contrast, contamination after adhesive curing is more difficult to reverse, and simple rinsing or drying alone is generally insufficient. Future research should focus on standardized contamination models, clinically relevant aging protocols, and long-term bond durability. Establishing evidence-based decontamination protocols specific to adhesive categories would substantially improve clinical decision-making in restorative dentistry.

Conclusion

Blood contamination consistently reduces dentin bond strength regardless of adhesive system, with the most severe effects typically occurring when contamination happens after polymerization. Although various decontamination methods can partially improve bonding, full recovery to uncontaminated levels is not reliably achieved. Some approaches, such as adhesive reapplication and phosphoric acid re-etching in etch-and-rinse systems, as well as sodium hypochlorite combined with antioxidants, show the most promising results, but no single protocol is universally effective. Management should therefore be tailored to the adhesive system and contamination stage, and further research is needed to establish standardized clinical guidelines.
