Simulation-Based Predictions to Support the Development of a Pediatric Fixed-Dose Combination of Dolutegravir/Emtricitabine/Tenofovir Alafenamide Fumarate
Authors: Hylke Waalewijn, Lisanne A. H. Bevers, Linda L. Lewis, Margaret D. Louey, Sonoko Kawakatsu, Yanan Zheng, Carlo Giaquinto, Alessandra Nardone, David M. Burger, Rob ter Heine, Angela Colbers, Elin M. Svensson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and ObjectiveApproximately 1.4 million children (aged 0–14 years) are living with human immunodeficiency virus, most infected through mother-to-child transmission. Despite the availability of combination antiretroviral treatment, only 57% of children had access to combination antiretroviral treatment in 2023, with suboptimal formulations contributing to high mortality. Limited access to age-appropriate formulations is a key barrier, particularly in low- and middle-income countries. This study presents stochastic simulations with previously published population pharmacokinetic models to determine an optimal fixed-dose combination (FDC) ratio of dolutegravir (DTG), emtricitabine (FTC), and tenofovir alafenamide (TAF) for children weighing 3–20 kg. For children weighing more than 20 kg, the use of the adult FDC formulation is proposed.MethodsPopulation pharmacokinetic models were used for exposure simulation of DTG, TAF, and its metabolite tenofovir. The adequacy of the proposed FDC was based on whether the simulated drug exposures met predefined efficacy and safety targets. For DTG, the minimum trough concentration target of 0.32 mg/L; for TAF, the area under the curve (AUC) target of 55 ng*h/mL; and for tenofovir, the maximum AUC of 2586 ng*h/mL were used. Simulated exposures were also compared to adult reference values: geometric mean trough concentration for DTG (0.83 mg/L), median AUC for TAF (185 h*ng/mL), and median AUC for tenofovir (256 h*ng/mL). The US Food and Drug Administration-recognized dosing range for FTC was used to verify appropriate dosing in each weight band.ResultsThe modeling approach identified a practical FDC dosing ratio for children across weight bands (3–20 kg). Simulated DTG exposure exceeded the minimum target in over 98% of cases, while TAF exposure met the efficacy threshold in more than 90% of children. Tenofovir exposure remained below toxic concentrations in all weight bands, ensuring safety. In a small subset of children aged under 3 months, FTC doses exceeded the approved maximum, though overall dosing remained within safe limits.ConclusionsThis paper presents a modeling approach to determine an optimal FDC ratio for pediatric DTG/FTC/TAF. The strategy predicted effective drug exposure and safety across age and weight bands, supporting the development of a child-friendly FDC that could accelerate access to human immunodeficiency virus treatment in low- and middle-income countries. This dosing regimen is being further evaluated in the UNIVERSAL-1 clinical trial.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s40262-026-01671-z.

Key Points

This study used modeling and simulation techniques to design an optimal dosing ratio for a pediatric combination tablet of dolutegravir, emtricitabine, and tenofovir alafenamide fumarate for human immunodeficiency virus treatment in children weighing 3–20 kg.The proposed dosing strategy showed that drug concentrations were sufficient to be effective in children weighing more than 3 kg, with safety levels well within the approved ranges, ensuring a minimal risk of toxicity.This research supports the development of a pediatric-friendly human immunodeficiency virus treatment that could improve access to care for children in low- and middle-income countries, with further clinical testing underway.

Introduction

Approximately 1.4 million children (aged 0–14 years) are living with human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), most infected through vertical transmission [1]. Despite efforts to prevent vertical transmission using combination antiretroviral treatment, new infections still occur, with an estimated 120,000 infants newly infected in 2023, mainly in sub-Saharan Africa [1]. While prompt and continuous antiretroviral treatment enables these children to survive and thrive, only 57% had access to combination antiretroviral treatment in 2023, and many received suboptimal formulations, contributing to 84,000 child deaths in that year [1]. Limited access to age-appropriate formulations remains a key barrier, as pediatric drug development lags behind adult formulations. This challenge is further exacerbated by recent political shifts and funding cuts to global health programs, which threaten access to life-saving therapies and put prevention efforts at risk. To address these ongoing challenges, the World Health Organization (WHO) Paediatric ARV Drug Optimization group has defined optimal formulations essential for children living with HIV (CLHIV) [2].

One of the formulations on the Paediatric ARV Drug Optimization priority list is a fixed-dose combination (FDC) of dolutegravir/emtricitabine/tenofovir alafenamide fumarate (DTG/FTC/TAF) as a dispersible tablet (DT). Dolutegravir (DTG) DT has been widely introduced and is approved for children aged ≥4 weeks and weighing ≥ 3 kg, while a co-formulated FTC/TAF tablet for oral suspension is in development [3, 4]. Although an FTC/TAF 120/15 mg film-coated tablet (FCT) is approved for children weighing 14–25 kg, it is not suitable for children who cannot swallow tablets or weigh less than 14 kg. Fixed-dose combinations improve adherence, minimize dosing errors, facilitate logistics, and reduce the risk of resistance by preventing monotherapy [5, 6]. In this article, we refer to both the DTG DT tablets and the FTC/TAF tablet for oral suspension tablets as DT, as both formulations can be dissolved in water to form an oral suspension. This terminology is used throughout the text for ease of recognition.

The WHO HIV guidelines (2021) recommend DTG/FTC/TAF for adults in specific cases, such as renal impairment or osteoporosis [7]. Tenofovir alafenamide fumarate is increasingly preferred over tenofovir disoproxil fumarate (TDF) in these cases because of its better renal and bone safety profile. Unlike TDF, which is converted to tenofovir (TFV) in the bloodstream, TAF is primarily converted to the active metabolite tenofovir-diphosphate (TFV-DP) inside the cells, resulting in much lower TFV plasma concentrations [8]. The intra-cellular conversion leads to a reduced risk of renal and bone toxicity, making TAF a safer option for adults with pre-existing conditions. Although potential weight gain has been a concern with TAF-based regimens in adults, particularly in combination with integrase inhibitors such as DTG, studies suggest that benefits generally outweigh risks [9, 10]. For children, TAF is preferred over TDF owing to its more favorable safety profile, especially regarding bone toxicity. It is also effective against both HIV and hepatitis B virus, making it the recommended choice for pediatric treatment in many regions, including the USA and Europe [11].

Currently, the DTG 50-mg FCT is approved for children weighing ≥ 20 kg, and the FTC/TAF 200/25 mg FCT is approved for children weighing ≥ 25 kg [12, 13]. Generic DTG/FTC/TAF 50/200/25 mg FDC products are available in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) for adults. If children weighing 20–25 kg could use this adult FDC, they would gain quicker access to existing formulations. However, for children weighing 3–20 kg, an FDC DT is essential to optimize care in LMICs. Identifying a suitable fixed ratio would allow for concurrent FDC development and clinical studies, accelerating access to this essential therapy in LMICs.

To develop such an FDC, a fixed-dose ratio must achieve acceptable exposure across all weight bands. Dolutegravir dosing increases in 5-mg increments, while FTC/TAF doses double per weight band until reaching adult doses. As a result, deviations from current dosing guidelines are unavoidable for this FDC.

In this study, we used population pharmacokinetic (popPK) modeling to design an optimal pediatric DTG/FTC/TAF FDC that aligns as closely as possible with existing dosing recommendations. We focused on practicality by restricting our approach to the approved DTG 5 mg DT and the FTC/TAF 15/1.88 mg DT (currently in development), in which we preferred not deviating from the approved dolutegravir dosages. Our goal was to develop effective FDC options for children weighing 3–25 kg, and to accelerate the development of a pediatric FDC by simulating drug exposures with popPK models. Identifying a suitable fixed ratio of the approved or in-development originator products would allow for concurrent FDC development and clinical studies, accelerating access to this essential therapy in LMICs. This dosing strategy will be confirmed in the ongoing Penta UNIVERSAL-1 study (NCT05993767) to optimize pediatric HIV treatment.

Methods

We investigated if using pediatric DTG 5 mg DT and FTC/TAF 15/1.88 mg DT aligned with WHO weight band cut-off points (3 to < 6 kg, 6 to < 10 kg, 10 to < 14 kg, and 14 to < 20 kg) could yield a dosing table with acceptable exposure to each FDC component. Similarly, we examined the use of the DTG/FTC/TAF 50/200/25 mg FCT for the 20–25 kg weight band. Pharmacokinetic Models

The popPK models in our study were those used previously by the originator companies for dose selection in pediatric populations. The originator pop PK model for DTG was a one-compartment model with first-order absorption and elimination [4]. Body weight, age, fasting status, and formulation (DT or FCT) were significant predictors of DTG pharmacokinetics. The model was developed with data from 239 children with a mean (standard deviation) weight of 21.1 (14.8) kg, including children down to 3 kg and older than 4 weeks of age [14, 15]. Maturation was included using a sigmoidal function based on postmenstrual age. The originator TAF model was a one-compartment model with sequential zero- and first-order absorption, first-order elimination, and fixed allometric scaling of clearance and volume of distribution (allometric exponents of 0.75 and 1, respectively). This model incorporated maturation of clearance based on the ontogenic profile of carboxylesterase 1, the primary enzyme responsible for intracellular TAF hydrolysis [16, 17]. Asian race and coadministration with cobicistat were found to significantly affect TAF pharmacokinetics but are not relevant for our intended population and were therefore not included in the model. The TFV model used individual post-hoc estimates from the TAF model as input for the central compartment of the sequential TFV model. The TFV model contained a second compartment and TFV was cleared via first-order elimination. Like the TAF model, the TFV model had fixed allometric scaling of clearance and volumes of distribution. The TAF and TFV analysis dataset included a pediatric population of 337 individuals aged 3–17 years and weighing at least 14 kg with a median weight 38.0 kg. We added maturation of clearance to the TFV model based on maturation of kidney function, using the post-menstrual age as a covariate on clearance, to account for differences in renal function for young children [18]. The models were selected based on the inclusion of children of weights and ages closest to our intended population and the fact that they were the established models that informed the current dosing of both products in pediatric populations. At the time this study was conducted, no suitable models were available for FTC. We therefore chose to base the FTC dosing on a mg/kg target, as further explained under ‘Pharmacokinetic targets’. Figure 4 of the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM) illustrates how age and weight affect apparent clearance in the three models.

Simulations

The simulations were performed based on a dataset including at least 2541 subjects per weight band. Both inter-individual variability and residual variability were incorporated as included in the selected popPK models. Drug exposures were simulated in NONMEM (Version 7.4.1) assuming fasted conditions, a covariate for the DTG model. Simulations were performed including residual and inter-individual variability. For the population, we used an established virtual population that included age, height, and weight based on the US Center for Disease Control and Prevention and WHO growth curves for girls and boys [19]. This virtual population was adjusted to fit African CLHIV based on demographic data from the pediatric HIV ARROW trial [20, 21]. The appropriateness of our simulated population was evaluated against the weight for age and the height for age of children weighing 3–25 kg included in the ODYSSEY trial, a pivotal trial that contributed to the widespread roll-out of dolutegravir in CLHIV [22]. Figures 1a and 1b of the ESM show that the simulated weight and height for age appropriately matched the ODYSSEY data. The estimated glomerular filtration rate was derived for our population by generating serum creatinine values. We plotted reference values reported for children, fitted a regression line, and added variability to approximate the clinical reference values for serum creatinine [23]. The serum creatinine was calculated based on age with a normally distributed between-subject variation with a standard deviation of 0.07 mg/dL (Formula 1) [24]. The creatinine clearance was calculated from serum creatinine using the Schwartz formula for children aged younger or older than 1 year (formulas 2 and 3; Figs. 2 and 3 of the ESM). All data visualization and data handling were performed in R (Version 4.3).

Pharmacokinetic Targets

The primary pharmacokinetic target related to DTG efficacy is the steady-state trough concentration (Ctrough), defined as the concentration at the end of the dosing interval. As it stands, no upper pharmacokinetic target is known for toxicity. Therefore, we compared the geometric mean (GM) Ctrough of our simulated population with the GM Ctrough achieved in adults taking 50 mg of DTG FCT once daily without food (0.83 mg/L) [25]. In addition, we calculated the proportion of individuals in each weight band with Ctrough values exceeding 0.32 mg/L, the concentration associated with 90% of maximal viral load reduction, as derived from a 10-day monotherapy study [25] and confirmed in the SPRING-1 study [26], and by the in vitro protein-adjusted 90% maximum inhibitory concentration (0.064 mg/L) [27]. The dosing strategy was considered acceptable if at least 90% of simulated individuals achieved Ctrough values above the protein-adjusted 90% maximum inhibitory concentration.

We chose the AUC from dose until 24 hours after dose (AUC0–24h) as the primary pharmacokinetic parameter for evaluating TAF exposure. In a TAF monotherapy study, TAF AUC0–24h of 55 ng*h/mL demonstrated efficacy comparable to that of TDF 300 mg in adults [28]. This threshold was further supported by data from phase III efficacy studies, where a wide range of TAF exposures showed robust virological suppression, including individuals in the lowest 10% of exposure with a median AUC0–24h of 53 ng*h/mL [29]. Based on these findings, we selected 55 ng*h/mL as the minimum acceptable exposure threshold. Additionally, the median AUC0–24h per weight band was compared with the TAF adult mean AUC0–24h reported in the EU Descovy label based on population pharmacokinetics, which was 206.4 ng*h/mL [5]. The safety of the proposed TAF dose was evaluated by comparing the simulated TFV AUC0–24h to historical exposures observed in adults receiving TAF 25 mg and in children receiving TDF. As TDF is associated with greater renal and bone toxicity and is generally not recommended for long-term use in children, TFV exposure in our simulated population was considered acceptable only if it remained well below levels historically observed in pediatric patients aged 2–12 years treated with TDF 8 mg/kg (up to a maximum of 300 mg). In that population, a mean TFV AUC0–24h of 2586 ng*h/mL was reported [30]. As an additional benchmark, we targeted simulated TFV exposure to approximate the adult mean AUC0–24h reported in the Descovy EU label based on population pharmacokinetics, which was 292.6 ng*h/mL [5]. For efficacy, the dosing strategy was considered acceptable if at least 90% of simulated individuals achieved a TAF AUC0–24h above the minimum target exposure of 55 ng*h/mL. For safety, no more than 10% of simulated TFV AUC0–24h values were allowed to exceed the predefined toxicity threshold of 2586 ng*h/mL. Emtricitabine targets were not incorporated in this study because it is generally regarded as safe and dosing was fixed to the TAF dose by using the TAF/FTC DT. We aimed for dosing to remain within dosing limits established for Emtriva® prescribing information, which is 3.0 mg/kg/day in children under 3 months of age and 6 mg/kg/day dosing in children above 3 months of age with a margin of 50–200% of the mg/kg dose appropriate for their age (1.5–6 mg/kg/day for children aged < 3 months and 3–12 mg/kg/day in children aged > 3 months [31].

Results

A practical dosing table with a fixed-dose ratio was tested as the first option for the FDC dose, based on registered DTG dosing and doses established for TAF. The suggested dosing table was our first choice because it followed the registered dose of the anchor drug DTG and, given the pharmacokinetic target values, we expected that the TAF dose had enough margin for a changed dose while maintaining therapeutic exposure levels. Relative to the originator’s TAF doses, the dosing of weight bands 6 to < 10 kg, 14 to < 20 kg, and 20 to < 25 kg are different in our proposed FDC-compatible dosing table. As shown in Table 4, the simulated exposure levels exceeded the target TAF AUC0–24h of 55 ng*h/mL in more than 90% of children across all five weight bands. The GM (coefficient of variation %) AUC0–24h for TAF was comparable to or higher than the adult mean of 206.4 ng*h/mL in each weight band, with simulated exposures that ranged from 90 to 145% relative to the reference parameter (Fig. 2b, Table 4).

The safety of children receiving TAF was determined based on simulated TFV exposures shown in Fig. 2c and Table 4. The TFV AUC0–24h was lower than the GM adult exposure (292.6 ng*h/mL) in all weight bands except for the 20–25 kg group, which received the adult TAF dose of 25 mg. However, even in this weight band, the maximum TFV AUC0–24h remained below the maximum exposure target for TFV based on children receiving TDF (2586 ng*h/mL).

The FTC dosing is tied to TAF dosing because of the use of FTC/TAF FDC. For children < 3 months of age and weighing 6–10 kg, the maximum approved dose would be 36 mg (6 kg times the maximum approved dose of 6 mg/kg/day for this age), while in our suggested dosing table, 45 mg is given to these children irrespective of age. This means that children who are not yet 3 months of age or who are at least 3 months but weighing 6–7.5 kg will receive a higher than approved dose. In our simulated dataset, 0.0021% of children in this weight band would be affected by this. However, aside from the children who are younger than 3 months of age and weigh between 6 and 7.5 kg, the proposed FTC dosing generally falls within the approved minimum and maximum dose ranges. Discussion

In this study, we used popPK modeling to identify a fixed-dose ratio of DTG, FTC, and TAF for a pediatric FDC DT for CLHIV weighing 3 to < 20 kg. In addition, we simulated the use of the existing adult DTG/FTC/TAF 50/200/25 mg tablet in children weighing 20 to < 25 kg, to assess whether a safe and effective transition to the adult formulation would be possible in this weight band. This work was conducted as part of the preparation for the UNIVERSAL-1 pharmacokinetic and safety study. The goal was to support the development of a practical FDC suitable for children, using formulations that were either available or in development at the time, and to accelerate access to optimized HIV treatment for children, particularly in LMICs.

A key design decision in this approach was to base the fixed-dose ratio on the registered DTG dose per WHO weight bands. To maintain a fixed ratio, FTC and TAF doses were adjusted proportionally across weight bands. This resulted in differences between the Penta-proposed and originator-proposed FTC/TAF dosing, particularly in the 6 to < 10 kg, 14 to < 20 kg, and 20 to < 25 kg weight bands. These modifications were justified by pharmacokinetic simulations, which demonstrated that exposure targets for TAF and its active metabolite TFV could still be safely and effectively achieved within these adapted doses.

For each weight band, simulated exposures were evaluated against established pharmacokinetic targets for efficacy and safety. At the time of these simulations (2020), these targets reflected the best available data from originator companies’ models and registrational studies. Our results show that more than 90% of children across weight bands are expected to achieve therapeutic drug exposures for DTG and TAF, while maintaining TFV exposures well below the thresholds historically associated with potential toxicity in pediatric TDF use.

In the case of FTC, no suitable model was available at the time of simulation. As a result, FTC dosing was guided by the approved dose range, aiming to remain within 50–200% of the approved pediatric dose. A small proportion of children younger than 3 months of age and weighing 6 to < 10 kg may receive FTC doses above the currently approved maximum, highlighting the need for close monitoring and eventual clinical validation in this subgroup. Nevertheless, the overall exposure levels remained within acceptable safety ranges for most children.

While our results support the feasibility of the proposed FDC dosing approach, it is important to acknowledge the limitations. The pharmacokinetic targets and models used in this study were those available at the time of preparation for the Penta UNIVERSAL-1 pharmacokinetic and safety study, which began in 2020. Since then, new models and more extensive pediatric data have become available. Notably, the TAF-TFV model did not include data from subjects younger than 2 years of age or weighing less than 14 kg; therefore, assumptions regarding the maturation effect had to be made to extrapolate beyond the age and weight ranges represented in the modeling dataset. This reliance on extrapolation introduces uncertainty in the predicted exposures for the youngest and lightest children, and the dosing recommendations for this subgroup should therefore be interpreted with caution until validated in clinical studies.

Our simulation assumes similar bioavailability between the current formulations and the target FDC and assumes no interaction exists between the different compounds in the FDC. An additional relative bioavailability study in which DTG DT and FTC/TAF DT were administered both together and separately in healthy volunteers was conducted to test if an interaction could be expected. The study showed that DTG, FTC, and TFV met the pre-defined criteria for no clinically relevant pharmacokinetic interaction [33]. However, TAF did not meet these criteria, as the 90% confidence intervals for TAF AUC0–24h and maximum concentration fell outside the predefined acceptance limits. Overall, TAF exposure was 17% lower when both formulations were co-administered, but despite this, no consistent effect on TAF pharmacokinetics was observed, with both higher and lower exposures seen, likely due to high inter-subject variability.

As a prodrug of TFV-DP, TAF exposures are commonly reported and used as a marker of clinical efficacy. However, TFV exposures have also served as a surrogate for TFV-DP concentrations [34]. In this study, TFV-DP data were unavailable, making it difficult to predict the clinical impact of reduced TAF exposures alongside sustained TFV exposures. Reassuringly, no clinically relevant pharmacokinetic interaction was observed for TFV. What is additionally known is that when TAF is co-administered with rifampicin, TAF concentrations may decrease slightly, but TFV-DP concentrations remain elevated [35]. This suggests that the clinical efficacy of TAF may still be maintained despite a 17% decrease in exposure (with comparable TFV concentration).

A strength of our approach lies in its ability to leverage existing data to propose a practical dosing strategy that can be applied in real-world settings and adapted to ongoing clinical trials. This approach demonstrates how popPK models can be strategically used in the preparatory phase to simulate dosing ratios and associated exposure, allowing for faster development of a FDC tablet. This approach also highlights the strategy we used in preparation for the UNIVERSAL-1 pharmacokinetic study, which will further investigate the proposed DTG/FTC/TAF (5/15/1.88 mg) dosing regimen. Specifically, the UNIVERSAL-1 trial will assess the pharmacokinetics, short-term efficacy, and safety of the proposed dosing, using co-administered originator DTG 5 mg DT and FTC/TAF 15/1.88 mg DT formulations to confirm our proposed dosing. The long-term safety and effectiveness of this dosing regimen will also be monitored in UNIVERSAL-3, ensuring comprehensive pharmacovigilance data are collected over time.

Conclusions

Using modeling and simulation techniques, we developed a dosing table based on WHO-recommended weight bands to enable a fixed-dose ratio of DTG, FTC, and TAF (DTG/FTC/TAF 5/15/1.88 mg DT) for children aged 4 weeks and older, weighing 3–20 kg. The DTG/FTC/TAF 50/200/25 mg FCT is proposed for 20–25 kg. This dosing strategy is being applied in the clinical phase of the UNIVERSAL-1 study and supports the development of a pediatric FDC, potentially accelerating its availability in LMICs through affordable generics. The proposed DTG/FTC/TAF 5/15/1.88 mg DT offers a child-friendly option with strong antiviral activity and a high barrier to resistance, helping to address treatment disparities in pediatric HIV care.

By utilizing modeling and simulation, we have not only optimized the dosing strategy but also facilitated regulatory filings, ensuring that the pediatric FDC development meets regulatory standards and accelerates the approval process. This approach underscores the critical role of modeling and simulation in both clinical and regulatory development, providing an efficient framework for the timely introduction of pediatric FDCs in LMICs.
