Periconceptional neighborhood deprivation and risk of gestational and postpartum hypertensive disorders in a population-based cohort
Authors: Rana F Chehab, Amanda L Ngo, William A Grobman, Mara B Greenberg, Meti Sima, Ana K Rosen Vollmar, Assiamira Ferrara, Yeyi Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hypertensive disorders during pregnancy and postpartum are major causes of maternal morbidity and mortality, yet associations with social determinants of health, including neighborhood deprivation, remain understudied. We examined the association of periconceptional neighborhood deprivation with the risk of gestational and postpartum hypertensive disorders in a population-based cohort of 281,621 pregnant individuals aged 15–44 years without chronic hypertension who delivered at Kaiser Permanente Northern California from 2011 to 2020. Neighborhood deprivation index (NDI) quartiles were assigned based on geocoded residential addresses before and in early pregnancy. Gestational hypertension and preeclampsia/eclampsia were identified from 20 gestational weeks through delivery, postpartum preeclampsia/eclampsia through six weeks postpartum, and postpartum hypertension through one year postpartum. Modified Poisson regression estimated adjusted relative risks (aRRs) across NDI quartiles. Overall, 16.2% versus 25.3% resided in the least (Q1) versus most deprived (Q4) neighborhoods. Greater neighborhood deprivation was associated with higher risk of gestational hypertension (Q4 vs Q1: aRR, 1.12 [1.07–1.17]), gestational preeclampsia/eclampsia (1.18 [1.12–1.24]), postpartum preeclampsia/eclampsia (1.30 [1.02–1.66]), and postpartum hypertension (1.67 [1.42–1.97]), with dose–response patterns across NDI quartiles. After adjusting for pre-pregnancy body mass index, associations attenuated, remaining significant only for postpartum hypertension (Q4 vs Q1: 1.30 [1.11–1.54]). Stratified analyses showed similar patterns among individuals with overweight/obesity but not those with underweight/healthy weight. These findings underscore the importance of considering neighborhood-level social determinants alongside individual-level risk factors when assessing risk of gestational and postpartum hypertensive disorders and the need for targeted interventions to reduce disparities and improve maternal health outcomes.

Background

Maternal morbidity and mortality rates remain high in the United States, with hypertensive disorders during pregnancy and the postpartum period being leading contributors. The prevalence of new-onset gestational hypertension and preeclampsia increased from 5.7% in 2011 to 10.0% in 2019 [1]. Although less frequently studied, postpartum hypertension is also a growing concern, with recent data in 2016–2018 indicating that 12.1% of postpartum individuals with develop chronic hypertension within a year after delivery [2]. Pregnancy and the postpartum period are increasingly recognized as a critical window to improve cardiovascular health across the life course [3]. However, despite advances in clinical care, the multifactorial and complex etiology of gestational and postpartum hypertensive disorders continue to challenge efforts to predict and prevent these conditions. Existing risk assessment tools often fail to account for upstream social determinants of health that shape maternal risk [4,5].

Neighborhood deprivation is a critical upstream determinant of health, associated with limited access to health-promoting resources such as high-quality healthcare, nutritious food, walkable environments, stable housing, and employment opportunities, as well as increased exposure to health risk factors including psychosocial stress and environmental hazards [[6], [7], [8]]. Although emerging evidence links neighborhood deprivation to adverse perinatal outcomes, including preterm birth, low birth weight, and gestational diabetes [[9], [10], [11], [12]], its role in the development of gestational and postpartum hypertensive disorders is less studied. Prior investigations have yielded mixed findings, often constrained by small sample sizes or selected populations, underscoring the need for large, population-based studies [[13], [14], [15]]. Further, pre-pregnancy obesity is a prevalent and well-established risk factor for hypertensive disorders and may interact with neighborhood deprivation [16]; however, the independent contribution of neighborhood deprivation beyond obesity to gestational and postpartum hypertensive disorders remains unclear.

To address these gaps, we examined whether neighborhood deprivation was associated with the risk of gestational and postpartum hypertensive disorders in a population-based cohort within an integrated healthcare system. We also examined whether these potential associations differed by pre-pregnancy body mass index (BMI) categories.

Methods

Study design and population

This is a population-based cohort study of pregnant individuals in the Blood Pressure in Pregnancy, Obesity, Diabetes, and Perinatal Outcomes (BIPOD) cohort who received prenatal care at Kaiser Permanente Northern California (KPNC) during January 1, 2011 to December 31, 2020. KPNC is an integrated health care system serving 4.6 million members in 21 hospitals and >255 outpatient clinics in Northern California. KPNC members are highly representative of the general population in the served geographic area in terms of age, sex, race/ethnicity, and neighborhood-level income and education [17].

We identified 410,982 pregnant individuals through KPNC databases on all hospitalizations, outpatient visits, laboratory tests, and medications maintained through the electronic health record (EHR) (Fig. 1). We excluded those who were <15 or ≥44 years old at delivery (n = 1193; 0.3%), had a stillbirth (n = 1982; 0.5%), or had a second or higher-order pregnancy during the study period (n = 100,188; 24.4%), leaving only individuals with their first pregnancy during the study period. We also excluded individuals who were diagnosed with chronic hypertension before pregnancy or before 20 gestational weeks (n = 18,200; 5.9%) or were missing data on neighborhood deprivation (n = 422; 0.1%) or gestational hypertension (n = 7798; 2.7%). The final analytic cohort included 281,621 pregnant individuals. For analyses of postpartum hypertension, we further excluded individuals with missing data on postpartum hypertension (n = 19,422; 6.9%).Fig. 1Flowchart of study participants.BIPOD, Blood Pressure in Pregnancy, Obesity, Diabetes, and Perinatal Outcomes; KPNC, Kaiser Permanente Northern California.

This study was approved by the KPNC Institutional Review Board, which waived informed consent due to the exclusive use of de-identified EHR data (IRBNet#1693313). The study adhered to the STROBE reporting guidelines.

Periconceptional neighborhood deprivation

Neighborhood deprivation was assessed using the Neighborhood Deprivation Index (NDI), a census-based indicator of neighborhood-level socioeconomic status derived via factor analysis from 13 variables related to income, education, occupation, and housing conditions (Supplementary Table 1) [18,19]. These variables were obtained from the U.S. Census Bureau’s 5-year American Community Survey. The NDI has been previously linked to a range of health outcomes in both general and pregnant populations [9,20]. Following prior methodology [21,22]. NDI scores ranging from –3.6 to +2.8 with higher values indicating greater deprivation, were categorized into quartiles (Q1–Q4) based on the distribution of the full KPNC population, with Q1 representing the least deprived and Q4 the most deprived neighborhoods.

Residential addresses were extracted from the EHR for the periconceptional period, including the two years before the last menstrual period (LMP) and early pregnancy up to 20 gestational weeks. The longest residence within this period was geocoded using ArcGIS Pro 3.4 and linked to census tracts to assign NDI values.

Gestational and postpartum hypertensive disorders

Gestational hypertension was diagnosed after 20 gestational weeks by any of the following using a validated case-ascertainment algorithm [23]: (1) ≥1 inpatient diagnosis [International Classification of Diseases (ICD)-9: 642.3, 642.9; ICD-10: O13, O16; excluding O13.1, O16.1]; (2) ≥2 outpatient diagnoses; (3) ≥1 outpatient diagnosis and ≥1 antihypertensive medication prescription; or (4) ≥2 outpatient blood pressure readings ≥140/90 mmHg at least four hours apart, based on the current thresholds from the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) [24]. Gestational preeclampsia and eclampsia were diagnosed after 20 gestational weeks by ≥1 inpatient diagnosis (ICD-9: 642.4, 642.5, 642.6; ICD-10: O14, O15), and they were combined due to few eclampsia cases.

Postpartum preeclampsia/eclampsia were diagnosed until six weeks postpartum using the same case ascertainment algorithm for gestational preeclampsia/eclampsia [23]. These conditions are understudied yet clinically important, as recognized by ACOG [26]. Postpartum hypertension was diagnosed up to one year postpartum by any of the following using a validated case-ascertainment algorithm [27]: (1) ≥1 inpatient diagnosis (ICD-9: 401–405, 437.2, 459.2, 572.3; ICD-10: I10-I16, I67.4, I87.4, K76.6, N26.2); (2) ≥2 outpatient diagnoses; (3) ≥1 outpatient diagnosis and ≥1 anti-hypertensive medication dispensed; or (4) ≥2 outpatient blood pressure measurements ≥140/90 mmHg on at least two occasions at least three months apart. We conducted a sensitivity analysis applying the 2017 American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association guideline updates with lower thresholds [25], defining stage 1 hypertension as 130–139/80–89 mmHg and stage 2 hypertension as ≥140/90 mmHg. We focus on postpartum complications within one year after delivery, a period when individuals are typically most engaged with the healthcare system. This timeframe may offer a practical window for effective care transitions and multidisciplinary follow-up to guide future screening and intervention strategies, as emphasized by the ACOG Presidential Task Force on the Postpartum Visit [28,29].

Covariates

We selected covariates in our models based on clinical relevance and prior literature [[30], [31], [32], [33], [34]]. These included age at delivery, education level, insurance type during pregnancy, parity, smoking and alcohol use during pregnancy, and pre-pregnancy BMI.

Pre-pregnancy BMI was calculated using clinician-measured weight in kilograms (measured within 12 weeks before LMP) divided by height in meters squared (measured within 12 months before LMP). BMI categories were defined using racial and ethnic-specific cutoffs for Asian/Pacific Islander (PI) individuals: underweight (<18.5 kg/m²), healthy weight (18.5–22.9 kg/m²), overweight (23.0–27.4 kg/m²), and obesity (≥27.5 kg/m²); and for non-Asian/PI individuals: underweight (<18.5 kg/m²), healthy weight (18.5–24.9 kg/m²), overweight (25.0–29.9 kg/m²), and obesity (≥30.0 kg/m²). Pre-pregnancy obesity is a well-established risk factor for hypertensive disorders [16]; however, temporality with the exposure could not be established because BMI in our study was measured before pregnancy and thus cannot be positioned on the causal pathway between periconceptional neighborhood deprivation and hypertensive disorders in pregnancy and postpartum. Accordingly, similar to previous studies [35,36], we adjusted for pre-pregnancy BMI in sequential models and conducted stratified analyses by BMI categories (underweight/healthy weight vs overweight/obesity) to account for its potential confounding and effect-modifying roles, respectively.

We conceptualize race and ethnicity as social rather than biological constructs, serving as imperfect proxies for underlying social inequities. By focusing on periconceptional neighborhood deprivation, we directly measured an upstream social determinant that captures structural and environmental factors contributing to disparities in hypertensive risk [37].

Statistical analysis

Participant characteristics were summarized across NDI quartiles. Adjusted relative risks (aRR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) of gestational and postpartum hypertensive disorders by NDI quartiles were estimated using modified Poisson regression models, since our outcomes were not rare [38], with parameter estimation by generalized estimating equations (GEE) to account for correlation within census tract [39]. Models were adjusted sequentially as follows: Model 1 was unadjusted; Model 2 adjusted for age at delivery, education level, insurance type, parity, and smoking and alcohol use during pregnancy; and Model 3 additionally adjusted for pre-pregnancy BMI. P-for-trend was calculated by modeling the median NDI within each quartile as a continuous variable using log-Poisson regression.

Based on the literature [35,36], we tested our a priori hypothesis of effect modification by pre-pregnancy BMI by including an interaction term between NDI and BMI in the regression models, with the P-for-interaction calculated using a Wald joint test. Models were also stratified by pre-pregnancy BMI to examine subgroup-specific associations. Similarly, we evaluated effect modification by race and ethnicity by including corresponding interaction terms in the models and conducting stratified analyses.

To address missing data (ranging from 0.2% for parity to 11.8% for education), we used multiple imputations incorporating all covariates, exposures, and outcomes to generate 10 complete datasets. Results from each dataset were pooled using Rubin’s rules. Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided P value ≤ 0.05. All analyses were conducted using R version 4.4.1.

Results

Among 281,621 pregnant individuals in the analysis, 16.2% resided in the least deprived neighborhoods (Q1) and 25.3% in the most deprived (Q4) (Table 1). Younger individuals (aged 15–25 years) were more common in the most versus least deprived neighborhoods (24.9% versus 5.3%). The most versus least deprived neighborhoods had a higher proportion of Hispanic individuals (44.0% versus 10.9%) and lower proportions of Asian/PI (18.5% versus 37.3%) and White individuals (19.9% versus 40.5%). Compared with the least deprived neighborhoods, individuals in the most deprived neighborhoods were more likely to have lower educational attainment (high school or less; 31.0% versus 5.9%) and public insurance coverage during pregnancy (16.2% versus 5.1%). Associations of periconceptional neighborhood deprivation with gestational and postpartum hypertensive disorders

In unadjusted and partially adjusted models, greater periconceptional neighborhood deprivation was associated with a dose-response increase in the risk of all gestational and postpartum hypertensive disorders (Fig. 2). After further adjustment for pre-pregnancy BMI in fully adjusted models, associations attenuated, with the direction reversed for gestational hypertension. Compared with the least deprived neighborhoods (Q1), the risk of gestational hypertension was lower in more deprived neighborhoods (Q2: 0.97 [0.93–1.00], Q3: 0.98 [0.94–1.02], Q4: 0.94 [0.90–0.98]; P-for-trend = 0.01), although confidence intervals were narrow and overlapped across quartiles. For postpartum hypertension, the positive trend persisted (Q2: 1.24 [1.06–1.45], Q3: 1.25 [1.07–1.46], Q4: 1.30 [1.11–1.54]; P-for-trend = 0.01). In sensitivity analyses applying the 2017 American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association lower blood pressure thresholds, associations remained significant for both stage 1 and stage 2 postpartum hypertension, with stronger associations observed for stage 2 hypertension (Supplementary Table 3). Associations for gestational preeclampsia/eclampsia (P-for-trend = 0.04) and postpartum preeclampsia/eclampsia (P-for-trend = 0.25) remained positive in direction, but confidence intervals included the null.

Associations of periconceptional neighborhood deprivation with hypertensive disorders varied by pre-pregnancy BMI, with significant interactions for gestational hypertension (P-for-interaction = 0.004) and gestational preeclampsia/eclampsia (0.002), a borderline interaction for postpartum preeclampsia/eclampsia (0.14), and no significant interaction for postpartum hypertension (0.96) (Fig. 3). Among individuals with overweight/obesity, we observed a dose-response increase in the risk of gestational preeclampsia/eclampsia and both postpartum hypertensive disorders across increasing levels of neighborhood deprivation. Specifically, risks increased with greater NDI quartiles compared with Q1 for gestational preeclampsia/eclampsia (Q2: 1.09 [1.02–1.16], Q3: 1.13 [1.06–1.21], Q4: 1.12 [1.04–1.20]; P-for-trend = 0.01), postpartum preeclampsia/eclampsia (Q2: 1.20 [0.86–1.66], Q3: 1.24 [0.89–1.73], Q4: 1.45 [1.04–2.00]; P-for-trend = 0.02), and postpartum hypertension (Q2: 1.25 [1.04–1.51], Q3: 1.29 [1.06–1.56], Q4: 1.41 [1.17–1.72]; P-for-trend = 0.001). In contrast, the risk of gestational hypertension was elevated only in Q3 versus Q1 (1.06 [1.01–1.12]; P-for-trend = 0.41). Discussion

In this large population-based cohort of 281,621 pregnant individuals, greater neighborhood deprivation was associated with a dose-response increase in the risk of gestational hypertension and preeclampsia/eclampsia as well as postpartum preeclampsia/eclampsia and hypertension in models adjusted for key demographic and clinical characteristics. After additional adjustment for pre-pregnancy BMI, the associations were largely attenuated. Stratified analyses revealed similar dose-response increase in risk across greater neighborhood deprivation among individuals with pre-pregnancy overweight/obesity, but not among those with underweight/normal weight, except for postpartum hypertension. The pregnancy and postpartum period are increasingly recognized as a pivotal stage for identifying and addressing long-term cardiovascular risk. Our findings imply that upstream socio-contextual conditions during the periconceptional period may contribute to cardiometabolic health during this window, suggesting potential opportunities for early interventions with benefits across the life course [3].

Few studies examined the association between neighborhood deprivation and hypertensive disorders during and after pregnancy [[13], [14], [15]]. Existing evidence remains limited and somewhat inconsistent, often constrained by smaller sample sizes, selected populations, and variations in exposure definitions and outcomes. For example, a retrospective study of 11,137 high-risk pregnancies, complicated by chronic hypertension, diabetes, or fetal growth restriction, found that residence in moderately, but not highly, deprived neighborhoods, categorized using Area Deprivation Index (ADI) quartiles, was associated with higher risk of preeclampsia/eclampsia [13]. In contrast, a prospective analysis of 10,038 nulliparous individuals from the NuMoM2b cohort, which categorized neighborhood deprivation using ADI tertiles, reported a more consistent positive association between greater deprivation and risk of hypertensive disorder of pregnancy [14]. Postpartum hypertension remains understudied, with only one prior study, to our knowledge, examining its association with neighborhood deprivation. In a cohort of 4193 Black and White individuals with a history of hypertensive disorder during pregnancy or delivery, greater neighborhood deprivation assessed using ADI quintiles, was linked to persistent hypertension at 3 and 6 weeks postpartum [15]. While informative, this study’s follow-up was limited to the immediate postpartum period, whereas our analysis extends the observation window up to one year postpartum, providing a more comprehensive assessment of postpartum hypertensive risk.

We examined whether pre-pregnancy obesity modifies the association between neighborhood deprivation and risk of gestational and postpartum hypertensive disorders. Prior work suggested that both neighborhood socioeconomic disadvantage and obesity independently increase the risk of hypertensive disorders [36]. In our study, greater neighborhood deprivation was associated with higher risk of hypertensive disorders during pregnancy and postpartum, but the pattern of associations shifted after accounting for pre-pregnancy BMI. Specifically, among individuals with underweight/healthy weight, neighborhood deprivation was inversely associated with gestational hypertension but not associated with other gestational or postpartum hypertensive disorders. In contrast, consistent positive associations were observed among individuals with pre-pregnancy overweight or obesity, who are already at elevated risk. These findings suggest that the interplay between neighborhood and individual-level risk factors is more complex than additive models imply. Neighborhood deprivation and obesity, while each associated with hypertensive disorders, may act synergistically in some subgroups but not in others. One possibility is that the physiologic burden of obesity amplifies vulnerability to adverse neighborhood environments, whereas individuals at lower BMI may be less biologically susceptible or may be resilient to neighborhood-level risk factors [40,41]. Alternatively, residual confounding, measurement limitations, or differential health behaviors across BMI strata may partially contribute to these patterns [42]. Taken together, these results suggest the importance of moving beyond single-exposure frameworks to examine how multilevel determinants jointly shape maternal cardiometabolic health [43]. A more nuanced understanding of these interdependencies is critical for informing precision prevention strategies, particularly for populations disproportionately affected by both neighborhood deprivation and obesity.

Strengths and limitations

Our study has several notable strengths. First, our algorithm for identifying gestational and postpartum hypertensive disorders was rigorously validated through detailed chart review, supporting the accuracy of our outcome ascertainment [23,27]. Second, we leveraged a large, diverse cohort at KPNC whose sociodemographic characteristics reflect those of the surrounding population [17]. Further, the availability of longitudinal EHR data capturing pre-pregnancy BMI enabled us to examine nuanced associations across racial and ethnic groups and explore potential heterogeneity by weight status. Third, the ability to capture hypertensive outcomes through one year postpartum within an integrated healthcare system where 97% of individuals remain members, allowed us to examine this high-risk but often understudied period of maternal health [28,44].

Some limitations warrant consideration. Neighborhood deprivation was assessed based on the longest duration of residence between two years before the last menstrual period and 20 gestational weeks. We did not account for residential mobility during pregnancy; however, prior studies have reported low rates of mobility and short moving distances during this period, suggesting that most individuals likely remained within the same NDI quartile [45]. Additionally, we could not disentangle the temporal sequence of periconceptional NDI and pre-pregnancy BMI and thus could not evaluate causal pathways linking NDI, BMI, and hypertensive disorders; however, we assessed BMI both as a covariate and potential effect modifier to account for its potential confounding and interaction effects. Finally, we did not have direct measures of adiposity or visceral fat, which may more strongly correlate with the risk of hypertensive disorders than BMI alone.

Conclusions

Greater periconceptional neighborhood deprivation was associated with higher risk of hypertensive disorders during pregnancy and postpartum, with associations attenuated after adjustment for pre-pregnancy BMI. Stratified analyses indicated that associations persisted primarily among individuals with pre-pregnancy overweight or obesity, who already are at an elevated risk of hypertensive disorders. These findings underscore the importance of considering neighborhood-level social determinants alongside individua-level risk factors including pre-pregnancy BMI when assessing the risk of gestational and postpartum hypertensive disorders. Future research should investigate the mechanisms linking neighborhood deprivation to hypertensive disorders and explore targeted interventions to reduce disparities and improve maternal health outcomes.Central illustration.
