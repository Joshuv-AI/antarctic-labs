The role of asymmetric time delay and its structure in 1D swarmalators
Rommel Tchinda Djeudjo, Gourab Kumar Sar, Timoteo Carletti

Abstract
Swarmalators are a class of coupled oscillators that simultaneously synchronize in both space and phase, providing a minimal model for systems ranging from biological microswimmers to robotic swarms. Time delay is ubiquitous in such systems, arising from finite signal propagation speeds and sensory processing lags, yet its structural form, whether symmetric or asymmetric, has received little attention. Here, we study a one-dimensional swarmalator model with asymmetric time delay, in which the delay enters only the self-interaction terms of the spatial and phase dynamics, breaking the symmetry assumed in prior work. We identify various collective states such as async, static phase wave, static π \pi , and active π \pi , and derive analytical stability boundaries for each as a function of the coupling parameters and delay. Our analysis reveals that the asymmetric delay structure fundamentally reshapes the collective phase diagram: in particular, for the asymmetric delay models, increasing the delay systematically expands the active π \pi state at the expense of other ordered states, in contrast to the symmetric delay model, which more strongly promotes the presence of unsteady states that are generally not well ordered. By providing closed-form stability conditions validated against numerical simulations, our work establishes that the internal structure of the delay, not merely its magnitude, is a decisive factor in determining the emergent collective behavior of swarmalator populations.



I
Introduction

Many natural and engineered systems exhibit spatial self-organization, moreover systems possessing some internal degree of freedom, can also synchronize the latter variables with the spatial ones. Japanese tree frogs reposition themselves around a pond while entraining their calling rhythms
1
. Starfish embryos assemble into spinning
living crystals
by coupling their swimming trajectories to their internal beating cycles
29
. Magnetic domain walls, active colloids, and sperm cells align both their direction of motion and their phase of oscillation
11
;
31
;
20
, and modern robotic swarms are increasingly designed to coordinate location and internal state in parallel
5
. The minimal framework that captures such joint space-phase organization is the
swarmalator
model introduced by O’Keeffe et al.
16
, in which Kuramoto-type phase oscillators are bidirectionally coupled to their own spatial dynamics so that synchronization and aggregation feed back on one another.

Among the various formulations
26
, the one-dimensional (1D) variant
14
has emerged as a particularly useful benchmark. The 1D model of swarmalators is governed by the pair of equations

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{J}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sin\bigl(x_{j}-x_{i}\bigr)\,\cos\bigl(\theta_{j}-\theta_{i}\bigr),

(1)

\displaystyle\dot{\theta}_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{K}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sin\bigl(\theta_{j}-\theta_{i}\bigr)\,\cos\bigl(x_{j}-x_{i}\bigr)\,.

(2)

By placing both the position
x_{i}
and the phase
\theta_{i}
on the circle
\mathbb{S}^{1}
, the model retains a rich phenomenology of the two-dimensional model (2D)
16
, while being amenable to analytical treatment. This balance between tractability and richness has made the 1D swarmalator model a natural starting point for exploring how additional physical ingredients reshape collective behavior. Building on this framework, several extensions have been investigated, by including swarmalators with nonidentical velocities and frequencies
33
, disordered coupling
15
;
9
, random pinning
22
;
23
;
24
, external forcing
2
;
3
, phase frustration
13
, and other related effects and analytical techniques
25
;
10
;
8
;
4
;
28
;
21
;
17
;
18
.

Another ingredient, ubiquitous in both biological and engineered systems, is time delay. Finite signal propagation speeds, sensory integration times, and communication or actuation latencies all prevent agents from responding instantaneously to one another. The consequences of delay are well documented in the pure phase-oscillator setting: in the Kuramoto model, even modest delays destabilize the incoherent state in nontrivial ways, generate multistability between synchronized branches, and can induce oscillation death
32
;
27
;
7
. For swarmalators, by contrast, the effect of delay has only recently begun to be explored. Initial studies have focused on the 2D model, where numerical investigations have revealed that delay can qualitatively alter the phase diagram and give rise to new collective states
6
;
12
; however, the full 2D setting is analytically demanding, as the coupled delay-differential equations for position and phase do not generally admit closed-form stability conditions, motivating thus the study of lower-dimensional variants in which the essential effects of delay can be isolated. A first step in this direction has been taken very recently where a symmetrically delayed version of the 1D model – in which the same delay enters every coupling channel – and showed that delay substantially reorganizes the phase diagram
19
.

However, physical interactions are rarely uniformly delayed across all coupling channels. The alignment-generating terms and the modulating factors that gate them typically originate from distinct physical processes and are subject to different latencies: an agent may receive a delayed estimate of its neighbors’ phases through finite-speed sensing while evaluating their spatial proximity with negligible lag, or, in a designed system, a latency may be deliberately introduced in one channel but not in another. Whether the structural form of the delay (which terms carry it, and which do not) matters for the emergent dynamics, or only its magnitude does, has not, to the best of our knowledge, been addressed for swarmalators.

In this paper, we take a first step in this direction by analyzing a 1D swarmalator model with asymmetric time delay, in which
\tau
enters only the self-interaction (sine) terms of the spatial and phase equations, while the cross-modal (cosine) factors remain instantaneous. For completeness, we also examine the complementary asymmetric case, in which the delay enters only the cross-modal (cosine) factors and the self-interaction terms remain instantaneous (Appendix
A
). Moreover, we compare both asymmetric variants with the symmetric delay model in which
\tau
enters every coupling channel (Appendix
B
). We identify the collective states supported by the model, derive closed-form stability boundaries for each as functions of the coupling parameters
(J,K)
and
\tau
, and validate our analytical predictions against direct numerical simulations. A notable finding is that the delay can play relevant role of control parameter and, depending on where it is introduced, it can lead to dynamics that do not exist in the case without delay. For instance, when incorporated into the “sine” terms, increasing the delay promotes the presence of the
\pi
state, especially the active one. However, when incorporated into the “cosine” terms, it promotes the emergence of unsteady or non-stationary states.

II
Model

We study the collective dynamics of a population of
N>>1
interacting swarmalators,
each characterized by a spatial position
x_{i}\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
and an internal
phase
\theta_{i}\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
. Their coupled dynamics is governed by the
following system of delay differential equations:

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{i}

\displaystyle=v_{i}+\frac{J}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sin\bigl(x_{j}(t-\tau)-x_{i}(t)\bigr)\cos\bigl(\theta_{j}(t)-\theta_{i}(t)\bigr),

(3)

\displaystyle\dot{\theta}_{i}

\displaystyle=\omega_{i}+\frac{K}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sin\bigl(\theta_{j}(t-\tau)-\theta_{i}(t)\bigr)\cos\bigl(x_{j}(t)-x_{i}(t)\bigr).

(4)

Here,
(v_{i},\omega_{i})
denote the natural velocity and frequency of the
i
-th swarmalator.
In this study, however, we set them to zero for all swarmalators. The parameters

(J,K)
represent the coupling constants. The spatial dynamics (
3
) describe phase-dependent aggregation, whereas the phase
dynamics (
4
) capture position-dependent synchronization.
By anticipating on the following, we will show that this model can lead to a variety of collective behaviors.

To characterize the collective behavior of the system, we use a set of
scalar observables and complex order parameters.

Mean speed.

To differentiate the stationary state from the non-stationary states, we measure the mean velocity
V
, defined as follows:

V(t)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\|\mathbf{v}_{i}(t)\|,\qquad\|\mathbf{v}_{i}(t)\|=\sqrt{\dot{x}^{2}_{i}(t)+\dot{\theta}^{2}_{i}(t)}.

(5)

A value
V\approx 0
signals a
static
regime, whereas
sustained positive values indicate persistent collective motion.

Spatial–phase coherence.

To quantify the correlation between spatial positions and internal phases, we
use the two complex order parameters

S_{\pm}e^{\iota\phi_{\pm}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}e^{\,\iota(x_{j}\pm\theta_{j})},\quad\iota=\sqrt{-1}

(6)

where
S_{+}
and
S_{-}
measure the degree of spatial-phase alignment. If the positions and the phases are perfectly correlated, i.e.,
x_{j}=\pm\theta_{j}+c
then
S_{\pm}=1
.

III
Results

In this section, we present the main dynamical regimes observed in the swarmalator model with asymmetric delay. We first describe the different collective states obtained from numerical simulations and characterize them by using the metrics presented before. We then analyze the stability of the emerging states and derive analytical conditions for their existence and stability. We will eventually conclude by comparing the analytical findings with numerical simulations.

III.2
Mathematical analysis

The goal of this section is to analytically determine the conditions on the model parameters ensuring the existence and stability of some of the dynamical states presented in the previous section.

III.2.1
Stability of the async state

We study the linear stability of the spatially and phase-uniform static state shown in panel (g) of Fig.
1
; in the continuum limit of infinitely many swarmalator, the latter can be described by the constant density

f_{0}(x,\theta)=\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}},\qquad(x,\theta)\in\mathbb{S}^{1}\times\mathbb{S}^{1}.

(7)

To parametrize this equilibrium, we introduce Lagrangian labels
(a,b)\in\mathbb{S}^{1}\times\mathbb{S}^{1}
and write

x(a,b,t)=a+u(a,b,t),\;\theta(a,b,t)=b+v(a,b,t),

(8)

where
u
and
v
are small perturbations. Linearizing the dynamics around the uniform state yields

\partial_{t}u(a,b,t)=\frac{J}{4\pi^{2}}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\Bigl[u(a^{\prime},b^{\prime},t-\tau)\cos(a^{\prime}-a)\cos(b^{\prime}-b)-v(a^{\prime},b^{\prime},t)\sin(a^{\prime}-a)\sin(b^{\prime}-b)\Bigr]\,da^{\prime}\,db^{\prime},

(9)

and

\partial_{t}v(a,b,t)=\frac{K}{4\pi^{2}}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\Bigl[v(a^{\prime},b^{\prime},t-\tau)\cos(a^{\prime}-a)\cos(b^{\prime}-b)-u(a^{\prime},b^{\prime},t)\sin(a^{\prime}-a)\sin(b^{\prime}-b)\Bigr]\,da^{\prime}\,db^{\prime}.

(10)

Since the domain is the torus
\mathbb{S}^{1}\times\mathbb{S}^{1}
, we expand the perturbations in Fourier series:

\displaystyle u(a,b,t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m,n\in\mathbb{Z}}u_{mn}(t)e^{\iota(ma+nb)},

(11)

\displaystyle v(a,b,t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m,n\in\mathbb{Z}}v_{mn}(t)e^{\iota(ma+nb)},

(12)

A direct computation shows that the kernels
\cos(a^{\prime}-a)\cos(b^{\prime}-b)
and
\sin(a^{\prime}-a)\sin(b^{\prime}-b)
act nontrivially only on the modes with
|m|=|n|=1
. More precisely we obtain

\dot{u}_{mn}(t)=\frac{J}{4}\delta_{\{|m|,1\}}\delta_{\{|n|,1\}}\bigl(u_{mn}(t-\tau)+mn\,v_{mn}(t)\bigr),

(13)

\dot{v}_{mn}(t)=\frac{K}{4}\delta_{\{|m|,1\}}\delta_{\{|n|,1\}}\bigl(v_{mn}(t-\tau)+mn\,u_{mn}(t)\bigr).

(14)

Hence, all Fourier modes are linearly neutral except the four active modes

(m,n)\in\{(1,1),(1,-1),(-1,1),(-1,-1)\}.

Introducing
s=mn\in\{\pm 1\}
, the dynamics of each active mode reduces to

\dot{u}(t)=\frac{J}{4}u(t-\tau)+\frac{Js}{4}v(t),\;\dot{v}(t)=\frac{K}{4}v(t-\tau)+\frac{Ks}{4}u(t).

(15)

Looking for normal modes of the form

\displaystyle u(t)=Ue^{\lambda t},\qquad v(t)=Ve^{\lambda t},

(16)

with
(U,V)\neq(0,0)
, one obtains the characteristic equation

\left(\lambda-\frac{J}{4}e^{-\lambda\tau}\right)\left(\lambda-\frac{K}{4}e^{-\lambda\tau}\right)-\frac{JK}{16}=0.

(17)

Therefore, the uniform steady state is linearly stable whenever all roots
\lambda
of (
17
) satisfy
\Re(\lambda)<0
, up to neutral modes associated with the symmetries of the problem.

In the nondelayed case
\tau=0
, (
17
) simplifies to

\lambda\left(\lambda-\frac{J+K}{4}\right)=0.

(18)

Thus, besides the neutral eigenvalue
\lambda=0
, the nontrivial eigenvalue is

\displaystyle\lambda=\frac{J+K}{4},

(19)

and the uniform state is linearly stable if and only if

J+K<0.

(20)

For
\tau>0
, the loss of stability is detected by setting
\lambda=\iota\omega
with
\omega>0
. Separating real and imaginary parts gives

\omega^{2}+\frac{J+K}{4}\omega\sin(\omega\tau)+\frac{JK}{8}\sin^{2}(\omega\tau)=0,

(21)

\cos(\omega\tau)\left(\frac{J+K}{4}\omega+\frac{JK}{8}\sin(\omega\tau)\right)=0.

(22)

The second factor cannot vanish when
\omega\neq 0
. Therefore, one must have

\cos(\omega\tau)=0,

(23)

which implies

\omega\tau=\frac{\pi}{2}+k\pi,\qquad k\in\mathbb{Z}.

(24)

Since
\omega>0
and
\tau>0
, the admissible branches are obtained for

k=0,1,2,\ldots
. The smallest positive value of
\tau
corresponds to
the first branch
k=0
, namely

\omega\tau=\frac{\pi}{2}.

(25)

Hence

\omega=\frac{\pi}{2\tau}.

(26)

Substituting into the real part yields

\omega^{2}+\frac{J+K}{4}\omega+\frac{JK}{8}=0.

(27)

Solving for
\omega
gives

\omega_{\pm}=\frac{-(J+K)\pm\sqrt{(J+K)^{2}-8JK}}{8},

(28)

which requires

(J+K)^{2}-8JK\geq 0.

(29)

The corresponding critical delays are

\tau_{c}^{\pm}=\frac{\pi}{2\omega_{\pm}}=\frac{4\pi}{-(J+K)\pm\sqrt{(J+K)^{2}-8JK}}.

(30)

Equivalently, using the quadratic relation, we get

\tau_{c}^{\pm}=\frac{\pi}{2JK}\left[-(J+K)\pm\sqrt{(J+K)^{2}-8JK}\right],JK\neq 0.

(31)

Zero-level curve of the zero-eigenvalue.

Consider the characteristic equation
17
and by substituting
\lambda=0
, we obtain

\displaystyle\Delta(0)=\left(-\frac{J}{4}\right)\left(-\frac{K}{4}\right)-\frac{JK}{16}=\frac{JK}{16}-\frac{JK}{16}=0.

(32)

Hence,
\lambda=0
is always a root of the characteristic equation.

To determine the boundary associated with this root, we require that
\lambda=0
be a
multiple root
. Therefore, besides
\Delta(0)=0
, we impose

\displaystyle\Delta^{\prime}(0)=0.

(33)

Expanding
\Delta(\lambda)
gives

\Delta(\lambda)=\lambda^{2}-\frac{J+K}{4}\lambda e^{-\lambda\tau}+\frac{JK}{16}e^{-2\lambda\tau}-\frac{JK}{16}.

(34)

Differentiating with respect to
\lambda
, we obtain

\displaystyle\Delta^{\prime}(\lambda)=2\lambda-\frac{J+K}{4}\frac{d}{d\lambda}\!\left(\lambda e^{-\lambda\tau}\right)+\frac{JK}{16}\frac{d}{d\lambda}\!\left(e^{-2\lambda\tau}\right),

(35)

which eventually yields

\displaystyle\Delta^{\prime}(\lambda)=2\lambda-\frac{J+K}{4}\left(e^{-\lambda\tau}-\tau\lambda e^{-\lambda\tau}\right)-\frac{\tau JK}{8}e^{-2\lambda\tau}.

(36)

Evaluating at
\lambda=0
, we get

\displaystyle\Delta^{\prime}(0)=-\frac{J+K}{4}-\frac{\tau JK}{8}.

(37)

Therefore, the condition
\Delta^{\prime}(0)=0
yields

\displaystyle-\frac{J+K}{4}-\frac{\tau JK}{8}=0.

(38)

Thus, the boundary curve in the
(J,K)
-plane associated with the root
\lambda=0
is

\,2J+2K+\tau JK=0.

(39)

III.2.2
Stability of the phase wave

Still considering the model (
3
)–(
4
), we now study the steady phase wave state (see panel (j) of Fig.
1
) characterized by

x_{i}^{*}=\frac{2\pi i}{N},\qquad\theta_{i}^{*}=\frac{2\pi i}{N},\qquad i=1,\dots,N.

(40)

Proposition 1
.

The state (
40
) is a steady state of (
3
)–(
4
).

Proof.

Let
\Delta_{ji}=\frac{2\pi(j-i)}{N}
. The summand in (
3
) equals
\sin\Delta_{ji}\cos\Delta_{ji}=\frac{1}{2}\sin(2\Delta_{ji})
. Setting
m=j-i
, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sin\Delta_{ji}\cos\Delta_{ji}=\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Im}\!\left(\sum_{m=0}^{N-1}e^{4\pi\iota m/N}\right)=0,

(41)

since the geometric sum with ratio
e^{4\pi\iota/N}\neq 1
(for
N\geq 3
) vanishes. The computation is identical for (
4
), so
\dot{x}_{i}=\dot{\theta}_{i}=0
.
∎

Linearization.

We perturb the steady state by setting

x_{i}(t)=\frac{2\pi i}{N}+\xi_{i}(t),\qquad\theta_{i}(t)=\frac{2\pi i}{N}+\phi_{i}(t),

(42)

with
|\xi_{i}|,|\phi_{i}|\ll 1
. Expanding to first order using
\sin(a+\varepsilon)\approx\sin a+\varepsilon\cos a
and
\cos(a+\varepsilon)\approx\cos a-\varepsilon\sin a
, and noting that the zeroth-order terms cancel (as in the proof above), the linearized equations read

\displaystyle\dot{\xi}_{i}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{J}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\Bigl[\cos^{2}\!\Delta_{ji}\,\bigl(\xi_{j}(t-\tau)-\xi_{i}(t)\bigr)

\displaystyle\quad-\sin^{2}\!\Delta_{ji}\,\bigl(\phi_{j}(t)-\phi_{i}(t)\bigr)\Bigr],

(43)

\displaystyle\dot{\phi}_{i}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{K}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\Bigl[\cos^{2}\!\Delta_{ji}\,\bigl(\phi_{j}(t-\tau)-\phi_{i}(t)\bigr)

\displaystyle\quad-\sin^{2}\!\Delta_{ji}\,\bigl(\xi_{j}(t)-\xi_{i}(t)\bigr)\Bigr].

(44)

Fourier decomposition.

Since the coefficients in (
43
)–(
44
) depend only on
m=j-i
, we seek solutions of the form

\xi_{i}(t)=\hat{\xi}_{k}\,e^{\lambda t}\,e^{2\pi\iota ki/N},\qquad\phi_{i}(t)=\hat{\phi}_{k}\,e^{\lambda t}\,e^{2\pi\iota ki/N}.

(45)

The relevant Fourier sums are

\displaystyle S_{k}^{c}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{m=0}^{N-1}\cos^{2}\!\!\left(\frac{2\pi m}{N}\right)e^{2\pi\iota km/N},

(46)

\displaystyle S_{k}^{s}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{m=0}^{N-1}\sin^{2}\!\!\left(\frac{2\pi m}{N}\right)e^{2\pi\iota km/N}.

(47)

Using the identities
\cos^{2}\alpha=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{4}e^{2\iota\alpha}+\frac{1}{4}e^{-2\iota\alpha}
and
\sin^{2}\alpha=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{4}e^{2\iota\alpha}-\frac{1}{4}e^{-2\iota\alpha}
, together with the geometric sum
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{m=0}^{N-1}e^{2\pi\iota\ell m/N}=\delta_{\ell\equiv 0\pmod{N}}
, one obtains for
N\geq 5
:

\displaystyle S_{k}^{c}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\delta_{k,0}+\frac{1}{4}\delta_{k,2}+\frac{1}{4}\delta_{k,N-2},

(48)

\displaystyle S_{k}^{s}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\delta_{k,0}-\frac{1}{4}\delta_{k,2}-\frac{1}{4}\delta_{k,N-2}.

(49)

Substituting (
45
) into (
43
)–(
44
) and using
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j}\cos^{2}\!\Delta_{ji}=\frac{1}{2}
, the modal equations are

\displaystyle\lambda\hat{\xi}_{k}

\displaystyle=J\!\left(S_{k}^{c}e^{-\lambda\tau}-\tfrac{1}{2}\right)\hat{\xi}_{k}-J\!\left(S_{k}^{s}-\tfrac{1}{2}\right)\hat{\phi}_{k},

(50)

\displaystyle\lambda\hat{\phi}_{k}

\displaystyle=K\!\left(S_{k}^{c}e^{-\lambda\tau}-\tfrac{1}{2}\right)\hat{\phi}_{k}-K\!\left(S_{k}^{s}-\tfrac{1}{2}\right)\hat{\xi}_{k}.

(51)

Modes
k\neq 0,2,N-2
.

For these modes,
S_{k}^{c}=S_{k}^{s}=0
, and (
50
)–(
51
) reduce to

\displaystyle\lambda\begin{pmatrix}\hat{\xi}_{k}\\
\hat{\phi}_{k}\end{pmatrix}=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}-J&J\\
K&-K\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\hat{\xi}_{k}\\
\hat{\phi}_{k}\end{pmatrix}.

(52)

The eigenvalues of this matrix are

\lambda_{1}=0,\qquad\lambda_{2}=-\frac{J+K}{2}.

(53)

Thus, these modes are stable if and only if
J+K>0
.

Mode
k=0
.

For
k=0
, we have
S_{0}^{c}=S_{0}^{s}=\tfrac{1}{2}
, and therefore both coupling terms vanish.
The equations decouple and become

\lambda\hat{\xi}_{0}=\frac{J}{2}\bigl(e^{-\lambda\tau}-1\bigr)\hat{\xi}_{0},\;\lambda\hat{\phi}_{0}=\frac{K}{2}\bigl(e^{-\lambda\tau}-1\bigr)\hat{\phi}_{0}.

(54)

Each equation can be written in the form

\displaystyle(\lambda+a)e^{\lambda\tau}=a,

(55)

with
a=\tfrac{J}{2}
for the
\hat{\xi}_{0}
mode and
a=\tfrac{K}{2}
for the
\hat{\phi}_{0}
mode.
Using the Lambert
W
function, the complete sets of characteristic roots are therefore

\lambda_{n}^{(\xi)}=\frac{1}{\tau}W_{n}\!\left(\frac{J\tau}{2}e^{J\tau/2}\right)-\frac{J}{2},\qquad n\in\mathbb{Z},

(56)

and

\lambda_{n}^{(\phi)}=\frac{1}{\tau}W_{n}\!\left(\frac{K\tau}{2}e^{K\tau/2}\right)-\frac{K}{2},\qquad n\in\mathbb{Z}.

(57)

Thus, the characteristic roots are given by the different branches of the Lambert
W
function.

Modes
k=2
and
k=N-2
.

For
k=2
(with
N>4
) we obtain
S_{2}^{c}=\frac{1}{4}
and
S_{2}^{s}=-\frac{1}{4}
. Setting

\mu(\lambda):=\frac{e^{-\lambda\tau}}{4}-\frac{1}{2},

(58)

the modal equations become

\displaystyle\lambda\hat{\xi}=J\mu(\lambda)\,\hat{\xi}+\frac{3J}{4}\,\hat{\phi},\;\lambda\hat{\phi}=K\mu(\lambda)\,\hat{\phi}+\frac{3K}{4}\,\hat{\xi}.

(59)

A nontrivial solution exists if and only if the determinant vanishes, giving the characteristic equation

\lambda^{2}-(J+K)\mu(\lambda)\,\lambda+JK\!\left[\mu(\lambda)^{2}-\frac{9}{16}\right]=0.

(60)

Case
\tau=0
.

Here
\mu(0)=-\frac{1}{4}
, and (
60
) reduces to the polynomial

\lambda^{2}+\frac{J+K}{4}\,\lambda-\frac{JK}{2}=0.

(61)

For all roots of (
61
) to satisfy
\Re(\lambda)<0
, the coefficients must be positive, namely

\frac{J+K}{4}>0\Longleftrightarrow J+K>0,-\frac{JK}{2}>0\Longleftrightarrow JK<0.

(62)

The stability condition for mode
k=2
without delay is therefore

J+K>0\quad\text{and}\quad JK<0.

(63)

Case
\tau>0
: Hopf bifurcation.

We seek a purely imaginary eigenvalue
\lambda=\iota\omega
,
\omega>0
. Writing
\mu(\iota\omega)=\mu_{R}-\iota\mu_{I}
with

\displaystyle\mu_{R}=\frac{\cos\omega\tau-2}{4},\qquad\mu_{I}=\frac{\sin\omega\tau}{4},

(64)

and separating real and imaginary parts of (
60
), we obtain

\displaystyle-\omega^{2}-(J+K)\omega\,\mu_{I}+JK\!\left(\mu_{R}^{2}-\mu_{I}^{2}-\tfrac{9}{16}\right)

\displaystyle=0,

(65)

\displaystyle-(J+K)\omega\,\mu_{R}-2JK\,\mu_{R}\mu_{I}

\displaystyle=0.

(66)

From (
66
), factoring out
\frac{\cos\omega\tau-2}{4}\neq 0
yields

\sin\omega\tau=-\frac{2(J+K)}{JK}\,\omega.

(67)

Substituting (
67
) into (
65
) and using
\cos^{2}\omega\tau+\sin^{2}\omega\tau=1
to eliminate
\sin\omega\tau
, one arrives at the quadratic equation in
c=\cos\omega\tau
:

\displaystyle c^{2}-2c-3+\frac{4(J^{2}+K^{2})}{J^{2}K^{2}}\,\omega^{2}=0,

(68)

whose solution is

\cos(\omega\tau)=-1-\frac{4\omega^{2}}{JK}.

(69)

Enforcing
\cos^{2}\omega\tau+\sin^{2}\omega\tau=1
with (
67
) and (
69
), setting
u=\omega_{c}^{2}
, one finds

\displaystyle u\!\left(4u+(J+K)^{2}+2JK\right)=0,

(70)

so the non-trivial Hopf frequency satisfies

\omega_{c}^{2}=-\frac{(J+K)^{2}+2JK}{4}.

(71)

This is real and positive only if

(J+K)^{2}+2JK<0,

(72)

which requires
J
and
K
to have opposite signs with
|JK|
sufficiently large. The corresponding critical delay is

\tau_{c}^{(n)}=\frac{1}{\omega_{c}}\left(\mathrm{Arg}(c_{c}+\iota s_{c})+2\pi n\right),\qquad n\in\mathbb{Z}.

(73)

where

c_{c}=-1-\frac{4\omega_{c}^{2}}{JK},\qquad s_{c}=-\frac{2(J+K)}{JK}\,\omega_{c}.

(74)

Thus,

\tau_{c}(J,K)=\frac{4}{\sqrt{\tilde{A}}}\arctan\!\left(\frac{J+K}{\sqrt{\tilde{A}}}\right),

(75)

where
\tilde{A}=-(J^{2}+4JK+K^{2})
.

We also consider the case
\lambda=0
, since stability may change when a root crosses the imaginary axis at the origin. Substituting
\lambda=0
into the characteristic equation gives
JK=0
, so we can have
J=0
or
K=0
. These lines therefore define stationary critical boundaries in the parameter plane.

III.2.3
Stability of the active
\pi
state

We consider a two-cluster solution of the form

(x_{i}(t),\theta_{i}(t))=\begin{cases}(x_{s}+\omega_{1}t,\theta_{s}+\omega_{2}t),&i\in G_{1},\\[2.0pt]
(x_{s}+\pi+\omega_{1}t,\theta_{s}+\pi+\omega_{2}t),&i\in G_{2}\,,\end{cases}

(76)

where
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
form a partition of
\{1,\dots,N\}
(see panel (d) of Fig.
1
). Substituting the latter into the equations of motion and using that the cluster shift is either
0
or
\pi
, one finds that all interaction terms are identical. This yields the existence conditions

\omega_{1}=-J\sin(\omega_{1}\tau),\qquad\omega_{2}=-K\sin(\omega_{2}\tau).

(77)

To study linear stability, we perturb the solution as

\displaystyle x_{i}(t)=x_{s}+\chi_{i}+\omega_{1}t+\delta x_{i}(t),

(78)

\displaystyle\theta_{i}(t)=\theta_{s}+\chi_{i}+\omega_{2}t+\delta\theta_{i}(t),

(79)

with
\chi_{i}\in\{0,\pi\}
and
|\delta x_{i}|,|\delta\theta_{i}|\ll 1
. A first-order expansion of the delayed interaction terms gives

\displaystyle\dot{\delta x_{i}}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{J}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\cos(\omega_{1}\tau)\big(\delta x_{j}(t-\tau)-\delta x_{i}(t)\big),

(80)

\displaystyle\dot{\delta\theta_{i}}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{K}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\cos(\omega_{2}\tau)\big(\delta\theta_{j}(t-\tau)-\delta\theta_{i}(t)\big).

(81)

Expanding the perturbations in discrete Fourier modes and separating the
uniform and nonuniform components, one obtains for all
m\neq 0

\mu_{m}=-J\cos(\omega_{1}\tau),\qquad\lambda_{m}=-K\cos(\omega_{2}\tau).

(82)

Hence the nonuniform modes are stable if and only if

J\cos(\omega_{1}\tau)>0,\qquad K\cos(\omega_{2}\tau)>0.

(83)

For the uniform mode
m=0
, the characteristic roots can be obtained in closed form. One finds

\mu_{n}=\frac{1}{\tau}W_{n}\!\Big(J\tau\cos(\omega_{1}\tau)\,e^{J\tau\cos(\omega_{1}\tau)}\Big)-J\cos(\omega_{1}\tau),

(84)

and similarly

\lambda_{n}=\frac{1}{\tau}W_{n}\!\Big(K\tau\cos(\omega_{2}\tau)\,e^{K\tau\cos(\omega_{2}\tau)}\Big)-K\cos(\omega_{2}\tau),

(85)

where
W_{n}
denotes the
n
th branch of the Lambert
W
function.

The above analysis provides the existence and stability conditions of the active
\pi
-state. We now complement these analytical results by examining how the corresponding frequency branches are organized as the delay
\tau
varies. More precisely, the branches are obtained by solving the transcendental equations in (
77
) for each value of
\tau
, and then reconstructing the corresponding frequencies
\omega_{x}
and
\omega_{\theta}
. Introducing

\displaystyle y_{x}=\omega_{x}\tau,\qquad y_{\theta}=\omega_{\theta}\tau,

(86)

the existence conditions become

y_{x}+J\tau\sin(y_{x})=0,\;y_{\theta}+K\tau\sin(y_{\theta})=0,

(87)

so that the frequencies are recovered through

\displaystyle\omega_{x}=\frac{y_{x}}{\tau},\qquad\omega_{\theta}=\frac{y_{\theta}}{\tau}.

(88)

For
J=1
, we consider two values of the phase-coupling parameter, namely
K=-2
and
K=1
. The resulting branch diagrams are shown in Fig.
3
. Panels (a,b) correspond to
K=-2
, whereas panels (c,d) correspond to
K=1
. The left panels, (a,c), display the branches of
\omega_{x}
, while the right panels, (b,d), display those of
\omega_{\theta}
. The stability of each branch is determined from condition (
83
). In the figure, stable branches are represented by solid blue curves, whereas unstable branches are shown by red dashed curves. As
\tau
increases, additional branches appear because of the periodicity of the sine function, leading to the coexistence of several active
\pi
-states with alternating stability.

Remark 2
.

When several stable branches coexist for the same parameter values, the asymptotic frequency selected by the dynamics may depend on the initial condition. In that case, each branch may possess its own basin of attraction, and the observed long-time behavior is determined by the basin in which the initial condition lies.

For
J>0
, the trivial solution
y_{x}=0
is always stable. For
J<0
, a stable nontrivial branch exists only if
y_{x}\in(\pi/2,\pi)
(up to symmetry), which requires

\displaystyle-J\tau>\frac{\pi}{2}.

(89)

Thus, stable solutions for the
x
dynamics exist when

J>0\qquad\text{or}\qquad J<-\frac{\pi}{2\tau}.

(90)

By the same argument, the
\theta
dynamics admits stable solutions when

K>0\qquad\text{or}\qquad K<-\frac{\pi}{2\tau}.

(91)

Hence the stable region in the
(J,K)
plane lies inside

\left((-\infty,-\tfrac{\pi}{2\tau})\cup(0,\infty)\right)\times\left((-\infty,-\tfrac{\pi}{2\tau})\cup(0,\infty)\right).

(92)

The portion of this region that lies in the first quadrant (i.e.,
J>0
and
K>0
) corresponds to the solution
\omega_{x}=\omega_{\theta}=0
, which indicates the static
\pi
state.

III.3
Bistability

The stability analyses in the previous section establishes parameter regions where the async state is linearly stable and the active
\pi
state exist, but it does not by itself preclude these regions from overlapping. Indeed, direct numerical integration of Eqs. (
3
)-(
4
) reveals that such overlap does occur: for a range of coupling parameters, the two states coexist as stable attractors, and the asymptotic dynamics selected by the system depends on the initial condition.

To quantify this phenomenon, we fix
K=1.35
and
\tau=2.5
and vary
J
across the region near the boundary of these states. For each value of
J
, we integrate the system from an ensemble of random initial conditions, with
x_{i}
and
\theta_{i}
drawn independently and uniformly from
[-\pi,\pi]
, and classify the long-time state reached in each realization using the order parameters and the mean velocity. The fractions of realizations converging to the active
\pi
and async states are plotted as functions of
J
in Fig.
5
.

Three regimes emerge. For
J\lesssim 1.95
, every initial condition converges to the active
\pi

J\gtrsim-1.65

\pi

J

J\approx-1.8
, the two basins are approximately balanced, with roughly half of the random initial conditions selecting each state. This value is close to the theoretical critical
J_{c}=-1.86
(derived by solving Eq. (

K=1.35
, and
\tau=2.5
) where the static async state is predicted to lose its stability. This intermediate window is therefore a genuine bistable region, in which the asymptotic behavior is not determined by the coupling parameters alone but also by the initial data.

IV
Discussion

We have presented a theoretical study of delay-coupled swarmalators, with a particular focus on the asymmetric case. We derived the stability boundaries of the three main collective states of the model: the async state, the phase wave state, and the
\pi
states. This analysis allowed us to construct the full phase diagram in the
(J,K)
parameter space. We observe that the delay can play the role of a control parameter. Moreover, depending on the way the asymmetric model is defined, the presence of delay can promote ordered states, such as the active
\pi
state, or generate unsteady states that are absent in the model without delay. For instance, when the delay is introduced only in the “sine” interaction term, increasing the delay enlarges the stability region of the
\pi
state. Such dynamics may be relevant to populations of interacting organisms, including predator-prey systems
30
. Similar anti-phase patterns can also be found in biological motor systems, for example in left-right neural activity during swimming or locomotion. Also, as discussed in
19
, unsteady states may be observable in populations of vinegar eels, sperm cells, or other real-world swarmalators.

Future work could extend our study to the regime where the natural frequencies and velocities are non-identical. Another possible direction would be to extend the model to two or three spatial dimensions. It would also be interesting to investigate the effects of external forcing, or higher-order interactions with delay.

Appendix A
Another variant of asymmetric delay

There are several ways to define asymmetric delay. For the following asymmetric model, we observe several dynamical regimes, including the async state, the phase wave state, the static async state, and the synchronized state with an unsteady profile. We briefly show below how the linear stability analysis can also be carried out for this alternative asymmetric model:

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{J}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sin\bigl(x_{j}-x_{i}\bigr)\cos\bigl(\theta_{j}(t-\tau)-\theta_{i}\bigr),

(A1)

\displaystyle\dot{\theta}_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{K}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sin\bigl(\theta_{j}-\theta_{i}\bigr)\cos\bigl(x_{j}(t-\tau)-x_{i}\bigr).

(A2)

A.1
Async state

We consider the async state

\displaystyle f_{0}(x,\theta)=\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}},\qquad(x,\theta)\in\mathbb{S}^{1}\times\mathbb{S}^{1},

(A3)

and introduce Lagrangian labels
(a,b)\in\mathbb{S}^{1}\times\mathbb{S}^{1}
such that

\displaystyle x(a,b,t)=a+u(a,b,t),\;\theta(a,b,t)=b+v(a,b,t),

(A4)

with
u,v
small perturbations. Linearizing around
f_{0}
gives

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u(a,b,t)

\displaystyle=\frac{J}{4\pi^{2}}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\!\!\int_{0}^{2\pi}\Big[u(a^{\prime},b^{\prime},t)\cos(a^{\prime}-a)\cos(b^{\prime}-b)-v(a^{\prime},b^{\prime},t-\tau)\sin(a^{\prime}-a)\sin(b^{\prime}-b)\Big]\,da^{\prime}\,db^{\prime},

(A5)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}v(a,b,t)

\displaystyle=\frac{K}{4\pi^{2}}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\!\!\int_{0}^{2\pi}\Big[v(a^{\prime},b^{\prime},t)\cos(a^{\prime}-a)\cos(b^{\prime}-b)-u(a^{\prime},b^{\prime},t-\tau)\sin(a^{\prime}-a)\sin(b^{\prime}-b)\Big]\,da^{\prime}\,db^{\prime}.

(A6)

Expanding in Fourier series,

\displaystyle u(a,b,t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m,n\in\mathbb{Z}}u_{mn}(t)e^{\iota(ma+nb)},

(A7)

\displaystyle v(a,b,t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m,n\in\mathbb{Z}}v_{mn}(t)e^{\iota(ma+nb)},

(A8)

one finds that only the modes
|m|=|n|=1
are active. For these modes,

\displaystyle\dot{u}_{mn}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{J}{4}\Big(u_{mn}(t)+mn\,v_{mn}(t-\tau)\Big),

(A9)

\displaystyle\dot{v}_{mn}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{K}{4}\Big(v_{mn}(t)+mn\,u_{mn}(t-\tau)\Big).

(A10)

Setting
s=mn\in\{\pm 1\}
and seeking normal modes

\displaystyle u(t)=Ue^{\lambda t},\qquad v(t)=Ve^{\lambda t},

(A11)

yields the characteristic equation

\displaystyle\left(\lambda-\frac{J}{4}\right)\left(\lambda-\frac{K}{4}\right)-\frac{JK}{16}e^{-2\lambda\tau}=0.

(A12)

For
\tau=0
, this reduces to

\displaystyle\lambda\left(\lambda-\frac{J+K}{4}\right)=0,

(A13)

so the async state is stable iff

\displaystyle J+K<0,

(A14)

For
\tau>0
, setting
\lambda=\iota\omega
with
\omega>0
gives

\displaystyle\omega^{2}=\frac{JK}{8}\sin^{2}(\omega\tau),\;\frac{J+K}{4}\,\omega=\frac{JK}{16}\sin(2\omega\tau).

(A15)

Combining these two relations with
\sin^{2}(\omega\tau)+\cos^{2}(\omega\tau)=1
leads to

\displaystyle 16\omega^{2}+J^{2}+K^{2}=0,

(A16)

which is impossible for
\omega>0
. Hence there is no nontrivial Hopf bifurcation for the async state in this model.

Therefore, the stability boundary is determined only by the zero eigenvalue. Imposing that
\lambda=0
be a double root gives

\displaystyle\Delta^{\prime}(0)=0\Longrightarrow-\frac{J+K}{4}+\frac{\tau JK}{8}=0,

(A17)

that is,

\displaystyle 2J+2K-\tau JK=0.

(A18)

However, this condition describes the stability boundary only in the parameter region where no other unstable eigenvalue is already present. In particular, it is relevant in the mixed-sign sector
JK<0
, where the loss of stability can occur through a real eigenvalue crossing at
\lambda=0
. In that case, the async state is stable when

\displaystyle 2J+2K-\tau JK<0.

(A19)

By contrast, when
J>0
and
K>0
, a positive real eigenvalue already exists, so the condition
\Delta^{\prime}(0)=0
is not the global stability boundary in that sector.

A.2
Phase wave

The phase wave state

\displaystyle x_{i}^{(0)}=\theta_{i}^{(0)}=\frac{2\pi i}{N},\qquad i=1,\dots,N,

(A20)

is again a steady state. We perturb it as

\displaystyle x_{i}(t)=\frac{2\pi i}{N}+\xi_{i}(t),\theta_{i}(t)=\frac{2\pi i}{N}+\phi_{i}(t),

(A21)

where
|\xi_{i}|,|\phi_{i}|\ll 1
. At linear order, writing
\Delta_{ji}=2\pi(j-i)/N
, we obtain

\displaystyle\dot{\xi}_{i}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{J}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\Big[\cos^{2}\!\Delta_{ji}\,(\xi_{j}(t)-\xi_{i}(t))

\displaystyle\quad-\sin^{2}\!\Delta_{ji}\,(\phi_{j}(t-\tau)-\phi_{i}(t))\Big],

(A22)

\displaystyle\dot{\phi}_{i}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{K}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\Big[\cos^{2}\!\Delta_{ji}\,(\phi_{j}(t)-\phi_{i}(t))

\displaystyle\quad-\sin^{2}\!\Delta_{ji}\,(\xi_{j}(t-\tau)-\xi_{i}(t))\Big].

(A23)

Using the discrete Fourier ansatz

\displaystyle\xi_{i}(t)=\hat{\xi}_{k}e^{\lambda t}e^{2\pi\iota ki/N},\;\phi_{i}(t)=\hat{\phi}_{k}e^{\lambda t}e^{2\pi\iota ki/N},

(A24)

together with

\displaystyle S_{k}^{c}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{m=0}^{N-1}\cos^{2}\!\Big(\frac{2\pi m}{N}\Big)e^{2\pi\iota km/N},

(A25)

\displaystyle S_{k}^{s}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{m=0}^{N-1}\sin^{2}\!\Big(\frac{2\pi m}{N}\Big)e^{2\pi\iota km/N},

(A26)

the modal equations become

\displaystyle\lambda\hat{\xi}_{k}

\displaystyle=J\Big(S_{k}^{c}-\frac{1}{2}\Big)\hat{\xi}_{k}-J\Big(e^{-\lambda\tau}S_{k}^{s}-\frac{1}{2}\Big)\hat{\phi}_{k},

(A27)

\displaystyle\lambda\hat{\phi}_{k}

\displaystyle=K\Big(S_{k}^{c}-\frac{1}{2}\Big)\hat{\phi}_{k}-K\Big(e^{-\lambda\tau}S_{k}^{s}-\frac{1}{2}\Big)\hat{\xi}_{k}.

(A28)

As in the main text, only the modes
k=0,2,N-2
are exceptional.

For the modes
k\neq 0,2,N-2
, one has
S_{k}^{c}=S_{k}^{s}=0
, hence

\displaystyle\lambda\begin{pmatrix}\hat{\xi}_{k}\\
\hat{\phi}_{k}\end{pmatrix}=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}-J&J\\
K&-K\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\hat{\xi}_{k}\\
\hat{\phi}_{k}\end{pmatrix},

(A29)

with eigenvalues

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}=0,\qquad\lambda_{2}=-\frac{J+K}{2}.

(A30)

Therefore these modes are stable iff

\displaystyle J+K>0.

(A31)

For the mode
k=0
, since
S_{0}^{c}=S_{0}^{s}=1/2
, the characteristic equation is

\displaystyle\lambda^{2}-\frac{JK}{4}\bigl(1-e^{-\lambda\tau}\bigr)^{2}=0.

(A32)

Thus, if
JK<0
, purely imaginary roots
\lambda=\iota\omega
satisfy

\displaystyle\omega_{c}=\sqrt{-JK},\;\tau_{n}=\frac{(2n+1)\pi}{\sqrt{-JK}},\;n\in\mathbb{N},

(A33)

whereas for
JK>0
the zero root becomes multiple when

\displaystyle JK\,\tau^{2}=4.

(A34)

For the modes
k=2
and
k=N-2
, one has

\displaystyle S_{2}^{c}=\frac{1}{4},\qquad S_{2}^{s}=-\frac{1}{4},

(A35)

so that the characteristic equation becomes

\displaystyle\Bigl(\lambda+\frac{J}{4}\Bigr)\Bigl(\lambda+\frac{K}{4}\Bigr)-JK\Bigl(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{4}e^{-\lambda\tau}\Bigr)^{2}=0.

(A36)

For
\tau=0
, this reduces to

\displaystyle\lambda^{2}+\frac{J+K}{4}\lambda-\frac{JK}{2}=0,

(A37)

hence the nondelayed condition

\displaystyle J+K>0,\qquad JK<0.

(A38)

For
\tau>0
, setting
\lambda=\iota\omega
shows that a Hopf crossing is possible only if
JK<0
. If
u_{c}=\omega_{c}^{2}>0
is a positive root of

256u^{3}+32(J^{2}+8JK+K^{2})u^{2}+\\
\bigl(J^{4}+16J^{3}K+66J^{2}K^{2}+16JK^{3}+K^{4}\bigr)u+2J^{3}K^{3}=0,

(A39)

then the corresponding critical delays are

\displaystyle\tau_{c,n}^{(2)}=\frac{\Arg(c_{c}+\iota s_{c})+2\pi n}{\omega_{c}},\qquad n\in\mathbb{Z},

(A40)

where

\displaystyle c_{c}=-1+\sqrt{\frac{-8u_{c}}{JK}},\;s_{c}=-\frac{2(J+K)\omega_{c}}{JK(2+c_{c})}.

(A41)

The same expression holds for the mode
k=N-2
.

A.3
\pi
states

Following the same approach as for the active
\pi
state in the main text, we look for a two-cluster solution of the form

\displaystyle x_{i}(t)=x_{s}+\chi_{i}+\omega_{x}t,

(A42)

\displaystyle\theta_{i}(t)=\theta_{s}+\chi_{i}+\omega_{\theta}t,

(A43)

where
\chi_{i}\in\{0,\pi\}
. Hence, for any pair
(i,j)
,

\displaystyle x_{j}(t)-x_{i}(t)=\theta_{j}(t)-\theta_{i}(t)=\Delta_{ji},

(A44)

with

\displaystyle\Delta_{ji}:=\chi_{j}-\chi_{i}\in\{0,\pm\pi\},

(A45)

and

\displaystyle\theta_{j}(t-\tau)-\theta_{i}(t)=\Delta_{ji}-\omega_{\theta}\tau,

(A46)

\displaystyle x_{j}(t-\tau)-x_{i}(t)=\Delta_{ji}-\omega_{x}\tau.

(A47)

Substituting into the equations of motion gives

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{J}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sin(\Delta_{ji})\cos(\Delta_{ji}-\omega_{\theta}\tau),

(A48)

\displaystyle\dot{\theta}_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{K}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sin(\Delta_{ji})\cos(\Delta_{ji}-\omega_{x}\tau).

(A49)

Since
\Delta_{ji}\in\{0,\pm\pi\}
, one has
\sin(\Delta_{ji})=0
for all
(i,j)
, and therefore

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{i}=0,\qquad\dot{\theta}_{i}=0.

(A50)

Then

\displaystyle\omega_{x}=0,\qquad\omega_{\theta}=0.

(A51)

Thus, the standard active
\pi
does not appear ; it necessarily reduces to a static
\pi
state.

We therefore consider the static
\pi
equilibrium

\displaystyle x_{i}^{*}=x_{s}+\chi_{i},\;\theta_{i}^{*}=\theta_{s}+\chi_{i},\;\chi_{i}\in\{0,\pi\},

(A52)

and perturb it as

\displaystyle x_{i}(t)=x_{i}^{*}+\xi_{i}(t),\theta_{i}(t)=\theta_{i}^{*}+\eta_{i}(t),

(A53)

where
|\xi_{i}|,|\eta_{i}|\ll 1
. At linear order, using
\sin\Delta_{ji}=0
,
\cos^{2}\Delta_{ji}=1
, and
\sin^{2}\Delta_{ji}=0
, one obtains

\displaystyle\dot{\xi}_{i}=\frac{J}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}(\xi_{j}-\xi_{i}),\;\dot{\eta}_{i}=\frac{K}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}(\eta_{j}-\eta_{i}).

(A54)

Writing

\displaystyle\bar{\xi}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\xi_{j},\qquad\bar{\eta}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\eta_{j},

(A55)

this becomes

\displaystyle\dot{\xi}_{i}=J(\bar{\xi}-\xi_{i}),\qquad\dot{\eta}_{i}=K(\bar{\eta}-\eta_{i}).

(A56)

The uniform modes correspond to the neutral eigenvalue
\lambda=0
, associated with global translations in
x
and
\theta
. For all nonuniform modes, one finds

\displaystyle\lambda_{x}=-J,\qquad\lambda_{\theta}=-K.

(A57)

Hence the static
\pi
state is linearly stable iff

\displaystyle J>0,\qquad K>0.

(A58)

.

We emphasize that the mathematical analysis framework developed in the main body of this work may also be applied to other cases involving asymmetric delays.

We then visualized the stability regions corresponding to the different observed dynamical regimes, namely the phase wave in cyan, the async state in gray, the static
