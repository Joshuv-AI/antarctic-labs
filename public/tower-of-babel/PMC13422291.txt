GPR17 Suppresses Triple-Negative Breast Cancer Progression and Serves as an Independent Prognostic Biomarke
Authors: Guannan Liu, Meng Wei, Yichuan Zhang, Zhong Lu, Min Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundG protein-coupled receptor 17 (GPR17), a Gi protein-coupled receptor, has been defined poorly about its roles and clinical significance in triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC).PurposeThe biological expression profile, prognostic value and functional roles of GPR17 in TNBC were elucidated in the present study.MethodsDifferentially expressed genes were screened through comprehensive bioinformatics analyses of the GSE233242 dataset. Introduction

Breast cancer represents the most prevalent and fatal malignancy in women worldwide, with prominent molecular heterogeneity that creates major obstacles for clinical diagnosis and therapeutic management [1]. Breast cancer molecular subtypes are classified according to the expression status of estrogen receptor (ER), progesterone receptor (PR), human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2), and the proliferation marker Ki-67 [2, 3]. Even within these well-characterized subtypes, considerable prognostic heterogeneity remains, which highlights the inadequacy of current biomarkers for precise risk stratification [4]. Identification of novel and reliable molecular signatures is therefore critical for optimizing prognostic evaluation and advancing personalized therapeutic strategies for breast cancer.

Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) presents a significant clinical challenge, as it lacks effective therapeutic targets. Currently, systemic treatment for TNBC is predominantly dependent on chemotherapy, though emerging therapies, such as immune checkpoint inhibitors, PARP inhibitors, and antibody-drug conjugates, are reshaping its treatment landscape [5, 6]. Despite these advances, TNBC still has the worst prognosis among all breast cancer subtypes; metastatic patients show a substantially lower 5-year survival rate than those with other subtypes [7]. This is largely attributed to TNBC’s high molecular heterogeneity, which leads to marked variability in patient treatment response and long-term survival [8]. Thus, moving beyond traditional pathological classification to identify novel molecular biomarkers for prognosis prediction and clinical decision-making is crucial for optimizing personalized management of TNBC patients. Recent studies have continued to uncover the complex molecular landscape driving TNBC progression, identifying diverse mechanisms ranging from metabolic reprogramming to epigenetic regulation [9–11].

To identify novel candidate genes in TNBC, GO enrichment analysis was performed on differentially expressed genes derived from the GSE233242 dataset. The chemokine-mediated signaling pathway emerged as one of the most significantly enriched terms. Within this pathway, most differentially expressed genes, including CXCR1/CXCR2, CXCL10, CXCL13, CCL14 and CCL24, have been well characterized in TNBC [12–17]. In contrast, the role of GPR17 has not been explored in any breast cancer subtype. Given this unaddressed research gap, along with previous evidence that GPR17 functions as a tumor suppressor in glioma [18], focus was placed on GPR17 in the present study.

GPR17 (G protein-coupled receptor 17) is a dual-ligand G protein-coupled receptor activated by uridine nucleotides and cysteinyl leukotrienes that regulates myelination and inflammatory responses [19, 20]. The tumor microenvironment provides abundant ligands for GPR17, including cysteinyl leukotrienes from infiltrating immune cells and UDP-glucose enriched in hypoxic regions [21]. These ligands activate downstream signaling pathways that converge on key oncogenic cascades, such as PI3K-AKT [22] and mTOR, to promote breast cancer cell proliferation and metastasis [23]. G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs) constitute the largest family of cell-surface receptors. They regulate diverse cellular processes, including proliferation, migration and invasion. Aberrant GPCR signaling has been implicated in breast cancer progression, and several GPCRs have emerged as prognostic biomarkers or therapeutic targets [24, 25]. While GPR17 is implicated in various diseases [26, 27], its specific functions and clinical significance in breast cancer remain poorly understood, with its mechanism of action and clinical value yet to be clarified.

However, the potential of GPR17 as a novel prognostic biomarker specifically in TNBC has not been systematically investigated, representing a gap in our current understanding. This study aims to characterize the expression profile, prognostic value, and functional roles of GPR17 in TNBC. Elucidating the biological and clinical significance of GPR17 in this subtype is critical, as it might shed light on novel aspects of TNBC biology and identify potential therapeutic targets.

Method

Collection of clinical data

Clinical data from 163 patients diagnosed with TNBC at The Affiliated Hospital of Panzhihua University were analyzed retrospectively from 2020 to 2023. Paired tumor and adjacent non-tumor tissue samples were collected intraoperatively, immediately snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at -80 °C for subsequent experiments. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of The Affiliated Hospital of Panzhihua University (approval number: 2020023).

Patients met the following inclusion criteria: females diagnosed with primary breast cancer; complete clinicopathological and follow-up data; primary surgical treatment at the institution. Exclusion criteria were male breast cancer, prior neoadjuvant chemotherapy or radiotherapy, synchronous malignancies, and incomplete clinical or pathological records. Clinicopathological data, including age, menopausal status, tumor stage (T), lymph node metastasis (LNM) status, and histological grade (HG), were extracted from the hospital’s electronic medical record system. Tumor staging was classified per the 8th edition American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) criteria.

The survival status and overall survival of the included patients were determined by reviewing up to 5 years of electronic medical records and follow-up archives. Recurrence-free survival (RFS) was defined as the time from surgery to the first documented local recurrence, regional recurrence, distant metastasis, or death from any cause. Patients without recurrence were censored at the date of last follow-up.

Bioinformatic analysis of differentially expressed genes

Data retrieval was conducted in the NCBI (National Center for Biotechnology Information) database using the keyword “Breast cancer”. Differential expression analysis was then carried out on the GSE233242 dataset using the GEO2R online tool (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/geo2r/) with default parameters. The analysis is based on the limma package v3.48.1. The Benjamini-Hochberg method applied for false discovery rate (FDR) correction. This dataset includes 43 pairs of clinical samples, each consisting of tumor tissue and its adjacent normal breast tissue from the same patient. For molecular subtype classification, 29 sample pairs are Luminal A, 3 are Luminal B, 2 are HER2-enriched, and 9 are TNBC. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using stringent thresholds: |log₂ (fold change) | > 2 and false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05. Data quality was assessed using box plots and UMAP visualization; no significant batch effects were observed for the GSE233242 dataset.

Functional enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes

Functional annotation of DEGs was performed with DAVID (Database for Annotation, Visualization and Integrated Discovery) Bioinformatics Resources v6.8 (https://david.ncifcrf.gov/). Significant Gene Ontology (GO) terms (across three categories: Biological Process [BP], Molecular Function [MF], and Cellular Component [CC]) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways were identified using a cutoff of FDR < 0.05, with the default EASE score (modified Fisher’s Exact P‑value) threshold set at 0.1 and the minimum gene count threshold set at 2.

Integrated multi-database analysis of GPR17 expression and regulation

To characterize the expression pattern of GPR17 in breast cancer and its prognostic relevance, expression profiling was conducted using the UALCAN platform (http://ualcan.path.uab.edu; TCGA-BRCA dataset) and bc-GenExMiner v4.8 (https://bcgenex.centregauducheau.fr; integrated multi-cohort database). The analysis focused on GPR17 mRNA expression differences between primary breast tumors and normal breast tissues (including adjacent normal tissues), as well as variations among major molecular subtypes: Luminal, HER2-enriched, and TNBC. KM-Plotter was used to examine associations between GPR17 expression and survival outcomes in TNBC patients. For all analyses, patients were stratified into high- and low-expression groups using the median GPR17 expression value in the sample set as the cutoff.

Cell culture

Human breast cancer cell lines MDA-MB-231 and MDA-MB-453 were obtained from Sunncell (lot numbers: SNL-073, SNL-057) and maintained as monolayer cultures. Both cell lines were grown in Leibovitz’s L-15 complete medium (specifically formulated for CO₂-independent culture) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin/streptomycin, in a 37 °C incubator with 100% air and no CO₂. Culture medium was replaced every 2–3 days. For routine passaging, cells were detached by trypsinization; digestion was stopped with complete medium when cells showed increased intercellular spacing but had not fully detached. Cells were collected by centrifugation, resuspended, and seeded into new culture vessels at a split ratio of 1:2 to 1:4.

Transfection experiment

For transfection experiments, full-length human GPR17 cDNA was synthesized and cloned into the pcDNA3.1(+) expression vector (ThermoFisher) to construct the GPR17 overexpression plasmid (OE-GPR17). The empty pcDNA3.1(+) vector was used as a negative control (OE-NC). Transient transfection was performed with Lipofectamine™ 3000 (ThermoFisher, Cat# L3000015) when cells reached 70–80% confluency. After 48 h, GPR17 mRNA levels were quantified by RT-qPCR with TaqMan™ Gene Expression Assays (ThermoFisher, Cat#4448489) to assess transfection efficiency.

RNA extraction and RT-qPCR

Total RNA was isolated from clinical tissue samples and cultured cells using TRIzol™ Reagent (#AM9738). For tissue homogenization, 30 mg of frozen tissue was pulverized in liquid nitrogen before being homogenized in TRIzol. RNA quality was evaluated via NanoDrop™ (A₂₆₀/A₂₈₀ > 1.9; A₂₆₀/A₂₃₀ > 2.0) and agarose gel electrophoresis. Reverse transcription was conducted with 1 µg of total RNA using the PrimeScript™ RT Reagent Kit (#RR037Q). qPCR was carried out on a QuantStudio™ 5 system with TB Green® Premix Ex Taq™ (#RR420Q). The following primer pairs were used: GPR17 (forward 5‘-CTCTGACTCCAGCCAAAGCATG-3’, reverse 5‘-GGTAGAAGGAGGCGAACAGCAT-3’) and glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) (forward 5‘-GTCTCCTCTGACTTCAACAGCG-3’, reverse 5‘-ACCACCCTGTTGCTGTAGCCAA-3’). qPCR amplification specificity for each gene was verified by melt curve analysis. Gene expression levels were quantified using the 2−ΔΔCt method, with GAPDH as the endogenous control gene.

Western blot analysis

Cells were harvested 48 h post-transfection and lysed in RIPA buffer (Beyotime, #P0013B) supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche, #04693159001). Protein concentration was determined using the BCA assay (Thermo Fisher, #23225). Equal amounts of protein (30 µg per lane) were separated by 12% SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore, #IPVH00010). Membranes were blocked with 5% nonfat milk in TBST for 1 h at room temperature and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies: anti-GPR17 (OriGene, #TA350166) and anti-GAPDH (Proteintech, #60004-1Ig). After washing with TBST, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, #7074) for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) substrate (Millipore, #WBKLS0500) and imaged with a ChemiDoc MP system (Bio-Rad). Densitometric quantification was performed using ImageJ software, and GPR17 expression was normalized to GAPDH.

Cell proliferation

Cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 3 × 10³ cells per well. At 0, 24, 48, 72, and 96 h, 10 µL of Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) reagent (Dojindo, #CK04) was added to each well. Following 2 h of incubation at 37 °C, absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a SpectraMax i3x microplate reader (Molecular Devices). Each experiment was performed in triplicate wells, with three independent biological replicates.

Transwell assays

Migration and invasion were evaluated using Transwell® assays (Corning). Serum-starved cells were seeded into the upper chambers, while medium supplemented with 10% FBS was added to the lower chambers as a chemoattractant. For invasion assays, inserts were pre-coated with Matrigel (BD Biosciences). After 24 h, cells were fixed with paraformaldehyde (PFA), stained with crystal violet, and counted in five or more random fields per membrane for quantification.

Statistical analysis

Bioinformatic analyses were performed using built-in statistical tools within each platform. Student’s t-test was used to assess differential expression in UALCAN, while the non-parametric Wilcoxon test was applied for bc-GenExMiner analyses. Survival differences were examined via the log-rank test. In the institutional clinical cohort, associations between GPR17 expression and clinicopathological characteristics were analyzed using the Chi-square test. Univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression models were applied to identify independent prognostic factors. Normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro‑Wilk test. Parametric tests were applied when data were normally distributed; otherwise, non‑parametric tests were used. All in vitro experiments were performed with three independent biological replicates, each with at least three technical replicates, and data are presented as the mean ± SD. Statistical significance (p < 0.05) for functional assays was determined using two-way ANOVA or Student’s t-test with GraphPad Prism v9.0.

Results

Identification and enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes in breast cancer

A total of 19,580 genes were analyzed in the GSE233242 dataset. Volcano plots of differential gene expression analysis illustrate transcriptomic changes between the experimental and control groups (Fig. 1A). A total of 827 DEGs were identified, including 416 upregulated and 411 downregulated, while 18,753 genes did not show significant differential expression.

DAVID functional annotation indicated that DEGs are implicated in breast cancer pathogenesis. As shown in Fig. 1B, GO enrichment analysis identified three notably enriched categories. For the BP category, enriched terms included chromosome segregation, extracellular matrix organization, and chemokine-mediated signaling pathways, with the latter being critical for tumor cell proliferation, migration, and invasion during carcinogenesis. For the CC category, the extracellular space and extracellular region were highly enriched. For the MF category, extracellular matrix structural components and serine-type endopeptidase activity were significantly enriched. Notably, GPR17, a key DEG in the chemokine-mediated signaling pathway, may modulate cancer progression by participating in this pathway.

KEGG enrichment analysis demonstrated that these DEGs were significantly enriched in multiple signaling pathways, including peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor (PPAR), extracellular matrix (ECM), receptor interaction, and AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) (Fig. 1C), suggesting coordinated regulation of DEGs through these pathways.

Expression of GPR17 in breast cancer and its association with prognosis analysis

At the gene expression level, UALCAN platform analysis revealed that GPR17 expression was markedly lower in primary tumor samples than in normal samples (p < 0.001, Fig. 2A). Consistent results were obtained using the bc-GenExMiner platform, confirming that GPR17 expression was significantly downregulated in tumor tissues compared with healthy (p < 0.0001, Fig. 2B).

This downregulation had clear clinical implications: low GPR17 expression correlated with poorer patient outcomes. To assess the prognostic value of GPR17 across breast cancer subtypes, survival analyses were performed. Notably, GPR17 exerted the strongest protective effect in TNBC patients, with high GPR17 expression associated with favorable outcomes (Fig. 2C). Survival curves for this subtype showed the most notable separation, and the high-expression group had significantly better prognosis (hazard ratio HR = 0.20, 95% CI 0.06–0.61; log-rank test p = 0.0016). High GPR17 expression also predicted better survival in HER2 + and Luminal A cohorts (2D: HR = 0.33, P = 0.0095; 2E: HR = 0.16, p = 0.0038). In contrast, no significant correlation between GPR17 expression and survival was observed in Luminal B patients (HR = 1.41, p = 0.37, Fig. 2F). Given its prominent prognostic significance in TNBC, subsequent mechanistic studies focused on this subtype.

External validation of the clinical significance of GPR17 in an institutional TNBC cohort

To verify the clinical significance of GPR17 in breast cancer, its expression was analyzed in paired tumor and adjacent normal tissues. As shown in Fig. 3A, GPR17 mRNA levels were markedly lower in tumor tissues compared with matched normal tissues (p < 0.001), indicating that GPR17 is a functionally important differentially expressed gene in breast cancer.

During a median follow-up of 31 months, patients with high GPR17 expression exhibited substantially better overall survival than those with low expression (log‑rank p < 0.001; Fig. 3B). The 5-year overall survival rate was 70.6% in the high-expression group versus 24.4% in the low-expression group. Similarly, high GPR17 expression was associated with significantly better RFS (log-rank p < 0.001; Fig. 3C). The 5-year RFS rates were 67.05% in the high-expression group and 21.79% in the low-expression group, consistent with the OS findings.

Univariate Cox proportional hazards regression identified three key prognostic factors: high GPR17 expression served as a robust protective factor (HR = 0.373, 95% CI = 0.233–0.598, p < 0.001), whereas advanced T stage (HR = 1.985, 95% CI = 1.263–3.118, p = 0.003) and LNM (HR = 1.560, 95% CI = 1.006–2.420, p = 0.047) were independent risk factors. The inhibitory effect of GPR17 on the migration and invasion abilities of breast cancer cells

To verify the tumor-suppressive role of GPR17 indicated by clinical data, stable GPR17-overexpressing cell lines were generated in MDA-MB-231 and MDA-MB-453 cells. RT‑qPCR confirmed successful GPR17 overexpression in both cell lines, with an approximately 1.8‑fold increase in mRNA levels compared to OE‑NC controls (Fig. 4A-B). Western blot analysis confirmed significantly elevated GPR17 protein expression in GPR17-overexpressing cells versus empty vector controls (p < 0.01; Fig. 4C-D), validating the overexpression model at the protein level. GPR17 overexpression gradually and significantly suppressed cell viability in both cell lines (p < 0.05, Fig. 5A-B). Transwell assays showed that GPR17 overexpression markedly reduced the migration of MDA-MB-231 and MDA-MB-453 cells (Fig. 5C-D, p < 0.01), and also significantly inhibited their invasive capacity (Fig. 5E-F, p < 0.01).

Discussion

Breast cancer is the leading cause of cancer-related deaths in women worldwide, with its progression driven by multiple factors that synergize via intricate signaling networks [28–30]. Bioinformatics analyses in this study corroborated this, as GSE233242 database analysis identified significant enrichment of differentially expressed genes in several cancer-associated pathways, including the chemokine signaling pathway, PPAR signaling pathway, and ECM-receptor interaction. These results highlight the systemic dysregulation underlying breast cancer pathogenesis. Given that GPR17 is enriched in the chemokine signaling pathway, a key mediator of tumor cell migration and inflammation, and its role in breast cancer remains understudied, this study focused on GPR17 to explore its functional relevance.

GPR17 is a context-dependent orphan G protein-coupled receptor whose function exhibits tissue microenvironment-regulated heterogeneity [31]. It contributes to tissue repair in multiple sclerosis, while promoting inflammatory responses and tissue degradation in osteoarthritis [32, 33]. In glioma, its expression directly regulates cancer cell proliferation and apoptosis [34, 35]. This functional diversity highlights the need to explore GPR17’s role in breast cancer, particularly in highly invasive subtypes. Prior database analyses linked GPR17 to poor prognosis in HER2-positive breast cancer and TNBC subtypes with inherently poor clinical outcomes [36]. Within the TNBC subtype, clinical cohort analysis confirmed that low GPR17 expression correlates with poor tumor differentiation and higher lymph node metastasis rates. Multivariate analysis identified GPR17 as an independent prognostic indicator for TNBC patients, and survival follow-up data further confirmed shortened overall survival and poorer prognosis in patients with low GPR17 expression.

In light of these clinical observations, the functional validation through the overexpression of GPR17 in TNBC cells was performed. This demonstrated that GPR17 overexpression markedly reduced cellular proliferation, invasion, and migration. Although GPR17 is known to be activated by purine-based ligands instead of classical chemokines [37], it belongs to the superfamily of Gi protein-coupled receptors. These receptors play a central role in the pathophysiology of breast cancer [38, 39]. Previous studies have reported the functional regulation of several specific receptors, which substantiates the view that Gi - GPCR signaling is a crucial mechanism mediating breast cancer progression. Given this receptor family classification, it is reasonable to postulate that the anti-migratory and anti-proliferative effects of GPR17 observed in our study may be mediated through Gi-dependent pathways. In other cell types, GPR17 has been shown to regulate cell motility [40]. Additionally, Gi-protein downstream effectors such as Rho GTPases are known to influence cytoskeletal dynamics [41]. Similarly, canonical Gi-GPCR signaling is well-documented to modulate cell cycle progression [18, 42]. This signaling pathway involves the inhibition of adenylate cyclase and the reduction of intracellular cAMP levels. However, the present study did not directly investigate these signaling events. Therefore, whether GPR17 utilizes these pathways in TNBC cells requires further experimental validation.

The mechanism of GPR17 downregulation in TNBC is unclear, but insights from other cancers and models offer potential explanations. In glioma, the transcription factor ETS1 binds the GPR17 promoter to drive its expression, and in oligodendrocytes, WNT/β-catenin signaling negatively regulates GPR17 transcription [43, 44]. Given TNBC’s complex molecular landscape, these transcriptional regulatory mechanisms may also play a role. Beyond transcription, epigenetic silencing via promoter hypermethylation, a well-documented mechanism for GPCR downregulation in various cancers, could be significant in TNBC, considering that aberrant DNA methylation is a hallmark of this subtype [45, 46]. Although these mechanisms have not been directly studied in TNBC, they present a promising framework for future research to clarify GPR17 downregulation in this aggressive cancer.

Collectively, this study validates the tumor suppressive role of GPR17 using integrated bioinformatics, clinical, and functional approaches. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. The functional characterization of GPR17 was confined to in vitro models, precluding a full understanding of its activity within the complex in vivo tumor microenvironment. Furthermore, the downstream signaling mechanisms mediating the suppression of TNBC progression by GPR17 remain unexplored, leaving open questions about its mode of action, such as potential Gi-protein dependency. Additionally, the median follow‑up period of 31 months is relatively short; an extended follow‑up would further validate the long‑term prognostic value of GPR17. Retrospective data hindered DMFS analysis. RFS captured metastasis, but DMFS absence limits assessing GPR17 on distant spread. Future studies should include DMFS as an independent endpoint for validation. Finally, as a retrospective cohort study, the lack of systematic data on patients’ dietary habits and long-term non-cancer medication use introduces potential confounding factors that cannot be ruled out. Future work will address these gaps through in vivo validation and mechanistic investigations.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study identifies GPR17 as a tumor suppressor in TNBC. Downregulation of GPR17 correlates with aggressive tumor phenotypes and unfavorable clinical outcomes. Although limitations exist, our findings validate GPR17 as a promising independent prognostic biomarker and therapeutic target for TNBC, providing a basis for further mechanistic exploration and translational applications.
