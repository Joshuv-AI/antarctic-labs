Characterization of [18F]SMBT‐1 binding substrates in Alzheimer's disease and related disorders: A postmortem biochemical and immunohistochemical study
Authors: Eric E Abrahamson, Julia K Kofler, Oscar L Lopez, Anne D Cohen, Alexandra Gogola, Howard J Aizenstein, Thomas K Karikari, Elliott J Mufson, Victor L Villemange, Milos D Ikonomovic
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONNeuroimaging studies report associations of amyloid beta (Aβ) positron emission tomography (PET) with [18F](S)‐(2‐methylpyrid‐5‐yl)‐6‐[(3‐fluoro‐2‐hydroxy)propoxy]quinoline ([18F]SMBT‐1), a novel reactive astrogliosis surrogate radiotracer with high affinity for monoamine oxidase B (MAO‐B) in Alzheimer's disease (AD). Postmortem association of [18F]SMBT‐1 binding with pathology of AD and non‐AD tauopathies is undefined.METHODS[18F]SMBT‐1 binding, [3H]Pittsburgh compound B ([3H]PiB) binding, and MAO‐B activity assays in brain homogenates, with [18F]SMBT‐1 autoradiography and MAO‐B immunoreactivity in relation to glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), major histocompatibility complex II (MHC‐II), Aβ, phosphorylated tau, cyano‐Pittsburgh compound B (cyano‐PiB), and X‐34 on brain sections from AD, non‐AD tauopathies, and nondemented controls.RESULTS[18F]SMBT‐1 binding correlated with MAO‐B activity and [3H]PiB binding, with highest levels in AD. [18F]SMBT‐1 autoradiography corresponded to MAO‐B/GFAP‐immunoreactive astrocytes associated with Aβ deposits in plaques and vasculature, more closely than to tau pathology. [18F]SMBT‐1 binding and MAO‐B activity in non‐AD tauopathies partially overlapped controls and AD, with MAO‐B/GFAP‐immunoreactive astrocytes in areas with tau pathology.DISCUSSION[18F]SMBT‐1 is a promising biomarker of MAO‐B reactive astrocytes in AD and non‐AD tauopathies.Keywords: amyloid‐beta, astrocytes, gliosis, monoamine oxidase B, neuroinflammation, plaques

BACKGROUND

Alzheimer's disease (AD) biomarker research has provided novel clinical diagnostic and research tools to evaluate the presence and burden of classic amyloid beta (Aβ) plaque and neurofibrillary tangle (NFT) pathology in living subjects. Recently, the importance of neuroinflammation as an active neuropathological process that occurs during the early disease stages in relation to AD lesions has been emphasized. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 The revised 2024 Alzheimer's Association research framework criteria 9 developed to facilitate the diagnosis and staging of AD in clinical practice and in treatment trials now recommends biomarkers for other pathological processes in AD including (neuro)inflammation (I), as well as amyloid, tau, and neurodegeneration [AT(N)], vascular brain injury (V), and α‐synucleopathy (S) to better capture the complex pathobiology of the disease. 9 In this research framework, the current biomarker for neuroinflammation is plasma and/or cerebrospinal fluid assay of astrocytic glial acidic fibrillary protein (GFAP). 9 The rationale for this is the observation that GFAP immunoreactive astrogliosis is extensive in AD brain 4 , 10 and GFAP immunoassays in biofluids provide indirect tools for evaluating such a process. In support of this, several studies have reported a relationship between plasma GFAP and AD pathology biomarkers. 1 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 However, changes in plasma GFAP are not necessarily exclusive for AD pathobiology 9 and do not inform on regional involvement of astrogliosis in the diseased brain. In contrast, positron emission tomography (PET) can inform on the topographical distribution of neuroinflammation in AD brain to further improve the current AD research framework strategy.

There have been important advances in the development of PET radioligands for neuroinflammation, by targeting brain gliosis. 20 , 21 These include development of PET tracers for microglia, but each has limitations, such as specificity for microglia activation [e.g., translocator protein (TSPO)] 22 and/or need for validation [e.g., macrophage colony‐stimulating factor 1 receptor binding ligand CPPC (5‐cyano‐N‐(4‐(4‐methylpiperazin‐1‐yl)‐2‐(piperidin‐1‐yl)phenyl)furan‐2‐carboxamide)]. 21 As a surrogate marker of astrogliosis, PET studies have used [11C]L‐deprenyl‐D2 ([11C]DED), 23 , 24 which binds selectively to monoamine oxidase B (MAO‐B), a catecholamine‐metabolizing enzyme expressed on the outer mitochondrial membrane of astrocytes. 25 However, irreversible kinetics of [11C]DED result in low sensitivity for astrogliosis and C‐11 labeling prevents its widespread clinical use. Recently, the discovery of [18F](S)‐(2‐methylpyrid‐5‐yl)‐6‐[(3‐fluoro‐2‐hydroxy)propoxy]quinoline ([18F]SMBT‐1), a tracer with selective, high affinity binding to MAO‐B and optimal properties for use as a PET radiopharmaceutical, has overcome these limitations. 20 , 26 , 27 , 28 [18F]SMBT‐1 PET in AD patients and, importantly, Aβ PET positive (A+) cognitively unimpaired elderly controls or individuals with mild cognitive impairment (MCI), was higher than in Aβ PET negative (A–) elderly nondemented subjects. 27 Several studies have conducted cross‐validation of PET imaging and biofluid assays of astrogliosis and reported that plasma GFAP levels correlate with [18F]SMBT‐1 PET retention in brain regions affected with Aβ deposition early in AD. 13 , 14 Although this suggests that [18F]SMBT‐1 PET and plasma GFAP measures can be useful biomarkers of AD pathological processes during the preclinical stage of the disease, both require further characterization in relation to postmortem evidence of AD brain neuropathology including astrogliosis. Histopathology studies have described the presence of MAO‐B immunoreactive astrocytes in AD pathology burdened brain regions, 29 , 30 , 31 , 32 but the relation of these cells as well as MAO‐B expression and enzymatic activity levels to SMBT‐1 binding remains to be characterized. To further understand [18F]SMBT‐1 ligand binding in AD and related dementias, we applied complementary in vitro binding, biochemistry, autoradiography, and immunohistochemistry assays to postmortem brain tissues from autopsy confirmed AD, non‐AD tauopathies, and nondemented control cases.

Brain autopsy consent was obtained under a protocol approved by institutional review boards and the use of autopsy tissue for research was approved by the Committee for Oversight of Research and Clinical Training Involving Decedents (CORID) at the University of Pittsburgh and Rush University Medical Center. Brain tissue samples were obtained from 44 autopsy cases from the University of Pittsburgh Alzheimer's Disease Research Center's brain bank and four cases from the Rush Religious Orders Study (RROS) a longitudinal clinicopathologic study of aging and AD involving retired Catholic clergy. 33 , 34 , 35 , 36 Case demographics and neuropathological characteristics are listed in Table 1. Neuropathology evaluation included immunohistochemical analyzes of Aβ, phosphorylated tau, α‐synuclein, and transactive response DNA‐binding protein (TDP‐43), as well as routine hematoxylin and eosin and Bielschowsky silver stain. Study included AD cases with high levels of Alzheimer's disease neuropathologic change (ADNC) (n = 19), control cases with low levels of ADNC (n = 9), and non‐AD tauopathy cases (n = 16) with diagnosis of progressive supranuclear palsy (PSP (n = 5), corticobasal degeneration (CBD, n = 2), Pick's disease (PiD, n = 2), frontotemporal dementia TDP‐43 (FTLD‐TDP, n = 1), FTLD‐tau/atypical PSP (n = 1), primary age‐related tauopathy (PART, n = 4), or severe aging‐related tau astrogliopathy (ARTAG, n = 1) based on consortium recommendations. 37 , 38 , 39 , 40 , 41 , 42 , 43

Brain tissue collection and processing

From each case, a 1–2 cm3 block of middle temporal cortex [MT, Brodmann area (BA21)] was dissected at autopsy (median postmortem interval = 7 hours), flash‐frozen on dry ice, and stored at –80°C until processed. Sections (10 µm thick) for autoradiography and immunohistochemical studies were obtained from each frozen block using a cryostat prior to processing the remainder of the tissue block for biochemistry studies, allowing us to perform these assays on the same biospecimen. From select cases, additional samples of MT were dissected fresh at autopsy, immersed in ice‐cold 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) made in phosphate buffer (PBS, pH 7.4) for 48 hours at 4°C, and cryoprotected by sequential immersion in 20% and 30% sucrose made in PBS prior to cutting 40 µm thick free‐floating sections for immunohistochemistry. Free‐floating sections were stored at –20°C in cryoprotectant (30% sucrose, 1% polyvinylpyrrolidone, 30% ethylene glycol made in 0.05% PBS, pH 7.4) until use.

Biochemistry

After obtaining cryostat sections, the remaining portion of frozen gray matter was homogenized in PBS using a glass dounce homogenizer (Wheaton, 357538, Fisher Scientific, Pittsburgh, PA) and briefly sonicated on wet ice at a concentration of 300 mg wet brain tissue/mL. Each homogenate was divided into separate aliquots for [18F]SMBT‐1 and [3H]PiB binding as well as MAO‐B activity assays to ensure that the same homogenate was available for all biochemical assays.

[18F]SMBT‐1 and [3H]PiB binding assays

Homogenates were diluted from 300 to 10 mg/mL with PBS and then processed with a Polytron tissue homogenizer (PT 10/35, Brinkman Instruments Inc., Westbury, NY, USA) at room temperature (RT) for 30 s at Setting 6. Unlabeled SMBT‐1 or PiB was dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) at 400 mM (to yield 51% DMSO in the final assay). This stock solution was diluted with PBS to achieve the desired concentration in the final assay. SMBT‐1 was fluorinated in house prior to each binding experiment. The average specific activity of [18F]SMBT‐1 was 39.276 Ci/µmol. [3H]PiB (American Radiolabeled Chemicals, St. Louis, MO, USA) had a specific activity of 72.4 Ci/mmol. One hundred milligrams of tissue in 1 mL PBS was incubated with [18F]SMBT‐1 (1.443 µCi/mL) or with [3H]PiB (2.654 µCi/mL). Non‐specific binding was defined as the number of counts remaining in the presence of 1 mM unlabeled ligands. The binding mixtures were filtered through a Whatman GF/B glass filter using a Brandel M‐24R cell harvester (Brandel, Gaithersburg, MD, USA) and rapidly washed five times with 3 mL PBS. Filters were counted in Cytoscint‐ES (MP Biomedicals, Inc, Santa Anna, CA, USA) after thorough vortexing and resting overnight. Results were corrected for non‐specific binding, which represents radiotracer that is not displaced by saturating concentrations of unlabeled compound and expressed as picomoles [18F]SMBT‐1 or [3H]PiB bound per gram of wet tissue weight in the homogenate.

MAO‐B activity assay

Aliquots from the initial homogenate used for binding assays were diluted 1:8 in tissue assay buffer and assayed for MAO‐B activity following the instructions of a commercial kit (MAK136, Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA). The principle of the assay is the reaction of MAO with p‐tyramine resulting in the formation of H2O2, which is then detected by a fluorometric method (λ excitation = 530 nm/λ emission = 585 nm) measured using a SpectraMax i3X spectrophotometer (Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA, USA). The assay was run in duplicate; four wells were used for each sample (two control and two sample wells) in black 96‐well plates with a clear bottom. MAO‐B activity was assayed by adding 5 µM of MAO‐B–specific inhibitor pargyline into the control well and subtracting the remaining MAO‐A activity from total MAO activity in the sample well. Absolute amounts were determined using an H2O2 standard curve prepared with each assay. The results were adjusted for total protein determined by microplate bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein assay (23227, Thermo, Waltham, MA) evaluated at 562 nm using the spectrophotometer.

Autoradiography

An aliquot of the same batch of [18F]SMBT‐1 prepared in house for binding assays was used for autoradiography studies. Frozen sections, obtained prior to homogenizing the brain tissue blocks for biochemical assays, were processed using storage‐phosphor screen autoradiography. 44 Sections were thawed from –80°C storage and air‐dried at RT for 15 min, incubated in ice‐cold PBS for 10 min, and then immersed in 70 mL ice‐cold [18F]SMBT‐1 (1.433 µCi/mL 0.1 M PBS; total radioactivity = 101 µCi) for 1 h. Sections were then rinsed twice for 2 min in ice‐cold PBS and dried at RT under gently flowing air for 10 min. The sections were placed in an exposure cassette (#29‐1755‐23, GE Healthcare Bio‐Sciences AB, Uppsala, Sweden) in close apposition to a storage phosphor screen (BAS‐IP TR 2025 tritium screen, Fujifilm Corp, Tokyo, Japan) overnight (≈16 h). The phosphor screen was scanned using a Typhoon FLA7000 imager (GE Healthcare, Amersham, UK) equipped with a 390 nm band‐pass integrated/packaged filter and images were saved as tiff format for analysis.

Immunofluorescence and histofluorescence

[18F]SMBT‐1 autoradiograms were assessed in relation to cyano‐PiB fluorescence staining of the same sections as well as immunofluorescent stains on adjacent thin cryosections by overlaying semi‐transparent images and assessing overlap qualitatively. After exposure to the tritium screen, sections were immersed in ice‐cold 4% PFA for 15 min, washed for 5 min twice in PBS, and stained with 1 µM cyano‐PiB using published protocols. 45 , 46 , 47 Slide‐mounted thin cryostat sections directly adjacent to those processed for autoradiography and cyano‐PiB were fixed as described and then rinsed for 2 min twice in PBS prior to immunofluorescence procedures. Free‐floating sections derived from 4% PFA fixed blocks were removed from –20°C storage and rinsed in PBS for 5 min three times prior to immunofluorescence procedures. For immunofluorescence processing, both section types were then immersed in 0.1 M Tris buffered saline (TBS, pH 7.4) for 5 min three times, followed by immersion in 3% normal goat serum made in TBS with 2.5% (volume/volume) Triton X‐100 (TBST) for 30 min at RT. Sections were then rinsed in 1% normal goat serum made in TBST for 10 min two times and placed in a cocktail of two primary antibodies, a polyclonal rabbit anti‐MAO‐B antibody (Invitrogen, HPA002328) diluted 1:250 and a polyclonal chicken anti‐GFAP antibody (Chemicon, AB5541) diluted 1:500 overnight at 4°C. An additional set of slide‐mounted thin cryostat sections adjacent to those used for autoradiography/cyano‐PiB and MAO‐B/GFAP immunofluorescence were immersed in 80% formic acid made in dH2O for 2 min at RT after fixation and prior to incubation in 3% goat serum. The sections were then processed as described, using a cocktail of a monoclonal rabbit anti‐Aβ antibody (Clone D54D2, Cell Signaling Technology, 8243) diluted 1:250 and a monoclonal anti‐tau antibody (clone AT8, Invitrogen, MN1020) diluted 1:2000 overnight at 4°C. After primary antibody incubation, sections were rinsed in 1% normal goat serum made in TBST for 10 min two times. For MAO‐B/GFAP dual immunofluorescence, the sections were then incubated in goat‐derived secondary antibodies generated against rabbit immunoglobulin (Ig) G (conjugated to Alexa488, Jackson ImmunoResearch, 111‐545‐144) and against chicken IgY (conjugated to Alexa647, Jackson, 103‐605‐155) for 2 h and 30 min at RT. For Aβ/tau dual immunofluorescence, the sections were incubated in goat‐derived secondary antibodies generated against rabbit IgG (conjugated to Alexa594, Jackson, 111‐585‐144) and mouse IgG (conjugated to Alexa488, Jackson, 115‐545‐166). The sections were then rinsed in TBS for 5 min three times, dried at RT, and coverslipped with Fluoromount. Select thin cryosections processed for immunofluorescence were overstained with X‐34 48 or cyano‐PiB prior to coverslipping. Additional sets of thick free‐floating sections were processed using the procedures described with the addition of either a mouse monoclonal antibody generated against collagen IV (clone COL‐94, Invitrogen, MA1‐22148) diluted 1:3000, a mouse monoclonal antibody generated against major histocompatibility complex (MHC) II (clone LN3, Invitrogen, MA5‐11966) diluted 1:250, or a mouse monoclonal antibody generated against the N‐terminus of Aβ (clone NAB228, Invitrogen, 374200) diluted 1:5000. These antibodies were visualized using a goat derived secondary antibody generated against mouse IgG and conjugated to Alexa594 (Jackson, 115‐585‐146). After mounting onto microscope slides, select sections processed free floating for immunofluorescence markers were overstained with either X‐34 or cyano‐PiB.

Fluorescent signal was imaged using an Olympus BX53 microscope connected to a fluorescence illuminator (X‐cite 120Q) and equipped with an Olympus DP72 digital camera connected to a Dell Precision T5500 computer running Olympus cell Sens Standard 1.6 imaging software (Olympus, Center Valley, PA, USA). For histofluorescence analyses, cyano‐PiB fluorescence was visualized using a hydroxycoumarin filter set (excitation peak: 405 nm, beam splitter: 425 nm, emission peak: 460 nm; #31016, Chroma, Bellows Falls, VT, USA). Fluorescence of X‐34 was visualized using a violet filter set (excitation peak 405 nm, dichroic mirror DM440, emission peak: 455 nm; #11005, Chroma). For immunofluorescence analyzes, fluorescence of the Alexa488 fluorophore was visualized using a fluorescein isothiocyanate‐compatible filter (excitation peak 480 nm, beam splitter 505 nm, emission peak 535 nm; #41001, Chroma). Fluorescence of the Alexa594 fluorophore was visualized using a tetramethylrhodamine isothiocyanate‐compatible filter (excitation peak 535 nm, beam splitter 565 nm, emission peak 610 nm; #41002, Chroma). Fluorescence of the Alexa647 fluorophore was visualized using a far‐red filter (excitation peak 620 nm, beam splitter 700 nm, emission peak 660 nm; #49006, Chroma).

Degree of involvement of MAO‐B and/or GFAP immunoreactive astrocytes with cored compared to diffuse plaques was assessed by percent area coverage analysis using sections stained with triple immunofluorescence for GFAP, MAO‐B, and Aβ. This was achieved by expanding the perimeter of each measured plaque 75 µm away from the plaque edge and measuring the percent area coverage of GFAP and MAO‐B within the resulting circle. Five cored plaques and five diffuse plaques were measured in each of five AD cases in this analysis. Astrocyte morphology was assessed qualitatively by GFAP immunofluorescence.

Statistical analysis

Groups were compared using one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) or unpaired t‐tests. Associations of measured variables were assessed using simple linear regression. Statistical analyzes and graphs were done in GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Prism version 10.0.0 for Windows, GraphPad Software, Boston, MA, USA). A p‐value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

In vitro [18F]SMBT‐1 binding and autoradiography: homogenate and tissue section analyses

MAO‐B activity, [18F]SMBT‐1 binding, and [3H]PiB binding in Controls, AD, and non‐AD tauopathies

The three assays were performed using aliquots of the same initial frozen tissue homogenate from each sample of middle temporal cortex from all cases.

MAO‐B activity (Figure 1A) was highest in the AD group (median = 0.4055 units MAO‐B activity/mg BCA protein; range = 0.2620–0.6919 units MAO‐B activity/mg BCA protein) followed by the non‐AD tauopathy group (median = 0.2664 units MAO‐B activity/mg BCA protein; range = 0.1656–0.5613 units MAO‐B activity/mg BCA protein) and the control group (median = 0.1854 units MAO‐B activity/mg BCA protein; range = 0.1198–0.3054 units MAO‐B activity/mg BCA protein) (Table 2). ANOVA showed a group effect [F (2, 41) = 13.62, p < 0.0001] and Tukey multiple comparisons test showed higher levels in AD compared to the control group (p < 0.0001) and the non‐AD tauopathy group (p = 0.0218) as well as higher levels in the non‐AD tauopathy group compared to the control group (p = 0.0244) (Figure 1A, Table 2). Both AD and non‐AD had substantial within‐group variability in MAO‐B activity levels. In the non‐AD tauopathy group, MAO‐B activity levels were higher in cases with Pick's disease, FTLD‐tau, or FTLD‐TDP, that were separated from those with a diagnosis of PSP, CBD, PART, or ARTAG.

Associations of MAO‐B activity, [18F]SMBT‐1 binding, and [3H]PiB binding with demographic and neuropathology variables

Linear regression analysis showed that higher levels of MAO‐B activity were significantly related to higher levels of [18F]SMBT‐1 binding [F(1, 42) = 29.15, p < 0.0001, R2 = 0.4127] (Figure 1D, Table 2). Higher [3H]PiB binding levels were significantly, although weakly, related to higher levels of [18F]SMBT‐1 binding [F(1, 40) = 8.986, p = 0.0047, R2 = 0.1831] (Figure 1E, Table 2). Higher levels of MAO‐B activity were significantly related to higher levels of [3H]PiB binding [F(1, 40) = 10.08, p = 0.0029, R2 = 0.2013] (Figure 1F, Table 2). More advanced Braak NFT stages and Thal Aβ phases were significantly related to higher levels of [18F]SMBT‐1 binding, [3H]PiB binding, and MAO‐B activity (Table 2). There were no associations between [18F]SMBT‐1 binding, [3H]PiB binding, and MAO‐B activity with age at death (Table 2) or other demographic/biological variables including cerebral amyloid angiopathy (CAA) severity and apolipoprotein E genotype (data not shown).

We next assessed patterns of MAO‐B and GFAP immunolabeling in relation to Aβ plaques and NFT labeled with the pan‐amyloid marker X‐34 in AD cases. Morphological variations of MAO‐B immunoreactive astrocytes in AD

In plaque‐burdened regions of AD cases, the morphology of astrocytes varied depending on the extent of GFAP and MAO‐B co‐labeling, which often showed an inverse relationship within individual astrocytes. Astrocytes with MAO‐B staining restricted to a portion of the soma displayed strong GFAP reactivity in both the soma and processes (Figure 5A,A1 ). Astrocytes with intensely MAO‐B immunolabeled soma appeared hypertrophic, with varying levels of GFAP immunoreactivity in processes and only a thin rim of GFAP‐immunoreactive cytoplasm, giving the cells a “swollen, hollow body” morphology (Figure 5A,A1 , 5B,B1 ). MAO‐B immunoreactivity was also observed to spread diffusely within a dense network of GFAP immunoreactive processes (Figure 5C,C1 ). Astrocytes expressing little or no MAO‐B immunoreactivity had GFAP reactivity throughout their soma and highly ramified processes that appeared to contact multiple blood vessels (Figure 5C,C1 ).

Analyses of MAO‐B and GFAP immunolabeling in non‐AD tauopathies

As in AD, the MAO‐B immunolabeling in non‐AD tauopathy cases was not as widespread as GFAP and varied across diseases. Consistent with the results of our biochemical analysis of MAO‐B activity in brain tissue homogenates, non‐AD tauopathies had lower MAO‐B levels compared to AD, except for a single Pick's disease case, which exhibited severe gliosis marked by GFAP immunoreactivity and concomitant high MAO‐B expression in areas containing AT8 tau‐immunoreactive Pick bodies, but no instances of MAO‐B co‐localization with AT8 (Figure 6A, Figure S1A). This case had the highest [18F]SMBT‐1 binding and MAO‐B activity of non‐AD tauopathy cases (see Figure 1A). Cases with a primary diagnosis of PSP showed scattered MAO‐B/GFAP co‐expressing astrocytes in areas most severely affected with tau pathology consisting of AT8‐immunoreactive tufted astrocytes (Figure 6B, Figure S1B), with instances of MAO‐B/GFAP/AT8 co‐localization (Figure S1B). Individuals with a primary diagnosis of CBD had a unique presentation of MAO‐B immunoreactivity, which appeared mainly as clusters of fine, dispersed puncta resembling endings or cut profiles of small caliber astrocytic processes in areas with GFAP immunostaining and AT8‐immunoreactive astrocytic plaques (Figure 6C, Figure S1C). Other non‐AD tauopathies examined in our study, including FTLD‐TDP‐tau (atypical PSP), PSP/severe ARTAG, FTLD‐TDP‐43, severe ARTAG, and PART exhibited isolated foci of prominent MAO‐B/GFAP immunoreactive astrocytes (not shown).

DISCUSSION

Astrogliosis is an important but often overlooked pathophysiological process in chronic neurodegenerative disorders including AD. 50 , 51 , 52 Studies in astrocyte cell culture and human astrocytoma tissue demonstrated that a subset of GFAP expressing reactive astroglia contain high levels of MAO‐B 25 and led to extensive research into utility of this enzyme as a biomarker for AD. 53 Here, we combined in vitro binding, autoradiography, enzyme activity, and immunohistochemical assays in postmortem brain tissues to assess the value of [18F]SMBT‐1, a novel selective reversible MAO‐B tracer, 20 as a biomarker of astrogliosis and to characterize its relation to regional changes in MAO‐B enzyme activity, astrocytic MAO‐B and GFAP expression, and diagnosis‐specific neuropathology of AD and various non‐AD tauopathies. Our in vitro analyses of split aliquots of cortical tissue homogenates demonstrated significantly higher levels of [18F]SMBT‐1 binding and MAO‐B enzyme activity in AD with moderate to high level of ADNC compared to controls and non‐AD tauopathies, with the two measures correlating significantly across study groups. Complementary analyses of tissue sections from the same samples demonstrated that [18F]SMBT‐1 autoradiography signal in AD corresponded to MAO‐B overexpressing reactive astrocytes in brain areas burdened with pathology, in agreement with a recent immunohistochemical study that reported greater areal fraction of MAO‐B immunoreactivity in AD with more severe ADNC. 29 In our AD cases, MAO‐B/GFAP‐labeled astroglia consistently made close contacts with classic (cored) Aβ plaques, in agreement with previous histopathological descriptions of plaque‐associated MAO‐B expressing astrocytes 29 , 30 , 54 and clinical imaging studies showing high [18F]SMBT‐1 PET retention in brain regions determined to be Aβ PET positive (A+, presumed to contain substantial Aβ plaque load) and a weaker associations with tau PET. 27 These findings are in accord with our observation that [18F]SMBT‐1 autoradiographic signal and MAO‐B immunoreactive astrogliosis did not correspond to dense cortical laminar patterns of p‐tau immunoreactive NFT and neuropil threads (Figures 2 and 3). However, we noted that many of the Aβ deposits associated with MAO‐B astrogliosis, as well as some CAA, contained tau‐immunoreactive neurites, consistent with dystrophic neurites defining mature neuritic Aβ plaques and reports of their associations with dyshoric CAA. 55 , 56 These results warrant future studies to further assess astrocytic MAO‐B overexpression and [18F]SMBT‐1 binding in relation to tau‐containing neuritic pathology. Although MAO‐B overexpressing astrocytes consistently engaged with Aβ deposits present in and around blood vessels affected by CAA (including dyshoric CAA), there was no direct association of these cells with diffuse Aβ plaques, microglia, or p‐tau immunoreactive NFT.

In AD brain tissue we found heterogeneous populations of astrocytes with varying proportions of GFAP and MAO‐B labeling. MAO‐B expression was robust in a subset of astrocytes with a strikingly hypertrophic morphology that differed from GFAP‐immunoreactive astrocytes with little or no detectable MAO‐B expression, suggestive of a change in function. In such MAO‐B overexpressing astrocytes, GFAP signal was typically restricted to blunted processes and a narrow rim of somal cytoplasm producing an appearance of a swollen body filled with MAO‐B. These morphological and biochemical changes could reflect a unique reactive state of astrocytes, potentially impacting their function. 57 Indeed, transcriptomic studies of astrocytes in relation to AD pathology reported complex spatial and temporal gene expression changes that could result in biochemical, morphological, and metabolic remodeling of these cells, resulting in gain of new toxic functions or upregulation of homeostatic functions in AD. 58 These changes can have widespread effects on neuroinflammatory responses, neurovascular unit function, brain homeostasis, regulation of blood–brain barrier, synaptic plasticity, and neurotransmitter levels. 59 , 60 , 61 The ring‐like congregations of MAO‐B overexpressing reactive astrocytes we observed around classic Aβ plaques and CAA‐laden blood vessels likely represent their response to Aβ fibrils and promote local neuroinflammation. Whether such a response contributes to plaque formation 62 or plaque clearance, 63 , 64 , 65 and the potential role of MAO‐B in astrocyte–microglia crosstalk, requires further investigation.

The group of non‐AD tauopathy cases also had MAO‐B activity levels above controls but below levels measured in AD (Figure 1). Like in AD, MAO‐B expression localized to cell bodies and processes in a subset of astrocytes (Figure 6; Figure S1), most notable in Pick's and FTLD‐tau, and less so in CBD, PSP, PART, or ARTAG. Previous immunohistochemical studies reported elevated MAO‐B immunosignal in Pick's disease, FTLD‐TDP, and CBD, but not in PSP or Lewy body dementia (LBD). 29 Here, we observed MAO‐B/GFAP co‐expression in areas with diagnosis‐specific tau lesions, most prominently around Pick bodies in Pick's disease. MAO‐B/GFAP astrogliosis also marked areas with tufted astrocytes in PSP and with astrocytic plaques in CBD. These results suggest that MAO‐B‐overexpressing astrocytes may react, albeit to different extents, to a wider spectrum of neuropathology changes. This supports the potential utility of [18F]SMBT‐1 PET in neuroimaging studies of Alzheimer's disease and related dementias (ADRD), combined with other disease‐specifying biomarkers. However, whether the relatively low level of MAO‐B/GFAP co‐expression in non‐AD tauopathies exceeds the currently undefined [18F]SMBT‐1 PET sensitivity threshold for reactive astrogliosis is unclear and needs to be assessed in future same‐case antemortem–postmortem studies.

A unique strength of our study is complementary in vitro binding, enzyme activity, autoradiography, and immunohistochemistry assays performed on tissue homogenates and sections derived from the same brain sample. This approach provides a high level of rigor to the correlative analyses performed across assays. In addition, our binding and autoradiography analyses used aliquots of [18F]SMBT‐1 synthesized for clinical research imaging studies performed at our center. Several limitations also need to be considered. First, we used GFAP immunochemistry to identify astrocytes, as in other histology studies. A recent consensus report recommended that a panel of molecular (and if possible, functional) markers be used to better define populations of reactive astrocytes. This panel includes GFAP as well as others (e.g., aldehyde dehydrogenase 1, chitinase‐3‐like protein 1, heat shock proteins, and S100 calcium‐binding protein B), which together can provide greater insight into changes in astrocytic cytoskeleton, metabolism, production of molecular chaperones, and signaling molecules 61 to better understand astrocytes in AD and non‐AD tauopathy cases. Second, although the non‐AD tauopathy group included a variety of neuropathological diagnoses with three‐repeat or four‐repeat tau, allowing us to explore diagnostic specificity of SMBT‐1 and MAO‐B assays for AD, the number of cases per each non‐AD diagnosis was small. Nevertheless, levels of SMBT‐1 and MAO‐B measures in the non‐AD tauopathy cases were significantly lower than in the AD group, despite substantial within‐group variability. Although these data should be considered conservatively, they suggest that PET imaging of reactive astrogliosis with [18F]SMBT‐1 may be most reliable for imaging of AD. Third, our labeling of Aβ deposits in plaques and CAA focused on fibrillar Aβ aggregates detectable by amyloid‐binding dyes cyano‐PiB and X‐34 as well as immunochemically using N‐terminus‐directed anti‐Aβ antibodies. Future studies designed to assess the relation of MAO‐B expressing astrocytes to different Aβ species (e.g., Aβ42, Aβ40, pyroglutamate Aβ) and apolipoprotein E are warranted to better understand the molecular stimulus for astroglial MAO‐B upregulation, the high variance of [18F]SMBT‐1 binding, and the lack of a strong correlation with [3H]PiB binding within the AD group. Individual differences in Aβ species aggregated in plaques (as we have discussed) and/or differences in the abundance and proportions of classic cored and diffuse plaques, which are both detectable with PiB, 45 , 46 , 47 could contribute to individual differences in the neuroinflammatory response to AD pathology. SMBT‐1/MAO‐B astrogliosis appears to associate exclusively with mature plaques, suggesting that high MAO‐B activity levels and [18F]SMBT‐1 PET retention are indicative of an enhanced neuroinflammatory response to the presumably immunogenic mature Aβ plaques and CAA. Thus, [18F]SMBT‐1 PET may have clinical utility in identifying patients most likely to benefit from anti‐inflammatory therapy, or who may be responsive to anti‐Aβ monoclonal antibody infusion in therapeutic trials, although the possibility of underlying CAA must be considered due to risk of amyloid‐related imaging abnormalities (ARIA). 66 , 67 Integration of [18F]SMBT‐1 PET into AD diagnostic and research frameworks will help bring the field closer to much needed personalized medicine approaches to studies of AD. 68 It should be noted that our case cohort included aged non‐demented control cases that were free of AD pathology (i.e., “resistant”), and therefore may not be representative of all cognitively unimpaired elderly cohorts. Clinical‐neuropathological and imaging studies report that a significant proportion of non‐demented elderly show Aβ and tau pathology, possibly representing a preclinical stage of the disease. 69 , 70 , 71 These cases have been considered cognitively “resilient” to AD pathology 72 and may be classified by PET as A+ (or A+T+) cognitively unimpaired. Future studies of [18F]SMBT‐1 binding and MAO‐B astrogliosis in relation to pathology forms present in resilient/preclinical cases are needed to determine the status of astrogliosis at this early stage of AD. In addition, imaging to autopsy studies of neuropathologically well‐characterized cases with antemortem [18F]SMBT‐1 PET as well as Aβ and tau PET scans will be needed to determine the sensitivity of SMBT‐1 for MAO‐B expressing astrocytes in relation to AD pathology burdens.

In conclusion, this postmortem study provides novel insights into binding patterns of [18F]SMBT‐1 in relation to regional MAO‐B activity, astrocytic MAO‐B expression, and [3H]PiB binding in the neocortex from AD, non‐AD tauopathy, and non‐demented control cases. It also provides further characterization of the immunohistochemical patterns of astrocytic MAO‐B expression relative to the more widespread expression of GFAP and in association with astrocyte morphology changes and regional Aβ and tau pathology. The observation that a unique subset of astrocytes expressing high levels of MAO‐B is closely associated with fibrillar Aβ in mature plaques and CAA supports the use of [18F]SMBT‐1 PET as a biomarker of reactive astrogliosis in AD. Furthermore, our data indicate the potential utility of [18F]SMBT‐1 PET for evaluation of astrogliosis in non‐AD tauopathies. However, the overlapping ranges of [18F]SMBT‐1 binding values observed in AD and non‐AD cases warrant further investigations to better understand the diagnostic value of the tracer.
