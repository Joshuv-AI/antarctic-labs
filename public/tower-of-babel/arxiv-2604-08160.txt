Joint Range-Angle Estimation in Near-Field ISAC System using Uniform Circular Array
Lorenzo Zaniboni 1 , Mark F. Flanagan 2

Abstract
This paper studies joint range-angle estimation and communication in the near-field (NF) integrated sensing and communication (ISAC) systems where the base station (BS) serves a single user equipment (UE) whose position is simultaneously estimated via monostatic sensing. Unlike the uniform linear array (ULA), the uniform circular array (UCA) provides an angle-invariant NF region due to its rotational symmetry. To capture the full wideband NF propagation environment, we develop a continuous-time channel model incorporating per-element delay, Doppler shifts, and spherical wavefront geometry under orthogonal frequency-division multiplexing (OFDM) signaling. Building on this model, we derive the closed-form Cramér-Rao lower bound (CRLB) for joint range-angle estimation of the UE position, design an optimal transmit beamformer via Riemannian gradient descent, and formulate a joint range-angle maximum likelihood (ML) estimator. Monte Carlo simulations confirm a fundamental aperture-versus-signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) trade-off in NF-ISAC: while a larger UCA radius tightens the CRLB, it simultaneously reduces the received SNR at any given distance, pushing the maximum likelihood estimator below its convergence threshold and degrading practical performance. Among the evaluated configurations, R = 0.5 R=0.5 m achieves the best joint estimation and communication performance at the BS by sustaining the highest received SNR throughout the evaluated range.



I
Introduction

ISAC is emerging as a key enabling technology for sixth-generation (6G) wireless networks, where sensing and data transmission share the same spectrum and hardware infrastructure
[
1
,
2
]
. The convergence of extremely large-scale antenna arrays (ELAAs)s and millimeter wave (mmWave) frequencies in future 6G deployments push wireless systems into the radiative NF region, where the spherical wavefront structure of the propagating signal must be explicitly accounted for
[
3
,
4
]
. Unlike the far-field (FF) regime, where only angle information is extractable from array measurements, NF propagation introduces an additional variable, the distance dimension, that enables joint range-angle estimation from a single BS
[
5
]
. This makes NF operation particularly attractive for ISAC applications, where accurate target localization is a primary objective alongside high-rate communication. The majority of existing NF-ISAC contributions adopt ULAs
[
5
,
6
]
or discrete channel models that neglect delay and Doppler shifts
[
7
]
.

Although these simplifications enable tractable analysis, they fail to capture the full richness of the wideband NF propagation environment. In particular, as shown in
[
8
]
, the spherical wavefront introduces strong angular-delay correlations and non-uniform Doppler frequencies in wideband NF systems – effects that cannot be modeled within a narrowband or discrete-time framework. Furthermore, ULA-based NF analysis reveals a fundamental limitation: the effective array aperture – and consequently the near-field region – shrinks dramatically as the target moves away from broadside, leaving large portions of the coverage area without NF benefit
[
9
]
. The work in
[
6
]
further shows that as the ULA aperture grows sufficiently large to enter geometric near-field regime, where the array size becomes comparable to the target distance, the CRLB for joint range-angle estimation saturates. This saturation stems from phase profile nonlinearities and non-uniform path loss across elements. These limitations motivate a rethinking of the array geometry for NF-ISAC systems.

The UCA has recently attracted attention as an alternative geometry for NF communications. Due to its rotational symmetry, the UCA maintains a uniform effective aperture at all azimuth angles, providing an angle-invariant near-field region that the ULA cannot achieve. It has been shown in
[
9
]
that the UCA’s effective Rayleigh distance is uniform across all directions, while that of the ULA collapses; though
[
9
]
focuses solely on communication coverage and does not address joint sensing. For monostatic radar sensing – where the target can be located at any angle relative to the BS – this angle-symmetric coverage is a decisive advantage. Indeed, as we show in this work, with
N_{\rm a}
= 64 elements at 60 GHz, a UCA with radius R = 0.5 m achieves a near-field region of approximately 400 m, compared to only 10 m for a ULA with half-wavelength spacing – a 40× advantage with identical hardware.
A concurrent work
[
10
]
recently proposed a UCA-based NF-ISAC framework and derived the CRLB on azimuth angle and perpendicular distance and designed an optimal beamformer, but under a narrowband channel model neglecting delay and Doppler shifts. To the best of our knowledge, no prior work combines wideband OFDM, spherical wavefront geometry, CRLB-optimal beamforming, and ML estimation in a unified UCA NF-ISAC framework.
The contributions of this work are summarized below.

•

We develop a continuous-time wideband NF-ISAC channel model for an UCA-equipped BS incorporating delay, Doppler shifts, and spherical wavefront propagation – extending prior narrowband UCA models
[
10
]
to the wideband regime.

•

We derive the closed-form CRLB for joint range-angle estimation, revealing how the UCA radius governs a fundamental trade-off between aperture size and received SNR at the BS in the NF regime.

•

We design an optimal transmit beamformer via Riemannian gradient descent and formulate a gradient-based ML estimator via Levenberg–Marquardt initialized by a two-stage grid search, demonstrating that
R=0.5
m achieves the best performance in the low-SNR regime.

II
System Model

Consider a NF system consisting of a BS and a single UE as in Fig.
1
. The BS is equipped with a UCA of radius
R
lying in the azimuth plane. The
k
-th antenna element is located at angular position
\psi_{k}=2\pi(k-1)/N_{\rm a}
relative to a reference direction. The distance
d

\theta
, measured from the reference direction are the parameters to be estimated. The UE is located within the NF region of the BS, meaning that the distance
d
is less than the Rayleigh distance, i.e.
\frac{2D^{2}}{\lambda}
, where
D
is the diameter of the UCA. The distance from the
k
-th element to the UE then follows from the law of cosines as

\displaystyle r_{k}=\sqrt{d^{2}+R^{2}-2dR\cos(\phi_{k})},

(1)

where
\phi_{k}\triangleq\theta-\psi_{k}
is the relative angle between the UE direction and the
k
-th element.

Schematic of the system model.

The UCA steering vector, which captures the phase and amplitude response of each element at position (
d
,
\theta
), is

\displaystyle{\bf a}(d,\theta)=\frac{e^{j\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}d}}{\sqrt{N_{\rm a}}}\left[e^{-j\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}r_{1}},\dots,e^{-j\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}r_{N_{\rm a}}}\right]\in\mbox{\bb C}^{N_{\rm a}\times 1}.

(2)

Since the transmitter (Tx) and receiver (Rx) are co-located (monostatic), the same vector
{\bf a}(d,\theta)
describes both the Tx and Rx array responses. Unlike the ULA geometry studied in
[
5
,
6
]
, the rotational symmetry of the UCA ensures that this NF region is angle-invariant
[
9
]
.
In the FF regime, the Friis formula gives a single amplitude gain
\lambda/(4\pi d)
shared by all elements, since the wavefront is planar and the array-to-target distance is well approximated by
d
for every element
[
11
]
. In the NF, the per-element distance
r_{k}
varies across the array, so a free-space channel gain
g_{k}(r_{k})
is introduced, which takes also into account the angle component. The channel gain can be written then as

\displaystyle g_{k}(r_{k})=\frac{\lambda}{4\pi r_{k}}.

(3)

The channel is characterized in the time-delay (TD) domain by a single propagation path with delay
\tau_{0}=2d/c
, Doppler shift
\nu_{0}
, and per-element gain
g_{k}(r_{k})
as defined in (
3

BS array to the single-antenna UE, is the
N_{a}\times 1
vector

\displaystyle{\bf h}_{\rm comms}(t,\tau)=g_{k}(r_{k})\,{\bf a}(d,\theta)\,\delta(\tau-\tau_{0}/2)\,e^{j2\pi\nu_{0}t}.

(4)

The UE is modeled as a point target that reflects the incident waveform back toward the BS. The resulting round-trip sensing channel — from the BS Tx array, via the UE, back to the BS Rx array — is the
N_{a}\times N_{a}
matrix

\displaystyle{\bf H}_{\rm sens}(t,\tau)=g_{k}(r_{k})\,{\bf a}(d,\theta)\,{\bf a}^{\sf T}(d,\theta)\,\delta(\tau-\tau_{0})\,e^{j2\pi\nu_{0}t},

(5)

where
\tau_{0}=2d/c
reflects the two-way propagation path, compared to the one-way delay
\tau_{0}/2
in (
4
).

II-A

OFDM signaling

An OFDM waveform for the Tx signal is considered, which carries both pilots and data symbols and is written as

\displaystyle{\bf s}(t)={\bf f}\sum_{m=0}^{M-1}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}x[n,m]{\sf rect}\left(\frac{t-nT_{o}}{nT_{o}}\right)

\displaystyle\times\,e^{j2\pi m\Delta f(t-nT_{o}-T_{\rm cp})},

(6)

where
T_{o}=T+T_{\rm cp}
is the total OFDM symbol duration and
x[n,m]
is the complex symbol at the
m
-th subcarrier and the
n
-th OFDM symbol. The magnitude of the transmitted symbols is chosen to fulfill the average power constraint as

\displaystyle\mbox{\bb E}[|x[n,m]|^{2}]=P_{t}\quad\forall(n,m).

(7)

Another condition is that the subcarrier spacing is chosen to satisfy

\displaystyle\nu_{\rm max}\ll\Delta f,

(8)

where
\nu_{\max}
is the maximum Doppler shift in the system. The component
{\bf f}\in\mbox{\bb C}^{N_{\rm a}\times 1}
denotes a generic beamforming (BF) vector of unit norm.

II-B

Received Signal Model

The signal at the UE is formed by convolving the transmitted waveform
{\bf s}(t)
with the communications channel in (
4
) and adding receiver noise
w(t)
. On the
n
-th OFDM symbol interval, the cyclic prefix (CP) is first discarded, and a length-
M
discrete Fourier transform (DFT) is applied to recover the frequency-domain samples. Substituting (
4
) and exploiting (
8
), the sampled signal at the
m
-th subcarrier of the
n
-th symbol at the UE is

\displaystyle\bar{y}[n,m]=

\displaystyle g_{k}(r_{k}){\bf a}^{\sf T}(d,\theta){\bf f}e^{j\pi\nu_{0}nT_{o}}x[n,m]

\displaystyle e^{-j2\pi m\Delta f(T_{\rm cp}+\tau_{0}/2)}+w[m,n],

(9)

where
w[m,n]\sim\mathcal{C}\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
is additive white Gaussian noise (AWGN) and the exponentials capture the residual Doppler phase accumulated over
n
symbol periods and the subcarrier-dependent delay phase, respectively.
Similarly, the received (back-scattered) signal at the BS can be written as

\displaystyle\bar{{\bf r}}[n,m]=

\displaystyle g_{k}(r_{k}){\bf a}(d,\theta)\beta(d,\theta)x[n,m]

\displaystyle e^{-j2\pi m\Delta f(T_{\rm cp}+\tau_{0})}e^{j2\pi\nu_{0}nT_{o}}+\bar{{\bf n}}[n,m],

(10)

where
\bar{{\bf n}}[n,m]\sim\mathcal{C}\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
and we define
\beta(d,\theta)={\bf a}^{\sf T}(d,\theta){\bf f}
. The explicit delay term
e^{-j2\pi m\Delta f(T_{\rm cp}+\tau_{0})}
and Doppler term
e^{j2\pi\nu_{0}nT_{o}}
distinguish the model from the narrowband discrete-time formulation of
[
10
]
, and are essential for accurate wideband NF sensing
[
8
]
.

III
Optimization and Estimation Analysis

III-A

Cramér–Rao Lower Bound

The CRLB is derived for the estimation of the parameter vector
\boldsymbol{\eta}=[d,\theta]^{\sf T}
. The noiseless measurement in (
II-B
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mu}(\boldsymbol{\eta})=C_{n,m}g_{k}(r_{k}){\bf a}(d,\theta)\beta(d,\theta),

where
C_{n,m}=x[n,m]\,e^{j2\pi\nu_{0}nT_{o}}\,e^{-j2\pi m\Delta f(T_{cp}+\tau_{0})}
.

Under the noise model in (
II-B
), the
2\times 2
Fisher information matrix (FIM) can be derived as

\displaystyle\mathbf{J}(\boldsymbol{\eta})=\frac{2}{\sigma^{2}}\begin{bmatrix}{\rm Re}\left\{\sum_{m,n}\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\mu}^{H}}{\partial d}\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\mu}}{\partial d}\right\}&{\rm Re}\left\{\sum_{m,n}\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\mu}^{H}}{\partial\theta}\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\mu}}{\partial d}\right\}\\[6.0pt]
{\rm Re}\left\{\sum_{m,n}\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\mu}^{H}}{\partial d}\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\mu}}{\partial\theta}\right\}&{\rm Re}\left\{\sum_{m,n}\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\mu}^{H}}{\partial\theta}\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\mu}}{\partial\theta}\right\}\end{bmatrix}.

(11)

In order to determine the entries of the FIM, the derivatives with respect to
\boldsymbol{\eta}
should be expressed as

\displaystyle\left[\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\mu}}{\partial d}\right]_{k}

\displaystyle=C_{m,n}g_{k}[{\bf a}(d,\theta)]_{k}\,\gamma_{k}^{d},

\displaystyle\left[\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\mu}}{\partial\theta}\right]_{k}

\displaystyle=C_{m,n}g_{k}[{\bf a}(d,\theta)]_{k}\,\gamma_{k}^{\theta},

(12)

where
\gamma_{k}^{d}=-\frac{\alpha_{k}^{d}}{r_{k}}\beta+j\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}\bigl(z^{d}+\beta(1-\alpha_{k}^{d})\bigr)
and
\gamma_{k}^{\theta}=-\frac{\alpha_{k}^{\theta}}{r_{k}}\beta-j\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}\bigl(z^{\theta}+\beta\alpha_{k}^{\theta}\bigr)
. Therefore, we define
\alpha_{k}^{d}=\frac{r-R\cos(\phi_{k})}{r_{k}}
,
\alpha_{k}^{\theta}=\frac{rR\sin(\phi_{k})}{r_{k}}
,
z^{d}={\bf a}^{\sf T}(d,\theta){\bf D}^{d}{\bf f}
and
z^{\theta}={\bf a}^{\sf T}(d,\theta){\bf D}^{\theta}{\bf f}
, respectively.

The diagonal matrices are defined as
{\bf D}^{d}={\hbox{diag}}(1-\alpha_{k}^{d})
and
{\bf D}^{\theta}={\hbox{diag}}(\alpha_{k}^{\theta})
.
Denoting
[{\bf v}_{i}]_{k}=g_{k}\gamma_{k}^{i}[{\bf a}(d,\theta)]_{k}
for
i\in\{d,\theta\}
, the FIM entries in (
11
) satisfy
\sum_{m,n}(\partial\boldsymbol{\mu}_{m,n}/\partial\eta_{i})^{\sf H}(\partial\boldsymbol{\mu}_{m,n}/\partial\eta_{j})=MNP_{t}\,{\bf v}_{i}^{\sf H}{\bf v}_{j}
, where the inner product decomposes as

\displaystyle{\bf v}_{i}^{\sf H}{\bf v}_{j}=\sum_{k}(\gamma_{k}^{i})^{\ast}\gamma_{k}^{j}\underbrace{|g_{k}|^{2}}_{\lambda^{2}/16\pi^{2}r_{k}^{2}}\underbrace{|[{\bf a}(d,\theta)]_{k}|^{2}}_{1/N_{\rm a}}.

The (
i,j
)-th entry of the FIM can be written as

\displaystyle J_{i,j}=\frac{2NMP_{t}\lambda^{2}}{16\pi^{2}\sigma^{2}N_{\rm a}}\mathrm{Re}\left\{\sum_{k=0}^{N_{\rm a}-1}\frac{(\gamma_{k}^{i})^{\ast}\gamma_{k}^{j}}{r_{k}^{2}}\right\}.

(13)

The CRLB matrix is therefore obtained by inverting the FIM in (
11
)

{\bf C}(\boldsymbol{\eta,{\bf f}})={\bf J}(\boldsymbol{\eta})^{-1}=\frac{1}{{\hbox{det}}({\bf J}(\boldsymbol{\eta}))}\begin{bmatrix}J_{\theta,\theta}&-J_{d,\theta}\\
-J_{d,\theta}&J_{d,d}\end{bmatrix},

(14)

where the FIM determinant can be computed as

\displaystyle{\hbox{det}}\,{\bf J}=J_{d,d}J_{\theta,\theta}-J_{d,\theta}^{2}.

In order to minimize the CRLB, the beamforming vector
{\bf f}
is optimized by minimizing
\Tr({\bf C}(\boldsymbol{\eta,{\bf f}}))
. The optimization problem can be formulated as

\displaystyle\hat{{\bf f}}=

\displaystyle\min_{{\bf f}}\quad

\displaystyle\Tr({\bf C}(\boldsymbol{\eta,{\bf f}}))

(15)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle\left\lVert{\bf f}\right\rVert^{2}_{2}=1,

where the unit norm constraint enforces a fixed Tx power budget.
Substituting (
14
) and (
13
), the objective becomes

\displaystyle\Tr({\bf C}(\boldsymbol{\eta,{\bf f}}))=\frac{J_{d,d}+J_{\theta,\theta}}{J_{d,d}J_{\theta,\theta}-J_{d,\theta}^{2}}.

(16)

All three FIM entries depend on
{\bf f}
through the scalars
\beta,z^{d}
and
z^{\theta}
, so (

{\bf f}
. The optimization problem in (

\Tr({\bf C})
with respect to
{\bf f}
, denoted
\partial\Tr({\bf C})/\partial{\bf f}

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\Tr({\bf C})}{\partial{\bf f}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{K(\mathcal{RT}-\mathcal{X}^{2})^{2}}\Bigl[(\mathcal{RT}-\mathcal{X}^{2})\Bigl(\tfrac{\partial\mathcal{R}}{\partial{\bf f}}+\tfrac{\partial\mathcal{T}}{\partial{\bf f}}\Bigr)

\displaystyle\quad-(\mathcal{R}+\mathcal{T})\Bigl(\mathcal{T}\tfrac{\partial\mathcal{R}}{\partial{\bf f}}+\mathcal{R}\tfrac{\partial\mathcal{T}}{\partial{\bf f}}-2\mathcal{X}\tfrac{\partial\mathcal{X}}{\partial{\bf f}}\Bigr)\Bigr],

(17)

where
\mathcal{R}=J_{d,d}/K
,
\mathcal{T}=J_{\theta,\theta}/K
and
\mathcal{X}=J_{d,\theta}/K
, with
K=\frac{2NMP_{t}\lambda^{2}}{16\pi^{2}\sigma^{2}N_{\rm a}}
. The individual gradients follow from the chain rule since
\mathcal{R}
,
\mathcal{T}
and
\mathcal{X}
depend on
{\bf f}
through
\beta,z^{d}
and
z^{\theta}
, where
\nabla_{\bf f}\Tr({\bf C})
expands via the chain rule as

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\Tr({\bf C})}{\partial{\bf f}}={\bf a}^{\sf T}\frac{\partial\Tr({\bf C})}{\partial\beta}+{\bf a}^{\sf T}{\bf D}^{d}\frac{\partial\Tr({\bf C})}{\partial z^{d}}+{\bf a}^{\sf T}{\bf D}^{\theta}\frac{\partial\Tr({\bf C})}{\partial z^{\theta}}.

The Riemannian gradient is obtained by projecting the Wirtinger derivative onto the tangent space of the unit sphere at
{\bf f}^{(t)}

\displaystyle\nabla^{\perp}_{\bf f}\Tr({\bf C})=\frac{\partial\Tr({\bf C})}{\partial{\bf f}}-\mathrm{Re}\left\{\frac{\partial\Tr({\bf C})}{\partial{\bf f}}\cdot\left({\bf f}^{(t)}\right)^{\sf H}\right\}{\bf f}^{(t)}.

The iterate
{\bf f}^{(t)}
follows the negative gradient direction, and the result is projected onto the unit sphere by normalization and the iterate updated as

\displaystyle{\bf f}^{(t+1)}=\frac{{\bf f}^{(t)}-\alpha_{t}\left(\frac{\partial\Tr({\bf C})}{\partial{\bf f}}\right)^{\sf H}}{\left|{\bf f}^{(t)}-\alpha_{t}\left(\frac{\partial\Tr({\bf C})}{\partial{\bf f}}\right)^{\sf H}\right|},

where the step size
\alpha_{t}
is chosen by backtracking line search, and the algorithm terminates when
\|\nabla_{{\bf f}}^{\bot}\Tr({\bf C})\|<\varepsilon
. Finally, the individual bound on the variances of the CRLB can be expressed as

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(\hat{d})=\frac{J_{\theta,\theta}}{{\hbox{det}}\,{\bf J}},\quad\quad\mathrm{Var}(\hat{\theta})=\frac{J_{d,d}}{{\hbox{det}}\,{\bf J}}.

The closed-form expressions of the CRLB are given in (
18
)–(
19
), where
\beta_{R}\triangleq\mathrm{Re}\{\beta\}
,
\beta_{I}\triangleq\mathrm{Im}\{\beta\}
, and the geometry-dependent sums are

\displaystyle A_{1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}\frac{(\alpha^{d}_{k})^{2}}{r^{4}_{k}},\quad A_{2}=\sum_{k}\frac{|\beta(1-\alpha^{d}_{k})+z^{d}|^{2}}{r^{2}_{k}},

\displaystyle A_{3}

\displaystyle=(\beta_{R}\mathrm{Im}\{z^{d}\}-\beta_{I}\mathrm{Re}\{z^{d}\})\sum_{k}\frac{\alpha^{d}_{k}}{r^{3}_{k}},

\displaystyle B_{1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}\frac{(\alpha^{\theta}_{k})^{2}}{r^{4}_{k}},\quad B_{2}=\sum_{k}\frac{|\beta\alpha^{\theta}_{k}+z^{\theta}|^{2}}{r^{2}_{k}},

\displaystyle B_{3}

\displaystyle=(\beta_{R}\mathrm{Im}\{z^{\theta}\}-\beta_{I}\mathrm{Re}\{z^{\theta}\})\sum_{k}\frac{\alpha^{\theta}_{k}}{r^{3}_{k}},C_{1}=\sum_{k}\frac{\alpha^{d}_{k}\alpha^{\theta}_{k}}{r^{4}_{k}},

\displaystyle C_{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}\frac{\mathrm{Re}\{((z^{d})^{*}+(1-\alpha^{d}_{k})\beta^{*})(z^{\theta}+\alpha^{\theta}_{k}\beta)\}}{r^{2}_{k}},

\displaystyle C_{3}

\displaystyle=(\beta_{R}\mathrm{Im}\{z^{\theta}\}-\beta_{I}\mathrm{Re}\{z^{\theta}\})\sum_{k}\frac{\alpha^{d}_{k}}{r^{3}_{k}}

\displaystyle\quad+(\beta_{I}\mathrm{Re}\{z^{d}\}-\beta_{R}\mathrm{Im}\{z^{d}\})\sum_{k}\frac{\alpha^{\theta}_{k}}{r^{3}_{k}}.

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(\hat{d})\geq\frac{|\beta|^{2}B_{1}+\frac{4\pi^{2}}{\lambda^{2}}B_{2}+\frac{4\pi}{\lambda}B_{3}}{\frac{NMP_{t}\lambda^{2}}{8\pi^{2}\sigma^{2}N_{\rm a}}\left[\left(|\beta|^{2}A_{1}+\frac{4\pi^{2}}{\lambda^{2}}A_{2}+\frac{4\pi}{\lambda}A_{3}\right)\left(|\beta|^{2}B_{1}+\frac{4\pi^{2}}{\lambda^{2}}B_{2}+\frac{4\pi}{\lambda}B_{3}\right)-\left(|\beta|^{2}C_{1}-\frac{4\pi^{2}}{\lambda^{2}}C_{2}-\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}C_{3}\right)^{2}\right]}

(18)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(\hat{\theta})\geq\frac{|\beta|^{2}A_{1}+\frac{4\pi^{2}}{\lambda^{2}}A_{2}+\frac{4\pi}{\lambda}A_{3}}{\frac{NMP_{t}\lambda^{2}}{8\pi^{2}\sigma^{2}N_{\rm a}}\left[\left(|\beta|^{2}A_{1}+\frac{4\pi^{2}}{\lambda^{2}}A_{2}+\frac{4\pi}{\lambda}A_{3}\right)\left(|\beta|^{2}B_{1}+\frac{4\pi^{2}}{\lambda^{2}}B_{2}+\frac{4\pi}{\lambda}B_{3}\right)-\left(|\beta|^{2}C_{1}-\frac{4\pi^{2}}{\lambda^{2}}C_{2}-\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}C_{3}\right)^{2}\right]}

(19)

III-B

Maximum Likelihood Estimation

The ML estimator uses the optimal beamformer
\hat{{\bf f}}
derived in Section
III-A
. Stacking all
MN
received samples into
\tilde{{\bf r}}\in\mbox{\bb C}^{MNN_{\rm a}\times 1}
and
\bar{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(\boldsymbol{\eta})\in\mbox{\bb C}^{MNN_{\rm a}\times 1}
, the signal model becomes
\tilde{{\bf r}}=\bar{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(\boldsymbol{\eta})+{\bf n}
where
{\bf n}\sim\mathcal{CN}(0,\sigma^{2}{\bf I})
. Maximizing the log-likelihood is equivalent to minimizing
\|\tilde{{\bf r}}-\bar{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(\boldsymbol{\eta})\|_{2}^{2}
, yielding the cost function

\displaystyle L(d,\theta)=\bar{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{\sf H}\bar{\boldsymbol{\mu}}-2\,\mathrm{Re}\{\tilde{{\bf r}}^{\sf H}\bar{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\}.

(20)

Setting
\partial L(d,\theta)/\partial d=\partial L(d,\theta)/\partial\theta=0
jointly leads to

\begin{cases}\mathrm{Re}\left\{(\tilde{{\bf r}}-\bar{\boldsymbol{\mu}})^{\sf H}\tfrac{\partial\bar{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}{\partial d}\right\}=0\\[4.0pt]
\mathrm{Re}\left\{(\tilde{{\bf r}}-\bar{\boldsymbol{\mu}})^{\sf H}\tfrac{\partial\bar{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}{\partial\theta}\right\}=0\end{cases}.

(21)

To solve the ML conditions (
21
), the Levenberg–Marquardt algorithm is employed. This requires the Jacobian of the residual, which we obtain by substituting the derivatives from (
III-A
) into (

). Defining the matched filter output at the

\xi_{k}=\frac{\lambda}{4\pi r_{k}\sqrt{N_{a}}}e^{-j\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}(d-r_{k})}\sum_{m,n}C_{m,n}^{*}[\tilde{{\bf r}}_{m,n}]_{k}
, the
\tilde{{\bf r}}
-dependent components can be written as

\displaystyle\tilde{{\bf r}}^{\sf H}\frac{\partial\bar{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}{\partial d}=\sum_{k}(\gamma_{k}^{d})^{\ast}\xi_{k},\qquad\tilde{{\bf r}}^{\sf H}\frac{\partial\bar{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}{\partial\theta}=\sum_{k}(\gamma_{k}^{\theta})^{\ast}\xi_{k}.

On the other side, the
\boldsymbol{\mu}
-dependent components, which are purely deterministic, can be written as

\displaystyle\bar{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{\sf H}\frac{\partial\bar{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}{\partial d}=\frac{NMP_{t}\lambda^{2}}{16\pi^{2}N_{\rm a}}\beta^{\ast}\sum_{k}\frac{(\gamma_{k}^{d})^{\ast}}{r_{k}^{2}}

\displaystyle\bar{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{\sf H}\frac{\partial\bar{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}{\partial\theta}=\frac{NMP_{t}\lambda^{2}}{16\pi^{2}N_{\rm a}}\beta^{\ast}\sum_{k}\frac{(\gamma_{k}^{\theta})^{\ast}}{r_{k}^{2}}.

From the equations above, the weighted residual can be defined as
\rho_{k}=\xi_{k}-\frac{NMP_{t}\lambda^{2}}{16\pi^{2}N_{\rm a}}\frac{\beta^{\ast}}{r_{k}^{2}}
, resulting in

\displaystyle F_{d}(d,\theta)=\mathrm{Re}\left\{\sum_{k=0}^{N_{\rm a}-1}(\gamma_{k}^{d})^{\ast}\rho_{k}\right\}

(22)

\displaystyle F_{\theta}(d,\theta)=\mathrm{Re}\left\{\sum_{k=0}^{N_{\rm a}-1}(\gamma_{k}^{\theta})^{\ast}\rho_{k}\right\}

(23)

Finally, the two equations can be expressed analytically as in
(
24
)–(
25
). The gradients
F_{d}
and
F_{\theta}
in (
22
) –(
23
) serve directly as the Levenberg–Marquardt Jacobian, enabling superlinear convergence. However, since

is highly non-convex – exhibiting multiple local minima in angle – pure gradient descent tends to converge to the nearest local minimum rather than the global one. We therefore initialize with a coarse 2D grid search over

V
Conclusions

This paper presented a continuous-time wideband NF-ISAC framework for a UCA-equipped BS, addressing limitations of prior work that relied on narrowband models or ULA geometries. We derived the CRLB for joint range-angle estimation in closed form, designed an optimal transmit beamformer via Riemannian gradient descent, and formulated an ML estimator solved by Levenberg–Marquardt initialized with a 2D grid search. Monte Carlo results show that all configurations operate in the threshold regime throughout the evaluated range, with the RMSE exceeding the CRLB by large factors even at the shortest distances. The convergence rate drops from above
89\%
at
d=10
m to approximately
50\%
beyond
50
m. Among the evaluated radii,
R=0.5
m achieves the best estimation and communication performance by maintaining the highest received SNR, while larger radii provide a tighter theoretical bound that cannot be exploited in practice, revealing a fundamental trade-off between aperture size and robustness to the threshold effect.
