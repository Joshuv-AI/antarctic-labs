Exploration of extracellular vesicles from porcine endometrial cells for enhancing the development of parthenogenetic embryos
Authors: Shao-Yu Peng, Yen Feng, Min-Jung Lin, Shen-Chang Chang, Jung-Wen Huang, Perng-Chih Shen, Kuo-Tai Yang, Chao-Wei Huang, Chih-Jen Chou, Steven W. Shaw
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Porcine endometrial mesenchymal stem cells (PEMSCs) secrete extracellular vesicles (EVs) containing bioactive microRNAs (miRNAs).•PEMSC-conditioned medium promotes porcine oocyte maturation during in vitro maturation (IVM).•miR-21-5p supplementation during IVM significantly increases blastocyst formation rate in parthenogenetic porcine embryos.•miR-21-5p acts via downregulation of CDK2AP1, thereby promoting Oct4 (POU5F1) expression in blastocysts.•miR-143-3p increases the eight-cell cleavage rate, suggesting a role in early embryonic cell division.

Introduction

In recent years, assisted reproductive technologies (ARTs) have been widely utilized, such as in vitro production (IVP) and vitrification, to produce and preserve embryos. These artificial technologies have been used to treat human reproductive disorders and breed commercial livestock. However, the maturity rate of in vitro maturation (IVM) and the development rate of in vitro culture (IVC) through the IVP of porcine oocytes, such as in parthenogenetic activation and in vitro fertilization (IVF), still exhibit lower efficiency compared to those achieved under in vivo conditions. The pig is used as a model in this study because of its physiological similarity to humans and its direct relevance to livestock production.

Many studies have demonstrated that co-culturing oocytes, cumulus cells, and embryos can alleviate the embryo blocking period during in vitro embryo culture, promote embryo growth to the morula and blastocyst stages, increase the expression of genes related to oocyte maturation and embryo development, reduce reactive oxygen species levels, and improve the development rate and viability after embryo cryopreservation and thawing (Godke et al., 2014; Lee et al., 2018; Lopera-Vasquez et al., 2017; Schmaltz-Panneau et al., 2015). These effects are largely dependent on various molecules secreted by these cells.

Cells release extracellular vesicles (EVs) into the extracellular space through paracrine secretion, acting as carriers to transport various molecules. EVs can be classified as exosomes, microvesicles, and apoptotic bodies based on size, and they carry specific molecules, including proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids (mRNA, miRNA, DNA), among others. Receptor cells absorb EVs through mechanisms such as direct fusion, endocytosis, and pinocytosis, thereby affecting cellular and tissue mechanisms (Tannetta et al., 2014).

In recent years, EVs have been extensively researched for their involvement in various reproductive processes, including follicle development, embryo development, and implantation. EVs may play an important role in cellular communication between the endometrium and blastocysts, which is related to increasing blastocyst adhesion (Cuman et al., 2015; Es-Haghi et al., 2019; Homer et al., 2017). In porcine reproduction specifically, EVs in uterine fluid have been shown to regulate conceptus elongation and maternal-embryonic communication. Utilizing EVs to improve the development and quality of in vitro embryos is significant for assisted reproductive technologies and regenerative medicine research.

Nowadays, comprehensive research often uses pregnant sow uterine fluid, which contains EVs secreted by the endometrium, stromal cells, and embryos. The impact of miRNA in porcine endometrial mesenchymal stem cell (PEMSC)-derived EVs on porcine blastocysts needs further investigation. Although EVs produced by luminal and glandular epithelial cells represent the most physiologically direct source for uterine-embryo communication, PEMSCs provide an accessible and abundant in vitro source of MSC-derived EVs that can be characterized and applied in experimental settings. Therefore, the objectives of this study are as follows: Experiment 1 aims to establish a PEMSC culture system; Experiment 2 aims to isolate and identify EVs secreted by PEMSCs and analyze their miRNA content; Experiment 3 aims to select miRNAs that promote porcine embryo development and add them to the in vitro medium to observe maturation and development; Experiment 4 aims to examine changes in gene expression in IVC blastocysts after the addition of miRNAs.

Materials and methods

Animals

Porcine ovaries and uteri were obtained from a local abattoir (Pingtung County, Linluo Township, Taiwan). Animals were of mixed reproductive status, and the specific stage of the estrous cycle was not recorded, as tissues were collected from routine commercial slaughter. This is acknowledged as a limitation of the study.

Establishment of PEMSCs culture

Preparation of Culture Medium: Minimum essential medium (MEM; Sigma, M-0894, USA) was dissolved in ultrapure water with 2.2 g NaHCO3, adjusted to pH 7.22, filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane, and supplemented with 1% penicillin-streptomycin (P/S) and 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). Medium was stored at 4°C.

Establishment of Primary PEMSCs: Porcine uterine tissue was transported to the laboratory within 30 minutes in 0.9% saline with 0.1 mg/mL P/S at 37–39°C. Under sterile conditions, approximately 0.5 cm² of endometrial tissue was excised and placed into MEM with 10% FBS. Tissue was cut into ∼0.1 cm² pieces and cultured in 10 cm dishes using an explant outgrowth method, without enzymatic digestion. Cells migrating from the explants and adhering to the dish surface were identified as PEMSCs based on their fibroblast-like morphology. Medium was replaced every three days. This explant method selectively favors outgrowth of stromal mesenchymal cells over epithelial cells. Cells were subcultured until the 5th generation.

Characterization of PEMSCs: PEMSC identity was confirmed by: (1) fibroblast-like spindle-shaped morphology; (2) successful adipogenic differentiation confirmed by Oil Red O staining; (3) successful osteogenic differentiation confirmed by Alizarin Red S staining; and (4) immunophenotyping showing expression of MSC-positive markers (CD44, CD90) and absence of hematopoietic markers (CD34, CD45).

Subculture, Cryopreservation, and Thawing: At 80% confluence, PEMSCs were passaged using 0.25% trypsin-EDTA, centrifuged at 270 × g for 5 min, and replated. For cryopreservation, cells at 90% confluence were resuspended in DMSO:FBS (1:9) and stored in liquid nitrogen. Thawed cells were plated directly and medium was changed within 24 h to remove cryoprotectant.

Collection of PEMSC-conditioned medium (CM)

Fifth-generation PEMSCs grown to 80–90% confluence were washed three times with DPBS. After washing three times with DPBS, the cells were incubated in 3 mL of IVM (TCM-199) or IVC (PZM) medium for 3 hours at 38.5°C with 5% CO2 to prepare the conditioned medium. The medium was then filtered through a 0.22 μm filter and stored at 4°C for use within one week.

EV collection and analysis

Fifth-generation PEMSCs at 80–90% confluence were washed three times with DPBS, and 3 mL of EV extraction medium was added and incubated for 3 hours. EVs were isolated using the miRCURY Exosome Kit (Qiagen, 76743, Germany) per manufacturer's instructions, then filtered (0.22 μm) and centrifuged at 3,200 × g for 30 minutes. The EV pellet was resuspended in 100 μL preservative buffer and stored at −20°C. Nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) was performed using a Malvern Panalytical NanoSight LM10HS instrument (UK) at the Micro and Nano Technology Research Center, National Cheng Kung University, to determine EV size distribution and concentration.

Western blotting analysis

Proteins (40 μg/lane) were separated by SDS-PAGE using TGX Stain-Free™ FastCast™ Acrylamide (Bio-Rad, 161-0185, USA) at 200 V for 40–60 min, then transferred to PVDF membrane at 0.2 A for 2.5 h. Membranes were blocked and incubated with primary antibodies: β-Actin (1:5,000; Thermo Fisher, MA5-11869), Cyt c (1:5,000; Proteintech, 10993-1-AP), and CD9 (1:1,000; Sigma, SAB4503606). HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies were applied, and bands were visualized using Immobilon Crescendo HRP substrate. Band density was quantified using Bio-imaging camera system analysis software and normalized to β-Actin as the endogenous loading control.

miRNA analysis (NGS)

Total miRNA was extracted from EVs using the Vazyme NR801 kit and submitted to Topgen Biotechnology Co., Ltd. for next-generation sequencing (NGS). The 12 most highly expressed miRNAs were used for Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analyses.

In vitro maturation (IVM) of porcine oocytes

IVM medium was prepared by supplementing TCM-199 (GibcoTM, 12340-030, USA) with 10% FBS, 10 μL FSH, 20 μL hCG, 1% antibiotic-antimycotic (ABAM), and 10% porcine follicular fluid (PFF). Note: PFF was included in all groups equally; although PFF may contain endogenous EVs, its consistent use across all experimental and control groups means that any EV contribution from this source would not confound group comparisons. Cumulus-oocyte complexes (COCs) were aspirated from follicles, washed with modified PBS, and cultured in TCM-199 under mineral oil at 38.5°C, 5% CO2 for 44 hours. Maturity was confirmed by first polar body extrusion.

Parthenogenetic activation and in vitro culture (IVC)

Mature oocytes were activated with 5 μM A23187 for 5 min in darkness at 38.5°C, followed by 2 mM 6-DMAP for 4 h. Oocytes were washed three times with Porcine Zygote Medium (PZM) and cultured in PZM (PZM: Porcine Zygote Medium) under mineral oil for 7–8 days at 38.5°C, 5% CO2. Fresh PZM was replaced every two days. Embryonic stage, blastocyst rate, and cell count were recorded.

Synthetic miRNA transfection

Synthetic miRNAs (DSMIR02, TopGen, Taiwan) were prepared at 20 μM in ddH2O. Before use, 1 μL miRNA was mixed with 3 μL HiPerFect® Transfection Reagent (QIAGEN, 301707, Germany) for 5–10 min at room temperature, then diluted in TCM-199 to a final concentration of 200 nM. miRNAs (ssc-miR-21-5p, ssc-miR-143-3p, ssc-miR-381-3p) were added at 50 nM to IVM or IVC medium as per experimental design. RT-qPCR analysis

Oocytes and day-8 blastocysts were washed three times with DPBS and stored at −80°C in QIAzol® lysis reagent. RNA was extracted using RNA Isolater Total RNA Extraction Reagent (Vazyme, China), reverse-transcribed with HiScript III RT SuperMix (37°C, 15 min; 85°C, 5 sec), and amplified by SYBR Green qPCR (ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix, Vazyme) on a StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR system (Applied Biosystems). Data were normalized to GAPDH using the 2−ΔΔCT method. Coefficient of variation was maintained below 2%.

As shown in Table 4, supplementation with PEMSC-conditioned medium during IVM significantly increased oocyte maturation rates compared to standard TCM-199 (p < 0.05). Supplementation with PEMSC-conditioned medium during the IVC stage significantly influenced the proportion of embryos reaching the 4-cell stage (p < 0.05). Discussion

In Experiment 1, PEMSC-conditioned medium enhanced oocyte maturation (polar body extrusion) but did not improve blastocyst rates. The observed variability in oocyte cytoplasmic quality may be attributed to differences in pig breed, age, rearing environment, and feed composition, as ovaries were obtained from a commercial abattoir. Commercial pigs are slaughtered at approximately six months of age, when uterine and ovarian development may still be incomplete, potentially limiting the impact of endometrial secretions on embryo development.

Regarding the physiological route by which stromal-derived EVs might access the uterine lumen in vivo: stromal-epithelial EV exchange is documented in the endometrium, and mesenchymal-derived EVs may act indirectly by modulating luminal or glandular epithelial cells, which in turn secrete EVs into the uterine lumen. In our experimental design, PEMSCs serve as an accessible and well-characterized in vitro source of MSC-derived EVs for supplementation, not as a simulation of the in vivo physiological route. Future work should evaluate EVs derived from luminal and glandular epithelial cells as more physiologically direct candidates for uterine-embryo communication.

Studies on polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) have shown that high levels of miR-143-3p in follicular fluid lead to abnormal glycolysis in granulosa cells (Cao et al., 2022). Despite this negative follicular effect, upregulation of miR-143-3p promotes proliferation and migration of porcine trophoblast cells (Ding et al., 2022). The significant increase in eight-cell cleavage rate with miR-143-3p addition in our study warrants further investigation into its stage-specific regulatory role in early porcine embryogenesis.

The role of miR-381-3p in embryo development remains unexplored in pigs. In human IVC media associated with successful implantation, miR-381-3p has been linked to cell adhesion and stem cell pluripotency pathways (Timofeeva et al., 2021). The negative effect observed in T3 (IVM+IVC) suggests that dual-stage supplementation may disrupt developmental signaling, but further investigation is required.

In Experiment 4, the CDK2AP1–miR-21-5p–Oct4 regulatory axis was confirmed in porcine blastocysts. These findings are consistent with reports in mouse embryonic stem cells where CDK2AP1 silences Oct4 and miR-21 relieves this suppression (Alsayegh et al., 2018; Mihalič et al., 2023). Species-specific differences in this regulatory axis — particularly between mouse, human, and pig — should be considered when interpreting these results and designing future translational studies.

A key limitation of this study is the use of slaughterhouse-derived tissues from animals at unknown stages of the estrous cycle. Endometrial cell composition and secretory activity are known to vary with cycle stage, and future studies should use tissues collected at defined cycle stages to better characterize PEMSC-derived EVs. Additionally, the presence of endogenous EVs in PFF used in IVM medium represents a confounding factor that should be controlled for in future work, for instance by using EV-depleted PFF.

Conclusion

The conditioned medium of porcine endometrial mesenchymal stem cells (PEMSCs) promotes porcine oocyte maturation in vitro. Specifically, miR-21-5p effectively enhances Oct4 expression, thereby improving the blastocyst formation rate, through suppression of CDK2AP1. miR-143-3p supplementation increases the eight-cell cleavage rate, suggesting a regulatory role in early embryonic cell division. The miRNA cargo of PEMSC-derived EVs holds promise for improving in vitro production of porcine embryos, with future translational applications in human infertility treatment, livestock production, and conservation of endangered species.
