Systematic validation of graph neural network explanations against adverse outcome pathway reactive centers for skin sensitization
Authors: ByoungJun Jeon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWhether graph neural network (GNN) attributions capture the reactive chemistry encoded in adverse outcome pathway (AOP) annotations for skin sensitization has not been tested under a label-permutation control. We trained AttentiveFP on a 436-molecule LLNA-labeled subset (81 sensitizers) of a curated skin-sensitization dataset and extracted atom-level attributions from seven methods (integrated gradients, GradCAM, attention, GNNExplainer, PGExplainer, GraphMask, and a rank-aggregated ensemble); GCN and GIN were retrained as architecture controls. Attributions were evaluated against 50 sensitization-specific SMARTS-based reactive-center annotations spanning six molecular initiating event (MIE) mechanism classes. The LLNA-trained model reached test AUC 0.73, compared with AUC 0.49 for a shuffled-label retraining control. Across all seven attribution methods, paired real-vs-shuffled atom-AUC differences crossed zero, indicating that most apparent attribution–mechanism correlation reflects structural priors of the attribution methods rather than learned sensitization chemistry. Mechanism-class stratification surfaced one candidate exception—pre-hapten autoxidation chemistry, with a Δ = +0.26Δ=+0.26 atom-AUC gap and non-overlapping confidence intervals—on a stratum of approximately two unique molecules, reported as a hypothesis pending replication on dedicated pre-hapten benchmarks. Per-element residual analysis converges on the same interpretation: alignment for gradient- and attention-based methods drops to chance once elemental identity is regressed out.Scientific contributionThis work pairs a reproducible evaluation framework (50 skin-sensitization-specific SMARTS patterns scored against atom-level GNN attributions) with a label-permutation negative control that separates learned chemistry from structural priors of the attribution method.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13321-026-01249-8.

Introduction

Skin sensitization is one of the most common adverse effects associated with chemical exposure, affecting both consumer safety and occupational health [1]. Regulatory frameworks, including the European Union’s ban on animal testing for cosmetics (Regulation EC No 1223/2009), have increased demand for reliable computational alternatives (new approach methodologies, NAMs) to replace traditional animal tests such as the murine local lymph node assay (LLNA) [2, 3].

The adverse outcome pathway (AOP) framework provides a structured, mechanistic basis for understanding skin sensitization [4]. AOP-40, the canonical pathway for skin sensitization, describes a sequence of key events (KEs) from the molecular initiating event (MIE), the covalent binding of an electrophilic chemical to nucleophilic amino acid residues in skin proteins, through dendritic cell activation (KE1), T-cell proliferation (KE2), and ultimately clinical allergic contact dermatitis [5]. The MIE is well-characterized: electrophilic sensitizers react with proteins through specific mechanisms including Michael addition, SN2 alkylation, acyl transfer, Schiff base formation, and SNAr substitution [6, 7]. Pro-haptens and pre-haptens require metabolic or oxidative activation, respectively, to form reactive intermediates [8].

Graph neural networks (GNNs) operate directly on molecular graphs rather than requiring hand-crafted descriptors [9, 10]. AttentiveFP [11] combines graph attention with gated recurrent pooling, providing native attention weights for interpretation. GNN-based models have shown competitive performance against descriptor-based approaches across molecular property prediction benchmarks [11, 12]. Fragment-level attention has been applied to toxicity prediction [13], though validation against mechanistic ground truth remains unexplored.

Regulatory frameworks increasingly require mechanistic interpretability of computational models [14, 15]. Attribution methods for GNNs (integrated gradients [16], GradCAM [17], attention weights [18], GNNExplainer [19], PGExplainer [20], GraphMask [21]) produce atom-level importance scores that can be compared to known chemistry. XAI methods applied to SMILES-based models can produce inconsistent explanations [22], motivating graph-based approaches. Computational QSAR approaches have also been developed for AOP-related molecular targets [23], though explanation quality was not the focus of those studies.

Whether GNN attributions align with AOP mechanisms for skin sensitization has not been tested quantitatively. Prior studies applied GNN attributions to molecular property prediction [20, 24], and recent work has introduced substructure-aware training losses [25], quantitative evaluation frameworks [26], and substructure masking [27] for molecular GNN explanations. However, evaluation against AOP-specific mechanistic ground truth has not yet been reported. Reviews have called for such validation [22, 28].

We evaluate whether a GNN trained on binary sensitization outcomes attends to the atoms involved in the MIE (Fig. 1). We compare seven attribution methods against 50 SMARTS-based reactive center annotations across six mechanism classes, investigate alignment differences across reaction mechanisms, and test whether explicit mechanistic supervision improves attribution quality. Methods

Dataset

A dataset of 4,223 compounds was compiled from public sources (JRC, SkinSensDB, NICEATM, Cosmetics Europe, ICE database). After deduplication and quality control (removal of 12 structural duplicates and non-numeric assay responses), 1,247 compounds had binary human sensitization labels derived from clinical patch test data (human maximization test, HMT, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n = 1{,}071$$\end{document}n=1,071; human repeat insult patch test, HRIPT, n = 318n=318), with a compound classified as a sensitizer if either assay was positive: 158 sensitizers (12.7%) and 1,089 non-sensitizers (87.3%). 436 of these compounds carried a concordant LLNA EC3 value (81 sensitizers, 355 non-sensitizers), from which we derived a binary LLNA label by the standard threshold of EC3 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$< 100\,\mu$$\end{document}<100μg/cm\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^2$$\end{document}2 [3]. Because human patch-test data does not always discriminate sensitizers from irritants, whereas the LLNA EC3 endpoint is a direct measure of murine immune response, the primary analyses use the 436-molecule LLNA subset as the supervision and evaluation target.

Molecules were represented as molecular graphs using RDKit [29] with standard atom and bond features (Table S1).

Data splitting

We used Bemis–Murcko scaffold-based splitting [30] with a 70/15/15 train/validation/test ratio. Generic Murcko scaffolds were extracted, molecules grouped by scaffold, and groups assigned sequentially (largest-first) to splits. The scaffold assignment is deterministic given the seed. All experiments were repeated across 20 random seeds (Table S2) to quantify variability.

GNN architecture and training

We used AttentiveFP [11] as implemented in PyTorch Geometric [31]. The model used 3 graph attention layers with 256-dimensional hidden representations. Training used binary cross-entropy loss with positive class weighting to address class imbalance (87.3% non-sensitizers), AdamW optimizer with early stopping on validation AUC. Full architectural and training hyperparameters are provided in Table S1.

Fingerprint baseline

As a structural baseline, we trained Random Forest classifiers on Morgan circular fingerprints [32]. To generate atom-level importance scores comparable to GNN attributions, we tracked which atoms contributed to each fingerprint bit using RDKit’s bit information, then distributed each bit’s Gini importance back to its constituent atoms. Both the GNN and fingerprint models thus produce per-atom importance scores evaluated against the same mechanistic reference. Fingerprint and Random Forest parameters are in Table S1.

Attribution methods

We extracted atom-level importance scores using six attribution methods (spanning gradient-based, attention-based, and perturbation-based approaches) plus a rank-aggregated ensemble, for seven methods total.

Integrated gradients (IG) [16] computed path-integrated gradients from a zero baseline (all atom feature values set to zero, representing an uninformative reference state) to the actual molecular input over 50 integration steps, targeting the sensitization logit. Atom-level importance was obtained by summing absolute gradient values across feature dimensions (L1 norm) for each atom. GradCAM [17] produced per-atom importance scores from gradient-weighted activation maps at the final GNN layer. For attention weights, we extracted the native attention coefficients from AttentiveFP’s graph attention layers and summed across all heads and layers.

Among perturbation-based methods, GNNExplainer [19] optimized a soft edge mask over 200 steps (learning rate = 0.01) to identify the minimal subgraph preserving the model’s prediction, with edge-level importance aggregated to atoms. PGExplainer [20] trained an MLP to predict edge importance on 50 test set graphs per seed, then applied the learned model to all molecules; edge scores were summed to atoms. GraphMask [21] optimized per-instance binary edge gates to identify minimal predictive subgraphs, with gate values aggregated to atoms via summation.

Finally, a rank-aggregated ensemble averaged per-atom rank vectors across all six methods. Rank aggregation was chosen because it is distribution-free, handles different score scales across methods without normalization, and avoids dominance by high-variance methods.

AOP mechanistic reference labels

We constructed atom-level reference annotations based on the molecular initiating event chemistry of AOP-40 [5]. Because the Enoch et al. 2011 set [6] covers protein-binding chemistry across multiple toxicity endpoints (including aquatic toxicity and liver effects) rather than the skin-sensitization MIE specifically, we use 50 SMARTS patterns drawn from skin-sensitization-specific sources: Enoch, Madden and Cronin 2008 [33], Patlewicz et al. 2007 (TIMES-SS) [34], and Roberts and Aptula 2008 [35]. Three pattern categories are excluded because they are not mechanistically linked to the skin-sensitization MIE: generic metal chelators, hydroperoxides and peroxides, and nitroso/azo radical generators. The 50-pattern set spans six mechanism classes: Michael addition, Schiff base formation, acyl transfer, SN2 alkylation, SNAr substitution, and pro-hapten activation. Pro-hapten is further split into pre-hapten and pro-hapten enzymatic for stratified analyses. The full provenance of the 50 patterns is provided in Section S1; a 135-pattern superset that additionally includes non-skin-sensitization protein-binding alerts (metal chelators, peroxides, nitroso/azo radical generators) is documented alongside in §3.5–§3.7 and §3.10–§3.11.

For each molecule, we generated a binary atom-level mask indicating reactive center membership. A molecule was annotated if at least one SMARTS pattern matched; the mask value was 1 for atoms within a matched reactive center and 0 otherwise. We also recorded the primary mechanism class for mechanism-stratified analyses.

Two granularities of reference labels were evaluated: reactive center masks (only the atoms directly involved in the electrophilic reaction) and full substructure masks (the entire matched SMARTS substructure including adjacent atoms). Results are reported primarily for reactive center masks unless otherwise noted. In addition to the union annotation mode, we also evaluated an intersection annotation mode in which an atom is flagged only when it is matched by at least two distinct SMARTS patterns; for the reactive-center reference this reduces to the union mode by construction, but for the full-substructure reference the two modes are compared in Section S4.

These patterns are not specific to sensitizers (53.9% of non-sensitizers in the LLNA test set match at least one pattern vs. 15.1% of sensitizers), and should be understood as reactive substructure annotations rather than sensitization-specific MIE labels. Alignment is reported under two denominators. The TP-with-match subset (true positive sensitizers with at least one SMARTS match) is the narrowest, prediction-conditioned denominator; under the sensitization-specific alert set combined with the LLNA test subset this collapses to approximately one molecule per seed. Under the 135-pattern set, this subset comprises 25 molecules spanning 19 unique Bemis–Murcko scaffolds, MW 130–713 Da (mean 339), and seven 135-pattern mechanism categories (12 pro-haptens, 5 Michael addition, 2 SN2, 2 ring opening, 2 Schiff base, 1 radical, 1 acyl transfer; Table S3); per-molecule diversity tables are in Additional file 2. The all-molecule denominator includes every test molecule that carries a reactive-center mask, irrespective of the model’s prediction on that molecule (pooled N ≈ 620N≈620), and is used as the primary alignment metric to avoid prediction-conditioning bias.

Alignment metrics

Attribution-to-mechanism alignment was quantified per molecule and averaged across valid molecules (those with at least one annotated MIE atom). We used five complementary metrics. Atom-AUC is the area under the ROC curve treating atom importance scores as predictions and the binary MIE mask as ground truth, where 0.5 indicates chance-level alignment. Atom-AP is the area under the precision–recall curve, which is more sensitive to performance on the minority class (MIE atoms). HitRate@K sets K equal to the number of MIE atoms in the molecule and measures the fraction of top-K important atoms that are MIE atoms, providing an adaptive threshold that reflects varying reactive center sizes. Precision@K with fixed K = 3K=3 and K = 5K=5 measures the fraction of top-K atoms that are MIE atoms, providing a fixed-threshold view of attribution quality. IoU@K computes the intersection-over-union between the top-K important atoms and the MIE atom set (where K equals the number of MIE atoms), penalizing both false positives and false negatives (Fig. 2).Fig. 2Case study molecules. Statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted across the seed set defined in §2.5.

Effective sample size depends on the denominator. For §3.5–§3.7 and §3.10–§3.11, the same 25 valid molecules are evaluated across every seed; paired tests across seeds therefore assess model-initialization stability rather than molecule-level generalization. For the LLNA-primary all-molecule denominator (Tables 2, 3, 8), the per-seed denominator is approximately 31 molecules, pooling to N ≈ 620N≈620; bootstrap CIs treat repeated evaluations of the same molecule as independent observations, modestly overstating precision on strata with few unique molecules.

Whether atom-AUC exceeded 0.5 (chance level) was assessed using one-sample t-tests across 20 seeds, with Bonferroni correction across 7 tests (six methods plus ensemble). The parametric test was chosen given adequate sample size (n = 20n=20) for the central limit theorem to apply. Four control baselines isolated different sources of alignment: (1) random uniform attribution (100 repeats per seed); (2) untrained GNN attention from a randomly initialized AttentiveFP with identical architecture; (3) a heteroatom heuristic (importance = 1 for non-carbon atoms, 0 for carbon); and (4) atom degree. Paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests compared trained attention against each baseline across seeds, with Bonferroni correction across 4 comparisons.

Paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests also compared atom-AUC distributions between GNN attributions and the fingerprint baseline across seeds (Bonferroni-corrected across 7 tests), and compared atom-AUC between direct electrophiles and pro-haptens for each attribution method. Cohen’s d was computed for one-sample and paired comparisons. For Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, the rank-biserial correlation \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$r_{rb} = 1 - 2W^{-}/(n(n+1)/2)$$\end{document}rrb=1-2W-/(n(n+1)/2) was reported, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$W^{-}$$\end{document}W- is the smaller of the positive and negative rank sums. Bootstrap 95% confidence intervals (10,000 resamples) were computed for all reported metrics.

Residual analysis

For each valid molecule and each attribution method, atoms were classified as heteroatoms (non-carbon, non-hydrogen) or carbon. The group mean importance was subtracted from each atom’s score, yielding residual scores with zero mean within each group. Atom-AUC was recomputed using residual scores. As a more stringent control, we repeated this analysis using per-element grouping (separate means for C, N, O, S, F, Cl, Br, etc.) instead of binary heteroatom/carbon grouping. Wilcoxon signed-rank tests assessed whether residual atom-AUC exceeded 0.5 under each correction.

Size-normalized pro-hapten comparison

Because atom-AUC is sensitive to the fraction of positive labels per molecule, differences in reactive center annotation size between pro-haptens and direct electrophiles can bias comparisons. To address this, we compared the two categories using Precision@K with fixed K values (K = 3, 5K=3,5), which evaluate only the top K atoms regardless of the total number of annotated atoms. Wilcoxon signed-rank tests compared pro-hapten and direct electrophile P@K across seeds for each attribution method.

Multi-architecture comparison

To assess whether alignment patterns are architecture-specific, we repeated the analysis with two additional GNN architectures: Graph Convolutional Network (GCN) [37] and Graph Isomorphism Network (GIN) [38]. Both used the same atom features, training pipeline, and hyperparameters as AttentiveFP for fair comparison. Because GCN and GIN lack native attention mechanisms, we restricted explanation extraction to model-agnostic methods: IG, GNNExplainer, and GraphMask, plus a rank-aggregated ensemble of these three. Each model-agnostic method’s alignment was compared between AttentiveFP and each alternative architecture using paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, with Bonferroni correction across 6 comparisons (3 methods ×× 2 architecture pairs).

We also applied split-conformal prediction [39, 40] to assess uncertainty calibration and its relationship to attribution quality (Table S7, Fig. S1).

Use of large language models

A large language model was used to assist with manuscript drafting and code development. All scientific content, experimental design, data analysis, and interpretation were performed by the author.

Shuffled-label negative control

To separate attribution–mechanism alignment attributable to learned sensitization chemistry from alignment attributable to structural priors of the attribution method, we implemented a shuffled-label negative control. For each seed, the LLNA training labels were deterministically permuted and the AttentiveFP architecture was retrained end-to-end with identical hyperparameters, scaffold split, and early-stopping criterion. All seven attribution methods (integrated gradients, GradCAM, attention, GNNExplainer, PGExplainer, GraphMask, and the rank-aggregated ensemble) were then extracted from each shuffled-label checkpoint and atom-AUC was recomputed against the same sensitization-specific reactive-center masks. The decision criterion was that the primary (real-label) mean atom-AUC must exceed the upper bound of the shuffled-label bootstrap 95% CI by at least +0.05+0.05 for the alignment signal to be considered learned chemistry rather than a structural prior of the attribution method.

Positive-weight robustness sweep

The LLNA-primary AttentiveFP model was retrained at three fixed positive-class weights (pos_weight \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\in \{4, 8, 12\}$$\end{document}∈{4,8,12}), spanning and bracketing the data-derived value (4.45) used in the primary configuration. All other hyperparameters were held constant. Classification AUC and confusion-matrix metrics at both the 0.5 threshold and each model’s balanced-accuracy-optimal threshold are reported in Section S3.

Neutral attribution baselines

In addition to the Morgan-fingerprint Random-Forest baseline, six data-free per-atom baselines were evaluated: uniform random per-atom importance, atom degree, a binary aromatic indicator, a binary heteroatom indicator, per-atom atomic mass, and the molecule’s heavy-atom count assigned uniformly to every atom. Each baseline assigns per-atom importance scores that can be evaluated under the same alignment metrics as GNN attributions, without any trained model.

Results

Prediction performance

Under the primary configuration (LLNA labels, 436 molecules), AttentiveFP achieved test AUC of 0.727 (bootstrap 95% CI [0.698, 0.752]) with balanced accuracy 0.707 at each model’s balanced-accuracy-optimal threshold on the LLNA scaffold-split test partition (66 molecules; Table 1). A shuffled-label negative control, retrained end-to-end with the same architecture and hyperparameters on the same partition, collapsed test AUC to 0.494 (range 0.39–0.66) and balanced accuracy to 0.514 (MCC 0.06), confirming that the LLNA-primary classifier learns genuine skin-sensitization signal rather than memorizing spurious patterns. For context, the original human-label-trained AttentiveFP achieves balanced accuracy 0.628 on its 156-molecule human-label scaffold-split test partition; the two partitions are not the same molecules, but both reflect deterministic scaffold splits at the same train/val/test ratio. Shuffled-label negative control

The alignment results above do not by themselves rule out alignment driven by structural priors of the attribution method (atom degree, heteroatom neighbors, aromaticity) rather than learned sensitization chemistry. We tested this directly by retraining the model on shuffled LLNA labels and recomputing the same alignment metrics. The decision criterion was that the primary mean atom-AUC must exceed the upper bound of the shuffled-label bootstrap 95% CI by at least +0.05+0.05.

Discussion

Alignment with AOP reactive centers

The shuffled-label negative control in Table 3 places every method’s paired real-vs-shuffled ΔΔ within zero of the real-label level under the LLNA-primary configuration on the all-molecule reactive-center reference (largest gap: attention at +0.023+0.023 [-0.018-0.018, +0.063+0.063]; perturbation methods within +0.003+0.003). The shuffled classifier does not learn, so the near-equivalent attribution alignment cannot be attributed to learned sensitization chemistry on any method. The bulk of the attribution–reactive-center correlation in this task therefore reflects a structural prior of the attribution methods themselves: reactive-center atoms have distinctive local graph topology that attention layers up-weight and that gradient and perturbation methods identify independently of the training signal. The residual analyses in Table 5 (binary heteroatom decomposition) and §3.5 with Table S9 (per-element decomposition) reach the same conclusion through elemental-identity controls.

Without a label-permutation control, GNN attention alignment with reactive chemistry overstates the model’s mechanistic learning.

Under the sensitization-specific 50-pattern alert set, neither a fingerprint baseline (Section S5) nor the heteroatom indicator (0.492 on the all-molecule denominator) exceed trained attention. Under the 135-pattern alert set the heteroatom heuristic reaches 0.685 (Table S4) because that set includes nitrogen-rich metal chelators and oxygen-rich peroxides outside the skin-sensitization MIE; the heuristic’s apparent strength is therefore an alert-set artifact rather than an upper bound on what the model can learn.

The mechanism-stratified comparison (Table 8) surfaces one class with apparent learned localization: pre-hapten autoxidation chemistry (hydroquinones and catechols) shows a +0.26+0.26 AUC gap between real-label and shuffled-label attention, with non-overlapping bootstrap CIs. Mechanistically this would be interpreted as the model having learned that phenolic hydroxyl groups adjacent to a second activating substituent are the reactive sites in pre-hapten oxidative activation, beyond what the attention mechanism would highlight from topology alone. Two caveats apply. First, the stratum pools 39 molecule–seed observations across approximately two unique molecules; the bootstrap CI treats these as independent observations, which overstates precision. Second, the related pro-hapten (enzymatic) stratum, which shares the “heteroatom-rich aromatic system” structural profile, shows only a +0.10+0.10 gap. Replication on dedicated pre-hapten benchmarks is needed before treating this as a confirmed result.

Mechanism-dependent alignment

Under the 50-pattern alert set, direct-electrophile atom-AUC equals or exceeds pro-hapten atom-AUC across the seven attribution methods (§3.8, Table S5), with the gap reaching significance for PGExplainer, GraphMask, and the ensemble; the size-normalized Precision@K comparison (Table S6) confirms the same direction. A mechanism-independent caveat applies: pro-hapten SMARTS patterns mark the activation site on the parent compound, but the MIE occurs after metabolic transformation, and the GNN sees the parent structure rather than the reactive metabolite. Per-mechanism sample sizes (Table 7) are too small for definitive conclusions beyond pro-haptens. The low acyl transfer alignment may reflect the distributed nature of acyl transfer reactivity, which resists atom-level attribution.

Under the LLNA-primary stratification (Table 8), the chemistry-stratified miss patterns are: SN2 alkylation and Schiff base formation are easy to localize from graph topology alone (LLNA and shuffled-null attention both 0.75−0.80); the model adds little learned signal, and SN2 even shows a small negative gap. Michael-addition misses are concentrated on extended-conjugation acceptors where the electrophilic ββ-carbon is two bonds removed from the nearest heteroatom, against attention’s heteroatom-neighbor prior. Acyl-transfer attention attends less to the carbonyl in the LLNA-trained model than under the shuffled null (Δ = -0.19Δ=-0.19), but the stratum is two unique molecules under the deterministic scaffold split and we do not interpret this difference. Pre-hapten autoxidation chemistry (hydroquinones, catechols) is the one stratum with a clear learned signal beyond topology (Δ = +0.26Δ=+0.26), pending replication on dedicated pre-hapten benchmarks (§4.7).

Attribution method comparison

Attention weights directly encode message-passing preferences, which may explain their advantage over gradient-based methods (IG, GradCAM), which measure input–output sensitivity. GraphMask showed anti-alignment on the TP-with-match subset (Table 5, raw AUC 0.486), likely because its edge gates optimize to retain minimal predictive subgraphs, favoring backbone C–C bonds over heteroatom-containing reactive centers. The residual analysis (Tables 5, S9) supports this: GraphMask was the only method to survive both binary and per-element corrections, indicating that its edge gates capture structural features independent of element type.

Jain and Wallace [42] showed that attention can be unfaithful. Our masking test (Table 9) confirmed that AttentiveFP’s attention weights are causally faithful (5.9x ratio, p < 0.001p<0.001). Faithfulness and mechanistic correctness are distinct: faithfulness confirms that high-attention atoms drive the prediction, not that they correspond to the correct mechanism.

Multi-architecture comparison

AttentiveFP and GCN alignment were statistically indistinguishable across all three model-agnostic methods (Table 11), indicating the observed alignment is not architecture-specific. GIN’s lower alignment tracked its lower predictive accuracy. Across 60 models, predictive AUC and IG alignment correlated at r = 0.509r=0.509 (p < 0.0001p<0.0001), suggesting that better models produce better explanations.

Explicit MIE supervision

Adding atom-level MIE supervision improved the dedicated MIE head but did not transfer to post-hoc attribution alignment (Table 10), because post-hoc methods trace the sensitization prediction pathway, which the parallel MIE head does not modify. The different λ = 0λ=0 baseline (0.634 vs. 0.612 in Table 5) reflects the different training conditions noted in Results; the within-table comparison across λλ controls for this. The 10-seed design limits power for detecting small effects.

Practical implications

For practitioners using GNN attributions for regulatory submissions: (1) attention weights are the most aligned method for AttentiveFP, but users should verify that highlighted atoms correspond to known reactive substructures rather than treating attributions as mechanistic proof; (2) the heteroatom heuristic’s strong performance suggests that practitioners should report whether their model’s explanations exceed this simple baseline before claiming mechanistic learning; (3) the 50-pattern SMARTS framework can serve as a standardized evaluation benchmark for new attribution methods applied to skin sensitization.

Limitations

The LLNA-labeled subset used as primary supervision is 436 molecules (81 positives), with a scaffold-split test partition of 66 molecules (∼∼12 sensitizers); both the attribution analyses and the LLNA-primary classification metrics in Table 1 are computed on this 66-molecule LLNA test partition (pooled N ≈ 620N≈620 molecule–seed observations for the attribution analyses). The human-label-trained baselines (plain AttentiveFP, GCN, GIN) in the same table are evaluated on the original 156-molecule human-label scaffold-split test partition. Seed-level variation primarily reflects model-initialization stochasticity.

The per-mechanism strata in Table 8 pool molecule–seed observations without collapsing repeated evaluations of the same molecule; bootstrap 95% CIs therefore treat dependent observations as independent, modestly overstating precision on strata with few unique molecules (pre-hapten and acyl-transfer in particular). Replication on dedicated sensitization datasets and on the emerging defined-approach (DA) human-reference datasets would strengthen generalizability. The pre-registered criterion (primary mean atom-AUC must exceed the shuffled-label bootstrap 95% CI upper bound by +0.05+0.05) was missed by -0.053-0.053 on the primary method; raw per-seed metrics are provided in Additional file 2.

Confusion-matrix metrics in Table 1 are reported at each model’s balanced-accuracy-optimal threshold selected on the same test split, which inflates balanced accuracy by approximately 0.02−0.03 relative to a validation-tuned threshold; threshold-==−0.5 numbers are tabulated alongside in Additional file 2.

The SMARTS patterns identify reactive substructures, not sensitization-specific MIE sites: 53.9% of non-sensitizers also match at least one pattern (see Methods). High alignment therefore indicates that the GNN attends to known reactive substructures in correctly classified sensitizers, but does not confirm MIE-specific mechanistic learning. The reference labels serve as a structured, literature-grounded proxy for mechanistic ground truth rather than definitive MIE annotations.

Under the 135-pattern alert set the heteroatom heuristic (0.685) outperforms all trained methods. This does not invalidate the GNN alignment: the heuristic cannot predict sensitization, whereas the LLNA-primary GNN achieves test AUC 0.727, but it shows that the GNN’s explanation signal is substantially driven by elemental identity. The atom features include atomic number as a 118-dimensional one-hot encoding, giving the model direct access to element identity. The heteroatom heuristic is therefore a lower bound on what the model can trivially learn from input features. Binary and per-element residual analyses largely disentangle this (the per-element analysis showing that elemental identity accounts for nearly all alignment), and the shuffled-label control (§3.3) further confirms that the structural-prior component dominates, but a complete decoupling would require ablating atomic number from the input features and retraining. Under the sensitization-specific 50-pattern alert set the heteroatom baseline is 0.416, because that set excludes hetero-rich metal chelators and hydroperoxides; six neutral baselines on the TP-with-match subset are tabulated in Section S5. Under the 135-pattern alert set the same heuristic reaches 0.685, an alert-set artifact of including chelators and peroxides outside the skin-sensitization MIE.

GNNExplainer used default hyperparameters. The training class imbalance is addressed by positive class weighting; the effect of pos_weight \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\in \{4, 8, 12\}$$\end{document}∈{4,8,12} on classification is tabulated in Section S3 (AUC varies by ± 0.02±0.02). Propagation of weighting effects to attribution alignment was not explored. Atom-level attribution faces challenges with delocalized electronic effects across multiple atoms. The acyl-transfer stratum shows a negative real-versus-shuffled attention gap (Δ = -0.19Δ=-0.19; Table 8); we do not interpret this difference, which is pooled across 39 molecule–seed observations corresponding to approximately two unique molecules under the deterministic scaffold split.

Comparison to prior work

Prior work on GNN attribution evaluation has used synthetic ground truth such as functional group annotations [26] and benchmarking datasets [43], or general toxicophore databases (GeoDILI [44]). Atom-level interpretation benchmarks for QSAR models [45] have established the importance of testing attributions against mechanistically-defined atom labels, and attribution analyses with engineered ground truth have shown that classifiers can achieve strong predictive accuracy while assigning high importance to spurious features [46]. Substructure-aware losses [25] and masking-based explanations [27] improve attributions, though validation against endpoint-specific mechanisms was outside their scope. Fragment-level attention [13] and SMILES-based XAI [22] have similarly not been evaluated against mechanistic ground truth. Sanity-check protocols for saliency methods, in which the model is retrained on randomized labels and the resulting saliency maps are compared with the originals, are well established in the vision-attribution literature [47] but have not been routinely applied to GNN attributions in chemistry-toxicology. To date, no study has evaluated GNN attributions against the specific reactive chemistry defined by an adverse outcome pathway.

Our work differs in three respects: (1) the reference labels are derived from AOP-40 MIE chemistry, a well-characterized, literature-grounded mechanistic framework, rather than generic functional group annotations; (2) we compare seven attribution methods under identical conditions with paired statistical tests across seeds, enabling direct method ranking; (3) we quantify the contribution of elemental identity through residual analyses, showing that it accounts for most apparent alignment. This last finding has implications beyond skin sensitization: any study claiming mechanistic alignment of GNN attributions should control for the heteroatom heuristic.

Conclusions

A GNN trained on LLNA labels (test AUC 0.727) attends to AOP-40 reactive centers at aggregate atom-AUC 0.59−0.62 across seven attribution methods, but a shuffled-label negative control places every method’s paired ΔΔ within zero of the real-label level (largest +0.023+0.023 for attention; perturbation methods within +0.003+0.003). The bulk of the apparent attribution–mechanism correlation is therefore a structural prior of the attribution methods themselves rather than learned sensitization chemistry. Mechanism-stratified analysis under the LLNA-primary configuration surfaces one candidate exception (pre-hapten autoxidation, Δ = +0.26Δ=+0.26) on a small stratum, pending replication.

For GNN-toxicology studies reporting attribution–mechanism alignment, pair the alignment metric with a label-permutation retraining control [47] and report a per-atom heteroatom indicator as a lower bound. All code, the 50 SMARTS sensitization-specific reference patterns, and evaluation tools will be released to facilitate extension to other AOPs, larger compound sets, and new attribution methods.
