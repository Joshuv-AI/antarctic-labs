Pedicled pectoralis major transfer for anterior deltoid and acromial deficiency following reverse total shoulder arthroplasty: a case report and novel surgical technique
Authors: Ariel Aminov, Kevin Berardino, Sophia Blaine, Carl M Cirino, Jeffrey J Brooks, Theodore A Blaine
Published: 2026

Introduction

Glenohumeral arthritis often results in a significant decline in quality of life, with impairments in activities of daily living becoming especially pronounced in advanced stages. Reverse shoulder arthroplasty (rTSA) has become a widely accepted surgical intervention, offering excellent outcomes, particularly in patients with concurrent rotator cuff pathology.1,11

Deltoid deficiency, whether iatrogenic or traumatic, poses a significant challenge in rTSA, as prosthesis function depends heavily on a competent deltoid muscle. The reverse prosthesis medializes the center of rotation relative to the glenoid, increasing the mechanical advantage and activation of the anterior and middle deltoid heads, thereby enabling these muscles to serve as the primary shoulder abductors.1,11 In the setting of deltoid deficiency, this mechanical advantage is lost, often necessitating adjunctive procedures to restore shoulder function. Reported strategies include nerve transfer, tendon transfer, and glenohumeral arthrodesis. Nerve transfers in patients with brachial plexus injuries have demonstrated reliable improvement in shoulder abduction in multiple case series, although the prolonged recovery period complicates early outcome assessment.9 Glenohumeral arthrodesis is typically reserved as a salvage procedure due to its significant limitation of shoulder range of motion. Given these limitations, a tendon transfer may offer improved functional outcomes by restoring active shoulder motion while preserving joint mobility.

The concept of the pedicled pectoralis major transfer to substitute for deltoid insufficiency was first described in 1991 by Hou and Tai7, who reported successful functional restoration in patients with deltoid paralysis. Since that initial report, several authors have further evaluated and incorporated the technique in the treatment of patients with deltoid deficiency in the setting of rTSA. Elhassan et al5 evaluated 31 shoulders in patients with chronic deltoid paralysis treated with a combined primary rTSA and pedicled pectoralis major transfer, demonstrating mean gains in active forward flexion of 83°, improvement in pain, and significant improvements in validated outcome scores at a mean follow-up of 37 months. In a separate case report, Burkhard et al2 described a similar surgical approach for irreparable deltoid dehiscence following rTSA, achieving excellent functional recovery. These findings collectively support the pedicled pectoralis major transfer as a versatile and effective reconstructive option in cases of deltoid insufficiency.

Acromial pathology adds significant complexity to rTSA, as the acromion provides a critical origin point for the deltoid. Pre-operative acromial pathology has not been conclusively linked to poorer outcomes, as demonstrated in a retrospective case–control study of 47 patients by Walch et al12 Post-operative acromial fractures, though uncommon, have been reported in approximately 5% of cases. These fractures may lead to diminished shoulder function due to loss of deltoid tension and a reduced lever arm.13, 3, 6, 8 Management strategies include sling immobilization or open reduction and internal fixation depending on fracture severity and functional impairment. Sling immobilization has been associated with nonunion rates of 50–60%, although patients achieving union demonstrate improved outcomes.10 In contrast, open reduction and internal fixation has shown higher union rates and superior functional recovery.4

To our knowledge, no prior published reports describe pedicled pectoralis major transfer as a salvage procedure for combined post-operative deltoid deficiency and acromial insufficiency following rTSA. We present this case to illustrate a potential reconstructive strategy in a patient with this combined pathology who failed conservative management, detailing the surgical rationale, technique, and early outcomes.

Case presentation

Patient history

A 57-year-old female presented with persistent left shoulder pain and limited range of motion. Her history was notable for a left rTSA performed at an outside facility approximately one year prior. One month post-operatively, she sustained a ground-level fall, resulting in acute worsening of shoulder pain and progressive loss of motion. Computed tomography (CT) at that time showed intact prosthetic components without fracture or dislocation but revealed anterior acromial insufficiency. Despite adherence to physical therapy, her symptoms persisted, significantly impairing activities of daily living, including dressing, showering, and cooking.

Physical examination

On physical examination, the patient had a well-healed surgical incision over the left shoulder. There was mild tenderness to palpation over the acromioclavicular joint, greater tuberosity, and bicipital groove. Active range of motion was significantly limited, with forward elevation to 50°, no measurable external rotation, and internal rotation restricted to the level of the flank. Strength testing revealed 3/5 power in forward elevation, external rotation, and internal rotation. Neurovascular examination of the left upper extremity was unremarkable.

Imaging

Radiographic evaluation, including X-rays and CT of the left shoulder, demonstrated well-positioned and intact prosthetic components with no evidence of periprosthetic fracture or dislocation. However, imaging was notable for anterior acromial deficiency (Figs. 1 A, B and 2 A, B). Magnetic resonance imaging revealed an intact rotator cuff but with atrophy of the deltoid muscle (Fig. 3 A, B). Electromyography of the left upper extremity revealed a structurally intact but functionally weak deltoid muscle, along with decreased axillary nerve activity, suggestive of partial axillary nerve dysfunction.

Surgical planning

Due to this patient's significantly decreased range of motion resulting from a deficient deltoid lever arm, several surgical options were considered. These included revision to a hemiarthroplasty with a large head, revision rTSA, resection arthroplasty, acromial reconstruction with a bone graft, and pectoralis major transfer. Given the patient's functional goals, a pectoralis major transfer was ultimately selected. This approach aimed to restore the mechanical lever arm on the prosthesis while preserving a functional range of motion, thereby optimizing her ability to perform her activities of daily living.

Surgical technique

The procedure was performed in collaboration with a microvascular specialist to facilitate safe dissection and preservation of the pectoral neurovascular structures. The patient was placed in the beach chair position, and a standard deltopectoral approach was utilized via the previous incision.

Upon exposure, the deltoid muscle was found to be severely atrophic, particularly in the anterior and middle heads. Intraoperative neuromonitoring using a Checkpoint stimulator confirmed minimal contractility of the anterior deltoid. The middle head demonstrated some residual function but was notably diminished. Dissection proceeded with identification and preservation of the cephalic vein, which was followed proximally to the axillary vessels. The thoracoacromial trunk was carefully isolated and preserved. The brachial plexus was explored, and both the medial and lateral pectoral nerves were identified, traced, and protected throughout the procedure.

Attention was then directed toward exploration of the rTSA components. The distal insertion of the pectoralis major tendon was visualized, and the deltoid was elevated to allow exposure of the subscapularis tendon. The old repair sutures were removed, and the subscapularis was tagged with 1-0 Vicryl sutures before being elevated from its insertion on the lesser tuberosity. The rTSA implant was examined and found to be stable and well-fixed. The rotator interval was repaired using #2 FiberWire suture.

Dissection of the pectoralis major was carried medially toward the sternoclavicular joint. Anatomically, the muscle is divided into 3 components: the clavicular head, the upper sternal head, and the lower sternal head. This distinction is clinically relevant, as the clavicular head is innervated by the lateral pectoral nerve, whereas the upper and lower sternocostal heads receive innervation from separate branches of the medial pectoral nerve. In this case, the clavicular and upper sternocostal heads were released and tagged with sutures, while the inferior sternocostal head was preserved with its branch of the medial pectoral nerve (Fig. 4).

The dissected clavicular and upper sternal head of the pectoralis major prior to its transfer over the deltoid.

Anterior acromial deficiency was noted upon exposure of the acromion. A pedicled pectoralis major transfer was performed by rotating the muscle in an arc over the residual deltoid (Fig. 5). Two transosseous drill tunnels were placed in the remaining acromion. The transferred pectoralis major tendon was prepared with running Krackow stitches using #2 FiberWire in 4 parallel rows. Sutures were passed through the acromial drill tunnels, then back through the tendon, achieving secure fixation. Additional reinforcement was performed with further Krackow sutures securing the transfer to the distal clavicle. The remaining deltoid tissue was repaired to the muscle-tendon unit using #2 FiberWire. Hemostasis was achieved, and the wound was closed in standard layered fashion. The patient tolerated the procedure well and was transferred to the postanesthesia care unit in stable condition.

Blue stars demonstrate the medial pectoral nerve to the transferred clavicular head. Orange arrows show the medial pectoral nerve branch to the untransferred lower sternocostal head.

Post-operative management

The patient was immobilized in a sling with an abduction pillow for 12 weeks. Passive range of motion with physical therapy was initiated at 6 weeks, progressing to active-assisted and active motion at 10 weeks. Strengthening was delayed until 12 weeks post-operatively.

Outcome

At the 6-month follow-up, the patient achieved forward elevation of 140°, external rotation of 45°, and internal rotation to her flank. At the 1-year follow-up, the patient maintained the same range of motion, and radiographs as well as CT demonstrated stable implants. There were no complications noted (Figure 6, Figure 7).

Discussion

Deltoid deficiency following rTSA presents a significant reconstructive challenge, as prosthesis function relies heavily on an intact deltoid for both motion and stability. This challenge is compounded by acromial insufficiency, which compromises the deltoid's origin and lever arm. While a prior case series by Elhassan et al5 demonstrated the utility of pectoralis major transfer as an adjunct to primary rTSA in patients with deltoid deficiency, this case is unique in describing its use as a salvage procedure in the setting of combined post-operative deltoid and acromial deficiency. The patient achieved excellent outcomes in pain relief and range of motion at a 1-year follow-up, suggesting that pectoralis major transfer may offer a functional alternative to more extensive surgery such as acromial bone graft reconstruction or more limiting interventions such as resection arthroplasty or arthrodesis.

From a biomechanical standpoint, the pedicled pectoralis major is well-suited to substitute for anterior deltoid function in patients with deltoid deficiency. When the clavicular and upper sternocostal heads are transferred laterally and superiorly, the resultant force vector approximates that of the anterior deltoid, restoring the mechanical lever arm for active forward elevation. In the setting of rTSA, the medialized center of rotation increases the moment arm for elevation.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. This report describes a single patient, limiting the generalizability of the findings. Additionally, although the patient maintained excellent function at one year, the long-term durability of the transfer remains unknown. Despite these limitations, this case highlights an alternative strategy to restore motion in patients with combined deltoid and acromial deficiency. The rarity of this pathology constrains larger studies, but as the volume of shoulder arthroplasties increases, further reports may help validate pectoralis major transfer as a viable salvage option in complex shoulder reconstruction.

Conclusion

Pectoralis major tendon transfer can be successfully utilized in complex cases of deltoid insufficiency following rTSA complicated by anterior acromial deficiency. This combined reconstructive approach restores active shoulder function and alleviates pain, offering a viable option in challenging clinical scenarios.
