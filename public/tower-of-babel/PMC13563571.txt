Impact of COPD on Lung Cancer Screening Positivity in a Specialist-Managed Cohort From Brazil
Authors: Fábio M. Svartman, Marina I. de Azambuja, Maurício M.R. Leite, Ana P.G. Sartori, Júlia M. Righi, Pedro H.R. Dal Toé, Cristiano F. Andrade
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIndividuals with COPD are frequently included in low-dose CT (LDCT) lung cancer screening programs because eligibility criteria are typically based on smoking exposure and age. However, the impact of COPD on LDCT scan results remains incompletely understood and has been little studied among patients under follow-up for chronic respiratory diseases and in middle-income countries.Research QuestionWhat is the association between COPD—using different definitions—and positive LDCT screening results in a cohort of patients with chronic, stable respiratory diseases in Brazil?Study Design and MethodsThis cross-sectional analysis included patients who underwent lung cancer screening at a tertiary hospital and met US Preventive Services Task Force eligibility criteria. Clinical, pulmonary function, and imaging data were retrospectively collected. COPD was defined as follows: (1) physician-diagnosed COPD, (2) airflow obstruction (FEV1/FVC < 0.70), (3) both criteria combined, and (4) CT scan-detected emphysema. Positive screening was defined as Lung Imaging Reporting and Data System (Lung-RADS) category 3 or 4. Regression analyses adjusted for demographic factors, smoking history, and postinflammatory residual lung opacities.ResultsOf the 1,337 screened patients, 1,034 (77%) met US Preventive Services Task Force eligibility criteria and had available data for analysis (mean age, 63.6 years; 53.2% female; mean FEV1, 64% predicted). COPD prevalence by physician diagnosis, airflow obstruction, and both criteria was 73.3%, 54.4%, and 51%, respectively; emphysema was present in 76.5%. Positive screening and Lung-RADS category 4 rates were 12.6% and 5.6%, with lung cancer detected in 2.2%. Emphysema was independently associated with positive screening (prevalence ratio, 1.74; 95% CI, 1.08-2.78; P = .02) and Lung-RADS category 4 (prevalence ratio, 3.27; 95% CI, 1.30-8.20; P = .01). Other COPD definitions were not significantly associated with screening positivity.InterpretationThese findings suggest that emphysema may be the COPD-related feature most strongly linked to screening positivity in this high-respiratory morbidity setting.

Take-Home PointsStudy Question: Among individuals undergoing low-dose CT lung cancer screening, is COPD—defined by clinical, spirometric, or imaging criteria—associated with screening positivity?Results: CT scan-detected emphysema, but not physician-diagnosed COPD or airflow obstruction, was independently associated with higher rates of positive screening and Lung Imaging Reporting and Data System category 4 findings.Interpretation: Emphysema appears to be the COPD-related feature most strongly associated with screening positivity and may help refine risk assessment in lung cancer screening populations.

Take-Home Points

Study Question: Among individuals undergoing low-dose CT lung cancer screening, is COPD—defined by clinical, spirometric, or imaging criteria—associated with screening positivity?

Results: CT scan-detected emphysema, but not physician-diagnosed COPD or airflow obstruction, was independently associated with higher rates of positive screening and Lung Imaging Reporting and Data System category 4 findings.

Interpretation: Emphysema appears to be the COPD-related feature most strongly associated with screening positivity and may help refine risk assessment in lung cancer screening populations.

Lung cancer screening with low-dose CT (LDCT) scan is widely recommended as an effective strategy to reduce cancer mortality among individuals at high risk, typically defined by age and a history of heavy smoking.1, 2, 3 In addition to being risk factors for lung cancer, smoking and older age are also strongly associated with COPD, making the inclusion of individuals with COPD in screening programs common.4

In fact, previous studies have shown that the prevalence of COPD among screening participants is high, reaching, for example, 35% in a subgroup analysis of > 18,000 participants from the National Lung Screening Trial.5 As expected, COPD prevalence varies according to the demographic and smoking profiles of the populations enrolled, but part of this variation can also be attributed to the heterogeneous definitions of COPD used in published studies, ranging from self-reported disease6 and International Classification of Diseases/billing codes in the electronic health record7,8 to spirometric airflow obstruction only5,9,10 or a combination of spirometry and symptoms.11 The presence of CT scan-detected emphysema has also been investigated, either qualitatively or through automated quantification, with a prevalence in screened populations estimated at approximately 45%.4

Beyond being prevalent, COPD may potentially influence screening outcomes in several ways.12,13 One relevant aspect is that, because both airflow obstruction and emphysema are independent risk factors for lung cancer,14, 15, 16 screening programs with a high prevalence of COPD might reasonably expect higher lung cancer detection rates,12,17 with predictable consequences for the need for diagnostic evaluations, invasive procedures, and treatments. However, the specific impact of COPD on LDCT scan performance as a screening tool for lung cancer remains incompletely understood. Studies have found evidence that COPD may indeed be associated with an increased frequency of positive screening results7 and, importantly, a higher false-positive rate.8,18 It is not clear, however, what the relative impact of different disease components (emphysema, airflow obstruction, and symptoms) might be, nor how these influences behave across different clinical and demographic settings.

Our group implemented a lung cancer screening program serving a population with a high frequency of chronic respiratory diseases—particularly COPD—under specialist follow-up in Brazil.19 A recent meta-analysis that included our program’s early results identified it as 1 of the cohorts with the highest prevalences of emphysema and airflow obstruction reported to date.4 The present study aims to investigate the association between COPD and screening positivity within this program, using available data from pulmonary function tests, imaging, and documented clinical information.

Study Design and Methods

Setting and Study Oversight

This was a cross-sectional analysis using retrospectively collected data from the first round of a lung cancer screening program in Brazil. The primary objective was to evaluate the association between COPD—using different definitions—and screening positivity. The study was conducted at a large public tertiary hospital located within the state with the highest incidence of lung cancer in the country (overall incidence, 32 per 100,000 inhabitants20). The region also has a high prevalence of granulomatous diseases, particularly TB (36.5 cases per 100,000 inhabitants21).

The lung cancer screening program began in 2014, integrated into the outpatient care of patients with chronic respiratory diseases (eg, asthma, COPD, bronchiectasis) and on tobacco cessation counseling. The decision to request the LDCT scan and to include a patient in the program is made by the attending pulmonologist during routine follow-up visits, based on established inclusion criteria (subsequently described) and clinical judgment about patient’s disease severity and stability. Recommendations for the management of positive findings (eg, follow-up CT scan, biopsy) are provided in the radiology report according to the Lung Imaging Reporting and Data System (Lung-RADS) system,22 but final decisions are left to the attending pulmonologist.

Because this program was implemented as part of standard clinical care, no formal written consent was required for LDCT scan requests or enrollment. However, discussion of the potential risks and benefits of screening is routinely performed during medical visits.

As a referral center, the hospital routinely manages patients with advanced or very symptomatic respiratory disease—many of whom are not typical candidates for lung cancer screening given their limited eligibility for surgical treatment. Nevertheless, a survey in the same setting showed that approximately one-third of the patients met all inclusion criteria for screening, and at least 1 in 8 were eligible after excluding advanced disease or other contraindications.23 A detailed description of the program’s initial results, including early stage detection rates, has been published elsewhere.19

Patients

Data from all patients who underwent at least 1 LDCT scan at the institution between June 2014 and June 2024 were analyzed. Patients were included in this analysis if their medical records contained the following: (1) a clear documentation indicating that the LDCT scan was performed for lung cancer screening purposes, and (2) data on age and smoking history meeting the eligibility criteria of the US Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF).

Because the study period covered 2 USPSTF recommendations (2013 and 2021), patients were included if they met the version in effect at the time of screening. Thus, individuals screened between 2014 and March 2021 were 55 to 80 years of age, had a smoking history of at least 30 pack-years, and were currently smoking or had quit within the past 15 years. From April 2021 onward, inclusion criteria were 50 to 80 years of age, ≥ 20 pack-years of smoking, and current or former smoking within the previous 15 years. All included participants met the current, broader criteria.1

Patients were excluded if LDCT scan was performed for nonscreening purposes or if insufficient clinical data were available to confirm screening intent. Eligibility regarding comorbidities, respiratory disease severity, and fitness for screening was assessed by the attending pulmonologist at the time of LDCT scan referral; reassessment of this judgment was beyond the scope of this study.

LDCT Scan and Spirometry

All LDCT scans were performed in accordance with technical parameters recommended by the American College of Radiology.24 Reports followed the Lung-RADS classification system from the program’s inception, updated to the 2022 version thereafter.22

All data relating to LDCT scan used in this study were manually extracted by the researchers from the radiologic report. Beyond the Lung-RADS score, the presence of postinflammatory residual lung abnormalities was systematically recorded given its high prevalence in TB-endemic areas and the potential to confound screening results.25 The following terms were also accepted as equivalent descriptors for postinflammatory residual lung abnormalities: fibroatelectatic lesions, postinflammatory sequelae (or changes), and focal fibrotic changes/abnormalities.

Spirometry was performed by trained technicians following American Thoracic Society standards26 and was interpreted by pulmonologists using reference values validated for the Brazilian population.27,28 All spirometry data used in this study are post-bronchodilator.

Data Collection From Electronic Medical Records

In addition to LDCT scan and spirometry reports, electronic medical records were reviewed to extract demographic data and smoking history. For patients with positive screening results, data on nodule investigation and cancer confirmation or exclusion were collected.

All chart reviews were conducted manually by the authors. Any discrepancies were resolved by consensus with a senior investigator. Data were anonymized for analysis. Missing data are explicitly reported in the results.

Outcomes and Variables of Interest

A positive screening result was defined as Lung-RADS category 3 or 4, and a negative result was defined as Lung-RADS category 1 or 2. A secondary analysis considered Lung-RADS category 4 alone, given its higher positive predictive value29,30 and clinical relevance. The 4 following COPD definitions were applied: (1) physician-diagnosed COPD: recorded in the medical chart, either as an explicit diagnosis or via International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision code J44, including overlap with other diseases (eg, asthma-COPD overlap); (2) airflow obstruction: postbronchodilator FEV1/FVC < 0.70, with severity classified according to Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease criteria3; (3) confirmed COPD: presence of both a clinical diagnosis and airflow obstruction, as previously defined; and (4) emphysema: considered present if explicitly described in the radiology report, regardless of extent. It should be noted that the presence or absence of emphysema is systematically reported in the standard LDCT scan report. Emphysema detection by the radiologist is based on visual (nonautomated) assessment. Quantitative assessment of emphysema was not performed; thus, it was treated as a dichotomous variable.

As an exploratory analysis, follow-up data for all Lung-RADS category 3 and 4 cases were reviewed to describe lung cancer detection rates in the overall sample and across groups. A case was considered true positive when malignancy was confirmed histologically or by unequivocal radiologic progression. A false-positive result required 1 of the following: CT scan-demonstrated complete regression of the nodule, documented stability for ≥ 2 (solid nodules) or ≥ 5 years (ground-glass nodules), partial but evident regression with inflammatory features, or biopsy confirming a benign etiology. Results of patients with incomplete workup (eg, lost to follow-up, refusal of workup, insufficient duration to establish benignity) were reported as incomplete data; therefore, benignity was not presumed without meeting the aforementioned criteria.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to characterize clinical, demographic, and spirometric variables, and screening positivity and confirmed cancer rates. The association between a positive screening result and COPD was initially tested separately for each of the 4 COPD definitions using the χ2 test. We then planned a multivariable analysis only for the definition that reached statistical significance in the χ2 test to assess the effect of potential confounders. The multivariable model was built using Poisson regression with robust variance estimation. Potential confounders were selected based on clinical relevance and plausibility and included demographic variables (age and gender) and 2 factors potentially associated with nodules on imaging (postinflammatory residual lung abnormalities and smoking exposure). We avoided including multiple COPD definitions simultaneously in the same model because of their high potential for collinearity and to prevent issues related to multiple comparisons. The same analytical steps were followed to evaluate Lung-RADS category 4 separately as the secondary outcome. Adjusted prevalence rate ratios (PRs) and 95% CIs were reported. Missing data were not imputed. All analyses were performed using SPSS software (IBM Corp). Statistical significance was set at P < .05. A sample size of 912 individuals (684 with COPD and 228 without COPD) was calculated as necessary to detect an association between COPD and a positive screening result, based on an estimated 15% prevalence of positive screenings in the sample,7 a 75% prevalence of COPD,19 80% statistical power, and a 2-sided alpha of 0.05. Originally, an OR of 1.787 was used as the parameter for this calculation, corresponding to a PR of 1.59 under the same assumptions.

Ethical Considerations

This study was approved by the Institutional Scientific and Research Ethics Committees of Hospital Nossa Senhora da Conceição (7.345.291). The requirement for written informed consent was waived because of the retrospective design. All data were analyzed anonymously.

Results

Population Characteristics

Of the 1,337 patients who underwent at least 1 LDCT scan during the study period, 1,034 were included in this analysis (Fig 1). The main reason for exclusion was LDCT scan performed for indications other than lung cancer screening (n = 221). The mean age of participants was 63.6 years, with a slight predominance of female individuals (53%) and individuals currently smoking (56.2%) (Table 1). Heavy (median, 53 pack-years) and long-term smoking (mean, 45.7 years) were notable features of the sample. Airflow obstruction and the combination of obstruction with a physician diagnosis of COPD were present in 54.4% and 51% of participants, respectively. Emphysema was reported in 76.5% of LDCT scans. Radiologic Findings and Screening Positivity

There were 130 positive screens (12.6%) (Lung-RADS categories 3 and 4), with 23 confirmed cancers (2.2%) (Table 1). Postinflammatory residual lung abnormalities were present in 38.7% of cases.

Discussion

In this analysis of a screening program embedded in the routine care of patients followed by pulmonologists, we found a significant association between the presence of emphysema and the rate of positive screening examinations classified according to the Lung-RADS system. This association was not attenuated by clinical or demographic factors, nor by the presence of residual CT scan opacities, and it remained evident when we examined separately those scans classified as more suspicious for lung cancer (Lung-RADS category 4). It is important to highlight that the study population consisted of patients with a high burden of COPD, airflow obstruction, and emphysema, in a setting characterized by a high prevalence of granulomatous diseases and residual imaging abnormalities. Pulmonary function test results depicted a population with significant physiological impairment: more than two-thirds of all patients had a reduced FEV1, and over one-quarter had values < 50% of predicted.

Despite the expected overlap among emphysema, airflow obstruction, and a clinical diagnosis of COPD in this population, the effect of each of these characteristics on screening outcomes was not identical. Unlike emphysema, neither the frequency of positive screens nor the frequency of Lung-RADS category 4 was statistically higher when clinical and spirometric definitions of COPD were used. Given the nonsignificant numerical trends in our results and a positive association found in previous work,7 it is possible that this negative result in our study reflects limited statistical power to detect smaller differences. Nonetheless, the consistently positive findings for emphysema suggest that its association with positive screening outcomes may be of greater magnitude than the potential impact of airflow obstruction or a clinical COPD diagnosis. To our knowledge, this is the first study to compare all these definitions of COPD and emphysema within the same sample, providing an opportunity to investigate the specific impact of each component.

COPD is recognized as a complex and heterogeneous disease.3,31 A multidimensional approach to its diagnosis was recently proposed,32 acknowledging that clinical, functional, and histopathologic features may occur at different frequencies in individuals equally affected by the condition. In the present sample, a markedly larger proportion of patients had emphysema (76.5%) or a clinical COPD diagnosis (73%) compared with the traditional requirement of both symptoms and airflow obstruction (51%). We think these figures reflect real-world patterns and underscore the importance of evaluating the specific contribution of each component to screening outcomes, lung cancer risk, and subsequent clinical events.

The frequencies of COPD and emphysema in this sample were higher than those reported in other studies,4 likely reflecting referral to pulmonology care for patients with COPD-related symptoms and a high smoking burden. However, other exposures and etiologic factors common in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) (eg, indoor biomass fuel exposure, early life factors related to malnutrition and infections3) were not assessed and may be more prevalent than in high-income settings. Given that > 80% of COPD cases occur in LMICs,3 further investigation of these factors and of the relationship between COPD and lung cancer screening in settings such as Brazil is essential.

Studies have shown that emphysema33 and COPD7 are associated with higher screening positivity, possibly reflecting increased lung cancer incidence in these individuals. However, this explanation may be incomplete: at least 2 studies, including a reanalysis of the National Lung Screening Trial, reported higher false-positive rates among patients with COPD,8,18 indicating a greater burden of benign nodules. The coexistence of higher cancer prevalence and higher false-positive rates poses a major challenge for screening programs in which individuals with COPD predominate. Increased false positives may lead to unnecessary invasive procedures, a particular concern in patients with COPD because of their higher complication risk. Conversely, overly conservative management in a population with high pretest cancer probability may limit the benefits of early diagnosis and cure. In our exploratory analysis, false-positive rates appeared similar across COPD definitions; however, the small number of events among individuals without COPD precludes formal comparisons. Further research is needed to clarify false-positive rates in COPD, particularly in LMICs, where limited resources and a high disease burden coexist.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, its single-center, cross-sectional design limits causal inference and may reduce generalizability to screening programs with different organizational structures or population profiles. The associations observed in this specific epidemiologic context may not be applicable to populations recruited outside specialist pulmonology care settings or in high-income regions. Regarding the retrospective nature of data collection, we sought to mitigate misclassification and measurement bias by relying on objective and routinely collected clinical data, including standardized spirometry and radiology reports.

Another important limitation is that emphysema was assessed qualitatively and dichotomously based on radiologist reports, without quantitative assessment or severity grading, which may have limited our ability to detect dose-response relationships or more nuanced associations. In addition, although key confounders (eg, age, smoking burden, postinflammatory residual lung abnormalities) were included in multivariable models, residual confounding from unmeasured factors (eg, occupational or domestic exposures, air pollution, nonrespiratory comorbidities) cannot be excluded.

Finally, beyond the limited statistical power of the exploratory analyses, our data lack a comprehensive assessment of postscreening clinical outcomes, which could allow more clinically meaningful conclusions. A key limitation of our exploratory analysis of cancer confirmation rates is the large proportion of patients with incomplete diagnostic workup after a positive result, possibly because of loss to follow-up, refusal, or clinical decision-making. Suboptimal evaluation of positive findings is a recognized concern, with less intensive care than recommended reported in approximately one-third of individuals in a large real-world study.34 This represents a critical area for improvement in lung cancer screening programs, particularly in low-resource settings such as ours.

Interpretation

Our findings indicate that, in a lung cancer screening program embedded in the care of patients with a high burden of chronic respiratory disease, the presence of emphysema is associated with higher LDCT screening positivity and a greater proportion of Lung-RADS category 4 results. Airflow obstruction, physician-diagnosed COPD, and their association did not show the same effect in this sample. These findings suggest that emphysema may be the COPD-related feature most strongly linked to screening positivity in this setting. Advancing knowledge on lung cancer screening in individuals with COPD, particularly in LMICs, is essential to optimize benefits, minimize harm, and improve the overall effectiveness of screening programs.
