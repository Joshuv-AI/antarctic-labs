Plasma proteomics reveal SERPINA1 and CD59 as candidate biomarkers for COVID-19 severity stratification and prognosis prediction
Authors: Zizhen Zhou, Qiwei Liu, Shuangshuang Ma, Anke Shi, Yuzhi Tao, Tianpeng Hu, Shengtao Yan, Yinong Chen, Xiaofan Ji, Lu Sun, Hong Zhang, Wanlu Song, Zhu Zhang, Peiran Yang, Zhenguo Zhai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCOVID-19 has been closely associated with coagulation abnormalities. However, existing biomarkers, including D-dimer and fibrin degradation products (FDP), exhibit limited accuracy in stratifying disease severity and predicting long-term clinical outcomes.ObjectivesThis study aimed to use proteomic analysis to identify plasma biomarkers associated with COVID-19 severity and prognosis, and validate their predictive utility for mortality and thromboembolic complications.MethodsPlasma proteomic profiles were analyzed across three COVID-19 severity classes. Differential expression analysis and functional analysis were performed. Clustering analysis was used to identify proteins correlated with disease severity. Candidate biomarkers were validated in an independent cohort. Predictive performance of the biomarkers for mortality, sepsis and venous thromboembolism was evaluated using bootstrap-corrected ROC analyses and multivariable regression analyses.ResultsProteomic analysis revealed progressive involvement of the coagulation and complement pathway with increasing disease severity. SERPINA1 and CD59 were identified as candidate biomarkers and exhibited significantly higher plasma levels in severe cases. Bootstrap-corrected ROC analyses demonstrated strong predictive performance: SERPINA1 achieved AUCs of 0.775 and 0.924 for 30-day and 12-month mortality, and CD59 achieved AUCs of 0.720 for sepsis; the combined model further improved prediction of 12-month mortality (AUC 0.946) and sepsis (AUC 0.904), outperforming D-dimer and FDP. Multivariable regression confirmed their independent prognostic value.ConclusionThis exploratory study identifies SERPINA1 and CD59 as candidate prognostic biomarkers in COVID-19, highlighting the role of coagulation and complement-related pathways in disease severity and warranting further prospective validation.

Introduction

Severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) infection manifests in diverse clinical outcomes, including asymptomatic cases, mild to moderate symptoms, severe pneumonia, and long COVID, according to the World Health Organization (WHO) [1]. While most patients recover without complications, some rapidly progress from mild symptoms to severe or even fatal conditions [2]. Early identification of patients at risk of severe outcomes remains crucial for timely intervention and reducing mortality. The rapid progression of COVID-19 involves complex mechanisms, including viral characteristics such as a high initial viral load [3], and host characteristics, including age, comorbidities, genetic predispositions, immune dysregulation, and vaccination status [4]. Among these, systemic inflammation and hypercoagulability have emerged as critical contributors to adverse outcomes such as respiratory failure, multiorgan dysfunction, and mortality [5]. Elevated levels of interleukin-6 (IL-6) and C-reactive protein (CRP) are widely used as markers of inflammation [6], while D-dimer and fibrin degradation products (FDP) are used as markers of coagulation [7,8]. However, these traditional biomarkers of coagulation exhibit significant inter-individual variability, limited specificity, and narrow detection ranges, restricting their utility in clinical risk stratification and therapeutic decision-making.

Evidence from a meta-analysis of COVID-19 patients (94% hospitalized) reported an in-hospital venous thromboembolism (VTE) prevalence of 14.1% [9]. Moreover, previous reports revealed that COVID-19 infection was associated with significant and prolonged elevations in the risks of deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary thromboembolism, both in hospital and community settings [10]. Our single-center cohort study, which employed systematic ultrasound screening, identified acute VTE in 39.3% of patients despite prophylactic measures, highlighting the previously underestimated VTE prevalence, especially among critically ill patients [11]. More importantly, emerging evidence highlights that coagulation abnormalities represent a fundamental pathway driving severe COVID-19 pathogenesis rather than being secondary consequences of systemic inflammation [12,13]. Hypercoagulability in severe cases is characterized by widespread thrombotic events, fibrinolytic dysregulation, and complement activation, emphasizing the need for a deeper understanding of these mechanisms [14,15]. Yet, the precise role of coagulation pathways in disease progression and their potential as biomarkers for therapeutic intervention remain inadequately explored.

Through an integrated proteomic analysis of patients at varying disease severities, we discovered that coagulation pathways were consistently disrupted in COVID-19, with progressively greater involvement from mild to severe cases, leading to the identification of specific coagulation-related proteins as potential biomarkers. By focusing on the interplay between coagulation pathways and COVID-19 severity, this study aimed to address a critical gap in understanding the pathophysiology of severe disease and to propose novel biomarkers for improving patient outcomes through early prediction and intervention.

Methods

Study design and sample collection

This study was conducted using a two-cohort design comprising a discovery cohort and a validation cohort. The discovery cohort included patients with SARS-CoV-2 infections admitted to the Intensive Care Unit (ICU) and general wards between December 2022 and January 2023. The validation cohort included patients admitted between May and June 2023, matched for age and gender with the discovery cohort.

SARS-CoV-2 infections were diagnosed based on the New Coronavirus Pneumonia Prevention and Control Program (6th edition) by the National Health Commission of China. Infection was confirmed by RT-PCR testing for SARS-CoV-2, with clinical symptoms and exposure history also being important factors in diagnosis. Patients were categorized into three severity levels. Class I: symptomatic patients not requiring oxygen supplementation; Class II: patients requiring oxygen or ventilator support but with clinical improvement; Class III: patients requiring high-flow nasal oxygen or ventilator support with in-hospital mortality. Healthy controls were volunteers recruited from the same hospital during the same period as the patients, matched by age and sex, with no history of symptomatic COVID-19 and negative SARS-CoV-2 PCR tests. For ICU patients in the discovery cohort, given the prolonged course of illness, plasma samples were collected at three time points: within 24 h of a positive PCR test, between 7 and 10 days, and at 15 days post-diagnosis. Plasma samples from general ward patients were collected within 24 h of hospital admission. In the validation cohort, blood samples were collected within 24 h of hospital admission, prior to any therapeutic interventions. Blood samples (5 mL) were drawn into EDTA tubes, centrifuged at 1000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C, and plasma was stored at −80 °C until analysis.

Clinical and laboratory data collection

All patients had been infected with the Omicron variant and had been vaccinated against COVID-19. Their clinical management adhered strictly to the China-Japan Friendship Hospital’s Initial Diagnosis and Treatment Protocol for Adult Omicron Virus Infection, including the use of corticosteroids and antithrombotic medications as clinically indicated [16]. Detailed information on baseline characteristics and treatment regimens is provided in Table S1. Clinical parameters, including PaO2/FiO2 ratio, mean arterial pressure (MAP), platelet count, plateletcrit (PCT), C-reactive protein (CRP), and Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) scores, were collected concurrently with plasma sampling. Standard coagulation parameters, including D-dimer, FDP, thrombin time (TT), and activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT), were analyzed during hospitalization. All patients were followed up for 12 months to assess mortality and monitor VTE incidence within three months. Demographic data, comorbidities, radiological findings, laboratory results, and clinical outcomes were extracted from electronic medical records.

Data-independent acquisition proteomics analysis

We used BioRAD depletion to extract total proteins [17–19], followed by protein quantification using a Bradford protein assay kit. Protein samples for analysis were obtained after enzymatic digestion (trypsin, in-solution) and desalting [20,21]. Mass spectrometry was performed using the data-independent acquisition (DIA) mode for proteomic data collection [22]. LC-MS/MS analysis was performed on a Q Exactive HF-X (Thermo) in line with an Easy-nLC 1200 HPLC system. The specific workflow involved fractionating a portion of 200 μg digested peptides for each plasma sample using the Easy-nLC 1200 HPLC system, and LC–MS/MS data acquisition using the data-dependent acquisition (DDA) mode as described. The whole mass spectra obtained were used for DDA library construction via the pulser module in the Spectronaut software. The digested peptides of 2 μg for each plasma sample were then loaded onto the LC–MS/MS system, and data were acquired using the DIA mode as described. The MS/MS spectra obtained were matched to the DDA library using Spectronaut software for protein identification and quantification [23–25].

Differential expression and functional analyses

We first conducted orthogonal projection to latent structures (OPLS) analysis between all patients and healthy controls, followed by partial least squares (PLS) analysis between the patients from Class I, II, or III and the healthy controls, generating a Venn diagram from these comparisons. Subsequently, we performed differential expression protein (DEP) analysis, Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis, KEGG enrichment analysis, and protein–protein interaction (PPI) analysis on the quantitative data. DEP analysis was performed on the three disease groups separately against the healthy control group, and volcano plots were generated. The data correction method used was linear fitting. Proteins with a fold change (FC) > 1.414 (log2FC > 0.5) and p-value < 0.05 were considered significantly upregulated, while proteins with FC < 0.707 (log2FC < −0.5) and p-value < 0.05 were considered significantly downregulated. Based on the enrichment and PPI analysis results, we conducted C-means clustering analysis, selecting potential biomarkers from clusters positively correlated with disease severity, and performed KEGG enrichment analysis on the proteins within this cluster.

ELISA assays

Validation of candidate biomarkers was conducted using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits (Human alpha 1 Antitrypsin (SERPINA1) ELISA Kit, Abcam, ab108799; Human CD59 ELISA Kit, Abcam, ab263893) in the validation cohort. ELISA assays were performed in triplicates for each sample, employing the sandwich method for SERPINA1 and the SimpleStep method for CD59, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a Biotek Synergy HTX plate reader.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R and SPSS Statistics (version 28.0). For proteomic data, OPLS and PLS analyses were conducted using the ropls package. The Venn diagram was generated using the VennDiagram package. DEP analysis, GO enrichment analysis, and KEGG enrichment analysis were conducted using the limma package and clusterProfiler package, with the database package org.Hs.eg.db utilized for both enrichment analyses. Result visualization was performed using the pheatmap, ggplot2, RColorBrewer, and circlize packages. PPI analysis was conducted using StringDB (http://string-db.org/), and the results were visualized using Cytoscape. C-means clustering analysis was performed using the TCseq package, and visualization was achieved using the ggplot2 package. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± SD or median (IQR), and categorical variables as counts and percentages. Group comparisons were conducted using one-way ANOVA for normally distributed variables, or Kruskal–Wallis tests for non-normally distributed variables. Spearman’s correlation coefficients were used to evaluate associations between variables. The diagnostic and prognostic performance of candidate biomarkers was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, with area under the curve (AUC) values calculated. The primary endpoints for ROC curve analysis included death within 30 days, death within 12 months, and the occurrence of VTE and sepsis during follow-up. To assess the robustness of the predictive models and correct for potential overfitting, internal validation was conducted using bootstrap resampling with 1000 replicates, yielding bias-corrected 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the AUC estimates. Mortality status was determined based on the presence or absence of death within 1-month and 12-month periods, as documented in patient medical records and follow-up evaluations. SERPINA1 and CD59 were evaluated in separate age-adjusted Cox proportional hazards models, and hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% CIs were estimated. Subdistribution hazard ratios (sHRs) for VTE were estimated using Fine–Gray competing-risk regression, treating death as a competing event. The number of patients at risk, cumulative number of VTE events, competing events (death), and censored observations were summarized at key time points in Table S2. SERPINA1 and CD59 were evaluated in separate multivariable models, each adjusted for age, and sHRs with 95% CIs were reported. Associations of SERPINA1, CD59, age, and SOFA with VTE were assessed using multivariable Firth penalized logistic regression, and odds ratios (ORs) with 95% CIs were estimated.

Results

Study cohort characteristics

Patients were stratified into three severity classes based on clinical outcomes: Class I (mild disease), Class II (severe disease with recovery), and Class III (critical disease with in-hospital mortality). Demographic characteristics, including age distribution, were consistent across groups. The majority of patients in all groups were male, reflecting the gender disparity observed in severe COVID-19 cases.

In the discovery cohort, 26 patients with confirmed SARS-CoV-2 infection were enrolled. Of these, 15 patients were managed in the general ward (Class I group n = 15), while 11 patients required transfer to the Intensive Care Unit (ICU) due to disease progression (Class II group n = 5; Class III group n = 6). An age- and sex-matched control group of 10 healthy individuals was also included for comparison (Table 1).

Across the disease severity spectrum, a progressive worsening of clinical and laboratory parameters was observed, including a decrease in oxygenation levels (PaO2/FiO2), mean arterial pressure, and platelet counts as disease severity increased. Markers of systemic inflammation, such as CRP, showed a stepwise increase, with mean values of 53.83 mg/L, 68 mg/L, and 103.49 mg/L in Classes I, II, and III, respectively. Additionally, SOFA scores increased significantly from 1.8 in Class I to 4.2 in Class II and 13.17 in Class III, indicating more severe multi-organ dysfunction in critically ill patients. Among Class III patients, the disease was most severe, resulting in a 100% all-cause mortality rate during hospitalization. Detailed comparisons of clinical and laboratory parameters across patient groups are presented in Table 1.

In the validation phase, an additional cohort of 30 patients was recruited, consisting of 20 ICU patients and 10 general ward patients. No significant differences in demographic characteristics, disease severity, or coagulation-related markers were observed between the discovery and validation cohorts, confirming the reproducibility of patient profiles and clinical features across both phases.

Data quality control

After the acquisition of DDA and DIA data, data quality control was performed. To construct the DDA library, molecules with a mass-to-charge ratio ranging from 400 to 1200 were included, covering 92.9% of the detected molecules (Fig. S1). According to the distribution results of proteome coverage, the vast majority of detected peptides corresponded to well-defined parent ions, indicating that the confidence of the identified proteins was relatively high (Fig. S2). Through DIA, the number of detected proteins was relatively even across all samples (Fig. S3).

Analysis of differentially expressed proteins

Plasma samples from the four groups, including Class I (15 samples), Class II (15 samples from 5 patients at 3 time points), Class III (18 samples from 6 patients at 3 time points), and healthy controls (10 samples), were subjected to proteomic analysis. Protein abundance profiles revealed significant differences between the healthy control group and the patient groups (Figure 1(A)). Notably, the protein abundance patterns in Class II exhibited partial overlap with Class I and Class III, reflecting its intermediate disease severity and transitional biological status (Figure 1(B)). An overview of the shared and unique protein abundance profiles across the groups is shown in a Venn diagram, further illustrating the overlaps and differences in proteomic signatures (Figure 1(C)). As illustrated by volcano plots (Figure 1(D–F)), in the comparison between Class I and Controls, 152 proteins (11.9% of total) exhibited significant downregulation, while 96 proteins (7.5%) were significantly upregulated. The Class II vs. Control comparison demonstrated a similar trend, with 135 proteins (10.6%) significantly downregulated and 106 proteins (8.4%) significantly upregulated. Strikingly, in the Class III vs. Control comparison, 166 proteins (12.9%) were significantly upregulated, while 135 proteins (10.6%) were significantly downregulated. Collectively, the results revealed distinct alterations in protein expression across disease severities, with Class III showing the most pronounced changes, reflecting progressive disruptions in the plasma proteome.

Functional analysis of dysregulated plasma proteins

KEGG and GO pathway enrichment analyses were performed on the DEPs identified in Class I, Class II, and Class III groups compared with the control group. GO analysis, with a focus on biological processes, revealed that coagulation-related processes were significantly enriched across all groups. These included platelet activation, blood coagulation, hemostasis, wound healing, and regulation of blood coagulation (Figure 2(A–C)). The consistent enrichment of these pathways highlighted their relevance in COVID-19. Similarly, KEGG pathway analysis demonstrated a recurring prominence of complement and coagulation cascades, with the number of enriched proteins increasing with disease severity (Figure 2(D–F)). The ‘complement and coagulation cascades’ pathway was notably enriched across all patient groups and emerged as the shared pathway (Fig. S4).

Protein-protein interaction (PPI) network analysis further elucidated the interplay between dysregulated proteins, particularly those associated with the complement and coagulation cascades pathway. This pathway became increasingly dominant with disease progression, reflecting its role in the worsening clinical stages and outcomes. In Class I patients, the enriched pathways within the PPI network were diverse, including regulation of actin cytoskeleton, cholesterol metabolism, platelet activation, and complement and coagulation cascades (Figure 2(G)). Class II patients displayed intermediate enrichment patterns, consistent with their intermediate disease severity. The upregulation of key coagulation-related hub proteins, such as F7, F8, F11, and SERPINA5, corresponded with their clinical and proteomic profiles (Figure 2(H)). However, in Class III patients, characterized by severe disease and universally fatal outcomes, the coagulation pathway emerged as the central mechanism. This shift was associated with an increased number of hub proteins in the complement and coagulation cascades pathway, including C1S, C4A, C4B, C5, SERPINA1, SERPING1, FGA, FGB, VTN, and PLG (Figure 2(I)). Notably, as disease severity increased, the number of proteins within the coagulation cascade increased, and their interactions with proteins in other pathways became more pronounced. Taken together, the increasing dominance of the complement and coagulation cascades pathway underscored its critical role in the progression of COVID-19.

Identification of a protein cluster correlated with disease progression

In order to further elucidate the relationship between protein abundance and disease progression, we focused on key proteins positively correlated with disease severity and their potential involvement in the coagulation pathway. DEPs were subjected to C-means clustering analysis, which revealed distinct clusters associated with varying disease stages (Figure 3(A and B)). Among these, cluster 6 exhibited a strong positive correlation with disease severity. To uncover its biological relevance, we performed KEGG enrichment analysis on the proteins within this cluster (Figure 3(C)). The complement and coagulation cascades pathway emerged as the most significantly enriched. Among the proteins enriched in this pathway within cluster 6, the top two ranked proteins, SERPINA1 and CD59, were selected as candidate biomarkers (Figure 3(D)).

For Classes II and III, proteomic data were further analyzed by dividing into subgroups based on the time point of plasma sample collection. The results revealed distinct clinical trajectories between Class II and Class III groups over 3 time points: Class II patients showed progressive clinical improvement or recovery, marked by declining SOFA scores, whereas Class III patients experienced disease deterioration, as indicated by rising SOFA scores (Fig. S5A). Protein abundance levels of the candidate biomarkers SERPINA1 and CD59 across the 3 time points were consistent with the trajectories of disease progression (Fig. S5B-C), showing reductions in Class II and elevations in Class III. Notably, the levels of SERPINA1 and CD59 were significantly correlated with the SOFA score (Fig. S5D). Taken together, SERPINA1 and CD59 demonstrated dynamic changes reflecting the disease severity.

Validation of biomarkers for COVID-19 severity and coagulation abnormalities

Validation of the identified biomarkers SERPINA1 and CD59 involved measuring their plasma levels by ELISA in an independent cohort. Plasma concentrations of SERPINA1 and CD59 showed progressive increases from Class I to III patients, and were significantly elevated in patients with severe COVID-19 (Classes II and III) compared to the control group (Figure 4(A–B)). Results from routine clinical coagulation tests were collected for COVID-19 patients in this study. These tests showed increases in D-dimer, FDP, international normalized ratio (INR), activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT), and prothrombin time (PT) related to disease severity, as well as a reduction in prothrombin activity (PTA) and no apparent changes in fibrinogen (FIB) or thrombin time (TT) across patient classes (Figure 4(C–J)). To further evaluate the clinical utility of the candidate biomarkers, we analyzed the correlation between SERPINA1 and CD59 and routinely tested clinical indicators, including coagulation factors, cytokines (IL6, TNFα), complement factors (C3, C4), and inflammatory markers (CRP, LDH) (Figure 4(K)). Among these, the strongest correlations were observed between CD59 and D-dimer (Spearman r = 0.664), SERPINA1 and D-dimer (Spearman r = 0.644), and SERPINA1 and FDP (Spearman r = 0.630). Collectively, these findings suggested that the candidate biomarkers SERPINA1 and CD59 were correlated with existing parameters and showed potential as indicators of the hypercoagulable state observed in severe COVID-19.

Predictive value of biomarkers for mortality and venous thromboembolism

To evaluate the clinical utility of candidate biomarkers in predicting short-term and long-term outcomes including VTE, sepsis, and mortality, we conducted a 12-month follow-up of all individuals in the validation cohorts. ROC analysis with bootstrap validation showed that SERPINA1 predicted 30-day mortality with an AUC of 0.775 (95% CI: 0.553–0.947) (Figure 5(A)), while the AUC values for 12-month mortality for SERPINA1, CD59, and their combination were 0.924 (95% CI 0.796–1.00), 0.781(95% CI 0.580–0.932), and 0.946(95% CI 0.844–1.00), respectively (Figure 5(B)). Considering the high incidence of VTE in critically ill COVID-19 patients and those with a poor prognosis, we evaluated the capability of candidate biomarkers to predict VTE occurrence. Among these, CD59 (AUC = 0.719, 95% CI 0.519–0.898) demonstrated superior predictive capability for VTE compared to the other biomarkers (Figure 5(C)). Importantly, sepsis, a common complication in critically ill COVID-19 patients, was predicted by SERPINA1 with an AUC of 0.872 (95% CI: 0.752–0.986) and by CD59 with an AUC of 0.720 (95% CI: 0.464–0.962) (Figure 5(D)). For VTE prediction, D-dimer (AUC = 0.733, 95% CI 0.543–0.902) and FDP (AUC = 0.751, 95% CI 0.561–0.919) demonstrated favorable performance (Figure 5(E)). In comparison, SERPINA1 showed relatively higher AUC values for mortality and sepsis prediction than D-dimer or FDP, suggesting that it may have potential as a biomarker for these outcomes (Figure 5(F)).

The prognostic value of the candidate biomarkers was further assessed using age-adjusted Cox models, both SERPINA1 (HR = 1.000216, 95% CI: 1.000076–1.000355, p = 0.002) and CD59 (HR = 1.01911, 95% CI: 1.00599–1.03240, p = 0.004) were significantly associated with all-cause mortality. However, after adjusting for age in Fine–Gray models, neither SERPINA1 (sHR = 1.000067, p = 0.367) nor CD59 (sHR = 1.009147, p = 0.219) was significantly associated with VTE, suggesting they may be potential but non-significant risk factors. Collectively, SERPINA1 and CD59 are linked to mortality but show no significant association with VTE after accounting for competing risks. In addition, multivariable logistic regression analysis adjusting for age and SOFA score showed that SERPINA1 (p = 0.0186) and CD59 (p = 0.0429) were independently associated with mortality (Table S3). These findings suggest that SERPINA1 and CD59 may serve as useful adjunctive biomarkers for risk stratification in COVID-19, particularly for mortality outcomes.

Discussion

In this study, we explored the molecular association between coagulation abnormalities and COVID-19 severity, and identified biomarkers for disease severity and prognosis. Our proteomic data showed that complement and coagulation cascades emerged as a central pathway closely related to disease progression. Among a cluster of proteins correlated with disease severity, the top two coagulation-related proteins were selected as candidate biomarkers and validated in an independent cohort. Notably, the biomarkers could be used to predict COVID-19 outcomes, including short- and long-term mortality, as well as VTE occurrence. Therefore, SERPINA1 and CD59, either alone or in combination, may serve as potential biomarkers for monitoring COVID-19 disease progression and provide reference for clinical management.

Numerous studies have demonstrated abnormal coagulation in COVID-19 patients, which may manifest as distinctive alterations in coagulation and fibrinolytic systems compared to healthy individuals and critically ill patients with sepsis [26]. This led us to hypothesize that novel biomarkers of coagulation in COVID-19 could be identified through proteomic screening and may reflect disease severity and progression. Previous research has sought predictors of COVID-19 outcomes using proteomic approaches. For instance, machine learning models trained on plasma proteomic profiles have been useful in predicting survival in critically ill patients [27]. However, studies have primarily focused on acute inflammation and short-term prognosis or have lacked validation in independent cohorts [28], overlooking coagulation abnormalities and long-term outcomes in severe patients [29,30]. This study, to some extent, addresses the limitations of previous research and provides preliminary evidence for the value of plasma biomarkers in assessing long-term prognosis of COVID-19, particularly for mortality. In our study, classifying patients into Classes I, II, and III based on disease severity revealed a clear escalation in mortality and VTE incidence, with Class III patients showing a 100% mortality rate and an 83.3% VTE incidence. Remarkably, based on this classification, coagulation transitioned from a peripheral to a central role as disease severity increased and was found to be the dominant pathway in severe patients, reinforcing its critical role in disease pathophysiology.

Intriguingly, SERPINA1 and CD59 demonstrated improved predictive performance for mortality in severe patients relative to current clinical indicators such as D-dimer and FDP, although these findings require validation in larger cohorts. While conventional markers such as D-dimer and fibrinogen are commonly used to assess disease severity, their utility is limited by elevated baseline levels in critically ill patients, which impede their capacity to effectively differentiate the stages of disease progression [31,32]. In order to confirm our findings from a relatively small number of patients, future studies involving larger patient populations and possibly multiple centers are needed to confirm the utility of SERPINA1 and CD59, as well as their combination with existing coagulation markers in COVID-19.

Recently, Smadja et al. conducted a comprehensive analysis of 21 biomarkers, including coagulation markers, in the DisCoVeRy trial and identified soluble ST2 (sST2) as a key prognostic biomarker for short‑term outcomes in hospitalized COVID‑19 patients, including time to hospital discharge and improvement in NEWS‑2 score [33]. Their findings, validated in an independent SARCODO cohort, demonstrated that sST2 added significant predictive value to established clinical scores such as ISARIC 4 C and D‑dimer. Our study complements and extends these findings in several important aspects. While Smadja et al. focused on a panel of inflammation‑ and endothelial‑related markers, our proteomic analysis pointed to the coagulation and complement pathways and identified SERPINA1 and CD59 as candidate biomarkers associated with disease severity and long‑term outcomes. Second, whereas their study examined short‑term endpoints (day 29 discharge, day 15 NEWS‑2 change), we provide 12‑month follow‑up data on VTE, sepsis, and mortality, offering novel evidence for the long‑term prognostic value of our candidate biomarkers. Furthermore, we demonstrated that combining SERPINA1 and CD59 with conventional markers (D‑dimer, FDP) yielded high predictive accuracy for mortality, suggesting that a multi‑marker panel may offer additional value. Taken together, both studies highlight the central role of coagulation‑ and complement‑related pathways in COVID‑19 pathophysiology, yet they identify distinct molecular players: sST2 as a mediator of inflammation and fibrosis, and SERPINA1/CD59 as regulators of the protease‑antiprotease balance and complement activation. These complementary findings reflect the multifaceted nature of severe COVID‑19. Future studies integrating these biomarker panels may enable more precise risk stratification and therapeutic targeting.

SERPINA1, also known as alpha-1-antitrypsin [34], is a well‑characterized acute‑phase protein whose plasma levels increase rapidly during inflammation, alongside classical markers such as C‑reactive protein (CRP) and ferritin[35]. It plays a critical role in regulating inflammation and immune responses and is thought to modulate coagulation through its effects on neutrophil elastase and thrombin [36,37]. Its upregulation during acute-phase responses may provide innate defense against SARS-CoV-2 by inhibiting protease activity and preventing the exacerbation of thrombosis associated with inflammation, potentially explaining its increased levels in COVID-19 [38,39]. As for CD59, previous studies have indicated its crucial role as a complement regulatory protein that protects endothelial cells from complement-mediated damage by inhibiting membrane attack complex (MAC) formation [40]. Consistent with this, SARS-CoV-2 infection has been shown to suppress CD59 expression, leading to enhanced endothelial injury and thrombotic microangiopathy [41]. In addition to its role in complement, CD59 indirectly modulates coagulation, possibly through complement inhibition, endothelial protection, and platelet regulation [40]. Therefore, altered levels of soluble CD59 in plasma may reflect both complement activation and endothelial injury, with any effect on coagulation being indirect – primarily through complement-mediated endothelial damage and subsequent prothrombotic changes [41,42]. Further investigation is needed to distinguish the modulation of complement versus coagulation by CD59 and SERPINA1 in COVID-19. Importantly, for the first time, our study identified SERPINA1 and CD59 as key proteins in the pathogenesis of severe COVID-19, suggesting their potential as biomarkers and therapeutic targets. However, the underlying mechanisms behind their altered levels in disease remain to be explored in future studies.

In addition to its well‑recognized role in complement regulation, SERPINA1 (alpha‑1‑antitrypsin) also functions as a key endogenous inhibitor of the kallikrein–kinin system (KKS) and the intrinsic coagulation pathway. As highlighted by Di Micco et al. SERPINA1 directly inhibits plasma kallikrein, thereby limiting bradykinin generation and vascular permeability, a mechanism of particular relevance in COVID‑19‑associated angioedema and acute respiratory distress syndrome [43]. Furthermore, SERPINA1 forms covalent complexes with activated coagulation factor XIa (FXIa), modulating the contact pathway of coagulation. Elevated levels of FXIa:SERPINA1 complexes have been reported in severe COVID‑19, suggesting consumption of antiprotease capacity and sustained contact system activation [44]. These observations suggest that SERPINA1 may lie at the intersection of multiple proteolytic cascades, complement, KKS, and coagulation, highlighting its potential relevance in COVID‑19 pathophysiology and supporting further investigation as a candidate biomarker or a therapeutic target.

Several limitations of this study should be considered. First, the overall sample size remained relatively small, particularly in the severe subgroups and the validation cohort, limiting the statistical power and generalizability of our findings. Second, the biological interpretation of SERPINA1 and CD59 is inherently complex. SERPINA1 is an acute‑phase protein whose elevation may partly reflect systemic inflammation rather than a specific coagulation‑driven process, and CD59 is primarily a complement regulator with indirect effects on coagulation. Given these considerations, our findings should be regarded as exploratory and hypothesis‑generating, warranting validation in larger, well‑controlled prospective cohorts.

In conclusion, this study elucidated the proteomic landscape of coagulation abnormalities in COVID-19 and identified SERPINA1 and CD59 as key plasma biomarkers associated with severe disease. The validated biomarkers show potential application prospects in disease monitoring and prognostic assessment.
