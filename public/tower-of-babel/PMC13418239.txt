Utilizing a Bi24O31Br10/MIL-88A(Fe)-derived mixed-phase α,β-Fe2O3@C heterojunction photocatalyst for the visible-light-driven degradation of resorcinol
Authors: Jyoti Kumari, Adarsh Singh, Ashok Kumar Gupta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In recent years, pernicious organic pollutants, including pharmaceuticals and personal care products (PPCPs), have been ubiquitous in water bodies. Resorcinol (RCL), as a major constituent of PPCPs, is used extensively in industries and households, contributing to its occurrence in water matrices. Owing to the persistence and endocrine-disrupting potential of RCL, its complete mineralization is imperative. In this regard, Bi24O31Br10/Fe2O3@C composites were synthesized via a solvothermal route, wherein mixed-phase α,β-Fe2O3@C was obtained through the thermal conversion of MIL-88A(Fe). Among the as-prepared samples, including the individual components, the heterostructure containing 5 wt% α,β-Fe2O3@C (Bi24O31Br10/α,β-Fe2O3@C-5%, denoted as BOBFC(5%)) exhibited the highest photocatalytic degradation in 10 mg L−1 RCL (98.8%) after 180 min of visible light irradiation. The enhanced performance of BOBFC(5%) is attributed to its increased specific surface area, narrower band gap, and reduced charge-carrier recombination, as validated by the Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET), UV-vis diffuse reflectance spectroscopy (UV-DRS), and photoluminescence (PL) spectroscopy results. Moreover, the efficient charge transfer across the heterointerface resulted in the generation of superoxide (˙O2−) radicals and holes (h+), which combinedly contributed to the photocatalytic activity. Furthermore, the agar well diffusion test against E. coli exhibited no visible inhibition zone, indicating the non-toxic behaviour of BOBFC(5%). Subsequently, BOBFC(5%) retained its degradation efficacy over 5 consecutive cycles, highlighting its remarkable stability. Additionally, the in silico evaluation revealed that the transformation products formed during the photocatalysis of RCL were less toxic. Hence, this work presents an efficient novel photocatalyst that can be used in sustainable wastewater treatment systems.

Introduction

Pharmaceuticals and personal care products (PPCPs) have become a significant source of contamination of water resources.1 In accordance with the United States Environmental Protection Agency (USEPA), any substance that is utilized for health or cosmetic purposes, to increase agricultural production or to maintain livestock health can be termed as PPCPs.2 Generally, pharmaceuticals consist of antibiotics, antipsychotic drugs, β-blockers, hormones, analgesics, antidepressants, etc., while personal care products (PCPs) consist of disinfectants, preservatives, fragrances, insect repellents, UV filters, etc.2 The production and demand of PPCPs are increasing; specifically, the global pharmaceutical market is expected to reach US$ 2776.74 billion by 2033, with a compounded annual growth rate of 6.08% from 2026 to 2033.3 Hence, their extensive use leads to the discharge of PPCPs in water bodies through various sources, including domestic discharges, hospital effluents, pharmaceutical industries, sewage treatment plants (STPs), and landfill leachates.4 For example, resorcinol (RCL) that is used in the preparation of PPCPs poses significant risks to human health and aquatic ecosystems by interfering with biological and endocrine functions and exhibiting peroxidative capacity.5–8

Resorcinol is widely used across several industries, including tanneries and food processing, and products such as adhesives, resins, dyes, and flame retardants.7 In addition, RCL has been used to treat skin infections, acne, and hyperpigmentation as well as in anti-ageing products.8,9 However, repeated consumption of RCL can cause endocrine disruption, leading to hormonal imbalance and altered growth. It also inhibits thyroid peroxidase activity, which can, in turn, cause hypothyroidism.9,10 Yet, there is limited knowledge regarding the environmental persistence and toxicological impacts of RCL compared to those of other PPCPs. Reportedly, studies in rats have shown that over 90% of ingested RCL is excreted within 24 h via urine,11 indicating a potentially similar excretion pathway in humans following absorption via dermal, oral, and subcutaneous routes.9 As a result, substantial amounts of unmetabolized RCL are discharged into wastewater streams, leading to its persistence in aquatic environments and highlighting the need for efficient remediation technologies. Although industrial and domestic effluents pass through wastewater treatment plants, conventional treatment employing physical, chemical, and biological processes cannot mineralize RCL.12 Contrarily, advanced oxidation technologies (AOTs), particularly photocatalysis, have recently attracted much attention due to their simplicity, mineralization potential, eco-friendliness, and low toxicity.13,14 Among various visible-light-driven photocatalysts, bismuth (Bi)-based materials have been widely explored in recent years due to their superior photocatalytic removal efficiency. In particular, bismuth oxyhalides (BiOX, where X = Br, Cl, and I) have been extensively studied owing to their unique physical and chemical properties, such as an open crystalline structure and indirect optical transition.15,16 In the case of indirect band gap (Eg) semiconductors, electron transitions require phonon assistance due to momentum mismatch, which lowers the probability of electron–hole recombination and prolongs the charge-carrier lifetime.16,17 BiOX exhibits a tetragonal matlockite structure (PbFCl-type), characterized by [Bi2O2] layers that are interwoven with double layers of halogen ions.18,19 All [X-Bi-O-Bi-X] layers consist of Bi as the central element encircled by four oxygen and halogen atoms, generating an asymmetric decahedral structure.20,21 Notably, halogens in oxyhalides enhance photocatalytic activity due to their electronegativity.22

Recently, research focus has shifted towards bismuth oxybromide (BiOBr) as a potent photocatalyst. However, BiOBr still suffers from a limited visible-light absorption range, with a broad Eg of 2.6–2.9 eV,23 suggesting the need for further improvements. In this regard, several oxygen-rich modifications of BiOBr have been comprehensively reported, primarily including Bi24O31Br10.24 Although the Bi24O31Br10 has a relatively narrow Eg, its photocatalytic activity remains limited due to the rapid recombination rate of photogenerated charge carriers. Addressing this limitation, a few heterojunctions of Bi24O31Br10 have been reported to suppress charge recombination and reduce Eg. For example, AgBr/Bi24O31Br10,25 graphene-Bi24O31Br10,26 and Bi24O31Br10/NiFe2O4 (ref. 27) are heterostructures that exhibit enhanced photocatalytic performance compared to pristine Bi24O31Br10. In view of this, we incorporated MIL-88A-derived Fe2O3@C with Bi24O31Br10 (Bi24O31Br10/α,β-Fe2O3@C, abbreviated as BOBFC). In general, metal–organic frameworks (MOFs) are characterized by high specific surface area (SSA), rich topologies, large specific pore volumes, tunability, and porous structures.28 MOFs consist of metal nodes and organic ligands arranged in a crystal lattice. Owing to ligand-to-metal charge transfer (LMCT) and the activation of metal-oxo clusters, several metal–organic frameworks (MOFs) such as MIL-88A(Fe),30 MIL-88B(Fe),31 and MIL-101(Fe)32 exhibit semiconductor-like properties.29 Among them, MIL-88A(Fe) features a three-dimensional framework comprising octahedrally coordinated Fe3+ centres bridged by oxygen atoms from fumarate ligands. Moreover, the pyrolysis of MIL-88A(Fe) at high temperatures can produce a carbon-anchored material with Fe nanoparticles (NPs) bound to its surface while retaining its refined morphological structure and SSA.33 Reportedly, at 450 °C, the pyrolysis of MIL-88A leads to the formation of carbon-coated Fe2O3 (Fe2O3@C).34 It has been widely incorporated into other materials to augment active sites, and the carbon coating prevents the agglomeration of Fe2O3 NPs during the reaction, thereby enhancing the photocatalytic performance.35,36

In this study, a novel heterojunction Bi24O31Br10/α,β-Fe2O3@C composite was prepared using a solvothermal approach. Various BOBFC composites were prepared by varying the amount of Fe2O3@C as per the proportion of Bi in bismuth nitrate pentahydrate, the precursor of Bi24O31Br10. The photocatalytic ability of the BOBFC composites was examined over RCL under visible light irradiation for various operational parameters in water matrices. The as-synthesized composite (BOBFC(5%)) was characterized for the analysis of its physical and chemical properties using several material characterization techniques. The toxicity assessment of the transformation products (TPs) formed during the mineralization of RCL and photocatalyst was done in silico and in vivo, respectively, along with the stability and reusability of the composite.

Materials

Composite preparation

Preparation of MIL-88A(Fe)-derived Fe2O3@C

With a slight modification to a methodology previously reported, MIL-88A(Fe)-derived Fe2O3@C was prepared.34 Briefly, 3.43 g of ferric chloride hexahydrate (FeCl3·6H2O) and 1.47 g of fumaric acid (C4H4O4) were dissolved in 65 mL of dimethyl formamide (DMF) (C3H7NO) and stirred at 300 rpm for half an hour. This solution was then kept in an autoclave solvothermal reactor at 110 °C for 12 h. After the autoclave cooled to room temperature, the obtained material was rinsed multiple times with DI water, ethanol, and acetone. This was followed by keeping the resulting material at 60 °C overnight. Subsequently, the obtained MIL-88A(Fe) was calcinated in a muffle furnace at 450 °C for 4 h at a rate of 2 °Cmin−1, resulting in brownish-colored Fe2O3@C, as shown in Fig. 1a.

Synthesis procedure for (a) MIL-88A(Fe)-derived Fe2O3@C and (b) Bi24O31Br10/Fe2O3@C (BOBFC) composites.

Preparation of Bi24O31Br10/Fe2O3@C composites

0.970 g of bismuth nitrate pentahydrate (Bi(NO3)3·5H2O) was added to 10 mL of ethylene glycol (C2H6O2), followed by ultrasonication for 20 min. A separate solution was prepared by adding 0.196 g of ammonium bromide (NH4Br) to 25 mL of DI under rapid mixing for 10 min. The latter solution was mixed with the former one, and 1.2 mL of ethanolamine (C2H7NO) was added dropwise. Subsequently, a certain amount of Fe2O3@C was introduced, and the mixture was stirred continuously for 30 min. The final solution was then kept in an autoclave solvothermal reactor and maintained at 160 °C for 12 h, as depicted in Fig. 1b. After naturally cooling to ambient temperature, the obtained light pink-colored material was washed several times with DI water and ethanol, followed by drying overnight at 60 °C. The as-synthesized composites were denoted as BOBFC(2.5%), BOBFC(5%), and BOBFC(10%) based on the Fe2O3@C content. The Fe2O3@C loading (2.5–10 wt%) was varied proportionally with respect to the Bi content in Bi(NO3)3·5H2O. Notably, pristine Bi24O31Br10 was synthesized following a previously reported procedure37 by omitting the introduction of Fe2O3@C, yielding a yellow-coloured material.

Results and discussion

Structure, morphology, composition and surface characteristics

Crystallinity, phase determination and topographical features

The crystallinity and phase analyses of MIL 88A(Fe), MIL-88A-derived Fe2O3@C, Bi24O31Br10, and Bi24O31Br10/Fe2O3@C were performed with the help of X-ray diffraction (XRD). As shown in Fig. 2a, the diffraction peaks of MIL-88A(Fe) at 2θ values of 8°, 10.3°, and 13.2° belong to the (100), (101), and (002) crystallographic planes, respectively, and are consistent with the reference code (CCDC-644019).39,40 The XRD pattern of MIL-88 A-derived Fe2O3@C exhibited diffraction peaks at 32.9°, 35.6°, and 49.3°, corresponding to the (104), (110), and (024) planes of α-Fe2O3 (JCPDS No. 00-033-0664, Fig. S2a), with space group R-3c and unit cell parameters a = 5.0356 Å, b = 5.0356 Å, and c = 13.7489 Å (α = 90°, β = 90°, and γ = 120°).41,42 The additional peaks at 38.3°, 45.2°, and 55.2° can be indexed to the (400), (332), and (440) crystallographic planes, respectively, of β-Fe2O3 (JCPDS 00-039-0238) (Fig. S2b),43 thereby confirming the formation of the mixed-phase (α and β) of Fe2O3. β-Fe2O3 belongs to the space group Ia-3 and has the lattice parameters of a = 9.40 Å, b = 9.40 Å, and c = 9.40 Å (α = 90°, β = 90°, and γ = 90°). Furthermore, the characteristic diffraction peaks of Bi24O31Br10 are observed at 29.8°, 31.8°, and 57.0°, which are assigned to its (213), (117), and (011) crystal planes, respectively, supported by the JCPDS card No. 01-075-0888 (Fig. S2c).44,45 For the as-synthesized BOBFC(5%) composite, the characteristic peaks of both the pristine materials are well-positioned at their respective 2θ values. For illustration, the peaks at 29.8°, 31.8°, and 57° resemble Bi24O31Br10, while the peaks at 35.6°, 45.2°, and 55.2° are reasonably aligned with Fe2O3.

Moreover, as depicted in Fig. 2b, Rietveld refinement was performed using the GSAS-II software to quantitatively analyze the phases of Fe2O3.46,47 The obtained statistical indicators-χ2 = 1.06, goodness of fit (GOF) = 1.03 and wR = 9.028-confirmed the reliability of refinement. The refined scale factors for the α-Fe2O3 and β-Fe2O3 phases are 0.3265 and 0.1937, corresponding to the weight fractions of 0.387 and 0.613, respectively.

Atomic force microscopy (AFM) was used for the determination of the surface topography of the BOBFC(5%) composite (Fig. 2c). Fig. 2d shows 83.1 nm as the root mean square height and 54.8 nm as the arithmetic mean height of the composite. The positive skewness value of 1.54 revealed the presence of protrusions on the surface of the photocatalyst. Additionally, the kurtosis value of 6.39 explained the sharp peaks of the composite. Moreover, the maximum peak and valley heights were 402 nm and 251 nm, respectively. These results explain the composite's surface roughness, which may lead to a higher surface area, providing more active sites and facilitating photocatalytic activity.

Morphological characteristics

Field emission gun-scanning electron microscopy (FEG-SEM) was used for analyzing the morphological features of the as-synthesized photocatalysts. Compared with previous studies, the spindle shape of MIL-88A (Fe) was distorted due to high-temperature synthesis for a very long duration. The prolonged preparation conditions changed the 3D structure into an irregular, rough 2D shape (Fig. 5a). Additionally, the calcination of the prepared MIL-88 A(Fe) led to the formation of α- and β-Fe2O3, which was further supported by the XRD observations. The porous aggregated nanospheres of carbon-anchored mixed-phase Fe2O3 are illustrated in Fig. 5b.43,60 The pristine Bi24O31Br10 has demonstrated a nanoflake-like morphology with irregular orientation (Fig. 5c). The FEG-SEM image (Fig. 5d) of the composite reveals the aggregation of mixed-phase α,β-Fe2O3@C over the nanoflakes, which is expected to contribute to an increase in the Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) SSA. In addition, the EDS spectrum of BOBFC(5%) presented in Fig. 5e confirms the presence of Fe, Bi, Br, O, and C, consistent with the XPS results. The corresponding elemental mapping reveals a uniform distribution of these elements (Fig. 5f).

Consistent with the SEM micrographs, BOBFC(5%) exhibited a similar morphology in the high resolution-transmission electron microscopy (HR-TEM) results, as presented in Fig. 6a and b. Textural characteristics

The N2 adsorption–desorption study was conducted to analyze the SSA and pore-size distribution, thereby examining the textural features of the as-prepared materials. Initially, the samples were degassed at 150 °C for 8 h. The BET surface areas of Fe2O3@C, Bi24O31Br10, and BOBFC(5%) were found to be 15.30, 16.42, and 18.90 m2 g−1, respectively. The total pore volume computed at P/P0 = 0.99 was 0.146 cm3 g−1 for Fe2O3@C, 0.183 cm3 g−1 for Bi24O31Br10, and 0.161 cm3 g−1 for BOBFC(5%). Additionally, the Barrett–Joyner–Halenda (BJH) pore volume graph of the materials depicted in Fig. 6h shows that pore volume is distributed over a broad pore size range. In alignment with these results, it can be seen that Fe2O3@C exhibited a low pore volume (0.146 cm3 g−1) with mesopores majorly distributed in the range of 10–40 nm. In contrast, pristine Bi24O31Br10 had a moderate surface area but the highest pore volume (0.183 cm3 g−1), which can reasonably be ascribed to the presence of large mesopores and macropores, resulting from the interparticle gaps between the irregularly oriented nanoflakes. These large pores contributed to increased pore volume even at a moderate surface area. Notably, the BOBFC(5%) composite exhibited the highest SSA despite its lower pore volume (0.161 cm3 g−1) than that of pristine Bi24O31Br10, as supported by the BJH plot (Fig. 6h), which shows a sharp and pronounced peak at 12–15 nm, indicating mesopores up to 80 nm. The predominance of small mesopores can be the appropriate reason for the increased surface area. Also, this behaviour is consistent with the FEG-SEM observations (Fig. 5d), where similar aggregation features were identified, suggesting their possible contribution to the increased SSA. Thus, these analyses explicitly demonstrate the synergistic integration of MIL-88A(Fe)-derived Fe2O3@C and Bi24O31Br10, which is anticipated to enhance the photocatalytic degradation efficiency A summary of the BET and BJH analyses is provided in Table S3. Fig. 6g shows an H3 hysteresis loop following a type IV isotherm at relative pressures of P/P0 > 0.8, highlighting the presence of a mesoporous material with slit-like pores.61,62

Band structure and optical properties

The electronic band structure and optical absorption characteristics of the as-prepared materials were determined by UV-vis diffuse reflectance spectroscopy (UV-DRS). As revealed in Fig. 7a, the absorption edges of MIL-88A(Fe)-derived Fe2O3@C, Bi24O31Br10, and BOBFC(5%) are identified at 726 nm, 482 nm, and 517 nm, respectively. It is worth noting that the prepared BOBFC(5%) composite exhibited a red shift as compared to Bi24O31Br10, showing slightly enhanced visible light absorption potential (Fig. 7a). The Eg of the materials was estimated using the Tauc plot based on the Kubelka–Munk (KM) method given in eqn (1):631αhν = A(hν − Eg)n/2,where α denotes the absorption coefficient, h is the Planck's constant, ν is the frequency, A is a constant, Eg is the band gap energy, and the value of n depends on the type of semiconductor transition. Here, n = 4 is used for indirect transition, as reported in the literature for the BiOX family (X = Cl, Br, and I).64 Then, the Eg of Fe2O3@C, Bi24O31Br10, and BOBFC(5%) was calculated by plotting (αhν)1/2vs. hν, yielding the values of 1.43 eV, 2.29 eV, and 1.79 eV, respectively. Of note, the introduction of 5% Fe2O3@C into Bi24O31Br10 narrowed the Eg (Fig. 7b), permitting the utilization of a wider spectrum of visible light.

Photoluminescence (PL) spectroscopy was employed to evaluate charge recombination. The solid-state PL spectra of Fe2O3@C, Bi24O31Br10, and BOBFC(5%) at an excitation wavelength of 270 nm are presented in Fig. 7c. As expected, the PL intensity centered at 470 nm is markedly lower for the BOBFC(5%) composite than for the pristine Bi24O31Br10 material. The reduced PL intensity indicates suppressed recombination of photoinduced charge carriers after heterojunction formation, thereby facilitating efficient charge separation.

Adsorption and photocatalytic degradation of RCL

The photocatalytic efficiency of MIL-88A-derived Fe2O3@C, pristine Bi24O31Br10, BOBFC(2.5%), BOBFC(5%), and BOBFC(10%) for RCL degradation was examined under visible light. Initially, each material (dose = 1 g L−1) was mixed with 50 mL of RCL solution (initial concentration = 10 mg L−1) and stirred in the absence of light for 30 min to achieve adsorption–desorption equilibrium. The adsorption of RCL on the photocatalysts was found to be within 5–20% depending on the material (Fig. 8a). Afterwards, the photocatalytic efficiency attained by Bi24O31Br10, Fe2O3@C, BOBFC(2.5%), BOBFC(5%), and BOBFC(10%) was found to be 62.1%, 23.9%, 87.5%, 98.8%, and 90.2% for RCL degradation under 180 min of light exposure (Fig. 8a). The addition of BOBFC(5%) substantially increased the RCL degradation efficiency; it provided the maximum degradation efficiency among all the considered materials. This can be synergistically attributed to the enhanced visible light absorption ability and superior charge separation efficiency, as validated by the UV-DRS and PL spectroscopy results mentioned above. Factors influencing the photocatalytic degradation of RCL

To evaluate the role of the photocatalyst dosage, different concentrations (0.1, 0.25, 0.5, 1, and 2 g L−1) of the BOBFC(5%) photocatalyst were employed for the degradation of RCL at an initial concentration of 10 mg L−1. The degradation rates were substantially enhanced from 0.0004 min−1 to 0.0295 min−1 with an increase in the BOBFC(5%) dose from 0.1 g L−1 to 1 g L−1, respectively, boosting the photocatalytic efficiency from 15% to 98.8% correspondingly (Fig. 8b and S5b). Due to the increase in active surface sites, which are responsible for the generation of radicals, the degradation efficiency was also enhanced. Contrarily, upon further increasing the dose from 1 g L−1 to 2 g L−1, the degradation rate and efficiency dropped to 0.0134 min−1 and 90.1%, respectively. This could be augmented by the agglomeration of the photocatalyst or the shielding effect that occurs due to the turbidity.65

With the variation in the initial concentration from 1 mg L−1 to 10 mg L−1, the reaction rates were boosted from 0.0108 min−1 to 0.0295 min−1, respectively, and the improvement in degradation efficiency was observed from 82.1% to 98.8% correspondingly. However, the subsequent increase in C0 from 10 mg L−1 to 20 mg L−1 led to a drop in the reaction rate to 0.0100 min−1 and a decline in efficiency to 83.12%, ascribed to the full occupation of the active surface sites of the photocatalyst (Fig. 8c and S5c).66 The saturation of active sites on the surface of the catalyst lowers the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS).67

The effect of pH on the photocatalytic mineralization of RCL was observed, ranging from lower to higher pH values (3–11). The pH of the solution was adjusted using 1 M HCl and 0.5 M NaOH. As reported in previous studies, RCL undergoes deprotonation at pKa1 = 9.44 and pKa2 = 12.32 (eqn (3) and (4)):683C6H4(OH)2 + H2O ↔ C6H4(OH)(O)− + H3O+ pKa1 = 9.444C6H4(OH)(O)− + H2O ↔ C6H4(O)2− + H3O+ pKa2 = 12.32

RCL predominantly exists in the neutral form at pH < pKa1 and converts into a negatively charged form at pH > pKa1.69 The charge variation on the surface of the photocatalyst was analyzed by zeta potential as a function of solution pH. BOBFC(5%) exhibited two isoelectric points (IEPs) at pH 5.25 and 8.24, where the zeta potential turned out to be zero.70 It can be attributed to different surface sites,71 electronic structure,72 and the presence of different phases of Fe2O3.73 The point of zero charge (pzc) represents the overall neutrality of the solid surface of the material.71,74 The surface was positively charged between pH 5.25 and 8.24 and negatively charged at other pH values,14,75 as mentioned in Fig. 8d.

The influence of pH on the photocatalytic process, along with changes in the degradation rate constant, was assessed in correlation with the surface charges of the photocatalyst, pKa values of RCL, and observed degradation efficiencies (Fig. 8e and S5d). Under acidic conditions, 64% and 88.57% efficiencies were achieved, ascribed to the rapid protonation of superoxide (˙O2−) radicals into ˙HO2 radicals, thereby lowering the concentration of ˙O2−.76 Moreover, ˙HO2 has different redox potential and reactivity towards RCL, resulting in a reduction in the photocatalytic efficiency under acidic conditions. As the pH increased to a neutral state, efficiency was found to be the maximum, which further decreased upon increasing the pH. The efficiency dropped from 80.91% at pH 9 to 69.92% at pH 11, associated with increased electrostatic repulsion between the negatively charged photocatalyst surface and the anionic form of the RCL molecule.77 The maximum efficiency was observed to be 98.8% at pH 7 due to the absence of any repulsion and adequate generation of ˙O2−.

The effects of co-existing ions and natural organic matter (NOM) on the visible-light-driven catalytic activity were investigated (Fig. 8f). The influence of cations (Al3+ and Fe2+), anions (SO42−, PO43−, Cl−, HCO3−, and NO3−) and humic acid as NOM was tested for the optimized condition of 1 g L−1 of catalyst dose and 10 mg L−1 of initial concentration of RCL. The photocatalytic degradation was reduced to 69.8%, 92.7%, 79.8%, 60.2%, and 66.15% in the presence of Cl−, NO3−, HCO3−, SO42−, and PO43−, respectively. Majorly,Cl−, SO42−, and PO43− had a pronounced negative effect on the photocatalytic efficiency. This drop in efficiency might be because of the competition of Cl− and SO42− ions to bind with the active sites on the catalyst's surface and their reaction with the generated ROS.78 Moreover, phosphate ions possess an acidic nature and may bind to the reactive site, which may also lead to a deterioration in efficiency.79 Further, the presence of Fe2+ significantly decreased the degradation efficiency to 57.5%, and the presence of Al3+ led to a slight reduction to 89.9%. Fe2+ ions adversely affected the degradation efficiency of RCL, ascribed to the formation of a complex, which led to a decline in catalytic efficiency.80 Additionally, the introduction of humic acid diminished the catalytic efficiency, reducing it to 42.7%.81

The large-scale applicability and practical viability of BOBFC(5%) were studied in several water matrices, including DI water, river water, secondary effluent wastewater, and pond water. The physico–chemical water quality parameters for these different water samples are summarized in Table S5. The lowest degradation efficiency of 78.4% was observed in secondary effluent wastewater, with the highest efficiency of 98.8% observed in DI water; the lowest degradation efficiency observed in secondary effluent wastewater can be probably attributed to the higher turbidity and presence of suspended solids in it, which may obstruct light penetration. As depicted in Fig. 8g, a reduction in efficiency was also observed in river water (89.8%) and pond water (90.5%), mainly ascribed to the presence of inorganic ions and induced turbidity in surface water. Hence, the results aligned with the literature and suggest for the appropriate pre-treatment of water matrices before photocatalysis for minimal interference, which will enhance photocatalytic degradation efficiency.82

Photocatalytic degradation mechanism of RCL

The VB spectra of Fe2O3@C and pristine Bi24O31Br10 were obtained using XPS, as provided in Fig. 9a and b, illustrating their VB potential (EVB) to be 0.91 and 1.94 eV, respectively. The conduction band potential (ECB) was determined using eqn (5):5ECB = EVB − Eg

Hence, the ECB of Fe2O3@C and pristine Bi24O31Br10 turns out to be −0.52 and −0.35 eV, respectively. The irradiation of visible light leads to the creation of charge carriers in Bi24O31Br10 and Fe2O3@C. As depicted in Fig. 9c, the more negative CB of Fe2O3@C facilitates the movement of e− towards the less negative CB of Bi24O31Br10. Consequently, e− gets accumulated on the CB of Bi24O31Br10, which has an ECB value that is more negative than the standard redox potential of O2/˙O2− (−0.33 eV/NHE), indicating the sufficient potential of e− to react with O2 and resulting in the generation of ˙O2−. Moreover, the VB of Bi24O31Br10 (+1.94 eV) is significantly more positive than the VB of Fe2O3@C (+0.91 eV), causing the migration of h+ from Bi24O31Br10 to Fe2O3@C. Contrarily, the VB potential of both Bi24O31Br10 and Fe2O3@C is less positive than the redox potentials for ˙OH generation, indicating that ˙OH is not produced either through water (1.99 eV/NHE) or hydroxide (2.34 eV/NHE) oxidation. Therefore, the plausible mechanism and charge transfer pathway support the conventional type II heterojunction in the BOBFC composite, managing the spatial separation of e− and h+ on each side of the heterojunction. Accordingly, the degradation of RCL was mainly facilitated by ˙O2− and h+, consistent with the scavenging study and electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) analysis in the subsequent section.

Mineralization potential of the photocatalyst

As illustrated in Fig. S6, the mineralization potential of BOBFC(5%) was analyzed using total organic carbon (TOC) measurement. The TOC measurement exhibited a final 72.8% mineralization of RCL after 180 min of visible light irradiation for C0 = 10 mg L−1 and a catalyst dose of 1 g L−1, suggesting the breakdown of RCL molecules into CO2, H2O and other compounds. Notably, ˙O2− and h+ majorly contribute to the degradation and formation of intermediate products, which undergo further transformation.

Stability and regeneration ability of the photocatalyst

The structural stability and reusability of BOBFC(5%) were studied for five repetitive photocatalytic cycles. After each repetitive cycle, the BOBFC(5%) composite was collected by filtration, thoroughly rinsed with DI water and ethanol several times, and then dried for 12 h at 60 °C temperature in an oven. The composite exhibited a 16% decline in the photocatalytic degradation efficiency for RCL upon the completion of five consecutive runs, as depicted in Fig. 8h. The subtle decrease in efficiency may be attributed to the blocking of active sites by the adsorbed contaminant molecules on the catalyst surface. In addition, there may be a possibility of agglomeration of the catalyst during repetitive cycles. However, the morphology of the reused catalyst remained the same as that of pristine BOBFC(5%), proving the stability of the as-synthesized composite (Fig. S7).

Radical scavenging study

To interpret the mechanism of the photocatalytic degradation of RCL over BOBFC(5%), a radical scavenging study was conducted. Isopropanol (IPA), potassium iodide (KI), ascorbic acid (AA), and sodium thiosulfate (STF) were used as scavengers, each with a concentration of 1 mmol L−1 for ˙OH, h+, ˙O2−, and H2O2, respectively. The addition of IPA, KI, AA and STF decreased the degradation efficiency to 92.60%, 58.43%, 34.5%, and 90.95%, respectively (Fig. 10a). IPA and STF had a very minimal impact on the catalytic activity. It is worth noting that AA caused greater inhibition than other scavengers, suggesting the dominance of ˙O2− among other photogenerated radicals. Moreover, KI had a pronounced impact on the photocatalytic degradation of RCL. Thus, majorly ˙O2−, followed by h+, significantly influenced the photocatalytic efficiency of BOBFC(5%) under visible light irradiation. This is also validated by the EPR spectra of ˙O2− in Fig. 10b and is aligned with the proposed photocatalytic mechanism.

Eco-toxicity assessment of BOBFC(5%)

The agar well diffusion test was utilized for the toxicity assessment of BOBFC(5%) against E. coli, as depicted in Fig. 10c. The test showed an inhibition zone growth of 2.71 cm around the positive control, implying the toxic effect of LFX on E. coli. Contrarily, no inhibition zone was found around BOBFC(5%), showing similarity with the surroundings of the negative control and presenting the absence of anti-bacterial activity. This exhibits the non-toxic property of the prepared composite towards E. coli.14 Although the agar well diffusion test supports the non-toxicity of the composite towards bacterial activity, it requires comprehensive assessment for the general toxicity analysis over other species.

Plausible degradation pathways

The possible degradation pathways of RCL over BOBFC(5%) were observed using the liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LCMS) technique, as mentioned in Table S6. As per the reported studies, the molecules of RCL are susceptible to being attacked by ˙OH radical, ˙O2− radical, photogenerated holes, and the oxygen dissolved in water.7 Three pathways in blue, green and red colours are shown in Fig. 11a. The attacking mechanism over the RCL (m/z = 110) initiated by the photogenerated radical forms the hydroxylated intermediate compound in the form of ortho and para isomers represented as TP1 and TP2 (m/z = 126).12 Further, breaking of the benzyl ring is observed during the photocatalysis process owing to the attack of ˙O2− and h+, producing TP3 (m/z = 147) and TP4 (m/z = 130). TP3 and TP4 are subsequently oxidized to smaller aliphatic carboxylic acids, namely, TP5 (m/z = 43), TP6 (m/z = 89), TP7 (m/z = 106), and TP8 (m/z = 104). On the other pathway, RCL is directly oxidized to produce TP9 (m/z = 108), and its subsequent oxidation leads to TP10 (m/z = 116), TP11 (m/z = 46), and TP12 (m/z = 90). Further breakdown of TP12 produces TP13 (m/z = 73). These TPs are finally degraded into low-molecular-weight products and then mineralized into CO2 and H2O.8,64

The toxicity assessment of RCL and its TPs was performed using the TEST software. Based on the globally harmonized system of classification and labelling of substances (GHS), TPs were classified into nonharmful (LC50 > 100 mg L−1), harmful (10 < LC50 ≥ 100 mg L−1), toxic (1 < LC50 ≥ 10 mg L−1), and highly toxic (LC50 ≤ 1 mg L−1), where LC50 is the 50% lethal concentration, implying the concentration in air or water causing 50% death of the test species.14,22,83,84 Furthermore, the toxicity of TPs on Daphnia magna, Fathead minnow, and their corresponding bioconcentration factors were assessed (Fig. 11b–d). The median lethal concentration factor for Daphnia and minnow is estimated at LC50, 48 h and LC50, 96 h, respectively. The LC values for RCL and its possible TPs, along with their bioconcentration factors, are provided in Table S7. The toxicity of most TPs was reduced with respect to RCL following photocatalytic degradation, ascribing to the inclusion of RCL in the harmful category. As per the analysis, TPs are found to be less harmful than the initial contaminant, except for TP1 and TP2. Moreover, a significant reduction in the bioconcentration factor of TPs was observed compared to that of RCL, except for TP9.

Conclusions

A novel mixed-phase photocatalyst Bi24O31Br10/α,β-Fe2O3@C is synthesized, which efficiently degraded RCL under the illumination of visible light. Results showed the notable improvement in the BET surface area of the BOBFC(5%) composite after the introduction of MIL-88A(Fe)-derived α,β Fe2O3@C, which contributed as a main factor for achieving 98.8% RCL degradation at an optimal composite dose of 1 g L−1 with C0 of 10 mg L−1 over 180 min of visible light irradiation. UV-DRS and PL spectra showed a remarkable reduction in band gap, which provided a wider range of light absorption and decreased charge carrier recombination in the composite, respectively. Moreover, the scavenging and EPR studies in correlation with the possible charge transfer pathways established ˙O2− and h+ as the major ROS. Additionally, the in vivo toxicity assessment by the agar well diffusion test proved the composite as a non-toxic material, and the in silico assessment using TEST validated the less harmful nature of TPs compared with RCL. Furthermore, this work demonstrated the structural stability and regeneration ability of the as-synthesized composite for 5 continuous cycles. In conclusion, a stable and efficient Bi24O31Br10/α,β-Fe2O3@C photocatalytic material is synthesized, especially for degrading RCL, offering the best remedy for wastewater treatment.
