CT and finite element analysis of mandibular advancement device mechanics in a participant with obstructive sleep apnea: a proof-of-concept study
Authors: Ying Yang, Qian Zhang, Pei Li, Ningning Zhang, Panjun Pu, Yuxia Hou, Qing Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study constructed three-dimensional finite element (3D-FE) models of the upper airway and surrounding structures from spiral computed tomography (CT) data. An optimized fluid-structure interaction (FSI) analysis was applied to investigate the biomechanical effects of the mandibular advancement device (MAD) on peri-airway tissues, refining its therapeutic mechanism in obstructive sleep apnea (OSA).MethodsOne adult patient diagnosed with moderate OSA underwent CT scans in natural sleep before and after 2 months of MAD therapy. 3D-FE models of the upper airway and surrounding structures were constructed from CT data to quantify morphological changes and displacements of the hyoid bone, soft palate, tongue, epiglottis, and the upper airway. FSI analysis was performed to assess airflow pressure and velocity in both the sagittal plane and cross-sections of the upper airway, as well as the displacement of the aforementioned soft tissues, at both peak inspiration and expiration.ResultsMAD-induced mandibular protrusion resulted in anterosuperior displacement of the tongue, soft palate, epiglottis, and hyoid bone, accompanied by shortening of the soft palate and lengthening of the epiglottis. These alterations dilated the upper airway in both sagittal and coronal dimensions, thereby increasing airway volume and minimum cross-sectional area. MAD also reduced and homogenized airflow pressure and velocity in both the sagittal plane and key cross-sections of the upper airway at peak inspiration and expiration, while attenuating the displacement of the pharyngeal wall, tongue, soft palate, and epiglottis, with effects more pronounced at peak inspiration.ConclusionsMAD treats OSA through a synergistic biomechanical mechanism. It enlarges the pharyngeal airway via anterosuperior repositioning of peri-airway tissues. This resultant upper airway dilation attenuates and homogenizes airflow pressure and velocity, thereby stabilizing the pharyngeal wall and peripharyngeal structures, with more pronounced effects during inspiration.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-026-08818-1.Keywords: Obstructive sleep apnea, Mandibular advancement device, Fluid-structure interaction, Airway pressure, Airflow velocity, Tissue displacement

Background

Obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) is the most prevalent sleep disorder. Its primary etiologies encompass upper airway anatomical variants, soft tissue hypertrophy/hyperplasia, and diminished neuromuscular tone of upper airway dilator muscles. These factors collectively induce upper airway collapse and narrowing, leading to recurrent partial/complete upper airway collapse during sleep with subsequent hypopnea or apnea [1]. The prevalence of OSA in adults with an apnea-hypopnea index (AHI) ≥ 5 events/h ranges from approximately 9% to 38%. It is higher in men and increases with age, reaching up to 90% in elderly men and 78% in elderly women. For an AHI ≥ 15 events/h, the prevalence in the general adult population varies from 6% to 17% and can be as high as 49% in older age groups [2]. OSA poses severe hazards to human health. In children, it impairs craniofacial development as well as physical and mental health [3]. In adults, it causes daytime sleepiness, decreased cognitive function, and multi-organ and multi-system damage such as inducing cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases, with severe cases even leading to asphyxiation [4, 5]. Expanding the upper airway is the key to treating OSA, and conservative treatment, continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP), surgical treatment, and oral appliance (OA) therapy are the four common treatment methods [6]. As the most common OA, mandibular advancement device (MAD) is an effective treatment for patients with mild to moderate OSA, especially those who are intolerant to CPAP or refuse surgical treatment [7].

OSA is a disorder characterized by repeated upper airway collapse during sleep, leading to oxygen desaturation and sleep disruption. The mechanism of upper airway collapse is multifactorial and includes obesity, craniofacial deformities, alterations in upper airway muscle function, pharyngeal neuropathy, and fluid shift toward the neck [1, 8]. In particular, maxillofacial skeletal deformities, such as mandibular retrognathia and maxillary hypoplasia, directly shorten the anteroposterior diameter of the upper airway, reduce the pharyngeal cavity volume, and thus result in upper airway stenosis or occlusion [9, 10]. By advancing the mandible, MAD modifies the craniofacial skeletal configuration and indirectly stretches the surrounding tissues, thereby alleviating airway stenosis and treating OSA [11]. The hyoid bone, soft palate, tongue, and epiglottis, as critical peripharyngeal structures, play essential roles in both the pathogenesis and treatment of OSA [12]. To date, a number of imaging and three‑dimensional finite element (3D-FE) studies have investigated the roles of the tongue and soft palate in MAD therapy for OSA [13–16]; however, research focusing on the epiglottis remains relatively limited.

Constructing a biomechanical model of the upper airway based on spiral computed tomography (CT) and performing fluid‑structure interaction (FSI) analysis is a common approach to study upper airway biomechanics [17, 18]. In this study, CT was used to assess morphological and displacement changes of the aforementioned critical peripharyngeal structures. FSI analysis was performed to characterize airflow field patterns and displacement of the aforementioned soft tissues during inspiration and expiration. This work aims to further elucidate the biomechanical mechanism underlying MAD therapy for OSA and to provide biomechanical insights and methodological references for future large‑scale, multi‑type investigations.

Methods

Research object

In this study, a 32‑year‑old female patient with moderate OSA confirmed by polysomnography (PSG; AHI 22.2 events/h) was enrolled. The patient had a body mass index of 23 kg/m², a neck circumference of 36 cm, and presented with skeletal Class II characteristics, including mandibular hypoplasia and an average vertical facial pattern. This study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the Stomatological Hospital of Xi’an Jiaotong University (No. 2025-XJKQIEC-KY-QT-0078-001), and written informed consent was obtained from the patient. The detailed inclusion and exclusion criteria are provided in the Supplementary Materials. Clinical trial number: not applicable.

CT image acquisition

Under natural sleep conditions, two sets of CT data were acquired using the same CT scanner, with identical scanning parameters and consistent head-and-neck position: one without MAD and the other after two months of continuous MAD use. CT scanning was performed using a multi‑slice spiral CT scanner (United Imaging UCT780, Shanghai United Imaging Healthcare Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). Thin‑slice volume scanning covered the craniofacial and upper airway regions from the skull base to the lower margin of the sixth cervical vertebra. The scanning parameters were as follows: tube voltage 120 kV, tube current 200 mA, slice thickness 0.5 mm, slice interval 0.5 mm, reconstruction matrix 512 × 512, field of view 200 mm × 200 mm, reconstruction algorithm (standard bone algorithm combined with soft‑tissue algorithm), bone window (width 2500 HU/level 500 HU), soft‑tissue window (width 350 HU/level 50 HU). All images were stored and exported in DICOM 3.0 format for subsequent 3D-FE model construction (Fig. 1A).

DICOM datasets were imported into Mimics 21 (Materialise, Belgium) to reconstruct serial cross-sectional images. Grayscale threshold segmentation was applied to extract the upper airway, soft palate, and epiglottis. The segmented geometries were exported as STL files and further processed in Geomagic Studio 12.0 (3D Systems, United States) for surface optimization and reverse modeling. A uniform offset of 2 mm was applied to the airway surface to generate the pharyngeal wall [19].

All components were assembled in Siemens NX 1911 (Siemens, Germany) to establish two complete three-dimensional (3D) FSI models (Fig. 1B). The final models included the maxilla, mandible, hyoid bone, cervical vertebrae, upper airway, pharyngeal wall, soft palate, tongue, epiglottis, and the MAD (Fig. 1C, D,E).

Both fluid and solid domains were discretized in ANSYS Workbench 2019 (ANSYS, United States) (Fig. 2A, B). Material properties were assigned to each anatomical structure, and mesh generation was performed for both solid and fluid domains, according to previously published data (Table 1; Fig. 2a, c). Material properties

To improve physiological relevance while maintaining numerical stability, anatomically informed boundary conditions were applied. Fixed constraints were imposed on the airway wall only at the inlet and outlet regions, representing mechanical support from adjacent rigid anatomical structures. The superior margin of the soft palate, which is anatomically connected to the hard palate, was defined as fixed. Similarly, the inferior end of the epiglottis, corresponding to its attachment to the thyroid cartilage, was constrained.

For the tongue, lateral displacement was restricted to represent confinement by surrounding tissues, while anterior–posterior displacement was allowed to enable physiologically relevant motion during breathing. Bonded contact conditions were defined between the tongue, soft palate, epiglottis, and pharyngeal wall, assuming negligible relative sliding between adjacent soft tissues during quiet breathing. This contact definition ensured displacement continuity and numerical convergence of the two-way FSI simulations.

The posterior nasal aperture was specified as the inlet, and the inferior margin of the larynx served as the outlet (Fig. 2b). Identical tidal volumes were imposed in both FSI models.

FSI numerical modeling

Airflow within the upper airway was assumed to be incompressible and governed by the Navier–Stokes equations. Physiological breathing was simulated using a time-dependent volumetric flow rate corresponding to a tidal volume of 500 mL [28]. The total breathing cycle was set to 5.0 s, including an inspiratory phase of 1.8 s and an expiratory phase of 3.2 s (Fig. 3A).

The realizable k-ℇ turbulence model was employed to capture flow separation and velocity fluctuations in anatomically constricted regions of the upper airway. Enhanced wall treatment was applied to ensure accurate near-wall resolution across regions with varying Reynolds numbers. The turbulent Prandtl numbers for turbulent kinetic energy and dissipation rate were set to 1.0 and 1.2, respectively.

A partitioned two-way FSI coupling strategy was adopted. At each time step, pressure and velocity fields computed in the fluid domain were transferred to the structural domain, and the resulting deformation of the pharyngeal wall was fed back to the fluid solver to update the computational mesh iteratively.

Morphometric and displacement analysis based on spiral CT

Four cross-sectional planes parallel to the FH plane were constructed at anatomical landmarks PNS, U, Ep, and Eb, dividing the upper airway into three regions: velopharynx, glossopharynx, and epiglottopharynx (Fig. 3B). Regional airway volumes (Vv, Vg, Ve), as well as the total volume comprising these three regions (Vt), and the minimum cross-sectional area (CSAmin) were calculated (Fig. 3C). Displacement-related parameters, including H-CV3, H-CVP, H-SN, H-FH, H-PP, U-PW, U-PNS, (U-PNS)/PP, TB-PW, ET-PW, V-PW, Eb-PW, and Et-Eb, were measured to quantify positional changes of the hyoid bone, soft palate, tongue, and epiglottis (Fig. 4).

Pressure, velocity, and displacement quantification

Five airway cross-sections, designated as CA1-s, CS2-s, CS2-i, CV4-s, and CV5-s, were acquired by sectioning the airway at the following anatomical landmarks with a plane parallel to the FH plane: the superior border of the anterior arch of the first cervical vertebra, the superior border of the spinous process of the second cervical vertebra, the inferior border of the spinous process of the second cervical vertebra, the superior border of the vertebral body of the fourth cervical vertebra, and the superior border of the vertebral body of the fifth cervical vertebra (Fig. 3D). Airflow pressure and velocity at these cross-sections were measured, along with the displacement of the soft palate, tongue, epiglottis, and pharyngeal wall was measured.

Results

Craniofacial characteristics

Cephalometric measurements were as follows: FMA = 22.9°, GoGn-SN = 30.2°, SNA = 81.0°, SNB = 74.7°, ANB = 6.3°. These values confirmed the patient for 3D model construction as a Class II skeletal case with normal maxillary development, mandibular retrognathism, and an average growth pattern [29](Fig. 4A, B).

PSG outcomes

The PSG results showed that following MAD application, the patient’s AHI decreased from 22.2 events/h to 12.1 events/h.

Mandibular positional change

Post-MAD placement, the mandible was vertically separated by 4 mm and sagittally advanced by 4 mm. The direction of mandibular movement was aligned parallel to the OP, which prevented clockwise rotation of the mandible (Fig. 5A).

Displacement contour revealed that post-MAD placement, the maximum displacement of the soft palate, tongue, epiglottis, and pharyngeal wall at both peak inspiration and expiration was decreased relative to pre-application levels. Discussion

The primary pathogenic mechanism of OSA involves upper airway narrowing resulting from craniofacial anatomical abnormalities or dysfunction of the peripharyngeal soft tissues. Individuals with skeletal Class II malocclusion exhibit the most pronounced narrowing of the upper airway [30]. These patients present with 3D craniofacial developmental anomalies, including transverse maxillary arch constriction, sagittal mandibular retrognathia, vertical facial disproportion. Specific manifestations include abnormal lower facial third height, increased ANB angle, underdeveloped mandibular body and ramus, which contribute to OSA pathogenesis [31]. Given the known association between skeletal Class II morphology and upper airway narrowing, the study deliberately focuses on this phenotype to explore MAD induced biomechanical changes.

Abnormalities in soft tissue anatomy, dimension, dynamics, and interactions induce airway narrowing via tissue collapse [8]. Previous morphological studies on the upper airway in OSA have mostly focused on the palatopharyngeal and glossopharyngeal levels, with insufficient attention paid to the epiglottic level. The epiglottis, a key structure at the laryngopharyngeal inlet, influences airway patency via its curvature, vertical position, and mobility [32]. In this study, the epiglottic level was included in the analysis to further clarify its correlation with the upper airway ventilation function and supplement the existing research framework for the upper airway in OSA.

Morphometric and displacement analysis of the upper airway and surrounding tissues was performed using CT imaging. The aim of this analysis was to elucidate the structural characteristics of airway collapse and to reveal the therapeutic mechanism of the MAD. The increases in H-CV3 and H-CVP and the decreases in H-PP, H-FH, and H-SN represent anterior and superior displacement of the hyoid bone following MAD wear, respectively. This finding is consistent with the results of previous imaging studies [33–35]. In addition, we observed more pronounced vertical changes in hyoid position; however, whether this conclusion is generalizable requires further validation in future large-sample studies. The anterosuperior displacement of the hyoid bone results from the traction exerted by the MAD on the genioglossus and geniohyoid muscles, and this displacement counteracts airway collapse caused by gravity and soft tissue relaxation during sleep [36, 37].

The increase in U-PW and the decrease in the (U-PNS)/PP ratio suggest anterosuperior displacement of the soft palate after MAD wear, whereas the increase in U-PNS indicates shortening of the soft palate. The increase in TB-PW reflects an increased, indicating anterior displacement of the tongue following MAD wear. These changes in the soft palate and tongue are consistent with previous studies [33, 38, 39]. The increases in Et-PW, V-PW, and Eb-PW indicate anterosuperior displacement of the epiglottis after MAD wear, which is in agreement with the findings of previous research [40]. In addition, we found that Et-Eb increased after MAD wear, suggesting elongation of the epiglottis. Whether this morphological change of the epiglottis is generalizable remains to be further validated in future studies with larger sample sizes. The above-mentioned MAD-induced changes in tissue morphology and displacement effectively enlarge the pharyngeal cavity volume, as reflected by the increases in Vt, Vv, Ve, and CSAmin. Moreover, the present study found that the narrowest segment of the upper airway in patient with OSA was at the velopharyngeal level, followed by the glossopharyngeal level, which is consistent with previous reports [41].

Amatoury et al. constructed a rabbit upper airway model under anesthesia and tracheostomy. Using graded mandibular advancement, they demonstrated that mandibular advancement can effectively enlarge the upper airway. While the use of anesthesia and tracheostomy may alter muscle tone and thus differ from natural sleep conditions, their findings nonetheless provide a useful foundation for subsequent research [13]. Ayyalasomayajula et al. developed a localized three‑dimensional finite element model of the airway within a 2.5 cm width around the midsagittal plane based on CT data from OSA patients [42]. By manually modifying tissue geometry and position, they compared the effects of four surgical procedures on the critical closing pressure of the airway. Although this model did not include the influence of muscles and other soft tissues and was limited to a localized airway region, its representation may not fully capture the complexity of the physiological environment. Nevertheless, it still offers valuable reference for further studies. Xiong et al. reconstructed a model of the upper airway and surrounding tissues using MRI, but the vertebrae were not incorporated into their model [43]. Given that the vertebrae serve as a key rigid supporting structure of the posterior airway wall, the absence of these structures may affect the realism and accuracy of airway deformation simulations [44]. Building on these previous efforts, the present study further refines the modeling strategy. It reconstructs a model that includes the vertebrae, hyoid bone, epiglottis, and other important peripharyngeal structures, thereby enabling a more comprehensive and realistic analysis of upper airway biomechanical properties.

Computational fluid dynamics (CFD) and FSI are widely used approaches to investigate airflow characteristics within the upper airway in patients with OSA [45, 46]. Previous CFD studies have demonstrated that MAD can enlarge upper airway volume, modify airflow distribution, and optimize airway pressure gradients by MAD in OSA patients [47–49]. FSI extends CFD by incorporating the structural domain and employs a two‑way coupling algorithm to simultaneously solve the fluid dynamics equations and the mechanical response of the structural domain. This enables a more physiologically realistic representation of the real‑time interactions among airflow loading, airway wall deformation, and subsequent flow field alterations.

In healthy individuals, the inspiratory‑expiratory (I: E) ratio during resting spontaneous breathing ranges from 1:1.5 to 1:2. Previous studies investigating the mechanisms of MAD for OSA have often adopted a ratio of 1:1 [18, 50]. The present study used an I: E ratio of approximately 1:1.75 to explore the therapeutic mechanisms of MAD for OSA under different I: E ratios. Moreover, earlier studies applied the same flow velocity to both pre‑ and post‑treatment models without considering the differences in inlet cross‑sectional areas [26], resulting in unequal tidal volumes under the same flow velocity. In contrast, the present study employed a time‑dependent volumetric flow rate to simulate physiological breathing while maintaining equal tidal volumes, representing an improvement in model construction.

The most characteristic upper airway airflow pattern observed in patients with OSA is periodic interruption or significant reduction of airflow [51]. In FSI analysis, upper airway airflow velocity and pressure parameters act as indicators for OSA severity assessment and direct metrics for MAD treatment efficacy evaluation [52–55]. Previous studies have confirmed that MAD reduces overall upper airway pressure and flow velocity [47, 56, 57]. However, these studies lack detailed analyses at different cross‑sections, making it difficult to identify the specific sites of action. Moreover, OSA is a dynamic obstructive disorder. The physiological differences between inspiration and expiration significantly affect airflow field characteristics. Currently, it remains unclear whether the therapeutic effect of MAD is more pronounced during inspiration or expiration [45, 58]. In this study, maximum pressure and flow velocity on the upper airway sagittal plane and at five key cross‑sections were higher at peak inspiration than at peak expiration in this OSA patient. This finding, that inspiratory velocity and pressure are greater than expiratory values, is consistent with the clinical findings of Sériès et al. [59]. This may be attributed to more severe airway collapse during inspiration [59]. After MAD wear, the reduction in these parameters was significantly more pronounced during inspiration. This may be because the dilatory and supportive effects of MAD are more prominent in the upper airway during inspiration, when collapse is more severe [60, 61].

The present study observed that in this OSA patient, the deformation of the pharyngeal wall and the displacement of the soft palate, tongue, and epiglottis were greater during inspiration than during the expiration. This suggests a more pronounced movement of these tissues toward the pharyngeal wall during inspiration, a phenomenon closely associated with the differences in airflow pressure and velocity between the two phases [62–64]. After MAD placement, both pharyngeal wall deformation and soft tissue displacement were reduced during the inspiration and expiration, with a more pronounced improvement observed in the inspiration. This reduction in airflow pressure and velocity mitigates the mechanical stimulation imposed by airflow on the surrounding soft tissues, which in turn suppresses the aberrant deformation and displacement of the pharyngeal wall, soft palate, tongue, and epiglottis. Thus, the therapeutic effect of MAD is primarily achieved through a synergistic mechanism involving physical upper airway dilation, uniform airflow distribution, and soft tissue stabilization. What’s more, the reduction in airflow pressure diminishes persistent stimulation to soft tissues, which we hypothesize may prevent pathological hypertrophy and interrupt the OSA vicious cycle of airway narrowing, airflow impact, tissue hyperplasia, and worsening narrowing [65]. Studies using dynamic imaging and FSI analyses have also confirmed that soft palate displacement toward the posterior pharyngeal wall during inspiration is significantly greater than that during expiration [26, 45, 46, 62, 66, 67].

In this study, we incorporated the epiglottis into the analysis and constructed a more comprehensive model of the upper airway and surrounding tissues, including the soft palate, tongue, and epiglottis, to more comprehensively elucidate the therapeutic mechanism of MAD for OSA. Furthermore, we applied a more physiologically realistic I: E and equal tidal volumes as boundary conditions, thereby establishing a FSI model with improved physiological compliance and verifying the feasibility of this analytical approach. Using this model, we analyzed the therapeutic effects of MAD on this OSA patient during inspiration and expiration separately, emphasizing the phase‑specific nature of MAD therapy.

However, this study also has several limitations. First, as a single‑case study, the findings has not been validated. Second, certain simplifications were made in model construction. Soft tissues were assumed to be homogeneous, isotropic, and linearly elastic, while inter‑tissue sliding friction, neuromuscular regulation, and material nonlinearity were not considered. Furthermore, bonded contacts were applied between the tongue, soft palate, epiglottis, and pharyngeal wall; fixed constraints were applied at the airway inlet and outlet; and the same flow boundary conditions were applied to both the pre‑ and post‑MAD models. These simplifications cannot fully replicate the entire complexity of the physiological state; therefore, the accuracy of the simulation may be affected to some extent, which could lead to deviations in the absolute values of the outcomes. Thus, the results should be interpreted primarily in terms of relative changes rather than overreliance on absolute numerical values.

Furthermore, although patients with OSA exhibit individual differences in anatomical and physiological characteristics, their clinical manifestations and underlying pathogenesis still follow common patterns. Accordingly, we hypothesize that while the absolute values of MAD treatment effects may vary among patients, the overall trends remain consistent. Future studies aim to increase the sample size and include patients with different skeletal patterns to further validate the generalizability of our findings. Overall, this study provides a methodological reference for future large‑scale, multi‑type investigations. It can inform clinical practice in several aspects, including patient selection, pre‑treatment prediction of individual therapeutic responses, identification of high‑risk sites for airway collapse, and treatment planning, thereby contributing to advancing precision medicine in the field of sleep disorders.

Conclusions

Based on spiral CT and the optimized FSI model established for this participant, we draw the following conclusions:

(1) MAD-induced mandibular protrusion results in anterosuperior displacement of the tongue, soft palate, epiglottis, and hyoid bone, shortening the soft palate and lengthening the epiglottis, thereby dilating the upper airway in both sagittal and coronal dimensions and increasing airway volume and minimum cross-sectional area.

(2) MAD reduces and homogenizes airflow pressure and velocity in the upper airway during both inspiration and expiration, with effects more pronounced during inspiration.
