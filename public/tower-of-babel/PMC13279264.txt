Nuclear export of R-loop by the DDX1 and XPO1 complex promotes senescence-associated secretory phenotype and inflammaging
Authors: Xue Hao, Bo Zhao, Qingqing Yan, Mitsutake Yano, Ayumu Matsuoka, Yuan Qi, Liping Liao, Rafal J. Zielinski, Hsin-Yao Tang, Andrew V. Kossenkov, M. Anna Zal, Kavitha Sarma, Rugang Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cellular senescence contributes to inflammaging in part through the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP). R-loops, three-stranded nucleic acid structures, contribute to innate immune response in cancers; however, the role of R-loops in senescence and inflammaging remains largely unknown. Here we show that nuclear-derived cytoplasmic R-loops promote the SASP and inflammaging. We detect an accumulation of nuclear-derived R-loops in the cytoplasm of senescent cells with an enrichment in alpha-satellite repeats. These cytoplasmic R-loops localize into cytoplasmic chromatin fragments (CCFs) and activate the cGAS–STING innate immune pathway to drive the SASP. We identify the exportin-1 (XPO1)–DEAD-Box helicase 1 (DDX1) complex as essential for the nuclear export of R-loops and their subsequent localization into CCFs. Inhibition of XPO1 with KPT-330 suppresses nuclear R-loop export and its localization into CCFs, attenuates the SASP, mitigates age-associated inflammation and extends healthspan. These findings reveal nuclear export of R-loops as a potential target for suppressing age-associated inflammation.

Main

Cellular senescence is characterized by a stable cell cycle arrest and senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) that contributes to chronic inflammation during tissue aging, known as inflammaging1–3. Notably, the SASP is linked to innate immune response through formation of cytoplasmic chromatin fragments (CCFs) and the associated persistent DNA damage response4–7. R-loops are DNA–RNA hybrid-containing three-stranded nucleic acid structures that are distributed across the genome. R-loops are important intermediates in transcription, DNA replication and repair8,9. Persistent R-loops stalling causes double-stranded breaks and compromise genome stability9,10. Thus, formation and timely removal of R-loops in the genome are tightly regulated. Cells employ various mechanisms such as helicases to resolve R-loop structures by unwinding the duplex hybrids8,11. Beyond their nuclear roles, DNA damage triggers the release of cytoplasmic single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) and DNA–RNA hybrids to activate innate immune responses in pathological contexts such as chronic inflammation and cancer12–14. Despite both the SASP and R-loops being linked to persistent DNA damage response, innate immune response and chronic inflammation, the role of R-loops in SASP and its potential implication in inflammaging remains largely unexplored.

Results

Cytoplasmic accumulation of nuclear-derived R-loops in senescent cells

R-loops are typically associated with the loci of actively transcribed genes9,15,16. We next examined whether the subcellular distribution of R-loops is altered during senescence. Consistent with the MapR results, we observed an overall decrease in R-loop levels in senescent compared to proliferating cells (Fig. 1b); however, while the levels of nuclear R-loops were reduced, we observed an increase in its cytoplasmic levels in senescent cells (Fig. 1b). To determine the origin of cytoplasmic R-loops accumulated in senescent cells, we conducted cytoplasmic DNA–RNA hybrid immunoprecipitation (DRIP) sequencing (CytoDRIP-seq) analysis13 using S9.6 antibody in proliferating and senescent cells (Fig. 1c). To limit the issue that S9.6 antibody can also bind to double-stranded RNA (dsRNA)19, we used ssDNA moiety of the hybrids for sequencing and obtained highly reproducible signals (Extended Data Fig. 1a). The sequencing reads were predominantly mapped to nucleus with the rest mapped to mitochondria genome (Extended Data Fig. 1b). Notably, the peaks identified in senescent cells were less than those in control proliferating cells (Extended Data Fig. 1c). Of note, nearly half of these peaks were mapped to intergenic regions (Fig. 1d). As nuclear R-loops are enriched for certain repetitive elements8, we determined whether cytoplasmic R-loops overlapped with those repeats in senescent cells. We discovered that cytoDRIP regions were specifically enriched for alpha-satellite repeats (Extended Data Fig. 1d), which overlapped with sites that form nuclear R-loops in senescent cells (Fig. 1e and Extended Data Fig. 1e). Moreover, despite cytoDRIP regions mapped to smaller areas in the genome compared to nuclear DRIP regions, their peak strength was highly correlated with nuclear DRIP-seq levels (Extended Data Fig. 1f). These data suggest that cytoplasmic R-loops accumulated during senescence are primarily derived from a small subset of nuclear R-loops with an enrichment in alpha-satellite repeats.

Nuclear R-loops and their derived RNA–DNA hybrids can be targeted by nucleotide excision repair endonucleases and exported to the cytoplasm in a nuclear export protein exportin-1(XPO1)-dependent manner where they engage innate immune sensors including cGAS12,13,20,21. For example, endonucleases XPF and XPG are involved in R-loops excision from nuclear chromatin10,13,21. Thus, we next asked whether they play a role in the cytoplasmic accumulation of R-loops. Notably, both XPF and XPG were upregulated in senescent cells (Extended Data Fig. 2a). Knockdown of either XPF or XPG in senescent cells led to a decrease in the cytoplasmic R-loop level (Extended Data Fig. 2a,b), further supporting the nuclear origin of cytoplasmic R-loops. Finally, to determine whether cytoplasmic R-loops are exported from nucleus, we treated senescent cells with KPT-330, an inhibitor that blocks nuclear export22. Indeed, treatment with KPT-330 reduced the accumulation of cytoplasmic R-loops, while increased R-loop levels in the nucleus (Fig. 1f). A similar observation was made by short hairpin RNA (shRNA)-mediated knockdown of XPO1 in senescent cells (Fig. 1f). Together, these data support that cytoplasmic R-loops originate from the nucleus and are actively exported in senescent cells.

Cytoplasmic R-loops localize to CCFs and promote the SASP

Cytoplasmic nucleic acids are known to activate innate immune signaling, which drives SASP during senescence4. To determine whether cytoplasmic R-loops accumulated in senescent cells contribute to the SASP, we expressed cytoplasm-localized RNase H1 with nuclear export signal (RH-NES)13 in senescent cells to selectively digest cytoplasmic R-loops (Supplementary Fig. 2a). Indeed, RH-NES significantly reduced cytoplasmic R-loop levels without affecting nuclear R-loop levels (Fig. 1g), which correlated with the suppression of IRF3 signaling and SASP genes expression (Fig. 1h,i). This suggests that cytoplasmic R-loops contribute to the innate immune response in senescent cells. Notably, senescence markers such as senescence-associated β-galactosidase activity (SA-β-gal) and p21 expression remained unchanged (Fig. 1h and Supplementary Fig. 2b,c). This indicates that the SASP suppression by RH-NES expression was not a consequence of overcoming senescence. Similar observations were made in therapy-induced senescent ovarian cancer OVCAR3 cells (Supplementary Fig. 3). Moreover, reducing cytoplasmic R-loop levels in senescent cells through XPF or XPG knockdown (Extended Data Fig. 2c) or by inhibiting nuclear export via KPT-330 or XPO1 knockdown also impaired the SASP (Fig. 1f,j and Extended Data Fig. 2d,e). These findings support that cytoplasmic R-loops promote the SASP.

Senescent cells undergo a global chromatin reorganization and fragmentation12. Following the nuclear envelope breakage, the chromatin fragments appear in the cytoplasm as CCFs23. CCFs play a critical role in driving the SASP5,24. Given that cytoplasmic R-loops were accumulated during senescence, we next examined the spatial localization of cytoplasmic R-loops. Immunofluorescence analysis revealed strong colocalization of R-loops and CCFs, marked by γH2AX, in senescent cells (Fig. 2a,b). Notably, these signals were sensitive to RNase H treatment, confirming that the antibody-detected signal was derived from R-loop structure (Supplementary Fig. 4a,b). Further, as the S9.6 antibody also recognizes dsRNA, though the affinity is much lower compared to that for R-loop structures, we stained dsRNA in senescent cells. Notably, the dsRNA signal was not detected in CCFs (Supplementary Fig. 4c,d). To determine whether CCFs are required for cytoplasmic R-loop-induced SASP, we treated the senescent cells with trichostatin A (TSA), a histone deacetylase inhibitor (HDACi), to suppress CCF formation24. Indeed, a low dose of TSA reduced both CCF formation and SASP gene expression in senescent cells without affecting senescence-associated growth arrest (Extended Data Fig. 3a–d). Notably, RH-NES expression in TSA-treated senescent cells failed to further suppress the SASP (Extended Data Fig. 3e,f), suggesting that CCFs are required for cytoplasmic R-loop-induced SASP in senescent cells. Together, these findings are consistent with the notion that cytoplasmic R-loops localize to CCFs and contribute to the SASP induction.

We next explored the relationship between CCF formation and cytoplasmic R-loops accumulation. As downregulation of lamin B1 compromises integrity of the nuclear envelope and elevates CCF23,25, we knocked down lamin B1 in senescent cells (Fig. 2c). Indeed, CCF formation was significantly increased by lamin B1 knockdown (Fig. 2d and Extended Data Fig. 3g), which was accompanied by an increase in R-loop positive CCFs (Fig. 2e); however, the level of cytoplasmic R-loops was not increased by lamin B1 knockdown as determined cytoDRIP (Fig. 2f). These findings suggest that cytoplasmic R-loops are not a consequence of nuclear envelope breakdown and CCF formation. Indeed, senescent cells with KPT-330 treatment that suppressed the R-loop nuclear export (Fig. 1f) decreased R-loop localization into CCF without affecting CCF formation (Fig. 2g,h and Extended Data Fig. 3h). Consistently, shXPF or shXPG that decreased cytoplasmic R-loop levels (Extended Data Fig. 2b) also decreased R-loop localization into CCF without decreasing CCF formation (Extended Data Fig. 3i–k).

We next directly determined whether cytoplasmic R-loops can localize into CCF. Toward this goal, we transfected fluorescently labeled RNA–DNA hybrids into senescent cells. Indeed, the transfected hybrids localized into CCFs in senescent cells (Fig. 2i,j). In contrast, transfected fluorescently labeled dsRNA failed to localize into CCFs in senescent cells (Supplementary Fig. 4c,e). These results show that the cytoplasmic RNA–DNA hybrids can be recruited to CCFs. Consistently, analysis of eluted fractions of purified CCFs from increasing salt concentrations showed that the hybrids were most abundant in the soluble fractions of CCFs (Fig. 2k–m). Together, these results are consistent with a model whereby R-loop nuclear export and CCF formation are two independent processes and localization of R-loops into CCFs occurs in the cytoplasm after their nuclear export.

The XPO1–DDX1 complex mediates R-loop export and localization to CCFs

We next investigated the mechanism underlying R-loop nuclear export and localization to CCFs. Notably, XPO1 does not directly bind R-loops (Fig. 3a), implying the involvement of adaptor proteins. To identify candidate R-loop-binding proteins (RBPs), we performed cytoplasmic R-loop pulldown followed by liquid chromatography (LC)–tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS) analysis in control proliferating and senescent cells (Extended Data Fig. 4a). Among 1,745 RBPs identified, 1,087 were enriched in senescent cells (more than 1.5-fold enrichment in two biologically independent experiments) (Fig. 3b and Supplementary Table 1). To enable R-loop export, candidate proteins are expected to be nuclear-localized. By combining Gene Ontology Cellular Component annotation and UniProtKB database, we identified 367 nuclear-localized RBPs. Cross-referencing with a known CCF protein component26 yielded 194 candidates that showed enrichment for 11 functional subsets, including helicases (Fig. 3b,c). Notably, helicases are known to bind to R-loops11,27. We focused on DDX1, a DEAD-box RNA helicase with a known role in the DNA damage response28,29, due to its strong enrichment (Extended Data Fig. 4b). Indeed, we validated that DDX1 interacted with R-loops (Fig. 3d). We next examined the localization of DDX1 in senescent cells. Consistent with its interaction with R-loops in senescent cells, we observed a colocalization of DDX1 and R-loops in CCFs (Fig. 3e,f and Extended Data Fig. 4c,d). Moreover, analysis of CCF fractionations revealed the presence of DDX1 in soluble fractions in which R-loops are enriched (Fig. 2o,p and Extended Data Fig. 4e). To determine whether DDX1 directly binds to the R-loop structure, we assembled R-loops for biochemical assays30 (Supplementary Fig. 5). The electrophoresis mobility shift assay (EMSA) showed that DDX1 bound to R-loop structures in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 3g). To examine whether DDX1 interacts with XPO1, we performed co-immunoprecipitation analysis in control proliferating and senescent cells. Despite a decrease in total XPO1 protein expression in senescent cells, we observed an enhanced interaction between DDX1 and XPO1 in senescent compared to proliferating cells (Extended Data Fig. 4f). Indeed, DDX1 level in the cytoplasmic fraction was elevated, whereas its level in the nuclear fraction was decreased in senescent compared to proliferating cells (Extended Data Fig. 4g). Treatment with nuclear export inhibitor leptomycin B (LMB) in senescent cells rescued the decrease in nuclear DDX1 staining (Extended Data Fig. 4h,i). These data are consistent with the notion that DDX1 is exported from nuclear to cytoplasm during senescence.

XPO1 recognizes the leucine-rich NES, which is typically composed of four hydrophobic residues separated by 1–3 linker residues (Φ-X1–3- Φ -X2-3- Φ -X1–3- Φ motif)31. Notably, DDX1 contains an NES in its C-terminal domain (Fig. 3h). To determine its role in DDX1 nuclear export, we knocked down endogenous DDX1 in IMR90 cells expressing an inducible HRASG12V to induce senescence and replaced it with an ectopic V5-tagged wild-type or NES-mutant DDX1 (Fig. 3h and Extended Data Fig. 4j). Notably, NES-mutant DDX1 failed to interact with XPO1, which correlated with an increase in its nuclear distribution in senescent cells (Fig. 3i and Extended Data Fig. 4k,l). These data support that C-terminal NES mediates DDX1’s nuclear export.

We next sought to elucidate whether DDX1 is required for R-loop’s localization in CCFs. Immunofluorescence analysis revealed that DDX1 colocalized with R-loops in approximately 50% of CCFs in senescent cells (Fig. 3e,f). Notably, 84 % of DDX1-positive CCF were also positive for R-loops, whereas 56% of R-loop-positive CCFs were positive for DDX1 (Extended Data Fig. 5a). DDX1 knockdown reduced cytoplasmic R-loops while increasing their nuclear retention in senescent cells (Fig. 3j and Extended Data Fig. 5b). This was accompanied by impaired R-loop localization into CCFs without affecting CCF formation itself (Fig. 3k and Extended Data Fig. 5c,d). Consistently, the SASP was impaired by DDX1 knockdown in senescent cells (Fig. 3l and Extended Data Fig. 5e), and this suppression is not due to an overcome of senescence per se (Extended Data Fig. 5f,g). Replacement of endogenous DDX1 with ectopically expressed wild-type DDX1, but not an NES-mutant DDX1, in senescent cells restored R-loop’s localization into CCFs (Fig. 3m,n). Consistently, wild-type, but not the NES-mutant, DDX1 rescued the decrease in SASP induced by DDX1 knockdown (Extended Data Fig. 5h,i). These data suggest that the nuclear export function of DDX1 is required for R-loop export and associated SASP induction. Notably, both wild-type and NES-mutant DDX1 interacted with R-loops, suggesting the observed changes in NES-mutant DDX1 expressing cells are not due to its inability to interact with R-loops (Extended Data Fig. 5j). Indeed, XPO1 inhibitors significantly decreased the colocalization of DDX1 and R-loops in CCFs without impairing their interaction (Extended Data Fig. 5k–m). These findings suggest that DDX1 is required for R-loop nuclear export and its subsequent localization into CCF.

As DDX1 is an RNA helicase, we next determined whether its enzymatic activity is important for its role in R-loop nuclear export. Toward this goal, we ectopically expressed an enzymatic activity defective K52A-mutant DDX1 (ref. 32) in senescent cells with endogenous DDX1 knockdown and observed no significant changes in R-loop localization into CCFs or the SASP gene expression (Fig. 3m,n and Extended Data Fig. 5h,i). This is consistent with our finding in a biochemical assay that DDX1 failed to resolve the R-loop structure (Fig. 3g). These data are consistent with the notion that DDX1 acts as a transport adaptor rather than through its enzymatic activity in this process.

Finally, to demonstrate directly the role of DDX1 in R-loop nuclear export, we performed cytoDRIP-seq in senescent cells with or without DDX1 knockdown (Extended Data Fig. 6a–e). We noticed a trend toward decrease in nuclear-derived cytoplasmic R-loops in DDX1 knockdown cells compared to controls (Extended Data Fig. 6b). Notably, DDX1 knockdown led to loss of alpha-satellite repeats from cytoplasm of senescent cells (Figs. 1e and 3o and Extended Data Fig. 6d,e). We further validated this finding by performing fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) assay with probes designed from these alpha-satellite regions (Fig. 3p–r). Consistently, RNA-immunoprecipitation (RIP)-seq using an anti-DDX1 antibody also showed a significant enrichment for alpha-satellite repeats in senescent compared to control cells (Extended Data Fig. 6f). Together, these findings support that the XPO1–DDX1 complex function as a key mediator of R-loop nuclear export and its localization to CCFs to promote the SASP in senescent cells.

Rescue experiments showed that wild-type and helicase-dead K52A DDX1 restored cGAS localization to CCFs, whereas NES-mutant DDX1 did not (Fig. 4i,j). This result further supports the role of DDX1-mediated R-loop export in cGAS activation. Reduced cytoplasmic R-loops significantly decreased cGAS localization into CCFs without affecting CCF formation (Extended Data Fig. 7f,h–j), suggesting that R-loops are at least partially required for cGAS recruitment to CCFs.

To examine directly the activation of cGAS by the R-loop structure, we performed a biochemical assay using assembled R-loops (Supplementary Fig. 5). EMSA showed that cGAS bound to R-loop structures in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 4k). These data suggested that R-loop structure can directly bind to cGAS and trigger its activity at a comparable level as dsDNA; however, DDX1 did not affect cGAS activity triggered by R-loops (Fig. 4l). Together, these findings support that DDX1-mediated export of nuclear R-loops enables their sensing by cGAS in CCFs, thereby triggering innate immune signaling and the associated SASP in senescent cells.

The SASP also protects premalignant senescent cells from tumorigenesis through immune surveillance39. To evaluate the role of nuclear-derived cytoplasmic R-loops induced SASP in immune surveillance, we performed hydrodynamic tail-vein injection26,39 of a vector expressing sleeping beauty transposase and a transposon vector expressing both oncogenic NRasG12V and shRNA targeting either Ddx1 (shDdx1) or a negative control Renilla (shRen) (Fig. 6b,c and Extended Data Fig. 8d). Co-injection of both vectors causes stable integration of the transposon selectively into hepatocytes26,39. Expression of oncogenic NRasG12V acutely triggers senescence in hepatocytes and the concomitant SASP to recruit immune clusters for the subsequent clearance of premalignant hepatocytes (Fig. 6b). On day 6 post injection, we observed a marked senescence induction by NRasG12V expression in mouse liver. Consistent with the in vitro observations, R-loop-containing CCFs were decreased in senescent hepatocytes with Ddx1 knockdown compared to their control shRen counterparts (Fig. 6d,e). This was not due to a decrease in CCF formation itself (Fig. 6f). Reduced R-loops localization led to a decrease in Cgas-positive CCFs in hepatocytes with Ddx1 knockdown (Fig. 6g,h). We observed comparable numbers of NRas-expressing and SA-β-gal-positive hepatocytes in both control and Ddx1 knockdown groups, suggesting that the delivery of the transposon vectors was equally effective in these groups (Fig. 6i–l). Although Ddx1 knockdown did not affect SA-β-gal-positive population, it significantly reduced the number of the immune clusters recruited by NRas-expressing hepatocytes (Fig. 6k,m). On day 14 post injection, the number of NRas-expressing and SA-β-gal-positive cells were significantly reduced in the control group (Fig. 6i–l), suggesting an efficient immune-mediated clearance of senescent cells. Consistently, immune cell recruitment to senescent hepatocytes was significantly impaired in the Ddx1 knockdown group, resulting in reduced clearance of NRas-expressing and SA-β-gal-positive senescent cells (Fig. 6i–m). Collectively, these data support that DDX1-mediated export of nuclear R-loops is essential for their localization to CCFs, activation of cGAS and subsequent SASP-driven immune surveillance.

Targeting R-loop nuclear export suppresses inflammaging and extends lifespan

Sterile inflammation, also known as ‘inflammaging’, is a hallmark of tissue aging2,3. Cellular senescence contributes to tissue aging partially through the SASP. Given the critical role of cytoplasmic R-loops in promoting the SASP, we next explored the implications of this mechanism in inflammaging. Due to lack of commercially available inhibitors for DDX1, we treated chronologically aged mice (22 months old) with the XPO1 inhibitor KPT-330 to suppress the R-loop’s nuclear export. We focused on our analysis in liver because cellular senescence is widely implicated in age-dependent liver dysfunction and senescent hepatocytes in aged liver is associated with the SASP and senescence-associated distension of centromeric satellites40,41. We first stained mouse livers for expression of p21, a marker of senescence41. Discussion

R-loops are critical regulators of genomic stability, while their dysregulation has been implicated in cancers and inflammation13,14. Here, we identify a mechanism by which a subset of nuclear R-loops is exported to the cytoplasm during senescence, where it localizes to CCFs and activates the cGAS–STING pathway, thereby driving the SASP and contributing to inflammaging. Notably, these R-loops are released from chromatin in the nuclear in an XPF/XPG-dependent manner, and are exported into cytoplasm in a XPO1/DDX1-dependent and CCF formation-independent manner. Thus, our findings provide a mechanistic basis by which cytoplasmic R-loops promote innate immunity through their nuclear export and localization into the CCFs during senescence.

Our data support a sequential mechanism at cell biology level for R-loop localization to CCFs (Extended Data Fig. 7l). We demonstrated that DDX1 is required for R-loop export from nuclei to cytoplasm and their subsequent localization to CCFs (Fig. 4e). Due to the compartmental distribution (nuclei versus cytoplasm), the interaction between DDX1 and cGAS can only occur in the presence of hybrid in the cytoplasm because they can each interact with R-loops (Fig. 4b), but not themselves directly (Extended Data Fig. 7d,e). Once in cytoplasm, the localization of DDX1 and R-loops to CCFs are cGAS dependent because of the binding between cGAS and R-loops and localized concentration of cGAS at CCFs. Hence, both cGAS and DDX1 are required for R-loop localization to CCFs (Fig. 5e,h). Biochemically, cGAS can bind an R-loop without DDX1 (Fig. 4k). Indeed, DDX1 does not affect cGAS activity toward R-loops as determined by 2′3′-cGAMP production (Fig. 4l). Thus, DDX1 is required for the cGAS–R-loop association in the cytoplasm (Fig. 4b) due to DDX1-dependent R-loop nuclear export.

Although DDX1 was the focus of this study, our data suggest that additional RBPs may contribute to R-loop export and/or its localization into CCFs, as not all R-loop-positive CCFs are DDX1-positive (Extended Data Fig. 5a). Based on the RBPs that we discovered (Extended Data Fig. 4b), future studies are warranted to systematically define the R-loop export machinery. Notably, while cGAS has been reported to interact with XPO1 in several cancer cell lines44, we did not detect this interaction in senescent primary human IMR90 cells (Fig. 5a), highlighting cell- and/or context-specific differences in this interaction.

Our work also links centromeric R-loops (previously associated with replication stress8) to innate immune response during senescence. Consistently, centromeres undergo distension of centromeric satellites during senescence45, which facilitates their transcription during senescence and aging46,47. The large size and repetitive nature of these nuclear-derived transcripts as determined by cytoDRIP-seq may protect them from degradation and promote robust immunogenicity to activate the cGAS–STING pathway34. In addition to the presence of nuclear-exported R-loops, we observed mitochondria-derived R-loops in the cytoplasm of senescent cells (Extended Data Fig. 1b); however, inhibition of R-loop nuclear export was sufficient to attenuate activation of the cGAS–STING pathway and the associated SASP. These findings suggest that, although we cannot exclude a potential contribution from mitochondria-derived R-loops, nuclear-derived R-loops play a dominant role in this process.

Consistent with our findings, selective inhibitors of nuclear export extend lifespan in worms and flies by nuclear enrichment of longevity-associated transcription factors such as HLH-30/TFEB48. Our study revealed that in addition to regulating transcription in the nuclei, inhibition of nuclear export suppresses inflammaging by preventing activation of innate immunity by exported R-loop in the cytoplasm. Complementing results obtained using nuclear export inhibitor KPT-330, we identified XPO1 adaptor-DDX1 and performed DDX1 knockdown and rescuing with ectopic wild-type or mutant DDX1 experiments together with biochemical, molecular and cellular assays to show that the DDX1/XPO1-mediated nuclear export of R-loops is critical for cytoplasmic function in senescent cells; however, a limitation of our study is that we cannot formally rule out the possibility that other XPO1 substrates may also contribute to the observed phenotypes. In addition, although targeting this process was associated with a beneficial effect on lifespan (Extended Data Fig. 9h), this finding remains preliminary, given the small sample size and the exclusive use of female mice. Future studies employing larger and sex-balanced cohorts are warranted to definitively establish the potential of targeting this pathway for lifespan extension.

In summary, we uncover a role for R-loop nuclear export in driving innate immune response through the CCF–cGAS–STING signaling axis during senescence. Our findings suggest that targeting R-loop nuclear export offers a promising strategy for mitigating inflammaging.

R-loop annealing

The oligonucleotides used in all the biochemical assays were DNA-1: Cy5-GCCGTCGCATGACGCTGCCGAATTCTACCACGCGATTCATACCTGTCGTGCCAGCTGCTTTGCCCACCTGCAGGTTCACCTCGTCCCTGG; DNA-2: Cy3-CCAGGGACGAGGTGAACCTGCAGGTGGGCAAAGCAGCTGGCACGACAGGTATGAATCGCGTGGTAGAATTCGGCAGCGTCATGCGACGGC; RNA: AF488-GCAGCUGGCACGACAGGUAUGAAUC. DNA oligonucleotide stock was made at 10 μM and RNA oligonucleotide stock at 100 μM and stored at −80 °C. Duplex DNA or duplex hybrids were formed by mixing 0.5 μl of each oligonucleotide in annealing buffer 1 (10 mM Tris, pH 7.6, 100 mM NaCl and 1 mM EDTA) and incubated at 95 °C for 5 min followed by slowly cooling to 21 °C in a Thermo Cycler (Bio-Rad). R-loops were assembled by mixing 8 μl DNA duplex and 1 μl RNA in annealing buffer 2 (90 mM Tris, pH 7.5, 90 mM borate and 10 mM MgCl2) in a total volume of 20 μl for 2 h at room temperature. Excess RNA was removed using NucAway spin columns (Ambion). The purified assembled R-loops were used for unwinding assays, EMSA and cGAS activity assay.

LC–MS/MS analysis and data processing of DNA–RNA binding proteins

Samples were briefly electrophoresed on a 10% TGX precast gel (Bio-Rad, cat no. 4561033), after which, the entire gel lanes were excised and subjected to trypsin digestion. LC–MS/MS was conducted using a Q Exactive HF mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific) interfaced with an UltiMate 3000 nano UPLC system (Thermo Scientific). Peptides were first loaded onto a PepMap100 trap column (0.3 × 5 mm, 5 μm C18 resin; Thermo Scientific) and then separated via reverse-phase LC using a BEH C18 nanocapillary analytical column (75 μm i.d. × 25 cm, 1.7-μm particle size; Waters). Separation was achieved over a 90-min gradient formed by solvent A (0.1% formic acid in water) and solvent B (0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile). Eluted peptides were analyzed by the mass spectrometer set to repetitively scan m/z from 350 to 1,950 in positive ion mode. Full MS scans were acquired at a resolution of 60,000, followed by data-dependent MS/MS scans at 15,000 resolution for the 20 most abundant ions surpassing a 20,000 intensity threshold. Peptide match was set as preferred, the exclude isotopes option was enabled and charge-state screening was applied to discard unassigned ions as well as singly charged and >4 charged species.

Peptide sequences were identified using MaxQuant (v.2.3.1.0)51. MS/MS spectra were searched against the UniProt human protein database (downloaded on 21 July 2022) and a common contaminants database. The search parameters included full tryptic specificity allowing up to two missed cleavages, static carbamidomethylation of cysteine residues and variable modifications for Met oxidation, protein N-terminal acetylation and Asn deamidation. Consensus identification lists were generated with false discovery rates set at 1% for both protein and peptide identification.

Slot-blot analysis for DNA–RNA hybrids

Precipitated DNA was washed with 70% ethanol, resuspended gently in TE buffer and digested with digestion buffer (50 U HindIII, EcoRI, BsrGI, XbaI and SspI, 10 mM DTT, 2 mM spermidine and BSA) at 37 °C overnight. Half the DNA was treated with digestion buffer containing 3 μl of RNase H as negative control. The digested DNA was purified using phenol–chloroform extraction, DNA from equal cell counts were loaded onto a nylon membrane (Cytiva, cat. no. RPN203B), UV-crosslinked and incubated with S9.6 antibody for R-loop detection.

For R-loop detection in cytoplasmic and nuclear fractions, 5 × 106 cells were lysed in 1.6 ml Cyto-lysis buffer (20 mM HEPES, pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, 0.5% NP-40 and 2 mM EDTA) on ice for 30 min. After centrifuging at 11,200g for 30 min at 4 °C, the supernatant was collected as the cytoplasmic fraction. Pelleted nuclei were resuspended in 1.6 ml TE buffer and labeled as the nuclear fraction. Both fractions were incubated with 41.5 μl of 20% SDS and 5 μl of proteinase K at 55 °C overnight followed by phenol–chloroform extraction as described above.

A total of 5 × 108 cells were incubated in DMEM containing 10 μg ml−1 cytochalasin B for 30 min at 37 °C. The cell pellet was dounced and homogenized in prechilled lysis buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl, 2 mM magnesium acetate, 3 mM CaCl2, 0.32 M sucrose, 0.1 mM EDTA, 1 mM DTT, 0.1% NP-40, 0.15 mM spermine, 0.75 mM spermidine and 10 μg ml−1 cytochalasin B, pH 8.5 at 4 °C). After fixation with 1% formaldehyde for 10 min, an equal volume of 1.6 M sucrose buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl, 5 mM magnesium acetate, 0.1 mM EDTA, 1 mM DTT, 0.3% BSA, 0.15 mM spermine and 0.75 mM spermidine, pH 8.0 at 4 °C) was added. In a 50-ml tissue culture tube, 10 ml of the homogenate was added on top of the sucrose buffer gradient (20 and 15 ml containing 1.8 and 1.6 M sucrose, respectively). The gradient was centrifuged (12,000g) for 20 min at 4 °C. After removing the top 3 ml of the gradient, the next 15-ml CCF-containing fraction was diluted with an equal volume of ice-cold PBS. The solution was filtered through a 5-μm low-protein-binding Durapore polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membrane (Millipore, cat. no. SLSV025LS). Ice-cold 5× PBS was added into the CCFs, which were centrifuged (12,000g) for 30 min at 4 °C. The pellet was collected as CCF fraction.

To assess the solubility of CCF-localized R-loops, CCF pellet was resuspended in 300 μl buffer A (50 mM HEPES, pH 7.5, 50 mM KCl, 2.5 mM MgCl2 and 0.05% Triton X-100), samples were then centrifuged at 10,000g for 15 min at 4 °C and the supernatant was collected as fraction A. Next, the pellet was resuspended in 300 μl buffer B (50 mM HEPES, pH 7.5, 100 mM KCl, 2.5 mM MgCl2 and 0.05% Triton X-100), samples were then centrifuged at 10,000g for 15 min at 4 °C and the supernatant was collected as fraction B. Next, the pellet was resuspended in 300 μl buffer C (50 mM HEPES, pH 7.5, 250 mM KCl, 2.5 mM MgCl2 and 0.05% Triton X-100), samples were then centrifuged at 10,000g for 15 min at 4 °C and the supernatant was collected as fraction C. Next, the pellet was resuspended in 300 μl buffer D (50 mM HEPES, pH 7.5, 500 mM KCl, 2.5 mM MgCl2 and 0.1% Triton X-100), samples were then centrifuged at 10,000g for 15 min at 4 °C and the supernatant was collected as fraction D. The remaining pellet was resuspended in buffer B supplemented with 5 mM CaCl2 and 3 U micrococcal nuclease (Thermo Fisher), incubated at 37 °C for 10 min, and the supernatant was collected as chromatin fractionation after centrifugation at 15,000g for 10 min at 4 °C. Whole-cell lysates and isolated chromatin using Pierce Chromatin Prep Module (Thermo Fisher, cat. no. 26158) were prepared as controls.

DNA–RNA hybrid immunoprecipitation sequencing

DRIP-seq was performed as previously described with minor modifications13. A total of 1.2 × 107 cells were collected and fractionated using the Nuclear and Cytoplasmic Extraction kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The cytoplasmic or nucleoplasmic fractions were incubated with 0.4% SDS and 40 μg ml−1 of proteinase K for 90 min at 37 °C. The samples were resuspended in ultrapure water and normalized to equal cell counts, adjusted to 550 mM NaCl and treated with RNase A (1 μg ml−1) for 30 min. For RNase H treatment, the samples were digested overnight with RNase H (0.4 U μl−1). For immunoprecipitation, the samples were resuspended in TE buffer and precleared with Dynabeads protein A/protein G for 1 h in binding buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 2 mM EDTA, 1% Triton X-100, 150 mM NaCl and 0.5% sodium deoxycholate). Precleared samples were then incubated with S9.6 antibody or control mouse IgG overnight at 4 °C with rotation. Then, Dynabeads protein A/protein G magnetic beads were added, and the mixture was incubated for 2 h at 4 °C with rotation. Bound beads were washed with TSE buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 2 mM EDTA, 1% Triton X-100, 0.1% SDS and 150 mM NaCl) and then with TE buffer. Elution was performed in 200 μl elution buffer (50 mM Tris, pH 8.0, 10 mM EDTA, 0.5% SDS and 8 μl of 20 mg ml−1 proteinase K) for 50 min at 50 °C. The eluted samples were resuspended in TE buffer and sonicated to a peak fragment size of 300 bp using a Covaris ME220 machine (10% duty factor, 200 cycles per burst, 75 peak incident power and 60 s per tube). DNA libraries were synthesized from ssDNA using xGen ssDNA & Low-Input DNA Library Prep kit (IDT) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The libraries were sequenced in a 150-base pair paired-end run on an Illumina NextSeq 2000 system by the Wistar Genomics Facility.

MapR-seq

A total of 2 × 106 cells were washed twice with 1 ml wash buffer (20 mM HEPES. pH 7.5, 0.15 M NaCl, 0.5 mM Spermidine and 1 mM protease inhibitors) and immobilized on concanavalin A-coated beads at room temperature. For each group, immobilized cells were divided equally into two tubes and resuspended in 50 ml wash buffer containing 0.02% digitonin (Dig-wash). GST-MNase and GST-RHΔ-MNase proteins were added to a final concentration of 1 mM and incubated at 4 °C with rotation. The beads were washed three times, resuspended in 100 μl Dig-wash buffer and placed on ice. Then, 2 μl 0.1 M CaCl2 was added to activate MNase and digestion was carried out for 30 min on ice. Reaction was stopped by adding equal volume of 2× STOP buffer (340 mM NaCl, 20 mM EDTA, 4 mM EGTA, 0.02% digitonin, 5 μg RNase A, 5 μg linear acrylamide and 2 pg ml−1 heterologous spike-in DNA). The samples were incubated at 37 °C for 10 min to release the chromatin fragments and centrifuge at 16,000g for 5 min at 4 °C. Supernatants were transferred to new tubes with 2 μl 10% SDS and 5 μg proteinase K added and incubated at 70 °C for 10 min. Quickly spun down and add 300 μl phenol:chloroform:isoamyl alcohol mix, centrifuge at 16,000g for 5 min at room temperature, the upper aqueous phase was transferred to a new tube containing 0.5× volume of 7.5 M NH4OAc and 4 μl of linear acrylamide, followed by addition of 2.5× volume of ice-cold 100% ethanol and stored at 80 °C for at least 1 h. After centrifuging at 16,000g for 30 min at 4 °C, the pellets were washed with 1 ml 75% ethanol and spun down at 16,000g for 10 min at 4 °C. Then, we removed the supernatant and air dried the pellets for 2 min. We dissolved the pellets in 20 μl TE buffer (1 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8 and 0.1 mM EDTA).

We took 7 μl DNA for library construction using a NEBNext Ultra DNA Library Prep kit for Illumina (NEB, cat. no. 7370) and the libraries were sequenced in a 75-bp single-end run on Illumina NextSeq 500 system by the Wistar Genomics Facility.

RNA-IP and RT–qPCR analysis

Anti-DDX1 RIP was carried out using a Magna RIPRNA-binding protein immunoprecipitation kit (Millipore, cat. no. 17-700). In brief, for each immunoprecipitation, 2 × 107 cells were collected and lysed in 100 μl RIP Lysis Buffer with RNase inhibitor and protease inhibitor. The lysate was subjected to immunoprecipitation using 20 μg anti-DDX1 antibody (Invitrogen, cat. no. 703141). The immunoprecipitated RNA was extracted using phenol:chloroform:isoamyl alcohol (125:24:1) (Thermo Fisher, cat. no. AM9720), followed by RNA library construction using Illumina Stranded Total RNA Prep with Ribo-Zero Plus kit (Illumina) and sequenced in a 75-bp paired-end run on an Illumina NextSeq 2000 system by the Wistar Genomics Facility.

In vivo mouse models

For xenograft mouse model, cells (TOV21G: senescent fibroblasts at a ratio of 1:1, 2 × 106 cells) were suspended in 100 μl PBS: Matrigel (1:1) and unilaterally injected subcutaneously into the right dorsal flank of 6–8-week-old female immunocompromised nonobese diabetic/severe combined immunodeficiency (NOD/SCID) gamma (NSG) mice. Tumor size was measured on the indicated days. Tumor size was determined using the following equation: tumor size (mm3 = (d2 × D)/2, where d and D are the shortest and the longest diameter, respectively.

For hydrodynamic tail-veil injection, 8-week-old female C56BL/6 mice (Charles Rivers Laboratory) were used. For each injection, endotoxin-free transposon-based construct expressing NRasG12V (25 μg) together with endotoxin-free transposase plasmid (5 μg) were mixed at a volume of 10% of the mouse body weight were delivered into the mice within 5–8 s as previously described39. Mice were killed 6 or 14 days after injection, livers were collected and embedded in OCT (Fisher, cat. no. 23-730-571) followed by flash freezing.

For the XPO1 inhibitor treatment, the aged C57BL/6J mice (22 months) were obtained from the National Institute on Aging aged rodent colonies and were randomly grouped into three groups. Oral gavage administration was given once every 3 days with 20 mg kg−1 KPT-330 in solvent (2% dimethylsulfoxide + 40%PEG300 + 5% ddH2O) or solvent alone for 5 weeks. The mice were killed and the tissues and blood samples were collected for correlative studies. For body composition analysis, treatments were given in aged C57BL/6J female mice (23 months) weekly with 20 mg kg−1 KPT-330 for 2 months. Mouse body composition (visceral fat mass and posterior skeletal muscle mass) was performed by MRI analysis. Posterior skeletal muscles include hindlimb and paraspinal muscles starting from the top level of the right kidney. Lifespan in aged C57BL/6J female mice (23 months) treated with vehicle or KPT-330 (20 mg kg−1, weekly, for 2 months) was followed using reported criteria52. Mice were medicated for non-life-threatening conditions by experienced veterinarians. The experimental end point was recorded when the mice were found dead or at the time of killing if they were unlikely to survive longer than 48 h (for example severely moribund).

Mouse plasma levels of IL-6, TNF, cholesterol and ALT activity measurement

Mouse plasma levels of IL-6 and TNF were determined using ELISA kits (Proteintech, cat. no. KE10007 and KE10002). In brief, mouse plasma was diluted at 1:8 for IL-6 measurement and 1:16 for TNF measurement. Then, 100 μl of diluted serum was added to each well and incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. After a total of four washes, 100 μl of detection antibody solution was added to each well and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. After a total of four washes, 100 μl of streptavidin–HRP solution was added to each well and incubated at 37 °C for 40 min. After a total of four washes, 100 μl of TMB substrate solution was added to each well and incubated at room temperature for 15–20 min with protection from light. Then, 100 μl of Stop solution was added to each well and absorbance at a wavelength of 450 nm was recorded on a microplate reader.

Mouse plasma levels of cholesterol were determined using RayBio Total Cholesterol Colorimetric Assay kit (RayBiotech, cat. no. MA-TC). The plasma samples were diluted at 1:5 for the measurement following the manufacturer’s instruction.

Mouse plasma levels of ALT activity were determined using ALT activity assay kit (RayBiotech, cat. no. MA-ALT). No dilution of plasma samples was required for the assay following the manufacturer’s instruction.

Picrosirius red staining

Liver collagen fibers were stained using a Picrosirius Red Staining kit (Polysciences, cat. no. 24901). The paraffin-embedded mouse liver sections were used following the manufacture’s instruction. After staining, the fibrotic area was quantified.

Immunohistochemistry

Fresh mouse liver tissues were fixed overnight in 4% PFA in PBS at 4 °C or embedded in OCT compound for cryosection. For immunohistochemistry staining, the slides were deparaffinized, rehydrated, quenched in 0.6% hydrogen peroxide in methanol for 15 min and boiled for 40 min in either 10 mM citrate (pH 6.0) buffer or Tris–EDTA (pH 9.0) buffer for antigen retrieval. The slides were incubated with BlockAid Blocking Solution (Invitrogen, cat. no. B10710) for 1 h at room temperature and then incubated overnight with primary antibodies diluted in blocking buffer at 4 °C. The slides were further incubated with biotinylated secondary antibodies, followed by ABC solution and developed with 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (Vector Laboratories). The slides were finally counterstained with hematoxylin, dehydrated and mounted with Permount (Thermo Fisher). For quantification, 20–30 ×10 fields per mouse were counted.

Bioinformatic analysis

MapR-seq

FastQC (v.0.11.9) was used to check qualities of the raw FASTQ reads. Raw MapR reads were aligned to human genome reference GRCh38 using Bowtie2 (v.2.4.5) algorithm53. Peaks were called for each sample using corresponding background sample as control by MACS2 (v.2.2.7.1)54 with parameters -broad broad-cutoff of 0.1.

Peaks were annotated using R library ChIPseeker (v.1.39.0)55. Promoter regions were defined as ±2 kb of transcription start site (TSS) and transcription termination site (TTS) regions were defined as 300 bp downstream of the TTS. Gene bodies include exons and introns but exclude the promoter regions. The difference in count per million mapped reads normalized MapR signals between testing samples and their corresponding background samples were calculated using deepTools (v.3.5.4)56 bamCompare. The relative signals around TSS were calculated and plotted using deepTools (v.3.5.4) computeMatrix and plotHeatmap. All statistical analyses were carried out in R (v.4.2.3).

CytoDRIP-seq

Quality assessment of CytoDRIP and nuclear DRIP reads were carried out using fastQC (v.0.11.9). Adaptor sequence and low-quality ends were trimmed using trim-galore (v.0.6.10) and cutadapt (v.4.5). Trimmed cytoDRIP and nuclear DRIP reads were aligned to human genome reference GRCh38 using Bowtie2 (v.2.4.5) algorithm53. Picard v.2.27.4 (http://broadinstitute.github.io/picard) MarkDuplicates was used to remove PCR duplicates. Peaks were called against corresponding IgG/RH samples using MACS2 (v.2.2.7.1)54 with narrow peak settings and visualized using signal per million reads normalization in IGV57.

Peak annotation was carried out similarly to MapR. Read coverages for the entire genome were calculated using deepTools (v.3.5.4) command multiBamSummary bins. Pearson correlation to show reproducibility between replicates were calculated using deepTools (v.3.5.4) command plotCorrelation with options -skipZeros-removeOutliers. SAMtools (v.1.16.1)58 idxstats was used to calculate reads mapped to nuclear or mitochondrial standard chromosomes. bedtools (v.2.30.0)59 was used to obtain coverage in each experiment over the peaks. University of California, Santa Cruz RepeatMasker annotations were used for DNA repeat elements. The cytoDRIP peaks were subsampled 10,000 times using bedtools shuffle from within all nuclear DRIP peaks. A Jaccard coefficient by bedtools Jaccard was calculated for each randomized peak set and the z-score was calculated from the resulting distribution, as previously described13. Overlapping cyto-peaks and repeat elements were identified by bedtools intersect. Nuclear DRIP-seq signals at cytoDRIP regions and cytoDRIP-seq signals were calculated using bedtools multicov.

RIP-seq

Quality assessment of RIP-seq reads were carried out using fastQC (v.0.11.9). Adaptor sequence and low-quality ends were trimmed using trim-galore (v.0.6.5) and cutadapt (v.1.18). Trimmed RIP-seq reads were aligned to human genome reference GRCh38 using STAR (v.2.7.10b)60 with parameters –winAnchorMultimapNmax 100 –outFilterMultimapNmax 100 for TE elements.

Aligned reads were summarized at the gene and TE level by TEtranscripts (v.2.2.3)61. Annotation of transposable elements (TEs) was performed using ‘GRCh38_GENCODE_rmsk_TE.gtf’ annotation (https://hammelllab.labsites.cshl.edu/software/#TEtranscripts) based on the University of California, Santa Cruz RepeatMasker database. Differential expression analysis of TEs was carried out by TEtranscripts and R package DESeq2 (ref. 62).

Statistics and reproducibility

No statistical test was used to predetermine sample size but our sample sizes are similar to those reported in previous publications5,26. The sample size for each experiment is stated in the figure legends. For measuring 2′3′-cGAMP level in mouse plasma, one sample from aged vehicle group was beyond the detection range according to the ELISA kit instructions and was thus excluded (Extended Data Fig. 9g). Data distribution was assumed to be normal, but this was not formally tested. Experimental groups were indiscriminately allocated. The investigators were blinded to group allocation during data collection and analysis. Experiments were repeated at least three times independently unless otherwise stated in figure legends with similar results. All statistical analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism 10 (GraphPad). All bar graphs show mean values with error bars (s.e.m. as defined in figure legends). An unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test was used for comparison between two groups.
