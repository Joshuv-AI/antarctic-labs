Extensive intraventricular hemorrhage, complicated by dengue-induced coagulopathy, initially misdiagnosed as gastroenteritis in a toddler: A case report
Authors: Ibrahim A Bakari, Lulu Sakafu, Mwajabu A Saleh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIncomplete physical examinations especially omissions of Central nervous system (CNS) assessments remain a major cause of missed or delayed diagnoses of serious neurological conditions in febrile children with nonspecific symptoms. I report the case of a 22-month-old male initially presenting with prolonged fever, coffee-ground vomiting, nonbloody diarrhea, and recurrent convulsions, leading to a presumptive diagnosis of gastroenteritis with sepsis at Mwananyamala regional referral hospital. Despite initial management, his condition deteriorated. Subsequent evaluation at Muhimbili National Hospital revealed focal neurological deficits, hepatomegaly with jaundice, and severe coagulopathy. Brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) demonstrated extensive intraventricular hemorrhage with obstructive hydrocephalus and multifocal acute cerebral infarcts in the setting of severe dengue-associated coagulopathy. Although an underlying vascular malformation was considered, vascular imaging was not performed and this remained unconfirmed. This case highlights the critical diagnostic challenge in pediatric care where common gastrointestinal symptoms can mask severe systemic and neurological conditions, particularly in dengue-endemic areas. It highlights the urgent need for high suspicion of central nervous system pathology, comprehensive neurological examinations, and early neuroimaging in children presenting with persistent nonspecific symptoms and new-onset seizures, crucial for timely diagnosis and management.Keywords: Convulsions, Dengue hemorrhagic fever, Arteriovenous malformation, Intraventricular hemorrhage

Clinical presentation

A 22-month-old male from, Dar es Salaam, who was transferred to Muhimbili National Hospital (MNH) on 16th June 2025 from Regional Referral Hospital with complaints of persistent fever, vomiting, diarrhea, and recurrent convulsions. The illness began 3 weeks prior to referral with intermittent fevers that were temporarily relieved by paracetamol. He then developed nonbloody diarrhea (3-7 motions per day), coffee-ground vomiting, yellowish discoloration of eyes, and progressive abdominal distension. Two weeks prior to referral, he started experiencing convulsions, occurring up to 7 times a day, each lasting approximately 10 seconds. These episodes were characterized by upward eye rolling, rhythmic jerky movements of all limbs, and associated urinary and fecal incontinence. There was no reported tongue biting, mouth deviation, or facial asymmetry.

At the regional referral hospital, where he was admitted for 7 days, the child was managed for gastroenteritis with sepsis and seizure disorder, but his condition did not improve. The medication he received were Ceftriaxone sulbactam Intravenous for 7 days, Vitamin K injection for 3 days, Intravenous Pantoprazole for 3 days, Intravenous phenobarbitone 3 doses, and was transfused whole blood and platelets 2 times. There was no history of trauma, recent vaccination, or other localized infections. His prenatal, natal, and postnatal histories were unremarkable.

Upon admission to MNH, physical examination revealed a lethargic, febrile, pale, and jaundiced child with signs of respiratory distress. He had significant hepatomegaly, with the liver palpable 6 cm below the right costal margin. Neurological assessment demonstrated left-sided weakness and hypertonia in all limbs, more pronounced on the left side.

Investigations/imaging findings

Hematology

Full blood count showed severe anemia (Hb 7-8 g/dL), low RBCs, mild thrombocytopenia, and elevated white cell count with neutrophilia—consistent with bacterial sepsis and significant blood loss. HB electrophoresis quantitative test was normal (HB A—97% and HB A2 3%).

Coagulation profile

Prothrombin time (PT) and activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT) were prolonged with elevated international normalized ratio (INR), indicating significant coagulopathy (likely disseminated intravascular coagulation or liver dysfunction), explaining both gastrointestinal (GI) and CNS bleeding risk.

Liver function tests

Markedly elevated bilirubin levels, raised liver enzymes (AST, ALT, ALP, and GGT) and low albumin support liver dysfunction, consistent with jaundice and impaired clotting factor production.

Infection screen

Positive dengue NS1 antigen and Immunoglobulin M (IgM) suggest possible dengue co-infection worsening bleeding risk. Hepatitis, HIV, and other serology were negative.

Stool and urine

Stool culture showed normal flora; urinalysis was normal. Blood group O positive.

Interpretation

The laboratory investigations demonstrated severe systemic illness characterized by anemia, coagulopathy, hepatic dysfunction, and positive dengue serology. Prolonged PT, APTT, and elevated INR indicated significant impairment of hemostasis, likely resulting from disseminated intravascular coagulation and hepatic dysfunction. Positive dengue NS1 antigen and IgM supported recent dengue infection, which may have contributed to the observed bleeding tendency. Collectively, these findings suggest severe dengue-associated coagulopathy with multiorgan involvement.

The laboratory abnormalities may explain the gastrointestinal bleeding, intracranial hemorrhage, and progressive multiorgan dysfunction. However, laboratory findings alone cannot determine the precise cause of the intracranial hemorrhage.

Imaging findings

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain was performed on June 22, 2025 using a 3.0-T MRI scanner. The imaging protocol included axial T1-weighted, T2-weighted, fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR), diffusion-weighted imaging with corresponding apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) maps, fast field echo (FFE)/susceptibility-sensitive sequences, and postcontrast T1-weighted sequences following intravenous gadolinium administration. Diffusion-weighted imaging was acquired using a single-shot echo-planar sequence with b-values of 0 and 1000 s/mm2 and a slice thickness of 4 mm. Additional imaging parameters included TR of 6400 milliseconds, TE of 116 milliseconds, matrix size of 192 × 192, and field of view of 23.5 × 23.5 cm. Magnetic resonance angiography (MRA) and magnetic resonance venography (MRV) were not performed (Figs. 1 and 2).

MRI findings

MRI demonstrated extensive intraventricular hemorrhage predominantly involving the bilateral lateral ventricles, greater on the left, with extension into the third ventricle. No definite blood products were identified within the fourth ventricle.

FFE susceptibility-sensitive sequences demonstrated marked blooming corresponding to intraventricular blood products. No convincing evidence of significant intraparenchymal hemorrhage or hemorrhagic transformation of the infarcts was identified.

The lateral and third ventricles were dilated, consistent with obstructive hydrocephalus. Periventricular T2/FLAIR hyperintensity suggested transependymal cerebrospinal fluid seepage.

Multiple foci of restricted diffusion involved the right parietal cortex, left frontal lobe, and bilateral basal ganglia. The distribution was multifocal and nonwatershed, without a single major arterial territorial pattern. No hemorrhagic transformation was identified.

No significant midline shift, transtentorial herniation, or tonsillar herniation was demonstrated.

The combination of extensive intraventricular hemorrhage, multifocal infarcts, and hydrocephalus raised the possibility of an underlying vascular lesion. However, this remains unconfirmed because MRA and MRV were not performed.

Outcome/follow up and discussion

Outcome/follow up

The child remained admitted for 10 days and continued to receive supportive treatment. During hospitalization, seizure episodes, fever, and respiratory distress persisted despite therapy, although the gastrointestinal symptoms gradually improved.

On June 28, 2025, the child developed sudden severe oxygen desaturation despite receiving supplemental oxygen. Resuscitative measures including intravenous fluids, high-flow oxygen, and adrenaline administration were unsuccessful. Examination revealed fixed dilated pupils, absent carotid pulse, absent spontaneous respiration, loss of brainstem reflexes, and cold extremities. Death was certified at 0104 hours.

The immediate cause of death was cardiac arrest.

The underlying cause of death was extensive intraventricular hemorrhage with obstructive hydrocephalus occurring in the setting of severe dengue-associated coagulopathy, with a possible underlying vascular malformation that remained unconfirmed.

Discussion

This case illustrates the diagnostic complexity of severe dengue presenting with nonspecific gastrointestinal symptoms and delayed neurological recognition in a young child. The initial manifestations of prolonged fever, nonbloody diarrhea, coffee-ground vomiting, and recurrent seizures resulted in a presumptive diagnosis of gastroenteritis with sepsis, a common presentation in resource-limited settings. However, subsequent investigations demonstrated severe coagulopathy, hepatic dysfunction, positive dengue serology, and extensive intracranial abnormalities, indicating severe dengue with multiorgan involvement.

Neurological manifestations of dengue are increasingly recognized and include encephalopathy, ischemic stroke, intracranial hemorrhage, cerebral edema, and acute disseminated encephalomyelitis. Carod-Artal [1] described neurological complications as uncommon but potentially devastating manifestations of dengue infection, emphasizing the importance of early recognition and neuroimaging in affected patients [1]. The marked hepatic dysfunction observed in our patient, including jaundice, elevated liver enzymes, and hypoalbuminemia, is consistent with previous pediatric studies reporting significant hepatic involvement in severe dengue [2].

The laboratory findings demonstrated profound coagulopathy with prolonged coagulation parameters and evidence of hepatic dysfunction. Severe dengue is known to produce hemorrhagic complications through multiple mechanisms including thrombocytopenia, platelet dysfunction, endothelial injury, disseminated intravascular coagulation, and impaired synthesis of clotting factors [3,4]. Therefore, severe dengue-associated coagulopathy alone may explain the spontaneous intracranial hemorrhage observed in this patient.

An important neuroradiological teaching point in this case is the unusual combination of extensive intraventricular hemorrhage, obstructive hydrocephalus, and multifocal acute cerebral infarcts. MRI demonstrated hemorrhage involving both lateral ventricles, predominantly on the left, with extension into the third ventricle and associated ventricular dilatation. FFE sequences demonstrated susceptibility blooming corresponding to intraventricular blood products without convincing evidence of significant intraparenchymal hemorrhage. Associated periventricular FLAIR hyperintensity indicated transependymal cerebrospinal fluid seepage.

Additionally, multifocal acute infarcts involving the right parietal lobe, left frontal lobe, and bilateral basal ganglia were identified. These lesions demonstrated restricted diffusion and did not conform to a single arterial territory or watershed distribution. No definite hemorrhagic transformation was identified. The coexistence of multifocal infarcts, intraventricular hemorrhage, and obstructive hydrocephalus represents an uncommon imaging pattern in children with severe coagulopathy.

Although the hemorrhage pattern and associated infarcts raised the possibility of an underlying vascular abnormality, this diagnosis remains unconfirmed because vascular imaging studies were not performed. Intracranial hemorrhage associated with dengue has been increasingly reported, particularly in patients with severe coagulopathy [5,6]. Therefore, the extensive intraventricular hemorrhage in this patient may be explained entirely by dengue-associated coagulopathy. Nevertheless, the presence of multifocal infarcts and the unusual hemorrhagic distribution suggest that an underlying vascular lesion cannot be completely excluded. Further evaluation with MRA, MRV, or catheter angiography would have been necessary to establish such a diagnosis.

Another important teaching point is the delay between neurological symptom onset and neuroimaging. Convulsive episodes began approximately 2 weeks before MRI was performed, during which time the patient underwent treatment for presumed gastroenteritis and sepsis. Previous studies from Tanzania have shown that subtle neurological findings may be overlooked in busy primary care and referral settings, potentially delaying diagnosis of serious central nervous system pathology [7]. In this patient, focal neurological deficits became apparent only after referral to the tertiary center.

This case demonstrates the overlap between severe dengue, disseminated intravascular coagulation, hepatic dysfunction, severe anemia, and neurological complications, consistent with recognized manifestations of dengue-associated multiple organ dysfunction syndrome. Despite aggressive supportive management, the extensive intraventricular hemorrhage, obstructive hydrocephalus, multifocal cerebral infarcts, and severe coagulopathy resulted in progressive neurological deterioration and death.

Recognition of the neuroradiological pattern of extensive intraventricular hemorrhage, obstructive hydrocephalus, and multifocal acute infarcts in a child with severe coagulopathy may facilitate earlier diagnosis, prompt vascular investigation when appropriate, and improve clinical decision-making in similar cases.

Conclusion

This case demonstrates an unusual neuroradiological pattern consisting of extensive intraventricular hemorrhage, obstructive hydrocephalus, and multifocal acute infarcts occurring in the setting of severe dengue-associated coagulopathy. Recognition of this constellation of imaging findings in a coagulopathic child may facilitate earlier diagnosis and prompt further vascular evaluation. Although an underlying vascular abnormality was considered, this remained unconfirmed because MRA and MRV were not performed. Early recognition of neurological symptoms and timely neuroimaging remain essential for improving clinical outcomes in similar cases.
