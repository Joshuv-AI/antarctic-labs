A High-Fidelity Multi-Model Benchmark Dataset for General Aviation Anomaly Detection Generated via Physics-Based Fault Injection
Authors: Jing Lu, Yali Fang, Ziyi Huang, Yuan Zhang, Songhan Fan, Xuan Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Data-driven safety monitoring in general aviation (GA) faces a critical bottleneck: the scarcity of high-quality flight data containing verified anomalies, particularly across heterogeneous aircraft fleets. Existing datasets often lack physical realism or are limited to single aircraft models. Here, we present a comprehensive benchmark dataset derived from 120 real flight sorties (approximately 1 million data points) collected from Cessna 172 (R/S) and Cirrus SR20 (G1/G6) training aircraft. We developed a physics-based synthetic injection framework to generate four types of distinct anomalies: throttle surge, flight path deviation, cylinder misfire, and pitch excursion. The dataset is structured hierarchically by anomaly type and aircraft model, providing paired normal and abnormal samples for direct counterfactual analysis. We validate the dataset’s utility through statistical distribution analysis and baseline anomaly detection benchmarks. This resource bridges the gap between simulation and reality, enabling the development of robust, model-agnostic monitoring algorithms for aviation safety.

Background & Summary

General aviation (GA) constitutes a vital segment of the global airspace system but suffers from a disproportionately higher accident rate compared to commercial air carriers. Safety analyses indicate that operational exceedances, such as violations of weight and center of gravity limits, significantly contribute to fatal accidents1. Furthermore, loss of control in-flight (LOC-I) has been identified as a leading cause of hull losses and fatalities, necessitating robust monitoring systems to detect precursors before critical failures occur2, promoting data-driven safety assurance3.

However, the development of automated flight data monitoring (FDM) for GA is hindered by the high variability of pilot proficiency and the heterogeneity of aircraft fleets. Unlike commercial aviation with standardized data streams, GA fleets often consist of mixed aircraft types, requiring algorithms capable of handling heterogeneous data distributions4, a key challenge in transfer learning5. While recent studies have made progress in predicting safety-critical metrics using supervised learning6, a critical barrier remains: the scarcity of open-access, high-fidelity flight datasets containing verified anomalies.

This scarcity persists even with the availability of several large-scale repositories. As summarized in Table 1, existing resources force a choice between fleet diversity, data fidelity, and the availability of appropriate anomaly labels. For instance, the extensive NGAFID7 repository is the source for valuable subsets like the GATS dataset8, but their focus lies on self-supervised tasks like data reconstruction rather than providing explicit fault labels. Conversely, the NASA DASHlink dataset9 offers high-fidelity data but is limited to a single commercial aircraft type. This collective lack of a benchmark with labeled, physically-consistent, and multi-model data creates a significant “sim-to-real” gap, restricting the validation of advanced deep learning architectures. These include hybrid models like LSTM-VAE10,11 that capture temporal-stochastic dependencies, and recent transformer-based approaches12,13 which utilize self-attention mechanisms14 for long-term anomaly modeling. To bridge the specific gaps identified in Table 1, we introduce a high-fidelity benchmark dataset constructed from 120 real flight sorties operated by the Civil Aviation Flight University of China. The dataset covers a heterogeneous fleet of Cessna 172 (R/S) and Cirrus SR20 (G1/G6) aircraft. To address the lack of ground truth labels, we employed a physics-based injection framework to generate four types of safety-critical faults: throttle surge, flight path deviation, cylinder misfire, and pitch excursion. By modeling the kinematic and thermodynamic responses of the aircraft, our dataset provides a rigorous benchmark for evaluating anomaly detection algorithms, ranging from traditional methods like isolation forest15 to modern deep learning architectures.

Methods

Data Acquisition

The raw flight data were acquired from the quick access recorders (QAR) utilizing secure digital (SD) cards installed on training aircraft at the Civil Aviation Flight University of China. The data collection covers a heterogeneous fleet comprising four distinct aircraft models: Cessna 172R, Cessna 172S, Cirrus SR20, and Cirrus SR20 G6. These aircraft represent both conventional analogue gauge cockpits and advanced Garmin glass cockpits. All flight parameters were sampled at a frequency of 1 Hz, capturing critical telemetry including geospatial coordinates, engine performance metrics, and attitude indicators.

Physics-Based Anomaly Injection Framework

To generate high-fidelity anomalous samples, we developed a synthetic fault injection engine that adheres to aircraft kinematics and thermodynamics. We report the exact implementation settings for each anomaly type below. Rather than using one universal anomaly-window duration, each anomaly class followed its own window-sampling rule, parameter ranges, and coupling coefficients.

Cylinder Misfire (Partial Power Loss)

This scenario mimics a partial power loss event, such as cylinder misfire or localized fuel starvation. Unlike a complete cooling system failure (which would result in an engine overheating and temperature spike), a loss of combustion in specific cylinders directly causes a progressive change in thermal output. We selected a random subset of cylinders and applied a noisy temperature trend to the CHT. In the released benchmark generation pipeline, anomaly segments for this fault type occupied approximately 2% of each trajectory, with segment lengths sampled uniformly between 30 and 60 timestamps. The target thermal perturbation for each segment was sampled from U(10, 30)°F, and the stochastic term was instantiated as additive Gaussian noise with standard deviation 1.5°F. Pitch Excursion (Aerodynamic Coupling)

This scenario simulates abrupt attitude upsets, a precursor to LOC-I. We modeled the aerodynamic coupling between pitch attitude, vertical speed, and normal load factor (Nz). The pitch is modified by an additive term ΔP ~ U(±5°, ±15°). To guarantee the anomaly represents a critical exceedance, the perturbed pitch was strictly forced to exceed the nominal operational envelope (below −15° or above 18°) by an additional 2° margin if the initial perturbation did not breach these limits. In the released benchmark generation pipeline, anomaly segments for this fault type occupied approximately 1.2% of each trajectory (controlled by a segment generation ratio of 0.03), and each segment duration was sampled between 1 and 4 timestamps. The sign and magnitude of ΔP were sampled independently for each segment under a fixed global random seed of 1314. The vertical speed (Vspd) and normal acceleration (NormAc) are updated to reflect the change in the lift vector and G-load using aerodynamic gain coefficients of 80 and 0.08, respectively. Limitations of Cross-Coupling Modeling

While our physics-based injection framework captures the primary thermodynamic and kinematic couplings for each anomaly type, it does not model every possible cross-coupling effect across the entire six degrees of freedom (6-DOF) aircraft dynamics. For instance, the throttle surge scenario focuses on thermal lag and fuel flow but does not explicitly modify airspeed or pitch attitude; these variables evolve naturally based on the original flight dynamics and any pilot/autopilot responses present in the baseline data. The current design prioritizes controlled benchmarking of detection algorithms on clearly defined fault signatures, avoiding overly complex synthetic feedback loops, while the underlying real flight data preserves the full richness of actual aircraft behavior.

Data Records

The data is organized within the root directory, which is further split into normal_data (baseline operations) and abnormal_data (synthetic faults).

Validation of preprocessing fidelity confirmed that steep turns and stall recovery exercises remained intact in the cleaned data, supporting the dataset’s suitability for high-dynamics analysis.

Evaluation Metrics

To quantitatively assess the performance of the baseline anomaly detection methods, we adopt four standard metrics. For the traditional point-based methods, Precision, Recall, F1-score, and AUC were computed at the timestamp level. For the deep sequence-based methods, we formed sliding windows of 30 timestamps with a stride of 5 timestamps; a window was labeled anomalous if it contained at least one anomalous timestamp, and the metrics were computed at the window level.

To evaluate the theoretical maximum detection capability of the baseline models independent of specific threshold-selection heuristics, we adopt the standard Best-F1 evaluation protocol, which is widely utilized in state-of-the-art time-series anomaly detection literature12. For each algorithm, the final decision threshold was determined by exhaustively searching over the percentiles of the output anomaly scores to maximize the global F1-score on the evaluation subset. This metric explicitly demonstrates the representation capacity of the models to distinguish anomalous events from normal operational manifolds, thus avoiding under-reporting true algorithmic capability due to sub-optimal thresholding.

These differences are directly tied to the distinct physical characteristics encoded in each anomaly type, which were designed to exhibit varying degrees of separability in the feature space. Throttle surge incorporates time-dependent thermal lag (τ = 2–8 s), a known hysteresis effect in piston engine thermodynamics. Pitch excursion induces strong aerodynamic coupling between attitude perturbation (ΔP ∈ [±5°, ±15°]) and normal acceleration (Equations 7-8). Flight path deviation maintains kinematically consistent coordinates (Equations 3-4) with limited heading deviation (θ ∈ [±15°, ±30°]). Cylinder misfire follows a gradual thermal decay trajectory with constrained secondary effects (Equations 5-6).

As a result, the anomalies present structured heterogeneity in detectability: Throttle surge (thermodynamic anomaly) is difficult for traditional point-based methods (AUC ≈ 0.41–0.48) but well-handled by sequence-aware models (AUC > 0.83), as they can leverage the temporal lag structure encoded in the data (Equation 2). Pitch excursion is highly detectable across all algorithms (AUC up to 1.00) due to its direct coupling with load factor. Flight path deviation and cylinder misfire present greater challenge, with AUC remaining close to chance (≈0.50) for several methods.

The physical consistency of all mechanisms was independently verified in Fig. 1. This structured heterogeneity makes the dataset particularly valuable as a benchmark: it encompasses both pronounced anomalies suitable for validating basic detection capability and subtle deviations that stress-test advanced algorithms25,26.

Usage Notes

The dataset supports supervised anomaly detection, unsupervised outlier detection, and transfer learning research across heterogeneous fleets. To facilitate easy adoption, a starter Python script demonstrating how to effectively load and pair the normal-abnormal flight data has been provided alongside the dataset in our GitHub repository.

Ethics statement

This study utilizes retrospective operational flight data collected from routine training sorties at the Civil Aviation Flight University of China. The data collection involved passive recording via quick access recorders (QAR) and did not involve any direct intervention, physical risk, or deviation from standard operating procedures for the flight crews. Crucially, all anomalous samples presented in this dataset were generated synthetically using a physics-based simulation framework post-flight; no actual in-flight failures were induced.
