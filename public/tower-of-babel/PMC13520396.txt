Primary left atrial spindle cell sarcoma with low-grade malignant features mimicking recurrent myxoma: a case report
Authors: Jing-wei Zhang, Yi Wang, Wei Li, Feng-juan Yao, Dong-hong Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Primary cardiac spindle cell sarcoma is extremely rare, with atypical clinical manifestations and imaging features. This case report details a 54-year-old female patient with a primary cardiac spindle cell sarcoma of the left atrium, initially thought to represent a recurrent cardiac myxoma. Despite utilizing multiple imaging techniques, the patient’s complex clinical history complicated the diagnostic process, and the correct diagnosis was established after surgical resection and pathological evaluation. This case highlights the diagnostic pitfalls associated with presumed recurrent cardiac myxomas and underscores the importance of careful pathological assessment and long-term surveillance, even in tumors with low-grade malignant features.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13019-026-03980-7.

Introduction

Primary cardiac tumors are exceedingly rare, with an incidence ranging from 0.001% to 0.03%. Approximately 25% of these tumors are malignant, and sarcomas accounting for the majority of malignant cases. The most common cardiac sarcoma subtypes include angiosarcoma, leiomyosarcoma, rhabdomyosarcoma, and osteosarcoma, whereas cardiac spindle cell sarcomas are among the least frequently reported. According to the World Health Organization (WHO) Classification of Tumours of the Heart(4th and 5th editions) [1, 2], spindle cell sarcoma is considered as a descriptive diagnosis within primary cardiac sarcomas, with variable morphologic patterns and differentiation, and may exhibit low- to intermediate-grade malignant features. Clinically, patients often present with nonspecific manifestations, such as embolism, intracardiac obstruction or local invasion, making timely diagnosis and treatment challenging [3–5].

This case report details a patient with a primary cardiac spindle cell sarcoma of the left atrium, initially thought to represent a recurrent cardiac myxoma, but correctly diagnosed following surgical resection.

Case presentation

A 54-year-old female was referred to the cardiothoracic surgery unit for further management with an initial diagnosis of recurrent left atrial myxoma. The patient was asymptomatic, reporting no chest pain, cough, exertional dyspnea, unexplained weight loss, or fever. Two days prior to admission, a routine echocardiogram at a local hospital revealed an intracardiac mass in the left atrium. Given her history of cardiac myxoma, which has been surgically resected via thoracoscopy under cardiopulmonary bypass at an outside institution three years earlier, clinicians suspected a recurrence. However, histopathological slides and detailed pathology reports from the previous surgery were not available despite repeated attempts to retrieve them. Therefore, it could not be definitively confirmed whether the initial diagnosis of cardiac myxoma had been histologically validated, or whether the lesion represented an alternative neoplasm that may have been misdiagnosed. Additionally, in the past two years, the patient had undergone surgical resection for lung adenocarcinoma, a hysterectomy, and a gastric polypectomy.

On physical examination, her blood pressure was 122/75mmHg, heart rate was 66 bpm, and her respiratory rate was 20 breaths per minute. Cardiovascular and respiratory examinations were unremarkable, except for postoperative scars observed on her right chest and abdomen. Laboratory tests revealed that the inflammatory markers such as C-reactive protein, ESR and white blood cell counts were within the normal range. Regarding cardiac biomarkers, NT-proBNP was slightly elevated at 177.0 pg/mL (normal range < 125 pg/mL), while creatine kinase-MB (CK-MB) and Troponin T remained within normal limits. Parameters indicating liver function, kidney function and coagulation were almost normal. Tumor biomarkers, including alpha-fetoprotein (AFP), carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), cancer antigen 125 (CA125), and carbohydrate antigen 199 (CA199) were not elevated. A 12-lead electrocardiogram showed sinus rhythm with mild ST-T changes.

Investigations

Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) revealed a large, broad-based, irregular mass measuring approximately 34 mm×23 mm, attached to the posterior wall of the left atrium, close to the posterior mitral annulus (Fig. 1, Supplementary Video1&Video 2). Color doppler flow imaging showed accelerated blood flow through the mitral valve, with a peak gradient (PG) of 6 mmHg and a mean gradient (MG) of 2 mmHg. Mild to moderate mitral valve regurgitation was also observed (Supplementary Video 3).

To further investigate her condition, the patient underwent coronary computed tomography angiography as part of the preoperative evaluation, which demonstrated no significant coronary artery abnormalities. The examination also incidentally revealed an irregular mass in the left atrium, measuring approximately 46 mm × 23 mm, with an indistinct boundary and a broad-based attachment to the posterior left atrial wall (Fig. 2). The enhanced scan showed heterogeneous progressive enhancement. The mass located near the posterior leaflet of mitral valve and the bilateral inferior pulmonary veins. However, tumor vascular supply was not specifically evaluated in this examination. Based on the patient’s medical history, the radiologist suspected the left atrial mass could be a recurrence of a myxoma. Given her history of lung adenocarcinoma, a positron emission tomography (PET) scan was also performed, which showed no significantly elevated metabolic activity in the area, ruling out the possibility of cardiac metastasis.

Management

Based on the patient’s history of cardiac myxoma and multiple imaging findings, clinicians suspected that the mass in the left atrium might be a recurrence of the myxoma, and open-heart surgery was scheduled.

The patient underwent cardiac reoperation, during which the left atrial tumor was resected via conventional median sternotomy under cardiopulmonary bypass, with simultaneous left atrial reconstruction and mitral valve repair. Intraoperative transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) revealed a solid slightly hyperechoic mass with a broad base attached to the posterior wall of the left atrium, extending to the ostia of bilateral inferior pulmonary veins. The tumor measured approximately 58 mm ×25 mm, with strip-shaped blood flow signals detected. The mass exhibited slight movement with cardiac cycle, protruding toward the mitral orifice and adjacent to the posterior leaflet and posteromedial commissure of the mitral valve (Fig. 3, Supplementary Video 4–7). The diastolic blood flow through the mitral orifice was accelerated, and color doppler imaging revealed mild to moderate systolic regurgitation.

During the operation, the left atrium was accessed via a right atrial-atrial septal approach. A large mass was observed extending from the posterior wall of the left atrium to the medial junction of the anterior and posterior mitral valve leaflets. The mass had a long diameter of approximately 60 mm with a broad base, and it could not be pushed forward. The head of the mass protruded toward the mitral orifice, closely related to the medial junction of the anterior and posterior mitral valve leaflets, but the pulmonary vein ostia were unaffected. After the tumor resection, a small defect was identified in the A1 area of the mitral valve, which was subsequently repaired. Reconstruction of the posterior wall of the left atrium and the inferior part of the atrial septum was also performed. Continuous intraoperative transesophageal echocardiography demonstrated no residual intracardiac mass within the left atrial cavity following resection, and only mild mitral regurgitation was observed.

Unfortunately, intraoperative frozen biopsy revealed that the lesion was not a recurrence of myxoma but a spindle cell tumor in the left atrium, with positive margins. This diagnosis was confirmed by subsequent routine paraffin biopsy (Fig. 4). Histologically, the tumor was composed of spindle cells in a woven arrangement, with abundant cytoplasm and weak eosinophilia. Some cells exhibited prominent nucleoli and pathological mitotic figures. No obvious myxoid areas or tumor necrosis were identified. On permanent sections, microscopic tumor involvement was present at the submitted surgical margin and at the tumor base, consistent with a positive margin (R1 resection).

Immunohistochemical analysis demonstrated focal reactivity for Actin, CD34, and CDK4, whereas Desmin, H-Caldesmon, P16, HMB-45, Melan-A, S-100, SOX10, STAT-6, and ERG were negative. The Ki-67 proliferation index was approximately 10%. Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) showed no amplification of the MDM2/CEP12 gene. Although the absence of MDM2 amplification does not entirely exclude the possibility of undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcoma, integrating the histomorphological features, immunophenotype, and molecular findings, the tumor was diagnosed as a primary cardiac spindle cell sarcoma with low-grade malignant features and focal myofibroblastic differentiation, in accordance with the WHO Classification of Tumours of the Heart (4th and 5th editions) [1, 2].

The left atrial tumor was surgically resected, and the patient remained clinically stable postoperatively. Permanent histopathology demonstrated microscopic residual tumor at the submitted surgical margin and at the tumor base (R1 resection). Postoperative cardiac CT showed no radiographically detectable residual left atrial mass, with a small amount of pericardial and pleural effusion. Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated preserved cardiac function. The patient was discharged three weeks after surgery. After discharge, she was followed up at an outside institution, and did not receive adjuvant chemotherapy or radiotherapy. At approximately 1 year postoperatively, follow-up CT showed no evidence of tumor recurrence at that time.

Discussion

Spindle cell tumors, characterized by elongated fibrous cells, are commonly found in the skin, soft tissues, bones, and other organs, but they are exceptionally rare in the heart, especially in the left atrium. To date, only a few case reports of primary cardiac spindle sarcoma have been published [5–8]. These reports describe tumors arising in different cardiac locations, including the left atrium and mitral valve, with marked heterogeneity in histological grade, clinical course, and outcomes, underscoring both the rarity and heterogeneity of this entity.

Primary cardiac tumors are rare, with approximately 75% being benign and myxomas accounting for nearly half of all cardiac tumors. In contrast, cardiac spindle cell tumors are far less frequent and often exhibit variable clinical behavior, ranging from low-grade to highly aggressive forms. Reported outcomes appear to depend on multiple factors, including tumor grade, completeness of surgical resection, and duration of follow-up [7, 9]. The differential diagnosis of cardiac tumors is inherently challenging due to the nonspecific clinical presentation and overlapping imaging characteristics with other cardiac lesions [7, 10]. Therefore, pathological examination combined with immunohistochemical analysis remain crucial for establishing an accurate diagnosis and guiding management.

In the present case, a primary cardiac spindle cell sarcoma arising in the left atrium posed significant diagnostic challenges despite the use of multiple imaging modalities. The lesion was initially presumed to represent a recurrence of cardiac myxoma. However, definitive diagnosis was established only after surgical resection and pathological assessment, and the tumor was ultimately classified as a descriptive spindle cell sarcoma within the spectrum of primary cardiac sarcomas, in accordance with the WHO Classification of Tumours of the Heart (4th and 5th editions).

Advanced imaging techniques are essential in the differential diagnosis of cardiac tumors. Echocardiography remains the first-line modality for initial detection and assessment of tumor morphology and mobility. Cardiac CT and/or MRI can provide complementary anatomical information regarding involvement of the pericardium and adjacent structures, and may help evaluate vascularity when dedicated contrast protocols and post-processing are applied. Additionally, PET-CT is highly effective for evaluating the extra-cardiac involvement and detecting possible distant metastasis [11–15]. Nevertheless, in the present case, dedicated imaging specifically optimized for assessment of tumor vascularity was not available, limiting detailed evaluation of tumor perfusion for surgical planning.

Given the rarity and poor prognosis associated with primary cardiac spindle cell sarcoma, early diagnosis and timely management are crucial for optimizing patient outcomes. This case highlights not only the diagnostic complexity of cardiac spindle cell tumors, but also a common real-world diagnostic pitfall—the inability to review histopathological material from a prior surgery performed at an outside institution. consequently, it remains uncertain whether the initial lesion represented a true cardiac myxoma or an alternative neoplasm that was misdiagnosed. This uncertainty emphasizes the importance of comprehensive pathological documentation, cautious interpretation of presumed tumor recurrence, and the need for long-term surveillance. Continued research is needed to better understand the biological behavior and optimal management strategies for this rare cardiac malignancy.

Conclusions

In summary, primary cardiac spindle cell sarcoma is extremely rare, with atypical clinical presentations and imaging features. This case demonstrates that tumor recurrence may occur several years after initial surgical resection, underscoring the importance of long-term surveillance, even in patients with low-grade pathological features. By summarizing the clinical features, imaging characteristics, diagnostic and treatment process of this case, we aim to enhance the understanding of echocardiographers and clinicians regarding cardiac spindle cell tumors, ultimately promoting early detection and timely intervention. Further research is needed to better understand the tumor’s biological behavior, prognosis, and optimal therapeutic strategies for primary cardiac spindle cell sarcoma.
