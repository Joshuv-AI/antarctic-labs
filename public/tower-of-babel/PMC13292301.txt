Effects of late-life captivity: preliminary findings following the 7 October attack
Authors: Yaakov Hoffman, Amit Shrira, Moshe Bensimon, Yuval Palgi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground: Posttraumatic responses (PTR, i.e. posttraumatic stress disorder [PTSD], complex PTSD, prolonged grief, depression, and anxiety) have not been examined following late-life captivity. Most of the captivity literature focuses on young prisoners of war and, to a lesser extent, on younger civilians.Objective: To assess PTR levels in older ex-captives and potential risk factors for elevated PTR following late-life captivity.Methods: We assessed 26 Jewish Israeli older adults (mean age = 82.11±7.23, from Kibbutzim [rural close-knit agricultural communities] in the Gaza envelope) who were exposed directly to the 7 October attack. Half the participants were ex-captives (released from Hamas captivity), and another 13 were non-captive displaced survivors. Groups were comparable on exposure variables and most demographics.Results: Except for ex-captives exhibiting higher prolonged grief levels, participants reported similar low PTR levels with no group differences. Furthermore, ex-captives who experienced malicious maltreatment during captivity showed higher PTR levels. Finally, two unique correlations were found only in ex-captives: Current loneliness correlated with higher prolonged grief levels, and current lower sense-of-safety correlated with higher PTSD levels.Conclusion: Results are discussed via a gero-traumatology perspective. The data are aligned with the inoculation hypothesis, suggesting that older adults are resilient even following exposure to extreme trauma. Risk factors for elevated PTR were identified, suggesting that captivity is not a monolithic entity, yet it may still distinctly differ from other forms of trauma in terms of its psychological concomitants. Although preliminary, the results illuminate the effects of extreme trauma on older adults.KEYWORDS: 7 October attack, captivity, trauma, prolonged grief, old age

This study focuses on late-life captivity following the 7 October 2023, attack (Alonso, 2025; Clarfield & Levine, 2024; Palgi et al., 2024). This one-day attack was so extreme and barbaric, it was contextualized in Holocaust terminology (Alonso 2025). Beginning with intense missile fire, thousands of Hamas terrorists infiltrated the Israeli border, to burn, rape and kill, resulting in 1,200 murdered Israeli citizens with thousands injured (Palgi et al. 2024). Terrorists took 251 captives into Gaza, 33 of them were older than 65 (Clarfield & Levine, 2024; Palgi et al. 2024).

Captivity is a devastating traumatic event, leaving significant and enduring psychological effects in younger adults, from both military (Solomon and Dekel 2007) and civilian contexts (Hoffman et al. 2018). Posttraumatic responses (PTR) following late-life captivity have not been hitherto explored. Addressing this gap may be illuminating, as the aftermath of late-life trauma is theoretically disputed (Lapp et al. 2011). The inoculation hypothesis posits that, due to prior life experience and more adaptive emotional regulation, older adults are less affected by trauma (Siskind et al. 2016). This approach is supported by the general finding of lower PTSD prevalence for older adults (Pless Kaiser et al. 2019). In contrast, the vulnerability hypothesis suggests that aging processes already tax coping resources, leaving older adults with less available resources to cope with trauma, rendering them more vulnerable (Parker et al. 2016). Literature reviews and a meta-analysis (Cook & Simiola 2017; Siskind et al. 2016) generally support the inoculation hypothesis, but in some cases (e.g. natural disasters), older adults show higher distress (Parker et al. 2016).

Given the literature gap regarding PTR in late-life captivity, and considering the above-mentioned theoretical dispute, the first goal was to assess PTR levels in older ex-captives. PTR typically includes (a) posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD), (b) complex-PTSD (CPTSD), which fulfills PTSD criteria along with three clusters addressing disturbances in self-organization (DSO), and (c) prolonged grief (Maercker et al. 2013). The latter refers to continued longing for- or preoccupation with- the deceased; older adults typically experience more bereavement yet may be better at coping with bereavement (Redican et al. 2025). Based on previous captivity data (Pfeiffer 2011) we also included d) depression, and e) anxiety.

Additional unique features of captivity highlight two more goals. In captivity, one's fate is under another's control for prolonged periods (Hoffman et al. 2018), thus captives may experience wide ranges of maltreatment, extending from malicious maltreatment (e.g. physical abuse) to inadvertent maltreatment (e.g. food lacking for both captors and captives). Accordingly, the second goal examined whether malicious maltreatment was linked with increased PTR levels in older adults. Finally, captivity is also characterized by a diminished sense-of-safety (Levkovich et al. 2025) and a type of isolation/loneliness (Stein et al. 2015), captured by ‘a form of separation distress that results from failure to have one’s basic attachment needs fulfilled’ (Mikulincer & Shaver, 2007, p. 280). Following, the third goal examined if PTR in older ex-captives uniquely associates with current feelings of loneliness and reduced sense-of-safety.

Methods

Study sample and procedure

Sixteen (of the 33) older ex-captives were alive at the beginning of this study. One died shortly after, leaving 15 eligible participants, of whom 13 agreed to participate. All ex-captives were from Kibbutzim, i.e. rural agricultural collective communities, from the Gaza-envelope region. To discern net effects of captivity, we also sampled a comparison group of older Jewish-Israeli Kibbutz members from the same communities as the ex-captives, who survived the attack but were not taken into captivity (n = 13). Inclusion criteria related to being over 65; exclusion criteria addressed cognitive impairment, psychosis, or dissociative symptoms. To reiterate, both groups comprised Jewish-Israeli older Kibbutz members from the same close-knit communities who were attacked on 7 October. These communities lost many members in the attack. As houses were also destroyed, participants were temporarily resettled at the time of the study (e.g. hotels, retirement communities). During the trauma and its aftermath, comparison group members were with their loved ones, and many remained within their resettled, albeit dispersed, communities.

The study began after obtaining approval from the Bar-Ilan University Institutional Review Board (IRB, Approval No. 160524101). In line with the IRB approval, data collection (i.e. questionnaires) was carried out in a face-to-face interview format (with clinical psychologists/gerontologists) to monitor potential re-traumatization.

Participants were sampled with the aid of the Israeli Ministry of Welfare and Social Affairs, whose clinical staff initially approached regional social workers who knew the participants, and explained the study to them. After obtaining initial consent, direct contact was sensitively established. Participant recruitment was challenging, as a trusting rapport had to be established. The study took place between 7/31/2024 and 2/16/2025, 10–16 months after the 7 October attack.

Measures

Demographic variables included age, gender, education, and marital status. Previous trauma exposure was indexed by a 4-item adaptation of the traumatic life events questionnaire (Palgi et al. 2024); yes/no responses were summed. 7 October trauma exposure was indexed by summing yes/no questions (e.g. witnessing/experiencing murder/shooting/injuries/rape, knowing family/close friends who were killed/injured/taken captive/raped).

Captivity maltreatment experiences were indexed by 11 items, e.g. experiencing physical abuse, psychological abuse, medical neglect, etc., (α = .74). PTSD (α = .78) and DSO (α = .59) symptom levels were measured via the ICD-11 International Trauma Questionnaire (ITQ, Cloitre et al., 2018). Anxiety and depression were assessed by the PHQ-4 (Kroenke et al. 2009), a 4-item questionnaire (2 items each for anxiety and depression, α = .73). Prolonged grief was assessed by an 8-item measure (Killikelly et al., 2019, ICG-R, α = .81); participants were asked 10–16 months after the 7 October attack about their grief for persons killed in the attack. Loneliness was measured by the 3-item brief scale (Hughes et al., 2004, UCLA, α = .85). Sense-of-safety was assessed by two items (rated on a scale from 1-not at all to 5-very much) addressing one's current sense-of-safety (‘how safe’, ‘how protected’).

Group comparisons on demographic, exposure and PTR scores.

For the second goal, correlations between captivity experiences and PTR were conducted. Following previous studies (Favaro et al. 2000), we divided captivity experiences into malicious maltreatment vs. inadvertent maltreatment. The sum of malicious maltreatment items correlated with three PTR measures: PTSD (r = .70, p<.01), DSO (r = .65, p<.01), and anxiety (r = .72, p<.01); inadvertent maltreatment items did not significantly correlate with PTR (rs = .10 – .26, ps>.05).

The third goal assessed the linking of PTR with loneliness and one's sense-of-safety. Only in ex-captives did prolonged grief correlate with loneliness (r = .78, p<.001; comparisons: r = .05, p>.05); the difference between correlations was significant, Z = 2.44, p<.01. Likewise, only in ex-captives did PTSD symptom level correlate with a sense-of-safety (r = −.84, p<.001; comparisons: r = .02, p>.05), these correlations also differed significantly, Z = −2.77, p<.01.

Discussion

Aligned with the inoculation hypothesis (Cook and Simiola 2017; Siskind et al. 2016), the study's first goal revealed that most PTR measures were low and similar across groups, reflecting impressive resilience following extreme trauma. Moreover, following decades of missile attacks, these rural Gaza-envelope communities were recognized for their high resilience, even before 7 October, leading to the proposal that older adults in these rural areas preserved their resilience through a strong sense of belonging to the communities they built (Nuttman-Shwartz et al. 2015). Pending further examination, it could also be that the widespread volunteering of ex-captives in public awareness campaigns addressing the plight of fellow captives, may have given ex-captives greater meaning (Park 2010), which in turn could have increased their resilience and delayed symptom onset. It is thus possible that if such meaningful activities were to subside PTR risk may increase.

Elevated prolonged grief following trauma was observed in ex-captives. Two possible reasons are speculated. First, ex-captives were unaware of the death/loss magnitude of their close ones until their release, wherein the overwhelming impact of this information hit at once, rendering processing of such grief difficult. Second, their extreme commitment to those remaining in captivity may have possibly reinforced survivor guilt and related grief.

Regarding the second goal, aligned with previous data (Favaro et al. 2000), those who experienced malicious maltreatment displayed higher PTR risk. These results indicate that captivity is not a monolithic entity, warranting consideration of a more tailored policy for older released ex-captives. The third goal revealed unique correlations only for ex-captives, whereby loneliness was linked with prolonged grief, and sense-of-safety was linked with PTSD symptoms. Bearing in mind the correlational nature of these results, we tentatively suggest the following explanation for each result. First, experiencing post-captivity loneliness can possibly echo the social loss endured in captivity (Mikulincer & Shaver, 2007; Stein et al., 2015), in turn this may possibly re-activate losses due to death. Second, the linking of a lower sense-of-safety with higher post-captivity PTSD symptom levels, a finding previously observed in Yazidi ex-captives (Hoffman et al., 2018), seemingly aligns with captivity being characterized by helplessness and a lack of control (Hoffman et al., 2018; Levkovich et al., 2025). Following, a reduced post-captivity sense-of-safety may possibly reactivate similar experiences of captivity trauma.

Findings should be interpreted considering the study's limitations. First, the small sample, along with a cross-sectional correlational design, allows neither discernment of directionality nor causality. Second, brief questionnaires (e.g. PHQ-4), albeit important to secure participant agreement, may hinder precise diagnosis. Despite these limitations, this study is the first to examine PTR in older ex-captives. Results highlight the resilience of older ex-captives while also showing unique effects of captivity. The data suggest that the desired post-captivity environment should be sensitive to ‘safety’ and ‘loneliness’. Given both the rise in terror attacks and increased longevity, greater intersections of severe trauma and older adulthood are expected (Avidor, 2024). Following, these data may unfortunately be relevant beyond the current context. These preliminary findings contribute to the corpus of knowledge addressing effects of experiencing extreme trauma in older adulthood.
