GIS-based landslide susceptibility zonation using weighted overlay analysis with AHP: a case study of Malappuram district, Kerala, India
Authors: K Shanu, P Senthil Kumar, B Senthil Rathi, Gayathri Rangasamy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMalappuram district, Kerala, ranked 7th among the most landslide-prone districts in India, according to the Landslide Atlas of India 2023 from the National Remote Sensing Centre (NRSC). The aim of this study is to map landslide susceptibility zones at the district level in Malappuram, Kerala, using GIS-based Weighted Overlay Analysis (WOA). The most commonly used factors for slope failure preparation were slope, elevation, aspect, curvature, land use/land cover, annual rainfall, distance to road, distance to river, soil depth, geology, LS factor, drainage density, Stream Power Index, and Topographic Wetness Index. The AHP technique has been applied to weight the 14 conditioning factors, and Pearson correlation has been used to assess the relation among these variables and between these variables and the landslide points. We have found that most variables are independent, with a maximum correlation coefficient of 0.72. Five respondents ranked 14 conditioning variables using a structured AHP matrix in a paired comparison. The Consistency Ratio obtained is 0.047, which is < 0.10. Slope was given the highest weightage (18%), followed by geology (13%) and annual rainfall (10%), which have the highest contributions to slope failures. The 14 conditioning factors were reclassified into five categories with values ranging from 1 to 5. The 14 classified factors were then combined to produce the landslide susceptibility map of the study area. The landslide susceptible maps were classified into low (39%, ~ 1384 km2), moderate (51%, ~ 1811 km2), and high landslide susceptibility zones (10%, ~ 355 km2). The high landslide susceptible areas are distributed over, in, and around the north-east highland of the district and are characterised by a combination of high slopes, charnockite geology, and high rainfall. The model has been validated using historical landslides point and random non-landslide points. The ratio of landslide and non-landslide points is 70:30 for training and testing, respectively. We obtained AUC values of 0.921 and 0.897 for training and testing, respectively. At the village level, the most affected villages due to landslides have been selected based on the percentage of land area falling within the highly landslide susceptible zones among all 135 revenue villages of the district. The most susceptible villages, in descending order, are Kerala Estate, Chokkad, Akampadam, Karulai, and Kurumbalangode. A detailed landslide susceptibility map of Malappuram district will enable scientists and authorities to plan mitigation actions, regulate construction activities in highly affected areas, and implement an early warning system at the local level.Keywords: Landslide susceptibility, Weighted overlay analysis, Analytic hierarchy process (AHP), Malappuram, Western Ghats, GIS, Remote sensing, Hazard zonation, ROC-AUC validationSubject terms: Environmental sciences, Environmental social sciences, Geography, Geography, Natural hazards

Introduction

Landslides are the most dominant natural hazard in tropical and subtropical mountain terrain, owing to a combination of steep slopes, heavy rainfall, and rapidly changing land-use factors that contribute to slope instability. It is scientifically proven that monsoon-triggered landslides cause the majority of casualties and economic losses among geohazards across Asia1,2. Landslides have also been reported in the Western Ghats of peninsular India, which is characterised by high monsoonal rainfall, large-scale deforestation, quarrying, and a transport network. It is evident that these human and climatic factors affect and destabilise natural hydrologic systems, resulting in landslide hazards3,4. Landslides are also observed over the peninsular India region of the Western Ghats5. The Western Ghats region is characterised by heavy monsoon rainfall, widespread deforestation, quarrying and construction of a transport network. These human and climate-related factors affect and destabilise the natural hydrologic system, leading to landslides3,4. Landslide frequency has increased during the past 20 years in the southwest part of the Western Ghats of Kerala state. In 2018 and 2019 heavy monsoon triggered massive landslide and more than 400 casualties were reported in the Kerala State and caused migration of population. Malappuram was the most landslide-affected district in Kerala during 2018 and 2019, and more than 26 landslide casualties were reported. Landslides also produce major environmental impacts, including damage to landforms, vegetation and river systems6.

The hazard assessment helps plan land use and structure installation in and around landslide-prone areas, and to plan for disaster mitigation by understanding the risk of slope failure in a specific area. With the advancement of satellite data and GIS technology, susceptibility mapping can be achieved even in data-poor areas (such as the South and Southeast Asian regions) with available resources7,8. Numerous methods like statistical approach, machine learning approach, knowledge driven multi-criteria approach have been developed and used for landslide susceptibility mapping, although the results varied from one technique to another9–16 .Machine-learning approaches have also been widely used for landslide susceptibility modelling17.Hybrid and neuro-fuzzy models have also been used for spatial landslide prediction18.

The WOA based on AHP has been extensively applied at the district level in Kerala, in Wayanad and Kottayam districts19, and a high-resolution district-level landslide susceptibility assessment map for Malappuram district is currently lacking. Although several studies have been conducted for landslide susceptibility analysis for different regions of Kerala state and Malappuram district as part of a larger state-wide hazard assessment study, which was found to be too low for local planning, or a lack of scale appropriate for watershed scale analysis or at sub-district scale3,19,20. There is no high-resolution susceptibility assessment map with risk classes for the Malappuram district that could guide land-use planners in mitigating hazard factors. Hence, this study aims to develop a high-resolution district-level landslide susceptibility map of Malappuram district through a robust landslide assessment procedure that applies correlation-screened factors and AHP, and uses ROC-AUC-validated results, as well as to assign landslide susceptibility values to 135 revenue villages.

Methodology

A five-stage methodology was adopted: (1) acquisition and pre-processing of landslide inventory data; (2) derivation and reclassification of fourteen landslide conditioning parameters; (3) screening for multicollinearity and calculating landslide conditioning parameter weights using AHP method; (4) deriving landslide susceptibility zonation map using weighted overlay analysis; (5) validation of the map using ROC-AUC analysis on landslide and non-landslide points.

Study area

The district Malappuram (10–12 N, 75–77 E) spreads over an area of about 3550 km2 and shares 9.13% of the total area of Kerala state. The district is bounded by the Nilgiris district of Tamil Nadu to the east, the Arabian Sea to the west, the districts of Kozhikode & Wayanad to the north, & district Palakkad & Thrissur to the south. The district comprises three wide physiographic zones. The highlands are located in the eastern & northeastern parts of the district (> 500 m above sea level); the midlands consist of the intermediate zone (50–500 m); and the coastal lowlands extend to the western part of the district (< 50 m). Steep slopes exist in the northeast of the Western Ghats, consisting of charnockite, hornblende-biotite gneiss, etc. The annual average rainfall exceeds 3000 mm in some regions. The population density of Malappuram district in 2021 is estimated to be 1158 per km2. Increasing encroachment in hilly areas intensifies the landslide hazard(https://ildm.kerala.gov.in/wp-content/uploads/2025/04/Report-on-wayanad-landslide-1.pdf)21. The landslides that occurred in Malappuram killed 26 people in 2018 & 60 people in 2019, respectively. Both deaths are higher than in any other district of Kerala in the respective years, as per the NRSC landslide atlas 2023(https://www.isro.gov.in/Landslide_Atlas_India.html)22 (Fig. 1).

Study area layout map Malappuram district.

Topography

The district has a prominent topographic variation from west to east. The terrain changes from nearly sea level near the coast to around 2484 m elevation towards the northeastern side of the Nilgiri massif. The topography is characterised by steep slopes, narrow river valleys, and deep gorges in the northeastern part, and by undulating terrain in the midlands and coastal areas (https://malappuram.nic.in/en/about-district/)23. The increased topographic relief contributes significantly to the landslide hazard as it directly influences the magnitude of slope and hence, the ratio between driving and resisting forces12,24.

Climate and hydrology

The district of Malappuram has a hot, humid, monsoon-type climate. It receives an average annual rainfall of about 2900 mm. Almost 75% of this rainfall occurs during the southwest monsoon period (June–September). The very short, high-intensity rainfall events are the predominant triggers of the Malappuram landslides. These events are triggered during the southwest monsoon season3,25. There are four main river systems, namely Chaliyar (169 km), Kadalundippuzha, Bharathapuzha, and Tirurpuzha, that drain the district, along with many smaller streams that cascade down the steep Western Ghats escarpment(https://malappuram.nic.in/en/about-district/)23.

Land use and vegetation

Based on the land use and land cover (LULC) classifications derived from Landsat OLI imagery (2017–2023), Malappuram district had settlements as 41.02% of the total area, followed by vegetation 39.95%, agricultural land 14.13%, bare land 3.29%, water bodies 1.55%, and wetland 0.05%. The area of forest land in Malappuram is 758.87 km, with the majority in the Nilambur and Wandoor regions. Constant loss of forest land reduces slope stability due to a lack of root strength. In 2023, according to Global Forest Watch data, the loss of 553 ha of natural forest and 663 kt CO2 equivalent emission resulted from it. Data source

Data required to derive the 14 conditioning factors includes: Landsat 8 OLI data (2017–2023) for land-use land-cover mapping, CartoDEM data for topographic factors, geological maps of the GSI for lithology, NASA POWER rainfall data (1992–2023) for rainfall, FAO Harmonised World Soil Database for soil depth data, road network data of OpenStreetMap and the data of HydroSHEDS rivers. All raster layers were projected to WGS 1984 UTM zone 43N and then resampled to 30 m resolution.

Landsat 8, developed by the U.S. Geological Survey (USGS), carried two sensors—Operational Land Imager (OLI) and Thermal Infrared Sensor (TIRS) that are widely used for regional land cover studies at 30 m resolution owing to radiometrically consistent data, and it also proved to be good for the LULC classification and NDVI computation27,28. NDVI was calculated using the formula: NDVI = (NIR-Red)/(NIR + Red). NIR Band is the Band 5, and Red band is Band 4 (0.64–0.67 m) (Table 1).

Derivation of conditioning factors

Topographical parameters

Slope, Elevation, aspect, curvature, LS factor, Drainage Density, SPI, and TWI were derived from the 30 m CartoDEM using the Spatial Analyst tools available in ArcGIS pro version 3.6.0 (https://www.arcgis.com)36. Slope is defined as the maximum rate of change in elevation over a neighbourhood of a cell and is expressed in degrees. Elevation is extracted directly from the DEM, while aspect is the direction in which the slope is at its maximum decrease. Profile curvature was used to indicate convex, concave, and relatively flat terrains. The LS factor is derived using the RUSLE formulation by Rozos et al.37, and SPI is approximated as SPI = As tan β38, where As is the specific catchment area and β is the local slope angle. TWI = ln(As/tan β)39. Drainage Density (DD) was obtained using a 1 km moving window calculation from the stream network, with the total length of streams per area40.

Non-topographical parameters

LULC was created from supervised classification of the Landsat 8 OLI image (cloud-free composite), by applying the Maximum-Likelihood algorithm. Six classes are recognised: water body, barren land, vegetation, settlement, agricultural land and wetland. The accuracy of this classification was evaluated using 200 training points, yielding an overall accuracy of 87.5% and a Kappa coefficient of 0.8441,pp. 537–564]. The geological unit from the GSI 1:250,000 map was used and classified into 5 lithological groups, such as charnockite, hornblende-biotite gneiss, Neogene sedimentary rock, quaternary sediment and water body. Annual rainfall was calculated by interpolating the 30-year average precipitation from NASA POWER data (1992–2023) using kriging in ArcGIS pro version 3.6.0, resulting in a 1 km grid that was resampled to 30 m. Road and River distances were calculated using Euclidean distances from the OpenStreetMap road network and the HydroSHEDS stream network, respectively. Soil depth was downloaded from the Harmonised World Soil Database from FAO. The spatial interpolation tool was used and classified into 5 soil depth categories (Fig. 3).

Multicollinearity analysis

In a multi-criteria overlay model, it is vital not to consider heavily correlated conditioning factors. It may lead to overestimation of the effect or to multiple counting of the influence of terrain properties, especially when many features are extracted from the same DEM, as in this study. Pearson’s r was computed for all 14 factors to detect interrelationships within the whole district sample of 3550 pixels (approx. 1 pixel/km2) from a stratified random sample (Table 2).

Determining weights with AHP

To derive the weight of each factor, Saaty’s44 Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP) was used, with pairwise comparisons systematically conducted. The study included five experts with knowledge of geomorphology, geology, and geospatial hazards. The comparison was made on a 1–9 point scale as suggested by Saaty44. The factor comparison was conducted independently by experts in a 14 × 14 matrix, and the final matrix was composed by multiplying each cell by the geometric mean for each expert. The resultant weight was extracted by taking the eigen vector corresponding to the normalised priority vector.

Weighted overlay analysis

Using the weighted overlay analysis tool within ArcGIS pro version 3.6.0, the 14 classified factors were combined. Each layer was standardised into 1 to 5 classes of landslide suitability, where 1 and 5 indicate the least to most susceptible terrain. For the weighted overlay, per cent values equal to the relative factor weights determined in the AHP analysis were assigned to each factor and summed. Finally, the continuous result map of landslide susceptibility was classified into three classes: Low (1.0–2.2), Moderate (2.2–3.4), and High (3.4–5.0), and a 30 m spatial-resolution landslide susceptibility map was generated.

Validation protocol

The model’s performance was rigorously analysed using separate training and testing datasets.

Landslide inventory: The total number of landslides identified, confirmed, and validated through field surveys was 120. These were taken from KSDMA field surveys of Malappuram district (2018 & 2019 landslide events), NRSC Landslide Atlas of India22 and Visual analysis of satellite images before and after the landslide events (pre and post Sentinel-2). These points represent the actual crown or the most active part of the landslide scarp.

Non-landslide points selection: Selected an equal number (n = 120) of points as landslides based on a stratified random sampling technique that satisfies 3 criteria: slope < 5° and altitude < 200 m45, distance from landslide feature > 500 m, proportions of landslide classes for non-landslide data points are the same as for landslides.

Training–testing split. A random split of 70% for the training data set (84 landslide & 84 non-landslide points) and 30% for the testing data set (36 landslide & 36 non-landslide points) was obtained, and spatially stratified random selection was applied to remove spatial correlation.

ROC-AUC Analysis: extracted the susceptibility score of each training/testing points from the raster and plotted ROC curves of these points; Calculated AUC value that corresponds to performance of each subset of data points, interpretation was based on interpretation of AUC value where > 0.9 is excellent, 0.8–0.9 very good and 0.7–0.8 good46, Kappa coefficient was calculated from confusion matrix derived using the optimum value determined from Youden index.

Results

Conditioning factor maps

The subsequent descriptions refer to the conditioning factor maps produced using the GIS methodology. It should be noted that Table 3 below summarizes the spatial distribution of each susceptibility class within the district for each mapped factor, enabling comparison of these characteristics within the district. Visual interpretation is provided by referring to Fig. 4 showing the distribution of the different classes of slope within the district of Malappuram, where it can be observed that the majority of steep slopes are concentrated in the northeastern highland area of the district, where slopes are highest. Table 3 then provides a quantitative interpretation that allows the distribution pattern to be examined numerically. This format has been used to discuss all the mapped factors.

Slope map.

Slope

The slope map revealed that a large majority of the study area (78.77%) consists of slopes under 15%, and that this land characteristic is predominantly confined to the riverine and coastal plain areas of Malappuram. Moderate slopes (15–25) cover 12.99% of the study area and are generally attributed to moderate instability risk, whilst steep and very steep slopes (> 25) cover 8.23% of Malappuram and occur predominantly in the northeast of the district within the highland region. The steepness of slopes increases substantially beyond the 25° threshold, with a significant risk increase for slopes over this threshold, as gravitational driving forces will overwhelm resistive forces in charnockitic and lateritic terrain during the monsoon period12.

Elevation

The majority of the district (81.22%) is less than 120 m, meaning the most critically situated area lies to the northeast of the district in the mountainous region. Even though only 1.79% of the study area lies at elevations between 1539 and 2484 m, it is given the highest rank value. This is due to terrain consisting of extremely steep slopes and often exposed or weathered bedrock, with a higher chance of orographic precipitation at higher elevations (Fig. 5).

Elevation map.

Aspect

Southeast-facing aspects represent the largest portion of the district at 12.45% and receive the highest rank value because they receive direct rainfall laden with moisture during the monsoon. We assign north- and north-west-facing aspects a lower rank value because we did not find a link between these aspects and a higher frequency of landslide occurrence within the study area inventory (Fig. 6).

Aspect map.

Curvature

Very convex terrain (> 0.5) accounts for 49.03% of the district, while very concave terrain accounts for 46.17%. Both features represent different types of instability processes, where the former can promote the removal of material by runoff and exposure of a shallow layer of soil during the monsoon, whilst the latter encourages the pooling of groundwater on the subsurface during heavy precipitation47,48 (Fig. 7).

Curvature map.

LULC

Land use/cover for the majority of the district consists of settlements (41.02%) and vegetated areas (39.95%). Settlements on hillsides receive the highest rating due to increased runoff from impervious surface coverage and loads placed on already unstable areas. Vegetated areas receive the lowest rank value, as it has been shown that roots increase soil shear strength, thereby providing a degree of support49,50 (Fig. 8).

Land use land cover.

Annual rainfall

Rainfall increases significantly from east to west and north to south within the study area, with annual rainfall over 2686–2896 mm falling over 28.21% of the area of the district in the highland areas. This is due to orographic lifting of saturated south-west monsoon winds up the Western Ghats, hence increasing orographic rainfall within this part of the district. Antecedent high rainfall means the soil becomes saturated, thereby decreasing matrix suction. High, short bursts of heavy rain increase pore water pressure within the soil, thereby making landslides more likely25 (Fig. 9).

Rainfall map.

Distance from road and river

A relatively high percentage of the Malappuram district falls within this low-ranking parameter, as indicated by its road density. Road construction on slopes increases the risk as cut and fill operations are utilised, thereby removing toe support and altering the natural profile of the slopes; they divert surface flow, increasing infiltration of water into the slopes and the soil51. Similarly, close proximity to river bodies poses an increased risk (Figs. 10 and 11).

Distance from road map.

Distance from river map.

Soil depth

The predominant range of soil depth is between 100 and 200 m, and the second range (0–25 m) represents the shallowest amount and therefore is likely to be less prone to mass movement than if the soil were a greater depth; only 2.04% of the district is shallower than 25 m. Shallow soil over rock substrata allows shallow translational slips to occur, as the failure plane can develop at the soil-rock interface45 (Fig. 12).

Soil depth map.

Geology

Charnockite is the most dominant lithological unit in Malappuram, accounting for 52.27% of the area. Although fresh charnockite is mechanically sound, under tropical weathering, a regolith forms, which is susceptible to failure under saturated conditions and, due to its lower mechanical strength, is less likely to cause rock slides than debris slides, as stated above4. Hornblende-biotite Gneiss is the second-largest component of Malappuram district (22.04%) and is similarly susceptible to weathering, as is a proportion of the Neogene and Quaternary Formations, which cover only 7.59% of the district, and receive the lower class rating for slope stability analysis (Fig. 13).

Geology map.

LS factor, drainage density, SPI and TWI

The slope length and steepness factors indicated that 43.28% of Malappuram consists of areas with a lowest factor value of 0–10 m, whilst only 40.43% have the highest class values of > 58 m. High LS factors occur predominantly along long, steep hill slopes to the northeast, where flow is more efficiently concentrated. The majority of the district has a relatively low drainage density, with only 2.06% over 4 km/km. Areas with higher values consist of predominantly impermeable substrate. SPI is predominantly very low with 92.08% under 200. TWI shows high moisture accumulation across the majority of the district, with 80.68% and 16.87% of the area having TWI values of 2.13–8.17 and 8.18–12.69, respectively (Figs. 14, 15, 16 and 17).

The AUC values of the training (0.921) and test (0.897) land cover maps clearly indicate strong discrimination between landslide and non-landslide regions, as described by Kavzoglu et al.46. Though the AUC for the test data set is slightly lower than that for the training data, it shows strong discrimination, and the model successfully predicts new data rather than simply reproducing the calibration data. The difference in training and testing AUC (0.024) also indicates the model’s stability. The high Kappa coefficient value for testing (0.779) confirms the strong agreement between observed and predicted classes12.

The above validation results are important because the weighted overlay model used in this research is not purely data-driven, unlike most machine learning techniques. It doesn’t seek the optimum directly from the landslide inventory but rather uses expert judgment to weight the contributing factors. Even so, the model achieves an AUC of 0.89 on the test dataset, which is similar to previous AHP-based landslide susceptibility models for the Western Ghats, such as Wayanad (AUC = 0.87145;) and Kottayam (AUC = 0.88411) (Fig. 21).

Discussion

The produced landslide susceptibility map for Malappuram is well correlated with the existing landslide inventory, field observations, and previous landslide susceptibility mapping of the Western Ghats. The high susceptibility zones like Kerala Estate, Karulai, Chokkad, Akampadam, and Kurumbalangode all belong to regions of extreme landslide events during the 2018 & 2019 monsoons52, thereby building confidence in the result with enormous implications in the risk management of the region and land use planning policies for disaster-prone regions. A well-demarcated hazard map would assist local and state governments in effectively planning mitigation efforts, allocating appropriate financial and technological resources, and establishing strict zoning laws to prevent further human intervention in the risky terrain. Similarly, targeted landslide warning systems and public awareness programs could be developed based on a well-delineated susceptibility map.

Slope had the highest weight (18%), reflecting its role in gravitational driving stress. Geology was the second weightiest (13%) and stressed on deeply weathered charnockitic terrains prevailing in Kerala; fresh charnockite has a reasonable strength, however, prolonged weathering under hot, humid conditions commonly forms a lateritic mantle, which might collapse under wet conditions4,19. Rainfall was the third weightiest (10%), as it coincides with field observation of an extreme landslide event in Kerala, that was due to antecedent saturation and rainfall amount rather than an earthquake trigger3,25.

Roads were a major factor in landslides in Malappuram. The district has a wide road network connecting the hill area to the lower area. The cut and fill methods adopted in the construction of these roads had disturbed natural slope stability by reducing toe support, removing landslide-resisting material, and thus destabilising the slope, and this could be exacerbated by increased rainfall15,53. A similar correlation between the road network and landslides has been observed in many parts of the Western Ghats.

The 10% high-susceptibility area for Malappuram is in line with similar studies carried out in the Western Ghats. Wayanad reported a high-susceptibility zone of about 15.3%11 due to high ruggedness and steep slope values, while Kottayam reported a high-susceptibility zone of about 4.8%45 with comparatively less steep slopes and lower terrain. This means that the physiography of the region plays an important role in determining the distribution of highly susceptible zones. A good similarity of testing AUC value (0.897) compared to11, test AUC value 0.884) and45, AUC value 0.871) indicates better accuracy of the model, and the model did not overfit even with the inclusion of other procedural steps like Correlation screening, documentation of AHP, and stratified validation in different regions.

The method has limitations as a small landslide event was not accounted for due to a coarser grid and low-resolution DEM and landslide inventory; therefore, there may be errors in the landslide-susceptible zone and in the risk estimation for people vulnerable to landslides. The result might underestimate risk and coverage for some groups. High-resolution data may be incorporated with further studies. Also, more detailed modelling, such as machine learning, would improve the accuracy of the results. Moreover, the result needs validation by local experts.

As the AHP- weighted overlay method is deterministic, the same factor weighting results in the same slope failure result once the factors and weights are fixed, so it cannot replicate the probabilistic nature of slope failure; therefore, future research could integrate the AHP-weighted result with a machine learning algorithm13,14. The AHP weighting results can be used as input to machine learning algorithms such as Random Forest or XGBoost, combining a knowledge-driven and a data-driven approach for more reliable outputs and effective zoning. Also, the integration of real-time rainfall from IMD may produce an event based prediction of landslides for effective warning systems and better response strategies at the ground level.

Conclusion

In this paper, we have proposed and generated a comprehensive landslide susceptibility map for Malappuram district, Kerala, at both district and village levels, aimed at supporting state and local policy-makers in addressing the district’s land-use planning and emergency response policies to reduce landslide-related risks. In the context of one of the most vulnerable districts in Kerala and India to landslides, this research systematically applied an integrated analysis of terrain, geology, and land use using GIS-based Weighted Overlay Analysis, with weights obtained from an AHP analysis, to identify landslide-susceptible regions in the district and categorise them into different levels. The proposed analysis revealed that the combined mapping of terrain, geological, hydrologic, and land-use parameters can predict landslide-susceptible regions and help design policies for risk reduction and mitigation planning for vulnerable regions of the district.

The result revealed that about 355 km2 (10% of the total area) in Malappuram district falls within a highly susceptible area. Highly susceptible areas were mostly located in the north-east highlands, where a high slope (> 25°), weathered charnockitic geology, high mean annual rainfall (> 2,686 mm), and a high hill-road density coexisted. At the village level, the most susceptible areas were Kerala Estate, Chokkad, Akampadam, Karulai and Kurumbalangode.

The weights were determined using five expert opinions in a pairwise comparison analysis, with a Consistency Ratio of 0.047, indicating internal consistency of the judgments. A multicollinearity test also revealed that the 14 factors retained were sufficiently independent of each other, with a maximum Pearson correlation of 0.72 and a maximum VIF of 3.84.

The result revealed that the statistical indices also confirm that the derived landslide susceptibility maps are reliable. A training AUC of 0.921, a test AUC of 0.897 and test Kappa of 0.779 are good indications that the susceptibility map has good accuracy and consistency, and that the predictions made by it have good correspondence with the actual landslide occurrences.

To guide appropriate policy response action, policy-makers should integrate the proposed landslide susceptibility map with the district land-use planning and it should mandate scrutiny of all proposed development and construction projects in high-susceptible zones before issuing any approval; new construction in high susceptible zone must be banned, or subject to extensive geotechnical assessment; village-level susceptibility rank of villages should be used for planning public investment in the construction of protection structures like soil nailing, anchored buttresses; targeted afforestation in the vulnerable zone; re-grading and improving drainage systems of areas prone to landslides; modification or upgrading structures for retrofitting at vulnerable sites; immediate update of land use planning and building codes for enforcing new guidelines or prohibition of new construction in high susceptible areas; link of the map and vulnerability to the early warning and response plans for rapid local warnings and early evacuation; make map and risk levels widely accessible through public portal, training programs; public awareness and campaigns for landslide risk. With these evidence-based measures, policymakers can translate the results into reduced landslide risk for the district of Malappuram.

The landslide susceptibility map will also be of great utility to state and district agencies in appropriate planning and allocation of funds for landslide mitigation, such as slope stabilisation, afforestation programs, drainage improvement works, and controlling construction activities in vulnerable regions. Sharing the map and the underlying data with stakeholders, district authorities, and the state disaster management authority should be done through state official websites (such as the Kerala State Disaster Management Authority) as well as through regular training programs for local-level officials, planners, and villagers. Sharing with a large number of users and stakeholders will help in developing robust policy and disaster management plans. A map can also be utilised for selecting appropriate, safer sites for critical public facilities, guiding evacuation routes, and selecting shelters during a landslide disaster; the susceptibility map will support developing and implementing appropriate land use policies and building codes in prone areas in relation to land use control and building construction; a high susceptibility map will enable better local early warning and a landslide risk reduction plan.

The study demonstrated that by clearly documenting, applying, and justifying weight assessment via AHP, screening factor correlations and performing appropriate validation, we can obtain reasonably robust results that can be useful for planning district-level hazards, even without a comprehensive landslide inventory, which is essential for sophisticated machine learning-based models.

Although the recommendations are for the district of Malappuram, their methodological approach and policy implications could be used for landslide-prone areas within Kerala and any mountain region within the Western Ghats; integrating this map within district-level planning could help in land use and zoning, permitting, and site selection of the new infrastructure to avoid disaster zones, while the mitigation plan and early warning should target highly prone communities. Hence, this study will help develop a targeted risk-reduction plan in any comparable high-hazard area.

The reliable assessment of the susceptible zone can assist the insurance and financial sectors in underwriting risk and in developing appropriate premiums, especially as towns develop very rapidly; the academic community will also benefit with this framework in standardizing a methodology and validation protocol to assist in the comparison of susceptibility analysis results between different districts or regions and for integration with real time remote sensing and hydrometeorological data for building scalable early warning systems.

It is imperative to direct action toward equity and environmental justice. Communities that are already disadvantaged possess inadequate resources and adaptive capacity to address disaster risk. Application of techniques for hazard assessment and mitigation must directly incorporate and reflect the needs of marginalised populations.

Widespread adoption of standardised methods for creating and validating a landslide inventory can significantly improve the quality of hazard assessment, not only for this case study but also at the regional and state levels for policy and planning. Integrating scientific methods with the implementation of evidence-based risk management policies at the district, state, and national levels will produce more responsive and equitable resilience to landslides.
