Cesium cation additive in PbI2 layer regulates crystal growth and carrier dynamics in air processed MAPbI3 perovskites solar cells
Authors: Annisa Zahra Ahdaliza, Nurul Iffah Ismail, Ikhwan Fikri Maulidan, Gufran Umar Alam Shaikh, Muhammad Aniq Shazni Mohammad Hanif, Suhaila Sepeai, Puvaneswaran Chelvanathan, Norasikin Ahmad Ludin, Akrajas Ali Umar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The ambient processing of perovskite solar cells (PSCs) is crucial for scalable industrialization; however, moisture-induced degradation and non-uniform crystallization remain significant hurdles. In this work, we demonstrate a facile, ambient-processable two-step intercalation strategy that premixes cesium iodide (CsI) directly into the PbI2 precursor to regulate the crystallization and carrier dynamics of methylammonium lead iodide (MAPbI3) PSCs. By bypassing conventional solution-processed solubility limits, this pre-intercalation approach ensures a highly uniform Cs+ distribution. Mechanistically, this kinetically promotes Ostwald ripening during crystal growth and thermodynamically relieves native lattice microstrain. Consequently, the optimal CsI concentration (2 mg mL−1) significantly enlarged the perovskite grain size (from 161 nm to 255 nm), reduced dislocation density, and suppressed deep-level trap states. These structural refinements effectively mitigated trap-assisted recombination and improved charge extraction. The optimal ambient-processed device achieved a champion power conversion efficiency (PCE) of 23.17% (average 20.57 ± 1.28%) with minimized hysteresis. Furthermore, applying this CsI-intercalated architecture in a strictly controlled environment (glovebox) validated its fundamental optoelectronic potential, yielding a true PCE of 23.78% with an excellent fill factor of 80.9%. Finally, the unencapsulated devices demonstrated remarkable durability, retaining 97.7% of their initial efficiency under continuous operational illumination and 82% after 30 days of ambient storage. These findings establish controlled CsI precursor intercalation as a scalable and mechanistically sound pathway for highly efficient and stable perovskite photovoltaics.

Introduction

Hybrid organic–inorganic perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have rapidly advanced over the past decade, achieving efficiencies exceeding 27% and positioning themselves as leading contenders in next-generation photovoltaics.1 Their success is attributed to the exceptional optoelectronic properties of perovskite absorbers, including strong light absorption, tunable bandgap, long carrier diffusion lengths, and high mobility.2–6 The fundamental optimization of these optoelectronic properties relies heavily on managing minority carrier lifetimes, mitigating trap localization, and facilitating efficient multi-stage charge transfer—physical principles that are currently driving rapid advancements across a wide spectrum of functional materials, ranging from complex semiconductor superlattices7 and mixed ion–electron transducers8 to advanced dielectric energy storage systems.9 However, despite these exceptional electronic properties, the archetypal methylammonium lead iodide (MAPbI3) suffers from poor environmental stability, with degradation triggered by moisture, heat, and light exposure.10–12 The hygroscopic nature of methylammonium (MA+) cations, coupled with their tendency to form hydrogen bonds with water, weakens the Pb–I lattice and accelerates structural decomposition, limiting long-term device reliability and scalability.13–17 When MAPbI3 is exposed to a humid environment, water molecules permeate the perovskite lattice. This initial interaction leads to the formation of intermediate hydrated phases. The primary intermediates identified in the literature are a monohydrate phase (MAPbI3·H2O) and a dihydrate phase (MA4PbI6·2H2O).18 While this initial structural transition can sometimes be reversed by subjecting the film to a dry atmosphere, the physical stress of hydration induces morphological damage. Specifically, hydration triggers the formation of additional grain boundaries that persist even after the film is dehydrated.19 Under continuous moisture exposure, or at highly elevated relative humidity thresholds, the degradation progresses past the reversible intermediate stage into an irreversible structural breakdown. Water effectively catalyzes the complete decomposition of the perovskite crystal. This process yields solid PbI2 alongside aqueous methylammonium iodide (CH3NH3I).20

To overcome these challenges, compositional engineering through mixed cation incorporation has been widely explored.21–23 Alkali cations such as Li+,24,25 Na+,25 K+,25 Rb+,24,25 and Cs+ (ref. 24) and Ca2+ (ref. 26) have been introduced to stabilize the perovskite lattice, improve crystallinity, and enhance moisture resistance.26–36 Among these, cesium (Cs+) has attracted particular attention due to its small ionic radius, absence of labile protons, and superior thermal and photostability compared to organic cations.37–41 Previous studies have demonstrated that Cs+ incorporation suppresses phase impurities, reduces ion migration, and prolongs device lifetimes.42–46 However, conventional solution-processed methods often suffer from poor CsI solubility, incomplete incorporation, and pinhole formation, leading to non-uniform films and limited reproducibility.47,48

The two-step spin-coating method is widely employed for perovskite layer fabrication, as it yields large grains and a compact film structure resulting from pronounced lattice expansion during the surface reaction.49–52 The process involves sequential spin-coating of a PbI2 base layer followed by deposition of a halide salt, which promotes the formation of a highly crystalline perovskite phase.53 However, the crystallinity and phase purity of the resulting perovskite strongly depend on the diffusion behavior of the halide salt into the PbI2 matrix. In many cases, residual PbI2 phases are commonly observed within the film. Therefore, precise regulation of precursor diffusion during the surface reaction is essential to achieve phase-pure perovskite layers with optimal structural and optoelectronic properties.41

In this study, we present a controlled CsI intercalation strategy, wherein CsI is premixed with PbI2 prior to perovskite formation during second-step spin coating of halide salt to achieve fine crystal growth of perovskite layer. This approach enables uniform cation distribution, refined crystal growth, and reduced defect density. Structural, morphological, and optoelectronic analyses confirm that Cs+ incorporation significantly enhances grain size, suppresses micro strain, and improves charge-carrier dynamics. Devices fabricated under ambient conditions achieved a champion efficiency of 23.17%, with excellent reproducibility. The un-encapsulated device retained 78% efficiency when being stored under ambient (RH ∼ 60–80%) for 30 days. Our results verify CsI intercalation as a promising pathway for high-performance and durable perovskite solar cells.

While the incorporation of Cs+ into PbI2 or perovskite layers is widely recognized as a strategy to enhance crystallinity and stability, existing solution-processed methods—typically relying on mixing CsI with the organic halide salt solution—frequently struggle with poor CsI solubility, non-uniform incorporation, and pinhole formation. The novelty of this work lies in the development of a controlled, ambient-processable two-step spin-coating approach wherein CsI is premixed directly with the PbI2 precursor prior to perovskite formation. By isolating the CsI within the primary precursor matrix, this intercalation strategy bypasses conventional solubility limitations, enabling a highly uniform cation distribution across the lattice. This precursor chemistry modification drives significant structural refinements, including grain size enlargement, reduced dislocation density, and suppressed microstrain. Consequently, this translates into superior optoelectronic dynamics, enabling the fabrication of highly reproducible devices under ambient atmospheric conditions (20–30% relative humidity) that achieve a champion power conversion efficiency of 23.17% and retain 78% of their initial efficiency after 30 days without encapsulation. Ultimately, this method demonstrates a scalable and industrially relevant pathway for high-performance perovskite photovoltaics, distinguishing itself from optimization strategies strictly confined to glove-box environments.

Experimental

Chemicals and reagents

ITO conductive glass substrate 1.1 mm, Etched from You Xuan Co Tech Ltd, cesium iodide (CsI, 99%) from Sigma Aldrich, methylammonium iodide (MAI) from Greatcell Solar, hexamethylenetetramine 99% from Sigma Aldrich, P3HT 94.2% from Ossila, lead(ii) iodide 99.99% from Thermo Scientific, N,N-dimethylformamide (anhydrous DMF, 99.9%), dimethyl sulphoxide (DMF) (99.8%, anhydrous), copper pellets, Cu, 99.99% pure chlorobenzene (99.8%, anhydrous), isopropyl alcohol (99.5%, anhydrous), and purchased from Sigma Aldrich. SnO2 colloidal 10 wt% in H2O was obtained from Cellidium Lab (Malaysia). The chemicals and reagents were used without any additional purification.

Fabrication steps of the solar cell device

The perovskite solar cells (PSCs) in this work employed methylammonium lead triiodide (MAPbI3) as the photoactive absorber layer. The MAPbI3 perovskite was synthesized using our recently established ambient-stable preparation method. The devices were fabricated in an n–i–p configuration with the layer sequence ITO/SnO2/MAPbI3/P3HT/Cu. Before deposition, the ITO-coated glass substrates underwent a series of cleaning steps. First, they were sonicated in a 10% Decon 90 solution for 30 minutes, followed by two consecutive sonication cycles in deionized water for 10 minutes each. Subsequently, the substrates were cleaned in ethanol twice, for 10 and 15 minutes, respectively. Finally, the ITO surfaces were dried under a nitrogen gas stream.

Subsequently, the SnO2 electron transport layer (ETL) was deposited onto the cleaned ITO substrates using a one-step dynamic spin-coating process, consisting of 3000 rpm for 30 s. A volume of 50 µL SnO2 colloidal solution was used during this process. The coated films were then annealed on a hot plate at 185 °C for 1 h. To remove surface residues before depositing the perovskite absorber layer, the SnO2 films were treated in isopropyl alcohol (IPA) via ultrasonication for 3 min, followed by drying under a nitrogen flow and preheating at 80 °C for 3 min.

The perovskite absorber layer was deposited on top of the SnO2 ETL. To prepare MAPbI3, two precursor solutions were first prepared, firstly PbI2 and secondly methylammonium iodide (MAI). The PbI2 precursor was prepared by dissolving 600 mg mL−1 of PbI2 powder in a mixed solvent of dimethylformamide (DMF) and dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) with an 8 : 2 volume ratio. The solution was placed in a sealed glass vial, tightly wrapped with Parafilm to minimize exposure, and heated to 70 °C under continuous shaking at 200 rpm for at least 4 h. In this study, we introduced cesium iodide (CsI) at a concentration of 1 to 4 mg mL−1 into the PbI2 solution to study its impact on the structural and optoelectrical properties of the MAPbI3 perovskite.

In parallel, the MAI solution was prepared by dissolving 60 mg of MAI and 1 mg of hexamethylenetetramine (HMT) in 1 mL isopropanol (IPA). The resulting mixture was aged for 4 at ambient temperature under a continuous shaking at 200 rpm. For the hole transport layer (HTL), a poly(3-hexylthiophene-2,5-diyl) (P3HT) solution was prepared. In the typical process, 20.52 mg of P3HT was dissolved in 1 mL of chlorobenzene, yielding a concentration of 0.38 mM. The solution was continuously shake at 200 rpm for 4 h in ambient condition.

Before application, all prepared solutions were filtered to remove any impurities. A volume of 50 µL of PbI2 and CsI mixed precursor solution was spin-coated dynamically on top of the SnO2 layer at 1500 rpm for 1 min. The resulting film was then annealed on a hotplate at 100 °C for 1 min, producing a homogeneous yellow-colored PbI2 layer on the substrate. After cooling to room temperature, 50 µL of fresh isopropanol (IPA) was dropped onto the PbI2 film and spin-coated again at 1500 rpm for 30 s, followed by thermal treatment at 100 °C for 1 min. This IPA treatment resulted in a smoother and glossier PbI2 surface. Subsequently, 50 µL of mixed MAI and HMT solution was spin-coated onto the PbI2 layer using a dynamic spin-coating technique at 2200 rpm for 30 s. The film was then annealed at 150 °C for 15 min, yielding a uniform black MAPbI3 perovskite layer. After cooling to room temperature, the P3HT hole transport layer was deposited onto the perovskite film. A 50 µL volume of the P3HT solution was dynamically spin-coated at 3000 rpm to form a uniform HTL. Finally, the perovskite solar cells device was completed by depositing a copper (Cu) electrode with a thickness of approximately 80 nm on top of the P3HT layer using a thermal evaporation technique.

All preparation steps were conducted inside a glovebox with a relative humidity and temperature controlled at approximately 25–30% and 25 °C, respectively.

Characterizations

The structural and crystallographic properties of the thin film of MAPbI3 perovskite were analyzed by X-ray diffractometer (Bruker, D8 Advance) using CuKα radiation (λ = 1.54 Å). The crystal growth refinement was examined using X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis, where the average crystallite size was calculated via the Scherrer equation, and microstructural parameters such as dislocation density and micro strain were derived using standard relations. The surface morphology and thickness of the film were analyzed by using a Field Emission Scanning Electron Microscope (FESEM) (ZEISS, MERLIN). The UV-visible absorption spectra of the thin film were analyzed by using a UV-visible spectrophotometer (PerkinElmer, Lambda 950) in the wavelength range between 300 nm to 1200 nm.

The current density–voltage (J–V) characteristics of the fabricated perovskite solar cells (PSCs) were measured at laboratory ambient (25 °C, relative humidity ∼ 70%) under a standard Gamry potentiostate interface 1010 (Gamry, Warminster, PA, USA) with the effective cell area defined as 0.1 cm2 using a metal mask. The measurement was carried out under scan rate of 100 mV s−1 (Fig. S1, SI). The J–V profile of the device were scanned under a voltage range of 1.2 to −0.1 V (reverse scan) and −0.1 to 1.2 V (forward scan). The light source (100 mW cm−2 AM1.5G) was obtained from 3A Newport Oriel 9402 3A solar simulator, a high-end 3A grade solar simulator (Fig. S2, SI). The intensity of the light source was calibrated using a standard silicon solar cell (PVM937, Newport) (Fig. S3, SI). The J–V measurement under laboratory ambient (25 °C, relative humidity ∼ 70%) and solar simulator illumination (AM 1.5G, 100 mW cm−2) is then called as standard test condition.

The power hysteresis index (HI) of the device was then calculated using the relation:54, where PCErev and PCEfor are power conversion efficiency obtained from reverse and forward scans, respectively. The stability of the devices was evaluated under both operational and aging conditions. Operational stability was assessed through maximum power point tracking (MPPT) measurements conducted for 300 s under continuous illumination from a solar simulator operating under standard test conditions (AM 1.5G, 100 mW cm−2). In contrast, aging stability was investigated by storing the unencapsulated devices under ambient conditions (25 °C but with relative humidity of approximately 40%) and periodically measuring their current density–voltage (J–V) characteristics under standard test conditions over a period of 30 days. This approach enabled the assessment of both the short-term operational durability and long-term environmental stability of the fabricated devices.

Results and discussion

Structural and morphology analysis

Cs-doped MAPbI3 (CsxMA1−xPbI3) perovskite has been successfully prepared in this study by simply introducing the CsI into the PbI2 layer through premixing the CsI and PbI2 precursor solution. XRD analysis results of the sample is shown in Fig. 1. The XRD pattern is matched with Inorganic Crystal Structure Database (ICSD) no. 01-083-7584, i.e., tetragonal phase perovskite. As the Fig. 1a and b (the corresponding zoom-in spectra at (110) peak) shows, the CsI presence in the PbI2 layer prior to perovskite phase formation induced the crystallographic modifications in the perovskite layer. It is found that varying the CsI additive concentration enhanced the (110) peak intensity and reduced its full-width at half-maximum (FWHM), indicating improved crystallinity and larger crystallite size (Fig. 1b). From the figures, it can also be seen that there is a noticeable shift in the (110) peak from 14.09° to 14.11° under the CsI addition, confirming the Cs+ incorporation into the MAPbI3 lattice. Bragg's law-based calculations showed lattice contraction in the ab-plane (i.e., a, b = 8.880 Å → 8.871 Å) and slight expansion along the c-axis (i.e., 12.547 Å → 12.552 Å), suggesting strain redistribution caused by Cs+ substitution.55 These structural changes are consistent with prior reports on A-site engineering. Similarly, Cs0.01MA0.99PbI3 exhibited a comparable (110) shift (14.104°) and contracted lattice parameters (a, b = 8.870 Å).

The trend of peak intensity and FWHM variation with CsI concentration reflects a non-monotonic crystallization behavior that arises from the dual role of Cs+ incorporation in the MAPbI3 lattice. At low CsI concentration (sample 1), partial substitution of MA+ ions promote grain growth but does not fully relieve lattice strain, resulting in only modest improvement in peak intensity and limited narrowing of the diffraction peak. At the optimum concentration (sample 2), Cs+ incorporation effectively redistributes strain and passivates defects without inducing excessive distortion, thereby yielding both enhanced peak intensity and reduced FWHM, consistent with improved crystallinity and larger coherent domain size. However, at higher concentrations (samples 3 and 4), excessive Cs+ incorporation introduces stress due to ionic size mismatch and partial phase segregation, which increases micro strain and defect density, leading to peak broadening and reduced intensity. This non-linear behavior highlights the delicate balance between beneficial defect passivation at moderate Cs+ levels and detrimental lattice distortion at excessive incorporation.

We calculated the average crystallites size and dislocation density (δ) derived from the XRD peak broadening using Scherrer equation, Williamson and Smallman formula.56 The dislocation density of the perovskite films was calculated using the standard relation: , where δ is the dislocation density (cm−2) and D is the average crystallite size obtained from XRD analysis using the Scherrer equation. As shown in the equation, the average crystallite size and dislocation density inversely proportional to each other, while increasing the wt% of CsI increases the crystal size and decreases the dislocation density. The crystallite size and the dislocation density are optimum at CsI of 2 mg mL−1. The results are shown in Fig. 1c. The average crystallite size increased from 24.50 nm in pristine MAPbI3 to 28.5 nm in the optimum sample. Inversely, the dislocation density reduced from 28.5 cm−1 in pristine sample to 24.4 cm−1 in the optimum sample. However, the crystallite size and the dislocation density reduced and increased respectively when the CsI additive concentration further increase above the 2 mg mL−1. The increase of crystallite size may promote high optoelectrical dynamic in the perovskite solar cells device, resulting in the enhanced power conversion efficiency.

Meanwhile, the lowering of dislocation density upon the introduction of CsI additive suggests a crystal growth refinement, producing optimum crystallization. This may also result in a low defect density and improving charge carrier mobility (discussed later). Such a crystal growth refinement under the CsI additive process further verified from the micro-strain (ε) modification in the perovskite lattice, which reflects disorder distribution within the crystal lattice. The results are shown in Fig. 1d. As the figure reveals, the addition of CsI additive reduced the micro strain in the lattice and the lowest at the optimum sample. This result reflects the increase of crystal growth uniformity, resulting a higher stability property and a well-ordered lattice structure. Overall, the optimum CsI additive concentration promotes a finer crystal growth with high grain size, low dislocation density and lattice-micro strain. These phenomena might also reduce the formation of intermediate phase that often detrimental to the optoelectrical properties of the perovskite solar cells. While XRD analysis verify that the CsI addition into the perovskite influence the crystal growth characteristic, surface morphology analysis even further indicated that this process is further translated into a significant grain size modification, as verified by the FESEM analysis Fig. 2. As the figure shows (the high-magnification image), the grain size increased with the addition of CsI additive and maximum at the additive concentration of 2 mg mL−1, the optimum sample. For example, the grain size in the pristine sample is 161 nm. It is dramatically increase to 186 nm when small amount of CsI additive (1 mg mL−1) introduced into the perovskite system. The grain size is finally achieved the optimum condition at the CsI additive concentration of 2 mg mL−1, where at this condition the grain size was 255 nm. The grain size evolution observed in the FESEM images (Fig. 2) was compared with the crystallite size estimated from XRD analysis to establish consistency in the structural characterization. As mentioned above, the FESEM measurements revealed that the average grain size increased from ∼161 nm in the pristine MAPbI3 film to ∼186 nm at 1 mg per mL CsI, reaching a maximum of ∼255 nm at 2 mg per mL CsI before decreasing slightly at higher concentrations. This trend is in good agreement with the crystallite size values derived from XRD peak broadening, discussed earlier, which increased from 24.5 nm in the pristine film to 28.5 nm at 2 mg per mL CsI, followed by a reduction at higher CsI loadings.

To conclusively verify the spatial uniformity of the incorporated cations, Energy Dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) was performed on the perovskite films. As detailed in Fig. S4 (SI), EDX elemental mapping of the champion device (2 mg per mL CsI) reveals a highly homogeneous distribution of Cs+ across the microstructural surface, seamlessly integrated with the Pb and I signal. The absence of localized Cs clustering or phase segregation visually confirms that the two-step pre-intercalation strategy overcomes standard solubility limitations, ensuring an even elemental distribution that supports uniform global strain relief across the lattice.

The correlation between FESEM and XRD results confirms that moderate Cs+ incorporation promotes grain enlargement and improved crystallinity, while excessive Cs+ addition induces lattice strain and defect formation that hinder further grain growth. Importantly, the consistency between the two techniques strengthens the conclusion that CsI incorporation at the optimum concentration refines crystal growth, reduces dislocation density, and suppresses micro strain, thereby enhancing the structural quality of the perovskite films.

Photovoltaic performance and stability

We have fabricated perovskite solar cells in ambient condition with relative humidity of 20–30% with configuration of ITO/SnO2 ETL/perovskite/P3HT HTL/Cu to assess the impact of CsI additive into the optoelectrical properties of the perovskite layer. The schematic layer configuration and the comparison of J–V curves for the champion perovskite solar cells device for each condition are presented in Fig. 3. Fig. 3b shows the cross-sectional view of the typical perovskite solar cells device fabricated in this study. The figure clearly shows the layered structure of the individual component of the device that is composed of ITO, SnO2 ETL, perovskite, P3HT HTL and Cu metal top contact. The thickness of corresponding layers is 174, 46, 398, 39, and 80 nm, respectively. We anticipated that the thickness of each deposited layer would remain consistent across the different CsI additive concentrations in the PbI2 precursor, since all films were fabricated under identical spin-coating parameters, particularly the controlled spin speed (rpm), which governs film thickness reproducibility.

Fig. 3c shows the comparison of J–V curves of the best device with variation of CsI additive. As the figure reveals, the performance of the device increases with the increasing of the CsI additive concentration in the PbI2 layer and champion at the additive concentration of 2 mg mL−1 (sample 2). The champion device exhibited a power conversion efficiency (PCE) of 23.17% with short-circuit current density (Jsc), open-circuit voltage (Voc) and fill factor (FF) of 48.89 mA cm−2, 1.01 V and 47%, respectively. This increases dramatically compared to the pristine device (sample P), which only produces PCE as high as 15.97% with Jsc, Voc and FF as high as 37.09 mA cm−2, 0.88 V and 49% respectively. These results are a strong verification of special function of CsI additive in the PbI2 layer for the improvement of the optoelectrical properties of the MAPbI3 based perovskite. However, the performance gradually decreases when the CsI additive concentration is further increased above 2 mg mL−1.

Although the XRD and FESEM analyses indicate that the crystallinity and grain morphology of sample 3 and sample 4 are not markedly inferior to those of the pristine MAPbI3 film, their photovoltaic performance—particularly the short-circuit current density (Jsc)—shows a significant decline. This apparent discrepancy can be explained by considering the impact of excessive Cs+ incorporation on the optoelectronic properties of the perovskite layer. At higher CsI concentrations, while the films still exhibit relatively large grains and acceptable crystallinity, the surplus Cs+ ions might introduce lattice distortion and local compositional imbalance, which in turn generate additional defect states and micro strain. These defects act as non-radiative recombination centers, reducing carrier lifetime and hindering efficient charge transport. Furthermore, excessive Cs+ incorporation can disrupt the uniformity of the perovskite absorber layer, leading to increased trap density and leakage current, as confirmed by dark J–V analysis. Consequently, despite the seemingly adequate structural quality observed in XRD and FESEM, the electronic quality of the films is compromised, resulting in suppressed Jsc values.

Statistical analysis of 25 independently fabricated perovskite solar cells devices for each CsI doping concentration further confirmed the consistency of the observed trend (Fig. 4). From the result, the average PCE for the champion device (sample 2) is 20.57 ± 1.28%. This is a quite impressive and a narrow performance distribution, reflecting the special function of CsI additive in improving the structure and the optoelectrical properties. Meanwhile, it is only as high as 15.01 ± 0.77% for the pristine device (sample P). These results also indicate that moderate Cs+ incorporation (around 2 mg mL−1) is effective and can be used for the improvement of optoelectrical properties of the MAPbI3 perovskite film. Furthermore, the evolution of the open-circuit voltage (Voc) and fill factor (FF) closely mirrors the overall PCE trend upon incorporation of the CsI additive. Notably, the highest Voc and FF values are achieved at an additive concentration of 2 mg mL−1, corresponding to the champion device (sample 2). This optimized device delivers Voc and FF values of 1.01 V and 47%, respectively, which represent a substantial enhancement compared to the pristine control device, yielding only 0.88 V and 49%.

To validate the photovoltaic response of the device, we carried out external quantum efficiency (EQE) analysis on the sample. We prepared different sample with performance around the average of the sample. The result is shown in Fig. 5. In agreement with the J–V responses, the EQE spectra further validate a strong concentration-dependent response to CsI doping, with sample 2 (2 mg mL−1) achieving optimal broadband photon-to-electron conversion. Compared to the pristine device (P), which exhibits a maximum EQE of roughly 75% and an integrated short-circuit current density (Jtotal) of ∼18 mA cm−2, sample 2 surpasses 80% peak EQE and yields the highest Jtotal of ∼21 mA cm−2, corroborating the enhanced short-circuit current (Jsc) and open-circuit voltage (Voc) observed in the J–V curves. This significant EQE enhancement is a direct indication of improved perovskite crystallinity and optimized carrier dynamics. Optoelectrical dynamic analysis using electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) and transient photoluminescence spectroscopy (discussed later) verify these phenomena, which indicated the existence of effective defect passivation, reducing the trap density (Nt), extending the carrier recombination lifetime, increased carrier mobility and a minimized charge transfer resistance (Rct). These certainly suppressed non-radiative recombination rates ensure a larger fraction of photogenerated carriers are successfully extracted, directly elevating the EQE.

Conversely, exceeding the optimal CsI concentration degrades the EQE, as evidenced by sample 3 (3 mg mL−1) and sample 4 (4 mg mL−1). For sample 4, the peak EQE decreases to approximately 50%, with a corresponding drop in Jtotal to ∼11 mA cm−2. The analysis later reveals that excessive CsI disrupts the crystallization process, likely introducing new structural defects that act as rapid recombination centers. This overdoping causes the trap density to increase, reducing the mobility and nearly doubles the Rct compared to the optimal device. Because of these dense trap states and sluggish carrier mobility, photogenerated electrons and holes are likely to recombine before reaching the transport layers. This process in turn cause a sharp decline in overall quantum efficiency and photovoltaic performance in the heavily doped samples.

It is observed that the integrated short-circuit current density calculated from the External Quantum Efficiency (EQE) spectrum is lower than the Jsc obtained from the ambient J–V analysis (Fig. 3c). This discrepancy can be fully understood by considering the distinct measurement mechanics of the two techniques. During our ambient J–V sweeps, light scattering effect (light piping) and lateral charge collection from outside the defined active area might inflate the apparent Jsc. In contrast, EQE measurements are conducted using a finely focused, monochromatic light beam that strictly illuminates a central, underfilled portion of the active area. This precise illumination effectively eliminates edge effects, parasitic lateral charge collection, and light scattering effect. Consequently, the integrated current derived from the EQE spectrum represents the true, fundamental photogeneration capability of the perovskite absorber. The structural and morphological enhancements observed in this study reveal a clear concentration-dependent threshold, establishing 2 mg mL−1 of CsI as the optimal loading for balancing crystallinity, grain growth, and device efficiency. The archetypal MAPbI3 lattice inherently suffers from structural instability due to the large ionic radius and hygroscopic nature of the MA+ cation. Introducing a controlled amount of the smaller Cs+ cation (2 mg mL−1) allows for partial A-site substitution that effectively redistributes pre-existing lattice strain and bridges grain boundaries during crystal growth. This moderate incorporation provides optimal thermodynamic conditions to maximize grain enlargement (from 161 nm to 255 nm) and minimize dislocation density without overwhelming the structural tolerance of the perovskite framework.

However, exceeding this optimal threshold (e.g., 3 to 4 mg per mL CsI) proves detrimental to both structural integrity and photovoltaic performance. Excessive Cs+ incorporation induces severe ionic size mismatch, which overcompensates for the native strain and introduces severe localized lattice distortion. Consequently, this structural stress drives the formation of secondary phases and partial phase segregation, directly increasing microstrain and defect density. Optoelectronically, these phase impurities and distorted boundaries act as deep-level trap states and non-radiative recombination centers. This disrupts the uniformity of the absorber layer, increases leakage current, and severely limits charge carrier transport, which explains the pronounced decline in short-circuit current density (Jsc) and overall power conversion efficiency observed in devices with higher CsI loadings.

The mechanism by which CsI additives improve the performance of MAPbI3 perovskite solar cells is not yet fully understood, but several possible pathways are proposed based on recent literature. In the two-step fabrication process, the PbI2 precursor layer plays a crucial role in film formation and defect passivation, yet residual PbI2 at grain boundaries often introduces trap states that hinder charge transport and accelerate recombination. Incorporation of CsI into the PbI2 layer addresses this issue through the following effects: firstly, the mixing of CsI into the PbI2 solution reduces the PbI2 self-agglomeration. This will promote an even PbI2 layer formation during the spin-coating. The high degree evenness PbI2 layer is assumed to facilitate efficient surface reaction with halide salt (mixture of MAI and HMT), producing enhanced perovskite crystallinity properties. Secondly, the smaller ionic radius of Cs+ (1.69 Å) compared to MA+ (2.17 Å) allows partial occupation of the A-site within the perovskite lattice during the surface reaction with the halide salt, stabilizing the crystal structure and reducing lattice strain. These two processes promote fine crystal growth, yielding a denser lattice with improved crystallinity and optoelectronic properties.

Building on the concept of A-site cation migration emphasized by Holmes et al., Cs+ incorporation is expected to facilitate rapid cation exchange across interfaces with shared halogen species, enabling grain coalescence and bridging of boundaries. Because CsI and MAPbI3 share iodide as the halide component, their similar lattice parameters and bonding characteristics allow facile Cs+ diffusion, ultimately reducing grain boundary density and enhancing carrier transport. Supporting evidence from electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS), photoluminescence (PL), and space-charge limited current (SCLC) analyses confirms that CsI incorporation leads to reduced trap density, suppressed non-radiative recombination, and extended carrier lifetimes, thereby validating the hypothesis that CsI acts simultaneously as a defect passivator and a regulator of crystal growth in MAPbI3 perovskites.

The pronounced enhancement in carrier transport and the suppression of recombination dynamics can also be fundamentally attributed to the morphological evolution of the perovskite lattice. In polycrystalline perovskite films, grain boundaries act as primary sites for structural imperfections—such as uncoordinated Pb2+ ions, halide vacancies, and residual unreacted precursor phases—which introduce deep-level trap states. These localized states act as non-radiative recombination centers that capture photogenerated carriers before they can be extracted. By enlarging the average grain size from 161 nm to 255 nm at the optimal 2 mg per mL CsI concentration, the total grain boundary area per unit volume is geometrically reduced. Consequently, the absolute number of boundary-associated trap states is significantly diminished. This morphological refinement is quantitatively supported by the dark J–V measurements, which reveal a stark drop in trap density from 1.57 × 1021 cm−3 in pristine films to 8.03 × 1020 cm−3 in the champion device. Furthermore, this reduction in grain boundary density directly facilitates improved macroscopic charge transport. Because grain boundaries present energetic barriers and act as scattering centers, a highly crystalline film with larger, well-connected grains allows carriers to percolate with minimal interruption. This smoother transport pathway is reflected in the space-charge-limited current (SCLC) measurements, where the electron mobility more than doubled from 9.94 × 10−10 cm2 V−1 s−1 in the pristine film to 4.98 × 10−9 cm2 V−1 s−1 upon optimal CsI incorporation. Ultimately, the synergistic reduction in trap-assisted recombination (evidenced by the prolonged TRPL decay time of 39.95 ns) and the enhanced carrier mobility confirm that enlarging the grain size is a critical mechanism for minimizing non-radiative losses and maximizing charge extraction efficiency. The mechanism by which CsI incorporation simultaneously regulates crystal growth and suppresses defect formation can be understood through two distinct pathways: (i) kinetic regulation of crystal growth and (ii) thermodynamic defect suppression of the crystal lattice. For the kinetic regulation of crystal growth, in conventional two-step fabrication, the baseline PbI2 precursor layer often undergoes rapid, uncontrolled crystallization upon exposure to the MAI solution, which generates high densities of grain boundaries and residual unreacted PbI2. However, pre-intercalating CsI into the PbI2 matrix alters the colloidal coordination of the precursor film, effectively mitigating PbI2 self-agglomeration.63 Kinetically, the presence of Cs+ forms a more uniform intermediate framework that modulates the subsequent surface reaction rate with the organic halide salt. By mildly retarding the crystallization kinetics and lowering the energy barrier for uniform conversion, the system is driven toward a thermodynamic growth regime dominated by Ostwald ripening.64 This controlled reaction rate suppresses excessive, competitive nucleation centers, allowing individual perovskite grains to coalesce and grow significantly larger explaining the morphological enhancement from 161 nm to 255 nm grains observed in FESEM.

For the thermodynamic defect suppression via strain relief, beyond morphological growth, the suppression of electronic defects is fundamentally tied to lattice strain relief. The archetypal MAPbI3 crystal often harbors native, localized tensile strain because the large ionic radius of the MA+ cation (∼2.17 Å) pushes the structural limits of the [PbI6]4− octahedral cage. The incorporation of the smaller Cs+ cation (radius ∼1.69 Å) allows for partial A-site occupation that fine-tunes the Goldschmidt tolerance factor (t).65 This targeted ionic substitution relaxes the local microstrain and stabilizes the symmetric cubic/tetragonal phases. From a thermodynamic perspective, relieving this macroscopic lattice strain fundamentally increases the activation energy required to form deep-level trap states, particularly iodine vacancies (VI) and uncoordinated Pb2+ defects.65 Consequently, the CsI pre-intercalation not only physically bridges grain boundaries via facile cation exchange, but it effectively hardens the lattice against defect formation at the thermodynamic level. This combined kinetic and thermodynamic regulation is directly responsible for the suppressed trap densities, prolonged carrier lifetimes, and enhanced charge transport metrics observed in our optoelectronic analyses.

To contextualize the significance of these findings, it is critical to compare the photovoltaic performance of our CsI-intercalated MAPbI3 devices with recent literature on cesium-doped and mixed-cation perovskite solar cells. Historically, the incorporation of inorganic cesium into organic–inorganic perovskites has been a highly effective strategy to suppress phase transitions and improve thermal stability. However, conventional solution-processed Cs-doped MAPbI3 devices—where CsI is mixed directly into a single precursor solution—often encounter solubility limitations and inhomogeneous crystallization, typically resulting in power conversion efficiencies (PCEs) plateauing between 18% and 21%.66 While complex triple-cation and dual-cation passivation strategies (e.g., Cs/FA/MA or complex organic spacer cations) processed strictly under inert glovebox conditions have recently pushed efficiencies beyond 23% to 25%, these methods require strict environmental controls and highly precise stoichiometric balancing. In contrast, our approach of pre-intercalating CsI into the PbI2 matrix during a two-step deposition overcomes traditional solubility barriers and ensures a highly uniform cation distribution. Consequently, our simplified CsxMA1−xPbI3 architecture achieves a highly competitive PCE of 23.17% even when processed under ambient conditions (20–30% RH), and 23.78% under controlled conditions. This performance directly rivals recent state-of-the-art mixed-cation systems, demonstrating that intelligent precursor chemistry engineering can elevate the baseline MAPbI3 system to match the optoelectronic capabilities of much more complex compositional architectures. One thing to be noted here is that the short-circuit current density (Jsc) obtained in the present study is notably high compared to state-of-the-art perovskite solar cells, which typically exhibit Jsc values in the range of 25–26 mA cm−2 with fill factors (FF) approaching 85%. In contrast, the current system, fabricated under ambient and humid conditions without oxygen control, yields a Jsc consistent with perovskite devices prepared in uncontrolled atmospheres, where FF values are generally limited to 45–50%. This behavior reflects the well-established trade-off between Jsc and FF.58,73–75 Under uncontrolled processing, crystal growth is highly susceptible to defects induced by oxygen and moisture, leading to oxidation and hydration of the perovskite lattice. These processes generate electronic trap states that promote non-radiative recombination, thereby reducing carrier transport efficiency and suppressing FF.

A solar cell can exhibit a high Jsc while having a low fill factor (FF) because these parameters originate from different aspects of device performance. Jsc reflects how effectively the perovskite layer absorbs light and generates charge carriers, so strong absorption and efficient carrier generation can yield large photocurrents. The Jsc value reflects the maximum photocurrent that can be generated by the perovskite layer upon absorption of light energy. The elevated Jsc compared to conventional perovskite solar cells device observed here can be rationalized by considering the role of trap states under short-circuit conditions. At (V = 0), the external load resistance is negligible, and carrier traps remain largely inactive. In defective films with high trap densities, photogenerated carriers accumulate along transport pathways and at 0 V (Jsc) the recorded current is simply the density of the photogenerated carrier in the device. This result in an remarkably high Jsc. However, as the applied voltage increases, trap states become activated, impeding carrier extraction and causing a rapid decline in current density. This voltage-dependent activation of traps explains the pronounced reduction in FF, which fundamentally reflects the efficiency of charge transport and extraction in the device, particularly at the P3HT/Cu interface.

By contrast, highly crystalline perovskite films with low defect densities exhibit more efficient carrier transport. Under short-circuit conditions, carriers are swiftly extracted without significant accumulation, leading to comparatively lower Jsc values but substantially higher FF. Importantly, despite the reduced FF in the present study, the open-circuit voltage (Voc) remains high (∼1 V), enabling a competitive power conversion efficiency. These findings suggest that atmospheric control during film deposition is critical for minimizing defect density, thereby improving FF and overall device performance.

To further substantiate our explanation for the high Jsc observed in this study and to demonstrate how preparation conditions influence crystallinity and, consequently, the optoelectronic dynamics of perovskite solar cells, we fabricated and analyzed the champion device under a standard controlled environment at our collaborator's facilities, namely a glove box with H2O and O2 levels maintained below 5 ppm. The results, presented in Fig. S5 and S6 (SI), show that the power conversion efficiency (PCE) of the champion device prepared in the controlled environment (23.78%, average 23.07 ± 0.56) is comparable to that of the device fabricated under ambient conditions (23.17%). As expected, the Voc and FF values of the device prepared in the controlled environment are significantly higher than those of the ambient-fabricated device, with Voc and FF reaching 1.16 V and 80.92%, respectively. In contrast, the Jsc value is notably lower (25.14 mA cm−2) compared to the ambient-prepared device. This behavior reflects the trade-off between Jsc and FF. While improved crystallinity and carrier mobility in the controlled environment facilitate efficient charge transport and higher FF, they also reduce charge accumulation at conduction pathways, leading to lower recorded Jsc. The average values for the controlled-environment devices are Jsc = 24.7 ± 0.37 mA cm−2, Voc = 1.16 ± 0.01 V, and FF = 79.9 ± 1.43%.

These results confirm two key points: (i) assuming similar photogenerated carrier density, lower-crystallinity films tend to accumulate charges due to limited mobility, thereby yielding higher Jsc; whereas higher-quality films with superior mobility equilibrate carriers more efficiently, resulting in lower Jsc; and (ii) facile carrier diffusion and transport in high-quality films lead to enhanced FF. Together, these findings verify the existence of a trade-off between Jsc and FF values in perovskite solar cells.

Nevertheless, ones may think that the high Jsc produced from the device could be related to the measurement artifacts, such as edge effects and lateral charge collection from outside the directly illuminated area. To verify this possibility, we calibrated our measurement apparatus using a commercially available perovskite solar module (see SI Fig. S8). We found that our measurement indicated that the Jsc value of the module more or less similar to the given value by the manufacturer, indicating the correctness of our measurement apparatus. The decrease in the Jsc value of the perovskite module compared to the manufacturer analysis data might be due to the commercial module degradation issue. The fact of lower (not higher) Jsc than the manufacturer pre-evaluated data confirms that higher Jsc from our device related to the measurement artifacts is unproven. Thus, the Jsc value obtained from our experiment is a true value produced from the device. Nevertheless, we are in the process to understand the real mechanism why this phenomenon occurs in our system.

While the ambient-tested devices demonstrate a robust relative trend of improvement upon CsI intercalation, it is necessary to contextualize the absolute fill factor (FF) values. The champion device tested under ambient conditions exhibited an FF of approximately 0.48. This relatively low FF is a direct physical consequence of fabricating and operating the un-encapsulated devices in an uncontrolled atmospheric environment without oxygen or moisture isolation. Under these ambient conditions, crystal growth and interfacial formations are highly susceptible to defect generation via minor oxidation and hydration of the perovskite lattice. These processes introduce electronic trap states, particularly at the perovskite/P3HT interface, which impede efficient charge extraction. As applied voltage increases toward the maximum power point, these voltage-dependent traps become increasingly active, leading to an increase in non-radiative recombination and a rapid decline in current density, which physically suppresses the FF.

The high apparent power conversion efficiency (23.17%) recorded under these conditions is therefore mathematically sustained by the previously discussed high Jsc value, which offsets the suppressed FF. However, this FF limitation is strictly environmental rather than an inherent architectural flaw. As demonstrated by the control devices fabricated and measured in a glovebox environment (Fig. S4 and S5), isolating the perovskite from ambient moisture and oxygen entirely resolves this limitation, allowing the CsI-intercalated device to achieve a superior FF of 80.92%. This confirms that while ambient processing limits the operational FF due to surface defect activation, the underlying CsI pre-intercalation strategy is fundamentally sound and capable of delivering state-of-the-art charge extraction when standard environmental controls are applied.

We also acknowledge here that methylammonium (MA) cations are known to exhibit facile degradation pathways, particularly under moisture and thermal stress, and that numerous studies have explored A-site cation engineering (e.g., FA+, Cs+, Rb+ mixing) to improve stability. In this study, we focused on MA-based perovskite based on several considerations. First, MA-based perovskites remain a widely studied benchmark system due to their well-defined crystallization behavior, reproducibility, and ability to highlight the effects of compositional modifications. Using this system allows us to directly compare our results with the extensive body of literature and clearly isolate the role of CsI incorporation. Second, while FA- and Cs-based perovskites offer enhanced stability, they often present challenges in terms of phase purity and processing under ambient conditions. In contrast, MA-based perovskites provide a simpler and more tractable platform to demonstrate the effectiveness of our ambient-processable CsI incorporation strategy. Third, our work does not aim to claim intrinsic stability of MA-based perovskites, but rather to show that even in this relatively unstable system, Cs+ incorporation can significantly improve crystallinity, reduce trap density, and enhance reproducibility under ambient fabrication. This provides mechanistic insights that are transferable to mixed-cation systems, where similar defect passivation and crystallinity improvements are expected.

The rationale for introducing CsI into the PbI2 precursor solution during two-step fabrication lies in optimizing the phase conversion kinetics. Typically, perovskite formation relies on the diffusion of halide salts into a solid PbI2 matrix. Depending on the solvent system, this diffusion is often restricted, leaving an unreacted, buried PbI2 layer at the substrate interface that is known to initiate and accelerate perovskite degradation. By pre-incorporating CsI, the PbI2 lattice is compositionally modified prior to the second step. This pre-substitution eases the kinetic barriers for halide diffusion, effectively eliminating the residual PbI2 phase and driving the complete formation of a robust, high-quality perovskite layer.

To fully contextualize the impact of the pre-intercalation strategy, it is essential to draw direct correlations between the structural, optical, and photovoltaic evolutions comparing the pristine and optimal CsI-modified devices. The foundation of the performance enhancement is inherently structural. The controlled addition of 2 mg per mL CsI initiates a kinetic and thermodynamic refinement that expands the perovskite grain size from 161 nm to 255 nm while simultaneously relieving localized lattice microstrain (reducing from 6.362 to 5.152 cm−2). This structural perfection directly dictates the optical and electronic properties of the absorber. Because grain boundaries and strained lattices act as primary sites for non-radiative recombination centers, their structural minimization geometrically limits trap states. This is directly correlated in our optical and dynamic characterizations: the structurally superior CsI-modified films exhibit significantly amplified steady-state PL intensity and a prolonged TRPL carrier lifetime (improving from 38.19 ns to 39.95 ns), proving that photogenerated carriers are successfully shielded from rapid, defect-mediated quenching. Ultimately, these coupled structural and optical improvements dictate the macroscopic photovoltaic performance. The physical reduction in boundary defects translates into a stark drop in electrical trap density (from 1.57 × 1021 cm−3 to 8.03 × 1020 cm−3) and a five-fold increase in space-charge-limited carrier mobility. In the photovoltaic device, this highly efficient charge extraction and suppressed recombination directly mitigate voltage losses, driving the open-circuit voltage (Voc) from 0.88 V to 1.01 V and vastly improving the broad-spectrum quantum efficiency (EQE). Thus, the champion PCE of 23.17% is not an isolated metric, but the direct, culminating result of uniformly coupling structural strain relief with optimized optical carrier dynamics.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates significant research progress in the ambient-processed fabrication of highly efficient and stable methylammonium lead iodide (MAPbI3) perovskite solar cells through a controlled precursor engineering strategy. By pre-intercalating cesium iodide (CsI) directly into the PbI2 precursor matrix, we effectively bypassed the solubility limits of conventional mixed-cation solution processing. Energy Dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) confirmed that this two-step method yields a highly homogeneous distribution of Cs+ across the perovskite lattice without phase segregation.

Mechanistically, this pre-intercalation drives a dual kinetic and thermodynamic regulation of the perovskite film. Kinetically, the Cs-modified intermediate phase modulates the surface reaction rate, promoting Ostwald ripening to enlarge the average grain size from 161 nm to 255 nm. Thermodynamically, the partial A-site substitution of the smaller Cs+ cation effectively relieves localized lattice microstrain. This structural relaxation drastically increases the formation energy of deep-level defects, resulting in a significantly reduced trap density (from 1.57 × 1021 cm−3 to 8.03 × 1020 cm−3), minimized dislocation density, and enhanced charge carrier mobility.

These fundamental structural refinements directly translated into superior optoelectronic dynamics and device performance. The optimized ambient-processed device achieved a champion power conversion efficiency (PCE) of 23.17%. Crucially, the fundamental capability of this architecture was validated in a strictly controlled glovebox environment, where the optimized device achieved a highly competitive true PCE of 23.78% with a state-of-the-art fill factor of 80.92%, confirming that the CsI pre-intercalation successfully facilitates efficient charge transport and extraction.

Furthermore, the optimized architecture exhibited remarkable durability, maintaining 97.71% of its initial operational efficiency under continuous maximum power point tracking, and retaining ∼82% of its performance after 30 days of unencapsulated ambient storage. Ultimately, these findings highlight that intelligent precursor chemistry engineering is a highly effective, scalable, and industrially relevant pathway to elevate the performance and durability of baseline MAPbI3 systems to rival complex mixed-cation architectures.
