Partial degenerate Stirling numbers
Beáta Bényi, Sithembele Nkonkobe

Abstract
. In this paper, we study some combinations of the degenerate and incomplete Stirling numbers of the second kind. We use a combinatorial approach and provide some asymptotic results.

1.
Introduction

Stirling numbers of the second kind,
\begin{Bmatrix}n\\
k\end{Bmatrix}
, occur in several areas of mathematics, such as combinatorics, analysis, algebra, probability theory. The basic combinatorial meaning is the number of ways the set
[n]=\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
can be partitioned into
k
non-empty subsets. Probably this is the reason for their frequent occurrences throughout the different mathematical branches. The number array can also be defined by the recursion that states for
0<k<n

\displaystyle\begin{Bmatrix}n+1\\
k\end{Bmatrix}=k\begin{Bmatrix}n\\
k\end{Bmatrix}+\begin{Bmatrix}n-1\\
k\end{Bmatrix},

(1)

with initial conditions
\begin{Bmatrix}0\\
0\end{Bmatrix}=1
and
\begin{Bmatrix}n\\
0\end{Bmatrix}=\begin{Bmatrix}0\\
n\end{Bmatrix}=0
for
n>0
.
The analytical importance is revealed by the generating function

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}\begin{Bmatrix}n\\
k\end{Bmatrix}\frac{x^{n}}{n!}=\frac{\begin{pmatrix}e^{x}-1\end{pmatrix}^{k}}{k!}.

(2)

Numerous articles have addressed the introduction and study of generalizations of Stirling numbers for decades
[
2
,
8
,
15
,
24
,
26
,
29
]
. Moreover, efforts to unify different approaches were made again and again in order to clarify the connections and the differences. In this way, the literature of the Stirling numbers is growing like a great tree. In the case of special functions, one direction is the study of the incomplete versions of the functions, the other direction is the study of the degenerate versions of the functions. We focus on the connection of these two concepts for Stirling numbers of the second kind, emphasizing the combinatorial nature of our mathematical objects.

In the next section, we recall these two concepts, then we define the new type of Stirling numbers comprising both of them using a combinatorial approach, and in the remaining sections we investigate the properties of these numbers, in how far the properties of the generalizations may be kept further.

Why is it worth studying such new generalizations and combinations of ideas? This is what mathematics always does because the more we play around with conditions, the more we understand not only the underlying object but also the techniques that are useful in their study, and eventually it leads to the development of new techniques. Why this particular topic? As we outlined before, the importance of partitions cannot be questioned in mathematics.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Incomplete Stirling numbers

Restrictions on the size of the subsets in a partition arise in many applications, hence there was an obvious need to consider this in general using combinatorial tools.

The
restricted
Stirling numbers
\begin{Bmatrix}n\\
k\end{Bmatrix}_{\leq\ell}
are defined as the number of partitions of
[n]
into
k
non-empty subsets such that each subset contains
at most

\ell
elements
[
21
,
25
,
10
]
. The recursive relation (
1
) modifies

\displaystyle\begin{Bmatrix}n+1\\
k\end{Bmatrix}_{\leq\ell}=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{\ell-1}\binom{n}{i}\begin{Bmatrix}n-i\\
k-1\end{Bmatrix}_{\leq\ell}.

(3)

Similarly, the number of partitions of
[n]
into
k
non-empty subsets such that each subset contains
at least

\ell
elements is called the
associated
Stirling number of the second kind and is denoted by
\begin{Bmatrix}n\\
k\end{Bmatrix}_{\geq\ell}

[
14
,
19
,
21
,
25
]
. The basic recursion and the generating function of associated Stirling numbers of the second kind are as follows. Let
n\geq k\ell
, then

\displaystyle\begin{Bmatrix}n+1\\
k\end{Bmatrix}_{\geq\ell}=\sum_{i=\ell-1}^{n}\binom{n}{i}\begin{Bmatrix}n-i\\
k-1\end{Bmatrix}_{\geq\ell},

Using the following notation of incomplete exponential functions

\displaystyle e_{\leq\ell}(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{\ell}\frac{x^{i}}{i!}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad e_{<\ell}(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{\ell-1}\frac{x^{i}}{i!},

the generating functions for incomplete Stirling numbers of the second kind are
[
21
]

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\begin{Bmatrix}n\\
k\end{Bmatrix}_{\leq\ell}\frac{x^{n}}{n!}=\frac{(e_{\leq\ell}(x)-1)^{k}}{k!}

\displaystyle\sum_{n=k\ell}^{\infty}\begin{Bmatrix}n\\
k\end{Bmatrix}_{\geq\ell}\frac{x^{n}}{n!}=\frac{(e^{x}-e_{<\ell}(x))^{k}}{k!}.

(4)

2.2.
Degenerate Stirling numbers

We recall now the notion on degenerate special numbers and functions. Let
\lambda
be any nonzero real number.
The
degenerate (or generalized)
falling factorial is defined as

\displaystyle(t)_{n,\lambda}=t(t-\lambda)\cdots(t-(n-1)\lambda),\quad(t)_{0,\lambda}=1.

For
\lambda=1
, we get the falling factorial
(t)_{n}=(t)_{n,1}=t(t-1)\cdots(t-n+1)
, while
\lim_{\lambda\to 0}(t)_{n,\lambda}=t^{n}
. (Other special values for
\lambda
lead to other often used combinatorial numbers, as for example
\lambda=-1
gives the increasing factorial.)
The
degenerate
Stirling numbers of the second kind can be defined by the relation

\displaystyle(t)_{n,\lambda}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\begin{Bmatrix}n\\
k\end{Bmatrix}_{\lambda}(t)_{k}.

In order to have the generating function, let us recall the definition of the
degenerate exponential function

[
20
]

\displaystyle e_{\lambda}^{x}(t)=(1+\lambda t)^{\frac{x}{t}}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(x)_{n,\lambda}\frac{t^{n}}{n!};\quad e_{\lambda}(t)=e^{1}_{\lambda}(t).

Note that
\lim_{\lambda\to 0}e^{x}_{\lambda}(t)=e^{xt}
.
Then we have

\displaystyle\sum_{n=k}^{\infty}\begin{Bmatrix}n\\
k\end{Bmatrix}_{\lambda}\frac{t^{n}}{n!}=\frac{(e_{\lambda}(t)-1)^{k}}{k!}.

2.3.
Generalized Stirling numbers

Hsu and Shiue
[
15
]
introduced the so called Stirling pairs using a generalization of Stirling numbers as connecting coefficients between polynomials. Let
\alpha,\beta,\gamma
be complex numbers and
(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)\not=(0,0,0)
. Let
\begin{Bmatrix}n\\
k\end{Bmatrix}_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}
denote the
generalized Stirling numbers
defined by the relation

\displaystyle(t)_{n,\alpha}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\begin{Bmatrix}n\\
k\end{Bmatrix}_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}(t-\gamma)_{k,\beta}.

We adopt the notation introduced by Maltenfort
[
22
]
instead of the original
S(n,k,\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
notation.
Then, the generating function for
\begin{Bmatrix}n\\
k\end{Bmatrix}_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}
is
[
15
]

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\begin{Bmatrix}n\\
k\end{Bmatrix}_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}\frac{t^{n}}{n!}=\frac{(e^{\beta}_{\alpha}(t)-1)^{k}}{\beta^{k}k!}e^{\gamma}_{\alpha}(t).

Generalized Stirling numbers involve an amount of special generalizations that have arisen earlier (or later) as special cases. They were studied from many points of view, combinatorial and probabilistic models were given, and combinatorial, analytical, and algebraic properties were derived.
The recursion (
1
) has the form in the case of the generalized Stirling numbers as follows:

\displaystyle\begin{Bmatrix}n+1\\
k\end{Bmatrix}_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}=\begin{Bmatrix}n\\
k-1\end{Bmatrix}_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}+(k\beta-n\alpha+\gamma)\begin{Bmatrix}n\\
k\end{Bmatrix}_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}.

Maltenfort
[
22
]
used this recursion to extend the set of parameter values, i.e., for negative
n
and
k
values, and for
\alpha
,
\beta
,
\gamma
lying in any ring.
Corcino
[
12
]
derived an elegant formula for
n\geq 0

\displaystyle\begin{Bmatrix}n\\
k\end{Bmatrix}_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}=\frac{\Delta^{k}(\beta t+\gamma)_{\alpha,n}|_{t=0}}{\beta^{k}k!},

where
\Delta^{k}
denotes the forward difference. Considering the definition of forward difference,
\Delta^{k}a_{n}=\sum_{i=0}^{k}(-1)^{i}\binom{k}{i}a_{n+k-i}
, we see that the above formula is the translation of the relation:

\displaystyle\begin{Bmatrix}n\\
k\end{Bmatrix}_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}=\frac{1}{\beta^{k}k!}\sum_{j=0}^{k}(-1)^{k-j}\binom{k}{j}(\beta j+\gamma)_{n,\alpha}.

The interested reader can dive into the deep sea of beautiful identities that can be found in the wide literature of generalized Stirling numbers. See, for example
[
6
,
11
,
12
,
18
,
22
,
28
]
and references therein.

We recall one of the basic combinatorial models presented in
[
6
]
, which we want to extend in this current work.
First, in order to talk about the properties of the partition easier, we introduce some simple expressions. We distribute elements into cells that have inner structures.
Cells are separated into compartments and each compartment can contain only
1
element. We refer to the number of compartments in a cell as
order
of the cell.
We say that a cell has the
\alpha
-closing property
if their compartments have cyclic ordered numbering, each element is placed at a time, and for each available
\alpha
compartments only the first compartment may be occupied by an element.

Definition 2.1
.

[
6
]
Let
\alpha,\beta,\gamma,n,k
be non-negative integers such that
\alpha|\gamma
,
\alpha|\beta
, and
k\leq n
.
By a
(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
-partition we mean a partition of
[n]
into
k+1
cells with
\alpha
-closing properties such that

1.

there is a cell of order
\gamma
that may be empty,

2.

there are
k
cells of order
\beta
that are non-empty and each cell has a ,,favorite” compartments, i.e., the first element in the cell goes to this specific compartment.

The generalized Stirling numbers,
\begin{Bmatrix}n\\
k\end{Bmatrix}_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}
, count the number of
(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
-partitions of
[n]
into
k+1
subsets
[
6
]
.

In the following proofs, we refer to the cell with
\gamma
compartments as
special cell
and to the cells with
\beta
compartments as
ordinary cells
.
We use the notation
\begin{Bmatrix}n\\
k\end{Bmatrix}_{\alpha,\beta}
for the special case
\gamma=0
in the remainder of the paper.
Throughout the literature, we have models that are not substantially different from this model; one can view them as reformulations. For more details, examples, and explanations and related studies, see
[
1
,
6
]
.

2.4.
Weighted mixed partitions

We now introduce a model to interpret the generalized Stirling numbers which was not defined yet in the literature in this form. However, the idea is not novel. We explain what we mean below.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a ring,
\alpha,\beta,\gamma
elements in
\mathcal{A}
,
n
and
k
be integers. Let
(G,P_{k})
be a pair where
G
is a possible empty set and
P_{k}=B_{1}/B_{2}/\ldots/B_{k}
is a set partition with
k
non-empty blocks. We associate the weights with the sets
G
and
B_{i}
s as follows. The weight of
G
is
(\gamma)_{|G|,\alpha}
, where
|G|
denotes the size of
G
, i.e., the number of elements in
G
. Set
w(G)=1
, if
G
is empty. The weight of a block
B_{i}
is
w(B_{i})=(\beta-\alpha)_{|B_{i}|-1,\alpha}=\frac{(\beta)_{|B_{i}|,\alpha}}{\beta}
. Note that
w(B)=1
for a singleton block
B
. The weight of partition
P_{k}
is the product of the weights of its blocks, i.e.,
w(P_{k})=\sum_{i=1}^{k}w(B_{i})
. Set
w(P_{0})=1
for the empty partition. Let the weight of a pair
(G,P_{k})
defined as
w((G,P_{k}))=w(G)w(P_{k})
.
Let
\Pi^{*}_{n,k}
denote all distributions of
[n]
in a possibly empty set,
G
, and
k
non-empty blocks,
P_{k}
. Then, the following combinatorial definition coincides with the generalized Stirling numbers

\displaystyle\begin{Bmatrix}n\\
k\end{Bmatrix}_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}=\sum_{(G,P_{k})\in\Pi^{*}_{n,k}}w((G,P_{k})).

As a demonstration of the usefulness of this definition, we recover some statements about generalized Stirling numbers.

•

\begin{Bmatrix}n\\
k\end{Bmatrix}_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}=0
, if
n<k
and
\begin{Bmatrix}n\\
k\end{Bmatrix}_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}=1
, if
n=k
, since in this case we have a partition such that each block in
P_{n}
is a singleton and
G
is empty. Both the empty set
G
and any singleton block have weight
1
.

•

\begin{Bmatrix}n\\
0\end{Bmatrix}_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}=(\gamma)_{n,\alpha}
, since if we have
k=0
,
w(P_{0})=1
, and all elements are in the set
G
.

•

\begin{Bmatrix}n\\
1\end{Bmatrix}_{\alpha,\beta}=(\beta-\alpha)_{n-1,\alpha}
, since
G
is empty and there is only one non-empty block of size
n
.

•

\begin{Bmatrix}n+1\\
k\end{Bmatrix}_{\alpha,\beta}=\begin{Bmatrix}n\\
k-1\end{Bmatrix}_{\alpha,\beta,\beta-\alpha}
.
The left hand side of the identity count partitions of
[n+1]
into
k
non-empty blocks; pairs
(G,P_{k})
such that
G
is empty. Consider the block
B
that contains
1
.
w(B)=(\beta-\alpha)_{|B|-1,\alpha}
. Now, delete
1
from it,
B^{\prime}=B\setminus\{1\}
. Further, if we delete the block
B
from the set partition
P_{k}
, we obtain a partition
P^{\prime}_{k-1}
. Together,
(B^{\prime},P^{\prime}_{k-1})
construct a pair on
n
elements such that
\gamma=\beta-\alpha
.

•

\begin{Bmatrix}n\\
n-1\end{Bmatrix}_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}=n\gamma+\binom{n}{2}(\beta-\alpha)
. The right hand side of this identity
counts the pairs according to whether
G
is empty or not. If
G
is not empty, all sets contain only one element. There are
n
ways to choose the element in
G
, and this set has the weight
\gamma
. On the other hand if
G
is empty, then one of the blocks in
P_{k}
has two elements, the others are singletons. Only the block with
2
elements has weight other than 1, namely
\beta-\alpha
. There are
\binom{n}{2}
possibilities to choose these two elements.

•

\begin{Bmatrix}n\\
k\end{Bmatrix}_{c\alpha,c\beta,c\gamma}=c^{n-k}\begin{Bmatrix}n\\
k\end{Bmatrix}_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}
, since changing the parameters
\alpha
,
\beta
,
\gamma
by a multiplication factor
c
has an effect on the total weight as a multiplication factor
c^{n-k}
.

The idea of mixed weighted partitions roots in previous results. Maltenfort
[
22
]
extends
\alpha,\beta,\gamma
values to any ring. The authors
[
30
]
introduced
mixed partition numbers
and as a particular case
mixed Stirling numbers of the second kind
, the papers
[
2
,
7
]
investigate them in more detail. Finally, the notion of partition enumeration with a given weight associated with the blocks goes back to Carlitz
[
9
]
, who defined a version of Stirling numbers with
\lambda^{j}
as a weight on a
j
element block. The authors
[
3
,
4
]
make use of weights for their studies and an even more general idea is the
vector weighted Stirling numbers
introduced by the authors of
[
13
]
.

The connection with
(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
-partitions is that the weights correspond to the inner structure of the cells. In this sense, the mixed weighted partitions are abstracter compared to the
(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
-partitions. However, this approach makes enumerations in certain cases easier because it is closer to the original set partition definition.

3.
Incomplete degenerate Stirling numbers of the second kind

We consider now two possibilities to combine the two concepts, degenerateness and incompleteness of Stirling numbers of the second kind.

3.1.
Degenerate restricted Stirling numbers of the second kind

Definition 3.1
.

Let
\begin{Bmatrix}n\\
k\end{Bmatrix}_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}^{\leq\ell}
denote the number of
(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
-partitions of
[n]
in
k+1
cells such that
k
ordinary cells contain at most
\ell
elements.

Alternatively, let
\Pi^{\leq\ell}_{n,k}
denote all distributions of
[n]
into a possibly empty set,
G
, and
k
non-empty blocks,
P_{k}
, such that each block has cardinality at most
\ell
. Then, we have

\displaystyle\begin{Bmatrix}n\\
k\end{Bmatrix}_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}^{\leq\ell}=\sum_{(G,P_{k})\in\Pi^{\leq\ell}_{n,k}}w((G,P_{k})).

First, let us show the fundamental recursive relation,

Theorem 3.1
.

We have

\displaystyle\begin{Bmatrix}n+1\\
k\end{Bmatrix}_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}^{\leq\ell}=\gamma\begin{Bmatrix}n\\
k\end{Bmatrix}_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma-\alpha}^{\leq\ell}+\sum_{i=k-1}^{n-\ell-1}\binom{n}{i}(\beta-\alpha)_{n-i,\alpha}\begin{Bmatrix}i\\
k-1\end{Bmatrix}_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}^{\leq\ell}.

(5)

Proof.

The left hand side counts
(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
-partitions of
[n+1]
into
k+1
cells.
The element
n+1
is either in the special cell or in one of the ordinary
k
cells. If it is in the special cell, we choose the compartment in
\gamma
ways, and the remaining elements can be partitioned in
\begin{Bmatrix}n\\
k\end{Bmatrix}_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma-\alpha}^{\leq\ell}
ways. On the other hand, if the element
n+1
is in an ordinary cell, let
i
be the number of elements that are not in the same cell as
n+1
, choose them in
\binom{n}{i}
ways. Furthermore, assume
n+1
is in the favorite compartment, then distribute the
n-i
elements that join the cell with
n+1
in
(\beta-\alpha)(\beta-2\alpha)\cdots(\beta-(n-i)\alpha)
ways. Finally, the
i
elements construct a
(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
-partition into
k-1
non-empty ordinary cells.
∎

The only substantial difference between identity (
3
) and its generalized version (
5
) is in the part dealing with the special cell and in an additional factor
(\beta-\alpha)_{n-i,\alpha}
which arises because of the additional inner structure of a cell.

Let us denote the incomplete degenerate exponential function as follows:

\displaystyle e_{\lambda;\leq\ell}^{x}(t)=\sum_{n=0}^{\ell}(x)_{n,\lambda}\frac{t^{n}}{n!}.

The generating function of
\begin{Bmatrix}n\\
k\end{Bmatrix}_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}^{\leq\ell}
is given as follows

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\begin{Bmatrix}n\\
k\end{Bmatrix}_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}^{\leq\ell}\frac{x^{n}}{n!}=e^{\gamma}_{\alpha}(x)\frac{\left(e^{\beta}_{\alpha;\leq\ell}(x)-1\right)^{k}}{\beta^{k}k!}.

Note that for
\gamma=0
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\begin{Bmatrix}n\\
k\end{Bmatrix}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\leq\ell}\frac{x^{n}}{n!}=\frac{\left(e^{\beta}_{\alpha;\leq\ell}(x)-1\right)^{k}}{\beta^{k}k!}.

Let
A(x)
denote the generating function for short.
The first derivative of the generating function according to the variable
x

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dx}A(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\begin{Bmatrix}n+1\\
k\end{Bmatrix}_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}^{\leq\ell}\frac{x^{n}}{n!}.

On the other hand,

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dx}A(x)=\gamma e^{\gamma-\alpha}_{\alpha}(x)\frac{(e^{\beta}_{\alpha;\leq\ell}(x)-1)^{k}}{\beta^{k}k!}+e^{\gamma}_{\alpha}(x)\frac{(e^{\beta}_{\alpha;\leq\ell}(x)-1)^{k-1}}{\beta^{(k-1)}(k-1)!}\frac{d}{dx}\frac{(e^{\beta}_{\alpha;\leq\ell}(x)-1)}{\beta},

where

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dx}\frac{(e^{\beta}_{\alpha;\leq\ell}(x)-1)}{\beta}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\begin{Bmatrix}n+1\\
1\end{Bmatrix}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\leq\ell}\frac{x^{n}}{n!},

and since

\displaystyle\begin{Bmatrix}n\\
1\end{Bmatrix}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\leq\ell}=\left\{\begin{matrix}0,&\mbox{if }n>\ell\\
(\beta-\alpha)_{n;\alpha}&\mbox{if }n\leq\ell.\end{matrix}\right.

we end up with another proof of the recursion in Theorem
3.1
.

A three term recurrence is given in the next theorem.

Theorem 3.2
.

We have

\displaystyle\begin{Bmatrix}n\\
k\end{Bmatrix}_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}^{\leq\ell}

\displaystyle=\gamma\begin{Bmatrix}n\\
k\end{Bmatrix}_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma-\alpha}^{\leq\ell}+\gamma\sum_{i=k-1}^{\ell}\binom{n}{i}(\beta-\alpha)_{n-i+1,\alpha}\begin{Bmatrix}i-1\\
k-1\end{Bmatrix}_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma-\alpha}^{\leq\ell}

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=k-1}^{\ell}\binom{n}{i}(\beta-\alpha)_{n-i+1,\alpha}\sum_{j=0}^{i-1}\binom{i-1}{j}(\beta-\alpha)_{i-j,\alpha}\begin{Bmatrix}j\\
k-2\end{Bmatrix}_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}^{\leq\ell}.

Proof.

Algebraically, we get the theorem by applying the previous identity twice. In the following, we provide a combinatorial argument.

Case 1:
The
(n+1)
th element goes into the special cell. There are
\gamma
ways to choose a compartment for the
(n+1)
th element which closes
\alpha-1
further compartments, hence; the remaining
n
elements form an
(\alpha,\beta,\gamma-\alpha)
-partition which can be done in
\begin{Bmatrix}n\\
k\end{Bmatrix}_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma-\alpha}^{\leq\ell}
ways.

Case 2:
The
(n+1)
th element goes in an ordinary cell together with
n-i
other elements. Also, from the
i
remaining elements the maximal element (call it
\epsilon
) is in the cell having
\gamma
compartments. There are
\binom{n}{i}
ways to choose
i
elements that are not in the same cell as
(n+1)
. The
n-i
other elements, and
(n+1)
may be distributed in the cell in
\begin{Bmatrix}n-i+1\\
1\end{Bmatrix}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\leq\ell}
ways. As from the
i
elements
\epsilon
is in the cell having
\gamma
compartments, there are
\gamma
ways of placing
\epsilon
in the cell. The remaining
i-1
elements may be distributed in the
k
cells including the one with
\gamma
compartments in
\begin{Bmatrix}i-1\\
k-1\end{Bmatrix}_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}^{\leq\ell}
ways.

Case 3:
The element
n+1
is in one of the ordinary
k
cells each together with
n-i
other elements. Also, from the
i
remaining elements the maximal element (
\epsilon
) is also in an ordinary cell. The
n-i
other elements, together with
n+1
may be distributed in
\begin{Bmatrix}n-i+1\\
1\end{Bmatrix}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\leq\ell}
ways. From the
i
elements besides
\epsilon
we can choose in
\binom{i-1}{j}
ways,
j
elements which are not in the same cell as
\epsilon
. The
j
elements may be distributed in
k-1
cells including the special cell in
\begin{Bmatrix}j\\
k-2\end{Bmatrix}_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}^{\leq\ell}
ways. Now,
\epsilon
together with the
i-j-1
other elements may be distributed into the cell in
\begin{Bmatrix}i-j\\
1\end{Bmatrix}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\leq\ell}
ways.
∎

3.2.
Generalized associated Stirling numbers

Similarly, we can define
\begin{Bmatrix}n\\
k\end{Bmatrix}_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}^{\geq\ell}
as the number of
(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
-partitions such that ordinary cells contain at least
\ell
elements, or as mixed weighted partitions, i.e., the distribution of
[n]
into
(G,P_{k})
pairs such that the blocks of
P_{k}
contain at least
\ell
elements. We omit the details of the study of these numbers.
We turn our attention to partitions into cells with unlimited compartments. We call a cell whose compartments are unlimited a
free cell
. (Actually, free cells correspond to the special case
\alpha=0
,
\beta=1
). Otherwise, as before, a cell has compartments limited to
1
element.
We introduce and investigate the following generalization od Stirling numbers in this section.

Definition 3.2
.

Let
n,k,\ell,\gamma
non-negative integers such that
k,\ell\leq n
. Let
\begin{Bmatrix}n\\
k\end{Bmatrix}_{\gamma}^{>\ell}
denote the number of partitions of
[n]
in
k
non-empty free cells containing at least
\ell+1
elements and a possible empty free cell with
\gamma
compartments.

We can verify that
\begin{Bmatrix}n\\
k\end{Bmatrix}^{>\ell}_{\gamma}
counts mixed weighted partitions
(G,P_{k})
of
[n]
with weight
w((G,P_{k}))=\gamma^{|G|}
such that each block in
P_{k}
has more than
\ell
elements.

Theorem 3.3
.

We have

\displaystyle\begin{Bmatrix}n\\

In the previous sections, we presented some concepts of generalizations and some examples how these concepts can be combined. In this section, we demonstrate a new idea for combining these notions. We emphasize that our definition is one variation of the many possibilities.
