Executive Summary
In the formulations of many composite materials, polymeric materials are often used as
binder materials to enhance processability, functionality, and safety during manufacturing.
For instance, poly(ester urethane), such as Estane® 5703, is used in the formulation of PBX
9501, and vinyl copolymer elastomer (VCE) is used in the formulation of VCE/filler compos-
ites. The stability of these binders directly impacts the overall stability and performance of
their composites. Estane and VCE are prone to hydrolytic degradation, with water sorption
behavior playing a significant role in their long-term stability and aging behavior. Hence,
it is critical to study their water sorption behavior under various humidity and tempera-
ture conditions. To improve the accuracy and reduce labor-intensive work involved in water
sorption experiments, an automated Dynamic Vapor Sorption (DVS) system was employed,
which can generate large datasets. To efficiently process and analyze these datasets, Python
scripts were developed. These scripts not only streamlined data processing, but also accu-
rately derived the Arrhenius parameters related to water diffusion and sorption properties
for the tested materials. This approach offers a robust framework for evaluating water trans-
port and sorption processes in polymeric materials and adds valuable capabilities to future
water sorption testing efforts.

1 Introduction
Moisture-induced changes in material properties are a major concern for the processing,
packaging, storage, and shelf life of products. These concerns are particularly relevant to
some polymeric materials used in LANL weapon applications. For example, Estane® 5703
(E03) is used as a binder in plastic-bonded explosive (PBX) formulations such as PBX
9501. E03 is known to undergo hydrolytic degradation, which results in reduced molecular
weight and poor mechanical properties. Consequently, the performance of PBX materials
is compromised [1, 2]. Because water content strongly influences the hydrolysis of E03,
understanding its water sorption behavior is essential for assessing long-term aging risks
in the PBX composites. Although the water sorption properties of E03 have been studied
previously [1,2], these studies were only carried out at ambient temperatures. In reality,
these materials are often used at the temperatures above the ambient conditions, and the
temperature effects on the water sorption can be significant. In this study, the water sorption
behavior of E03 and its composites were investigated under varying temperature and relative
humidity (RH) conditions. Additionally, the sorption properties of other relevant polymers
and composites were evaluated. These materials include: nitroplasticizer (NP) which is used
together with Estane as a binder in PBX 9501 formulation; Estane® 5714 (E14), a type of
poly(ether urethane) with improved hydrolysis resistance [3]; NP-treated Estane materials
(E03 and E14); mock composites of E03/NP and E14/NP, which serve as surrogates for
explosive crystals in PBX 9501; and VCE polymer and VCE/filler composites with varying
VCE concentrations.
 Water vapor sorption isotherms provide a simple and direct method to investigate the
interactions of a material with moisture. Traditionally, sorption measurements are conducted
using a desiccator. To control RH conditions inside the desiccator, saturated salt solutions are
used. To control temperatures, the desiccator is placed inside an oven. Although inexpensive
in the experimental setting, this operation is highly labor-intensive for sampling and weight
monitoring. Since frequent human interruption during the measurement is required (e.g.,
removing the samples from the desiccator for the weight measurement), this not only alters
the atmosphere of the desiccator and delays thermal equilibrium, but also increases workload.
As a result, inaccurate and poorly reproducible data are obtained with complication of salty
particles deposed on tested materials. To overcome these disadvantages, the current study
employs a dynamic vapor sorption (DVS) apparatus, which automatically controls and varies
the temperature and RH settings in the sample chamber. When the settings are changed,
the system maintains the environment until the sample reaches equilibrium with the new
settings. The sample weights are continuously measured with high accuracy (1µg), but
without human interference. The combination of accurate weight measurement and large
sample size allows the use of thin samples, which optimizes surface-to-volume ratio and
accelerates in the sorption process. Since the samples are directly underneath the gas flow, as
shown in Figure 1 (right), the boundary layer is minimized and the humidity gradient in the
sample is reduced. The Rotronic solid state sensor used in the ProUmid systems is calibrated
at 0.15% RH and maintains high accuracy near 0% RH. Additionally, its sample holder can
accommodate up to 23 samples, as shown in Figure 1 (left), which enables simultaneous
water sorption testing across multiple materials. These advanced engineering designs enable
reliable water sorption measurements for a wide range of materials in a single test.

 For polymeric materials, due to the low water diffusivity, their water sorption tests are

processing was essential for this study. To streamline data processing, Python scripts were

under various conditions and determine Arrhenius parameters for water diffusivity and sorp-
tion capacity. Finally, the limitations of the DVS instrumentation and data interpretation
are also addressed.

as the natural, randomly driven, net transfer of water molecules from a region of higher
concentration to one of lower concentration. This means that each water molecule diffuses
solely due to the concentration gradient of water. This differs from the mechanism known
as “bulk flow,” in which water movement is driven by a pressure gradient or other physical
potential. The law that governs diffusion is well established, particularly Ficks Second Law,
as presented in Equation (1) [5].

3 Experimental Methods
3.1 DVS Apparatus Settings
During a sorption run, the DVS system was programmed to follow a predefined set of con-
ditions with varying temperature and RH settings. Prior to each run, the DVS chamber
was held at 5% RH and the initial temperature setting for an extended period of time to
ensure both dryness and thermal equilibrium of the samples. Each run was classified as
either a short-term or long-term run. For exploratory purposes, short-term runs (typically
lasting a few days) were conducted to quickly evaluate the water sorption behavior of sev-
eral materials under different conditions so that sorption equilibrium might not be reached
before test conditions were changed in some runs. In a long-term run, a tighter threshold
was used, such as less than 0.01 wt% of the weight change within 8-10 minutes for all tested
samples. Long-term runs typically lasted more than 10 days, and in some cases, extended
to a few months. Typically, two RH conditions were tested in each long-term run across
various temperature settings. At each temperature, RH was cycled between a high level (for
adsorption) and a low level (for desorption). The RH was adjusted only after the weight
change for all samples dropped below a threshold at which the equilibrium was presumably
reached. To ensure reproducibility, two to three replicates of each material were often used,
as shown in the DVS apparatus in Figure 3 (left and middle). Representative water sorption
profiles from a long-term run are shown in Figure 3 (right).

3.2 Estane Sample Preparation
E03 polymer (pearl form), containing 23 wt% hard segments [1,2], was purchased from B. F.
Goodrich (lot number 5EE1100SK7). Following its purchase around 1995, it was stored in
a freezer. Prior to the tests, E03 flat sheets (≤ 1.0 mm thick) were prepared for consistent
analysis. E14 polymer (pearl form) was purchased from Lubrizol (lot number H38356) in
August 2018. Similarly, E14 flat sheets (≤ 1.5 mm thick) were prepared for consistent
analysis. Circular disks, ∼19 mm in diameter, were cut from the flat sheets. These were
referred to as film samples and pristine samples. The thickness of each sample (including
those discussed in later sections) was measured three times prior to the testing. The averaged
thickness of each sample was used in the diffusivity calculations.
 To study the effect of NP uptake on the water sorption behavior of Estane, some E03
and E14 films were saturated with NP prior to the testing, which were referred to as NP
treated samples.

3.3 Mock Sample Preparation
Due to safety concerns, idoxuridine (IDOX) was used as a surrogate for HMX (cyclotetra-
methylene-tetranitramine), a high explosive compound used in the PBX 9501 formulation.
To mimic the PBX formulation, ∼95 wt% IDOX crystals and ∼5 wt% plasticized Estane
binder (with an NP:Estane ratio of 1:1) were compounded to form mock materials. In 2021,
the first set of samples was prepared to train new staff and to explore the feasibility of
producing mock materials using different Estanes. In 2022, a second set of mock samples
was prepared using the same formulation. Each set of mock materials included five types of
Estane materials: two pristine and three aged variants. The two pristine Estane materials
had molecular weights of 163 ± 10 kilodaltons (kDa) for E14 and 128 ± 10 kDa for E03,
respectively. The three aged Estane materials included: two 14-month-aged Estane materi-
als, with molecular weights of 162 ± 5 kDa for E14 and 110 ± 5 kDa for E03, respectively;

 7

and one naturally aged E03 stored in laboratory conditions for ≥35 years, with a molecular
weight of 20 kDa.
 These five Estane materials were each compounded with IDOX powder to produce five
distinct mock materials, which were then pressed into pellets measuring 3 mm in thickness
and 19 mm in diameter. Due to their substantial thickness and low D/L ratio (approximately
6.3), the assumption of one-dimensional diffusion in these mock samples is compromised. As
a result, the calculated diffusivity may be overestimated. In later discussion, “E03 mock”
refers to mock samples made with E03 binder, while “E14 mock” refers to those made with
the E14 binder. All mock materials and samples were prepared by Q-5, High Explosives
Science and Technology, engineers (John Yeager and Matthew Herman).

3.4 VCE Sample Preparation
VCE polymer and its composites with varying VCE concentrations were prepared at Kansas
City Plant (now Kansas City National Security Campus, or KCNSC). The VCE-containing
filler particles are referred to as VCE composites. Four VCE composites with different
VCE polymer concentrations (20, 30, 50, and 75 wt%) were tested alongside 100 wt% VCE
polymer samples. The thickness of these samples was ≤ 1.0 mm. The diameter of these
samples was ∼19 mm.

4 Python Code Development
4.1 Data Organization
To analyze water sorption data in Python, the data were organized into a structured folder
hierarchy, as shown in Figure 4. Initially, a folder named “Water Absorption Project”
was created. Within this parent folder, subfolders were created according to the sample
types, such as, “Estane”, “Mock Estane”, “VCE” and “Estane Films & NP”. Each subfolder
contains additional folders named by the experiment date in the YYYYMMDD format (e.g.,
“20230301”), with no spaces or separators. Each date-labeled folder contains four subfolders:
“Raw Data”, “Sorption Figures”, “Arrhenius Plots”, and “Summarized Water Properties”.
 The “Raw Data” folder contains water sorption data recorded by the DVS apparatus in
Excel format, along with converted files saved in CSV format.
 The “Sorption Figures” folder contains three subfolders: “Original Figures”, “Shifted
Figures”, and “Shifted Data”. The “Original Figures” subfolder contains regenerated sorp-
tion profiles for all samples in a run, validated against the original DVS system recordings.
The “Shifted Figures” subfolder contains sorption profiles adjusted for a new set of condi-
tions, where the sample weight change (dm) and run time (t) were reset to zero. Accordingly,
these shifted data were saved in CSV format and stored in the “Shifted Data” subfolder.
 The “Arrhenius Plots” folder contains three subfolders: “D (or Qe or Evc ) vs Temp”,
“D (or Qe ) vs 1000/RT”, and “Average Arrhenius Plots”. The “D (or Qe ) vs 1000/RT”
subfolder contains scatter plots between D (or Qe ) and 1000/RT for all tested samples. The
“D (or Qe or Evc ) vs Temp” subfolder contains the plots of D (or Qe ) versus temperature
for all tested samples in the same run. The “Average Arrhenius Plots” subfolder contains
the averaged Arrhenius laws derived from replicates of each material type. All figures in this
folder are saved in PNG format.
 The “Summarized Water Properties” folder contains three sub-folders: “Arrhenius Pa-
rameters for D and Qe ”, “Raw Data CSV Tables”, and “Raw Data PNG Tables”. The
calculated mass transfer properties (D and Qe ) under various settings are summarized in
these tables and saved in both CSV and PNG formats. For example, the “Arrhenius Parame-
ters for D and Qe ” subfolder contains the calculated diffusivities (D) and water capacities (or
water concentration change, Qe ) at near room temperatures and various RH conditions. The
Arrhenius law parameters (pre-exponential factor, A and activation energy, Ea ) at various
RH levels are also included. All of the data is in CSV format.
 This concludes the list of folders generated by the Python code for each run. This folder
structure was essential to generate in Python avoid the repetitive and time-consuming task
of manually creating folders during data processing. The folders named by experiment date

4.3 Data Shifting
To process the original data shown in Figure 7 (bottom) using Python, the run time was
reset to zero whenever experimental conditions were altered during a run. Because each RH
setting was tested under isothermal conditions, the temperature was first adjusted and held
constant before changing the RH. To ensure thermal equilibrium, the weight of all samples
was monitored until each was below a predetermined threshold (e.g., ≤ 0.01wt%), after
which a new RH level was applied. Therefore, the time at each changed RH was reset to
zero, thereby marking the beginning of a new setting. Correspondingly, the final dm values
recorded before each RH change were set to zero and used as the starting point for subsequent
weight measurements. The Python code recorded these offsets for each sample and applied
them to the remaining data in the run. With this adjustment, the original sorption profile
(Figure 7, bottom) was shifted and replotted as shown in Figure 8. For each temperature
setting, two sorption tests were conducted: adsorption (low to high RH) and desorption
(high to low RH). The experimental data were plotted alongside fitted curves generated
using Equations (9) or (10), depending on the exposure conditions. Dashed lines represent
adsorption (positive weight change), and solid lines represent desorption (negative weight
change). Figure 8 shows that the calculated results fit the experimental data well. The same
color is used for adsorption and desorption data collected at the same temperature, allowing
them to be easily observed as a pair. Black indicates the first segment in the water sorption
profile. The color order for the following segments is red, blue, green, orange, magenta, light
blue, and so on. In this long-term run, the weight changes of E03 Film 1 reached plateaus
during both adsorption and desorption, indicating that equilibrium had been achieved.

 Ideally, when adsorption and desorption reach true equilibrium, the Qe values from both
processes should be equal. However, this equality is not always observed in practice. In the
desorption test of the DVS system, a vacuum was applied to reduce RH, which often results
in the measured weight change being greater than that observed during adsorption. One of
reasons is the loss of volatiles, which can be mistaken for water weight loss. Additionally,
if sorption occurs when the sample is only partially saturated, the measured weight change
reflects a change in water concentration due to different RH settings, rather than the sample’s
full water capacity. Hence, caution must be exercised when interpreting Qe values. Although
the term ”equilibrium” is used in this document, it should be regarded as pseudo-equilibrium
in some cases.

also be underestimated due to possible volatile loss during measurement. Here, more dis-

4.5 Data Plotting and Arrhenius Parameter Calculation
With the obtained D and Qe values at different temperatures (Figure 10), the corresponding
Arrhenius plots were generated. The natural logarithms of D and Qe were plotted against
1000 divided by the natural gas constant, R (8.314 J/mol) and the temperature in Kelvin,
as shown in Figure 11. Data from a short-term run conducted on 07/05/2020 were used for
illustrative purposes in this section.
 Upon producing the Arrhenius plots, outliers were removed so that the data could be
properly fitted with the Arrhenius equation, as shown in Figure 12 (note that the scaling of
the plot changes due to the removal of outliers. Outliers often originate from samples tested
at low temperatures, when water sorption does not reach equilibrium and/or when water
condensation occurs.
 All data obtained from the same material, and its replicates were plotted in the same
figure. For each sample at one temperature, two points were typically obtained - one from
adsorption and one from desorption. If the same conditions (temperature and RH) were
tested more than once for a sample, additional data points were obtained. There were
some discrepancies among these data points. Furthermore, discrepancies were also observed
among replicates, as shown in Figure 12. Many factors can contribute to these discrepan-
cies, including uneven thickness throughout the sample, variations in chemical composition
between samples, or other unforeseen factors. To account for all these variations, the data
were averaged. Hence, the Arrhenius law for one type of material was produced at a specific
RH condition, as shown in Figure 13. In this summary plot, the correlation coefficient of
the curve fit, the calculated activation energy (Ea ) and the Arrhenius law are shown at the
bottom. Similar treatments were applied to Qe data and were used in the rest report.
 With the developed Python code, the raw data collected from the DVS apparatus were
effectively analyzed to obtain the water transport properties of various materials. Using these
Arrhenius laws at specific RH conditions, the averaged water diffusivity and capacity among
replicates at various temperatures can be calculated. Figure 14 highlights this comprehensive
data process. The Python code is available up on requesting.

only the binder absorbs water, these capacities are remarkably high for samples that contain
only 5 wt% binder. One possible explanation for this unexpectedly high water capacity is
that IDOX also adsorbs water, as it contains NH and OH functional groups. Porosity in the
mock samples might also trap water within the material, contributing to unexpectedly high
Qe values.

samples, as indicated by the blue lines in Figure 26 (left column). This phenomenon was
most likely due to increased weight loss of the samples during desorption at 15% RH. As the
temperature increased from 20 °C to 50 °C, the samples lost more weight at the same RH
(15%). Since NP was added to all mock samples, this weight loss was most likely associated
with NP evaporation during the measurement.

 In the summer of 2022, the second set of mock samples was produced and tested. To more

equilibrium within 12 hours, but desorption did not reach equilibrium even after more than

for all samples when RH was switched between 30% and 50%, which is more than 5 times

of a sample directly affects its water uptake. When desorption was conducted at 30% RH,
the mock samples were partially saturated with water. On the other hand, when adsorption
was conducted at 55% RH after desorption at 30% RH, the weight gain would be expected
to be less than when RH was switched between 15% and 75%. Therefore, even though the
Qe notation is used here, the Qe value actually represents the change in water concentration
in a sample due to altered RH conditions and is not equal to its total water capacity. Hence,
extra caution is needed when comparing this quantity across different RH conditions.

from the mock samples show that this intrinsic property of the polymer also affects their

should not have such a significant impact on water sorption behavior. It is suspected that the
interactions between two Estane/NP binders and IDOX also affect water transport properties
in the mock materials.

differ substantially. In contrast to what was observed in the Estane polymers, the diffusivity
obtained from desorption (15% RH) for the mock samples was always higher than that from
adsorption (75% RH), suggesting that the applied vacuum force accelerates the diffusion
process in the mock composites.
 Regarding aging, the results indicate that it has minimal effect on water diffusivity for
both Estane types. However, additional studies are required before this conclusion can be
confirmed.

maximum weight changes (dm) at each temperature were significantly smaller than those
observed when the RH was switched between 15% and 75% at the same temperatures. The
dm values measured only reflect the changes in water concentration due to the RH% changes,
and do not equal to the water capacity of the samples. These changes in water concentration
are directly related to the VCE concentration within the composites, as shown in Figure 33.

 Arrhenius plots of water diffusivity in the VCE samples under various RH% conditions are
shown in Figure 34. The calculated water diffusivity at 25 °C and the Arrhenius parameters
of water diffusivity are given in Table 22 for 15% and 75% RH. Likewise, the calculated

water diffusivity at 25 °C, and the Arrhenius parameters for water diffusivity are provided in
Table 23 for 30% and 55% RH. Overall, the obtained diffusivities ranged from approximately
0.9 to 2.0 × 10−6 cm2 /s. The RH switching range has minimal effect on the D value. This
observation suggests that water saturation in the VCE samples does not significantly affect
water diffusivity in the VCE composites, which implies that the filler particles (up to 80 wt%)
in the VCE composites have minimal impact on water sorption due to its water insoluble
nature, unlike the IDOX crystals (∼95 wt%) in the mock materials. On the other hand,
the D slightly decreases as the filler concentration increases. It is suspected that the filler
particles might act like a physical barrier to hinder the water diffusion. A similar trend
was observed for NP transport in the VCE composites [11]. The activation energy of water
diffusion ranges from approximately 17 to 58 kJ/mol, which falls within the typical range
for water diffusion in polymer materials.
 Arrhenius plots of water sorption capacity in VCE samples under different RH% levels
are presented in Figure 35. The calculated water sorption capacity changes at 25 °C and
the activation energy of water sorption are listed in Tables 24 and 25. When the RH was
switched between 15% and 75%, the obtained water sorption capacities (Qe ) ranged from
approximately 4 to 0.8 mg/g, as shown in Figure 35 (two columns on left). However, when
the RH% was switched between 30% and 55%, the Qe values decreased to a range of approx-
imately 1.5 to 0.3 mg/g, as shown in Figure 35 (two columns on right). In the second run,
due to partial water saturation of the VCE samples, the obtained Qe values reflect changes
in water concentration with changing RH conditions, but do not equal to the water capacity
of the samples.
 The small values of Evc in the VCE run at the 15/75% RH switch suggest that sorption
reaches the equilibrium condition with minimal weight loss during the measurement. When
the RH was switched between 30% and 55%, the values of Evc slightly increases, revealing
the slower sorption process.

6 Conclusion
In this study, we have demonstrated the versatility of the dynamic vapor sorption (DVS)
system in water sorption measurements, which can be readily used to investigate various
material sorption behaviors in a single run under various humidity and temperature settings.
Due to the high throughput of experimental data generated by the DVS system, Python
code was developed not only to visualize the water sorption profiles of tested materials (NP,
E03, E14, Estane/NP/IDOX mock, VCE polymer and VCE/filler composites), but also to
effectively process the data and extrapolate their water transport properties. This capability
can be applied to other materials of interest in LANL materials science studies.
 It is also demonstrated that Fick’s second law can be used to describe the water transport

 61

process in NP, Estane, VCE, and VCE composites, but might not be applicable to the IDOX
mock materials.
 Overall, the effect of temperature on water diffusion is noticeable for all materials tested
in this study. As temperature increases, their water diffusivity increases. However, the effect
of temperature on the water sorption capacity (concentration) is insignificant.
 As expected, the humidity conditions also exhibit some impacts on water sorption capac-
ity and transport of a material. Depending on the interaction strength between water and
materials, the RH effect on the water capacity varies.
 With obtained Arrhenius laws of water diffusivity and water capacity of the tested ma-
terials, these properties can be predicted between 5 and 50 °C at various RH conditions.
