GEP-based ecological functional zoning and land use drivers in coastal southeast China
Authors: Xiaodong Liu, Xiangyonghong Yang, Chencheng Zheng, Ling Lin, Pan Huang, Jurong Liu, Rong Wang, Youlian Xu, Guangchun Li, Yuantao Xu, Jinfu Liu, Zhongsheng He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryGross ecosystem product (GEP) assesses ecological value, but aggregate growth in urbanizing coastal zones often masks severe structural imbalances. Using the Fujian coast (2003–2023), we integrated spatiotemporal GEP accounting and spatial clustering to quantify land-use drivers and delineate functional zones. We reveal that while GEP doubled, foundational regulating services plummeted from 66.95% to 43.89%. Urban expansion triggered a “value substitution trap”, where surging monetized cultural services mathematically masked the physical depletion of regulating capacities, creating a deceptive illusion of sustainability. Based on these structural shifts, five distinct ecological-economic zones were identified. These findings demonstrate that coastal governance must transcend aggregate GEP targets. Implementing spatially differentiated strategies based on internal service structures is essential to mitigate hidden ecological deficits and ensure sustainable coastal planning.

Introduction

As the interface between land and ocean, the coastal zone is one of the regions with the richest global biodiversity and ecological functions, as well as a hotspot for intensive socio-economic activities.1,2 However, with the rapid urbanization and industrialization since China’s reform and opening-up, high-intensity economic development and widespread coastline modification have led to the degradation of coastal wetlands, intensifying the conflict between regional economic growth and ecological protection.3,4 In this context, gross ecosystem product (GEP), as an indicator quantifying the aggregate value of ecological products and services provided by ecosystems for human well-being,5 has become a critical policy instrument for assessing regional sustainable development and practicing the concept that “lucid waters and lush mountains are invaluable assets”.6,7,8 However, while aggregate GEP serves as a vital macroeconomic indicator, a critical scientific question emerges: does a rising GEP always signify improving ecosystem health, or might it mask internal structural vulnerabilities induced by intense human activities?

Although existing studies have yielded rich results regarding GEP accounting methods and spatiotemporal evolution analysis,9,10,11 the current literature predominantly focuses on the net increase or decrease of total value. Few studies have delved into the structural shifts of internal GEP service functions and the underlying land-use driving mechanisms. Particularly in economically developed coastal areas, the continuous growth of GEP is often accompanied by a drastic transformation of its internal structure. On one hand, urban expansion leads to the loss of regulating services (e.g., water purification and flood regulation) by encroaching upon water bodies and wetlands. On the other hand, it generates a monetized surge in cultural service value through tourism development and the improvement of human settlements.12,13 This dynamic can create a value substitution trap, where explicit market values mask implicit ecological losses, leading to an apparent growth in GEP. If left unexamined, this apparent growth can cause decision-makers to overlook a “potential ecological deficit risk”—a state where the ecosystem’s fundamental, irreversible regulating functions are structurally compromised. Consequently, it is urgent to move beyond mere total quantity analysis and quantitatively reveal the deep-seated mechanisms of GEP structural transformation driven by land use changes.

Facing the complex ecological pressures induced by land use changes, implementing differentiated spatial regulation is key to achieving refined governance of coastal zones.14,15,16 Although some studies have attempted functional zoning based on ecosystem service value (ESV) or GEP,17,18most rely on simple hierarchical classifications based on total values (i.e., high, medium, or low-value zones). However, coastal regions exhibit significant spatial heterogeneity in resource endowments and development patterns. Relying solely on total value fails to identify complex ecological-economic patterns such as “high provision-low regulation” or “high regulation-low culture.” Therefore, introducing multi-dimensional feature indicators and utilizing unsupervised learning algorithms, such as K-means clustering, to identify functional zones based on service structure characteristics is of great significance for formulating localized and tailored management strategies.

To address these critical gaps, this study selects the coastal zone of Fujian Province—China’s first National Ecological Civilization Pilot Zone—as an ideal case study to examine the human-land relationship under extreme spatial constraints. Located on the southeast coast of China (23°30′N–28°22′N, 115°50′E−120°40′E), the region features a tortuous coastline stretching 3,752 km and a subtropical marine monsoon climate that nurtures valuable ecological resources, including mangroves, coastal wetlands, and seagrass beds. However, the terrain presents a unique “mountain-sea-city” configuration where steep hills and low mountains severely constrain available construction land. Despite these limitations, the coastal zone serves as a highly concentrated socio-economic hub. With the rapid economic rise of six major coastal cities (Fuzhou, Xiamen, Quanzhou, Zhangzhou, Putian, and Ningde), high-intensity urbanization has placed immense pressure on the local environment. The intense spatial competition often leads to direct encroachment on narrow but ecologically critical aquatic and forest systems, making it an excellent case study for examining the human-land relationship under extreme spatial constraints.19,20,21 To facilitate consistent socio-economic and spatial analysis, the 37 coastal districts and counties under these cities were consolidated into 24 distinct spatial units (Figure 1).Figure 1Overview of the study area

Overview of the study area

Therefore, this study focuses on the coastal zone of Fujian Province from 2003 to 2023. Based on the construction of a localized GEP accounting framework, this research aims to: (1) calculate the spatiotemporal evolution of GEP at five key time points (2003, 2008, 2013, 2018, and 2023) and analyze the overall trends and structural changes; (2) construct a unit area GEP change intensity index to quantitatively analyze the potential “value replacement” mechanism driven by land use changes; and (3) select the densities of provisioning, regulating, and cultural services in 2023 as core indicators to identify ecological-economic functional zones using the K-means clustering algorithm. This study aims to uncover the ecological costs behind GEP evolution and provide a zoning scheme based on service structure characteristics, thereby offering a scientific basis for sustainable coastal development, refined territorial space management, and the formulation of ecological compensation policies.

In summary, the spatial distribution of GEP and its three constituent services along the Fujian coast exhibits uneven patterns, revealing significant spatial heterogeneity and evolutionary characteristics. To more objectively and comprehensively identify this spatial heterogeneity and its scale effects, this study further employed global and local spatial autocorrelation analysis methods to examine the spatial connectivity of GEP at two distinct scales: the county scale and the 1 km grid scale.

Global spatial autocorrelation analysis

Spatial autocorrelation analysis (Table 3) demonstrated a pronounced scale effect. GEP distribution exhibited strong and strengthening positive spatial correlation at the 1 km grid scale (Moran’s I peaking at 0.717 in 2023), whereas county-level patterns remained weaker and unstable (0.102–0.294). However, analyzing the three constituent services at the county scale revealed critical spatial divergence. Regulating services exhibited strong and continuously enhancing clustering (Moran’s I rising from 0.278 to 0.627), indicating the distinct formation of contiguous ecological barriers. In contrast, provisioning services showed weak and fluctuating clustering, while cultural services presented a completely random distribution (Moran’s I near 0). Local spatial autocorrelation analysis

Consistent with the global analysis, county-scale LISA maps (Figure 3) exhibited mostly insignificant or transient clustering, reaffirming that aggregate administrative units mask true spatial patterns. In contrast, the 1 km grid scale revealed a pronounced “cold spot-hot spot-cold spot” segmented pattern along the coast. Spatiotemporally, “low-low” cold spots expanded across the central-southern regions (e.g., Zhangpu, Putian) between 2008 and 2013, driven by rapid urbanization and industrialization. Conversely, “high-high” hot spots emerged and stabilized around the Xiamen and Fuzhou metropolitan circles from 2013 to 2023, which spatially coupled with the explosive growth of cultural service premiums. Coupling analysis of GEP and land use

Spatiotemporal characteristics of land use change

Observing the land use distribution map of the Fujian coastal zone from 2003 to 2023 (Figure 4), it can be found that the land use changes show significant regional heterogeneity. The land use types in the study area are mainly forest land and cropland land, with forest land widely distributed in the hilly and mountainous areas, and cropland mainly concentrated in the coastal plains and river valleys. The urban land expansion shows a spreading pattern from point to point, highly concentrated in the core cities such as Fuzhou, Xiamen, and Quanzhou as well as their neighboring districts and counties. Meanwhile, the loss of cropland mainly occurs in the agricultural areas of coastal plains and river valleys, and its spatial distribution is highly coincident with the urban expansion areas. In order to explore the inner law of land use change, this study adopts the transfer matrix method to quantitatively analyze the land use conversion process from 2003 to 2023 (Table 4), and draws the land use conversion Sankey diagram (Figure 5) to visually present the main conversion paths and flow directions. Driven by rapid urbanization, urban land surged by 75.25% (from 1,814.41 to 3,179.77 km2). Specifically, cropland and water bodies constituted 81.2% (1,108.30 km2) and 13.9% (189.99 km2) of the newly expanded urban footprint respectively, highlighting an intense spatial squeeze on foundational ecological assets.

Consequently, cropland and water bodies suffered the most severe net losses. Water bodies shrank drastically from 936.21 to 643.70 km2, driven by both urban encroachment and tidal flat reclamation. Meanwhile, forest land—the largest spatial component—maintained a dynamic large-scale equilibrium with cropland through frequent mutual conversions. For instance, while 1,241.71 km2 of forest was cleared for agriculture, 982.70 km2 of cropland successfully reverted to forest, demonstrating the localized effectiveness of the grain-for-green program. In summary, the evolution of the study area’s land use spatial pattern primarily manifested as urban land encroaching upon farmland and water bodies, alongside mutual conversion between farmland and forest land. This reflects the combined impact of rapid urbanization and regional ecological conservation policies on the land use pattern.

Analysis of GEP contribution by land use type

Through Zonal Statistics, we quantified the specific GEP contributions of diverse land-use types (Table 5). As shown in Table 5, forests and water bodies functioned as the absolute ecological pillars. Except for the impact of abnormal weather in 2003, Forest ecosystems consistently provided the largest GEP share (reaching 43.68% by 2023) with stable growth. Conversely, while Water bodies ranked second, their proportional contribution suffered a sharp and continuous decline, plunging from 43.73% in 2003 to 24.31% in 2023. Meanwhile, driven by explosive cultural service premiums and continuous spatial expansion, the GEP contribution of urban land more than quadrupled, with its share expanding from 4.65% to 10.38%. As shown in Table 6, the transition paths to urban land were the most intense, exposing the dual driving mechanisms of rapid urbanization.

First, urban expansion drives a severe physical depletion of high-value ecosystems. Specifically, the conversion from Water bodies to urban land exhibited an extreme negative change intensity, causing a net GEP loss of 144.79 million CNY/km2. This quantitatively proves that the ecological regulating functions of aquatic ecosystems are virtually irreplaceable by subsequent urban development.

Second, urban expansion triggers a deceptive “value substitution trap.” Notably, the transition paths from Cropland and Forest to urban land achieved a seemingly counter-intuitive net growth in unit area GEP (+20.65 million CNY/km2 and +13.14 million CNY/km,2 respectively). This does not imply ecological improvement; rather, it reveals a profound structural driving mechanism: rapid urbanization drastically inflated monetized cultural service premiums (e.g., real estate and coastal tourism). Under the aggregate accounting framework, this massive cultural increment mathematically masked the concurrent loss of foundational provisioning and regulating capacities. This matrix directly quantifies how land-use transitions drive GEP evolution, highlighting that policymakers must scrutinize structural trade-offs rather than relying solely on total net growth.

In summary, the unit area GEP change intensity matrix quantitatively reveals the impact of different land use transition processes on regional GEP. It not only quantifies the cost of encroaching on key ecosystems such as water bodies but also uncovers the complex value substitution mechanism behind urban expansion and its potential impact on GEP changes, while validating the feasibility of ecological restoration projects.

Spatial distribution patterns of functional zones

Spatially (Figure 8), the ecological economic functional zones in the Fujian coastal zone exhibit significant north-south differentiation characteristics. The northern coast (predominantly the Ningde region) is extensively clustered with the Zone IV Regulation Conservation Zone. Conversely, the southern coast—centered on the highly urbanized “Golden Triangle” (Xiamen-Zhangzhou-Quanzhou)—presents a complex and fragmented spatial mosaic. Notably, Dongshan County (Zone I) emerges as an isolated high-value ecological-economic highland within this southern matrix, demonstrating the spatial feasibility of optimal synergistic development.

Discussion

Spatiotemporal evolution characteristics and structural transformation risks of GEP

From 2003 to 2023, the GEP of the Fujian coastal zone showed a significant growth trend; however, profound structural transformation and crisis are concealed behind this quantitative growth. The growth of GEP was not driven by the enhancement of the foundational functions of the ecosystem (i.e., regulating services) but rather by the stable growth of provisioning service value and the explosive growth of cultural service value.

Specifically, the value of provisioning services increases steadily from 155,574.49 million CNY in 2003 to 290,148.32 million CNY in 2023, nearly doubling. Its proportion remained relatively stable across different years, always at around 25%. In contrast, the value of cultural services increased from 34,214.09 million CNY in 2003 to 322,536.78 million CNY in 2023, with an annual average growth rate as high as 11.87%, an increase of nearly 10-fold. Its proportion rose from 5.96% in 2003 to 29.54% in 2023. A review of statistical yearbooks reveals that the growth trends of provisioning and cultural services are consistent with the rapid development of the primary and tertiary industries in the region over the same period.

In sharp contrast, the value of regulating services, traditionally the mainstay of GEP, declines sharply from 66.95% in 2003 to 43.89% in 2023 (Table 2). This structural shrinkage is driven by two main factors. First is the “denominator effect” caused by the explosive growth of cultural services, which diluted the relative share of regulating services. Second, and more critically, is the absolute physical loss of foundational ecological spaces. Although the Mann-Kendall test showed no significant monotonic change in the total value of regulating services over the 20 years (p = 1.000), this stems mainly from an unusually low value of some regulating services due to an extreme drought in 2003. Excluding this outlier and considering the negative trend exhibited by key services such as climate regulation and water purification, it is highly likely that the overall regulation service is on a downward trend. This decline is directly linked to the massive encroachment on aquatic ecosystems; as shown in our land-use analysis, water bodies shrank from 936.21 km2 to 643.70 km2 due to coastal reclamation and urban expansion. This structural shift suggests that regional development is overly reliant on marketable services while fundamentally compromising the life-supporting functions of ecosystems. This finding coincides with the phenomenon of structural transformation of ecosystem service values observed by some scholars in other rapidly urbanizing coastal regions.22,23

Spatially, GEP values generally exhibited strong positive spatial correlation (Moran’s I > 0.61) and a clustered distribution at the fine scale of the 1 km grid. LISA analysis further revealed a “cold spot-hot spot-cold spot” spatial clustering pattern. The “low-low” cold spot areas were concentrated in the central-southern region, and their range expanded between 2008 and 2013, coinciding with the period of accelerated urbanization and industrialization. The “high-high” hot spot areas were concentrated in the Xiamen and Fuzhou metropolitan circles and their peripheries (e.g., Shishi, Dongshan). The clustering intensity strengthened continuously after 2013, which was spatially coupled with the explosive growth of cultural service value during this period. Meanwhile, the northern cold spot area (Ningde City) shrank rapidly after overcoming the impact of abnormal weather in 2003, demonstrating good ecological resilience. Simultaneously, at the county scale, the strong and increasingly enhanced positive spatial correlation of regulating services (Moran’s I increased from 0.278 to 0.627) revealed that ecosystems providing core regulating functions tended to become spatially concentrated among counties, mainly within the scope of Ningde City in the north, which is related to the abundant local forest resources. Conversely, provisioning services showed weak spatial clustering among counties, while cultural services exhibited spatial randomness. This heterogeneity among service types suggests that differentiated spatial management strategies should be adopted for different service types in regional policy formulation.

Driving mechanism of land use change on GEP

Land use change is one of the direct factors driving GEP evolution. By establishing a land use transfer matrix and a unit area GEP change intensity matrix, this paper further empirically verified the complex and diverse drivers of land use change. This provides a fundamental mechanistic explanation for the spatiotemporal evolution of GEP and the formation of functional zones observed in (spatiotemporal evolution characteristics and structural transformation risks of GEP) and (spatial pattern and driving factors of ecological economic functional zones). Furthermore, it is essential to trace the macro-socioeconomic drivers behind these intense land-use shifts. The massive expansion of urban land and the shrinking of water bodies were profoundly driven by regional development strategies—specifically the construction of the “economic zone of the western straits” and rapid port industrialization, which accelerated population influx and infrastructural demands in the Fujian coastal zone.

Driven by these macro-factors, urban land expansion became the most significant characteristic of land use change during the study period. This is basically consistent with the findings of other scholars regarding the impact of urban land expansion on ecosystem service value (ESV) or GEP,24,25 and its impact on GEP acts as a “double-edged sword.”

On one hand, the expansion of urban construction land directly encroached upon high-value cropland and water body ecosystems. In particular, the conversion path from water bodies to urban land resulted in a GEP change of −144.79 million CNY/km.2 This quantifies the huge ecological damage caused by the destruction of important aquatic ecosystems such as wetlands and tidal flats. Comparing our findings with existing literature reveals that this physical depletion represents a universal challenge across China’s coastal gradient. For instance, in the northern Yellow River Delta, the expansion of artificial aquaculture ponds caused the shrinkage of natural tidal wetlands, weakening the regulating function of water bodies,26 in the central Yangtze River Delta urban agglomeration, studies have similarly confirmed that rapid urbanization and high-intensity land development were the main causes of the continuous decline in ESV between 2006 and 2020. Moreover, high-value areas (such as woodlands) are highly sensitive to land development; once encroached upon, the value loss is often irreversible. Similar to the Yangtze River Delta, for the Fujian coastal zone, the value of regulating services lost due to the encroachment on aquatic ecosystems far exceeds the cultural services added by urban construction. This provides direct data support for delineating coastal ecological red lines and implementing strict controls.

On the other hand, this study identified a significant value substitution trap. In the conversion path from cropland land and forest land to urban land, GEP still shows a net increase (+20.65 million CNY/km2 and +13.14 million CNY/km,2 respectively). This does not imply an improved ecological environment; rather, the urbanization process generated immense monetized cultural premiums (e.g., coastal tourism and real estate), which numerically offset the physical loss of regulating and provisioning capacities. This dynamic mirrors the structural trade-offs observed in other highly developed estuaries. For example, the ecological evolution of the Yellow River Delta similarly demonstrated that while aquaculture inflated provisioning values, it implicitly degraded original wetland functions. Moreover, we must critically acknowledge that this surge in cultural service value comes with unmeasured negative environmental externalities. Dense tourist activities inevitably increase carbon footprints, coastal water pollution, and habitat disturbance for marine biodiversity. Currently, the standard GEP accounting framework does not deduct these “ecological costs.” This limitation exacerbates the value substitution trap, as the calculated cultural value may be an overestimation that masks simultaneous environmental degradation. This phenomenon reminds us that when interpreting the ecological impacts of urbanization, it is important not to focus only on GEP indicators, but also to look at the depletion of resources and the loss of basic ecological functions caused by internal structural changes.

At the same time, the study validated the high payback potential of ecological restoration pathways. The most effective way to enhance regional GEP is to convert wasteland into water bodies or forests (Table 6). Forests and water bodies are major contributors to GEP in coastal Fujian, and implementing land management measures that prioritize these key natural assets will genuinely amplify ecological benefits without falling into the value substitution trap.

Policy implications and management insights

Based on our spatiotemporal analysis and the identified value substitution trap, simply restricting land development is insufficient to resolve the conflict between regional economic growth and ecological protection. To effectively internalize ecological externalities, it is essential to build a comprehensive policy framework that integrates scientific accounting, institutional synergy, and market-based transformation, tailored to the specific functional zones of the Fujian coastal area.

The prerequisite for sustainable ecological management lies in establishing a rigorous accounting system to prevent the illusion of “apparent growth.” For cultural service-led zones (zone II, e.g., Xiamen), policymakers must recognize that the monetized surge in cultural GEP does not negate the associated environmental externalities, such as the carbon footprints of tourism. Rather than conflating these metrics, regional assessments must introduce an “ecological footprint” (EF) cross-reference mechanism alongside GEP. This dual-ledger approach ensures that the monetized surge in cultural services does not overshadow the underlying environmental costs unique to high-intensity coastal development. Concurrently, for ecologically Imbalanced zones (zone III, e.g., Shishi) undergoing high-intensity industrial development, natural resource departments must mandate a “GEP impact assessment.” Developers must quantitatively restore or financially compensate for the specific regulating services they encroach upon, ensuring that physical ecological assets are protected at the source.

To address the spatial mismatch of ecological benefits, institutional innovations are urgently needed to facilitate cross-regional cooperation and equitable cost-sharing. Highly urbanized areas like zone II should leverage their fiscal advantages to establish a “Bay area ecological compensation fund.” Using the unit-area regulating GEP deficit calculated in this study as a pricing baseline, these cities can purchase regulating service quotas (e.g., water purification) from upstream comprehensive potential zones (zone V), thereby balancing regional environmental carrying capacities through horizontal fiscal transfer payments. Furthermore, local organizational departments must optimize performance evaluations by enforcing a “GDP + GEP dual-target appraisal system.” Setting a rigid minimum threshold for the proportion of regulating services ensures that regulation conservation zones (zone IV, e.g., Ningde) are politically and financially supported to maintain regional ecological security without sacrificing their development rights.

Ultimately, translating conserved ecological assets into sustainable economic returns requires localized market mechanisms customized for different resource endowments. For the high-value composite zone (zone I, e.g., Dongshan), local governments should pioneer a GEP-backed green credit system, allowing enterprises to use certified “abalone farming—seaweed carbon sink” assets as collateral for green financing. Similarly, the ecological barriers in zone IV should operationalize “blue carbon tickets” for coastal mangroves and salt marshes, linking these tickets to the national carbon emission trading market to provide a sustained financial return. Finally, to overcome low-level equilibrium in zone V, local governments should establish “two mountains” resource aggregation platforms (Liangshan Bank). Functioning as centralized hubs for ecological resource collection, enhancement, and trading, these platforms can package fragmented rural ecological assets into large-scale, standardized modern agriculture and eco-tourism projects, successfully attracting social capital while strictly guarding ecological bottom lines.

Limitations of the study

While this study has achieved certain research results, there are still some limitations in some aspects due to the limitations in data acquisition, accounting methodology and depth of analysis. First, there is inherent uncertainty in the assessment of non-market ecological values. Because GEP accounting relies heavily on substitute market methods (e.g., using industrial oxygen prices or reservoir construction costs as proxy parameters), the selection of these socioeconomic parameters can influence the absolute GEP values. To enhance the robustness of the accounting framework, future research should introduce one-at-a-time (OAT) sensitivity analyses—such as systematically adjusting key price parameters by ±50% to calculate the coefficient of sensitivity (CS). This standard parameter perturbation approach will help quantitatively evaluate whether the GEP estimation is elastic or inelastic to specific parameter uncertainties. Second, in terms of driving mechanisms, this study mainly focuses on the impact of land use, and future studies should incorporate more natural and socio-economic drivers, such as temperature, rainfall, national policies, industrial structure, population density, and GDP per capita, etc., and conduct more in-depth attribution analyses using advanced methods such as geo-detectors and regression models. Third, the current analyses are limited to retrospective features of patio-temporal evolution. Future work should endeavor to project GEP dynamics under different future climate change scenarios. Finally, given the high complexity of coastal zone ecosystems, future research should endeavor to combine dynamic policy scenarios with disaster risk assessment, expand interdisciplinary approaches, and refine methods for identifying ecological function zones, so as to provide more systematic decision support for the sustainable development of Fujian’s coastal zone.

Method details

GEP accounting framework

Scientifically and reasonably constructing a regional GEP accounting system is the primary task for assessing regional GEP.27 Based on the Technical Guidelines for Gross Ecosystem Product (GEP) Accounting in Fujian Province (Trial) and existing GEP accounting research, this study constructed a GEP accounting system suitable for the coastal zone of Fujian Province (Table S1). This system was developed by closely integrating the unique ecosystem functions and socio-economic development status of the region, while fully considering data availability and operational feasibility. The framework covers three core functional categories provided by ecosystems for human well-being: provisioning services, regulating services, and cultural services.28,29

In this study, the GEP was calculated as the sum of the values of provisioning, regulating, and cultural services. The core calculation formula is as follows:(Equation 1)GEP=EPS+ERS+ECS

Where: GEP represents the gross ecosystem product (Million CNY); EPS represents the value of Ecosystem Provisioning Services; ERS represents the value of Ecosystem Regulating Services; and ECS represents the value of Ecosystem Cultural Services.

Regarding provisioning services, the Fujian coastal zone possesses abundant resources in agriculture, forestry, animal husbandry, and fisheries. Meanwhile, water resources serve as the fundamental guarantee for regional sustainable development. Therefore, this study selected “agriculture, forestry, animal husbandry, and fishery products” and “water resource products” as the core secondary accounting indicators to reflect the material product value directly provided by the ecosystem.

Regarding regulating services, given the complex terrain and diverse ecosystem types in the study area, which face a series of ecological issues such as soil erosion, flood disasters, and water pollution, this study selected 10 secondary indicators: “soil conservation,” “flood regulation,” “water conservation,” “water purification,” “climate regulation,” “carbon sequestration,” “atmospheric purification,” “air quality maintenance,” “coastal protection,” and “biodiversity conservation.” This selection aims to comprehensively and systematically reflect the critical role of the Fujian coastal ecosystem in maintaining environmental stability, mitigating natural disasters, and ensuring ecological security.

Regarding cultural services, the Fujian coastal zone boasts unique natural scenery and rich ecological resources. The cultural service function refers to the process by which ecosystems provide humans with leisure, entertainment, eco-tourism, cultural experiences, and spiritual enjoyment through their unique natural landscapes, biodiversity, and cultural heritage.30 Therefore, this study selected “tourism and sightseeing” as the core secondary indicator for cultural services.

The detailed GEP accounting system and parameters are presented in Table S1 in the Supplemental Information.

GEP accounting methods

This study adopts the functional value approach as the main method for GEP accounting.31 The core premise of the approach is to select appropriate valuation techniques to monetize the value of different services provided by ecosystems. Specifically, in order to assess the sub-indicators in the accounting system, a combination of valuation techniques such as the market value approach, the alternative cost approach, the shadow engineering approach, and the treatment cost approach are utilized. This integrated approach aims to quantify the economic value of each ecosystem service in a scientifically sound manner.

Provisioning service value

In this study, the provisioning services of the Fujian coastal ecosystem were categorized into five major types: agricultural, forestry, animal husbandry, and fishery products, as well as water resources. The actual yield of each product was used as the indicator for physical supply, and the market value method was applied to calculate the economic value. The specific calculation formula is as follow: (Equation 2)VS=∑i=15(MSi×Pi)

Where: VS represents the total value of provisioning services provided by the Fujian coastal ecosystem; MSi denotes the annual yield of the i-th product type (agricultural, forestry, animal husbandry, fishery products, and water resources) (t/a); Pi represents the average unit price of the i-th product type (CNY/t).

Soil conservation value

This study assessed the physical amount of soil conservation by calculating the difference between potential soil erosion and actual soil erosion. The Revised Universal Soil Loss Equation (RUSLE),32which is widely applied, was used to estimate soil erosion. Subsequently, the replacement cost method was employed to calculate its economic value. The specific formulas are as follows: (Equation 3)VSW=λ×Aρ×PRD(Equation 4)Ac=SRP−SRA=Rz×Kz×Lz×Sz−Rz×Kz×Lz×Sz×Cz×Pz

Where: Vsw is the value of soil conservation services; λ is the sediment deposition coefficient, set at 24%33; Ac is the amount of soil conservation; ρ is the soil bulk density, taken as 1.297 (t/m3) according to the Technical Guidelines for Gross Ecosystem Product (GEP) Accounting; PRD is the cost of reservoir dredging; SRP and SRA represent potential soil erosion and actual soil erosion, respectively; Rz, Kz, Lz, Sz, Cz and Pz represent the rainfall erosivity factor, soil erodibility factor, slope length factor, slope steepness factor, vegetation cover factor, and support practice factor, respectively.

Flood regulation value

Reservoirs, wetlands, and lakes within the Fujian coastal ecosystem all possess flood regulation functions.34 However, given constraints regarding data availability and estimation methods, this study only assessed the flood regulation function of large and medium-sized reservoirs and wetlands, employing the shadow engineering method to calculate their economic value. The specific formulas are as follows: (Equation 5)Vfm=Pwe×(Mfs+Mcc)(Equation 6)Mfs=0.35×Ct(Equation 7)Mcc=2.47×106×S+1×S×106

Where: Vfm is the value of flood regulation; Pwe is the construction cost per unit of reservoir capacity (CNY/m3), taken as 30.79 CNY/m3 based on the Fujian GEP pilot accounting report; Mfs is the flood control storage capacity of reservoirs (m3); Ct is the total storage capacity of reservoirs (m3); Mcc is the flood regulation capacity of marshes/wetlands (104 m3);S is the wetland area (m2).

Water conservation value

This study adopted the water balance method to calculate the physical quantity of water conservation.35The shadow engineering method was then employed to estimate its economic value, using the construction cost per unit of reservoir capacity as the price parameter. The specific formulas are as follows: (Equation 8)Qwr=∑i=1nAi×(Pi−Ri−ETi)×10−3(Equation 9)Vwr=Qwr×PweWhere: Vwr is the total value of water conservation services; Qwr is the total physical quantity of water conservation (m3);Ai is the area of the i-th land use type (m2); Pi, Ri and ETi represent the annual precipitation, annual surface runoff, and annual actual evapotranspiration of the i-th land use type (mm), respectively; Pwe is the construction cost per unit of reservoir capacity (CNY/m3).

Water purification value

This study primarily assessed the purification capacity of water bodies for Total Nitrogen (TN), Total Phosphorus (TP), and Chemical Oxygen Demand (COD). The replacement cost method (specifically, the treatment cost method) was employed to calculate the economic value. The specific formulas are as follows: (Equation 10)VWP=MP×PP+MN×PN+MCOD×PCOD(Equation 11)Mi=A×QiWhere: VWP is the value of water purification services; MP、MN and MCOD represent the functional purification amounts of the aquatic ecosystem for Total Phosphorus, Total Nitrogen, and COD, respectively; A is the wetland area; Qi is the purification capacity of the wetland for the i-th water pollutant; PP、PN and PCOD represent the treatment costs per unit volume for Phosphorus, Nitrogen, and COD, respectively.

Climate regulation value

Climate regulation value refers to the function of ecosystems in improving local climate conditions by absorbing heat through vegetation transpiration and water body evaporation.36 This study adopted the replacement cost method for assessment. The core concept equates the natural cooling effect provided by ecosystems to the electrical energy value consumed by artificial climate regulation measures (i.e., air conditioning). The specific formulas are as follows: (Equation 12)Vtt=Ett×Pe(Equation 13)Ett=Ept+Ewe(Equation 14)Ept=∑i3EPPi×Si×D×1063600×r(Equation 15)Ewe=Ew×q×1033600×rWhere: Vtt is the value of ecosystem climate regulation services; Ett represents the total energy consumed by transpiration and evaporation of the terrestrial ecosystem (kW·h); Ept is the energy consumed by vegetation transpiration (kW·h); Ewe is the energy consumed by water body evaporation (kW·h); EPPi is the heat consumed by transpiration per unit area of the i-th ecosystem type(kJ⋅m−2⋅d−1); Si is the area of the i-th ecosystem type(km2); r is the energy efficiency ratio (EER) of air conditioners; D is the number of days air conditioning is required, calculated as the average number of days in the study area with a daily mean temperature above 26°C; Ew is the annual evaporation volume of water bodies (m3); q is the latent heat of vaporization of water, i.e., the heat required to evaporate 1g of water (J/g); i represents the ecosystem type, including forest, shrub, and grassland; Pe is the electricity price in Fujian Province.

Carbon sequestration value

Ecological carbon sequestration service refers to the function of ecosystems in absorbing and storing carbon dioxide through photosynthesis to mitigate climate change. This study employed the carbon sequestration mechanism model to estimate the physical quantity of carbon sequestration based on Net Primary Productivity (NPP) data. The market value method was then used to calculate its economic value based on the average trading price in the Fujian carbon market. The specific formulas are as follows: (Equation 16)Qtc=Qfwc+Qsc(Equation 17)Qfwc=MCO2MC×NEP(Equation 18)Qsc=A×K(Equation 19)NEP=α×NPP×MC6MC6H10O5(Equation 20)VCf=Qtc×CCWhere: Qtc is the total amount of carbon sequestration by the ecosystem (t); Qfwc is the carbon sequestration amount of forest, wetland, and grassland ecosystems (t); Qsc is the carbon sequestration amount of cropland (t); A is the cropland area (m2); MCO2MC is the coefficient for converting C to CO2 (44/12); NEP is the Net Ecosystem Productivity (t·C); K is the carbon sequestration rate of cropland, taken as 0.38 t/hm2; VCf is the value of terrestrial ecosystem carbon sequestration (CNY); CC is the carbon price (CNY/t); α is the conversion coefficient between NEP and NPP; NPP is the Net Primary Productivity (t·dry matter); MC6MC6H10O5 is the coefficient for converting dry matter to C.

Oxygen release value

The accounting of physical quantity for oxygen release services is closely related to carbon sequestration services. Based on the stoichiometric relationship of photosynthesis (the molecular weight ratio of CO2 to O2 is 44:32), the physical quantity of oxygen released is derived from the calculated amount of carbon dioxide fixed. Subsequently, the replacement cost method was employed to calculate the economic value, using the market price of industrial oxygen production as the replacement unit price. The specific formulas are as follows:(Equation 21)QO2=MO2MCO2×QCO2(Equation 22)VO2=QO2×CO2Where: VO2 is the value of oxygen release services; MO2/MCO2 is the conversion coefficient of CO2 to O2, taken as 32/44; QCO2 is the amount of carbon sequestration by the ecosystem; CO2 is the price of industrial oxygen.

Air purification value

Air purification service refers to the function of ecosystems (especially vegetation) in reducing atmospheric pollutants (such as sulfur dioxide, nitrogen oxides, and particulate matter) through processes like leaf adsorption, absorption, and under-canopy deposition, thereby improving air quality. This study adopted the replacement cost method to calculate this value, taking the treatment cost required to remove an equivalent amount of atmospheric pollutants through artificial means (e.g., industrial desulfurization, denitrification, and dust removal) as the economic value. The specific formulas are as follows: (Equation 23)Qai=∑j=1nqai×Aj(Equation 24)Vap=∑i=1nQai×CgiWhere: Vap is the value of air purification services; Qai is the purification amount of the i-th atmospheric pollutant (kg); qai is the purification amount per unit area of the i-th pollutant by the j-th ecosystem type (kg/m2); Aj is the area of the j-th ecosystem type (m2);Cgi is the treatment cost of the i-th atmospheric pollutant (CNY/kg).

Coastal protection value

Coastal protection function refers to the ability of wetland ecosystems (such as mangroves and salt marshes) to protect the coastline from erosion by attenuating waves and resisting storm surges. This study selected the area of offshore and coastal wetlands in Fujian Province as the physical quantity for coastal protection. The replacement cost method was used to calculate its economic value, using the construction and maintenance cost per unit area of seawall protection engineering as the replacement price. The specific formula is as follows: (Equation 25)Vcl=Mcl×Pcl

Where: Vcl is the value of coastal protection services; Mcl is the total coastal area protected by the ecosystem (hm2), i.e., the wetland area; Pcl is the construction and maintenance cost per unit area of seawall protection engineering (CNY/hm2).

Biodiversity conservation value

Nature reserves play a crucial role in protecting biodiversity and promoting the sustainable development of ecosystems. For marine nature reserves, the replacement cost method (i.e., seawall protection engineering construction cost) was applied to assess the value of ecosystems such as coastal salt marshes, mangroves, and coral reefs in wind protection and dike consolidation. For forest nature reserves, the Shannon-Wiener index was adopted for assessment.37 The specific formula is as follows:(Equation 26)Vbi=Mbiop×Pbiop+Mbio×PbioWhere: Vbi is the value of biodiversity conservation; Mbiop is the physical quantity of marine species conservation, i.e., the area of marine nature reserves (hm2); Pbiop is the seawall protection engineering construction cost for marine nature reserves (CNY/hm2); Mbio is the physical quantity of forest species conservation, i.e., the area of forest nature reserves (hm2); Pbio is the biodiversity conservation value per unit area of forest.

Cultural service value

Cultural service function refers to the process by which ecosystems provide humans with leisure, recreation, eco-tourism, and spiritual enjoyment. This function plays a vital role in eco-tourism and cultural industries and is a source of regional economic vitality. This study adopted the market value method to calculate this value. Referring to existing studies, 70% of the total tourism revenue of each district and county was taken as the proxy for its cultural service value.38

Time series trend analysis

In order to quantitatively characterize the temporal evolution of GEP in the coastal region of Fujian from 2003 to 2023, this study adopts the Mann-Kendall (M-K) trend test.39The M-K test, as a robust nonparametric statistical method, does not require the data to satisfy specific distributional assumptions, and is also effective in counteracting outlier disturbances. The method is effective in identifying trends in time series data. The direction of the trend is determined by the Z statistic: Z > 0 indicates an upward trend and Z < 0 indicates a downward trend. The trend significance test uses the α = 0.05 significance level, and the trend is considered statistically significant when p < 0.05. Multi-scale Spatial analysis

The spatial distribution of GEP is often accompanied by significant spatial heterogeneity; however, the characteristics of spatial patterns may vary according to the scale of analysis, i.e., there is a certain scale effect. The traditional analysis method based on administrative districts, such as county-level administrative districts, although easy to integrate with socio-economic statistics, often hides the real spatial distribution characteristics due to the artificial demarcation of administrative boundaries and irregularities in the shape and area of districts.

In order to overcome the scale effect and reveal the spatial variation pattern of GEP in coastal Fujian more comprehensively, this study adopts a multi-scale spatial analysis framework. With the help of GeoDa software, the spatial autocorrelation analysis method in the exploratory spatial data analysis (ESDA) method40 was applied to explore the spatial distribution of GEP at two different scales, namely, the county scale and the 1 km × 1 km grid. A spatial weight matrix was constructed based on the adjacency relationship using the Queen Contiguity rule.

Global Spatial autocorrelation

Global spatial autocorrelation reflects the overall spatial distribution characteristics of GEPs across the entire study area, encompassing clustering, dispersion, and random distribution. This study employs the global Moran’s I index for analysis. The Moran’s I index ranges from −1 to 1. A value greater than 0 indicates positive spatial correlation (clustering), less than 0 indicates negative spatial correlation (dispersion), and a value close to 0 indicates random spatial distribution. Statistical significance is tested using Z-values and p-values. The calculation formula is as follows:(Equation 31)Moran′sI=n∑i=1n∑j=1nwij(xi−x¯)(xj−x¯)(∑i=1n∑j=1nwij)∑i=1n(xi−x¯)2Where: n is the total number of spatial units (number of counties or grid cells); xiand xjare the GEP values of the i-th and j-th spatial units, respectively; x¯ is the average GEP value of all units; wijis the spatial weight matrix.

Local Spatial autocorrelation

Based on global spatial autocorrelation analysis, this study employs the Local Moran’s I index (LISA) to further identify local spatial clustering patterns.41,42 LISA analysis detects association patterns between each spatial unit and its neighborhood, thereby identifying statistically significant “hot spots,” “cold spots,” and spatial outliers. Visualizing these patterns through LISA cluster maps reveals four primary types of local spatial associations: high-high clusters (hot spots), low-low clusters (cold spots), high-low outliers, and non-significant areas. This study conducted LISA analyses on GEP data at both county-level and 1-kilometer grid scales to compare local spatial pattern characteristics across different scales. Coupling analysis of GEP and land use change

Land use transfer matrix

The land use transfer matrix is a classic method for revealing the spatiotemporal evolution process and direction of regional land use.43,44 This matrix quantitatively describes the area and flow direction of mutual conversions between different land use types within the study period (beginning and end), thereby clearly reflecting the evolution characteristics of land use structure.

This study applies the matrix to the Fujian coastal region, revealing the conversion pathways and spatial extent of various land use types between 2003 and 2023. Based on this analysis, it examines the driving effects of land use change on regional ecological product value. Its mathematical expression is as follows: (Equation 34)Sij=(S11S12⋯S1nS21S22⋯S2n⋮⋮⋱⋮Sn1Sn2⋯Snn)

Analysis of GEP contribution by land use type

To quantify the contribution of different land use types to the GEP and identify the land use types that play a key supporting role, an analysis of GEP contribution by land use type was conducted. Using land use raster data from 2003 to 2023 as “Zone Data” and the corresponding GEP total value raster as “Value Raster,” the “Zonal Statistics as Table” tool in ArcGIS was utilized to calculate the GEP and its proportion for each land use type across different periods in batches. This allowed for the assessment of the contribution degree of different land use types to the GEP.

Analysis of unit area GEP change intensity

Unlike traditional indicators that merely report absolute net value changes—which are inherently skewed by the vast area differences of various land-use types—this study introduced the concept of “Unit Area GEP Change Intensity.” The theoretical necessity of this index lies in its ability to mathematically filter out area-scale interference. To more objectively compare the relative intensity of different land use transfer paths on GEP impact and achieve a standardized comparison per square kilometer, a net GEP change matrix (ΔGEPij) was constructed through overlay analysis in ArcGIS, which was then divided by the corresponding elements in the land use area transfer matrix (ΔSij). This yielded the unit area GEP change intensity matrix. The specific formula is as follows:(Equation 35)Intensityij=ΔGEPijΔSij

Where: Intensityij is the unit area GEP change intensity (CNY/km2); ΔGEPij is the net change in GEP for the transfer path from land use type i to land use type j (CNY); ΔSij is the total area of this transfer path (km2).

Identification of ecological-economic functional zones

The administrative areas of districts and counties in the Fujian coastal zone vary significantly; thus, directly comparing the GEP values would result in scale effects, making it difficult to reveal their respective ecological-economic functions. To overcome this scale effect, this study calculated GEP density (Unit: CNY/km2) by dividing the GEP of each district or county from 2003 to 2023 by its administrative area. The introduction of the GEP density concept allows for comparable value output intensity across different regions. Furthermore, to investigate internal structural differences in GEP, the concepts of provisioning service value density, regulating service value density, and cultural service value density were introduced. Finally, to objectively and scientifically identify the ecological-economic development models of different regions in the Fujian coastal zone, the K-Means Clustering Algorithm was employed to construct the functional zoning system.

Indicator selection and data standardization

To ensure that the functional zoning results reflect current realities and provide future guidance, this study focused on data from 2023, which represents the latest regional ecological-economic pattern. The provisioning service value density, regulating service value density, and cultural service value density of each district (county) in 2023 were selected as the three core clustering indicators. Since the magnitudes of different service values vary significantly (e.g., cultural service value is often much higher than regulating service value), the Z Score standardization method was applied to eliminate dimensional influence. The formula is as follows:(Equation 36)zij=xij−μjσjWhere: zij is the standardized value; xij is the original density value of the j-th service for the i-th district/county; μjis the mean value of the j-th service; σjis the standard deviation of the j-th service.

K-means cluster analysis

K-Means is a classic unsupervised learning algorithm that partitions samples into K clusters through iterative calculations, minimizing intra-cluster variance and maximizing inter-cluster variance.45 This study implemented the clustering using the Scikit-learn library on the Python platform. To determine the optimal number of clusters (K), the Elbow Method, Silhouette Coefficient, and the interpretability of ecological-economic meanings were comprehensively considered. After testing, K = 5 was found to most clearly identify typical development models with significant heterogeneity. Finally, based on the numerical characteristics of the cluster centers (Centroids), the functional zones were defined and named.
