Focused ultrasound-mediated blood-brain barrier opening enhances delivery of Rg3 ginseng nanoparticles in a Parkinson's disease mouse model
Authors: Cynthia Imtiaz, Changsoo Kim, Dong-Guk Paeng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe blood–brain barrier (BBB) severely restricts the delivery of neuroprotective compounds to the brain, limiting therapeutic strategies for Parkinson's disease (PD). Ginsenoside Rg3, a bioactive component of ginseng, has demonstrated neuroprotective potential, but its efficacy is constrained by poor BBB permeability. Here, we evaluated focused ultrasound (FUS)-mediated BBB opening (FUS BBBO) to enhance delivery of Rg3-loaded nanoparticles in a rotenone-induced mouse model of PD. Localized FUS sonications were applied to induce transient BBB disruption, followed by intraperitoneal administration of FITC-labeled Rg3 nanoparticles. In vivo and Ex vivo fluorescence imaging confirmed a significant increase in brain accumulation of nanoparticles after FUS BBBO. While Rg3 nanoparticle treatment alone showed moderate increases in ATP levels and Complex I activity, FUS alone produced comparable trends with slightly higher recovery. The combination treatment with FUS-mediated BBBO demonstrated higher mean values (ATP: 0.27 ± 0.06; Complex I: 1.98 ± 0.41) compared to the PD group, along with improvements in motor performance. These findings suggest that FUS-BBBO may enhance the delivery of Rg3 nanoparticles to the brain and support mitochondrial function. Overall, the combination approach showed a trend toward improved outcomes; however, further studies are required to confirm these effects and establish therapeutic potential for solid statistical analysis.Keywords: Focused ultrasound, Blood–brain barrier disruption, Parkinson's disease, Rg3 ginsenoside nanoparticles, Mitochondrial function

Introduction

Parkinson's disease (PD) is the 2nd most progressive and prevalent neurodegenerative disease following Alzheimer's disease. It imposes a growing socioeconomic burden as populations age. (Poewe et al., 2017). Global estimates suggest that 5 to over 35 individuals per 100,000 are newly diagnosed with PD each year, with incidence rising sharply after the age of 50, reflecting escalating healthcare expenditures and long-term supportive care requirements. Clinically, PD is recognized as a progressive movement disorder characterized by bradykinesia, resting tremors, and rigidity, often accompanied by cognitive decline (Simon et al., 2020). At the pathological level, PD is marked by degeneration of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra, aggregation of α-synuclein into Lewy bodies, and widespread mitochondrial dysfunction (Golpich et al., 2017). Inhibition of Complex I within the electron transport chain leads to ATP depletion, elevated oxidative stress, and neuronal vulnerability. Because of its central role in PD pathogenesis, mitochondrial dysfunction has emerged as a critical therapeutic target, yet current treatment options primarily offer symptomatic relief without addressing the underlying bioenergetic failure(Dickson, 2012).

Korean red ginseng (Panax ginseng), a traditional medicinal herb, has long been studied for its neurotrophic and neuroprotective properties. Preclinical evidence has demonstrated that ginseng extracts protect against neurotoxicity in PD models. For example, oral administration of G115, a standardized ginseng extract, preserved tyrosine hydroxylase activity in the substantia nigra of rodent models exposed to PD toxins (Van Kampen et al., 2003). Other investigations have reported that ginseng attenuates dopaminergic neuronal loss, reduces microglial activation, and suppresses inflammatory signaling through nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) and caspase-3 pathways following MPTP intoxication (M. Y. Lee and Kim, 2024; Zaafan et al., 2019). Additional studies showed that ginseng extracts decrease α-synuclein aggregation, reduce oxidative stress, and protect against apoptosis both in vitro and in vivo (Choi et al., 2018; M. Jiang et al., 2025; Ratan et al., 2021; Xu et al., 2024). These findings highlight ginseng's multitarget neuroprotective effects, particularly in modulating oxidative stress and inflammatory pathways. However, much of the existing research has focused on ginsenosides such as Re and Rd or on crude ginseng extracts. A growing body of work now suggests that ginsenoside Rg3, a minor yet potent bioactive component of ginseng, may have unique therapeutic potential. Rg3 has been shown to protect against mitochondrial dysfunction in cellular and Drosophila PD models (Huang et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2015) but systematic evaluation in mammalian models remains limited. Importantly, despite its pharmacological promise, Rg3 faces major translational hurdle's, its inadequate ability to cross the blood–brain barrier (BBB).

The BBB is one of the greatest obstacles to the development of neuroprotective therapies. Formed by tight junctions between endothelial cells, the BBB restricts the passage of most small molecules and nearly all macromolecules, thereby preventing therapeutic concentrations of many promising agents from reaching brain parenchyma (Di Wu et al., 2023). While ginsenosides have shown efficacy in vitro, there is little evidence that they achieve sufficient brain penetration to exert mitochondrial protection in vivo. This pharmacokinetic barrier underscores the need for strategies that can safely and effectively enhance central delivery of Rg3(Huang et al., 2021).

To address these limitations, our study explored the use of ginseng nanoparticles in conjunction with focused ultrasound (FUS) to enhance a targeted and non-invasive delivery; FUS has emerged as a potential technique to temporarily disrupt the BBB and provide targeted delivery in combination with microbubbles, which oscillates in the presence varying acoustic pressure exerting mechanical forces on endothelial cells to transiently open the barrier(Burgess and Hynynen, 2014; Fan et al., 2014). This approach aims to enhance the permeability and bioavailability of ginseng, assisting its crossing to the brain(Imtiaz et al., 2023; Niazi, 2023). This technique allows spatially targeted delivery of therapeutic agents into specific brain regions without systemic toxicity. Preclinical and clinical studies have validated the safety and efficacy of FUS in enhancing delivery of nanoparticles, antibodies, and gene therapy vectors (Durham et al., 2024; Kofoed and Aubert, 2024; Lai et al., 2025; H. R. Li et al., 2024).

When paired with nanoparticle formulations, FUS-mediated BBB opening (FUS BBBO) offers several advantages. Nanoparticles can improve drug stability, control release kinetics, and optimize physicochemical properties such as size and surface charge for efficient transport. For ginsenosides like Rg3, encapsulation into nanoparticles may overcome solubility and stability issues, while FUS ensures their localized delivery into affected brain regions (Yao and Zhu, 2025; Zhu et al., 2021). Despite the promise of this approach, no prior study has systematically evaluated whether FUS can enhance Rg3 nanoparticle delivery to the brain and whether this strategy can restore mitochondrial function in a PD model.

Based on these gaps, we hypothesized that FUS BBBO could significantly enhance delivery of Rg3-loaded ginseng nanoparticles to target brain tissue, thereby restoring mitochondrial function and improving behavioral outcomes in PD. Unlike previous studies that primarily emphasized inflammatory modulation, our approach directly targets mitochondrial bioenergetics–a central driver of PD pathology (Van Kampen et al., 2003). To test this hypothesis, we employed a rotenone-induced PD mouse model, which recapitulates dopaminergic neurodegeneration and mitochondrial dysfunction. Mice were treated with Rg3 nanoparticles with or without FUS-BBBO, and therapeutic efficacy was evaluated through behavioral testing, measurement of ATP production and Complex I activity, and histological analyses of nanoparticle delivery.

This work addresses three major limitations in current PD research and therapy. First, while ginseng has demonstrated neuroprotective properties, the role of Rg3 in mammalian models remains poorly explored. Second, the limited BBB permeability of ginsenosides has hindered their translational potential. Third, few studies have directly targeted mitochondrial bioenergetics as a primary therapeutic endpoint in PD. In summary, this study investigates whether FUS-enhanced delivery of Rg3-loaded ginseng nanoparticles can improve mitochondrial function and motor performance in a PD mouse model. The findings aim to provide preliminary insights into the potential of combining traditional medicine with nanotechnology and ultrasound-based approaches as a therapeutic strategy.

Material and methods

Animal use and study design

This study utilized a cohort of 40 male C57BL/6 mice aged 7 weeks, each with an average weight ranging from 20 to 22 g. The animals were purchased from Kronex Labs, Seoul, South Korea. Mice were randomly assigned to experimental groups. Behavioral and biochemical analyses were performed under consistent experimental conditions. Investigators were not blinded during data collection. Sample sizes were determined based on preliminary exploratory experiments. A subset of twenty mice was employed to optimize the parameters for BBB opening and to confirm drug delivery effectiveness. The remaining 16 mice were designated for the induction of the PD model and 4 were control mice.

All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with institutional guidelines and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at Jeju National University (Ethical approval No.: 2024–0003), and all experimental procedures adhered to the relevant ethical standards in animal testing.

The mice were housed in a controlled environment, with an ambient temperature maintained at 22 ± 2 °C, relative humidity of 60–70%, a 12-h light-dark cycle, and free access to standard animal feed.

PD inductions and verification

To establish a PD model, we employed Rotenone (PESTANAL®, analytical standard, cat. no. 45656; Supelco, Sigma-Aldrich Korea, Republic of Korea), an environmental pesticide that induces PD-like symptoms through the direct inhibition of mitochondrial complex I with dosage of 5 mg/kg for a 22-25 g mouse (X. W. Jiang et al., 2017). Rotenone was dissolved in 2% DMSO in normal saline to achieve a final concentration of 1 mg/ml. Each animal received a daily dose of 0.1 mg administered in a total volume of 0.15 ml via the intraperitoneal route for 28 days (four weeks)(L. Huang et al., 2022). The mice were observed for two hours post-injection to monitor acute toxicity and general health. In contrast, the control mice received no treatment. PD model development and assessment were conducted PD model development was assessed by tracking the progressive emergence of motor deficits using weekly behavioral tests throughout the treatment period. To evaluate PD-associated neuroinflammatory responses, inflammatory chemokine profiling was performed at the time of the final rotenone injection.

Behavioral analysis

The animals underwent training one week prior to the behavioral testing, which was conducted between 9:00 AM and 2:00 PM in a dimly lit, light and noise-controlled room. Mice were acclimatized by remaining undisturbed in the testing room for 1 h prior to the initiation of the tests. Their behavior was recorded using a video camera positioned above the apparatus, and all recordings were analyzed. To eliminate potential odor cues, the apparatus was thoroughly cleaned with a 10% ethanol solution between the testing of individual animals.

Pole test

The pole test was employed to assess motor coordination impairment and bradykinesia in mice models of PD. A vertical wooden pole, measuring 50 cm in height and 1 cm in diameter with a rough surface, was positioned within the home cage that contained fresh bedding. Mice were positioned at the apex of the pole, and their descent was recorded on video. The software measured the time taken for the mice to reach the floor of the cage following their placement on the pole, while also monitoring their methods of descent, which could be categorized as Walk, Slide, Walk/Slide, or Fall. In instances where the mice slipped or fell due to an inability to maintain their grip on the pole, a score of 15 s was assigned(Matsuura et al., 1997).

Buried pellet test

This test was conducted to assess the olfactory capabilities of mice. Before the experiment, the mice were deprived of food but had free access to water for 24 h. They were placed in a clean test cage for 10 min to habituate before commencement of the test. Subsequently, the mice were relocated to the same test cage, which contained 3 cm of bedding with a food pellet weighing approximately 400 mg buried 1 cm beneath the surface in a corner of the cage. The mice were positioned at a consistent distance from the hidden food and were given 300 s to locate the buried pellet. The time it took for the mice to find the food pellet was recorded as latency. In cases where a mouse failed to find the buried pellet within 300 s, the test was halted, and its latency score was recorded as 300 s. The latency for locating the food pellet was defined as the interval from the moment the mouse was placed in the cage until it made contact with the food pellet with its forepaws or nose and began to eat(X. Y. Liu et al., 2023).

Open field

Open field analysis was performed to assess spontaneous locomotor activity. Mice were placed individually in the center of a large open-field arena and allowed to freely explore the area for 5 min while behavior was recorded by a video camera positioned 1 m above the arena. The arena was cleaned with 75% ethanol followed by water between trials to eliminate odor cues. Locomotor activity, including total lateral distance traveled, was quantified using custom-written MATLAB scripts, and movement patterns were visualized using heat map analysis.

Inflammatory markers analysis

Serum samples from the control n = 4 and PD mouse models from n = 5 out of random 16 mice were pooled, and measurements represent technical duplicates. The data are presented as exploratory and no statistical analysis was performed. Following the manufacturer's guidelines, the pooled sera were analyzed using the Proteome Profiler Mouse Array Kit, Panel A (ARY006, R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA) to quantitatively assess the levels of peripheral inflammatory cytokines and chemokines. This analysis played a crucial role in verifying the induction of PD and in identifying inflammatory markers explicitly associated with the PD pathology. Membrane immunoreactivity was detected after the addition of a chemiluminescent reagent mix. Array images were captured using the Chemi-Doc imaging system (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA). The results were analyzed with ImageJ software for blot quantification and expressed as the mean change in pixel density values relative to the different groups presented in Supplementary Fig. S1.

Synthesis and characterization of the Ginseng nanoparticles

Ginseng nanoparticles (GnNPs) were synthesized utilizing a series of methods, including wet grinding, mechanical shaking, bath sonication, ultrasound probe sonication, and centrifugation (V. Lee, 2019). Korean Red Ginseng extract, rich in Rg3, was obtained from Korean Ginseng Corp (Daejeon, South Korea) presented in Supplementary Fig. S2(b).

The physicochemical characterization of the ginseng nanoparticles was conducted using various characterization techniques presented in Supplementary Fig. S3, Fig. S4 and Fig. S5.

The crystal system and phase structure of all samples were investigated using an X-ray diffractometer (Panalytical X'PERT PRO, Almelo, Netherlands). The elemental composition and surface morphology were analyzed using a Field Emission Scanning Electron Microscope (FESEM, MIRA-3 TESCON, Břeclav, Czech Republic) with an integrated EDS detector (Oxford Instruments, Oxfordshire, UK). Fourier Transform Infrared (FT-IR) spectra were recorded using an ALPHA II spectrometer over a range of 4000 to 400 cm−1. Ultraviolet-visible (UV–Vis) absorption spectroscopy was used to elucidate the optical properties of the samples, using a Lambda 25 UV–Vis spectrophotometer. Additionally, surface charge analysis was performed using Zeta potential measurements utilizing a Zeta Potential Analyzer (Zetasizer Ultra, Malvern Instruments, UK).

Acoustic characterization and stable and inertial cavitation thresholds

The ultrasound emitted from a 1 MHz single-element focused ultrasound transducer (Dongil Tech, Seoul, South Korea) was utilized to induce the stable cavitation of microbubble. This transducer features a radius of curvature of 25 mm, an inner diameter of 17 mm, an outer diameter of 21 mm, and a focal length of 22 mm. The transducer was securely mounted in a custom 3D-printed holder. A function generator (Agilent 33500B, Colorado, USA), controlled via MATLAB, transmitted signals to an RF amplifier (Ultra X-20, 40 dB gain, CA, USA) that powered the transducer. Acoustic field measurements were captured using a needle hydrophone (HNP-0400, ONDA Corporation, Sunnyvale, CA), which was connected to a preamplifier with a 40 dB gain. The sensitivity (dB re 1 V/ μPa) datasheet of the hydrophone was utilized to convert the hydrophone-acquired voltage to pressure value as presented in Supplementary Fig. S7. The hydrophone was mounted in a 3D positioner and controlled through a custom MATLAB script to automate the scanning process presented in Supplementary Fig. S6. The signals were acquired using a digital oscilloscope (Keysight Infini Vision DSOX30127, CA, USA) with the data subsequently transferred to a PC for further analysis. The focal point was localized, 1D and 2D scanning with and without skull was executed to map the beam profile while determining parameters such as focal spot size, focal depth, focal length, and full width at half maximum (FWHM) as provided in Supplementary Fig. S8 and Fig. S9.

Cavitation experiments were conducted in a controlled water-tank setup designed to reproducibly measure ultrasound-induced microbubble activity. A degassed water tank was used to minimize unwanted acoustic scattering, and a 4-mm-diameter silicone tube was positioned at the focal region of the focused ultrasound (FUS) transducer, where the full width at half maximum (FWHM) of the acoustic beam was approximately 3 mm. A commercial microbubble contrast agent (SonoVue, Bracco, Switzerland; 5% v/v) was continuously circulated through the tube at a constant flow rate of 50 μl/min using a syringe pump to maintain a stable microbubble concentration during sonication. To reduce acoustic reflections and standing waves, an acoustic absorber was placed at the bottom of the tank. A schematic of experimental setup is provided in Supplementary Fig. S10.

Acoustic emissions generated by microbubble cavitation were detected using a needle hydrophone positioned near the focal region and connected to a digital oscilloscope (Keysight InfiniVision DSOX3012, CA, USA). A 1-MHz FUS transducer was aligned with the tube using a custom 3D-printed targeting system to ensure consistent positioning of the focal beam. The acquired acoustic signals were used to quantify both stable and inertial cavitation activity. An overview of the signal-processing workflow used to calculate stable and inertial cavitation doses is shown in Supplementary Fig. S11, while representative time- and frequency-domain signals are provided in Supplementary Fig. S12. The resulting cavitation dose measurements used to determine cavitation thresholds are summarized in Supplementary Fig. S13.(Lin et al., 2017).

Focused ultrasound microbubbles assisted BBB opening

The mice head hair was shaved with depilatory cream and fixed in a stereotactic frame. A degassed ultrasound coupling gel (EcoSonic Co., Ltd., South Korea) was applied to the shaved scalp of the mice, followed by placement of a degassed water bath separated by a thin plastic membrane that allowed efficient transmission of the ultrasound beam. This setup ensured stable acoustic coupling between the focused ultrasound (FUS) transducer and the mouse head. For precise targeting, a custom 3D-printed positioning jig was designed using SolidWorks (version 2014; Dassault Systèmes, France) based on the focal length of the FUS transducer provided in Supplementary Fig. S14. The jig was mounted on a motorized three-axis positioning system to accurately align the ultrasound focus with the right parietal region of the mouse brain, as illustrated in Fig. 1. Ultrasound signals were generated using a function generator and amplified with a 40-dB power amplifier before being delivered through a 1-MHz transducer as shown in Fig. 1(a). Sonication parameters included a pulse duration of 25 ms, a pulse repetition frequency of 10 Hz, a duty cycle of 25%, and a total exposure time of 3 min. These parameters were empirically optimized in preliminary experiments to achieve consistent BBBO, with an average temperature rise of 0.65 ± 0.01 °C at the focus as presented in Supplementary Fig. S15.

Schematic illustration of the focused ultrasound (FUS)-mediated blood–brain barrier opening procedure. (a) Experimental setup showing FUS transducer, stereotactic fixation, and intravenous administration of microbubbles and ginseng nanoparticles (GnNPs). (b) Timeline of the sonication protocol. Target location within the striatum relative to bregma (AP: −1 mm, ML: +1.5 mm, DV: −3 mm).

Targeting was performed using bregma as a reference point, with coordinates set at −1.0 mm anterior–posterior, +1.5 mm mediolateral, and − 3.0 mm dorsoventral. These coordinates correspond to the dorsal striatum (caudate putamen), a brain region critically involved in motor control and prominently affected in PD.

Furthermore, the mice's tail veins were cannulated using tail vein catheters, followed by the administration of 2% Evans blue at a dosage of 4 ml/kg based on the body weight of the mice. The rapid blue coloration of the skin indicated successful cannulation [34]. Microbubbles were prepared according to the manufacturer's instructions and administered intravenously at a total volume of 150 μl using a syringe pump at a constant flow rate of 50 μl/min. An initial infusion period of 60 s was allowed prior to sonication to ensure systemic circulation and distribution. FUS sonication was then initiated while microbubble infusion continued, maintaining a stable circulating concentration throughout the exposure period. Following infusion, the catheter was flushed with 50 μl of sterile saline to ensure complete delivery, as illustrated in Fig. 1(b).

Following FUS treatment, mice were transcardially perfused with cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) removed intravascular dye. The skulls were then opened, and brains were extracted and examined for BBBO based on Evans blue extravasation for varying mechanical indices, as shown in Supplementary Fig. S16(a) and Fig. S17. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was performed to evaluate tissue integrity and red blood cell (RBC) extravasation as shown in Supplementary Fig. S16(b).

Histological confirmation of BBB opening and drug delivery quantification

Three mechanical indices (MI) values–0.35, 0.8, and 1.1 a measure of ultrasound intensity and cavitation potential were employed to assess the efficacy of BBB opening. The mice were closely monitored for 1 h to allow for complete circulation of the administered Evans blue. Subsequently, 5 mg/kg of fluorescein isothiocyanate-labeled ginseng nanoparticles were administered through the tail vein 15 min before euthanization with cold 1× phosphate-buffered saline (Sigma-Aldrich, Seoul, Korea). The brains were excised and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and then sectioned using a microtome for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining and immunofluorescence microscopy to confirm the efficacy of drug delivery.

IVIS imaging results represent data from a single animal per group (n = 1) and are presented as qualitative, representative observations. Although the experiment was independently repeated with consistent trends, quantitative statistical analysis across biological replicates was not performed.

Fluorescence microscopy was used to confirm the efficacy of drug delivery. To evaluate the early-phase accumulation of nanoparticles in the brain following FUS-induced BBB opening, in vivo imaging was performed using an IVIS Lumina III system (PerkinElmer Health Sciences, MA, USA) with FITC-labeled ginseng nanoparticles at 5, 25, 45, and 65 min post-injection. Mice were divided into control, FITC-labeled nanoparticle, and FITC-labeled nanoparticle with BBB opening groups. Fluorescence intensity was quantified as radiant efficiency (p/s/cm2/sr)/(μW/cm2), with a corresponding pseudo-color scale. Regions of interest (ROIs) were defined over the predefined FUS-targeted brain regions and applied consistently across animals.

The imaging time window was limited by the photophysical properties of the FITC fluorophore, which exhibits signal reduction over time due to photobleaching. Therefore, this experiment was designed to assess the initial delivery efficiency of nanoparticles following BBB opening rather than to establish a full pharmacokinetic profile.

Treatment of the Parkinson mice with Rg3 Ginseng nanoparticle

Following a one-week acclimatization period, the mice were randomly assigned to groups. The study was initially designed with n = 4 animals per group; however, due to mortality in PD-induced groups during the experimental period, the final analyses were performed with n = 3 per group. One control group consisting of 3 mice and one experimental group comprising 12 mice for PD induction. Following the PD group was further divided into four subgroups, which included: PD alone, PD + GnNPs, PD + FUS and PD + FUS + GnNPs.

The mouse was treated for 3 weeks, during which PD + FUS + GnNPs group were administered through the BBB opening via FUS and microbubbles twice a week, and the Rg3 ginseng nanoparticles (5 mg/kg) were simultaneously injected intraperitoneally. While the parallel PD + FUS group receives only FUS + BBBO through microbubbles and PD + Ginseng nanoparticles(5 mg/kg) only via IP injection, all three groups receive two doses per week for three weeks. Performance assessments, including the open field test and pole test, were conducted both at baseline and following the treatment period to evaluate the effects of the interventions.

Mitochondrial complex 1 and ATP levels assays

Mitochondrial and adenosine triphosphate (ATP) levels were assessed in the brain homogenate of mice across all experimental groups using an ATP assay kit (E-BC-K157-S) and a colorimetric assay kit for mitochondrial respiratory chain complex I (NADH-coenzyme Q reductase) (E-BC-K149-M). Both kits were obtained from Elab Science, Houston, TX, USA. The absorbance for ATP analysis was recorded at 636 nm employing a microplate reader.

Furthermore, the activity of mitochondrial complex I was quantified spectrophotometrically by monitoring the oxidation of NADH, indicated by the reduction in absorbance at 340 nm. Concentrations were determined by the manufacturer's specifications.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed utilizing (R version 4.2.2) and MATLAB (R2024, The MathWorks Inc., Natick, Massachusetts). Statistical analyses were carried out employing one-way ANOVA, followed by Tukey's multiple comparison test, with statistical significance established at P < 0.05. The results are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Statistical significance was denoted as follows: ****p < 0.0001, ***p < 0.001, **p < 0.01, *p < 0.05, and ns = not significant.

A schematic overview of the experimental design, including PD induction, treatment schedule, behavioral assessments, and biochemical analyses, is illustrated in Fig. 2.

Results

Rotenone-treated animals displayed an increased latency to descend on the pole compared to controls at week 4 (****p < 0.0001), suggesting impaired motor coordination and bradykinesia as shown in Fig. 3(a). Furthermore, rotenone-treated mice exhibited a significant increase in latency to locate the pellet across all weeks, notably in week 4 compared to control (****p < 0.0001), as shown in Fig. 3(b). Statistical comparisons within the control group (Week 1 vs. Week 4) showed no significant differences, confirming stable baseline motor performance throughout the study period.

The effect of systemic inflammatory response after chronic rotenone administration was evaluated by a chemokine profile of 40 inflammatory chemokines in serum samples collected after 4 weeks of treatment. Fig. 3(c) displays representative blots with control and rotenone-treated mice, indicating a significant increase in mean pixel density for inflammatory chemokines. Each blot in the panel represents a specific chemokine and the mean pixel density. Compared to the control, the rotenone-treated group exhibited markedly elevated levels of several proinflammatory chemokines. In vivo and ex vivo confirmation of delivery of fluorescent labeled ginseng nanoparticles

Confocal microscopy imaging was used to assess the extent of BBB opening and confirm the presence of FITC-tagged ginseng nanoparticles delivery. Experiments were repeated with similar qualitative trends; no statistical analysis was performed. This brain section was imaged with three different stains: DAPI (nuclei), which was used as a counterstain to stain nuclei, Evans blue (vascular leakage marker), and FITC-labeled nanoparticles to confirm drug delivery, as shown in Fig. 4 (a) and (b). The imaging was done at both higher (100×) and lower (10×) magnifications. The control group exhibited a clearly defined nuclear architecture, with no or minimal signal observed in the Evans blue and FITC channels at both 10× and 100× magnification, indicating intact vasculature and an uncompromised BBB. Merged images also revealed the absence of the colocalization of Evans blue or ginseng nanoparticles.

At 0.35 MPa, the DAPI stain remains consistent with morphology. However, Evans blue and FITC-labeled ginseng fluorescence was visibly increased in perivascular regions at both magnifications, suggesting successful BBB disruption. Merged images revealed colocalization between Evans blue and ginseng nanoparticles, yet controlled BBB opening for nanoparticle delivery. For the 0.8 MPa, significantly stronger fluorescence was observed, with a more diffuse spread throughout the brain parenchyma. FITC-labeled ginseng nanoparticles were present in large quantities and were more widely distributed throughout the tissue. Confocal fluorescence imaging revealed successful delivery of FITC-labeled GnNPs following FUS-mediated BBB opening. At the whole-tissue level (10× magnification), control brains exhibited negligible FITC signal, whereas FUS-treated groups displayed progressively higher fluorescence intensities with increasing acoustic pressure. Fluorescence intensity was quantified using the entire 10× and 100× field of view without manual ROI selection to minimize selection bias. The 100× images were acquired using fluorescence microscopes to provide an overview, while the 10× images were obtained using a confocal laser microscopy for high-resolution analysis; therefore, the 100× images do not represent direct magnifications of the 10× fields. Quantitative differences were determined based on integrated fluorescence intensity across the full image and may not be visually apparent in representative images. Quantitative analysis (10×) confirmed a significant elevation in GnNPs accumulation at 0.35 MPa, with maximal uptake observed at 0.8 MPa as shown in Fig. 4(c). High-magnification analysis (100×) further validated these findings at the cellular level, showing marked FITC signal within brain parenchyma in FUS-treated mice compared to controls panel as shown in Fig. 4(d). Together, these data demonstrate that FUS-BBBO facilitates robust and pressure-dependent nanoparticle delivery into the brain, with the strongest effect achieved at 0.8 MPa.

In vivo fluorescence demonstrated successful delivery of FITC-labeled ginseng nanoparticles (FITC-GnNPs) into the brain following FUS BBBO. In the control group (no nanoparticles and no BBB opening), only low-intensity background fluorescence was observed, consistent with intrinsic tissue autofluorescence. The FITC-GnNPs-only group (no BBB opening) showed weak fluorescence signals primarily confined to superficial cranial regions, indicating limited nanoparticle penetration across the intact BBB. In contrast, the FITC-GnNPs + BBBO group exhibited a strong and localized fluorescent signal in the targeted brain region, confirming successful BBB permeabilization and effective nanoparticle delivery as shown in Fig. 5(a).

Interestingly, a slightly higher apparent fluorescence was observed in the control group compared to the FITC-GnNPs-only group. This is likely due to optical artifacts such as subtle variations in skin pigmentation, hair reflection, or uneven illumination rather than genuine differences in fluorescence intensity. Such effects can occasionally produce localized dark shadows or contrast shifts that affect apparent signal intensity. Quantitative analysis of the targeted region of interest (ROI) shown in Fig. 5(b) revealed that the FITC-GnNPs + BBBO group maintained significantly higher radiant efficiency than both the control and non-BBBO groups at all post-injection time points. The fluorescence signal peaked at approximately 5 min and gradually decreased thereafter, reflecting initial nanoparticle accumulation followed by systemic redistribution. This decline is also influenced by the relatively short effective fluorescence lifetime of FITC (∼1 h), and the experiment was therefore designed to primarily capture the early-phase accumulation of GnNPs in the brain rather than long-term retention. By focusing the analysis on the FUS-targeted region rather than the entire cranial area, these measures more accurately reflect local delivery efficiency and confirm targeted nanoparticle accumulation mediated by FUS-induced BBB opening.

Assessment of mitochondrial symptom improvement

The improvement results of PD following FUS- BBBO of the ginseng nanoparticle are displayed in Fig. 6. The administration of rotenone in the PD model group resulted in a substantial decrease in Complex I activity, with a mean value of 0.528 U/g protein. This represents an approximate 82% reduction compared to the control group, which exhibited a mean value of 2.935 U/g protein. This decline is consistent with the effective inhibition of NADH dehydrogenase. Treatment with ginseng nanoparticles alone (PD + GnNPs) led to a modest recovery of Complex I activity, increasing it to 0.693 U/g protein. Conversely, the application of FUS alone (PD + FUS) resulted in a more pronounced enhancement in activity, achieving a mean value of 1.352 U/g protein. The combination of FUS and Ginseng nanoparticles (PD + FUS + GnNPs) yielded the highest recovery, resulting in a mean activity of 1.979 U/g protein with 67% restoration compared to the control, as shown in Fig. 6(a). Assessment of ATP showed a parallel trend to mitochondrial Complex I activity output. The PD-induced groups exhibited a significant reduction in ATP levels compared to the control group. The PD model group recorded a mean ATP level of 0.043 mmol/Kg, reflecting an approximate 88% decrease from the control mean of 0.359 mmol/Kg, indicating severe mitochondrial impairment resulting from chronic rotenone exposure. Treatment with ginseng alone (PD + GnNPs) resulted in a modest increase in ATP levels (mean: 0.077 mmol/Kg), suggesting a partial protective effect. The PD + FUS group showed a higher ATP level (0.124 mmol/Kg), suggesting that FUS alone may contribute to partial ATP restoration, potentially through enhanced delivery or transient modulation of the BBB. The PD + FUS + GnNPs group exhibited the highest mean ATP level (0.244 mmol/Kg), corresponding to approximately 68% recovery relative to the control as demonstrated in Fig. 6(b).

To assess the effects of ginseng treatment on motor deficits associated with PD, a pole test was conducted at four distinct time points: Baseline, week 1, week 2, and week 3. The average time (in seconds) taken by mice to descend the pole was recorded for each treatment group: Control, PD, PD + GnNPs, PD + FUS, and PD + FUS + GnNPs. Error bars in the results indicate the standard deviation of the mean (SD). An increased descent time denotes impaired motor coordination and bradykinesia. At baseline, all PD groups exhibited comparable performance, with average descent times ranging from 12 to 13 s (p = ns).

At week 3, the PD group continued to exhibit reduced performance, with a time of 12–13 s, indicating persistent bradykinesia. The PD + GnNPs group showed a modest improvement (9–10 s), suggesting a gradual effect over time. The PD + FUS group showed comparable performance (10–11 s), indicating limited improvement.

The PD + FUS + GnNPs group demonstrated the lowest time among the treatment groups (7–8 s), approaching the control group (6–7 s), with no significant difference observed between them (p > 0.05). When compared to the PD group, this improvement was statistically significant (**p < 0.001). These findings suggest a potential combined effect of ginseng nanoparticles and FUS-mediated BBB opening, although further studies are required to statistically confirm this interaction with more mice as shown in Fig. 7(a).

Discussion

This study presents a multimodal therapeutic approach integrating FUS-BBBO with the delivery of ginseng-derived nanoparticles rich in ginsenoside Rg3. The results suggest that the combination of ginseng nanoparticle treatment and FUS-BBBO may provide enhanced neuroprotective effects compared to either treatment alone.

This study lies in the use of FUS to enhance the targeted delivery of ginsenoside Rg3, an underexplored ginsenoside with potent mitochondrial-protective properties across the BBB in vivo. Previous studies have largely focused on Rd and Re, with limited in vivo therapeutic outcomes of Rg3, particularly in mammalian models (M. Liu et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2015). In this work, Rg3 was delivered via stable ginseng nanoparticles, and FUS-mediated BBB opening facilitated nanoparticle penetration into the striatum, as supported by fluorescence imaging and IVIS analysis. In vivo imaging indicated increased nanoparticle accumulation in FUS-treated mice, with peak signal observed between 5 and 45 min post-injection. Behavioral assessments showed that the combined FUS + Rg3 group exhibited improved motor performance (pole and open field tests), olfactory function (buried food test), and affective behavior (tail suspension test) compared to untreated and monotherapy groups. At the biochemical level, this group also demonstrated higher mean ATP levels and Complex I activity, suggesting partial restoration of mitochondrial function. These effects may be associated with the antioxidant and mitochondrial-supporting properties of ginsenosides. Overall, the findings suggest that enhanced delivery via FUS-BBBO, together with the pharmacological action of Rg3, may contribute to the observed improvements.

A central pathogenic mechanism underlying PD involves mitochondrial dysfunction, particularly the impairment of Complex I of the electron transport chain (ETC), leading to compromised ATP production and increased oxidative stress [34]. While various therapeutic strategies have attempted to address symptomatic motor deficits in PD, few have effectively targeted the underlying bioenergetic failure in neurons. Mitochondrial dysfunction has long been recognized as a pathological hallmark of PD, with rotenone serving as a well-established mitochondrial Complex I inhibitor used to recapitulate PD-like phenotypes in animal models. In our study, rotenone-treated mice exhibited profound motor impairment, reduced ATP levels, and diminished Complex I activity—indicative of mitochondrial dysfunction—alongside increased neuroinflammation and depressive-like behavior. Ginsenosides such as Rg3 are known to exert neuroprotective effects through several mechanisms, including antioxidative stress responses and mitochondrial stabilization. Rg3 has been reported to activate the Nrf2/ARE pathway, which upregulates antioxidant enzymes such as SOD2 and HO-1, thereby reducing oxidative damage(Paik et al., 2023; Zaafan et al., 2019). Additionally, Rg3 influences apoptotic signaling by upregulating anti-apoptotic proteins (e.g., Bcl-2) and downregulating pro-apoptotic markers such as Bax and caspase-3 (R.-L. Li et al., 2022). Our data is consistent with these mechanisms: the Rg3-treated group showed moderate restoration of mitochondrial function, and behavioral recovery corresponded with improved energy metabolism and reduced inflammatory cytokine levels. Although statistical significance was not achieved, the observed upward trend in ATP recovery across the ginseng- and FUS-treated groups suggests a possible additive or synergistic effect, as shown in Fig. 6(b). The restoration of ATP levels in the PD + + FUS + GnNPs group may result from ginseng's recognized antioxidant and mitochondrial protective properties (Choi et al., 2018; Huang et al., 2021; Van Kampen et al., 2003), which could counteract the effects of rotenone-induced Complex I inhibition. Moreover, FUS- BBBO may enhance ginseng delivery to the striatum, thereby improving its therapeutic availability. These findings are consistent with literature report indicating that ginsenosides improve mitochondrial biogenesis and preserve electron transport efficiency (Huang et al., 2021). However, these effects were markedly amplified when Rg3 was administered in conjunction with FUS BBBO opening. This supports the idea that suboptimal drug penetration across the BBB is a major barrier to therapeutic efficacy for neuroprotective compounds in PD, and that addressing mitochondrial bioenergetics through improved CNS delivery.

The impermeability of the BBB restricts the entry of many bioactive molecules, especially large or hydrophilic compounds such as ginsenosides. In this study, we demonstrate that FUS, when paired with microbubble contrast agents, enables safe and localized BBBO in mice, with a focal beam width of approximately 3.25 mm and minimal distortion in the presence of skull attenuation (19% reduction in acoustic pressure). Cavitation threshold analysis confirmed that both stable and inertial cavitation were reliably achieved within the therapeutic pressure range (0.4–0.7 MPa), validating the safety and efficacy of the ultrasound parameters used. The real-time delivery efficacy of Rg3 nanoparticles was confirmed via in vivo fluorescence imaging. In the absence of FUS BBBO, FITC-labeled nanoparticles exhibited minimal brain accumulation, likely due to passive diffusion or vascular retention. However, following FUS-BBBO, fluorescence intensity significantly increased in the brain, particularly within the striatum. This indicates a defined temporal window post-BBBO during which brain penetration is improved. These findings suggest that FUS-BBBO not only improves the spatial targeting of drug delivery and optimizes pharmacokinetics by temporarily increasing BBB permeability without permanent disruption but may also exert neuromodulatory and mitochondrial-stimulating effects. Prior studies have shown that FUS can transiently activate glial cells, promote mitochondrial biogenesis via PGC-1α signaling, and potentially upregulate mitochondrial quality control proteins such as PINK1, Parkin, and Mito fusions (Karmacharya et al., 2017). Further studies are required to quantitatively measure that biological effects may further augment the therapeutic benefit of ginseng compounds by enhancing mitochondrial turnover and repair.

In terms of translational application, a key consideration lies in the physiological and anatomical differences between the murine model and the human brain. Mouse models, including the rotenone-induced PD model used here, exhibit a rapid onset of mitochondrial dysfunction and dopaminergic neuron degeneration. However, these models do not fully recapitulate the chronic, progressive, and multifactorial nature of human PD, where both genetic and environmental factors interact over the years. The murine BBB is also structurally and functionally different from that of humans, particularly in terms of tight junction composition and transporter expression (O'Brown et al., 2018). These differences may influence both permeability and pharmacokinetics of nanoparticle delivery post-BBBO.

In humans, the primary translational challenge for FUS-BBBO arises from skull-induced acoustic attenuation, which requires adjustment of ultrasound parameters to maintain safe and precise BBB opening. The acoustic attenuation due to the thicker human skull is considerably greater; however, several clinical-grade FUS systems (e.g., InSightec Exablate Neuro) have already been developed to compensate for these challenges through advanced phase correction algorithms and real-time monitoring (Kim et al., 2023). These technologies demonstrate that safe and effective BBB opening in humans is feasible and are currently being tested in clinical trials for neurodegenerative diseases(D. Wu et al., 2023). Additionally, while rodents tolerate repeated FUS-BBB disruption with minimal adverse effects in short-term studies, the long-term safety, risk of edema, or hemorrhage in humans remains insufficiently characterized and must be rigorously evaluated(Wasielewska and White, 2022).

Despite these encouraging findings, the study faces certain limitations. The sample size (n = 3 per group for biochemical assays) may have limited statistical power, contributing to the marginal significance observed in some comparisons. Biological variability inherent in animal models could also affect the robustness of the data. Additionally, while trends strongly suggest possible synergy between FUS-BBBO and ginseng treatment, definitive mechanistic studies are required to elucidate the precise pathways involved, including the kinetics of BBBO, nanoparticle distribution, and long-term effects on neuronal survival and function. Furthermore, potential off-target effects or safety concerns related to repeated BBB disruption have not been extensively explored and warrant further investigation. Due to the exploratory nature of the study, a limited sample size (n = 3 per group) was used, and therefore the observed motor improvements should be interpreted as preliminary trends rather than definitive outcomes. Another limitation of this study is the absence of a vehicle-injected control group; although all groups were treated identically, future studies should include vehicle controls to fully isolate the effects of rotenone. Lastly, the use of a single PD model induced by rotenone limits the generalizability of the results, and validation across other PD models would strengthen the translational potential. In this study, the dose of Rg3 used was informed by previous literature but was not optimized for maximal efficacy. Nonetheless, even at this dose, Rg3 treatment significantly improved behavioral performance and mitochondrial function, that were potentiated by FUS-BBBO delivery. This underscores the importance of efficient delivery systems, and future work should aim to define optimal dosing parameters to fully leverage the therapeutic potential of Rg3 in PD. Although the focus of this study was mitochondrial dysfunction (ATP depletion and Complex I inhibition), future work will include TH (tyrosine hydroxylase) immunostaining to further validate dopaminergic neurodegeneration in the rotenone model. While this study focused on ATP levels and Complex I activity, future work will include oxidative stress markers (e.g., ROS, SOD2) to provide a more comprehensive assessment of mitochondrial function. Though no overt structural damage was observed by H&E staining, the potential for cumulative neuroinflammation following repeated FUS-BBBO cannot be excluded. Given recent reports of increased microglial and astrocytic activation after repeated BBB opening, future studies will include immunohistochemical analysis (e.g., Iba-1, GFAP) to more comprehensively assess neuroinflammatory responses and long-term safety. Another limitation of the present study is the absence of direct quantification of Rg3 concentrations in brain tissue. Although FITC-labeled GnNPs demonstrated enhanced accumulation in the brain following FUS-mediated BBB opening, the amount of Rg3 delivered to the target tissue was not directly measured. Therefore, the relationship between brain Rg3 exposure and the observed improvements in mitochondrial and behavioral parameters remains inferential. Future studies employing quantitative analytical techniques such as HPLC or LC-MS/MS will be necessary to determine brain Rg3 concentrations and establish exposure–response relationships.

In summary, this study provides compelling evidence that combining FUS- BBBO with ginseng nanoparticle delivery offers a promising therapeutic strategy to restore motor function and mitochondrial integrity in PD. The approach enhances drug bioavailability in the brain and addresses one of the significant hurdles in treating neurodegenerative diseases. Future work should aim to optimize treatment parameters, expand sample sizes, and explore the long-term safety and efficacy of this combined modality. While the murine results are promising and provide foundational insights, caution must be exercised in extrapolating these findings directly to clinical settings. Future studies should integrate non-human primate models or human organoid systems, incorporate real-time imaging of BBB dynamics, and explore combinatorial biomarkers to assess mitochondrial function and inflammatory status. This will strengthen the translational pathway for FUS-assisted delivery of neuroprotective compounds such as ginsenosides in the context of PD.

Conclusion

This study provides preliminary evidence that FUS-mediated BBB opening may enhance the delivery of GnNPs in a rotenone-induced PD mouse model. FUS enabled localized and transient BBB disruption, facilitating increased brain accumulation of FITC-labeled GnNPs, as supported by imaging and histological observations. The combination of FUS and GnNPs showed trends toward improved mitochondrial function, including partial restoration of Complex I activity and ATP levels, along with indications of improved motor performance compared to individual treatments. However, due to the exploratory design and limited sample size, these findings should be interpreted as preliminary. Further studies with larger, adequately powered cohorts are required to confirm these effects, optimize treatment parameters, and evaluate long-term safety and mechanisms. Future studies will be needed to optimize dosing, evaluate long-term safety, and investigate underlying mechanisms to support clinical translation.
