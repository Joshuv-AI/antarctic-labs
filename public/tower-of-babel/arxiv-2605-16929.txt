Emulating the Forced Response of Climate Models with Flow Matching
Graham Clyne, Julia Kaltenborn, Peer Nowack, Claire Monteleoni, Anasatase Charantonis

Abstract
Global climate models are essential tools to simulate past and potential future pathways of climate change, as well as associated climate impacts. Shared Socioeconomic Pathways (SSPs) describe a range of future scenarios of global economic and demographic development. These SSPs are intrinsically linked to changes in climate forcings—the external drivers, such as greenhouse gas and aerosol emissions, which in turn lead to the human impact on Earth’s energy balance over time. These forcings are fundamental boundary conditions in climate models in order to gain insight into the potential climatic impacts of these changes described by each SSP. Running a climate model, however, is extremely computationally expensive, conflicting with the need for large ensembles of simulations for each model to give, e.g., more robust estimates in the presence of internal variability (the inherent, chaotic fluctuations within the climate system) and scenario uncertainty. Recent research has demonstrated the ability to capture climate model dynamics using machine learning when conditioned on forcings from different climatic scenarios. We here train a Deep Learning (DL) model on multiple SSPs and successfully generate scenarios unseen during training. Our emulator is validated against MESMER-M, a statistical emulator of land surface temperature. Our research demonstrates the capacity to generate such changing climate states in response to a variety of simultaneous climate forcings (e.g., carbon dioxide, methane, nitrous oxide, sulphate aerosols, and ozone). In particular, our ablation studies underline a need to include a range of different forcings to represent long-term atmospheric trends with a DL emulator.



1
Introduction

Humanity is facing the consequences of a changing climate, ranging from vanishing ecosystem services (benefits that humans derive from the natural environment) to increased exposure to extreme events, putting the lives of humans, flora, and fauna at risk
[
61
]
. To move forward, we need to mitigate, adapt, and develop our future sustainably
[
15
]
.
A cornerstone of climate change adaptation is humanity’s capacity to project the future of our climate under different scenarios. Policymakers need to understand how different emission plans might affect Earth’s climate to make informed decisions on behalf of the groups they represent. For decades, scientists have contributed to understanding Earth’s future climate by developing climate models
[
12
]
. Climate models started by modeling only the atmosphere and ocean, but recently they have developed into Earth System Models (ESMs), i.e. they simulate different parts of the Earth in tandem, using physical equations that formalize what we know about how the atmosphere, ocean, cryosphere and biosphere operate and interact
[
49
,
48
]
. ESMs are run for many different “what-if” experiments, helping scientists better understand the Earth’s climate
[
12
,
27
]
. One set of such experiments is called ScenarioMIP
[
60
]
, which compares the climate outputs of different ESMs under different future scenarios. These experiments essentially ask “how will our Earth’s climate look like if humanity behaves in a certain way?”. Those future scenarios of human behaviour are called Shared Socio-Economic Pathways (SSPs)
[
66
]
. They describe the future in terms of economic, societal, and demographic changes over the coming decades and as such also formulate assumptions about future emissions of greenhouse gases and aerosols. SSPs have further implications for the secondary formation of other forcing agents through atmospheric chemical reactions, in particular tropospheric and stratospheric concentrations of ozone and concerning the atmospheric oxidizing capacity (relevant for the degradation of greenhouse gases such as methane). Changes in anthropogenic greenhouse gases and aerosols expected from each SSP are provided through
Input4MIPs
, a coordinated activity that provides standardized, version-controlled datasets, providing time-dependent emission maps or atmospheric concentration fields that are then used as inputs for the ESMs participating in ScenarioMIP
[
53
]
. In summary, we can, e.g., input a future emission scenario into an ESM to simulate its long-term climate response for a given SSP.

The resulting projections have several types of uncertainty. In the near-term, i.e. 30 years, model uncertainty (how unsure we are about the output of the model) is a major component of the the total uncertainty, followed by the climate system’s internal variability (the natural, stochastic fluctuations within the climate system), and a small amount of scenario uncertainty (the range of potential future climate outcomes resulting from different plausible pathways of human activity). As the projection moves farther into the future, scenario uncertainty can start to become the dominant uncertainty given a wide range of SSPs
[
47
,
43
]
.
Unlike model uncertainty, which could be narrowed through, e.g., improved representations of model physical processes and computing power, scenario uncertainty is irreducible because it pertains to future human agency. Understanding and quantifying this uncertainty is critical for policymakers; it underpins the exploration of strategies that remain effective across various trajectories. By generating different scenarios, researchers can better demonstrate how immediate policy interventions could directly alter the long-term climate. ESMs are computationally expensive, restricting the generation of a wide variety of future scenarios.
This work aims to help climate models in exploring additional scenarios by providing a robust method to quickly generate spatially and temporally explicit future climate simulations that respond appropriately to a multitude of external forcings.

In this study, we investigate whether generative Deep Learning (DL) can generate unseen future climate scenarios. We aim to investigate the need to accurately represent a combination of different forcings on the emulated climate scenarios. We use ArchesClimate as the DL framework, an autoregressive probabilistic climate model emulator based on ArchesWeather
[
16
,
17
]
and here train ArchesClimate on a set of climate projections produced with the IPSL-CM6A-LR ESM
[
7
]
. We extend the original ArchesClimate framework by conditioning on sets of forcing agents that represent trajectories under different SSP scenarios. We will refer to the extended model generally as ArchesClimate-SSP, with each specific forcing setup being indicated as
AC_{forcing\_setup}
, where
forcing\_setup
will be defined in
subsection 4.2
. We train our model using monthly averages from 2015-2100 for 8 different future scenarios, in addition to historical runs and control runs. We held out three SSP scenarios (SSP4-3.4, SSP5-3.4 and SSP5-8.5) for testing.

We structure the paper as follows: we first provide a review of the current state of climate model emulation. We then explain the methods used in our research. Following this, we show that we can accurately emulate SSP scenarios unseen at training time. To do so, we first evaluate if the generated scenarios represent temporal and spatial variability of the IPSL climate model, and we compare our results to MESMER-Ms, a state-of-the-art spatial statistical emulator,
[
57
,
4
]
. Finally, we perform ablations of the model by removing different forcings. This shows how different forcings inform the accuracy of ArchesClimate-SSP when generating future scenarios.

The contributions of this research are as follows:

•

We introduce two mechanisms that help long-term stability and flexibility: training with random future timesteps, and using dropout of the forcing conditions.

•

We show that we are able to train a DL model that correctly reproduces the temporal and spatial dynamics of SSPs unseen at training time

•

We show that the explicit inclusion of a range of different forcings impacts the accuracy of ArchesClimate-SSP.

2
Background

To contextualize our proposed method for accelerating SSP generation, it is necessary to examine previous efforts to improve the computational efficiency of ESMs. We begin by outlining the computational bottlenecks of traditional models, followed by a review of traditional non-DL emulators. We then explore the recent paradigm shift toward machine learning approaches, concluding with the ongoing challenge of incorporating external forcings into these frameworks.

Historically, the primary alternative to ESMs has been the development of reduced-order or intermediate complexity models. These tools trade the complexity of an ESM for computational efficiency, allowing for the rapid simulation of diverse scenarios
[
42
,
52
,
55
]
. To generate spatially explicit data at low computational costs, these emulators are often combined with pattern scaling techniques
[
69
,
71
,
65
]
or functions based on historical temperature and precipitation relationships
[
11
]
. More advanced statistical emulators, such as MESMER and its monthly extension MESMER-M, successfully model internal variability and regional trends for temperature based on global yearly values
[
4
,
57
]
.
The most direct method for improving the speed of ESMs is the expansion of computational resources. However, ESMs are already computationally expensive, and the associated engineering and operational costs remain prohibitively high
[
1
]
. While researchers are increasingly leveraging automatic differentiation and GPU computing to optimize the performance of modern physics-based climate simulations
[
31
,
38
,
41
]
, further research is needed to represent full ESMs, driving the need for alternative modeling approaches.

Deep learning (DL) has emerged as a means to decouple computational performance from physical complexity. While DL applied to weather forecasting has already demonstrated the ability to match or exceed state-of-the-art physics-based models for short-term predictions
[
3
,
62
,
17
]
, DL applied to climate models translate these gains to decadal and centennial timescales.

Architectures used to tackle these problems are categorized into deterministic models, which produce a single trajectory from a given initial condition
[
18
,
32
]
, and generative models, such as diffusion or flow-matching networks, which generate an ensemble of trajectories for a single state
[
9
,
8
]
. Generative models create plausible samples that are particularly effective at capturing the stochastic variability inherent in climate systems. While recent models have successfully emulated both isolated ocean components and coupled ocean-atmosphere dynamics, these efforts remain largely focused on stationary climate conditions
[
67
,
26
,
23
]
.

Despite these advancements, capturing the non-stationarity of a long-term forced climate response to external drivers like greenhouse gases and solar irradiation remains a significant hurdle. While some models utilize solar irradiation or prescribed sea surface temperatures (SSTs) as inputs, this approach is often criticized as it avoids the fundamental task of simulating how the atmosphere responds to greenhouse gases and aerosols, as the resulting climate trajectory is already largely dictated by the input. There is a notable scarcity of ML emulators capable of prognostic modeling the Earth’s response to Shared Socioeconomic Pathways (SSPs) internally rather than relying on pre-determined ocean conditions, which remains a critical gap for contributing to meaningful policy-making. The core challenge lies in capturing shifting distributions. For example, recent work like ACE2-SOM has explored carbon dioxide (
CO_{2}
) sensitivity at fixed warming levels (e.g., 2°C or 4°C), but these models often exhibit extreme sensitivity to distribution shifts
[
14
,
39
]
. Similarly, AeroGP uses Gaussian processes to capture regional temperature future responses to changing aerosols
[
22
]
. While more approaches are beginning to attempt future state generation based on SSPs
[
36
,
50
]
, accurately modeling the spatiotemporal trajectory of a forced climate remains an open question that our methodology seeks to address.

3
Methods

This section introduces the dataset used in the project. We then detail the model architecture and training procedures, concluding with an overview of the metrics and analytical methods used for evaluation.

3.1
Forcings

Our model incorporates forcings from multiple sources, including greenhouse gases, aerosols, solar irradiation and ozone. Here we describe each forcing in detail. Please see
Table 1
for a summary of the forcings included in ArchesClimate-SSP.

We incorporate concentrations for the major well-mixed greenhouse gases (GHGs)—carbon dioxide (CO2), methane (CH4), and nitrous oxide (N2O)—sourced from the
input4MIPs
repository
[
54
,
53
]
. These three gases, measured in parts per billion (ppb) for
CH_{4}
and nitrous oxide (
N_{2}O
) and parts per million (ppm) for
CO_{2}
, were selected as our primary forcing agents due to their dominant roles in the historical anthropogenic greenhouse effect and under typical future SSPs
[
10
]
.
CO_{2}
is the major anthropogenic greenhouse gas by historical radiative forcing and also under future SSPs, driving long-term climate change due to its longevity and large, persistent increases in emissions. Methane also poses a substantial climate forcing but has a much shorter e-folding time (the time it takes for the concentration of a gas to decrease to approximately 37% of its original amount) of around 10 years, resulting in potentially faster, scenario-dependent fluctuations in its atmospheric concentrations.
N_{2}O
has an estimated e-folding time of more than 100 years and is the third most important anthropogenic greenhouse gas directly emitted into the atmosphere, e.g. from agricultural activities. These gases are well mixed, meaning they are relatively equally distributed throughout the atmosphere due to their sufficiently long lifetimes for the atmospheric circulation to distribute them across the entire atmosphere.
The GHG concentrations from input4MIPS are 0.5 degree latitudinal monthly resolution, which we regrid to the resolution of the IPSL model ( 2.5x1.25 degrees) using first-order conservative regridding. Conservative regridding is a numerical method for transferring data between different grids that ensures the total integral of a quantity, like heat or moisture, is perfectly preserved so that no energy or mass is artificially created or lost. Following ArchesClimate, we also include these forcings as non-spatial (spatially averaged means) conditionings using Condition Layer Normalization
[
16
]
. Conditional Layer Normalization is a technique that adapts the standard normalization process by making the gain and bias parameters dependent on external information, allowing the model to dynamically shift its activations based on the context
[
13
]
.

Next to greenhouse gases, anthropogenic aerosol emissions pose another major human impact on Earth’s energy budget, which we consider here. Such aerosols are tiny solid particles or liquid droplets suspended in the atmosphere. We use the atmospheric load for four aerosol types: Aerosol Soluble Nitrate (ASNO3M), Coarse Soluble Nitrate (CSNO3M), Coarse Insoluble Nitrate (CINO3M) and Sulfate (SO4). In particular sulfate aerosols are known to reflect sunlight, causing a counteracting cooling balance on the historical climate
[
58
]
, including important non-linear temperature effects
[
29
,
50
,
2
]
. We use data from INCA, a chemistry and aerosol model developed at the Laboratoire des Sciences du Climat et de l’Environnement
[
45
,
33
]
INCA is ran beforehand to generate aerosols states that are then used downstream in the IPSL model. We again conservatively regrid this data to a 144x144 resolution from 143x144. Here we simply include the atmospheric load (kg m
-2
) of the aerosols as a rough proxy of Aerosol Optical Depth.

Solar Spectral Irradiance (SSI) is sunlight that reaches different parts of the atmosphere, determined by its wavelength. We chose to include this forcing to give the model more information to understand radiative forcing
[
10
]
. As it is the only consistent forcing across different SSP and historical/control scenarios, we also expect it to act as a temporal signal. The solar irradiation included is non-spatial at a monthly temporal resolution.

Finally, we differentiate ozone from the other greenhouse gases, because ozone is formed through chemical reactions in the atmosphere rather than being directly emitted from the Earth’s surface
[
63
]
. In addition, its concentrations differ by orders of magnitude between the troposphere and the stratosphere. This is down to very different ozone production and loss pathways depending on the availability of sunlight, ozone-depleting substances, and (tropospheric) ozone-precursors. For example, in the troposphere, primary ozone production pathways depend on the availability of volatile organic compounds (VOCs) – including methane – and of nitrogen oxides (NOx) from both biogenic and anthropogenic sources at Earth’s surface. In the stratosphere, ozone production is primarily driven by the Chapman reactions in the presence of high-frequency ultraviolet sunlight, in balance with a variety of major catalytic ozone loss reactions. The latter also depend on anthropogenic emissions of
N_{2}O
and, famously, on a variety of halogen-containing ozone-depleting substances, which have led to the development of substantial Antarctic ozone holes over the last decades
[
40
]
. In our framework, we use ozone mole fractions from
input4mips
covering the troposphere, stratosphere, and even the mesosphere, and the data is conservatively regridded from 96x144 (latxlon) to 144x144m
[
56
]
. There are 66 vertical layers ranging from 0.0001 hPa to 1000 hPa, but we only include the following layers: [1000, 850, 500, 200, 100, 50, 10, 1, 0.01, 0.0001]. We expect ozone to help inform both stratospheric and tropospheric cooling and warming trends
[
59
,
46
,
64
,
68
]
.

We still only include a subset of the forcings provided to IPSL-CM6A-LR due to computational restraints and our focus on atmospheric dynamics
[
45
]
. For example, we assumed volcanic activity would be implicit in the aerosol data provided, and that the climatic influence of ocean biogeochemical data and land-use cover data were of lower magnitude and could be omitted as a compromise with computational cost and model complexity, and left for future work.

Climate Model Data

We select the IPSL-CM6A-LR model
[
7
]
as our primary data source for several reasons. Its grid resolution (
1.25^{\circ}\times 2.5^{\circ}
) provides a computationally efficient yet physically dense dataset ideal for training deep generative emulators. Furthermore, the model’s atmospheric component, LMDZ6, incorporates advanced parameterizations for tropical convection and boundary layer dynamics, providing a state-of-the-art target for our machine learning objectives
[
35
]
.
We include data from the IPSL-CM6A-LR for 8 SSP scenarios, the
historical
scenario and the
piControl
scenario. Following ScenarioMIP conventions, the scenarios range from an approximate global radiative forcing of 1.9 Wm
-2
– a strong climate change mitigation scenario – to 8.5 Wm
-2
by 2100. In general, we train our model on all ensemble members (which sample internal variability uncertainty) of each scenario; however, we include only 10 of the 31 available members of the historical run to keep the model focused on future scenarios. Please see
Table 2
for the number of members used for each scenario. A high-level description of the scenarios can be found in
Table 2
. The variables included in ArchesClimate-SSP are summarized in
Table 3
.

For the evaluation of our model, we utilize SSP4-3.4 and abrupt-4xCO2 for the validation, and SSP5-3.4 and SSP5-8.5 for the test set. We use
historical
,
piControl
, SSP1-1.9, SSP1-2.6, SSP2-4.5, SSP3-7.0 and SSP4-6.0 as training data. SSP5-3.4 offers a valuable testing scenario due to its overshoot profile, in which
CO_{2}
concentrations peak mid-century before declining. Overshoot dynamics are becoming increasingly likely as a future, and are therefore important to accurately model
[
24
]
. In contrast, SSP5-8.5 provides a critical assessment of the model’s ability to handle out-of-distribution data. Specifically, around the year 2081—approximately 66 years into the dataset—the
CO_{2}
concentrations in SSP5-8.5 exceed the maximum levels seen in the training data (SSP3-7.0 giving the highest
CO_{2}
concentration). SSP5-3.4 starts in 2040, and therefore only provides 60 years of data. SSP4-3.4 and SSP5-8.5 start in 2015 and provide 85 years of data. The inclusion of abrupt4xCO2 in the validation set specifically allows us to assess ArchesClimate-SSP’s ability to distinguish between internal climatic responses and external forced signals. As the experiment is an immediate 4x step of
CO_{2}
, it requires the model to understand the difference between the
CO_{2}
signal and the previous climate state. We hypothesize that this experiment will show if the model is overfitting to the
CO_{2}
signal.

3.3
Architecture

For our deep learning framework, we employ ArchesClimate-SSP
[
16
,
17
]
, an extension of the ArchesWeather architecture. ArchesWeather is a Sliding Window (Swin) Vision Transformer based on the Pangu-Weather model. It efficiently processes weather/climate states by partitioning spatial regions into tokens, which are then processed through successive attention blocks. These blocks utilize a windowing mechanism that shifts in size and location to capture multi-scale dependencies. Following the methodology of both ArchesWeather and ArchesClimate, the prediction task is split into two tasks. First, the model first learns a deterministic mean for the subsequent timestep, which is followed by a generative residual to capture probabilistic uncertainty
[
16
,
17
,
44
,
6
]
.

We incorporate external forcings through two distinct pathways based on their dimensionality. Spatially resolved forcings (ozone, aerosols, and spatial GHGs) are concatenated directly to the input state. In contrast, non-spatial forcings (global GHG concentrations and SSI) are integrated via conditional layer normalization, as implemented in ArchesClimate
[
13
]
. This technique modulates the normalization process by scaling and shifting the layer outputs using a mean and bias derived from the forcing inputs. Our configuration utilizes an embedding dimension of 384, resulting in a total of 460M parameters, while all other hyperparameters remain consistent with the original ArchesClimate specifications.

The model input comprises all variables listed in
Table 3
for both the current and previous timesteps, along with the forcings detailed in
Table 1
for the current timestep. While these forcings inform the dynamics in the next state, the model does not predict their future states; instead, it specifically targets the next-step evolution of the climate variables in
Table 3
. A visual representation of this training and prediction scheme is provided in
Figure 1
.

). The deterministic output is evaluated against the target climate state using a standard Mean Squared Error (MSE) loss. Note: The subsequent generative stage (not shown) utilizes a composite loss function incorporating both spectral and MSE components.

3.4
Training and Inference

While training and inference follow the ArchesClimate framework, we implement the following modifications. To prevent overfitting on forcings, we mask the input forcing data with a probability of
\lambda
(as shown in
Figure 1
); this restricts the model to rely on the previous timestep and internalize the climate response. To ensure long-term stability, we randomly sample a forecast horizon
h
from the set of discrete lead times
\mathcal{H}=\{1,6,12\}
, which is provided to the model as an embedded value.
subsection 5.2
describes how the values for these hyperparameters were chosen. The Mean Squared Error (MSE) loss is then calculated over the specific interval
\Delta t=h
. This approach encourages the model to learn long-term dependencies, thereby enhancing stability (see
subsection A.1
for related ablations of
\lambda
and
\Delta{t}
). The randomized timestep is only applied to the deterministic model. While the deterministic model utilizes a standard MSE loss, the generative model employs a combination of MSE and spectral loss, detailed in the following section. As in both
[
16
,
17
]
, we use an ensemble of 4 deterministic models to train the generative model.

Following ArchesClimate, a spectral loss is included to improve variability
[
16
]
. The generative loss
\mathcal{L}_{total}
is a weighted sum of an MSE loss and a spectral loss. We define the set of variable groups as
\mathcal{V}
and the set of spatial dimensions as
\mathcal{D}=\{h,w\}
.

MSE Loss
:
This term measures the difference between the predicted and target velocity fields.

(1)

where
\hat{u}
is the predicted velocity field and
u
is the target velocity field.
The spectral loss is applied to the reconstructed state
x_{1}
, weighted by the timestep
w_{t}
. See
subsection A.3
for a definition of the spectral loss. The final objective function uses weights (
\lambda
) to balance these components:

\mathcal{L}_{total}=\mathcal{L}_{mse}+\lambda_{spec}\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}\mathbb{E}\left[w_{t}\cdot\text{SpectralLoss}(\hat{x}_{1,v},x_{1,v})\right]

(2)

We utilize 12 inference steps with an Euler solver. See
subsection A.6
to see the impact of inference steps on rollouts.

To perform inference with ArchesClimate-SSP, we generate climate state time series autoregressively, initialized by IPSL model conditions for a given SSP. For example, to emulate SSP4-3.4, the model is initialized using the first two months of the IPSL scenario (e.g. January and February 2015) and the corresponding forcings. ArchesClimate-SSP then predicts the subsequent month’s state, which is fed back into the model to extend the simulation. We define these generated time series as rollouts, with each rollout serving as a distinct ensemble member. A detailed visualization of this pipeline is available in
[
16
,
17
]
.

4
Experiment Design

In this section, we describe how we evaluate the model. We then explain the metrics we used to assess the performance of the model. Finally, we describe the baseline used and give a description of the significance testing done in the results section.

4.1
Metrics

We use Latitude-Weighted Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE), Normalized RMSE (for comparison across variables), and Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE) to evaluate the accuracy of ArchesClimate-SSP. To evaluate the variance of ArchesClimate-SSP, we use Interannual Variability (IAV), Ensemble Spread and Spatial Standard Deviation. We use the Hydrostatic Balance as a physical sanity check when comparing ablated models. In meteorology, hydrostatic balance is expressed by combining the Hydrostatic Equation and the Ideal Gas Law
[
34
]
. Please see
subsection A.4
for precise definitions of the listed metrics.

4.2
Ablated Models

To determine which forcings are essential for successfully emulating a variety of SSP scenarios, we conduct a systematic ablation study. Each generative model in this study is initialized and trained following a deterministic training phase using the same forcing configuration. We do not perform a separate hyperparameter search for the ablated deterministic models. While this would allow for the most optimal performance for each model, it is computationally expensive and therefore left for further work. It is important to note that when a forcing is removed in this context, it is not physically absent from the underlying climate data; rather, the emulator is no longer explicitly provided with that specific forcing. These forcings act as cues that emphasize dynamics the model has already encountered during training, rather than introducing entirely new information. We evaluate several model configurations to discern the impact of individual forcings on simulated climate trends:

Full Model (
AC_{\text{full}}
): Our primary configuration, which includes all forcings. This model is used for the scenario emulations discussed in
subsection 5.3
.

Ablated Models: Three variations where a forcing is withheld to test its necessity:
AC_{\neg\text{O}_{3}}
(no ozone),
AC_{\neg\text{ghg}}
(no greenhouse gases) and
AC_{\neg\text{aero}}
(no aerosols).

4.3
Checkpoint Selection

Selecting a point in a model’s training (i.e. a checkpoint) that will provide the best performance presents a significant challenge, as long-term stability in climate rollouts can be elusive. As it is too computationally expensive to generate full 85-year rollouts during training, we have to verify the stability after training. To identify the most robust deterministic model for integration into our generative framework, we execute full 85-year rollouts across several training epochs. These epochs are evaluated based on their Normalized Root Mean Square Error (NRMSE) across all surface variables. All deterministic models are trained until convergence, which is assessed by finding the lowest NRMSE of the final decade of a rollout. Once the superior checkpoint is identified, it serves as the checkpoint for the generative model. Following
[
17
,
16
]
, four deterministic models are trained and served as an ensemble to best predict the mean state. If the deterministic models were not fully converged, the Flow Matching (FM) model would have to work harder to resolve the remaining residuals, resulting in slower convergence and requiring more training epochs and possibly creating further bias.

For the generative phase, we evaluate different Flow Matching (FM) configurations using NRMSE. We intentionally maintain constant hyperparameters across all forcing ablation experiments. By holding the architecture and optimization settings static, we ensure that any observed change in performance is attributable to the removal of the physical forcing, rather than shifts in the model’s learning capacity or optimization efficiency. To assess the performance of each ablated model, we calculated the Normalized Root Mean Square Error (NRMSE) for all ablation experiments across checkpoints from training steps 16,000 to 40,000. This interval was selected to capture checkpoints both prior to and following the inclusion of the spectral loss, with the upper limit defined by the stabilization of the training loss over the final 5,000 steps. To optimize computational efficiency, full validation was conducted post-hoc rather than during active training. For each ablated generative model, as with the corresponding deterministic model, we take the two model checkpoints that have the lowest NRMSE.

4.4
Baseline

We utilize MESMER-M
[
57
,
4
]
as our primary baseline, a statistical climate model emulator designed to produce spatially explicit monthly temperature fields. While the original MESMER framework
[
5
]
focuses on annual temperature maps by accounting for both the long-term local warming signal and grid-point-specific internal variability, MESMER-M extends this capability to a monthly temporal resolution
[
57
]
. We selected MESMER-M as a benchmark because it is a well-established, non-machine-learning method that decomposes the climate signal into distinct mean and residual components.

The mean component of MESMER-M is modeled as a harmonic Fourier series that represents a smooth seasonal cycle. To account for non-stationarity, both the baseline and the seasonal amplitude are fitted as linear combinations of the annual mean temperature. The remaining residual component, which represents internal variability, is modeled using a first-order autoregressive process.

For our experimental setup, we calibrated MESMER-M using a single ensemble member from five different scenarios: SSP1-1.9, SSP1-2.6, SSP2-4.5, SSP3-7.0, and SSP5-8.5. Anomalies were calculated relative to the 1850–1900 pre-industrial reference period. During the evaluation phase, we provided MESMER-M with the target annual mean surface temperatures from the IPSL model to predict the monthly temperature of two withheld scenarios, SSP4-3.4 and the overshoot scenario SSP5-3.4. Further technical details regarding the MESMER-M architecture can be found in
[
57
]
.

5.2
Stability of Ablated Models

As described in
subsection 4.3
, we identify the most accurate checkpoints for final analysis, requiring at least two stable checkpoints per model to ensure results were robust and to mitigate the potential for selecting a stochastic "lucky" iteration. Notably, the ozone-ablated model produced only a single stable checkpoint and was consequently omitted from the comparative analysis, as it failed to meet our stability criteria. This divergence in stability highlights the varying importance of specific forcings within the emulator. The failure of the ozone-ablated model underscores that vertically explicit ozone is a critical forcing for resolving the vertical structure of the atmosphere over decadal timescales. As the primary driver of the thermal structure of the stratosphere, the
input4MIPs
ozone data provides the necessary physical constraints for the model to learn vertical heat distribution. In its absence, we hypothesize that the emulator lacks the information required to account for stratospheric heating and cooling dynamics, which fundamentally govern tropospheric circulation. The rest of the paper omits analysis of
AC_{\neg ozone}
.

5.3.7
Extrapolation Test with SSP5-8.5

To evaluate out-of-distribution generalization, we examine model performance under the SSP5-8.5 scenario. As the most extreme high-emission pathway in the CMIP6 suite, SSP5-8.5 represents a climatic regime characterized by high

The results, illustrated in

6
Discussion and Conclusion

We present a deep-learning climate emulator that responds flexibly to forcings and can differentiate between the forced response and climate response. We can properly reproduce different SSP pathways for several variables with explicit spatial dynamics. Our results demonstrate that ArchesClimate-SSP can successfully capture shifting atmospheric dynamics across both the troposphere and stratosphere when provided with sufficient forcings.

The competitive performance of ArchesClimate-SSP relative to MESMER-M is a promising sign for climate modeling. While MESMER-M is valued for its transparent structure and computational speed, ArchesClimate-SSP provides unique and important advantages. Its capacity to produce diverse ensemble members is particularly useful because it enables thorough probabilistic analysis for scenarios that originally only had one run. ArchesClimate-SSP also emulates the entire atmospheric profile rather than just surface temperatures, which allows it to capture a broader range of atmospheric behavior. By creating these ensembles from single instances like those from IPSL, the model gives researchers a more flexible way to study the full range of potential outcomes in various climate scenarios.

Each forcing integrated into our framework serves a distinct role in maintaining the physical consistency of the emulator. The inclusion of greenhouse gas (GHG) concentrations is essential for capturing long-term trends and ensuring stability, a result consistent with the well-documented linear relationship between
CO_{2}
and global temperature response
[
51
]
. Furthermore, vertically explicit ozone is integral to the generation of stable and accurate emulations, providing a coherent structural anchor across the atmospheric column.

In contrast, we find that incorporating the Aerosol Optical Depth (AOD) from the INCA model, which is characterized by high spatial and temporal variability, of multiple aerosol species tends to degrade model performance. We hypothesize that while ozone provides a stable, spatially coherent signal from which the model can effectively learn, aerosols introduce significant stochasticity. Because many aerosol processes, such as aerosol-cloud interactions, evolve on timescales much shorter than a month, their representation in monthly-resolution data likely presents a noisy signal. This high-frequency variability may confound the emulator’s learning process, whereas the vertically explicit ozone profile provides the structural information necessary to resolve the atmosphere’s thermal and dynamical state. These findings prompt further investigation into whether simplified representations, such as vertically integrated ozone or more explicitly constrained aerosol fields, could better guide model convergence and stability.

We posit that since the training data implicitly has these dynamics, these forcings act more as attention-guiding mechanisms rather than new information. The selection of specific forcings, therefore, can depend on the application and the desired level of complexity, as different inputs emphasize distinct system behaviours. Including more diverse forcings could help the model emulate more subtle dynamics, perhaps at the cost of overall accuracy. Prescribed land-use change, volcanic activity, and biogeochemical information could all be used to improve different aspects of the emulation, depending on the goal of the emulator. Future research should investigate the extent to which these forcings can be compressed without loss of fidelity to facilitate easier integration into a climate model emulator. Furthermore, employing masked autoencoders or adaptable embeddings could yield a more versatile framework for representing these forcings. A comprehensive experimental suite, such as the one detailed in this research, is essential to fully quantify the impact of these drivers. Further work could also include a more varied set of idealized experiments to explore further the impact of these forcings. To further improve the model’s ability to learn slower timescales, subsequent iterations could incorporate deep ocean dynamics and land-use changes. Additionally, exploring multi-timestep training or leveraging recent advances in generative video modeling could better resolve the temporal dependencies inherent in climate evolution.

Despite these successes, the lack of inter-annual variability in ArchesClimate-SSP warrants further investigation. It remains to be determined whether this deficiency can be rectified through alternative modeling strategies, such as introducing temporal attention or increasing the temporal resolution of the training data. While monthly-averaged data is well-suited for capturing centennial temperature trends, it may not be sufficient to resolve shorter-term oscillations. Currently, the model struggles to capture wind dynamics, which remain highly stochastic at the monthly scale and are therefore prone to significant error. Refining the emulation of these high-variance variables is a necessary next step for the development of a comprehensive climate emulator. Without accurately resolving these dynamic fields, the model will likely remain unable to capture climatic extremes and rare events that are driven by rapid atmospheric fluctuations.

A common challenge identified throughout this research is the systematic under-representation of regions characterized by high gradients or abrupt transitions. This limitation is particularly evident in surface precipitation and flux maps, at the tropopause, and along surface boundaries in the geopotential height (
zg
) fields. To address these spatial discrepancies, future work could leverage recent advancements in architectural design that move beyond standard convolutions. For instance, recent research employing a hierarchical map of residuals could better capture the multiscale interactions between global trends and localized gradients
[
70
]
. Furthermore, transitioning to a HEALPix grid structure would likely mitigate the inherent spatial biases and distortion issues common at the poles in traditional latitude-longitude projections.

While these datasets are rooted in physical processes, they can be effectively conceptualized through the lens of classical machine learning theory. Our results mirror foundational principles regarding feature selection and the bias-variance tradeoff. Specifically, incorporating features with low signal-to-noise ratios (e.g. aerosols) can increase model variance without improving accuracy. In this context, the model attempts to learn spurious correlations from stochastic noise rather than capturing a robust physical signal. As we transition toward larger, high-capacity architectures, determining the relevant set of forcings remains a critical bottleneck. Discerning the predictive relevance of specific forcings will be integral to developing comprehensive climate emulators that are both computationally efficient and physically grounded, ensuring that increased model complexity translates into genuine predictive skill rather than mere overfitting.

In summary, this research demonstrates that atmospheric dynamics under a shifting climate can be accurately captured by integrating specific forcings into a generative framework. We show that our model can effectively emulate scenarios not seen during training, confirming that GHG concentrations are a prerequisite for trend stability. Furthermore, we establish that a correct set of forcings is essential for resolving the vertical thermal structure of the atmosphere. These findings provide a vital roadmap for future climate modeling, offering insights into how we can build more efficient, accurate, and physically grounded emulators for long-term climate projections.
