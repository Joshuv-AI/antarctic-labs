Assessing Patient Rights Awareness and Perceived Healthcare Compliance in Surgical Care: Evidence From Southern Iran
Authors: Narjes Abshorshori, Mina Mosallanezhad, Farzad Abaszadeh, Mobin Mottahedi, Marzieh Kargar Jahromi, Fateme Beheshtaeen, Azadeh Amiri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis multicenter, cross-sectional study aimed to assess patient awareness of their rights and the perceived compliance of healthcare teams with these rights in surgical settings across five hospital systems in southern Iran. A total of 582 surgical inpatients participated, with data collected using two validated scales: the Patient Rights Awareness Questionnaire (PRAQ) and the Healthcare Team Compliance Scale (HTCS). The results revealed that the majority of patients had moderate awareness of their rights, with significant variability across domains. Notably, privacy and confidentiality had the lowest awareness scores. Perceived healthcare team compliance was generally high, but gaps were identified in privacy protection and access to medical records. Key factors influencing awareness and compliance included age, education, type of surgery, and hospital setting. These findings underscore the need for targeted interventions to improve patient rights education and healthcare team training, particularly in high-stress emergency settings. Enhancing patient awareness and compliance with patient rights can ultimately improve the quality and transparency of care.Keywords: healthcare team compliance, patient experience, patient rights awareness, surgical care

Introduction

Ensuring that patients receive safe, dignified, and participatory care is a fundamental imperative of contemporary health systems.1,2 Central to this responsibility is the Patient Bill of Rights (PBR), a foundational ethical and legal framework that safeguards autonomy, informed decision-making, confidentiality, and equitable treatment. 3 Surgical care, where patients face heightened physiological vulnerability 4 and must consent to invasive procedures under time-sensitive conditions,5,6 represents one of the most critical domains in which the PBR must be upheld. In such settings, inadequate communication, information asymmetry, and variable observance of ethical standards can undermine trust, compromise safety, and erode the legitimacy of care delivery.7,8

Despite growing global emphasis on patient-centered care, 9 evidence indicates that many patients remain unaware of their rights, and healthcare teams variably adhere to them in practice. 10 A recent scoping review covering 22 studies and 6853 participants reported striking heterogeneity in PBR awareness worldwide, with “good awareness” ranging from as low as 4.6% to 90%, and typically under 20% in low- and middle-income countries. 11 Moreover, patients’ perceptions of healthcare team compliance, a critical yet underexplored dimension of rights-based care, frequently lag behind formal policy commitments. For example, a 2024 study among Palestinian inpatients demonstrated suboptimal compliance in essential domains, such as transparent communication, disclosure of treatment costs, and provision of complaints mechanisms, particularly in high-acuity public hospitals. 12

Iran, an upper-middle-income country with a high surgical workload and a universal public healthcare model, formally instituted its national PBR in 2009. 13 Although the framework explicitly guarantees informed consent, privacy, and the right to information, real-world implementation remains inconsistent. 13 Existing Iranian studies, often single-center, suggest moderate public awareness (approximately 50–60%) and even lower adherence in specific domains, particularly privacy and access to information. 14 Evidence from surgical wards, where vulnerability is intensified and consent processes are often compressed, is limited and geographically confined. A 2021 study reported that although surgical patients highly valued their rights, perceived compliance lagged substantially, pointing to persistent gaps between policy and practice. 15 However, there is a notable lack of multicenter data from southern Iran, a region with diverse socioeconomic and cultural backgrounds that may significantly influence both patient awareness and their perceptions of care. Understanding these regional variations is crucial for developing targeted interventions.

To address these gaps, we conducted a multicenter, cross-sectional study across five major hospital systems in southern Iran. The primary objectives of this study were: (1) to quantify the level of patient awareness of the Iranian PBR using a validated knowledge score; (2) to evaluate patients’ perceptions of healthcare team compliance with the PBR using a standardized scale; and (3) to identify key sociodemographic and clinical factors associated with these outcomes. Generating robust, region-specific evidence from the surgical domain is vital to inform rights-based quality improvement strategies and ensure that patients undergoing operative care in diverse settings receive equitable, transparent, and dignified treatment.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

Study Design

This was a multicenter, cross-sectional, analytical study designed to assess two primary outcomes: (1) the level of awareness of the Iranian PBR among hospitalized patients, and (2) patients’ perceptions of healthcare team compliance with the PBR. The study was conducted across five independent hospital systems located in southern Iran, each operating under its own administrative and clinical governance structure. Data collection occurred from January 1, 2024, to June 30, 2024. These hospitals represent a mix of tertiary referral centers and regional facilities, providing a broad spectrum of elective and emergency services in southern Iran. The multicenter approach enhanced generalizability across diverse socioeconomic and cultural contexts in the region. The study is reported according to the STROBE guidelines for cross-sectional studies, 16 including a participant flow diagram (Supplementary S1 Figure) and a completed STROBE checklist.

Rationale for Design

A multicenter cross-sectional design was chosen to capture the point prevalence of patient awareness and perceived compliance with the PBR at a specific time, while allowing comparison across different hospital systems in southern Iran. This design facilitates the exploration of regional variations and associations with demographic and clinical factors, addressing gaps in prior single-center studies.

Study Population and Eligibility Criteria

Target Populations

The target population consists of adult patients (aged 18 years or older) who were admitted to surgical wards, either on an elective or emergency basis, within the five participating hospital systems during the study period.

Eligibility Criteria

Eligible participants must meet the following inclusion criteria: they should be 18 years of age or older, admitted to a surgical ward or scheduled for surgery, medically and hemodynamically stable as assessed by the attending clinical team, capable of providing informed consent, and fluent in Persian. Exclusion criteria include individuals with documented cognitive impairment or altered mental status that would hinder their ability to provide reliable consent or responses, those experiencing severe pain or hemodynamic instability at the time of data collection, patients discharged or transferred to a non-surgical unit before data collection completion, and those who had previously participated in the study during the same hospitalization period.

Sample Size and Sampling Procedures

Sample Size Calculation

The sample size was calculated based on the primary outcome of the Patient Awareness Score (range 0–25). Using a standard deviation of 4.6, 17 a 95% confidence level, and a precision (margin of error) of 0.5 points, the initial estimated sample size was 326 participants. To account for the multicenter design, a design effect of 1.5 was applied, resulting in 489 participants. An additional adjustment for an anticipated 20% non-response/refusal rate increased the minimum required sample size to 612 patients.

To ensure adequate power for pre-specified subgroup analyses, multivariable modelling, and potential stratification by center, we aimed to recruit approximately 700 patients in total, with a balanced distribution across the five participating hospital systems. Ultimately, 700 patients were approached, and 582 provided complete data (response rate 83.1%), yielding a near-proportional contribution from each center (range: 108–124 participants per hospital).

Sampling Procedure

A quota sampling method was employed to ensure a balanced representation across the five hospital systems. Eligible patients were identified from daily admission lists and surgical ward registries. All patients who met the inclusion criteria were consecutively invited to participate until the predetermined quota for each hospital, aligned with the overall target sample size of 700, was filled. This process resulted in the recruitment of a near-proportional number of participants from each center (range: 108–124 participants per hospital).

Recruitment Flow

A total of 700 patients were approached; 582 provided complete responses (response rate 83.1%), with relatively balanced distribution across the five hospital systems.

Variables and Operational Definitions

Primary Outcomes

A continuous score derived from the 25-item Patient Rights Awareness Questionnaire (PRAQ), with three response options (“True”, “False”, “I don’t know”). Only correct answers were scored as 1; both incorrect answers and “I don’t know” were scored as 0 to prevent inflation from guessing, consistent with standard scoring of knowledge tests (total score range 0–25). For descriptive purposes, scores were categorized as low (0–10), moderate (11–19), and high (20–25). Patient rights awareness was defined as the patient’s knowledge and understanding of the rights established under the Iranian Patient Bill of Rights.

Healthcare Team Compliance Score: A continuous score (theoretical range: 10–50) measured from a 10-item patient-reported questionnaire. Patients indicate the frequency with which they experienced specific compliance behaviors (e.g., “My surgeon explained the benefits and risks of my surgery in a way I could understand”) on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = Never to 5 = Always). This score reflects the patient’s subjective perception of compliance, not an objective external assessment. Perceived compliance refers to patients’ subjective evaluation of the extent to which healthcare professionals respected and implemented patient rights during care delivery.

Secondary Outcomes

Awareness Subscales: The total awareness score is subdivided into four domains derived from the national PBR: 1) Right to Informed Consent (6 items, score 0–6), 2) Right to Privacy and Confidentiality (5 items, score 0–5), 3) Right to Access Information (7 items, score 0–7), and 4) Right to Respect and Dignity (7 items, score 0–7).

Clinical and Sociodemographic Covariates: Patient: Age (years), sex (male/female), education level (categorized), socioeconomic status (using a standardized index), type of surgery (elective/emergency), length of hospital stay (days), number of comorbidities, ASA physical status classification (I-V), and number of previous hospitalizations in the last 5 years.

Data Collection Instruments and Psychometric Validation

Questionnaire Development

Due to the absence of a validated Persian instrument specifically aligned with the Iranian Patient Bill of Rights (PBR) for surgical inpatients, we created a new structured questionnaire in Persian. The development of the items was based directly on the official national PBR document, supplemented by consultations with experts in the field. The final instrument comprised two main scales: (1) the Patient Rights Awareness Questionnaire (PRAQ) with 25 items, and (2) the Healthcare Team Compliance Scale (HTCS) with 10 items. Both the Persian and English versions of the questionnaire can be found in Supplementary File 1.

A. Patient Rights Awareness Questionnaire (PRAQ)

The PRAQ comprises 25 knowledge-based items aligned with the Iranian Patient Bill of Rights, each with three response options (“True”, “False”, “I don’t know”). Correct responses were scored 1; incorrect responses and “I don’t know” were both scored 0. This binary scoring approach follows best practice for knowledge assessments to avoid score inflation from guessing. A sensitivity analysis modeling “I don’t know” as a separate category (0/0.5/1 scoring) yielded virtually identical results in both descriptive statistics and regression models. Total score range: 0–25 (higher = greater awareness). The four predefined domains were: Informed Consent (6 items), Privacy & Confidentiality (5 items), Access to Information (7 items), and Respect & Dignity (7 items).

B. Healthcare Team Compliance Scale (HTCS)

This scale measured patients’ subjective perceptions of compliance, not an objective assessment. It was a continuous score (theoretical range: 10–50) derived from a 10-item, patient-reported questionnaire. Patients indicated the frequency with which they believed they experienced specific PBR-related behaviors (e.g., “My surgeon explained the benefits and risks…”) on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = Never to 5 = Always).

Scoring: The total score was the sum of responses to all 10 items, yielding a continuous variable with a theoretical range of 10 to 50. A higher score indicates a higher perceived level of compliance.

Psychometric Evaluation

The psychometric properties of the questionnaires were evaluated in our sample, and the results indicated good overall reliability and validity. Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) revealed excellent model fit, with CFI values between 0.93 and 0.95, RMSEA between 0.049 and 0.055, and high internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.83–0.91 for HTCS and KR-20 = 0.83 for PRAQ). These results suggest that the instrument is both reliable and well-suited for use in the target population.

Face and Content Validity

The expert panel consisted of 10 specialists, including three medical ethicists, two health services researchers, two surgeons, one nursing faculty member, one patient rights officer, and one psychometrician. All experts had at least five years of professional or academic experience in healthcare quality, ethics, patient rights, or questionnaire development. The I-CVI ranged from 0.80 to 1.00, and the overall content validity index (S-CVI/Ave) was 0.94, indicating excellent content validity. Items with an I-CVI < 0.78 (n = 3) were revised based on expert feedback.

Pilot Testing

Pilot testing was conducted with 30 surgical patients to assess clarity and feasibility, leading to minor adjustments in wording. The test-retest reliability, conducted with 20 patients at a one-week interval, demonstrated good stability, with ICC values of 0.77 for PRAQ (95% CI 0.62–0.88) and 0.81 for HTCS (95% CI 0.66–0.90). The average completion time for the questionnaire was 16 minutes.

Construct Validity (EFA & CFA)

To establish the construct validity of the instrument, we employed both Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) and Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA). The sample (n = 582) was randomly split into two subsamples: EFA (n = 290) and CFA (n = 292). EFA using principal axis factoring with oblimin rotation showed that the PRAQ had a four-factor structure consistent with the four domains of the national Patient Bill of Rights (PBR): Informed Consent, Privacy & Confidentiality, Access to Information, and Respect & Dignity. These factors accounted for 64.3% of the variance, with factor loadings ranging from 0.42 to 0.78. For the HTCS, a one-factor solution was identified, explaining 59.2% of the variance, with loadings between 0.58 and 0.81.

CFA on the second subsample confirmed adequate model fit for both scales: for PRAQ (4-factor model), the fit indices were χ2/df = 2.1, CFI = 0.93, TLI = 0.92, RMSEA = 0.055 (90% CI 0.047–0.063), and SRMR = 0.048. For HTCS (1-factor model), CFI = 0.95, TLI = 0.94, RMSEA = 0.049, and SRMR = 0.041. These results suggest that the model fits well according to widely accepted psychometric standards.

Criterion Validity

Criterion validity was assessed by correlating the PRAQ and HTCS scores with an external measure of patient satisfaction with care communication. The moderate correlations obtained, r = 0.41 (p < 0.001) for PRAQ and r = 0.57 (p < 0.001) for HTCS, support the validity of the scales in measuring patient rights awareness and healthcare team compliance.

Reliability and Inter-rater Reliability

For internal consistency, the PRAQ (binary items) had a KR-20 of 0.83, while the HTCS (Likert items) had a Cronbach’s α of 0.91. Inter-rater reliability, assessed through interviewer-administered structured interviews (n = 40), showed substantial to excellent agreement, with Cohen’s κ = 0.79 for categorical items and ICC(2,1) = 0.84 for continuous HTCS scores.

Data Collection Procedures

Data collection took place during patients’ postoperative hospital stay to capture their experiences while still fresh, minimizing recall bias. Interviews were scheduled after patients were deemed clinically stable, typically between the second and fifth postoperative day for elective procedures and between the third and seventh day for emergency procedures. All interviews were conducted face-to-face in a quiet, private room to ensure confidentiality and minimize distractions. Prior to each interview, participants were informed that their responses would remain anonymous, would not be shared with treating clinicians, and would have no influence on their care. Interviews were conducted by research assistants who were independent of the clinical teams.

Data Collectors and Training

Data were collected by three trained research assistants with master’s degrees in public health, ensuring independence from the clinical teams. The research assistants underwent a 4-hour standardized training session covering the study protocol, ethical principles, and interview techniques. Competency was assessed through role-playing exercises.

Minimizing Bias

To minimize bias, we used a standardized script for introducing the study and administering the questionnaire. While complete blinding was not feasible during data collection, the data analysts were blinded to participant identities during analysis. Additionally, non-responders were followed up with a neutral reminder to improve response rates.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 27.0) and R (Version 4.3.2). Descriptive statistics were used to summarize continuous and categorical variables, and bivariate analyses were conducted using appropriate tests (e.g., t-tests, ANOVA, χ2 tests). Because the study included only five hospital systems, multilevel or random-effects modeling was not employed, as estimation of random effects and variance components is generally unreliable with a very small number of clusters. Instead, hospital sector (private vs. public/university) was included as a covariate in the multivariable regression models to account for major institutional differences across participating hospitals. Multicollinearity was assessed using the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF), and model assumptions were checked through residual plots and statistical tests.

Multivariable Modeling and Sensitivity Analyses

Two prespecified multivariable linear regression models were estimated to predict the Patient Rights Awareness Score (PRAQ) and the Healthcare Team Compliance Score (HTCS). Sensitivity analyses, including logistic regression for dichotomized outcomes and robustness checks for influential observations, were conducted to ensure the reliability of the results. Multiple testing correction was applied using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure to control for type I error inflation in exploratory analyses.

Results

During the six-month study period (January 1 to June 30, 2024), a total of 700 eligible surgical inpatients across five hospital systems in southern Iran were approached for participation. Of these, 582 patients completed the survey, yielding an overall response rate of 83.1%. The reasons for non-participation included refusal (n=72), early discharge (n=28), and fatigue or pain (n=18) at the time of the interview. Recruitment across hospitals was balanced, with patient numbers per site ranging from 108 to 124, ensuring minimal cluster-level imbalance.

The sociodemographic and clinical characteristics of the 582 participants are summarized in Table 1. The mean age of participants was 46.8 ± 15.7 years, with 54.6% of them being female. Approximately one-third (31.3%) had only primary education or were illiterate, while 26.1% had a university degree or higher. Most patients lived in urban areas (68.4%), were married (79.7%), and underwent elective surgery (68.0%). The average hospital stay was 5.4 ± 3.2 days, with a mean of 1.6 ± 1.3 comorbidities. Notably, 20.6% were classified as ASA physical status III–IV, and 28.9% reported three or more hospitalizations in the past five years.

Patient Awareness of Their Rights

The overall awareness of patient rights, as measured by the PRAQ, showed a mean score of 16.2 ± 4.8 out of 25, with a range of 4–24. Specifically, 26.3% of patients had high awareness (≥80% of the maximum score), 54.8% had moderate awareness, and 18.9% demonstrated low awareness (Table 2). Domain-level analysis revealed variability across rights categories. The Access to Information domain had the highest mean score (5.0 ± 1.6 out of 7), while Privacy & Confidentiality had the lowest (3.0 ± 1.2 out of 5). Awareness regarding informed consent-related rights was moderate (mean 4.1 ± 1.4 out of 6), and the Respect & Dignity domain scored 4.1 ± 1.7 out of 7.

Item-level analysis (see Supplementary S1 Table) showed that four critical items had notably low awareness: the right to have a companion during procedures (28.9% correct), advance directives and decision-making capacity (31.3% correct), disclosure of research involvement (32.8% correct), and the right to access or copy medical records (37.5% correct). These items also had the highest frequency of “I don’t know” responses (55.2% to 62.2%), indicating significant uncertainty and a lack of exposure to these rights in practice.

Patients’ Perceived Healthcare Team Compliance

The Healthcare Team Compliance Scale (HTCS) showed a mean total score of 37.8 ± 7.9 out of 50, which corresponds to a mean item score of 3.78 ± 0.79 on a 5-point Likert scale, indicating that patients perceived their rights as being respected “often,” though with considerable variability across individuals and domains. The highest levels of perceived compliance were reported in respect for dignity and polite treatment (mean 4.1 ± 1.0, 71.5% rating “often/always”), non-discriminatory care (mean 4.0 ± 1.1, 67.7% “often/always”), and clear explanations of diagnosis and treatment (mean 4.0 ± 1.1). However, items related to privacy protections and access to medical records received lower scores, such as companion presence during procedures (mean 3.2 ± 1.4, 38.5% “often/always”) and access to medical records (mean 3.3 ± 1.4, 39.5% “often/always”). These patterns aligned with the gaps in awareness observed in the PRAQ (Table 3).

Factors Associated With Awareness and Perceived Compliance

Bivariate analysis found that higher awareness was significantly associated with younger age (Spearman’s ρ = -0.29, p<0.001), female sex (p=0.002), higher education (p<0.001), elective surgery (p<0.001), and care in private hospitals (p<0.001). Perceived compliance showed similar demographic patterns and was moderately correlated with awareness (Spearman’s ρ = 0.46, p<0.001).

Multivariable regression analysis confirmed these associations (Table 4). Higher education, elective surgery, and private hospital admission were the strongest independent predictors of both awareness and perceived compliance. For example, patients with university education scored 5.68 points higher in awareness and 7.52 points higher in perceived compliance compared to those with primary education. Private hospital patients scored 2.81 points higher in awareness and 6.94 points higher in compliance, independent of sociodemographic factors. A significant interaction was observed between type of surgery and awareness level in predicting compliance (p for interaction = 0.004). Among emergency surgery patients, higher awareness was paradoxically associated with lower perceived compliance (adjusted β = -0.58 per 5-point increase in awareness, 95% CI: -0.98 to -0.18), suggesting heightened expectations and critical appraisal during urgent or stressful clinical encounters.

Sensitivity Analyses

The robustness of these findings was supported by several sensitivity analyses, including multiple imputation of missing responses, alternative treatment of “I don’t know” answers, removal of influential cases, and the Benjamini-Hochberg correction for multiple testing. None of these analyses materially changed the results.

Discussion

This multicenter study provides insights into the relationship between patient rights awareness and perceived healthcare compliance in surgical settings in southern Iran. Several findings have important implications for patient-centered care and healthcare delivery.

The moderate level of patient rights awareness observed in this study is consistent with previous reports from similar healthcare settings. As reported by Agrawal et al., 18 awareness varied across sociodemographic groups. However, our participants demonstrated slightly higher awareness levels than those in settings with limited patient-rights education infrastructure. 19 The strong association between education and awareness further highlights the role of health literacy in enabling patients to understand and exercise their rights, consistent with previous studies.11,20

The significant difference between elective and emergency surgery patients suggests that the context of care strongly influences patient engagement. Higher awareness among elective patients may reflect greater opportunities for pre-admission counseling and information exchange. In contrast, emergency patients often face time constraints and acute stress, which can limit their ability to process and retain information. 21 These findings emphasize the need for tailored strategies to improve communication and rights awareness in emergency care settings.

Although overall perceptions of compliance with patient rights were favorable, important shortcomings were identified, particularly in privacy and confidentiality. Similar challenges have been reported across different healthcare settings.22-24 Low ratings for companion presence during procedures and access to medical records indicate areas where operational pressures and infrastructural limitations may hinder adherence to ethical standards.

These findings are consistent with Talhaoğlu’s qualitative study, which identified workload pressures and insufficient ethics training as barriers to effective privacy protection. 22 Likewise, Hartigan et al. 23 and Koskimies et al. 24 highlighted the influence of environmental conditions and organizational practices on privacy implementation. Together, these studies suggest that improving compliance requires not only individual professional commitment but also organizational and structural changes.

The moderate correlation between awareness and perceived compliance suggests that these constructs are related. This relationship may have implications for healthcare service improvement. As documented in regional investigations,12,25 better-informed patients may possess enhanced capabilities to recognize and assess healthcare team behaviors, while providers might adapt their practices in response to more engaged patients. However, the counterintuitive finding that more aware emergency patients reported diminished compliance reveals the intricate nature of this relationship in high-acuity clinical situations.

Several explanations may account for this finding. More knowledgeable patients may have higher expectations regarding communication and rights observance. Alternatively, emergency and trauma care environments often involve time constraints, rapid decision-making, limited privacy, and reduced opportunities for detailed communication, which may negatively influence patients’ perceptions of healthcare team compliance. In addition, these structural realities may limit opportunities for healthcare teams to fully operationalize certain patient rights, such as comprehensive information sharing, participation in decision-making, and privacy protection, particularly when urgent clinical interventions are required. The enduring disconnect between ethical recognition and practical implementation, noted by Al-Saadi et al., 26 emphasizes the necessity for organizational reinforcement of patient rights principles.

Although socioeconomic status was not directly measured in this study, the strong effects of education level and hospital type, both of which may partially reflect broader socioeconomic differences, suggest that social determinants likely influence how patients perceive compliance with their rights. Patients with higher educational attainment may have greater health literacy and greater confidence navigating the healthcare system, potentially shaping their communication experiences and evaluations of professional compliance.27,28

This pattern highlights how patient-provider interactions are shaped by broader social determinants. The parallel deficiencies observed in both awareness and perceived compliance, particularly regarding privacy-related rights and information accessibility, suggest the presence of systemic rather than individual-level challenges. This interpretation is further reinforced by the concerning pattern identified among emergency surgery patients, highlighting the critical intersection between patient expectations and systemic vulnerabilities during high-stress healthcare encounters.

These results align with and extend previous research conducted in similar healthcare environments,25,29-31 while providing enhanced methodological rigor through multivariable modeling and comprehensive sensitivity analyses. The relatively elevated awareness and compliance levels detected in our investigation may reflect institutional policies promoting patient education and standardized consent processes at the participating sites, suggesting potential avenues for quality improvement initiatives in comparable settings.

The consistent international evidence regarding educational attainment as a determinant of patient rights awareness across diverse healthcare systems underscores the universal importance of health literacy in patient empowerment.14,32,33 Our findings corroborate and extend this understanding, demonstrating that educational disparities significantly influence patients’ comprehension of rights related to informed consent, privacy, and information access. This pattern reinforces the necessity for tailored educational and communication approaches, particularly during preoperative counseling when comprehension is paramount for genuine informed consent.

Although awareness of informed consent rights was higher than some other domains, the observed level remains concerning given the central ethical and legal importance of informed consent in surgical care. Possible explanations include reliance on standardized consent forms without sufficient discussion, variability in communication practices, limited patient health literacy, and time constraints during preoperative preparation. Structured preoperative educational sessions, decision aids, plain-language consent materials, and teach-back communication techniques may help improve patient understanding and engagement.

The identification of specific rights with critically low awareness, such as companion presence during procedures, advance directives, research disclosure, and medical record access, provides precise targets for educational interventions and system redesign. These areas represent not merely knowledge gaps but potential vulnerabilities in the patient care experience that warrant prioritized attention in quality improvement initiatives.

The observed differences between private and public hospitals may also reflect unmeasured organizational characteristics. These include staffing levels, provider workload, continuity of care, communication time, institutional culture, and availability of patient-centered services.

Clinical Implications

The findings have several practical implications for clinicians. Surgical teams should prioritize structured informed-consent discussions, particularly for patients with lower educational attainment. Privacy-protection practices should be reinforced through regular staff training and environmental modifications. Emergency surgical units may benefit from standardized communication protocols that ensure essential patient-rights information is communicated despite time constraints. Additionally, clinicians should be aware that patients’ perceptions of compliance may be shaped by their baseline awareness levels, and addressing knowledge gaps through patient education may enhance the overall care experience. Healthcare organizations should also implement ongoing patient-rights education programs using plain-language educational materials, digital resources, and admission-based orientation strategies to improve awareness among vulnerable patient groups.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings of this study. First, the cross-sectional design precludes establishing causal relationships between the identified factors and patient awareness or perceived compliance. Second, data collection occurred during hospitalization, which may have introduced social desirability bias, as patients might have been hesitant to criticize their care providers while still under their treatment. Although several measures were implemented to reduce social desirability bias, including independent interviewers, confidential data collection, and explicit reassurance regarding the absence of any impact on care, some participants may still have provided more favorable assessments of healthcare team compliance. Third, the study was conducted in a single region of Iran (southern provinces), which may limit the generalizability of findings to other regions with different cultural or healthcare contexts. Fourth, we relied on patient self-report for both awareness and compliance measures, which is subject to recall bias and subjective interpretation. The compliance measure specifically captured patients’ perceptions rather than objective compliance assessments. Future studies should combine patient-reported measures with objective assessments of compliance, such as direct observation, medical record audits, or independent quality evaluations. Fifth, although we employed rigorous psychometric validation for our instruments, the questionnaires were newly developed for this study, and further validation in other populations is warranted. Sixth, we were unable to account for all potential confounding variables, such as detailed socioeconomic status indicators and specific provider- and organizational-level characteristics, including staffing levels, staff-to-patient ratios, workload, professional experience, and institutional resources, which may have influenced both patient awareness and perceived compliance. Finally, the moderate response rate (83.1%), while acceptable, raises the possibility of non-response bias if non-participants differed systematically from those who completed the study. Despite these limitations, the multicenter design, robust sample size, and comprehensive validation of measures strengthen the credibility of our findings.

Conclusion

This multicenter study reveals significant gaps in patient awareness of their rights and varying levels of perceived healthcare compliance within surgical settings in southern Iran. The findings highlight critical areas for improvement in health system quality and policy reform. Although patients demonstrated moderate overall awareness of their rights, substantial gaps remain regarding privacy, confidentiality, and access to information, especially concerning companion presence during procedures, advance directives, research disclosure, and medical record access.

The parallel deficiencies in both awareness and perceived compliance, particularly around privacy-related rights and information access, point to systemic challenges. The concerning finding that more aware emergency surgery patients reported lower compliance underscores the role of patient expectations and the heightened vulnerability of this group during stressful healthcare encounters.

From a health policy and clinical practice perspective, several targeted interventions may address the identified gaps. These include structured privacy-protection workshops for healthcare personnel, communication-skills training focused on informed consent discussions, emergency communication protocols for high-acuity settings, standardized patient-rights orientation sessions during hospital admission, and streamlined systems facilitating patient access to medical records. Such targeted initiatives, combined with digital health solutions for patient education, may help reduce the identified awareness–compliance gaps and strengthen patient-centered surgical care in Iran and similar healthcare contexts.
