Design matters: structural variation in experimental huts significantly alters entomological endpoints in insecticide treated net (ITN) evaluation –a comparative experimental hut trial using a latin square design in Tanzania and Côte d’Ivoire
Authors: Alphonce A. Assenga, John Bradley, Ludovic P. Ahoua Alou, Raphael N’Guessan, Alphonsine A. Koffi, Graham Small, Janneke Snetselaar, Jason Moore, Sarah J. Moore
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundExperimental huts are the gold standard for the semi-field evaluation of insecticide-treated nets (ITNs) but the design of the huts used varies markedly between sites. Structural differences may influence mosquito behaviour – and, consequently, the measured efficacy of ITNs. This raises concerns about the comparability, validity, and policy relevance of trial outcomes. We assessed the influence of four commonly used hut designs on entomological endpoints central to ITN evaluations.MethodsA 126-night comparative trial was conducted in Tanzania, using 18 experimental huts in two contiguous 9 × 9 Latin squares. Four hut types–East African, West African, Ifakara, and Rapley–were tested with PRONet Duo, Interceptor® G2, MAGNet®, and untreated nets. Sleepers and treatments were rotated throughout the trial. The primary outcome was 72-h mortality (M72); secondary outcomes were mosquito density, blood-feeding success, and exiting. Endpoint estimates for each hut type were compared using East African huts as the reference. Non-inferiority analysis of PRONet Duo in each hut type were compared with findings from a sister trial conducted exclusively in West African huts in West Africa.ResultsA total of 22611 Anopheles gambiae s.l. (99.9% An. arabiensis) were collected. Rapley and Ifakara huts captured over six times more mosquitoes than East African huts (rate ratio [RR] = 6.50 and 6.22, respectively; both p < 0.0001), whereas West African huts caught 88% fewer mosquitoes (RR = 0.12, p < 0.0001). West African huts recorded the highest mortality (70%) and blood feeding (15%) whilst the other hut types recorded 40–46% mortality and 3–8% blood feeding. All hut designs and the West African sister trial detected the same direction of effect between PRONet Duo and Interceptor® G2 and predicted non-inferiority of PRONet Duo, although smaller sample sizes limited statistical certainty in East African and West African huts in Tanzania.ConclusionsExperimental hut design substantially affected key entomological outcomes, influencing both the magnitude of measured efficacy of ITNs and the statistical power of trials. Whilst relative product performance was broadly consistent across designs, absolute values varied widely, underscoring the need for greater standardisation of hut architecture in ITN evaluations. Harmonising experimental hut designs and reporting standards would strengthen cross-site comparability, improving the reliability of vector control product assessments, and enhancing the evidence base for global malaria control policy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12936-026-06066-w.

Introduction

Experimental huts are the gold standard for assessing vector control tools including insecticide-treated net (ITN), indoor residual spray (IRS) and spatial emanator (SE) products under semi-field conditions that simulate real-world settings. They provide a robust method for evaluating the bioefficacy of vector control products while reducing the need for cluster randomised controlled trials of each new product within a product class [1–5]. Experimental huts are routinely used in non-inferiority trials to demonstrate that a new product is similarly effective against mosquitoes as a first-in-class product with established clinical efficacy [6] within a predetermined non-inferiority margin [7, 8].

Originally developed in the 1940s and used to evaluate the efficacy of IRS products [9] during the Global Malaria Eradication Program [10], experimental huts are now considered the gold-standard approach for assessing the bioefficacy of ITNs, IRS and SE products as part of dossiers used for World Health Organisation (WHO) prequalification [11, 12], a prerequisite for procurement by the United Nation agencies for deployment in malaria endemic regions [13].

The design of experimental huts has been standardised to control factors such as internal volume, mosquito entry and exit points, and the absence of furniture. Huts are designed so that mosquitoes that enter are retained and can be more easily recaptured than in local village homes. Ant control methods are incorporated to minimise mosquito predation and loss from scavenging ants. Experimental hut trials are designed to measure key entomological endpoints, primarily mosquito mortality, as well as blood-feeding, deterrence, and induced exophily [2, 3]. However, there are several experimental hut designs currently in use which differ in size and in the area and position of mosquito entry and exit points [11]. Evidence suggests that these structural variations can influence mosquito behaviour and, consequently, the efficacy outcomes measured [2, 14]. However, the use of different hut types across geographical regions in sub-Saharan Africa, with heterogeneous vector bionomics and insecticide-resistance levels complicates the interpretation of trial data, and it is unclear whether observed differences in the outcomes of different trials reflect true vector population differences or are to some degree an effect of hut design. To address this gap in our knowledge, we conducted a comparative evaluation of four experimental hut designs in one site in Tanzania using pyrethroid and chlorfenapyr–pyrethroid ITNs and benchmarked the findings against a contemporaneous sister trial conducted in West Africa [15]. The study aimed to (1) assess how each hut type affects key entomological endpoints and (2) determine whether each hut design produces consistent estimates of the relative efficacy of a second-in-class chlorfenapyr–pyrethroid ITN compared with the first-in-class product, using a predefined non-inferiority margin of 7%.

Methods

Study locations

Tanzania: The trial was conducted in Lupiro Village (8.385ºS, 36.670ºE) in the Kilombero river valley, a large irrigated rice-growing area with year-round production of Anopheles gambiae sensu lato (s.l), of which, > 99.9% are Anopheles arabiensis. This population is resistant at the 1 × discriminating concentration to all classes of pyrethroids, but is susceptible to chlorfenapyr [15]. Four experimental hut types were evaluated at this site (Table 1). Côte d’Ivoire: The parallel trial was conducted at the M'Bé field site (5.209 W, 7.970 N), another irrigated rice ecosystem producing year-round An. gambiae s.l., of which > 99.8% are An. coluzzii. Study arms

Although a total of 18 net arms were tested across the full trial, only the seven Prequalified (PQ) listed [16] arms are presented in this analysis: 1) Unwashed PRONet Duo (VKA Polymers, India); 2) 20 × washed PRONet Duo; 3) Unwashed Interceptor® G2 (BASF, USA); 4) 20 × washed Interceptor® G2; 5) Unwashed MAGNet® (VKA Polymers, India); 6) 20 × washed MAGNet® and 7) Negative control was an untreated SafiNet (A to Z, Tanzania).

PRONet Duo contains bifenthrin (PYR) and chlorfenapyr, while Interceptor® G2 contains alpha-cypermethrin (PYR) and chlorfenapyr (CFP), with Interceptor® G2 being the first in class PYR-CFP ITN [15, 17–19]. MAGNet® contains alpha-cypermethrin but no CFP, and it was included in this trial to demonstrate the additional benefit of CFP. A negative control (untreated net) was used to assess the quality of the study. All trial procedures followed WHO guidelines [11, 20].

Study design

In Tanzania, 18 huts were used to evaluate the four hut designs: four East African huts, four West African huts, four Rapley huts, and six Ifakara huts. In the Côte d’Ivoire trial all 18 huts were of the West African design, and the same 18 experimental arms were tested. To ensure statistical power 14 experimental rounds of nine nights (N = 126 nights) were conducted in Tanzania and 12 (N = 108 nights) in Côte d’Ivoire.

At both sites, the trial used two simultaneous 9 × 9 Latin square designs (LSDs), using 18 huts per site: nine allocated to unwashed ITN arms and nine to washed ITN arms. Within each LSD, one arm served as the untreated net negative control, five arms were investigational products, and three were active comparators. Eighteen human volunteers and 18 net arms were used in the 18 huts. Both volunteers and net arms were systematically rotated using the LSD to minimise bias from hut design or inter-individual differences in attractiveness [2]. Volunteers rotated nightly using a pre-prepared roster. Each net type remained in a hut for nine nights per round before rotating. Between rounds, huts were cleaned and aired for one day to prevent carry-over of insecticide residues.

Endpoints

All products were evaluated against wild, free-flying pyrethroid-resistant Anopheles gambiae s.l. The primary endpoint was mosquito mortality measured at the 72-h holding time; secondary endpoints were blood-feeding, density and exiting.

Experimental huts methods

Before testing, both sites conducted a two-night baseline assessment using untreated nets with sleepers, to confirm that the volunteers had similar levels of mosquito attractiveness. Hut performance was further evaluated through a retention experiment in which 20 marked mosquitoes were released into each hut at 19:00 h and recaptured the following morning at 06:00 h. Additionally, approximately ten dead mosquitoes were placed in each hut overnight to assess potential predation by ants.

Volunteers entered the huts and went to bed under their bednet at 19:00 h and remained inside until 06:00 h the following morning. Each morning, mosquitoes were collected from inside of the net, followed by dead mosquitoes on the floor, resting mosquitoes on walls and ceilings and those from the exit traps or veranda were collected using aspiration. Mosquitoes were classified as dead-fed, dead-unfed, alive-fed, and alive-unfed. The alive mosquitoes were held for up to 120 h although the primary outcome was mortality at 72 h in line with WHO guidance, in a temperature-controlled room and provided with access to a 10% sugar solution to assess delayed mortality. All nets, including the untreated control, were holed with six 4 cm × 4 cm holes per WHO guidelines to permit mosquito access for blood-feeding. Blood-meal analysis was conducted on a sub-sample of blood-fed mosquitoes to determine host blood source; results are presented in the Supplementary Material 1.

Data processing

Data were recorded on paper forms and then digitised using Open Data Kit (ODK) on tablets with the ODK Collect application in Tanzania and entered into Excel in Côte d’Ivoire. Data cleaning and analysis were performed in STATA 17 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA). Outliers and observation balance were assessed using graphical and tabular methods, and balancing was confirmed to be consistent with the study design.

Data analysis

Mixed effects logistic regression was used for the analysis of binary variables (mortality, blood feeding, and exiting) and mixed effects negative binomial logistic regression was used for the analysis of mosquito density. In each model, fixed effects for hut type, sleeper, net type, and day were included, along with a random effect for hut. For each analysis, the models were used to estimate means for each hut averaging over all the covariates in the model. For non-inferiority, the odds ratio used as the non-inferiority margin comes from previously published guidelines [7].

Sample size and study power

Sample size was estimated for both trials using simulation-based power analysis in R statistical software before the trials were initiated. Post-hoc power calculations of actual study power were also performed using simulation-based power analysis in R version 4.3.2 http://www.r-project.org using lme4 package [2]. One thousand (1000) simulations for generalized linear mixed models were run using a Latin square design for 81 nights of data collection with one hut per treatment arm. An estimated mean number of 7 An. arabiensis mosquitoes per night, variability of 0.75 was used for hut night and an estimated 72-h mortality of 44% for Interceptor® G2 based on previous data [21]

Results

Hut designs impact mosquito density

A total of 22,611 Anopheles gambiae s.l. were collected over the 126 nights in the Tanzania trial and 6338 over the 108 nights in the Côte d’Ivoire trial. PCR tests confirmed that 99.9% of the collected Anopheles gambiae s.l. were Anopheles arabiensis in Tanzania and 100% Anopheles coluzzi in Côte d’Ivoire. Mosquito retention in Tanzania was 75% for East African, 69% for Ifakara, 74% for Rapley and 79% for West African huts.

Mosquito entry varied markedly by hut type (Table 2). East African huts collected a mean of 2.30 mosquitoes per night, whereas both Ifakara and Rapley huts captured substantially higher numbers, with model-estimated means of 15.83 and 16.55 mosquitoes per night, respectively. These corresponded to rate ratios of 6.22 (95% CI 5.11–7.56) for Ifakara huts and 6.50 (95% CI 5.25–8.04) for Rapley huts compared with East African huts (p < 0.0001). In contrast, West African huts collected significantly fewer mosquitoes, with a model-estimated mean of 0.31 per night and a rate ratio of 0.12 (95% CI 0.09–0.16; p < 0.0001). These findings demonstrate marked differences in mosquito entry rates across hut designs likely due to the surface area of entry points for mosquitoes (Table 1). In Côte d’Ivoire, West African huts caught a mean of 6.78 mosquitoes per night (Fig. 1d), considerably higher than the 0.31 observed in the Tanzania trial. It is not possible to determine whether this difference reflects underlying vector bionomics of Anopheles arabiensis and Anopheles coluzzii or may be due to diversion effect in which higher mosquito entry into other hut types reduced capture rates in West African huts. Hut designs impact mosquito mortality

Mosquito mortality at 72 h holding for all net types combined (ITNs and the untreated control net) differed across hut types, although three designs produced broadly similar estimates (Table 4). East African huts showed a 46% model-estimated mortality, which was comparable to Ifakara huts (46%; OR 1.12, 95% CI 0.80–1.57; p = 0.52) and Rapley huts (40%; OR 0.74, 95% CI 0.51–1.07; p = 0.11). In contrast, West African huts demonstrated substantially higher mortality, with an estimated 70% mortality and more than three-fold higher odds of death compared with East African huts (OR 3.38, 95% CI 1.95–5.84; p < 0.0001) (Fig. 1a; cf. Fig. 2a). Although mosquito populations in Côte d’Ivoire are substantially more resistant, M72 reached 53%, higher than in the East African, Ifakara or Rapley huts tested in Tanzania. These results indicate that hut design can markedly influence observed absolute mortality outcomes. Hut designs impact mosquito blood feeding

Blood-feeding rates for all net types combined differed substantially across hut designs (Table 5). East African huts showed a model-estimated mean blood-feeding rate of 8%, while Ifakara huts had a significantly lower blood-feeding rate of 3% (OR = 0.41, 95% CI 0.30–0.58; p < 0.0001). Rapley huts demonstrated a similar feeding rate to East African huts at 7% (OR = 1.10, 95% CI 0.78–1.53; p = 0.59). In contrast, West African huts exhibited markedly higher blood-feeding rate of 15% (OR = 2.44, 95% CI 1.41–4.24; p = 0.0015). Blood feeding rates in West African huts were similar between the two trials, with 22% recorded in Côte d’Ivoire (Fig. 1b). These findings indicate a strong effect of hut design on mosquito blood feeding success, with Ifakara huts having lower feeding and West African huts having elevated feeding success compared with that in the East African design. Hut designs impact mosquito exit

Mosquito exit rates for all nets combined differed significantly between hut designs (Table 6). East African huts showed an estimated exit rate of 87% and served as the reference. Ifakara huts demonstrated a slightly higher exit rate, with a model-estimated mean of 92% and a 69% increase in the odds of exit compared with East African huts (OR = 1.69; 95% CI 1.28–2.22; p = 0.0002). Rapley huts exhibited a similar exit rate to East African huts, with an estimated 89% exit and no statistically significant difference (OR = 1.22; 95% CI 0.91–1.63; p = 0.18). In contrast, West African huts showed a substantially lower exit rate, with a model-estimated mean of 72% and markedly reduced odds of mosquitoes exiting relative to East African huts (OR = 0.31; 95% CI 0.19–0.49; p < 0.0001). This was lower still in the trial in Côte d’Ivoire at 54% (Figure 1c). Similar results were seen when the control nets were analysed separately to eliminate any product specific effects on exophily (Supplementary Material 1). Differences in mosquito exiting behaviour linked to hut design likely explain the variation in mortality and blood feeding between the three hut types with exit traps (East African, Rapley, Ifakara), where mosquitoes can exit but not re-enter huts, and the West African huts, where mosquitoes can continue to interact with the sleeper and the ITN throughout the night. Comparison of relative product efficacy in different hut types with the results of the Côte d’Ivoire reference trial

The relative performance of the ITNs was broadly consistent across hut types and aligned with the Côte d’Ivoire reference trial, with PRONet Duo giving the highest mortality, followed by Interceptor® G2 (Figure 2a). Both PYR-CFP ITNs generally out-performed the pyrethroid only net. These differences were most clearly observed in the Côte d’Ivoire reference trial and in the Ifakara huts, likely reflecting the larger sample sizes in these settings—six Ifakara huts were used compared with only four of each alternative hut type in Tanzania. In the Tanzanian trial greater uncertainty in the M72 estimates was evident for the East African and West African huts, likely driven by lower mosquito densities in these hut types.

Blood feeding was reduced relative to the control across all treated nets, with the largest reduction observed for PRONet Duo in the Côte d’Ivoire reference trial (Figure 2b). In Tanzania a similar pattern was observed in the West African huts, where no blood fed mosquitoes were recorded for PRONet Duo; however, differences in blood feeding between products was less distinct in the three hut types that have exit traps, which showed uniformly low blood-feeding rates.

Non-inferiority analysis of relative product efficacy in different hut types with the results of the West African reference trial

Regarding the primary endpoint of M72 the relative performance of PRONet Duo was consistent across hut types and aligned with the Côte d’Ivoire reference trial, with PRONet Duo measuring superior mortality to Interceptor® G2 (Figure 3). The East African hut measured the same direction of effect but the result was indeterminate due to insufficient mosquitoes collected. Data from the West African huts in Tanzania could not be analysed because too few mosquitoes were caught. For the blood feeding endpoint, all hut types showed the same direction of effect as the Côte d’Ivoire reference trial. In both the reference trial and in the East African huts, PRONet Duo was superior to Interceptor® G2. In the Ifakara and Rapley huts PRONet Duo was non-inferior. However, both Rapley and Ifakara huts recorded low blood feeding in all ITN arms reducing their statistical power to detect differences between products.

Discussion

This study provides the first direct comparative evaluation of the four experimental hut designs currently included in the WHO guidance on ITN evaluation for prequalification [11] -East African, West African, Ifakara, and Rapley – in a single site, with results benchmarked against an identical and concurrent Côte d’Ivoire reference trial. By assessing critical entomological endpoints relevant to product efficacy—mosquito mortality and blood-feeding [22], as well as mosquito density (which determines statistical power) [2], and proportion of mosquitoes exiting (which affects mosquito contact with insecticide treated surfaces) [21], we demonstrate that hut architecture can substantially influence measured outcomes. While it is reassuring that all hut designs predicted the same outcome in the comparative efficacy evaluation hut design should be carefully considered when designing vector control product evaluations across sites and studies. The observed patterns in density, mortality, feeding and exiting align with structural differences in hut volume, the presence or absence of exit traps, and the size of available entry points.

Mosquito density differed markedly among hut types. Rapley and Ifakara huts collected more than six times the number of An. arabiensis than captured in East African huts, while West African huts collected only 12% of the East African hut catch. These patterns align with structural differences in the size and, for West African huts, position of available entry points, which influence host-seeking mosquito entry [23–27] (Table 1). Ifakara and Rapley huts have larger entry areas (4500 cm2 and 2600 cm2, respectively) compared to 400 cm2 in the East African huts and 240 cm2 in the West African hut, reflecting the relative densities observed. Comparable differences in density have been observed in previous studies comparing East African and Ifakara huts in Muheza, Tanzania [14], and of Ifakara versus West African huts in Benin [28]. Increasing the entry area can markedly increase mosquito catch: a recent modification of East African experimental huts in Tanzania nearly tripled mosquito entry by widening the baffle openings [27], and a study in West Africa showed that opening eave entry points in West African huts increased indoor densities by 90% compared to window slits [41] demonstrating how structural adjustments can improve the statistical power of studies.

It is important to note that the West African hut design is the most widely adopted across sub-Saharan Africa [3], with East African, Rapley and Ifakara designs used predominantly at Tanzanian sites. The generalisability of comparisons across these hut types is therefore particularly relevant for interpreting trials conducted at West African sites, which form the majority of the global experimental hut evidence base.

Given the dominance of West African hut designs across Africa and the very low mosquito entry rates observed in this study in Tanzania (0.31 mosquitoes per hut-night),in line with lower densities recorded in East Africa [14] and West Africa [28], modifications to West African entry geometry deserve serious consideration. Moving entry points from window level to eave height – where mosquitoes most naturally enter homes – and enlarging the entry opening could substantially increase mosquito capture rates to enhance the sample size of hut trials while retaining the design’s simplicity and cost-effectiveness.

It is noteworthy that the Rapley hut – one of the designs used in the present study – is structurally equivalent to a West African hut without the verandah exit trap, yet consistently captured substantially higher number of mosquitoes, which demonstrates that low-cost structural modifications to existing West African huts, without full replacement of infrastructure, can substantially improve entomological sensitivity.

It should also be noted that the 1 cm slit specification for West African hut entry points may not be uniformly applied in practice. Coleman et al. (2021) reported using a 3 cm opening, widened because a 1 cm opening yielded insufficient mosquito entry – a pragmatic modification that would not necessarily be captured in published methods sections [29]. This highlights an additional source of structural variability that standard reporting of West African hut dimensions does not currently capture, and further underscores the need for more detailed reporting of entry point geometry, and ideally photographic documentation, in all experimental hut trial publications.

Hut “openness” also affects mosquito retention. East African, Ifakara and Rapley huts use angled eave baffles which enhance mosquito retention [27, 30] In this study, mosquito retention was fairly similar across hut types but followed expected patterns, with the highest retention in the small and more closed West African huts (79%) and lowest in the largest and relatively open Ifakara huts (74%). Mosquito retention is a vital consideration in evaluations of vector control products because loss of live mosquitoes alters the denominator of both mortality and blood-feeding endpoints.

Differences in mosquito mortality and blood feeding were largely explained by hut design. West African huts demonstrated far higher mortality (70%) and blood-feeding (15%) than other designs. This has direct implications for vector control product evaluation, as low mortality or low blood feeding rates reduces the power to discriminate between interventions [2]. The elevated mortality and feeding in West African huts may be attributable to their smaller volume (13.3 m3) and to the verandah that allows mosquitoes to remain inside the hut and make repeated attempts to feed – thereby increasing contact with ITNs. Similar effects in increasing mortality ITNs were observed previously when window traps were blocked [21]. Recognising the importance of maximising mosquito interaction with insecticidal surfaces and in an attempt to make experimental hut bioassays as similar and statistically powerful as possible, Ifakara Huts [31] were redesigned to half their original size [32]. The Ifakara Ambient Chamber test was also developed to allow mosquitoes to interact with ITNs in a closed system and measure relatively higher mortality than Ifakara huts [21]. In addition, Rapley huts [33] were also adopted at the Ifakara Health Institute testing facility due to their low cost, small volume and large mosquito entry area, that has proven highly effective for IRS evaluations [20, 33].

Blood-meal analysis confirmed that 100% of blood-fed mosquitoes in this study had fed on human blood, indicating that feeding occurred on the sleeper after hut entry rather than on animals or other humans outside the hut. The low blood-feeding rates observed in the East African, Ifakara, and Rapley huts likely reflect their open-system design: high exit rates (87–92%) in these designs suggest that mosquitoes chose to leave the hut upon encountering the net rather than persisting to locate one of the six 4 cm × 4 cm holes, whereas the closed West African design compelled repeated host contact and a correspondingly higher feeding rate (15%). These control-arm blood-feeding rates are broadly consistent with previous experimental hut studies in Tanzania against wild, pyrethroid-resistant An. arabiensis: using Ifakara huts [34, 35], and using East African huts, blood-feeding rates of 26–36.5% have been reported across several trials against An. gambiae s.s., An. arabiensis and An. funestus [36–38].

East African, Ifakara, and Rapley huts all recorded lower mortality and blood feeding than West African huts because they incorporate exit traps and are therefore an open system (Table 1). Across these designs, even in the control arm, suggesting that mosquitoes encountering a net tend to leave the hut before feeding – thus reducing both blood-feeding and mortality rates. We have presented comparisons of mortality and blood feeding as odds ratios rather than absolute differences. This is because generalised linear models that estimate differences in proportions do not converge when multiple covariates are included and we need to adjust for factors such as sleeper and night of collection.

It is notable that Ifakara and Rapley huts have relatively low mortality for control nets, MAGNet® and Interceptor® G2, but much higher mortality for PRONet Duo. This could be because mosquitoes in this setting are more susceptible to bifenthrin than alpha-cymethrin [15], or could be due to chance. The susceptibility of mosquitoes to bifenthrin was not evaluated and remains a limitation of the study. However, an additional study in West Africa also showed greater mosquito mortality with PRONet Duo than Interceptor® G2 [15]. A lower exiting (50%) was observed in an IRS trial in Rapley huts using an unprotected cow where mosquitoes could freely blood feed (Lekundayo, pers comm).

The results of this study were obtained with Anopheles arabiensis, representing the dominant vector at the Lupiro site. An. funestus was also collected at this site, albeit at much lower density, and the same pattern of hut-design effects on entry was observed (Table 3), broadly mirroring the density ranking seen for An. arabiensis. The same pattern was observed with Culex mosquitoes also collected at the site at the time of the study. Similar differences in mosquito catches among hut designs have been previously reported by Massue et al. [14] and Oumbouke et al. [28]. This suggests that the structural drivers of mosquito entry identified in this study are not specific to An. arabiensis.

The study is limited by the fact that it was conducted at a single site. While it was benchmarked against a gold standard Côte d’Ivoire reference trial, a more rigorous design would involve replicating the evaluation with all four hut types across multiple locations with different mosquito populations. In addition, the use of only four to six huts of each design reduced the power of the study to measure non-inferiority when stratified by hut type.

Our findings demonstrate that experimental hut design can substantially alter mosquito entry, retention, mortality, and blood-feeding outcomes, with direct consequences for the interpretation of ITN efficacy. Hut designs with larger entry areas (e.g., Ifakara and Rapley) increase mosquito catch and statistical power but facilitate more rapid exit of mosquitoes through the use of exit traps thereby reducing mosquito–net contact. Smaller, more enclosed huts (e.g., West African) generate higher mortality and blood feeding due to increased and prolonged mosquito–host interaction, increasing the probability of a mosquito interacting with the vector control intervention and enhancing the ability of trials to discriminate between products, but their low mosquito entry means that more replication is needed to precisely measure outcomes. Despite these structural differences, all hut types predicted the same direction of effect in relative product comparisons. Recent advances in sample size estimation for experimental hut trials [2, 39] address low mosquito entry through increased replication, though low mosquito entry inevitably increases trial duration and cost. Greater standardisation of hut design should be considered, with emphasis on focusing on maximising mosquito entry rates (e.g., [27, 41]) and consideration of whether closed (no exit) or open (with exit traps) systems are most appropriate for assessing indoor vector control interventions. Further cross-hut calibration in additional sites is warranted as it may lead to more cost-effective and robust comparisons of new vector control tools across sites, and studies.

Conclusion

Our findings confirm that experimental hut design strongly influences entomological outcomes measured in experimental huts, with direct implications for the statistical power and interpretation of ITN trials. While high exit rates can reduce measured blood feeding and mortality, all four hut types tested preserved the relative direction of effect between products, ensuring that comparative efficacy assessments remain informative. From a trial design perspective, smaller huts with fewer exit traps that maximise mosquito entry and mortality and maximise host-vector contact offer the most efficient platform for detecting meaningful differences in ITN performance, thereby strengthening the evidence base underpinning vector control product evaluation and policy.
