Diet–Microbiome–Immune Interactions at the Gut Mucosa in Food Allergy: Mechanisms, Gaps, and Therapeutic Implications
Authors: Clara Delaroque, Liam O'Mahony, Inge Kortekaas Krohn, Charlotte G Mortz, Markus Ollert, Mahesh S Desai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMucosal surfaces are sites of highly dynamic interactions among epithelial and immune cells, environmental exposures, particularly dietary inputs, and the diverse microbial communities and their metabolites. These elements continually influence each other to maintain homeostasis and ensure appropriate immune discrimination between pathogens and innocuous stimuli, such as commensal microbes and dietary antigens. When this balance is disrupted, immune tolerance breaks down, leading to maladaptive responses characteristic of food allergy. Targeting mucosal interactions to restore homeostasis, therefore, holds substantial promise for preventing and treating food allergies and other atopic diseases. However, the complexity of the interconnected networks linking host factors, the microbiome, and diet presents major challenges. In this review, we synthesize recent advances in understanding the mechanisms governing mucosal crosstalk in both health and allergic disease. We provide an in‐depth examination of the intestinal mucus layer and its components as active mediators of microbiota‐host communication and potential initiators of allergic responses. We also discuss emerging mechanistic and therapeutic insights into how diet, food‐derived molecules or treatments shape mucosal immunity, and highlight key knowledge gaps that warrant further investigation to clarify how disruptions in these interactions contribute to allergic sensitization.

Introduction

Food allergies have become a major global health concern, with rising prevalence, especially in industrialized populations, and substantial clinical and socioeconomic impacts [1, 2, 3, 4]. Although genetic predisposition contributes to susceptibility, it remains unclear why only a subset of individuals develop allergic responses to common dietary antigens while most maintain lifelong tolerance [5]. A growing body of research identifies mucosal interfaces, sites of interaction between host cells, dietary molecules, and the microbiome, as major regulators of immune outcomes following exposure to food antigens [6, 7, 8, 9]. While sensitization to dietary allergens may occur through different routes, such as the skin (Text Box 1), lung or intestinal mucosal surfaces, each involves distinct regulatory and immune mechanisms. Environmental factors that influence mucosal compartments—for example diet, in the gastrointestinal tract [10, 11, 12, 13]—are now recognized as central drivers of allergic disease, shaping the balance between tolerance and protective immunity [8, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19]. For example, the early‐life period of immune and microbiome development is a crucial window for the induction of oral tolerance [20, 21, 22, 23, 24]. Specific mucosal mechanisms (e.g., goblet cell–associated antigen trafficking of luminal allergens, as well as a propensity toward tolerance‐inducing immune polarization) during this period promote tolerance to dietary antigens and commensal microbes, ultimately influencing whether mucosal antigen exposure later in life elicits T helper 2 (type‐2)‐skewed immune responses [10, 25, 26, 27]. The translational significance of these processes is highlighted by findings from the Learning Early About Peanut Allergy (LEAP) and Enquiring About Tolerance (EAT) trials, which demonstrated that early oral exposure, rather than avoidance to peanut allergens, promotes mucosal tolerance and reduces the risk of subsequent allergy [28, 29, 30, 31, 32]. These findings led to new guidelines for early food introduction, which have recently been shown to be effective in lowering the incidence of peanut allergy [33].

The skin microbiota and food allergy.

Allergic sensitization can occur via cutaneous exposure to food allergens, particularly when the skin barrier is impaired and/or inflamed [34]. Mutations in the filaggrin (FLG) gene, encoding an important epidermal barrier protein, were shown to increase the likelihood for sensitization to peanut allergens [35, 36]. Cutaneous sensitization due to a dysfunctional skin barrier can hamper the development of oral tolerance, as the immune system may be activated against the allergen before oral tolerance mechanisms are initiated. An impaired skin barrier enables allergen entry and IL‐33 signaling, leading to IgE‐ and type 2‐driven responses via activation of dendritic cells, ILC2, skin homing T cells, and mast cells, promoting food sensitization instead of Treg‐mediated tolerance [37]. This is also called the “outside‐to‐inside hypothesis”. Host–microbe interactions on the skin have been identified as central drivers of both local and systemic immunity. Colonization of the skin by commensal organisms promotes humoral immune responses as well as the local formation of tertiary lymphoid structures [38]. Normal human skin harbors a diverse community of symbiotic microorganisms, including bacteria, fungi, bacteriophages, and eukaryotic viruses. These microorganisms interact with the host cells and the immune system, contributing to skin homeostasis. The composition and richness of the skin bacterial community in early life have been reported to correlate with skin barrier integrity and the later onset of allergic diseases in a population‐based birth cohort study, suggesting a potential role for the skin microbiome in the development of such conditions [39]. Skin dysbiosis can promote colonization by pathogenic bacteria, such as Staphylococcus aureus [40]. Colonization of the skin by S. aureus has been associated with increased susceptibility to eczema and persistent food allergies [41]. Antibiotic‐mediated elimination of S. aureus was reported to abolish eczematous inflammation in mice, suggesting an active role for this bacterium in allergic reactions [42]. Additionally, enterotoxins produced by S. aureus can contribute to this process by directly activating immune cells, creating an inflammatory environment and thereby increasing the susceptibility to food allergies [43, 44].

However, further leveraging of these mechanisms therapeutically is hindered by the complexity of mucosal immunoregulation. Disruption of mucosal homeostasis through impaired epithelial barrier function, altered mucus architecture, dysbiosis of key microbial taxa, or shifts in microbial metabolite production can redirect immune responses toward type 2 inflammation and IgE sensitization [8, 9, 19, 23, 45, 46, 47]. Notably, mucin‐degrading bacteria [8], epithelial antigen transport pathways, such as goblet cell‐associated antigen passages [22, 26, 48], and microbiota‐immunoglobulin interactions [8, 49, 50] have emerged as potential active contributors to allergic sensitization rather than being passively correlated. In parallel, microbe‐derived metabolites, including short‐chain fatty acids (SCFAs), tryptophan derivatives, secondary bile acids, and trace amines, act as potent regulators of epithelial integrity, antigen sampling, and regulatory T cell (Treg) differentiation [7, 51, 52, 53, 54, 55]. These mechanistic elements are strongly shaped by dietary habits [7, 8, 10, 56, 57], positioning diet as a central environmental factor influencing mucosal tolerance and susceptibility to allergic priming.

Defining how these diverse mucosal mechanisms integrate to establish, maintain, or break tolerance is essential for guiding the development of targeted interventions. As dietary strategies, microbiota‐directed therapies, and barrier‐supporting approaches gain traction, a deeper mechanistic understanding of host–microbiome–diet interactions is urgently needed. In this review, we discuss recent advances in the roles of goblet cells, mucus structure, mucosal immunoglobulins, intestinal microbiota composition, and microbial metabolites in shaping oral tolerance and food allergy, including immunological and mucosal modulation in food allergy therapeutic approaches. We highlight key mechanistic pathways, context‐dependent interactions, and outstanding knowledge gaps that must be addressed to enable the next generation of preventive and therapeutic strategies.

Goblet Cells: A Central Element in Oral Tolerance

Intestinal walls are covered with a mucus layer that serves as a crucial barrier, physically separating the intestinal lumen, home to the microbiome and food‐derived molecules, from the epithelial lining. This separation prevents direct contact between luminal contents and the intestinal epithelium, while also enabling the controlled presentation of bacterial and dietary antigens to the underlying immune system [58, 59]. One of the mechanisms responsible for such controlled presentation relies on mucus‐producing goblet cells. Beyond maintaining the mucus layer through continuous replenishment, recent evidence points to a pivotal role for goblet cells in mediating the trafficking of luminal antigens and modulating immune responses. These cells perform endocytic uptake and transcytosis of luminal material across the epithelium [60]. This process, known as goblet cell‐associated antigen passage (GAP), is recognized as a critical mechanism for the induction of oral tolerance [26, 48, 60, 61], but also contributes to allergy promotion by enabling allergen translocation to the lamina propria (Figure 1).

Mice sensitized with the dietary allergen ovalbumin and subjected to transient GAP inhibition at the time of antigen exposure exhibited exacerbated allergic reactions, which underscores the essential role of goblet cells in establishing oral tolerance [26, 48, 62]. Immune tolerance is mediated by Treg induction and maintenance (Figure 1) [48] resulting from GAPs‐mediated antigen delivery to antigen‐presenting cells that acquire, process, and present relevant antigens [63, 64, 65]. GAPs are tightly regulated along the gastrointestinal tract (GIT) and throughout an individual's lifespan [60, 61, 66]. This regulation coincides with the heightened capacity of the mucosal immune system to establish tolerance during the early‐life window of opportunity, which spans from birth to the introduction of solid foods [67, 68]. Notably, immune tolerance is predominantly established in mucosal compartments during this early developmental window, which is characterized by multiple immunological features that favor tolerogenic rather than inflammatory responses to microbial or dietary antigens [67, 68, 69]. During this period, GAPs are tightly regulated [26, 68]: in the first 10 days of life, GAPs remain closed in both the small intestine and colon due to epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) ligands derived from maternal milk, which suppress antigen passage [26, 70, 71]. As milk‐derived EGFR ligands decline, colonic GAPs open, allowing luminal antigens to be trafficked across the epithelium and supporting the differentiation of mucosal tolerogenic immune cells [26]. At weaning, the introduction of solid food induces a sharp increase in bacterial load and microbial diversity, which in turn drives the closure of colonic GAPs through enhanced Toll‐like receptor (TLR) signaling in a MyD88‐dependent manner [10, 26]. Simultaneously, the cessation of milk consumption enables GAP opening in the small intestine, which is essential for dietary antigen tolerance. Because most dietary molecules are absorbed in the small intestine rather than reaching the colon, this shift ensures that antigen exposure occurs at the optimal site for inducing targeted immune tolerance to dietary and commensal antigens. GAP function has been shown not only to support the differentiation of peripheral Tregs (pTregs) but also to maintain pre‐existing lamina propria Tregs, imprint dendritic cells with tolerogenic properties, and promote interleukin (IL)‐10 production, an essential cytokine for immune regulation, by intestinal macrophages [48, 71]. Taken together, these findings place goblet cells as central regulators of immune‐tolerance mechanisms within the mucosal compartment of the gastrointestinal tract. However, although GAPs have been extensively characterized in mice, they have so far only been observed in ex vivo jejunal sections from human adults [61]. Consequently, a significant knowledge gap remains regarding the extent to which these mechanisms operate in humans, representing an important area for future translational research.

However, during the development of allergic responses, GAPs are also directly involved in promoting reactivity to dietary antigens. IL‐13 and IL‐4, both signaling through the type II IL‐4 receptor (IL‐4Rα/IL‐13Rα1), have been shown to induce GAP opening in the small intestines, thereby promoting food‐induced anaphylaxis by facilitating antigen exposure to reactive immune cells (Figure 1) [72]. Similar antigen transport mechanisms have been observed in human intestinal organoids, suggesting that comparable processes operate in humans [72]. Recent evidence shows that impaired activity of epithelium‐derived dipeptidase 1 (DPEP1), an enzyme responsible for catabolizing inflammatory leukotrienes, leads to elevated leukotriene levels and increased food allergen transport through goblet cells (Figure 1), which can result in anaphylactic responses [73]. Regulation of Dpep1 expression has been reported to be associated with the abundance of specific microbiota members (e.g., Ruminococcus) [74] and microbial metabolites such as D‐lactate [75], suggesting that the microbiome may modulate this pathway.

The role of goblet cells in allergic responses extends beyond their capacity to transport antigens. Goblet‐cell‐derived resistin‐like molecule β (RELMβ) was shown to modulate food allergy development by altering fecal microbiota composition, specifically reducing bacterial species that produce indole derivatives (Figure 1) [55]. The resulting reduction in indole derivatives, in turn, led to a decrease in Treg populations and heightened susceptibility to food allergy. The effect was goblet cell‐specific, as targeted deletion of the gene encoding RELMβ in intestinal goblet cells using a Math1‐driven Cre construct in mice prevented the increased susceptibility to food allergy observed in mice carrying a gain‐of‐function mutation in the IL‐4 receptor [55]. Hence, goblet cells can directly modulate immune responses through multiple mechanisms, including antigen trafficking and intestinal microbiota regulation, ultimately influencing allergic reactions. These findings underscore the importance of understanding the interplay between IL‐13/IL‐4R signaling, microbiome‐derived cues, and GAP function. Such insights may not only deepen our knowledge of food allergy mechanisms, but also identify novel therapeutic targets beyond the classical type 2 immune pathways [76].

The Intestinal Mucus Layer Contributes to Oral Tolerance

The mucus layer is essential for maintaining intestinal homeostasis by supporting the growth of specific microbiota members and modulating the host immune system. The importance of mucus in food allergy development was first demonstrated in Muc2 −/− mice, which lack MUC2, the predominant protein of the intestinal mucin backbone [58]. These mice failed to develop tolerance to ovalbumin, a process mediated by dendritic cells [77]. Notably, this loss of tolerance was not solely due to microbiota‐induced inflammation resulting from mucus deficiency, but also the absence of MUC2 itself. Indeed, supplementation with MUC2 restored tolerance, highlighting its direct role in imprinting tolerogenic properties onto dendritic cells in the colon. This, in turn, promoted oral tolerance by expanding Tregs and reducing IgE production [77]. Further supporting this mechanism, exposing lamina propria dendritic cells to MUC2 protein led to a significant decrease in the expression of CD86, a key co‐stimulatory factor. This is particularly relevant as CD86 is essential for T cell priming into Th2 cells and is associated with IL‐4 levels [78, 79] (Figure 2). Similarly, in Vamp8 −/− mice, which lack VAMP8, a key regulator of mucus secretion, reduced mucus production associates with changes in gene expression in goblet cells and disruption of the balance between tolerogenic and inflammatory immune responses in the gut [80]. Thus, mucus contributes to immune modulation by delivering tolerogenic signals that restrain excessive immune activation against microbial and dietary antigens, complementing its physical barrier function that limits host exposure to these antigens [80].

Mucin structure itself has been linked to susceptibility to food allergy in mouse models. Significant differences in the O‐glycan profile of intestinal mucins have been observed in ovalbumin‐sensitized mice compared with controls [45] (Figure 1). Mucins are highly O‐glycosylated proteins, and their degree of glycosylation is closely tied to their protective role in the intestinal epithelium as well as to their interactions with members of the microbiota. The study reported a marked reduction in O‐glycosylation, particularly in acidic O‐glycans, in sensitized mice [45], which is in line with findings from the same group [81]. Notably, treatment with sea cucumber chondroitin sulfate or goat milk oligosaccharides successfully restored O‐glycan levels and reinforced the intestinal barrier, both of which were associated with protection against food allergy [45, 81].

Additionally, mucus covering other mucosal surfaces has also been reported to play a central role in shaping atopy. In the ocular compartment, mucin sialylation has been shown to be required for the encapsulation of pollen in an impenetrable layer, thereby protecting mice from the development of allergic conjunctivitis [82]. Additionally, analyses of mucus plugs from the lungs of asthma patients have revealed that these plugs are not only enriched in type 2 immune cells, but are also associated with goblet cell hyperplasia [83]. Exposure of eosinophils to mucus derived from epithelial cells stimulated in vitro with IL‐13 resulted in enhanced cytolytic degranulation [83]. This immune response was mediated by mucin glycosylation, as eosinophils exposed to mucus treated with periodate to remove glycans showed a marked reduction in degranulation [83]. Furthermore, mucin fucosylation in asthma has been linked to disease development in both humans and mice, with evidence suggesting a causal role as mice lacking the fucosyltransferase enzyme responsible for this glycosylation were protected from asthma‐like airway hyperreactivity and mucus plugging [84].

Gut Microbiota Interaction With Host Immunoglobulin in Food Allergy

Mucosal immunoglobulins (Igs) play a central role in immune responses, particularly in allergic reactions. In IgE‐mediated food allergies, IgE binds to Fcε receptors on effector cells, like mast cells and basophils. Re‐exposure to the allergens leads to degranulation. Recent research, however, suggests that both IgA and IgE may have additional roles in the development of food allergies through interactions with the gut microbiota. While studies investigating correlation between food‐specific IgA titers and food allergy outcomes have not established a clear link [85, 86], growing evidence indicates a significant reduction in IgA‐coated bacteria in the gut of food‐allergic patients [50, 87, 88] and mouse models [49]. Under healthy conditions, IgA is secreted into the lumen, where it profoundly influences microbiota composition and function, particularly in the mucosal compartment, to promote homeostasis. Consequently, a reduction in IgA‐coated bacteria may contribute to dysbiosis in children with food allergy. Additionally, elevated fecal IgE levels have been observed in patients with food allergies [50, 89, 90, 91] and mouse models of food anaphylaxis [8]. This phenomenon may not be independent of reduced luminal IgA, as studies in peanut‐allergic individuals suggest that local isotype switching from IgG, but also IgA, to IgE occurs in these patients [92, 93, 94]. Notably, this represents an important difference between IgE+ B cell development in humans and mice, since mice cannot undergo class switching from IgA to IgE because of the isotype ordering within their IgH locus. These findings indicate that immunoglobulin class switching may contribute to disease pathogenesis, positioning the gastrointestinal tract as a reservoir for IgE‐producing B cells [92]. IgE has been shown to translocate into the intestinal lumen in mouse models of food allergy (Figure 2). More than three decades ago, studies reported elevated fecal IgE levels in samples from patients with food allergies, suggesting a local immune response to food antigen exposure [89, 95]. Nevertheless, the physiological and pathological significance of such fecal IgE levels remains poorly understood. More recent studies have confirmed these findings, demonstrating elevated fecal IgE levels in infants with food allergies [50, 96], which correlate with abdominal pain scores in peanut‐allergic children [50]. Notably, fecal IgE levels have also been associated with allergic rhinitis in children [97]. Additionally, increased allergen‐specific IgE levels have been reported in colon homogenates of mice exposed to bacterial infections or toxins, suggesting that the microbiome may contribute to the regulation of intestinal IgE [98].

Enhanced IgE transcytosis into the intestinal lumen may also contribute to food allergy. IgE can be actively transported across the intestinal epithelium via its low‐affinity receptor, CD23 (FcεRII), a type II membrane glycoprotein with a C‐type lectin head that binds IgE in a calcium‐dependent manner [99] (Figure 2). The receptor's affinity for IgE varies depending on whether it exists in a monomeric or trimeric form [100]. Allergen exposure has been shown to induce CD23 upregulation in epithelial cells [96, 101, 102], a process regulated by IL‐4 signaling [103, 104]. Notably, upon allergen challenge, small intestinal tissues from sensitized CD23‐knockout (CD23KO) mice exhibit reduced antigen transport compared to control mice [105]. This impaired transport may, in part, contribute to the attenuated allergic inflammation observed in CD23KO mice or in mice treated with anti‐CD23 neutralizing antibodies [106], suggesting that IgE translocation across the epithelium actively contributes to disease pathogenesis rather than merely reflecting immune activation. Since IgE transcytosis is bidirectional, its primary role in food allergy may be the capture of dietary allergens from the intestinal lumen, thereby amplifying mucosal immune system activation (Figure 2). Interestingly, recent studies comparing the fecal microbiota of children with IgE‐mediated versus non‐IgE‐mediated food allergies revealed notable differences, suggesting that IgE itself may influence microbiota composition [107] (Figure 2). Similar observations were made in ovalbumin‐sensitized wild type and IgE‐knock‐in mice, with increased IgE levels correlating with significant microbiota alterations [91]. Although it remains unclear whether IgE directly modulates intestinal microbiota composition, fecal IgE from patients with peanut allergy has been shown to exhibit increased reactivity to fecal proteins compared to controls [50], potentially contributing to increased IgE binding to commensal bacteria and subsequent modulation of microbiota composition. Another possible hypothesis arises from an unbiased analysis of glycosylation patterns in total IgE from individuals with peanut allergy [108]. This study identified an increase in sialic acid residues on circulating IgE from peanut allergy patients compared to non‐atopic individuals [108]. Upon IgE translocation, this sialic acid enrichment could serve as a nutrient source for certain microbiota members, such as Ruminococcus gnavus , which possesses specific enzymatic machinery enabling the release and uptake of sialic acids for its metabolism [109]. Interestingly, although these findings are based on correlations with species‐level assignments from 16S rRNA sequencing—which can be misleading due to limited taxonomic resolution and may generate false associations— R. gnavus abundance was found to be elevated in children with IgE‐mediated food allergies [46, 47, 110], and its abundance has been positively correlated with fecal IgE levels in patients with peanuts allergic [111]. Moreover, analysis of IgE‐bound bacteria in children with cow's milk allergy revealed an enrichment of R. gnavus [87], suggesting a direct interaction between fecal IgE and this bacterium in allergy context. Such direct interactions between intestinal bacteria and IgE could potentially lead to microbiota modulation, similar to what has been observed for IgA–bacteria interactions [112]. However, these mechanisms and their consequences for host health remain to be fully investigated.

The Mucosal Microbiota in Food Allergy

Specific microbiota members, and particularly those residing in the intestinal mucus layer (designated mucosal microbiota), have been reported to be impacted in food allergy. Accumulating evidence points to these alterations in mucosal microbiota as being both a cause and a consequence of food allergy [59, 113]. Indeed, all changes discussed above, including changes in mucin glycosylation, IgA and IgE levels in the mucus layer, as well as factors produced by goblet cells, could contribute to variation in mucosal taxa abundance but also function. Among those taxa, mucin‐degrading bacteria have gathered much attention. These selected microbiota members have the ability to feed on the glycoprotein‐rich mucus layer that overlies the gut epithelium [114]. This mucin‐degrading microbiome significantly impacts the host by regulating mucus production, maintaining barrier integrity, and shaping the mucosal immune system. As mentioned, increases in mucin‐utilizing R. gnavus have been reported in food‐allergic patients, including in a cohort of twins discordant for allergic disease [46, 47]. Moreover, supplementation of mice with R. gnavus prior to ovalbumin sensitization displayed an aggravated allergic response, including increased IgE production and local inflammation [47]. While this observation was made regarding relative abundance, more recent findings point toward specific function of food allergy‐associated R. gnavus strains. Indeed, strains from food‐allergic patients exhibited functional specializations, including enhanced ability to adhere to the gut epithelium, with a higher prevalence of genes encoding pilin and anchoring factors [46] as well as shifted coating profile by luminal IgA and IgE [87]. However, these associations were inferred from V3‐V4 16S rRNA gene sequencing‐based species‐level identification and will require further confirmation using functional and strain‐level resolution sequencing approaches. Moreover, although no association between atopic dermatitis (AD) and specific taxa was observed in 16S‐based fecal microbiota profiling of children with atopic dermatitis, metagenomic analysis revealed differences in specific functional genes in the microbiomes of children with atopic disease compared to controls [110]. This suggests the involvement of specific bacterial functions, rather than individual bacterial species, in the etiology of these diseases. Altogether, such observations indicate that specific functions from particular strains may contribute to food allergy development, rather than merely the presence or absence of a given species [113].

We recently reported that mice colonized with a minimal community of 14 strains mimicking the human colonic microbiota and fed either a high‐ or low‐fiber diet prior to sensitization with ovalbumin and peanut allergens exhibited markedly different allergic outcomes. Fiber deprivation resulted in an exacerbated anaphylactic response, accompanied by increased levels of IgE‐coated fecal bacteria in the colonic compartment [8]. Notably, a similar increase in IgE‐bound bacteria was also observed in mice fed with a fiber‐free diet, but not challenged with any allergens, underscoring the potent impact of diet in modulating allergy‐related immune mechanisms within the intestinal compartment. Importantly, both disease exacerbation and increased IgE coating of bacteria were associated with an enrichment of the mucin‐degrading bacterium Akkermansia muciniphila . Removal of A. muciniphila from the microbial community was sufficient to prevent the deleterious effects of fiber deprivation, indicating that these effects were mediated by this bacterium within this specific dietary context [8, 113, 115, 116]. This raises the concept that specific bacterial activity triggered by environmental factors, such as diet, might contribute to food allergy development. However, which microbiota members and triggers are specifically involved in food allergies in the human population requires dedicated further investigations.

Microbiota‐Derived Molecules in FA, and Dietary Modulation

Microbial‐derived molecules are key regulators of immune and metabolic functions essential for host health and survival. These effects are mediated not only through the activation of host pattern recognition receptors in response to microbial danger signals, but also via bacterial metabolites that significantly influence mucosal immune cell populations and epithelial barrier function. These metabolites act through diverse signaling mechanisms, including G protein‐coupled receptors (GPCRs), aryl hydrocarbon receptors (AhRs), nuclear hormone receptors such as the farnesoid X receptor (FXR), muscarinic acetylcholine receptors, and epigenetic mechanisms [53, 117, 118, 119, 120, 121]. Importantly, many immunoregulatory bacterial metabolites are derived from dietary components (e.g., fiber, tryptophan), by bacterial strain‐specific and context‐dependant processes [113, 122, 123, 124, 125], linking diet and lifestyle to immune health via microbial pathways. Well‐characterized microbial metabolites include SCFAs, tryptophan derivatives, secondary bile acids (BAs) and their derivatives, histamine, sphingolipids, polyamines, and p‐cresol.

SCFAs are produced by gut bacteria through fermentation of complex carbohydrates typically found in dietary fibers. The major intestinal SCFAs (acetate, propionate, butyrate, and valerate) play crucial roles in maintaining intestinal homeostasis [126, 127]. SCFAs exert their effects through receptors such as GPR43, GPR41, GPR109A, and LFR78/OR51E2, while butyrate additionally modulates gene transcription via inhibition of histone deacetylases (HDACs) [128, 129]. SCFAs have been shown to inhibit human mast cell degranulation, ameliorate murine anaphylaxis, and regulate inflammatory responses [130, 131, 132]. Reduced levels of Bifidobacteria and SCFA‐producing bacteria, or lower butyrate levels in early life fecal samples, are associated with an increased risk of food allergen sensitization [52, 133].

Tryptophan‐derived metabolites, including indole, indole‐3‐lactate (ILA), indole‐3‐propionate (IPA), indoleacetic acid (IAA) are generated by diverse gut microbes through direct metabolism of tryptophan [118]. Many of these tryptophan metabolites act as ligands for the Aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) or pregnane X receptor (PXR). Microbial tryptophan metabolites directly influence epithelial barrier integrity, mucosal lymphocytes, innate lymphoid cells (ILC)s, macrophages, dendritic cells, and neutrophils, promoting anti‐inflammatory responses and production of associated cytokines, such as IL‐10 and IL‐22. IPA has been shown to reduce food allergy severity in murine models, while microbial indole derivatives promote protective RORγt+ Treg cells, a process disrupted by high levels of goblet‐cell‐derived RELMβ [55, 134, 135, 136]. Moreover, AhR plays a key role in maintaining the balance between intestinal ILC3 and ILC2 populations by repressing the IL‐33–ST2 signaling axis, thereby limiting allergy‐associated Th2 immunity [137]. AhR signaling activated by these tryptophan‐derived metabolites has also been implicated in the regulation of the mucus layer and the maintenance of intestinal homeostasis. Beyond its immunomodulatory effects, AhR signaling enhances mucus production and increases mucus layer thickness, thereby supporting intestinal barrier function [138, 139]. Conversely, AhR deficiency in the gut compromises barrier integrity, through loss of mature IECs such as goblet cells as well as reduced tight junction expression, and increases bacterial translocation [140, 141].

Secondary BAs and their derivatives are produced in the intestine via bacterial conversion of primary BAs. These metabolites modulate immune cell function by signaling through multiple receptors, including FXR, liver X receptor (LXR), PXR, and others [142]. Notably, failure of peanut oral immunotherapy has been associated with distinct patterns of microbial bile and amino acid metabolism [51]. In addition, gut‐specific deletion of FXR has been associated with increased mucus penetrability, suggesting that disruption of FXR signaling may enhance host exposure to intestinal allergens [143].

Bacterially derived histamine is immunologically active and modulates both innate and adaptive immune responses through four different GPCRs, histamine receptor 1–4 (H1‐4R) [144]. In the gut, microbial histamine influences mucosal Th2 cytokine responses and can reduce allergic inflammation in murine models via H2R signaling. Elevated levels of trace amines, including tyramine, tryptamine, and phenylethylamine, have been associated with atopic diseases in infants, including food allergy, often linked to aberrant colonization by Firmicutes members, such as Tyzzerella nexilis, Enterococcus faecalis , and Ruminococcus gnavus [145]. In particular, increased abundance of R. gnavus is thought to promote Th2 immune responses in food‐allergic children through secretion of trace amines and expression of pro‐inflammatory polysaccharides in the gut [46, 146]. More recently, another host‐relevant microbiome‐derived molecule has been reported. Acetylcholine (ACh) production has been described in select Bifidobacterium strains, including B. breve and B. longum , which are among the most prevalent Bifidobacterium species in the infant microbiome [121]. Microbiome‐derived ACh has been reported to promote intestinal IgA production and modulate intestinal homeostasis in a way that protects the host from infection and inflammation [120]. This acetylcholine is produced by commensal bacteria through biosynthetic pathways that utilize dietary choline. This is particularly relevant in early life, as human breast milk is enriched in choline‐containing compounds. While findings in human settings and the implications for allergy development remain to be fully investigated, these observations highlight a novel mechanism of the diet–microbiome–host axis that supports mucosal immunity and may influence type 2 immune responses.

Recent changes in dietary habits and intestinal microbiota composition have resulted in altered levels of immunoregulatory metabolites critical for optimal immune development and function [147, 148]. Emerging evidence suggests that a deficiency of microbial‐derived immunoregulatory molecules, potentially coupled with an excess of proinflammatory mediators, can result in a hypersensitive immune system that responds inappropriately to dietary components. Given the essential role of dietary fiber in fueling SCFA production, targeted fiber‐based interventions hold considerable potential to modulate the gut microbiome and its metabolic functions to promote immune health [149]. Moreover, non‐dietary components have been reported to modulate the intestinal microbiota and its resulting metabolites, functions, and composition in ways that may influence susceptibility to food allergy development. This has been observed for multiple components of ultra‐processed foods, including advanced glycation end products and various food additives, often in a microbiota‐dependent manner. For example, advanced glycation end products induce alterations in the intestinal microbiota as well as changes in microbiota‐derived metabolites, which have been reported to promote Th2 polarization and pro‐inflammatory responses [19]. Food additives have also been shown to modulate microbiota composition and function in ways that may promote deleterious host consequences. This has been demonstrated, for instance, for dietary emulsifiers, which induce strong shifts in microbiota composition, alter bacterial localization within the mucus layer, and modify levels of microbiota‐derived short‐chain fatty acids as well as pro‐inflammatory molecules such as LPS and flagellin. Collectively, these changes promote inflammatory responses, type 2 immune responses, and increased susceptibility to allergy [14, 150, 151]. This concept may be extended to other components, such as artificial sweeteners [152] and nano‐ and microplastics [153]. Overall, these findings suggest that multiple factors associated with ultra‐processed food, including additives, processing‐derived compounds, and exposure to packaging materials, can impact the intestinal microbiome in ways that may promote allergic disease development. Further characterization of these effects will be important in the context of food allergies and other atopic diseases, particularly to inform targeted dietary recommendations for susceptible individuals.

Immunological and Mucosal Modulation in Food Allergy Therapeutic Approaches

Therapeutic strategies for food allergy can be broadly grouped into three main approaches: (1) avoidance of trigger foods, (2) symptomatic control of allergic reactions, and (3) immune reprogramming aimed at restoring tolerance to dietary allergens [154]. While allergen introduction strategies in infancy and early childhood have demonstrated efficacy [28, 29], no curative treatment is currently available. As a result, most pharmacological approaches primarily target effector pathways of allergic inflammation rather than disease resolution. Among these, treatments targeting IgE‐mediated responses have shown clinical benefit. Omalizumab, a monoclonal anti‐IgE antibody, was recently proven efficient in neutralizing allergic response in individuals with multiple food allergies [155]. Interestingly, decreased expression of an IgE receptor has been reported in peripheral blood mononuclear cells from treated patients [156]. Although direct evidence in the intestinal mucosa remains limited, a similar reduction in the gut could limit IgE translocation into the intestinal lumen and reduce its interactions with resident bacteria, potentially influencing host–microbiota crosstalk. However, this remains to be formally demonstrated. More recently, alternative IgE‐targeting strategies, including vaccine‐based approaches against human IgE, have been proposed as potential long‐term therapies for IgE‐mediated allergic disease [157]. Long‐term investigation of the effects of these treatments on microbiome composition, function, and mucosal homeostasis could provide valuable insights into the role of IgE in shaping the intestinal microbiota and clarify whether treatment‐induced microbial changes contribute to the modulation of allergic responses.

Another major therapeutic axis targets type 2 cytokine signaling through blockade of IL‐4Rα (e.g., dupilumab), thereby inhibiting both IL‐4 and IL‐13 pathways. Beyond their immunological effects, these cytokines are central regulators of epithelial barrier biology, including mucus integrity [72, 158]. Accordingly, IL‐4Rα blockade has been associated with changes in mucus composition and potential mucin deficiency across mucosal tissues [159, 160, 161]. While data on the intestinal mucus layer remain limited, these findings raise the possibility that IL‐4/IL‐13 inhibition may indirectly influence host–microbiota interactions at the epithelial surface. In line with this, isolated case reports describing inflammatory bowel disease onset under dupilumab treatment further suggest that perturbations of mucus and resulting host‐microbiota interactions may lead to deleterious consequences in susceptible individuals [162, 163]. In contrast, other commonly used symptomatic treatments, including antihistamines, corticosteroids, leukotriene receptor antagonists, and eosinophil‐targeting therapies, have not been reported to substantially modify mucosal barrier biology.

Beyond symptom control, immune re‐education strategies such as oral immunotherapy (OIT) aim to induce sustained desensitization to food allergens. OIT efficacy appears to be influenced by the baseline composition and functional capacity of the gut microbiome, particularly its ability to metabolize dietary antigens, which has been associated with both disease severity and treatment responsiveness [51, 164]. Conversely, OIT itself may reciprocally modulate microbial composition. In a pediatric cohort, OIT has been associated with a shift toward microbial profiles resembling non‐allergic controls, including reductions in Bacteroidota and Verrucomicrobiota, suggesting partial restoration of a “health‐associated” microbial signature [165], although these observations need to be confirmed in separated cohorts. Moreover, OIT may also influence the intestinal mucosal compartment. In murine models, OIT has been shown to modulate epithelial barrier integrity and gene expression programs in intestinal epithelial cells [166]. Formulation of OIT with prebiotic components such as inulin has been reported to enhance therapeutic efficacy in a microbiota‐dependent manner, as antibiotic treatment abrogated these benefits, further supporting a functional role of the microbiome in shaping treatment outcomes [167]. Together, these findings suggest that successful immunotherapy may depend not only on immune modulation but also on concurrent remodeling of host–microbial interactions.

In this context, increasing attention has been directed toward therapeutic strategies that directly target the gut microbiota. Evidence from experimental models indicates that specific microbial taxa can confer protection against food allergy [49, 134, 168], and that modulation of the microbial ecosystem can restore immune balance, including normalization of Th1/Th2 responses [169, 170]. This has led to growing interest in microbiome‐based interventions such as fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT). Preclinical studies in mice have demonstrated that FMT can reshape immune polarization and restore immune homeostasis in allergic settings [49, 134, 168, 171, 172]. Clinically, a randomized, double‐blind, placebo‐controlled trial (NCT05695261) is currently evaluating the safety and efficacy of microbial transplantation therapy in peanut‐allergic patients, including individuals undergoing maintenance OIT. Patients will undergo oral food challenge at study completion to assess changes in tolerated allergen dose, representing an important step toward translating microbiome‐based modulation into food allergy therapeutics.

Perspectives

Collectively, the evidence reviewed here reframes food allergy as a disease involving disrupted mucosal communication rather than a failure of immune recognition alone. Goblet cells, mucus structure and components, and microbiota‐derived metabolites emerge as active and interdependent regulators of antigen handling, barrier integrity, and immune education. When these systems are appropriately coordinated, particularly during early life, dietary antigens are sampled in a controlled manner that favors regulatory pathways and durable oral tolerance [173]. Conversely, perturbations in epithelial transport, mucin structure, immunoglobulin‐microbiota interactions, or microbial metabolic output, all of which are modulated by dietary inputs [8, 10, 26, 71, 81, 174, 175], can shift the mucosal environment toward type 2‐skewed immunity and IgE sensitization.

Previous studies showed that the same mucosal pathways that support tolerance under homeostatic conditions can also promote allergic reactivity when shaped by inflammatory cytokines, dysbiosis, or dietary imbalance [113, 116]. For example, by goblet cell‐associated antigen passages, which can facilitate anaphylaxis in a type 2‐inflamed setting, and by mucin‐degrading bacteria whose impact depends on diet, strain‐level functions, and host immune tone. Similarly, IgE is not merely an effector molecule, but may contribute as a dynamic participant in shaping the mucosal microbiota, potentially reinforcing allergic responses within the gut lumen [91, 176, 177].

These insights carry important translational implications. They suggest that effective prevention and treatment of food allergy will likely require strategies that restore mucosal homeostasis rather than targeting single immune pathways. Dietary interventions, microbiota‐ and metabolite‐based therapies, and approaches that reinforce epithelial and mucus barrier function may act synergistically to recalibrate antigen sampling and immune regulation. Moving forward, key challenges include defining causal mechanisms in humans and resolving strain‐ and metabolite‐specific effects. Addressing these gaps will be essential for translating mucosal immunology into durable, mechanism‐based therapeutic and/or preventive approaches for food allergy.
