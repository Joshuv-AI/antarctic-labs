Precise Verification of Transformers through ReLU-Catalyzed Abstraction Refinement
Hengjie Liu, Zhenya Zhang (✉) {}^{\text{({\char 0\relax})}}, Jianjun Zhao

Abstract
Formal verification of transformers has become increasingly important due to their widespread deployment in safety-critical applications. Compared to classic neural networks, the inferences of transformers involve highly complex computations, such as dot products in self-attention layers, rendering their verification extremely difficult. Existing approaches explored over-approximation methods by constructing convex constraints to bound the output ranges of transformers, which can achieve high efficiency. However, they may sacrifice verification precision, and consequently introduce significant approximation error that leads to frequent occurrences of false alarms. In this paper, we propose a transformer verification approach that can achieve improved precision. At the core of our approach is a novel usage of ReLU, by which we represent a precise but non-linear bound for dot products such that we can further exploit the rich body of literature for convex relaxation of ReLU to derive precise bounds. We extend two classic approaches to the context of transformers, a rule-based one and an optimization-based one, resulting in two new frameworks for efficient and precise verification. We evaluate our approaches on different model architectures and robustness properties derived from two datasets about sentiment analysis, and compare with the state-of-the-art baseline approach. Compared to the baseline, our approach can achieve significant precision improvement for most of the verification tasks with acceptable compromise of efficiency, which demonstrates the effectiveness of our approach.

Introduction

Over the past decade, the rapid development of deep learning has driven unprecedented breakthroughs in different application domains. As a ground-breaking milestone, transformers
[
27
]
, empowered by the
self-attention
mechanism, have brought revolutionary performance advances to multiple fields, such as computer vision and natural language processing (NLP). However, similar to other types of neural networks, transformers are notoriously vulnerable to adversarial perturbations, which brings significant doubts on their trustworthiness. For instance,
synonym substitution

[
21
,
1
]
has proved to be an effective attack method in NLP that can fool the model by replacing some words of an input sentence with their synonyms. Given the increasing deployment of transformers in safety-critical applications (e.g., autonomous driving, healthcare), ensuring their robustness in noisy and sophisticated environments has become an urgent demand.

Verification is a preferred approach that can formally prove the safety and robustness of systems, thereby alleviating the concerns about their misbehaviors. In the context of neural networks, it has been extensively studied (see a survey
[
19
]
and the competition report
[
6
]
) with well-established methodological foundations. The challenge mainly involves how to deal with their
non-linear components
, e.g., activation functions in classic feed-forward neural networks. Due to this, computing an exact bound of neural network outputs often incurs exponential time complexity and thus is not scalable to real-world models. By contrast,
abstract interpretation
is an approach that constructs convex
over-approximation
of their outputs and assesses whether the approximated outputs satisfy the desired property—as long as the over-approximated outputs satisfy the property, the original outputs must also satisfy the property, thereby verifying the neural networks. Owning to the convexity of the approximated output bounds, these approaches are much more efficient and thus preferable in practice.

Overview of Related Work
Despite the prosperity of research in classic neural network verification, verification of
transformers
still remains at a very early stage. Nevertheless, standing on the shoulders of classic verification, the research community get straight to abstract interpretation
[
23
,
3
,
35
]
, which is more scalable to transformers of large sizes. For example, as the state-of-the-art, Shi et al.
[
23
]
extend the idea of linear relaxation from classic neural network verification, and devise different linear bounds for different non-linear components of transformers, such as the dot product operations in self-attention layers. Compared to the verification approaches that bound transformer outputs exactly (e.g.,
[
18
]
), computation of convex over-approximation often requires polynomial time complexity, so they are much faster and more scalable, and therefore they are feasible to be used to handle complex transformers in real world.

Completeness Issues of Abstract Interpretation
As an intrinsic issue of abstract interpretation, if the over-approximated outputs violate the given property, it does not imply that the original outputs also violate the property, and in this case, the reported violation (i.e., the violation by the over-approximated outputs) could be a
false alarm
. This is known as the
completeness issue

[
19
]
, and essentially, it arises from the error introduced during the approximation of the transformers’ non-linear components. In particular, we show that existing over-approximation approaches are often
suboptimal
and thus may introduce significant approximation error and bring many false alarms. Given that verification is typically expensive, frequent occurrences of false alarms can lead to substantial waste of time and resources, highlighting the need for more precise approximation methods.

Key Idea of Abstraction Refinement
While there are various non-linear operations in transformers, dot products happen most frequently in matrix multiplications of self-attention layers and pose unique challenges that do not exist in classic neural network verification. In
[
23
]
, the main contribution involves the design of a provably correct planar bound for dot products, which is given in terms of a rigid strategy that always covers one side of the hyperbolic paraboloid derived from the dot product (see Fig.
2(b)
for illustration).
While it is a sound choice, it is not the unique one, e.g., there can be a dual bound that covers another side of the hyperbolic paraboloid (see Fig.
2(c)
for illustration).

However, none of the two planar bounds, including the one in
[
23
]
and the dual bound, is the optimal under all different situations. To refine the approximation, we need to devise new approaches that fuse the two planar bounds, meanwhile holding the linearity of the bounds to maintain verification efficiency.

Contributions

In this paper, we explore a novel usage of ReLU functions, by which we propose
\mathsf{BuFFeT}
, standing for
B

U
nd
F

F
in

The advantage of doing this is that, we can then leverage the relaxed linear bounds of the ReLU-based representations, by exploiting the rich body of existing literature in classic neural network verification, in which linear relaxation of ReLUs has been extensively studied and a variety of bound-tightening techniques have been developed. Consequently, we can derive tighter bounds for dot products—the fundamental operations in self-attention layers—which in turn leads to tighter bounds on transformer outputs. Moreover, based on our representation, we also show that the bounds proposed in
[
23
]
and its dual case, are two specific instantiations that can be derived by our representation, and we explain why they are suboptimal.

Furthermore, we leverage the insights from two existing strategies from classic neural network verification to derive improved approximation. The first strategy, adapted from
[
24
]
, is simple but effective, which, based on the input ranges of ReLUs, selects the optimal planar bound to refine the approximation. The second strategy, inspired by
[

, is more complex—it formalizes the refinement of approximation as an optimization problem and searches in the space of ReLUs’ bounds to find the optimal solution. Thanks to this formalization, we can call modern optimization solvers, such as Adam, to iteratively try different bounds of ReLUs and refine the verification results. Then, as soon as it finds the bounds that suffice to certify the robustness of transformers, verification can be terminated.

We evaluate our approaches on transformer classifiers and TinyBERT
[
13
]
trained with datasets about sentiment analysis. In total, we experiment on 10 transformer-based models with different complexities.
We evaluate the robustness of each model with 140 verification tasks, resulting in 1400 verification tasks totally in our evaluation. We select the state-of-the-art approach
[
23
]
as our baseline and compare our approach with it in terms of verification precision and efficiency. By our results, we observe that, for most of the verification tasks, our approaches, especially o-
\mathsf{BuFFeT}
, demonstrate evident precision improvement, which significantly enhances the reliability of verification results.

Impact

Notably, this work not only delivers two effective verification approaches, but more importantly, by the use of ReLU, it bridges verification methodologies between transformers and classic neural networks, potentially allowing more established analysis methods for ReLUs to be applied in transformer verification. In this regard, it paves the path to future research in transformer verification.

Robustness Verification Problem
Robustness refers to the behavioral consistency of transformers under adversarial perturbations and has been widely recognized as an essential property for their reliability. Consider a natural language processing task where
X
represents a given sentence and each row
X_{\mathit{i,:}}
of
X
represents a word. We define a predicate
\Phi\colon\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}\mapsto\{\mathsf{true},\mathsf{false}\}
to identify the valid space of the perturbed input
X^{\prime}
, as follows:

\Phi(X^{\prime}):=\forall d\in D.\left(\left\lVert X^{\prime}_{\mathit{d,:}}-X_{\mathit{d,:}}\right\rVert_{p}\leq\epsilon\right)
,
where
D\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}
is a subset of the words selected to add perturbations. The predicate
\Phi
imposes the following requirement to the perturbed input
X^{\prime}
: for each perturbed word
X^{\prime}_{\mathit{d,:}}
, the distance from the original word
X_{\mathit{d,:}}
should be less than a threshold
\epsilon
under
L_{p}
-norm.

The robustness property requires that, for any perturbed input
X^{\prime}
that holds
\Phi(X^{\prime})
, the output
f(X^{\prime})
of the transformer should satisfy
\Psi(f(X^{\prime}))
, where
\Psi\colon\mathbb{R}^{c}\mapsto\{\mathsf{true},\mathsf{false}\}
is a predicate defined as follows:

\displaystyle\textstyle\Psi(Y):=\left(\min_{1\leq i\leq c,i\neq i_{l}}\left(Y_{{i_{l}}}-Y_{{i}}\right)\right)>0

(3)

where
Y_{{i}}
denotes the
i
-th element of the vector
Y
, and
i_{l}
is the label of
X
inferred by the transformer, i.e.,

\forall i\in\{1,\ldots,c\}.f(X)_{i_{l}}-f(X)_{i}>0
.

The robustness verification problem asks whether a transformer satisfies the robustness property, under a given input
X
and a given
\epsilon
. Moreover, as a common extended application of verification techniques, in this paper, we also aim to find the maximal
\epsilon
under which the transformer remains robust. Notably, this is meaningful in practice, e.g., the certified robustness within
\epsilon
can relief the concern about any synonym substitution attack (see §
1
) within this region.

2.3
Transformer Verification via Linear Relaxation

While verification for neural networks with simple architectures, such as feed-forward neural networks, has been extensively studied, verification for transformers still remains at a very early stage. The challenge arises from the high non-linearity of their inference function
f
(see §
2.2
), which consists of multiple non-linear components such as self-attention layers. While pursuing an exact bound of
f
is difficult, linear relaxation
[
23
]
provides a promising way to over-approximate the output range of
f
, thereby achieving high efficiency.

At the core of this approach are the construction and propagation of linear constraints used to bound the output ranges of non-linear operations throughout transformer inferences, which finally lead to linear bounds of transformer outputs. These linear bounds constitute over-approximation of transformer outputs, such that, as long as the over-approximation satisfies the robustness property, the original output must also satisfy it, thereby certifying robustness satisfaction.

In
[
23
]
, the linear constraints are constructed depending on the operations in each layer. As shown in Eq.
1

2

\mathrm{ReLU}

\mathrm{ReLU}
activation functions, pooling) have been extensively studied in classic neural network verification
[
24

33

6
]
, we mainly focus on the self-attention layers uniquely existing in transformers and leave the relaxation of other functions in §
0.B
. As shown in Eq.
2
, a self-attention layer mainly consists of two matrix multiplications and a softmax function. Below, we elaborate on the linear relaxation for the first matrix multiplication

\displaystyle\geq f_{\mathsf{qk}}^{\mathrm{L_{1}}}(Q_{\mathit{ih}},K_{\mathit{jh}})+f_{\mathrm{relu}}^{\mathrm{L}}\left(f_{\mathsf{qk}}^{\mathrm{L_{2}}}(Q_{\mathit{ih}},K_{\mathit{jh}})-f_{\mathsf{qk}}^{\mathrm{L_{1}}}(Q_{\mathit{ih}},K_{\mathit{jh}})\right)

(11)

\displaystyle=f_{\mathsf{qk}}^{\mathrm{L^{*}}}(Q_{\mathit{ih}},K_{\mathit{jh}})

Consequently, we need to find an optimal linear bound
f_{\mathrm{relu}}^{\mathrm{L}}
for ReLU that can minimize the approximation error to improve the precision of verification.

Finding linear bounds for ReLUs is an important issue and has been extensively studied
[
24
,
33
,
32
,
30
,
36
]
in classic neural network verification (i.e., verification of feed-forward neural networks with fully-connected layers).
In general, given the definition of ReLU, the lower bound
f_{\mathrm{relu}}^{\mathrm{L}}(x)
of ReLU can be identified by a parametric function:

\displaystyle f_{\mathrm{relu}}^{\mathrm{L}}(x)=\alpha\cdot x,\text{ where }\alpha\in[0,1]

(12)

The selection of the parameter
\alpha
is a tricky question and has a significant impact to the approximation precision. The approach for deciding it constitutes our main technical contribution, which will be elaborated on in §
4
.

Explanation of Existing Approach
We show how the ReLU-based bound representation in Eq.
8
and Eq.
9
can be used to explain the approximation approach in
[
23
]
, highlighting the connection between transformer verification and classic neural network verification. Specifically, when
\alpha=0
, by Eq.
10
, Eq.
11
and Eq.
12
, we can derive the following:

\displaystyle f_{\mathsf{qk}}^{\mathrm{U^{*}}}(Q_{\mathit{ih}},K_{\mathit{jh}})=f_{\mathsf{qk}}^{\mathrm{U_{1}}}(Q_{\mathit{ih}},K_{\mathit{jh}}),\quad f_{\mathsf{qk}}^{\mathrm{L^{*}}}(Q_{\mathit{ih}},K_{\mathit{jh}})=f_{\mathsf{qk}}^{\mathrm{L_{1}}}(Q_{\mathit{ih}},K_{\mathit{jh}})

Namely,
\alpha=0
is essentially the selection in
[
23
]
. Dually, when
\alpha=1
, by Eq.
10
, Eq.
11
and Eq.
12
, we can derive that:

\displaystyle f_{\mathsf{qk}}^{\mathrm{U^{*}}}(Q_{\mathit{ih}},K_{\mathit{jh}})=f_{\mathsf{qk}}^{\mathrm{U_{2}}}(Q_{\mathit{ih}},K_{\mathit{jh}}),\quad f_{\mathsf{qk}}^{\mathrm{L^{*}}}(Q_{\mathit{ih}},K_{\mathit{jh}})=f_{\mathsf{qk}}^{\mathrm{L_{2}}}(Q_{\mathit{ih}},K_{\mathit{jh}})

Namely,
\alpha=1
is essentially the selection of Eq.
5
.

However, existing works
[
30
,
33
,
36
]
have shown that, none of the above strategies, i.e., by fixing
\alpha
to be
1
or to be
0
, is the optimal way of ReLU relaxation. This motivates us to explore more effective approaches to refine the approximation.

4
Our Proposed Abstraction Refinement Approach

As stated in §
3.2
, given the ReLU-based representation in Eq.
8
and Eq.
9
, we can reduce the problem of finding optimal planar bounds for dot products, to the problem of finding optimal
\alpha
, i.e., the slope of linear lower bounds of ReLUs. To this end, we extend two approaches from classic neural network verification: the first strategy inspired by
[
24
]
selects
\alpha
based on the input ranges of ReLUs, with the aim of minimizing the approximation error, measured by the area of the over-approximation; the second one inspired by
[
32
]
formalizes the approximation refinement as an optimization problem with
\alpha
as decision variables, and calls modern optimization solvers to iteratively refine the approximation until verifying the problem. In the following sections, we elaborate on these two approaches.

4.1
r-
\mathsf{BuFFeT}
: Rule-Based
\alpha
Selection

Selecting proper
\alpha
in Eq.
12
as the lower bound of ReLU is an important but challenging problem in classic neural network verification. Depending on the input range of ReLU, there could be distinct approximation error due to the selection of
\alpha
. Here, the approximation error is often evaluated according to the area of the over-approximation brought by the linear bounds.

Let
\mathit{u}
and
\mathit{l}
be the upper and lower bounds of the input range of a ReLU. In our context, for the upper bounds of dot products
Q_{\mathit{ih}}K_{\mathit{jh}}
, by Eq.
8
and Eq.
4
&
5
,
\mathit{u}
and
\mathit{l}
are the upper and lower bounds of the following ReLU input:

\displaystyle f_{\mathsf{qk}}^{\mathrm{U_{1}}}(Q_{\mathit{ih}},K_{\mathit{jh}})-f_{\mathsf{qk}}^{\mathrm{U_{2}}}(Q_{\mathit{ih}},K_{\mathit{jh}})=(k^{\mathrm{U}}-k^{\mathrm{L}})Q_{\mathit{ih}}+(q^{\mathrm{L}}-q^{\mathrm{U}})K_{\mathit{jh}}-q^{\mathrm{L}}k^{\mathrm{U}}+q^{\mathrm{U}}k^{\mathrm{L}}

(13)

Similarly, we can also derive for the lower bounds of dot products by Eq.
9
and Eq.
4
&
5
, in which case the ReLU input is as follows:

\displaystyle f_{\mathsf{qk}}^{\mathrm{L_{2}}}(Q_{\mathit{ih}},K_{\mathit{jh}})-f_{\mathsf{qk}}^{\mathrm{L_{1}}}(Q_{\mathit{ih}},K_{\mathit{jh}})=(k^{\mathrm{U}}-k^{\mathrm{L}})Q_{\mathit{ih}}+(q^{\mathrm{U}}-q^{\mathrm{L}})K_{\mathit{jh}}-q^{\mathrm{U}}k^{\mathrm{U}}+q^{\mathrm{L}}k^{\mathrm{L}}

(14)

Given Eq.
13
and Eq.
14
, we can derive the values of
\mathit{u}
and
\mathit{l}
, by computing the upper and lower bounds of these ReLU inputs. For instance, the upper bound
\mathit{u}
and the lower bound
\mathit{l}
of Eq.
13
equals to:

\displaystyle\mathit{u}=(k^{\mathrm{U}}-k^{\mathrm{L}})Q_{\mathit{ih}}^{\mathrm{U}}+(q^{\mathrm{L}}-q^{\mathrm{U}})K_{\mathit{jh}}^{\mathrm{L}}-q^{\mathrm{L}}k^{\mathrm{U}}+q^{\mathrm{U}}k^{\mathrm{L}}

(15)

\displaystyle\mathit{l}=(k^{\mathrm{U}}-k^{\mathrm{L}})Q_{\mathit{ih}}^{\mathrm{L}}+(q^{\mathrm{L}}-q^{\mathrm{U}})K_{\mathit{jh}}^{\mathrm{U}}-q^{\mathrm{L}}k^{\mathrm{U}}+q^{\mathrm{U}}k^{\mathrm{L}}

where
Q_{\mathit{ih}}^{\mathrm{U}}
and
Q_{\mathit{ih}}^{\mathrm{L}}
are respectively the upper and lower bounds of
Q_{\mathit{ih}}
, and
K_{\mathit{jh}}^{\mathrm{U}}
and
K_{\mathit{jh}}^{\mathrm{L}}
are respectively the upper and lower bounds of
K_{\mathit{jh}}
. As explained in §
2.3
, these bounds can be derived from the preceding operations in transformer inferences, hence we can obtain the value of
\mathit{u}
and
\mathit{l}
. The bounds for Eq.
14
can be obtained similarly.

Below, we consider how to choose
\alpha
in the case when
\mathit{u}\in(0,\infty)
and
\mathit{l}\in(-\infty,0)
only; in other cases (i.e., when
\mathit{u}\in(-\infty,0)
or
\mathit{l}\in(0,\infty)
), the ReLU becomes linear and so we do not need to choose
\alpha
.

As shown in Fig.
3(b)
and
3(c)
, the area of over-approximation is
\frac{|\mathit{u}|^{2}+|\mathit{u}||\mathit{l}|}{2}
if
\alpha
is set to be 0, and the area of over-approximation is
\frac{|\mathit{l}|^{2}+|\mathit{u}||\mathit{l}|}{2}
if
\alpha
is set to be 1. Therefore,
[
24
]
introduces the following condition-based policy:

i)

when
|\mathit{l}|>|\mathit{u}|
,
\alpha
is set to be
0
, because the area of over-approximation when
\alpha=0
is smaller than that when
\alpha=1
;

ii)

when
|\mathit{u}|>|\mathit{l}|
,
\alpha
is set to be
1
, because the area of over-approximation when
\alpha=1
is smaller than that when
\alpha=0
.

Namely, this policy takes the better selection in both of the cases.

In our context, as we can compute
\mathit{u}
and
\mathit{l}
for each ReLU, we can apply the same policy for selecting proper
\alpha
:

1)

when
|\mathit{l}|>|\mathit{u}|
, we set
\alpha
to be 0, which essentially equals to adopting the linear bounds in Eq.
4
;

2)

when
|\mathit{u}|>|\mathit{l}|
, we set
\alpha
to be 1, which essentially equals to adopting the linear bounds in Eq.
5
.

4.2
o-
\mathsf{BuFFeT}
: Optimization-Based Iterative Refinement

While the rule-based strategy in §
4.1
can refine the approximation, it only offers two choices for bounding each ReLU, i.e.,
\alpha=0
or
\alpha=1
, which may not be the optimal choices under all different cases. To further refine the approximation, we propose an optimization-based approach o-
\mathsf{BuFFeT}
, which treats
\alpha
as the decision variables and iteratively searches for the optimal
\alpha
to optimize the refinement.

Recall the specification of transformer verification formalized in Eq.
3
; as we require that

\left(\min_{1\leq i\leq c,i\neq i_{l}}\left(Y_{{i_{l}}}-Y_{{i}}\right)\right)>0
,
verification consists in showing that the lower bound
\underline{\mathsf{Margin}}
of
\mathsf{Margin}
, as defined below, is greater than 0.

\displaystyle\mathsf{Margin}:=\left(\min_{1\leq i\leq c,i\neq i_{l}}\left(Y_{{i_{l}}}-Y_{{i}}\right)\right)

(16)

The bounds of
\mathsf{Margin}
are decided by the bounds of
Y
, i.e., the output vector of a given transformer. Further, the bounds of
Y
can be obtained by layer-by-layer propagation, depending on the operations in the transformer. Below, we only elaborate on the computation of the bounds for dot products in self-attention layers, as we have done in previous sections; for other layers, we adopt the bounding methods in literature as introduced in §
0.B
.

Given
f_{\mathrm{relu}}^{\mathrm{L}}(x)=\alpha\cdot x
as a linear lower bound of ReLU in Eq.
12
, we can expand
f_{\mathsf{qk}}^{\mathrm{U^{*}}}(Q_{\mathit{ih}},K_{\mathit{jh}})
in Eq.
10
as follows:

\displaystyle f_{\mathsf{qk}}^{\mathrm{U^{*}}}(Q_{\mathit{ih}},K_{\mathit{jh}})

\displaystyle=f_{\mathsf{qk}}^{\mathrm{U_{1}}}(Q_{\mathit{ih}},K_{\mathit{jh}})-f_{\mathrm{relu}}^{\mathrm{L}}\left(f_{\mathsf{qk}}^{\mathrm{U_{1}}}(Q_{\mathit{ih}},K_{\mathit{jh}})-f_{\mathsf{qk}}^{\mathrm{U_{2}}}(Q_{\mathit{ih}},K_{\mathit{jh}})\right)

(17)

\displaystyle=(1-\alpha)f_{\mathsf{qk}}^{\mathrm{U_{1}}}(Q_{\mathit{ih}},K_{\mathit{jh}})+\alpha f_{\mathsf{qk}}^{\mathrm{U_{2}}}(Q_{\mathit{ih}},K_{\mathit{jh}})

\displaystyle=\left((1-\alpha)k^{\mathrm{U}}+\alpha k^{\mathrm{L}}\right)Q_{\mathit{ih}}+\left((1-\alpha)q^{\mathrm{L}}+\alpha q^{\mathrm{U}}\right)K_{\mathit{jh}}-\left((1-\alpha)q^{\mathrm{L}}k^{\mathrm{U}}+\alpha q^{\mathrm{U}}k^{\mathrm{L}}\right)

Similarly, we can expand
f_{\mathsf{qk}}^{\mathrm{L^{*}}}(Q_{\mathit{ih}},K_{\mathit{jh}})
as follows:

\displaystyle f_{\mathsf{qk}}^{\mathrm{L^{*}}}(Q_{\mathit{ih}},K_{\mathit{jh}})

\displaystyle=f_{\mathsf{qk}}^{\mathrm{L_{1}}}(Q_{\mathit{ih}},K_{\mathit{jh}})+f_{\mathrm{relu}}^{\mathrm{L}}\left(f_{\mathsf{qk}}^{\mathrm{L_{2}}}(Q_{\mathit{ih}},K_{\mathit{jh}})-f_{\mathsf{qk}}^{\mathrm{L_{1}}}(Q_{\mathit{ih}},K_{\mathit{jh}})\right)

(18)

\displaystyle=\alpha f_{\mathsf{qk}}^{\mathrm{L_{2}}}(Q_{\mathit{ih}},K_{\mathit{jh}})+(1-\alpha)f_{\mathsf{qk}}^{\mathrm{L_{1}}}(Q_{\mathit{ih}},K_{\mathit{jh}})

\displaystyle=\left(\alpha k^{\mathrm{U}}+(1-\alpha)k^{\mathrm{L}}\right)Q_{\mathit{ih}}+\left(\alpha q^{\mathrm{U}}+(1-\alpha)q^{\mathrm{L}}\right)K_{\mathit{jh}}-\left(\alpha q^{\mathrm{U}}k^{\mathrm{U}}+(1-\alpha)q^{\mathrm{L}}k^{\mathrm{L}}\right)

In this way,
f_{\mathsf{qk}}^{\mathrm{U^{*}}}(Q_{\mathit{ih}},K_{\mathit{jh}})
and
f_{\mathsf{qk}}^{\mathrm{L^{*}}}(Q_{\mathit{ih}},K_{\mathit{jh}})
are two parametric planar bounds that can be propagated throughout the layers of transformers, finally participating in the computation of transformer output
Y
.

Optimization Approach
Now we present our optimization-based approach. In this optimization, we treat the bound computations in Eq.
17
and Eq.
18
for dot products, as well as the bound computations for other operations, as constraints.
Then, as the usual practice in optimization (e.g., neural network training) and for the sake of numerical stability, we adopt the standard softplus/logistic loss function as the objective of optimization:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}:=\log(1+\exp(-\,\underline{\mathsf{Margin}})).

(19)

This leads to an optimization problem as follows:

\displaystyle\min\mathcal{L},\quad\text{s.t. }\forall\alpha\in[0,1]

In this optimization, as the value of
\alpha
for each ReLU varies, the loss function
\mathcal{L}
also varies, because the variation of
\alpha
can change the upper and lower bounds of each dot product operation, which subsequently affects the upper and lower bounds of the input of the succeeding operation, i.e., the ReLU input of the succeeding operation (see Eq.
15
for an explanation). The optimization can be terminated as soon as the value of
\underline{\mathsf{Margin}}
is positive, which signifies that such a group of
\alpha
has been found that can ensure
\mathsf{Margin}
to be positive, thereby certifying the robustness satisfaction of the transformer.

One notable advantage of our formalization consists in that, the derivative of the loss w.r.t. each
\alpha
can be easily computed by the automatic differentiation features in modern machine learning platforms (e.g., PyTorch), so we can call off-the-shelf optimization solvers, such as Adam, a widely used gradient-based optimization solver, to efficiently solve this problem. Consequently, this allows us to optimize
\alpha
in a manner similar to neural network training, leveraging the extensive advances and optimizations developed in that domain.

Theorem 4.1
(Soundness)

Both r-
\mathsf{BuFFeT}
and o-
\mathsf{BuFFeT}
are sound.

Proof sketch
As long as
\alpha\in[0,1]
, by Eq.
8
-
11
, the derived bounds for each dot product are sound over-approximation, hence both approaches are sound.

Related Work

Classic Neural Network Verification

Verification for feed-forward networks has been studied widely; overviews can be found in recent surveys
[
19
,
17
,
4
]
and the competition reports
[
6
,
16
,
5
]
. These works can be split into two families:
complete
methods and
incomplete
methods. Complete methods aim to prove a property by exhaustive exploration of state space, often with SMT
[
14
]
, MILP
[
26
]
or branch and bound
[
7
]
; they are more expensive but precise. Incomplete methods use abstract interpretation
[
12
,
24
,
33
]
to build convex bounds layer by layer, which scales better to large models.
Typical bounding methods include DeepPoly
[
24
]
, CROWN
[
33
]
, which construct linear upper/lower bounds for nonlinear layers (e.g., ReLU) and propagate them through the network.
Refinement on top of these relaxations (e.g., via branch-and-bound
[
7
,
22
]
) further tightens bounds when needed, achieving both efficiency and completeness. Our approaches are in line with them, but we target transformer verification that remains underexplored.

Verification of Transformers

Compared with classic neural network verification, transformer verification is new and more challenging because self-attention introduces bilinear terms and softmax.
Existing works extend abstract-interpretation-style relaxations to attention. Shi et al.
[
23
]
give a foundational framework that designs linear bounds for transformer components, including a planar relaxation for dot products in self-attention, and then propagates these bounds end-to-end.
Later, Bonaert et al.
[
3
]
study faster and more precise certification techniques for transformers, which introduces a new multi-norm zonotope abstract domain.
Another study
[
18
]
considers
sparsemax
-based transformers and reduces robustness verification to a Mixed Integer Quadratically Constrained Programming (MIQCP) problem. This enables exact reasoning but is less scalable.
Zhang et al.
[
35
]
propose a general linear relaxation for multi-dimensional functions such as softmax. Meanwhile, Wei et al.
[
29
]
give convex lower bounds and concave upper bounds for the softmax that fit convex optimization formulations.

Our view is that attention is the core mechanism and dot-products appear everywhere, bringing significant impact to verification results. While the planar dot-product bound of
[
23
]
is promising, it may be not sufficiently tight to handle real-world models. To this end, we devise a more precise construction that can significantly reduce over-approximation, leading to tighter certified bounds.

There is also a line of work
[
8
,
9
]
that considers the meaningfulness of verifying transformers in the context of NLP. Normal NLP pipelines involve an embedding function that encodes raw textual words into vectors, so an
\epsilon
-ball input region of a given textual word in embedding space may contain few words that are practically meaningful. Our problem definition in §
2.2
also suffers from this problem, because we consider perturbations to inputs in embedding space but bypass the embedding function; while this brings us the generality to handle continuous input modalities in domains, such as audio processing and computer vision, its direct application to local robustness verification of NLP models may lead to limited practical implication. Nevertheless, various techniques that can handle this issue, have been proposed in literature
[
8
,
9
]
, e.g., using a “hyperrectangle” to encompass meaningful words; in practice, our approach can be adopted collaboratively with those techniques, and serve as an off-the-shelf verifier in the general pipeline proposed in
[
9
]
, with improved precision compared to existing verifiers.

7
Conclusion and Future Work

We present an abstract refinement approach for verification of transformers, inspired by linear relaxation of ReLU. We represent the aggregated bound by ReLUs and exploit two strategies to achieve refined linear bounds. r-
\mathsf{BuFFeT}
is rule-based, which decides ReLUs’ bound based on the input ranges of ReLUs. o-
\mathsf{BuFFeT}
formalizes the problem as an optimization problem, and calls solvers to iteratively explore the optimal bounds. Our evaluation results demonstrate the strengths of our approach in verification precision.

As future work, we consider two directions:

1)

we can combine the different strategies to improve efficiency, e.g., initialize
\alpha
in the optimization of o-
\mathsf{BuFFeT}
by using the
\alpha
from the baseline;

2)

we also plan to evaluate our approaches on transformers in other domains, e.g., transformer-based controllers
[
15
]
.

Relaxation for Unary Nonlinear Function

In the case of unary nonlinear operations, such as the ReLU function
\sigma(\cdot)
, the output of the
l
-th layer is obtained by applying a elemental-wise nonlinearity to the
(l-1)
-th layer:

\Phi^{(l)}_{i,:}(X)=\sigma(\Phi^{(l-1)}_{i,:}(X))

(24)

To enable linear bound propagation through such nonlinear activation function, we relax the
\sigma(\cdot)
using a pair of linear functions that tightly bound it from below and above.
Specifically, for each neuron indexed by
j
, we define a linear lower and upper relaxation:

\displaystyle w^{(l-1,l),L}_{i,j}\Phi^{(l-1)}_{i,j}(X)+b^{(l-1,l),L}_{i,j}\leq\sigma(\Phi^{(l-1)}_{i,j}(X))\leq w^{(l-1,l),U}_{i,j}\Phi^{(l-1)}_{i,j}(X)+b^{(l-1,l),U}_{i,j}

Here,
w^{(l-1,l),L}_{i,j}
and
w^{(l-1,l),U}_{i,j}
are the slopes of the linear abstract transformation of lower and upper bounds, respectively, while
b^{(l-1,l),L}_{i,j}
and
b^{(l-1,l),U}_{i,j}
are the corresponding intercepts.
These parameters are typically determined based on the concrete lower and upper bounds of the input to the nonlinearity, and they ensure that the relaxation tightly encloses the true nonlinear activation within a convex region.

Specially, for an upper bound,
w^{(l-1,l),U}_{i,j}
is set to the slope of the line connecting maximum and minimum point in cross-zero situation.
b^{(l-1,l),U}_{i,j}
is the corresponding intercept. There is a judgment when considering lower bounds. If the negative domain is greater than the positive domain, set the slope to 0; otherwise, set it to 1. The intercept is always 0

33

a:=f_{\mathsf{qk}}^{\mathrm{U_{1}}}(Q_{\mathit{ih}},K_{\mathit{jh}})

a>b

\text{ReLU}(a-b)=a-b

f_{\mathsf{qk}}^{\mathrm{U}}(Q_{\mathit{ih}},K_{\mathit{jh}})=a-(a-b)=b=\min(a,b)

f_{\mathsf{qk}}^{\mathrm{U}}(Q_{\mathit{ih}},K_{\mathit{jh}})=\min(f_{\mathsf{qk}}^{\mathrm{U_{1}}}(Q_{\mathit{ih}},K_{\mathit{jh}}),f_{\mathsf{qk}}^{\mathrm{U_{2}}}(Q_{\mathit{ih}},K_{\mathit{jh}}))

\text{ReLU}(b-a)=0

Appendix 0.F
Binary Search

As mentioned in Section
2.2
, an important application of verification is to find the maximum certified safe radius
\epsilon
, under perturbing a given input
X
is provably safe. There could be different ways to search for such
\epsilon
. In our experiments, we adopt a natural and common method following
[
33
,
23
]
, which involves a binary search method.

Algorithm
1
outlines the procedure for searching such
\epsilon
, under a fixed verification method. The algorithm begins by initializing the minimum and maximum of
\epsilon
as
0
and
0.01
, respectively. It then exponentially increases the upper bound until the safety condition
\texttt{verify\_safety}(X,\epsilon)
fails, thereby identifying an interval
[\texttt{min\_eps},\texttt{max\_eps}]
that contains the boundary of safety.

Subsequently, a binary search is conducted within this interval for a fixed number of iterations (num_iters) to refine the estimate of the maximum safe
\epsilon
. At each step, the midpoint
m
is checked using the verification oracle. If
m
satisfies the safety property, it becomes the new minimum value; otherwise, it becomes the maximum value. After convergence, the algorithm returns
\epsilon:=\texttt{min\_eps}
as the largest radius for which the model can be formally verified to behave correctly.

Appendix 0.G
Horizontal Comparison with LSE
[
29
]

We additionally conduct a small-scale horizontal comparison with the LSE
softmax relaxation proposed by Wei et al.
[
29
]
. The goal of
this experiment is not to position o-
\mathsf{BuFFeT}
and LSE as mutually exclusive
alternatives. Instead, the two techniques refine different sources of
approximation error in transformer verification. LSE directly tightens the
relaxation of the softmax operator, while o-
\mathsf{BuFFeT}
refines the linear relaxation
of attention dot products by fusing alternative planar bounds through
ReLU-based abstractions. Therefore, the two methods can be naturally combined
