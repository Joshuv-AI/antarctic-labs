Hypocoercivity-preserving space-time Galerkin methods for kinetic Fokker-Planck equations
Zhaonan Dong, Emmanuil H. Georgoulis

Abstract
We design and analyse a family of hypocoercivity-preserving fully discrete Galerkin methods for the (inhomogeneous) kinetic Fokker–Planck (kFP) equations, a class of evolution partial differential equations (PDE) with degenerate diffusion. The key idea in the method design is to mimic the framework of enhanced quadratic forms of Villani [ 23 ] and, thus, to arrive at a coercive bilinear form in a sufficiently strong exponentially weighted norm, admitting spectral gap/Poincaré inequality, despite the degeneracy. The problem is posed as a fourth order in space evolution PDE on the whole space ℝ d × ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d} . The spatial discretisation employs continuous piecewise polynomial approximation spaces, subordinate to simplicial and/or box-type meshes comprising finite, as well as ‘infinite’ elements. The non-conformity is treated by constructing appropriate numerical fluxes in the spirit of C 0 C^{0} -interior penalty ( C 0 C^{0} -IP) methods for fourth order elliptic problems. The proof of coercivity of the method requires new trace inverse inequalities for exponentially weighted norms on d d -dimensional simplicial and box-type domains, and also for prismatic domains with infinite length. We prove weighted trace inverse inequalities for all the above cases of domains and for a variety of exponential weights. These may be of independent interest. Once coercivity is established for the Galerkin method, the proof of exponential convergence to the equilibrium state follows by employing an exponentially weighted Poincaré inequality. A fully discrete extension of the method is also considered by coupling the non-standard spatial discretisation with an h ​ p hp -version discontinuous Galerkin time-stepping scheme, allowing for arbitrary-order temporal approximations. The exponential convergence to the equilibrium state for the fully discrete scheme is also proven. The proposed methods preserve the total mass and exhibit provably exponential convergence to the equilibrium state, a manifestation of ‘numerical hypocoercivity,’ making them well suited for long-time kFP simulations. A series of numerical experiments is presented to validate the theoretical results and investigate the convergence behaviour of the proposed method.

Introduction

Numerous physical, chemical, biological and social dynamic processes are often characterised by convergence to long-time equilibria. In many important cases the diffusion/dissipation required to arrive to such equilibria is explicitly present in
some
of the spatial directions only. This, somewhat counter-intuitive at first, state of affairs suggests that decay to equilibrium is due to finer hidden structure, which allows for the transport terms in the dynamics to ‘propagate dissipation’ also to the directions which no explicit dissipation exists in the model.

An archetypical such model problem is the classical inhomogeneous kinetic Fokker-Planck equation, reading: find

The proof of trend to equilibrium for the kinetic Fokker-Planck equation was first given by Hérau & Nier in

The importance of designing computational methods for kinetic equations cannot be overestimated, due to their abundance of in modern statistical physics, which has sprawled into other modelling areas also
[
21

19
]
in recent years. Monte-Carlo (MC) approaches have enjoyed the attention of the computational physics and chemistry community over the last 50 years or so
[
19
]
. The slow and statistical-in-nature convergence rate of MC simulations and their structural difficulty in retaining conservation properties of the solutions at the discrete level, have lead researchers to seek alternatives; see
[
6
]
and the references therein, including finite differences, spectral methods, and Galerkin/finite element approaches. In particular, discontinuous Galerkin (dG) methods have been used for the approximation of Fokker-Planck; see, the influential work
[
17
]
for a DG method for a class of equations including Fokker-Planck.

Despite their important scientific value, the aforementioned works are not concerned with preserving hypocoercivity-inducing structures at the discrete level. Nonetheless, preserving such hypocoercivity properties appears to be important to guarantee quantitatively correct simulations, especially for long-time simulations. To see this, we write (
1
) in the abbreviated form
f_{t}+\mathcal{L}f=0
and we consider a discretisation method

\partial f_{h}+\mathcal{L}_{h}f_{h}=0,

of (
1

be the discretisation error. Subtracting the method from the equation gives

\partial e+\mathcal{L}_{h}e=\mathcal{R}

\mathcal{R}:=\partial f-f_{t}+(\mathcal{L}_{h}-\mathcal{L})f
the discretisation residual.
If
\mathcal{L}_{h}
does not preserve hypocoercivity, we cannot infer a spectral gap for
\mathcal{L}_{h}
. Thus, we can only estimate the error via classical energy argument, for which classical (discrete) Grönwall-type estimates give bounds of the form

\|{e(t)}\|_{{L_{2}(\mu)}}^{2}\leq e^{\delta t}\big(\delta^{-1}\|{\mathcal{R}}\|_{{L_{2}(I;L_{2}(\mu))}}^{2}+\|{e(0)}\|_{{L_{2}(\mu)}}^{2}\big).

(11)

for any

Given the importance of kinetic simulations in various high-risk (e.g., nuclear, space, etc.) industries, the development of numerical methods with realistic error behaviour is of significance.

To address this challenge, it is desirable to port hypocoercivity ideas to numerical methods. To date, there exist extremely few, yet quite inspiring, works investigating the hypocoercivity properties of finite difference operators. By applying the abstract semigroup result of Villani

The first proof of a non-standard hypocoercivity-compatible Galerkin finite element method for the Kolmogorov equation given
[

]

In this work, we are concerned with designing and analysing the first provably hypocoercivity-preserving fully discrete Galerkin method for the kinetic Fokker-Planck (kFP) equation (
1
), (
2
). This is achieved by first constructing a special variational form of of the kFP equation in the spirit of
[

and, subsequently, by addressing the additional challenges posed by 1) the lack of coercivity of the resulting bilinear form due to the presence of the confining potential

\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}

[
23
]
estimating the Hessian of the potential

(see Lemma
3.1
below).

The second challenge is considerably more involved and requires a number of technical developments. First, the 3rd order derivatives are treated via a non-standard set of numerical fluxes, which are able to transport information to the correct directions without introducing additional numerical diffusion and, crucially, are also compatible with respect to the algebraic structure that we seek to preserve. Furthermore, to address the discretization of the 4th-order partial derivatives in
v
appearing in the modified variational form, we employ a non-conforming
C^{0}
-interior penalty-type set of numerical fluxes. The proof of stability of such discretisations require the availability of, so-called, trace inverse inequalities bounding norms of polynomials on the boundary of a domain (element) by the same norms on the domain itself. To that end, we prove new trace inverse inequalities in
L_{2}
-norms with a variety of exponentially weighted measures. Moreover, we also prove trace inverse inequalities with exponential weights on

The third challenge, i.e., the problem posed on the whole

The spatial discretisation is complemented by an
hp

The remainder of this work is structured as follows. In Section

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{d}\!\|{\sqrt{\mathcal{A}}{\nabla}_{\!v,x}u_{v_{j}}}\|_{{L_{2}(\mu)}}^{2}\!=

\displaystyle\alpha\|{{\nabla}_{\!v}{\nabla}_{\!v}^{T}u}\|_{{L_{2}(\mu)}}^{2}\!+\!2\beta\int\!{\nabla}_{\!v}{\nabla}_{\!v}^{T}u:{\nabla}_{\!x}{\nabla}_{\!v}^{T}u\,{\rm d}\mu\!+\!\gamma\|{{\nabla}_{\!x}{\nabla}_{\!v}^{T}u}\|_{{L_{2}(\mu)}}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\alpha(1-\epsilon_{3})\|{{\nabla}_{\!v}{\nabla}_{\!v}^{T}u}\|_{{L_{2}(\mu)}}^{2}+\Big(\gamma-\frac{\beta^{2}}{\alpha\epsilon_{3}}\Big)\|{{\nabla}_{\!x}{\nabla}_{\!v}^{T}u}\|_{{L_{2}(\mu)}}^{2}.

Finally, for any
\epsilon_{4}>0
, we have

\displaystyle(\Big(\begin{array}[]{cc}(1+\alpha)I&\alpha I\\
\beta I&\beta I\end{array}\Big){\nabla}_{\!v,x}u,{\nabla}_{\!v,x}w)_{L_{2}(\mu)}\geq

\displaystyle\Big(1+\alpha-\frac{\epsilon_{4}(\alpha+\beta)^{2}}{2\beta}\Big)\|{{\nabla}_{\!v}u}\|_{{L_{2}(\mu)}}^{2}

\displaystyle+\beta\big(1-(2\epsilon_{4})^{-1}\big)\|{{\nabla}_{\!x}u}\|_{{L_{2}(\mu)}}^{2}.

Combining the above, we deduce

\displaystyle a(u,u)\geq

\displaystyle\Big(1+\alpha-\frac{\beta^{2}\epsilon_{1}C_{1}+\epsilon_{1}^{-1}}{2}-\frac{\gamma^{2}\epsilon_{2}C_{1}}{2\beta}-\frac{\epsilon_{4}(\alpha+\beta)^{2}}{2\beta}\Big)\|{{\nabla}_{\!v}u}\|_{{L_{2}(\mu)}}^{2}

\displaystyle+\beta\Big(1-(2\epsilon_{2})^{-1}-(2\epsilon_{4})^{-1}\Big)\|{{\nabla}_{\!x}u}\|_{{L_{2}(\mu)}}^{2}+\alpha(1-\epsilon_{3})\|{{\nabla}_{\!v}{\nabla}_{\!v}^{T}u}\|_{{L_{2}(\mu)}}^{2}

\displaystyle+\Big(\gamma-\frac{\beta^{2}}{\alpha\epsilon_{3}}-\frac{\beta^{2}\epsilon_{1}C_{1}}{2}-\frac{\gamma^{2}\epsilon_{2}C_{1}}{2\beta}\Big)\|{{\nabla}_{\!x}{\nabla}_{\!v}^{T}u}\|_{{L_{2}(\mu)}}^{2}.

We want to optimise now all the parameters
\epsilon_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,4
and
\alpha,\beta,\gamma
to maximise the coefficients of
\|{{\nabla}_{\!v}u}\|_{{L_{2}(\mu)}}^{2}
and
\|{{\nabla}_{\!x}u}\|_{{L_{2}(\mu)}}^{2}
. For instance, letting
\epsilon_{1}=(C_{1}\alpha)^{-1}
,
\epsilon_{2}=(2C_{1}\alpha)^{-1}
,
\epsilon_{3}=5/6
,
\epsilon_{4}=3/5
,
as well as
\beta=\alpha^{2}/2
and
\gamma=2\alpha^{3}/3
, this becomes

\displaystyle a(u,u)\geq

\displaystyle\Big(\frac{2}{5}+\frac{4-5C_{1}}{10}\alpha-\frac{3}{20}\alpha^{2}-\frac{25}{72}\alpha^{3}\Big)\|{{\nabla}_{\!v}u}\|_{{L_{2}(\mu)}}^{2}+\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}\Big(\frac{1}{6}-\alpha C_{1}\Big)\|{{\nabla}_{\!x}u}\|_{{L_{2}(\mu)}}^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{\alpha}{6}\|{{\nabla}_{\!v}{\nabla}_{\!v}^{T}u}\|_{{L_{2}(\mu)}}^{2}+\frac{7\alpha^{3}}{360}\|{{\nabla}_{\!x}{\nabla}_{\!v}^{T}u}\|_{{L_{2}(\mu)}}^{2}.

Selecting now
\alpha>0
small enough, the result follows.
∎

Thus, the presence of a full
H^{1}(\mu)
seminorm allows for the use of a Poincaré inequality on weighted spaces, thereby, allowing to conclude exponential decay as
t_{f}\to\infty
.

Inverse estimates with exponential weights

The proof of (hypo)coercivity for the interior penalty terms in
s_{h}
will require the estimation of the third and fourth terms on the right-hand side of (
20

\|W\|_{L_{2}(\nu,F)}\leq C(T,p)\|W\|_{L_{2}(T,\mu)}
on exponentially weighted norms, with
F

T
, holding for all polynomials
W
up to degree
p
, for some constant
C(T,p)
depending on the shape and size of
T
and
p
. In the present context of weighted norms, we are required to prove new trace inverse inequalities involving exponential weights. These may be of independent interest.

We first prove an one-dimensional estimate for Gaussian-like weights that will be used for the derivation of the multivariate versions.

Theorem 5.1
.

For any
s\in\mathcal{P}_{p}([0,1])
,
p\in\mathbb{N}
, and for
A\geq 0
, we have the bound

s^{2}(0)\leq(2\lfloor(A+B_{+})/4\rfloor+p+3)^{2}\int_{0}^{1}s^{2}(x){\rm e}^{-(Ax^{2}+Bx)/2}\,{\rm d}x,

(22)

with
\lfloor\cdot\rfloor
the integer part and
(\cdot)_{+}
the positive part of a real number, respectively.

Proof.

Let
g:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
with

g(x)=\tilde{g}^{m}(x){\rm e}^{(Ax^{2}+Bx)/4}
, whereby
\tilde{g}(x):=1-(Ax^{2}+Bx)/(4m)
,
for
m\in\mathbb{N}
, to be determined below; cf., also
[
12
]
for a use of a similar function used in the proof of weighted Markov-type inequalities.
We compute

g^{\prime}(x)=-(2Ax+B)(Ax^{2}+Bx)\tilde{g}^{m-1}(x){\rm e}^{(Ax^{2}+Bx)/4}/(16m).

First, we consider the case
B\geq 0
. Then,
(2Ax+B)(Ax^{2}+Bx)\geq 0
for
x\in[0,1]
. If, additionally,
m:=\lfloor(A+B)/4\rfloor+1
, we have
0\leq\tilde{g}(x)
and, thus,

g
is decreasing. Hence,
g(x)\leq g(0)
, for
x\in[0,1]
, we get

{\rm e}^{-(Ax^{2}+Bx)/2}\geq\tilde{g}^{2m}(x)
.
Now, let
s\in\mathcal{P}_{p}([0,1])

|w(a)|^{2}\leq(p+1)^{2}/(b-a)\|{w}\|_{{L_{2}((a,b))}}^{2}

[
24
]
, yields

\displaystyle s^{2}(0)=\big(s(0)\tilde{g}^{m}(0)\big)^{2}\leq

\displaystyle(2\lfloor(A+B)/4\rfloor+p+3)^{2}\int_{0}^{1}s^{2}(x)\tilde{g}^{2m}(x)\,{\rm d}x

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(2\lfloor(A+B)/4\rfloor+p+3)^{2}\int_{0}^{1}s^{2}(x){\rm e}^{-(Ax^{2}+Bx)/2}\,{\rm d}x.

For the case
B<0
, we note that
{\rm e}^{-Bx}\geq 1
for
x\in[0,1]
. Now, starting from (
22
) for

, we have
s^{2}(0)\leq(2\lfloor A/4\rfloor+p+3)^{2}\int_{0}^{1}s^{2}(x){\rm e}^{-Ax^{2}/2}\,{\rm d}x

∎

((\mathcal{V}\cdot{\nabla}_{\!v,x})W,W)_{L_{2}(\mu)}=0.

The (lack of higher) regularity of functions in
V_{h}
requires partially different treatment compared to the proof of Lemma
3.2
for the terms involving higher order derivatives. This is because additional terms now appear on the mesh skeleton
\Gamma
, which will eventually be counteracted by the choice of the stabilisation
s_{h}
. Indeed, we have

\displaystyle((\mathcal{V}\cdot{\nabla}_{\!v,x}^{h}){\nabla}_{\!v,x}W,\mathcal{A}{\nabla}_{\!v,x}W)_{L_{2}(\mu)}=

\displaystyle\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}}\int_{\partial T}\frac{\mathcal{V}\cdot\mathbf{{n}}}{2}|\sqrt{\mathcal{A}}{\nabla}_{\!v,x}W|^{2}\,{\rm d}\nu

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma}\{\kern-4.2679pt\{{{\nabla}_{\!v,x}W}\}\kern-4.2679pt\}^{T}\mathcal{A}[\kern-1.70717pt[{{\nabla}_{\!v,x}W}]\kern-1.70717pt]\mathcal{V}\,{\rm d}\nu.

In the following we will use Young’s inequality exactly as in the proof of Lemma
3.2
and we use the choices
\beta=\alpha^{2}/2
and
\gamma=2\alpha^{3}/3
.
The corresponding bounds to (
16
) and (
17
), using (
39
) (instead of (
15
),)
produce the additional term

+\frac{25\alpha^{3}C_{0}}{144C_{1}}\int_{\Gamma}\{\kern-4.2679pt\{{{\nabla}_{\!v}W}\}\kern-4.2679pt\}^{T}[\kern-1.70717pt[{{\nabla}_{\!v}W}]\kern-1.70717pt]_{x}\nabla V\,{\rm d}\nu,

whenever
[\kern-1.70717pt[{{\nabla}_{\!v}W}]\kern-1.70717pt]_{x}\neq 0
.
From the above, we conclude

\displaystyle a_{h}(W,W)\geq

\displaystyle c_{hc,1}\big(\|{{\nabla}_{\!v}W}\|_{{L_{2}(\mu)}}^{2}+\beta\|{{\nabla}_{\!x}W}\|_{{L_{2}(\mu)}}^{2}\big)+c_{hc,2}\alpha\|{{\nabla}_{\!v}^{h}{\nabla}_{\!v}^{T}W}\|_{{L_{2}(\mu)}}^{2}

(41)

\displaystyle+c_{hc,3}\gamma\|{{\nabla}_{\!x}^{h}{\nabla}_{\!v}^{T}W}\|_{{L_{2}(\mu)}}^{2}+\int_{\Gamma}\{\kern-4.2679pt\{{{\nabla}_{\!v,x}W}\}\kern-4.2679pt\}^{T}\mathcal{A}[\kern-1.70717pt[{{\nabla}_{\!v,x}W}]\kern-1.70717pt]\mathcal{V}\,{\rm d}\nu

\displaystyle+\frac{25\alpha^{3}C_{0}}{144C_{1}}\int_{\Gamma}\{\kern-4.2679pt\{{{\nabla}_{\!v}W}\}\kern-4.2679pt\}^{T}[\kern-1.70717pt[{{\nabla}_{\!v}W}]\kern-1.70717pt]_{x}\nabla V\,{\rm d}\nu,

with
c_{hc,1}=\min\big\{\frac{2}{5}+\frac{4-5C_{1}}{10}\alpha-\frac{3}{20}\alpha^{2}-\frac{25}{72}\alpha^{3},\frac{1}{6}-\alpha C_{1}\big\}
,
c_{hc,2}=1/6
, and
c_{hc,3}=7/240
. Select
\alpha>0
small enough, so that
c_{hc,1}>0
.

Set
\tau=25\alpha^{3}C_{0}/(144C_{1})
, whenever
[\kern-1.70717pt[{{\nabla}_{\!v}W}]\kern-1.70717pt]_{x}\neq 0
, or
\tau=0
otherwise. From the definition of the stabilisation, we have

\displaystyle s_{h}(W,W)=

\displaystyle-\int_{\Gamma}\{\kern-4.2679pt\{{{\nabla}_{\!v,x}W}\}\kern-4.2679pt\}^{T}\mathcal{A}[\kern-1.70717pt[{{\nabla}_{\!v,x}W}]\kern-1.70717pt]\mathcal{V}\,{\rm d}\nu-\frac{25\alpha^{3}C_{0}}{144C_{1}}\int_{\Gamma}\{\kern-4.2679pt\{{{\nabla}_{\!v}W}\}\kern-4.2679pt\}^{T}[\kern-1.70717pt[{{\nabla}_{\!v}W}]\kern-1.70717pt]_{x}\nabla V\,{\rm d}\nu

\displaystyle-2\sum_{j=1}^{d}\int_{\Gamma}\{\kern-4.2679pt\{{{\nabla}_{\!v,x}^{T}W_{v_{j}}}\}\kern-4.2679pt\}\mathcal{A}[\kern-1.70717pt[{{\nabla}_{\!v,x}W}]\kern-1.70717pt]_{v_{j}}\,{\rm d}\nu+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\int_{\Gamma}\sigma|\sqrt{\mathcal{C}}[\kern-1.70717pt[{{\nabla}_{\!v,x}W}]\kern-1.70717pt]_{v_{j}}|^{2}\,{\rm d}\nu,

noting that, crucially, the first two terms on the right-hand side cancel out with the last two terms on the right-hand side of (
41
).

So it remains to bound the last two terms appearing in the stabilisation. To that end,
recall the definition of matrix
\mathcal{C}
and observe the identity

\int_{\Gamma}\{\kern-4.2679pt\{{{\nabla}_{\!v,x}W_{v_{j}}}\}\kern-4.2679pt\}^{T}\mathcal{A}[\kern-1.70717pt[{{\nabla}_{\!v,x}W}]\kern-1.70717pt]_{v_{j}}\,{\rm d}\nu=\int_{\Gamma}\{\kern-4.2679pt\{{{\rm diag}(\sqrt{\alpha}I,\sqrt{\gamma}I){\nabla}_{\!v,x}W_{v_{j}}}\}\kern-4.2679pt\}^{T}\sqrt{\mathcal{C}}[\kern-1.70717pt[{{\nabla}_{\!v,x}W}]\kern-1.70717pt]_{v_{j}}\,{\rm d}\nu,

for each
j=1,\dots,d
. The right-hand side of the last identity can further bounded, using the trace inverse estimate from Lemma
5.11
, by:

\displaystyle\Big(\int_{\Gamma}\sigma^{-1}|\{\kern-4.2679pt\{{{\rm diag}(\sqrt{\alpha}I,\sqrt{\gamma}I){\nabla}_{\!v,x}W_{v_{j}}}\}\kern-4.2679pt\}^{T}|^{2}\,{\rm d}\nu\Big)^{1/2}\Big(\int_{\Gamma}\sigma|\sqrt{\mathcal{C}}[\kern-1.70717pt[{{\nabla}_{\!v,x}W}]\kern-1.70717pt]_{v_{j}}|^{2}\,{\rm d}\nu\Big)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\Big(\frac{c_{hc,2}}{2}\alpha\|{{\nabla}_{\!v}^{h}W_{v_{j}}}\|_{{L_{2}(\mu)}}^{2}+\frac{c_{hc,3}}{2}\gamma\|{{\nabla}_{\!x}^{h}W_{v_{j}}}\|_{{L_{2}(\mu)}}^{2}\Big)^{1/2}\Big(\int_{\Gamma}\sigma|\sqrt{\mathcal{C}}[\kern-1.70717pt[{{\nabla}_{\!v,x}W}]\kern-1.70717pt]_{v_{j}}|^{2}\,{\rm d}\nu\Big)^{1/2},

due to the choice of
\sigma
. Adding now all contributions for
j=1,\dots,d
, we conclude

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{d}\int_{\Gamma}\{\kern-4.2679pt\{{{\nabla}_{\!v,x}W_{v_{j}}}\}\kern-4.2679pt\}^{T}\mathcal{A}[\kern-1.70717pt[{{\nabla}_{\!v,x}W}]\kern-1.70717pt]_{v_{j}}\,{\rm d}\nu\leq

\displaystyle c_{hc,2}\frac{\alpha}{4}\|{{\nabla}_{\!v}^{h}{\nabla}_{\!v}^{T}W}\|_{{L_{2}(\mu)}}^{2}+c_{hc,3}\frac{\gamma}{4}\|{{\nabla}_{\!x}^{h}{\nabla}_{\!v}^{T}W}\|_{{L_{2}(\mu)}}^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\int_{\Gamma}\sigma|\sqrt{\mathcal{C}}[\kern-1.70717pt[{{\nabla}_{\!v,x}W}]\kern-1.70717pt]_{v_{j}}|^{2}\,{\rm d}\nu.

Combining the above already gives the result.
∎

For all
w
in
H^{1}(\mu)
, we define the ‘
\mathcal{A}
-norm’ by

|\!|\!|{w}|\!|\!|:=\sqrt{(\kern-1.70717pt({w},{w})\kern-1.70717pt)}=\big(\|{w}\|_{{L_{2}(\mu)}}^{2}+\|{\sqrt{\mathcal{A}}{\nabla}_{\!v,x}w}\|_{{L_{2}(\mu)}}^{2}\big)^{1/2}.

(42)

Let
\Pi
denote the
\mathcal{A}
-orthogonal projection
(\!({\Pi u_{0}},{W})\!)=(\!({u_{0}},{W})\!)
for all
W\in V_{h}
.

We will also use the spectral gap/Poincaré inequality:

\|{w-\bar{w}}\|_{{L_{2}(\mu)}}^{2}\leq C_{PF}\|{{\nabla}_{\!v,x}w}\|_{{L_{2}(\mu)}}^{2},

(43)

for all
w\in H^{1}(\mu)
, with
\bar{w}:=\int w\,{\rm d}\mu
. As discussed in
[
23
]
, (see, in particular,
[
23
, (7.4)]
and
[
23
, Theorem A.2]
),) (
43
) holds if
V
satisfies (
3
) and if
|\nabla V(x)|\to\infty
as
|x|\to\infty
, which is the case here. Note that (
43
) holds with
C_{PF}=1
for Gaussian
V
.

We now prove the decay of the numerical method as
t\to\infty
in this norm.

Theorem 6.4

(Numerical hypocoercivity)
.

Set
U_{0}:=\Pi u_{0}
. With the assumptions of Lemma
6.3
, we have

|\!|\!|{U(t_{f})-\bar{u}}|\!|\!|^{2}\leq{\rm e}^{-\kappa t_{f}}|\!|\!|{U_{0}-\bar{u}}|\!|\!|^{2},

(44)

with
\kappa=2c_{hc,1}\min\{\lambda_{\rm min}C_{PF}^{-1},1\}>0
, and
\lambda_{\rm min}:=\min\{1-2\alpha,\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}(1-\frac{11\alpha}{6})\}
.

Proof.

Let
U\in V_{h}
be the solution of (
18
). From Lemma
6.3
, we have

a_{h}(U,U)+s_{h}(U,U)\geq c_{hc,1}\big(\|{{\nabla}_{\!v}U}\|_{{L_{2}(\mu)}}^{2}+\beta\|{{\nabla}_{\!x}U}\|_{{L_{2}(\mu)}}^{2}\big)=c_{hc,1}\|{\sqrt{\mathcal{D}}{\nabla}_{\!v,x}U}\|_{{L_{2}(\mu)}}^{2},

for the matrix
\mathcal{D}:={\rm diag}(I,\beta I)
. We consider the decomposition

\|{\sqrt{\mathcal{D}}{\nabla}_{\!v,x}U}\|_{{L_{2}(\mu)}}^{2}=\|{\sqrt{\mathcal{D-A}}{\nabla}_{\!v,x}U}\|_{{L_{2}(\mu)}}^{2}+\|{\sqrt{\mathcal{A}}{\nabla}_{\!v,x}U}\|_{{L_{2}(\mu)}}^{2}.

Now,

\displaystyle\|{\sqrt{\mathcal{D-A}}{\nabla}_{\!v,x}U}\|_{{L_{2}(\mu)}}^{2}\!=

\displaystyle(1-\alpha)\|{{\nabla}_{\!v}U}\|_{{L_{2}(\mu)}}^{2}\!-\!2\beta({\nabla}_{\!v}U,{\nabla}_{\!x}U)_{L_{2}(\mu)}\!+\!(\beta-\gamma)\|{{\nabla}_{\!x}U}\|_{{L_{2}(\mu)}}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle(1-2\alpha)\|{{\nabla}_{\!v}U}\|_{{L_{2}(\mu)}}^{2}+(\beta-\gamma-\beta^{2}/\alpha)\|{{\nabla}_{\!x}U}\|_{{L_{2}(\mu)}}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\lambda_{\rm min}\|{{\nabla}_{\!v,x}U}\|_{{L_{2}(\mu)}}^{2},

by Cauchy-Schwarz and Young’s inequalities, and substituting
\beta=\alpha^{2}/2
,
\gamma=2\alpha^{3}/3
,
with
\lambda_{\rm min}=\min\{1-2\alpha,\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}(1-\frac{11\alpha}{6})\}
. Thus, using (
43
), and recalling (
21
), gives

\displaystyle a_{h}(U,U)+s_{h}(U,U)\geq

\displaystyle\ c_{hc,1}\big(\lambda_{\rm min}\|{{\nabla}_{\!v,x}U}\|_{{L_{2}(\mu)}}^{2}+\|{\sqrt{\mathcal{A}}{\nabla}_{\!v,x}U}\|_{{L_{2}(\mu)}}^{2}\big)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\ c_{hc}\big(\lambda_{\rm min}C_{PF}^{-1}\|{U-\bar{u}}\|_{{L_{2}(\mu)}}^{2}+\|{\sqrt{\mathcal{A}}{\nabla}_{\!v,x}U}\|_{{L_{2}(\mu)}}^{2}\big)\geq\frac{\kappa}{2}|\!|\!|{U-\bar{u}}|\!|\!|^{2}.

(45)

Recalling Remark
4.2
, for
\eta\in V_{h}
, we observe the identity
a_{h}(U,\eta)+s_{h}(U,\eta)=a_{h}(U-\bar{u},\eta)+s_{h}(U-\bar{u},\eta)
. Thus, setting
\eta=U-\bar{u}
for brevity, (
18
) implies

\displaystyle 0=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}|\!|\!|{\eta}|\!|\!|^{2}+a_{h}(\eta,\eta)+s_{h}(\eta,\eta)\geq\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}|\!|\!|{\eta}|\!|\!|^{2}+\frac{\kappa}{2}|\!|\!|{\eta}|\!|\!|^{2}.

The result follows by Grönwall’s Lemma,
and the identity
U_{0}=\Pi u_{0}=\bar{u}
.
∎

7
A fully discrete scheme

We now further equip the above with a variable order (
hp
-version) discontinuous Galerkin (dG) time-stepping for the discretization of the temporal derivatives, thereby arriving at a fully discrete scheme.
hp
-dG timestepping is preferred to effectively capture possible singularities in the neighbourhood of the initial time
[
22
]
.

To that end, we consider a subdivision
\{I_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N_{t}}
of the time interval
(0,t_{f}]
, subordinate to the time nodes
0=:t_{0}<t_{1}<\dots<t_{N_{t}}:=t_{f}
, with
I_{n}=(t_{n-1},t_{n}]
.
Let also
\tau_{n}:=t_{n}-t_{n-1}
. To each
I_{n}
we associate the local polynomial degree
q_{n}\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
of the temporal basis functions, and we collect them in the vector
\textbf{q}=(q_{1},\dots,q_{N})
. On each
I_{n}
, we define the local
space-time finite element space
by

V^{q_{n}}_{h}:=\{u\in L_{2}(I_{n};H^{1}(\mu)):u|_{I_{n}}\in\mathcal{P}_{q_{n}}(I_{n})\times V_{h},\ n=1,\dots,N_{t}\},

with
\mathcal{P}_{q}(I)
denoting the space of univariate polynomials of degree at most
q
on the interval
I
. The global space-time finite element space is then given by
\mathcal{V}^{\textbf{q}}_{h}=\bigoplus_{n=1}^{N_{t}}V^{q_{n}}_{h}
. Functions in
\mathcal{V}^{\textbf{q}}_{h}
are allowed to be discontinuous at the time nodes
\{t_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N_{t}-1}
. Defining

u_{n}^{\pm}:=\lim_{s\rightarrow 0^{+}}u(t_{n}\pm s)
,
n=0,1,\dots,N_{t}
,
the
time-jump
across
t_{n}
is given by

\lfloor u\rfloor_{n}:=u_{n}^{+}-u_{n}^{-}
, for
n=1,\dots,N_{t}
and
\lfloor u\rfloor_{0}:=u_{n}^{+}
. The fully discrete scheme then reads: find
U\in\mathcal{V}^{\textbf{q}}_{h}
, such that

B(U,W)=(\kern-1.70717pt({u_{0}},{W^{+}_{0}})\kern-1.70717pt),\quad\text{ for all }W\in\mathcal{V}^{\textbf{q}}_{h},

(46)

with
u_{0}=u(0,v,x)
and
B:\mathcal{V}^{\textbf{q}}_{h}\times\mathcal{V}^{\textbf{q}}_{h}\to\mathbb{R}
given by

B(U,W):=\sum_{n=1}^{N_{t}}\!\bigg(\int_{I_{n}}\Big((\kern-1.70717pt({U_{t}},{W})\kern-1.70717pt)+a_{h}(U,W)+s_{h}(U,W)\Big)\,{\rm d}t+(\kern-1.70717pt({\lfloor U\rfloor_{n-1}},{W^{+}_{n-1}})\kern-1.70717pt)\bigg).

Of course, in practice, the method solves for each time interval separately: for
n=1,\dots,N_{t}
,
U|_{I_{n}}\in V^{q_{n}}_{h}
is computed by

\int_{I_{n}}\Big((\kern-1.70717pt({U_{t}},{W})\kern-1.70717pt)+a_{h}(U,W)+s_{h}(U,W)\Big)\,{\rm d}t+(\kern-1.70717pt({U^{+}_{n-1}},{W^{+}_{n-1}})\kern-1.70717pt)=(\kern-1.70717pt({U^{-}_{n-1}},{W^{+}_{n-1}})\kern-1.70717pt),

(47)

for all
W\in V^{q_{n}}_{h}
,
with
U_{n-1}^{-}
the initial datum at time step
I_{n}
; for
n=1
set
U_{0}^{-}=u_{0}
.

We now present a result, corresponding to Theorem
6.4
for the fully discrete scheme (
46
). They key underlying mathematical challenge to be addressed is that (
46
) is defined in integral form and, thus, Grönwall’s Lemma with negative exponents does not apply.

The lack of point-wise error control in the
t
variable of
U
solving (
46
) is counteracted by an inverse inequality.

Theorem 7.1

(Numerical hypocoercivity via inverse inequality)
.

With the assumptions and notation of Lemma
6.3
, the solution
U
to (
46
) satisfies

|\!|\!|{U(t_{f})-\bar{u}}|\!|\!|^{2}\leq\prod_{n=1}^{N_{t}}\Big(1+\frac{\kappa\tau_{n}}{(q_{n}+1)^{2}}\Big)^{-1}|\!|\!|{U_{0}-\bar{u}}|\!|\!|^{2}.

(48)

Proof.

Set
\eta=U-\bar{u}
. Since
a_{h}(U,\eta)+s_{h}(U,\eta)=a_{h}(\eta,\eta)+s_{h}(\eta,\eta)
, (
47
) implies

\begin{split}\int_{I_{n}}\Big(\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}|\!|\!|{\eta}|\!|\!|^{2}+a_{h}(\eta,\eta)+s_{h}(\eta,\eta)\Big)\,{\rm d}t+(\kern-1.70717pt({\lfloor\eta\rfloor_{n-1}},{\eta^{+}_{n-1}})\kern-1.70717pt)=0.\end{split}

Hence,

\frac{1}{2}|\!|\!|{\eta_{n}^{-}}|\!|\!|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}|\!|\!|{\eta_{n-1}^{+}}|\!|\!|^{2}-(\kern-1.70717pt({\eta^{-}_{n-1}},{\eta^{+}_{n-1}})\kern-1.70717pt)+\int_{I_{n}}\big(a_{h}(\eta,\eta)+s_{h}(\eta,\eta)\big)\,{\rm d}t=0.

Since
\frac{1}{2}|\!|\!|{\eta_{n-1}^{+}}|\!|\!|^{2}-(\kern-1.70717pt({\eta^{-}_{n-1}},{\eta^{+}_{n-1}})\kern-1.70717pt)=\frac{1}{2}|\!|\!|{\lfloor\eta\rfloor_{n-1}}|\!|\!|^{2}-\frac{1}{2}|\!|\!|{\eta_{n-1}^{-}}|\!|\!|^{2}
, the above gives

|\!|\!|{\eta_{n}^{-}}|\!|\!|^{2}+|\!|\!|{\lfloor\eta\rfloor_{n-1}}|\!|\!|^{2}\\
+2\int_{I_{n}}\big(a_{h}(\eta,\eta)+s_{h}(\eta,\eta)\big)\,{\rm d}t=|\!|\!|{\eta_{n-1}^{-}}|\!|\!|^{2}.

Following the proof in Theorem
6.4
, we deduce

\begin{split}|\!|\!|{\eta_{n}^{-}}|\!|\!|^{2}+|\!|\!|{\lfloor\eta\rfloor_{n-1}}|\!|\!|^{2}+\kappa\int_{I_{n}}|\!|\!|{U-\bar{u}}|\!|\!|^{2}\,{\rm d}t\leq|\!|\!|{\eta_{n-1}^{-}}|\!|\!|^{2}.\end{split}

(49)

Employing the classical
hp
-version trace inverse inequality in the
t
variable, we have

\tau_{n}|\!|\!|{\eta_{n}^{-}}|\!|\!|^{2}\leq(q_{n}+1)^{2}\int_{I_{n}}|\!|\!|{\eta}|\!|\!|^{2}\,{\rm d}t,

which upon substitution into (
49
) gives

\Big(1+\frac{\kappa\tau_{n}}{(q_{n}+1)^{2}}\Big)|\!|\!|{\eta_{n}^{-}}|\!|\!|^{2}\leq|\!|\!|{\eta_{n-1}^{-}}|\!|\!|^{2}.

The result follows by repeated application of the last bound for
n=1,\dots,N_{t}
.
∎

Remark 7.2

(Asymptotic bound)
.

Assuming constant
\tau_{n}=\tau
and
q_{n}=q
for all
n=1,\dots,N_{t}
, we deduce

|\!|\!|{U(t_{f})-\bar{u}}|\!|\!|^{2}\leq\Big(1+\frac{\kappa\tau}{(q+1)^{2}}\Big)^{-t_{f}/\tau}|\!|\!|{U_{0}-\bar{u}}|\!|\!|^{2}\rightarrow e^{-\kappa t_{f}/(q+1)^{2}}|\!|\!|{U_{0}-\bar{u}}|\!|\!|^{2},

as
\tau\rightarrow 0
. Comparing with (
44
), the exponent coincides with that of the semidiscrete setting for
q=0
. The inverse estimate used results into a reduction of the exponent for higher order. Nevertheless, the exponential convergence to equilibrium is established for all
q
.

9
Concluding remarks

The above class of hypocoercivity-preserving fully discrete Galerkin methods for the (inhomogeneous) kinetic Fokker–Planck (kFP) equations allows for extremely general unstructured mesh scenarios, thereby facilitating mesh and order adaptivity frameworks in the context of kinetic equations. The above proof of exponential convergence to the equilibrium state is oblivious the specificities of the meshes used and, in general, of the Galerkin spaces, as long as the produce a globally continuous, element-wise polynomial approximation and contain the global constant function to allow for mass conservation. An interesting direction of further research is to the proof of long-time robust error bounds for the proposed method. This will be considered elsewhere.
