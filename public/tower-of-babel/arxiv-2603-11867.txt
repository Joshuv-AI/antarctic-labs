Data Fusion with Distributional Equivalence Test-then-pool
Linying Yang, Xing Liu, Robin J. Evans

Abstract
Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) are the gold standard for causal inference, yet practical constraints often limit the size of the concurrent control arm. Borrowing control data from previous trials offers a potential efficiency gain, but naive borrowing can induce bias when historical and current populations differ. Existing test-then-pool (TTP) procedures address this concern by testing for equality of control outcomes between historical and concurrent trials before borrowing; however, standard implementations may suffer from reduced power or inadequate control of the Type-I error rate. We develop a new TTP framework that fuses control arms while rigorously controlling the Type-I error rate of the final treatment effect test. Our method employs kernel two-sample testing via maximum mean discrepancy (MMD) to capture distributional differences, and equivalence testing to avoid introducing uncontrolled bias, providing a more flexible and informative criterion for pooling. To ensure valid inference, we introduce partial bootstrap and partial permutation procedures for approximating null distributions in the presence of heterogeneous controls. We further establish the overall validity and consistency. We provide empirical studies demonstrating that the proposed approach achieves higher power than standard TTP methods while maintaining nominal error control, highlighting its value as a principled tool for leveraging historical controls in modern clinical trials.

Introduction

A central objective in causal inference is to determine whether an intervention produces a measurable effect in a target population. Although randomized controlled trials (RCTs) are the gold standard, they can be time-consuming and sometimes face ethical or feasibility constraints. Such challenges can usually affect the recruitment of patients into the placebo arm, inflating the variances of estimation and reducing the test power. At the same time, substantial amounts of clinical data are often available prior to the start of a study, particularly for control arms of similar trials
(
38
)
. Thus, a common remedy is to supplement current data with individual patient data from earlier control arms, which would reduce costs, shorten trial duration, and ease recruitment
(
27
;
9
;
28
)
. This follows the valid incentive that, despite each trial’s unique characteristics, multiple previous trials often share similar control treatments
(
9
)
.

Nevertheless, borrowing historical controls without accounting for potential discrepancies between studies can introduce bias. Differences such as regional bias or assessment bias can be problematic
(
5
)
. To address this, various methods have been proposed to examine the homogeneity before combining historical or external control data with current control arms in settings where fully powered randomized controlled trials are not feasible, including Bayesian approaches such as meta-analytic-predictive priors
(
27
)
, frequentist techniques such as matching
(
6
)
and hybrid strategies like Bayesian dynamic borrowing
(
38
)
. We refer to
38
,
9
and
15
for the review of these methods.

Another mainstream approach is the test-then-pool (TTP) approach
(
38
;
20
;
10
)
. This approach proceeds in two stages: a fusion test and a causality test.
The fusion test evaluates whether historical and current data are compatible. If there is insufficient evidence to reject compatibility, the historical data are pooled with the current data, and a causal analysis is performed on the combined sample.

While relatively straightforward, a major criticism of the classic TTP is its limited power in identifying heterogeneity between historical and current controls. As a result, heterogeneous data may be inappropriately pooled, introducing additional bias, and the probability of such happening is
not
controlled by a test targeting the standard point hypotheses.

To mitigate this problem,
18
replaces the fusion test with an equivalence test. This modification provides the Type-I error control when concluding that the two controls are similar. However, since “similar” does not imply “identical”, the subsequent causality test requires an appropriate adjustment to account for the differences between the control arms to guarantee the Type-I error control for the overall procedure. Such an adjustment was not explicitly derived or formally specified in their framework.
Moreover, existing TTP methods remain limited to testing the similarity via mean, thereby ignoring the richer information contained in the full distributions. A detailed review of these approaches is provided in
Section

2.3
.

In this paper, we propose a new TTP framework for fusing control arms, aimed at increasing the power to detect
distributional treatment effects
(DTE) while rigorously controlling the Type-I error rate. Our method addresses a common critique of classic TTP procedures that they can inflate Type-I errors by providing a formal guarantee of validity for the final treatment effect test. Unlike traditional mean-based equivalence tests, our approach captures the full distributional differences between arms via the
Maximum Mean Discrepancy
(MMD), offering a more comprehensive evaluation of treatment effect heterogeneity. We further provide practical guidance for choosing the equivalence margin to achieve desired power levels.

Our key contributions include:

•

Extension of TTP to distributional testing, allowing detection of treatment effects beyond mean differences and enabling broader applications.

•

Partial bootstrap and partial permutation procedures for valid approximation of the null distribution when fused control arms contain differences.

•

Equivalence-based fusion test using MMD, enabling principled pooling decisions.

•

Overall TTP validity proof, establishing that the treatment effect test after fusion retains the nominal Type-I error.

In

Section

3

Section

Prospera
, in

Background

Let
Q_{c}
,
Q_{h}
,
Q_{t}
be probability measures on
\mathbb{R}
, which denote the outcome distributions of the current control, historical control and treatment groups, respectively. We assume we observe independent random variables
\{x_{i}^{c}\}_{i=1}^{m}\sim Q_{c}
,
\{x_{i}^{h}\}_{i=1}^{\ell}\sim Q_{h}
and
\{x_{i}^{t}\}_{i=1}^{n}\sim Q_{t}
. For any distribution
Q
, its empirical distribution based on independent samples
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\sim Q
is denoted by
Q^{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{x_{i}}
, where
\delta_{x}
denotes the Dirac measure at
x
. The empirical distribution based on
\{x_{i}^{c}\}_{i=1}^{m}
is denoted by
Q_{c}^{m}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\delta_{x_{i}^{c}}
, and similarly for
Q_{t}^{n}
and
Q_{h}^{\ell}
. Our main goal is to test for statistically significant difference between the treatment group
Q_{t}
and the control group
Q_{c}
, possibly after augmenting by the historical control
Q_{h}
. This is phrased as testing the DTE by setting
\mathcal{H}_{0}:Q_{c}=Q_{t}
. We refer to it as the
causality
test. For two sets of observations,
\{x_{i}^{c}\}_{i=1}^{m}\sim Q_{c}
and
\{x_{i}^{h}\}_{i=1}^{\ell}\sim Q_{h}
, the empirical distribution of their pooled sample,
\{x_{i}^{c}\}_{i=1}^{m}\bigcup\{x_{i}^{h}\}_{i=1}^{\ell}
, is denoted
Q_{f}^{m+\ell}=\frac{m}{m+\ell}Q_{c}^{m}+\frac{\ell}{m+\ell}Q_{h}^{\ell}
.

2.1
Distributional treatment effect

While the average treatment effect (ATE) is the most widely studied causal estimand, it summarizes impact only through differences in means. This focus can obscure important heterogeneity: two treatments may have the same mean effect but very different impacts on the distribution of outcomes. Thus, ATE provides only limited insights. In contrast, DTEs address this limitation by characterizing how an intervention shifts the entire outcome distribution, rather than just its first moment. Increasing attention is being paid to distributional treatment effects, which offer more comprehensive information beyond the mean
(
1
;
14
)
.

A growing body of work develops nonparametric methods for estimating DTEs. One strand leverages kernel embeddings of probability distributions, which represent distributions in a reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS). This approach allows treatment effects to be evaluated through differences between embedded distributions, making it possible to capture complex shifts without imposing strong parametric assumptions. Kernel-based methods also provide a natural way to compare entire distributions, and can be integrated with flexible machine learning models
(
22
;
23
;
24
)
.

By moving beyond comparing only the first moments, estimation of this kind offers a more flexible and informative framework, particularly in settings where heterogeneity or tail behavior is crucial, or when the outcomes are of high-dimensional.

Remark 2.1

(Connection with ATE)
.

An appealing feature of the proposed kernel two-sample testing framework is its flexibility. By choosing a characteristic kernel, the method captures full distributional differences and thus enables inference on distributional treatment effects. At the same time, if we choose the linear kernel
k(x,y)=x^{\top}y
, the framework reduces to a test for mean differences, recovering the classical average treatment effect. This dual capability highlights that our approach unifies ATE and DTE inference within a single methodological framework.

2.2
MMD and two-sample testing

Our TTP framework requires a notion of dissimilarity that quantifies distributional differences. We use the Maximum Mean Discrepancy
(
12
;
11
, MMD;)
, which we review next.

Let
k:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a bounded, positive definite kernels. Denote by
\mathcal{H}_{k}
be its associated Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Space
(
3
, RKHS;)
, equipped with the RKHS inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}
. The MMD between two distributions
Q
and
P
is the maximal difference between their expectations of all functions in the unit ball of
\mathcal{H}_{k}
, defined as

\displaystyle D(Q,P)\coloneqq\sup_{f\in\mathcal{H}_{k}:\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}\leq 1}\big|\mathbb{E}_{X\sim Q}f(X)-\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim P}f(Y)\big|,

where
\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}=\langle f,f\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}^{1/2}
is the induced RKHS norm. Under regularity conditions, the MMD can be rewritten as the RKHS-norm of the difference between the so-called
kernel mean embeddings
of the two distributions
Q
and
P
. For a probability measure
R
, we define its kernel mean embedding as
\xi_{R}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{X\sim R}\,k(\cdot,X)
. Throughout, we will assume the kernel
k
satisfies the following condition, which guarantees the existence of
\xi_{Q},\xi_{P}

(
11
, Lemma 3)
.

Assumption 2.2

(Finite first moment)
.

The kernel
k
satisfies
\mathbb{E}_{X\sim R}\sqrt{k(X,X)}<\infty
, for both
R=Q
and
R=P
.

Under
2.2
, the
squared
MMD admits the following equivalent forms
(
11
, Lemma 4, 6)

\displaystyle D^{2}(Q,P)

\displaystyle=\big\|\xi_{Q}-\xi_{P}\big\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}^{2}=\mathbb{E}_{X,X^{\prime}\sim Q}k(X,X^{\prime})+\mathbb{E}_{Y,Y^{\prime}\sim P}k(Y,Y^{\prime})-2\mathbb{E}_{X\sim Q,Y\sim P}k(X,Y).

We further assume that
k
is
characteristic

(
35
)
throughout the paper, meaning that the mapping
Q\mapsto\xi_{Q}
is injective. In this case, the associated MMD is a statistical metric. In particular, MMD separates distributions, meaning that
D(Q,P)=0
if and only if
Q=P

(
11
, Theorem 5)
. Most common kernels, such as Gaussian kernels, Inverse Multi-Quadric (IMQ) kernels, and Matérn kernels, are characteristic. The former two kernels also satisfy
2.2
for all
Q,P
, since they are bounded.

The above identities also suggest a natural finite-sample estimator for MMD using Monte-Carlo estimation. Given random samples
\{X_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
and
\{Y_{j}\}_{j=1}^{m}
drawn independently from
Q
and
P
, respectively, the MMD can be estimated by the following biased but consistent estimator, formed by replacing the expectations with empirical averages

\displaystyle D^{2}(Q^{n},P^{m})=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}k(X_{i},X_{j})+\frac{1}{m^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}k(Y_{i},Y_{j})-\frac{2}{nm}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}k(Y_{i},Y_{j}).

(1)

It can be shown that (
1
) can be rewritten as a
two-sample V-statistic of degree
(2,2)

(
11
;
32
)
, whose asymptotic properties are well-known
(
30
, Chapter 5)
.

Remark 2.3

(U-statistics)
.

An alternative estimator in the literature is the following estimator

\displaystyle U_{nm}=\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}^{n}k(X_{i},X_{j})+\frac{1}{m(m-1)}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}^{m}k(Y_{i},Y_{j})-\frac{2}{nm}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}k(X_{i},Y_{j}).

(2)

Similarly to (
1
), the estimator above can be rewritten as a two-sample U-statistic
(
13
)
. U-statistics share similar statistical properties as V-statistics and have the advantage of being unbiased
(
30
, Chapter 5)
. For these reasons, (
2
) is the more commonly used estimator in the MMD literature. However, U-statistics can take negative values, whereas V-statistics are always non-negative. As will become clear in
Section

3.1
, non-negativity is essential for the fusion test used in our proposed method. Therefore, we will focus on V-statistics in this work, and defer a discussion on extensions to U-statistics to
Appendix

D
.

MMD has been widely applied in two-sample testing for the homogeneity of two distributions
(
12
;
11
;
29
;
32
;
7
)
. To test the null hypothesis
\mathcal{H}_{0}:Q=P
, MMD checks first the separation property to reformulate the null hypothesis to the equivalent form
\mathcal{H}_{0}:D(Q,P)=0
. It then rejects the null hypothesis if the estimate
D(Q^{n},P^{m})
exceeds a critical value, which is the
(1-\alpha)
-quantile of the distribution of
D(Q^{n},P^{m})
under
\mathcal{H}_{0}
, for a prescribed significance level
\alpha
.

One major challenge in MMD tests is that the critical value is typically intractable and thus needs to be estimated. Common approaches include permutation and bootstrapping, of which both aim to construct empirical samples from the null distribution. The permutation method proceeds by randomly permuting the pooled sample
\big\{Z_{\ell}\big\}_{\ell=1}^{n+m}=\big\{X_{i}\big\}\cup\big\{Y_{j}\big\}
to form the permuted sample
\big\{Z_{\ell}^{\pi}\big\}_{\ell=1}^{n+m}
, and computing the permuted MMD statistic

\displaystyle T_{\pi}\coloneqq D^{2}\bigg(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{Z_{i}^{\pi}},\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=n+1}^{n+m}\delta_{Z_{i}^{\pi}}\bigg).

(3)

Repeating this over
B
random permuted samples yields the empirical quantity
\big\{T_{\pi^{(b)}}\big\}_{b=1}^{B}
. The critical value
\hat{q}^{m,n}_{1-\alpha}
is then given by the Monte Carlo estimate based on this sample.

Alternatively, the critical value can be estimated using bootstrapping approaches, with the most common method being wild bootstrapping
(
31
;
29
)
, which constructs the bootstrap samples using Rademacher weights. In our work, we instead follow
19
to use Efron’s bootstrapping, where the bootstrapped statistics are formed by drawing samples from
\{Z_{l}\}_{l=1}^{n+m}
with replacement. The critical value is then the
(1-\alpha)
-quantile based on the bootstrapped statistics. As argued in
19
, Efron’s bootstrapping gives similar empirical performance while allowing existing theoretical tools to be used to prove statistical properties for their equivalence MMD test.

2.3
Existing mean-based TTP

We provide a more detailed review of existing TTP methods. As introduced in
Section

1
, TTP is a general two-stage framework consisting of a fusion test and a causality test. The fusion test determines whether a historical control group with outcome distribution
Q_{h}
is sufficiently compatible with the current control group
Q_{c}
to justify pooling. If compatibility is not rejected, the external and current controls are merged, and the resulting pooled control is compared with the treatment group
Q_{t}
in the causal stage.

The classic TTP does so by performing a statistical test targeting
\check{\mathcal{H}}_{0}^{f}:\mu_{c}=\mu_{h}
, where
\mu_{c}
,
\mu_{h}
are the means of
Q_{c},Q_{h}
, respectively. If the fusion test fails to be rejected, then the external control group is merged with the current control group to form a pooled group. The causality test then compares the resulting control group—either alone or pooled—with the treatment group,
Q_{t}
, to assess the presence of a treatment effect. This is formalized by performing the causality test targeting
\check{\mathcal{H}}_{0}:\mu_{c}=\mu_{t}
.

While this approach is simple, it is often insufficient to conclude equivalence between the control groups. Indeed, failure to reject
\check{\mathcal{H}}_{0}^{f}
does not imply that
\mu_{c}
and
\mu_{h}
are equivalent; instead, it can simply be a result of lack of statistical power. Consequently, the TTP framework can incorrectly merge heterogeneous historical control arms into the causal analysis, especially when the current control size
m
is small relative to the treatment size
n
. This, in turn, can then lead to inflated Type-I error in the causality test
(
38
)
.

To address this limitation,
18
proposed to replace the fusion test with an equivalence test, targeting instead the null hypothesis
\check{\mathcal{H}}_{0}^{f}:|\mu_{c}-\mu_{h}|\geq\delta
for some pre-specified margin
\delta>0
. Equivalence testing is more natural for assessing the similarity between the control arms, because it provides probabilistic guarantee on the error rate of falsely merging heterogeneous historical controls. However, this equivalence-based TTP test till relies solely on
mean
comparisons and does not account for differences in the broader outcome distributions, which can be important when the goal is to assess effects beyond the mean.

2.4
Warm-up: Distributional TTP

The TTP framework is general in nature: any statistical test can be used in the fusion stage. To align with the classic TTP procedure and facilitate comparison, we extend it here from mean-based to distribution-based testing, allowing the framework to target the DTE instead of the ATE. This formulation is not proposed in prior work; rather, we introduce it to formalize a “classic TTP” analogue at the distributional level, which will serve as a conceptual baseline and benchmark for our later developments. Specifically, we define the causal null hypothesis as

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{0}:Q_{c}=Q_{t}\quad\text{against}\quad\mathcal{H}_{1}:Q_{c}\neq Q_{t}.

When using an MMD two-sample test with a characteristic kernel, this is equivalent to

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{0}:D(Q_{c},Q_{t})=0\quad\text{against}\quad\mathcal{H}_{1}:D(Q_{c},Q_{t})>0.

Without merging from external control group, the causality test uses merely
Q_{c}^{m}
and
Q_{t}^{n}
. When one sample size is small—here we assume
m\ll n
—the test may have insufficient power to detect differences in treatment effects. This motivates fusing the current control
Q_{c}^{m}
with an external or historical control, whose response distribution
Q_{h}
is sufficiently similar to
Q_{c}
.

The fusion test assesses

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{0}^{f}:Q_{c}=Q_{h}\quad\text{against}\quad\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{1}^{f}:Q_{c}\neq Q_{h}.

or equivalently,

\displaystyle\tilde{{\mathcal{H}}}_{0}^{f}:D\big(Q_{h},Q_{c}\big)=0\quad\text{against}\quad\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{1}^{f}:D\big(Q_{h},Q_{c}\big)>0.

(4)

The corresponding test function for the fusion stage is

\displaystyle\phi_{f}^{0}\big(Q_{c}^{m},Q_{h}^{\ell}\big)=\mathbbm{1}\Big\{D^{2}\big(Q_{c}^{m},Q_{h}^{\ell}\big)>\hat{q}_{1-\alpha_{f}}^{m,\ell}\Big\},

(5)

where
\alpha_{f}
is the prescribed significance level. Here,
\hat{q}_{1-\alpha_{f}}^{m,\ell}
is the permutation-based
(1-\alpha_{f})
-quantile of the test statistic.

The intuition is straightforward: if
\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{0}^{f}
is rejected,
Q_{h}^{\ell}
is excluded from pooling, and the causality test remains to be
\phi^{0}\big(Q_{c}^{m},Q_{t}^{n}\big)
. Otherwise, the fused control-arm response distribution is
Q_{f}^{m+\ell}=\frac{m}{m+\ell}Q_{c}^{m}+\frac{\ell}{m+\ell}Q_{h}^{\ell}
, which is then compared to
Q_{t}^{n}
in the causality testing stage using an MMD test based on
D^{2}\big(Q_{f}^{m+\ell},Q_{t}^{n}\big)
.

We denote the overall TTP decision rule by
\phi^{0}\big(Q_{c}^{m},Q_{h}^{\ell},Q_{t}^{n}\big):\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{l}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
, where
\phi^{0}=1
indicates rejection of
\mathcal{H}_{0}
. The function
\phi^{0}
is defined conditional on the fusion stage test function
\phi_{f}^{0}
, written as

\displaystyle\phi^{0}\big(Q_{c}^{m},Q_{h}^{\ell},Q_{t}^{n}\big)=\begin{cases}\mathbbm{1}\Big\{D^{2}\big(Q_{c}^{m},Q_{t}^{n}\big)>\hat{q}_{1-\alpha}^{m,n}\Big\}\,,&\phi_{f}^{0}\big(Q_{c}^{m},Q_{h}^{\ell}\big)=1,\\
\\[-6.0pt]
\mathbbm{1}\Big\{D^{2}\big(Q_{f}^{m+\ell},Q_{t}^{n}\big)>\hat{q}_{1-\alpha}^{m+\ell,n}\Big\}\,,&\phi_{f}^{0}\big(Q_{c}^{m},Q_{h}^{\ell}\big)=0.\end{cases}

In both cases, the causality test decision at significance level
\alpha
is made via the permutation-based test.

This formulation generalizes the classical TTP to a distributional setting. However, the fundamental issue remains: the fusion test
\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{f}
may be underpowered when the current control sample size
m
is small, leading to an incorrect merge and inflated Type-I error in the causal stage. This limitation motivates the development of our proposed method.

3.1
Fusion test: MMD equivalence testing

We introduce the fusion test in this section. We propose to test

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{0}^{f}:D(Q_{c},Q_{h})\geq\theta\quad\text{against}\quad\mathcal{H}_{1}^{f}:D(Q_{c},Q_{h})<\theta,

(6)

where
\theta>0
is the
equivalence radius
.

The benefits of testing the above hypotheses are two-fold. First, unlike (
4
) where the null hypothesis is that
Q_{c}
and
Q_{h}
are identical, we employ an equivalence testing setting, where the null hypothesis is that they differ by at least
\theta
in MMD. With this formulation, we reject only when there is statistical evidence for
D(Q_{c},Q_{h})<\theta
. This ensures control over the probability of an “incorrect merge”, where an external control
Q_{h}
is distinct from
Q_{c}
but the fusion test still suggests merging (namely, not rejecting the
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{f}
in (
4
)) due to insufficient power. Meanwhile, we use MMD as a notion of dissimilarity
between
Q_{c}
and
Q_{h}
, which captures not only differences in their means, but in their entire distributions.

To test (
6
), we employ the MMD equivalence test proposed in
19
. This test uses the test statistic

\displaystyle\Delta_{m,\ell}^{f}\,\coloneqq\,\theta-D(Q_{c}^{m},Q_{h}^{\ell}),

and rejects
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{f}
in (
6
) if
\Delta_{m,\ell}^{f}>q^{m,\ell}_{1-\alpha_{f}}
, where
q^{m,\ell}_{1-\alpha_{f}}
is the
(1-\alpha_{f})
-th quantile of the distribution of
S_{m,\ell}\coloneqq D(Q_{c}^{m},Q_{c})+D(Q_{h}^{\ell},Q_{h})
.

The intuition behind
S_{m,\ell}
is that, under
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{f}:D\big(Q_{h},Q_{c}\big)\geq\theta
, it upper bounds
\Delta_{m,\ell}^{f}
through a triangle inequality. It can also be shown that
S_{m,\ell}
is close to 0 with high probability under
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{f}
. Hence, the MMD equivalence test rejects the equivalence null hypothesis if
\Delta_{m,\ell}^{f}
is sufficiently large, or, equivalently, if
D(Q_{c}^{m},Q_{h}^{\ell})
is sufficiently smaller than
\theta
. Using this intuition,
19
establishes the validity and consistency of this equivalence MMD test. We restate their result below with slight adaptations to suit our setting. Its proof is in
Section

B.1
.

Theorem 3.1

(Calibration and consistency of MMD equivalence test)
.

Let
\alpha_{f}\in(0,1)
. Denote by
q_{1-\alpha_{f}}^{m,\ell}
the
(1-\alpha_{f})
-th quantile of the distribution of
S_{m,\ell}=D(Q_{c}^{m},Q_{c})+D(Q_{h}^{\ell},Q_{h})
. Assume
\mathbb{E}_{X,X^{\prime}\sim Q_{c}}\big|k(X,X^{\prime})\big|^{2}<\infty
and
\mathbb{E}_{Y,Y^{\prime}\sim Q_{h}}\big|k(Y,Y^{\prime})\big|^{2}<\infty
. Then there exists
a\in(0,\alpha_{f}]
such that

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\Pr\big(\Delta_{m,\ell}^{f}>q_{1-\alpha_{f}}^{m,\ell}\big)\;=\;\begin{cases}0,&D\big(Q_{h},Q_{c}\big)>\theta,\\
a,&D\big(Q_{h},Q_{c}\big)=\theta,\\
1,&D\big(Q_{h},Q_{c}\big)<\theta.\end{cases}

However, a challenge is that
q_{1-\alpha_{f}}^{m,\ell}
is often intractable, because the distribution of
S_{m,\ell}
is typically unknown.
19
proposed to use a bootstrap procedure to approximate this unknown quantile. Define

\displaystyle D^{2}_{W}(Q_{c}^{m})\coloneqq\frac{1}{m^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}(W_{i}-1)(W_{j}-1)k(x_{i},x_{j}),

where
W=(W_{1},\ldots,W_{n})\sim\textrm{Multinomial}(n;1/n,\ldots,1/n)
, and similarly define
D^{2}_{W}(Q_{h}^{\ell})
. We then define the bootstrap statistic

\displaystyle S^{b}_{m,\ell}\,\coloneqq\,D_{W^{b}}(Q_{c}^{m})+D_{\widetilde{W}^{b}}(Q_{h}^{\ell}),

(7)

where
\{W^{b}\}_{b=1}^{B}
and
\{\widetilde{W}^{b}\}_{b=1}^{B}
are drawn independently from
\textrm{Multinomial}(n;1/n,\ldots,1/n)
.
19
shows that
q^{m,\ell}_{1-\alpha_{f}}
can be approximated by the
(1-\alpha_{f})
-th quantile of the distribution of the bootstrap sample
\{S_{m,\ell}^{b}\}_{b=1}^{B}
, namely

\displaystyle q^{m,\ell}_{1-\alpha_{f},B}=\inf\Big\{u\in\mathbb{R}:\,\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\mathbbm{1}\big\{S_{m,\ell}^{b}\leq u\big\}\geq 1-\alpha\Big\}.

(8)

To summarize, the MMD equivalence test rejects
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{f}
if
\Delta_{m,\ell}^{f}>q^{m,\ell}_{1-\alpha_{f},B}
. We will denote this test by
\phi_{f}:\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{\ell}\to\{0,1\}
, where
\phi_{f}\big(Q_{c}^{m},Q_{h}^{\ell}\big)=\mathbbm{1}\Big\{\Delta_{m,\ell}^{f}>q^{m,\ell}_{1-\alpha_{f}}\Big\}
.

3.2
Causality test: partial bootstrap and partial permutation

Let
\phi_{\ast}:\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{\ell}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
denote the causality test for
\mathcal{H}_{0}:D\big(Q_{c},Q_{t}\big)=0
, so that
\phi_{\ast}(Q_{c}^{m},Q_{h}^{\ell},Q_{t}^{n})=1
if
\mathcal{H}_{0}
is rejected, and
0
otherwise. In particular, conditional on
\phi_{f}\big(Q_{c}^{m},Q_{h}^{\ell}\big)=0
, no merging is performed, and hence the causality test
\phi_{\ast}\big(Q_{c}^{m},Q_{h}^{\ell},Q_{t}^{n}\big)
does
not
depend on
Q_{h}^{\ell}
.

One of the key step in the causal stage is to approximate the null distribution of the test statistic under
\mathcal{H}_{0}
, so that we can estimate the testing threshold. Unlike traditional TTP, the
Q_{h}^{\ell}
we decide to merge can be different from
Q_{c}^{m}
(by at most
\theta)
. Consequently, the merged control-arm distribution
Q_{f}^{m+\ell}
may also differ from
Q_{c}^{m}
. This creates a problem for directly applying a permutation test between
Q_{f}^{m+\ell}
and
Q_{t}^{n}
, as such permutation approximates the distribution of
D^{2}\big(Q_{f}^{m+\ell},Q_{t}^{n}\big)
when
Q_{f}=Q_{t}
, where
Q_{f}
is the population-level mixture

\displaystyle Q_{f}=\frac{m}{m+\ell}Q_{c}+\frac{\ell}{m+\ell}Q_{h}.

The null distribution of
D^{2}\big(Q_{f}^{m+\ell},Q_{t}^{n}\big)
coincides with that under
Q_{f}=Q_{c}
only when
Q_{h}=Q_{c}
. If
Q_{h}\neq Q_{c}
, even by a small margin, the direct permutation approach no longer yields the correct null distribution, leading to potential size distortion. We thus propose the following methods to mitigate this problem. For the convenience of the following discussion, we impose the assumption of fixed ratios:

Assumption 3.2

(Fixed sample ratios)
.

The samples
Q_{c}^{m}
,
Q_{h}^{\ell}
and
Q_{t}^{n}
are independent with
c_{1}\coloneqq\frac{m}{n}\in(0,1]
and
c_{2}\coloneqq\frac{\ell}{n}\in(0,1]
, where
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
are constants.

Remark 3.3

(Generalization of fixed ratios)
.

Although we assume fixed sample size ratios
\ell/n
and
m/n
, the results below extend to cases where these ratios converge to finite limits, as long as the limits are greater than
0
. The proofs remain valid under this relaxation by adding error terms controlling the convergence of ratios. We provide a simple example in
Section

B.11
.

3.2.1
Partial bootstrap

To approximate the distribution of

under
\mathcal{H}_{0}
in the fused setting, we propose the

algorithm. This procedure resamples the current control and treatment groups from the same source distribution
Q_{c}
under the null, while independently resamples the historical control group
Q_{h}
. This preserves the correct dependence structure of the fused control under

even when

3.2.2
Normal approximation

We introduce partial bootstrap to approximate the asymptotic distribution of the test statistic
\Delta
. Since we have the form of limiting distribution of
\Delta
in
Proposition

3.4
, it is straightforward to have an alternative approach to approximate the limiting normal distribution. We estimate the variance
\sigma_{c}^{2}
with empirical observed samples:

\displaystyle\hat{\sigma}_{c}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{(1-\gamma)^{2}}{m-1}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\Big(\hat{g}_{i}-\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\hat{g}_{i}\Big)^{2},

\displaystyle\text{where}\qquad\hat{g}_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\ell}\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}k(x_{i}^{c},x_{j}^{h})-\frac{1}{m-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}^{m}k(x_{i}^{c},x_{j}^{c}).

We then take the
(1-\alpha)
-quantile,
\hat{q}_{1-\alpha}^{\prime}
, from
\mathcal{N}\big(0,(1+1/c_{1})\hat{\sigma}_{c}^{2}\big)
as the critical value, and we reject
\mathcal{H}_{0}
if

. Since we directly take the critical value by estimating the limiting distribution, the asymptotic validity and consistency of such normal approximation is straightforward. The proofs are similar to
Section

B.3
and
Section

B.4
excluding the discussions associated with bootstrap. However, we still recommend partial bootstrap for better approximation of the null distribution, if computation cost is not a concern. Details are provided in
Section

3.2.4
.

3.2.4
Comparison between partial bootstrap and partial permutation

The partial bootstrap and partial permutation procedures generate treatment- and control-arm samples under the causal null from the same empirical source distribution
Q_{c}^{m}
. The former samples from
Q_{c}^{m}
to form the bootstrap samples
Q_{t,b}^{n}
. This ensures that, under
\mathcal{H}_{0}
, the resampled
Q_{t,b}^{n}
and the fused
Q_{f,b}^{m+\ell}
have the correct joint distribution, so the empirical distribution of the test statistic converges to its true null distribution
Q_{c}=Q_{t}
when
Q_{c}\neq Q_{h}
. This is the key distinction between partial bootstrap and partial permutation lies in what role

Q_{h}^{\ell}
plays in them. When
Q_{c}^{m}
and
Q_{t}^{n}
are drawn from different distributions, treating

Q_{h}^{\ell}
merely as an auxiliary sample can lead to a poor approximation of the null distribution of
T_{n,m,\ell}

Q_{c}^{m}
and
Q_{h}^{\ell}
, effectively breaking the natural distance between them. .

, merging

introduces bias into the MMD statistic used in the causality test, and hence it can no longer consistently reject

unless the difference between

and

is substantially larger than that between
Q_{h}
and
Q_{c}
.

One practical mitigation is to merge with smaller probability as the sample sizes grows. This is sensible because, when the current control arm is sufficiently large, the causality test based only on the current control already has adequate power, making merging unnecessary. One way of achieving this is to let the margin
\theta
depend on the sample size, namely taking
\theta=\theta_{n}\in o(1)
. In

, we assess how varying

Although both the fusion and the causality tests in our proposed TTP framework are built on MMD, the underlying principle extends naturally to other statistical metrics, such as the Kolmogorov-Smirnov (KS) metric and the Wasserstein metric. Computationally, MMD has a quadratic cost of
\operatorname{O}\!\big((n+m+\ell)^{2}\big)
, whereas the KS and Wasserstein metrics require only
\operatorname{O}\!\big((m+\ell)\log(m+\ell)+n\log n\big)
. However, one major advantage of MMD is the flexibility in the kernel selection. By designing suitable kernels, the fusion and the causality tests can down-weight or up-weight certain forms of distributional discrepancy, such as mean shifts or tail behavior; see the next section for a discussion. In contrast, the standard KS statistic does not offer this type of tuning and, because it is based on the supremum norm between empirical distribution functions, it is less sensitive to discrepancies in the tails or to isolated outliers
(
4
;
39

, making it more restrictive in practice. We leave the discussion of using Wasserstein metric to

3.4
Choosing the kernel and bandwidth

Our framework allows flexible choices of kernels and bandwidth, which can be selected according to specific objectives. In general, we recommend employing the RBF (radial basis function) kernel
k(x,y)=\exp(-\frac{1}{2\zeta}\|x-y\|^{2})
with the bandwidth
\zeta^{2}
set according to the median heuristic
(
11
)
, defined as

\displaystyle\zeta^{2}_{\mathrm{med}}\coloneqq\mathrm{Median}\big\{\|v-v^{\prime}\|_{2}^{2}:v,v^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D},v\neq v^{\prime}\big\},

(17)

where
\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{D}_{n,m,\ell}
if the historical sample is merged, and
\mathcal{D}=Q_{c}^{m}\cup Q_{t}^{n}
.

It is also possible to tailor the kernel to target particular aspects of distributions. For example, if one is only interested in testing equality of the first moment, namely the ATE, then a linear kernel
k(x,y)=x^{\top}y
can be used. Moreover, heavy-tailed or even unbounded kernels, such as the Inverse Multi-Quadric kernel
(
35
, Example 3)
or polynomial kernels, are more sensitive to differences in the tails
(
26
, S.13.2)
. However, linear and polynomial kernels are not characteristic: for example, a linear kernel cannot distinguish difference in variances
(
36
, Example 2)
. One possible solution is to combine such a kernel with a characteristic kernel, such as the RBF, and use
k(x,y)=x^{\top}y+\epsilon\exp(-\frac{1}{2\zeta}\|x-y\|^{2})
for some
\epsilon>0
. This kernel remains characteristic and thus ensure that the MMD can distinguish all distributions, while still up-weighting first-moment differences.

In our experiments, we find that bounded kernels, such as the RBF kernel, typically yields better performance, whereas unbounded ones such as the linear kernel often require large sample sizes for the asymptotic properties to hold accurately, thus leading to poor Type-I error rate control with small samples.

3.5
Choosing the equivalence radius

In our setup,
\theta
controls the tolerance for differences between the historical and current data. A larger
\theta
allows greater heterogeneity, leading to a higher merging rate. Although the choice of
\theta
does not affect the validity of the proposed testing procedure, it influences consistency. As discussed in
Section

4.2
, a large
\theta
may reduce power when
Q_{c}
,
Q_{h}
, and
Q_{t}
satisfy certain geometric conditions. Therefore, we recommend choosing a small
\theta
to ensure a power gain. Nevertheless, the key advantage of our method lies in its robustness: it guarantees Type-I error control regardless of how different the merged and original datasets are.

Note that
\theta
is a hyperparameter that must be specified a priori. In
Remark

3.19
, we discuss a potential extension of choosing decaying
\theta_{n}
as a function of sample size
n
. Another alternative is to adjust
\theta
after observing the test results, but doing so may violate Type-I error control for
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{f}
and, consequently, inflate the Type-I error for
\mathcal{H}_{0}
. A promising line of future work is to explore adaptive or sequential updating of
\theta
as the function of observed data, for example, via e-values
(
25
)
.

6
Discussion

In this paper, we focus on the fusion of control arms (and, symmetrically, treatment arms) within randomized clinical trials, and therefore make no assumptions such as unconfoundedness. In practice, however, it is increasingly common to consider fusing real-world observational data with experimental data. Extending our framework to such settings requires additional assumptions, most notably ignorability and the comparability of populations—lack of ignorability in the observational data may induce confounding bias; even under ignorability, substantial differences in covariate distributions between datasets may alter the target estimand.

Our proposed method can be adapted to address these concerns. For instance, MMD-based tests can be applied to assess distributional equivalence of covariates between observational and experimental samples, providing a principled diagnostic for population alignment in high-dimensional settings. Similarly, when imbalance is detected, our framework could be combined with covariate adjustment or weighting strategies to mitigate bias before fusion. In this way, the same equivalence-testing tools that validate control-arm pooling in trials can also serve as safeguards when extending pooling to broader causal inference contexts.

The choice of radius
\theta
presents an interesting direction for future work. As discussed in
Section

3.5
, intuitively,
\theta
should not be too large, so that the merged historical control distribution
Q_{h}^{\ell}
remains similar to the current control distribution
Q_{c}^{m}
; however, setting
\theta
too small may make the fusion test overly conservative. We provide ideas on potential extensions in
Section

3.5
,
Remark

3.19
and
Section

4.2
.

We discuss at the end of
Section

3.3
on using other distributional distance metrics, and one of the potential direction is to use the Wasserstein metric. Different from the Kolmogorov-Smirnov metric, the Wasserstein distance can in principle be made more flexible through an appropriate choice of cost function, but we do not pursue this direction here, as both the equivalence testing employed in this paper and most existing methods for distributional treatment effects are formulated in terms of MMD. Extending the proposed TTP framework to Wasserstein-based test statistics is therefore a natural direction for future work.

Another potential extension involves merging multiple data sources. Instead of relying on binary “accept or reject” decisions, one could assign weights based on their similarity to the current control group. Specifically, the kernel-based distance
D(Q_{c},Q_{h})
from the kernel equivalence test could be used to construct a smooth weighting function, allowing closer sources to contribute more heavily to the fused control distribution while down weighting or excluding more distant sources. Combining information from several candidate sources in this manner would require developing procedures for joint calibration.

Finally, while this work is motivated by causal inference, the proposed framework has potential applications beyond causal settings, such as domain adaptation, transfer learning, and robust statistical testing in machine learning contexts.

The pooled sample
Q_{f}^{m+\ell}
is formed by combining two mutually independent samples
Q_{c}^{m}
and
Q_{h}^{\ell}

Q_{c}
and
Q_{h}

and

Q_{f}^{m+\ell}

and
Theorem

3.8
to the case where we condition on the event that the fusion test,
\mathcal{H}^{f}_{0}
, is rejected, namely that historical and current controls are merged. We present the results for both partial bootstrap (
Section

A.2.1
) and partial permutation (
Section

A.2.2
).

A.2.1
Partial bootstrap

We first show that the same weak limit in
Proposition

3.4
holds conditional on the event that the fusion test is rejected.

Proposition A.2

(Weak convergence of the partial bootstrap provided merged controls under non-identical control groups)
.

Assume
2.2
holds under
R=Q_{c},Q_{h},Q_{t}
, and
3.2
is satisfied. Let
\mathcal{A}_{n}=\big\{\phi_{f}(Q_{c}^{m},Q_{h}^{\ell})=1\big\}
denote the event of rejecting the fusion null, and assume
\tau\coloneqq\lim_{n\to\infty}\Pr(\mathcal{A}_{n})>0
. Under the causal null
\mathcal{H}_{0}:Q_{c}=Q_{t}
, the following holds conditionally on
\mathcal{A}_{n}
:

\displaystyle\Delta\rightsquigarrow_{\Pr}\mathcal{N}\big(0,4(1+1/c_{1})\sigma_{c}^{2}\big)\quad\text{and}\quad\Delta^{\ast}\rightsquigarrow_{\Pr}\mathcal{N}\big(0,4(1+1/c_{1})\sigma_{c}^{2}\big),

where

\displaystyle\sigma_{c}^{2}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\Var_{X\sim Q_{c}}\Big[\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim Q_{f}}k(X,Y)-\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim Q_{c}}k(X,Z)\Big].

We thus obtain

\displaystyle\sup_{z\in\mathbb{R}}\Big|{\Pr}^{\ast}\big\{\Delta^{\ast}\leq z\big\}-\Pr\big\{\Delta\leq z\big\}\Big|\overset{a.s.}{\longrightarrow}0.

Proof.

Most parts in the proof of
Proposition

3.4
(present in
Section

B.2
) remain valid, except the proof of
A_{n}-A=\operatorname{O}_{\Pr}(n^{-1/2})
. We can not we cannot apply the standard CLT in Hilbert space directly on
A_{n}=(1-\gamma)\big(\hat{\xi}_{h,n}-\hat{\xi}_{c,m}\big)
, as it is no longer weighted average of independent samples when conditioning on the event
\mathcal{A}_{n}=\big\{\phi_{f}(Q_{c}^{m},Q_{h}^{\ell})=1\big\}
. Nevertheless, we show that this claim still holds, so long as
\lim_{n\to\infty}\Pr(\mathcal{A}_{n})>0
.

We aim to show that
A_{n}-A=\operatorname{O}_{\Pr}(n^{-1/2})
holds under the sequence of conditional probabilities
\Pr(\cdot\;|\;\mathcal{A}_{n})
, namely there exists a constant
c
such that

\displaystyle\Pr\Big(\big\|A_{n}-A\big\|>cn^{-\frac{1}{2}}\,\big|\,\mathcal{A}_{n}\Big)\to 0.

Using the CLT argument in
Proposition

3.4
, we know that this holds
unconditionally
. That is, we can find a constant
c
such that

\displaystyle\Pr\Big(\big\|A_{n}-A\big\|>cn^{-\frac{1}{2}}\Big)\to 0.

(18)

Define
\tau\coloneqq\lim\nolimits_{n\to\infty}\Pr\big(\mathcal{A}_{n}\big)
, which exists and is positive by assumption. In particular, there exists
N\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
such that
\Pr(\mathcal{A}_{n})>0
for all
n\geq N
. In what follows, we restrict attention to
n\geq N
, so that
\Pr(\cdot\;|\;\mathcal{A}_{n})
is well-defined. For such
n
,

\displaystyle\Pr\Big(\big\|A_{n}-A\big\|>cn^{-\frac{1}{2}}\,\big|\,\mathcal{A}_{n}\Big)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Pr\Big(\big\{\big\|A_{n}-A\big\|>cn^{-\frac{1}{2}}\big\}\cap\mathcal{A}_{n}\Big)}{\Pr\big(\mathcal{A}_{n}\big)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\Pr\Big(\big\|A_{n}-A\big\|>cn^{-\frac{1}{2}}\Big)}{\Pr\big(\mathcal{A}_{n}\big)}

\displaystyle\to\frac{\lim\nolimits_{n\to\infty}\Pr\Big(\big\|A_{n}-A\big\|>cn^{-\frac{1}{2}}\Big)}{\tau}=0,

where in the last line we have used the assumption that
\tau>0
and (
18
). This shows that
A_{n}-A=\operatorname{O}_{\Pr}(n^{-1/2})
holds conditionally under
\Pr(\cdot\;|\;\mathcal{A}_{n})
.
∎

Using this result, we can then show a conditional version of the partial bootstrap validity.

Theorem A.3

(Partial bootstrap validity provided merged controls under non-identical control groups)
.

Suppose
2.2
holds under
R=Q_{c},Q_{h},Q_{t}
, and
3.2
is satisfied. Let
\hat{q}_{1-\alpha,B}^{m+\ell,n}
be the partial bootstrap critical value defined in (
9
). Moreover, let
\mathcal{A}_{n}=\big\{\phi_{f}(Q_{c}^{m},Q_{h}^{\ell})=1\big\}
denote the event of rejecting the fusion null, and assume
\tau\coloneqq\lim_{n\to\infty}\Pr(\mathcal{A}_{n})>0
. Under the causal null hypothesis
\mathcal{H}_{0}:Q_{c}=Q_{t}
and assuming
D(Q_{c},Q_{h})\leq\theta
, the test, conditionally on merging, has asymptotic level
\alpha
:

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}\lim_{B\to\infty}\Pr\Big\{\Delta>\hat{q}_{1-\alpha,B}^{m+\ell,n}\,\Big|\,\mathcal{H}_{0},\mathcal{A}_{n}\Big\}\leq\alpha.

Proof.

The claim holds by following the same proof in
Theorem

3.5
and using
Proposition

A.2
instead of
Proposition

3.4
.
∎

A.2.2
Partial permutation

The following result shows that the partial permutation also remains valid conditionally on the event that the fusion test is rejected.

Theorem A.4

(Partial permutation validity provided merged controls)
.

Suppose
2.2
holds under
R=Q_{c},Q_{h},Q_{t}
, and
3.2
is satisfied. Let
\mathcal{A}_{n}=\big\{\phi_{f}(Q_{c}^{m},Q_{h}^{\ell})=1\big\}
denote the probability of rejecting the fusion null, and assume
\tau\coloneqq\lim_{n\to\infty}\Pr(\mathcal{A}_{n})>0
. Under the null hypothesis
H_{0}:Q_{c}=Q_{t}
,

\displaystyle\Pr\Big\{T_{n,m,\ell}\geq\hat{c}_{1-\alpha,B}^{m+\ell,n}\,\big|\,\mathcal{A}_{n}\Big\}\leq\alpha.

Proof.

Since
\tau>0
, there exists
N\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
such that
\Pr(\mathcal{A}_{n})>0
for all
n\geq N
. In what follows, we restrict attention to
n\geq N
, so that
\Pr(\cdot\;|\;\mathcal{A}_{n})
is well defined.
Following the proof in
Section

B.5
, we have (
26
). Given
\mathcal{A}_{n}
is
\sigma\big(Z,Q_{h}^{\ell}\big)
-measurable. We have

\displaystyle\Pr\Big\{T_{n,m,\ell}\geq\hat{c}_{1-\alpha,B}^{m+\ell,n}\,\big|\,\mathcal{A}_{n}\Big\}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigg\{\Pr\Big\{T_{n,m,\ell}\geq\hat{c}_{1-\alpha,B}^{m+\ell,n}\,\big|\,\mathcal{A}_{n},Z,Q_{h}^{\ell}\Big\}\Big|\mathcal{A}_{n}\bigg\}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigg\{\Pr\Big\{T_{n,m,\ell}\geq\hat{c}_{1-\alpha,B}^{m+\ell,n}\,\big|\,Z,Q_{h}^{\ell}\Big\}\,\Big|\,\mathcal{A}_{n}\bigg\}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\big[\alpha\,\big|\,\mathcal{A}_{n}\big]

\displaystyle=\alpha.

The first equation builds on tower property; the second equation is due to
\sigma(\mathcal{A}_{n})\subset\sigma\big(Z,Q_{h}^{\ell}\big)
. Taking limits as
n\to\infty
gives the asymptotic statement

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}\Pr\Big\{T_{n}^{m+\ell}\geq\hat{c}_{1-\alpha,B}^{m+\ell,n}\,\big|\,\mathcal{A}_{n}\Big\}\leq\alpha.

∎

A.3
Consistency with merged samples

Similar to
Section

A.2
, we show that
Theorem

3.6
and
Theorem

3.9
can be extended to case where we condition on the event
\mathcal{A}_{n}=\big\{\phi(Q_{c}^{m},Q_{h}^{\ell})=1\big\}
. Since both the partial bootstrap and partial permutation satisfy condition (
19
), as shown in
Theorems

3.6
and
3.9
, the following result applies to both methods.

Proposition A.5

(Consistency provided merged controls)
.

Let
\mathcal{A}_{n}=\big\{\phi_{f}(Q_{c}^{m},Q_{h}^{\ell})=1\big\}
denote the event of rejecting the fusion null, and assume
\tau\coloneqq\lim_{n\to\infty}\Pr(\mathcal{A}_{n})>0
. Suppose

\lim_{n\to\infty}\Pr\Big(\phi_{\ast}\big(Q_{c}^{m},Q_{h}^{\ell},Q_{t}^{n}\big)=1\Big)=1

(19)

under
\mathcal{H}_{1}
. Then

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\Pr\Big(\phi_{\ast}\big(Q_{c}^{m},Q_{h}^{\ell},Q_{t}^{n}\big)=1\,|\,\mathcal{A}_{n}\Big)=1.

Proof.

There exists
N\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
such that
\Pr(\mathcal{A}_{n})>0
for all
n\geq N
. In what follows, we restrict attention to
n\geq N
, so that
\Pr(\cdot\;|\;\mathcal{A}_{n})
is well defined. Using the definition of conditional probability, we have

\displaystyle\Pr\Big(\phi_{\ast}\big(Q_{c}^{m},Q_{h}^{\ell},Q_{t}^{n}\big)=1\,|\,\mathcal{A}_{n}\Big)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Pr\Big(\big\{\phi_{\ast}\big(Q_{c}^{m},Q_{h}^{\ell},Q_{t}^{n}\big)=1\big\}\cap\mathcal{A}_{n}\Big)}{\Pr(\mathcal{A}_{n})}

\displaystyle=1-\frac{\Pr\Big(\big\{\phi_{\ast}\big(Q_{c}^{m},Q_{h}^{\ell},Q_{t}^{n}\big)=0\big\}\cap\mathcal{A}_{n}\Big)}{\Pr(\mathcal{A}_{n})}

\displaystyle\geq 1-\frac{\Pr\Big(\phi_{\ast}\big(Q_{c}^{m},Q_{h}^{\ell},Q_{t}^{n}\big)=0\Big)}{\Pr(\mathcal{A}_{n})}

\displaystyle\to 1-\frac{1-\lim_{n\to\infty}\Pr\Big(\phi_{\ast}\big(Q_{c}^{m},Q_{h}^{\ell},Q_{t}^{n}\big)=1\Big)}{\tau}=1,

where the last line is resulted in by
\tau>0
and (
19
), which is not conditional on
\mathcal{A}_{n}
. We thus show the consistency provided the controls are merged.
∎

B.2
Proof of
Proposition

3.4

Proof.

We define
A\coloneqq\xi_{f}-\xi_{c}=(1-\gamma)\big(\xi_{h}-\xi_{c}\big)
,
B\coloneqq\xi_{t}-\xi_{c}
. Then
B=0
, since we assume
Q_{c}=Q_{t}
. Furthermore, define
A_{n}\coloneqq\hat{\xi}_{f,m+\ell}-\hat{\xi}_{c,m}=(1-\gamma)\big(\hat{\xi}_{h,n}-\hat{\xi}_{c,m}\big)
,
B_{n}\coloneqq\hat{\xi}_{t,n}-\hat{\xi}_{c,m}
.

With these notations, we have
T=\|A\|^{2}
,
T_{n,m,\ell}=\|A_{n}-B_{n}\|^{2}
,
\widetilde{T}_{n,m,\ell}=\|A_{n}\|^{2}
. Thus

\displaystyle T_{n,m,\ell}-\widetilde{T}_{n,m,\ell}=\|A_{n}-B_{n}\|^{2}-\|A_{n}\|^{2}=-2\langle A_{n},B_{n}\rangle+\|B_{n}\|^{2}.

By the central limit theorem (CLT) in Hilbert space (
3
, Section 9.1
;
40
, Section 1.2
):

\displaystyle B_{n}=B_{n}-B=\operatorname{O}_{\Pr}(n^{-1/2})\implies\sqrt{n}\big\|B_{n}\big\|^{2}=\operatorname{o}_{\Pr}(1).

Similarly,
A_{n}-A=\operatorname{O}_{\Pr}(n^{-1/2})
. Thus

\displaystyle\Delta=\sqrt{n}\big(T_{n,m,\ell}-\widetilde{T}_{n,m,\ell}\big)

\displaystyle=-2\sqrt{n}\big\langle A_{n},B_{n}\big\rangle+\sqrt{n}\big\|B_{n}\big\|^{2}

\displaystyle=-2\sqrt{n}\big\langle A_{n}-A,B_{n}\big\rangle-2\big\langle A,\sqrt{n}B_{n}\big\rangle+\operatorname{o}_{\Pr}(1)

\displaystyle=-2\sqrt{n}\big\langle A,B_{n}\big\rangle+\operatorname{o}_{\Pr}(1).

(20)

Note that

B_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}k\big(\cdot,x_{i}^{t}\big)-\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}k\big(\cdot,x_{j}^{c}\big)
,
A=\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim Q_{f}}k(\cdot,Y)-\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim Q_{c}}k(\cdot,Z)
. Thus

\displaystyle\big\langle A,B_{n}\big\rangle=\underbrace{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big[\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim Q_{f}}k(x_{i}^{t},Y)-\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim Q_{c}}k(x_{i}^{t},Z)\Big]}_{\hat{\psi}_{t}^{n}}-\underbrace{\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\Big[\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim Q_{f}}k(x_{j}^{c},Y)-\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim Q_{c}}k(x_{j}^{c},Z)\Big]}_{\hat{\psi}_{c}^{m}}.

Denote

\displaystyle\psi_{t}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{X\sim Q_{t}}\Big[\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim Q_{f}}k(X,Y)-\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim Q_{c}}k(X,Z)\Big],

\displaystyle\psi_{c}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{X\sim Q_{c}}\Big[\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim Q_{f}}k(X,Y)-\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim Q_{c}}k(X,Z)\Big];

\displaystyle\sigma_{t}^{2}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\Var_{X\sim Q_{t}}\Big[\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim Q_{f}}k(X,Y)-\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim Q_{c}}k(X,Z)\Big],

\displaystyle\sigma_{c}^{2}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\Var_{X\sim Q_{c}}\Big[\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim Q_{f}}k(X,Y)-\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim Q_{c}}k(X,Z)\Big].

We have
\psi_{t}=\mathbb{E}_{X\sim Q_{t}}\hat{\psi}_{t}^{n}
,
\psi_{c}=\mathbb{E}_{X\sim Q_{c}}\hat{\psi}_{c}^{m}
. Under the assumption
Q_{t}=Q_{c}
, we also have
\psi_{t}=\psi_{c}
,
\sigma_{t}^{2}=\sigma_{c}^{2}
.

By the classical central limit theorem(CLT) and independence of samples,

\displaystyle\Big(\sqrt{n}\big(\hat{\psi}_{t}^{n}-\psi_{t}\big),\,\sqrt{n}\big(\hat{\psi}_{c}^{m}-\psi_{c}\big)\Big)\rightsquigarrow\mathcal{N}\Big(0,\operatorname{diag}\big(\sigma^{2}_{c},\sigma_{c}^{2}/c_{1}\big)\Big).

Note that
\sqrt{n}\big\langle A,B_{n}\big\rangle=\sqrt{n}\big(\hat{\psi}_{t}^{n}-\psi_{t}\big)-\sqrt{n}\big(\hat{\psi}_{c}^{m}-\psi_{c}\big)
is a linear continuous map of
\Big(\sqrt{n}\big(\hat{\psi}_{t}^{n}-\psi_{t}\big),\,\sqrt{n}\big(\hat{\psi}_{c}^{m}-\psi_{c}\big)\Big)
. By the continuous mapping theorem, we obtain

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}\big\langle A,B_{n}\big\rangle\rightsquigarrow\mathcal{N}\big(0,\sigma^{2}_{t}+\sigma_{c}^{2}/c_{1}\big).

(21)

With
\sigma_{c}^{2}=\sigma_{t}^{2}
we get

\displaystyle\Delta\rightsquigarrow\mathcal{N}\Big(0,4\big(1+1/c_{1}\big)\sigma_{c}^{2}\Big).

For
\Delta^{\ast}\coloneqq\sqrt{n}\big(T_{n,m,\ell}^{\ast}-\widetilde{T}_{n,m,\ell}^{\ast}\big)
, we follow a similar procedure. Denote
A_{n}^{\ast}\coloneqq\hat{\xi}_{f,m+\ell}^{\ast}-\hat{\xi}_{c,m}^{\ast}=(1-\gamma)\big(\hat{\xi}_{h,\ell}^{\ast}-\hat{\xi}_{c,m}^{\ast}\big)
,
B_{n}^{\ast}\coloneqq\hat{\xi}_{t,n}^{\ast}-\hat{\xi}_{c,m}^{\ast}
. Thus

\displaystyle T_{n,m,\ell}^{\ast}-\widetilde{T}_{n,m,\ell}^{\ast}=\big\|A_{n}^{\ast}-B_{n}^{\ast}\big\|^{2}-\big\|A_{n}^{\ast}\big\|^{2}=-2\big\langle A_{n}^{\ast},B_{n}^{\ast}\big\rangle+\big\|B_{n}^{\ast}\big\|^{2}.

Conditional on
\mathcal{D}_{n,m,\ell}
, we get

\displaystyle B_{n}^{\ast}-\mathbb{E}^{\ast}B_{n}^{\ast}=B_{n}^{\ast}-\big(\hat{\xi}_{c,m}-\hat{\xi}_{c,m}\big)=B_{n}^{\ast}=\operatorname{O}_{{\Pr}^{\ast}}\big(n^{-1/2}\big).

This is because both
\hat{\xi}_{t,n}^{\ast}
and
\hat{\xi}_{c,m}^{\ast}
are embeddings of bootstrap samples around
\hat{\xi}_{c}^{m}
. Again we have
\sqrt{n}\|B_{n}^{\ast}\|^{2}=\operatorname{o}_{{\Pr}^{\ast}}(1)
.
Similarly,
A_{n}^{\ast}-A_{n}=\operatorname{O}_{{\Pr}^{\ast}}(n^{-1/2})
. So

\displaystyle\Delta^{\ast}=-2\sqrt{n}\big\langle A_{n},B_{n}^{\ast}\big\rangle+\operatorname{o}_{{\Pr}^{\ast}}(1).

Note that

B_{n}^{\ast}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}k\big(\cdot,x_{i}^{t,\ast}\big)-\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}k\big(\cdot,x_{j}^{c,\ast}\big)
,
A_{n}=\frac{1}{m+\ell}\Big[\sum_{i=1}^{m}k\big(\cdot,x_{i}^{c}\big)+\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}k\big(\cdot,x_{j}^{h}\big)\Big]-\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}k\big(\cdot,x_{i}^{c}\big)
. Thus

\displaystyle\big\langle A_{n},B_{n}^{\ast}\big\rangle=\hat{\psi}_{t}^{n,\ast}-\hat{\psi}_{c}^{m,\ast}

where

\displaystyle\hat{\psi}_{t}^{n,\ast}\coloneqq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{n}\bigg\{\frac{1}{m+\ell}\Big[\sum_{i=1}^{m}k(x_{i^{\prime}}^{t,\ast},x_{i}^{c})+\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}k(x_{i^{\prime}}^{t,\ast},x_{j}^{h})\Big]-\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}k(x_{i^{\prime}}^{t,\ast},x_{i}^{c})\bigg\},

\displaystyle\hat{\psi}_{c}^{m,\ast}\coloneqq\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{m}\bigg\{\frac{1}{m+\ell}\Big[\sum_{i=1}^{m}k(x_{i^{\prime}}^{c,\ast},x_{i}^{c})+\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}k(x_{i^{\prime}}^{c,\ast},x_{j}^{h})\Big]-\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}k(x_{i^{\prime}}^{c,\ast},x_{i}^{c})\bigg\}.

Denote

\displaystyle\hat{\psi}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}^{\ast}_{X\sim Q_{c}^{m}}\bigg\{\frac{1}{m+\ell}\Big[\sum_{i=1}^{m}k(\cdot,x_{i}^{c})+\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}k(\cdot,x_{j}^{h})\Big]-\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}k(\cdot,x_{i}^{c})\bigg\},

we apply bootstrap CLT conditional on
\mathcal{D}_{n,m,\ell}
:

\displaystyle\Big(\sqrt{n}(\hat{\psi}_{t}^{n,\ast}-\hat{\psi}),\sqrt{n}(\hat{\psi}_{c}^{m,\ast}-\hat{\psi})\Big)\rightsquigarrow_{\Pr^{\ast}}\mathcal{N}\Big(0,\operatorname{diag}\big({\sigma_{c}^{\ast}}^{2},{\sigma_{c}^{\ast}}^{2}/c_{1}\big)\Big),

where
{\sigma_{c}^{\ast}}^{2}\coloneqq\Var^{\ast}_{X\sim Q_{c}^{m}}\bigg\{\frac{1}{m+\ell}\Big[\sum_{i=1}^{m}k\big(X,x_{i}^{c}\big)+\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}k\big(X,x_{j}^{h}\big)\Big]-\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}k\big(X,x_{i}^{c}\big)\bigg\}
, when the number of bootstrap samples
B\to\infty
.
With continuous mapping theorem we obtain

\displaystyle\Delta^{\ast}\rightsquigarrow_{\Pr^{\ast}}\mathcal{N}\Big(0,4\big(1+1/c_{1}\big){\sigma_{c}^{\ast}}^{2}\Big).

The convergence of
\Delta
and
\Delta^{\ast}
, as well as
{\sigma_{c}^{\ast}}^{2}\rightarrow\sigma_{c}^{2}
together give us

\displaystyle\sup_{z\in\mathbb{R}}\bigg|{\Pr}^{\ast}\Big\{\sqrt{n}\big(T_{n,m,\ell}^{\ast}-\widetilde{T}_{n,m,\ell}^{\ast}\big)\leq z\Big\}-\Pr\Big\{\sqrt{n}\big(T_{n,m,\ell}-\widetilde{T}_{n,m,\ell}\big)\leq z\Big\}\bigg|\overset{a.s.}{\longrightarrow}0.

∎

B.3
Proof of
Theorem

3.5

Proof.

We write
F_{n}^{\ast}(z)={\Pr}^{\ast}\Big\{\sqrt{n}\big(T_{n,m,\ell}^{\ast}-\widetilde{T}_{n,m,\ell}^{\ast}\big)\leq z\Big\}
as the conditional bootstrap CDF, and
F_{n}(z)=\Pr\Big\{\sqrt{n}\big(T_{n,m,\ell}-\widetilde{T}_{n,m,\ell}\big)\leq z\Big\}
as the unconditional CDF. Also write
F(\cdot)
as the CDF of
\mathcal{N}(0,4(1+1/c_{1})\sigma_{c}^{2})
. By
Proposition

3.4
and Portmanteau theorem, we have

F_{n}(z)\overset{a.s.}{\longrightarrow}F(z)

at every continuity point
z
of
F
. Moreover, we have the uniform consistency

\sup_{z\in\mathbb{R}}\Big|F_{n}^{\ast}(z)-F_{n}(z)\Big|\overset{a.s.}{\longrightarrow}0.

(22)

Define
q_{n,1-\alpha}^{\ast}\coloneqq\inf_{z\in\mathbb{R}}\{z:F_{n}^{\ast}(z)\geq 1-\alpha\}
,
q_{n,1-\alpha}\coloneqq\inf_{z\in\mathbb{R}}\{z:F_{n}(z)\geq 1-\alpha\}
and
q_{1-\alpha}\coloneqq\inf_{z\in\mathbb{R}}\{z:F(z)\geq 1-\alpha\}
.

Decompose

\displaystyle\Pr(\Delta>q_{1-\alpha,B}^{m+\ell,n})

\displaystyle\leq\underbrace{\Pr\big\{\Delta>q_{1-\alpha,B}^{m+\ell,n}\big\}-\Pr\nolimits^{\ast}\big\{\Delta^{\ast}>q_{1-\alpha,B}^{m+\ell,n}\big\}}_{\epsilon_{n,B}}+\Pr\nolimits^{\ast}\big\{\Delta^{\ast}>q_{1-\alpha,B}^{m+\ell,n}\big\}.

(23)

We have for any fixed
B
:

\epsilon_{n,B}\leq\epsilon_{n}\coloneqq\sup_{z\in\mathbb{R}}\Big|F_{n}^{\ast}(z)-F_{n}(z)\Big|,

(24)

while
\epsilon_{n}\overset{a.s.}{\longrightarrow}0
as
n\to\infty
by (
22
).

Recall that
F_{n}^{\ast}
is the population CDF of the bootstrap draws
\Delta^{\ast}
; correspondingly, we denote
F_{n,B}
as the empirical CDF of
\Delta^{\ast}
. Define
\delta_{B}^{\prime}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{2B}\log(2B)}
. By the Dvoretzky–Kiefer–Wolfowitz inequality
(
8
)
, the event
\mathcal{A}=\Big\{\sup_{u}\big|F_{n,B}(u)-F_{n}^{\ast}(u)\big|\leq\delta_{B}^{\prime}\Big\}
occurs with probability
\Pr^{\ast}(\mathcal{A})\geq 1-B^{-1}
. Moreover, on
\mathcal{A}
,

\displaystyle q_{1-\alpha,B}^{m+\ell,n}=\inf\big\{u\in\mathbb{R}:1-\alpha\leq F_{n,B}(u)\big\}

\displaystyle\geq\big\{u\in\mathbb{R}:1-\alpha\leq F_{n}^{\ast}(u)+\delta_{B}^{\prime}\big\}

\displaystyle=\inf\big\{u\in\mathbb{R}:1-\alpha-\delta_{B}^{\prime}\leq F_{n}^{\ast}(u)\big\}

\displaystyle=q_{n,1-\alpha-\delta_{B}^{\prime}}.

Thus

\Pr\nolimits^{\ast}\big\{\Delta^{\ast}>q_{1-\alpha,B}^{m+\ell,n}\big\}\leq\Pr\nolimits^{\ast}\Big(\big\{\Delta^{\ast}>q_{n,1-\alpha-\delta_{B}^{\prime}}\big\}\,,\,\mathcal{A}\Big)+\Pr\nolimits^{\ast}(\mathcal{A})\leq\alpha+\delta_{B}^{\prime}+\frac{1}{B}\eqqcolon\alpha+\delta_{B},

(25)

where
\delta_{B}\to 0
as
B\to\infty
.

Combining (
23
), (
24
) and (
25
), we have

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}\lim_{B\to\infty}\Pr\big(\Delta>q_{1-\alpha,B}^{m+\ell,n}\big)\leq\alpha,

which completes the proof.
∎

, which is the

-quantile of the distribution of

. We write

. Using the notations in

, we denote
B\coloneqq\xi_{t}-\xi_{c}
,
A\coloneqq\xi_{f}-\xi_{c}
. Define the population gap

\displaystyle\Delta_{\infty}\coloneqq T-\widetilde{T}=\big\|\xi_{f}-\xi_{t}\big\|^{2}-\big\|\xi_{f}-\xi_{c}\big\|^{2}=\|A-B\|^{2}-\|A\|^{2}=-2\langle A,B\rangle+\|B\|^{2}.

Assume the alternative hypothesis and that
2(1-\gamma)D\big(Q_{h},Q_{c}\big)\cos\beta<\,D(Q_{c},Q_{t})
. Since
A=\xi_{f}-\xi_{c}=(1-\gamma)(\xi_{h}-\xi_{c})
, we have
2(1-\gamma)\|A\|\cos\beta<\|B\|
. Consequently,

\displaystyle\Delta_{\infty}=\|B\|^{2}-2\langle A,B\rangle=\|B\|^{2}-2\|A\|\|B\|\cos\beta>0.

With the assumption that all kernel mean embeddings have finite first RKHS moment, the Bochner integral is well defined and the empirical embedding satisfies a strong law of large numbers (
2
, Theorem 1.1
;
16
, Corollary 7.10
). Together with
Lemma

A.1
, we have
T_{n,m,\ell}\rightarrow T
,
\widetilde{T}_{n,m,\ell}\rightarrow\widetilde{T}
almost surely. Hence

\displaystyle\big(T_{n,m,\ell}-\widetilde{T}_{n,m,\ell}\big)\to\Delta_{\infty}>0.

Thus,

\displaystyle\Delta=\sqrt{n}\big(T_{n,m,\ell}-\widetilde{T}_{n,m,\ell}\big)\to\infty.

As proved in
Section

B.2
, the distribution of
\sqrt{n}(T_{n,m,\ell}^{\ast}-\widetilde{T}_{n,m,\ell}^{\ast})
converges to
\mathcal{N}\big(0,4(1+1/c_{1})\sigma_{c}^{2}\big)
. Thus
q_{1-\alpha}^{\ast}=\operatorname{O}_{\Pr}(1)
. By the conditional Glivenko-Cantelli, we have the quantile of bootstrap samples
\hat{q}_{1-\alpha,B}^{m+\ell,n}\rightarrow q_{1-\alpha}^{\ast}
. We thus obtain

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}{\Pr}\big(\Delta>\hat{q}_{1-\alpha,B}^{m+\ell,n}\,\big|\,\mathcal{H}_{1}\big)=1.

Note that
\Delta_{\infty}>0
is the sufficient and necessary condition for consistency to hold. We next show that if
\Delta_{\infty}=0
, the test is not consistent.

Using the same notations in
Section

B.3
, we have

\displaystyle A_{n}-A=\operatorname{O}_{\Pr}(n^{-1/2}),\quad B_{n}-B=\operatorname{O}_{\Pr}(n^{-1/2}),

and

\displaystyle\widetilde{V}_{n}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\Delta-\Delta_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{n}\Big(-2\big\langle A_{n},B_{n}\big\rangle+\big\|B_{n}\big\|^{2}+2\big\langle A,B\big\rangle-\|B\|^{2}\Big)

\displaystyle=-2\sqrt{n}\big\langle A_{n}-A,B\big\rangle-2\sqrt{n}\big\langle A,B_{n}-B\big\rangle+2\sqrt{n}\big\langle B,B_{n}-B\big\rangle+\operatorname{o}_{\Pr}(1)

\displaystyle=2\sqrt{n}\Big(\underbrace{\big\langle B-A,\hat{\xi}_{t,n}-\xi_{t}\big\rangle}_{\widetilde{\psi}_{t}^{n}-\widetilde{\psi}_{t}}-\underbrace{\big\langle B,\hat{\xi}_{f,m+\ell}-\xi_{f}\big\rangle}_{\widetilde{\psi}_{f}^{m+\ell}-\widetilde{\psi}_{f}}+\underbrace{\big\langle A,\hat{\xi}_{c,m}-\xi_{c}\big\rangle}_{\widetilde{\psi}_{c}^{m}-\widetilde{\psi}_{c}}\Big)+\operatorname{o}_{\Pr}(1),

where we have

\displaystyle\widetilde{\psi}_{t}(X)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim Q_{t}}k(X,Y)-\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim Q_{f}}k(X,Z),

\displaystyle\widetilde{\psi}_{f}(X)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim Q_{t}}k(X,Y)-\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim Q_{c}}k(X,Z),

\displaystyle\widetilde{\psi}_{c}(X)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim Q_{f}}k(X,Y)-\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim Q_{c}}k(X,Z).

Denote the corresponding variances

\displaystyle\widetilde{\sigma}_{t}^{2}\coloneqq\Var_{X\sim Q_{t}}\widetilde{\psi}_{t}(X),\quad\widetilde{\sigma}_{f}^{2}\coloneqq\Var_{X\sim Q_{f}}\widetilde{\psi}_{f}(X),\quad\widetilde{\sigma}_{c}^{2}=\Var_{X\sim Q_{c}}\widetilde{\psi}_{c}(X).

Let the empirical counterparts be

\displaystyle\widetilde{\psi}_{t}^{n}\coloneqq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widetilde{\psi}_{t}(x_{i}^{t}),\quad\widetilde{\psi}_{f}^{m+\ell}\coloneqq\frac{1}{m+\ell}\Big[\sum_{i=1}^{m}\widetilde{\psi}_{c}(x_{i}^{c})+\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\widetilde{\psi}_{h}(x_{i}^{h})\Big],\quad\widetilde{\psi}_{c}^{m}\coloneqq\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\widetilde{\psi}_{c}(x_{i}^{c}).

By the CLT in Hilbert space, the linearity and continuous mapping, we get

\displaystyle\widetilde{V}_{n}\rightsquigarrow_{\Pr}\mathcal{N}\Big(0,4\big(\widetilde{\sigma}_{t}^{2}+\widetilde{\sigma}_{c}^{2}/c_{1}+\widetilde{\sigma}_{f}^{2}/(c_{1}+c_{2})\big)\Big).

Thus when
\Delta_{\infty}=0
, we have
\Delta\rightsquigarrow_{\Pr}\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{1}^{2})
where
\sigma_{1}^{2}\coloneqq 4\big(\widetilde{\sigma}_{t}^{2}+\widetilde{\sigma}_{c}^{2}/c_{1}+\widetilde{\sigma}_{f}^{2}/(c_{1}+c_{2})\big)
can be different from
\sigma_{0}^{2}\coloneqq 4(1+1/c_{1})\sigma_{c}^{2}
. As a result, we have

\displaystyle\Pr\big\{\Delta>q_{1-\alpha}^{\ast}\big\}=1-\Phi\Big(\frac{\sigma_{0}}{\sigma_{1}}z_{1-\alpha}\Big)

where
\Phi(\cdot)
is the standard Gaussian CDF and
z_{1-\alpha}
is the
(1-\alpha)
-quantile of standard Gaussian. Since
\frac{\sigma_{0}}{\sigma_{1}}z_{1-\alpha}>0
,
1-\Phi\big(\frac{\sigma_{0}}{\sigma_{1}}z_{1-\alpha}\big)<0.5
. By replacing
q_{1-\alpha}^{\ast}
with
\hat{q}_{1-\alpha,B}^{m+\ell,n}
and taking
n\to\infty
, we obtain
\lim_{n\to\infty}{\Pr}\big(\Delta>\hat{q}_{1-\alpha,B}^{m+\ell,n}\,\big|\,\mathcal{H}_{1}\big)<0.5
, thus the test is not consistent.
∎

Proof of
Theorem

3.8

Proof.

Write

Z:=\{Z_{1},\dots,Z_{m+n}\}
for the pooled sample
Q_{c}^{m}\cup Q_{t}^{n}
. Let
G
be the set of all permutations
\pi
of
I_{Z}\coloneqq\{1,\ldots,m+n\}
. Denote
the observed control indices as
I_{0}
.

For any
\pi\in G
, write the permuted control index set
I_{c}(\pi)
, permuted treatment index set
I_{t}(\pi)
, with
\big|I_{c}(\pi)\big|=m
,
\big|I_{t}(\pi)\big|=n
. Define the permuted empirical measures

\displaystyle Q_{c,\pi}^{m}\coloneqq\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i\in I_{c}(\pi)}\delta_{Z_{i}},\quad Q_{t,\pi}^{n}\coloneqq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in I_{t}(\pi)}\delta_{Z_{i}},

with the fused control

\displaystyle Q_{f,\pi}^{m+\ell}:=\frac{m}{m+\ell}Q_{c,\pi}^{m}+\frac{\ell}{m+\ell}Q_{h}^{\ell},

and let
T_{n,m,\ell}^{\pi}:=D^{2}(Q_{f,\pi}^{m+\ell},Q_{t,\pi}^{n})
. The observed statistic is
T_{n,m,\ell}=T_{n,m,\ell}^{\pi^{0}}
where
\pi^{0}
is the identity
\pi^{0}(i)=i
.

Under
H_{0}:Q_{c}=Q_{t}
, the pooled sample
Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{m+n}\overset{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}{\sim}Q_{c}
has a joint distribution that is invariant under permutation. Moreover, since
Q_{h}^{\ell}
is independent of
Z
and not permuted, the joint distribution, on the population level, satisfies

\displaystyle\big(Z,Q_{h}^{\ell}\big)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\big(\pi\cdot Z,Q_{h}^{\ell}\big),\quad\forall\pi\in G.

This is precisely the randomization hypothesis: the joint law of the data is invariant under the group
G
. Since
T_{n,m,\ell}^{\pi}
is a measurable function of
(\pi\cdot Z,Q_{h}^{\ell})
, the collection
\big\{T_{n,m,\ell}^{\pi}:\pi\in G\big\}
is invariant under the group so satisfies the assumptions of the randomized test theorem
(
17
, Definition 17.2.1; Theorem 17.2.2)
.

In our algorithm, we only samples a subset of permutations
\pi^{1},\ldots,\pi^{B}
of size
B
uniformly from
G
, and calculate
\hat{c}_{1-\alpha,B}^{m+\ell,n}
as the empirical quantile of
\big\{T_{n,m,\ell}^{\pi_{b}}\big\}_{b=0}^{B}
. Conditional on the data
\mathcal{D}_{n,m,\ell}
, the collection
\{T_{n,m,\ell}^{\pi_{b}}\}_{b=1}^{B}
is exchangeable. By exchangeability, the rank of
T_{n,m,\ell}=T_{n,m,\ell}^{\pi_{0}}
among
\big\{T_{n,m,\ell}^{\pi_{b}}\big\}_{b=1}^{B}
is uniform on
\{1,\ldots,B+1\}
. We choose
\hat{c}_{1-\alpha,B}^{m+\ell,n}
so that at most an
\alpha-
fraction of the sample with value larger than or equal to it. Hence, by the Monte Carlo randomization tests
(
17
)
, we have

\displaystyle\Pr\Big\{T_{n,m,\ell}\geq\hat{c}_{1-\alpha,B}^{m+\ell,n}\,\big|\,Z,Q_{h}^{\ell}\Big\}\leq\alpha.

(26)

Finally, take expectations over
\big(Z,Q_{h}^{\ell}\big)
we get the unconditional result under
\mathcal{H}_{0}
:

\displaystyle\Pr\big\{T_{n,m,\ell}\geq\hat{c}_{1-\alpha,B}^{m+\ell,n}\big\}\leq\alpha,

for all
n
,
m
,
\ell
,
B
.
∎

B.6
Proof of
Theorem

3.9

We first introduce the Hadamard differentiability of the squared MMD.

Lemma B.1

(Hadamard differentiability of the squared MMD)
.

For
\gamma\in(0,1)
, the squared MMD function

D^{2}\big((1-\gamma)Q_{c}+\gamma Q_{h},Q_{t}\big)
is Hadamard differentiable with respect to probability measures
Q_{c}
,
Q_{h}
and
Q_{t}
.

Proof of
Lemma

B.1
.

Define
\Psi:\mathcal{H}_{k}^{3}\times[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle\Psi(\xi_{c},\xi_{h},\xi_{t};\gamma)\coloneqq\Big\|\xi_{t}-\big(\gamma\xi_{c}+(1-\gamma)\xi_{h}\big)\Big\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}.

Since
\Psi
is a quadratic form on a Hilbert product space, it is Fréchet, hence Hadamard, differentiable with respect to
(\xi_{c},\xi_{h},\xi_{t})
. Composing with the linear embedding map
R\mapsto\xi_{R}
, where
R
is a probability measure, gives Hadamard differentiability of
(Q_{c},Q_{t},Q_{h})\mapsto T(Q_{c},Q_{t},Q_{h})=\Psi(\xi_{c},\xi_{h},\xi_{t};\gamma)
.
∎

Now we provide the proof for
Theorem

3.9
.

Proof of
Theorem

3.9
.

Denote
Q_{d}\coloneqq\lambda Q_{c}+(1-\lambda)Q_{t}
,
Q_{f,d}=\gamma Q_{d}+(1-\gamma)Q_{h}
. The corresponding population kernel mean embeddings are written as
\xi_{d}\coloneqq(1-\lambda)\xi_{c}+\lambda\xi_{t}
,
\xi_{f,d}\coloneqq\gamma\xi_{d}+(1-\gamma)\xi_{h}
.

Denote the pooled empirical measure of
Z\coloneqq(Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{m+n})
(defined in
Section

B.5
) as
Q_{d}^{m+n}\coloneqq(1-\lambda)Q_{c}^{m}+\lambda Q_{t}^{n}
. In each permutation
b
, let
Q_{c,\pi^{b}}^{m}
be the empirical measure of the
m
observations drawn without replacement from the
m+n
pooled observations, and similarly
Q_{t,\pi^{b}}^{n}
for the remaining
n
. The fused control in permutation
b
is thus
Q_{f,\pi^{b}}^{m+\ell}=\gamma Q_{c,\pi^{b}}^{m}+(1-\gamma)Q_{h,b}^{\ell}
; the empirical measure of the
b
-th pooled data is denoted
Q_{d,\pi^{b}}^{m+n}\coloneqq(1-\lambda)Q_{c,\pi^{b}}^{m}+\lambda Q_{t,\pi^{b}}^{n}
. The statistic
T_{n,m,\ell}^{b}
of the
b
-th permutation is thus
D^{2}(Q_{f,\pi^{b}}^{m+\ell},Q_{t,\pi^{b}}^{n})
.

Conditional on the pooled sample, by the extended Glivenko–Cantelli theorem for finite populations
(
21
, Corollary 3.2)
, the permutation empirical measures satisfy

\displaystyle Q_{c,\pi^{b}}^{m}\Rightarrow Q_{d}^{m+n},\qquad Q_{t,\pi^{b}}^{n}\Rightarrow Q_{d}^{m+n},

almost surely as
m,n\to\infty
, where
Q_{d}^{m+n}
denotes the empirical distribution of the pooled data.

For
Q_{h}
, the empirical kernel mean embedding satisfies
\xi_{h}^{\ell}\to\xi_{h}
almost surely. By linearity of kernel mean embeddings and the continuous mapping theorem, it follows that

\displaystyle Q_{f,\pi^{b}}^{m+\ell}\Rightarrow Q_{f,d}.

As
D^{2}(\cdot,\cdot)
is Hadamard differentiable according to
Lemma

B.1
, together with continuous mapping theorem we get

\displaystyle T_{n,m,\ell}^{b}=D^{2}\big(Q_{f,\pi^{b}}^{m+\ell},Q_{t,\pi^{b}}^{n}\big)\to D^{2}\big(Q_{f,d},Q_{d}\big).

So the partial permutation yields
T_{n,m,\ell}^{b}
that converges to

\displaystyle D^{2}\big(Q_{f,d},Q_{d}\big)

\displaystyle=\Big\|\frac{m}{m+\ell}\xi_{d}+\frac{\ell}{m+\ell}\xi_{h}-\xi_{d}\Big\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\big\|(1-\gamma)(\xi_{h}-\xi_{d})\big\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\big\|(1-\gamma)(\xi_{h}-\xi_{c})-\lambda(1-\gamma)(\xi_{t}-\xi_{c})\big\|^{2}.

While
T_{n,m,\ell}
converges to

\displaystyle\big\|(1-\gamma)(\xi_{h}-\xi_{c})-(\xi_{t}-\xi_{c})\big\|^{2}.

We want them to be separable, which means

\displaystyle\underbrace{\big\|(1-\gamma)(\xi_{h}-\xi_{c})-\lambda(1-\gamma)(\xi_{t}-\xi_{c})\big\|^{2}}_{(1)}<\underbrace{\big\|(1-\gamma)(\xi_{h}-\xi_{c})-(\xi_{t}-\xi_{c})\big\|^{2}}_{(2)}.

We have

\displaystyle(1)-(2)

\displaystyle=\big(1-\lambda(1-\gamma)\big)\Big\{2(1-\gamma)\big\langle\xi_{h}-\xi_{c},\xi_{t}-\xi_{c}\big\rangle-\big(1+\lambda(1-\gamma)\big)\big\|\xi_{t}-\xi_{c}\|^{2}\Big\}.

Since
\lambda\in(0,1)
,
\gamma\in(0,1)
, we have
1-\lambda(1-\gamma)>0
. Thus

\displaystyle(1)<(2)\iff 2(1-\gamma)\big\langle\xi_{h}-\xi_{c},\xi_{t}-\xi_{c}\big\rangle-\big(1+\lambda(1-\gamma)\big)\big\|\xi_{t}-\xi_{c}\|^{2}<0.

Since we have
\big\|\xi_{t}-\xi_{c}\big\|\neq 0
, the above inequality stands if and only if
\xi_{h}-\xi_{c}=0
or
2\cos\beta\,\big\|\xi_{h}-\xi_{c}\big\|<\Big(\frac{1}{1-\gamma}+\lambda\Big)\big\|\xi_{t}-\xi_{c}\big\|
.
∎

B.7
Proof of
Theorem

3.12

Proof.

Assuming first that
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{f}:D\big(Q_{h},Q_{c}\big)\geq\theta
holds, we can rewrite the Type-I error rate using law of total probability as

\displaystyle\,\Pr\Big(\phi_{\ast}\big(Q_{c}^{m},Q_{h}^{\ell},Q_{t}^{n}\big)=1\,\big|\,\mathcal{H}_{0},\,\mathcal{H}_{0}^{f}\Big)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\Pr\Big(\phi_{\ast}\big(Q_{c}^{m},Q_{h}^{\ell},Q_{t}^{n})=1\,\big|\,\mathcal{H}_{0},\,\mathcal{H}_{0}^{f},\,\phi_{f}\big(Q_{c}^{m},Q_{h}^{\ell}\big)=1\Big)}_{T_{11}}\underbrace{\Pr\Big(\phi_{f}\big(Q_{c}^{m},Q_{h}^{\ell}\big)=1\,\big|\,\mathcal{H}_{0},\,\mathcal{H}_{0}^{f}\Big)}_{T_{12}}

\displaystyle\quad+\underbrace{\Pr\Big(\phi_{\ast}\big(Q_{c}^{m},Q_{h}^{\ell},Q_{t}^{n}\big)=1\,\big|\,\mathcal{H}_{0},\,\mathcal{H}_{0}^{f},\,\phi_{f}\big(Q_{c}^{m},Q_{h}^{\ell}\big)=0\Big)}_{T_{13}}\underbrace{\Pr\Big(\phi_{f}\big(Q_{c}^{m},Q_{h}^{\ell}\big)=0\,\big|\,\mathcal{H}_{0},\,\mathcal{H}_{0}^{f}\Big)}_{T_{14}}

\displaystyle=\,T_{11}T_{12}+T_{13}\big(1-T_{12}\big),

where the last line holds since
T_{14}=1-T_{12}
. Intuitively,
T_{11}
is the probability of rejecting the causality test given that the fusion test is also rejected, i.e.,
Q_{c}^{m}
and
Q_{h}^{\ell}
are merged, and
T_{12}
is the probability of rejecting the fusion test. The terms
T_{13}
and
T_{14}
have similar interpretations. We will bound each term separately. We first observe that the limiting value of
T_{12}
is

\displaystyle T_{12}^{\infty}\,\coloneqq\,\lim_{n\to\infty}T_{12}\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{=}}\,\lim_{n\to\infty}\Pr\Big(\phi_{f}\big(Q_{c}^{m},Q_{h}^{\ell}\big)=1\,\big|\,\mathcal{H}_{0}^{f}\Big)\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{=}}\,\begin{cases}0\,,&\quad D\big(Q_{c},Q_{h}\big)>\theta\,,\\
a,&\quad D\big(Q_{c},Q_{h}\big)=\theta,\end{cases}

(27)

where
a
is defined in (
10
), step
(a)
holds since the events
\big\{\phi_{f}(Q_{c}^{m},Q_{h}^{\ell})=1\big\}
and
\big\{\mathcal{H}_{0}:Q_{c}=Q_{t}\big\}
are independent, and
(b)
holds by noting that the LHS is the limiting Type-I error rate of the fusion test under
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{f}:D\big(Q_{h},Q_{c}\big)\geq\theta
, which is either
0
or
\alpha
by the assumption (
10
). Moreover, we can bound
T_{13}
as

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}T_{13}

\displaystyle=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\Pr\Big(\phi_{\ast}(Q_{c}^{m},Q_{h}^{\ell},Q_{t}^{n})=1\,\big|\,\mathcal{H}_{0}\Big)\leq\alpha,

where the equality holds since
\phi_{f}\big(Q_{c}^{m},Q_{h}^{\ell}\big)=0
means no merging, so
\phi_{\ast}\big(Q_{c}^{m},Q_{h}^{\ell},Q_{t}^{n}\big)
does not depend on
Q_{h}^{\ell}
, and the last step follows by the assumed Type-I error control of the causality test
\phi_{\ast}
, namely (
11
). We now bound
T_{11}
, the Type-I error of the causality test given merging, by treating it separately for
D\big(Q_{h},Q_{c}\big)>\theta
and
D\big(Q_{h},Q_{c}\big)=\theta
. When
D\big(Q_{h},Q_{c}\big)>\theta
, we bound the probability
T_{11}
by 1 and combine with the above results to yield

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}\Pr\Big(\phi_{\ast}\big(Q_{f}^{m+\ell},Q_{t}^{n}\big)=1\,\big|\,\mathcal{H}_{0},\,D\big(Q_{c},Q_{h}\big)>\theta\Big)

\displaystyle\leq 1\times\lim_{n\to\infty}T_{12}+\limsup_{n\to\infty}T_{13}\times\lim_{n\to\infty}(1-T_{12})

\displaystyle\leq 0+\alpha\times 1

\displaystyle=\alpha.

(28)

where in the first step we have used the inequality
\limsup_{n\to\infty}f_{n}g_{n}\leq\big(\limsup_{n\to\infty}f_{n}\big)\big(\limsup_{n\to\infty}g_{n}\big)
for non-negative sequences
f_{n},g_{n}
, and the second line holds due to (
27
) and that we have assumed
D(Q_{c},Q_{h})>\theta
.

When instead
D\big(Q_{c},Q_{h}\big)=\theta
, we invoke assumption (
12
) to give

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}T_{11}=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\Pr\Big(\phi_{\ast}\big(Q_{c}^{m},Q_{h}^{\ell},Q_{t}^{n}\big)=1\,\big|\,\mathcal{H}_{0},\,D\big(Q_{c},Q_{h}\big)=\theta,\,\phi_{f}\big(Q_{c}^{m},Q_{h}^{\ell}\big)=1\Big)\leq\alpha.

In this case, the overall Type-I error rate can be bounded as

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}\Pr\Big(\phi_{\ast}(Q_{f}^{m+\ell},Q_{t}^{n})=1\,\big|\,\mathcal{H}_{0},\,D\big(Q_{h},Q_{c}\big)=\theta\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}T_{11}\times\lim_{n\to\infty}T_{12}+\limsup_{n\to\infty}T_{13}\times\lim_{n\to\infty}\big(1-T_{12}\big)

\displaystyle\leq\alpha T_{12}^{\infty}+\alpha\big(1-T_{12}^{\infty}\big)

\displaystyle=\alpha.

(29)

This proves the desired result when
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{f}:D\big(Q_{h},Q_{c}\big)\geq\theta
.

We now prove the claimed result assuming
\mathcal{H}_{1}^{f}:D\big(Q_{h},Q_{c}\big)<\theta
. Decomposing the Type-I error probability in a similar way as before, we have

\displaystyle\Pr\Big(\phi_{\ast}\big(Q_{c}^{m},Q_{h}^{\ell},Q_{t}^{n}\big)=1\,\big|\,\mathcal{H}_{0},\,\mathcal{H}_{1}^{f}\Big)

\displaystyle=\,\underbrace{\Pr\Big(\phi_{\ast}\big(Q_{c}^{m},Q_{h}^{\ell},Q_{t}^{n}\big)=1\,\big|\,\mathcal{H}_{0},\,\mathcal{H}_{1}^{f},\,\phi_{f}\big(Q_{c}^{m},Q_{h}^{\ell}\big)=1\Big)}_{T_{21}}\underbrace{\Pr\Big(\phi_{f}\big(Q_{c}^{m},Q_{h}^{\ell}\big)=1\,\big|\,\mathcal{H}_{0},\,\mathcal{H}_{1}^{f}\Big)}_{T_{22}}

\displaystyle\,\quad+\underbrace{\Pr\Big(\phi_{\ast}\big(Q_{c}^{m},Q_{h}^{\ell},Q_{t}^{n}\big)=1\,\big|\,\mathcal{H}_{0},\,\mathcal{H}_{1}^{f},\,\phi_{f}\big(Q_{c}^{m},Q_{h}^{\ell}\big)=0\Big)}_{T_{23}}\underbrace{\Pr\Big(\phi_{f}\big(Q_{c}^{m},Q_{h}^{\ell}\big)=0\,\big|\,\mathcal{H}_{0},\,\mathcal{H}_{1}^{f}\Big)}_{T_{24}}

\displaystyle=T_{21}+(T_{23}-T_{21})T_{24},

where in the last line we have used
T_{22}=1-T_{24}
. Under the assumed condition (
10
) on the fusion test, we have
\lim_{n\to\infty}T_{24}=0
. Since furthermore
\big|T_{23}-T_{21}\big|
is bounded, this implies that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\big(T_{23}-T_{21}\big)T_{24}=0
. Moreover, under the event
\big\{\phi_{f}\big(Q_{c}^{m},Q_{h}^{\ell}\big)=1\big\}
, the empirical sample
Q_{f}^{m+\ell}
is the merged sample based on the independent samples
Q_{c}^{m}
and
Q_{h}^{\ell}
. This implies that

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}T_{21}=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\Pr\Big(\phi_{\ast}\big(Q_{c}^{m},Q_{h}^{\ell},Q_{t}^{n}\big)=1\,\big|\,\mathcal{H}_{0},\,D\big(Q_{c},Q_{h}\big)<\theta\Big)\leq\alpha,

where the inequality holds by the validity of the causality test assumed in (
12
). Combining these results gives

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}\Pr\Big(\phi_{\ast}\big(Q_{c}^{m},Q_{h}^{\ell},Q_{t}^{n}\big)=1\,\big|\,\mathcal{H}_{0},\,\mathcal{H}_{1}^{f}\Big)\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}T_{21}+\lim_{n\to\infty}\big(T_{23}-T_{21}\big)\,T_{24}\leq\alpha.

The above inequality, together with (
28
) and (
29
), completes the proof.
∎

Proof of
Theorem

3.16

Proof.

Q_{c},Q_{h},Q_{t}

Q_{c}\neq Q_{t}

\lim_{n\to\infty}T_{2}=T_{2}^{\infty}

\displaystyle T_{2}^{\infty}=\begin{cases}0,\qquad&\textrm{if }D(Q_{c},Q_{h})>\theta,\\
a,\qquad&\textrm{if }D(Q_{c},Q_{h})=\theta,\\

B.11
Proof of
Theorem

3.9
with converging sample size ratios

In this section we illustrate
Remark

3.3
, building on

Section

B.6
, by showing how to adapt the argument
to the case where the sample ratios merely converge to constants, rather than
being exactly fixed. Concretely, we now assume

\ell/n\to c_{1}
and
m/n\to c_{2}
. Correspondingly, we define

\displaystyle\gamma_{n}\coloneqq\frac{m}{m+\ell},\qquad\lambda_{n}\coloneqq\frac{n}{m+n},

and denote by
\gamma
and
\lambda
the fixed ratios appearing in the main
text. Under the convergence of the sample ratios we then have

\gamma_{n}\to\gamma
and
\lambda_{n}\to\lambda
.

Proof.

To adapt the proof in
Section

B.6
to this
setting, it suffices to show, for example, that

\displaystyle T_{n,m,\ell}=\big\|(1-\gamma_{n})\big(\hat{\xi}_{h,\ell}-\hat{\xi}_{c,m}\big)-\big(\hat{\xi}_{t,n}-\hat{\xi}_{c,m}\big)\big\|^{2}

converges to

\displaystyle\big\|(1-\gamma)\big(\xi_{h}-\xi_{c}\big)-\big(\xi_{t}-\xi_{c}\big)\big\|^{2}.

One could view
D^{2}(Q_{c}^{m},Q_{t}^{n},Q_{h}^{\ell},\gamma_{n})
as a function of not only
Q_{c}^{m}
,
Q_{t}^{n}
,
Q_{h}^{\ell}
, but also
\gamma_{n}
, and
invoke
Lemma

B.1
directly. Here we instead pursue a simple
decomposition which makes the technical steps explicit and can be reused when
extending other results to converging ratios.

We rewrite
T_{n,m,\ell}
as

\displaystyle T_{n,m,\ell}

\displaystyle=\big\|(1-\gamma_{n})\big(\hat{\xi}_{h,\ell}-\hat{\xi}_{c,m}\big)-\big(\hat{\xi}_{t,n}-\hat{\xi}_{c,m}\big)\big\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\big\|(1-\gamma_{n})\big(\hat{\xi}_{h,\ell}-\hat{\xi}_{c,m}\big)-(1-\gamma)\big(\hat{\xi}_{h,\ell}-\hat{\xi}_{c,m}\big)

\displaystyle\hskip 40.00006pt+(1-\gamma)\big(\hat{\xi}_{h,\ell}-\hat{\xi}_{c,m}\big)-\big(\hat{\xi}_{t,n}-\hat{\xi}_{c,m}\big)\big\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\big\|\underbrace{(\gamma-\gamma_{n})\big(\hat{\xi}_{h,\ell}-\hat{\xi}_{c,m}\big)}_{E_{n}}+\underbrace{(1-\gamma)\big(\hat{\xi}_{h,\ell}-\hat{\xi}_{c,m}\big)-\big(\hat{\xi}_{t,n}-\hat{\xi}_{c,m}\big)}_{F_{n}}\big\|^{2}.

The term
F_{n}
is exactly the finite sample expression appearing in the
fixed-ratio case (with
\gamma
fixed).

Therefore, with Cauchy-Schwartz, we obtain

\displaystyle\|E_{n}\|

\displaystyle=|\gamma-\gamma_{n}|\big\|\hat{\xi}_{h,\ell}-\hat{\xi}_{c,m}\big\|

\displaystyle\leq|\gamma-\gamma_{n}|\sup\Big(\big\|\hat{\xi}_{h,\ell}\|+\|\hat{\xi}_{c,m}\big\|\Big)

\displaystyle\leq 2K|\gamma-\gamma_{n}|,

where
K
is a finite
constant such that the kernel embeddings are bounded by it given
2.2
. With
|\gamma-\gamma_{n}|\to 0
, we obtain
\|E_{n}\|\to 0
.

From the fixed ratio analysis in
Section

B.6
we
know that
F_{n}\to(1-\gamma)(\xi_{h}-\xi_{c})-(\xi_{t}-\xi_{c})
in
\mathcal{H}_{k}
, hence

\|F_{n}\|
is almost surely bounded for all large
n
. Together with

\|E_{n}\|\to 0
this implies

\displaystyle\Big|T_{n,m,\ell}-\big\|F_{n}\big\|^{2}\Big|\to 0

almost surely. Finally, since we already established in the fixed-ratio case
that

\displaystyle\|F_{n}\|^{2}\longrightarrow\big\|(1-\gamma)\big(\xi_{h}-\xi_{c}\big)-\big(\xi_{t}-\xi_{c}\big)\big\|^{2},

we conclude that

\displaystyle T_{n,m,\ell}\longrightarrow\big\|(1-\gamma)\big(\xi_{h}-\xi_{c}\big)-\big(\xi_{t}-\xi_{c}\big)\big\|^{2},

as claimed.
∎

Appendix D
Extending Equivalence-TTP to U-statistic Estimators

We discuss how to extend our proposed TTP framework when the U-statistic (
2
) is used to estimate MMD. Throughout this work, we have relied on the V-statistic estimator (
1
) because it is guaranteed to be non-negative. This property is essential in the fusion test, namely the equivalence MMD test described in
Section

3.1
. Specifically, the test statistic in the fusion test is

\displaystyle\Delta_{m,\ell}^{f}\,\coloneqq\,\theta-D(Q_{c}^{m},Q_{h}^{\ell}),

which requires computing the
non-squared
MMD
D(Q_{c}^{m},Q_{h}^{\ell})
. With the V-statistic (
1
), this is computed by simply taking the square root, which is always well-defined due to non-negativity. However, with a U-statistic (
2
), the MMD estimate can take negative values, particularly when the current control
Q_{c}
and the historical control
Q_{h}
are similar so that
D\big(Q_{h},Q_{c}\big)\approx 0
, or when the sample sizes are small so that (
2
) has a large variance. In those cases, the square root required to compute
\Delta_{m,\ell}^{f}
is not well-defined. A simple workaround is to never reject the null hypothesis (
6
) whenever the U-statistic is negative. However, this would prevent merging historical samples even when
D\big(Q_{h},Q_{c}\big)
is essentially zero, which would lead to loss of test power.

For these practical reasons, we adopted the V-statistics estimator in the fusion test. In contrast, the
causality test
can be readily extended to U-statistics, because neither the partial bootstrap nor the partial permutation procedures require taking square roots of the MMD estimator.
