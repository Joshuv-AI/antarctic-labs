Vectorized Generalized Nearest Neighbor Decoding for In-block Memory Channel
Yuhao Liu, Xinwei Li, Shuqin Pang, Hao Wu, Wenyi Zhang

Abstract
This work extends the generalized nearest neighbor decoding (GNND), originally developed as a receiver architecture for memoryless channels, to a vectorized GNND (Vec-GNND) suitable for in-block memory (IBM) channels. Leveraging the generalized mutual information (GMI) as an operational lower bound on the mismatch capacity, an analytical characterization of the optimal Vec-GNND is obtained for general IBM channels with Gaussian codebooks. The formalism further provides closed-form optimality conditions and achievable GMIs for restricted variants of the receiver architecture. Furthermore, we formulate a GMI-based joint design viewpoint for Gaussian codebook covariance and decoding metrics. Since the metric optimization admits a closed-form solution for each fixed covariance, the joint design is reduced to an input-covariance optimization problem; for the diagonal covariance family, we derive first-order self-consistent optimality conditions. Numerical evaluations on block noncoherent additive white Gaussian noise channels and phase noise channels demonstrate consistent performance gains over conventional scaling-based baselines, highlighting the substantial advantages and potential relevance of the proposed Vec-GNND in realistic communication scenarios.



I
Introduction

The modern theory of reliable communication begins with Shannon’s channel coding theorem
[
37
]
, which establishes the operational meaning of channel capacity and shows that, over additive white Gaussian noise (AWGN) channels, Gaussian codebooks together with maximum-likelihood decoding achieve the Shannon limit. In this canonical setting, the maximum-likelihood rule coincides with nearest-neighbor (NN) decoding in Euclidean distance, providing a simple geometric interpretation fundamental to both theory and practice of communication systems
[
18
]
.

While nearest-neighbor decoding is optimal for AWGN and conditionally AWGN channels, many practical communication environments deviate from this idealized model. Real-world channels are often non-Gaussian, uncertain, or even entirely unknown due to fading phenomena and non-ideal transceiver distortions
[
3
]
. In such settings, identifying the capacity-achieving input distribution together with the corresponding optimal decoding rule becomes highly intractable and computationally burdensome
[
24
,
34
]
, hindering their deployment in practice. This tension between information-theoretic optimality and implementability has long motivated the study of low-complexity decoding rules that trade a calibrated loss in rate for robustness and simplicity.

Mismatched decoding provides a principled framework that employs a fixed decoding metric not necessarily aligned with the true channel characteristics (i.e., conditional probability distributions)
[
27
,
7
,
33
]
, offering a realistic model for communication over uncertain or time-varying channels. Within this framework, the mismatch capacity denotes the supremum of achievable rates with arbitrarily small error probability, given the specified mismatched decoding metric but optimized over all possible codebooks. However, the exact characterization of mismatch capacity is notoriously intractable for general channels and decoding metrics
[
7
]
. To address this challenge, two widely-considered achievable rates, namely the generalized mutual information (GMI)
[
12
,
21
]
, derived from random-coding analysis, and the LM rate
[
27
]
, obtained through constant-composition coding, provide tractable lower bounds on the mismatch capacity. Building upon these formulations, subsequent research has explored GMI and LM rate as principled objectives for the design of constellations and codebooks
[
1
,
45
,
20
,
22
,
5
]
.

Beyond optimizing codebooks to enhance achievable rates under mismatched decoding, some parallel efforts have focused on improving these rates by developing more effective and implementable decoding rules for fixed codebooks through GMI optimization. For Gaussian codebooks,
[
47
]
maintains the NN decoding rule while introducing and optimizing a scaling coefficient on the channel input to enhance the achievable GMI for general memoryless channels. In
[
48
,
46
]
, an additional output processing function is introduced alongside the scaling coefficient, and both are jointly optimized to further enhance the GMI. The optimal processing function serves to linearize the nonlinear channel response effectively, rendering it to behave as closely as possible to an additive-noise channel, consistent with the Bussgang decomposition formalism
[
4
]
. Furthermore, a generalized nearest-neighbor decoding rule (GNNDR) was proposed in
[
42
]
to further boost the GMI without sacrificing the simplicity of NN decoding principles. The GNNDR augments the NN decoding with two tunable ingredients: a processing function applied to the observation and a scaling function applied to codewords, both potentially dependent on the received signal and the channel state information (CSI). Within this formalism, the optimal processing and scaling functions, as well as the corresponding GMI, can be derived analytically for Gaussian codebooks, and the formalism remains applicable and extendable when the processing and scaling functions are subject to restrictions. The GNNDR formalism has subsequently been extended and applied to a variety of scenarios, including general input constellations
[
30
]
, channels with imperfect CSI
[
29
]
, and quasi-static fading channels
[
38
]
.

Despite the theoretical and practical appeal of the GNNDR scheme, existing studies have largely been confined to symbol-wise memoryless channels. Many practical communication systems, however, exhibit statistical dependence within finite blocks, arising from bandlimitation and dispersion
[
32
]
, multipath propagation and mobility
[
2
]
, phase noise
[
14
]
, and related effects. We model such scenarios through in-block memory (IBM) channels
[
40
,
23
]
, where different blocks are statistically independent while the symbols within each block may be dependent.

We emphasize that the IBM model is not introduced here as a new channel model in itself: mathematically, one may regard each block as a vector-valued channel use. The key point is instead architectural. A direct element-wise application of GNNDR ignores the statistical structure shared by symbols within the same block, whereas a block-level nearest-neighbor metric can exploit this structure through matrix-valued scaling and vector-valued processing. This distinction is essential, for example, in block noncoherent AWGN channels
[
25
,
28
]
, where all symbols in a block share a common unknown phase, and in phase-noise channels
[
49
]
, where the phase process is correlated within a block. Motivated by this observation, the present work introduces the vectorized GNNDR (Vec-GNNDR) and characterizes its GMI-optimal metric for Gaussian codebooks. The main contributions of this work are summarized as follows:

•

We formulate Vec-GNNDR as a block-level mismatched nearest-neighbor architecture for IBM channels. Unlike element-wise GNNDR, the proposed metric allows vector-valued processing and matrix-valued codeword scaling, thereby preserving the statistical structure within each block. For Gaussian codebooks, we derive an analytical expression for the GMI of Vec-GNNDR using the moment-generating function technique.

•

By maximizing the GMI, the optimal processing function, scaling function, and the corresponding optimal GMI are obtained in closed form under a weaker assumption than that adopted in
[
42
]
, notably by removing the power constraint on the conditional variance.

•

Building upon the derivation of the optimal Vec-GNNDR, we formulate a joint codebook-metric design problem over Gaussian input covariance matrices and Vec-GNNDR metrics. For every fixed covariance, the inner metric optimization is solved in closed form, reducing the joint design to an input-covariance optimization problem. As a tractable first instance, we derive first-order self-consistent optimality conditions for the diagonal covariance family.

•

The optimal Vec-GNNDR is further characterized under several restricted configurations of the processing and scaling functions, including constant scalar scaling, constant matrix scaling, CSI-dependent scalar scaling, CSI-dependent matrix scaling, and linear processing. The corresponding optimal GMI for each restricted scenario is obtained analytically.

•

We numerically evaluate the optimal GMI achieved by the optimal Vec-GNNDR over block noncoherent AWGN channels and phase-noise channels, demonstrating the performance superiority of the proposed Vec-GNNDR scheme.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section
II
presents the system model. Section
III
derives the GMI for the proposed Vec-GNNDR, provides an analytical characterization of the optimal Vec-GNNDR and its corresponding GMI, and develops a joint codebook-metric design perspective through first-order conditions for diagonal Gaussian covariance matrices. Section
IV
investigates a number of restricted or suboptimal configurations of the Vec-GNNDR and establishes their achievable GMIs. Section
V
numerically validates the performance of the Vec-GNNDR in two representative IBM channel scenarios. Finally, Section
VI
concludes the paper and highlights directions for future work.

II
System Model

We consider a general in-block memory channel with input
\bm{X}\in\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{C}^{B_{x}}
, output
\bm{Y}\in\mathcal{Y}=\mathbb{C}^{B_{y}}
,
state
\bm{S}\in\mathcal{S}
, and receiver channel state information (CSI)
\bm{V}\in\mathcal{V}
. The sets
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{V}
are general, not necessarily scalar-valued. We assume that the state is independent of the input, and that the channel is used without feedback. A code of length
N:=LB_{x}
is divided into
L
blocks, each associated with an input of length
B_{x}
. Formally, we have

\displaystyle P_{\bm{S},\bm{V}}\left(\bm{s}^{L},\bm{v}^{L}\right)

\displaystyle=\prod_{l=1}^{L}P_{\bm{S},\bm{V}}\left(\bm{s}_{l},\bm{v}_{l}\right),

(1)

\displaystyle P_{\bm{Y}\mid\bm{X},\bm{S}}\left(\bm{y}^{L}\mid\bm{x}^{L},\bm{s}^{L}\right)

\displaystyle=\prod_{l=1}^{L}P_{\bm{Y}\mid\bm{X},\bm{S}}\left(\bm{y}_{l}\mid\bm{x}_{l},\bm{s}_{l}\right).

This formulation indicates that the in-block memory channel is memoryless across blocks while potentially exhibiting memory within each block, as captured by the joint conditional probability
P_{\bm{Y}|\bm{X},\bm{S}}
. We further assume that, throughout each block transmission, the relation
\bm{V}\leftrightarrow\bm{S}\leftrightarrow(\bm{X},\bm{Y})
forms a Markov chain, which implies that the receiver CSI
\bm{V}
is generated through a mechanism independent of the current channel realization. The channel model is illustrated in Figure
1
.

Remark 1
.

For an in-block memory channel, the received vector
\bm{y}_{l}
of length
B_{y}
need not have the same length
B_{x}
as the transmitted vector
\bm{x}_{l}
.
Examples with
B_{x}>B_{y}
include the multiple-input single-output (MISO) systems
[
36
]
. Examples with
B_{x}<B_{y}
include the single-input multi-output (SIMO) channel
[
31
]
, fractionally-spaced channel
[
19
]
.

Fig. 1:
Illustration of an in-block memory channel. The message
m
is encoded into a sequence
\bm{x}^{L}
, where each input block
\bm{x}_{l}
has dimension
B_{x}
. The channel is characterized by the conditional distribution
P_{\bm{Y}|\bm{X},\bm{S}}
, which maps the input
\bm{x}_{l}
and state
\bm{s}_{l}
to an output vector
\bm{y}_{l}
of length
B_{y}
. The receiver utilizes the received sequence
\bm{y}^{L}
and the CSI
\bm{v}^{L}
to produce the estimate
\hat{m}
.

We employ an i.i.d. Gaussian random codebook ensemble, where the codebook is composed of mutually independent codewords drawn from
\mathcal{CN}(0,P\bm{I}_{N})
under an average power constraint
P
. For a code rate
R
, the encoder selects a message
m
uniformly at random from
\mathcal{M}=\left\{1,\ldots,\left\lceil e^{NR}\right\rceil\right\}
for transmission. The encoding function
\mathcal{E}:\mathcal{M}\mapsto\mathcal{X}^{L}=(\mathbb{C}^{B_{x}})^{\otimes L}
assigns each
m\in\mathcal{M}
to an
N
-symbol codeword
\bm{x}^{L}(m)
comprising
L
blocks, with
\bm{x}^{L}(m)\sim\mathcal{CN}(0,P\bm{I}_{N})
.

Remark 2
.

Indeed, the codebook can be generated such that each block follows a colored Gaussian distribution
\bm{x}_{l}(m)\sim\mathcal{CN}(\mathbf{0},P\bm{\Sigma})
with power constraint
\mathrm{Tr}(\bm{\Sigma})=B_{x}
, explicitly allowing for intra-block correlation. However, via the whitening transformation
\hat{\bm{x}}_{l}(m)=\bm{\Sigma}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\bm{x}_{l}(m)
, we can transform the inputs into the white Gaussian form
\tilde{\bm{x}}_{l}(m)\sim\mathcal{CN}(\mathbf{0},P\mathbf{I}_{B_{x}})
. Consequently, the problem reduces to transmission through an equivalent channel with transition probability
P_{\bm{Y}\mid\tilde{\bm{X}},\bm{S}}(\bm{y}_{l}\mid\hat{\bm{x}}_{l},\bm{s}_{l})=P_{\bm{Y}\mid\bm{X},\bm{S}}(\bm{y}_{l}\mid\bm{\Sigma}^{\frac{1}{2}}\hat{\bm{x}}_{l},\bm{s}_{l})
with white Gaussian random codebook. This observation motivates a joint codebook-metric design viewpoint, in which
\bm{\Sigma}
specifies the Gaussian codebook ensemble while the Vec-GNNDR metric is optimized conditionally on that ensemble; see Section
III-C
.

Upon receiving the channel output
\bm{y}^{L}
and the corresponding CSI
\bm{v}^{L}
, the decoder employs a decoding function
\mathcal{D}:\mathcal{X}^{L}\times\mathcal{Y}^{L}\mapsto\mathcal{L}
to map the received signals
(\bm{y}^{L},\bm{v}^{L})
to a decoded message
\hat{m}
. As discussed in the introduction, the optimal decoding strategy is the maximum likelihood (ML) decoding. For Gaussian channels, ML decoding simplifies to the computationally efficient nearest-neighbor decoding rule (NNDR) based on Euclidean distance. However, for general in-block memory channels, ML decoding can be computationally intractable. Motivated by the efficiency of NNDR, we adopt the following Vec-GNNDR:

\hat{m}=\arg\min_{m\in\mathcal{M}}\sum_{l=1}^{L}\left\|\bm{g}\left(\bm{y}_{l},\bm{v}_{l}\right)-\bm{f}\left(\bm{y}_{l},\bm{v}_{l}\right)\bm{x}_{l}(m)\right\|_{2}^{2},

(2)

where
\bm{g}:\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{V}\mapsto\mathbb{C}^{B}
is a processing function and
\bm{f}:\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{V}\mapsto\mathbb{C}^{B\times B_{x}}
is a scaling matrix. For the convenience of subsequent theoretical analysis, we assume

\bm{g}(\bm{y},\bm{v})=[g_{1}(\bm{y},\bm{v}),g_{2}(\bm{y},\bm{v}),\cdots,g_{B}(\bm{y},\bm{v})]^{\top},

(3)

and let
\bm{f}(\bm{y},\bm{v})
admit the following singular value decomposition (SVD):

\bm{f}(\bm{y},\bm{v})=\sum_{i=1}^{B\wedge B_{x}}\sigma_{i}(\bm{y},\bm{v})\bm{u}_{i}(\bm{y},\bm{v})\bm{w}^{H}_{i}(\bm{y},\bm{v}),

(4)

where
\sigma_{i}:\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{V}\mapsto\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
,
\bm{u}_{i}:\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{V}\mapsto\mathbb{C}^{B}
for
1\leq i\leq B
and
\bm{w}_{i}:\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{V}\mapsto\mathbb{C}^{B_{x}}
for
1\leq i\leq B_{x}
. We further define

\displaystyle\bm{U}(\bm{y},\bm{v})

\displaystyle=[\bm{u}_{1}(\bm{y},\bm{v}),\bm{u}_{2}(\bm{y},\bm{v}),\cdots,\bm{u}_{B}(\bm{y},\bm{v})],

\displaystyle\bm{W}(\bm{y},\bm{v})

\displaystyle=[\bm{w}_{1}(\bm{y},\bm{v}),\bm{w}_{2}(\bm{y},\bm{v}),\cdots,\bm{w}_{B_{x}}(\bm{y},\bm{v})],

hence
\bm{U}:\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{V}\mapsto\mathbb{U}(B)
and
\bm{W}:\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{V}\mapsto\mathbb{U}(B_{x})
.

Noting that
\bm{U}(\bm{y},\bm{v})\in\mathbb{U}(B)
is an isometric transformation, it preserves distances, as demonstrated by the following equality:

\left\|\bm{U}^{H}(\bm{y},\bm{v})\left[\bm{g}(\bm{y},\bm{v})-\bm{f}(\bm{y},\bm{v})\bm{x}\right]\right\|_{2}=\left\|\bm{g}(\bm{y},\bm{v})-\bm{f}(\bm{y},\bm{v})\bm{x}\right\|_{2},

(5)

without changing the decoding rule (
2
). Thus, without loss of generality, we may assume
\bm{U}(\bm{y},\bm{v})=\bm{I}_{B}
.

For the case of
B<B_{x}
, the scaling matrix projects
\bm{x}_{l}(m)
onto a lower-dimensional subspace, incurring information loss and reducing the achievable rate. For this reason, this case is not considered further in the paper. For the case of
B\geq B_{x}
,
the Vec-GNNDR exhibits an inherent redundancy: the additional dimensions beyond
B_{x}
do not contribute to the decoding decision, as established in the following lemma.

Lemma 1
.

The Vec-GNNDR with
B\geq B_{x}
can be equivalently reduced to the case of
B=B_{x}
without altering the decoding result.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we set
\bm{U}=\bm{I}_{B_{x}}
.
When
B\geq B_{x}
, by applying the SVD in (
4
), the Vec-GNNDR can be rewritten as

\hat{m}=\arg\min_{m\in\mathcal{M}}\sum_{l=1}^{L}\sum_{i=1}^{B_{x}}\left|g_{i}(\bm{y}_{l},\bm{v}_{l})-\sigma_{i}(\bm{y}_{l},\bm{v}_{l})(\bm{w}_{i}^{H}\bm{x}_{l}(m))\right|^{2}+L\sum_{i=B_{x}+1}^{B}\left|g_{i}(\bm{y}_{l},\bm{v}_{l})\right|^{2}.

The last
B-B_{x}
components are independent of
\bm{x}(m)
and thus have no influence on the minimization in the decoding rule. Consequently, the Vec-GNNDR effectively reduces to the case
B=B_{x}
. This completes the proof.
∎

In view of Lemma
1
, subsequent analyses are henceforth restricted to the case
B=B_{x}
, without loss of generality.

Although the Vec-GNNDR is not generally optimal, it is a tractable solution, and the inclusion of carefully designed processing and scaling functions further boosts its performance. In the next section, we employ the GMI approach to characterize the achievable rate with decoding rule (
2
) and derive the optimal processing-scaling pair by maximizing the GMI.

III
GMI and Optimal Vec-GNNDR

In this section, we derive the GMI for arbitrary Vec-GNNDR schemes and determine the optimal Vec-GNNDR by maximizing the GMI with respect to both the processing and scaling functions.

III-A

GMI of Vec-GNNDR

We extend the standard terminology and notation of mismatched decoding from memoryless channels to in-block memory channels. Consider an in-block memory channel depicted by the conditional probability density
P_{\bm{Y}\mid\bm{X}}
with input
\bm{X}\in\mathcal{X}
and
\bm{Y}\in\mathcal{Y}
. For a target rate
R
and block length
B_{x}
, a codeword of length
N=LB_{x}
is partitioned into
L
blocks. A codebook
\mathcal{C}
contains
\lceil e^{NR}\rceil
codewords. Each message
m\in\mathcal{M}\triangleq\left\{1,\ldots,\left\lceil e^{NR}\right\rceil\right\}
is mapped to
\bm{x}^{L}(m)=(\bm{x}_{1}(m),\bm{x}_{2}(m),\cdots,\bm{x}_{L}(m))
with
\bm{x}_{l}(m)\in\mathcal{X}
,
1\leq l\leq L
. Let
d:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\to R
be a so-called ”decoding metric”. The induced mismatched decoder selects

\mathcal{D}_{d}:\hat{m}=\arg\min_{m\in\mathcal{M}}\sum_{l=1}^{L}d\left(\bm{x}_{l}(m),\bm{y}_{l}\right),

with ties resolved arbitrarily. A rate
R
is achievable if there exists a sequence of
(L,R)
-codebooks for which the maximal decoding-error probability approaches zero asymptotically as
L\to\infty
. The supremum of all achievable rates is called the mismatch capacity of the in-block memory channel.

In
[
40
]
, the Shannon capacity of in-block memory channels is characterized from a game-theoretic perspective. However, for mismatched decoding, the exact mismatch capacity remains an open problem even for memoryless channels, with only upper and lower bounds currently available
[
24
,
33
]
. Determining the mismatch capacity for in-block memory channels is therefore also intractable in general. We resort to the GMI, which provides a convenient lower bound on the mismatch capacity for in-block memory channels.

To characterize the GMI, we consider the Vec-GNNDR with processing function
\bm{g}
and scaling function
\bm{f}
satisfying the following assumption.

Assumption 1
.

The processing function
\bm{g}
and scaling function
\bm{f}
satisfy

\mathbb{E}_{\bm{Y},\bm{V}}\left[|g_{i}(\bm{Y},\bm{V})|^{2}\right]<+\infty,\quad\mathbb{E}_{\bm{Y},\bm{V}}\left[\sigma_{i}^{2}(\bm{Y},\bm{V})\right]<+\infty,\quad\mathrm{for}\;1\leq i\leq B_{x}

Remark 3
.

This is a technical assumption ensuring the applicability of the strong law of large numbers (Lemma
2
), which yields

\displaystyle\lim_{L\to\infty}\frac{1}{L}\sum_{l=1}^{L}|g_{i}(\bm{y}_{l},\bm{v}_{l})|^{2}\overset{a.s.}{\to}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{Y},\bm{V}}\left[|g_{i}^{2}(\bm{Y},\bm{V})|\right],\;\;1\leq i\leq B_{x}

\displaystyle\lim_{L\to\infty}\frac{1}{L}\sum_{l=1}^{L}\sigma^{2}_{i}(\bm{y}_{l},\bm{v}_{l})\overset{a.s.}{\to}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{Y},\bm{V}}\left[\sigma_{i}^{2}(\bm{Y},\bm{V})\right],\;\;1\leq i\leq B_{x},

where the almost sure convergence plays a key role in the derivation of the GMI.

Proposition 1
.

Consider the in-block memory channel defined in (
1
).
Let
\bm{g}
and
\bm{f}
be fixed functions as specified in (
3
) and (
4
), respectively, satisfying Assumption
1
.
Under this setting, the decoding metric is

d(\bm{x},(\bm{y},\bm{v}))=\left\|\bm{g}(\bm{y},\bm{v})-\bm{f}(\bm{y},\bm{v})\bm{x}\right\|_{2}^{2}.

The resulting GMI is given by

I_{\mathrm{GMI},\bm{g},\bm{f}}=B_{x}^{-1}\max_{\theta<0}\left\{\theta\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|\bm{g}(\bm{Y},\bm{V})-\bm{f}(\bm{Y},\bm{V})\bm{X}\|_{2}^{2}\right]-\sum_{i=1}^{B_{x}}\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[\frac{\theta|g_{i}(\bm{Y},\bm{V})|}{1-\theta P\sigma_{i}^{2}(\bm{Y},\bm{V})^{2}}+\log\left(1-\theta P\sigma_{i}^{2}(\bm{Y},\bm{V})\right)\right]\right\}.

(6)

Proof.

We begin by analyzing the average decoding error probability over messages and codebook ensembles, denoted by
P(\hat{m}\neq m)
. Due to the inherent symmetry of the i.i.d. Gaussian codebook ensemble, we assume without loss of generality that the transmitted codeword corresponds to the message
m=1
. Consequently, we have

P(\hat{m}\neq m)=P(\hat{m}\neq 1|m=1).

Under the assumption
m=1
, the normalized decoding metric becomes

\displaystyle\mathrm{D}(1)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{l=1}^{L}d\left(\bm{X}_{l}(1),\left(\bm{Y}_{l},\bm{V}_{l}\right)\right)

(7)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{l=1}^{L}\|\bm{g}(\bm{Y},\bm{V})-\bm{f}(\bm{Y},\bm{V})\bm{X}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|\bm{g}(\bm{Y},\bm{V})-\bm{f}(\bm{Y},\bm{V})\bm{X}\|_{2}^{2}\right],

where the convergence holds almost surely by the strong law of large numbers (Lemma
2
), which is ensured by Assumption
1
. For any
\delta>0
, we define the event

\mathcal{A}_{\delta}=\left\{\mathrm{D}(1)\geq\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|\bm{g}(\bm{Y},\bm{V})-\bm{f}(\bm{Y},\bm{V})\bm{X}\|_{2}^{2}\right]+\delta\right\}.

Thus, the average decoding error probability can be decomposed as follows:

\displaystyle P(\hat{m}\neq 1\mid m=1)

\displaystyle=P\left(\hat{m}\neq 1\mid m=1,\mathcal{A}_{\delta}\right)P\left(\mathcal{A}_{\delta}\right)+P\left(\hat{m}\neq 1\mid m=1,\mathcal{A}_{\delta}^{c}\right)P\left(\mathcal{A}_{\delta}^{c}\right)

\displaystyle\leq P\left(\mathcal{A}_{\delta}\right)+P\left(\hat{m}\neq 1\mid m=1,\mathcal{A}_{\delta}^{c}\right)P\left(\mathcal{A}_{\delta}^{c}\right).

The first term on the right-hand side vanishes asymptotically for sufficiently large
L
. The second term can be bounded as

\displaystyle P\left(\hat{m}\neq 1\mid m=1,\mathcal{A}_{\delta}^{c}\right)P\left(\mathcal{A}_{\delta}^{c}\right)

\displaystyle\leq P\left(\exists m^{\prime}\neq 1,\mathrm{D}\left(m^{\prime}\right)<\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|\bm{g}(\bm{Y},\bm{V})-\bm{f}(\bm{Y},\bm{V})\bm{X}\|_{2}^{2}\right]+\delta\mid\mathcal{A}_{\delta}^{c}\right)P\left(\mathcal{A}_{\delta}^{c}\right)

\displaystyle\leq e^{LB_{x}R}P\left(\mathrm{D}(2)<\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|\bm{g}(\bm{Y},\bm{V})-\bm{f}(\bm{Y},\bm{V})\bm{X}\|_{2}^{2}\right]+\delta\mid\mathcal{A}_{\delta}^{c}\right)P\left(\mathcal{A}_{\delta}^{c}\right)

\displaystyle=e^{LB_{x}R}P\left(\mathrm{D}(2)<\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|\bm{g}(\bm{Y},\bm{V})-f(\bm{Y},\bm{V})\bm{X}\|_{2}^{2}\right]+\delta,\mathcal{A}_{\delta}^{c}\right)

\displaystyle\leq e^{LB_{x}R}P\left(\mathrm{D}(2)<\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|\bm{g}(\bm{Y},\bm{V})-\bm{f}(\bm{Y},\bm{V})\bm{X}\|_{2}^{2}\right]+\delta\right).

Using the law of total expectation, we have

\displaystyle P\left(\mathrm{D}(2)<\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|\bm{g}(\bm{Y},\bm{V})-\bm{f}(\bm{Y},\bm{V})\bm{X}\|_{2}^{2}\right]+\delta\right)=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[P\left(\mathrm{D}(2)<\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|\bm{g}(\bm{Y},\bm{V})-\bm{f}(\bm{Y},\bm{V})\bm{X}\|_{2}^{2}\right]+\delta\mid\bm{Y}^{L},\bm{V}^{L}\right)\right],

(8)

where the expectation is taken over
(\bm{Y}^{L},\bm{V}^{L})=\{(\bm{Y}_{l},\bm{V}_{l})_{l=1}^{L}\}
, a collection of
L
i.i.d. random variables
(\bm{Y},\bm{V})
.

Conditioned upon
(\bm{Y}^{L},\bm{V}^{L})
, the normalized decoding metric

\mathrm{D}(2)=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{l=1}^{L}\underbrace{\|\bm{g}(\bm{Y}_{l},\bm{V}_{l})-\bm{f}(\bm{Y}_{l},\bm{V}_{l})\bm{X}(2)\|_{2}^{2}}_{\tilde{\chi}^{2}_{l}},

is the empirical mean of
L
independent generalized
\chi^{2}
random variables
\{\tilde{\chi}^{2}_{l}\}_{l=1}^{L}
. Specifically,

\tilde{\chi}_{l}^{2}=\sum_{l=1}^{B_{x}}\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{i}^{2}(\bm{Y}_{l},\bm{V}_{l})\chi^{2}\left(2,\frac{|g_{i}(\bm{Y}_{l},\bm{V}_{l})|^{2}}{\sigma_{i}^{2}(\bm{Y}_{l},\bm{V}_{l})}\right),

where
\chi^{2}(k,\lambda)
denotes a non-central chi-square variable with
k
degrees of freedom and non-centrality parameter
\lambda
. The moment generating function of
\tilde{\chi}^{2}_{l}
is given by

M_{l}(\theta)=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}e^{\theta\tilde{\chi}_{l}^{2}}=\exp{\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{B_{x}}\frac{|g_{i}(\bm{Y}_{l},\bm{V}_{l})|^{2}\theta}{1-\sigma_{i}^{2}(\bm{Y}_{l},\bm{V}_{l})\theta}\right\}}\prod_{i=1}^{B_{x}}\left[1-\sigma^{2}_{i}(\bm{Y}_{l},\bm{V}_{l})\theta\right]^{-1}

for any
\theta<0
.
Applying Markov’s inequality, we obtain

\displaystyle P\Big(D(2)<\mathbb{E}\big[\|\bm{g}(\bm{Y},\bm{V})-\bm{f}(\bm{Y},

\displaystyle\bm{V})\bm{X}\|_{2}^{2}\big]\Big)=P\left(\exp\left\{\theta LD(2)\right\}>\exp\left\{\theta L\mathbb{E}\left[\|\bm{g}(\bm{Y},\bm{V})-\bm{f}(\bm{Y},\bm{V})\bm{X}\|_{2}^{2}\right]\right\}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\min_{\theta<0}\mathbb{E}_{(\bm{Y}^{L},\bm{V}^{L})}\left[\exp\left\{\theta LD(2)-\theta L\mathbb{E}\left[\|\bm{g}(\bm{Y},\bm{V})-\bm{f}(\bm{Y},\bm{V})\bm{X}\|_{2}^{2}\right]\right\}\right]

\displaystyle=\min_{\theta<0}\mathbb{E}_{(\bm{Y}^{L},\bm{V}^{L})}\exp{\left\{L\left(\frac{1}{L}\sum_{l=1}^{L}\log M_{l}(\theta)-\theta\mathbb{E}\left[\|\bm{g}(\bm{Y},\bm{V})-\bm{f}(\bm{Y},\bm{V})\bm{X}\|_{2}^{2}\right]\right)\right\}}.

With Assumption
1
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{Y},\bm{V}}\left|\left[\frac{|g_{i}(\bm{Y},\bm{V})|^{2}\theta}{1-\sigma_{i}^{2}(\bm{Y},\bm{V})\theta}\right]\right|\leq\mathbb{E}_{\bm{Y},\bm{V}}|\theta|\left|g_{i}(\bm{Y},\bm{V})\right|^{2}<+\infty,\quad 1\leq i\leq B_{x},

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{Y},\bm{V}}\left|\log(1-\theta\sigma_{i}^{2}(\bm{Y},\bm{V}))\right|\leq\mathbb{E}_{\bm{Y},\bm{V}}|\theta|\sigma_{i}^{2}(\bm{Y},\bm{V})<+\infty,\quad 1\leq i\leq B_{x}.

Therefore, by the strong law of large numbers (Lemma
2
),

\frac{1}{L}\sum_{l=1}^{L}\log M_{l}(\theta)\to\sum_{i=1}^{B_{x}}\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\bm{Y},\bm{V}}\left[\frac{|g_{i}(\bm{Y},\bm{V})|^{2}\theta}{1-\sigma_{i}^{2}(\bm{Y},\bm{V})\theta}\right]-\sum_{i=1}^{B_{x}}\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\bm{Y},\bm{V}}\left[\log\left(1-\theta\sigma^{2}_{i}(\bm{Y},\bm{V})\right)\right],

(9)

and thus, the conditional probability in (
8
) exponentially decays to zero at a rate given by

B_{x}^{-1}\max_{\theta<0}\theta\left(\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|\bm{g}(\bm{Y},\bm{V})-\bm{f}(\bm{Y},\bm{V})\bm{X}\|_{2}^{2}\right]\right)-\sum_{i=1}^{B_{x}}\left(\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[\frac{\theta|g_{i}(\bm{Y},\bm{V})|^{2}}{1-\theta P\sigma_{i}^{2}(\bm{Y},\bm{V})}\right]+\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[\log\left(1-\theta P\sigma_{i}^{2}\left(\bm{Y},\bm{V}\right)\right)\right]\right).

(10)

Consequently, for any rate
R<I_{\text{GMI},\bm{g},\bm{f}}
, we can find sufficiently small
\delta>0
and sufficiently large
L
such that the average decoding error probability approaches zero.
∎

Remark 4
.

For channels with memory extending across blocks, the preceding analysis no longer holds. Specifically, the almost sure convergence of
\mathrm{D}(1)
in (
7
) and of the empirical mean of the moment generating function in (
9
) cannot be guaranteed because the strong law of large numbers (Lemma
2
), as invoked under Assumption
1
, fails when blocks are correlated rather than independent.

If convergence can nevertheless be established for all bounded continuous functions
f
and
g
in (
7
) and (
9
), the analysis continues to hold. For instance, if
{(\bm{y}_{l},\bm{v}_{l})}_{l=1}^{\infty}
is identically distributed and the sequences

\frac{1}{L}\sum_{l=1}^{L}g_{i}(\bm{y}_{l},\bm{v}_{l})-\mathbb{E}g_{i}(\bm{Y},\bm{V})\quad\mathrm{and}\quad\frac{1}{L}\sum_{l=1}^{L}\sigma_{i}(\bm{y}_{l},\bm{v}_{l})-\mathbb{E}\sigma_{i}(\bm{Y},\bm{V}),

form martingales satisfying the following conditions

\mathbb{E}_{\bm{Y},\bm{V}}|g_{i}(\bm{Y},\bm{V})|^{2}\log\left(1+|g_{i}(\bm{Y},\bm{V})|^{2}\right)<+\infty,\quad\mathbb{E}_{\bm{Y},\bm{V}}\sigma_{i}^{2}(\bm{Y},\bm{V})\log\left(1+\sigma_{i}(\bm{Y},\bm{V})^{2}\right)<+\infty,\quad\mathrm{for}\;1\leq i\leq B_{x},

then the strong law of large numbers for identically distributed marginal differences sequence (Lemma
3
)
still applies, and the proposition remains valid. In practice, however, verifying these conditions for general channels with memory is rarely tractable.

III-B

GMI-maximizing Vec-GNNDR

In this subsection, we investigate the optimal choices of the functions
\bm{g}(\bm{y},\bm{v})
and
\bm{f}(\bm{y},\bm{v})
that maximize the GMI
I_{\text{GMI},\bm{g},\bm{f}}
given in Proposition.
1
. Since rescaling both
\bm{g}(\bm{y},\bm{v})
and
\bm{f}(\bm{y},\bm{v})
by the same factor does not affect the decoded message
\hat{m}
, the scalar parameter
\theta
can be absorbed into both processing and scaling functions.
For simplicity, we henceforth set
\theta=-1
.
To facilitate the optimization for GMI, we impose the following assumption on the channel:

Assumption 2
.

For the in-block memory channel (
1
), the conditional covariance matrix
\mathrm{Cov}[\bm{X}\mid\bm{Y},\bm{V}]
is positive definite almost surely.

Remark 5
.

This is both a meaningful and technically essential assumption. If
\operatorname{Cov}[\bm{X}\mid\bm{Y},\bm{V}]
has zero eigenvalues with nonzero probability, then there exists a measurable set
A
with probability mass
p=P\{(Y,V)\in A\}>0
, such that for any
(\bm{y},\bm{v})\in A
, the support of the conditional distribution of
\bm{X}\mid\bm{y},\bm{v}
is contained in an affine subspace of dimension strictly smaller than
B_{x}
. However, since the prior distribution
\mathcal{CN}(0,P\bm{I}_{B_{x}})
is absolutely continuous and has strictly positive density everywhere on
\mathbb{R}^{B_{x}}
, the corresponding mutual information is given by

\displaystyle I(\bm{X};\bm{Y},\bm{V})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{Y},\bm{V}}\left[D(P_{\bm{X}\mid\bm{Y},\bm{V}}(\cdot\mid\bm{Y},\bm{V})||\mathcal{CN}(0,P\bm{I}_{B_{x}}))\right]

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}_{\bm{Y},\bm{V}}\left[D(P_{\bm{X}\mid\bm{Y},\bm{V}}(\cdot\mid\bm{Y},\bm{V})||\mathcal{CN}(0,P\bm{I}_{B_{x}}))\mathbb{I}\{(\bm{Y},\bm{V})\in A\}\right]\geq p\cdot(+\infty)=+\infty,

where
D(\cdot\|\cdot)
denotes the Kullback–Leibler divergence. Consequently, the achievable rate of the in-block memory channel is unbounded. To illustrate, consider the special case
B_{x}=B_{y}=1
with no CSI
V
. If the channel output
y\in A
, then
\operatorname{Var}[X\mid y]=0
, which means the channel input can be exactly recovered via
\mathbb{E}[X\mid y]
. Suppose each codeword is transmitted
n
times independently. Then the probability of exact recovery is
1-(1-p)^{n}\to 1
as
n\to\infty
. This exact recovery capability allows the minimum codeword distance to be chosen arbitrarily small, thereby enabling the codebook to contain an arbitrarily large number of codewords. Consequently, the achievable rate can be made arbitrarily large. It is of course not meaningful to consider scenarios where the achievable rate is infinite.

Meanwhile, this assumption also serves a technical role: it guarantees that all eigenvalues of the conditional covariance matrix are strictly positive, thereby avoiding singularity issues that would otherwise arise in the optimization of the processing function
\bm{g}
and the scaling function
\bm{f}
in the subsequent analysis.

The Vec-GNNDR with its optimal processing and scaling functions, together with the corresponding GMI, is characterized in the following theorem.

Theorem 1
.

Consider the in-block memory channel described in Section
II
, which satisfies Assumption
2
. For a given channel output
\bm{y}
and receiver CSI
\bm{v}
,
define the conditional mean and the conditional covariance matrix respectively as

\bm{\mu}(\bm{y},\bm{v})=\mathbb{E}[\bm{X}\mid\bm{y},\bm{v}],\qquad\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{y},\bm{v})=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[\bm{X}\bm{X}^{H}|\bm{y},\bm{v}\right]-\bm{\mu}(\bm{y},\bm{v})\bm{\mu}^{H}(\bm{y},\bm{v}).

(11)

Let
\lambda_{i}(\bm{y},\bm{v})
denote the
i
-th largest eigenvalue of
\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{y},\bm{v})
, and let
\bm{W}(\bm{y},\bm{v})
be the corresponding diagonalizing matrix such that

\bm{W}^{H}(\bm{y},\bm{v})\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{y},\bm{v})\bm{W}(\bm{y},\bm{v})=\operatorname{diag}\left(\lambda_{1}(\bm{y},\bm{v}),\cdots,\lambda_{B_{x}}(\bm{y},\bm{v})\right).

Define the truncated eigenvalues for any
\epsilon>0
by

[\lambda_{i}(\bm{y},\bm{v})]_{\varepsilon}=\lambda_{i}(\bm{y},\bm{v})\,\mathbb{I}\{\lambda_{i}(\bm{y},\bm{v})<P\}+\frac{P}{P+\delta_{\varepsilon}(\bm{y},\bm{v})}\,\mathbb{I}\{\lambda_{i}(\bm{y},\bm{v})\geq P\},

where
\delta_{\varepsilon}(\bm{y},\bm{v})=0
if
\lambda_{1}(\bm{y},\bm{v})<P
, and otherwise
\delta_{\varepsilon}(\bm{y},\bm{v})
is uniquely determined as the solution to

\log(1+P\delta)-\delta\lambda_{1}(\bm{y},\bm{v})=-\varepsilon.

(12)

Then the following statements hold:

•

The optimal value of the GMI in Proposition
1
is given by

I_{\mathrm{GMI,opt}}=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{Y},\bm{V}}\left[\frac{1}{B_{x}}\sum_{i=1}^{B_{x}}\psi\left(\lambda_{i}(\bm{Y},\bm{V})\right)+\frac{1}{B_{x}P}\|\bm{\mu}(\bm{Y},\bm{V})\|_{2}^{2}\right],

(13)

where the function
\psi:(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
is given by:

\psi(q)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\log\!\frac{P}{q}-1+\frac{q}{P},&0<q<P,\\[6.0pt]
0,&q\geq P.\end{cases}

(14)

•

With the Vec-GNNDR employing the processing function
\bm{g}^{\varepsilon}
and the scaling function
\bm{f}^{\varepsilon}
defined by

\bm{g}^{\varepsilon}(\bm{y},\bm{v})=\mathrm{diag}\left(\sqrt{\frac{P}{(P-[\lambda_{i}(\bm{y},\bm{v})]_{\varepsilon})[\lambda_{i}(\bm{y},\bm{v})]_{\varepsilon}}}\right)\bm{W}^{H}(\bm{y},\bm{v})\bm{\mu}(\bm{y},\bm{v}),

(15)

\bm{f}^{\varepsilon}(\bm{y},\bm{v})=\mathrm{diag}\left(\sqrt{\frac{P-[\lambda_{i}(\bm{y},\bm{v})]_{\varepsilon}}{P[\lambda_{i}(\bm{y},\bm{v})]_{\varepsilon}}}\right)\bm{W}^{H}(\bm{y},\bm{v}),

(16)

the corresponding GMI is at least
I_{\mathrm{GMI,opt}}-\varepsilon
.

Proof.

Setting
\theta=-1
in (
6
) and applying the law of total expectation, the first term expands as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\|\bm{g}(\bm{Y},\bm{V})-\bm{f}(\bm{Y},\bm{V})\bm{X}\|_{2}^{2}\right]=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{Y},\bm{V}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{\bm{X}|\bm{Y},\bm{V}}\left[\|\bm{g}(\bm{Y},\bm{V})-\bm{f}(\bm{Y},\bm{V})\bm{X}\|_{2}^{2}\right]\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{B_{x}}\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\bm{Y},\bm{V}}\left|g_{i}(\bm{Y},\bm{V})\right|^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{B_{x}}\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\bm{Y},\bm{V}}\left[\sigma_{i}^{2}(\bm{Y},\bm{V})\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\bm{X}|\bm{Y},\bm{V}}\left[\bm{X}^{H}\bm{w}_{i}(\bm{Y},\bm{V})\bm{w}_{i}^{H}(\bm{Y},\bm{V})\bm{X}\right]\right]

\displaystyle-2\sum_{i=1}^{B_{x}}\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\bm{Y},\bm{V}}\left[\sigma_{i}(\bm{Y},\bm{V})\Re\left(g_{i}(\bm{Y},\bm{V})^{H}\bm{w}_{i}^{H}(\bm{Y},\bm{V})\bm{\mu}(\bm{Y},\bm{V})\right)\right].

Hence, we can express
I_{\text{GMI},\bm{g},\bm{f}}
as

\displaystyle\max_{\bm{g},\bm{f}}I_{\text{GMI},\bm{g},\bm{f}}=B_{x}^{-1}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{Y},\bm{V}}\sum_{i=1}^{B_{x}}\bigg[\max_{\bm{g},\bm{f}}\Big\{\left[-|g_{i}(\bm{Y},\bm{V})|^{2}-\sigma_{i}^{2}(\bm{Y},\bm{V})\mathbb{E}_{\bm{X}|\bm{Y},\bm{V}}\left[\bm{X}^{H}\bm{w}_{i}(\bm{Y},\bm{V})\bm{w}_{i}^{H}(\bm{Y},\bm{V})\bm{X}\right]\right]

(17)

\displaystyle+2\sigma_{i}(\bm{Y},\bm{V})\Re\left(g_{i}(\bm{Y},\bm{V})^{H}\bm{w}_{i}^{H}(\bm{Y},\bm{V})\bm{\mu}(\bm{Y},\bm{V})\right)+\frac{|g_{i}(\bm{Y},\bm{V})|^{2}}{1+P\sigma_{i}^{2}(\bm{Y},\bm{V})}+\log(1+P\sigma^{2}_{i}(\bm{Y},\bm{V}))\Big\}\bigg].

For given
\bm{y},\bm{v}
, we now explore the optimal values of
\{g_{i}(\bm{y},\bm{v}),\sigma_{i}(\bm{y},\bm{v}),\bm{w}_{i}({\bm{y},\bm{v}})\}_{i=1,\cdots,B_{x}}
. To proceed, we first optimize the phase of
g_{i}(\bm{y},\bm{v})
by aligning it with
\bm{w}_{i}^{H}(\bm{y},\bm{v})\bm{\mu}(\bm{y},\bm{v})
:

\text{Arg}(g_{i}(\bm{y},\bm{v}))=\text{Arg}(\bm{w}_{i}^{H}(\bm{y},\bm{v})\bm{\mu}(\bm{y},\bm{v})),

(18)

so that the third term in (
17
) becomes

2\sigma_{i}(\bm{y},\bm{v})|g_{i}(\bm{y},\bm{v})|\left|\bm{w}_{i}^{H}(\bm{y},\bm{v})\bm{\mu}(\bm{y},\bm{v})\right|.

Next, to optimize
|g_{i}(\bm{y},\bm{v})|
, we face a quadratic maximization problem. The optimal value of
|g_{i}(\bm{y},\bm{v})|
is given by

|g_{i}(\bm{y},\bm{v})|=\frac{1+P\sigma_{i}^{2}(\bm{y},\bm{v})}{P\sigma_{i}(\bm{y},\bm{v})}\left|\bm{w}_{i}^{H}(\bm{y},\bm{v})\bm{\mu}(\bm{y},\bm{v})\right|.

(19)

Thus, the maximization problem for GMI reduces to the following form:

\displaystyle\max_{\{\sigma_{i},\bm{w}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{B_{x}}}\frac{1}{B_{x}}\sum_{i=1}^{B_{x}}\bigg\{

\displaystyle\log(1+P\sigma_{i}^{2}(\bm{y},\bm{v}))+\frac{1+P\sigma_{i}^{2}(\bm{y},\bm{v})}{P}\left|\bm{w}_{i}^{H}(\bm{y},\bm{v})\bm{\mu}(\bm{y},\bm{v})\right|^{2}

(20)

\displaystyle-\sigma_{i}^{2}(\bm{y},\bm{v})\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}[\bm{X}^{H}\bm{w}_{i}(\bm{y},\bm{v})\bm{w}^{H}_{i}(\bm{y},\bm{v})\bm{X}|\bm{y},\bm{v}]\bigg\}.

With the identity:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\bm{X}^{H}\bm{w}_{i}(\bm{y},\bm{v})\bm{w}_{i}^{H}(\bm{y},\bm{v})\bm{X}\mid\bm{y},\bm{v}]

\displaystyle=\bm{w}_{i}^{H}(\bm{y},\bm{v})\mathbb{E}[\bm{X}^{H}\bm{X}\mid\bm{y},\bm{v}]\bm{w}_{i}(\bm{y},\bm{v})

\displaystyle=\bm{w}_{i}^{H}(\bm{y},\bm{v})\left[\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{y},\bm{v})+\bm{\mu}(\bm{y},\bm{v})\bm{\mu}^{H}(\bm{y},\bm{v})\right]\bm{w}_{i}(\bm{y},\bm{v}),

we can rewrite the optimization problem in (
20
) as follows:

\max_{\{\sigma_{i},\bm{w}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{B_{x}}}\frac{1}{B_{x}}\sum_{i=1}^{B_{x}}\left\{\log(1+P\sigma_{i}^{2}(\bm{y},\bm{v}))-\sigma_{i}^{2}(\bm{y},\bm{v})\bm{w}_{i}^{H}(\bm{y},\bm{v})\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{y},\bm{v})\bm{w}_{i}(\bm{y},\bm{v})\right\}+\frac{1}{B_{x}P}\|\bm{\mu(\bm{y},\bm{v})}\|_{2}^{2},

(21)

where we have used the identity
\sum_{i=1}^{B_{x}}\bm{w}_{i}(\bm{y},\bm{v})\bm{w}_{i}^{H}(\bm{y},\bm{v})=\bm{I}_{B_{x}}
. Next, we determine the optimal value of
\sigma_{i}(\bm{y},\bm{v})
.
Differentiating the objective function in (
21
) with respect to
\sigma_{i}^{2}(\bm{y},\bm{v})
and setting the derivative equal to zero yields

\frac{P}{1+P\sigma^{2}_{i}(\bm{y},\bm{v})}-\sigma_{i}^{2}(\bm{y},\bm{v})\bm{w}_{i}^{H}(\bm{y},\bm{v})\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{y},\bm{v})\bm{w}_{i}(\bm{y},\bm{v})=0,

whence the stationary point

\sigma_{i}^{2}(\bm{y},\bm{v})=\frac{1}{\bm{w}_{i}^{H}(\bm{y},\bm{v})\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{y},\bm{v})\bm{w}_{i}(\bm{y},\bm{v})}-\frac{1}{P}.

(22)

We must enforce the constraint
\sigma_{i}^{2}(\bm{y},\bm{v})\geq 0
, but a naive truncation at zero when
\bm{w}_{i}^{H}(\bm{y},\bm{v})\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{y},\bm{v})\bm{w}_{i}(\bm{y},\bm{v})\geq P

would result in a loss of GMI.
Specifically, setting
\sigma_{i}(\bm{y},\bm{v})=0
directly in (
17
) forces the GMI contribution of index
i
to vanish.
In contrast, taking the limit
\sigma_{i}^{2}(\bm{y},\bm{v})\to 0
in (
20
) yields a non-negative contribution of
P^{-1}|\bm{w}_{i}^{H}(\bm{y},\bm{v})\bm{\mu}(\bm{y},\bm{v})|^{2}
. To capture this contribution, we truncate
\sigma_{i}^{2}(\bm{y},\bm{v})
at a sufficiently small
\delta(\bm{y},\bm{v})>0
and then let
\delta(\bm{y},\bm{v})\to 0
.

To choose an appropriate
\delta(\bm{y},\bm{v})
, we consider the following function

\varphi_{\bm{y},\bm{v}}(\sigma^{2})=\log\!\big(1+P\sigma^{2}\big)-\sigma^{2}\lambda_{\max}(\bm{y},\bm{v}),

where
\lambda_{\max}(\bm{y},\bm{v})
denotes the largest eigenvalue of
\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{y},\bm{v})
.
By the Rayleigh quotient (Lemma
4
),
if there exists
i
with
\bm{w}_{i}^{H}(\bm{y},\bm{v})\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{y},\bm{v})\bm{w}_{i}(\bm{y},\bm{v})\geq P
, then
\lambda_{\max}(\bm{y},\bm{v})\geq P
, and hence
\varphi_{\bm{y},\bm{v}}
is monotonically decreasing on
(0,\infty)
.
Therefore, for any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
\delta_{\varepsilon}(\bm{y},\bm{v})>0
such that

\varphi_{\bm{y},\bm{v}}\big(\delta_{\varepsilon}(\bm{y},\bm{v})\big)=-\varepsilon.

Using the Rayleigh quotient (Lemma
4
) again, we have

\bm{w}_{i}^{H}(\bm{y},\bm{v})\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{y},\bm{v})\bm{w}_{i}(\bm{y},\bm{v})\leq\lambda_{\max}(\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{y},\bm{v})).

Consequently, we obtain

\log(1+P(\delta_{\varepsilon}(\bm{y},\bm{v})))-\delta_{\varepsilon}(\bm{y},\bm{v})\bm{w}_{i}^{H}(\bm{y},\bm{v})\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{y},\bm{v})\bm{w}_{i}(\bm{y},\bm{v})\geq-\varepsilon,

which implies that the overall GMI loss induced by truncation at
\delta_{\varepsilon}(\bm{y},\bm{v})
is bounded above by
\varepsilon
. We thus truncate
\sigma_{i}^{2}(\bm{y},\bm{v})
as follows:

\left[\sigma_{i}^{2}(\bm{y},\bm{v})\right]_{\varepsilon}=\sigma_{i}^{2}(\bm{y},\bm{v})\mathbb{I}\{\sigma_{i}^{2}(\bm{y},\bm{v})>0\}+\delta_{\varepsilon}(\bm{y},\bm{v})\mathbb{I}\{\sigma_{i}^{2}(\bm{y},\bm{v})\leq 0\},

(23)

where
\sigma^{2}_{i}(\bm{y},\bm{v})
is given by (
22
).
Plugging (
23
) into (
21
) and letting
\varepsilon\to 0
, the maximization problem for GMI is transformed into:

\sup_{\bm{g},\bm{f}}I_{\mathrm{GMI},\bm{g},\bm{f}}=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{Y},\bm{V}}\left\{\max_{\{\bm{w}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{B_{x}}}\frac{1}{B_{x}}\sum_{i=1}^{B_{x}}\psi(\bm{w}_{i}^{H}(\bm{Y},\bm{V})\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{Y},\bm{V})\bm{w}_{i}(\bm{Y},\bm{V}))+\frac{1}{B_{x}P}\|\bm{\mu}(\bm{Y},\bm{V})\|_{2}^{2}\right\},

(24)

where
\psi
is given by (
14
).
Then by spectral majorization inequality (Lemma
8
) and the convexity of
\psi
(Lemma
7
), we obtain

I_{\mathrm{GMI,opt}}=\sup_{\bm{g},\bm{f}}I_{\mathrm{GMI},\bm{g},\bm{f}}=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{Y},\bm{V}}\left[\frac{1}{B_{x}}\sum_{i=1}^{B_{x}}\psi\left(\lambda_{i}(\bm{Y},\bm{V})\right)+\frac{1}{B_{x}P}\|\bm{\mu}(\bm{Y},\bm{V})\|_{2}^{2}\right].

Equality is attained if
\bm{W}(\bm{y},\bm{v})
diagonalizes
\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{y},\bm{v})
, i.e.,

\bm{W}^{H}(\bm{y},\bm{v})\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{y},\bm{v})\bm{W}(\bm{y},\bm{v})=\text{diag}\left(\lambda_{1}(\bm{y},\bm{v}),\cdots,\lambda_{B_{x}}(\bm{y},\bm{v})\right).

(25)

Substituting (
25
) into (
22
) and
(
23
), we obtain

[\sigma_{i}(\bm{y},\bm{v})]_{\varepsilon}=\sqrt{\frac{P-[\lambda_{i}(\bm{y},\bm{v})]_{\varepsilon}}{P[\lambda_{i}(\bm{y},\bm{v})]_{\varepsilon}}},

(26)

where
[\lambda_{i}(\bm{y},\bm{v})]_{\varepsilon}
is defined by

[\lambda_{i}(\bm{y},\bm{v})]_{\varepsilon}=\lambda_{i}(\bm{y},\bm{v})\mathbb{I}\{\lambda_{i}(\bm{y},\bm{v})<P\}+\frac{P}{P+\delta_{\varepsilon}(\bm{y},\bm{v})}\mathbb{I}\{\lambda_{i}(\bm{y},\bm{v})\geq P\}.

Next, substituting (
26
) and (
25
) into (
4
), we get

\bm{f}^{\varepsilon}(\bm{y},\bm{v})=\mathrm{diag}\left(\sqrt{\frac{P-[\lambda_{i}(\bm{y},\bm{v})]_{\varepsilon}}{P[\lambda_{i}(\bm{y},\bm{v})]_{\varepsilon}}}\right)\bm{W}^{H}(\bm{y},\bm{v}).

Then, substituting (
26
) into (
19
), we obtain the optimal value of
|g^{\varepsilon}_{i}(\bm{v},\bm{v})|
:

|g_{i}^{\varepsilon}(\bm{y},\bm{v})|=\sqrt{\frac{P}{(P-[\lambda_{i}(\bm{y},\bm{v})]_{\varepsilon})[\lambda_{i}(\bm{y},\bm{v})]_{\varepsilon}}}|\bm{w}_{i}^{H}(\bm{y},\bm{v})\bm{\mu}(\bm{y},\bm{v})|.

(27)

Finally, combining (
18
) and (
27
), we get the optimal form for
\bm{g}^{\varepsilon}(\bm{y},\bm{v})
:

\bm{g}^{\varepsilon}(\bm{y},\bm{v})=\mathrm{diag}\left(\sqrt{\frac{P}{(P-[\lambda_{i}(\bm{y},\bm{v})]_{\varepsilon})[\lambda_{i}(\bm{y},\bm{v})]_{\varepsilon}}}\right)\bm{W}^{H}(\bm{y},\bm{v})\bm{\mu}(\bm{y},\bm{v}),

which completes the proof.

∎

Remark 6
.

The optimal Vec-GNNDR can be interpreted as a two-stage procedure that combines principal component analysis (PCA) with GNNDR. In the first stage, PCA is applied to the conditional covariance matrix
\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{y},\bm{v})
to obtain eigenvectors
\{\bm{w}_{i}(\bm{y},\bm{v})\}_{i=1}^{B_{x}}
, ordered according to their associated eigenvalues. In the second stage, the input
\bm{x}
is projected onto each eigenspace spanned by
\bm{w}_{i}(\bm{y},\bm{v})
, and the GNNDR is then applied sequentially to the resulting projections
\bm{w}_{i}(\bm{y},\bm{v})^{H}\bm{x}
. When the conditional variance along the
i
-th eigen-direction, given by
\mathrm{Var}[\bm{X}\mid\bm{y},\bm{v}]=\lambda_{i}(\bm{y},\bm{v})
, exceeds
P
, the eigenvalue is first truncated to
[\lambda_{i}(\bm{y},\bm{v})]_{\varepsilon}
prior to applying GNNDR. Although
[\lambda_{i}(\bm{y},\bm{v})]_{\varepsilon}
is close to
P
, it cannot be directly set equal to
P
. Setting
\lambda_{i}(\bm{y},\bm{v})=P
would imply
\sigma_{i}(\bm{y},\bm{v})=0
, causing the scaling function
\bm{f}
to project
\bm{x}_{l}(m)
onto an affine subspace of inherent dimension less than
B_{x}
. Such projection would inevitably discard information and thereby reduce the GMI.

The complexity of the Vec-GNNDR characterized in Theorem
1
arises primarily from the truncation of the eigenvalues
\lambda_{i}(\bm{y},\bm{v})
whenever
\lambda_{i}(\bm{y},\bm{v})\geq P
. In the special case where all conditional eigenvalues are strictly less than
P
, both the optimal Vec-GNNDR and the corresponding GMI admit simplified closed-form expressions.

Assumption 3
.

For any channel output
\bm{y}
and receiver CSI
\bm{v}
obtained from the in-block memory channel (
1
),
the conditional covariance matrix satisfies
0\prec\operatorname{Cov}[\bm{X}\mid\bm{y},\bm{v}]\prec P
almost surely.

Under Assumption
3
, the optimal Vec-GNNDR given in Theorem
1
simplifies to the following result.

Theorem 2
.

Consider the in-block memory channel described in Section
II
, which satisfies Assumption
3
. Let
\bm{\mu}(\bm{y},\bm{v})
,
\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{y},\bm{v})
, and
\lambda_{i}(\bm{y},\bm{v})
be defined as in Theorem

. Then the processing and scaling functions of the optimal Vec-GNNDR are given by

\bm{g}(\bm{y},\bm{v})=\mathrm{diag}\left(\sqrt{\frac{P}{(P-\lambda_{i}(\bm{y},\bm{v}))\lambda_{i}(\bm{y},\bm{v})}}\right)\bm{W}^{H}(\bm{y},\bm{v})\bm{\mu}(\bm{y},\bm{v}),

(28)

\bm{f}(\bm{y},\bm{v})=\mathrm{diag}\left(\sqrt{\frac{P-\lambda_{i}(\bm{y},\bm{v})}{P\lambda_{i}(\bm{y},\bm{v})}}\right)\bm{W}^{H}(\bm{y},\bm{v}),

(29)

and the corresponding optimal GMI admits the closed form

I_{\mathrm{GMI,opt}}=\frac{1}{B_{x}}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{Y},\bm{V}}\left[\log\frac{P^{B_{x}}}{\det(\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{Y},\bm{V}))}\right].

Proof.

Under Assumption
3
, we have
\lambda_{i}(\bm{y},\bm{v})<P
for all indices
i
and for any

. Hence, no truncation is required, and
[\lambda_{i}(\bm{y},\bm{v})]_{\varepsilon}=\lambda_{i}(\bm{y},\bm{v})
. Substituting this into (
15
) and (
16
) given in Theorem

, we obtain the optimal processing and scaling functions as given in (
28
) and (
29
), respectively. For the corresponding optimal GMI, we have

\displaystyle I_{\mathrm{GMI,opt}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{Y},\bm{V}}\left[\frac{1}{B_{x}}\sum_{i=1}^{B_{x}}\left(\log\frac{P}{\lambda_{i}(\bm{Y},\bm{V})}-1+\frac{\lambda_{i}(\bm{Y},\bm{V})}{P}\right)+\frac{1}{B_{x}P}\|\bm{\mu}(\bm{Y},\bm{V})\|_{2}^{2}\right]

(30)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{Y},\bm{V}}\left[\frac{1}{B_{x}}\log\frac{P^{B_{x}}}{\det(\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{Y},\bm{V}))}+\frac{1}{B_{x}P}\mathrm{Tr}(\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{Y},\bm{V}))+\frac{1}{B_{x}P}\|\bm{\mu}(\bm{Y},\bm{V})\|_{2}^{2}\right]-1

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{Y},\bm{V}}\left[\frac{1}{B_{x}}\log\frac{P^{B_{x}}}{\det(\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{Y},\bm{V}))}+\frac{1}{B_{x}P}\mathrm{Tr}\left[\bm{X}\bm{X}^{H}\mid\bm{Y},\bm{V}\right]\right]-1

where the last equality follows from the law of total expectation and the fact that

∎

Remark 7
.

The derivation of the optimal GNNDR in
[

]
can be regarded as a special case with

III-C

Joint Codebook-Metric Design Perspective

In this subsection, we use the optimal Vec-GNNDR characterization to formulate a joint design perspective for the Gaussian codebook ensemble and the decoding metric. The full optimization over all positive semidefinite covariance matrices is generally nonconvex and analytically intractable. Our goal here is therefore more modest: we first show that, for any fixed Gaussian covariance matrix
\bm{\Sigma}
, the optimal Vec-GNNDR metric retains the same closed-form structure after whitening. This reduces the joint codebook-metric design to an optimization over the input covariance. We then derive first-order self-consistent optimality conditions for the tractable diagonal covariance family.

Corollary 1
.

Consider a Gaussian random codebook ensemble with codewords
\bm{x}_{l}(m)\sim\mathcal{CN}(\mathbf{0},P\bm{\Sigma})
, subject to the power constraint
\mathrm{Tr}(\bm{\Sigma})=B_{x}
. For transmission over the in-block memory channel described in Section
II
satisfying Assumption
2
, the optimal processing and scaling functions and the corresponding GMI retain the functional forms established in Theorem
1
, with the conditional mean vector and the conditional covariance matrix in (
11
) substituted by:

\hat{\bm{\mu}}(\bm{y},\bm{v})=\bm{\Sigma}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\bm{\mu}_{\mathrm{color}}(\bm{y},\bm{v}),\quad\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}(\bm{y},\bm{v})=\bm{\Sigma}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathrm{color}}(\bm{y},\bm{v})\bm{\Sigma}^{-\frac{1}{2}},

(32)

where
\bm{\mu}_{\mathrm{color}}(\bm{y},\bm{v})
and
\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathrm{color}}(\bm{y},\bm{v})
denote the conditional mean and conditional covariance, respectively, evaluated under the colored Gaussian codebook
\bm{X}_{\mathrm{color}}\sim\mathcal{CN}(\mathbf{0},P\bm{\Sigma})
, the jointly state-CSI distribution
P_{\bm{S},\bm{V}}
and channel transition law
P_{\bm{Y}\mid\bm{X},\bm{S}}
.

Proof.

As discussed in Remark
2
, the communication system can be transformed into an equivalent system employing a standard Gaussian random codebook
\hat{\bm{x}}_{l}(m)\sim\mathcal{CN}(0,P\bm{I}_{B_{x}})
via the whitening transformation
\hat{\bm{x}}_{l}(m)=\bm{\Sigma}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\bm{x}_{l}(m)
. The induced transition probability is given by
P_{\bm{Y}\mid\hat{\bm{X}},\bm{S}}(\bm{y}_{l}\mid\hat{\bm{x}}_{l},\bm{s}_{l})=P_{\bm{Y}\mid\bm{X},\bm{S}}(\bm{y}_{l}\mid\bm{\Sigma}^{\frac{1}{2}}\hat{\bm{x}}_{l},\bm{s}_{l})
.

Let
f_{\hat{\bm{X}}}(\hat{\bm{x}})=\frac{1}{\det(\pi P\bm{I}_{B_{x}})}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{P}\hat{\bm{x}}^{H}\hat{\bm{x}}\right)
denote the probability density function (PDF) of the whitened input. For a given channel output
\bm{y}
and receiver CSI
\bm{v}
, the effective likelihood function is
L(\bm{\Sigma}^{\frac{1}{2}}\hat{\bm{x}}\mid\bm{y},\bm{v})
, with

L(\bm{x}\mid\bm{y},\bm{v})=\int P_{\bm{Y}\mid\bm{X},\bm{S}}(\bm{y}\mid\bm{x},\bm{s})P_{\bm{S},\bm{V}}(\bm{s},\bm{v})\mathrm{d}\bm{s}.

By Bayes’ theorem, the conditional expectation of
\hat{\bm{X}}
given
(\bm{y},\bm{v})
is

\hat{\bm{\mu}}(\bm{y},\bm{v})=\frac{\int\hat{\bm{x}}f_{\hat{\bm{X}}}(\hat{\bm{x}})L(\bm{\Sigma}^{\frac{1}{2}}\hat{\bm{x}}\mid\bm{y},\bm{v})\mathrm{d}\hat{\bm{x}}}{\int f_{\hat{\bm{X}}}(\hat{\bm{x}})L(\bm{\Sigma}^{\frac{1}{2}}\hat{\bm{x}}\mid\bm{y},\bm{v})\mathrm{d}\hat{\bm{x}}}.

Applying the change of variables
\bm{u}=\bm{\Sigma}^{\frac{1}{2}}\hat{\bm{x}}
, the differential volume transforms as
\mathrm{d}\hat{\bm{x}}=\det(\bm{\Sigma})^{-1}\mathrm{d}\bm{u}
(the complex case). Then, we can rewrite
\hat{\bm{\mu}}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{v})
as

\hat{\bm{\mu}}(\bm{y},\bm{v})=\bm{\Sigma}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\frac{\int\bm{u}f_{\bm{X}_{\mathrm{color}}}(\bm{u})L(\bm{u}\mid\bm{y},\bm{v})\mathrm{d}\bm{u}}{\int f_{{\bm{X}}_{\mathrm{color}}}(\bm{u})L(\bm{u}\mid\bm{y},\bm{v})\mathrm{d}\bm{u}}=\bm{\Sigma}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\bm{\mu}_{\mathrm{color}}(\bm{y},\bm{v}),

where
f_{\bm{X}_{\mathrm{color}}}(\bm{u})=\frac{1}{\det(\pi P\bm{\Sigma})}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{P}\bm{u}^{H}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{u}\right)
is the density of the colored Gaussian distribution
\mathcal{CN}(0,P\bm{\Sigma})
. Similarly, utilizing the definition of the conditional covariance
\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}(\bm{y},\bm{v})=\mathbb{E}[\hat{\bm{X}}\hat{\bm{X}}^{H}\mid\bm{y},\bm{v}]-\hat{\bm{\mu}}\hat{\bm{\mu}}^{H}
and applying the identical change of variables yields:

\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}(\bm{y},\bm{v})=\bm{\Sigma}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\left(\frac{\int\bm{u}\bm{u}^{H}f_{\bm{X}_{\mathrm{color}}}(\bm{u})L(\bm{u}\mid\bm{y},\bm{v})\mathrm{d}\bm{u}}{\int f_{\bm{X}_{\mathrm{color}}}(\bm{u})L(\bm{u}\mid\bm{y},\bm{v})\mathrm{d}\bm{u}}-\bm{\mu}_{\mathrm{color}}\bm{\mu}_{\mathrm{color}}^{H}\right)\bm{\Sigma}^{-\frac{1}{2}}=\bm{\Sigma}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathrm{color}}(\bm{y},\bm{v})\bm{\Sigma}^{-\frac{1}{2}},

which concludes the proof.
∎

We now consider the covariance-domain problem induced by Corollary
1
. Since the optimization over the cone of positive semidefinite matrices subject to the trace constraint
\mathrm{Tr}(\bm{\Sigma})=B_{x}
is generally analytically intractable, we restrict our first-order analysis to the class of diagonal covariance matrices, i.e.,
\bm{\Sigma}=\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{B_{x}})
, subject to
\sum_{i=1}^{B_{x}}\lambda_{i}=B_{x}
and
\lambda_{i}\geq 0
. Such a structural constraint is also consistent with the power allocation problem in practical multi-carrier communication systems
[
44
,
43
]
.

Theorem 3
.

Consider a Gaussian random codebook with an input covariance matrix
\bm{\Sigma}=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{B_{x}})\succ 0
subject to the power constraint
\sum_{i=1}^{B_{x}}\lambda_{i}=B_{x}
.
For transmission over the in-block memory channel described in Section
II
satisfying Assumption
2
, any interior stationary point of the diagonal covariance optimization problem associated with the optimal Vec-GNNDR must satisfy the following system of self-consistent equations:

\displaystyle\lambda_{i}=\frac{1}{P(\nu-\Delta_{i}(\bm{\Sigma}))}\left(P+\mathbb{E}_{\bm{Y},\bm{V}}\left[\left[(\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}(\bm{Y},\bm{V})-P\bm{I})_{+}\right]_{ii}\right]\right),\quad i=1,\cdots,B_{x},

(33a)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{B_{x}}\frac{1}{P(\nu-\Delta_{i}(\bm{\Sigma}))}\left(P+\mathbb{E}_{\bm{Y},\bm{V}}\left[\left[(\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}(\bm{Y},\bm{V})-P\bm{I})_{+}\right]_{ii}\right]\right)=B_{x},

(33b)

where
\Delta_{i}
is defined as:

\Delta_{i}(\bm{\Sigma})=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{Y},\bm{V}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{\bm{X}\mid\bm{Y},\bm{V}}\left[q(\hat{\bm{X}}\mid\bm{Y},\bm{V})\tilde{S}_{i}(\bm{X}\mid\bm{Y},\bm{V})\right]+G(\hat{\bm{\Sigma}})\mathbb{E}_{\bm{X}\mid\bm{Y},\bm{V}}[S_{i}(\bm{X})]\right].

(34)

Here,
\hat{\bm{X}}=\bm{\Sigma}^{-1/2}\bm{X}
, while the score function
S_{i}(\bm{x})
and the centered score function
\tilde{S}_{i}(\bm{x}\mid\bm{y},\bm{v})
are given by

S_{i}(\bm{x})=\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda_{i}}\log\mathcal{CN}(\bm{x};0,\bm{\Sigma})=\frac{|x_{i}|^{2}}{\lambda_{i}^{2}}-\frac{1}{\lambda_{i}},\quad\tilde{S}_{i}(\bm{x}\mid\bm{y},\bm{v})=S_{i}(\bm{x})-\mathbb{E}_{\bm{X}\mid\bm{y},\bm{v}}[S_{i}(\bm{X})].

The auxiliary functions
G(\cdot)
and
q(\cdot\mid\cdot)
are defined respectively as

G(x)=-\log\left(\frac{x}{P}\right)+\begin{cases}0&0\leq x<P,\\
\frac{x}{P}-\log\left(\frac{x}{P}\right)-1,&x\geq P,\end{cases}

(35)

q(\hat{\bm{x}}\mid\bm{y},\bm{v})=(\hat{\bm{X}}-\hat{\bm{\mu}}(\bm{y},\bm{v}))^{H}G^{\prime}(\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}(\bm{y},\bm{v}))(\hat{\bm{X}}-\hat{\bm{\mu}}(\bm{y},\bm{v})),

where
\hat{\bm{\mu}}(\bm{y},\bm{v})
and
\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}(\bm{y},\bm{v})
are specified in (
32
).

Proof.

Based on the GMI expression in (
31
) and Corollary
1
, the diagonal covariance subproblem can be formulated as the following constrained optimization problem:

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{\Sigma}}

\displaystyle J(\bm{\Sigma})=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{Y},\bm{V}}\left[\operatorname{Tr}\left(G(\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}(\bm{Y},\bm{V}))\right)\right]

(36)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle\bm{\Sigma}=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{B_{x}})\succ 0,\quad\sum_{i=1}^{B_{x}}\lambda_{i}=B_{x},

where
G(\cdot)
is defined in (
35
) and
\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}(\bm{y},\bm{v})
is given in (
32
). To derive first-order optimality conditions for (
36
), we introduce the following Lagrangian function

L(\bm{\Sigma},\nu)=J(\bm{\Sigma})-\nu\left(\sum_{i=1}^{B_{x}}\lambda_{i}-B_{x}\right),

where
\nu
is the Lagrange multiplier associated with the power constraint. By applying the chain rule for derivatives, we obtain

\frac{\partial L}{\partial\lambda_{i}}=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{Y},\bm{V}}\left[\mathrm{Tr}\left(G^{\prime}(\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}(\bm{Y},\bm{V}))\frac{\partial\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}(\bm{Y},\bm{V})}{\partial\lambda_{i}}\right)+G(\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}(\bm{Y},\bm{V}))\frac{\partial\log P(\bm{Y},\bm{V})}{\partial\lambda_{i}}\right]-\nu.

(37)

Applying the product rule to
\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}(\bm{y},\bm{v})=\bm{\Sigma}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathrm{color}}(\bm{y},\bm{v})\bm{\Sigma}^{-\frac{1}{2}}
, the partial derivative of
\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}(\bm{y},\bm{v})
with respect to
\lambda_{i}
is given by

\frac{\partial\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}(\bm{y},\bm{v})}{\partial\lambda_{i}}=-\frac{1}{2\lambda_{i}}(\bm{E}_{ii}\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}(\bm{y},\bm{v})+\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}(\bm{y},\bm{v})\bm{E}_{ii})+\bm{\Sigma}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\frac{\partial\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathrm{color}}(\bm{y},\bm{v})}{\partial\lambda_{i}}\bm{\Sigma}^{-\frac{1}{2}},

(38)

where
\bm{E}_{ii}
denotes the elementary matrix with
1
at index
(i,i)
and
0
elsewhere. Here, the derivative of the colored covariance matrix is given by

\frac{\partial\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathrm{color}}(\bm{y},\bm{v})}{\partial\lambda_{i}}=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{X}\mid\bm{y},\bm{v}}\left[(\bm{X}-\bm{\mu}_{\mathrm{color}}(\bm{y},\bm{v}))(\bm{X}-\bm{\mu}_{\mathrm{color}}(\bm{y},\bm{v}))^{H}\tilde{S}_{i}(\bm{X}\mid\bm{y},\bm{v})\right],

(39)

the detailed derivation of which is provided in Appendix
B
. Furthermore, the derivative of the log-marginal likelihood can be calculated as

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\log P(\bm{y},\bm{v})}{\partial\lambda_{i}}=\frac{1}{P(\bm{y},\bm{v})}\int P(\bm{y},\bm{v}\mid\bm{x})\frac{\partial P(\bm{x};\bm{\Sigma})}{\partial\lambda_{i}}\mathrm{d}\bm{x}=\int\frac{P(\bm{y},\bm{v}\mid\bm{x})P(\bm{x};\bm{\Sigma})}{P(\bm{y},\bm{v})}S_{i}(\bm{x})\mathrm{d}\bm{x}=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{X}\mid\bm{y},\bm{v}}[S_{i}(\bm{X})].

(40)

Given the identity
xG^{\prime}(x)=-\frac{1}{P}(P+(x-P)_{+})
, it follows that
\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}(\bm{y},\bm{v})G^{\prime}(\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}(\bm{y},\bm{v}))=-\frac{1}{P}(P\bm{I}+(\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}(\bm{y},\bm{v})-P\bm{I})_{+})
. Substituting this into the trace term yields

\mathrm{Tr}\left(G^{\prime}(\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}(\bm{y},\bm{v}))\left[-\frac{1}{2\lambda_{i}}(\bm{E}_{ii}\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}(\bm{y},\bm{v})+\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}(\bm{y},\bm{v})\bm{E}_{ii})\right]\right)=-\frac{1}{\lambda_{i}}[\hat{\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{y},\bm{v})}G^{\prime}(\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}(\bm{y},\bm{v}))]_{ii}=\frac{1}{P\lambda_{i}}\left(P+[(\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}(\bm{y},\bm{v})-P\bm{I})_{+}]_{ii}\right).

(41)

Substituting (
38
)—(
41
) into (
37
), we get the partial derivative of
L
with respect to
\lambda_{i}

\frac{\partial L}{\partial\lambda_{i}}=\frac{1}{P\lambda_{i}}\left(P+\mathbb{E}_{\bm{Y},\bm{V}}\left[[(\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}-P\bm{I})_{+}]_{ii}\right]\right)+\Delta_{i}(\bm{\Sigma})-\nu,

where
\Delta_{i}
is given in (
34
). Solving for
\frac{\partial L}{\partial\lambda_{i}}=0
leads to the self-consistent equations (
33a
), and the enforcement of the power constraint yields (
33b
). This completes the proof.
∎

The first-order conditions above exhibit a structural affinity with the classical water-filling algorithm
[
13
,
6
]
. Specifically, (
33a
) reveals a thresholding mechanism: additional power is assigned according to the whitened covariance
\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}
relative to the reference level
P
, together with the correction term
\Delta_{i}(\bm{\Sigma})
induced by the dependence of the posterior distribution on the codebook covariance. This water-filling-like structure suggests iterative numerical schemes, although convergence and global optimality are not asserted here.

The main role of this subsection is to clarify how codebook design and metric design can be placed in a single GMI-based framework. The inner problem over Vec-GNNDR metrics is solved in closed form for each fixed Gaussian covariance, while the outer covariance problem remains difficult in general. The diagonal result above should therefore be interpreted as a first-order characterization for a tractable subfamily rather than a complete solution of the full joint optimization problem. Extending this framework to full covariance matrices and to more general codebook ensembles
[
30
]
is left for future work.

\displaystyle P_{Y\mid X,S}\left(y\mid x,s\right)

Since
B_{x}=1

instead of the block index

, and the processing and scaling functions
g
and

The following corollary characterizes the optimal
(g,f)
pair and the corresponding

(Memoryless channel)
.

Consider the memoryless channel specified by the conditional distributions in (

\ [w(y,v)]_{\varepsilon}=w(y,v)\mathbb{I}\{w(y,v)<P\}+\frac{P}{P+\delta_{\varepsilon}(y,v)}\mathbb{I}\{w(y,v)\geq P\},

For the GNNDR employing the processing function

See Appendix

.

.

Consider the ACGNC given in (

and scaling function
\bm{f}
are respectively given by

\bm{f}(\bm{y},\bm{v})=\bm{\Lambda}\bm{W}^{H},\quad\bm{g}(\bm{y},\bm{v})=\bm{U}^{H}\bm{\Sigma}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\bm{y},

where
\bm{U},\bm{\Lambda},\bm{W}
are given by the singular value decomposition of
\bm{M}=\bm{\Sigma}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\bm{A}
as
\bm{M}=\bm{U}\bm{\Lambda}\bm{W}^{H}
with
\bm{U}\in\mathbb{U}(B_{y}),\bm{V}\in\mathbb{U}(B_{x})
, and
\bm{\Lambda}=\operatorname{diag}(\sigma_{1},\cdots,\sigma_{B_{x}})
satisfying
\sigma_{1}\geq\sigma_{2}\geq\cdots\geq\sigma_{B_{x}}\geq 0
. The corresponding optimal GMI is given by

I_{\text{GMI},\bm{g},\bm{f}}=B_{x}^{-1}\log\left[\det(P\bm{A}^{H}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{A}+\bm{I}_{B_{x}})\right].

Proof.

See Appendix
C
.
∎

Remark 8
.

With the optimal
\bm{g}(\bm{y},\bm{v})
and
\bm{f}(\bm{y},\bm{v})
given in Corollary
3
, the decoding rule recovers the ML decoder. Specifically,

\displaystyle\|\bm{g}(\bm{y},\bm{v})-\bm{f}(\bm{y},\bm{v})\bm{x}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|\bm{U}^{H}\bm{\Sigma}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\bm{y}-\bm{\Lambda}\bm{W}^{H}\bm{x}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|\bm{\Sigma}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\bm{y}-\bm{U}\bm{\Lambda}\bm{W}^{H}\bm{x}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|\bm{\Sigma}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\bm{y}-\bm{\Sigma}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\bm{A}\bm{x}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=(\bm{y}-\bm{A}\bm{x})^{H}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}(\bm{y}-\bm{A}\bm{x}).

Remark 9
.

If
\bm{\Sigma}=N_{0}\bm{I}_{B_{x}}
, then

I_{\mathrm{GMI,opt}}=B_{x}^{-1}\log[\det(\bm{I}_{B_{x}}+P\bm{A}^{H}\bm{A})],

which coincides with the Shannon capacity
for Gaussian channels in
[
41
, Chapter 8]
. In this case,
\bm{\Lambda}
and
\bm{W}
are the singular value matrix and the right singular matrix of
\bm{A}
, respectively, matching the structure of the optimal capacity-achieving decoder.

IV
Vec-GNNDR with Restricted Forms

In this section, we investigate several constrained variants of Vec-GNNDR in which the processing and scaling functions take restrictive forms. While such suboptimal Vec-GNNDRs may result in a reduced GMI, they simultaneously incur less computational complexity and provide valuable insights into the structural properties and theoretical understanding of Vec-GNNDR.

IV-A

Constant Scalar Scaling Function

In this scenario, the Vec-GNNDR adopts the following formulation:

\hat{m}=\arg\min_{m\in\mathcal{M}}\sum_{l=1}^{L}\left\|\bm{g}(\bm{y}_{l},\bm{v}_{l})-\alpha\bm{x}_{l}(m)\right\|^{2}_{2},

(45)

where
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}
is an adjustable scalar. This implies that the scaling function is given by
\bm{f}(\bm{y},\bm{v})=\alpha\bm{I}_{B_{x}}

\bm{g}(\bm{y},\bm{v})
, aims to maximize the GMI. The following proposition establishes the optimal processing and scaling functions, along with the corresponding maximized GMI under the decoding rule (
45

(Constant scalar scaling function)

IV-E

Linear Processing Function

Within this formalism, we simplify the processing function by restricting it to be linear in the channel output
\bm{y}

\bm{g}(\bm{y},\bm{v})=\bm{\Gamma}^{H}(\bm{v})\bm{y}

Under these restrictions, the Vec-GNNDR takes the form

\hat{m}=\arg\min_{m\in\mathcal{M}}\sum_{l=1}^{L}\|\bm{\Gamma}^{H}(\bm{v}_{l})\bm{y}-\bm{\Pi}(\bm{v}_{l})\bm{x}_{l}(m)\|^{2}_{2}.

(50)

.

(51)

(53)

Now we provide a heuristic justification for Vec-GNNDR with a linear processing function by employing the generalized Bussgang decomposition
[
4
,
46
,
9
]
. For a given CSI realization
\bm{v}
, following the Bussgang formalism, we decompose the channel output
\bm{Y}
as

\bm{Y}=\underbrace{\frac{\mathbb{E}[\bm{Y}\bm{X}^{H}\mid\bm{v}]}{P}\bm{X}}_{\text{Signal:\;}\bm{A}(\bm{v})\bm{X}}+\underbrace{\bm{Y}-\frac{\mathbb{E}[\bm{Y}\bm{X}^{H}\mid\bm{v}]}{P}\bm{X}}_{\mathrm{Noise:\;}\bm{n}(\bm{v})},

(55)

where
\bm{A}(\bm{v})
denotes the effective linear operator associated with
\bm{v}
, and
\bm{n}(\bm{v})
represents the residual noise.
This decomposition ensures that the signal and residual noise components are conditionally uncorrelated given
\bm{v}
, i.e.,

\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{n}^{H}(\bm{v})\bm{A}(\bm{v})\bm{X}\mid\bm{v}\right]=0.

Furthermore, the conditional correlation matrix of
\bm{n}(\bm{v})
is expressed as

W(\bm{v})=\mathbb{E}[\bm{n}(\bm{v})\bm{n}^{H}(\bm{v})\mid\bm{v}]=\mathbb{E}[\bm{Y}\bm{Y}^{H}\mid\bm{v}]-\frac{1}{P}\mathbb{E}[\bm{Y}\bm{X}^{H}\mid\bm{v}]\mathbb{E}[\bm{X}\bm{Y}^{H}\mid\bm{v}].

By invoking the
worst-case uncorrelated additive noise
theorem
[
15
,
39
]
, the capacity for channel (
55
) under the worst-case scenario is attained when the residual noise follows a circularly symmetric complex Gaussian distribution, i.e.,
\bm{n}(\bm{v})\sim\mathcal{CN}(0,W(\bm{v}))
. Then according to
[
15
,
39
]
, the corresponding worst-case rate for a given
\bm{v}
is

\displaystyle R(\bm{v})

\displaystyle=B_{x}^{-1}\log\left[\det(P\bm{A}^{H}(\bm{v})\bm{W}^{\dagger}(\bm{v})\bm{A}(\bm{v})+\bm{I}_{B_{x}})\right]

(56)

\displaystyle=B_{x}^{-1}\log\left[\det\left(\frac{1}{P}\mathbb{E}[\bm{X}\bm{Y}^{H}\mid\bm{v}]\left(\mathbb{E}[\bm{Y}\bm{Y}^{H}\mid\bm{v}]-\frac{1}{P}\mathbb{E}[\bm{Y}\bm{X}^{H}\mid\bm{v}]\mathbb{E}[\bm{X}\bm{Y}^{H}\mid\bm{v}]\right)^{\dagger}\mathbb{E}[\bm{Y}\bm{X}^{H}\mid\bm{v}]+\bm{I}_{B_{x}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=B_{x}^{-1}\log\frac{P^{B_{x}}}{\det\left(P\bm{I}_{B_{x}}-\mathbb{E}[\bm{X}\bm{Y}^{H}\mid\bm{v}]\left(\mathbb{E}[\bm{Y}\bm{Y}^{H}\mid\bm{v}]\right)^{\dagger}\mathbb{E}[\bm{Y}\bm{X}^{H}\mid\bm{v}]\right)},

where the last step follows from the Sherman–Morrison–Woodbury identity (Lemma
11
) to compute the inverse of
\bm{W}(\bm{v})
. Comparing (
56
) with the GMI expression in (
52
), we obtain the relationship

I_{\mathrm{GMI,lin}}=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{V}}[R(\bm{V})]=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{V}}\left[B_{x}^{-1}\log\left(\det\left(\bm{I}_{B_{x}}+\bm{A}^{H}(\bm{V})\bm{W}^{\dagger}(\bm{V})\bm{A}(\bm{V})\right)\right)\right].

This connection enables the following heuristic interpretation: the Vec-GNNDR with a linear processing function first observes the CSI
\bm{v}
and applies the Bussgang decomposition, which effectively absorbs the effect of (possibly noisy) nonlinearities into a residual noise term that is uncorrelated with the channel input. The resulting residual noise then governs the worst-case achievable rate, thereby guaranteeing the achievable rate of
I_{\mathrm{GMI,lin}}
.

For the special case
B_{x}=1
, we have

I_{\mathrm{GMI,lin}}=\mathbb{E}_{V}\left[\log\frac{P}{P-\mathbb{E}[XY^{H}\mid V](\mathbb{E}[YY^{H}\mid V])^{\dagger}\mathbb{E}[YX^{H}\mid V]}\right]

and

\Gamma(v)=\sqrt{\frac{P}{S(v)}}\left(\mathbb{E}[YY^{H}\mid v]\right)^{\dagger}\mathbb{E}[YX^{H}\mid v],\qquad\Pi(v)=\sqrt{\frac{P}{S(v)}}\frac{1}{P}\mathbb{E}[XY^{H}\mid V](\mathbb{E}[YY^{H}\mid V])^{\dagger}\mathbb{E}[YX^{H}\mid V],

where
S(v)
is given by

S(v)=\left[\mathbb{E}[XY^{H}\mid V](\mathbb{E}[YY^{H}\mid V])^{\dagger}\mathbb{E}[YX^{H}\mid V]\left(P-\mathbb{E}[XY^{H}\mid V](\mathbb{E}[YY^{H}\mid V])^{\dagger}\mathbb{E}[YX^{H}\mid V]\right)\right]^{-1}.

This expression coincides with the result reported in
[
42
, Proposition 4]
.

IV-F

Comparison of GMI under Restricted Vec-GNNDR Schemes

In this subsection, we present a comparative analysis of the GMI achieved by the five restricted Vec-GNNDR schemes introduced in this section. By examining the relative dimensions of the optimization domains associated with the processing functions
\bm{g}
and the scaling functions
\bm{f}
, one readily recognizes the following ordering:

I_{\mathrm{GMI,opt}}\geq I_{\mathrm{GMI,csi-msf}}\geq\max\{I_{\mathrm{GMI,csi-ssf}},I_{\mathrm{GMI,cmsf}}\}\geq I_{\mathrm{GMI,cssf}},

Moreover, for the Vec-GNNDR with a linear processing function, we have

I_{\mathrm{GMI,lin}}\leq I_{\mathrm{GMI,csi-msf}}.

This example also illustrates why the block-level formulation is not merely a notational vectorization of symbol-wise GNNDR. Although the IBM channel can be viewed as a vector-valued memoryless channel across blocks, the restriction to element-wise metrics destroys the shared phase information inside each block. The Vec-GNNDR metric, by contrast, adapts its eigendirections to the posterior covariance and separates the dominant phase-uncertainty direction from the remaining orthogonal directions.
By comparing the optimal GMI associated with Vec-GNNDR and element-wise GNNDR, we observe that Vec-GNNDR effectively exploits the structural property that all elements within a block are rotated by the same unknown phase
\theta

In contrast, element-wise GNNDR does not exploit the structure information. Instead, it treats each element independently and attempts to estimate a distinct phase for each one, thereby ignoring the same rotational phase among elements within a block. This results in a greater rate penalty, with a GMI as

VI
Conclusion and further work

The GNNDR offers a simple yet effective approach to enhancing achievable rates in mismatched decoding while retaining the structural simplicity of nearest-neighbor (NN) decoding, thereby ensuring practical implementability. However, it is inherently limited to memoryless channels. We propose the Vec-GNNDR formalism to generalize GNNDR to IBM channels, a class of channels exhibiting structured memory within each block. The contribution is therefore not the IBM abstraction alone, but the identification of a GMI-optimal block-level metric that exploits intra-block statistical structure unavailable to element-wise GNNDR. The resulting optimal Vec-GNNDR admits a clear signal-processing interpretation: it performs GNNDR along the principal directions of the channel, thereby preserving as much information as possible. In addition, the closed-form metric optimization for each fixed Gaussian covariance naturally leads to a GMI-based codebook-metric design framework; within the diagonal covariance family, we provide first-order self-consistent conditions for stationary points. Numerical results demonstrate significant rate gains over conventional receiver designs, highlighting the strong potential of Vec-GNNDR for practical deployment in realistic communication scenarios.

There are several promising directions for future research. First, it would be of interest to extend the Vec-GNNDR framework to general memory channels, where memory spans across entire codewords rather than being confined within individual blocks. We conjecture that the GMI in this setting remains characterized by (
10
) by interpreting the entire codeword sequence as a single block, so that the GMI maximization follows the same form as in Theorem
1
. However, it remains an open question to identify the classes of channels with specific memory structures for which the probability convergence analysis in Proposition
1
continues to hold. Second, in the present analysis, the dimension of the output after the processing function is required to be greater than that of the original received signal (i.e.,
B_{x}\geq B
). However, in practical implementations, it may be desirable to compress the transformed output dimension (i.e.,
B_{x}<B
) to facilitate faster decoding, albeit at the cost of potential information loss. An interesting direction for future work is to characterize the optimal scaling and processing functions, as well as the corresponding GMI, under this reduced-dimension setting. Third, the codebook-metric framework developed here leaves the full covariance optimization problem open; establishing global optimality conditions, convergent algorithms, or useful sufficient conditions beyond the diagonal covariance family would substantially strengthen the framework. Finally, while the present analysis is predicated on Gaussian codebook ensembles, practical communication systems typically utilize discrete constellations. Accordingly, extending the Vec-GNNDR formalism to accommodate finite constellations—and investigating the joint optimization of the constellation geometry and decoding metrics therein—constitutes an important direction for subsequent research.

From a practical standpoint, several implementation challenges remain for the Vec-GNNDR. The computation of the optimal scaling and processing functions requires evaluating the conditional mean and the conditional covariance matrix. For general channel models, obtaining closed-form expressions for these quantities is often intractable. Although Monte Carlo approximations can be employed, they typically require a large number of samples drawn from the posterior distribution, thereby motivating the development of more efficient sampling algorithms
[
26
]
. For scenarios with lower precision requirements, an alternative practical approach is to employ machine learning techniques to train a neural network that learns the mapping from the received signals and CSIs to the conditional mean and conditional covariance; see, for example,
[
30
]
.

Lemma 7

(Convexity of

Lemma 9

(Maximum eigenvalue inequality)
.

Let
\bm{A}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
be a Hermitian matrix and
\bm{B}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}

\lambda_{\max}(\bm{A}-{\bm{B}})\leq\lambda_{\max}(\bm{A}).

\lambda_{\max}(\bm{A})\geq\bm{w}^{H}\bm{A}\bm{w}=\bm{w}^{H}(\bm{A}-\bm{B})\bm{w}+\bm{w}^{H}\bm{B}\bm{w}=\lambda_{\max}(\bm{A}-\bm{B})+\bm{w}^{H}\bm{B}\bm{w}.

which completes the proof.

\bm{X}\perp\bm{V}
, we have

\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{X}\bm{X}\mid\bm{v}}=\mathbb{E}[\bm{X}\bm{X}^{H}\mid\bm{v}]=\mathbb{E}[\bm{X}\bm{X}^{H}]=P\bm{I}_{B_{x}}.

For any
\bm{u}\in\mathbb{C}^{B_{x}}
and
\bm{c}\in\mathbb{C}^{B_{y}}
, it holds that

\mathbb{E}\left[|\bm{u}^{H}\bm{X}-\bm{c}^{H}\bm{Y}|^{2}\mid\bm{v}\right]\geq 0,

(66)

with a fixed
\bm{v}
.
Expanding the quadratic form and taking conditional expectation yields

\bm{u}^{H}\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{X}\bm{X}\mid\bm{v}}\bm{u}-2\Re\left\{\bm{u}^{H}\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{X}\bm{Y}\mid\bm{v}}\bm{c}\right\}+\bm{c}^{H}\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{Y}\bm{Y}\mid\bm{v}}\bm{c}\geq 0,

(67)

Minimizing the left-hand side with respect to
\bm{c}
, we obtain

\bm{u}^{H}\left(\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{X}\bm{X}\mid\bm{v}}-\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{X}\bm{Y}\mid\bm{v}}\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{Y}\bm{Y}\mid\bm{v}}^{\dagger}\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{X}\bm{Y}\mid\bm{v}}^{H}\right)\bm{u}\geq 0,

for all
\bm{u}\in\mathbb{C}^{B_{x}}
. Hence,

\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{X}\bm{Y}\mid\bm{v}}\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{Y}\bm{Y}\mid\bm{v}}^{\dagger}\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{X}\bm{Y}\mid\bm{v}}^{H}\preccurlyeq\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{X}\bm{X}\mid\bm{v}}=P\bm{I}_{B_{x}},

which immediately implies

\lambda_{\max}\left(\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{X}\bm{Y}\mid\bm{v}}\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{Y}\bm{Y}\mid\bm{v}}^{\dagger}\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{X}\bm{Y}\mid\bm{v}}^{H}\right)\leq P.

Next, we establish that if
\mathrm{Cov}[\bm{X}\mid\bm{y},\bm{v}]\succ 0
, then the inequality in (
66
) holds strictly for any
\bm{u}\in\mathbb{C}^{B_{x}}\backslash{\bm{0}}
and
\bm{c}\in\mathbb{C}^{B_{y}}
. We proceed by contradiction. Suppose that there exist
\bm{u}\in\mathbb{C}^{B_{x}}\backslash{\bm{0}}
and
\bm{c}\in\mathbb{C}^{B_{y}}
such that

\mathbb{E}\left[|\bm{u}^{H}\bm{X}-\bm{c}^{H}\bm{Y}|^{2}\mid\bm{v}\right]=0,

Then it must follow that

\mathrm{Var}[\bm{u}^{H}\bm{X}-\bm{c}^{H}\bm{Y}\mid\bm{v}]=0.

Consequently, there exists
\bm{y}
such that

\mathrm{Var}[\bm{u}^{H}\bm{X}-\bm{c}^{H}\bm{Y}\mid\bm{y},\bm{v}]=0,

which implies

\bm{u}^{H}\mathrm{Cov}[\bm{X}\mid\bm{y},\bm{v}]\bm{u}=\mathrm{Var}[\bm{u}^{H}\bm{X}\mid\bm{y},\bm{v}]=\mathrm{Var}[\bm{u}^{H}\bm{X}-\bm{c}^{H}\bm{Y}\mid\bm{y},\bm{v}]=0,

This contradicts the assumption
\mathrm{Cov}[\bm{X}\mid\bm{y},\bm{v}]\succ 0
. Hence, the inequality in (
66
) is strict for all
\bm{u}\in\mathbb{C}^{B_{x}}\backslash{\bm{0}}
. It follows that the inequality in (
67
) is also strict for all
\bm{u}\in\mathbb{C}^{B_{x}}\backslash{\bm{0}}
, and therefore

\lambda_{\max}\left(\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{X}\bm{Y}\mid\bm{v}}\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{Y}\bm{Y}\mid\bm{v}}^{\dagger}\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{X}\bm{Y}\mid\bm{v}}^{H}\right)<P,

which completes the proof.
∎

Appendix B
Codebook Optimization

In this section, we evaluate the partial derivative of the colored covariance matrix
\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathrm{color}}(\bm{y},\bm{v})
with respect to the
i
-th power allocation parameter
\lambda_{i}
.

Let
p(\bm{x};\bm{\Sigma})
denote the probability density function of
\bm{X}\sim\mathcal{CN}(0,P\bm{Sigma})
. Then by Bayes Theorem, given
\bm{y}
and
\bm{v}
, the posterior density is given by

p(\bm{x}\mid\bm{y},\bm{v};\bm{\Sigma})=\frac{p(\bm{y},\bm{v}\mid\bm{x})p(\bm{x};\bm{\Sigma})}{p(\bm{y},\bm{v};\bm{\Sigma})},

where the marginal distribution is
p(\bm{y},\bm{v};\bm{\Sigma})=\int p(\bm{y},\bm{v}\mid\bm{x})p(\bm{x};\bm{\Sigma})\mathrm{d}\bm{x}
. Recalling the definition of score function with
\bm{\Sigma}=(\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{B_{x}})

S_{i}(\bm{x})=\frac{\partial\log p(\bm{x};\bm{\Sigma})}{\partial\lambda_{i}},

this implies
\frac{\partial p(\bm{x};\bm{\Sigma})}{\partial\lambda_{i}}=p(\bm{x};\bm{\Sigma})S_{i}(\bm{x})
. Then differentiating the marginal distribution yields:

\frac{\partial p(\bm{y},\bm{v};\bm{\Sigma})}{\partial\lambda_{i}}=\int p(\bm{y},\bm{v}\mid\bm{x})p(\bm{x};\bm{\Sigma})S_{i}(\bm{x})\mathrm{d}\bm{x}=p(\bm{y},\bm{v};\bm{\Sigma})\mathbb{E}_{\bm{X}\mid\bm{y},\bm{v}}[S_{i}(\bm{X})].

Applying the quotient rule to the posterior density, we obtain:

\frac{\partial p(\bm{x}\mid\bm{y},\bm{v};\bm{\Sigma})}{\partial\lambda_{i}}=\frac{p(\bm{y},\bm{v}\mid\bm{x})\frac{\partial p(\bm{x};\bm{\Sigma})}{\partial\lambda_{i}}p(\bm{y},\bm{v};\bm{\Sigma})-p(\bm{y},\bm{v}\mid\bm{x})p(\bm{x};\bm{\Sigma})\frac{\partial p(\bm{y},\bm{v};\bm{\Sigma})}{\partial\lambda_{i}}}{p(\bm{y},\bm{v};\bm{\Sigma})^{2}}.

Substituting the derivatives and factoring out the posterior density
p(\bm{x}\mid\bm{y},\bm{v};\bm{\Sigma})
gives:

\frac{\partial p(\bm{x}\mid\bm{y},\bm{v};\bm{\Sigma})}{\partial\lambda_{i}}=p(\bm{x}\mid\bm{y},\bm{v};\bm{\Sigma})\left(S_{i}(\bm{x})-\mathbb{E}_{\bm{X}\mid\bm{y},\bm{v}}[S_{i}(\bm{X})]\right)=p(\bm{x}\mid\bm{y},\bm{v};\bm{\Sigma})\tilde{S}_{i}(\bm{x}\mid\bm{y},\bm{v}),

where
\tilde{S}_{i}(\bm{x}\mid\bm{y},\bm{v})
is the centered score function, which satisfies
\mathbb{E}_{\bm{X}\mid\bm{y},\bm{v}}[\tilde{S}_{i}(\bm{X}\mid\bm{y},\bm{v})]=0
.

Next, we differentiate the conditional mean
\bm{\mu}_{\mathrm{color}}(\bm{y},\bm{v})=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{X}\mid\bm{y},\bm{v}}[\bm{X}]
. By passing the derivative inside the integral, we have:

\frac{\partial\bm{\mu}_{\mathrm{color}}(\bm{y},\bm{v})}{\partial\lambda_{i}}=\int\bm{x}\frac{\partial p(\bm{x}\mid\bm{y},\bm{v};\bm{\Sigma})}{\partial\lambda_{i}}\mathrm{d}\bm{x}=\int\bm{x}p(\bm{x}\mid\bm{y},\bm{v};\bm{\Sigma})\tilde{S}_{i}(\bm{x}\mid\bm{y},\bm{v})\mathrm{d}\bm{x}=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{X}\mid\bm{y},\bm{v}}[\bm{X}\tilde{S}_{i}(\bm{X}\mid\bm{y},\bm{v})].

Because
\mathbb{E}_{\bm{X}\mid\bm{y},\bm{v}}[\tilde{S}_{i}(\bm{X}\mid\bm{y},\bm{v})]=0
, we can subtract
\bm{\mu}_{\mathrm{color}}(\bm{y},\bm{v})\mathbb{E}_{\bm{X}\mid\bm{y},\bm{v}}[\tilde{S}_{i}(\bm{X}\mid\bm{y},\bm{v})]=0
to write the derivative strictly as a conditional covariance between the state vector and the scalar score:

\frac{\partial\bm{\mu}_{\mathrm{color}}(\bm{y},\bm{v})}{\partial\lambda_{i}}=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{X}\mid\bm{y},\bm{v}}[(\bm{X}-\bm{\mu}_{\mathrm{color}}(\bm{y},\bm{v}))\tilde{S}_{i}(\bm{X}\mid\bm{y},\bm{v})].

Finally, we evaluate the derivative of the conditional covariance matrix
\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathrm{color}}(\bm{y},\bm{v})=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{X}\mid\bm{y},\bm{v}}[\bm{X}\bm{X}^{H}]-\bm{\mu}_{\mathrm{color}}(\bm{y},\bm{v})\bm{\mu}_{\mathrm{color}}^{H}(\bm{y},\bm{v})
. Applying the product rule to the second moment and the outer product of the mean yields:

\frac{\partial\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathrm{color}}}{\partial\lambda_{i}}=\int\bm{x}\bm{x}^{H}\frac{\partial p(\bm{x}\mid\bm{y},\bm{v};\bm{\Sigma})}{\partial\lambda_{i}}\mathrm{d}\bm{x}-\frac{\partial\bm{\mu}_{\mathrm{color}}(\bm{y},\bm{v})}{\partial\lambda_{i}}\bm{\mu}_{\mathrm{color}}^{H}(\bm{y},\bm{v})-\bm{\mu}_{\mathrm{color}}(\bm{y},\bm{v})\frac{\partial\bm{\mu}_{\mathrm{color}}^{H}(\bm{y},\bm{v})}{\partial\lambda_{i}}.

Substituting the identity for the posterior density derivative into the first term provides the third-order moment
\mathbb{E}_{\bm{X}\mid\bm{y},\bm{v}}[\bm{X}\bm{X}^{H}\tilde{S}_{i}(\bm{X}\mid\bm{y},\bm{v})]
. Substituting the derived expression for
\frac{\partial\bm{\mu}_{\mathrm{color}}(\bm{y},\bm{v})}{\partial\lambda_{i}}
into the remaining terms, we obtain:

\frac{\partial\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathrm{color}}(\bm{y},\bm{v})}{\partial\lambda_{i}}=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{X}\mid\bm{y},\bm{v}}[\bm{X}\bm{x}^{H}\tilde{S}_{i}(\bm{X}\mid\bm{y},\bm{v})]-\mathbb{E}_{\bm{X}\mid\bm{y},\bm{v}}[\bm{X}\tilde{S}_{i}(\bm{X}\mid\bm{y},\bm{v})]\bm{\mu}_{\mathrm{color}}^{H}(\bm{y},\bm{v})-\bm{\mu}_{\mathrm{color}}(\bm{y},\bm{v})\mathbb{E}_{\bm{X}\mid\bm{y},\bm{v}}[\bm{X}^{H}\tilde{S}_{i}(\bm{X}\mid\bm{y},\bm{v})].

To construct a complete square, we add the strictly zero matrix
\bm{\mu}_{\mathrm{color}}(\bm{y},\bm{v})\bm{\mu}_{\mathrm{color}}^{H}(\bm{y},\bm{v})\mathbb{E}_{\bm{X}\mid\bm{y},\bm{v}}[\tilde{S}_{i}(\bm{X}\mid\bm{y},\bm{v})]=0
. The expansion algebraically collapses into the expected value of a single quadratic form:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathrm{color}}(\bm{y},\bm{v})}{\partial\lambda_{i}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{X}\mid\bm{y},\bm{v}}\left[\left(\bm{X}\bm{X}^{H}-\bm{X}\bm{\mu}_{\mathrm{color}}^{H}(\bm{y},\bm{v})-\bm{\mu}_{\mathrm{color}}(\bm{y},\bm{v})\bm{X}^{H}+\bm{\mu}_{\mathrm{color}}(\bm{y},\bm{v})\bm{\mu}_{\mathrm{color}}^{H}(\bm{y},\bm{v})\right)\tilde{S}_{i}(\bm{X}\mid\bm{y},\bm{v})\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{X}\mid\bm{y},\bm{v}}\left[(\bm{X}-\bm{\mu}_{\mathrm{color}}(\bm{y},\bm{v}))(\bm{X}-\bm{\mu}_{\mathrm{color}})^{H}(\bm{y},\bm{v})\tilde{S}_{i}(\bm{X}\mid\bm{y},\bm{v})\right].

We next demonstrate that the optimal Vec-GNNDR coincides with maximum-likelihood decoding for the additive colored Gaussian noise channel by proving Corollary

\bm{X}

\bm{X}\mid\bm{y}\sim\hat{\bm{\mu}}+\mathcal{C}\mathcal{N}(0,\hat{\bm{\Sigma}})

Proof for Proposition

with the similar analysis in the proof for Proposition

Proof for Proposition

(Linear processing function)

Proof.

Following the proof of Proposition
5

\sigma_{i}(\bm{y},\bm{v})=\sigma_{i}(\bm{v})
and
\bm{w}_{i}(\bm{y},\bm{v})=\bm{w}_{i}(\bm{v})

\displaystyle I_{\mathrm{GMI,lin}}=B_{x}^{-1}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{V}}\bigg[\sum_{i=1}^{B_{x}}\max_{\{\bm{\gamma_{i}},\sigma_{i},\bm{w}_{i}\}}\bigg\{-\bm{\gamma}_{i}^{H}(\bm{V})\mathbb{E}[\bm{Y}\bm{Y}^{H}\mid\bm{V}]\bm{\gamma}_{i}(\bm{V})-\sigma_{i}^{2}(\bm{V})\bm{w}_{i}^{H}(\bm{V})\mathbb{E}[\bm{X}\bm{X}^{H}\mid\bm{V}]\bm{w}_{i}(\bm{V})

77

\displaystyle I_{\mathrm{GMI,lin}}=B_{x}^{-1}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{V}}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{B_{x}}\max_{\{\sigma_{i},\bm{w}_{i}\}}\left\{\frac{1+P\sigma_{i}^{2}(\bm{V})}{P}\bm{w}_{i}^{H}(\bm{V})\bm{Q}(\bm{v})\bm{w}_{i}(\bm{V})+\log(1+P\sigma_{i}^{2}(\bm{V}))-P\sigma_{i}^{2}(\bm{V})\right\}\right],

\bm{w}_{i}^{H}(\bm{V})\bm{Q}(\bm{V})\bm{w}_{i}(\bm{V})=\bm{w}_{i}^{H}(\bm{V})\mathbb{E}[\bm{X}\bm{Y}^{H}\mid\bm{V}]\left(\mathbb{E}[\bm{Y}\bm{Y}^{H}\mid\bm{V}]\right)^{\dagger}\mathbb{E}[\bm{Y}\bm{X}^{H}\mid\bm{V}]\bm{w}_{i}(\bm{V})<P,\quad\mathrm{almost}\;\mathrm{surely}.

under Assumption

\sigma_{i}^{2}(\bm{v})=\frac{1}{P-\bm{w}_{i}^{H}(\bm{v})\bm{Q}(\bm{v})\bm{w}_{i}(\bm{v})}-\frac{1}{P}.

E-A

Proof for Proposition
7
(Optimal Vec-GNNDR for block noncoherent AWGN channel)

Proof.

Given the rotation phase
\theta
and observation
\bm{y}
, the log-posterior density of
\bm{x}
is given by

\log p(\bm{x}\mid\theta,\bm{y})\propto-\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\|\bm{y}-e^{i\theta}\bm{x}\|_{2}^{2}-\frac{1}{P}\|\bm{x}\|_{2}^{2}.

Thus
\bm{x}\mid\theta,\bm{y}
is circularly symmetric complex Gaussian with

\mathbb{E}[\bm{x}\mid\theta,\bm{y}]=\frac{P}{P+\sigma^{2}}e^{-i\theta}\bm{y},\quad\operatorname{Cov}[\bm{x}\mid\theta,\bm{y}]=\frac{P\sigma^{2}}{P+\sigma^{2}}\bm{I}_{B_{x}}.

To compute
\mathbb{E}[\bm{x}\mid\bm{y}]
, we integrate over
\theta
with respect to the conditional distribution
p(\theta\mid\bm{y})
. Since

p(\theta\mid\bm{y})\propto p(\bm{y}\mid\theta)p(\theta)=p(\bm{y}\mid\theta)\frac{1}{2\pi},

and the distribution of
\bm{y}\mid\theta
is given by

\bm{y}\mid\theta\sim\mathcal{CN}(0,e^{i\theta}P\bm{I}_{B_{x}}e^{-i\theta}+\sigma^{2}\bm{I}_{B_{x}}),

which is independent of
\theta
, we have
p(\theta\mid\bm{y})=\frac{1}{2\pi}
. Therefore,

\bm{\mu}(\bm{y})=\mathbb{E}[\bm{x}\mid\bm{y}]=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{P}{P+\sigma^{2}}e^{-i\theta}\bm{y}\frac{1}{2\pi}\mathrm{d}\theta=\bm{0}_{B_{x}},

(82)

so the optimal processing function is
\bm{g}(\bm{y})=\bm{0}_{B_{x}}
.
For the conditional covariance
\Sigma(\bm{y})=\operatorname{Cov}[\bm{x}\mid\bm{y}]
, we apply the law of total covariance as

\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{y})=\mathbb{E}_{\theta\mid\bm{y}}[\operatorname{Cov}(\bm{x}\mid\theta,\bm{y})]+\operatorname{Cov}_{\theta\mid\bm{y}}(\mathbb{E}[\bm{x}\mid\theta,\bm{y}]).

(83)

The first term equals
\frac{P\sigma^{2}}{P+\sigma^{2}}\bm{I}_{B_{x}}
. For the second term,

\operatorname{Cov}_{\theta\mid\bm{y}}(\mathbb{E}[\bm{x}\mid\theta,\bm{y}])=\mathbb{E}_{\theta\mid\bm{y}}\left[\left(\frac{P}{P+\sigma^{2}}\right)^{2}e^{i\theta}\bm{y}e^{-i\theta}\bm{y}^{H}\right]-\mathbb{E}_{\theta\mid\bm{y}}\left[\frac{P}{P+\sigma^{2}}e^{i\theta}\bm{y}\right]\mathbb{E}_{\theta\mid\bm{y}}\left[\frac{P}{P+\sigma^{2}}e^{-i\theta}\bm{y}^{H}\right]=\left(\frac{P}{P+\sigma^{2}}\right)^{2}\bm{y}\bm{y}^{H},

since
\mathbb{E}_{\theta\mid\bm{y}}[e^{i\theta}]=0
.
Hence by (
83
), we obtain

\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{y})=\frac{P\sigma^{2}}{P+\sigma^{2}}\bm{I}_{B_{x}}+\left(\frac{P}{P+\sigma^{2}}\right)^{2}\bm{y}\bm{y}^{H}.

(84)

The eigenvalues of
\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{y})
are therefore

\lambda_{1}(\bm{y})=\frac{P\sigma^{2}}{P+\sigma^{2}}+\left(\frac{P}{P+\sigma^{2}}\right)^{2}\bm{y}^{H}\bm{y},\quad\lambda_{2}(\bm{y})=\cdots=\lambda_{B_{x}}(\bm{y})=\frac{P\sigma^{2}}{P+\sigma^{2}}.

(85)

Clearly,
\lambda_{i}(\bm{y})<P
for
i=2,\ldots,B_{x}
, and
\lambda_{1}(\bm{y})<P
if
\bm{y}^{H}\bm{y}<P+\sigma^{2}
, whereas
\lambda_{1}(\bm{y})\geq P
if
\bm{y}^{H}\bm{y}\geq P+\sigma^{2}
.

Substituting (
82
)and (
84
) into (
13
) yields

\displaystyle I_{\mathrm{GMI,opt}}

\displaystyle=\frac{B_{x}-1}{B_{x}}\left[\log\left(1+\frac{P}{\sigma^{2}}\right)-\frac{P}{P+\sigma^{2}}\right]+\frac{1}{B_{x}}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{Y}}\left[\left(\log\left(\frac{P+\sigma^{2}}{\sigma^{2}+\frac{P}{P+\sigma^{2}}\bm{Y}^{H}\bm{Y}}\right)+\frac{P}{P+\sigma^{2}}\left(\frac{\bm{Y}^{H}\bm{Y}}{P+\sigma^{2}}-1\right)\right)\mathbb{I}\{\bm{Y}^{H}\bm{Y}<P+\sigma^{2}\}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{B_{x}-1}{B_{x}}\left[\log\left(1+\frac{P}{\sigma^{2}}\right)-\frac{P}{P+\sigma^{2}}\right]+\frac{1}{B_{x}}\mathbb{E}_{T\sim\chi^{2}(2B_{x})}\left[\left(\log\frac{P+\sigma^{2}}{\sigma^{2}+PT/2}+\frac{P(T-2)}{2(P+\sigma^{2})}\right)\mathbb{I}\{T<2\}\right],

where we use the fact
\frac{1}{\sqrt{P+\sigma^{2}}}\bm{Y}\sim\mathcal{CN}(0,\bm{I}_{B_{x}})
.

We further notice that
\bm{\mu}(\bm{y})=\bm{0}_{B_{x}}
for all observations
\bm{y}
. Hence, choosing
\sigma_{1}(\bm{y})
sufficiently small does not yield any additional contribution in (
20
) compared with directly setting
\sigma_{1}(\bm{y})=0
. Consequently, there is no need to truncate
\lambda_{1}(\bm{y})
when
\bm{y}^{H}\bm{y}\geq P+\sigma^{2}
; instead, it suffices to set
\sigma_{1}(\bm{y})=0
.

The principal eigenvector corresponding to
\lambda_{1}(\bm{y})
is given by
\bm{w}_{1}(\bm{y})=\bm{y}/\|\bm{y}\|_{2}
. Define

\bm{W}(\bm{y})=\big(\bm{w}_{1}(\bm{y}),\bm{W}_{\perp}(\bm{y})\big),

where
\bm{W}_{\perp}(\bm{y})
denotes any orthonormal basis of the subspace orthogonal to
\bm{w}_{1}(\bm{y})
.

Substituting
\{[\lambda_{i}(\bm{y})]_{\varepsilon}\}_{i=1}^{B_{x}}
and
\bm{W}(\bm{y})
into (

) and (
16
) yields the following optimal processing and scaling functions:

•

, then

\bm{g}(\bm{y})=\bm{0}_{B_{x}},\qquad\bm{f}(\bm{y})=\mathrm{diag}\!\left(\sqrt{\tfrac{P-\lambda_{1}(\bm{y})}{P\lambda_{1}(\bm{y})}},\tfrac{1}{\sigma},\ldots,\tfrac{1}{\sigma}\right)\bm{W}^{H}(\bm{y}).

•

If
\bm{y}^{H}\bm{y}\geq P+\sigma^{2}

\bm{g}(\bm{y})=\bm{0}_{B_{x}-1},\qquad\bm{f}(\bm{y})=\mathrm{diag}\!\left(\tfrac{1}{\sigma},\ldots,\tfrac{1}{\sigma}\right)\bm{W}_{\perp}^{H}(\bm{y}).

This completes the proof.

, truncation of
w(y)
is not required; it suffices to set
f(y)=0
whenever
|y|^{2}\geq P+\sigma^{2}
. Therefore, the optimal processing and scaling functions are

g(y)=0,\qquad f(y)=\sqrt{\frac{P-w(y)}{Pw(y)}},\qquad\text{if }|y|^{2}<P+\sigma^{2},

and
g(y)=f(y)=0
otherwise. This completes the proof.

∎

Remark 13
.

The element-wise processing function
g
and scaling function
f
derived in the above proof coincide with those obtained by directly applying GNNDR as in
[
42
]
. However, this equivalence holds only under the condition that all elements within the same block share the same marginal distribution. When the marginal distributions differ across elements, the optimization problem in (
10
) can no longer be reduced to the simplified form in (
86
).

E-C

Calculation for limit in (
60
)

Proof.

When
0<T<2
, we have

0\leq-\log\left(1+\frac{P(T-2)}{2(P+\sigma^{2})}\right)+\frac{P(T-2)}{2(P+\sigma^{2})}=\log\left(\frac{P+\sigma^{2}}{\sigma^{2}+PT/2}\right)+\frac{P(T-2)}{2(P+\sigma^{2})}\leq\log\left(1+\frac{P}{\sigma^{2}}\right),

where we use the inequality
\log(1+x)\leq x
.
Then we can bound
I_{\mathrm{GMI,opt}}
in (
58
) as

\frac{B_{x}-1}{B_{x}}\left[\log\left(1+\frac{P}{\sigma^{2}}\right)-\frac{P}{P+\sigma^{2}}\right]\leq I_{\mathrm{GMI,opt}}\leq\frac{1}{B_{x}}\log\left(1+\frac{P}{\sigma^{2}}\right)+\frac{B_{x}-1}{B_{x}}\frac{P}{P+\sigma^{2}},

then taking the limit

and by the squeeze theorem, we have

Appendix F
Phase Noise channel

In this section, we present the sampling strategy and the computation of
I_{\mathrm{GMI,lin}}
for the phase noise channel, as discussed in Section
V-B
.

F-A

Sampling from
p(\bm{x}\mid\bm{y})
for phase noise channel with MCMC

Direct sampling from the posterior distribution
p(\bm{x}\mid\bm{y})
is analytically intractable due to the nonlinearity introduced by the phase terms
e^{i\phi_{i}}
. To address this, we employ a MCMC approach targeting the joint posterior
p(\bm{x},\bm{\phi}\mid\bm{y})
. Specifically, we use Gibbs sampling combined with Metropolis-Hastings (MH) steps to alternately generate samples from
p(\bm{\phi}\mid\bm{x},\bm{y})
and
p(\bm{x}\mid\bm{\phi},\bm{y})
. By collecting the resulting
\bm{x}
samples, we effectively marginalize over
\bm{\phi}
and can subsequently approximate the posterior mean
\mathbb{E}[\bm{x}\mid\bm{y}]
and posterior covariance matrix
\operatorname{Cov}[\bm{x}\mid\bm{y}]
. (Refer to Algorithm
1
).

We first give some analyze for the joint posterior
p(\bm{x},\bm{\phi}\mid\bm{y})
. Given
\bm{y}
, the joint posterior distribution of
\bm{x}
and
\bm{\phi}
is expressed as:

p(\bm{x},\bm{\phi}\mid\bm{y})\propto p(\bm{y}\mid\bm{x},\bm{\phi})p(\bm{x})p(\bm{\phi}),

where

•

p(\bm{y}\mid\bm{x},\bm{\phi})
denotes the likelihood function, given by:

p(\bm{y}\mid\bm{x},\bm{\phi})=\prod_{i=1}^{B_{x}}\frac{1}{\pi\sigma^{2}}\exp\left(-\frac{|y_{i}-e^{i\phi_{i}}x_{i}|^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\right).

(87)

•

p(\bm{x})
represents the signal prior, expressed as:

p(\bm{x})=\prod_{i=1}^{B_{x}}\frac{1}{\pi P}\exp\left(-\frac{|x_{i}|^{2}}{P}\right).

•

p(\bm{\phi})
corresponds to the phase prior, defined as:

p(\bm{\phi})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{(2\pi)^{B_{x}}\det(\Sigma_{\phi})}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\bm{\phi}^{T}\Sigma_{\phi}^{-1}\bm{\phi}\right),

where
\Sigma_{\phi}
is the covariance matrix given in Section
V-B
. The phase prior can be generated by
\phi_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{i}cz_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq B_{x}
, where
\bm{z}=[z_{1},\cdots,z_{B_{x}}]^{\top}
is drawn from a standard complex Gaussian distribution with the probability density function:

p(\bm{z})=\frac{1}{(\sqrt{2\pi})^{B_{x}}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\|\bm{z}\|_{2}^{2}\right).

Then we have the following MCMC sampling algorithm for the phase noise channel.

Proof for Proposition
9
(vec-GNNDR with linear processing function for the phase noise channel)

Proof.

Let
\bm{\Theta}=\operatorname{diag}\{e^{i\phi_{1}},\dots,e^{i\phi_{B_{x}}}\}
. The phase noise channel defined in (
61
) can be rewritten as:

\bm{Y}=\bm{\Theta}\bm{X}+\bm{N},

where
\bm{X}\sim\mathcal{CN}(\bm{0},P\bm{I}_{B_{x}})
,
\bm{N}\sim\mathcal{CN}(\bm{0},\sigma^{2}\bm{I}_{B_{x}})
, and
\bm{\phi}=[\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{B_{x}}]^{\top}\mathcal{N}\sim(\bm{0},\bm{\Sigma}_{\phi})
with
\bm{\Sigma}_{\phi}
defined in Section
V-B
. No CSI is available in the phase noise channel.

First, we calculate the cross-correlation matrix
\mathbb{E}[\bm{X}\bm{Y}^{H}]
:

\mathbb{E}[\bm{XY}^{H}]=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\phi}}[\mathbb{E}_{\bm{X}}[\bm{X}(\bm{\Theta}\bm{X}+\bm{N})^{H}\mid\bm{\phi}]]=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\phi}}[P\bm{\Theta}^{H}].

The
(j,k)
-th element of this matrix is given by:

(\mathbb{E}[\bm{XY}^{H}])_{jk}=P\cdot\mathbb{E}[e^{-i\phi_{j}}]\cdot\delta_{jk}.

Since
\phi_{j}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,c^{2}j)
, and recalling the characteristic function for a Gaussian variable
Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
is
\mathbb{E}[e^{itZ}]=e^{-\frac{1}{2}t^{2}\sigma^{2}}
, we obtain
\mathbb{E}[e^{-i\phi_{j}}]=\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}c^{2}j\right)
. Consequently,

\mathbb{E}[\bm{XY}^{H}]=P\operatorname{diag}(e^{-\frac{1}{2}c^{2}},e^{-c^{2}},\dots,e^{-\frac{1}{2}c^{2}B_{x}}).

(88)

Next, we compute the autocorrelation matrix
\mathbb{E}[\bm{Y}\bm{Y}^{H}]
:

\mathbb{E}[\bm{YY}^{H}]=\mathbb{E}[(\bm{\Theta}\bm{X}+\bm{N})(\bm{\Theta}\bm{X}+\bm{N})^{H}]=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\phi}}[\bm{\Theta}(P\bm{I})\bm{\Theta}^{H}+\sigma^{2}\bm{I}]=P\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\phi}}[\bm{\Theta}\bm{\Theta}^{H}]+\sigma^{2}\bm{I}=(P+\sigma^{2})\bm{I}.

(89)

Taking (
88
) and (
89
) into (
51
), we obtain

\bm{Q}=\mathbb{E}[\bm{XY}^{H}](\mathbb{E}[\bm{YY}^{H}])^{\dagger}\mathbb{E}[\bm{Y}\bm{X}^{H}]=\operatorname{diag}\left(\frac{P^{2}e^{-c^{2}}}{P+\sigma^{2}},\cdots,\frac{P^{2}e^{-c^{2}B_{x}}}{P+\sigma^{2}}\right).

Since
\bm{Q}
is diagonal, its eigenvalues
\lambda_{i}
corresponding to the
i
-th diagonal element are given by:

\lambda_{i}=\frac{P^{2}e^{-c^{2}i}}{P+\sigma^{2}}>0,\quad i=1,\dots,B_{x}.

(90)

and the associated eigenvector matrix
\bm{W}=\bm{I}_{B_{x}}
.

According to Proposition
54
, the optimal GMI under linear processing functions is given by:

\displaystyle I_{\mathrm{GMI,lin}}=\frac{1}{B_{x}}\sum_{i=1}^{B_{x}}\log\left(\frac{P}{P-\lambda_{i}}\right)=\frac{1}{B_{x}}\sum_{i=1}^{B_{x}}\log\left(\frac{P+\sigma^{2}}{P(1-e^{-c^{2}i})+\sigma^{2}}\right).

Since the correlation matrices
\mathbb{E}[\bm{XY}^{H}]
and
\mathbb{E}[\bm{YY}^{H}]
are diagonal, and observing that the eigenvector matrix is
\bm{W}=\bm{I}_{B_{x}}
, it follows from (
53
) and (
54
) that the linear processing matrix
\bm{\Gamma}
and scaling matrix
\bm{\Pi}
are also diagonal. They take the following forms:

\bm{\Gamma}=\operatorname{diag}\left(\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{B_{x}}\right),\qquad\bm{\Pi}=\operatorname{diag}\left(\pi_{1},\dots,\pi_{B_{x}}\right),

where the diagonal elements are derived as:

\displaystyle\pi_{i}=\sqrt{\frac{P}{(P-\lambda_{i})\lambda_{i}}}=\frac{P+\sigma^{2}}{Pe^{-\frac{1}{2}c^{2}i}\sqrt{P(1-e^{-c^{2}i})+\sigma^{2}}},

\displaystyle\gamma_{i}=\pi_{i}\cdot\frac{1}{P+\sigma^{2}}\cdot(\mathbb{E}[\bm{YX}^{H}])_{ii}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{P(1-e^{-c^{2}i})+\sigma^{2}}}.

This completes the proof.
∎

Proof for Proposition
10
(Phase noise channel with identity decoding rule)

Proof.

Consider the identity decoding rule
d(\bm{x},\bm{y})=|\bm{y}-\bm{x}|_{2}^{2}
.
Then the GMI in (
10
) takes the form

I_{\text{GMI, id}}=\max_{\theta<0}\left\{\theta\left[\sigma^{2}+\frac{P}{B_{x}}\sum_{i=1}^{B_{x}}\mathbb{E}_{\theta_{i}}(2-2\cos\theta_{i})\right]-\frac{(P+\sigma^{2})\theta}{1-\theta P}+\log(1-P\theta)\right\},

(91)

with
\theta_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,c^{2}i)
. We first evaluate the expectation with respect to
\theta_{i}
:

\mathbb{E}_{\theta_{i}}(2-2\cos\theta_{i})=2(1-e^{-c^{2}i/2}).

Define
A=\sigma^{2}+\frac{2P}{B_{x}}\sum_{i=1}^{B_{x}}(1-e^{-c^{2}i/2})
, then the maximization problem for
\theta
reduces to

h(\theta)=\theta A-\frac{(P+\sigma^{2})\theta}{1-\theta P}+\log(1-\theta P).

Taking the derivative of
h(\theta)
with respect to
\theta
and setting it to zero yields

A-\frac{(P+\sigma^{2})}{(1-\theta P)^{2}}-\frac{P}{1-\theta P}=0.

The solution is

\theta^{*}=\frac{2A-P-\sqrt{P^{2}+4A\left(P+\sigma^{2}\right)}}{2AP}.

Substituting
\theta^{*}
into (
91
) gives the desired expression, which completes the proof.
∎

Calculation for limit in (

Proof.

We first analyze the high-SNR limit of

Introduce
\alpha_{P}:=A/P=s+\sigma^{2}/P
, then
\theta^{*}

\theta^{*}=\frac{2A-P-\sqrt{P^{2}+4A\left(P+\sigma^{2}\right)}}{2AP}=\frac{2a_{P}-1-\sqrt{1+4a_{P}\left(1+\sigma^{2}/P\right)}}{2a_{P}P}.

Taking the limit
\mathrm{SNR}\to\infty
(equivalently,
P/\sigma^{2}\to\infty

\lim_{\mathrm{SNR}\to\infty}P\theta^{*}=\frac{2s-1-\sqrt{1+4s}}{2s}:=\kappa.

(92)

Next, consider each term in (

\lim_{\mathrm{SNR}\to\infty}\theta^{*}A=s\kappa,\quad\lim_{\mathrm{SNR}\to\infty}\frac{(P+\sigma^{2})\theta^{*}}{1-\theta^{*}P}=\frac{\kappa}{1-\kappa},\quad\lim_{\mathrm{SNR}\to\infty}\log(1-P\theta^{*})=\log(1-\kappa),

Substituting into (

), we obtain

\lim_{\mathrm{SNR}\rightarrow\infty}I_{\mathrm{GMI},\mathrm{id}}=s\kappa-\frac{\kappa}{1-\kappa}+\log(1-\kappa).

(93)

Finally, substituting (
92
) into (
93
) and simplifying yields

\lim_{\mathrm{SNR}\to\infty}I_{\text{GMI, id}}=s+1-\sqrt{1+4s}+\log\left(\frac{2}{\sqrt{1+4s}-1}\right),
