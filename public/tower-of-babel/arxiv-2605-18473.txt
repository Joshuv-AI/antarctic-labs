Accelerating charging dynamics of electric double-layer capacitors
Megh Dutta, Ivan Palaia, Emmanuel Trizac, Benjamin Rotenberg

Abstract
Electric double-layer capacitors (EDLCs), consisting of an ionic fluid between two metallic electrodes, are electrochemical energy storage devices complementary to batteries, allowing for a faster charge/discharge. The charging dynamics in response to a voltage step features a variety of regimes and relaxation timescales, depending on the applied voltage and the various lengths characterizing the system, most importantly the inter-electrode distance and the Debye length over which electrostatic effects are screened in the electrolyte. Inspired by recent works on “shortcut to adiabaticity” in colloidal systems, here we investigate the possibility to control the charge and discharge of planar EDLCs using time-dependent voltages. Specifically, our aim is to achieve a full charge or discharge within a finite time shorter than their intrinsic relaxation timescales. Within the Poisson-Nernst-Planck model and the small-voltage regime, we derive time-dependent protocols that can eliminate an arbitrary number of relaxation modes. This permits to approach the equilibrium charged state within a finite time, that can be in practice an order of magnitude faster than the natural equilibration time. We illustrate the relevance and efficacy of the method on polynomial drivings and show that the surface charge density, charge-density profiles, and global deviation from equilibrium (quantified by a Kullback–Leibler-like divergence) can all be significantly accelerated, even for driving times comparable to or shorter than the natural R ​ C RC time of the system.



I
Introduction

Ionic fluids, formed by ions usually dissolved in a polar solvent, exhibit interesting properties near surfaces or under confinement. If the surface is electrically charged, an electrolytic double-layer (EDL) forms at the interface, with a local net charge in the liquid arising from an imbalance in counter-ions (charge of opposite sign compared to the surface) and co-ions (same sign)
Parsons 1990
. The surface charge can be induced by applying a voltage between two conductors separated by an electrolyte, resulting in an electric double layer capacitor (EDLC)
Simon and Gogotsi 2020
. In contrast to standard capacitors, which develop a polarization throughout the polarizable insulating material separating the electrodes, in these systems, at equilibrium the electric field is screened at the interface so that the rest of the liquid is not polarized
Ji et al. 2014
;
Frivaldsky et al. 2018
;
Torki, Joubert, and Sari 2023
. In contrast to batteries, their charging does not involve electron transfer reactions at the interface, which enables faster charge/discharge (at the price of lower energy density) and their stability over many cycles, fostering their use in a wealth of applications
Miller and Burke 2008
;
Durganjali et al. 2022
.

Given the relevance of charging dynamics in EDLCs, several strategies have been proposed to address different aspects from the theoretical point of view, from analytical theories based on continuous descriptions to atomistic simulations via mesoscopic models, in order to identify generic scaling laws or to understand specific molecular aspects of the electrode/electrolyte interface or the transport of ions inside porous electrodes
Péan et al. 2014
;
Péan et al. 2016
;
Noh and Jung 2019
;
Jeanmairet, Rotenberg, and Salanne 2022
;
Waysenson et al. 2025
;
Asta et al. 2019
;
Lin et al. 2022
;
Ma et al. 2022
;
Wu 2022
;
Kondrat et al. 2023
. Experimentally, the dynamics of charging is investigated using electrochemical impedance spectroscopy
Wang et al. 2021
;
Vivier and Orazem 2022
, which characterizes the frequency-dependent response of the systems to small voltages. This linear response can be predicted based on various models of the dynamics, at the continuous level
Barbero and Evangelista 2005
;
Barbero and Alexe-Ionescu 2005
;
Barbero, Batalioto, and Figueiredo Neto 2008
;
Barbero 2017
or from mesoscopic and molecular simulations
Pireddu and Rotenberg 2023
;
Pireddu et al. 2024
. This allows to link transport through the EDLC with the equivalent circuit models used to interpret the experiments, such as the simple
RC
circuit to capture the accumulation of charge at the interface and the dissipation due to ionic transport through the electrolyte.

Following the pioneering investigation of Bazant
et al.

Bazant, Thornton, and Ajdari 2004
, theoretical work based on the Poisson-Nernst-Planck model to describe the dynamics of ions between the electrodes, or its extensions, allowed to identify various regimes depending on the applied voltage and the competition between various length scales (see Fig.
1
): the inter-electrode distance
2L
, the Debye length
\lambda_{D}
characterizing the screening of electrostatic interactions between the ions and the extent of the EDL, or the Bjerrum length beyond which the electrostatic energy between two unit charges is smaller than the thermal energy
Bazant, Thornton, and Ajdari 2004
;
Beunis et al. 2008
;
Lee et al. 2015
;
Janssen and Bier 2018
;
Palaia 2019
;
Palaia et al. 2025a
;
Palaia et al. 2025b
;
Fertig and Janssen 2025
. These works highlighted in particular the importance of the timescale
L\lambda_{D}/D
, with
D
a typical diffusion coefficient of the ions, corresponding to the
RC
charging time in the thin EDL limit (
\lambda_{D}\ll L)
.

Since the key advantage of EDLCs compared to batteries is their charging dynamics, a natural question arises: Is it possible to reduce their charge and discharge using time-dependent voltages,
i.e.
achieve a full charge or discharge within a finite time shorter than their intrinsic relaxation timescales? This could be useful not only in the context of energy storage/delivery, but also for other applications. This question has already fostered several attempts. For example, Breitsprecher
et al.
showed using molecular simulations that the discharge of a capacitor with nanoporous electrodes can be accelerated using a voltage inversion followed by a linear decay, compared to simply switching the voltage off
Breitsprecher et al. 2020
, while Lian
et al.
used mesoscopic modelling of EDLCs with porous electrodes to discuss the necessary trade-off between capacitance and charging time
Lian et al. 2020
.

In the present work, we address this question more systematically. To this end, we build upon recent developments in other contexts, where protocols have been derived to enable faster transitions between two equilibrium states. Inspired from the notion of “shortcut to adiabaticity” in quantum systems, several strategies have been developed for stochastic systems where the dynamics depends on thermal fluctuations, with successful applications in soft matter
Martínez et al. 2016
;
Bayati and Trizac 2021
;
Plata et al. 2021
;
Raynal et al. 2023
;
Guéry-Odelin et al. 2019
;
Guéry-Odelin et al. 2023
. Restricting ourselves to the linear response regime,
i.e.
sufficiently small voltages, we obtain analytical results for time-dependent protocols eliminating an arbitrary number of relaxation modes in the system, in order to approach the equilibrium charged state within a finite time. Section
II
introduces the Poisson-Nernst-Planck model used to describe the ion dynamics in the EDLC. The response to a time-dependent charging protocol is then described in Section
III
: the general solution is given in Section
III.1
, while the method to shortcut the charging time is introduced in Section
III.2
. Finally, we illustrate the relevance and efficacy of the method on polynomial drivings in Section
III.3
.

We consider the dynamics of cations and anions with charges

For sufficiently low concentrations, one can treat electrostatic interactions between ions and with the electrodes at the mean-field level, neglect short-range interactions between ions, and only consider their exclusion from the solid walls via a no-flux boundary condition. We work in the canonical ensemble, with a fixed number of ion pairs per unit surface
2n_{0}L
, where
n_{0}
is the uniform density of the salt in the absence of voltage. For a symmetric electrolyte with

The ionic densities

\frac{\partial n_{\pm}}{\partial t}(z,t)=-\frac{\partial j_{\pm}}{\partial z}(z,t)

(1)

with an expression of the fluxes including drift due to the electric field and diffusion due to concentration gradients:

{j}_{\pm}(z,t)=-\beta D_{\pm}n_{\pm}(z,t)q_{\pm}e\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial z}(z,t)-D_{\pm}\frac{\partial n_{\pm}}{\partial z}(z,t)\;.

(2)

Here
e

\beta=1/k_{B}T
with
k_{B}
the Boltzmann constant and
T

i.e.
that the diffusion of one of the species is not influenced by any other species. In the following, we will also assume that
D_{+}=D_{-}=D
. Both equations result in the Nernst-Planck equation:

\frac{\partial n_{\pm}}{\partial t}(z,t)=D_{\pm}\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\left(n_{\pm}(z,t)\beta q_{\pm}e\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial z}(z,t)+\frac{\partial n_{\pm}}{\partial z}(z,t)\right)

(3)

where the electric field
-\partial\phi/\partial z
driving the ions includes that due to the external voltage and that arising from the charge distribution inside the liquid,
\rho=q_{+}en_{+}+q_{-}en_{-}
. The potential satisfies the Poisson equation:

\frac{\partial^{2}\phi}{\partial z^{2}}(z,t)=-\frac{\rho(z,t)}{\varepsilon_{0}\varepsilon_{r}}

(4)

with
\varepsilon_{0}
the vacuum permittivity and
\varepsilon_{r}
the relative permittivity of the solvent. The PNP equations
4
and
3

\phi(\pm L,t)=\pm V(t)

\pm\sigma(t)
of the electrodes, while the no-flux boundary conditions for the ions at
z_{b}=\pm L
read:

\displaystyle-\beta q_{\pm}en_{\pm}(z_{b},t)\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial z}(z_{b},t)-\frac{\partial n_{\pm}}{\partial z}(z_{b},t)

\displaystyle=0\;.

(5)

III
Response to a time-dependent charging protocol

A full description of the various regimes (in terms of
\epsilon
and
v
) for the response to a step potential
V(t)=v\,\Theta(t)
, with
\Theta
the Heaviside step function, can be found in Refs.
31
;
32
. We address here a different problem, which is how to get as close as possible to the steady-state in a finite driving time
t_{f}
, using a time-dependent voltage protocol reaching the final value
v
at
t_{f}
. To that end, we restrict ourselves to the linear response regime, which implies in particular
v\ll 1
. For convenience, we separate the steady-state and time-dependent parts of the potential as

V(t)=v\,\Theta(t)+\Delta V(t)

(9)

where
\Delta V(t)
only varies between
t=0
and
t_{f}
. The initial and final conditions
V(0)=0
and
V(t_{f})=v
impose
\Delta V(0)=-v
and
\Delta V(t_{f})=0
.

III.1
Relaxation dynamics

Using the adimensionalized quantities of Table
1
, the linear response of the charge density profile
\rho(z,t)=[n_{+}(z,t)-n_{-}(z,t)]/2
to a time-dependent driving voltage
V(t)
can be conveniently found in the Laplace domain, introducing for time-dependent functions
f(t)
:

\widehat{f}(s)=\mathscr{L}\{f(t)\}=\int_{0}^{\infty}f(t)e^{-st}\,{\rm d}t\;.

(10)

The solution for the charge density is
Palaia 2019
;
Palaia et al. 2025a
;
Palaia et al. 2025b

\displaystyle\widehat{\rho}(z,s)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\sinh\frac{z\sqrt{1+\epsilon s}}{\epsilon}}{\sinh\frac{\sqrt{1+\epsilon s}}{\epsilon}}H(s)\widehat{V}(s)

(11)

with the transfer function

H(s)=\frac{1+\epsilon s}{1+s\sqrt{1+\epsilon s}\coth\frac{\sqrt{1+\epsilon s}}{\epsilon}}\;.

(12)

The corresponding surface charge density, obtained from the field at the electrode surface, is

\displaystyle\widehat{\sigma}(s)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\sqrt{1+\epsilon s}}{\epsilon}\coth\frac{\sqrt{1+\epsilon s}}{\epsilon}H(s)\widehat{V}(s)\;.

(13)

We denote the complex poles of the transfer function
H(s)
by
s_{n}
and those of the driving potential
\widehat{V}(s)
by
s_{n}^{V}
. The presence and nature of poles of the driving potential depends on the specific choice of
V(t)
. The complex poles of the transfer function can be determined numerically
Janssen and Bier 2018
;
Palaia 2019
;
Palaia et al. 2025a
;
Palaia et al. 2025b
. They are all simple poles, real and negative and we order them by increasing absolute value (
|s_{0}|<|s_{1}|<\dots
). Their number is infinite and the corresponding timescales depend on
\epsilon=\lambda_{D}/L
. For strong EDL overlap (
\epsilon\to\infty
), the slowest mode corresponds to diffusion between the electrodes, with timescale
4L^{2}/(\pi^{2}D)
(see Refs.
29
;
32
). In the thin EDL limit (
\epsilon\to 0
), the slowest two modes correspond to
RC
charging time
L\lambda_{D}/D
and Debye relaxation time
\lambda_{D}^{2}/D
as also expected from frequency-domain analysis (see
e.g.
Ref.
42
). For
\epsilon=0.1
, considered in the illustrations below, the slowest mode corresponds to
s_{0}\approx-1.06
,
i.e.
a relaxation time of
0.94
in units of
L\lambda_{D}/D
, while the next ones correspond to
s_{1}\approx-12.05
,
s_{2}\approx-16.04
,
s_{3}\approx-22.00
and
s_{4}\approx-29.92
,
i.e.
faster and faster relaxation.

In the general case, the residue theorem allows us to express the evolution of the charge density profile in the time domain, using Eq.
11
, as

\displaystyle\rho(z,t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s\in\{s_{n},s_{n}^{V}\}}\text{Res}_{\hat{\rho}(z,s)e^{st}}(s)\,,

(14)

where the sum runs over poles of the transfer function and the driving potential. For the specific form of driving considered here, in the Laplace domain Eq.
9
leads to

\widehat{V}(s)=\frac{v}{s}+\widehat{\Delta V}(s)\;.

(15)

\hat{\rho}(z,s)
has a simple pole at
s=0
, coming not from the transfer function
H
but from the forcing
\widehat{V}(s)
, and corresponding to the steady-state solution. Indeed, computing the residue with respect to that pole gives the equilibrium distribution:

\displaystyle\rho_{eq}(z)

\displaystyle=\text{Res}_{\widehat{\rho}(z,s)e^{st}}(s=0)=\lim_{s\rightarrow 0}(s\widehat{\rho}(z,s)e^{st})

\displaystyle=-v\frac{\sinh\frac{z}{\epsilon}}{\sinh\frac{1}{\epsilon}}\;,

(16)

where we have used Eqs.
11
and
12
.
The full transient response from Eq.
14
can then be rewritten, using Eq.
III.1
, as

\displaystyle\rho(z,t)

\displaystyle=\rho_{eq}(z)+\sum_{s\in\{s_{n}^{\Delta V}\}}\text{Res}_{\widehat{\rho}(z,s)e^{st}}(s)

\displaystyle\hskip 2.84544pt+2\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{s_{n}(1+\epsilon s_{n})}{3-s_{n}^{2}}\frac{\sinh\frac{z\sqrt{1+\epsilon s_{n}}}{\epsilon}}{\sinh\frac{\sqrt{1+\epsilon s_{n}}}{\epsilon}}\widehat{V}(s_{n})e^{s_{n}t}

(17)

where the first sum runs over poles of
\widehat{\Delta V}
and the second over that of the transfer function
H
(for the derivation of the last term, see Refs.
Janssen and Bier 2018
;
Palaia et al. 2025b
).

Similarly, the surface charge density can be expressed in the time domain, using Eq.
13
, as

\displaystyle\sigma(t)

\displaystyle=\sigma_{eq}+\sum_{s\in\{s_{n}^{\Delta V}\}}\text{Res}_{\widehat{\sigma}(s)e^{st}}(s)

\displaystyle+\frac{2}{\epsilon}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1+\epsilon s_{n}}{s_{n}^{2}-3}\widehat{V}(s_{n})e^{s_{n}t},

(18)

where the equilibrium value

\displaystyle\sigma_{eq}

\displaystyle=-\frac{v}{\epsilon}\coth\frac{1}{\epsilon}

(19)

allows one to define the capacitance
\sigma_{eq}/v
of the system.

The ionic density profiles can be deduced from that of the charge in the linear regime (where in reduced units
n_{+}+n_{-}=2
and
n_{+}-n_{-}=2\rho
) as:

n_{\pm}(z,t)=1\pm\rho(z,t)\;.

(20)

This allows us in particular to quantify the deviation of the system from its equilibrium state reached for
t\to\infty
. Inspired by the notion of Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence
Kullback and Leibler 1951
, we normalize the density profiles (by
2n_{0}L
so that their integrals are equal to 1) to introduce

\displaystyle D_{KL}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2n_{0}L}\int_{-L}^{L}\left[n_{+}^{eq}(z)\ln\frac{n_{+}^{eq}(z)}{n_{+}(z,t)}\right.

\displaystyle\left.\hskip 56.9055pt+n_{-}^{eq}(z)\ln\frac{n_{-}^{eq}(z)}{n_{-}(z,t)}\right]\,{\rm d}z

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2n_{0}L}\int_{-L}^{L}\left[(1+\rho_{eq}(z))\ln\frac{1+\rho_{eq}(z)}{1+\rho(z,t)}\right.

\displaystyle\left.\hskip 28.45274pt+(1-\rho_{eq}(z))\ln\frac{1-\rho_{eq}(z)}{1-\rho(z,t)}\right]\,{\rm d}z\,.

(21)

This object allows to quantity the completeness of convergence towards the steady state distributions for both cation and anion densities. When the profiles have reached equilibrium,
D_{KL}
vanishes. Note that by construction, such a quantity is always positive.

III.2
Shortcutting the charging time

Given that
\widehat{\rho}(z,s)\propto H(s)\hat{V}(s)
, we can use
V
to eliminate poles of
H
that correspond to slow relaxation modes. This can be done by simply prescribing that
V
has a zero where
H
has a pole. The solution Eq.
17
for an arbitrary driving potential of the form Eq.
9
thus provides a way to relax to the equilibrium density profile (and corresponding equilibrium charge of the electrodes) faster than with a step potential, which corresponds to a driving
\Delta V(t)=0
and a solution for the charge density profile with
\widehat{V}(s_{n})=v/s_{n}
in Eq.
17
. By choosing a driving
V(t)
that does not introduce additional poles (other than
s=0
corresponding to the equilibrium) and such that
\widehat{V}(s_{n})=0
for
n\in\{0,...,m-1\}
, we ensure that the
m
slowest modes of the density do not contribute to the relaxation dynamics. Indeed, for such a choice Eq. (
18
) reduces to

\displaystyle\sigma(t)

\displaystyle=\sigma_{eq}+\frac{2}{\epsilon}\sum_{n=m}^{\infty}\frac{1+\epsilon s_{n}}{s_{n}^{2}-3}\widehat{V}(s_{n})e^{s_{n}t}

(22)

and, similarly, for the density profiles Eq.
17
reduces to

\displaystyle\rho(z,t)

\displaystyle=\rho_{eq}(z)

\displaystyle\hskip-5.69046pt+2\sum_{n=m}^{\infty}\frac{s_{n}(1+\epsilon s_{n})}{3-s_{n}^{2}}\frac{\sinh\frac{z\sqrt{1+\epsilon s_{n}}}{\epsilon}}{\sinh\frac{\sqrt{1+\epsilon s_{n}}}{\epsilon}}\widehat{V}(s_{n})e^{s_{n}t}\;.

(23)

In order to find a suitable protocol satisfying the cancellation of
m
modes (
\widehat{V}(s_{n})=0
for
n\in\{0,...,m-1\}
) as well as the initial and final conditions (
V(0)=0
,
V(t_{f})=v
),
i.e.
a total of
m+2
constraints, we look for a combination of the form
\Delta V(t)=v\sum_{i=0}^{m+1}a_{i}f_{i}(t)
, where
f_{i}(t)
are
m+2
linearly independent functions such that they do not all vanish at
t=0
and that they all vanish for
t>t_{f}
. We further introduce the Laplace transforms (see Eq.
10
, recalling that
f_{i}
vanishes for

):

\ell_{i}(s,t_{f})=\int_{0}^{t_{f}}f_{i}(t)e^{-st}dt\;.

(24)

The mentioned constraints can then be expressed as a system of linear equations for the coefficients
a_{i}
:

\begin{bmatrix}f_{0}(0)&...&f_{m+1}(0)\\
f_{0}(t_{f})&...&f_{m+1}(t_{f})\\
\ell_{0}(s_{0},t_{f})&...&\ell_{m+1}(s_{0},t_{f})\\
:&:&:\\
\ell_{0}(s_{m-1},t_{f})&...&\ell_{m+1}(s_{m-1},t_{f})\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}a_{0}\\
a_{1}\\
a_{2}\\
:\\
a_{m+1}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}-1\\
0\\
-\frac{1}{s_{0}}\\
:\\
-\frac{1}{s_{m-1}}\end{bmatrix}

(25)

where the first two raws impose the initial and final conditions, and the remaining rows ensure pole cancellation. For a given choice of suitable functions
f_{i}(t)
, the
m+2
coefficients
a_{i}
obtained by inverting the matrix in Eq.
25
define a protocol allowing us to eliminate the
m
slowest modes from the relaxation towards the equilibrium, which are all present when a step function is used.

III.3
Polynomial driving

We now illustrate the proposed approach to shortcut the charging dynamics, using the specific choice of polynomial driving for
\Delta V(t)
,
i.e.

f_{i}(t)=t^{i}
for
t\in[0,t_{f}]
and 0 elsewhere. The Laplace transform

\widehat{V}(s)=\frac{v}{s}+v\sum_{i=0}^{m+1}\dfrac{a_{i}}{s^{i+1}}\left[i!-\Gamma(1+i,st_{f})\right]\;,

(26)

where
\Gamma
is the upper incomplete Gamma function, reveals no pole apart from
s=0
, so that with the proper choice of coefficients the charge density will evolve according to Eq.
23
. For polynomial driving, the boundary condition
V(0)=0
leads to

, and the other coefficients can be obtained by solving Eq.
25
numerically. In practice, the infinite sum over modes

in Eq.
23
has to be truncated at a finite

value, which can be chosen to achieve a prescribed numerical precision thanks to the fast decay with

. Results shown below were obtained by truncation at
n_{\rm max}=10^{2}
.

IV
Conclusion

In this work, we investigated the possibility of accelerating the charging dynamics of electric double-layer capacitors (EDLCs) using time-dependent voltage protocols, inspired by the concept of “shortcuts to adiabaticity.” Within the Poisson-Nernst-Planck model and the linear response regime, we derived analytical protocols capable of eliminating an arbitrary number of relaxation modes, thereby enabling the system to approach its equilibrium charged state within a finite time —potentially orders of magnitude faster than the intrinsic relaxation time (provided that the system remains in the linear regime). Our approach relies on designing driving protocols that suppress the slowest relaxation modes, allowing for rapid and controlled charging. Through this method, we demonstrated that the surface charge density, charge-density profiles, and global deviation from equilibrium (quantified by a Kullback–Leibler-like divergence) can all be significantly accelerated, even for driving times comparable to or shorter than the natural
RC
time of the system.

The results highlight the efficacy of these protocols in achieving near-equilibrium states at the electrode-electrolyte interface, both locally and globally. The elimination of relaxation modes not only reduces the time required to reach equilibrium but also provides flexibility in optimizing the charging process under additional constraints, such as limiting the maximum applied voltage or minimizing energy dissipation. The present approach could in principle be extended to take into account many non-ideal effects that could be present in the system like electrostatic correlations
Gillespie, Valiskó, and Boda 2021
;
Storey and Bazant 2012
;
Agrawal and Wang 2022
;
Palaia et al. 2020
;
Palaia et al. 2022
;
Palaia et al. 2025b
, chemical reactions
Eden et al. 2019
;
Noroozi and Smith 2019
, solvation effects
Petsev, van Swol, and D Frink 2021
, or advection in the electrolyte. While the present study focuses on planar EDLCs and symmetric electrolytes, the methodology and insights offer promising avenues for enhancing the performance of energy storage devices and other applications where rapid charging dynamics are critical, such as digital memory systems. This work underscores the potential of time-dependent voltage protocols as a powerful tool for controlling the dynamics of electrochemical systems by systematically eliminating slow modes.
