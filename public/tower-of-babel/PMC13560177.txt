Serial cardiac MR assessment and histopathological evaluation of doxorubicin-induced cardiomyopathy in a miniature swine model
Authors: Rui Shi, Wen Zeng, Li-ting Shen, Rong Xu, Xi Liu, Yuan Li, Zhen-lin Li, Chun-chao Xia, Zhi-gang Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveDoxorubicin-induced cardiomyopathy remains challenging to detect at an early stage. This study aims to assess cardiac MR changes and their pathological correlates in a swine model of doxorubicin-induced cardiomyopathy.MethodsMiniature pigs received repeated intravenous doxorubicin administration and underwent serial cardiac MR examinations from baseline to 32 weeks. Left ventricular function, global strain, and T2 relaxation time were assessed longitudinally. Histological and ultrastructural analyses were performed at predefined time points to evaluate myocardial injury.ResultsLVEF remained relatively preserved during doxorubicin administration and showed only a non-significant declining trend during follow-up. LVGLS showed a non-significant decreasing trend during the treatment (weeks 0–8, P = 0.068) and stabilized during the follow-up period (weeks 8–32, P = 0.35); GCS demonstrated deterioration (week 4: P = 0.006; week 32: P < 0.001); while GRS exhibited significant impairment, with maximal reduction at week 20 (β=-12.87, P = 0.04) that persisted through week 32 (P = 0.001). T2 mapping analysis showed significant elevation during active treatment (χ²=10.5, P = 0.033), followed by partial recovery by the final follow-up (week 32: 36.74 ± 2.80 ms, P = 0.020). Histopathological evaluation demonstrated mitochondrial swelling/vacuolization during treatment, with substantial resolution by week 32. However, persistent abnormalities in myocardial fiber architecture were observed at the study endpoint.ConclusionsIn this miniature swine model, T2 prolongation during doxorubicin treatment was temporally associated with mitochondrial-related ultrastructural alterations and may reflect potentially reversible components of myocardial injury. Persistent strain abnormalities despite T2 normalization suggest incomplete structural recovery, mainly reflecting ultrastructural alterations rather than fibrotic progression. Combined T2 mapping and strain assessment may provide complementary information for characterizing doxorubicin-related myocardial injury in preclinical settings.Keywords: Cardiac MR, Cardiotoxicity, T2 mapping, Strain analysis, Animal model

Introduction

Anthracycline drugs, such as Doxorubicin (DOX), are widely used in the treatment of breast cancer and hematologic malignancies, effectively reducing the risk of death in cancer patients. However, the cardiotoxicity limits the broader clinical application [1, 2]. Researches indicate that mitochondria are a key target of doxorubicin [3–5]. Cardiomyocytes, with high mitochondria content and low antioxidant levels, are prone to oxidative damage, leading to anthracycline accumulation [6–8]. A deep understanding of the mechanisms and harmful effects of Doxorubicin-induced cardiomyopathy (DIC) is crucial for monitoring cardiac function and managing the clinical treatment of chemotherapy patients.

Left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) is a commonly used indicator for monitoring DIC, and a decline in LVEF often reflects the later stages of myocardial injury, at which point treatment options are typically limited [9–11]. Studies in miniature pig models suggest that prolonged T2 time, with normal T1 and ECV, may signal intracellular edema in cardiomyocytes, serving as a potential early and reversible imaging marker for DIC [12, 13].However, whether T2 prolongation can serve as a marker for clinical intervention and improve patient outcomes remains inconclusive. These findings prompt further investigation into the mechanisms and clinical outcomes of DIC in clinical settings, as well as the development of more selective imaging techniques for early diagnosis. This approach helps balance the risks and benefits between cancer treatment and cardiac health [14]. Miniature pigs, due to the high similarity of their cardiac anatomical structure and physiological functions to those of humans, are considered an ideal model for studying heart diseases and assessing drug-induced cardiotoxicity [15–17].This similarity ensures the translatability of research findings, providing a solid foundation for clinical applications [18].

The aim of this study is to construct a chronic myocardial injury model in miniature pigs induced by Doxorubicin, based on simulated clinical dosing regimens. Continuous and long-term follow-up using multimodal cardiac magnetic resonance imaging is performed both during and after Doxorubicin administration to evaluate its impact on the structure and function of the porcine heart. Simultaneously, detailed pathological analysis is performed to validate the cardiac magnetic resonance imaging findings and explore the underlying pathological mechanisms of DIC.

Materials and methods

Study design

Figure 1 summarizes the experimental design of this study. The study was approved by the Institutional Animal Research Committee (approval No. 2020062 A), which serves as the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of our institution, and was conducted in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and Directive 2010/63/EU. All Bama miniature pigs were obtained from an accredited experimental animal breeding center and were institutionally owned. Therefore, informed consent from animal owners was not applicable. In this study, a total of 18 pigs (Bama miniature pig) were treated with doxorubicin, with an additional 2 pigs serving as normal controls for baseline pathological data collection. The pigs were sexually mature females, aged 4–5 months, and weighed 15–20 kg. All animals underwent a one-week acclimatization period prior to the commencement of the experiment.

The 18 pigs receiving doxorubicin were euthanized and tissue collected at predefined time points: three pigs at 4 weeks, three at 8 weeks, three at 12 weeks, three at 20 weeks, and three at 32 weeks. One pig died unexpectedly at 6 weeks and two at 23 weeks, preventing electron microscopy analysis (Fig. 1). Only planned tissue samples were included in the ultrastructural evaluation. The two control pigs, not treated with doxorubicin, were euthanized at baseline to provide reference pathological data. Doxorubicin was administered intravenously at a dose of 60 mg/m2 every two weeks for a total of four doses (iv, q2w, 60 mg/m2). Cardiac MR scans were performed at baseline (week 0) and at two-week intervals during doxorubicin administration (weeks 2, 4, 6, and 8). Following the completion of doxorubicin administration, cardiac MR scans were repeated at 1 month (week 12), 3 months (week 20), and 6 months (week 32).

Throughout the medication and post-medication phases, the physiological states of the pigs, including respiration, body temperature, general behavior, and skin condition, were closely monitored and recorded. All experimental procedures were designed to minimize discomfort and distress to the animals, considering animal welfare throughout the study [19].

Animal model construction

Anesthesia protocol

Animals were anesthetized through intramuscular injections of atropine (0.02–0.05 mg/kg) and stresnil (4–6 mg/kg). During the anesthesia, the entire body of the animal was covered with warming blankets for thermoregulation. Appropriate endotracheal intubation was performed to secure the airway, which was then connected to a ventilator. The ventilator settings were adjusted based on the miniature pigs’ weight and physiological condition, with a tidal volume set at 8 ml/kg and a respiratory rate at 15 breaths per minute, with an inspiratory/expiratory ratio of 1/2. Isoflurane was used to maintain anesthesia, with concentrations adjusted between 1.5% and 3% based on monitored vital signs and the animal’s response. This approach allowed for timely adjustments to the concentration of isoflurane and ventilator settings to ensure appropriate and stable depth of anesthesia.

Doxorubicin administration

Doxorubicin powder was dissolved in 0.9% saline to achieve a final concentration of 2 mg/mL and was used immediately after preparation, stored at room temperature protected from light. For modeling, the prepared doxorubicin solution was further diluted to 0.5 mg/mL to ensure suitability for intravenous injection. Venous access was established via the auricular margin veins for the administration of doxorubicin at a clinical single-dose of 60 mg/m² to the miniature pigs every two weeks, alternating between different sides of the auricular margin veins to avoid vascular and skin complications.

Cardiac MR Scan and image analysis

Prior to imaging, animals underwent a 12-hour fast with water withheld for 4 h. All cardiac MR examinations were performed on a 3.0T clinical scanner (Magnetom Skyra, Siemens Healthineers) equipped with an 18-channel body coil, following standardized cardiac MR protocols [20]. Retrospective ECG gating was applied throughout the acquisition. During CMR acquisition, heart rate was continuously monitored under general anesthesia and was generally maintained within 70–100 beats/min. T2 mapping was performed with ECG triggering during end-expiratory ventilator suspension when feasible. No additional motion-correction algorithm beyond ECG triggering and ventilator suspension was applied.

Balanced steady-state free precession (bSSFP) cine sequences were acquired in short-axis and long-axis views. Imaging parameters included: field of view 280 × 280 mm, slice thickness 6 mm, voxel size 1.8 × 1.8 mm, temporal resolution approximately 40 ms (25 phases per cardiac cycle), TR/TE 2.8/1.4 ms, flip angle 45°, and bandwidth 930 Hz/pixel. The short-axis stack covered the entire left ventricle from base to apex with 8–12 contiguous slices. T2 mapping was performed using a gradient-recalled echo (GRE) sequence with the following parameters: field of view 300 × 300 mm, slice thickness 6 mm, voxel size 1.8 × 2.0 mm, three echo times (2.4, 4.8, and 7.2 ms), TR 10 ms, and flip angle 15°. The acquisition was triggered to mid-diastole with appropriate adjustment for heart rate variations. T2 maps were automatically reconstructed using the manufacturer’s inline software (MyoMaps, Siemens Healthineers), with regions of interest manually drawn on the myocardium while carefully excluding blood pool and imaging artifacts.

Cardiac MR data processing was performed using CVI42 software (Circle Cardiovascular Imaging). End-diastolic and end-systolic LV borders were manually contoured in short-axis views to derive LVEF, left ventricular end-diastolic volume (LVEDV), left ventricular end-systolic volume (LVESV), and left ventricular mass (LVM). Feature tracking analysis was applied to two-chamber and four-chamber long-axis views, automatically generating three-dimensional strain parameters including left ventricular global longitudinal strain (LVGLS), left ventricular global radial strain (LVGRS), and left ventricular global circumferential strain (LVGCS). For T2 mapping analysis, manual delineation of endocardial and epicardial contours was performed while carefully excluding left ventricular papillary muscles to avoid blood pool interference. Following mapping reconstruction, the software generated quantitative T2 images with corresponding relaxation time values, and all results were exported in standardized report format for further statistical analysis. All imaging analyses were performed by one observer with experience in cardiac MR image analysis, who was blinded to imaging timepoints and histopathological results. To assess reproducibility, all measurements were repeated in a subset of randomly selected examinations by the same observer and by a second independent observer. Intra-observer and inter-observer reproducibility were evaluated using intraclass correlation coefficients.

Euthanasia and tissue collection

At predefined time points, animals were deeply anesthetized prior to euthanasia. General anesthesia was induced and maintained using isoflurane (1.5–3.0%) delivered via endotracheal intubation, as described in the anesthesia protocol above. Adequate depth of anesthesia was confirmed by the absence of corneal and pain reflexes. While under deep anesthesia, euthanasia was performed by intravenous administration of a concentrated potassium chloride (KCl) solution, resulting in rapid cardiac arrest. Immediately thereafter, the hearts were rapidly excised for subsequent histological and ultrastructural analyses. All procedures were conducted in accordance with institutional animal care guidelines and established animal welfare recommendations, with efforts made to minimize pain and distress.

Histological assessment

After extraction, the hearts were immediately placed on a cooled dissection board. Following the American Heart Association’s guidelines, hearts were quickly divided into 17 segments using chilled surgical instruments to minimize tissue damage. Each section, approximately 2 × 5 × 10 mm, was placed on chilled slides and submerged in 3% glutaraldehyde at 4 °C for fixation.

For light microscopic histological assessment, myocardial tissue samples were fixed, dehydrated, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned. Paraffin sections were cut at a thickness of 4 μm using a rotary microtome (Leica RM2235, Leica Biosystems, Germany). The sections were then subjected to Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) and Masson’s trichrome staining, coverslipped, and examined under a light microscope to assess myocardial tissue architecture and pathological changes. Digital images were obtained using a Pannoramic 250 scanner (3DHISTECH, Hungary) at 40x magnification, with further imaging of selected areas at 200x magnifications for detailed analysis. Masson’s trichrome staining was first viewed at 40x magnification, and three representative areas were imaged at 200x for fibrous tissue evaluation. Fibrosis was quantified using Image-Pro Plus 6.0 software, calculating the percentage of stained area based on integrated optical density (IOD) and area measurements.

For transmission electron microscopy, ultrathin sections of approximately 70 nm were prepared using an ultramicrotome (Leica EM UC7, Leica Microsystems, Germany). The sections were examined using a JEM-1400FLASH transmission electron microscope (JEOL, Japan) for ultrastructural analysis. Initial wide-field observations were conducted at 2000x magnification to overview each grid. Specific regions were then selected for detailed imaging at 5000x and 8000x magnifications to assess cellular or subcellular pathologies. Experienced pathologists evaluated the frequency and severity of doxorubicin-induced cardiac lesions, focusing on mitochondrial swelling, cristae alterations, matrix density changes, and outer membrane integrity [21], as well as myofibrillar loss, breaks, and cytoplasmic vacuolation. The Billingham scoring system was applied to score these findings.

Statistical analysis

In this study, we utilized Generalized Estimating Equations (GEE) with natural cubic splines and an independence working correlation structure to evaluate how time influences cardiac function metrics and explore their potential nonlinear relationships. Variability in measures such as LVEF, LVGLS, LVGRS, LVGCS, and T2 relaxation times was assessed using the Friedman test, suitable for non-normally distributed related samples. Significant findings prompted further analysis with Dunn’s post-hoc test with Bonferroni correction for specific time-point comparisons. Missing data resulting from unexpected animal deaths were not imputed, and analyses were performed using available observations at each time point. All statistical procedures were conducted in R Studio, employing packages such as gee for GEE, splines for spline transformations, and ggplot2 for visualization. The significance level for all tests was set at 0.05.

Results

During doxorubicin administration, one pig exhibited generalized erythema and cardiac arrest (successfully resuscitated but later died). Two additional deaths occurred post-treatment. Seventeen pigs completed four biweekly doxorubicin doses (weeks 0, 2, 4, and 6). Scheduled multiparametric cardiac MR was performed prior to euthanasia, with hearts harvested for histopathological analysis.

Cardiac MR structural and functional changes

Cardiac MR structural parameters

During doxorubicin treatment, LVM demonstrated a progressive increase (χ²=41.7, P < 0.001), with significant elevation beginning at 4 weeks (adjusted P = 0.001) and reaching peak levels at 8 weeks (adjusted P < 0.001). LVEDV (χ²=3.02, P = 0.554) and LVESV (χ²=3.73, P = 0.443) remained stable during treatment.

During post-treatment follow-up, LVM continued to increase (χ²=29.925, P < 0.001), becoming statistically significant at 12 weeks (adjusted P = 0.0375) and peaking at 32 weeks (adjusted P < 0.001). Similarly, both LVEDV (χ²=22.6, P < 0.001) and LVESV (χ²=25.52, P < 0.001) showed significant upward trends during follow-up. The volume increases became apparent at 12 weeks (LVESV: adjusted P = 0.007) and were most pronounced at 20 weeks (LVEDV: adjusted P = 0.007; LVESV: adjusted P < 0.001) and 32 weeks (LVEDV: adjusted P = 0.002; LVESV: adjusted P = 0.001).

During the administration period, LVEF showed no significant temporal variation (χ²=0.4, P = 0.982). LV global strain

Left ventricular strain analysis revealed distinct temporal patterns during and after doxorubicin treatment (Figs. 2 and 3). During the treatment period, LVGLS showed a non-significant downward trend (ns(Time, df = 3)1: β=-1.869, P = 0.251; ns(Time, df = 3)2: β=-3.187, P = 0.021; ns(Time, df = 3)3: β=-1.052, P = 0.150, χ²=8.76, P = 0.0675), while neither LVGCS (χ²=5.07, P = 0.281) nor LVGRS (χ²=2.04, P = 0.728) demonstrated significant changes.

However, LVGCS exhibited a downward trend (ns(Time, df = 3)1: β=-3.771, P = 0.006; ns(Time, df = 3)2: β=-3.589, P = 0.22; ns(Time, df = 3)3: β=-2.113, P < 0.001), and LVGRS showed a downward trend (ns(Time, df = 3)1: β=-10.07, P = 0.035; ns(Time, df = 3)2: β=-12.87, P = 0.04; ns(Time, df = 3)3: β=-6.96, P = 0.001, χ²=9.16, P = 0.057).

T2 relaxation time dynamics

T2 relaxation time exhibited a progressive increase during doxorubicin treatment (ns(Time, df = 3)1: β = 1.685, P = 0.024; ns(Time, df = 3)2: β = 2.065, P = 0.144; ns(Time, df = 3)3: β = 2.490, P = 0.015). Non-parametric repeated measures ANOVA indicated significant differences in T2 relaxation time during the doxorubicin treatment period (χ²=10.5, P = 0.033), though post-hoc pairwise comparisons did not reveal significant differences between groups (all P values > 0.05). During follow-up, T2 relaxation time showed partial recovery, decreasing significantly to 36.74 ± 2.80 ms by week 32 (χ²=15.5, P = 0.020), with the most pronounced reduction occurring in late follow-up (β=-2.197, P = 0.003).

Histopathological validation

Myocardial structural changes

Doxorubicin treatment induced time-dependent structural alterations. At 4 weeks, myocardial cells maintained compact architecture with only occasional vacuolization. Vacuolization became more prominent by 8 weeks (Fig. 4; Table 1), but resolved completely by 32 weeks follow-up, with cells showing normal tight arrangement. Fibrosis quantification by Masson staining revealed stable levels throughout the study period (baseline: 2.25 ± 0.21%; 4 weeks: 2.57 ± 0.56%; 8 weeks: 2.3 ± 0.1%; 32 weeks: 2.33 ± 0.66%; all P > 0.05).

Myocardial ultrastructural changes

Electron microscopic examination demonstrated time-dependent ultrastructural changes in myocardial tissue (Fig. 5). At baseline (0 weeks), all specimens (2/2, 100%) exhibited normal morphology (Billingham grade 0). By 4 weeks post-treatment, 67% of samples (2/3) remained normal, while 33% (1/3) showed early mitochondrial changes (grade 0.5). The 8-week timepoint revealed the most heterogeneous findings, with equal distribution between normal (1/3, 33%), grade 0.5 (1/3, 33%), and grade 1 (1/3, 33%) morphologies. Subsequent examinations at 12 and 20 weeks showed partial recovery, with 67% of specimens returning to normal architecture at 20 weeks. The final evaluation at 32 weeks demonstrated persistent normal ultrastructure in 67% of samples (2/3), with the remaining 33% (1/3) showing residual grade 0.5 alterations. Notably, myocardial fibers exhibited sparse and uneven distribution at 32 weeks without evidence of structural discontinuity. No specimens progressed beyond grade 1 involvement at any timepoint, and characteristic features of advanced cardiotoxicity (grades ≥ 2) were not observed.

Reproducibility analysis

The intra-observer ICCs for LVGLS, LVGCS, and LVGRS were 0.94 (95% CI: 0.88–0.97), 0.96 (95% CI: 0.91–0.98), and 0.92 (95% CI: 0.84–0.96), respectively. The corresponding inter-observer ICCs were 0.91 (95% CI: 0.82–0.95), 0.93 (95% CI: 0.86–0.97), and 0.89 (95% CI: 0.78–0.94), respectively.

Discussion

Current cardiotoxicity monitoring relies on late-stage indicators like LVEF, yet most patients show incomplete functional recovery post-treatment. While T2 mapping shows promise for early detection, clinical evidence remains limited. This study effectively establishes a doxorubicin-induced chronic myocardial injury model in miniature pigs, closely mirroring clinical conditions. Serial Cardiac MR demonstrated that while global cardiac function remained stable during treatment, subtle myocardial injury was detected through T2 mapping and strain analysis. T2 mapping detected early myocardial injury during doxorubicin treatment, correlating with mitochondrial damage, while showing partial recovery during follow-up. In contrast, ventricular strain remained stable during treatment but progressively declined post-treatment, consistent with ultrastructural evidence of myocardial remodeling.

Ekaterina Podyacheva and colleagues established acute and chronic cardiomyopathy models in rats using different doses of Doxorubicin, which enabled a detailed examination of Doxorubicin-induced cardiomyopathy and fibrosis [22]. Similarly, Naresh NK and others used small animal models to study left ventricular function and strain changes, highlighting significant cardiac effects during Doxorubicin treatment, including reduced left ventricular mass, decreased ejection fraction, and lowered strain [23–25]. However, clinical translation of these findings is challenging due to individual variability, treatment complexity, and differences between animal models and human physiology. In larger animal models, Nakamori et al. were the first to apply sequential cardiac MR in miniature pigs, reinforcing cardiac MR’s effectiveness in early detection of Doxorubicin cardiotoxicity [12]. Yet, the intracoronary Doxorubicin delivery in this model may intensify cardiac effects, limiting applicability to clinical settings where systemic factors also contribute. Furthermore, the narrow 2–3 week window between T2 elevation and LVEF decline restricts this model’s utility in capturing potentially reversible stages of cardiotoxicity. In this study, Bama miniature pigs were selected as an experimental model to study Doxorubicin-induced cardiotoxicity. Four- to five-month-old sexually mature females were chosen to minimize hormonal and developmental variability that could impact results. At this age, their cardiac physiology and drug response are stable and predictable, providing a controlled, standardized model for cardiotoxicity research. Clinical dosing was simulated to align closely with real-world scenarios, and a 32-week follow-up—among the longest in large animal studies—enabled detailed observation of Doxorubicin’s long-term cardiac effects. This design may improves translational relevance, but the clinical applicability of these findings requires further validation in human cardio-oncology cohorts.

The 2022 ESC guidelines highlight that CTR-CVT risk varies and may increase over time, with a small peak in acute myocardial injury risk during Doxorubicin treatment and a steady rise post-treatment [26, 27]. Accurate LVEF measurement is critical in cardio-oncology, with recommendations to discontinue Doxorubicin if LVEF drops by 10% from baseline or below 55% to prevent further dysfunction [28]. Compared to LVEF, strain analysis is more sensitive for early cardiotoxicity detection, especially in chemotherapy-induced ventricular dysfunction, and is now widely recommended for patient monitoring. The SUCCOUR trial’s three-year follow-up showed no significant EF difference between EF-guided and GLS-guided cardioprotection, with both groups showing stable [29]. A study in 136 breast cancer patients found early cardiac MR biomarker peaks for inflammation and edema, linked to ventricular remodeling, though temporary and unrelated to LVEF or strain [30]. In our Doxorubicin-treated miniature pig model, LVEF, LV end-diastolic, and end-systolic volumes showed no significant trends during treatment, consistent with Naresh et al.‘s murine findings. However, GLS decreased, especially in later stages, with circumferential and radial strain also declining during follow-up. These MRI changes corresponded with early pathologies, such as mild vacuolization and mitochondrial swelling, and hinted at ongoing myocardial remodeling, progressing from subclinical injury to visible dysfunction by week 32.

Current multiparametric cardiac MR techniques offer a unique perspective on the myocardial effects of doxorubicin, especially at early disease stages. Unlike traditional markers such as LVEF and global longitudinal strain (GLS), T2 mapping can detect subtle, potentially reversible intracellular edema within cardiomyocytes. Prolonged T2 relaxation time may reflect early-stage, potential reversible cellular edema and therefore holds potential as an early imaging marker of myocardial injury. A recent study in a pig model of cardiotoxicity identified early cardiac MR tissue changes linked to histopathology [12]. Besides T1 mapping and ECV, T2 mapping detected myocardial edema. With coronary-administered Doxorubicin (0.45 mg/kg per dose, three to five doses), weekly cardiac MR revealed T2 prolongation as the earliest marker by week 6, while T1, ECV, and LVEF remained unchanged, suggesting the absence of interstitial edema. Histopathology, however, showed vacuolization, indicating intracellular edema. Discontinuing Dox upon T2 elevation prevented further LV dysfunction and reversed vacuolization.

In our study, T2 significantly increased during Dox administration, pointing to myocardial edema as an early injury marker, with histopathology showing mild vacuolization and mitochondrial swelling. During follow-up, T2 returned near baseline, suggesting partial resolution of acute myocardial injury, although this should not be interpreted as complete structural recovery. T2 elevation occurred in the absence of significant fibrotic progression, whereas long-term follow-up revealed myocardial fiber disarray. Given the absence of significant changes in collagen volume fraction, these late pathological changes should be interpreted primarily as ultrastructural alterations, including myocardial fiber disorganization, rather than fibrotic progression. T2 mapping may capture subclinical myocardial edema and provide complementary information for early cardiotoxicity assessment. However, its short detection window and uncertain relationship with long-term remodeling warrant further study, particularly in human cardio-oncology cohorts.

Limitations

Our study has several limitations. First, although the miniature pig model closely resembles human cardiac anatomy and physiology, interspecies differences remain, so findings may not fully translate to human disease management. Second, the sample size was limited by the ethical, logistical, and cost constraints inherent to longitudinal large-animal studies, which may reduce statistical power; additionally, the exclusive use of healthy adult female pigs may affect the generalizability of the findings. Third, although baseline CMR measurements served as internal references for within-animal longitudinal comparisons, no longitudinal untreated imaging control group was included, partly in consideration of ethical constraints and reduction of animal use in large-animal studies. Therefore, temporal changes in strain and T2 values cannot be completely separated from potential effects related to age-related growth, repeated anesthesia, or serial imaging variability. Lastly, despite a 32-week follow-up, further research is needed to understand the long-term effects and recovery from Doxorubicin-induced cardiotoxicity.

Conclusion

Building upon previous findings that T2 relaxation time may serve as an early marker of myocardial injury, this miniature swine model showed progressive T2 prolongation during doxorubicin treatment, followed by a decrease toward baseline after treatment cessation. These T2 changes were temporally associated with mitochondrial swelling and intracellular vacuolization, suggesting potentially reversible components of myocardial injury. However, persistent myocardial fiber disorganization and late-phase strain deterioration despite T2 normalization indicate incomplete structural recovery. Combined T2 mapping and strain assessment may provide complementary information for characterizing doxorubicin-related myocardial injury in preclinical settings, while clinical applicability requires further validation in human cardio-oncology cohorts.
