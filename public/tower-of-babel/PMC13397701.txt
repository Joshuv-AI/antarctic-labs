Sepsis-related immunosuppression: a comprehensive review of current research breakthroughs
Authors: Shuai Ren, Jun Wen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis article focuses on Sepsis-related immunosuppression, systematically expounding on its pathological mechanisms, monitoring methods, and treatment strategies. Immunosuppression in sepsis involves multi-level functional exhaustion of the innate and adaptive immune systems, such as dysfunctions of monocytes/macrophages, neutrophils, and dendritic cells, depletion of T and B cells, and imbalance of the cytokine network. The monitoring of the immune state mainly relies on the detection of the quantity and function of immune cells, HLA-DR expression, and cytokine levels. Treatment methods include immunostimulation (such as IL-7, GM-CSF), immune checkpoint inhibitors, other immunomodulatory therapies (such as IFN-γ, mesenchymal stem cells), and intravenous immunoglobulin. However, the clinical transformation effects of existing treatment strategies remain to be verified. In-depth research on sepsis-related immunosuppression is of great significance for improving clinical diagnosis and treatment and reducing mortality.Importantly, the pathogenesis and trajectory of immunosuppression in children differ significantly from adults due to age‑specific immune development, necessitating a separate discussion of pediatric‑specific mechanisms and monitoring strategies.Keywords: Sepsis, Immunosuppression, Cytokine storm

Sepsis (sepsis 3.0) is a life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection. Sepsis is diagnosed when the Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score increases by at least 2 points [1]. Recent epidemiological data indicate that sepsis affects approximately 48.9 million individuals globally each year, among whom 11 million people die from sepsis and sepsis-related complications. There are 20 million cases of pediatric sepsis, and the number of sepsis-related deaths in children under 5 years old reaches 2.9 million, accounting for 19.7% of all global deaths. The threat of sepsis to children’s survival cannot be ignored [2]. When a sepsis patient requires the use of vasopressors to maintain a mean arterial blood pressure of 65 mmHg or higher, with a serum lactate level exceeding 2 mmol/L (18 mg/dL), and there is no hypovolemia, it is defined as septic shock [1]. Most pediatric septic shock cases exhibit rapid clinical deterioration, with approximately 50% of fatalities occurring within 48 h of hospitalization [3–6]. However, most of the current understanding of sepsis‑induced immunosuppression is derived from adult studies. Historically, adult sepsis has been conceptualized as a biphasic trajectory: an initial hyperinflammatory “cytokine storm” followed by a prolonged immunosuppressive phase. However, accumulating evidence challenges this long-standing dogma. Recent studies demonstrate that not all adult patients present with a dominant hyperinflammatory state at ICU admission; instead, hyperinflammation and immunosuppression frequently coexist from the earliest stages of critical illness, and immunosuppressive features may persist into the recovery phase in a subset of patients [7]. Children, especially neonates and infants, exhibit distinct immune ontogeny that fundamentally alters the host response to sepsis. Consistent with the heterogeneous adult phenotype, children may develop concurrent hyperinflammation and immunosuppression within the first 72 h of PICU admission [8], with an even higher prevalence of early simultaneous immune dysfunction than that observed in adult cohorts [9]. This overlapping trajectory, alongside the simultaneous rather than sequential onset of multiple organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS), contributes to significantly higher early mortality (within 72 h) in pediatric sepsis relative to adults [10]. These differences challenge the direct extrapolation of adult immunomodulatory strategies to pediatric populations and underscore the need for a dedicated review on sepsis-related immunosuppression.

Notably, approximately 50% of pediatric septic shock deaths occur within 48 h of hospitalization [11], suggesting that immunosuppression may develop much earlier in children than in adults. Traditional sepsis pathophysiology has long divided the disease into discrete, sequential inflammatory and immunosuppressive phases, a framework originally derived from adult clinical and preclinical models. However, this biphasic model is increasingly debated. As noted above, concurrent hyperinflammation and immunosuppression are common in adult ICU patients at presentation, rather than occurring as strictly separate stages [7]. Furthermore, the clinical impact of prolonged post-sepsis immunosuppression remains incompletely resolved: while immunosuppression is associated with increased risk of secondary infections and late mortality in some cohorts, evidence from septic shock survivors indicates that patients who survive the initial acute phase do not predominantly die from nosocomial infections — a finding that challenges the assumption that a sustained immunosuppressive state is the primary driver of prolonged morbidity in most sepsis survivors [12].

In contrast to the heterogeneous adult trajectory, pediatric sepsis more consistently presents with concurrent or dynamically alternating hyperinflammation and immunosuppression within the early disease course [13]. Sepsis patients may die at either early or late time points due to either unchecked inflammation or immunosuppression-related secondary infections and pathogen persistence. In recent years, the occurrence of immunosuppression has increasingly been recognized as a key factor in sepsis mortality, though its relative contribution varies across patient populations and disease timepoints. Therefore, understanding the pathophysiology of immunosuppression in sepsis is crucial for the prevention and treatment of sepsis [14, 15].

Under normal circumstances, the host’s immune response to infection is a balanced process, including both pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory responses. In sepsis, the balance between pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory responses is disrupted. The pro-inflammatory response exceeds the normal homeostatic limits and turns into a systemic inflammatory process, leading to inflammation in tissues far from the source of infection [16].Specifically, the innate immune system utilizes pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) to identify pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) and damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) [17]. Among them, Toll-like receptors (TLRs) are important PRRs. For example, TLR2, TLR4, and TLR9 can bind to high-mobility group box 1 protein (HMGB1), initiating the immune response signaling pathway, activating immune cells, and prompting them to secrete cytokines and chemokines. Chemokines guide immune cells such as neutrophils, monocytes, and macrophages to migrate to the site of infection or injury [18].

Multiple components of the innate immune system exhibit significant bidirectional regulatory characteristics during the maintenance of immune homeostasis. When the inflammatory response gets out of control, an oxidative stress cascade reaction can be induced through the following mechanisms: On the one hand, overly activated inflammatory cells (such as neutrophils and mononuclear macrophages) produce a large amount of reactive oxygen species (ROS). On the other hand, pro-inflammatory mediators (such as TNF-α and IL-1β) can inhibit the expression and activity of the intracellular antioxidant defense system (including key enzyme systems such as SOD and CAT), ultimately leading to the imbalance of the oxidative-antioxidative dynamic equilibrium.Under this pathological condition, oxidative stress products and pro-inflammatory cytokines form a synergistic effect, triggering vascular endothelial dysfunction. Its clinical characteristic manifestation is the capillary leak syndrome, and its typical pathological changes include: ① Plasma proteins and liquid components extravasate into the tissue space, causing systemic edema; ② A decrease in the effective circulating blood volume leads to organ hypoperfusion; ③ Endothelial injury products enter the circulatory system, further activating the inflammatory and coagulation cascade reactions.It is worth emphasizing that persistent endothelial dysfunction can exacerbate microcirculatory disorders through the following mechanisms: ① Endothelial swelling and microthrombus formation lead to a reduction in the effective cross-sectional area of the microvascular bed; ② An imbalance between vasodilatory substances (such as NO) and vasoconstrictive substances (such as ET-1) triggers abnormal vasomotor function.The above changes ultimately lead to tissue hypoperfusion and an ischemic and hypoxic state, which in turn triggers the imbalance of the coagulation-anticoagulation equilibrium: On the one hand, the hypoxic environment induces the abnormal expression of tissue factor (TF), initiating the extrinsic coagulation pathway; on the other hand, endothelial injury leads to the impairment of the anticoagulant regulation system (such as the PC/S-TM complex). The excessive activation of this coagulation cascade and the inhibition of the anticoagulant mechanism work together, ultimately forming disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) characterized by the widespread formation of microthrombi [19].This pathological process not only exacerbates microcirculatory disorders but also, due to the excessive consumption of coagulation factors and platelets, can lead to a bleeding tendency, forming a vicious cycle (Fig. 1).

Pathophysiology of microvascular injury and DIC Formation

Sepsis-related immunosuppression, as the core pathological mechanism leading to the high mortality rate of patients, is essentially a compensatory immunosuppressive state triggered by the body after an excessive inflammatory response. It involves the functional exhaustion at multiple levels and the imbalance of the regulatory network in both the innate and adaptive immune systems.It is worth emphasizing that this pathological process weakens the host’s defense ability through multi-dimensional mechanisms:①Dysfunction of innate immune cells;②Exhaustion of the adaptive immune response;③Imbalance of the immune regulatory network.The cascade effect of this multi-system dysfunction ultimately leads to a significant decline in the ability to clear pathogens, an increased risk of endogenous opportunistic infections, and obstruction of the tissue repair process. Consequently, the risk of death increases [20, 21].

This article will analyze in detail the research foundation and progress of immunosuppression in sepsis, revealing the complex biological logic behind immunosuppression. It will help clinicians better understand sepsis-induced immunosuppression and provide assistance for the monitoring of the immune status in sepsis and the development of treatment strategies for immunosuppression.

Mechanisms of sepsis-induced immunosuppression

The functional inhibition of the innate immune system

Dysfunction of monocytes/macrophages

Decrease in antigen-presenting ability

During sepsis, the expression of HLA-DR on the surface of monocytes is significantly reduced. HLA-DR belongs to the major histocompatibility complex class II molecules (MHC II), which plays a crucial role in the antigen presentation process. Under normal circumstances, after monocytes uptake antigens such as pathogens, they present the antigen peptide-MHC II complex on the cell surface for T cells to recognize, thereby initiating the adaptive immune response.However, in the state of sepsis, on the one hand, the expression of HLA-DR on the cell surface decreases, reducing the presentation of the antigen peptide-MHC II complex. At the same time, the expression of other costimulatory molecules is abnormal, making it difficult for T cells to normally recognize antigens and be fully activated, which greatly inhibits the adaptive immune response. On the other hand, there is an imbalance in cytokine secretion. The secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF and IL-1β decreases, thus weakening the autoimmune function of monocytes. In contrast, the secretion of the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 increases. It inhibits the activation of monocytes and the expression of molecules such as HLA-DR, further suppressing the immune response. Eventually, it leads to a significant decrease in the antigen-presenting ability of monocytes [17].

Pediatric considerations: In children with sepsis, monocytes/macrophages exhibit a unique cytokine profile compared to adults. Stimulation of pediatric peritoneal macrophages with LPS results in a 15‑fold higher IL‑10/TNF‑α ratio than that observed in adult macrophages, indicating a stronger anti‑inflammatory feedback propensity. This exaggerated anti‑inflammatory response may predispose children to early immunoparalysis despite a concurrent hyperinflammatory state. Furthermore, HLA‑DR downregulation on monocytes has been documented as early as 72 h after PICU admission in children with severe sepsis, correlating with prolonged organ dysfunction.

Polarization phenotype transformation

The M2 macrophages are an anti-inflammatory phenotype of macrophages and participate in immunosuppression by secreting interleukin-4 (IL-4) and interleukin-10 (IL-10). IL-4 inhibits the phagocytic and bactericidal functions of macrophages. IL-10 serves as a hub for the interaction among M2 macrophages, regulatory T cells (Tregs), and myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) [22].M2 macrophage-derived IL-10 drives Treg expansion, while Treg-secreted cytokines reciprocally enhance M2 polarization, establishing a self-amplifying immunosuppressive feedback loop. Moreover, IL-10 also stimulates the expansion of MDSCs by upregulating the expression of programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) and programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) [23]. MDSCs inhibit the proliferation of T lymphocytes and promote their apoptosis, suppress the maturation of dendritic cells, induce the phenotype of M2 macrophages, and facilitate the expansion of Tregs, collectively exacerbating immunosuppression [24].

Dysfunction of neutrophils

Decreased chemotaxis

When the body is stimulated by factors such as pathogen invasion or tissue injury, damaged tissue cells and immune cells release inflammatory mediators, such as tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interleukin-1 (IL-1), etc., which promote the activation of vascular endothelial cells and the expression of adhesion molecules. Neutrophils in the blood circulation adhere tightly to the ligands on the surface of endothelial cells under the action of activation signals such as chemokines. Subsequently, neutrophils migrate through the endothelial cells and penetrate the blood vessel wall, and then chemotactically move along the concentration gradient of chemokines towards the site of inflammation. Once they arrive, they are activated, and they clear pathogens by phagocytosis and releasing bactericidal substances, thus exerting their immune defense function.Numerous studies have shown that although the levels of chemotactic molecules in the infection foci of septic patients and septic mice are relatively high, the number of neutrophils in these areas does not increase significantly, suggesting that there is a dysfunction in the migration of neutrophils during sepsis [25, 26]. The expression levels of Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4), chemokine receptor CXCR2, and other molecules in septic patients who died are often lower than those in septic patients who survived [27] (Fig. 2).

Cytokine storm and immune cell migration

Increased release of extracellular traps

Neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) are composed of DNA, histones, and serine proteases [28]. The release of NETs by neutrophils enhances vascular inflammation and coagulation, and NET release does not always lead to neutrophil death. NETs can capture pathogens, thus contributing to the elimination of pathogens. However, NETs can also cause collateral tissue damage and thrombosis. Elevated circulating NETs correlate with multi-organ dysfunction in sepsis [29]. NETs can serve as a scaffold for the entrapment and aggregation of platelets and red blood cells, thereby promoting coagulation and thrombosis. This function is mediated by the binding of NETs to proteins such as fibrinogen, fibronectin, and von Willebrand factor. Therefore, the disassembly of NETs can reduce thrombosis in animal models [28].

Pediatric considerations: Neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) play an especially prominent role in pediatric sepsis. Children with sepsis have significantly higher serum concentrations of NETs than adults, and NET levels positively correlate with disease severity. The exaggerated NETosis in children may contribute to the simultaneous multi‑organ dysfunction and early mortality observed in this population. Additionally, neonatal neutrophils exhibit lower TLR4 expression and impaired chemotaxis due to reduced downstream signaling molecules (e.g., MyD88), which limits early cytokine responses but does not prevent late NET‑mediated tissue damage.

Induce apoptosis of lymphocytes

Studies have shown that neutrophils have a negative regulatory effect on lymphocytes [30]. Sepsis induces the upregulation of programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) in neutrophils. PD-L1 + neutrophils have lower levels of cluster of differentiation 11a (CD11a), cluster of differentiation 62 ligand (CD62L), and C-C chemokine receptor type 2 (CCR2), while having higher levels of cluster of differentiation 16 (CD16) and cluster of differentiation 64 (CD64). This phenotypic change is associated with impaired migratory ability of neutrophils. Moreover, neutrophils can induce the apoptosis of lymphocytes through PD-L1 mediation.In clinical studies, it has also been found that the level of PD-L1 in neutrophils is negatively correlated with the level of human leukocyte antigen-DR (HLA-DR) in monocytes, and positively correlated with the severity of illness in septic patients. This implies that the upregulation of PD-L1 is involved in the process of sepsis-induced immunosuppression, and has an important impact on the development and prognosis of the disease [31].

Depletion of dendritic cells (DCs)

Dendritic cells (DCs) are antigen-presenting cells that bridge innate immunity and adaptive immunity. In sepsis, various factors may induce apoptosis of DCs, resulting in a decrease in the number of DCs. For example, the inflammatory response triggered by an infection may release some pro-apoptotic factors that directly act on DCs and initiate the apoptosis program. In recent years, with the increasing research on regulated cell death (RCD), the currently known types of RCD can be divided into several subtypes according to their molecular basis, including apoptosis, necroptosis, pyroptosis, ferroptosis, autophagy-dependent cell death (ADCD), and so on [32].

Apoptosis: It is an important cause of the reduction in the number of DCs in sepsis. It is mediated through the intrinsic mitochondrial pathway and the extrinsic death receptor pathway and is affected by multiple signaling pathways. The apoptosis of splenic dendritic cells can be induced by the activation of the TLR4 and TLR2 signaling pathways. This process involves the following molecular mechanisms: Through a TLR4-dependent but myeloid differentiation primary response gene 88 (MyD88)-independent signal transduction pathway, the expression and activation of interferon regulatory factor 1 (IRF1) are regulated [33].

Necroptosis: It is mediated by the activation of the RIPK1-RIPK3-MLKL pathway by members of the TNFR and TLR families, etc. It releases damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), exacerbating the inflammatory response [34].

Pyroptosis: Initiated by the inflammasome, it is dependent on the inflammatory caspases and the Gasdermin protein family.

Ferroptosis: Induced by iron-dependent phospholipid oxidation, it affects the maturation of DCs. Ferroptosis occurs in DCs in the state of sepsis [35].

Autophagy-dependent cell death (ADCD): It relies on the autophagic mechanism and includes types such as excessive autophagy, excessive organelle - specific autophagy, and autophagic death [36].

Pediatric considerations: The developmental immaturity of dendritic cells in neonates and young infants may render them more susceptible to sepsis‑induced depletion. However, direct comparative studies on DC apoptosis, necroptosis, or ferroptosis between pediatric and adult sepsis are lacking, representing a significant knowledge gap.

Widespread suppression of the adaptive immune system

T cell exhaustion and dysregulation of subsets

Upregulation of checkpoint molecules

Studies have shown that the expression of programmed cell death 1 (PD-1) in CD4 + T cells obtained from septic patients who died is increased. Meanwhile, their macrophages and endothelial cells show an increased expression of PD-1 ligand 1 (PD-L1), which may impair the function of T cells at the local tissue level [20].

Th1/Th2 Imbalance

In septic patients, the functions of CD4 + helper T cell 1 (Th1) cells, Th2 cells, and Th17 cells are inhibited [21]. Early studies have shown that during the initial immune response to sepsis and trauma, the production of cytokines related to Th1 and Th2 cells is reduced [37]. Secondly, in the autopsy of septic patients, the amounts of IFN - γ and TNF produced by Th1 cells harvested from the spleen are lower than those of splenic T cells obtained from patients who died of non - infectious causes, indicating signs of T - cell exhaustion and T - cell exhaustion in septic patients [20].

Expansion of regulatory T cells (Tregs)

The proportion of regulatory T (Treg) cells in septic patients increases. Regulatory T (Treg) cells inhibit the function of effector T cells during homeostasis to maintain self-tolerance, which may lead to a decrease in the function of effector T cells. Treg cells can also inhibit the functions of monocytes and neutrophils, and these effects further enhance their immunosuppressive effects [38].

Pediatric considerations: The developmental trajectory of T cell subsets differs fundamentally between children and adults. Newborns predominantly harbor regulatory T cells (Tregs), which gradually shift toward pro‑inflammatory T cells (Th1) with age. This Treg‑to‑Th1 transition makes young children more vulnerable to immunosuppression when exposed to septic insults. Moreover, the Th17/Treg ratio in septic children has been shown to correlate strongly with SOFA scores, and an inverted ratio indicates poor prognosis. Importantly, recent evidence suggests that pediatric sepsis survivors exhibit resistance to long‑term immune dysfunction (via insufficient IL‑33 axis activation), a phenomenon not observed in adults.

B - cell exhaustion

Studies have found that in the immunosuppressive phase of septic patients, there is a redistribution and selective exhaustion of peripheral blood B - cell subsets, and the function of B cells to secrete IgM is impaired [39–41]. Among them, the expression levels of CD95 in immature/transitional B cells, naive B cells, resting memory B cells, and activated memory B cells are higher than those in the healthy control group. CD95 is a cell - surface molecule related to apoptosis, and the increase in its expression level may promote the apoptosis of B cells, leading to the exhaustion of B - cell subsets [42].

Pediatric considerations: B‑cell immunity in infants is highly dependent on T‑cell help for antibody production against polysaccharide antigens until approximately 2 years of age. This developmental limitation may increase susceptibility to encapsulated bacteria and contribute to sepsis‑induced immunosuppression in young children. Furthermore, serum immunoglobulin levels (particularly IgG subclasses) vary significantly by age, complicating the interpretation of IVIG trial results in pediatric sepsis (Fig. 3).

Adaptive immunity dusfunction: B cell mechanisms

Cytokine storm

During sepsis, the body’s immune response is dysregulated, and a variety of inflammatory cells produce a large number of cytokines, such as TNF - α, IL − 1, IL − 6, IL − 12, interferons (IFN - α, IFN - β, IFN - γ), monocyte chemoattractant protein − 1 (MCP − 1), and IL − 8. These cytokines can promote the occurrence and development of downstream inflammatory responses [43]. When these cytokines are produced in large quantities and out of control, a cytokine storm will be triggered, damaging multiple tissues and organs in the body.

The cytokine storm in the body after sepsis can lead to immune system dysregulation and Th1/Th2 cell population disorder, making it difficult for the body to effectively clear the infectious pathogen. Instead, it attacks its own tissues. The cytokine storm also induces the production of a large number of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and cell - free DNA (cfDNA). These molecules further promote inflammatory responses and tissue damage in the body. They can amplify the immune response by activating pattern - recognition receptors (such as Toll - like receptors), forming a vicious cycle and further aggravating sepsis [44].

Pediatric considerations: Although cytokine storm is a hallmark of both adult and pediatric sepsis, children with septic shock often present with a “cold shock” phenotype (high systemic vascular resistance, low cardiac output) rather than the typical adult “warm shock” (low SVR, high output). This hemodynamic difference reflects distinct cytokine‑mediated vascular tone regulation and myocardial responses in children. Additionally, neonates with early‑onset sepsis may develop persistent pulmonary hypertension (PPHN) due to infection‑driven elevation of pulmonary vascular resistance, requiring right ventricular support strategies not commonly needed in adults.

Monitoring of the immune status in sepsis

Immunosuppression leads to ineffective clearance of the infection foci in sepsis, and makes patients more susceptible to secondary infections and reactivation of latent infections [39]. The risk of “secondary infections” increases sharply, such as the recurrence of cytomegalovirus infection and infections associated with interventional catheters. This significantly increases the late mortality rate of septic patients [45]. Timely monitoring of the immune status of patients and rational use of immunosuppressants or immunostimulants are the keys to reversing the persistent immunosuppressive state and reducing the risk of late death in septic patients.

Monitoring of the quantity and function of immune cells

An observational retrospective study has shown that monitoring the lymphocyte count, monocyte count, and neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) can predict the severity and 28-day mortality rate of patients with intra-abdominal infections due to sepsis [46].

T cells are the main adaptive immune regulatory cells, and the evaluation of the status of T cells can accurately reflect the immune status of the body. Currently, the detection methods for T cell function are mature and the procedures are simple. Clinically, the main tests include the detection of the proportion of T cell subtypes, and the use of flow cytometry to detect the expression of T cell-related CD molecules, so as to evaluate the composition of different T cells and the proliferative capacity of T cells, which can further reflect the immune status of septic patients [47].

Patients with septic shock often experience immune failure, which is characterized by a decrease in HLA-DR expression and an increase in Tregs, especially when it ultimately leads to death [48, 49]. The Th17/Treg ratio is strongly positively correlated with the SOFA score, indicating that the higher the Th17/Treg ratio, the worse the prognosis of septic patients [50]. An abnormal increase in Tregs accompanied by an inverted Th17/Treg ratio suggests the occurrence of immunosuppression [51]. Th17 cells mediate the immune response by producing cytokines such as IL-17, IL-6, and IL-23. Tregs exert an immunosuppressive effect by regulating T cell apoptosis to avoid excessive activation of inflammatory damage [52].

Similar to T cells, septic shock is associated with B - lymphocyte depletion [53]. Functionally, the concentrations of serum IgG, IgA, and IgM directly reflect the status and activity of B cells. The peripheral blood immunoglobulin concentrations in septic patients can be used to evaluate the B - cell immune status. However, some studies have shown that there is no association between the initial decrease in IgG levels and survival rate or prognosis in septic patients. In contrast, patients with higher IgG levels tend to have a higher mortality rate [54]. Therefore, the combined application of serum IgG, IgM, and IgA has better predictive value.

Monitoring of HLA-DR expression

After being stimulated by bacterial products in vitro, monocytes produce a large number of inflammatory cytokines. The status of monocytes can be further evaluated by detecting the levels of inflammatory factors related to peripheral blood monocytes. HLA-DR is mainly expressed on the surface of monocytes/macrophages, dendritic cells and B cells. Flow cytometry-based quantification of monocyte HLA-DR (mHLA-DR) is currently the most widely recognized monitoring method, which is achieved through absolute lymphocyte count and the detection of mHLA-DR expression by flow cytometry. In addition, real-time quantitative PCR can also be used to evaluate the expression of MHC class II molecules and assess the immune status of monocytes [55]. A prospective observational study found that the HLA-DR level is an independent predictor of the prognosis of septic patients [56].

Monitoring of cytokine levels

Interleukin-10 (IL-10)

IL-10 is an important anti-inflammatory cytokine. In patients with sepsis, the levels of IL-10 increase to varying degrees. Studies have shown that the excessive production of IL-10 is a major predictor of the severity of sepsis and poor prognosis [57]. IL-10 can inhibit the production of various pro-inflammatory cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and so on, thereby alleviating the inflammatory response. However, the excessive production of IL-10 will lead to immunosuppression, making it difficult for patients to eliminate pathogens and increasing the risk of infection. Studies have indicated that monitoring the level of IL-10 from the first day to the third day after the onset of sepsis may help predict the prognosis of patients with sepsis [56].

Programmed death protein 1 (PD-1) and its ligand

PD-1 and its ligand also play an important role in the immunosuppression of sepsis. In the subacute phase of sepsis, apoptosis and autophagy increase, which are closely related to immunosuppression. PD-1 is a negative costimulatory pathway on T cells, which widely enhances the immunosuppressive signals of both the innate and adaptive immune systems [58].

Interleukin-35 (IL-35)

IL-35 plays an important role in the immunosuppression of sepsis. Studies have found that IL-35 forms a network with other cytokines in sepsis. In the acute phase of sepsis, IL-35 is closely related to IL-6, IL-10, IL-17 A, TNF-α and IFN-γ [59].

TNF-α

TNF-α is a pro-inflammatory cytokine mainly produced by monocytes/macrophages. During the stage of sepsis-induced immunosuppression, the release of TNF-α by monocytes is significantly reduced. In septic patients, the TNF-α levels at different time points in the survival group are significantly lower than those in the death group, indicating that the TNF-α level is closely related to the prognosis of patients [60]. In addition, monocytes can be stimulated by LPS in vitro, and the production capacity of TNF-α can be monitored to evaluate the reactivity of monocytes [61].

Treatment Strategies for Sepsis - Induced Immunosuppression

Immunostimulatory therapy

IL-7 therapy

IL-7 can increase the number of lymphocytes by promoting proliferation and inhibiting apoptosis. Studies have shown that IL-7 treatment does not cause excessive inflammatory responses or exacerbate organ dysfunction, but it significantly increases the counts of CD4 + and CD8 + T lymphocytes in septic patients [62]. Therefore, IL-7 treatment is beneficial for promoting the proliferation of T cells in septic patients. However, whether it can relieve the organ dysfunction caused by sepsis and reduce the mortality rate still needs further verification.

Granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF) Therapy

GM-CSF can enhance the functions of monocytes and neutrophils, restore the antigen-presenting ability, and reduce immunoparalysis. Studies have shown that intravenous injection of low-dose GM-CSF is beneficial for improving the oxygenation index, shortening the treatment time of antibiotics, and reducing infection-related complications. However, it cannot improve the 30-day survival rate [63]. Research has found that GM-CSF can enhance the phagocytosis of neutrophils and promote the expression of mHLA-DR, thereby reducing the occurrence of secondary infections [64, 65].

Immunological checkpoint inhibitors

Sepsis-induced immunosuppression is caused by the disruption of immune homeostasis, which is characterized by the release of anti-inflammatory cytokines, the abnormal death of immune effector cells, the excessive proliferation of immunosuppressive cells, and the expression of immunological checkpoints. Through targeting immunosuppression, especially the use of immunological checkpoint inhibitors, preclinical studies have demonstrated the reversal of immune cell dysfunction and the establishment of host resistance. Treatment with anti-PD-1 or PD-L1 antibodies can restore the functions of neutrophils, monocytes, natural killer (NK) cells, and T cells, highlighting the role of the PD-1/PD-L1 axis in sepsis-induced immunosuppression, as well as the ability of a single immunomodulator to treat infectious diseases [66]. However, currently, the application of immunological checkpoint inhibitors in the treatment of sepsis is still in the preclinical research stage, and further clinical trials are needed to verify their safety and effectiveness.

Other immunomodulatory treatments

IFN-γ

IFN-γ can enhance the phagocytic and bactericidal abilities of macrophages, and strengthen their capacity to eliminate pathogens. IFN-γ treatment significantly upregulates the expression of mHLA-DR and increases the secretion of TNF-α by monocytes, thus helping to eliminate pathogenic bacteria [67]. However, since IFN-γ is also a pro-inflammatory cytokine, its clinical safety still needs to be further verified.

Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs)

MSCs can alleviate organ damage and ultimately reduce the mortality rate of sepsis by improving bacterial clearance, regulating the immune response, reducing apoptosis, and promoting damage repair [68]. The antibacterial effect of MSCs is mediated by the increase in the release of the antibacterial peptide LL-37 and the enhancement of macrophage endocytosis [69]. Bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) also exert an anti-inflammatory effect by inhibiting the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome/caspase 1 and the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-6. In addition, MSCs promote the maturation of M2 macrophages and regulatory T cells (Tregs), thereby facilitating damage repair and limiting excessive inflammation [70].

Intravenous immunoglobulin

Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIg) may exert its effects through multiple mechanisms. On the one hand, IVIg can neutralize bacterial toxins and pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs), reducing their stimulation of the immune system. On the other hand, IVIg contains a variety of antibodies that can regulate the functions of immune cells. For example, it can promote the phagocytosis of macrophages and enhance the innate immune function of the body. In addition, IVIg can also regulate the activities of T cells and B cells, and modulate the adaptive immune response [71, 72]. Although the application of intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIg) has a certain theoretical basis, there is still some controversy regarding whether patients can benefit from it, as well as the appropriate timing of application, dosage, and treatment course. Some studies suggest that administering IVIgGM treatment for 3 days in the early stage can reduce the 30-day mortality risk of patients [73].There is also an opinion that a treatment course of at least 5 days is required to improve the prognosis [74].Some studies have shown that intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIg) cannot reduce the mortality rate [75, 76].

Conclusion

This review systematically examines the mechanisms, monitoring, and treatment of sepsis-related immunosuppression. A key theme emerging from the literature is that children are not “small adults” in terms of sepsis immunobiology. Age‑dependent differences in TLR signaling, cytokine balance, T‑cell ontogeny, NETosis, and organ dysfunction trajectories all contribute to a distinct immunosuppressive phenotype in pediatric sepsis.

Importantly, this review also acknowledges unresolved controversies in adult sepsis immunobiology that have broader implications for the field. The long-standing biphasic model of sequential hyperinflammation followed by immunosuppression is not universally supported by clinical data, as concurrent immune activation and suppression are frequently observed at ICU admission in adult patients. Additionally, the causal link between prolonged immunosuppression and long-term morbidity/mortality in sepsis survivors remains incompletely validated, given evidence that most late deaths in septic shock survivors are not attributable to nosocomial infections. These unresolved questions highlight the need for more granular, time-resolved immune phenotyping in both adult and pediatric cohorts.

Future research should prioritize pediatric‑specific clinical trials of immunostimulatory agents (IL‑7, GM‑CSF, IFN‑γ) and immune checkpoint inhibitors, as well as head-to-head comparative studies characterizing age-specific immune trajectories to refine patient stratification for immunomodulatory therapies.This review systematically examines the mechanisms, monitoring, and treatment of sepsis-related immunosuppression.
