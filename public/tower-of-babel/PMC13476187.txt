Use of Isavuconazole in the Treatment of Cryptococcosis: A Real-World Case Series
Authors: Marta Albanell-Fernández, Andrea Vergara, Ana Martinez-Urrea, Carolina Garcia-Vidal, Marta Ulldemolins, Josep Mensa, Álex Soriano, Marta Bodro
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionCryptococcosis is a severe invasive fungal infection with limited therapeutic options beyond fluconazole-based regimens. Isavuconazole, a broad-spectrum triazole antifungal, has emerged as a potential alternative, although clinical data supporting its use remain scarce. We aimed to evaluate the real-world effectiveness and safety of isavuconazole in patients with different forms of cryptococcosis.MethodsA retrospective observational study was conducted at a tertiary-care hospital, including patients with cryptococcosis who received isavuconazole at any treatment phase. Standard microbiological methods were used for pathogen identification and susceptibility testing. Demographic, clinical, and microbiological data were collected. Clinical and microbiological responses and tolerability were assessed at end of treatment or until death.ResultEight patients with cryptococcosis received isavuconazole, most of whom were immunocompromised. Clinical presentations included pulmonary and disseminated disease, with Cryptococcus neoformans as the predominant species. Isavuconazole was primarily used during the consolidation and maintenance phases, after induction therapy with amphotericin B and flucytosine for 2 weeks in most cases, and as salvage therapy in two patients. It was well tolerated during prolonged treatment (6–12 months). In the two patients with isavuconazole therapeutic drug monitoring, plasma total trough concentrations were within the therapeutic range (5 and 3.5 µg/mL, respectively), whereas cerebrospinal fluid total concentration levels were undetectable. A favorable clinical response was observed in four patients, while three remain on treatment with ongoing clinical improvement; one patient died early. Microbiological clearance was achieved in all culture-positive cases.ConclusionsIsavuconazole demonstrated clinical effectiveness in this cohort of patients across different presentations of cryptococcosis. Treatment was safe and well tolerated, supporting its role as an alternative antifungal option against Cryptococcus, particularly when fluconazole is limited by adverse effects or drug–drug interactions. However, data on central nervous system penetration were limited, and further studies are needed to better define its role in cryptococcal meningitis management.

Key Summary Points

The therapeutic armamentarium for the treatment of cryptococcosis remains limited, and there is a need for safer alternatives with a more favorable tolerability profile than fluconazole.Isavuconazole is a reasonable alternative to fluconazole for the treatment of cryptococcosis, especially for the consolidation and maintenance phase.This case series provides real-world evidence supporting the use of isavuconazole in immunocompromised patients for the treatment of cryptococcosis.Further data are required to describe its central nervous system penetration to support its use in cryptococcal meningitis.

Introduction

Cryptococcosis is a globally distributed invasive mycosis associated with substantial morbidity and mortality, largely owing to its rapid progression and potential for fatal outcomes [1]. The wide spectrum of clinical manifestations, the diversity of host populations at risk, and the heterogeneous healthcare settings in which the disease is managed pose significant diagnostic and therapeutic challenges [2].

For the treatment of cryptococcal meningitis, disseminated cryptococcosis, and severe isolated pulmonary cryptococcosis, induction therapy with liposomal amphotericin B and flucytosine is recommended, followed by fluconazole for consolidation and maintenance therapy [1, 2]. However, the emergence of fluconazole resistance is an increasing concern. Resistance rates have been reported in a systematic review at 10.6% (95% confidence interval [CI] 5.5 to 15.6) among incident isolates and 24.1% (95% CI −3.1 to 51.2) in relapse cases [3]. Accordingly, the most recent global guideline for the diagnosis and management of cryptococcosis includes the use of voriconazole, posaconazole, isavuconazole, or itraconazole as alternative agents for consolidation and maintenance therapy [2].

Isavuconazole is an extended-spectrum triazole antifungal with broad activity against yeasts, molds, and dimorphic fungi [4]. It has been approved by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and the European Medicines Agency (EMA) for the treatment of invasive aspergillosis and mucormycosis [5, 6]. Its pharmacokinetic profile is favorable: oral administration is not significantly affected by food intake, interpatient pharmacokinetic variability is relatively low, and the intravenous prodrug formulation does not require cyclodextrin for solubility, allowing its use in patients with impaired renal function [7, 8].

The VITAL trial suggested that isavuconazole may be effective for the treatment of cryptococcosis; however, only nine patients with this infection were included [9]. To date, clinical experience with isavuconazole for cryptococcosis remains limited, with evidence largely restricted to isolated real-world reports describing its use as salvage therapy [10–12].

Additional clinical data are therefore needed to better guide its use in this setting. This study aimed to describe our real-world experience with isavuconazole in patients with different forms of cryptococcosis and to evaluate their clinical outcomes.

Case Presentations

Design

We conducted a retrospective, observational study in a tertiary-care hospital in Spain (Hospital Clinic of Barcelona). Patients diagnosed with cryptococcosis who had received isavuconazole during any phase of treatment were included. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee of the center (HCB/2026/0388). Data from all patients were anonymously collected in an electronic dataset.

Isavuconazole Administration

Isavuconazole was prescribed according to clinical judgment by a multidisciplinary team, following the recommendations of an infectious diseases specialist and based on the patient’s clinical condition and microbiological isolates. In all cases, a loading dose of 200 mg every 8 h was administered during the first 2 days, followed by 200 mg once daily.

Data Collection

We collected demographic, clinical, and microbiological data from each patient until completion of cryptococcosis treatment or discontinuation of isavuconazole. Overall response and microbiological response at the end of treatment, as well as treatment tolerability, were evaluated.

When available, isavuconazole total plasma and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) concentrations obtained during therapy as part of the therapeutic drug monitoring program were also collected.

Definitions

Disseminated cryptococcosis was defined as the involvement of at least two noncontiguous sites or evidence of high fungal burden based on cryptococcal antigen titer ≥ 1:512 [1].

Successful clinical outcome was defined as the complete resolution of all the signs and symptoms attributable to Cryptococcus infection, including radiological findings when applicable, without the need for additional antifungal therapy.

Microbiological response was defined as negative follow-up cultures in patients with initially positive cultures.

Microbiological Procedures

Clinical specimens were cultured on the appropriate media depending on sample type and incubated under standard laboratory conditions. Fungal isolates obtained from positive cultures were identified by matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS). Antifungal susceptibility testing was carried out using the Sensititre YeastOne panel, with interpretation according to EUCAST criteria. In parallel, cryptococcal antigen detection was performed in serum and/or CSF using the Remel Cryptococcus Antigen Latex Test until 2021 and the IMMY CrAg® Lateral Flow assay thereafter.

Plasma Isavuconazole Concentrations

Plasma samples collected within 1 h before the next scheduled dose. CSF samples were obtained at time points corresponding to planned lumbar punctures performed for the management of intracranial hypertension or during external ventricular drain manipulation, under standard isavuconazonium sulfate therapy. Total isavuconazole concentrations were quantified in plasma and CSF using a liquid chromatography–ultraviolet (LC–UV) assay (lower limit of quantification [LLOQ] = 0.25 μg/mL). Notably, this method was validated for the plasma matrix but not for CSF.

Results

Patient Demographics and Infection Sources

Between 2018 and 2026, a total of 32 patients were diagnosed with cryptococcosis, of whom 8 received isavuconazole during treatment. The patients’ characteristics, type of infections, isolated pathogens, and clinical and microbiological outcomes are presented in Table 1. Seven of the eight patients were male, with a median age of 63 years (interquartile range [IQR] 55–74 years). In the six cases with positive cultures, C. neoformans was the isolated species. Regarding comorbidities, five patients were kidney transplant recipients, two patients had decompensated liver cirrhosis, and one patient had B cell lymphoma in addition to a severe secondary immunodeficiency. Antifungal susceptibility testing was available for those six patients. Treatment

The induction regimens and treatment duration were tailored according to the clinical characteristics of the infection and patient type. Induction therapy with liposomal Amphotericin B 3–5 mg/kg + Flucytosine, with dose adjustment according to renal function, for approximately 2 weeks, was the most commonly used regimen. In two patients, the regimen of liposomal amphotericin B 10 mg/kg (single dose) + flucytosine + fluconazole was used to reduce the nephrotoxicity associated with amphotericin B, as both were kidney transplant recipients with impaired renal function. In one patient (P2) with a recent kidney transplant (5 months), flucytosine was replaced with fluconazole to minimize nephrotoxicity and avoid interactions with immunosuppressants, whereas in the other patient (P4), flucytosine was replaced with fluconazole due to a shortage of stock. One patient (P3) received induction therapy only with fluconazole due to localized pulmonary infection with low fungal burden (negative cultures and Ag Cryp > 1/8).

Isavuconazole was used during the consolidation or maintenance phase in all patients, except in P8, in whom, after 1 week of induction therapy with fluconazole 1200 mg/day, treatment was switched to isavuconazole due to suspected hepatotoxicity (elevated transaminases). All patients, except P5, who died early, received isavuconazole for 6–12 months according to the clinical course of the infection. Three patients remain on isavuconazole therapy. In all eight patients, the drug was well tolerated, with no adverse events leading to treatment discontinuation.

Isavuconazole was not used as first-line therapy in any patient but rather as rescue therapy or as an alternative to fluconazole. The main reasons for switching to isavuconazole included its lower MIC values, the need to avoid interactions with immunosuppressants, and the aim of preventing hepatotoxicity associated with prolonged high-dose fluconazole therapy. In three patients (P1, P2, and P6), isavuconazole was used as rescue therapy owing to lesion progression under treatment with fluconazole (P2 and P6) or voriconazole (P1).

Isavuconazole Concentrations

Isavuconazole total concentrations in plasma and CSF were available in two patients (P4 and P8). In P4, after 11 days of isavuconazole therapy, plasma total trough concentration was 5 µg/mL, and CSF total concentration was < 0.25 µg/mL obtained from an external ventricular drain 18 h after the previous dose; however, the dose was not modified as the clinical course was favorable and microbiological eradication in the CSF was achieved at day 12. In P8, concentrations were obtained on day 8 of isavuconazole treatment; plasma total trough concentration was 3.5 µg/mL, and CSF total concentration was < 0.25 µg/mL, obtained from lumbar puncture 20 h after the previous dose.

Clinical Outcomes

At the time of writing this manuscript, three patients (P6, P7, and P8) remained on isavuconazole therapy; two had received treatment for > 5 months with favorable clinical evolution. One patient (P6) experienced disease progression with new lesions on the contralateral leg prior to isavuconazole initiation and suffered kidney graft loss; however, after starting isavuconazole the original cryptococcosis lesions on the initial limb eventually resolved completely. Among the remaining five patients, one (P5) died during treatment whereas the other four (P1, P2, P3, and P4) showed a successful clinical response to isavuconazole, with complete resolution of the pulmonary infiltrates in cases of pulmonary cryptococcosis and absence of neurological symptoms with sterilisation of the CSF cultures in cases of cryptococcal meningitis. Despite the favorable clinical response, in some patients, low cryptococcal antigen titers remained detectable for several months in some patients, although this were not considered indicative of microbiological failure.

Discussion

In this study, we report our real-world experience with isavuconazole as salvage or alternative therapy in patients with various forms of cryptococcosis, including meningitis, pulmonary disease, and other nonmeningeal, nonpulmonary manifestations. Overall, a favorable response was observed in four of the five patients who had completed treatment, while the remaining three patients who are still receiving therapy are also showing a favorable clinical course. No significant drug-related adverse events were observed during prolonged treatment durations (6–12 months).

Isavuconazole is considered an alternative to fluconazole for consolidation and maintenance therapy in cryptococcosis [2]; however, the VITAL study also demonstrated its potential utility as primary therapy, including during the induction phase, in patients with proven Cryptococcus infection [9]. In our cohort, all patients except P8 received isavuconazole during the consolidation and maintenance phases, following an induction therapy, which consisted mostly of amphotericin B plus flucytosine. In P8, isavuconazole was introduced during the induction phase, after documented microbiological clearance of Cryptococcus in the CSF culture.

Fluconazole is the first-line azole for cryptococcosis; however, the high doses commonly used (800–1200 mg/day) may lead to toxicity, particularly hepatotoxicity. In addition, as a moderate inhibitor of CYP3A4, fluconazole may interact with immunosuppressive agents, which may limit its use in recipients of solid organ transplants [13]. By contrast, isavuconazole offers several potential advantages over other azoles. It has a favorable oral tolerability, as it can be administered with or without food, and exhibits relatively low interpatient pharmacokinetic variability [7–9]. Moreover, it may represent a highly relevant therapeutic option to consider in cases with documented fluconazole resistance, which has been reported in approximately one in four patients experiencing relapse [3]. However, its higher cost compared with fluconazole and limited accessibility may limit its use, particularly in low-income settings.

In our cohort, the main reasons for its use included minimizing hepatotoxicity associated with high-dose fluconazole (≥ 800 mg daily), the reduction of drug–drug interactions with immunosuppressants, as isavuconazole is a moderate CYP3A4 inhibitor compared with other azoles [13], and prior fluconazole treatment failure. In addition, the low MIC values of the isolates against isavuconazole may facilitate attainment of PK–PD targets. In our cohort, no patients required treatment discontinuation due to adverse events or disease progression under isavuconazole treatment. Furthermore, no dose adjustments of isavuconazole were required in any patient receiving concomitant immunosuppressive therapy.

Overall, isavuconazole was associated with a favorable response in patients who completed treatment; patient P5 died early, precluding assessment of treatment response. In all cases, isavuconazole was introduced after microbiological clearance had been achieved in the six patients with culture-confirmed Cryptococcus infection. In addition, isavuconazole was used as salvage therapy in two patients. In patients P1 and P6, who experienced clinical deterioration during the maintenance phase with voriconazole and fluconazole, respectively, initiation of isavuconazole was associated with clinical improvement, supporting its potential role as a salvage therapy.

Isavuconazole demonstrated efficacy in the treatment of cryptococcosis and dimorphic fungal infections in the VITAL trial. Among cryptococcosis cases, clinical response was observed in six of nine patients, including three of four with pulmonary involvement and four of five with central nervous system (CNS) disease [9]. Similarly, in a phase III study conducted in Japan, isavuconazole was effective in nine of ten cases of pulmonary cryptococcosis [4]. However, real-world data supporting its use remains limited and is largely restricted to individual case reports. These include a case of HIV-associated disseminated cryptococcosis relapse during fluconazole maintenance therapy, successfully managed with liposomal amphotericin B plus isavuconazole [10], and a case of a cerebral cryptococcoma in an immunocompetent woman caused by both Cryptococcus neoformans and Cryptococcus gattii, who responded favorably to isavuconazole in combination with amphotericin B [11]. Conversely, treatment failure has also been reported in a patient who progressed to disseminated disease while receiving isavuconazole for pulmonary cryptococcosis, despite an adequate isavuconazole exposure (plasma total trough concentrations = 2.1 μg/mL) [12].

In the VITAL trial, median plasma total trough concentrations of isavuconazole were 3.2 μg/mL (range 0.6–5.1) at day 7 and 4.0 μg/mL (range 0.8–7.8) at day 28 [9]. In the case reported by Kawaguchi et al. [10], oral administration of isavuconazole achieved stable plasma total trough concentrations ranging from 4.6 to 10.6 μg/mL. However, CSF concentrations were not reported in any of the patients with cryptococcal meningitis.

Human data on isavuconazole CNS penetration remain limited. In healthy volunteers, isavuconazole showed low CSF distribution (AUCCSF/AUCplasma: 0.02) [14]. However, clinical observations suggest a more complex distribution pattern in infected tissues. Davis et al. [15], reported undetectable isavuconazole concentrations (LLOQ = 0.25 μg/mL) in CSF samples obtained from extraventricular drains in patients with refractory coccidioidal meningitis, while measurable concentrations were found in CSF obtained by lumbar puncture (0.7 and 1.7 μg/mL at plasma concentrations of 1.8 and 5.6 μg/mL, respectively). Similarly, in a case of cerebral aspergillosis, plasma total trough isavuconazole concentrations on day 3 and day 6 of therapy were 0.6 and 2.9 μg/mL, respectively, and isavuconazole concentration in the brain tissue measured on day 4 was 1.5 μg/mL. The estimated curve showed that the expected plasma concentration on day 4 (timing of brain biopsy) was 1.7 μg/mL with an estimated accumulation of 44% [16].

Assuming the limitations with the interpretation of tissue homogenate concentrations, these findings suggest that isavuconazole penetration may differ between CSF and inflamed brain tissue, with potentially greater accumulation in brain tissue. In contrast to the findings reported by Davis et al. [15], in our cohort, both patients (P4 and P8) had CSF concentrations < 0.25 µg/mL despite higher plasma concentrations. This is an important remark about the interpretation of our data, as the bioanalytical method used at our center had an LLOQ for isavuconazole of 0.25 mg/L. However, as a pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic index and target associated with efficacy in plasma and CSF has not been established for isavuconazole in cryptococcal infections, including meningitis, the trough plasma therapeutic range of 2.0–5.0 µg/mL may not be applicable to this indication, as it is derived from invasive aspergillosis and mucormycosis studies rather than cryptococcal infections specifically [17]. This is particularly relevant considering the much lower MICs of Cryptococcus spp. to isavuconazole (ranging from 0.008 µg/mL to 0.50 µg/mL, with an overall modal MIC of 0.03 µg/mL in a collection of > 800 Cryptococcus neoformans isolates, MIC90 0.12–0.25 µg/mL) compared with the MICs exhibited by filamentous molds [18]. Consequently, in the CSF, concentrations below our LLOQ threshold but still above the MIC for Cryptococcus spp. may provide sufficient exposure for treating cryptococcal CNS infections, which may partially explain the favorable clinical outcomes observed in patients with cryptococcal meningitis. In fact, Schwartz et al. [19], evaluated isavuconazole for invasive fungal infections with CNS involvement, and reported overall survival rates of 80.6% at day 42 and 69.4% at day 84; notably, the five patients with Cryptococcus infection were alive at day 84.

Nevertheless, combination therapy with amphotericin B and flucytosine remains the optimal initial approach for cryptococcal meningitis as part of induction therapy. Amphotericin B exhibits limited penetration into the CNS [13, 19], whereas flucytosine achieves moderate CSF penetration (AUCCSF/AUCplasma: 0.69) [20, 21]. These findings underscore the importance of maintaining flucytosine during induction therapy, particularly in the presence of meningeal involvement.

Overall, this study has several limitations. First, the small sample size and descriptive design preclude definitive conclusions. Second, therapeutic drug monitoring of isavuconazole was available in only two patients, with a single CSF concentration measurement per patient, limiting our ability to draw firm conclusions regarding CNS penetration, also considering that the therapeutic exposure of isavuconazole in CSF for the treatment of cryptococcal meningitis has not been defined. Third, isavuconazole was not used as first-line therapy in any case and was introduced only after microbiological clearance had been achieved, which limits conclusions about its efficacy during the induction phase for microbiological eradication. Fourth, data on the use of isavuconazole in complex clinical presentations, including central nervous system abscesses or cryptococcomas, are limited due to the small number of such cases in our cohort. Nevertheless, our series provides real-world data that may help inform the clinical positioning of isavuconazole in the management of cryptococcosis.

Conclusions

In summary, isavuconazole was effective in this cohort of immunocompromised patients, including solid organ transplant recipients and individuals with hematological malignancies, across different forms of cryptococcosis. Treatment was safe and well tolerated during prolonged therapy (6–12 months), supporting its role as an alternative within the antifungal armamentarium against Cryptococcus, particularly in scenarios where fluconazole may be contraindicated owing to its adverse effects or drug–drug interactions.
