Plant embodied intelligence: A paradigmatic perspective
Authors: Yi Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryThe concept of intelligence is expanding beyond neural systems to include plants. Following this trend, we turn our focus on a key but undeveloped branch—plant embodied intelligence (PEI), where adaptive problem-solving activities emerge from dynamic interactions between distributed plant structures and their environment. Specifically, this review first outlines PEI’s theoretical foundations and then proposes research avenues, including adaptive morphogenesis and growth, distributed information processing and memory, ecological communication and swarm-like behavior, and decision-making under risk and competition. Next, a comprehensive analysis of global research highlights pioneering work, spanning plant neurobiology, systems biology, and bio-inspired materials, while identifying critical gaps. Accordingly, a convergent methodology of quantitative phenotyping, molecular systems biology, ecological modeling, and plant-inspired robotics/software. Finally, we articulate how the pursuit of PEI can reciprocally advance both plant sciences and intelligent systems sciences, ultimately positioning plants as active collaborators for sustainable innovation.

Introduction: why plant embodied intelligence (PEI)?

The conventional understanding of intelligence, a concept historically monopolized by animal nervous systems and anthropocentric benchmarks of problem-solving, is undergoing a profound and necessary re-evaluation. One important branch of this re-evaluation is plant intelligence (PI).1 Recently, the community has given attention to the embodied intelligence in plants,2 and the rising research on plant-inspired robots3 has strengthened this trend. To elucidate the context, this review focuses on the concept of PEI from a paradigmatic perspective, instead of the traditional plan of developing various PEI algorithms. This introductory Section first establishes the philosophical, theoretical, and practical imperatives for adopting the paradigm of PEI. We argue that plants are not passive, stimulus-response automata but active, perceptive agents whose sophisticated adaptive behaviors constitute a legitimate and illuminating form of intelligence—one that is fundamentally embodied, distributed, and ecological in nature.

Redefining intelligence: from brain-centric to embodied, enactive, and ecological

For much of scientific history, intelligence has been implicitly defined by its neural substrates, measured by capacities such as abstract reasoning, learning, and memory as instantiated in brains. This brain-centric view is a legacy of what Crick termed “the astonishing hypothesis”—that all human experience and cognition are merely the behavior of a vast assembly of nerve cells.4 While productive for neuroscience, this perspective has marginalized non-neural organisms, relegating them to the realm of simple, genetically programmed tropisms.

This paradigm is robustly challenged by frameworks emerging from embodied, enactive, and ecological cognitive science.5,6,7 These schools of thought posit that cognition is for action. Intelligence is not about building abstract internal representations of the world, but about guiding successful, adaptive behavior in an environment. The thoughts also posit that the body is constitutive. The morphology, material properties, and physiological dynamics of an organism are not mere peripherals to a central cognitive processor; they are integral components of the cognitive process. The body shapes and constrains how information is acquired and what problems can be solved.8 The thoughts further posit that cognition is situated and enacted. Intelligent behavior emerges from the real-time, sensorimotor coupling between an agent and its environment. An organism enacts or brings forth its world through its history of interactions.9

Plants, as sessile, autotrophic organisms, present the ultimate test case for these non-neural, embodied theories of intelligence. Subjected to immense evolutionary pressure without the option of locomotion, they have evolved to perceive, compute, and act upon their environment through the very growth and form of their bodies. A root apex navigating soil heterogeneity or a vine circumnutating to find a support structure are not mere reflexes but goal-directed, exploratory behaviors arising from distributed computation.10,11 As Trewavas compellingly argued, if intelligence is defined as the capacity to solve problems, then plants are demonstrably intelligent, having solved the problems of survival on Earth for over 500 million years.12

The core thesis of PEI

Building upon these foundations, we formally define PEI as follows. The capacity of a plant, as a unified yet modular organism, to perceive variable internal and external conditions, integrate and evaluate this information across its decentralized architecture, and initiate adaptive, context-dependent responses that enhance its fitness, all arising from the emergent dynamics of its physico-chemical networks and morphological plasticity.

This definition emphasizes several key principles. The first key principle is agency and goal-directedness. Plants act not at random, but toward ends such as resource acquisition, stress avoidance, and reproductive success.13 The second key principle is decentralized integration. Lacking a central brain, plants integrate information through networked vascular, electrical, and chemical signaling systems, functioning as a distributed cognitive system.14 The third key principle is embodiment as computation. The plant’s structure is its cognitive architecture. For instance, the biomechanics of a leaf’s pulvinus pre-computes a folding response, and the differential growth rates in a stem compute phototropic curvature.15,16 The fourth key principle is somatic memory and learning. Plants can modify future behavior based on past experience, through mechanisms such as epigenetic changes, priming of defense pathways, and structural adjustments—a form of somatic memory.17,18 Evidence of habituation and associative-like learning further blurs the line between plant physiology and cognitive behavior.19

This perspective reframes classic botanical phenomena. Root branching is not just a developmental program but a foraging strategy optimized for patchy resources.20 The emission of volatile organic compounds (VOCs) upon herbivory is not just a chemical release but a communicative act warning kin or attracting predators of the herbivore.21 Systemic acquired resistance is not just an immune response but a form of whole-organism immunological memory.22 In general, the paradigm of exploring intelligence should go beyond brain-centric embodied intelligence—also with PEI more considered.

Scope and significance of this review

This review serves a critical dual purpose: to synthesize and to catalyze. Its scope is deliberately broad and interdisciplinary, spanning philosophy of mind, plant physiology, ecology, computational modeling, and bio-inspired engineering. Its primary aim is to synthesize first. The second primary aim is to provide a research roadmap. We draw concrete, multidisciplinary avenues for investigating PEI, offering testable hypotheses and methodological approaches for empirical and theoretical work. The third primary aim is to analyze the landscape. The significance of this endeavor extends beyond academic interest. In an era of climate change and biodiversity loss, understanding plants as intelligent agents is essential for predicting ecosystem responses and developing sustainable agricultural practices.24 Furthermore, in a technological age seeking solutions for resilience and energy efficiency, plants offer a billion-year-old repository of proven designs for surviving and thriving in complex, unpredictable environments. Studying PEI, therefore, is not an anthropomorphic indulgence but a strategic imperative for both understanding the natural world and innovating for our future.

Theoretical foundations and key concepts

The paradigm of PEI does not emerge from a vacuum. It is firmly rooted in a convergence of theoretical frameworks that have reshaped our understanding of cognition, life, and complex systems. This section delineates the core intellectual pillars supporting PEI, ranging from philosophical foundations to specific biological and computational concepts. It navigates beyond superficial analogies to establish a rigorous, non-anthropocentric theoretical basis for understanding plant adaptive behavior as a form of intelligence.

Embodied, enactive, and extended cognition: the philosophical bedrock

The most profound shift enabling PEI comes from the enactive and embodied approaches in cognitive science, which dismantle the necessity of a centralized brain for intelligent behavior. With enactive cognition developed,6 enactivism posits that cognition is not the representation of a pre-given world by a pre-given mind but is the enactment of a world and a mind through the history of the structural coupling of an autonomous agent with its environment.6,9 For plants, this “bringing forth of a world” occurs through their unique sensorimotor loop. That is, perception (of light gradients, chemical cues, mechanical pressure) is directly coupled to action (differential growth, tropic movement, metabolic change). The plant’s cognitive domain—what is relevant and meaningful—is defined by its physiological and morphological capacities. A climbing plant’s world, for instance, is enacted through its circumnutation, a behavior that actively explores and creates the affordance of support.1

As to embodied cognition, this framework, articulated by researchers like Clark7 and Pfeifer and Bongard,8 argues that an agent’s body—its shape, materials, and dynamics—plays a constitutive role in cognitive processing. Morphological computation refers to the way an organism’s physical structure can perform information-processing tasks, offloading computational burden from a central controller. This is vividly illustrated in plants. The Venus flytrap’s snapping action is a mechanically pre-computed, rapid movement where the storage and release of elastic energy in the leaf’s lobes executes the capture, with electrical signaling serving merely as a trigger.15 A pine cone’s opening and closing with humidity is a direct computation performed by the bilayer structure of its scales, requiring no neural circuitry.25 These examples demonstrate that “smart” behavior can be an intrinsic property of a body’s material and structural design, a principle central to PEI.

When we regard extended and distributed cognition, the research on extended mind suggests that cognitive processes are not skull-bound but can extend into the environment.26 While controversial in human cognition, a distributed view is almost literal in plant ecology. A plant’s cognitive system arguably extends into its rhizosphere. The first aspect relates to mycorrhizal networks. Common mycorrhizal networks (CMNs) interconnect root systems of multiple plants, facilitating the exchange of nutrients, water, and information signals about herbivore attack or drought.27,28 These fungal hyphae function as a kind of biological internet, distributing information-processing and resource management across a plant community. The second aspect involves the chemical and hydraulic environment. The cloud of VOCs emitted by a plant and the chemical gradients in the soil created by root exudates are not merely signals but components of an extended signaling and decision-making apparatus that mediates interactions with neighbors, predators, and pollinators.21,29

Plant neurobiology and the concept of plant-specific signaling integration

The “Plant Neurobiology” movement, spearheaded by Brenner et al.,30 has been both provocative and productive.31 Its central, contentious claim is that plants possess neuron-like cells and synapse-like structures, particularly in the root apex, which form a network for electrical signaling and information integration. However, the concept of “Plant Neurobiology” meets controversies and critiques. Mainstream plant biologists have rightly criticized the direct neurological analogies as misleading, arguing that plant action potentials differ fundamentally from animal neurons in speed, mechanism, and function, and that there is no evidence for a centralized coordinating center akin to a brain.23,32 This critique underscores the danger of uncritical anthropomorphism.

Further, “Plant Neurobiology” needs a productive legacy and refinement. Despite its contentious terminology, the movement made two crucial contributions to the theoretical foundation of PEI. One is that it highlighted systemic integration. It forcefully argued that plants are not collections of independent tissues but integrated organisms. The study of long-distance electrical signaling (variation potentials, systemic potentials) and rapid calcium waves demonstrates that plants can transmit information about local stimuli (e.g., wounding and herbivory) across their entire body to coordinate a systemic defense response.33,34 This is a form of whole-organism information processing. The other foundation is that it emphasized the root apex as a command center. Research showed the root cap, with its statocytes for gravity sensing and its sensitive perception of countless soil parameters (touch, humidity, chemicals, oxygen), functions as a sophisticated sensory organ. Baluška and colleagues proposed the “root brain” hypothesis, suggesting the transition zone of the root apex acts as an integration center for sensory input, guiding root navigation.14 While the “brain” metaphor is problematic, the concept of the root tip as a decentralized, autonomous agent performing complex perception-action cycles is a cornerstone of PEI.

A more accurate and productive theoretical stance is to view plants as possessing a plant-specific signaling integration system, composed of overlapping vascular, electrical, chemical, and hydraulic networks, which achieves functional integration without neuronal or centralized architecture.

Swarm intelligence and stigmergy in modular organisms

A powerful theoretical lens for understanding PI comes from the study of collective behavior in social insects and other decentralized systems. The first lens is to consider the plant as a superorganism or swarm. A plant is fundamentally modular. It grows by reiterating structural units (phytomers: node, internode, leaf, and axillary bud). Each apical meristem can be viewed as a semi-autonomous agent with local sensory capacities and behavioral rules. The collective growth of these modules—how they space branches, orient leaves, or allocate roots—results in globally adaptive architectures.35 This mirrors swarm intelligence, where simple agents following local rules produce complex, intelligent group-level phenomena (e.g., ant colony optimization, bird flocking).

The second lens is stigmergic coordination. Stigmergy is a mechanism of indirect coordination between agents via modifications left in the environment. Plants extensively use stigmergic principles. The first stigmergic principle is shade avoidance: A plant elongates its stem in response to the low red:far-red light ratio reflected from neighbors. This modification of its own structure changes the light environment for its lower leaves and neighboring plants, triggering further reactions in a feedback loop. The second stigmergic principle is root foraging. As roots deplete a local nutrient patch, they alter the soil chemistry. This environmental modification can guide subsequent root growth (either of the same plant or its neighbors) toward unexplored areas. The environment itself becomes a shared memory and coordination medium.36

This framework reframes plant development not as the execution of a deterministic genetic blueprint, but as a self-organizing, emergent process where global form emerges from local agent-based interactions constrained by genetics and environment.

Predictive physiology, Bayesian inference, and teleonomic behavior

Recent theoretical advances suggest plant behavior is not merely reactive but can be predictive, involving internal models of the environment. This is reflected by predictive plasticity and teleonomy. Many plant responses are anticipatory. The classic shade avoidance response is triggered not by the actual loss of light, but by the spectral signal that predicts future shading from a neighbor.37 This is a teleonomic behavior oriented toward a future goal (avoiding shade). Similarly, cold hardening in autumn prepares the plant for winter temperatures not yet experienced. To evaluate whether cognitive frameworks provide genuine explanatory value, we can adopt a comparative approach. For each phenomenon discussed, we consider an alternative explanation grounded in biophysical or metabolic principles, serving as a “null model,” and assess whether the cognitive account offers distinct predictions, captures phenomena that the null model cannot, or reveals novel empirical avenues.

Specifically, plant behaviors can be predicted based on a Bayesian interpretation. This predictive capacity can be framed within a Bayesian brain (or, more aptly, a Bayesian body) theory. The plant can be conceived as holding a prior “belief” (based on its genetic makeup and past experience) about its environment. Sensory data (likelihood) update this belief (posterior), which then guides action to minimize predicted surprise or stress.38 For example, upon sensing herbivore-related VOCs, a plant updates its internal model to a high probability of attack and decides to invest resources in pre-emptive defense compound synthesis. This is a form of active inference, where the organism acts to make its sensory inputs align with its preferred (survival-conducive) states.

Plant behaviors may follow game-theoretic strategies. In competitive settings, plants engage in strategies that can be modeled by game theory. The “tragedy of the commons” in root competition, where over-proliferation in a shared soil volume leads to collective detriment, or the strategic investment in stem elongation versus stability in dense stands, are classic game-theoretic problems.13,39 This demonstrates a capacity for strategic decision-making in social contexts.

In general, the theoretical foundations of PEI are robust and multidisciplinary, as illustrated in Figure 1. They displace the brain as the sine qua non of intelligence, replacing it with a focus on embodied interaction, decentralized integration, emergent self-organization, and predictive, goal-directed agency. By synthesizing enactive philosophy, the insights (and cautions) of plant signaling biology, swarm intelligence principles, and predictive processing models, we construct a non-reductive yet scientifically rigorous framework. This framework allows us to see plants not as passive objects but as cognitive subjects—agents that solve the problem of living through the intelligent dynamics of their embodied form.

Multifaceted research avenues for exploring PEI

Having established the theoretical foundations, we now delineate concrete research avenues to empirically investigate and validate the PEI paradigm. These avenues are not mutually exclusive; they represent interconnected facets of a plant’s cognitive existence, each requiring specific experimental and analytical approaches. Moving from morphological computation to ecological games, this framework provides a roadmap for transforming PEI from a compelling hypothesis into a rigorous scientific research program.

Avenue 1: adaptive morphogenesis and growth as embodied exploration and problem-solving

This avenue focuses on the intelligence embedded in the plant’s physical form and its dynamic growth, viewing morphology as both a product and a tool of cognition. First, tropisms can be considered as directed search algorithms. Tropisms are not simple, deterministic reflexes but adaptive, goal-directed exploration strategies. Research should move beyond characterizing stimuli to modeling the underlying decision-making process.

The first representative kind of tropisms is gravitropism and proprioception. Roots and shoots do not just grow toward or away from gravity; they exhibit complex proprioception—a sense of their own curvature and posture. Mathematical models now integrate gravitropic response with proprioceptive feedback to explain how stems achieve straight growth and roots navigate obstacles, revealing an internal computation of body mechanics.10 Future work involves manipulating this feedback loop genetically or mechanically to test predictions about optimal growth paths under constraint.

The second representative kind of tropisms is phototropism and resource optimization. The classic phototropic bending is a solution to the problem of optimizing light capture while minimizing structural cost. Research should investigate trade-offs: When does a plant prioritize stem elongation toward a light gap versus reinforcing existing structure? High-throughput phenotyping combined with functional-structural plant models (FSPMs) can quantify the efficiency of different growth strategies in complex light environments.40

The third representative kind of tropisms is hydronasty and thigmotropism. The coiling of tendrils or roots around supports (thigmotropism) and the hygroscopic movements of seed pods (hydronasty) are exquisite examples of morphological computation. The energy for movement is stored in the pre-stressed tissue, and the specific environmental trigger (touch, humidity) releases it. Studying the molecular control of cell wall elasticity and the evolution of these structures can reveal principles for designing passive, energy-autonomous actuators.

Then, phenotypic plasticity can be considered as a calculated bet-hedging strategy. Plasticity—the ability of a single genotype to produce different phenotypes in different environments—is a cornerstone of PI.41 PEI research asks: How do plants decide on the optimal phenotype? The first deciding factor is the root:shoot allocation. The classic functional equilibrium model posits that plants allocate biomass to acquire the most limiting resource. This can be framed as a continuous optimization problem. Experiments using split-root systems, stable isotope labeling, and transcriptomic analysis can dissect the signaling networks that integrate local and systemic resource status to command this allocation decision.42

The second deciding factor involves shade avoidance vs. tolerance. Upon perceiving neighbor proximity via low red:far-red light, a plant faces a critical decision: invest in rapid stem elongation to overtop competitors (shade avoidance) or enhance photosynthetic efficiency under shade (shade tolerance). This decision involves weighing the costs (energetic, structural fragility) against the probabilistic benefit of winning the competition. Genetic screens for mutants that “misjudge” this situation can identify the molecular components of this evaluative process.43

Avenue 2: distributed information processing, integration, and somatic memory

This avenue targets the “software” of PEI: how plants acquire, process, store, and act upon information across their decentralized bodies. The first primary research direction is aimed at mapping the plant “connectome.” A major challenge is to move beyond studying single signaling pathways to understanding the network architecture of plant information processing.

The first specific exploration task in this research direction is investigating electrical and ion signaling networks. Using microelectrode arrays and genetically encoded voltage/calcium indicators (e.g., GCaMP), researchers can map the spatiotemporal dynamics of electrical and calcium waves in response to localized stimuli (wounding, light).44 This allows us to characterize the plant’s systemic “neural” geography—identifying propagation pathways, integration hubs, and signal gating mechanisms.

The second specific research task is studying hormone interaction networks. Phytohormones (auxin, jasmonate, abscisic acid, and so forth) are not isolated messengers but interact in complex networks. Combining hormone profiling, mutant analysis, and network inference algorithms can reveal how different stress signals are weighted, integrated, and translated into prioritized responses. For instance, how does a plant resolve the conflict between responding to drought (ABA signal) and an herbivore attack (JA signal)?45

The second primary research direction is quantifying learning and somatic memory. Demonstrating associative learning and long-term memory in plants provides powerful evidence for advanced information processing.

The first specific exploration task in this research direction is examining habituation and sensitization. Gagliano et al. showed Mimosa pudica can habituate to repeated harmless dropping, ceasing to fold its leaves—and retain this memory for weeks.19 Replicating and extending this work to other species and stimuli (e.g., habituation to specific herbivore-associated VOCs) is crucial. The search for the cellular basis of this memory (e.g., epigenetic changes at specific loci, persistent metabolic states) is a key frontier.

The second specific exploration focuses on priming of defense. Systemic acquired resistance and induced systemic resistance are well-established forms of immunological memory. PEI research seeks to understand their cognitive parameters: How specific is the memory (general vs. pathogen-specific)? What is the memory’s capacity and duration? How is the cost of maintaining a primed state balanced against the probability of future attack?46

Avenue 3: ecological communication and emergent “swarm” behavior

This avenue scales up from the individual plant to the collective, examining intelligence in the context of plant-plant and plant-other organism interactions. The first representative research direction is deciphering the chemical language. Plant volatile and root exudate blends constitute a rich, complex chemical language.

The first specific exploration task in this research direction is probing kin recognition and altruism. Plants can often distinguish self from non-self and kin from non-kin through root exudates or VOCs, adjusting competitive behaviors accordingly.47,48 Research must identify the specific chemical fingerprints involved and the genetic basis of their perception. Does this recognition lead to true cooperation (e.g., reduced root competition with kin), and what are the evolutionary dynamics? The second specific exploration task concerns eavesdropping and deception. Plants emit VOCs upon herbivory that attract predators of the herbivore (a “call for help”). Unattacked neighbors can “eavesdrop” on these signals to prime their own defenses.21 Conversely, some orchids emit sex pheromones to deceive male insects into pollinating them. Studying the evolution of this communication—its reliability, costs of production, and susceptibility to manipulation—places it within the framework of signaling theory from behavioral ecology.

The second representative research direction is considering the mycorrhizal internet as a cognitive extension. The CMN represents a radical form of extended cognition. Experiments must test their functional role in community-level intelligence. In this research direction, the first specific exploration task is analyzing resource redistribution. Do CMNs facilitate the directed transfer of carbon from source to sink plants in a community, acting as a communal resource-sharing grid?27 The second exploration task is developing early warning systems. Does stress signaling (e.g., aphid attack on one plant) travel faster or more efficiently via CMNs than through the air, enabling pre-emptive defense in connected neighbors?49 Network analysis of CMN architecture and controlled isotope/signal molecule tracing are key methods here.

Avenue 4: decision-making, risk assessment, and game-theoretic interactions

This most advanced avenue applies frameworks from economics, psychology, and theoretical biology to plant behavior, probing the depth of their evaluative capacities. The first representative research direction is seeking optimal foraging under uncertainty. Root foraging is an ideal model for testing decision-making theories. Experiments can present plants with soil environments containing resource patches of differing quality, predictability, and spatial distribution.

The specific research tasks involve risk-sensitive foraging. Will a plant’s roots preferentially forage in a stable, low-nutrient patch versus a variable, high-nutrient patch? This tests whether plants exhibit risk-aversion or risk-proneness, a classic metric in animal behavioral ecology.50 The specific research tasks also involve establishing the marginal value theorem. Do roots leave a depleting resource patch at the time predicted by the marginal value theorem (when the instantaneous harvest rate drops below the average for the entire environment)? Testing this requires precise tracking of individual root growth and nutrient uptake in heterogeneous environments.51

The second representative research direction is addressing the game theory in plant competition. Plant interactions can be modeled as strategic games. The specific research tasks are aimed at elaborating the tragedy of the commons at its roots. In shared soil, individuals may over-proliferate roots at the expense of collective yield. Research can use isogenic lines to test whether plants adjust root proliferation differently when competing with self, kin, or strangers, and whether “cooperative” genotypes can invade “selfish” populations under different selection regimes.52 The specific research tasks also concern the shade avoidance game. In a dense stand, the benefit of elongating depends on what neighbors do. This creates a game similar to the Prisoner’s Dilemma. Modeling and experiments with mutants altered in their perception (phytochrome mutants) or response (gibberellin biosynthesis mutants) can reveal the evolutionarily stable strategies in canopy competition.13

The third representative research direction is regarding pollinator attraction as a dynamic investment. Floral display size, nectar production, and scent emission represent a costly investment to attract pollinators. This can be viewed as a dynamic optimization problem where the plant must adjust its investment based on pollinator visitation rates, resource availability, and the behavior of co-flowering competitors. Experiments that manipulate these factors and measure the plant’s plastic response in floral traits can reveal sophisticated adaptive tracking abilities.53

In total, these four research avenues, as illustrated in Figure 1, provide a structured, interdisciplinary program to explore PEI. From the biomechanics of a coiling tendril to the strategic games played in a forest canopy, each avenue offers testable hypotheses that move the field beyond descriptive phenomenology. By employing tools from molecular biology, quantitative phenotyping, ecological modeling, and theoretical biology, researchers can begin to crack the code of how plants—through their embodied, distributed, and interactive existence—solve the complex problems of life.

Global research landscape: status and gaps

The investigation of PEI is a global, interdisciplinary endeavor, with distinct intellectual traditions and focal points shaping research across different regions. Understanding this landscape—its centers of gravity, methodological approaches, and inherent limitations—is crucial for identifying synergies and bridging critical gaps that currently constrain the field.

European leadership: philosophical foundations, integrated physiology, and ecological behavior

Europe has been the historic epicenter for pioneering the conceptual shift toward viewing plants as active, intelligent agents, blending strong traditions in philosophy, physiology, and ecology.•Italy—the international plant neurobiology movement: The International Laboratory of Plant Neurobiology (LINV) in Florence, under the leadership of Stefano Mancuso, has been the most vocal and visible advocate for PI. Mancuso’s work emphasizes the decentralized, network-based nature of plant cognition, drawing analogies to swarm intelligence and the internet. His group’s experimental focus includes root apex electrophysiology, the study of plant-plant communication via volatiles, and the development of plant-based biosensors.24,54 The LINV also spearheads the application of PEI principles to architecture and design, envisioning plants as components of “bio-urban” systems. North American focus: systems biology, molecular mechanisms, and bio-robotics

North American research tends to be more pragmatic, focusing on deconstructing mechanisms and applying insights to engineering, often with a more cautious stance on the language of intelligence.•USA—From molecular networks to bio-inspired systems: The dominant paradigm is systems biology. Research at institutions such as the Salk Institute, Stanford, and the University of California focuses on mapping the incredibly complex interaction networks of phytohormones, transcription factors, and signaling molecules that underpin plasticity and systemic responses.42,45 This work provides the essential mechanistic “parts list” but often stops short of interpreting these networks in a cognitive framework. Concurrently, a strong bio-inspired robotics and soft robotics community actively draws inspiration from plants. Emerging interdisciplinary hubs and initiatives

The field is coalescing around formal interdisciplinary structures. The first structure is the Society for Plant Signaling and Behavior. This international society provides a crucial forum for dialogue between physiologists, ecologists, and theorists sympathetic to the PEI perspective. The second structure is the large-scale collaborative projects. EU-funded projects like PLANTSWARM (aimed at developing a swarm of plant-inspired robots) and FET-Open GROW have explicitly framed their goals around PI principles. In the U.S., projects like Distributed Autonomous Robotic Technologies—DART explore swarm behaviors that are indirectly informed by plant colony models.

There are also gaps at the consortium level. Despite these initiatives, sustained, large-scale funding specifically dedicated to testing the core hypotheses of PEI—as opposed to applied bio-inspired engineering—remains scarce. True interdisciplinary collaboration, where biologists and roboticists/computer scientists co-design experiments and models from the outset, remians the exception rather than the rule.

In general, the global research landscape for PEI is vibrant and diverse, yet fragmented. Europe provides the philosophical and integrative physiological depth, North America excels in mechanistic deconstruction and bio-inspired engineering, and Asia leads in technological fusion and data-driven approaches. The critical gaps lie in: (1) bridging the molecular-to-cognitive divide, (2) developing universally accepted quantitative metrics for PI, (3) fostering genuinely co-equal interdisciplinary collaborations, and (4) establishing robust experimental paradigms for studying plant learning and decision-making that can withstand scrutiny from skeptical mainstream biology. The future of PEI depends on integrating these regional strengths into a coherent global effort that respects biological complexity while boldly pursuing its cognitive implications.

Core research challenges and bottlenecks

The ambitious pursuit of PEI as a rigorous scientific discipline is fraught with significant conceptual, methodological, and technical obstacles. These challenges are not merely impediments to progress but also define the very frontiers of the field. Successfully addressing them is a prerequisite for moving PEI from a provocative perspective to a predictive and transformative science. This section delineates and analyzes these core bottlenecks.

Conceptual and semantic hurdles: overcoming anthropocentric bias

The most fundamental challenge is not empirical but philosophical and linguistic. The leading one involves anthropocentric and zoocentric bias. A deep-seated, often unconscious, bias equates intelligence with animal-like attributes: rapid movement, a central brain, and conscious awareness. This bias leads to a categorical rejection of the idea that sessile, non-neural organisms could be intelligent. As Taiz et al. argued, this conflation risks obscuring the unique and sophisticated mechanisms plants use to adapt.23 Overcoming this requires a sustained effort to educate and promote the enactive and embodied frameworks that decouple intelligence from neural architecture, emphasizing function over substrate.

The second fundamental challenge relates to the lexical minefield. The terminology of cognition, including learning, memory, decision-making, and intelligence, is heavily laden with animal-centric connotations. Their application to plants is often met with accusations of anthropomorphism, which can stifle discourse.32 The field needs to develop a rigorous, operationalized lexicon. For example, plant memory should be explicitly defined as a change in physiological state, based on prior experience, that alters future behavior in a functionally adaptive manner, distinct from declarative or episodic memory in animals. Similarly, decision-making should be framed as the process by which conflicting sensory or internal signals are integrated to produce a non-random, context-appropriate phenotypic output.

Quantification and metrics: defining and measuring “smartness”

Without quantifiable metrics, claims about intelligence remain subjective. How do we objectively compare the intelligence of a Mimosa pudica to an oak tree, or to a robot? While rough indices of PI have been proposed,33 the degrees of PI in Mimosa habituation19 and CMNs27,28 need to be quantified for assessment. The first solution plan is to develop standardized behavioral assays. Animal cognition research relies on standardized tests (mazes, associative learning paradigms). PEI requires its own suite of assays. These could include learning speed and retention. Standardized protocols for habituation (e.g., repeated mechanical stimulus) and latent inhibition, measuring the number of trials to the cessation of response and the duration of memory retention.19 The suite of assays also needs to consider problem-solving efficiency. Tasks like navigating a root through a complex obstacle maze or re-orienting leaves after a canopy disturbance. Metrics could be time-to-solution, energy expenditure, or final biomass accrued compared to a non-adaptive control. Further, the suite of assays shall present adaptive flexibility.65 The ability to switch strategies when a previously successful one fails. For example, if a nutrient patch is moved, how quickly and efficiently does the root system re-allocate its foraging effort?

Another solution plan is going from qualitative description to quantitative modeling. Many observations in PEI are descriptive. The field needs to adopt mathematical frameworks to quantify behavior. Information theory can be used to measure how much environmental information a plant’s response reduces (e.g., how a tropic response reduces uncertainty about resource location). Control theory and optimality modeling can be used to assess how close a plant’s response is to a theoretically optimal solution given constraints, providing a benchmark for “smartness.”10 In a whole sense, a taxonomy of plant responses that discriminates between adaptive plasticity, distributed regulation, and predictive inference needs to be explored next.

Temporal and spatial scale integration

Plant cognition operates across scales that are difficult to observe simultaneously, creating a disconnect in our understanding. This “disconnect” is first rooted in the timescale problem. Plant processes span milliseconds (action potentials), minutes (leaf movements), hours/days (tropic growth), seasons (phenology), and generations (epigenetic memory). A key challenge is to understand how rapid signaling events (e.g., a calcium wave) are transduced into slow morphological changes, and how short-term experiences are encoded into long-term somatic memory. Current experimental methods are rarely designed to capture this multi-scale causality.

The “disconnect” also stems from the spatial scale problem. Intelligence emerges from the interaction of modules (cells, tissues, organs) distributed across the plant body. However, most molecular and physiological data are collected from homogenized tissues or localized measurements. We lack technologies for real-time, whole-organism, multi-modal phenotyping that can correlate a local stimulus (e.g., a herbivore bite on one leaf) with systemic changes in gene expression, hormone fluxes, electrical activity, and eventual growth responses across the entire plant, over relevant time scales.

Experimental control vs. ecological relevance

There is a fundamental tension between the need for controlled experiments and the ecological reality in which PI evolved. This is reflected by the situation of the laboratory plant vs. the ecological plant.62 Model organisms like Arabidopsis thaliana are bred for laboratory tractability: small, fast-growing, with a sequenced genome. However, their behavioral repertoire and ecological interactions are severely reduced. Studying intelligence may require working with “smarter,” more ecologically complex species (vines, carnivorous plants, long-lived trees), which are genetically and experimentally intractable.

The other fundamental tension relates to the complexity of the environment. Plant cognition is an open-loop interaction with an immensely complex environment—soil microbiomes, fluctuating light and water, and numerous other organisms.65 Reducing this to a controlled chamber with sterile agar and single-wavelength light strips away the very context that shapes intelligent behavior. Designing experiments that are both controlled enough for causality and complex enough to be ecologically meaningful is a major experimental design challenge.

Lack of unified theoretical and computational models

Unlike neuroscience, which has computational models of neural networks, or ecology, which has population models, PEI lacks a standard theoretical framework to integrate data and generate testable predictions. This is due to the lack of a “model plant” in silico. There is no equivalent of a “connectome” model for plants that integrates physiology, morphology, and environment. While FSPMs such as OpenAlea or GroIMP exist, they primarily simulate resource acquisition and growth based on biophysical rules, not decision-making or learning.40 Agent-based models (ABMs) that simulate meristems or root tips as autonomous agents with simple behavioral rules are a promising start but need to be grounded in deeper biological data.35

Another reason is the shortage of an integrating mechanism and function. A grand challenge is to build a multi-scale model that can take molecular signaling network data (e.g., hormone crosstalk) as input and predict whole-plant behavioral outcomes (e.g., a shift in root:shoot ratio) in a variable environment. Such a model would be the ultimate tool for testing hypotheses about PEI but requires bridging vastly different fields of expertise.

Funding and institutional recognition

Finally, the field faces pragmatic hurdles. Grant review panels in traditional plant science or NSF/NIH programs may be dismissive of proposals framed around the PI. The interdisciplinary nature of PEI—spanning biology, computer science, robotics, and philosophy—makes it difficult to fit into conventional funding categories. Building institutional homes and dedicated funding streams (similar to those for bio-inspired engineering or complex systems science) is essential for attracting and sustaining the talent needed to tackle these deep challenges.

As a result, the path forward for PEI is paved with significant, but not insurmountable, challenges. They range from the philosophical (redefining intelligence) to the intensely practical (measuring behavior). Addressing them will require more than incremental advances; it will demand the development of new experimental paradigms, novel measurement technologies, unifying theoretical frameworks, and a cultural shift within the biological sciences. Each bottleneck, however, represents a fertile ground for innovation. Successfully navigating them will not only validate PEI but also yield methodologies and insights with broad applicability across the life and engineering sciences.

A convergent methodology: bridging biology and intelligent systems science

To overcome the formidable challenges outlined in Section 5, the study of PEI requires a radical, convergent methodology. This approach must form a closed-loop cycle between biological discovery and engineering synthesis, where each discipline rigorously informs and tests the other. Step 1: high-resolution, multi-modal quantitative phenotyping (“the plant dashboard”)

The foundation is the creation of dense, multi-dimensional datasets capturing plant behavior and internal states in real-time under controlled yet ecologically relevant scenarios. This moves beyond single-trait measurement to a holistic “phenotype as behavior” perspective. The output of this step is to generate high-dimensional time-series data—a “plant behavioral fingerprint” —linking external stimuli, internal signaling (electrical, chemical), and ultimate phenotypic outcome.

To achieve the goal, we need to first develop advanced imaging and sensing suites. Such suites need to deploy automated, high-throughput phenomics platforms capable of non-invasive, 3D imaging (e.g., laser scanning, photogrammetry, MRI for roots) to track morphological dynamics of shoots and roots with high temporal resolution (e.g., minutes to hours). The platforms can integrate with hyperspectral and chlorophyll fluorescence imaging, which can map photosynthetic activity, water stress, and pigment composition as proxies for physiological state. The platforms can also integrate with distributed electrophysiology, which can use flexible microelectrode arrays and/or genetically encoded voltage indicators (e.g., Voltron) to create a spatial map of electrical activity across the plant in response to stimuli.33 The platforms can integrate with VOC profiling, which can do real-time mass spectrometry (e.g., PTR-MS) to monitor the chemical “broadcast” of the plant as a dynamic output signal.

We also need to conduct spatially and temporally resolved omics. Instead of whole-plant homogenates, we can use techniques such as laser-capture microdissection or spatial transcriptomics to sample specific tissues (e.g., the stimulated leaf, the systemic root tip, and the vascular tissue) at key time points during the behavioral response. This allows mapping of transcriptomic and proteomic shifts.

Further, we need to design controlled environmental stress scenarios. Plants are placed in “cognitive challenge chambers” where they face controlled, complex decision-making tasks. The specific examples include patchy resource foraging. It focuses on soil columns with dynamically controlled, spatially heterogeneous nutrient or water patches to test risk-sensitive foraging and resource allocation decisions.51 Examples also involve social interaction arenas. They are the chambers where plants can interact with kin, non-kin, or competitors via light, volatiles, and shared rhizospheres, quantifying behavioral plasticity in social contexts.47 We are also concerned with predictive cue paradigms. They are aimed at exposing plants to cues that reliably predict a future event (e.g., a specific green leaf volatile preceding simulated herbivory) to test for associative learning.19

Step 2: molecular systems biology deconstruction (“the mechanism map”)

Concurrently, a deep molecular dissection is performed on the same experimental plants to uncover the mechanistic basis of the observed behaviors. The output of this step is to generate a mechanistic network model—a causal map linking genetic elements, molecular pathways, and signaling events to specific components of the observed intelligent behavior.

First, omics analysis of mapped transcriptomic and proteomic shifts can identify genes and proteins activated in different parts of the plant during information processing and response execution. This also allows hormone and metabolite profiling, which can quantify the fluxes of key signaling molecules (auxin, jasmonate, ABA, and ROS) to reconstruct the chemical “logic” of the decision.

Second, perturbation analysis uses genetic mutants (e.g., in hormone signaling, ion channels, and electrical propagation), pharmacological inhibitors, or optogenetic tools (where available) to selectively disrupt specific pathways. The impact on the quantified behavioral metrics from Step 1 is then measured. Does disrupting a specific calcium channel abolish the systemic warning signal? Does silencing a VOC synthase alter kin recognition behavior? Such questions need to be answered.

Step 3: computational modeling and simulation (“the in silico plant”)

The data from steps 1 and 2 are used to construct and refine computational models that embody hypotheses about the algorithms of PI. This requires multi-scale, multi-paradigm modeling. The representative models include the ABMs, which simulate the plant as a population of semi-autonomous agents (meristems, root tips). Each agent follows simple local rules (e.g., growing toward higher moisture, branching if nutrient uptake is high) derived from biological data. The global behavior (canopy form, root architecture) emerges from their interactions, allowing testing of swarm intelligence hypotheses.35 The representative models also include dynamical systems models, which formalize the hormone and electrical signaling networks identified in Step 2 as systems of differential equations. These models can simulate how the network integrates multiple inputs (e.g., wound signal and light signal) to produce a coherent output (e.g., prioritized defense response). The third kind of representative models are finite element biomechanical models, which simulate the mechanical aspects of embodied cognition—how tissue stresses, growth, and external forces interact. This is essential for modeling tropisms, nastic movements, and morphological adaptation.10

Then, the next step mission is to finish model integration and validation. The ultimate goal is a hierarchical model where the ABM’s agent rules are modulated by the outputs of the dynamical systems model (hormone levels), while the ABM’s growth alters the geometry input for the biomechanical model. These models are iteratively validated and refined against the high-fidelity phenotypic data from Step 1. A successful model should not only replicate known behavior but also generate novel, testable predictions.

Step 4: physical instantiation: plant-inspired robotics and algorithms (“the synthetic testbed”)

This is the critical synthesis step. Insights and algorithms from the biological system and its models are used to engineer novel, non-biological systems. The performance of these synthetic systems serves as the ultimate test of our understanding. The output of this step includes functional prototypes of robots, materials, and software algorithms whose design is directly informed by biological principles of PEI. Moreover, we can refer to the experiences and strategies from the non-biological systems for promoting plant-inspired robotics. For example, chemists found that inanimate chemical systems in wetware can reproduce some biological intelligence competencies,66 and even, an ambitious goal of chemical AI is to develop chemical robots.67 These non-biological findings may give new ideas for advancing the physical instantiation of PEI, even characterizing its underlying mechanism.

The specific programs start from developing soft, growable robotics. This is to design and build robots that embody key PEI principles. A representative example is vine and root robots—systems that navigate by tip-based growth (through material extrusion or eversion), capable of decentralized exploration and anchoring in complex, unstructured environments such as rubble or soil.55,58 Another example is material intelligence. We need to create synthetic materials and actuators that perform morphological computation, e.g., 4D-printed structures that change shape autonomously in response to humidity or light, mimicking pine cones or heliotropic flowers.

Soft, growable robotics can be enhanced into swarm robotic systems. We need to deploy large collectives of simple, cheap robots whose collective behavior is governed by plant-inspired algorithms (e.g., stigmergy, decentralized resource allocation). For example, a swarm tasked with mapping and sampling a contaminated area could use a root-foraging algorithm to efficiently cover the space. Further, the soft, growable robotics can be strengthened by developing novel distributed algorithms. Implement the control logic of plant signaling networks in software for non-robotic applications. An auxin-transport-inspired algorithm could be used for adaptive resource routing in peer-to-peer networks or data centers. A plant systemic acquired resistance model could inspire new intrusion detection and response protocols for cybersecurity.

Step 5: validation and the feedback loop (“the crucible of comparison”)

The engineered systems are not merely applications; they are rigorous experimental tools. The validation is operated by using comparative performance analysis. The plant-inspired robot or algorithm is tested on the same tasks given to the biological plant in Step 1 (e.g., the patchy foraging task, the obstacle navigation task). Does the synthetic system achieve similar or superior performance metrics (speed, efficiency, robustness)? Where does it fail? These failures are particularly informative, highlighting gaps in our biological understanding or aspects of PI not yet captured by the model.

The validation can derive generating novel biological questions. The engineering process forces a distillation of biological complexity into minimal, functional principles. This abstraction often reveals new, fundamental questions: “What is the minimal set of local rules needed to generate this complex root architecture?,” “Is the plant’s signaling network optimally robust, or is there redundancy we missed?” These questions are then fed back into Step 1, designing new biological experiments to probe these refined hypotheses.

Totally, the proposed convergent methodology, as summarized in Figure 1, is a blueprint for a new kind of science. It rejects the linear model of basic research followed by applied development. By building a bridge where data, models, and physical instantiations constantly cross-fertilize, this approach offers the most promising path to not only understanding the profound intelligence inherent in plants but also to harnessing its principles to solve some of our most pressing technological and environmental challenges.

Reciprocal impacts: how PEI drives dual advancement

The exploration of PEI is not a one-way street of extracting biological inspiration. It establishes a powerful, bidirectional flow of knowledge and innovation. As the convergent methodology (Section 6) is implemented, it generates profound, reciprocal impacts that can fundamentally advance both plant sciences and intelligent systems sciences, as schematically illustrated in Figure 1. This virtuous cycle transforms PEI from an interesting subfield into a central engine for discovery and invention.

Transformative insights for plant sciences: from mechanism to meaning

Embracing the PEI framework forces a paradigm shift within plant biology itself, moving from a reductionist cataloging of parts to an integrative understanding of function and purpose. First, PEI can supply a unifying theoretical framework for decoding complex phenomena. Plant biology is rich in detailed descriptions of isolated processes: phototropism, systemic acquired resistance, root architecture plasticity, and shade avoidance. PEI provides a meta-framework that connects these disparate phenomena under the umbrella of adaptive problem-solving. It asks not just “how does shade avoidance work?” but “what computational problem does it solve, and what are the trade-offs involved in its algorithm?” This shifts the focus to understanding the logic of life—how plants weigh costs and benefits, integrate conflicting signals, and make decisions that enhance fitness. It can reconcile seemingly contradictory observations, such as why a plant might sometimes tolerate herbivory rather than mount a costly defense, by framing it as a resource allocation decision under uncertainty.29

Next, the research on PEI can derive novel questions and the discovery of new mechanisms. Viewing plants as cognitive agents prompts experimental questions that would not arise from a purely mechanistic perspective. This leads to the discovery of new biological capacities and underlying mechanisms: (1) beyond basic learning: The search for associative learning in plants19 has opened a new field. Future work may look for more complex forms of learning, such as latent learning (learning about an environment in the absence of immediate reward) or social learning (acquiring information by observing the responses of neighboring plants). The hunt for the molecular and cellular substrates of these forms of memory—beyond simple priming—will push the frontiers of plant epigenetics and systems physiology. (2) quantifying “plant personality:” PEI provides the tools to study consistent behavioral variation among individual plants of the same species—their “personality” (e.g., bold vs. cautious foragers, tolerant vs. competitive neighbors). This intra-specific variation, often treated as noise, can be studied as a potential adaptive strategy,65 linking genetics to behavioral ecology in plants.68 (3) deciphering plant “connectome:” The drive to understand information integration will accelerate the mapping of the plant’s long-distance signaling networks with unprecedented spatial and temporal resolution. This could reveal specialized signaling tissues or hubs, akin to information routing centers, and novel signaling molecules beyond the known hormones and peptides.

Then, the PEI study facilitates revolutionizing applied plant sciences such as precision agriculture and forestry: Understanding crops and forests as intelligent communities, rather than passive production units, will lead to transformative management practices. This may lead to predictive and communicative agronomy. By decoding the specific electrical or VOC signatures associated with particular nutrient deficiencies, water stress, or earstage pathogen attack, we can develop real-time, plant-driven diagnostic tools. Farmers could receive alerts from their fields before symptoms are visibly apparent. Furthermore, we could move toward “cue-based priming:” applying specific, benign environmental cues (e.g., light patterns, sound frequencies, specific VOCs) to intentionally trigger beneficial adaptive responses in crops, such as pre-emptive defense activation or optimized root growth, reducing reliance on pesticides and fertilizers.54 Further, the revolution brought by PEI may support resilient ecosystem design. In forestry and restoration ecology, understanding the intelligent, communicative networks within plant communities (via CMNs and VOCs) allows for smarter planting strategies. We could design communities that foster cooperation, efficient resource sharing, and collective resilience to pests and climate stress, accelerating restoration and enhancing carbon sequestration.27

Pioneering innovations for intelligent systems science: biology as a guide

Plants offer a billion-year-old R&D portfolio for solving problems of adaptation, resilience, and efficiency in complex, unpredictable environments. PEI serves as the interpreter of this portfolio for engineers and computer scientists. The first aspect focuses on resilient, energy-autonomous, and adaptive robotics. Current robots excel in structured environments but fail in unstructured, dynamic ones. Plants offer a blueprint for a new robotics paradigm, involving (1) growth as a strategy: Plant-inspired growing robots that add material at the tip to navigate confined spaces (e.g., rubble after disasters, pipelines, or extraterrestrial soil) represent a radical departure from traditional locomotion.58 Their embodied intelligence lies in the tip-based sensory guidance of the growth process itself. (2) material intelligence and passive adaptation: Plants achieve complex movements without motors, using passive material properties and environmental energy. This inspires 4D-printed structures and soft robots that change shape in response to ambient humidity, temperature, or light, enabling maintenance-free environmental monitoring or adaptive building skins.25 (3) decentralized control and morphological computation: PEI principles lead to robots with radically simplified central controllers. Intelligence is offloaded to the body’s morphology and the local interactions of many simple modules (a swarm of rootlet robots). This enhances robustness; damage to one part does not cripple the whole system, much like a plant can lose a branch and thrive.8

The second representative aspect relates to deriving novel algorithms for distributed computing and network design. Plant signaling networks are masterclasses in scalable, robust, and energy-frugal information management. The potential novel algorithms may cover: (1) auxin-transport-inspired routing: The polar transport of auxin, which directs resources to areas of greatest need/sink strength, is a brilliant algorithm for dynamic resource allocation. This can inspire new protocols for load balancing in data centers, traffic routing in vehicular networks, or resource distribution in smart grids.69 (2) systemic signaling for cybersecurity: a plant’s systemic acquired resistance, where a local infection triggers a slow, systemic immune preparation, is analogous to an intrusion detection and response system. Local detector nodes identify a threat and broadcast a slow, reliable alert that primes the entire network (the plant) for a future, similar attack. This inspires novel cybersecurity architectures that are proactive and resilient rather than merely reactive. (3) stigmergic coordination for swarm robotics and IoT: the use of environmental modification (stigmergy) for coordination, as seen in root foraging, is perfect for swarms of drones or ground robots exploring an unknown area. Robots could leave temporary digital or physical markers, creating a collectively built map that guides others, without direct communication.

The third aspect may project new paradigms in AI and machine learning—moving beyond the data-hungry, energy-intensive paradigm of deep learning. This may lead to the following innovations. (1) Embodied and developmental AI: current AI lacks a body and a developmental trajectory. PEI inspires research into “growing” artificial neural networks whose architecture is not fixed but develops based on interaction with a task environment, optimizing itself for efficiency and robustness—much like a plant’s body plan emerges from experience. (2) Active inference and energy-aware computation: The plant’s behavior can be viewed as minimizing “surprise” or prediction error about its future states (active inference within a Bayesian framework). This offers a formal, mathematically rigorous framework for designing AI agents that actively seek information and maintain themselves in viable states with minimal energy expenditure—a critical step toward true artificial autonomy.38 (3) Lifelong learning in open worlds: plants must learn continuously in a non-stationary world without catastrophic forgetting. Studying how they integrate new experiences with old memories (e.g., through epigenetic modifications that create a “somatic gradient”) could provide biological clues for solving the lifelong learning problem in AI.

Overall, the reciprocal impacts of PEI research are profound and wide-ranging. For plant science, it offers a unifying, purpose-driven narrative that elevates the field from descriptive cataloging to a science of adaptive agency. It promises smarter, more sustainable interactions with the plant world. For intelligent systems science, it provides a vast, untapped reservoir of proven solutions for resilience, adaptation, and decentralized control, guiding us toward a new generation of robots, algorithms, and AI that are more in harmony with the complex, real world. By seriously studying PI, we stand to gain not only a deeper appreciation for our photosynthetic partners but also the keys to building a more adaptive and sustainable technological future.

Future perspectives and concluding remarks

The journey into PEI represents more than a niche scientific curiosity; it signifies a fundamental reorientation in our relationship with the living world and our conception of intelligence itself. As this review has outlined, the theoretical foundations are robust, the research avenues are clear, and the potential for reciprocal impact is immense. Looking ahead, the future of PEI is poised at the threshold of profound transformation, driven by technological convergence, evolving ethical considerations, and its inevitable integration into the scientific mainstream. This final section envisions the future and underscores the paradigm’s ultimate significance.

Envisioning the future of PEI research: speculative horizons

The next decade will likely witness the maturation of PEI from a provocative hypothesis into an established, data-rich discipline, characterized by several key developments. The first potential key development is triggering the rise of the “cognitome” and standardized model systems. We will move beyond Arabidopsis thaliana as the sole model. A new generation of “behavioral model plants” will be established—species chosen for their rich cognitive repertoire, such as the problem-solving pea (Pisum sativum), the communicative lima bean (Phaseolus lunatus), or the long-lived, strategically competitive oak (Quercus spp.). For these species, we will strive to build a complete “cognitome,” i.e., a comprehensive atlas linking specific genes and molecular pathways to quantifiable behavioral traits and learning capacities, much like the connectome in neuroscience.

The second potential key development is the emergence of ubiquitous biohybrid and cyborg systems. The boundary between plant and machine will blur. We will see the widespread development of plant-cyborg systems where living plants are seamlessly integrated with micro-electronics and nano-sensors. These will not be mere monitors but closed-loop control systems. That is, a plant’s electrical signal could directly control irrigation drones, or its VOC emission could trigger the release of beneficial insects in a precision agriculture setup.70 Plants will become active, sensing, and decision-making components of our built environment and agricultural infrastructure.

The third potential key development is establishing a formal mathematical language for plant cognition. The field will develop its own rigorous mathematical frameworks. Information-theoretic measures will quantify how much environmental uncertainty a plant’s behavior reduces. Control theory and optimality models will provide null hypotheses against which to measure the efficiency of plant decisions. Topological data analysis may be used to characterize the complex shape of root systems or canopies as a signature of explored versus unexplored environmental spaces. This formalization will be the key to making PEI a predictive, rather than merely descriptive, science.

The fourth potential key development is opening ethical, philosophical, and legal frontiers. As evidence for PI, learning, and perhaps even forms of sentience (e.g., valence-based assessment of stimuli) solidifies, it will force a societal reckoning. Plant ethics will emerge as a serious field of inquiry.71 Questions will arise: Do intelligent plants have moral standing? What are the implications for industrial agriculture, deforestation, and genetic modification? While the debate around plant consciousness is distinct from intelligence, the demonstration of sophisticated cognitive capacities will undoubtedly influence public perception and potentially inform new legal frameworks for plant welfare and conservation, paralleling the evolving status of animals.

The transformative potential: a concluding synthesis

In conclusion, the pursuit of PEI is a necessary and timely scientific revolution with ramifications that extend far beyond the laboratory. As a critical branch, its research can advance PI to develop in parallel with AI.72 Its scientific revolution may also mean redefining life’s narrative. PEI challenges the last bastion of human exceptionalism—the monopoly on complex intelligence. By demonstrating that sophisticated problem-solving, learning, memory, and communication are emergent properties of life itself, achievable through myriad biological architectures, it places humans back within, not above, the continuum of natural intelligence. It tells a new story of life on Earth, one of pervasive, embodied cleverness.

The scientific revolution may also change our cognition of plants as partners, not products. This paradigm shift has practical urgency. In the face of climate change and biodiversity collapse, we can no longer afford to view plants as passive resources. Understanding them as intelligent agents within resilient, communicative ecosystems is critical for predicting their responses, fostering their health, and collaborating with them to restore planetary balance. Sustainable agriculture, adaptive forestry, and ecosystem restoration must be guided by principles learned from the intelligence inherent in plant communities.

The scientific revolution may further project a wellspring for sustainable innovation. Perhaps most compellingly, plants offer a masterclass in sustainability. They are solar-powered, self-repairing, recycle all their components, and thrive on feedback loops with their environment. By decoding the principles of PEI—decentralized control, morphological computation, stigmergic coordination, and predictive plasticity as pointed out in Figure 1—we gain a blueprint for building a regenerative technology. The robots, networks, and materials of the future, inspired by plants, will be inherently adaptive, resilient, and low-energy, helping to solve human challenges without exacerbating environmental ones.

The scientific revolution may inspire some ultimate interdisciplinary endeavors. The study of PEI is perhaps the ultimate interdisciplinary challenge, demanding the integration of cell biology, ecology, physics, chemistry, computer science, complex systems science, robotics, philosophy, and ethics. Its success will depend on creating a new generation of scientists—phytocognitivists, who are fluent in this broad language. In doing so, it can serve as a model for tackling other complex, systemic challenges that defy narrow disciplinary boundaries.

The root of this new understanding lies not in a quest to find a brain in a stem, but in the recognition that intelligence was here all along—in the slow, deliberate turn of a leaf toward the sun, in the patient exploration of a root through the soil, in the silent chemical conversation of a forest. By choosing to see it, we open ourselves to a deeper wisdom. We begin to learn from the oldest and most successful life forms on our planet, not just about them. In exploring PEI, we may ultimately cultivate a smarter, more humble, and more sustainable future for all.
