Plasma Biomarkers for Alzheimer’s Disease Across the Continuum: A Systematic Review of SIMOA-Based Studies
Authors: María de los Ángeles Fernández-Ceballos, José M. Prieto-González, Roberto Carlos Agís-Balboa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is the most common cause of dementia, and its overall incidence is rising as the proportion of older individuals in the population increases. Traditional diagnostic methods, such as lumbar puncture and neuroimaging, are costly and invasive, driving the search for blood-based biomarkers (BBB) for early diagnosis. The quantification of these biomarkers requires ultrasensitive techniques, such as the single-molecule array (SIMOA) platform. This systematic review synthesizes the available evidence on the biomarkers β-amyloid (Aβ42, Aβ40, and their ratio), tau protein (t-Tau, p-Tau181, p-Tau231, p-Tau217), neurofilament light chain (NfL), and glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) in studies analyzing their blood concentrations using SIMOA, regardless of the platform model (SR-X, HD-1, HD-X). Findings support the potential of plasma biomarkers as an alternative or complement to invasive methods, although further validation is required before clinical implementation.

Introduction

The incidence of age-related neurological disorders has increased due to the rise in life expectancy worldwide. Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is the leading cause of death among individuals with cognitive impairment (CI) within this pathological domain, accounting for 60–80% of dementia cases and affecting approximately 50 million people globally (Giacomucci et al. 2023; Zheng and Wang 2025). It is also one of the major causes of mortality in the United States (WYLIE CM 1961; Tsering and Prokop 2024). AD arises through genetic and epigenetic mechanisms, manifesting either as sporadic cases or as a consequence of pathogenic genetic mutations, the latter accounting for less than 5–10% of cases (Selkoe 2001; Agís-Balboa et al. 2017).

AD exhibits a broad spectrum of symptoms that develop along a continuum, progressing at varying rates depending on the individual. Typically, the earliest reported signs are minor memory complaints, associated with brief episodes of amnesia or mental lapses (Zheng and Wang 2025). These gradually evolve into difficulties with language and communication or problem-solving, reflecting impaired reasoning (McKhann et al. 2011). CI interferes with daily life and independence, affecting memory, thinking, orientation, comprehension, and judgment, ultimately rendering patients unable to perform simple, routine tasks such as managing finances or completing sequential activities (McKhann et al. 2011). Furthermore, as patients become aware of their declining cognitive abilities, depressive episodes often occur, accompanied by mood and behavioral changes as the disease advances through its stages, with depression not only emerging as a consequence but also representing a significant risk factor for cognitive decline (McKhann et al. 2011; D’Onofrio et al. 2012; Fernández-Pereira et al. 2024).

There are three main stages in the AD continuum: a presymptomatic phase, mild cognitive impairment (MCI), and clinical dementia (Dong et al. 2023). In the initial stage, individuals are asymptomatic, and this phase may begin up to 20 years before the onset of clinical signs. When the first symptoms appear, the individual is considered to be in the MCI stage. Finally, once clinical symptoms such as cognitive deficits emerge, a diagnosis of dementia is established (Dong et al. 2023). This progression is illustrated in Fig. 1.

Because this presymptomatic phase manifests biologically in the brain, the need for early detection has driven the use of biomarkers capable of identifying these alterations before clinical symptoms appear (Oeckl et al. 2023). Biologically, the brain of a person affected by dementia undergoes a series of changes that worsen over time. These alterations are primarily observed as amyloid protein deposits forming plaques (Wippold et al. 2008), the presence of neurofibrillary tangles (Wippold et al. 2008), and changes in gray and white matter (Lv et al. 2025), leading to atrophy in brain regions such as the hippocampus and amygdala (Planche et al. 2022), thalamus (Yang et al. 2023), cingulate cortex (Wang et al. 2025a), temporal area (Platero 2022), and frontal and occipital lobes (Riverol et al. 2024), all of which are domains associated with memory (Rugg et al. 2002).

Extracellular amyloid plaques in the brain result from the aggregation of insoluble peptides generated through successive proteolytic cleavages of the transmembrane glycoprotein amyloid precursor protein (APP) (Tsering and Prokop 2024; Grewal et al. 2025). Under physiological conditions, APP is primarily processed via the non-amyloidogenic pathway through the action of α-secretase (Grewal et al. 2025). However, in the amyloidogenic pathway, β-secretase and γ-secretase mediate APP cleavage, producing insoluble isoforms such as Aβ40 and Aβ42. Among these, Aβ42 exhibits a greater propensity for aggregation, leading to amyloid plaque formation and triggering a neuroinflammatory response (Grewal et al. 2025; Zheng and Koo 2011).

Neurofibrillary tangles, on the other hand, result from the aggregation of tau protein, which organizes into paired helical filaments within neurons (Hardy and Allsop 1991). These aggregates accumulate and disrupt neuronal function and architecture, contributing to synaptic dysfunction that ultimately leads to neuronal death and cognitive decline (Imbimbo et al. 2005), particularly affecting brain regions such as the hippocampus and parahippocampal area (Price and Morris 1999). The hippocampus is among the first regions impacted by the disease, undergoing early atrophy and loss of connectivity (Geigenmüller et al. 2024). This structure is closely associated with episodic memory, playing a critical role in learning and memory processes (Rao et al. 2022).

These neurological alterations—including amyloid accumulation, tau aggregation, and brain atrophy—are currently integrated into a widely accepted conceptual model: the AT(N) framework, which classifies AD based on specific biomarkers (Hampel et al. 2021). The AT(N) framework, proposed by the National Institute on Aging and Alzheimer’s Association (NIA-AA), is a biomarker-based system that characterizes AD and other dementias across three biological domains: A (amyloid), reflecting amyloid pathology through techniques such as Positron Emission Tomography (PET) imaging or quantification of Aβ42/Aβ40 in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and plasma; T (tau), indicating pathological tau accumulation assessed by PET or phosphorylated tau (p-tau) levels; and N (neurodegeneration), representing neuronal damage and structural loss measured by brain atrophy on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), hypometabolism on FDG-PET, or biomarkers such as neurofilament light chain (NfL) (Jack et al. 2018). This framework summarizes the typical disease sequence: amyloid accumulation (A) occurs first, followed by tau aggregation (T), and finally neurodegeneration (N)—processes that correlate with clinical progression and cognitive decline. Thus, the previously described amyloid plaques correspond to component A, neurofibrillary tangles to T, and atrophy in regions such as the hippocampus, amygdala, and cingulate cortex to N, providing an integrated view that connects neuropathological findings with the current biomarker-based classification (Jack et al. 2013).

Nevertheless, AD diagnosis is generally established after symptoms have been present for a considerable period. Historically, it has relied on neuroimaging techniques such as amyloid PET(Bao et al. 2021), 18 F-fluorodeoxyglucose PET (FDG-PET) (Sanchez-Catasus et al. 2017), tau PET (Rabinovici et al. 2025), or MRI (Rao et al. 2022; Schuff et al. 2009), which allow visualization of morphological brain changes. Disease staging has also been characterized through CSF biomarker analysis (Olsson et al. 2016). Although effective, these methods have significant drawbacks: neuroimaging is costly and not widely accessible (Sanchez-Catasus et al. 2017), while lumbar puncture is invasive and traumatic for patients (Andreasson et al. 2016). Consequently, a new alternative has emerged: blood-based biomarker analysis using ultrasensitive immunoassay techniques, such as the SIMOA technology (Dong et al. 2024).

Blood Biomarkers and SIMOA Technology

After reviewing traditional methods based on neuroimaging and CSF biomarkers, it is essential to address the emerging role of blood biomarkers and ultrasensitive technologies, such as the SIMOA platform, which are transforming the diagnostic landscape toward less invasive and more accessible procedures (Deng et al. 2025; Mielke and Fowler 2024).

The most extensively studied blood biomarkers in the field of CI for AD, using SIMOA technology and other ultrasensitive platforms, are discussed in this systematic review. These include amyloid-beta (Aβ), total tau (t-Tau) and p-Tau, NfL, and glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) (Zhang et al. 2024; Grande et al. 2025; Dark et al. 2024).

Aβ exists primarily in two insoluble isoforms, Aβ40 and Aβ42, which aggregate to form amyloid plaques, leading to their accumulation in the brain and impairing neuronal function (Gouras et al. 2015). Although aggregation of Aβ42 contributes to reduced levels in peripheral circulation, additional mechanisms—including altered clearance, peripheral production and transport dynamics—also influence its detectability in plasma. Importantly, Aβ40 remains comparatively stable, and this differential behaviour underlies the diagnostic utility of the Aβ42/Aβ40 ratio rather than either isoform alone (Schindler et al. 2019).

Tau protein is associated with microtubules and is predominantly found in neuronal axons, where it stabilizes microtubules essential for axonal transport and structural integrity (Wang and Mandelkow 2016). Under pathological conditions, tau hyperphosphorylation reduces its affinity for microtubules, causing their disorganization and the formation of neurofibrillary tangles, which reflect neurodegenerative damage characteristic of AD. The p-Tau epitopes addressed in this review include p-Tau181 (Karikari et al. 2020), p-Tau231 (Ashton et al. 2021), and p-Tau217—the latter being the most promising due to its high specificity for AD and recent validation for diagnostic purposes under specific clinical conditions (Palmqvist et al. 2020).

NfL is a structural component of the axonal cytoskeleton, released into CSF and blood following axonal damage (Khalil et al. 2018). NfL is considered a sensitive biomarker of neurodegeneration, although its use is not exclusive to AD, as it also increases in other neurodegenerative diseases such as multiple sclerosis, Parkinson’s disease, and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (Khalil et al. 2018). Its plasma concentration correlates with clinical progression and neuronal loss, even in presymptomatic stages. Moreover, NfL is associated with acute axonal injury in the central nervous system, with plasma levels remaining elevated for up to three months after the insult (Preische et al. 2019).

Finally, GFAP is a component of the astrocytic cytoskeleton, and its elevation reflects glial activation and neuroinflammatory processes. In AD, GFAP increases in early stages, even before overt neurodegeneration, reflecting sustained astrocytic activation and neuroinflammatory responses. In contrast, NfL primarily reflects acute axonal injury, with transient elevations that return toward baseline within a few months, making it less suitable as an indicator of ongoing inflammatory processes. Its measurement in plasma using ultrasensitive techniques has shown great potential for identifying individuals at risk (Bellaver et al. 2025).

Together, these biomarkers cover the broad continuum of AD, and their assessment in blood offers a much more accessible and convenient alternative compared to traditional methods. However, their concentrations in blood are lower than in CSF, requiring ultrasensitive detection techniques such as SIMOA, which is increasingly incorporated into studies of cognitive impairment in the context of Alzheimer’s disease.

The SIMOA HD-X platform uses paramagnetic microbeads coated with specific capture antibodies to bind target proteins present in plasma, serum, or other biofluids. Upon analyte binding, the capture antibody on each microbead forms the first part of the immune complex, which is completed only after addition of the detection antibody—either simultaneously in a 2-step assay or following a wash step in a 3-step assay. After washing, an enzyme label is added, and the microbeads are isolated into more than 200,000 microwells—one bead per well—enabling single-molecule detection. The resulting signal is read digitally, providing ultrasensitive quantification at femtomolar ranges, far exceeding conventional immunoassays. SIMOA also surpasses automated chemiluminescent platforms such as Lumipulse—widely implemented in clinical settings but operating with lower analytical sensitivity—and other immunoassay systems such as Ella or ELISA (Wojdała et al. 2024; Rissin et al. 2010). It is important to note, however, that these biomarkers are not exclusive to the SIMOA platform and can also be quantified using other immunoassay systems, including automated chemiluminescent platforms such as Lumipulse or multiplex platforms like Ella.

This technology has enabled reliable measurement of biomarkers such as Aβ, p-Tau, NfL, and GFAP in plasma, facilitating studies in preclinical stages and clinical trials. Its high sensitivity and minimally invasive sample requirements make it a promising tool compared to CSF-based or neuroimaging techniques, which are more costly and less accessible.

Objectives

This systematic review aims to synthesize the available evidence on the biomarkers Aβ (Aβ42, Aβ40, and the Aβ42/Aβ40 ratio), tau biomarkers (t-Tau, p-Tau181, p-Tau231, and p-Tau217), NfL, and GFAP in studies that analyze their blood concentrations using SIMOA technology, regardless of the platform model (SR-X, HD-1, HD-X). The review has three main goals: (1) to describe the methodological characteristics of studies employing SIMOA for the quantification of blood biomarkers related to AD; (2) to compare the evidence on the diagnostic performance of these biomarkers against more invasive and costly methods, such as lumbar puncture and neuroimaging; and (3) to identify advantages, limitations, and knowledge gaps in the use of plasma biomarkers for the early diagnosis of AD.

Methods

This systematic review was conducted in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 guidelines. The literature search was performed on June 7, 2024, using the databases PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science. The objective was to identify studies on Alzheimer’s disease (AD) that employed SIMOA technology to measure the following target proteins: Aβ-40, Aβ-42, total t-Tau, various forms of p-Tau, NfL and GFAP.

The search strategy used was: “Alzheimer dementia AND SIMOA AND (Aβ OR t-tau OR p-tau OR GFAP OR NfL) AND (plasma biomarker OR serum biomarker)” (see Fig. 3).

Filters were applied to include only studies involving human participants aged over 18 years, of both sexes, and published in English since 2016.

Studies were excluded if they: Included animal cohorts, focused on non-AD dementias, involved participants with metabolic, genetic, renal, or psychiatric comorbidities. These exclusion criteria were established a priori to reduce confounding, as several metabolic, vascular, renal and psychiatric conditions are known to independently influence plasma concentrations of Aβ, tau, NfL, and GFAP. Therefore, the exclusion of such studies was necessary to isolate the biomarker signal specifically related to Alzheimer’s disease pathology.

A total of 206 articles were initially identified. After removing 44 duplicates and 6 ineligible articles due to restricted access, 156 articles remained. Title and abstract screening led to the exclusion of 80 articles, leaving 76 for full-text review.

An additional 5 articles were excluded based on the following criteria: Reason 1: “Cohorts with comorbidities such as hypertension, hypercholesterolemia, or diabetes”. Reason 2: “Presence of psychiatric disorders such as anxiety or depression”. Reason 3: “Inclusion of participants with impaired renal function”.

Ultimately, 71 studies were included in the final analysis.

Discussion

This systematic review highlights the growing interest in the use of blood-based biomarkers (BBB) as diagnostic tools in the context of cognitive impairment related to Alzheimer’s disease. An analysis of the included studies reveals that these biomarkers offer a promising alternative to traditional techniques based on CSF or neuroimaging, which, although highly informative, present significant limitations in terms of invasiveness, cost, and accessibility.

In this context, SIMOA technology has represented a key advancement, enabling the ultrasensitive detection of brain-derived proteins in peripheral blood samples. Compared to conventional techniques such as ELISA, SIMOA offers superior sensitivity and specificity. Its application across numerous studies has facilitated the quantification of biomarkers such as β-amyloid, p-Tau, t-Tau, NfL, and GFAP, with results that in many cases closely approximate those obtained through CSF analysis.

In the following sections, we discuss the advantages of using blood biomarkers, the role of SIMOA technology in their implementation, comparisons with conventional diagnostic methods, and the biomarkers that currently show the greatest clinical promise.

Advantages of Using Blood Biomarkers

One of the main advantages of using BBB is that sample collection is considerably less invasive and faster than techniques such as lumbar puncture or diagnostic imaging tests (PET, MRI, among others). This procedure is also less traumatic for patients, especially considering the discomfort associated with imaging noise or the reluctance that lumbar puncture for CSF collection may generate (Ingannato et al. 2024; Altuna-Azkargorta and Mendioroz-Iriarte 2018). Moreover, several studies have demonstrated a strong correlation between biomarker levels in blood, CSF, and neuroimaging (Wu et al. 2024; Arendonk et al. 2023; Silva-Spínola et al. 2023; Therriault et al. 2023; Truffi et al. 2023; Tsiknia et al. 2022), as will be discussed later.

Another notable advantage is the ability of these biomarkers to detect alterations in preclinical stages, even before symptom onset or when symptoms are very subtle (Ingannato et al. 2024; Wojdała et al. 2023; Ally et al. 2023; Chatterjee et al. 2021). Additionally, some markers allow estimation of disease progression time based on their plasma levels. Feizpour et al. illustrate how p-Tau217 is a highly sensitive and specific marker for detecting cerebral amyloidosis and predicting cognitive decline (Feizpour et al. 2023).

Numerous studies have documented variations in serum levels of the biomarkers included in this review, enabling characterization of their behaviour compared to healthy individuals. For example, NfL and GFAP tend to increase with age and disease progression (Foley et al. 2024; Asken et al. 2022; Benedet et al. 2021; Santangelo et al. 2021), whereas the Aβ ratio decreases as symptoms advance (Ni et al. 2023; Huang et al. 2022; Sun et al. 2022; Xiao et al. 2021; Chatterjee et al. 2021; Chatterjee et al. 2019; Álvarez-Sánchez et al. 2022).

In addition to their compatibility with SIMOA technology, these biomarkers can be detected using other analytical platforms, making them a versatile option for early diagnosis and clinical monitoring, with advantages in terms of cost, speed, and accessibility (Ingannato et al. 2024; Wojdała et al. 2023; Truffi et al. 2023; Mielke et al. 2021; Bayoumy et al. 2021). However, it is important to note that measured levels may vary depending on the technique used, with SIMOA being one of the most sensitive and specific currently available. Udeh-Momoh et al. highlight that Quanterix and and MS-based platforms (mass spectrometry) offer better performance offer better performance, supporting their clinical use and suggesting that standardization and cost are key factors for adoption (Udeh-Momoh et al. 2022).

Although biomarkers can be analyzed individually their combination improves diagnostic performance by providing complementary information, particularly when confirming a diagnosis, establishing disease stage, or monitoring progression. For example, p-Tau217 has demonstrated high specificity for ruling out AD (Therriault et al. 2023; Janelidze et al. 2023a; Mielke et al. 2021; Bayoumy et al. 2021); combining the Aβ ratio with GFAP enables detection of amyloid pathology in early stages (Cicognola et al. 2021; Benedet et al. 2021); and the association between NfL and GFAP is useful for monitoring progression (Ally et al. 2023; Asken et al. 2022). This differentiation between diagnostic utility and clinical monitoring utility is crucial and should be considered alongside factors such as age or the presence of genetic mutations.

Sex-related differences have also been described for some biomarkers, such as p-Tau181, whose levels are associated with a higher risk of cognitive decline or conversion to dementia in women (Wu et al. 2024; Tsiknia et al. 2022). Tsiknia et al. studied plasma levels of p-Tau181 and found that, although levels were similar between sexes, women exhibited a higher risk of cognitive decline or conversion to dementia, highlighting the need to consider this factor (Tsiknia et al. 2022). Likewise, serum biomarkers complement genetic factors such as the APOE ε4 allele, as observed with the Aβ ratio (Giacomucci et al. 2023; Foley et al. 2024; Kwon et al. 2023; Rippon et al. 2022; Jiao et al. 2021; Vergallo et al. 2019; Janelidze et al. 2016). Janelidze et al. used SIMOA technology to measure Ab42 and Ab40 isoforms, reporting that plasma Ab42 is decreased in APOE ε4 carriers compared to non-carriers (Janelidze et al. 2016).

From a clinical and structural perspective, biomarker levels have been associated with brain atrophy and loss of white and gray matter. For example, NfL is related to alterations in white matter (Hall et al. 2021; Benedet et al. 2020; Ashton et al. 2019), while the t-Tau/Aβ42 ratio can predict tau deposition in the brain (Li and Mielke 2019). The study by Raffaele Cacciaglia et al. provides another example, showing that the p-Tau/Aβ42 ratio significantly correlates with gray matter volume and cortical Aβ deposition, even in cognitively normal individuals (Cacciaglia et al. 2024). Furthermore, correlations have been observed between these biomarkers and cognitive functions such as processing speed and executive functions, with Aβ40 and NfL being representative examples. Jacob et al. measured Ab42, Ab40, and t-Tau biomarkers in a cohort of 2,336 cognitively normal individuals. Two assessments were conducted to evaluate grip strength and fast walking speed, and lower plasma levels of tau and Ab40 were associated with greater grip strength and faster walking speed, suggesting their potential as early markers of preclinical neurodegeneration (Jacob et al. 2022).

Finally, BBB are compatible with the AT(N) diagnostic framework, allowing differentiation of biological profiles between individuals without pathology (A-/T-/N-) and those with positive biomarkers (ATN+), supporting their potential for future clinical implementation (Giacomucci et al. 2023; Sun et al. 2022; Benedet et al. 2021; Benedet et al. 2020; Li and Mielke 2019). In the study by Sun et al., the combined use of AT(N) biomarkers in diagnostic models is advocated, as it improves differentiation between mild/moderate AD and cognitively normal controls (CN) compared to applying them individually (Sun et al. 2022).

Correlations have also been evidenced between some of these biomarkers and clinical scales of CI, such as p-Tau subtypes with amyloid PET, as shown in the study by Therriault et al. (2023), or elevated NfL levels with lower MMSE and CERAD scores, as reported in the study by Kern et al. (2022).

The Role of SIMOA Technology: Advantages and Limitations

The use of SIMOA technology offers multiple advantages, as it is simple and automated, enabling faster results with higher specificity and sensitivity compared to previously employed techniques for biomarker quantification, such as ELISA, from which it is derived (Udeh-Momoh et al. 2022; Koychev et al. 2021). SIMOA technology is available in three main platforms—SR-X, HD-1, and HD-X—which differ in their level of automation, throughput, and infrastructure requirements. The SR-X is a benchtop, semi-automated instrument that requires manual preparation of assay plates and external washing steps before plate reading, offering greater workflow flexibility but lower automation and throughput compared with fully automated systems (Lambert et al. 2018; Celerion Switzerland 2026). In contrast, the HD-1 is the first-generation fully automated analyzer, designed for high-throughput workflows and minimal hands-on time, integrating all assay steps internally and enabling consistent sample-to-answer performance (Lambert et al. 2018).The most recent HD-X platform represents an improved version of the HD-1, incorporating major productivity enhancements, superior assay precision, expanded multiplexing capability, and full automation of all assay steps, making it the flagship and most robust system currently available for ultrasensitive biomarker detection (Quanterix). As these platforms have evolved, the instruments have become larger, more complex, and increasingly dependent on controlled laboratory environments. For example, HD-X analyzers are commonly installed in dedicated rooms with strict temperature control to ensure assay stability and instrument reliability (Instituto de Investigación Sanitaria de Santiago de Compostela (IDIS) 2026). These differences imply not only technical variability across studies but also divergent operational demands—which have important implications for the feasibility of implementing SIMOA in routine clinical practice (Quanterix). Udeh-Momoh et al. advocate for the use of this technology due to its cost-effectiveness and speed (Udeh-Momoh et al. 2022). In dementia-related research, Stockmann et al. demonstrated that SIMOA enables the measurement of absolute concentrations of specific isoforms such as Aβ1–42 and Aβ1–40, showing sensitivity to biological variations and extracellular factors (Stockmann et al. 2020).

However, like other measurement methods, this technology has limitations, as it influences biomarker sensitivity and specificity (Rippon et al. 2022; Tanaka et al. 2021; Álvarez-Sánchez et al. 2022). It is not yet approved for clinical use (Suárez-Calvet et al. 2025), and although several studies support its clinical potential, practical barriers such as cost, limited automation, platform-specific variability and the current lack of large-scale standardization still limit its feasibility in primary-care workflows. Tanaka et al., suggest that standardization and cost remain critical factors, as SIMOA, while useful, does not yet offer the throughput and scalability required for primary care diagnostics (Tanaka et al. 2021). Another important consideration is that, although assays such as SIMOA, Elecsys or Lumipulse often show high correlations, their absolute readouts are not interchangeable, and each platform requires its own assay-specific cutoffs. Krishna et al. emphasize that this platform-dependent variability limits the direct transferability of thresholds and highlights the need for further standardization before universal clinical reference ranges can be established (Krishna et al. 2024).

Regarding direct comparison with other emerging techniques, although blood biomarkers such as Aβ42/Aβ40 measured with SIMOA are useful. Tanaka et al., in a cohort of 68 individuals spanning cognitively unimpaired, CIND, vascular dementia and Alzheimer’s disease groups, compared plasma Aβ42 measurements obtained with SIMOA and the APEX-Aβ42 assay. They observed notable inter-platform differences, with APEX yielding higher analytical performance for Aβ42 quantification in their dataset. These findings highlight that platform-dependent variability persists and that further assay harmonization is needed before establishing clinically interchangeable thresholds (Tanaka et al. 2021).

Bayoumy et al. present an analytical and clinical comparison of six new p-Tau assays developed on the high-sensitivity SIMOA platform, including three different p-Tau181 assays (Eli Lilly, ADx NeuroSciences, and Quanterix), one p-Tau217 assay (Eli Lilly), and two p-Tau231 assays (ADx NeuroSciences, Gothenburg). Differences were observed even for the same isoforms, which could be attributed to variations in antibodies used, calibration standards, and sample processing conditions (Bayoumy et al. 2021). This underscores the importance of considering the platform employed, as differences exist even within SIMOA technology itself.

Furthermore, although Quanterix and mass spectrometry platforms offer superior performance in some studies, performance differences between mass spectrometry (MS) and SIMOA technologies are significant, highlighting the need to consider the measurement method when interpreting results (Janelidze et al. 2023a; Rippon et al. 2022). This is illustrated in the study by Mielke et al., where MSD, SIMOA (Janss), and SIMOA (UGOT) were compared in the same cohort of 200 participants with MCI, and the observed differences warrant careful consideration (Mielke et al. 2021).

Regarding other platforms, in the case of Lumipulse, Wojdala et al. support that both techniques can complement each other, as significant correlations between CSF and plasma were found (Wojdała et al. 2023). Similarly, Truffi et al. compared SIMOA with Ella, showing a high correlation in the quantification of biomarkers such as NfL, although systematic differences were noted. Specifically, Ella tends to overestimate NfL levels compared to SIMOA, particularly in dementia patients, where greater variability in values was observed. Despite these quantitative differences, the overall clinical validity of the biomarker remains consistent across platforms. Therefore, the choice of technology may influence numerical interpretation but does not compromise diagnostic or prognostic utility (Truffi et al. 2023).

In summary, despite current limitations regarding standardization, cost, and clinical approval, SIMOA remains one of the most promising platforms for ultrasensitive blood biomarker detection, enabling minimally invasive assessment of Alzheimer’s pathology and supporting early diagnosis and large-scale research applications.

Comparison of Plasma Biomarkers with CSF and Neuroimaging

Plasma biomarkers allow inference of pathological processes such as cortical atrophy, axonal damage, or gliosis, which correlate with findings from structural imaging (MRI) or CSF concentrations. This section addresses how BBB relate to cerebral amyloid burden and neurodegeneration, as well as compares their measurements in blood with those obtained from CSF and structural imaging (MRI, PET).

Among all biomarkers reviewed, those most closely associated with amyloid burden are the Aβ isoforms (Aβ42 and Aβ40), their Aβ42/Aβ40 ratio, and phosphorylated tau epitopes p-Tau217 and p-Tau181. Amyloid protein has traditionally been the primary focus of study; however, the identification of specific phosphorylated tau epitopes has demonstrated their ability to reflect varying degrees of tau pathology. This discovery opened a new avenue for investigating cognitive decline progression, which was subsequently extended to detection assays in fluids such as blood and CSF. Regarding neurodegeneration, biomarkers such as NfL, GFAP, and t-Tau enable monitoring of disease progression or severity, although they are not specific to AD.

Plasma Biomarkers Measured with SIMOA Technology and Their Correlation with CSF levels

Plasma Aβ42/Aβ40 ratios show moderate correspondence with CSF measurements, with both fluids reflecting decreases in Aβ42 relative to Aβ40 as amyloid pathology progresses. Aschenbrenner et al. evaluated paired plasma and CSF samples and found that CSF levels were more sensitive for detecting early pathological changes, showing significant associations with cognition at baseline and longitudinally, whereas plasma displayed weaker trends (Aschenbrenner et al. 2022). Similarly, Xiong et al. reported that differences between amyloid‑positive and amyloid‑negative individuals are substantially larger in CSF (up to 50%) than in plasma (7–12%), underscoring the stronger dynamic range of CSF Aβ biomarkers (Xiong et al. 2024). This reduced dynamic range in plasma relative to CSF has been replicated across multiple studies, including the systematic review by Ebbesen et al. (2023), which highlights the consistently weaker separation of plasma Aβ measures; the comparative analysis by Brand et al. (2022), showing platform-dependent variability and lower discriminative margins in plasma compared with CSF; and the modeling study by Wisch et al. (Wisch et al. 2023), where CSF Aβ42/40 predicted amyloid PET burden across a wider pathological range than plasma Aβ42/40. Nevertheless, CSF Aβ ratios, together with Tau-PET and FDG-PET, remain more robust for AD diagnosis within the AT(N) framework (Xiong et al. 2024).

Beyond conventional amyloid measures, misfolded Aβ species in plasma have been proposed as early markers of abnormal protein folding. Stockmann et al. analysed misfolded Aβ in individuals with subjective cognitive decline and compared these values with CSF Aβ42 concentrations (Stockmann et al. 2020). This study provides complementary insight into how misfolded Aβ species interact with soluble Aβ measurements, supporting the broader concept that combining markers capturing distinct pathological processes may enhance plasma-based models.

Several phosphorylated tau epitopes show parallel behaviour across plasma and CSF. p‑Tau181 correlates strongly between both fluids, with consistent elevations observed in amyloid‑positive individuals in paired plasma and CSF datasets (Dark et al. 2024; Wu et al. 2024; Ni et al. 2023; Wojdała et al. 2023; Gerards et al. 2022; Meyer et al. 2022). Total tau concentrations are also systematically higher in CSF than in plasma, as described in Danni Li et al.’s review (Li and Mielke 2019), reflecting the expected higher CNS contribution to CSF analyte levels.

The p-Tau231 has been proposed as an early marker of amyloid-related changes, although its performance in plasma is more variable than that of other phosphorylated tau epitopes. While it is present at higher concentrations in CSF, plasma p-Tau231 can also distinguish between clinical groups, showing increased levels in cognitively impaired individuals compared with controls (Bucci et al. 2023; Therriault et al. 2023). Therriault et al. reported associations between plasma p-Tau231 and Aβ-PET burden, although their analysis did not assess dichotomous discrimination between amyloid-positive and amyloid-negative participants. In contrast, other studies have shown that plasma p-Tau231 can differentiate A+ from A– individuals and increases progressively across the Alzheimer’s continuum. Therriault et al. also demonstrated a significant correlation between CSF and plasma concentrations, supporting its potential use as a less invasive monitoring marker (Therriault et al. 2023). Also, plasma p-Tau217 shows diagnostic performance comparable to established CSF biomarkers, offering a less invasive alternative for detecting AD pathology (Janelidze et al. 2023a).

NfL displays robust correlations between CSF and plasma, reflecting its ability to diffuse from the central nervous system into peripheral circulation. Aschenbrenner et al. noted similar directional changes in both fluids, although CSF presented a wider dynamic range (Aschenbrenner et al. 2022). Wojdala et al. also confirmed strong CSF–plasma associations, supporting plasma NfL as a less invasive alternative for monitoring axonal injury (Wojdała et al. 2023).

GFAP similarly demonstrates significant plasma–CSF correlations. Studies by Cicognola et al. and Silva‑Spínola et al. show that plasma GFAP parallels CSF levels, likely reflecting shared astroglial activation mechanisms (Silva-Spínola et al. 2023; Benedet et al. 2021). Silva‑Spínola et al. further linked plasma GFAP and NfL with CSF markers of tau pathology and astroglial reactivity, supporting their consistency across both fluids (Silva-Spínola et al. 2023).

Relationship of Plasma Biomarkers with Neuroimaging (MRI, PET)

CSF Aβ42/Aβ40 ratios show strong correspondence with amyloid PET, and this pattern is partially mirrored in plasma. Chatterjee et al. reported that individuals with high cerebral amyloid burden exhibit lower plasma Aβ42/Aβ40 ratios, reflecting decreased Aβ42 relative to Aβ40 in PET-positive subjects (Chatterjee et al. 2019). Ni et al. observed that plasma Aβ40 does not correlate significantly with amyloid PET, whereas plasma Aβ42 and the Aβ42/Aβ40 ratio show negative correlations with amyloid PET burden (Ni et al. 2023). Similar patterns were reported by Aschenbrenner et al., who described negative associations between plasma Aβ42/Aβ40 and amyloid PET uptake in frontal, temporal, parietal, cingulate and precuneus regions (Aschenbrenner et al. 2022).

p-Tau181 shows robust associations with neurodegeneration markers on MRI. Dark et al. demonstrated that plasma p-Tau181 predicts total and hippocampal brain volume loss, even in cognitively unimpaired individuals (Dark et al. 2024). Associations were stronger among individuals with lower amyloid burden, suggesting early susceptibility. Ni et al. further highlighted that plasma p-Tau181 correlates positively with amyloid PET signal and reflects increasing amyloid-β deposition in symptomatic stages (Ni et al. 2023).

Across tau epitopes, p-Tau217 shows the strongest correspondence with PET. Therriault et al. found that plasma p-Tau217 correlates more strongly with amyloid plaque concentration than with tau PET signal (Therriault et al. 2023), reflecting its close relationship with amyloid-driven phosphorylation processes. Multiple studies support p-Tau217 as the most accurate plasma biomarker for identifying amyloid and tau PET positivity (Therriault et al. 2023; Janelidze et al. 2023a; Mielke et al. 2021).

Although elevated t-Tau levels are observed in AD, their association with neuroimaging markers is modest. Ni et al. found no significant correlation between plasma t-Tau and PET SUVR (Ni et al. 2023). However, structural MRI studies indicate that plasma t-Tau is associated with reduced cortical thickness (particularly temporal and parietal regions), lower fractional anisotropy in corpus callosum fibres, and broader patterns of brain atrophy (Li and Mielke 2019). Frank et al. confirmed similar findings, reporting reduced cortical thickness and white-matter integrity in association with plasma t-Tau levels(Frank et al. 2022).

Plasma NfL exhibits strong relationships with MRI markers of neurodegeneration. Dark et al. described increased NfL in later pathological stages, coinciding with brain atrophy (Dark et al. 2024). NfL correlates with reduced temporal cortical thickness, hippocampal volume loss, and decreased fractional anisotropy, supporting its role as a marker of axonal degeneration (Frank et al. 2022; Kern et al. 2022). Structural MRI analyses further show that elevated NfL corresponds to white-matter abnormalities (Hall et al. 2021; Benedet et al. 2020; Ashton et al. 2019), although van Arendonk et al. noted that these changes may not always evolve longitudinally (Arendonk et al. 2023).

GFAP demonstrates consistent associations with MRI markers of neuroinflammation and structural decline. Dark et al. reported that plasma GFAP predicts future brain atrophy (Dark et al. 2024), while Bucci et al. found that GFAP is more accurate than other individual biomarkers for capturing PET-defined amyloid positivity (Bucci et al. 2023). GFAP appears to track early astroglial responses to amyloid pathology, complementing plasma and CSF Aβ measures (Cicognola et al. 2021). According to the AT(N) framework, plasma GFAP levels are elevated in A+T– individuals relative to A–T+, reinforcing its close link to amyloid-related imaging changes (Parvizi et al. 2022; Benedet et al. 2021).

Clinical Applications and Diagnostic Value

Overall, BBB demonstrate better performance when combined, achieving models with greater specificity and sensitivity for stratifying diagnostic groups within cohorts. For the Aβ42/Aβ40 ratio, its diagnostic value and that of its isoforms are limited when considered alone.

Diagnostic Performance of Aβ Biomarkers (Aβ42/40, Isoforms, Combinations)

Although plasma Aβ42/Aβ40 ratios show diagnostic value, their performance as standalone biomarkers is modest due to the limited dynamic range observed in peripheral blood. Consequently, they are more informative when interpreted in combination with other risk or biomarker indicators. Rippon et al. and Janelidze et al. demonstrated that adding APOE ε4 status substantially enhances the predictive ability of the plasma Aβ42/Aβ40 ratio for identifying amyloid-positive individuals (Rippon et al. 2022; Janelidze et al. 2016). Similarly, in Stockmann et al.’s study, where plasma Aβ42/40 was measured with SIMOA and combined with misfolded Aβ (immuno infrared sensor), it was reported that combining the Aβ42/Aβ40 ratio with misfolded Aβ burden yield superior risk stratification in SCD over either measure alone (Stockmann et al. 2020). These findings indicate that plasma Aβ-based biomarkers gain diagnostic strength when integrated into multimodal models rather than when used in isolation.

Diagnostic Value of p-tau181

Plasma p-Tau181 shows solid diagnostic performance across the Alzheimer’s continuum. Gerards et al. and Wojdala et al. supported the ability of p-Tau181 to distinguish between AD stages in their studies Wodjala et al. 2023; Gerards et al. 2022). In addition, p-Tau181 could be employed clinically when imaging or CSF-based diagnosis is unavailable, as it is particularly useful in that context. Despite this, its discriminatory capacity has limitations. Its specificity and ability to differentiate between healthy individuals and those with dementia or MCI, p-Tau181 does not discriminate between MCI subtypes (amnestic MCI [aMCI] vs. non-amnestic MCI [naMCI]), indicating that additional biomarkers are needed to better characterize these groups. This was demonstrated in studies by Meyer et al. and Wu et al., where attempts to distinguish between amnestic and non-amnestic individuals within MCI cohorts resulted in good stratification based on CI presence but failed to separate the subtypes (Colmant et al. 2024; Tsiknia et al. 2022). This suggests that complementary biomarkers are required for finer clinical characterization.

In preclinical stages, pTau181 remains informative. Its association with Aβ positivity and its ability to discriminate AD pathology carriers regardless of cognitive status position it as a useful biomarker in preclinical stages when measured in both plasma and CSF, especially in individuals with amyloidosis and/or neurodegeneration classified as A + and N+ within the AT(N) framework (Giacomucci et al. 2023; Wu et al. 2024; Meyer et al. 2022). Overall, p-Tau181 is a clinically useful and accessible diagnostic biomarker, although less specific than p-Tau217 for early AD detection.

Diagnostic Value of p-tau217

Regarding p-Tau217, its utility in cognitively normal individuals is limited as a predictor but valuable as a prescreening tool, as described by Feizpour et al. (2023). Its utility is particularly evident in preclinical stages, where other markers do not appear to show significant changes. Across the disease continuum, p-Tau217 demonstrates high sensitivity, especially during early symptomatic phases. Janelidze et al. studied different p-Tau variants in plasma, reporting the best results for p-Tau217, which was able to discriminate between amyloid-positive and amyloid-negative MCI individuals Janelidze et. al 2023). These findings reinforce the role of p-Tau217 as a key marker for predicting future cognitive decline, supporting its use in accessible and scalable plasma-based screening approaches.

Taken together, the evidence reinforces the role of p‑Tau217 as a key marker within the AT(N) framework, particularly reflecting the influence of amyloid pathology on plasma tau phosphorylation in preclinical stages. Overall, p‑Tau217 emerges as one of the most clinically informative and accurate plasma biomarkers for early AD detection and stratification.

Limitations of t-tau as Diagnostic Marker

Evidence indicates that plasma t‑Tau has limited diagnostic utility. Frank et al. studied its plasma levels and concluded that, although associated with some cognitive and structural measures, this biomarker does not provide additional prognostic value and its utility is more limited, as it does not predict longitudinal changes. Elevated plasma t-Tau levels did not contribute to cognitive decline prognosis (Frank et al. 2022), indicating lower diagnostic value (Ni et al. 2023). Overall, the reduced dynamic range of t-Tau in plasma and its lack of specificity limit its usefulness as a standalone clinical marker.

Clinical Applications of NfL

Plasma NfL is a valuable biomarker of neuroaxonal injury and offers meaningful clinical information across the Alzheimer’s disease continuum. Although its predictive precision is modest, it reflects baseline status and pathological burden, as Benedet et al. described this in their study, dividing two cohorts into cognitively unimpaired and cognitively impaired individuals. Plasma NfL emerged as a candidate biomarker for amyloid-related neuronal damage in presymptomatic AD, while also correlating with symptomatic disease progression and tau burden once CI is present (Hall et al. 2021; Benedet et al. 2020; Ashton et al. 2019). In presymptomatic stages, plasma NfL serves as a candidate marker for amyloid-related neuronal damage, while in symptomatic phases it correlates with tau-related neurodegeneration and overall disease progression.

Wojdala et al. reported in a cohort including cognitively normal individuals, pre-AD, MCI-AD, AD dementia, and FTD that neurofilament levels increase with disease progression, showing similar patterns in FTD and AD dementia (Wojdała et al. 2023). Overall, while NfL lacks disease specificity, it is a robust clinical marker of neurodegeneration and a useful complement to other plasma biomarkers for monitoring disease severity and progression.

Clinical Relevance of GFAP as a Biomarker of Astrogliosis

Plasma GFAP has consistently demonstrated strong clinical utility across the Alzheimer’s disease continuum. It increases markedly in individuals progressing from mild impairment to dementia (Dark et al. 2024; Cicognola et al. 2021; Verberk et al. 2020), and its association with cognitive decline is more pronounced in the presence of high amyloid burden (Dark et al. 2024). GFAP also performs well in detecting amyloid positivity in MCI (Bucci et al. 2023) and shows sensitivity in very early stages, including cognitively unimpaired individuals with underlying Aβ pathology.

Across multiple cohorts, GFAP has been described as a versatile biomarker capable of capturing both amyloid-related and neurodegenerative processes. Benedet et al. reported higher plasma GFAP levels in A+T− individuals relative to A−T−, supporting a close association with amyloid pathology Benedet et al. 2021). GFAP is also informative across symptomatic stages, where elevations reflect both astroglial activation and clinical severity (Cicognola et al. 2021; Verberk et al. 2020).

Importantly, GFAP demonstrates robust diagnostic value when used in combination with other biomarkers. Verberk et al. showed that pairing GFAP with the Aβ42/40 ratio improves early identification of amyloid positivity, while the combination of GFAP with NfL aids in monitoring disease progression (Verberk et al. 2020). Further evidence indicates that GFAP combined with p-Tau181 enhances detection of individuals with underlying AD pathology (Dark et al. 2024; Silva-Spínola et al. 2023).

Taken together, plasma GFAP emerges as one of the most robust and clinically informative blood-based biomarkers, providing consistent value for detecting amyloid positivity, stratifying impairment states, and monitoring disease progression across multiple stages of Alzheimer’s disease.

In general, plasma biomarkers provide substantial diagnostic value across the Alzheimer’s continuum. While none of them can yet replace neuroimaging or CSF studies, they offer meaningful clinical information for early-stage screening, risk stratification and patient monitoring, particularly in settings where access to more invasive or costly procedures is limited. Their ability to detect early pathological changes, capture different components of the AT(N) framework, and complement one another when combined strengthens their role as practical tools for improving diagnostic workflows and supporting clinical decision-making in diverse healthcare environments.

Limitations and Methodological Considerations

Despite their established utility in CSF-based assays, plasma Aβ isoforms and the Aβ42/Aβ40 ratio remain insufficient as standalone diagnostic markers. Studies such as Ni et al. show that plasma measurements require combination with other modalities, including PET, to reach clinically meaningful accuracy (Ni et al. 2023). Similarly, Xiao et al. observed that although plasma Aβ42/Aβ40 levels differ between amyloid-positive and amyloid-negative individuals, their predictive value diminishes after adjustment for covariates (Xiao et al. 2021). Furthermore, although the plasma Aβ42/Aβ40 ratio can help detect amyloid positivity, results vary across platforms and analytical methods and are strongly influenced by pre-analytical conditions, as reflected by Udeh-Momoh et al.’s study (Udeh-Momoh et al. 2022). In particular, Aβ peptides are thermosensitive and exhibit a pronounced tendency to aggregate, which decreases measurable concentrations over time and compromises assay accuracy. This effect is exacerbated when samples are kept at room temperature, with significant signal loss occurring within a few hours, whereas storage at 4 °C preserves stability for up to 72 h; immediate freezing at − 80 °C is recommended to minimize aggregation-related loss of signal (Schoonenboom et al. 2005; Walter et al. 2020; Sunde et al. 2023). These pre-analytical considerations are not unique to SIMOA and affect all plasma Aβ42/Aβ40 assays, including immunoassays and IP-MS-based methods, as highlighted in recent reviews addressing platform-dependent variability (e.g., Brand et al.) and in large-scale modelling work by Wisch et al., who showed that CSF Aβ42/40 predicts amyloid-PET burden across a wider pathological range than plasma Aβ42/40, underscoring the importance of standardized protocols for plasma-based Aβ assays (Brand et al. 2022; Wisch et al. 2023).

Mielke et al. compared the accuracy of four plasma p-Tau measurements for tau PET imaging of the entorhinal cortex. Correlations between tau PET (Meta-ROI, ERC) and plasma biomarkers were weak to moderate, indicating that while a relationship exists, correlations are consistently weaker for tau PET than for amyloid PET, which limits the analytical interchangeability of plasma biomarkers with imaging readouts. Additionally, measurement technology significantly influences the strength of these correlations. Thus, correlation with neuroimaging is useful but limited, reinforcing the need for multimodal models for diagnosis and monitoring (Mielke et al. 2021).

Another limitation relates to pre-analytical stability. For NfL, Lewczuk et al. examined the influence of pre-analytical conditions using samples from five donors. Their discussion highlights observations of NfL levels under different sample conditions. A slight but significant increase was noted in aliquots thawed and refrozen two or three times, stored for five days at room temperature, or refrigerated. In contrast, a single freeze-thaw cycle did not systematically alter NfL concentration but resulted in non-systematic changes—some samples increased while others decreased—leading to greater variability without a change in the mean. This suggests that, despite apparent preservation of the mean, behavior under these cycles is sample-dependent, which could be critical for individualized studies. CSF data are somewhat ambiguous: some studies report a rapid concentration drop at room temperature or 4 °C, while others observe no significant changes until after three days. Therefore, rapid processing or immediate freezing after collection is recommended for CSF, plasma, and serum. Nevertheless, serum measurements appear more stable, even after freeze-thaw cycles (Lewczuk et al. 2018).

Another important consideration is comorbidities and the non-specificity of certain markers such as NfL, t-Tau, and GFAP, which may be altered by acute or progressive pathologies unrelated to cognitive decline. Suárez-Calvet et al. emphasize the need to consider comorbidities when interpreting blood biomarkers, particularly in clinical contexts (Suárez-Calvet et al. 2025). This can occur in individuals with renal disease, type 2 diabetes, psychiatric disorders, etc., where biomarker alterations could generate false positives in dementia studies if comorbidity is not accounted for. Walwyn-Hernández et al. found that patients with type 2 diabetes exhibit alterations in biomarkers such as ERC-tau or APOE, suggesting a pathophysiological correlation between diabetes and AD and a potential risk of false positives (Walwyn-Hernández et al. 2023). Edwards et al., in a multicohort study, observed that GFAP, NfL, and serum tau correlate with neurobehavioral dysfunction following mild, moderate, and severe TBI, demonstrating that these biomarkers can be altered by brain injury unrelated to dementia (Edwards et al. 2023).

Most Promising Biomarkers

Challenges in Biomarkers Selection

Throughout research on biomarkers associated with cognitive decline, several challenges hinder the selection of a marker that meets all the ideal conditions for early detection of CI and AD. The low concentration of biomarkers in plasma and the need for ultrasensitive techniques such as SIMOA are key challenges for clinical implementation (Pacoova Dal Maschio et al. 2025). Additionally, the blood–brain barrier limits the release of neuronal proteins into the bloodstream, affecting their detectability (Sharma et al. 2025; Dan and Chiam 2024). Peripheral interference further reduces diagnostic specificity, particularly in the presence of comorbidities (Dark et al. 2024; Lomi et al. 2025). Biomarkers also exhibit distinct dynamics across the Alzheimer’s continuum, with GFAP increasing in preclinical stages and p-Tau217 emerging in early phases (see Fig. 6) (Guo et al. 2023). There must be a correlation between measurements obtained using platforms such as SIMOA and cognitive scales like Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) and Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA). Finally, biomarkers should be cost-effective while ensuring that sample collection remains minimally invasive for patients, as discussed earlier in this review regarding the advantages of BBB over CSF or neuroimaging.

The biomarkers discussed in this review satisfy several of these criteria to varying degrees, although clear differences in performance justify a comparative synthesis in the following subsections. Some are more limited due to variability across measurement techniques or the influence of comorbidities and peripheral factors. Below, we summarize and integrate this information.

Amyloid-β Protein and Aβ42/ Aβ40 Ratio

Amyloid-β has historically been one of the most extensively investigated plasma biomarkers owing to its central role in Alzheimer’s disease pathophysiology (Pradeepkiran et al. 2024). However, despite its biological relevance, the diagnostic performance of plasma Aβ42, Aβ40 and their ratio remains limited when used independently. One contributing factor is the small dynamic range of these peptides in blood, partly explained by their high affinity for aggregation into amyloid plaques (Morris et al. 2024), which restricts their availability in circulation and reduces their passage across the blood–brain barrier (Preis et al. 2024; Petrushanko et al. 2023). Moreover, plasma Aβ concentrations are strongly influenced by peripheral production, circadian oscillations and comorbid conditions, and substantial analytical variability persists across measurement platforms, further limiting their reliability as standalone diagnostic markers.

Although reduced Aβ42/Aβ40 ratios are consistently observed in amyloid-positive individuals, their sensitivity and specificity in plasma are insufficient for reliable standalone diagnosis, and associations with cognition are weak and inconsistent across cohorts. For these reasons, plasma Aβ biomarkers are considered supportive rather than primary diagnostic tools. Their utility is greatest when integrated into multimodal models or combined with other markers such as APOE ε4 or phosphorylated tau, rather than being classified among the most promising independent biomarkers for clinical implementation.

Total Tau

Although total tau is a key cerebrospinal fluid biomarker, its performance in plasma is considerably weaker. Plasma t-Tau shows low specificity for Alzheimer’s disease, as its concentrations are influenced by multiple non-AD conditions, including other neurodegenerative disorders and acute neurological injuries. Moreover, its dynamic range in blood is small and associations with clinical severity or cognitive decline are inconsistent across cohorts. For these reasons, t-Tau is considered a complementary marker within multimodal models rather than a promising standalone plasma biomarker for early Alzheimer’s disease detection.

Phosphorylated Tau Isoforms (p-Tau181, p-Tau231, p-Tau217)

Phosphorylated tau isoforms represent the most promising plasma biomarkers for the detection and monitoring of Alzheimer’s disease. Among these, p-Tau181 has shown robust and reproducible diagnostic performance across the AD continuum. It increases progressively from preclinical stages through MCI and dementia and reliably differentiates individuals according to amyloid status, making it valuable for early detection and for use in clinical settings where CSF or PET confirmation is not available. Although it offers strong sensitivity, its specificity is lower than that of later-generation tau epitopes.

p-Tau231 has demonstrated potential as an early marker of amyloid-related changes and shows consistent increases in impaired individuals compared with controls. However, its diagnostic performance is more modest and less stable across studies than that of p-Tau181 and p-Tau217, limiting its suitability as a primary biomarker but supporting its role as a complementary marker.

Among all tau isoforms, p-Tau217 stands out as the most accurate and specific plasma biomarker for identifying amyloid positivity, detecting early AD pathology and predicting clinical progression. Its superior discriminatory ability has been consistently demonstrated across independent cohorts and analytical platforms. Recently, the p-Tau217/β-Amyloid 1–42 Plasma Ratio received regulatory approval for clinical use on the Lumipulse platform in adults aged 55 years or older presenting symptoms of cognitive impairment, representing a major milestone in the transition toward clinically deployable blood-based biomarkers. Although not intended as a standalone screening tool, this assay provides a less invasive alternative to lumbar puncture and can reduce the need for advanced neuroimaging, with performance approaching that of CSF-based measures (Food and Drug Administration 2025).

Taken together, phosphorylated tau epitopes provide the most reliable diagnostic information among plasma biomarkers, with p-Tau217 emerging as the leading candidate for widespread clinical implementation, p-Tau181 offering strong but more generalizable diagnostic value, and p-Tau231 supporting early-stage detection in a complementary role.

Neurofilament Light Chain

Neurofilament light chain serves as a sensitive indicator of neuroaxonal injury and performs consistently well as the “N” component within the AT(N) framework. Plasma NfL increases across the cognitive continuum and provides valuable information on disease severity and clinical progression. However, its diagnostic value for Alzheimer’s disease is limited by its lack of specificity: NfL rises in a wide range of neurological and systemic conditions, and its strong age-dependency further reduces its discriminative utility in older populations. Although not suited for early diagnosis or for distinguishing AD from other neurodegenerative disorders, NfL remains a useful biomarker for monitoring neurodegeneration and for enhancing predictive accuracy when incorporated into multimarker panels alongside phosphorylated tau and GFAP.

Glial Fibrillary Acidic Protein

GFAP has emerged as one of the strongest plasma biomarkers for detecting Alzheimer related pathological change. It reflects astrocytic activation, a process that begins early in the disease course and continues throughout symptomatic stages, making GFAP particularly sensitive to preclinical Aβ pathology. Its levels increase in cognitively unimpaired Aβ positive individuals and rise progressively with cognitive impairment, offering robust discrimination between major clinical groups and supporting its role as a valuable marker for early detection and disease monitoring.

Despite its high sensitivity, GFAP lacks absolute specificity for Alzheimer’s disease, as elevations can also occur in other neurological and systemic conditions associated with inflammatory or astroglial responses (Dan and Chiam 2024). Nevertheless, its performance improves further when incorporated into multimarker models, where GFAP complements phosphorylated tau isoforms and NfL to enhance diagnostic accuracy and stratification. Taken together, GFAP stands out as one of the most promising and clinically informative blood based biomarkers for identifying early pathological change and tracking progression across the Alzheimer’s continuum.

Comparative Summary and Clinical Implications

In summary, GFAP is a robust, sensitive, and early biomarker for detection and clinical stratification, due to its progressive increase with amyloid accumulation and tauopathy severity. These features make GFAP advantageous for clinical trial applications, facilitating early recruitment. Associated with future dementia risk, GFAP’s use alone or in combination with other biomarkers or techniques improves diagnostic specificity, refuting the hypothesis that plasma elevations solely reflect blood–brain barrier disruption.

Plasma biomarkers exhibit heterogeneous behaviour regarding sensitivity, specificity, and timing of appearance along the Alzheimer’s continuum. In preclinical stages, p-Tau217 has proven the most specific biomarker, now employed for diagnosis via the Lumipulse platform (Food and Drug Administration 2025). GFAP and p-Tau181 have demonstrated excellent utility in early cognitive decline, with GFAP being less specific due to its inflammatory nature. NfL is valuable for patient monitoring but requires combination with other biomarkers such as those mentioned above Mazzeo et al. (2024). Finally, amyloid protein isoforms, despite their close relationship with amyloid deposition and brain damage, must be combined with other methods to provide a robust diagnosis. In summary, p-Tau217, p-Tau181, and GFAP currently represent the most promising biomarkers for studying and monitoring AD Janelidze et al. (2023b). Limitations of Included Studies

It is essential to acknowledge the methodological limitations present in the analyzed studies, as these affect the interpretation and generalization of findings.

Beyond analytical performance, the translation of SIMOA-based biomarkers into routine clinical practice faces several practical challenges. First, SIMOA platforms remain costly, both in terms of instrument acquisition and per-sample assay expenses, limiting their feasibility in many healthcare settings. Second, the workflow requires trained laboratory personnel and dedicated infrastructure, as the assays involve multiple manual steps and are not yet fully automated. These factors reduce throughput and hinder scalability, particularly in primary care or resource-limited environments. Consequently, despite their strong diagnostic potential, SIMOA-based biomarkers are not yet positioned for widespread clinical implementation, underscoring the need for more accessible, automated, and cost-effective platforms. Also, a methodological limitation of this review is that the literature search used standardized nomenclature (e.g., ‘p-TauXXX’, ‘AβXX’) and may have missed studies indexed under alternative naming formats such as ‘ptauXXX’, ‘pTauXXX’, or ‘AbetaXX’. Although these terms are used interchangeably in the field, variability in naming conventions across databases could have limited the retrieval of some records.

Second, some included studies feature small cohorts, such as Thanapornsangsuth et al. (2023) with 51 participants, or Yu et al. (2022) and Sewell et al. (2024), which barely reached 100 patients in total. Although most studies lack validation cohorts, some utilize globally recognized longitudinal and multimodal research platforms such as ADNI (Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative) (Jack et al. 2008), TRIAD (Translational Biomarkers in Aging and Dementia) (Stevenson et al. 2022), and SPIN (Sant Pau Initiative on Neurodegeneration) (Alcolea et al. 2019), widely used for biomarker validation. Examples include studies by Benedet et al. (2021) and Ashton et al. (2023) using TRIAD, and others by Benedet et al. (2020) and Therriault et al. (2023), which employed both cohorts.

Another aspect concerns participant nationality. Most studies focus on European populations (Parvizi et al. 2022; Kern et al. 2022; Santangelo et al. 2021; Barker et al. 2021), North American (Mexico, Canada, and the United States) (Xiong et al. 2024; Udeh-Momoh et al. 2022; Hall et al. 2021), or Asian (China) (Dong et al. 2023; Liu et al. 2024; Huang et al. 2022; Xiao et al. 2021), with few studies from other regions, such as Fowler et al. (2022) or Chatterjee et al. (2021) in Australian populations. No studies meeting this review’s inclusion criteria were found in African populations, except Aschenbrenner et al., which included African American individuals (2022). This absence reflects our strict inclusion criteria and does not imply that such studies have not been conducted using alternative methodologies or biomarker platforms. Global research on CI and AD is essential to establish standardized biomarker thresholds applicable across ethnicities, and this gap limits worldwide generalization. This also highlights another limitation: the absence of standardized cut-off points, resulting in discrepancies between studies that may appear contradictory, such as Truffi et al. (2023) and Barker et al. (2021), where the former states sex does not affect NfL levels, while the latter reports higher plasma NfL in women than men. These discrepancies underscore the need to consider cohort characteristics and emphasize the urgency of globally harmonized protocols and cut-off points.

Additionally, some studies, such as Xiong et al. (2024) and Tanaka et al. (2021), employed multiple techniques beyond SIMOA. While some results are relevant to this review, they were measured using other platforms. However, this also contributes to assessing SIMOA’s validity by comparing its performance with other currently active research technologies, some already implemented clinically, such as Lumipulse.

Finally, the publication years of included studies vary considerably, although this review confirms that the information remains consistent with current evidence.

Clinical Implications and Future Research Directions

Recent advances in disease-modifying therapies and BBB mark a turning point in AD clinical practice, although challenges remain for global implementation.

New Pharmacological Therapies

Although the studies reviewed in this section do not use SIMOA directly, emerging pharmacological therapies are intrinsically linked to the development of accurate and scalable diagnostic tools. Plasma biomarkers measured with ultrasensitive platforms such as SIMOA are increasingly incorporated into clinical trials and early-intervention strategies, as they allow treatment effects and amyloid-related changes to be monitored through minimally invasive blood sampling (Cummings et al. 2025). This connection is particularly relevant in the context of the recently FDA-approved anti-amyloid monoclonal antibodies lecanemab and donanemab, which are indicated for early symptomatic AD and show maximal efficacy when initiated before substantial neurodegeneration has occurred (Paczynski et al. 2025). Thus, the availability of reliable blood-based biomarkers is essential for identifying suitable candidates at the earliest stages, enabling more timely and effective therapeutic intervention.

Lecanemab exhibits high affinity for soluble and diffusible aggregated Aβ species, such as oligomers or protofibrils, which represent the most synaptotoxic forms (Rabinovici et al. 2025; Chen et al. 2025; Fertan et al. 2025; Söderberg et al. 2023). Trials have been conducted in diverse populations, including Chinese (Chen et al. 2025) and French cohorts (Villain et al. 2025). Villain et al. report that in France, Lecanemab use is not recommended for patients at high risk of complications, such as APOE4 carriers, individuals on anticoagulants, or those with cerebral amyloid angiopathy (CAA), due to increased risk of amyloid-related intracerebral hemorrhage. Administration requires personalized biological and genetic diagnosis, incorporating plasma biomarkers and genetic testing to determine eligibility. MRI monitoring adapted to APOE status and specific protocols for ARIA (Amyloid-Related Imaging Abnormalities) detection and management are also required. Ensuring equitable access will demand healthcare system adaptation, strengthened collaboration between primary care and specialized units, and professional training in ARIA management and treatment discontinuation protocols (Villain et al. 2025).

Donanemab, as observed in murine models and human trials, acts by inducing macrophage-mediated clearance of Aβ plaques, targeting the N3pE-Aβ epitope—a truncated, post-translationally modified form found only in insoluble amyloid plaques and cerebrovascular deposits. This mechanism makes donanemab more oriented toward removing established plaques, differing from Lecanemab’s neutralization of soluble oligomers. Donanemab crosses the blood–brain barrier after intravenous administration and binds N3pE-Aβ in plaques and blood vessels. Its clinical indication targets patients in early AD stages (MCI or mild dementia) and is not recommended for moderate/severe dementia, preclinical stages, or subjective memory complaints without functional or neuropsychological impairment (Rabinovici et al. 2025).

As with Lecanemab, Donanemab is also associated with a substantial risk of amyloid-related imaging abnormalities (ARIA), including both ARIA-E (vasogenic edema/effusion) and ARIA-H (microhemorrhages and superficial siderosis). As with Lecanemab, Donanemab is also associated with a substantial risk of amyloid-related imaging abnormalities (ARIA), including both ARIA-E (vasogenic edema/effusion) and ARIA-H (microhemorrhages and superficial siderosis). In clinical trials, ARIA-E occurred in 20–24% of treated patients and ARIA-H in 27–31%, with cases of intracerebral hemorrhage reported, particularly among APOE ε4 carriers (Zimmer et al. 2025). The FDA prescribing information for donanemab likewise highlights the risk of ARIA-related edema and hemorrhage, including serious and occasionally life-threatening events (Food and Drug Administration 2024).

These monoclonal antibodies represent a promising future for dementia treatment and require complementary monitoring through BBB, such as those discussed in this review.

New Advances in Biomarkers for Clinical Use

Among BBB for clinical practice, p-Tau217 has demonstrated high sensitivity for AD detection, showing strong compatibility with clinical implementation (Wojdała et al. 2024; Wang et al. 2025b). Wojdala et al. compared eight SIMOA and Lumipulse assays, concluding that p-Tau217 was the most robust marker regardless of platform (Wojdała et al. 2024).

The FDA authorized the Lumipulse G pTau217/β-Amyloid 1–42 Plasma Ratio as the first blood test to aid AD diagnosis in adults. Its use is indicated for patients aged 55 and older with CI signs. The pTau217/Aβ42 ratio correlates with amyloid plaque presence, reducing the need for PET and offering a less invasive alternative to lumbar puncture (Hu et al. 2025). This milestone demonstrates the compatibility of BBB with clinical practice.

SIMOA technology remains a reference tool for biomarker research (Wang et al. 2025b), although its clinical implementation is in progress, as evidenced by the FDA’s recent “Breakthrough Device” designation for the plasma p-Tau217 test based on SIMOA, marking a step toward future regulatory approval (Quanterix 2024).

These advances reflect the transition of blood biomarkers from research to clinical practice, anticipating a future where less invasive tests complement or occasionally replace traditional methods such as lumbar puncture and advanced neuroimaging (Hansson et al. 2023; Wisch and Ances 2025).

Future Research Directions

Beyond the biomarkers addressed in this review, recent studies explore new phosphorylated tau epitopes. Islam et al. investigated p-Tau262 and p-Tau356 as biomarkers of pre-tangle stages, showing potential for ultra-early diagnosis and development of anti-tau therapies (Islam et al. 2025).

Integration of biomarkers such as p-Tau217, GFAP, NfL, and Aβ42/40 into predictive algorithms is proposed to improve stratification and cost-effectiveness (Chang et al. 2025), alongside the need for validation in global cohorts. In this context, the incorporation of explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) into predictive models represents a promising avenue, as demonstrated in neuroimaging-based approaches for multiple sclerosis, where XAI improved interpretability and diagnostic accuracy (Campanioni et al. 2023, 2024).

Furthermore, future research could explore additional BBB associated with psychiatric disorders, such as insulin-like growth factors (IGFs), given their reported links to major depressive disorder, schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, autism, and dementia (Agbemenyah et al. 2014). Combining these with cognitive decline biomarkers may enable comprehensive panels addressing both risk factors and disease progression (Fernández-Pereira and Agís-Balboa 2025).

However, challenges slow clinical implementation. These include the high cost of monoclonal antibodies and infrastructure requirements for infusions and monitoring (Aye et al. 2024), accessibility of blood biomarkers in primary care to democratize diagnosis (Schöll et al. 2024), and international standardization of cut-off points and protocols (Mielke and Fowler 2024), all of which require resolution to fully realize the advantages of biomarker use in clinical practice.

Conclusions

This systematic review aimed to evaluate the utility of BBB for the early diagnosis and monitoring of Alzheimer’s-type CI in studies using SIMOA technology, comparing their performance with standard-of-care techniques in specialized settings such as lumbar puncture and neuroimaging.

The evidence suggests that plasma biomarkers analyzed through SIMOA offer high performance in detecting and monitoring cognitive decline associated with AD proving overall more cost-effective than CSF analysis and neuroimaging. Moreover, their assessment enables early prognosis and longitudinal monitoring across the Alzheimer’s continuum, as they show utility at different disease stages. For instance, p-Tau217 stands out for its high specificity in early diagnosis, while GFAP demonstrates value throughout all phases. These biomarkers have shown strong diagnostic potential both individually and in combination with each other or with others, such as NfL, which emerges as a reliable marker for monitoring in later stages.

Overall, the findings indicate that BBB measured with SIMOA represent a promising alternative and/or complement for clinical practice. However, their implementation faces limitations, including the lack of FDA approval, limited accessibility, and the absence of standardized reference values, which restricts the generalization of results. Therefore, longitudinal studies and validation in clinical settings are required to consolidate their diagnostic utility.

In summary, this review reinforces the notion that incorporating SIMOA technology into clinical practice could transform the approach to CI, enabling more streamlined protocols and precision medicine that improve care for patients, families, and healthcare professionals.
