Strain- and temperature effects on structure–property relationships in nanocrystalline tungsten thin films
Authors: Ivan Buryk, Larysa Odnodvorets, Yurii Shabelnyk, Mykola Buryk, Tetiana Hovorun, Khrystyna Berladir, Guido Schmitz, Vitalii Ivanov
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNanocrystalline tungsten (nc-W) thin films are promising for nanoelectronic applications owing to their high thermal stability, mechanical robustness, and tunable electronic transport properties. In this work, the electrical resistivity, temperature coefficient of resistance (TCR), and strain coefficient (SC), correlating transport behaviour with microstructural evolution under thermal and mechanical loading was systematically investigated. Electrical conduction is found to be governed primarily by electron scattering at grain boundaries and film surfaces. The effective electron mean free path, limited by grain boundaries, is ~ 8.0 nm, while the grain-boundary reflection coefficient is R ≈ 0.49 ± 0.02, indicating intermediate interfacial scattering strength. Under quasi-elastic longitudinal strains of 1–2%, the films exhibit enhanced strain sensitivity with SC ≈ 4.7, whereas the TCR remains stable at ~ 1.3 × 10⁻³ K⁻¹ over the 300–800 K range, demonstrating robust thermal stability of the transport response. These findings indicate that grain-boundary scattering dominates charge transport and can be modulated through strain- and temperature-induced microstructural evolution, suggesting that grain boundaries act as structurally tunable interfaces governing macroscopic electronic behaviour in nanocrystalline metals.Keywords: Nanocrystalline tungsten, Thin-film resistivity, Strain–temperature coupling, Electron scattering, Grain-boundary-dominated transportSubject terms: Engineering, Materials science, Nanoscience and technology, Physics

Introduction

Nanocrystalline materials are a key platform in modern nanoelectronics owing to their combination of scale-dependent physical properties and extensive opportunities for microstructure engineering1. Of particular interest are nanocrystalline tungsten (nc-W) films, which combine high hardness, thermal stability, chemical inertness, and significant electrical conductivity. This combination makes them promising for use in vertically integrated structures of integrated circuits, where the stability of electrical parameters, mechanical reliability, and resistance to electromigration are critically important2,3.

The integration of nc-W films into three-dimensional architectures enables an increase in device density and efficient utilisation of chip volume. At the same time, the role of grain boundaries and interfaces, which determine the scattering mechanisms of charge carriers, increases significantly in nanoscale films4. As the average crystallite size decreases, electrical transport is increasingly controlled by grain-boundary, surface, and defect scattering, leading to an increase in resistivity and changes in its temperature or strain dependence5. In such systems, external stimuli such as temperature6,7, mechanical stress8,9, or adsorption10–12 lead to a noticeable modification of conductivity, which directly affects the operational stability of nanoelectronic components.

The high surface-area-to-volume ratio in nc-W films significantly alters their adsorption properties compared with bulk tungsten. Reducing the grain size increases the fraction of atoms at boundaries and surfaces, thereby enhancing their interactions with the gaseous environment. Adsorbed particles modify both the surface potential landscape and the effective electron mean free path, thereby changing the contribution of surface scattering to the total resistance.

Additionally, quantum-size effects can influence the density of states near the Fermi level, determining the sensitivity of electrical conductivity to external factors. From the perspective of transition-metal physics, the transport properties of tungsten are determined by the specific features of its electronic structure with partially filled 5d bands4. The strong coupling between the electronic subsystem, lattice vibrations, and crystal-lattice deformation results in a high sensitivity of electrical resistance to temperature and mechanical loading. In the temperature range relevant for device operation (300–1000 K), resistivity is primarily controlled by electron–phonon scattering, whereas in nanocrystalline films additional contributions arise from grain boundaries and structural defects. The values of TCR and SC reflect changes in the density of states at the Fermi level and its dependence on interatomic distance. The non-monotonic dependence of TCR and SC on the concentration of valence (s + d) electrons indicates their common electronic nature. For tungsten, as a 5d transition metal with a broad conduction band, the total concentration of (s + d) electrons can be considered a unifying parameter that determines the temperature and strain sensitivity of electrical resistance. Furthermore, the strong spin–orbit coupling of 5d electrons leads to pronounced spin-dependent transport effects. It has been shown that hydrogen adsorption on the W(110) surface can alter surface scattering mechanisms and conductivity10, while the metastable β-phase of W (A15 structure) exhibits high values of the spin Hall angle (~ 0.33–0.4) under optimal growth conditions11. The stabilisation of epitaxial metallic WO₂ expands the possibilities for spin-current generation in 5d oxides12.

Thus, nc-W films serve as a convenient model system for investigating the interrelationship between microstructure, electronic structure, and electrical properties under external stimuli. Understanding the mechanisms of charge-carrier scattering, the temperature dependence of electrical resistance and strain sensitivity, as well as the impact of adsorption processes and phase transformations, is key to optimising nc-W as a functional material for modern nanoelectronics.

Materials and methods

Formation of film structures and study of their electrical properties

The nc-W films with thicknesses of 5–50 nm were obtained by electron-beam evaporation (Fig. 1) of high-purity tungsten (99.99%) in high vacuum (p ≈ 10⁻⁴ Pa) using a VUP-5 M vacuum system equipped with a diffusion pump5,13. The vacuum chamber also included devices for film deformation and thermal annealing, as well as thermocouples, thickness sensors, and various substrates, including glass-ceramic, polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE), and NaCl single crystals. The electrical resistance of the films (± 0.05%) and the substrate temperature (± 1 K) were measured synchronously, while the thickness was monitored in situ using a quartz-crystal microbalance (± 5%). Heat treatments in the range from 300 K to 800 K were carried out with an average heating/cooling rate of 3 K min⁻¹.

Schematic of the nc-W thin-film fabrication and measurement setup.

The film growth parameters were adjusted by varying the condensation rate and substrate temperature. The phase evolution observed in this study cannot be attributed to a specific critical deposition rate. Instead, the formation of amorphous, metastable β-W, and crystalline α-W (BCC) phases is governed by kinetic growth conditions, including the interplay between deposition flux, surface diffusion, and the homologous substrate temperature (T/Tₘ), in agreement with the structural zone model for physical vapour deposition14.

Under conditions of low deposition flux and limited adatom mobility, kinetically trapped amorphous or β-W-like structures are favoured. In contrast, enhanced surface diffusion or increased thermal activation promotes the transition to the thermodynamically stable α-W phase. This behaviour has been consistently reported for tungsten thin films deposited by sputtering and evaporation techniques, indicating that phase selection is governed by growth kinetics rather than by a unique deposition-rate threshold14–17.

The piezoresistive properties of the films were investigated on polished PTFE substrates using a micrometre-controlled deformation device. Copper film contact pads were pre-deposited on the substrates and annealed at 450 K. The geometry of the substrate and contacts remained constant, ensuring high reproducibility of the results. Strain sensitivity was determined by the standard strain-gauge method based on the slope of the ΔR/R dependence upon longitudinal strain during loading–unloading cycles.

The thermoresistive characteristics of nc-W films on glass-ceramic substrates were determined in situ during thermal treatment by recording the electrical resistance synchronously with the heater temperature. Copper film contact pads were deposited on the substrates and annealed at 800 K. After thermal stabilisation, macroscopic contacts were pressed against them. Cyclic heating and cooling enabled evaluation of the temperature coefficient of resistance (TCR) and the stabilisation of resistivity.

Comprehensive analysis of the structure and phase composition of nc-W (TEM, SIMS)

The phase composition and structural characteristics of the films were investigated using transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and electron diffraction with a SELMI TEM-125 K microscope. Electron diffraction analysis was performed using ring diffraction patterns to determine interplanar spacings, which were subsequently compared with PDF-2 (JCPDS) reference data. This allowed the identification of crystalline phases, the determination of lattice type, and the evaluation of the degree of crystallinity of the films. The morphology and microstructure of nc-W were studied in bright-field and dark-field modes, as well as via selected-area electron diffraction (SAED), which enabled the determination of average grain size, grain-size distribution, and the presence of texture, amorphous regions, or defects. Samples were prepared by depositing films 5–50 nm thick on carbon substrates mounted on copper grids, ensuring electron transparency and minimising multiple scattering.

The phase composition of nc-W films is determined by the condensation conditions, among which the pressure and chemical composition of residual gases are of decisive importance. Active adsorption of residual-atmosphere components on the growth surface can lead to the formation of chemical compounds, which significantly affects the structure and properties of the films.

The elemental composition was investigated by secondary ion mass spectrometry (SIMS) using an MS-7201 M mass spectrometer. The analysis was performed in ion-sputtering mode with secondary-ion detection, enabling qualitative and semi-quantitative depth profiling of thin films and monitoring of oxidation, diffusion, and potential contamination processes. The interpretation of mass spectra for metallic samples was carried out in stages: first, peaks of singly charged isotopes were identified, considering their natural abundances; then, the possible presence of doubly charged ions was analysed; finally, contributions from molecular complexes such as MeO⁺, MeN⁺, and MeC⁺, as well as hydride ions MeH⁺, were accounted for. This approach enabled the correct separation of isotopic, elemental, and molecular components in the mass spectra, providing a reliable determination of the chemical composition and its evolution under different deposition conditions and thermal treatments of the nc-W films.

Results and discussion

Structural phase composition

The structural–phase state of nc-W films is governed by deposition conditions, including condensation rate, substrate temperature, and vacuum level. At a low condensation rate (~ 0.1 nm s⁻¹) and substrate temperatures up to 400 K under technological vacuum conditions (~ 10⁻⁴ Pa), the limited adatom flux leads to kinetically constrained growth with insufficient surface diffusion, resulting in predominantly amorphous W films, as confirmed by electron microscopy and diffraction analyses. With increasing film thickness and/or enhanced adatom mobility during post-deposition annealing up to 800 K, structural relaxation and progressive atomic ordering occur, leading to the development of metastable β-W, which subsequently transforms toward the thermodynamically stable α-W phase. The observed amorphous → β-W → α-W evolution at low growth rates is consistent with a kinetic-stabilisation scenario, in which early-stage disorder and oxygen incorporation favour β-W nucleation, whereas progressive adatom rearrangement and oxygen redistribution drive the transition toward thermodynamically stable α-W, in line with reported oxygen-assisted stabilisation and phase evolution in sputtered nc-W films15–17.

Increasing the condensation rate to 1.6 nm s⁻¹ and the substrate temperature to 450 K promotes the formation of nc-W films with a body-centred cubic (BCC) structure (Fig. 2a, b; Table 1), which is characteristic of bulk tungsten. The average grain size in these films is approximately L ≈ 12 nm (Fig. 2c). After annealing up to 800 K, the films retain their crystalline structure, as confirmed by the presence of sharp diffraction maxima corresponding to BCC-W planes in the electron diffraction patterns.

Increasing the deposition rate and substrate temperature enhances phase stability at lower temperatures and promotes the formation of a stable nanocrystalline state. This enables controlled tuning of the structural–phase composition of nc-W films and ensures their thermal stability over a wide temperature range. Notably, nc-W films retain structural integrity even after heating to ~ 1000 °C under vacuum conditions18, while in situ TEM studies reveal stable microstructural evolution with controlled grain growth up to similar temperatures19. This stability is intrinsic to tungsten and arises from its refractory nature, including its high melting point, low diffusion rates, and resistance to thermally activated degradation.

The mass spectra of nc-W films recorded in the mass range 180–220 amu (Fig. 3) are characterised by intense peaks corresponding to matrix ions and molecular fragments of W. The identification of singly charged W⁺ isotope peaks was performed by accounting for their natural abundances. Dominant signals in the m/z = 182–186 region correspond to ¹⁸²W⁺, ¹⁸³W⁺, ¹⁸⁴W⁺, and ¹⁸⁶W⁺ ions, with their relative intensities being consistent with standard isotopic ratios. The predominance of the ¹⁸⁴W⁺ and ¹⁸⁶W⁺ peaks confirms that the W matrix makes the primary contribution to the spectrum.

Secondary ion mass-spectra of nc-W films obtained under the specified technological conditions.

In the investigated spectral interval, there are no pronounced signals that could be unambiguously attributed to doubly charged W²⁺ ions, indicating the predominance of singly charged particles in this mass range. The spectrum also exhibits peaks of molecular ions WN⁺, WC⁺, and WO⁺ in the m/z ≈ 194–202 region, along with more massive fragments such as WCO⁺ and WNO⁺ in the m/z ≈ 208–216 range. Their positions correspond to the sum of the masses of tungsten isotopes and light elements (N, C, O). The presence of WO⁺ indicates partial surface oxidation (e.g., W₃O), whereas WN⁺ and WC⁺ may result from the incorporation of nitrogen and carbon from residual gases during deposition or from post-deposition interaction with the environment15. Given the proximity of the masses of hydride ions WH⁺ to the W⁺ isotopic peaks, their potential contribution was evaluated by analysing deviations from the expected isotopic ratios; the results obtained indicate that this contribution is negligible.

Thus, a comprehensive analysis of elemental and molecular components ensures the correct interpretation of mass spectra and the reliable determination of the chemical composition of nc-W films, while also allowing tracking of changes under different deposition conditions and subsequent thermal treatment.

Electrical properties

The nc-W films demonstrated high thermal stability of electrical parameters within the investigated temperature range, confirming their suitability for electronic and sensor applications. Measurements showed that electrical resistivity significantly depends on film thickness and microstructural characteristics, indicating the dominant role of charge-carrier scattering at grain boundaries and surfaces. Strain-sensitivity studies have demonstrated that electrical resistance is sensitive to applied mechanical deformation, owing to changes in the effective electron mean free path and modification of the intergranular potential barrier. The temperature dependence of resistance exhibits metal-like behaviour with a TCR whose magnitude correlates with the phase state and the degree of film oxidation. The results obtained allow quantitative estimation of the contributions of structural and thermal factors to the electrical conductivity of nc-W and the determination of optimal conditions for the stable operation of sensing elements.

Figure 4a shows a typical temperature dependence of the electrical resistance for nc-W films with a thickness of ~ 10 nm during the second thermal-stabilisation cycle. Analysis of the cooling curve enabled estimation of the TCR ≈ 1.0 × 10⁻³ K⁻¹ and confirmed the stabilisation of the resistivity after completion of the structural transformations that occurred during the first heating cycle. As an example of the thermoresistive behaviour, several characteristic temperature regions in the resistivity response of nc-W films were identified during the first heating cycle (Fig. 4b). In the 300–650 K interval, a slight decrease in resistivity was observed, indicating gradual relaxation of internal stresses and partial ordering of the defect structure. In the 650–770 K range, a sharp drop in resistivity occurred, which is associated with intensive defect-recovery processes in as-deposited samples. A further increase in temperature (T > 770 K) was accompanied by a gradual decrease in resistivity, which is consistent with thermally activated structural-relaxation processes in nc-W films. The obtained results suggest that thermally activated structural transformations may significantly influence the electrical conductivity of nc-W films.

Temperature coefficient of resistivity and strain sensitivity in nc-W films: (a) resistivity (inset), resistivity (blue), and TCR (red) versus temperature for the second thermal-stabilisation cycle during cooling; (b) loading in tension (red curve) and unloading (blue curve).

Figure 4b shows the dependence of the relative resistance change (ΔR/R) on longitudinal strain for nc-W films with a thickness of ~ 10 nm under loading and unloading conditions. Their piezoresistive properties were investigated in the range of longitudinal quasi-elastic strains of 1–2% during loading–unloading cycles5,8. As the strain increases, ΔR/R increases monotonically, indicating pronounced strain sensitivity. Deviations from linearity are associated with a combination of plastic-deformation mechanisms20, particularly the rearrangement of defects21, changes in grain size, and grain rotation22,23. The character of the strain–resistance dependencies is preserved across the entire range of film thicknesses, and the sensitivity stabilises after four to five cycles. For thicker nc-W films, the SC value is primarily determined by the scattering of conduction electrons in the bulk and at grain boundaries, yielding an SC of approximately 4.7. As the film thickness decreases to 5 nm, the SC value increases severalfold, which is caused by the enhanced scattering of conduction electrons at the film surfaces. This indicates an increasing contribution of the surface-scattering mechanism to the formation of the electrical properties of ultrathin films. A comparison of the loading–unloading curves reveals slight hysteresis, indicating a predominantly quasi-elastic nature of the deformation and good reproducibility of the electrical response. The obtained results confirm the promising potential of nc-W films for use in high-temperature strain sensors owing to the linearity of the response characteristic, sufficient signal amplitude, and small hysteresis component.

With decreasing thickness and grain size in nanocrystalline tungsten (nc-W) films, the increasing fraction of grain boundaries enhances intergranular electron scattering, leading to a rise in resistivity (Fig. 5a). The combined application of the Fuchs–Sondheimer (FS) surface-scattering model24 and the Mayadas–Shatzkes (MS) grain-boundary scattering framework25 provides good agreement with experimental data, with remaining deviations primarily attributed to surface-scattering contributions. For nc-W, the bulk electron mean free path is λ₀ ≈ 15.5 nm26, whereas the effective mean free path, limited by grain-boundary scattering, is reduced to ~ 8.0 nm, confirming the dominant role of boundary scattering in these thin films.

Thickness-dependent properties of nc-W films: (a) electrical resistivity; (b) longitudinal SC and TCR.

In nc-W films, grain size scales with film thickness and remains comparable in the nanometre range, depending on growth conditions. Analysis25,27 indicates moderate coupling between mean free path and grain-boundary reflection (R = 0.55 ± 0.0627, stable for diffuse surface scattering (p ≈ 0), while grain-size variations have minor effects and the use of TEM-based constraints improves parameter robustness.

Because λ₀ exceeds the characteristic grain size (L ≈ 12 nm), charge transport occurs in a grain-boundary-dominated regime. This is further supported by the experimentally observed strain coefficient (SC ≈ 4.7), which significantly exceeds both the geometric (Poisson) contribution (≈ 1.5–1.7) and the intrinsic strain coefficient of bulk tungsten (≈ 1.16)28, indicating that neither geometric effects nor bulk electron–phonon scattering are sufficient to explain the observed response. Within the MS framework, using a reflection coefficient R ≈ 0.49 yields a scattering parameter α = (λ₀/L)·R/(1 − R) ≈ 1.2, placing the system in the strong-scattering regime (α ≳ 1)25, where resistivity depends nonlinearly on α. In this regime, strain-induced variations in grain size and grain-boundary transparency produce amplified changes in resistivity8. The enhanced strain sensitivity is therefore attributed primarily to strain-mediated modulation of grain-boundary scattering, while geometric contributions remain secondary. Overall, the results support a transport mechanism governed by grain-boundary-limited conduction in nanocrystalline tungsten films operating in the strong-scattering regime.

Films of various thicknesses were obtained by high-rate electron-beam evaporation at a substrate temperature of T/Tₘ ≈ 0.12. Under these conditions, growth occurs primarily in the kinetic regime with limited surface diffusion of adatoms, which promotes the formation of a fine-grained structure dominated by random high-angle boundaries. Low-energy Σ3 twin boundaries are characterised by a minimum reflection coefficient (R ≈ 0.47); Σ5 and Σ9 boundaries exhibit higher values (≈ 0.56 and 0.61, respectively), whereas Σ11 and Σ17 correspond to an average or near-maximum scattering level (≈ 0.53 and 0.62)4. The value of R ≈ 0.49 ± 0.02 obtained for the grain-boundary reflection coefficient is consistent with a statistical mixture of boundaries of different crystallographic nature and indicates a moderate barrier-like character of intergranular transport. As the grain size increases, the frequency of electron interaction with boundaries decreases, reducing the contribution of grain-boundary scattering to the total electrical resistance. Thus, the electrical conductivity of nc-W is determined by both the grain size and the statistical distribution of Σ-boundaries; increasing the fraction of Σ3-type boundaries represents an effective pathway to resistivity reduction.

As shown in Fig. 5b, a strain coefficient (SC) of ≈ 4.67 was obtained for the investigated films, indicating an enhanced sensitivity of electrical conductivity to mechanical deformation under conditions of limited thickness and a well-developed network of grain boundaries. The temperature coefficient of resistance is TCR ≈ 1.28 × 10⁻³ K⁻¹, which is lower than the bulk value for α-W. This may be attributed to increased defect contributions, grain-boundary scattering, and the potential formation of a thin surface layer of β-W, which is characterised by higher resistivity and a reduced TCR.

Quantitative atomic-scale characterisation of grain boundaries in nanocrystalline metal films remains challenging: although in situ TEM can capture structural evolution29,30, projection effects and current spreading limit the isolation of intrinsic grain-boundary conductivity, yielding only effective transport properties31,32. In nc-W thin films, charge transport is dominated by grain-boundary scattering, consistent with an MS-type description. Thermal and mechanical stimuli induce microstructural changes that produce measurable variations in effective transport parameters without compromising thermal stability. Grain boundaries thus act as structurally responsive interfaces whose effective contribution to transport can be systematically influenced by strain- and temperature-driven evolution, offering a key means of tailoring electronic transport in robust nanoscale metallic systems.

Conclusions

A systematic analysis of the electrical transport and structural evolution of nc-W films under thermal and mechanical stimuli established a framework for controlling their structure–property relationships. The films exhibited pronounced strain sensitivity (SC ≈ 4.7 under 1–2% quasi-elastic deformation) and stable thermal behaviour with TCR ≈ 1.3 × 10⁻³ K⁻¹ in the 300–800 K range.

The increase in resistivity is primarily governed by surface and grain-boundary electron scattering, consistent with the Fuchs–Sondheimer and Mayadas–Shatzkes approaches. The electron mean free path, limited by grain boundaries, is ~ 8.0 nm, and the grain-boundary reflection coefficient R ≈ 0.49 ± 0.02 confirms moderate interfacial scattering strength. Increasing grain size reduces electron–boundary interactions and lowers the contribution of grain-boundary scattering to the total resistivity. Importantly, electrical transport is influenced not only by average grain size but also by the statistical distribution of Σ-boundaries; increasing the fraction of Σ3-type boundaries represents an effective pathway to resistivity reduction.

Thickness reduction to the nanoscale enhances strain sensitivity owing to amplified surface scattering, while stable behaviour during loading–unloading cycles indicates the formation of a quasi-equilibrium defect configuration. The combination of electrical stability, thermal resilience, and compatibility with silicon substrates makes nc-W films attractive for next-generation metallization, nanoscale interconnects, and strain-sensitive micro- and nanoelectronic systems.
