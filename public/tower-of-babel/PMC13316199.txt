Effectiveness and uptake of ROOM to grow: Results of a Randomized Controlled Trial
Authors: Tajda Laure, Marilisa Boffo, Stefan Konigorski, Djameela Dulloo, Rutger C.M.E. Engels, Danielle Remmerswaal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOne in four university students face mental health challenges, underscoring the need for effective support systems. Within Erasmus University Rotterdam student wellbeing initiative, we developed a mobile intervention providing universal and targeted preventive support, focusing on emotion regulation to address common issues like depression and anxiety. This study reports a superiority Randomized Controlled Trial evaluating intervention impact and uptake among students with elevated stress.Methods students(N = 202) were randomized to immediate ROOM app access (n = 101) or a 3 month waitlist control (n = 101); 171 (85%) completed the trial. Questionnaires were completed at baseline and weeks 3, 8, and 12, alongside continuous in-app activity logs. Primary outcomes were perceived stress and emotion regulation; secondary outcomes included depressive and anxiety symptoms, resilience, and objective and subjective engagement. Analyses followed an intent-to-treat approach, with exploratory subgroup analyses assessing differences by engagement level.ResultsThe intervention showed no statistically significant impact on any outcomes. Exploratory analyses suggested that higher engagement with the intervention was associated with greater reductions in stress. Engagement was initially high but declined sharply after week 1, stabilizing by week 6. More than half of participants still used the intervention at day 30. Breathing and relaxation exercises were favoured, whereas transfer-elements and self-assessments were used less than expected.ConclusionsROOM did not demonstrate significant effects at the sample level, although greater engagement was associated with reduced stress. Future research is needed to better understand what drives engagement with the intervention, strengthen real-world skill application, and evaluate its preventive potential in larger and more representative student samples.

Introduction

College students face numerous challenges, including academic pressure, financial strain, social demands, and adjusting to university life - all intensified by broader concerns like climate change and the housing crisis. Around 25% of university students experience depression symptoms, and 14% face suicide-related thoughts and behaviours (Sheldon et al., 2021). University students' mental health is increasingly recognized as a public health priority (European Commission, 2023). In response, educational institutions worldwide started to integrate student wellbeing into their policies and daily operations, by expanding mental health services, offering coaching and peer-to-peer support, implementing digital health services, hiring more psychologists, and training faculty staff to better support struggling students (Abrams, 2022). Erasmus University Rotterdam (EUR) launched its Student Wellbeing Programme in 2019, facilitating policy changes and practical support aimed at promoting student mental health and resilience. As part of this programme, ROOM to grow was developed - a mobile app to support students in managing their mental health and self-development.

Digital solutions for university student mental health: current trends and gaps

In the past 25 years, the landscape of digital interventions has seen rapid development, resulting in a market flooded with mental health and wellness apps. Yet only about 3–5% (Lau et al., 2020; Leong et al., 2022) are evidence-based and scientifically validated. In addition, many commercial mental health app companies sell user data for profit, exploiting vulnerable individuals seeking help and raising concerns about privacy and the potential harm of such apps (Gross, 2023). Studies on acceptability of mobile mental health tools among university students highlight a demand for reliable mental health information, intuitive and user-friendly interfaces, and personalization. Students often struggle to identify reliable sources online, expressing a need for secure platforms that handle their sensitive data responsibly (Becker and Torous, 2019; Melcher et al., 2022; Montagni et al., 2020; Torous et al., 2018). These gaps highlight the need for user-centred, scientifically validated, and privacy-conscious mental health tools.

Among the small subset of mental health tools for university students that have been scientifically evaluated, studies show generally favourable outcomes. Digital interventions are effective in reducing stress, anxiety and depressive symptoms (Harith et al., 2022; Oliveira et al., 2021; LaMontagne et al., 2024), and are found to be a feasible and well accepted intervention modality in this population (Oliveira et al., 2021). However, privacy and security aspects are rarely addressed, despite their growing importance and university students' heightened awareness and aversion to privacy risks (Oliveira et al., 2021). While findings suggest going digital is promising, systematic reviews highlight the need for more rigorous testing and reporting effectiveness, including larger sample sizes, higher retention, reliable outcome measures, and intention-to-treat analyses (Conley et al., 2022).

ROOM to grow: a transdiagnostic mobile intervention

This paper comprehensively evaluates ROOM to grow (ROOM), a universal and indicated transdiagnostic mobile intervention developed by the EUR Student Wellbeing Programme (Laure et al., 2025). ROOM was designed to address key gaps in existing mental health tools for university students by offering credible content, personalization, and robust privacy and security measures, tailored to students' unique needs and preferences.

ROOM was developed iteratively following the Centre for eHealth Research (CeHReS) roadmap, with university students, experts and other stakeholders (e.g., the university) involved throughout (Laure et al., 2025). The CeHReS framework combines human-centred design, business modelling, and persuasive systems design across five phases (i.e., contextual inquiry, value specification, design, operationalization, and summative evaluation) (van Gemert-Pijnen et al., 2011; Hanneke et al., 2024). Additionally, the app development followed a ‘privacy-by-design’ framework to ensure strong privacy and safety measures from the start (Cavoukian, 2020).

ROOM was designed to teach students adaptive emotion regulation (ER) strategies and increase their self-awareness. ER is a key transdiagnostic factor underlying various mental health problems, including symptoms of anxiety and depression (Aldao et al., 2016) - the most common issues among university students (Auerbach et al., 2018). Supporting students in developing ER skills is therefore a promising approach to preventing and managing distress in this population. ROOM offers a range of micro-interventions, short evidence-based behaviour change exercises drawn from established therapeutic approaches like Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT; Hayes et al., 2006), Cognitive Behavioural Therapy (CBT; Beck, 2011), mindfulness (Creswell, 2017), positive psychology (Seligman, 2002), and self-compassion (Neff et al., 2005), ensuring that students engage with strategies that best fit their needs and preferences.

Furthermore, our contextual inquiry showed that students often do not recognize declines in their mental health until symptoms become severe (Laure et al., 2025). Awareness of one's emotional states is crucial for selecting an appropriate regulation strategy in each moment (Berking and Lukas, 2015; Im and Kahler, 2022). Therefore, ROOM includes a self-assessment module that helps students monitor their emotional states and mental health over time, supporting early identification of mental health difficulties and facilitating adaptive ER.

Lastly, given evidence that engagement is linked to greater clinical benefit in digital mental-health interventions (Gan et al., 2021) and that our target population often experiences digital environments as stressful (Laure et al., 2025), ROOM includes several features designed to support engagement both within and beyond the app. These include reminders, collectable mechanics, tailored recommendations, and transfer elements that support applying strategies learnt in the app in daily life (Laure et al., 2024a, Laure et al., 2025; Torous et al., 2018a; Valentine et al., 2023).

Study objectives

A superiority parallel-group Randomized Controlled Trial (RCT) was used to evaluate ROOM's impact and uptake among university students experiencing distress. Distressed students were targeted for their higher likelihood of using mental health apps (Laure et al., 2024a; Melcher et al., 2022), and greater potential benefit.

The primary outcomes were perceived stress levels and ER skills, with expected improvements in the intervention group over the 12-week period compared to a wait-list control group (Laure et al., 2024a). Secondary outcomes included depressive symptoms, anxiety symptoms, and resilience, expected to improve in the intervention group at 12-week follow up through enhanced ER relative to the control group. Another secondary outcome focused on intervention uptake. A subgroup exploratory analysis examined associations between engagement patterns with ROOM and changes in distress, resilience, and ER skills.

Methods

Study design

This study evaluated ROOM's effectiveness and uptake in distressed university students using a 3-month RCT. Participants were randomized to the intervention condition (IC) (12-week ROOM access; daily use encouraged for 3 weeks) or a wait-list control condition (CC) (Laure et al., 2024a) upon completing the baseline questionnaire. Randomization was stratified by gender identity (i.e., male, female, or other), education level (i.e., bachelor or master), and student origin (i.e., national or international). Both groups completed assessments at baseline, week 3, 8, and 12; the IC group answered additional questions related to engagement patterns at weeks 8 and 12 (Laure et al., 2024a).

Participants

Recruitment took place from November 2023 until March 2024 via social media, university emails, and university screens. Interested students registered to the study via a sign-up page and, once the study began (5 February 2024), received a link to the study details, the consent form, and eligibility screening. Inclusion criteria were being an EUR student, age 18–30, comfortable in completing the study and engaging with an app in English, having a smartphone, and elevated stress (Perceived Stress Scale score > 13; Cohen et al., 1983). Exclusion criteria included a diagnosed mental health disorder, ongoing pharmacological therapy, clinical levels of distress symptoms (Patient Health Questionnaire ≥20 PHQ9; Kroenke et al., 2001). Exclusion criteria ensured a non-clinical sample, as the intervention is preventive rather than therapeutic. Sample size was calculated using G*Power for a mixed ANOVA (two groups, four time points), assuming a small effect (f = 0.10, α = 0.05, power = 0.80, and r = 0.50) resulting in N = 138 in total (Laure et al., 2024a, Laure et al., 2024b). To account for expected high dropout (> 40%) in digital intervention studies, we aimed to recruit 250 students to ensure adequate statistical power (Laure et al., 2024a). We believe that this power analysis provides an appropriate approximation for the required sample size in the GEE and LME models used in the primary analyses to reach 80% statistical power.

Compensation

Participants received a digital gift card as appreciation for completing the assessments: €5 gift card for each of the first three assessments and €15 gift card for the final follow-up, totalling €30.

Ethics approval

The study was approved by the institutional review board at the EUR (Reference: ETH2324–0193) and registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (Identifier: NCT06224647). Study materials will be available on OSF post-embargo period: https://osf.io/g57wu.

Intervention and control condition

Participants randomized to the IC received an e-mail with a unique user ID to activate and access the app while those in the CC received it after the final week-12 assessment. Use of other mental health support during the trial was unrestricted, but participants were asked to report these at the final assessment.

ROOM to grow app: mobile transdiagnostic emotion regulation intervention

The user journey begins with onboarding, where users are introduced to ROOM's features and set personal goals – such as (i) managing difficult thoughts and feelings, (ii) being kinder to oneself, (iii) increasing positive feelings, (iv) relaxing, (v) focusing on what matters most, (vi) being more present - and receive guidance on how to optimize its benefits (i.e., daily monitoring and exercise completion for a minimum of 3 weeks). ROOM includes 7 main components (Laure et al., 2025, Laure et al., 2024a):1.Monitoring of emotional states: Users rate seven emotional states (i.e., stress, frustration, sadness, tiredness, happiness, relaxation, energy) and receive feedback visualizing their current emotional state.2.Mental health exercises: Exercises are grouped into six categories -Breathing and Relaxation, Mindfulness, Self-Compassion, Cognitive Defusion, Cognitive Restructuring, Positive Psychology - each including an introduction, a practice segment, debriefing information, and an evaluation (i.e., 0–100 scale evaluating likeability and helpfulness).3.Learning transfer elements: This feature provides practical tools such as when-then implementation intentions (Golwitzer et al.,), associative cues (i.e., stickers), and shortened version of exercises to help users learn and integrate the techniques into participants' daily life (Villegas Meija et al., 2024; Laure et al., 2025).4.Self-Assessment Module (Map-Myself): Includes 14 validated questionnaires assessing psychological traits (e.g., perfectionism) and states (e.g., perceived stress) and offers normative feedback and relevant exercise recommendations to the user (see Laure et al., 2024a).5.Personal collection of objects (collectable game mechanic): Users can customize their virtual “room” with objects representing completed exercises creating a personal toolbox.6.Recommendation System: Using a federated reinforcement learning approach, the recommendation system tailors exercise suggestions to user history, exercise evaluations, and momentary emotional state, ensuring relevance and encouraging exploration of ROOM's content. The recommender system includes safety protocols such as suggesting relaxation exercises for high stress or not suggesting mindfulness when the user experiences strong feelings of sadness (Laure et al., 2024a).7.Notifications: users could choose between 3 reminder options: a 21-day journey, 3 days of inactivity reminder, or no notifications. The 21-day challenge encouraged users to assess their emotional state and complete a related exercise daily over 21 days, a strategy previously shown to improve users' stress levels and emotion regulation skills (Laure et al., 2024a, Laure et al., 2024b).

Outcomes

Primary outcomes

Primary outcomes were perceived stress and ER skills, measured at all 4 time points. Perceived stress was assessed with the 10-item Perceived Stress Scale (PSS-10; Cohen et al., 1983; IC Cronbach's α baseline (t0) = 0.84, week 3 (t1) = 0.82, week 8 (t2) = 0.86, and week 12 (t3) = 0.87; CC Cronbach's α: t0 = 0.83, t1 = 0.87, t2 = 0.87, t3 = 0.83). ER skills were measured with the 27-item Emotion Regulation Skills Questionnaire (ERSQ; Grant et al., 2018), with Cronbach's α for IC t0 = 0.92, t2 = 0.92, t3 = 0.92, and for CC at t0 = 0.93, t2 = 0.91, t3 = 0.92).

Secondary outcomes

Secondary outcomes included depressive and anxiety symptoms, resilience level, measured at baseline and after 12 weeks, and ROOM engagement and uptake levels, monitored automatically or assessed at weeks 8 and 12. Depressive symptoms were assessed with the 9-item PHQ9 (Kroenke et al., 2001; Cronbach's α - IC: t0 = 0.76, t3 = 0.82; and α CC: t0 = 0.77, t3 = 0.77). Anxiety symptoms were measured with the 7-item General Anxiety Disorder Questionnaire (GAD7; Spitzer et al., 2006; α - IC: t0 = 0.87, t3 = 0.85; α CC: t0 = 0.82, t3 = 0.87). Resilience was assessed using the 10-item Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale (CDRISC; Campbell-Sills and Stein, 2007; α - IC: t0 = 0.84, t3 = 0.83; α CC at t0 = 0.81, t3 = 0.81).

Intervention uptake was measured through log data (i.e., exercise, questionnaire, and mood-monitoring completion rates and usage duration) and subjective user experience. Participants rated the likability and helpfulness of app features (e.g., exercises, self-assessment module, recommender system, transfer elements, collectable mechanic) and fit of the exercise recommendations with their momentary affect, all on 1 (not at all) to 100 (very much) scales. Exercises were rated in-app upon completion; other app features at weeks 8 and 12. Participants also completed a self-developed questionnaire at weeks 8 and 12, asking how often they spontaneously applied techniques learnt in the app (e.g., self-compassion, breathing, savouring, being mindful) over the past month rated on a scale from 0 (not at all) to 4 (daily).

Participants completed the 26-item User Experience Questionnaire (UEQ; Hinderks et al., 2019) at weeks 8 and 12 assessing six dimensions: Attractiveness, Perspicuity, Efficiency, Dependability, Stimulation, and Novelty. The UEQ results were compared to the scale benchmark, with Cronbach's α values for different dimensions ranging between 0.60 and α = 0.83, consistent with previous studies (Schrepp et al., 2017).

At week 12, participants also rated their likelihood of continued use of the app post-study and recommending it to their peers, both on a scale from 1 (definitely not) to 5 (definitely).

For an overview of the assessment schedule see Laure et al., 2024a.

Statistical analyses

All analyses were conducted in RStudio (version 3.0.1). Missing data analysis indicated that 10% of data was missing across primary and secondary outcomes (i.e., PSS10, PHQ9, GAD7, ERSQ, and CDRISC). Logistic regressions across all assessment time points (t1, t2, t3), including baseline outcome values, demographic variables, and group (intervention, control) showed that missing data was conditional on participants' ER skills and study level, suggesting the data was missing at random (MAR). Missing data was handled using multivariate imputation by chained equation (MICE) in RStudio. We generated 10 imputed datasets using the participants' baseline characteristics as predictors.

To evaluate ROOM's effectiveness, analyses followed the intent-to-treat (ITT) approach and evaluated average treatment effects of the intervention. Furthermore, to ensure that follow-up differences reflected real changes rather than baseline variation, baseline values were subtracted from the follow-up scores. Subsequently, assumptions for mixed ANOVA were evaluated for primary (ERSQ, PSS10) and secondary (GAD7, PHQ9, CDRISC) outcomes (see Appendix 1). Normality of residuals was met for ERSQ and CDRISC but not for PSS10, PHQ9, and GAD7. Homogeneity of variances was satisfied for all outcomes. Sphericity was satisfied for PSS10. Due to data complexity analyses varied by outcome: for PSS10 a Generalized Estimating Equations was used (GEE; R package: Geepack) and for the ERSQ we used linear mixed effect model (LME; R package: lmer). In both types of primary analyses predictors included Group (Intervention vs. Control), Time, and their interaction (Group x Time). Kruskal-Wallis test, a non-parametric alternative to ANOVA, assessed the differences in PHQ9 and GAD7 scores changes between the groups. CRISC was analysed with a linear LME. All the analyses were conducted separately on each of the 10 imputed datasets and the results were pooled across datasets (White et al., 2011).

Engagement patterns (i.e., usage and ratings of intervention components) were analysed using descriptive statistics. UEQ scores were analysed with the official analysis tool (https://www.ueq-online.org/). Frequency distribution described skill application and likelihood to use/recommend the app. The latter were subsequently categorized into three categories - Unlikely (‘Definitely not’, ‘Probably not’), Likely (‘Probably yes’, ‘Definitely yes’), middle response was classified as ‘Unsure’. Due to the outliers concerning both the usage duration and ratings of the ROOM components we report Mdn and IQR to provide a more accurate representation of the data. For details refer to Appendix 2.

Exploratory analyses

To explore associations between engagement levels and study outcomes, we performed exploratory subgroup analyses. IC participants were categorized into three engagement levels (Low, Moderate, and High) based on the 33rd and 66th percentiles of completed therapeutic elements in ROOM (mood assessments, self-assessment questionnaires, exercises) (i.e., post-randomization). High, moderate, and low engagement groups completed a median of 29, 14, and 3.5 therapeutic elements (IQR = 16.8, 5, 3) respectively. CC participants were used as the reference category.

To address the normality violations, GEE and General Linear Models (GLM) were used to analyse PSS-10, PHQ-9, and GAD-7 outcomes, while LME and linear regression were applied to ERSQ and CDRISC. A GEE model with exchangeable correlation structure examined associations between engagement levels and PSS changes over weeks 3, 8 and 12. LME models evaluated whether changes in emotion regulation skills differed according to engagement level. GLM analysed associations between engagement and changes in PHQ-9 and GAD, while linear regression assessed the association between engagement levels and CDRISC scores. All analyses were performed separately on 10 imputed datasets, with results subsequently pooled across imputations (White et al., 2011). To account for multiple comparisons, a False Discovery Rate (FDR) correction (Benjamini–Hochberg procedure) was applied (Benjamini and Hochberg, 1995; Storey, 2002; details are provided in Appendix 4).

Results

Study retention and sample characteristics

819 university students signed up for the study, of whom 202 (24.7%) enrolled in the study. Of these, 171 (85%) completed the study, indicating high retention (see Fig. 1). Participants' mean age was 21.5 (SD = 2.93) with 148 (73.3%) bachelor students. Nearly half of the participants were from the School of Social Sciences (n = 98, 48,5%) and the majority were women (162, 80.2%). Intervention effects on primary outcomes

GEE results for PSS-10 showed no main effect of condition (b = −0.38, SE = 0.29, z = −1.31, p = .19, 95% CI [−0.95, 0.19]), time (b = 0.06, SE = 0.19, z = 0.31, p = .76, 95% CI [−0.31, 0.43]), or their interaction (b = 0.14, SE = 0.29, z = 0.50, p = .61, 95% CI [−0.43, 0.71]), indicating no significant differences in stress levels over time between groups. The mixed-ANOVA for ERSQ showed no main effect of condition (b = 0.12, SE = 0.20, t(estimated) = 0.58, p = .56, 95% CI [−0.27, 0.51]), time (b = 0.04, SE = 0.05, t(estimated) = 0.76, p = .45, 95% CI [−0.06, 0.14]), and interaction between them (b = −0.01, SE = 0.08, t(estimated) = −0.14, p = .89, 95% CI [−0.17, 0.15]) indicating no significant differences in ER between groups over time.

Intervention effects on secondary outcomes

Kruskal-Wallis test showed no significant group differences in PHQ9 (χ2(1, N = 202) = 3.61, p = .06) or GAD7 (χ2(1, N = 202) = 0.34, p = .68). LME analysis of resilience (CDRISC) revealed no significant intervention effect of the intervention at t3 (b = 1.23, SE = 0.65, t(estimated) = 1.88, p = .09, 95% CI [−0.04, 2.50]).

ROOM uptake and user engagement

General engagement patterns with ROOM to grow

Of 101 IC participants, 86 (85.15%) registered and used the app. Among them, 74 (86.05%) committed to the 21-day challenge, 73 (84.88%) enabled the reminder. Median engagement duration was 7 days (IQR = 10.25), with users re-engaging with the app every 3 days (IQR = 3.62) and using it for a median of 20.2 min (IQR = 6.76) per active day. Retention rates were promising: 76.7% of participants used the app beyond 15 days from their initial engagement, 58% were still active after 30 days.

Objective engagement

Usage patterns with ROOM's therapeutic features

Over three months, participants completed 666 ER exercises (Mdn = 6, IQR = 7.25 exercises per participant; usage duration: Mdn = 3.7 min, IQR = 3.85). Breathing and Relaxation was the most frequently used category, while Positive Psychology was least utilized. Cognitive Defusion and Restructuring were most often left incomplete. See Appendix 2 for details. Participants also completed 745 mood check-ins (Mdn = 6 times, IQR = 9.25 per participant; duration: Mdn = 0.32 min, IQR = 0.15) and 212 questionnaires (Mdn = 1 time, IQR = 3.25 per participant; duration: Mdn = 0.90 min, IQR = 1.08).

As shown in Fig. 2, use of ER exercises and mood assessments steeply declined after the first two weeks, with ER exercises remaining the most used feature. Usage patterns with ROOM engagement features

Transfer elements, designed to help users apply learnt skills in real-life, were used 176 times (Mdn = 2 times per participant, IQR = 3) with only six participants logging exercises completed outside the app.

Inspection of the recommender system revealed a technical bug, causing the model to reset during every recommendation page visit and rely only on the initialization weights based on the chosen goals during the onboarding, instead of updating with user's app history (e.g., exercises completed) and exercise ratings. Despite that, participants followed recommendations 696 times with a median of 4.5 times (IQR = 8.25) per participant.

For the collectibles gamification feature, users unlocked 528 objects (Mdn = 5, IQR = 6 per participant) linked to the exercises and placed or changed the objects' position in the ‘Virtual ROOM’ 742 times (Mdn = 6.5, IQR = 9.25 per participant).

Use of techniques outside of app

Participants (n = 83) reported how often they spontaneously applied ER techniques in daily life at weeks 8 and 12. Most techniques were applied a median of 2 times (IQR = 2) over three weeks with no notable changes between the two assessment points (see Appendix 2, Table A2.1).

User Experience Questionnaire (UEQ)

The UEQ results at weeks 8 and 12 indicated overall positive experiences with ROOM. Improvements from weeks 8 to 12 were observed across several subscales - Attractiveness, Efficiency, and Dependability - with most scores falling within the ‘Good’ or ‘Above Average’ categories. For detailed statistics, see Appendix 3.

Future use and recommendations

When asked about continued use of ROOM after the study, 19 (23%) participants stated they likely would not use it, 45 (56%) might, and 17 (21%) likely would. Regarding recommending ROOM to their peers, 10 (12%) participants said they likely would not, 44 (54%) might, and 27 (33%) would likely recommend it.

Exploratory analyses

Exploratory analyses indicated several associations between different levels of intervention usage and mental health outcomes, with the strongest one being a reduction in stress among highly engaged participants. Following correction for multiple comparisons, only the association between high usage and decreased stress remained statistically significant. For detailed results, see Appendix 4.

Discussion

This two-arms superiority RCT evaluated the effectiveness and uptake of ROOM to grow, a transdiagnostic mobile intervention, in a sample of distressed university students. The primary goal was evaluating the intervention impact on students' stress levels and ER skills. Secondary goals focused on the evaluation of the intervention impact on depressive symptoms, anxiety symptoms, and resilience as well as university students' engagement and perception of ROOM. Lastly, exploratory analyses were conducted to investigate whether the engagement levels are associated with changes in primary and secondary mental health outcomes.

Contrary to the expectations, ROOM had no significant effect on any of its main targets. These results diverge from systematic reviews and meta-analyses indicating that digital mental health interventions reduce distress among university students (Harith et al., 2022; LaMontagne et al., 2024; Oliveira et al., 2021). The interventions showing strong effects and good adherence typically include human support, and target individuals with more serious mental health conditions (Lehtimaki et al., 2021). Currently, the evidence for transdiagnostic interventions in university students is limited and mixed. Recent RCTs report no effects on students' depressive and anxiety symptoms (Farrer et al., 2024; Karyotaki et al., 2022), whereas older studies found that guided, transdiagnostic interventions can lead to improvements in these symptoms (Day et al., 2013; Mullin et al., 2015). Since digital interventions work best among populations with specific mental health needs and with human support (Lehtimaki et al., 2021), ROOM may be more effective as a targeted preventive intervention, ideally integrated into counselling services at or outside university to provide students with more guided support facilitating development of adaptive ER strategies. Such integration could boost engagement with the app and enhance its benefits for those who need it the most. Another explanation may be that ROOM lacks engagement mechanisms that promote the use required for its therapeutic potential to be realized. Moreover, this study sample had relatively high ER skills at baseline (mean IC = 2.73 and CC = 2.52 out of 4), thus substantial improvement over the three-month period may not have been feasible. Lastly, our participants were compensated for assessment completion, which may have influenced their engagement beyond genuine interest in working on their mental health and could partially explain the lack of intervention effects. Future research should consider reducing or eliminating compensation so that participation reflects students' intrinsic motivation and provides a clearer picture of ROOM's effectiveness.

Engagement with ROOM: in app and beyond the screen

During the 3-month trial, participants were active in the app for about seven days, typically with gaps of three days or less between active days. On active days, they spent around 20 min in ROOM – similar to users' behaviour in mindfulness and mediation apps (Baumel et al., 2019). Engagement with ROOM peaked in the first week and declined sharply by week 4, stabilizing at lower levels over the following weeks - a trend common in digital health interventions (Baumel et al., 2019). Whereas typical mental health apps retain about 4% of users after 15 days and 3% after 30 (Baumel et al., 2019), ROOM retained 74% and 58% at those points, respectively. The sustained engagement level over time may be partly attributed to the study design, which involved reminder e-mails and calls to prompt follow-up survey completion. Additionally, it may also be that the engagement strategies like the 21-day challenge with daily prompts, a 3-day inactivity reminder, and a collectibles gamification element (users collecting objects and placing them into their virtual room) contributed to this outcome. For instance, research indicates that apps with more features focused on user interaction with the digital tool tend to achieve better in-app engagement (Wu et al., 2021).

Simultaneously, ROOM was not designed for long-term use, but instead focuses on macro-engagement (Yardley et al., 2016), using transfer-elements to encourage users to learn and apply the skills taught in the app in daily life. Therefore, the decline in use of ROOM was expected. However, students applied the learned techniques in daily life less often than expected, suggesting limited engagement with the content beyond the screen. Transfer elements were also used infrequently, and some (e.g., implementation intentions and associative cues) received mixed feedback, indicating areas for design improvement. Strengthening these components – or developing alternative strategies – may be necessary to better support real-life skill application and enhance engagement beyond the app.

Participants reported an overall positive user experience with ROOM with most aspects rated ‘Good’ by week 8 and all reaching ‘Good’ or ‘Above average’ by week 12 according to UEQ benchmark comparison (Schrepp et al., 2017). This indicates that ROOM performed well relative to established norms for digital products. Users perceived ROOM as visually appealing, innovative, easy to use, and motivating.

ROOM's exercises were also well received. Notably, the Breathing and Relaxation category received the highest ratings and was the most frequently used, suggesting a strong preference for this exercise type. In terms of exercises targeting unhelpful thinking patterns, Cognitive Restructuring was well-liked and considered helpful. Conversely, Cognitive Defusion received the lowest ratings for both helpfulness and likability, suggesting a lower acceptance among users, similar to previous studies (i.e., Laure et al., 2024b). Both Cognitive Defusion and Cognitive Restructuring exercise categories had the highest incompletion rates. This trend is unsurprising as these exercises require more cognitive effort and practice to understand and master, and users generally tend to avoid challenging cognitive tasks (Otto and Daw, 2019). Mindfulness, Self-Compassion, and Positive Psychology received favourable ratings. Surprisingly, the Positive Psychology category was the least used among all the intervention categories and assessed lower in likeability and helpfulness compared to previous findings (Laure et al., 2024b), indicating that this sample was less drawn to this intervention type. This variation may reflect differences in preferences and needs (e.g., stress severity) between the study samples.

Furthermore, the use of self-assessment questionnaires targeting self-awareness was lower compared to other therapeutic elements in the app (i.e., mood monitoring and ER exercises). This may reflect limited clarity during onboarding about how this module benefits users or a tendency among users to only engage with the questionnaires they perceived as interesting and relevant to them. Although the module received a general positive rating, substantial variability in the ratings and modest engagement suggests mixed attitudes towards this feature. Further refinement of this module is needed, including clearer communication of its relevance to students and possibly topics that better capture their interest.

Lastly, tailoring and personalization are highly valued among this generation of students (Laure et al., 2024b,c) as they help match interventions to users' needs, and foster a sense of being understood and supported, forming a digital therapeutic alliance (D'Alfonso et al., 2020) that may enhance tool's effectiveness. In this study, the recommendation system did not function as planned. While it provided rule-based tailoring (i.e., the exercises were recommended based on users' selected goals during the onboarding), it did not adapt to users' ongoing input (e.g., moods, usage patterns, and ratings). As a result, a key personalization feature did not function as intended during the trial, which may have limited user engagement and ROOM's overall effectiveness. Participants still perceived the system as useful but reported only moderate alignment with their needs, likely reflecting the system's limited adaptivity. The findings suggest that even a non-adaptive recommendation system may be valuable by reducing cognitive load and helping users navigate a broad set of content, consistent with prior research indicating that recommender systems reduce information overload and support decision-making (Valentine et al., 2023). Nevertheless, more advanced personalization is necessary to optimize relevance and effectiveness. ROOM's recommendation system will be adjusted in future versions to enhance its ability to tailor recommendations to individual users over time.

Intent for future use and peer recommendations of ROOM to grow

Despite generally favourable evaluations of ROOM and it's features, intention for future use and peer recommendations were relatively low. Only 21% of participants planned to continue using the app after the study, while 33% would recommend it to friends. Conversely, 23% stated they would not use it anymore, and 12% were unlikely to recommend it. The discrepancy between the positive feature ratings and the low intent for continued use or peer recommendations warrants attention. Users may have felt they got what they needed from the app during the 3-month access period, which aligns with the fact that ROOM was not designed for sustained engagement (Laure et al., 2025; Villegas Meija et al., 2024). However, the hesitance to recommend ROOM to their peers is concerning. Identifying factors that deter peer-recommendations may be important to overcome the »valley of death« phenomenon common in digital innovations (Ellwood et al., 2022), especially given this generation's reliance on personal recommendations (Laure et al., 2025).

Associations between engagement with ROOM and study outcomes

Because engagement plays a role in the effectiveness of digital interventions (Gan et al., 2021), we explored whether the extent of use of ROOM's therapeutic elements was associated with the intervention outcomes. Results indicated an association between high engagement with ROOM and greater reductions in stress levels relative to the control group. No other associations in relation to engagement metrics and mental health outcomes survived the correction for multiple comparisons. This result raises the question about why certain groups of students engage more with this intervention than others (e.g., were they more motivated to work on their wellbeing, did they experience more distress than others, were they more comfortable with digital interventions). The scope of this project was primarily to examine therapeutic outcomes of ROOM to grow, however, future studies should pay more attention to user engagement with the app and the reasons behind it.

Strengths, limitations and future recommendations

This study has several strengths. It included a relatively large sample and demonstrated low attrition (i.e., 15%), notably lower than typical rates in digital behavioural health studies (>40%; Torous et al., 2020). It also provides detailed insights into user engagement by combining usage data with subjective ratings, providing a clearer understanding of user behaviours and factors that may explain the intervention's limited impact. Although there were no improvements in the primary and secondary mental health outcomes, the results, the results identify areas of the app that require refinement and raise questions about the mechanisms behind different engagement patterns among users, which may potentially underlie the intervention's impact.

The study also has limitations. The wait-list control group enables comparison with no intervention but also prevents participant blinding, introducing expectation effects that may bias outcomes. Additionally, knowing they will later receive the intervention can delay behaviour change or help-seeking, potentially inflating or deflating the intervention's apparent effectiveness (Laure et al., 2024a). Another significant limitation is the predominance of women participants, limiting the generalizability of the results. The underrepresentation of men and non-binary genders, also seen in previous studies (Laure et al., 2024b, Laure et al., 2025) reflects the broader trend of women being more likely to engage with mental health support (Auerbach et al., 2018). It is important to find ways to engage a more diverse user group and assess whether different gender identities require different tools to support their wellbeing. Another limitation is the use of monetary incentives, which may have attracted people more motivated by compensation than interest to improve their mental health. Lastly, this study relied solely on quantitative outcomes. Qualitative interviews and additional measures beyond self-reports (e.g., psychophysiological measurements) could better capture how the app affects user's mental health as well as the areas for improvements in the tool.

Conclusion

ROOM to grow did not demonstrate significant short-term effects on stress, emotion regulation or other mental health outcomes at the population level, despite positive user experiences. Higher usage was associated with greater reductions in stress, suggesting potential benefits under sufficient engagement. However, in the overall sample, use declined over time and the application of skills in daily life remained limited. Together with the relatively high baseline emotion regulation skills of the sample, these factors likely constrained the intervention's impact. Future research should focus on understanding which students engage with the intervention and why, as well as strengthening sustained engagement, real-life skill transfer, and evaluating its potential as a preventive tool in larger and more representative samples.
