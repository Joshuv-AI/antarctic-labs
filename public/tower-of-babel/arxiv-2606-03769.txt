Bregman Meets Lévy: Stochastic Mirror Descent with Heavy-Tailed Noise in Continuous and Discrete Time
Pierre-Louis Cauvin c,∗, Panayotis Mertikopoulos ∗

Abstract
. We study the robustness of SMD ( SMD ) under heavy-tailed noise, focusing on whether the method retains its convergence guarantees when run with infinite-variance stochastic gradient input. To address this question in a principled manner, we begin by introducing a continuous-time model of SMD as a SDE ( SDE ) driven by a centered Lévy noise process with finite p p -th order moments, 1 < p ≤ 2 1<p\leq 2 . This scheme—which we call the LMF ( LMF )—arises naturally as the scaling limit of SMD in the presence of heavy-tailed noise. In particular, when p < 2 p<2 —the heavy noise regime—the trajectories of LMF ( LMF ) generically exhibit jump discontinuities of arbitrary magnitude which, if frequent enough, lead to infinite variance. Nonetheless, despite this highly singular behavior, we show that LMF attains ε \varepsilon -optimality within 𝒪 ( ε − p / ( p − 1 ) ) \bigoh(\varepsilon^{-p/(p-1)}) time in the convex case, and within 𝒪 ~ ( ε − 1 / ( p − 1 ) ) \tildeoh(\varepsilon^{-1/(p-1)}) time for (relatively) strongly convex objectives. These guarantees provide a transparent characterization of the impact of frequent long jumps on the convergence of the process, and percolate to a series of matching discrete-time guarantees for several variants of SMD under heavy-tailed noise.

Background and motivation.

SMD
(
SMD
) and its variants comprise one of the most widely studied classes of first-order methods in stochastic optimization.
Originally conceived by
78
and
77
as a general scheme for solving constrained convex minimization problems,
SMD
has since grown into a mature algorithmic template with applications in min-max problems and variational inequalities
[

,
80

, online optimization and bandits
[
7
,

,

,

]
,

[

,
24
,
42
]
,

[

,
26
]
,

[

,

,

At a high level,
MD
methods proceed by
replacing Euclidean projections with a non-Euclidean “prox-type” step—or, dually to this, by aggregating gradient steps in an unconstrained, dual space, and performing a “mirror step” at each iteration to generate a new query point and obtain a new gradient sample.
This mechanism links the geometry of the underlying problem with the prox-step defining the method and which, if chosen appropriately, can lead to superior, almost-dimension-free convergence guarantees.

A major challenge in this setting occurs when the gradient samples that are fed into the algorithm have infinite variance, thus jeopardizing the method’s convergence—which usually presumes tame, light-tailed noise.
This is a particularly pernicious issue as, even in
1
-dimensional quadratic problems, running “vanilla”
SGD
(
SGD
) with heavy-tailed noise could lead to divergence in cases where the method is otherwise convergent
[

,
32
,
116

Due to the massive—and massively complex—structure of deep neural nets, this “heavy noise” regime is ubiquitous in contemporary machine learning models and architectures, cf.

,

,

,

Especially in the context of deep learning, the heavy-tailed behavior of gradient noise was first highlighted by

Even though these findings were later challenged by

[

]
, deep convolutional architectures
[

,

, large language models
[

[

Several theoretical works further attribute these phenomena to multiplicative noise effects leading to the convergence of the iterates toward a heavy-tailed stationary distribution

]

Our contributions in the context of related work.

Our aim in this paper is precisely this:

to examine the robustness of

Building on a long-standing trend in optimization, we begin with a continuous-time viewpoint and we introduce the

(

In contrast to existing

,

If the

are inherently “diffusive”:

(
i.5pt
)

(
ii.5pt

and

(

By contrast, under Lévy noise,

(

(

(
iii.5pt
)

A key technical contribution of our paper—which, to the best of our knowledge, is new in the stochastic analysis literature—is a
weak Itô formula
for convex functions that are not

(so the standard Itô formula does not apply).

Thanks to this new technical tool, we analyze several variants of the method, with both constant and variable learning rate, and we establish the following series of results:

(1)

In convex problems, time-averaged

\varepsilon
-approximate solution within
\tildeoh\big(\varepsilon^{-p/(p-1)}\big)

(2)

In strongly convex problems,

\delta

\bigoh(\delta_{\eta}^{2}/\delta^{2})

(3)

If the objective function is strongly convex relative to the method’s Bregman function,

\delta

\tildeoh(\delta^{-2p/(p-1)})

Subsequently, we turn to the bona fide, discrete-time setting, and we study different variants of
SMD

under heavy-tailed noise.
To connect with existing results, we first establish an
\tildeoh\big(\varepsilon^{-p/(p-1)}\big)

77
and
107

under a “uniform smoothness” assumption.
We then specialize to functions that are also strongly convex relative to the method’s generating Bregman function:
here, we establish the convergence of the method’s trajectories in mean square, and as in the continuous-time setting, we show that

(
i.5pt
)

the algorithm’s orbits converge at a geometric rate to an “uncertainty ball” whose size scales with the intensity of the noise;
and

(
ii.5pt
)

the algorithm attains an
\varepsilon

This is similar to the convergence rate estimates obtained by

Importantly, our discrete-time results mirror our continuous-time findings, both qualitatively and quantitatively.
In fact, in all cases, the discrete-time bounds decompose into a term stemming from the continuous-time analysis, plus a “discretization” term.
This indicates that the proposed Lévy model is a faithful proxy for studying
SMD
in the heavy-noise regime, thus opening up an important direction for further research in the heavy-noise regime.

1

There are several points worth discussing at this point.

First, the choice of results to present in this section has not been arbitrary;
our aim was to show the broadest range of phenomena that arise.
Specifically, by the equivalence of (

In

Second, a salient pattern that arises when connecting the corresponding continuous- and discrete-time results

f(x_{1})-\min f

which essentially stems from the “discretization” of (

).
This suggests that (

) is a particularly faithful surrogate for the heavy-noise regime in continuous time—so the study of its properties proves highly beneficial for the discrete-time regime as well.

In terms of rates, the guarantees of

are fairly involved.
First, to provide some context,

in

in the other cases, the step-size

seems to be preferable.

Finally, we see that (

A key difference between our setting and

by contrast, the lower bounds of

Beyond this difference, we conjecture that our rates can be tightened further with a different choice of energy function;
we leave this as an open question for future work.

5.
Concluding remarks

An important take-away of our results is that
SMD
remains remarkably robust in the presence of heavy noise, and maintains its convergence and concentration guarantees despite the very long jumps exhibited by the noise.
A second is that our discrete-time results closely mirror our continuous-time findings, both qualitatively and quantitatively;
this indicates that the proposed Lévy model is itself a particularly faithful proxy for studying
SMD
in the heavy-noise regime, thus opening up several important directions for further research in the heavy-noise regime.
A first such question would be to sharpen

6
under relative strong convexity, where we conjecture that the lower bound of

a second would be to investigate the use of (

) for sampling from constrained spaces in the spirit of
[

,
117
]
.
We leave these questions for the future.

Appendix A
Related works

In this first appendix, we discuss in detail some literature threads which, while relevant, would have otherwise disrupted the flow of our discussion.

Stochastic mirror flows and Mirrored Langevin dynamics.

Stochastic mirror flows
were introduced and studied by
90
as a noisy variant of the continuous-time mirror flow of
77
, with subsequent works refining their convergence properties and extending the framework to state-dependent diffusion terms and variational inequalities
[
71
,
72
]
. Accelerated variants were later proposed, notably by
48
and
112
,
113
, the latter also establishing convergence rates under relative strong convexity. All these works suppose
L
-smoothness of the objective function. Closely related are the
mirrored Langevin dynamics
, which extend Langevin diffusions to non-Euclidean geometries for sampling measures on constrained spaces. The initial formulation, due to
37
, considered a constant diffusion coefficient, while later studies introduced a diffusion term adapted to the geometry via the Hessian of the regularizer
[
117
,
26
,
56
]
, thereby placing mirrored Langevin dynamics as a special case of Riemannian Langevin methods
[
93
]
. Convergence guarantees for convex and relatively strongly convex objectives were also established for these dynamics by
106
under a constant stepsize schedule.

Lévy-driven SDEs in stochastic optimization.

Stochastic differential equations driven by Lévy processes have gained attention in machine learning as models for stochastic optimization under heavy-tailed noise.
A first line of work focuses on sampling algorithms based on Lévy-driven dynamics, most notably the
fractional Langevin Monte Carlo
method introduced by
104
, and further studied by
[
114
,
82
,
103
,
38
,
118
]
, where Brownian motion is replaced by a stable Lévy process.
More general
Lévy Langevin Monte Carlo
methods allowing arbitrary Lévy noise were also recently studied in
85
and
13
.
Beyond sampling, Lévy-driven SDEs have been proposed as continuous-time approximations of stochastic gradient methods, notably to explain metastability and escape phenomena observed in deep learning
[
101
,
83
,
100
,
119
,
10
]
.
Finally, Lévy-driven models have also been used to study generalization properties of SGD, suggesting that heavier-tailed processes should achieve better generalization errors
[
102
,
91
,
92
,
31
,
30
]
.

Heavy-tailed noise in stochastic algorithms.

For
standard SGD
,
116
showed that iterates may fail to converge for any fixed stepsize under heavy-tailed noise, even for strongly convex objectives, indicating that additional structure is generally required.
Convergence can nonetheless be recovered under strong assumptions on the objective, such as a bounded and uniformly
p
-positive definite Hessian
[
108
]
, or by modifying the algorithm.

In the context of
mirror descent
,
77
established convergence for convex objectives with uniformly continuous mirror maps and proved the optimal in-expectation lower bound
\Omega\left(t^{-(p-1)/p}\right)
.
This result was later refined by
107
for uniformly convex regularizers, with improved dimension dependence, and extended to zeroth-order methods in
46
.
For
projected SGD on bounded domains
, recent work by
32
and
60
showed that the optimal convex rates can in fact be achieved without modifying the algorithm; the latter also establishing this property for Dual Averaging and AdaGrad in online convex optimization.

A large body of work instead focuses on
gradient clipping
as a stabilization mechanism under heavy-tailed noise.
This line of research was initiated by
116
, who proved optimal lower bounds in expectation of order
\Omega\left(t^{-2(p-1)/p}\right)
for strongly convex objectives and
\Omega\left(t^{-(p-1)/(3p-2)}\right)
for nonconvex smooth ones, and showed that clipped SGD can match these bounds.
Subsequent works derived high-probability guarantees matching these rates
[
96
]
and extended the analysis to broader settings, including nonconvex
[
28
,
57
,
6
]
, composite
[
34
]
and distributed optimization
[
34
,
115
]
, as well as to more general
(L_{0},L_{1})
-smooth objectives
[
27
]
.

Further refinements include variance-reduced and stabilized variants that can achieve even faster rates under additional structure
[
89
,
59
]
.
The recent work of
109
complements this line of work by providing a large-deviations and metastability analysis of clipped SGD without relying on a Lévy-driven SDE approximation.
Alternatives to clipping have also been proposed to address heavy-tailed noise without truncation.
Notably, normalized gradient methods achieve optimal or near-optimal convergence rates for nonconvex optimization
[
58
,
39
,
105
,
115
]
.

Further comparison with
[
60
]
.

For discrete-time algorithms, the closest work to ours is
[
60
]
, which derives several convergence guarantees for Online Gradient Descent and Dual Averaging on bounded domains under heavy-tailed noise.
Importantly, while the same general strategy is used to handle unbounded variance (cf. the chain of inequalities leading to
[
60
, Eq. 5]
), that work is restricted to standard Euclidean norms, whereas our results hold under general Bregman divergences.
This extension to non-Euclidean norms was in fact already alluded to by
60
, but carrying it out requires substantially more care than what one might initially expect, notably the need to distinguish between
standard
and
relative
strong convexity—the former being norm-agnostic while the latter accommodates the geometry induced by the regularizer.
In particular, when the regularizer is steep, relative strong convexity requires the objective function to have unbounded gradients, making it necessary to work under the refined
relative smoothness
framework of
11
.
By contrast, gradients are assumed bounded throughout
[
60
]
, except for the last-iterate convergence rate of
[
60
, Theorem 10]
and its corollaries, which rely instead on the condition

(A.1)

\frac{\mu}{2}\lVert x-x^{\prime}\rVert^{2}\leq f(x)-f(x^{\prime})-\langle\nabla f(x^{\prime}),x-x^{\prime}\rangle\leq 2G\lVert x-x^{\prime}\rVert+\frac{H}{2}\lVert x-x^{\prime}\rVert^{2}\,,

for some
\mu,G,H\geq 0
such that
G+H>0
, and for all
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
.
One may in fact observe that, in the Euclidean and compact setting of
60
, the above condition effectively reduces to a bounded-gradient assumption, despite being formulated as a nonsmoothness condition.
However, unlike the other results of
[
60
]
, its extension to the relatively smooth / relatively strongly convex setting now becomes rather natural, as it effectively amounts to replacing Euclidean norms by Bregman divergences.
With that said, this condition was only introduced in a revised version of
[
60
]
, posted online concurrently with the initial drafting of the present paper, and whose existence was brought to our attention only later by a reviewer.

A primer on Lévy processes

In this section, we review the necessary background on Lévy processes and stochastic analysis, with an emphasis on the tools required for the convergence analysis developed in the main text. The material presented here is largely standard, and we refer the interested reader to the monographs of
5
,
15
and
98
for comprehensive introductions to the subject. Throughout this section, we assume a filtered probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},(\mathcal{F}(t))_{t\geq 0},\prob)
satisfying the usual conditions
3
3

3

That is, (a) the probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\prob)
is complete, meaning that every subset of a
\prob
-null set is measurable; and (b) the filtration
(\mathcal{F}(t))_{t\geq 0}
is right-continuous, i.e.,
\mathcal{F}(t)=\bigcap_{u>t}\mathcal{F}(u)
.
.

C.1.
Lévy processes

A Lévy process
L=(L(t))_{t\geq 0}
is a continuous-time stochastic process taking values in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and satisfying:

(1)

Initial condition:

L(0)=0
(a.s.)

(2)

Independent increments:
For every
0\leq t_{1}<\dots<t_{k}
, the increments
L(t_{i+1})-L(t_{i})
are independent.

(3)

Stationary increments:
For every
t,s>0
, the distribution of
L\left(t+s\right)-L(t)
is the same as
L(s)
.

(4)

Stochastic continuity:
For every
\varepsilon>0
and
t\geq 0
,
\lim_{s\to 0^{+}}\prob\left(\lVert L(t+s)-L(t)\rVert>\varepsilon\right)=0
.

Under the standard assumptions made on the filtered space, one may always assume that
L
admits càdlàg (i.e., right-continuous with left limits) paths.

Lévy processes encompass many important continuous-time stochastic model; among the most prominent examples are the following:

Example C.1

(Brownian motion)
.

A
Brownian motion

W=(W(t))_{t\geq 0}
is a Lévy process whose increments
W(t+s)-W(t)
are distributed as
\mathcal{N}(0,sI_{n})
. In particular, Brownian motions are the only Lévy processes that admit continuous sample paths, and they admit finite moments of any order.

Example C.2

(Poisson process)
.

A
Poisson process

N=(N(t))_{t\geq 0}
with intensity
\lambda>0
is a nondecreasing, integer-valued Lévy process whose increments
N(t+s)-N(t)
are distributed as
\mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda s)
. In particular, it also admits moments of all orders.

Example C.3

(Compound Poisson process)
.

Let
Z=(Z_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of independent and identically distributed random variables, and let
N
be an independent Poisson process with intensity
\lambda>0
. Then the stochastic process
X(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{N(t)}Z_{i}
is called a
compound Poisson process
. In particular,
X
is a Lévy process. Moreover, a compound Poisson process admits moments of order
p
if and only if the common distribution of the
Z_{i}
admits moments of order
p
.

Example C.4

(Stable processes)
.

A rotationally invariant stable process with stability index
\alpha\in(0,2]
is a Lévy process
L^{\alpha}
such that

(C.1)

\ex\left[e^{i\langle u,L^{\alpha}(t)\rangle}\right]=e^{-\sigma^{\alpha}\lVert u\rVert^{\alpha}t}\quad\text{for all }u\in\mathbb{R}^{n},t\geq 0,

where
\sigma>0
is a scale factor. When
\alpha=2
, this definition recovers the Brownian motion. By contrast, when
\alpha<2
, an
\alpha
-stable process admits finite moments only of order
p<\alpha
, making such processes particularly well suited for modeling infinite-variance phenomena. More general definitions of stable processes without rotational invariance are available in the literature, although their explicit characterization is substantially more involved. We refer the interested reader to the monograph of
97
for further details.

C.2.
Jump processes and Poisson random measures

As illustrated by the previous examples, Lévy processes are typically discontinuous in nature, making it essential to characterize the behavior of their
jumps
(i.e., of their discontinuities) in order to understand their long-term behavior.

Let
\Delta L(t)=L(t)-L(t-)
denote the jump size at time
t
. We define a (random) measure
N(dt,dz)
on
\mathbb{R}^{+}\times(\mathbb{R}^{n}-\{0\})
by counting the jumps:

(C.2)

N([s,t],B)=\#\left\{s<r\leq t:\Delta L(r)\in B\right\}=\sum_{s<r\leq t}\one_{B}(\Delta L(r))

for any Borel set
B\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}-\{0\}
. Thus,
N([s,t],B)
counts the number of jumps of size in
B
occurring in the time interval
[s,t]
. In particular,
(N(t,B))_{t\geq 0}\defeq(N([0,t],B))_{t\geq 0}
is a Poisson process with intensity
\nu(B)t
, where
\nu
is the measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}-\{0\}
defined by

(C.3)

\nu(B)=\ex\left[N(1,B)\right]=\ex\left[\sum_{0<t\leq 1}\one_{B}(\Delta L(t))\right].

This measure is known as the
Lévy measure
associated with
L
, and it also satisfies the integrability condition

(C.4)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}-\{0\}}(\lVert z\rVert^{2}\wedge 1)\nu(dz)<\infty.

An important consequence of this condition is that
\nu
is finite on any Borel set that is bounded away from
0
, which implies that a Lévy process admits only finitely many large jumps on any finite time interval. In contrast,
\nu
may be infinite at
0
, allowing for infinitely many jumps of small size. Another useful relation links the moments of
L
to those of
\nu

[
98
, Theorem 25.3]
:

(C.5)

\ex\left[\lVert L(t)\rVert^{p}\right]<\infty\text{ for all }t\geq 0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\int_{\lVert z\rVert\geq c}\lVert z\rVert^{p}\nu(dz)<\infty\text{ for all }c>0.

This result illustrates that the tail behavior of a Lévy process—namely, whether it is heavy-tailed or light-tailed—is entirely determined by the contribution of large jumps, and not by the small jumps nor by the continuous component.

Example C.5

(Brownian motion, cont’d)
.

A Brownian motion
W
has continuous sample paths and therefore admits no jumps; consequently, its Lévy measure is identically zero.

Example C.6

(Poisson process, cont’d)
.

All jumps of a Poisson process with intensity
\lambda
are of size
1
, and the expected number of jumps occurring over the unit time interval
[0,1]
is
\lambda
. Hence, its Lévy measure is given by
\nu=\lambda\delta_{1}
, where
\delta_{1}
denotes the Dirac measure at
1
.

Example C.7

(Compound Poisson process, cont’d)
.

If
X
is a compound Poisson process generated by a sequence of independent random variables with common distribution
\mu_{Z}
, then each jump of
X
is conditionally distributed according to
\mu_{Z}
, and the expected number of jumps up to time
1
is
\lambda
. As a result, the associated Lévy measure is
\nu=\lambda\mu_{Z}
.

Example C.8

(Stable processes, cont’d)
.

If
L^{\alpha}
is a rotationally invariant
\alpha
-stable process, then its Lévy measure takes the form
\nu(dz)=C/\lVert z\rVert^{n+\alpha}
for some scaling constant
C>0
. In contrast to compound Poisson processes, this Lévy measure explodes at
0
, implying that stable processes exhibit infinitely many jumps of small magnitude.

The random measure
N(dt,dz)
is commonly referred to as a
Poisson random measure
on
\mathbb{R}^{+}\times(\mathbb{R}^{n}-\{0\})
with intensity
\nu(dz)dt
4
4

4

In this section we have introduced Poisson random measures only through the specific construction arising from Lévy processes. More generally, Poisson random measures can be defined independently of Lévy processes and in much greater generality. We refer to
[
5
]
and

for more comprehensive treatments of this topic.
. Associated to

(C.6)

\tilde{N}(dt,dz)=N(dt,dz)-\nu(dz)dt.

In contrast to

, the compensated measure enjoys the property that
(\tilde{N}\left(t,B\right))_{t\geq 0}

. This property makes
\tilde{N}(dt,dz)

C.3.
Stochastic integration for jump processes

To define stochastic integration of a stochastic process
(h(t,z))_{t\geq 0,z\in B}
with respect to the measures

and
\tilde{N}(dt,dz)

predictable
, meaning that

is determined by
\mathcal{F}(t-)
5
5

5

Rigorously, a stochastic process
(h(t))_{t\geq 0}
is said to be
predictable
if it is measurable with respect to the smallest
\sigma

(\psi(t))_{t\geq 0}
. In particular, this condition is satisfied whenever the mapping
t\mapsto h(t,z)
is
left-continuous
. We then introduce the following class of stochastic processes:

(C.7)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\nu}^{#1}(T,B;\mathbb{R}^{n})=\bigg\{h\colon[0,T]\times B\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{n}:

\displaystyle h(\cdot,z)\text{ is }\text{predictable for all }z\in B

\displaystyle\text{and }\ex\left[\int_{0}^{T}\int_{B}\lVert h(s,z)\rVert^{p}\nu(dz)ds\right]<\infty\bigg\}

for
p\in[1,2]
,
T\geq 0
, and any Borel set
B\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}-\{0\}
.

(1)

Integration with respect to
N(dt,dz)
:
For integration against the raw measure
N(dt,dz)
, the integral is a sum over the jumps of the process. Specifically, if
h(\cdot,z)
is a predictable process, then

(C.8)

\int_{0}^{t}\int_{B}h(s,z)N(ds,dz)=\sum_{0<s\leq t}h(s,\Delta L(s))\one_{B}(\Delta L(s))\quad\text{for every }t\leq T.

(2)

Integration with respect to
\tilde{N}(dt,dz)

There are two standard approaches to defining stochastic integrals with respect to the compensated measure
\tilde{N}(dt,dz)

to get

\displaystyle M(t)

\displaystyle\defeq\int_{0}^{t}\int_{B}h(s,z)\tilde{N}(ds,dz)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{B}h(s,z)N(ds,dz)-\int_{0}^{t}\int_{B}h(s,z)\nu(dz)ds\quad\text{for every }t\leq T.

This construction results to the process

Alternatively, if

(C.10)

\ex\left[\left\lVert\int_{0}^{t}\int_{B}h(s,z)\tilde{N}(ds,dz)\right\rVert^{2}\right]=\ex\left[\int_{0}^{t}\int_{B}\lVert h(s,z)\rVert^{2}\nu(dz)ds\right].

In this case,

Another class of stochastic processes that will be useful in the remainder of the paper is

\textstyle 2o2f[of{T}{\mathbb{R}^{n}}=\left\{g\colon[0,T]\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{n}:\text{$g$ is predictable and }\ex\left[\int_{0}^{t}\lVert g(s)\rVert^{2}\kern 0.0pt\>ds\right]<\infty\right\},\quad T\geq 0.

\ex\left[\left\lVert\int_{0}^{t}g(s)^{\top}dW(s)\right\rVert^{2}\right]=\ex\left[\int_{0}^{t}\lVert g(s)\rVert^{2}ds\right].

\tilde{N}(dt,dz)

C.4.
The Lévy-Itô decomposition

Any Lévy process

can be uniquely represented as a sum of a continuous part and independent jump components

(C.13)

\displaystyle L\left(t\right)=\underbrace{b_{c}t+\Sigma W\left(t\right)}_{\text{Continuous part}}+\underbrace{\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\lVert z\rVert<c}z\tilde{N}(ds,dz)}_{\text{Compensated small jumps}}+\underbrace{\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\lVert z\rVert\geq c}zN(ds,dz)}_{\text{Large jumps}},

where
b_{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a deterministic drift,
W
is a

is a deterministic diffusion matrix. The constant

acts as a cutoff separating between bounded and unbounded jumps, and may be chosen arbitrarily. For example, if the jumps of

are uniformly bounded by some

It is important to note that the small jumps must be integrated against the compensated measure
\tilde{N}(dt,dz)
, rather than the raw measure

, to ensure that the stochastic integral is well defined even when
\int_{\lVert z\rVert<c}\lVert z\rVert\nu(dz)=\infty

In particular, if

\displaystyle L(t)

\displaystyle=-\left(\int_{\lVert z\rVert\geq c}z\nu(dz)\right)t+\Sigma W\left(t\right)+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\lVert z\rVert<c}z\tilde{N}(ds,dz)+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\lVert z\rVert\geq c}zN(ds,dz)

\displaystyle=\Sigma W\left(t\right)+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\lVert z\rVert<c}z\tilde{N}(ds,dz)+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\lVert z\rVert\geq c}z\tilde{N}(ds,dz)

(C.14)

\displaystyle=M(t)+#1(t)+#1(t)

where

is a continuous square-integrable martingale,

With this decomposition in mind, the intensity of each component of

(
a.5pt
)

Diffusion intensity:

(C.15)

\sigma_{0}^{2}=\ex\left[\lVert M(1)\rVert^{2}\right]=\lVert\Sigma\rVert_{F}^{2}=\tr[\Sigma^{\top}\Sigma].

(

C.5.
Asymptotic behavior of discontinuous martingales

In this subsection, we present two classical results from stochastic analysis—accordingly the
law of large numbers
and the

We begin with a strong law of large numbers for stochastic integrals with respect to compensated Poisson random measures. Throughout, we let
N(dt,dz)
denote the random Poisson measure associated with a Lévy process

having Lévy measure

Lemma C.1

(Strong law of large numbers)
.

Fix some index
p\in[1,2]
and let

with

for all

. If there is a predictable increasing process

\lim A(t)=\infty

(
a.s.
)
and

(C.18)

\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{B}\dfrac{\lVert H(s,z)\rVert^{2}\wedge\lVert H(s,z)\rVert^{p}}{\left[1+A(s)\right]^{p}}\nu(dz)ds<\infty\quad\textup{(}a.s.\textup{)},

then
M(t)/A(t)\to 0

(
a.s.
)
.

Proof.

This is a consequence of
[
54
, Lemma 3]
. Indeed,
M
is a centered purely discontinuous martingale since

and, following the notations from
[
54
]
, we can introduce the auxiliary process
W_{p}
given by

(C.19)

W_{p}(t)=\sum_{0<s\leq t}\lVert\Delta M(s)\rVert^{2}\wedge\lVert\Delta M(s)\rVert^{p}=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{B}\lVert H(s,z)\rVert^{2}\wedge\lVert H(s,z)\rVert^{p}N(ds,dz).

In particular, its integrand satisfies

(C.20)

\ex\left[\int_{0}^{t}\int_{B}\lVert H(s,z)\rVert^{2}\wedge\lVert H(s,z)\rVert^{p}\nu(dz)ds\right]\leq\ex\left[\int_{0}^{t}\int_{B}\lVert H(s,z)\rVert^{p}\nu(dz)ds\right]<\infty

since
H\in\mathcal{H}_{\nu}^{#1}(T,B;\mathbb{R}^{n})
, and thus

(C.21)

\overline{W_{p}}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{B}\lVert H(s,z)\rVert^{2}\wedge\lVert H(s,z)\rVert^{p}\nu(dz)ds

is the compensator of
W_{p}
, i.e., the unique predictable càdlàg process starting from
0
with finite variation over bounded intervals and such that
W_{p}-\overline{W_{p}}
is a (local) martingale. According to
[
54
, Lemma 3]

therefore holds true whenever

(C.22)

\infty>\int_{0}^{\infty}\dfrac{d\overline{W_{p}}(s)}{\left[1+A(s)\right]^{p}}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{B}\dfrac{\lVert H(s,z)\rVert^{2}\wedge\lVert H(s,z)\rVert^{p}}{\left[1+A(s)\right]^{p}}\nu(dz)ds,

which is exactly the condition stated in the lemma.
∎

To state the law of iterated logarithm for discontinuous martingales, we first need to introduce another essential object from stochastic analysis: the
predictable quadratic variation
. For any càdlàg square-integrable local martingale

predictable quadratic variation
(also sometimes known as

\langle M\rangle
such that

is a local martingale. In particular, when

arises as a stochastic integral with respect to either a Brownian motion or a compensated Poisson measure, the following identities hold:

(C.23a)

\displaystyle\left\langle\int_{0}^{t}g(s)^{\top}dW(s)\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\lVert g(s)\rVert^{2}ds,\quad g\in 2o2f[of{T}{\mathbb{R}^{n}};

(C.23b)

\displaystyle\left\langle\int_{0}^{t}\int_{B}h(s,z)\tilde{N}(ds,dz)\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{B}\lVert h(s,z)\rVert^{2}\nu(dz)ds,\quad h\in\mathcal{H}_{\nu}^{#1}(T,B;\mathbb{R}^{n}).

We can now formulate the law of iterated logarithm, which characterizes the asymptotic fluctuations of a martingale in terms of its predictable quadratic variation:

Lemma C.2

(Law of iterated logarithm,
[
55
, Theorem 3]
)
.

Let
M
be a square-integrable local martingale and assume that there exists a constant
C\geq 0
such that
\lvert\Delta M(t)\rvert\leq C

(
a.s.

for every

(C.24)

\limsup_{t\to\infty}\dfrac{\lvert M(t)\rvert}{\sqrt{2\langle M\rangle(t)\log\log\langle M\rangle(t)}}=1\quad\textup{(}a.s.\textup{)}

on the event
\left\{\lim_{t\to\infty}\langle M\rangle(t)=\infty\right\}
.

C.6.
An Itô’s formula for smooth convex function

Analogous to the chain rule in classical differential calculus, Itô’s formula allows one to describe the evolution of a composition
\psi\circ Y
, where
Y
is some
n
-dimensional stochastic process and

—which requires
\psi
to be at least twice continuously differentiable for the formula to hold.

However, in many applications, such as the analysis of stochastic mirror flows, the function

L
-smooth.

Recall that a function

is said to be
L
-smooth
if it satisfies

(C.25)

\lVert\nabla\psi(y)-\nabla\psi(y^{\prime})\rVert\leq L\lVert y-y^{\prime}\rVert_{\ast}\quad\text{for all }y,y^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

In particular,
L
-smooth functions that are also convex enjoy the following property:

Lemma C.3
.

If

L
-smooth convex function, then
\psi
is almost everywhere twice differentiable with Hessian

and

(C.26)

0\leq\nabla^{2}\psi\leq LI\quad\text{Lebesgue-a.e.}

Proof.

The proof of this result is standard, see e.g.,
[
71

.
∎

A weak Itô’s formula for smooth convex functions was previously established by

To simplify notations, throughout this section we identify the dual space
\mathcal{Y}
with
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, which allows us to define Lévy processes, Brownian motions and Poisson random measures in the standard way presented in
Appendix

. In particular,

admits the Lévy-Itô decomposition

(D.1)

L(t)=-\left(\int_{\lVert z\rVert_{\ast}\geq c}\!\!z\nu(dz)\right)t+\Sigma W(t)+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\lVert z\rVert_{\ast}<c}\!\!z\tilde{N}(ds,dz)+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\lVert z\rVert_{\ast}\geq c}\!\!zN(ds,dz)

where

is an arbitrary cutoff constant (cf.

). Under this decomposition, the quantities introduced in the main text to quantify the noise intensity are given by:

(
a.5pt
)

Diffusion intensity:

(D.2)

\sigma_{0}^{2}=\lVert\Sigma\rVert_{F}^{2}=\tr[\Sigma^{\top}\Sigma].

(
b.5pt
)

Short jump intensity:

(D.3)

\sigma_{\textup{short}}^{2}=\int_{\lVert z\rVert<c}\lVert z\rVert^{2}\nu(dz).

(
c.5pt
)

Long jump intensity:

\sigma_{\textup{long}}^{p}=\int_{\lVert z\rVert\geq c}\lVert z\rVert^{p}\nu(dz).

Moreover, to highlight that

correspond to the components of

captures the intensity of the component that may exhibit infinite variance, we also set

(D.5)

\sigma_{\textup{tame}}^{2}=\sigma_{0}^{2}+\sigma_{\textup{short}}^{2}\quad\text{and}\quad\sigma_{\textup{heavy}}^{p}=\sigma_{\textup{long}}^{p}

for the tame and heavy part of the noise respectively.

D.1.
Existence and uniqueness of solutions

We begin this section by addressing an important aspect that was largely left implicit in the main text, namely the well-posedness of the system (
3.1
). Specifically, under which conditions (
3.1
) admits a unique global solution for every initial condition.

In order to maintain a sufficiently general framework—one under which the various assumptions considered in the main text can coexist—we study the existence and uniqueness of solutions to (
3.1
) under the following conditions on the objective function
f
:

Assumption 1
.

\nabla f\circ Q
is locally Lipschitz on
\mathcal{Y}
: for every
r>0
, there exist a nonnegative constant
L_{r}\geq 0
such that

(
\mathcal{H}_{1}
)

\lVert(\nabla f\circ Q)(y)-(\nabla f\circ Q)(y^{\prime})\rVert_{\ast}\leq L_{r}\lVert y-y^{\prime}\rVert_{\ast}\quad\text{for all }y,y^{\prime}\in\mathbb{B}_{r}(0).

Assumption 2
.

\nabla f\circ Q
satisfies a global growth condition on
\mathcal{Y}
: there exists
M>0
such that

(
\mathcal{H}_{2}
)

\lVert(\nabla f\circ Q)(y)\rVert_{\ast}\leq M(1+\lVert y\rVert_{\ast})\quad\text{for all }y\in\mathcal{Y}.

Assumption (

H
1

) guarantees local existence and uniqueness of solutions, while (

H
2

) prevents finite-time explosion and thus ensures global well-posedness. Together, these conditions yield the following result:

Proposition D.1

(Existence and uniqueness of solutions)
.

Under assumptions (

H
1

) and (

H
2

), the stochastic differential equation (
3.1
) admits a unique càdlàg strong solution
X
staying in
\mathcal{X}
at all times. Moreover, if
h
is steep, then
X(t)\in\relint\mathcal{X}

(
a.s.
)
whenever
X(0)\in\relint\mathcal{X}
.

Proof.

Replacing
X(t)
by
Q(\eta(t)Y(t))
, the system (
3.1
) can be written as the stochastic differential equation

(D.6)

dY(t)=-(\nabla f\circ Q)(\eta(t)Y(t))dt+dL(t)

evolving in
\mathcal{Y}
. Let
r>0
and
y,y^{\prime}\in\mathbb{B}_{r}(0)
. For every
t\geq 0
, we have
\eta(t)y,\eta(t)y^{\prime}\in\mathbb{B}_{\eta(t)r}(0)\subseteq\mathbb{B}_{\eta(0)r}(0)
thanks to the fact that
\eta
is a nonincreasing positive function. In particular, the local Lipschitzness (

H
1

) of
\nabla f\circ Q
yields

(D.7)

\lVert(\nabla f\circ Q)(\eta(t)y)-(\nabla f\circ Q)(\eta(t)y^{\prime})\rVert_{\ast}\leq L_{\eta(0)r}\lVert\eta(t)(y-y^{\prime})\rVert_{\ast}\leq\eta(0)L_{\eta(0)r}\lVert y-y^{\prime}\rVert_{\ast}

for all
y,y^{\prime}\in\mathbb{B}_{r}(0)
and every
r>0
. Moreover, we also get

(D.8)

\lVert(\nabla f\circ Q)(\eta(t)y)\rVert_{\ast}\leq M(1+\lVert\eta(t)y\rVert_{\ast})\leq M(1+\eta(0)\lVert y\rVert_{\ast})\leq\max(1,\eta(0))M(1+\lVert y\rVert_{\ast})

by the global growth condition (

H
2

). Since the upper bounds of both
Eqs.

D.7
and
D.8
are uniform in
t
, we can therefore use a standard existence theorem for solutions to SDEs driven by semimartingales (see e.g.,
[
65
, Theorem 1.3.4]
) to obtain that (
3.1
) admits a unique strong solution
Y
having càdlàg paths.
7
7

7

To be rigorous, the argument above only establishes existence and uniqueness for the SDE
dY^{\prime}(t)=-(\nabla f\circ Q)(\eta(t-)Y^{\prime}(t-))dt+dL(t)
, where the left limit of the drift is taken. This is due to the fact that stochastic processes are required to be predictable—and therefore almost left-continuous—for being used as integrands, see
Section

C.3
. However, in our case,
Y^{\prime}
has càdlàg paths and thus it can only have at most countably many jumps. Consequently,
Y^{\prime}(t-)=Y^{\prime}(t)
Lebesgue almost everywhere. Since
\eta
and
\nabla f\circ Q
are continuous, it follows that
\int_{0}^{t}(\nabla f\circ Q)(\eta(s-)Y^{\prime}(s-))ds=\int_{0}^{t}(\nabla f\circ Q)(\eta(s)Y^{\prime}(s))ds
, which implies that
Y^{\prime}
is in fact also the unique solution to (
3.1
).

Since
X(t)=Q(\eta(t)Y(t))\in\mathcal{X}
by definition of (
3.1
) and of the mirror map
Q
, it follows that the primal trajectories are also càdlàg and remain in
\mathcal{X}
at any times. Furthermore, if
h
is steep, then
\im(Q)=\relint\mathcal{X}
(cf.
Proposition

B.1
), which immediately yields the second claim.
∎

Remark D.1
.

It is worth noting that, by standard arguments, (

2

) follows directly from (

1

) if the local Lipschitz condition on

In the next two lemmas, we discuss standard settings under which the objective function

Lemma D.1
.

If

) and (

) hold true.

\lVert(\nabla f\circ Q)(y)-(\nabla f\circ Q)(y^{\prime})\rVert_{\ast}\leq L\lVert Q(y)-Q(y^{\prime})\rVert\leq\frac{L}{K}\lVert y-y^{\prime}\rVert_{\ast}.

This leads to

).
∎

Remark D.2
.

The globally Lipschitz setting of

); see for instance

. By contrast, relaxing this requirement to (

) and (

) allows the use of objective functions whose gradients may blow up at the boundary of the state

.

Lemma D.2
.

If
h
is steep and
\nabla f
is locally Lipschitz on
\relint\mathcal{X}
, then
f
satisfies (

H
1

).

Proof.

Fix
y_{0}\in\mathcal{Y}
. Since

is steep, we know that
x_{0}\defeq Q(y_{0})
belongs to
\relint\mathcal{X}
. In particular, there exists a relatively compact subset
\mathcal{K}\subset\relint\mathcal{X}
such that
x_{0}\in\mathcal{K}
. By the local Lipschitzness of
\nabla f
, it follows that it is Lipschitz continuous on
\mathcal{K}
with some finite constant

. Now, due to

being continuous on
\mathcal{Y}
, we can find a neighborhood
\mathcal{U}
of

(D.10)

\lVert(\nabla f\circ Q)(y)-(\nabla f\circ Q)(y^{\prime})\rVert_{\ast}\leq L(\mathcal{K})\lVert Q(y)-Q(y^{\prime})\rVert\leq\frac{L(\mathcal{K})}{K}\lVert y-y^{\prime}\rVert_{\ast},

where we have used that

is

-Lipschitz continuous on

(see

). It follows that, for every

is Lipschitz continuous on a neighborhood of

∎

Remark D.3
.

If

is not steep, the conclusion of

remains valid provided that

is locally Lipschitz on
\mathcal{X}_{h}\equiv\dom\partial h
.

Beyond providing existence and uniqueness of solutions to (

) and (

2

) also allows us to obtain the finiteness of the moments of

up to order

Lemma D.3

(Moments of the dual process)
.

Under assumptions (

H
1

) and (

H
2

), the solution
Y(t)
to (
3.1
) satisfies
\ex[\sup_{s\leq t}\lVert Y(s)\rVert_{\ast}^{p}]<\infty
for every
t\geq 0
.

Proof.

Fix an horizon
T>0
and let
0\leq t\leq T
. Applying the standard inequality
(a+b+c)^{p}\leq 3^{p-1}(a^{p}+b^{p}+c^{p})
to the definition of (
3.1
), we obtain

\displaystyle\lVert Y(t)\rVert_{\ast}^{p}

\displaystyle\leq 3^{p-1}\left[\lVert Y(0)\rVert_{\ast}^{p}+\left\lVert\int_{0}^{t}(\nabla f\circ Q)(\eta(s)Y(s))ds\right\rVert_{\ast}^{p}+\lVert L(t)\rVert_{\ast}^{p}\right]

\displaystyle\leq 3^{p-1}\left[\lVert Y(0)\rVert_{\ast}^{p}+t^{p-1}\int_{0}^{t}\lVert(\nabla f\circ Q)(\eta(s)Y(s))\rVert_{\ast}^{p}ds+\sup_{r\leq T}\lVert L(r)\rVert_{\ast}^{p}\right]

\displaystyle\leq 3^{p-1}\left[\lVert Y(0)\rVert_{\ast}^{p}+\sup_{r\leq T}\lVert L(r)\rVert_{\ast}^{p}+t^{p-1}\int_{0}^{t}M^{p}(1+\lVert\eta(s)Y(s))\rVert_{\ast})^{p}ds\right]

\displaystyle\leq 3^{p-1}\left[\lVert Y(0)\rVert_{\ast}^{p}+\sup_{r\leq T}\lVert L(r)\rVert_{\ast}^{p}+\frac{\big(2Mt\big)^{p}}{2t}+\frac{\big(2M\eta(0)t\big)^{p}}{2t}\int_{0}^{t}\lVert Y(s)\rVert_{\ast}^{p}ds\right]

(D.11)

\displaystyle\leq 3^{p-1}\left[\lVert Y(0)\rVert_{\ast}^{p}+\sup_{r\leq T}\lVert L(r)\rVert_{\ast}^{p}+\frac{\big(2MT\big)^{p}}{2T}+\frac{\big(2M\eta(0)T\big)^{p}}{2T}\int_{0}^{t}\lVert Y(s)\rVert_{\ast}^{p}ds\right]

where the second line comes from Hölder’s inequality and the third one from the global growth condition (

H
2

).
For notational convenience, let

(D.12)

Z(T)=3^{p-1}\left\{\lVert Y(0)\rVert_{\ast}^{p}+\sup_{r\leq T}\lVert L(r)\rVert_{\ast}^{p}+\frac{\big(2MT\big)^{p}}{2T}\right\}\quad\text{and}\quad C(T)=\frac{\big(6M\eta(0)T\big)^{p}}{6T}.

Taking the supremum over
s\leq t
therefore yields

(D.13)

\sup_{s\leq t}\lVert Y(s)\rVert_{\ast}^{p}\leq Z(T)+C(T)\int_{0}^{t}\sup_{u\leq s}\lVert Y(u)\rVert_{\ast}^{p}ds.

Given the particular form of the above inequality, one would be tempted to take the expectation and proceed by applying Grönwall’s lemma. However, at this stage we do not know if the left-hand side of
Eq.

D.13
admits a finite expectation since this is the result we aim to prove, hence rendering the argument slightly more intricate.
To proceed, we instead need to localize our bound using the stopping times
\tau_{n}=\inf\{t\geq 0:\lVert Y(t)\rVert_{\ast}\geq n\}
.
We first show that
\ex\left[\sup_{s\leq\tau_{n}\wedge t}\lVert Y(s)\rVert_{\ast}^{p}\right]
is finite. Indeed, note that if
s<\tau_{n}\wedge t
then
\lVert Y(s)\rVert_{\ast}<n
by definition. On the other hand, for
s=\tau_{n}\wedge t
,

\displaystyle\lVert Y(\tau_{n}\wedge t)\rVert_{\ast}

\displaystyle=\lVert Y(\tau_{n}\wedge t-)+\Delta Y(\tau_{n}\wedge t)\rVert_{\ast}=\lVert Y(\tau_{n}\wedge t-)+\Delta L(\tau_{n}\wedge t)\rVert_{\ast}

\displaystyle\leq\lVert Y(\tau_{n}\wedge t-)\rVert_{\ast}+\lVert L(\tau_{n}\wedge t)\rVert_{\ast}+\lVert L(\tau_{n}\wedge t-)\rVert_{\ast}

(D.14)

\displaystyle\leq n+2\sup_{r\leq T}\lVert L(r)\rVert_{\ast}.

But
L(r)
is a martingale with finite
p
-th moment, so Doob’s
L^{p}
-maximal inequality (see e.g.,
45
) can be applied to the positive submartingale
\lVert L(r)\rVert_{\ast}
to obtain

(D.15)

\ex[\sup_{r\leq T}\lVert L(r)\rVert_{\ast}^{p}]\leq\left(\dfrac{p}{p-1}\right)^{p}\ex[\lVert L(T)\rVert_{\ast}^{p}]<\infty.

Combined with
Eq.

D.14
, this yields

(D.16)

\ex[\sup_{s\leq\tau_{n}\wedge t}\lVert Y(s)\rVert_{\ast}^{p}]\leq 2^{p-1}\left\{n^{p}+2\ex[\sup_{r\leq T}\lVert L(r)\rVert_{\ast}^{p}]\right\}<\infty,

which proves that
g_{n}(t)\defeq\ex[\sup_{s\leq\tau_{n}\wedge t}\lVert Y(s)\rVert_{\ast}^{p}]
is a well-defined function on
[0,T]
. We can therefore apply
Eq.

D.13
with
t\leftarrow\tau_{n}\wedge t
and take the expectation on both sides, leading to

(D.17)

g_{n}(t)\leq\ex[Z(T)]+C(T)\ex\left[\int_{0}^{\tau_{n}\wedge t}\sup_{u\leq s}\lVert Y(u)\rVert_{\ast}^{p}ds\right]\leq\ex[Z(T)]+C(T)\int_{0}^{t}g_{n}(s)ds,

where
\ex[Z(T)]
is finite thanks to
Eq.

D.16
. Invoking Grönwall’s lemma then provides the estimate

(D.18)

g_{n}(t)\leq\ex[Z(T)]e^{C(T)t}\leq\ex[Z(T)]e^{C(T)T}.

Note that this upper bound does not depend on
n
and that
\sup_{s\leq\tau_{n}\wedge t}\lVert Y(s)\rVert_{\ast}^{p}
increases to
\sup_{s\leq t}\lVert Y(s)\rVert_{\ast}^{p}
as
n\to\infty
, so the monotone convergence theorem yields

(D.19)

\ex[\sup_{s\leq t}\lVert Y(s)\rVert_{\ast}^{p}]\leq\ex[Z(T)]e^{C(T)T}<\infty,

hence concluding the proof.
∎

In particular,
Lemma

D.3
implies that the drift term of (
3.1
) admits finite moments of order
p
even when the gradient of the objective function explodes at the boundary of
\mathcal{X}
.

Corollary D.1
.

Under assumptions (

H
1

) and (

H
2

),

(D.20)

\ex\left[\int_{0}^{t}\left\lVert\nabla f(X(s))\right\rVert_{\ast}^{p}ds\right]<\infty\quad\text{for every }t\geq 0.

Proof.

Thanks to the growth condition (

H
2

), we can write

\displaystyle\ex\left[\int_{0}^{t}\lVert\nabla f(X(s))\rVert_{\ast}^{p}ds\right]

\displaystyle=\ex\left[\int_{0}^{t}\lVert(\nabla f\circ Q)(\eta(s)Y(s))\rVert_{\ast}^{p}ds\right]

\displaystyle\leq\ex\left[\int_{0}^{t}M^{p}(1+\lVert\eta(s)Y(s)\rVert_{\ast})^{p}ds\right]

(D.21)

\displaystyle\leq 2^{p-1}M^{p}\left(1+\eta(0)^{p}\ex[\sup_{s\leq t}\lVert Y(s)\rVert_{\ast}^{p}]\right)

where the right-hand side is finite due to

D.3

∎

D.2.

Remark D.4
.

When

Lemma D.5

(Estimation of

\displaystyle I(t)\leq\dfrac{\lVert\mathcal{X}\rVert^{2-p}\sigma_{\textup{heavy}}^{p}}{K^{p-1}}\int_{0}^{t}\eta(s)^{p-1}ds

∎

In particular, for a sufficiently small target radius

Corollary D.2

Under the same assumptions as in

(Convergence rate for relatively strongly convex functions, constant learning rate)
.

Let

Before proving

Lemma D.8
.

Let

be a function such that

(D.81)

F(t)-F(s)\leq\int_{s}^{t}g(r)dr\quad\text{for every }t\geq s\geq 0,

where

is a locally integrable function. Then

is almost everywhere differentiable with
F^{\prime}(t)\leq g(t)

Proof.

Let
G(t)=\int_{0}^{t}g(r)dr
and
H=F-G
. Then

D.81
implies that
H(t)\leq H(s)
for all
t\geq s\geq 0
, and thus
H
is a nonincreasing function. In virtue of Lebesgue differentiation theorem,

is therefore almost everywhere differentiable (on open intervals) and satisfies
H^{\prime}(t)\leq 0
. Since
F=G+H
is the sum of two almost everwhere differentiable function, it implies in turn that
F
is also as such and that
F^{\prime}(t)=G^{\prime}(t)+H^{\prime}(t)\leq g(t)
, which concludes the proof.
∎

Proof of
Eq.

D.80
.

Starting from
Lemma

D.4
and the estimates of

D.5
,
D.6
and
8

D(x^{\ast},X(t))=F(x^{\ast},\eta Y(t))
since

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{\eta}

\displaystyle\ex\left[D(x^{\ast},X(t))\right]=\ex\left[E(t)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\ex\left[E(s)\right]-\ex\left[\int_{s}^{t}\left\langle X-x^{\ast},\nabla f(X)\right\rangle dr\right]+\dfrac{\eta}{2K}\sigma_{\textup{tame}}^{2}\int_{s}^{t}dr+\dfrac{\eta^{p-1}\lVert\mathcal{X}\rVert^{2-p}}{K^{p-1}}\sigma_{\textup{heavy}}^{p}\int_{s}^{t}dr

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{1}{\eta}\ex\left[D(x^{\ast},X(s))\right]

\displaystyle\quad-\int_{s}^{t}\left[\ex\left[f(X)-f(x^{\ast})+\mu D(x^{\ast},X)\right]-\left(\dfrac{\eta\sigma_{\textup{tame}}^{2}}{2K}+\dfrac{\eta^{p-1}\lVert\mathcal{X}\rVert^{2-p}\sigma_{\textup{heavy}}^{p}}{K^{p-1}}\right)\right]dr

(D.82)

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{1}{\eta}\ex\left[D(x^{\ast},X(s))\right]-\int_{s}^{t}\left[\mu\ex\left[D(x^{\ast},X)\right]-\left(\dfrac{\eta\sigma_{\textup{tame}}^{2}}{2K}+\dfrac{\eta^{p-1}\lVert\mathcal{X}\rVert^{2-p}\sigma_{\textup{heavy}}^{p}}{K^{p-1}}\right)\right]dr,

where the second inequality comes from the relative strong convexity of

being the minimum of

. Since the above inequality holds true for every

that the function

(D.83)

\varphi^{\prime}(t)\leq-\mu\eta\varphi(t)+c_{1}\eta^{2}+c_{2}\eta^{p},

where we have set

In particular, we can therefore use Grönwall’s lemma to obtain

\displaystyle\ex\left[D(x^{\ast},X(t))\right]=\varphi(t)

\displaystyle\leq\varphi(0)e^{-\mu\eta t}+\dfrac{c_{1}\eta^{2}+c_{2}\eta^{p}}{\mu\eta}\left(1-e^{-\mu\eta t}\right)

(D.84)

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{\sigma_{\textup{tame}}^{2}}{2K\mu}\eta+\dfrac{\lVert\mathcal{X}\rVert^{2-p}\sigma_{\textup{heavy}}^{p}}{\mu K^{p-1}}\eta^{p-1}+D(x^{\ast},x_{0})e^{-\mu\eta t},

and the result then follows thanks to the inequality

B.2
).
∎

Theorem D.7

(Convergence rate for relatively strongly convex functions, decreasing learning rate)
.

Let
h
be a steep regularizer. If
f
is
\mu
-strongly convex relative to
h
with minimum
x^{\ast}\in\mathcal{X}
and if
\eta(t)=(1+t)^{-1/p}
, then

\displaystyle\ex[\lVert X(t)-x^{\ast}\rVert^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{\sigma_{\textup{tame}}^{2}}{\mu K^{2}}t^{-\frac{1}{p}}+\dfrac{2}{\mu K}\left(\dfrac{\lVert\mathcal{X}\rVert^{2-p}\sigma_{\textup{heavy}}^{p}}{K^{p-1}}+\dfrac{H}{p}\right)t^{-\frac{p-1}{p}}

(D.85)

\displaystyle+\dfrac{2D(x^{\ast},x_{0})}{K}\exp\left[-\frac{\mu p}{p-1}\left(t^{\frac{p-1}{p}}-1\right)\right].

In particular,
X(t)\to x^{\ast}
in mean square as
t\to\infty
.

Proof.

Following the same arguments as in the proof of
Eq.

D.80
but with a non-constant learning rate, we obtain that the function
\psi(t)\defeq\frac{1}{\eta(t)}\ex\left[D(x^{\ast},X(t))\right]
is almost-everywhere differentiable and that it satisfies

\displaystyle\psi^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle\leq-\mu\ex\left[D(x^{\ast},X)\right]+c_{1}\eta(t)+c_{2}\eta(t)^{p-1}-\dfrac{\dot{\eta}(t)}{\eta^{2}(t)}\ex\left[h(x^{\ast})-h(X)\right]

(D.86)

\displaystyle\leq-\mu\eta(t)\psi(t)+c_{1}\eta(t)+c_{2}\eta(t)^{p-1}-\dfrac{\dot{\eta}(t)}{\eta^{2}(t)}H,

where we recall that
c_{1}=\frac{\sigma_{\textup{tame}}^{2}}{2K}
and

. Letting

(D.87)

\psi^{\prime}(t)\leq-\dfrac{\mu}{(1+t)^{\frac{1}{p}}}\psi(t)+\dfrac{c_{1}}{(1+t)^{\frac{1}{p}}}+\dfrac{c_{2}}{(1+t)^{\frac{p-1}{p}}}+\dfrac{H}{p(1+t)^{\frac{p-1}{p}}},

and Grönwall’s lemma can then be invoked to write

\displaystyle\psi(t)

\displaystyle\leq\psi(0)\exp\left(-\mu\int_{0}^{t}\dfrac{1}{(1+s)^{\frac{1}{p}}}ds\right)

(D.88)

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}\left(\dfrac{c_{1}}{(1+s)^{\frac{1}{p}}}+\dfrac{c_{3}}{(1+s)^{\frac{p-1}{p}}}\right)\exp\left(-\mu\int_{s}^{t}\dfrac{1}{(1+r)^{\frac{1}{p}}}dr\right)ds,

where we have set
c_{3}=c_{2}+\frac{H}{p}
. To estimate the integral appearing in the right-hand side of (

Lemma D.9
.

For all

,
c>0
, and all exponents

(D.89)

I(t)\defeq\int_{0}^{t}\left(\dfrac{a}{(1+s)^{\frac{1}{p}}}+\dfrac{b}{(1+s)^{\frac{p-1}{p}}}\right)\exp\left(-c\int_{s}^{t}\dfrac{1}{(1+r)^{\frac{1}{p}}}dr\right)ds\leq\dfrac{1}{c}\left(a+b(1+t)^{\frac{2-p}{p}}\right).

Proof.

To begin with, note that

(D.90)

\int_{s}^{t}\dfrac{1}{(1+r)^{1/p}}dr=\dfrac{p}{p-1}\left[(1+t)^{\frac{p-1}{p}}-(1+s)^{\frac{p-1}{p}}\right],

so

can be written as

(D.91)

I(t)=\exp\left(-\dfrac{cp}{p-1}(1+t)^{\frac{p-1}{p}}\right)\int_{0}^{t}\left(a(1+s)^{-\frac{1}{p}}+b(1+s)^{-\frac{p-1}{p}}\right)\\
\times\exp\left(\dfrac{cp}{p-1}(1+s)^{\frac{p-1}{p}}\right)ds.

Using the substitution
r\leftrightarrow(1+s)^{\frac{p-1}{p}}
, we further get

(D.92)

I(t)=\exp\left(-\dfrac{cp}{p-1}(1+t)^{\frac{p-1}{p}}\right)\dfrac{p}{p-1}\int_{1}^{(1+t)^{\frac{p-1}{p}}}\left(a+br^{\frac{2-p}{p-1}}\right)\exp\left(\dfrac{cp}{p-1}r\right)dr.

Now, since
\frac{2-p}{p-1}\geq 0
and
0\leq r\leq(1+t)^{\frac{p-1}{p}}
, we also have
r^{\frac{2-p}{p-1}}\leq(1+t)^{\frac{2-p}{p}}
and thus

\displaystyle I(t)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-\dfrac{cp}{p-1}(1+t)^{\frac{p-1}{p}}\right)\dfrac{p}{p-1}\left(a+b(1+t)^{\frac{2-p}{p}}\right)\int_{1}^{(1+t)^{\frac{p-1}{p}}}\dfrac{p}{p-1}\exp\left(\dfrac{cp}{p-1}r\right)dr

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{c}\left(a+b(1+t)^{\frac{2-p}{p}}\right)\left[1-\exp\left(\dfrac{cp}{p-1}\left(1-(1+t)^{\frac{p-1}{p}}\right)\right)\right]

(D.93)

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{1}{c}\left(a+b(1+t)^{\frac{2-p}{p}}\right),

which concludes the proof.
∎

Remark D.10
.

For a learning rate
\eta\propto t^{-1/p}
,
Theorem

D.7
implies that for any
\varepsilon>0
, the accuracy
\ex\left[\lVert X(t)-x^{\ast}\rVert^{2}\right]<\varepsilon
is reached for times
t\geq\bigoh\left(\varepsilon^{-\frac{p}{p-1}}\right)
. Unlike the complexity bound for constant learning rates, this rate is of the same order as those for convex or strongly convex functions, and therefore does not even attain the optimal
\bigoh\left(1/\varepsilon\right)
complexity for
p=2
. As we discuss further in the discrete-time section, this limitation arises because a positive term proportional to
-\dot{\eta}/\eta^{2}
always appears in the computations. This is a consequence of the learning rate acting on the aggregated gradient steps rather than on individual steps, which prevents the use of a
1/t
-type learning rate to achieve sharper convergence rates, as is standard in the analysis of stochastic gradient methods.

Appendix E
Results in discrete time: Omitted proofs from
Section

4

In this section, we draw inspiration from the continuous-time analysis of (
3.1
) to study the convergence properties of the Stochastic Dual Averaging (
SDA
) algorithm given by

(SDA)

\displaystyle y_{t+1}

\displaystyle=y_{t}-g_{t}

\displaystyle x_{t+1}

\displaystyle=Q(\eta_{t+1}y_{t+1})

where
\eta_{t}>0
is a nonincreasing
learning rate
sequence and
g_{t}
is a
stochastic gradient
of the form
g_{t}=\nabla f(x_{t})+U_{t}
with gradient noise term
U_{t}=\err(x_{t};\omega_{t})
satisfying the following assumptions:

(
a.5pt
)

Unbiasedness:

\ex[\err(x;\omega)]=0
for all
x\in\dom\partial f
.

(
b.5pt
)

Bounded
p
-th central moments:

\ex[\lVert\err(x;\omega)\rVert_{\ast}^{p}]\leq\sigma^{p}
for some
p\in(1,2]
and all
x\in\dom\partial f
.

Throughout this section, we further assume that
f
is
L
-smooth relative to
h
, that is,

(E.1)

f(x^{\prime})\leq f(x)+\langle\nabla f(x),x^{\prime}-x\rangle+LD(x^{\prime},x)\quad\text{for all }x\in\mathcal{X}_{h},x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}\,..

E.1.
Convex setting

We begin by exploring the case where
f
is a convex function. As in the continuous-time analysis, our approach is based on tracking the evolution of the
\eta
-deflated Fenchel coupling

(E.2)

E_{t}(q,y)=\dfrac{1}{\eta_{t}}F(q,\eta_{t}y)\ \quad q\in\mathcal{X},y\in\mathcal{Y}

along the iterates of (
SDA
). In contrast to (
3.1
), however, the discrete-time nature of (
SDA
) allows us to derive the evolution of
E_{t}
without needing a weak stochastic chain rule (Itô’s formula), which considerably simplifies the analysis.
This leads to the following result, which can be viewed as a discrete-time analogue of the classical
three-point identity
(
Lemma

B.2
) for the
\eta
-deflated Fenchel coupling.

Lemma E.1

(Three-point identity for
\eta
-deflated Fenchel coupling)
.

For every
q\in\mathcal{X}
, the process
E_{t}\defeq E_{t}(q,y_{t})
along trajectories of (
SDA
) satisfies

\displaystyle E_{t+1}-E_{t}

\displaystyle=\left(\dfrac{1}{\eta_{t+1}}-\dfrac{1}{\eta_{t}}\right)\left(h(q)-h(x_{t+1})\right)

(E.3)

\displaystyle+\left\langle x_{t}-q,y_{t+1}-y_{t}\right\rangle+\left\langle x_{t+1}-x_{t},y_{t+1}-y_{t}\right\rangle-\dfrac{1}{\eta_{t}}F(x_{t+1},\eta_{t}y_{t}).

Proof.

First, note that

(E.4)

F(x_{t+1},\eta_{t}y_{t})-F(q,\eta_{t}y_{t})=h(x_{t+1})-h(q)-\eta_{t}\left\langle x_{t+1}-q,y_{t}\right\rangle\,,

and, by optimality of
x_{t+1}
, we also have

\displaystyle F(q,\eta_{t+1}y_{t+1})

\displaystyle=h(q)+h^{\ast}(\eta_{t+1}y_{t+1})-\eta_{t+1}\left\langle q,y_{t+1}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=h(q)+\eta_{t+1}\left\langle x_{t+1},y_{t+1}\right\rangle-h(x_{t+1})-\eta_{t+1}\left\langle q,y_{t+1}\right\rangle

(E.5)

\displaystyle=h(q)-h(x_{t+1})+\eta_{t+1}\left\langle x_{t+1}-q,y_{t+1}\right\rangle.

This leads to

\displaystyle E_{t+1}-E_{t}+\dfrac{1}{\eta_{t}}F(x_{t+1},\eta_{t}y_{t})

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{\eta_{t+1}}F(q,\eta_{t+1}y_{t+1})+\dfrac{1}{\eta_{t}}\left(F(x_{t+1},\eta_{t}y_{t})-F(q,\eta_{t}y_{t})\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\dfrac{1}{\eta_{t+1}}-\dfrac{1}{\eta_{t}}\right)\left(h(q)-h(x_{t+1})\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\left\langle x_{t+1}-q,y_{t+1}-y_{t}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left(\dfrac{1}{\eta_{t+1}}-\dfrac{1}{\eta_{t}}\right)\left(h(q)-h(x_{t+1})\right)

(E.6)

\displaystyle\quad+\left\langle x_{t}-q,y_{t+1}-y_{t}\right\rangle+\left\langle x_{t+1}-x_{t},y_{t+1}-y_{t}\right\rangle\,,

which concludes the proof after rearranging the terms.
∎

We now invoke the relative smoothness of the objective together with the bounded
p
-th central moment assumption on the gradient noise to obtain the following estimate:

Lemma E.2

(Heavy-tailed noise estimate)
.

If
f
is relatively
L
-smooth and
\eta_{t}\leq\frac{1}{pL}
, then the iterates of (
SDA
) satisfy

(E.7)

\left\langle x_{t+1}-x_{t},y_{t+1}-y_{t}\right\rangle-\dfrac{1}{\eta_{t}}F(x_{t+1},\eta_{t}y_{t})\leq f(x_{t})-f(x_{t+1})+\dfrac{2^{p-1}\lVert\mathcal{X}\rVert^{2-p}}{pK^{p-1}}\eta_{t}^{p-1}\lVert U_{t}\rVert_{\ast}^{p}.

Proof.

Since
y_{t+1}-y_{t}=-g_{t}=-(\nabla f(x_{t})+U_{t})
and
p\in(1,2]
, we can decompose the left-hand side of
Eq.

E.7
as

(E.8)

\displaystyle\left\langle x_{t+1}-x_{t},y_{t+1}-y_{t}\right\rangle-\dfrac{1}{\eta_{t}}F(x_{t+1},\eta_{t}y_{t})

\displaystyle=\left\langle\nabla f(x_{t}),x_{t}-x_{t+1}\right\rangle-\dfrac{1}{p\eta_{t}}F(x_{t+1},\eta_{t}y_{t})

(E.9)

\displaystyle+\left\langle U_{t},x_{t}-x_{t+1}\right\rangle-\dfrac{p-1}{p\eta_{t}}F(x_{t+1},\eta_{t}y_{t}).

Focusing first on bounding (
E.8
), the relative smoothness of
f
yields

(
E.8
)

\displaystyle\leq f(x_{t})-f(x_{t+1})+LD(x_{t+1},x_{t})-\dfrac{1}{p\eta_{t}}F(x_{t+1},\eta_{t}y_{t})

\displaystyle\leq f(x_{t})-f(x_{t+1})+LF(x_{t+1},\eta_{t}y_{t})-\dfrac{1}{p\eta_{t}}F(x_{t+1},\eta_{t}y_{t})

\displaystyle=f(x_{t})-f(x_{t+1})-\dfrac{L}{\eta_{t}}\left(\dfrac{1}{pL}-\eta_{t}\right)F(x_{t+1},\eta_{t}y_{t})

(E.10)

\displaystyle\leq f(x_{t})-f(x_{t+1}),

where the second inequality comes from
D(x,Q(y^{\prime}))\leq F(x,y^{\prime})
(cf.
Proposition

B.2
) and the last inequality from the assumption that
\eta_{t}\leq 1/(pL)
. On the other hand, for (
E.9
), we use the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and the fact that
F(x,y)\geq\frac{K}{2}\lVert x-Q(y)\rVert^{2}
(see
Proposition

B.2
) to get

(E.11)

\text{\eqref{eq:xi_2_discrete}}\leq\lVert U_{t}\rVert_{\ast}\lVert x_{t+1}-x_{t}\rVert-\dfrac{p-1}{p\eta_{t}}\dfrac{K}{2}\lVert x_{t+1}-x_{t}\rVert^{2}.

Since the diameter
\lVert\mathcal{X}\rVert
of
\mathcal{X}
is assumed to be finite, we know that
\lVert x_{t+1}-x_{t}\rVert\leq\lVert\mathcal{X}\rVert
and therefore

\displaystyle\lVert x_{t+1}-x_{t}\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle=\lVert\mathcal{X}\rVert^{-\frac{2-p}{p-1}}\lVert\mathcal{X}\rVert^{\frac{2-p}{p-1}}\lVert x_{t+1}-x_{t}\rVert^{2}

(E.12)

\displaystyle\geq\lVert\mathcal{X}\rVert^{-\frac{2-p}{p-1}}\lVert x_{t+1}-x_{t}\rVert^{\frac{2-p}{p-1}}\lVert x_{t+1}-x_{t}\rVert^{2}=\lVert\mathcal{X}\rVert^{-\frac{2-p}{p-1}}\lVert x_{t+1}-x_{t}\rVert^{\frac{p}{p-1}}.

Putting this estimate back into
Eq.

E.11
then yields

(
E.9
)

\displaystyle\leq\lVert U_{t}\rVert_{\ast}\lVert x_{t+1}-x_{t}\rVert-\dfrac{p-1}{p}\dfrac{K\lVert\mathcal{X}\rVert^{-\frac{2-p}{p-1}}}{2\eta_{t}}\lVert x_{t+1}-x_{t}\rVert^{\frac{p}{p-1}}

(E.13)

\displaystyle\leq\max_{w\geq 0}\left(\lVert U_{t}\rVert_{\ast}w-\dfrac{p-1}{p}Cw^{\frac{p}{p-1}}\right)\,,

where we have defined
C=\frac{K}{2\eta_{t}}\lVert\mathcal{X}\rVert^{-\frac{2-p}{p-1}}
for conciseness. The maximization problem is attained at
w=(\lVert U_{t}\rVert_{\ast}/C)^{p-1}
and thus

(
E.9
)

\displaystyle\leq\lVert U_{t}\rVert_{\ast}\left(\dfrac{\lVert U_{t}\rVert_{\ast}}{C}\right)^{p-1}-\dfrac{p-1}{p}C\left(\dfrac{\lVert U_{t}\rVert_{\ast}}{C}\right)^{\frac{p}{p-1}(p-1)}

(E.14)

\displaystyle=\left(1-\frac{p-1}{p}\right)C^{1-p}\lVert U_{t}\rVert_{\ast}^{p}=\dfrac{2^{p-1}\lVert\mathcal{X}\rVert^{2-p}}{pK^{p-1}}\eta_{t}^{p-1}\lVert U_{t}\rVert_{\ast}^{p}.

Combining the upper bounds for (
E.8
) and (
E.9
) therefore yields the desired result.
∎

We can now combine the first two lemmas to obtain the following theorem, which—similarly to its continuous-time analogue—serves as the baseline for most of our subsequent results.

Theorem E.1
.

If
f
is relatively
L
-smooth, then for every
q\in\mathcal{X}
and every nonincreasing learning rate sequence with
\eta_{1}\leq\frac{1}{pL}
, the iterates of (
SDA
) satisfy

(E.15)

\ex\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\langle x_{t}-q,\nabla f(x_{t})\right\rangle\right]\leq\left\langle x_{1}-q,y_{1}\right\rangle+f(x_{1})-\min f\\
+\dfrac{H}{\eta_{T+1}}+\dfrac{2^{p-1}\lVert\mathcal{X}\rVert^{2-p}}{pK^{p-1}}\sigma^{p}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\eta_{t}^{p-1}.

Proof.

Summing the equality of
Lemma

E.1
over
t=1,\dots,T
and using the upper bound of
Eq.

E.7
, we get

\displaystyle 0\leq\ex[E_{T}]\leq E_{1}

\displaystyle+\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(\dfrac{1}{\eta_{t+1}}-\dfrac{1}{\eta_{t}}\right)\ex\left[h(q)-h(x_{t+1})\right]

\displaystyle-\ex\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\langle x_{t}-q,\nabla f(x_{t})\right\rangle\right]+f(x_{1})-\ex\left[f(x_{T+1})\right]

(E.16)

\displaystyle-\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ex\left[\left\langle x_{t}-q,U_{t}\right\rangle\right]+\dfrac{2^{p-1}\lVert\mathcal{X}\rVert^{2-p}}{pK^{p-1}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\eta_{t}^{p-1}\ex\left[\lVert U_{t}\rVert_{\ast}^{p}\right].

Note that since
\eta_{t}
is a nonincreasing sequence and since
h
is continuous on the compact set
\mathcal{X}
, we have

(E.17)

\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(\dfrac{1}{\eta_{t+1}}-\dfrac{1}{\eta_{t}}\right)\ex\left[h(q)-h(x_{t+1})\right]\leq\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(\dfrac{1}{\eta_{t+1}}-\dfrac{1}{\eta_{t}}\right)H=\dfrac{H}{\eta_{T+1}}-\dfrac{H}{\eta_{1}}.

Moreover, by optimality of
x_{1}
,

(E.18)

E_{1}=\frac{1}{\eta_{1}}F(q,\eta_{1}y_{1})=\dfrac{1}{\eta_{1}}\left(h(q)-h(x_{1})-\left\langle\eta_{1}y_{1},q-x_{1}\right\rangle\right)\leq\dfrac{H}{\eta_{1}}-\left\langle q-x_{1},y_{1}\right\rangle.

Now, by the blanket assumptions made on the noise term
U_{t}
and by definition of the filtration
\mathcal{F}_{t}
, we have

(E.19)

\ex\left[\lVert U_{t}\rVert_{\ast}^{p}\right]=\ex\left[\ex\left[\lVert\err(x_{t};\omega_{t})\rVert_{\ast}^{p}\nonscript\,|\nonscript\,\mathopen{}\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]\right]\leq\sigma^{p}

and

(E.20)

\ex\left[\left\langle x_{t}-q,U_{t}\right\rangle\right]=\ex\left[\ex\left[\left\langle x_{t}-q,\err(x_{t};\omega_{t})\right\rangle\nonscript\,\middle|\nonscript\,\mathopen{}\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]\right]=\ex\left[\left\langle x_{t}-q,\ex\left[\err(x_{t};\omega_{t})\nonscript\,\middle|\nonscript\,\mathopen{}\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]\right\rangle\right]=0.

All of these estimates ultimately lead to the bound

(E.21)

\ex\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\langle x_{t}-q,\nabla f(x_{t})\right\rangle\right]\leq\left\langle x_{1}-q,y_{1}\right\rangle+f(x_{1})-\min f\\
+\dfrac{H}{\eta_{T+1}}+\dfrac{2^{p-1}\lVert\mathcal{X}\rVert^{2-p}}{pK^{p-1}}\sigma^{p}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\eta_{t}^{p-1}\,,

which concludes our proof.
∎

Specializing

E.9
to convex objectives yields the following result, which generalizes
Theorem

5
from the main text to arbitrary learning rates and arbitrary initial conditions.

Theorem E.2

(Convergence rate of (
SDA
) for convex functions)
.

If
f
is convex and relatively
L
-smooth, then for every nonincreasing learning rate sequence with
\eta_{1}\leq\frac{1}{pL}
and for every
x^{\ast}\in\argmin f
, the iterates of (
SDA
) satisfy

(E.22)

\ex\left[f(\bar{x}_{T})-\min f\right]\leq\dfrac{\left\langle x_{1}-x^{\ast},y_{1}\right\rangle+f(x_{1})-\min f}{T}\\
+\dfrac{H}{T\eta_{T+1}}+\dfrac{2^{p-1}\lVert\mathcal{X}\rVert^{2-p}\sigma^{p}}{pK^{p-1}}\dfrac{\sum_{t=1}^{T}\eta_{t}^{p-1}}{T}\,,

where
\bar{x}_{T}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}x_{t}
.
In particular, the choice
\eta_{t}=\beta t^{-1/p}
with
\beta\leq\frac{1}{pL}
yields

(E.23)

\ex\left[f(\bar{x}_{T})-\min f\right]\leq\dfrac{\left\langle x_{1}-x^{\ast},y_{1}\right\rangle+f(x_{1})-\min f}{T}\\
+\left(\dfrac{H}{\beta}+\dfrac{2^{p-1}\beta^{p-1}\lVert\mathcal{X}\rVert^{2-p}\sigma^{p}}{K^{p-1}}\right)T^{-\frac{p-1}{p}}.

Proof.

Due to
f
being convex,

(E.24)

\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\langle x_{t}-x^{\ast},\nabla f(x_{t})\right\rangle\geq\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left[f(x_{t})-f(x^{\ast})\right]\geq T\left[f(\bar{x}(T))-\min f\right]\,,

and the convergence rate for general learning rates is therefore an immediate consequence of
Eq.

E.15
. The specialized bound for
\eta_{t}=\beta t^{-1/p}
is then established by using the upper bound
\sum_{t=1}^{T}t^{-\frac{p-1}{p}}\leq pT^{1/p}
obtained from a standard integral estimation.
∎

Remark E.1
.

For any precision
\varepsilon>0
, running (
SDA
) with learning rate
\eta=\bigoh\left((\varepsilon/\sigma^{p})^{\frac{1}{p-1}}\right)
yields
\ex\left[f(\bar{x}_{T})-\min f\right]\leq\varepsilon
for every

, thus matching the complexity obtained in the continuous-time setting, cf.
Remark

.

E.2.

We now turn to the convergence analysis of (

Theorem E.3

(Convergence rate of (

) for strongly convex functions)
.

If
f
is
\mu
-strongly convex with minimum
x^{\ast}\in\mathcal{X}

+\dfrac{2H}{\mu T\eta_{T+1}}+\dfrac{2^{p}\lVert\mathcal{X}\rVert^{2-p}\sigma^{p}}{\mu pK^{p-1}}\dfrac{\sum_{t=1}^{T}\eta_{t}^{p-1}}{T}.

By strong convexity of

(E.27)

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\langle x_{t}-x^{\ast},\nabla f(x_{t})\right\rangle\geq\dfrac{\mu}{2}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\lVert x_{t}-x^{\ast}\rVert^{2}\geq\dfrac{\mu T}{2}\lVert\bar{x}_{T}-x^{\ast}\rVert^{2},

E.15
.
∎

Remark E.2
.

Similarly to

E.1
, we get that for every
\varepsilon>0
, the learning rate
\eta=\bigoh\left((\varepsilon/\sigma^{p})^{\frac{1}{p-1}}\right)

.

We now proceed by estimating the fraction of time that (

8

of

.

Theorem E.4

(Concentration around global minimum)
.

Assume that
f
is
\mu
-strongly convex with minimum
x^{\ast}
and
L
-relatively smooth. Let

(E.28)

\mu_{T}(\mathcal{U}_{\delta})=\dfrac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\one\{x_{t}\in\mathcal{U}_{\delta}\}

be the fraction of time that
x_{t}
spends in
\mathcal{U}_{\delta}=\{x\in\mathcal{X}:\lVert x-x^{\ast}\rVert\leq\delta\}
up to time
T
. If (
SDA
) is run with constant learning rate
\eta_{t}\equiv\eta
, we have

(E.29)

\ex\left[\mu_{T}(\mathcal{U}_{\delta})\right]\geq 1-\dfrac{2\left\langle x_{1}-x^{\ast},y_{1}\right\rangle}{\mu\delta^{2}T}-\dfrac{2\big(f(x_{1})-\min f\big)}{\mu\delta^{2}T}-\dfrac{2H}{\eta\mu\delta^{2}T}-\dfrac{2^{p}\eta^{p-1}\lVert\mathcal{X}\rVert^{2-p}\sigma^{p}}{\mu pK^{p-1}\delta^{2}}.

Proof.

Markov’s inequality together with the strong convexity of

yield

\displaystyle\ex\left[\mu_{T}(\mathcal{X}\setminus\mathcal{U}_{\delta})\right]

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\prob(\lVert x_{t}-x^{\ast}\rVert>\delta)

(E.30)

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\dfrac{\ex\left[\lVert x_{t}-x^{\ast}\rVert^{2}\right]}{\delta^{2}}\leq\dfrac{2}{\mu\delta^{2}T}\ex\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\langle x_{t}-x^{\ast},\nabla f(x_{t})\right\rangle\right]\,,

and the concentration bound therefore follows directly from
Eq.

E.15
.
∎

Theorem E.5

(Hitting time estimate)
.

Assume that
f
is
\mu
-strongly convex with minimum
x^{\ast}\in\mathcal{X}
and relatively
L
-smooth. Let

(E.31)

\tau_{\delta}=\inf\{t\in\mathbb{N}:\lVert x_{t}-x^{\ast}\rVert\leq\delta\}

denote the first time
x_{t}
gets within
\delta
of
x^{\ast}
for some precision
\delta>0
. If (
SDA
) is run with constant learning rate

(E.32)

\eta<\left(\dfrac{\mu pK}{2^{p}K^{2-p}\lVert\mathcal{X}\rVert^{2-p}\sigma^{p}}\right)^{\frac{1}{p-1}}\delta^{\frac{2}{p-1}}\,,

then the average value of
\tau_{\delta}
is bounded as

(E.33)

\ex\left[\tau_{\delta}\right]\leq\dfrac{2pKF(x^{\ast},\eta y_{1})+2pK\big(f(x_{1})-\min f+\frac{\mu\delta^{2}}{2}\big)\eta}{\mu pK\delta^{2}\eta-2^{p}K^{2-p}\lVert\mathcal{X}\rVert^{2-p}\sigma^{p}\eta^{p}}.

In particular, running (
SDA
) with
y_{1}=0
and

(E.34)

\eta=\left(\dfrac{\mu K}{2^{p}K^{2-p}\lVert\mathcal{X}\rVert^{2-p}\sigma^{p}}\right)^{\frac{1}{p-1}}\delta^{\frac{2}{p-1}}

yields the hitting-time estimate

(E.35)

\ex\left[\tau_{\delta}\right]\leq\dfrac{p}{p-1}\left\{\dfrac{2H}{\mu K}\left(\dfrac{2^{p}\lVert\mathcal{X}\rVert^{2-p}\sigma^{p}}{\mu}\right)^{\frac{1}{p-1}}\delta^{-\frac{2p}{p-1}}+\dfrac{2\big(f(x_{1})-\min f\big)}{\mu}\delta^{-2}+1\right\}.

Remark E.3
.

Since
y_{1}=0
, the quantity
f(x_{1})-\min f
in
Eq.

E.35
is in fact equal to
f(\argmin h)-f(x^{\ast})
, that is, to the value difference between the
prox-center

x_{c}=\argmin h
and the minimizer
x^{\ast}
of
f
.

Proof.

Since the learning rate
\eta
is assumed constant, summing the equality of
Lemma

E.1
over
t=1,\dots,T
and invoking the upper bound of
Eq.

E.7
yields

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq E_{T}

(E.36)

\displaystyle\leq E_{1}-\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\langle x_{t}-x^{\ast},\nabla f(x_{t})\right\rangle+f(x_{1})-\min f

(E.37)

\displaystyle-\,\,

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\langle x_{t}-x^{\ast},U_{t}\right\rangle+\dfrac{2^{p-1}\lVert\mathcal{X}\rVert^{2-p}\eta^{p-1}}{pK^{p-1}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\lVert U_{t}\rVert_{\ast}^{p}.

By analogy with the arguments used to prove the hitting time estimate for continuous (
3.1
) dynamics (cf.
Theorem

D.5
), we then apply the above inequality to the bounded stopping times
T\leftarrow\tau_{\delta}\wedge T
and take the expectation on both sides, which leads to

\displaystyle 0\leq E_{1}

\displaystyle+f(x_{1})-\min f-\ex\left[\sum_{t=1}^{\tau_{\delta}\wedge T}\left\langle x_{t}-x^{\ast},\nabla f(x_{t})\right\rangle\right]

(E.38)

\displaystyle+\dfrac{2^{p-1}\lVert\mathcal{X}\rVert^{2-p}\eta^{p-1}}{pK^{p-1}}\ex\left[\sum_{t=1}^{\tau_{\delta}\wedge T}\lVert U_{t}\rVert_{\ast}^{p}\right]-\ex\left[\sum_{t=1}^{\tau_{\delta}\wedge T}\left\langle x_{t}-x^{\ast},U_{t}\right\rangle\right].

Before proceeding further, recall that
\mathcal{F}_{t}
is the filtration generated by
x_{s}
,
1\leq s\leq t
, and that
U_{t}
is a martingale difference sequence. In particular,
U_{t}
is
\mathcal{F}_{t+1}
-measurable and
\ex[U_{t}\nonscript\,|\nonscript\,\mathopen{}\mathcal{F}_{t}]=0
.
By the tower property of conditional expectations, we can therefore write

\displaystyle\ex\left[\sum_{t=1}^{\tau_{\delta}\wedge T}\left\langle x_{t}-x^{\ast},U_{t}\right\rangle\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ex\left[\one\{\tau_{\delta}\geq t\}\left\langle x_{t}-x^{\ast},U_{t}\right\rangle\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ex\left[\ex\left[\one\{\tau_{\delta}\geq t\}\left\langle x_{t}-x^{\ast},U_{t}\right\rangle\nonscript\,\middle|\nonscript\,\mathopen{}\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]\right]

(E.39)

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ex\left[\one\{\tau_{\delta}\geq t\}\left\langle x_{t}-x^{\ast},\ex\left[U_{t}\nonscript\,\middle|\nonscript\,\mathopen{}\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]\right\rangle\right]=0,

where we have used that both
x_{t}
and
\one\{\tau_{\delta}\geq t\}
are
\mathcal{F}_{t}
-measurable. Indeed, to prove the latter, one notes that

(E.40)

\{\tau_{\delta}\geq t\}=\intersect_{s=1}^{t-1}\{\lVert x_{s}-x^{\ast}\rVert>\delta\}\in\mathcal{F}_{t}

since all events
\{\lVert x_{s}-x^{\ast}\rVert>\delta\}
,

, belong to
\mathcal{F}_{t}
. Similarly, the bounded

-th central moment condition satisfied by
U_{t}
yields

\displaystyle\ex\left[\sum_{t=1}^{\tau_{\delta}\wedge T}\lVert U_{t}\rVert_{\ast}^{p}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ex\left[\one\{\tau_{\delta}\geq t\}\lVert U\rVert_{\ast}^{p}\right]

(E.41)

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ex\left[\one\{\tau_{\delta}\geq t\}\ex\left[\lVert U\rVert_{\ast}^{p}\nonscript\,|\nonscript\,\mathopen{}\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]\right]\leq\sigma^{p}\ex\left[\sum_{t=1}^{\tau_{\delta}\wedge T}1\right]=\sigma^{p}\ex\left[\tau_{\delta}\wedge T\right].

Moreover, by strong convexity of
f
and definition of
\tau_{\delta}
, we also get

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{\tau_{\delta}\wedge T}\left\langle x_{t}-x^{\ast},\nabla f(x_{t})\right\rangle

\displaystyle\geq\dfrac{\mu}{2}\sum_{t=1}^{\tau_{\delta}\wedge T}\lVert x_{t}-x^{\ast}\rVert^{2}

(E.42)

\displaystyle\geq\dfrac{\mu}{2}\sum_{t=1}^{\tau_{\delta}\wedge T-1}\lVert x_{t}-x^{\ast}\rVert^{2}\geq\dfrac{\mu}{2}\sum_{t=1}^{\tau_{\delta}\wedge T-1}\delta^{2}=\dfrac{\mu\delta^{2}}{2}(\tau_{\delta}\wedge T-1).

The previous computations lead to

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq E_{1}+f(x_{1})-\min f-\dfrac{\mu\delta^{2}}{2}(\ex\left[\tau_{\delta}\wedge T\right]-1)+\dfrac{2^{p-1}\lVert\mathcal{X}\rVert^{2-p}\eta^{p-1}\sigma^{p}}{pK^{p-1}}\ex\left[\tau_{\delta}\wedge T\right]

(E.43)

\displaystyle=E_{1}+f(x_{1})-\min f+\dfrac{\mu\delta^{2}}{2}-\left(\dfrac{\mu\delta^{2}}{2}-\dfrac{2^{p-1}\lVert\mathcal{X}\rVert^{2-p}\sigma^{p}\eta^{p-1}}{pK^{p-1}}\right)\ex\left[\tau_{\delta}\wedge T\right]\,,

where the multiplicative coefficient in front of
\ex\left[\tau_{\delta}\wedge T\right]
is (strictly) positive whenever the learning rate
\eta
satisfies

(E.44)

\eta<\left(\dfrac{\mu pK}{2^{p}K^{2-p}\lVert\mathcal{X}\rVert^{2-p}\sigma^{p}}\right)^{\frac{1}{p-1}}\delta^{\frac{2}{p-1}}.

Rearranging the terms therefore yields the upper bound

\displaystyle\ex\left[\tau_{\delta}\wedge T\right]

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{E_{1}+f(x_{1})-\min f+\frac{\mu\delta^{2}}{2}}{\frac{\mu\delta^{2}}{2}-\frac{2^{p-1}\lVert\mathcal{X}\rVert^{2-p}\sigma^{p}\eta^{p-1}}{pK^{p-1}}}

(E.45)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{2pKF(x^{\ast},\eta y_{1})+2pK\big(f(x_{1})-\min f+\frac{\mu\delta^{2}}{2}\big)\eta}{\mu pK\delta^{2}\eta-2^{p}K^{2-p}\lVert\mathcal{X}\rVert^{2-p}\sigma^{p}\eta^{p}}\,,

which proves
Eq.

E.33
when taking the monotone limit as
T\to\infty
.
Now let us assume
y_{1}=0
so that
F(x^{\ast},\eta y_{1})=h(x^{\ast})-h(x_{1})\leq H
, and choose

(E.46)

\eta=\left(\dfrac{\mu K}{2^{p}K^{2-p}\lVert\mathcal{X}\rVert^{2-p}\sigma^{p}}\right)^{\frac{1}{p-1}}\delta^{\frac{2}{p-1}}\,,

which is the learning rate maximizing the denominator of
Eq.

E.33
.
For notational convenience, we also define

\displaystyle c_{1}=2pKH,\,\,c_{2}=2pK\big(f(x_{1})-\min f+\frac{\mu\delta^{2}}{2}\big),\,\,c_{3}=\mu pK\delta^{2},\,\,c_{4}=(2\sigma)^{p}(K\lVert\mathcal{X}\rVert)^{2-p}.

In particular
\eta=[c_{3}/(pc_{4})]^{1/(p-1)}
and we therefore obtain from
Eq.

E.33
that

(E.47)

\displaystyle\ex\left[\tau_{\delta}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{c_{1}+c_{2}\eta}{c_{3}\eta-c_{4}\eta^{p}}

\displaystyle=\left[c_{3}\left(\dfrac{c_{3}}{pc_{4}}\right)^{\frac{1}{p-1}}-c_{4}\left(\dfrac{c_{3}}{pc_{4}}\right)^{\frac{p}{p-1}}\right]^{-1}\left[c_{1}+c_{2}\left(\dfrac{c_{3}}{pc_{4}}\right)^{\frac{1}{p-1}}\right]

\displaystyle=\left[\left(\dfrac{c_{3}^{p}}{pc_{4}}\right)^{\frac{1}{p-1}}\big(1-\frac{1}{p}\big)\right]^{-1}\left[c_{1}+c_{2}\left(\dfrac{c_{3}}{pc_{4}}\right)^{\frac{1}{p-1}}\right]

\displaystyle=\dfrac{p}{p-1}\left(\dfrac{pc_{4}}{c_{3}^{p}}\right)^{\frac{1}{p-1}}\left[c_{1}+c_{2}\left(\dfrac{c_{3}}{pc_{4}}\right)^{\frac{1}{p-1}}\right]

\displaystyle=\dfrac{p}{p-1}\left\{c_{1}\left(\dfrac{pc_{4}}{c_{3}^{p}}\right)^{\frac{1}{p-1}}+\dfrac{c_{2}}{c_{3}}\right\}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{p}{p-1}\left\{2pKH\left(\dfrac{p2^{p}K^{2-p}\lVert\mathcal{X}\rVert^{2-p}\sigma^{p}}{\mu^{p}p^{p}K^{p}\delta^{2p}}\right)^{\frac{1}{p-1}}+\dfrac{2pK\big(f(x_{1})-\min f+\frac{\mu\delta^{2}}{2}\big)}{\mu pK\delta^{2}}\right\}

(E.48)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{p}{p-1}\left\{\dfrac{2H}{\mu K}\left(\dfrac{2^{p}\lVert\mathcal{X}\rVert^{2-p}\sigma^{p}}{\mu}\right)^{\frac{1}{p-1}}\delta^{-\frac{2p}{p-1}}+\dfrac{2\big(f(x_{1})-\min f\big)}{\mu}\delta^{-2}+1\right\}\,,

which concludes our proof.
∎

We next consider objectives whose convexity is adapted to the geometry induced by the regularizer, namely objectives that are
strongly convex relative to
h
. Specifically, we assume that

(E.49)

f(x^{\prime})\geq f(x)+\langle\nabla f(x),x^{\prime}-x\rangle+\mu D(x^{\prime},x)

for all
x\in\mathcal{X}_{h}
and all
x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
.

We begin by establishing a convergence guarantee for constant learning rates, yielding a discrete-time analogue of
Eq.

D.80
. The result also slightly refines
Theorem

6
from the main text, as it no longer requires (
SDA
) to be initialized at the prox-center of
\mathcal{X}
.

Theorem E.6

(Convergence rate of (
SDA
) for relatively strongly convex functions, constant learning rate)
.

Let
h
be a steep regularizer. If
f
is relatively
L
-smooth and relatively
\mu
-strongly convex with minimum
x^{\ast}\in\mathcal{X}
, then running (
SDA
) with a constant learning rate
\eta\leq\frac{1}{pL}
achieves

(E.50)

\ex\left[\lVert x_{t}-x^{\ast}\rVert^{2}\right]\leq\dfrac{2^{p-1}\lVert\mathcal{X}\rVert^{2-p}}{p\mu K^{p}}\sigma^{p}\eta^{p-1}+\dfrac{2D(x^{\ast},x_{1})}{K}(1-\eta\mu)^{t-1}.

Proof.

Starting from
Lemmas

E.1
and
E.7
with
q=x^{\ast}
and then using the relative strong convexity of
f
, we readily get

\displaystyle\ex\left[E_{t+1}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\ex\left[E_{t}\right]+\ex\left[\left\langle x^{\ast}-x_{t},\nabla f(x_{t})\right\rangle\right]

\displaystyle\qquad+\ex\left[f(x_{t})-f(x_{t+1})\right]+\dfrac{2^{p-1}\lVert\mathcal{X}\rVert^{2-p}}{pK^{p-1}}\eta^{p-1}\sigma^{p}

\displaystyle\leq\ex\left[E_{t}\right]+\ex\left[f(x^{\ast})-f(x_{t})\right]-\mu\ex\left[D(x^{\ast},x_{t})\right]

(E.51)

\displaystyle\qquad+\ex\left[f(x_{t})-f(x_{t+1})\right]+\dfrac{2^{p-1}\lVert\mathcal{X}\rVert^{2-p}}{pK^{p-1}}\eta^{p-1}\sigma^{p}.

But since
h
is steep, we have
\eta E_{t}=F(x^{\ast},\eta y_{t})=D(x^{\ast},x_{t})
for all times
t\geq 1
, and thus

\displaystyle\ex\left[D(x^{\ast},x_{t+1})\right]

\displaystyle\leq(1-\eta\mu)\ex\left[D(x^{\ast},x_{t})\right]+\eta\ex\left[f(x^{\ast})-f(x_{t+1})\right]+\dfrac{2^{p-1}\lVert\mathcal{X}\rVert^{2-p}}{pK^{p-1}}\eta^{p}\sigma^{p}

(E.52)

\displaystyle\leq(1-\eta\mu)\ex\left[D(x^{\ast},x_{t})\right]+\dfrac{2^{p-1}\lVert\mathcal{X}\rVert^{2-p}}{pK^{p-1}}\eta^{p}\sigma^{p}\,,

where we have used in the second inequality that
x^{\ast}
is the minimum of
f
. Note in particular that
\eta\mu\leq 1
due to the assumption that
\eta\leq 1/(pL)
and the fact that
\mu\leq L
(cf.
[
63
, Proposition 1.1]
). By recurrence, we therefore get

(E.53)

\ex\left[D(x^{\ast},x_{t})\right]\leq(1-\eta\mu)^{t-1}D(x^{\ast},x_{1})+\dfrac{2^{p-1}\lVert\mathcal{X}\rVert^{2-p}}{p\mu K^{p-1}}\eta^{p-1}\sigma^{p}\,,

and it only remains to use the lower bound
\lVert x-x^{\prime}\rVert^{2}\leq\frac{2}{K}D(x,x^{\prime})
of
Proposition

B.2
to obtain the claimed inequality.
∎

Remark E.4
.

Due to
Eq.

E.50
, we note that the learning rate
\eta=\bigoh\left((\varepsilon/\sigma^{p-1})^{\frac{1}{p}}\right)
for any accuracy
\varepsilon>0
achieves the last iterate estimate
\ex\left[\lVert x_{t}-x^{\ast}\rVert^{2}\right]\leq\varepsilon
for all times
t\geq\Omega\left(\sigma^{\frac{p}{p-1}}\varepsilon^{-\frac{1}{p-1}}\log(1/\varepsilon)\right)
. This complexity rate is better than the one obtained for convex or strongly convex functions, and, as mentioned in the continuous-time section, this also recovers the optimal rate known for first-order stochastic methods when
p=2
.

Finally, when the learning rate sequence is proportional to
t^{-1/p}
, we leverage relative strong convexity together with a discrete-time Grönwall inequality to obtain the following convergence guarantee:

Theorem E.7

(Convergence rate of (
SDA
) for relatively strongly convex functions, decreasing learning rate)
.

Let
h
be a steep regularizer. If
f
is relatively
L
-smooth and relative
\mu
-strongly convex with minimum
x^{\ast}\in\mathcal{X}
, then running (
SDA
) with learning rate
\eta_{t}=\beta t^{-1/p}
where
\beta\leq\frac{1}{pL}
achieves

\displaystyle\ex\left[\lVert x_{t}-x^{\ast}\rVert^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{2}{\mu Kp}\left(\dfrac{H}{\beta}+\dfrac{2^{p-1}\beta^{p-1}\lVert\mathcal{X}\rVert^{2-p}}{K^{p-1}}\sigma^{p}\right)t^{-\frac{p-1}{p}}

(E.54)

\displaystyle+\dfrac{2D(x^{\ast},x_{1})}{K}t^{-\frac{1}{p}}\exp\left[-\dfrac{\mu\beta p}{p-1}\left(t^{\frac{p-1}{p}}-1\right)\right].

In particular,
x_{t}\to x^{\ast}
in mean square as
t\to\infty
.

Proof.

Following the same idea as in the proof of
Eq.

E.50
but with learning rate
\eta_{t}=\beta t^{-1/p}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\ex\left[E_{t+1}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\ex\left[E_{t}\right]+\left(\dfrac{1}{\eta_{t+1}}-\dfrac{1}{\eta_{t}}\right)H-\mu\ex\left[D(x^{\ast},x_{t})\right]+\dfrac{2^{p-1}\lVert\mathcal{X}\rVert^{2-p}}{pK^{p-1}}\eta^{p-1}\sigma^{p}

(E.55)

\displaystyle=(1-\mu\eta_{t})\ex\left[E_{t}\right]+\left((t+1)^{1/p}-t^{1/p}\right)\dfrac{H}{\beta}+\dfrac{2^{p-1}\lVert\mathcal{X}\rVert^{2-p}}{pK^{p-1}}\eta^{p-1}\sigma^{p}.

Let
\psi(x)=x^{1/p}
. Then, by the mean value theorem, there exists
\xi\in(t,t+1)
such that

(E.56)

(t+1)^{1/p}-t^{1/p}=\psi(t+1)-\psi(t)\leq\psi^{\prime}(\xi)=\frac{1}{p}\xi^{-\frac{p-1}{p}}\leq\frac{1}{p}t^{-\frac{p-1}{p}}=\frac{1}{p\beta^{p-1}}\eta_{t}^{p-1}.

This yields

(E.57)

\ex\left[E_{t+1}\right]\leq(1-\mu\eta_{t})\ex\left[E_{t}\right]+\dfrac{1}{p}\left(\dfrac{H}{\beta^{p}}+\dfrac{2^{p-1}\lVert\mathcal{X}\rVert^{2-p}\sigma^{p}}{K^{p-1}}\right)\eta_{t}^{p-1}.

Letting
C
be the constant in front of
\eta_{t}^{p-1}
and iterating the inequality for all
t=1,\dots,T-1
then leads to

(E.58)

\ex\left[E_{T}\right]\leq E_{1}\prod_{t=1}^{T-1}(1-\mu\eta_{t})+C\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}\eta_{t}^{p-1}\prod_{s=t+1}^{T-1}(1-\mu\eta_{s})\eqdef E_{1}S_{1,T}+CS_{2,T}.

To estimate
S_{1,T}
, we use the classic inequality
1-x\leq e^{-x}
and an integral bound to obtain

\displaystyle S_{1,T}

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{t=1}^{T-1}e^{-\mu\eta_{t}}=\exp\left(-\mu\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}\eta_{t}\right)=\exp\left(-\beta\mu\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}t^{-1/p}\right)

(E.59)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-\beta\mu\int_{1}^{T}t^{-1/p}dt\right)=\exp\left(-\dfrac{\mu\beta p}{p-1}\left(T^{\frac{p-1}{p}}-1\right)\right).

On the other hand, note that
\eta_{t}^{p-1}=\eta_{t}^{p-2}\eta_{t}\leq\eta_{T}^{p-2}\eta_{t}
for every
t=1,\dots,T-1
due to
p\leq 2
and the fact that
\eta_{t}
is a nonincreasing sequence. This allows us to write

\displaystyle S_{2,T}\leq\eta_{T}^{p-2}\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}\eta_{t}\prod_{s=t+1}^{T-1}(1-\mu\eta_{s})

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\eta_{T}^{p-2}}{\mu}\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}(1+\mu\eta_{t}-1)\prod_{s=t+1}^{T-1}(1-\mu\eta_{s})

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\eta_{T}^{p-2}}{\mu}\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}\left(\prod_{s=t+1}^{T-1}(1-\mu\eta_{s})-\prod_{s=t}^{T-1}(1-\mu\eta_{s})\right)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\eta_{T}^{p-2}}{\mu}\left(1-\prod_{s=1}^{T-1}(1-\mu\eta_{s})\right)

(E.60)

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{\eta_{T}^{p-2}}{\mu}.

Injecting the bounds for
S_{1,T}
and
S_{2,T}
back into
Eq.

E.58
and replacing
E_{T}
by its expression, it follows that

\displaystyle\ex\left[D(x^{\ast},X_{t})\right]=\eta_{t}\ex\left[E_{t}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{D(x^{\ast},X_{0})}{t^{1/p}}\exp\left(-\dfrac{\mu\beta p}{p-1}\left(t^{\frac{p-1}{p}}-1\right)\right)

(E.61)

\displaystyle+\dfrac{1}{\mu p}\left(\dfrac{H}{\beta}+\dfrac{2^{p-1}\beta^{p-1}\lVert\mathcal{X}\rVert^{2-p}}{K^{p-1}}\sigma^{p}\right)t^{-\frac{p-1}{p}}\,,

and the claim is therefore proved by using the inequality
\lVert x^{\ast}-X_{t}\rVert^{2}\leq\frac{2}{K}D(x^{\ast},X_{t})
.
∎

Remark E.5
.

Analogous to its associated continuous-time result (
Theorem

D.7
),
Theorem

E.7
only implies that (
SDA
) achieves a complexity rate of order
t=\Omega\left(\varepsilon^{-\frac{p}{p-1}}\right)
for learning rates
\eta(t)\propto t^{-1/p}
, which thus does not reach the optimal lower bound even for the tame regime
p=2
.

E.3.
Extension: Lazy Mirror Descent (
LMD
) under heavy-tailed noise

In this section, we extend our previous results to the stochastic mirror descent with lazy updates, described by the system

(LMD)

\displaystyle y_{t+1}

\displaystyle=y_{t}-\gamma_{t}g_{t}

\displaystyle x_{t+1}

\displaystyle=Q(y_{t+1})

Following the discussion in the main text, the key difference between (
LMD
) and (
SDA
) is that, in (
LMD
), gradient steps are
pre-multiplied
by a stepsize
\gamma_{t}
, whereas in (
SDA
) they are multiplied by a learning rate
\eta_{t}

after
aggregation.

As will become clear in the proofs, the convergence results for (
LMD
) under heavy-tailed noise rely on the same arguments as those used for (
SDA
) with a constant learning rate. In particular, we no longer need to introduce the
\eta
-deflated Fenchel coupling and can instead directly analyze the evolution of the Fenchel coupling along the iterates of (
LMD
):

Lemma E.3

(Three-point identity)
.

For every
q\in\mathcal{X}
, the iterates of (
LMD
) satisfy

(E.62)

F(q,y_{t+1})-F(q,y_{t})=\left\langle x_{t}-q,y_{t+1}-y_{t}\right\rangle+\left\langle x_{t+1}-x_{t},y_{t+1}-y_{t}\right\rangle-F(x_{t+1},y_{t}).

Proof.

On one hand, we have

(E.63)

F(x_{t+1},y_{t})-F(q,y_{t})=h(x_{t+1})-h(q)-\left\langle x_{t+1}-q,x_{t}\right\rangle\,,

and, on the other one, using the optimality of
x_{t+1}
,

(E.64)

F(q,y_{t+1})=h(q)+h^{\ast}(y_{t+1})-\left\langle q,y_{t+1}\right\rangle=h(q)-h(x_{t+1})+\left\langle x_{t+1}-q,y_{t+1}\right\rangle.

Combining both of these equalities therefore yields

\displaystyle F(q,y_{t+1})+F(x_{t+1},y_{t})-F(q,y_{t})

\displaystyle=\left\langle x_{t+1}-q,y_{t+1}-y_{t}\right\rangle

(E.65)

\displaystyle=\left\langle x_{t}-q,y_{t+1}-y_{t}\right\rangle+\left\langle x_{t+1}-x_{t},y_{t+1}-y_{t}\right\rangle\,,

hence proving the claim.
∎

Analogously to (
SDA
), leveraging the relative smoothness of the objective and the moment bounds on the gradient noise leads to the following essential estimate:

Lemma E.4

(Heavy-tailed noise estimate)
.

If
f
is relatively
L
-smooth and
\gamma_{t}\leq\frac{1}{pL}
, then trajectories of (
LMD
) satisfy

(E.66)

\left\langle x_{t+1}-x_{t},y_{t+1}-y_{t}\right\rangle-F(x_{t+1},y_{t})\leq\gamma_{t}\left[f(x_{t})-f(x_{t+1})\right]+\dfrac{2^{p-1}\lVert\mathcal{X}\rVert^{2-p}}{pK^{p-1}}\gamma_{t}^{p}\lVert U_{t}\rVert_{\ast}^{p}.

Proof.

Since
y_{t+1}-y_{t}=-\gamma_{t}g_{t}=-\gamma_{t}(\nabla f(x_{t})+U_{t})
, we have

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{\gamma_{t}}\left[\left\langle x_{t+1}-x_{t},y_{t+1}-y_{t}\right\rangle-F(x_{t+1},y_{t})\right]

\displaystyle=\left\langle\nabla f(x_{t}),x_{t}-x_{t+1}\right\rangle-\dfrac{1}{p\gamma_{t}}F(x_{t+1},y_{t})

(E.67)

\displaystyle+\left\langle U_{t},x_{t}-x_{t+1}\right\rangle-\dfrac{p-1}{p\gamma_{t}}F(x_{t+1},y_{t}),

and the rest of the proof can therefore be carried on by invoking the same computations as in
Eq.

E.7
.
∎

From the two lemmas above, we obtain the following theorem, providing the analogue of
Theorem

E.9
for (
LMD
).

Theorem E.8
.

If
f
is relative
L
-smooth, then for every
q\in\mathcal{X}
and every nonincreasing stepsize sequence with
\gamma_{1}\leq\frac{1}{pL}
, the iterates of (
LMD
) satisfy

(E.68)

\ex\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\gamma_{t}\left\langle x_{t}-q,\nabla f(x_{t})\right\rangle\right]\leq F(q,y_{1})+\gamma_{1}\left[f(x_{1})-\min f\right]+\dfrac{2^{p-1}\lVert\mathcal{X}\rVert^{2-p}\sigma^{p}}{pK^{p-1}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\gamma_{t}^{p}.

Proof.

After rearranging the terms,
Eqs.

E.62
and
E.66
yield

(E.69)

\gamma_{t}\left\langle x_{t}-q,g_{t}\right\rangle\leq F(q,y_{t})-F(q,y_{t+1})+\gamma_{t}\left[f(x_{t})-f(x_{t+1})\right]+\dfrac{2^{p-1}\lVert\mathcal{X}\rVert^{2-p}}{pK^{p-1}}\gamma_{t}^{p}\lVert U_{t}\rVert_{\ast}^{p}.

Summing over
t=1,\dots,T
and taking the overall expectation, we therefore get

\displaystyle\ex\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\gamma_{t}\left\langle x_{t}-q,\nabla f(x_{t})\right\rangle\right]\leq F(q,y_{1})-F(q,y_{t+1})

\displaystyle+\ex\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\gamma_{t}\left[f(x_{t})-f(x_{t+1})\right]\right]

(E.70)

\displaystyle+\dfrac{2^{p-1}\lVert\mathcal{X}\rVert^{2-p}\sigma^{p}}{pK^{p-1}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\gamma_{t}^{p}\,,

where we have used the blanket assumptions on
U_{t}
as in the proof of
Eq.

E.15
. Now, note that we can write

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\gamma_{t}\left[f(x_{t})-f(x_{t+1})\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\gamma_{t+1}f(x_{t+1})-\sum_{t=1}^{T}\gamma_{t}f(x_{t+1})

(E.71)

\displaystyle=\gamma_{1}f(x_{1})-\gamma_{T}f(x_{T+1})+\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}(\gamma_{t+1}-\gamma_{t})f(x_{t+1}),

but
\gamma_{t+1}-\gamma_{t}\leq 0
by assumption and
f(x_{t+1})\geq\min f
, so

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\gamma_{t}\left[f(x_{t})-f(x_{t+1})\right]

\displaystyle\leq\gamma_{1}f(x_{1})-\gamma_{T}f(x_{T+1})+\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}(\gamma_{t+1}-\gamma_{t})\min f

\displaystyle=\gamma_{1}\left[f(x_{1})-\min f\right]+\gamma_{T}\left[\min f-f(x_{T+1})\right]

(E.72)

\displaystyle\leq\gamma_{1}\left[f(x_{1})-\min f\right].

Moreover,
F(q,y_{t+1})\geq 0
by
Proposition

B.2
, and thus

(E.73)

\ex\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\gamma_{t}\left\langle x_{t}-q,\nabla f(x_{t})\right\rangle\right]\leq F(q,y_{1})+\gamma_{1}\left[f(x_{1})-\min f\right]+\dfrac{2^{p-1}\lVert\mathcal{X}\rVert^{2-p}\sigma^{p}}{pK^{p-1}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\gamma_{t}^{p}\,,

which concludes the proof.
∎

As a first consequence, we obtain a convergence guarantee of (
LMD
) for convex objectives that are smooth relative to
h
:

Theorem E.9

(Convergence rate of (
LMD
) for convex functions)
.

If
f
is convex and relative
L
-smooth, then for every nonincreasing stepsize sequence with
\gamma_{1}\leq\frac{1}{pL}
, the iterates of (
LMD
) satisfy

\displaystyle\ex\left[f(\tilde{x}_{T})-\min f\right]

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{F(\argmin f,y_{1})+\gamma_{1}\left[f(x_{1})-\min f\right]}{\sum_{t=1}^{T}\gamma_{t}}

(E.74)

\displaystyle+\dfrac{2^{p-1}\lVert\mathcal{X}\rVert^{2-p}\sigma^{p}}{pK^{p-1}}\dfrac{\sum_{t=1}^{T}\gamma_{t}^{p}}{\sum_{t=1}^{T}\gamma_{t}}\,,

where
\tilde{x}_{T}=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\gamma_{t}x_{t}/\sum_{t=1}^{T}\gamma_{t}
.
In particular, the choice
\gamma_{t}=\beta t^{-1/p}
with
\beta\leq\frac{1}{pL}
yields

\displaystyle\ex\left[f(\tilde{x}_{T})-\min f\right]

\displaystyle\lesssim\left(\frac{1}{\beta}F(\argmin f,y_{1})+f(x_{1})-\min f\right)T^{-\frac{p-1}{p}}

(E.75)

\displaystyle+\dfrac{2^{p-1}\beta^{p-1}\lVert\mathcal{X}\rVert^{2-p}\sigma^{p}}{pK^{p-1}}(\log T)T^{-\frac{p-1}{p}}.

Proof.

Let
q=x^{\ast}\in\argmin f
satisfying
F(x^{\ast},y_{1})=F(\argmin f,y_{1})
. Then, invoking the convexity of
f
yields

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\gamma_{t}\left\langle x_{t}-x^{\ast},\nabla f(x_{t})\right\rangle

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{t=1}^{T}\gamma_{t}\left[f(x_{t})-f(x^{\ast})\right]=\left[\dfrac{\sum_{t=1}^{T}\gamma_{t}f(x_{t})}{\sum_{t=1}^{T}\gamma_{t}}-\min f\right]\sum_{t=1}^{T}\gamma_{t}

(E.76)

\displaystyle\geq\left[f(\tilde{x}_{T})-\min f\right]\sum_{t=1}^{T}\gamma_{t},

and it only remains to apply
Eq.

E.15
and divide both sides by
\sum\nolimits_{t=1}^{T}\gamma_{t}
to obtain the result.
∎

Remark E.6
.

Theorem

E.9
also recovers exactly the convergence rates established by
77
and
107
for optimizing convex function with (
SMD
). Two points are worth emphasizing: (a) our result remains valid when
f
is only
relatively
smooth with respect to the regularizer, whereas both of the cited works assume smoothness relative to the standard Euclidean norm, thus making our setting more flexible—especially when
h
is steep; and (b) their convergence rates also hold for regularizers that are not strongly convex but merely
(1,p/(p-1))
-uniformly convex
, that is, if

(E.77)

D(x^{\prime},x)=h(x^{\prime})-h(x)-\left\langle\nabla h(x),x^{\prime}-x\right\rangle\geq\dfrac{p-1}{p}\lVert x-x^{\prime}\rVert^{\frac{p}{p-1}}\quad\text{for all }x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}_{h}.

Importantly,
Eq.

E.7
and, by extension,
Eq.

E.66
, can be easily extended to this setting, since we then directly obtain
F(x,y^{\prime})\geq\frac{p-1}{p}\lVert x-Q(y^{\prime})\rVert^{\frac{p}{p-1}}
by
Proposition

B.2
. Hence, all of our results remain valid when strong convexity is relaxed to uniform convexity of the regularizer.

Analogously to stochastic dual averaging, we can then derive convergence rates for the
last iterate
of (
LMD
) under relative strong convexity. For a constant stepsize schedule, this gives the following bound, which coincides with that of (
SDA
).

Theorem E.10

(Convergence rate of (
LMD
) for relatively strongly convex functions, constant stepsize)
.

Let
h
be a steep regularizer. If
f
is relatively
L
-smooth and relatively
\mu
-strongly convex with minimum
x^{\ast}\in\mathcal{X}
, then running (
LMD
) with constant stepsizes
\gamma\leq\frac{1}{pL}
achieves

(E.78)

\ex\left[\lVert x_{t}-x^{\ast}\rVert^{2}\right]\leq\dfrac{2^{p-1}\lVert\mathcal{X}\rVert^{2-p}}{p\mu K^{p}}\sigma^{p}\gamma^{p-1}+\dfrac{2D(x^{\ast},x_{1})}{K}(1-\gamma\mu)^{t-1}.

Proof.

Since the stepsizes are constant, this result follows from the exact same arguments and computations used to prove
Eq.

E.50
for (
SDA
) with constant learning rate.
∎

On the other hand, for a variable stepsize of order
1/t^{1/p}
we obtain the following convergence guarantee, which requires a slightly more refined non-asymptotic version of Chung’s lemma in order to be derived.

Theorem E.11

(Convergence rate of (
LMD
) for relatively strongly convex functions, decreasing stepsize)
.

Let
h
be a steep regularizer. If
f
is relatively
L
-smooth and relative
\mu
-strongly convex with minimum
x^{\ast}\in\mathcal{X}
, then running (
LMD
) with stepsizes
\gamma_{t}=\beta t^{-1/p}
where
\beta\leq\frac{1}{pL}
achieves the convergence rate

(E.79)

\ex\left[\lVert x_{t}-x^{\ast}\rVert^{2}\right]\leq\dfrac{2^{p+1}\beta^{p-1}\lVert\mathcal{X}\rVert^{2-p}\sigma^{p}}{\mu K^{p}}t^{-\frac{p-1}{p}}+\dfrac{2D(x^{\ast},x_{1})}{K}\exp\left(-\dfrac{\mu\beta}{2}t^{\frac{p-1}{p}}\right)

for all times
t\geq\left[\frac{2(p-1)}{p\mu\beta}\right]^{\frac{p}{p-1}}
. In particular,
x_{t}\to x^{\ast}
in mean square as
t\to\infty
.

Proof.

Following the same arguments as in the proof of
Theorem

E.7
but starting from
Eqs.

E.62
and
E.66
instead, we get

\displaystyle\ex\left[D(x^{\ast},x_{t+1})\right]

\displaystyle\leq\ex\left[D(x^{\ast},x_{t})\right]-\mu\gamma_{t}\ex\left[D(x^{\ast},x_{t})\right]+\dfrac{2^{p-1}\lVert\mathcal{X}\rVert^{2-p}\sigma^{p}}{pK^{p-1}}\gamma_{t}^{p}

(E.80)

\displaystyle=(1-\mu\gamma_{t})\ex\left[D(x^{\ast},x_{t})\right]+\dfrac{2^{p-1}\lVert\mathcal{X}\rVert^{2-p}\sigma^{p}}{pK^{p-1}}\gamma_{t}^{p}.

In particular, letting
\gamma_{t}=\beta t^{-1/p}
, we obtain

(E.81)

\ex\left[D(x^{\ast},x_{t+1})\right]\leq\left(1-\frac{\mu\beta}{t^{1/p}}\right)\ex\left[D(x^{\ast},x_{t})\right]+\dfrac{2^{p-1}\lVert\mathcal{X}\rVert^{2-p}\sigma^{p}\beta^{p}}{pK^{p-1}}\frac{1}{t}.

Defining
c=\mu\beta
and
d=2^{p-1}K^{1-p}\lVert\mathcal{X}\rVert^{2-p}\sigma^{p}\beta^{p}/momexp
, we can then use a non-asymptotic version of Chung’s lemma (cf.
[
41
, Theorem 24]
) to get that, for every
t\geq t_{0}\geq\left[\frac{2(p-1)}{pc}\right]^{p/(p-1)}
,

\displaystyle\ex\left[D(x^{\ast},x_{t})\right]

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{2d}{c}\left(t+t_{0}\right)^{-\frac{p-1}{p}}+D(x^{\ast},x_{1})\exp\left(-\dfrac{ct}{(t+t_{0})^{1/p}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{2d}{c}t^{-\frac{p-1}{p}}+D(x^{\ast},x_{1})\exp\left(-\dfrac{ct}{(2t)^{1/p}}\right)

(E.82)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{2d}{c}t^{-\frac{p-1}{p}}+D(x^{\ast},x_{1})\exp\left(-\frac{c}{2}t^{\frac{p-1}{p}}\right)\,,

and it only remains to use the upper bound
\lVert x_{t}-x^{\ast}\rVert^{2}\leq\frac{2}{K}D(x^{\ast},x_{t})
to prove the claimed convergence rate.
∎

Remark E.8
.

Analogous to the convergence rates, we obtain that for every target
\varepsilon>0
, the stepsize sequence
\gamma_{t}=\beta/t
achieves the accuracy
\ex\left[\lVert x_{t}-x^{\ast}\rVert^{2}\right]\leq\varepsilon
for every times

(E.84)

t=\begin{cases}\Omega\left(\varepsilon^{-\frac{1}{p-1}}\right)&\text{if }p<1+\beta\mu\\
\Omega\left(\left[\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\log\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)\right]\right)&\text{if }p=1+\beta\mu\\
\Omega\left(\varepsilon^{-\frac{1}{\beta\mu}}\right)&\text{if }p>1+\beta\mu.\end{cases}

In particular, the first two regimes—that is, when
p\leq 1+\beta\mu
—matches the complexity rate obtained with a optimally tuned constant stepsize. By contrast, when
p>1+\beta\mu
, the resulting complexity is always worse than that of the constant stepsize rule, although it remains sharper than the convex case as long as
p\leq 1+\frac{\beta\mu}{1-\beta\mu}
. This gap with the optimal lower bounds stems from our use of relative smoothness. Indeed, if one could take
\beta=1/\mu
, the complexity would become
\Omega(\varepsilon^{-1/(p-1)})
for all
p\in(1,2]
, thereby recovering the optimal lower bound for
p=2
and matching the constant-stepsize complexity when
p<2
. However, the argument used in
Eq.

E.66
additionally requires the condition
\beta\leq 1/(pL)

64

\Omega(1/\varepsilon)

on the stochastic oracle, showing that optimal rates are achievable in stochastic mirror descent under refined assumptions. Extending this approach to heavy-tailed noise is, however, nontrivial. In particular, we conjecture that matching the optimal convergence rate
