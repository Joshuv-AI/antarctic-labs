Information-Geometric Quantum Process Tomography of Single Qubit Systems
T. Koide, A. van de Venn

Abstract
We establish an exact information-geometric inequality that remains valid regardless of the underlying dynamics, encompassing both Markovian and non-Markovian evolutions within the mixed-state domain. This inequality can be viewed as an extension of thermodynamic speed limits, which are typically formulated as inequalities. For single qubits, we show that this inequality saturates into a strict equality because the density matrix belongs to the quantum exponential family, with the Pauli matrices serving as sufficient statistics. From a practical perspective, this identity enables a non-iterative linear regression approach to continuous-time quantum process tomography, bypassing the local minima issues common in non-linear optimization. We demonstrate the efficiency of this method by estimating the Hamiltonian and dissipation parameters of the Gorini-Kossakowski-Sudarshan-Lindblad (GKSL) master equation. Numerical simulations confirm the validity of this geometric estimator and highlight the necessity of error mitigation near the pure-state boundary where the inverse metric becomes singular.



I
Introduction

Information-theoretic approaches are fundamental in characterizing correlations, entanglement, and the complexity of quantum systems
nielsenbook
;
hayashibook
;
wildbook
;
watrousbook
.
Among these, information geometry offers a powerful perspective by employing geometric concepts such as metrics, curvature, and geodesics to analyze statistical models
Amari1985
;
AmariNagaoka2000
;
Ay2017
.
The utility of information geometry is most profound when the underlying probability distributions (or density matrix operators) belong to an exponential family, where geometric structures such as metrics and affine connections are well-established (at least in classical case).

A remarkable property of single-qubit systems is that their density matrix operators inherently form a quantum exponential family. Crucially, this geometric structure holds regardless of the specific details of the underlying dynamics, whether Markovian or non-Markovian, provided the time evolution is smooth and strictly confined to the interior of the Bloch sphere (i.e., mixed states).
Leveraging this inherent geometric property, we derive an information-geometric identity that is strictly satisfied by any single-qubit system.
We establish this identity based on the general principles of information geometry, specifically by projecting the time-evolution operator onto subspace spanned by the fluctuations of relevant observable.
Theoretically, this identity can be viewed as an exact geometric generalization of Thermodynamic Speed Limits (TSLs), a topic actively debated in recent quantum and stochastic thermodynamics
ItoDechant2020
;
Ito2018
;
Shiraishi2018
;
Nicholson2020
;
NishiyamaHasegawa2025
;
Funo2019
;
Hasegawa2020
;
Pires2016
;
Vu2023
.
While TSLs are typically formulated as inequalities bounding the rate of state evolution which are often based on the Cramér-Rao bound, our geometric projection allows us to refine these bounds into a strict equality for qubits.

Beyond its theoretical significance, this geometric identity provides a substantial practical advantage for parameter estimation in open quantum systems, specifically in continuous-time quantum process tomography.
Traditional approaches to quantum process tomography heavily rely on maximum likelihood estimation (MLE)

;
sacchi2001
;

Alternatively, linear regression offers a highly tractable and computationally efficient approach. However, while widely utilized in quantum state tomography

;

;

, its application to process tomography remains quite limited (see, e.g., Ref.
Yu2018

The primary drawback is that selecting an appropriate linearizing loss function for the optimization is highly nontrivial.
(Although Ref.
Dobrynin2025
employs MLE, it considers the linearization of the likelihood, allowing the approach to be regarded as a form of linear regression).

In this paper, we resolve this challenge by utilizing our exact geometric identity to construct a natural loss function for the linear regression approach.
Unlike classical probability distributions, where the Fisher information metric is typically regarded as unique, the quantum state space admits a family of monotone metrics

While the symmetric logarithmic derivative (SLD) metric is standard in quantum estimation theory and bounds the speed of dynamical evolution

Because the BKM metric corresponds to the Hessian of the exponential family potential, it uniquely enables the linearization of the inverse problem.
By translating our abstract geometric identity into a concrete equation of motion for the Bloch vector, we propose a non-iterative method to simultaneously extract the Hamiltonian and dissipation parameters of the Gorini-Kossakowski-Sudarshan-Lindblad (GKSL) master equation directly from time-series data.
As demonstrated through numerical simulations, this approach reduces the complex estimation task to a computationally efficient linear regression problem, offering a robust and accurate alternative.

II
Universal Information-Geometric Inequalities

In this section, we present the theoretical foundation of our approach. The central result for our subsequent analysis of qubit dynamics is the quantum information-geometric inequality derived in Sec.
II.1
. To elucidate our framework within the existing literature, we also discuss its classical analogue in Sec.
II.2
and provide physical interpretations in Sec.
II.3
, specifically highlighting its connection to the Cramér-Rao bound and thermodynamic speed limits.

II.1
Quantum Systems

We first consider a quantum system described by a density matrix operator
\hat{\rho}(\bm{\theta}(t))
, which depends on a set of
N
time-dependent parameters
\bm{\theta}(t)=(\theta^{1}(t),\theta^{2}(t),\cdots,\theta^{N}(t))
.
The expectation value of an arbitrary time-independent operator
\hat{A}
is given by:

\langle\hat{A}\rangle=\mathrm{Tr}[\hat{\rho}(\bm{\theta}(t))\hat{A}]\,.

(1)

We assume that the density matrix operator is strictly positive (i.e., full-rank, having no zero eigenvalues). This assumption is necessary because, for pure states, the logarithm
\ln\hat{\rho}
diverges, making the score operator ill-defined. Within this strictly positive domain, the density matrix operator can be written as
\hat{\rho}(\bm{\theta}(t))=e^{\hat{H}(\bm{\theta}(t))}
with a self-adjoint operator
\hat{H}(\bm{\theta}(t))
. The time derivative of the density matrix operator is expressed using the total score operator
\hat{\mathbb{L}}
:

\frac{d\hat{\rho}}{dt}=\int_{0}^{1}ds\,\hat{\rho}^{s}\hat{\mathbb{L}}\hat{\rho}^{1-s}\,,

(2)

where the total score operator is defined as the time derivative of the logarithm of the density matrix operator:

\hat{\mathbb{L}}:=\frac{d}{dt}\hat{H}(\bm{\theta}(t))=\dot{\theta}^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}\hat{H}(\bm{\theta}(t))\,,

(3)

with
\partial_{\mu}=\partial/\partial\theta^{\mu}
, and Greek indices running from
1
to
N
.

Substituting this into the time derivative of the expectation value yields:

\frac{d}{dt}\langle\hat{A}\rangle=\mathrm{Tr}\left[\frac{d\hat{\rho}}{dt}\hat{A}\right]=\langle\hat{A},\hat{\mathbb{L}}\rangle_{cc}=\langle\delta\hat{A},\hat{\mathbb{L}}\rangle_{cc}\,.

(4)

Here, we introduce the Kubo-Mori canonical correlation for two operators
\hat{X}
and
\hat{Y}

Mori1965
:

\langle\hat{X},\hat{Y}\rangle_{cc}:=\int_{0}^{1}ds\,\mathrm{Tr}[\hat{\rho}^{s}\hat{X}\hat{\rho}^{1-s}\hat{Y}]\,.

(5)

This canonical correlation satisfies the axioms of an inner product, and thus the space of operators equipped with
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{cc}
forms a Hilbert space.
In the last equality,
\delta\hat{A}=\hat{A}-\langle\hat{A}\rangle
represents the fluctuation of
\hat{A}
, and we utilized the property
\langle\hat{I},\hat{\mathbb{L}}\rangle_{cc}=\mathrm{Tr}[d\hat{\rho}/dt]=0
.

The geometry of the parameter space is characterized by the BKM metric
g_{\mu\nu}(\bm{\theta})
, defined as:

g_{\mu\nu}(\bm{\theta}):=\langle\partial_{\mu}\hat{H},\partial_{\nu}\hat{H}\rangle_{cc}\,.

(6)

The interpretation of
g_{\mu\nu}(\bm{\theta})
as a metric measuring the distinguishability of quantum states will be detailed later.
The squared norm of the total score operator thus corresponds to:

\langle\hat{\mathbb{L}},\hat{\mathbb{L}}\rangle_{cc}=g_{\mu\nu}(\bm{\theta})\dot{\theta}^{\mu}\dot{\theta}^{\nu}\,.

(7)

Let us now introduce a set of
n(\leq N)
relevant observables
\hat{F}=(\hat{F}_{1},\dots,\hat{F}_{n})^{T}
, defining the covariance matrix
\Xi(\bm{\theta})
for their fluctuations:

\Xi_{ij}(\bm{\theta}):=\langle\delta\hat{F}_{i},\delta\hat{F}_{j}\rangle_{cc}\,.

(8)

We approximately express the total score operator
\hat{\mathbb{L}}
by projecting it onto the subspace spanned by these fluctuations
\{\delta\hat{F}_{k}\}
:

\tilde{\mathbb{L}}=C^{i}\delta\hat{F}_{i}\,,

(9)

where the Latin index runs from
1
to
n(\leq N)
.
The coefficients
C^{i}
are determined by the orthogonality condition,

\langle\hat{e},\delta\hat{F}_{i}\rangle_{cc}=0\,,

(10)

where the residual score is defined by
\hat{e}:=\hat{\mathbb{L}}-\tilde{\mathbb{L}}
. This yields the following relation:

C=\Xi^{-1}(\bm{\theta})\frac{d}{dt}\langle\hat{F}\rangle\,,

(11)

where
C:=(C^{1},\cdots,C^{n})^{T}
,
[\Xi]_{ij}:=\Xi_{ij}
, and
\langle\hat{F}\rangle:=(\langle\hat{F}^{1}\rangle,\cdots,\langle\hat{F}^{n}\rangle)^{T}
.

Based on the Pythagorean theorem in the operator space defined by the canonical correlation, we have the orthogonal decomposition:

\langle\hat{\mathbb{L}},\hat{\mathbb{L}}\rangle_{cc}=\langle\tilde{\mathbb{L}},\tilde{\mathbb{L}}\rangle_{cc}+\langle\hat{e},\hat{e}\rangle_{cc}\geq\langle\tilde{\mathbb{L}},\tilde{\mathbb{L}}\rangle_{cc}\,,

(12)

owing to the non-negativity of the norm
\langle\hat{e},\hat{e}\rangle_{cc}\geq 0
.
Substituting the explicit forms into this relation, we arrive at the Quantum Information-Geometric Inequality:

\left(\frac{d}{dt}\langle\hat{F}\rangle\right)^{T}\Xi^{-1}(\bm{\theta})\frac{d}{dt}\langle\hat{F}\rangle\leq g_{\mu\nu}(\bm{\theta})\dot{\theta}^{\mu}\dot{\theta}^{\nu}\,.

(13)

The equality holds if and only if the relevant observables
\hat{F}
completely capture the time evolution of the system (i.e., the residual
\hat{e}
vanishes, meaning
\hat{F}
constitutes sufficient statistics).
Crucially, this inequality is derived solely from the geometric projection in the operator space and does not depend on the specific details of the system’s dynamics. It is universally satisfied for any Markovian or non-Markovian time evolution.

Finally, it is worth noting that approximately replacing the time evolution using a limited set of observables aligns with the philosophy of Mori’s projection operator formalism
Mori1965
.
In that framework, the projection captures the coarse-grained dynamics of relevant variables, while the orthogonal component which corresponds to our residual score
\hat{e}
is interpreted as the fluctuating (random) force. Dissipation is then expressed as the correlation of these fluctuating forces through the fluctuation-dissipation theorem of the second kind.
Physically, the left-hand side of Eq. (
13
) represents the “coarse-grained” speed of the system observed through
\hat{F}
, while the right-hand side represents the “microscopic (exact)” information-geometric speed.
The inequality implies that coarse-graining always underestimates the intrinsic speed of state evolution.

We introduce the relative entropy as a measure of the distance between two states
\hat{\rho}(\bm{\theta}(t))
and
\hat{\rho}(\bm{\theta}(t+dt))
:

D_{RE}[\hat{\rho}(\bm{\theta}(t))||\hat{\rho}(\bm{\theta}(t+dt))]:=\mathrm{Tr}\left[\hat{\rho}(\bm{\theta}(t))(\ln\hat{\rho}(\bm{\theta}(t))-\ln\hat{\rho}(\bm{\theta}(t+dt)))\right]\,.

(14)

Expanding the relative entropy up to the second order in
dt
, and using the geometric constraints
\langle\hat{\mathbb{L}}\rangle=0
and
\langle\dot{\hat{\mathbb{L}}}\rangle=-\langle\hat{\mathbb{L}},\hat{\mathbb{L}}\rangle_{cc}
, we obtain:

g_{\mu\nu}(\bm{\theta})\dot{\theta}^{\mu}\dot{\theta}^{\nu}=\lim_{dt\to 0}\frac{2D_{RE}[\hat{\rho}(\bm{\theta}(t))||\hat{\rho}(\bm{\theta}(t+dt))]}{(dt)^{2}}\,.

(15)

Consequently, the general inequality (
13
) can be rewritten in terms of the relative entropy as:

\left(\frac{d}{dt}\langle\hat{F}\rangle\right)^{T}\Xi^{-1}(\bm{\theta})\frac{d}{dt}\langle\hat{F}\rangle\leq\lim_{dt\to 0}\frac{2D_{RE}[\hat{\rho}(\bm{\theta}(t))||\hat{\rho}(\bm{\theta}(t+dt))]}{(dt)^{2}}\,.

(16)

This relation establishes that the coarse-grained evolution rate is fundamentally bounded by the intrinsic information-geometric rate.

II.2
Classical Systems

The universal inequality (
16
) derived for quantum systems has a direct counterpart in classical stochastic systems.
Let us consider a probability distribution
p(\bm{x};\bm{\theta}(t))
defined over a state
\bm{x}
, parametrized by
N
time-dependent parameters
\bm{\theta}(t)=(\theta^{1}(t),\dots,\theta^{N}(t))
.

The geometry of the parameter space is governed by the classical Fisher information metric
g_{ij}(\bm{\theta})
, defined as:

g_{ij}(\bm{\theta})=\int d\bm{x}\,p(\bm{x};\bm{\theta})\frac{\partial\ln p(\bm{x};\bm{\theta})}{\partial\theta^{i}}\frac{\partial\ln p(\bm{x};\bm{\theta})}{\partial\theta^{j}}:=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\theta}}[(\partial_{i}\ln p)(\partial_{j}\ln p)]\,.

(17)

We define the classical total score function
\mathbb{L}(\bm{x},t)
as the logarithmic time derivative of the probability density:

\mathbb{L}(\bm{x},t):=\frac{d}{dt}\ln p(\bm{x};\bm{\theta}(t))=\dot{\theta}^{i}\partial_{i}\ln p(\bm{x};\bm{\theta}(t))\,.

(18)

Note that
\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\theta}}[\mathbb{L}]=0
due to probability conservation.

Now, consider
n(\leq N)
relevant observables
\bm{f}=(f_{1},\dots,f_{n})^{T}
. Let
\Xi(\bm{\theta})
be the covariance matrix of their fluctuations
\delta f_{i}:=f_{i}-\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\theta}}[f_{i}]
:

\Xi_{ij}(\bm{\theta}):=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\theta}}[\delta f_{i}\delta f_{j}]\,.

(19)

We project the total score function
\mathbb{L}
onto the subspace spanned by these fluctuations:

\tilde{\mathbb{L}}=C^{i}\delta f_{i}\,.

(20)

The coefficients
C^{i}
are determined by the orthogonality condition
\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\theta}}[(\mathbb{L}-\tilde{\mathbb{L}})\delta f_{j}]=0
, which yields

C=\Xi^{-1}(\bm{\theta})\frac{d}{dt}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\theta}}[\bm{f}]\,.

(21)

By applying the Pythagorean theorem
\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\theta}}[\mathbb{L}^{2}]=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\theta}}[\tilde{\mathbb{L}}^{2}]+\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\theta}}[(\mathbb{L}-\tilde{\mathbb{L}})^{2}]\geq\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\theta}}[\tilde{\mathbb{L}}^{2}]
, we arrive at the classical inequality:

\left(\frac{d}{dt}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\theta}}[\bm{f}]\right)^{T}\Xi^{-1}(\bm{\theta})\frac{d}{dt}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\theta}}[\bm{f}]\leq g_{ij}(\bm{\theta})\dot{\theta}^{i}\dot{\theta}^{j}\,.

(22)

As is well-known in information geometry
Amari1985
;
AmariNagaoka2000
;
Ay2017
, the right-hand side can be expressed in terms of the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence:

g_{ij}(\bm{\theta})\dot{\theta}^{i}\dot{\theta}^{j}=\lim_{dt\rightarrow 0}\frac{2D_{KL}[p(\bm{x};\bm{\theta}(t))||p(\bm{x};\bm{\theta}(t+dt))]}{(dt)^{2}}\,,

(23)

where

D_{KL}[A(\bm{x})||B(\bm{x})]=\int d\bm{x}\,A(\bm{x})\ln\frac{A(\bm{x})}{B(\bm{x})}\,.

(24)

This inequality formally states that the rate of change of macroscopic observables is bounded by the intrinsic information-geometric rate of the underlying probability distribution.

II.3
Physical and Statistical Interpretations

The inequalities derived above, Eq. (
16
) and Eq. (
22
), can be understood as dynamic generalizations of the Cramér-Rao bound in estimation theory.
Consider the scenario where we wish to estimate a single scalar parameter
\theta(t)
using a single observable estimator
f(\bm{x})
(
n=1
in Eq. (
22
)).
Assume that
f(\bm{x})
is an unbiased estimator of the parameter, i.e.,
\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[f]=\theta
.
In this case, the time derivative of the expectation value is simply the speed of the parameter itself:

\frac{d}{dt}\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[f]=\dot{\theta}\,.

(25)

The covariance matrix
\Xi
then reduces to the variance of the estimator,
\Xi=\text{Var}(f)
, and the Fisher metric
g_{\mu\nu}\dot{\theta}^{\mu}\dot{\theta}^{\nu}
simplifies to
I(\theta)\dot{\theta}^{2}
, where
I(\theta)
is the Fisher information.
Substituting these into the inequality (
22
), we obtain:

\frac{(\dot{\theta})^{2}}{\text{Var}(f)}\leq I(\theta)(\dot{\theta})^{2}\,.

(26)

Assuming
\dot{\theta}\neq 0
, this immediately yields the standard Cramér-Rao bound:

\text{Var}(f)\geq\frac{1}{I(\theta)}\,.

(27)

This result confirms that our information-geometric inequality is intrinsically linked to the precision of parameter estimation. The “squared norm” on the right-hand side of our universal inequalities sets the fundamental bound on the rate at which any physical observable can evolve.

Furthermore, these inequalities are deeply connected to the concept of Thermodynamic Speed Limits (TSLs), a subject of extensive recent study
ItoDechant2020
;
Ito2018
;
Shiraishi2018
;
Nicholson2020
;
NishiyamaHasegawa2025
;
Funo2019
;
Hasegawa2020
;
Pires2016
;
Vu2023
.
In the classical regime, our derived bound, Eq. (
22
), is formally equivalent to the speed limits based on Fisher information discussed by Ito and Dechant
Ito2018
;
ItoDechant2020
, and is closely related to the limits imposed by entropy production and dynamical activity derived by Shiraishi
et al.

Shiraishi2018
and Nicholson
et al.

Nicholson2020
.
It is worth emphasizing that while TSLs are often discussed by taking the Cramér-Rao inequality as a phenomenological starting point, our approach derives them explicitly from a geometric identity based on mathematical projection.
This suggests that these dynamic bounds are intrinsic geometric features of state evolution, existing independently of any specific estimation context.

While the geometric interpretation of information is uniquely established for classical systems, the quantum case admits a family of metrics, as mentioned in the Introduction.
Standard quantum estimation theory typically employs the SLD metric to define the ultimate theoretical bound on estimation precision
AmariNagaoka2000
;
petz1996
;
helstrom1976
.
In contrast, our geometric projection method naturally yields a Cramér-Rao-type inequality characterized by the BKM metric.
We adopt this BKM formulation for its unique structural properties rather than for achieving the tightest possible estimation bound.
As shown by Scandi and Perarnau-Llobet
Scandi2019
(see also Ref.
Dechant2022
), the BKM metric is the natural geometric measure for describing thermodynamic length and dissipation in open quantum systems within the linear response regime.
Crucially for our practical purpose, the BKM metric corresponds to the Hessian of the potential in the exponential family. This mathematical property uniquely simplifies the complex inverse problem of parameter estimation into a highly tractable linear regression (as will be demonstrated in Sec.
III.2
).
Attempting to use the SLD metric, on the other hand, would entangle the equations in a highly non-linear optimization problem, making the analytical and numerical extraction of parameters vastly more difficult.

Remarkably, if a physical system inherently satisfies the equality condition (i.e., the residual vanishes unconditionally,
\hat{e}=0
), this inequality becomes an exact geometric identity. In such scenarios, the identity offers practical utility, enabling parameter estimation. In the subsequent sections, we demonstrate that the open single-qubit system is exactly such a case, and we develop a robust parameter estimation method leveraging this exact identity.

III
Exact Geometric Identity for Single Qubit Systems

In this section, we apply our general framework to a single qubit system. While the relations derived in the previous section are inequalities due to the potential loss of information during the projection step, we show here that for a single qubit, this inequality becomes a strict identity.
Because the Pauli matrices provide sufficient statistics for the quantum exponential family of a qubit, they capture all information about the state evolution without any loss.

III.1
Derivation of the Universal Identity

The state of a single qubit is conventionally described using the Bloch vector representation:

\hat{\rho}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathbb{I}_{2}+\sum_{\mu=1}^{3}a_{\mu}\sigma_{\mu}\right)\,,

(28)

where
a_{\mu}=\mathrm{Tr}[\hat{\rho}\sigma_{\mu}]
corresponds to the expectation values of the Pauli matrices. The vector
\bm{a}=(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3})^{T}
resides within the unit ball in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, which is defined by the standard Euclidean norm
|\bm{a}|^{2}:=\sum_{\mu=1}^{3}(a_{\mu})^{2}\leq 1
.
This space is naturally equipped with the standard Euclidean metric
\delta_{\mu\nu}
.
In the following discussion, whenever we use vector notation such as
|\bm{a}|
or
\bm{a}\times\bm{b}
, it implies operations with respect to this Euclidean geometry.

From the perspective of information geometry, however, the set of mixed states of a single qubit forms a smooth statistical manifold equipped with the BKM metric
g_{\mu\nu}
. This metric quantifies state distinguishability and governs the information-geometric cost of transitions.
The qubit state belongs to a quantum exponential family where the density matrix operator in Eq. (
28
) can be reexpressed in the following canonical exponential form:

\hat{\rho}(\bm{\theta})=\exp\left(\theta^{\mu}\sigma_{\mu}-\psi(\bm{\theta})\right)\,,

(29)

where
\theta^{\mu}
are the natural parameters given by

\theta^{\mu}=\frac{\text{arctanh}(|\bm{a}|)}{|\bm{a}|}a_{\mu}\,,

(30)

and the potential
\psi(\bm{\theta})
is determined from the normalization condition of the density matrix operator,
\mathrm{Tr}[\hat{\rho}]=1
, yielding

\psi(\bm{\theta})=\ln\mathrm{Tr}\left[\exp\left(\theta^{\mu}\sigma_{\mu}\right)\right]\,.

(31)

Within the framework of the quantum exponential family, the Pauli matrices
\bm{\sigma}
constitute the sufficient statistics of the distribution, where
N=n=3
.
Consequently, the relevant observables
\hat{F}=\bm{\sigma}
capture all information about the state evolution. The residual score
\hat{e}
in the derivation of the general inequality (
13
) vanishes identically because the total score operator
\hat{\mathbb{L}}
is completely contained in the subspace spanned by the fluctuation of the sufficient statistics defined by
\sigma_{\mu}
. Thus, for any single qubit system, whether the dynamics are Markovian or non-Markovian, the inequality is replaced by a strict equality:

\left(\frac{d}{dt}\langle\bm{\sigma}\rangle\right)^{T}\Xi^{-1}(\bm{\theta})\frac{d}{dt}\langle\bm{\sigma}\rangle=\lim_{dt\to 0}\frac{2D_{RE}[\hat{\rho}(\bm{\theta}(t))||\hat{\rho}(\bm{\theta}(t+dt))]}{(dt)^{2}}\,.

(32)

In this case, the BKM metric
g_{\mu\nu}
, which coincides with the canonical covariance matrix
\Xi_{\mu\nu}(\bm{\theta})
, can be calculated explicitly:

g_{\mu\nu}(\bm{\theta})=\Xi_{\mu\nu}(\bm{\theta})=\frac{|\bm{a}|}{\text{arctanh}(|\bm{a}|)}\left(\delta_{\mu\nu}-\frac{a_{\mu}a_{\nu}}{|\bm{a}|^{2}}\right)+(1-|\bm{a}|^{2})\frac{a_{\mu}a_{\nu}}{|\bm{a}|^{2}}\,.

(33)

This metric tensor fundamentally characterizes the geometry of the qubit state space.
Using this explicit form, we arrive at the universal information-geometric equality for a single qubit:

\frac{1}{1-|\bm{a}|^{2}}\left(\frac{d|\bm{a}|}{dt}\right)^{2}+\frac{\text{arctanh}(|\bm{a}|)}{|\bm{a}|^{3}}|\bm{a}\times\dot{\bm{a}}|^{2}=\lim_{dt\to 0}\frac{2D_{RE}[\hat{\rho}(\bm{\theta}(t))||\hat{\rho}(\bm{\theta}(t+dt))]}{(dt)^{2}}\,,

(34)

where we utilized the inverse matrix

\Xi^{-1}_{\mu\nu}(\bm{\theta})=g^{-1}_{\mu\nu}(\bm{\theta})=\frac{\text{arctanh}(|\bm{a}|)}{|\bm{a}|}\left(\delta_{\mu\nu}-\frac{a_{\mu}a_{\nu}}{|\bm{a}|^{2}}\right)+\frac{1}{1-|\bm{a}|^{2}}\frac{a_{\mu}a_{\nu}}{|\bm{a}|^{2}}\,.

(35)

The two terms on the left-hand side of Eq. (
34
) have distinct physical meanings. The first term, proportional to
(d|\bm{a}|/dt)^{2}
, corresponds to the radial contribution associated with the change in purity.
Conversely, the second term, proportional to
|\bm{a}\times\dot{\bm{a}}|^{2}
, represents the angular contribution associated with the rotation of the state.
Due to the divergent factor
1/(1-|\bm{a}|^{2})
near
|\bm{a}|\longrightarrow 1
, the first term indicates that nearly pure states are geometrically rigid and difficult to control precisely, since an infinite information-geometric cost is associated with time evolutions touching the boundary.
We emphasize that this identity is universal and holds for any time evolution of a single qubit, regardless of the physical mechanism driving the dynamics. It applies not only to standard Markovian master equations but also to non-Markovian evolution, provided the evolution is described by a differentiable trajectory of mixed states.

III.2
Application to GKSL Dynamics

Let us now apply this identity to a single qubit system governed by the GKSL equation.
Suppose the system Hamiltonian is given by
\hat{H}=\bm{e}\cdot\bm{\sigma}
with a constant vector
\bm{e}
.
The dynamics of the Bloch vector is then given by

\dot{\bm{a}}=2\bm{e}\times\bm{a}-2D_{r}\bm{a}\,,

(36)

where
D_{r}=\operatorname{diag}(d_{1},d_{2},d_{3})
is the dissipation constant matrix characterizing the interaction with the environment.
Here, we assume for simplicity that the affine term defined in Appendix
A
vanishes, namely
\bm{c}=\bm{0}
, which implies that the system simply relaxes toward the maximally mixed state, meaning
\bm{a}\to\bm{0}
.

Substituting this Euclidean equation of motion into the left-hand side of Eq. (
34
), we obtain:

\frac{4(\bm{a}^{T}D_{r}\bm{a})^{2}}{|\bm{a}|^{2}(1-|\bm{a}|^{2})}+\frac{4\text{arctanh}(|\bm{a}|)}{|\bm{a}|^{3}}\left||\bm{a}|^{2}\bm{e}_{\perp}-\bm{a}\times(D_{r}\bm{a})\right|^{2}=\lim_{dt\to 0}\frac{2D_{RE}[\hat{\rho}(\bm{\theta}(t))||\hat{\rho}(\bm{\theta}(t+dt))]}{(dt)^{2}},

(37)

where we have defined the orthogonal component
\bm{e}_{\perp}=\bm{e}-\frac{(\bm{a}\cdot\bm{e})}{|\bm{a}|^{2}}\bm{a}
.

IV
Parameter Estimation Method for GKSL Dynamics

In this section, we propose a method to estimate the Hamiltonian vector
\bm{e}
and the dissipation matrix
D_{r}
from the time-series data of the Bloch vector
\bm{a}(t)
, utilizing the information-geometric framework established above.

IV.1
Loss Function Obtained from Quantum Information-Geometric Equality

Consider an experimentally observed time series of the Bloch vector
\bm{a}(t)
.
We assume that the system dynamics are governed by the GKSL equation, which is characterized by the Hamiltonian vector
\bm{e}
and the dissipation matrix
D_{r}=\operatorname{diag}(d_{1},d_{2},d_{3})
.
Ideally, this time evolution must strictly satisfy the information-geometric identities derived in the previous sections.
By equating the left-hand sides of Eqs. (
34
) and (
37
), we obtain the following relation linking the microscopic parameters to the observed kinematics:

\frac{4(\bm{a}^{T}D_{r}\bm{a})^{2}}{|\bm{a}|^{2}(1-|\bm{a}|^{2})}+\frac{4\operatorname{arctanh}(|\bm{a}|)}{|\bm{a}|^{3}}\left||\bm{a}|^{2}\bm{e}_{\perp}-\bm{a}\times(D_{r}\bm{a})\right|^{2}=\frac{1}{1-|\bm{a}|^{2}}\left(\frac{d|\bm{a}|}{dt}\right)^{2}+\frac{\operatorname{arctanh}(|\bm{a}|)}{|\bm{a}|^{3}}|\bm{a}\times\dot{\bm{a}}|^{2}\,.

(38)

We define the residual velocity vector
\Delta\bm{v}
as the difference between the observed velocity
\dot{\bm{a}}_{\text{ob}}(t)
and the theoretical velocity predicted by the GKSL model
\bm{v}_{\text{model}}(t)
:

\Delta\bm{v}(t):=\dot{\bm{a}}_{\text{ob}}(t)-\bm{v}_{\text{model}}(t)\,,

(39)

where

\bm{v}_{\text{model}}(t)=2\bm{e}\times\bm{a}(t)-2D_{r}\bm{a}(t)\,.

(40)

A crucial feature of the GKSL dynamics is that Eq. (
40
) is linear with respect to the unknown parameters.
Let us define the parameter vector
\bm{p}
containing the components of the Hamiltonian vector and the diagonal elements of the dissipation matrix:

\bm{p}=(e_{1},e_{2},e_{3},d_{1},d_{2},d_{3})^{T}\,.

(41)

The model velocity (
40
) can then be expressed as a linear transformation of
\bm{p}
:

\bm{v}_{\text{model}}(t)=H_{t}\bm{p}\,,

(42)

where the matrix
H_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 6}
depends only on the instantaneous state
\bm{a}(t)=(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3})^{T}
:

H_{t}=\begin{pmatrix}0&2a_{3}(t)&-2a_{2}(t)&-2a_{1}(t)&0&0\\
-2a_{3}(t)&0&2a_{1}(t)&0&-2a_{2}(t)&0\\
2a_{2}(t)&-2a_{1}(t)&0&0&0&-2a_{3}(t)\end{pmatrix}\,.

(43)

In Eq. (
38
), only the right-hand side contains the velocity
\dot{\bm{a}}
. This velocity can be expressed as
\dot{\bm{a}}=\bm{v}_{\text{model}}(t)+\Delta\bm{v}(t)
, keeping in mind the relation
d|\bm{a}|/dt=(\bm{a}\cdot\dot{\bm{a}})/|\bm{a}|
.
Equation (
38
) can thus be reexpressed as:

\frac{4(\bm{a}^{T}D_{r}\bm{a})^{2}}{|\bm{a}|^{2}(1-|\bm{a}|^{2})}+\frac{4\operatorname{arctanh}(|\bm{a}|)}{|\bm{a}|^{3}}\left||\bm{a}|^{2}\bm{e}_{\perp}-\bm{a}\times(D_{r}\bm{a})\right|^{2}=(\bm{v}_{\text{model}}+\Delta\bm{v})^{T}G^{-1}(\bm{a})(\bm{v}_{\text{model}}+\Delta\bm{v})\,,

(44)

where
G^{-1}(\bm{a})
is the matrix representation of the inverse BKM metric
g^{\mu\nu}(\bm{\theta})
.
In the ideal case without experimental noise, the left-hand side simply equals
\bm{v}_{\text{model}}^{T}G^{-1}(\bm{a})\bm{v}_{\text{model}}
. Expanding the right-hand side then leads to the following relation:

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=(\bm{v}_{\text{model}}+\Delta\bm{v})^{T}G^{-1}(\bm{a})(\bm{v}_{\text{model}}+\Delta\bm{v})-\bm{v}_{\text{model}}^{T}G^{-1}(\bm{a})\bm{v}_{\text{model}}

\displaystyle=2\bm{v}_{\text{model}}^{T}G^{-1}(\bm{a})\Delta\bm{v}+\Delta\bm{v}^{T}G^{-1}(\bm{a})\Delta\bm{v}\,.

(45)

Therefore, the true parameters of the GKSL equation correspond to the minimum of a positive-definite quantity
\mathcal{J}_{2}
, which we choose as the loss function for our optimization:

{\cal J}_{2}:=\Delta\bm{v}^{T}G^{-1}(\bm{a})\Delta\bm{v}\,.

(46)

Because
G^{-1}(\bm{a})
is positive-definite, finding parameters that yield
\mathcal{J}_{2}=0
strictly guarantees
\Delta\bm{v}=\bm{0}
. Consequently, the first term of Eq. (
45
) also vanishes, and Eq. (
38
) is exactly satisfied. This yields the exact parameters of the GKSL equation that perfectly reproduce the ideal experimental data without any errors.

However, for a single time point
t
, the matrix
H_{t}
is rank-deficient, meaning the parameters cannot be uniquely determined from a single observation.
To uniquely identify all parameters, it is necessary to accumulate geometric information over the entire time evolution.
Furthermore, accumulating this data over time may effectively average out potential experimental errors.
Therefore, we define the total loss function
\mathcal{L}(\bm{p})
by summing
\mathcal{J}_{2}(t)
over all observed time points.
Substituting
\Delta\bm{v}(t)=\dot{\bm{a}}_{\text{ob}}(t)-H_{t}\bm{p}
into the expression for
\mathcal{J}_{2}(t)
, the total loss function
\mathcal{L}(\bm{p})
becomes:

\mathcal{L}(\bm{p})=\sum_{t}\mathcal{J}_{2}(t)=\sum_{t}(\dot{\bm{a}}_{\text{ob}}(t)-H_{t}\bm{p})^{T}G^{-1}(\bm{a}(t))(\dot{\bm{a}}_{\text{ob}}(t)-H_{t}\bm{p})\,.

(47)

We then seek the parameters that satisfy the variational condition:

\left.\frac{\delta\mathcal{L}(\bm{p})}{\delta\bm{p}}\right|_{\bm{p}=\bm{p}^{*}}=0\,.

(48)

This minimization of the loss function
\mathcal{L}
with respect to
\bm{p}
leads to the following system of linear equations:

\sum_{t}H_{t}^{T}G^{-1}(\bm{a}(t))H_{t}\bm{p}^{*}=\sum_{t}H_{t}^{T}G^{-1}(\bm{a}(t))\dot{\bm{a}}_{\text{ob}}(t)\,.

(49)

This can be written compactly as:

\bm{p}^{*}=A^{-1}\bm{b}\,,

(50)

where the matrix
A
and the vector
\bm{b}
are obtained by accumulating data over the time series:

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle=\sum_{t}H_{t}^{T}G^{-1}(\bm{a}(t))H_{t}\,,

(51)

\displaystyle\bm{b}

\displaystyle=\sum_{t}H_{t}^{T}G^{-1}(\bm{a}(t))\dot{\bm{a}}_{\text{ob}}(t)\,.

(52)

Furthermore, to enhance numerical stability and ensure the full rank of
A
, it is advantageous to combine time-series data from multiple time evolutions starting from different initial states.
Since the estimation problem is linear, these independent datasets can be seamlessly aggregated into the summations defining
A
and
\bm{b}
, thereby improving the robustness of the estimation against experimental noise.

VI
Discussion and Concluding Remarks

In this work, we have derived a universal information-geometric identity valid for any single-qubit system and applied it to estimate the parameters of the GKSL master equation from observed time-evolution data.
This formulation can be regarded as an exact information-geometric extension of thermodynamic speed limits, which are generally formulated as inequalities bounding the rate of state evolution in stochastic and quantum thermodynamics. Our mathematical analysis demonstrates that for a single qubit, this inequality saturates into a strict equality, fundamentally because the qubit density matrix belongs to the quantum exponential family where the Pauli matrices serve as sufficient statistics.
Consequently, the derived identity can be reinterpreted not merely as a bound, but as a constitutive equation of motion that strictly constrains the quantum trajectory.

From a practical perspective, this identity enables a linear regression approach to quantum parameter estimation.
To achieve this, we utilized the BKM metric, which corresponds to the Hessian of the exponential family potential.
While the SLD metric is commonly used in standard quantum information theory, the choice of the BKM metric offers a decisive advantage here: it perfectly linearizes the inverse problem.
This linear formulation inherently avoids the problematic local minima issues associated with the non-linear optimization methods typically required in standard maximum likelihood estimation.
Our numerical results confirm that the proposed method estimates the Hamiltonian and dissipation parameters with remarkable accuracy under ideal conditions.
Under the influence of experimental noise, however, the convergence naturally slows down, and the converged values exhibit some deviation from the true GKSL parameters, primarily due to the singular nature of the inverse metric near pure states.
This highlights the practical importance of introducing robust error-mitigation procedures to estimate the parameters more precisely within our current scheme.

The proposed method also provides a natural framework for assessing model validity.
Because the linear regression does not inherently constrain the parameter space, the observation of negative dissipation rates would provide direct, regression-based evidence of non-Markovian dynamics or other fundamental deviations from the standard GKSL framework.
Combined with appropriate error-mitigation techniques, this geometric approach could offer a powerful and computationally efficient tool for characterizing noise environments in Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum (NISQ) devices.
Extending this approach to multi-qubit systems remains a critical subject for future research, as the exponential family structure becomes significantly more complex in the presence of general interacting quantum systems.

Additionally, our current framework relies entirely on the Riemannian structure (the metric tensor) of the statistical manifold. However, a profound and distinct feature of information geometry is its dualistic structure, which arises from the asymmetry of the divergence and is mathematically captured by the cubic tensor (or dual affine connections)
Amari1985
;
AmariNagaoka2000
;
Ay2017
.
Recent studies have highlighted the physical significance of this asymmetry, relating it to the “gravitational” aspects of information in stochastic processes
KoideVenn2025
;
KoideVenn2026
.
Fascinatingly, this cubic tensor is also related to the violation of metricity in differential geometry. Such non-metricity may play an essential role in extended theories of general relativity for naturally incorporating the cosmological effects of dark matter and dark energy
KoideVenn2026
;
Iosifidis2023
.
From this broader perspective, the study of the cubic tensor has become increasingly important in recent years.
How these dualistic geometric effects encoded in the cubic tensor influence parameter estimation, or how they might provide deeper physical insights connecting open quantum dynamics to generalized theories of gravity, remains a highly non-trivial and intriguing question left for future investigation.

Appendix A
Derivation of the Bloch Vector Equation with an Affine Term

In this appendix, we derive the general equation of motion for the Bloch vector.

The time evolution of the density matrix
\hat{\rho}
is governed by the GKSL master equation:

\frac{d\hat{\rho}}{dt}=\mathcal{L}(\hat{\rho})=-i[\hat{H},\hat{\rho}]+\sum_{k}\gamma_{k}\left(\hat{L}_{k}\hat{\rho}\hat{L}_{k}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\{\hat{L}_{k}^{\dagger}\hat{L}_{k},\hat{\rho}\}\right)\,.

(53)

Using the Bloch representation
\hat{\rho}=\frac{1}{2}(I+\bm{a}\cdot\bm{\sigma})
, the time derivative of the Bloch vector components
a_{i}=\mathrm{Tr}[\hat{\rho}\sigma_{i}]
is given by:

\dot{a}_{i}=\mathrm{Tr}[\mathcal{L}(\hat{\rho})\sigma_{i}]=\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Tr}[\mathcal{L}(I)\sigma_{i}]+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j}a_{j}\mathrm{Tr}[\mathcal{L}(\sigma_{j})\sigma_{i}]\,.

(54)

This equation can be expressed in the following vector form:

\dot{\bm{a}}=\Lambda\bm{a}+\bm{c}\,,

(55)

where

\displaystyle\Lambda_{ij}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Tr}[\mathcal{L}(\sigma_{j})\sigma_{i}]\,,

(56)

\displaystyle c_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Tr}[\mathcal{L}(I)\sigma_{i}]\,.

(57)

We now evaluate the affine term
\bm{c}
. The unitary part
-i[\hat{H},I]
vanishes identically. The dissipative part acts on the identity operator as:

\mathcal{L}(I)=\sum_{k}\gamma_{k}\left(\hat{L}_{k}I\hat{L}_{k}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\{\hat{L}_{k}^{\dagger}\hat{L}_{k},I\}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k}\gamma_{k}[\hat{L}_{k},\hat{L}_{k}^{\dagger}]\,.

(58)

Thus, the affine vector is determined by the commutators of the Lindblad operators:

\bm{c}=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{k}\gamma_{k}\mathrm{Tr}\left([\hat{L}_{k},\hat{L}_{k}^{\dagger}]\bm{\sigma}\right)\,.

(59)

If all Lindblad operators are Hermitian such that
\hat{L}_{k}=\hat{L}_{k}^{\dagger}
, as occurs in dephasing or bit-flip channels, the commutators vanish and result in
\bm{c}=\bm{0}
. This case corresponds to unital dynamics. However, for non-Hermitian operators such as the lowering operator
\hat{L}=\sigma_{-}
used in amplitude damping, the commutator is given by
[\sigma_{-},\sigma_{+}]=-\sigma_{z}
. This lead to a non-zero affine term
\bm{c}\propto\bm{e}_{z}
that drives the system toward the ground state.

In the main text, we assumed
\bm{c}=\bm{0}
for simplicity. This assumption corresponds to restricting our analysis to unital channels or effectively shifting the coordinate system if the steady state of the dynamics is known.
