Quantum-Inspired Simulation of 2D Turbulent Rayleigh-Bénard Convection
Nis-Luca van Hülst, Mario Guillaume Cecile, Hai-Yen Van, Tomohiro Hashizume, Eugene de Villiers, Dieter Jaksch

Abstract
Turbulent thermal convection governs heat transport in systems ranging from stellar interiors to industrial heat exchangers. Two-dimensional Rayleigh–Bénard convection serves as a paradigm for these flows, reproducing key features such as thin boundary layers, large-scale circulation, and sustained plume dynamics. While Matrix Product State (MPS) methods have demonstrated significant compression of isothermal turbulent fields, their application to buoyancy-driven flows with active thermal coupling has remained unexplored. We apply MPS to two-dimensional Rayleigh–Bénard convection with dynamical simulations up to Ra = 10 10 \mathrm{Ra}=10^{10} . An a priori decomposition of DNS snapshots up to Ra = 10 11 \mathrm{Ra}=10^{11} shows that the bond dimension χ \chi required to represent the flow fields grows without saturation, in contrast to the plateauing of χ \chi reported for velocity fields in isothermal 2D turbulence. Crucially, however, dynamical simulations solving the governing equations directly in the compressed MPS format at fixed χ \chi show that the χ \chi required to recover statistical observables, such as the Nusselt number, scales significantly more favorably with Ra \mathrm{Ra} than the a priori complexity suggests. At Ra = 10 10 \mathrm{Ra}=10^{10} , a relative error of 1.8 % 1.8\% in the mean Nusselt number is achieved with a nearly 9-fold reduction in degrees of freedom, using a χ \chi comparable to that required at Ra = 10 9 \mathrm{Ra}=10^{9} . Spectral analysis confirms the progressive recovery of spatial and temporal scales with increasing χ \chi . These findings establish MPS as a scalable tool for simulating thermally driven turbulence, suggesting the method may remain viable for investigations of the ultimate regime at substantially higher Ra \mathrm{Ra} .

II
Physical Model and Numerical Discretization

In this section, we establish the physical and numerical foundations of our study. We first define the governing equations for thermal convection and subsequently describe the staggered finite difference scheme used for spatial and temporal discretization. Furthermore, it outlines the numerical scheme to solve the equations and introduces the Nusselt number, which will be used throughout this manuscript as a figure of merit.

II.1
Governing Equations

The system is described by the incompressible Navier-Stokes equations under the Oberbeck-Boussinesq approximation
45
;
6
;
52
. This approach treats the fluid density
\rho
as a constant in the inertial and continuity terms, accounting for its variation only in the buoyancy term. The resulting non-dimensional equations for RBC read as
10
;
1
;
63

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u_{i}+u_{j}\partial_{j}u_{i}

\displaystyle=-\partial_{i}p+\frac{1}{\real}\partial_{j}\partial_{j}u_{i}+\frac{\mathrm{Ra}}{\mathrm{Pr}\real^{2}}\theta\delta_{iy},

(1)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\theta+u_{j}\partial_{j}\theta

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\real\mathrm{Pr}}\partial_{j}\partial_{j}\theta,

(2)

\displaystyle\partial_{j}u_{j}

\displaystyle=0,

(3)

where repeated indices imply summation following the Einstein notation,
u_{i}
denotes the
i
-component (
i\in\{x,y\}
) of the non-dimensional velocity field, namely
u_{x}=u
and
u_{y}=v
,
p
is the non-dimensional kinematic pressure,
\theta
is the non-dimensional temperature and
t
the non-dimensional time. The delta
\delta_{iy}
indicates that buoyancy acts in the wall-normal direction.
The non-dimensional variables are defined through the Rayleigh number (
\mathrm{Ra}=g\beta\Delta L^{3}/\nu\kappa
), representing the buoyancy-to-dissipation ratio, and the Prandtl number (
\Pr=\nu/\kappa
). Following the free-fall scaling convention
1
;
63
, we adopt the cell height
L
, the temperature difference
\Delta
between the walls, and the free-fall velocity
U_{\mathrm{FF}}=\sqrt{g\beta\Delta L}
as characteristic scales. Here,
g
is gravity,
\beta
is the thermal expansion coefficient,
\nu
is the kinematic viscosity, and
\kappa
is the thermal diffusivity. This choice yields the characteristic time
t_{\mathrm{FF}}=L/U_{\mathrm{FF}}
and relates the Reynolds number to the primary parameters as
\real=\sqrt{\mathrm{Ra}/\Pr}
.
In the wall-parallel
x
-direction, we assume periodic boundary conditions over a domain of width
H=\Gamma\,L
, where
\Gamma=2
is the aspect ratio. At the horizontal plates (
y=\pm L/2
), we enforce no-slip conditions for the velocity,
u=v=0
, and isothermal Dirichlet conditions for the temperature,
\theta(x,y=\pm L/2)=\pm\Delta/2

1
;
10

II.2
Numerical Scheme

The state variables are discretized on a staggered grid consisting of
N_{x}\times N_{y}
points per subgrid
31
;
23
. To eliminate interpolation errors in the buoyancy term,
v
and
\theta
are co-located on the same subgrid, which is chosen to include the upper boundary. In contrast, the pressure nodes
p
are located at the cell centers, while the horizontal velocity
u
is staggered horizontally relative to the pressure (Fig.
1
)
63
. For the operators, we employ a second-order centered finite-difference scheme
39
. This low-order approximation is specifically chosen for its robustness in handling the sharp temperature gradients characteristic of high-Rayleigh number convection
63
. Furthermore, from a tensor-network perspective, these low-order operators offer a highly efficient representation of the discrete Laplacian and gradient operators
36
.
For temporal discretization, we employ a second-order implicit-explicit Runge-Kutta scheme (IMEX-RK2)
53
. In this framework, the advective and buoyancy terms are treated explicitly, while the viscous and thermal diffusion terms are treated semi-implicitly to bypass the stringent quadratic stability constraint (
\delta t\sim\delta x^{2}
). While higher-order variants, such as the third-order RK3 schemes described by Rai and Moin
51
and van der Poel
et al.

63
, are frequently utilized in Rayleigh-Bénard studies for enhanced temporal accuracy, the present work utilizes a robust two-stage IMEX-RK2 approach. This choice provides a balance between computational efficiency and the second-order accuracy required for the flow regimes studied. The incompressibility constraint (
\partial_{j}u_{j}=0
) is enforced via a fractional-step Chorin projection method
13
applied at each stage. Given the fields at time step
m
, the advancement to
m+1
proceeds as follows:

Stage 1

A provisional velocity field
u_{i}^{*}
is computed, followed by a projection to obtain the divergence-free field

u_{i}^{m+1/2}
:

\displaystyle u_{i}^{*}=u_{i}^{m}+\frac{\delta t}{2}\Bigg[\Big(-u_{j}^{m}\partial_{j}u_{i}^{m}

\displaystyle\qquad\ +\frac{\mathrm{Ra}}{\mathrm{Pr}\,\mathrm{Re}^{2}}\,\theta^{m}\delta_{iy}\Big)+\frac{1}{\mathrm{Re}}\partial_{j}\partial_{j}u_{i}^{*}\Bigg]

\displaystyle\partial_{j}\partial_{j}\phi^{m+1/2}=\frac{2}{\delta t}\,\partial_{j}u_{j}^{*}

\displaystyle u_{i}^{m+1/2}=u_{i}^{*}-\frac{\delta t}{2}\,\partial_{i}\phi^{m+1/2}

Stage 2

Using the half-step velocity, the final velocity
u_{i}^{m+1}
is computed

\displaystyle u_{i}^{*}=u_{i}^{m}+\delta t\Bigg[\Big(-u_{j}^{m+1/2}\partial_{j}u_{i}^{m+1/2}

\displaystyle\qquad\ \ +\frac{\mathrm{Ra}}{\mathrm{Pr}\,\mathrm{Re}^{2}}\,\theta^{m+1/2}\delta_{iy}\Big)+\frac{1}{\mathrm{Re}}\,\partial_{j}\partial_{j}u_{i}^{m+1/2}\Bigg]

\displaystyle\partial_{j}\partial_{j}\phi^{m+1}=\frac{1}{\delta t}\,\partial_{j}u_{j}^{*}

\displaystyle u_{i}^{m+1}=u_{i}^{*}-\delta t\,\partial_{i}\phi^{m+1}

The pressure field is updated as
p^{m+1}=\phi^{m+1}
. The temperature
\theta
is advanced similarly through the same two stages using the implicit operator
\frac{1}{\text{Re}\,\text{Pr}}\partial_{j}^{2}\theta
in the first stage. This approach ensures that the time step
\delta t
is governed by the linear Courant-Friedrichs-Lewy (CFL) condition

C=\delta t\left(\frac{|u|}{\delta x}+\frac{|v|}{\delta y}\right)\leq C_{\max}

where
C_{\max}
is the Courant number (typically
C_{\max}<1
)
16
, significantly improving efficiency for high-
Ra
simulations.

II.3
Nusselt Number

The Nusselt number, denoted as
\mathrm{Nu}
in the following, serves as our most important system response quantifying the efficiency of convective heat transport relative to pure conduction
9
. Physically, it is defined as the ratio of total vertical heat flux to the conductive flux. Below the critical Rayleigh number
\mathrm{Ra}_{c}
for the onset of convection, heat transfer is purely diffusive, yielding
\mathrm{Nu}=1
. As
\mathrm{Ra}
exceeds
\mathrm{Ra}_{c}
, fluid
motion enhances transport, leading to
\mathrm{Nu}>1
.

We define the instantaneous Nusselt number as

\mathrm{Nu}_{\text{inst}}(t)=1+\sqrt{\mathrm{Ra}\,\mathrm{Pr}}\,\langle v(t)\,\theta(t)\rangle_{V}\,,

(4)

where
\langle\cdot\rangle_{V}
denotes the spatial average over the domain. To characterize the statistically stationary heat transport, we consider the time-averaged Nusselt number

\mathrm{Nu}(\mathcal{T})=\frac{1}{\mathcal{T}}\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{0}+\mathcal{T}}\mathrm{Nu}_{\text{inst}}(t)\,dt\,,

(5)

where
\mathcal{T}
is the averaging window and
t_{0}
is chosen to exclude initial transients.

Empirically, the Nusselt number follows a power-law scaling with the Rayleigh number,
\mathrm{Nu}\sim\mathrm{Ra}^{\gamma}
, where the exponent
\gamma
transitions from
\approx 1/4
in the moderate regime to
\approx 1/3
in the fully turbulent regime
1
;
29
.

Furthermore, the distribution of instantaneous Nusselt-number fluctuations,

\mathrm{Nu}_{\mathrm{inst}}(t)
, serves as a robust metric for assessing the numerical solver, and we use it here to benchmark the tensor-network-based simulations against a DNS reference. To quantify the discrepancy between two such distributions, we employ
the first Wasserstein distance
\tilde{W}_{1}

61
;
24
. For distributions with
cumulative distribution functions (CDFs)
U(x)
and
V(x)
, it is defined as the

\mathrm{L}_{1}
distance between the CDFs
61

\tilde{W}_{1}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|U(x)-V(x)|\,dx\,.

(6)

To facilitate scale-independent comparisons across varying thermal driving regimes, we introduce the normalized Wasserstein distance,
W_{1}=\tilde{W}_{1}/\mathrm{Nu}
, which scales the distribution error relative to the mean macroscopic heat transport.

III
Tensor Network Framework

To mitigate the memory and computational demands of high-
\mathrm{Ra}
simulations, we adopt a tensor-network representation. Instead of storing discretized fields as conventional multidimensional arrays, each field is encoded as a Matrix Product State (MPS). We consider a two-dimensional discretized field
f(x_{i_{x}},y_{i_{y}})
sampled on a uniform grid. The mapping from the integer index pair
(i_{x},i_{y})
to the physical domain is given by

(x,y)=(x_{0},y_{0})+(i_{x}\Delta x,i_{y}\Delta y)

where
(x_{0},y_{0})
is the origin and
\Delta x
,
\Delta y
are the grid spacings, and
i_{x}\in\{0,\dots,2^{n_{x}}-1\}
,
i_{y}\in\{0,\dots,2^{n_{y}}-1\}
. Each integer index can be expressed as a binary string of length
n_{x}
or
n_{y}
, respectively, as
i_{x}=\sum_{k=1}^{n_{x}}s_{k}2^{n_{x}-k}
, where
s_{k}\in\{0,1\}
. By concatenating these bitstrings, the 2D field is reinterpreted as a rank-
n
tensor
f(s_{1},\dots,s_{n})
with
n=n_{x}+n_{y}
. This tensor can then be factorized via a sequence of singular value decompositions (SVDs) into a chain of
n
third-order tensors, or MPS cores
A^{(l)}
. Under this ansatz, the field value at any grid location is recovered by contracting these cores along their virtual indices
\alpha

;
67

\displaystyle\simeq\sum_{\alpha_{1}=1}^{\chi_{1}}..\,\sum_{\alpha_{n-1}=1}^{\chi_{n-1}}A_{s_{1}}^{(1)\alpha_{1}}A_{s_{2}}^{(2)\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}}\dots A_{s_{n}}^{(n)\alpha_{n-1}}

To assess the attainable compression of the turbulent flow fields obtained by DNS, we analyze the Schmidt spectrum of the resulting MPS upon decomposition. We define a truncation criterion based on the cumulative spectral weight, calculating the minimum bond dimension
r
required at each bond to retain a fraction
1-\epsilon
of the total squared weight,

\frac{\sum_{\alpha=1}^{r}\lambda_{\alpha}^{2}}{\sum_{\alpha=1}^{\chi}\lambda_{\alpha}^{2}}\geq 1-\epsilon,

(7)

where
\lambda_{\alpha}
are the Schmidt coefficients at a given bond, sorted in descending order.
This metric allows us to map the information content of the flow, for example, a tolerance of
\epsilon=10^{-2}
corresponds to retaining
99\%
of the spectral weight at each bond, denoted as
\chi_{99}

27
. Finally, we introduce the fraction in the number of parameters
P_{T}
of the MPS compared to the DNS
48

\displaystyle P_{T}=\frac{\sum_{l=1}^{n}\chi_{l-1}\,d\,\chi_{l}}{2^{n}}\ ,

(8)

where
d=2
is the physical dimension in our simulations. This figure of merit will be used throughout the results as a measure of the parametric efficiency of the MPS.

For the representation of operators and the implementation of algebraic operations in the MPS format, including operator application, element-wise state multiplication, and the solution of linear systems, we refer the reader to our recent work
65
. The momentum equations are solved using a single-site density matrix renormalization group approach
56
;
33
;
18
;
19
, also known as the alternating minimization scheme, employing Krylov-based methods for the occurring local linear systems. The pressure Poisson equation is solved via a spectral method adapted for the MPS format, inspired by
12
. The required Fourier transform operators in MPS format are generated using the library introduced in
22
.

Matrix Product State Representation of Turbulent Flow Fields

This section examines the data compression capability of the MPS format for turbulent RBC flow fields. Snapshots of the velocity components

,

Figure

at
\mathrm{Ra}=10^{10}

The flow exhibits the characteristic phenomenology of high-Ra convection, featuring thin thermal boundary layers and the detachment of thermal plumes that drive the heat transport across the bulk. To quantify the potential compression of this turbulent state, we decompose the DNS flow fields into MPS via successive SVDs (as detailed in Sec.

To understand the characteristics of the MPS representation in more depth, we analyse the normalized
Schmidt spectrum across every bond of the MPS.
We define the normalized Schmidt values

\tilde{\lambda}_{\alpha}=\lambda_{\alpha}/\sum_{k}\lambda_{k}^{2}

where the accumulated sum of the largest

defines the strength of the

In Figures

Overlaid on these heatmaps are the

which indicate the

99\%
and

Finally, to examine the convergence rate, we plot the full Schmidt spectrum at the central bipartition for
u,v
, and
\theta
in Fig.

\chi

\chi

The scaling of the required

equilibration times and grid parameters are provided in Appendix

A related analysis was reported by Hölscher

In our case, the growth of

For

IV.3
Accuracy and Convergence of the MPS Solver in Dynamical Simulations

While the
a priori
decomposition of DNS snapshots provides an initial measure of the informational content of the flow, it tests representability only at discrete points in time.
In the dynamical simulations, the system is evolved directly in the
compressed MPS format at fixed
\chi

\chi
suggested by the DNS data reflect small-scale features or fluctuations that have limited influence on the macroscopic evolution of the flow. Consequently, a substantially smaller
\chi

To assess this, we validate the MPS solver by comparing against a DNS reference
at
\mathrm{Ra}=10^{8}

66

to permit direct pointwise comparison over relevant timescales.
The relative

\epsilon_{\ell_{2}}(t)
(Eq.
9

and
\theta

Panel (c) shows the relative error in

where

As illustrated in panels (a)–(c), the MPS solution converges systematically
toward the DNS reference as

Panel (d) summarizes this convergence at

The Nusselt number error

This is expected because

At higher Rayleigh numbers,
\text{Ra}=10^{9}
and
10^{10}
, the chaotic nature of the flow renders individual trajectory tracking unsuitable, as infinitesimal perturbations lead to rapid divergence of initially close solution trajectories. We therefore assess the MPS approach by its ability to reproduce long-term statistics rather than individual paths. Assuming the ergodic hypothesis
73
;
70
, where the time average of a single realization is equivalent to the ensemble average over independent trajectories, we adopt a parallelized sampling strategy to circumvent the prohibitive cost of computing a single long-duration trajectory. We construct an ensemble of
M
independent realizations, treating them as statistically uncorrelated samples from the attractor.

To construct this ensemble, all realizations are initialized from a common statistical stationary state obtained via DNS, with distinct thermal perturbations
\theta_{\text{pert}}
introduced to trigger trajectory divergence (see Appendix
D
). A slightly higher perturbation intensity is prescribed for the DNS reference to match the inherent truncation errors of the MPS representation, which can be interpreted as a persistent noise source.
Following a transient decorrelation period, we compute the ensemble-averaged Nusselt number
\overline{\mathrm{Nu}}=1/M\,\sum_{m=1}^{M}\mathrm{Nu}_{m}

\mathrm{Nu}_{m}
is the Nusselt number of the
m

\epsilon_{\overline{\mathrm{Nu}}}(\mathcal{T})=|\overline{\mathrm{Nu}}^{\text{MPS}}(\mathcal{T})-\overline{\mathrm{Nu}}^{\text{DNS}}(\mathcal{T})|/\overline{\mathrm{Nu}}^{\text{DNS}}(\mathcal{T})
for a given sampling window
\mathcal{T}
. The results for ensembles of
M=19
realizations at
\text{Ra}=10^{9}
and
M=24
realizations at
\text{Ra}=10^{10}
are summarized in Fig.
5
. Panels (a) and (b) display the evolution of the relative error
\epsilon_{\overline{\mathrm{Nu}}}(\mathcal{T})
over increasing sampling windows
\mathcal{T}
. In addition, Fig.
5
(c) and (d) show the probability density functions (PDFs) of the spatio-temporally-averaged Nusselt numbers
\text{Nu}_{m}(\mathcal{T})
of the individual realizations based on the maximum sampling window (
\mathcal{T}=24.3\,t_{\mathrm{FF}}
and
10.0\,t_{\mathrm{FF}}

For the
\mathrm{Ra}=10^{9}

\epsilon_{\overline{\mathrm{Nu}}}(\mathcal{T})
indicates an abrupt transition in the accuracy of the results rather than a smooth improvement. There is a stark contrast between the results at the lowest
\chi
and all subsequent increments. At
\chi=60
(

At the higher Rayleigh number (

of heat transport shows as a completely detached distribution shifted
toward unphysically high values (
W_{1}\approx 111.4\%
).
By
\chi=120
, the MPS achieves significant statistical overlap
with the DNS reference, with the Wasserstein distance reduced to

W_{1}\approx 6.1\%
and the mean error to
5.3\%
.
At
\mathrm{Ra}=10^{10}
(Fig.
8
e–h),

\chi=60
and
\chi=80
substantially overpredict the heat transport
(
\epsilon_{\overline{\mathrm{Nu}}}\approx 208.3\%
),
driving the model toward a more efficient yet unphysical convective state.
An increment to
\chi=100
initiates a transition toward the DNS reference,
while
\chi=120
reduces the mean error to

\epsilon_{\overline{\mathrm{Nu}}}\approx 1.8\%
and aligns the PDF
with the DNS qualitatively.
The MPS distribution remains broader than the DNS, however, with

\epsilon_{\sigma_{\mathrm{Nu}}}\approx 30.6\%
and

W_{1}\approx 12.6\%
, indicating that the full variance of the
fluctuations is not yet resolved at this
\chi
,
which is to be compared with the spectral analysis above.

V
Discussion and Outlook

Previous studies exploring MPS applications to computational
fluid dynamics have focused predominantly on the isothermal, incompressible
Navier-Stokes equations in spatially homogeneous, doubly-periodic
configurations such as 2D decaying turbulence, the 3D Taylor-Green vortex,
and 3D homogeneous isotropic turbulence
28
;
32
;
49
.
In these settings, the absence of walls, boundary layers, and thermal
coupling favors low-rank representations, and
a priori
evaluations
of DNS snapshots indicated a plateauing of the required bond
dimension
\chi_{99}
at high Reynolds numbers.
Dynamical MPS simulations, however, have thus far been demonstrated for Reynolds numbers
within the regime where
\chi
grows as a power law with Re
32
.

Rayleigh–Bénard convection differs from these configurations
in several fundamental respects.
It is wall-bounded, with thin thermal and velocity boundary layers
whose thickness decreases with
\mathrm{Ra}
, creating spatial inhomogeneity
between the near-wall and bulk regions.
The Boussinesq approximation couples the velocity field to an active
temperature field
\theta
, adding an additional dynamical field absent
in isothermal configurations
63
;
74
.
The flow is continuously driven by the imposed temperature gradient,
sustaining plume dynamics and large-scale circulation throughout the
simulation.
How these features affect the practicality of the MPS ansatz has,
to our knowledge, not been previously investigated.

Our
a priori
analysis shows that while the velocity components
exhibit initial signs of plateauing in the required
\chi
with
increasing
\mathrm{Ra}
, the temperature field shows no evidence of saturation
within the investigated range.
Despite this more demanding
a priori
outlook, our dynamical MPS
simulations demonstrate that mean heat transport and its statistical
distribution remain robustly captured at moderate
\chi
, with the
required
\chi
growing more slowly with
\mathrm{Ra}
than the

a priori
snapshot analysis would suggest.
Increasing
\chi
systematically captures more inter-scale
correlations
27
;
28
, as demonstrated
by the convergence of the mean Nusselt number, its statistical
distribution, and the progressive recovery of its spectral content.

Reaching higher
\mathrm{Ra}
within the present framework will require
addressing the element-wise product, which remains the dominant
computational bottleneck.
For the values of
\chi
investigated here, the variational
implementation proved most robust, but the algorithms introduced in Refs.
43
;
42
represent promising alternatives
with improved asymptotic scaling that offer significant speedups
at larger
\chi
.
Adaptive time stepping, operating close to the CFL stability limit,
offers a complementary route to reducing computational cost.
Incorporating parallelization strategies along the lines of
Refs.
60
;
57
could further accelerate
time integration, analogous to the domain decomposition employed in conventional DNS
63
. Moreover, TN algorithms map naturally onto GPU architectures
32
. In Appendix
E
, we compare the memory scaling of MPS and DNS with increasing
\mathrm{Ra}
, indicating that the compressed format could enable high-
\mathrm{Ra}
simulations to run entirely within the VRAM of a single GPU.

Future extensions to other architectures, such as tree-tensor networks, provide a route to capturing the anisotropic, multiscale correlations of three-dimensional thermal turbulence. Crucially, this framework also serves as a bridge to quantum computing. By replacing the field ansatz with parameterized variational quantum circuits and compiling differential operators into unitary gate sequences
41
;
34
;
59
, our approach establishes a foundation for hybrid quantum-classical simulations of high-
\mathrm{Ra}
regimes.

Appendix D
Perturbations to Initial Conditions

We first compute the root-mean-square (RMS) fluctuation of the initial temperature field
\theta_{0}(x,y,t)
as:

\theta_{\text{rms}}=\sqrt{\langle\theta_{0}^{2}\rangle-\langle\theta_{0}\rangle^{2}},

(12)

where
\langle\cdot\rangle
denotes the spatial average over the domain area
A=W\times L
.
The spatial structure of the perturbation is constructed to vanish at the walls (
y=\pm L/2
) while introducing low-wavenumber mixing

P(x,y)=\left(\frac{L^{2}}{4}-y^{2}\right)\cos\left(\frac{2\pi kx}{W}+\phi\right).

(13)

Here, the wavenumber
k
is selected uniformly from
\{1,2,3\}
and the phase
\phi
from
\mathcal{U}[0,2\pi)
. To ensure zero net heat addition, we remove the spatial mean of the shape function,
P^{\prime}=P-\langle P\rangle
.
Finally, the perturbation is normalized to a target intensity
\varepsilon
and superimposed onto the field

\theta_{\text{perturbed}}=\theta_{0}+\varepsilon\,\theta_{\text{rms}}\,\frac{P^{\prime}}{\sigma_{P}},

(14)

where
\sigma_{P}=\sqrt{\langle P^{\prime 2}\rangle}
is the standard deviation of the perturbation shape.

Appendix F
Statistical Estimation and Error Propagation

To characterize the convergence of the heat transport statistics, we define the
time-averaged Nusselt number for each independent realization
m\in\{1,\dots,M\}

over a sampling window
\mathcal{T}
as

\mathrm{Nu}_{m}(\mathcal{T})=\frac{1}{\mathcal{T}}\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{0}+\mathcal{T}}\mathrm{Nu}_{\text{inst},m}(t)\,dt\,,

(15)

where
\mathrm{Nu}_{\text{inst},m}
is given by Eq. (
4
).
The ensemble mean, representing the spatio-temporal average across all
M
realizations, is then

\overline{\mathrm{Nu}}(\mathcal{T})=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\mathrm{Nu}_{m}(\mathcal{T})\,.

(16)

The statistical uncertainty of this estimator is quantified by the Standard Error of the Mean (SEM), derived from the variance of the independent temporal histories

\mathrm{SEM}(\mathcal{T})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{M}}\sqrt{\frac{1}{M-1}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\left(\mathrm{Nu}_{m}(\mathcal{T})-\overline{\mathrm{Nu}}(\mathcal{T})\right)^{2}}\,.

(17)

We define the relative error
\epsilon_{\overline{\mathrm{Nu}}}(\mathcal{T})
as

\epsilon_{\overline{\mathrm{Nu}}}(\mathcal{T})=\frac{\left|\overline{\mathrm{Nu}}^{\text{MPS}}(\mathcal{T})-\overline{\mathrm{Nu}}^{\text{DNS}}(\mathcal{T})\right|}{\overline{\mathrm{Nu}}^{\text{DNS}}(\mathcal{T})}\,,

(18)

and propagate the uncertainties from both ensembles via the total standard error

\mathrm{SEM}_{\text{tot}}=\sqrt{\left(\frac{\mathrm{SEM}^{\text{MPS}}}{\overline{\mathrm{Nu}}^{\text{DNS}}}\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{\overline{\mathrm{Nu}}^{\text{MPS}}\cdot\mathrm{SEM}^{\text{DNS}}}{(\overline{\mathrm{Nu}}^{\text{DNS}})^{2}}\right)^{2}}\,,

(19)

where
\mathrm{SEM}^{\text{DNS}}
and
\mathrm{SEM}^{\text{MPS}}
are the cumulative SEM
values for the DNS and MPS ensembles, respectively.
