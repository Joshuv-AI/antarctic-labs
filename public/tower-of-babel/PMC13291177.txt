Climate and plant traits drive a cross-continental imbalance in atmospheric Hg uptake
Authors: Longyu Jia, Jen-How Huang, Xun Wang, Wei Yuan, Qiang Pu, Hui Zhang, Nantao Liu, Jinglun Zhou, Xueying Zhu, Meiqing Sun, Charles T Driscoll, Feiyue Wang, Che-Jen Lin, Xinbin Feng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTropical rainforests, particularly the Amazon, function as the Earth’s lungs yet absorb mercury (Hg) emitted worldwide. By introducing climate-driven variations in foliar functional traits into a global model of forest Hg uptake, we uncovered an inter-continental spatial decoupling between Hg sources and sinks. Unexpectedly, the minimally industrialized rainforests of South America and Africa exhibit the world’s highest atmospheric Hg accumulation rates and greatest biomass, thus disproportionately sequestering Hg released from industrialized regions. This imbalance arises from climate-specific leaf traits that enhance Hg fixation towards lower latitudes. The model constrains global forest Hg uptake to 1155 ± 422 Mg yr–1, sharply reducing prior uncertainties (320–3138 Mg yr–1) and nearly equilibrating with global litterfall deposition (1180 ± 710 Mg yr–1). These findings urge a re-assessment of the Minamata Convention’s effectiveness and highlight the vulnerability of tropical forests to anthropogenic Hg inputs and to climate-induced shifts in vegetation and terrestrial Hg reservoirs.Subject terms: Environmental impact, Natural hazards

Introduction

Forests are central to the stability of the Earth’s biogeochemical and climatic systems1. By regulating the carbon, water and energy exchange at the atmosphere-land interface, they sustain the ideal environmental conditions which essentially support life2. Particularly, tropical rainforests, the lungs of the earth, absorb atmospheric CO2 and release oxygen continuously while filtering air and moisture. Their dense canopies not only moderate global climate, but also serve as powerful chemical interfaces, capturing trace gases and aerosols which circulate through the atmosphere3. Accordingly, rainforests play an indispensable role in maintaining the planet’s respiratory balance and increasingly in mitigating the spread of anthropogenic contaminants such as atmospheric mercury (Hg)4. Understanding how these ecosystems interact with global pollution cycles is therefore essential to assess the resilience of the Earth system under accelerating environmental change.

Mercury, a globally significant atmospheric pollutant, poses serious threats to human health and ecosystems5,6. To alleviate the severity of global Hg pollution, the United Nations adopted the Minamata Convention on Mercury in 2013, which entered into force in 2017. This international treaty aims to protect human health and the environment from anthropogenic Hg emissions. Elemental Hg (Hg0) is the dominant form of atmospheric Hg and represents the primary species emitted from anthropogenic sources7,8. Hence, a clear understanding of the sources and sinks of atmospheric Hg0 provides the foundation for the implementation of the Convention and evaluation of its effectiveness.

Forest foliage takes up atmospheric Hg0 and then transfers it to soils through litterfall9,10, a process that contributes more than 60% of the Hg stored in global surface soils11. Furthermore, foliar uptake of Hg0 forces seasonal fluctuations in atmospheric Hg concentrations across the Northern Hemisphere12. Thus, elucidating the spatial patterns of Hg0 uptake by forest foliage worldwide is crucial for assessing the global Hg budget.

The exchange of Hg0 between the atmosphere and forest foliage is governed by a complex set of processes. Foliar Hg0 uptake is influenced simultaneously by atmospheric Hg concentrations, plant functional traits and climatic conditions that shape leaf function and canopy structure13–15. Spatial variability in anthropogenic emissions creates large gradients in atmospheric Hg0 concentration, which in turn lead to pronounced regional differences in foliar Hg accumulation13,16. Functional traits such as stomatal conductance, photosynthetic rate, transpiration and leaf lifespan modulate the diffusion and oxidation of Hg0 within foliage17–19, while climatic heterogeneity determines forest composition and functional traits that collectively regulate global uptake patterns20. Consequently, the spatial variability of forest Hg0 assimilation reflects both anthropogenic influence and long-term ecological adaptation.

Despite its importance, modeling this process at a global scale remains a major challenge. Existing estimates of forest foliar Hg0 uptake differ by almost an order of magnitude (320–3138 Mg yr–1)21–28, mainly because of the simplified treatment of physiological processes and the scarcity of reliable input data. The earlier assessments infer foliar Hg uptake indirectly from litterfall deposition, assuming that annual canopy uptake is balanced by litter export21,29. While useful at large scales, this approach does not explicitly resolve the physiological accumulation of Hg in foliage nor account for spatial variability in leaf biomass and longevity that regulates canopy storage and turnover. Current mechanistic models, such as GEOS-Chem, incorporate multiple drivers of vegetation Hg uptake, including atmospheric Hg0 concentration, leaf area index, meteorological conditions (e.g., wind speed, temperature and humidity) and land surface characteristics (e.g., land use type and vegetation cover)24,27,30. However, these models rely largely on empirical parameterizations that do not explicitly represent key plant functional traits, particularly leaf age. Additionally, data-driven approaches depend heavily on the quality and representativeness of input datasets31–34. Both methods are constrained by incomplete knowledge of canopy Hg dynamics and the lack of globally consistent foliar biomass data. The common use of multi-year foliar Hg concentrations and indirect proxies such as net primary productivity or specific leaf area introduces substantial biases, often overestimating annual uptake by 50–300%22,23,26.

To overcome these limitations, we introduce a trait-constrained modeling architecture that resolves atmospheric Hg0 uptake as a physiological accumulation process within forest canopies. The framework integrates three key components: spatially variable leaf lifespan controlling Hg residence time in foliage, trait-regulated foliar Hg accumulation rates and observation-constrained foliar biomass determining canopy storage capacity. By coupling these processes, atmospheric Hg0 uptake can be quantified mechanistically from leaf-level accumulation to ecosystem-scale fluxes rather than inferred indirectly from litterfall deposition. Building on this framework, we developed a high-resolution model of global forest foliar Hg0 uptake by integrating reconstructed datasets for foliar Hg accumulation, leaf lifespan and foliar biomass (Fig. 1). Building on these advances, we aimed to (1) propose an internally consistent, high-resolution model capable of reliably mapping global forest foliar Hg0 uptake; (2) use these spatial patterns to assess compliance with the Minamata Convention and to evaluate potential responses of terrestrial Hg cycling to global environmental change; and (3) identify the mechanistic drivers underlying the spatial distribution of foliar Hg accumulation across global forest ecosystems.

Results and Discussion

Leaf traits and climate shape foliar Hg accumulation

Global comparisons reveal a globally coherent yet previously unrecognized pattern. We first analyzed paired observations of atmospheric Hg0 and foliar Hg concentrations collected at the same sites. The results show a clear threshold response: when atmospheric Hg0 concentrations exceed ～5 ng m–3, they exert a significant control over foliar Hg content (p < 0.01); in contrast, when concentrations are below 5 ng m–3, the effect is insignificant (p > 0.05) (Figure S1). Given that atmospheric Hg0 concentrations across forest regions worldwide remain below 5 ng m–3, this explains why regional-scale atmospheric Hg0 concentrations do not significantly influence foliar Hg content (Figure S2–S3). This finding challenges the long-standing assumption that atmospheric Hg0 concentration dominates foliar Hg0 uptake and instead points to a mechanistic and trait-based framework governing Hg accumulation across forest biomes.

Structural equation modeling (SEM; see Text S1 for SEM construction) analyses highlight plant traits as the dominant driver of spatial heterogeneity in foliar Hg concentration among all examined variables (Fig. 2a, b), with significant standardized path coefficients for both broadleaves (β = 0.59, p < 0.001) and needles (β = –0.62, p < 0.001). Among the trait factors, leaf age exerts the strongest influence, consistent with the accumulation patterns shown in Fig. 1c, d. Geographic location, particularly latitude, plays an important yet indirect role by modulating plant trait variability (indirect effect: broadleaves = −0.28, needles = 0.07), supporting the observed latitudinal gradient of decreasing foliar Hg concentration with increasing latitude (Figure S4). Climatic effects influence both broadleaves and needles, mainly via temperature and precipitation. Broadleaves primarily experience indirect effects mediated through tree traits (indirect effect = 0.31). In contrast, climate exerts both direct and indirect control in needles, with an indirect effect of −0.35 and a direct effect of 0.28.

The SEM findings are fully supported by the global dataset. Foliar Hg concentrations differ significantly among forest functional types (Fig. 1b). The mean concentration in evergreen broadleaves (46.1 ± 29.6 ng g–1) is markedly higher than in deciduous broadleaves (31.1 ± 18.8 ng g–1), evergreen needles (28.9 ± 19.2 ng g–1) and deciduous needles (23.4 ± 4.0 ng g–1) (K–W test, p < 0.05). The elevated Hg concentrations in evergreen broadleaves are attributed to their higher stomatal conductance, faster photosynthetic rate and larger specific leaf area, which collectively enhance Hg accumulation15,35. This pattern is supported by Figs. 1c, d, where broadleaves show substantially higher monthly Hg accumulation rates (2.36–7.60 ng g–1 month–1) than the annual rates observed in needles (4.17–10.58 ng g–1 year–1) (K–W test, p < 0.05). Furthermore, the prolonged leaf lifespan of evergreen broadleaves allows for extended accumulation compared with deciduous species36.

The disparity in foliar Hg concentration among functional types reflects a compensatory mechanism between accumulation rate and leaf lifespan. The multi-year lifespan of evergreen needles (1–5 years) offsets their lower instantaneous accumulation rate, while the short-lived foliage of deciduous species accumulates Hg rapidly but over a limited period15,37. This compensatory pattern results in no significant regional difference in foliar Hg concentration between evergreen needleleaf and deciduous broadleaf forests (K–W test, p > 0.05; Fig. 1b), despite their contrasting physiologies.

Accordingly, both SEM and global comparisons consistently indicate that leaf traits, particularly accumulation rate and lifespan, govern foliar Hg concentrations under climatic regulation. Incorporating these parameters into future Hg0 uptake models is essential to minimize bias across forest functional types and to accurately represent global forest Hg sinks.

Global pattern of foliar Hg accumulation rates

By integrating 2415 observational records with a machine learning framework (see Text S2), we generated the globally continuous map of forest leaf lifespan (Figure S5) and subsequently a global map of forest foliar Hg accumulation rates (Fig. 3a). These datasets provide the spatially resolved evidence linking vegetation functional traits to atmospheric Hg uptake on a global scale. The spatial distribution of leaf lifespan is largely controlled by interactions among temperature, solar radiation and nutrient availability, exhibiting longer durations in high-latitude and arid regions but shorter ones in tropical zones (Text S3). Such variation reflects adaptive strategies that allow plants to optimize resource investment and enhance nutrient-use efficiency under cold, high-light or nutrient-limited conditions37,38.

In contrast, foliar Hg accumulation rates decrease from tropical to boreal forests and differ significantly among forest types (evergreen broadleaf: 48.6 ± 13.6 ng g–1 yr–1 > deciduous broadleaf: 33.3 ± 6.3 ng g–1 yr–1 > deciduous needleleaf: 22.5 ± 5.7 ng g–1 yr–1 > evergreen needleleaf: 17.5 ± 4.6 ng g–1 yr–1; K–W test, p < 0.05). Notable hotspots of foliar Hg accumulation were observed in tropical South America (50°S–3°S, 80°W–35°W) and central Africa (30°S–5°N, 10°W–40°E), where rates reached 50–70 ng g–1 yr–1. These regions are dominated by dense evergreen broadleaf forests, which sustain high photosynthetic activity and continuous canopy gas exchange throughout the year18,39. Warm, humid conditions combined with strong solar radiation promote stomatal conductance and photosynthetic rates, enhancing both the diffusion of Hg0 into leaves and its subsequent oxidation and sequestration (Figure S6a, b)40–42. The year-round presence of evergreen foliage further supports persistent Hg0 uptake, making these tropical forests important sinks for atmospheric Hg36. By contrast, accumulation rates were considerably lower (<20 ng g–1 yr–1) in northern high-latitude forests, where low temperatures, short growing seasons and the prevalence of needleleaf species with low stomatal density limit Hg0 assimilation (Figure S6c, d)15,42,43. Together, these two global maps indicate that spatial variation in foliar Hg accumulation is controlled primarily by vegetation traits and related physiological processes rather than by local atmospheric Hg concentrations14,15,20. The maps therefore provide a quantitative basis for assessing the role of forests in global Hg sequestration and for predicting their response to future climatic change.

Global pattern of foliar Hg0 sink

A trait-constrained global mapping of forest foliar Hg0 uptake reveals pronounced tropical dominance and a strong latitudinal gradient in the terrestrial Hg sink. To achieve this, we first harmonized multiple aboveground biomass datasets (Text S4) to generate a consistent global map of foliar biomass distribution (Figure S7), thereby reducing regional biases among existing products (Figure. S8–S9). This harmonization markedly reduces regional biases, previously up to a factor of 2-3, and provides a coherent foundation for estimating global foliar Hg sequestration. Integrating harmonized foliar biomass, trait-based Hg accumulation rates and spatially resolved leaf lifespan enables the observation-constrained mapping of global foliar Hg0 uptake, resolving canopy Hg sequestration as a biological accumulation process rather than an inferred ecosystem flux (Text S5–S9), which (1) greatly reducing global uncertainty in forest Hg0 uptake estimates from 320–3138 to 1155 ± 422 Mg yr–1, (2) revealing tropical hotspots of atmospheric Hg sequestration and (3) confirming near-steady-state Hg cycling between canopy and soil.

We developed a modeling framework to estimate foliar Hg0 uptake in ASGM (small-scale gold mining) and Au (industrial-scale gold mining) and Hg mining regions because they are typical sources of Hg pollution, while other mining types are not considered at this stage due to insufficient observational data (Text S5 and Figure S10–S12). Our results indicate that foliar Hg0 uptake by ASGM-proximal forests ranges from 10.4 to 19.6 Mg yr⁻¹ and by Au/Hg-mining-proximal forests from 5.1 to 14.9 Mg yr⁻¹, with the large ranges reflecting the choice of estimation method (see detailed discussion in Text S5). Regionally, the highest uptake in source-affected areas occurs in Africa, South America and Southeast Asia, with their contributions ranging from roughly 17% to 64%, while other regions show lower uptake between 0.6–2.4 % (Figure S12). These results indicate that although local emission sources can elevate foliar Hg concentrations near emission areas, vegetation traits and climate remain the dominant controls on foliar Hg uptake at the global scale.

Tropical rainforests, especially those in the Amazon and central Africa, stand out as the Earth’s primary sinks for atmospheric Hg0. Together, they remove about two-thirds of the global forest Hg0 uptake (792 ± 309 Mg yr–1; Figure S13), sustained by exceptionally high foliar Hg accumulation rates (50–70 ng g–1 yr–1) and dense canopy biomass (6–10 Mg ha–1). These combined effects create clear hotspots of Hg0 sequestration (40–70 μg m–2 yr–1; Fig. 3c), underscoring the disproportionate role of tropical forests in filtering globally emitted Hg despite their limited local industrial sources.

On a global scale, forests assimilate 1155 ± 422 Mg Hg0 each year, with foliage accounting for almost the entire sink (1095 ± 421 Mg yr–1) and woody tissues contributing only a minor fraction (60 ± 29 Mg yr–1; Figure S14, Text S6). Evergreen broadleaf forests dominate this process (767 ± 342 Mg yr–1), far exceeding deciduous broadleaf (213 ± 82 Mg yr–1), evergreen needleleaf (163 ± 72 Mg yr–1) and deciduous needleleaf forests (12 ± 6 Mg yr–1; p < 0.05; Figure S15). A pronounced latitudinal decline is evident, from the tropical belt towards boreal regions (Fig. 3d), reflecting the combined influence of leaf physiology, canopy structure and climatic control on global Hg sequestration.

Our modeling further highlights the strong agreement between simulated foliar Hg0 uptake and observed litterfall deposition21, providing compelling evidence that forest canopies function as a near steady-state sink for atmospheric Hg0. On a global scale, the modeled foliar uptake (1155 ± 422 Mg yr–1) closely matches litterfall Hg deposition (1180 ± 710 Mg yr–1), with consistent spatial and latitudinal patterns (slope = 0.95, R2 = 0.85; Figure S16). Differences across climatic and ecological zones remain within 30% (Figure S17), indicating that nearly all Hg assimilated by foliage is transferred to soils through litterfall. This quasi-equilibrium holds at the global scale and in mature, undisturbed forests. However, the interannual climate variability, forest succession and extreme events such as droughts, fires or deforestation may disrupt this balance at local and regional scales.

Although the spatial pattern of Hg uptake broadly resembles earlier litterfall-based assessments, the conceptual basis of the present framework differs fundamentally. Previous global estimates, including the litterfall synthesis of Wang et al. (2016)21, inferred foliar Hg uptake indirectly from litter deposition under the assumption that annual canopy uptake equals litterfall export (see detailed comparison in Text S10). Here, we resolve this flux explicitly by linking foliar Hg accumulation rates with independently constrained leaf biomass and leaf lifespan. This integration allows Hg uptake to be calculated as a physiological accumulation process rather than inferred from a terminal ecosystem flux. As a result, the model identifies the climatic and trait-based drivers that regulate Hg sequestration across biomes and provides a mechanistic explanation for the pronounced tropical dominance of the global forest Hg sink. Importantly, incorporating spatially variable leaf longevity constrains the residence time of Hg in foliage and removes a major source of uncertainty inherent in previous extrapolations. The framework therefore shifts the interpretation of the forest Hg sink from a statistical scaling of litter deposition to a trait-regulated canopy process that can be projected under changing climate and vegetation structure.

Model credibility and uncertainties

The robustness of the global foliar Hg0 uptake map is supported by multiple independent lines of validation, including cross-model comparison and observational constraints. Previous mechanistic models such as GEOS-Chem and CESM2-CLM5 report a wide uncertainty range of 320–3138 Mg yr–1 (Table 1) and rely primarily on parameterizations linked to atmospheric Hg concentrations while representing vegetation physiological controls only in simplified form. In contrast, our framework resolves Hg assimilation from leaf-level accumulation processes to canopy-scale biomass storage, thereby explicitly linking plant functional traits with global Hg uptake. Consequently, these models tend to generate spatial patterns that closely follow anthropogenic emission gradients, with elevated uptake predicted in high-emission regions such as eastern China (56–70 μg m–2 yr–1) but substantially lower values in tropical forests (21–42 μg m–2 yr–1)25,27,28. However, these patterns are inconsistent with observational evidence from litterfall deposition and foliar measurements, which show that tropical forests, despite having lower atmospheric Hg concentrations, exhibit the highest foliar Hg uptake globally44,45. In comparison, our estimates (15–30 μg m–2 yr–1 for eastern China and 40–70 μg m–2 yr–1 for the Amazon and central Africa) closely agree with observed litterfall deposition (China: 30.4 ± 16.9 and Brazil: 44.4 ± 5.7 μg m–2 yr–1, respectively; Figure S18).

The statistical approaches that multiply foliar Hg concentrations by leaf net primary production (NPP) (e.g., Zhou and Obrist, 2021, listed in Table 1) are subject to two important biases. First, these methods typically use multi-year cumulative foliar Hg concentrations that represent the total Hg accumulated over the entire leaf lifespan. Multiplying these cumulative concentrations by annual NPP effectively treats multi-year accumulation as if it occurred within a single year, leading to substantial overestimation of annual uptake. Second, leaf NPP is commonly estimated by multiplying total aboveground NPP by fixed leaf allocation coefficients. However, the fraction of biomass allocated to leaves varies considerably across forest types and climatic gradients. Applying fixed allocation factors therefore introduces systematic errors that propagate into global uptake estimates (Figure. S19, S20). For transparency, the higher NPP-based estimates listed in Table 1 for this study are shown only as methodological comparisons, illustrating the magnitude of overestimation that arises when cumulative foliar Hg concentrations are multiplied by annual productivity. The higher estimate listed in Table 1 as “this study” (1654 Mg yr–1) was derived using this NPP-based approach and is included solely to demonstrate the extent of overestimation when these methodological limitations are not accounted for (see Text S11 for details).

The machine learning approach by Chen et al. listed in Table 1 represents an important advance but differs from our method in two critical respects. First, their framework estimates Hg uptake flux by dividing foliar Hg pools by leaf lifespan, a conceptually sound formulation. However, they assume uniform leaf lifespan values for evergreen species (3–6 years) and do not account for the substantial spatial variability in leaf longevity driven by climate and nutrient availability. Second, leaf biomass is estimated indirectly from leaf area index (LAI) and specific leaf area (SLA) using the relationship leaf biomass = LAI / SLA. However, converting between these metrics requires accurate representation of leaf orientation, canopy structure and vertical gradients in SLA, which introduces additional uncertainty. In comparison, our approach harmonizes multiple foliar biomass datasets to generate observation-constrained leaf biomass estimates, thereby avoiding the propagation of uncertainties associated with indirect LAI–SLA conversions.

The uncertainties in this work fall into two categories: quantifiable and non-quantifiable. Quantifiable uncertainties arise from variability during model training and evaluation. The deep-learning models developed for foliar Hg concentration and leaf lifespan and the random forest model for leaf biomass, each contain inherent stochasticity due to weight initialization, mini-batch gradient descent and non-convex loss landscapes46,47. To assess this variability, we trained each deep-learning module independently 30 times, retaining the best model from each realization. This ensemble-based approach yielded an uncertainty range of roughly 7–23% for the foliar Hg accumulation rate model (Figure S21a). For the random forest model of leaf biomass, uncertainty was evaluated using bootstrap re-sampling to account for randomness in both training data and feature selection48, producing variability of 5–51% (Figure S21b). When propagated across modules, the combined uncertainty in the estimated global foliar Hg0 sink was 20–47% (Figure S21c), with the largest deviations (>30%) in the Amazon and African tropical rainforests. This ensemble evaluation provides one of the most comprehensive uncertainty assessments yet conducted for global Hg modeling. Importantly, the ensemble mean converged toward a stable global total, substantially reducing the one-order-of-magnitude uncertainty (320–3138 Mg yr–1) that characterizes earlier models.

Non-quantifiable uncertainties stem mainly from uneven data coverage and necessary simplifications in leaf age assignment. Observations of foliar Hg concentration, leaf lifespan and biomass remain concentrated in Asia, Europe and North America, while tropical regions, despite being major sinks, are critically underrepresented. Because age-resolved measurements from tropical forests (e.g., central Africa, the Amazon and tropical Southeast Asia) remain scarce, we conducted several indirect validations to evaluate the robustness of the assumed linear increase of foliar Hg accumulation over time.

First, for subtropical and temperate broadleaf species where age-resolved observations are available (up to six months for deciduous species and up to 1–2 years for some evergreen broadleaf species), leaf Hg concentrations increase approximately linearly with leaf age (R2 = 0.89–0.97 across sites in Figs. 1c and 1d), with no evidence of saturation within the observed age ranges. This behavior is consistent with the mechanistic understanding that Hg0 uptake is largely a passive, diffusion-limited process that proceeds as long as stomata remain active and atmospheric Hg0 is available, without apparent internal saturation or feedback inhibition.

Second, we compared our modeled foliar Hg uptake for evergreen broadleaf forests with independent litterfall Hg deposition measurements from subtropical and tropical regions, providing an additional test of the linear accumulation assumption. Across 18 sites with paired observations, the modeled foliar uptake closely matches measured litterfall Hg deposition (slope = 1.00, R2 = 0.67; Figure S22). This agreement provides strong indirect support for the assumption that foliar Hg accumulation remains approximately linear over the full leaf lifespan of tropical evergreen broadleaf forests. Nevertheless, direct age-resolved measurements of foliar Hg concentrations from tropical forests remain a key priority for future research. Such observations would further improve the precision of global Hg models and strengthen confidence in their predictive capability.

Implications for environmental sustainability and global health

Beyond refining the global Hg budget, this study establishes a trait-regulated canopy framework that links leaf lifespan, foliar Hg accumulation and canopy biomass to global atmospheric Hg uptake. Our results show that tropical rainforests, particularly the Amazon and central African basins, function as the dominant biological filter for atmospheric Hg, linking global Hg pollution directly to the integrity and stability of forest ecosystems. Exceptional canopy interception and foliar fixation rates (40–70 μg m–2 yr–1) enable these ecosystems to capture a disproportionate share of global anthropogenic Hg, functioning as an unintended biogeochemical shield for the planet. In the Peruvian Amazon, our estimates (anthropogenic Hg0 emission: 50 ± 15 t yr–1, based on anthropogenic Hg0 emission inventories from EDGAR v8.1 (2000–2020)49; foliar uptake: 30 ± 11 t yr–1) are consistent with independent inventory-based estimates (emission: 45 t yr–1; uptake: 44 t yr–1)50. When scaled to the entire Amazon Basin using the same inventory, forests assimilate 321 ± 126 t yr–1 while regional emissions reach 181 ± 54 t yr–1, showing that vegetation uptake exceeds local emissions and includes substantial contributions from long-range transported Hg. This consistency reinforces the reliability of our model and demonstrates that natural Hg assimilation by tropical forests exerts a stronger influence on regional Hg cycling than direct anthropogenic inputs.

To place the modeled foliar Hg0 uptake fluxes in a broader ecosystem context, we compared them with published throughfall Hg deposition fluxes (Figure S23). The comparison shows comparable magnitudes across climatic zones, and both fluxes display similar latitudinal gradients, indicating that tropical forests constitute the major regions of atmospheric Hg inputs. However, Hg(II) delivered via throughfall may be subject to lateral transport through surface runoff or rapid re-emission to the atmosphere owing to its relatively high reactivity51,52. In contrast, Hg transferred through litterfall decomposes more slowly and is more effectively retained in soils through association with organic matter53. Accordingly, the latitudinal pattern of global forest foliar Hg0 uptake closely mirrors that of surface soil Hg storage (0-5 cm depth), both decreasing from the equator toward high-latitude boreal regions (Figure S24). This coherent gradient indicates that foliar Hg uptake, followed by transfer to soils via litterfall, is a dominant pathway governing Hg accumulation in surface organic soil horizons. Together, the near balance between canopy assimilation and litterfall transfer suggests that intact rainforest canopies function as a large-scale Hg sink in the global Hg cycle.

Yet this extraordinary filtering capacity also imposes long-term Hg ecological risks associated with Hg accumulation. The absorbed Hg is largely stored in dynamic, climate-sensitive reservoirs, including the canopy, litter and organic-rich surface soils. In tropical ecosystems, high temperatures, humid conditions and active microbial processes facilitate the reduction of soil Hg, leading to substantial Hg0 re-emission fluxes (63 ± 9.3 μg m–2 yr–1)54. Nevertheless, sustained canopy inputs generally exceed these re-emission losses, resulting in net Hg accumulation in surface soils and making tropical forest top soils the largest Hg reservoir globally (Figure S24). Moreover, Hg in tropical forests accumulates not only in surface soils but also in deeper soil layers (Figure S25). This pattern is largely driven by strong leaching under intense precipitation, which transports a fraction of surface Hg downward, where it is retained in mineral soils as adsorbed or particle-bound Hg(II)55,56. However, this apparent stability can be rapidly disrupted by disturbances such as deforestation, fire or prolonged drought, which may remobilize stored Hg from soils and litter back to the atmosphere57,58. For instance, one study reported that net Hg0 emissions from forest soils increased from ~6 μg m–2 yr–1 before deforestation to 230 μg m–2 yr–1 afterward59. In addition, inorganic Hg in soils and litter can be converted to methylmercury and subsequently biomagnified through terrestrial and aquatic food webs by factors of 106−107, increasing Hg exposure risks for wildlife and human populations in these regions60–62.

Globally, the harmonized estimate of forest foliar Hg0 uptake (1155 ± 422 Mg yr–1) reduces the former uncertainty range (320–3138 Mg yr–1) and better constrains the terrestrial Hg uptake flux in the Hg mass balance. This refinement improves confidence in model predictions of Hg transport and deposition and facilitates more accurate assessment of emission-control effectiveness under the Minamata Convention. Quantitatively, tropical forest regions (20°S–10°N) emit 484 ± 145 Mg yr–1 of Hg0 based on anthropogenic Hg0 emission inventories from EDGAR v8.1 (2000–2020)49, mainly from artisanal gold mining63, yet remove 704 ± 279 Mg yr–1 through foliar uptake (Fig. 4b). In contrast, industrialized regions of Asia (10°N–40°N), responsible for 35–45% of current anthropogenic emissions, show weaker vegetation sinks (150 ± 48 Mg yr–1) due to lower forest cover (Fig. 4c). This hemispheric imbalance indicates that the Hg emitted in temperate and subtropical zones is partly scavenged by tropical forests thousands of kilometers away.

Over the industrial era (1850–2010), cumulative anthropogenic Hg emissions are estimated at about 336 000 t, with North America and Europe contributing ~56 %, Asia ~16 % and South America and Africa together <15 %64. A considerable fraction of this legacy Hg has been redistributed via long-range transport and is now stored in remote tropical ecosystems. This spatial mismatch between emission sources and deposition sinks intensifies ecological and human exposure in regions that contribute least to global Hg pollution. Hence, equitable global risk governance requires that both historical and ongoing emissions be incorporated into international mitigation strategies.

Future trajectories of foliar Hg0 uptake will depend not only on declining atmospheric concentrations but also on climate-driven changes in vegetation productivity and structure. Warming is projected to intensify at high latitudes, while precipitation patterns diverge regionally65. As an initial exploration, we conducted a simplified scenario analysis under the SSP1–2.6 pathway (a low greenhouse gas emissions scenario) to examine the isolated effects of projected changes in temperature and precipitation on the global forest Hg0 uptake flux during 2080–2100 (see Text S12 for details). Our results show that when precipitation alone was varied, projected changes in the global forest Hg0 uptake relative to present conditions were minimal (<0.2%, Figure S26). When temperature alone was modified, the overall response likewise remained small (~1%, Figure S27), although clear regional contrasts emerged. In high-latitude boreal regions (60–70°N), the forest Hg sink is projected to decrease slightly (by ~3 Mg yr–!), whereas temperate regions of the Northern Hemisphere show an increase of ~15 Mg yr–! (Figure S27d). Future increases in atmospheric CO2 concentrations may further influence forest Hg⁰ uptake (see Text S12). Elevated CO2 can induce partial stomatal closure, potentially reducing gas exchange and suppressing foliar Hg0 uptake66. However, stomatal conductance is co-regulated by multiple environmental drivers, including water availability, temperature and light67,68. Consequently, future changes in temperature and precipitation may partly offset CO2-induced stomatal closure, potentially moderating or even counteracting reductions in Hg0 uptake. Overall, these simplified scenario analyses highlight the complex and regionally variable responses of forest Hg cycling to future climate change. Such responses may either reinforce or offset the benefits of emission reductions, thereby complicating evaluation of the long-term effectiveness of the Minamata Convention.

In summary, our findings reveal that tropical forests have become central to the planetary Hg cycle, intercepting atmospheric Hg far beyond their regional emissions. This service, while mitigating global Hg pollution, imposes a growing burden on ecosystems and communities that are least responsible for historical releases. Environmental sustainability therefore depends not only on emission control but also on the protection and restoration of forest integrity. Safeguarding these ecosystems will preserve carbon and biodiversity stocks, prevent remobilization of legacy Hg, and sustain the biogeochemical buffering capacity that underpins atmospheric quality and global health in the Anthropocene.

Data collection

Global Hg concentration data

We compiled global datasets of Hg concentrations in forest foliage and woody tissues. Literature searches were conducted in Google Scholar and CNKI using the keywords “forest mercury” and “mercury concentration” included studies that (1) were in conducted in non-contaminated regions or sufficiently distant from point sources; (2) provided clear sampling strategies and analytical procedures; and (3) documented QA/QC protocols. In total, 2375 foliar and 1670 records of woody tissue Hg records were complied (Fig. 1a, Text S7).

Global leaf lifespan data

Leaf lifespan data were primarily obtained from the TRY database70 and the GLOPNET database38, supplemented with additional records from CNKI and Google Scholar using the keyword “leaf lifespan”. In total, 2415 LLS records were complied (Figure S28, Text S2).

Global leaf biomass data

Data on the proportion of leaf biomass to aboveground biomass (AGB) were primarily sourced from the Chinese Forest Biomass Database71 and the Eurasian regional AGB dataset72, supplemented with additional records from CNKI and Google Scholar using the keywords “forest aboveground biomass” and “forest leaf biomass”. Totally, 7510 records of leaf biomass fraction were compiled (Figure S29, Text S4).

Data preprocessing

Data preprocessing involved data cleaning, outlier removal, missing-value imputation and standardization. All predictor variables were converted to Z-scores before model fitting to remove scale differences and improve convergence.

Machine Learning

Modeling setup

The estimation framework consists of three modules: foliar Hg concentration, leaf lifespan and foliar biomass, all jointly regulated by topographic, climatic and plant traits (see Text S8 and S9 for more detail). Anthropogenic effects were additionally incorporated into the foliar Hg concentration model. The modules were hierarchical linked, with outputs from the leaf lifespan model integrated into the foliar Hg concentration model to estimate Hg concentrations in senesced foliage (i.e., litterfall Hg concentration). Annual foliar Hg accumulation rates were then derived by dividing these concentrations by the corresponding leaf lifespan and combined with foliar biomass outputs to quantify global forest foliar Hg⁰ uptake (Figure S30). All modules were implemented at a 0.1° × 0.1° spatial resolution, with predictor variables extracted on the same grid for consistency.

Feature selection

We evaluated multicollinearity among predictors using the variance inflation factor (VIF). Climate variables (temperature, precipitation, and radiation) showed strong correlations with VIF values exceeding 20. These variables were retained, as machine learning algorithms are generally tolerant to multicollinearity and prioritize predictive accuracy and generalization. Retaining correlated predictors also allows for capturing potential nonlinear interactions, thereby improving model performance. To mitigate risks associated with high feature dimensionality, we further assessed the sample-to-feature ratio (SFR). All models achieved SFR values above 50, confirming sufficient sample sizes for robust training and minimal overfitting31.

Model selection

We initially applied multiple linear regression (MLR) to model leaf lifespan, foliar Hg concentration and the leaf-to-AGB biomass ratio. However, performance was poor (R² <0.5), indicating that linear models could not capture the complexity of these relationships (Figure. S31–33). To improve predictive accuracy, we subsequently employed several machine learning approaches, including generalized linear model (GLM), support vector machines (SVM) with the Gaussian radial basis function (RBF) kernel, k-nearest neighbors (KNN), gradient boosting trees (LightGBM) and deep neural networks (DNN) (Figure S34–42).

Model optimization

Optimal model parameters were tuned using grid search with cross-validation. Model performance was evaluated using the coefficient of determination (R²), root mean square error (RMSE) and residual diagnostics (QQ and scale–location plots). R² and RMSE quantified model fit and predictive accuracy, while residual diagnostics examined error distribution and variance homogeneity. Results indicated that deep learning models performed best for foliar Hg concentration and leaf lifespan, whereas LightGBM achieved the highest accuracy for leaf biomass (Figure S43–51, model details are provided in the Text S8 and S9).

Model interpretation

We applied permutation feature importance (PFI), partial dependence plots (PDP), and SHAP values to evaluate the effects of predictors on leaf lifespan, foliar Hg concentration, leaf biomass fraction and foliar Hg assimilation. PFI measures predictor importance by the decrease in model performance after random feature permutation. PDP illustrates the response of the prediction to changes in a single feature while averaging over all other variables. SHAP values quantify each feature´s contribution to the predicted outcome.
