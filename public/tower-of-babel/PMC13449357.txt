Extensive calcified inferior vena cava and renal vein thrombosis in a preterm infant: A case report
Authors: Mohammed Almuhanna, Razan Alwabel, Moayad Almaimani, Ahmed Aldraihem, Mody Almarshad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
We present the case of a 9-month-old preterm male infant admitted to neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) in whom extensive calcified thrombosis of the inferior vena cava (IVC) and left renal vein was incidentally detected via x-ray (XR). The diagnosis was subsequently confirmed by ultrasound (US) and computed tomography (CT). Initial imaging via radiography revealed lace-like abdominal calcification. Subsequent computed tomography (CT) demonstrated dense calcification extending from the inferior vena cava (IVC) into the distal left renal vein, as well as the bilateral common iliac veins, the right external iliac vein, and the left internal iliac vein. Extensive collateral circulation was also observed, consistent with chronic venous occlusion.

Introduction

The diagnosis of venous thromboembolism (VTE) in neonates is increasing due to improvements in diagnostic techniques and advances in interventional treatments [1]. According to a later study, the incidence of VTE has increased over the past decade, rising by up to 70% between 2001 and 2007 [2]. Although neonatal venous thrombosis is widely reported, calcified thrombosis involving both the IVC and renal veins is rare, with only a few reported cases [3].

The development of neonatal VTE may occur during the prenatal, perinatal, or postnatal periods and may be associated with maternal risk factors, such as infection, diabetes mellitus, pre-eclampsia, or emergency cesarean section, or neonatal risk factors, such as central venous catheters, sepsis, congenital heart disease, or dehydration [1]. Calcified VTE commonly involves the renal veins and IVC, which are susceptible to thrombosis in the presence of VTE risk factors [4,5].

We report a case of calcified VTE involving the IVC, left renal veins, common iliac veins, and both the external and internal iliac veins.

Case presentation

A 9-month-old male infant, born preterm at 29 weeks’ gestation via spontaneous vaginal delivery, presented with a four-day history of foul-smelling, mucoid, yellow-to-green diarrhea. Associated symptoms included transit hypertension, feeding intolerance, lethargy, jaundice, and diaper dermatitis; notably, there was no reported history of fever, emesis, or respiratory distress. Additionally, the infant had a very low birth weight (VLBW) of 1.1 kg and was subsequently admitted for the management of neonatal cholestatic jaundice. This initial hospitalization was characterized by feeding intolerance and biochemical evidence of cholestasis, including marked serum elevations of total bilirubin, alanine aminotransferase (ALT), and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and abnormal renal function.

The patient had a history of neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) admission for three months due to extreme prematurity and bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD). He required prolonged invasive and non-invasive ventilatory support. He experienced neonatal sepsis on multiple occasions, treated with several courses of antibiotics and supported with respiratory care. Dysmorphic features noted were a short neck, depressed nasal bridge, macroglossia, and hypertelorism. The patient developed neonatal seizures and antiepileptic medication are given. A brain MRI performed showed imaging features of periventricular leukomalacia (PVL) and grade 2 intraventricular hemorrhage (IVH). During the NICU study, the patient developed some complication like necrotizing enterocolitis and peritonitis and meningitis.

Serial follow-up radiographs demonstrated the development of lace-like calcifications localized to the anatomical course of the inferior vena cava (IVC). Consequently, ultrasonography (US) was performed, which corroborated the presence of IVC calcification. Discussion

Coexisting IVC and renal vein thrombosis is an exceptionally rare clinical entity, with few cases reported in the literature. Calcified IVC thrombus should be considered in infants or children presenting with right-sided upper abdominal calcifications on plain radiographs. Both plain radiographs and CT may reveal characteristic lace-like, branching, or bullet-shaped para vertebral calcifications in the right upper abdomen when the IVC and renal veins are involved [4]. In our case, a preterm infant admitted to the NICU due to underlying sepsis who developed a calcified thrombus obstructing the IVC and extending into the left renal veins, common iliac veins, and both the external and internal iliac veins. X-ray revealed classic lace-like calcification, and Doppler ultrasound confirmed the absence of venous flow. The clinical course included transient hypertension, which resolved spontaneously. Minimal collateral vein formation was observed. Over six months, the thrombus resolved with no surgical intervention, highlighting the self-resolving nature of some cases and supporting conservative management in select cases.

Multiple risk factors predispose neonates to renal vein thrombosis, including dehydration, low renal plasma flow, polycythemia, anoxia, and septicemia. Additional factors include maternal diabetes mellitus, congenital renal vein anomalies, primary renal disease (rare), traumatic delivery, anemia, maternal drug use, indwelling catheters, and genetic prothrombotic disorders. Cyanotic congenital heart disease is also a contributor [4,5,6]. Like in our case the patient was septic and dehydrated. While renal vein thrombosis can occur spontaneously, thrombus formation in other venous territories, such as the IVC, is often associated with central venous catheters.

The pathogenesis of calcified IVC thrombosis remains poorly understood [1,5]. One prevailing theory suggests that IVC thrombosis may originate during fetal development, as cases can be identified as early as the first day of life [1,2]. Kidneys are susceptible to renal vein thrombosis due to their slow dual-capillary circulation and are especially vulnerable in the presence of hemoconcentration, dehydration, hypercoagulability, or hyperosmolarity [4,5,6]. Renal vein thrombosis may arise in any phase of neonatal life: prenatal, perinatal, or postnatal [4].

On ultrasound, renal vein thrombosis typically presents with enlarge kidney, increased transverse renal diameter, loss of corticomedullary differentiation, and vascular streaking. Antenatal and postnatal Doppler ultrasound studies may show absent or reduced venous flow, raising clinical suspicion. Renal vein thrombosis typically occurs during the first month of life, with a male predominance (1.6:1) [4,5]. Renal vein thrombosis accounts for approximately 10% of all neonatal venous thromboses, and 70% of cases are unilateral, of which two-thirds involve the left renal vein [7].

Importantly, complete IVC obstruction in infants and children may remain clinically silent. Neonatal renal vein thrombosis should be suspected in the presence of unilateral or bilateral renal enlargement, hematuria, proteinuria, oliguria or anuria, and abnormal renal function test [4,5,7]. In early ultrasound stages, the interlobular and interlobar sulci appear hyperechoic, followed by renal enlargement with hypoechoic pyramids. In later ultrasound stages, the kidney appears heterogeneous, with loss of corticomedullary differentiation. Doppler ultrasound typically reveals decreased or absent venous flow in multiple renal vein branches [6].

The differential diagnosis for retroperitoneal calcifications must include neoplastic (eg, neuroblastoma, ganglioneuroma, teratoma, hepatoma) and non-neoplastic conditions (eg, hepatic portal vein calcification, lymphadenopathy, paraspinal abscess). IVC thrombus, particularly when calcified, often has a characteristic lace-like or bullet-shaped appearance [2].

Renal vein thrombosis can have serious consequences, including renal failure if bilateral or renal atrophy if unilateral. Additional complications may include adrenal hemorrhage or renal rupture, potentially requiring emergency nephrectomy. Hypertension is a long-term sequela [4,6].

Regarding management, no treatment is typically necessary for unilateral renal vein thrombosis or calcified IVC thrombosis. The risks associated with anticoagulation often outweigh potential benefits for unilateral renal vein thrombosis or calcified IVC thrombosis [1]. Although thrombectomy has been successful in isolated reports, the general consensus supports observation and supportive care. Anticoagulation may be warranted in bilateral renal vein thrombosis [7]. Furthermore, angiographic investigations are not recommended due to their invasive nature and limited clinical value [1].

In conclusion, this case highlights a rare but instructive presentation of calcified IVC and renal vein thrombosis in a preterm infant with sepsis. The presence of transient hypertension and spontaneous resolution without intervention reinforces the importance of individualized, conservative management in select cases. Recognition of imaging features and awareness of associated risk factors remain key to timely diagnosis and appropriate follow-up.
