The Role of the Notch1 Signaling Pathway in the Pathogenesis and Treatment of Diabetic Foot: A Review
Authors: Yang Zhi, Hong Weiqi, Zhou Caibo, Chen Binqi, Zeng Duo, Liu Lang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Diabetic foot (DF) is a severe and common complication of diabetes mellitus, involving peripheral neuropathy, vascular disease, immune-inflammatory disorders, and impaired tissue repair, with high amputation rates and a heavy medical burden. The Notch1 signaling pathway is highly conserved and participates in regulating cell proliferation, differentiation, apoptosis, angiogenesis, and inflammatory responses. Emerging evidence indicates that dysregulated Notch1 signaling is closely associated with the pathological progression of diabetic foot ulcers (DFU). This review systematically summarizes the role of Notch1 signaling in angiogenesis, inflammation, peripheral neuropathy, and cell fate regulation during DF pathogenesis, and discusses the potential therapeutic strategies targeting Notch1 pathway for DFU. Introduction

Diabetes mellitus poses a significant challenge to global public health, and the prevention and management of its complications have become a major focus of medical research. DF refers to infection, ulceration, or tissue destruction in the feet of patients with diabetes resulting from peripheral neuropathy (PNP) and/or peripheral arterial disease (PAD) [1]. As a common and severe complication in patients with diabetes, DF not only significantly reduces patients’ quality of life but also increasingly burdens healthcare systems. Diabetic foot infection (DFI) is the leading cause of nontraumatic amputation worldwide. Statistics indicate that approximately 20% of diabetes-related hospitalizations in the USA are due to DF. Its exceptionally high disability and mortality rates substantially diminish patients’ quality of life and reduce average life expectancy by approximately 2 years [2]. The pathogenesis of DF is complex. With ongoing in-depth research, the Notch1 signaling pathway, a crucial mediator of intercellular communication, has been identified as critically important in the pathological progression of DF. It not only participates in regulating angiogenesis and maintaining endothelial cell function but also plays an indispensable role in key physiological processes such as the homeostasis of inflammatory responses and the orderly progression of cell proliferation and apoptosis. Research has found that abnormal activation of the Notch1 signaling pathway can significantly affect multiple key aspects of DF lesions. Furthermore, aberrant activation of the Notch signaling pathway may also promote the proliferation, survival, and migration of tumor cells and surrounding stromal cells, potentially increasing patients’ risk of developing tumors [3]. Therefore, investigating the Notch1 signaling pathway to mitigate the incidence of DF is of paramount importance.

This article is based on previously published studies and does not contain any new studies with human participants or animals performed by any of the authors.

Notch1 Signaling Pathway

The Notch gene was first discovered in Drosophila during the 1910s, and cloned and sequenced in the 1980s. It was named after the characteristic notched wing phenotype observed in mutants, marking the inception of research into the Notch signaling pathway. The Notch pathway exhibits remarkable universality and high conservation across vertebrate and invertebrate systems. Furthermore, it participates in embryonic development, tissue homeostasis, and disease pathogenesis, exerting profound and extensive influences on organ morphogenesis and development [4]. From an evolutionary biology perspective, this universality and conservation observed throughout biological evolution robustly affirm the pivotal role of the Notch signaling pathway in maintaining fundamental life processes and shaping biological structures.

Molecular Mechanism of the Notch Signaling Pathway

The Notch signaling pathway primarily consists of four receptors (Notch1–4) and five ligands (JAG1/2, DLL1/3/4), typically activated via cell-to-cell contact [5]. Its core components—receptors, ligands, and the DNA-binding transcription factor RBPJκ (CSL, C-promoter binding factor 1)—constitute a highly conserved intercellular signaling cascade. Upon receptor–ligand binding, it regulates cell differentiation, proliferation, and survival, while modulating immune functions such as macrophage and T-cell activity [6]. Notch signaling operates through canonical and noncanonical pathways. The canonical pathway involves ligand binding followed by ADAM10/17-mediated cleavage, γ-secretase-dependent release of the Notch intracellular domain (NICD), and activation of the CSL transcriptional complex to regulate target genes (e.g., HES/HEY, Hairy/enhancer-of-split/ Hairy/enhancer-of-split related with YRPW motif). In contrast, the noncanonical pathway is CSL-independent and cross-regulates cell fate through interactions with Wnt, nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB ), and phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase/protein kinase B (PI3K/AKT ) pathways [5]. Following ligand–receptor engagement, proteolytic cleavage releases NICD, which translocates to the nucleus via its nuclear localization signal. NICD binds RBPJκ to form a transcriptional activation complex that precisely modulates downstream target gene expression, thereby determining cellular differentiation fates. For example, Delta ligand binding (primarily to Notch1/Notch4) triggers Notch signaling cascades, NICD release and transcriptional complex formation regulate angiogenesis, cell development, and differentiation through intercellular communication [7]. Notch signaling exerts broad physiological effects: In bone-related cells (e.g., mesenchymal stem cells [MSCs], osteoblasts, and osteoclasts), it modulates differentiation and activity, indirectly influencing bone formation/resorption and contributing to osteoporosis pathogenesis [8]. In chronic myeloid leukemia (CML) cells, Notch acts as a tumor suppressor by promoting proliferation or inhibiting differentiation. Conversely, its hyperactivation in vascular endothelial cells induces vascular toxicity, causing developmental anomalies and dysfunction [9]. In diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL), Notch1 activation in bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) triggers the inositol-requiring enzyme 1/spliced X-box binding protein 1 (IRE1/XBP1s) endoplasmic reticulum stress pathway. This drives macrophage secretion of programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) and pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-6, IL-4), ultimately suppressing chimeric antigen receptor T-cell (CAR-T) cytotoxicity [10].

Role of the Notch1 Signaling Pathway in Cellular Proliferation, Differentiation, and Apoptosis

Regarding cellular proliferation, the Notch1 signaling pathway exhibits a dual regulatory role, capable of either promoting or inhibiting proliferation. This pathway regulates epithelial homeostasis in the gastric corpus of mice and humans, playing a fundamental role in promoting the proliferation of stem/progenitor cells. Activation of Notch signaling in the adult gastric corpus enhances proliferation while blocking differentiation [11]. Concurrently, the Notch1 signaling pathway is a critical factor for maintaining the differentiation and proliferation of human ventricular-like cardiomyocytes. Notch1 deficiency reduces first heart field (FHF) progenitors, ventricular-like cardiomyocytes, and cardiomyocyte proliferation, leading to left ventricular hypoplasia and diminished ventricular volume [12]. Conversely, Notch1 may suppress proliferation in certain cancer contexts. In triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), Notch1 is a key mediator through which miR-3178 exerts its tumor-suppressive effects. Reduced miR-3178 decreases Notch1 expression, inhibiting NF-κB activity, epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), and cellular proliferation, migration, and invasion, thereby suppressing tumor growth [13]. In laryngeal carcinoma (one of the most common head and neck malignancies with a rising incidence), SNHG1 promotes cancer progression by facilitating laryngeal cancer cell proliferation via inhibition of the Notch1 signaling pathway [14].

In addition, the Notch1 signaling pathway plays a central regulatory role in cellular differentiation and apoptosis. It modulates multiple signal transduction pathways and the expression levels of associated genes, thereby maintaining normal physiological states of cells and tissues. Specifically, in intestinal epithelial cell differentiation, the Notch1 signaling pathway maintains the stem cell state by preserving the leucine-rich repeat-containing G-protein coupled receptor 5 positive (Lgr5+) stem cell pool at the crypt base, preventing premature stem cell differentiation. Active Notch1 signaling pathway promotes differentiation toward absorptive cell lineages while suppressing secretory cell fate commitment [15]. Notch1 activity is closely linked to apoptosis. In malignant pleural mesothelioma (MPM), high Notch1 expression inhibits pro-apoptotic proteins (e.g., Noxa, phorbol-12-myristate-13-acetate-induced protein 1), promoting cell survival. Pharmacological blockade of Notch1 signaling pathway (e.g., by Metformin or DAPT, N-[N-(3,5-Difluorophenacetyl)-l-alanyl]-S-phenylglycine t-butyl ester) upregulates Noxa, activates caspase cascades, and ultimately induces apoptosis [16]. Similarly, in other cancers, Notch1 activation stimulates proliferation and suppresses apoptosis, whereas targeted Notch1 inhibition restores apoptotic sensitivity and inhibits proliferation [17]. In summary, the Notch1 signaling pathway critically regulates numerous signaling cascades and gene expression networks essential for maintaining normal cellular and tissue physiology, exerting significant influence over fundamental processes including proliferation, differentiation, and apoptosis.

Diabetic Foot

Overview of Diabetic Foot

DF is a severe chronic complication of diabetes, with a global DFU prevalence of approximately 6.3%. It is a leading cause of nontraumatic lower limb amputation worldwide [18]. Approximately 15% of patients with diabetes develop DF in their lifetime, and 14–24% of these patients undergo amputation due to infection, osteomyelitis, or gangrene [19]. Analysis of infected DFU has identified 12,292 microbial isolates, predominantly including, Gram-negative bacteria and Escherichia coli [18]. The pathogenesis involves peripheral neuropathy, peripheral arterial disease (PAD), immune dysfunction, and persistent hyperglycemia, all of which impair tissue repair and promote chronic inflammation and infection. To reduce the prevalence of DF, preventive measures are essential. And combined strategies—either ultrasonic debridement + negative pressure or platelet-rich plasma (PRP) + negative pressure should be prioritized for DF management [20, 21].

Pathophysiological Basis of DF

DF arises from combined effects of lower extremity neuropathy and vasculopathy [22]. Patients with diabetes exhibit a higher risk and faster progression of lower extremity arteriosclerosis, mainly affecting infrapopliteal arteries [23, 24].

PAD is highly prevalent but often asymptomatic in patients with DF owing to concurrent neuropathy, which masks typical symptoms and increases amputation risk. Therefore, it is particularly important to use the six-category Wagner Grading System for assessment. Chronic hyperglycemia promotes advanced glycation end-product (AGE) accumulation, oxidative stress, and inflammatory cascade amplification, further disrupting angiogenesis, cell proliferation, and wound healing [25–29].

Pathophysiological Mechanisms of Diabetic Foot

DF represent one of the most severe complications of diabetes and the most common cause of diabetes-related hospitalizations. Their pathogenesis involves complex mechanisms, including peripheral vascular disease, neuropathy, infection, hyperglycemia, and foot structural abnormalities [30]. Atherosclerosis induces endothelial injury, leading to foam cell formation, plaque rupture, and ultimately thrombosis. In patients with diabetes, this primarily affects infrapopliteal arteries (anterior/posterior tibial arteries), resulting in lower limb ischemic ulcers or gangrene that constitute DF. Hyperglycemia, advanced glycation end-products (AGEs), and oxidized low-density lipoprotein (LDL) damage neural cells, causing sensory/motor/autonomic nerve dysfunction that contributes to ulcer formation. Wound infections predominantly arise from bacterial or fungal pathogens in the context of immunodeficiency [31].

Diabetic peripheral neuropathy (DPN) refers to peripheral nerve dysfunction in patients with diabetes after excluding other causes, which in severe cases may progress to foot ulceration and even amputation. Current evidence indicates that hyperglycemia, dyslipidemia, and impaired insulin signaling constitute the initiating factors for the pathophysiological cascade of DPN. When proteins and lipids are exposed to hyperglycemia, they form highly reactive AGEs. These bind to receptor for advanced glycation end-products (RAGE), activating chemokines and pro-inflammatory markers. This triggers downstream inflammatory pathways, induces sustained inflammatory responses, and causes microvascular damage and glial cell dysfunction [32]. Hyperglycemia is a key feature of the DF microenvironment and is associated with insulin dysregulation (deficiency or resistance). Hyperglycemia impairs wound healing through multiple pathways, including the suppression of antioxidant expression, the induction of advanced glycation end products (AGEs) generation, and the promotion of oxidative stress, thereby exacerbating the inflammatory response. Furthermore, hyperglycemia can lead to activation of the polyol pathway within nerve cells. In this pathway, excessive glucose is converted to sorbitol and fructose, causing elevated intracellular osmotic pressure, cellular swelling, and deformation. This adversely affects the normal structure and function of nerve cells. Chronic hyperglycemia also weakens the function of the body’s antioxidant defense system in patients with diabetes, leading to nerve cell dysfunction. Sensory nerve damage results in the loss of protective sensation (LOPS) in the feet, increasing the risk of bacterial infection and hindering the resolution of inflammation. Consequently, the wound healing process is impeded, and wounds remain in the inflammatory phase for prolonged periods, failing to progress normally through the healing stages [33]. Integrated multiomic analyses have further revealed that diabetic ulcers present global dysregulation of transcriptomic, proteomic, and metabolic networks, in which disordered protein–nucleic acid interactions act as a central hub leading to metabolic disturbance, persistent inflammation, and defective tissue repair [81]. The invasion of pathogens or fungi, coupled with factors such as uncontrolled infection, ultimately contributes to the development of DF.

Summary of key studies on the impact of Notch1 on diabetic foot

Notch1 Signaling Pathway in Angiogenesis and Vasculopathy

Angiogenesis and vasculopathy play critical roles in the pathological process of diabetic foot. Notch1 signaling is a key regulator of endothelial function and angiogenic activity [34, 35]. In diabetes, the hyperglycemic microenvironment activates the Notch1 signaling pathway, leading to endothelial dysfunction, insufficient angiogenesis, and peripheral ischemia, which further promote the development of diabetic foot ulcers [36, 37]. This deleterious Dll4–Notch1 feedback loop has been directly confirmed to impair angiogenesis and delay re-epithelialization in diabetic wounds, thereby establishing a critical mechanistic link between Notch1 hyperactivation and impaired DFU healing [53]. Dysregulated Notch1 signaling impairs vascular repair and increases the risk of persistent wound formation, thereby accelerating the pathological progression of diabetic foot.

Notch1 Signaling Pathway in Inflammatory Response

The Notch1 signaling pathway is a crucial inflammatory regulator that promotes the expression of pro‑inflammatory factors, such as TNF‑α, IL‑6, and IL‑1β [38–41]. In diabetic foot, aberrantly activated Notch1 signaling exaggerates the local inflammatory response and prolongs the inflammatory phase, which further inhibits fibroblast proliferation and collagen synthesis essential for wound repair [42]. Notably, Notch1 signaling directly governs the plasticity and reparative function of fibroblasts in diabetic wounds; its sustained activation impairs myofibroblast differentiation and extracellular matrix remodeling, further perpetuating ulcer nonhealing [75]. Overactive Notch1 signaling therefore acts as a key driver of chronic nonhealing diabetic foot ulcers by maintaining a sustained pro‑inflammatory microenvironment.

Notch1 Signaling Pathway in Diabetic Neuropathy

Abnormal activation of the Notch1 signaling pathway may be implicated in the pathogenesis of diabetic neuropathy. In diabetic neuropathy models, the mRNA and protein expression levels of Notch1 and its downstream gene HES1 are significantly upregulated. Furthermore, exposure to hyperglycemia in vitro also increased the expression of Notch1 and HES1 in cultured dorsal root ganglion (DRG) neurons. This indicates that the hyperglycemic environment may activate the Notch1 signaling pathway. The Notch1 signaling pathway is activated in diabetic neuropathy, and its activation is associated with increased neuronal hyperexcitability. This increased hyperexcitability may underlie pain behaviors observed in diabetic neuropathy, such as mechanical allodynia and thermal hyperalgesia [43].

Notch1 Signaling in Cell Proliferation, Differentiation, and Apoptosis

The Notch1 signaling pathway plays a pivotal role in cell fate determination, differentiation, proliferation, and apoptosis, and its dysregulation is closely implicated in the pathogenesis of multiple diseases, especially diabetic foot. The Notch1 pathway is critical for intestinal epithelial cell differentiation by governing stem cell proliferation and lineage commitment. Under physiological conditions, Notch1 signaling promotes the differentiation of absorptive cells while inhibiting secretory cell formation, whereas indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase 1 (IDO1) can interact with aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AHR) to suppress Notch1 activation and shift differentiation toward secretory cells [15].

Notch1 signaling also controls immune regulation, cell metabolism, and growth. In CCRF-CEM (an acute lymphoblastic leukemia cell line) cells, Notch1 overexpression promotes proliferation and suppresses apoptosis, whereas Notch1 silencing inhibits cell cycle progression and induces apoptosis in Jurkat (an acute T-cell leukemia cell line) cells [44]. In cervical cancer cells, knockdown of Notch1 reduces viability and migration and promotes apoptosis [45]. In tongue cancer, upregulated Notch1 enhances proliferation, invasion, and migration, while Notch1 inhibition exerts the opposite effects [46]. In human pulmonary arterial endothelial cells, Notch1 promotes proliferation via downregulating p21 and inhibits apoptosis through B-cell lymphoma 2 (Bcl-2) and Survivin (BIRC5; baculoviral IAP repeat-containing 5) [47]. Moreover, Notch1 signaling suppresses proliferation and promotes apoptosis in gastric and pancreatic cancer cells [48, 49] but enhances proliferation and reduces apoptosis in glioblastoma cells [50].

In summary, Notch1 signaling regulates proliferation, differentiation, and apoptosis in a variety of cell types, supporting its value as an important therapeutic target. Role and related research summary of Notch1 signaling pathway in diabetic foot

Current Status of Clinical Experiments on the Notch1 Signaling Pathway in Promoting Diabetic Foot Healing

The Notch1 signaling pathway plays a crucial role in normal wound healing. However, in diabetic wounds, the Notch1 signaling pathway is aberrantly activated. The significantly upregulated expression of Notch1 and its downstream genes (such as Hes1 and Hey1) inhibits the proliferation and differentiation of epidermal stem cells (EPSCs), leading to delayed wound healing. Therefore, inhibition of the Notch1 signaling pathway can significantly improve diabetic wound healing [51]. Studies have shown that activation of the Notch1 signaling pathway can regulate macrophage differentiation and promote the inflammatory response. Notch1 signaling pathway is activated by hyperglycemia in diabetic skin; this activation inhibits diabetic wound healing. Conversely, blocking the Notch1 signaling pathway promotes macrophage polarization towards the M2 phenotype, thereby suppressing the inflammatory response and enhancing wound healing [52]. Furthermore, in a study using diabetic animal models, topical application of γ-secretase inhibitors (GSIs), DAPT, or L-685,458 significantly accelerated the wound healing rate. In addition, local inhibition of the Notch1 signaling pathway markedly improved wound healing in diabetic animal models [53]. Kallikrein-binding protein (KBP) is a key regulator of excessive inflammation in DFU. By activating both the Notch1 and NF-κB signaling pathways, KBP promotes M1 macrophage polarization, resulting in delayed wound healing. Thus, blocking KBP activity can improve diabetic wound healing [54]. Collectively, these findings unequivocally demonstrate the significant role of the Notch1 signaling pathway in diabetic wound healing and provide a theoretical foundation and research direction for developing novel therapeutic approaches for DFU.

Potential Applications of the Notch1 Signaling Pathway in Diabetic Foot Treatment

Activation of the Notch1 signaling pathway promotes the proliferation and migration of vascular endothelial cells, accelerating the angiogenesis process. Notch1 signaling pathway can enhance the sensitivity of endothelial cells to angiogenesis-related growth factors by regulating specific gene expression within them, thereby promoting the formation of new blood vessels. For instance, insulin-like growth factor 2 mRNA-binding protein 2 (IGF2BP2 ) increases the stability of TRPM2-AS (TRPM2 antisense RNA) through N6-methyladenosine (m6A) modification. TRPM2-AS is then transported via exosomes to human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs), activating the Notch1 signaling pathway and promoting angiogenesis [55]. miR-21-5p targets Notch1, inhibiting the expression of Notch1 and DLL4, thereby activating the expression of vascular endothelial growth factor A (VEGFA) and promoting angiogenesis [56]. Another example is salidroside, which promotes the regeneration of brain microvascular endothelial cells and angiogenesis by activating the Notch/integrin subunit beta 1 (ITGB1) signaling pathway [57]. Furthermore, the Notch1 signaling pathway possesses the capacity to regulate the activity of inflammatory cells, such as macrophages and T lymphocytes. Taking macrophages as an example: phosphatase and tensin homolog (PTEN ) in macrophages controls stimulator of interferon genes (STING )-mediated inflammatory responses and hepatocyte necrosis by regulating the Notch1/NRF2 signaling pathway. Under inflammatory conditions, the Notch1 intracellular domain (NICD) and NRF2 colocalize within the macrophage nucleus and interact through RBPjκ. Loss of Notch1 reduces the binding of RBPjκ and nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (NRF2), activating the STING signaling pathway and subsequently influencing the initiation and progression of the inflammatory response [58]. By inhibiting the activation of relevant inflammatory signaling pathways, the Notch1 signaling pathway can further attenuate the severity of the inflammatory response, fostering a favorable microenvironment for tissue repair in DF. Single-cell RNA sequencing has further uncovered endothelial cell heterogeneity in diabetic foot ulcers, identifying distinct functional subpopulations regulated by Notch1 signaling that determine angiogenic capacity and wound repair efficiency [82].

The role of the Notch1 signaling pathway in the pathogenesis of DFU and its potential therapeutic targets can be broadly summarized as follows: Factors in the DFU microenvironment, including high glucose molecules, advanced glycation end products (AGEs), pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6), and hypoxia, can activate the Notch1 receptor in endothelial cells, macrophages, and keratinocytes. The Notch1 receptor undergoes S2 cleavage mediated by ADAM10/17 metalloproteases and S3 cleavage mediated by the γ-secretase complex, releasing the Notch1 intracellular domain (NICD). NICD forms a complex with mastermind-like protein (MAML) and CSL/RBPJ, regulating the expression of target genes such as HES1, HEY1, VEGF, and inflammatory mediators. This process leads to endothelial dysfunction (impaired angiogenesis, microvascular ischemia), macrophage M1 polarization (triggering chronic inflammation), and fibroblast/keratinocyte dysfunction (reduced proliferation and migration, increased apoptosis), ultimately resulting in clinical outcomes such as delayed wound healing, chronic ulceration, infection, and tissue necrosis. Therapeutic targets for this pathway include γ-secretase inhibitors (e.g., DAPT, L-685,458), DLL4–Notch1 blockers, miRNA/exosome therapy, and anti-inflammatory and pro-angiogenic combination therapy. Treatment and Challenges

Therapeutically, intervention strategies targeting the Notch1 signaling pathway, such as Notch1 inhibitors, have been proposed as potential treatments for diabetic neuropathy. A study investigating the therapeutic effect of Schwann cell (SC)-derived exosomes (SC-Exo) on peripheral neuropathy demonstrated that SC-Exo can reduce Notch1 expression by modulating its target miRNAs (miR-34a and miR-146a), thereby exerting a therapeutic effect on peripheral neuropathy [59]. Inhibiting the Notch1 signaling pathway can reduce neuronal damage and promote nerve repair, while targeting its roles in regulating apoptosis, angiogenesis, and tissue repair also constitutes a viable therapeutic approach: Palbociclib significantly inhibits gastric cancer cell proliferation and induces apoptosis by suppressing Notch signaling pathway activity [60]. Inhibiting the Notch1 signaling pathway or activating autophagy can reduce corneal epithelial cell (CEC) apoptosis induced by herpes simplex virus type 1 (HSV-1) infection [61]. miR-200b, induced under high-glucose conditions, targets and inhibits Notch1, so activating the Notch1 signaling pathway can restore endothelial cell function, promote angiogenesis, and accelerate wound healing [62]. Dragon Blood Derivative (DBD) promotes the tube-forming ability of high glucose-induced human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) and accelerates diabetic wound healing by activating the Notch signaling pathway [63]. Peimine, an alkaloid from Fritillaria, significantly accelerates skin wound repair in mice by activating the Notch1 signaling pathway in fibroblasts [64]. In summary, modulating the Notch1 signaling pathway according to specific contexts (e.g., using Notch1 inhibitors or activators) can serve as a novel therapeutic strategy for DF.

In addition, gene editing technology can be used to modulate Notch1 expression levels, or natural Notch1 antagonistic molecules such as Delta-like ligands can be utilized. For instance, DLL4 can effectively stimulate Notch1 expression in Notch1-mutated Mino cells, enhancing tumor cell migration and angiogenesis [65], suggesting that specific inhibition of the Notch1-ligand–receptor interaction may also represent a novel therapeutic direction for DF. Other therapeutic methods for DF include topical oxygen therapy (TOT) [66]; negative pressure wound therapy (NPWT), which evacuates deep necrotic tissue and exudate from the wound to reduce infection risk and promote wound healing [67]; and topical gel therapy (e.g., growth factor gels), which maintains wound moisture, promotes keratinocyte migration, collagen formation, and angiogenesis, while reducing scar formation [68].

Although activation of the Notch1 signaling pathway exerts a positive effect on DF treatment, therapeutic intervention targeting this pathway carries significant potential risks. First, abnormal activation or inhibition of the Notch1 signaling pathway has carcinogenic potential. Research investigating the impact of forkhead box M1 (FOXM1) on gastric cancer cell proliferation, invasion, and drug resistance via regulation of circular RNA circ_NOTCH1 (circular RNA NOTCH1) showed that FOXM1 interference silenced the Notch1 signaling pathway, suppressing gastric cancer cell proliferation, invasion, and drug resistance [69]. Notch1 exhibits oncogenic properties in certain cancers: in T-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (T-ALL), it promotes cell growth, metabolism, and survival by activating pathways such as PI3K/AKT/mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) and NF-κB, and by directly regulating transcription factors such as myc proto-oncogene protein (MYC) [70]. In chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL), Notch1 gene mutations are a validated prognostic marker, and aberrant Notch1 signaling pathway activation confers a growth and survival advantage to tumor clones [71]. In breast cancer, Notch1 signaling pathway activation leads to nuclear translocation of the Notch1 intracellular domain (NICD), which binds to p65 of the NF-κB pathway to enhance its activity, thereby promoting MenalNV expression and augmenting tumor cell invasion and migration [72]. Second, while the pro-angiogenic role of the Notch1 signaling pathway facilitates DF wound healing, excessive activation may induce pathological angiogenesis, such as IL-33, promoting pathological retinal neovascularization by enhancing deubiquitination and stabilization of NICD [73]. Third, patients with DF often have compromised immune function, since the Notch1 signaling pathway participates in immune cell differentiation and functional regulation, its modulation may trigger excessive inflammatory responses or immunosuppression, increasing infection susceptibility. Mouse model studies demonstrate that the Notch1 signaling pathway promotes inflammation and multi-organ inflammation by inducing CD22 expression, which destabilizes regulatory T cells (Tregs) [74]. Fourth, the Notch1 signaling pathway regulates the physiological activities of fibroblasts in diabetic wounds; increased Notch1 activity in these cells inhibits their growth, migration, and differentiation into myofibroblasts. Both inhibition and activation of the Notch1 signaling pathway can prolong healing time, and dysregulation may lead to scar formation or hinder wound closure; inter-individual variability may also result in inconsistent therapeutic outcomes [75].

The aforementioned risks pose potential threats to patient health. As indicated, aberrant Notch1 activation generally impedes wound healing (warranting inhibition), while context-dependent activation can support angiogenesis and repair processes. Further in-depth research is required to explore how to integrate Notch1 signaling pathway-related findings with other therapeutic modalities to develop combinatorial treatment strategies for DF.

Summary

The Notch1 signaling pathway acts as a key regulator in the pathogenesis of diabetic foot by modulating angiogenesis, inflammatory response, peripheral neuropathy, and cell behavior [76]. In diabetic microenvironments, aberrant activation of Notch1 signaling pathway impairs endothelial function, promotes chronic inflammation, and inhibits tissue repair [77–79]. Targeted inhibition or precise modulation of the Notch1 signaling pathway represents a promising strategy for accelerating diabetic wound healing. Further studies are needed to explore safe and specific Notch1 modulators for clinical translation in diabetic foot treatment [80].

Overall, within the frontiers of medical research, the in-depth elucidation of the Notch1 signaling pathway has emerged as a critical focus requiring further breakthroughs for addressing the complex condition of DFU.
