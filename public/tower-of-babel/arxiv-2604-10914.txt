Cusp Form Dimensions, Lattice Uniqueness, and LP Sharpness for Sphere Packing in Dimensions 8 and 24
Jian Zhou

Abstract
The Cohn–Elkies linear programming (LP) bound for sphere packing is known to be sharp in dimensions 8 and 24—the dimensions of the E 8 E_{8} and Leech lattice packings—but in no other dimension above 2. We investigate why by examining three independent necessary conditions for LP sharpness, drawn from number theory, lattice theory, and conformal field theory. The first condition, dim S d / 2 ​ ( SL 2 ​ ( ℤ ) ) ≤ 1 \dim S_{d/2}(\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}))\leq 1 , bounds the freedom in theta series and rules out all d ≥ 48 d\geq 48 . The second, derived from Cohn and Triantafillou’s dual LP obstruction via cusp forms for the congruence subgroup Γ 0 ​ ( 2 ) \Gamma_{0}(2) , explains why LP sharpness fails in dimensions 16 and 32 despite the first condition being satisfied. The third, via the Hartman–Mazáč–Rastelli correspondence between LP bounds and the modular bootstrap for Narain conformal field theories, reinterprets LP sharpness as the existence of an extremal CFT. We formulate a conjecture that these three conditions are equivalent for d ≡ 0 ( mod 8 ) d\equiv 0\pmod{8} , and observe that the Bost–Connes quantum statistical system provides a natural algebraic framework in which all three perspectives are connected through the Hecke algebra.

1.1
Two miraculous dimensions

The sphere packing problem in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
has been solved in only four dimensions:
d=1
(trivial),
d=2
(hexagonal, Thue 1910),
d=3
(Kepler’s conjecture, Hales
[

[

E_{8}
and Leech) were shown to be
universally optimal

[
4

The proofs in dimensions 8 and 24 use the Cohn–Elkies linear programming bound
[
5
]
, which provides upper bounds on packing density via auxiliary radial “magic functions.” The LP bound is
sharp
—matching the density of the best known packing—only in
d=1,2,8,24

[
6

not

This situation motivates a precise question:
among dimensions
d\equiv 0\pmod{8}

d=8
and
d=24
?

1.2
Three perspectives, one answer

We argue that the answer emerges from the intersection of three independent conditions, each arising from a different mathematical domain:

(A)

Number theory (primal constraint):
The cusp form dimension
\dim S_{d/2}(\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}))
must be at most 1. This constrains the freedom in theta series of even unimodular lattices and rules out
d\geq 48
.

(B)

Lattice theory (dual obstruction):
Cusp forms for the congruence subgroup
\Gamma_{0}(2)
provide dual LP obstructions (Cohn–Triantafillou
[
6
]
). When
\dim S_{d/2}(\Gamma_{0}(2))
exceeds
\dim S_{d/2}(\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}))
, extra cusp forms supply dual feasible points that prevent LP sharpness—unless the target lattice has exceptional structure (as the Leech lattice does in
d=24
).

(C)

Physics (modular bootstrap):
The LP bound for sphere packing in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
equals the modular bootstrap bound for Narain CFTs with
U(1)^{d/2}\times U(1)^{d/2}
symmetry (Hartman–Mazáč–Rastelli
[
16
]
). LP sharpness is equivalent to the existence of an extremal Narain CFT saturating the bootstrap bound.

Conditions (A)–(C) are individually necessary but not individually sufficient. We observe that they are satisfied simultaneously only for
d=8
and
d=24
, and conjecture that their conjunction is both necessary and sufficient.

2.2
The Bost–Connes system

The Bost–Connes system
[
2
]
is a quantum statistical mechanical system whose partition function is
\zeta(\beta)
and whose Hecke operators recover the classical operators of Hecke
[
17
]
. The system exhibits a phase transition at
\beta_{c}=1
: a unique KMS state for
\beta\leq 1
gives way to a family parametrized by
\mathrm{Gal}(\mathbb{Q}^{\mathrm{ab}}/\mathbb{Q})
for
\beta>1
.

We include the BC system not as a source of new theorems, but as a
conceptual framework
: the Hecke algebra that appears in the BC system is the same algebra that controls modular forms (Section
3
), the Cohn–Elkies LP bound (Section
5
), and the modular bootstrap (Section
6
). The BC phase transition provides a useful (if informal) analogy for the transition from LP-sharp to LP-non-sharp dimensions.

3.1
Gleason’s theorem

Theorem 3.1

(Gleason, 1970;
[
14
]
)
.

The weight enumerator of any Type II (doubly-even self-dual) binary code of length
n
lies in
M_{n/2}(\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}))
under the substitution
x\mapsto\theta_{3}
,
y\mapsto\theta_{2}
.

Remark 3.2
.

Gleason’s theorem bounds the number of
distinct weight enumerators
, not the number of codes. At
n=16
,
\dim M_{8}=1
gives a unique weight enumerator, but there are two inequivalent Type II codes (
d_{16}^{+}
and
e_{8}\oplus e_{8}
).

4
The Primal Constraint:
\dim S_{k}(\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}))

Proposition 4.1
.

If
\dim S_{d/2}(\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}))=0
, then every even unimodular lattice of rank
d
has the same theta series. If
\dim S_{d/2}\leq 1
, the theta series has at most one free parameter.

This suggests a necessary condition for LP sharpness:

Conjecture 4.2
.

If the Cohn–Elkies LP bound is sharp in dimension
d
(with
d\equiv 0\pmod{8}
), then
\dim S_{d/2}(\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}))\leq 1
.

Remark 4.3
.

The motivation is as follows: when
\dim S_{d/2}\geq 2
, the theta series of even unimodular lattices have at least two free parameters, and the proliferation of distinct lattices with different local structure appears to prevent the tight sign constraints required by the Cohn–Elkies method. This is consistent with all known data—no LP proof exists or is expected for
d\geq 48
—but we do not have a rigorous proof. For
d=32
and
d=40
, where
\dim S_{d/2}=1
, LP non-sharpness is established by other means (Cohn–Triantafillou
[
6
]
).

The condition
\dim S_{k}\leq 1
is satisfied for
d=8,16,24,32,40
, but LP sharpness holds only for
d=8
and
24
. The condition is necessary but far from sufficient.

5
The Dual Obstruction: Cohn–Triantafillou and
\Gamma_{0}(2)

5.1
LP duality and modular form obstructions

Cohn and Triantafillou
[
6
]
introduced a systematic method to prove LP
non-sharpness
: by constructing feasible points in the dual LP using modular forms for
\Gamma_{0}(2)
, they showed that the LP bound in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
exceeds the best known packing density by a provable factor in
d=12,16,20,28,32
.

The key insight is that Viazovska’s magic functions live in the space of (quasi)modular forms for
\Gamma_{0}(2)
, not just
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
. The relevant space is therefore
S_{k}(\Gamma_{0}(2))
, whose dimension grows faster than
\dim S_{k}(\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}))
.

5.2
The role of
\Gamma_{0}(2)

Viazovska’s interpolation formula
[
27
,
25
]
constructs the magic function from integral transforms of quasimodular forms for
\Gamma_{0}(2)
. The interpolation problem requires specifying values and derivatives at points
\sqrt{2n}
(
n=1,2,\ldots
). The number of constraints is controlled by the shell structure of the target lattice, while the degrees of freedom are controlled by
\dim S_{k}(\Gamma_{0}(2))
.

Table
1
shows that
d=8
is the
only
dimension where both
\dim S_{k}(\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}))=0
and
\dim S_{k}(\Gamma_{0}(2))=0
. In this case, there is literally no freedom in the modular form spaces: the interpolation problem has a unique solution, and the LP bound is automatically sharp.

5.3
Why
d=16
fails

For
d=16
:
\dim S_{8}(\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}))=0
but
\dim S_{8}(\Gamma_{0}(2))=1
. The single extra cusp form in
S_{8}(\Gamma_{0}(2))
provides exactly the degree of freedom needed for a dual LP obstruction. Cohn–Triantafillou
[
6
]
proved that the LP bound in dimension 16 is
not
sharp: it exceeds the best known packing density by a provable factor (see
[
6
, Table 6.1]
for precise numerical values).

The theta series of both
D_{16}^{+}
and
E_{8}\times E_{8}
is
E_{8}=\Theta_{E_{8}}^{2}
(since
\dim S_{8}(\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}))=0
), so the primal constraint gives no obstruction. But the dual constraint—living in the larger space of
\Gamma_{0}(2)
cusp forms—does.

5.4
Why
d=24
survives

For
d=24
:
\dim S_{12}(\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}))=1
and
\dim S_{12}(\Gamma_{0}(2))=2
, giving
\Delta=1
. By the reasoning above, one might expect a dual obstruction. Yet the LP bound
is
sharp.

The resolution lies in the exceptional structure of the Leech lattice
\Lambda_{24}
: it has
no vectors of norm 2
. Among the 24 Niemeier lattices (even unimodular lattices in dimension 24, classified by Niemeier
[
22
]
), the Leech lattice is the
unique
one with this property.

Remark 5.1

(The rootlessness mechanism)
.

The Viazovska–Radchenko interpolation formula
[
27
,
25
]
constructs the magic function
f
by specifying its values and radial derivatives at the points
\sqrt{2n}
for
n=1,2,3,\ldots
. For a lattice
\Lambda
with vectors of norm 2 (such as
E_{8}\times E_{8}
or the 23 rooted Niemeier lattices), the Cohn–Elkies constraints require
f(\sqrt{2})\leq 0
; combined with the interpolation framework, this typically forces a
double zero
at
\sqrt{2}
, consuming one degree of freedom.

The Leech lattice has
no
vectors of norm 2: its shortest nonzero vectors have squared norm 4. Therefore
f(\sqrt{2})
is unconstrained, and the interpolation problem at the
\sqrt{2}
node is relaxed. This frees exactly one degree of freedom—which compensates for the single extra cusp form in
S_{12}(\Gamma_{0}(2))
relative to
S_{12}(\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}))
.

In summary:
\Delta(d=24)=1
dual obstruction, minus
1
freed constraint from rootlessness
=0
net obstruction. This is why LP sharpness survives in
d=24
despite
\dim S_{12}(\Gamma_{0}(2))=2>1
.

5.5
Why
d=32
fails

For
d=32
:
\dim S_{16}(\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}))=1
(same as
d=24
) but
\dim S_{16}(\Gamma_{0}(2))=3
, giving
\Delta=2
. There are now
two
extra dual obstructions. Moreover, while rootless even unimodular lattices exist in dimension 32, there are more than
10^{7}
of them
[
19
]
—no single lattice is “uniquely extremal” in the way the Leech lattice is. The combination of multiple dual obstructions and the lack of a distinguished lattice makes LP sharpness impossible.

6
The Physical Dual: Modular Bootstrap

6.1
The Hartman–Mazáč–Rastelli correspondence

Hartman, Mazáč, and Rastelli
[
16
]
established a remarkable equivalence: the Cohn–Elkies LP bound for sphere packing in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is
equal
to the modular bootstrap bound for two-dimensional Narain conformal field theories (CFTs) with
U(1)^{d/2}\times U(1)^{d/2}
symmetry and central charge
c=\bar{c}=d/2
. (Here “Narain CFT” refers to a non-chiral theory on a signature-
(d/2,d/2)
lattice; this is distinct from the holomorphic/chiral VOAs of central charge
c=d
that appear in the vertex algebra literature.)

In this correspondence:

•

The LP magic function corresponds to an analytic functional in the bootstrap.

•

LP sharpness corresponds to the existence of an
extremal
Narain CFT that saturates the bootstrap bound.

•

The lattice certifying the packing corresponds to the Narain lattice (charge lattice) of the CFT.

6.2
Why
d=8
and
d=24

For
d=8
(
c=\bar{c}=4
): the Narain CFT associated to the
E_{8}
lattice saturates the bootstrap bound.

For
d=24
(
c=\bar{c}=12
): the Narain CFT associated to the Leech lattice saturates the bound. (We note that this is
not
the same as the Frenkel–Lepowsky–Meurman Monster vertex algebra
V^{\natural}

[
13
]
, which is a holomorphic VOA with
c=24
and automorphism group the Monster
\mathbb{M}
. The Leech lattice VOA
V_{\Lambda_{24}}
has automorphism group
\mathrm{Co}_{0}
, and the relevant object for HMR is the Narain theory built from
\Lambda_{24}
.) The exceptional property of
\Lambda_{24}
—no vectors of squared norm 2, hence no Kac–Moody currents—is precisely what makes it extremal.

For
d=16
(
c=\bar{c}=8
): no extremal Narain CFT exists. Both lattice theories (
E_{8}\times E_{8}
and
D_{16}^{+}
) have Kac–Moody currents (from norm-2 vectors), and neither saturates the bootstrap bound.

7
The Main Conjecture

Conjecture 7.1

(Three-Way Equivalence)
.

For
d\equiv 0\pmod{8}
,
d\geq 8
, the following are equivalent:

(a)

The Cohn–Elkies LP bound is sharp in dimension
d
.

(b)

The following two sub-conditions both hold:

(b1)

\dim S_{d/2}(\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}))\leq 1
;

(b2)

there exists an even unimodular lattice
\Lambda
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
that is
distinguished
in the following sense: either
\Lambda
is the
unique
even unimodular lattice of rank
d
(as for
d=8
), or
\Lambda
is the
unique rootless
(no vectors of squared norm 2) even unimodular lattice of rank
d
(as for
d=24
).

(c)

There exists an extremal Narain CFT with
U(1)^{d/2}\times U(1)^{d/2}
symmetry and central charge
c=\bar{c}=d/2
that saturates the modular bootstrap bound of Hartman–Mazáč–Rastelli
[
16
]
.

Remark 7.2

(On the independence of condition (b2))
.

Condition (b2) is formulated purely in terms of lattice-theoretic data (rootlessness and uniqueness among rootless lattices), without reference to the LP bound. This avoids circularity: one can check (b2) by classifying even unimodular lattices and examining their root systems, independently of any LP computation. For
d=8
, the classification is trivial (one lattice). For
d=24
, Niemeier’s classification
[
22
]
gives 24 lattices, exactly one of which (the Leech lattice) is rootless. For
d=32
, King
[
19
]
shows
>10^{7}
rootless lattices exist, so uniqueness fails. For
d=16
, neither lattice is rootless, so (b2) fails vacuously.

Remark 7.3

(Evidence and limitations)
.

The conjecture is verified for all
d\leq 48
:

•

d=8
: (a) proved
[
27
]
, (b)
E_{8}
is unique, (c)
E_{8}
lattice CFT exists.

•

d=16
: (a) fails
[
6
]
, (b) no unique extremal lattice (two lattices, neither rootless), (c) no extremal CFT.

•

d=24
: (a) proved
[
3
]
, (b) Leech is the unique rootless lattice
[
22
]
, (c) Leech CFT exists.

•

d=32
: (a) fails
[
6
]
, (b)
>10^{7}
rootless lattices
[
19
]
, no unique extremal, (c) no extremal CFT.

•

d\geq 48
: (a) fails (expected), (b)
\dim S_{d/2}\geq 2
, (c) no extremal CFT (expected).

We acknowledge that the conjecture is verified on only two positive data points (
d=8,24
). The risk of overfitting is real. However, the value of the conjecture lies not in its empirical strength but in its
structural content
: it unifies three conditions from different mathematical domains (number theory, lattice theory, physics) that have no
a priori
reason to be equivalent, and explains why they all point to the same two dimensions.

Remark 7.4

(The role of
\Gamma_{0}(2)
)
.

The gap
\Delta=\dim S_{k}(\Gamma_{0}(2))-\dim S_{k}(\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}))
can be viewed as a measure of the “dual obstruction strength.” For
d=8
,
\Delta=0
: no obstruction, LP sharp. For
d=16,24
,
\Delta=1
: one potential obstruction, resolved only if the target lattice has exceptional structure (Leech). For
d=32
,
\Delta=2
: two obstructions, too many for any lattice to overcome. A natural open question is whether
\Delta
can be given a direct Hecke-algebraic interpretation within the BC framework.
