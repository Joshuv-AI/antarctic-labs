Glycine-Copper Nanozyme with Dual Oxidase Activity: for Colorimetric Detection of Epinephrine and Oxidation of Phenolic Pollutants
Authors: Bitania Teklu Yilma, Menbere Leul Mekonnen, Kaayyoof Fikadu Gutema, Tesfaye Eshete Asrat, Jan Dellith, Marco Diegel, Andrea Csáki, Wolfgang Fritzsche
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Developing nanozymes with well-defined yet synthetically simple coordination environments remains essential from a rational design perspective. Here, we present a minimalistic glycine-copper (GCu) nanozyme, where the smallest amino acid acts as the only ligand to stabilize mixed-valence Cu­(I)/Cu­(II) centers. Structural analysis confirms the formation of a glycine-coordinated copper complex in a distinct mixed-valence state, supporting efficient phenol oxidase-like catalysis. The GCu nanozyme shows catechol oxidase activity toward epinephrine (EP), catalyzing its oxidation to adrenochrome, which strongly absorbs at 486 nm. Kinetic studies across different buffer systems reveal that GCu exhibits the highest catalytic turnover (Kcat = 0.353 min–1) in Tris buffer (pH 7.0). Compared with the phenylalanine-copper (PheCu) nanozyme, which contains bulkier ligand side chains, GCu exhibited 3-fold higher catalytic turnover. This indicates the effectiveness of smaller ligands in improving substrate accessibility. The color change of the oxidized product correlates directly with EP concentration, enabling colorimetric detection of EP at levels as low as 0.21 μg/mL. Analysis of EP-spiked urine samples showed recovery rates of 90–98%, confirming the potential of this colorimetric sensor for diagnostic applications. In addition to catechol oxidase activity, GCu also oxidizes various phenolic pollutants, indicating broader substrate versatility and dual oxidase activity. Overall, this work demonstrates glycine as a minimal coordination ligand for stabilizing a mixed-valence copper nanozyme, thereby providing a simpler structure for colorimetric sensing and the oxidation of phenolic pollutants.

Introduction

Epinephrine (EP) is a monoamine neurotransmitter (catecholamine) secreted by the adrenal gland. It acts as a chemical messenger in the blood, sending signals to help specific cells respond to stress or excitement. A change in EP concentration in serum or urine can serve as a biomarker for neurological and physiological problems. High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) is a reliable analytical method for determining EP. , Other methods, such as luminescence and radioenzymatic assays are also currently practiced. However, these methods often involve various reagents, lengthy procedures, complex instrumentation, and higher expertise. As a result, simpler, rapid, yet reliable analytical methods suitable for point-of-care (POC) applications are required to facilitate clinical diagnostics procedures. This has stimulated increasing interest in nanozyme-enabled colorimetric sensing.

Nanozymes are nanomaterials that exhibit intrinsic enzyme-mimetic properties, providing a more robust, stable, and inexpensive alternative to natural enzymes. − Among these phenol oxidase nanozymes are multicopper oxidase enzyme mimics with intrinsic catechol oxidase, laccase, and tyrosinase activity. Among these, catechol oxidase nanozymes can readily oxidize monoamine NTs (catecholamines) into chromogenic products with strong absorption in the visible region. This chromogenic catalytic reaction enables the colorimetric detection of catecholamines by leveraging the concentration-dependent color intensity of the oxidized product. While various phenol oxidase nanozymes, mainly with laccase activity, have been reported for colorimetric detection of NTs and catalytic oxidation of phenolic pollutants, the distinction between catechol oxidase and laccase activities is often unclear. − Although laccase (EC 1.10.3.2) can catalyze one-electron oxidation of ortho or para diphenols with diverse substrate ranges, catechol oxidase (EC 1.10.3.1) performs better, particularly on the ortho diphenol (catechol and its derivatives) substrates. Hence, mechanistically, the oxidation of monoamine NTs such as EP would be best described by catechol oxidase. ,

Recent studies have shown that nucleotides and peptides coordinated with copper have significant phenol oxidase activity. − However, such ligands are often complex and not cost-effective. Hence, exploring simplified and rational designs could improve practicality while maintaining catalytic properties. As a result, single amino acid-copper complexes, such as Cu-l-phenylalanine, are reported as simpler structures with oxidase activity. , Yet these single amino acids contain bulky side chains/ring structures that directly influence metal coordination and substrate access. This demands more effort to identify the simplest possible ligand.

Hence, in this study, glycine, the smallest amino acid, is reported as a minimalist coordination ligand for copper (Figure ). Our approach is based on the idea that glycine, the smallest and structurally simplest amino acid, with no side chain, can provide the most minimal possible coordination motif. Accordingly, the prepared Glycine-Copper (GCu) catechol oxidase mimic demonstrated efficient catalytic oxidation of EP at neutral pH, enabling sensitive and rapid colorimetric detection of EP in solution and in EP-spiked urine samples. In addition to its catechol oxidase activity, GCu demonstrated laccase activity for oxidizing various phenols, including phenol, 2,4-dichlorophenol, and hydroquinone. This versatility in phenol oxidase activity could enable GCu to find environmental applications in the degradation of phenolic pollutants.

Experimental Section

Chemicals and Reagents

All chemicals and reagents used in this research were of analytical grade. 2, 4-dichlorophenol (C6H4Cl2O, 99%),4-amino antipyrine (C11H13N3O, 99%), copper chloride (CuCl2.5H2O, 97%), Tris (hydroxymethyl)-aminomethane hydrochloride (Tris-HCl buffer, C4H11NO3, 99%), phosphate buffer, L-epinephrine (C9H13O3N,99%), phenol (C6H6O, 99%), 2-(N-morpholino) ethanesulfonic acid (MES,99%), 4-(2-hydroxyethyl)-1-piperazineethanesulfonic (HEPES,99%), hydrochloric acid (HCl, 37%), sodium hydroxide (NaOH, 99%), sodium chloride (NaCl, 99%), potassium chloride (KCl, 99%), calcium chloride (CaCl2.2H2O, 99%), and sodium acetate (C2H3NaO2, 99%) were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich.

Synthesis of Nanozyme

GCu was synthesized via a wet chemical method using a modified protocol. Under magnetic stirring, 0.05 mmol of copper chloride was added to 3 mL of hot water (70 °C). One mL of hot ethanol was then added to this solution. This was followed by the addition of 2 mL of a previously prepared hot glycine solution (1 mmol in distilled H2O, 70 °C) while stirring. Finally, the reaction was cooled slowly, then quenched in an ice bath. The precipitate was then washed with ethanol and water under vacuum filtration. Finally, the product was dried under vacuum at 40 °C for 24 h and sealed in a vial for further use.

Characterization of the Nanozyme

All parameter optimizations, kinetics, and absorption measurements were done using a UV–vis spectrophotometer (JASCO V770 and V670, Japan). A scanning electron microscope (JSM-6700F; JEOL Ltd., Japan) was used to study the morphology of the synthesized GCu nanozyme. Energy-dispersive X-ray elemental mappings (EDX) were performed using a silicon drift detector (SDD; XFlash 7100; Bruker Nano GmbH, Berlin, Germany). An X-ray diffraction spectrophotometer (XRD) (X’Pert PRO; Panalytical, Almelo, The Netherlands) was utilized to investigate the phase composition and crystal structure of the nanozyme. The chemical state and surface composition of GCu were analyzed using an X-ray photoelectron spectrometer (XPS) (AXIS Supra+ Kratos Analytical, UK).

Nanozyme Activity of GCu

The catechol oxidase activity was studied using epinephrine (EP) as a substrate. In a typical experiment, 200 μL of EP (200 μM) was first mixed with 1000 μL of Tris-HCl buffer in an Eppendorf tube, followed by the addition of 200 μL GCu (1 mg/mL). The reaction was stopped at the desired time, and the mixture was centrifuged at 10000 rpm for 10 min. The absorption of the supernatant was measured in a spectrometer. The steady-state kinetics was investigated by measuring the initial rate of the reaction at different EP concentrations. The kinetic parameters were then calculated by fitting the experimental values into the Michaelis–Menten enzyme kinetics model using OriginPro2025 software (eq ). The potential of GCu for catalytic oxidation of other substrates was also studied using various phenolic compounds, which are environmental pollutants. Briefly, 200 μL of a phenolic compound (6.14 mM) and a slight excess of 4-AP (7.00 mM) were mixed with 1000 μL of Tris buffer, followed by the addition of 200 μL of GCu (1 mg/mL). The absorbance of the resulting mixture at the maximum wavelength was recorded for 1 h using a UV–vis spectrometer. The percent oxidation was calculated by taking the ratio of the concentration of each compound to its initial concentration. Colorimetric Detection of EP

Different concentrations of EP standard solutions were subjected to catalytic oxidation under the previously investigated optimum conditions. 200 μL of EP was mixed with 1000 μL of Tris buffer (pH 7) and 200 μL of GCu (1 mg/mL). After 30 min, the solutions were centrifuged, and the supernatant spectra were recorded. Following baseline correction of the spectra, a calibration curve (absorbance at λ max vs C) was prepared. The analytical figures of merit (linearity and limit of detection) were determined from this calibration curve. The effect of different potential interferents on the colorimetric detection of EP was evaluated by conducting the reaction in their presence.

Detection of Epinephrine in Human Urine Matrix

Urine samples were collected from volunteers in a clinical laboratory, in accordance with ethical considerations (Ethical protocol number AASTU-1019/5585/24). The urine samples were first filtered using a syringe filter (0.22 μm). One mL of the filtered sample was then spiked with different EP concentrations diluted to 3 mL, and the mixture was centrifuged. The supernatant was subsequently analyzed using a protocol similar to that described in Section . Results and Discussion

Synthesis and Characterization of GCu

A blue GCu complex with an absorption maximum at 635 nm was obtained using a facile wet-chemical method. The resulting nanozyme exhibited a cuboid shape, with the expected elements of Cu, N, and O uniformly distributed (Figure a and Figure S1). The resulting nanozyme is also fairly dispersed in water and buffer media; however, further improvement is needed for industrial scale, especially to reduce the time required for full-scale dispersibility. Figure a shows the XRD pattern of the GCu, indicating mixed phases of copper oxides. The diffraction peak from α-glycine appeared at 30.87 °, 31.71 °, and 33.09 ° (ICSD-96–450–4970), while the peaks at 40.65 o and 44.83 ° represent γ-glycine (ICSD-96–450–4972). The peaks at 2θ of 36.14 ° and 42.77 ° belong to diffractions from the (111) and (002) phases of cubic cuprous oxide, respectively. The peak at 45.35° belongs to diffraction from cubic copper (ICSD-98–009–1827). While the peaks at 35.35°, 35.61°,38.28°, 39.72°, 46.42 and 49.06° represent diffractions from the (1̅ 12),(002), (111), and (200),(11 2̅) and (20 2̅) planes of cupric oxide, respectively. The crystallite size, calculated from the Williamson-Hall plot of the copper peaks, is 35 nm.

The FTIR spectrum of the GCu shows clear changes relative to the G (Figure S2), confirming the incorporation of copper coordination. The N–H stretching region around 3150 cm–1 in glycine is shifted and split in the GCu spectrum (3414 and 3250 cm–1). This indicates the deprotonation of the -NH3 + and subsequent coordination with copper through the nitrogen of the amino group. In addition, the band around 1600 cm–1, corresponding to carboxylate asymmetric stretching, is shifted and significantly attenuated, indicating that the carboxylate oxygen is bound to the metal. Furthermore, the disappearance of the small peak at 2100 cm–1 can be attributed to the disruption of the hydrogen-bonded zwitterionic structure upon coordination to the metal. The low-wavenumber region (500–600 cm–1) and below 500 cm–1 also exhibit a characteristic new peak, which can serve as additional evidence for the formation of Cu–O and Cu–N bonds. −

Furthermore, the wide-scan XPS spectrum (Figure b) confirms the presence of Cu, O, N, and C, consistent with the EDX results above. The high-resolution spectrum of Cu 2p (Figure c) revealed peaks at 935.18 and 955.07 eV, corresponding to the Cu 2p3/2 and Cu 2p1/2 electrons of Cu­(II). The peaks at the lower BEs of 932.99 and 953.08 eV correspond to the Cu 2p3/2 and Cu 2p1/2 electrons of Cu­(I), respectively. The satellite peak at 942.87 eV further confirms the Cu (II) state. Deconvolution of the Auger Cu LMM peak (Figure d) revealed peaks at 570.62 and 576.33 eV, which can be assigned to Cu­(II) and Cu­(I), respectively. The ratio of Cu­(II) to Cu­(I) was calculated to be 2.33. The presence of the Cu­(I) state could result from the disproportionation of Cu­(II), consistent with previous work on multicopper phenolic oxidase nanozymes. ,

The structural analyses confirm that glycine, despite being the simplest amino acid and lacking a coordinating side chain, can stabilize a mixed-valence Cu­(I)/Cu­(II) complex with a defined crystal structure. Hence, the successful creation of a stable mixed-valence copper center using glycine demonstrates that a minimal coordination motif is sufficient to support the redox environment required for oxidase-like activity without the need for imidazole or thiol groups of larger amino acids or dipeptides, which could create steric hindrance at the substrate–metal interface.

Catechol Oxidase Activity of GCu

Catechol oxidase enzymes are a class of phenol oxidase containing binuclear type-3 copper centers coordinated with histidine groups (Figure ). These enzymes catalyze the two-electron oxidation of ortho-diphenols (catechol). Hence, the catechol oxidase mimetic activity of GCu is investigated using EP as a model substrate. The various factors affecting this reaction (such as pH and time) are optimized using a one-factor-at-a-time approach. As seen in Figure a, GCu oxidizes EP into a reddish-brown product (adrenochrome) with a typical absorption maximum at 486 nm. The GCu-catalyzed oxidation of EP is favored at neutral pH (Figure b) and achieves a rapid turnover within 10 min (Figure S3). The activity of the nanozyme was determined by measuring the reaction at different GCu doses with a fixed substrate concentration (200 μM). Accordingly, the optimum unit activity (b) in μmol/min is obtained when 1 mg/mL GCu is used (Figure c), which is equivalent to a specific unit activity of 0.05 U/mg.

(a) UV–vis absorption spectra of EP oxidation using GCu as catechol oxidase mimic (the inset is the digital photo of oxidized EP); (b) effect of pH on EP oxidation by GCu; (c) activity of the nanozyme at different concentrations of GCu; (d) Michaelis–Menten curve for the EP oxidation at different buffers; (e) proposed mechanism of GCu-catalyzed EP oxidation.

With a proportional amount of Cu NP (synthesis protocol described in the ESI), the presence of glycine as a coordination environment improved the oxidase activity by 37.33% (Figure S4). This can be attributed to enhanced electron transfer facilitated by amino acid functional groups. This finding is consistent with our previous results using polymer-based oxidase mimics, in which polymer-Cu exhibited enhanced catalytic activity compared to pristine copper nanoparticles (Cu NPs). , This optimal improvement in activity is achieved at a 2:1 G-to-Cu ratio (Figure S5).

Although EP oxidation is favored under neutral conditions, the catalytic activity varies with buffer species (Figure d and Figure S6). Table shows the kinetic parameters for each buffer species, obtained from the double reciprocal plot of the Michaelis–Menten plots. Accordingly, EP exhibited enhanced oxidation turnover in the Tris buffer than in the HEPES, PBS, and MES buffers, at similar pH and concentrations. In contrast, MES significantly suppressed turnover, suggesting an unfavorable interaction with the Cu active sites. Since nanozymes do not have the real binding pocket that natural enzymes do, it is unreliable to compare them based on their binding affinity (k m). Hence, in this study, we used overall turnover differences across different buffer conditions. Accordingly, the maximum turnover (Kcat) in the Tris buffer is 2.8 and 1.8 times higher than HEPES and PBS, respectively. In calculating Kcat, the copper (Cu) concentration obtained from XPS peak-fitting results is used as an approximation for the active sites in the nanozyme (GCu). Here, it is noteworthy that for nanozymes with composite nanostructures, determining Kcat depends on how the active-site concentration is defined. For example, using the total nanozyme concentration versus only the metal atom content can lead to different Kcat values. Future research should focus on standardizing active-site concentrations to enable consistent Kcat estimation across different nanozyme structures. The observed difference in oxidase activity among different buffer species may be due to variations in structure and degree of ionization, which affect the ease of EP interaction with the nanozyme. For instance, HEPES shows a lower Km, indicating an enhanced “apparent affinity” of the GCu nanozyme for EP; however, the concurrent decrease in V max and turnover suggests that catalytic turnover is hindered, possibly due to weaker coordination of HEPES to Cu active sites, thereby partially blocking copper centers. In contrast, the opposite trend in Tris buffer shows its less inhibitory effect. Importantly, the observed trend highlights the role of buffer components in modulating the catalytic microenvironments (pH microenvironments) through their interactions with metal active sites. ,

Kinetic Parameters of EP Oxidation under Different Buffers

The catalytic performance of GCu was further compared with that of a phenylalanine–copper nanozyme (PheCu), a related single amino acid–based copper nanozyme with a bulkier aromatic side chain. PheCu showed a Km of 90.43 μM, V max of 1.53 × 10–3 mM/min, and Kcat of 0.11 min–1 for EP oxidation (Figure S7a). As shown in Figure S7b, the normalized kinetic parameter values suggested a higher catalytic turnover (Kcat) for Gcu. This higher performance is likely due to the smaller size of glycine, which reduces steric hindrance around the Cu active sites and facilitates substrate access. −

Using previous works , and the findings of the XPS study, we propose a mechanism for the catechol activity in EP oxidation (Figure e). Accordingly, the reaction involves a two-electron oxidation of EP into adrenochrome, reducing Cu­(II) to Cu­(I). Later, oxygen binds to Cu­(I), promoting its oxidation back to Cu­(II) and the formation of H2O. Here, it is essential to consider that the absence of bulky or electronically complex side chains reduces competing interactions and facilitates efficient Cu­(II)/Cu­(I) cycling during EP oxidation. This reinforces our hypothesis that the main catalytic behavior of type-3 copper centers can be achieved through minimal chemical design, supporting a new paradigm in rational nanozyme development.

Laccase Activity: Substrate Versatility of GCu

Laccase is a type of oxidase enzyme that catalyzes the one-electron oxidation of phenols and is used as an environmental catalyst for the remediation of phenolic compounds. Hence, the laccase activity of GCu toward the oxidation of phenolic pollutants is also investigated to demonstrate the substrate versatility of the developed nanozyme. As shown in Figure a, GCu can also oxidize monophenols, such as phenol and 2,4-diphenol, as well as para-diphenols, such as hydroquinone. This indicates that GCu exhibits dual oxidase activity and thus catalyzes a diverse range of phenolic substrates. However, unlike the EP, these phenolic substrates require a chromogenic agent (4-AP) for visualization (Figure b). As shown in Figure c, GCu can oxidize 5–12% of these pollutants in 1 h. Although further optimization of reaction conditions could improve oxidation efficiency, work on such environmental applications is beyond the scope of this study. As shown in Figure S8, GCu, as a phenol oxidase mimic, retained 68% of its original activity after the fourth catalytic cycle and 25% at 95 °C. Furthermore, the nanozyme retains nearly half of its original activity after storage at room temperature for over 7 weeks. This demonstrated robust oxidase activity for the remediation of environmental phenolic pollutants.

Colorimetric Detection of EP

EP, a monoamine neurotransmitter and hormone, can be used as a clinical biomarker in urine or serum for the diagnosis of various neurological and physiological problems. EP levels in biological fluids serve as diagnostic markers for evaluating the sympathetic nervous system and identifying catecholamine-secreting tumors. Additionally, tracking EP fluctuations is essential for managing conditions such as chronic stress, heart failure, and Parkinson’s disease. , Hence, developing a rapid diagnostic tool based on colorimetric readouts is relevant. In the presence of GCu, EP readily oxidizes to adrenochrome, exhibiting a characteristic absorption at 486 nm. Hence, the principle of colorimetric detection of EP is based on measuring the absorbance at this wavelength. Different concentrations of EP were catalyzed by GCu in Tris buffer (pH 7.0). As shown in Figure a, the absorption intensity at 486 nm increases with EP concentration from 5 to 500 μM. The absorption intensity obtained after background subtraction demonstrated linearity in the 50–500 μM range (R2=0.997). The limit of detection (LOD) calculated from the calibration curve (Figure b) is 3.3 × SD/m, which is 0.21 μg/mL (1.15 μM), where SD and m represent the standard deviation of the blank response and the slope of the calibration curve, respectively. In a standard clinical test of EP from urine collected over 24 h, a normal adult needs to have 20 μg/24 h or less. Hence, the LOD obtained can be sufficient to detect the EP labels in pathological states, based on a 24-h urine sample collection, indicating the analytical potential of the developed nanozyme-based colorimetric sensor as a rapid diagnostic tool. Furthermore, the sensor accuracy was evaluated by analyzing EP-spiked human urine samples collected over a 24-h period. As shown in Table , the percent recoveries calculated using eq range from 90.0% to 99.75%, which falls within the theoretical range (80–120%) for analytical method validations. This suggests the potential utility of the sensor for practical diagnostic applications in various neurological conditions. In addition, the effect of potential interferents on colorimetric detection was assessed by conducting a colorimetric assay in the presence of equal concentrations of EP and interferents (200 μM each).

Conclusions

In summary, a dual oxidase nanozyme based on a single amino acid was developed through rational biomimetic design of the oxidase metalloenzyme core, in which the natural dipeptide microenvironment was replaced with the simplest amino acid, glycine. This results in a minimalistic yet effective biomimetic structure with efficient oxidase activity. The synthesized GCu complex contains mixed-valence copper coordinated by glycine, providing the nanozyme with structural and functional features similar to the catalytic center of natural catechol oxidase. As a result, GCu efficiently catalyzes the oxidation of EP into a colored product, with the absorbance intensity linearly related to EP concentration. This enables rapid colorimetric detection within clinically relevant ranges. Analysis of epinephrine-spiked urine samples showed acceptable recovery values (90–98%), confirming the potential of this nanozyme-based colorimetric platform for diagnostic applications. Additionally, GCu demonstrated substrate versatility by oxidizing several phenolic pollutants, showcasing its dual oxidase functionality and extending its applicability beyond biosensing.
