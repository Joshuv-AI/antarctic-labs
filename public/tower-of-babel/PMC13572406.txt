Theoretical study of drug delivery behavior of graphitic carbon nitride nanosheets for mesalazine in the gas and solvent phases
Authors: Somayeh BeyranvandRaad, Abedien Zabardasti, Mohammad N AL-Baiati
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNanomaterials have attracted considerable attention as targeted drug delivery systems due to their ability to transport therapeutic agents directly to specific cells, thereby minimizing side effects and enhancing treatment efficacy. Inefficient delivery of mesalazine (5 - aminosalicylic acid; 5 - ASA) to target cells may result in reduced therapeutic outcomes, persistent chronic inflammation, damage to the intestinal mucosa, and an increased risk of systemic side effects. Consequently, the design of targeted drug delivery platforms, such as graphitic carbon nitride (g - C₃N₄) nanosheets, is crucial to improve therapeutic efficacy while reducing adverse effects. In this study, the interaction between the (5 - ASA) molecule and the pristine graphitic carbon nitride nanosheets was investigated in both gas and aqueous phases to evaluate the effect of the environment on the electronic properties of the adsorption complexes. All calculations were examined using density functional theory (DFT) at the B3LYP/6-311G**(d,p) level of theory. The adsorption of 5 - ASA on the g - C₃N₄ surface is thermodynamically favorable, with adsorption energies of approximately - 19.68 kcal·mol⁻1 in the gas phase and - 21.50 kcal·mol⁻1 in the aqueous phase for the most stable configuration, indicating consistent interactions in both environments. Analyses based on the noncovalent index (NCI) and the quantum theory of atoms in molecules (AIM), and the reduced density gradient (RDG) map indicated that van der Waals interactions primarily stabilize the 5 - ASA@g - C₃N₄ complex. The weak interactions between 5 - ASA and the g - C₃N₄ carrier are crucial for facilitating the release of the drug at the target site. Additionally, analyses of the molecular electrostatic potential (MEP), and natural bond orbitals (NBO) revealed charge transfer from the 5 - ASA molecule to the g - C₃N₄ nanosheet, providing valuable insights into the electronic communication between the drug and its carrier. Recovery times were calculated to evaluate both the biocompatibility of the system and the efficiency of drug desorption from the carrier. Spectroscopic analyses revealed a red shift of 168 nm in λmax in the gas phase, while a blue shift was observed in aqueous media. These findings demonstrate that g - C₃N₄ is a promising nanocarrier for the efficient delivery of 5 - ASA to biological systems, providing valuable insights for the design of advanced nanoscale drug delivery systems.Keywords: g - C₃N₄, Graphitic carbon nitride, 5 - aminosalicylic acid, Mesalazine, Drug delivery, Noncovalent interactionsSubject terms: Chemistry, Materials science

Introduction

According to the latest reports by the World Health Organization Federation, Ulcerative colitis (UC) is an intractable disease that is progressively emerging as a global menace1. Two of the most common types of inflammatory bowel illnesses (IBD) are Crohn’s disease (CD) and ulcerative colitis (UC)2. The most common treatments for ulcerative colitis and Crohn’s disease are corticosteroids, 5 - ASA, sulfasalazine, methotrexate, and thiopurine3. Mesalazine, reported as 5 - aminosalicylic acid, with its anti - inflammatory effect, is a drug and the oldest therapy of choice for therapies in some cases of IBD4. 5 - ASA has been a first - line therapy in the treatment of mild to moderately active UC in the past decades, which improves a patient’s condition, but when 5 - ASA is orally administered, a major quantity of the drug is absorbed by the upper gastrointestinal. Thus, only a small amount of the active compound reaches the inflamed areas of the target colon5. However, targeting 5 - ASA to the site of inflammation remains a challenge and a major therapeutic need in UC. To show therapeutic defects and the side effects of 5 - ASA, drug delivery systems can be used to control the toxic condensation and minimize the effective dosage. In recent years, researchers have concentrated on the applications of nanomaterials in drug delivery systems6. Many drug delivery carriers are used for drug transfer; a great revolution occurred with the introduction of nanoparticles, which made an important change for the purposeful delivery of anti - cancer medicines7. These small nanoparticles, which are small and have a large surface area, can quickly penetrate cell barriers and are thus accepted as carriers8. Different nanostructures, such as fullerenes, boron nitride fullerenes, and carbon nanotubes, have been experimentally or theoretically studied to investigate their drug - delivery ability, surface attributes, and non - toxic features. These nano - structures prepare purposeful drug delivery without affecting healthy cells. The small size of these nano - structures simplifies their composition with drug molecules9–14. In the expanding field of nanotechnology, carbon - based nanomaterials have attracted interest from researchers and are efficient treatment materials15. Because of their unique chemical and physical characteristics, they are useful as drug carriers16,17. Among all carbon nitride allotropes, graphitic carbon nitride, with its elevated thermal stability, chemical stability, and wide bandgap, has attracted the attention of researchers18.

The thin nanosheets of g - C₃N₄ exhibit improved water solubility, higher biocompatibility, and lower toxicity compared to their bulk counterparts19. Moreover, the high surface - to - volume ratio of g - C₃N₄ significantly enhances the load carrying capacity of g - C₃N₄ is high20. In addition, since g - C₃N₄ is primarily composed of carbon and nitrogen, it shows reduced toxicity toward biological systems and improved bioavailability. Therefore, g - C₃N₄ is considered a metal - free and biocompatible material suitable for various biomedical applications21. Owing to these unique properties, g - C₃N₄ can be utilized as an effective drug delivery system by incorporating drug molecules into the pores of its nanostructure22. Molecules with weak donor - acceptor interactions are suitable for improving drug delivery performance. Such weak interactions between the carrier and drug are crucial for efficient intracellular release23. If the drug binds too strongly to the carrier, release at the target site is impeded, diminishing therapeutic efficacy24. Moreover, g - C₃N₄ has also been synthesized experimentally, and the results have demonstrated that it possesses a stable structure, high specific surface area, and tunable surface properties, making it a promising candidate for biomedical applications, particularly in targeted drug delivery systems25. In addition to experimental developments, computational modeling has played a crucial role in elucidating the molecular mechanisms underlying drug - carrier interactions, providing insights into adsorption behavior, charge transfer, and stability. Density Functional Theory (DFT) and Molecular Dynamics (MD) simulations have been extensively employed to study the physicochemical properties of g - C₃N₄ in drug delivery. For example, DFT analyses of the cisplatin– g - C₃N₄ complex reported a moderate adsorption energy (∼ - 1.25 eV), indicating that drug attachment occurs mainly through weak non - covalent forces, allowing for reversible adsorption and controlled release26. Likewise, studies of carboplatin adsorption showed energies of - 1.39 eV in vacuum and - 0.52 eV in aqueous environments, demonstrating the influence of solvation effects on drug–carrier binding27. In other study, g - C₃N₄ was utilized as a carrier for loading the anticancer drug flutamide, and its efficacy as a drug delivery system was comprehensively evaluated. The nitrogen atoms within the g - C₃N₄ framework were found to establish weak van der Waals interactions with the oxygen and fluorine atoms of the flutamide molecules. These weak noncovalent interactions play a crucial role in facilitating the controlled and efficient release of the drug from the g - C₃N₄ surface at the targeted site28. Another study examined the influence of metal doping on the structural, electronic, and drug delivery properties of g - C₃N₄. The calculated adsorption energies revealed that Ca - and Mg - doped systems exhibit strong binding interactions, promoting enhanced drug retention, whereas Al - and Cu - doped systems display weaker adsorption characteristics, which are advantageous for targeted drug release19. Overall, these computational studies reveal that g - C₃N₄ interacts with drug molecules mainly via weak van der Waals and electrostatic forces, allowing for adjustable drug release profiles while preserving molecular stability. They also emphasize the importance of environmental conditions, surface functionalization, and adsorption energy in optimizing carrier efficiency. To our knowledge, no previous computational or experimental studies have explored the adsorption of 5 - aminosalicylic acid on graphitic carbon nitride. In the present theoretical work, the adsorption behavior of 5 - ASA on g - C3N4 was systematically investigated using density functional theory (DFT) to evaluate the potential of g - C3N4 as a nanocarrier in gaseous and aqueous environments. The obtained results provide valuable insights into the suitability of g - C₃N₄ as a biocompatible, metal - free, and efficient platform for controlled drug delivery of 5 - ASA.

Computational details

All Density Functional Theory (DFT) calculations were performed using the Gaussian 09 software package29,30. The pristine g - C₃N, 5 - ASA, and their complexes were fully optimized in the gas phase without applying any symmetry constraints. The Becke’s three parameter hybrid functional combined with the Lee–Yang–Parr correlation functional (most negative value of adsorption energy) was employed together with the 6-311**(d,p) basis set31–37. The B3LYP/6-311G** (d, p) level of theory has low computational cost and provides reliable results. Therefore, this method is a very good choice for accurately estimating molecular geometry and electronic properties. Previous reports in the literature have confirmed that this method provides accurate and reliable results38–40. To explore the possible adsorption configurations and looking for more suitable sites for interactions, several initial geometries were generated by placing the 5 - ASA molecule at different orientations and positions on the g - C₃N₄ surface. Each structure was fully optimized to identify the most energetically favorable configuration. Frequency calculations were performed at the same theoretical level to ensure that all optimized structures correspond to true minima on the potential energy surface, confirmed by the absence of imaginary frequencies. Grimme’s D3(BJ) empirical dispersion correction41 was incorporated to better describe long range van der Waals interactions, which play a crucial role in adsorption phenomena. Results and discussion

Molecular geometry optimization

The geometrical structures of pristine g - C₃N₄, the 5 - ASA molecule, and their adsorption complexes (5 - ASA@g - C₃N₄) were fully optimized at the B3LYP/6-311G**(d,p) level of theory (Fig. 1). Different molecular orientations of 5 - ASA along with various sites on the g - C₃N₄ surface were considered to find their stronger intermolecular interactions and more prefect adducts configurations. The optimized geometries revealed that the interaction between 5 - ASA and g - C₃N₄ primarily occurs through noncovalent forces, such as hydrogen bonding and van der Waals interactions, with intermolecular distances ranging from 1.7 to 3.5 Å, characteristic of chemisorption. Frequency calculations confirmed the absence of imaginary frequencies, indicating that all optimized structures correspond to true minima on the potential energy surface. Different proposals (denoted A - E) were considered for the interaction of 5 - ASA with g - C₃N₄. In the complex A with Eads= - 19.68 kcal.mol-1, the O and H atoms of 5 - ASA interact with the H and N atoms of g - C₃N₄ with interaction distances of 1.78 and 1.80 Å, respectively. In complex B (Eads= - 18.72 kcal.mol-1), 5 - ASA is oriented perpendicular to the surface of g - C3N4, forming interactions via H65…N22, H61…N29, O70…N16, and H62…N58 at 2.11, 2.39, 3.01 and 3.64 Å, respectively. In complex C (Eads= - 16.91 kcal.mol-1), interactions occur through H65…N25, O70…H33, H61…N24, at 1.81, 2.13, and 3.49, Å. Complex D (Eads= - 15.83 kcal.mol-1) involves H68…N24, H63…N25, and O71…H33 interactions at 2.25, 2.77, and 3.04 Å, while in complex E (Eads= - 8.62 kcal.mol-1), the drug interacts face - to - face with the g - C₃N₄ surface through H68…N20, O70…N51, O71 - H72…C10, and C59…N20 interactions at 2.54, 3.03, 3.28, and 3.80 Å, respectively. According to results among the examined configurations, complex A, in which the hydroxyl group of 5 - ASA form hydrogen bond interactions with H and N atoms on the g - C₃N₄, exhibited the highest structural stability with the most negative value of adsorption energy.

The optimized structure of pristine carrier g - C₃N₄ and 5 - ASA@g - C₃N₄ complexes.

In contrast, complex E, in which the drug molecule is positioned face - to - face on the g - C₃N₄ surface, exhibits the lowest structural stability and the least negative adsorption energy. The more negative adsorption energies indicate higher stability of the optimized complex and highlight the critical role of hydrogen bonding interactions in stabilizing the structures. Overall, lower adsorption energies correlate with easier release of the drug at the target site, which is a key feature for the design of efficient targeted drug delivery systems. Therefore, a more negative adsorption energy corresponds to a higher stability of the optimized complex and, consequently, a lower Eads value facilitates easier release of the drug at the target site. The calculated adsorption energies (Table 1) were all negative, confirming that the adsorption process is spontaneous and thermodynamically favorable. These results demonstrate that molecular orientation and adsorption site selection significantly influence the adsorption strength and electronic response, consistent with previous DFT studies on g - C₃N₄ based nanomaterials. The dipole moment of pristine g - C₃N₄ was initially calculated to be 2.34 Debye, indicating relatively low polarity. Upon formation of the 5 - ASA@g - C₃N₄ complex (complex A), this value increased significantly to 7.47 Debye in the gas phase, reflecting enhanced polarity. Higher dipole moments are associated with stronger interactions with polar solvents, such as water, thereby increasing the hydrophilicity of the complex. Notably, in an aqueous environment, the dipole moment of complex A further increased to 10.89 Debye, demonstrating a substantial enhancement in polarity. This increase in hydrophilicity improves solvation, facilitates efficient transport, and supports the controlled release of the drug within biological systems, which is essential for effective drug delivery63.

Furthermore, the increased dipole moment and enhanced hydrophilicity of the complex, particularly in an aqueous environment, improve solubility and enable controlled drug release at the target site, confirming the efficiency of the drug delivery system.

IR spectral analysis

The infrared (IR) spectra of pristine g - C₃N₄ and the 5 - ASA@g - C₃N₄ adsorption complexes (A–E) are presented in Fig. 2. The pristine g - C₃N₄ exhibited characteristic absorption bands in the region of 1200–1650 cm–1, which correspond to the stretching vibrations of C–N and C=N bonds within the heptazine units. The strong and sharp peak near 810 cm–1 is attributed to the breathing mode of the s - triazine rings, confirming the preserved graphitic framework of g - C₃N₄. After adsorption of 5 - ASA, noticeable spectral modifications were observed in the complexes (A–E). The intensity of the bands between 1200 and 1650 cm–1 slightly decreased, accompanied by minor redshifts, indicating charge redistribution and weak electronic coupling between 5 - ASA and the g - C₃N₄ surface. Moreover, new vibrational features appeared in the region of 3200–3500 cm–1, corresponding to the O–H and N–H stretching modes of 5 - ASA. These broadened and shifted peaks clearly confirm the formation of intermolecular hydrogen bonds between the hydroxyl or amino groups of the drug and nitrogen atoms of the nanosheet. Among all the complexes, complex A exhibited the most significant redshift in the O–H stretching region, consistent with its most negative value of adsorption energy and strongest hydrogen bonding interaction. Overall, the IR spectra indicate that the structural integrity of g - C₃N₄ is maintained after adsorption, while the observed vibrational shifts confirm the successful noncovalent attachment of 5 - ASA onto the nanosheet through hydrogen bonding and van der Waals interactions.

IR of g - C₃N₄ and complex (A - E).

The IR spectral analysis confirms that 5 - ASA is successfully adsorbed onto g - C₃N₄ via noncovalent interactions, primarily hydrogen bonding. The observed redshifts in the O–H and N–H stretching regions, along with the preservation of g - C₃N₄ characteristic bands, indicate both the stability of the complex and the maintained structural integrity of the nanosheet, supporting its potential for controlled drug release and targeted drug delivery.

HOMO–LUMO analysis

Analysis of frontier molecular orbitals (FMOs) such as HOMO and LUMO provides valuable information about the electronic characteristics, charge distribution, and reactivity of molecular systems. The energy difference between these two orbitals (energy gap or Eg) is a key parameter for determining the chemical reactivity, stability, UV - vis spectrum, optical polarizability, hardness, and softness of a molecule64. The highest - occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) acts as an electron donor or nucleophile, whereas the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) exhibits electrophilic character or electron - ccepting ability65. The HOMO and LUMO orbitals of 5 - ASA, g - C₃N₄, and the 5 - ASA@g - C₃N₄ complexes are shown in Figs. 3 and 4, providing important insights into the electronic distribution and chemical reactivity of these systems.

HOMO and LUMO 5 - ASA@g - C₃N₄ complexes (A - E) in the gas phase.

In all the complexes, the HOMO orbital was located on the 5 - ASA fragment, and the LUMO orbital was located on the g - C3N4 fragment with complete density separation, showing a charge distribution between the 5 - ASA and g - C₃N₄. The energies of the HOMO, LUMO, maximum absorption wavelength, and energy gap (Eg) in the gas phase are reported in Table 2. By comparing the complexes between the drug and carrier, the Eg value of 5 - ASA@g - C₃N₄ complexes (2.71 to 3.21 eV) was smaller than that of the 5 - ASA (4.25 eV) and g - C₃N₄ (3.99 eV). The HOMO on 5 - ASA behaves as a nucleophile or electron donor, whereas the LUMO on g - C₃N₄ acts as an electrophile or electron acceptor in complex structures. The charge transfer from the HOMO to the LUMO depends on the change in energy gap (Eg), which enables to computation of polarizability, reaction stability, and chemical reactivity of the compound. Complex A with a low Eg exhibits high polarizability, which reduces kinetic stability and increases chemical reactivity66. In 5 - ASA, LUMO is mainly distributed at the benzene ring, carboxylic acid group, and hydroxyl group of the molecules, while the electron density of HOMO is focused at the hydroxyl group and benzene ring of the molecules. The electron density of the LUMO and HOMO of g - C₃N₄ is monotonously distributed all over the structure of the molecule. In the complex, the HOMO and LUMO were on 5 - ASA and g - C₃N₄. These results show the charge transfer from the 5 - ASA molecule to g - C₃N₄. Frontier molecular orbitals (LUMO - HOMO) analysis focuses on the charge transfer from 5 - ASA to g - C₃N₄ upon excitation from the HOMO to the LUMO. The energy gap of 5 - ASA was 4.25 eV, and the energy values for the FMOs were - 1.07 eV (LUMO) and - 5.32 eV (HOMO). The Eg value for g - C₃N₄ was 3.99 eV with EHOMO and ELUMO are - 6.17 eV and - 2.18 eV, respectively. The 5 - ASA@g - C₃N₄ complexes have an energy gap between 2.71 eV and 3.21 eV, which is much smaller than that of 5 - ASA (4.25 eV) and g - C₃N₄ (3.99 eV). This alludes that the complexes require lesser energy for excitation. It is noteworthy that this pattern agrees well with the density of FMOs. The decrease in Eg indicates that electronic excitation requires less energy and facilitates charge transfer. The direction of charge transfer is consistent with the electron donating nature of the drug and the electron accepting nature of g - C₃N₄, enhancing the overall efficiency of the process. A smaller Eg is also associated with increased chemical reactivity and improved charge mobility, which are favorable for effective drug - carrier interactions. In summary, FMO analysis offers mechanistic insights into the electronic factors dictating drug - carrier interactions. Proper alignment and distribution of HOMO and LUMO orbitals enable efficient charge transfer and favorable thermodynamic stability, establishing g - C₃N₄ as a biocompatible, stable, and metal - free nanoplatform for targeted delivery of 5 - ASA, Aspirin, and other structurally analogous therapeutic agents. In the UV–vis region, g - C₃N₄ represented λmax at 388 nm, whereas 5 - ASA@g - C₃N₄ complexes showed the red - shifted excitation wavelengths by 168 nm from the g - C₃N₄ with the λmax at 556 nm (in complex A). This red - shifted excitation wavelength is because of the reduction in the energy band gap of 5 - ASA@g - C₃N₄ complexes. Thus, redshifts of 129, 84, 80, and 70 nm occurred for the B, C, D, and E complexes, respectively. As the gap value decreases, the red - shifted excitation wavelength increases for these complexes.

Electronic properties analysis

The electronic properties of the studied complexes (A, B, C, D, E), the drug, and the carrier (g - C₃N₄) were investigated using DFT calculations in the gas phase. Frontier molecular orbital energies (HOMO and LUMO), the band gap (Eg), chemical hardness (η), softness (σ), chemical potential (μ), electrophilicity index (ω), maximum charge transfer capacity (Nmax), and dipole moments are summarized in Table 2. The HOMO and LUMO energies reflect the electron donating and electron accepting abilities of the complexes. The energy gaps of the complexes range from 2.71 eV (complex A) to 3.21 eV (complex E), whereas the isolated 5 - ASA drug and g - C₃N₄ carrier exhibit larger gaps of 4.25 eV and 3.99 eV, respectively. The reduction in Eg upon complex formation indicates enhanced electronic reactivity and improved potential for interactions with biological targets. The chemical potential (μ) values of all complexes are negative, demonstrating thermodynamic stability. The μ values of the complexes (−3.62 to −3.79 eV) lie between those of g - C₃N₄ (- 4.17 eV) and 5 - ASA (- 3.19 eV), suggesting a spontaneous electron transfer pathway from the drug to the carrier. Chemical hardness (η) ranges from 1.35 to 1.60 eV, while softness (σ) varies between 0.62 and 0.74 eV-1. These moderate values indicate a balanced combination of stability and reactivity, favorable for drug delivery applications. Higher softness facilitates electron transfer and enhances interactions with target cells. The electrophilicity index (ω) of the complexes ranges from 4.49 to 4.86 eV. Higher ω values in combination with increased softness correspond to greater chemical reactivity. Nmax represents the maximum amount of electrical charge that an electrophilic system can accept and serves as a measure of a molecule’s charge - accepting capacity67. According to the Nmax relationship, an increase in chemical potential results in a decrease in chemical hardness and a corresponding increase in electrophilicity. Systems with higher electron deficiency carry a greater positive charge, which enhances their ability to accept electrons. The calculated Nmax values for the studied complexes indicate that A complex exhibits the highest charge - accepting capacity (2.67 eV), demonstrating its superior potential for electron transfer compared to the other complexes. Dipole moments vary from 3.01 Debye (complex E) to 7.46 Debye (complex A). Higher dipole moments indicate increased polarity, improved solubility, and stronger electrostatic interactions, which are critical for effective drug delivery. AIM analysis

The properties of bond critical points (BCPs) in the investigated complexes were analyzed using the Atoms in Molecules (AIM) theory. The parameters Laplacian of the electron density at BCP, (ρ), is the electronic density, and HBCP is the electron density at BCP, which is the sum of the kinetic electron energy density (GBCP) and the potential electron density (VBCP), derived from the Bader theory to demonstrate the type of interaction. According to AIM theory, chemical bonds are characterized and classified based on these parameters at the bond critical points. Shared covalent bonds typically have higher ρBCP values (∼10–1 a.u.), whereas weak intermolecular interactions, such as hydrogen bonds or van der Waals interactions, have lower values of ρBCP (∼10–2- 10–3 a.u.). The value of Laplacian of the electron density (ρ) is important for determining whether depletion or electronic accumulation occurs in the internuclear region: If ρBCP < 0, it indicates a type of covalent interaction that concentrates the electron charge concentration in the region between nuclei, and this interaction is related to polar and covalent bonds. Whereas, if ρBCP > 0, it indicates that there is a characteristic electron charge between atoms, which means that there is an interaction between closed - shell systems of H bonds or van der Waals interactions. MEP and NBO analysis

To investigate the nature of electronic interactions and the charge transfer mechanism between the 5 - ASA drug and the g - C₃N₄ substrate, molecular electrostatic potential (MEP) and natural bond orbital (NBO) analyses were performed. The MEP maps of the g - C3N4 and 5 - ASA@g - C₃N₄ complexes are shown in Fig. 9. The MEP maps for the g - C₃N₄ surface and the 5 - ASA@g - C₃N₄ complex (Fig. 9) clearly illustrate the redistribution of charge density upon complex formation. In these maps, regions with negative electrostatic potential (shown in red) indicate electron - rich sites that are favorable for electrophilic attacks, whereas blue regions correspond to electron deficient sites susceptible to nucleophilic reactions. Green regions represent areas with neutral potential. The MEP analysis revealed that the oxygen atoms in the hydroxyl and carboxyl groups of 5 - ASA exhibit the highest negative potential, indicating a strong tendency to interact with electron deficient sites on the g - C₃N₄ surface. In contrast, the nitrogen and carbon atoms within the g - C₃N₄ framework possess positive potential and act as electron accepting centers. Based on these observations, it can be concluded that the charge transfer predominantly occurs from the 5 - ASA molecule to the g - C₃N₄ surface, playing a crucial role in stabilizing and enhancing the interaction between the two components. This approach provides quantitative insight into donor - acceptor interactions through the examination of electron delocalization from filled (donor) to empty (acceptor) orbitals. MEP and NBO analyses were carried out to investigate the nature of electronic interactions and charge transfer between 5 - ASA and the g - C₃N₄ nanosheet. The MEP maps indicate that the electron - rich sites of 5 - ASA preferentially interact with electron - deficient regions on g - C₃N₄, suggesting that charge transfer primarily occurs from 5 - ASA to the nanosheet. NBO analysis confirms that this electron delocalization stabilizes the complexes through significant donor–acceptor interactions from the lone pairs on 5 - ASA to the antibonding orbitals of g - C₃N₄. Mulliken population analysis further corroborates the direction of electron flow. Overall, these results demonstrate that electronic delocalization and charge transfer are key factors in stabilizing the complexes and play a critical role in enhancing the efficiency and controllability of 5 - ASA delivery via g - C₃N₄.

Effect of solvent

To examine the influence of the solvent on the physical and chemical properties of the 5 - ASA@g - C₃N₄ system, the interaction between 5 - ASA and the g - C₃N₄ nanosheet was investigated in the aqueous phase. Water was chosen as the solvent due to its biological relevance. The IEFPCM implicit solvation model was employed to simulate the aqueous environment and to evaluate its effects on the adsorption, solvation, and electronic properties of the complexes. According to Tables 1 and 5, the adsorption energies (Eads) of the 5 - ASA@g - C₃N₄ complexes in the aqueous phase range from −7.66 to −21.50 kcal·mol–1, showing a slight increase compared to the corresponding values in the gas phase (−8.57 to −19.68 kcal·mol–1). This indicates that the presence of the solvent does not significantly reduce the adsorption strength and may be attributed to the stabilizing effect of the solvent, which modulates the interaction between the drug molecule and the nanosheet. The negative Eads values confirm that the adsorption process is spontaneous and exothermic. Moreover, the relatively small difference between the gas and aqueous phases suggests that the complexes retain good thermodynamic stability in the aqueous environment. To evaluate the solvation effects on the 5 - ASA@g - C₃N₄ system, the solvation energies were calculated using Eq. 16, and the corresponding results are presented in Table 5. The calculated solvation energies for 5 - ASA (- 10.60 kcal·mol–1), g - C₃N₄ (- 34.97 kcal·mol–1), and the 5 - ASA@g - C₃N₄ complexes (configurations A–E: - 43.86, - 40.57, - 35.96, - 41.41, and - 39.40 kcal·mol–1) are all negative, indicating spontaneous solvation in water. Furthermore, the more negative solvation energies of the complexes compared to those of the isolated components suggest enhanced solubility and improved thermodynamic stability of the assembled systems. The EHOMO, ELUMO, λmax, band gap energies (Eg), chemical hardness (η), softness (σ), dipole moments, electrophilicity indices (ω), and chemical potentials (μ) of the 5 - ASA molecule, g - C₃N₄ nanosheet, and 5 - ASA@g - C₃N₄ complexes are summarized in Table 6. Analysis of the frontier molecular orbitals revealed a slight increase in the band gap energies (Eg) of the complexes in water (2.87–2.99 eV) compared to those in the gas phase (2.71–3.21 eV), indicating a small decrease in electronic reactivity and an improvement in chemical stability under aqueous conditions. This behavior can be attributed to the dielectric stabilization of the frontier orbitals by the polar solvent.

Overall, these results demonstrate that the 5 - ASA@g - C₃N₄ system is suitable and stable for effective drug delivery under physiological aqueous conditions.

Drug release

Finally, the process of 5 - ASA release from the g - C₃N₄ was studied in the target site. We know that the pH of target cells is lower than that of normal cells, drug - release to the expected target site after protonation was investigated78. Therefore, the impact of pH was explored on the most stable Structure between 5 - ASA and the g - C₃N₄ (complex A). In addition, there is a tendency for linkage between the nucleophilic head, oxygen atoms, in 5 - ASA and H+ species. Because of the ability of oxygen atoms to be protonated in an acidic environment, their binding to g - C₃N₄ in the target site becomes weaker. Therefore, the 5 - ASA drug, which has O atoms in its structure, has an increased ability for g - C₃N₄ to action as a drug – carrier. The interaction bond length between 5 - ASA and the g - C₃N₄ increase to 3.12 Å in the acidic environment compared to with the 5 - ASA@g - C₃N₄ complex in the normal environment. Moreover, there is a decrease in absorption energy (Eads+ EBSS) from - 16.24 to - 7.14 kcal.mol-1 in the acidic environment. As a result, the interaction between drug and the carrier weakens, leading to the separation of 5 - ASA from the g - C₃N₄ also its release into the target cell. The release mechanism of 5 - ASA from g - C₃N₄ at the target site was investigated by considering the acidic environment of target cells. Protonation of the oxygen atoms in 5 - ASA under low pH conditions weakens the interaction with g - C₃N₄, as reflected by the increased bond length (3.12 Å) and the decreased adsorption energy (from −16.24 to −7.14 kcal·mol–1). This weakening of the drug–carrier interaction facilitates the detachment and release of 5 - ASA at the target site, demonstrating that g - C₃N₄ can effectively act as a pH - responsive carrier for controlled drug delivery.

Conclusions

In this study, the adsorption of the 5 - ASA drug on g - C3N4 was systematically investigated using DFT calculations in both aqueous and gaseous phases. The adsorption occurs primarily through noncovalent interactions, forming stable and spontaneously generated 5 - ASA@g - C3N4 complexes. Analysis of electronic properties revealed significant decreases in the HOMO - LUMO gap (Eg), with reductions up to ∼ 32% in the gas phase and ∼ 29% in the aqueous phase, indicating enhanced electronic reactivity. Among the studied complexes, complex A showed the highest dipole moment (μ = 10.89 Debye in water, 7.46 Debye in gas), lowest global hardness (η), and highest softness (σ), confirming its superior stability, reactivity, and suitability for controlled drug release. TD - DFT calculations indicated redshifts in λmax, reflecting changes in electronic transitions upon adsorption. Adsorption energies (Eads) and recovery times (τ) further highlight the performance of the complexes. In aqueous media, complexes with moderate adsorption energies (∼ - 21.50 to - 7.66 kcal.mol-1) achieve the best balance between stability and controlled drug release, while higher Eads values result in more stable but slower - release complexes, and lower Eads values favor rapid release but lower stability. Comparisons between aqueous and gas phases show that solvent effects enhance polarity, stability, and controlled release, making the aqueous environment particularly favorable for drug delivery. Overall, the results indicate that g - C₃N₄ is an effective nanocarrier for 5 - ASA, and complex A emerges as the optimal choice, providing high stability, polarity, and electronic reactivity in both phases. These findings provide valuable insights for the rational design of nanocarrier - based drug delivery systems with controlled release properties and targeted therapeutic applications. The calculated results showed that g - C₃N₄ was a significant carrier for the delivery of 5 - ASA to treat Crohn’s disease (CD) and ulcerative colitis (UC).
