The Mechanism and Application of Traditional Chinese Medicine Nano-Formulations in the Treatment of Renal Fibrosis
Authors: Lu Zhang, Tianlong Liu, Xiaolin Li, Sirui Chen, Shengguang Wang, Huiping Ma, Minna Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChronic kidney disease (CKD) is a global public health threat, and renal fibrosis represents the common pathological pathway toward end-stage disease. Current clinical treatments are limited by single-target mechanisms and their inability to reverse established fibrosis. Traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) offers multi-target advantages but encounters challenges including low bioavailability and lack of targeting. Nanodelivery systems overcome these limitations by enabling precise renal targeting and enhanced efficacy. This review provides a comprehensive and systematic overview of the mechanisms and applications of TCM nanoformulations for anti-renal fibrosis, with an emphasis on bridging nanocarrier material properties to clinical translation potential. To achieve this, we summarize the pathogenesis of renal fibrosis, elucidate the principles of renal-targeted nano-delivery (glomerular filtration, tubular reabsorption, and secretion), categorize five classes of nanocarriers with their physicochemical properties and in vivo fate, review the anti-fibrotic mechanisms and nanodelivery strategies of four major TCM active components (flavonoids, anthraquinones, phenolic compounds, and glycosides), and present preclinical evidence in CKD and acute kidney injury (AKI) models. In conclusion, TCM nanoformulations represent a promising strategy for renal fibrosis treatment by integrating multi-component synergy with precise renal targeting. However, significant hurdles remain, including batch-to-batch consistency, long-term safety evaluation, and regulatory pathway establishment. Addressing these challenges is essential for successful clinical translation.Keywords: chronic kidney disease, renal fibrosis, traditional Chinese medicine, nano-preparations, renal targeting

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) has emerged as a significant challenge in global public health sector. CKD affects 800 million people worldwide, and causes millions of deaths every year, ranking as the 16th leading cause of death worldwide.1,2 In Southeast Asia, the leading causes of advanced CKD are diabetic kidney disease (29.2%), glomerulonephritis (20.0%), and hypertension (16.8%), with polycystic kidney disease accounting for 0.7% of cases.3

Renal fibrosis represents the core pathological process and serves as the common final pathway for the progression of various chronic kidney diseases toward end-stage renal disease (ESRD).4 Research indicates that the onset of fibrosis is closely associated with the aberrant and excessive deposition of the extracellular matrix (ECM).5,6 Within this process, activated myofibroblasts, acting as the primary source of ECM, play a pivotal role in the complex regulatory network composed of renal tubular epithelial injury, immune cell infiltration, and various cytokines.7–9

The etiology of CKD is complex and multifactorial. Owing to socioeconomic development and an aging population,10,11 the prevalence of metabolic diseases, cardiovascular diseases, and other CKD-related conditions has been increasing rapidly.12–14 Current therapeutic strategies for CKD primarily rely on pharmacological interventions such as immunosuppressants, glucocorticoids, sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT-2) inhibitors, and angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEI)/angiotensin receptor blockers (ARB).15,16 In recent years, mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (MRAs), including finerenone, have emerged as promising therapeutic agents, demonstrating renal and cardiovascular protective effects in patients with diabetic kidney disease.17 Additionally, glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) agonists, such as semaglutide and liraglutide, have shown renoprotective effects beyond their glucose-lowering actions, including reduced albuminuria and slowed eGFR decline.18 However, despite these advances, current treatments—both conventional and emerging—are generally limited by their single-target mechanisms, the development of resistance during long-term use, and their inability to reverse established fibrosis. Furthermore, they lack specific anti-fibrotic effects. In the face of these therapeutic bottlenecks, Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) has emerged as a potentially vital therapeutic strategy, owing to its unique advantages rooted in holistic concepts, multi-target regulation, and extensive clinical practice.11,19,20

According to TCM theory, the pathogenesis of CKD is characterized by “deficiency in origin and excess in superficiality” with the theories of “renal collateral stasis” and “micro-accumulation” (micro-Zhengjia) provide core guidance. In TCM, Qi (vital energy) is the fundamental substance that maintains physiological functions and defends against pathogenic factors, and “healthy Qi” (Zheng Qi) specifically refers to the body’s inherent resistance and self-repair capacity. Modern research confirms that “renal collaterals” correspond to the renal microvascular network, where collateral stasis-induced microcirculatory disturbances and hypoxia initiate fibrosis, and “micro-accumulation” biologically manifests as excessive ECM deposition and renal remodeling.21 Based on this, TCM has established therapeutic strategies such as “strengthening the healthy Qi (ie enhancing the body’s resistance) and unblocking collaterals” and “dissipating accumulations and resolving masses”, aiming to restore renal homeostasis through multi-pathway interventions.22 TCM active ingredients exhibit multi-target anti-fibrotic effects. However, their clinical translation is restricted by low bioavailability, lack of targeting, and poor stability.23,24 Nanodelivery systems overcome these limitations by enabling precise renal targeting and controlled release through regulation of nanocarrier physicochemical properties, making TCM nanoformulations a promising research hotspot.25–28

Despite these advances, no comprehensive review has systematically summarized the mechanisms and applications of TCM nanoformulations specifically for anti-renal fibrosis, particularly from the perspective of bridging nanocarrier material properties to clinical translation.29–33 This review provides a comprehensive and systematic overview of TCM nanoformulations for anti-renal fibrosis, with an emphasis on linking nanocarrier characteristics to pharmacokinetic behavior and clinical translation potential. Specifically, we focus on the following five aspects: (i) summarize the pathogenesis of renal fibrosis; (ii) elucidate the principles of renal-targeted nano-delivery; (iii) categorize and characterize five classes of nanocarriers; (iv) review the anti-fibrotic mechanisms and nanodelivery strategies of four major TCM active components; and (v) discuss current challenges and future perspectives for clinical translation. The novelty of this review is reflected in its integration of material properties with in vivo fate, provision of quantitative in vitro-in vivo correlation data, and practical roadmap for clinical translation of TCM nano-formulations in renal fibrosis treatment.

The Pathogenesis of Renal Fibrosis

Renal fibrosis is the common pathological pathway for all CKD progressing to ESRD. Its pathological feature is the excessive deposition of ECM, which leads to the destruction of normal kidney structure and loss of function (Figure 1). Under normal physiological conditions, the glomeruli are responsible for filtration function, while the renal tubules are responsible for reabsorption and secretion functions. However, driven by various pathogenic factors over time, these structures ultimately converge on renal fibrosis as the common terminal pathological manifestation.9,34 This process not only involves changes in the quality and quantity of ECM, but also accompanies the systematic remodeling of the fine structure of the kidneys.6

Pathological Changes in the Renal Tubular System

Under chronic stress, renal tubular epithelial cells exhibit significant dysfunction. Injured epithelial cells trigger the epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) program, directly contributing to the source of myofibroblasts. Severe ongoing injury leads to cell death and tubular atrophy, which is the key anatomical basis for irreversible renal function loss.9,34

Structural Damage to Podocytes and the Mesangial Area

The disruption of the glomerular filtration barrier is the initiating event of the fibrotic process. Podocytes are terminally differentiated cells that, under pathological stressors such as high glucose, elevated hydraulic pressure, or immune-mediated injury, undergo a cascade of structural and functional alterations. These include actin cytoskeletal reorganization, foot process effacement, dedifferentiation, and eventual detachment from the glomerular basement membrane (GBM), events that collectively lead to the development of proteinuria. Beyond compromising the glomerular filtration barrier, podocyte injury also triggers the activation of mesangial cells through paracrine signaling.35 For instance, injured podocytes secrete factors such as platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF), which stimulate quiescent mesangial cells to transition into a proliferative phenotype. The activated mesangial cells subsequently produce large amounts of extracellular matrix components, including fibronectin, resulting in mesangial expansion and compression of the surrounding capillaries. As the disease progresses, the exposed GBM is prone to adhesion with the epithelial cells of the Bowman’s capsule wall layer, forming segmental sclerosis lesions. This “podocytemesangial cell” pathological interaction disrupts the integrity of the glomerulus, leading to sclerosis and functional impairment of the glomerulus, laying the pathological foundation for subsequent fullrange fibrosis of the entire kidney.36

Activation of Pericytes and Fibroblasts

Renal microcirculation disorders are closely related to the origin of myofibroblasts. Accumulating evidence has identified pericytes—the contractile cells surrounding capillary endothelium—as the major source of myofibroblasts in renal fibrosis.37 Under continuous damage, pericytes undergo phenotypic transformation, downregulating PDGFR-β and upregulating Alpha-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA), detaching from the vascular wall and migrating to the interstitial area to directly participate in matrix synthesis.38 The detachment of pericytes leads to instability and degeneration of the capillary wall, triggering microvascular rarefaction. This process causes chronic ischemia and hypoxia in the renal tissue, continuously activating the HIF-1α signaling pathway, which in turn further promotes the transcription of profibrotic genes, forming a “damage-hypoxia-fibrosis” vicious cycle.39 In addition, some vascular endothelial cells can also replenish the fibroblast pool through the endothelial-mesenchymal transition (EndMT) process, jointly accelerating the damage of the vascular bed and the remodeling of the interstitial structure.40,41

Activation of the Immune Microenvironment

The persistence and imbalance of immune inflammatory responses are the driving forces behind the progression of fibrosis.42 Damaged kidney tissues release chemokines to recruit a large number of immune cells such as macrophages and T lymphocytes for infiltration. Among them, the shift in the polarization state of macrophages is particularly crucial: during the chronic injury period, macrophages undergo polarization from the proinflammatory M1 type to the profibrotic M2 type, secreting cytokines such as TGF-β1 and Galectin-3, which continuously stimulate the activation and proliferation of resident fibroblasts.43,44 At the same time, the activation of the NLRP3 inflammatory body mediates the release of IL-1β and other mediators between immune cells and innate cells, triggering pyroptosis and amplifying the inflammatory response. This unresolved chronic inflammation constructs the fibrotic microenvironment, not only hindering tissue repair, but also continuously maintaining the fibrotic process.45,46

Pathological Manifestations

The aforementioned multicellular reactions ultimately converge into a severe imbalance in the metabolism of the extracellular matrix (ECM). Activated myofibroblasts extensively synthesize type I, type III collagen and fibronectin, resulting in excessive accumulation of matrix within the interstitium.47 At the same time, the local matrix degrading enzyme system in the kidney is dysfunctional.48 Although the expression of some matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) changes, the excessive upregulation of their endogenous inhibitors (TIMPs) leads to a relatively insufficient degradation capacity, causing irreversible deposition of ECM.49 This pathological matrix remodeling not only physically disrupts the normal architecture of renal tubules and blood vessels, but also hinders the diffusion of oxygen and nutrients, leading to extensive tubular atrophy and interstitial fibrosis.50 As functional renal units continue to be lost, the kidneys gradually harden and eventually result in the occurrence of ESRD (Table 1).51,52

The Principle of Renal-Targeted Nano-Delivery

The nephron is the basic structural and functional unit of the kidney, consisting of the glomerulus, proximal tubule, loop of Henle, distal tubule, collecting duct, and peritubular capillaries. The kidney maintains homeostasis through three steps: filtration, reabsorption, and secretion.59 Accordingly, the targeting efficiency of nanoparticles in the kidney depends on their glomerular filtration, tubular reabsorption, and tubular secretion.60,61

Glomerular Filtration of Nanoparticles

The glomerular filtration barrier (GFB) is a tri-layer structure: the endothelial cell layer (with 60–80 nm fenestrations and a glycocalyx), the glomerular basement membrane (GBM, ~10 nm pore size, negatively charged), and the podocyte layer (~12 nm slit diaphragm).62–65

Size-Dependent Filtration

Under physiological conditions, the GFB forms a highly selective filtration system.66 Nanoparticles smaller than approximately 6–8 nm readily pass through the GFB, while larger nanoparticles are effectively blocked.67 At the subnanometer scale, interactions with the glycocalyx may lead to abnormal clearance behaviors (Figure 2A).68

Schematic of the glomerular filtration barrier (GFB) in normal and injured kidneys. (A) Normal kidney. The GFB comprises three layers: fenestrated endothelial cells (60–80 nm fenestrae), the glomerular basement membrane (GBM, ~10 nm pores), and podocyte slit diaphragms (~12 nm). Adjacent structures include mesangial cells, Bowman’s space, and peritubular capillaries lined by endothelial cells (~5 nm fenestrae). These components collectively establish size- and charge-selective filtration. Solid arrows denote the direction of glomerular filtration. (B) Injured kidney. Under pathological conditions (eg, CKD, diabetic nephropathy), the GFB exhibits mesangial expansion and ECM deposition, glycocalyx disruption with loss of negative charges, podocyte detachment and widened slit gaps, endothelial fenestrae enlargement, GBM thickening with reduced crosslinking, tubular atrophy with blocked reabsorption (red cross), and albumin leakage (dashed arrow).

In renal fibrosis, the GFB undergoes significant changes: endothelial glycocalyx degradation, GBM thickening with collagen deposition, and podocyte damage.69,70 These changes disrupt barrier integrity, trigger proteinuria, and accelerate renal function decline.71 Consequently, the distribution, retention, and clearance of nanoparticles in fibrotic kidneys are significantly altered (Figure 2B).72,73

Charge-Dependent Filtration

Under physiological conditions, the negatively charged GBM confers significant charge selectivity. Cationic nanoparticles exhibit higher filtration efficiency than neutral or anionic counterparts,74 though they also show greater glomerular retention due to charge-induced biomolecular adsorption or cellular uptake.75–77 The fluid dynamic diameter, configurational flexibility, and charge density jointly determine filtration outcomes.78,79 Clarifying charge-dependent transport mechanisms remains a core challenge in designing precision drug delivery systems.80,81

In renal fibrosis, charge selectivity is significantly degraded. Podocyte damage reduces heparan sulfate proteoglycans and sialic acid proteins, weakening the charge barrier.82 Pathological collagen deposition lacks sufficient negative charges to block anionic proteins, resulting in proteinuria.83 In addition, anionic nanoparticles exhibit abnormally elevated filtration, and damaged endothelium enhances non-specific nanoparticle capture.84 Consequently, the glomerulus degenerates from a “charge-size dual filter” to a barrier that relies mainly on size obstruction.85

Tubular Reabsorption of Nanoparticles

Nanoparticles that pass through the glomerulus can be reabsorbed by proximal tubular epithelial cells via endocytic pathways, including receptor-mediated, caveolin-mediated, and micropinocytosis.86,87 The brush border membrane and its endocytic receptors provide the anatomical basis for efficient uptake.88

The reabsorption efficiency is governed by nanoparticle physicochemical properties: smaller nanoparticles are more easily filtered and reabsorbed; positively charged nanoparticles exhibit higher cellular uptake due to electrostatic attraction.65,89–91This reabsorption is a “double-edged sword”: moderate endocytosis enables targeted drug delivery, but excessive accumulation may cause lysosomal dysfunction, ROS production, and inflammation, leading to tubular damage.10,92,93 In fibrotic kidneys, EMT, extracellular matrix deposition, and chronic inflammation impair tubular polarity and endocytic function. Injury markers such as KIM-1 alter nanoparticle recognition and uptake, reshaping their accumulation and metabolism in fibrotic kidneys.94–96

Tubular Secretion of Nanoparticles

Renal tubular secretion actively transports substances from blood to urine via specific transporters. On the basolateral membrane, organic anion transporters (OAT1/3) and organic cation transporters (OCTs) uptake nanoparticles or their components from plasma.97–99 On the apical membrane, P-glycoprotein (P-gp) and multidrug resistance-associated proteins (MRPs) efflux them into the lumen.100,101 This energy-dependent process relies on ATP hydrolysis and ionic gradients.80,102 In renal fibrosis, chronic inflammation and loss of cell polarity downregulate or mislocalize these transporters, reducing secretion efficiency.103 This delayed clearance may prolong nanoparticle retention in renal tissue, altering pharmacokinetics and toxicity profiles.104–106

Types and Characteristics of Nanocarriers

The drug loading capacity, systemic circulation time, targeting ability, and biosafety of nanomedicine materials are fundamentally determined by their chemical composition, size, surface properties, and structural design. Applying the criterion of mutual exclusivity between chemical composition and biological origin, we systematically categorize current nanocarriers into five classes: lipid-based, polymeric, inorganic, biological, and hybrid and biomimetic nanocarriers (Figure 3). Each class exhibits unique features and translational benefits, as elaborated in the following sections.

Classification and characteristics of nanocarriers for drug delivery. Nanocarriers are divided into five categories based on their material properties and biological functions. Lipid-based nanocarriers (liposomes, SLN/NLC, and LNPs) offer good biocompatibility, high drug loading for hydrophobic drugs, and strong clinical translation potential. Polymeric nanocarriers (polymeric nanoparticles, protein nanoparticles, and virus-like particles) enable controlled and sustained release with versatile surface modifications. Inorganic nanocarriers (gold, iron oxide, mesoporous silica, quantum dots, upconversion, and bismuth-based nanoparticles) possess unique physicochemical properties, strong imaging capabilities, and high stability. Biological nanocarriers (exosomes, cell membrane vesicles, and virus-like particles) are naturally derived with low immunogenicity, excellent biocompatibility, and intrinsic targeting abilities. Hybrid and biomimetic nanocarriers (cell membrane-coated and biomimetic nanoparticles) combine the advantages of multiple systems to achieve immune evasion, prolonged circulation, and synergistic therapeutic effects. Collectively, these platforms improve drug solubility, stability, targeting, controlled release, and overall therapeutic efficacy.

Lipid-Based Nanocarriers

Physicochemical Properties and Structural Design

Lipid-based nanocarriers are self-assembled from amphiphilic lipid molecules into closed vesicles or particles, encompassing liposomes, solid lipid nanoparticles (SLNs), nanostructured lipid carriers (NLCs), and lipid nanoparticles (LNPs). Typical diameters range from 20 nm to 250 nm: liposomes generally fall between 50 nm and 200 nm; SLNs and NLCs are commonly 50–250 nm; LNPs used for nucleic acid delivery are typically 60–120 nm.107 In liposomes, drugs are entrapped in the aqueous core or partitioned into the lipid bilayer. SLNs utilize a solid lipid matrix, while NLCs blend solid and liquid lipids (typically 70:30) to form an imperfect crystal lattice that reduces drug leakage. LNPs, composed of ionizable cationic lipids, helper phospholipids, cholesterol, and PEG-lipids, are particularly optimized for nucleic acid delivery. Lipid nanoparticles and liposomes represent highly adaptable nanocarrier systems that have gained significant traction in oncology for both therapeutic and diagnostic purposes.108

Drug Loading and Encapsulation Efficiency

Drug loading in lipid-based nanocarriers is facilitated by the flexible bilayer and tunable hydrophobic domain. NLCs are specifically designed to enhance medication stability, loading capacity, and controlled release through their mixed lipid matrix architecture. NLCs blend solid and liquid lipids (typically 70:30), leading to the formation of an imperfect matrix that is more efficient in drug loading and reduces premature drug leakage from the matrix.109

In vivo Fate: Circulation and Targeting

Prolonged circulation is achieved mainly by PEGylation, which shields against protein adsorption and mononuclear phagocyte system (MPS) recognition. However, the “PEG dilemma”—the inherent conflict between long circulation and efficient cellular uptake—remains a challenge. The accelerated blood clearance (ABC) phenomenon presents a significant hurdle for PEGylated nanocarriers, causing rapid clearance of repeated doses and undermining therapeutic efficacy.110 In response, biomimetic approaches and stimuli-responsive PEG shedding strategies (pH-sensitive, enzyme-responsive, light-triggered) are being explored to dynamically resolve this dilemma by balancing stealth properties with effective cellular interactions.111 Targeting ability can be imparted via size/charge modulation and conjugation of ligands (eg, TPP, TAT, NLS, KDEL), even enabling organelle-specific delivery to mitochondria, lysosomes, nucleus, endoplasmic reticulum, and Golgi apparatus, enabled by coupling chemistries like bio-orthogonal click reactions and EDC/NHS activation.112

Safety and Clinical Translation

Biosafety of lipid-based nanocarriers is generally excellent due to their biomembrane-mimetic structures, although certain cationic lipids warrant long-term toxicity evaluation. Lipid-based nanocarriers are the most clinically advanced class, with successful examples including Onpattro (patisiran) for hereditary transthyretin-mediated amyloidosis and the COVID-19 mRNA vaccines. Their structural flexibility has enabled applications across a broad spectrum of cancers, including colorectal, liver, breast, ovarian, pancreatic, brain tumors, sarcoma, neuroblastoma, and leukemia. Recent reviews have comprehensively evaluated various lipid-based platforms across diverse cancer types, comparing their structural characteristics, drug loading efficiencies, release profiles, and targeting capabilities.108

Polymeric Nanocarriers

Physicochemical Properties and Structural Design

Polymeric nanocarriers are built from natural or synthetic macromolecules, offering precise control over drug release kinetics. Representative systems include biodegradable polyesters (PLGA, PLA, PCL), natural polymers (chitosan, gelatin), amphiphilic block copolymer micelles, and dendrimers.113 Their size typically ranges from 10 nm to 400 nm: PLGA nanoparticles are often 50–300 nm; polymeric micelles are 10–200 nm; dendrimers are 2–15 nm; chitosan nanoparticles commonly fall between 100 nm and 400 nm.114 PLGA consists of a family of FDA-approved biodegradable polymers that are robust and biocompatible, having been extensively researched as delivery systems for drugs, proteins, and macromolecules, including DNA and RNA. PLGA allows degradation rate tuning by adjusting the lactide-to-glycolide ratio, facilitating the engineering of block copolymer architectures (eg, POEGMA and PDXO based blocks) and hybrid platforms (including natural polymer hybrids) that offer unique targeting and stability advantages.115,116 Chitosan nanoparticles have emerged as versatile delivery systems capable of enhancing anticancer drug activity and reducing negative impacts on normal cells, with applications spanning targeted drug release, sustained retention, biocompatibility, and biodegradability. Dendrimers, as highly branched monodisperse macromolecules, offer precise control over surface functionality and cargo loading.

Drug Loading and Encapsulation Efficiency

Drug loading capacity in polymeric nanocarriers is generally higher than that of liposomes. Hydrophobic drugs are encapsulated in the polymer matrix, while hydrophilic ones can be loaded into dendrimer interiors or nanocapsule cavities. PLGA-based nanocarriers offer several therapeutic advantages, including improved solubility and targeted delivery of anticancer agents. Co-delivery nanoformulations based on PLGA have been designed to address key challenges including chemoresistance and off-target toxicity. PEG–PLGA nanoparticles have been shown to substantially improve the selectivity, bioavailability, and cytotoxic efficacies of anticancer drugs in breast cancer in vitro models. A diverse range of pH-responsive nanosystems—including polymeric nanoparticles, micelles, liposomes, dendrimers, SLNs, and NLCs—have been developed for precision oncology applications, highlighting their mechanisms, materials, and therapeutic advantages.117

In vivo Fate: Circulation and Targeting

Prolonged circulation in polymeric nanocarriers relies extensively on PEGylation. The PEGylation of PLGA nanoparticles results in the formation of a polyethylene glycol corona on their surface, which enhances systemic circulation by reducing opsonization and immune system recognition. However, the steric hindrance of PEG can reduce targeting efficiency; consequently, pH-sheddable PEG or alternative hydrophilic coatings are being explored to resolve the “PEG dilemma”. Beyond PEGylation, advanced nanoparticle surface engineering strategies include control of PEG chain length and surface density, reduction of non-specific interactions to improve active targeting efficiency, stimuli-responsive PEG shedding strategies (pH-sensitive, enzyme-responsive, light-triggered) that dynamically balance stealth properties with effective cellular interactions, and alternative hydrophilic polymer coatings (eg, polyglycerol PG, polysarcosine PSar, zwitterionic polymers) that overcome PEG immunogenicity. Active targeting is achieved by surface conjugation of ligands, though a delicate balance between “stealth” and “recognition” is necessary.118

Safety and Clinical Translation

In terms of safety, acid degradation products from PLGA may cause local inflammation, and batch-to-batch variability remains an issue for natural polymers such as chitosan. Nevertheless, polymeric nanocarriers, including dendrimers and micelles, have been systematically evaluated for targeted therapy, with comparative analyses of their advantages and disadvantages relative to liposomes, mesoporous silica nanoparticles, gold nanoparticles, quantum dots, and MOFs. It has been outlined how PLGA-based nanosystems can evolve into robust, precision drug delivery systems with a clear path towards clinical adoption.

Inorganic Nanocarriers

Physicochemical Properties and Structural Design

Inorganic nanocarriers rely on metals, metal oxides, silica, or carbon-based materials, leveraging unique optical, magnetic, and electrical properties for theranostic applications. Major examples include gold nanoparticles (Au NPs), superparamagnetic iron oxide nanoparticles (SPIONs), mesoporous silica nanoparticles (MSNs), quantum dots (QDs), carbon nanotubes (CNTs), and graphene derivatives. Size varies widely: spherical gold nanoparticles are typically 5–100 nm (commonly 10–30 nm for biomedical use); gold nanorods have lengths of 30–100 nm with aspect ratio-dependent properties; SPIONs are generally 10–100 nm (optimally 20–50 nm for MRI); MSNs range from 50–300 nm (pore size 2–10 nm); quantum dots are 2–10 nm; carbon nanotubes have diameters of 1–20 nm and lengths up to several micrometers.117 MSNs yield high drug loading due to ordered mesopores, while CNTs enable excellent cell penetration for drug and gene delivery. Inorganic multifunctional nanoparticles have been extensively explored for cancer theranostic applications, offering simultaneous diagnosis and therapy with spatiotemporal drug release and reduced systemic toxicity.

Drug Loading and Encapsulation Efficiency

Drug loading mechanisms vary significantly across inorganic platforms. Gold nanoparticles utilize surface adsorption or Au-S covalent bonds for drug conjugation. MSNs exhibit ultra-high loading via ordered mesopores and can be surface-modified with gatekeepers for controlled release. Graphene derivatives load aromatic drugs through π-π stacking interactions. The high surface area and versatile surface chemistry of inorganic nanomaterials enable efficient loading of chemotherapeutic agents, photosensitizers, and imaging contrast agents.

In vivo Fate: Circulation and Targeting

Circulation time of inorganic nanocarriers is determined mainly by size, shape, and surface hydrophilicity, as most inorganic materials are non-degradable. Long circulation still depends on PEGylation or hydrophilic coatings, and stability in circulation remains a challenge due to immune clearance.117 The standout feature of inorganic nanocarriers is their outstanding imaging capability. Gold-based nanomaterials provide LSPR-based computed tomography (CT) and photoacoustic imaging (PAI), demonstrating superior contrast to traditional iodine agents. SPIONs serve as MRI T2 contrast agents, combining biocompatibility with magnetic targeting capabilities. QDs enable size-tunable fluorescence imaging for diagnostics and tracing. These properties enable multimodal imaging and image-guided therapy. Targeting can be enhanced by ligand conjugation or external fields (magnetic, light). To address challenges in clinical translation, strategies such as enhancing targeting through ligand modification, achieving low-temperature therapy by inhibiting heat shock proteins, and combining chemotherapy/immunotherapy have been proposed. Particular emphasis has been placed on the innovative design of bimetallic nanomaterials that improve photothermal performance through high-entropy effects and synergistic catalytic actions.117

Safety and Clinical Translation

Biosafety is a major translational bottleneck for inorganic nanocarriers. Non-degradable particles may cause chronic toxicity upon long-term retention in the body. CNTs raise concerns about fiber-induced carcinogenesis. Heavy metals (eg, CdSe) in QDs pose release risks. Reproductive toxicity has been reported for various types of nanoparticles, including carbon nanomaterials, dendrimers, QDs, silica, gold, and magnetic nanoparticles. Despite these concerns, inorganic nanocarriers continue to be developed for advanced diagnostic and therapeutic applications, offering mechanical and conductive properties beneficial in both diagnostic and therapeutic approaches. Comprehensive safety profiling is essential before clinical translation. Recent progress in the utilization of IMNPs for imaging and therapy of multidrug resistant cancers has highlighted their ability to facilitate real-time molecular tracking and enhance therapeutic potential.

Biological Nanocarriers

Physicochemical Properties and Structural Design

Biological nanocarriers are directly derived from living organisms or self-assembled from biomacromolecules, including extracellular vesicles (exosomes, microvesicles), virus-like particles (VLPs), protein nanocages (ferritin, encapsulin, viral capsids), and nucleic acid nanostructures (DNA origami, DNA tetrahedra, RNA nanoparticles). Their sizes are highly distinctive: exosomes range from 30–150 nm (peak around 70–100 nm); microvesicles are 100–1000 nm; VLPs typically fall between 20–200 nm; ferritin nanocages are uniform at approximately 12 nm outer diameter with a 7–8 nm inner cavity; DNA origami structures range from 20–200 nm depending on design (eg, 2D triangle ~120 nm side length); DNA tetrahedra are 5–20 nm edge length.119,120 Exosomes, originating from the endosomal pathway, carry parent-cell-derived proteins, lipids, metabolites, RNAs, and DNAs. From a pharmaceutics and drug-delivery standpoint, EVs have attracted strong interest as biologically derived nanocarriers that may address persistent limitations of conventional synthetic delivery systems—such as suboptimal biocompatibility, limited barrier penetration, and inefficient delivery to hard-to-reach tissues.119

Drug Loading and Encapsulation Efficiency

Drug loading in biological nanocarriers can be achieved by endogenous methods (engineering parent cells to produce cargo-loaded vesicles) or exogenous approaches (sonication, electroporation, extrusion). However, loading efficiency is generally lower than that of synthetic carriers, and batch consistency remains poor.120 Protein-based nanocarriers, including albumin, lipoproteins, ferritin, viral protein capsids, and silk proteins, have gained considerable attention due to their inherent biocompatibility, biodegradability, and facile functionalization. Engineering strategies for improving payload control, targeting precision, and dosing accuracy include endogenous enrichment, exogenous loading, and surface functionalization.119

In vivo Fate: Circulation and Targeting

The greatest advantage of biological nanocarriers lies in their innate biocompatibility and targeting capabilities. Exosomes retain surface integrins and adhesion molecules, enabling active homing to specific tissues and even crossing of the blood-brain barrier (BBB), placental barrier, and other physiological barriers that are difficult for synthetic carriers to penetrate.119 Virus-like particles enhance antigen presentation and activate both humoral and cellular immune responses. Ferritin nanocages are highly appealing as carriers for delivering drug molecules and antigen proteins due to their distinctive structural and biochemical properties, including multivalent antigen display that aids immune activation. DNA nanostructures (eg, DNA origami, tetrahedral frameworks) have emerged as programmable and biocompatible platforms offering precise control over size and shape, enabling organ-specific drug delivery with emphasis on biodistribution profiles and in vivo targeting strategies. This review comprehensively summarizes the recent advancements in DNA nanostructure-based delivery systems for RNA drugs, with a specific focus on cancer applications, and examines strategies for efficiently loading RNA payloads and achieving controlled, stimuli-responsive release (eg, pH-responsive).121

Safety and Clinical Translation

Exosomes hold substantial promise for precision oncology through enhanced drug delivery and diagnostic applications. However, clinical translation requires standardized manufacturing, comprehensive safety profiling, and scalable production methods. The field faces serious challenges including low yield, purity variation, GMP scale-up difficulties, incomplete understanding of pharmacokinetics (lack of quantitative PK/PD relationships and potency assays), and regulatory classification ambiguity. Delineated translational priorities required to advance exosome-based products toward clinical development include standardized dose metrics (particle- and cargo-normalized), quantitative PK/biodistribution–PD relationships, potency assays and critical quality attributes (CQAs), manufacturing consistency under GMP, and regulatory-compliant characterization.121 Major gaps identified include lack of standardized manufacturing protocols, incomplete pharmacokinetic understanding, and unresolved safety concerns.122,123

Hybrid and Biomimetic Nanocarriers

Physicochemical Properties and Structural Design

Hybrid and biomimetic nanocarriers integrate two or more material systems or incorporate biomimetic elements to synergistically combine advantages. Representative platforms include lipid–polymer hybrid nanoparticles (LPHNPs), cell-membrane-coated nanoparticles (CM-NPs), metal–organic frameworks (MOFs), and virus-mimicking nanoparticles. Size is highly dependent on the core and coating: LPHNPs typically range from 50–300 nm (core ~50–200 nm, lipid shell ~5–20 nm thickness); cell-membrane-coated nanoparticles generally fall between 50–250 nm, depending on the synthetic core (eg, PLGA core 100 nm plus membrane coating adds ~10 nm); MOFs range from 20–500 nm, with common nanoscale MOFs (eg, ZIF-8) at 50–200 nm.109,124 LPHNPs integrate the mechanical strength and sustained-release capacity of polymeric cores (eg, PLGA) with the biocompatibility and high drug-loading efficiency of lipid shells. Various design strategies and architectures that enhance encapsulation efficiency, stability, and targeted delivery of diverse therapeutic agents have been reviewed.125

Drug Loading and Encapsulation Efficiency

MOFs offer exceptionally high drug loading capacities and controllable release behaviors that outperform many conventional nanocarriers. Metal–organic frameworks (MOFs) and their nanoscale counterparts (NMOFs) represent a versatile class of porous materials characterized by the modular assembly of metal nodes and organic linkers. MOFs combine modular coordination chemistry with tunable porosity, and this comprehensive review critically examines and synthesizes recent, groundbreaking progress in three pivotal biomedical domains: intelligent drug delivery systems, multimodal medical imaging, and high-performance electrochemical biosensors. Stimuli-responsive MOFs have been developed for natural medicine delivery and synergistic cancer therapy, emphasizing controlled release, combination strategies, and biosafety.

In vivo Fate: Circulation and Targeting

Cell-membrane coating is a landmark biomimetic strategy. Membranes from red blood cells, platelets, macrophages, or cancer cells are coated onto synthetic cores via sonication or extrusion, endowing the nanoparticles with the intact antigen and receptor repertoire of the source cells. This confers immune evasion, homologous adhesion (homotypic targeting), and prolonged circulation. Coating NPs with natural cell membranes addresses various challenges in drug delivery, such as prolonging circulation time, reducing immunogenicity, and improving targeting efficiency and cellular communication. The versatile characteristics of LNPs synergize well with cell membranes’ biomimetic properties, creating hybrid structures with enhanced functionalities for diverse biomedical applications.126 CM-NPs have emerged as versatile and multifunctional platforms with wide applicability in medicine, particularly in cancer therapy, phototherapy, and immunotherapy. Biomimetic nanoparticle formulations integrating different types of coatings, cores and cargoes for precise delivery of anti-fibrotic and anti-cancer therapies have been developed for the treatment of liver fibrosis and hepatocellular carcinoma. Hybrid cell membrane-coated nanosystems have been developed for treating cardiovascular diseases and neurodegenerative disorders. Cell membrane-coated nanoparticle-based drug delivery systems have been comprehensively reviewed for the management of a range of neurodegenerative disorders such as Alzheimer’s disease, with the cell-membrane coating conferring upon the inner nanomaterials the properties of the cells from which the membrane is derived.

Safety and Clinical Translation

While biosafety is improved because the coating is derived from natural cell membranes, manufacturing complexity, quality control, and characterization standardization remain major hurdles for clinical translation. Recent reviews have critically examined innovations in biomimetic nanocarriers, particularly those leveraging cell membranes, membrane proteins, or native cellular structures. These platforms have therapeutic potential, but challenges of manufacturing, scalability, quality control, in vivo stability, and regulatory barriers persist. MOF-based platforms remain in early clinical stages. Biomimetic approaches, including cell membrane coating, cellular hitchhiking, exosomes, pathogen mimicking, and natural polymers, have been presented as potential countermeasures to the ABC phenomenon associated with PEGylated nanocarriers. However, the manufacturing complexity, large-scale production, reproducibility, safety, and regulatory standardization must be addressed through quality by design approaches and advanced manufacturing technologies to fully realize the clinical and commercial potential of next-generation LPHNPs.109 Despite these challenges, hybrid and biomimetic nanocarriers represent a frontier in nanomedicine, with emerging directions including stimuli-responsive nanocarriers, scalable green production, and precise surface engineering for targeted distribution.

Absorption and Biodistribution

After administration via different routes (oral, intravenous, or intraperitoneal), TCM nanoformulations are absorbed and distributed to tissues including the kidneys. Nanoparticles with sizes between 10–200 nm and neutral or slightly negative surface charges tend to exhibit prolonged circulation time and enhanced renal accumulation. Surface modification with PEG (PEGylation) can reduce opsonization and subsequent clearance by the mononuclear phagocyte system (MPS), thereby extending blood circulation half-life. For renal targeting, nanoparticles smaller than 10 nm undergo rapid renal filtration and excretion, while those larger than 200 nm are primarily taken up by the liver and spleen. Therefore, optimal renal targeting is often achieved with nanoparticles in the size range of 50–150 nm.

Metabolism of Nanocarriers and Loaded Cargo

The metabolic fate of TCM nanoformulations involves two components: the nanocarrier itself and the encapsulated active ingredients. Lipid-based and polymeric nanocarriers (eg, liposomes, PLGA nanoparticles) are biodegradable and undergo hydrolysis or enzymatic degradation in vivo, releasing the encapsulated TCM components gradually. The released active ingredients are then metabolized via classic Phase I (oxidation, reduction, hydrolysis) and Phase II (conjugation) reactions primarily in the liver. Inorganic nanocarriers (eg, gold nanoparticles, mesoporous silica) are generally non-biodegradable and are eliminated through renal or hepatobiliary routes depending on their size.

Elimination Pathways

The elimination of TCM nanoformulations occurs through two primary pathways: renal clearance and hepatobiliary excretion. Nanoparticles smaller than 10 nm are efficiently filtered by the glomerulus and excreted in urine. Larger nanoparticles are typically taken up by Kupffer cells in the liver and cleared via the bile. Surface modifications such as PEGylation can delay elimination and prolong circulation time. Understanding these elimination pathways is essential for predicting drug accumulation, potential toxicity, and dosing intervals.

Factors Affecting Pharmacokinetics of TCM Nanoformulations

Several factors influence the in vivo behavior of TCM nanoformulations: (1) Particle size: smaller particles (<50 nm) show better tissue penetration but faster clearance; (2) Surface charge: neutral or slightly negative charges reduce non-specific protein adsorption and MPS uptake; (3) Surface modifications: PEGylation and ligand conjugation can enhance targeting and prolong circulation; (4) Carrier material: biodegradable polymers (PLGA, chitosan) exhibit different degradation kinetics compared to inorganic carriers; (5) Route of administration: intravenous administration achieves highest bioavailability, while oral administration faces gastrointestinal barriers.

SalB-PCS-NPs: PEGylated Chitosan Nanoparticles for Oral Delivery of Salvianolic Acid B

Salvianolic acid B (SalB) is a naturally occurring renoprotective compound with potent anti-fibrotic activity, yet its clinical application is severely limited by poor oral bioavailability.148 To overcome this limitation, researchers developed SalB-loaded PEGylated chitosan nanoparticles (SalB-PCS-NPs) for oral administration in the treatment of renal fibrosis.

In vitro studies: Using Caco-2 cell monolayer models, SalB-PCS-NPs demonstrated significantly enhanced gastrointestinal stability and mucus-penetrating ability compared to conventional chitosan nanoparticles (SalB-CS-NPs). The PEGylated formulation exhibited superior transport across the intestinal epithelial barrier. Importantly, the structural integrity of SalB-PCS-NPs after traversing the intestinal barrier was confirmed using FRET (Förster resonance energy transfer) technology, demonstrating that the nanoparticles remained intact upon reaching the systemic circulation. Pharmacokinetic studies revealed that SalB-PCS-NPs achieved a relative oral bioavailability of 367.0% compared to free SalB, and 206.2% compared to SalB-CS-NPs.148

In vivo studies: In a unilateral ureteral obstruction (UUO) mouse model of renal fibrosis, oral administration of SalB-PCS-NPs once daily for 14 days resulted in significantly improved renal function and markedly reduced extracellular matrix (ECM) accumulation. FITC-labeled SalB-PCS-NPs exhibited 4.6-fold higher renal accumulation compared to free FITC, and 2.1-fold higher than FITC-labeled SalB-CS-NPs.148 The in vivo structural integrity was further validated by FRET imaging, confirming that the nanoparticles maintained their integrity after intestinal absorption. Histological analysis demonstrated that SalB-PCS-NPs significantly attenuated pathological changes associated with renal fibrosis, including reduced collagen deposition and tubular injury.

HCS-LGP NPs: Chitosan Nanoparticles for Linagliptin Delivery

Linagliptin, a dipeptidyl peptidase-4 (DPP-4) inhibitor, has shown potential renoprotective effects beyond its glucose-lowering actions. To enhance its renal targeting and therapeutic efficacy, researchers developed linagliptin-loaded chitosan nanoparticles (HCS-LGP NPs) with acid-responsive drug release properties.87

In vitro studies: Using TGF-β1-induced HK-2 cells (human renal proximal tubular epithelial cells), HCS-LGP NPs demonstrated efficient cellular uptake. The formulation significantly inhibited cell proliferation and downregulated the expression of fibrotic markers, including TGF-β1 and Collagen I.87 The acid-responsive release profile ensured that the drug was preferentially released in the acidic microenvironment of injured renal tissues.

In vivo studies: In a rat model of renal fibrosis, oral administration of HCS-LGP NPs resulted in effective renal accumulation. Treated animals showed significantly reduced serum creatinine (SCr) and blood urea nitrogen (BUN) levels, indicating improved renal function.87 Fibrotic marker expression, including TGF-β1 and Collagen I, was markedly downregulated. Histological examination confirmed significant recovery from renal injury and reduced collagen fiber deposition. Importantly, histopathological analysis of major organs (heart, liver, spleen, and lungs) revealed no detectable toxicity, confirming the excellent biocompatibility of the formulation.

Nanodelivery Systems of Traditional Chinese Medicine for Anti-Renal Fibrosis

Anti-Renal Fibrosis Active Components of Traditional Chinese Medicine and Their Nanodelivery Strategies

Traditional Chinese medicine has a history of over 2,000 years. In both clinical and preclinical studies, traditional Chinese herbs and natural product components have demonstrated significant efficacy in alleviating renal fibrosis through various molecular mechanisms acting on key pathways involved in the fibrotic process.

In clinical practice, traditional Chinese medicines are most commonly used as standardized formulations, including proprietary Chinese medicines, water decoctions, granules, capsules, and injections. In patients with chronic kidney disease (CKD), several TCM formulations have been evaluated in randomized controlled trials and prospective cohort studies. Representative examples include Niaoduqing granules, Shenkang injection, Kunxian capsules (containing Tripterygium wilfordii), and Astragalus-based compound formulas.149–153 These formulations have demonstrated clinical benefits such as reducing proteinuria, slowing the decline of estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), and alleviating renal fibrosis. Preclinically, the anti-fibrotic effects of these TCM formulations are attributed to their multiple bioactive constituents. The active components of traditional Chinese medicine for anti-renal fibrosis mainly include flavonoids, anthraquinones, phenolic compounds, and glycosides (Figure 4). Flavonoids

Flavonoids are abundant in several TCM herbs commonly used in CKD management, such as Astragalus membranaceus (Huangqi), Pueraria lobata (Gegen), and Glycyrrhiza uralensis (Gancao).154,155 Clinically, Astragalus-containing formulations have been tested in CKD patients and shown to reduce serum creatinine levels and delay renal fibrosis progression. Representative flavonoid compounds, such as Quercetin and Puerarin, have demonstrated significant renal protective effects in various kidney injury models.156 Mechanistic studies have shown that flavonoids can exert anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and cell death regulatory effects through multiple targets and pathways. They can reduce the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines by eliminating excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS), reducing oxidative stress levels, and inhibiting the activation of inflammatory signaling pathways such as NF-κB.157 Additionally, flavonoids can regulate key signaling pathways such as PI3K/Akt, EGFR, and TGF-β/Smad, JAK2/STAT3 and inhibit EMT and the process of renal fibrosis.158–161

Despite these benefits, flavonoids generally suffer from poor water solubility, insufficient in vivo stability and low bioavailability. To address these limitations, delivery systems such as polymer nanoparticles, liposomes, mesoporous silica and nanoemulsions have been widely used.162 Researchers have developed a quercetin nanoparticle formulation that improves the solubility and bioavailability of various bioactive compounds. Male C57BL/6 mice were induced with chronic kidney failure using 50 mg/kg of adenine over four weeks. These animals were randomly grouped and given quercetin at doses of 25, 50, or 100 mg/kg, either as large particles or nanoparticles. Biochemical, histological, and molecular analyses confirmed that even with reduced quercetin intake, small-dose nanoparticles could still achieve the expected therapeutic effects in preventing kidney damage.163 Furthermore, a novel sigma-1 receptor (σ1R)-targeted nanodelivery system using resveratrol as the therapeutic agent has been developed to overcome its low bioavailability and lack of targeting.164

Anthraquinones

Anthraquinones are primarily derived from TCM herbs including Rheum palmatum (Dahuang), Polygonum multiflorum (Heshouwu), and Cassia obtusifolia (Juemingzi). Among these, Dahuang-containing preparations such as Niaoduqing granules and Shenkang injection have been widely used in CKD patients and have shown efficacy in reducing uremic toxins and attenuating renal interstitial fibrosis.150,151 Anthraquinone compounds, represented by Rhein and Emodin, have demonstrated significant renal protective potential.165–168 Current studies indicate that these compounds exert effects through anti-inflammatory, anti-fibrotic, and metabolic regulation. Rhein protects against renal aging and fibrotic injury by multiple targets through inhibition of TNF-α-mediated autophagy and necroptosis crosstalk and downregulate the activation of TLR4-related inflammatory signaling pathways.169,170 Emodin can regulate the AMPK/mTOR signaling pathway to promote autophagy, inhibit cell apoptosis, and further intervene in TGF-β/Smad and p38 MAPK pathways.171,172

Anthraquinone compounds exhibit strong lipid solubility and significant non-specific distribution. Therefore, solid lipid nanoparticles (SLN), nanostructured lipid carriers (NLC), and polymer nanoparticles have become the delivery options of choice.167,173–175 Jinxia Zhang et al developed αKIM-1-modified rhein microbubbles and used ultrasound-targeted microbubble destruction (UTMD) to achieve targeted drug delivery. These microbubbles are 2–4 μm in size and are quite stable In the UUO mouse model, the ultrasound-mediated treatment group showed improved kidney function, reduced levels of inflammatory factors IL-1β and TNF-α, and less kidney damage and collagen deposition. Immunohistochemistry also showed that TGF-β, Smad2/3, and α-SMA were downregulated, while E-cadherin was upregulated.176 Y. Hou and others created self-assembling nanoparticles (NPYs) by coating Emodin and asiatic acid with yeast cell wall particles and embedded them together with Lactobacillus casei Zhang into chitosan/sodium alginate microgels. These microgels have good sustained-release properties, significantly extending the retention time of probiotics in the gut and achieving notable accumulation of the nano-drugs in obstructed kidneys. By regulating the TGF-β/Smad signaling pathway, they effectively improved kidney fibrosis in a UUO rat model.177

Phenolic Compounds

Phenolic compounds are widely present in TCM herbs such as Curcuma longa (Jianghuang), Salvia miltiorrhiza (Danshen), Polygonum cuspidatum (Huzhang), and Rubia cordifolia (Xiancao). Among these, Danshen-based formulations, including Salvianolic acid B as a major active component, have been used as adjunctive therapy in CKD patients with reported benefits in reducing proteinuria and improving renal microcirculation.178,179 Phenolic compounds include Curcumin, Salvianolic acid B, Resveratrol, and others. They demonstrate significant advantages in renal protection through antioxidant, anti-inflammatory and regulation of hemodynamic functions.180,181 Studies have shown that curcumin and resveratrol have strong free radical scavenging capabilities, effectively reducing ROS accumulation in renal tissues.182,183 Salvianolic acid B can significantly reduce local inflammatory responses by inhibiting the activation of the TLR4/NF-κB/NLRP3 inflammasome signaling pathway.184

However, their chemical instability and rapid in vivo metabolism limit their clinical translation potential. To overcome these drawbacks, liposomes, mesoporous silica, as well as exosome - or cell membrane-encapsulated nanosystems provide effective delivery solutions. In the study of curcumin nano lipid carriers (Cur-NLCs), acrylamide (AA) induced body weight loss, gait abnormalities, histopathological damage in brain tissues, and decreased levels of neurotransmitters (DA, NE, 5-HT, GABA), GSH, and ATP in rats. Both free curcumin (Cur, 100 mg/kg) and Cur-NLCs (10 mg/kg) alleviated abnormal behaviors and histopathological changes. Free curcumin effectively restored most oxidative stress and neurotransmitter markers in the cortex and hippocampus to control levels. Cur-NLCs also showed protective effects (eg, reducing cortical MDA, restoring NE), but were less effective than free curcumin at an equivalently higher dose across several endpoints. The study suggests tissue-specific responses in the central nervous system and recommends that a combination of free curcumin and its nanoformulation may be more effective.185 Some researchers have synthesized organic quantum dots loaded with SaB and functionalized them with cyclophosphamide. The results show that this nanoplatform combines targeted therapy and intraoperative imaging, and can be used for managing ischemia-reperfusion injury.186

Glycosides

Glycosidic compounds are characteristic constituents of TCM herbs such as Astragalus membranaceus (Huangqi), Panax ginseng (Renshen), and Rehmannia glutinosa (Dihuang). Among these, Astragalus-based granules and injections have been evaluated in multiple clinical studies in CKD patients, showing protective effects against renal fibrosis and improvement in eGFR. Glycosidic compounds, such as Astragaloside IV and Ginsenoside Rg1, possess multi-target and multi-mechanistic properties.153,187 They can effectively attenuate renal interstitial fibrosis by inhibiting aberrant fibroblast activation, modulating immune responses, and reducing the expression levels of profibrotic cytokines.188 Astragaloside IV has been shown to markedly alleviate inflammatory responses through activation of the Nrf2/HO-1 antioxidant signaling pathway and suppression of NF-κB-mediated inflammatory signaling.189

Glycosidic compounds generally exhibit high hydrophilicity and are prone to rapid systemic clearance. Given these characteristics, delivery systems such as liposomes, lipid nanoparticles, as well as exosome- and cell membrane-coated nanosystems are considered suitable.190,191 Astragaloside IV has low solubility and poor bioavailability. To address this, Ast-loaded Soluplus/TPGS/Gas hybrid polymer micelles (Ast-STG-M) were prepared via thin-film hydration. The micelles were spherical and uniformly dispersed, with a small particle size (50.92 ± 0.13 nm) and high encapsulation efficiency (95.24 ± 0.53%). In vitro release of Ast from Ast-STG-M was higher than free Ast under various pH conditions. After oral administration, Ast-STG-M showed delayed elimination and significantly increased bioavailability. Moreover, Ast-STG-M markedly inhibited TPC-1 cell viability and suppressed solid tumor growth in vivo, demonstrating enhanced anti-cancer activity.192

Other Categories (Alkaloids and Terpenoids)

Beyond the four major classes described above, alkaloids and terpenoids derived from TCM also exhibit anti-renal fibrosis potential and have been investigated using nanocarrier strategies.193–196

Alkaloid compounds generally exhibit pronounced anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory activities. However, due to their nitrogen-containing heterocyclic structures, they are typically weakly basic and display considerable variability in solubility. Based on their physicochemical properties, pH-responsive polymeric nanoparticles or liposomal carriers may be preferentially selected. For example, triptolide-loaded high-density lipoprotein nanoparticles (particle size ~70 nm) were tested in a 5/6 nephrectomy rat model, showing reduced renal fibrosis and modulated gut-microbiota-kidney axis at a dose of 0.2 mg/kg twice weekly. Terpenoid compounds possess anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and immunomodulatory activitie.197,198 Their structures are predominantly composed of hydrophobic carbon skeletons, conferring strong lipophilicity and poor aqueous solubility. To address this, delivery systems such as solid lipid nanoparticles, nanoemulsions, or polymeric micelles may be employed. Docetaxel and triptolide polymer micelles were tested in anti-cancer activity.199 In vitro release studies of DTX/TPL-PMs revealed that the loaded drug exhibited a slow-release phase in physiological fluids and a rapid-release phase under acidic conditions. Compared with the free DTX/TPL drugs, DTX/TPL-PMs were more readily taken up by cancer cells. The cytotoxicity of DTX/TPL-PMs was significantly higher than that of the free drugs, which showed stronger growth inhibitory effects and higher apoptosis rates in cancer cells.199

Limitations and Future Perspectives: Bridging Material Characteristics to Clinical Translation

Building upon the unique perspective of this review—bridging nanocarrier material properties with pharmacokinetic behavior and clinical translation potential, this section critically examines the current limitations and future opportunities for TCM nanoformulations in renal fibrosis treatment. We analyze these challenges through the lens of material-to-clinical translation, identifying specific barriers at each stage of the development pipeline.

Current Limitations: From Bench to Bedside

Standardization: A Prerequisite for Clinical Translation

Most traditional Chinese medicines consist of complex systems with dozens to hundreds of components. Variations in the place of origin, collection season, and processing techniques all lead to fluctuations in the composition and content of the components, directly affecting the drug loading capacity, encapsulation rate, and reproducibility of the therapeutic efficacy of nanoformulations. From a translational perspective, this variability poses a major barrier to regulatory approval, as consistent manufacturing is a prerequisite for clinical trials.211

It is necessary to establish quality control standards for biological efficacy and develop nanocarrier materials and processes that are compatible with complex systems to enhance the tolerance to component fluctuations.212 Recent advances have proposed novel analytical tools—such as the h multiple similarity index (HMSI) for evaluating batch consistency of herbal fingerprints and pharmacodynamic-based quality consistency evaluation methods for multi-batch compound preparations—to address these challenges. Future efforts should focus on developing batch-to-batch consistency protocols and reference standards for TCM nanoformulations, in alignment with evolving regulatory frameworks such as China’s NMPA standards for TCM.212

Stability and Storage

The macromolecular or easily oxidizable components in traditional Chinese medicine can accelerate the degradation of the carrier and the leakage of the drug, resulting in a short shelf life and strict storage conditions for the formulation.213 As summarized in Table 4, different nanocarriers exhibit distinct stability profiles—PEGylated liposomes show superior storage stability (<10% size change over 6 months) compared to PLGA nanoparticles, which are more susceptible to hydrolysis. It is necessary to conduct in-depth research on the interaction mechanism among traditional Chinese medicine, the carrier, and the environment. By optimizing the prescription (such as adding surfactants, freeze-drying protectants), developing stabilization techniques such as freeze-drying or spray drying, and combining multi-dimensional stability evaluation methods including physical, chemical, and biological ones, these problems can be solved.213

Safety Evaluation

The cytotoxicity of synthetic nanomaterials and their metabolites poses potential toxicity risks. Moreover, the existing toxicological evaluation systems are not applicable to the nano-medicine composite system.214 This discrepancy highlights the importance of in vivo validation and the limitations of relying solely on in vitro cytotoxicity data. It is necessary to strengthen the screening of material biocompatibility and utilize live imaging and omics technologies to analyze the toxicity mechanism.214

Real-World Clinical Translation Barriers

While the scientific principles of TCM nanoformulations are well-established, several real-world barriers currently impede their clinical translation. These barriers span regulatory, economic, and clinical adoption domains, and must be addressed before TCM nanoformulations can benefit patients with renal fibrosis.

TCM nanoformulations currently occupy an ambiguous regulatory space between traditional herbal products and nanomedicines, with regulatory agencies such as the FDA, NMPA, and EMA lacking specific guidelines for this hybrid category. Adding to this uncertainty, the inherent variability of TCM raw materials, which can differ by 10–20% in active component content depending on source and processing, poses substantial challenges for establishing batch-to-batch consistency and meeting the stringent quality requirements expected of nanomedicines. Furthermore, unlike single-component nanomedicines, TCM nanoformulations require comprehensive toxicological evaluation of not only the nanocarrier and the TCM extract itself, but also potential synergistic effects and degradation products, substantially increasing the regulatory documentation burden.

From a manufacturing perspective, the GMP-compliant production of TCM nanoformulations is considerably more resource intensive than conventional TCM preparations. This involves specialized equipment for nanoparticle fabrication, rigorous quality control testing for each batch, and extensive stability studies under both accelerated and real-time conditions, all of which contribute to a final product that typically costs 3 to 5 times more than conventional counterparts. Meanwhile, the supply chain itself is inherently complex, encompassing both agricultural sourcing that is subject to seasonal and geographic variability and advanced nanomaterial manufacturing that demands tightly controlled environments, creating multiple points of potential failure.

On the clinical adoption front, most healthcare providers lack formal training in both TCM principles and nanotechnology, creating a knowledge gap that hinders informed prescribing and limits integration into routine nephrology practice. While TCM is widely accepted in Asian countries, Western patients may remain skeptical of “herbal nanoparticles”, making patient education and robust clinical trial data essential for broader acceptance. Finally, TCM nanoformulations currently lack dedicated reimbursement codes, and their cost-effectiveness relative to standard therapies such as ACE inhibitors, ARBs, and SGLT2 inhibitors must be convincingly demonstrated to secure insurance coverage and facilitate clinical uptake.

Future Prospects: Toward Clinical Translation

Based on the challenges identified above and the unique material-to-pharmacokinetics correlations established in this review, we propose the following prioritized directions for future research:

Development of New Nano-Materials and Technologies

To enhance the delivery efficiency and therapeutic effect of traditional Chinese medicine components, efforts should be made to develop various advanced nanocarrier strategies. Specifically, we recommend: (1) developing intelligent responsive carriers that are sensitive to the disease microenvironment (such as pH, enzymes, ROS, and temperature), to achieve precise and on-demand drug release at the lesion site, thereby improving efficacy and reducing systemic toxicity; (2) constructing biomimetic nanocarriers encapsulated by cell membranes (such as red blood cell membranes, tumor cell membranes, and macrophage membranes), endowing them with long circulation or active targeting capabilities, significantly enhancing delivery efficiency; and (3) exploring the application of advanced delivery technologies in the treatment of gene-related diseases.

Integration of Traditional Chinese Medicine and Western Medicine

This strategy fully leverages the unique advantages of the multi-component, multi-target, and multi-pathway synergistic effects of traditional Chinese medicine, combined with the precise delivery capabilities of nanotechnology. The implementation paths include synergistic delivery, loading different active ingredients of traditional Chinese medicine (or traditional Chinese medicine active ingredients and Western medicine) together onto the same nanocarrier to achieve simultaneous delivery and enhance efficacy; targeted regulation, using nanocarriers to target deliver traditional Chinese medicine components to key nodes of disease pathways (such as inflammatory factors, fibrosis signaling molecules, oxidative stress pathways), to achieve overall regulation of complex disease networks. This strategy provides a more effective new strategy for treating complex diseases (such as renal fibrosis, tumors, neurodegenerative diseases), and is an important direction for the modernization of traditional Chinese medicine.

Actionable Research Gaps and Roadmap for Future Studies

Despite the promising potential of TCM nanoformulations for renal fibrosis treatment, several critical research gaps remain unaddressed. Based on our systematic analysis, we identify the following actionable research gaps for future investigations. Identified actionable research gaps: (1) Lack of standardized in vitro-in vivo correlation (IVIVC) models specifically validated for TCM nanoformulations in renal fibrosis.(2) Insufficient understanding of long-term safety and chronic toxicity profiles of biodegradable nanocarriers (eg, PLGA, chitosan) beyond the typical 8-week study duration.(3) Absence of batch-to-batch consistency standards for TCM-derived active ingredients, which vary significantly due to agricultural and processing factors.(4) Limited data on the pharmacokinetic interactions between multiple TCM components co-encapsulated in the same nanocarrier.(5) No established regulatory pathways or quality control frameworks specifically designed for complex TCM nanoformulations.

Conclusion

This review provides a systematic and comprehensive summary of TCM nanoformulations for the treatment of renal fibrosis. The principal findings are summarized as follows: 1. Pathogenesis of renal fibrosis. Renal fibrosis is a multifaceted pathological process driven by tubular epithelial injury, podocyte damage, pericyte activation, immune dysregulation, and excessive ECM deposition. 2. Principles of renal-targeted nano-delivery. Renal targeting is governed by three interrelated processes—glomerular filtration (size-dependent, with a cutoff of 6–8 nm, and charge-dependent, favoring cationic particles), tubular reabsorption (via endocytic pathways, exhibiting a “double-edged sword” effect), and tubular secretion (mediated by OAT/OCT and P-gp/MRP transporters), all of which are markedly altered in the fibrotic kidney. 3. Classification of nanocarriers. Nanocarriers are categorized into five classes: lipid-based (the most clinically advanced), polymeric (offering tunable degradation), inorganic (enabling multimodal imaging but raising biosafety concerns), biological (providing innate targeting yet facing manufacturing challenges), and hybrid/biomimetic (combining multiple advantages but encountering regulatory uncertainty). 4. Anti-fibrotic TCM components and their nanodelivery strategies. Five major TCM compound classes exhibit anti-fibrotic effects—flavonoids, anthraquinones, phenolic compounds, glycosides, and others (alkaloids, terpenoids). Nanodelivery strategies have successfully overcome the inherent limitations of these compounds, including poor solubility, rapid clearance, and lack of targeting. 5. Preclinical efficacy of nanoencapsulation. Converging preclinical evidence demonstrates that nanoencapsulation consistently improves renal accumulation, prolongs circulation half-life, enhances anti-fibrotic efficacy, and reduces systemic toxicity relative to free drugs.

Despite the considerable promise of TCM nanoformulations, several critical limitations remain unaddressed. These include: batch-to-batch variability of TCM raw materials, which poses a major challenge to standardization; insufficient long-term chronic toxicity data (≥6 months) and limited immunogenicity profiling for comprehensive safety evaluation; the intrinsic difficulty of deconvoluting multi-component synergistic mechanisms; the inadequate recapitulation of human CKD comorbidities in current preclinical models; and formidable clinical translation barriers, including regulatory uncertainty, high manufacturing costs, and the absence of dedicated reimbursement codes.

To address these challenges, future research should prioritize the development of Quality by Design (QbD) approaches and reference materials to ensure standardization, alongside long-term toxicity studies with integrated immunogenicity profiling. Multi-omics technologies should be employed to elucidate synergistic mechanisms at the systems level, while more clinically relevant animal models that better reflect human CKD pathophysiology need to be established. Ultimately, clinical translation must be advanced through stepwise regulatory pathways, leveraging emerging technologies such as AI-assisted formulation design and stimulus-responsive nanocarrier systems.
