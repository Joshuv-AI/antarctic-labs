Oppenheimer-Snyder Collapse in f ⁡ ( R ) f(R) Gravity : Stalemate or Resolution?
Soumya Chakrabarti, Apratim Ganguly, Radouane Gannouji, Chiranjeeb Singha

Abstract
We study the Oppenheimer–Snyder (OS) collapse problem in metric f ⁡ ( R ) f(R) gravity by matching a homogeneous dust Friedmann–Lemaître–Robertson–Walker (FLRW) interior to a generalized Vaidya exterior across a timelike hypersurface. In metric f ⁡ ( R ) f(R) gravity, regular matching requires the continuity not only of the induced metric and extrinsic curvature, but also of the Ricci scalar and its normal derivative. These additional conditions generically exclude the usual Ricci-flat exteriors, such as the Schwarzschild solution. We show that, for an unrestricted generalized Vaidya exterior, the matching conditions fix the boundary data but do not uniquely determine the bulk extension, leaving open the possibility of a physical resolution of the collapse problem. However, once the exterior matter content is restricted to the generalized Vaidya form, the field equations impose a strong constraint, forcing f , R f_{,R} to be linear in the areal radius, f , R = A ( v ) r + B ( v ) f_{,R}=A(v)\,r+B(v) . For locally invertible f , R f_{,R} with f , R R ≠ 0 f_{,RR}\neq 0 , this sharply reduces the admissible class of exteriors, so that the matching data uniquely determine the exterior solution on each interval where the boundary map is locally invertible. We further show that, for generic viable f ⁡ ( R ) f(R) models, the branch with A ⁡ ( v ) ≠ 0 A(v)\neq 0 does not admit a global extension with finite asymptotic curvature, while the branch A ⁡ ( v ) = 0 A(v)=0 places the interior on a constant-curvature sector. This excludes nontrivial dust collapse, although it does not rule out collapse for more general interior matter with constant trace. Thus, generalized Vaidya exteriors reopen the collapse problem at a formal level, but within the restricted matter sector considered here, the OS dust collapse problem remains unresolved and the physically acceptable branch is highly constrained.



I
Introduction

The Oppenheimer–Snyder (OS) model remains the canonical idealized description of gravitational collapse, a homogeneous dust-filled FLRW interior matched across a timelike comoving hypersurface to an exterior Schwarzschild geometry
18
. In general relativity (GR), this construction is governed by the Darmois–Israel junction conditions, namely the continuity of the first and second fundamental forms across the matching surface
5
;
13
. More general radiating configurations are commonly described by Vaidya-type exteriors, and their matching to collapsing interiors has long served as a standard framework for dissipative collapse
24
;
19
. In particular, generalized Vaidya spacetimes provide a natural extension in which the mass function depends on both the null coordinate and the areal radius, thereby accommodating a broader class of matter sources than pure null dust
12
;
25
.

The situation is considerably more restrictive in metric
f(R)
gravity, one of the simplest higher-curvature extensions of GR, widely studied in cosmology, astrophysics, and inflationary model building
22
;
21
;
6
;
4
. In addition to the usual spin-2 graviton, metric
f(R)
theories propagate an additional scalar degree of freedom (the scalaron), reflecting their equivalence to scalar–tensor theories
23
. The viability of such theories requires standard conditions such as
f_{,R}>0
and
f_{,RR}>0
, together with the absence of tachyonic instabilities. Because the field equations are fourth order in the metric, the matching problem is no longer governed solely by the Darmois–Israel conditions. For regular matching with
f_{,RR}\neq 0
, one must also impose the continuity of the Ricci scalar and of its normal derivative across the hypersurface
7
;
20
. Physically, these additional conditions reflect the requirement that the scalar degree of freedom and its flux remain continuous across the matching surface.

These extra junction conditions severely constrain collapse scenarios. In particular, they obstruct the naive OS construction in metric
f(R)
gravity whenever one attempts to match a nontrivial homogeneous interior directly to a Ricci-flat exterior
20
;
10
;
2
. This obstruction is not merely technical but reflects a fundamental incompatibility between the dynamics of the scalar sector and the assumed exterior geometry. A closely related difficulty has also been identified in the context of static compact objects, where the same junction conditions constrain viable neutron star solutions in specific models such as the Starobinsky model
9
. This raises a natural question: can relaxing the exterior solution from the standard Vaidya metric to the generalized Vaidya class restore sufficient functional freedom to evade this obstruction, or does the higher-order structure of
f(R)
gravity reimpose a hidden rigidity? This question is particularly timely in light of recent analyses emphasizing that the standard OS collapse is generically incompatible with the stricter matching conditions and that more general exteriors must be considered
2
.

In this work, we address this question by matching a homogeneous FLRW interior to a generalized Vaidya exterior in metric
f(R)
gravity. Our analysis leads to the following main results. First, without imposing restrictions on the exterior matter content, the matching conditions determine only the restriction of the mass function on the matching hypersurface and do not fix its extension into the bulk, resulting in a non-uniqueness of exterior solutions. Second, once the exterior matter content is restricted to the generalized Vaidya form, the field equations impose a strong rigidity condition: the derivative
f_{,R}
must be linear in the areal radius,
f_{,R}=A(v)\,r+B(v)
. Third, for locally invertible
f_{,R}
with
f_{,RR}\neq 0
, this constraint reduces the admissible class of exteriors so drastically that the matching data determine the exterior solution uniquely on each interval where the boundary map is locally invertible.

However, this mathematical resolution comes at a physical cost. We show that the branch with
A(v)\neq 0
generically leads to an unbounded curvature and divergent matter variables at large radius, rendering it physically unacceptable as an exterior describing an isolated collapsing object. On the other hand, the branch
A(v)=0
places the interior on a constant-curvature sector, which enforces a constant trace of the energy–momentum tensor and excludes nontrivial dust collapse. While this branch does not forbid collapse altogether, it restricts the admissible interior matter content to a narrow class of constant-trace sources.

The resulting picture is therefore more subtle than a simple no-go theorem. Generalized Vaidya exteriors reopen the collapse problem at a formal level by introducing functional freedom in the exterior geometry. However, once physically motivated restrictions on the matter sector are imposed, this freedom collapses into a highly constrained structure, and the standard OS dust collapse remains incompatible with metric
f(R)
gravity. This suggests that any physically viable realization of gravitational collapse in these theories may require going beyond the generalized Vaidya framework and considering more general exterior matter configurations.

II
Metric
f(R)
gravity

We consider metric
f(R)
gravity, defined by the action

\displaystyle S=\frac{1}{2\kappa}\int d^{4}x\,\sqrt{-g}\,f(R)+S_{m}\,,\quad\kappa=8\pi G.

(1)

Varying Eq. (
1
) with respect to the metric yields the field equations

\displaystyle f_{,R}R_{\mu\nu}-\frac{1}{2}f(R)g_{\mu\nu}+\left(g_{\mu\nu}\Box-\nabla_{\mu}\nabla_{\nu}\right)f_{,R}=\kappa\,T_{\mu\nu},

(2)

where

\displaystyle T_{\mu\nu}\equiv-\frac{2}{\sqrt{-g}}\frac{\delta S_{m}}{\delta g^{\mu\nu}}\,.

(3)

Eq. (
2
) implies that, in addition to the spin-2 graviton, metric
f(R)
gravity propagates an additional scalar degree of freedom encoded in
f_{,R}
. This can be made explicit by considering the trace of the field equations,

\displaystyle 3\Box f_{,R}+f_{,R}\,R-2f(R)=\kappa T,

(4)

which governs the dynamics of the scalaron. Metric
f(R)
gravity is dynamically equivalent to a scalar–tensor theory in which
f_{,R}
plays the role of an effective scalar field.

To analyze the propagating degrees of freedom, we linearize the theory around a background metric
\bar{g}_{\mu\nu}
,

\displaystyle g_{\mu\nu}=\bar{g}_{\mu\nu}+h_{\mu\nu},

(5)

and decompose the perturbation into its transverse-traceless (TT) component
h^{\text{TT}}_{\mu\nu}
and the remaining modes. The quadratic action for the TT spin-2 sector takes the schematic form

\displaystyle S^{(2)}_{\text{spin-2}}=\frac{f_{,R}(\bar{R})}{8\kappa}\int d^{4}x\,\sqrt{-\bar{g}}\;h^{\mu\nu}_{\text{TT}}\,\mathcal{E}\,h^{\text{TT}}_{\mu\nu}+\cdots,

(6)

where
\bar{R}
is the background scalar curvature and
\mathcal{E}
denotes the usual second-order Einstein kinetic operator. Requiring a positive kinetic term for the spin-2 graviton leads to the first viability condition

\displaystyle f_{,R}>0.

(7)

The scalar degree of freedom is governed by Eq. (
4
). Perturbing the curvature around background curvature scalar
\bar{R}
in a region filled with matter, one obtains at leading order a Klein–Gordon type equation

\displaystyle\left(\Box-M^{2}(\bar{R})\right)\delta R=\frac{\kappa}{3f_{,RR}(\bar{R})}\,\delta T,

(8)

where the effective mass squared is given by

\displaystyle M^{2}(\bar{R})=\frac{1}{3}\left(\frac{f_{,R}(\bar{R})}{f_{,RR}(\bar{R})}-\bar{R}\right).

(9)

The absence of tachyonic instabilities requires
22
;
17

\displaystyle M^{2}(R)=\frac{f_{,R}-Rf_{,RR}}{3f_{,RR}}>0.

(10)

A further constraint arises from the Dolgov–Kawasaki instability
8
, which reflects the possibility of rapid growth of curvature perturbations
\delta R
in matter if the scalar sector has the wrong sign. Avoiding this instability requires from Eq. (
8
)

\displaystyle f_{,RR}>0.

(11)

In summary, viable metric
f(R)
models must satisfy the conditions

\displaystyle f_{,R}>0\,,\qquad f_{,RR}>0\,,\qquad f_{,R}-Rf_{,RR}>0.

(12)

These conditions ensure a positive, effective gravitational coupling, stability of the scalar sector, and the absence of tachyonic modes.

Junction conditions

Let
\Sigma
be a timelike hypersurface separating an interior region (
-
) from an exterior region (
+
). In a neighborhood of
\Sigma

(y,\xi^{i})

to
\Sigma
, and
\xi^{i}
(

\Sigma
. The hypersurface is located at
y=0
, and the metric takes the form

\displaystyle ds^{2}=g_{ab}\,d\xi^{a}d\xi^{b}=dy^{2}+\gamma_{ij}(y,\xi)\,d\xi^{i}d\xi^{j},

(13)

where
\gamma_{ij}
is the induced metric (first fundamental form) on the constant-
y
hypersurfaces, and in particular
\gamma_{ij}\big|_{y=0}
is the intrinsic metric on
\Sigma
.

The extrinsic curvature (second fundamental form) of
\Sigma
is defined by

\displaystyle K_{ij}\equiv\gamma_{i}{}^{\mu}\gamma_{j}{}^{\nu}\nabla_{\mu}n_{\nu},

(14)

where
\gamma_{i}{}^{\mu}
is the projector onto
\Sigma
. For an embedding
x^{\alpha}=x^{\alpha}(\xi^{i})
, this can be written as

\displaystyle K_{ij}=-n_{\alpha}\,\Bigl(\frac{\partial^{2}x^{\alpha}}{\partial\xi^{i}\partial\xi^{j}}+\Gamma^{\alpha}{}_{\beta\gamma}\frac{\partial x^{\beta}}{\partial\xi^{i}}\frac{\partial x^{\gamma}}{\partial\xi^{j}}\Bigr)\,.

(15)

We restrict attention to regular matchings across
\Sigma
, i.e., configurations without distributional sources localized on the hypersurface. In metric
f(R)
gravity, and assuming
f_{,RR}\neq 0
on
\Sigma
, the junction conditions take the form
7
;
20

\displaystyle\big[\gamma_{ij}\big]^{+}_{-}

\displaystyle=0,

(16)

\displaystyle\big[K_{ij}\big]^{+}_{-}

\displaystyle=0,

(17)

\displaystyle\big[R\big]^{+}_{-}

\displaystyle=0,

(18)

\displaystyle\big[n^{\mu}\nabla_{\mu}R\big]^{+}_{-}

\displaystyle=0,

(19)

where
[X]^{+}_{-}\equiv X^{+}|_{\Sigma}-X^{-}|_{\Sigma}
denotes the jump of any quantity across
\Sigma
, and

The first two conditions, Eqs. (
16
) and (

;
13
. The new requirements in metric

18
) and (
19

) contain second derivatives of
f(R)
and therefore involve, in general, fourth derivatives of the metric.

From a physical perspective, these additional conditions reflect the presence of the scalar degree of freedom: regular matching requires not only the continuity of the geometry but also the continuity of the scalaron and of its normal flux across the hypersurface. As a consequence, the space of admissible matchings in metric
f(R)
gravity is significantly more constrained than in GR, and configurations that are allowed in GR may fail to satisfy these stricter conditions.

IV
Generalized Vaidya exteriors

The Vaidya spacetime
24
and its generalizations
25
;
12
are a special case of a more general spherically symmetric line element

\displaystyle ds^{2}

\displaystyle=-e^{2\psi(v,r)}\left[1-\frac{2M(v,r)}{r}\right]dv^{2}+2\,\epsilon\,e^{\psi(v,r)}dv\,dr

\displaystyle\quad+r^{2}d\Omega^{2},\qquad(\epsilon=\pm 1),

(20)

which can accommodate a combination of Type-I and Type-II matter fields. Indeed, following the Hawking–Ellis algebraic classification of the stress-energy tensor
T^{a}{}_{b}

11
, a
Type-I
matter field is one for which
T^{a}{}_{b}
admits a timelike eigenvector
u^{a}
(and three spacelike eigenvectors). Equivalently, there exists an orthonormal frame in which
T^{a}{}_{b}
is diagonal,

\displaystyle T^{a}{}_{b}=\mathrm{diag}\!\left(-\rho,\,p_{1},\,p_{2},\,p_{3}\right),

(21)

so that the matter possesses a local rest frame in which the energy flux vanishes, as in the case of dust or perfect fluids. By contrast, a
Type-II
matter field is characterized by the existence of a
repeated null
eigenvector
k^{a}
(a double principal null direction), so that
T^{a}{}_{b}
is not fully diagonalizable in an orthonormal frame. Physically, this corresponds to intrinsically radiative flow with no rest frame that eliminates the energy flux; the canonical example is null dust,

\displaystyle T_{ab}=\Phi\,k_{a}k_{b},\qquad k^{a}k_{a}=0,

(22)

see
11
;
15
.

In the metric (
20
), the function
M(v,r)
acts as a generalized mass function, representing the gravitational energy enclosed within a radius
r
. For
\epsilon=+1
, the null coordinate
v
denotes the Eddington advanced time, with
r
decreasing along ingoing null rays at constant
v
. Conversely,
\epsilon=-1
corresponds to an outgoing null congruence. The condition
\psi(v,r)=0
selects a particular subclass leading to the generalized Vaidya geometry
25
;
12
. We choose
\epsilon=+1
and adopt the corresponding form of the metric,

\displaystyle ds^{2}=-\left(1-\frac{2M(v,r)}{r}\right)dv^{2}+2dvdr+r^{2}d\Omega^{2}.

(23)

Choosing two null vectors
l_{\mu}
and
n_{\mu}
such that

\displaystyle l_{\mu}

\displaystyle=\delta^{0}_{\mu},\qquad n_{\mu}=\frac{1}{2}\left[1-\frac{2M(v,r)}{r}\right]\delta^{0}_{\mu}-\delta^{1}_{\mu},

\displaystyle l_{\mu}l^{\mu}

\displaystyle=n_{\mu}n^{\mu}=0,\qquad l_{\mu}n^{\mu}=-1,

(24)

and assuming general relativity, one can define the corresponding energy-momentum tensor associated to this spacetime, in the form
14
;
25

\displaystyle T_{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=T^{(n)}_{\mu\nu}+T^{(m)}_{\mu\nu},

(25)

\displaystyle T^{(n)}_{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=\mu\,l_{\mu}l_{\nu},

(26)

\displaystyle T^{(m)}_{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=(\rho+p)(l_{\mu}n_{\nu}+l_{\nu}n_{\mu})+p\,g_{\mu\nu}.

(27)

In this setup, the Einstein field equations yield

\displaystyle\mu

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\kappa r^{2}}\frac{\partial M(v,r)}{\partial v}\,,

(28)

\displaystyle\rho

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\kappa r^{2}}\frac{\partial M(v,r)}{\partial r}\,,

(29)

\displaystyle p

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\kappa r}\frac{\partial^{2}M(v,r)}{\partial r^{2}}.

(30)

An energy-momentum tensor written in this form is commonly employed to represent the source supported by a generalized Vaidya metric. The term
T^{(n)}_{\mu\nu}
represents null radiation propagating along the hypersurfaces
v=\mathrm{const}
and therefore of type-II, while
T^{(m)}_{\mu\nu}
is the Type-I, non-null part of the source and may be interpreted as an anisotropic matter sector
16
;
1
. The full tensor
T_{\mu\nu}=T^{(n)}_{\mu\nu}+T^{(m)}_{\mu\nu}
is of Type-II whenever
\mu\neq 0
. In this paper, we assume that the exterior spacetime is sourced by a fluid described by Eqs. (
25
-
27
), without imposing a specific functional form as in Eqs. (
28
–
30
).

Matching FLRW Interior to a Generalized Vaidya Exterior

V.1
Geometric Matching and Non-Uniqueness

We consider the matching of a spatially flat FLRW interior spacetime to a generalized Vaidya exterior across a timelike hypersurface
\Sigma
. The interior metric is

\displaystyle ds_{-}^{2}=-dt^{2}+a^{2}(t)\left(dr^{2}+r^{2}d\Omega^{2}\right),

(31)

while the exterior metric is taken to be of generalized Vaidya form

\displaystyle ds_{+}^{2}=-\left(1-\frac{2M(v,r_{v})}{r_{v}}\right)dv^{2}+2\,dv\,dr_{v}+r_{v}^{2}d\Omega^{2},

(32)

where
M(v,r_{v})
is the generalized mass function.

The matching hypersurface
\Sigma
is defined in the interior coordinates by
r=r_{0}
, and parametrically in the exterior coordinates as

\displaystyle r_{v}=\mathcal{R}(t),\qquad v=\mathcal{T}(t).

(33)

The induced metrics on
\Sigma
are

\displaystyle ds_{-}^{2}|_{\Sigma}

\displaystyle=-dt^{2}+a^{2}(t)r_{0}^{2}d\Omega^{2},

(34)

\displaystyle ds_{+}^{2}|_{\Sigma}

\displaystyle=-\left(V_{\Sigma}(t)\dot{\mathcal{T}}^{2}-2\dot{\mathcal{T}}\dot{\mathcal{R}}\right)dt^{2}+\mathcal{R}(t)^{2}d\Omega^{2},

(35)

where dot represents derivative with respect to
t
, and

\displaystyle V_{\Sigma}(t)\equiv 1-\frac{2M_{\Sigma}(t)}{\mathcal{R}(t)},\qquad M_{\Sigma}(t)\equiv M(\mathcal{T}(t),\mathcal{R}(t)).

(36)

Matching the first fundamental form on
\Sigma
yields

\displaystyle V_{\Sigma}\dot{\mathcal{T}}^{2}-2\dot{\mathcal{T}}\dot{\mathcal{R}}=1,

(37)

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(t)=r_{0}a(t).

(38)

Imposing continuity of the extrinsic curvature, we compute the unit normals to
\Sigma
. For the interior, the unit normal is

\displaystyle n_{-}^{\mu}=\left(0,\;\frac{1}{a(t)},\;0,\;0\right),\qquad n^{-}_{\mu}=\left(0,\;a(t),\;0,\;0\right).

(39)

For the exterior, the tangent vector is

\displaystyle u^{\mu}\equiv\frac{dx^{\mu}}{dt}=(\dot{\mathcal{T}},\dot{\mathcal{R}},0,0),

(40)

and defining

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{\prime}(t)\equiv\frac{d\mathcal{R}}{d\mathcal{T}}=\frac{\dot{\mathcal{R}}}{\dot{\mathcal{T}}},

(41)

a convenient outward-pointing unit normal (normalized with respect to the exterior metric) is

\displaystyle n_{+}^{\mu}=\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{V_{\Sigma}-2\mathcal{R}^{\prime}}},\;\frac{V_{\Sigma}-\mathcal{R}^{\prime}}{\sqrt{V_{\Sigma}-2\mathcal{R}^{\prime}}},\;0,\;0\right).

(42)

For the interior, one finds

\displaystyle K^{-}_{tt}

\displaystyle=0,

(43)

\displaystyle K^{-}_{\theta\theta}

\displaystyle=a(t)\,r_{0},

(44)

\displaystyle K^{-}_{\varphi\varphi}

\displaystyle=a(t)\,r_{0}\,\sin^{2}\theta.

(45)

and for the exterior,

\displaystyle K^{+}_{tt}=-\frac{1}{\sqrt{V_{\Sigma}-2\mathcal{R}^{\prime}}}\Bigl[\ddot{\mathcal{R}}-\mathcal{R}^{\prime}\ddot{\mathcal{T}}

\displaystyle~~+\dot{\mathcal{T}}^{2}\Bigl((V_{\Sigma}-3\mathcal{R}^{\prime})\Bigl(\frac{M_{\Sigma}}{\mathcal{R}^{2}}-\frac{1}{\mathcal{R}}M_{,r_{v}}\Bigr)+\frac{1}{\mathcal{R}}M_{,v}\Bigr)\Bigr]_{\Sigma}\,,

(46)

\displaystyle K^{+}_{\theta\theta}=\mathcal{R}(t)\frac{V_{\Sigma}-\mathcal{R}^{\prime}}{\sqrt{V_{\Sigma}-2\mathcal{R}^{\prime}}}\,,

(47)

\displaystyle K^{+}_{\varphi\varphi}=\mathcal{R}(t)\sin^{2}\theta\frac{V_{\Sigma}-\mathcal{R}^{\prime}}{\sqrt{V_{\Sigma}-2\mathcal{R}^{\prime}}}\,.

(48)

Matching
K_{\theta\theta}
yields

\displaystyle V_{\Sigma}=1-\dot{\mathcal{R}}^{2},\qquad\dot{\mathcal{T}}=\frac{1}{1-\dot{\mathcal{R}}}.

(49)

Imposing
K^{+}_{tt}=K^{-}_{tt}
gives

\displaystyle(1-\dot{\mathcal{R}})(1-2\dot{\mathcal{R}})\left(\ddot{\mathcal{R}}+\frac{M_{\Sigma}}{\mathcal{R}^{2}}-\frac{M_{,r_{v}}}{\mathcal{R}}\right)+\frac{M_{,v}}{\mathcal{R}}=0.

(50)

Using the previous relations, this reduces to

\displaystyle\left.M_{,r_{v}}\right|_{\Sigma}=\frac{M_{\Sigma}}{r_{0}a}+r_{0}^{2}a\ddot{a}.

(51)

The complete set of Darmois–Israel matching conditions is therefore

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(t)

\displaystyle=r_{0}a(t),

(52)

\displaystyle\dot{\mathcal{T}}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-r_{0}\dot{a}(t)},

(53)

\displaystyle M_{\Sigma}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{r_{0}^{3}}{2}a(t)\dot{a}(t)^{2},

(54)

\displaystyle\left.M_{,r_{v}}\right|_{\Sigma}

\displaystyle=r_{0}^{2}\Bigl(\frac{1}{2}\dot{a}(t)^{2}+a(t)\ddot{a}(t)\Bigr).

(55)

Standard Vaidya Limit
:

In the standard Vaidya case one has
\left.M_{,r_{v}}\right|_{\Sigma}=0
which reduces Eq. (

\displaystyle a\ddot{a}+\frac{1}{2}\dot{a}^{2}=0,

It then follows from Eqs. (

Generalized Vaidya
:

For the generalized Vaidya case,
M=M(v,r_{v})
. Therefore, for a given interior dynamics
a(t)
, the matching conditions determine only

\displaystyle M_{\Sigma}(t),\qquad\left.M_{,r_{v}}\right|_{\Sigma}.

(58)

This information is specified along a one-dimensional curve in the
\left(v,r_{v}\right)

in a neighborhood of
\Sigma

\Sigma

Proposition 1
.

Let
\Sigma
be the matching hypersurface parametrized by
t\mapsto(\mathcal{T}(t),\mathcal{R}(t))
in the
(v,r_{v})
plane.
Prescribing
M
and finitely many of its partial derivatives on
\Sigma
does not uniquely determine the function
M(v,r_{v})
in any neighborhood of
\Sigma
.

Proof.

The hypersurface
\Sigma
defines a smooth embedded one-dimensional embedded curve in the
(v,r_{v})
plane.
In a neighborhood of
\Sigma
, one can introduce local coordinates
(s,n)
such that
\Sigma
is given by
n=0
, where
s
parametrizes the curve and
n
is a transverse coordinate.

Let
M(s,n)
be a smooth function representing the mass function in these adapted coordinates. Suppose that, for some fixed integer
N\geq 0
, we prescribe all partial derivatives of
M
up to total order
N
on
\Sigma
, i.e.

\displaystyle\left.\partial_{s}^{p}\partial_{n}^{q}M\right|_{n=0}\qquad\text{for all }p,q\geq 0\text{ with }p+q\leq N.

(59)

We now construct a nontrivial deformation of
M
that preserves all these data on
\Sigma
but differs away from it.
Let
\psi(s,n)
be an arbitrary smooth function which is not identically zero, and define

\displaystyle\delta M(s,n)=n^{N+1}\psi(s,n).

(60)

By construction,
\delta M
vanishes to order
N+1
in the transverse direction
n
. Therefore, for all nonnegative integers
p,q
such that
p+q\leq N
, one has

\displaystyle\left.\partial_{s}^{p}\partial_{n}^{q}\delta M\right|_{n=0}=0.

(61)

Indeed, any derivative involving at most
N
total derivatives cannot remove the overall factor of
n^{N+1}
, so the result still vanishes at
n=0
.

Now define a new function

\displaystyle\widetilde{M}(s,n)=M(s,n)+\delta M(s,n).

(62)

Then, by linearity,

\displaystyle\left.\partial_{s}^{p}\partial_{n}^{q}\widetilde{M}\right|_{n=0}=\left.\partial_{s}^{p}\partial_{n}^{q}M\right|_{n=0}\qquad(p+q\leq N).

(63)

Thus
\widetilde{M}
agrees with
M
on
\Sigma
up to all derivatives of total order
\leq N
.
However, since
\psi
is not identically zero,
\delta M\not\equiv 0
for
n\neq 0
, and therefore

\displaystyle\widetilde{M}(s,n)\neq M(s,n)

(64)

in any neighborhood of
\Sigma
.

Finally, since the coordinate transformation
(v,r_{v})\leftrightarrow(s,n)
is smooth with nonvanishing Jacobian, equality of derivatives up to order
N
in
(s,n)
is equivalent to equality of the derivatives
\partial_{v}^{p}\partial_{r_{v}}^{q}
up to the same order in
(v,r_{v})
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\left.\partial_{v}^{p}\partial_{r_{v}}^{q}\widetilde{M}\right|_{\Sigma}=\left.\partial_{v}^{p}\partial_{r_{v}}^{q}M\right|_{\Sigma}\qquad(p+q\leq N),

(65)

while
\widetilde{M}\neq M
away from
\Sigma
.
This proves that prescribing
M
and finitely many of its derivatives on
\Sigma
does not uniquely determine
M(v,r_{v})
in any neighborhood of
\Sigma
.

Apparent freedom
:

In metric

gravity, when the exterior is taken to be the standard Vaidya spacetime
M=M(v)
, the junction conditions overconstrain the system, indicating that homogeneous collapse generically admits no solution. By contrast, within the generalized Vaidya class
M=M\left(v,r_{v}\right)
, the matching determines only the restriction of
M
(and one of its derivatives) on
\Sigma
, leaving genuine functional freedom away from
\Sigma
. This additional freedom allows the two extra
f(R)
junction conditions to be satisfied, making the collapse problem a priori consistent. However, as we show below, this apparent freedom is in fact strongly constrained by the field equations.

V.2
Matching in
f(R)
Gravity

We now impose the junction conditions of Sec.
III
to the FLRW–generalized Vaidya matching. For the FLRW interior,

\displaystyle R_{-}=6\left(\frac{\ddot{a}}{a}+\frac{\dot{a}^{2}}{a^{2}}\right),

(66)

while for the exterior,

\displaystyle R_{+}=\frac{2}{r_{v}^{2}}\left(r_{v}M_{,r_{v}r_{v}}+2M_{,r_{v}}\right).

(67)

Continuity of
R
yields

\displaystyle\left.M_{,r_{v}r_{v}}\right|_{\Sigma}=r_{0}\left(\ddot{a}+\frac{2\dot{a}^{2}}{a}\right).

(68)

The condition on the normal derivative (
19
) gives

\displaystyle\left.\Bigl(\partial_{v}R_{+}+(1-\dot{\mathcal{R}})\partial_{r_{v}}R_{+}\Bigr)\right|_{\Sigma}=0.

(69)

Using Eq. (
67
), this leads to

\displaystyle\left.\Bigl((r_{0}a)M_{,r_{v}r_{v}v}+2M_{,r_{v}v}\Bigr)\right|_{\Sigma}=-\,r_{0}\frac{1-r_{0}\dot{a}}{a}\Bigl(a^{2}M_{,r_{v}r_{v}r_{v}}

\displaystyle\left.-3a\ddot{a}\Bigr)\right|_{\Sigma}.

(70)

The above conditions constrain only derivatives of the mass function
M(v,r_{v})
along the hypersurface
\Sigma
. In particular, they determine
M
and a finite number of its derivatives on
\Sigma
, but do not provide information about its extension into the bulk spacetime. Consequently, by Proposition
1
, the additional
f(R)
junction conditions do not uniquely determine the exterior mass function.

We now analyze how much information about the exterior mass function
M(v,r_{v})
is fixed by the matching conditions on the hypersurface
\Sigma
. In particular, we determine which derivatives of
M
are fixed by the interior dynamics encoded in the scale factor
a(t)
.

Proposition 2
.

All partial derivatives of the mass function
M(v,r)
up to total order 3 are fixed on
\Sigma
by
a(t)
and its derivatives (up to
\left.a^{(4)}\right)

Proof.

We begin by recalling that the junction conditions determine the value of the mass function
as well as its radial derivative on
\Sigma
as given in Eqs. (
54
) and
55
.
To determine the derivative
M_{,v}
on
\Sigma
, we
use the chain rule to obtain

\displaystyle\frac{dM_{\Sigma}}{dt}=\left.M_{,v}\right|_{\Sigma}\dot{\mathcal{T}}+\left.M_{,r_{v}}\right|_{\Sigma}\dot{\mathcal{R}}.

(71)

Substituting the relations (
52
-
55
) into the above equation, and assuming
\dot{\mathcal{T}}\neq 0
,
shows that the resulting expression is identically satisfied if and only if

\displaystyle\left.M_{,v}\right|_{\Sigma}=0.

(72)

We now proceed to determine second-order derivatives. Differentiating
\left.M_{,r_{v}}\right|_{\Sigma}
with respect to
t
yields

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\left(M_{,r_{v}}\right)_{\Sigma}=\left.M_{,r_{v}v}\right|_{\Sigma}\dot{\mathcal{T}}+\left.M_{,r_{v}r_{v}}\right|_{\Sigma}\dot{\mathcal{R}}.

(73)

From the
f(R)
junction condition, we already have Eq. (
68
).
Substituting this and the expressions for
\dot{\mathcal{T}}
(
53
) and
\dot{\mathcal{R}}~\eqref{eq:defR}
, one can solve for
\left.M_{,r_{v}v}\right|_{\Sigma}
in terms of
a(t)
and its derivatives. Similarly, differentiating
\left.M_{,v}\right|_{\Sigma}=0
determines
\left.M_{,vv}\right|_{\Sigma}
. We obtain

\displaystyle\left.M_{,r_{v}v}\right|_{\Sigma}

\displaystyle=r_{0}^{2}\,a\,\Delta\,P,

(74)

\displaystyle\left.M_{,vv}\right|_{\Sigma}

\displaystyle=-r_{0}^{3}\,Q\,\Delta^{2}\,P,

(75)

where we introduced
\Delta=1-r_{0}\dot{a}=\dot{\mathcal{T}}^{-1}
,
Q=a\dot{a}
, and

\displaystyle P=\dddot{a}+\frac{\dot{a}\ddot{a}}{a}-2\frac{\dot{a}^{3}}{a^{2}}.

(76)

Proceeding iteratively, one can determine all mixed derivatives up to third order. In particular, by repeated differentiation and use of the junction conditions, one finds

\displaystyle\left.M_{,r_{v}r_{v}r_{v}}\right|_{\Sigma}=3\frac{\ddot{a}}{a}-\frac{3r_{0}}{\Delta}P,

(77)

\displaystyle\left.M_{,r_{v}r_{v}v}\right|_{\Sigma}=r_{0}\left(1+2r_{0}\dot{a}\right)P,

(78)

\displaystyle\left.M_{,r_{v}vv}\right|_{\Sigma}=r_{0}^{2}\Delta\left(\Delta a\dot{P}-3r_{0}\dot{a}^{2}P\right),

(79)

\displaystyle\left.M_{,vvv}\right|_{\Sigma}=-r_{0}^{3}\Delta^{2}\left[\Delta\left(2Q\dot{P}+P\dot{Q}\right)-r_{0}\dot{a}P\left(2a\ddot{a}+3\dot{a}^{2}\right)\right].

(80)

∎

Proposition 3
.

The matching data on
\Sigma
do not uniquely determine the local exterior solution.

Proof.

From the previous proposition, we know that all derivatives of
M(v,r_{v})
up to total order three are fixed on
\Sigma
. This corresponds to fixing the full third-order jet of
M
along the hypersurface. However, the exterior field equations in metric
f(R)
gravity are fourth-order in derivatives of the metric, and therefore involve derivatives of
M
up to fourth order. Importantly, not all fourth-order derivatives of
M
appear in the field equations when restricted to the hypersurface.

To see this, note that the Ricci scalar for the generalized Vaidya metric takes the form (
67
), and therefore depends only on radial derivatives of
M
. Consequently, any derivative of
R_{+}
contains at least one derivative with respect to
r_{v}
. The fourth-order terms in the field equations arise from derivatives of
R_{+}
and hence involve only combinations of derivatives of
M
that contain at least two radial derivatives. In particular, the only fourth-order derivatives of
M
that can appear are

\displaystyle M_{,r_{v}r_{v}r_{v}r_{v}},\quad M_{,r_{v}r_{v}r_{v}v},\quad M_{,r_{v}r_{v}vv}.

(81)

Crucially, derivatives such as

\displaystyle M_{,r_{v}vvv},\qquad M_{,vvvv}

(82)

do not appear in the field equations.

Therefore, even after imposing the field equations on
\Sigma
, there remain undetermined components of the fourth-order jet of
M
. Since these derivatives are not constrained, one can construct distinct functions
M
that agree up to third order on
\Sigma
but differ at fourth order and beyond. This implies that the local solution for
M(v,r_{v})
is not uniquely determined by the matching data and the field equations restricted to
\Sigma
.

∎

Proposition 4
.

In metric
f(R)
gravity with
f_{,RR}\neq 0
, a homogeneous FLRW interior with non-vanishing Ricci scalar
R_{-}(t)\neq 0
cannot be matched to a Ricci-flat exterior across a regular timelike hypersurface
\Sigma
.

Proof.

A Ricci-flat exterior satisfies
R_{+}=0
. The continuity condition
[R]_{\Sigma}=0
then implies
R_{-}(t)=0
on
\Sigma
. Since the FLRW interior is homogeneous,
R_{-}(t)
has no spatial dependence, and therefore the boundary condition forces
R_{-}(t)=0
everywhere. This contradicts the assumption
R_{-}(t)\neq 0
.
∎

Remark 2
.

This result is consistent with previous analyses in the literature (see, e.g.,
20
;
2
).

Remark 3
.

If both the interior and exterior satisfy
R_{+}=R_{-}=0
, then the additional junction conditions of metric
f(R)
gravity are automatically satisfied. In this case, the matching reduces to the standard Darmois–Israel conditions.

For the spatially flat FLRW interior, the condition
R_{-}(t)=0
implies

\displaystyle a(t)=a(t_{0})\Bigl(1+A(t-t_{0})\Bigr)^{1/2}.

(83)

The matching then determines the exterior mass function uniquely within the scalar-flat branch. Thus, the obstruction discussed above disappears in this special case.

Remark 4
.

In the generalized Vaidya class, the condition
R_{+}=0
constrains the mass function to the form

\displaystyle M(v,r)=m_{1}(v)+\frac{m_{2}(v)}{r}.

(84)

The Schwarzschild solution is recovered by taking
m_{2}(v)=0
and
m_{1}(v)=\mathrm{const}
. This illustrates that Ricci-flat generalized Vaidya spacetimes form a highly restricted subclass.

Proposition 5
.

For the generalized Vaidya metric (
32
) with the energy–momentum tensor given by Eqs. (
25
-
27
), the
(1,1)
component of the field equations in metric

gravity (
2

\displaystyle f_{,R}(R(v,r_{v}))=A(v)\,r_{v}+B(v),

(85)

where
A(v)
and
B(v)
are arbitrary functions of the null coordinate
v

Proof.

The field equations of metric
f(R)
gravity are given by Eq. (
2

We consider the
(1,1)
component of these equations in the coordinate system
(v,r_{v},\theta,\varphi)
adapted to the metric (
32
). For the generalized Vaidya metric, one has

\displaystyle g_{r_{v}r_{v}}=0,\qquad R_{r_{v}r_{v}}=0.

(86)

Moreover, for the matter content described by Eqs. (
25
-
27
), the
(1,1)
component of the energy–momentum tensor also vanishes,

\displaystyle T_{r_{v}r_{v}}=0.

(87)

Substituting these results into Eq. (

\displaystyle-\nabla_{r_{v}}\nabla_{r_{v}}f_{,R}=0.

Since

\displaystyle\partial_{r_{v}}^{2}f_{,R}(R(v,r_{v}))=0,

(89)

∎

Remark 5
.

The result shows that the scalar degree of freedom
f_{,R}
is restricted to be at most linear in the areal radius
r_{v}
along each null slice. This represents a strong constraint on the allowed scalar configurations in the exterior spacetime.

In particular, this restriction implies that the scalar field does not admit localized radial profiles in the generalized Vaidya background, a feature that will play a crucial role in the discussion of physically admissible solutions.

V.3
Starobinsky Model

We now consider the Starobinsky model,

\displaystyle f(R)=R+\alpha R^{2},

(90)

for which

\displaystyle f_{,R}=1+2\alpha R.

(91)

From Proposition
5
, we know that in the generalized Vaidya geometry the quantity
f_{,R}(R(v,r_{v}))
must take the form (
85

It follows that the Ricci scalar is given by

\displaystyle R(v,r_{v})=\frac{A(v)\,r_{v}+B(v)-1}{2\alpha},

and, using Eq. (
92

\displaystyle M(v,r_{v})=\frac{A(v)}{80\alpha}r_{v}^{4}+\frac{B(v)-1}{48\alpha}r_{v}^{3}+\frac{C(v)}{r_{v}}+D(v),

(93)

where
C(v)
and
D(v)
are arbitrary functions of

arising as integration functions.

Proposition 6
.

The matching conditions (
52

A(v)
,
B(v)
,
C(v)
, and
D(v)
.

Proof.

We first use the continuity of the Ricci scalar across
\Sigma

\displaystyle R_{+}(\mathcal{T}(t),\mathcal{R}(t))=R_{-}(t),

(94)

where
R_{-}(t)
is given in Eq. (
66
).
Substituting the expression for
R_{+}
from Eq. (
92

\displaystyle A(\mathcal{T})\,r_{0}a+B(\mathcal{T})-1=2\alpha R_{-}(t),

(95)

where
\mathcal{T}

Next, we impose continuity of the normal derivative of the Ricci scalar. Using the matching condition (

\displaystyle A(\mathcal{T})+\frac{r_{0}aA^{\prime}(\mathcal{T})+B^{\prime}(\mathcal{T})}{1-r_{0}\dot{a}}=0.

Differentiating Eq. (

\displaystyle A(\mathcal{T})=-\frac{2\alpha}{1-r_{0}\dot{a}}\dot{R}_{-}(t)\,,

\displaystyle B(\mathcal{T})=1+2\alpha R_{-}(t)-A(\mathcal{T})r_{0}a\,.

\displaystyle\frac{A(\mathcal{T})}{80\alpha}\,r_{0}^{4}a^{4}+\frac{B(\mathcal{T})-1}{48\alpha}\,r_{0}^{3}a^{3}+\frac{C(\mathcal{T})}{r_{0}a}+D(\mathcal{T})=\frac{r_{0}^{3}}{2}a\dot{a}^{2}\,,

Remark 6
.

The complete determination of the exterior solution in the
Starobinsky model does not contradict the Proposition

3
. In Proposition
3
, the mass function
M(v,r_{v})
was treated as a generic unknown of a fourth-order system. In that case, the matching conditions fix the boundary
3
-jet, while the restricted field equations do not involve the derivatives
M_{,r_{v}vvv}
,
M_{,vvvv}
so that the exterior is not uniquely determined.

By contrast, in the Proposition
5
one does not allow an arbitrary matter content. One has
T_{r_{v}r_{v}}=0
, and the
(1,1)
field equation implies Eq. (
92
). Thus the admissible exterior geometries are restricted to a much smaller class, and within this class the matching conditions are sufficient to determine the solution uniquely.

Remark 7
.

Although the solution is uniquely determined, it is generically not physically viable. Indeed, if
A(v)\neq 0
, the Ricci scalar grows linearly with
r_{v}
at large radius,

\displaystyle R(v,r_{v})\sim r_{v},

(101)

and the corresponding matter variables obtained from the field equations grow unboundedly.

\displaystyle\rho\sim\mathcal{O}(r_{v}),\qquad p\sim\mathcal{O}(r_{v}),\qquad\mu\sim\mathcal{O}(r_{v}^{2}).

(102)

This indicates that the exterior is not sourced by localized matter but instead corresponds to a divergent medium extending to infinity. Imposing the condition
A(v)=0
removes this divergence, but then Eq. (
97
) implies that
R_{-}(t)
must be constant, which is incompatible with a generic collapsing FLRW interior. Thus, while mathematically consistent solutions exist, they do not correspond to physically acceptable collapse scenarios.

V.4
Generic
f(R)
Models

We now extend the previous analysis beyond the specific case of the Starobinsky model to a general class of metric
f(R)
theories. In particular, we assume that
f_{,R}
is locally invertible along the branch determined by the matching hypersurface
\Sigma
. Denoting the inverse function by

\displaystyle R=\Phi(f_{,R}),\qquad\Phi=(f_{,R})^{-1},

(103)

we analyze the consequences of the matching conditions and field equations for the exterior solution.

Proposition 7
.

For generic
f(R)
models such that
f_{,R}
is locally invertible along
\Sigma
and
f_{,RR}(R_{\Sigma})\neq 0
, the matching conditions determine the exterior solution uniquely on every interval where
\mathcal{T}(t)
is locally invertible.

Proof.

From the
(1,1)
component of the field equations (
2
), we get Eq. (
85
).
Since
f_{,R}
is assumed to be locally invertible, we can express the Ricci scalar as

\displaystyle R(v,r_{v})=\Phi\bigl(A(v)\,r_{v}+B(v)\bigr).

(104)

On the other hand, for the generalized Vaidya metric, the Ricci scalar satisfies Eq. (
67
).
Substituting Eq. (
104
) into this relation, we obtain a second-order differential equation in
r_{v}
for the mass function
M(v,r_{v})
:

\displaystyle\frac{2}{r_{v}^{2}}\left(r_{v}M_{,r_{v}r_{v}}+2M_{,r_{v}}\right)=\Phi\bigl(A(v)\,r_{v}+B(v)\bigr).

(105)

For each fixed value of
v
, this is an ordinary differential equation in
r_{v}
. Integrating twice with respect to
r_{v}
, we obtain

\displaystyle M(v,r_{v})=M_{f}(v,r_{v};A,B)+\frac{C(v)}{r_{v}}+D(v),

(106)

where
M_{f}
is a particular solution determined by
A(v)
and
B(v)
.
C(v)
and
D(v)
are arbitrary functions of
v
arising as integration functions.

We now determine the functions
A(v)
and
B(v)
using the matching conditions on
\Sigma
.
The continuity of the Ricci scalar
and using Eq. (
104
), we obtain

\displaystyle\Phi\bigl(A(\mathcal{T})\,r_{0}a+B(\mathcal{T})\bigr)=R_{-}(t).

(107)

Applying
f_{,R}
to both sides yields

\displaystyle A(\mathcal{T})\,r_{0}a+B(\mathcal{T})=f_{,R}(R_{-}(t)).

(108)

Next, we impose the continuity of the normal derivative of the Ricci scalar (
18
).
Using the chain rule,

\displaystyle\nabla_{\mu}R_{+}=\Phi^{\prime}(f_{,R})\,\nabla_{\mu}f_{,R}=\frac{1}{f_{,RR}(R_{+})}\,\nabla_{\mu}f_{,R},

(109)

and since
f_{,RR}(R_{\Sigma})\neq 0
, this condition is equivalent to

\displaystyle\left.n^{\mu}\nabla_{\mu}f_{,R}\right|_{\Sigma}=0.

(110)

Using Eq. (
85

\displaystyle A(\mathcal{T})+\frac{r_{0}aA^{\prime}(\mathcal{T})+B^{\prime}(\mathcal{T})}{1-r_{0}\dot{a}}=0.

(111)

Eqs. (
108

A(\mathcal{T}(t))
and
B(\mathcal{T}(t))
uniquely in terms of
a(t)
and its derivatives. In particular, differentiating Eq. (
108

t
and using Eq. (
111
), one obtains

\displaystyle A(\mathcal{T})=-\frac{f_{,RR}(R_{-})}{1-r_{0}\dot{a}}\,\dot{R}_{-}(t),

(112)

and therefore

\displaystyle B(\mathcal{T})=f_{,R}(R_{-}(t))-A(\mathcal{T})\,r_{0}a.

(113)

Thus,
A(\mathcal{T}(t))
and
B(\mathcal{T}(t))
are uniquely determined on
\Sigma
. Since
\dot{\mathcal{T}}\neq 0
whenever

, the function
\mathcal{T}(t)
is locally invertible, and therefore
A(v)
and
B(v)
are uniquely determined as functions of

The functions
C(v)
and
D(v)
are determined by the matching conditions on the mass function and its radial derivative, Eqs. (
54
) and (
55
), which provide two linear equations for
C(\mathcal{T})
and

. These equations uniquely determine
C(\mathcal{T}(t))
and
D(\mathcal{T}(t))
.
Finally, since
\mathcal{T}(t)
is locally invertible, the functions
C(v)
and
D(v)
are uniquely determined as functions of
v
. The remaining field equations then determine only the fluid variables

\mu
,
\rho
and
p
.
∎

Remark 8
.

The uniqueness obtained here does not contradict the earlier non-uniqueness results. In the general analysis, the mass function
M(v,r_{v})
was treated as an arbitrary function subject only to the matching conditions and the structure of the field equations, leaving higher-order derivatives unconstrained. In contrast, the restriction (
85
) reduces the space of admissible solutions to a finite-dimensional functional family. Within this restricted class, the matching conditions are sufficient to determine the solution uniquely.

This result shows that, for a broad class of
f(R)
models, the apparent freedom of the generalized Vaidya exterior is completely eliminated once the energy-momentum tensor is described by Eqs. (
25
-
27
).

Corollary 1
.

Given (
85
)

\displaystyle f_{,R}(R(v,r_{v}))=A(v)\,r_{v}+B(v),

(114)

with
f_{,R}>0
, and assume that the solution extends to arbitrarily large
r
. Then either
A(v)=0
with
B(v)>0
, or
A(v)>0
. In the nontrivial case
A(v)\neq 0
, one has

\displaystyle f_{,R}(R(v,r))\to+\infty\qquad(r\to\infty).

(115)

Assuming that the inverse branch
\Phi=(f_{,R})^{-1}
can be continued along the solution for sufficiently large
f_{,R}
(i.e., on the range of
f_{,R}(R(v,r))
for large
r
), the asymptotic behavior of the Ricci scalar

\displaystyle R(v,r)=\Phi(f_{,R}(R(v,r)))

(116)

is determined by the limit
f_{,R}\to+\infty
, leading to the following possibilities:

1.

If
f_{,R}
is bounded from above along the corresponding inverse branch
\Phi
(equivalently, on the curvature range attained by the solution), then the relation

\displaystyle F(v,r)=f_{,R}(R(v,r))

(117)

cannot hold for arbitrarily large
r
, and therefore no global extension to arbitrarily large
r
exists.

2.

If
f_{,R}(R)\to+\infty
only when
R\to\infty
, then

\displaystyle R(v,r)\to\infty\quad\text{as}\quad r\to\infty.

(118)

3.

A finite asymptotic limit

\displaystyle R(v,r)\to R_{*},\qquad R_{*}<\infty,

(119)

is possible only if

\displaystyle f_{,R}(R)\to+\infty\quad\text{as}\quad R\to R_{*}.

(120)

In particular, for generic viable
f(R)
models in which
f_{,R}
remains finite at all finite curvature, the branch
A(v)\neq 0
cannot admit a global extension with finite asymptotic curvature.

Proof.

If the branch extends to arbitrarily large
r
and
f_{,R}>0
, then either
A(v)=0
(in which case
f_{,R}(v,r)\equiv B(v)>0
), or
A(v)\neq 0
. In the nontrivial case
A(v)\neq 0
, the positivity of
f_{,R}
for arbitrarily large
r
forces
A(v)>0
, since otherwise

\displaystyle f_{,R}(v,r)=A(v)\,r+B(v)\to-\infty

(121)

as
r\to\infty
, contradicting
f_{,R}>0
. Hence

\displaystyle f_{,R}(v,r)\sim A(v)\,r,\qquad f_{,R}(v,r)\to+\infty\quad\text{as}\quad r\to\infty.

(122)

If
f_{,R}
is bounded from above along the corresponding inverse branch
\Phi
(equivalently, on the curvature range attained by the solution), then
f_{,R}(R(v,r))
cannot match the unbounded quantity
f_{,R}(v,r)
for arbitrarily large
r
. Hence no global extension exists.

If
f_{,R}
becomes unbounded only in the limit
R\to\infty
, then the condition
f_{,R}(v,r)\to+\infty
implies

\displaystyle R(v,r)\to\infty\quad\text{as}\quad r\to\infty.

(123)

Finally, if one requires

\displaystyle R(v,r)\to R_{*},\quad R_{*}<\infty,

(124)

while

\displaystyle f_{,R}(v,r)\to+\infty,

(125)

then necessarily

\displaystyle f_{,R}(R)\to+\infty\quad\text{as}\qquad R\to R_{*}.

(126)

This proves the three alternatives. The final statement follows immediately: if
f_{,R}
stays finite at every finite curvature, then the third case is excluded, so for the branch with
A(v)\neq 0
, no global extension with finite asymptotic curvature is possible.
∎

Remark 9
.

The three alternatives in Corollary
1
can be illustrated by simple examples.

For the first case, one may take

\displaystyle f(R)=R+\frac{\mu}{R},\qquad\mu>0,

(127)

for which

\displaystyle f_{,R}(R)=1-\frac{\mu}{R^{2}}.

(128)

On the physical branch
f_{,R}>0

, one must have

\displaystyle R>\sqrt{\mu},

(129)

and therefore

\displaystyle 0<f_{,R}(R)<1.

(130)

Thus
f_{,R}
is bounded from above and cannot match a branch satisfying

\displaystyle f_{,R}(v,r)=A(v)\,r+B(v)\to+\infty\quad\text{as}\quad r\to\infty.

(131)

This realizes the first alternative.

For the second case, one may take the Starobinsky model which has already been discussed.

Finally for the third case, one may take

\displaystyle f(R)=-R_{*}\ln\!\left(1-\frac{R}{R_{*}}\right),\qquad R<R_{*},

(132)

with
R_{*}>0
. Then

\displaystyle f_{,R}(R)=\frac{1}{1-R/R_{*}},

(133)

so that
f_{,R}(R)\to+\infty
as
R\to R_{*}<\infty
. The inverse relation is

\displaystyle R=R_{*}\left(1-\frac{1}{f_{,R}}\right).

(134)

Therefore, if

\displaystyle f_{,R}(v,r)=A(v)\,r+B(v)\to+\infty,

(135)

one finds

\displaystyle R(v,r)\to R_{*}.

(136)

This realizes the third alternative.

These examples are meant only as simple prototypes of the three possible asymptotic behaviors. In particular, the third example is not viable in the usual sense precisely because
f_{,R}
diverges at finite curvature.

Remark 10
.

Another illustration of the third case in Corollary
1
is provided by the power-law model
3

\displaystyle f(R)=R^{1+\delta}.

(137)

In this case,

\displaystyle f_{,R}(R)=(1+\delta)R^{\delta}.

(138)

Hence, on the branch singled out by Proposition
5
,

\displaystyle(1+\delta)R^{\delta}=A(v)\,r+B(v).

(139)

In the nontrivial case
A(v)\neq 0
, one obtains asymptotically

\displaystyle R(v,r)\sim\left(\frac{A(v)}{1+\delta}\right)^{1/\delta}r^{1/\delta}.

(140)

If one restricts to

\displaystyle-1<\delta<0,

(141)

then

\displaystyle R(v,r)\to 0\qquad(r\to\infty),

(142)

while

\displaystyle f_{,R}(R)=(1+\delta)R^{\delta}\to+\infty\qquad\text{as}\qquad R\to 0^{+}.

(143)

Therefore this model realizes precisely the third case of Corollary
1
, with the finite asymptotic value

\displaystyle R_{*}=0.

(144)

The corresponding mass does not have a single universal large-
r
behavior throughout the whole interval
-1<\delta<0
. Using Eq. (
67
) one finds that, for generic
\delta\neq-1/4,-1/3
, the mass contains the particular term

\displaystyle M_{\text{part}}(v,r)\propto r^{3+1/\delta},

(145)

but this term is asymptotically dominant only when

\displaystyle-1<\delta<-\frac{1}{3}.

(146)

For

\displaystyle\delta=-\frac{1}{3},

(147)

the behavior becomes logarithmic,

\displaystyle M(v,r)\sim\ln r,

(148)

while for

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{3}<\delta<0

(149)

the particular term decays and the mass approaches a finite limit, up to subleading corrections. Thus the relevant asymptotic feature is not a universal divergence of
M
, but rather the fact that the curvature tends to the finite value
R_{*}=0
while
f_{,R}
diverges there.

However, this branch is physically excluded. Indeed, for

\displaystyle-1<\delta<0,

(150)

one has

\displaystyle f_{,RR}(R)=\delta(1+\delta)R^{\delta-1}<0,

(151)

so the Dolgov–Kawasaki stability condition (
11
) is violated. Moreover, Clifton and Barrow
3
found that observational constraints confine the allowed values to a tiny neighborhood of general relativity, namely

\displaystyle 0\leq\delta<7.2\times 10^{-19},

(152)

thereby excluding the negative-
\delta
branch altogether. Hence, although the model provides a concrete example of case 3, it is not physically viable.

The corollary shows that the asymptotic behavior of the exterior solution is entirely controlled by the large-
f_{,R}
behavior of the inverse function
\Phi=(f_{,R})^{-1}
. In particular, for viable
f(R)
models in which
f_{,R}
remains finite at all finite curvature, the branch
A(v)\neq 0
necessarily leads either to divergent curvature or to the absence of a global exterior solution.

This result has a direct physical interpretation. Since
f_{,R}
represents the scalar degree of freedom in metric
f(R)
gravity, the relation (
85
) forces the scalaron to grow without bound in the nontrivial branch
A(v)\neq 0
. This, in turn, leads to an unbounded growth of curvature and matter variables at large radius, indicating that the exterior is not sourced by a localized distribution but rather by a divergent medium extending to infinity. Consequently, this branch cannot describe a physically acceptable exterior spacetime.

Remark 11
.

To obtain a physically acceptable exterior, one is naturally led to consider the branch
A(\mathcal{T})=0
. Assuming
f_{,RR}(R_{-}(t))\neq 0
, Eq. (
112
) implies that the interior Ricci scalar is constant,
R_{-}(t)=R_{0}
. This excludes dust collapse, since Eq. (
4
) then requires
\rho=\text{constant}
, implying a static configuration with
\dot{a}=0
.

However, this does not rule out collapse altogether. Instead, it restricts the interior to a constant-curvature branch. In this case,
f_{,R}(R_{0})
is constant, the derivative terms in the field equations vanish, and the dynamics reduce to

\displaystyle f_{,R}(R_{0})\,G^{-}_{\mu\nu}+\frac{1}{2}\bigl(f_{,R}(R_{0})R_{0}-f(R_{0})\bigr)g^{-}_{\mu\nu}=\kappa\,T^{-}_{\mu\nu},

(153)

i.e., Einstein equations with an effective cosmological constant (for
f_{,R}(R_{0})\neq 0
).

The trace Eq. (
4
) gives

\displaystyle\kappa\,T^{-}=f_{,R}(R_{0})R_{0}-2f(R_{0}),

(154)

so the trace of the interior energy–momentum tensor is constant. Under FLRW symmetry, this implies

\displaystyle T^{-}=-\rho+3p=T_{0},

(155)

leading to the equation of state

\displaystyle p=\frac{\rho+T_{0}}{3}.

(156)

Using energy–momentum conservation,

\displaystyle\dot{\rho}+3H(\rho+p)=0\,,

(157)

it follows that

\displaystyle\dot{\rho}+H(4\rho+T_{0})=0,

(158)

and therefore

\displaystyle\rho(a)=\frac{C}{a^{4}}-\frac{T_{0}}{4},\quad p(a)=\frac{C}{3a^{4}}+\frac{T_{0}}{4},

(159)

for some constant
C
. Thus, the admissible matter content consists of a radiation-like component plus an effective vacuum contribution.

Therefore, the condition
A(\mathcal{T})=0
does not forbid collapse in general; it restricts the interior matter sector to constant-trace sources.

The above analysis reveals a strong structural constraint on the scalar degree of freedom in metric
f(R)
gravity when the exterior geometry is restricted to the generalized Vaidya class. In particular, the relation (
85
) implies that the scalaron is at most linear in the areal radius along each null slice. As a result, the scalar field cannot develop a localized radial profile and is instead forced into a rigid configuration determined entirely by
A(v)
and
B(v)
.

This has direct physical consequences. In a generic scalar–tensor description, the scalaron behaves as a propagating degree of freedom governed by a second-order differential equation. By contrast, the generalized Vaidya ansatz suppresses this dynamics and admits only two qualitatively distinct behaviors:

•

If
A(v)\neq 0
, the scalaron grows linearly with radius, leading to divergent curvature and matter variables at large distances. The exterior is then sourced by a non-local, divergent medium rather than a localized distribution.

•

If
A(v)=0
, the scalaron becomes spatially constant, freezing its dynamics. The matching conditions then force the interior Ricci scalar to be constant, restricting the interior to a constant-curvature sector and excluding generic collapsing solutions.

In both cases, the scalar degree of freedom fails to behave as a physically acceptable dynamical field in the exterior region. This reflects a deeper incompatibility between the generalized Vaidya ansatz and the propagation of the scalaron in
f(R)
gravity.

We therefore conclude that the obstruction to OS-type collapse in metric
f(R)
gravity is not merely a consequence of the junction conditions, but arises from the restricted dynamical structure of the scalar degree of freedom in null-radiating geometries. This suggests that physically viable collapse scenarios, if they exist, require either more general exterior geometries or alternative matter configurations.

VI
Conclusion

In this work, we have revisited the OS collapse problem in metric
f(R)
gravity, taking into account the additional junction conditions implied by the fourth-order nature of the field equations. In contrast to GR, where matching requires only the continuity of the induced metric and extrinsic curvature, metric
f(R)
gravity further imposes the continuity of the Ricci scalar and of its normal derivative. These additional conditions severely constrain admissible collapse scenarios. In particular, we have shown that a homogeneous FLRW interior cannot, in general, be matched to a Ricci-flat exterior whenever
f_{,RR}\neq 0
, thereby obstructing the standard OS construction.

Allowing for a generalized Vaidya exterior enlarges the space of admissible configurations. In the absence of restrictions on the exterior matter sector, the matching conditions determine only the boundary data of the mass function, leaving its extension into the bulk undetermined. This leads to a genuine non-uniqueness of exterior solutions and shows that, at a formal level, the obstruction to homogeneous collapse can be relaxed by considering more general exteriors.

However, this degeneracy is removed once the exterior matter content is restricted to the generalized Vaidya form. In that case, the field equations impose a strong structural constraint, Eq. (
85
), which drastically reduces the functional freedom of the exterior solution. For the Starobinsky model, this condition is sufficient to determine the exterior mass function uniquely in terms of the interior scale factor. More generally, for locally invertible
f_{,R}
with
f_{,RR}\neq 0
, the matching data uniquely fix the exterior solution on intervals where the boundary map is locally invertible.

Despite this apparent mathematical resolution, the resulting solutions are not physically viable in generic
f(R)
models. The branch with
A(v)\neq 0
leads to an unbounded growth of the scalar degree of freedom, resulting in divergent curvature and non-localized matter distributions at large radius. On the other hand, the branch
A(v)=0
suppresses the radial dynamics of the scalaron and forces the interior onto a constant-curvature sector, thereby excluding nontrivial dust collapse and restricting the admissible matter content to constant-trace sources.

These results indicate that the obstruction to OS-type collapse in metric
f(R)
gravity is not merely a consequence of over-constrained junction conditions, but reflects a deeper incompatibility between homogeneous collapse and the allowed configurations of the scalar degree of freedom in null-radiating geometries. In particular, the generalized Vaidya ansatz constrains the scalaron in a way that prevents it from supporting a physically acceptable exterior configuration.

We therefore conclude that, within the restricted generalized Vaidya matter sector considered here, the OS dust collapse problem in metric
f(R)
gravity does not admit a physically viable resolution. This suggests that any consistent description of gravitational collapse in these theories may require a more general class of exterior geometries, in which the scalar degree of freedom is treated dynamically rather than being constrained by an effective fluid description.
