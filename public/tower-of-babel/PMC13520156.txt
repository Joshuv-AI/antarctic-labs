Season and soil properties structure bacterial communities in hyperacid (pH ≤ 2) fumarolic soils of the Tatun Volcanic Group
Authors: Jia-Ho Shiu, Zi-Ting Lu, Ying-Ping Song, Meng-Che Hsieh, Min-Chun Liao, Yo-Chia Chen, Yu-Ting Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundVolcanic fumaroles are extreme habitats with high temperatures, sulfur‑rich gases, and low pH, creating challenging niches for life. Soil microbiota in these environments remain underexplored, particularly at near‑vent sites with pH below 2. We characterized bacterial communities in hyperacid soils of Taiwan’s Tatun Volcanic Group to examine how season and soil properties shape these communities. We sampled 39 soils and 8 hot spring waters from four fumaroles during dry and wet seasons. Soil pH, nutrients (nitrate, ammonium), organic matter, and altitude were recorded, and bacterial diversity was profiled via 16S rRNA gene sequencing.ResultsPseudomonadota overwhelmingly dominated the communities, followed by Bacillota and Actinomycetota. Wet-season soils displayed more homogeneous assemblages, whereas dry-season soils exhibited greater heterogeneity. Soil taxonomy and texture also modulated patterns, with Andisols supporting more consistent communities than Inceptisols. Environmental variables such as nitrate, ammonium, organic matter, and altitude correlated with community composition. Heatmap and network analyses highlighted Delftia and Pseudomonas as key taxa.ConclusionsExtreme acidity and seasonal hydrology thus select for specialized bacterial assemblages in these fumarolic soils. These findings advance understanding of microbial adaptation to geochemical extremes and reveal how seasonal and edaphic factors structure communities, offering implications for biogeochemical cycling and astrobiology.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40793-026-00916-5.

Introduction

Understanding life in extreme environments, such as volcanic environments, is important for investigating the evolutionary processes that have led to the evolution of life on Earth [1, 2]. Since volcanic systems are widespread on Earth (≈1350 potentially active subaerial volcanoes; mid-ocean ridges span ~ 65,000 km), it is crucial to comprehend the characteristics of microbes that contribute to fundamental nutrient cycles (C, S, N, Fe/Mn and H2) in these environments, as it enhances our understanding of how geophysical and geochemical factors influence microbial diversity and physiology [3–6].

Soil bacteria are central to Earth’s biogeochemical cycles: they decompose organic matter, recycle nutrients, and contribute to soil formation [7–9]. In volcanic environments, soil bacterial communities are influenced by extreme environmental factors such as high temperature, acidity, and redox substrates [4, 10]. Large-scale surveys show pH is the primary diversity driver below ~ 70 °C, with additional constraints in highly acidic fumarolic soils [11]. Globally, microbial life has been documented from deep-sea vents and mud volcanoes to geothermal soils (e.g., methane-cycling consortia at Haakon-Mosby; novel taxa in Taupō geothermal soils) [3, 12, 13]. Mud-volcano systems have already been investigated for microbial ecology, with studies resolving methane-cycling communities in expelled fluids and mud and demonstrating spatiotemporal shifts in resident bacterial assemblages [14–17]. In highly acidic geothermal ecosystems, aerobic methane oxidation is frequently mediated by acidophilic verrucomicrobial methanotrophs, including the genera Methylacidiphilum and Methylacidimicrobium [18, 19]. By contrast, the Tatun Volcanic Group (TVG) in Yangmingshan National Park is a potentially active hydrothermal system characterized by abundant fumaroles and acid-sulfate springs, yet its fumarolic soils remain underexplored microbiologically [20, 21].

National Park surveys show that these fumarolic soils are extremely acidic, often ≤ 2 across bare substrates, and near-vent soils commonly exceed ~ 70 °C in immediate vent fields, providing a distinct edaphic filter for microbial life [22–24]. Prior microbiological work in the TVG has focused mainly on hydrothermal waters, where community surveys and limited cultivation of thermophiles revealing archaeal-dominated, low-diversity assemblages in boiling acid-sulfate pools (e.g., Sulfolobus, Acidianus, Vulcanisaeta, Caldisphaera) and Hydrogenobaculum-rich waters, contrasted with more diverse outflow and biofilm communities enriched in sulfur/iron oxidizers and photosynthetic taxa [25–27]. Recent studies in the Tatun area show that volcanic soils host niche-structured bacterial assemblages shaped by heavy-metal loads and land use (pH 3.8–4.9) [28], while fumarolic-field work at Dayoukeng revealed compositional and functional shifts along rock–soil–plant continua and with distance from vents (pH not reported) [24]. Despite recent works in the TVG, the most chemically extreme, near-vent fumarolic soils (pH ≤ 2; > 70 °C) remain insufficiently resolved in seasonal and pedological contexts, leaving environment–community linkages unknown.

Based on these considerations, we hypothesize that fumarole soils in the Tatun Volcanic Group harbor distinct structured bacterial communities shaped by both abiotic stressors and edaphic properties. To test this hypothesis, we: (1) characterized the physicochemical properties of fumarole soils and associated hot spring waters across dry and wet seasons; (2) investigated the diversity, taxonomic composition, and co-occurrence patterns of bacterial communities; (3) evaluated the influence of environmental factors, soil taxonomy, and soil texture on community structure; and (4) identified potential indicator taxa and inferred their ecological functions in this extreme volcanic ecosystem.

Materials and methods

Geographic description of study site and sampling procedures

All field activities and sample collections were conducted under research permits issued by Yangmingshan National Park, in accordance with park regulations and applicable laws. Four fumaroles in Tatun Volcanic Group, Taipei, Taiwan were selected for sampling (Fig. 1), which is Longfenggu (LFG: 25° 08′ N, 121° 31′ E, 230 m above sea level), Sishuiping (SHP: 25° 11′ N, 121° 36′ E, elevation 300–350 m a.s.l.), Dayoukeng (DYK: 25° 10′ N, 121° 34′ E, elevation 600 m a.s.l.), and Xiaoyoukeng (XYK: 25° 10′N, 121° 32′ E, elevation 800 m a.s.l.). Soil samples were collected from five different sites at each fumarole location. Five soil samples and one water sample were collected at each sampling location (Table 1) in both the dry (April 27 to 28, 2018) and wet seasons (September 7 to 8, 2018). The lowest and highest precipitation occurred in April and September of 2018, respectively (Fig. 1B). Soil samples were collected in a sealed plastic bag (~ 5kg) for physicochemical analyses and in a 50 mL tube for the bacterial community. The soil samples in a 50 mL tube were immediately put in liquid nitrogen for transportation and stored at − 20 °C before DNA extraction. The soil samples in a sealed plastic bag were air-dried, sieved (pore size = 2 mm), and stored in polyethylene bags at room temperature for physicochemical property analyses.

Approximately 15 L of water was collected from the hot spring water of each fumarole and stored in sterile containers for transportation. An additional 1 L sample was used for physicochemical analyses, and 15 L samples were filtered through an 11 μm pore size membrane (Whatman No.1), then centrifuged at 12,000 xg for 40min at 4 °C. The pellet after centrifugation was stored at − 20 °C for DNA extraction.

Physical and chemical analyses

The temperature of the hot spring water was measured directly in the field. The water samples were analyzed for pH, nitrate, nitrite, ammonium, and total nitrogen concentrations by pH meter and S12 photometer (PhotoLab®, WTW, US), respectively after being filtered through 0.45 μm membranes (Merck Millipore®, Germany) in the laboratory. The concentration of sulfates in the water samples was analyzed by U.S. EPA Method 375.4—Sulfate [29].

The soil samples were analyzed for physical (soil moisture, soil temperature, soil texture) and chemical (pH, total nitrogen, organic matter, inorganic nitrogen, sulfur) properties. The temperature of the soil samples was measured directly in the field. The soil texture was determined by the soil particle-size analysis and the soil textural triangle [30]. USDA soil taxa for Yangmingshan samples were assigned following the park-wide soil survey of Yangmingshan National Park using the soil-order map [31]. For chemical parameters, the soil was analyzed for total nitrogen using Semimacro Kjeldahl method [32], organic matter using Walkley–Black procedure [33], and sulfur concentrations using a CHNS-Elemental Analyzer (Elementar Analysensysteme GmbH, Hanau, Germany).

DNA extraction, bacterial amplicon libraries, and DNA sequencing

Total DNA was extracted from 3 g of the soil sample and 15L of hot spring water samples using the Quick-DNA™ Fecal/Soil Microbe Miniprep Kit (Zymo Research, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. To generate the bacterial community composition, the V6-V8 hypervariable regions of the 16S rRNA gene were amplified using the bacterial universal primer pair 968F (5′-AACGCGAAGAACCTTAC-3′) and 1391R (5′-GACGGGCGGTGTGTRCA-3′) [34, 35]. The PCR reaction was carried out in a total volume of 50 μL, containing 1X Taq Master Mix (Biauan), 1 mg/mL BSA, 0.2μM of each primer, and 2–5 μg of DNA (final concentration 100 ng). The PCR program included an initial denaturation at 95°C for 5min, followed by 30 cycles of 95°C for 30 s, 50°C for 20 s, and 72°C for 45 s, with the final step of 72°C for 10min. Thereafter, PCR products (~ 424 bp) were purified by gel extraction using a GeneKlean Gel Recovery Kit (MDBio).

Each bacterial V6-V8 amplicon was tagged with a unique barcode sequence by designing tag primers with 8-base overhangs at the 5′ end of the universal primer pair (968F/1391R). The DNA-tagging PCR contained 1X Taq Master Mix (Biauan), 0.4μM of each tagged primer, and 100 ng V6-V8 amplicon in a final volume of 50 μL, and the PCR program had an initial denaturation at 94°C for 3min, followed by 20 cycles of 94°C for 20 s, 52°C for 10 s, and 72°C for 30 s, with the final step of 72°C for 2min. The Purified products were pooled and sequenced with Illumina MiSeq 2000 paired-end (2 × 300 bp) sequencing system at Genomics® (New Taipei City, Taiwan). All soil physicochemical variables were log(x + 1) transformed prior to ANOVA to reduce right-skewness and stabilize variances commonly observed in concentration data, an offset of 1 was applied to accommodate zero or near-zero values. Transformed variables were then evaluated using analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey’s post hoc HSD test for multiple comparisons.

Negative and process controls

A DNA extraction negative control (“extraction blank”) was included by processing nuclease-free water through the entire extraction protocol alongside all samples. Extraction blanks were below the quantification limit of the Qubit dsDNA HS assay and yielded no visible DNA on agarose gels. Extraction blanks were also carried through PCR together with study samples and produced no detectable 16S rRNA gene amplicons; therefore, negative controls were not subjected to library preparation or sequencing. In addition, PCR no-template controls (NTCs) containing nuclease-free water instead of DNA template were included in each amplification run and consistently showed no amplification. Representative gel images of negative controls are provided in Supplementary Fig. S1A and B.

To assess potential workflow-associated contamination, two independent environmental samples collected outside the fumarolic sites (LSK1–2 and ZIH1–2) were included as process controls and processed in parallel with the study samples using the same extraction, amplification, library preparation, and sequencing workflow (Supplementary Fig. S2).

Data processing

Paired-end reads were firstly merged using USEARCH (v8.0.1623) with default settings and then subjected to MOTHUR (v1.35.1) for quality control to retain those (1) of a length between 400–450 base pairs (bp), (2) with homopolymer no longer than 8 bp, (3) without any ambiguous base, and (4) of average quality score greater than 20. Chimeric 16S rRNA gene sequences were detected and removed using UCHIME (implemented in USEARCH) with reference mode and 3% minimum divergence. Quality-filtered and non-chimeric reads were further analyzed using the UPARSE pipeline [36] to generate Operational Taxonomic Units (OTUs) at a 97% identity level per sample. The OTU representative sequences were searched against the Greengenes 13_5 database using USEARCH global alignment to identify the best hits’ corresponding taxonomy. OTUs with no hit or a weak hit (i.e., a hit of an averaged % sequence identity and alignment coverage < 93%) were removed from downstream analysis.

Data analysis

Alpha-diversity of bacterial communities was calculated after rarefying to an even 5000 sequence depth in each sample by USEARCH (v10; options: -otutab_norm). The Shannon (H′), Simpson(λ), and Pielou’s evenness indices were calculated, where J′ was defined as Shannon entropy (H′) divided by the natural logarithm of OTU richness (S), J′ = H′/ln(S) [37]. Kruskal–Wallis tests with Dunn’s post hoc test were conducted to detect significant differences in the indices among different groups. Comparisons were made between two seasons, among soil textures, USDA soil taxonomy, and across four different locations. Statistical analyses were performed using R ver. 3.6.2 [38].

We analyzed the community structure with R software. Count data were transformed into relative abundance by dividing by sample size for normalizing bacterial community composition. To assess beta-diversity, the Bray–Curtis distance was estimated between samples [39]. Nonmetric Multidimensional Scaling (NMDS) based on Bray–Curtis distances was performed using the Vegan and ggplot2 packages to visualize the distribution of the bacterial community composition, followed by post hoc regression of individual explanatory variables on the ordination scores.

Agglomerative hierarchical clustering (CLUSTER) with complete-linkage was performed on the separate wet- and dry-season data sets using the Primer 6 software (PRIMER-E, Lutton, Ivybridge, UK). To assess significant variations between soil and water samples, as well as between dry and wet samples, we employed Analysis of Similarity (ANOSIM) in the Primer 6 software. Co-occurrence networks were inferred using SparCC for each site (XYK, DYK, SHP, LFG) by pooling dry- and wet-season soil samples (9–10 samples per site). Taxa were filtered to retain those present in ≥ 20% of samples and the top 100 taxa ranked by mean abundance were used for network inference after adding a pseudocount (1) and applying compositional closure to relative abundance. SparCC correlations were estimated with SpiecEasi::sparcc (iter = 20, inner_iter = 10, th = 0.1) with bootstrap resampling (500 replicates). For visualization, the top 50 associations ranked by absolute correlation (|ρ|) were retained to build an undirected graph; isolated nodes were removed and the largest connected component was plotted. Edge color denotes correlation sign, edge width scales with |ρ|, and node size represents log10 mean relative abundance.

In the wet season, the chemical characteristics of soil samples from SHP also differed significantly from the other locations. Similar to the dry season, SHP had a notably higher nitrate concentration. Additionally, SHP had the highest average total nitrogen (Ntot) content (0.05%), compared to the other locations (Ntot = 0.02 to 0.03%). SHP and LFG also showed significantly higher ammonia (NH4+) concentrations (1.46 and 1.56 ppm, respectively) than DYK and XYK (0.86 and 0.82 ppm, respectively). Furthermore, SHP had a significantly higher organic matter content (6.38 ± 0.75%) than the other locations (2.7–3.61%; Table 2).

Between the dry and wet seasons, several physicochemical characteristics of soil samples showed significant variation, including pH, total nitrogen, organic matter, and ammonia. However, soil moisture did not vary significantly between the two seasons (Table 2). Notably, pH values shifted in three of the four locations, with LFG becoming more acidic to pH = 1.07, while DYK and SHP showed increased pH during the wet season. Total nitrogen decreased slightly in DYK and XYK but increased in SHP during the wet season, while the concentration of ammonia had significantly increased only in LFG. Organic matter content decreased across all four locations, with significant variations observed in LFG and XYK. The organic matter in all 4 locations decreased in the wet season, but only LFG and XYK had significant variations.

Regarding soil physical properties (Table 3), except for the first sampling site in DYK and the third sampling site in SHP (which were classified as sandy), most of the soil textures were identified as loamy sand or sandy loam. Discussion

Soil bacterial communities under pH < 2 at active fumaroles

This is the first study to reveal the bacterial communities in volcanic soil with a pH value lower than 2 using a culture-independent method. Soil pH is the main abiotic element in shaping the alpha diversity [40, 41], composition, and distribution of bacterial communities [42]. The soil pH was even considered a reliable factor in predicting the abundance and functional genes in soil [43]. While previous research has identified soil pH as the primary driver of microbiome assembly in volcanic soils [44, 45], little is known about bacterial communities in soils with pH values below 2 [42, 46]. Our study fills this gap by documenting the unique microbial composition under extreme conditions.

The soil bacterial communities in our result differed from those of fumarole soil from previous research. In previous studies, the pH values were around 4, and the most dominant bacterial phyla were usually Actinomycetota (formerly Actinobacteria) [47] or Acidobacteriota (formerly Acidobacteria) [48]. However, the most abundant bacteria in this study were Pseudomonadota (formerly Proteobacteria; mainly the classes Gammaproteobacteria, Betaproteobacteria, and Alphaproteobacteria), followed by Bacillota (formerly Firmicutes; class Bacilli) and Actinomycetota (Fig. 3B). The difference might be due to co-occurrence of lower pH value (pH1-2) and high temperature (57–99.3 °C) near fumaroles and therefore creating a specific microenvironment. The co-occurrence of extreme abiotic stressors, including low pH, elevated temperatures, and sulfur-rich conditions, underscores the role of these factors in shaping unique bacterial assemblages. These findings align with the hypothesis that extreme environments drive the selection and adaptation of specialized microbial taxa.

The fumaroles in Yangmingshan National Park, part of the Tatun Volcano Group, are characterized by solfataric activity, emitting gases such as hydrogen sulfide (H2S) and sulfur dioxide (SO2). Upon cooling and condensation, these gases precipitate elemental sulfur around vents, generating acid-sulfate niches enriched in sulfuric acid and reduced sulfur species [20, 23]. The dominance of sulfide-oxidizing bacteria, particularly within Pseudomonadota, suggests adaptation to chemolithotrophic use of reduced sulfur compound as energy sources [49–51]. Concordantly, hot-spring waters from the Tatun Volcano Group with pH as low as 1.8 also harbor Pseudomonadota-rich communities [24].

Similarly, in Yellowstone National Park, fumarolic/geothermal soils can be both hot and acidic as well, near-surface temperatures reach ~ 70 °C, and pH can drop to ~ 2.7 at Amphitheater Springs. Yet community-level surveys of bacterial assemblages in such extremely low-pH soils remain scarce [52, 53]. Where soil bacterial communities have been profiled, they mostly concern geothermally heated soils across thermal gradients with acidic-to-neutral pH (≈3.9–7.5), showing marked shifts in community structure after heating events [54]. Within this context, the dominance of sulfide-oxidizing Alpha- and Gammaproteobacteria aligns with the broad distribution of sulfur oxidation pathways that enable chemolithotrophic use of reduced sulfur under acidic conditions [49]. This mirrors patterns documented in Yellowstone’s acid-sulfate systems, where low pH and sulfur-rich geochemistry tightly couple with enrichment of sulfur-oxidation genes and sulfur-oxidizing members of Pseudomonadota, and where hot, acidic soils support abundant sulfur oxidizers capable of in situ sulfuric acid generation [4, 51, 55].

Besides, the higher abundance of Bacilli (Bacillota) in wet season soil samples and dry season spring samples (Fig. 3B) likely reflects their spore-forming capacity, which confers resilience to temperature and hydration fluctuations typical of fumarolic fields [56]. Interestingly, heavy metals, such as Cr, Pb, Zn, and Cu, previously reported to structure microbial communities in soils with pH 3.76–4.92 [28], appear to play a lesser role in our system. The absence of Koribacteraceae (Acidobacteriota), which is commonly reported in comparable acidic soils, indicates extreme acidity and high temperatures, not heavy metals, primarily filter community assembly in these fumarolic soils.

Season primarily affected the alpha and beta diversity of the bacterial communities

Changes in precipitation can have cascading effects by directly changing water availability and altering soil physiochemical properties that mediate microbial composition, responses, and function. In this study, although the precipitation in the wet season was 800 mm higher than in the dry season, the soil moisture was not significantly different because of the high evaporation rate in high temperatures (52.3–99 °C in Table 3) and the humid climate in the subtropical areas. However, significant variations between the 2 seasons were shown in pH value and the sulfate concentration in water samples (Table S1); a higher sulfate concentration decreased the water samples’ pH value. The dissolved solid sulfur from soil to hot spring water could be observed from the decrease of S (%) in the soil during the wet season, though there was no significant variation (Table 2). Similar phenomena may lead to decreased organic matter in the soil (SOM) during the wet season (Table 2).

The relationship between precipitation and soil organic matter is complex. High precipitation levels potentially lead to SOM accumulation due to oxygen limitation and reduced decomposition rates. However, higher precipitation can also accelerate leaching and loss of mineral phases that could otherwise stabilize SOM [57–59]. Another study stated that soils from higher rainfall areas with lower iron content exhibited lower stable soil organic carbon [60]. Our results showed consistent with this pattern (Table 2).

Moreover, the decrease of organic matter in the soil significantly affected bacterial communities (Fig. 6A and B). The alpha diversity of bacterial communities significantly varied between seasons (Table 4), with higher Evenness and lower Simpson indices in the wet season (Fig. 2), indicating that higher precipitation in the wet season may homogenize the bacterial communities in the soil. The β-diversity results likewise demonstrate higher between-sample similarity in the wet season (Figs. 3A and 4), supporting this homogenization effect.

Previous studies have demonstrated that variations in precipitation significantly influence the structure and function of soil microbial communities [61]. Under arid conditions, increased precipitation generally enhances microbial diversity and abundance [62]. However, other studies have reported that alpha diversity negatively correlates with precipitation, such as in maize soils [63] or high-altitude wetlands [60]. Our findings align with this latter pattern. The possible reason is that the higher dissolved organic carbon (DOC) levels during the wet season provide an increased carbon source and nutrients for microbes, favoring dominant bacterial groups and promoting their abundance, or enabling dormant microorganisms to become active. These results underscore precipitation as a key driver of soil bacterial communities, with its effects varying depending on local environmental and climatic conditions.

The effect of soil taxa on the bacterial communities in different seasons

After the bacterial communities were grouped by different seasons, they were secondarily affected by different sample types, i.e., water or soil samples, and soil taxa in both seasons (Table 5). Although there was no significant variation in the alpha diversities of bacterial communities (Table 4) between Inceptisols and Andisols soil samples, the beta diversities were significantly varied between soil taxa (Fig. 5 and Table 5), especially in the wet season (Fig. 5B).

In general, Andisol samples exhibited relatively consistent bacterial community compositions, whereas Inceptisol samples displayed more significant variation among groups (Fig. 5). This difference is likely attributable to the distinct soil properties of these taxa. Andisols, formed from volcanic materials such as those in the Tatun Volcanic Group, are rich in pyrophosphate-extractable aluminum (Alp) and iron (Fep), which contribute to the formation of organo-mineral complexes that stabilize soil organic matter (SOM) [21, 64]. These stabilization mechanisms reduce the bioavailability of organic substrates and create a more chemically stable environment, supporting consistent bacterial communities [26]. In contrast, Inceptisol, with its simpler mineralogy and lower Alp and Fep concentrations, may provide a less chemically stable environment, leading to more significant variability in bacterial composition [65].

In the wet season, SOM, Nitrate, ammonium, and altitude significantly affected the bacterial communities (Fig. 6B). Notably, Inceptisols (SHP and LFG) exhibited significantly higher ammonium levels than Andisols (DYK and XYK), indicating more active microbial decomposition of dissolved organic matter [66]. This process likely led to increased nitrogen mineralization, releasing ammonium as a key inorganic nitrogen source [67]. This observation aligns with previous studies showing that soil type is crucial in regulating microbial activity and nutrient cycling [24].

The effect of soil texture on the bacterial communities in the dry seasons

In the dry season, soil texture was a key driver of bacterial α‐diversity, as the sand samples (e.g., DYK1 and SHP3) had the highest Shannon and evenness scores. Texture also shaped β‐diversity: sand communities were distinct from those in sandy loam and loamy sand. These patterns disappeared in the wet season, likely because rainfall equalizes soil conditions and reduces textural differences.

The elevated microbial diversity in sandy soils under dry conditions can be explained by their unique physicochemical properties [68–70]. Sandy soils have higher porosity and better aeration, which facilitates oxygen diffusion and thereby supports a broader range of aerobic bacterial taxa [71]. In addition, the low cation exchange capacity of sand leads to poor nutrient retention, creating oligotrophic niches that favor bacteria adapted to nutrient scarcity [72]. The rapid drainage and frequent disturbances in coarse sand further prevent any single group from dominating, promoting greater evenness in the community. In contrast, finer-textured soils (sandy loam and loamy sand) retain more water and nutrients and tend to foster fast-growing copiotrophic microbes that can outcompete others, resulting in comparatively lower α-diversity. These findings underscore the pivotal role of soil texture in shaping bacterial community diversity, particularly under the water-limited conditions of the dry season.

The potential function of core bacteria Pseudomonas and Delftia

Among the dominant bacterial taxa in our samples, Pseudomonas and Delftia showed two distinct but complementary dominance patterns. Pseudomonas comprised the most dominant OTUs in the dry and wet seasons, whereas Delftia was notable for its broader prevalence and consistently high relative abundance across samples (Fig. 4).

In particular, Delftia (OTU202662) remained the second most abundant lineage across seasons and exceeded 5% relative abundance in nearly all samples, indicating that it represents a persistent core taxon in these fumarolic habitats. Although Delftia is more commonly described from near-neutral environments and moderate temperatures [73], previous studies indicate that Delftia-related lineages can also occur in geothermal acidic systems. In the Kirishima geothermal area of Japan, 16S rRNA gene sequences highly similar to Delftia tsuruhatensis were detected in strongly acidic solfataric ponds, including Pond-C (88 °C, pH 2.4) and Pond-D (67 °C, pH 2.3) [74]. Consistent with this observation, our dominant OTU202662 showed 99.75% identity to Delftia tsuruhatensis (100% identity to Delftia lacustris)in NCBI BLAST searches. These findings suggest that Delftia-related bacteria are not restricted to mild environments and may also persist in geothermal habitats characterized by strong acidity and elevated temperatures.

Taxonomically, Delftia was transferred from the genus Comamonas [75], belongs to the Betaproteobacteria (family Comamonadaceae), and has been reported from diverse environments, including volcanic settings, with broad metabolic capabilities [76]. Comparative genomic analysis further suggests that the genus Delftia possesses substantial adaptive potential. A phylogenomic study based on 61 published genomes revealed a highly open pan-genome containing more than 28,000 gene families but only 884 core genes, indicating marked genomic plasticity and ecological versatility [77]. Such genomic flexibility may help explain why Delftia was able to maintain a high and stable relative abundance across sites and seasons in our study.

In volcanic fumaroles, Delftia may potentially contribute to heavy-metal stabilization and transformation that are often abundant in such extreme environments. Strains such as Delftia sp. JD2 and Delftia lacustris LZ-C exhibit resistance to multiple metals, including chromium (Cr), lead (Pb), cadmium (Cd), and mercury (Hg) [78, 79]. Mechanistically, Delftia can reduce toxic hexavalent chromium [Cr(VI)] to the less harmful trivalent form [Cr(III)], bioaccumulate Pb, and even biomineralize gold via the secretion of delftibactin [80], which precipitates extracellular gold nanoparticles [78, 81]. Although such functions were not directly measured in this study, they provide a plausible ecological context for the persistence of Delftia in geochemically harsh soils.

Pseudomonas is a metabolically versatile and environmentally resilient genus that is widely distributed in soils, aquatic habitats, and diverse natural environments [82]. Previous studies have shown that members of this genus can persist in volcanic and geothermal systems characterized by elevated temperature, low pH, and high metal ion concentrations, including submarine volcanic vents, geothermal springs, and other geochemically stressed habitats [83, 84]. In acidic submarine volcanic waters under high CO2 conditions, Pseudomonas strains have been reported to tolerate acidity while also carrying multiple antibiotic resistance-related genes, suggesting that volcanic environments may serve as natural hotspots for stress adaptation and horizontal gene transfer [83].

In thermal spring systems, some Pseudomonas strains, including thermotolerant P. aeruginosa lineages, are able to grow at 45–50 °C and may possess heat-adaptation traits such as heat shock proteins and membrane-stabilizing mechanisms, which help maintain cellular function under elevated temperatures [84]. These observations suggest that Pseudomonas possesses diverse physiological and genomic strategies for persistence in volcanic, high-temperature, and acidic environments.

In our study, the dominant Pseudomonas OTU differed between the two seasons (Fig. 4), indicating seasonal turnover within the genus rather than persistence of a single dominant lineage. Seasonal environmental change is well known to restructure bacterial communities, and previous high-resolution community studies have shown that seasonal shifts can involve replacement among closely related bacterial strains or OTUs with different environmental preferences [85, 86]. Thus, the replacement of one dominant Pseudomonas OTU by another in our dataset is consistent with ecological differentiation among closely related lineages, with different taxa favored under changing seasonal and geochemical conditions. Therefore, the ecological importance of Pseudomonas in these fumarolic soils may lie not in the long-term dominance of a single lineage, but in the capacity of multiple Pseudomonas taxa to assume similar ecological roles under shifting environmental conditions, complementing the broader and more stable prevalence pattern observed for Delftia.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study provides new insights into microbial life in hyperacidic (pH ≤ 2) and high-temperature (> 70 °C) fumarolic soils of the Tatun Volcanic Group in Taiwan. Soil physicochemical properties and seasonal variation strongly influenced bacterial diversity and community composition under these extreme conditions. Among the dominant taxa, Pseudomonas and Delftia emerged as two major genera with different ecological patterns: Pseudomonas contained the most dominant seasonal OTUs, whereas Delftia remained a consistently abundant core lineage across most samples. These findings suggest that bacterial persistence in fumarolic soils may be supported by both broad ecological versatility and stable adaptation to geochemical stress. Overall, this work advances our understanding of how bacterial communities are structured in hyperacidic volcanic environments and provides a framework for future culture-based, genomic, and functional studies on dominant taxa in extreme terrestrial ecosystems.
