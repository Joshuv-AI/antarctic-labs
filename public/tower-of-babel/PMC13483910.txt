Ultra-broadband ultraviolet detection and imaging enabled by copper-halide inside transparent glass
Authors: Hao Zhang, Hong Jia, Yiwen He, Xiongjian Huang, Daiyuan Liu, Yakun Le, Bozhao Yin, Weiwei Chen, Jiahao Mai, Hanjing Wei, Xiudi Xiao, Xiaofeng Liu, Jiajia Zhou, Jianrong Qiu, Zhongmin Yang, Guoping Dong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe development of broadband ultraviolet (UV) detection and imaging systems is in high demand for applications such as security early warning and environmental monitoring. However, conventional UV detection and imaging systems suffer from limited bandgap tunability, leading to poor compatibility with deep UV wavelengths due to their restricted spectral response. To address this, we fabricated a composite glass device with multi-bandgap design, where Gd3+ ions bridge the bandgap mismatch between the host glass and embedded Cs3Cu2I5 nanocrystals (NCs), enabling efficient carrier cascade cooling and the conversion of deep UV photons into visible light. Owing to an energy transfer efficiency approaching 100%, the device exhibits a spectral response from 180 to 350 nm, including the vacuum UV region ( < 200 nm) that was previously inaccessible to single-band UV detectors. Moreover, the visible-light transparency and scalability of the glass subtract enable seamless integration with silicon-based photoresistors and complementary metal-oxide semiconductor imaging systems, achieving large-area, high-resolution (34.7 lp mm−1) imaging performance that significantly surpasses conventional UV detector arrays. This work therefore establishes a scalable strategy for the rational design and fabrication of stable, ultra-broadband UV detectors, paving the way for next-generation UV detection and imaging technologies.Subject terms: Lasers, LEDs and light sources

Introduction

Ultraviolet detection and imaging systems, which integrate both functionalities, exhibiting tremendous potential across a wide range of fields1–4. Beyond the atmosphere, vacuum ultraviolet light (VUV) enables precise imaging and analysis of solar flares and other high-energy solar phenomena1. Within the atmosphere, the ozone layer completely absorbs natural solar UVC radiation (200–280 nm), creating a background-free environment at the Earth’s surface2. Consequently, any UVC signal detected in this region must originate from artificial or local sources. This allows high-contrast detection with minimal solar interference for applications such as early warning and tracking of high-speed aircraft, engine diagnostics, flame sensing, and corona discharge detection (Supplementary Fig. 1)3,4. Across the entire UV spectrum, materials with distinct ultraviolet absorption characteristics can be exploited for applications including pollutant monitoring, dermatological studies, trace gas detection (e.g., atmospheric ozone and sulfur dioxide), and non-destructive testing in semiconductor manufacturing, precision machinery, and coating analysis5,6. These applications underscore the emerging trend toward UV detection and imaging systems that demand both broad spectral response and exceptional imaging resolution. These critical performance parameters are primarily determined by the photoconversion materials—key components in UV detection and imaging systems7,8. However, current mainstream broadband UV imaging systems typically rely on photomultiplier tubes (PMTs) and wide-bandgap semiconductors such as SiC, GaN, II–VI compound semiconductors, and various emerging materials as their photoconversion media9. The detection range of such systems is largely limited to the UVA (320–400 nm) and UVB (280–320 nm) regions, primarily due to the mismatch between the intrinsic bandgap of the sensing materials and the deep ultraviolet wavelength range10–13. For carriers possessing excessively high energy, relaxation and diffusion to lower energy states are often inefficient. As a result, these carriers fail to recombine radiatively to emit light and instead dissipate their excess energy non-radiatively through lattice interactions involving optical and acoustic phonon emission4,14,15. Furthermore, conventional broadband semiconductor photoconversion materials are difficult to process into high-quality, large-area, and uniform wafers. Imaging is therefore commonly achieved through array assembly or mechanical scanning, both of which require complex mechanisms and yield limited imaging resolution16. Consequently, the design and fabrication of ultraviolet imaging systems that integrate broadband spectral response with high-resolution imaging performance remain major challenges to date.

Developing heterojunction structures that combine semiconductors with different bandgaps offers a promising strategy for extending the detection range of UV photodetectors17,18. Mechanistically, high-energy UV radiation excites high-energy carriers that relax from higher conduction states to lower energy levels, whereas lower-energy UV radiation generates carriers that recombine directly at these lower levels, enabling responses across different UV photon energies. However, the energy difference between semiconductors in such heterojunctions must not be excessively large—ideally between 0.5 and 1.5 eV—to avoid impeding carrier transport pathways19. Consequently, single-layer heterojunctions provide only limited spectral enhancement, requiring multilayer architectures to achieve coverage across the full UV range (7–3.1 eV). Nevertheless, these heterostructures are complex, difficult to fabricate, and often exhibit poor stability, significantly constraining their practical applicability19. Conceptually, integrating materials with varied bandgaps to regulate carrier cooling enables more carriers to relax toward the band edges and undergo radiative recombination. This mechanism effectively broadens the UV response beyond the limits of conventional wide-bandgap semiconductors. To realize high-resolution imaging on a large scale, the optical conversion medium should possess high transparency, compositional uniformity, and a large continuous area, thereby overcoming the constraints of single-pixel mechanical scanning or array-based imaging techniques. Consequently, the development of large-area, transparent, and compositionally homogeneous materials incorporating multiple bandgaps is essential for constructing high-resolution and broadband UV imaging systems.

Based on this concept, we employ glass materials as the primary matrix to integrate components with different bandgaps. As an outstanding carrier medium, amorphous glass can incorporate a wide range of functional materials through doping, in-situ crystallization, or post-synthesis modification20–22. This approach greatly enhances the diversity and compatibility of material systems, encompassing traditional wide-bandgap semiconductors—such as sulfides (ZnS, CaS), oxides (Y2O3, ZnO, BaTiO3), SiC, GaN, II–VI compounds, and other emerging semiconductors as well as various luminescent materials, including rare-earth ions and novel metal halides23,24. Furthermore, glass itself is amenable to large-scale fabrication and exhibits high visible-light transparency, low defect density, long carrier transport length, and tunable bandgaps through compositional design, making it an excellent candidate for imaging and light-conversion applications25,26.

As illustrated in Fig. 1a, we demonstrate the effective integration of semiconductors with different bandgaps via in-situ crystallization of Cs3Cu2I5 nanocrystals (NCs) and doping rare-earth ions (Gd3+) into the glass matrix. By tuning the energy levels of the glass substrate and introducing Gd3+ as an intermediate energy-transfer channel, an efficient stepwise carrier-cooling mechanism is established. This mechanism gradually relaxes high-energy carriers within the glass matrix toward the band-edge of Cs3Cu2I5 NCs (Fig. 1b), enabling their effective excitation in the VUV region (~ 6.88 eV). Our proof-of-concept device exhibits a broadband spectral response spanning 180–350 nm, extending beyond the range previously inaccessible to single-band ultraviolet detectors (Fig. 1c)2,7,8,27–31. More importantly, this composite glass exhibits high visible transparency and compatibility, enabling effective integration into conventional visible complementary metal-oxide semiconductor (CMOS) imaging systems. The imaging test demonstrates sharp-edged, high-contrast VUV images, indicating that this device possesses superior imaging resolution compared to conventional array UV imaging and single-pixel UV imaging.

Results

In this work, we selected borosilicate glass as the host material, Gd3+ ions as the dopants, and induced in-situ crystallization of Cs3Cu2I5 NCs to construct a composite UV detector (Fig. 1a). The multicomponent borosilicate glass provides a balanced processing window for incorporating rare-earth ions and inducing the controlled precipitation of copper-halide nanocrystals. Its flexible yet interconnected network structure, tunable [BO3]/[BO4] units, and suitable viscosity during heat treatment regulate the migration of Cs+, Cu+, and I- ions, thereby enabling the confined nucleation and growth of Cs3Cu2I5 NCs while maintaining high optical transparency. This structural flexibility is essential for simultaneously achieving efficient UV absorption, homogeneous spectral conversion, and large-area optical imaging. In this structure, the glass matrix serves as the primary absorber of high-energy UV photons (λ < 275 nm). The resulting hot carriers then transfer energy to Gd3+ ions, driving their excitation to higher-lying electronic states. The followed relaxation of Gd3+ ions leads to the excitation of Cs3Cu2I5 NCs to produce visible emission. However, the lower energy UV (Wavelength greater than 275 nm) is mainly directly absorbed by Cs3Cu2I5 NCs to achieve the emission of Cs3Cu2I5 NCs. It should be noted that, owing to its limited doping concentration, Gd3+ absorbs only a small fraction of the incident ultraviolet light, contributing only 2.3% of the absorption near 275 nm (Supplementary Fig. 2). Therefore, Gd3+ primarily serves as an energy-transfer channel, mediating the transfer of high-energy ultraviolet excitation from the glass matrix to Cs3Cu2I5 NCs.

The detailed mechanism is illustrated in Fig. 2a. By varying the Cs2O content in the glass, the optical bandgap of the host matrix can be continuously tuned from 4.91 eV for the Cs2O-free glass to 4.54 eV at higher Cs2O contents (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Fig. 3). Similarly, increasing the Gd3+ doping concentration also gradually narrows the bandgap of the glass matrix. This bandgap narrowing is closely related to the network-modifying effect of Cs+, which weakens the Si-O-Si and B-O bridging linkages and promotes the formation of non-bridging oxygen species. Consistently, the DSC results show that the glass-transition-related temperatures gradually decrease with increasing Cs2O and Gd3+ contents Supplementary Fig. 4), while Raman spectra reveal systematic structural evolution from [BO4] to [BO3] units and from bridging oxygen to non-bridging oxygen species (Supplementary Fig. 5). High-resolution O 1 s XPS spectra further confirm the increased contribution of non-bridging oxygen after the introduction of Gd3+ (Supplementary Fig. 6).

Notably, the energy levels of Gd3+ ions remain fixed. Their primary absorption of Gd3+ ions arise from the 8S7/2 → 6IJ transition at approximately 275 nm (4.5 eV), complemented by a secondary 8S7/2 → 6DJ transition around 240 nm (4.88 eV) (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Fig. 3)32. Gd3+ ions excited to these two high energy states gradually relax to the 6PJ level, corresponding to an energy of 3.94 eV33. Consequently, for efficient energy transfer from the glass matrix to the Gd3+ ions, the bandgap of glass should be as close as possible to the Gd3+ 6IJ level (4.54 eV), ensuring efficient energy transfer (see cooling process in Fig. 2a). During this process, the photoexcitation spectrum of Gd3+ in the ultraviolet region shifts from excitation by the isolated ion band gap to excitation by the broad peak of the glass excitation peak (Fig. 2c). Considering bandgap matching, controlled crystallization, and optical transparency, 16 mol% Cs2O and 1.2 mol% Gd3+ were selected as the optimal composition. Lower dopant contents lead to a rigid glass network with limited ion migration, suppressing Cs3Cu2I5 NC precipitation and weakening Gd3+-nanocrystal energy matching. In contrast, excessive Cs2O or Gd3+ induces network depolymerization, spontaneous crystallization, and optical opacity. The optimized glass maintains good transparency while enabling uniform nanocrystal precipitation (Supplementary Fig. 7), thereby balancing structural stability and efficient ultraviolet energy transfer. At the optimal Cs2O concentration (16 mol%), the excitation intensity at 200 nm increased by a factor of ninefold compared to the 4 mol% Cs2O concentration (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Fig. 8a). Importantly, the emission band of Gd3+ ions overlaps almost perfectly with the excitation spectrum of Cs3Cu2I5 NCs, enabling efficient sensitization of Cs3Cu2I5 by Gd3+ ions (Supplementary Fig. 8b).

Furthermore, a series of samples were prepared by systematically varying key parameters, including the Gd³⁺ doping concentration in Cs3Cu2I5 NC composite glasses, the CuI content in the glass composition at a fixed Gd³⁺ concentration to regulate the Cs3Cu2I5 NC concentration, and the annealing temperature, with 490 °C identified as the optimal condition (Supplementary Fig. 9). These comparative experiments were designed to demonstrate the efficient sensitization effect of Gd³⁺ ions on Cs3Cu2I5 NCs. First, we measured the photoluminescence spectra under selective excitation at 275 nm for three samples: glass doped with Gd3+ ions (P: 1.2Gd3+), glass containing only Cs3Cu2I5 NCs (G: 0Gd3+), and Gd3+ doped glass containing Cs3Cu2I5 NCs (G: 1.2Gd3+) (Fig. 2d). As Cs3Cu2I5 NCs precipitate within the glass matrix (Supplementary Fig. 10a), the 315 nm emission attributed to Gd3+ gradually weakens, with the luminescence intensity reduced to 5.8% of its original value. Further crystallization results in an additional reduction of the 315 nm emission intensity (Supplementary Fig. 10b). Under 275 nm excitation, the 450 nm emission attributed to Cs3Cu2I5 NCs is markedly enhanced with Gd3+ doping (Supplementary Fig. 10c). This result leads to a blue shift and broadening of the excitation spectrum for the G: 1.2Gd3+ sample in the ultraviolet range (Supplementary Fig. 11).

The apparent energy-transfer efficiency from Gd3+ to Cs3Cu2I5 NCs was estimated from the quenching of the Gd3+ emission at 315 nm using ηET = 1−IGd,G/IGd,P, where IGd,P and IGd,G represent the integrated Gd3+ emission intensities of the precursor Gd3+-doped glass and the crystallized Gd3+-doped Cs3Cu2I5 NC glass under identical excitation conditions, respectively. The strong suppression of the 315 nm emission, together with the pronounced enhancement of the 450 nm Cs3Cu2I5 emission, indicates highly efficient Gd3+ to Cs3Cu2I5 sensitization. This process also indicates that the distance of Gd3+ and Cs3Cu2I5 NCs is very close. To demonstrate this, a mixture of Gd3+ doped glass and Cs3Cu2I5 NCs (1:1 molar ratio) is used as a counterexample. When the distance between Gd3+ and Cs3Cu2I5 NCs is too far, the emission of Gd3+ at 315 nm wavelength is only partially transferred to Cs3Cu2I5 NCs under 275 nm excitation, and the corresponding quantum efficiency is only 52.25%. It is much lower than Cs3Cu2I5 NCs (90.6%) in glass matrix doped with Gd3+ and crystallized in situ. This indicates that Gd3+ and Cs3Cu2I5 NCs in G: 1.2Gd3+ are very close, which is also consistent with previous reports that rare earth ions in glass usually act as nucleation centers to promote NCs formation (Supplementary Fig. 12)21. Prior to heat treatment, Gd3+ ions are homogeneously dispersed in the precursor glass matrix. Upon annealing, they primarily act as local structure-inducing and heterogeneous nucleation-promoting centers, facilitating Cs3Cu2I5 NC nucleation and confined growth by generating microregions with favorable local environments and enriching key crystallization components. Structural characterization via Raman spectroscopy shows no significant peak shifts or broadening, indicating minimal incorporation of Gd3+ into the Cs3Cu2I5 lattice (Supplementary Fig. 13). XPS measurements confirm the retention of Gd3+ in the glass network and at NC/glass interfaces without altering the lattice of Cs3Cu2I5, while broadening of Cs 3d, Cu 2p, I 3d, and O 1s peaks reflects the formation of abundant nanocrystal/glass interfaces enriched with Gd3+ (Supplementary Fig. 14 and Supplementary Fig. 15). Electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectra further corroborate the presence of paramagnetic Gd3+ centers, whose intensity and broadening increase with Cu⁺ introduction and higher annealing temperature, consistent with interfacial enrichment (Supplementary Fig. 16). These results indicate that after heat treatment, Gd3+ remains predominantly trivalent and localizes at the Cs3Cu2I5 NC/glass interfaces, modulating local coordination and electronic environments, while a fraction remains in the amorphous glass matrix.

We systematically investigated the sensitization mechanism of Cs3Cu2I5 NCs by Gd3+ ions in the composite glass. Time-resolved photoluminescence under 275 nm excitation shows that isolated Gd³⁺ ions in the precursor glass exhibit long lifetimes (~ 4.3 ms), whereas the introduction of Cs3Cu2I5 NCs markedly accelerates the decay of Gd3+ emission to a few microseconds, concomitant with enhanced NC emission (Fig. 2e and Supplementary Fig. 17–22). Analysis using the Inokuti-Hirayama model indicates that the energy transfer from Gd3+ to Cs3Cu2I5 NCs is predominantly dominated by dipole-dipole interactions. To further elucidate the spatial distribution of Gd3+, the decay curves were decomposed into three components corresponding to: (i) Cs3Cu2I5 NCs (fast decay), (ii) Gd3+ ions at the NC/glass interface (intermediate decay), and (iii) Gd3+ ions uniformly dispersed in the glass matrix (slow decay). Comparison of lifetimes and relative amplitudes across samples with varying NC concentrations shows that at low NC content, ~34% of Gd3+ resides in the NC/glass interface, whereas at higher NC content, ~66% of Gd3+ is associated with the NC/glass interface (Supplementary Fig. 22). This redistribution is consistent with the stepwise increase in energy-transfer efficiency as more Gd3+ ions are positioned at the NC interface, providing the necessary structural environment for effective sensitization without requiring lattice incorporation. Together, these results demonstrate that Gd3+ ions act as energy-transfer mediators via dipole-dipole coupling and that their distribution between the amorphous matrix and NC interfaces can be quantitatively inferred from the photoluminescence decay behavior. This dual insight directly correlates the structural arrangement within the composite glass to the observed high-efficiency sensitization of Cs3Cu2I5 NCs.

This process was further confirmed through time-resolved fluorescence spectroscopy (Fig. 2f and Supplementary Figs. 24 and 25). Notably, the time-resolved fluorescence intensity reveals a strong emission band peaking at 315 nm within the initial 0.8 μs, followed by a clear spectral shift from ~315 nm to ~450 nm within the first 1 μs. This dynamic transition further verifies the highly efficient energy transfer from Gd3+ ions to Cs3Cu2I5 NCs. Consequently, as the Gd3+ ion concentration increases, the intensity of the Cs3Cu2I5 emission peak at 450 nm under solar-blind excitation is enhanced by a factor of 12 compared with the undoped sample, confirming the critical role of Gd3+-mediated sensitization in improving the emission efficiency (Fig. 2g).

Demonstrating ultraviolet detection using fabricated composite glass material is not straightforward, as it requires optical detection within a compact system while simultaneously avoiding interference from visible light. To evaluate these aspects through experimentation, we combine silicon-based photoresistors with the fabricated composite glass material to fabricate a UV detection device based on a self-reference method (Fig. 3a). In this setup, the composite glass is employed as a spectral converter. By measuring the difference in photocurrent generated by the two Si-BPRs, the resulting signal is acquired using either an electrochemical workstation or a digital oscilloscope, enabling effective ultraviolet light detection. The device fabricated based on the G: 1.2Gd3+ composite exhibits a broad excitation band across the entire UV spectrum, with a minimum detectable wavelength of 180 nm and a maximum photo-response at 350 nm (Fig. 3b and Supplementary Fig. 26). This result indicates the extension of the ultraviolet detection capability of composite microcrystalline glass into the VUV range. Under excitation at 180–190 nm, the emission intensity gradually increases with increasing excitation wavelength. Similar detection behavior is observed in the solar-blind UV region and the UVB/UVC regions, consistent with the excitation spectrum. To examine the device’s temporal response, we employ pulsed excitation light and repeated responses to solar-blind ultraviolet irradiation are recorded over multiple switching cycles (Fig. 3c and Supplementary Figs. 27 and 28). The response signal gradually increases with increasing excitation wavelength under UV excitation, with the shortest detection wavelength extended to 180 nm (Supplementary Fig. 29). Under 250 Hz modulated illumination, the detector shows stable switching behavior with response and recovery times of ~21 and 440 ms, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 30).

To investigate its photo-response, the Cs3Cu2I5 glass sample used as the spectral converter was exposed to UV light, and current-voltage (I–V) curves were recorded over a voltage range of -0.8 to 0.8 V. The photocurrent and photovoltage signals were recorded by an electrochemical workstation. The I–V curves obtained at different wavelengths exhibit good linearity, consistent with Ohm’s law. From the I–V curves shown in Fig. 3d, the current is minimal under dark conditions, with the device exhibiting a low dark current of approximately 1.0 × 10−12 A. Upon UV irradiation within the 180–200 nm spectral range, the I–V curves begin to separate, indicating excellent photoelectric response performance. Similar detection capabilities are observed in the solar-blind UV range and UVB/UVC bands, consistent with excitation spectra (Supplementary Fig. 31). These results indicate that efficient energy transfer occurs that converts the vacuum UV, solar-blind UV, and UVB/UVC photons to visible emission by the G: 1.2Gd3+ for ultraviolet detection. The quantitative photo response of the glass/Si-coupled detector was evaluated at 300 nm under an applied bias of 0.8 V. The photocurrent follows a power-law dependence on incident optical power, Iph = aPβ, with β = 0.87, indicating a near-linear response to UV intensity. The device exhibits a low dark current of approximately 1.0 × 10−12 A and a detection limit of 17 nW cm−2 at a signal-to-noise ratio of 3 (Supplementary Fig. 32). Through computational simulations, we evaluated the influence of glass thickness on the calculated photocurrent for samples heat-treated at different temperatures (Supplementary Fig. 33). The results indicate that the photocurrent generally decreases with increasing glass thickness, suggesting that the thickness of the conversion layer affects ultraviolet absorption and visible-light propagation, thereby reducing the coupling efficiency with the Si detector. The sample annealed at 490 °C maintained a relatively stable and high photocurrent across different thicknesses, implying an optimal balance between nanocrystal precipitation and optical transparency. Notably, the incident wavelength and optical power primarily govern the spectral conversion performance of the nanocrystal composite glass, whereas the bias voltage determines the intrinsic performance of the Si photodetector. Under a fixed bias of 0.8 V, the photocurrent dependence on the incident wavelength closely follows the excitation spectrum, indicating that the response is mainly dictated by the spectral conversion efficiency (Supplementary Fig. 33d). Consequently, Gd3+ doping effectively enhances high-energy ultraviolet absorption and spectral conversion efficiency, which is particularly advantageous for deep-ultraviolet detection. Under excitations at 185, 250, and 300 nm, the responsivity improved significantly, with enhancement factors of 15.43, 11.49, and 3.84, respectively.

Additionally, we demonstrate the system’s capability for remote detection of UV lamp at distances of up to 10 m based on a simple design (Fig. 4f, g). It should be emphasized that no external optical lenses were employed to concentrate incident light, suggesting that the current detection range remains far from its theoretical limit. This further confirms the inherently strong ultraviolet photo response of the system.

Traditional single-pixel spectral detection systems cannot capture spatial information about light, necessitating the development of large-area, high-resolution real-time ultraviolet imaging to advance various detection applications and material identification. We fabricated large-format glass substrates (Supplementary Fig. 34) and integrated them with conventional standardized CMOS sensors to design a real-time ultraviolet imaging system. Figure 4a illustrates the process by which the imaging system records optical signals. An ultraviolet beam passes through a mask patterned with “S, C, U, T” (SUCT = South China University of Technology) and illuminates the composite glass UV imaging sensor, converting the UV light into visible PL that can be detected by a standard CMOS sensor. Figure 4b clearly demonstrates distinct boundaries and high contrast between light and dark areas for different letters, confirming the system’s uniform, high signal-to-noise device response. Figure 4c, using a line-pair card as the mask, shows finer imaging results while still not reaching its resolution limit (clearly visible at 30 lp mm−1). The resolution limit of the glass’s Modulation Transfer Function (MTF), calculated using the oblique edge method, is 34.7 lp mm−1 (Supplementary Fig. 35), significantly exceeding (one to two orders of magnitude) that of typical array-based and single-pixel UV imaging systems (Supplementary Table 2)39–46. Figure 4e shows the UV-induced luminescence of the Gd3+-doped Cs3Cu2I5 NCs composite glass and its corresponding 3D grayscale map, revealing a uniform spectral conversion across a large area that ensures stable spatial response for ultraviolet imaging. Real-time images during five consecutive UV on/off cycles (Fig. 4e, f and Supplementary Movie 1) indicate that the normalized integrated intensity in selected regions remains essentially unchanged, while the grayscale profiles exhibit sharp boundaries and high signal-to-background contrast. These results demonstrate that the imaging system provides reliable cycling repeatability, high spatial uniformity, and rapid real-time response, confirming its suitability for large-area ultraviolet imaging applications.

We further optimize this imaging system for flame detection. A normal flame generates radiation encompasses ultraviolet, visible and infrared light47. Conventional digital cameras typically detect visible light and part of the infrared spectrum but do not respond to the ultraviolet component48. For the demonstration of the imaging system (Fig. 4g, h), we employed a visible-light filter and a visible-light-bandpass filter. Visible light within the flame is blocked by the cutoff filter, and the remaining ultraviolet spectrum partially transmits through the composite glass, converting it into visible light Supplementary Fig. 36). After passing through the visible-bandpass filter, which blocks the infrared portion, the Butane flame and oxyhydrogen flame is clearly captured by the camera. Not only the shape of the flame, but also the inner and outer flame can be well distinguished. This result demonstrates that the ultraviolet imaging system possesses high resolution and excellent responsivity. Notably, the detection system exhibits long-term environmental and UV stability. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 37, after 30 h of irradiation with a 30 W mercury lamp and 30 days of immersion in water, the composite glass remains stable without the formation of color centers and the degradation of luminescence efficiency. These results further confirm the outstanding long-term stability of our composite glass UV detection system.

Discussion

We have demonstrated a highly sensitive, wide-response ultraviolet spectral converter using a nanocrystalline composite glass. Based on a cascade energy transfer mechanism, the composite glass offers a broad excitable spectral range from 180 nm to 350 nm, which is the widest spectral range currently achieved in nanocrystalline composite glass. This unique energy transfer strategy can be applied to other wide-bandgap semiconductor materials. Simultaneously, by integrating the developed UV spectral converter with silicon photoresistors, we realized a stable UV detector based on a simple operating mechanism. Furthermore, the wide-response UV spectral converter can be facilely integrated with conventional CMOS chips, enabling high-resolution (34.7 lp mm−1), large-area UV imaging. Our results may have strong implications for the development of next-generation, large-scale, portable, high-resolution UV imagers for scientific, industrial and biomedical applications.

Methods

Preparation of glass precursor samples

Amorphous and transparent glass samples were synthesized using the conventional melt quenching method. Matrix glasses were uniformly mixed according to the nominal molar composition of 20SiO2-50B2O3-8K2O-3BaO-6CsI-XCs2O (mol%, X = 4–20). Gd3+ containing glasses: Uniformly mixed according to the nominal molar composition of 20SiO2-50B2O3-8K2O-3BaO-6CsI-XCs2O-YGd2O3 (mol%, X = 0–20, Y = 0–2.5). Cs3Cu2I5 nanocrystal containing glasses: Uniformly mixed according to the nominal molar composition of 20SiO2-50B2O3-8K2O-3BaO-6CsI-XCs2O-YGd2O3-1CuI (mol%, X = 4–20, Y = 0–2.5).

All raw materials were heated in a furnace under an argon atmosphere at 1150 °C for 20 min. The molten mixture was then poured into stainless steel molds for quenching, forming precursor glass. This was subsequently annealed at 400 °C to relieve internal thermal stresses. Finally, the glass samples were cut and polished for subsequent crystallization and optical analysis. Crystallization of the Cs3Cu2I5 nanocrystals within the glass was achieved by heat treatment at 470–500 °C (above the glass transition temperature, Tg) for 10 h. Due to the introduction of Cu⁺ ions, Cs3Cu2I5 recrystallized back into its original phase within the glass matrix after heat treatment.

Sample characterizations

X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of samples annealed at different temperatures were obtained using a Bruker D8 Advance diffractometer (Farenda, Switzerland) equipped with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 0.1541 nm). The glass transition temperatures of all glasses were determined by differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) using a STA449C Jupiter thermal analyzer (Netzsch, Bavaria, Germany) under ambient conditions at a heating rate of 10 °C min−1. The morphology and size distribution of nanocrystals (NCs) formed after glass crystallization were characterized using transmission electron microscopy (TEM, JEM-2100F, JEOL, Japan). Optical absorption spectra were recorded using a PerkinElmer Lambda 900 spectrophotometer (Waltham, USA). The photoluminescence (PL) spectra and decay lifetimes of the bulk glass were measured using an Edinburgh FLS 920 fluorescence spectrometer (Edinburgh Instruments Ltd., Livingston, UK) in conjunction with a Hamamatsu photomultiplier tube.

Construction of the ultraviolet detection system

Transparent high-purity silica glass and our prepared glass samples were placed on two silicon photoresistors, respectively. The high-purity silica glass served as a reference component and did not undergo ultraviolet light conversion. Our detector operates by measuring the ultraviolet-induced photovoltage difference between the two silicon photoresistors using an electrochemical workstation (Wuhan Corr Test Instruments Corp., Ltd.). The photo-voltage responses of the Gd3+-doped Cs3Cu2I5 NC glass were measured under wavelength-dependent bias conditions to reduce noise. Specifically, the applied bias was fixed at 20 mV for excitation wavelengths below 200 nm, while a bias of 0.8 mV was used for all other wavelengths. For the I–V measurements, the incident optical power density was fixed at 3 mW cm−2. In all measurements of the relationship between optical power density and photocurrent, the applied bias was fixed at 0.8 mV.

Construction of the ultraviolet imaging system

The imaging system consists of a simple visible-light-cutoff bandpass filter and a visible-light-transmitting filter. The sample to be converted to ultraviolet light is positioned between these two filters. A convex lens is used to collect and converge the light. Finally, a Nikon D610 digital SLR camera captures the converted ultraviolet image.
