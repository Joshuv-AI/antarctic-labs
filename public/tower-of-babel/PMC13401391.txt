Central Precocious Puberty and Sjogren-Larsson Syndrome in a Child: A Rare Case Report
Authors: Canmiao Zhao, Na Tao, Liping Ge, Meiyuan Sun, Yanfang Su, Yang Yang, Fang Xu, Qi Huang, Lijun Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractExtremely rare cases show co-occurrence of Sjogren-Larsson syndrome (SLS) and central precocious puberty (CPP), and our understanding of it is still limited. In this case report, we reported a 9-year-old boy (weight: 20 kg; height: 120 cm) showing SLS and CPP simultaneously. The patient presented to our hospital with testicle enlargement and presence of pubic hair. SLS was diagnosed based on the presence of ichthyosis and intelligent disability, together with genetic confirmation by next-generation sequencing, which identified two pathogenic variants (c.608_609del and c.1157A>G) in the ALDH3A2 gene. The diagnosis of CPP was established based on mildly advanced bone age, ultrasonographic evidence of pubertal development, and elevated basal gonadotropin and testosterone levels. The patient subsequently received triptorelin acetate (3.75mg) for treating CPP, while merely moisturization and rehabilitation was used for treating SLS. In the follow-up, he showed growth delay, with a body height of 120 cm at age 8.5 years old and 123.5 cm at 9.5 years old.Keywords: Sjogren-Larsson syndrome, central precocious puberty, ALDH3A2 gene, mutation, sequencing

Introduction

Sjögren-Larsson syndrome (SLS) is a rare autosomal recessive neurocutaneous disorder caused by pathogenic variants in the ALDH3A2 gene, which encodes fatty aldehyde dehydrogenase (FALDH).1 The deficiency of this enzyme leads to the accumulation of toxic lipid intermediates, resulting in a distinct clinical phenotype2 characterized by the triad of congenital ichthyosis, spastic diplegia or quadriplegia, and intellectual disability.3 Additional manifestations may include seizures, ophthalmologic abnormalities (such as glistening white dots in the fundus), and skeletal involvement.4 Due to significant phenotypic heterogeneity and the rarity of the disease, the diagnosis of SLS based solely on clinical features can be challenging, particularly in atypical cases or early stages.5

Central precocious puberty (CPP) is a common pediatric endocrine disorder characterized by the premature activation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal (HPG) axis.6 CPP manifests as either idiopathic or organic/lesion related (intracranial lesion) with the former more common in girls and the latter in boys. The diagnosis is clinically established by the onset of secondary sexual characteristics before the age of 8 in girls or 9 in boys, accompanied by advanced bone age and a pubertal luteinizing hormone (LH) response to gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) stimulation.7 Given that SLS primarily affects the central nervous system and that the onset of CPP is closely regulated by hypothalamic function, a potential link between these two conditions may be hypothesized. However, to date, no studies have reported an association between SLS and CPP, and no case report has reported the co-occurrence of these two conditions. Here, we report a pediatric case initially presenting with central precocious puberty, who was subsequently identified to have Sjögren-Larsson syndrome via whole-exome sequencing.

Case Presentation

A 9-year-old boy (weight: 20 kg; height: 120 cm, approximately −2 SD) presented to our hospital with bilateral testicular enlargement and pubic hair development. On physical examination, the penile size was approximately 2.5 cm, with a width of 1.5 cm, and the testicular volume was estimated at 4.0 mL. The penis measurement was performed based on the stretched penile length method.

Laboratory evaluations were performed to assess endocrine and biochemical parameters. Serum testosterone (TES) was markedly elevated at 4.28 ng/mL (reference range: 0.03–0.68 ng/mL), and LH was increased to 2.52 IU/L (reference range: <0.02 IU/L). In addition, serum uric acid (UA) was elevated at 407 μmol/L (reference range: 119–327 μmol/L). Progesterone (PGN) and total 25-hydroxyvitamin D (VitD) levels were decreased, measuring 0.54 nmol/L (reference range: 0.7–4.3 nmol/L) and 63.76 nmol/L (reference range: 75–250 nmol/L), respectively. Further hormonal analysis revealed dihydrotestosterone at 309.9 pg/mL (reference range: <200 pg/mL), while 17-hydroxyprogesterone (17-OHP) was at 1.08 ng/mL (reference range: <2.32 ng/mL).

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain or pituitary gland showed no structural abnormalities. The pituitary measured 4.3 mm×6.7 mm×8.9 mm. Bone age assessment estimated a bone age of 9 years and 6 months, which is mildly advanced by approximately 6 months relative to chronological age, based on the Greulich and Pyle atlas (Figure 1A). Scrotal ultrasonography showed that the left testis measured 2.9×1.8×1.6 cm in size, while the right testis measured 3.0×1.8×1.5 cm. The caput epididymis on both sides measured approximately 0.6×0.5 cm. Color Doppler flow imaging demonstrated short, rod-shaped vascular signals in both testicles and epididymis (Figure 1B). The findings of bilateral testicular enlargement and elevated serum TES and LH levels were consistent with a diagnosis of CPP. Accordingly, treatment with intramuscular triptorelin acetate (3.75 mg once monthly) was initiated.

Notably, increased muscle tone in the limbs was observed, suggesting underlying neurological involvement. In addition, the patient showed generalized dry skin (Figure 2) and exhibited global developmental delay with an inability to walk. Given the combination of neurological abnormalities and dermatological manifestations, an underlying inherited metabolic or neurocutaneous disorder was suspected. Trio-based NGS of the patient and his parents was performed on the proband and his parents using Illumina platform. The mean coverage depth of the target regions was approximately 100×, with over 95% of the exonic regions covering at a minimum depth of 20×. Raw sequencing data were aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh37/hg19 or GRCh38) using Burrows–Wheeler Aligner (BWA). The sequencing identified compound heterogeneous mutations in ALDH3A2 gene (NM_000382) on chromosome 17, including c.608_609del (p.Pro203Argfs*4) and c.1157A>G (p.Asn386Ser) (Figure 3). According to the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) guidelines, both mutations were classified as pathogenic for SLS (c.608_609del, PVS1_Very Strong, PM2, PM3; c.1157A>G, PM3_Very Strong, PM1, PM2, PP3_Moderate). Therefore, the patient was diagnosed with SLS.

Treatment with intramuscular triptorelin acetate (3.75 mg once monthly) was initiated upon CPP diagnosis, while merely moisturization and rehabilitation was used for treating SLS. Over a 12-month follow-up period, linear growth was relatively slow, with height increasing from 120 cm (−2 SD) at 8.5 years of age to 123.5 cm at 9.5 years. In contrast, body weight increased from 22 kg to 29.5 kg during the same period.

Discussion

In this case, we reported a rare case of CPP and SLS in a Chinese pediatric patient. As is known is to all, there has been no confirmed association between CPP and SLS, although both conditions would show neurological involvement. Our study may contribute to our understanding of the co-occurrence of these two diseases.

With the advances of medical research, CPP is not only recognized as a disorder characterized by the premature activation of the neuroendocrine axis, but is also accompanied by profound metabolic reprogramming, particularly involving abnormalities in mitochondrial-centered energy and amino acid metabolism.8,9 Wang et al shown that three major classes of metabolic pathways are predominantly enriched in CPP: starch and sucrose metabolism; amino acid metabolism (including β-alanine, tryptophan, and histidine); and purine/pyrimidine metabolism, which reflects enhanced cellular proliferation and transcription, consistent with the accelerated growth and differentiation observed during puberty10 Differential protein analysis indicates that mitochondria are key targets, with upregulation of ALDH2 (which maintains mitochondrial homeostasis) and MLYCD, and downregulation of ALDH3A2 and ALDH9A1, both of which are involved in multiple amino acid metabolic pathways.10 These enzymes may represent critical regulatory factors in CPP that have not yet been fully recognized.

ALDH3A2 is a well-established pathogenic gene in SLS, where its deficiency results in impaired fatty aldehyde metabolism and neurodevelopmental abnormalities.11 Loss of ALDH3A2 activity results in the accumulation of toxic lipid aldehydes and alcohols, causing disturbances in lipid homeostasis, oxidative stress responses, and mitochondrial-associated metabolic processes that are critical for normal brain development.12 These metabolic perturbations primarily underlie the neurological manifestations of SLS. Given the central role of ALDH3A2 in lipid and mitochondrial metabolism, its dysfunction highlights a broader link between metabolic homeostasis and neurodevelopmental regulation.13,14 Although a direct clinical association between CPP and SLS has not been established, and ALDH3A2 is not currently recognized as a gene associated with CPP, its involvement in mitochondrial function and oxidative stress regulation raises the possibility that alterations in related metabolic pathways may have context-dependent effects on neuroendocrine and developmental processes.15,16 Our case may raise the possibility of shared neuro-metabolic mechanisms and warrant further investigation into potential comorbidity or shared susceptibility between these two conditions.

The mutations in the ALDH3A2 gene leading to SLS exhibit geographic variability, with specific mutations being more common in certain populations.17,18 These pathogenic mutations include deletions, insertions, missense mutations, splicing defects, and complex rearrangements. The most common mutation in the Swedish population is the homozygous missense mutation of c.943C>T (p.Pro315Ser),19 and the common mutation in patients in other European countries is the c.1297–1298del (p.Glu433Argfs*3).1,20 Most mutations reported in Mainland, China are c.1157A>G.21 For the first time, we reported a case of SLS concurrent with CPP in a 9-year-old boy, carrying c.608_609del (p.Pro203Argfs*4) and c.1157A>G (p.Asn386Ser). In the follow up, the patient received intramuscular administration of Triptorelin (3.75mg) for the treatment, but the growth of the patient was slow, with a body height of 120 cm (−2SD) at age 8.5 years old and 123.5 cm at 9.5 years old.

There are some limitations in this study. We could not illustrate the potential link between CPP and SLS although we for the first time reported a case with such two conditions simultaneously. One limitation of this study is that genes previously reported to be associated with CPP (eg, DLK1, MKRN3, and KISS1) were not specifically prioritized or analyzed. However, in this patient, CPP was clinically diagnosed as idiopathic based on endocrine evaluation and imaging findings, and therefore genetic analysis specifically targeting CPP-related genes was not the primary focus. Besides, this is a case report not a large sample size study, and the treatment outcome is not good. Furthermore, it is still a challenge for us to establish a correlation between phenotype and genotype. In the future, a close follow-up will be given to the patient with the compliance of the patient and his parents, in order to further investigate the treatment efficiency and outcome.

Conclusions

We reported an extremely rare case of CPP and SLS in a Chinese patient. The diagnosis of SLS is highly depending on the ichthyosis and neurological features, as well as ALDH3A2 mutation analysis. CPP was diagnosed with the mildly advanced bone age, ultrasonic findings, along with the elevation of TES and LH. Upon the treatment using triptorelin acetate for CPP, along with moisturization and rehabilitation for SLS, he showed growth delay, with a body height of 120 cm at age 8.5 years old and 123.5 cm at 9.5 years old. Although a direct causal relationship between SLS and CPP has not been established, this case expands the phenotypic spectrum of SLS and may provide useful insights for future clinical observation and research. Given the limitations of a single case report and the lack of functional evidence, these findings should be interpreted with caution.
