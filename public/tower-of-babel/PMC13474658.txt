Sweet potato starch noodle enriched with purple sweet potato anthocyanins: Improving anthocyanin retention and noodle quality by adding citrus peel pectin
Authors: Sevenur Sarıkaya, Filiz Altay, Guohua Zhao, Fayin Ye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Due to chemical instability, anthocyanins easily degrade during processing and cooking. This study investigated whether citrus peel pectin (CP) could increase purple sweet potato anthocyanin (PSPA) retention in sweet potato starch noodles. Particle size analysis, ζ-potential measurements, and small-angle X-ray scattering confirmed that CP formed nanoscale complexes with PSPA, which showed heterogeneous multilayer adsorption on CP fitting the Freundlich isotherm model. Adding 0.5% and 3% CP significantly increased PSPA retention from 37.82% to 48.75% and 52.24%, respectively. CP and PSPA altered starch structure and noodle gel network, modifying texture and tensile properties without affecting cooking yield or loss. PSPA reduced hardness and chewiness of noodle. Notably, 0.5% CP combined with PSPA maximized tensile force and extensibility of cooked noodles. This study suggests CP effectively elevates PSPA retention via complex formation, with 0.5% CP balancing PSPA retention and noodle quality, offering insights for improving pigmented starch products with natural polysaccharides.

Introduction

Purple sweet potato (Ipomoea batatas L. Lam) is a member of the family Convolvulaceae and is commonly cultivated worldwide with high yield and consumed as human food (Tang et al., 2018). According to Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations (2025), 90,930,337.83 tons of sweet potatoes were produced in a 7,215,012-ha area worldwide. Purple sweet potato is consumed in different ways, such as boiling, baking, or processing into flour and starch to use as an ingredient in starch noodles, pasta, cereal-based foods, bakery products (bread, cookies, crackers), and snacks (Maqsood et al., 2025). Purple sweet potato contains essential macronutrients and health-beneficial components, including starch, dietary fiber, and bioactive phenolic compounds (Li, Xiao, et al., 2019). Purple sweet potato is rich in anthocyanin, which has gained popularity in the food industry and among consumers due to its natural pigment, water solubility, and antioxidant effects that protect against various health issues (Huang et al., 2021).

As a food additive, purple sweet potato anthocyanin (PSPA) is widely used in the formulation of foods, such as 3D-printed ready-to-eat starch gels (Huang et al., 2024) and jelly drinks (Tensiska et al., 2017). However, PSPA loss and degradation have been observed in food processing, such as steaming, boiling, microwave, drying, extrusion, and other novel techniques, due to its instability. Mulyawanti et al. (2018) found pigment loss during the cooking process of purple sweet potato pasta due to anthocyanin degradation by an increase in oxidation reactions. Therefore, techniques were applied to enhance anthocyanin retention, including high-pressure processing to fruit samples (Marszałek et al., 2017), steam-blanching (Mahmudatussa'adah et al., 2019), ultrasound-pretreatment, and ohmic-heat treatment to purple sweet potatoes (Karacabey et al., 2023). Besides these techniques, various types of polysaccharides, hydrocolloids, and proteins are also utilized to enhance the stability of anthocyanins, such as chitosan, sodium carboxymethylcellulose-pectin, and xanthan gum (Herrman et al., 2022; Kraithong & Rawdkuen, 2020; Liu et al., 2023).

Previous studies utilized pectins to increase the stability of anthocyanins and reported positive results. For instance, Liu et al. (2024) found that applying sunflower pectin significantly increased the retained anthocyanin from 22.3% to 31.5% and from 43.4% to 52.1% after heating treatment. Moreover, Liu et al. (2023) observed that sunflower pectin remarkably reduced the thermal degradation of purple sweet potato anthocyanin and improved the half-life of anthocyanin at pH 5. In another study, water-soluble blueberry pectin fractions significantly decreased free anthocyanin content at pH 4.5, binding with anthocyanin (Lin et al., 2016). The studies suggested that this binding between pectin and anthocyanin was formed via non-covalent interactions, such as hydrogen bonds and electrostatic interactions (Liu et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2024).

Starch noodles have been produced and consumed as a traditional staple food in Asian countries for thousands of years. Starch noodles are elastic, have a high shelf life, and have less cooking loss (Wang et al., 2015), which provides better mouthfeel and yield. Moreover, starch noodles are preferred by celiac patients and people who are sensitive to gluten. However, starch noodles demonstrate limited visual appeal and low functional and nutritional value, which are important for consumer acceptance. Research interest has increased in starch noodles to improve the nutritional and other qualities of noodles (Wang et al., 2015), and pigmented noodles are gaining popularity. PSPA could contribute to starch noodles to enhance sensory appeal, consumer acceptance, and nutritional functionality.

To date, despite the well-documented stabilizing effect of pectin on anthocyanin retention in previous studies, the application of purple sweet potato anthocyanins (PSPA) in starch noodles via citrus peel pectin (CP) remains underexplored. The retention of PSPA in starch noodles continues to pose significant challenges due to cooking-induced degradation and poor incorporation into the noodle matrix. We hypothesize that CP enhances PSPA retention through the formation of CP-PSPA complexes, driven by their opposite charges. These complexes not only improve anthocyanin retention but also enhance the color properties, structural integrity and cooking quality of the starch noodles. This study focused on unraveling CP-PSPA complex formation to improve anthocyanin retention in purple sweet potato starch noodles, while also evaluating the synergistic effects of PSPA and CP on the noodle properties. Our findings would provide experimental evidence for enhancing anthocyanin retention and advancing the functionalization of anthocyanins in starchy food products.

Materials and methods

Materials

Sweet potato starch (denoted as SPS, 84.4 g/100 g carbohydrate, food grade) was purchased from Chongqing Zhongweitang Organic Food Co., Ltd. (Zhongxian, Chongqing, China). Anthocyanin from purple sweet potato (PSPA) (E1cm1%= 100, Lot number: 20240315) was purchased from Qingdao Pengyuan Kanghua Natural Source Co., Ltd. (Qingdao, Shangdong, China). Citrus peel pectin (CP, Galacturonic acid ≥74.0%, dried basis, P9135) was obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Anhydrous ethanol (99.7%, reagent grade) and hydrochloric acid (AR, 36–38% w/w) were provided by Chuandong Chemical (Group) Co., Ltd. (Chongqing, China). Other chemicals used were of analytical grade and were used as received.

Characterization of the binding behavior of PSPA to CP in solutions

The CP concentrations of 0.5% (w/v) and 3.0% (w/v) were selected to represent relatively low and high CP ratios, respectively, according to preliminary experiments and previous studies investigating pectin-anthocyanin interactions.

Preparation of CP-PSPA complexes

To prepare CP stock solutions, 0.2 g and 1.2 g of CP were individually dissolved in 20 mL distilled water at 50 °C in a water bath, yielding 1% (w/v) and 6% (w/v) solutions, respectively. These samples were centrifuged at 9000 ×g and 20 °C for 20 min (for subsequent SAXS analysis only) to obtain clear supernatants. Subsequently, 5 mL of each CP stock solution (1% and 6%, w/v) was diluted with 5 mL distilled water to create samples labeled P0.5 and P3.0, respectively. Separately, 0.2 g PSPA was dissolved in 20 mL distilled water to prepare a 1% (w/v) PSPA stock solution. This stock was diluted (5 mL + 5 mL distilled water) to obtain a 0.5% (w/v) solution labeled ANT. CP-PSPA complexes (AP0.5 and AP3.0) were formed by mixing 5 mL of the PSPA stock solution (1%, w/v) with 5 mL of the CP stock solutions (1% and 6%, w/v), respectively. All prepared solutions (Supplementary Table S1) were incubated at 50 °C for 10 min before ζ-potential and particle size analysis.

Measurements of ζ-potential and particle size of CP-PSPA complexes

Once the solutions P0.5, P3.0, ANT, AP0.5, and AP3.0 (Section 2.2.1) were prepared, the particle size and ζ-potential were measured immediately with a Malvern Laser Particle Size Analyzer (Nano ZS90, Malvern Panalytical Ltd., Malvern, UK) at 25 ± 0.1 °C under the following conditions: equilibration time, 20 s, number of runs, 4, run duration, 4 s. Samples were in their natural pH levels (pH (ANT) = 3.28, pH (AP0.5) = 2.96, pH (P0.5) = 2.96, pH (AP3.0) = 2.61, pH (P3.0) = 2.53).

Small-angle X-ray scattering analysis (SAXS) of CP-PSPA complexes

SAXS analyses were performed on BL16B1 beamline at the Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility (Shanghai, China). The solutions (P0.5, P3.0, ANT, AP0.5, and AP3.0) were exposed to the incident X-ray beam (λ = 0.124 nm) with exposure time of 40 s. The distance from the sample to the detector was 1900 mm. The scattering data were collected using a Pilatus 2MF detector at 25 °C with an active surface area of 288.8 × 253.7 mm (length × width) and a pixel size of 172 μm. The SAXS pattern was analyzed by the Fit2D software (European Synchrotron Radiation Facility, Grenoble, France). The scattering vector q = 4π sin θ/λ, where 2θ was the scattering angle, and λ was the X-ray wavelength. The scattering vector (q) ranged from 0.122 nm−1 to 5.374 nm−1.

PSPA/CP adsorption study

The adsorption isotherm was determined following methods from references (Koh et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2023) with modifications. Briefly, PSPA solutions (0.2–1%, w/v) in citrate buffer (25 mM, pH 4.0) were analyzed using a microplate reader (Epoch2, BioTek Instruments, Vermont, USA) to generate a calibration curve (y = 6.0364x - 0.3043, where y - absorbance and x - PSPA concentration, R2 = 0.9969). A 1% (w/v) CP stock solution and a 4% (w/v) PSPA stock solution were prepared by dissolving 0.2 g CP (db) or 0.8 g PSPA (db) in 20 mL citrate buffer (25 mM, pH 4.0), respectively, with heating at 50 °C until complete dissolution. PSPA working solutions (0.2–4%, w/v) were obtained by diluting the PSPA stock solution with citrate buffer. For the adsorption experiment, 2.5 mL of each PSPA working solution (0.2–4%, w/v) was mixed with 2.5 mL CP stock solution (1%, w/v), yielding 5 mL PSPA-CP solutions (0.1–2% PSPA, w/v). These mixtures were incubated at 50 °C for 1.5 h and then at 4 °C for 24 h. After centrifugation (14,000 ×g, 4 °C, 20 min; Cence H1850R centrifuge, Hunan Xiangyi, Hunan, China), the absorbance was measured at 520 nm using the microplate reader. Control samples (0.1–2% PSPA, pH 4.0) were prepared without CP. The calibration curve was used to convert absorbance values to concentrations. The bound anthocyanin content was calculated by Eq. (1). Freundlich isotherm was applied to fit the data by the following Eq. (2):(1)Bound anthocyanin content=C0−Cwhere the C0 was the concentration of anthocyanins in the control group, and the C was the concentration of free anthocyanins in the experimental group (with CP).(2)Γ=KF.C1/n

Rapid visco analysis (RVA)

The pasting properties of sweet purple potato starch samples with adding CP, PSPA or both (SPS, P0.5, P3.0, ANT, AP0.5, AP3.0) were determined using a Rapid Visco Analyzer (RVA-Tec Master, Perten Instruments AB, Stockholm, Sweden). Samples (3 g dry base) mixed with 25 mL distilled water were poured into aluminum canisters. The test began with a 10 s initial paddle speed of 960 r/min to prevent clumping, followed by 160 r/min throughout the test. Temperature conditions: held at 50 °C for 1 min, heated to 95 °C over 2.5 min, cooled to 50 °C in 4 min, and held for 2 min. Pasting parameters recorded in this test included peak viscosity (PV), trough viscosity (TV), final viscosity (FV), breakdown (BD), setback (SB), and pasting temperature (PT).

Preparation of sweet potato starch noodles

Sweet potato starch noodles (Supplementary Table S2) were prepared according to Li, Ye, et al. (2019). Firstly, 60 g of distilled water was mixed with 27 g of sweet potato starch (with/ without CP, PSPA, or both) to make a starch slurry (starch-to-water ratio: 27:60, w/w). The slurry was poured into an aluminum tray (21.0 cm × 16.5 cm) and spread evenly. The tray was heated by floating on the surface of a boiling water bath (95 ± 2 °C) for 15 s and subsequently immersed in boiling water (95 ± 2 °C) to cook for 3 min. Secondly, the tray was placed on the surface of the cooling water (15–20 °C) to cool down for approximately 1 min. The formed starch gel was carefully lifted off from the tray and covered with plastic wrap and placed overnight in the refrigerator at 4 °C. Thirdly, the starch gel was cut into uniform strips with dimensions of 1.5 mm (thickness) × 10 mm × 170 mm and dried in a constant temperature and humidity incubator (BSC-150, Shanghai Boxun Industry & Commerce Co., Ltd. Medical Equipment Factory, Shanghai, China) at 60 °C and 40% relative humidity for 5 h. The dried starch noodles were stored in a desiccator until further use. The moisture content of dried sweet potato starch noodles was determined according to the National Standard of the People's Republic of China (GB 5009.3–2016).

Determination of cooking properties of sweet potato starch noodles

10 g of sweet potato starch noodles were cooked in 400 mL of boiling water (1:40, w/v) for 15 min. The cooked noodles were placed in cool water (20–24 °C) for 30–40 s. The cooked noodles were weighed. The cooking water and rinse water were dried in a forced-air drying oven (DHG-9203 A, Shanghai Yiheng Scientific Instruments Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) at 105 °C for 5 h. After drying, the samples were taken out and weighed. The cooking loss and cooking yield were calculated by using the following Eqs. Measuring the color of dried and cooked sweet potato starch noodles

The photographs of the sweet potato starch noodles were taken by a digital camera (Canon 80D, Canon Inc., Tokyo, Japan) under standard lighting with a white background.

The color of dried and cooked sweet potato starch noodles was characterized by a colorimeter (WR-18, Wave Optoelectronics, Shenzhen, China). The colorimeter was adjusted using a white standard plate (L* = 92.12, a* = 0.37, b* = −0.06). L*, a*, and b* values were recorded. WI (whiteness index), the color difference (ΔE), hue angle (h*; basic color), and chroma (C*; color intensity or saturation) were calculated according to Eqs. Determination of anthocyanin retention in dried and cooked sweet potato starch noodles

Extraction of anthocyanins from the noodles

The dried noodles and cooked noodles (freeze-dried prior to use) were cut into ∼1 mm fragments with laboratory scissors, then ground intermittently (15 s on / 30 s off, 3 cycles) in a high-speed grinder (Supor-SHM2005, Zhejiang Shaoxing Supor Household Products Co., Ltd., Shaoxing, China) to avoid anthocyanin overheating degradation. The powder was sieved through 100-mesh sieve for use. 2.00 g (db) of sieved powder was mixed with 5 mL pre-chilled 25% ethanol (1:2.5, w/v; pH 3.3, acidified with 0.1 M HCl, freshly prepared), vortexed 30 s for full dispersion, and then extracted in a light-shielded shaking incubator (ZWY-2102C, Shanghai Zhicheng Analytical Instruments Manufacturing Co. Ltd., Shanghai, China) at 35 °C, 180 rpm for 2 h. After 15 min centrifugation at 9000 ×g (4 °C), the supernatant was collected. The residue was repeatedly extracted with 5 mL pre-chilled acidified ethanol until the supernatant absorbance at 520 nm was below 0.005 (typically 3–4 cycles). The combined supernatants were diluted to 25 mL with acidified ethanol for subsequent measurement.

Determination of anthocyanin retention rate

The anthocyanin content was determined by using the method of Shiau et al. (2023). 1 mL of the sample (from Section 2.7.1) was mixed with 4 mL of HCl-KCl buffer (0.025 M, pH 1.0) and sodium acetate buffer (0.4 M, pH 4.5), respectively. The samples were measured for absorbance at 520 nm and 700 nm with a microplate reader (Epoch2, BioTek Instruments, Inc., Vermont, USA). The total anthocyanin content of samples was calculated by Eq. (9):(9)TACμg/gdb=A×F×Mw×1000×Vε×x×Wwhere A = [(A520nm – A700nm) pH1.0 - (A520nm - A700nm) pH4.5]; F: dilution factor; MW: 449.2 g/mol of cyanidin-3-glucoside; V: extract volume (mL); ε (molar extinction coefficient of cyanidin-3-glucoside): 26,900 L/mol; x: path length of cuvette (cm); W: sample weight (g); and db: dry weight basis.

Characterizing physical properties of dried sweet potato starch noodles

The sweet potato starch noodles were ground into powder by a grinder (Supor-SHM2005, Zhejiang Shaoxing Supor Household Products Co., Ltd., Zhejiang, China) and sieved through a 100-mesh sieve, followed by equilibration at room temperature for 24 h for further analyses, including XRD, ATR-FTIR, SAXS, DSC, and RVA.

SAXS

The test conditions were applied as described in Section 2.2.3. 1 g of the powdered sample and SPS were mixed with 1 mL of distilled water to prepare a slurry (50%, w/v), respectively. Then, the slurry was placed into a sample cell and measured with exposure times of 60 s. The scattering vector (q) ranged from 0.122 nm−1 to 5.374 nm−1.

X-ray diffraction (XRD)

The crystalline structures of powdered samples were determined using an X-ray diffractometer (X'Pert3 Powder, Malvern Panalytical B.V., Almelo, the Netherlands) under the tube pressure of 40 kV (Cu-Kα, λ = 1.54 nm) and tube flow of 40 mA. Signals were recorded in a range of diffraction angles (2θ) from 4° to 40° at a scanning speed of 2°/min (Yang et al., 2021). The relative crystallinity (RC, %) was calculated by MDI-Jade 6.5 software (Materials Data Inc., CA, USA) for the starch and the noodles according to the following Eq. (10):(10)RC%=∑i=1nAciAt×100%where Aci represents the area of the crystalline peak with index i, and At is the total area of the XRD pattern.

Attenuated total reflectance–fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (ATR-FTIR)

Regarding the reported method by Yang et al. (2021), with some modifications, SPS and the noodles were analyzed by an FTIR spectrometer (Spectrum100, PerkinElmer, Inc., MA, USA) equipped with an ATR sensor. The samples were analyzed with 32 scans at a resolution of 4 cm−1 in the wavenumber range of 600–4000 cm−1. Then, OMNIC software (version 9.2.86, Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., MA, USA) was used to deconvolve the acquired data with a bandwidth of 40 cm−1 and an enhancement factor of 1.9.

Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC)

The thermal behaviors of SPS and sweet potato starch noodles were measured using a differential scanning calorimeter (DSC4000, PerkinElmer, Inc., MA, USA). 5 mg of powdered noodle and 15 μL deionized water were mixed and equilibrated at room temperature overnight before analysis. Then, the samples were placed in an aluminum DSC pan and scanned from 25 °C to 100 °C at a heating rate of 10 °C/min. The onset temperature (To), peak temperature (Tp), conclusion temperature (Tc), and gelatinization enthalpy (ΔH) were calculated by using the PerkinElmer Pyris software (Ma et al., 2024).

RVA of the ground noodle samples

Pasting parameters of purple sweet potato starch noodles were determined using a Rapid Visco Analyzer (RVA-Tec Master, Perten Instruments AB, Stockholm, Sweden). According to the Standards of the People's Republic of China (NY/T 1753–2009), 3 g of the powdered sample was mixed with 35 mL of distilled water to prepare a suspension. Then, the suspension was poured into an aluminum canister. The test conditions were applied as described in Section 2.3.

Characterizing physical properties of cooked sweet potato starch noodles

Microstructures

Dried purple sweet potato starch noodles (2 g) were cooked in boiling water (200 mL, 100 °C) without a lid for 15 min. The cooked samples were immediately transferred to an −80 °C refrigerator (DW-86 L626, Qingdao Haier Special Electric Appliance Co., Ltd., Shangdong, China) and freeze-dried for 48 h using a freeze-drier (Scientz-10 N/C, Ningbo Scientz Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Zhejiang, China). The samples were manually broken to obtain a cross-section, then cut transversely with a sharp blade. The sample was adhered to an aluminum stub with conductive double-sided adhesive tape. Before observation, the samples were coated with a thin layer of gold (approximately 10 nm) using a sputter coater (Quorum Q300TD, Quorum Technologies Ltd., Lewes, United Kingdom) for 50 s with sputter current of 15 mA. Images of the microstructure were obtained using a field-emission scanning electron microscope (Sigma 360, Carl Zeiss, Leicester, United Kingdom) under an accelerating voltage of 2.0 kV with 500 × magnification.

Textural analysis

Prior to analysis, dried noodles were cooked in boiling water (100 °C) for 15 min and soaked in cool deionized water (15–20 °C) for 30 s. Texture profile analysis (TPA) was performed using a texture analyzer (TA.XT Plus, Stable Micro Systems, Surrey, UK) with a P/36 R probe, following method of Hu et al. (2023) with modifications. TPA parameters were set as follows: pre-measurement rate, test rate, post-measurement rate, trigger force, and compression ratio at 1.0 mm/s, 1.0 mm/s, 1.0 mm/s, 5.0 g, and 50%, respectively. Hardness (g), adhesiveness (g·s), springiness, cohesiveness, gumminess, chewiness, and resilience were recorded. Tensile properties were analyzed using the same texture analyzer equipped with a tensile testing device (TA. XT Plus, Stable Micro Systems, Surrey, UK). Parameters included pre-test rate, test rate, post-test rate, trigger force, and drawing distance set to 5.0 mm/s, 1.0 mm/s, 10.0 mm/s, 5.0 g, and 80.0 mm, respectively. Each tensile analysis was repeated at least four times.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate or more, with data presented as mean ± standard deviation. Statistical analysis was conducted using one-way ANOVA with Tukey's post hoc test (p < 0.05) via SPSS 27.0 (IBM Corp., Chicago, IL, USA). Independent-sample t-test (p < 0.05) was conducted to determine the differences between two groups. OriginPro 2025 (OriginLab Corp., Northampton, MA, USA) was used for plotting, spectral analysis, and Pearson correlation analysis of sweet potato starch noodle properties.

The average particle size reflects colloidal stability, with larger sizes indicating instability due to increased gravitational separation (Herrman et al., 2022). The elevated particle size of P3.0 and AP3.0 likely stems from larger-scale structures formed via hydrogen bonding, hydrophobic interactions, and electrostatic forces between CP and CP-PSPA (Xing et al., 2023). Similarly, Xing et al. (2025) observed that rhamnogalacturonan-I-anthocyanin complexes (1000–2500 nm) exhibited smaller particle sizes than homogalacturonan-anthocyanin complexes (3000–4000 nm), attributing this difference to the branched structure of rhamnogalacturonan-I promoting intramolecular binding and aggregation.

ζ-Potential reflects sample stability. P0.5 and AP0.5 showed significantly higher ζ-potentials (−14.57 mV and − 12.03 mV, respectively) than other samples (p < 0.05), while increasing CP concentration decreased ζ-potential (p < 0.05). P3.0, AP3.0, and ANT exhibited similar low ζ-potentials (p > 0.05). ζ-Potentials near ±30 mV indicate strong electrostatic repulsion and high stability, whereas values close to 0 mV suggest instability (Herrman et al., 2022). The ζ-potential shift from −14.57 mV (P0.5) to −12.03 mV (AP0.5) with increasing CP concentration (0.5–3.0%, w/v) might result from aggregation, reducing surface area and altering surface charge intensity. Elmizadeh et al. (2024) reported that pectin had pKa of 3.5, and pH > pKa enhanced negative charge and stability. In our study, P3.0 (pH 2.53) and P0.5 (pH 2.96) had pH values below pKa, with P0.5's slightly higher pH potentially explaining its higher ζ-potential. In contrast, prior studies linked increasing pectin concentration to more negative ζ-potentials (Elmizadeh et al., 2024). Our results suggested PSPA introduced positive charge, neutralizing CP's ζ-potential, with 0.5% CP yielding greater stability (−12.03 mV) than 3% CP. These results also suggest that pH conditions might play a critical role on CP-PSPA interactions; therefore, the behavior of these complexes under different pH conditions should be further investigated for broader food applications.

SAXS analysis of CP-PSPA complexes in solutions

SAXS analysis (Fig. 1C-D) revealed interactions between CP and PSPA molecules in solution. In the low q range (0.01–0.23 nm−1), samples P3.0 and AP3.0 exhibited higher scattering intensity than P0.5, AP0.5, and ANT, indicating stronger intermolecular aggregation. ANT showed a significantly smaller radius of gyration (Rg, 1.62 nm) than CP-loaded samples (p < 0.05). Among these, P3.0 (2.21 nm) had a higher Rg than P0.5 (2.17 nm), suggesting increased aggregation. Conversely, AP0.5 (2.26 nm) had a higher Rg than AP3.0 (2.18 nm) (p > 0.05), likely due to CP-PSPA interactions forming more compact complexes. Rg reflects structural dimension and aggregation state of the polymer chain, with smaller values indicating greater structure compactness (Du et al., 2025). The observed trends highlighted the role of CP in modulating PSPA aggregation through molecular interactions.

Adsorption isotherm

The binding capacity of PSPA to 0.5% (w/v) and 3.0% (w/v) CP was determined after 24 h incubation at 4 °C and pH 4.0 (Fig. 1E). No significant difference in the bound anthocyanin ratio was observed between AP0.5 (28.91 ± 0.80%) and P3.0 (28.08 ± 1.35%) (p > 0.05). It indicated that the binding sites between pectin and anthocyanin had been fully saturated at the concentration of 0.5%. Therefore, selecting 0.5% CP for subsequent adsorption isotherm analysis not only ensures the full progress of the binding reaction, but also avoids the excessive viscosity interference of high-concentration CP on the isotherm test, which makes the test results more accurate and reliable. Furthermore, AP0.5 exhibited a smaller particle size and a higher absolute zeta potential value than AP3.0 (Section 3.1.1), indicating improved colloidal stability. Therefore, 0.5% CP was selected for the adsorption isotherm analysis.

The Freundlich isotherm (Fig. 1F) was employed to model the interaction between bound anthocyanin (mg/g pectin) and free anthocyanin concentration (mg/L), based on four replicate measurements. The data showed that bound anthocyanin increased with free anthocyanin concentration, but failed to fit the Langmuir model, which assumes monolayer adsorption without intramolecular interactions (Du et al., 2025). In contrast, the Freundlich model accounts for multilayer adsorption on heterogeneous sites, providing insights into surface heterogeneity and adsorption intensity via the parameter 1/n. Values of 1/n < 1 indicate high affinity, 1/n > 1 low affinity, and 1/n = 1 non-reversible adsorption (Gao et al., 2013). A higher 1/n value further reflects greater surface heterogeneity.

The good fit of the Freundlich model (R2 = 0.903) suggested heterogeneous multilayer adsorption in the PSPA-CP system. The 1/n value (1.313) indicated higher adsorption intensity and lower surface heterogeneity compared to microporous resins (KF = 1–6.19 L/g, 1/n = 1.85–1.67) used in prior PSPA adsorption studies at 30 °C (Liu et al., 2007). This enhanced intensity likely stems from strong PSPA-CP interactions. However, our results differ from anthocyanin-chitosan/alginate bead studies (25 °C) (Pinheiro et al., 2021), where chitosan (KF = 0.51 mg/g, 1/n = 1.11) and alginate (KF = 36.7 mg/g, 1/n = 0.21) exhibited higher adsorption intensity or lower heterogeneity, respectively. In contrast, Koh et al. (2020) found the Langmuir model better described anthocyanin adsorption to blueberry pectin (KL = 0.0016–0.0021). These comparisons implied that CP's adsorption behavior aligned with other polymers despite source-dependent variations.

Effects of CP and PSPA on pasting properties of sweet potato starch

As shown in Fig. 2, the pasting curve of SPS exhibited a typical profile, with a distinct breakdown phase following peak viscosity. The incorporation of CP, PSPA, or their combination primarily influenced peak viscosity (PV), trough viscosity (TV), and final viscosity (FV), while having a negligible effect on pasting onset. Pasting properties of the tested samples are summarized in Table S3. Among these, the control sample (CTRL) and ANT showed no significant differences (p > 0.05). The PV of sweet potato starch was 5749.0 cP, which was not significantly affected by PSPA addition (ANT). However, CP addition significantly reduced PV. This reduction was attributed to pectin's higher water-binding capacity compared to starch (Mahmood et al., 2017), which inhibited starch granule swelling and reduced leached amylose in the aqueous phase. Similarly, Ma et al. (2019) reported a decrease in PV of corn starch from 1219 cP to 1134 cP with 0.5% (w/w) pectin due to suppressed granule swelling. In contrast, higher pectin concentrations (2.0%–10.0% w/w) increased PV (1261 cP to 1696 cP) through pectin's thickening effect in the continuous phase, unlike our observation with 3% (w/w) CP. This discrepancy might stem from pectin source differences. Trough viscosity (TV) reflects granule disintegration under shear and heating. While TV was marginally affected by PSPA (ANT) and 0.5% CP (P0.5) (p > 0.05), it significantly decreased with 3.0% CP (P3.0) and the combined addition of CP and PSPA (AP3.0). Effects of CP and PSPA on the short-range ordered structure of dried starch noodles

As shown in Fig. 5C, SPS and sweet potato starch noodles exhibited similar FT-IR characteristic bands. The broad absorption at 3000–3600 cm−1 corresponded to O—H groups in hydrogen-bonded starch molecules, with the band broadening attributed to overlapping N—H absorption and O—H frequency shifts due to intramolecular/intermolecular hydrogen bonding. CP addition enhanced infrared absorption in this region compared to the control (CTRL), with AP3.0 showing the most pronounced band, likely due to non-covalent interactions among PSPA, CP, and starch, including hydrogen bonds (Liu et al., 2024). Bands at 2930 cm−1 (anti-symmetrical CH2 stretching) and 1642 cm−1 (amorphous region absorption) were observed, alongside a band around 1415 cm−1 assigned to C-O-O stretching (Zhang et al., 2020). To analyze short-range ordered structure, spectra in 1200–900 cm−1 were deconvoluted (Fig. 5D). Bands around 995 cm−1 and 1047 cm−1 were linked to starch crystallinity and molecular order, while 1022 cm−1 corresponded to amorphous/disordered regions (Mutungi et al., 2011; Yang et al., 2021). The ratios R1047/1022 and R1022/995 reflected crystalline order and molecular disorder, respectively (Mutungi et al., 2011). As shown in Table 2, no significant differences in R1047/1022 were observed between SPS and CTRL (p > 0.05), or among most starch noodle samples (except AP3.0). AP3.0 exhibited a lower R1047/1022 value, indicating that CP-PSPA interactions hindered starch chain alignment during starch noodle formation. Additionally, R1022/995 showed no significant differences among SPS and all noodle samples (p > 0.05).

Effects of CP and PSPA on thermal properties of dried starch noodles

The DSC curves of SPS and sweet potato starch noodles are shown in Fig. 5E. Both SPS and all noodle samples displayed a single endothermic peak. The SPS peak was sharp and intense, whereas noodle peaks were broader and of lower intensity, likely due to reduced crystalline order requiring less thermal energy for gelatinization.

Thermal properties are summarized in Table 2. The onset (To), peak (Tp), and conclusion (Tc) temperatures reflect the melting of starch microcrystals with varying thermal stability, while enthalpy change (ΔH) corresponds to the energy required for double-helix and crystalline region melting (Ma et al., 2024). To among SPS, CTRL, and other noodle samples showed no significant differences (p > 0.05). However, SPS exhibited significantly higher Tp (74.53 °C) and Tc (82.19 °C) than CTRL (Tp: 63.55 °C; Tc: 70.37 °C) (p < 0.05). Processing-induced disruption of crystalline structure and formation of short-chain starch molecules led to a decrease in Tp and Tc of noodles (Fang et al., 2025). The addition of CP or ANT alone did not significantly alter Tp or Tc versus CTRL (p > 0.05).

ΔH values were comparable between SPS and most noodle samples, except for AP0.5, which showed a significantly lower ΔH (0.23 J/g) than SPS (1.98 J/g) (p < 0.05). This observation is partially supported by the reduced relative crystallinity of AP0.5, while the unchanged short-range order parameters from FTIR indicate that the thermal behavior cannot be fully explained by molecular order variations. It suggests that specific interactions between starch and the low-concentration (0.5% w/w) CP-PSPA complex may reduce ΔH. In contrast, a high CP concentration (3% w/w) might promote CP self-aggregation over interaction with amylose.

Effects of CP and PSPA on pasting properties of dried starch noodles

RVA analysis was conducted to assess viscosity behavior, which reflects water absorption and amylose leaching during cooking and correlated with gel network formation and amylose rearrangement. As shown in Fig. 5F, pasting curves of sweet potato starch noodles were characterized and altered by CP, PSPA, or their combination. The curves clustered into three distinct groups. The first group (CTRL) showed a gradual, less pronounced viscosity increase likely due to limited amylose leaching and particle swelling (Ma et al., 2024). It would be resulted from its relatively intact structure, as observed in SEM images (Fig. 3B). The second group (P3.0 and AP3.0, containing 3% w/w CP) exhibited a marked viscosity rise versus CTRL. This was attributed to water-absorption capacity of CP (Mahmood et al., 2017) and CP-amylose interactions that disrupted structure, and promoted swelling and enhanced the pasting profile. The third group (PSPA and 0.5% w/w CP samples) presented the highest pasting curves, surpassing both CTRL and 3% CP groups. This was associated with nanoscale CP-PSPA complexes formed via non-covalent interactions, which increased the effective surface area for water contact.

Effects of CP and PSPA on the physical properties of cooked sweet potato starch noodles

Effects of CP and PSPA on microstructures of cooked starch noodles

Fig. 3B shows cross-section images of cooked and freeze-dried sweet potato starch noodles. All samples exhibited porous structures due to freeze-drying-induced water evaporation, but pore morphology varied with CP and/or PSPA addition. The control (CTRL) had a denser structure than other samples, while ANT displayed larger, irregularly shaped pores, likely due to disrupted starch gel networks from limited amylose-amylose interactions. Similarly, Ma et al. (2021) reported that anthocyanin from black soybean coats reduced the three-dimensional network of wheat noodles. CP addition (0.5% and 3%, w/w) further increased pore size versus CTRL, with 3% CP showing the largest and most irregular pores. Gelatinized starch formed a gel network via amylose/amylopectin association, whereas hydrocolloids created a distinct network by interacting with amylose and low-molecular-weight amylopectin during gelatinization (Srikaeo et al., 2018). The type and concentration of hydrocolloids critically influenced the starch gel network, which in turn governed noodle properties. Srikaeo et al. (2018) observed that carboxymethyl cellulose, xanthan gum, and guar gum disrupted the dense structure of rice noodles compared to controls.

Effects of CP and PSPA on the texture properties of cooked starch noodles

The textural properties of sweet potato starch noodles are presented in Table 1. Hardness significantly (p < 0.05) decreased with PSPA addition compared to the control (CTRL), likely due to PSPA disrupting the starch gel network by inhibiting amylose re-association via hydrogen bonding, resulting in a softer structure. Moreover, the hardness significantly decreased from 5367.78 g (CTRL) to 4299.63 g when supplemented with 0.5% (w/w) CP (p < 0.05). By contrast, the 3% (w/w) CP group showed no significant difference in hardness versus the CTRL group (p > 0.05). These different results suggesting a concentration-dependent effect of CP. Increasing polysaccharide concentration might enhance intermolecular entanglement and binding sites, potentially elevating hardness (Mahmood et al., 2017). AP3.0 exhibited higher hardness than ANT (p < 0.05), whereas 0.5% CP addition did not alter hardness compared to ANT. Adhesiveness was similar between ANT and CTRL (p > 0.05), and unaffected by adding 0.5% or 3% CP versus CTRL (p > 0.05). However, P3.0 (−188.89 g·s) showed significantly higher adhesiveness than P0.5 (−114.42 g·s) (p < 0.05). It was attributed to increased surface free water from CP hydration, creating a sticky surface during probe withdrawal. Springiness did not differ significantly between CTRL and other noodles (p > 0.05), but P0.5 (0.73) differed from P3.0 (0.93) (p < 0.05). This higher springiness in P3.0 might stem from its more ordered crystalline structure. Cohesiveness was similar among samples except AP3.0, which had higher cohesiveness (0.94) than CTRL (0.84) (p < 0.05), likely due to enhanced intermolecular entanglement and cross-linking (Mahmood et al., 2017). Chewiness significantly decreased with PSPA versus CTRL (p < 0.05). P0.5 (2750.98 g·s) had lower chewiness than CTRL (4076.09 g·s), while P3.0 showed no difference versus CTRL (p > 0.05). AP3.0 (4323.27 g·s) exhibited higher chewiness than ANT (2232.12 g·s) (p < 0.05), reflecting the energy required to chew noodles to a swallowable state (Ma et al., 2021). Resilience, indicating elastic response to deformation, showed no significant differences among samples (p > 0.05).

Effects of CP and PSPA on the tensile properties of cooked starch noodles

Tensile testing evaluated the impact of PSPA and CP on sweet potato starch noodle stretching properties. Force and extensibility data are presented in Table 1. No significant differences were observed between the control (CTRL) and ANT (p > 0.05), but P0.5 (331.75 g) and P3.0 (153.10 g) exhibited significantly lower tensile force than CTRL (417.67 g) (p < 0.05). Additionally, 3% (w/w) CP reduced tensile force to 266.12 ± 24.61 g in PSPA-loaded noodles versus ANT (448.33 ± 30.43 g), while AP0.5 showed higher force (533.46 g) than ANT (p < 0.05). Extensibility was significantly lower in ANT (22.27 ± 3.16 mm) than CTRL (29.05 mm), with P0.5 (21.11 mm) and P3.0 (19.84 mm) also reduced versus CTRL (p < 0.05). However, 0.5% (w/w) CP increased extensibility to 31.74 mm compared to ANT (p < 0.05). CP likely enhanced water absorption, yielding a looser noodle structure (Mahmood et al., 2017). Conversely, CP-PSPA interactions (e.g., van der Waals forces, hydrogen bonds, electrostatic interactions) between starch-CP and PSPA-CP might elevate force and extensibility (He et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2023). Lower crystallinity, as indicated by our XRD data (Table 2), could reduce tensile strength and extensibility (Kraithong & Rawdkuen, 2020). Supporting this, Kraithong and Rawdkuen (2020) observed that 0.4% (w/w) xanthan gum decreased extruded red Jasmine rice flour noodle tensile strength from 65.45 g to 53.19 g.

Correlation between the structure and properties of sweet potato starch noodles

Pearson correlation analysis was performed to evaluate relationships between microstructural properties and starch noodle quality (Fig. 6). The periodic thickness of starch lamellae (dBragg) was positively correlated with cooking loss (r = 0.82, p < 0.05) and cooking yield (r = 0.91, p < 0.05), suggesting that lamellar structure influences water interaction and component retention during cooking. A negative correlation was observed between R1022/995 (indicative of molecular disorder) and chewiness (r = −0.89, p < 0.05). Higher R1022/995 values corresponded to a more disordered starch structure and weaker gel network, resulting in lower chewiness. This finding aligned with Ma et al. Conclusion

In this study, citrus peel pectin (CP) was confirmed to enhance purple sweet potato anthocyanin (PSPA) retention in sweet potato starch noodles while maintaining noodle quality. This result was attributed to the complex formation between CP and PSPA, supported by evidence from particle size and zeta potential measurements and small-angle X-ray scattering analysis. PSPA exhibited heterogeneous multilayer adsorption on CP, which was well-fitted by the Freundlich isotherm model. Notably, AP0.5 (PSPA and 0.5% CP) formed a nano-scale complex, and its noodle demonstrated improved PSPA retention, with overall quality comparable to the control. Moreover, 3% CP ‌brought no extra functional benefits to justify its high addition levels, although CP concentration influenced certain noodle quality attributes. Using a lower CP concentration is advantageous from an industrial perspective by reducing CP consumption while maintaining comparable adsorption performance. This finding provides nutritional and industrial significance in terms of the anthocyanin enrichment and the color quality enhancement of starch noodles, which are valuable for consumer acceptance and industrial implementation. Nevertheless, this study has certain limitation. Specifically, CP–PSPA interactions are evaluated only at their natural pH, which may restrict the generalization and application of the complex across food products with different pH environments. To further promote PSPA retention and improve the quality of the PSPA-loaded starch noodles, several points could be considered in future research, such as (1) strengthening the CP-PSPA binding interface through polysaccharide structure tailoring and/or physical treatments (e.g., ultrasound-assisted complexation) to improve anthocyanin encapsulation efficiency; and (2) improving the textural properties of PSPA-loaded starch noodles via cross-linking techniques (e.g., Ca2+ cross-linking) to mitigate cooking-induced softening.
