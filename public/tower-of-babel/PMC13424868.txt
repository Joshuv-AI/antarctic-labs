Single-center Experience with Comprehensive Genomic Profiling Using Circulating Tumor DNA for Metastatic Colorectal Cancer
Authors: Kazuma Ito, Kozo Kataoka, Yoshiko Muroi, Mizuki Koba, Yuko Fukumoto, Ayako Imada, Song Jihyung, Zhenxin Rao, Hiroki Osumi, Motoi Uchino, Kei Kimura, Masataka Ikeda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Comprehensive genomic profiling (CGP) using circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) has recently become available in Japan, but its clinical utility in daily practice remains unclear. We retrospectively evaluated 19 patients with metastatic colorectal cancer (mCRC) who underwent plasma-based CGP (Guardant360 CDx) between November 2023 and May 2025. ctDNA was detected in 18 of 19 patients (95%) with previously treated mCRC. The median turnaround time from blood collection to result was 11 days (range: 7–15). The median number of gene alterations was 5 (range: 1–29), and pathogenic/likely pathogenic alterations were identified in 15 patients (79%). ctDNA was preferentially selected over tissue CGP in patients with small tumor burden, lack of contemporary tumor sample, or double cancer (considering spatial and temporal heterogeneity). Three patients initially identified with RAS mutant tumors by tissue were subsequently identified as RAS wild-type by plasma CGP. Those were treated with anti-epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) therapy, resulting in partial response or stable disease. These observations suggest that plasma-based CGP may provide additional molecular information that could be clinically informative in mCRC in a real-world setting. Although the findings should be interpreted as exploratory, re-evaluation of molecular profiles using ctDNA enabled the exploratory identification of NeoRAS wild-type patients, who could potentially benefit from anti-EGFR therapy.

Introduction

Comprehensive genomic profiling (CGP) has been approved and reimbursed by national health insurance in Japan since 2019 for advanced cancers, including metastatic colorectal cancer (mCRC). Initially, CGP was limited to tissue-based testing; however, plasma-based CGP testing obtained regulatory approval in 2022. CGP is allowed only once in a lifetime and simultaneous or sequential use of tissue and plasma CGP is not allowed by public insurance in Japan. This unique situation requires us to carefully examine the patients for the timing and selection of CGP. These conditions contrast sharply with international guidelines[1,2], which allow consideration of CGP prior to first-line treatment, further testing during subsequent lines of therapy, and sequential use of tissue- and plasma-based CGP.

Circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) testing offers several advantages over tissue-based testing such as providing a minimally invasive alternative to tissue, especially in cases where tissue samples are difficult to obtain or unsuitable for genomic analysis[3]. For mCRC, the detection frequency of actionable molecular profiles is similar between ctDNA and standard tissue-based testing[4]. Plasma-based CGP can capture dynamic and comprehensive molecular landscape of tumors, reflecting both spatial and temporal tumor heterogeneity, with a shorter test turnaround time compared with tissue-based CGP[5]. However, plasma-based assays have limitations: low tumor burden or minimal shedding of tumor DNA into the bloodstream may lead to undetectable levels of ctDNA, resulting in false-negative findings[6].

In Japan, 89% of solid cancer patients receive standard tissue testing only and 11% receive CGP[7]. Tissue-based CGP is more frequently used and recommended over plasma-based tests under the domestic guideline and plasma-based CGP using ctDNA is recommended only when tissue acquisition is infeasible or when there is a technical issue with the tissue. However, clinical studies in Japan have shown that ctDNA detects actionable molecular profiles frequently found in mCRC at rates similar to those for tissue tests[8] and improves enrollment in studies of targeted therapies[3] with clinical outcomes similar to those for patients who receive targeted therapy based on tumor tissue testing[9]. Nevertheless, real-world evidence on the use and impact of plasma-based CGP in routine clinical practice in Japan remains limited. Against this background, we report our institutional experience with real-world plasma-based CGP in patients with mCRC. This study was designed as a single-center retrospective case series, including descriptive cohort findings, exploratory comparative analysis, and case presentations.

Methods

This study was conducted as a single-center retrospective case series of mCRC patients who underwent plasma-based CGP during routine clinical care. mCRC patients who underwent plasma-based CGP (Guardant360 CDx, Guardant Health) between November 2023 and May 2025 were retrospectively evaluated. Clinical backgrounds, reasons for selecting plasma-based CGP, detection rate of ctDNA, and actionable gene alterations were assessed. Oncogenic, likely oncogenic, and known resistance alterations were considered clinically informative. Among them, patients with RAS mutations identified from the primary tumor who were initially identified as RAS wild-type with ctDNA after at least one line of systemic therapy were classified as NeoRAS and considered to have an actionable molecular profile. RAS wild-type was defined as cases with ctDNA detected however without any oncogenic KRAS/NRAS variants (KRAS/NRAS Exon2 [codon12, 13], Exon3 [codon59, 61], Exon4 [codon117, 146]). Tumor response was assessed according to the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) version 1.1. Comparisons between groups were conducted using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test for continuous variables and Fisher's exact test for categorical variables. This retrospective study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Hyogo University Hospital (No. 4976). All aspects of the study design, data collection, data management, and analysis were conducted independently by the investigators at Hyogo Medical University.

Results

Patient characteristics

Between November 2023 and May 2025, 19 mCRC patients underwent plasma-based CGP (Table 1). The most common sites of metastasis were liver (13 patients) and peritoneum (11 patients). CGP was most frequently performed before initiation of third-line treatment (14 patients), while the remainder received testing during later-line treatment. The association between metastatic sites and tumor burden was shown in the Supplementary Table 1. The patients with liver metastases had higher Max VAF compared to those without liver metastases. No significant differences were observed according to lung, lymph node, or peritoneal metastasis.

Plasma-based CGP was selected over tissue-based CGP in these patients based on several factors. Nine patients were unsuitable for tissue-based CGP owing to insufficient amount of DNA isolated from small biopsy specimens of unresected primary lesions (#5, #6, #8, #12, #13, #15, #16, #17, #19). In four patients, more than three years had elapsed since tissue sampling, suggesting quality of tissue specimen may be deteriorated during the storage (#2, #3, #7, #9). In four patients, tissue specimens may carry the damages affected by chemotherapy received prior to sampling (#4, #10, #11 and #14). In two patients with double cancer, plasma-based CGP was chosen to account for tumor heterogeneity and tissue-based CGP cannot be used for both tumors under insurance (#1 and #18). Details of systemic therapy and examination intervals between tissue RAS and plasma-CGP, and the brief summary of systemic therapies were provided in the Supplementary Table 2. Median interval between tissue and liquid tests was 17 months, and median number of therapies was 2 lines.

Genomic findings

ctDNA was detected in 18 out of 19 patients (95%) with median turnaround time from blood collection to test results of 11 days (range, 7 to 15 days). ctDNA was not detected in one patient with only a small peritoneal nodule. Among patients with ctDNA detected, the median number of gene alterations was 5 (range:1–29). Gene alterations classified as “Oncogenic” or “Likely Oncogenic” were identified in 15 of the 19 patients (Figure 1, Table 2). Five patients received targeted therapies based on CGP results (KRAS G12C, n=1; ERBB2 amplification, n=1; NeoRAS wild-type, n=3). One patient with ERBB2 amplification detected by CGP had a history of ERBB2 amplification by tumor tissue analysis. Because many of these patients were referred to our hospital, subsequent enrollment in clinical trials is unknown.Among those, three patients with RAS mutations detected from the primary tumor were identified as RAS wild-type based on the plasma-based CGP results (Table 3). ctDNA other than RAS mutation was detected in all these patients.

Case presentations of NeoRAS (N=3)

We identified three patients with tissue RAS mutations at diagnosis who were diagnosed later as RAS wild-type by plasma-based CGP (NeoRAS) and treated with anti- (epidermal growth factor receptor) EGFR monoclonal antibodies. Patient characteristics of patients with NeoRAS and non-NeoRAS are shown in Table 1.

#5

A 64-year-old man underwent robot-assisted intersphincteric resection for lower rectal cancer. The pathological diagnosis was pT4N2M0, pStageIII, with KRAS G13D mutation, BRAFV600 wild-type, and proficient mismatch repair (pMMR). Postoperatively, the patient received adjuvant chemotherapy with eight courses of capecitabine plus oxaliplatin (CAPOX). Two years after surgery, follow-up computed tomography (CT) revealed peritoneal dissemination. First-line chemotherapy with modified fluorouracil, leucovorin, and oxaliplatin (mFOLFOX6) + bevacizumab (Bmab) was initiated. Following nine courses, progression of peritoneal dissemination was observed. Second-line therapy with fluorouracil, leucovorin, and irinotecan (FOLFIRI) + Bmab was started. During treatment, plasma-based CGP test was performed without detection of KRAS or NRAS mutations but with FGFR2 mutation (Variant of Uncertain Significance; VUS) at VAF (variant allele frequency) 5.2%. After 15 courses of FOLFIRI + Bmab, serum tumor markers increased and ascites progressed. Tissue-based CGP using the resected primary tumor was initially intended, but the tissue specimen was not suitable for analysis. Therefore, plasma-based CGP was performed instead. The regimen was switched to FOLFIRI + panitumumab (Pmab). After four courses, tumor markers stabilized, and the disease remained controlled (Figure 2a).

Treatment and tumor marker transition (#5; 2a, #6; 2b, #13; 2c).

#7

An 83-year-old male was diagnosed with ascending colon cancer and multiple liver metastases. KRAS G12D mutant, BRAFV600 wild-type and microsatellite stable (MSS) were identified by tissue biopsy. Six cycles of mFOLFOX6 + Bmab were performed followed by hepatic resection and ileocecal resection. Postoperatively, recurrence of lung metastasis was observed, and mFOLFOX6 + Bmab was reintroduced. However, after 4 courses of mFOLFOX6 + Bmab, CT showed progression of lung metastasis. The patient was treated with FOLFIRI + Bmab for 11 courses but experienced progression of lung metastasis, recurrence of liver metastasis and peritoneal dissemination. Plasma-based CGP testing was performed, and mutations of TP53 (VAF: 3.76%), APC (VAF: 3.78%), EGFR amplification (copy number: 2.2), and BRCA1 (VAF: 0.4%) were detected without KRAS or NRAS mutations. As 37 months had passed since the resection of the primary tumor, plasma-based CGP was selected due to concerns regarding tissue preservation. Irinotecan (CPT-11) + Pmab was administered as 3rd-line treatment. After 4 courses, CT showed reduction in lung metastases, liver metastases and peritoneal dissemination. After 7 courses of chemotherapy, tumor markers significantly elevated (Figure 2b) and chemotherapy was changed to trifluridine/tipiracil (FTD/TPI) + Bmab.

#13

A 74-year-old woman was diagnosed with descending colon cancer with pancreatic and renal invasion and peritoneal dissemination. KRAS G13D mutant, BRAFV600 wild-type and MSS were detected in tissue biopsy. The patient was treated with 5-fluorouracil, leucovorin, oxaliplatin, and irinotecan (FOLFOXIRI) + Bmab after transverse colostomy. CT showed progression of primary tumor and peritoneal dissemination after 8 courses chemotherapy. Plasma-based CGP testing was performed because of insufficient tumor volume from biopsy. BRCA2 L139L (VAF: 1.33%, synonymous variant), and RET L712L (VAF: 0.22%, synonymous variant) were identified without mutations in KRAS or NRAS. CT after 6 courses of FOLFIRI + Pmab showed stable disease in primary tumor and peritoneal dissemination (Figure 2c).

Discussion

In Japan, a unique constraint is imposed by the national insurance system, under which CGP testing can in principle be performed only once in a lifetime, and only after standard treatment has been completed or is expected to be completed. Moreover, Plasma-based CGP can be considered only when tissue-based CGP is not available or considered to be inappropriate; this may lead to lack of real-world evidence from Japan in plasma-based CGP, whose diagnostic performance is similar with tissue-based CGP[3]. Furthermore, in colorectal cancer treatment, it has been reported that consideration of spatial and temporal heterogeneity is essential, as a single-site tissue biopsy may fail to adequately reflect the overall disease status. DNA testing from tumor samples may not reflect the molecular changes that have sometimes occurred after systemic targeted therapy or chemotherapy. On the other hand, liquid biopsy, being minimally invasive and requiring only blood sampling, offers the advantage of capturing ctDNA derived from the entire tumor burden, thereby enabling timely molecular re-assessment. In this cohort, the median turnaround time was 11 days, allowing rapid detection of dynamic changes in molecular profiles[4]. In the MRD (molecular residual disease) setting, in cases with low tumor burden, ctDNA may not be detected, which raises the risk of false-negative results. In particularly, patients with small (e.g. less than 2 cm) peritoneal dissemination or lung metastases alone have been reported to exhibit lower ctDNA detection rates[6]. However, the majority of patients with metastatic CRC affecting only lung or peritoneum will have ctDNA detected. In our series, patients with liver metastasis showed significantly higher maxVAF values compared with those without liver involvement. This finding is consistent with previous reports suggesting that ctDNA shedding is influenced by tumor burden and metastatic sites. In our cohort, ctDNA was detected in 18 out of 19 patients (95%), with very short turnaround time of 11 days, supporting the feasibility of plasma-based CGP in routine clinical practice.

In our cohort, three patients were considered to have the NeoRAS wild-type, in which patients initially diagnosed with RAS mutation in tumor tissue were subsequently showed no detectable KRAS or NRAS mutations in ctDNA after at least one line of systemic therapy. Several feasibility and clinical trial-based studies have described treatment outcomes in patients with NeoRAS wild-type[10,11]. Osumi et al. reported six NeoRAS patients treated with panitumumab or cetuximab, with one partial response and two patients of stable disease[10]. Albuquerque et al. reported a disease control rate of 44.4% with anti-EGFR antibodies in NeoRAS patients[12]. Reported incidence varies from 1.4% to 41.7%, depending on ctDNA sensitivity, metastatic sites, and tumor burden[13]. However, real-world data are limited. In the present study, among 19 plasma-based CGP testing three of 14 patients (21.4%) with a history of RAS mutations by tissue and/or plasma-based hotspot testing were identified as RAS wild-type (NeoRAS) by ctDNA. Introduction of anti-EGFR monoclonal antibodies in these patients was associated with partial response or stable disease, which was in line with previous reports. This current report may provide clinically meaningful real-world evidence indicating that re-evaluation of molecular profiles under the constraints of clinical practice may improve treatment strategies. Larger scale analysis is warranted. A single-arm, phase II trial to assess the efficacy and safety of Pmab plus irinotecan for patients with NeoRAS wild-type mCRC is ongoing[14]. In addition to informing treatment decisions such as anti-EGFR therapy, CGP testing may also facilitate identification of patients eligible for clinical trials targeting specific genomic alterations. In our cohort, several patients were considered for other targeted therapies based on CGP results, including alterations in KRAS G12C and ERBB2 amplification, although detailed follow-up information regarding trial enrollment was not available because these patients were referred from outside hospitals. These findings highlight the potential role of plasma-based CGP in expanding therapeutic opportunities, including access to molecularly targeted therapies and clinical trials in routine clinical practice.

Aoki et al. reported that Guardant360 CDx showed 88.7% of positive percent agreement with tissue RAS testing and 97.5% in samples with ≥ 1% maxVAF[15]. Low ctDNA shedding or low tumor burden may result in variants in circulation below the limit of detection. When ctDNA does not detect a RAS mutation, it is important to check for other gene alterations, tumor burden, and consistency with imaging and tumor markers.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, this was a retrospective analysis with a relatively small sample size. Second, baseline (prior chemotherapy) ctDNA RAS status and detailed information on other metastatic sites were not available, which may have limited insights into the mechanisms underlying NeoRAS wild-type. Nevertheless, our cohort shows that plasma-based CGP is feasible and provides clinically relevant molecular information for patients with mCRC in a real-world clinical setting in Japan.

In conclusion, plasma-based CGP appears feasible and may provide clinically relevant molecular information in mCRC patients in a real-world setting. Re-evaluation of molecular profiles using ctDNA allowed the exploratory identification of NeoRAS wild-type cases, which might help inform treatment considerations such as anti-EGFR therapy in selected patients. However, given the exploratory and hypothesis-generating nature of this study, these findings should be interpreted with caution. Evaluation of NeoRAS should be performed with careful consideration of tumor burden, including metastatic sites and tumor volume, and with awareness of the potential for false-negative RAS results. Further studies with larger cohorts are warranted to validate these findings.
