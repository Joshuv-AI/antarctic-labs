Central Limit Theorem for Mutation Systems
Liav Koram, Ohad Elishco

Abstract
DNA-based storage has emerged as a promising alternative to traditional data storage methods, offering unmatched advantages in data density, longevity, and sustainability. Two main approaches have developed: in-vitro storage, where information is synthesized in controlled environments, and in-vivo storage, where data is embedded within an organism’s DNA for enhanced confidentiality and protection. While in-vivo DNA storage provides unique advantages, it faces significant challenges from mutations, including duplications, deletions, and substitutions, which cause sequence evolution over time. Thus, in-vivo systems experience continuous sequence alterations that increase length and change composition, making error correction particularly challenging. We study the asymptotic behavior of mutation systems, which model the probabilistic evolution of sequences over a finite alphabet, and are central to the analysis of in vivo DNA-based data storage. Building upon prior works that established the limit of empirical k k -tuple frequencies, we characterize the stochastic fluctuations around these values by establishing a Central Limit Theorem (CLT). Our approach leverages the spectral properties of the k k -substitution matrix to project the centered count vectors, allowing us to approximate the system via a martingale difference sequence, and then verifying the classical martingale CLT conditions. In addition, we explicitly derive the limiting covariance matrix.

Introduction

The study of mutation systems, which describe the evolution of sequences over a finite alphabet through repeated applications of a probabilistic mutation law, has gained increasing prominence in various scientific and technological domains. These systems, characterized by a finite alphabet, an initial word, and a mutation rule defined by probability measures and a random function, offer a mathematical framework for understanding processes where sequences undergo random transformations.
The dynamic nature of these systems, where words evolve step by step through the selection of a random position and the application of the mutation rule, makes them relevant to fields ranging from evolutionary biology to DNA-based data storage

To gain deeper insights into the evolution of mutation systems, a valuable approach involves analyzing the frequency and distribution of

A

As a first step in this line of inquiry, previous research has focused on establishing the limit composition of a sequence under various mutation models.
Using stochastic approximation methods,
[
24
]
investigated the evolution of
k
-tuple frequencies and entropy in systems undergoing duplication and substitution mutations, establishing almost sure convergence.
Later,
[
7
]
studied empirical
k
-tuple frequencies in somewhat more general mutation systems, establishing a limit in probability under mild assumptions.
While these results provide a basic understanding of the eventual deterministic state towards which a system tends, convergence alone (whether almost sure or in probability) does not characterize the stochastic fluctuations around this average or the rate at which convergence occurs.

To further understand the asymptotic behavior of
k
-tuples in mutation systems, it is natural to draw inspiration from the rich literature on limit theorems for general stochastic processes
[
13
]
, and specifically on urn models
[
26
]
.
Urn models, which involve the probabilistic drawing and replacement of balls of different colors, share conceptual similarities with mutation systems.
Both evolve through a sequence of stochastic steps where each transition depends on the system’s current state.
However, while urn models primarily track the aggregate frequency of individual symbols, mutation systems focus on
k
-tuples, meaning the exact sequential order of the symbols is strictly taken into account.

In
[
30
]
, the author provides central limit theorems for both the composition of the urn (the number of balls of each type) and the number of times balls of each type are drawn. These theorems typically rely on the spectral properties (eigenvalues and eigenvectors) of the system’s generating matrix. Given that the previous result on the limit in probability for
k
-tuples also hinges on the spectral properties of a matrix (the
k
-substitution matrix), the techniques and results from
[
30
]
on urn models offer potentially valuable insights and a framework for investigating a central limit theorem for
k
-tuples in mutation systems.
Different replacement rules in urn models might provide parallels to how various mutation laws (e.g., substitution, duplication) could influence the central limit theorem for
k
-tuples.

Building upon
[
7
]
, the natural next step in understanding the stochastic behavior of
k
-tuple frequencies in mutation systems is to seek a central limit theorem (CLT). Establishing such a theorem would provide a more complete characterization of the system’s long-term dynamics by describing the limiting distribution of the fluctuations around the expected frequencies.
A CLT can also offer crucial information about the rate at which the distribution of these frequencies converges to its limiting form.
Furthermore, the power of a CLT lies in its ability to enable statistical inference, such as the construction of confidence intervals and the formulation of hypothesis tests regarding the distribution of
k
-tuples within the evolving sequences.
Determining whether these fluctuations are asymptotically normal is essential for making accurate predictions about sequence compositions.
Therefore, this paper establishes a central limit theorem for
k
-tuple frequencies.
Drawing inspiration from the spectral techniques used in urn models
[
30
,
7
]
, we extend the matrix-based probability limit analysis by leveraging martingale limit theorems.

Preliminaries

In this section, we introduce basic notation and definitions used throughout the paper.

For consistency, we adopt the notation from

Let
\mathcal{A}=\left\{a_{0},\dots,a_{d-1}\right\}

A word (or a string)

We use

For

, we use

to denote all

-tuples, over

.
We denote by

the set of all finite words over
\mathcal{A}
.

For an integer

, we denote
[n]:=\left\{0,1,\dots,n-1\right\}
and for

and

we write

r+I:=\left\{r+j~:~j\in I\right\}
.
For a word

and for

, we denote by

the restriction of

to the positions in
A
.
For

, we also use

to denote
w_{i+[j-i]}=w_{i}w_{i+1}\dots w_{j}
.
If

denotes the empty word.

For

,
uv
denotes the concatenation of
u
and

represents the concatenation of
u
with itself
n
times.
We say that

is a subword (or substring) of
v
if
|u|\leqslant|v|
and there exists
i\in[|v|]
such that
v_{i+[|u|]}=u
,
with coordinates taken modulo

.

We denote by
\mathsf{ct}_{v}(u)
the number of times

appears in
v
as a subword (the count of

), i.e.,

\mathsf{ct}_{v}(u):=\sum_{i=0}^{|v|}\mathbbm{1}_{[u=v_{i+[|u|]}]}

where coordinates are taken modulo
|v|
and

\mathbbm{1}_{[a=b]}=\begin{cases}1&\text{ if }a=b\\
0&\text{ otherwise}\end{cases}

is the indicator function.
We denote by
\mathsf{fr}_{v}(u)
the empirical frequency (empirical probability) of
u
in
v
, that is,

\mathsf{fr}_{v}(u)=\begin{cases}0&\text{ if }|u|>|v|\\
\frac{1}{|v|}\mathsf{ct}_{v}(u)&\text{ if }|u|\leqslant|v|\end{cases}.

For a given
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, the count vector
\mathsf{\mathbf{ct}}^{(k)}_{v}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d^{k}}
has coordinates corresponding to
k
-tuples. For any
u\in\mathcal{A}^{k}
,
\mathsf{\mathbf{ct}}^{(k)}_{v}(u)
represents the count of occurrences of
u
in
v
. When
k
is evident or does not affect the result, we simplify it to
\mathsf{\mathbf{ct}}_{v}
. Similarly,
\mathsf{\mathbf{fr}}^{(k)}_{v}
denotes the vector of empirical frequencies, and when
k
is clear from the context (or does not affect the result) we write
\mathsf{\mathbf{fr}}_{v}
.

We now work our way to define a mutation system.
For a set
A
, let
\mathbf{P}
denote a distribution over
A
. The
support
of
\mathbf{P}
, denoted as
\supp(\mathbf{P})
, is a subset of
A
containing the arguments with positive probability, i.e.,
\supp(\mathbf{P})=\left\{a\in A~:~\mathbf{P}(a)>0\right\}
.

Definition II.1

For an alphabet
\mathcal{A}
of size
d
, a
mutation law
is a pair
(\vartheta,\mathbf{P})
, where
\mathbf{P}=\left\{\mathbf{P}_{a_{t}}\right\}_{t\in[d]}
is a set of
d
non-degenerate, finitely supported probability measures on
\mathcal{A}^{\star}
, and
\vartheta:\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{A}^{\star}
is a (random) function such that
for every
a\in\mathcal{A}
,
\vartheta(a)
is a random word chosen according to
\mathbf{P}_{a}
.

If there exists
\tau\in{\mathbb{R}}
such that for every
a\in\mathcal{A}
,
{\mathbb{E}}\left(\left|\vartheta(a)\right|\right)=\tau
, we say that
(\vartheta,\mathbf{P})
is an

average
\tau
-mutation law
. Meaning, the average length of the word that replaces any symbol is
\tau
.

Let
w\in\mathcal{A}^{m}
be a word of length
m
and consider a mutation law
(\vartheta,\mathbf{P})
. A
mutation step
(or round) on
w
is performed by selecting a position
i\in[m]
uniformly at random, and then applying the random function
\vartheta
on the symbol in this position
\vartheta(w_{i})
,
resulting in a random word
w_{0}\dots w_{i-1}\vartheta(w_{i})w_{i+1}\dots w_{n-1}
.
With a slight abuse of notation, we denote the obtained (random) word by
\vartheta(w)
.
We consider mutation systems which are processes that evolve in time according to a mutation law.

Definition II.2

A
mutation system
is defined by
S=\left(\mathcal{A},w,(\vartheta,\mathbf{P})\right)
, where
\mathcal{A}
is a finite alphabet,

S(0)=w\in\mathcal{A}^{\star}
is a non-empty starting word, and
(\vartheta,\mathbf{P})
is a mutation law defined over
\mathcal{A}
.
We call
S
an
average
\tau
-mutation system
if
(\vartheta,\mathbf{P})
is an
average
\tau
-mutation law
.

A mutation system evolves according to mutation steps and is defined recursively. For
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, set
S(n)=\vartheta(S(n-1))=\vartheta^{n}(w)
, where
\vartheta^{n}(w)=\vartheta(\vartheta(\dots(\vartheta(w))\dots))
is the random word obtained by applying
n
mutation steps, starting with the word
w
.

Let
v_{1},v_{2},\dots,v_{d^{k}}\in\mathcal{A}^{k}
be all the
k
-tuples in lexicographic order. We mark the following expressions with respect to
S(n)
.

1.

Y_{n}=\left|S(n)\right|
denotes the (random) length of
S(n)
.

2.

\mathsf{\mathbf{ct}}_{n}=\mathsf{\mathbf{ct}}_{S(n)}:=\begin{bmatrix}\mathsf{ct}_{S(n)}(v_{1})&\mathsf{ct}_{S(n)}(v_{2})&\dots&\mathsf{ct}_{S(n)}(v_{d^{k}})\end{bmatrix}^{T}
denotes the count (column) vector of
S(n)
.

3.

\mathsf{\mathbf{fr}}_{n}=\mathsf{\mathbf{fr}}_{S(n)}:=\begin{bmatrix}\mathsf{fr}_{S(n)}(v_{1})&\mathsf{fr}_{S(n)}(v_{2})&\dots&\mathsf{fr}_{S(n)}(v_{d^{k}})\end{bmatrix}^{T}
denotes the frequency (column) vector of
S(n)
.

Example II.3

(See

Let
\mathcal{A}=\left\{0,1\right\}
be the binary alphabet, let the starting word be

Definition II.4

Let

-mutation system over the alphabet

The

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}^{(k)}_{u,v}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{l\in{\mathbb{N}}}\sum_{\eta\in\mathcal{A}^{l}}\sum_{t\in[d]}\Pr(\vartheta(a_{t})=\eta)\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\mathbbm{1}_{[v_{j}=a_{t}]}\mathbbm{1}_{[(v_{0}^{j-1}\eta v_{j+1}^{k-1})_{[k]}=u]}+\sum_{j=0}^{l-1}\mathbbm{1}_{[v_{0}=a_{t}]}\mathbbm{1}_{[\eta_{j}^{l-1}v_{1}^{k-l+j}=u]}\right)

(1)

where for

is clear from the context, we write
\mathbf{M}

In the above definition, we assume that

, or in words, we assume that

The same definition holds for smaller

as the empty word and replace

Theorem II.6

[
12

Let
\mathbf{M}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n}
be a non-negative, irreducible square matrix. Then

1.

\rho(\mathbf{M})>0
is a simple eigenvalue of
\mathbf{M}
(called the Perron eigenvalue).

2.

The left and right eigenvectors associated with
\rho(\mathbf{M})
are the only strictly positive eigenvectors.

3.

The maximum modulus eigenvalues of
\mathbf{M}
are
e^{2\pi\mathbf{i}p/k}\rho(\mathbf{M})
for
p\in[k]
, and each has an algebraic multiplicity
1
.

4.

if
\mathbf{M}\geqslant\mathbf{N}
are real, non-negative matrices and
\mathbf{M}

\rho(\mathbf{M})\geqslant\rho(\mathbf{N})
, with equality iff
\mathbf{M}=\mathbf{N}
.

Additionally, every Jordan block corresponding to a maximum modulus eigenvalue is of size one.
Meaning,
\mu_{a}(\lambda_{0})=\mu_{g}(\lambda_{0})

, where

Furthermore, the vector of all ones,

Due to

implies that the right eigenvector

Theorem II.8

(Lebesgue-Vitali’s Theorem)

Let
\left\{f_{n}\right\}_{n}
be non-negative, Borel measurable, uniformly bounded functions on a finite measure space.
Assume

f_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle L^{p}}}{{\to}}f

.

Since
\mathsf{\mathbf{fr}}^{(k)}
is bounded in every entry, as an immediate corollary, we obtain the

.

Corollary II.9

With the same notation and assumptions as in Theorem

\mathsf{\mathbf{fr}}^{(k)}_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle L^{1}}}{{\to}}\mathbf{r}

III
Main Results, Problem Setup, and Proof outline

The main theorem of this paper is a Central Limit Theorem for the count vector in mutation systems. We present the theorem, and then outline the proof strategy by constructing a key decomposition using the eigenvector structure of the substitution matrix.

Theorem III.1

Let
S
be a
\tau
-average mutation system, fix
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, and let
\mathbf{M}^{(k)}=\mathbf{M}
be the
k
-substitution matrix of
S
.
Assume that
\mathbf{M}
is irreducible and, for simplicity, suppose that it is diagonalizable.

Denote by
\mathsf{\mathbf{ct}}_{n}
the count vector of
k
-tuples in
S(n)
. From Theorem IV.11 of
[
7
]
, we know that

\frac{1}{n}\mathsf{\mathbf{ct}}_{n}\xrightarrow{\Pr}(\tau-1)\mathbf{r},

where
\mathbf{r}
is the leading right eigenvector of
\mathbf{M}
normalized such that
\|\mathbf{r}\|_{1}=1
.

Suppose that for every non-leading eigenvalue
\lambda_{i}
of
\mathbf{M}
, the real part satisfies

\Re(\lambda_{i})<k+\frac{1}{2}(\tau-1).

Furthermore, assume that the frequency vector
\mathsf{\mathbf{fr}}_{n}^{(2k-1)}
of
(2k-1)
-tuples converges to some deterministic vector.
Then the count vector satisfies a Central Limit Theorem:

\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\left(\mathsf{\mathbf{ct}}_{n}-n(\tau-1)\mathbf{r}\right)\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0},\Sigma),

for some covariance matrix
\Sigma
.

Overall, the count vector exhibits the following asymptotic normal behavior:

\mathsf{\mathbf{ct}}_{n}=n(\tau-1)\mathbf{r}+\sqrt{n}\boldsymbol{\xi}+o(\sqrt{n}),

where
\boldsymbol{\xi}
is a (zero-mean) normal random vector.

Remark III.2

The case where
\mathbf{M}
is not diagonalizable will be addressed at the end of the paper.

Instead of proving Theorem
III.1
directly, we prove a slightly different theorem as explained below.
The core idea relies on decomposing the centered count vector
\mathsf{\mathbf{ct}}_{n}-n(\tau-1)\mathbf{r}
along the left eigenvectors of
\mathbf{M}
. We first establish notation for the eigenstructure of
\mathbf{M}
.

III-
1
Eigenvalue and Eigenvector Notation

Since
\mathbf{M}
has dimension
d^{k}\times d^{k}
, there are
d^{k}
eigenvalues. As
\mathbf{M}
is real and diagonalizable, complex eigenvalues come in conjugate pairs.

Let
\lambda_{0}
denote the leading (Perron) eigenvalue of
\mathbf{M}
. Suppose that among the remaining
d^{k}-1
eigenvalues,
2m
form complex conjugate pairs and
d^{k}-2m-1
are purely real. We index the eigenvalues as follows:

•

\lambda_{0}
denotes the leading eigenvalue,

•

\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{2m}
denote the complex eigenvalues, where
\lambda_{i+m}=\overline{\lambda_{i}}
for
i=1,\ldots,m
, and

•

\lambda_{2m+1},\ldots,\lambda_{d^{k}-1}
denote the purely real, non-leading eigenvalues.

For each eigenvalue
\lambda_{i}
, we denote by
\mathbf{u}_{i}
its corresponding left eigenvector of
\mathbf{M}
. We express each eigenvector and eigenvalue in terms of their real and imaginary parts:

\mathbf{u}_{i}=\mathbf{u}_{i}^{r}+\mathbf{i}\mathbf{u}_{i}^{c}\quad\text{and}\quad\lambda_{i}=\lambda_{i}^{r}+\mathbf{i}\lambda_{i}^{c}.

III-
2
Construction of the Transformation Matrix

We now construct a matrix
\mathbf{U}
whose rows are the real and imaginary parts of the left eigenvectors.

Definition III.3

Define the matrix
\mathbf{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{d^{k}\times d^{k}}
by

\mathbf{U}:=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{u}_{0}^{r}\\
\mathbf{u}_{1}^{r}\\
\mathbf{u}_{1}^{c}\\
\mathbf{u}_{2}^{r}\\
\mathbf{u}_{2}^{c}\\
\vdots\\
\mathbf{u}_{m}^{r}\\
\mathbf{u}_{m}^{c}\\
\mathbf{u}_{2m+1}^{r}\\
\vdots\\
\mathbf{u}_{d^{k}-1}^{r}\end{bmatrix}.

Note that we include only the real and imaginary parts of the first
m
eigenvectors
\mathbf{u}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{u}_{m}
.
Since
\lambda_{i+m}=\overline{\lambda_{i}}
, the corresponding eigenvectors are conjugate pairs as well
\mathbf{u}_{i+m}=\overline{\mathbf{u}_{i}}
. This maintains linear independence and keeps
\mathbf{U}
at dimension
d^{k}\times d^{k}
as shown next.

Lemma III.4

The matrix
\mathbf{U}
is full rank and invertible.

Proof:

We verify that all rows of
\mathbf{U}
are linearly independent.

Since
\mathbf{M}
is diagonalizable, its left eigenvectors
\mathbf{u}_{0},\mathbf{u}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{u}_{d^{k}-1}
form a basis for
\mathbb{C}^{d^{k}}
and are therefore linearly independent. For the non-diagonalizable case we use the general eigen decomposition, the general left eigenvectors form a basis for
\mathbb{C}^{d^{k}}
and are therefore linearly independent.

We need to show that replacing each complex conjugate pair
\{\mathbf{u}_{i},\mathbf{u}_{i+m}\}
with their real and imaginary parts
\{\mathbf{u}_{i}^{r},\mathbf{u}_{i}^{c}\}
preserves linear independence.

For a complex conjugate pair where
\mathbf{u}_{i+m}=\overline{\mathbf{u}_{i}}
, we have

\mathbf{u}_{i}=\mathbf{u}_{i}^{r}+\mathbf{i}\mathbf{u}_{i}^{c}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbf{u}_{i+m}=\mathbf{u}_{i}^{r}-\mathbf{i}\mathbf{u}_{i}^{c}.

Note that this transformation is invertible:

\mathbf{u}_{i}^{r}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathbf{u}_{i}+\mathbf{u}_{i+m}\right)\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbf{u}_{i}^{c}=\frac{1}{2\mathbf{i}}\left(\mathbf{u}_{i}-\mathbf{u}_{i+m}\right).

Therefore,
\mathbf{u}_{i}^{r}
and
\mathbf{u}_{i}^{c}
lie in the span of
\mathbf{u}_{i}
and
\mathbf{u}_{i+m}
, and conversely,
\mathbf{u}_{i}
and
\mathbf{u}_{i+m}
lie in the span of
\mathbf{u}_{i}^{r}
and
\mathbf{u}_{i}^{c}
. This shows that

\text{span}\{\mathbf{u}_{i},\mathbf{u}_{i+m}\}=\text{span}\{\mathbf{u}_{i}^{r},\mathbf{u}_{i}^{c}\}.

Since replacing conjugate pairs with their real and imaginary parts preserves the span at each step, and the original eigenvectors are linearly independent, the rows of
\mathbf{U}
remain linearly independent. Thus,
\mathbf{U}
is full rank and due to being square matrix, is invertible.
∎

Remark III.5

Note that Lemma
III.4
holds independently of the diagonalization assumption. It requires only that the matrix
\mathbf{U}
be real-valued, a condition that is satisfied in our setting.

III-
3
Projection onto Eigenvector Components

We apply the transformation
\mathbf{U}
to the centered count vector to obtain projections onto the eigenvector directions.

Definition III.6

For
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, define the projection vector
\mathbf{X}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d^{k}}
by

\mathbf{X}_{n}=\mathbf{U}\left(\mathsf{\mathbf{ct}}_{n}-n(\tau-1)\mathbf{r}\right).

The components of
\mathbf{X}_{n}
are given by

\mathbf{X}_{n}:=\begin{bmatrix}X_{n}^{r(0)}\;X_{n}^{r(1)}\;X_{n}^{c(1)}\;X_{n}^{r(2)}\;X_{n}^{c(2)}\;\cdots\;X_{n}^{r(m)}\;X_{n}^{c(m)}\;X_{n}^{r(2m+1)}\;\cdots\;X_{n}^{r(d^{k}-1)}\end{bmatrix}^{T},

where for each eigenvalue index
i
, we define

X_{n}^{(i)}=\mathbf{u}_{i}\cdot\left(\mathsf{\mathbf{ct}}_{n}-n(\tau-1)\mathbf{r}\right)=X_{n}^{r(i)}+\mathbf{i}X_{n}^{c(i)},

with

\displaystyle X_{n}^{r(i)}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{u}_{i}^{r}\cdot\left(\mathsf{\mathbf{ct}}_{n}-n(\tau-1)\mathbf{r}\right),

\displaystyle X_{n}^{c(i)}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{u}_{i}^{c}\cdot\left(\mathsf{\mathbf{ct}}_{n}-n(\tau-1)\mathbf{r}\right).

Remark III.7

Recall that the leading left eigenvector is a vector of all ones,
\mathbf{u}_{0}=\mathbf{1}
. since the leading right eigenvector
\mathbf{r}
is normalized to be a probability vector, we have
\mathbf{u}_{0}\cdot\mathbf{r}=1
. Additionally, by orthogonality of the left non-leading eigenvectors with
\mathbf{r}
, we have
\mathbf{u}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{r}=0
for all
i>0
.
Moreover,
\mathbf{u}_{0}^{r}\cdot\mathsf{\mathbf{ct}}_{n}
sums the number of
k
-tuples overall, meaning the number of symbols as well.
Consequently, the projection components simplify to

\displaystyle X_{n}^{r(0)}

\displaystyle=Y_{n}-n(\tau-1),

\displaystyle X_{n}^{r(i)}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{u}_{i}^{r}\cdot\mathsf{\mathbf{ct}}_{n}\quad\text{for }i>0,

\displaystyle X_{n}^{c(i)}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{u}_{i}^{c}\cdot\mathsf{\mathbf{ct}}_{n}\quad\text{for }i>0.

Thus, to prove Theorem
III.1
, it is enough to show that

\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\mathbf{X}_{n}\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0},\Sigma^{\prime})

for some covariance matrix
\Sigma^{\prime}
.
This is because
\mathbf{U}
is invertible and therefore

\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\left(\mathsf{\mathbf{ct}}_{n}-n(\tau-1)\mathbf{r}\right)=\mathbf{U}^{-1}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\mathbf{X}_{n}\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0},\Sigma),

where
\Sigma=\mathbf{U}^{-1}\Sigma^{\prime}(\mathbf{U}^{-1})^{T}
.

Thus, our main goal is to prove the following theorem.

Theorem III.8

Let
S
be an average
\tau
-mutation system with irreducible substitution matrix
\mathbf{M}^{(k)}
, and assume that the frequency of
(2k-1)
-tuples converges in probability to some (non-negative) vector
\mathsf{fr}_{n}^{(2k-1)}\xrightarrow{\Pr}\mathbf{r}^{(2k-1)}\geqslant 0
.
If for every non-leading eigenvalue
\lambda=\lambda^{r}+\mathbf{i}\lambda^{c}
, we have
\lambda^{r}<k+\frac{1}{2}(\tau-1)
, then
n^{-\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{X}_{n}
has an asymptotic zero mean joint normal distribution.

Recall that a vector has a joint normal distribution if and only if every linear sum of its entries has a normal distribution.
Thus, to prove
III.8
, we show that for every set of real coefficients
\alpha_{i}^{r},\alpha_{i}^{c}\in{\mathbb{R}}
, assuming at least one coefficient is non-zero, the sum

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\alpha_{0}^{r}X^{r(0)}_{n}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(\alpha_{i}^{r}X^{r(i)}_{n}+\alpha_{i}^{c}X^{c(i)}_{n}\right)+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=2m+1}^{d^{k}-1}\alpha_{i}^{r}X^{r(i)}_{n}

(2)

converges in distribution to a random variable
Z
, with a characteristic function
{\mathbb{E}}[e^{(-\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}t^{2})}]
, where
\sigma^{2}
is a non-negative constant. In case
\sigma^{2}=0
, the sum converges to
0
in probability.
In the next section, we show how to find
\sigma^{2}
and find a condition to ensure
\sigma^{2}>0
.

Example III.9

The left eigenvectors of

for every set of

Standing Assumptions

Throughout the paper, we will make the following assumptions.

1.

S
is an average
\tau
-mutation with
\tau>1
, meaning the length of the sequence steadily grows.

2.

The matrix
\mathbf{M}^{(k)}
is irreducible and has a regular eigendecomposition (
\mu_{a}(\lambda)=\mu_{g}(\lambda)
for every eigenvalue
\lambda
). This assumption may be removed as explained at the end of the paper.

3.

We assume that
\mathsf{\mathbf{fr}}_{n}^{(2k-1)}
converges in probability to some (non-negative) vector
\mathbf{r}^{(2k-1)}
.

4.

For every non-leading eigenvalue
\lambda=\lambda^{r}+\mathbf{i}\lambda^{c}

Outline of the Proof

We now outline the high-level strategy for proving Theorem
III.1
.

Step 1: Eigendecomposition and projection (Section
II
).

We construct the transformation matrix
\mathbf{U}
(Definition
III.3
) whose rows are the real and imaginary parts of the left eigenvectors of
\mathbf{M}^{(k)}
. Applying
\mathbf{U}
to the centered count vector yields the projection vector
\mathbf{X}_{n}=\mathbf{U}(\mathsf{\mathbf{ct}}_{n}-n(\tau-1)\mathbf{r})
. Since
\mathbf{U}
is invertible (Lemma
III.4
), establishing a CLT for
\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\mathbf{X}_{n}
is equivalent to proving the CLT for
\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}(\mathsf{\mathbf{ct}}_{n}-n(\tau-1)\mathbf{r})
. Thus, it suffices to show that every linear combination of the entries of
\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\mathbf{X}_{n}
is asymptotically normal.

Step 2: Martingale difference approximation.

For each non-leading eigenvalue
\lambda_{i}
, the projection
X^{(i)}_{n}=\mathbf{u}_{i}\cdot(\mathsf{\mathbf{ct}}_{n}-n(\tau-1)\mathbf{r})
satisfies a linear recurrence driven by a martingale difference
M_{n}^{(i)}
. Because the sequence length
Y_{n}
is random, we first approximate
M_{n}^{(i)}
by a simpler process
\overline{M}_{n}^{(i)}
, where the random length
Y_{n}
is replaced by its deterministic mean
(n-1)(\tau-1)+Y_{0}
. The approximation error is shown to be asymptotically negligible in
L^{1}
(Claim
IV.6
). This allows us to express each projection as an explicit linear combination of the terms
\overline{M}_{j}^{(i)}
, up to a small error term (Claim
IV.8
). However, the resulting linear combination is no longer martingale.

Step 3: Verifying the martingale CLT conditions.

To leverage the martingale CLT, we take the coefficients derived for the linear combination of
\overline{M}_{j}^{(i)}
and apply them instead to the original martingale differences
M_{n}^{(i)}
.
We show that these two linear combinations are asymptotically equivalent in
L^{1}
.
This substitution yields a martingale sequence.
We can then apply the classical martingale CLT (Theorem
V.1
) to the array
\mathcal{X}_{n,j}
, defined in (
25
), using
M_{n}^{(i)}
.
This requires verifying the following two conditions.

•

Conditional Lindeberg condition
: The individual terms
|\mathcal{X}_{n,j}|
become uniformly negligible relative to
\sqrt{n}
, which follows from the spectral gap assumption
\lambda_{i}^{r}<k+\frac{1}{2}(\tau-1)
(Section
V
).

•

Conditional variance condition
: The conditional sum
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\mathbb{E}}[\mathcal{X}_{n,j}^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{j-1}]
converges in probability to a deterministic limit
\sigma^{2}\geqslant 0
(Claim
V.12
). This relies on the
L^{1}
convergence of the
(2k-1)
-tuple frequency vector (Corollary
II.9
) and on explicitly computing the limits of the Cesàro averages
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j}\beta_{j,n}^{(i)}\beta_{j,n}^{(l)}
via Riemann-sum-to-integral approximations (Lemmas
V.14
,
V.15
). Establishing this condition requires a preliminary calculation of the asymptotic differences covariance matrix.

Completing this step establishes Theorem
III.8
.

Step 4: Strict positivity (non-degenerate case).

Under the supplementary assumption that the distribution of the count increment
\nabla\mathsf{\mathbf{ct}}_{t}^{(k)}-(\tau-1)\mathbf{r}
is non-degenerate, the limiting covariance matrix
\Theta
of the increments is strictly positive definite (Lemma
V.10
).
This implies
\sigma^{2}>0
for every non-zero choice of coefficients (Claim
V.17
).

Step 5: Explicit covariance matrix.

By evaluating
\sigma^{2}
across all canonical choices of coefficient vectors, we determine the full covariance matrix
\Sigma^{\prime}
for the limiting distribution of
\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\mathbf{X}_{n}
(Corollary
VI.1
).
We then recover the original covariance matrix via
\Sigma=\mathbf{U}^{-1}\Sigma^{\prime}(\mathbf{U}^{-1})^{T}
.
This concludes the proof of Theorem
III.1
for the diagonalizable case.

Step 6: Non-diagonalizable case.

When
\mathbf{M}^{(k)}
is not diagonalizable, we rely on its Jordan decomposition.
The primary difference is that the coefficients
\beta_{j,n}^{(i,h)}
acquire additional logarithmic factors of the form
O(n^{(\lambda_{i}^{r}-k)/(\tau-1)}\log^{H_{i}-1}n)
, where
H_{i}
denotes the size of the corresponding Jordan block.
Under our spectral gap assumptions, these factors remain
o(\sqrt{n})
, ensuring that the overall proof strategy remains valid (Section
VII
).

IV
Approximation by Martingale Difference

In this section, we lay the basis for the proof of Theorem
III.8
. Since in each mutation step, the length of the sequence increases with random length, the length of the sequence after
n
mutation steps is random. We denote its length as
Y_{n}
, i.e.,
Y_{n}=|S(n)|
.
We start by constructing a sequence that is “close” to a martingale difference sequence and captures the behavior of the system. The main reason to do so is that those sequences will be easier to analyze. We follow a similar approach to that of
[
7
]
, using analogous notation and relying on several results established therein.
Throughout this section, we will drop the
i
index from expressions like
\mathbf{u}_{i}^{r}
and
X^{(i)}_{n}
, as the following claims are true for all non-leading eigenvalues
1\leqslant i\leqslant d^{k}-1
. Because the analysis for the leading eigenvalue is different, we will keep the index notation in that case.
To ensure the paper is self-contained, we restate the relevant definitions and results from
[
7
]
.

Definition IV.1

Fix
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Since the composition of
k
-tuples in the word changes with every mutation step, to capture the change in the composition of
k
-tuples in step
n
, we define

\displaystyle\nabla Y_{n}

\displaystyle:=Y_{n}-Y_{n-1}.

\displaystyle\nabla\mathsf{\mathbf{ct}}^{(k)}_{n}

\displaystyle:=\mathsf{\mathbf{ct}}^{(k)}_{n}-\mathsf{\mathbf{ct}}^{(k)}_{n-1}.

Similarly, we define

\nabla X^{r(0)}_{n}:=X^{r(0)}_{n}-X^{r(0)}_{n-1}=\mathbf{u}_{0}^{r}\cdot(\nabla\mathsf{\mathbf{ct}}_{n}^{(k)}-(\tau-1)\mathbf{r})=\nabla Y_{n}-(\tau-1)

and for non-leading eigenvalues

\displaystyle\nabla X^{r}_{n}

\displaystyle:=X^{r}_{n}-X^{r}_{n-1}=\mathbf{u}^{r}\cdot(\nabla\mathsf{\mathbf{ct}}_{n}^{(k)}-(\tau-1)\mathbf{r})=\mathbf{u}^{r}\cdot\nabla\mathsf{\mathbf{ct}}_{n}^{(k)}

\displaystyle\nabla X^{c}_{n}

\displaystyle:=X^{c}_{n}-X^{c}_{n-1}=\mathbf{u}^{c}\cdot(\nabla\mathsf{\mathbf{ct}}_{n}^{(k)}-(\tau-1)\mathbf{r})=\mathbf{u}^{c}\cdot\nabla\mathsf{\mathbf{ct}}_{n}^{(k)}

\displaystyle M^{r}_{n}

\displaystyle:=\nabla X^{r}_{n}-\frac{1}{Y_{n-1}}\left((\lambda^{r}-k)X^{r}_{n-1}-\lambda^{c}X^{c}_{n-1}\right)

\displaystyle M^{c}_{n}

\displaystyle:=\nabla X^{c}_{n}-\frac{1}{Y_{n-1}}\left((\lambda^{r}-k)X^{c}_{n-1}+\lambda^{c}X^{r}_{n-1}\right).

Since the distribution of
\vartheta
is finitely supported, the length of the added random words is finite, i.e.,
\nabla\mathsf{\mathbf{ct}}_{n}
is bounded, which implies that
\nabla X^{r}_{n}
and
\nabla X^{c}_{n}
are also bounded. We recall that since the
i
index is dropped, the definitions above are actually a family of definitions and are defined for every
i=1,2,\dots,d^{k}-1
.

The following lemma is a strengthening of a result from
[
7
]
.

Lemma IV.2

Define
\mathcal{F}_{n}
as the
\sigma
-algebra generated by
\mathsf{\mathbf{ct}}_{j},\;j\leqslant n
, then:

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\nabla\mathsf{\mathbf{ct}}_{n}^{(k)}~|~\mathcal{F}_{n-1}\right]\xrightarrow{L^{1}}\left(\tau-1\right)\mathbf{r}^{(k)}

Proof:

From
[
7
, Lemma III.9 and Remark III.10]
we have

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\nabla\mathsf{\mathbf{ct}}_{n}^{(k)}~|~\mathcal{F}_{n-1}\right]=\left(\mathbf{M}^{(k)}-k\mathbf{I}\right)\frac{\mathsf{\mathbf{ct}}_{n-1}^{(k)}}{Y_{n-1}}=\left(\mathbf{M}^{(k)}-k\mathbf{I}\right)\mathsf{\mathbf{fr}}^{(k)}_{n-1}.

From Corollary
II.9
we obtain

\left(\mathbf{M}^{(k)}-k\mathbf{I}\right)\mathsf{\mathbf{fr}}^{(k)}_{n-1}\xrightarrow{L^{1}}\left(\mathbf{M}^{(k)}-k\mathbf{I}\right)\mathbf{r}^{(k)}=\left(\tau+k-1-k\right)\mathbf{r}^{(k)}=\left(\tau-1\right)\mathbf{r}^{(k)}

∎

If
\mathcal{F}_{n}
denotes the
\sigma
-algebra generated by
\mathsf{\mathbf{ct}}_{j}^{(k)},\;j\leqslant n
, the following claim are proved in
[
7
, Claim IV.1]
.

Claim IV.3

(See Claim IV.1 in
[
7
]
)

M^{r}_{n}
and
M^{c}_{n}
are Martingale differences, with respect to
\mathcal{F}_{n}
, with zero mean
E[M^{r}_{n}]=E[M^{c}_{n}]=0
.

As an immediate result, we obtain the following claim.

Claim IV.4

\nabla X^{r(0)}_{n}
is Martingale differences, with respect to
\mathcal{F}_{n}
, with zero mean as well.

Proof:

As we know from the definition of average
\tau
-Mutation, in every mutation step, every chosen symbol is replaced with a sub-word of average length
\tau
. Meaning:

E\left[\nabla X^{r(0)}_{n}~|~\mathcal{F}_{n-1}\right]=E\left[\nabla Y_{n}-(\tau-1)~|~\mathcal{F}_{n-1}\right]=E\left[\nabla Y_{n}~|~\mathcal{F}_{n-1}\right]-(\tau-1)=\tau-1-(\tau-1)=0.

∎

Definition IV.5

While the processes
M_{n}^{r},M_{n}^{c}
are martingale, the random length
Y_{n}
makes them difficult to study.
Similarly to
[
7
]
, we approximate those processes by replacing
Y_{n}
with its mean. Clearly, the obtained processes are no longer martingale.
Define the approximations

\displaystyle\overline{M}^{r}_{n}:=\nabla X^{r}_{n}-\frac{1}{(n-1)(\tau-1)+Y_{0}}\left((\lambda^{r}-k)X^{r}_{n-1}-\lambda^{c}X^{c}_{n-1}\right)

\displaystyle\overline{M}^{c}_{n}:=\nabla X^{c}_{n}-\frac{1}{(n-1)(\tau-1)+Y_{0}}\left((\lambda^{r}-k)X^{c}_{n-1}+\lambda^{c}X^{r}_{n-1}\right)

with
Y_{0}
being the length of the starting word
w
which is deterministic.

To claim that we may analyze
\overline{M}_{n}^{r},\overline{M}_{n}^{c}
instead of
M_{n}^{r},M_{n}^{c}
, we need to show asymptotic equivalence.

Claim IV.6

\overline{M}^{r}_{n}
and
\overline{M}^{c}_{n}
are asymptotically
L^{1}
equivalent to
M^{r}_{n}
and
M^{c}_{n}
, respectively, i.e.,

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left|\overline{M}_{n}^{r}-M_{n}^{r}\right|\right]

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left|\overline{M}_{n}^{c}-M_{n}^{c}\right|\right]

\displaystyle=0.

We denote this asymptotic equivalence by
\overline{M}^{r}_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle L^{1}}}{{\sim}}M^{r}_{n}
and
\overline{M}^{c}_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle L^{1}}}{{\sim}}M^{c}_{n}
.

Proof:

To prove the claim we write

\overline{M}^{r}_{n}-M^{r}_{n}=\left((\lambda^{r}-k)X^{r}_{n-1}-\lambda^{c}X^{c}_{n-1}\right)\left(\frac{1}{Y_{n-1}}-\frac{1}{(n-1)(\tau-1)+Y_{0}}\right).

Notice that

\displaystyle\left((\lambda^{r}-k)X^{r}_{n-1}-\lambda^{c}X^{c}_{n-1}\right)\left(\frac{1}{Y_{n-1}}-\frac{1}{(n-1)(\tau-1)+Y_{0}}\right)

\displaystyle=\left((\lambda^{r}-k)\frac{X^{r}_{n-1}}{Y_{n-1}}-\lambda^{c}\frac{X^{c}_{n-1}}{Y_{n-1}}\right)\frac{Y_{n-1}-((n-1)(\tau-1)+Y_{0})}{(n-1)(\tau-1)+Y_{0}}

\displaystyle=\left((\lambda^{r}-k)\mathbf{u}_{r}\frac{\mathsf{\mathbf{ct}}_{n-1}}{Y_{n-1}}-\lambda^{c}\mathbf{u}_{c}\frac{\mathsf{\mathbf{ct}}_{n-1}}{Y_{n-1}}\right)\frac{Y_{n-1}-((n-1)(\tau-1)+Y_{0})}{(n-1)(\tau-1)+Y_{0}}.

Overall, we get

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left|\overline{M}^{r}_{n}-M^{r}_{n}\right|\right]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\left[\left|\left((\lambda^{r}-k)\mathbf{u}_{r}\frac{\mathsf{\mathbf{ct}}_{n-1}}{Y_{n-1}}-\lambda^{c}\mathbf{u}_{c}\frac{\mathsf{\mathbf{ct}}_{n-1}}{Y_{n-1}}\right)\frac{Y_{n-1}-((n-1)(\tau-1)+Y_{0})}{(n-1)(\tau-1)+Y_{0}}\right|\right].

From Cauchy-Schwartz inequality, we get

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left|\left((\lambda^{r}-k)\mathbf{u}_{r}\frac{\mathsf{\mathbf{ct}}_{n-1}}{Y_{n-1}}-\lambda^{c}\mathbf{u}_{c}\frac{\mathsf{\mathbf{ct}}_{n-1}}{Y_{n-1}}\right)\frac{Y_{n-1}-((n-1)(\tau-1)+Y_{0})}{(n-1)(\tau-1)+Y_{0}}\right|\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\left({\mathbb{E}}\left[\left((\lambda^{r}-k)\mathbf{u}_{r}\frac{\mathsf{\mathbf{ct}}_{n-1}}{Y_{n-1}}-\lambda^{c}\mathbf{u}_{c}\frac{\mathsf{\mathbf{ct}}_{n-1}}{Y_{n-1}}\right)^{2}\right]{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left(\frac{Y_{n-1}-((n-1)(\tau-1)+Y_{0})}{(n-1)(\tau-1)+Y_{0}}\right)^{2}\right]\right)^{1/2}.

In
[
7
, Corollary IV.12]
it was shown that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left(\frac{Y_{n-1}-((n-1)(\tau-1)+Y_{0})}{(n-1)(\tau-1)+Y_{0}}\right)^{2}\right]\leqslant\frac{C_{0}}{n-1}

(3)

for some constant
C_{0}
.
Plugging this in, we get

\displaystyle\left({\mathbb{E}}\left[\left((\lambda^{r}-k)\mathbf{u}_{r}\frac{\mathsf{\mathbf{ct}}_{n-1}}{Y_{n-1}}-\lambda^{c}\mathbf{u}_{c}\frac{\mathsf{\mathbf{ct}}_{n-1}}{Y_{n-1}}\right)^{2}\right]{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left(\frac{Y_{n-1}-((n-1)(\tau-1)+Y_{0})}{(n-1)(\tau-1)+Y_{0}}\right)^{2}\right]\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\left({\mathbb{E}}\left[\left((\lambda^{r}-k)\mathbf{u}_{r}\frac{\mathsf{\mathbf{ct}}_{n-1}}{Y_{n-1}}-\lambda^{c}\mathbf{u}_{c}\frac{\mathsf{\mathbf{ct}}_{n-1}}{Y_{n-1}}\right)^{2}\right]\frac{C_{0}}{n-1}\right)^{1/2}.

Since
\frac{\mathsf{\mathbf{ct}}_{n}}{Y_{n}}=\mathsf{\mathbf{fr}}_{n}
is bounded, and since
\mathbf{u}
is fixed, we obtain that

\left((\lambda^{r}-k)\mathbf{u}_{r}\frac{\mathsf{\mathbf{ct}}_{n-1}}{Y_{n-1}}-\lambda^{c}\mathbf{u}_{c}\frac{\mathsf{\mathbf{ct}}_{n-1}}{Y_{n-1}}\right)^{2}
is bounded as well.
Overall, we obtain

\lim_{n\to\infty}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left|\overline{M}_{n}^{r}-M_{n}^{r}\right|\right]\leqslant\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{C}{\sqrt{n-1}}=0,

for some constant
C
.
Thus,

\overline{M}^{r}_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle L^{1}}}{{\sim}}M^{r}_{n}.

Similarly, we obtain

\overline{M}^{c}_{n}~\stackrel{{\scriptstyle L^{1}}}{{\sim}}~M^{c}_{n}.

∎

Following similar lines as
[
7
]
, for fixed
\alpha^{r},\alpha^{c}\in{\mathbb{R}}
, we seek constants
\beta^{r}_{j,n},\beta^{c}_{j,n}
for
j=1,\dots,n
such that

\displaystyle\sum^{n}_{j=1}\left(\beta^{r}_{j,n}\overline{M}^{r}_{j}+\beta^{c}_{j,n}\overline{M}^{c}_{j}\right)=\alpha^{r}X^{r}_{n}+\alpha^{c}X^{c}_{n}+\varepsilon_{n}

(4)

where
\varepsilon_{n}=O\left(n^{\frac{\lambda^{r}-k}{\tau-1}}\right)
is a small error term.
Note that the leading eigenvalue is not a part of these sums.
We also define the following sums.

\displaystyle v_{n}

\displaystyle:=\sum^{n}_{j=1}\left(\beta^{r}_{j,n}M^{r}_{j}+\beta^{c}_{j,n}M^{c}_{j}\right),

(5)

\displaystyle\overline{v}_{n}

\displaystyle:=\sum^{n}_{j=1}\left(\beta^{r}_{j,n}\overline{M}^{r}_{j}+\beta^{c}_{j,n}\overline{M}^{c}_{j}\right)=\alpha^{r}X^{r}_{n}+\alpha^{c}X^{c}_{n}+\varepsilon_{n}.

(6)

We utilize the set of estimators
\left\{\overline{M}_{j}\right\}
to derive the coefficients
\beta_{j}
for the sum
\bar{v}_{n}=\alpha^{r}X^{r}_{n}+\alpha^{c}X^{c}_{n}+\varepsilon_{n}
. These
\beta_{j}
coefficients are subsequently applied to the martingale process
v_{n}
to construct approximations for
X^{c}_{n}
and
X^{r}_{n}
. Finally, by invoking the central limit theorem for martingale difference sequences, we establish that
\mathbf{X}_{n}
converges to an asymptotic normal distribution.
To that end, we first present the values of
\beta_{j,n}^{c},\beta_{j,n}^{r}
, and then show and asymptotic
L^{1}
equivalence between
v_{n}
and
\overline{v}_{n}
.

Remark IV.7

if
\lambda
is real, meaning
\lambda^{c}=0
, its eigenvector is real as well.
Then
\mathbf{u}_{c}=\mathbf{0}
and
X^{c}_{n}=0
as well. In that case, for convenience, we set
\alpha^{c}:=0
.

The first step is to find the values of
\beta_{j,n}^{r}
and
\beta_{j,n}^{c}
, together with a simple bound.

Claim IV.8

The values of
\beta^{r}_{j,n}
and
\beta^{c}_{j,n}
are given below.

\displaystyle\beta^{r}_{j,n}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{n}{j+1}\right)^{\frac{\lambda^{r}-k}{\tau-1}}\left(\alpha^{r}\cos\left(\frac{\lambda^{c}}{\tau-1}\log\left(\frac{n}{j+1}\right)\right)+\alpha^{c}\sin\left(\frac{\lambda^{c}}{\tau-1}\log\left(\frac{n}{j+1}\right)\right)\right)+O\left(n^{\frac{\lambda^{r}-k}{\tau-1}-1}\right)

(7)

\displaystyle\beta^{c}_{j,n}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{n}{j+1}\right)^{\frac{\lambda^{r}-k}{\tau-1}}\left(\alpha^{c}\cos\left(\frac{\lambda^{c}}{\tau-1}\log\left(\frac{n}{j+1}\right)\right)-\alpha^{r}\sin\left(\frac{\lambda^{c}}{\tau-1}\log\left(\frac{n}{j+1}\right)\right)\right)+O\left(n^{\frac{\lambda^{r}-k}{\tau-1}-1}\right).

(8)

The absolute values of
\beta_{j,n}^{c}
and
\beta_{j,n}^{r}
are bounded above by

\displaystyle\left|\beta^{r}_{j,n}\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\left(|\alpha^{r}|+|\alpha^{c}|\right)\left(\frac{n}{j+1}\right)^{\frac{\lambda^{r}-k}{\tau-1}}+O\left(n^{\frac{\lambda^{r}-k}{\tau-1}-1}\right)

(9)

\displaystyle\left|\beta^{c}_{j,n}\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\left(|\alpha^{r}|+|\alpha^{c}|\right)\left(\frac{n}{j+1}\right)^{\frac{\lambda^{r}-k}{\tau-1}}+O\left(n^{\frac{\lambda^{r}-k}{\tau-1}-1}\right)

(10)

For
\varepsilon_{n}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{n}

\displaystyle=-X^{r}_{0}\left(\left(1+\frac{\lambda^{r}-k}{m}\right)\beta^{r}_{1,n}+\frac{\lambda^{c}}{m}\beta^{c}_{1,n}\right)-X^{c}_{0}\left(\left(1+\frac{\lambda^{r}-k}{m}\right)\beta^{c}_{1,n}-\frac{\lambda^{c}}{m}\beta^{r}_{1,n}\right)

\displaystyle=O\left(n^{\frac{\lambda^{r}-k}{\tau-1}}\right).

As the proof follows a methodology similar to that in
[
7
]
, wherein the values for
\beta^{r}_{j,n}
and
\beta^{c}_{j,n}
were derived for the specific case
\alpha^{r}=1
and
\alpha^{c}=\mathbf{i}
, we defer the full derivation to the Appendix.

We now work our way to show an
L^{1}
asymptotic equivalence between
v_{n}
and
\overline{v}_{n}
.
Recall that for each non-leading eigenvalue, we attach such
v_{n}
and
\overline{v}_{n}
(together with
M_{n},\overline{M}_{n}
).

We begin with the following useful lemma (recall that convergence in
L^{2}
implies convergence in
L^{1}
).

Lemma IV.9

Fix a non-leading eigenvalue
\lambda
and its corresponding
X_{n}
. We have that

\frac{X_{n}}{Y_{n}}
converges to
0
in
L^{2}
, i.e.,

\lim_{n\to\infty}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left|\frac{X_{n}}{Y_{n}}\right|^{2}\right]=0.

Proof:

We have shown in Theorem
II.7
that
\mathsf{fr}_{n}\xrightarrow{\Pr}\mathbf{r}
.
Since the non-leading eigenvector
\mathbf{u}
is orthogonal to
\mathbf{r}
, we get

\frac{X_{n}}{Y_{n}}=\frac{\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathsf{\mathbf{ct}}_{n}}{Y_{n}}=\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathsf{\mathbf{fr}}_{n}\xrightarrow{\Pr}\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{r}=0.

Since
\mathsf{\mathbf{fr}}_{n}
is a frequency (probability) vector, its entries are bounded in
[0,1]
, and hence
\frac{X_{n}}{Y_{n}}=\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathsf{\mathbf{fr}}_{n}
is uniformly bounded. By Theorem
II.8
, convergence in probability together with uniform boundedness implies
\frac{X_{n}}{Y_{n}}\xrightarrow{L^{2}}0
.
∎

We now show that for every non-leading eigenvalue,
\overline{v}_{n}
and
v_{n}
are asymptotically similar.

Claim IV.10

Let
\lambda=\lambda^{r}+\mathbf{i}\lambda^{c}
be a non-leading eigenvalue with
\lambda^{r}<k+\frac{1}{2}(\tau-1)
.
Let
v_{n}
and
\overline{v}_{n}
be the sums as in (
5
) and (
6
), respectively, corresponding to
\lambda
.
Then

\frac{\overline{v}_{n}}{\sqrt{n}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle L^{1}}}{{\sim}}\frac{v_{n}}{\sqrt{n}}.

Proof:

We begin by writing the expected value

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\left|\overline{v}_{n}-v_{n}\right|\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left|\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(\beta_{j,n}^{r}(\overline{M}_{j}^{r}-M_{j}^{r})+\beta_{j,n}^{c}(\overline{M}_{j}^{c}-M_{j}^{c})\right)\right|\right].

(11)

From the triangle inequality and the linearity of expectation, we get

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left|\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(\beta_{j,n}^{r}(\overline{M}_{j}^{r}-M_{j}^{r})+\beta_{j,n}^{c}(\overline{M}_{j}^{c}-M_{j}^{c})\right)\right|\right]

(12)

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(\left|\beta_{j,n}^{r}\right|{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left|\overline{M}_{j}^{r}-M_{j}^{r}\right|\right]+\left|\beta_{j,n}^{c}\right|{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left|\overline{M}_{j}^{c}-M_{j}^{c}\right|\right]\right).

(13)

Similar to
IV.6
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left|\overline{M}_{j}^{r}-M_{j}^{r}\right|\right]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\left[\left|\left((\lambda^{r}-k)X^{r}_{j-1}-\lambda^{c}X^{c}_{j-1}\right)\left(\frac{1}{Y_{j-1}}-\frac{1}{(j-1)(\tau-1)+Y_{0}}\right)\right|\right],

(14)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left|\overline{M}_{j}^{c}-M_{j}^{c}\right|\right]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\left[\left|\left((\lambda^{r}-k)X^{c}_{j-1}+\lambda^{c}X^{r}_{j-1}\right)\left(\frac{1}{Y_{j-1}}-\frac{1}{(j-1)(\tau-1)+Y_{0}}\right)\right|\right].

(15)

Focusing on (
14
), we get

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left|\left((\lambda^{r}-k)X^{r}_{j-1}-\lambda^{c}X^{c}_{j-1}\right)\left(\frac{1}{Y_{j-1}}-\frac{1}{(j-1)(\tau-1)+Y_{0}}\right)\right|\right]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\left[\left|\left((\lambda^{r}-k)\frac{X^{r}_{j-1}}{Y_{j-1}}-\lambda^{c}\frac{X^{c}_{j-1}}{Y_{j-1}}\right)\left(\frac{(j-1)(\tau-1)+Y_{0}-Y_{j-1}}{(j-1)(\tau-1)+Y_{0}}\right)\right|\right]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle c.s}}{{\leqslant}}\left|\lambda^{r}-k\right|\left({\mathbb{E}}\left[\left|\frac{X_{j-1}^{r}}{Y_{j-1}}\right|^{2}\right]{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left|\frac{(j-1)(\tau-1)+Y_{0}-Y_{j-1}}{(j-1)(\tau-1)+Y_{0}}\right|^{2}\right]\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle+\left|\lambda^{c}\right|\left({\mathbb{E}}\left[\left|\frac{X_{j-1}^{c}}{Y_{j-1}}\right|^{2}\right]{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left|\frac{(j-1)(\tau-1)+Y_{0}-Y_{j-1}}{(j-1)(\tau-1)+Y_{0}}\right|^{2}\right]\right)^{1/2}.

(16)

Similarly, for (
15
) we get

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left|\left((\lambda^{r}-k)X^{c}_{j-1}-\lambda^{c}X^{r}_{j-1}\right)\left(\frac{1}{Y_{j-1}}-\frac{1}{(j-1)(\tau-1)+Y_{0}}\right)\right|\right]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle c.s}}{{\leqslant}}\left|\lambda^{r}-k\right|\left({\mathbb{E}}\left[\left|\frac{X_{j-1}^{c}}{Y_{j-1}}\right|^{2}\right]{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left|\frac{(j-1)(\tau-1)+Y_{0}-Y_{j-1}}{(j-1)(\tau-1)+Y_{0}}\right|^{2}\right]\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle+\left|\lambda^{c}\right|\left({\mathbb{E}}\left[\left|\frac{X_{j-1}^{r}}{Y_{j-1}}\right|^{2}\right]{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left|\frac{(j-1)(\tau-1)+Y_{0}-Y_{j-1}}{(j-1)(\tau-1)+Y_{0}}\right|^{2}\right]\right)^{1/2}.

(17)

From Lemma
IV.9
, for every
\epsilon>0
, there exists
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that for every
j\geqslant N
,
{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left|\frac{X_{j-1}^{r}}{Y_{j-1}}\right|^{2}\right]<\epsilon
and
{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left|\frac{X_{j-1}^{c}}{Y_{j-1}}\right|^{2}\right]<\epsilon
.
In addition, from (
3
) we have

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left|\frac{(j-1)(\tau-1)+Y_{0}-Y_{j-1}}{(j-1)(\tau-1)+Y_{0}}\right|^{2}\right]\leqslant\frac{C_{0}}{j-1}

for some constant
C_{0}
.
Thus, taking
N
large enough, for
n,j\geqslant N
, (
16
) and (
17
), together with bounding
\left|\beta_{j,n}^{r}\right|
and
\left|\beta_{j,n}^{c}\right|
with (
9
) and (
10
) we get:

\displaystyle\left|\beta_{j,n}^{r}\right|{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left|\overline{M}_{j}^{r}-M_{j}^{r}\right|\right]+\left|\beta_{j,n}^{c}\right|{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left|\overline{M}_{j}^{c}-M_{j}^{c}\right|\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant 2\left((|\alpha^{r}|+|\alpha^{c}|)\left(\frac{n}{j+1}\right)^{\frac{\lambda^{r}-k}{\tau-1}}+O\left(n^{\frac{\lambda^{r}-k}{\tau-1}-1}\right)\right)\left(\left|\lambda^{r}-k\right|+\left|\lambda^{c}\right|\right)\sqrt{\frac{C_{0}}{j-1}}\sqrt{\epsilon}.

(18)

Notice that for the sum of the first
N
arguments,
{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left|\overline{M}_{j}^{r}-M_{j}^{r}\right|\right]
and
{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left|\overline{M}_{j}^{c}-M_{j}^{c}\right|\right]
are bounded and
\left|\beta_{j,n}^{r}\right|,\left|\beta_{j,n}^{r}\right|=O\left(n^{\frac{\lambda^{r}-k}{\tau-1}}\right)
thus we get:

\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left(\left|\beta_{j,n}^{r}\right|{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left|\overline{M}_{j}^{r}-M_{j}^{r}\right|\right]+\left|\beta_{j,n}^{c}\right|{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left|\overline{M}_{j}^{c}-M_{j}^{c}\right|\right]\right)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}O\left(n^{\frac{\lambda^{r}-k}{\tau-1}}\right)=O\left(n^{\frac{\lambda^{r}-k}{\tau-1}-\frac{1}{2}}\right)

Since we take
n\to\infty
, this sum decays to
0
.
Using the fact that
|\alpha^{r}|+|\alpha^{c}|
is bounded, putting everything together we get:

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left|\overline{v}_{n}-v_{n}\right|\right]=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(\left|\beta_{j,n}^{r}\right|{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left|\overline{M}_{j}^{r}-M_{j}^{r}\right|\right]+\left|\beta_{j,n}^{c}\right|{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left|\overline{M}_{j}^{c}-M_{j}^{c}\right|\right]\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{C}{\sqrt{n}}\sqrt{\epsilon}\sum_{j=N}^{n}\frac{1}{\sqrt{j-1}}\left(\left(\frac{n}{j+1}\right)^{\frac{\lambda^{r}-k}{\tau-1}}+O\left(n^{\frac{\lambda^{r}-k}{\tau-1}-1}\right)\right)+O\left(n^{\frac{\lambda^{r}-k}{\tau-1}-\frac{1}{2}}\right)

(19)

for some constant
C
.
We may increase the sum and write

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left|\overline{v}_{n}-v_{n}\right|\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{C}{\sqrt{n}}\sqrt{\epsilon}\sum_{j=2}^{n}\frac{1}{\sqrt{j-1}}\left(\left(\frac{n}{j+1}\right)^{\frac{\lambda^{r}-k}{\tau-1}}+O\left(n^{\frac{\lambda^{r}-k}{\tau-1}-1}\right)\right)+O\left(n^{\frac{\lambda^{r}-k}{\tau-1}-\frac{1}{2}}\right).

(20)

Since
\lambda^{r}<k+\frac{1}{2}(\tau-1)
,
we have
\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}O\left(n^{\frac{\lambda^{r}-k}{\tau-1}-1}\right)=o(1/n)
, and thus

\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{j=2}^{n}\frac{1}{\sqrt{j-1}}O\left(n^{\frac{\lambda^{r}-k}{\tau-1}-1}\right)\to 0

as
n\to\infty
(one may use integration to bound the sum and obtain that
\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\sqrt{j}}=O(\sqrt{n})
).
Moreover, we have
O\left(n^{\frac{\lambda^{r}-k}{\tau-1}-\frac{1}{2}}\right)=o(1)
.
Thus,

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left|\overline{v}_{n}-v_{n}\right|\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\lim_{n\to\infty}Cn^{\frac{\lambda^{r}-k}{\tau-1}-\frac{1}{2}}\sqrt{\epsilon}\sum_{j=2}^{n}\frac{1}{\sqrt{j-1}}\left(\frac{1}{j+1}\right)^{\frac{\lambda^{r}-k}{\tau-1}}

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}Cn^{\frac{\lambda^{r}-k}{\tau-1}-\frac{1}{2}}\sqrt{\epsilon}\sum_{j=2}^{n}\left(\frac{1}{j-1}\right)^{\frac{\lambda^{r}-k}{\tau-1}+\frac{1}{2}}\left(\frac{j-1}{j+1}\right)^{\frac{\lambda^{r}-k}{\tau-1}},

(21)

where the last equality follows by multiplying by
\left(\frac{j-1}{j-1}\right)^{\frac{\lambda^{r}-k}{\tau-1}}
.
Since
\left(\frac{j-1}{j+1}\right)\to 1
as
j
increases, we may disregard it, and get

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left|\overline{v}_{n}-v_{n}\right|\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\lim_{n\to\infty}Cn^{\frac{\lambda^{r}-k}{\tau-1}-\frac{1}{2}}\sqrt{\epsilon}\sum_{j=2}^{n}\left(\frac{1}{j-1}\right)^{\frac{\lambda^{r}-k}{\tau-1}+\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}Cn^{\frac{\lambda^{r}-k}{\tau-1}-\frac{1}{2}}\sqrt{\epsilon}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(\frac{1}{j}\right)^{\frac{\lambda^{r}-k}{\tau-1}+\frac{1}{2}}.

(22)

For
0<t<1
, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(\frac{1}{j}\right)^{t}}{n^{1-t}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-t}.

(23)

Since
\frac{\lambda^{r}-k}{\tau-1}<\frac{1}{2}
we may write (
22
) as

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left|\overline{v}_{n}-v_{n}\right|\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\lim_{n\to\infty}Cn^{\frac{\lambda^{r}-k}{\tau-1}-\frac{1}{2}}\sqrt{\epsilon}\frac{n^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\lambda^{r}-k}{\tau-1}}}{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\lambda^{r}-k}{\tau-1}}

\displaystyle=\tilde{C}\sqrt{\epsilon}

(24)

for some constant
\tilde{C}
.
Since this is true for every
\epsilon>0
, we obtain the wanted result.
∎

As an immediate corollary, we obtain the following.

Corollary IV.11

If for every non-leading eigenvalue
\lambda=\lambda^{r}+\mathbf{i}\lambda^{c}
of
\mathbf{M}^{(k)}
,
\lambda^{r}<k+\frac{1}{2}(\tau-1)
, then

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\left(\alpha_{0}^{r}X^{r(0)}_{n}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(\alpha_{i}^{r}X^{r(i)}_{n}+\alpha_{i}^{c}X^{c(i)}_{n}\right)+\sum_{i=2m+1}^{d^{k}-1}\alpha_{i}^{r}X^{r(i)}_{n}\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle L^{1}}}{{\sim}}

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\left(\alpha_{0}^{r}\nabla X^{r(0)}_{j}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(\beta_{j,n}^{r(i)}M_{j}^{r(i)}+\beta_{j,n}^{c(i)}M_{j}^{c(i)}\right)+\sum_{i=2m+1}^{d^{k}-1}\beta_{j,n}^{r(i)}M_{j}^{r(i)}\right)

Proof:

The proof follows immediately from Claim
IV.10
, together with recalling that
\varepsilon_{n}=O\left(n^{\frac{\lambda^{r}-k}{\tau-1}}\right)
, and the assumption that
\frac{\lambda^{r}-k}{\tau-1}<1/2
. First notice that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\alpha_{0}^{r}X^{r(0)}_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\alpha_{0}^{r}\left(X^{r(0)}_{0}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\nabla X^{r(0)}_{j}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\alpha_{0}^{r}X^{r(0)}_{0}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\alpha_{0}^{r}\nabla X^{r(0)}_{j}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle L^{1}}}{{\sim}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\alpha_{0}^{r}\nabla X^{r(0)}_{j}.

In addition we notice for every
i
we have:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\left(\alpha^{r}X^{r}_{n}+\alpha^{c}X^{c}_{n}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum^{n}_{j=1}\left(\beta^{r}_{j,n}\overline{M}^{r}_{j}+\beta^{c}_{j,n}\overline{M}^{c}_{j}\right)-\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\varepsilon_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum^{n}_{j=1}\left(\beta^{r}_{j,n}\overline{M}^{r}_{j}+\beta^{c}_{j,n}\overline{M}^{c}_{j}\right)-O\left(n^{\frac{\lambda^{r}-k}{\tau-1}-\frac{1}{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle L^{1}}}{{\sim}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum^{n}_{j=1}\left(\beta^{r}_{j,n}\overline{M}^{r}_{j}+\beta^{c}_{j,n}\overline{M}^{c}_{j}\right)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle L^{1}}}{{\sim}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum^{n}_{j=1}\left(\beta^{r}_{j,n}M^{r}_{j}+\beta^{c}_{j,n}M^{c}_{j}\right).

Putting everything together proves the claim.
∎

In the next section, we use the martingale central limit theorem to prove Theorem
III.8
.

V
Martingale Central Limit Theorem

In this section, we use the sums
\overline{v}_{n}
and
v_{n}
, together with the properties shown above, to prove the CLT for the projection vector
\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\mathbf{X}_{n}
which implies asymptotic joint normal distribution of
\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\mathbf{X}_{n}
.
We use the following CLT for martingales
[
10
, Theorem 3.2 and Corollary 3.1]
, stated here using our notations and adjusted to our needs.

Theorem V.1

(Th. 3.2 and Cor. 3.1 in
[
10
]
)

Let
\left\{S_{n,j},\mathcal{F}_{n,j},1\leqslant j\leqslant n,n\geqslant 1\right\}
be a zero-mean, square integrable martingale array and let
\left\{\mathcal{X}_{n,j}\right\}_{n,j}
be its corresponding difference sequence.
Let
\sigma^{2}
be an a.s. finite random variable.
Suppose that the following conditions hold:

1.

The conditional Lindeberg condition, i.e., for every
\epsilon>0
,

\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\mathcal{X}_{n,j}^{2}\;\mathbf{I}_{\left[|\mathcal{X}_{n,j}|>\epsilon\right]}\mid\mathcal{F}_{n,j-1}\right]\xrightarrow{\Pr}0,

as
n\to\infty
, where
\mathbf{I}_{\left[\text{condition}\right]}
is an indicator function for the condition listed in the square brackets.

2.

The conditional variance condition, i.e.,

\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\mathcal{X}_{n,j}^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{n,j-1}\right]\xrightarrow{\Pr}\sigma^{2},

as
n\to\infty
.

Then
\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathcal{X}_{n,j}\xrightarrow{\Pr}Z
, where
Z
is a random variable with characteristic function
{\mathbb{E}}\left[\exp(-\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}t^{2})\right]
.

We use Theorem
V.1
with
\mathcal{F}_{n,j}
being the
\sigma
-algebra generated by
\mathsf{\mathbf{ct}}_{l}
for
l\leqslant j
, and

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}_{n,j}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\left(\alpha_{0}^{r}\nabla X^{r(0)}_{j}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(\beta_{j,n}^{r(i)}M_{j}^{r(i)}+\beta_{j,n}^{c(i)}M_{j}^{c(i)}\right)+\sum_{i=2m+1}^{d^{k}-1}\beta_{j,n}^{r(i)}M_{j}^{r(i)}\right).

(25)

Clearly, since
M_{j}^{r(i)},M_{j}^{c(i)}
and
\nabla X_{j}^{(0)}
are martingale differences for every
i
, their sum over
i
is also a martingale difference.

First, notice that proving that
\mathcal{X}_{n,j}
satisfies the conditional Lindeberg condition and the conditional variance condition implies that

\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\alpha_{0}X^{r(0)}_{j}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(\alpha_{i}^{r}X^{r(i)}_{n}+\alpha_{i}^{c}X^{c(i)}_{n}\right)+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=2m+1}^{d^{k}-1}\alpha_{i}^{r}X^{r(i)}_{n}

converges in distribution to a normally distributed random variable
Z
.
Indeed, from Corollary
IV.11
, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\alpha_{0}X^{r(0)}_{j}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(\alpha_{i}^{r}X^{r(i)}_{n}+\alpha_{i}^{c}X^{c(i)}_{n}\right)+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=2m+1}^{d^{k}-1}\alpha_{i}^{r}X^{r(i)}_{n}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle L^{1}}}{{\sim}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(\alpha_{0}^{r}\nabla X^{r(0)}_{j}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(\beta_{j,n}^{r(i)}M_{j}^{r(i)}+\beta_{j,n}^{c(i)}M_{j}^{c(i)}\right)+\sum_{i=2m+1}^{d^{k}-1}\beta_{j,n}^{r(i)}M_{j}^{r(i)}\right)

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{d}Z.

(26)

We now show the conditions, starting with the conditional Lindeberg condition.

V-A

Conditional Lindeberg condition

In this section we show the conditional Lindeberg condition.
Before diving into the proof, we provide a bird’s-eye view of the proof.
At first, we bound above
|M_{j}^{r(i)}|
and
|M_{j}^{c(i)}|
for every
j
and
i
.
We then use this bound to claim that the expected value of a sum of
\beta
s and
M
s goes to
0
.
Using this, we show that the nonconditional Lindeberg condition follows, and that in our case, the unconditional Lindeberg condition is equivalent to the conditional Lindeberg condition.

We begin with the upper bound on
|M_{j}^{r(i)}|
and
|M_{j}^{c(i)}|
.

Lemma V.2

Let
\lambda_{i}
be a non-leading eigenvalue. Then there exists a constant
C_{1}
such that for every
j
,
|M_{j}^{r(i)}|<C_{1}
and
|M_{j}^{c(i)}|<C_{1}
.

Proof:

Fix
i
, by definition we have that
\nabla X^{r(i)}_{j}
,
\nabla X^{c(i)}_{n}
and
\mathsf{\mathbf{fr}}_{j}
are bounded. For simplicity, since
i
is fixed we remove it from the notation and write
X^{r}_{j},X^{c}_{j},M_{j}^{r},M_{j}^{c}
.
Thus:

\displaystyle\left|M^{r}_{j}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\nabla X^{r}_{j}-\frac{(\lambda^{r}-k)X^{r}_{j-1}-\lambda^{c}X^{c}_{j-1}}{Y_{j-1}}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\nabla X^{r}_{j}-\frac{(\lambda^{r}-k)\mathbf{u}_{r}\mathsf{\mathbf{ct}}_{j-1}-\lambda^{c}\mathbf{u}_{c}\mathsf{\mathbf{ct}}_{j-1}}{Y_{j-1}}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\nabla X^{r}_{j}-\left((\lambda^{r}-k)\mathbf{u}_{r}-\lambda^{c}\mathbf{u}_{c}\right)\mathsf{\mathbf{fr}}_{j-1}\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant C

for some constant
C
. Similar arguments also imply that
|M^{c}_{n}|<C^{\prime}
for some constant
C^{\prime}
. We now take
C_{1}^{(i)}=\max\left\{C,C^{\prime}\right\}
.
Since this is true for every
i
, we define
C_{1}
to be the maximum of
C_{1}^{(i)}
over all
i
.
∎

Next, we show that the sum decays to zero.

Claim V.3

Assume that for every non-leading eigenvalue
\lambda_{i}
, the real part satisfies
\lambda_{i}^{r}<k+\frac{1}{2}(\tau-1)
.
Then for
n\to\infty

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\max_{1\leqslant j\leqslant n}\left|\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\left(\alpha_{0}^{r}\nabla X^{r(0)}_{j}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(\beta^{r(i)}_{j,n}M^{r(i)}_{j}+\beta^{c(i)}_{j,n}M^{c(i)}_{j}\right)+\sum_{i=2m+1}^{d^{k}-1}\beta^{r(i)}_{j,n}M^{r(i)}_{j}\right)\right|\right]\to 0.

Proof:

Fix
j
. We first notice that we can take
\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}
out of the expectation.
From the triangle inequality, we get

\displaystyle\left|\alpha_{0}^{r}\nabla X^{r(0)}_{j}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(\beta^{r(i)}_{j,n}M^{r(i)}_{j}+\beta^{c(i)}_{j,n}M^{c(i)}_{j}\right)+\sum_{i=2m+1}^{d^{k}-1}\beta^{r(i)}_{j,n}M^{r(i)}_{j}\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\left|\alpha_{0}\right|\left|\nabla X^{r(0)}_{j}\right|+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(\left|\beta^{r(i)}_{j,n}\right|\left|M^{r(i)}_{j}\right|+\left|\beta^{c(i)}_{j,n}\right|\left|M^{c(i)}_{j}\right|\right)+\sum_{i=2m+1}^{d^{k}-1}\left|\beta^{r(i)}_{j,n}\right|\left|M^{r(i)}_{j}\right|.

We know
\left|\nabla X^{r(0)}_{j}\right|
is bounded, thus
\left|\alpha_{0}\right|\left|\nabla X^{r(0)}_{j}\right|\leqslant\bar{C}
for some
\bar{C}
.
We now use Claim
V.2
and obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(\left|\beta^{r(i)}_{j,n}\right|\left|M^{r(i)}_{j}\right|+\left|\beta^{c(i)}_{j,n}\right|\left|M^{c(i)}_{j}\right|\right)+\sum_{i=2m+1}^{d^{k}-1}\left|\beta^{r(i)}_{j,n}\right|\left|M^{r(i)}_{j}\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant C_{1}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(\left|\beta^{r(i)}_{j,n}\right|+\left|\beta^{c(i)}_{j,n}\right|\right)+\sum_{i=2m+1}^{d^{k}-1}\left|\beta^{r(i)}_{j,n}\right|\right).

Using the bounds (
9
) and (
10
) on
\beta_{j,n}
, we get

\displaystyle C_{1}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(\left|\beta^{r(i)}_{j,n}\right|+\left|\beta^{c(i)}_{j,n}\right|\right)+\sum_{i=2m+1}^{d^{k}-1}\left|\beta^{r(i)}_{j,n}\right|\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant C_{1}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}2\left(|\alpha_{i}^{r}|+|\alpha_{i}^{c}|\right)\left(\frac{n}{j+1}\right)^{\frac{\lambda_{i}^{r}-k}{\tau-1}}+\sum_{i=2m+1}^{d^{k}-1}|\alpha_{i}^{r}|\left(\frac{n}{j+1}\right)^{\frac{\lambda_{i}^{r}-k}{\tau-1}}+\sum_{i=1}^{d^{k}-1}O\left(n^{\frac{\lambda_{i}^{r}-k}{\tau-1}-1}\right)\right).

(27)

Since for every
i
, we have
\lambda_{i}^{r}<k+\frac{1}{2}(\tau-1)
, there exists some
\epsilon>0
such that for all
i
,

\left(\frac{n}{j+1}\right)^{\frac{\lambda_{i}^{r}-k}{\tau-1}}=O\left(n^{\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon}\right).

Thus, we get

\displaystyle\left|\alpha_{0}\right|\left|\nabla X^{r(0)}_{j}\right|+C_{1}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}2\left(|\alpha_{i}^{r}|+|\alpha_{i}^{c}|\right)\left(\frac{n}{j+1}\right)^{\frac{\lambda_{i}^{r}-k}{\tau-1}}+\sum_{i=2m+1}^{d^{k}-1}|\alpha_{i}^{r}|\left(\frac{n}{j+1}\right)^{\frac{\lambda_{i}^{r}-k}{\tau-1}}+\sum_{i=1}^{d^{k}-1}O\left(n^{\frac{\lambda_{i}^{r}-k}{\tau-1}-1}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\bar{C}+C_{1}\left(O\left(n^{\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=O\left(n^{\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon}\right).

Putting everything together, we get

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\max_{1\leqslant j\leqslant n}\left|\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\left(\alpha_{0}^{r}\nabla X^{r(0)}_{j}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(\beta^{r(i)}_{j,n}M^{r(i)}_{j}+\beta^{c(i)}_{j,n}M^{c(i)}_{j}\right)+\sum_{i=2m+1}^{d^{k}-1}\beta^{r(i)}_{j,n}M^{r(i)}_{j}\right)\right|\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\max_{1\leqslant j\leqslant n}O\left(n^{\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant O\left(n^{-\epsilon}\right)

\displaystyle\to 0,

and the claim follows.
∎

We now show that Claim
V.3
implies the conditional Lindeberg condition.
Let us denote

\mathcal{X}_{n,j}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\left(\alpha_{0}^{r}\nabla X^{r(0)}_{j}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(\beta_{j,n}^{r(i)}M_{j}^{r(i)}+\beta_{j,n}^{c(i)}M_{j}^{c(i)}\right)+\sum_{i=2m+1}^{d^{k}-1}\beta_{j,n}^{r(i)}M_{j}^{r(i)}\right).

First, notice that from Markov’s inequality

\displaystyle\Pr\left(\max_{1\leqslant j\leqslant n}|\mathcal{X}_{n,j}|>\epsilon\right)\leqslant\frac{{\mathbb{E}}\left[\max_{1\leqslant j\leqslant n}|\mathcal{X}_{n,j}|\right]}{\epsilon}.

Thus, for every
\epsilon>0
, as
n\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\Pr\left(\max_{1\leqslant j\leqslant n}|\mathcal{X}_{n,j}|>\epsilon\right)\to 0.

(28)

For every
n
, denote also
T_{n}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathcal{X}_{n,j}^{2}
and we claim that
T_{n}
is uniformly bounded under
L^{1}
, meaning
\sup_{n}{\mathbb{E}}\left[|T_{n}|\right]<\infty
, which implies that
\left\{T_{n}\right\}_{n}
is uniformly integrable. This, will be used to show the unconditional Lindeberg condition and then the conditional Lindeberg condition.
Following the same calculation as in the proof of Claim
V.3
until (
27
), we get

\displaystyle\sup_{n}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left|\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathcal{X}_{n,j}^{2}\right|\right]\leqslant\sup_{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left|\mathcal{X}_{n,j}^{2}\right|\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\sup_{n}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left|\left(\bar{C}+C\sum_{i=1}^{d^{k}-1}\left(\frac{n}{j+1}\right)^{\frac{\lambda_{i}^{r}-k}{\tau-1}}+O\left(n^{\frac{\lambda_{i}^{r}-k}{\tau-1}-1}\right)\right)^{2}\right|,

for some constant
C
.
We may further increase the sum by selecting the non-leading eigenvalue with the maximal real part.
Denote
\lambda^{r}_{max}=\max_{i=1,2,\dots,(d^{k}-1)}\lambda_{i}^{r}
, we have

\displaystyle\sup_{n}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left|\left(\bar{C}+C\sum_{i=1}^{d^{k}-1}\left(\frac{n}{j+1}\right)^{\frac{\lambda_{i}^{r}-k}{\tau-1}}+O\left(n^{\frac{\lambda_{i}^{r}-k}{\tau-1}-1}\right)\right)^{2}\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\sup_{n}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left|\left(\bar{C}+C(d^{k}-1)\left(\frac{n}{j+1}\right)^{\frac{\lambda_{max}^{r}-k}{\tau-1}}+O\left(n^{\frac{\lambda_{max}^{r}-k}{\tau-1}-1}\right)\right)^{2}\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\sup_{n}\left(C(d^{k}-1)\right)^{2}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left|\left(\frac{\bar{C}}{C(d^{k}-1)}+\left(\frac{n}{j+1}\right)^{\frac{\lambda_{max}^{r}-k}{\tau-1}}+O\left(n^{\frac{\lambda_{max}^{r}-k}{\tau-1}-1}\right)\right)^{2}\right|

\displaystyle=\sup_{n}C_{2}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(\bar{C}_{1}+\left(\frac{n}{j+1}\right)^{\frac{\lambda_{max}^{r}-k}{\tau-1}}+O\left(n^{\frac{\lambda_{max}^{r}-k}{\tau-1}-1}\right)\right)^{2},

where
C_{2}=\left(C(d^{k}-1)\right)^{2}
and
\bar{C}_{1}=\frac{\bar{C}}{C(d^{k}-1)}
are constants.
We fix
n
and obtain (ignoring the constant
C_{2}
at the beginning)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(\bar{C}_{1}+\left(\frac{n}{j+1}\right)^{\frac{\lambda_{max}^{r}-k}{\tau-1}}+O\left(n^{\frac{\lambda_{max}^{r}-k}{\tau-1}-1}\right)\right)^{2}=

\displaystyle\bar{C}_{1}^{2}+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(\frac{n}{j+1}\right)^{2\frac{\lambda_{max}^{r}-k}{\tau-1}}+\frac{2}{n}\bar{C}_{1}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(\frac{n}{j+1}\right)^{\frac{\lambda_{max}^{r}-k}{\tau-1}}+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}O\left(n^{2\frac{\lambda_{max}^{r}-k}{\tau-1}-1}\right).

We recall that
\frac{\lambda^{r}-k}{\tau-1}<\frac{1}{2}
, thus for some small
\varepsilon>0
and constant
C_{3}
, we use (
23
) to bound the expression above by

\displaystyle\bar{C}_{1}^{2}+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(\frac{n}{j+1}\right)^{1-2\varepsilon}+\frac{2}{n}\bar{C}_{1}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(\frac{n}{j+1}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}-\varepsilon}+O(n^{-\varepsilon})

\displaystyle\leqslant\bar{C}_{1}^{2}+\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}+\frac{2}{\varepsilon+\frac{1}{2}}\bar{C}_{1}+C_{3}

\displaystyle=O(1).

Plugging this into the original expression, we get

\displaystyle\sup_{n}C_{2}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(\bar{C}+\left(\frac{n}{j+1}\right)^{\frac{\lambda_{max}^{r}-k}{\tau-1}}+O\left(n^{\frac{\lambda_{max}^{r}-k}{\tau-1}-1}\right)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant C_{2}\left(\bar{C}_{1}^{2}+\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}+\frac{2}{\varepsilon+\frac{1}{2}}\bar{C}_{1}+C_{3}\right)

\displaystyle=O(1).

(29)

Thus,
\left\{T_{n}\right\}_{n}
is uniformly bounded in
L^{1}
, which implies that it is uniformly integrable.
By the definition of uniform integrability,

\lim_{L\to\infty}\left(\sup_{n}{\mathbb{E}}\left[|T_{n}|\mathbf{I}_{[|T_{n}|>L]}\right]\right)=0.

For any
L
and
\epsilon
, we notice that

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\mathcal{X}_{n,j}^{2}\mathbf{I}_{[|\mathcal{X}_{n,j}|>\epsilon]}\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathcal{X}_{n,j}^{2}\mathbf{I}_{[\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathcal{X}_{n,j}^{2}>L]}\right]+L\Pr\left(\max_{1\leqslant j\leqslant n}|\mathcal{X}_{n,j}|>\epsilon\right).

Indeed, if
\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathcal{X}_{n,j}^{2}\leqslant L
, then for all
j
,
\mathcal{X}_{n,j}^{2}\leqslant L
, and

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\mathcal{X}_{n,j}^{2}\mathbf{I}_{[|\mathcal{X}_{n,j}|>\epsilon]}\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathcal{X}_{n,j}^{2}\mathbf{I}_{[\max_{j}|\mathcal{X}_{n,j}|>\epsilon]}\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant L{\mathbb{E}}\left[\mathbf{I}_{[\max_{j}|\mathcal{X}_{n,j}|>\epsilon]}\right]

\displaystyle=L\Pr\left(\max_{1\leqslant j\leqslant n}|\mathcal{X}_{n,j}|>\epsilon\right).

Else, we have
\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathcal{X}_{n,j}^{2}>L
and we have

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\mathcal{X}_{n,j}^{2}\mathbf{I}_{[|\mathcal{X}_{n,j}|>\epsilon]}\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathcal{X}_{n,j}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathcal{X}_{n,j}^{2}\mathbf{I}_{[\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathcal{X}_{n,j}^{2}>L]}\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathcal{X}_{n,j}^{2}\mathbf{I}_{[\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathcal{X}_{n,j}^{2}>L]}\right]+L\Pr\left(\max_{1\leqslant j\leqslant n}|\mathcal{X}_{n,j}|>\epsilon\right).

Let
\delta>0
. From uniform integrability, select
L
large enough so that

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathcal{X}_{n,j}^{2}\mathbf{I}_{[\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathcal{X}_{n,j}^{2}>L]}\right]\leqslant\delta

and then use (
28
) to obtain that for large
n
,

L\cdot\Pr\left(\max_{1\leqslant j\leqslant n}|\mathcal{X}_{n,j}|>\epsilon\right)<\delta.

We get

\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\mathcal{X}_{n,j}^{2}\mathbf{I}_{[|\mathcal{X}_{n,j}|>\epsilon]}\right]<2\delta,

so for every
\epsilon>0
,

\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\mathcal{X}_{n,j}^{2}\mathbf{I}_{[|\mathcal{X}_{n,j}|>\epsilon]}\right]\to 0

which is the
unconditional
Lindeberg condition.

To show that the conditional Lindeberg condition holds, we use
[
10
, Theorem 2.23]
, which claims that if
T^{\prime}_{n}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\mathcal{X}_{n,j}^{2}~|~\mathcal{F}_{j-1}\right]
is uniformly integrable, then the conditional Lindeberg condition is equivalent to the unconditional Lindeberg condition. To show uniform integrability, we show (again) that
\sup_{n}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left|T^{\prime}_{n}\right|\right]<\infty
. We have

\displaystyle\sup_{n}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left|T^{\prime}_{n}\right|\right]

\displaystyle=\sup_{n}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left|\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\mathcal{X}_{n,j}^{2}~|~\mathcal{F}_{j-1}\right]\right|\right]

From the triangle inequality and Jensen’s inequality, we get

\displaystyle\leqslant\sup_{n}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left|{\mathbb{E}}\left[\mathcal{X}_{n,j}^{2}~|~\mathcal{F}_{j-1}\right]\right|\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\sup_{n}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left|\mathcal{X}_{n,j}^{2}\right|~|~\mathcal{F}_{j-1}\right]\right]

\displaystyle=\sup_{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\mathbb{E}}\left[{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left|\mathcal{X}_{n,j}^{2}\right|~|~\mathcal{F}_{j-1}\right]\right].

From the law of total expectation and (
29
), we obtain

\displaystyle=\sup_{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left|\mathcal{X}_{n,j}^{2}\right|\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant C_{2}\left(\bar{C}_{1}^{2}+\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}+\frac{2}{\varepsilon+\frac{1}{2}}\bar{C}_{1}+C_{3}\right)

\displaystyle=O\left(1\right).

Thus
T^{\prime}_{n}
is uniformly bounded and uniformly integrable, meaning the conditional Lindeberg condition holds.

Next, we work our way to show the conditional variance condition.
To that end, we require the notion of the asymptotic differences covariance matrix, explained in the next subsection.

V-B

The Asymptotic Differences Covariance Matrix

In this subsection, we derive several auxiliary results needed to verify the conditional variance condition in the following section.
The primary objective is to determine the asymptotic covariance matrix of the increments of the counting vector,
\nabla\mathsf{\mathbf{ct}}_{n}
.
We begin by computing, for
k
-tuples
m_{a},m_{b}\in\mathcal{A}^{k}
, the conditional second moment

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\nabla\mathsf{ct}^{(k)}_{n}(m_{a})\nabla\mathsf{ct}^{(k)}_{n}(m_{b})~\mid~\mathcal{F}_{n-1}\right],

where
\mathcal{F}_{n-1}
is the
\sigma
-algebra generated by
\mathsf{\mathbf{ct}}^{(k)}_{j}
for
j\leqslant n-1
.

We begin with a lemma regarding the limit of the above expression.

Lemma V.4

Let
S=(\mathcal{A},w,(\vartheta,\mathbf{P}))

Lemma V.8

Proof:

We start by noticing that symmetry follows immediately from the definition

\displaystyle\Theta^{T}

\displaystyle:=\lim_{n\to\infty}\left({\mathbb{E}}\left[\left(\nabla\mathsf{\mathbf{ct}}^{(k)}_{n}-(\tau-1)\mathbf{r}\right)\left(\nabla\mathsf{\mathbf{ct}}^{(k)}_{n}-(\tau-1)\mathbf{r}\right)^{T}~|~\mathcal{F}_{n-1}\right]\right)^{T}

To show positive semi-definite, we need to show that for every real column vector
\mathbf{u}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d^{k}}
, we have
\mathbf{u}^{T}\Theta\mathbf{u}\geqslant 0
.
To that end, let
\mathbf{v}
be a real column vector

\mathbf{u}^{T}(\mathbf{v}\mathbf{v}^{T})\mathbf{u}=(\mathbf{u}^{T}\mathbf{v})(\mathbf{u}^{T}\mathbf{v})^{T}

\mathbf{u}^{T}(\mathbf{v}\mathbf{v}^{T})\mathbf{u}=(\mathbf{u}^{T}\mathbf{v})^{2}\geqslant 0.

Thus, for every column vector
\mathbf{v}

\mathbf{v}\mathbf{v}^{T}
is positive semi-definite.
Now notice that for every

(2k-1)
-tuple

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}\Theta\mathbf{u}^{T}

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\mathbf{u}\cdot(\nabla\mathsf{\mathbf{ct}}_{n}-(\tau-1)\mathbf{r})\cdot(\nabla\mathsf{\mathbf{ct}}_{n}-(\tau-1)\mathbf{r})^{T}\cdot\mathbf{u}^{T}~|~\mathcal{F}_{n-1}\right]

-algebra generated by
\mathsf{\mathbf{ct}}_{j}
for

\sigma
-algebra generated by
\mathsf{\mathbf{ct}}^{(2k-1)}_{j}

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\mathbf{u}\cdot(\nabla\mathsf{\mathbf{ct}}_{n}-(\tau-1)\mathbf{r})(\cdot\nabla\mathsf{\mathbf{ct}}_{n}-(\tau-1)\mathbf{r})^{T}\cdot\mathbf{u}^{T}~|~\mathcal{F}_{n-1}\right]

VII
Non-Diagonalizable Substitution Matrices

In this section, we outline the proof for the case in which the substitution matrix
\mathbf{M}^{(k)}
is non-diagonalizable.
The proof follows a similar structure to the regular case, replacing the standard eigendecomposition with the
Jordan decomposition
.
The analysis in this case is much more involved, mainly due to the difference in the
\beta
s.
Since the core ideas are the same as in the diagonalizable case, we only sketch how to obtain the value for
\beta
s, while the rest of the analysis follows the exact same steps as in the diagonalizable case.
Notice that Lemma
V.18
, as stated above, requires the matrix
\mathbf{M}
to be diagonalizable. The lemma still holds even for the non-diagonalizable case, only stated with ”general eigenvectors” instead of ”eigenvectors”.

Suppose the Jordan decomposition of
\mathbf{M}^{(k)}
yields a Jordan block of size
H_{i}
for the eigenvalue
\lambda_{i}
.
We denote the corresponding chain of left generalized eigenvectors as:

\left(\mathbf{u}_{i,1},\,\mathbf{u}_{i,2},\,\mathbf{u}_{i,3},\,\dots,\,\mathbf{u}_{i,H_{i}}\right)

where
\mathbf{u}_{i,1}M^{(k)}=\lambda_{i}\mathbf{u}_{i,1}
and
\mathbf{u}_{i,h}M^{(k)}=\lambda_{i}\mathbf{u}_{i,h}+\mathbf{u}_{i,h-1}
for
h>1
.
For convenience, we define
\mathbf{u}_{i,0}:=\boldsymbol{0}
for all
i
, so that the recurrence relation holds uniformly:

\mathbf{u}_{i,h}M^{(k)}=\lambda_{i}\mathbf{u}_{i,h}+\mathbf{u}_{i,h-1}\quad\text{for all }h\geqslant 1.

We define the projections for the general case as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}_{i,h}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{u}_{i,h}^{r}+\mathbf{i}\,\mathbf{u}_{i,h}^{c},

\displaystyle X_{n}^{r(i,h)}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{u}_{i,h}^{r}\cdot\left(\mathsf{\mathbf{ct}}_{n}-n(\tau-1)\mathbf{r}\right),

\displaystyle X_{n}^{c(i,h)}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{u}_{i,h}^{c}\cdot\left(\mathsf{\mathbf{ct}}_{n}-n(\tau-1)\mathbf{r}\right).

Our goal is to establish a central limit theorem for the following linear combination of projections:

\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\alpha_{0}^{r}X^{r(0)}_{n}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{p-1}\sum^{H_{i}}_{h=1}\left(\alpha_{i,h}^{r}X^{r(i,h)}_{n}+\alpha_{i,h}^{c}X^{c(i,h)}_{n}\right)\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})

for every collection of real-valued constants
\alpha_{0}^{r},\,\alpha_{i,h}^{r},\,\alpha_{i,h}^{c}\in\mathbb{R}
.
We note that for real eigenvectors
X^{c(i,h)}_{n}=0
and
\alpha_{i,h}^{c}=0
, while for complex conjugate eigenvalue pairs
we retain only one representative from each pair and decompose it into its real and imaginary parts, as in the regular case.
Here
p
denotes the number of Jordan blocks in the decomposition, excluding blocks corresponding to the discarded eigenvalue
from each complex conjugate pair.

By Theorem
II.6
, the Jordan block associated with the maximal eigenvalue
\lambda_{0}
has size
1
,
hence
H_{0}=1
and the analysis of
X^{r(0)}_{n}
is identical to the regular case.
For Theorem
II.7
, we additionally require that for any defective eigenvalue satisfying
\mu_{a}(\lambda)>\mu_{g}(\lambda)
,
the real part is strictly bounded above by
k
, i.e.,
\lambda^{r}<k
.

To prove the CLT

\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\mathbf{X}_{n}\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma^{\prime}),

we compute the asymptotic covariance matrix of the projection vector

\displaystyle\mathbf{X}_{n}

\displaystyle:=\begin{bmatrix}X_{n}^{r(0)}\;&X_{n}^{r(1,1)}\;&X_{n}^{c(1,1)}\;&X_{n}^{r(1,2)}\;&X_{n}^{c(1,2)}\;&\cdots\;&X_{n}^{r(p-1,\,H_{p-1})}\;&X_{n}^{c(p-1,\,H_{p-1})}\end{bmatrix}^{T}.

To this end, we compute the conditional mean of the projection increments:

\displaystyle\nabla X_{n}^{(i,h)}

\displaystyle:=X_{n}^{(i,h)}-X_{n-1}^{(i,h)},

\displaystyle\nabla X_{n}^{(i,h)}

\displaystyle=\nabla X_{n}^{r(i,h)}+\mathbf{i}\,\nabla X_{n}^{c(i,h)},

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[\nabla X_{n}^{(i,h)}\mid\mathcal{F}_{n-1}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{u}_{i,h}\,\frac{1}{Y_{n-1}}(M^{(k)}-kI)\mathsf{\mathbf{ct}}_{n-1}=\frac{1}{Y_{n-1}}\!\left((\lambda_{i}-k)X_{n-1}^{(i,h)}+X_{n-1}^{(i,h-1)}\right),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[\nabla X_{n}^{r(i,h)}\mid\mathcal{F}_{n-1}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Y_{n-1}}\!\left((\lambda_{i}^{r}-k)X_{n-1}^{r(i,h)}-\lambda_{i}^{c}X_{n-1}^{c(i,h)}+X_{n-1}^{r(i,h-1)}\right),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[\nabla X_{n}^{c(i,h)}\mid\mathcal{F}_{n-1}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Y_{n-1}}\!\left((\lambda_{i}^{r}-k)X_{n-1}^{c(i,h)}+\lambda_{i}^{c}X_{n-1}^{r(i,h)}+X_{n-1}^{c(i,h-1)}\right),

and approximate the cumulative sum as a sum of martingale differences:

\displaystyle M^{r(i,h)}_{n}

\displaystyle:=\nabla X^{r(i,h)}_{n}-\frac{1}{Y_{n-1}}\!\left((\lambda_{i}^{r}-k)X_{n-1}^{r(i,h)}-\lambda_{i}^{c}X_{n-1}^{c(i,h)}+X_{n-1}^{r(i,h-1)}\right),

\displaystyle M^{c(i,h)}_{n}

\displaystyle:=\nabla X^{c(i,h)}_{n}-\frac{1}{Y_{n-1}}\!\left((\lambda_{i}^{r}-k)X_{n-1}^{c(i,h)}+\lambda_{i}^{c}X_{n-1}^{r(i,h)}+X_{n-1}^{c(i,h-1)}\right),

\displaystyle\overline{M}^{r(i,h)}_{n}

\displaystyle:=\nabla X^{r(i,h)}_{n}-\frac{1}{(n-1)(\tau-1)+Y_{0}}\!\left((\lambda_{i}^{r}-k)X_{n-1}^{r(i,h)}-\lambda_{i}^{c}X_{n-1}^{c(i,h)}+X_{n-1}^{r(i,h-1)}\right),

\displaystyle\overline{M}^{c(i,h)}_{n}

\displaystyle:=\nabla X^{c(i,h)}_{n}-\frac{1}{(n-1)(\tau-1)+Y_{0}}\!\left((\lambda_{i}^{r}-k)X_{n-1}^{c(i,h)}+\lambda_{i}^{c}X_{n-1}^{r(i,h)}+X_{n-1}^{c(i,h-1)}\right),

\displaystyle\overline{v}^{(i)}_{n}

\displaystyle:=\sum^{H_{i}}_{h=1}\sum^{n}_{j=1}\left(\beta^{r(i,h)}_{j,n}\overline{M}^{r(i,h)}_{j}+\beta^{c(i,h)}_{j,n}\overline{M}^{c(i,h)}_{j}\right):=\sum^{H_{i}}_{h=1}\left(\alpha_{i,h}^{r}X^{r(i,h)}_{n}+\alpha_{i,h}^{c}X^{c(i,h)}_{n}\right)+\varepsilon_{n}^{(i)},

\displaystyle v^{(i)}_{n}

\displaystyle:=\sum^{H_{i}}_{h=1}\sum^{n}_{j=1}\left(\beta^{r(i,h)}_{j,n}M^{r(i,h)}_{j}+\beta^{c(i,h)}_{j,n}M^{c(i,h)}_{j}\right).

The derivation of the coefficients
\beta^{r(i,h)}_{j,n}
and
\beta^{c(i,h)}_{j,n}
is done similarly to the diagonalizable case, only more involved, and therefore is deferred to the appendix.
We obtain the following.

Claim VII.1

The values of
\beta^{r(i,h)}_{j,n}
and
\beta^{c(i,h)}_{j,n}
are given below.

\displaystyle\beta^{r(i,h)}_{j-1,n}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{n}{j}\right)^{\!\frac{\lambda_{i}^{r}-k}{\tau-1}}\Bigg\{\cos\!\left(\frac{\lambda_{i}^{c}}{\tau-1}\log(n/j)\right)\left(\alpha^{r}_{i,h}+\sum_{l=1}^{H_{i}-h}\left(e^{r,l}_{i,j}\alpha^{r}_{i,h+l}+e^{c,l}_{i,j}\alpha^{c}_{i,h+l}\right)\right)

and

are defined recursively by

\displaystyle e^{1}_{i,j}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{t=j}^{n}\frac{1}{(t-1)(\tau-1)+m+\lambda_{i}-k},

\displaystyle e^{h}_{i,j}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{t=j}^{n-h+1}\frac{e^{h-1}_{\lambda,\,t+1}}{(t-1)(\tau-1)+m+\lambda_{i}-k}\quad\text{for }n-h+1\geqslant j,\quad\text{and }0\text{ otherwise},

\displaystyle:=e^{r,h}_{i,j}+\mathbf{i}\,e^{c,h}_{i,j}.

The associated error term satisfies

\varepsilon_{n}^{(i)}=O\!\left(n^{\frac{\lambda_{i}^{r}-k}{\tau-1}}\ln^{H_{i}-1}n\right).

Applying analogous arguments to those in Corollary
IV.11
, we establish the following asymptotic equivalence:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\alpha_{0}^{r}X^{r(0)}_{n}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{p-1}\sum^{H_{i}}_{h=1}\left(\alpha_{i,h}^{r}X^{r(i,h)}_{n}+\alpha_{i,h}^{c}X^{c(i,h)}_{n}\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle L_{1}}}{{\sim}}

\displaystyle\quad\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\left(\alpha_{0}^{r}\nabla X^{r(0)}_{j}+\sum_{i=1}^{p-1}\sum^{H_{i}}_{h=1}\left(\beta^{r(i,h)}_{j,n}\overline{M}^{r(i,h)}_{j}+\beta^{c(i,h)}_{j,n}\overline{M}^{c(i,h)}_{j}\right)\right),

and thus the projection sum may be approximated by a sum of martingale differences,

We observe that the error term is

This additional logarithmic factor poses no difficulty, since under our assumptions it remains

and therefore does not affect any of the subsequent proofs.

The remainder of the proof for the general case mirrors the structure of the diagonalizable case; the overall arguments proceed along the same lines.

VIII
Conclusion

In this paper, we studied the asymptotic behavior of mutation systems by establishing a Central Limit Theorem (CLT) for the frequencies of
k
-tuples.
Building upon prior work, our results fully characterize the stochastic fluctuations of sequence compositions around their deterministic expectations.
By exploiting the spectral properties of the
k
-substitution matrix and projecting the centered count vectors, we successfully approximated the system using a martingale difference sequence.
This framework allowed us to verify the classical martingale CLT conditions and to explicitly derive the limiting covariance matrix.

The establishment of this CLT provides a mathematical foundation for statistical inference in evolving sequence systems.
Moving beyond average-case behavior, our theorem enables the construction of confidence intervals and the formulation of hypothesis tests regarding sequence composition.
In the context of
in vivo
DNA-based data storage, quantifying these compositional fluctuations may be critical.
Understanding that these variations are asymptotically normal allows for more accurate modeling of biological error channels, which is essential for evaluating the long-term reliability of stored information subject to continuous, probabilistic mutations.
For example, since the limiting frequency depends entirely on the mutation process, one cannot expect perfect error protection over an indefinite number of mutations. Nevertheless, characterizing the convergence rate can guide the design of codes capable of protecting against mutation errors for a bounded duration (i.e., a bounded number of mutations), where the probability of decoding failure naturally increases over time.

Several promising avenues for future research remain. First, while our analysis covers a broad class of mutation rules under standard spectral gap assumptions, extending this framework to systems where the generating matrix exhibits negative entries, analogous to generalized urn models without strict replacement, would capture scenarios where certain
k
-tuples actively degrade or are selected against over time.
Second, generalizing these results to reducible substitution matrices is of particular interest, as it would significantly broaden the family of mutation rules known to exhibit this limiting behavior.
Finally, investigating the exact rate of convergence and integrating the covariance calculations into the design and analysis of specialized error-correcting codes represent natural and important next step toward achieving highly robust DNA data storage.

\displaystyle\beta_{n-1,n}^{r}

\displaystyle=\left(1+\frac{\lambda^{r}-k}{(n-1)(\tau-1)+Y_{0}}\right)\alpha^{r}+\frac{\lambda^{c}}{(n-1)(\tau-1)+Y_{0}}\alpha^{c},

\displaystyle\beta_{n-1,n}^{c}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\lambda^{c}}{(n-1)(\tau-1)+Y_{0}}\alpha^{r}+\left(1+\frac{\lambda^{r}-k}{(n-1)(\tau-1)+Y_{0}}\right)\alpha^{c}.

Using the same method for all
j\leqslant n
we get

\displaystyle\beta_{j-1,n}^{r}

\displaystyle=\left(1+\frac{\lambda^{r}-k}{(j-1)(\tau-1)+Y_{0}}\right)\beta^{r}_{j,n}+\frac{\lambda^{c}}{(n-1)(\tau-1)+Y_{0}}\beta^{c}_{j,n},

\displaystyle\beta_{j-1,n}^{c}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\lambda^{c}}{(n-1)(\tau-1)+Y_{0}}\beta^{r}_{j,n}+\left(1+\frac{\lambda^{r}-k}{(j-1)(\tau-1)+Y_{0}}\right)\beta^{c}_{j,n}.

To simplify the analysis, we write the recursion relation in vector form.
Let
\bbeta_{j,n}=\begin{bmatrix}\beta^{r}_{j,n}\\
\beta^{c}_{j,n}\end{bmatrix}
and define
\bbeta_{n,n}=\begin{bmatrix}\alpha^{r}\\
\alpha^{c}\end{bmatrix}
.
We get:

\displaystyle\bbeta_{j-1,n}

\displaystyle=\left(\mathbf{I}+\frac{1}{(j-1)(\tau-1)+Y_{0}}\mathbf{A}\right)\bbeta_{j,n}

\displaystyle=\prod_{t=j}^{n}\left(\mathbf{I}+\frac{1}{(t-1)(\tau-1)+Y_{0}}\mathbf{A}\right)\begin{bmatrix}\alpha^{r}\\
\alpha^{c}\end{bmatrix}.

(68)

where
\mathbf{I}
is the
2\times 2
identity matrix and

\mathbf{A}=\begin{bmatrix}\lambda^{r}-k&\lambda^{c}\\
-\lambda^{c}&\lambda^{r}-k\end{bmatrix}.

The eigenvalues of
\mathbf{A}
are

\displaystyle\rho_{1}

\displaystyle=\lambda^{r}-k+\mathbf{i}\lambda^{c}

\displaystyle\rho_{2}

\displaystyle=\lambda^{r}-k-\mathbf{i}\lambda^{c}

with eigenvectors

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}1\\
\mathbf{i}\end{bmatrix}\;\;\mathbf{v}_{2}=\begin{bmatrix}1\\
-\mathbf{i}\end{bmatrix}

Notice that if
\lambda^{c}\neq 0

\rho_{1}=\lambda-k
and
\rho_{2}=\overline{\lambda}-k
, where
\overline{\lambda}
is the complex conjugate of
\lambda
, and if
\lambda^{c}=0
then
\rho_{1}=\rho_{2}=\lambda^{r}-k
,
\mathbf{A}
is diagonal, and the eigenvectors are the standard basis vectors.

We distinguish between the two cases.
Let us assume first that
\lambda^{c}\neq 0
.
Then

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}1\\
\mathbf{i}\end{bmatrix},\;\;\mathbf{v}_{2}=\begin{bmatrix}1\\
-\mathbf{i}\end{bmatrix}.

If
\mathbf{V}
is the eigenvector matrix
\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{v}_{1}&\mathbf{v}_{2}\end{bmatrix}
then we can write

\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{V}\begin{bmatrix}\lambda-k&0\\
0&\overline{\lambda}-k\end{bmatrix}\mathbf{V}^{-1}.

Plugging it back to (
68

\displaystyle\bbeta_{j-1,n}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{V}\begin{bmatrix}\prod_{t=j}^{n}\left(1+\frac{\lambda-k}{(t-1)(\tau-1)+Y_{0}}\right)&0\\
0&\prod_{t=j}^{n}\left(1+\frac{\overline{\lambda}-k}{(t-1)(\tau-1)+Y_{0}}\right)\end{bmatrix}\mathbf{V}^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}\alpha^{r}\\
\alpha^{c}\end{bmatrix}.

Using Stirling’s approximation, we have

\displaystyle\prod_{t=j}^{n}\left(1+\frac{\lambda-k}{(t-1)(\tau-1)+Y_{0}}\right)

\displaystyle=\prod_{t=j}^{n}\left(\frac{(t-1)+\frac{m+\lambda-k}{\tau-1}}{(t-1)+\frac{m}{\tau-1}}\right)=\left(\frac{n}{j}\right)^{\frac{\lambda-k}{\tau-1}}+O\left(n^{\frac{\lambda-k}{\tau-1}-1}\right),

as

\displaystyle\left(\frac{n}{j}\right)^{\frac{\lambda-k}{\tau-1}}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{n}{j}\right)^{\frac{\lambda^{r}-k}{\tau-1}}\left(\frac{n}{j}\right)^{\mathbf{i}\frac{\lambda^{c}}{\tau-1}}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{n}{j}\right)^{\frac{\lambda^{r}-k}{\tau-1}}\left(\cos\left(\frac{\lambda^{c}}{\tau-1}\log(n/j)\right)+\mathbf{i}\sin\left(\frac{\lambda^{c}}{\tau-1}\log(n/j)\right)\right),

and similarly

Plugging this back into (

), and noticing that
\mathbf{V}^{-1}=\frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}1&-\mathbf{i}\\
1&\mathbf{i}\end{bmatrix}
we obtain the results:

\displaystyle\bbeta_{j-1,n}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\left(\frac{n}{j}\right)^{\frac{\lambda^{r}-k}{\tau-1}}\left(\alpha^{r}\cos\left(\frac{\lambda^{c}}{\tau-1}\log(n/j)\right)+\alpha^{c}\sin\left(\frac{\lambda^{c}}{\tau-1}\log(n/j)\right)\right)+O\left(n^{\frac{\lambda^{r}-k}{\tau-1}-1}\right)\\
\left(\frac{n}{j}\right)^{\frac{\lambda^{r}-k}{\tau-1}}\left(\alpha^{c}\cos\left(\frac{\lambda^{c}}{\tau-1}\log(n/j)\right)-\alpha^{r}\sin\left(\frac{\lambda^{c}}{\tau-1}\log(n/j)\right)\right)+O\left(n^{\frac{\lambda^{r}-k}{\tau-1}-1}\right)\end{bmatrix}.

Similar calculations for the case of
\lambda^{c}=0
yields

\displaystyle\bbeta_{j-1,n}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\alpha^{r}\left(\frac{n}{j}\right)^{\frac{\lambda^{r}-k}{\tau-1}}+O\left(n^{\frac{\lambda^{r}-k}{\tau-1}-1}\right)\\
0\end{bmatrix},

because as we stated in Remark
IV.7
, in the case that
\lambda^{c}=0
, we set
\alpha^{c}
to 0, meaning
\bbeta^{c}_{j-1,n}=0
for all
j
.

For every
\lambda^{c}
we get the upper bound:

\displaystyle|\bbeta_{j-1,n}|

\displaystyle\leqslant\begin{bmatrix}\left(\alpha^{r}+\alpha^{c}\right)\left(\frac{n}{j}\right)^{\frac{\lambda^{r}-k}{\tau-1}}+O\left(n^{\frac{\lambda^{r}-k}{\tau-1}-1}\right)\\
\left(\alpha^{r}+\alpha^{c}\right)\left(\frac{n}{j}\right)^{\frac{\lambda^{r}-k}{\tau-1}}+O\left(n^{\frac{\lambda^{r}-k}{\tau-1}-1}\right)\end{bmatrix},

which is crucial for most of the analysis in the work.
Plugging the results into (
66
) we get for
\varepsilon_{n}

\varepsilon_{n}=-X^{r}_{0}\left(\left(1+\frac{\lambda^{r}-k}{Y_{0}}\right)\beta^{r}_{1,n}+\frac{\lambda^{c}}{Y_{0}}\beta^{c}_{1,n}\right)-X^{c}_{0}\left(\left(1+\frac{\lambda^{r}-k}{Y_{0}}\right)\beta^{c}_{1,n}-\frac{\lambda^{c}}{Y_{0}}\beta^{r}_{1,n}\right)=O\left(n^{\frac{\lambda^{r}-k}{\tau-1}}\right),

which is a small error term we will be able to neglect throughout the analysis.

Proof of Lemma
V.14

The proof of Lemma
V.14
is straightforward.

Proof:

We prove only (
48
), the proof of (
49
) and (
50
) are analogous.
First, we use the formula for the product of cosines to obtain

\displaystyle\cos\left(a\log(t)\right)\cos\left(b\log(t)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\cos\left((a+b)\log(t)\right)+\cos\left((a-b)\log(t)\right)\right).

Thus, we are to calculate

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}t^{-r}\left(\cos\left((a+b)\log(t)\right)+\cos\left((a-b)\log(t)\right)\right)dt.

(70)

Let us focus on the first part. By taking
u=\log(t)
, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}t^{-r}\cos\left((a+b)\log(t)\right)dt

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{0}e^{-ru}\cos\left((a+b)u\right)e^{u}du

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{0}e^{(1-r)u}\cos\left((a+b)u\right)du.

Notice that

\int e^{(1-r)u}\cos\left((a\pm b)u\right)du=\frac{e^{(1-r)u}}{(1-r)^{2}+(a\pm b)^{2}}\left((1-r)\cos\left((a\pm b)u\right)+(a\pm b)\sin\left((a\pm b)u\right)\right).

Thus, since
r<1
, we get

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{0}e^{(1-r)u}\cos\left((a+b)u\right)du=\frac{1-r}{(1-r)^{2}+(a+b)^{2}},

and

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{0}e^{(1-r)u}\cos\left((a-b)u\right)du=\frac{1-r}{(1-r)^{2}+(a-b)^{2}}.

Plugging everything together into (
70
), we obtain (
48
).
∎

(81)

Finally, plugging (
81
) back into (
78
), we get a close term for
\beta^{r(i,h)}_{j,n}
and
\beta^{c(i,h)}_{j,n}
:

\displaystyle\beta^{r(i,h)}_{j-1,n}=\left(\frac{n}{j}\right)^{\frac{\lambda^{r}-k}{\tau-1}}\Bigg\{

\displaystyle\cos\left(\frac{\lambda^{c}}{\tau-1}\log\left(\frac{n}{j}\right)\right)\left(\alpha^{r}_{i,h}+\sum_{l=1}^{H_{i}-h}\left(e^{r,l}_{\lambda,j}\alpha^{r}_{i,h+l}+e^{c,l}_{\lambda,j}\alpha^{c}_{i,h+l}\right)\right)+

\displaystyle\sin\left(\frac{\lambda^{c}}{\tau-1}\log\left(\frac{n}{j}\right)\right)\left(\alpha^{c}_{i,h}+\sum_{l=1}^{H_{i}-h}\left(e^{r,l}_{\lambda,j}\alpha^{c}_{i,h+l}-e^{c,l}_{\lambda,j}\alpha^{r}_{i,h+l}\right)\right)\Bigg\}+O\left(n^{\frac{\lambda^{r}-k}{\tau-1}-1}\log^{H_{i}-h}n\right)

(82)

\displaystyle=O\left(n^{\frac{\lambda^{r}-k}{\tau-1}}\log^{H_{i}-h}n\right)

,

\displaystyle\beta^{c(i,h)}_{j-1,n}=\left(\frac{n}{j}\right)^{\frac{\lambda^{r}-k}{\tau-1}}\Bigg\{

\displaystyle\cos\left(\frac{\lambda^{c}}{\tau-1}\log\left(\frac{n}{j}\right)\right)\left(\alpha^{c}_{i,h}+\sum_{l=1}^{H_{i}-h}\left(e^{r,l}_{\lambda,j}\alpha^{c}_{i,h+l}-e^{c,l}_{\lambda,j}\alpha^{r}_{i,h+l}\right)\right)-

\displaystyle\sin\left(\frac{\lambda^{c}}{\tau-1}\log\left(\frac{n}{j}\right)\right)\left(\alpha^{r}_{i,h}+\sum_{l=1}^{H_{i}-h}\left(e^{r,l}_{\lambda,j}\alpha^{r}_{i,h+l}+e^{c,l}_{\lambda,j}\alpha^{c}_{i,h+l}\right)\right)\Bigg\}+O\left(n^{\frac{\lambda^{r}-k}{\tau-1}-1}\log^{H_{i}-h}n\right)

(83)

\displaystyle=O\left(n^{\frac{\lambda^{r}-k}{\tau-1}}\log^{H_{i}-h}n\right)

.

We find the small error term to be:

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{(i,H_{i})}_{n}=

\displaystyle\sum_{h=1}^{H_{i}}-X_{0}^{r(i,h)}\left(\left(1+\frac{\lambda^{r}-k}{Y_{0}}\right)\beta^{r(i,h)}_{1,n}+\frac{\lambda^{c}}{Y_{0}}\beta^{c(i,h)}_{1,n}\right)-X_{0}^{c(i,h)}\left(-\frac{\lambda^{c}}{Y_{0}}\beta^{r(i,h)}_{1,n}+\left(1+\frac{\lambda^{r}-k}{Y_{0}}\right)\beta^{c(i,h)}_{1,n}\right)+

\displaystyle\sum_{h=1}^{H_{i}-1}-X_{0}^{r(i,h)}\frac{1}{Y_{0}}\beta^{r(i,h+1)}_{1,n}-X_{0}^{c(i,h)}\frac{1}{Y_{0}}\beta^{c(i,h+1)}_{1,n}=O\left(n^{\frac{\lambda^{r}-k}{\tau-1}}\ln^{H_{i}-1}n\right).

As we can see, we got results similar to the diagonalizable case, but with logarithmic residuals
e^{h}_{\lambda,j}
.
Those residuals make the analysis slightly more involved, but the core ideas remain the same.
