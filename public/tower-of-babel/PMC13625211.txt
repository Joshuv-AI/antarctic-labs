Who leaves a suicide note? A systematic review of differences between note writers and non‐note writers

Giulia Gotti, Federica Turolla, Alessandra Bordessa, Claudia Bruni, Fabio Madeddu, Lorenzo Montali, Guendalina Gentile, Riccardo Zoja, Riccardo Primavera, Stefano Tambuzzi, Cristina Cattaneo, Raffaella Calati

Abstract

Suicide notes represent one of the few first‐person sources of information on the suicidal process. However, only a minority of individuals who die by suicide leave a note, raising the question of whether note writers (NW) are representative of the broader population of suicide decedents. This systematic review aimed to synthesize the available evidence on differences between NW and non‐note writers (NNW) across sociodemographic, clinical, psychosocial, and suicide‐related variables. A systematic search was conducted in PubMed and ProQuest databases up to June 22, 2026, following PRISMA guidelines. Studies were included if they compared NW and NNW on at least one variable, or investigated predictors of note‐leaving. Thirty‐three studies met the inclusion criteria. Evidence was heterogeneous and suggested that NW cannot be clearly distinguished from NNW based on sociodemographic and clinical variables. Concerning suicide‐related variables, the method of suicide was the most consistently different between NW and NNW; in particular, the most frequent methods of suicide in NW, compared to NNW, were non‐violent methods like poisoning/overdose and gas inhalation. Rather than solely reflecting a distinct psychological profile, suicide note‐leaving may also be influenced by the circumstances surrounding the suicidal act. One possible, although untested, explanation is that leaving a note requires a degree of cognitive and emotional organization that may be less compatible with highly disorganized or impulsive suicidal states.

INTRODUCTION

Suicide is a major public health concern worldwide, accounting for more than 720,000 deaths each year. Despite extensive research efforts, understanding the factors associated with suicide remains challenging, particularly because the subjective experiences that precede suicide death cannot be directly investigated. To address this limitation, researchers have relied on a range of postmortem sources, including psychological autopsies, medical and psychiatric records, and interviews with relatives and acquaintances.

Among these sources, suicide notes occupy a unique position. Unlike other postmortem data, which are reconstructed retrospectively by third parties, suicide notes represent first‐person communications written by individuals shortly before their death. As such, they may provide valuable insights into the thoughts, emotions, motivations, and interpersonal concerns that accompanied the suicidal process. Previous research suggests that suicide notes may reveal intentions, psychological pain, hopelessness, perceived burdensomeness, interpersonal difficulties, and meaning‐making processes that are often inaccessible through other postmortem methods., Moreover, findings from suicide note analyses have informed several influential theories of suicide, including conceptualizations emphasizing psychache and interpersonal processes., Although suicide notes are often examined within the broader framework of psychological autopsy investigations, they constitute one of the few sources that directly reflect the deceased individual's own perspective.

A key methodological question therefore concerns whether individuals who leave a suicide note are representative of all suicide decedents. If note writers (NW) differ systematically from non‐note writers (NNW), conclusions drawn from suicide note analyses may reflect the characteristics of a selected subgroup rather than those of suicide decedents as a whole. Available evidence indicates substantial variability across studies in the prevalence of suicide NW. In large‐scale studies, prevalence estimates ranged from 18.25% to 31% in the United States (with sample sizes ranging from 2936 to 32,151 suicides), while a prevalence of 30.1% was reported in Japan (based on a sample of 5161 suicides). Because only a minority of individuals who die by suicide leave a note, findings derived from suicide note analyses may not necessarily generalize to the broader population of suicide decedents. Previous studies comparing NW and NNW have yielded inconsistent findings regarding sociodemographic characteristics, psychiatric history, stressful life events, and suicide‐related factors. Clarifying these differences is essential both for interpreting the existing suicide note literature and for determining the extent to which suicide notes can be used to understand suicide risk more generally.

This systematic review aimed to identify and synthesize the evidence regarding differences between NW and NNW. Specifically, we examined (a) sociodemographic characteristics (e.g., sex, age, and marital status), (b) clinical and psychosocial factors (e.g., mental and physical illnesses, alcohol, and substance use), and (c) suicide‐related variables (e.g., suicide method and previous suicide attempt(s) (SA)) associated with the presence of a suicide note.

METHODS

This systematic review was performed according to the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analysis) statement. The use of ChatGPT in the review process was documented in accordance with the principles of the PRISMA‐trAIce reporting framework.

A literature search was conducted using PubMed and ProQuest databases (up to June 22, 2026), searching across all ProQuest subject collections available through the institutional subscription (MEDLINE, PTSDpubs, Periodicals Index Online, International Bibliography of the Social Sciences (IBSS), ProQuest Dissertations & Theses Global, Linguistics and Language Behavior Abstracts (LLBA), Library & Information Science Abstracts (LISA), Natural Science Collection, Education Research Index, and Social Science Premium Collection), and Rayyan software was employed to identify and exclude duplicates across the two databases. The search strings are reported in Supporting Information : Appendix 1. Articles were independently screened for eligibility by three authors (GG, AB, and RC). In cases of disagreement, the three reviewers discussed the issue until an agreement was reached.

Studies were eligible if they compared NW and NNW with respect to sociodemographic, clinical, psychosocial, or suicide‐related characteristics, or examined factors associated with the likelihood of leaving a suicide note. Studies were included if written in English, Italian, French, German, and Spanish.

Studies were excluded if they did not include NW and NNW, compared real with simulated suicide notes, or compared different types of notes (e.g., notes from suicides vs. notes from suicide attempters). Reviews, meta‐analyses, editorials, protocols, and dissertations were excluded. In case of studies performed on overlapping samples, the study with the wider sample was included (e.g., Salib et al. and Salib and Maximous were performed on overlapping samples, thus only was included).

From each included article, GG and AB independently extracted the main characteristics: authors and year of publication, setting, type of population, sample size, and percentage of NW and NNW, type of statistical analysis, investigated variables, and main results. Quality of reporting assessment

The quality of reporting was assessed using the Quality Assessment Tool for Observational Cohort and Cross‐Sectional Studies developed by the National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute (NHLBI). Items that were not applicable to the study design (e.g., exposure assessed prior to outcome, repeated exposure assessment, or loss to follow‐up) were coded as “Not Applicable” (NA) and excluded from the overall score calculation. Overall study quality was classified as good, fair, or poor based on the proportion of positive ratings among applicable items (excluding NA responses): Good = ≥75% “Yes” responses; Fair = 50%–74% “Yes” responses; Poor = <50% “Yes” responses.

An independent quality assessment was first performed by GG using the NHLBI Quality Assessment Tool. Subsequently, an AI‐assisted assessment was conducted using ChatGPT (OpenAI GPT‐5.5). Full‐text articles, together with the NHLBI checklist and the explanation of each item, were provided to ChatGPT, which generated a preliminary quality assessment for each study. The independent assessment performed by GG and the AI‐generated assessments were then compared. Any discrepancies were reviewed and resolved through discussion among the authors, resulting in the final quality ratings. ChatGPT was used solely as a decision‐support tool, and the final quality ratings were determined by the authors. At every stage, the AI‐generated ratings were treated as provisional suggestions rather than final judgments: each rating was reviewed by GG against the source article, any disagreement with the AI output was discussed among the authors, and the human‐agreed rating, not the AI output, was recorded as the final score. No quality rating was accepted without independent human verification.

Because most studies were cross‐sectional, retrospective, registry‐based, or based on suicide note content analyses, several items of the NHLBI tool were frequently not applicable. General decision rules: Q1 (Research question): scored “Yes” when the study objective or research question was explicitly stated; Q2 (Study population): scored “Yes” when the population and source of cases were clearly described; Q3 (Participation rate ≥50%): scored “Yes” only when the study included a clearly defined population‐based sample or when participation/completeness could reasonably be assumed. If information was unavailable, “Cannot Determine” (CD) was assigned; Q4 (Selection criteria): scored “Yes” when inclusion/exclusion criteria or case definitions were described; Q5 (Sample size justification/power): scored “Yes” only when an explicit power analysis, sample size justification, or variance/effect estimate was reported. Large sample size alone was not sufficient; Q6 (Exposure measured before outcome): generally coded “NA” because most studies were cross‐sectional or retrospective; Q7 (Sufficient timeframe): generally coded “NA” for cross‐sectional studies without temporal follow‐up; Q8 (Different levels of exposure): scored “Yes” when exposures were analyzed across categories, groups, or gradients; Q9 (Exposure measures valid and reliable): scored “Yes” when exposure variables were clearly defined and obtained through standardized records, validated instruments, official databases, or systematic coding procedures; Q10 (Exposure assessed more than once): generally coded “NA” because repeated assessments were uncommon; Q11 (Outcome measures valid and reliable): scored “Yes” when outcomes were clearly operationalized and consistently measured across participants; Q12 (Blinding): usually coded “CD” because blinding procedures were rarely reported; Q13 (Loss to follow‐up): generally coded “NA” because follow‐up was not part of most study designs; Q14 (Confounding): scored “Yes” only when multivariable analyses or statistical adjustment for confounders were performed. Descriptive analyses without adjustment were coded “No.”

The preliminary agreement between GG and the AI‐assisted assessment was high, with concordant ratings for 435 of 462 checklist items (94.2%). Only 27 ratings (5.8%) required discussion. Nearly half of all discrepancies (48.1%) concerned Item 5 (sample size justification/power calculation), whereas the remaining disagreements were mainly related to participation rate (Item 3), selection criteria (Item 4), validity of exposure measures (Item 9), and adjustment for confounding (Item 14). All discrepancies were subsequently resolved by consensus among the authors.

RESULTS

The search initially identified 870 references; after eliminating duplicates with Rayyan software, 439 papers were screened for eligibility. Thirty‐three studies met the inclusion criteria and were included. See Supporting Information for the study flowchart.

For the purpose of synthesis, variables were grouped into three broad domains: (a) sociodemographic variables, (b) clinical and psychosocial variables, and (c) suicide‐related variables.

Sociodemographic variables included sex, age, marital status, children, education, occupation, socioeconomic status, living condition (living alone or with someone else, and living in an urban or rural zone), ethnicity, country of origin (locally born or immigrant), sexual orientation and gender identity, religious beliefs, and military career variables (veteran status and military service characteristics).

Clinical and psychosocial variables included mental and physical health conditions, contact with healthcare services, substance use, stressful life events (e.g., relationship, financial, occupational, legal, or school‐related problems), interpersonal violence, recent crises, and other psychological characteristics.

Suicide‐related variables included method, past suicidal ideation (SI), past SA, place, time, having disclosed suicide intent to others, evidence of planning, family history of suicide or SA, precipitating circumstances, psychological state and behavior before the suicide or SA, severity of SA, and alcohol or drug use at the time of suicide.

Some studies had suicide as the main outcome, some had SA, and some had both. Results are reported separately for suicides and SA.

Suicide

Twenty‐nine of the 33 included studies (87.9%) investigated suicide deaths. One study (Weeke & Oberwittler) included both suicide deaths and SA but did not report the findings separately according to the outcome. Consequently, whenever this study is cited in the present review, the reported findings should be interpreted as referring to a mixed sample of suicide deaths and SA.

Clinical and psychosocial variables

The most frequently investigated clinical and psychosocial variables were mental health problems (k = 17, 51.5%), physical health problems (k = 14, 42.4%), contact with mental health services (k = 7, 21.2%), and alcohol or substance abuse (k = 5, 15.2%). Among the studies investigating mental health problems, six (35.3%) out of 17 reported significant differences between NW and NNW, although findings were inconsistent. Four studies found that NW were less likely to have psychological or psychiatric disorders,, , , whereas 2 studies reported the opposite association., In summary, the available evidence does not support a consistent relationship between the presence of mental health problems and the likelihood of leaving a suicide note.

Four studies (12.1%) out of 14 investigating physical health problems reported significant differences between NW and NNW, but the findings were inconsistent. Two studies found that NW were more likely to have physical illnesses,, whereas 2 studies reported that they were less likely to have physical illnesses., Therefore, no clear association emerged between physical health problems and suicide note writing.

Two studies (28.6%) out of 7 found that NW were less likely than NNW to have had previous contact with mental health services., However, the limited number of investigations precludes firm conclusions.

Significant differences in alcohol or substance misuse were reported in 4 studies (80%) out of 5. Three studies found that NW were less likely to misuse alcohol or drugs,, , whereas one study reported the opposite association. The available evidence suggests that alcohol or substance misuse may be less common among NW, although this finding requires confirmation in future studies.

Suicide‐related variables

The most frequently investigated suicide‐related variables were the method of suicide (k = 24, 72.7%), past SA (k = 16, 48.5%), precipitating circumstances (k = 13, 39.4%), place of suicide (k = 7, 21.2%), and time of suicide (k = 5, 15.2%). Variables examined in fewer than 5 studies are summarized in Table .

The method of suicide was the variable most consistently different between NW and NNW. Among the 24 studies that investigated this characteristic, 16 (66.7%) reported that one or more suicide methods were significantly more frequent among NW. Specifically, NW were more likely to die by poisoning or overdose in 8 studies,, , , , , , , by gas inhalation in 6 studies,, , , , , by hanging in 5 studies,, , , , and by firearms in 4 studies., , , Two additional studies reported a higher prevalence of non‐violent methods among NW,, whereas other methods (e.g., drowning, asphyxiation, electrocution, plastic bag suffocation, and sharp instruments) were each identified in only one study. Collectively, the available evidence suggests that suicide note writing is more frequently associated with suicide methods requiring greater preparation or allowing a longer interval between the suicidal decision and death.

Conversely, 9 studies (37.5% of the 24 studies investigating suicide method) reported that 1 or more methods were significantly less frequent among NW. Specifically, NW were less likely to use firearms in 2 studies,, drowning in 2 studies,, and jumping from a height in 2 studies., Other methods, including vehicle or train‐related suicide, wrist cutting, poisoning, and hanging, were each reported by only one study. In summary, although findings for individual methods were not entirely consistent, the evidence suggests that suicide note writing is more commonly associated with methods involving greater planning and less frequently associated with highly immediate or impulsive methods.

Five of the 16 studies (31.3%) investigating previous SA reported significant differences between NW and NNW. Three studies found that NW were more likely to have a history of previous SA,, , whereas 2 studies reported the opposite association., In summary, the available evidence does not support a consistent relationship between previous SA and the likelihood of leaving a suicide note.

Six studies (46.1%) out of 13 investigating precipitating circumstances reported that 1 or more precipitating circumstances were significantly more frequent among NW than NNW. The most frequently reported circumstances were relationship problems (k = 4, , , ) and financial or occupational problems (k = 4, , , ). Grieving the death of a significant other was reported in 2 studies,, whereas having perpetrated interpersonal violence was identified in 1 study. Overall, the available evidence suggests that suicide note writing may be more strongly associated with interpersonal and psychosocial stressors than with demographic or clinical characteristics.

Three of the 13 studies (23.1%) investigating precipitating circumstances reported that one or more precipitating factors were significantly less frequent among NW than NNW. Specifically, NW were less likely to have psychiatric disturbance identified as a precipitating circumstance in 2 studies,, and were less likely to have physical health problems reported as a precipitating circumstance in 2 studies.,

Finally, no significant differences between NW and NNW were reported with respect to the place and time of suicide.

Suicide attempts

Four studies out of 33 (12.1%) investigated individuals who had attempted suicide., , , One of these studies included both SA and suicide deaths but did not report the findings separately according to the outcome. Therefore, results derived from this study should be interpreted as referring to a mixed sample. Variables investigated in only 1 study are reported in Table .

Among sociodemographic variables, age and marital status were each investigated in 3 studies, whereas sex and parental status were investigated in 2 studies. Results were scarce and inconsistent. Only one study reported that NW were more likely to be older and to have children compared to NNW.

Regarding clinical and psychosocial variables, mental health problems were investigated in 3 studies and hopelessness in 2 studies. One study found that NW reported higher levels of hopelessness, whereas another reported higher vocabulary scores and lower alcohol or substance misuse among NW. No consistent pattern emerged for the remaining clinical variables.

Among suicide‐related variables, previous SA were investigated in all 4 studies, whereas SI, disclosure of suicidal intent, and SA severity were examined in 2 or 3 studies. Two studies consistently found that NW had more severe SA., Individual studies also reported higher suicidal intent, greater evidence of planning, more frequent disclosure of suicidal intent, and a higher likelihood of having perpetrated interpersonal violence among NW. In summary, the limited number of studies and their methodological heterogeneity preclude firm conclusions regarding the characteristics of suicide attempters who leave suicide notes.

Quality of reporting

Of the 33 included studies, 15 (45.5%) were rated as good quality, 17 (51.5%) as fair quality, and 1 (3.0%) as poor quality (Table ). Overall, the methodological quality of the included studies was moderate to high. Most studies clearly stated their research objectives, adequately described the study population, and employed well‐defined and consistently measured study variables. The most common methodological limitations concerned the absence of a sample size justification or power calculation, incomplete reporting of participation rates, limited adjustment for potential confounding variables, and the lack of information on assessor blinding. These shortcomings were particularly common among older studies and descriptive investigations of suicide notes, reflecting the methodological standards prevailing at the time they were conducted.

DISCUSSION

This systematic review aimed to investigate the differences between NW and NNW across sociodemographic, clinical, psychosocial, and suicide‐related variables. Results suggest that leaving a suicide note is not associated with a distinctive sociodemographic or clinical profile. Rather, note‐writing may reflect a complex interaction between individual characteristics and the circumstances surrounding the suicidal act.

Across all the included studies, NW consistently represented a minority of individuals who died by suicide or engaged in a SA; thus, suicide notes seem to be the exception rather than the rule. This raises important questions about the representativeness of this subgroup and the extent to which findings based on note writers can be generalized to the broader population of individuals who die by suicide or attempt suicide. Although some variables showed recurring associations with note‐writing, the overall evidence was heterogeneous and often inconsistent across studies. Rather than solely reflecting a distinct psychological profile, leaving a suicide note may depend on the circumstances surrounding the suicidal act. For example, methods involving greater planning or a longer interval between the suicidal decision and death may provide both the opportunity and the cognitive resources necessary to compose a note. In this view, leaving a suicide note should be conceptualized as a behavior emerging from the interaction between personal characteristics, contextual factors, and the circumstances immediately preceding death. Importantly, our findings suggest that note‐leaving should not be viewed solely as a reflection of individual characteristics. Rather, it appears to emerge from the interplay between personal characteristics, situational opportunity, and the dynamics of the suicidal act.

As a speculative interpretation, we hypothesize that composing a suicide note may require a degree of cognitive organization, planning, and emotional regulation immediately before death. This interpretation is consistent with, though not directly tested by, linguistic analyses showing complex semantic organization and emotional expression. Thus we hypothesize that note‐leaving behavior may require a degree of cognitive and emotional organization that is inconsistent with disorganized or impulsive suicidal states. Acute psychosis, severe intoxication, or highly impulsive suicidal acts may reduce the opportunity or ability to produce a written communication. However, none of the included studies directly measured planning, cognitive organization, or emotional regulation, and this explanation should therefore be regarded as a hypothesis for future research rather than an established finding.

Additionally, the lower level of psychopathology and the lack of contact with mental health services found in NW might reflect low detection by healthcare services, or differences in help‐seeking behavior, rather than an actual lower level of symptomatology. In registry‐ and autopsy‐based studies, the absence of a recorded diagnosis does not necessarily indicate the absence of clinically significant symptoms, as psychiatric disorders may remain undetected or subthreshold. Likewise, lack of contact with mental health services may result from barriers to care or differences in help‐seeking behavior rather than lower clinical need.

These findings have important clinical implications. A common misconception, also reported among healthcare professionals, is that those who talk about killing themselves rarely die by suicide. On the contrary, a large proportion of people who die by suicide have given at least some kind of verbal clue or non‐verbal warning of their intentions. Although the presence of a suicide note, or the expressed plan to write one, has been hypothesized to be associated with planning or suicidal intentionality, with some studies linking higher intentionality to higher lethality, this association was not directly examined in the present review, and the absence of a note should not be taken as a reassurance, given that the majority of suicide decedents do not leave a note. Clinicians should be equally attentive to suicidal risk regardless of whether a note is present and should not allow the absence of a note to lower their level of concern.

These findings also have important methodological implications. Suicide notes remain one of the few first‐person sources available for studying the suicidal process, but they should not automatically be considered representative of all suicide decedents. Instead, they may preferentially reflect the experiences of individuals whose suicidal acts involved sufficient planning, opportunity, or intention to communicate with others.

Finally, the direction of findings among SA was overall consistent with that among suicides. If note‐writing is similarly associated with both SA and suicide death, future studies may be able to investigate this behavior in survivors of SA, allowing prospective assessment of psychological correlates that cannot be examined among suicide decedents. Some differences did emerge; however, these must be interpreted with caution, considering the small number of studies focusing on SA, the heterogeneity of their samples, the fact that some studies did not separate results by suicide‐related outcome, and the poor quality of reporting of Beck et al.

Strengths and limitations

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first systematic review to systematically examine and synthesize evidence on differences between NW and NNW across sociodemographic, clinical, psychosocial, and suicide‐related variables. The fact that the screening process was done by independent assessors is a strength of the present work, as well as the variety of languages in the inclusion criteria.

However, some limitations should be considered. First, the included studies spanned almost five decades, during which the social meaning, accessibility, and modalities of suicide communication may have substantially changed. Furthermore, this review did not distinguish between single and multiple note writers: as NW were more likely to have a history of previous SA, it is likely that some of them already left a note in the past. Single‐ and multiple‐note leavers might have some different features that this review was not able to grasp. Another limitation concerns the definition of a suicide note itself. Variability in how suicide notes were defined across decades, including differences in whether digital communications (e.g., text messages, emails, social media posts) were counted as notes, likely contributed to heterogeneity in prevalence estimates and sample composition. The review focused exclusively on traditional suicide notes and did not consider digital farewell communications, which have become increasingly common in recent years. Furthermore, PsycINFO, Scopus, and Web of Science were not searched; however, although additional databases were not searched, ProQuest indexes a broad range of multidisciplinary and psychological literature, thus maximizing retrieval. Additionally, the search strategy relied primarily on title‐field terms, which, while increasing precision, may have missed studies in which relevant comparisons between NW and NNW were not reflected in the title. This risk was partly mitigated by the broad scope of the search strings (see Appendix ) and by screening at the abstract and full‐text level, but it cannot be fully excluded. Finally, no backward or forward citation searching (e.g., of reference lists or citing articles) was performed, which represents an additional limitation and may have resulted in the omission of eligible studies not captured by the database search.

Other limitations that should be discussed are not problems of this review itself, but they mainly concern inherent weaknesses of the included studies. One limitation concerns the fact that included studies are heterogeneous in terms of sample size, participant profiles (such as age and the eventual presence of a clinical condition), research time and place settings, and investigated variables, further hindering the possibility of generalization of results. Another limitation concerns the design of the included studies, as most of them were cross‐sectional or retrospective studies, based on the gathering of information from forensic medical or police archives, and from interviews with family members. Thus, the use of standardized and validated measures was not always implemented, leading to risks like recall bias and eventual misreporting. Furthermore, the majority of included studies relied on bivariate or descriptive analyses without adjustment for confounding variables, which limits the interpretability of observed associations.

Future directions

While suicide notes provide valuable insights into the thoughts, emotions, motivations, and interpersonal concerns that accompanied the suicidal process, it is also important to take into consideration not only who writes them but also the loved ones who receive them. It might seem intuitive to assume that a suicide note might have an effect on the mental health of the person who receives it; for example, comforting and supportive messages might reduce some sense of guilt. Unexpectedly, existing evidence shows no difference between people who received a note and people who did not in terms of mental health outcomes. Instead, the most salient effect appears among people who expected some kind of communication by their loved one but did not receive it: unmet expectations are associated with worse mental health. This finding underscores that the psychological impact of a suicide note is not determined solely by its content, but also by the pre‐existing relational dynamic and the expectations of those who survive, a topic that is only marginally covered by existing literature.

CONCLUSION

This systematic review provides a comprehensive synthesis of the available evidence on differences between suicide NW and NNW across sociodemographic, clinical, psychosocial, and suicide‐related variables. The findings do not support a simple answer to the question of whether NW are representative of all suicide decedents. On one hand, the majority of investigated variables did not show consistent significant differences between the two groups, suggesting that NW and NNW are not radically distinct populations. On the other hand, where differences did emerge, they pointed in a meaningful direction: NW tended to use less violent methods, showed less severe psychopathology, were less likely to be known to mental health services, and appeared to be engaged in a relatively more deliberate suicidal process. Taken together, these patterns may suggest, but do not establish, that suicide note‐leaving is more likely to occur in the context of suicidal acts characterized by greater planning, cognitive organization, and communicative intent.

These findings have both methodological and clinical implications. From a clinical point of view, the presence of a suicide note, or an expressed intention to write one, may be worth considering as a possible indicator of deliberate suicidal planning that warrants careful assessment, though this should not be treated as an established or validated marker. Conversely, the absence of a note should never be interpreted as reassurance, given that the majority of individuals who die by suicide do not leave one.

Suicide notes should therefore be regarded as a valuable, but potentially selective, window into the suicidal process rather than as a representative account of all suicide deaths.
