Untapped enzymatic potential: dehalogenase- and peroxidase-driven PVC degradation by gene carriers in Tibetan plateau pikas
Authors: Chunxiu Zhou, Baotong Fu, Xiaoxiao Hou, Wei-Min Wu, Aman Khan, Chunjie Li, Huawen Han, Xiangkai Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWide distribution of polyvinyl chloride microplastics (PVC-MPs) has been documented in remote regions such as the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau (QTP). Microbial degradation of plastics is frequently coupled with lignocellulose-degrading enzymatic machinery. As a ubiquitous biological sampler on the QTP, the herbivore plateau pika (Ochotona curzoniae), which harbors diverse lignocellulose-degrading enzymes, represents a promising reservoir for novel PVC-degrading enzymes.ResultsIn this study, a PVC-MPs feeding trial of plateau pikas revealed gut microbiota recruitment of plastic degraders. Subsequent enrichment experiment yielded a PVC-degrading consortium that depolymerized PVC into long-chain alkanes, with Rhodococcus and Leifsonia identified as PVC-response specialist and generalist, respectively. Multi-omics analysis supported a putative degradation pathway initiated by haloalkane dehalogenase (HLD) and involving oxidases. Furthermore, novel RhHLD (from Rhodococcus MAG) released 11.5 mg L−1 chloride ions from PVC films, whereas dye-decolorizing peroxidase LeDyP from Leifsonia MAG generated PVC-degrading intermediates. Analysis of 39 metagenomic datasets further confirmed that haloalkane dehalogenase and dye-decolorizing peroxidase are prevalent in the gut of wild pikas.ConclusionsThis study elucidates the PVC-degrading potential of herbivore gut microbiota and expands the catalytic toolkit for plastic bioremediation, underscoring the bioprospecting potential in extreme ecosystems Video AbstractGraphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40168-026-02478-7.Keywords: PVC microplastics, Tibetan plateau pikas, Gut microbiota, Dehalogenase, Peroxidase, Degradation mechanism

Background

Global plastic waste is projected to exceed 12 billion metric tons by 2050, including major polymers such as polyethylene (PE), polystyrene (PS), polyvinyl chloride (PVC), and polyethylene terephthalate (PET) [1]. These plastics release microplastics (MPs, ≤5 mm) through photodegradation or advanced oxidation processes [2]. PVC accounts for 12.8% of global plastic production due to its widespread use in medical and construction applications [3]. Despite its thermodynamic favorability (ΔG0 ≈ −1259 kJ/mol), PVC biodegradation is kinetically hindered by high bond dissociation energies (327–410 kJ/mol), thus requiring highly active enzymes to overcome substantial barriers [4]. While the microbial breakdown of PET, PE, and PS is well-studied [5, 6], PVC biodegradation remains underexplored [7]. Given the low efficiency of plastic-degrading bacteria, research efforts have shifted toward discovering high-efficient plastic-degrading enzymes [8, 9], such as PETase Kubu-PM12 [10]. However, a recent survey of 95 enzymes from PMBD database showed activity against 13 plastics, excluding PVC [11]. Known PVC-degrading enzymes only include catalase-peroxidase [12] and phosphoserine phosphatase SerB [13]. This limited diversity constrains PVC biodegradation [8, 9], underscoring the urgent need to expand the repertoire of such biocatalysts.

Extreme environments have become a frontier for discovering novel enzymes with unprecedented catalytic properties, including hot spring, compost, and deep sea [14, 15]. For instance, an α-amylase from the hot spring exhibits potent hydrolytic activity against xylan and cellulose at 60 °C [16]. Halophilic dsPETase from deep sea achieved an 83% depolymerization rate toward PET compared to the canonical IsPETase [17]. The extreme niches of the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau (QTP), characterized by extreme cold, strong radiation, and hypoxia, may drive the evolution of enzymes with specialized structures and functions [18]. The Tibetan glacier-derived GlacPETase features a disulfide bond topology distinct from that of type I and II PET hydrolases [18], while QTP grasslands harbor SerB involved in PVC dechlorination [13]. Notably, widespread contamination of MPs including PVC has been reported in remote QTP [19], with soil concentrations ranging 47 to 2800 items kg−1. Recent evidence revealed an additional route of MP accumulation in plants via leaf absorption [20], confirming the presence of MPs in the feces of Tibetan wild ass [21, 22]. Thus, exploring plastic-degrading enzyme in wildlife microbiomes is essential for understanding host adaptation in fragile ecosystems.

Despite the short co-evolutionary period with synthetic plastics, microbes may have co-opted lignocellulose-degrading enzymes for plastic degradation, owing to their similar backbone bonds [23–25]. This is evidenced by the ability of cutinases, laccases, and manganese peroxidases to degrade plastics [23, 26]. Our recent studies revealed a feruloyl esterase-like PET hydrolase TmFae-PETase from lignocellulose degradation-related enzymes of mealworm’s gut [27]. These findings suggest that lignocellulose-rich gut is a candidate pool for novel plastic-degrading enzymes. Among herbivores, yaks and Tibetan sheep are known for lignin degradation [28, 29], with QTP mammals exhibiting higher gut CAZyme abundance than lowland relatives (<1000 m) [30]. As a generalist herbivore that forages within 1000 m of their burrows throughout their lifetime [31], the plateau pikas (Ochotona curzoniae) regulate grassland plant diversity via stimulatory and compensatory effects [32]. The dietary profiles of pikas encompass Taraxacum mongolicum, Oxytropis kansuensis, and Medicago archiducis-nicolai [33], thereby leading to potential exposure to MPs. Pika gut encoded 3692 glycoside hydrolases (GHs) and 469 carbohydrate esterases (CEs) via CAZy database annotation [34]. These high gene copy numbers facilitate the metabolism of complex substrates (e.g., plastics and lignin) [35]. Given its status as a widespread biological sampler in QTP, we speculate that the gut microbiota of pikas is a promising source of novel plastic-degrading enzymes.

Approximately 50 plastic-degrading bacteria species and 250 plastic-degrading enzymes have been identified from different sources including insect and human gut [36–38], but those active against PVC account for 0.8% [5, 13]. This scarcity presents a critical bottleneck for PVC bioremediation. To explore the PVC-degrading potential of herbivore gut microbiomes, we established a pika feeding model and enriched a PVC-degrading consortium (PGD) from fecal samples using a top-down enrichment strategy. The consortium’s ability to degrade PVC was confirmed by multiple complementary analyses (SEM, FTIR, XPS, and GC–MS), and its differential responses to natural versus synthetic polymers allowed us to distinguish polymer-degrading specialists from generalists. Integrated metagenomic and metabolomic analyses revealed PVC-degrading pathways and key enzymes. The PVC dechlorination activity of RhHLD from Rhodococcus and the oxidative depolymerization activity of LeDyP from Leifsonia were systematically evaluated. Additionally, we confirmed the widespread distribution of these plastic-degrading enzymes in wild pikas. This study demonstrates that pika gut microbiomes harbor untapped PVC-degrading potential, expanding the catalytic toolkit for plastic bioremediation in natural ecosystems.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and reagents

Additive-containing PVC powders were procured from ARKEMA (Mw = 98 kDa, Mn = 50 kDa, Mz = 134 kDa; particle size: 120 μm) (Table S1). Additive-free PVC powders were obtained from Macklin (Mw = 128 kDa, Mn = 69 kDa, Mz = 196 kDa; particle size: 125 μm) (Text S1; Fig. S1). PVC films were prepared from the above-described PVC powder following the protocol of Zhang et al. [12] and cut into 10 × 10 mm squares for degradation assays.

Responses of pikas to various concentrations of PVC-MPs

Plateau pikas are the small mammal in the QTP, Qinghai Province, China [33]. An animal experiment was conducted in Haibei Alpine Meadow Ecosystem Research Station (37°29′N, 101°23′E) (Fig. S2), approved by the Institution of Animal Ethics and Welfare Committee of Lanzhou University (protocol No. 2024001) and carried out in accordance with the ethical guidelines of the 1975 Declaration of Helsinki.

A total of 21 healthy adult plateau pikas (110–150 g) were captured from the research site. Given that previous pika feeding experiments lasted 1–2 weeks [33], a 10-day PVC feeding experiment was conducted in an indoor facility (Text S2). Three dose levels of additive-free PVC-MPs were administered based on oral exposure levels used in mice studies [39]: control (0 mg kg−1), TA (30 mg kg−1), and TB (60 mg kg−1) (Table S2). All pikas were provided with free access to water and a diet of mixed forage and full-nutrient basic rabbit food (Beijing Xishuai Technology Co, Tables S3 and S4). Fresh fecal pellets were collected from each group on days 4, 7, and 10. After the 10-day experimental period, pikas were anesthetized with 5% isoflurane and euthanized by cervical dislocation for liver tissue collection. All fecal and liver samples were stored at −80 °C.

The detailed MP extraction procedures from liver and fecal samples were presented in Text S3. MP types in liver samples were identified via Raman spectroscopy (WITec alpha 300 R, Germany), with pristine PVC-MPs as standards. Surface morphological changes of fecal PVC-MPs were examined by scanning electron microscopy (Apreo S, Thermo Fisher, USA). Functional group alterations were analyzed by Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR, Thermo Scientific Nicolet iS50, USA) over the range of 500–5000 cm−1.

To assess the effect of PVC exposure to pika gut microbiota, total genomic DNA from fecal samples collected on day 10 was subjected to Accu16S™ absolute quantitative sequencing (Shanghai Tianhao Biotechnology). The detailed procedures were shown in Text S4. Absolute abundance of ASVs was visualized using bar plots generated with the R package ggplot2 (v3.5.1), and bubble plots were constructed in GraphPad Prism to display the abundances of microbial taxa associated with plastic degradation and host health.

Enrichment and functional characterization of PVC-degrading consortium

Fresh fecal samples from captive and wild pikas were collected for consortium enrichment, with wild pika sampling details provided in Text S5. Feces (1 g) were inoculated into 100 mL of liquid carbon-free basal medium (LCFBM) containing 1 g of additive-containing PVC powder (sterilized with 75% ethanol) and incubated at 25 °C with shaking at 180 rpm (designated T1). After 30 days, 1% (v/v) of the T1 culture was transferred into fresh LCFBM with 1 g of sterilized PVC powder and incubated for another 30 days (T2). To monitor the succession of microbial communities under PVC stress, 16S rRNA gene sequencing targeting the V3–V4 region was performed on T1, T2, and T3 samples (Text S6). This procedure was repeated through six enrichment cycles (T1–T6), yielding a stable PVC-degrading consortium designated PGD [12, 40]. The growth curves of PGD were measured the optical density at 600 nm (OD600) every 12 h in 100 mL LCFBM containing 1 g of additive or pure PVC-MPs as sole carbon, with heat-inactivated PGD and sterile medium as negative controls. The PVC mass reduction (%, w/w) was calculated as the difference between the initial weight (g) and residual weight (g) divided by the initial weight (g) after 60-day incubation [12].

Evaluation the PVC-degrading capacity of PGD

Biofilm viability and matrix composition on PGD-colonized and control PVC films were evaluated after 30 days using confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM, Stellaris 5-2) with fluorescent staining for live/dead cells and EPS, following established procedures [13]. Physical and chemical alterations of PGD-treated films or powders were examined by SEM and FTIR as described previously [25]. The chemical composition of plastic surfaces was investigated via X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) (Kratos AXIS SUPR+ XPS); spectral data were processed with CASA XPS software for peak fitting [13].

The released chloride ion concentrations were quantified in PGD-treated pure PVC and untreated samples by IC, and visualized by silver nitrate titration [13]. Briefly, 10 mL of culture supernatant was concentrated to 3 mL, then reacted with 25 mM potassium chromate (200 µL) and 0.5 M silver nitrate (100 µL). The competitive precipitation of AgCl (Ksp = 1.0 × 10−10) and Ag2CrO4 (Ksp = 1.0 × 10−12) yielded an inverse relationship between chloride concentration and red precipitate intensity, as Ag+ was preferentially consumed by Cl− (GB/T 11896–1989). Theoretical chloride yields were calculated assuming stoichiometric release from complete PVC dechlorination (56.7% Cl content) [41].

The degradation products of PGD-treated PVC cultures were analyzed by GC–MS [13]. After 30 days of incubation, culture supernatants were freeze-concentrated by vacuum centrifugation, reconstituted in tetrahydrofuran (THF), and filtered through 0.22-µm nylon membranes. GC–MS analysis was performed with a temperature gradient from 40 °C (hold, 4 min) to 280 °C (hold, 5 min) at 10 °C/min. Detector conditions were maintained at a transfer line temperature of 250 °C, ion source at 280 °C, ionization energy of 70 eV (electron impact), and scan time of 0.3 s. The compounds were identified by comparison with the NIST mass spectral library.

Response of PGD consortia to plastic and lignin degradation

To evaluate the adaptive strategy of consortium PGD toward PVC and lignin biodegradation, PGD was inoculated into minimal salt media with PVC or corn straw for 30 days, designated as the PVC group and CS group, respectively. To resolve community composition at the species level [34], full-length 16S rRNA gene sequencing was performed on the PVC and CS groups, with the original fecal inoculum as control.

Pooled DNA was sequenced on the PacBio SMRT platform (Wekemo, China). Raw reads were quality-filtered, denoised, merged, and chimera-filtered in QIIME 2 (v2019.10) [42], and taxonomically assigned by BLAST against SILVA (release 138) at 97% similarity for OTU picking. The Shannon indices and beta diversity at the OTU levels were calculated in QIIME 2. Co-occurrence networks were constructed using the “igraph” package in R, retaining edges with Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient |ρ| > 0.8 and Benjamin-Hochberg adjusted P value < 0.05, where nodes represent ASVs and edges denote significant correlations. Network visualization and module detection were performed in Gephi (v0.10) [43]. All analyses were conducted on the Wekemo Cloud Platform (https://bioincloud.tech). The 20 most abundant genera were visualized as stacked bar plots.

To interpret ecological roles, “specialist” and “generalist” species were defined according to niche breadth [44]. Specialists exhibit narrow ecological niches and dependence on specific substrates, whereas generalists display broad niche adaptability and resource utilization. Based on this framework, Leifsonia and Microbacterium, which exhibited high abundance in both the PVC group and CS group, were classified as generalists, while Rhodococcus and Paenibacillus, dominant in only one community, were designated as specialists.

Metagenomic and metabolomic analyses of PVC degradation

Metagenomic sequencing was performed to identify genes encoding putative PVC-degrading enzymes in the PVC and CS groups. Libraries were prepared with end-repair, A-tailing, adapter ligation, and purification, then sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq platform (150-bp paired-end). Raw reads were quality-filtered and host-depleted using KneadData (v0.7.4) with Bowtie2 (v2.3.5.1), and clean reads were de novo assembled into contigs with MEGAHIT (v1.2.9) [40]. Gene prediction was performed with Prodigal (v2.6.3), and gene abundance was quantified in transcripts per million (TPM) using Salmon (v1.2.1). Functional annotation was performed by aligning predicted protein sequences against the KEGG and CAZy databases using DIAMOND (v0.8.22) with an e value threshold of 1e−5 and an identity cutoff of 80% [45]. Taxonomic annotation of proteins was performed using BASTA (v1.3.2.3).

Given the high dimensionality and inter-sample variability typical of metagenomic count data, differential abundance analysis of genes was performed using DESeq2, a method originally developed for RNA-seq data that is now routinely used in metagenomics [46]. Based on the CAZy and KEGG annotations, 25 candidate PVC-degrading enzymes were identified, including dehalogenases, dehydrogenases, peroxidases, esterases, monooxygenases, and oxidases.

Untargeted metabolomics was performed to elucidate the PVC metabolic pathway. Metabolites were extracted with methanol/water (1:1, v/v), and the extracts were reconstituted in acetonitrile prior to LC–MS/MS analysis on a Thermo Scientific UltiMate 3000 HPLC system coupled to a TripleTOF 6600 mass spectrometer. Raw data were processed using Compound Discoverer 3.3.0 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) [34], with metabolites identified by mass-to-charge ratio (m/z) and retention time (RT), and annotated against the KEGG database. Metabolome profiles from PVC and CS groups were compared by PCA and PLS-DA. Differential metabolites (adjusted P < 0.05, fold change ≥ 1) were visualized as volcano plots.

Identification of PVC-degrading enzymes via metagenome-assembled genomes

Metagenomic binning was applied to recover uncultured bacteria and reconstruct complete metabolic pathways [34, 40]. Using the assembly described above, read alignment was processed with SAMtools (v1.21), and contig depths were calculated using MetaBAT2 (v2.15). Initial bins were generated from contigs > 1500 bp using MetaBAT2 and refined with RefineM (v0.1.2) [47], yielding 83 metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs). Redundant MAGs were dereplicated using dRep (v2.6.2), and 9 MAGs were retained for downstream analysis. Following MIMAG standards [30, 40, 47], high-quality MAGs were defined as completeness > 90% and contamination < 5%, and medium-quality MAGs as completeness > 75% and contamination < 10% (Table S5) [30, 40, 47]. The completeness and contamination of nine MAGs were evaluated with CheckM (v1.1.3).

Biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs) were predicted using antiSMASH (v5.0) using default parameters; clusters carrying genes implicated in plastic degradation were manually inspected, such as alkane 1-monooxygenases and catalases. To assess the distribution of plastic degradation genes across MAGs, we performed orthologous clustering and visualized the gene cluster in Adobe Illustrator 2022. The operon structures of key degradation genes were predicted based on their genomic coordinates and transcriptional orientation (the distance between adjacent genes is less than 100 bp) (Table S6). A Venn diagram showed shared and unique species between PVC and CS groups. Taxonomic assignment of MAGs was performed by alignment against the MetaRefSGB database. Phylogenetic relationships among nine MAGs were inferred with PhyloPhlAn3 (v3.0.60) and visualized using the R packages ggplot2 and ggtree. In addition, phylogenetic relationships between the nine MAGs and two known degraders, R. jostii RHA1 and Rhodococcus sp. A34, were constructed using 16S rRNA gene sequences or conserved marker genes depending on sequence availability (Text S7).

Distribution of PVC-degrading bacteria and enzymes in plateau pika gut

To evaluate the distribution of PVC-degrading bacteria in pikas, 16S rRNA gene amplicon data of 183 gut microbiota samples were downloaded from NCBI (Table S7). Samples were collected from four regions in Qinghai Province, including Gangcha, Menyuan, Guinan, and Dulan. Each dataset (>20 MB) was generated using the Illumina platform with the primers 341 F and 806 R. Sequences were denoised and merged with Deblur via Wekemo Bioincloud, then identified by BLASTN with >97% sequence identity. Relative abundances of PVC-response bacteria and known plastic-degrading bacteria were calculated, including Rhodococcus, Paenibacillus, and Microbacterium. Geographic distribution was reconstructed using the BDP cloud platform.

To map the abundance of PVC-degrading enzymes in wild pika, 39 metagenomic datasets from seven Qinghai locations were retrieved from NCBI (Table S8). Raw reads were assembled with MEGAHIT (v1.2.9) and dereplicated using MMseqs2 (v15.6). Gene sets were annotated against the NCBI NR and GTDB databases with DIAMOND (v2.0.14). Gene counts were obtained with Bowtie2 and normalized to TPM with R. The abundances of 13 enzymes involved in PVC and plastic degradation were quantified, including haloalkane dehalogenase, catalase-peroxidase, alkane 1-monooxygenase, catechol 2,3-dioxygenase, carboxylesterase, dye-decolorizing peroxidase, alcohol oxidase, cutinase, polyphenol oxidase, alcohol dehydrogenase, aldehyde dehydrogenase, long-chain-fatty-acid-coA ligase, and Fe/Mn superoxide dismutase.

Activity assay of PVC-degrading enzymes

Dechlorination plays a crucial role in the biodegradation of PVC [44]. A haloalkane dehalogenase (RhHLD) from MAG3.12 was expressed in Escherichia coli and purified via Ni–NTA affinity chromatography (see details in Text S8). The dehalogenase activity of RhHLD toward three halogenated substrates was measured using the thiocyanate-ferric nitrate colorimetric assay (see Text S9) [48]. PVC depolymerization activity of RhHLD was assessed by incubating pure PVC films (15 mg mL−1) with 100 μg mL−1 RhHLD in 50 mM phosphate buffer (pH 8.0) for 14 days. Surface morphology of RhHLD-treated PVC was analyzed by SEM and AFM [13]. The generated degradation intermediates and released chloride ion were detected as previously described [12]. A phylogenetic tree of 89 haloalkane dehalogenases was constructed using MEGA12, including all putative haloalkane dehalogenases from nine MAGs and other pika gut metagenomes.

Oxidative depolymerization further fragments PVC into small molecules [49]. The dye-decolorizing peroxidase (LeDyP) from MAG 1.14, which encodes 11 genes linked to PVC and lignin degradation, was expressed and purified in E. coli BL21 (see details in Text S10). The enzyme activity of LeDyP was analyzed using 2,2′-azino-bis(3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) (ABTS) assays as previously described [50, 51] (Text S11). As for PVC degradation, LeDyP was incubated with pure PVC films (15 mg mL−1) for 7 days in 50 mM acetate buffer (pH 5.0) containing 0.2 μM enzyme and 0.2 μM H2O2 at 25 °C. Degradation products and surface changes were analyzed by SEM, AFM, and GC–MS. In addition, the sequence similarity network (SSN) of LeDyP was generated using the Enzyme Function Initiative-Enzyme Similarity Tool (https://efi.igb.illinois.edu/efi-est/) [43, 52]. The LeDyP amino acid sequence was compared against NCBI via BLAST; proteins with >70% identity were clustered using an E value threshold of 10−5 and an alignment score cutoff of 100. The resulting protein clusters were visualized in Cytoscape (v3.10.4).

Molecular docking and dynamics analysis of RhHLD and LeDyP

The molecular structure of PVC was obtained from PubChem (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). Molecular docking was performed using AutoDock 4.2 with a 110 × 70 × 90 Å cubic grid encompassing the active site, employing default genetic algorithm parameters for ligand placement [53]. Molecular dynamics simulations were run in GROMACS 2022 with GAFF (ligands) and AMBER14SB (proteins) force fields and TIP3P water [54]. The complex was solvated in a periodic box under isothermal isobaric (NPT) conditions. Hydrogen bond constraints were applied with LINCS (2 fs time step), electrostatics were treated with particle mesh Ewald (1.2 nm cutoff), and van der Waals interactions were truncated at 1.0 nm and updated every 10 steps [55]. Temperature (298 K) and pressure (1 bar) were maintained with the V rescale and Berendsen methods, respectively. After 100 ps NVT and NPT equilibration, a 100 ns production MD was performed with conformations saved every 10 ps. Trajectories were analyzed in VMD and PyMOL; binding free energies were estimated with g_mmpbsa using the MM/PBSA approach. Protein structures were generated using AlphaFold 3 and visualized with PyMOL. Free energy landscapes were plotted in Origin 2021 to map conformational dynamics and their functional implications.

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed and visualized using SPSS 24.0, GraphPad Prism (v8.0), and OriginPro 2022. Results are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and each experiment was performed in biological triplicate unless otherwise stated. Unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-tests were used for comparisons between two groups. For comparisons involving multiple groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed, followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test. A two-tailed P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

PVC-fed plateau pikas exhibit PVC-degrading capacity

To explore the response of animals in fragile ecosystems to PVC exposure, plateau pikas, as natural samplers, were fed low (30 mg·kg−1) and high (60 mg·kg−1) doses of PVC-MPs (Table S1). Visible MPs were observed in the liver and feces (Fig. 1A), with TB liver samples containing 32.3 particles/g dry weight (Fig. 1B, C). Raman spectroscopy confirmed PVC-MPs accumulation in the liver through characteristic C–Cl (500–800 cm−1) and C–H (2900–2950 cm−1) vibrational bands (Fig. 1D). Furthermore, SEM revealed size reduction and surface pitting of fecal PVC-MPs relative to pristine PVC (Fig. 1E). Fecal-extracted PVC displayed clear C–Cl and C–H stretching bands as well as enhanced C–O and C=O absorption peaks (Fig. 1F). These results indicated the PVC degradation during gastrointestinal transit. High-dose PVC exposure markedly enriched Bacteroidota, Bacillota, Patescibacteria, and Cyanobacteriota (Fig. 1G). The absolute abundances of plastic-degrading taxa showed 2–3-fold increases in the TB group relative to the control, including Xylanibacter, Corynebacterium, Ruminococcus, and Clostridia UCG-014 (Fig. 1H). TB group also elevated the abundances of butyrate-producing bacteria, such as Lachnospiraceae NK4A136 group and Clostridia vadinBB60 group (Fig. 1I).

Domesticated consortium PGD from pika gut can degrade PVC

A stable consortium capable of degrading PVC, designated PGD, was isolated from fresh pika feces via six consecutive enrichment cycles (Fig. 2A; Figs. S3 and S4). The PGD consortium was cultured with pure and additive-containing PVC-MPs as the sole carbon source (Fig. 2B; Fig. S5), achieving a 6% reduction in polymer mass (Fig. 2E; Fig. S6A). SEM image observed significant pits and cracks on the PGD-treated PVC films, in contrast to the smooth surface of the untreated controls (Fig. 2C). The consortium formed a dense biofilm on the PVC surface, composed of both live and dead cells (Fig. 2D). Furthermore, a 12% increase in chloride ion concentration was observed in the supernatant of PGD-treated group, and the measured ion levels were equivalent to 4.5% of the theoretical value (P < 0.05) (Fig. 2F; Fig. S6B). This result was visually corroborated by a colorimetric precipitation assay, where the treated sample exhibited a lighter color than the control (Fig. 2G). XPS analysis demonstrated an increase in C=O/C–O functional groups and a concurrent reduction in C–Cl bonds (Fig. 2H), as evidenced by the FTIR results (Fig. S7). The formation of specific degradation products included 2,4-dimethyldodecane (C14H30), decane, 2,9-dimethyl- (C12H26), tridecane, 2-methyl- (C14H30), 1-chloroeicosane (C20H41Cl), and 14-methyl-14-(3-oxobutyryloxy)-hexadec-15-enoic acid, methyl ester (C22H38O5) (Fig. 2I).

The core PVC-degrading genera from PGD exist in wild pika gut

Plastic biodegradation involves a functional division of labor between specialist and generalist [52]. Although corn straw (CS) recruited a larger microbiota, PCA revealed that CS and PVC groups exhibited similar communities, allowing discrimination between specialist and generalist polymer-degrading taxa (Fig. 3A and B). The initial Acinetobacter-dominated inoculum (72%) shifted to a community co-dominated by the generalists Leifsonia and Microbacterium on both PVC and CS. Simultaneously, polymer-specific specialists emerged, such as Rhodococcus on PVC (25.1%) and Paenibacillus on CS (24.1%) (Fig. 3C and D). The co-occurrence network of PVC group was relatively simple (24 nodes, 44 edges) compared to CS group, and dominated by Firmicutes_D (37.5%) and Actinobacteriota (29.2%) (Fig. 3E–G). The CS network incorporated all its members while adding phyla such as Campylobacterota, Planctomycetota, and Desulfobacterota, suggesting a more synergistic microbial consortium (Fig. 3G). The PVC network exhibited lower modularity and clustering coefficient than CS and original fecal inoculum networks (Fig. 3H–J), including a Bacteroidota-dominated Module 1 (7 nodes). In contrast, the CS treatment converged into a single large module (24 nodes) dominated by Firmicutes, Proteobacteria, Fusobacteriota, and Bacteroidota (Fig. 3J). To assess the prevalence of these degraders, analysis of 183 wild pika guts revealed that the relative abundance of Rhodococcus ranged from 0.06 to 1.2‰ (Fig. 3K and L), whereas Microbacterium and Paenibacillus maintained a minimum abundance of 0.01‰. Additionally, known-degrading genera including Achromobacter, Pseudomonas, Bacillus, and Klebsiella were also detected in samples from Gangcha and Dulan.

Metagenomics reveal key enzymes for PVC degradation

Metagenomic sequencing (~40 Gb) of PVC and CS groups revealed distinct taxonomic and functional profiles (Fig. 4). De novo assembly quantified 661 shared species, and 433 taxa were uniquely enriched in PVC group (Fig. 4A). The PVC group exhibited enrichment of specialized CAZyme families with 2.4-fold increases in AA3, GH7, and AA4, while a 1.4-fold increase in GH3 compared to the CS group (Fig. 4B, C; Figs. S8 and S9). Furthermore, DESeq2 analysis revealed that 6961 genes (35.8%) showed a significant increase in PVC group (log2FC > 1, padj < 0.05) (Fig. S10), including 19 key proteins like catalase-peroxidase (KatG), feruloyl esterase, and acetylxylan esterase (CE1) (Fig. 4D). To link functional genes to specific organisms, a total of 83 metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs) were reconstructed from host-free reads, yielding 21 high-quality MAGs and 6 medium-quality MAGs (Fig. 4E). After dereplication, 9 unique high-quality MAGs were retained for analysis (Fig. 4F; Figs. S11–S13), including three Rhodococcus (MAG2.11, MAG3.16, MAG3.12), two Leifsonia (MAG1.17, MAG1.14), one Cryocola (MAG1.13), one Paenibacillus (MAG2.10), one Mycolicibacter (MAG3.6), and an unclassified Proteobacterium (MAG2.2). Genomic analysis showed that four of these MAGs harbored more than 20 genes related to plastic degradation, encoding haloalkane dehalogenases (HLDs), alkane 1-monooxygenases (AlkB), and dye-decolorizing peroxidases (DyPs) (Fig. 4F and G; Figs. S14 and S15). These findings suggest that these MAGs are significant reservoirs of plastic-degrading enzymes.

A putative PVC degradation pathway in consortium PGD

To identify potential PVC degradation pathways, a comparative metabolomic analysis of PVC and CS groups was performed by LC–MS/MS (Fig. 5). The PVC group exhibited a distinct metabolic profile, with 426 upregulated and 365 downregulated differentially expressed metabolites (DEMs) relative to the CS control (Fig. 5A; Fig. S16). Typical plastic-derived degradation products, including 16-hydroxyhexadecanoic acid and palmitic acid, were enriched in the PVC group, whereas 9-octadecenoic acid and pentadecanoic acid appeared in both groups (Fig. 5B–D; Fig. S17). Integrating these key metabolomic metabolites with metagenomic data, we proposed a putative PVC-degrading pathway by consortium PGD (Fig. 5E). The pathway was initiated by a hydrolytic dechlorination catalyzed by haloalkane dehalogenase (HLD), which substituted chlorine atoms with hydroxyl groups. This was accompanied by the non-specific cleavage of C–C and C–H bonds by a suite of oxidoreductases, including KatG, manganese peroxidase (MnP), dye-decolorizing peroxidase (DyP), monooxygenases, and dioxygenases. The initial depolymerization yielded a complex mixture of long-chain alkanes and chlorinated compounds. These products were channeled into a sequential oxidation pathway: hydroxylated intermediates were converted to aldehydes and formed long-chain fatty acids via the concerted action of alcohol dehydrogenase/oxidase (Adh/AOX) and aldehyde dehydrogenase (Aldh). Following activation by long-chain fatty acyl-CoA synthetase (ACSL), the fatty acids entered β-oxidation to produce acetyl-CoA for assimilation into the TCA cycle. Furthermore, six of these key enzymes were significantly enriched in the PVC group.

Haloalkane dehalogenase RhHLD drives hydrolytic dechlorination of PVC

Given the critical role of dechlorination in PVC biodegradation, phylogenetic analysis of 11 HLDs from nine MAGs revealed their distributions across the HLD-I, HLD-II, and epoxide hydrolase subfamilies (Fig. 6A). Among them, the molecular weight of purified haloalkane dehalogenase RhHLD, from MAG 3.12 (Rhodococcus), was 32.5 kDa (Fig. 6B). RhHLD exhibited the typical α/β-hydrolase fold architecture of the HLD-II subfamily, including the α5′ helix and a catalytic pentad that employed specific Glu and Asn residues (Fig. 6C, D). Molecular docking indicated a stable binding of PVC to RhHLD (Fig. 6D). The RMSF values of most residues were below 0.3 nm, based on molecular dynamics simulations (Fig. S18). Free-energy landscape analysis also observed stable conformational states upon ligand binding (Fig. 6E). RhHLD dechlorinated 25.5% of the model substrate 1-chlorobutane (Fig. S19). Co-incubation of RhHLD and PVC films resulted in obvious surface pits and cracks (Fig. 6F, G), quantified by a 10-fold increase in surface roughness (Ra) and a 16.4-fold increase in depression depth (Fig. 6H). Compared to the control, chloride ion release from RhHLD-treated powder increased by 11.5 mg L−1 (Fig. 6I). GC–MS further identified PVC degradation intermediates, including 1-tetradecanol-14-chloro (C14H29ClO), 9-octadecen-12-ynoic acid, methyl ester (C19H32O2), 1,6,11,16-tetraoxacycloeicosane (C16H32O4), hexadecanoic acid, 1-(hydroxymethyl)−1,2-ethanediyl ester (C35H68O5), and octadecanoic acid, 4-hydroxybutyl ester (C22H44O3) (Fig. 6K).

Dye-decolorizing peroxidase LeDyP oxidatively depolymerizes PVC

PVC degradation requires synergistic dechlorination and oxidation. A dye-decolorizing peroxidase (LeDyP) from Leifsonia MAG 1.14 was identified from a functionally distinct cluster through sequence similarity network analysis (Fig. 7A, B). AlphaFold3 predicted that LeDyP adopts a fold with a core antiparallel β-sheet barrel surrounded by α-helices (Fig. 7C). A stable ligand conformation of PVC was observed within the active site of LeDyP through interactions involving residues, such as His213, Arg231, and Asp140 (Fig. 7C, D). Structural superposition of LeDyP and R. jostii DyPB (PDB: 3QNR) showed that LeDyP possessed a flexible loop (residues 201–210) (Fig. 7D). LeDyP, with a molecular weight of 32.8 kDa, showed optimal activity at pH 5 and 25 °C when using ABTS as the substrate (Fig. 7E–G). As expected, treatment with LeDyP increased the PVC film surface roughness by 4.2-fold and the depression depth by 2.4-fold (Fig. 7I and J). Long-chain alkanes (C24H50, C22H46) and esters (C24H48O2, C16H32O2) were detected in the LeDyP-treated supernatant (Fig. 7K). These findings demonstrate the oxidative depolymerization of PVC.

PVC-degrading enzymes are widespread in wild pika gut

To assess the natural distribution of plastic-degrading enzymes, we analyzed 39 publicly available metagenomic samples from wild plateau pikas across diverse geographical regions (Table S8). The relative abundance of haloalkane dehalogenase was only 0.03–0.39 TPM (Fig. 8), which was 85-fold lower than that in the consortium PGD (Fig. 6E). This trend extended to other upstream enzymes (e.g., AlkB) for bond cleavage, hydrogenation, and oxygenation. Although dye-decolorizing peroxidase was detected only in the gut of plateau pikas from Reshui (0.16 TPM), other oxidoreductases were widely distributed across the intestinal microbiota of most plateau pikas (Fig. 8). Additionally, downstream metabolism-related enzymes, such as Adh, Aldh, and ACSL, were consistently abundant across all pikas. The abundance of these enzymes was more than two orders of magnitude higher than that of upstream polymer-targeting enzymes, suggesting these core metabolic pathways are available to process intermediate products once initial depolymerization occurs.

Discussion

MP pollution has pervaded even in the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau (QTP) and local Tibetan wild ass, such as PVC [21, 22]. These MPs can also adversely affect plants and animals in fragile ecosystems, even at concentrations as low as 10 μg mL−1 [21, 56]. Unlike the broad tissue dissemination observed in mice [57], PVC-MPs were detected exclusively in the livers of plateau pikas, potentially reflecting a higher intrinsic tolerance in wildlife species [30, 58]. We also observed significant alterations in the surface morphology of PVC-MPs following their passage through pika gut, consistent with findings of PS and PVC particles recovered from insect feces [25, 59]. Actinobacteriota, Fusobacteriota, and Bacteroidota represent the most prevalent phyla in the gut of microplastic-ingesting seabirds [60]. Exposure to MPs markedly enriched Clostridia and Bacteroidota in the mouse gut, exemplified by a 2-fold increase in Ruminococcus abundance and the displacement of Lactobacillaceae by Lachnospiraceae [61]. PVC-MPs exposure of pikas increased the abundance of potential plastic-degrading taxa and recruited gut barrier-protective bacteria. Akkermansia contributes to intestinal barrier integrity by suppressing inflammation, whereas Lachnospiraceae supports a healthy gut ecosystem by competitively inhibiting pathogens [62, 63].

PVC biodegradation is thermodynamically feasible but requires proper enzymes to overcome kinetics barriers. Although numerous plastic-degrading bacteria have been isolated from extreme environments (compost, glaciers), typical habitats (landfills and oceans), and insect gut, representative PVC-degrading bacteria include Pseudomonas, Campylobacter, Rhodococcus, and Klebsiella [12, 38]. The gut microbiota of herbivores represent a promising frontier for bioremediation [12, 36, 38]; this is supported by the established functional overlap between plastic- and lignocellulose-degrading enzymes [23, 27]. The pika preferentially feeds on lignocellulose-rich plants, with gut-derived Prevotellaceae involved in lignin degradation [33, 34]. Given its role as a keystone herbivore in the QTP ecosystem, the pika is considered as an ideal model for mining novel plastic-degrading enzymes [32, 64]. Biodegradation of PVC is initiated by biofilm formation on the polymer surface, which is a prerequisite for enzymatic breakdown [65]. The PGD-treated films exhibited characteristic pits and cracks, consistent with known degradation patterns reported for PS and PET [12, 25]. However, the growth of PGD was concurrently inhibited (Fig. 2B), an effect likely attributable to the accumulation of toxic intermediates generated during PVC degradation [38, 40]. The gut-enriched consortium EF1 from Tenebrio molitor larvae removed 6.1% of medium-molecular-weight pure PVC [38], whereas the PGD achieved a weight loss of 6.7% for high-molecular-weight pure PVC (Fig. 2E). This indicates a synergistic degradation capacity [66]. Chloride ion content of PGD-treated group increased by 1.2 mg L−1, while a gut-derived consortium doubled the supernatant chloride concentration, resulting in an increase of 2.2 mg L−1 [38]. Octadecenoic acid (C18H34O2) represents a PVC-degrading intermediate by the larval gut microbiota [38, 44]; our findings revealed the formation of long-chain alkanes (C12, C14), ester compounds (e.g., C22H38O5), and 1-chloroeicosane (C20H41Cl). Other PVC-degrading consortium observed the release of C15H32 and C16H34 [13]. These discrepancies result from stochastic and non-specific cleavage of carbon bonds by different microbial species.

Existing evidence shows that PE degradation is driven by Rhodococcus and Pseudomonas [52]. Our results reveal distinct adaptive strategies for PVC and CS, identifying Rhodococcus as a PVC-responsive specialist, in contrast to Paenibacillus, which acts as a CS-responsive specialist. This aligns with the established role of Rhodococcus in degrading recalcitrant polymers like plastics and lignin [40, 49, 51]. In contrast, Leifsonia and Microbacterium were identified as the generalists in both PVC and CS groups due to broad substrate utilization and ecological niches [52]. Furthermore, Leifsonia serves as a core gut symbiont linked to PS degradation in Spodoptera frugiperda larvae [44, 67], as well as hydantoins and caffeine [35, 68]. Microbacterium is involved in the PVC degradation of larvae, and also breaks down lignocellulose by secreting cellulolytic and xylanolytic enzymes [44, 69]. Although individual strains show modest activity, cross-species cooperation facilitates plastic mineralization [66]. The co-occurrence network of PVC group displayed a simplified structure, characterized by fewer nodes and connections. This structural reduction suggests that PVC utilization selectively enriches a specialized microbial community equipped with the necessary enzymes [12, 38, 44]. PVC-degrading consortia from QTP grasslands recruit Planctomycetota and Bacteroidota in response to CS [13]. This restructured process enables originally rare taxa to thrive under substrate-specific selection pressures [40, 70]. Paenibacillus is a well-known degrader of complex biopolymers, including lignocellulose, rice straw fiber, and polypropylene [71, 72]. Our studies observed the significant enrichment of Paenibacillus in the CS group (24.1%) likely attributable to its secretion of peroxidases, laccases, and various hydrolases [72]. In contrast, its abundance in the PVC group was two orders of magnitude lower (0.5%). The presence of 14 plastic-degrading taxa within the wild plateau pika gut reveals a specialized community for recalcitrant substrates (Fig. 3K and L). This adaptation, which enables the digestion of a lignin-rich diet, positions the pika gut as an underexplored reservoir for degrading synthetic polymers like PS, PE, and PP [73].

CAZymes play direct or auxiliary roles in lignin and plastic degradation, including representative families such as AAs, CEs, and GHs [24, 25, 74]. The enzymes from AA3 family can generate H2O2, which synergizes with other enzymes to facilitate the oxidative degradation of plastics [74, 75]. Our study found that 76% targeted PVC-degrading enzymes showed high abundances in the PVC group, including monooxygenase, dioxygenase, peroxidase, and esterase. Microbial-mediated degradation pathways for plastics (e.g., PVC, PS, and PE) are largely inferred from multi-omics analyses [12, 13], whereas the detailed degradation mechanisms remain unclear. The initial step in PVC biodegradation is the dehalogenase-mediated substitution of chlorine with a hydroxyl group [13, 76]. Subsequent PVC depolymerization proceeds through oxidative enzymes, which commonly participate in the degradation of PE and PS [77]. A series of peroxidases (e.g., KatG, MnP, DyP) convert dechlorinated PVC oligomers into low-molecular-weight polymers [12, 59], whereas CYP450s and AlkB hydroxylate alkanes at terminal and mid-chain positions [75]. Adh transforms the resulting alcohols to aldehydes and ketones, while Aldh oxidizes aldehydes to long-chain fatty acids [78]. ACSL catalyzes the conversion of long-chain fatty acids to acyl-CoA, which enters the β-oxidation cycle [25]. Other studies further suggest that alcohol oxidases and CYP450s oxidize the PVC backbone to produce ketones and aldehydes, which are then hydrolyzed by esterases into alcohols and long-chain fatty acids [77, 78]. Metagenome-assembled genomes expedite the identification of novel plastic-degrading bacteria and their enzymes [40, 77]. Among the nine MAGs, Rhodococcus dominated the PVC-enriched consortium (38.0%), whereas Leifsonia was more prevalent in the CS group (57.4%) than in the PVC group (22.5%), consistent with their distinct substrate preferences (Fig. S20). Phylogenetic analysis further revealed that MAG2.11 and MAG1.14 formed close clusters with the known plastic-degrading strains R. jostii RHA1 and Rhodococcus sp. A34 (Fig. 4G; Figs. S21 and S22) [49, 51], respectively, supporting their potential roles in PVC and lignin degradation [13]. Despite lacking the ecological adaptation mechanisms of our reassembled Cryocola [79], this MAG encodes 10 PVC-degrading enzymes, including haloalkane dehalogenase and aldehyde dehydrogenase. By contrast, the unclassified Proteobacteria MAG only harbors HLD, Mn-dependent superoxide dismutase, and catechol-2,3-dioxygenase. This uneven gene distribution drives metabolic division and niche differentiation [52, 66, 77]. Notably, Mycolicibacter (MAG3.6), despite its low abundance (0.22%, Fig. S20), harbored highest number PVC-degrading candidate genes, echoing the functional prominence of Mycolicibacter on PE-MPs [80].

Enzymatic PVC degradation proceeds via dehalogenation and oxidation [13], including reductive, oxidative, and hydrolytic pathways [76, 81]. The enzyme RhHLD, a member of the HLD-II subfamily, catalyzes the hydrolytic dehalogenation of carbon-halogen bonds [76]. A similar phenomenon was observed in dehalogenase DmmA, which acts on 29 halogenated substrates, including 1,2-dichloroethane and 1,2-dichloropropane [82]. RhHLD dechlorinates PVC via an SN2-type nucleophilic attack [83], with a 1.2-fold higher activity than that of NAD-dependent oxidoreductase [13, 44]. Although haloacid dehalogenase SerB shares a similar hydrolytic dechlorination mechanism, RhHLD does not produce the specific depolymerization products generated by SerB, such as C18H33Cl3O2 and C39H78O3 [13]. This observation strongly supports a non-specific mode of action, where the enzyme cleaves the polymer backbone at stochastic sites [84]. Oxidases catalyze the cleavage of C–C and C–H bonds in non-hydrolyzable plastics [78]. A catalase-peroxidase RpKatG oxidizes C–H sites to hydroxyl groups and generated water-soluble fragments [85]. Co-incubation of PS with laccase-like Lac640 reduced 14% Mw and released undecanoic acid (C11H22O2) and hexadecanoic acid (C18H36O2) [25]. As a heme-containing peroxidase, DyP mediates electron transfer from peroxidase substrates to H2O2 while oxidizing structurally complex substrates such as lignin, alkenes, and plastics [51, 75]. LeDyP-treated PVC films exhibited pronounced surface pitting, and the detection of C24H50 and C24H48O2, indicating the oxidative depolymerization of PVC. DyP from Nostocaceae (AnaPX) reduced the Mw of UV-pretreated polystyrene films by 19.7% [86]. Similar to the cleavage of the Cα–Cβ bond in lignin model compounds by DyPs, AnaPX cleaves C–C bonds activated by carbonyl (C=O) and hydroxyl (C–OH) groups [51, 86].

While the accumulation of MPs in plateau pikas has not been reported, our study firstly confirmed that their gut microbiota can degrade PVC and harbor novel plastic-degrading enzymes. This finding aligns with extensive reports of widespread PET/PVC-degrading enzymes in marine and grassland ecosystems [11, 13]. Recent investigation showed that bacteria carrying PET-degrading enzymes (PETases) with a conserved functional motif are detected in nearly 80% of global ocean regions [87]. While diverse PVC-degrading enzymes are widespread in grassland soils (0.013–0.214 hits/Mb) [13], the gut microbiome of plateau pikas harbors PVC-degrading enzymes at abundances ranging from 0.00001 to 0.455 hits/Mb. The widespread occurrence of plastic-degrading enzymes predicted by bioinformatics necessitates experimental validation to distinguish active enzymes from their inactive counterparts (pseudo-enzymes) [87]. Our work supports the hypothesis that lignocellulose-rich environments, such as the herbivore gut, are fertile grounds for discovering plastic-degrading enzymes, owing to cross-reactivity between plastic- and lignocellulose-degrading enzymes [24, 88]. This points to a previously unrecognized role of wildlife gut microbiomes in plastic transformation and highlights their potential as a source of biocatalysts for environmental remediation. Despite these promising findings, our results are based on a single captive pika population under controlled feeding conditions, which cannot fully capture the complexity of natural dietary and environmental exposures in wild populations; whether the observed degradation capacity extends to other herbivore species remains unclear. Although RhHLD and LeDyP served as key enzymes mediating dechlorination and oxidative depolymerization, their structural basis of substrate recognition needs to be elucidated through crystallography and QM/MM simulations. While mass balance and product profiling suggested PVC depolymerization, the absence of isotope-labeling experiments (e.g., 13C-labeled PVC or H218O) precludes direct tracking of polymer mineralization. This concern is reinforced by the observation that chloride release reached only 4.5% of the theoretical value, suggesting possible accumulation of degradation intermediates. Finally, the scalability and environmental robustness of the PGD consortium or purified enzymes need further study, including temperature, pH, or coexisting pollutants. Given that PVC mineralization depends on synergistic enzymatic cascades [77], future efforts should focus on the rational design of multi-enzyme cocktails and protein engineering to enhance catalytic efficiency and stability.

Conclusions

Our findings show that plateau pika gut microbiota recruits plastic-degrading taxa in response to PVC. The specialist Rhodococcus and generalist Leifsonia are the primary taxa associated with PVC degradation within consortia enriched on PVC and corn straw, highlighting species-level divergence between plastic- and lignin-degrading microbes. We identified RhHLD and LeDyP as responsible for PVC dechlorination and oxidative depolymerization, respectively. These findings position the pika gut serves as a potential reservoir of plastic-degrading enzymes, a notion supported by public metagenomic data from wild pikas, and suggest microbial adaptation to MP-contaminated QTP ecosystems. Furthermore, the cross-reactivity between plastic- and lignocellulose-degrading enzymes also points to the potential application of these oxidases in valorizing lignin-rich biomass.
