S100a4 mediated peritoneal B1 lymphocytes play a critical role in host protection against helminth infection
Authors: Fei Guan, Xi Luo, Yanmei Huang, Jiang Chang, Ju Liu, Wenwen Zheng, Jinyuan Zhang, Yiyan Gong, Heng Gu, Yi Wang, Yonggui Wu, Wenqi Liu, Jiahui Lei, Chaohong Liu

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTrichinellosis is a globally prevalent parasitic disease caused by infection of Trichinella spiralis. Background

Helminth infections affect billions of people worldwide. Trichinellosis is a prevalent zoonotic helminth disease caused by Trichinella spiralis, which infects humans and animals by ingesting raw or undercooked larvae-contaminated meat. A unique trait of T. spiralis is its stage-specific tissue tropism within a single host: adult worms reside in the small intestine, while newborn larvae migrate to and parasitize skeletal muscle [1]. The intestine phase initiates 3–7 days post-infection (PI), when ingested larvae invade the intestinal mucosa and mature into adults, which subsequently release newborn larvae into the circulation. These larvae disseminate widely in host tissues but only mature in skeletal muscle, triggering persistent muscle damage. This establishes a long-lasting muscle infection phase starting at 2–3 weeks PI and peaking at approximately 5 weeks PI [2, 3].

Host immune responses to T. spiralis infection are stage-specific and dynamically regulated [4]. Most studies have focused on T cell immunity, with infection inducing an early T helper 1 (Th1) dominant response during the intestine stage and a predominant Th2 response in the muscle stage. Beyond cellular immunity, B lymphocyte-mediated humoral immunity is critical for host defense against T. spiralis [5], yet the functions and regulatory mechanisms of specific B cell subsets during infection remain poorly defined.

B cells comprise two distinct subsets, B1 and B2 cells. They differ in progenitors, phenotypic features, self-renewal capacity, and immune functions. Conventional B2 cells derive from bone marrow progenitors, mediate T cell-dependent specific humoral immunity, and include splenic marginal zone (MZ) and follicular (FO) B cells [6, 7]. In contrast, B1 cells originate from fetal liver progenitors, display a unique surface phenotype (high IgM/CD43, low B220/IgD/CD23), and predominantly populate the peritoneal and pleural cavities [8–11]. Mouse B1 cells are divided into major CD5 B1a (55–80%) and minor CD5 B1b (20–45%) subsets [12]. Functionally, B1 cells produce immunoglobulins and immunomodulatory cytokines [6, 9, 13, 14]. B1a cells generate natural IgM, and B1b cells confer T-independent long-term humoral protection [8, 15]. In addition, B1 cells are a main source of interleukin 10 (IL-10) during infections [16], endowing them with dual antibody-producing and regulatory immune function.

B1 cells have been well characterized in autoimmune, ischemia/reperfusion injury, metabolic and inflammatory diseases [17], but are poorly investigated in parasitic infections. Recently, some experimental models indicate that B1 cells modulate host susceptibility to parasitic infections in a context-dependent manner [13]. Although B cells contribute to anti-T. spiralis immunity [18, 19], the high heterogeneity and functional plasticity of B1 cells make their specific roles in trichinellosis largely unclarified.

B cell receptors (BCRs) signaling is important for B cell selection and maintenance, and B1 cells require stronger BCR signaling for functional maturation than B2 cells [20–22]. Enhanced BCR signaling drives B1 cell differentiation during viral and bacterial infections [23], whereas the BCR-based regulatory mechanisms governing B1 cell activation in parasitic infection remain undefined.

S100A4 (FSP1/metastasin), a key S100 family protein [24, 25], regulates cell migration, proliferation and inflammation. It is involved in lung, liver and kidney tumors or fibrosis diseases [26, 27]. S100A4 upregulation is reported in multiple infectious and inflammatory disorders, implying its immunoregulatory role in infection [28–30]. While S100A4 is predominantly expressed in macrophages and fibroblasts, data from the Gene skyline database verify B1 cells as a major S100A4-expressing population (www.immgen.org/). However, the function of S100A4 in B1 cell biology and T. spiralis infection remains unreported.

In this study, we explored the dynamic changes and functions of peritoneal B1 cells during T. spiralis infection. The results indicated that peritoneal B1 cells were increased with enhanced BCR signaling at the muscle phase. B1 cell deficiency impaired murine immune responses against T. spiralis. RNA sequencing analysis indicated elevated expression of S100A4 in infected peritoneal B1 cells. Mechanistically, S100A4 regulated B1 cell abundance, BCR signaling, and antibody production, modulating B1 cell-mediated anti-parasitic immunity in T. spiralis infection.

Methods

Mice and animal model

B cell-specific Btk (bruton’s tyrosine kinase) conditional knockout mice on a C57BL/6 N background were obtained by crossbreeding Mb1/CD79Acre mice with Btkflox/flox mice. S100a4 global knockout (KO) mice with a C57BL/6 N background were kindly provided by Professor Zhihai Qin in the Institute of Biophysics, Chinese Academy of Sciences (Beijing, China). Wild type (WT) C57BL/6 N mice were purchased from the Hubei Center for Disease Control and Prevention (Wuhan, China). All the mice were maintained in a standard specific pathogen-free animal facility of Tongji Medical College of Huazhong University of Science and Technology, Wuhan.

T. spiralis was maintained by serial passages in female and male Kunming (KM) mice according to our previous publication [31]. Female C57BL/6 N mice aged 6–8 weeks were orally ingested with 300 larvae of T. spiralis. Mice were sacrificed at 1 (intestine stage) or 5 weeks PI (muscle stage) for subsequent experiments.

Ethics statement

All animal experimental procedures were approved by the Animal Ethical Committee of Tongji Medical College of Huazhong University of Science and Technology.

Determination of skeletal muscle larvae burden

Muscle larvae were collected and counted according to the previous publication [32]. Briefly, infected mice were euthanized at 5 weeks PI and total skeletal muscle tissues were harvested after removal of skin, viscera, and bones. After weighing, muscle tissues were digested for 3 h in artificial digestion buffer containing 1% hydrochloric acid (HCl) and 1% pepsin. Larvae were enriched by repeated sedimentation and manually counted under a microscope.

Preparation of soluble antigens of T. spiralis larvae

Crude soluble antigens were extracted from purified T. spiralis muscle larvae following the protocol described by Huseein et al. [33]. Larvae were washed several times by sedimentation in Phosphate Buffered Saline (PBS), followed by intermittent sonication for 30 min. The homogenate was centrifuged at 4,000 rpm for 30 min. The supernatant was collected and filtered with a 0.22 μm filter. The protein concentration of larvae soluble antigens (TS ag) was quantified using a BCA protein assay kit (# P0012, Beyotime Biotechnology).

Hematoxylin-eosin (HE) staining

Skeletal muscle tissues were collected from the T. spiralis infected mice at 5 weeks PI and fixed in 4% formalin solution. Fixed tissues were embedded, cut into 5 μm sections and subjected to HE staining for histological observation.

Preparation of single cells from peritoneal cavity (PC), thoracic cavity (TC), spleen and lymph nodes

Cells from PC or TC were collected by lavage with 5 mL sterile PBS containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). Cell suspensions were centrifuged, and cell pellets were resuspended and quantified. Spleen and lymph nodes were collected from the mice and single-cell suspensions were obtained by gently ground through a 70 μm cell strainer. Splenic single cells were quantified following red blood cell lysis as previously described [34].

Isolation of lymphocytes from muscle

For muscle lymphocyte isolation, 0.5 g of mouse quadriceps femoris tissues were dissected, with tendon or adipose tissues removed, and cut into small pieces. Tissues were incubated with 1 mL digestion buffer containing 0.75 U/mL collagenase D, 1.2 U/mL Disparse II, and 2.5 mM CaCl2 at 37 ℃ for 45 min. The digestion reaction was terminated with PBS and cell suspension was filtered using a 70 μm cell strainer. After red blood cell lysis, purified single muscle lymphocytes were obtained for further use.

Flow cytometry and cell sorting

PC cells were stained with fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC) or PerCP/Cy5.5 conjugated anti-mouse CD19 (#152404, #115533, Biolegend), Brilliant Violet (BV) 421 conjugated anti-mouse IgM (#406518, Biolegend), PerCP conjugated anti-mouse IgD (#405736, Biolegend), phycoerythrin (PE)/Cy7 conjugated anti-mouse CD5 (#100622, Biolegend) and APC/Cy7 conjugated anti-mouse CD11b (#101226, Biolegend). Isolated cells with surface markings CD19+IgM+IgD− and CD19+IgMIntIgD+ were defined as B1 and B2 cells, respectively. Intracellular staining for IgM (#553437, BD Pharmingen), IgG1 (#553443, BD Pharmingen) and IL-10 (#554467, BD Pharmingen) was followed with fixation and permeabilization. For stimulation, partial PC cells were treated with 20 µg/mL TS ag, 10 µg/mL biotin-sp-conjugated affinipure F(ab’)2 fragment anti-mouse Ig(M + G) (F(ab’)2, #115-066-068, Jackson) or 20 µg/mL recombinant S100A4 protein (#HY-P71084, MCE) for 24 h. Cells were stained with surface markings of CD19, CD23 for 30 min and Fixable Viability Stain 700 (#564997, BD Pharmingen) for 15 min. After fixation/ permeabilization, cells were incubated with primary antibodies against BTK (#8547S, Cell signaling Technology), SYK (#13198S, Cell signaling Technology), SHIP1 (#A3571, Abclonal), phosphorylated phosphotyrosine (pY, #ab179530, Abcam), pERK (#AP0974, Abclonal) or pWASP (#ab59278, Abcam) for 30 min, followed by Alexa fluor (AF) 405 conjugated goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody IgG (#A-31556, Thermo fisher) for 30 min.

Splenic or lymph nodes single cells were surface-stained with the following conjugate-labelled antibodies: BV510-B220 (#103247, Biolegend), AF647-GL7 (#144606, Biolegend), FITC-CD95 (#152606, Biolegend), BV421-IgM, PerCP-IgD, APC-CD21 (#123412, Biolegend), PE-CD23 (#101608, Biolegend), PE/Cy7-CD5 and APC-CD43 (#143208, Biolegend). Splenic B cell subsets were gated as follows: FO B cells (B220hiIgMlowIgDhi), MZ B cells (B220hiCD21hiCD23low), and germinal center (GC) B cells (B220+GL7hiCD95hi). B1 cells in spleen and lymph nodes were identified as CD19+CD43+IgM+, while B1 cells in muscle tissues were marked with eFluor 450 CD45 Monoclonal Antibody (#48-0451-82, Invitrogen) and identified as CD45+CD19+CD43+. All the data were performed by flow cytometer (Attune Nxt, ThermoFisher) and analyzed with FlowJo V10 software.

For purification of peritoneal B1 cells, peritoneal cells were stained with anti-mouse CD19, anti-mouse CD43 and anti-mouse CD23 antibodies, and B1 cells were sorted as CD19+CD43+CD23− population using a BD flow cytometer.

In vivo BrdU labeling

For in vivo cell proliferation detection, each mouse was injected intraperitoneally with 1 mg of BrdU (#B9285, Sigma-Aldrich) diluted in 100 µL PBS daily from days 7 to 13 PI, with an additional 1 mg BrdU booster dose administered 24 h before tissue isolation [35]. At 5 weeks PI, single cell suspension of PC, TC and spleen were collected from the mice. BrdU staining was performed according to standard protocol. Briefly, cells were surface-stained prior to fixation and permeabilization, and then incubated with freshly prepared digestion solution of DNase I (300 µg/ml) in 37 ℃ for 1 h. Lastly, cells were stained with APC-conjugated anti-BrdU antibody and analyzed by a flow cytometer.

Intracellular Calcium (Ca2+) level measurement

Isolated peritoneal B1 cells were incubated with 1 µM Fluo-4 AM (#S1060, Beyotime Biotechnology) in Ca2+ free Hank’s Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS) for 25 min. After washing twice, cells were surface stained with PerCP/Cy5.5-anti-CD19, PE-anti-CD23 and APC-anti-CD43 antibodies for 30 min. Following another two washes, cells were resuspended in RPMI 1640 medium containing 10% FBS. For flow cytometric calcium flux analysis, cells were stably recorded for 30 s to obtain baseline fluorescence, followed by immediate stimulation with 10 µg/mL F(ab’)2, for a continuous 270 s detection.

Immunofluorescence assay

Sorted peritoneal B1 cells were incubated with larvae antigens (20 µg/mL) at 37 °C for 24 h. Approximately 2–5 × 105 treated B1 cells were seeded onto slides and stained with AF594 conjugated affinipure F(ab’)2 fragment anti-mouse Ig(M + G) (#115-586-068, Jackson) at 4 °C for 30 min. Cells were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, rinsed with PBS, and permeabilized with 0.05% saponin (Sigma-Aldrich). For intracellular staining, cells were incubated with primary antibodies against pY, phospho-BTK (#Ab52192, Abcam), phospho-SYK (#2710S, Cell signaling technology), or phospho-SHIP (#3941S, Cell signaling technology) at 4 °C for 30 min, followed by incubation with AF405 conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody for 30 min.

For tissue immunofluorescence, diaphragm muscle tissues were collected and embedded with optimal cutting temperature (OCT) compound for frozen sectioning. Serial 5 μm tissue slices were permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 15 min and blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 30 min. Sections were subsequently incubated with AF488 conjugated goat anti-mouse IgM for 45 min and counterstained with 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) for 5 min.

All fluorescence images were captured with a Nikon confocal fluorescence microscope (C2 + N-SIM, Nikon, Japan) and analyzed using NIS Elements software.

RNA isolation and real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted from purified peritoneal B1 cells using the chloroform /isopropyl alcohol method. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was reverse transcribed using a commercial reverse transcription Kit (#RK20429, Abclonal) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Real-time PCR was performed on a qTower 3 PCR system (Germany) with SYBR green qPCR mix (#RK21203, Abclonal) and the following probes: Gapdh and S100a4. The primer sequences were listed in Table 1.

Transcriptomic analyses of peritoneal B1 cells by RNA-sequencing

Total RNA was isolated from purified peritoneal B1 cells of unifected and and infected WT mice, followed by quality and integrity assessment. RNA libraries were constructed using the MGIEasy RNA Directional Library Prep Kit (MGI Tech) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Sequencing was performed on the DNBSEQ-G400 platform (MGI Tech), generating approximately 20 million reads per sample. Reads were aligned to the mouse reference genome GRCm38. RNA sequencing data were processed and analyzed using DEGseq to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and gene ontology (GO) enrichments in Dr. Tom platform (https://biosys.bgi.com). The thresholds for identifying between-group DEGs include an adjusted p-value ≤ 0.05 (Benjamini-Hochberg correction - false discovery rate, FDR) and fold change expression in log2 (log2FC) ≥ 1. The phyper in R software was used for GO enrichment analysis.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISA)

ELISA wells were coated overnight at 4 °C with crude larvae antigens (20 µg/mL) of T. spiralis or with commercial antibodies, then blocked with 5% BSA in Tris-buffered saline (TBS) for 1 h. Peritoneal lavage fluid was separated from PC lavage fluid by removing cells. Sera were isolated by centrifugation from the whole blood and serially diluted. 100 µL of peritoneal lavage fluid or diluted sera was added into each well, and incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. After washing thrice with TBST solution, rabbit-anti-mouse IgM, IgG or IgG3 secondary antibodies were added and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. Tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) substrate was added and optical density (OD) values at 450 nm were detected by a Tecan Infinite 200-pro microplate reader (Switzerland).

Quantification and statistical analysis

Data were presented as mean ± SD (standard deviation) and analyzed with GraphPad Prism 9 software. Comparisons of two groups were analyzed by Student’s unpaired t-test, while comparison among multiple groups was performed by one-way ANOVA and Dunnett post-hoc tests. P value of less than 0.05 was considered significant. Statistically significant differences were shown as follows: * p < 0.05; ** p < 0.01; *** p < 0.001; **** p < 0.0001.

Results

Peritoneal B1 cells are increased at the muscle stage of T. spiralis infection

Given the important roles of B cells in pathogen infections, we detected the changes of B cells in infected mice at the intestine (1 week PI) and muscle (5 weeks PI) stages. The results indicated no difference in peritoneal B cells at 1 week PI between the uninfected and infected mice. However, the infected mice had enhanced total B cells (CD19+) and B1 cells (CD19+IgM+IgD−) in the PC at 5 weeks PI (Fig. 1A-D), accompanied by a decrease of B2 cells (Fig. S1A). In addition, the proportion of the B1b subgroup (CD5−) was increased while the ratio of B1a (CD5+) decreased at 5 weeks PI in the infected mice, though both subgroups had increased cell number as the total B cell number rose upon infection (Fig. 1C, D). TC B1 cells, especially B1b, were also increased in mice at 5 weeks PI (Fig. 1E). The above results showed that the body cavity B1 cells were enhanced in the infected mice at the muscle phase.

Studies have reported that B1 cells commonly traffic from body cavities to secondary lymphoid organs upon antigen challenge or infection [36]. Next, we detected percentages of B1 cells in the spleen and lymph nodes. Unexpectedly, there was no difference in B1 cells in the spleen, mesenteric lymph nodes (mLN) or groin lymph nodes (gLN) between the uninfected and infected mice at 5 weeks PI (Fig. 1F-H, Fig. S1B). As the proportion of B1 cells in TC was increased, we further detected B1 cells in lung tissues and found no changes in lung B1 cells too (Fig. S1C). To clarify the driver of peritoneal B1 expansion, we performed in vivo BrdU proliferation labeling. Neither PC, TC nor splenic B1 cells displayed altered proliferative activity after infection, and B1-cell apoptotic rates in the peritoneal cavity were also indistinguishable across groups (Fig. 1I; Fig. S1D–G).

As skeletal muscle tissues are the primary site of infection during T. spiralis infection, we isolated tissue-infiltrating lymphocytes and found that T. spiralis infection induced a significant increase of muscle-resident B1 cells (Fig. 1J). In addition, splenic B cells of FO, MZ and GC B cells were detected in infected mice. As the supplemental Fig. 2 depicted, the proportions of MZ and GC B cells were increased in the infected mice at both 1 and 5 weeks PI (Fig. S2B, C), while no difference in FO B cells in infected mice compared to the control (Fig. S2D). Taken together, these data demonstrate specific expansion of peritoneal B1 cells during the muscle phase of T. spiralis infection.

BCR signaling is enhanced in peritoneal B1 cells from infected mice

BCR signaling is critical for B cell growth, survival, and activation [37]. To evaluate the effects of T. spiralis infection on BCR signaling of B1 cells, murine peritoneal B1 cells were collected and then stimulated with T. spiralis larvae antigens for 24 h in vitro. The protein levels of positive BCR signaling molecules of BTK and SYK, and negative signaling molecule of SHIP were detected with flow cytometry. The results showed that there were no differences in expression levels of these proteins with or without antigens stimulation (Fig. 2A-C, Fig. S3). But T. spiralis infection led to increased expression levels of pY, pBTK, and pSYK in peritoneal B1 cells (Fig. 2D-F, Fig. S3C). In addition, the expression levels of pY, pBTK, pSYK, and pSHIP in the peritoneal B1 cells from the uninfected mice were increased to varying degrees after in vitro antigen stimulation (Fig. 2D-G). Furthermore, compared to those from the uninfected mice, the infected peritoneal B1 cells had increased intensity of pBTK and pSHIP after stimulation with larval antigens (Fig. 2B, D). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that T. spiralis muscle stage infection strongly potentiates BCR signaling activation in peritoneal B1 cells.

B1 cell deficiency impairs murine immune responses against T. spiralis at the muscle stage

Considering the significant increase of peritoneal B1 cells at the muscle phase of murine T. spiralis infection, we further explored the role of elevated B1 cells in host immunity against infection. Accumulated evidence has demonstrated that Btk gene mutation or deficiency leads to profound B1 cell depletion and impaired immunoglobulin production [38]. In this study, Btk conditional knockout (CKO) mice, as a B1 cell-deficient model, were administered by oral gavage with 300 T. spiralis larvae and sacrificed at the muscle stage. Flow cytometry results confirmed significant B1 cell reduction in murine PC (Fig. 3A, B), spleen (Fig. 3E), mLN (Fig. 3F), and gLN (Fig. 3G) of Btk CKO mice, as well as a decreased B1a subgroup in PC (Fig. 3C), validating the reliability of this mouse model for B1 cell deficiency studies.

Consistent with our previous findings, T. spiralis infection resulted in elevated peritoneal B1 cells (Fig. 3B), along with an increased peritoneal B1b subgroup (Fig. 3D) in Btk CKO mice. However, peritoneal B1 cells (Fig. 3B), spleen (Fig. 3E), and gLN (Fig. 3G) were all decreased in infected Btk CKO mice compared to the infected WT controls. It is noteworthy that, in addition to natural IgM secretion, B1 cells actively produce class-switched antibodies, specifically IgG3, which provides immune protection against a broad spectrum of pathogens, including bacteria, fungi, viruses, and parasites [22, 39]. Our results indicated that the serum levels of total IgM, IgG, and IgG3 antibodies were reduced in the infected Btk CKO mice relative to the infected WT controls (Fig. 3H-J), along with impaired anti-T. spiralis specific IgM antibody (Fig. 3K). Functionally, impaired B1 cell-mediated immunity led to markedly increased muscle larval burdens in Btk CKO mice compared with control littermates (Fig. 3L). Taken together, these results indicate that B1 cell deficiency, caused by Btk ablation, compromises host humoral immune responses against T. spiralis infection via suppressing antibody production, thereby exacerbating parasitic infection at the muscle stage.

S100A4 deficiency reduces peritoneal and muscle-resident B1 cells in infected mice

To further explore the molecular mechanism underpinning the regulation of the B1 cells in T. spiralis infection, we performed transcriptomic profiling of peritoneal B1 cells from naive and infected mice. A total of 643 differentially expressed genes were identified, including 248 upregulated and 395 downregulated. Among the upregulated genes, S100A4 exhibited the prominent elevation in the infected peritoneal B1 cells (Fig. 4A and supplemental Table 1), which was confirmed by the mRNA expression result of S100a4 (Fig. 4B). Subsequent GO enrichment analysis showed that most DEGs enriched in process of immune response, signal transduction and protein binding (Fig. S4). Given that S100A4 is expressed in B1 cells and involved in cell development and differentiation [24], we utilized S100a4-knockout (KO) mice to investigate its regulatory role in B1 cell homeostasis and infection response. Under physiological conditions, S100A4 deficiency resulted in increased B1 cells in the spleen, mLN, and gLN (Fig. 4C, Fig. S5A), along with decreased TC B1 cells and no effect on peritoneal B1 cells (Fig. 4D, Fig. S5A). The results suggested that S100A4 is critically involved in tissue redistribution of B1 cells.

Next, we explored the impact of S100A4 deficiency on B1 cells in T. spiralis infection. Consistent with the previous results, T. spiralis infection led to elevated B1 cells in the murine PC and TC, especially enhanced peritoneal B1b cells in the WT group (Fig. 4E, F, Fig. S5B). Compared to the infected WT, the infected KO mice had reduced B1 cells in the PC, TC, and muscle tissues (Fig. 4E-G, Fig. S5B), accompanied by increased B1 cells in the spleen, mLN, and gLN (Fig. 4H-J). In vivo BrdU proliferation assays showed no difference in the proliferation of peritoneal B1 cells between the infected KO and WT mice, although the proliferation of B1b subgroup was slightly increased in the infected KO mice (Fig. 4K). In addition, no differences were found in the splenic FO, GC, and MZ cells between the infected KO and WT mice (Fig. 4L-N, Fig. S5C). Moreover, the infected KO mice had a higher muscular larvae burden than the infected control (Fig. 4O, P). Altogether, our data suggest that S100A4 deficiency impairs the accumulation of peritoneal and muscle-resident B1 cells, thereby attenuating B1 cell-mediated anti-helminthic immunity and exacerbating T. spiralis infection.

S100A4 deficiency inhibits BCR signaling activation in peritoneal B1 cells from infected mice

Since T. spiralis infection enhanced BCR signaling in peritoneal B1 cells, we then explored whether S100A4 affected BCR signaling in B1 cells. The result of flow cytometry detection showed no difference in expression of BTK, SYK and SHIP in peritoneal B1 cells between WT and KO mice with or without larval antigen stimulation (Fig. S6). Immunofluorescence analysis showed no difference in pY, pBTK and pSYK expression of peritoneal B1 cells between the WT and KO mice. However, following in vitro stimulation with T. spiralis larval antigens, expression levels of pY, pSYK, and pBTK were all decreased in the KO B1 cells relative to WT controls (Fig. 5A-C). Flow cytometric quantification further revealed a trend toward reduced mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of pY in KO B1 cells after infection, though the difference was not statistically significant (Fig. 5D, Fig. S7A).

The ERK pathway is closely associated with S100A4 and regulates cell motility and maturation [24, 40, 41]. We thus detected MFI of pERK in peritoneal B1 cells and found that S100A4 defect led to inhibited MFI of pERK in peritoneal B1 cells from the infected mice (Fig. 5E, Fig. S7A). To confirm the effect of S100A4 on BCR signaling, we performed rescue experiments via exogenous supplementation with the recombinant protein of S100A4. The results showed that S100A4 treatment significantly elevated expression levels of pY and pERK in peritoneal B1 cells from both WT and KO mice (Fig. 5D, E).

Given that wiskott-aldrich syndrome protein (WASP) is involved in B-cell diffusion and BCR signaling by controlling actin depolymerization and repolymerization [42], we further detected the expression of pWASP. The results showed that S100A4 deficiency resulted in decreased pWASP MFI in mouse peritoneal B1 cells under both naive and infected conditions (Fig. 5F, Fig. S7A). S100A4, as a member of S100 Ca2+-binding proteins, can regulate intracellular Ca2+ homeostasis, which is essential for BCR-mediated B cell activation [43]. We thus detected the intracellular Ca2+ level in peritoneal B1 cells upon BCR crosslinking. Anti-IgM stimulation triggered weaker intracellular Ca2+ mobilization in S100a4 KO B1 cells compared with WT counterparts during infection (Fig. S7B, Fig. 5G). Combined, these data demonstrate that S100A4 deficiency inhibits BCR signaling activation, along with decreased activation of downstream ERK and WASP signaling as well as impaired Ca2+ mobilization during T. spiralis infection.

S100A4 deficiency impairs IgM secretion by peritoneal B1 cells from the infected mice

Of note, B1 cells play a crucial role in infections through producing antibodies and secreting cytokines such as IL-10 [9, 22, 39]. Thus, we detected the effect of S100A4 loss on antibody and IL-10 production of peritoneal B1 cells with flow cytometry. There was no difference in the levels of IgG1 and IL-10 produced by peritoneal B1, B1a, and B1b cells between the infected KO and WT mice (Fig. 6A-D and J-L). In contrast, the level of IgM produced by the infected KO B1 cells was lower than that of the infected WT ones, as well as that of the infected KO B1a and B1b cells (Fig. 6F-H). For peritoneal B2 cells in infected mice, S100A4 deficiency resulted in a higher level of IgM production (Fig. 6I) with no effect on IgG1 and IL-10 secretion (Fig. 6E, M).

Consistent with previous results, both the total antibody and specific antibodies against T. spiralis of IgM and IgG were increased in the infected WT mice at the muscle stage. Compared with the control group, the level of total and T. spiralis-specific IgM in peritoneal lavage fluid from S100a4 KO mice was decreased, while no difference in the total and specific levels of IgG between the two infected groups (Fig. 6N, O). Discussion

During the past 30 years, considerable progress has been made since the discovery of B1 cells [44], which has promoted our understanding of their roles in various inflammatory diseases. B1 cells have been increasingly recognized as critical regulators of host immune defense against diverse pathogens [22]. Accumulating evidence indicates that B1 cells exert context-dependent protective or pathogenic effects during parasitic infections, varying substantially by pathogen species and infected tissues [45–50]. While the immunological roles of conventional B2 cells in T. spiralis infection have been well documented, the functional characteristics and regulatory mechanisms of B1 cells during trichinellosis remain poorly defined. In this study, we systematically delineated the dynamic changes, functional contributions, and upstream molecular regulation of peritoneal B1 cells during the muscle stage of T. spiralis infection, identifying a novel S100A4-mediated B1 cell regulatory function that mediates host anti-parasitic defense.

Consistent with the functional plasticity of B1 cells reported in other parasitic infection models [47, 50, 51], our results demonstrated unique tissue-specific expansion patterns of B1 cells during T. spiralis infection. We found that peritoneal and thoracic cavity B1 cells, particularly the B1b subset, were markedly expanded at the muscle stage, accompanied by increased muscle-resident B1 cells. Unlike the canonical inflammatory response in which body cavity B1 cells migrate to secondary lymphoid organs upon antigen stimulation [36], T. spiralis infection did not alter B1 cell frequencies in the spleen and lymph nodes. This distinct migration pattern differs from that observed in Trypanosoma cruzi and Schistosoma japonicum infections where peritoneal B1 cell reduction or lymphoid tissue redistribution dominates the immune response [52, 53]. Combined with unchanged proliferation and apoptosis of peritoneal B1 cells in infected mice, our findings suggest that local accumulation rather than de novo proliferation drives peritoneal B1 cell expansion, and these cells preferentially infiltrate damaged muscle tissues to execute local immune functions during the muscle stage of trichinellosis.

BCR signaling is the core determinant for B1 cell maintenance, activation and function [37]. Our study verified that T. spiralis infection significantly potentiates BCR signaling in peritoneal B1 cells, characterized by elevated activation of key upstream (pSYK, pBTK) and downstream (pERK, pWASP) signaling molecules, as well as enhanced intracellular Ca2+ mobilization. Enhanced BCR signaling is essential for sustaining the peritoneal B1 cell pool and improving their antigen responsiveness during infection [54, 55], which explains the stable expansion and functional activation of PC cells at the muscle stage.

To rule out potential TCR-derived signal interference [56], we purified T cell-free peritoneal B1 cells via flow cytometry for all in vitro assays. Of note, crude parasite antigens are complex mixtures that may initiate BCR activation via direct receptor ligation or indirect bystander effects, and the precise activating mechanism remains to be further clarified. B1 cells, different substantially from conventional B2 cells, are weakly responsive to acute anti-IgM stimulation and require persistent antigen exposure for full activation [23]. Accordingly, we assessed BCR signaling at 24 h post antigen treatment to recapitulate chronic antigen stimulation during ongoing T. spiralis infection. In addition, anti-Ig(M + G) (F(ab’)2) stimulation was included as a control, and no obvious alterations were observed in the total protein levels of core BCR signaling molecules, including BTK, SYK and SHIP. These findings further validate the specificity of antigen-triggered BCR signaling in peritoneal B1 cells.

Functional validation using Btk CKO mice further confirmed the protective role of B1 cells in anti-T. spiralis immunity. B1 cell deficiency significantly impaired the production of total and parasite-specific IgM and IgG3, consequently increasing muscle larval burden. Consistent with previous studies, B1 cell-derived antibodies are critical for pathogen clearance in parasitic and viral infections [57–59]. Although Btk ablation moderately affects B2 cell populations, previous literature has confirmed that B2 cells exert negligible effects on T. spiralis larval clearance [60], supporting that the impaired anti-parasitic immunity in Btk CKO mice is primarily attributed to B1 cell dysfunction.

Notably, our study further clarified the distinct functional characteristics of B1 cell subsets during trichinellosis. The CD5− B1b subset was preferentially expanded in the peritoneal cavity of infected mice, implying that B1b cells, rather than innate immune-dominant B1a cells, serve as the major functional B1 subset responding to T. spiralis antigen stimulation. This finding complements the current understanding of B1 cell subset division of labor in helminth infections, and provides a new subset-specific mechanism for B1 cell-mediated humoral immunity against parasites.

Mechanistically, our transcriptomic screening identified S100A4 as a key upstream regulator of B1 cell function during T. spiralis infection. As a Ca2+-binding protein involved in immune cell migration and inflammatory regulation [61], S100A4 expression was significantly upregulated in peritoneal B1 cells upon infection. Genetic ablation of S100A4 disrupted the redistribution of B1 cells in the tissue, blocking their accumulation in the peritoneal cavity and muscle tissues while promoting their aberrant aggregation in secondary lymphoid organs. Furthermore, S100A4 deficiency profoundly suppressed BCR signaling activation, Ca2+ flux, and downstream ERK/WASP signaling, specifically impairing IgM secretion by peritoneal B1 cells, without affecting IgG and IL-10 production. These results demonstrate that S100A4 maintains B1 cell-mediated anti-parasitic immunity by coordinating B1 cell tissue localization, BCR signaling activation, and specific antibody secretion. Consistent with previous reports highlighting the critical role of S100A4 in maintaining lymphocyte functional activity [62–64], our findings expand the immunological function of S100A4 to parasitic diseases and establish a novel S100A4-BCR-IgM regulatory axis in B1 cell responses against parasites.

Despite these novel findings, several limitations remain in the present study. First, although we confirmed the regulatory effect of S100A4 on B1 cell migration and BCR signaling, the direct molecular interaction between S100A4 and BCR signaling components still requires further exploration. Second, this study focused primarily on the regulatory mechanism of peritoneal B1 cells. It is not clear whether S100A4 regulates the function of muscle-resident B1 cells through a similar mechanism. Third, given the complex microenvironment of muscle infection, the potential crosstalk between B1 cells and other immune cells during T. spiralis infection warrants further investigation.

In summary, this study demonstrates that peritoneal B1 cells undergo unique local expansion and BCR-dependent functional activation during the muscle stage of T. spiralis infection, and these cells confer potent host protective immunity via IgM secretion. Mechanically, S100A4 acts as a critical upstream regulator to maintain B1 cell tissue distribution, BCR signaling activation and antibody production. Our findings reveal a previously unrecognized B1 cell immune regulatory mechanism mediated by S100A4 in trichinellosis, providing novel insights into host-parasite immune interaction and potential targets for the prevention and treatment of trichinellosis.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study is the first to explore the protective role of peritoneal B1 cells in T. spiralis infection, which relies on enhanced BCR signaling and antibody production to resist parasitic infection. Mechanistically, S100A4 modulates peritoneal B1 cell function through regulating their tissue redistribution, BCR signaling activation, and IgM antibody production at the muscle stage, thereby reinforcing host immunity against T. spiralis. These findings define a novel S100A4-dependent B1 cell regulatory axis in parasitic infection. Further exploration of B1 cell-mediated immune mechanisms will deepen the understanding of host-parasite immune crosstalk and provide potential therapeutic targets for immunological intervention in parasitic diseases.
