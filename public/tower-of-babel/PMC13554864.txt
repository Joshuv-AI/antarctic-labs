Anomalous aortic origin of a coronary artery (AAOCA) is an uncommon congenital coronary anomaly that has been associated with myocardial ischemia, ventricular arrhythmias, and sudden cardiac death. Although often recognized in younger populations, AAOCA may present for the first time in adulthood, frequently with nonspecific or exercise-related symptoms. Risk is highest when the anomalous vessel follows an interarterial or intramural course between the aorta and pulmonary artery. We present a case of an adult with previously normal functional status who developed exercise-related ventricular arrhythmias, ultimately leading to the diagnosis of high-risk AAOCA.

Clinical Presentation

A 43-year-old man presented with 2 months of intermittent dizziness and palpitations. He had been physically active throughout his life and denied any prior cardiac symptoms during childhood or adolescence. His initial episode occurred while walking and was associated with palpitations, mild chest tightness, and dizziness, without syncope. A subsequent episode occurred while standing, again with transient dizziness but no loss of consciousness. Concerned by the recurrent symptoms, he presented to the emergency department. On arrival, he was alert and hemodynamically stable, with a heart rate of 96 beats/min, blood pressure of 138/84 mm Hg, and oxygen saturation of 97% on room air. Cardiopulmonary examination was normal, without murmurs, gallops, or signs of heart failure.

Past Medical History

The patient has a history of hypertension, prediabetes, and asthma. Antihypertensive therapy was reviewed, including amlodipine and losartan, and no medication-related adverse effects were suspected.

Diagnostic Investigations

Electrocardiogram monitoring showed sinus rhythm with intermittent premature ventricular contractions but no sustained arrhythmia (Figure 1). Laboratory results, including electrolytes, thyroid function, and cardiac biomarkers, were within normal limits. Chest radiograph and baseline electrocardiogram were unremarkable. Given the resolution of symptoms and the absence of sustained arrhythmia or ischemic changes, he was discharged with plans for outpatient exercise stress testing given the exertional nature of his symptoms.

Electrocardiography Showing Sinus Rhythm With Intermittent Premature Ventricular Contractions

During an exercise stress test, he exercised for over 6 minutes without symptoms, but in recovery, he developed frequent premature ventricular contractions that escalated to nonsustained monomorphic ventricular tachycardia (Figure 2). He was stabilized with intravenous metoprolol and transferred to a tertiary center. His coronary computed tomography angiography (CCTA; Figure 3, Video 1) showed an anomalous origin of the right coronary artery (RCA) from the left sinotubular junction above the left coronary cusp, adjacent to the left coronary artery ostium. The RCA took an intramural intraarterial course with a slit-like opening and an acute angle takeoff. Invasive coronary angiography confirmed the anomalous origin and course of the RCA without evidence of obstructive coronary artery disease. Both ventricles were normal in size and systolic function, with a left ventricular ejection fraction of 57% and a right ventricular ejection fraction of 49%. Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging demonstrated no myocardial scar and only mild basal septal thickening without left ventricular outflow tract obstruction, reducing concern for an alternative arrhythmogenic substrate.

Management

Given the presence of high-risk coronary anatomy and exercise-related ventricular arrhythmias, the patient underwent surgical unroofing of the anomalous RCA. Postoperative recovery was uncomplicated, and he was discharged on aspirin, a beta-blocker, losartan, and empagliflozin, with a wearable cardioverter-defibrillator for interim arrhythmia protection.

Outcome and Follow-Up

At 2-week follow-up, he reported improved exercise tolerance and energy, with no recurrent dizziness, syncope, or palpitations.

Discussion

AAOCA is a congenital anomaly associated with sudden cardiac death, most often mediated by transient myocardial ischemia and malignant ventricular arrhythmias rather than fixed coronary obstruction. Although it is classically identified in younger populations, adult presentation may be subtle and nonspecific, contributing to delayed or missed diagnosis. This case is notable for an otherwise healthy adult who presented with exercise-related ventricular arrhythmias in the absence of prior symptoms or structural heart disease, ultimately leading to the diagnosis of high-risk anomalous right-coronary-artery anatomy.1

Prior case reports have described adult presentations of anomalous RCA identified during evaluation for ischemic chest pain requiring invasive angiography or following cardiac arrest.2,3 Such reports underscore the malignant potential of high-risk coronary anatomy but typically involve overt ischemic syndromes or catastrophic arrhythmic events. In contrast, the present case illustrates a more subtle, arrhythmia-driven presentation, with nonsustained ventricular tachycardia occurring during the recovery phase of exercise stress testing. This distinction is clinically important, as recovery-phase ventricular arrhythmias are frequently dismissed as nonspecific, particularly in patients without known structural heart disease.

The dynamic nature of coronary flow limitation in AAOCA contributes to the diagnostic challenge in adult presentations. Transient ischemia may occur only under specific physiologic conditions and may not coincide with maximal oxygen demand, limiting the sensitivity of conventional functional testing. In this case, ventricular ectopy progressed to nonsustained ventricular tachycardia during recovery despite the absence of ischemic electrocardiographic changes at peak exercise, a pattern that may reflect ischemia-related electrical instability rather than benign ectopy. Normal transthoracic echocardiography and cardiac magnetic resonance imaging further underscore the importance of anatomical evaluation in patients with unexplained exercise-related arrhythmias.1

CCTA plays a central role in risk stratification by allowing precise delineation of coronary origin, course, and ostial morphology. Although anomalous left coronary artery anatomy has traditionally been considered higher risk, particularly in younger populations, CCTA enables identification of high-risk features in anomalous RCA variants that are not necessarily benign. High-risk anatomical characteristics such as an interarterial course, intramural proximal segment, slit-like ostium, and acute takeoff angle may result in dynamic luminal narrowing during exertion or changes in vascular tone, predisposing to transient ischemia and ventricular arrhythmias.4,5 Although the anomalous origin of the left coronary artery has been more frequently associated with sudden cardiac death, anomalous RCA variants have also been implicated in fatal events, particularly in the presence of high-risk anatomical features. This distinction is clinically important, as anomalous RCA is often perceived as lower risk, which may contribute to delayed recognition and underestimation of clinical significance in adults.6 These findings also highlight the importance of multimodality imaging in the evaluation of AAOCA. While CCTA provides superior delineation of coronary origin and high-risk anatomical features, stress imaging, cardiac magnetic resonance imaging, and invasive angiography provide complementary functional and confirmatory information that may guide risk stratification and management decisions.7

The pathophysiology underlying malignant arrhythmias in AAOCA is thought to relate to dynamic coronary compression rather than permanent myocardial injury. Compression of an intramural or interarterial coronary segment between the great vessels can occur during exercise, increased cardiac output, or changes in intrathoracic pressure. Brief, reversible ischemia may be sufficient to induce electrical instability without producing myocardial scar, explaining why many patients, including the present case, demonstrate preserved ventricular function and normal findings on cardiac magnetic resonance imaging despite clinically significant arrhythmic events.4,5 Autonomic shifts during recovery from exercise, including abrupt sympathetic withdrawal and relative parasympathetic predominance, may further exacerbate transient flow limitation and myocardial repolarization abnormalities, providing a plausible mechanism for recovery-phase ventricular arrhythmias as an early warning sign of clinically significant disease.8

Current guidelines and expert consensus support surgical intervention in symptomatic patients with AAOCA and high-risk anatomical features, including interarterial and intramural coronary courses.9 In the present case, the combination of exercise-related ventricular arrhythmias and high-risk anatomy identified on CCTA supported the decision to pursue surgical unroofing despite preserved ventricular function and absence of myocardial scar. The patient's favorable postoperative course reinforces the importance of early recognition and anatomy-driven management in preventing progression to more severe arrhythmic events or sudden cardiac death.10

Conclusions

This case highlights AAOCA as an important and potentially underrecognized cause of exercise-related ventricular arrhythmias in adults. Ventricular arrhythmias occurring during recovery from stress testing should prompt careful anatomical evaluation, particularly with CCTA. Early recognition of high-risk anatomy can guide timely surgical intervention and lead to favorable clinical outcomes.

Coronary computed tomography angiography revealed anomalous origin of the right coronary artery from the left sinotubular junction with an intramural, interarterial course and slit-like ostium.
