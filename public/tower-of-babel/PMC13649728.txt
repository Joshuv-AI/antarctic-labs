Comparative evaluation of methanolic extracts from black pepper varieties: yield, GC-MS characterization, and antioxidant and antibacterial activities

Shaimaa A M Abdelmohsen, Arooj Anees, Mohsin Javed, Sana Mansoor, Misbah Umar, Urooj Fatima, Zafar Iqbal, Muhammad Faizan, Chi-Hsien Liu, Syed Kashif Ali, Shahid Iqbal, Sajid Mahmood, Meznah M Alanazi

Abstract

One of the most commonly used culinary spices, black pepper (Piper nigrum L.) is also a rich source of bioactive phytochemicals. The present study aims to investigate the oleoresin extracted from black and white pepper using a Soxhlet extraction system and describe its physicochemical characterization, GC-MS analysis, and antioxidant and antibacterial activity evaluation. The antioxidant capacity was determined by the 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) free radical scavenging assay, and the antibacterial activity was determined by the agar well diffusion method. The extract had the highest antibacterial activity against Gram-positive Bacillus subtilis, with zones of 20 mm and 16 mm for black and white pepper extracts, respectively. The antioxidant activity of both extracts was found to be high, with DPPH radical scavenging activities of 86.80% for black pepper extract and 87.62% for white pepper extract. The oleoresin yield was 4.2% for black pepper and 3.2% for white pepper. GC–MS analysis showed a variety of phytochemical constituents such as alkaloids, terpenoids, fatty acids, and sterols. Major alkaloids of black pepper were found as piperine (8.54%) and yohimbine (12.27%), while the sterols were the most abundant class of compounds present in white pepper (28.67%). The total alkaloids present in the oleoresins of black pepper and white pepper were approximately 35%. This indicated that the oleoresins may serve as a natural source of biologically active compounds.

Introduction

The blooming vine that is popularly referred to as black pepper is a member of the Piperaceae family (; ). Of the 1,000 species in the Piperaceae family, P. nigrum L. is one of the most significant cultivated species because of its economic significance (). Pepper is frequently used in curry masalas and is a key ingredient in Ayurvedic, folk, and traditional medicine treatments (). The presence of piperine, including its many isomers, makes Piper nigrum L. extremely valuable (). Due to its many therapeutic benefits, P. nigrum is frequently used in Indian, Latin American, and West Indies medicine (). Black pepper can be used as a biocontrol agent, a medication, a preservative, as well as in human diets (; ). In addition to its nutritional benefits, black pepper’s physiological actions make it a useful agent for preventing chronic illnesses (). Black pepper is also used for its antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, anti-cancer, and gastroprotective properties ().

The main components that give white and black pepper their spicy flavor are oleoresin, piperine, and essential oil (). One of the pepper’s main bioactive ingredients, piperine, is a nitrogen-containing molecule that gives pepper its pungency (). Piperine can be sensitive to light and oxygen (). The Piperaceae family contains a wide range of piperine concentrations. For instance, black pepper has been reported to contain approximately 2.0% and 7.4% piperine, respectively, while white pepper has been discovered to contain up to 9.0% piperine (; ). Overall, pepper’s worth is determined by its flavor and pungency, which are typically ascribed to the presence of oleoresin, the piperine alkaloid, and the essential oils ().

One of pepper’s primary ingredients, pepper oleoresin, is an oily material that is extracted from pepper powder using organic solvents. Pepper oleoresin is a dark green, viscous liquid that tastes and smells peppery. It tastes harsh and spicy with a touch of clove and lemon. Its primary constituents are piperine and volatile oil, which contain pepper’s strong stimulant compounds and other fragrant components (). Because of its unique aroma, pepper oleoresin is used in a wide range of dishes, such as meats, sauces, chutneys, soups, and snacks. Piper oleoresin’s widespread usage in the food processing sector is made possible by its convenient storage and mobility. The primary pungent component of pepper oleoresin, piperine, has been shown in several studies to offer functional health advantages. According to the reports, piperine has anti-inflammatory, anti-cancer, antibacterial, and anti-diabetic effects (; ; ; ). Additionally, piperine has been shown to have a strong inhibitory effect on the severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (COVID-19) because of its high affinity for the RNA-binding site (). The majority of expensive, highly processed goods, such as pepper essential oil, pepper oleoresin, and pepper capsules, are imported into Pakistan, whereas China mostly processes and manufactures primary pepper products (; ). The chosen technique and solvent can be used to extract these advantageous chemicals.

Subcritical water extraction (), continuous subcritical water extraction (; ; ), traditional solvent extraction (; ; ), supercritical fluid extraction (; ), and hydrotropic extraction are some of the several extraction methods that have been previously documented. According to other research, of five distinct extraction techniques, the traditional Soxhlet method produces the best overall results (). Thus, in this investigation, Soxhlet extraction using dry and ground sediment was selected. It is well known that different types of extractants have varying reactions and capacities to draw out the bioactive substances found in food samples. Pepper can be extracted using organic solvents such as ethanol, acetone, or chlorinated hydrocarbons (), dichloromethane (), petroleum ether and chloroform (), a combination of solvents such as n-hexane, ethyl acetate, and glacial acetic acid, as well as ethanol and water (), according to earlier publications. Because the various solvents have distinct effects and produce different outcomes, the findings from these experiments varied. This study aims to ascertain the physicochemical, antibacterial, and antioxidant properties of pepper oleoresin extracted from black pepper.

Although numerous studies have investigated black pepper constituents, direct comparative evaluation of methanolic oleoresin extracts from black and white pepper varieties with respect to extraction yield, GC-MS chemical profiling, antioxidant activity, antibacterial activity, and physicochemical characteristics remains limited. The aim of the present study was to compare these parameters on a comprehensive basis using a single extraction method, offering valuable information about the potential application of pepper oleoresins in food and pharmaceutical fields.

Experimental work

Soxhlet extract preparation

Approximately 500 g of the dried black pepper was taken and crushed into a ground form. Then, approximately 1000 mL of methanol was added as a solvent. Following this, the extract was separated using a funnel and a Soxhlet extraction apparatus. Filtration was done using Whatman filter paper No. 41. Following distillation, an extract was made () and continued for additional examination, which we assessed quantitatively for antibacterial and antioxidant properties and qualitatively using several assays.

Antioxidant activity

Using a UV-visible spectrophotometer to monitor 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) solution with methanol is a very accurate, straightforward, and efficient method. Because of absorption by an oxidant, the hue changed from purple to yellow. The capacity of the stable free radical DPPH to donate hydrogen was used to measure antioxidant activity. The antioxidant activity of methanolic extract of Piper nigrum was determined using the DPPH radical UV-Visible spectrophotometer (). A UV-Visible spectrophotometer (Model 1700, Shimadzu, Japan) was the device used. Methanol was used as the solvent, and DPPH was used as a solute to create the DPPH solution, which was prepared by dissolving 4 mg of DPPH in 100 mL of methanol. The solution concentration was approximately 0.10 mM. The mixture was wrapped in aluminum foil. Various concentrations of 10 μm, 30 μm, and 50 μm were prepared using the sample. Next, in separate test tubes, 3 mL of the DPPH:methanol solution was combined with the various sample concentrations. Because there were no antioxidants present, the blank had the maximum absorbance of 0.982. Following this, a UV–visible spectrophotometer was used at room temperature in a light-protected location to measure the absorbance of the blank and the succeeding solutions at 517 nm. The accompanying condition was used to calculate the % inhibition.

Antibacterial activity of varieties of Piper nigrum

Various bacterial strains, including Gram-negative bacteria such as Escherichia coli and Gram-positive bacteria such as Staphylococcus aureus and Bacillus subtilis, were tested for antibacterial activity. In Petri dishes containing a sample of P. nigrum methanolic extract, imipenem (0.1%) was employed as the standard against bacterial strains. These strains of bacteria were gathered at the Lahore laboratory. The disc diffusion method was used to determine antibacterial activity. Petri plates were coated with a nutritious agar mixture and allowed to cool. Wells of 6 mm diameter were made in nutrient agar plates and filled with the respective extracts. Then, the bacterial suspensions of Gram-negative bacteria such as E. coli and Gram-positive bacteria such as S. aureus and B. subtilis were introduced to the Petri plates. After that, these were incubated for a 24 h period at 37 °C to test for bacterial strains (Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines). When comparing different types of P. nigrum extract, the zone of inhibition was calculated by measuring the disc’s diameter in millimeters from the disc plate.

GC–MS analysis of black and white pepper methanolic extract

The investigation of oleoresin was performed on a gas chromatograph (GC-14A) combined with a mass spectrometer for information preparation. A PEG narrow-bore column (25 m × 0.2 mm) was utilized for oleoresins. The injector and detector temperatures were maintained at 150 °C and 250 °C, respectively. The flow rate of the carrier gas (helium) was 1.5 mL/min, and the split proportion of the segments was based on their retention time compared to those obtained from oleoresins analyzed under similar conditions. Electron ionization was used at 70 eV, and the scan range was 30–550 m/z. The temperature of the MS transfer line was adjusted to 200 °C, and the injector temperature was maintained at 180°. The retention time of unknown compounds was identified by comparison, and further interpretations were based on the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) library system.

Proximate analysis

The moisture, crude protein, ash, crude fiber, crude fat, and carbohydrate contents of black and white pepper samples were assessed using the conventional Association of Official Analytical Chemists (AOAC) methodologies. All assays were conducted in triplicate, and findings are presented as % composition based on the dry weight.

Statistical analysis

Every experiment was carried out three times. The mean ± standard deviation is used to represent the results. Data were analyzed using Microsoft Excel. DPPH scavenging equation and measurement of the zone of inhibition (mm) were used to calculate the antioxidant activity and antibacterial activity, respectively.

Results and discussion

Chemical analysis of methanolic extracts of black pepper

The methanolic extract of Piper nigrum was subjected to qualitative phytochemical screening, and the alkaloids, glycosides, terpenoids, saponins, and tannins were found (). The occurrence of these secondary metabolites indicates that methanol is a good solvent for bioactive compounds, which are responsible for the medicinal properties of black pepper. The biological activities observed in the present study can be attributed to these phytochemicals, which have been reported to possess antimicrobial, antioxidant, and anti-inflammatory properties. The results are in accordance with previous reports that identified alkaloids, tannins, saponins, terpenoids, and glycosides in methanolic extract of P. nigrum and attributed the therapeutic potential to these bioactive constituents (; ). Thus, the phytochemical profile of this study confirmed the traditional use of black pepper as a medicinal plant and its potential as a source of natural therapeutic agents.

The chromatogram () showed the presence of several phytochemical compounds that were identified by comparing the retention time and mass spectra with reference compounds present in the NIST mass spectrum library. GC–MS analysis revealed that black pepper oleoresin contains a diverse range of bioactive phytochemicals, predominantly alkaloids, terpenoids, fatty acids, and alcohol derivatives. Piperine (8.54%), (E)-9-(Benzo[d] dioxol-5-yl)-(pyrrolidin-1-yl)non-8-en-1-one (7.69%), and ethchlorvynol (7.21%) were identified as major constituents (; ). Monoterpenes (4.63%), oleic acid (4.61%), and sesquiterpenes (4.26%) were found in relatively high concentrations, while caryophyllene oxide and the other fatty acids were present in relatively lower concentrations. Additionally, n-acyl amines and benzyl ether derivatives were also detected. The high percentage of alkaloids, especially piperine, is consistent with the results of the previous studies, which have identified piperine as the main bioactive compound with many of the pharmacological properties of black pepper, such as antioxidant and antimicrobial activities and contributing to the characteristic pungent flavor of black pepper (; Ravindran, 2012).

GC–MS analysis of white pepper oil

The chemical composition of white pepper oleoresin was characterized by gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS). The chromatogram () showed several phytochemical components that were identified based on their retention time and mass spectra matching with the NIST mass spectrum library of reference compounds.

The GC–MS analysis revealed that white pepper oleoresin is rich in various bioactive compounds, mainly alkaloids, sterols, and hydrocarbons. The alkaloids and the sterols were the major chemical classes of the extract, represented by the major constituents identified: yohimbine (12.27%) and cholesta-3,5-diene (28.67%) (). A relatively high level of octadecanenitrile was also found (10.28%), and caffeine, a purine alkaloid, was found at 2.91%. Very small quantities (0.12%) of dodecane (1,1′-oxybis-) were found. Sterols and alkaloids are predominant; they indicate that white pepper has high biological potential (; ).

Determination of the antioxidant activity of Piper nigrum

Piper nigrum has been used medically to treat many diseases for thousands of years. It reduces the formation of oxidative species by acting as a natural antioxidant. Antioxidant activity was determined using DPPH, a stable free radical. Both synthetic and natural antioxidants convert DPPH free radicals into the yellow diphenylpicrylhydrazine. In this work, antioxidant activity was determined for the extract obtained from P. nigrum varieties by using DPPH (). Methanolic black and white pepper extracts indicated the strongest cancer-preventive agent action of 86.80% and 87.62% at 50 μg/mL. As the concentration increased to 50 μg/mL, the percentage inhibition also increased. The strong antioxidant activity observed in both extracts may be attributed to piperine, phenolic compounds, and terpenoid constituents. Similar antioxidant activities of P. nigrum extracts have been reported by and , who demonstrated significant radical scavenging activity associated with that of the piperine-rich extracts.

Antibacterial activity of varieties of Piper nigrum

Dried varieties of Piper nigrum extract were tested by the disc diffusion method against three strains of bacteria: Gram-positive (+ve) Bacillus subtilis and Staphylococcus aureus and Gram-negative (−ve) Escherichia coli. Methanolic extract of P. nigrum indicated high antibacterial activity. Standard imipenem showed antibacterial activity in the presence of methanolic extract of varieties of P. nigrum. The temperature required for antibacterial activity was 37 °C. The higher antibacterial activity observed against Bacillus subtilis may be associated with the disruption of bacterial membrane integrity by alkaloids and terpenoid compounds present in extracts. Meanwhile, Staphylococcus aureus showed the minimum zone of inhibition. Both black and white pepper extracts contain phenolic contents, but it’s comparatively higher in black pepper extract. The sugars readily dissolve in the moisture of the agar gel, and the phenolic hydroxyl (-OH) groups in the pepper oil bond with the methanol solvent, preventing the active antibacterial compounds from properly diffusing into this aqueous sugar matrix.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that methanolic oleoresins of both the black and white pepper are rich sources of biologically active phytochemicals with promising antioxidant and antibacterial properties. The oleoresin content of black pepper was higher than that of white pepper. The GC–MS analysis revealed the presence of a variety of bioactive compounds such as alkaloids, terpenoids, sterols, and fatty acids. The major alkaloids in black pepper were found to be piperine, and the major constituents of white pepper were sterols. The extracts had high DPPH radical scavenging activity (>86%) and significant antibacterial activity, especially against Bacillus subtilis, suggesting that the extracts can be used as natural antioxidant and antimicrobial agents. The results confirm the traditional uses of Piper nigrum for its medicinal properties and suggest its use in the pharmaceutical, food, and nutraceutical sectors. Further investigations should be conducted involving isolation of individual bioactive compounds and in vivo investigations to better understand their therapeutic efficacy and mechanisms of action.
