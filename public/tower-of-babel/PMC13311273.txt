Social expectations in early-stage psychosis: Less stereotypical facial expression predictions
Authors: Tijana Tomic, Irene Ramos-Grille, Eva Cortés-Velasco, Neus Barrantes-Vidal, Lorena Chanes
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Psychosis is conceptualized as existing along a continuum, with schizotypal traits and psychotic-like experiences observed in the general population and schizophrenia at its extreme. Predictive processing has recently been applied to the understanding of psychotic experiences, suggesting that symptoms may arise from disruptions at different levels in the processing hierarchy. We studied facial expression expectations and their impact on social perception in a group of 34 individuals with early-stage psychosis (<5 years from a first psychotic episode) and 34 control individuals. Individuals with psychosis exhibited less stereotypical expectations about facial expressions. Similar results were observed in exploratory analyses considering dimensional schizotypy scores within nonclinical participants. These findings suggest that psychosis may involve the development of less stereotypical, more nuanced, individualized predictions, possibly as a result of an overly prioritization of prediction errors, leading to a world that is hard to predict and may seem uncertain, magical and/or too complex to extract general (possibly stereotyped) rules. These results also support the continuum hypothesis of psychosis by highlighting shared phenomena across early-stage psychosis and schizotypy. These insights into predictive processing and less stereotypical social evaluations offer valuable opportunities for improving early detection and developing targeted interventions for social functioning and mental health in psychosis.

Introduction

Predictive processing views of psychosis have mainly focused on positive symptoms, notably hallucinations and delusions. According to predictive processing, the brain generates predictions about the world based on past experiences (priors), aiming to minimize the difference between such predictions and incoming sensory evidence (prediction errors) to explain the causes of such evidence. Early studies explored how altered priors and prediction errors might underlie these symptoms. Some suggested shifts in the type of prior knowledge used in perception (Davies et al., 2018; Teufel et al., 2015), while others debated whether priors were generally stronger (i.e., greater influence on perceptual outcome) or weaker (i.e., less influential on perceptual outcome) in psychosis (Sterzer et al., 2018). More recent works attempted to reconcile these findings by emphasizing the hierarchical levels. In this hierarchical view, high-level priors operate at more abstract, conceptual levels (e.g., beliefs about the identity or intentions of others), whereas low-level priors operate closer to the level of sensory input (e.g., expectations about basic perceptual features). Positive symptoms may result from stronger high-level priors with excessive precision, weaker low-level priors with reduced precision, and stronger low-level prediction errors due to increased precision at low-level sensory levels (Sterzer et al., 2018; Corlett et al., 2019; Harding et al., 2024; Heinz et al., 2019; Keller and Sterzer, 2024; Petrovic and Sterzer, 2023; Reeder et al., 2024; Sheffield et al., 2024; Stuke et al., 2021; Bott et al., 2024). More specifically, with regards to hallucinations, weak low-level priors may lead to perceptual uncertainties, which are then compensated for by strong high-level priors, resulting in an overreliance on top-down signaling in sensory cortices, thus facilitating hallucinations (Sterzer et al., 2018; Corlett et al., 2019; Keller and Sterzer, 2024; Reeder et al., 2024; Powers et al., 2016). Similarly, delusions may arise from the compensation of weak low-level priors with strong high-level priors in an effort to minimize surprise and account for excessive prediction error processing (Sterzer et al., 2018; Corlett et al., 2019; Harding et al., 2024; Heinz et al., 2019; Keller and Sterzer, 2024; Petrovic and Sterzer, 2023; Reeder et al., 2024; Sheffield et al., 2024). Furthermore, the nature and direction of delusional beliefs may evolve over time. In early stages of psychosis, an overweighting of prediction errors may generate an uncertain, threatening world, leading to a “delusional mood” in later stages, as a compensatory process, due to reduced confidence in sensory input, hindering the ability to effectively update based on relevant prediction errors (as discussed in (Petrovic and Sterzer, 2023; Griffin and Fletcher, 2017). Thus, weak low-level perceptual priors, or an inability to adequately attenuate prediction errors, may contribute to the development of delusional beliefs, while stronger priors operating at higher hierarchical levels shape perception, leading to hallucinations and the maintenance of those delusional beliefs. The commonly proposed underlying predictive processing mechanisms and the strong connections between hallucinations and delusions as observed clinically (e.g., certain types of hallucinations tend to co-occur more frequently with specific delusions (Corlett et al., 2019)), support the notion that they represent different labels for the same underlying experiences (Fletcher and Frith, 2009), “two sides of the same coin” (Notredame et al., 2014). Consistent with these proposals, recent empirical work has provided supporting evidence, including overweighted high-level semantic priors in schizotypy associated with increased task-based hallucinations (Knolle et al., 2026), altered prediction error signaling in the daily life of individuals with psychotic-like experiences (Misiak et al., 2026), increased sensory precision associated with hallucination- and delusion-like experiences (Scaramozzino et al., 2025), altered perception of environmental volatility during social learning in early psychosis (Hauke et al., 2024), and neural signatures of abnormal top-down and bottom-up signaling in schizophrenia (Alamia et al., 2025).

Despite a growing interest in predictive processing models of psychosis and key perceptual disruptions in the social domain (e.g., (Eriksdotter et al., 2023; Green et al., 2015; Pinkham, 2014) for schizophrenia; (Healey et al., 2016) for early-onset psychosis; (Mondragón-Maya et al., 2017) for schizophrenia and ultra-high risk for psychosis), studies of facial expression perception and social evaluation in psychosis from a predictive processing approach remain scarce. Moreover, within social cognition, facial expression perception has been studied primarily from a basic emotion perspective, which posits that emotions are biologically hardwired and expressed through specific, recognizable facial patterns that are universally identifiable across cultures (Ekman, 1992; Ekman and Friesen, 1971; Tracy and Randles, 2011). In contrast to basic emotion theory, constructionist approaches to emotion argue that emotions are not fixed, universal states but are instead shaped by personal experiences and cultural aspects (Barrett, 2017). We sought to investigate facial expression predictions and social evaluation in early-stage psychosis, using a behavioral paradigm that allows us to study the stereotypicality of predictions rather than assuming correct or incorrect answers about emotion labeling. This paradigm has been extensively tested in healthy populations (Chanes et al., 2018; Draganov et al., 2023), and mental-health related conditions such as depression (Ramos-Grille et al., 2022) and autism (Robles et al., 2024), showing condition-specific results. To address both categorical and dimensional aspects of psychosis vulnerability, we combined a case–control comparison with a dimensional analysis of schizotypy within the control group. We hypothesized that individuals with early-stage psychosis (vs. controls) would exhibit stronger prediction error in a social task, reflected by overall lower predictability ratings for facial expressions, possibly interacting with stereotypicality. Furthermore, consistent with a dimensional view where psychosis is conceptualized as a dynamic continuum in which schizophrenia stands as the most severe extreme of a more broadly distributed expression of psychosis liability such as schizotypy traits and psychotic-like experiences in the general population (Barch et al., 2013; Barrantes-Vidal et al., 2015; Debbane et al., 2015; Guloksuz and van Os, 2018; Kwapil and Barrantes-Vidal, 2015), we expected such effects to be observed in exploratory analyses when considering dimensional schizotypy scores within nonclinical participants and not only when comparing patient vs. control groups.

Materials and method

Participants

A total of 68 adult participants were recruited for the present study (Table 1). Thirty-four individuals (16 females and 18 males), aged 19 to 37 (Mean ± SE: 27 ± 6.01) years, having had one or more psychotic episodes, integrated the psychosis group. Sample size was based on prior studies with clinical populations and comparable designs (Ramos-Grille et al., 2022; Robles et al., 2024), proving sufficient to show significant effects across clinical and control groups. They were recruited from the Centre de Salut Mental d'Adults de Terrassa (n = 32) and the Fundació Sanitària Sant Pere Claver (n = 2). Inclusion criteria were as follows: participants had to have presented psychotic symptoms, whether their diagnosis was Bipolar I Disorder or a diagnosis in the Schizophrenia Spectrum; participants had to be within the critical period of five years since their first psychotic episode, the psychotic episode(s) were not explained by any organic cause and no prior history of brain injury, age ≥ 18 years old, native Spanish or bilingual Catalan-Spanish speaker, and normal or corrected-to-normal vision. The diagnosis was made by clinical experts at the center according to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders fourth edition – text revised (DSM-IV-TR; (Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, 2000) diagnostic criteria using the Structured Clinical Interview for DSM-IV Axis I Disorders (SCID-ID; (First et al., 2002). The diagnostic criteria for Bipolar I Disorder or Schizophrenia Spectrum and Other Psychotic Disorders are similar across the DSM-5 and DSM-IV-TR. Thirty-four healthy individuals reporting no history of mental disorder, matched in gender and approximate age with the individuals of the psychosis group, integrated the control group (6 participants were specifically recruited for this study; 27 participants were recruited as controls for a different study including the same task (Robles et al., 2024); and one participant was recruited for another ongoing study also including the same task). Exclusion criteria for the control group included having been diagnosed with a psychiatric or neurological condition, taking psychoactive medication, having history of brain injury or other neurological conditions, not having normal or corrected-to-normal vision, and not being a native Spanish speaker or bilingual Catalan-Spanish. The group consisted of 16 females and 18 males, aged between 16 and 42 (Mean ± SE: 27.8 ± 6.0) years. Participants' age did not differ significantly across groups (Mann Whitney two-tailed U test, U = 602.50, p = 0.77, rB = 0.04, 95% CI [− 0.23, 0.31]). Participation in the study was voluntary, with written informed consent obtained, and no monetary compensation provided for participating. The study was approved by the Universitat Autònoma de Barcelona's or the Consorci Sanitari de Terrassa's Ethics Committees as relevant depending on participants.

Experimental procedure

The experimental session for the psychosis group took place at a healthcare center (Centre de Salut Mental d'Adults de Terrassa or Fundació Sanitària Sant Pere Claver). The experimental session for the control group took place at either a healthcare center (Fundació Sanitària Sant Pere Claver or Institut Global d'Atenció Integral del Neurodesenvolupament) or at the university (Cognitive and Affective Science Laboratory, Universitat Autònoma de Barcelona). Demographic information, such as age, gender, dominant hand, and educational background was collected from all participants. Information regarding the first psychotic episode, diagnoses, and treatment/medication for the psychosis group were retrieved from medical records. All individuals were given a brief description of the study and asked to provide written informed consent. Individuals were reminded that their participation was voluntary and that they could leave the study at any time. Then, participants completed a facial expression expectations and social perception task and filled out a series of questionnaires.

Facial expression expectations and social perception task

Participants performed a ~ 40-min task on a computer ((Chanes et al., 2018); Fig. 1). Visual stimuli were presented on an HP ProBook 640 G4 laptop with a 14-in. display and a resolution of 1920 × 1080 pixels, using the E-Prime 3 software (Psychology Software Tools, Inc., Pittsburgh, PA, USA). Each trial began with a black fixation screen lasting 4 s. Then, a photograph of a target individual displaying a neutral facial expression appeared at the center of the screen for 5 s. We used color photographs (400 × 600 pixels) of human faces with closed mouths and a direct gaze, which belong to the Interdisciplinary Affective Science Laboratory (www.affective-science.org). Then, a text (scenario) was presented in white font for a duration of 20 s. Scenarios (Draganov et al., 2023; Ramos-Grille et al., 2022; Robles et al., 2024) described situations involving the target individual and were designed to evoke one of three emotions: fear, happiness, or sadness. Participants were instructed to imagine, while reading, how the target individual would look in the described scenario. Following the scenario, a second photograph of the same target individual was displayed for 5 s. This time, the photograph depicted a stereotypical facial expression corresponding to one of the three possible evoked emotions: fear, happiness, or sadness. Participants were asked to rate, using a 4-point scale, how closely the appearance of the target individual matched what they had imagined while reading the scenario (predictability rating; from 1, indicating “not at all similar,” to 4, indicating “very similar”). Finally, participants were asked to rate how likable they found the target individual to be (likability rating; from 1, indicating “very unlikable,” to 4, indicating “very likable”). After each block, participants were allowed to take a brief break. The task included written instructions, followed by 3 practice trials and 45 experimental trials (divided into 5 blocks, each containing 9 trials). The 45 experimental trials included 15 scenarios evoking fear, 15 scenarios evoking happiness, and 15 scenarios evoking sadness. Out of the 15 scenarios designed to evoke a specific emotion, 9 were followed by the corresponding stereotypical facial expression (matched trials). Six scenarios were followed by a stereotypical facial expression for a different emotion than the one evoked by the scenario, with 3 scenarios for each of the two remaining emotion categories (nonmatched trials). Questionnaires

The psychosis group completed the Positive and Negative Affect Schedule (PANAS; (Watson et al., 1988); Spanish version used: (Sandín et al., 1999), Emotion Regulation Questionnaire (ERQ; (Gross and John, 2003); Spanish version used: (Cabello et al., 2013), Impulsive behavioral scale (UPPS-P; (Lynam et al., 2006); Spanish version used: (Cándido et al., 2012), the Multidimensional Schizotypy Scale-Brief (MSS-B; (Gross et al., 2018) and the suspiciousness and ideas of reference subscales of the Schizotypal Personality Questionnaire (SPQ; (Raine, 1991); Spanish version used: (Fonseca-Pedrero et al., 2009). The control group completed the same questionnaires plus additional ones not used in this study.

Multidimensional Schizotypy Scale-Brief (MSS-B)

The MSS-B is designed to capture the multidimensional structure of schizotypy and assesses three distinct dimensions: the positive or psychotic-like symptom dimension, the negative or deficit dimension, and the cognitive-behavioral disorganization dimension. The scale contains three subscales representing each of these dimensions: positive (MSSB-P), negative (MSSB-N), and disorganized (MSSB-D). The MSSB-P encompasses disruptions in thought content, perceptual oddities, and suspiciousness. The MSSB-N involves diminished experiences and expressions, such as anhedonia and social withdrawal. The MSSB-D relates to disturbances in organizing thoughts and behaviors.

Schizotypal Personality Questionnaire (SPQ)

The SPQ is a self-reported scale designed to evaluate the full spectrum of schizotypal personality traits. Participants respond to each item on a “yes/no” scale, with all “yes” responses scoring 1 point each. These scores are then summed to reflect the intensity of the trait being assessed. The SPQ comprises 9 subscales targeting different aspects of schizotypal personality traits, many overlapping with those assessed with the MSS-B. Thus, only two complementary subscales were used: the Suspiciousness subscale (SPQ-SU), which includes 8 items, and the Ideas of Reference subscale (SPQ-IR), which includes 9 items. The SPQ-SU measures levels of suspiciousness, known as the tendency to view others as having harmful intent or motives. Sample items include “I am sure I am being talked about behind my back” and “Do you often feel that other people have it in for you?”. The SPQ-IR measures ideas of reference, reflecting the belief that everyday occurrences, people, or objects may hold significant, personal meaning. The subscale includes items such as “I am aware that people notice me when I go out for a meal or to see a film” and “Do you sometimes feel that things you see on the TV or read in the newspaper have a special meaning for you?”

Data analyses

Hierarchical linear modeling (HLM 7.0; Scientific Software International, Inc., Skokie, IL, USA) was used to analyze non-aggregated data from individual task trials. A continuous sampling model was used with participants treated as a random factor, and a restricted maximum likelihood method of estimation for model parameters (Raudenbush and Bryk, 2002). Continuous trial-level variables (e.g., predictability ratings) were centered around each participant's mean. Dummy-coded variables (e.g., match condition) were uncentered (Enders and Tofighi, 2007). All models had random intercepts, and all predictor variables had random slopes. Any additional statistical analyses, such as t-tests or Mann-Whitney U tests (when normality assumptions were not met), were performed using JASP (JASP, 2021). Prism 10 (GraphPad Prism Software, 2024) was used for data visualization. Data analyses are available (https://osf.io/jngsy).

Results

Schizotypy-related scores across groups

One participant from the psychosis group did not complete the SPQ, resulting in a total sample size of 67. As expected, SPQ-SU scores differed significantly across groups (Mann-Whitney two-tailed U test, U = 319.00, p = 0.002, rB = −0.431, 95% CI [−0.629, −0.181]), with the psychosis group obtaining higher scores (Mean ± SE: 3.36 ± 0.39) than the control group (Mean ± SE: 1.74 ± 0.30). Similarly, for SPQ-IR, scores differed significantly across groups (U = 375.00, p = 0.018, rB = −0.332, 95% CI [−0.553, −0.066]), with the psychosis group obtaining higher scores (Mean ± SE: 3.36 ± 0.42) than the control group (Mean ± SE: 2.03 ± 0.37).

For the MSS-B, total scores differed significantly across groups (U = 230.00, p < 0.001, rB = −0.602, 95% CI [−0.750, −0.397]), with the psychosis group obtaining higher scores (Mean ± SE: 11.88 ± 1.38) than the control group (Mean ± SE: 4.50 ± 0.95). Specifically, significant differences across groups were observed for the three subscales: MSSB-P (U = 376.50, p = 0.011, rB = −0.349, 95% CI [−0.565, −0.087]; Mean ± SE for the psychosis group: 3.50 ± 0.56; Mean ± SE for the control group: 1.62 ± 0.43), MSSB-N (U = 373.50, p = 0.011, rB = −0.354, 95% CI [−0.569, −0.093]; Mean ± SE for the psychosis group: 3.71 ± 0.59; Mean ± SE for the control group: 1.71 ± 0.32) and MSSB-D (U = 255.00, p < 0.001, rB = −0.559, 95% CI [−0.720, −0.341]; Mean ± SE for the psychosis group: 4.68 ± 0.66; Mean ± SE for the control group: 1.18 ± 0.39).

Predictability of facial expressions across groups

An HLM analysis was computed, with trial-level predictability ratings as the outcome variable and group (psychosis and control) as a dummy-coded subject-level predictor variable. This analysis revealed that, overall, individuals in the psychosis group rated facial expressions as significantly less similar to what they expected (Mean ± SE: 2.38 ± 0.06), i.e. lower predictability ratings, than control individuals (Mean ± SE: 2.61 ± 0.04) (B = −0.23, SE = 0.07, t(66) = 3.15, p = 0.002).

To investigate the effect of match condition on predictability ratings across groups, an HLM analysis was run with trial-level predictability ratings as the outcome variable, match condition (matched and nonmatched trials) as a dummy-coded trial-level predictor variable, and group (psychosis and control) as a dummy-coded subject-level predictor variable, with the two-way interaction term included in the model. In line with previous studies (Chanes et al., 2018; Draganov et al., 2023; Ramos-Grille et al., 2022; Robles et al., 2024), these analyses revealed that matched facial expressions were rated as significantly more predicted (Mean ± SE: 3.19 ± 0.05) than nonmatched facial expressions (Mean ± SE: 1.75 ± 0.05) for the control group (B = 1.44, SE = 0.06, t(66) = 23.85, p < 0.001). For the psychosis group, matched facial expressions (Mean ± SE: 2.85 ± 0.08) were also rated as significantly more predicted than nonmatched facial expressions (Mean ± SE: 1.67 ± 0.06, B = 1.17, SE = 0.07, t(66) = 17.13, p < 0.001). Importantly, however, the difference in predictability ratings for matched vs. nonmatched trials differed significantly across groups (B = −0.27, SE = 0.09, t(66) = 2.91, p = 0.005), indicating that the difference in predictability ratings for matched vs. nonmatched facial expressions (i.e., the ‘match effect’), was smaller for individuals in the psychosis group than for control individuals. These results indicate that individuals in the psychosis group, despite expecting stereotypical facial expressions for the emotion evoked by the scenario (e.g., a frowning face after a sad scenario) to a certain extent, did so less than controls. Upon further exploration of this interaction, results indicated that individuals in the psychosis group rated the expressions as significantly less predicted than the control group on matched trials (B = 0.34, SE = 0.09, t(66) = 3.67, p < 0.001). However, differences did not reach significance in predictability ratings between the two groups for nonmatched trials (B = 0.08, SE = 0.08, t(66) = 0.97, p = 0.334).

To explore whether differences in predictability ratings observed across the psychosis and control groups were driven by specific emotion categories or rather observed consistently across them, we conducted the above-mentioned analysis separately for trials with each of the three evoked emotions (happiness, sadness, and fear) (Fig. 2). Individuals in the psychosis group showed a significantly smaller difference in predictability ratings for matched vs. nonmatched trials than control individuals across scenarios evoking sadness (Mean ± SE: 2.69 ± 0.09 vs. 1.78 ± 0.08 for the psychosis group; Mean ± SE: 3.12 ± 0.07 vs. 1.95 ± 0.06 for the control group; B = −0.26, SE = 0.11, t(66) = 2.29, p = 0.025) and fear (Mean ± SE: 2.59 ± 0.09 vs. 1.83 ± 0.07 for the psychosis group; Mean ± SE: 2.96 ± 0.07 vs. 1.91 ± 0.07 for the control group; B = −0.29, SE = 0.13, t(66) = 2.21, p = 0.031), being marginally significant for happiness (Mean ± SE: 3.26 ± 0.09 vs. 1.41 ± 0.07 for the psychosis group; Mean ± SE: 3.49 ± 0.05 vs. 1.38 ± 0.07 for the control group; B = −0.25, SE = 0.14, t(66) = 1.87, p = 0.066). Additionally, individuals in the psychosis group rated matched expressions as significantly less predicted than controls across all three emotion scenarios (happiness: B = 0.23, SE = 0.11, t(66) = 2.15, p = 0.036; sadness: B = 0.43, SE = 0.12, t(66) = 3.69, p < 0.001; fear: B = 0.37, SE = 0.11, t(66) = 3.21, p = 0.002), whereas no significant differences in predictability ratings between the psychosis and control groups were observed for nonmatched trials (happiness: B = −0.03, SE = 0.10, t(66) = 0.31, p = 0.761; sadness: B = 0.17, SE = 0.10, t(66) = 1.69, p = 0.096; fear: B = 0.08, SE = 0.10, t(66) = 0.82, p = 0.417).Fig. 2Predictability ratings by scenario evoked emotion and match condition for the psychosis and control groups (means and standard errors). Predictability of facial expressions and schizotypy: an exploratory dimensional analysis

As an exploratory analysis, we investigated whether the reduced match effect observed in psychosis could also be observed when considering schizotypy dimensionally in healthy individuals not having experienced any psychotic episode. For the following analysis, MSS-B data and SPQ data from the control group were used (n = 34). The distribution of schizotypy scores within the control group is shown in Supplementary Material (Supplementary Fig. 1).

An HLM model was run per MSS-B subscale (MSSB-P, MSSB-N, and MSSB-D), with trial-level predictability ratings as the outcome variable, match condition (matched and nonmatched trials) as a dummy-coded trial-level predictor and the MSS-B subscale scores as a subject-level predictor, with the interaction term included in the model. The analyses revealed that the difference in predictability ratings between matched and nonmatched trials (match effect) depended on the MSS-B subscale scores. Specifically, higher schizotypy scores were associated with a reduced match effect (MSSB-P: B = −0.06, SE = 0.02, t(32) = −3.30, p = 0.002; MSSB-N: B = −0.12, SE = 0.03, t(32) = −4.76, p < 0.001; and MSSB-D: B = −0.05, SE = 0.02, t(32) = −2.83, p = 0.008). Moreover, an HLM model was run per SPQ subscale (SPQ-SU and SPQ-IR), which failed to reveal a significant relationship between SPQ subscales and match effect (SPQ-SU: B = −0.03, SE = 0.04, t(32) = −0.71, p = 0.481; SPQ-IR: B = −0.04, SE = 0.03, t(32) = −1.58, p = 0.123). Similar results were found when analyses across evoked emotions were performed (Supplementary Material).

Thus, consistent with what was observed across groups, within the control group, higher levels of schizotypy (as measured by the MSS-B) were associated with a reduced match effect, indicating less pronounced differences in predictability ratings between matched and nonmatched conditions.

Predictability of facial expressions and social evaluation

To investigate the association between predictability and likability ratings, HLM analyses were conducted with trial-level likability ratings as the outcome variable, predictability ratings as a trial-level predictor variable, and group (psychosis and control) as a dummy-coded subject level predictor variable, with the two-way interaction term included in the model. Consistent with previous studies (Chanes et al., 2018; Draganov et al., 2023; Ramos-Grille et al., 2022; Robles et al., 2024), a positive relationship was observed between predictability and likability ratings for the control group. Facial expressions that were rated as more predicted were also rated as more likable, in other words, individuals liked others more when they successfully anticipated their expressions (B = 0.27, SE = 0.03, t(66) = 8.74, p < 0.001). This was also observed for the psychosis group (B = 0.30, SE = 0.05, t(66) = 6.59, p < 0.001), and this relationship did not differ significantly across groups (B = −0.02, SE = 0.05, t(66) = −0.42, p = 0.676), suggesting that both groups relied to a similar extent on their predictions to evaluate others.

Discussion

This study explored predictive processing in facial expression perception in individuals within the critical period of five years from a first psychotic episode. We found that, overall, such individuals rated facial expressions as significantly less similar to what they expected compared to the control group, reflecting lower predictability ratings and, thus, heightened prediction error. However, this effect was modulated by match condition. The match effect (difference in predictability ratings for matched versus nonmatched trials) was smaller for the psychosis than for the control group. Individuals in the psychosis group rated matched expressions as significantly less predicted than control individuals, with no differences in nonmatched trials, suggesting that although stereotypical to a certain extent, their predictions were less stereotypical than those of controls. Moreover, this effect was consistent across emotion categories, although slightly more pronounced for negatively valenced ones (fear and sadness). The possible relationship of these observations with the specific characteristics of the psychosis sample may be addressed in future studies, notably assessing the affective component and the potential impact of depressive symptoms, as these have been shown to impact predictability ratings in a way that could be consistent with the emotion specificity observed here (Ramos-Grille et al., 2022).

The reduced match effect was also observed when considering schizotypy traits dimensionally (as measured by the MSS-B) within the control group, which included individuals with varying levels of schizotypy traits in a nonclinical sample. Although these analyses were exploratory and should be interpreted with caution and replicated in larger samples given the limited sample size (n = 34), this suggests that the observed effects are unlikely to be fully explained by nonspecific disruptions of memory, attention, mentalizing capabilities or other domain-general processes possibly associated with the mental-health-related clinical condition. Instead, they may reflect a more fundamental mechanism that can be observed in the continuum and does not necessarily represent a deficit, but rather a feature. The three schizotypy dimensions (positive, negative and disorganized) were associated with the reduced match effect, which is consistent with what has been observed for psychosis and schizotypy, particularly for the negative dimension, characterized by impaired “emotion recognition” (Dawes et al., 2021; Ventura et al., 2013), diminished affect and expression (Kemp et al., 2023), as well as impairments in several social cognition domains (Green et al., 2019). In contrast, this effect was not observed specifically for suspiciousness and ideas of reference. Subclinical paranoia has been associated with strong negative prior beliefs about the intentions of other people (Wellstein et al., 2020), and meta-analytic evidence suggests that the association between paranoia and misinterpretation of ambiguous cues (including emotional cues) is found across clinical and nonclinical samples, with paranoia severity being related to a worse interpretation bias (Trotta et al., 2021). It is possible that in our study effects may not be observed for very specific features of trait paranoia that tap stable personality traits rather than subclinical paranoid symptoms. Additionally, unlike many studies conducted in nonclinical samples, we did not oversample participants with high levels of subclinical paranoia. Furthermore, the absence of an association might be consistent with the notion that low levels of mistrust might reflect adaptive mechanisms of heightened vigilance to social threats without necessarily distorting the accuracy of emotional judgments (Green and Phillips, 2004). Overall, these exploratory results support the value of examining predictive processing across the psychosis continuum to unravel the mechanisms that are shared between subclinical and clinical manifestations, thus offering a more comprehensive understanding of the factors that contribute to vulnerability for psychosis-spectrum disorders (Kwapil and Barrantes-Vidal, 2015).

The reduced match effect in the psychosis group is consistent with prior literature reporting lower accuracy when identifying facial expressions using basic emotion recognition tasks (for reviews, see (Barkl et al., 2014; Tsui et al., 2024)), which assume that there are stereotypical expressions that can be uniquely linked to emotion categories. We suggest that what has traditionally been interpreted as a deficit in facial emotion recognition and the reduced stereotypicality of expectations observed here may not be independent phenomena but rather may reflect two descriptions of the same underlying pattern. Our paradigm offers a complementary approach in that it does not presuppose what the correct response is, allowing us to reframe what has traditionally been interpreted as a deficit in terms of less stereotypical, more individualized predictions. As mentioned above, this effect is unlikely to be fully explained by a pathological deficit in facial emotion recognition, as evidenced by the schizotypy findings within the control group: higher schizotypy scores in non-clinical participants were associated with the same pattern (a reduced match effect) in the absence of any clinical condition. This dimensional finding suggests that the reduced stereotypicality of facial expression expectations may reflect a more fundamental characteristic distributed along the psychosis continuum, rather than a consequence of pathological impairment in facial processing. Less stereotypical expectations may lead to interpersonal difficulties and alternative interpretations in social contexts (Kozhuharova et al., 2020). The reduced match effect could possibly reflect an overweighting of prediction errors that has been posited to occur in early psychosis (Petrovic and Sterzer, 2023; Griffin and Fletcher, 2017), leading to more nuanced, less stereotypical predictions. Despite differences in the stereotypicality of predictions, individuals in the psychosis group appeared to use their own predictions to a similar extent as the control group.

This study is not without limitations. First, most individuals in the psychosis group were using medication. Second, while our task focused on how similar expectations were to the displayed expressions, we did not specifically assess their content. Future research may investigate the nature of these expectations in terms of level of detail, clarity, or other specific aspects that may differ between groups. Additionally, since only three emotion categories (happiness, sadness, and fear) and one social evaluation aspect (likability) were assessed, future studies could explore a broader range of emotion categories (e.g., anger) and social evaluation aspects (e.g., trustworthiness (Chanes et al., 2018)) to better explore and characterize the observed effects. Similarly, while we aimed to assess predictability as a whole, more specific aspects of the facial expression or emotion scenarios— such as facial configuration, intensity of the expression, specific cognitive aspects of image perception, narrative interpretations, and affective response to narratives— remain to be further explored. An analysis of the content of facial expression predictions could shed light on characterizing the results observed here (reduced match effect) as compared to similar results observed for autistic individuals (Robles et al., 2024), with the difference that while here the effect was driven by a reduction of matched trials predictability ratings, autistic individuals showed a reduced match effect primarily driven by higher predictability ratings on nonmatched trials. Finally, in this work, the possible relationship between specific symptoms (delusions, hallucinations, etc.) and facial expression predictions was not analyzed in a differentiated manner, and future studies along these lines are necessary. Furthermore, the dimensional schizotypy analyses conducted within the control group were exploratory in nature and based on a limited sample size (n = 34); these findings should therefore be interpreted with caution and await replication in larger samples.

The current study contributes to a growing body of literature on social perception in psychosis through the role of predictive processing in facial emotion perception, demonstrating disruptions among individuals with psychotic experiences, notably, a reduced stereotypicality of facial expression expectations, further highlighting the relevance of a continuum approach.
