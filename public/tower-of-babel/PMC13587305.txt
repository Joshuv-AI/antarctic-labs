Static and dynamic balance performance and associated factors among children with and without hearing impairment in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia: a comparative cross-sectional study
Authors: Haimanot Andualem Ayalsew, Zeleke Endalew Admass, Mitku Tessafa Shiferaw, Abebaye Aragaw Leminie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBalance is essential for daily activities, motor development, and overall well-being. The vestibular system plays a key role in balance control, and impairments associated with hearing loss may negatively affect balance in children. However, balance assessment is not routinely performed in children with hearing impairment.ObjectivesThis study assessed static and dynamic balance performance and associated factors among children with and without hearing impairment in selected public schools in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia.MethodsA comparative cross-sectional study was conducted from September 2023 to June 2024 among 148 schoolchildren aged 7–18 years (74 with hearing impairment and 74 sex-matched peers with normal hearing). Balance performance was evaluated using the single-leg stance test (eyes open and closed), functional reach test, and Pediatric Balance Scale. Data were analyzed using independent t-tests, Pearson’s correlation, and regression analyses in SPSS version 27. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.ResultsChildren with hearing impairment scored significantly lower than their normal-hearing peers in the single-leg stance (eyes open and closed), functional reach test, and pediatric balance scale (all p < 0.001). When analyzed by sex, males with hearing impairments had significantly lower scores in single-leg stance (eyes open and closed, p < 0.001), functional reach test (p < 0.002), and pediatric balance scale (p < 0.001) compared to males with normal hearing. Similarly, females with hearing impairments demonstrated significantly lower scores in single-leg stance (eyes open and closed, p < 0.001), functional reach test (p < 0.003), and pediatric balance scale (p < 0.001) compared to females with normal hearing.ConclusionChildren with hearing impairment demonstrated significantly lower static and dynamic balance performance than age- and sex-matched peers with normal hearing. These findings highlight the importance of incorporating routine balance assessment in this population.Keywords: Static Balance, Dynamic Balance, Vestibular Function, Postural Control, Balance Performance, Hearing Impairment, Children

Introduction

Balance is a fundamental motor skill that enables individuals to maintain equilibrium during static postures and restore it during movement [1]. It is essential for daily activities, motor development, sports participation, and overall well-being, particularly during childhood when postural control systems are still maturing [2]. Balance can be categorized as static and dynamic, both of which rely on the integration of sensory inputs, with the vestibular system playing a central regulatory role [1, 3, 4].

The vestibular system comprises the otolith organs (utricle and saccule) which detect linear acceleration and head position, and semicircular canals, which sense angular acceleration across multiple planes. Together, these structures coordinate eye movements, head position, and postural adjustments necessary for balance control [5–8]. Due to its close anatomical and functional relationship with the auditory system, children with hearing impairments are at increased risk of concomitant vestibular dysfunction [9]. Vestibular deficits have been reported in approximately 30%–85% of children with sensorineural hearing loss, particularly in those with severe or profound impairment [4, 10]. Even in the absence of measurable vestibular pathology, hearing impairment alone may delay motor development due to reduced sensory integration [11]. These deficits may manifest as impairments in postural control, limb coordination, movement speed, and both static and dynamic balance [10, 12, 13].

Previous studies show that children with hearing impairment demonstrate poorer balance performance than their normal-hearing peers, with reduced static and dynamic balance abilities [4, 9, 12–14]. These impairments increase the risk of falls and injuries [10]. Balance-related complaints are reported in approximately 20.9% of children with hearing impairment, compared with 4.9% in those without hearing difficulties [15].

Balance disorders in childhood impose significant functional and psychosocial challenges, affecting neurodevelopmental processes such as motor development, sensorimotor integration, and participation in age-appropriate activities [14, 16]. Impaired postural stability may limit engagement in activities such as cycling, skipping, and playground play, reducing opportunities for physical activity and social interaction [4, 16]. Over time, these limitations may hinder motor skills acquisition and negatively influence physical, emotional, and social development [17].

Balance is a critical component of child development but is often omitted from routine motor evaluations unless overt musculoskeletal abnormalities are present [18]. Early assessment of postural stability is essential to identify impairments, guide interventions, and support optimal developmental outcomes [14].

Most studies on balance in children with hearing impairment have been conducted in high-income countries, frequently using small or unmatched samples and focusing on a single dimension of balance. Consequently, balance deficits may remain under-recognized, particularly in sub-Saharan Africa. In Ethiopia, no studies have assessed balance performance in children with hearing impairments, and routine balance screening is not practiced in clinical or school settings.

In low-resource settings, there is a need for simple, reliable, and clinically applicable assessment tools that require minimal equipment, space, and training. This study addresses these gaps using an age- and sex-matched comparative design, a broad age range (7–18 years), and multiple standardized measures to assess static, dynamic, and functional balance in hearing-impaired and normal-hearing schoolchildren, as well as associated factors. By providing context-specific evidence from Ethiopian schoolchildren, this study offers novel insights for clinicians, educators, and public health practitioners working in similar settings.

Materials and methods

Study period and setting

This study was conducted in Addis Ababa, the capital city of Ethiopia, at three public educational institutions: Menelik II Primary School and Menelik II Secondary School, which provide inclusive education, and Alpha Special School for the Deaf, which offers education from kindergarten (KG1) to Grade 8 for children with hearing impairment. The study was carried out from September 2023 to June 2024.

Study design

A school-based, comparative multicenter cross-sectional study was conducted to compare static, dynamic, and functional balance performance between age- and sex-matched children with hearing impairment and those with normal hearing in selected public schools.

Population

Source population

The source population comprised all schoolchildren aged 7–18 years with normal hearing and those with hearing impairment attending the selected public schools.

Study population

The study population consisted of randomly selected schoolchildren aged 7–18 years with normal hearing and those with hearing impairment who were present during the data collection period.

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion criteria

Children aged 7–18 years with normal hearing and those with hearing impairment enrolled in the selected schools during the study period, whose parents or legal guardians provided written informed consent, were included.

Exclusion criteria

Children with known neurological disorders, orthopedic conditions, visual impairments, history of head injury, systemic illnesses, intellectual disability, or a history of alcohol or substance use were excluded.

Sample size determination

The sample size was calculated using a two-population proportion formula with a 95% confidence level (Zα/2 = 1.96) and 80% statistical power (Zβ = 0.84). Based on previous studies, the prevalence of balance disorders was 4.9% among children with normal hearing (p₁ = 0.049) and 20.9% among children with hearing impairment (p₂ = 0.209) [15].

Using this formula, the minimum required sample size was 138 participants (69 per group). After accounting for a 10% non-response rate, the final sample size was adjusted to 152 participants (76 per group).

Sampling technique and procedure

Eligible schools and study participants were selected using a simple random sampling (lottery) method. Participant selection was conducted in classrooms in the presence of the researcher, a sign language teacher, and a school clinic nurse. Proportional allocation across grades (Grades 1–12) was applied to ensure representativeness, with approximately equal numbers of children with normal hearing and those with hearing impairment selected from each grade (Fig. 1).

Data collection tools and procedure

A structured questionnaire prepared in Amharic was used to collect sociodemographic and medical information. Parents or guardians completed the questionnaire at home, after which the responses were translated into English and entered into Kobo Toolbox.

Instructions for balance assessments were provided verbally to children with normal hearing by the researcher and school nurse, and through sign language to children with hearing impairment by a trained sign language teacher. Trained school nurses conducted anthropometric and balance assessments, while a trained audiometrist independently assessed hearing status.

Anthropometry measurements

Height was measured in centimeters using a stadiometer, and weight was measured in kilograms using a digital scale, with participants barefoot and wearing light clothing [12].

Hearing assessments

Hearing assessments were conducted in a quiet room by a trained school audiometrist proficient in sign language, using a pure-tone audiometer (Micro-Mate 304, Denmark). Pure-tone audiometry was performed at frequencies of 500, 1000, 2000, 3000, 4000, 6000, and 8000 Hz [13].

A modified Hughson–Westlake procedure was used to determine hearing thresholds. Tones were initially presented at an audible intensity and subsequently decreased in 10-dB steps until no response was obtained, followed by increases in 5-dB steps until the child responded reliably. Children were instructed to raise their hand when they heard a tone and lower it when the sound stopped. The lowest intensity level at which responses were consistently obtained was recorded as the hearing threshold in decibels (dB) [19].

The degree of hearing loss was determined using the pure-tone average (PTA) calculated at 500, 1000, 2000, and 4000 Hz in the better-hearing ear. Hearing loss severity was categorized according to World Health Organization criteria as follows: normal (< 20 dB), mild (20–34 dB), moderate (35–49 dB), moderately severe (50–64 dB), severe (65–79 dB), profound (80–94 dB), and complete deafness (≥ 95 dB) [20, 21].

Static balance assessment

Static balance was assessed using the Single-Leg Stance Test under both eyes-open and eyes-closed conditions. The SLST is a widely used and validated clinical measure of postural stability in pediatric populations [12, 22, 23]. Participants were instructed to stand barefoot on their dominant leg with hands on their hips and the non-dominant leg flexed at the knee, with the sole positioned at the knee level of the supporting leg [12, 22, 23]. Timing began once a stable position was achieved.

The test was terminated if the participant placed the raised foot on the floor, removed either hand from the hips, hopped, opened their eyes during the eyes-closed condition, or shifted the supporting foot. Each trial was limited to a maximum of 60 s [24, 25]. After one practice trial, two trials were performed under both eyes-open and eyes-closed conditions, with 3–5-minute rest intervals [12, 22, 23]. The best performance time was used for analysis.

Dynamic balance assessment

Dynamic balance was evaluated using the Functional Reach Test (FRT), a reliable and widely used clinical measure that assesses the maximum distance an individual can reach forward beyond arm’s length while maintaining a stable base of support [12, 26]. The FRT is recognized for its precision, simplicity, and applicability in both home and outpatient clinical settings [26]. Participants performed the test using their dominant arm, defined as the hand preferred for writing or throwing [12]. A measuring tape was fixed to the wall parallel to the floor, with the zero mark aligned at the level of the participant’s acromion. Participants stood with their feet parallel, raised the dominant arm to 90°, and made a fist. The initial reach distance was measured from the acromion process to the third metacarpal in centimeters. Participants then reached forward as far as possible without touching the wall or taking a step and maintained the position for three seconds [26, 27]. The final reach distance was recorded. The procedure was repeated three times, and the mean reach distance was calculated. The difference between the initial and final reach distances was used for analysis [12, 26, 27].

Functional balance assessment

The Pediatric Balance Scale (PBS), adapted from the Berg Balance Scale (BBS), is a reliable tool for assessing functional balance in school-aged children. It is quick to administer (under 15 min), easy to score, and requires no specialized equipment, making it ideal for routine physical therapy [28, 29]. The PBS evaluates 14 tasks—6 static and 8 dynamic—reflecting daily functional activities at home, school, and in the community [28–30].

The tasks include sitting to standing, standing to sitting, transfers, standing and sitting unsupported, standing with eyes closed, standing with feet together or on one foot, tandem standing, turning 360°, turning to look behind, retrieving an object from the floor, placing an alternate foot on a stool, and forward reaching. Each task is scored on a scale from 0 to 4, yielding a maximum total score of 56, with higher scores indicating better balance performance. Healthy children aged seven years and older typically score near the maximum, whereas scores ≤ 45 indicate an increased risk of falls [28–31].

The SLST, FRT, and PBS were chosen for this study due to their simplicity, reliability, and clinical relevance, particularly in low-resource settings. These tools require minimal equipment, are quick to administer, and can be performed in limited spaces without specialized training, making them practical for pediatric populations in community or school settings. They provide valid measures of static, dynamic, and functional balance, reflecting everyday postural control and fall risk, and have been widely validated across diverse pediatric populations [12, 22, 23, 28–30]. Their combined application enables a comprehensive yet feasible and cost-effective evaluation of balance performance in settings where access to sophisticated vestibular or laboratory-based assessments is limited.

Data quality control and analysis

Data quality was ensured using standardized tools and procedures. The questionnaire was translated from English to Amharic and back-translated to ensure consistency. Data collectors received one day of training before data collection.

A pretest was conducted on 10% of the sample one week before the main study, and necessary modifications were made based on the findings. The principal investigator reviewed submitted data daily via the Kobo Toolbox server to identify and correct inconsistencies.

Data were exported from Kobo Toolbox into SPSS version 27 for cleaning and analysis. Data normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov and Shapiro–Wilk tests. Independent samples t-tests were used to compare the balance outcomes between children with normal hearing and hearing impairment. Descriptive statistics were summarized using means and standard deviations. Correlation and linear regression analyses were performed to examine relationships among continuous variables. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Participants were aged 7–18 years, with the largest proportion in the 15–18-year age group (45.3%). The distribution of age categories and educational grade levels was comparable between the two groups due to the matching design. Overall, two-thirds of participants were enrolled in grades 1–8, while one-third were in grades 9–12 (Table 1).

Balance performance comparison

Children with hearing impairment demonstrated significantly lower performance in static, dynamic, and functional balance compared to their normal-hearing peers (all p < 0.001). This pattern was consistent across single-leg stance with eyes open and closed, Functional Reach Test (FRT), and Pediatric Balance Scale (PBS) measures (Fig. 3). Although PBS scores in both groups clustered near the upper limit of the scale, suggesting a potential ceiling effect that may reduce its discriminatory sensitivity, a statistically significant difference was still observed between groups. This finding indicates comparatively reduced functional balance performance among children with hearing impairment; however, the magnitude of this difference should be interpreted with caution given the possible ceiling limitation of the instrument.

Effect of sex on balance performance

Children with normal hearing demonstrated significantly better balance than children with hearing impairment across both sexes. Among males, SLSEO (45.5 ± 12.6 s vs. 29.2 ± 15.8 s, p < 0.001) and SLSEC (14.2 ± 7.5 s vs. 5.4 ± 5.2 s, p < 0.001) were higher in normal-hearing children, with similar patterns observed for FRT (25.7 ± 6.3 cm vs. 21.8 ± 4.1 cm, p < 0.002) and PBS scores (55.8 ± 0.5 vs. 54.5 ± 1.2, p < 0.001). Among females, SLSEO (49.7 ± 11.5 s vs. 32.0 ± 15.9 s, p < 0.001) and SLSEC (16.6 ± 8.4 s vs. 3.2 ± 2.0 s, p < 0.001) also favored normal-hearing children, alongside higher FRT (26.9 ± 6.9 cm vs. 22.6 ± 4.5 cm, p < 0.003) and PBS scores (55.9 ± 0.5 vs. 54.3 ± 1.1, p < 0.001) (Fig. 4).

Across all age groups, children with normal hearing demonstrated significantly better balance than children with hearing impairment. In the 7–10 years group, normal-hearing children outperformed their hearing-impaired counterparts on SLSEO, SLSEC, FRT, and PBS scores (all p < 0.05). Similar patterns were observed in the 11–14 years group (all p < 0.05) and the 15–18 years group, with normal-hearing children consistently showing higher scores across all balance measures (all p < 0.001) (Table 4).

Discussion

Static, dynamic and functional balance performance

This study demonstrated significantly reduced static, dynamic, and functional balance performance among schoolchildren with hearing impairment compared with age- and sex-matched peers with normal hearing. Differences were consistently observed across the single-leg stance test (eyes open and closed), Functional Reach Test (FRT), and Pediatric Balance Scale (PBS), indicating that hearing impairment is associated with both static and dynamic balance deficits.

Static balance, assessed using the single-leg stance test under both eyes-open and eyes-closed conditions, was consistently reduced in the hearing-impaired group. These findings are consistent with previous studies reporting poorer static balance performance in children with hearing impairment compared with normal-hearing peers [4, 12, 13, 32–34], including evidence from Egypt [22].

In contrast, a study from Poland [10] and other studies [35, 36] reported superior static balance among hearing-impaired children. This discrepancy may be explained by methodological differences, including small and unmatched sample sizes and a narrower age range, compared with the balanced and broader design of the present study.

Dynamic balance, assessed using the FRT, was also significantly lower in children with hearing impairment. This finding aligns with studies from India [37], Brazil [4], Germany [38], Egypt [22], and China [18].

However, studies from Nigeria [12] and India [33] found no significant differences in dynamic balance between children with hearing impairment and normal-hearing. These discrepancies may be explained by differences in age range, sociodemographic characteristics, and sample size.

PBS scores were significantly lower in children with hearing impairment. Although a ceiling effect is expected in children aged ≥ 7 years [28, 29], significant group differences were still detected, suggesting that the PBS can identify functional balance deficits despite reduced sensitivity at higher performance levels. This finding aligns with studies from India [26], Turkey [30] and Brazil [32].

Mechanisms linking hearing loss and balance

Vestibular and inner ear mechanisms

Postural control depends on the integration of visual, somatosensory, and vestibular inputs [17]. The cochlear and vestibular receptors share the membranous labyrinth [31]; therefore, inner ear pathology may affect both hearing and balance [39]. Cochlear damage may also impair vestibular function [38, 40], contributing to balance deficits and delayed motor development [41, 42].

Vestibular dysfunction is common in children with sensorineural hearing loss and is associated with increased postural instability [18, 43]. However, even children with normal vestibular test results may demonstrate impaired balance compared with normal-hearing peers, suggesting that balance deficits are not solely explained by measurable vestibular abnormalities [44].

Children with bilateral cochleovestibular loss exhibit poorer balance across sensory conditions [45]. Improvements following cochlear implantation have been reported, possibly due to enhanced spatial orientation, although central compensation and sensory reweighting likely also contribute [45, 46].

Even in the absence of confirmed vestibular pathology, hearing impairment may delay motor development due to reduced sensory integration and environmental interaction [11]. Reduced auditory input may impair spatial orientation and increase postural instability [10]. While auditory cues contribute to postural control in normal-hearing children [45, 47], this mechanism is diminished in children with hearing loss.

Although previous studies have reported modest improvements in balance following restoration of auditory input through cochlear implants or hearing aids [45, 48], most participants in this study did not use hearing aids, which may have contributed to poorer balance performance. Due to the small number of users (n = 2), subgroup analysis was not feasible.

The majority of participants had severe-to-profound hearing loss, and balance impairment has been reported to increase with severity [1, 13, 38]. Consistent with this, poorer performance was observed in the eyes-closed condition, suggesting greater reliance on non-visual sensory inputs. However, no significant differences were observed between severity groups for PBS, FRT, or SLSEO. These findings should be interpreted cautiously because the moderate-to-moderately severe group included only 11 participants compared with 63 in the severe-to-profound group, potentially limiting statistical power for subgroup comparisons.

The absence of significant differences in PBS, FRT, and SLSEO contrasts with previous studies reporting better balance performance among children with moderate hearing impairment than those with severe or profound impairment [13, 49], but is consistent with similar PBS findings across hearing-loss severity levels [32]. Differences in sample size, severity distribution, and hearing impairment characteristics may explain this discrepancy. In the present study, 85.7% of participants had severe-to-profound hearing loss, whereas 14.3% had moderate-to-moderately severe hearing loss.

Importantly, objective vestibular assessments were not performed in this study. Therefore, causal inference regarding the contribution of vestibular dysfunction to balance deficits should be interpreted with caution.

Contemporary perspectives beyond vestibular and inner ear dysfunction

Balance impairment in children with hearing loss is likely multifactorial. Biomechanical, cognitive, and behavioral factors may also contribute. Forward head posture, which is more common in children with hearing impairment, may negatively affect postural stability [50, 51]. Increased attentional demands for communication may also reduce cognitive resources available for balance control [52].

Psychosocial and lifestyle factors may further influence balance outcomes. Children with hearing impairments often demonstrate reduced participation in physical activity [2], which may delay motor development and contribute to increased body weight both associated with poorer balance [18, 53]. In the present study, none of the hearing-impaired participants engaged in structured physical activity. This may have contributed to the observed deficits, particularly given evidence that training improves balance performance in this population [54, 55]. Reduced environmental stimulation may also delay motor development [10, 37, 41].

Effect of sex and age on balance performance

Both boys and girls with hearing impairment demonstrated lower balance performance than same-sex peers with normal hearing, suggesting that hearing status, rather than sex, is the primary determinant, consistent with previous studies [22, 32, 56].

Across all age groups, children with hearing impairment performed worse than their peers, indicating that balance deficits are not solely explained by developmental variation [4, 12, 13, 22, 30, 32]. Although balance improves with age, the persistent gap suggests delayed or altered sensory integration.

Correlation and associated factors of balance performance

A significant correlation between static and dynamic balance measures was observed, consistent with previous studies [23, 44]. In contrast, Ayanniyi et al. [12] reported no significant correlation. This discrepancy may be explained by methodological differences. While Ayanniyi et al. included unequal group sizes, the present study employed a balanced design with equal numbers of children with hearing impairment and normal-hearing participants, which may have improved comparability and statistical power.

Multivariable regression analyses demonstrated that age and height were positively associated with balance performance, particularly FRT and SLSEO, consistent with previous research showing developmental improvements with increasing age and body size [14, 27, 31]. Although body weight was initially considered in the regression model, it was excluded because of multicollinearity with other predictors, which may limit interpretation of its independent contribution to balance performance. Hearing impairment status was negatively associated with balance outcomes, consistent with earlier reports [4, 10, 13, 22, 32, 56, 57].

Conclusion and recommendations

Conclusion

Children with hearing impairment demonstrated significantly poorer static and dynamic balance performance compared with age- and sex-matched peers with normal hearing. Hearing impairment is significantly associated with reduced balance performance across all balance measures.

Recommendations

Routine balance screening should be integrated into follow-up care for children with hearing impairment using simple, low-cost tools. Schools implementing inclusive education should consider incorporating basic balance assessments to support functional development. Future research should employ longitudinal and interventional designs with comprehensive vestibular assessments.

Strengths of the study

The comparative design, with age- and sex-matched groups of equal sample size, strengthened comparability and internal validity. In addition, the use of multiple validated and clinically feasible balance assessment tools, including the Single-Leg Stance Test (SLST), Functional Reach Test (FRT), and Pediatric Balance Scale (PBS), enabled a comprehensive evaluation of balance performance and enhanced the applicability of the findings in resource-limited settings.

Limitations of the study

The study did not differentiate between types of hearing loss, limiting interpretation of underlying mechanisms. Objective vestibular assessments were not performed, precluding direct evaluation of vestibular function. In addition, although physical activity and hearing aid use were assessed, the small number of participants reporting regular activity or consistent use limited further analysis and may affect generalizability.
