Alternative splicing rewires breast cancer and opens therapeutic avenues
Authors: Yuhan Jin, Yiran Liang, Mahsa Maghsoudy Kasvaei, Qifeng Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBreast cancer exhibits profound molecular and clinical heterogeneity, manifested through diverse subtypes, metastatic patterns, and therapeutic responses. Emerging evidence underscores that this complexity stems not merely from genetic and epigenetic aberrations but also from post-transcriptional regulatory mechanisms, particularly alternative splicing (AS), which amplifies proteomic diversity and functional versatility. AS generates multiple mRNA isoforms from a single gene, directly influencing oncogenic processes including cell migration, metabolic reprogramming, immune evasion, and therapy resistance. Recent advances in high-throughput sequencing and bioinformatics have enabled systematic mapping of AS landscapes in breast cancer. This review delineates the mechanistic underpinnings of AS regulation and synthesizes cutting-edge discoveries in AS-driven oncogenic pathways. We further evaluate the translational potential of AS signatures in clinical applications, such as molecular subtyping, prognostic biomarkers, and splice-switching therapeutics. Finally, we address persisting challenges, including tumor heterogeneity and the lack of splicing specificity, that must be overcome to harness AS modulation as a precision oncology strategy.Keywords: Breast cancer, Alternative splicing, Splicing factor, Biomarker, Therapeutic target

Background

Breast cancer remains the most frequently diagnosed malignant tumor among women worldwide, accounting for 32% of all female malignancies and causing 14% of cancer-related deaths, ranking second in female cancer mortality [1]. Despite advances in early detection and systemic therapies, clinical outcomes vary significantly, underscoring the profound heterogeneity of the disease [2]. This heterogeneity manifests at multiple levels, including molecular, histopathological, and clinical aspects, which are deeply rooted in complex genetic and epigenetic regulatory mechanisms, collectively influencing tumor progression, metastatic potential, and response to treatment [3]. Among these diverse mechanisms, AS has emerged as a crucial regulatory layer that significantly enriches the transcriptomic and proteomic diversity of breast cancer, thereby amplifying its heterogeneity and driving disease progression.

In eukaryotic cells, precursor messenger RNAs (pre-mRNAs) undergo post-transcriptional splicing, a process where introns are removed and exons are ligated to form mature mRNAs [4]. For most multi-exonic genes, the selective combination of exons often occurs during splicing, a process known as AS, enabling a single gene to produce distinct transcripts and multiple protein isoforms with diverse structures and functions [5]. Over 95% of human genes are affected by AS to generate proteins with different or even opposite functions, making it a central mechanism for expanding proteomic diversity. The ongoing evolution of whole genome and transcriptome sequencing technologies has revealed the tremendous intricacy of splicing regulation and the prevalence of splicing abnormalities in cancers, including breast cancer. Dysregulated AS often inactivates tumor suppressors or promotes oncogene expression, thereby participating in many aspects of cancer pathogenesis [6], including oncogenic transformation, cancer progression, and drug resistance. Consequently, identifying cancer-specific AS events could not only provide crucial molecular markers, but also offer novel opportunities for developing cancer therapeutics.

This review aims to provide insights into the major aspects of AS dysregulation in breast cancer, focusing on the mechanisms governing AS patterns and emphasizing their therapeutic implications. Given the growing evidence linking AS dysregulation to breast cancer development, we also describe how aberrant AS isoforms contribute to various hallmark cancer phenotypes. Finally, we discuss the current and emerging strategies targeting AS to enhance therapeutic outcomes, as well as the existing challenges that need to be addressed.

Basic process and regulatory networks of pre-mRNA splicing

Spliceosome-mediated splicing cascade

The splicing process is catalyzed by the spliceosome, a highly dynamic multiprotein machinery composed of five small nuclear ribonucleoprotein particles (snRNPs, U1, U2, U4, U5, and U6) and approximately 200 accessory proteins (non-snRNP proteins). The spliceosome assembles in a stepwise manner (Fig. 1a). Initially, specific spliceosome components recognize and bind to the regulatory sequences in pre-mRNA [7], such as the 5’splice site (5’ss), 3’splice site (3’ss), branch point (BP), and the polypyrimidine tract (PPT). The snRNA molecule of U1 snRNP forms a base-pairing interaction with the GU dinucleotide at the 5’ss. Splicing factor 1 (SF1) recognizes and binds to BP, while U2 small nuclear RNA auxiliary factor 1 and 2 (U2AF1 and U2AF2) attach to the 3’ss and adjacent PPT, respectively, forming complex E. SF1 is then substituted by U2 snRNP, which binds to the BP through base pairing, forming the complex A. The interaction of U1 and U2 snRNPs brings the 5’ss and 3’ss into proximity, promoting the recruitment of a tri-snRNA complex (U4, U5, and U6) to the 5’ss and forming the complex B. After a series of conformational rearrangements, U1 and U4 are released from the spliceosome complex, and the activated complex B (complex B*) forms. Subsequently, two transesterification reactions are initiated. In the first step, the 2’-hydroxyl group of the adenine at the BP attacks the 3’,5’-phosphodiester bond at the 5’ss, releasing the 5’ exon from the intron. This marks the transition to the catalytically active complex C. Subsequent conformational remodeling gives rise to the complex C*, which facilitates the second transesterification reaction. In this step, the free 3’-OH of the exon attacks the phosphodiester bond at the 3’ss, releasing the lariat intron and joining the two exons [8]. Finally, the snRNPs are released from the spliceosome complex and recycled in subsequent splicing cycles.

Recognition between regulatory proteins and splice sites

The selection of splice sites is regulated by the intricate interplay between cis-acting elements within the pre-mRNA and diverse trans-acting factors, ensuring the splicing accuracy (Fig. 1b) [9]. Cis-acting elements, specific nucleotide sequences located in both exons and introns of the pre-mRNA, can be recognized by spliceosome components. Additionally, splicing regulatory elements (SREs) are primarily located in the proximity of splice sites and can be classified into four categories: exon splicing enhancers (ESEs), exon splicing silencers (ESSs), intron splicing enhancers (ISEs), and intron splicing silencers (ISSs). Trans-acting factors mainly consist of RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) known as splicing factors (SFs). The serine/arginine-rich (SR) proteins and heterogeneous nuclear ribonucleoproteins (hnRNPs) are two major families of SFs [10], which can interact with ESEs/ISEs or ESSs/ISSs, either enhancing or repressing splice site recognition and exon inclusion. The complex antagonistic or cooperative interactions among these SFs can influence splice site selection, enabling the generation of multiple AS variants through different RNA splicing patterns. Notably, while this regulatory plasticity enables tissue-specific or stress-responsive splicing patterns, its dysregulation contributes to over 50% of pathogenic splicing errors.

Alternative splicing of pre-mRNA

Alternative splicing diversity

Apart from canonically spliced mRNAs, over 95% of human genes produce multiple transcript isoforms through intricate AS regulation [11], which greatly expands the protein diversity and phenotypic complexity. Various splicing events, including exon skipping, intron retention, alternative 5’ splice site usage, alternative 3’ splice site usage, and mutually exclusive exons (Fig. 1c), contribute to the diversity of mature mRNAs. Additionally, rare phenomena such as exon scrambling (exons are correctly spliced but improperly arrayed) and trans-splicing (exons from different pre-mRNAs are linked) further expand the heterogeneity of mature mRNAs. Notably, exon skipping and intron retention are frequently observed in breast cancer splice events.

Multidimensional regulation of alternative splicing

AS regulation operates through multilayered molecular crosstalk spanning transcriptional, epitranscriptomic, and post-translational dimensions.

At the transcriptional level, AS is dynamically governed by kinetic coupling between RNA polymerase II (Pol II) elongation rates and chromatin architecture. According to the kinetic model, slower Pol II elongation enhances the recognition of weak splice sites by providing a wider temporal window for spliceosome assembly. This process is integrated with histone modification landscapes through chromatin-splicing adapters [12], establishing a spatial link between nuclear organization and AS outcomes. Concurrently, the 3D chromatin architecture serves as a higher-order regulatory layer that orchestrates spatial enhancer-promoter communication [13, 14], effectively reshaping the physical microenvironment where active transcription and alternative splicing machinery operate.

Transitioning to RNA-centric regulation, m6A (N6-Methyladenosine) epitranscriptomic marks deposited by METTL3 can structurally remodel pre-mRNA to expose cryptic splice sites, enhancing the recruitment of splicing factors (SFs). The m6A reader protein YTHDC1 executes functional decoding by recruiting pro-inclusion factors like SRSF3 while antagonizing exon-skipping factors [15]. Additionally, the secondary structure of RNA, such as the GC-rich motifs bound by SNRPA1, can operate as a splicing enhancer to promote cassette exon inclusion [16].

The regulatory hierarchy for AS is further integrated by RBP networks. Mutant RNA-binding SFs can recognize cryptic splice sites, leading to aberrant splicing of downstream genes. Additionally, a given splicing event is typically co-regulated by multiple RBPs, and their effects depend on specific binding sites. For instance, hnRNPs generally suppress splicing when bound to exons but exert context-dependent positive or negative effects on splicing when bound to introns [17]. Post-translational modifications of RBPs add another regulatory tier, as Sam68 acetylation enhances its binding affinity and inclusion of CD44 variant exons [18], linking covalent protein modifications with splicing decisions.

Collectively, this intricate regulatory framework encompasses DNA templating, RNA conformation, and protein interactions, enabling cells to generate context-specific isoform repertoires through precisely coordinated spatial and temporal molecular interactions.

Key mechanisms underlying alternative splicing dysregulation in breast cancer

In breast cancer, AS is frequently disrupted through multi-layered mechanisms, including mutations and abnormal expression of SFs (Table 1), biomolecular modifications, and non-coding RNA dysregulation, collectively shaping tumor progression and therapeutic resistance. Below, we dissect the core mechanisms through molecular hierarchies, emphasizing their functional convergence on oncogenic signaling networks.

Key splicing factors and their functions in breast cancer

Mutations in splicing factors

Mutations in SFs represent a critical mechanism underlying AS dysregulation, primarily occurring in four genes. SF3B1, a key component of the U2 snRNP critical for 3′ splice site (3′ss) recognition, is mutated in 1.8% of breast cancer cases, representing the highest mutation rate among SFs [35]. Notably, due to synthetic lethality, mutations in SF3B1 and other SFs rarely occur concurrently. The most common mutation in SF3B1 is the K700E mutation (lysine-to-glutamic acid at codon 700), which accounts for 74% of all SF3B1 point mutations [36]. This mutation enables the recognition of cryptic 3′ss hidden within RNA secondary structures, thereby altering splicing patterns, which is exemplified by aberrant MAP3K7 and PPP2R5A splicing. Importantly, SF3B1 K700E mutations are more prevalent in ER+ breast cancers, where they induce aberrant splicing that downregulates MAP3K7 and PPP2R5A [37]. The resultant suppression of these tumor suppressors activates downstream NF-κB and AKT signaling pathways, further promoting cell migration and tumorigenesis.

Although mutations in the other three commonly altered SFs, SRSF2, U2AF1, and ZRSR2, have not been well characterized in breast cancer, their alterations result in aberrant splicing of hundreds of genes and are implicated in enhancing oncogenic pathways. For example, mutations in SRSF2 are found in various cancers and are concentrated at proline residue 95 (P95). Mutated SRSF2 preferentially recognizes C-rich ESEs over G-rich ESEs, and one notable example is the promotion of exon skipping in EZH2 mRNA, leading to the creation of a new stop codon and subsequent degradation of the mRNA through nonsense-mediated decay [38]. Mutated U2AF1 recognizes different sites as 3′ss [39], targeting a number of downstream genes involved in DNA damage, apoptosis, and epigenetic regulation. ZRSR2 is an X-chromosome-encoded RNA-binding protein that interacts with the 3′ss of U12-type introns. Mutations in ZRSR2 typically result in loss-of-function effects, causing retention of minor introns and aberrant splicing events that produce nonsense or frameshift mutations [40]. This mutation reduces the production of functional ZRSR2 protein, disrupting key pathways such as MAPK and E2F.

Beyond the above-mentioned mutations, mutations in other SFs like PRPF8, PHF5A, and RBM10 have also been identified [41], and are closely associated with cancer progression through modulating various signaling pathways. The high frequency and functional impact of these mutations underscore the critical role of splicing dysregulation in cancer development and progression.

Aberrant expression of splicing factors

In addition to mutations, the overexpression or underexpression of SFs also plays a significant role in AS dysregulation in breast cancer.

SF3B1

Beyond frequent mutational events, SF3B1 is also overexpressed in breast cancer tissues [35]. In MDA‑MB‑231 and ZR‑75‑30 breast cancer cells, SF3B1 knockdown leads to differential expression of genes involved in the Ras and MAPK signaling pathways, cytokine receptor interactions, tight junctions, and amino acid metabolism, suppressing proliferation, migration, and invasion while enhancing apoptosis. Exon skipping is the most common molecular effect of SF3B1 knockdown, suggesting that SF3B1 could be a key molecular target for breast cancer treatment [42].

SRSFs

Serine/arginine-rich splicing factors (SRSFs) are a family of proteins that consist of 12 members (SRSF1-12) in mammals, each containing one or two RNA recognition motifs (RRMs) at the N-terminus and an arginine/serine-rich (RS) domain at the C-terminus that mediates diverse protein-protein and protein-RNA interactions [43]. Dysregulation of SRSFs, particularly SRSF1, SRSF2, SRSF3, and SRSF5, has been implicated in various cancers, particularly in breast cancer, highlighting their importance in tumorigenesis and cancer progression.

SRSF1, the first identified member of this family, exemplifies the structural and functional characteristics of SRSFs. It contains an RS domain that facilitates interactions with other RS-domain-containing SFs, an RRM, and an RRM homology (RRMH) region that binds to pre-mRNAs at specific target motifs [44]. Research has shown that in SRSF1, the RRM1 preferentially binds to cytosines, while the RRMH recognizes a GGA motif using a non-canonical interaction mode centered on its α1-helix. Additionally, the flexible inter-RRM linker allows RRM1 to bind RNA sequences both upstream and downstream of the RRMH-binding site, underscoring the complexity of SRSF1’s role in splicing regulation. Notably, SRSF1 is frequently overexpressed in breast cancer cells, driving aberrant cell proliferation, regulating autophagy, modulating cell cycle checkpoints, and altering histone modifications. Through distinct modes of AS regulation, SRSF1 promotes the production of oncogenic splicing isoforms. For instance, SRSF1 promotes the skipping of exon 11 in RON, exons 2/3 in BIM, and exon 6b in S6K1. These isoforms respectively activate cancer invasion, block apoptosis pathways, and increase mTORC1 activity [20]. Conversely, SRSF1 also promotes the inclusion of exon 3 in PTPMT1, exon 13b in Mnk2b, and exon 12a in BIN1, thereby activating the PI3K/AKT pathway, oncogenic transformation, and c-Myc activity [19]. These findings highlight the dual role of SRSF1 in promoting both oncogenic and tumor-suppressive splicing events, depending on the specific isoforms generated.

Beyond SRSF1, other members of the SRSF family also exhibit significant regulatory effects in breast cancer. SRSF2 regulates the AS of the MBD2 gene, which encodes methyl-CpG binding protein 2, promoting stemness and metastasis in breast cancer cells [21, 22]. SRSF3 is aberrantly expressed in both HER2-dependent and HER2-independent cells. In HER2 + breast cancer cells, knockdown of SRSF3 leads to a switch from the oncogenic Δ16HER2 to the p100 HER2 mRNAs, which inhibits HER2 signaling [23]. In triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), SRSF3 depletion suppresses cancer progression partially through the regulation of PAR3 and NUMB splicing [24]. SRSF5 overexpression facilitates lymph node metastasis by promoting CD44 variant splicing and cooperating with SRSF1 to enhance the anti-apoptotic Mcl-1 isoforms in MCF-7 and MDA-MB-231 cell lines [25]. These examples illustrate the diverse roles of SRSFs in breast cancer, emphasizing their potential as therapeutic targets.

hnRNPs

HnRNPs are SFs that contain RNA-binding motifs and auxiliary domains, which vary in number and type. The RNA binding motifs include RRMs, RGG (arginine-glycine-glycine) motifs, and KH (K homology) domains, while auxiliary domains include glycine-rich and other domains [45]. The overall effects of hnRNPs on cancer progression are complex and multifaceted, as they can both promote oncogenic processes and support tumor-suppressive mechanisms.

The effects of hnRNPH1 on cancer progression are particularly intricate. For example, hnRNPH1 upregulates oncogenic variants such as isoforms containing MADD exon 16 or RON exon 11 [27], which can drive tumor growth and metastasis. However, it also promotes pro-apoptotic splice variants like Bcl-xS [26] and decreases the level of oncogenic Δ16 (exon 16 skipping) isoforms of HER2 in breast cancer cells [23]. This dual role underscores the complexity of hnRNPH1’s impact on cancer progression.

HnRNPA1 knockdown alters CD44 mRNA isoforms, decreasing CD44v6-10 and c5v6v8v9v10c6 while increasing CD44v6, thereby promoting apoptosis and reducing metastasis in MDA-MB-231 cells [28]. Higher arginine methylation of hnRNPA1 can lead to aberrant AS and tumor growth in breast cancer [46]. Moreover, hnRNPA1 collaborates with RNA-binding protein LIN28 to control immune response gene expression [47], highlighting its role in modulating the tumor microenvironment.

HnRNPA2B1 has four splicing isoforms: B1 (including all exons), A2 (excluding exon 2), B1b (excluding exon 9), and A2b (excluding exons 2 and 9), with A2 and B1 being the most abundant in human cells [29]. HnRNPA2B1 inhibits the association of Bcl-x pre-mRNA with the Bcl-xS-promoting factor Sam68, suppressing the expression of pro-apoptotic Bcl-xS [30]. Importantly, hnRNPA2B1 specifically recognizes m6A modifications deposited by METTL3 and METTL16 to regulate downstream AS events [48]. Overexpression of hnRNPA2B1 in breast cancer cells is associated with metastatic signatures, including increased migration, invasion, clonogenicity, and colony size [49].

HnRNPM governs epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) by controlling CD44 splicing [10]. Genome-wide deep sequencing analysis shows that hnRNPM enhances the TGF-β signaling pathway and precisely controls the conversion of CD44 splice isoforms during EMT in a mesenchymal-specific manner [31]. Interestingly, hnRNPM and ESRP1 co-regulate a portion of AS events, especially in the generation of cassette exons as they harbor common GU-rich binding sites [34], influencing several key processes in breast cancer, including proliferation, migration, and stemness. Previous perspectives indicated that ESRP1 and hnRNPM exhibit antagonistic roles in regulating EMT through AS of key genes in breast cancer progression [34]. A well-documented example is their opposing effects on the renowned CD44 splicing switch: ESRP1 promotes the inclusion of variable exons (v6–v10) to generate the epithelial-enriched CD44v isoform, whereas hnRNPM favors constitutive exon selection to produce the mesenchymal-associated CD44s isoform [34]. Notably, their antagonism extends to other cancer-related AS events. In SPAG9 and ZMYND8, hnRNPM-driven inclusion of SPAG9 exon 24 (pro-metastatic isoform) and skipping of ZMYND8 exon 22 (anti-apoptotic isoform) is correlated with poor survival, whereas ESRP1’s opposing splicing patterns associate with favorable outcomes [32]. Intriguingly, hnRNPM can paradoxically suppress EMT in specific contexts. For instance, when hnRNPM co-regulates cassette exons with ESRP1 and concordantly controls the same splicing event, such as exon inclusion or skipping, hnRNPM can inhibit EMT in coordination with ESRP1 [34]. This reveals a more complex interplay between hnRNPM and ESRP1 in splicing regulation, which goes beyond simple antagonism.

HnRNPs K, F, and H regulate apoptotic splicing switches, as their knockdown biases Mcl-1 splicing towards pro-apoptotic isoforms (Mcl-1s, exon 2 skipped) in breast cancer cells, underscoring their role in survival signaling [33].

ESRP1

Epithelial splicing regulatory protein 1 (ESRP1) is a key regulator of AS events in breast cancer, influencing the expression of both metabolic and EMT-related genes [50]. This dual role bridges splicing dysregulation with metabolic reprogramming and metastatic plasticity, highlighting its multifaceted impact on cancer progression.

ESRP1 overexpression significantly increases the expression of key metabolic enzymes, such as SCD1 (Stearoyl-CoA Desaturase 1) and FASN (Fatty Acid Synthase), in ER+ breast cancer cell lines with tamoxifen resistance [32]. These metabolic changes are crucial for cancer progression: FASN maintains membrane function, promotes DNA replication, and prevents cell cycle arrest and apoptosis [51], while SCD1 desaturates stearic acids to generate oleic acids, enhancing cell growth, ERK 1/2 activation, and β-catenin nuclear translocation [52]. In addition to these lipid metabolism-related enzymes, ESRP1 overexpression also upregulates PHGDH (phosphoglycerate dehydrogenase) in ER+ breast cancers, a critical enzyme involved in serine and NADPH synthesis, thus promoting cell proliferation [50, 53]. Intriguingly, previous studies have illustrated that PHGDH is overexpressed in 70% of ER- breast cancer cells that exhibit innate tamoxifen resistance [54].

ESRP1’s regulatory role extends beyond metabolism to cancer cell adhesion and polarity [32], and it affects breast cancer metastasis in a complex manner. On the one hand, ESRP1 acts as an EMT inhibitor by promoting the shift from EMT-required CD44s to CD44v [34]. On the other hand, ESRP1 upregulates circANKS1B, which enhances lymph node metastasis [55]. This dual function of ESRP1 can partly explain why its knockdown has not shown significant effects on EMT in ER+ breast cancers [50]. Furthermore, as previously mentioned in the context of hnRNPM, the synergistic effect of hnRNPM might compensate for the loss of ESRP1 at their concordant exons, resulting in EMT inhibition [34].

In summary, the dysregulation of SFs significantly contributes to the development and progression of breast cancer through the generation of oncogenic splicing isoforms. Understanding the specific mechanisms by which these factors influence splicing events can provide valuable insights for developing targeted therapies to combat breast cancer.

Structural and biomolecular modifications in alternative splicing

Recent advances have underscored the pivotal roles of spatial chromatin organization and modifications across diverse biomolecules, including DNA, RNA, and proteins, in regulating AS. Collectively, these multidimensional layers contribute to the intricate landscape of splicing dysregulation and significantly impact the progression of breast cancer.

At the epigenetic level, DNA methylation acts as a critical bridge linking the transcriptional elongation kinetics to specific AS outcomes in breast cancer. Intragenic DNA methylation sites can recruit methyl-sensitive adapter proteins, which act as physical roadblocks to promote RNA Pol II pausing. A hallmark example is the PKM gene: DNMT3B-mediated hypermethylation at exon 10 facilitates the recruitment of the methyl-sensitive protein BORIS. This binding induces Pol II enrichment at the locus, driving a splicing switch from the normal PKM1 to the cancer-specific PKM2 isoform, which is essential for sustaining the Warburg effect and pro-tumorigenic growth in breast cancer cells [56]. Beyond these localized covalent modifications, the higher-order spatial architecture of the genome provides a crucial, dynamic layer of regulatory control. Recent studies have revealed that 3D chromatin organization is dynamically reorganized in breast cancer [13, 14]. Importantly, this reorganization not only influences gene expression programs but also acts as a critical spatial regulator of RNA processing. By reshaping the physical proximity of genomic loci and forming intragenic chromatin loops, this dynamic architecture directly alters local Pol II elongation kinetics to determine context-specific alternative splicing outcomes in breast cancer [57, 58].Therefore, these structural rearrangements redefine the physical environment in which the transcriptional and splicing machineries operate, mechanistically coupling dynamic transcription with oncogenic AS. Collectively, these findings underscore that epigenetic regulation of splicing is closely intertwined with 3D chromatin remodeling in breast cancer progression and resistance.

The field of epitranscriptomics, focusing on RNA modifications, has advanced significantly due to the development of high-throughput sequencing technologies. RNA modifications can affect various aspects of RNA function and homeostasis [59]. N6-methyladenosine (m6A) stands out as the most prevalent and functionally consequential internal modification on eukaryotic mRNA [60], particularly in its capacity to orchestrate co-transcriptional AS. In breast cancer, m6A dynamics serve as a molecular rheostat for splicing machinery recruitment through two distinct mechanistic axes. First, as a cis-acting signal, the core methyltransferase METTL3 catalyzes m6A deposition at or near splice sites. These marks are recognized by “reader” proteins which directly recruit or stabilize SFs (e.g., SRSF3) at the pre-mRNA, thereby promoting the inclusion of oncogenic exons and driving malignant phenotypes like hyper-proliferation and EMT [60]. Second, m6A exerts trans-regulatory effects by modulating the expression of the splicing machinery itself. For instance, METTL3-mediated m6A modification in the 3′UTR of MYC mRNA enhances its translational efficiency or stability, leading to the upregulation of downstream SFs such as SRSF11, which further amplifies the cancer-associated splicing landscape [15]. Conversely, the demethylase FTO acts as a critical “eraser” that disrupts this oncogenic splicing program. By removing m6A marks from the transcripts of tumor suppressor genes, FTO interferes with the recognition of pro-apoptotic splice sites. A prime example is the regulation of BIM splicing; FTO-mediated demethylation shifts the splicing balance away from pro-apoptotic isoforms, thereby conferring apoptosis resistance to breast cancer cells and promoting therapeutic escape [61].

Arginine methylation is a major post-translational modification found on histones and RNA-binding proteins, catalyzed by the protein arginine methyltransferase (PRMT) family. Notably, PRMTs are frequently dysregulated in breast cancer [62]. These enzymes can undergo AS themselves and also modulate the methylation status of various SFs, leading to disordered pre-mRNA splicing patterns. For instance, PRMT1, one of the most extensively studied PRMTs, has an alternatively spliced isoform called PRMT1v2 that promotes breast cancer cell survival and invasion [63]. Furthermore, PRMT1-mediated arginine methylation of the splicing factor SRSF1 alters its steric conformation and RNA-binding affinity, which subsequently enhances the inclusion of exons in genes such as AURKA and RAD1, contributing to breast cancer progression and drug resistance [64]. Similarly, PRMT5 regulates AS by methylating hnRNPs (e.g., hnRNPA1) and Sm proteins, modulating their protein-protein interactions which are essential for spliceosome assembly [65]. Arginine methylation of hnRNPA1, catalyzed by PRMT4, PRMT5, and PRMT7, further promotes the growth of MCF-7 breast cancer cells by influencing the AS of downstream genes, including PPARA, SREBF2, RAB27B, and WDPCP [46].

Non-coding RNA-mediated regulation on alternative splicing

Non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs) have emerged as major regulators of tumorigenesis, and expanding research reveals their roles as spatial organizers of splicing complexes in cancers, sequestering factors or masking splice sites to redirect isoform outcomes [66]. In breast cancer, both circular RNAs (circRNAs) and long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) show significant effects on the modulation of AS, serving as splicing factor decoys or architectural guides for spliceosome assembly.

CircRNAs have emerged as essential regulators in tumorigenesis, exerting their influence through a variety of mechanisms [67], such as acting as miRNA sponges, interacting with RBPs, regulating transcription, and serving as templates for translation. These versatile molecules can sequester miRNAs, thereby relieving miRNA-mediated repression of target SFs. For instance, circFBXL5 acts as a miR-660 sponge in breast cancer, facilitating cancer progression via the circFBXL5/miR-660/SRSF6 axis [68]. Beyond miRNA sponging, circRNAs interact with SFs to modulate their functions. In breast cancer, the highly expressed circRPAP2 was demonstrated to interfere with the binding between the splicing factor SRSF1 and PTK2 pre-mRNA, blocking SRSF1-mediated exon inclusion in PTK2, further impeding cell proliferation and migration [69]. Another study pioneered the exploration of antisense circRNA-mediated AS in breast cancer metastasis. The antisense circSCRIB, derived from antisense splicing of one intron and two exons of tumor suppressor SCRIB, is highly enriched in breast cancer specimens [70]. The study suggests that antisense circSCRIB can modulate the expression and function of its parental transcripts through indirect mechanisms, such as affecting pre-mRNA splicing and mRNA translation, thereby promoting oncogenic phenotypes. Together, these studies significantly enrich our understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying circRNA-mediated AS in breast cancer.

In the realm of lncRNAs, a typical example is DSCAM-AS1, which is overexpressed in breast cancer and strongly associated with ER expression [71, 72]. DSCAM-AS1 may regulate AS by acting as a scaffold for hnRNPL to specific pre-mRNAs, enforcing exon skipping and 3’UTR events. Notably, DSCAM-AS1 knockdown causes significant changes in AS via physical contact with hnRNPL, restoring tumor-suppressive isoforms, thereby inhibiting cell proliferation and tamoxifen resistance in breast cancer [73]. Another lncRNA, MALAT1, primarily localizes to nuclear speckles (NS) and is associated with a poorly differentiated and aggressive phenotype of breast cancer [74]. The mutant p53-ID4 complex hijacks the oncogenic MALAT1 to act as a molecular scaffold, indirectly reprogramming VEGFA AS by recruiting SRSF1, ultimately promoting the production of pro-angiogenic VEGFA isoforms [75]. Additionally, MALAT1 can be induced in response to hypoxia and further regulate hypoxia-responsive AS, mainly through promoting the recruitment of oncogenic splicing factor SRSF1 and the formation of SRSF1 condensates near NS, further enhancing the AS of pre-mRNAs such as CD44 and GALT [76]. Antisense lncRNAs can also orchestrate the AS of their cognate pre-mRNAs through sequence-specific interactions, acting as spatial guides for splicing site selection and spliceosome assembly. For instance, the lncRNA ZEB2-AS1 can bind to ZEB2 pre-mRNA, masking splice sites and preventing spliceosome recognition, leading to intron retention in the ZEB2 5’UTR [77]. The retained intron harbors an internal ribosome entry site (IRES), which can be bound by ribosomes to drive ZEB2 protein synthesis [66], thereby activating EMT and metastatic dissemination in breast cancer.

Functional effects of alternative splicing dysregulation in breast cancer

AS is a critical mechanism that can significantly alter gene splicing patterns in breast cancer, leading to changes in protein structure and function. Aberrant AS events disrupt cellular homeostasis and reconfigure signaling networks, driving multiple facets of cancer progression [78], such as unchecked proliferation, stemness maintenance, metastatic dissemination, cell death regulation, treatment resistance, and metabolic reprogramming. This section systematically dissects the functional consequences of AS dysregulation in breast cancer, with a focus on key examples and underlying mechanisms (Fig. 2).

Functional impact of alternative splicing dysregulation in breast cancer. Aberrant AS events affect critical cancer-related processes including proliferation, stemness, metastasis, cell death, therapy resistance, and metabolic reprogramming, partly through the dysregulation of splice variants of key genes

Proliferation and cell cycle control

AS can accelerate tumor proliferation by modulating cell cycle checkpoints and oncogenic signaling cascades, highlighting its critical roles in breast cancer development.

Cyclin D1, a key regulatory protein in cell cycle progression, primarily drives the transition from the G1 phase to the S phase. Overexpression of cyclin D1 is oncogenic and linked to uncontrolled proliferation in various cancers, such as breast cancer, lymphoma, and squamous cell carcinoma. AS has been reported to regulate cyclin D1 expression by generating pro- or anti-tumor splice variants that modulate transcriptional activation or oncogenic signaling (e.g., PI3K/Akt), thereby influencing cancer proliferation. For instance, downregulation of NFIC exon 9/10 skipping decreases the expression of tumor suppressor CTF5, which normally suppresses cyclin D1 transcription, thereby fueling TNBC growth [79]. Similarly, IL-7δ5, an exon 5-skipped IL-7 splicing variant, promotes cell proliferation by activating PI3K/Akt pathway and accelerating G1-to-G2/M phase progression while increasing cyclin D1 expression [80]. Conversely, the PRMT2β splice variant decreases cyclin D1 expression and suppresses Akt signaling to inhibit breast cancer progression [81].

PI3Kδ-S (exon 20-skipped) isoforms hyperactivate AKT/mTOR signaling pathway, enabling uncontrolled proliferation in breast cancer [82]. The HNRNPU-DDX5 complex blocks intron retention to prevent nonsense-mediated decay (NMD)-mediated degradation of MCM10, thereby activating the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway and enhancing TNBC proliferation [83]. Additionally, SNRPB2 knockdown promotes exon 6 skipping in MDM4 pre-mRNA, forming MDM4-S transcript and downregulating its protein level, further suppressing Rb1 expression and E2F1 signaling [84]. These examples illustrate how AS can modulate key signaling pathways to promote cell growth.

Structural alterations in splicing isoforms further modulate proliferation dynamics. Exon 3 inclusion leads to a shift from PTPMT1-S to PTPMT1-L, which increases the Ki-67 index and boosts P-AKT and C-MYC expression, facilitating breast cancer cell proliferation, whereas PTPMT1-S exerts opposite activities [19]. Another key example involves the RPS6KB1 gene, which encodes the mTOR substrate S6K1, a critical regulator of translation and cell growth. SRSF1-mediated AS of RPS6KB1 produces two distinct isoforms: RPS6KB1-1, a full-length protein with canonical function, and RPS6KB1-2, a truncated isoform generated through the inclusion of three cassette exons (6a, 6b, and 6c). This structural alteration in RPS6KB1-2 results in partial loss of the kinase domain, enabling differential activation of downstream mTORC1 signaling pathways that sustain cell proliferation and tumor growth [20].

Stemness maintenance

Tumors contain heterogeneous cells despite their clonal nature, with a small subset possessing long-term self-renewal and differentiation plasticity, known as cancer stem cells (CSCs), which are critical for tumor recurrence and therapy resistance [85]. Previous studies have highlighted the pivotal regulatory effects of AS on CSC maintenance, and central to this process is the AS-mediated remodeling of CSC-associated surface markers and signaling hubs.

A paradigm of this regulatory axis is the CD44 gene, which encodes a non-kinase transmembrane glycoprotein critical for CSC identity [86]. The intracellular tail of CD44 can undergo conformational changes and activate downstream pathways such as Ras, MAPK, and PI3K via interactions with extracellular ligands like hyaluronic acid, thereby enhancing cell proliferation, adhesion, and migration. Human CD44 pre-mRNA has 10 constitutive exons (c1 ~ c10) and 9 variable exons (v2 ~ v10). In breast cancer cells, a few mature CD44 mRNAs still retain part of variable exons, the most common of which are v8 ~ v10 [87]. Notably, a switch from CD44v towards CD44s correlates with enhanced self-renewal and stemness, with CD44s isoform mediating CSC-like properties through activating PDGFRβ/Stat3 signaling pathway [86]. Moreover, CD44s upregulates the transcription factor Zeb1, which represses genes involved in cell-cell adhesion and cell polarity such as ESRP1, thus in turn promoting CD44s production and forming a positive feedback loop [88]. Another layer of complexity arises from integrin α6 splicing. This integrin mediates adhesion between cells and extracellular laminin, and undergoes AS to generate two isoforms, termed α6Aβ1 and α6Bβ1. In TNBC, a switch from α6Aβ1 to α6Bβ1 isoforms enhances laminin adhesion via extracellular domain remodeling [89]. This isoform shift not only strengthens CSC-matrix interactions but also activates GLI1, a Hedgehog pathway effector that reinforces stemness and tumor-initiating capacity.

Together, these AS events position splicing dysregulation as a master orchestrator of the CSC niche in breast cancer, balancing adhesion, signaling, and transcriptional reprogramming to maintain the stem-like phenotype.

Metastatic spread

Metastasis, the leading cause of mortality in breast cancer [53], exhibits organ tropism, with bone being the most common site of metastasis, followed by lung, liver, and brain. Significantly, metastasis to the brain is refractory and associated with low survival rates due to its unique immune landscape [90]. In the widely accepted metastatic cascade, individual cancer epithelial cells within the primary lesion acquire mesenchymal characteristics and the ability to intravasate into the bloodstream. A small percentage of surviving cancer cells eventually reach distant organs and undergo mesenchymal-to-epithelial transition to form metastatic lesions. This theory holds that EMT is a key process, which endows breast cancer cells with mesenchymal properties of migration and invasion, enabling them to survive in the circulatory system and form colonies in distant organs. Angiogenesis, the process of new blood vessel formation activated under hypoxia conditions, enables tissue growth and repair, which is a key feature of cancer progression [91]. Recent studies have revealed that AS intricately orchestrates metastatic dissemination through modulating EMT and vascular remodeling in breast cancer.

A central player for EMT is the CD44 gene, whose isoform switching between CD44v (variable exon-containing) and CD44s (standard) exhibits context-dependent roles. While a switch from CD44v towards CD44s generally promotes EMT and metastasis [88, 92], CD44v8-10 overexpression paradoxically enhances lung metastasis based on in vivo experiment [93]. A plausible explanation for these conflicting views is that, in addition to EMT-dependent metastasis, some epithelial cell clusters can actively or passively enter the vascular space and survive in the blood by sustained interepithelial connections, without undergoing EMT [86, 94]. This duality underscores the coexistence of CD44-mediated EMT-dependent and EMT-independent metastatic routes.

Further amplifying invasiveness, the hyperactive RAC1B isoform, generated by RAC1 exon 3b inclusion with additional 19 amino acids [95], is significantly increased in the most undifferentiated and invasive breast cancer cell lines. The RAC1B: RAC1 expression ratio is predictive of the cells’ invasive potential, and RAC1B regulators are potential therapeutic drugs for basal-like breast cancers [96]. Similarly, MBD2 is required for the expression of pro-metastatic genes uPA and MMP2 in breast cancer [97], and its splice isoform MBD2a activates the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, promoting metastasis in hypoxia-driven breast cancer [22]. Concurrently, AS disrupts epithelial integrity, where the overexpression of an E-cadherin splice isoform lacking the first 34 base pairs of exon 14 weakens cell-cell adhesion and promotes EMT [98]. SNRPA1, an RNA-binding protein overexpressed in lung-metastasized TNBC, can directly interact with RNA structural elements to promote exon 31 inclusion in PLEC pre-mRNAs, enhancing metastatic ability of breast cancer cells [16]. Additionally, oncogenic splicing further subverts tumor suppression. The tumor suppressor KLF6, a transcription factor governing cell proliferation, differentiation, and survival, is frequently inactivated in cancers through mutations or deletions. However, tumors can bypass its protective effects via AS, generating the oncogenic isoform KLF6-SV1. Elevated KLF6-SV1 expression has been identified in breast cancer and is associated with increased metastatic potential and poor survival [99]. This splice variant arises from the use of an alternative 5’ss, causing a frameshift that introduces 21 novel amino acids while eliminating all three zinc finger DNA-binding domains critical for its tumor-suppressive function, thereby promoting EMT and driving breast cancer metastasis. AS can also cause the production of functional ncRNAs. Alternative 3’ splice site selection in exon 12 of PNUTS pre-mRNA generates oncogenic lncRNA-PNUTS, which serves as a competitive sponge for miR-205, further driving EMT in breast cancer [100].

Conversely, AKAP8 impedes EMT and breast cancer metastasis by acting as both a competitive inhibitor of hnRNPM and a regulator of CLSTN1. On the one hand, AKAP8 antagonizes the splicing activity of hnRNPM through protein-protein interaction and binding to hnRNPM RNA, resulting in decreased metastasis [101]. On the other hand, AKAP8, a splicing factor targeting CLSTN1 pre-mRNAs, can favor the AS isoform switch of CLSTN1 from L-type to S-type. CLSTN1-S antagonizes EMT, while CLSTN1-L is required for cell survival during EMT, highlighting that AKAP8 suppresses tumor metastasis by inhibiting EMT-associated isoform switching in AS.

NMD evasion mechanisms can regulate the expression of splice variants, further deepening the mechanistic complexity in AS-mediated metastatic progression. AS can introduce premature termination codons (PTCs), triggering NMD when ribosomes encounter these termination signals, leading to the formation of a decay-inducing complex and mRNA degradation [102]. Conversely, specific splicing events, such as SRSF1-mediated DCUN1D5 exon 4 inclusion and U2SURP-driven SAT1 exon 3 skipping [103], bypass NMD to stabilize these pro-metastatic transcripts in TNBC, predicting poor prognosis.

AS concurrently remodels tumor vasculature to support metastatic growth through modulating pro-angiogenic factors. Vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) level and structure are affected by AS and play important roles in breast cancer development. The VEGF-A splice variant highly promotes angiogenesis and is overexpressed in invasive breast cancer cells, whereas isoform VEGF165b exhibits anti-angiogenic effects [91]. Another vascularization activator in breast cancer is Cyr61 (cysteine rich 61). The Cyr61 mRNA without intron 3 can be fully translated into an active protein with pro-angiogenic capability, promoting breast cancer invasiveness, while the intron-retention-type Cyr61 mRNA cannot. Notably, hypoxia strongly shifts Cyr61 splicing toward an intron-skipped isoform in MDA-MB-231 and T47D cells [104], creating a feedforward loop that sustains nutrient supply for metastatic colonies.

These insights underscore the complexity of AS in metastatic cascades and angiogenic regulation, highlighting its pervasive role in breast cancer progression.

Cell death regulation

AS balances pro- and anti-death signals in breast cancer, revealing its multifaceted roles in cell fate decisions.

Several SFs, such as SF3B1, SRSF3, hnRNPH1, hnRNPA2B1, and SF3A2, are associated with apoptosis of breast cancer cells. Knockdown of SF3B1 induces apoptosis through aberrant splicing [42]. The SRSF3 exon 4-included variants harbor an in-frame stop codon, rendering them susceptible to NMD-mediated decay [105], which can be induced by PTX treatment and ensures tight control over the expression of apoptosis-related transcripts. HnRNPH1 upregulates while hnRNPA2B1 downregulates the expression of pro-apoptotic Bcl-xS [26, 30]. Another splicing factor, SF3A2, controls the 5’ss use in MKRN1 pre-mRNA AS, promoting the inclusion of the short exon 5 to create the full-length oncogenic MKRN1 isoform, which lowers FADD levels and inhibits both extrinsic and intrinsic apoptosis in TNBC [106].

Splice variants of receptors and regulators further modulate survival. The full-length HER4 variant encodes the CYT1 receptor, which may directly interact with PI3K and suppress apoptosis, while HER4 exon 26 skipping results in a receptor CYT2 lacking a PPXY motif required for HER4-PI3K direct interaction, altering survival signals [107]. MDM2, a ubiquitin ligase that degrades the tumor suppressor p53, is overexpressed in breast cancer cells. However, DOX treatment induces the generation of MDM2 isoforms, including MDM2-Δ5 and P2-MDM2-10, which paradoxically promote apoptosis, suggesting context-dependent effects [108]. PHF5A is an important component of SF3B and a histone modification reader of the FASTK promoter sequence. PHF5A ablation increases the expression of a truncated FASTK isoform, sensitizing cells to Fas-mediated apoptosis [109].

AS significantly impacts breast cancer apoptotic outcomes through its modulation of the BCL2L12 gene, a member of the BCL-2 family. This gene undergoes extensive splicing, producing numerous variants with diverse functions: BCL2L12 variant v.4 suggests a potential role in resistance to apoptosis, whereas variant v.2 may act protectively by modulating apoptotic pathways favorably, thereby correlating with extended disease-free survival (DFS) in breast cancer patients [110]. Additionally, skipping of exons 2 and 3 in BIM produces isoforms lacking the Bcl-2-binding BH domain, effectively blocking the pro-apoptotic function of the BIM pathway [111].

Survivin isoforms play opposing roles in breast cancer: survivin is anti-apoptotic, whereas survivin-2B, a splice variant containing an inserted 69 bp exon and downregulated in aggressive tumors, antagonizes survivin’s pro-survival function [112]. AKT3-S472, the splice variant without the S472 phosphorylation site, promotes TNBC apoptosis by downregulating Bim and BAX [113].

Beyond apoptosis, AS can also regulate non-canonical cell death pathways. Ferroptosis is a type of cell death different from apoptosis, necrosis, and autophagy. Increased FMRP/hnRNPM interaction promotes SLC7A11 exon skipping, forming the SLC7A11-S splicing variant, which induces ferroptosis resistance in breast cancer cells, indicating poor prognosis in patients [114]. Autophagy is a significant regulatory and catabolic process activated by various stressful situations, including hypoxia. BNIP3L pre-mRNA splicing produces BNIP3L-F, promoting autophagy under hypoxia, while exon 1 skipping produces BNIP3L-Δ1, inhibiting autophagy under normoxia [57].

Treatment resistance

AS processes enable pro-oncogenic genes to gain new capabilities, allowing cancer cells to resist treatment impacts, such as chemotherapy, anti-HER2 targeting, and endocrine therapy.

Chemotherapy is the standard post-surgery treatment aimed at preventing breast cancer recurrence and metastasis. AS influences chemotherapeutic resistance in several ways. (1) SF mutations: A short isoform of SRSF3 lacking exon 4, which disrupts the normal function of SRSF3, confers paclitaxel resistance [105]. (2) Aberrant expression of SFs: SRSF3 overexpression promotes GR (glucocorticoid receptor) α isoform expression, which contributes to chemotherapy resistance [115]. HnRNPH1 binds to cis-acting regions in ITPR1 pre-mRNAs, promoting autophagosome accumulation and paclitaxel sensitivity [116], whereas TRA2A can activate AS of cancer-related genes, increasing paclitaxel resistance [117]. In TNBC, overexpressed splicing factor SF3A2 boosts cisplatin sensitivity through promoting the generation of oncogenic isoform MKRN1-T1 [106], highlighting the context-dependent effects of SFs. (3) SF activity: Highly activated SRPK1 enhances SRSFs activation and consequent overexpression of anti-apoptotic variants of downstream genes, such as Bcl-xL and MCL-1 L in cisplatin-resistant BRCA cells [118]. (4) Aberrant splicing of other genes: Exon 5 inclusion in ECT2 [119] and C-terminally truncated CCND1b promote doxorubicin resistance in BRCA cells [120]. Additionally, BRCA1-Δ11q, with a cryptic 5’ss in exon 11, can confer cisplatin and olaparib resistance [121]. MBD2c promotes mitochondrial gene expression, resulting in cisplatin resistance in TNBC [122]. These examples highlight the diverse mechanisms through which AS can modulate chemotherapeutic resistance.

Approximately 15–20% of breast cancers show HER2 overexpression or gene amplification, and anti-HER2 therapies have significantly improved patient survival and altered disease progression. However, numerous HER2-positive breast cancers express HER2Δ16, which is associated with enhanced metastatic and oncogenic properties and trastuzumab resistance [123]. Moreover, SRSF3 elevates HER2Δ16 expression by suppressing its switch to other isoforms [24], further exacerbating anti-HER2 therapy resistance.

Breast cancer progression is significantly impacted by estrogen and ER. Blocking the estrogen signaling pathway in ER+ breast cancer patients has been a long-established treatment, but the treatment response is typically followed by tumor relapse with intrinsic resistance, partially driven by AS. The splice variant ERα36 controls estrogen-triggered nongenomic membrane signaling pathways and imparts 4-hydroxytamoxifen resistance in breast cancer treatment [123]. HMGA1a facilitates ERα exon skipping, producing the ERα46 isoform, which antagonizes the proliferative full-length ERα66. Targeting the RNA-binding activity of HMGA1a offers potential therapeutic strategies to modulate AS, improve tamoxifen sensitivity, and counteract tumor growth [124]. Besides AS of ER, overexpression of CD44v6, Δ16HER2, and hnRNPA2B1 can also result in tamoxifen resistance in ERα + breast cancer cells [49, 123, 125]. Similarly, MSI1-mediated inclusion of exon 7.2 in ALDOA mRNA contributes to TAM resistance in breast cancer [126], linking metabolism to treatment resistance.

Metabolic reprogramming

AS serves as a pivotal driver of metabolic reprogramming in breast cancer, enabling tumors to meet the heightened bioenergetic demands of rapid proliferation and adapt to microenvironmental stress. Below, we synthesize key findings and their mechanistic implications.

A striking example is the PKM2 isoform, a product of PKM exon 10 inclusion [56], which not only enhances aerobic glycolysis but also stabilizes c-Myc to transcriptionally upregulate splicing factor SRSF10. This creates a feedforward loop favoring oncogenic isoforms such as MDM4-FL and Bcl-xL, linking metabolic rewiring to apoptosis evasion [127]. Beyond glycolytic enzymes, AS modulates metabolic plasticity through non-canonical regulators. The CD44ICD isoform, for instance, elevates glucose consumption and lactate production while reducing ROS levels, thereby balancing energy production with oxidative stress mitigation [128]. Similarly, the Lonp1 ISO2 variant enhances glycolytic activity by elevating the expression of LDHA and GLUT1 [129], illustrating how splicing diversifies metabolic dependencies. This glycolytic shift is further amplified by the insulin receptor (IR) isoform IR-A, generated by exon 11 skipping, which hyperactivates glucose uptake and lactate production, establishing a glycolytic phenotype that fuels tumor growth across breast cancer stages [130]. Hypoxia-driven splicing events further adapt tumors to metabolic stress. The MARCO-TST splice variant stabilizes HIF-1α, contributing to the hypoxic microenvironment in TNBC [131], while the MBD2c splice isoform translocates into mitochondria, activating mtDNA transcription and boosting mitochondrial respiration in TNBC [122]. Notably, AS extends its regulatory reach to lipid metabolism. In luminal B breast cancer, exon 8 skipping of CERS2 disrupts ceramide synthesis and decreases the level of very-long-chain ceramides, a metabolic alteration correlated with poor prognosis and enhanced proliferative/migratory capacity [132].

These AS-driven events, spanning glycolysis, lipid metabolism, and mitochondrial dynamics, establish a tumor-permissive metabolic state, ensuring energy flux and biomass synthesis under fluctuating nutrient conditions. This intricate interplay underscores AS as a master regulator of cancer bioenergetics, capable of fine-tuning metabolic pathways to sustain malignancy.

Collectively, splice variant rewiring equips tumors with phenotypic plasticity to circumvent therapeutic pressure and colonize distant organs. Targeting spliceosome components or oncogenic isoforms represents a promising therapeutic strategy. Future studies must resolve isoform-specific roles and elucidate context-dependent splicing networks to advance precision oncology.

Clinical implications of alternative splicing in breast cancer

Aberrant AS plays a pivotal role in breast cancer progression, offering dual utility as a source of diagnostic/prognostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets. This section synthesizes the clinical relevance of splicing alterations and emerging strategies to exploit these defects.

Global landscape of alternative splicing in breast cancer

Recent advancements in high-throughput sequencing technologies, particularly RNA sequencing (RNA-seq), have enabled comprehensive profiling of the transcriptome in breast cancer, revealing widespread AS events. For instance, using RNA-seq, Eswaran et al. have systematically investigated the splicing patterns of three major breast cancer subtypes: TNBC, non-TNBC, and HER2-positive breast cancer, revealing and validating several subtype-specific differentially spliced genes and splice isoforms that were previously unrecognized [133]. The study also identified that exon skipping and intron retention are the most frequently observed splice events in breast cancer. A recent large-scale data analysis study utilized RNA-seq data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) breast cancer cohorts, identifying 184,800 AS events after data filtering and providing a detailed landscape of AS dysregulation across different subtypes [134]. The findings highlight the significant heterogeneity in AS patterns among these subtypes, with distinct sets of genes undergoing AS events linked to specific molecular pathways and biological processes. Additionally, the combined analysis of RNA-seq and liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) can further contribute to the effective identification of novel peptide variants [135], enhancing biomarker discovery. Notably, the unique AS landscape of cancer cells versus normal cells provides a therapeutic window to selectively target malignancies.

Alternative splicing-derived diagnostic and prognostic biomarkers

The expression of AS isoforms of target genes can aid in diagnosing the malignancy level of breast cancer. For example, the Lamin A/C proteins are involved in various cellular functions, including apoptosis, cell cycle regulation, and chromatin organization. The Lamin C: Lamin A mRNA variant ratio is significantly higher in breast cancer tissues, making it a potential diagnostic marker across all stages of breast cancer [136]. A higher RAC1B: RAC1 splicing isoform ratio predicts a lower risk of breast cancer EMT and metastasis [96]. MBD2 is a biomarker of breast cancer invasiveness, as high MBD2a and low MBD2c levels in breast cancer indicate a higher degree of metastasis [22]. Additionally, differentially expressed splicing variants such as SPAG9 exon 24 inclusion and ZMYND8 exon 22 skipping can predict poor patient survival outcomes, serving as promising prognostic biomarkers [32]. The overexpression of splicing variant HIF-1αTAG with exon 14 skipping can be a prognostic biomarker predicting shorter metastasis-free survival and a significantly higher risk of relapse in high-grade ER- breast cancers [137].

Abnormal expression of some SFs can indicate different breast cancer prognoses. Overexpression of certain SFs, such as SRSFs, is associated with unfavorable outcomes. For instance, higher SRSF3 expression is linked to shorter survival time and poorer prognosis [24]. Conversely, higher expression of splicing factor AKAP8 predicts better distal metastasis-free survival and better prognosis [101]. Notably, some biomarkers are significant within particular subtypes of breast cancer. Overexpression of HNRNPA2B1 and ESRP1 indicates tamoxifen insensitivity in ER+ breast cancer [49, 50].

However, the nonspecific overexpression of splicing variant biomarkers in various diseases limits their clinical utility. For instance, splicing regulator lncRNA MALAT1 overexpression is a marker of poor prognosis in estrogen-dependent breast cancer, whereas it is also upregulated in diseases such as diabetes and kidney diseases [138].

Targeting aberrant alternative splicing in breast cancer therapy

AS is a potential therapeutic target for breast cancer. The dysregulation of AS contributes to abnormal gene expression in breast cancer, facilitating the transformation and progression of cancer cells. Moreover, breast cancer and normal cells exhibit distinct AS landscapes, so AS therapy may target the specific vulnerabilities of breast cancer cells while minimizing injury to normal cells. Abnormal expression or mutation of splicing factors, the abundance of aberrant mRNA transcripts, as well as AS-related aberrant biomolecular modifications serve as therapeutic targets in breast cancer (Fig. 3). Although a growing number of AS-directed therapeutic strategies have been developed, their translational potential varies considerably with respect to targeting specificity, clinical feasibility, and toxicity.

Targeting alternative splicing in breast cancer therapy. AS is emerging as a highly promising therapeutic avenue in breast cancer. Strategies targeting aberrant AS encompass small molecules targeting the core splicing machinery or biomolecular modifications, ASOs, CRISPR-Cas9, and PROTACs. These approaches are designed to correct splicing factor dysfunction, eliminate oncogenic isoforms, or leverage dependencies associated with aberrant splicing

Modulation of the core splicing machinery

Small-molecule modulators targeting core spliceosome components represent one of the most clinically advanced AS-directed strategies. For instance, the splicing modulator E7107 has been shown to successfully suppress the growth of basal-A TNBC cell lines and patient-derived xenograft models [139]. However, its broader clinical development was hindered by rare but severe toxicities, including ophthalmologic adverse events, which emerged during phase I clinical trials for advanced solid tumors [140]. Nevertheless, preclinical exploration of SF inhibitors in breast cancer continues to show promise. Mechanistically, compounds like the potent antitumor natural product FR901464 and its analog, spliceostatin A, stall spliceosome assembly by covalently binding to a nucleophilic zinc finger of the SF3B subunit [141]. This disruption of conserved splicing signatures allows spliceostatin A to selectively inhibit the growth of SF3B1-mutated breast cancer cells [35]. Similarly, Jerantinine A targets SF3B to induce the accumulation of mis-spliced pre-mRNAs, ultimately triggering apoptosis. However, the lack of splice event specificity remains a fundamental limitation of this class of agents. Because core splicing machinery is essential for normal cellular function, its inhibition leads to broad transcriptomic dysregulation, resulting in a narrow therapeutic window. In this context, naturally derived compounds with partial or context-dependent splicing modulatory activity may offer a complementary strategy. For instance, apigenin (4,5,7-trihydroxyflavone), a dietary flavonoid enriched in vegetables and fruits, has been shown to modulate TNBC-specific AS programs rather than inducing global spliceosome collapse [142].

Furthermore, to mitigate systemic toxicity and enhance precision, innovative delivery platforms have been developed. Apoferritin-encapsulated Jerantinine A utilizes a biocompatible nanocage to leverage receptor-mediated endocytosis via transferrin receptor 1 (TfR1) [143, 144]. Given that TfR1 is markedly overexpressed in breast cancer, particularly in aggressive ER-negative subtypes, this targeted delivery system significantly enhances tumor-specific uptake and potentiates cytotoxicity while sparing healthy tissues [144, 145].

Targeting biomolecular modifications

Epigenetic and post-transcriptional/post-translational modifications, particularly methylation of DNA, RNA, and proteins, play critical roles in breast cancer pathogenesis, drug resistance and stemness, providing novel diagnostic and prognostic markers, as well as therapeutic targets.

DNA methylation has been shown to directly influence splice site selection. DNMT3B-mediated DNA methylation at PKM exon 10 affects AS in breast cancer, promoting the Warburg effect and cancer growth. DNMT inhibitors, such as 5-Aza-2’-deoxycytidine, can reverse this effect and improve treatment outcomes [56]. Furthermore, combining DNMT inhibitors with chemotherapeutic agents has demonstrated synergistic efficacy in TNBC [146].

Targeting RNA modifications, such as m6A, has also shown potential in breast cancer treatment. A highly selective and orally active drug STM2457, which antagonizes the m6A writer METTL3 involved in AS regulation, has been shown to effectively suppress TNBC growth [147]. However, it remains unclear to what extent these therapeutic effects are mediated specifically through AS regulation versus broader RNA metabolic processes.

Protein arginine methyltransferases (PRMTs), especially PRMT5, regulate spliceosome assembly by symmetrically dimethylating Sm proteins, thereby influencing splice site selection. PRMT inhibitors have emerged as promising agents, with several in clinical trials. PRMT5 inhibitors, such as LLY283, significantly inhibit paclitaxel-resistant TNBC cells by inducing AURKB intron 5 retention and decreasing AURKB protein expression, causing mitosis perturbation [148]. In another study, pharmacological inhibition (using compounds such as EZM2302, GSK591, and SGC3027) and genetic ablation of PRMT4/5/7 have also exhibited anti-tumor effects [46]. The exploitation of PRMTs inhibitors provides a new route for breast cancer treatment.

Overall, while targeting biomolecular modifications offers an indirect yet powerful means of modulating AS, these approaches often lack splicing specificity and may exert pleiotropic effects beyond RNA processing, complicating their clinical application in breast cancer.

Antisense oligonucleotide-based interventions

ASOs are short, synthetic, chemically modified nucleic acids that can bind to RNA through complementary base pairing, modulating AS to exert therapeutic effects. While FDA-approved for treating diseases like Duchenne muscular dystrophy and spinal muscular atrophy, their application in cancer therapy remains preclinical [149]. In breast cancer cell lines or animal models, ASOs targeting AS events of genes involved in apoptosis (BCLX [150]), cell signaling (ERBB4 [107], STAT3 [151]), and DNA repair (BRCA1/2 [152]) have been demonstrated to be antitumorigenic. The effects of ASOs on AS modulation are mediated through several mechanisms.

First, ASOs can disrupt core splicing machinery components. For instance, SNRNP200-targeting ASOs inhibit tumor metabolism and synergize with immune checkpoint blockade (anti-PD-1) by enhancing intratumoral CD8 + T cell activity and suppressing regulatory T cells in glycolytic TNBC microenvironments [153].

Second, ASOs can be designed to directly modulate SFs or their transcript interactions. Specifically, ASOs can physically block regulatory sequences on SF transcripts to alter their expression. For example, ASOs that promote the inclusion of poison exons in TRA2B lead to widespread AS changes in downstream target transcripts, effectively decreasing cancer cell proliferation [154]. Alternatively, ASOs can block specific functional motifs on target mRNAs, preventing SF binding. The ASO SR-3 blocks the ESS motif of SRSF3 exon 4, increasing the proportion of the long isoform, thereby sensitizing tumor cells to paclitaxel (PTX) [105]. Furthermore, ASOs can disrupt interactions between SFs and intronic regions of oncogenes; SCRIB-targeting ASOs inhibit hnRNP A1 binding to SCRIB intron 15, suppressing the oncogenic SCRIB-S variant and effectively reversing ERK pathway activation to curb breast cancer metastasis [155]. Finally, ASOs can neutralize long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) that act as scaffolds or decoys for SFs. Targeting the lncRNA MALAT1, which regulates AS mainly by interacting with SFs such as SRSF1, induces a robust decline in tumor cell growth and metastasis in preclinical MMTV-PyMT mouse mammary carcinoma models [74].

Despite their high specificity, the clinical translation of ASOs in breast cancer remains limited by inefficient tumor delivery, susceptibility to nuclease degradation, and potential immune activation. Advances in delivery systems, such as nanoparticle-based carriers, will be essential to overcome these physical and immunological barriers. Meanwhile, to optimize target engagement and efficacy, artificial intelligence and machine learning are increasingly enabling the rational design of AS-switching ASOs. For instance, an ASO design strategy using XGBoost tree models trained on SF-binding and spliceosome assembly profiles can predict optimal ASO binding sites and utilizes SHAP/OOB and SF enrichment analysis to validate the SFs most likely to regulate the specific AS event [156].

CRISPR-Cas9-based therapeutic strategies

CRISPR-Cas9, a groundbreaking gene-editing platform, has emerged as a powerful strategy for targeted genomic disruption in numerous cancer types. By directly editing genomic loci that govern splicing, the lncRNA BC200, which is upregulated in ER+ breast cancer and contributes to oncogenic AS, can be successfully knocked out using CRISPR-Cas9. This deletion shifts splicing towards the pro-apoptotic Bcl-xS isoform, effectively suppressing tumor cell growth in preclinical models [30].

However, despite its conceptual precision at the DNA level, the clinical application of CRISPR-Cas9 in breast cancer remains highly challenging. Critical bottlenecks include inefficient and non-tumor-specific delivery vehicles, potential immunogenicity of the Cas endonuclease, and irreversible off-target genomic alterations. These barriers currently restrict its use primarily to preclinical and ex vivo studies [157].

PROTAC-mediated protein degradation

PROTACs (proteolysis-targeting chimeras) offer a transformative strategy for modulating AS in breast cancer. These bifunctional molecules consist of two ligands linked by a spacer: one binds the protein of interest (POI), and the other recruits an E3 ubiquitin ligase. This ternary complex triggers POI ubiquitination and subsequent proteasomal degradation [158]. Operating via an event-driven, catalytic mechanism, PROTACs can recycle and degrade multiple POI copies. This overcomes the occupancy-driven limitations of traditional inhibitors and expands the druggable proteome.

Although AS-targeting PROTACs in breast cancer remain nascent, preclinical models highlight their vast potential. For example, PROTAC-O4I2 degrades both wild-type and mutant SF3B1 (a core spliceosome component), effectively suppressing tumor growth in a Drosophila model [159]. This proof-of-concept establishes the feasibility of disrupting AS-driven oncogenesis via targeted SF degradation, though rigorous validation in breast cancer models remains necessary. Additionally, emerging RNA-PROTACs utilize RNA mimics conjugated to E3-recruiting peptides to selectively degrade RBPs, offering another precise avenue for AS modulation [160].

Currently, PROTAC development in breast cancer primarily focuses on canonical drivers (e.g., ER, CDKs, and HER2) rather than splicing machinery [161]. Notably, the ER-targeting PROTAC ARV-471 outperforms fulvestrant in preclinical ER+/HER2- models and has entered clinical trials [162]. Redirecting this degradation technology toward AS regulation could provide a robust framework to circumvent therapeutic resistance. However, clinical translation faces significant pharmacological hurdles. Due to their large molecular weight, PROTACs often exhibit poor cell permeability, low aqueous solubility, and suboptimal bioavailability. Furthermore, they are susceptible to the “hook effect,” where excessive dosages paradoxically diminish degradation efficiency by favoring inactive binary complexes over functional ternary complexes [163]. Additionally, the ubiquitous expression of recruited E3 ligases across normal tissues raises substantial concerns regarding off-target protein degradation and subsequent systemic toxicity, which must be rigorously evaluated before clinical application.

In summary, while these diverse strategies underscore the therapeutic potential of AS in breast cancer, each faces distinct trade-offs. Small molecules and modification-targeting agents are more readily translatable; however, their tendency to target core spliceosome components often results in broad-spectrum splicing disruptions and significant systemic toxicity. Conversely, ASOs and CRISPR-Cas9 provide exquisite sequence specificity, yet their clinical utility is constrained by in vivo delivery barriers and, in the case of CRISPR, potential off-target genomic alterations. While emerging modalities such as PROTACs uniquely overcome enzymatic resistance by degrading undruggable splicing factors, optimizing their pharmacokinetic profiles and mitigating off-target degradation remain critical prerequisites for their clinical success. Future progress will depend on integrating advances in delivery technologies, refining the functional annotation of cancer-specific splicing events, and identifying context-dependent vulnerabilities that maximize therapeutic index while minimizing toxicity.

Conclusions and future perspectives

AS dysregulation is recognized as a fundamental driver of breast cancer heterogeneity, metastasis, and therapeutic resistance. While targeting AS represents a compelling therapeutic vulnerability, translating these findings into clinical applications necessitates a rigorous synthesis of viable strategies alongside a critical evaluation of the remaining translational barriers.

From a translational perspective, the field has amassed a sophisticated arsenal of AS-targeted strategies, ranging from pharmacological modulation by small molecules and PROTACs to sequence-directed interventions via ASOs and CRISPR-Cas9. However, the successful clinical translation of any of these modalities hinges on overcoming two universal bottlenecks: achieving event-level splicing specificity to prevent systemic toxicity, and developing highly efficient, tumor-targeted delivery platforms. While tools like PROTACs and ASOs theoretically offer the precision required, their practical application remains stymied by complex pharmacokinetics, in vivo instability, and physical barriers to tumor penetration. Therefore, future developmental pipelines should maximize event-level splicing precision and prioritize next-generation delivery vehicles, such as antibody-oligonucleotide conjugates or advanced lipid nanoparticles, to safely navigate these pharmacokinetic hurdles and maximize the therapeutic index.

Beyond pharmacological challenges, addressing the profound pathological and genomic heterogeneity of breast cancer dictates that AS landscapes vary significantly across clinical subtypes, necessitating highly tailored interventions [134]. The identification of novel targets allows for the development of precision medicine approaches, where treatments can be tailored to the individual genetic and molecular profile of a patient’s tumor. In the era of high-throughput analysis, whole transcriptome sequencing is considered a critical technique for personalized therapies [149]. Research has also developed a long-read RNA sequencing (LR-seq) and analysis platform to identify previously unannotated breast cancer-specific and functional splicing events [164]. Additionally, single-cell LR-seq coupled with spatial transcriptomics holds promise for revealing how AS contributes to breast cancer [78]. Lastly, quantitative proteomics could help link AS changes to their functional consequences, accelerating the discovery of novel druggable targets [78].

In parallel with direct transcriptomic intervention, tumor-specific AS events serve as a rich, yet underexploited, source of highly immunogenic neoantigens. While AS-derived neoantigens recognized by T cells have been successfully targeted in malignancies such as melanoma, their experimental validation in breast cancer remains in its infancy [165]. With the advancement of AS analysis methods, more neoantigen vaccines and corresponding TCR-T cells may become candidates for breast cancer immunotherapy. However, exploiting this axis presents unique evolutionary challenges; researchers must anticipate and mitigate tumor immune evasion and clonal resistance, ensuring that therapies account for the dynamic survival propensity of heterogeneous cancer cell populations.

In summary, the future of breast cancer therapy based on AS is characterized by innovative approaches that leverage the unique splicing profiles of tumors, aiming to develop more effective and personalized treatment strategies. The integration of advanced technologies and a deeper understanding of AS mechanisms will likely enhance therapeutic outcomes for breast cancer patients.
