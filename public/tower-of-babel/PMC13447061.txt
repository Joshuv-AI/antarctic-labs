Radiographic evaluation, risk factors, and socio-economic impact of metapodial and phalangeal lameness in dairy cows
Authors: El-Sayed El-Shafaey, Yahia A. Amin, Mohamed Tharwat, Mohamed A. Hamed, Khaled Abouelnasr, Zakriya Al Mohamad, Mohamed Abdo Rizk, Ahmed M. A. Meligy, Yassir A. Almofti, Saad Shousha, Mahmoud G. El Sebaei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionLameness is a major welfare and economic concern in the dairy industry, yet lesions of the metapodial (canon bones) and phalanges remain relatively under-recognized compared with claw horn disorders. This study investigated the prevalence, radiographic characteristics, risk factors, and socio-economic impact of metapodial and phalangeal lameness in dairy cows.MethodsOver 12 months, a cross-sectional study was conducted on 1,050 Holstein–Friesian cows from three commercial farms with different flooring systems: concrete (n = 300), earthen (n = 350), and sand (n = 400). Risk factors including age, body weight, flooring type, limb involvement, and concurrent diseases were evaluated, along with the effects on weight gain, fertility, and milk yield.Results205 cows with clinical and radiographic evidence of metapodial and phalangeal lesions were identified. The overall prevalence of metapodial and phalangeal lesions was 19.52%. Flexural deformities (hyperflexion posture) were the most common lesion (29.27%), followed by exostosis (14.63%) and fractures (12.19%). Older cows (6–7 years) and heavier animals (500–600 kg) were significantly more affected (p < 0.05). Concrete flooring was strongly associated with increased lesion risk. Lesions occurred predominantly in the hindlimbs, especially the left hindlimb, and were commonly associated with clinical lameness and pain. Metapodial and phalangeal lesions significantly reduced weight gain, reproductive performance, and milk yield.ConclusionIn conclusion, metapodial and phalangeal lesions represent a significant clinical burden (n = 205/1,050) of cows represent a permanent loss of biological and economic longevity for the dairy farm. We have demonstrated that skeletal involvement is not an occasional complication but a primary driver of the lameness complex in modern dairy production. Radiographic evaluation is recommended for accurate early-stage detection to mitigate the substantial socio-economic burden of bovine lameness.

Introduction

Lameness in dairy cattle is among the most significant challenges to animal welfare, farm productivity, and economic sustainability in modern dairy production. It consistently ranks among the top three most prevalent and costly health disorders, alongside mastitis and infertility (1–3). Lameness causes direct (milk yield, impaired reproductive performance) and indirect losses (increased treatment costs, premature culling, labor) to cattle farms, requiring management (4–6). Beyond economics, lameness is a critical welfare issue (pain, discomfort, compromised natural behavior) which affects the cattle health and productivity (7–10).

While lameness in dairy cattle encompasses a wide range of etiologies, disorders affecting the metapodial region alongside the phalanges, including exostosis, osteitis, septic arthritis, fractures, and fetlock deformities, are particularly critical. However, they induce varying degrees of clinical lameness that is clinically and socio-economically vital in dairy herd management (11–14). The pathogenesis of metapodial and phalangeal lesions is complex and multifactorial, involving interactions among genetics, conformation, nutrition, management practices, and environmental factors (15, 16). Although digit disorders are reported worldwide across breeds and production systems, they are frequently overlooked in favor of more common claw horn lesions (17).

Digit disorders are frequently reported in dairy cattle worldwide, regardless of breed, use, or stabling. Surveys have shown that most (90%) lameness cases involve claws (1–3). Digit lameness is recognized at the clinical level in a lame cow, at the subclinical level during claw trimming, and at diagnostic investigation of the affected digit (18–24). Radiography has been used for years to diagnose bone disorders in cattle practice (25, 26). It provides detailed information on the nature and severity of osseous lesions, enabling a definitive diagnosis even when clinical findings are unclear (27). Early and accurate detection of abnormal changes allows for appropriate treatment and may prevent lameness from becoming a chronic problem that could affect the welfare and productivity of cattle (28–30).

Most previous studies on lameness prevalence have focused on claw horn and soft tissue disorders. However, comparative studies quantifying the risk of specific bone lesions are with limited emphasis in the literature, despite their potential to cause severe and persistent lameness. This lack of radiographic and epidemiological data on bony disorders limits veterinarians’ and producers’ ability to make informed prognostic judgments and implement targeted prevention strategies (31, 32). Therefore, this study primarily aimed to establish an integrated diagnostic and socio-economic framework for metapodial and phalangeal lesions in dairy cows. By combining radiographic characterization of internal osseous lesions with an epidemiological analysis of animal and environmental risk factors, we aimed to quantify the specific impact of these subclinical and clinical bone pathologies on herd productivity and economic viability parameters, including weight gain, fertility performance, and milk yield. This holistic approach seeks to refine early diagnostic protocols and evidence-based management strategies for bovine lameness.

Materials and methods

Animals and study design

A cross-sectional observational study was conducted from August 2024 to July 2025 across three commercial Holstein–Friesian dairy farms in Dakahlia Province, Egypt. Farms were purposively selected based on their primary flooring type: concrete (n = 300 cows), earthen (n = 350 cows), and sand (n = 400 cows). All farms maintained uniform management protocols, including ad libitum access to water, a standardized total mixed ration, and a three times daily milking schedule. Only cows exhibiting clinical signs consistent with the metapodial and phalangeal lameness were included in the study (Table 1). Animals were identified via systematic locomotion scoring of the entire herd by two trained observers blinded to the farm’s historical data. To ensure representativeness, cows were selected proportionally from each farm based on herd size, as detailed in Table 1. The selection was performed blindly to the specific radiographic history of the animals to minimize diagnostic bias. The diagnoses of infectious conditions like septic arthritis and osteitis were not taken from retrospective farm records. Instead, they were established through a rigorous prospective two-step clinical and radiographic protocol conducted specifically for this study. Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Committee of Animal Welfare and Ethics at the Faculty of Veterinary Medicine, Mansoura University, Egypt (Approval No. VM.R.25.09.33).

Clinical examination

All cows were initially screened for lameness during routine herd health visits and while moving to the milking parlor by trained farm personnel. Animals exhibiting abnormal gait, altered posture, or reluctance to bear weight were considered potential cases and subjected to a detailed clinical and orthopedic examination. Each affected cow was clinically evaluated whilst walking and resting for assessment of the clinical signs of lameness and pain across both forelimbs and hindlimbs. All these parameters were recorded and scored as clinical index scores by one person (Table 2). Lameness was graded on a scale 0–3 (0 = no lameness; 3 = severe lameness). Pain was defined by the presence of a withdrawal reflex during deep palpation and the percentage of weight-bearing. Pain was graded on a scale 0–3 (Absent score 0, mild, score 1, Moderate, score 2, Severe, score 3). To facilitate a safe and thorough examination, cows were restrained in a vertical hydraulic hoof-trimming chute equipped with belly straps and leg hoists. This allowed each limb to be safely lifted, cleaned, and properly positioned for close visual inspection and palpation. Standard therapeutic claw trimming was performed on all limbs of affected cows by an experienced bovine veterinarian utilizing the modified Toussaint Raven method to assess claw integrity, abnormalities, and reveal any hidden lesions. Clinical markers of infection, such as localized hyperthermia, circumferential swelling, and pain were recorded. During the examination, data were systematically collected on age, body weight, limb involvement, concurrent diseases, fertility status, milk yield, lesion site, lameness severity, and pain expression. These clinical parameters were subsequently analyzed to investigate associations with specific metapodial and phalangeal lesions and their impact on productivity and welfare.

Radiographic examination

Diagnosis of metapodial and phalangeal lesions was confirmed via radiographic examination. Animals were positioned in a standing, weight-bearing stance with the affected foot elevated on a custom block to facilitate image acquisition. Dorsopalmar/plantar (DP), lateromedial (LM), and oblique views of the affected phalanx were obtained using a portable X-ray unit (Samsung-dong, SY-31-100-P, Seoul, Korea) set at 70 kVp and 2 mAs, with a 70 cm focal distance. Interdigital views were captured by inserting a protected cassette directly up into the interdigital space. Radiographic pathognomonic signs of infection that are invisible to the naked eye, specifically osteolysis and periosteal reactions for osteitis, and joint space alterations for septic arthritis, were recorded. All images were assessed by one experienced veterinarian (E. E.) to ensure consistency in technique, diagnostic interpretation, and lesion classification according to the standardized radiographic criteria for bovine osseous pathology (33–37). Lesions were classified encompassing both structural and infectious conditions into 13 distinct types including fetlock hyperflexion, exostosis, fractures, pedal osteitis, laminitis, osteoperiostitis, pedal osteolysis, septic arthritis, toe-tip necrosis, non-infectious arthritis, subluxation, physitis, and fetlock hyperextension. Radiographic changes indicative of chronic laminitis (sequelae of laminar corium inflammation) were diagnosed based on established morphometric and bone-remodeling criteria of the third phalanx (P3) align with classic criteria documented for bovine chronic laminitis and distal phalanx remodeling (38, 39). These included a measurable loss of parallelism (rotation) between the dorsal hoof capsule and the dorsal wall of P3, an increased distance between the dorsal cortex of P3 and the external hoof wall indicating soft tissue thickening, and osteolysis or osteophytic remodeling along the distal apex and solar border of P3.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 21.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics comprised frequencies and percentages for categorical variables. The primary analysis evaluated associations between metapodial and phalangeal disorders and several independent predictors: flooring type, age, body weight category (300–400 kg, 401–500 kg, 501–600 kg), fertility status, milk yield, affected limb, lameness, pain, and weight gain impact. These variables were benchmarked against standard herd-level management targets, including optimal calving intervals and target service numbers. To control the family-wise Type I error rate from multiple parallel comparisons across lesion subgroups, post-hoc pairwise adjustments were applied using the Bonferroni-Holm correction. Association testing for nominal categorical variables was conducted via Chi-square tests. For ordinal variables (age and body weight category), Cochran–Mantel–Haenszel (CMH) tests were utilized to account for ordered data structures. Ordinal clinical index scores were analyzed using Spearman’s rank correlation and Kruskal-Wallis tests. To identify risk factors for metapodial and phalangeal lameness, a multivariable ordinal logistic regression model was constructed. Prior to modeling, Multiple Correspondence Analysis (MCA) was executed to explore relationships among categorical predictors, visualize variable clusters, and screen for multicollinearity, thereby guiding the selection of the most representative predictors. Measures of association were expressed as odds ratios (OR) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI). Two-tailed p-values (<0.05) defined statistical significance.

Results

Overall prevalence and distribution of metapodial and phalangeal lesions

Out of the total cohort (n = 1,050), 205 animals were diagnosed with primary metapodial and phalangeal lesions, resulting in an overall prevalence of 19.5%. In this study, a primary metapodial and phalangeal lesion was defined as a case where a cow presented with clinical lameness, underwent therapeutic claw trimming, which ruled out any primary claw capsule or horn lesions, and subsequently demonstrated radiographically confirmed osseous pathology. The lesions were categorized into thirteen distinct types encompassing both structural and infectious conditions (Table 3). Fetlock flexural deformity (hyperflexion posture) was the most frequently observed lesion, accounting for 29.27% of all diagnosed cases and 5.71% of the total cohort. This was followed by exostosis (14.63% of cases; 2.86% of the cohort) and fractures (12.19% of cases; 2.38% of the cohort). Other lesions, including pedal osteitis (7.32%), laminitis (7.32%), osteoperiostitis (6.34%), pedal osteolysis (4.88%), septic arthritis (4.88%), toe-tip necrosis (3.41%), non-infectious arthritis (2.93%), and subluxation (2.93%), were present at moderate prevalence. In contrast, physitis (2.44%) and Fetlock flexural deformity (hyperextension posture) (1.46%) were less commonly observed.

Effect of age on the frequency of metapodial and phalangeal lesions

The enrolled cows had a mean age of 4.5 ± 1.25 years (range: 2–7 years). Age had a significant effect on the relative distribution of metapodial and phalangeal affections, x2 (2, n = 1,050) = 18.42, p < 0.001. Cows aged 6–7 years exhibited the highest frequency (44.88%) of lesions, while the lowest proportion was recorded in cows aged 2–4 years (17.07%). Several lesions, including exostosis and laminitis, showed a significant linear trend toward increased frequency in older animals (P-trend < 0.01). Conversely, physitis was detected exclusively in the youngest age group. Fetlock hyperflexion and fractures were most common in middle-aged cows (4–6 years) (Figure 6).

Effect of body weight on the frequency of metapodial and phalangeal lesions

The enrolled cows had a mean body weight of 450 ± 75 kg (range: 300–600 kg). The distribution of metapodial and phalangeal lesions across body weight categories revealed a significant positive linear trend (Cochran–Mantel–Haenszel test), with the highest frequency in the 500-600 kg category x2 (2, n = 1,050, = 22.15, p < 0.001) (Table 5). Specifically, chronic degenerative lesions such as exostosis and osteoperiostitis showed a progressive increase in proportion as body weight climbed from the 300–400 kg category to the 500–600 kg category, reaching peak frequency of 53 and 62%, respectively. Notably, subluxation and laminitis were almost exclusively observed in the heaviest weight category (83 and 80%, respectively).

Effect of floor type on the prevalence of metapodial and phalangeal lesions

Floor type significantly influenced metapodial and phalangeal lesions prevalence, x2 (2, n = 205 = 31.08, p < 0.001). Concrete flooring (n = 300 total herd size, 102 enrolled lame cows) was consistently associated with the highest prevalence across nearly all lesion types, whereas sand flooring (n = 400 total herd size, 35 enrolled lame cows) showed the lowest prevalence; earthen flooring (n = 350 total herd size, 68 enrolled lame cows) fell between these two extremes (Table 1). The complete distribution of enrolled animals and lameness prevalence by farm and housing system is detailed in Table 1. Relative risk (RR) and odds ratio (OR) analysis revealed that concrete flooring markedly increased the risk of non-infectious arthritis (OR = 5.00), fetlock flexural deformities (hyperflexion posture) (OR = 5.00), laminitis (OR = 7.00, 95% CI = 3.15, 15.54), and pedal osteitis (OR = 4.00) (Table 6). The Multiple Correspondence Analysis (MCA) revealed two primary clusters: one associating concrete flooring with chronic lesions (exostosis, osteoperiostitis), and another associating earthen floors with infectious conditions (septic arthritis). While specific conditions such as physitis showing a 100% association with concrete environments. These findings indicate that hard flooring surfaces substantially elevate the likelihood of digit bones pathology.

Limb distribution of metapodial and phalangeal lesions

Digit bone lesions were significantly more prevalent in the hindlimbs than forelimbs x2 (3, n = 205 = 12.64, p < 0.005), with the left hindlimb being the most frequently affected site across the 205 lame cows. This asymmetrical pattern was statistically significant across most lesion types (Table 7), including pedal osteolysis (40.0% left hind vs. 10.0% right fore; p < 0.05), exostosis (50.0% left hind vs. 10.0% right fore; p < 0.001), osteoperiostitis (46.2% left hind vs. 15.4% right fore; p < 0.01), fetlock flexural deformities (hyperflexion posture) (41.7% left hind vs. 16.7% right fore; p < 0.001), pedal osteitis (46.7% left hind vs. 6.7% right fore; p < 0.001), and toe-tip necrosis (42.9% left hind vs. 14.3% right hind; p < 0.01).

Expression of lameness and pain of metapodial and phalangeal lesions

All diagnosed acute and chronic metapodial and phalangeal lesions were strongly associated with clinical lameness (100%) and overt signs of pain (100%), with the sole exception of laminitis (Table 8). Discussion

Among the primary causes of lameness in dairy cows, digit and claw lesions are particularly important due to their impact on locomotion, welfare, and overall herd productivity (4, 6). Metapodial and phalangeal lesions, although less frequently studied than soft tissue or claw horn disorders, contribute significantly to chronic lameness and can lead to substantial economic losses through reduced milk yield, impaired reproductive performance, increased treatment costs, and premature culling (35, 37). The present study provides a comprehensive epidemiological, radiographic, and socio-economic assessment of metapodial and phalangeal lameness in dairy cows, highlighting its high prevalence, multifactorial risk profile, and pronounced effects on animal welfare and productivity.

The observed 19.5% (205/1,050) prevalence of metapodial and phalangeal lesions is a striking finding that challenges the traditional focus on soft-tissue claw disorders. While clinical surveys often report lameness prevalence between 12 and 25% (2, 4), our study reveals that nearly one-fifth of the entire cohort suffered from digital bone lesions. This higher-than-expected bone involvement suggests that traditional clinical scoring significantly underestimates the internal burden of lameness. Unlike the work of (2, 3, 36), which focused on clinical hoof lesions, our radiographic approach allowed us to identify hidden pathologies like osteoperiostitis and subluxation that would remain undiagnosed in a standard farm check. This differentiates our work by proving that metapodial and phalangeal bone involvement is not a rare complication, but a central component of the lameness complex, and should be considered alongside soft tissue claw lesions in herd health management programs.

Fetlock hyperflexion and fractures (accounting for nearly one-third of cases) were most common in middle-aged cows (4–6 years), suggesting that chronic biomechanical stress and prolonged abnormal limb posture play a central role in the development of metapodial and phalangeal pathology. While (6, 7) noted age as a risk factor for lameness, we provide the anatomical evidence: as cows reach peak milk production and body mass (500–600 kg), the suspensory apparatus effectively fatigues. This leads to a shift from the developmental lesions seen in younger cows (e.g., physitis) to chronic, degenerative bone remodeling. By highlighting this age-weight-lesion interaction, our study provides a more nuanced risk profile than earlier publications that categorized age as a general, rather than a biomechanical risk factor (14, 18, 20).

The pronounced positive correlation between increased body mass (500–600 kg) and phalanges lesions (fetlock hyperextension, laminitis, and physitis) suggests a biomechanical tipping point where skeletal adaptation fails to keep pace with metabolic scaling. While the association between weight and lameness is established, our data uniquely highlights a structural mismatch in modern high-yielding phenotypes: genetic selection for production has potentially outpaced the tensile strength of the distal limb’s suspensory apparatus. Unlike earlier studies that treat weight as a static risk factor, our findings position excessive body mass as a chronic mechanical stressor that exacerbates the compression of the digital cushion and leads to P3 remodeling (3, 6, 23). This underscores a systemic conflict in dairy intensification, where the drive for higher milk energy output inadvertently compromises the animal’s structural longevity. Consequently, weight management must evolve beyond nutritional monitoring to become a strategic pillar of biomechanical risk mitigation in herd health protocols.

While body weight and age often increase concurrently in dairy herds, our Multiple Correspondence Analysis (MCA) and multivariable modeling allowed us to isolate weight as a distinct physical stressor. This suggests that the impact of flooring type and body weight on metapodial and phalangeal integrity is not merely a function of time (age), but also a result of the cumulative vertical pressure on the pedal bone and associated joints. Septic arthritis was also more prevalent in heavier animals; this is likely an indirect relationship. Higher mechanical loads exacerbate primary claw lesions such as sole ulcers and white line disease, which subsequently serve as portals for ascending deep sepsis into the distal interphalangeal joint.

Our findings identify floor topography as a primary driver of metapodial and phalangeal pathology, with concrete surfaces acting as a catalyst for a vicious cycle of mechanical and behavioral stress. The strikingly high risk associated with concrete (OR up to 7.00) suggests that the issue is not merely surface hardness, but a critical lack of shock-absorption and multidirectional friction. This rigid interface forces the distal phalanx to withstand unmitigated vertical impact, likely accelerating the micro-trauma to the subchondral bone and sensitive laminae observed in our radiographic data. While existing literature correlates hard flooring with reduced recumbency (3, 23, 29), our findings suggest that this behavioral shift, the standing-to-lying conflict, transforms a static housing flaw into a dynamic pathological state. Conversely, the protective effect of sand suggests a molding property that redistributes pressure across the entire solar surface, effectively neutralizing the focal loading that leads to phalanges remodeling (1, 20, 21). Transitioning from concrete to bioactive or compressible substrates, such as sand, should therefore be viewed not just as a comfort upgrade but also as a clinical intervention to preserve skeletal integrity.

From a socio-economic perspective, while concrete is favored for its durability and ease of cleaning (reducing labor costs), the hidden costs are substantial. The high odds ratios for chronic conditions like exostosis and pedal osteitis on concrete imply long-term productivity losses, including premature culling, reduced milk yield, and increased veterinary expenses. The narrow confidence intervals for these risks suggest that the negative impact of hard flooring is a consistent and predictable burden on farm profitability. Furthermore, the Multiple Correspondence Analysis (MCA) confirmed that these flooring risks exist independently of cow age or weight. This suggests that transitioning to sand-bedded stalls or rubber-matted alleys, despite the initial capital investment, could yield a significant return on investment by mitigating the high-prevalence bone lesions identified in this cohort.

The radiographic confirmation of laminitis in dairy cattle requires distinct diagnostic criteria compared to equine orthopedics. While distal phalangeal rotation or sinking is the hallmark of the equine disease, bovine laminitis is predominantly characterized by an increase in the thickness of the dorsal soft tissue zone (the distance between the outer dorsal cortex of the distal phalanx and the external claw wall), as demonstrated in Figure 3. This widening reflects chronic congestion, hemorrhage, and subsequent edema or cellular proliferation within the dorsal laminar corium. When coupled with the classic solar margin osteophytes seen in pedal osteitis (Figure 2C), these collective changes reflect the severe suspensory apparatus breakdown that drives herd-level subclinical laminitis and its associated socio-economic deficits.

The significant concentration of lesions in the hindlimbs (p < 0.05), specifically the left hindlimb, aligns with established propulsive models where the hindlimbs bear the majority of propulsive force, shearing stress, and lateral-to-medial weight shifts during locomotion, but adds a critical lateralized environmental dimension (6, 18). While genetic predisposition to poor conformation is often cited as a cause for limb issues, the asymmetrical distribution found here (left vs. right) strongly points toward extrinsic factors (3–6). We hypothesize that the left hindlimb turn patterns in milking parlors, combined with the rigidity of the hindlimbs connection to the axial skeleton, create a concussive trap on concrete surfaces (13). This finding offers a specific actionable insight for facility design, such as parlor entry flow, that is often overlooked in general lameness studies.

Beyond the acute cost of treatment, lesions like pedal osteitis and septic arthritis were associated with severe stasis in weight gain and reduced milk yield, which represents a massive economic leakage (10, 15). P -trends showed that bone lesions are not just a clinical issue but also a direct cause of poor growth performance, leading to the economic losses of dairy farms. This aligns with, but expands upon, the findings of (5, 21) by linking specific bone-level diagnoses to economic outcomes. We demonstrate that skeletal lameness is a silent driver of premature culling, as bone remodeling is often irreversible, unlike primary soft-tissue infections.

While global lameness studies often focus on temperate climates (e.g., Europe and North America), our findings provide a critical baseline for Egyptian dairy systems, where intensive management on concrete flooring, combined with high ambient temperatures that may affect standing behavior, creates a unique risk profile. A 19.5% prevalence of skeletal pathology in this region is a significant finding that suggests current Egyptian dairy management protocols may be overlooking deep-tissue digital health.

The significant decline in fertility and milk production (p < 0.05) across all lesion categories suggests that metapodial and phalangeal pathology serves as a profound disruptor of the animal’s productive homeostasis. Our findings reveal a hierarchy of impact: while most lesions incur costs, those characterized by structural instability and chronic nociception, specifically fetlock hyperflexion (knuckling), laminitis, and fractures, represent a catastrophic failure of the locomotor system. We posit that the severity of these specific pathologies triggers a sustained allostatic load, where the energetic cost of pain and tissue repair diverts metabolic precursors away from the mammary gland and the hypothalamic–pituitary-ovarian axis. The observed reduction in reproductive efficiency is likely not just a behavioral consequence of poor estrous expression, but also a physiological result of a negative energy balance exacerbated by chronic inflammatory stress. Ultimately, these data transition phalanges lameness from a welfare concern to a primary driver of socio-economic attrition, where the most severe skeletal failures dictate the farm’s break-even point.

The presentation of bilateral, advanced destruction of the distal interphalangeal joints (Figure 5) highlights a critical juncture where severe bone pathology intersects with herd welfare management. Pathologically, such extensive osteolysis, subchondral bone resorption, and articular collapse can only develop over months of unmanaged, severe inflammatory stress. This indicates that the animal experienced intense, chronic pain while remaining undetected or untreated within the production herd. Permitting digital disease to progress to bilateral joint collapse reflects an operational failure in early lameness identification and proactive locomotion scoring. From an economic standpoint, cows presenting with end-stage lesions of this magnitude represent a complete financial loss; therapeutic hoof trimming, orthopedic blocking, or joint flushing are entirely non-viable, leaving immediate euthanasia or emergency salvage slaughter as the only options.

Currently, most farm-level diagnoses are limited to what can be seen during claw trimming or visual gait scoring (18, 26). Our study demonstrates that radiography is not merely a research tool but a clinical necessity for a true differential diagnosis (11, 17). Unlike previous studies, our use of four radiographic views (DP, LM, oblique, and interdigital) allowed for the differentiation between periosteal reactions (osteoperiostitis) and true joint sepsis. Radiography proved essential for the structural characterization of metapodial and phalangeal fractures, demonstrating distinct radiolucent fracture lines and allowing differentiation between intra-articular and extra-articular involvement. In some cases, alterations in bone alignment and segment displacement were mapped, particularly in comminuted fracture configurations. In addition, it allows the clinician to rule out severe osteoarthrosis, periarticular osteophytes, or deep sepsis, which heavily influence the socio-economic decision between surgical intervention for fetlock flexural deformities (such as a superior check ligament or DDFT tenotomy) and culling. By identifying thirteen distinct types of lesions, we show that lameness is too broad a term. The ability to differentiate between infectious osteitis and mechanical exostosis via radiography is essential for determining whether a cow requires antibiotics, surgical intervention, or culling (22, 25–27). This shift from surface-level to deep-tissue diagnostics is the primary contribution of this work to the field of bovine orthopedics.

Our radiographic observations demonstrate that clinical recovery from severe pododermatitis or sole ulcers often results in permanent skeletal remodeling, as evidenced by the smooth-edged third phalanx defect in Figure 5. While the bone margins are entirely quiescent and rounded signaling a long-standing, healed lesion rather than active pedal osteitis the altered internal architecture permanently compromises the mechanical integrity of the digit. This structural deficit continuously predisposes the claw to localized gas, debris, and material pocketing within the sole-wall interface.

In the context of the Egyptian dairy cows, where bone-level disorders represent a major health burden, the integration of early radiographic screening is vital. Such precision allows for more targeted treatment protocols and informed prognostic decisions, ultimately curbing the long-term economic and welfare erosions associated with chronic skeletal lameness.

To mitigate the high prevalence of metapodial and phalangeal lesions, dairy management must shift toward systematic preventive protocols. Housing design is paramount; transitioning from abrasive concrete to softer, non-abrasive surfaces or providing consistent, deep bedding is critical to attenuate the concussive impact forces on the distal limb (3, 21, 28). Furthermore, nutritional management must be strategically calibrated to prevent excessive body weight particularly in late-lactation and multiparous cows, thereby reducing the chronic mechanical overload on the suspensory apparatus (5, 9).

We recommend that routine limb examinations be integrated with professional hoof trimming to detect subclinical skeletal changes before they reach the threshold of irreversible damage, and successfully prevent the development of recurrent, costly lameness events that drive premature herd culling. Targeted monitoring of high-risk demographics, specifically older, high-mass cows maintained on hard flooring, should be a cornerstone of modern herd health programs (22, 24). Ultimately, integrating genetic selection for locomotor soundness alongside production traits offers a long-term pathway to enhanced resilience (7, 19). Addressing these skeletal pathologies is not merely a welfare necessity but a vital strategy to recover hidden losses in milk yield, fertility, and overall farm profitability.

From a socio-economic standpoint, the high prevalence of these conditions, particularly fetlock hyperflexion and pedal osteitis, represents a substantial economic burden due to reduced weight gain, impaired fertility, and shortened productive lifespans. Furthermore, the application of ordinal statistical modeling confirmed a significant linear trend between increasing body weight and the prevalence of chronic bone pathologies, independent of cow age. The distinct clustering of variables identified via Multiple Correspondence Analysis highlights that while farm management often prioritizes the durability of concrete, the resulting bone lesions lead to hidden financial losses.

While this study provides a unique radiographic and epidemiological perspective on bovine digit health, certain limitations must be acknowledged. First, the research was conducted on a limited number of farms within a single geographic region, which may limit the generalizability of the results to other dairy production systems. Second, the cross-sectional nature of this study allows for the identification of significant associations but precludes the establishment of direct causality between specific risk factors and the onset of metapodial and phalangeal lesions. Third, the lack of specific claw-level anatomical tracking (lateral versus medial digits) during the radiographic examinations that would have allowed for a more granular verification of classic bovine weight-bearing models, which typically attribute higher claw-level lesion scores.

Notably, as this was a radiographic and clinical survey, the specific microbial landscape associated with infectious lesions like septic arthritis was not isolated via culture. Additionally, while we identified major risk factors, variables such as precise nutritional formulations, exact hoof-trimming frequencies, and individual genetic markers were not within the scope of this assessment. Future longitudinal research involving larger, multicenter populations and incorporating microbiological isolation is warranted to further elucidate the pathogenesis and bacterial etiology of these chronic metapodial and phalangeal pathologies.

In conclusion, metapodial and phalangeal lesions represent a significant clinical burden (n = 205/1,050). Because bone remodeling is often irreversible, these 19.5% of cows represent a permanent loss of biological and economic longevity for the farm. We have demonstrated that skeletal involvement is not an occasional complication but a primary driver of the lameness complex in modern dairy production. Radiographic evaluation is recommended for accurate early-stage detection to mitigate the substantial socio-economic burden of bovine lameness. Furthermore, improving floor quality and monitoring body weight categories are essential strategies for reducing bovine lameness. These findings provide a data-driven foundation for veterinarians and farm managers to implement targeted interventions that balance facility maintenance with the long-term orthopedic health and profitability of the dairy herd.
